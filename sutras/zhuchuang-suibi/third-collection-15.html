竹窗隨筆・三筆 · Entries 65–70

Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Third Collection, Part 15

Continuing the Third Collection (三筆). This section moves from a call for genuine focused concentration in study, through a warning against premature claims of "great awakening," a Han-dynasty official's remarkable equanimity used to shame complacent monastics, a careful scholarly assessment of famous Tang and Song laymen's Chan attainment, three admiring portraits of laywomen, and closes with a genuinely unsettling set of cautionary tales about the real spiritual danger of performing the food-offering rite without sufficient sincerity.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: work in progress, entries 65–70 of 三筆 Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

65. 學貴專精 — Learning Prizes Focused Concentration

Three vivid examples of legendary scholarly self-discipline become a sharp rebuke of monks who allow worldly distraction into a teaching meant to transcend the world entirely.

Chinese Source

古人為學，有三年不窺園者，有閉戶不踰檻外者，有得家書，見「平安」二字，即投水不展視者，庶幾乎專精不二者矣！而為僧者學出世法，反以世事亂其心乎？吾輩觀此，當汗顏悚骨，而惕于中矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the ancients devoted to study, there were those who did not so much as glance at their own garden for three years, those who shut their own door and never once crossed the threshold, and those who, receiving a letter from home, seeing only the two characters "all is well," threw it into the water without ever unrolling it further — these come close to what may truly be called single-minded, undivided concentration! And yet monks, who study a teaching that transcends this world entirely, instead allow worldly affairs to disturb their own minds? Seeing this, we ought to feel our own faces burn with shame, and our very bones tremble with real self-warning!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

These three vignettes of legendary scholarly discipline are recurring figures in Chinese literati culture — most famously the Han-dynasty scholar Dong Zhongshu, said not to have looked at his own garden for three years while immersed in study — and Zhuhong's use of them here directly parallels the argument already developed at length in this collection's entries 39–40 above, where even respectable-sounding activity was shown capable of quietly wasting a monk's own time.

66. 傳燈 — Transmitting the Lamp

A warning against the casual, unverified claims of "great awakening" made by inexperienced practitioners and confirmed by discernment-less elders, contrasted with the towering, historically verified stature of the masters recorded in the classic transmission histories.

Chinese Source

《傳燈錄》所載諸師，如六代相承、五燈分燄諸大尊宿，皆天下古今第一流人物，所謂「始知周孔外，別自有英豪」者是也，豈易言哉？而今人或得一知半見，或得些少輕安，便自以為大悟大徹；而無眼長老又或以東瓜印子印之，一盲眾盲，非徒無益而有害，可勝悼歟！

Ru-Yi Translation

The venerable masters recorded in the Transmission of the Lamp — the six patriarchs in unbroken succession, the flames divided among the five lamp-histories — are all figures of the very first rank across all of history, exactly as it was once said: "only then did I know that beyond Confucius and the Duke of Zhou, there existed heroes of an entirely different order." Is this a title easily earned? And yet people today, having grasped some half-understood insight, or experienced a moment of slight ease and lightness in meditation, immediately consider themselves greatly and thoroughly awakened; and elders lacking real discernment then go on to confirm them with a mere counterfeit seal — one blind man leading a crowd of other blind men, not merely useless but genuinely harmful. How this deserves real lament!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Transmission of the Lamp is a genuine, foundational eleventh-century Chan history, recording one hundred seventy-one generations of patriarchal succession across the tradition's five houses; the "counterfeit seal" Zhuhong invokes (東瓜印子, literally a "winter-melon seal," made by pressing a sliced melon rather than a real carved stamp) is a standard Chan idiom for exactly the careless, unverified confirmation this entry warns against — the same danger already illustrated concretely in this collection's own entry 53, where Attendant Ping's own unearned confidence in his realization led to genuine catastrophe.

67. 劉公真菩薩人 — Lord Liu, a True Bodhisattva Among Laypeople

A Han-dynasty official's genuine equanimity — governing through mercy rather than harsh punishment, and remaining unruffled even when soup was deliberately spilled on his own court robes — becomes a pointed challenge to monks who claim spiritual discipline without matching this layman's actual demonstrated character.

Chinese Source

劉公諱寬，其治郡也，有過者以蒲鞭示辱。夫人欲試其怒也，使婢故以羹汙朝衣，公但曰：「羹爛汝手乎？」終不怒。即此二事，知其真菩薩人，不可企及。且今之治民者，用格外之嚴刑尚不能折獄；蒲鞭而民自化之，非大威神力何以至此？今御下人，小不如意，動輒加刑；羹汙朝衣，反恤之而不責，非大慈悲力何以至此？臨朝逼迫，而乃從容易衣，心不動搖，非大禪定力何以至此？火宅中具如是操略、如是器量，勝出家兒蒲團上三十年工夫矣！吾輩觀此，可不愧乎？可不勉乎？

Ru-Yi Translation

Lord Liu, whose personal name was Kuan, governed his own district using only a reed whip to punish wrongdoing, shaming rather than injuring the offender. His own wife, wishing to test his temper, once had a servant girl deliberately spill soup on his court robes — and he only asked: "Did the soup scald your hand?" — never once growing angry. From these two incidents alone, we know he was truly a bodhisattva among laypeople, a standard most people could never hope to reach.

Consider: those who govern the people today, even using punishments of the harshest severity, still cannot bring their own legal cases to a proper resolution; and yet Lord Liu, using only a reed whip, was able to transform the hearts of his own people through moral example alone — without some vast, numinous power, how could this possibly have been achieved? Consider further: those who command servants today, at the slightest displeasure, immediately resort to punishment; and yet when soup was spilled on Lord Liu's own robes, he responded instead with concern rather than reproach — without some vast power of compassion, how could this possibly have been achieved? And when pressed for time before an urgent audience at court, he could still change his robes calmly, his mind entirely undisturbed — without some vast power of meditative stability, how could this possibly have been achieved?

For a layperson, still dwelling within the burning house of this turbid world, to possess such genuine bearing and such breadth of character, surpasses even thirty years of a monk's own meditation cushion! Seeing this, we ought to feel real shame, and strive all the harder to improve ourselves.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Liu Kuan is a genuinely documented Han-dynasty official whose biography in the Book of the Later Han records both anecdotes exactly as Zhuhong retells them here — his use of a reed whip specifically to shame rather than injure wrongdoers, and the deliberate soup-spilling test his own wife devised to probe his composure; Zhuhong's insistence that this single layman's demonstrated equanimity "surpasses even thirty years" of formal monastic discipline is one of the sharpest instances anywhere in this collection of a layperson's real conduct being held up as superior to merely nominal monastic practice.

68. 續原教論 — Continuing the Treatise on the Origin of Teaching

Zhuhong endorses a careful scholarly assessment of several famous Tang and Song officials' Chan attainment, agreeing that even genuine insight achieved while still immersed in worldly affairs cannot be assumed to have been fully sustained.

Chinese Source

國初翰林待詔沈士榮居士作《續原教論》，其詳品「名儒學佛」一篇，備舉唐宋諸君子，如白香山、蘇內翰，以至裴丞相、楊大年等諸公，禪學淺深，最為精覈。其言曰：「即裴楊諸公，不云無悟入，而保養受持則未可知也。豈有身居名利之場，又非果位菩薩，而能無細惑流注者哉？」遊戲法門者固不必論矣，我輩身為出家兒者，試靜思之。

Ru-Yi Translation

At the beginning of our own dynasty, Layman Shen Shirong, a court attendant in the Hanlin Academy, wrote his own Continuing Treatise on the Origin of Teaching, in which one carefully detailed chapter, "Confucian Scholars Studying Buddhism," assembles Tang and Song gentlemen of note — Bai Xiangshan, Su the Academician, all the way through Chancellor Pei and Vice Minister Yang — and examines the depth of each man's own understanding of Chan with genuine precision and rigor. He writes: "Even men like Pei and Yang cannot be said to have lacked genuine insight — and yet whether they truly sustained and preserved that insight over time remains an open question. For how could anyone dwelling amid the very arena of fame and profit, and not yet a bodhisattva of the fruit stage itself, possibly remain free of subtle, continuously flowing delusion?" Those whose realization has already reached free, unimpeded mastery need not be discussed at all — but as for the rest of us, monks who have left the household life, let us each quietly reflect on exactly where our own practice truly stands.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Chancellor Pei is the same Pei Xiu already discussed at length in the Second Collection's entries 65 and 104 above; Yang Dayan (Yang Yi) was a genuine Song scholar-official who compiled the imperial Buddhist canon catalogue and personally edited the Transmission of the Lamp discussed in the previous entry — Zhuhong's endorsement here of Shen Shirong's cautious, unresolved verdict on even these celebrated laymen's attainment directly reinforces the same standard of unearned claims to realization warned against in entries 53 and 66 immediately above.

69. 三賢女 — Three Worthy Women

Three personally witnessed portraits of devoted laywomen — a lifelong ascetic nun, a woman who hand-copied the entire Flower Ornament Sutra, and a wife who risked her own life to change her husband's livelihood — each held up as surpassing the ordinary standard of even monastic devotion.

Chinese Source

內人在道稱賢者，吾目擊三人焉：一曰出家尼嚴姓者，清修苦行，終身不干謁富貴家。一在家趙姓者，手書《華嚴經》八十一卷。一在家朱姓者，勸其夫休罷漁業，投身水中。夫末法僧尼，多遊族姓；苦行終身，誰似嚴者？募化書經，或昧因果；自力自書，誰似趙者？為救眾生，不顧身命，終化其夫，誰似朱者？吾謂此三內人，三丈夫也，三大丈夫也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among laywomen renowned for their devotion to the Way, I have personally witnessed three: one, a nun surnamed Yan, who practiced pure austerities her whole life long, never once seeking out wealthy or powerful families. One, a laywoman surnamed Zhao, who copied out the entire eighty-one fascicles of the Flower Ornament Sutra by her own hand. One, a laywoman surnamed Zhu, who, in urging her own husband to give up his fishing trade, threw herself into the water.

In this degenerate age, monks and nuns often cultivate ties with prominent families — who among them can match Nun Yan's own lifetime of austere practice? People who solicit donations or copy scripture often obscure cause and effect from their donors — who can match Laywoman Zhao's own self-supported, hand-copied labor? Who would risk her own life to save others, ultimately transforming her own husband's conduct, as Laywoman Zhu did? I would say these three women were truly great men — no, truly great heroes among men.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhuhong's closing line — calling three women "great men," then correcting himself to "great heroes among men" — deliberately echoes the same honorific he applied to Layman Yuan's own devout mother in the Second Collection's entry 112 above, extending across both collections a consistent willingness to apply his highest term of praise to women whose conduct exceeded the ordinary standard, regardless of gender.

70. 施食師 — Masters of the Food-Offering Rite

A genuinely unsettling set of cautionary accounts — a dying monk nearly dragged off by hostile ghosts, several officiants receiving visible retribution for their own distracted insincerity, and a vision of hundreds of suffering monks in the underworld, all former food-offering officiants — warning that this rite carries real spiritual danger when performed without complete sincerity.

Chinese Source

燄口施食，啟教於阿難，蓋瑜伽部攝也。瑜伽大興於唐之金剛智、廣大不空二師，能役使鬼神，移易山海，威神之力不可思議。數傳之後，無能嗣之者，所存但施食一法而已。手結印、口誦咒、心作觀，三業相應之謂瑜伽，其事非易易也。今印、咒未必精，而況觀力乎？則不相應矣！不相應，則不惟不能利生，而亦或反至害己。昨山中一方外僧病已篤，是晚外正施食，謂看病者言：「有鬼挈我同出就食，辭不往。俄復來云：『法師不誠，吾輩空返，必有以報之。』於是牽我臂偕行。眾持撓鉤套索云：『欲拽此法師下地。』我大怖，失聲呼救，一時散去。」越數日僧死。蓋未死前，已與諸鬼為伍矣；向非驚叫，臺上師危乎哉！不惟是耳！一僧不誠，被鬼舁至河中欲沈之；一僧失鎖衣篋，心存匙鑰，諸鬼見飯上皆鐵片，遂不得食；一僧曬氈衣未收，值天雨，心念此衣，諸鬼見飯上皆獸毛，遂不得食，各受顯報。又一人入冥，見黑房中有僧數百，肌體瘦削，顏色憔悴，似憂苦不堪之狀。問之，則皆施食師也。施食非易易事也，信夫！

Ru-Yi Translation

The burning-mouth food-offering rite originated with Ānanda, and belongs to the esoteric Yoga division of teaching. This Yoga tradition flourished greatly under the Tang masters Vajrabodhi and Amoghavajra, who could command spirits and ghosts, move mountains and seas — their spiritual power was truly inconceivable. Within a few generations after them, no one proved capable of inheriting their full tradition, and only the food-offering rite itself survived. Forming the mudra with the hand, reciting the mantra with the mouth, contemplating with the mind — the three actions working in unison is exactly what "yoga" means, and this is no easy thing to accomplish at all. Today, the mudra and mantra are rarely even performed with real precision, let alone the power of genuine contemplation — and so the three fail to unite at all! And when they fail to unite, not only can the rite fail to benefit any being, it may well turn back and harm the very officiant himself instead.

Recently, a wandering monk in the mountains fell gravely ill, and that same evening, while a food-offering rite was being performed elsewhere, he said to the one nursing him: "Some ghosts came to lead me out to join the feast, and I refused to go. A moment later they returned, saying: 'The officiating master lacks real sincerity — we have returned empty-handed, and someone must answer for it.' And so they seized me by the arm to drag me along. The crowd of them carried hooked poles and ropes, saying: 'We mean to drag this officiant down to the ground with us.' I was terrified, and cried out for help, and at once they all scattered." Several days later, the monk died. He must already have been keeping company with these very ghosts even before his own death — had he not cried out, how great the danger to the officiant on the platform that night would truly have been!

And this was not the only such case. One monk, lacking genuine sincerity, was carried by ghosts and nearly drowned in a river; another monk, having lost the key to his own locked chest, kept thinking of that very key, and the ghosts, seeing nothing but iron fragments on top of the offered rice, could not eat it at all; another monk, having left his own felt robe out to dry and unable to bring it in before a sudden rain, kept thinking of that robe, and the ghosts, seeing nothing but animal hair atop the rice, likewise could not eat it — each receiving visible retribution in turn.

And one man who journeyed into the underworld saw, within a dark chamber, several hundred monks, their bodies emaciated, their faces haggard, wearing an expression of unbearable suffering. Asked who they were, it was said they were all masters of the food-offering rite. Performing this rite is truly no easy matter — how true this proves to be!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Vajrabodhi and Amoghavajra were both genuine, historically documented Tang-dynasty esoteric masters credited with transmitting the full Yoga tantra tradition to China; Zhuhong's argument traces a real historical decline — from masters capable, by tradition, of commanding the elements themselves, down to later officiants who could not even perform the mudra and mantra correctly — using that decline to explain why a rite meant to relieve suffering ghosts could, when performed with divided or insincere attention, instead endanger the very monk performing it, a considerably darker companion piece to the more straightforwardly skeptical treatment of a showman's boiling-water trick in this collection's own entry 28 above.
