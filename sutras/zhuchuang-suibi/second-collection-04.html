Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Second Collection, Part 4

Continuing the Second Collection (二筆) in the source text's own order. This section pairs two entries on Wang Anshi and the Doctrine of the Mean with a sharp critique of Daoist ritual practice, a firsthand account of escaping death through a household's habit of refraining from killing, and a note recovering two little-known but genuinely accomplished lay Chan practitioners.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

72. 王介甫 — Wang Jiefu

A close reading of a Wang Anshi poem on the illusory self, arguing that his insight applies just as sharply to his own well-documented intolerance of criticism — which he failed to apply to himself.

Chinese Source

介甫擬寒山詩有云：「我曾為牛馬，見草荳歡喜；又曾為女人，歡喜見男子。我若真是我，祗合常如此；區區轉易間，莫認物為己。」介甫此言，信是有見，然胡不云：「我曾聞諛言，入耳則歡喜；又曾聞讜言，喜滅而瞋起。我若真是我，祗合常如此；區區轉易間，莫認物為己。」而乃悅諛惡讜，依然認物為己耶！故知，大聰明人說禪非難，而得禪難也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Jiefu, in his poem imitating Hanshan, wrote: "I was once an ox or a horse, and rejoiced at the sight of grass and beans; I was once also a woman, and rejoiced at the sight of a man. If this 'I' were truly myself, it ought to remain constant through it all — amid these petty, shifting changes, do not mistake the thing for the self." Jiefu's words here are certainly insightful — and yet why did he not also say: "I have heard flattering words, and felt joy on hearing them enter my ear; I have also heard blunt, honest words, and felt that joy vanish and anger rise in its place. If this 'I' were truly myself, it ought to remain constant through it all — amid these petty, shifting changes, do not mistake the thing for the self." And yet he delighted in flattery and despised honest speech — still mistaking the thing for the self! From this we know: it is not difficult for a person of great intelligence to talk about Chan — it is attaining Chan that is difficult.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Jiefu is Wang Anshi (1021–1086), the Song reformer whose sweeping New Policies famously collapsed under both conservative opposition and his own well-documented refusal to tolerate dissent among his own staff — a historical reputation that gives real bite to Zhuhong's closing line, since the fault he identifies in the poem's blind spot is not abstract but the specific, attested failing of its own author.

73. 喜怒哀樂未發一 — Before Joy, Anger, Sorrow, and Delight Arise, First Note

A close comparison of Zisi's Confucian "mean" with the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's warning against mistaking mere stillness for realization, arguing the two are not the same attainment at all.

Chinese Source

予初入道，憶子思以喜怒哀樂未發為中，意此「中」即空劫以前自己也。既而參諸楞嚴，則云：「縱滅一切見聞覺知，內守幽閒，猶為法塵分別影事。」夫見聞泯、覺知絕，似喜怒哀樂未發，而曰法塵分別者，何也？意，根也；法，塵也。根與塵對，順境感而喜與樂發，逆境感而怒與哀發，是意根分別法塵也。未發則塵未交於外，根未起於內，寂然悄然，應是本體；不知向緣動境，今緣靜境，向固法塵之麤分別也，今亦法塵之細分別也，皆影事也，非真實也。謂之「幽閒」，特幽勝顯、閒勝鬧耳，空劫以前自己尚隔遠在。此處更當諦審精察，研之又研，窮之又窮，不可草草。

Ru-Yi Translation

When I first entered the Way, I recalled Zisi's teaching that "before joy, anger, sorrow, and delight arise is called the mean" — and I took this "mean" to be one's own self before the empty kalpa itself. Later, investigating the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, I found it says: "Even should one extinguish all seeing, hearing, awareness, and knowing, and inwardly hold to a hidden stillness, this remains only a shadow-play of discrimination among mental objects." Seeing and hearing extinguished, awareness and knowing cut off — this resembles the state before joy, anger, sorrow, and delight arise — and yet the sutra calls it a discrimination of mental objects. Why?

The mind is the faculty; mental objects are the dust. Faculty and dust meet one another: a favorable condition arises and joy and delight follow; an unfavorable condition arises and anger and sorrow follow — this is the mind-faculty discriminating mental dust. Before these arise, dust has not yet met the outer world, and the faculty has not yet stirred within — silent, still, this seems to be the original substance itself. But one fails to realize: formerly the mind clung to moving conditions, and now it clings to still conditions instead; the former was a coarse discrimination of mental dust, and the latter is merely a subtle discrimination of the very same dust — both are mere shadow-plays, not the real thing at all. What the sutra calls "hidden stillness" is only stillness surpassing exposure, quiet surpassing noise — one's own self before the empty kalpa remains still worlds away. This point calls for the most careful, exhaustive investigation, examined again and again, never treated carelessly.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zisi (Kong Ji), Confucius's own grandson, is the traditionally credited author of the Doctrine of the Mean, from which the "before joy, anger, sorrow, and delight arise" line is drawn; Zhuhong is careful, in the companion entry that follows, to insist Zisi's formulation remains a genuine achievement within its own Confucian frame even while falling short of the Śūraṅgama's stricter standard — the point of this entry is not to dismiss Zisi, but to mark exactly where a Confucian and a Buddhist account of stillness part ways.

74. 喜怒哀樂未發二 — Before Joy, Anger, Sorrow, and Delight Arise, Second Note

A rebuke of the Song philosopher Yang Cihu for criticizing Zisi's own teaching while claiming Zisi's own tradition as his authority — and for describing an experience that itself matches exactly what he criticized.

Chinese Source

慈湖楊氏謂：「灼見子思、孟子病同原。」然慈湖自敘靜中所證，空洞寂寥、廣遠無際，則正子思所謂「喜怒哀樂未發」時氣象也。子思此語，以深經微細窮究，故云猶未是空劫以前自己；若在儒宗，可謂妙得孔氏之心法，其言至精至當，何所錯謬，而慈湖病之？慈湖既宗孔氏，主張道學，而乃病子思，則夫子亦不足法矣，將誰宗乎？儻慈湖於佛理妙悟，則宜直言極論儒佛同異，亦不應混作此語，似乎進退無據。

Ru-Yi Translation

Yang Cihu once said: "I have clearly seen that Zisi and Mencius share the same underlying error." And yet Cihu's own account of what he realized in stillness — vast and empty, boundless on every side — is exactly the state Zisi describes as "before joy, anger, sorrow, and delight arise." Zisi's statement, examined through the lens of the deepest, most subtle scriptural investigation, is indeed still short of one's own self before the empty kalpa; but within the Confucian tradition itself, it may fairly be called a subtle grasp of Confucius's own mind-teaching, expressed with the utmost precision and correctness — what fault could it possibly have, that Cihu should criticize it so? If Cihu himself upholds Confucius and advocates the Learning of the Way, and yet criticizes Zisi, then even Confucius himself would no longer be worth following — whom, then, should he follow instead? Had Cihu genuinely attained a subtle understanding of Buddhist principle, he ought to have argued the similarities and differences between Confucianism and Buddhism directly and thoroughly — he ought not to have made a claim like this so carelessly, with no real ground to stand on.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Yang Jian (Cihu, 1141–1226) was a genuine Southern Song philosopher in Lu Jiuyuan's "Learning of the Mind" tradition, whose own writings describe a documented meditative breakthrough at age twenty-eight — a sudden sense of boundless unity with no inside or outside — which Zhuhong points out is functionally identical to the very state Zisi describes and Cihu claims to reject; the entry's real target is the inconsistency of criticizing a teaching one has personally verified through experience.

75. 中峰示眾 — Master Zhongfeng's Instruction to the Assembly

A defense of radical simplicity for those without great spiritual capacity, arguing that conditioned meritorious works can become a dangerous distraction from the urgent matter of one's own birth and death.

Chinese Source

天目中峰和尚示眾云：「汝若無大力量，不若半間草屋棲身，鶉衣丐食，亦免犯人苗稼。」至論也。今出家者，多作有為功德，奔走一生，於自己腳跟下生死大事置之罔聞，不亦謬乎？或曰：「箇箇都是你，則像毀殿塌，僧將露居而枵腹矣！」曰：「非然也。汝力量大，任為之；古人此語，教我等無力量者急先務也。一者大事未明，如喪考妣，則不暇為。二者見理未徹，因果差錯，所謂『有為之功，多諸過咎，天堂未就，地獄先成』，則不敢為。」中峰又云：「一心為本，萬行可以次之也。」至論也。牛頭之於銜花巖，馬祖之於傳法院，遐哉高風，不可再見矣，噫！

Ru-Yi Translation

Master Zhongfeng of Tianmu instructed the assembly, saying: "If you lack great capacity, better to shelter under half a hut of thatch, wearing rags and begging for food, than to trespass on other people's crops." This is a truly penetrating statement. Monks today mostly devote themselves to conditioned meritorious works, running about busily their whole lives, while paying no attention whatsoever to the great matter of birth and death right beneath their own feet — is this not a serious mistake?

Someone might say: "If every monk were like you, the images would go unrepaired and the halls would collapse, and monks would be left sleeping in the open, starving!" I answered: "Not necessarily so. If your own capacity is great, do as you please — but Master Zhongfeng's words here were meant to teach those of us with lesser capacity what our first, most urgent priority ought to be. First, when the great matter is not yet clear, one grieves as though for the death of one's own parents, and has no time to spare for anything else. Second, when one's own understanding is not yet thorough, one risks confusing cause and effect — as the saying goes, 'conditioned merit brings many faults; before heaven's hall is built, hell is already complete' — and so one dares not act rashly."

Master Zhongfeng also said: "Make the one mind the root, and the ten thousand practices may follow as its branches." This too is a truly penetrating statement. Consider Farong of Ox-Head Mountain in his stone cell, or Mazu at the Chuanfa Cloister — such lofty, ancient conduct as theirs, I fear, can never be seen again. Alas!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhongfeng Mingben (1263–1323) was a genuinely renowned Yuan-dynasty Chan master honored as "the living buddha of Jiangnan"; the examples that close the entry are equally specific — Farong's own stone-cell meditation on Ox-Head Mountain, famous for the legend of birds bringing flowers in tribute, and Mazu's awakening at the Chuanfa Cloister under Nanyue Huairang's tile-polishing challenge (the same episode already referenced for its lineage significance in entry 55's discussion of "causes and conditions" above).

76. 醮事謝將殺生 — Killing to Thank the Guardian Generals After a Daoist Ritual

A pointed challenge to the Daoist custom of slaughtering animals to thank guardian-spirit generals after a ritual, arguing the generals themselves would never demand such a thing.

Chinese Source

道流作醮事竟，必謝將，大者殺羊豕，小者買見有三牲。其說曰：「酬將之護壇場也。不爾，且得罪。」嗟乎！「昨日設箇齋，今朝宰六畜，一度造天堂，百度造地獄。」其是之謂乎！夫將，其他吾不能知，只如雲長公之大義天植，王元帥之赤心忠良，彼豈以牲牢之謝介諸懷耶？相沿今古，道流中無一高行者止之，真可悲悼。如恐得罪於將，則近日一江湖無賴，以祈雨鎖械將身，而將不加禍，蓋不與小人較也。而區區為口腹故，反加禍於修功德之齋家也，有是理乎？敢以告夫明理之士君子。

Ru-Yi Translation

When Daoist priests finish performing a ritual, they invariably hold a rite of thanks to the guardian generals — the larger version slaughters a sheep or a pig, the smaller buys ready-made offerings of the "three sacrificial animals." Their explanation is: "This repays the generals for guarding the ritual space — otherwise, we would incur their displeasure." Alas! "Yesterday a vegetarian feast was held; this morning six kinds of livestock are slaughtered — one act builds heaven's hall, a hundred acts build hell instead." Does this not describe exactly such a case?

As for the generals themselves, I cannot speak to the others, but consider only Lord Guan Yunchang's own innate sense of great righteousness, or Marshal Wang's own utterly loyal and upright heart — would either of them truly hold it against anyone for withholding a sacrificial offering? This practice has been handed down from ancient times to the present, and among the whole body of Daoist priests, not a single one of truly high conduct has ever stopped it — a genuinely lamentable thing.

And if one truly fears incurring the generals' displeasure, consider this: recently a disreputable wanderer, praying for rain, actually shackled a statue of one of these very generals in chains to compel the rain — and yet the general brought no misfortune down on him at all, precisely because he does not stoop to contend with a petty, ill-mannered man. How, then, could these same generals possibly bring misfortune down on a family sponsoring a ritual of genuine merit, merely for the sake of a meal? Is there any reason in that at all? I venture to say this to men of principle who understand reason.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Lord Guan (Guan Yu) and Marshal Wang (Wang Lingguan) were both genuinely venerated protector-deities straddling Daoist and Buddhist practice in Zhuhong's own day — Guan Yu especially, whose association with Buddhism traces to a legend of the monk Zhiyi receiving the five precepts from him at Yuquan Mountain; Zhuhong's argument works precisely because both figures were understood, even by their own devotees, as embodiments of scrupulous loyalty and righteousness — making the claim that they would demand a bloody thank-offering self-evidently absurd on the ritual tradition's own terms.

77. 齋月戒殺 — Refraining from Killing During the Vegetarian Months

A historical explanation of the Tang-dynasty custom of restricting new official appointments in three months of the year, recovering its real origin in refraining from killing rather than superstition.

Chinese Source

唐制，正、五、九月，官不蒞任。以蒞任必多宴饗，宴饗必多宰牲，不蒞任者，戒殺也。世人訛傳，以此三月為惡月，而忌諸吉事，蓋迷其所自耳。今時亦戒正、五、九月及十齋日，不得行刑，愛物仁民，聖王好生之心一也。獨惜夫祈晴禱雨，官必禁屠，是明知殺生之為不善矣！胡不齋月齋日遵古戒殺，而必待難生然後禁？嗚呼！難生而始禁，難未平而禁已解，可勝歎哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

Under Tang law, officials did not take up new posts in the first, fifth, or ninth months — because taking up a new post always meant a great banquet, and a great banquet always meant much slaughtering of animals; refraining from taking office in these months was itself a form of refraining from killing. The world has mistakenly come to regard these three months as inauspicious, and avoids all celebratory occasions within them — simply failing to trace the custom back to its actual origin.

Today, too, the first, fifth, and ninth months, along with the ten vegetarian days each month, are set aside as months and days when executions may not be carried out — showing that the sage-kings' compassion for living things, ancient and modern, is one and the same heart. And yet it is a real pity that when praying for clear skies or for rain, officials always forbid butchering at that time — which shows they clearly know killing to be wrong! Why, then, do they not simply observe the ancient prohibition on killing during the vegetarian months and days themselves, rather than waiting for a real calamity before forbidding it? Alas — waiting until calamity strikes before forbidding it, and lifting the prohibition again the moment the calamity has not even fully passed — how this deserves real lament!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Tang prohibition Zhuhong describes is genuinely documented — a decree of Emperor Gaozu's Wude era forbidding executions and butchering in the first, fifth, and ninth months — and the Ming-dynasty scholar Gu Yanwu independently recorded the same custom and its origin in his own Record of Daily Knowledge, confirming that the popular Ming-era belief in these months as simply "inauspicious" had, by Zhuhong's time, already lost track of its own historical rationale.

78. 戒殺延壽 — Refraining from Killing Extends Life

A dated, first-person account of a household spared from death by underworld messengers because of its long-standing practice of refraining from killing.

Chinese Source

華亭趙某，詣清浦探親，舟行次，見一人立舟上，諦視，則亡僕也。驚問之。答云：「見役冥司，今將追取三人耳。」問三人為誰？則曰：「一湖廣人，一即所探親也。」其第三人不答。又問：「得非趙某否？」曰：「然。」趙大駭。至所探親，則已聞室中哭聲矣，益駭甚，趣棹還舍。僕曰：「君且無怖，及夜吾不至，則免矣。」趙問何故。曰：「於路見有為君解者，以君合門戒殺也。」後夜果不至，趙竟無恙，今尚在，已十年矣。萬歷丙午七月記此。

Ru-Yi Translation

A certain Mr. Zhao of Huating traveled to Qingpu to visit relatives. While his boat was underway, he saw a man standing on it, and looking closely, recognized him as a servant of his who had died. Startled, he asked what was happening. The man answered: "I am now serving in the office of the underworld, and today I am to take the lives of three people." Zhao asked who the three were. The man answered: "One is a man from Huguang, and one is the very relative you are going to visit." He did not answer about the third. Zhao asked again: "Could the third not be me myself?" The man answered: "It is." Zhao was greatly alarmed. When he arrived at his relative's house, he could already hear weeping inside, and grew still more alarmed, and hurried back to his boat to return home.

The servant said to him: "Do not be afraid — if I have not come for you by the middle of the night, you will be safe." Zhao asked why. The man answered: "On the road I met someone pleading on your behalf, because your entire household has always refrained from killing." That night, the servant indeed did not come, and Zhao remained unharmed — he is still alive today, ten years after this happened. I record this in the seventh month of the bingwu year of the Wanli era.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The bingwu year of the Wanli era corresponds to 1606 — one of the relatively few entries in this collection Zhuhong dates precisely, and by his own account written down when the man at its center, Mr. Zhao, was still alive ten years after the events described, giving the anecdote the character of contemporary reportage rather than received legend, in keeping with the collection's title as a set of jottings recorded as they came to him.

79. 宋元悟道居士 — Lay Practitioners Who Attained Awakening in the Song and Yuan

A brief act of historical recovery, naming two genuinely accomplished but little-known lay Chan practitioners so their achievement is not lost to history.

Chinese Source

自宋迄元，居士有悟入者，不一而足。宋居士劉興朝，其《悟道集》自敘悟處甚詳，蓋真有得者。元放牛居士，於無門老人「不是不是」處悟入，所作《是非關》，橫說豎說，非具大知見者不能道。此二老蹤跡不甚顯，興朝猶載《傳燈》，放牛罕有知者，吾故表而出之。

Ru-Yi Translation

From the Song through the Yuan dynasty, there have been no shortage of lay practitioners who attained genuine awakening. The Song layman Liu Xingchao described his own realization in great detail in his own Collection on Awakening the Way — clearly a man of genuine attainment. The Yuan-dynasty Oxherd Layman attained awakening through Elder Wumen's repeated response of "not this, not this," and the treatise he composed on it, Barrier of Right and Wrong, argues its point from every angle — only someone of truly great insight could have written it. Neither man's traces are especially well known — Xingchao's story is at least recorded in the Continued Transmission of the Lamp, but the Oxherd Layman is known to almost no one — and so I set them both down here, to bring them into the light.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"Elder Wumen" is Wumen Huikai (1183–1260), compiler of the famous Wumenguan (Gateless Barrier) koan collection — the very title of the Oxherd Layman's own treatise, Barrier of Right and Wrong (是非關), directly echoes his teacher's book title, built from the repeated "not this, not this" (不是不是) that provoked his awakening. This entry is a small, deliberate act of preservation on Zhuhong's part — both men were lay practitioners rather than monastics, and Zhuhong explicitly frames the entry as rescuing the less-documented of the two from obscurity.

80. 無義味語 — Sayings Without Ordinary Meaning

A caution against trying to explain Chan's deliberately non-discursive sayings through ordinary reasoning, which only produces imitation without genuine understanding.

Chinese Source

宗門答話，有所謂無義味語者，不可以道理會，不可以思惟通故也。後人以思惟心強說道理，則愈說而愈遠；豈惟謬說，直饒說得極是，亦只是鸚鵡學人語而已。圓悟老人曰：「汝但情識意解一切妄想都盡，自然於這裏會去。」此先德已驗之方，斷非虛語，吾輩所當深信而力行者也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the exchanges of the Chan school, there is what is called "a saying without ordinary meaning" — something that cannot be grasped through reasoning, nor penetrated through deliberate thought. Later people, trying to force an explanation through the discriminating mind, only drift further and further from the truth the more they speak; not only do they explain it wrongly, but even should they happen to speak with real accuracy, they would still be doing no more than a parrot repeating human speech. The elder Yuanwu said: "Simply exhaust every trace of your own discriminating consciousness and intellectual understanding, every scattered thought, and you will naturally come to understand this on your own." This is a method already tested and confirmed by the masters of the past, by no means an empty saying — it is something we ourselves ought to believe deeply and put into vigorous practice.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Yuanwu Keqin (1063–1135) compiled the Blue Cliff Record, already cited in this collection for its account of "Yunmen's lost powers" (entry 69 above) — here Zhuhong draws instead on Yuanwu's own recorded sayings, using his authority as compiler of Chan's most celebrated koan collection to underwrite a warning against exactly the kind of discursive over-explanation that a koan collection risks inviting from careless readers.
