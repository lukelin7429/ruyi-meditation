Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — First Collection, Part 4

Continuing the First Collection (初筆) of Zhuchuang Suibi in the source text's own order. This section runs from a defense of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's authenticity through two pieces of doctrinal classification (Tiantai's four teachings against Xianshou's five) to some of the collection's most vivid natural imagery — mountain color, night-blooming fragrance, and dreams.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: work in progress, 100 of 132 entries in 初筆 Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

76. 念佛 — Buddha-Recitation

A rebuttal of the common view that Buddha-recitation is only for the unlearned, distinguishing real recollection from mere rote repetition.

Chinese Source

世人稍利根，便輕視念佛，謂是愚夫愚婦勾當。彼徒見愚夫愚婦口誦佛名，心遊千里，而不知此等是名讀佛，非念佛也。念從心，心思憶而不忘，故名曰念。試以儒喻：儒者念念思憶孔子，其去孔子不亦庶幾乎。今念念思憶五欲，不以為非，而反以念佛為非。噫！似此一生空過，何如作愚夫愚婦耶？而惜乎智可能也，愚不可能也。

Ru-Yi Translation

People of the world with a bit of sharpness quickly look down on Buddha-recitation, dismissing it as the business of foolish men and foolish women. They see only that such foolish men and women recite the Buddha's name with their mouths while their minds wander a thousand miles away, and do not realize that this is properly called "reading the Buddha," not "recollecting the Buddha" at all.

Recollection arises from the mind — the mind holding something in memory, never letting it be forgotten; that is what "recollection" truly means. Consider the analogy of a Confucian scholar: a scholar who holds Confucius constantly in mind, recollecting him thought after thought, comes, over time, to draw remarkably close to Confucius himself. People today, by contrast, hold the five desires constantly in mind, thought after thought, without ever considering this wrong — and then turn around and criticize Buddha-recitation instead! Ah — to spend an entire lifetime this way, empty and wasted — how much better it would be to become one of those "foolish men and women" who actually recite the Buddha's name! The pity is only this: it takes real wisdom to be willing to recite the Buddha's name at all — mere foolishness alone is not enough to manage it.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The closing line inverts the reader's expectation twice over: not only is Buddha-recitation not beneath the wise, it is in fact beyond the reach of mere foolishness — a deliberately provocative final turn.

77. 僧性空 — The Monk Xingkong

A cautionary account of a monk who entered solitary retreat too soon, and died mad — read as a warning against faith without wisdom.

Chinese Source

吳泗洲寺僧性空，棄應院，閉關堯封山。嘗寄予所發誓願，及稟告十方等語，予嘉歎希有。俄而魔著，遂癲狂以死，予甚悼焉。揆其由，蓋由乍起信心，有信無慧故也。古人心地未通，不遠千里，參師訪道，出一叢林，入一保社，乃至窮遊遍歷，曾不休息。得意之後，方於水邊林下，長養聖胎耳。何得才離火宅，便入死關？有過不知，有疑莫辨，求升而反墮，又奚怪其然哉！頗有初心學人，結茅深山，孤孑獨居，自謂高致，雖未必魔癲，而亦頓失利益不少。明者試一思之。

Ru-Yi Translation

A monk named Xingkong at Sizhou Temple in Wu abandoned his regular monastery and went into solitary retreat on Mount Yaofeng. He once sent me a copy of the vows he had made, together with the declaration he had addressed to the buddhas of the ten directions, and I praised it as a rare and admirable thing. Before long, though, he became possessed by demonic disturbance, and died raving mad — I grieved deeply over it.

Reflecting on the cause, I believe it came from having only just given rise to faith, with faith present but wisdom still lacking. In ancient times, before their own minds had become truly clear, practitioners would travel a thousand miles without hesitation, seeking out teachers and inquiring after the Way — passing from one monastic community into another, even wandering ceaselessly from place to place, never resting. Only after they had truly grasped the essential point would they finally retire to some quiet spot by the water or under the trees, to nurture the embryo of sagehood within. How could this monk, having only just escaped the burning house, leap straight into death's own gate? Faults he did not recognize, doubts he could not resolve — seeking to rise, he fell instead. Is it any wonder it turned out this way?

There are quite a few students newly resolved on the path who build a thatched hut deep in the mountains and live there alone, believing this makes them lofty and transcendent. Such people may not necessarily go mad in this way, but they too lose no small benefit by abandoning the chance for real guidance. Let those with clear judgment give this some thought.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A companion warning to entry 24 ("The Moment Must Not Be Let Slip"): both entries urge urgency in early practice, but here the target is the opposite failure — moving too fast into isolated intensive practice before the necessary foundation of guidance has been laid.

78. 行腳 — Wandering on Foot

A personal reflection contrasting the hardship of Zhuhong's own early pilgrimage years with the complacency of monks who settle comfortably from the start.

Chinese Source

予單丁行腳時，忍饑渴，衝寒暑，備歷諸苦。今幸得把茆蓋頭，雖不識修行，而識慚愧。雲水乍到，供事唯勤；己身受用，不敢過分。蓋謂「曾為浪子偏憐客，窮漢起家惜土如金」也。今乍入緇門，便住現成菴院，事事如意，喻似富家兒不諳民間疾苦，縱才智兼人，無賴參訪，而閉門自大，習成我慢，增長無明，亦所失多矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

When I myself traveled alone on foot as a pilgrim, I endured hunger and thirst, braved cold and heat, and underwent every kind of hardship. Now that I am fortunate enough to have a thatched roof over my head, even though I cannot claim to truly understand practice, I do know what shame and gratitude mean. When traveling monks arrive unexpectedly, I make sure to serve and attend to them with all diligence; as for my own needs, I never dare to indulge them beyond what is necessary. This is exactly what is meant by the saying: "Someone who was once a wanderer himself feels a special tenderness for the traveler; a poor man who built his own fortune treasures even a clod of earth like gold."

There are people today who, having only just entered the monastic gate, settle straight into an established, comfortable monastery, with everything arranged conveniently for them. This is exactly like the son of a wealthy family who has no idea what hardship among ordinary people actually feels like. Even granting that such a person is unusually gifted and talented, and has no real need to travel and seek instruction elsewhere, still — to shut the door and grow complacent in one's own importance is to cultivate the habit of spiritual conceit, and to let ignorance grow — and this, too, means losing no small opportunity for real, upward progress.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Compare entry 93 below ("Wandering on Foot, Settling on a Mountain"), which addresses the same tension from the opposite direction — warning against travel undertaken as mere tourism rather than genuine seeking.

79. 妙宗鈔 — The Marvelous Tenets Commentary

A defense of a technically demanding Pure Land commentary against the charge that it makes an "easy path" needlessly difficult, followed by two companion notes on why Chan practitioners possess but deliberately refuse to exercise the power of projecting the spirit from the body.

妙宗鈔 — The Marvelous Tenets Commentary

Chinese Source

曩一僧謂予曰：「佛示西方，本為普利諸根，速超生死，是易行道。而知禮法師純以台教精深觀法釋之，使易反成難，失如來曲為凡夫本意。」此論亦甚有理。今思之，古人謂解佛經，寧以淺為深，毋以深為淺。則妙宗所說，利根者自悟深理，鈍根者亦不失依經直觀，求願往生，似無所礙。

Ru-Yi Translation

A monk once said to me: "The Buddha revealed the Western Pure Land precisely to benefit all capacities universally, and to allow swift transcendence of birth and death — this is the 'easy path of practice.' But Master Zhili's commentary explains it using nothing but the deep and subtle contemplative methods of the Tiantai teaching, turning what was easy into something difficult, and losing the Tathāgata's own original intention of accommodating ordinary, unenlightened people." This argument, too, has real merit.

But thinking it over now, I recall that the ancients held: in explaining a sutra, it is better to render something simple as something profound than to render something profound as something merely simple. Given this, what the Marvelous Tenets Commentary teaches leaves those of sharp capacity free to awaken to its profound principles on their own, while those of duller capacity lose nothing — they can still follow the sutra's direct method of contemplation and seek rebirth in the Pure Land just the same. There seems, then, to be no real obstacle in it after all.

出神一 — Projecting the Spirit, First Note

Chinese Source

或問：「仙出神，禪者能之乎？」曰：「能之而不為也。楞嚴云：『其心離身，反觀其面』是也。而繼之曰：『非為聖證，若作聖解，即受群邪。』是能之而不為也。」又問：「神之出也，有陰有陽，楞嚴所云，陰神也，仙出陽神，禪者能之乎？」曰：「亦能之而不為也。」或者愕。曰：「毋愕也。爾不見初祖已沒，隻履西歸乎？爾不見寶誌公獄中一身、市中一身乎？爾不見溈山晏坐靜室，乃於莊上喫油糍乎？然亦不名聖證，宗門呵之。昔一僧入定出神，自言：『我之出神，不論遠近，皆能往來，亦能取物，正陽神也。』先德責云：『圓頂方袍，參禪學道，奈何作此鬼神活計？』是故吾宗大禁，不許出神。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked: "Immortals can project their spirit out of the body — can a Chan practitioner do the same?" I said: "He can, but does not do it. The Śūraṅgama Sūtra says: 'his mind leaves the body, and he can turn and look back upon his own face' — this describes exactly this ability. But it goes on to say: 'This is not a sign of sagely attainment; if one takes it to be a sign of sagely attainment, one at once falls prey to every kind of deviant influence.' This is why the ability exists, and yet is not exercised."

The person asked further: "The spirit that leaves the body has both a yin and a yang aspect — what the Śūraṅgama describes is the yin spirit; immortals project the yang spirit instead. Can a Chan practitioner project the yang spirit too?" I said: "Also able to, and also not doing it." The questioner looked startled. I said: "Do not be startled. Have you not heard how, after the First Patriarch Bodhidharma had already died, someone met him on the road carrying a single sandal, walking west toward India? Have you not heard how the monk Baozhi appeared, at the very same time, both imprisoned and walking about in the marketplace? Have you not heard how Master Guishan sat quietly in his own room and yet, at that very moment, was eating fried rice-cakes at a shop in the village? And yet none of this is called sagely attainment — the Chan tradition itself has always rebuked it. There was once a monk who entered meditation and projected his spirit, and boasted: 'When my spirit goes forth, it can travel any distance, near or far, and even bring back objects — this is the true yang spirit.' An ancient worthy rebuked him for it, saying: 'You shaved your head and put on monastic robes to study Chan and the Way — why on earth are you playing at this trade of ghosts and spirits?' This is exactly why our own tradition strictly forbids projecting the spirit out of the body."

出神二 — Projecting the Spirit, Second Note

Chinese Source

又問神有何過？曰：神即識也，而分粗細。有出有入者粗也。直饒出入俱泯，尚住細識。細之又細，悉皆渾化，始得本體耳。而著於出入以為奇妙，前所謂「無量劫來生死本，癡人認作本來人」也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked further: "What fault is there in projecting the spirit?" I said: "The 'spirit' here is simply consciousness itself, and consciousness has both a coarse and a subtle aspect. Whatever spirit can leave and re-enter the body is the coarse kind. Even supposing one reached the point where such leaving and entering had entirely ceased, a subtle consciousness would still remain. Subtler and subtler still, until every trace of it has finally dissolved away entirely — only then has one truly attained the fundamental substance itself. To cling to this leaving and entering of the spirit as something marvelous is exactly the case described earlier, in 'The Mind's Vital Spirit Is Called Sagehood': 'A student of the Way who fails to recognize what is truly real, simply because he has all along mistaken consciousness for it — the very root of birth and death across countless kalpas — and the fool mistakes it for his own original face.'"

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhili (Siming Zhili, 960–1028) was the seventeenth patriarch of the Tiantai school; his commentary on the Contemplation Sūtra, the Miaozong Chao, was controversial precisely because it wove Tiantai's dense "one mind, three contemplations" framework into what many considered the simplest and most accessible sutra in the Pure Land canon. The two "projecting the spirit" notes revisit the bodily-spirit theme of "The Mind's Vital Spirit Is Called Sagehood" from Part 2 (entry 48), this time using named historical and legendary cases — Bodhidharma's sandal, Baozhi's simultaneous presence in two places, Guishan's rice-cake — to distinguish a real capacity from a spiritually significant one.

80. 聞訃 — Hearing News of a Death

A short reflection on why the ordinary shock at hearing of a death is misplaced.

Chinese Source

聞人訃音必大驚訝，此雖世間常情，然生必有死，亦世間常事。自古及今，無一人得免者，何足驚訝？特其虛生浪死而不聞道，是重可驚訝，而恬不驚訝，悲夫！

Ru-Yi Translation

On hearing news of someone's death, people are invariably deeply startled — this is ordinary human feeling, but where there is life there is inevitably death, and this too is simply the ordinary way of the world. From ancient times to the present, not a single person has ever been spared it — what is there to be so startled about? What truly deserves to startle us is this: to live an entire life in vain, wasted, without ever once hearing the Dharma — and yet to feel entirely unmoved and unconcerned about that. How sorrowful!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A brief entry building on the "impermanence" theme running through this collection since entry 2 in Part 1 — here relocating the proper object of alarm from death itself to a wasted life lived without ever encountering the Dharma.

81. 齋素 — Vegetarian Fasting

A practical argument, using the deer and the tiger as examples, that a vegetarian diet has nothing to do with lifespan — followed by pragmatic advice for those genuinely unable to fast strictly.

Chinese Source

富貴人不能齋素，其故有二：一者耽芻豢之悅口，二者慮藜藿之損身。不知肉食蔬食，體之肥瘠或因之，而壽夭不與也。且鹿之壽最永於諸獸，而所食者草耳；虎食肉，而壽之長短於鹿，何如也？鹿不肉而壽，人何獨不然？雖然，有厄於病苦，心雖欲齋而力不副者；有制於所尊，心雖欲齋而勢弗克者，則姑行月齋、日齋及三淨肉，但堅持不殺可也。久之，宿習當自斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wealthy and eminent people are unable to keep a vegetarian diet for two reasons: one, they are addicted to the delicious taste of meat; two, they worry that a coarse diet of vegetables will harm their health. What they fail to realize is that eating meat or eating vegetables may perhaps affect how fat or thin the body becomes, but it has nothing whatsoever to do with the length of one's life.

Consider: the deer lives longer than almost any other animal, and yet it eats nothing but grass; the tiger eats meat, and yet its lifespan is far shorter than the deer's — is this not perfectly plain? If a deer can live long without eating meat, why should a human being be any different? That said, there are those hindered by illness who, though they wish in their hearts to fast, lack the physical strength to sustain it; and there are those constrained by the wishes of their elders, who, though they wish in their hearts to eat vegetarian, are not in a position to insist on it. In such cases, it is enough to observe the monthly and daily fasts, or to eat the "three kinds of pure meat," while firmly holding to the precept against killing directly. Over time, the deeply ingrained habit will naturally wear away on its own.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Notably pragmatic in tone compared to the vegetarian polemics elsewhere in this collection (compare entry 44 in Part 2) — Zhuhong here allows for graduated compromise ("three kinds of pure meat," permitted under the Ten Recitations Vinaya for those genuinely unable to fast) rather than demanding an all-or-nothing standard.

82. 輪迴根本 — The Root of Saṃsāra

A meditation on craving as the root of rebirth, and a method — investigating the thought itself back to its source — offered as more workable than the classical contemplation of impurity.

Chinese Source

圓覺謂輪迴以愛欲為根本。而此愛欲，百計制之，莫可除滅。蓋賁育無所施其勇，良平無所用其智，而離婁公輸無所著其明巧者也。雖不淨觀正彼對治，而博地凡夫障重染深，祗見其淨，不見其不淨，觀法精微，鮮克成就。然則竟如之何？經云：「欲生於汝意，意以思想生。」今觀此想，復從何生？研之究之，又研究之，研之不休，究之不已，老鼠入牛角，當必有倒斷處。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment holds that saṃsāra is rooted in craving and desire. And this craving and desire, however one tries by a thousand different schemes to master it, cannot be eliminated. Even a warrior of Ben and Yu's legendary strength would find no use for his courage against it; even the wisest counselors like Zhang Liang and Chen Ping would find no use for their intelligence against it; even someone with the piercing eyesight of Li Lou or the miraculous craftsmanship of Gongshu Ban would find no use for their skill against it.

Although the sutras teach the contemplation of impurity as the proper antidote, we ordinary, deeply obstructed beings, weighed down by heavy karmic hindrance, see only what is pure and attractive, never what is impure — and so, subtle and profound as this contemplation method is, very few ever manage to complete it successfully. What, then, is truly to be done? A sutra says: "Desire arises from your own intention; intention arises from thought." So now, contemplate this thought itself — where, exactly, does it come from? Investigate it, and investigate it again, and go on investigating without pause, without rest — like a rat that has run into the hollow of an ox horn, with nowhere left to go — and there will surely come a point of final resolution.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The rat-in-the-ox-horn image (老鼠入牛角) is a common Chinese idiom for a dead end with no way out but forward — used positively here, since the "trap" is precisely what forces the investigation to its conclusion. Zhuhong's redirection from the classical contemplation of impurity (traditionally a mainstay antidote to desire) toward direct investigation of the thought itself anticipates the same method he recommends for Chan-style inquiry elsewhere in this collection.

83. 病者眾生之良藥 — Illness Is the True Medicine for Sentient Beings

A personal testimony, drawing on three near-fatal illnesses of his own, for why sickness can serve as a genuine spur to practice.

Chinese Source

世人以病為苦，而先德云：「病者眾生之良藥。」夫藥與病反，奈何以病為藥？蓋有形之身，不能無病，此理勢所必然。而無病之時，嬉怡放逸，誰覺之者？唯病苦逼身，始知四大非實，人命無常，則悔悟之一機，而修進之一助也。予出家至今，大病垂死者三，而每病發悔悟，增修進，由是信良藥之語，其真至言哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

People of the world regard illness as suffering, and yet a worthy of old said: "Illness is the true medicine of sentient beings." Medicine is supposed to be the opposite of illness — how, then, can illness itself be called medicine? Because a body with form cannot possibly be free of illness forever — this is simply the necessary logic of things. And when there is no illness, people grow careless and indulgent — who, in that state, ever wakes up to anything? Only when the suffering of illness presses upon the body does one come to know that the four elements are not truly real, and that human life is impermanent — and this becomes the very trigger for repentance and awakening, and an aid to further practice.

Since I left the household life to this day, I have suffered three grave illnesses that brought me to the point of death, and with each illness came a corresponding rise of repentance and awakening, which in turn deepened my resolve in practice. It is precisely because of this, my own direct experience, that I have come to believe the saying "illness is the true medicine" is truly the highest kind of statement.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Read alongside the four-part 湯厄 sequence in Part 3 (entries within 64), which recounts one of these three grave illnesses — the scalding accident of 1601 — in far more granular, unguarded detail than the general principle stated here.

84. 蛇成龍 — The Snake Becoming a Dragon

A naturalist's rebuttal of the folk belief that a snake becomes a dragon through deliberate self-cultivation, using a long list of classical transformation-lore to argue that such changes are simply nature, not spiritual attainment.

Chinese Source

昔人有喻：「如蛇成龍，不改其皮；如人成佛，不改其面。」此破愚夫著相求佛，蓋彷彿為比，非的喻也；斷章取義，非全喻也。又有謂：「蛇伏地內，由修鍊而成龍。」不知此性稟使然，非修鍊所致。是故污水中蟲化而為蚊，廁圂中蟲化而為蠅，蜣之為蟬，蠶之為蛾，雉之為蜃，雀之為蛤，鯊之為虎，鯤之為鵬，如是之類，種種非一，豈其有修鍊之術乎？又不見草之為螢，飯之為螺，瓦之為鴛鴦，無情而化有情，修鍊安在？吾恐不明理者，名為學道，潛作邪因，妄冀邪果，不得不辯。

Ru-Yi Translation

The ancients had an analogy: "Just as a snake becoming a dragon does not change its scales, a person becoming a buddha does not change his face." This was meant to break the foolish habit of clinging to outward form when seeking buddhahood — it is a rough approximation, not an exact analogy; it takes only part of the meaning out of context, not the whole.

There is also a claim that a snake, lying hidden underground, transforms into a dragon through cultivation. What people fail to realize is that this transformation is simply the creature's own inherent nature at work, not the result of any cultivation at all — just as larvae in filthy water metamorphose into mosquitoes, maggots in a latrine metamorphose into flies, dung beetles metamorphose into cicadas, silkworms metamorphose into moths, pheasants become giant clams, sparrows become mussels, hammerhead sharks become tigers, and the great kun-fish becomes the peng-bird. Cases like these are numerous and varied — do any of them involve any process of cultivation at all? And have you never heard, too, that rotting grass turns into fireflies, that fallen rice turns into snails, that roof-tiles turn into mandarin ducks — insentient matter transforming into sentient life? Where, in any of this, is there room for "cultivation"? I fear that people who do not understand this principle, calling themselves students of the Way, may secretly be planting deviant causes and hoping vainly for deviant results — and so I could not avoid setting the record straight.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The list of transformations Zhuhong cites (larvae to mosquitoes, dung beetles to cicadas, and so on) draws on a real body of pre-modern Chinese natural-history lore — recorded in works from the Guoyu to the Book of Rites to the Compendium of Materia Medica — which held that many creatures underwent spontaneous species transformation, a folk-biological framework distinct from, but genuinely believed alongside, Buddhist teaching in Zhuhong's own time. His argument does not challenge the folk science itself; it only denies that any of these transformations involve deliberate spiritual "cultivation" (修鍊) — the very point at issue.

85. 名利 — Fame and Fortune

A close reading of a proverb about the impossibility of escaping fame and fortune, pressed toward a fuller teaching on karmic causation and emptiness.

Chinese Source

榮名厚利，世所同競，而昔賢謂「求之既不可得，卻之亦不可免。」此「卻之不可免」一語最極玄妙，處世者當深信熟玩。蓋求不可得，人或知之；卻不可免，誰知之者？如知其不可免也，何以求為？又求之未得，不勝其慍；及其得之，不勝其喜。如知其不可免也，何以喜為？又己得則喜，他人得之則忌。如知其不可免也，何以忌為？庶幾達宿緣之自致，了萬境之如空，而成敗利鈍，興味蕭然矣！故知此語玄妙。

Ru-Yi Translation

Glorious fame and generous profit are what everyone in the world competes for alike, and yet a worthy of old once said: "Seeking them, one cannot obtain them; trying to decline them, one cannot escape them either." This second phrase — "trying to decline them, one cannot escape them" — is the most profound and subtle of the two, and anyone living in the world ought to believe it deeply and turn it over carefully in the mind.

That fame and fortune cannot simply be sought and obtained — people generally understand this much. But that they cannot even be declined and escaped — who really understands that? If you truly understood that they cannot be escaped, why would you go on seeking them at all? And again: when people fail to obtain what they seek, they cannot contain their resentment; and when they finally obtain it, they cannot contain their joy. If you truly understood that it cannot be escaped, what would there be to feel joyful about? And again: people feel joy when they themselves gain something, and jealousy when someone else gains it instead. If you truly understood that it cannot be escaped, what would there be to feel jealous about?

Coming close to this understanding, one comes close as well to grasping that success and failure are simply brought about by causes and conditions from past lives, and, going further still, to realizing that every circumstance whatsoever arises from causes and conditions and is, in the end, empty — at which point success and failure, gain and loss, lose all their savor entirely! This is why I say this saying is truly profound and subtle.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The entry builds through three progressively deeper responses — resentment, joy, and jealousy — to the same underlying realization, a structural pattern already seen in entry 67's treatment of slander (Part 3) and entry 46's treatment of merit (Part 2).

86. 臨終正念 — Right Mindfulness at the Moment of Death

A short, sharp correction of a local Hangzhou funerary custom that had inverted the whole point of a sutra teaching.

Chinese Source

經言人欲終時，聞鐘磬聲，增其正念。而杭俗亡者氣絕良久，方乃召僧擊磬，已無及矣。又訛為之說曰：「磬之鳴也，促亡人行赴閻羅也。」其謬一至於是。

Ru-Yi Translation

A sutra says that when a person is about to die, hearing the sound of a bell or a chime strengthens their right mindfulness. But the custom in Hangzhou is to wait until the dying person's breath has already ceased for a long while before summoning a monk to strike the chime — by which point it is already too late. There is even a mistaken popular saying that goes: "The sound of the chime hurries the departed spirit on its way to King Yama's court." That such a mistaken idea should exist at all is extraordinary.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The sutra passage Zhuhong cites is attributed to Zhiyi's own deathbed instruction to his attendant, recorded in the Tiantai vinaya literature: the bell was meant to steady the dying person's mind while they could still hear it, not to serve any function after death — the very inversion Zhuhong is correcting here.

87. 花香 — The Fragrance of Flowers

A brief observation about night-blooming fragrance, turned into a small lesson about undivided attention.

Chinese Source

庭中百合花開，晝雖有香，澹如也，入夜而香始烈。夫鼻非鈍於晝而利於夜也，白日喧動，諸境紛雜，目視焉，耳聽焉，鼻之力為耳目所分而不得專也。用志不分，乃凝於神，信夫！

Ru-Yi Translation

When the lilies bloom in my courtyard, during the day their fragrance, though present, is faint and pale; only after nightfall does the fragrance grow truly strong. It is not that the nose is duller by day and sharper by night — rather, during the bustling daylight hours, one's surroundings are filled with countless competing sights and sounds; the eyes are occupied with looking, the ears with listening, and the nose's own capacity is divided between them, unable to focus fully. "When intention is undivided, it becomes concentrated in spirit" — how true this saying is!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The closing line quotes the Zhuangzi's "Mastering Life" chapter — a rare case of Zhuhong reaching for a Daoist source to close an entry with no polemical edge at all, unlike his more combative citations of Zhuangzi elsewhere (compare the three-part "Zhuangzi" sequence in Part 1, entry 19).

88. 人虎傳 — The Tale of the Man-Tiger

A retelling of a well-known Ming-dynasty story about a prankster monk who is trapped in a tiger's body until remorse restores him — offered as literary evidence for the doctrine that all things are made by mind.

Chinese Source

說海載人虎傳：一僧戲披虎皮於山徑間，有見而怖走，遺其橐囊者，輒取之；皮忽著身，遂成虎，不敢歸寺，而心歷歷然人也。漸饑，不得已，食狐兔羊犬。既而捕得人，將食之，視之，僧也。大悔恨，恨極悲號，舉身自擲，皮忽墮地，還復人體。因感斯異，乃破衲行乞，遍參知識，刻心辦道，後竟成名德云。經云「一切唯心造」，觀於是，尤信。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Collected Tales Old and New records the story "The Man-Tiger": a certain monk, fond of practical jokes, liked to drape a tiger skin over himself and wander the mountain paths. Travelers who saw him, taking him for a real tiger, would flee in terror, sometimes dropping their belongings in their panic, which the monk would then help himself to. One day, the tiger skin suddenly stuck fast to his body of its own accord, and he was truly transformed into a tiger. He did not dare return to the monastery, though in his own mind he still knew clearly that he was really a man.

Growing hungry, he had no choice but to prey on foxes, hares, sheep, and dogs to survive. Then one day he caught a human being, and was about to devour him — only to discover it was a monk. Overcome with remorse, he wailed in anguish and flung his own body down in despair — and at that very moment the tiger skin suddenly fell away, and he returned to human form. Moved profoundly by this strange experience, he went on to practice as a mendicant ascetic, traveling to seek out spiritual teachers everywhere, applying himself wholeheartedly to the Way — and in time became a renowned and virtuous monk. A sutra says, "All things are made by mind alone" — reading this story, I believe it all the more.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This story descends, through the Ming anthology Gujin Shuohai, from the Tang-dynasty tale of Li Zheng in Zhang Du's Xuanshi Zhi — one of the most durable transformation tales in Chinese literature, later the basis for numerous retellings. Zhuhong reads it not as entertainment but as literal confirmation of a specific doctrinal claim, "all things are made by mind alone," a citation shared verbatim with the following entry.

89. 六道互具 — Each of the Six Realms Contains All Six Within It

A social taxonomy mapping the six realms of rebirth onto observable gradations of human life, arguing that each realm secretly contains all six within it.

Chinese Source

六道之中，復有六道。且以人言之，有人而天者，諸國王大臣之類是也；有人而人者，諸小臣及平民，衣食饒足，處世安然之類是也；有人而修羅者，諸獄吏、屠兒、劊子之類是也；有人而畜生者，諸負重力役，恆受鞭撻之類是也；有人而餓鬼者，諸貧窮乞人，啼饑號寒之類是也；有人而地獄者，諸刑戮剮割之類是也。天等五道亦復如是。所以然者，昔因持戒修福，今得人身，而所修戒福有上中下，此三種中復有三種，多多無盡，各隨其心，感報不一。經云「一切唯心造」，又觀於是，尤信。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within each of the six realms, there exist all six realms once again. Take the human realm as an example: some people, like kings and great ministers, live as if among the heavenly beings. Some people — lesser officials and ordinary commoners with sufficient food and clothing, living out their lives in peace — live simply as human beings among humans. Some people — prison wardens, butchers, executioners — live as if among the asuras. Some people — those bearing heavy burdens for others, constantly beaten and driven — live as if among the animals. Some people — the destitute, the beggars, crying out from hunger and cold — live as if among the hungry ghosts. Some people — those subjected to execution and dismemberment — live as if in the hells themselves. The heavenly realm and the other four realms each contain the same sixfold division within themselves.

The reason for this is that keeping the precepts and cultivating merit in a past life is what secures a human body in this one, and the differences within the human realm arise because the precepts and merit one has cultivated come in higher, middling, and lower grades — and each of those three grades further divides into higher, middling, and lower again, and so on, expanding without end into countless varieties, each shaped by what one's own mind has karmically produced, and each bringing a different result accordingly. A sutra says: "All things are made by mind alone" — observing this pattern, I believe it all the more.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"All things are made by mind alone" (一切唯心造) — the same closing citation as the previous entry — here draws on the Avataṃsaka Sūtra rather than the more general scriptural attribution used for the tiger story, giving these two consecutive entries a shared refrain across two different sources.

90. 智慧 — Wisdom

A scriptural argument, citing the Ekottarika Āgama, for why wisdom alone — not precepts, meditation, or supernormal power — is the decisive factor among the Buddhist trainings.

Chinese Source

增一阿含經：「佛言，戒律成就，是世俗常數；三昧成就，亦世俗常數；神足飛行成就，亦世俗常數。唯智慧成就為第一義。」則知戒定等三學，布施等六波羅蜜，唯智慧最重，不可輕也；唯智慧最先，不可後也；唯智慧貫徹一切法門，不可等也。經云：「因戒生定，因定發慧。」蓋語其生發之次第則然，而要當知所重、知所先、知所貫徹始得。雖然，此智慧者，又非聰明才辯之謂也，如前「世智當悟」中說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Ekottarika Āgama says: "The Buddha said: attaining accomplishment in precepts is a matter of ordinary, worldly reckoning; attaining accomplishment in samādhi is likewise a matter of ordinary, worldly reckoning; attaining the accomplishment of supernormal flight is likewise a matter of ordinary, worldly reckoning. Only the attainment of wisdom counts as the ultimate truth." From this we know: of the three trainings — precepts, meditation, and wisdom — and the six perfections beginning with giving, wisdom alone is what matters most, and must never be treated lightly; wisdom alone must come first, and must never be placed last; wisdom alone penetrates every Dharma-gate without exception, and cannot be placed on equal footing with the rest. A sutra says: "From precepts, samādhi is generated; from samādhi, wisdom is brought forth" — this describes only the sequence in which they arise, and it remains essential to understand what truly matters most, what truly comes first, and what truly penetrates all the rest. And yet, even so, this "wisdom" is by no means mere cleverness or eloquence, exactly as explained earlier in the entry "Worldly Wisdom Must Still Be Awakened."

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Ekottarika Āgama passage carries a specific narrative context: it was addressed to the Buddha's own cousin Devadatta, who pursued supernormal powers zealously but never attained real liberating wisdom, and eventually fell — the same cautionary figure who recurs in entry 94's "Karmic Retribution" notes below.

91. 外學 — Outside Learning

A historical anecdote about a Sui-dynasty meditation master rebuked, even in a vision, for never fully cutting off his study of non-Buddhist texts.

Chinese Source

隋梁州沙門慧全，徒眾五百，中一人頗粗異，全素所不錄；忽自云得那含果。全有疾閉門，其人徑至榻前問疾，而門閉如故。明日復然。因謂全曰：「師命過，當生婆羅門家。」全云：「我一生坐禪，何故生彼？」答云：「師信道不篤，外學未絕，雖有福業，不得超詣。」今時僧有學老莊者，有學舉子業經書者，有學毛詩楚騷及古詞賦者。彼以禪為務，但外學未絕，尚緣此累道；今恣意外學，而禪置之罔聞，不知其可也。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the Sui dynasty, the monk Huiquan of Liangzhou had five hundred disciples, among whom one behaved in a particularly coarse and unusual way, and whom Huiquan had never thought much of. One day this disciple suddenly declared that he had attained the fruit of an anāgāmin. Huiquan, being ill at the time, had shut his door to rest, and yet this disciple came directly to the foot of his bed to inquire after his health — even though the door remained shut exactly as before. The next day the same thing happened again, after which the disciple said to Huiquan: "Master, when your life ends, you will be reborn into a brahmin family." Huiquan asked: "I have spent my whole life seated in meditation — why would I be reborn there?" The disciple answered: "Master, your faith in the Way has not been wholly sincere and thorough — your engagement with outside teachings has never been entirely cut off. Though you have accumulated meritorious karma, it cannot carry you to true transcendence."

Monks today include some who study Daoist texts like Laozi and Zhuangzi, some who study the Confucian classics prepared for the civil examinations, and some who study the Book of Odes, the Songs of Chu, and classical poetry and rhapsody. Even Huiquan, who took Chan meditation as his primary occupation, still had his practice held back simply by never having fully cut off outside study. How much more so, then, for monks today who indulge freely in outside learning while leaving Chan practice entirely unattended — I do not see how that could possibly turn out well.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A direct companion to entry 52 in Part 3 ("A Monastic Habit"), making the same complaint through a narrative case rather than a general statement — this time with the added edge of a supernatural rebuke delivered by the master's own disciple.

92. 靈裕法師 — Master Lingyu

A defense of an unconventional, unpredictable lecturing style, used to warn against both reckless criticism of one's betters and reckless imitation of them.

Chinese Source

裕法師之說經也，或一字盤桓，動經累日；或片時之頃，便銷數卷；或分科已定，及至後講，更改前科，增減出沒，隨機顯晦，學者疑焉。裕曰：「此大士之宏規也，可以恆情斷乎？」裕師蓋得無礙辯才，庶幾乎於法自在。而拘名著相，以文害辭，以辭害意，與夫參死句之輩，何足以知之？今人不可執己見而蔑視勝流，輕談橫議；又不可昧己量而效嚬先德，妄行自用也。

Ru-Yi Translation

When Master Lingyu lectured on the sutras, he might dwell on a single character for days on end, or, in the space of a moment, race through several entire scrolls; he might establish a fixed outline of divisions for a text, only to alter that outline in a later lecture, adding, subtracting, revealing, or concealing points as the occasion demanded — students found this puzzling. Lingyu said: "This is simply the vast, unconstrained method of a great bodhisattva — how could it possibly be judged by ordinary standards?"

Master Lingyu had, in truth, attained unobstructed eloquence, and had come close to genuine freedom in the Dharma. Those who cling rigidly to names and forms, who let the letter obscure the phrase and the phrase obscure the meaning, or who remain stuck at the level of a merely literal reading without ever truly awakening — how could such people possibly understand him? People today must neither cling stubbornly to their own opinions and look down on their betters, criticizing recklessly and without restraint, nor overestimate their own capacity and imitate the ancient worthies carelessly, acting willfully on their own authority.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The two closing warnings are deliberately symmetrical — a matched pair of opposite errors — and echo entry 71's admonition against reflexive fault-finding (Part 3) on one side, and entry 75's account of Zhuhong's own youthful imitation of Chan rhetoric (Part 3) on the other.

93. 行腳住山 — Wandering on Foot, Settling on a Mountain

A balanced warning against two opposite misreadings of famous Chan precedents — using them to excuse either total seclusion or aimless wandering.

Chinese Source

今人見玄沙不越嶺，保福不度關，便端拱安居，眼空四海。及見雪峰三登投子、九上洞山，趙州八旬行腳，便奔南走北，浪蕩一生。斯二者皆非也。心地未明，正應千里萬里，親附知識，何得守愚空坐，我慢自高？既為生死，參師訪道，又何得觀山觀水，徒誇履歷之廣而已哉？正因行腳之士自不如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

People today, hearing of how Xuansha never crossed the mountain pass and Baofu never went beyond the border checkpoint, promptly settle into comfortable seclusion, viewing the whole world with lofty disdain. And then, hearing of how Xuefeng climbed Mount Touzi three times and Mount Dong nine times, or how Zhaozhou traveled on foot for eighty years, they rush off in every direction, wandering their whole lives away pointlessly. Both approaches are mistaken. When one's own mind is not yet clear, one truly ought to travel a thousand, ten thousand miles, seeking closeness to a genuine spiritual guide — how could one possibly sit in ignorant complacency, cultivating arrogance and self-importance instead? And having set out specifically to resolve the great matter of birth and death, how could one then spend the time merely sightseeing among mountains and rivers, boasting only of how widely one has traveled? Genuine pilgrims on foot, seeking the Way for its own sake, would certainly never behave this way.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhaozhou's "eighty years of pilgrimage" is a commonly garbled fact, corrected in the source annotation itself: he traveled from young adulthood but only settled at Guanyin Cloister at age eighty, rather than beginning his travels at eighty, as the popular version of the story often implies.

94. 楞嚴房融所作 — Was the Śūraṅgama Written by Fang Rong?

A defense of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's authenticity against a suspicion that its literary polish betrayed a Chinese, not Indian, origin — followed by two companion notes on the misuse of "mind-only" and "wisdom" doctrines as excuses for careless behavior.

楞嚴房融所作 — Was the Śūraṅgama Written by Fang Rong?

Chinese Source

有見楞嚴不獨義深，亦復文妙，遂疑是丞相房融所作。夫譯經館番漢僧及詞臣居士等，不下數十百人，而後一部之經始成，融不過潤色其文，非專主其義也。設融自出己意，創為是經，則融固天中天、聖中聖矣！而考諸唐史，融之才智，尚非柳韓元白之比，何其作楞嚴也？乃超孔孟老莊之先耶？嗟乎！千生百劫，得遇如是至精至微、至玄至極之典，不死心信受，而生此下劣乖僻之疑，可悲也夫！可悲也夫！

Ru-Yi Translation

There are those who, seeing that the Śūraṅgama Sūtra is profound not only in meaning but also marvelous in its literary style, have come to suspect it was actually written by the chancellor Fang Rong. But at the translation bureau, foreign and Chinese monks together with court scholars and lay officials — no fewer than several dozen or even a hundred people — all worked together before a single sutra translation could be completed. Fang Rong did no more than polish the text's literary style; he was not the one principally responsible for its meaning. Suppose Fang Rong had truly composed this sutra entirely out of his own understanding — then Fang Rong himself would have to be a heaven among heavens, a sage among sages! And yet, checking the Tang dynastic histories, Fang Rong's own talent and intelligence do not even measure up to writers like Liu Zongyuan, Han Yu, Yuan Zhen, or Bai Juyi — how, then, could he possibly have composed the Śūraṅgama? Would that mean he surpassed even Confucius, Mencius, Laozi, and Zhuangzi?

Alas! Across countless lifetimes and kalpas, to have the rare fortune of encountering a text so exquisitely refined, so profound and ultimate as this one — and then, rather than accepting and believing it wholeheartedly, to give rise instead to such a base and perverse doubt — how sorrowful this is! How sorrowful this is!

果報一 — Karmic Retribution, First Note

Chinese Source

經言：「萬法唯心。」錯會者，謂無心則無因無果，故不患有業，唯患有心；有業無心，閻老子其奈我何！遂安意造業，無復顧忌。不知無心有二：如理思惟，用心之極，而自然入於無心三昧者，真無心也；起心造業，又起心制心，強制令無，似得無心，心恰成有，心有則業有，閻老子鐵棒未放汝在！

Ru-Yi Translation

A sutra says: "The ten thousand dharmas are made by mind alone." Those who misunderstand this claim that without mind there is neither cause nor effect, and so worry not about having created karma, but only about having a mind at all — reasoning that if there is karma but no mind behind it, what could even King Yama do to them! And so they set their minds at ease and go on creating karma, with no further scruple at all.

What they fail to realize is that "no-mind" is of two kinds. The first is genuine no-mind: contemplating in full accordance with the truth, applying the mind to the utmost, until one naturally and spontaneously enters the samādhi of no-mind — this alone is true no-mind. The other is: giving rise to thoughts that create karma, and then giving rise to further thoughts in order to suppress those thoughts, forcibly trying to make the mind absent — this only appears to achieve no-mind, while the mind, in fact, has just become all the more present. Where mind exists, karma exists — and King Yama's iron staff will not let you off so easily!

果報二 — Karmic Retribution, Second Note

Chinese Source

又經言：「具足智慧菩薩脫使墮落，在畜生中，畜生中王；在餓鬼中，餓鬼中王。」錯會者，謂有智則能轉業，故不患有業，唯患無智；有業有智，閻老子其奈我何？遂安意造業，無復顧忌。不知經稱智慧，非等閒世智之謂也。且汝智慧，得如文殊、身子否？縱不及此，次而下之，得如善星、調達否？善星博學十八香象所載法聚，調達得羅漢神通，而俱不免生陷地獄，況汝智慧未必勝此二人乎！杯水不能熄車薪之火，螢光不能破幽谷之昏，今之小智，滅業幾何？閻老子鐵棒，未放汝在。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sutra also says: "Should a bodhisattva perfect in wisdom ever fall — if reborn among animals, he becomes king of the animals; if reborn among hungry ghosts, he becomes king of the hungry ghosts." Those who misunderstand this claim that having wisdom lets one transform karma at will, and so worry not about having created karma, but only about lacking wisdom — reasoning that if there is karma but wisdom to handle it, what could King Yama do to them! And so they set their minds at ease and go on creating karma, with no further scruple at all.

What they fail to realize is that the "wisdom" the sutra speaks of is nothing like ordinary worldly cleverness. And besides — does your own wisdom compare to that of Mañjuśrī, or of Śāriputra? Even granting it falls short of theirs, compare it further down the scale: does it compare even to Sunakṣatra's, or to Devadatta's? Sunakṣatra had studied broadly enough to master the entire Tripiṭaka — a body of teaching so vast it would take eighteen mighty elephants to carry it — and Devadatta had attained the supernormal powers of an arhat, and yet both of them still ended up falling alive into hell. How much less, then, could your own wisdom — almost certainly inferior to either of theirs — possibly protect you! A cup of water cannot extinguish a whole cartload of burning firewood; a firefly's glow cannot dispel the darkness of a deep ravine. How much karma, exactly, do you imagine your own small measure of wisdom is capable of dissolving? King Yama's iron staff will not let you off so easily.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Fang Rong (房融) was a real, historically documented Tang chancellor traditionally credited with polishing the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's Chinese prose during its translation in 705 — a genuine authorship controversy that has continued into modern scholarship, though on different grounds than the ones Zhuhong addresses here. The two "Karmic Retribution" notes both target the same misuse of profound doctrine (mind-only, transcendent wisdom) as a license for moral carelessness — the identical rhetorical structure ("misreading X as license, followed by 'King Yama's iron staff will not let you off'") repeated twice for emphasis.

95. 塞翁 — The Old Man at the Frontier

A personal application of the classic "old man loses his horse" parable to Zhuhong's own recovery from the scalding accident recounted in Part 3.

Chinese Source

得失曾無定形，禍福互為倚伏，塞翁一段因緣，人皆知之，而未必信之也。予失足沸湯，筋攣不伸，畜雙拐為二侍，若將終身焉，作跛腳法師歌自嘲，有「祇愁此腳不終疾」之句。既而足伸如故，笑以為詩讖。而依然奉以為詩規也，且感且懼，願無忘射鉤。

Ru-Yi Translation

Gain and loss have never had any fixed form; fortune and misfortune constantly give rise to one another in turn. Everyone knows the story of the old man at the frontier, yet few truly believe its lesson.

I myself once lost my footing and fell into boiling water, and my sinews contracted so badly that I could not straighten my legs; I relied on a pair of crutches as my constant companions, and thought this would be my condition for the rest of my life. I wrote a self-mocking "Song of the Lame Master" about it, which included the line: "My only worry is that this leg's affliction will never end." And yet, before long, my leg straightened again as before, and I laughed to think my own verse had become an unintended prophecy after all. I still, even now, keep that poem as a standing rule to guide myself — grateful, and also apprehensive — and I hope never to forget the lesson of "the arrow at the belt-hook."

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"The arrow at the belt-hook" refers to Guan Zhong, who once shot an arrow at the future Duke Huan of Qi while serving a rival prince, and was later appointed chancellor by the very man he had tried to kill — Zhuhong invokes it here as a reminder not to resent even a painful turn of fortune, since it may yet prove useful. Read together with the 湯厄 sequence in Part 3, this entry supplies the story's resolution: the leg did, in fact, recover.

96. 神通 — Supernormal Powers

A threefold classification of supernormal powers — by karmic reward, by cultivation, and by realization — extended into an analogy with how officials acquire rank.

Chinese Source

神通大約有三：一報得，一修得，一證得。報得者，福業自致，如諸天皆能徹視徹聽，及鬼亦有通是也。修得者，習學而成，如提婆達多學神通於阿難尊者是也。證得者，專心學道，無心學通，道具而通自具，但遲速不同耳；如古今諸祖諸善知識是也。較而論之，得道不患無通，得通未必有道。先德有言：「神通妙用不如闍黎，佛法還須老僧。」意有在矣！試為之喻：世間官人所有爵祿冠服府署儀衛等，若神通然，而亦有三種：其報得者，如功勳蔭襲，自然而有者也。其修得者，人力夤緣，古人所惡，不由其道者是也。其證得者，道明德立而位自隨之，仲尼云：「學也，祿在其中矣！」是也。是三者，勝劣可知也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Supernormal powers fall roughly into three kinds: those obtained as karmic reward, those obtained through cultivation, and those obtained through realization. Powers obtained as karmic reward arise naturally from meritorious karma — as with the heavenly beings, who all possess complete sight and hearing, or even ghosts and spirits, who possess minor powers of their own. Powers obtained through cultivation are acquired through deliberate practice — as when Devadatta studied supernormal powers under the Venerable Ānanda. Powers obtained through realization arise in someone who applies himself single-mindedly to the Way itself, without ever seeking supernormal powers as such — the powers simply come along, automatically, once the Way itself has been fulfilled, the only difference being how quickly or slowly this happens — as with the patriarchs and genuine spiritual teachers of every generation, past and present.

Comparing the three: one who has attained the Way need not worry about lacking supernormal powers, but one who has supernormal powers has not necessarily attained the Way. A worthy of old said: "Marvelous supernormal powers are no match for a true master; for the Buddha-dharma, you still need this old monk." There is real meaning packed into that remark. Let me offer an analogy: the rank, salary, official cap and robes, official residence, and ceremonial guard that a government official possesses are, in a sense, just like supernormal powers — and these, too, come in three corresponding kinds. Those obtained as reward are like inherited rank passed down from an ancestor's meritorious service — naturally acquired. Those obtained through "cultivation" are like rank acquired by currying favor with powerful patrons — something the ancients despised, since it is not obtained through the proper path. Those obtained through "realization" are like rank that follows naturally once one's own learning and virtue are genuinely established — exactly what Confucius meant when he said, "Study, and an official salary lies within it of itself." From these three, which is superior and which inferior should already be plain enough.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The saying "marvelous supernormal powers are no match for a true master" is attributed to Yangshan Huiji, in an exchange with a visiting Indian monk who had flown to China to demonstrate his powers — the story of that specific exchange (recorded in the Wudeng Huiyuan) supplies the concrete case behind Zhuhong's abstract classification here.

97. 大豪貴人 — Great and Eminent People

A three-tiered account of how past cultivation shapes worldly rank, followed by two companion notes reconciling the Tiantai and Xianshou (Huayan) schools' rival systems of doctrinal classification.

大豪貴人 — Great and Eminent People

Chinese Source

世間大豪貴人，多從修行中來，然有三等：其一持戒修福，而般若正智念念不忘，則來生雖處高位，五欲具足，而心則時時在道，真所謂有髮僧也。其二持戒修福，而般若之念稍疏，則來生遊戲法門而已。其三持戒修福，而於般若藐不繫念，則來生為順境所迷，背善從惡，甚而謗佛、毀法、滅僧者有矣！鞫其因地，則均之修行人耳，而差別如是，來生更來生，其差別又何如也？寒心哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

Great and eminent people in this world mostly arrive at their position through past cultivation, and yet there are three distinct grades among them. The first kind keeps the precepts and cultivates merit while never, thought after thought, forgetting the true wisdom of prajñā — such a person, though born in the next life to a position of high rank, fully endowed with the five sensory pleasures, still keeps his mind constantly turned toward the Way — truly what might be called "a monk who happens to have hair." The second kind keeps the precepts and cultivates merit, but lets the thought of prajñā grow somewhat sparse — such a person, in the next life, merely dabbles in the Dharma as a passing amusement. The third kind keeps the precepts and cultivates merit while paying no real attention to prajñā at all — such a person, in the next life, will be led astray by favorable circumstances, turning from good toward evil, and there are even cases of such people going on to slander the Buddha, destroy the Dharma, and persecute the Sangha!

And yet, tracing all three types back to their original ground, they were, every one of them, equally practitioners of the Way — and see how vastly they come to differ! Carry this forward another life, and yet another life again — how much further might that difference grow? It is enough to chill the heart!

天台清涼一 — Tiantai and Qingliang, First Note

Chinese Source

人有恆言曰：「天台賢首，愚嘗究之。南嶽舉其綱，而萬目畢張，則莫備乎天台；賢首持其衡，而千星交羅，則莫備乎清涼。蓋自有佛法以來，天台集其大成；自有天台以來，清涼集其大成矣！故當以二師相對而名宗也。」或曰：「人於天台無議矣，於賢首或置喙焉，何也？」曰：「喙賢首者，亦百喙而一中耳。又向不云乎？賢首之道，至清涼而始備，是則天台清涼二師，恩如父母，道亦如父母，且清涼可得議乎？」或未答，予笑曰：「毋勞爾思也。天台之後有清涼，猶堯舜之後有孔子也。而又何議也？」

Ru-Yi Translation

People often say: "I have studied both the Tiantai and the Xianshou schools myself. Nanyue first laid out its broad framework, and from that every particular detail followed — no school is more complete than Tiantai in this respect. Xianshou held the balance of the scale, and a thousand scattered points came together in perfect order under him — no school is more complete than Qingliang's in this respect. Ever since the Buddha-dharma first arrived in the East, Master Zhiyi of Tiantai may be said to represent its grand synthesis; and ever since the founding of Tiantai itself, Master Chengguan of Qingliang may be said to represent the grand synthesis of Tiantai's own teaching and contemplative method! This is why these two masters are spoken of together, as the namesakes of their respective schools."

Someone asked: "No one raises any objection to Master Zhiyi of Tiantai, and yet some do raise objections against Xianshou — why is that?" I said: "Of a hundred objections raised against Xianshou, perhaps one in a hundred might be fair — is that not how people always argue about such things? And besides, has it not already been said that Xianshou's own teaching was not complete until Qingliang came along to finish it? Given that, Tiantai and Qingliang are, to us, like parents — their kindness is like a parent's kindness, and the transmission of their teaching is like a parent's raising of a child. Surely no one would go so far as to object even to Qingliang himself?" The person had no answer. I laughed and said: "There is no need to trouble yourself over this any further. That Qingliang should come after Tiantai is exactly like Confucius coming after Yao and Shun — what is there left to object to?"

天台清涼二 — Tiantai and Qingliang, Second Note

Chinese Source

或曰：「彼四教，此五教，判然二宗矣，然亦有同歟？」曰：「毋以二為也。四之與五，猶五蘊六根，開合焉耳矣！五教之小，即攝入四中之藏；而藏之為言，猶迹涉於混，故另分曰小也。五教之頓，即攝入四中之圓；而達磨直指，正屬乎頓，欲其彰顯，故特標曰頓也。二宗之圓教，一也；而華嚴十玄之旨，四之圓猶含其意而未盡，故小始終頓之後，而獨冠以一乘之圓，有深意也。以其各為一時之所依歸，而均為萬代之所程式，以是名之二宗。宗常二而道常一，歧而二之，則非矣！」

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone said: "Tiantai speaks of four teachings, Xianshou of five — clearly two distinct schools. Do they nonetheless have anything in common?" I said: "Do not treat them as two separate things at all. The difference between 'four' and 'five' is exactly like the difference between the five aggregates and the six sense-faculties — merely a matter of how the same material is divided or combined. Xianshou's 'lesser vehicle teaching,' among the five, is already contained within Tiantai's 'storehouse teaching,' among the four — but since 'storehouse teaching' carries with it a somewhat blended sense, Xianshou's school gives it a separate name, 'lesser vehicle,' instead. Xianshou's 'sudden teaching,' among the five, is already contained within Tiantai's 'perfect teaching,' among the four — but since Bodhidharma's direct-pointing method belongs precisely to the 'sudden' category, Xianshou's school singles it out under its own name, 'sudden,' in order to give it due prominence. As for the 'perfect teaching' spoken of by both schools, that much is genuinely identical between them — though because the deeper principle of the Avataṃsaka's 'ten mysterious gates' is already implicit, but not yet fully drawn out, within Tiantai's own version of the perfect teaching, Xianshou's school, after its lesser, initial, final, and sudden teachings, crowns its list with a 'One Vehicle' perfect teaching all its own — and there is real depth of purpose behind that choice. Each of the two schools served as the guiding authority for its own age, and both stand as models to be followed for ten thousand generations to come — this is exactly why they are spoken of as two distinct schools. But though the schools are properly spoken of as two, the underlying Way they teach remains, always, one and the same. To split it in two and treat it as genuinely divided would simply be a mistake!"

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"Qingliang" is Chengguan (澄觀, 738–839), fourth patriarch of the Huayan school; "Xianshou" is Fazang (法藏, 643–712), its actual founder-figure, whom Zhuhong treats here as needing Chengguan's later systematization to be truly complete — a genuinely contestable historical judgment that Zhuhong states as settled fact. The doctrinal comparison in the second note (Tiantai's four teachings against Xianshou's five) is a substantive piece of comparative scholasticism, mapping each school's categories onto the other's with real precision rather than simply asserting they amount to the same thing.

98. 栯堂山居詩 — Youtang's "Mountain Dwelling" Poems

A brief piece of literary appreciation, praising a Yuan-dynasty monk's poetry as the finest of its genre precisely because it arises from genuine realization rather than technical skill.

Chinese Source

永明、石屋、中峰諸大老，皆有山居詩，發明自性，響振千古。而兼之乎氣格雄渾，句字精工，則栯堂四十詠尤為諸家絕唱。所以然者，以其皆自真參實悟，溢於中而揚於外。如微風過極樂之寶樹，帝心感乾闥之瑤琴，不搏而聲，不撫而鳴，是詩之極妙，而又不可以詩論也。不攻其本而擬其末，終世推敲，則何益矣！願居山者學古人之道，毋學古人之詩。

Ru-Yi Translation

Yongming, Shiwu, Zhongfeng, and other great venerable masters have all left behind "mountain dwelling" poems, giving expression to their own realized nature, resonant across the ages. But of all these, for combining a vigorous, sweeping poetic force with exquisitely precise craftsmanship of line and word, Youtang's forty verses stand as the finest example among them all. The reason is this: they arise entirely from genuine investigation and genuine realization, overflowing from within and expressed outward — like a gentle breeze passing through the jeweled trees of the Land of Ultimate Bliss, or like the sky-god Śakra moved by the celestial lute of the gandharvas: sound arising without being struck, music sounding without being played. This is poetry at its most exquisite — and yet it cannot properly be judged as mere poetry at all. To fail to work on the root, and instead merely imitate the outward branches, polishing and refining one's phrasing for an entire lifetime — what good could that possibly do? I hope that those who dwell in the mountains will learn the ancients' own realization of the Way, and not merely imitate the ancients' poetry.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Youtang Yi (栯堂益), a fourth-generation successor in Dahui Zonggao's lineage, is placed here alongside three towering figures of the "mountain dwelling" genre — Yongming Yanshou, Shiwu Qinggong, and Zhongfeng Mingben (already encountered as a Śūraṅgama commentator in entry 4 of Part 1) — and judged the finest of the four, a striking critical claim Zhuhong makes without hedging.

99. 山色 — The Color of the Mountains

A short observation about how the same mountains appear a different color depending on the viewer's distance, read as a lesson in the emptiness of all appearances.

Chinese Source

近觀山色，蒼然其青焉，如藍也；遠觀山色，鬱然其翠焉，如藍之成靛也。山之色果變乎？山色如故，而目力有長短也。自近而漸遠焉，青易為翠；自遠而漸近焉，翠易為青。是則青以緣會而青，翠以緣會而翠，非唯翠之為幻，而青亦幻也。蓋萬法皆如是矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

Seen up close, the color of the mountains is a deep, vivid green, like the plant indigo itself; seen from far away, the color turns a rich, dense blue, as if the indigo had been processed into dye. Has the mountain's color actually changed? The mountain's color remains exactly as it was — it is only the reach of our own eyesight that differs. As one moves gradually from near to far, green easily turns to blue; as one moves gradually from far to near, blue easily turns back to green. Green, then, is green only through the coming-together of conditions, and blue is blue only through the coming-together of conditions — it is not merely the blue that is illusory, but the green as well. And all the ten thousand things of this world are exactly the same.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

One of the most purely observational entries in the collection — no citation, no historical figure, just a direct perceptual demonstration of dependent origination, structurally close to entry 87's fragrance observation immediately above it in this same batch.

100. 見生夢 — Dreaming of This Life

A meditation on why dreams almost always draw on this present life rather than a previous one, closing with a hope for the state described in the Śūraṅgama as "waking and dreaming, constant and undivided."

Chinese Source

夜夢中多見生事，罕夢前生，何也？蓋夢以想成，想多見生，不及前生故也。且三乘賢聖，尚有隔陰出胎乍時之昏，況具縛凡夫，脫一殼，入一殼，從母腹中顛倒而下，尚何能記憶前生耶？惟據其目前紛紛紜紜，晝則為想，夜則為夢耳。而或時未見之物、未作之事、未歷之位，現於夢中者，則無始之境，任運而然，亦莫知其所以然而然也。想陰既破，寤寐恆一，幸相與致力焉。

Ru-Yi Translation

In dreams at night, we mostly see events from this present life, and rarely dream of a previous life — why is this? Because dreams are formed out of thought, and what we think about is mostly events from this life, not from a previous one. Even the sages of the three vehicles still experience a brief confusion at the moment of leaving one existence and entering the womb of the next — how much more so, then, an ordinary being still fully bound by affliction, slipping out of one shell and into another, emerging upside-down from the mother's womb? How could such a being possibly retain any memory of a previous life at all? One can only follow whatever confused impressions lie immediately at hand — by day, these become thoughts; by night, they become dreams.

And yet sometimes things never seen, deeds never done, circumstances never experienced in this life will appear in a dream all the same — these arise from the beginningless conditions of the mind, surfacing of their own accord, and no one can really say why they arise as they do. Once the aggregate of perception itself has been broken through, waking and dreaming become one and the same, constant and undivided — I hope we can all apply ourselves together toward this.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The closing phrase "waking and dreaming, constant and undivided" (寤寐恆一) is drawn directly from the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's description of what happens once the aggregate of perception (想陰) has been exhausted — the same Śūraṅgama framework of five aggregates already at work in entry 62's rebuttal of the Confucian "Mean" (Part 3). This entry closes the batch on a note of shared aspiration rather than argument, addressed in the first person plural.
