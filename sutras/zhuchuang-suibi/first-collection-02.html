Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — First Collection, Part 2

26. 鬼神 — Ghosts and Spirits

A short, dialectical answer to a stock question, followed by two companion notes on a different figure and theme — Su Dongpo's own struggle to keep his priorities straight — grouped under the same title in the source.

鬼神 — Ghosts and Spirits

Chinese Source

或問：有鬼神歟？無鬼神歟？曰：有。鬼神可信奉歟？不可信奉歟？曰：亦可亦不可。何謂也？曰：夫子不云乎「敬鬼神而遠之」！蓋一言盡其曲折矣！「敬」之云者，有也。「遠」之云者，信奉而不信奉也。祀之以時，交之以禮，如是而已耳。過信而諂奉焉，冀其報吉凶、降福佑、獲靈通，則駸駸然入於邪矣。噫！有可敬而不可遠者，諸佛諸菩薩是也。胡弗思也？

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked: "Do ghosts and spirits exist, or do they not?" I said: "They exist." "Should they be revered and served, or should they not?" I said: "Both — and neither." "What do you mean by that?" I said: "Did Confucius not say, 'Revere ghosts and spirits, but keep them at a distance'? That single line settles every twist of the question. The word 'revere' tells you they exist. The word 'distance' tells you to serve them and yet not serve them. Make offerings at the proper season, treat them with due ceremony, and that is all there is to it. To believe in them to excess, and to flatter and court their favor, hoping they will report good and ill fortune, send down blessings, or grant supernatural powers — that is to slide, step by step, into deviant practice. Ah, but there is one class of being that should be revered and yet never kept at a distance: the buddhas and bodhisattvas. Why does no one think of that?"

東坡一 — Su Dongpo, First Note

Chinese Source

洪覺範謂東坡文章德行炳煥千古，又深入佛法，而不能忘情於長生之術，非唯無功，反坐此病卒。予謂東坡尚爾，況其餘乎！今有口談無生，而心慕長生者；有始學無生，俄而改業長生者。蓋知之不真，見之不定耳。故道人不可剎那失正知見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hong Juefan held that Su Dongpo's writing and virtue shine brilliantly across the ages, and that he had also penetrated deeply into the Buddha-dharma — yet he could never let go of his attachment to the Daoist arts of immortality, which not only brought him no benefit but in the end contributed to the illness that killed him. If even Dongpo fell into this, how much more should the rest of us be on guard! There are people today who speak with their mouths of the unborn nature of all things, while their hearts still long for immortality; and others who begin by studying the unborn, only to switch before long to the pursuit of immortality instead. This all comes from knowing something without truly knowing it, and seeing something without any settled conviction. A person of the Way must not lose right knowledge and right view even for a single instant.

東坡二 — Su Dongpo, Second Note

Chinese Source

元禪師與東坡書云：「時人忌子瞻作宰相耳。三十年功名富貴，過眼成空，何不猛與一刀割斷。」又云：「子瞻胸中有萬卷書，筆下無一點塵，為何於自己性命便不知下落？」以東坡之穎敏，而又有如是善友策發，何慮不日進？今之縉紳與衲子交者，宜講此誼。

Ru-Yi Translation

Master Yuan wrote to Dongpo: "People these days simply fear that you, Zizhan, will become prime minister. Even thirty years of merit, fame, and riches pass before the eyes and come to nothing — why not cut the whole matter off in one decisive stroke?" He also wrote: "Your mind, Zizhan, holds ten thousand volumes, and your brush leaves not a speck of worldly dust on the page — why is it, then, that you have no idea where your own life and death are headed?" With Dongpo's own keen intelligence, and a good friend like this to spur him on, how could he have failed to make daily progress? Officials and scholars today who keep company with monks would do well to take up this same kind of friendship.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Hong Juefan (惠洪, 1071–1128) was a Song-dynasty Chan monk and prolific writer; "Master Yuan" is Foyin Liaoyuan, the Chan monk best known as Su Dongpo's close friend and frequent sparring partner in the many popular anecdotes about the two men. Both notes make the same point from different angles: even a brilliant and sincere Buddhist practitioner like Dongpo could not fully let go of the competing pull of Daoist immortality-cultivation and worldly ambition — Zhuhong's closing warning, that a person of the Way "must not lose right view even for a single instant," is aimed squarely at this kind of divided attention.

27. 憎愛 — Love and Hate

A short reflection on the instability of love and hatred, paired in the source with two further notes on the value of stillness and of restraint in speech.

憎愛 — Love and Hate

Chinese Source

語云：「愛其人及其屋上之烏。」言愛之極其至也。忽緣變而情遷，轉愛為憎，憎而又憎，向之愛安在哉？轉憎為愛，亦復如是。是故愛不必喜，憎不必怒，夢事空花，本非實故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A saying goes: "Love the person, and you love even the crow on his roof." This describes love carried to its furthest extreme. But let circumstances suddenly shift and feeling change along with them, and love turns to hatred — hatred piled upon hatred — and where, then, has that former love gone? The reverse, hatred turning to love, works exactly the same way. And so being loved need not bring joy, and being hated need not bring anger: both are like a dream, like flowers seen in empty air — never real to begin with.

靜之益一 — The Benefit of Stillness, First Note

Chinese Source

日間有事，或處分不定，睡去四五更起坐，是非可否忽自了然，日間錯處於此悉現。乃知爾來不得明見心性，皆由忙亂覆卻本體耳。古人云：「靜見真如性。」又云：「性水澄清，心珠自現。」豈虛語哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

In the daytime there are matters one cannot resolve or decide clearly; but sleep through to the fourth or fifth watch of the night, rise, and sit — and suddenly all the rights and wrongs of it become plain of themselves, and every error made during the day shows itself clearly here. From this one comes to know that our failure, up to now, to see our own mind-nature clearly comes entirely from busyness and confusion covering over its original substance. The ancients said: "In stillness, one sees one's true suchness-nature." And again: "When the water of one's nature grows clear and still, the mind's pearl reveals itself." Are these empty words?

靜之益二 — The Benefit of Stillness, Second Note

Chinese Source

世間釅醯醇醴，藏之彌久而彌美者，皆由封錮牢密，不泄氣故。古人云：「二十年不開口說話，向後佛也奈何你不得。」旨哉言乎！

Ru-Yi Translation

In this world, strong vinegar and rich, pure wine grow only finer the longer they are stored away, and this is entirely because they are sealed tight and airtight, letting nothing escape. The ancients said: "Go twenty years without opening your mouth to speak, and afterward even the buddhas will be unable to do anything with you." How profound these words are!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The two "benefit of stillness" notes work as a matched pair — direct experiential evidence (early-morning sitting resolving the day's confusion) followed by an analogy (aging vinegar and wine) for the same underlying point: that concealment and quiet, sustained over time, produce a depth that busy exposure cannot.

28. 華嚴不如艮卦 — The Avataṃsaka Is Not Inferior to the Gèn Hexagram

A sharp rebuttal of a Song Confucian's dismissive remark about the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, using a simple hierarchy of political rank as its closing image.

Chinese Source

宋儒有言：「讀一部華嚴經，不如看一艮卦。」此說高明者自知其謬，庸劣者遂信不疑。開邪見門，塞圓乘路，言不可不慎也。假令說讀一部易經，不如看一艮卦，然且不可，況佛法耶！況佛法之華嚴耶！華嚴具無量門，諸大乘經，猶是華嚴無量門中之一門耳。華嚴，天王也；諸大乘經，侯封也；諸小乘經，侯封之附庸也。餘可知矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

A Song-dynasty Confucian once said: "Reading the entire Avataṃsaka Sūtra is not as good as studying a single Gèn hexagram." Anyone of real intelligence recognizes this remark's error at once, but the mediocre and the dull believe it without a shred of doubt. It opens a door to deviant views and blocks the road to the perfect vehicle — one truly cannot be careless in what one says. Even to claim that reading the entire Yijing is not as good as studying a single Gèn hexagram would already be wrong — how much more so when the comparison is made against the Buddha-dharma! And how much more so still against the Avataṃsaka Sūtra within the Buddha-dharma! The Avataṃsaka contains an immeasurable number of Dharma-gates; every other Mahāyāna sutra is merely one gate among the Avataṃsaka's immeasurable gates. The Avataṃsaka is like the Son of Heaven; the other Mahāyāna sutras are like enfeoffed lords; the Hīnayāna sutras are like the vassal states of those lords. Everything else follows from this.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Gèn hexagram (艮, "Mountain," signifying stillness) is one of the eight trigrams of the Yijing; the unnamed Song Confucian's dismissal of the Avataṃsaka is a stock anti-Buddhist remark of the kind Zhuhong takes up repeatedly across this collection (compare entries 3 and 12 above). His feudal-rank analogy at the close deliberately escalates the comparison rather than merely rejecting it — not just "wrong," but wrong by an entire order of magnitude.

29. 韓淮陰 — Han Xin of Huaiyin

A brief historical reflection on the Han-dynasty general Han Xin, admired for repaying an old grudge with kindness, yet unable to escape a violent death.

Chinese Source

淮陰佐漢滅楚，既王矣，召漂母與之千金，召辱己少年，亦與之千金。夫報恩者人情之常也，不報怨而反酬以恩，可謂有大人之量、君子長者之風矣！而卒不獲以壽考終，千古而下，猶可扼腕。雖然，其故有二：一者仁有餘而智不足，二者多殺人，不免於自殺。理固應然，無足怪者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Han Xin of Huaiyin helped the Han dynasty destroy Chu, and once he had been made king, he summoned the washerwoman who had once fed him and gave her a thousand pieces of gold; he also summoned the young man who had once humiliated him, and gave that man a thousand pieces of gold as well. Repaying kindness is ordinary human feeling — but to repay a grudge not with revenge but with kindness in return, this can truly be called the bearing of a great man, the manner of a noble and mature gentleman! And yet, in the end, he did not live out his natural span of years, and even a thousand years later, one still clenches one's fist over it in regret. And yet the reason for it is twofold: first, his benevolence exceeded his wisdom; second, having killed so many men in his lifetime, he could not, in the end, escape being killed himself. This is simply how the principle works out, and nothing to be surprised at.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Han Xin (d. 196 BCE) was one of the founding generals of the Han dynasty, celebrated in later tradition for both his military brilliance and for the "humiliation of crawling between another man's legs" he endured as a young man in poverty — the same young man he later rewarded rather than punished. Zhuhong reads Han Xin's eventual execution not as tragic misfortune but as straightforward karmic consequence for a lifetime of battlefield killing, a reading that recasts a famous Confucian moral exemplum in explicitly Buddhist terms.

30. 誦經雜話 — Miscellaneous Remarks on Sutra-Chanting

A ghost story attributed to a named living witness, illustrating how a chanter's stray thought can partially undercut the merit of a recitation performed on someone else's behalf.

Chinese Source

總戎戚公，素持金剛經。其守越之三江也，有亡卒致夢云：「明當遣妻詣公，乞為誦經一卷，以資冥道。」翌日，果有婦人悲泣求見。詰之，如夢中語。公諾之，晨起誦經。夜夢卒云：「荷公大恩，然僅得半卷，以於中雜『不用』二字。」公思其故，乃內人使侍婢送茶餅，公遙見，揮手卻之，口雖不言，心謂不用。次早，閉戶誦經。是夜，夢卒謝云：「已獲超拔。」此予親聞於三江僧東林，東林誠篤有道行，不妄語者。噫！誦經僧可不慎歟！

Ru-Yi Translation

The general Lord Qi habitually recited the Diamond Sūtra. While he was garrisoned at Sanjiang in Yue, a deceased soldier appeared to him in a dream and said: "Tomorrow I will send my wife to see you, my lord, to beg you to recite one scroll of scripture on my behalf, to assist me on the dark path." The next day, sure enough, a woman came weeping and asking to see him. When questioned, her account matched exactly what the soldier had said in the dream. Lord Qi agreed to her request, and rose the next morning to chant the sutra. That night he dreamed of the soldier again, who said: "I am grateful for your great kindness, my lord — but I have received only half a scroll's worth of merit, because in the middle of it two words, 'not needed,' got mixed in." Lord Qi thought back over what could have happened, and realized: his wife had sent a maidservant to bring him tea and cakes while he chanted; seeing her approach from a distance, he had waved her away — and though he said nothing aloud, in his heart the thought had been, "Not needed." The next morning he shut his door fast and chanted the sutra again. That night he dreamed the soldier came to thank him: "I have already been delivered."

I heard this myself from the monk Donglin of Sanjiang, a man of great sincerity and real spiritual attainment, who does not speak falsely. Ah — how can a monk who chants sutras for others afford to be careless!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"Lord Qi" is Qi Jiguang (戚繼光, 1528–1588), the celebrated Ming general famous for defeating Japanese pirate raiders along the southeastern coast — a real historical figure, not an anonymous character, which lends the anecdote its documentary weight. Zhuhong is careful to name his own direct source (the monk Donglin) rather than presenting the story as received folklore, the same method used in entries 5 and 25 above.

31. 平心薦亡 — An Even Mind Delivers the Dead

A parallel story to the previous entry, this time praising a shrine-master's impartiality in handling donations as the true source of his ritual efficacy.

Chinese Source

杭郡多士坊，有東平廟。郡之窘人死，致夢其妻云：「諒汝無力修薦；縱多方修薦，不若東平廟廟主某公施一飯斛足矣！」妻詣廟主求請。主云：「我至期有七員主行醮事，奈何！然我寧辭彼就汝。」遂為施食。妻夢夫云：「已超脫矣！」此公平日臥榻上供王靈官像，像前置一瓶，凡得經嚫，目不視，即貯瓶中，隨取隨用，不欲較計厚薄也。一念平等，亡魂賴以津濟。噫！心平即有如是威德，況心空者乎？釋子當自勉矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

In the prefecture of Hangzhou, among its many commemorative arches, stands the Dongping Shrine. A poor man of the city died, and appeared to his wife in a dream, saying: "I know you have no means to arrange a proper funeral rite for me. Even if you managed one by every possible expedient, it would not equal simply having the master of the Dongping Shrine offer a single platform of food for the hungry spirits — that alone would be enough." His wife went to the shrine-master to make the request. The master said: "I already have seven engagements to perform Daoist rites at this time — what am I to do? And yet, I would rather turn those seven away and attend to you instead." So he performed the food-offering rite. That night the wife dreamed of her husband, who said: "I have already been delivered."

This shrine-master habitually kept an image of the god Wang Linggong on a low table by his bed, and in front of the image placed a jar: whatever donations he received for performing rites, he never even looked at, but simply dropped straight into the jar, drawing from it as needed, never troubling to calculate whether the amount given was generous or meager. It was this single thought of impartiality that let the departed spirit find deliverance through him. Ah — a single evenly-balanced thought already carries this much power; how much more, then, one whose mind is truly empty! Buddhist disciples ought to take this to heart and strive.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Wang Linggong is a Daoist deity, and the shrine-master is explicitly a Daoist ritual specialist, not a Buddhist monk — Zhuhong holds him up as a positive example anyway, using the story to make a point about the ethical quality of the officiant's mind (impartiality, indifference to the size of a donation) mattering more than sectarian affiliation.

32. 對境 — Facing One's Circumstances

An extended analogy — five substances placed near a fire — mapping the stages of a practitioner's growing resistance to temptation.

Chinese Source

人對世間財色名利境界，以喻明之。有火聚於此，五物在傍：一如乾草，纔觸即燃者也。其二如木，噓之則燃者也。其三如鐵，不可得燃者也，然而猶可鎔也。其四如水，不惟不燃，反能滅火者也，然而隔之釜甕，猶可沸也。其五如空，然後任其燔灼，體恆自如，亦不須滅，行將自滅也。初一凡夫，中屬修學，漸次最後，方名諸如來大聖人也。

Ru-Yi Translation

When people face the circumstances of the world's wealth, sensual pleasure, fame, and gain, an analogy may make it clear. Suppose there is a bonfire here, and five things placed near it. The first is like dry grass: the moment it is touched by the fire, it bursts into flame. The second is like wood: it does not ignite instantly, but blow on it a little and it, too, catches fire. The third is like iron: it will not catch fire at all — and yet, given enough heat, it can still be melted. The fourth is like water: not only does it not burn, it can actually put the fire out — and yet, set it in a pot over the flame, and it can still be brought to a boil. The fifth is like empty space: whatever the fire does to it, its own nature remains forever unaffected; it needs no putting-out at all, for the fire will simply burn itself down to nothing on its own. The first is the state of the ordinary unenlightened person; the middle three describe the successive stages of a practitioner's deepening cultivation; only the last is the state actually realized by the buddhas, the great sages.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A companion piece to entry 33 below, which offers a parallel five-stage analogy (layers of clothing rather than substances near a fire) for the same underlying progression from complete vulnerability to complete imperturbability.

33. 去障 — Removing Obstructions

The paired analogy to entry 32 above — five layers of clothing standing in for five stages of removing obstructions to practice — followed by an unrelated short note on the relativity of suffering across different beings.

去障 — Removing Obstructions

Chinese Source

修行去障，亦有五等。喻如一人之身，五重纏裹，最外鐵甲，次以皮裘，次以布袍，次以羅衫，又次貼肉極以輕綃。次第解之，輕綃俱去，方是本體赤自身也。行人外去麤障，去之又去，直至根本無明極微細障皆悉去盡，方是本體清淨法身也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Removing the obstructions to practice likewise happens in five stages. Consider a person's body wrapped in five layers of clothing: outermost, an iron coat of armor; next, a fur robe; next, a cloth gown; next, a silk shirt; and innermost, against the skin, the lightest possible gauze. To remove them, one must undo them in order, and only once even the lightest gauze has been taken off does one arrive at the body's own bare, original state. A practitioner must first remove the coarse, outer obstructions, and go on removing them layer after layer, until even the most subtle obstructions of fundamental ignorance itself have been entirely cleared away — only then does one arrive at the body's original state, the pure Dharma-body.

以苦為樂 — Taking Suffering for Happiness

Chinese Source

廁蟲之在廁也，自犬羊視之不勝其苦，而廁蟲不知苦，方以為樂也。犬羊之在地也，自人視之不勝其苦，而犬羊不知苦，方以為樂也。人之在世也，自天視之不勝其苦，而人不知苦，方以為樂也。推而極之，天之苦樂亦猶是也。知此而求生淨土，萬牛莫挽矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

A maggot living in a latrine, seen from a dog's or a sheep's point of view, would seem to be suffering unbearably — yet the maggot itself knows nothing of suffering, and even takes it for happiness. Dogs and sheep living on the bare ground, seen from a human point of view, would seem to be suffering unbearably — yet dogs and sheep themselves know nothing of suffering, and even take it for happiness. Human beings living in this turbid world, seen from the point of view of the heavenly beings, would seem to be suffering unbearably — yet human beings themselves know nothing of suffering, and even take it for happiness. Carried to its furthest extension, the happiness and suffering of the heavenly beings themselves works exactly the same way. Once you truly know this, and seek to be reborn in the Pure Land, not even ten thousand oxen pulling together could drag you back!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The chain of comparisons (maggot / dog / human / heavenly being) is a standard device for relativizing the ordinary sense that one's own current state is simply "normal" — each rung in the ladder is, from the rung above, a form of suffering its inhabitants cannot even perceive as such. Zhuhong's closing line turns this into a specifically Pure Land argument for urgency.

34. 二客對弈 — Two Guests at a Game of Go

A very short scene, almost a joke, about absorption in a game standing in for the way ordinary consciousness becomes lodged in whatever it fixates on.

Chinese Source

二客方對弈，有哂於傍者曰：「吾見二肉柱動搖耳。」客曰：「何謂也？」曰：「二君形存而神離，神在黑白子中久矣，相對峙者非肉柱而何？」客默然。

Ru-Yi Translation

Two guests were absorbed in a game of go, when someone standing nearby said with a smile: "All I see is two pillars of flesh, twitching." The players asked: "What do you mean by that?" He said: "Your two bodies are present, gentlemen, but your spirits have already departed — your consciousness has been lodged inside those black and white stones for quite some time now. What is it, facing each other here, if not two pillars of flesh?" The two players had nothing to say.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

One of the shortest and most purely anecdotal entries in the collection so far — no scripture is cited, and the point is left entirely to the image itself: consciousness fully absorbed into an object of attachment, leaving only an inert body behind.

35. 思惟修 — Cultivation Through Contemplation

A careful doctrinal note distinguishing right from wrong contemplation, resolving an apparent contradiction between two different sutra statements about the value of thought in meditation.

Chinese Source

禪那者，此云思惟修，故稱禪思比丘，是貴思也。經又言：「有思惟心，終不能入如來大涅槃海。」又言：「是法非思量分別之所能及。」是病思也。所以者何？蓋思有二：一正思惟，一邪思惟。無思之思，是正思惟也；有思之思，是邪思惟也。又思有二：一從外而思內，背塵合覺者也。一從內而思外，背覺合塵者也。從內思外者，思之思之，又重思之，思無盡而真彌遠也。從外思內者，思之思之，又重思之，思盡而還源也。由思而入無思，即念佛者由念而入無念也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Dhyāna, in Chinese, means "cultivation through contemplation" — hence the term "monk of contemplative meditation," which shows how highly contemplation is valued. Yet a sutra also says: "One who still has a contemplating mind can never enter the great ocean of the Tathāgata's nirvana," and again: "This Dharma cannot be reached by thought or discriminative analysis" — which points instead to contemplation's fault. Why is this? Because contemplation is of two kinds: right contemplation and wrong contemplation. Contemplation without discriminating thought is right contemplation; contemplation with discriminating thought is wrong contemplation. Contemplation is also of two further kinds: contemplating inward from without — turning away from the dust of the senses to unite with awareness; and contemplating outward from within — turning away from awareness to unite with the dust of the senses. For one who contemplates outward from within, thinking and thinking and thinking again, thought never comes to an end, and truth only grows more distant. For one who contemplates inward from without, thinking and thinking and thinking again, thought comes to its end, and one returns to the source. To pass, by way of contemplation, into no-contemplation — this is exactly like the practitioner of Buddha-recitation who passes, by way of recitation, into no-recitation.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The closing comparison — contemplation resolving into no-contemplation, exactly as recitation resolves into no-recitation — is characteristic of how Zhuhong repeatedly draws Chan-style meditation vocabulary back toward Pure Land practice, treating the two as structurally parallel disciplines rather than rival schools.

36. 諍友 — A Friend Who Speaks Frankly

A personal, somewhat melancholy memory of two blunt friends from Zhuhong's early monastic years, and a regret that friends of their kind have since become impossible to find.

Chinese Source

予初出家時，皋亭茶湯寺老僧，以誕日延予齋。時大嶺有立禪，北人也，戇直無諂，顧予曰：「彼延子為佛法耶？人情耶？彼以人情重子耳，何往為？」予大慚。又友古溟者，謂予言：「子以後不出世為妙。」予告以素所願，願終身居學地，而自鍛鍊。溟笑曰：「子卻有出世日在，未免也。」今思如二友者不可復得，淒然傷感者久之。

Ru-Yi Translation

When I first left the household life, an elderly monk at Chatang Monastery on Mount Gaoting invited me to a vegetarian feast in honor of his birthday. At the time there was a Chan practitioner at Daling, a northerner, honest and blunt without a trace of flattery, who looked at me and said: "Did he invite you for the sake of the Dharma, or out of mere social obligation? I suspect he values you chiefly out of social obligation — why go at all?" I was deeply ashamed.

There was also a fellow practitioner named Guming, who once said to me: "It would be best if you never came out to head a monastery." I told him of my own long-standing wish — to remain, my whole life, a student still on the path, continually forging myself. Guming laughed and said: "You will have your day of coming out to lead one nonetheless — there's no escaping it."

Thinking of it now, I can never find friends like these two again, and I have felt a lingering sorrow over it for a long time.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Guming's prediction proved correct: Zhuhong did eventually come out to lead a monastic community, restoring Yunqi into one of the most influential centers of the late Ming (see entries 13, 45, and 46, where he reflects on exactly this cost). The nostalgia here is unusually personal for the collection — a rare moment where Zhuhong writes about missing specific people rather than drawing a general lesson.

37. 鼓樂 — Drums and Music

A brief scene contrasting two monks' reactions to a celebratory procession, followed by a short definitional note on what makes someone a genuine "person of the Way."

鼓樂 — Drums and Music

Chinese Source

秋榜出，新舉子有鼓樂而過上方之門者，二僧趨而往覘之。甲云：「善哉，不亦樂乎！」乙云：「善哉，不亦悲乎！」甲問故。乙曰：「子徒知今日之鼓樂，而不知有後日之鼓樂也。」甲不解，歎羨如故。

Ru-Yi Translation

When the autumn examination results were posted, a procession of drums and music passed the gate of a monastery, celebrating a newly successful candidate, and two monks hurried out to watch. The first said: "How wonderful! Isn't this joyous!" The second said: "How wonderful! Isn't this sorrowful!" The first asked why. The second said: "You only know today's drums and music — you don't yet know there will be another set of drums and music, later on." The first monk did not understand, and went on admiring the scene just as before.

道人重輕 — What a Person of the Way Weighs Heavy and Light

Chinese Source

古所稱道人，以世所重者彼輕之，世所輕者彼重之故也。世所重者何？富貴也。世所輕者何？身心也。今與世同其重輕，是得為道人乎哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

What the ancients called a "person of the Way" was someone who took lightly what the world takes seriously, and took seriously what the world takes lightly. What does the world take seriously? Wealth and rank. What does the world take lightly? The cultivation of body and mind. If a person cultivating the Way today weighs these the same way the world does, can such a person still be called a person of the Way?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The second monk's cryptic remark — "another set of drums and music, later on" — is left unexplained in the text itself, but the sense is a funeral procession: today's celebratory drums for worldly success will, inevitably, one day be followed by the drums of a funeral cortege. The first monk's failure to grasp this is the entry's real subject.

38. 佛經不可不讀 — The Sutras Must Be Read

A personal account of Zhuhong's own youthful prejudice against Buddhism, followed by a threefold taxonomy of ways people fail to engage seriously with scripture even after picking it up.

Chinese Source

予少時見前賢闢佛，主先入之言，作矮人之視，罔覺也。偶於戒壇經肆，請數卷經讀之，始大驚曰：「不讀如是書，幾虛度一生矣！」今人乃有自少而壯、而老、而死，不一過目者，可謂面寶山而不入者也。又一類，雖讀之，不過採其辭，致以資談柄、助筆勢，自少而壯、而老、而死，不一究其理者，可謂入寶山而不取者也。又一類，雖討論，雖講演，亦不過訓字銷文、爭新競高，自少而壯、而老、而死，不一真修而實踐者，可謂取其寶，把玩之、賞鑑之、懷之、袖之而復棄之者也。雖然，一染識田，終成道種。是故佛經不可不讀。

Ru-Yi Translation

When I was young, I saw earlier worthies attacking Buddhism, and, taking their preconceived opinion as my own, I too looked down on it with the same shallow understanding, entirely unaware of my own ignorance. Then, by chance, at a sutra shop near an ordination platform, I obtained several scrolls of scripture and read them — and only then was I truly astonished, and sighed: "Had I never read books like these, I would have all but wasted my entire life!"

There are people today who go from youth to middle age to old age to death without ever once laying eyes on a Buddhist sutra — they might be said to stand before a mountain of treasure and never enter it. There is another kind of person who does read the sutras, but only in order to extract phrases for conversation or to lend flourish to their own writing — from youth to middle age to old age to death, never once truly investigating the profound meaning within; they might be said to enter the mountain of treasure and take nothing from it. And there is yet another kind who discuss the sutras, even lecture on them, but do no more than gloss individual characters and paraphrase the text, competing to seem novel or superior — from youth to middle age to old age to death, never once truly practicing what the teaching says; they might be said to have actually taken hold of the treasure, only to turn it over in their hands as a curiosity, admire it, tuck it in a sleeve or a pocket — and then discard it after all.

And yet, even so: once a seed has been planted in the field of consciousness, it will eventually become a seed of the path to buddhahood. This is why the sutras must be read.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhuhong's threefold taxonomy — never reading at all, reading only for literary ornament, and reading or even lecturing without practice — escalates in a pointed way: the third category is the most sophisticated engagement with scripture and also, in Zhuhong's telling, no better than the first two, since none of them results in actual practice. The closing "field of consciousness" (識田) is the ālayavijñāna, the storehouse consciousness in which even a merely superficial exposure to the Dharma is said to leave a lasting seed.

39. 蕭妃 — Consort Xiao

A grim historical anecdote about an imperial murder and the vow of revenge it provoked, used as an occasion to reflect on the limits of ritual deliverance in the face of deeply entrenched enmity.

Chinese Source

武后效人彘，殺王后等且死，誓願生生世世己為貓、武為鼠，生扼其喉而噉其肉。至今貓鼠中尚有二人受生，雖報復百千萬遍未已也。往時予作水陸齋，憫而薦之，只恐冤力深、薦力淺，未能遽釋耳。古來類此者頗眾。今人修善事，不辭多為津濟可也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Empress Wu, imitating the "human pig" punishment devised by Empress Lü of Han, put Empress Wang and the others to death; before dying, Consort Xiao made a vow that in life after life she herself would be reborn as a cat and Wu as a rat, so that she could seize Wu by the throat and eat her flesh, life after life. To this day the two of them are said to still be reborn among cats and rats, and even after hundreds of thousands of such cycles of revenge, it has still not come to an end.

In the past, when I held a Water-and-Land rite, I felt pity for them and had them included in the deliverance offered — though I feared their mutual enmity ran so deep, and the power of my own deliverance-rite was so shallow, that it could not release them all at once. Cases like this, going back through history, are far from rare. When people today perform good works, they should not shy away from doing as much as they can to help and ferry such beings across.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Consort Xiao and Empress Wang were both destroyed by the future Empress Wu Zetian (624–705), China's only reigning female emperor, in a well-documented palace power struggle recorded in the official Tang histories; the "human pig" torture Wu is said to have imitated was itself notorious from the early Han dynasty. Zhuhong treats the story as a live, ongoing case — a rare instance of him admitting the limits of his own ritual efforts rather than reporting an unambiguous success, as in entries 5 and 31 above.

40. 泰首座 — Head Monk Tai

A dense piece of internal Chan criticism, comparing two classic gōng'àn about death and awakening and arguing that outward accomplishment (a serene, controlled death) does not by itself prove genuine realization.

Chinese Source

或謂：「泰首座刻香坐脫，九峰不許，以不會石霜休去、歇去、寒灰枯木去等語也；而紙衣道者能去能來，將無會石霜意，而洞山亦不許者，何也？」愚謂紙衣若果已出息不涉眾緣，入息不居陰界，則去住自由，當與洞山作愚癡齋，把手共行，泰何可及？如或不然，未免是弄精魂漢，古人所謂鬼神活計者是也。而泰公卻有真實定力，特其「耽著靜境，不解轉身」一句，二者病則均也。然紙衣虛心就洞山理會，而泰公奮然長往，自失大利。滿招損，謙受益，學禪者宜知之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked: "Head Monk Tai passed away seated, within the time it takes a stick of incense to burn — yet Jiufeng would not certify his attainment, on the grounds that he had not truly grasped Shishuang's sayings 'rest,' 'cease,' 'become like cold ash and dead wood,' and so on. And the Robe-of-Paper practitioner could come and go between life and death at will — surely he, too, must not have grasped Shishuang's meaning, since Dongshan likewise refused to certify him? Why is that?"

My own view: if the Robe-of-Paper practitioner had truly reached the point where his outbreath was untouched by any external condition and his inbreath dwelt in no realm of the aggregates at all, then his freedom to come and go would be genuine, and he could have walked hand in hand with Dongshan at the Feast of Folly as an equal — how could Tai possibly compare to that? But if he had not actually reached that point, then he was, in the end, no more than a man playing tricks with his own spirit-essence — exactly what the ancients called "the trade of ghosts and spirits." Head Monk Tai, on the other hand, genuinely possessed real meditative power — his one fault lay in the single phrase "clinging to a state of stillness, unable to turn and move beyond it." On this point, the two men's faults were, in fact, equal. Yet the Robe-of-Paper practitioner emptied his mind and went to seek understanding from Dongshan, while Head Monk Tai, in a fit of resolve, simply departed for good — and so lost, of his own accord, the greater benefit he might have gained. "Fullness invites loss; humility receives benefit" — students of Chan ought to know this.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Both source cases are classic Chan gōng'àn from the Wudeng Huiyuan. Head Monk Tai, expecting to succeed Shishuang Qingzhu as abbot, was tested by Jiufeng on Shishuang's "seven departures" teaching and, unable to answer, died on the spot to prove his attainment — a demonstration Jiufeng pointedly refused to certify as genuine understanding. The Robe-of-Paper practitioner's death and near-revival before Caoshan Benji is the companion case. Zhuhong's verdict cuts against both a naive reading (impressive death = awakening) and simple partisanship for either master, insisting that a dramatic display of control over one's own death proves meditative power but not necessarily wisdom.

41. 睡著無夢時主人 — Who Is the Master When Asleep and Dreamless?

A classic Chan three-part question, with a caution against students jumping straight to its hardest stage.

Chinese Source

雪巖初問高峰：「日間浩浩作得主麼？」次問：「夜夢中作得主麼？」三問：「正睡著無夢時，主人公在甚麼處？」今人便向第三問，以情識卜度，錯了也。汝且日間作主不得，又何論最後極深深處？不如就初門著緊用心，以次理會去未晚。雖然，若於第三問了悟無疑，白日間、夜夢中無不帖帖地矣，過量人前，又不可以格例拘也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Xueyan asked Gaofeng: first, "In the tumult of the daytime, can you remain master of yourself?" Then: "In your dreams at night, can you remain master of yourself?" And third: "When you are sound asleep, without dreams, where is your true master then?" People today go straight to this third question and try to work it out through discursive guesswork — this is simply wrong. If you cannot even remain master of yourself in the waking day, how can you possibly speak of that final, deepest place at all? Better to apply yourself earnestly at the first question, and work through them in order — there is still time. That said, if someone can awaken directly to the third question, with no doubt remaining, then in the waking day or in dreaming sleep alike, everything will already be perfectly settled — and a person of truly exceptional capacity is, after all, not to be confined by any fixed order of stages.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Xueyan Zuqin and his student Gaofeng Yuanmiao were 13th-century Linji-school Chan masters; Gaofeng's own awakening account (traditionally triggered when a fellow student knocked his pillow to the floor) is one of the best-known in the later Chan tradition. Zhuhong's point is procedural rather than doctrinal: the three-stage question is meant to be worked through in order, and its popularity as a stand-alone koan in his own day had led students to skip straight to the hardest stage without doing the groundwork.

42. 布施 — Giving

A defense of a famously puzzling gesture by the Tang-dynasty layman Pang: sinking his own fortune in the sea rather than giving it to charity.

Chinese Source

龐居士以家財沈海，人謂：「奚不布施？」士云：「吾多劫為布施所累，故沈之耳。」愚人藉口，遂祕悋不施。不知居士為布施住相者解縛也，非以布施為不可也。萬行有般若以為導，三輪空寂，雖終日施，奚病焉？又凡夫膠著於布施，沈海之舉，是並其布施而布施之也，是名大施，是名真施，是名無上施，安得謂居士不施？

Ru-Yi Translation

Layman Pang sank his entire family fortune into the sea. People asked him: "Why not give it away in charity instead?" The layman answered: "Across many lifetimes I have been burdened by the practice of giving — that is exactly why I am sinking it." Foolish people then use this remark as an excuse, hoarding their wealth and refusing to give at all. What they fail to understand is that the layman was releasing those attached to the outward form of giving from their own bondage — he was not saying that giving itself is wrong.

The myriad practices are led by prajñā; once the three wheels — giver, receiver, and gift — are seen as empty and still, what fault could there be even in giving all day long? It is only the ordinary, unenlightened person who clings fixedly to the form of giving — and so the layman's act of sinking his fortune in the sea was itself a giving away of even the name and form of "giving." This may rightly be called the great giving, the true giving, the giving without peer — how, then, could one say the layman did not give?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Layman Pang (龐蘊, d. 808) is one of the most celebrated lay Chan practitioners in the tradition, sometimes called the "Vimalakīrti of the East." Zhuhong's argument turns on the doctrine of the "three wheels empty and still" (三輪空寂) — the standard Mahāyāna teaching that truly pure giving requires no attachment to the giver, the receiver, or the gift itself as separately real — used here to close off a misreading that would turn Pang's radical gesture into an excuse for ordinary stinginess.

43. 尚直尚理編 — On "Favoring Directness" and "Favoring Principle"

A measured defense of the Song Confucian philosopher Zhu Xi against a Ming-dynasty monk's charge of Buddhist plagiarism combined with public denunciation.

Chinese Source

國初空谷禪師，著尚直、尚理二編，極談儒釋之際，其間力辨晦菴先生暗用佛法而明排之。愚意晦菴恐無此心，或是見解未到耳。何以知之？記少年曾看朱子語類，自云：「昔於某老先生坐中，聽一僧議論，心悅之。後進場屋，便寫入卷中。試官被某哄動，遂中式。及見延平先生，方知有聖賢學問。」以是知晦菴之學佛，不過如今人用資文筆而已，原不曾得佛深理。其排佛，是見解未到。空谷責之，似為太過。

Ru-Yi Translation

At the start of our dynasty, Master Konggu wrote the two compilations Favoring Directness and Favoring Principle, discussing the relationship between Confucianism and Buddhism at great length, and in the course of it forcefully argued that Master Huian [Zhu Xi] secretly made use of Buddhist teaching while openly attacking it. I myself suspect Huian harbored no such intention — it is more likely simply that his understanding had not yet reached that depth. How do I know this? I recall that in my youth I once read the Categorized Sayings of Master Zhu, in which he says of himself: "Once, sitting in the presence of a certain elder, I heard a monk expound his views, and my heart was delighted by them. Later, when I entered the examination hall, I wrote what I had heard straight into my exam paper. The examiner was so taken with it that I passed. It was only later, when I met Master Yanping, that I came to know there was such a thing as the learning of the sages." From this passage one can see that Huian's engagement with Buddhism amounted to no more than what people today do when they borrow it to embellish their prose — he never actually attained the profound principle of the Dharma. His attacks on Buddhism, then, came from understanding not yet arrived, not from deliberate concealment. Konggu's reproach of him seems, in that light, somewhat excessive.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Master Konggu (空谷景隆) was an early Ming Chan monk whose two apologetic works directly accused Zhu Xi (1130–1200), the great synthesizer of Song Neo-Confucianism, of hypocrisy. Zhuhong's defense of Zhu Xi here is notable for its restraint — he does not defend Neo-Confucian anti-Buddhist polemic as correct, only argues that it came from a genuine (if shallow) engagement with Buddhism rather than deliberate bad faith, a more generous reading than Konggu's.

44. 戒殺 — Refraining from Killing

A forceful vegetarian polemic, closing with a personal note about the wide circulation of an earlier essay of Zhuhong's on the same subject.

Chinese Source

天地生物以供人食，如種種穀、種種果、種種蔬菜、種種水陸珍味。而人又以智巧餅之、餌之、鹽之、酢之、烹之、炮之，可謂千足萬足，何苦復將同有血氣、同有子母、同有知覺、覺痛覺癢、覺生覺死之物而殺食之，豈理也哉？尋常說：「只要心好，不在齋素。」嗟乎！戮其身而啖其肉，天下之言凶心、慘心、毒心、惡心，孰甚焉？好心當在何處？予昔作戒殺放生文勸世，而頗有翻刻此文，不下一二十本。善哉斯世，何幸猶有如是仁人君子在也！

Ru-Yi Translation

Heaven and earth bring forth living things for people to eat: every kind of grain, every kind of fruit, every kind of vegetable, every fine delicacy of land and water alike. And people, with their own cleverness and skill, go on to turn these into cakes, pastries, salted preserves, pickled dishes, boiled dishes, roasted dishes — surely this is already abundance enough, ten thousand times over. Why, then, must people go on to kill and eat creatures that share with us the same blood, the same bond between mother and child, the same awareness, the same capacity to feel pain and itch, the same knowledge of life and of death — how could this possibly be reasonable?

People commonly say: "It's only the heart that matters — you don't need to keep a vegetarian diet." Alas! To slaughter a creature's body and feast on its flesh — of all the things in the world called a vicious heart, a cruel heart, a poisonous heart, an evil heart, which could possibly be worse than this? Where, then, is the "good heart" supposed to be in all this? I once wrote an essay, On Refraining from Killing and Releasing Life, to exhort the world, and it has since gone through a good many reprintings by others, not fewer than ten or twenty editions. How wonderful — that in this world there are still such benevolent and noble people to be found!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The essay Zhuhong mentions, 《戒殺放生文》("On Refraining from Killing and Releasing Life"), is a real and historically influential work — one of the most widely circulated vegetarian and animal-release tracts in late imperial Chinese Buddhism, still reprinted today. His closing remark about "ten or twenty editions" is a rare moment of Zhuhong noting the practical reach of his own earlier writing.

45. 建立叢林 — Establishing a Monastic Community

A cautionary, personal reflection on the cost of institution-building, drawn from Zhuhong's own experience restoring Yunqi Monastery, followed by a short separate note on the surprising ranking of faith among monastics and laypeople.

建立叢林 — Establishing a Monastic Community

Chinese Source

叢林為眾，固是美事，然須己事已辦，而後為之。不然，或煩勞神志，或耽著世緣，致令未有所得者望洋而終，已有所得者中道而廢。予興復雲棲，事事皆出勢所自迫而後動作，曾不強為，而亦所損於己不少，況盡心力而求之乎！書此自警，並以告夫來者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Establishing a monastic community for the benefit of the assembly is certainly a fine undertaking — but it should only be taken up once one's own affairs are already settled. Otherwise, it may exhaust one's spirit and vitality, or draw one into worldly entanglements, so that those who have not yet attained anything end their lives merely gazing helplessly at the ocean of the task before them, and those who have already attained something abandon their practice halfway through. In restoring Yunqi, every single step I took was forced on me by circumstance, and I never pushed for any of it myself — and even so, my own practice suffered no small loss because of it. How much more so, then, for someone who pursues such a project with their whole heart and strength! I write this down to caution myself, and to tell those who come after.

僧俗信心 — The Faith of Monastics and Laypeople

Chinese Source

末法中，頗有出家比丘信心，不如在家居士者；在家居士信心，不如在家女人者。何惑乎學佛者多，而成佛者少也！

Ru-Yi Translation

In this age of the Dharma's decline, one often finds that a monastic's faith falls short of a lay householder's, and a lay man's faith falls short of a lay woman's. Is it any wonder, then, that so many study the Buddha's teaching, and so few actually attain buddhahood?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhuhong personally led the restoration of Yunqi Monastery from a ruined site into one of the most important centers of the late-Ming Buddhist revival — the "restoration" he refers to here is not a hypothetical case but his own decades-long project, which makes his admitted cost ("my own practice suffered no small loss") an unusually candid piece of self-assessment rather than abstract advice.

46. 損己利人 — Losing to Benefit Others

A close doctrinal reading of two great Tiantai patriarchs' famously humble self-assessments at the moment of death, arguing that their humility, while sincere, understates a form of loss that is not really loss at all.

Chinese Source

智者入滅，曰：「吾不領眾，必淨六根；由損己利人，止登五品。」南嶽亦自言：「坐是止證鐵輪。」二師雖是謙己誨人，然亦實語；但與我輩之損不同耳。何以故？我輩損則誠損，二師雖損而不損也。今以喻明：如一富室、一窘人，二俱捐財濟眾，其損不異。然窘人則窘益甚，富室則富自若也。又如溝渠江海，均用汲灌，而溝渠減涸，江海自若也。既無所損，何為限於五品、鐵輪？噫！天下以聖歸仲尼，仲尼言聖我不能；天下以道屬文王，文王顧望道未見。增上慢比丘，可弗思乎？

Ru-Yi Translation

As Master Zhiyi approached his death, he said: "Had I never led an assembly, I would certainly have achieved purification of all six sense-faculties; but because I sacrificed my own advancement to benefit others, I have attained only the fifth of the five ranks." Nanyue likewise said of himself: "For this same reason I have only reached the Iron-Wheel stage." Though both masters spoke this way out of humility, to instruct others, what they said was also literally true — yet the loss they speak of is not the same kind of loss as ours. Why is this? Because when we sacrifice something in serving others, it is a genuine loss; when these two masters did so, it was a loss that was, in fact, no loss at all.

Let me illustrate: suppose a wealthy household and a poor household both give away money to help the needy — the amount given away may be identical, and yet the poor household grows all the poorer for it, while the wealthy household remains just as wealthy as before. Or again: a ditch and the ocean are both drawn upon for irrigation, yet the ditch runs dry while the sea remains exactly as full as before. Since these two masters lost nothing at all, why should they describe themselves as limited to the fifth rank, or the Iron-Wheel stage? Ah — the whole world attributes sagehood to Confucius, and yet Confucius himself said, "How could I dare to be called a sage?" The whole world attributes the Way to King Wen, and yet King Wen himself said he still looked and had not yet seen the Way. Monks given to spiritual conceit — should they not reflect on this?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhiyi (538–597) and his teacher Nanyue Huisi (515–577) are, respectively, the effective founder and the second patriarch of the Tiantai school; both are recorded, at the end of their lives, ranking their own attainment modestly against the school's own graded scale of spiritual progress (the "five ranks" and the "six-wheel" stages). Zhuhong's argument — that their apparent humility describes a "loss" so vast in reserve that it barely counts as loss at all — closes with a pointed jab at ordinary monks who claim attainments they do not have (增上慢, "spiritual conceit"), the same target as entry 18 above.

47. 良知 — Innate Knowing

A precise, logically structured critique of the popular claim that Wang Yangming's Confucian doctrine of "innate knowing" is the same thing as the Buddha's "true knowing."

Chinese Source

新建創良知之說，是其識見學力深造所到，非強立標幟以張大其門庭者也。然好同儒釋者，謂即是佛說之真知，則未可。何者？「良知」二字，本出子輿氏，今以三支格之：良知為宗，不慮而知為因，孩提之童無不知愛親敬長為喻。則知良者美也，自然知之，而非造作者也。而所知愛敬涉妄已久，豈真常寂照之謂哉？「真」之與「良」固當有辨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Master Xinjian [Wang Yangming] founded the teaching of innate knowing out of the deep attainment of his own learning and insight — not as a banner deliberately raised to build up a following of his own. But those fond of reconciling Confucianism and Buddhism, who claim this "innate knowing" is simply the Buddha's own "true knowing," go too far. Why? The term "innate knowing" originates with Mencius himself. Let me analyze it now using the three-part form of Buddhist logic — thesis, reason, and example: the thesis is "innate knowing"; the reason is that it is known without deliberation; the example is that even a small child knows, without being taught, to love its parents and respect its elders. From this we see that "innate" means good, or excellent — something known spontaneously, not something constructed through effort. But the love and respect thus known has already, for a very long time, been entangled with delusion — how could it possibly be the same as the true, ever-still, ever-illuminating awareness the Buddha speaks of? Between "true" and "innate," a real distinction must be drawn.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhuhong deploys the formal three-part structure of Buddhist logic (thesis, reason, and example — the Indian syllogism used in Yogācāra epistemology) to analyze a piece of Confucian philosophy, a striking cross-disciplinary move. The following two entries continue the same investigation from different angles, testing two more candidate Confucian concepts — "vital spirit" and Confucius's own remarks on the wandering mind — against the same standard.

48. 心之精神是謂聖 — The Mind's Vital Spirit Is Called Sagehood

Continuing the previous entry's inquiry, this time testing a line from the Kongcongzi favored by the Song philosopher Yang Jian.

Chinese Source

孔叢子云：「心之精神是謂聖。」楊慈湖平生學問以是為宗，其於良知何似，得無合佛說之真知歟？曰：精神更淺於良知，均之水上波耳，惡得為真知乎哉？且「精神」二字，分言之，則各有旨；合而成文，則精魂神識之謂也，昔人有言：「無量劫來生死本，癡人認作本來人」者是也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Kongcongzi says: "The mind's vital spirit is called sagehood." Yang Cihu built his entire lifetime of learning on this single line as its foundation — how does this compare to "innate knowing"? Could it, perhaps, accord with the Buddha's own "true knowing"?

I would say: "vital spirit" is in fact a shallower notion even than "innate knowing" — both alike are no more than ripples on the surface of water. How could either be called true knowing? Moreover, taken separately, the two characters 精 [essence] and 神 [spirit] each carry their own meaning; but combined into a single compound term, they refer to nothing more than the deluded consciousness that clings to a sense of self. As a person of old once said: "The root of birth and death across countless kalpas — and the fool mistakes it for his own original face."

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Yang Jian ("Cihu," 1141–1226) was a Song philosopher in the Lu Jiuyuan school of Confucian mind-cultivation; the closing citation ("the root of birth and death... the fool mistakes it for his own original face") is a well-known line from the Chan classic Wumenguan, warning against mistaking ordinary discriminating consciousness for enlightened mind — the exact confusion Zhuhong argues Yang Jian has made.

49. 寂感 — Stillness and Response

The most sustained of the three linked entries on Confucian mind-philosophy, arguing that Confucius himself came genuinely close to the Buddhist doctrine of true knowing in the Yijing's "Appended Remarks," followed by two short, unrelated companion notes on karma and social rank across lifetimes.

寂感 — Stillness and Response

Chinese Source

慈湖，儒者也，不觀仲尼之言乎：「操則存，舍則亡，出入無時，莫知其鄉。」則進於精神矣，復進於良知矣！然則是佛說之真知乎？曰：亦未也。真無存亡，真無出入也。「莫知其鄉」則庶幾矣，而猶未舉其全也。仲尼又云：「無思也，無為也，寂然不動，感而遂通天下之故。」夫泯思為而入寂，是莫知其鄉也。無最後句，則成斷滅；斷滅，則無知矣！「通天下之故」，無上三句則成亂想，亂想則妄知矣！寂而通，是之謂真知也。然斯言也，論易也，非論心也，人以屬之蓍卦而已。蓋時未至、機未熟，仲尼微露而寄之乎易，使人自得之也。甚矣！仲尼之善言心也。信矣！仲尼之為儒童菩薩也。然則讀儒書足了生死，何以佛為？曰：佛談如是妙理，遍於三藏；其在儒書，千百言中而偶一及也。仲尼非不知也，仲尼主世間法，釋迦主出世間法也。心雖無二，而門庭施設不同，學者不得不各從其門也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Cihu was a Confucian scholar — did he never read Confucius's own words: "Hold on to it and it remains; let it go and it is lost; it comes and goes at no fixed time, and no one knows its direction"? This already goes beyond mere "vital spirit," and beyond "innate knowing" as well! Does this, then, count as the Buddha's own true knowing? I would say: not yet, either. True knowing has no coming or going, no remaining or being lost. "No one knows its direction" comes close — but it still does not capture the whole of it.

Confucius also said: "Without thought, without deliberate action, utterly still and unmoving — and yet, when stirred, it penetrates all the causes and conditions of the world." To extinguish thought and deliberate action, and enter stillness — this is "no one knows its direction." Without that final clause, it would collapse into mere annihilationism; and annihilationism is simply ignorance! "Penetrates all the causes and conditions of the world" — without the three clauses that precede it, this alone would collapse into chaotic, unmoored thought; and chaotic thought is simply deluded knowing! Still, and yet penetrating — this, and only this, is what may be called true knowing.

And yet these words of Confucius were spoken in the context of expounding the Yijing, not in a direct treatise on the mind itself — which is why people have taken them merely as a matter of divination. The truth is that the time had not yet come, the opportunity had not yet ripened, and so Confucius could only let a faint trace of it show through, entrusted to the language of the Yijing, leaving it for people to arrive at the deeper meaning themselves. How extraordinary — how skillfully Confucius spoke of the mind! And how true it is, then, that Confucius was himself the Bodhisattva Rútóng!

If that is so, does reading the Confucian classics alone suffice to resolve the great matter of birth and death — why, then, do we still need the Buddha? I would say: the Buddha expounds wondrous principles exactly like this one throughout the whole of the Tripiṭaka; in the Confucian classics, by contrast, among a thousand and ten thousand words, such a thing appears only once, in passing. It is not that Confucius did not know it — Confucius simply took worldly affairs as his own domain, while Śākyamuni took what transcends the world as his. The underlying mind is not two different things — only the gate through which it is approached differs, and students cannot help but follow whichever gate suits them.

來生一 — The Next Life, First Note

Chinese Source

今生持戒修福之僧，若心地未明、願力輕微，又不求淨土，是人來生多感富貴之報，亦多為富貴所迷，或至造業墮落者。有老僧搖手不之信。予謂無論隔世，親見一僧結茅北峰之陰，十年頗著清修；一時善信敬慕，為別創庵，徙居之，遂致沈溺，前所微得俱喪。現世且然，況來生耶！問此為誰？予云：「即老兄是。」其人默然。

Ru-Yi Translation

A monk who, in this present life, keeps the precepts and cultivates merit, but whose mind is not yet clear and whose aspiration is weak, and who moreover does not seek rebirth in the Pure Land — such a person, in the next life, will often experience the retribution of wealth and rank, and will often, in turn, be led astray by that very wealth and rank, even to the point of creating karma and falling into lower states. There was an old monk who, hearing me say this, waved his hand and refused to believe it.

I said to him: "Never mind other lifetimes — I have seen this with my own eyes, in this very life. A monk once built a simple thatched hut on the shady side of North Peak, and for ten years gained a considerable reputation for pure practice there. Then, all at once, devoted men and women of faith came to admire him, built him a separate hermitage, and moved him there to live — and this led him straight into decline, so that whatever small gain he had made before was entirely lost. If this can happen in the present life, how much more so in the next!" He asked who this monk was. I said: "It is you yourself, old friend." He fell silent.

來生二 — The Next Life, Second Note

Chinese Source

僧有見貴顯人而心生慕羨願似之者，復有見貴顯人而心生厭薄若不屑者，是二人皆過也。何也？爾徒知慕羨彼，而寧知彼之前生，即爾苦行修福僧人乎？則何必慕羨！爾徒知厭薄彼，而寧知爾之苦行，來生當作彼有名有位官人乎？則何可厭薄！既未離生死，彼此更迭，如汲井輪，互為高下，思之及此，能不寒心？但應努力前修，不捨寸陰以期出世，安得閒工夫為他人慕羨耶？厭薄耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Some monks, on seeing a person of high rank and standing, feel envy rise in their hearts and wish to be like him; others, on seeing the same person, feel only contempt and disdain. Both are mistaken. Why? You who envy him — how do you know that his previous life was not one just like your own, a monk of austere practice and accumulated merit? Then why envy him at all? And you who despise him — how do you know that your own austere practice will not, in a future life, make you a person of exactly his kind of rank and office? Then how could you despise him?

We have none of us yet escaped the cycle of birth and death; rich and poor, noble and base, take turns, one after another, like the wheel that draws water from a well, endlessly rising and falling in relation to one another. Thinking this through, how can one's heart not grow chilled with apprehension? One should simply apply oneself diligently to prior cultivation, not wasting a single inch of shadow, hoping to transcend the world entirely — how could there be any leisure left over for envying or despising other people?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"Rútóng Púsà" (儒童菩薩, the "child-like bodhisattva") reflects a popular but doctrinally unofficial tradition — cited from a text the Huazang commentary itself flags as apocryphal — identifying Confucius as a bodhisattva sent to China ahead of Buddhism's arrival; Zhuhong's endorsement of the idea here is a striking, if qualified, gesture of respect toward Confucius specifically for his handling of "stillness and response." 來生一 turns out to be another instance of Zhuhong's confrontational teaching method already seen in entry 18 — building a general argument, then revealing it describes the very person he is speaking to.

50. 棄捨所長 — Setting Aside One's Own Talent

A short closing reflection on how rarely people redirect a genuine natural gift toward spiritual practice instead of worldly accomplishment.

Chinese Source

凡人資性所長，必著之不能捨。如長於詩文者，長於政事者，長於貨殖者，長於戰陣者，乃至長於書者、畫者、琴者、棋者，皆弊精、竭神、殫智、盡巧以從事；而多有鉤深窮玄，成一家之名以垂世不朽。若能棄捨不用，轉此一回精神智巧，抵在般若上，何患道業之無成乎？而茫茫古今，千百人中，未見一二矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever natural gift a person happens to have, he inevitably clings to it and cannot let it go. Someone gifted in poetry and prose, someone gifted in statecraft, someone gifted in commerce, someone gifted in the arts of war, or even someone gifted in calligraphy, painting, the qin, or the game of go — all such people exhaust their spirit, drain their vitality, and use up every ounce of their intelligence and skill in pursuit of that gift; and not a few of them plumb its depths and its subtleties to the very bottom, founding a school of their own that endures, unforgotten, for generations. If such a person could set this gift aside and refuse to use it, and instead turn that same spirit and intelligence and skill toward prajñā, why should he ever worry about failing to accomplish the path? And yet, across the vast expanse of history, among a thousand such people, one scarcely ever finds even one or two who actually do this.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A fitting close to this batch: the argument echoes entry 22's ranked taxonomy of human delights, but shifts the frame from pleasure to talent — the harder case, since a genuine gift usually comes with real social reward attached, making it far more difficult to set aside than a mere indulgence.
