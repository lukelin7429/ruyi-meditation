Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — First Collection, Part 1

The opening quarter of the First Collection (初筆) of Zhuchuang Suibi, the notebook of the Ming-dynasty Pure Land master Yunqi Zhuhong (雲棲袾宏, 1535–1615), rendered here in the collection's own order rather than curated by theme. Several entries in the source text run two or three short pieces together under a single title — a habit of the original that this translation preserves, using sub-headings rather than inventing separate numbers for them.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

1. 僧無為 — The Monk Wúwéi

A short, personal anecdote about a monk who insisted on becoming Zhuhong's disciple despite being older and — in the ordinary way of reckoning seniority in a monastic community — his senior in every respect.

Chinese Source

吳江流慶菴無為能公，齒先予，德先予，出家先予；予蚤歲遊蘇湖間，與同堂坐禪。及予住雲棲，公來受戒，求列名弟子。予謝不允，則固請曰：「昔普慧、普賢二大菩薩尚求入匡廬蓮社，我何人斯，自絕佳會。」不得已，如董蘿石謁新建故事，許之。以賢下愚，有古人風，筆之以勸後進。

Ru-Yi Translation

The elder Wúwéi Néng of Liuqing Hermitage in Wujiang was my senior in age, my senior in virtue, and my senior in having left the household life. In my early years I traveled about Suzhou and the Lake Tai region, and sat together with him in meditation, in the same hall. Later, when I had taken up residence at Yunqi, he came to receive the precepts and asked to be entered on the roll as my disciple. I declined and would not permit it, but he pressed his request all the harder, saying: "Long ago, even the two great bodhisattvas Puhui and Puxian sought to enter the Lotus Society of Kuanglu — who am I, that I should cut myself off from so fine a gathering?" Unable to refuse him, I did as Dǒng Luóshí once did in seeking discipleship under Xīnjiàn, and granted his request.

That a man of such standing should place himself below one he judged beneath him has the bearing of the ancients. I set it down here to encourage those who come after.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"The Lotus Society of Kuanglu" refers to the tradition founded on Mount Lu by Huiyuan in the fourth century, revived under the Song-dynasty Master Zongze — a gathering said to have drawn even the bodhisattvas Puhui and Puxian to seek entry. "Dǒng Luóshí seeking discipleship under Xīnjiàn" recalls the Ming poet Dǒng Yún, who at sixty-eight sought to study under Wang Yangming (enfeoffed Earl of Xīnjiàn), then only fifty-two; Yangming protested that no student should be older than his teacher, but Dǒng bowed to him regardless. Both stories concern rank inverted by devotion — the shape of Zhuhong's own situation with Wúwéi Néng.

2. 人命呼吸間 — A Life Lasts No Longer Than a Breath

One of the shortest entries in the collection: a dying monk's superstition about his own birthday, and the single day by which he missed his chance to die with a settled mind.

Chinese Source

一僧瘵疾經年，久憊枕席，眾知必死，而彼無死想，語之死，輒不懌。予使人直告：「令速治後事，一心正念。」彼謂男病忌生日前，過期當徐議之耳。本月十七日乃其始生，先一日奄忽。吁！人命在呼吸間，佛為無病人言之也。況垂死而不悟，悲夫！

Ru-Yi Translation

A monk had lain consumptive for years, long wasted upon his pillow. Everyone around him knew he was going to die; he alone entertained no thought of death, and to speak of dying in his presence was to displease him at once. I had someone tell him plainly: "You should quickly settle your affairs, and fix your mind single-mindedly on right mindfulness." He answered that for a sick man it is inauspicious to speak of such things before his birthday — once the day had passed, there would be time enough to discuss it. The seventeenth of that month was the day of his birth. He died suddenly the day before.

Alas — "a human life lasts no longer than a single breath." The Buddha spoke those words to a man who was not yet ill. How much more should they hold for one who lay dying, and still would not wake to them! How sorrowful.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"A human life lasts no longer than a single breath" is a direct citation of the Sutra in Forty-Two Sections, where the Buddha asks a series of monks how long a life lasts; only the one who answers "the space of a single breath" is told he has understood. The teaching was originally given to healthy monks as a spur to urgency — here it falls on a man already dying, who still managed to postpone the one conversation that mattered over a single day's superstition.

3. 古今著述 — Old Writings and New

A brief personal memory, set down as a question Zhuhong says he has come to see differently with time.

Chinese Source

予在家時，於友人錢啟東家，一道者因予語及出家，渠云：「不在出家，祇貴得明師耳。」予時未以為然。又一道者云：「玄門文字，須看上古聖賢所作，近代者多出臆見，不足信。」予時亦未以為然。今思二言皆有深意；雖未必盡然，而未必不然也。以例吾宗，亦復如是。因識之。

Ru-Yi Translation

When I was still a layman, I was once at the home of my friend Qián Qǐdōng when a practitioner, hearing me speak of my wish to leave the household life, said to me: "It is not a matter of leaving the household — what matters is finding an enlightened teacher." At the time I did not think much of what he said. Another practitioner once told me: "When it comes to profound writings, one should read only the works of the ancient sages; most recent writings come merely from private opinion, and are not to be trusted." At the time I did not think much of that either.

Now, thinking it over, both remarks have real depth to them — not entirely so in either case, but not entirely otherwise either. The same holds, by the same reasoning, for the tradition I myself follow. I record it here for that reason.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A short, self-questioning entry rather than an argument — worth reading alongside entries 12 and 19–20 below, where Zhuhong takes up the "ancients versus moderns" question again from different angles. His closing gesture, applying both remarks to "the tradition I myself follow" (吾宗, i.e. Pure Land practice), is characteristically indirect: he never states outright which teacher or which text he has in mind.

4. 儒釋和會 — On Reconciling Chan and Confucianism

A single title covering three short, related pieces in the source text: a general remark on the fashion for reconciling Chan sayings with Confucian classics, and two companion notes (unnumbered in the source except by repeating the word "Śūraṅgama") on a specific, sharper controversy — whether discarding all commentary and reading the bare sutra text is really the more authentic way to study.

儒釋和會 — On Reconciling Chan and Confucianism

Chinese Source

有聰明人，以禪宗與儒典和會，此不惟慧解圓融，亦引進諸淺識者，不復以儒謗釋，其意固甚美矣。雖然，據麤言細語，皆第一義，則誠然誠然；若按文析理，窮深極微，則翻成戲論，已入門者又不可不知也。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are clever people who reconcile the sayings of the Chan school with the Confucian classics. This is not merely a matter of fluent, rounded understanding — it also draws in those of shallow discernment, so that they no longer slander the Buddha-dharma out of Confucian prejudice. The intention behind it is certainly a fine one. And yet: taken as the teaching that "coarse words and refined words alike return to the ultimate truth," this is entirely so, entirely so. But if one goes on to parse the texts and press the comparison down to its finest and most exacting point, it turns into idle argument instead — and this is something anyone who has already entered the gate ought not fail to know.

楞嚴一 — Whose Śūraṅgama Is It?

Chinese Source

天如集楞嚴會解，或曰：「此天如之楞嚴，非釋迦之楞嚴也。」予謂此語雖是，而新學執此，遂欲盡廢古人註疏，則非也。即盡廢註疏，單存白文，獨不曰：「此釋迦之楞嚴，非自己之楞嚴」乎？則經可廢也，何況註疏！又不曰：「自己之楞嚴遍一切處」乎？則諸子百家，乃至樵歌牧唱，皆不可廢也，何況註疏！

Ru-Yi Translation

Tiānrú compiled the Combined Commentary on the Śūraṅgama, and someone said of it: "This is Tiānrú's Śūraṅgama, not Śākyamuni's Śūraṅgama." I would say that although the remark is correct, when students newly come to their studies seize on it as grounds to discard the commentaries of the ancients entirely, that is wrong. For suppose one discarded every commentary and kept only the bare sutra text — could one not just as well say, "This is Śākyamuni's Śūraṅgama, not one's own Śūraṅgama"? Then the sutra itself could be discarded — how much more the commentaries! Or again, could one not say, "One's own Śūraṅgama pervades every place"? Then the hundred schools of philosophy, down to the woodcutter's song and the cowherd's tune, none of it could be discarded either — how much more the commentaries!

楞嚴二 — The Case for Keeping the Old Commentaries

Chinese Source

不獨楞嚴，近時於諸經大都不用註疏。夫不泥先入之言，而直究本文之旨，誠為有見；然因是成風，乃至逞其胸臆，冀勝古以為高，而曲解僻說者有矣！新學無知，反為所誤。且古人勝今人處極多，其不及者什一；今人不如古人處極多，其勝者百一。則孰若姑存之。喻如學藝者，必先遵師教以為繩矩，他時後日，神機妙手，超過其師，誰得而限之也？而何必汲汲於求勝也？而況乎終不出於古人之範圍也！

Ru-Yi Translation

Not only with the Śūraṅgama — in recent times, students of the sutras in general mostly make no use of commentary at all. Not clinging to received opinion, and going straight to investigate the meaning of the text itself, is indeed a position with real insight to it. But because this has become a fashion, some go so far as to indulge their own private conjecture, hoping merely to appear superior to the ancients, and end up twisting the meaning into eccentric readings! Students newly come to their studies, lacking real knowledge, are led astray by exactly this. And the truth is: the places where the ancients surpass people today are exceedingly many — what they fall short in is perhaps one part in ten; the places where people today fall short of the ancients are exceedingly many — what they surpass them in is perhaps one part in a hundred. Given that, would it not be better simply to preserve the old commentaries for now? It is like someone learning a craft: he must first follow his teacher's instruction as the standard measure; only later, once his own uncanny skill has developed, may he go on to surpass his teacher — and who, at that point, could hold him back? Why the urgent rush to surpass the ancients right away? All the more so since, in the end, one never actually goes beyond the bounds the ancients themselves set.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Tiānrú Wéizé (天如惟則), a Yuan-dynasty Chan monk in the lineage of Zhōngfēng Míngběn, compiled the standard nine-commentary anthology on the Śūraṅgama Sūtra that circulated for the following two centuries as essentially the only text students read. Zhuhong's argument in 楞嚴一 is a small masterclass in refusing a slogan on its own terms: he grants the objection to Tiānrú, then shows the identical reasoning, pushed further, would also require discarding the sutra itself, or else — pushed the other way — would require keeping everything, "down to the woodcutter's song." 楞嚴二 then broadens the same point into a general defense of reading with commentary rather than against it, closing with the craftsman's-apprentice analogy: follow the teacher's method first, and only surpass it once genuine mastery has actually been earned.

5. 禮懺功德 — The Merit of a Repentance Rite

A ghost story told to Zhuhong by a lay disciple, put to a specifically Buddhist use: an argument, by way of a striking case, for the real efficacy of repentance ritual.

Chinese Source

姑蘇曹魯川居士為予言：有女在夫家，夏坐室中，一蛇從牆上逐鴿，墮庭心，家人見而斃之。數日後，蛇附女作語。魯川往視，則云：「我昔為荊州守，高歡反，追我至江滸，遂死江中，我父母妻子不知安否？」魯川驚曰：「歡六朝時人，今歷隋唐宋元而至大明矣！」鬼方悟死久，並知為蛇。曰：「既作蛇，死亦無恨，但為我禮梁皇懺一部，吾行矣！」乃延泗洲寺僧定空禮懺。懺畢，索齋，為施斛食一壇。明日女安穩如故。懺之時義大矣哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

The layman Cáo Lǔchuān of Gusu [Suzhou] told me this: his daughter, living at her husband's family home, was sitting indoors one summer day when a snake, chasing a pigeon along the top of the wall, fell into the middle of the courtyard. The household saw it and killed it.

Some days later, the snake possessed the daughter and began to speak through her. Lǔchuān went to see her, and the voice said: "I was once the governor of Jingzhou. When Gao Huan rebelled, he pursued me to the river's edge, and I died there in the water. I do not know whether my parents, my wife, and my children are safe."

Lǔchuān, astonished, said: "Gao Huan lived in the era of the Six Dynasties — since then we have passed through the Sui, the Tang, the Song, and the Yuan, and have now come to the great Ming!" Only then did the ghost realize how long it had been dead, and understand as well that it had since been reborn as a snake. It said: "Since I have already become a snake, I hold no grudge in dying a second time. Only perform for me a single recitation of the Emperor Liang Repentance, and I will go." So Lǔchuān engaged the monk Dìngkōng of Sizhou Temple to perform the rite. When it was complete, the spirit asked for a feast as well, and so an additional platform of food was offered for the hungry ghosts on its behalf. The next day the daughter was calm and well again, just as before.

How great, indeed, is the power of repentance ritual, rightly performed!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The "Emperor Liang Repentance" (梁皇懺) was compiled at the order of Emperor Wu of Liang after his consort appeared to him in a dream as a python — so a snake spirit specifically requesting this rite, rather than repentance in general, is not incidental to the story. The joke embedded in Cáo Lǔchuān's astonished reply is the real hinge: a man who died in the wars at the end of the Six Dynasties has no idea that five more dynasties have since come and gone, meaning he had already been reborn as a snake, lived out a snake's life, and been killed as a snake, all without any memory of the far longer human death that preceded it.

6. 螯蠣充口 — Crabs, Clams, and the Question of Feeling

An argument against a specific historical excuse for eating shellfish — that creatures without visible eyes or mouths must lack awareness, and so may be eaten freely.

Chinese Source

晉何胤謂：「蟹就死，猶有知而可憫；至於車螯蚶蠣，眉目內缺，唇吻外緘，不榮不瘁，草木弗若，無聲無臭，瓦礫何異？固宜長充庖廚，永為口食。」噫！是何言歟此等雖無眉目唇吻、榮瘁聲臭，寧無形質運動乎？有形質而能運動者，皆有知也。汝不知其有知耳？況眉目等實無不具，特至微細，非凡目所見，而欲永為口食，胤之罪上通於天矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

He Yìn of the Jin dynasty said: "When a crab is put to death, it can still be pitied, since it plainly has awareness. But as for creatures like the giant clam, the ark shell, and the oyster — their eyes and brows lie folded away within, their lips and mouths sealed shut without; they neither flourish nor wither, not even the equal of grass and trees; soundless, scentless — how are they any different from a broken tile, a bit of gravel? Surely they may rightly be kept always in the kitchen, food for the mouth forever."

Ah — what manner of talk is this! Though such creatures have no visible eyes or brows, no lips or mouth, though they seem neither to flourish nor wither, to make no sound or give off no scent — can they truly be without physical form, without any capacity for movement? Whatever has physical form and is capable of movement has awareness. It is only that you fail to recognize the awareness that is there. And in truth, eyes and brows and the rest are not actually absent in them at all — they are merely so minute that ordinary eyes cannot make them out. To wish such creatures kept forever as food for the mouth — He Yìn's offense reaches up to Heaven itself!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

He Yìn (何胤, 5th–6th c.) was a scholar-official sincerely devoted to Buddhism yet unable to give up meat entirely. The commentary tradition records the fuller anecdote: pressed about a craving for fresh shellfish, He Yìn put the question to his students, and one supplied him exactly the rationalization Zhuhong quotes and demolishes here. One character in the source text — naming a second creature paired with "crab" before "the giant clam, the ark shell, and the oyster" — is missing from the digitized source consulted for this translation, a transcription gap rather than a deliberate omission; He Yìn's argument concerns the whole class of shelled creatures regardless.

7. 東門黃犬 — The Yellow Dog at the Eastern Gate

A short historical exemplum about the Qin-dynasty minister Li Si, given a specifically Buddhist reading. In the source text this entry's own title has been lost to a transcription error — the passage runs on directly from the previous entry, and a fragment of its final clause ("...李斯之父子歟") was mistaken for a heading. The title used here, and the placement of the text as its own entry, follow the piece's traditional title in the printed Zhuchuang Suibi.

Chinese Source

李斯臨刑，顧其子曰：「吾欲與汝復牽黃犬、臂蒼鷹，出上蔡東門逐狡兔，其可得乎？」遂父子相哭，而夷三族。斯蓋悔今之富貴而死，不若昔之貧賤而生也。寧思兔逢鷹犬，不猶己之罹斧鉞乎？兔滅群，汝夷族，適相當耳。不知其罪而反羨之，至死不悟者，李斯之父子歟。

Ru-Yi Translation

As Li Si went to his execution, he turned to his son and said: "I wish I could once more lead the yellow dog with you, carry the grey hawk on my arm, and go out through the eastern gate of Shangcai to chase the cunning hare — could such a day ever come again?" Father and son wept together, and then the punishment fell on three whole clans of their kin. What Li Si regretted, in the end, was having lived to see today's wealth and rank only to die for it — better, he felt, to have lived on poor and lowly as before. But did he never once consider that the hares his hawks and hounds once hunted down felt exactly the same terror he now felt at his own execution? He wiped out whole packs of them; his own three clans were wiped out in turn — the reckoning fits exactly. Not to recognize the offense in that, and to long instead for the very days when he committed it, dying still without having woken to it — such, perhaps, were both Li Si and his son.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The original anecdote, from the Records of the Grand Historian, is a purely secular exemplum about the vanity of official ambition — Li Si, on his way to execution under the Second Emperor of Qin, wishes only to return to the simple hunting life he gave up for a career at court. The equation of "hares hunted by hawks and hounds" with "Li Si executed by the state" is Zhuhong's own addition: a Buddhist reading of karmic symmetry laid over a Confucian-historiographical source.

8. 為父母殺生 — Killing on a Parent's Behalf

A possession narrative used to make a point about a common funerary custom: slaughtering animals to prepare a feast at a parent's grave.

Chinese Source

錢塘金某者，齋戒虔篤。以疾卒，附一童子云：「善業日淺，未得往生淨土，今在陰界，然亦甚樂，去住自由。」一日呵其妻子云：「何故為吾墳墓事，殺雞為黍？今有吏隨我，稍不似前之自由矣！」子婦懷妊，因問之。則曰：「當生男無恙。過此復當生男，則母子雙逝。」予謹記之，以候應否。俄而生男。復妊，復生男，男隨斃，母亦隨斃。乃知一一語皆不謬。然則為父母殺生，孝子豈為之乎！

Ru-Yi Translation

A certain Mr. Jīn of Qiantang was sincere and devoted in keeping the fast and precepts. He died of illness, and afterward possessed a child in the household, speaking through him: "My store of good karma was still shallow, and I have not been able to be reborn in the Pure Land. I am now in the realm of the dead — though it is quite pleasant here, and I come and go as I please." One day, still speaking through the child, he scolded his wife and children: "Why, over the matter of my grave, did you kill chickens and prepare a feast? Now I have an officer of the underworld assigned to follow me, and I am no longer quite as free as before!" His daughter-in-law was pregnant at the time, and the family asked him about it. He said: "This child will be a boy, and mother and son will both be well. The child after this one will also be a boy — but then both mother and child will die."

I made careful note of what he said, to see whether it would come true. Before long, a boy was born. She conceived again, and again bore a boy — and this time the boy died shortly after birth, and the mother died along with him. So it turned out that every word he had spoken was accurate.

If this is so, then killing living beings on behalf of one's parents — how could a filial child possibly do such a thing!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The argument works by a kind of documented prediction: Zhuhong is explicit ("I made careful note of what he said, to see whether it would come true") that he is reporting a case he tracked in real time, not a story received secondhand after the fact — the same rhetorical move as the following entry.

9. 鹿祀求名 — A Deer Promised for a Name on the List

A brief cautionary anecdote about a scholar who bargained with a god for examination success.

Chinese Source

士人有學成而久滯黌校者，禱於文昌：「設遂鄉科，當殺鹿以祀。」俄而中式。既酬願已，上春官，復許雙鹿，未及第而卒。噫！殺彼鹿，求己祿，於汝安乎？

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a scholar who had completed his studies but remained stalled for years at the provincial academy. He prayed to Wenchang [the god of literary destiny]: "If I succeed in the provincial examination, I will kill a deer in your honor." Before long he passed. Having fulfilled his vow, he went on to the metropolitan examination at the Board of Rites, and this time promised two deer — but he died before ever passing it.

Ah! Killing that deer to seek your own official salary and rank — could your heart truly rest easy with that?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Wenchang (文昌帝君) is the deity of literary destiny and examination success in the Chinese popular pantheon, distinct from any figure in the Buddhist canon; Zhuhong's closing question is addressed as much to the scholar's own conscience as to the god he prayed to.

10. 心喻 — An Analogy for the Mind

A short doctrinal note explaining, and then limiting, the classical "mind as mirror" analogy.

Chinese Source

心無可為喻，凡喻心者，不得已而權為彷彿，非真也。試舉一二：如喻心以鏡。蓋謂鏡能照物，而物未來時，鏡無將迎；物方對時，鏡無憎愛；物既去時，鏡無留滯。聖人之心，常寂常照，三際空寂，故喻如鏡。然取略似而已，究極而論，鏡實無知。心果若是之無知乎，則冥然不靈，何以云妙明真體？或喻寶珠，或喻虛空，種種之喻亦復如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

The mind admits of no true analogy. Whenever the mind is compared to something, it is only for lack of a better way — a rough approximation offered out of necessity, not the real thing. Take the analogy of the mind as a mirror: a mirror can reflect what stands before it — when the object has not yet arrived, the mirror does not go out to meet it or send it away in advance; while the object stands before it, the mirror feels neither fondness nor aversion; once the object has departed, the mirror retains no trace of it. The mind of a sage is likewise always still and always illuminating, empty and quiescent with respect to past, present, and future alike — and so it is compared to a mirror.

But this is only taking a rough resemblance. Pressed to its ultimate conclusion, a mirror in fact has no awareness at all. If the mind, too, were truly without awareness in just this way, it would be a mere blank, without any spark of life to it — how then could it be called the wondrously bright, true substance [of enlightenment]? The same holds for comparing the mind to a wish-fulfilling jewel, or to empty space, or to any of the other such analogies.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A caution against over-literalizing a very common figure of speech in Chan and Huayan writing (the mirror-mind analogy traces back to the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's language of "stillness and illumination"): the analogy captures the mind's non-attachment, but not its capacity for awareness — a mirror, unlike mind, is simply inert.

11. 換骨 — Changing One's Bones

A one-line borrowing from a Song-dynasty poet, repurposed as an encouragement to practice.

Chinese Source

陳後山云：「學詩如學仙，時至骨自換。」予亦云：「學禪如學仙，時至骨自換。」故學者不患禪之不成，但患時之不至；不患時之不至，但患學之不勤。

Ru-Yi Translation

Chen Houshan said: "Studying poetry is like studying to become an immortal — when the time comes, the very bones change of themselves." I would say the same of Chan: studying Chan is like studying to become an immortal — when the time comes, the very bones change of themselves. And so a student need not worry that Chan will fail to be accomplished — only that the time has not yet come; and need not worry that the time has not yet come — only that one's own practice has not yet been diligent enough.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Chen Houshan (Chen Shidao, 1053–1102) was a Northern Song poet of the Jiangxi school; "changing one's bones" (換骨) is originally Daoist alchemical language for the transformation of a mortal body into an immortal one, borrowed here — through Chen's poetic borrowing of it first — as a figure for the gradual, unforced ripening of practice.

12. 洪州不得珠體 — Guifeng's "Only Heze Grasped the Pearl Itself"

A piece of internal Chan-lineage criticism, disagreeing with the great Huayan and Chan scholar Guifeng Zongmi's ranking of two rival schools descended from the Sixth Patriarch.

Chinese Source

洪州者，馬大師也。圭峰敘：「如來傳法迦葉而至曹溪，曹溪之道，惟荷澤為正傳，諸宗皆屬旁出，如摩尼珠，唯荷澤獨得珠體。」其說析理極精，而品人不當。夫馬祖親承南嶽，南嶽親承曹溪，自後百丈、黃檗、臨濟、南泉、趙州，不可勝數諸大尊宿，皆從馬祖而出，而獨推荷澤，何以服天下？圭峰以荷澤表出「知」之一字為心，而諸宗於作用處指示，遂謂是徒得珠中之影。然古人為人解黏去縛，隨時逐機，原無定法。其言知者，正說也。其言作用處者，巧說也。巧者何？欲人因影而知現影者誰也。如執「知」之一字，則世尊拈花，曾無知字，將世尊不及荷澤耶？況諸宗直出知字處亦不少，豈專說作用耶？圭峰平日見地極高，予所深服，獨此不滿人意。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Hongzhou" refers to Great Master Ma [Mazu Daoyi]. Guifeng [Zongmi] wrote: "The Tathāgata transmitted the Dharma to Kāśyapa, and it passed down in turn to Caoxi [the Sixth Patriarch]. Of Caoxi's teaching, only Heze [Shenhui] received the correct transmission; every other school branches off to the side — like a wish-fulfilling pearl, of which Heze alone grasped the pearl itself." His analysis is extremely refined, but his judgment of the people involved is not right. Mazu received the teaching directly from Nanyue, and Nanyue received it directly from Caoxi; and afterward Baizhang, Huangbo, Linji, Nanquan, Zhaozhou, and countless other great venerable masters all came forth from Mazu's line — yet Guifeng singles out Heze alone for honor. How could this possibly satisfy the whole world?

Guifeng holds that Heze alone brought forth the single word "awareness" as [the essence of] mind, while the other schools only point to mind at the level of its functioning — and on this basis says the others merely grasped the shadow within the pearl, not the pearl itself. But the ancients, in loosening a person's attachments, always adapted to the moment and the capacity of the person before them; there was never any single fixed method. To speak of "awareness" was the direct teaching. To point to mind at the level of its functioning was the skillful teaching. And what made it skillful? The wish to let a person come to know, by way of the shadow, who it is that casts the shadow. If one clings fixedly to the single word "awareness," then consider: when the World-Honored One held up the flower, not a single word about "awareness" was spoken at all — does that mean the World-Honored One falls short of Heze? And besides, the other schools, too, point directly to this same "awareness" in no small number of places — how could they be said to speak only of functioning? Guifeng's insight is, in general, extremely deep, and I hold him in the greatest regard — it is only on this one point that I cannot be satisfied.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Guifeng Zongmi (780–841), fifth patriarch of the Huayan school and an heir of the Heze line, ranked the Chan schools in his own writings with Heze Shenhui's teaching of "awareness" (知) as the sole correct transmission and Mazu Daoyi's much larger and more influential Hongzhou school as merely derivative. Zhuhong does not dispute Zongmi's doctrinal analysis of the word "awareness" itself — he disputes the historical and institutional judgment built on it, pointing out that nearly the entire later Chan tradition (Baizhang, Linji, Zhaozhou, and so on) descends from the "lesser" line Zongmi demoted.

13. 墳墓 — My Grave

A short personal reflection on his own approaching death and burial.

Chinese Source

予既老病，眾為擇地作塔，數易之。予嘆曰：「世人極意營圖風水，冀子孫長永富貴耳。爾輩望蔭出紫衣國師耶？古人有言：『棄諸林莽以飼禽獸。』幸不置我於鴉腸狐腹足矣，餘非道人所知也。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Now that I am old and unwell, the community has been choosing a site to build my funerary stūpa, and has changed the site several times already. I said with a sigh: "People in the world exhaust themselves scheming over feng shui, hoping their descendants will enjoy wealth and rank for generations to come. Do you, too, hope that this burial site's good fortune will produce some future court-titled abbot in purple robes? The ancients had a saying: 'Cast the body out among the woods and grasses, to feed the birds and beasts.' I would be content enough simply not to end up in the belly of a crow or a fox — anything beyond that is no concern of a person of the Way."

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"Purple robes" and the title "state preceptor" (國師) were honors the imperial court bestowed on eminent monks; Zhuhong's point is that the community's careful feng shui site-selection for his stūpa unconsciously mimics lay ancestor-worship practice — seeking a burial site that will bring descendants worldly fortune — which he pointedly refuses for himself.

14. 菩薩度生 — Bodhisattvas and Saving the Living

A short doctrinal correction of a common misreading of a well-known line.

Chinese Source

經言：「菩薩未能自度，先能度人。」愚夫遂謂菩薩但度眾生，不復度己。不知己亦眾生數也，焉有度盡眾生，而獨遺自己一眾生乎？何得藉口菩薩，逐外忘內！

Ru-Yi Translation

A sutra says: "Before a bodhisattva has managed to save himself, he first saves others." Foolish people then conclude that a bodhisattva simply saves sentient beings and need not save himself at all. What they fail to realize is that he, too, is counted among sentient beings — how could one save every sentient being and yet leave out precisely one, namely oneself? How can people use "bodhisattva" as an excuse to chase after the external and forget the internal!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The cited line is a paraphrase of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's definition of bodhisattva resolve ("自未得度，先度人者，菩薩發心"). Zhuhong's point is narrow and precise: the sutra describes the order of a bodhisattva's aspiration, not a license to neglect one's own cultivation in favor of outward activity.

15. 悟後 — After Awakening

A citation from a Tang-dynasty Chan master used to correct a complacent view of what awakening actually accomplishes.

Chinese Source

溈山和尚云：「如今初心，雖從緣得一念頓悟自理，猶有無始曠劫習氣未能頓淨，須教渠淨除現業流識，即是修也，不道別有法教渠修行趨向。」溈山此語，非徹法源底者不能道。今稍有省覺，便謂一生參學事畢者，獨何歟？

Ru-Yi Translation

Master Guishan said: "A student newly come to the mind, though he may through some causal condition attain in a single thought a sudden awakening to his own true principle, still carries habitual tendencies accumulated over beginningless, boundless kalpas, which cannot be purified all at once. He must be taught to cleanse away, in the flow of his present karmic consciousness, what remains — this and nothing else is what cultivation means; there is no other separate method to teach him where to direct his practice."

Words like these could not be spoken by anyone but one who has plumbed the very source of the Dharma to its bottom. Why is it, then, that people today, the moment they have some small awakening, immediately declare the whole work of a lifetime's study and practice finished?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Guishan Lingyou (771–853), founder of the Guiyang school, is cited here for the classic Chan doctrine of "sudden awakening, gradual cultivation" (頓悟漸修) — that an initial breakthrough does not by itself dissolve the accumulated habitual tendencies of countless past lives, which still require ongoing practice to clear. Zhuhong's closing question targets a complacency he returns to elsewhere in the collection (compare entry 23 below).

16. 孚遂二座主 — The Two Lecture-Masters Fú and Liángsuì

Two short, paired Chan awakening stories, both turning on the same point: a scholar-lecturer humbling himself before someone of lower standing.

Chinese Source

太原孚上座，於揚州孝先寺講涅槃經，廣談法身妙理，有禪者失笑。孚講罷，請禪者茶，白云：「某甲狹劣，依文解義，適蒙見笑，且望教誨。」禪者云：「不道座主所說不是，然只說得法身量邊事，實未識法身在。」孚曰：「既如是，當為我說。」曰：「座主還信否？」曰：「焉敢不信！」曰：「請座主輟講旬日，端然靜坐，收心攝念，善惡諸緣一時放卻。」孚一依所教，從初夜至五更，聞角聲，忽大悟。又良遂座主參麻谷，谷荷鋤入園，不顧，便歸方丈閉卻門。次日復求見，又閉卻門，遂乃敲門。谷問是誰？遂方稱名，忽大悟。此二尊宿，祇緣是虛心下賢，不存我慢故。今人自高，焉得有此？

Ru-Yi Translation

Fú, a senior lecturer of Taiyuan, was expounding the Nirvana Sūtra at Xiaoxian Monastery in Yangzhou, speaking at length on the profound principle of the Dharma-body, when a Chan practitioner in the audience could not help laughing. When the lecture ended, Fú invited the practitioner for tea and said, deferentially: "I am shallow and limited — I only explain the text according to its words. Just now you were moved to laugh at me; I hope you will instruct me."

The practitioner said: "It is not that what you said, Master, was wrong — only that you spoke merely of the Dharma-body's dimensions, without truly knowing the Dharma-body itself." Fú said: "If that is so, please explain it for me." The practitioner asked: "Do you truly trust me, Master?" "How could I dare not to?" "Then set aside your lecturing for ten days. Sit upright and still, gather in your mind, and let go all at once of every condition, good and bad alike." Fú did exactly as he was told. From the first watch of the night until the fifth, he heard the sound of a horn — and suddenly attained great awakening.

There is also the case of Lecture-Master Liángsuì, who went to study under Magu. Magu, seeing him approach, shouldered his hoe and went into the garden without so much as a glance at him, then returned to his own quarters and shut the door. The next day Liángsuì came again seeking an audience, and again the door was shut in his face; so he knocked. Magu asked: "Who is it?" The moment Liángsuì gave his own name in reply, he suddenly attained great awakening.

These two venerable masters achieved what they did purely because they emptied their minds and placed themselves below the worthy, harboring no trace of self-conceit. How could people today, so full of their own superiority, ever reach such an awakening?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Both figures were accomplished sutra-lecturers (座主) — a distinct, text-based track in Tang-dynasty Buddhism from the practice-based Chan schools — and both stories turn on the same humbling: an established scholar submitting to a teaching method (silent sitting, a shut door) that offered him no verbal doctrine at all. The closing moral, "emptied their minds and placed themselves below the worthy," directly echoes entry 1 above.

17. 實悟 — Genuine Awakening

A citation from the Song-dynasty master Dahui Zonggao, warning against mistaking clever Chan-style speech for actual realization.

Chinese Source

妙喜云：「若是乾屎橛如是說得落時，如鋸解稱鎚、麻三斤、狗子佛性等，皆可如是說得。既不可如是說，須是悟始得。你若實得悟，師家故言不是，亦招因果不小。」學者當切記妙喜此語，息卻口頭三昧而求實悟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Master Miàoxǐ [Dahui Zonggao] said: "If a student can bring off a phrase like 'a dried shit-stick' the right way, then things like 'a saw cuts through the weight of a steelyard,' 'three catties of flax,' or 'does a dog have Buddha-nature' can all be brought off the same way. But it will not do simply to say such things by rote — one must actually be awakened before it counts. And if you are genuinely awakened, and your own teacher deliberately tells you that you are wrong, that teacher, too, will incur no small karmic consequence for it."

Students should hold fast to these words of Master Miàoxǐ, set aside mere clever talk, and seek genuine awakening instead.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The four phrases named are all classic Chan gōng'àn (公案): "a dried shit-stick," "a saw cuts through a steelyard's weight," "three catties of flax," and "does a dog have Buddha-nature" (Zhaozhou's famous "wú" 無). Dahui Zonggao (1089–1163) is the master most associated with "kànhuà chán" — meditation focused on a single such phrase held as a live question rather than answered discursively; his warning here is against students who learn to repeat a master's paradoxical answers without the realization behind them.

18. 出家父母反拜 — Do Parents Bow Back to a Child Who Has Left Home?

A defense of Zhuhong's own earlier scholarly ruling, against an indignant monk who cited a sutra passage against him.

Chinese Source

予作正訛集，謂反者還也，在家父母不受出家子拜，而還其禮，非反拜其子也。一僧忿然曰：「法華經言，大通智勝如來既成佛已，其父輪王向之頂禮，是反拜其子，佛有明訓，因刻之經末。」予合掌云：「汝號甚麼如來？」僧謝不敢。又問：「汝既未是如來，垂成正覺否？」僧又謝不敢。予謂曰：「既不敢，且待汝垂成正覺，更端坐十劫，實受大通如來位，納父母拜未晚。汝今是僧，未是佛也。佛為僧立法，不為佛立法也。且世人謗佛無父無君，吾為此懼，正其訛謬，息世譏嫌，冀正法久住，汝何為不畏口業，甘心乎師子蟲也？」悲夫！

Ru-Yi Translation

In my Correcting Errors, I explained that the fǎn (反) in fǎn bài (反拜) means "to return," not "to reverse": when a child who has left the household bows to his parents, the parents, unwilling to accept the bow of a monastic child, return the courtesy — it does not mean the parents bow back to the child in reverse. A monk, indignant at this, objected to me: "The Lotus Sūtra says that after the Tathāgata Mahābhijñā-jñānābhibhū became a buddha, his father, the Wheel-Turning King, bowed to him in worship — that is a father bowing in reverse to his son. The Buddha himself taught it plainly, and it is even carved into the closing pages of the sutra."

I put my palms together and asked him: "And what Tathāgata are you, may I ask?" The monk demurred, saying he would not presume to claim any such thing. I asked further: "Since you are not yet a Tathāgata, are you at least on the verge of perfect enlightenment?" Again he demurred. So I said to him: "Since you dare not claim even that, then wait until you have become a buddha, sit upright for ten more kalpas, and actually attain the rank of the Tathāgata Mahābhijñā-jñānābhibhū — it will not be too late then to accept your parents' bow. Right now you are a monk, not a buddha. The Buddha established rules for monastics; he did not establish rules for buddhas. And besides, the world already slanders the Buddha-dharma as having no regard for father or ruler — it is precisely out of fear of that charge that I wrote to correct such errors, hoping to put an end to the world's mockery and let the true Dharma endure. Why is it that you have no fear of the karma of speech, and are content to be a worm feeding on the lion's own body?" How sorrowful!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhuhong's own Correcting Errors (正訛集, 66 short entries correcting popular misconceptions among Buddhist clergy and laity) is the work being defended here. "A worm feeding on the lion's own body" is a stock Buddhist image — from the Sūtra on the Lotus-Faced One — for corruption from within the sangha, the point being that only the sangha's own members can destroy the Dharma, not outsiders; Zhuhong turns it back on the objecting monk himself.

19. 生愚死智 — Foolish in Life, Wise in Death

The main entry, on the excesses of eulogy-writing, followed by three short companion notes on a different subject that happen to share the same title-word pattern in the source — all three arguing, from different angles, that the Zhuangzi does not rival Buddhist scripture.

生愚死智 — Foolish in Life, Wise in Death

Chinese Source

洛陽伽藍記云：「史書皆非實錄，今人生愚死智，惑亦甚矣！」蓋言史多溢美，不足信也。但「皆非」二字，立言太過。古號史為直筆，則焉得非實？夫子言「文勝質則史」，則容有非實，當改「皆非」作「未必」耳。夫古人慎重許可，一語品題，芳播千古；而今乃視為故事，等為人情，虛諛浪褒，取笑識者，可嘆也。故洛陽記有激而發此論，切中末世之弊。不如是道破，傳燈錄前代真善知識，與今安排名姓插入祖圖者何辨？爾後為吾弟子，毋妄干名公大人，裝點吾之未到也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Record of the Monasteries of Luoyang says: "History books are none of them a true record — the people of our own day are fools in life and sages in death; how deeply confused this is!" What is meant is that history tends toward excessive praise, and so is not to be trusted. But the phrase "none of them" overstates the case. The ancients called the historian's brush the "straight brush" — how, then, could what it records be entirely untrue? Confucius said, "When ornament overcomes substance, you get an official chronicle" — which allows that some untruth may creep in, but the phrase should be softened from "none of them" to "not necessarily."

The ancients were careful and sparing in what praise they granted: a single line of judgment upon a person could carry that person's fame down a thousand years. People today, by contrast, treat such judgments as mere formality, doing favors for one another as a matter of course, praising extravagantly and without basis — which only earns them the mockery of anyone with real discernment. How lamentable! This is clearly what provoked the author of the Record of Luoyang to make his remark, and it strikes precisely at the failing of a degenerate age.

If one does not speak this plainly, then how is anyone to tell apart the genuinely accomplished teachers of past generations recorded in the Transmission of the Lamp from the people of today who arrange to have their own names inserted into the patriarchal lineage charts? Let it be known to my disciples hereafter: do not go seeking out eminent officials and great men to embellish, in my name, virtues I have not actually attained.

莊子一 — The Zhuangzi, First Note

Chinese Source

有俗士，聚諸年少沙彌講莊子，大言曰：「南華義勝首楞嚴。」一時緇流及居士輩無斥其非者。夫南華於世書誠為高妙，而謂勝楞嚴，何可笑之甚也！士固村學究，其品猥細不足較，其言亦無旨趣，不足辨，獨恐誤諸沙彌耳！然諸沙彌稍明敏者，久當自知；如言鍮勝黃金以誑小兒，小兒既長，必唾其面矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

A worldly scholar once gathered a group of young novice monks to lecture them on the Zhuangzi, and declared grandly: "The Nanhua [Zhuangzi] surpasses the Śūraṅgama in its meaning." Not one monastic or layman present objected to this. Now, the Nanhua is, among worldly books, genuinely lofty and subtle — but to say it surpasses the Śūraṅgama is laughable in the extreme! The man was no more than a village pedant, too petty and low in character to be worth arguing with, and his remark carried no real substance worth refuting either — I fear only for the young novices he might mislead. But those novices with any real intelligence will surely come to understand the truth in time; it is like telling a child that brass is better than gold — once that child grows up, he is bound to spit in the face of the one who deceived him.

莊子二 — The Zhuangzi, Second Note

Chinese Source

或曰：「莊子義則劣矣；其文玄曠疏逸，可喜可愕，佛經所未有也。諸為古文辭及舉子業者，咸靡然宗之。則何如？」曰：「佛經者，所謂至辭無文者也；而與世人較文，是陽春與百卉爭顏色也。置勿論。子欲論文，不有六經四子在乎？而大成於孔子，吾試喻之。孔子之文，正大而光明，日月也；彼南華，佳者如繁星掣電，劣者如野燒也。孔子之文，渟蓄而汪洋，河海也；彼南華，佳者如瀑泉驚濤，劣者如亂流也。孔子之文，融粹而溫潤，良玉也；彼南華，佳者如水晶琉璃，劣者如珉珂珷玞也。孔子之文，切近而精實，五穀也；彼南華，佳者如安南之荔、大宛之葡萄，劣者如未熟之梨與柿也。此其大較也。業文者宜何師也，而況乎為僧者之不以文為業也。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone said: "Granted, the Zhuangzi is inferior in its doctrine — but its prose is so vast and free-ranging, so capable of delighting and startling the reader, that Buddhist scripture has nothing like it. All the writers of classical prose and rhetoric, and all who prepare for the examinations, flock to it as their model. What do you make of that?"

I answered: "The sutras are the ultimate expression, with no [mere literary] embellishment at all — to set them beside worldly writing for comparison would be like matching the warm radiance of spring against the beauty of a hundred flowers; let us set that comparison aside. If you wish to discuss fine writing, are there not still the Six Classics and the Four Books? And it is Confucius who brings all these traditions to their fullest completion — let me offer a comparison using him. Confucius's writing is upright and luminous, like the sun and moon; the Nanhua, at its best, is like clustered stars and flashing lightning — brilliant but fleeting — and at its worst, like a wildfire burning without control. Confucius's writing is deep and containing, vast as a river flowing to the sea; the Nanhua, at its best, is like a waterfall or a startling wave, and at its worst, like a chaotic, disordered current. Confucius's writing is smoothly fused and gently warm, like fine jade; the Nanhua, at its best, is like crystal or glass, and at its worst, like inferior stone that only resembles jade. Confucius's writing is close at hand and substantial, like the five grains that sustain the people; the Nanhua, at its best, is like the lychee of Annam or the grapes of Dayuan, and at its worst, like an unripe pear or persimmon. This, roughly, is the comparison. Anyone devoted to writing should know, from this, which model to follow — how much more so a monk, for whom literary composition is not even supposed to be his proper occupation!"

莊子三 — The Zhuangzi, Third Note

Chinese Source

曰：「古尊宿疏經造論，有引莊子語者，何也？」曰：「震旦之書，周孔老莊為最矣。佛經來自五天，欲借此間語而發明，不是之引，而將誰引？然多用其言，不盡用其義，彷彿而已矣。蓋稍似而非真是也。南人之北，北人不知舟，指其車而曉之曰：『吾舟之載物而致遠，猶此方之車也。』借車明舟，而非以車為舟也。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked: "Why is it, then, that venerable masters of old, in their commentaries on the sutras and their treatises, sometimes quote the words of Zhuangzi?"

I answered: "Among the writings of this land, those of the Duke of Zhou, Confucius, Laozi, and Zhuangzi are the finest. The Buddhist sutras come from the Five Regions of India, and to make their meaning clear they must borrow the language of this land — if not from these, then from whose words could they possibly borrow? But for the most part only their language is used, not their full meaning — it is a matter of rough resemblance only, something that seems close but is not truly the same. It is like a southerner traveling north: the northerner does not know what a boat is, so the southerner points to a cart and explains: 'Our boats carry cargo across long distances, just as your carts do here.' He borrows the cart to explain the boat — he does not mean that the cart is a boat."

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Record of the Monasteries of Luoyang (洛陽伽藍記) is a sixth-century historical-geographical work by Yang Xuanzhi, not itself a Buddhist scripture — Zhuhong is quoting a secular historian to make a point about eulogy inflation, and closing with a warning to his own disciples not to seek grand patrons to inflate his own posthumous reputation. The three 莊子 notes that follow are a distinct sequence: together they argue, first, that a village lecturer's grandiose ranking of Zhuangzi above the Śūraṅgama is simply wrong; second, that even granting the Zhuangzi's literary brilliance, Confucius's prose is the better model and the sutras are not really commensurable with either; and third, that classical masters who do quote Zhuangzi in their commentaries are borrowing only its language, not endorsing its doctrine — the boat-and-cart analogy that closes the third note is Zhuhong's own.

20. 養老書 — A Book on Nourishing Old Age

A polemic against a popular manual of geriatric diet, followed by one further short reflection in the source text on how understanding is acquired — ear, eye, or mind — grouped here under the same entry as it appears without its own separate heading.

養老書 — A Book on Nourishing Old Age

Chinese Source

有集養老書，日用服食，多炮炙生物。至於曰雀、曰鴈、曰雉、曰鴛鴦、曰鹿、曰兔、曰駝、曰熊、曰猯，多豪貴少年所未及染指者。先德有言：「饒君善將息，難與死魔爭。」胡為老不息心，反勤殺害，誤天下老人並其子弟俱陷地獄者，是書也。孔子曰：「老者安之。」定不教渠殺生為安。孟子曰：「七十食肉。」亦定不教渠遍食眾生肉也。作俑者其思之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone compiled a book called Nourishing Old Age, on the everyday diet and clothing suited to old age — and much of it consists of recipes for roasting and grilling living creatures. It goes so far as to list sparrow, wild goose, pheasant, mandarin duck, deer, hare, camel, bear, and badger — creatures many wealthy young nobles themselves have never so much as tasted. A worthy of old once said: "However well you tend and rest your body, you still cannot out-argue the demon of death." Why, then, when already old, does one still fail to put the mind at rest, and instead labor all the harder at killing? The book that has misled old people across the world, and their children and grandchildren along with them, into the hells together — this is that very book.

Confucius said, "Let the old find peace." He surely did not mean that they should find that peace through killing. Mencius said, "One may eat meat at seventy." He surely did not mean that one should eat the flesh of every living creature indiscriminately. Let whoever first set this practice in motion give it careful thought.

心得 — A Reflection

Chinese Source

以耳聽受而得者，不如以目看讀而得者之廣也。以目看讀而得者，不如以心悟明而得者之極其廣也。以心為君、以目為臣、以耳為佐使，可也。用目當心，斯下矣。用耳當目，又下之下矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

What is gained through the ear by listening is not as broad as what is gained through the eye by reading. What is gained through the eye by reading is not nearly as broad as what is gained through the mind by direct realization. It is fine to let the mind serve as sovereign, the eye as minister, and the ear as attendant. To use the eye in place of the mind is already inferior. To use the ear in place of the eye is inferior to that, again.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Nourishing Old Age criticized here is very likely the Northern Song physician Chen Zhi's Yǎnglǎo Fèngqīn Shū (養老奉親書), a real and influential geriatric manual; Zhuhong's citations of Confucius and Mencius are deliberately turned against a reading of "七十食肉" ("at seventy one may eat meat," Mencius 1A.3) that had apparently been used to license extravagant meat consumption among the elderly, rather than the modest provision Mencius actually describes. The short closing "Reflection" is unrelated in subject but appears without its own heading in the source, immediately following.

21. 祀神不用牲 — Sacrificing to the Gods Without Animal Offerings

A first-person account of Zhuhong's own break, before he had even left the household life, with a local sacrificial custom.

Chinese Source

杭俗歲暮祀神，大則刲羊蒸豚，次則用豬首雞魚之屬。予未出家時，持不殺戒，乃易以蔬果；家人雖三尺童子無不愕然，以為必不可。予燃香秉燭，高聲白神云：「某甲奉戒不殺。殺生以祭，不惟某甲之過，亦非神之福。然此意某一人獨斷，其餘皆欲用牲，倘神不悅，凡有殃咎宜加予身；若濫無辜，非所謂聰明正直者。」家人猶為予危之。終歲合宅無恙，遂為例。

Ru-Yi Translation

According to Hangzhou custom, at the end of each year households make offerings to the gods — the grander households slaughter a sheep and steam a suckling pig, and lesser ones use a pig's head, chicken, and fish. Before I left the household life, I had already taken up the precept against killing, and so I changed the offering to vegetables and fruit instead. My own family — even the smallest children in the house — were startled, and were certain this could not be done.

I lit incense and candles, and announced in a loud voice to the gods: "I myself uphold the precept against killing. To offer sacrifice by killing would be a fault on my part, and would bring the gods no benefit either. This choice, however, is mine alone — everyone else in the household still wishes to use animal offerings. If the gods are displeased by this, let every misfortune fall on me alone; to let it fall on the innocent besides would hardly be the mark of a wise and upright god."

My family still worried for my sake. But by the end of that year the whole household remained safe and well, and from then on this became our custom.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Notable for its date: this is a layman's decision, made before Zhuhong's ordination, showing the precept against killing already governing his household practice well before he formally took monastic vows.

22. 好樂 — What People Delight In

A short taxonomy of human pleasures, arranged from coarsest to most refined, ending in the delight of a purified mind.

Chinese Source

人處世各有所好，亦各隨所好以度日而終老，但清濁不同耳。至濁者好財，其次好色，其次好飲。稍清，則或好古玩，或好琴棋，或好山水，或好吟詠。又進之，則好讀書。開卷有益，諸好之中，讀書為勝矣！然此猶世間法。又進之，則好讀內典。又進之，則好淨其心。好至於淨其心，而世出世間之好最勝矣！漸入佳境如食蔗喻。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every person in the world has his own delight, and lives out his life, to its very end, following that delight — only the refinement of the delight differs from person to person. At the coarsest level, one delights in wealth; next, in sensual pleasure; next, in drink. A little more refined, one delights in antiques and curios, or in the game of go and the qin, or in landscapes, or in composing verse. Higher still is delight in reading — opening a book always brings some benefit, and of all the delights, reading is the finest. But even this still belongs to worldly ways. Higher again is delight in reading the Buddhist scriptures. Higher still is delight in purifying one's own mind. When delight reaches the point of purifying the mind, it becomes the finest delight of all, whether within the world or beyond it — and one moves gradually into ever better territory, the way sugarcane grows sweeter toward the root, the further one chews.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The closing image, of sugarcane growing progressively sweeter as one chews toward the root, is a stock classical figure for gradual improvement (its earliest well-known use is a remark attributed to the painter Gu Kaizhi); Zhuhong uses it here to frame the whole ranked list as a single continuous path rather than separate categories of person.

23. 世智當悟 — Worldly Wisdom Must Still Be Awakened

A careful fourfold classification of wisdom, distinguishing mere cleverness from genuine insight on both the worldly and transcendent sides of the line.

Chinese Source

智有二：有世間智，有出世間智。世智又二：一者博學宏辭，長技遠略，但以多知多解而勝乎人者是也。二者明善惡、別邪正，行其所當行而止其所當止者是也。僅得其初，是謂狂智，當墮三塗。兼得其後，是謂正智，報在人天。何以故？德勝才謂之君子，才勝德謂之小人也。出世間智亦二：一者善能分別如來正法，四諦、六度等，依而奉行者是也。二者破無明惑，如實了了見自本心者是也。僅得其初，是出世間智也，名為漸入。兼得其後，是出世間上上智也，乃名頓超。何以故？但得本，不愁末。得末者，未必得本也。今有乍得世智初分，便謂大徹大悟者，何謬昧之甚！

Ru-Yi Translation

Wisdom is of two kinds: worldly wisdom and wisdom that transcends the world. Worldly wisdom is itself of two kinds. The first is broad learning, eloquence, outstanding skill, and far-reaching strategy — people of this kind excel over others simply through the range of what they know and understand. The second is the ability to distinguish good from evil and the crooked from the upright, doing what ought to be done and stopping short of what ought not to be done. To have only the first kind is what is called "wild wisdom," and it leads, in the end, straight down into the three lower realms. To have both together is called "right wisdom," and its karmic reward lies among humans and gods. Why is this so? Because someone whose virtue exceeds his talent is called a noble person, while someone whose talent exceeds his virtue is called a petty person.

Wisdom that transcends the world is likewise of two kinds. The first is the ability to skillfully discern the Tathāgata's true Dharma — the Four Noble Truths, the six perfections, and so on — and to practice according to it. The second is to break through fundamental delusion and, in full accordance with reality, see one's own original mind with total clarity. To have only the first is transcendent wisdom still, but it is called "gradual entry." To have both together is the highest transcendent wisdom, called "sudden transcendence." Why is this so? Because once you have grasped the root, you need not worry about the branches — whereas grasping the branches does not guarantee you have grasped the root. There are people today who, having only just glimpsed the first portion of worldly wisdom, immediately declare themselves greatly and thoroughly awakened — how deeply mistaken and confused this is!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry belongs with entry 15 above (悟後) as a matched pair on the same underlying worry: that a partial success — cleverness without ethical grounding on the worldly side, or textual understanding without direct realization on the transcendent side — is repeatedly mistaken for the whole. "Gradual entry" (漸入) and "sudden transcendence" (頓超) echo, without fully collapsing into, the sudden/gradual vocabulary already at issue in entry 12's Chan-lineage dispute.

24. 時不可蹉 — The Moment Must Not Be Let Slip

A short exhortation on the importance of early momentum in practice.

Chinese Source

凡人初出家，心必猛利，當趁此時，一氣做工夫，使有成立。若悠悠揚揚，蹉過此時，日後或住院，或受徒，或信施繁廣，多為所累，淪沒初志。修行人不可不知。

Ru-Yi Translation

When a person first leaves the household life, the resolve in his heart is invariably keen and forceful. He should take advantage of exactly this moment to apply himself single-mindedly to practice, so that something is actually accomplished. If instead he drifts along idly and lets this moment slip past, then later on — whether he comes to head a monastery, or takes on disciples of his own, or receives abundant offerings from the faithful — he will, more often than not, find himself burdened and encumbered by these very things, and his original resolve will sink and be lost. This is something a practitioner cannot afford not to know.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A brief, plainly stated piece of practical advice rather than doctrine — the same worry about early momentum lost to later institutional success (running a monastery, gathering disciples, receiving donations) that Zhuhong, as the reformer of what became one of the most influential monasteries of the late Ming, would have watched play out around him many times over.

25. 念佛鬼敬 — A Ghost's Reverence for Buddha-Recitation

A possession narrative offered as evidence for the power of even casual, untrained recitation of the Buddha's name.

Chinese Source

海昌村民某，有老媼死，附家人言平生事，及陰府報應甚悉，家人環而聽之。某在眾中忽攝心念佛，媼謂曰：「汝常如此，何患不成佛道？」問何故？曰：「汝心念阿彌陀佛故。」問何以知之？曰：「見汝身有光明故。」村民不識一字，瞥爾顧念，尚使鬼敬，況久修者乎？是故念佛功德不可思議。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a certain villager of Haichang whose elderly mother-in-law had died; her spirit possessed a member of the household and spoke of the events of her life, and of the retributions of the underworld, in great detail, while the family sat gathered around listening. In the middle of this, the villager himself suddenly gathered his mind and began silently reciting the Buddha's name. The old woman's spirit said to him: "If you keep on like this, why should you worry about failing to achieve buddhahood?" He asked why she said so. She said: "Because your mind is reciting Amitābha Buddha's name." He asked how she knew. She said: "Because I see light shining from your body."

This villager could not even read a single character, and had only, on a sudden impulse, turned his thoughts inward to recite the Buddha's name for a moment — and even this was enough to move a spirit to reverence. How much more so, then, for one who has cultivated this practice for a long time! This is why the merit of reciting the Buddha's name is truly inconceivable.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Structurally close to entries 5 and 6 above (possession narratives offered as evidence for a specifically Buddhist claim), and characteristic of Zhuhong's Pure Land emphasis in this collection: the argument is a fortiori — if even a single unlearned villager's momentary recitation produces a visible effect, sustained practice should be expected to accomplish far more.
