竹窗隨筆・三筆 · Entries 51–55

Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Third Collection, Part 12

Continuing the Third Collection (三筆). A paired set of interviews with monks who declined to vow rebirth in the West — one honestly craving worldly rank, two others claiming a lofty freedom from all direction — followed by a shocking account of a disciple's desecration of his own teacher's remains, a critique of a Daoist immortal's misunderstanding of arhatship, and a renewed call to treat the Buddha's own final testament with real seriousness.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: work in progress, entries 51–55 of 三筆 Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

51. 不願西方一 — Not Wishing for the West, First Note

A monk's blunt confession that his true ambition is worldly rank and domestic comfort, not rebirth in the Pure Land, prompts Zhuhong to trace out an escalating series of increasingly grim rebirths that could follow from failing to cultivate merit at all.

Chinese Source

或問一僧：「公願生西方否？」曰：「吾不願也。乃所願來生著綠袍，一妻一妾而處室也，此即吾之極樂國也。」問者嘿然。以告予，予謂人各有志，志在富貴，何西方之為？雖然，富貴雖非道人美事，而亦須修頑福以得之。儻不修福，未必得為綠袍郎，而或作綠衣人也；未必配淑女於名門，而或納六禮於齊人也。猶未也，儻有業焉，且不得為綠衣人，而或為金衣公子之流，事未可知也；且不得納禮於齊人，而或依棲於圉人、校人、庖人，事未可知也。猶未也，儻業重焉，金衣或變而為赤鍱焉，事未可知也；圉人、校人、庖人或變而為阿旁焉，事未可知也。悲夫！

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked a monk: "Do you wish to be reborn in the West?" He answered: "I do not. What I wish for instead is to wear the green robe of office in my next life, with a wife and a concubine to share my household — this is my own paradise." The questioner fell silent. He later told me of this, and I said: each person has his own aspirations; if his aspiration is wealth and rank, why would he wish for the West at all?

And yet, though wealth and rank are hardly a fitting goal for a practitioner of the Way, even these require the cultivation of ordinary worldly merit to obtain. Should he fail to cultivate such merit, he may not even become a minor official, but instead a mere servant in green livery; he may not marry a virtuous woman from a fine family, but instead take an ordinary commoner's daughter as his wife through the proper six rites.

Nor is even that certain. Should he have accumulated bad karma, he may not even qualify to become a servant, but instead fall among the ranks of some mere bird or beast — who can say? He may not even manage to marry a commoner's daughter, but instead find himself dwelling among the grooms, fish-keepers, and cooks who serve others — who can say?

Nor is even that certain. Should his karma be heavier still, that bird's golden plumage might transform instead into the red-hot copper sheeting of hell — who can say? Those grooms, fish-keepers, and cooks might transform instead into the very demons who torment the damned — who can say? How sorrowful!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The "golden-robed young lord" (金衣公子) is a genuine, poetic epithet for the oriole, recorded as a nickname Emperor Xuanzong of Tang used for the bird in his own palace garden — Zhuhong's escalating chain here, from human rank down through a songbird down to hell's own torments, mirrors the same structural device already used in this collection's own entries 39–40 on wasted time, building dread through incremental worsening rather than a single blunt statement.

52. 不願西方二 — Second Note

Two further monks answer the same question with lofty claims of freedom from any fixed direction — one following buddhas wherever they appear, the other refusing to move even for celestial guardians — and Zhuhong warns that such claims are empty unless genuinely earned.

Chinese Source

又問一僧：「公願生西方否？」曰：「吾不願，亦不不願。東方有佛，吾往東方，西方有佛，吾往西方；南北上下，亦復如是。吾何定於西方也？」又問一僧：「公願生西方否？」曰：「八金剛抬我過東方，吾不來；四天王抬我過西方，吾不去。吾何知所謂東西也？」合而觀之，前之一人，汩沒於五濁者也；此二人者，一則隨生，一則無生。雖然，曰隨生，未必其真能作主而不被業牽也；曰無生，未必其真得無生法忍而常住寂光也。如未能，則戲論而已。又未能，則大言不慚而已。難矣哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

Another monk was asked the same question: "Do you wish to be reborn in the West?" He answered: "I neither wish it, nor do I not wish it. If a buddha is in the east, I go east; if a buddha is in the west, I go west; and the same for south, north, above, and below. Why should I fix myself on the West alone?"

Yet another monk was asked: "Do you wish to be reborn in the West?" He answered: "Should the eight great vajra-guardians carry me toward the east, I would not go; should the four heavenly kings carry me toward the west, I would not go either. What, then, do I know of 'east' or 'west' at all?"

Considering these together: the first man was one sunk contentedly in the five turbidities; these two, one relies on rebirth wherever karma leads, the other claims to be beyond birth altogether. And yet, to say one "relies on rebirth wherever karma leads" does not necessarily mean one can truly remain master of oneself, unmoved by karmic pull; and to say one is "beyond birth" does not necessarily mean one has genuinely attained the forbearance of the unborn and dwells permanently in the light of quiescence. If one has not truly attained this, such words are nothing but empty talk. And if, beyond that, one still speaks this way without any real attainment at all, this is nothing but shameless boasting. How difficult this all is!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry directly answers the same lofty "a buddha in every direction" claim already examined at length in the Second Collection's own entry 117 ("隨處淨土"), where Zhuhong argued the freedom to go anywhere belongs only to the fully liberated, comparing a beginner to a fledgling bird that can only perch on its own branch; here he applies that same standard even more sharply, treating both monks' confident answers as unverified claims rather than settled proof of attainment.

53. 平侍者 — Attendant Ping

A shocking account of a longtime disciple who desecrated his own teacher's remains after claiming spiritual realization — his own violent death and posthumous fate offered as proof his attainment had never been genuine.

Chinese Source

平侍者久侍太陽，稱有悟入。奈何於後首創異議，徙太陽之塔，出其遺體，行破腦之慘毒，生報虎口，死入泥犁。則知其悟處，不過依稀見解、得少為足而已，何有真悟徹人而反作此大逆不道之事乎哉？淺解當悟，禍至此極，可戒也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Attendant Ping served Master Taiyang for a very long time, and claimed to have attained genuine realization. And yet later, he was the very first to raise objections against his own teacher, moving Taiyang's memorial pagoda, removing his remains, and subjecting his skull to a brutal act of desecration — and in this present life, his own retribution was to be devoured by a tiger, and after death, to fall into hell. From this we know that his own realization amounted to nothing more than a vague, approximate understanding, one he mistook for genuine attainment and rested content with — how could a truly, thoroughly awakened person ever commit such a monstrous, unfilial act as this? Mistaking shallow understanding for genuine awakening brought disaster to this very extreme — let this serve as a warning.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Master Taiyang (Jingxuan, 943–1027) was a genuine Song-dynasty Caodong-lineage master; the attendant Ping's later return to lay life, eventual desecration of his own former teacher's remains, and violent death by tiger attack are recorded in Buddhist biographical sources as a documented, named case — Zhuhong deploys it here as this collection's starkest possible illustration of the danger already warned against more gently in entries 20–21 above, where reckless, unearned confidence about one's own understanding was shown to carry real spiritual consequences.

54. 四果 — The Four Fruits

A precise doctrinal correction of the Daoist patriarch Zhang Boduan's claim that even arhats must "seize another body" to be reborn, arguing this fundamentally misunderstands the fourth fruit's complete freedom from further rebirth.

Chinese Source

紫陽真人謂四果人奪舍投胎，身有敗壞，不免離一舍入一舍。故其言曰：「若解降龍并伏虎，真金起屋幾時枯也。」夫初果七返生死，二果名一往來，猶可以胎論；三果已名不來；而四果則見思惑盡，不受後有，三明六通，號阿羅漢，又何用奪舍為？紫陽仙學超越倫類，《悟真》諸書多談理性，而為此言，似於內典未甚精究耳。噫！真金起屋，特不枯耳，寧思金不度火也與哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

The Perfected One Ziyang held that even those who have attained the four fruits of the śrāvaka path must, when their own body decays, seize another body and be reborn into it, passing from one dwelling to the next. And so he wrote: "If one masters subduing the dragon and taming the tiger, how could a house built of true gold ever wither away?" Yet the first fruit still requires seven more rounds of birth and death; the second fruit is called "one further return" — these, perhaps, could still be discussed in terms of rebirth into a womb. But the third fruit is already called "no further return"; and as for the fourth fruit, all delusions of view and thought are entirely exhausted, no further rebirth is received at all, and one possesses the three insights and six supernatural powers, bearing the title of arhat — what need, then, could there possibly be for seizing another body at all?

Ziyang's own attainment in the Daoist arts of transcendence was truly extraordinary, and his Understanding Reality and other writings speak extensively on matters of principle and nature — and yet this particular statement suggests he had never studied Buddhist scripture with real thoroughness. Ah! Even a house of true gold merely fails to decay — has he never considered that gold itself is not immune to fire?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhang Boduan (Ziyang) is the same Song-dynasty founder of Daoist Southern Lineage inner alchemy already discussed for his careful "closely resembles" comparison in the Second Collection's entry 123 above; here Zhuhong is considerably sharper — where the earlier entry credited Ziyang for his careful, qualified language, this one finds a genuine factual error in his understanding of the four fruits, correcting it point by point against the standard scriptural definitions of each stage.

55. 遺教經 — The Sutra of the Buddha's Final Teaching

A renewed, forceful call to treat the Buddha's own final testament with the same seriousness any family would give a parent's dying wishes, rebuking those who dismiss it as mere elementary reading.

Chinese Source

世人臨終，為言以示子孫，謂之遺囑，而子孫執之以作憑據，世守而不變者也；況三界大師，四生慈父，說法四十九年，最後之遺囑乎？為僧者，所當朝誦暮習，師授徒傳，終身奉之而不可一日廢忘者。乃等之以童蒙之書，置之閒處，不復論究，豈非如來之逆子，佛法之頑民也哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

When an ordinary person nears death, he leaves words behind to instruct his own descendants, calling this a testament — and his descendants hold fast to it as their own guide, honoring it faithfully across generations without ever changing it. How much more so, then, for the great teacher of the three realms, the compassionate father of all four kinds of birth, who taught the Dharma for forty-nine years, and whose very last testament this is! A monk ought to recite and study it morning and night, receiving it from his own teacher and passing it on to his own disciples, honoring it faithfully his whole life long, never once neglecting it even for a single day. And yet there are those who treat it as though it were a mere primer for children, setting it aside and never once seriously studying it — is this not exactly the conduct of a rebellious child of the Tathāgata, a stubborn, unreformable subject of the Buddha-dharma?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry directly returns to the same short text and the same argument already made in this collection's entry 24, and further echoes the treatment of the Sutra of Forty-Two Sections in the Second Collection's own entry 124 — Zhuhong's repeated insistence across all three collections that these two brief, foundational texts deserve lifelong study, not a beginner's quick dismissal, reflects a concern he clearly returned to more than once over the course of writing this project.
