Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Second Collection, Part 6

Continuing the Second Collection (二筆) in the source text's own order. This section closes with Zhuhong's own New Year's Eve reflection on treating each year's end as a rehearsal for death — a natural resting point for the year, and the last entry before the collection's numbering resumes with its most extended treatment of the Pure Land's difficulty of belief.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

87. 解禪偈 — Explaining Chan Through Verse

A careful, appreciative but firm critique of a famous Song statesman's popular verses equating Confucian virtue with Buddhist realization — useful as a remedy, Zhuhong argues, but dangerous if mistaken for literal doctrine.

Chinese Source

溫公作《解禪偈》，真學佛不明理者之龜鏡也。但其以「言行可法」為不壞身，「仁義不虧」為光明藏，特一時救病語，非覈實不易之論。夫謹言行、修仁義，在世間誠可貴重，然豈便是金剛不壞之身、神通大光明藏？何言之易也！又以君子坦蕩蕩為天堂，小人長戚戚為地獄，理則良然，而亦有執理失事之病。豈得謂愚癡即牛羊，凶暴即虎豹，此外更無真實披毛戴角之牛羊，利牙鋸爪之虎豹乎？吾恐世人見溫公辭致警妙，必大悅而深信，其流之弊，撥無因果，乃至世善自足，不復知有向上事；則此偈本以覺人，反以誤人，不可不闡。

Ru-Yi Translation

Lord Wen's Verses Explaining Chan may truly serve as a mirror for those who study Buddhism without understanding its principles. But his equating "words and conduct that could serve as a model for a hundred generations" with the Buddha's indestructible body, and "unwavering benevolence and righteousness" with the treasury of luminous wisdom, was only a remedy offered for a particular ill of his own time, not a settled, unshakeable truth. To be careful in word and deed, to cultivate benevolence and righteousness, is certainly valuable within the world — but how could this simply be equated with the vajra-indestructible body, the great treasury of spiritual light and supernatural power? How lightly he speaks of it! He also equates the noble man's broad and easy composure with heaven, and the petty man's constant anxious gloom with hell — this is reasonable enough in principle, but it commits the error of clinging to principle while losing sight of actual fact. Can it really be said that stupidity simply is being an ox or a sheep, and violence simply is being a tiger or a leopard, with no actual, literal ox or sheep covered in fur and horns, no actual tiger or leopard with sharp teeth and claws, existing beyond this metaphor at all? I fear that when people see how refined and striking Lord Wen's phrasing is, they will be delighted and believe it deeply — and the resulting harm may be that they come to deny cause and effect altogether, and even consider worldly goodness sufficient in itself, no longer aware that there is a matter still higher to pursue. Then these verses, originally meant to awaken people, would instead mislead them — this cannot go unexplained.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Lord Wen is Sima Guang (1019–1086), the great Song historian and statesman who led the conservative opposition to Wang Anshi's reforms (the same Wang Anshi treated less gently in entry 72 above); his Verses Explaining Chan were written specifically to address Song scholar-officials' fashionable but often superficial dabbling in Chan composition, making Zhuhong's critique here a case of one careful reader checking another's well-intentioned popularizing simplification against its likely misreading.

88. 范景仁 — Fan Jingren

A gentle but pointed skepticism toward a Song statesman's claim of twenty years without a single stray thought, measured against far more modest, hard-won claims from Confucius's own best student and a celebrated Chan master.

Chinese Source

景仁自謂：「吾二十年曾不起一思慮。」景仁之為賢者信矣，然二十年之久不生一念，或未易及此。顏子尚僅三月不違，則三月外容有念生；趙州尚假四十年方成一片，則未成一片時容有念生。如景仁者得無，麤念雖無，微細思慮潛滋暗發而不自覺歟！吾非輕視景仁，蓋恐得少為足，而預以自警也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Jingren said of himself: "For twenty years I have not once given rise to a single stray thought." That Jingren was a worthy man is beyond doubt — and yet to go twenty full years without a single stray thought arising is perhaps not so easily attained. Even Yan Hui could manage only three months without departing from benevolence — beyond those three months, a stray thought might well have arisen; even Zhaozhou needed forty years before his practice became "a single seamless whole" — before it became seamless, a stray thought might well have arisen too. Can it really be that a man like Jingren had none at all? Even if the coarse, obvious thoughts were absent, perhaps subtle, fine thoughts still quietly arose and stirred without his own awareness! I do not say this to belittle Jingren — I say it only because I fear settling for too little too soon myself, and offer this as a warning to myself in advance.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Fan Zhen (Jingren) was a real Song scholar-official, a close political and personal ally of Sima Guang who shared his opposition to Wang Anshi's reforms — placing this entry and the previous one, on Sima Guang's own verses, within the same circle of Song conservative statesmen Zhuhong repeatedly returns to across this collection. Zhaozhou's "forty years to become seamless" is his own recorded statement, already cited for its underlying discipline in entry 65's survey of well-earned titles above.

89. 習俗 — Custom

A wry catalogue of the passing fads that sweep through Buddhist practice communities, and a pointed question about why genuine, dedicated practice alone never seems to catch on the same way.

Chinese Source

先輩云：「習俗移人，賢智者不免。」今一衣一帽、一器一物、一字一語，種種所作所為，凡唱自一人，群起而隨之，謂之時尚。或尚坐關，群起而坐關；或尚禮懺，群起而禮懺；群起而背經，群起而持準提，群起而讀等韻，群起而去註疏、專白文，群起而齋十萬八千僧，群起而學書、學詩、學士大夫尺牘語，靡然成風，不約而合。獨於刻心勵志真實參禪念佛者，則有唱而無隨，謂之何哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

A predecessor once said: "Custom moves people along with it — even the worthy and wise cannot entirely escape it." Today, whether it concerns a single robe or hat, a single implement or object, a single word or phrase, whatever one person happens to initiate, the crowd rises up to follow, and this is called fashion. Someone favors solitary retreat, and the crowd rises up to sit in solitary retreat as well; someone favors ritual repentance, and the crowd rises up to perform ritual repentance as well; the crowd rises to recite sutras from memory, rises to chant the Cundī mantra, rises to study phonological tables, rises to set aside commentaries in favor of the bare text alone, rises to hold vegetarian feasts for a hundred and eight thousand monks, rises to study calligraphy, poetry, and the polished prose style of officials and scholars — sweeping through society like wind through grass, with no need for any prior agreement at all. Only genuine, wholehearted, dedicated Chan investigation and Buddha-recitation finds someone to initiate it, and yet no crowd ever rises to follow. What could possibly explain this?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Cundī mantra and "reading phonological tables" (等韻, the study of Chinese syllable structure modeled on Sanskrit phonetics) were both genuinely fashionable pursuits among literate Buddhist practitioners in Zhuhong's own day — the entry's real force comes from its sheer accumulation of concrete, period-specific fads before the final, deliberately anticlimactic question, which withholds any answer at all and leaves the reader to supply it.

90. 厭喧求靜 — Wearying of Noise, Seeking Stillness

A practical argument, using an escalating series of unstoppable noises, that seeking external silence is a losing strategy, and that only stilling the mind itself can succeed.

Chinese Source

有習靜者，獨居一室，稍有人聲，便以為礙。夫人聲可禁也，鴉鵲噪於庭，則如之何？鴉鵲可驅也，虎豹嘯於林，則如之何？虎豹猶可使獵人捕之也，風響水流、雷轟雨驟，則如之何？故曰：「愚人除境不除心，智者除心不除境。」欲除境，而境卒不可除，則道終不可學矣！或曰：「世尊不知五百車聲，蓋禪定中事，非凡夫所能。」然則高鳳讀書，不知驟雨漂麥，當是時鳳所入何定？不咎志之不堅，而嫌境之不寂，亦謬矣哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

There are those who practice stillness, dwelling alone in a single room, and who find even the slightest human voice an intolerable disturbance. Human voices, perhaps, can be forbidden — but what of crows and magpies clamoring in the courtyard? Crows and magpies, perhaps, can be driven off — but what of tigers and leopards roaring in the forest? Tigers and leopards, perhaps, can be caught by a hunter — but what of the sound of wind, the flow of water, the crash of thunder, the rush of sudden rain? And so it is said: "The foolish try to remove the environment rather than the mind; the wise remove the mind rather than the environment." Wishing to remove the environment, and yet finding the environment can never finally be removed at all, one would never be able to study the Way at all!

Someone might say: "The World-Honored One himself once failed to notice the sound of five hundred passing carts — but this happened within deep meditative absorption, something beyond an ordinary person's reach." And yet consider Gao Feng reading his books, failing to notice a sudden downpour washing away the wheat drying in his courtyard — what meditative state, then, had Gao Feng himself entered? To blame the environment for its lack of stillness, rather than blaming one's own lack of firm resolve, is a genuine error indeed!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Gao Feng was a genuine Later Han scholar recorded in the Book of the Later Han for exactly this episode — so absorbed in his reading that he failed to notice a sudden storm washing away wheat his wife had left him to guard — and Zhuhong's use of a documented Confucian exemplar to answer an objection framed around the Buddha's own meditative absorption is characteristic of his readiness to draw evidence from either tradition wherever the argument calls for it.

91. 除日 — New Year's Eve

A New Year's Eve meditation on treating each year's end as a rehearsal for death itself, and a careful warning against mistaking scholarly fluency or composed sitting for the real, total breakthrough the tradition actually demands.

Chinese Source

古人以除日當死日，蓋一歲盡處，猶一生盡處。故黃檗垂示云：「預先若打不徹，臘月三十日到來，管取你熱亂。」然則正月初一便理會除日事不為早，初生墮地時便理會死日事不為早，那堪荏荏苒苒，悠悠揚揚，不覺少而壯，壯而老，老而死；況更有不及壯且老者，豈不重可哀哉？今晚歲除，應當惕然自誓自要，不可明年依舊蹉跎去也。雖然，此「打徹」二字，不可容易看過，不是通幾本經論當得徹也，不是坐幾炷香不動不搖當得徹也，不是解幾則古德問答機緣、作幾句頌古拈古當得徹也，不是酬對幾句口頭三昧滑溜當得徹也。古人謂於此事洞然如桶底驟脫，爽然如大夢得醒，更無纖毫疑處，然後可耳。嗟乎！敢不努力？！

Ru-Yi Translation

The ancients treated New Year's Eve as though it were their own day of death — for the end of a year is, in miniature, exactly like the end of a whole life. This is why Huangbo taught his students, saying: "If you have not yet broken through before this, then when the thirtieth day of the twelfth month finally arrives, I guarantee you will be thrown into utter confusion." If this is so, then attending to the affairs of New Year's Eve starting from New Year's Day itself is not too early at all — attending to the matter of one's own death from the very moment of birth is not too early either. How, then, can one afford to drift along carelessly, aimlessly, unaware as childhood gives way to maturity, maturity to old age, old age to death — and this leaves aside those who never even reach maturity or old age at all, which is all the more profoundly sad!

Tonight is New Year's Eve — each of us ought to feel a real jolt of self-warning, and resolve firmly that we will not squander the coming year exactly as we squandered this one. And yet this phrase "breaking through" must not be taken lightly — it does not mean having read through a few sutras and treatises, nor sitting through a few sticks of incense without moving, nor being able to explain a few of the old masters' recorded exchanges or compose a few verses commenting on old cases, nor being able to trade a few slick, well-rehearsed phrases of Chan repartee. The ancients said: this matter must become as clear as the sudden falling away of a bucket's bottom, as bright as waking from a great dream, with not the slightest thread of doubt remaining — only then can one truly speak of having broken through. Alas! How can we not strive with all our might?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Huangbo saying Zhuhong quotes here — "when the thirtieth day of the twelfth month arrives, I guarantee you will be thrown into utter confusion" — uses the same traditional figure for the moment of death already invoked in the Oxherd Layman's teaching in entry 86 above, and Zhuhong's closing image of "a bucket's bottom suddenly falling away" is one of Chan literature's most common figures for sudden, total awakening, describing the complete and instantaneous collapse of a false floor rather than any gradual erosion.
