竹窗隨筆・三筆 · Entries 1–4

Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Third Collection, Part 1

The opening entries of the Third Collection (三筆), the final installment of Zhuhong's notebooks. This section opens with an uncompromising argument that killing is the single greatest evil in human life, followed by Zhuhong's own account of personally testing Yongming Yanshou's legendary hundred-thousand daily recitations, and two entries on the discipline of genuine humility before claiming any teaching authority.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: work in progress, entries 1–4 of 三筆 Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

1. 殺生人世大惡 — Killing Living Beings Is the Greatest Evil in the Human World

Opening the Third Collection, Zhuhong argues that killing surpasses robbery, filial betrayal, and incitement to wrongdoing as the gravest of all human evils — because its victims may be one's own parents from countless past lives, and because it alone goes entirely unpunished by human law.

Chinese Source

或問：「人所造惡，何者最大？」應之者曰：「劫盜也，忤逆也，教唆也。」予曰：「是則然，更有大焉，大莫大於殺生也。」或曰：「宰殺充庖，日用常事，何得名惡？而況最大？」噫！劫盜雖惡，意在得財，苟歡喜而與之，未必戕人之命；而殺生則剖腹剜心，肝腦鼎鑊矣！忤逆者，或棄不奉養，慢不恭敬，未必為阿闍、楊廣之舉。況闍、廣所害，一世父母；而經言有生之屬，或多夙世父母，殺生者自少至老，所殺無算，則害及多生父母矣！教唆者，惡積名彰，多遭察訪，漏網者稀；彼殺生者，誰得而詰之？則搆訟之害有分限，而殺生之害無終盡也。是故天地之大德曰生，天地間之大惡曰殺生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked: "Of all the evils people commit, which is the greatest?" Those who answered said: "Robbery, rebellion against one's parents, inciting others to wrongdoing." I said: "These are indeed grave, but there is something still greater — nothing in this world is a greater evil than killing living beings." Someone objected: "Slaughtering animals for the table is an everyday matter of ordinary life — how can this be called evil, let alone the greatest evil?"

Ah! Listen to what I have to say. Robbery, evil though it is, aims only at obtaining wealth — if the victim were to hand it over willingly, the robber need not necessarily take his life at all; but killing living beings means slitting open the belly, gouging out the heart, boiling flesh and brains in a cauldron! Rebellion against one's parents may take the form of abandoning them without support, or treating them with contemptuous disrespect, but need not go so far as the deeds of Ajātaśatru or Yang Guang. And besides, what Ajātaśatru and Yang Guang harmed were only the parents of a single lifetime — but the sutras teach that living beings themselves are, in many cases, one's own parents from past lives; and one who kills living beings, from youth to old age, kills beyond any possible count — this harms one's own parents across countless lifetimes! As for inciting others to wrongdoing, once such evil accumulates and becomes known, it is usually investigated, and few escape the law's reach; but who ever investigates those who kill living beings? Comparing the two, then, the harm caused by robbery, rebellion, or incitement has its own limits, while the harm of killing living beings has no end at all. This is why the Yijing says: "The greatest virtue of heaven and earth is to give life." In other words, the greatest evil in heaven and earth is to kill living beings.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Ajātaśatru and Yang Guang are both genuine, documented figures whose crimes against their own fathers were matters of historical record rather than legend — Ajātaśatru's imprisonment of King Bimbisāra is the narrative frame of the Contemplation Sutra itself, and Yang Guang (Emperor Yang of Sui) was widely believed, in the traditional historiography, to have killed his own father Emperor Wen to seize the throne. Zhuhong's argument works by first granting these two men the very worst reputation history affords, then arguing that even they only wronged "the parents of a single lifetime" — a rhetorical strategy of conceding the extreme case before surpassing it that recurs throughout this collection.

2. 晝夜彌陀十萬聲 — A Hundred Thousand Recitations of Amitābha's Name, Day and Night

Zhuhong personally tests the legend that Master Yongming Yanshou recited Amitābha's name a hundred thousand times every day, and reports his own honest findings — including a caution against taking the number too literally.

Chinese Source

世傳永明大師晝夜念彌陀十萬。予嘗試之，自今初日分，至明初日分，足十二時百刻，正得十萬；而所念止是四字名號，若六字則不及滿數矣。飲食抽解，皆無間斷，少間則不及滿數矣。睡眠語言，皆悉斷絕，少縱則不及滿數矣。而忙急迫促，如趕路人，無暇細心切念，細念則不及滿數矣。故知十萬云者，大概極言須臾不離之意，而不必定限十萬之數也。吾恐信心念佛者或執之成病，因舉吾所自試者以告。或曰：「此大師禪定中事也。」則非吾所知矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

It has long been said that Master Yongming recited Amitābha's name a hundred thousand times, day and night. I once tried this myself: starting from this morning's sunrise and continuing until tomorrow's sunrise — a full twenty-four hours, a hundred graduated marks on the water-clock — I managed exactly a hundred thousand recitations, but only by reciting the four-character name; had I recited the six-character form, I could not have reached the full count. Eating, drinking, and relieving myself all had to proceed without the recitation ever pausing — the slightest interruption, and the count would fall short. Sleep and conversation had to be entirely cut off — the slightest indulgence, and the count would fall short. And the pace had to be hurried and urgent, like a traveler rushing along a road, with no time at all to recite carefully and with real sincerity — reciting carefully would likewise leave the count short.

From this I understand: "a hundred thousand times" is, in general, simply a vivid way of expressing the idea that one should never depart from the Buddha's name even for a moment — it does not necessarily fix the fully specific number of a hundred thousand as a strict requirement. I worry that some sincere practitioners of Buddha-recitation might cling to this number and make themselves ill over it, and so I report my own experiment here, exactly as it happened. Should someone say: "This was something the master accomplished within deep meditative absorption" — that, then, is beyond what I myself can know.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This is one of the most directly empirical entries anywhere across all three collections — Zhuhong does not simply cite or defend the traditional account of Yongming Yanshou's daily practice, he runs the experiment on his own body and reports the exact operational constraints he found (no pause for sleep or conversation, rushed rather than contemplative recitation) before drawing the appropriately modest conclusion that the number is rhetorical rather than a literal target — a rare, concrete instance of the collection's habitual caution against clinging to outward form over genuine substance.

3. 己事辦方可為人 — Only Once One's Own Matter Is Settled Can One Teach Others

Zhuhong reflects on his own reluctance to claim the authority of an abbot, even after a genuine monastic community had gathered around him unbidden — holding fast to the old discipline of continued self-cultivation before claiming any teaching role.

Chinese Source

古人大徹大悟，參學事畢，且於水邊林下，長養聖胎，不惜口頭生醭；龍天推出，方乃為人。故辭法席者，願生生居學地，而自鍛鍊。予出家時，篤奉此語，佩之胸襟。後以病入山，久久不覺漸成叢林。然至今不敢目所居為方丈，不敢開大口妄論宗乘，蓋與眾同修，非領眾行道也，忝一日之長，互相激勸而已。諸仁者以友道待我而責善焉，幸甚！

Ru-Yi Translation

The ancients, once they had achieved great and thorough awakening and completed their own course of study, would still withdraw to some quiet spot by the water or beneath the trees, nurturing their own sacred embryo of realization, unconcerned even if mold grew upon their unused mouths — only once the dragon spirits and heavenly guardians themselves pushed them forward would they finally begin teaching others. This is why those who declined an invitation to teach vowed to remain, life after life, in the position of a student still under training, continuing to temper and refine themselves.

When I first left the household life, I devoutly upheld this very teaching, holding it close to my own heart. Later, when illness brought me into the mountains, I did not expect that, over time, a genuine monastic community would gradually form around me. And yet even now, I dare not regard my own dwelling as an abbot's quarters, and dare not speak recklessly, pronouncing grandly on the doctrines of the various schools — I am simply practicing together with the assembly, not leading them along the Way. Being somewhat senior in age is my only real qualification, and I offer nothing more than mutual encouragement between fellow practitioners. I hope every one of you will treat me as a companion on the path, and correct me freely — nothing would please me more!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry is a rare piece of direct institutional autobiography — Zhuhong describing, in his own words, how Yunqi Monastery came into being almost by accident, as visitors gathered around a sick monk in retreat rather than through his own deliberate founding of a teaching center; his continued refusal, even at this late point in his life and after two completed collections of widely read teaching notes, to call himself an abbot in more than name reflects the same disciplined humility already visible in his account of his own father's teaching against official entanglement (Second Collection, entry 83) and his regret at never having studied long under a single master (Second Collection, entry 110).

4. 自他二利 — Benefiting Oneself and Benefiting Others

A careful warning against a popular saying that can be misused to justify premature teaching — arguing that the bodhisattva's resolve to help others before helping oneself describes an initial vow, not a license to guide others before one's own realization is secure.

Chinese Source

古云：「未能自利，先能利人者，菩薩發心。」斯言甘露也，不善用之，則翻成毒藥。試反己而思之：我是菩薩否？況云發心，非實已能也。獨不聞「自覺已圓，復行覺他者，如來應世」乎？或謂：「必待已圓，而後利他，則利他終無時矣。」然自疾不能救，而能救他人，無有是處。是故當發菩薩廣大之心，而復確守如來真切之訓。不然，以盲引盲，欲自附於菩薩，而人己雙失，謂之何哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

The ancients said: "One who, before benefiting himself, first benefits others — this is the bodhisattva giving rise to the mind of awakening." This saying is truly like sweet dew — and yet, poorly understood, it can turn into poison instead. Try reflecting honestly on yourself: am I truly a bodhisattva? And besides, this only describes the bodhisattva's initial resolve, not something already actually accomplished. Have you never heard the saying: "One whose own awakening is already complete, who then goes on to awaken others — this is how the Tathāgata manifests in the world"?

Someone might say: "If one must wait until one's own awakening is complete before benefiting others, then the chance to benefit others would never come at all." But it is like a man unable to cure his own illness, and yet claiming he can cure others' illnesses — there is simply no such possibility. This is why one must give rise to the bodhisattva's own broad and expansive resolve, while also firmly upholding the Tathāgata's own genuine, earnest instruction. Otherwise, one blind man leading other blind men, wishing only to attach the name of "bodhisattva" to oneself, ends up losing both oneself and others at once — what, then, is the use of that?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Both quoted lines come from the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's own sixth fascicle, placed there side by side as two genuinely distinct descriptions — the bodhisattva's initial resolve versus the Tathāgata's own completed realization — which Zhuhong reads as deliberately graduated rather than interchangeable; this entry functions as a direct companion to entry 127 of the Second Collection, which catalogued the many ways borrowed bodhisattva language can excuse premature or counterfeit conduct, applied here specifically to the temptation to teach before one's own realization is settled.
