竹窗隨筆・三筆 · Entries 61–64

Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Third Collection, Part 14

Continuing the Third Collection (三筆). Zhuhong explains why genuine Pure Land awakening leaves so little historical trace, delivers a sharp rebuke to monks who neglect their own parents while lavishing attention on donors and disciples, questions a popular belief that thunder always punishes the wicked, and distinguishes true spiritual friendship from its counterfeits.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: work in progress, entries 61–64 of 三筆 Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

61. 念佛不見悟人 — No Awakened Practitioners Seen Among Buddha-Reciters?

An answer to the objection that Chan produces famous awakened masters while Buddha-recitation seemingly does not — arguing that genuine devotion to recitation is, by its own nature, far less likely to seek public recognition, using the Sixth Patriarch's own near-anonymous early life as the clearest possible example.

Chinese Source

或問參禪得悟者相望於冊，念佛得悟者，何寥寥其未聞也？噫！蓋有之矣，子未之見也。且參禪人得理之後，終不嘵嘵以自鳴也；龍天推出，然後聲振一時，而名垂後世。彼曹溪佩黃梅之心印，苟不失口於風旛，一獵人之守網夫而已；清素受慈明密記，苟非邂逅於荔枝，一叢林之閒老漢而已，子何自而知之？況實心念佛者，志出娑婆，精求淨土，念念如救頭然，即其悟本性之彌陀，了惟心之極樂，若終身隱而不出，子亦烏得而知之也？凡上上品生者，皆得悟人也，往生傳不可不讀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked: those who attained awakening through Chan investigation fill entire volumes of biographical record — why, then, is it so rare to hear of anyone attaining awakening through Buddha-recitation? Ah! Such people certainly exist — you simply have not encountered them. And besides, once a Chan practitioner has grasped the truth, he never makes noise about it to draw attention to himself; only once the dragon spirits and heavenly guardians themselves push him forward does his reputation finally resound, and his name come to be handed down to later generations.

Consider: had the Sixth Patriarch, who received the mind-seal from the Fifth Patriarch at Huangmei, never happened to blurt out his own subtle insight about the flag and the wind, he would have remained nothing more than an ordinary man watching over a hunter's nets. Had Master Qingsu, who received Master Ciming's own private confirmation, never happened to encounter Master Congyue over a dish of lychees, he would have remained nothing more than an idle old man within some monastic community. How, then, would you ever have come to know of their attainment at all?

How much more so, then, for one who sincerely, genuinely recites the Buddha's name, whose aspiration is to leave this Sahā world entirely and seek rebirth in the Pure Land with real diligence, every single thought as urgent as though putting out a fire on his own head — even should such a person have already awakened to the Amitābha of his own original nature, realized the Pure Land of his own mind, and yet remained hidden his whole life long, never once stepping forward — how could you possibly know of him either? All those who attain the highest of the highest grade of rebirth are, without exception, truly awakened people — the biographical Records of Rebirth are not a book one can afford to leave unread.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Both illustrative cases are genuine, well-documented episodes already introduced elsewhere in Zhuhong's own writing — the Sixth Patriarch's famous "wind and flag" pronouncement, which led directly to Master Yinzong's own remarkable act of deference (already discussed in the Second Collection's entry 109 above), and Master Qingsu's chance encounter over lychees with the monk Congyue, which alone revealed his own hidden decades of realization under Ciming — Zhuhong's argument works by showing that even history's most celebrated Chan breakthroughs depended on essentially accidental disclosure, not on the practitioners' own desire for recognition.

62. 為僧宜孝父母 — Monks Ought to Be Filial to Their Parents

A sharp rebuke of monks who neglect their own aging parents while cultivating close, gift-exchanging relationships with donors and doting on their own disciples — arguing this inverts the very renunciation it claims to practice.

Chinese Source

有為僧不孝父母者，予深責之。或曰：出家既已辭親割愛，責之則反動其恩愛心矣！曰：惡！是何言也？大孝釋迦尊，累劫報親恩，積因成正覺。而《梵網》云：「戒雖萬行，以孝為宗。」《觀經》云：「孝養父母，淨業正因。」古人有作堂奉母者，擔母乞食者，未嘗以恩愛累也。奈何於親割愛矣，而締交施主，不絕餽遺，畜養弟子，過於骨肉，是無親而有親，出一愛而復入一愛也，何顛倒乃爾！且己受十方供養，飽暖安居，而坐視父母之饑寒寥落，汝安則為之。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are monks who fail to be filial to their own parents, and I hold them severely to account for it. Someone objected: "Having already left home and cut off worldly attachments, to reproach him for this would only stir up his own emotional bonds again!" Ah! What kind of talk is this? The Great Filial One, the World-Honored Śākyamuni himself, repaid his own parents' kindness across countless kalpas, and it was precisely through accumulating this cause that he attained perfect enlightenment. The Brahma Net Sutra says: "Though the precepts number in the ten thousands, filial devotion is their very root." The Contemplation Sutra says: "Filial support of one's parents is the genuine cause of pure karma."

There have been those among the ancients who built a hall specifically to care for their own mother, and those who carried their own mother on their back while begging for alms — never once did such devotion prove a burden of attachment. Why, then, should someone who has already cut off attachment to his own parents go on to cultivate close ties with donors instead, exchanging gifts without pause, and dote on his own disciples more than his own flesh and blood? This is treating strangers as family while abandoning genuine family, casting off one attachment only to take up another in its place — how thoroughly upside down this is! And to accept offerings from every direction, living warmly clothed and well fed and secure, while sitting idly by as one's own parents suffer cold and hunger and utter loneliness — if such a person's own conscience can truly rest easy with that, then let him do as he pleases.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry pairs naturally with the Second Collection's own entry 83, in which Zhuhong describes his father's household teaching and his own grief at never having had the chance to guide his parents toward Buddha-recitation before their deaths — that earlier, deeply personal entry gives real weight to the severity of this one, showing his insistence on filial devotion within monastic life was not merely doctrinal but grounded in his own unresolved family history.

63. 雷霆 — Thunder

A skeptical examination of the popular belief that thunder punishes the unfilial and impious, arguing instead that karmic retribution takes countless different forms and cannot be measured by whether lightning strikes.

Chinese Source

蘇明允曰：「叛父母，褻神明，則雷霆下擊之。雷霆固不能盡擊此輩也，然有時而不測也。」明允此言，欲使為惡者懼，而漏網雷霆之擊者亦眾矣，終不能使之懼也。然為惡受報，蓋亦多途，有生惡疾而死者，有犯刑憲而死者，有遭虎狼而死者，有死於水溺者，有死於火焚者，有死於刀斧者，有死於砒鴆者，有死於牆崩石壓者，其為報一也。殺人以挺與刃之類也，豈必其盡擊於雷霆乎？況復有見生受報者，有來生受報者，有身報於陽世者，有魂報於冥司者，毋曰不擊於雷霆，而遽稱漏網也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Su Mingyun said: "Whoever rebels against his own parents, or shows contempt for the spirits, will be struck down by thunder." Thunder, of course, cannot possibly strike down every single person of this kind — but there are times when unforeseen calamity does befall them regardless. Mingyun's words were meant to instill fear in wrongdoers, and yet there are many who escape thunder's own reach entirely, and so in the end his words fail to instill real fear at all.

And yet retribution for evil deeds comes by many different paths: some die of an incurable illness, some are executed under the law, some are killed by tigers and wolves, some drown, some burn, some die by blade or by axe, some by poison, some crushed beneath collapsing walls or falling stones — the retribution is the same in every case. Killing a person with a club is no different from killing with a blade — must every single case of retribution really come down from the sky as thunder? And besides, some retribution comes within this very life, some in a life yet to come; some falls upon the body in this world, some upon the spirit in the world below. Do not say that whoever escapes thunder's own strike has therefore escaped judgment entirely.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Su Mingyun is Su Xun, the genuine Song-dynasty essayist and father of Su Dongpo, together forming the "Three Sus" among the Eight Great Prose Masters of the Tang and Song; Zhuhong's critique here is precise rather than dismissive — he does not deny that wrongdoing brings retribution, only that Su Xun's specific mechanism (literal thunder) is too narrow a lens for a moral law that, on Zhuhong's own account, operates across illness, accident, legal punishment, and rebirth alike.

64. 真友 — A Genuine Friend

A careful qualification of Zhongfeng Mingben's own famously self-reliant teaching on Chan investigation, warning it can become dangerous if misapplied by the wrong student, followed by a graded definition of what genuine spiritual friendship actually requires.

Chinese Source

中峰大師警策有：「參禪必待尋師友，敢保工夫一世休。」又曰：「縱饒達磨與釋迦，擬親早已成窠臼。」此醍醐至妙之言也。然不可聞於下士也，執此言而自用自專，不復知取友之益，則翻成毒藥矣！取友非難，得真友為難。飲食、財帛相徵逐者，惡友也；善相勸、惡相規者，好友也；開我以正修行路，示我以最上乘法，為我燈、為我眼、為我導師、為我醫王者，真善知識友也，不可一日而遠離者也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Master Zhongfeng's own admonitory verses say: "If Chan investigation must always wait upon finding some teacher or friend, I can hardly guarantee your whole life's effort will not come to nothing." He also says: "Even should Bodhidharma and Śākyamuni themselves stand before you, to seek intimacy with them is already to fall into a fixed rut." These are words of the most exquisite, essence-like truth. And yet they must not be spoken to those of lesser capacity — should such a person cling to these words and act entirely on his own authority, no longer recognizing the benefit of genuine companionship at all, this same essence would turn into poison instead.

Choosing a friend is not difficult — finding a genuine friend is what proves difficult. Those who associate with one another merely over food, drink, and material wealth are bad companions; those who encourage each other toward good and restrain each other from evil are good companions. But one who opens the correct path of practice for me, reveals to me the very highest teaching, and serves as my own lamp, my own eyes, my own guide, my own physician-king — such a person is a truly good and wise companion, one from whom I ought not to be separated even for a single day.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry's structure — quote a genuinely profound teaching, then caution that it can poison a student not ready for it — directly parallels Zhuhong's own treatment of Zhongfeng's teaching in the Second Collection's entry 75, where the same master's radical self-reliance was likewise qualified as suited only to advanced practitioners; here the qualification runs in the opposite direction, defending the value of genuine friendship against a teaching that could otherwise be misread as license for total isolation.
