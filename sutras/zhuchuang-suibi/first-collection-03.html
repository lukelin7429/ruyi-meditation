Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — First Collection, Part 3

Continuing the First Collection (初筆) of Zhuchuang Suibi in the source text's own order. This section includes some of the collection's most technical doctrinal entries — a defense of Sengzhao's Treatise on the Immutability of Things against a Ming-dynasty rebuttal, and a four-part personal account (湯厄一–四) of Zhuhong's own scalding accident and what it taught him about the gap between theoretical and tested equanimity.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: work in progress, 75 of 132 entries in 初筆 Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

51. 二種鼠 — Two Kinds of Rats

A short analogy contrasting the house rat and the tree squirrel, read as evidence of habits carried over from past lives.

Chinese Source

家鼠穿墉走梁、循床入篋，纍纍然與人近，而逃形避影，自古無能豢而狎之者；松鼠以山巖為國，樹杪為家，若方外之士、化外之民，而人得置之襟懷，馴如慈母之撫赤子。此其故何也？意者，宿習之使也。彼家鼠，其昔穿窬之盜者耶；彼松鼠，其昔為人之服役者耶。均之畜生，而不無彼善於此也，術不可不慎也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The house rat runs through walls and along roof-beams, creeps along the bed-frame and into chests and boxes — its traces constantly close to people, and yet it flees the moment it senses a shadow or a form; since ancient times, no one has ever managed to tame or grow familiar with one. The tree squirrel, by contrast, makes its country of the mountain crags and its home in the treetops — like a recluse beyond the dust of the world, a subject beyond the reach of any government — and yet people are able to catch one, and once caught, hold it close to their hearts, and it grows as tame and gentle as an infant in its mother's arms.

What could account for this difference? I suspect it comes down to habits carried over from past lives. That house rat may once have been a thief who broke through walls and slipped through holes; that tree squirrel may once have been someone's faithful servant. Both alike are now animals, and yet one is plainly better than the other — a warning that one cannot be too careful in choosing the trade one takes up.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The closing line — "one cannot be too careful in choosing the trade one takes up" — extends the animal analogy into a human warning: the habits and occupations formed in this life are read here as the direct seed of the "sundry rebirths" (雜類受生) to come.

52. 僧習 — A Monastic Habit

A brief, pointed complaint about monks spending their time on secular literary accomplishments precisely as educated laypeople are turning away from them toward Chan.

Chinese Source

末法僧有習書、習詩、習尺牘語，而是三者，皆士大夫所有事，士大夫捨之不習而習禪，僧顧攻其所捨，而於己分上一大事因緣置之度外，何顛倒乃爾！

Ru-Yi Translation

In this age of the Dharma's decline, there are monks who practice calligraphy, practice poetry, practice the polished language of letters — yet all three of these are properly the business of scholar-officials. And scholar-officials themselves have now set these aside in favor of studying Chan! Monks, meanwhile, devote themselves precisely to what scholar-officials have abandoned, while setting aside the one great matter that is properly their own concern — how upside-down can things possibly get!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The "one great matter" (一大事因緣) is the Lotus Sūtra's phrase for the Buddha's sole purpose in appearing in the world — opening sentient beings' own inherent wisdom — used elsewhere in this collection (entry 26 in Part 1) as shorthand for what a monk's whole life ought to be devoted to.

53. 古今人不相及 — Ancients and Moderns Do Not Measure Up to One Another

A reflection on the apparent scarcity of great monastic teachers in Zhuhong's own dynasty, balanced by a warning against either arrogance or self-abandonment.

Chinese Source

本朝尊宿，自洪武至今，殆不多見。無論唐宋，只如元之中峰、天如諸老，今代唯琦楚石一人可與馳騁上下，況古之又古耶！得非世愈降、障愈深耶？豪傑固無文王猶興，畢竟星中之月而已。然則末法中人，不可妄自尊大而輕視古德，又不可甘心暴棄而不為豪傑也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Venerable and virtuous monks of our own dynasty, from the founding Hongwu era down to the present day, are scarcely to be found. Never mind the Tang and Song — even compared just to the Yuan-dynasty elders Zhongfeng and Tianru, in our own age only Qi Chushi alone might be said to hold his own against them — and how much more so against the truly ancient masters, further back still! Could it be that as the age declines further, karmic obstructions only grow heavier? Even without a King Wen to inspire them, men of real heroic character will still rise — but such people are, in the end, no more than a single moon among the stars. And so people of this degenerate age must neither grow arrogant and look down on the ancient worthies, nor resign themselves to giving up and refusing to become such heroic figures themselves.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Qi Chushi (楚石梵琦, 1296–1370) was an early Ming Chan master widely regarded as the outstanding teacher of the dynasty's founding generation; Zhongfeng Mingben and Tianru Weize (already encountered in entry 4 of Part 1, on the Śūraṅgama commentary controversy) are his Yuan-dynasty predecessors. The Mencius allusion — "even without a King Wen, men of heroic character will still rise" — is used here to hold two warnings in tension at once, against both complacent self-importance and defeatist resignation.

54. 物不遷論駁 — A Rebuttal of "Things Do Not Shift," Rebutted

The most technically demanding entry in the collection so far: an extended defense of Sengzhao's classical Madhyamaka treatise On the Immutability of Things, against a real controversy that had recently divided Ming-dynasty Buddhist scholars.

Chinese Source

有為物不遷論駁者，謂肇公不當以物各住位為不遷，當以物各無性為不遷。而不平者反駁其駁。或疑而未決，舉以問予。予曰：為駁者，固非全無據而妄談；駁其駁者，亦非故抑今而揚古，蓋各有所見也。我今平心而折衷之：子不讀真空、般若、涅槃三論，及始之宗本義乎？使無此，則今之駁，吾意肇公且口掛壁上，無言可對、無理可伸矣！今三論發明性空之旨，罔不曲盡，而宗本中又明言緣會之與性空一也，豈不曉所謂性空者耶？蓋作論本意，因世人以昔物不至今，則昔長往，名為物遷，故即其言而反之。若曰：爾之所謂遷者，正我之所謂不遷也。此名就路還家，以賊攻賊，位不轉而易南成北，質不改而變鍮為金，巧心妙手，無礙之辯才也。故此論非正論物不遷也，因昔物今物二句而作耳。若無因自作，必通篇以性空立論，如三論矣！茲徑以不曉性空病肇公，肇公豈得心服？是故「求向物於昔，於昔未嘗無；責向物於今，於今未嘗有。」此數言者，似乖乎性空之旨；然昔以緣合不無，今以緣散不有，緣會性空既其不二，又何煩費辭以辨肇公之失哉？或問：何故彼論通篇不出此意？曰：以有「緣會不異性空」之語在宗本中，觀者自可默契耳。若知有今日，更於論尾增一二語結明此意，則駁何由生？吁！肇公當必首肯，而不知為駁者之信否也。

Ru-Yi Translation

There has arisen a rebuttal of Sengzhao's Treatise on the Immutability of Things, which holds that Master Zhao ought not to have grounded "immutability" in the claim that each thing rests fixed in its own position, but should instead have grounded it in the claim that each thing is without any fixed self-nature at all. And those who find this unfair have rebutted the rebuttal in turn. Someone, uncertain which side was right, put the question to me.

I said: those who raised the rebuttal were certainly not arguing from nothing, without any basis at all; and those who rebutted the rebuttal were likewise not simply defending the ancients over the moderns out of bias — each side, in fact, has seen something real. Let me now weigh the matter fairly and reconcile the two. Have you not read Master Zhao's other three treatises — On Emptiness Not Being Real, On Prajñā's Non-Knowing, and On Nameless Nirvāṇa — together with his opening Fundamental Meaning? Without having read these, I think Master Zhao himself, faced with today's rebuttal, would simply have to hang up his mouth on the wall, with nothing to say and no argument to make! But in fact these three treatises expound the meaning of emptiness of self-nature in the most thorough and exhaustive way, and the Fundamental Meaning states outright that dependent origination and emptiness of self-nature are one and the same thing — how could he possibly not have understood what "emptiness of self-nature" means?

The real point of the treatise was this: because ordinary people assume that a thing belonging to the past cannot reach into the present, and so conclude that the past has permanently departed — calling this "the shifting of things" — Master Zhao took up their own words and turned them back on themselves. As if to say: what you call "shifting" is exactly what I call "not shifting." This is what is meant by "returning home by the very road you are already on," or "using a thief to catch a thief" — the position never actually moves, and yet south becomes north; the substance never actually changes, and yet brass becomes gold. It is a piece of supremely skillful, unobstructed argumentative craft. This treatise, then, is not really a straightforward treatise on whether things shift or not — it was written specifically to address the two claims, "the thing of the past" and "the thing of the present." If it had had no such specific occasion, and had simply been composed on its own, it would surely have grounded its entire argument in emptiness of self-nature throughout, exactly like the other three treatises! To turn around now and fault Master Zhao for not understanding emptiness of self-nature — how could he possibly accept that verdict?

And so the treatise's lines — "seeking the thing of the past in the past, it was never, in the past, absent; holding the thing of the present accountable in the present, it has never, in the present, been present" — these words may seem, at first glance, to run against the very meaning of emptiness of self-nature. But in fact: the past thing existed, by virtue of the coming-together of conditions, not as something absent; the present thing does not exist, by virtue of the dispersal of conditions, not as something present. Since dependent origination and emptiness of self-nature are already not two different things, why go to all this trouble arguing over some supposed flaw in Master Zhao's treatise?

Someone might ask: why, then, does the treatise never state this point outright, from beginning to end? I would say: because the statement "dependent origination is no different from emptiness of self-nature" already appears in the Fundamental Meaning, and Master Zhao evidently expected his readers to grasp the connection silently, on their own. Had he known there would one day be a controversy like this one, he would surely have added a line or two at the end of the treatise to make the point explicit — and then how could any rebuttal have arisen at all? Ah! I feel certain Master Zhao himself would nod in agreement with this reading — though I cannot say whether those who raised the original rebuttal will find it convincing.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Sengzhao (僧肇, 384–414), a disciple of the great translator Kumārajīva, died at thirty-one and left four short treatises collectively known as the Zhao Lun, among the most philosophically sophisticated Madhyamaka writing produced in Chinese. The "rebuttal" Zhuhong addresses was real and current in his own lifetime: the monk Kongyin Zhencheng (空印鎮澄) had argued in his own treatise that Sengzhao's "each thing rests fixed in its own position" language dangerously implied a kind of static permanence, at odds with the doctrine of emptiness, and several major contemporaries of Zhuhong — including Zibo Zhenke and Hanshan Deqing — weighed in on the controversy. Zhuhong's resolution is characteristically dialectical: rather than picking a side, he shows both readings are correct depending on which of Sengzhao's four treatises one reads together with which.

55. 碧巖集 — The Blue Cliff Record

A defense of Dahui Zonggao's famous (and, to Zhuhong's contemporaries, controversial) burning of the printing blocks of his own teacher's celebrated commentary anthology.

Chinese Source

圓悟作碧巖集，妙喜欲入閩碎其板，淺智者遂病圓悟，不知妙喜特一時遣著語耳！夫雪竇百則頌古，先德謂是頌古之聖；而圓悟始為評唱，又評唱之聖也。而不免為文字般若。愚者執之，故妙喜為此說，碎學人之情識也，非碎碧巖集也。其言碎者，彷彿雲門一棒打殺之意也。神而明之，碧巖寸寸旃檀；執而泥之，一大藏板皆可碎也。噫！可與知者道也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Yuanwu compiled the Blue Cliff Record, and Miaoxi wanted to travel to Fujian to destroy its printing blocks. Those of shallow understanding then found fault with Yuanwu over this — not realizing that Miaoxi's remark was simply something said in the heat of a particular moment! Xuedou's hundred verses on old cases were, the worthies of old said, the very finest such verse ever composed; and Yuanwu, in being the first to add running commentary to them, was likewise the finest ever to do so — and yet even this remains, in the end, merely "prajñā expressed in words." Fools clung to the text itself, and it was for their sake that Miaoxi spoke as he did — to smash the students' own deluded clinging, not to smash the Blue Cliff Record itself. His talk of "smashing" carries roughly the same force as Yunmen's saying about "striking dead with a single blow."

Understood with real spiritual insight, every inch of the Blue Cliff Record is fragrant sandalwood; clung to and taken literally, the entire printed canon itself could just as well be smashed to pieces. Ah — but this can only be said to someone who already understands!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Yuanwu Keqin's Blue Cliff Record, built on Xuedou Chongxian's earlier hundred verses, is one of the most celebrated gōng'àn anthologies in Chan literature; Dahui Zonggao (Miaoxi) was Yuanwu's own student, and his historically attested destruction of the printing blocks was a real event, not a legend — a startling act often read as an attack on his own teacher's masterwork. Zhuhong reframes it not as repudiation but as a targeted piece of teaching, aimed at students who had begun mistaking eloquent commentary on awakening for awakening itself.

56. 兜率悅張無盡 — Doushuai Congyue and Zhang Wujin

A brief account of a Song-dynasty chancellor's awakening under a Chan master unafraid to risk his own position to push him hard.

Chinese Source

張無盡將見悅公，悅云：「吾當深錐痛劄此人。」或謂諸官人多喜承順，恐惡發。悅云：「我不過退院而已。」因盡力逼拶，無盡由此了悟。愚謂悅公妙手陶鑄，其賢固不必論，而無盡委身知識，窮參力究，終得發明，真士大夫學道之模範也。

Ru-Yi Translation

When Zhang Wujin was about to visit Master Yue, Yue said: "I must drive the awl in deep and make this man feel real pain." Someone warned him that officials of high rank generally like to be flattered and deferred to, and that he risked provoking anger. Master Yue said: "The worst that can happen is that I lose my abbotship." And so he pressed and pushed relentlessly at Wujin without letup, until Wujin, through this, came to genuine awakening.

I would say: that Master Yue possessed the skillful, masterful hand to forge a student in this way needs no further comment on his own worthiness; and that Wujin was willing to humble himself before a spiritual teacher, and to investigate with all his strength, until he finally attained genuine insight — this truly stands as a model for scholar-officials studying the Way.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhang Wujin (Zhang Shangying, 1043–1122) was an actual Northern Song chancellor and a documented convert to Buddhism, later author of the apologetic work In Defense of the Dharma; Doushuai Congyue's willingness to risk his own abbotship rather than flatter a powerful visitor is precisely what Zhuhong holds up as admirable — a real institutional risk, not merely a rhetorical flourish.

57. 宗門問答 — Chan Question-and-Answer

An extended analogy explaining why the strange, allusive dialogue of classical Chan exchanges should not be casually imitated by later students.

Chinese Source

古尊宿作家相見，其問答機緣，或無義無味，或可驚可疑，或如罵如謔，而皆自真參實悟中來，莫不水乳投、函蓋合，無一字一句浪施也。後人無知效嚬，則口業不小。譬之二同邑人，千里久別，忽然邂逅，相對作鄉語、隱語、諺語，傍人聽之，亦復無義無味，可驚可疑，如罵如謔，而實字字句句皆衷曲之談、肝膈之要也。傍人固不知是何等語，而二人者，則默契如水乳、如函蓋矣。今不如緘口結舌，但向本參上著力，祇愁不悟，不愁悟後無語。

Ru-Yi Translation

When ancient venerable masters, true adepts, met one another, the exchanges of question and answer between them sometimes sounded meaningless and without flavor, sometimes startling or perplexing, sometimes almost like cursing or mockery — and yet all of it arose out of genuine investigation and genuine realization, fitting together like water and milk, like a box and its lid, with not one word or phrase wasted or thrown away. When later people, out of ignorance, imitate such exchanges, the karmic offense of speech they incur is no small thing.

Consider the analogy: two people from the same hometown, long separated and meeting again by chance a thousand miles from home, fall into speaking their local dialect, private allusions, and proverbs together. To a bystander listening in, this too sounds meaningless and without flavor, startling or perplexing, almost like cursing or mockery — and yet, in truth, every single word and phrase of it comes straight from the heart, the innermost truth each has to tell the other. The bystander, of course, has no idea what any of it means — but between the two speakers themselves, the understanding fits together as perfectly as water and milk, as a box and its lid.

People today would do far better to hold their tongues in silence, and simply apply their effort to their own root investigation — worrying only about failing to awaken, never about having nothing left to say once awakening comes.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The hometown-dialect analogy is a rare instance in this collection of Zhuhong constructing his own extended illustrative story from ordinary life, rather than drawing on a historical or scriptural precedent — chosen precisely because it makes the same epistemic point as classical Chan dialogue: meaning fully present to the speakers, entirely opaque to any outside observer.

58. 醉生夢死 — Living Drunk and Dying in a Dream

A short meditation on a common Chinese idiom, arguing it describes rich and poor alike.

Chinese Source

醉生夢死，恆言也，實至言也。世人大約貧賤、富貴二種：貧賤者，固朝忙夕忙以營衣食；富貴者，亦朝忙夕忙以享欲樂，受用不同，其忙一也，忙至死而後已，而心未已也。齎此心以往，而復生，而復忙，而復死，死生生死，昏昏蒙蒙，如醉如夢，經百千劫，曾無了期。朗然獨醒，大丈夫當如是矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

"Living drunk and dying in a dream" is a common saying, and yet it is also, in truth, the highest kind of truth. People of the world fall roughly into two kinds: the poor and lowly, and the rich and eminent. The poor and lowly are, of course, busy from morning to night simply to secure food and clothing; but the rich and eminent are equally busy from morning to night, in their case to enjoy sensual pleasure. What each enjoys differs, but their busyness is exactly the same — busy right up until death, and even then the mind itself is not yet finished. Carrying this same mind forward, it is reborn, and grows busy all over again, and dies all over again — dying and being born, being born and dying, muddled and confused, as if drunk, as if dreaming, through hundreds and thousands of kalpas, with never an end in sight. To stand out clear-eyed and alone awake amid all this — a truly great man ought to be exactly this!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The entry deliberately refuses the usual moralizing move of blaming the wealthy for their busyness and excusing the poor — both, Zhuhong insists, are equally caught, only the object of the busyness differs.

59. 真道人難 — A Genuine Person of the Way Is Hard to Find

A short, cascading statistic on how rare sustained, unwavering practice actually is.

Chinese Source

凡人造業者百，而為善者一二。為善者百，而向道者一二。向道者百，而堅久者一二。堅久者百，而堅之又堅、久之又久，直至菩提，心不退轉者一二。如是最後，名真道人。難乎哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

Of a hundred ordinary people who create karma, only one or two do good. Of a hundred who do good, only one or two turn toward the Way. Of a hundred who turn toward the Way, only one or two persevere steadily and long. Of a hundred who persevere steadily and long, only one or two persevere steadfastly still further, and longer still, all the way to bodhi, with a mind that never turns back. Only these last may truly be called a "genuine person of the Way." How difficult it is!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A short entry whose structure is its whole argument: four successive one-in-a-hundred filters, compounding into odds of roughly one in one hundred million by the time a "genuine person of the Way" emerges.

60. 空所空盡 — Emptiness of the Very Emptying, Exhausted

A precise doctrinal correction of an attempted equation between a line from a Daoist scripture and a stage described in the Śūraṅgama Sūtra.

Chinese Source

或曰：老子清靜經云「觀空亦空，空無所空」等語，即楞嚴「空所空盡」之義。予謂：楞嚴初云「動靜二相，了然不生」，今以清靜名經，是動相不生而靜相猶生也。靜且未空，尚何論空空？

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone said: "Does not Laozi's Scripture of Clarity and Stillness say, 'observing emptiness is itself empty, and even emptiness has nothing left to be emptied' — and is this not the very meaning of the Śūraṅgama's 'emptiness of the very emptying, exhausted'?"

I would say: the Śūraṅgama begins by speaking of the two aspects, movement and stillness, as "utterly clear, and not arising at all." Now, this text you cite is named the Scripture of Clarity and Stillness — meaning the aspect of movement has ceased to arise, but the aspect of stillness has not. If stillness itself has not yet even been emptied, what is there left to say about emptying emptiness itself?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A tightly argued instance of Zhuhong's recurring method (compare entries 60–62 in this batch as a loose trio, and entries 47–49 in Part 2) for handling superficially similar-sounding claims across traditions: rather than dismissing the comparison outright, he shows precisely where the Daoist text's own chosen name — "clarity and stillness" — betrays that it has stopped one stage short of the Buddhist teaching's full depth.

61. 教外別傳 — A Separate Transmission Outside the Teachings

A resolution of an apparent contradiction in Chan's own claim to transmit awakening "outside the scriptures," using the classic finger-and-moon analogy.

Chinese Source

或謂：「教外果有別傳乎？則一代時教閒文也。教外果無別傳乎？則祖師西來虛行也。」曰：教外實有別傳，而亦實無別傳也。圓覺不云乎：「修多羅如標月指。」指非月也，謂指外別有月可也；而月正在所指中，謂指外別無月亦可也。執指為月，謂更無月者，愚也。違其所指，而別求所謂月者，狂也。神而明之，存乎其人而已。

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone said: "If there truly is a separate transmission outside the teachings, then the whole body of the Buddha's own scriptural teaching becomes so much irrelevant text. But if there truly is no separate transmission outside the teachings, then the Patriarch's long journey west from India was made for nothing."

I would say: there truly is a separate transmission outside the teachings, and there truly is no separate transmission outside the teachings, both at once. Does the Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment not say: "The sutras are like a finger pointing at the moon"? The finger is not the moon — one may say the moon exists apart from the finger; and yet the moon lies exactly in the direction the finger points — one may equally say there is no moon apart from what the finger indicates. To cling to the finger as if it were the moon, and claim there is no other moon at all, is simply foolish. To turn away from what the finger indicates, and go searching for some "moon" somewhere else entirely, is simply reckless. To grasp this with genuine clarity depends, in the end, entirely on the person.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The finger-pointing-at-the-moon image recurs across the Śūraṅgama, Laṅkāvatāra, and Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment, and became one of the most widely used analogies in Chinese Buddhist hermeneutics precisely because it resolves this exact tension — between scripture as indispensable guide and scripture as something ultimately to be seen through.

62. 發真歸元 — Manifesting Truth and Returning to the Source

A precise doctrinal rebuttal of a popular claim that the Confucian "Mean" of the Doctrine of the Mean is equivalent to the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's description of full awakening.

Chinese Source

楞嚴云：「一人發真歸元，十方虛空悉皆消殞。」而中庸以喜怒哀樂未發為中，既而曰：「致中則天地位。」會通儒釋者，謂中即真元也。然歸元則世界消，致中則世界立，胡因同果異如此？蓋喜怒哀樂，屬乎意根，第六識耳。今止意識不行，尚餘末那賴耶，洪濤息而微波在也；曾未歸元，如何得虛空消殞？

Ru-Yi Translation

The Śūraṅgama Sūtra says: "When a single person manifests truth and returns to the source, the empty space of the ten directions entirely dissolves and vanishes." Meanwhile the Doctrine of the Mean takes the state before joy, anger, sorrow, and delight have arisen as "the Mean," and then goes on to say: "Once the Mean is fully realized, heaven and earth find their proper places." Those fond of reconciling Confucianism and Buddhism claim that this "Mean" is the same as the Buddhist "true source." And yet: returning to the source dissolves the entire world, while fully realizing the Mean establishes the entire world — how can the same cause produce such utterly opposite effects?

The truth is that joy, anger, sorrow, and delight belong to the mental sense-faculty — the sixth consciousness, and nothing more. To halt only the sixth consciousness from operating still leaves the seventh, manas, and the eighth, the storehouse consciousness, entirely untouched — it is as if a great wave has subsided while the small ripples remain. One has not yet returned to the source at all — how, then, could empty space possibly dissolve and vanish?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry applies Yogācāra's eight-consciousness model (culminating in the ālayavijñāna, the "storehouse consciousness" already introduced in entry 38 of Part 2) as a precision instrument against a popular syncretist claim — the same basic move Zhuhong used against Wang Yangming's "innate knowing" and Yang Jian's "vital spirit" in entries 47–49 of Part 2, now aimed at a different, still more ambitious equation.

63. 道話 — Talk of the Way

A brief lament for a vanished culture of serious spiritual conversation among practitioners.

Chinese Source

古之學者，賓主相見，纔入門，便以此一大事因緣遞相研究。今群居雜談，率多世諦，漫遊千里，靡涉參詢。遐哉古風，不可復矣！嗟夫！

Ru-Yi Translation

In ancient times, when a student and teacher first met, the moment the student had entered through the gate, the two of them would take up this one great matter and investigate it together. Today, when people gather, their idle talk is for the most part all worldly chatter — even a journey of a thousand miles brings no occasion for genuine spiritual inquiry. How distant that ancient manner of practice now seems — never to be recovered again! Alas!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

One of the shortest entries in this batch, and a direct companion to entry 52 above — both mourn the same displacement of serious practice by ordinary secular concerns, one among monks specifically, this one among practitioners more broadly.

64. 楚失弓 — The King of Chu's Lost Bow

A classical anecdote pushed through three successive levels of Buddhist non-attachment, followed by a four-part personal account (湯厄一–四, "the scalding ordeal") of Zhuhong's own accident with boiling water and the hard lesson it taught him about the difference between theoretical and tested equanimity — among the most candid and personal writing in the entire collection.

楚失弓 — The King of Chu's Lost Bow

Chinese Source

楚王失弓，左右欲求之。王曰：「楚人失弓，楚人得之，何必求也？」仲尼曰：「惜乎其不廣也。胡不曰：人遺弓，人得之，何必楚也。」大矣哉！楚王固滄海之胸襟，而仲尼實乾坤之度量也。雖然，仲尼姑就楚王言之，而未盡其所欲言也。何也？尚不能忘情於弓也。進之則王失弓，王猶故也，無失也；假令王復得弓，王猶故也，無得也。雖然，猶未也，尚不能忘情於我也。又進之，求其所謂我者不可得，安求其所謂弓也、人也、楚也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The King of Chu lost his bow, and his attendants wanted to search for it. The King said: "A man of Chu lost the bow, and a man of Chu will find it — why bother searching?" Confucius, hearing of this, remarked: "A pity his generosity was not broader still. Why did he not simply say: a man lost the bow, and a man will find it — why must it be a man of Chu?" How magnificent! The King of Chu already possessed a heart as vast as the ocean, and yet Confucius's own capacity was vaster still, as vast as heaven and earth.

And yet — Confucius was, in this remark, speaking only in terms set by the King's own words, and had not yet said everything there was to say. Why? Because he, too, had not yet let go of his attachment to the bow itself. Take it a step further: whether the King loses the bow or not, the King remains exactly the King he was — nothing is lost. Suppose the King recovers the bow: the King remains exactly the King he was — nothing is gained either. And yet even this is not enough, for one still has not let go of attachment to the "self." Take it further still: seek for this so-called "self," and it cannot be found at all — how, then, could one go on seeking the so-called bow, or the man, or Chu itself?

湯厄一 — The Scalding Ordeal, First Note

Chinese Source

辛丑孟春十日，予隨例入浴，失足沸湯中，從踵及股。既而調治乖方，踰兩月而後愈。雖備歷諸苦，而於苦中，照見平日過咎，生大慚愧，發菩提心。蓋平日四大無恙，行坐隨意，眠起隨意，飲食隨意，談笑隨意，不知其為人天大福也。安享此福，無復思念六道眾生。且我此一餉安樂時，地獄眾生，挫燒舂磨者，不知經幾許苦矣！餓鬼眾生，飲銅食血者，不知經幾許苦矣！畜生眾生，銜鐵負鞍，刀割鼎烹者，不知經幾許苦矣！縱得為人，而饑寒逼迫者，服役疲勞者，疾病纏綿者，眷屬分離者，刑罰責治者，牢獄監禁者，徵輸困乏者，水溺火焚而死者，蛇螫虎囓而死者，含冤負枉而死者，其苦亦不知幾許，而我弗知也。自今以後，得一餉安樂，即當思念六道苦惱眾生，攝心正意，願早成道果，普濟含識，俾齊生淨土，得不退轉。剎那自肆，何以上報佛恩，而下酬檀信也。勵之哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

On the tenth day of the first month of the xinchou year [1601], I went, as customary, to bathe, and lost my footing in the boiling water — scalded from heel to thigh. The treatment that followed was mishandled, and it took more than two months before I recovered. Though I underwent every kind of suffering, in the midst of that suffering I came to see clearly the faults of my ordinary daily life, and felt great shame, and gave rise to the bodhi-mind.

I realized that, in ordinary times, when my body was free of illness — walking and sitting as I pleased, sleeping and rising as I pleased, eating and drinking as I pleased, talking and laughing as I pleased — I had never once recognized this as the great good fortune of a human or heavenly being. I simply enjoyed this good fortune without ever once thinking of the beings of the six realms. And in that very moment when I was enjoying a brief spell of ease, the hell-beings, being crushed and burned and pounded and ground, were undergoing who knows how much suffering; the hungry ghosts, drinking molten copper and eating blood, were undergoing who knows how much suffering; the animals, bearing bit and saddle, cut apart by the knife and boiled in the cauldron, were undergoing who knows how much suffering. And even among those born human: those pressed by hunger and cold, those exhausted by forced labor, those bound by lingering illness, those separated from their families, those punished under the law, those imprisoned, those crushed by unpayable taxes, those who drowned or burned to death, those bitten to death by snakes or mauled by tigers, those who died bearing unjust accusations — their suffering, too, is beyond counting, and I had never once known of it.

From now on, whenever I enjoy even a brief spell of ease, I will think at once of the suffering beings of the six realms, gather in my mind and set my intention right, and vow to attain the fruit of the path swiftly, so as to save all sentient beings universally, that they may all be reborn together in the Pure Land and reach the stage of non-retrogression. To indulge myself even for an instant — how could that possibly repay the Buddha's kindness above, or the faithful donors' generosity below? Let me strive at this!

湯厄二 — The Scalding Ordeal, Second Note

Chinese Source

佛言人命在呼吸間，予平日亦常舉此以警策大眾，而實未嘗身親經歷之也。及予之罹湯厄也。方其入浴，身安心泰，洋洋自如，俄而蹈沸釜中，幾死矣！其得生者，幸也，龍天救之也。夫為時剎那耳，而死生係焉。命在呼吸，豈不誠然乎哉！則知為僧者，於佛所說以勸他人恆切，而以勸自己或疏，通弊也。予於是大愧大駭而大自戢。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said that a human life lasts no longer than a single breath, and I myself have often used this very saying to warn and spur on the assembly — yet I had never actually experienced it in my own body. Then came my scalding ordeal. Just as I entered the bath, body at ease and mind untroubled, entirely carefree — and then, all at once, I stepped into the boiling cauldron, and very nearly died! That I survived was pure good fortune — the dragon and heavenly guardians saved me. The whole thing took no more than an instant, and yet life and death hung upon that instant. "Life lasts no longer than a breath" — how could this not be exactly, literally true?

From this I came to understand that we monks, in urging others with the Buddha's own teaching, are always most earnest and urgent — but in applying that same teaching to warn ourselves, we grow careless. This is a common failing. Over this I felt deep shame, deep alarm, and resolved to restrain and discipline myself with all my strength.

湯厄三 — The Scalding Ordeal, Third Note

Chinese Source

予平日論到病中做工夫處，亦知畢陵伽婆蹉所謂「純覺遺身」矣；亦知馬大師所謂「有不病者」矣；亦知永嘉所謂「縱遇風刀常坦坦，假饒毒藥也閒閒」矣；亦知肇公所謂「四大本空，五蘊非有」矣。及乎足入沸湯，從頭簡點，痛覺在身，誰是遺身者？我今受病，誰是不病者？鋒刀毒藥切於肌膚，誰是坦坦、閒閒者？四大五蘊實為吾身，實為吾累，誰是本空、非有者？乃知平日「乾慧」都不濟事。若無定力，甘伏死門，彼口頭三昧，祇自瞞耳。噫！可不勉歟

Ru-Yi Translation

In ordinary times, when discussing how to practice while sick, I knew well enough Pilindavatsa's teaching of "pure awareness that leaves the body behind"; I knew well enough Great Master Ma's saying about "the one who does not fall ill"; I knew well enough Yongjia's line, "even meeting the wind's blade, ever calm and untroubled; even given poison, still perfectly at ease"; I knew well enough Master Zhao's teaching that "the four elements are fundamentally empty, the five aggregates are fundamentally without existence."

But once my foot had actually plunged into the boiling water, and I checked each of these, one by one, from the very start: the pain was plainly present in my own body — who, then, was the one who had "left the body behind"? I was, at that very moment, actually suffering illness — who, then, was "the one who does not fall ill"? The pain of the scalding cut into my flesh like a blade, like poison — who, then, was "ever calm," "perfectly at ease"? The four elements and five aggregates were, in that moment, quite genuinely my own body, and quite genuinely the source of my suffering — who, then, was "fundamentally empty," "fundamentally without existence"? Only then did I understand: "dry wisdom" — knowledge without the water of genuine realization behind it — is entirely useless when the moment actually comes.

Without real meditative power, one is simply left to submit meekly at death's own gate; and all that so-called "samādhi" spoken only from the mouth is nothing but self-deception. Ah — how can one not strive with all one's might!

湯厄四 — The Scalding Ordeal, Fourth Note

Chinese Source

予見屠酤之肆，生置鼈鱔蝦蟹之屬於釜中，而以百沸湯烹之，則諭之曰：「彼眾生力弗汝敵，又微劣不能作聲耳！若力敵，則當如虎豹噉汝。若能作聲，冤號酸楚之聲，當震動大千世界。汝縱逃現報，而千萬劫中，彼諸眾生，不放汝在。汝試以一臂納沸湯中，少頃而出，則知之矣。」今不意此報乃我當之。因思自少至老，雖不作此業，而無量生來，既宿命未通，安保其不作也。乃不怨不尤，安意忍受，而益勤修其所未至。

Ru-Yi Translation

I once saw, at a butcher's stall or a tavern, live turtles, eels, shrimp, and crabs thrown into a pot and boiled alive in scalding water, and I would admonish the people there: "Those creatures cannot fight back against you, and are too weak and small even to cry out! If they were a match for you in strength, they would devour you exactly as a tiger or leopard would. And if they could cry out, their screams of anguish and pain would shake the entire universe. Even if you escape retribution for it in this present life, across countless thousands of kalpas to come, those creatures will not let you go. Try it yourself: plunge one arm into boiling water for just a moment, and you will understand exactly what it feels like."

I never expected that this very retribution would one day fall on me. And so I thought it through: from youth to old age, though I myself had not committed this particular karma directly, across countless past lives — since I have not yet attained knowledge of my former existences — how could I possibly be certain I never committed it? And so, without resentment or complaint, I accepted the suffering calmly, and resolved to apply myself all the more diligently to whatever I had not yet attained.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The dating in 湯厄一 (1601) places this accident when Zhuhong was around sixty-six years old, already the established abbot of Yunqi and author of most of his major works — making the four-part account all the more striking as a piece of unguarded self-examination from someone who had spent decades teaching others exactly the equanimity he found, under real pain, impossible to summon. 湯厄三's distinction between "dry wisdom" (乾慧, intellectual understanding without the "water" of realized meditative attainment) and genuine samādhi is the entry's sharpest point, and 湯厄四 closes the sequence by turning the suffering into confirmation of the very karmic argument Zhuhong had used for years to admonish others — see entry 6 in Part 1 on shellfish, and the vegetarian polemics in entries 44 and elsewhere, for the argument he had made from the other side of the pot.

65. 經教 — Sutras and Teachings

A defense of scriptural study against two opposite forms of anti-textual pride — in Chan and in Pure Land practice alike.

Chinese Source

有自負參禪者，輒云達磨不立文字，見性則休。有自負念佛者，輒云止貴直下有人，何必經典。此二輩人有真得而作是語者，且不必論；亦有實無所得而漫言之者，大都不通教理而護惜其短者也。予一生崇尚念佛，然勤勤懇懇勸人看教。何以故？念佛之說，何自來乎？非金口所宣，明載簡冊，今日眾生，何由而知十萬億剎之外有阿彌陀也？其參禪者，藉口教外別傳，不知離教而參，是邪因也；離教而悟，是邪解也。饒汝參而得悟，必須以教印證；不與教合，悉邪也。是故學儒者，必以六經四子為權衡；學佛者，必以三藏十二部為模楷。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are those who pride themselves on Chan practice, and habitually say: "Bodhidharma did not establish words and letters — once you see your own nature, that is the end of it." There are those who pride themselves on Buddha-recitation, and habitually say: "All that matters is having real conviction right here and now — why bother with scriptures at all?" If people of either kind speak this way because they have genuinely attained something, there is nothing more to discuss; but there are also those who have truly attained nothing at all and simply say such things carelessly — and these, for the most part, are people who do not understand the teachings and are merely protecting their own shortcomings.

I myself have devoted my whole life to venerating Buddha-recitation, and yet I urge people, earnestly and persistently, to study the teachings as well. Why? Where, after all, does the teaching of Buddha-recitation itself come from? Had it not been proclaimed by the Buddha's own golden mouth, and clearly recorded in the scriptures, how would sentient beings today ever know that, a hundred thousand million buddha-lands beyond our own, there exists an Amitābha Buddha at all?

As for those who pride themselves on Chan, using "a separate transmission outside the teachings" as an excuse — they do not realize that to practice apart from the teachings is a deviant cause, and to awaken apart from the teachings is a deviant understanding. Even granting that your practice genuinely leads to awakening, it must still be verified against the teachings; if it does not accord with the teachings, it is deviant, through and through. This is why students of Confucianism must take the Six Classics and the Four Books as their standard, and students of Buddhism must take the Tripiṭaka and its Twelve Divisions as their model.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhuhong deliberately criticizes his own side (Pure Land practitioners dismissing scripture) with the same force as the opposing side (Chan practitioners citing "separate transmission" as an excuse), a pattern of even-handedness that recurs throughout this collection.

66. 語錄 — Recorded Sayings

A firm instruction to his own disciples: do not compile his casual remarks into a formal book of "recorded sayings."

Chinese Source

古人道明德立，足為人天師表，然後有語錄垂世。大率有二：或門人所記，如六祖壇經之類是也。或手自作之，如中峰廣錄之類是也。我實凡夫，自救不了，為吾徒者，慎勿筆吾一時偶爾之談，刊為語錄。不唯妄自尊大，又偶爾之談，或有為而發，或因人而施，未是究竟了義，而況聽者草草入耳，便形諸紙墨，亦恐有誤人之過也。

Ru-Yi Translation

In ancient times, only once a person's understanding was complete and their virtue firmly established, fit to serve as a teacher to humans and gods alike, would their recorded sayings be handed down to later generations. Broadly speaking, such records are of two kinds: some are recorded by disciples, such as the Platform Sūtra of the Sixth Patriarch; others are written by the masters themselves, such as the Extensive Record of Zhongfeng. I am, in truth, an ordinary unenlightened person, unable even to save myself — let those who follow me take care never to write down some passing remark of mine and publish it as a "recorded sayings." Doing so would not only be a matter of unwarranted self-importance; a remark made in passing may also have been spoken for a particular occasion, or tailored to a particular person, and may not represent any final, complete teaching at all — and how much more so when the listener has heard it only carelessly, in passing, before setting it down in ink on paper. I fear this could do real harm, and mislead people.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

An instructive irony: Zhuhong's own instruction survives precisely as a "recorded saying" — an entry in Zhuchuang Suibi itself, the very kind of casual jotting he is warning his disciples not to formalize.

67. 聞謗 — Hearing Slander

A layered response to a stock Buddhist teaching on the unreality of insulting speech, extended to answer the harder case of slander preserved in writing.

Chinese Source

經言：「人之謗我也，出初一字時，後字未生；出後一字時，初字已滅。是乃風氣鼓動，全無真實。若因此發瞋，則鵲噪鴉鳴，皆應發瞋矣！」其說甚妙。而或謂：「設彼作為謗書，則一覽之下，字字具足，又永存不滅，將何法以破之？」獨不思白者是紙，黑者是墨，何者是謗？況一字一字，皆從篇韻湊合而成，然則置一部篇韻在案，是百千萬億謗書，無時不現前也。何惑之甚也！雖然，此猶是對治法門；若知我空，誰受謗者？

Ru-Yi Translation

A sutra says: "When someone slanders me — by the time the second word of the abuse is uttered, the first word is already gone; and when the first word was uttered, the second word had not yet come into being. It is nothing more than air stirred by wind — utterly without substance. If one gives rise to anger over this, then one ought to grow angry, too, at the chattering of magpies or the cawing of crows!" This teaching is truly marvelous.

But someone might object: "Suppose the slander is written down in a letter — then at a single glance, every word stands complete before you, and it persists indefinitely, never disappearing. What method could possibly overcome that?" Have you never considered: the white is merely paper, and the black merely ink — where, exactly, is the "slander" in that? And besides, every single character is only assembled from the characters found in some dictionary or rhyme-book — if you count these black marks on white paper as slander, then a single dictionary left open on your desk would already contain a hundred thousand million slanderous letters, present before you at every moment! How could you possibly be so confused?

Even so, this is still only a provisional remedy. If one truly understands the emptiness of the self, then who, exactly, is left to receive the slander at all?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The sutra cited is the Upāsaka Precepts Sūtra; Zhuhong builds three progressively deeper responses — the original impermanence argument for spoken slander, his own extension of it (via the paper-and-ink analogy) to written slander, and finally the deepest Buddhist response of all, the emptiness of the self that would need to be defended in the first place.

68. 愚之愚 — The Foolishness Within Foolishness

A short piece distinguishing ordinary ignorance from a deeper, more dangerous kind found among the highly educated.

Chinese Source

世人以不識字、不解事為愚，此誠愚也，非愚之愚也。讀盡五車書，無字不曉；收盡萬般巧，無事不能；乃至談玄說禪，靡不通貫。而究其真實處，顛倒迷惑，反見笑於向之所謂愚者，非愚中之愚而何？

Ru-Yi Translation

The world takes illiteracy and ignorance of practical affairs to be foolishness — and this is indeed a kind of foolishness, though not the deepest kind. There are people who have read through libraries of books, with not a single character unfamiliar to them; who have mastered every conceivable skill, with not a single task beyond their ability; who can discourse fluently on the most profound and subtle matters of Chan. And yet, when it comes to the truly essential matter, they remain confused and deluded through and through, and end up mocked by the very people they once dismissed as ignorant. If this is not foolishness within foolishness, what is?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A companion piece to entry 38 in Part 2 ("The Sutras Must Be Read") and entry 23 in Part 1 ("Worldly Wisdom Must Still Be Awakened") — all three target the same gap between erudition and realization from slightly different angles.

69. 預了 — Settling Things in Advance

A brief exhortation aimed specifically at the elderly, on the urgency of settling one's affairs before death arrives.

Chinese Source

無常迅速，雖老少無別。然年少人猶處未定之天，妄冀長壽；若老年人，則定然光景無多矣！須把身世事處分了當，從他無常朝到暮到，撒手便行，無所繫累。此晚境大要緊處，不可忽！不可忽！

Ru-Yi Translation

Impermanence strikes swiftly, and makes no distinction between the young and the old. And yet a young person still has an undetermined span of years ahead, and can foolishly hope for a long life; an old person, by contrast, quite certainly has little time left at all. One must settle the great matters of body and worldly affairs properly and in good order, so that whenever impermanence arrives — this morning or this evening, it makes no difference — one can simply let go and depart, with nothing left binding one down. This is the single most crucial task of one's later years. Do not neglect it! Do not neglect it!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhuhong himself was in his sixties or older through much of the period this collection was written, and several entries in this batch (69, and the 湯厄 sequence above) read as increasingly personal reflections from an aging author rather than general instruction.

70. 廣覽 — Reading Widely

An extended medical analogy arguing that clinging to a single sutra passage, out of context, produces exactly the same danger as a doctor who knows only one prescription.

Chinese Source

看經須是周遍廣博，方得融貫，不致偏執。蓋經有此處建立，彼處掃蕩；此處掃蕩，彼處建立；隨時逐機，無定法故。假使只看楞嚴，見勢至不入圓通，而不廣覽稱讚淨土諸經，便謂念佛法門不足尚矣。只看達磨對梁帝語，見功德不在作福，而不廣覽六度萬行諸經，便謂有為福德皆可廢矣！反而觀之，執淨土非禪宗，執有為非無為，亦復如是。喻如讀醫書不廣者，但見治寒用桂附而斥芩連，治虛用參耆而斥枳朴，不知芩連枳朴亦有時當用，而桂附參耆亦有時當斥也。是故執醫之一方者誤色身，執經之一義者誤慧命。予嘗謂六祖壇經不可使無智人觀之，正慮其執此而廢彼也。

Ru-Yi Translation

In studying the sutras, one must read broadly and comprehensively before one can achieve genuine synthesis, without falling into one-sided attachment. This is because the sutras sometimes affirm and establish a teaching in one place, and sweep it away and negate it in another; establish it here, and sweep it away there — adapting always to the moment and to the capacity of the listener, with no single fixed method.

Suppose a person reads only the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, and sees there that Mahāsthāmaprāpta's method of Buddha-recitation is not the one chosen for our world as the gateway to perfect penetration — and then, without going on to read broadly among the various sutras praising the Pure Land, concludes that the Buddha-recitation method is simply not worth honoring! Or suppose a person reads only Bodhidharma's remark to Emperor Wu of Liang, and sees there that merit does not lie in performing good works — and then, without going on to read broadly among the many sutras on the six perfections and the myriad practices, concludes that all conditioned works of merit may simply be discarded! Turn it around, and the same error appears: clinging to the Pure Land teaching in order to attack Chan, or clinging to conditioned practice in order to attack the unconditioned — these come to exactly the same thing.

It is like a doctor who has not read broadly in medicine: seeing only that cinnamon bark and aconite are used to treat cold conditions, he condemns scutellaria and coptis outright; seeing only that ginseng and astragalus are used to treat deficiency conditions, he condemns immature bitter orange and magnolia bark outright — not realizing that scutellaria, coptis, bitter orange, and magnolia bark, too, each have their proper occasion for use, just as cinnamon, aconite, ginseng, and astragalus each have their occasion to be set aside instead. This is why a doctor who clings to a single prescription endangers the physical body, and a student of the sutras who clings to a single teaching endangers the spiritual life. I have often said that the Platform Sūtra of the Sixth Patriarch should never be handed carelessly to someone without real wisdom — precisely because I fear such a person will cling to that one text and neglect all the rest of the teaching.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The two source texts Zhuhong names — the Śūraṅgama's ranking of the twenty-five methods of perfect penetration (with Avalokiteśvara's ear-faculty method chosen as foremost for this world, and Mahāsthāmaprāpta's Buddha-recitation method notably not singled out) and Bodhidharma's famous exchange with Emperor Wu of Liang denying that building temples earns "merit" — are both real and frequently cited by opponents of Pure Land practice; Zhuhong's medical analogy directly answers both without denying either passage's authenticity.

71. 求人過 — Hunting for Other People's Faults

A brief condemnation of reflexive fault-finding directed at people or writings that have earned a good reputation.

Chinese Source

見人飭躬立德，名稱頗聞，便多方求覓其過，此忌心也，薄道也。或見人有所著述，其求過也亦然。不知聞一善行，覽一好書，皆當隨喜讚歎；而反掩之滅之，是誠何心哉？若果行係偽行，書係邪書，自應正言公論，明斥其非，又不當半褒半譏，依阿進退。

Ru-Yi Translation

When people see someone who disciplines himself and establishes real virtue, gaining a good reputation for it, they often go to great lengths, by every means available, to dig up his faults — this is simple jealousy, a mean-spirited way of behaving. The same happens when people see that someone has written something worthwhile: they go hunting for its faults in exactly the same way. They fail to realize that on hearing of one good deed, or reading one good book, one ought simply to rejoice and offer praise — instead, they try to bury it and destroy it. What kind of heart does that come from?

If the conduct in question really is false, or the book really is heretical, then one should of course state one's judgment plainly and openly, and clearly point out the fault — but even then, one should not go half-praising and half-mocking, bending this way and that to suit one's own convenience.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A short entry with an unusually direct closing standard: criticism itself is not the fault Zhuhong condemns — mixed, evasive criticism, hedged to avoid taking a real position, is.

72. 謀斷 — Planning and Deciding

A rare piece of direct self-criticism, using a famous pair of Tang chancellors to diagnose Zhuhong's own recurring failure to act decisively.

Chinese Source

古稱玄齡善謀，如晦善斷。蓋謀與斷當兼備而不可一缺者。予於事，多有見之極明，而持之不武，以此致誤，常悔之恨之。故禪門貴悲智雙足。而謀與斷，俱智所攝。謀而乏斷，正能見而不能持也，此終是智淺而不深，偏而不全耳。大宜勉旃！

Ru-Yi Translation

The ancients said that Fang Xuanling was skilled at planning, and Du Ruhui was skilled at deciding — and truly, planning and deciding must go together; neither can be missing. In my own dealings with things, I have often seen a matter with perfect clarity, and yet failed to carry it through with the necessary resolve — and this has led me into error more than once, which I have often regretted and been pained by ever since.

This is why the Chan tradition prizes compassion and wisdom in equal measure — and both planning and deciding belong, in fact, to the domain of wisdom. To have the capacity to plan without the capacity to decide is exactly my own fault: the ability to see clearly without the ability to carry it through. This, in the end, comes down to wisdom that is shallow rather than deep, one-sided rather than whole. I must strive all the harder!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Fang Xuanling and Du Ruhui were the two chancellors most credited with the successful administration of the early Tang dynasty under Emperor Taizong, remembered as an inseparable pair precisely because their strengths complemented each other; Zhuhong's use of them here to diagnose his own personal weakness is a rare moment of unguarded self-assessment, comparable to the 湯厄 sequence above.

73. 禪佛相爭 — Chan and Buddha-Recitation in Dispute

A staged dialogue in which an outsider — a traveling actor — resolves a sectarian dispute between two arguing monks with an analogy drawn from his own profession.

Chinese Source

二僧遇諸途，一參禪，一念佛。參禪者謂本來無佛，無可念者，佛之一字，吾不喜聞。念佛者謂西方有佛，號阿彌陀，憶佛念佛，必定見佛。執有執無，爭論不已。有少年過而聽焉，曰：「兩君所言，皆徐六擔板耳。」二僧叱曰：「爾俗士也，安知佛法？」少年曰：「吾誠俗士，然以俗士為喻而知佛法也。吾，梨園子也。於戲場中，或為君，或為臣，或為男，或為女，或為善人，或為惡人。而求其所謂君臣男女善惡者，以為有，則實無；以為無，則實有。蓋有是即無而有，無是即有而無，有無俱非真，而我則湛然常住也。知我常住，何以爭為？」二僧無對。

Ru-Yi Translation

Two monks met on the road, one a Chan practitioner, the other a practitioner of Buddha-recitation. The Chan monk said: "Fundamentally there is no Buddha, and nothing to recite — I do not even like to hear the single word 'Buddha' spoken." The Buddha-recitation monk said: "In the west there is a Buddha, named Amitābha — recollect the Buddha, recite the Buddha's name, and one will surely see the Buddha." One clung to existence, the other to non-existence, and the argument went on without end.

A young man happened to pass by and overhear them, and said: "What you two gentlemen are saying is nothing but the talk of a plank-carrier — seeing only what is directly in front, never the whole picture." The two monks scolded him: "You are just an ordinary layman — what could you possibly understand of the Dharma?" The young man said: "I am indeed an ordinary man, but I can understand something of the Dharma even from an ordinary man's experience. I am an actor in a theater troupe. On stage, I sometimes play a ruler, sometimes a minister, sometimes a man, sometimes a woman, sometimes a good person, sometimes a villain. If you go looking for the 'ruler,' the 'minister,' the 'man,' the 'woman,' the 'good,' or the 'villain' as something truly, substantially there, in fact there is nothing there at all; and yet if you say there is nothing there, in fact something is there, on the stage. This 'existence' is existence precisely by virtue of non-existence; this 'non-existence' is non-existence precisely by virtue of existence. Neither existence nor non-existence is really true — only I myself remain, clear and unmoved, constantly present throughout. Once you know this constant 'I' that remains, what is there left to argue about?" The two monks had nothing to say.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"The talk of a plank-carrier" (徐六擔板) is a stock Chan idiom for one-sided vision — a laborer carrying a plank on his shoulder can see straight ahead but nothing to either side. The young actor's resolution does not side with either monk's position (Buddha exists / Buddha does not exist) but dissolves the dispute itself by pointing to the deeper "constant self" underlying both roles — structurally similar to the resolution Zhuhong offers for the Confucian-Buddhist terminology disputes in entries 47–49 and 60–62.

74. 武夷圖 — The Wuyi Mountains Painting

A reflection prompted by a gift received during illness, on why a painting of a real earthly landscape can move a sick viewer more immediately than any image of the Pure Land.

Chinese Source

予病中有贈以武夷九曲圖者，閱之忻然。因思古人沈疴不起，一友教翫輞川圖，不浹旬而愈；況西方極樂世界，繪畫流布，朝夕參禮而未聞奇驗速效如輞川者何耶？良由輞川蹟在寰中，易為描寫；極樂境超世外，難以形容，則不若繪輞川者之備極工巧，聳人心目故也。彼雞頭摩之所傳、十六觀經之所說，亦略示其概而已。夫極樂世界，忉利、兜率、化樂諸天所不能及其少分，使人得而詳覩，何止四百四病之俱忘，將八萬四千煩惱諸病皆消滅無餘矣！昔人謂神棲安養，又謂先送心歸極樂天，豈徒然哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

While I was ill, someone gave me a painting of the Nine Bends of Wuyi, and I found looking at it a joy. This made me think of an old story: a certain man, gravely and persistently ill, was advised by a friend to gaze at the Wangchuan Painting, and within ten days he had recovered. Yet paintings of the Western Pure Land of Ultimate Bliss are widely circulated, and people pay their respects before them morning and night — why have I never heard of any such miraculous, swift effect as with the Wangchuan Painting?

The reason, surely, is this: the scenery of Wangchuan exists right here in this world, and so is easy to depict; the realm of Ultimate Bliss transcends this world entirely, and is difficult to render in form — so no painting of it can ever achieve the same exquisite, captivating craftsmanship, striking so directly at the heart and eye, as a painting of Wangchuan can. Even the transmission from the Kukkuṭapāda-vihāra monks, and what the Sūtra of the Sixteen Contemplations describes, offer only a rough sketch of that world's outline. In truth, the world of Ultimate Bliss so far surpasses even the heavens of Trāyastriṃśa, Tuṣita, and Nirmāṇarati that those heavens cannot approach even the smallest fraction of it. Were a person able to see it in full detail, it would not merely make one forget the four hundred and four bodily ailments — it would dissolve every one of the eighty-four thousand afflictions of the mind, without remainder. When the ancients spoke of "the spirit dwelling at rest in the Land of Peace and Nurture," or said, "first send the mind home to the Land of Ultimate Bliss" — were these merely empty words?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Wang Wei's Wangchuan Painting, depicting the poet's own estate in Shaanxi, was among the most celebrated landscape paintings of the Tang dynasty; the comparison Zhuhong draws is unusually candid about a limitation of representational art itself, not a limitation of the Pure Land's reality — the Pure Land is harder to paint precisely because it exceeds anything found in this world, not because it is somehow less real. "First send the mind home to the Land of Ultimate Bliss" is attributed to Hanshan Deqing, Zhuhong's own eminent contemporary.

75. 談宗 — Discussing the Chan Tradition

A brief, wry personal anecdote about being teased for having apparently "regressed" from dazzling Chan rhetoric to plain Pure Land devotion.

Chinese Source

予未出家時，乍閱宗門語，便以情識模擬，與一座主書，左縱右橫，座主憚焉。出家數年後，重會座主於一宿菴。勞問間，見予專志淨土，語不及宗，矍然曰：「子向日見地超卓，今反卑近，何也？」予笑曰：「諺有之，初生牛犢不畏虎。識法者懼，君知之乎？」座主不答。

Ru-Yi Translation

Before I left the household life, I had only just begun reading Chan texts when I started imitating their language out of my own untrained impressions, writing to a certain abbot in a letter full of reckless, unchecked argument, sweeping this way and that without restraint — the abbot was quite alarmed by it. Some years after I became a monk, I happened to meet that same abbot again at an old hermitage. In the course of our polite exchange of greetings, he noticed that I had become entirely devoted to the Pure Land, with no more talk of Chan at all, and said, startled: "Your understanding used to be so far above the ordinary — why has it become so plain and shallow now?" I laughed and said: "There's a saying: a newborn calf is not afraid of the tiger. Once you truly understand the Dharma, you become afraid — did you know that?" The abbot had no reply.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A rare piece of self-mockery: Zhuhong presents his own youthful, "impressive" Chan rhetoric as the thing that ought to have alarmed people, and his later apparent plainness as the real sign of understanding — a pointed answer to anyone who might read his lifelong devotion to Pure Land practice, over the more intellectually flashy Chan tradition, as a step down.
