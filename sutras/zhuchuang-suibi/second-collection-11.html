Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Second Collection, Part 11

Continuing the Second Collection (二筆). This section ranges from the Flower Ornament Sutra's traditional standing as first among all scriptures, to an admiring portrait of a devout mother's unusual dowry, to a careful, balanced statement of how Confucianism and Buddhism divide their proper spheres — and closes with a sharp warning against a bizarre contemporary fad of standing bricked into a wall in the name of meditation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

111. 華嚴大藏一經 — The Flower Ornament Sutra Stands Alone Among All Scriptures

An argument for the Flower Ornament Sutra's traditional supremacy among all Buddhist scriptures, using Xuanzang's own remark to the Tang emperor and an anecdote of a sutra that seemed to return itself to its proper place.

Chinese Source

或問：「經無與《華嚴》等者，何謂也？」曰：「昔玄奘法師譯《般若》六百卷成，以進御。帝云：『《般若》如是浩瀚，何不居《華嚴》之先？』法師謂：『《華嚴》具無量門，《般若》雖多，乃《華嚴》無量門中之一門也。』」有僧作數格供經，《華嚴》供於最上。一日取誦訖，納之中格，明晨經忽在上，僧大驚異。蓋經之威神所致，亦持經者之精誠所感也。且三藏聖教，獨《華嚴》如天王專制宇內，諸侯、公卿、大夫、百執事，以至兆民，皆其所統馭也，夫孰與之等也？

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked: "It is said that no sutra can be compared to the Flower Ornament Sutra — what does this mean?" I answered: "Long ago, Master Xuanzang, having completed his translation of the six-hundred-fascicle Great Prajñāpāramitā Sutra, presented it to the emperor. The emperor said: 'The Great Prajñāpāramitā Sutra is so vast — why should it not be ranked ahead of the Flower Ornament Sutra?' The master answered: 'The Flower Ornament Sutra contains boundless gates of teaching; the Great Prajñāpāramitā Sutra, however extensive, is only a single gate among the Flower Ornament's boundless gates.'" There was once a monk who built a multi-tiered bookcase to hold the sutras, placing the Flower Ornament Sutra on the topmost shelf. One day, after reading and reciting from it, he set it back down on a middle shelf — and the next morning found it had returned to the topmost shelf on its own, to his great astonishment. This is surely the sutra's own spiritual power at work, and also the response drawn forth by the sincerity of whoever recites it. And besides, among the entire Tripiṭaka, the Flower Ornament Sutra alone governs like a heavenly king ruling the whole realm — feudal lords, ministers, officials of every rank, down to the common people, are all subject to its rule — what other sutra could possibly stand as its equal?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Xuanzang's own translation of the Great Prajñāpāramitā Sutra, completed near the very end of his life, remains the single largest text in the Chinese Buddhist canon, occupying roughly a quarter of the entire Prajñāpāramitā section of the Tripiṭaka; Zhuhong had already made the identical "heavenly king and feudal lords" analogy for the Flower Ornament Sutra's supremacy in the First Collection's own entry on the subject, making this a deliberate return to a settled conviction rather than a new argument.

112. 袁母 — Layman Yuan's Mother

An admiring portrait of a devout mother whose unusual choice — bringing a Guanyin image into her own dowry rather than fine clothing — becomes an occasion to praise sincere devotion undeterred by convention or ridicule.

Chinese Source

袁居士母張氏，自幼歸依普門大士甚嚴。其嫁也，奉大士像以俱。孕居士腹中十月，無一日怠緩禮敬。故居士在孩提，即知歸向三寶，蓋所謂胎教也。夫內人之能傾心事佛者，世亦恆有，至於將作新婦，不汲汲以服飾為光華，而供大士於奩具，可謂迥出凡情，耳目所未聞見。昔蘇子瞻繪像南行，葛大夫設像公署，不避嫌刺，識者高之；今袁母者，豈不卓然大丈夫哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

Layman Yuan's mother, of the Zhang family, had taken refuge in the Universal Gate Bodhisattva with great devotion since childhood. When she married, she brought the bodhisattva's image with her into her new household. Throughout the ten months she carried Layman Yuan in her womb, she never once let a single day pass without paying reverent homage to the image — this is exactly what is meant by prenatal education. It is common enough in this world for a wife to turn her heart wholly toward the Buddha — but for a woman on the very eve of her wedding, rather than rushing to adorn herself with fine clothing and ornaments for the occasion, to instead place the bodhisattva's image among her own dowry goods — this surpasses ordinary sentiment entirely, something never before seen or heard. In the past, Su Zizhan carried a painted image with him when banished to the south, and Grand Master Ge displayed a Buddha image openly in his own government office, undeterred by others' mockery — and those with real discernment admired them greatly for it. Is Layman Yuan's mother, then, not also an extraordinary great figure in her own right?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Su Zizhan is Su Dongpo, whose Amitābha portrait carried into southern exile was already discussed for its tragic ending at his own deathbed elsewhere in this project's account of his failed final recitation; Grand Master Ge is Ge Fan, a genuine Song magistrate remembered for openly maintaining a shrine in his own government office and for his own recorded account of forty unbroken years of daily good deeds — Zhuhong's closing question, applying the term "great man" to a woman, deliberately echoes his own definition of that term two entries earlier (entry 105 above).

113. 儒佛配合 — Confucianism and Buddhism, Matched to Their Own Purpose

A careful, balanced statement of how Confucianism and Buddhism divide their proper spheres of governance — worldly order and otherworldly liberation — rejecting both forced identification and false opposition between the two traditions.

Chinese Source

儒佛二教聖人，其設化各有所主，固不必歧而二之，亦不必強而合之。何也？儒主治世，佛主出世。治世，則自應如《大學》格致誠正修齊治平足矣；而過於高深，則綱常倫理不成安立。出世，則自應窮高極深，方成解脫，而於家國天下不無稍疏。蓋理勢自然，無足怪者。若定謂儒即是佛，則六經、《論》、《孟》諸典，璨然備具，何俟釋迦降誕、達磨西來？定謂佛即是儒，則何不以《楞嚴》、《法華》理天下，而必假羲農堯舜創制於其上，孔孟諸賢明道於其下？故二之、合之，其病均也。雖然，圓機之士，二之亦得，合之亦得，兩無病焉，又不可不知也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sages of Confucianism and Buddhism each direct their teaching toward a different purpose — there is no need to treat them as opposed, and no need to force them together as one, either. Why? Confucianism governs this world; Buddhism governs what lies beyond it. For governing this world, it is naturally enough to follow the Great Learning's own program — investigating things, extending knowledge, making the intentions sincere, rectifying the mind, cultivating the person, ordering the family, governing the state, bringing peace to the whole realm — and to go beyond this into excessive depth would leave the fundamental bonds and constant relationships of society without any stable footing at all. For what lies beyond this world, one must naturally exhaust the highest and deepest truth before genuine liberation can be reached — and this necessarily gives somewhat less attention to family, state, and the realm. This is simply the natural course of principle and circumstance, nothing to find strange.

If one insists that Confucianism simply is Buddhism, then the Six Classics, the Analects, and the Mencius already provide everything in full and complete form — why would there be any need at all for Śākyamuni to be born into this world, or for Bodhidharma to come from the West? If one insists that Buddhism simply is Confucianism, then why did no ruler ever govern the realm using the Śūraṅgama Sūtra or the Lotus Sutra, relying instead on the institutions established by Fuxi, Shennong, Yao, and Shun, and the principles set forth by Confucius, Mencius, and other worthies? Treating the two as opposed, and forcing them together as one, are therefore equally mistaken. And yet, for a person of truly all-encompassing capacity, treating them as separate is acceptable, and treating them as unified is acceptable too — neither approach carries any real fault for such a person. This, too, must not go unrecognized.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry gives Zhuhong's own clearest general statement of the Confucian-Buddhist relationship, functioning almost as a summary principle for the many individual case studies scattered across this collection — the Emperor Wu of Liang defense (entry 45), the "trading accusations" essay (entry 43), and the Wang Anshi and Sima Guang critiques (entries 72 and 87) — all of which apply, in specific instances, the same underlying division of labor articulated abstractly here.

114. 立禪 — Standing Meditation

A sharp warning against a bizarre contemporary fad of standing bricked into a wall in imitation of a genuine meditative discipline, whose actual purpose — guarding against drowsiness — had been entirely forgotten by its imitators.

Chinese Source

「立禪」出自《般舟三昧》，蓋精進之極，恐坐則易昏，非以立為道也。而不達此意者，遂有用鐵帶束腰以助僵直，亦可笑矣！近更有砌磚作垣，緊圍其身，植立於中，如劍在匣，而復假此以為募化之資。愚人無識，敬而事之，於是漸有效其所為者。奉勸高明，遇如是人，即應開導，勸之出垣，毋令末法現此魔異，以增僧門之醜。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Standing meditation" derives from the Pratyutpanna Samādhi teaching, meant only to guard against drowsiness during especially intense, sustained practice through sitting — it was never meant to suggest that standing itself constitutes the Way. Those who fail to grasp this point have gone so far as to bind an iron band around their own waist to help hold themselves rigidly upright — which is laughable enough! And more recently still, there are those who build a brick enclosure tightly around their own body and stand upright within it, like a sword sealed inside its own scabbard — and even use this display to solicit donations from others. Ignorant people, lacking any real discernment, revere and serve such a person, and gradually more and more begin imitating this same behavior.

I urge those of genuine wisdom: whenever you encounter such a person, guide them at once, and persuade them to leave their enclosure — do not let this degenerate age produce such bizarre, demonic displays, adding further disgrace to the monastic community.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sutra genuinely prescribes a ninety-day discipline of continuous standing or walking meditation, never sitting or lying down except briefly for meals, with the goal of directly beholding the buddhas of the ten directions — Zhuhong's complaint is not against this demanding but genuine practice, but against a superficial imitation of its outward posture (standing) stripped entirely of its actual method and purpose, turned instead into a spectacle for soliciting donations.

115. 論疏 — Treatises and Commentaries

A defense of scriptural commentary against the fashionable claim that the sutras need no explanation at all, tracing the dismissal of commentary to two underlying faults: laziness and arrogance.

Chinese Source

如來說經，而菩薩造論，後賢製疏，皆所以通經義，而開示眾生使得悟入，厥功大矣！或乃謂佛所說經，本自明顯，不煩註釋，以諸註釋反成晦滯。於是一概撥置，無論優劣，無論凡聖，盡以為不足觀。此其說似是而非，何者？不信傳而信經，是亦知本，但草忽鹵莽，以深經作淺解，則其失非細。是蓋有心病二焉：一者懶病，二者狂病。懶則憚於博究，疲於精思，惟圖省便，不勞心力故。狂則上輕古德，下藐今人，惟恣胸臆，自用自專故。新學無智，靡然樂從，予實憫之，為此苦口。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Tathāgata spoke the sutras; bodhisattvas composed treatises upon them; later worthies wrote commentaries — all of this was meant to make the sutras' meaning accessible, opening a path for sentient beings to attain genuine understanding, and its merit is truly great! And yet there are those who claim that the Buddha's own sutras are already clear enough in themselves, needing no further explanation at all, and that commentaries only make them more obscure — and on this basis reject every commentary altogether, without regard to quality, without regard to whether its author was an ordinary person or a sage, dismissing all of them as unworthy of attention. This position sounds plausible but is in fact mistaken. Why? To trust the sutra itself over any commentary on it does show some grasp of what matters most — but to then read a profound sutra carelessly and superficially, as though this alone were sufficient, is no small error either. This mistake, in fact, comes from two underlying afflictions: laziness, and arrogance. Laziness shrinks from broad, thorough study, wearies of careful reflection, and seeks only the easiest path, unwilling to expend real mental effort. Arrogance looks down on the ancient masters above and belittles present-day scholars below, indulging only its own private opinion, self-assured and unwilling to defer to anyone. New students, lacking real wisdom, are only too eager to follow this fashion — and I genuinely pity them for it, which is why I speak so earnestly on this point.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry is best read alongside entry 89's earlier complaint about the fashionable trend of "setting aside commentaries in favor of the bare text alone" (去註疏、專白文) among Zhuhong's own contemporaries — that entry names the fad as a passing custom among many others sweeping through Buddhist practice communities, while this one supplies the actual argument against it, diagnosing the underlying causes as laziness and intellectual arrogance rather than genuine doctrinal conviction.
