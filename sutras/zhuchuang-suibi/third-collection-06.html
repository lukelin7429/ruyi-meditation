Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Third Collection, Part 6

Continuing the Third Collection (三筆). Zhuhong defends Yunqi Monastery's own confessional discipline of plain speech, distinguishes identical outward manner from opposite inner reality using two very different historical figures, and closes with a sustained critique of monks who devote themselves to secular scholarship, fortune-telling, and unlicensed medicine instead of their own proper training.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

22. 直言 — Plain Speech

Zhuhong defends Yunqi Monastery's discipline of regular, mutual candid criticism among its monks against a resistant colleague, grounding the practice in the Buddha's own institution of the monsoon retreat's confessional close.

Chinese Source

前僧欲除「募化」、「妄拈」二禁，予不允，僧去。又一僧云：「雲棲半月直言、逐日直言，適起爭端耳。除直言，乃所以為直言也。」予謂：「汝非僧乎？僧宜從佛；而佛制九旬結夏，夏滿之日，名『僧自恣日』、『佛歡喜日』，任僧舉過，更無隱諱，故名『自恣』；雲棲半月直言，據此也。佛喜而子獨不喜，可乎？律載：僧有過，傍僧白佛，佛召本僧，種種呵責，因制為律；雲棲逐日直言，據此也。佛容其舉過，而子獨不容，可乎？且世法猶云君有諍臣，父有諍子，士有諍友，故曰興王賞直諫之臣，聖主立誹謗之木，夫子以知過為幸，仲由以聞過為喜；況為僧修出世法，可不須友以成其德乎？子惡直言，則讒諂面諛之人至矣；拒諫飾非，損德敗業，非小失也。慎之哉！」

Ru-Yi Translation

The earlier monk wanted to abolish the two prohibitions on "improper solicitation" and "reckless citation" — I refused, and the monk left. Then another monk said: "The Yunqi rules on biweekly and daily plain speech only stir up conflict. Abolishing plain speech would itself be the truest form of plain speech."

I said: "Are you not yourself a monk? A monk ought to follow the Buddha; and the Buddha established the ninety-day summer retreat, and on the day it concludes, called it 'the day of the monks' unrestrained confession,' or 'the Buddha's day of joy' — allowing monks to point out one another's faults freely, with nothing held back, which is exactly why it is called 'unrestrained.' Yunqi's biweekly plain speech is based on precisely this precedent. The Buddha himself rejoiced in it — why should you alone object? The Vinaya records: when a monk had committed some fault, a fellow monk would report it to the Buddha, who would summon the offending monk and rebuke him in every way, and from this the precept itself was established; Yunqi's daily plain speech is based on precisely this precedent as well. The Buddha himself permitted others to point out faults — why should you alone refuse to permit it? And besides, even in worldly law it is said that a ruler needs a remonstrating minister, a father needs a remonstrating son, a scholar needs a remonstrating friend — this is exactly why a flourishing ruler rewards ministers who speak candidly, why a wise sovereign erects a post inviting written criticism, why Confucius counted knowing his own faults a genuine fortune, and why Zhong You rejoiced at hearing of his own errors. How much more so, then, for a monk cultivating a path beyond this world — should he not need companions willing to speak plainly, to help him perfect his own virtue? If you hate plain speech, then flatterers and sycophants will come to surround you instead; refusing honest counsel and covering up one's own faults damages one's virtue and ruins one's own practice — this is no small failing. Be careful indeed!"

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhong You (Zilu) was a genuine disciple of Confucius, and the line Zhuhong invokes — that Zilu rejoiced whenever told of his own faults — comes from Mencius's own text; this entry, together with entries 19–21 immediately preceding it, forms a connected sequence of real institutional debates at Yunqi Monastery, each defending a specific written rule against a specific monk's objection, giving this portion of the collection an unusually documentary, blow-by-blow character.

23. 心跡 — Outward Trace and Inner Reality

A study in identical outward behavior masking opposite inner character — a stern incorruptible judge and a notorious traitor who both rarely smiled — extended to a warning about Chan masters whose severe manner has been imitated without their underlying substance.

Chinese Source

包孝肅公終日正色，人以其笑比黃河清；秦會之亦罕有笑容，一破顏於溪水涸來之對。外貌雖同，而中則天壤矣！神鼎諲禪師門庭高峻，衲子非久參上士，無敢登其門；後之禪和亦有然者。臨濟、德山動輒棒喝，如風如雷；後之禪和亦有然者。黃龍、妙喜陞座，則詬罵諸方；後之禪和亦有然者。其同異何如哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

Lord Bao Xiaosu maintained a grave, solemn expression all day long, so rare was his smile that people compared it to the Yellow River running clear; Qin Hui, too, rarely smiled, and once broke into a smile only when told that a certain stream had run dry. Their outward expressions were identical, but their inner hearts were as far apart as heaven from earth!

Master Shending Yin's own gate stood forbiddingly high — no monk, unless he were already an accomplished elder of long practice, dared to approach it; and among later Chan practitioners, some have imitated this very same manner. Linji and Deshan struck and shouted at every turn, like wind and thunder; and among later Chan practitioners, some have imitated this very same manner too. Huanglong and Miaoxi, ascending the teaching platform, would curse and revile every other school; and among later Chan practitioners, some have imitated this very same manner as well. How, then, are we to understand what is genuinely the same, and what is entirely different?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Bao Xiaosu is Bao Zheng ("Bao the Just"), the celebrated incorruptible Song magistrate whose legend survives to this day as "Justice Bao"; Qin Hui, already discussed for his reincarnation legend in the Second Collection (entry 69 above), remains one of Chinese history's most reviled figures for engineering the death of the loyalist general Yue Fei — Zhuhong's pairing of these two men's shared reticence sets up his real point: that "德山棒、臨濟喝" (Deshan's stick, Linji's shout), genuine expressions of realized masters' skillful severity, can be copied in outward form by lesser students with no corresponding inner attainment at all, the same warning against counterfeit conduct already developed at length in the Second Collection's entry 127.

24. 僧務外學 — Monks Devoted to External Learning

A complaint that monks increasingly neglect Buddhist scripture in favor of Confucian and Daoist texts, and beyond that, secular literary accomplishment — read as a clear sign of the Dharma-gate's institutional decline.

Chinese Source

儒者之學，以「六經」、《論》、《孟》等書為準的，而《老》、《莊》乃至佛經，禁置不學者，業有專攻，其正理也，不足怪也；為僧亦然。乃不讀佛經而讀儒書；讀儒書猶未為不可，又至於讀《莊》、《老》；稍明敏者，又從而註釋之；又從而學詩、學文、學字、學尺牘，種種皆法門之衰相也，弗可挽矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

Confucian scholars take the Six Classics, the Analects, and the Mencius as their own standard, and forbid themselves from studying the Laozi, the Zhuangzi, or even Buddhist scriptures — devoting themselves to a single field of study. This is simply sound principle, nothing strange about it; and the same ought to hold true for monks as well. And yet there are monks today who set aside the Buddhist sutras entirely in favor of reading Confucian texts — reading Confucian texts might still be excusable — and go on to read the Zhuangzi and the Laozi besides; and those with somewhat keener minds go on to write their own commentaries on these very texts; and further still, devote themselves to composing poetry, writing prose, practicing calligraphy in every style, and crafting elegant correspondence — every one of these is a sign of the Dharma-gate's own decline, and it seems there is no way left to reverse it!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry extends a concern already voiced in the Second Collection's own complaint about monks setting aside their fundamental task for external learning (entry 101 above); the escalating list here — Confucian texts, then Daoist texts, then original commentary on them, then poetry and calligraphy for their own sake — mirrors the same accumulating structure Zhuhong uses elsewhere to build a case incrementally rather than assert it all at once.

25. 僧務雜術一 — Monks Devoted to Miscellaneous Arts, First Note

A sharp rejection of monks moonlighting as geomancers, diviners, and physiognomists, dismissing the objection that even Baizhang Huaihai once relied on such arts to found Mount Wei's monastery.

Chinese Source

僧又有作地理師者，作卜筮師者，作風鑑師者，作醫藥師者，作女科醫藥師者，作符水、爐火燒煉師者，末法之弊極矣！或曰：「百丈大師令司馬頭陀擇地可作五百僧道場者，而得溈山，是地理家事；既而令擇溈山主人，得大祐禪師，是風鑑家事，則何如？」噫！此古聖賢為傳法利生之大機緣，非世人所測識者。而百丈、司馬是何等人品，今之術士，可以藉口也與哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

There are monks who work as geomancers, as diviners, as physiognomists, as physicians, as obstetric specialists, as practitioners of talismanic water and alchemical furnace arts — the corruption of this degenerate age has truly reached its extreme! Someone objected: "Master Baizhang once had Śramaṇa Sima select a site capable of housing a community of five hundred monks, and thereby found Mount Wei — is this not itself a matter of geomancy? And having then chosen a leader for Mount Wei, he obtained Great Master You — is this not itself a matter of physiognomy? How, then, do you explain this?"

Ah! This was a great and providential opportunity created by the ancient sages themselves, for the sake of transmitting the Dharma and benefiting sentient beings — something ordinary people could never truly fathom. And what sort of men were Baizhang and Śramaṇa Sima to begin with? How could today's fortune-tellers possibly cite them as their own justification?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Baizhang Huaihai (720–814) is the genuine founder of independent Chan monastic discipline, author of the "Baizhang's Pure Rules" that governed Chinese monasteries for centuries and coiner of the famous maxim "a day without work is a day without food"; the founding of Mount Wei's monastery through Śramaṇa Sima's site selection, followed by the famous water-pitcher test that chose Guishan Lingyou (the "Great Master You" of the text) as its founding abbot over the senior monk Hualin, is a well-documented episode in Chan historiography — Zhuhong's argument concedes the historical fact while denying it sets any general precedent for ordinary monks.

26. 僧務雜術二 — Monks Devoted to Miscellaneous Arts, Second Note

A narrower concession that medicine practiced competently and compassionately can count among a bodhisattva's own practices — followed by a specific, documented case of a monk's incompetent moxibustion method causing real harm.

Chinese Source

或曰：「雜術固非僧務，醫以全生，宜若無礙焉。」予謂：雜術亂心，則概所當捨。如其救濟為懷，則亦萬行之一端；而術儻不精，雖曰全生，反以傷生，則大為不可。近有僧行灸法者，其法和藥作餅，置艾炷於其上而然之，云治萬病。此不知出自何書，傳自何人？夫切肌而察穴，循穴而入內，灼艾之常法也；隔餅而灸，有痛苦而無功能者也。而師行焉，弟子紹焉，不自知其業之深且重也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone said: "Miscellaneous arts are certainly not proper business for a monk, but practicing medicine, since it preserves life, ought surely to raise no objection." I would say: whatever arts disturb the mind ought to be set aside entirely as a general rule. If undertaken out of genuine compassion for saving lives, medicine can indeed be counted among the countless practices of a bodhisattva — but if one's skill in the art is not genuinely sound, then even with the intention of preserving life, one may end up harming it instead, and this is entirely unacceptable.

Recently there has been a monk practicing a form of moxibustion in which medicine is kneaded into a cake, with a cone of mugwort placed on top and burned, claimed to cure every illness. I do not know from what medical text this method derives, nor from whom it was transmitted. The proper method of moxibustion is to first palpate the flesh to locate the correct point, and then apply the burning cone directly along that point so its heat penetrates within — this is the ordinary, established method. Burning through an intervening cake produces only pain, with no genuine therapeutic effect at all. And yet the teacher practices it, and his own students carry it on after him, neither one aware of how deep and serious a karmic burden they are creating for themselves.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry's careful concession — medicine is acceptable when practiced with real skill and genuine compassion, unacceptable when incompetent regardless of good intention — mirrors the same structure Zhuhong uses in the Second Collection's entry on eating meat during illness (entry 138 above), where a narrow exception is granted only under precise conditions rather than as a blanket allowance; his specific, almost clinical description of the flawed moxibustion method suggests firsthand observation rather than secondhand rumor.
