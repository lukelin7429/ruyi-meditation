竹窗隨筆・三筆 · Entries 43–47

Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Third Collection, Part 10

Continuing the Third Collection (三筆). Zhuhong closes his assessment of Li Zhi with a careful distinction between judging character and judging outcome, examines a famous but imprecise analogy between Confucian ethics and the Buddha's three bodies, praises a Song official's own rigorous nightly self-accounting, and warns against unfinished commissioned sutra recitation with a documented cautionary dream.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

43. 李卓吾二 — Li Zhuowu, Second Note

Zhuhong clarifies that his admiration for Li Zhi is not a judgment based on his tragic end, drawing a careful parallel to Confucius's own foreknowledge of Zilu's violent death — and finding real value in the disciplined testament Li Zhi left his own students, regardless of his more provocative public writings.

Chinese Source

或曰：「子以成敗論人物乎？」曰：「非然也。夫子記子路不得其死，非不賢子路也，非不愛子路也。行行兼人，有取死之道也。卓吾負子路之勇，又不持齋素而事宰殺，不處山林而遊朝市，不潛心內典而著述外書，即正首丘，吾必以為倖而免也。雖然，其所立遺約，訓誨徒眾者，皆教以苦行清修，深居而簡出，為僧者當法也。蘇子瞻譏評范增，而許以人傑，予於卓吾亦云。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked: "Do you judge a person's character by his success or failure?" I answered: "Not at all. Confucius foretold that Zilu would not die a natural death — this was not because he considered Zilu unworthy, nor because he did not love him. It was simply that Zilu's own fierce, contentious temperament was itself the path toward such a death. Zhuowu shared Zilu's own courage, and yet, unlike Zilu, he neither kept a vegetarian diet nor abstained from killing for food; he did not withdraw to mountain forests but instead wandered through the busy world of court and marketplace; he did not devote himself quietly to Buddhist scripture but instead threw himself into writing works outside it. That such a man still managed to be buried in his own native soil, I would consider sheer good fortune. And yet, even so, the testament and instructions he left for his own students all taught austere practice, quiet cultivation, deep seclusion, and rare public appearance — exactly what a monk ought to follow. Su Zizhan once criticized Fan Zeng's own poor timing, and yet still praised him as a genuine hero among men — I would say exactly the same of Zhuowu."

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Confucius's remark that Zilu "would not die a natural death" is recorded in the Analects itself, and was fulfilled when Zilu was killed defending his post during a political uprising in the state of Wey; Su Dongpo's essay on Fan Zeng, a genuine piece of his surviving prose, argues Fan Zeng was right to abandon Xiang Yu but far too slow to do so — Zhuhong's citation of that essay here completes the two-part structure of entries 42–43, moving from cataloguing Li Zhi's most provocative claims to a considered final judgment that separates the man's genuine virtues from his most reckless public positions.

44. 中庸性道教義 — The Doctrine of Nature, the Way, and Teaching in the Doctrine of the Mean

A careful evaluation of Dahui Zonggao's own elegant but imprecise mapping of the Doctrine of the Mean's opening terms onto the Buddha's three bodies, arguing the comparison illuminates a resemblance without establishing genuine doctrinal equivalence.

Chinese Source

妙喜以中庸性、道、教，配清淨法身、圓滿報身、千百億化身，體貼和合，可謂巧妙。細究之，則一時比擬之權辭，非萬世不易之定論也，作實法會則不可。何也？彼以仁義禮智言性，豈不清淨？然非法身纖塵不立之清淨也。彼以事物當然之理言道，豈不圓滿？然非報身富有萬德之圓滿也。彼以創制立法、化民成俗為教，豈無千百億妙用？然一身之妙用，非分身千百億之妙用也。大同而小異，不可以不察也。或曰：「仁義禮智，孟子之言也，《中庸》止言天命而已。」予謂「至誠能盡其性」，而繼之以「寬裕溫柔」十六字，非仁義禮智而何？故曰孟軻受業子思之門人也，不可不察也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Miaoxi matched the Doctrine of the Mean's own opening terms — nature, the Way, and teaching — with the Buddha's three bodies: the pure Dharma-body, the perfect Reward-body, and the countless billions of Transformation-bodies, weaving them together with genuine ingenuity. And yet, examined closely, this was only a skillful, provisional comparison suited to a particular moment — not a fixed truth valid for all time — and it would be a mistake to treat it as an actual doctrinal equivalence.

Why? He calls benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom "nature" — is this not, in a sense, pure? And yet it is not the Dharma-body's own purity, free of even the finest speck of dust. He calls the proper principle governing things and affairs "the Way" — is this not, in a sense, complete? And yet it is not the Reward-body's own completeness, possessed of countless myriad virtues. He calls the establishment of institutions and laws, transforming the people into good custom, "teaching" — does this lack countless billions of skillful applications? And yet it remains the skillful application of a single body, not the manifestation of countless billions of distinct bodies at once. Broadly similar, yet subtly different in each case — this cannot go unexamined.

Someone might say: "Benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom are Mencius's own words — the Doctrine of the Mean itself speaks only of Heaven's mandate." I would say: after "utmost sincerity alone can fully realize one's own nature," the text continues with the further sixteen characters describing breadth, generosity, warmth, and gentleness — is this not exactly benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom? From this we know that Mencius himself studied under Zisi's own disciples — this too must not go unexamined.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The original exchange behind Dahui Zonggao's three-body analogy is genuinely documented — a nephew of the Song official Zhang Jiucheng (already discussed for his own austere character in this collection's entry 9 above) posed the Doctrine of the Mean's opening line to Dahui as a challenge, and Dahui's improvised three-body mapping was his on-the-spot response; Zhuhong's careful qualification here — admiring the wit while denying it settles anything doctrinally — extends the same methodological caution he applies throughout this collection to clever but imprecise cross-tradition comparisons (compare his treatment of Daoist inner alchemy in entry 123 above).

45. 趙清獻 — Zhao Qingxian

A brief, admiring portrait of the Song official Zhao Bian's own rigorous nightly self-accounting to Heaven, read as the genuine foundation of his later, well-documented Chan awakening.

Chinese Source

公嘗自言：晝之所為，夜必焚香告天，不敢告者，則不為也。吾以為如是之人乃可學道。後得法於蔣山泉禪師，有「一聲霹靂頂門開，喚起從前自家底」之句，以如是精誠之心地，而參扣自心，其得悟非偶然也。若夫身雖歸佛，心不合天，止是遊戲法門而已。

Ru-Yi Translation

Lord Zhao once said of himself: "Whatever I do during the day, I must, that same night, burn incense and report it to Heaven — and whatever I would not dare report to Heaven, I simply do not do at all." I would say: only a person like this could truly study the Way. He later attained realization under Master Fa Quan of Jiangshan, composing the verse: "A single clap of thunder, and the crown of my head opened wide — awakening at last my own original self, from all along." With such genuinely sincere devotion, investigating his own mind directly, his awakening could hardly have been mere accident. As for those whose bodies have taken refuge in the Buddha while their hearts remain out of accord with Heaven — theirs is nothing more than playing at the Dharma-gate.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhao Bian was a genuine and celebrated Song official nicknamed the "Iron-Faced Censor" for impeaching corrupt officials without regard for their rank; his own recorded thunderclap awakening is preserved in Chan biographical literature, complete with his teacher Fa Quan's characteristically understated response — "Zhao Yuedao simply got lucky" — a wry deflation Zhuhong does not quote here but which is consistent with his own recurring insistence throughout this collection that genuine realization, however it arrives, must be distinguished from mere coincidence dressed up as attainment.

46. 經債 — A Debt of Unfinished Sutra Recitation

A cautionary tale about a temple's own prosperous but increasingly stingy elder monk, whose posthumous dream-visitation warns of severe underworld punishment for sutra recitations left incomplete.

Chinese Source

烏鎮利濟寺，有僧師徒二人，俱稱謹厚。託以經懺者日益眾，因致饒裕，而吝嗇，不自享用，亦不布施。後得疾，族人迎歸調治，俄而謝世，平生積貯盡為族有。十年後，現夢於所親曰：「經懺未完者，陰府考較甚急，苦不可言。人世所傳，閃電光中認字讀還，信不誣也。」筆之，以誡夫應緣者。

Ru-Yi Translation

At Liji Temple in Wuzhen, there lived a master and disciple, both known for their careful, honest conduct. Those entrusting them with sutra-recitation and repentance rites for the dead grew more numerous by the day, and the temple gradually became prosperous — and yet the master himself grew increasingly stingy, neither enjoying his own wealth nor giving any of it away. Later he fell ill, and his relatives brought him home to recuperate; he passed away not long after, and everything he had accumulated in his lifetime went entirely to his own relatives.

Ten years later, he appeared in a dream to someone close to him, saying: "For the sutra recitations I left unfinished, the underworld tribunal examines me with terrible severity — the suffering is beyond words. What is said among the living — that in the underworld, one must recognize and read back the missing characters by the light of lightning flashes — is indeed no fabrication." I record this here as a warning to those who take on such commissioned religious services.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Wuzhen was, and remains, a real market town on the Zhejiang-Jiangsu border, and "commissioned services" (應緣, taking payment to perform sutra recitation or repentance rites on a donor's behalf) were a genuine and sometimes controversial source of temple income in Zhuhong's own day — this entry's real warning is not against the practice itself but against accepting payment for religious labor left incomplete, echoing the same concern about honest exchange between donor and recipient already developed in this collection's entry 19 on improper solicitation above.

47. 淨土壽終 — Life's End in the Pure Land

A brief doctrinal clarification resolving an apparent contradiction between the Pure Land's own vow of freedom from the lower realms and its promise of immeasurable life, distinguishing an advanced practitioner's compassionate return to this world from ordinary birth and death.

Chinese Source

或問：「第二願云：『國中天人壽終，更無生三惡道者。』則有生有死，特不墮落耳，何謂生彼國者皆無量壽？」曰：「後不云乎？『國中天人壽皆無量，除其本願，願出度生者。』十疑論亦曰：『生彼國土，得無生忍已，還來此世救苦眾生。』則悲願行化，非此土死生比也。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked: "The second vow says: 'When the gods and humans of that land come to the end of their lives, none shall ever again be reborn into the three evil paths.' This implies there is still birth and death there, only without falling into the lower realms — why, then, is it said that all who are reborn in that land possess immeasurable life?" I answered: "Does the text not go on to say: 'The lifespan of the gods and humans of that land is immeasurable, except for those whose own original vow leads them to depart in order to deliver other beings'? The Treatise on Ten Doubts likewise says: 'Having attained the forbearance of the unborn in that land, one may return to this world to save suffering sentient beings.' This describes compassionate vow and active teaching, not birth and death as it occurs in this world at all."

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The forty-eight vows Zhuhong cites here come from the genuine Wei-dynasty translation of the Immeasurable Life Sutra attributed to Saṃghavarman, one of several surviving translations whose precise vow numbering and wording differ from one another — Zhuhong's careful cross-reference to the fifteenth vow to resolve an apparent contradiction in the second demonstrates the same close, comparative textual method he applies to Confucian classics elsewhere in this collection, here turned on the Pure Land's own foundational scripture.
