Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Second Collection, Part 2

Continuing the Second Collection (二筆) of Zhuchuang Suibi in the source text's own order and numbering. This section includes a defense of Emperor Wu of Liang against the popular slander that he starved to death, three linked entries on prajñā as a consuming fire, and Zhuhong's own account of nearly being robbed while riding an injured leg in a sedan chair.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

31. 看語錄須求古人用心處 — Reading the Ancients' Sayings, One Must Seek Their Own Effort

A warning against reading Chan masters' recorded sayings only for their striking phrases, rather than for the disciplined practice that actually produced them.

Chinese Source

凡看古人語錄文字，不可專就一問一答、一拈一頌，機鋒峻利，語妙言奇處，以爽我心目、資我談柄，須窮究他因何到此大徹大悟田地，其中自敘下手工夫，刻苦用心處，遵而行之，所謂「何不依他樣子修」也。若但剽竊模擬，直饒日久歲深，口滑舌便，儼然與古人亂真，亦只是剪綵之花、畫紙之餅，成得甚麼邊事？

Ru-Yi Translation

When reading the recorded sayings of the ancients, one must not limit oneself to a single striking question and answer, or a single sharp comment or verse on some old case, mining it only for its razor-edged, ingenious phrasing — to please the eye and ear, or to furnish material for later conversation. One must instead investigate exhaustively how the ancient master actually arrived at that state of great and thorough awakening: the account, given in his own words, of the effort he applied from the very first step, the painstaking, disciplined attention he brought to it — read this, and put it into practice, exactly as the saying goes, "why not simply cultivate after his own model?" If one merely plagiarizes and imitates the words themselves, then even after years and years, however smoothly and glibly one comes to speak, sounding almost indistinguishable from the ancients themselves, it remains nothing more than a flower cut from colored paper, a cake painted on a scroll — what could such a thing ever actually accomplish?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry states directly the underlying method behind Zhuhong's own recurring warnings against imitating Chan speech without realization (compare "Chan Sayings Must Not Be Recklessly Imitated," entry 30 in Part 1) — the fault is not in reading the sayings themselves, but in extracting their surface wit while skipping the account of practice that made the wit meaningful in the first place.

32. 夜氣 — Night Vigor

An anecdote about a dying elder's cryptic instruction to rise before dawn, unpacked through Mencius's own concept of the mind's clarity in the stillest hour of the night.

Chinese Source

蘇子瞻謂某公不學禪，臨終自知時至，諸子求教，教以第一五更早起。諸子不悟，謂為勤家。公曰不然，謂五更勾當臨死時將得去者是也。昔人云：「萬般將不去，惟有業隨身。」隨身之業，將得去者也。而業有二：一者事業，二者道業。事業有善有惡，惡業且置，善業則所修之福；道業則所修之慧也。而必以五更者，孟子所謂夜氣也。雖然，更有無所將來，無所將去者，此則不但五更，應念念勾當而不可須臾離也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Su Zizhan once told of a certain elder who had never studied Chan, and yet who, on his deathbed, knew the hour of his own death in advance; his sons asked him for instruction, and he taught them, first of all, to rise early during the fifth watch of the night. His sons did not understand, and thought this meant simply being diligent about household affairs. The elder said: "Not so — I mean that in the fifth watch you should attend to the one thing you can actually carry with you at death." The ancients said: "Of the ten thousand things, none can be carried away — only karma follows the body." It is precisely this karma that follows us that can be carried away.

But karma is of two kinds: worldly achievement and spiritual achievement. Worldly achievement includes both good and bad; setting the bad aside, the good is simply the merit one has cultivated. Spiritual achievement, by contrast, is the wisdom one has cultivated. And why insist on the fifth watch specifically? This is exactly what Mencius calls the "night vigor" — the mind, at that hour, is still and its effort concentrated. And yet there is still more: the truly formless, coming-from-nowhere-and-going-nowhere nature of one's own original mind remains, for most of us, still unrealized — and this must not be attended to only at the fifth watch, but held in mind, moment after moment, never once let go, even for an instant.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

"Night vigor" (夜氣) is Mencius's own term (Mencius 6A.8) for the mind's natural clarity during the stillest hours before dawn, before the day's activity has had a chance to disturb it — Zhuhong borrows the Confucian concept directly, treating it as pointing toward the same discipline Buddhist practice requires, while adding that ultimate realization cannot be confined to a single hour of the day at all.

33. 佛印 — Foyin

A skeptical reading of a famous Su Dongpo poem, arguing that its image of the monk Foyin roasting a pig for his friend cannot be taken literally, since it would license real harm if imitated.

Chinese Source

東坡詩有「遠公沽酒延陶令，佛印燒豬待子瞻」之句。予謂大解脫人不妨破格相與，然沽酒猶可，燒豬不已甚乎？假令俠客藉口子瞻，狂僧效嚬佛印，初始作俑，誰當其辜？故此事未可信。古謂詩人託物比興，不必實然，是也。脫有之，子瞻且置，佛印依律趁出院。

Ru-Yi Translation

Su Dongpo's poetry includes the lines: "Master Yuan bought wine to entertain Prefect Tao; Foyin roasted a pig to receive Zizhan." I would say: a person of truly great liberation may well relate to others outside the ordinary rules — buying wine, perhaps, is one thing, but is roasting a whole pig not going too far? Suppose some swashbuckler cited Zizhan as his own excuse, or some reckless monk imitated Foyin's own example — who, then, would bear the blame for having set this precedent in motion? This story, then, cannot be taken as literally true.

The ancients held that poets often speak through borrowed imagery, not necessarily describing literal fact — and this is exactly such a case. Even granting that it happened, let us set Zizhan's own conduct aside for the moment — under the precepts, Foyin himself, for roasting meat, would have been driven from the monastery.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Foyin (Liaoyuan) is the same Chan monk already encountered as Su Dongpo's close friend in the First Collection (Part 2, entry 26); Zhuhong's insistence on reading the famous couplet as poetic license rather than reportage is a rare instance of him applying textual skepticism specifically to protect the precepts, rather than to defend scriptural authenticity as in most of his other source-critical entries.

34. 學貴精專 — Learning Prizes Focused Devotion

A brief argument, from calligraphy and music, that single-minded focus rather than breadth is what actually produces mastery — applied directly to Chan investigation and Buddha-recitation alike.

Chinese Source

米元章謂學書須是專一於是，更無餘好，方能有成。而予聞古之善琴者，亦謂專攻三二曲，始得入妙。斯言雖小，可以喻大。佛言：「制心一處，無事不辦。」是故心分兩路，事不歸一；情專志篤，三昧速成。參禪念佛人不可不知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Mi Yuanzhang held that mastering calligraphy required single-minded devotion to that alone, with no other competing interest, before real accomplishment could follow. And I have heard that the ancient masters of the qin, too, held that one must concentrate on only two or three pieces to reach true refinement in them. These sayings may sound modest, yet they illustrate something far larger. The Buddha said: "Fix the mind in one place, and there is nothing that cannot be accomplished." This is why, when the mind is divided along two paths, nothing is ever truly brought to completion; while a single-minded, wholehearted devotion brings samādhi swiftly to fruition. Those who practice Chan investigation and those who practice Buddha-recitation alike should not fail to know this.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Mi Yuanzhang is Mi Fu (米芾, 1051–1107), one of the "Four Great Calligraphers" of the Song dynasty; the cited Buddhist line comes from the Sūtra of the Buddha's Final Teaching, already quoted once in this collection's discussion of monastic discipline (entry 49 below, "How Difficult It Is... to Set the Mind at Rest").

35. 菩薩慈勝聲聞 — A Bodhisattva's Compassion Surpasses the Śrāvaka's

A sutra citation contrasting a śrāvaka's mere endurance of insult with a bodhisattva's active compassion toward those who wrong him.

Chinese Source

經云：「聲聞人於罵者、害者，或嘿然，或遠離。菩薩則不然，更加慈心，愛之如子，方便濟度；故遠勝聲聞，不可為比。」予唯世人恆苦辱之難忍，況不唯忍辱而更慈愛之乎！經又云：「眾生無恩于菩薩，而菩薩常欲利益眾生。」予唯世人尚有受恩不報，況無恩於己而乃利益之乎！得斯旨者，天下無一人不可與，天下無一人不可化。

Ru-Yi Translation

A sutra says: "Toward those who curse or harm them, śrāvakas either remain silent or simply withdraw. A bodhisattva does not do this — he responds instead with even greater compassion, loving such a person as his own child, and delivers him by skillful means; and it is for this reason that a bodhisattva far surpasses the śrāvaka, beyond any comparison." I would only note: ordinary people already find mere insult almost unbearable to endure — how much more remarkable, then, not merely to endure it, but to respond with active loving-kindness besides!

The sutra also says: "Sentient beings show the bodhisattva no kindness at all, and yet the bodhisattva constantly wishes to benefit them." I would only note: ordinary people already fail, at times, to repay even a kindness genuinely shown to them — how much more remarkable, then, to actively benefit those who have shown one no kindness whatsoever! Whoever truly grasps this teaching will find there is not a single person in the whole world he cannot get along with, not a single person in the whole world he cannot help transform.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Both citations are drawn from the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom (Mahāprajñāpāramitāśāstra), attributed to Nāgārjuna; the entry's structure — sutra citation, then Zhuhong's own a fortiori commentary starting "I would only note" — is a recurring rhetorical pattern across this collection whenever he wants to sharpen an already-strong scriptural claim rather than qualify it.

36. 宗乘不與教合 — The Chan Vehicle Does Not Accord with the Scriptural Teaching

A gōng'àn in which a Confucian scholar's attempt to blend the Doctrine of the Mean with the Śūraṅgama Sūtra is rejected by a Chan master, followed by Zhuhong's own argument for why "a separate transmission outside the teaching" does not actually devalue the teaching itself.

Chinese Source

曾宗元學士，以《中庸》、《大學》參《楞嚴》，而和合宗門語句，質之雪竇顯禪師。顯云：「這箇尚不與教乘合，況中庸耶！學士須直捷理會。」乃彈指一下云：「但恁麼薦取！」宗元言下有省。夫一代時教，修行人所據以為準的者，不與教合，則魔說也。而云然者，是即教外別傳之旨也。傳在教外，則教之所談者何事？夫亦離指見月，而得意於語言文字之表云爾。且世尊拈花，迦葉微笑，萬代宗門傳法之始也。今翻案云：「這箇尚不與拈花合，花外有別傳也。」則何如？古人謂俱胝悟處，不在指頭上；今雪竇彈指，宗元有省，又翻案云：「這箇尚不與彈指合，指外有別傳也。」則何如？

Ru-Yi Translation

The scholar Zeng Zongyuan once used the Doctrine of the Mean and the Great Learning to investigate the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, blending in Chan phrases as well, and put his interpretation before Master Xuedou Chongxian for judgment. Xuedou said: "This matter is not even accorded with by the scriptural teaching itself — how much less by the Doctrine of the Mean! You must understand it directly." He then snapped his fingers once, and said: "Just grasp it exactly like this!" At these words, Zongyuan had a genuine awakening.

The Buddha's own lifetime of teaching is the very standard by which practitioners must guide themselves — anything that does not accord with that teaching is a demonic doctrine. And yet Xuedou's remark here is precisely the point of "a separate transmission outside the teaching." If the transmission lies outside the teaching, then what, exactly, do the Tripiṭaka's own twelve divisions actually discuss? The answer is: one must leave the pointing finger behind to see the moon itself, grasping the meaning that lies beyond mere language and text.

Consider: the World-Honored One held up a flower, and Kāśyapa smiled — this is the origin of the Chan transmission for ten thousand generations. Suppose someone now overturned this and said: "This matter does not even accord with the raising of the flower itself — there is a separate transmission outside even the flower." How, then, should this be understood? The ancients said: "Juzhi's own awakening did not lie in his raised finger." And yet here Xuedou's own snap of the fingers produced Zongyuan's genuine awakening. Suppose someone now overturned this too, and said: "This matter does not even accord with the snap of the fingers itself — there is a separate transmission outside even the finger-snap." How, then, should this be understood?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The recursive structure of Zhuhong's closing argument — apply the same "not even X itself" logic to the flower, then to the finger-snap that transmitted the teaching about the flower — demonstrates that the phrase "does not accord with the teaching" cannot be turned into an infinite regress without collapsing every possible teaching method, including the very gesture that first revealed it; the same move already used against a rival reading of the Śūraṅgama in the First Collection (Part 1, entry 4).

37. 放參飯 — The Evening "Release-from-Assembly" Meal

A sharp complaint about an increasingly lavish evening meal custom in Zhejiang monasteries, contrasted with the strict precept against eating after noon.

Chinese Source

越地安禪，夜作齋，其名曰放參飯，競為侈靡，勝於午齋，相沿成習久矣。昔有尊宿，聞鄰房僧午後作食，不覺泣下，悲佛法之陵夷也。故僧禁過午食，況夜食耶！律言人間碗缽作聲，餓鬼咽中起火。乃於漏深人靜，而砧几盤盂，音響徹其耳根，又煎煮烹炮，馨香發其鼻識，忘慈悲之訓，恣口腹之欲，於心安乎？或曰：「中夜飢，如之何？」則代以果核餅餌之類，不煩鍋銚者可也。況持過午者，午後至明，不食纖物；我等晚有藥石，何不知足之甚？

Ru-Yi Translation

In the region of Yue, meditation communities serve a meal at night, called the "release-from-assembly meal" — and it has, over time, become customary to compete in making this meal ever more lavish, even exceeding the noon meal, a habit that has now persisted for a very long time. There was once a venerable elder who, hearing a monk in a neighboring room eating in the afternoon, could not help weeping, grieving over the decline of the Buddha-dharma. Monks are forbidden even to eat past noon — how much more so at night!

The Vinaya says that the very sound of a bowl clattering in the human world causes fire to rise in the throats of hungry ghosts. And yet here, in the depth of night, when all is quiet, the sound of chopping boards and clanging bowls fills their ears entirely, while the rich aroma of frying, boiling, and roasting assaults their sense of smell — having forgotten the Buddha's own teaching of compassion, indulging only the appetite of one's own stomach and mouth — can the mind possibly rest easy with that?

Someone might say: "But what if hunger strikes in the middle of the night — what then?" In that case, fruit, nuts, or pastries may substitute — anything that does not require pots and pans is acceptable. And besides, those who keep the precept against eating past noon take not the slightest morsel from noon until the following dawn; we, by contrast, still have an evening meal at all — how could we possibly be so discontented?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The image of a hungry ghost's throat catching fire at the mere sound of a bowl is drawn from the Sūtra on the Essentials of Novice Discipline, already invoked once in the First Collection to explain hungry-ghost cosmology (Part 4, entry 89); Zhuhong applies it here to a concrete, contemporary institutional problem — lavish evening meals — rather than to abstract doctrine.

38. 僧堂 — The Monks' Hall

Zhuhong's own explanation for why he deliberately built a small monks' hall, using the historical precedent of small, genuinely accomplished practice groups over large, unaccomplished crowds.

Chinese Source

古尊宿開堂安眾，或三百五百，乃至黃梅七百，雪峰盈千，徑山千七百。予初慕之，自悲生晚，不得入彼龍象之聚。今老矣，始知正像末法信非虛語，廣群稠會之中，覓一二真實辦道人尚不可得。故金企羅尊者，三人為朋乞食；慈明圓禪師，六人結伴以參汾陽。而三人證羅漢，六人成大器。如其取數多，而證者希、成者寡，雖多奚為？予作僧堂，僅容四十八單，較古人什不及一，茲猶覺其多，仍狹而小之。非無普心，在末法中理應如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ancient venerable masters, when they opened a hall to gather the assembly, drew crowds of three or five hundred — even, in the case of Huangmei, seven hundred; Xuefeng, over a thousand; Jingshan, seventeen hundred. When I was young I longed for this, and grieved that I had been born too late to join such gatherings of dragons and elephants. Now that I am old, I have finally come to understand that the teaching of the True, Semblance, and Final Dharma is no empty saying at all — even among a vast and crowded assembly, one or two truly, genuinely serious practitioners can be almost impossible to find.

This is why the Venerable Kimbila and his two companions formed a group of only three to beg for food together; and why Master Ciming Chuyuan traveled with only six companions to study under Fenyang. And yet those three attained arhatship, and those six became great vessels of the Dharma. If one simply seeks large numbers, while genuine attainment and accomplishment remain rare, what good does the large number actually do?

The monks' hall I have built holds only forty-eight places — not even a tenth of what the ancients gathered — and even so, I still find this too many, and would gladly make it smaller and narrower still. This is not because I lack a heart for saving all beings universally — it is simply that, in this degenerate age, this is exactly how things ought to be.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This is one of the few entries in the collection that describes an actual, physical decision Zhuhong made in running Yunqi Monastery, with a specific number attached (forty-eight places) — direct, first-person evidence for how his repeated "quality over quantity" argument (compare entries 39 and 40 immediately following) shaped real institutional choices, not merely rhetorical positions.

39. 結社會 — Forming a Society

Practical guidance for Buddha-recitation and life-release societies, arguing that small, genuinely committed membership matters more than large numbers, and that men and women should not recite together in mixed company.

Chinese Source

結社念佛，始自廬山遠師。今之人，主社者得如遠師否？與社者得如十八賢否？則宜少不宜多耳。以真實修淨土者，亦如僧堂中人故也。至於男女雜而同社，此則廬山所未有。女人自宜在家念佛，勿入男群，遠世譏嫌。護佛正法，莫斯為要，願與同衣共守之。又放生社，亦宜少不宜多；以真實慈救生靈者，亦如佛會中人故也。愚意各各隨目所見，隨力所能，買而放之。或至季終，或至歲終，同詣一處，會計所放，考德論業，片時而散，毋侈費齋供，毋耽玩光陰，可也。願與同衣共守之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Forming societies for communal Buddha-recitation began with Master Hui Yuan of Mount Lu. Can those who lead such societies today truly match Master Hui Yuan himself? Can those who join them truly match the Eighteen Worthies of his own society? If not, then such a society's membership should be kept small rather than large — since those who truly, genuinely practice the Pure Land path are, as with the monks' hall discussed above, exceedingly rare in this degenerate age.

As for men and women mixing together within the same society — this is something Mount Lu's own society never had at all. Women ought properly to recite the Buddha's name within their own homes, rather than joining a men's assembly, so as to avoid worldly slander and suspicion. Nothing matters more than protecting the Buddha's own true Dharma in this way — I hope my fellow monastics will uphold this together with me.

As for release-of-life societies, too, membership should likewise be kept small rather than large, since those who truly, genuinely show compassion in rescuing living creatures are, like genuine Pure Land practitioners in this degenerate age, exceedingly rare as well. My own view is this: let each person simply purchase and release creatures according to what they themselves happen to see and what lies within their own means. At the end of each season, or at year's end, gather together in one place, tally up what each has released, examine and discuss one another's progress in virtue, and then disperse after a short while — without wasting food on lavish feasts, and without squandering time in idle indulgence. This, I believe, is enough. I hope my fellow monastics will uphold this together with me.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Eighteen Worthies (十八賢) were the most accomplished members of Hui Yuan's original White Lotus Society at Mount Lu, a named and specific historical group rather than a general reference — Zhuhong's rhetorical question ("can members today truly match them?") sets a deliberately high, named bar rather than a vague ideal.

40. 蓮社 — The Lotus Society

A careful distinction between Hui Yuan's genuine Lotus Society and the criminal secret societies that falsely borrowed its name, followed by practical instructions for lay practice that require no formal society at all.

Chinese Source

世有無賴惡輩，假仗佛名，甚而聚眾，至謀為不軌。然彼所假，皆云：「釋迦佛衰，彌勒佛當治世。」非廬山遠師蓮社也。遠師勸人舍娑婆而求淨土，其教以金銀為染心之穢物，以爵祿為羈身之苦具，以女色為伐命之斧斤，以華衣、美食、田園、屋宅為墮落三界之坑阱，惟願脫人世而胎九蓮，則何歆何羨？而彼假名彌勒者，正以金銀、爵祿、女色、衣食、田宅誘諸愚民，俾悅而從己。則二者冰炭相反，不可不辯也。然蓮社中人，亦自宜避嫌遠禍，向所謂宜少不宜多者，切語也。予曾有在家真實修行文勸世，其大意謂凡實修者不必成群作會，家有靜室，閉門念佛可也；不必供奉邪師，家有父母，孝順念佛可也；不必外馳聽講，家有經書，依教念佛可也；不必惟施空門，家有貧難宗戚鄰里知識，周急念佛可也。何以故？務實者不務外也。願為僧者，幸以此普告諸居士。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are unscrupulous, disreputable people in this world who falsely invoke the Buddha's name, even gathering crowds to plot outright rebellion. And yet what they invoke is always some claim that "Śākyamuni's own dispensation has declined, and Maitreya Buddha is now to govern the age" — this is nothing like Master Hui Yuan's own Lotus Society of Mount Lu at all.

Master Hui Yuan urged people to abandon this Sahā world and seek the Pure Land instead, teaching that gold and silver defile the mind, that official rank and salary bind the body in suffering, that sensual beauty is an axe that cuts down one's own life, that fine clothing, rich food, land, and houses are all pitfalls dragging one down into the three realms — wishing only to escape this human world and be born within the nine grades of the lotus. What, then, is there in this Sahā world left to admire or envy?

Those who falsely invoke Maitreya's name, by contrast, use precisely gold and silver, official rank, sensual beauty, food, clothing, land, and houses to lure the ignorant masses, making them delighted to follow along. The two are as opposite as ice and burning coal — the distinction must not go unexamined.

That said, members of a genuine Lotus Society should still be careful to avoid even the appearance of suspicion and steer clear of trouble — my earlier point that membership ought to be kept small rather than large was made in real earnest. I once wrote an essay urging genuine practice for laypeople, whose general point was this: a truly sincere practitioner need not gather in groups at all — one has a quiet room at home, and can simply shut the door and recite the Buddha's name there; need not seek out some deviant teacher — one has parents at home, and can recite the Buddha's name while being filial to them; need not run about outside listening to lectures — one has scripture at home, and can recite the Buddha's name while following its teaching directly; need not give exclusively to monasteries — one has poor relatives, needy neighbors, and struggling acquaintances close at hand, and can recite the Buddha's name while helping them in their urgent need. Why? Because whoever is devoted to what is genuinely real has no need to chase after outward show. I hope monastics will make this teaching widely known to their lay followers.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The criminal groups Zhuhong describes are real: "White Lotus" millenarian sects invoking Maitreya's descent were a recurring source of rebellion throughout late imperial China, and precisely because White Lotus societies enjoyed some legal protection, the name was widely borrowed by groups with no genuine connection to Hui Yuan's original devotional community — Zhuhong's careful distinction here addresses a live legal and social confusion of his own day, not merely a doctrinal one.

41. 心膽 — Mind and Courage

A reversal of a well-known maxim about courage and caution, arguing that for a monk the usual advice should be inverted: great mind, small courage.

Chinese Source

古人有言：「膽欲大而心欲小。」膽大者，謂其有擔當也。心小者，謂其有裁酌也。擔當，故千萬人吾往。裁酌，故臨事而懼，好謀而成。此正論也。至於僧，則反是，吾謂心欲大而膽欲小。心大，故帡包十界，荷負萬靈，而弘度無盡。膽小，故三千威儀、八萬細行，持之無敢慢。今初學稍明敏者，近蔑時輩，遠輕昔人，藐視清規，鄙薄淨土，膽則大矣！鞠其真實處，則唯知有己，不知有人，唯知保養顧愛其撮爾之血肉身，不知恢復充滿其廣大之法界量，心則小矣！或曰：「黃檗號麤行沙門，非膽大之謂乎？」噫！拙於畫虎者，不成虎而類狗；爾所謂膽大者，吾恐不成麤行沙門而成無賴僧也。可弗慎歟？

Ru-Yi Translation

The ancients had a saying: "One's courage should be great, and one's mind should be small." By "great courage" they meant a willingness to take on real responsibility; by "small mind" they meant careful, deliberate judgment. Because of that responsibility, one presses forward even against ten thousand opposing men; because of that careful judgment, one approaches every matter with real caution, planning well before acting. This is entirely sound advice.

For a monk, though, it should be exactly the reverse: I would say the mind should be great, and the courage should be small. Because the mind is great, it can embrace all ten realms of existence, bear the weight of every sentient being, and vow, without limit, to save them all. Because the courage is small, one holds fast to the three thousand dignified deportments and eighty thousand fine details of conduct, never daring to grow careless with them.

Students today, with a bit of quick intelligence, look down on their own contemporaries close at hand and dismiss the ancients from a distance, treating the monastic code with contempt and belittling the Pure Land teaching — their courage, certainly, is great enough! But examine the truth of their inner state, and one finds they know only themselves, not others at all; know only how to preserve and pamper this small handful of flesh and blood that is their own body, without any thought of restoring and filling out the vast measure of the Dharma-realm itself. Their mind, in other words, is small indeed!

Someone might say: "But Huangbo was himself called 'the coarse-mannered monk' — is this not exactly the mark of great courage?" Ah — one clumsy at painting tigers ends up with something that merely resembles a dog instead. I fear that what you call "great courage" will not produce another coarse-mannered monk, but only a disreputable, undisciplined one. How can this not call for real caution?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Huangbo's epithet, "the coarse-mannered monk" (麤行沙門), traces to a documented gōng'àn in which he struck the future Emperor Xuanzong of Tang (then a novice in hiding) three times to cut off his outward-seeking mind — a real historical exchange, not a general reputation for rudeness. Zhuhong's point is precise: Huangbo's roughness was the fruit of genuine realization applied with skill, and imitating its outward form without that realization produces something else entirely.

42. 太牢祀孔子 — The Full Sacrifice for Confucius

A reflection on Emperor Gao of Han's famous sacrifice to Confucius, arguing that even the grandest ritual offering falls short of what sincerity alone can achieve.

Chinese Source

漢高帝過魯，以太牢祀孔子，史官書而美之，此有二意：一則暴秦焚書坑儒之後，而有此舉；二則帝固安事詩書毀冠辱儒之主也，而有此舉，故特美其事耳。據孔子之道德，則賢堯舜、配天地、逾父母，雖烹龍炮鳳、煮象炙鯨，亦何足酬恩于萬一，而況騂且角之一物乎！「東鄰殺牛，不如西鄰之禴祭」，《易》之明訓也；儀不及物，神將吐之，況於聖人乎！用是例之，其餘可知矣。惜乎自古及今，相沿已久，而莫可挽也。

Ru-Yi Translation

When Emperor Gao of Han passed through Lu, he offered the full sacrifice — the tài láo — to Confucius, and the court historians recorded and praised the act. This carries two meanings: first, it came after the tyrant Qin had burned the books and buried the scholars alive, giving the gesture special weight; second, the emperor himself had otherwise shown little regard for the classics, even humiliating Confucian scholars on occasion, which is exactly why the act was singled out for praise.

Given Confucius's own virtue — worthy of comparison with Yao and Shun, matching heaven and earth itself, exceeding even one's own parents in what is owed to him — even the most extravagant sacrificial feast, dragon and phoenix, elephant and whale, could not repay one ten-thousandth part of that debt, let alone a single well-marked, properly-horned ox! The Yijing teaches plainly: "The eastern neighbor slaughters an ox, and yet this is not as good as the western neighbor's simple springtime offering" — meaning that even a modest offering, made with real sincerity, is received better than an extravagant one made without it. If the ritual outstrips the substance behind it, the spirits themselves reject it — how much more so a sage! By this same reasoning, everything else follows. It is a pity that this same hollow habit — form without real substance — has persisted from ancient times to the present, and can hardly now be reversed.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Emperor Gao's sacrifice to Confucius is a genuinely documented event (recorded in both the Shiji and the Book of Han) and the first recorded case of an emperor personally honoring Confucius's memory, establishing a precedent for imperial Confucian veneration that lasted throughout Chinese history; Zhuhong's use of it here is characteristic — a Confucian historical episode read through an explicitly Yijing-derived argument about sincerity over ritual extravagance, made without reference to Buddhism at all until its final, general application.

43. 儒佛交非 — Confucians and Buddhists Trading Accusations

A historically grounded argument that Confucian-Buddhist polemic, justified in its earliest phase, became excessive once repeated by later imitators on both sides — and that the two traditions actually reinforce one another's social function.

Chinese Source

自昔儒者非佛，佛者復非儒。予以為佛法初入中國，崇佛者眾，儒者為世道計，非之未為過；儒既非佛，疑佛者眾，佛者為出世道計，反非之亦未為過。迨夫傅韓非佛之後，後人又彷效而非，則過矣！何以故？雲既掩日，不須更作煙霾故。迨夫明教空谷非儒之後，後人又彷效而非，則過矣！何以故？日既破暗，不須更作燈火故。覈實而論，則儒與佛不相病而相資。試舉其略：凡人為惡，有逃憲典於生前，而恐墮地獄於身後，乃改惡修善，是陰助王化之所不及者，佛也。僧之不可以清規約束者，畏刑罰而弗敢肆，是顯助佛法之所不及者，儒也。今僧唯慮佛法不盛，不知佛法太盛，非僧之福，稍制之抑之，佛法之得久存於世者，正在此也。知此，則不當兩相非，而當交相贊也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since ancient times, Confucians have attacked Buddhism, and Buddhists have attacked Confucianism in return. I would say: when Buddhism first entered China and its followers grew numerous, it was hardly excessive for Confucians, concerned for the welfare of society, to criticize it; and once Confucians had done so, and doubts about Buddhism spread widely, it was likewise not excessive for Buddhists, concerned for the path beyond this world, to answer back in turn.

But once Fu Yi and Han Yu had already attacked Buddhism, and later people simply imitated them in attacking it further, this became excessive. Why? Once clouds have already covered the sun, there is no need to raise further smoke and haze to block it out as well. And once Mingjiao and Konggu had already answered back against Confucianism, and later people simply imitated them in attacking Confucianism further, this too became excessive. Why? Once the sun has already broken through the darkness, there is no need to light further lamps and candles besides.

Examined honestly, Confucianism and Buddhism do not actually undermine one another — they support one another instead. Consider a brief example: when a person does evil, he may escape the law's punishment in this life, and yet fear falling into hell after death — and so reforms and turns toward good instead; this is the Buddha-dharma quietly assisting where the state's own moral instruction cannot reach. And monks who cannot be restrained by monastic regulation alone, fearing worldly punishment, dare not act recklessly — this is Confucian governance openly assisting where the Buddha-dharma alone cannot reach.

Monks today worry only that the Buddha-dharma is not flourishing enough, without realizing that excessive flourishing is not necessarily a blessing for the sangha at all — some modest restraint and moderation is exactly what allows the Buddha-dharma to endure in this world over the long term. Once this is understood, the two traditions ought not attack one another at all, but should instead praise one another in return.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The four named polemicists — Fu Yi and Han Yu on the anti-Buddhist side, Mingjiao (Qisong) and Konggu on the pro-Buddhist side — have already each appeared individually elsewhere in this collection (Han Yu in entry 103 of the First Collection, Part 5; Konggu in entry 15 above); this entry is Zhuhong's own synthesis of the whole historical exchange, arguing that the original combatants were justified but their later imitators were not.

44. 好名 — Fondness for Reputation

A sharp observation that the desire for a good name is more insidious than the desire for wealth precisely because it is harder to detect — illustrated by an elder caught boasting of his own freedom from exactly this fault.

Chinese Source

人知好利之害，而不知好名之為害尤甚。所以不知者，利之害，麤而易見；名之害，細而難知也。故稍知自好者，便能輕利；至於名，非大賢大智不能免也。思立名，則故為詭異之行；思保名，則曲為遮掩之計，終身役役於名之不暇，而暇治身心乎？昔一老宿言：「舉世無有不好名者。」因發長歎。坐中一人作而曰：「誠如尊諭，不好名者惟公一人而已。」老宿欣然大悅解頤，不知已為所賣矣。名關之難破如是哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

People know the harm of loving profit, but do not realize that loving reputation causes even greater harm. The reason they fail to realize it is this: the harm of loving profit is coarse and easy to see, while the harm of loving reputation is subtle and hard to detect. And so anyone with even a little self-respect can easily set aside profit — but reputation is something only a person of truly great wisdom and virtue can escape.

Thinking to establish a reputation, one deliberately performs eccentric, attention-getting acts; thinking to protect a reputation, one contrives elaborate schemes of concealment — spending an entire lifetime chasing after reputation without rest, with no time left over to cultivate body and mind at all.

There was once a venerable elder who said: "There is no one in this whole world who does not love reputation" — and sighed deeply as he said it. Someone in the audience rose and said: "Just as you have taught us, sir — the only person in this world who does not love reputation is you yourself." The elder, delighted, laughed with real pleasure — never realizing he had just been thoroughly caught out. How difficult, indeed, this barrier of reputation truly is to break through!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The closing anecdote is a small, perfectly constructed joke — the elder's own sigh about universal vanity is itself an appeal for recognition as the one exception, and the audience member's flattering reply hands him exactly that recognition, which he accepts without noticing the trap. The entry's real subject is not the elder's error but how invisible the same error is likely to be in the reader.

45. 梁武帝 — Emperor Wu of Liang

An extended defense of the controversial "Bodhisattva Emperor," refuting several popular slanders one by one and arguing his real fault was compassion carried to excess, not moral failure.

Chinese Source

予《正訛集》中，既辨明武帝餓死之誣，而猶未及其餘也。如斷肉蔬食，人笑之，然田舍翁力耕致富，尚能窮口腹以為受用，帝寧不知己之玉食萬方乎？麵為犧牲，人笑之，然士人得一第，尚欲乞恩於祖考以為榮寵，帝寧不知己之貴為天子乎？斷死刑必為流涕，人笑之，然是即「下車泣罪」、「一民有罪我陷之」之心也，帝寧不知己之生殺唯其所欲為乎？獨其捨身僧寺，失君人之體，蓋有信無慧，見之不明，是以輕身重法，而執泥太過也。又晉宋以來，競以禪觀相高，不知有向上事，是以遇達磨之大法而不契，為可恨耳。若因其失國而遂為詆訾，則不可。夫武帝之過，過於慈者也。武帝之慈，慈而過者也，豈得與陳後主周天元之失國者同日而論乎？若因其奉佛而詆之，則吾不得而知之矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

In my own Correcting Errors, I have already refuted the false claim that Emperor Wu starved to death, but I have not yet addressed the other slanders against him. Take, for instance, his giving up meat for a vegetarian diet, at which people laughed — and yet even an old farmer, working hard to grow wealthy, still indulges his own appetite fully; could the Emperor possibly have been unaware that he himself could command every delicacy in the realm? Or his substitution of dough figures for the animal sacrifices at ancestral rites, at which people also laughed — and yet even an ordinary scholar, on passing the examinations, still wishes to seek his ancestors' blessing for the honor; could the Emperor possibly have been unaware that he himself, as Son of Heaven, owed his own ancestors even more? Or his weeping whenever he confirmed a death sentence, at which people laughed as well — and yet this is exactly the same mind as Yu the Great's own "weeping over a criminal from his own carriage," or Yao's own "if one of my people commits a crime, it is I who have failed him" — could the Emperor possibly have been unaware that he held the power of life and death entirely in his own hands, to do with as he pleased?

Only his repeated donation of himself as a temple servant genuinely compromised the dignity befitting a ruler — this came from having faith without sufficient wisdom, seeing the matter unclearly, and so valuing his own body too lightly relative to the Dharma, clinging to the gesture to an excessive degree. And ever since the Jin and Song dynasties, people had been competing to rank one another's meditative attainment, without knowing there was a further, higher matter beyond it — this is exactly why, when he met Bodhidharma's own great teaching in person, he failed to connect with it at all, a genuine matter for regret.

If people condemn him simply because he lost his throne, that is unjustified. Emperor Wu's fault was an excess of compassion; his compassion was a virtue carried too far — how could this possibly be spoken of in the same breath as the ruin brought on by the depravity of the Last Emperor of Chen or Emperor Tianyuan of Zhou? And if people condemn him simply for having devoted himself to the Buddha-dharma at all — that, I confess, I do not know how to answer.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Emperor Wu of Liang (r. 502–549), the historical figure behind the famous "no merit at all" exchange with Bodhidharma, is a genuinely contested figure in Chinese Buddhist historiography — celebrated as the dynasty's great patron and criticized for the catastrophic Hou Jing Rebellion that ended his reign. Zhuhong's essay is unusually systematic, refuting three separate popular jokes about the emperor point by point before conceding the one criticism he does think is fair (the temple-servant donations) and firmly rejecting the causal link between his Buddhist devotion and his political downfall.

46. 王所花 — The "King and Retinue" Flower

A close natural observation of an unnamed mountain flower, mapped onto the Yogācāra structure of "mind-king" and its fifty-one "attendant mental factors," and read as a lesson in the value of apparent uselessness.

Chinese Source

山中有花，共本同枝，而花分大小。大者如梅如李，環遶乎其外；小者如橘如桂，攢簇乎其中。外之數大約八，內之數百有餘。山氓莫之奇，亦莫知其名也。予見而奇之，夫同花而大小異，奇矣；大外圍而小內聚，抑又奇矣，因名之「王所」，大者心王，小者心所。王數八，外花以之；所數五十有一，內花以之。外於八，或有增減，而八者其常也；內恆倍於本數者，所雖五十有一，細分之則無盡也。王外而所內者，王能攝所，所不能攝王也。王五出，所亦五出，而有五鬚者，王單而所複也。外開先，內開晚者，王本而所末也。久沈而今顯，蓋時節因緣之謂也。或曰：「是花無豔色，燒之則煙氣惱人，樵者棄而不薪，奚奇焉？」嗟乎！此其所以奇也。莊生貴樗木，以其不可材；然不材，人取而薪之。今不可薪，則天下之至無用者極於是，《易》曰「肥遯」，其此之謂乎！

Ru-Yi Translation

In the mountains grows a flower, shared on a single stalk and branch, and yet the blossoms themselves come in two sizes: the larger ones, like plum or plum blossoms, encircle the outside; the smaller ones, like orange or osmanthus blossoms, cluster together in the middle. The outer number is roughly eight; the inner number runs to well over a hundred. The mountain folk see it often enough and think nothing of it, and none of them even know its proper name.

I saw it and found it remarkable — that flowers on the same stalk should differ so much in size is already remarkable enough; that the large ones should ring the outside while the small ones gather within is more remarkable still. And so I named it the "King-and-Retinue Flower" — the large blossoms are the "mind-king," the small blossoms are the "mind's attendant factors." The mind-king numbers eight, and the outer flowers correspond to this; the attendant factors number fifty-one, and the inner flowers correspond roughly to this as well. The outer number of eight may vary slightly, yet eight remains the usual count; the inner number always exceeds its base figure, since although the attendant factors are classified into fifty-one, subdivided further they are truly inexhaustible. The king stands outside, the attendants within, because the king can encompass the attendants, while the attendants cannot encompass the king. The outer blossoms open first, the inner ones later — the king is the root, the attendants the branch. That this flower has lain hidden for so long and only now comes to be noticed is simply a matter of the right conditions and timing finally converging.

Someone might say: "This flower has no bright color, and burning it produces smoke that irritates people — woodcutters won't even bother gathering it for fuel. What is so remarkable about it?" Ah — this is exactly what makes it remarkable! Zhuangzi prized the useless tree precisely because it could not be made into timber — and yet even a useless tree can still be gathered for firewood. This flower cannot even be used for that — making it, in all the world, about as thoroughly useless as anything could possibly be! The Yijing speaks of "retreating in comfortable abundance" — surely this is exactly what it means.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The "eight" mind-kings are the eight consciousnesses of Yogācāra psychology (the five sense-consciousnesses, plus mental consciousness, manas, and the storehouse consciousness); the "fifty-one" attendant factors are the standard Yogācāra classification of mental factors, already introduced in this collection's own vocabulary. This is one of the more unusual entries in the whole project — a piece of close botanical observation used as a genuine mnemonic diagram for technical doctrine, rather than an argument or anecdote.

47. 此道 — This Way

Building on a line from the Daodejing, Zhuhong extends the claim that no worldly rank compares to "this Way" all the way up through the entire Buddhist cosmology, then turns it into a rebuke of monks who envy worldly wealth.

Chinese Source

昔人有言：「雖有駟馬以先拱璧，不如坐進此道。」予因是推之：豈惟駟馬拱璧，雖王天下，亦不如坐進此道；豈惟王一天下，雖金輪聖王王四天下，亦不如坐進此道；豈惟王四天下，雖王忉利夜摩，乃至王大千世界，亦不如坐進此道。然昔云此道，指長生久視之道也。茲圓頂方袍，號稱衲子，將坐進無上菩提之大道，而反羨人間之富貴者，吾不知其何心也。

Ru-Yi Translation

A person of old once said: "Though one might possess a chariot drawn by four horses, and precede it with a jade disc of tribute, none of this compares to advancing seated in this Way." From this I would extend the point further: it is not only a chariot and jade tribute that fall short — even ruling over the whole world does not compare to advancing seated in this Way; and it is not only ruling over one world — even a Golden-Wheel Sage King ruling over all four continents does not compare to advancing seated in this Way; and it is not only ruling over four continents — even ruling over Trāyastriṃśa Heaven, or Yama Heaven, or the whole great trichiliocosm itself, still does not compare to advancing seated in this Way.

And yet the "Way" spoken of there originally referred only to the Daoist path of long life and lasting vision. How much more so, then, should this hold for one who has shaved his head and donned monastic robes, styling himself a mendicant monk, who ought to be advancing, seated, along the unsurpassed great Way to supreme bodhi itself — and yet instead envies the wealth and rank of ordinary human life! I truly do not know what could possibly be in such a person's heart.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The opening quotation is from the Daodejing (chapter 62); Zhuhong's technique — take a Daoist claim about worldly rank, then escalate it step by step through the full Buddhist cosmological hierarchy (a single kingdom, a cakravartin's four continents, the heavens, the trichiliocosm) — closely parallels his treatment of "This Life Was Never Anything Other Than the Full Path" reasoning elsewhere in the collection, using scale itself as the argument.

48. 金色身 — The Golden-Colored Body

A short clarification that the Buddha's traditional epithet "golden-colored" is meant approximately, not literally, and a warning against mistaking a gilded statue for the Buddha's actual radiance.

Chinese Source

贊佛身曰金色，蓋取其彷彿近似，非真若人世之所謂金也。天金天銀與世金世銀，例美玉之於碔砆，勝劣自判。蓋天金尚未足以擬佛，況世金耶？其精粹微妙，光瑩明徹，自非凡眼所睹，然不可不知。如今之土木成像，而飾之以金箔，果以為佛之色相亦只如是，則失之矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

To praise the Buddha's body as "golden-colored" is to draw only an approximate resemblance — it does not mean the Buddha's body is literally gold in the way human beings understand the word. Heavenly gold and heavenly silver, set against earthly gold and earthly silver, are like fine jade set against a mere jade-like stone — the difference in quality settles itself at once. And if even heavenly gold is not sufficient to compare with the Buddha's body, how much less earthly gold?

The Buddha's true radiance is pure and subtle beyond description, luminous and utterly translucent, and simply cannot be seen by ordinary eyes at all — and yet this is something we ought not fail to understand. If people today, seeing images of the Buddha made of clay and wood and gilded with gold leaf, conclude that the Buddha's actual bodily appearance is really nothing more than this, they have entirely missed the point!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

A concise instance of Zhuhong distinguishing a devotional or artistic convention (gilded statuary) from the doctrinal reality it merely gestures toward — the same "finger pointing at the moon" logic already articulated explicitly in the First Collection (Part 3, entry 61), here applied to religious imagery rather than to scripture.

49. 出家休心難 — How Difficult It Is, Even After Leaving Home, to Set the Mind at Rest

A list of ordinary worldly concerns that never quite let go, even after formally leaving the household life — and a reminder of the Buddha's own instruction to touch one's shaved head as a check against exactly this.

Chinese Source

人生寒思衣，饑思食，居處思安，器用思足，有男思婚，有女思嫁，讀書思取爵祿，營家思致富饒，時時不得放下。其奮然出家，為無此等累也，而依然種種不忘念，則何貴於出家？佛言：「常自摩頭，以捨飾好。」然豈惟飾好，常自摩頭曰：「吾僧也，頓捨萬緣，一心念道。」

Ru-Yi Translation

In this life, when cold, people think of clothing; when hungry, they think of food; wherever they live, they think of comfort; whatever tools they use, they think of having more of them; with a son, they think of his marriage; with a daughter, they think of her wedding; in study, they think of official rank and salary; in running a household, they think of growing wealthy — never, at any moment, able to set these concerns down.

If someone resolves to leave the household life precisely to escape entanglements like these, and yet still cannot forget every one of these same concerns, what, then, is the point of having left home at all? The Buddha said, in the Sutra of the Buddha's Final Teaching: "Constantly touch your own shaved head, to remind yourself that you have already renounced ornament and adornment." But this is hardly only about ornament and adornment — one ought also constantly touch one's own shaved head and say: "I am a monk. I have cast aside every worldly tie at once, and set my whole mind, single-mindedly, on the Way."

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Sutra of the Buddha's Final Teaching is the same short text already cited for its "fix the mind in one place" line in entry 34 above — a favorite source for Zhuhong on questions of monastic discipline, likely because of its documentary status as the Buddha's own last recorded instruction to the sangha before his death.

50. 蠶絲 — Silk

Two linked notes reconsidering the ethics of sericulture: whether Confucius's own preference for silk ceremonial caps implicates him in the killing involved, and whether the legendary origin of silk-raising can be reconciled with compassion for the silkworm.

蠶絲一 — Silk, First Note

Chinese Source

蠶之殺命也多而酷，世莫之禁者，謂上焉天子百官，藉以為章服；下焉田夫野婦，賴以為生計。然使自古無蠶，則必安於用布而已。若生計，則民之不蠶者什九，蠶者什一，未見不蠶者皆餓而死也。或曰：「夫子何為舍麻而用純？」蓋當夫子時，純之用已久，工簡於用麻，夫子姑隨之，知習俗之難變也。又禹惡衣服而美黻冕，冕用純，餘未必用也。意可知矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Raising silkworms takes many lives, and cruelly, and yet the world never forbids it — because, above, the emperor and his officials rely on silk for their ceremonial robes, and below, farming men and women rely on it for their livelihood. And yet, had silk-raising never existed at all since ancient times, people would simply have made do with cloth instead. As for livelihood: nine out of ten common people do not raise silkworms at all, and only one in ten does — and one never sees those who do not raise silkworms starving to death because of it.

Someone might ask: "Then why did Confucius himself abandon the linen ceremonial cap for a silk one?" In Confucius's own time, the practice of using silk had already been established for a long time, and its manufacture was simpler than linen's — Confucius simply went along with the prevailing custom, knowing how difficult established habits are to change. And besides, Confucius praised Yu the Great for wearing coarse everyday clothing while reserving fine, beautiful garments only for sacrificial rites — meaning that only the ceremonial cap itself need be made of silk, not necessarily everything else. The implication should be clear enough.

蠶絲二 — Silk, Second Note

Chinese Source

易云：「伏羲作結繩而為網罟，以佃以漁。」何聖人為殺生者作俑也？自古無辯之者，近槐亭王公奮筆曰：「洪荒之世，鳥獸魚鱉傷民之禾稼，網罟者，除物之為民害也，非取物而食之也。」此解不惟全物命，覺世迷，而亦有功於往聖矣！但史稱黃帝命元妃西陵氏教民蠶，則何說以通之？予聞有野蠶者，能吐絲樹之枝柯，而取之者不煩於煮繭。意者西陵之教，其野蠶之謂乎！彼家蠶或後人所自作，而非出於西陵乎！不然，成湯解三面之網，以開物之生路，而黃帝盡置之鑊湯無孑遺，是成湯解網，而黃帝一網打盡也？或曰：「東坡云：『待繭出蛾，而後取以為絲，則無殺蛹之業。』」不知出蛾之繭，縷縷斷續，而不可以為絲也；未必坡之有是言也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Yijing says: "Fuxi wove cords into nets, for hunting and for fishing." Why would a sage set the very precedent for killing living creatures? No one, from ancient times onward, has ever explained this — until recently, when a certain Mr. Wang of Huaiting took up his brush and wrote: "In the primeval age, birds, beasts, fish, and turtles damaged the people's own crops — the net was made to remove a danger to the people, not to capture creatures for food." This explanation not only preserves the lives of these creatures, it also dispels the confusion of the age, and does real service to the sages of the past!

But the histories also record that the Yellow Emperor commanded his primary consort, Lady Xiling, to teach the people to raise silkworms — how, then, are we to reconcile this? I have heard that there exists a kind of wild silkworm that spins its silk directly onto the branches of trees, from which one may gather thread without ever needing to boil the cocoon at all. Perhaps what Lady Xiling actually taught was the gathering of wild silk in exactly this way! The domesticated silkworm we know today may well be something people took up on their own only later, and not something that traces back to Lady Xiling at all. Otherwise, we would be left with the strange picture of Tang the Victorious opening three sides of his hunting net to let creatures escape, while the Yellow Emperor plunged every last one of them into the boiling pot without exception — Tang opening the net, and the Yellow Emperor sweeping it clean in one stroke?

Someone might say: "Su Dongpo held that one should wait until the moth has already emerged from the cocoon before gathering the silk, so as to avoid the offense of killing the pupa." What this fails to realize is that once the moth has emerged, the cocoon's threads are broken into short, disconnected fragments, and cannot be spun into silk at all — Dongpo, I suspect, never actually said any such thing.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Lady Xiling (西陵氏, better known as Leizu 嫘祖) is the traditional patron figure of Chinese sericulture, credited since at least the Sui dynasty with teaching silk cultivation to the people; Zhuhong's speculative rescue of her legend — proposing she taught wild-silk gathering rather than domesticated silkworm-boiling — mirrors his treatment of Fuxi's hunting net in the same entry, both efforts to reconcile a revered cultural founder-figure with Buddhist compassion for animal life without simply discarding the tradition.

52. 呂文正公 — Lord Lü Wenzheng

A skeptical retelling of a popular legend about a Song chancellor's debt to a temple, contrasting a documented account of quiet gratitude with a much more colorful but likely fabricated poem story.

Chinese Source

呂文正公既貴顯入相，上所賜予，皆封識不用。上知之，問故。公對曰：「臣有私恩未報。」蓋公微時，受恩於僧寺也。今相傳公少貧，讀書寺中，候僧食時鐘鳴即往赴。僧厭之，飯訖乃聲鐘。公至大窘，題壁云：「十度投齋九度空，可耐闍黎飯後鐘。」公及第，僧以紗籠其詩。公至寺續云：「二十年前塵土面，而今始見碧紗籠。」據前說，則僧何賢；據後說，則僧何不肖也。儻誣枉賢者，則成口業；而世所傳，出野史戲場中，恐不足信。

Ru-Yi Translation

Lord Lü Wenzheng, once he had risen to eminence and become chancellor, kept every gift the emperor bestowed upon him sealed and unused. The emperor, learning of this, asked him the reason. Lü answered: "Your servant has a private debt of gratitude not yet repaid." It was, in fact, a debt owed to a monastery, from the days before he had risen in the world.

It is now popularly told that Lü was poor in his youth and studied in a temple, where he would go to eat whenever the meal-bell rang. The monks, growing weary of him, began ringing the bell only after they had already finished eating, so that Lü, arriving, always found nothing left, and in his distress wrote on the wall: "Ten times I have gone to receive alms, nine times I found nothing there — how galling, that the abbot's bell rings only after the meal is done." Once Lü passed the imperial examination, the monks covered his poem with a gauze screen in reverence. When Lü visited the temple again, he added a further couplet: "Twenty years ago, my face was covered in dust; only now do I see it covered instead in green gauze."

Going by the first account, how virtuous the monks must have been; going by the second, how base and unworthy! If someone has wrongly slandered a genuinely worthy person, that itself is an offense of speech — and this story, as it is popularly told, seems to come from unofficial histories and stage dramas, and is probably not to be trusted at all.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Lü Mengzheng (呂蒙正, 946–1011) was a genuine and celebrated Song chancellor, remembered for his broad-minded restraint even toward those who had wronged him; the popular poem-story Zhuhong distrusts here is exactly the kind of "unofficial history and stage drama" embellishment he treats skeptically elsewhere in this collection (compare his treatment of the Su Dongpo–Foyin roast-pig anecdote, entry 33 above) — a consistent methodological preference for documented court record over colorful folk elaboration.

53. 學道無倖屈 — There Is Neither Undeserved Luck nor Undeserved Injustice in Studying the Way

A contrast between worldly fame, which depends on chance and other people's judgment, and spiritual accomplishment, which depends entirely on one's own sustained effort.

Chinese Source

世間求名者，有學未成而名成，是之謂倖，以不當得而得也；有學成而名不成，是之謂屈，以當得而不得也。故云：「我輩登科，劉蕡下第。」蓋倖與屈之謂也。學道則不然，未有名掛山林，身馳朝市，悠悠颺颺，一暴十寒，而成道業者；亦未有苦志力行，殫精竭神，不退不休，以悟為則，而道業無成者。蓋求名在人，求道在己，學道人惟宜決心精進而已，毋懷僥倖之圖，勿以枉屈為慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among those in the world who seek reputation, there are those whose learning is not yet complete and yet whose reputation is already made — this is called "undeserved luck," gaining what one has not properly earned; and there are those whose learning is complete and yet whose reputation never comes — this is called "undeserved injustice," failing to gain what one has properly earned. Hence the saying: "We pass the examination, while Liu Fen fails" — describing exactly this pair, luck on one side and injustice on the other.

Studying the Way, though, is nothing like this at all. No one has ever kept his name listed in some mountain retreat while his body ran about in the busy marketplace, drifting idly and inconsistently, warming the seat one day and letting it go cold for ten, and still somehow accomplished the Way; nor has anyone ever applied himself with bitter resolve, exhausting his whole spirit and strength, never once retreating, never once resting, taking awakening itself as the sole standard, and still failed to accomplish it. Seeking reputation depends on others; seeking the Way depends entirely on oneself. A student of the Way need only resolve firmly and apply himself diligently — with no thought at all of undeserved luck, and no worry at all about undeserved injustice.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Liu Fen was a genuine Tang examination candidate whose brilliant, politically fearless answer on eunuch power was too dangerous for examiners to reward with a passing grade, while a fellow candidate who passed publicly declared himself ashamed to have succeeded where Liu had failed — a documented case of "undeserved luck" and "undeserved injustice" occurring side by side in the very same examination.

54. 著述宜在晚年 — Writing Should Be Reserved for One's Later Years

A caution against premature commentary-writing, using Confucius's own painstaking study of the Yijing and Zhu Xi's deathbed revisions as models of the care that doctrinal writing actually demands.

Chinese Source

道人著述，非世間詞章傳記之比也，上闡先佛之心法，下開後學之悟門，其關係非小。而使學未精，見未定，脫有謬解，不幾於負先佛而誤後學乎？仲尼三絕韋編，而《十翼》始成；晦菴臨終，尚改定《大學》誠意之旨。古人慎重往往若此，況出世語論，談何容易！《青龍鈔》未遇龍潭，將謂不刊之典，而終歸一炬；妙喜初承印證，若遽自滿足，焉得有後日事？少年著述，固宜徐徐云爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

A practitioner's own writings are nothing like ordinary worldly poetry or biography — above, they set forth the mind-dharma of the buddhas of old; below, they open the gate of awakening for later students; the stakes involved are anything but small. And if one's own learning is not yet fully refined, one's own understanding not yet fully settled, and some mistaken interpretation slips through unnoticed, does this not come dangerously close to betraying the buddhas of the past while misleading the students of the future?

Confucius wore through the leather cords binding his own copy of the Yijing three times over before completing the Ten Wings; Master Huian, on his very deathbed, was still revising his own commentary on the chapter on sincerity in the Great Learning. The ancients showed this much caution even with worldly texts — how much more should this hold, then, for writing on matters beyond this world, which is no easy undertaking at all!

Before he ever met Longtan, Deshan believed his own Blue Dragon Commentary was an unalterable classic — and in the end it went up in flames on a single fire. Had Miaoxi, upon first receiving his own careless early certification, simply been satisfied with it, how could his later awakening ever have come to pass? It is only right, then, that a young writer should proceed slowly and with real patience.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry gathers three exemplars already introduced individually across this project — Deshan's burned commentary (First Collection, Part 5, entry 126), Miaoxi's careless early certification (entry 6 above), and Zhu Xi's habitual revision (already noted for his Great Learning commentary in the First Collection, Part 5, entry 103's companion material) — into a single, explicit argument about the specific discipline required for doctrinal writing, as opposed to spiritual practice generally.

55. 機緣 — Causes and Conditions

A survey of famous Chan masters who were deliberately redirected by their first teachers to a different teacher entirely, illustrating that awakening depends on matching conditions, not merely proximity to any qualified teacher.

Chinese Source

石頭之於六祖，祖知彼機緣不在此，指見青原而大悟；丹霞之於馬祖，亦復以機緣不在此，指見石頭而大悟；乃至臨濟之自黃檗而大愚，惠明之自黃梅而曹溪，皆然也。又不獨此，佛不能度者，度於目連，亦機緣使之也。故學人得遇真善知識，直須起大信敬，今世後世，由之津梁，不可漫焉空過而已。

Ru-Yi Translation

When Shitou first studied under the Sixth Patriarch, the Patriarch knew that his true causes and conditions for awakening did not lie there, and directed him instead to Qingyuan, under whom he attained great awakening. When Danxia first studied under Mazu, Mazu likewise knew his true causes and conditions did not lie there, and directed him instead to Shitou, under whom he too attained great awakening. So it was as well with Linji, who moved from Huangbo to Dayu, and Huiming, who moved from Huangmei to Caoxi. And this is not the only kind of case: even those the Buddha himself could not save were saved instead through Maudgalyāyana — this, too, was simply a matter of causes and conditions at work. This is why a student who is fortunate enough to encounter a genuine spiritual guide must give rise at once to great faith and reverence, since it is precisely through that guide, in this life and beyond, that one crosses over — this is not something to be casually let pass by, wasted.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Every redirection named here is a documented lineage transition in Chan historiography — Shitou from the Sixth Patriarch to Qingyuan, Danxia from Mazu to Shitou, Linji from Huangbo to Dayu (the same "three blows" story behind entry 41's Huangbo reference above), and Huiming from the Fifth Patriarch's own community to Huineng at Caoxi. Zhuhong's point is structural: even a fully realized master will sometimes recognize that his own teaching is not the right match, and send the student elsewhere — proximity to attainment is not the same as compatibility with it.

56. 般若 — Prajñā

Three linked images for prajñā as a consuming fire: earth and water dissolve things slowly, but fire consumes instantly; a personal near-robbery at dusk illustrates how quickly trouble arises the moment illumination dims; and a boiling pot of water, kept always at a rolling boil, never breeds the microscopic creatures that still water does.

般若一 — Prajñā, First Note

Chinese Source

土之能朽物也，水之能爛物也，必有殘質存焉，俟沈埋浸漬之久而後消滅；若火之燒物，頃刻灰燼。吾以是知般若智如大火聚，諸貪愛水逼之則涸，諸煩惱薪觸之則焚，諸愚癡石臨之則焦，諸邪見稠林、諸障礙蔀屋、諸妄想情識種種雜物，烈燄所灼，無復遺餘。古謂：「太末蟲處處能泊，惟不能泊於火燄之上。」以喻眾生心處處能緣，惟不能緣於般若之上。故學道人不可剎那而失般若智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Earth can rot a thing, and water can dissolve it, but some residue always remains, and it takes a long time — buried or soaked — before it finally disappears entirely; fire, by contrast, burns a thing to ash in an instant. From this I understand that the wisdom of prajñā is like a great blazing fire: press it with the water of craving and desire, and that water simply dries up; touch it with the fuel of afflictions, and that fuel simply burns away; bring the stone of ignorance near it, and that stone simply scorches to nothing; the dense thicket of deviant views, the roofed-over hut of every obstruction, the countless scattered fragments of delusion and discriminating consciousness — all are consumed by that fierce blaze, with nothing left over at all.

The ancients said: "The tiny insect called the taimo can perch anywhere at all — except on the tip of a flame." This illustrates how the ordinary mind of sentient beings can attach itself to anything at all — except to prajñā itself. This is why a student of the Way must never let go of prajñā-wisdom, even for a single instant.

般若二 — Prajñā, Second Note

Chinese Source

予病足，行必肩輿。一夕天始暝，輿人醉而躓，傾蓋，即有數男子攘臂攫予帽者，意謂內人，或有金寶嚴其首故也。已而大慚，疾走去。予以是知般若智如大日輪，日輪纔滅，而盜賊奸宄出矣；真照纔疏，而無明煩惱作矣。先德謂：「暫時不在，猶如死人。」故學道人不可剎那而失般若智。

Ru-Yi Translation

My leg had been injured, and I could only travel by sedan chair. One evening, as dusk was falling, the chair-bearer, drunk, stumbled and dropped the chair, and at that very moment several men rushed up, arms bared, and grabbed at my hat — thinking, no doubt, that I was some lady of the household, or perhaps wearing gold and jewels on my head. They soon realized their mistake, grew deeply ashamed, and ran off at once.

From this I understood that the wisdom of prajñā is like the great disc of the sun: the moment the sun sets, thieves and lawless men emerge; the moment true illumination even briefly dims, ignorance and affliction begin to stir. A worthy of old said: "Absent even for a moment, one is no better than a dead man." This is why a student of the Way must never let go of prajñā-wisdom, even for a single instant.

般若三 — Prajñā, Third Note

Chinese Source

經言：「暑月貯水在器，一宿即有蟲生，但極微細，非凡目所能睹，故濾水而後用。」若水在火上，火不熄，水不冷，則蟲不生。予以是知般若智如火煮水，觀照熾而不休，溫養密而無間，彼偷心雜惑將何從生？故學道人不可剎那而失般若智。

Ru-Yi Translation

A sutra says: "In the hot summer months, store water in a vessel, and by the next morning tiny creatures will already have appeared in it — too fine to be seen by ordinary eyes, which is why the water must be filtered before use." But if that same water is set over a fire, and the fire never goes out, and the water never cools, then no such creatures will appear at all. From this I understand that prajñā-wisdom is like water kept boiling over a flame: as long as the fire of contemplation burns fiercely and without pause, tended quietly and without interruption, how could the stealthy mind of confusion and doubt ever find room to arise? This is why a student of the Way must never let go of prajñā-wisdom, even for a single instant.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The second of these three notes is a rare piece of directly autobiographical writing, referencing the same scalding-injury aftermath already described at length in the First Collection's 湯厄 sequence (Part 3, entry 64) — the injured leg forcing Zhuhong to travel by sedan chair is what puts him, at dusk, in the path of the would-be thieves. All three notes share an identical refrain ("a student of the Way must never let go of prajñā-wisdom, even for a single instant"), giving the trio the character of a single sustained meditation approached from three different angles — natural process, personal incident, and scriptural citation.

59. 天台止觀 — Tiantai's Cessation and Contemplation

A clarification that Tiantai's own health-related meditation techniques are a minor offshoot of the teaching, not its central purpose — offered against Daoist practitioners who cited it to justify longevity techniques dressed up as Chan.

Chinese Source

止觀治病門中，有「六字氣」、「注心下視」等語。蓋止觀之道，廣無不該，即治病之法亦於中攝，大都與服藥同意，是以止觀代藥也。止觀之餘緒，非止觀之正旨也。後人不知此意，而養生家引以為據，遂有外飾禪名，而內修道術者，詰之，則藉口於天台。故辨之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the section on curing illness in the Tiantai teaching on cessation and contemplation, there appear practices like the "six-syllable breath" and "focusing the mind and looking downward." The Way of cessation and contemplation is, after all, broad enough to encompass everything, including even methods for treating illness — these are, for the most part, simply another way of taking medicine, using cessation and contemplation itself as the remedy. But this is only a minor offshoot of cessation and contemplation, not its true, central purpose at all.

Later people, failing to grasp this point, and practitioners of Daoist longevity techniques citing it as their own authority, have produced a phenomenon in which certain people dress themselves outwardly in the name of Chan while secretly practicing Daoist techniques within — and when questioned about it, they simply invoke Tiantai's own teaching as their excuse. This is exactly why the matter needs to be set straight here.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhiyi's own Concise Cessation and Contemplation genuinely contains a chapter on curing illness through meditative technique, including breathing exercises with real overlap in vocabulary and method to Daoist qi-cultivation practice — Zhuhong's argument is careful to concede the overlap exists rather than deny it, while insisting the section's marginal place within Tiantai's overall system does not license treating it as the tradition's real center of gravity.

60. 看忙 — Watching the Busy

A comparison of worldly leisure (watching others scramble for wealth or examination success) with a bodhisattva's compassionate awareness of all sentient beings' frantic cycling through birth and death — distinguishing pride from compassion as the two possible responses to the same observed busyness.

Chinese Source

世有家業已辦者，於歲盡之日，安坐而觀貧人之役役於衣食也，名曰看忙。世有科名已辦者，於大比之日，安坐而觀士人之役役於進取也，亦名曰看忙。獨不曰世有惑破智成、所作已辦者，安坐而觀六道眾生之役役於輪迴生死也，非所謂看忙乎？吁！舉世在忙中，誰為看忙者？古人云：「老僧自有安閒法。」此安閒法可易言哉？雖然，世人以閒看忙，有矜己心，無憐彼心；菩薩看忙，起大慈悲心，普覺群迷，冀彼同得解脫。則二心迥異，所以為凡聖小大之別。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are people in this world whose own household affairs are already well settled, who, at the end of the year, sit at ease and watch the poor scrambling for food and clothing — this is called "watching the busy." There are people whose own examination success is already secured, who, on the day of the great examination, sit at ease and watch other scholars scrambling for advancement — this too is called "watching the busy." And yet has no one ever said that there are people in this world whose delusion is already broken, whose wisdom is already complete, whose work is already done, who sit at ease and watch the beings of the six realms scrambling through the cycle of birth and death — is this not also, in its own way, "watching the busy"?

Alas! The whole world is caught up in busyness — who, then, is truly watching it? A person of old said: "This old monk has his own method of ease." But this "method of ease" is not something easily described in words at all! And yet, while all of this is called "watching," when an ordinary person watches the busy from a position of leisure, there is pride in it, and no real compassion for those still caught in it; when a bodhisattva watches the busy, by contrast, great compassion arises, universally awakening the deluded masses, hoping that they too might attain the same liberation. These two states of mind are utterly different — and this is exactly what marks the distinction between the ordinary and the sage, the small and the great.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The quoted verse, "this old monk has his own method of ease," comes from a poem by the Song-dynasty master Zhenxie Qingliao, written for monks living out their final illness in a monastery's dedicated infirmary hall — Zhuhong was moved enough by it to compose three companion verses of his own in the same style, and posted all four together in his own monastery's infirmary as a standing exhortation to the dying, a detail the source commentary preserves though it falls outside the entry itself.
