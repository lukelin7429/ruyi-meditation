108. 修福 — Cultivating Merit

Pushing back against monks who devote themselves entirely to fundraising, building, and feeding the monastic community, Zhuhong insists that inner spiritual work must come first — and reserves his sharpest criticism not for outright embezzlers but for well-meaning temple administrators who quietly misapply donated funds without ever recognizing the offense.

Chinese Source

古有偈：「修慧不修福，羅漢應供薄；修福不修慧，象身掛瓔珞。」有專執前之二句者，終日營營，惟勤募化，曰：「吾造佛也，吾建殿也，吾齋僧也。」此雖悉是萬行之門，而有二說：一則因果不可不分明，二則己事不可不先辦。或曰：「果如子言，則佛像湮沒，誰其整之？塔寺崩頹，誰其立之？僧餓於道路而不得食，誰其濟之？人人惟辦己事，而三寶荒蕪矣！」曰：不然，但患一體三寶荒蕪耳。世間三寶，自佛法入中國以來，造佛、建殿、齋僧者時時不休，處處相望，何煩子之私憂而過計也。吾獨慨夫僧之營事者，其瞞因昧果、不懼罪福、剋減常住、藏匿信施者，無論矣，即守分僧，而未諳律學，但知我不私用入己則已，遂乃移東就西、將甲補乙，或那還急債，或餽送俗家；不知磚錢買瓦、僧糧作堂，枉受辛勤，翻成惡報，是則天堂未就，地獄先成，所謂無功而有禍者也。中峰大師訓眾曰：「一心為本，萬行可以次之，則所謂己事先辦者也。己事辦而作福事，則所作自然當可矣。」至哉言乎！為僧者當銘之肺腑可也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The ancients left behind a verse: "Cultivating wisdom without cultivating merit, an arhat receives only meager offerings; cultivating merit without cultivating wisdom, an elephant walks about hung with fine jeweled ornaments." There are those who cling exclusively to the first half of this verse, spending their entire day busily occupied, devoting themselves solely to fundraising, saying: "I am building a Buddha image; I am constructing a temple hall; I am providing meals for the monastic community." Although all of these are indeed legitimate practices within the vast array of Buddhist activity, two points must be raised in response: first, cause and effect must never be treated carelessly; second, one's own inner spiritual work must always come first.

Someone objected: "If what you say is really true, then who will restore Buddha images once they have fallen into ruin? Who will rebuild pagodas and temples once they have crumbled? Who will feed monks starving by the roadside? If everyone attends only to their own inner work, the Three Jewels themselves will fall into neglect and ruin!" I answered: not so — the only thing truly worth worrying about is the neglect of the Three Jewels understood as inherent within one's own single mind. As for the Three Jewels in their worldly form, ever since Buddhism first came to China, there has never been a shortage of people building Buddha images, constructing temple halls, and feeding monks — such activity continues without pause, visible everywhere one looks. There is no need for you to trouble yourself with such excessive private worry on this account.

What I myself genuinely lament is this: setting aside entirely those monks who handle such projects while concealing their true motives and consequences, unafraid of the moral weight of their actions, skimming from the monastery's permanent assets and hiding away donated funds for their own use — even among monks who otherwise keep to their proper duties, but who have never actually studied the monastic precepts in depth, there are those who think it enough simply that they have not personally pocketed the funds for private use, and so freely shift money from one purpose to another, patching one shortfall with funds meant for something else entirely — repaying an urgent debt here, sending a gift to lay relatives there — without realizing that using money meant for bricks to buy roof tiles instead, or using rice meant to feed the monastic community to build a hall instead, however well-intentioned, brings about painful effort that turns instead into evil retribution. In such cases, the intended heavenly reward never actually materializes, while a hellish consequence arrives first instead — this is precisely what is meant by labor that earns no merit but brings misfortune all the same.

Master Zhongfeng once instructed his own community: "Let the single, unified mind be the foundation, and let every other activity follow only after it — this is precisely what is meant by attending to one's own inner work first. Once that inner work is properly attended to, whatever merit-generating activity follows will naturally turn out well." What a perfect statement this is! Every monk ought to engrave these words upon his own heart.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Master Zhongfeng is Zhongfeng Mingben, the celebrated Yuan-dynasty Chan master already cited elsewhere in this collection for his own teaching on single-minded practice, and Zhuhong's citation of him here to close an entry about fundraising and temple construction is a deliberate pairing of inner cultivation with outer works, consistent with the same priority — self-cultivation before institutional activity — that runs through this collection's earlier critique, in entry 77, of monks who build lavish monasteries at the expense of ordinary people; the distinction drawn here between the "worldly Three Jewels" (actual images, texts, and monastic communities) and the "inherent Three Jewels" within one's own mind reflects a standard doctrinal framework found throughout Chinese Buddhist commentarial literature, here deployed to relocate genuine concern for the Dharma's welfare away from institutional anxiety and back toward personal practice.
