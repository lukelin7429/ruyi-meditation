A Command to Transmit, and the Gate of Contemplation Opens

The narrative's final threshold: the Buddha addresses Ānanda and Vaidehī together, calling this teaching "rarely heard across boundless kalpas," then singles out Ānanda specifically, charging him alone with ensuring it is never lost. What follows is the doorway into the sixteen contemplations themselves.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 2 of 4: 序分義 (Meaning of the Preliminary Section)

A Command Addressed to Two, and the Six Thieves

Chinese Source

七、就定善示觀緣中，即有其七。一、從佛告阿難下，至清淨業已來，正明勅聽許說。此明韋提前請願生極樂，又請得生之行，如來已許。今就此文，正欲開顯正受之方便。此乃因緣極要，利益處深。曠劫希聞，如今始說。為斯義故，致使如來總命二人。言告阿難者，我今欲開說淨土之門，汝好傳持莫令遺失。言告韋提者，汝是請法之人，我今欲說，汝好審聽思量諦受莫令錯失。言為未來世一切眾生者，但如來臨化偏為常沒眾生，今既等布慈雲，望欲普沾來潤。言為煩惱賊害者，此明凡夫障重妄愛迷深。不謂三惡火坑，闇在人之足下，隨緣起行，擬作進道資糧。何其六賊知聞，競來侵奪。今既失此法財，何得無憂苦也。言說清淨業者，此明如來以見眾生罪故，為說懺悔之方，欲令相續斷除，畢竟永令清淨。又言清淨者，依下觀門，專心念佛，注想西方，念念罪除，故清淨也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, within the condition showing the contemplative practice, there are seven points.

First, from "the Buddha told Ānanda" down through "pure karma," this makes plain the command to listen, and the granting of permission to speak. This makes plain that the queen had earlier requested to be reborn in the Land of Ultimate Bliss, and had further requested the practice by which that rebirth is attained — and the Tathāgata had already granted this. Now, within this present passage, he means directly to reveal the expedient means of right reception itself. This is thus a matter of the very utmost importance, its benefit running exceedingly deep — rarely heard across boundless kalpas, and only now, for the first time, being spoken. It is for this very reason that the Tathāgata addressed his command to both of these two people together. As for "he told Ānanda": I now intend to open and expound the gate of the pure land — you must uphold and transmit it well, and let nothing of it be lost. As for "he told Vaidehī": you are the very one who requested this teaching — I now intend to speak it, and you must listen carefully, reflect upon it, and receive it with utmost attention, letting nothing of it go astray. As for "for the sake of all sentient beings of future ages": the Tathāgata's own transformative teaching is directed especially toward beings forever sunk in suffering, and now, spreading this cloud of compassion equally over all, he wishes it to moisten every being still to come. As for "harmed by the thieves of affliction": this makes plain that ordinary beings' own obstructions run deep, their false attachments and delusions profound. Unaware that the three evil destinies, like a pit of fire, lie hidden in darkness beneath their very own feet, they go about their lives according to circumstance, thinking to accumulate provisions for advancing along the path — yet how readily the six thieves, sensing this, come competing to plunder it away. Now, having lost this very treasure of the Dharma, how could one be without grief and suffering? As for "expounding pure karma": this makes plain that the Tathāgata, seeing sentient beings' own offenses, therefore taught the method of repentance, wishing to cause its continuation to be cut off entirely, so that purity might at last be made permanent. Further, as for "purity": relying on the gate of contemplation set forth below, single-mindedly holding the Buddha in mind, fixing one's thought upon the West, offense after offense is thereby eliminated, moment by moment — and hence it is called purity.

A note on this address

Shandao's own gloss on why the Buddha addresses this teaching to two people at once — Ānanda and Vaidehī together — draws out a division of labor easy to miss on a first reading: Vaidehī is charged with receiving the teaching for her own benefit, while Ānanda is charged with something larger, ensuring it is never lost to anyone who comes after her. The image of the "six thieves" — the six sense faculties, here cast as bandits competing to plunder away a treasure the practitioner has only just begun to accumulate — makes vivid why urgency belongs to this moment specifically: not because the teaching itself is fragile, but because the ordinary mind that would carry it is constantly under siege from its own senses.

A Brief Question, and a Command to Ānanda Alone

Chinese Source

二、從善哉已下，正明夫人問當聖意。

三、從阿難汝當受持下，至宣說佛語已來，正明勸持勸說此法深要好須流布。此明如來前則總告令安心聽受，此文則別勅阿難受持勿忘，廣多人處為說流行。言佛語者，此明如來曠劫已除口過，隨有言說，一切聞者自然生信。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, from "excellent" onward, this makes plain the queen's own question, addressed directly to the sage's own intent.

Third, from "Ānanda, you should receive and uphold this" down through "and proclaim the Buddha's own words," this makes plain the exhortation to uphold and to teach this profound and essential Dharma, that it might be well and widely spread abroad. This makes plain that, whereas the Tathāgata had earlier addressed his command generally, telling both to settle their minds and listen and receive it, this present passage now singles out Ānanda specifically, commanding him to uphold it and never forget it, and to expound it widely, in many places, so that it might spread and circulate. As for "the Buddha's own words": this makes plain that the Tathāgata, having across boundless kalpas already entirely removed every fault of speech, need only speak, and every single listener naturally gives rise to faith.

A note on this transmission

The shift from a general command to a specific one — Ānanda named individually, charged with keeping this teaching "widely spread abroad" — marks the moment this narrative becomes self-aware of its own future. Shandao is, in effect, glossing the very mechanism by which the text now being read came to exist at all: Ānanda's transmission is not incidental to the sūtra's content but is itself part of what the sūtra records happening. The closing note on the Buddha's own speech — that having "removed every fault of speech" across boundless kalpas, listeners naturally give rise to faith merely by hearing him — sets up the final claim this teaching is about to make: that what is coming is trustworthy not because of argument, but because of who is speaking it.

Part 25 Complete

This closes Part 25. The Buddha has now formally commissioned this teaching's transmission.
