A Smile, a Father's Light, and "Not Far Away"

The narrative pauses here for the first time since Vaidehī's own vision, as Shandao reveals that everything just witnessed — the confession, the radiant lands, the choice of Amitābha's own realm — was in fact narrated after the fact, relayed by Ānanda once the whole affair had concluded. Then the Buddha finally speaks, and opens with a claim built to unsettle: a destination beyond a hundred thousand koṭis of buddha-lands, described as near.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 2 of 4: 序分義 (Meaning of the Preliminary Section)

A Smile, and Light Reaching a Father in His Cell

Chinese Source

六、就散善顯行緣中，即有其五。

一、從爾時世尊即便微笑下，至成那含已來，正明光益父王。此明如來以見夫人願生極樂，更請得生之行，稱佛本心，又顯彌陀願意，因斯二請，廣開淨土之門。非直韋提得去，有識聞之皆往。有斯益故，所以如來微笑也。言有五色光從佛口出者，此明一切諸佛心口常威儀法爾，凡所出光必有利益。言一一光照頻婆頂者，正明口光不照餘方，唯照王頂。然佛光隨身出處必皆有益。佛足下放光，即照益地獄道。若光從膝出，照益畜生道。若光從陰藏出，照益鬼神道。若光從臍出，照益脩羅道。光從心出，照益於人道。若光從口出，照益二乘之人。若光從眉間出，照益大乘人。今明此光從口出，直照王頂者，即授其小果。若光從眉間出，即從佛頂入者，即授菩薩記也。如斯義者廣多無量，不可具述。言爾時大王雖在幽閉已下，正明父王蒙光照頂，心眼得開，障隔雖多，自然相見。斯乃因光見佛，非意所期。致敬歸依，即超證第三之果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth, within the condition revealing the practice of dispersed goodness, there are five points.

First, from "at that time, the World-Honored One thereupon smiled" down through "attained the fruit of non-returning," this makes plain that the light benefited the king, the queen's own husband. This makes plain that the Tathāgata, seeing the queen's own wish to be reborn in the Land of Ultimate Bliss, and her further request for the practice by which that rebirth is attained, found this to accord fully with the Buddha's own original intent, and further revealed Amitābha's own vow-intent — and, on account of these two requests together, broadly opened wide the gate to the pure land. Not the queen alone would be able to go there, but every sentient being who heard of it might go as well. It was on account of this very benefit that the Tathāgata smiled. As for "light of five colors emerged from the Buddha's own mouth": this makes plain that it is the natural way of every buddha's own mind and speech that whatever light emerges must bring some benefit. As for "each ray of light illuminated the crown of Bimbisāra's own head": this makes plain specifically that the light from his mouth illuminated no other place, but only the crown of the king's own head. For the Buddha's own light, wherever upon his body it emerges, must always bring some benefit. Light emitted from beneath the Buddha's own feet illuminates and benefits the path of hell-beings. Light emerging from his knees illuminates and benefits the path of animals. Light emerging from his hidden organ illuminates and benefits ghosts and spirits. Light emerging from his navel illuminates and benefits asuras. Light emerging from his heart illuminates and benefits the human path. Light emerging from his mouth illuminates and benefits those of the two lesser vehicles. Light emerging from between his brows illuminates and benefits those of the Great Vehicle. Now, this light in question emerged from his own mouth, and, directly illuminating the crown of the king's own head, thereby bestowed upon him the lesser fruit. Had the light instead emerged from between his brows, and entered through the crown of the recipient's own head, this would have bestowed a bodhisattva's own prediction of future buddhahood instead. Meanings of this kind extend broadly, beyond measure, and cannot here be set forth in full. As for "at that time, the great king, though still confined in seclusion" and what follows, this makes plain that the king's father, receiving this light upon the crown of his own head, had the eye of his own mind opened — and though many obstructions still stood between them, he beheld the Buddha of himself, quite naturally. This was thus a beholding of the Buddha brought about through this very light, entirely beyond what he himself had ever anticipated. Paying homage and taking refuge, he thereupon transcended directly to the third fruit.

A note on this light

Shandao's own catalogue of where light emerges from the Buddha's own body, and which realm of beings each location benefits, functions as a small, self-contained cosmology: feet for hell-beings, knees for animals, navel for asuras, heart for humans, mouth for the two lesser vehicles, the space between the brows for the Great Vehicle. What matters for the narrative is the specific choice made here — light from the mouth, reaching only the crown of the king's own head, granting him "the lesser fruit" rather than a bodhisattva's own prediction of buddhahood. Shandao is careful to note this was not a supreme attainment, but a benefit exactly proportioned to what the confined and grieving king, at this particular moment, was capable of receiving — a pattern of calibrated, rather than maximal, benefit that will recur throughout the rest of this commentary.

A Silence Explained, and a Destination "Not Far Away"

Chinese Source

二、從爾時世尊下，至廣說眾譬已來，正明答前夫人別選所求之行。此明如來從上耆闍沒、王宮出，訖至此文，世尊默然而坐，總未言說。但中間夫人懺悔、請問，放光、現國等，乃是阿難從佛王宮見此因緣，事了還山傳向耆闍大眾說如上事，始有此文。亦非是無時佛語也。應知。言爾時世尊告韋提已下，正明告命許說也。言阿彌陀佛不遠者，正明標境以住心，即有其三。一明分齊不遠，從此超過十萬億剎即是彌陀之國。二明道里雖遙，去時一念即到。三明韋提等及未來有緣眾生注心觀念，定境相應，行人自然常見。有斯三義故云不遠也。言汝當繫念已下，正明凡惑障深心多散動，若不頓捨攀緣，淨境無由得現。此即正教安心住行，若依此法名為淨業成也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, from "at that time, the World-Honored One" down through "broadly explained through many parables," this makes plain the answer to the queen's own earlier particular choice and her request for the practice. This makes plain that the Tathāgata, from the point where he vanished from Vulture Peak above and appeared within the royal palace, all the way down to this present passage, the World-Honored One sat in silence throughout, without speaking a single word. But everything that occurred in between — the queen's own repentance and questioning, the emitted light, the revealed lands, and all the rest — was witnessed by Ānanda from within the royal palace, in the Buddha's own presence; and only once this affair had concluded, and he had returned to the mountain and related it before the great assembly at Vulture Peak, did this present passage come to be recorded. This should not, then, be understood as the Buddha speaking these words without any elapsed time at all — this should be understood. As for "at that time, the World-Honored One addressed Vaidehī" and what follows, this makes plain that he issued his own command, granting permission to speak. As for "Amitābha is not far away": this makes plain specifically the marking out of this realm as an object upon which to fix the mind, and this carries three meanings. First, this makes plain that, in terms of measured distance, it is not far — beyond a hundred thousand koṭis of buddha-lands from this very place lies Amitābha's own land. Second, this makes plain that, though the road there is distant, in the very moment one goes, one arrives at once. Third, this makes plain that Vaidehī and all sentient beings of future ages who share this same karmic affinity, fixing their own minds and contemplating steadily, will find that their meditative state corresponds directly to that realm, so that the practitioner naturally and constantly beholds it. It is on account of these three meanings that it is said to be "not far away." As for "you should now fix your thoughts" and what follows, this makes plain that ordinary beings' own delusions run so deep, and their own minds are so often scattered and stirred, that unless they cast off entirely their own habitual grasping at objects, this pure realm has no way at all to appear before them. This is thus the direct teaching for settling the mind and abiding in this practice — and it is precisely by relying on this method that what is called the accomplishment of pure karma comes about.

A note on this distance

Shandao's own resolution of "not far away," measured against "a hundred thousand koṭis of buddha-lands," is worth pausing on, since it is easy to read past as mere devotional hyperbole. His three-part answer keeps the literal distance intact — he does not deny it — while adding two further senses in which nearness holds true regardless: that travel there, once begun, is instantaneous rather than gradual, and that sustained contemplative correspondence between practitioner and realm makes the realm continuously present rather than merely someday reachable. This is also the passage where Shandao pauses the narrative itself to explain something structural about how the sūtra was composed — that the entire preceding scene, confession through vision, was not spoken by the Buddha in real time but relayed afterward by Ānanda. It is a small aside, but it reframes everything already read: this was reportage of an event already concluded, not a transcript unfolding as it happened.
