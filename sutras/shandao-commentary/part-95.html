The Lower Rank Begins

The lower rank opens with its own eleven-gate framework, its sixth gate now marking a genuine departure — no longer "how the teaching is received," but the differing experience of suffering and joy each grade will pass through. The seventh grade begins with an ordinary being who has committed the ten evils, yet never once feels ashamed for having done so.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 4 of 4: 散善義 (Meaning of the Dispersed Good)

Eleven Gates, Applied a Third Time

Chinese Source

十六、就下輩觀善惡二行文前料簡，即為十一門。一者總明告命。二者辨定其位。三者總舉有緣生類。四者辨定三心以為正因。五者簡機堪與不堪。六者明受苦樂二法不同。七者明修業時節延促有異。八者明迴所修行向所求處。九者明臨終時聖來迎接不同去時遲疾。十者明到彼華開遲疾不同。十一者明華開已後得益有異。上來雖有十一門不同，總料簡下輩三位竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixteenth, before turning to the text on the practice of good and evil belonging to the lower rank, I shall first offer a general examination. This comprises eleven gates in all. The first generally makes plain the issuing of this command. The second makes plain the distinguishing and fixing of each grade's own position. The third makes plain the general raising up of the class of beings, born of this karmic affinity. The fourth makes plain the distinguishing and fixing of the three minds as the correct cause. The fifth makes plain the distinguishing of capacities, between those able to receive this teaching and those not. The sixth makes plain the differing ways in which suffering and joy, these two states, are received. The seventh makes plain the differing duration, whether extended or brief, of the time spent cultivating this practice. The eighth makes plain the dedication of this cultivated practice toward the destination sought. The ninth makes plain the differing ways in which the sages come to welcome the practitioner at the moment life is about to end, and the differing speed, swift or slow, with which they then depart. The tenth makes plain the differing speed, swift or slow, with which the lotus flower opens once that land is reached. The eleventh makes plain the differing benefits gained once that flower has opened. Though the above has unfolded across these eleven distinct gates, this concludes the general examination of the three grades of the lower rank.

A note on this shift

Compare this sixth gate to its counterparts in the upper and middle ranks, which addressed "the differing ways in which this teaching is received." Here it becomes "the differing ways in which suffering and joy are received" — a small change in wording that announces a large change in subject matter. The lower rank's three grades will not be occupied with precepts or meditative cultivation at all, but with beings who have done real harm, meeting death amid visions of hellfire before that fire itself turns to a cooling wind. The framework itself has been quietly reshaped to fit what is coming.

The Seventh Grade: Evil Without Shame

Chinese Source

次就下品上生位中，亦先舉，次辨，後結。即有其九。一、從佛告阿難已下，正明告命。二、從下品上生者，正明辨定其位，即是造十惡輕罪凡夫人也。三、從或有眾生下，至無有慚愧已來，正明第五門中簡機，舉出一生已來，造惡輕重之相。即有其五。一明總舉造惡之機。二明造作眾惡。三明雖作眾罪，於諸大乘不生誹謗。四明重牒造惡之人非智者之類也。五明此等愚人雖造眾罪，總不生愧心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, within the position of the highest rebirth of the lower rank, the text likewise first raises the matter, next distinguishes it in detail, and finally concludes it. There are nine points in all.

First, from "the Buddha told Ānanda" onward, this makes plain the issuing of this command.

Second, as for "the highest rebirth of the lower rank": this makes plain the distinguishing and fixing of this grade's own position — this is itself the ordinary being who has committed the ten evils, offenses of a lesser weight.

Third, from "should there be sentient beings" down through "without any shame or remorse," this makes plain, within the fifth gate, the distinguishing of capacity, raising up the mark of the offenses, light or heavy, this being has committed across this one life. There are five points within it. First, this makes plain the general raising up of the capacity for committing evil. Second, this makes plain the actual committing of manifold evils. Third, this makes plain that, though this being has committed manifold offenses, they do not go so far as to slander the Mahāyāna teachings. Fourth, this makes plain, restating it once more, that this being who commits evil does not belong to the class of the wise. Fifth, this makes plain that, though such foolish beings have committed manifold offenses, they never once give rise to any sense of shame at all.

A note on this grade

The lower rank's three grades are graded by the weight of the harm done, not by the strength of any prior practice: the seventh grade commits "the ten evils, offenses of a lesser weight," while the ninth grade, still to come, will commit the five heinous crimes themselves. What Shandao takes care to note here — that even this comparatively lighter offender never once feels shame — sets up the sharp reversal that follows: it is not remorse that saves this being, but a chance encounter with a good friend at the very moment life is about to end.
