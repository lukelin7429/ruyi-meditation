Teachers, Non-Killing, and the Precepts

Shandao closes the first blessing with a deliberate architecture: reverence directed upward, toward parents and teachers alike, paired against compassion directed downward, toward every being whose one shared foundation is simply wanting to stay alive. The second blessing then opens an entirely different register — not devotion, but a structured system of vows.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 2 of 4: 序分義 (Meaning of the Preliminary Section)

Reverence Upward, Compassion Downward

Chinese Source

奉事師長者，此明教示禮節，學識成德，因行無虧，乃至成佛，此猶師之善友力也。此之大恩最須敬重，然父母及師長者名為敬上行也。

言慈心不殺者，此明一切眾生皆以命為本。若見惡緣怖走藏避者，但為護命也。經云：「一切諸眾生無不愛壽命，勿殺勿行杖，恕己可為喻。」即為證也。言修十善業者，此明十惡之中殺業最惡，故列之在初。十善之中長命最善，故以之相對也。已下九惡九善者，至下九品中，次應廣述。此明世善，又名慈下行也。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "serving one's own teachers and elders": this makes plain that it is through their instruction in proper conduct, and through the learning and virtue thereby accomplished, that one's own causal practice remains unbroken, all the way through to the attainment of buddhahood itself — this too is the very power of a teacher's own good and virtuous friendship. This debt of kindness must be most greatly honored and revered — and so both parents and teachers and elders alike are called the practice of reverence toward those above oneself.

As for "a compassionate mind that refrains from killing": this makes plain that every sentient being alike takes its own life as its own foundation. When beings encounter some threatening circumstance and flee in terror to hide themselves away, this is done for no other reason than to protect their own life. As the sūtra says: "Among all sentient beings, there is not one that does not cherish its own life — do not kill, do not raise the rod against another; take your own self as the measure by which to judge." This very passage serves as proof. As for "cultivating the ten good deeds": this makes plain that, among the ten evils, the karma of killing is the very worst, and so it is listed first; and among the ten good deeds, the preservation of life is the very best, and so it is set here in direct correspondence with it. As for the remaining nine evils and nine goods, these shall be explained further and at length below, within the section on the nine grades. This makes plain the mundane good, also called the practice of compassion toward those below oneself.

A note on this pairing

Shandao's own labels for these two halves of the first blessing — "reverence toward those above oneself" and "compassion toward those below oneself" — are worth noticing as a structural claim in their own right. Parents and teachers occupy the position of those to whom a debt is owed; every living being, human or otherwise, occupies the position of those whose own bare wish to survive deserves protection regardless of any debt at all. The single scriptural line quoted here, "take your own self as the measure by which to judge," reduces the entire justification for non-killing to a form of reasoning any being can perform without further instruction: whatever you would not wish done to you, do not do to another. Between the story of the monk's three robes and this plain reciprocity principle, the first blessing closes having grounded itself in two very different registers of proof — a specific narrative example, and a general rule that requires no example at all.

The Second Blessing: Refuge, and the Full Body of Precepts

Chinese Source

二、言受持三歸者，此明世善輕微，感報不具。戒德巍巍，能感菩提之果。但眾生歸信，從淺至深。先受三歸，後教眾戒。言具足眾戒者，然戒有多種，或三歸戒，或五戒、八戒、十善戒、二百二十戒、五百戒、沙彌戒，或菩薩三聚戒、十無盡戒等，故名具足眾戒也。又一一戒品中亦有少分戒、多分戒、全分戒也。言不犯威儀者，此明身口意業，行住坐臥，能與一切戒作方便威儀也。若輕重麁細，皆能護持。犯即悔過，故云不犯威儀。此名戒善也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, as for "receiving and upholding the three refuges": this makes plain that mundane goodness alone is slight, and the retribution it brings is incomplete; whereas the virtue of the precepts towers loftily, and is able to bring about the very fruit of bodhi itself. Yet sentient beings' own taking of refuge and faith proceeds from the shallow toward the deep — first receiving the three refuges, and only afterward being taught the full body of precepts. As for "possessing the full body of precepts": precepts themselves are of many kinds — whether the precepts of the three refuges, the five precepts, the eight precepts, the ten good precepts, the two hundred and twenty precepts, the five hundred precepts, the novice precepts, or the bodhisattva's own threefold pure precepts, the ten inexhaustible precepts, and so forth — and it is for this reason that it is called possessing the full body of precepts. Further, within each and every category of precept, there is also a partial observance, a fuller observance, and a complete observance. As for "not transgressing proper deportment": this makes plain that the karma of body, speech, and mind, in walking, standing, sitting, and lying down alike, is able to serve as the skillful means and proper bearing for every precept whatsoever. Whether light or heavy, coarse or subtle, one is able to guard and uphold it in every case; and should any transgression occur, one repents of it at once — hence it is said, "not transgressing proper deportment." This is called the goodness of the precepts.

A note on this structure

Shandao's own framing — "mundane goodness alone is slight... whereas the virtue of the precepts towers loftily" — marks a clear escalation from the first blessing to the second, not a mere addition to it. What is worth noticing is the pastoral logic he supplies for why refuge must precede precepts rather than the reverse: "sentient beings' own taking of refuge and faith proceeds from the shallow toward the deep." The precepts are not offered as an entry requirement but as a second stage, reached only once trust has already been established. His closing gloss on "not transgressing proper deportment" — that any transgression is met with immediate repentance rather than permanent disqualification — keeps this blessing, for all its structure, still within reach of the same ordinary, faltering practitioner the entire teaching has been addressed to from its very first line.

Part 23 Complete

This closes Part 23. The first blessing is now complete in full — filial devotion, service to teachers, compassionate non-killing, and the ten good deeds — and the second blessing, the three refuges and the full body of precepts, is likewise complete.
