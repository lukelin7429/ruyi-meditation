The Third Grade: Belief Alone

The third and lowest grade of the upper rank marks a genuine descent from the first two: not settled faith, not composed understanding, but a wavering belief that admits its own inconsistency openly. And yet even here, a single sincere moment of weariness with suffering proves enough — so long as doubt never turns against the Mahāyāna teaching itself.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 4 of 4: 散善義 (Meaning of the Dispersed Good)

A Faith That Wavers, and a Single Thought That Suffices

Chinese Source

次就上品下生位中，亦先舉，次辨，後結。即有其八。一、從上品下生者已下，總舉位名，即是大乘下善凡夫人也。二、從亦信因果下至無上道心已來，正明第六門中受法不同。即有其三。一明所信因果不定，或信不信，故名為亦，或可亦同前深信也。又雖信不深，善心數退、惡法數起，此乃由不深信苦樂因果也。若深信生死苦者，罪業畢竟不重犯。若深信淨土無為樂者，善心一發永無退失也。二明信雖間斷，於一切大乘不得疑謗，若起疑謗者，縱使千佛遶身，無由可救也。三明已上諸善似亦無功，唯發一念厭苦，樂生諸佛境界，速滿菩薩大悲願行，還入生死普度眾生，故名發菩提心也。此義第三福中已明竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, within the position of the lowest rebirth of the upper rank, the text likewise first raises the matter, next distinguishes it in detail, and finally concludes it. There are eight points in all.

First, from "the lowest rebirth of the upper rank" onward, this generally names this grade — this is itself the ordinary being of the lesser degree of goodness who has studied the Mahāyāna.

Second, from "also believing in cause and effect" down through "the unsurpassed mind of the path," this makes plain, within the sixth gate, the differing way in which this teaching is received. There are three points within it. First, this makes plain that this being's own belief in cause and effect is not entirely settled — sometimes believing, sometimes not, hence the word "also" — or it may perhaps be equal in depth to the deep faith described earlier. Yet, though their faith is not deep, their own good mind frequently retreats, and evil ways frequently arise — this comes about precisely because their own faith in the causes and effects of suffering and happiness is not yet deep. Were one to hold deep faith in the suffering of birth and death, one's own offenses and karma would ultimately never be committed again so gravely; were one to hold deep faith in the unconditioned bliss of the pure land, once this good mind arose, it would never again retreat or be lost.

Second, this makes plain that, though their own faith may be interrupted, they must never give rise to doubt or slander regarding the Mahāyāna as a whole — should they give rise to such doubt or slander, then even should a thousand buddhas surround their own body, there would be no way at all to save them.

Third, this makes plain that, even should all the preceding forms of goodness seem to bring no merit at all, simply giving rise to a single thought of weariness with suffering, delighting in being reborn within the buddhas' own realm, swiftly fulfilling the bodhisattva's own great compassionate vow and practice, and then returning once more into birth and death to universally liberate sentient beings — this alone is called giving rise to the bodhi-mind. This very meaning has already been made plain within the third blessing described earlier.

A note on this descent

The honesty of this passage is worth noting: rather than describe an idealized practitioner, Shandao names directly what an inconsistent faith actually looks like — "their own good mind frequently retreats, and evil ways frequently arise" — and traces this instability to its precise cause, faith in cause and effect that has not yet become deep. What saves this grade from collapsing entirely is a single hard boundary: doubt may waver everywhere else, but never against the Mahāyāna teaching itself, since that particular doubt, once entertained, admits of no rescue "even should a thousand buddhas surround their own body." Within that one boundary, however, even the humblest possible spiritual event — "a single thought of weariness with suffering" — is declared sufficient, provided it genuinely gives rise to the bodhi-mind.

A Golden Lotus, and a Doubt Answered Directly

Chinese Source

三、從以此功德已下，正明第八門中迴前正行向所求處。四、從行者命欲終時下，至七寶池中已來，正明第九門中臨終聖來迎接去時遲疾。即有其九。一明命延不久。二明彌陀與諸聖眾持金華來應。三明化佛同時授手。四明聖眾同聲等讚。五明行者罪滅故云清淨，述本所修故云發無上道心。六明行者雖覩靈儀，疑心恐不得往生，是故聖眾同聲告言我來迎汝。七明既蒙告及即見自身已坐金華之上，籠籠而合。八明隨佛身後一念即生，九明到彼在寶池中。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, from "with this merit" onward, this makes plain, within the eighth gate, dedicating the preceding correct practice toward the very destination sought.

Fourth, from "when the practitioner's own life is about to end" down through "within the pond of seven jewels," this makes plain, within the ninth gate, the sages coming to welcome this being at the moment life is about to end, and the differing speed, swift or slow, with which they then depart. There are nine points within it. First, this makes plain that this being's own life is not long to continue. Second, this makes plain Amitābha and the assembly of sages coming there, holding a golden lotus flower, in response. Third, this makes plain the transformation-buddhas extending their own hands at the very same moment. Fourth, this makes plain the assembly of sages, with a single voice, offering praise together. Fifth, this makes plain that, since this practitioner's own offenses have been extinguished, this is called purity; and, recounting the practice originally cultivated, this is called giving rise to the unsurpassed mind of the path. Sixth, this makes plain that, though this practitioner beholds this sacred manifestation, their own mind still harbors doubt, fearing they might not attain rebirth after all — and it is for this very reason that the assembly of sages, with a single voice, declares to them: "I have come to welcome you." Seventh, this makes plain that, having received this very declaration, they behold themselves already seated upon this golden lotus flower, its petals gently closing around them. Eighth, this makes plain that, following behind the Buddha's own body, in but a single thought-moment, they are reborn at once. Ninth, this makes plain their arrival there, coming to rest within the very pond of seven jewels.

A note on this reassurance

Point six here extends the wavering faith already described into this practitioner's own final moment — even confronted directly with the sages' own arrival, "their own mind still harbors doubt, fearing they might not attain rebirth after all." The assembly's own response is not correction or rebuke but a direct, simple reassurance, "I have come to welcome you," addressing the doubt on its own terms rather than treating it as a further failure. This consistency, between the grade's own description and its own practitioner's very last recorded emotional state, is a small but deliberate touch — the text does not pretend this being becomes someone else at the moment of death.
