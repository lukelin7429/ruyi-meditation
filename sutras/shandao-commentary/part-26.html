A Mirror, and "You Are an Ordinary Being"

Just as the vision itself is about to open, Shandao pauses to close off a misreading that could undo the entire teaching's usefulness: if Vaidehī's own vision proves she is secretly already a bodhisattva, then nothing about her example applies to anyone else. The Buddha corrects this directly — and Vaidehī, working through the implication herself, arrives at the question the rest of this fascicle exists to answer.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 2 of 4: 序分義 (Meaning of the Preliminary Section)

A Vision Like a Mirror, and Joy That Grants Acceptance

Chinese Source

四、從如來今者下，至得無生忍已來，正明勸修得益之相。此明如來欲為夫人及未來等，顯觀方便，注想西方，捨厭娑婆，貪欣極樂。言以佛力故已下，此明眾生業障觸目生盲，指掌謂遠，他方隔竹篾即踰之千里。豈況凡夫分外，諸佛境內闚心，自非聖力冥加，彼國何由得覩？言如執明鏡自見面像已下，此明夫人及眾生等入觀住心，凝神不捨，心境相應悉皆顯現，當境現時，如似鏡中見物無異也。言心歡喜故得忍者，此明阿彌陀佛國清淨光明忽現眼前，何勝踊躍。因茲喜故即得無生之忍，亦名喜忍，亦名悟忍，亦名信忍。此乃玄談，未標得處。欲令夫人等悕心此益，勇猛專精心想見時，方應悟忍。此多是十信中忍，非解行已上忍也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, from "the Tathāgata now" down through "attained the acceptance of non-arising," this makes plain the exhortation to cultivate, and the marks of the benefit thereby gained. This makes plain that the Tathāgata wished, for the sake of the queen and of beings still to come, to reveal the expedient means of contemplation — fixing one's thought upon the West, casting off and wearying of this Sahā world, delighting in and longing for the Land of Ultimate Bliss. As for "through the Buddha's own power" and what follows: this makes plain that sentient beings' own karmic obstructions leave them blind before what is right in front of their own eyes — they take even the palm of their own hand to be distant, and, separated from some other place by no more than a bamboo screen, still imagine it a thousand miles away. How much more so, then, for an ordinary being, entirely beyond their own capacity, to peer with their own mind into a realm belonging to the buddhas themselves — unless the hidden power of the sages should come to their aid, how could that land ever be beheld at all? As for "as one holds up a bright mirror and beholds one's own reflection" and what follows: this makes plain that the queen, and sentient beings generally, entering contemplation and settling their own minds, their spirit fixed and unwavering, find that mind and object correspond to one another and both appear together in full — and, at the very moment that object appears, it is exactly as though one saw a thing reflected in a mirror, no different at all. As for "because the mind rejoices, one thereby attains this acceptance": this makes plain that Amitābha's own pure and radiant land suddenly appears before one's own eyes — what joy could surpass this? And it is on account of this very joy that one attains the acceptance of non-arising, also called the acceptance of joy, also called the acceptance of awakening, also called the acceptance of faith. This, however, is a profound and mysterious matter, and the precise stage at which it is attained is not here specified. The intent is only to cause the queen and others to set their own hearts hopefully upon this benefit, so that, striving with courage and single-minded diligence, at the very moment their own contemplative vision appears, this should indeed be understood as the acceptance of awakening. This is, for the most part, the acceptance belonging to the ten stages of faith, not the acceptance belonging to the stages of understanding and practice above them.

A note on this acceptance

Shandao's own caution at the close of this passage matters more than it might first appear: having just described an "acceptance of non-arising" — a genuinely advanced attainment in most Mahāyāna doctrinal schemes — he immediately clarifies that what is meant here belongs to "the ten stages of faith," among the very earliest tiers of the bodhisattva path, not to the far more rarefied "stages of understanding and practice above them." This is not hedging for its own sake. It is the same structural move already seen with the light that bestowed the king's own "lesser fruit" back in Part 21: a benefit calibrated to what an ordinary being can actually reach, offered without pretending it is more than what it is.

"You Are an Ordinary Being," and Borrowed Sight

Chinese Source

五、從佛告韋提下，至令汝得見已來，正明夫人是凡非聖。由非聖故，仰惟聖力冥加，彼國雖遙得覩。此明如來恐眾生置惑，謂言夫人是聖非凡。由起疑故，即自生怯弱。然韋提現是菩薩，假示凡身。我等罪人，無由比及。為斷此疑，故言汝是凡夫也。言心想羸劣者，由是凡故曾無大志也。言未得天眼者，此明夫人肉眼，所見遠近，不足為言。況淨土彌遙，云何可見？言諸佛如來有異方便已下，此明若依心所見國土莊嚴者，非汝凡能普，悉歸功於佛也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, from "the Buddha told Vaidehī" down through "and cause you to behold it," this makes plain that the queen is herself an ordinary being, not a sage. Precisely because she is not a sage, she must look up in reliance upon the hidden power of the sages themselves, and only thus, though that land lies so distant, is she able to behold it. This makes plain that the Tathāgata, fearing that sentient beings might fall into doubt, thinking that the queen must herself already be some sage rather than an ordinary being, and that, giving rise to this very doubt, they might then grow timid and lose heart themselves — since Vaidehī, they might think, is in truth already a bodhisattva, merely manifesting an ordinary body for our sake, while we, sinful beings that we are, could never hope to compare to her — in order to cut off this very doubt, the Buddha therefore said directly: "You are an ordinary being." As for "your own power of thought is weak and inferior": this is precisely because, being an ordinary being, she had never yet given rise to any vast aspiration. As for "not yet having attained the heavenly eye": this makes plain that the queen's own physical eyes, whatever they might behold near or far, are hardly worth speaking of at all — how much less, then, could they behold the pure land, lying at so much greater a distance still? As for "the buddhas and tathāgatas possess some further, extraordinary skillful means" and what follows: this makes plain that, if the splendor and adornment of that land is to be beheld through the mind at all, this is never something an ordinary being could accomplish through their own power alone — the entire credit for it belongs to the Buddha.

A note on this correction

This passage returns to the argument Shandao has been building since Fascicle 1: that the sūtra's own beneficiaries are ordinary beings, not sages already advanced along the path. Here the stakes of that argument become concrete, in the person of Vaidehī herself. Left uncorrected, her vision of the pure land could be read as proof that only someone secretly already a bodhisattva could ever see it — which would make her entire example useless as a model for anyone else. The Buddha's blunt correction, "you are an ordinary being," together with Shandao's own gloss that the vision is entirely "the Buddha's own skillful means" rather than any innate capacity of hers, keeps the whole teaching applicable to precisely the audience it was given for.

A Question That Anticipates the Buddha's Own Absence

Chinese Source

六、從時韋提白佛下，至見彼國土已來，明其夫人重牒前恩，欲生起後問之意。此明夫人領解佛意，如上光臺所見，謂是己能。向見世尊開示，始知是佛方便之恩。若爾者，佛今在世，眾生蒙念，可使得見西方。佛若涅槃，不蒙加備者，云何得見也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth, from "at that time, Vaidehī addressed the Buddha" down through "behold that land," this makes plain the queen reiterating her own gratitude for this earlier grace, so as to give rise to the intent behind the question that follows. This makes plain that the queen, now understanding the Buddha's own intent, realized that what she had earlier beheld upon that radiant platform, she had at the time mistaken for her own ability — but only now, having heard the World-Honored One's own explanation, did she come to understand that it was, in truth, the Buddha's own gracious skillful means. If this is so, she reasoned, then now, while the Buddha remains in the world, sentient beings, receiving his own mindful attention, might indeed be caused to behold the West. But should the Buddha enter final nirvāṇa, and beings no longer receive this assistance, how then could they ever behold it at all?

A note on this question

Vaidehī's own realization here — that what she had taken for her own ability was, all along, "the Buddha's own gracious skillful means" — sets up a question with consequences for every reader of this sūtra long after this scene ends. If direct divine assistance were the only way to see the pure land, the entire teaching would apply only to those fortunate enough to stand, as she did, in the Buddha's own living presence. Her question — what happens once the Buddha is gone — is thus not idle curiosity but the exact anxiety of every future practitioner reading this text after the Buddha's own final nirvāṇa, including everyone reading it now. What answers this question is precisely the sixteen contemplations that follow: a method, rather than a presence, that outlives the teacher who first demonstrated it.
