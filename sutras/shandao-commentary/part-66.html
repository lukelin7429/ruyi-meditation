Closing the Mahāsthāmaprāpta Contemplation

Shandao resolves the apparent contradiction between two sūtras with a genuinely subtle distinction: the suffering of the three realms is absolute, containing no true happiness at all, while whatever might be called "suffering" within the pure land itself is purely relative — a lower attainment measured only against a higher one, never against genuine misery.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 3 of 4: 定善義 (Meaning of the Contemplative Good)

Two Kinds of Suffering: Absolute, and Purely Relative

Chinese Source

答曰：「今言苦樂者有二種：一者三界中苦樂，二者淨土中苦樂。言三界苦樂者，苦則三塗八苦等；樂則人天五欲放逸繫縛等樂，雖言是樂然是大苦，必竟無有一念真實樂也。言淨土苦樂者，苦則地前望地上為苦；地上望地前為樂。下智證望上智證為苦，上智證望下智證為樂，此例舉一可知也。今言度苦眾生者，但為進下位令昇上位，轉下證令得上證。稱本所求即名為樂，故言度苦也。若不然者，淨土之中一切聖人皆以無漏為體，大悲為用，畢竟常住離於分段之生滅，更就何義名為苦也？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: "Now, when speaking of suffering and bliss, there are two distinct senses. The first is the suffering and bliss found within the three realms of ordinary existence. The second is the suffering and bliss found within the pure land itself. As for the suffering and bliss of the three realms: suffering here means the three evil destinies, the eight forms of suffering, and all the rest; bliss here means the pleasures of gods and humans, indulging in the five desires, bound and entangled by them — and though this is called bliss, it is in truth a great suffering, containing not even a single moment of genuine, true happiness at all. As for the suffering and bliss of the pure land: suffering here means that, measured against those who have already ascended the bodhisattva grounds, those who have not yet ascended them experience this as suffering; while, measured against those not yet ascended, those already ascended experience this as bliss. Likewise, measured against realization of the higher wisdom, realization of the lower wisdom is experienced as suffering; and, measured against realization of the lower wisdom, realization of the higher wisdom is experienced as bliss. This single example makes the whole pattern clear. Now, as for what is meant by 'liberating sentient beings from suffering': this refers only to advancing those in a lower position so that they might ascend to a higher one, transforming a lower realization into the attainment of a higher one. Since this accords with what each being originally sought, it is therefore called bliss — and hence it is spoken of as liberating from suffering. Were this not so, then, within the pure land itself, every one of its sages takes freedom from all defilement as their very substance, and great compassion as their very function, abiding there eternally, entirely apart from birth and death marked out in segments — by what possible reasoning, then, could anything there ever be called suffering at all?"

A note on this distinction

This resolution preserves both sūtras without contradiction by relocating the entire meaning of "suffering" once the frame shifts from this defiled world to the pure land itself: the same word names two categorically different things. In the three realms, "bliss" is exposed as false — even pleasure there is, in truth, "a great suffering" containing no genuine happiness at all. Within the pure land, by contrast, both words become purely comparative, meaningful only in relation to another practitioner's own further attainment — a bodhisattva not yet ascended experiencing something as suffering only because someone else has ascended further, never because any actual misery is present. Mahāsthāmaprāpta's own "liberating from suffering" is thus revealed as an act of advancement, not rescue from genuine harm — moving a being from a lower attainment to a higher one they already sought, which is itself what "bliss" already means.

Closing the Contemplation, and Two Bodies Roaming Free

Chinese Source

十、從作此觀者下，至十一觀已來，正明辨觀邪正總結分齊。十一、從觀此菩薩者已下，正明修觀利益除罪多劫。十二、從作此觀者下，至淨妙國土已來，正明總結前文重生後益。十三、從此觀成已下，正明總牒二身，辨觀成相。斯乃勢至威高坐搖他國，能使分身雲集演法利生，永絕胞胎常遊法界。上來雖有十三句不同，廣解勢至觀竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Tenth, from "one who forms this contemplation" down through "the eleventh contemplation," this makes plain the distinguishing of the mark of what is correct from what is mistaken in this practice, gathering together the general conclusion and marking out its own boundary. Eleventh, from "one who contemplates this bodhisattva" onward, this makes plain the benefit of cultivating this contemplation — the removal of offenses accumulated across many kalpas. Twelfth, from "one who forms this contemplation" down through "the pure and wondrous land," this makes plain the general conclusion of the preceding text, opening the further benefit that follows. Thirteenth, from "once this contemplation is accomplished" onward, this makes plain the general citing of both these bodily forms together, distinguishing the mark of this contemplation's own accomplishment. This, then, is Mahāsthāmaprāpta, whose own majestic dignity, even seated, shakes other realms, able to cause his own divided bodies to gather like clouds, expounding the Dharma to benefit sentient beings, forever severed from the womb's own confinement, roaming eternally throughout the entire Dharma-realm. Though the above has unfolded across thirteen distinct points, this concludes the full explanation of the Mahāsthāmaprāpta contemplation.

A note on this close

The phrase "forever severed from the womb's own confinement" closes this contemplation by naming exactly what Mahāsthāmaprāpta's own manner of appearance has left permanently behind: unlike beings still bound within the "unopened lotus" described back in the water contemplation's own verse, his own mode of existence — dividing bodies at will, roaming the entire Dharma-realm — represents a form of freedom no longer subject to any process of gradual emergence at all. This closes the eleventh of the sixteen contemplations.

This closes Part 66, and with it the Mahāsthāmaprāpta contemplation, the eleventh of the sixteen, in full — spanning Parts 64 through 66.
