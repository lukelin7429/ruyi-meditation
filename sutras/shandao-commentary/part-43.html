Eight Ponds, Eight Virtues

Even the most exquisite trees, Shandao insists, are not enough on their own — a land needs water to be truly complete. The fifth contemplation opens with eight ponds flowing from a single wish-fulfilling jewel, their water possessing eight distinct virtues mapped precisely onto the six sense-spheres, so that purity itself becomes something felt through sight, smell, touch, and taste all at once.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 3 of 4: 定善義 (Meaning of the Contemplative Good)

Why Even Exquisite Trees Are Not Enough

Chinese Source

五、就寶池觀中，亦先舉，次辨，後結。即有其七。一、從次當想水已下，總舉觀名，即是牒前生後。此明寶樹雖精，若無池水，亦未名好。一為不空世界，二為莊嚴依報，為斯義故，有此池渠觀也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, within the jeweled pond contemplation, the text likewise first raises the matter, next distinguishes it in detail, and finally concludes it. There are seven points in all.

First, from "next, you should contemplate water" onward, this generally names this contemplation, citing what precedes and opening what follows. This makes plain that, however exquisite these jeweled trees may be, without ponds of water, this land could not yet be called truly excellent. First, so that this world might not be left empty; second, so as to adorn its own dependent retribution — it is for these very reasons that this contemplation of ponds and channels exists.

Eight Virtues, Mapped to the Six Sense-Spheres

Chinese Source

二、從極樂國土下，至如意珠王生已來，正明池數并辨出處。即有其五。一明標指所歸之國。二明池有八數之名。三明一一池岸七寶合成，正由寶光映徹通照，八德之水一同雜寶之色，故名寶水也。四明是諸眾寶體性柔軟。五明八池之水皆從如意寶中出，即名如意水。此水即有八種之德。一者清淨潤澤，即是色入攝。二者不臭，即是香入攝。三者輕，四者冷，五者軟，即是觸入攝。六者美，是味入攝。七者飲時調適，八者飲已無患，是法入攝。此八德之義已在彌陀義中廣說竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, from "the Land of Ultimate Bliss" down through "arising from the wish-fulfilling jewel king," this makes plain the number of these ponds, and distinguishes their own place of origin. There are five points within it. First, this makes plain, pointing directly, the very land to which one goes. Second, this makes plain that these ponds are named by the number eight. Third, this makes plain that each and every pond's own bank is formed of seven jewels combined together, and, precisely because these jewels' own light shines through and illuminates everywhere, the water possessing these eight virtues shares the very same coloring as this assortment of jewels — hence it is called jeweled water. Fourth, this makes plain that the very substance and nature of these many jewels is itself soft and yielding. Fifth, this makes plain that the water of these eight ponds flows entirely from the wish-fulfilling jewel, and is thus called wish-fulfilling water. This water possesses eight distinct virtues. The first is purity and moistness, which belongs to the sense-sphere of form. The second is freedom from any foul odor, which belongs to the sense-sphere of smell. The third is lightness, the fourth is coolness, the fifth is softness, which belong to the sense-sphere of touch. The sixth is sweetness, which belongs to the sense-sphere of taste. The seventh is that, when drunk, it brings perfect comfort and ease; the eighth is that, once drunk, it leaves no affliction behind — these belong to the sense-sphere of mental phenomena. The meaning of these eight virtues has already been discussed at length within the commentary on Amitābha.

A note on this mapping

What makes this eightfold breakdown more than decorative enumeration is Shandao's explicit assignment of each virtue to a specific sense-sphere: purity to sight, freedom from odor to smell, lightness and coolness and softness to touch, sweetness to taste, and the two final virtues — comfort while drinking, and no affliction afterward — to the sphere of mental phenomena itself. This is a claim that the water's own purity is not a single quality experienced vaguely, but something that engages every mode of perception distinctly and completely, leaving no sense untouched by it — including, in those final two virtues, the mind's own experience of drinking it, before and after.

A Verse: From Fragrance to an Entire Palace Complex

Chinese Source

又讚云：「極樂莊嚴安養國，八德寶池流遍滿，四岸含暉間七寶，水色分明映寶光，體性柔軟無堅觸，菩薩徐行散寶香。寶香寶雲成寶蓋，寶蓋臨空覆寶幢，寶幢嚴儀圍寶殿，寶殿寶鈴垂珠網，寶網寶樂千重轉，隨機讚歎寶宮樓。一一宮樓有佛會，恒沙聖眾坐思量，願此有緣常憶念，捨命同生彼法堂。」

Ru-Yi Translation

A further verse of praise says: "The Land of Peace and Bliss, adorned in splendor, its jeweled ponds of eight virtues flow and fill it everywhere; its four banks hold radiant light, interspersed with seven jewels, the water's own color clear and distinct, reflecting jeweled light; its very substance is soft and yielding, free of any hardness to the touch, as bodhisattvas walk slowly by, scattering jeweled fragrance. This jeweled fragrance and these jeweled clouds form their own jeweled canopy, this jeweled canopy hanging in the sky, covering a jeweled banner; this jeweled banner, solemnly adorned, encircles a jeweled palace, this jeweled palace hung with jeweled bells and nets of pearl; this jeweled net's own jeweled music turns through a thousand layers, praising, according to each being's own capacity, these jeweled palaces and towers. Within each and every palace and tower is an assembly of the Buddha, sages as numerous as the sands of the river seated there in contemplation; may all who share this very affinity constantly bear it in mind, and, when this present life ends, be reborn together within that hall of the Dharma."

A note on this chain

This verse builds an unbroken causal chain from something as ordinary as a bodhisattva's own footsteps: walking releases fragrance, fragrance gathers into cloud, cloud forms a canopy, the canopy shelters a banner, the banner surrounds a palace, and the palace's own bells and nets produce music that praises the palace itself, "according to each being's own capacity" — a self-generating structure where each stage produces the material for the next, with no point at which an external builder or architect is required. The verse's final lines then turn from description to direct petition, exactly as seen in the earlier verses beneath the jeweled banners: "may all who share this very affinity constantly bear it in mind" is no longer explaining the pure land but inviting the reader into it.

Part 43 Complete

This closes Part 43. Part 44 continues with the fourteen channels these ponds divide into, each with its own golden banks and jeweled sand, followed by the water's own inconceivable capacity to weave among the jeweled trees themselves, rising and falling without obstruction.
