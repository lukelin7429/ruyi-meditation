Opening Fascicle 2, and "Thus Have I Heard"

Fascicle 2, the Xufen Yi (序分義, "Meaning of the Preliminary Section"), turns from Fascicle 1's doctrinal framework to the sūtra's own text — beginning with Shandao's map of its entire structure, preface through transmission, and his own reading of the four opening words every sūtra shares, "thus have I heard," as already carrying two full arguments about trustworthiness within them.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 2 of 4: 序分義 (Meaning of the Preliminary Section)

The Sūtra's Own Fivefold Structure

Chinese Source

沙門善導集記

從此以下就文料簡，略作五門明義：一、從如是我聞下，至五苦所逼云何見極樂世界已來，明其序分。二、從日觀初句佛告韋提汝及眾生下，至下品下生已來，明正宗分。三、從說是語時下，至諸天發心已來，正明得益分。四從阿難白佛下，至韋提等歡喜已來，明流通分。此之四義，佛在王宮一會正說。五、從阿難為耆闍大眾傳說，復是一會，亦有三分：一、從爾時世尊足步虛空還耆闍崛山已來，明其序分。二、從阿難廣為大眾說如上事已來，明正宗分。

三、從一切大眾歡喜奉行已來，明流通分。然化必有由，故先明序。由序既興，正陳所說，次明正宗。為說既周，欲以所說傳持末代，歎勝勸學，後明流通。上來雖有五義不同。略料簡序、正、流通義竟。

又就前序中復分為二：一、從如是我聞一句，名為證信序。二、從一時下至云何見極樂世界已來，正明發起序。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compiled and recorded by the śramaṇa Shandao

From this point on, following the sūtra's own text, I will make a brief fivefold analysis to explain its meaning. First, from "thus have I heard" down through "pressed by the five sufferings, how could she see the world of Ultimate Bliss," this makes plain the preface. Second, from the sun-contemplation's opening line, "the Buddha said to Vaidehī, you and every sentient being," down through the lowest of the lower grade of rebirth, this makes plain the main teaching. Third, from "just as he spoke these words" down through "the gods gave rise to the aspiration," this makes plain the section on benefit received. Fourth, from Ānanda's own address to the Buddha down through Vaidehī and the others' rejoicing, this makes plain the section on transmission. These four sections together make up the Buddha's own direct teaching, spoken in a single assembly in the royal palace. Fifth, Ānanda's own retelling of this teaching for the assembly at Vulture Peak is a further, separate assembly, which likewise has three sections: first, from "at that time the World-Honored One, walking through the air, returned to Vulture Peak" onward, this makes plain its own preface; second, from Ānanda's own full account of the foregoing events to the assembly onward, this makes plain its own main teaching.

Third, from "the entire assembly rejoiced and put this into practice" onward, this makes plain its own section on transmission. Now, any act of teaching must have its own occasion, and so the preface comes first; once the occasion for the preface has arisen, the actual teaching is set forth directly, and so the main teaching comes next; once the teaching has been given in full, wishing to have what has been taught carried forward and upheld into the final age, praising its excellence and urging others to study it, the section on transmission comes last. Though the above has unfolded across five distinct sections, this concludes the brief analysis of the preface, the main teaching, and the transmission.

And further, within the preface itself, there is a further division into two: first, the single phrase "thus have I heard," which is called the preface establishing trustworthiness; and second, from "at one time" down through "how could she see the world of Ultimate Bliss," which makes plain the preface of the arising occasion.

A note on this structure

Shandao's fivefold map is worth holding in mind as a table of contents for the rest of this commentary. Fascicle 2, the volume this page opens, covers only the first of the five: the preface. The sūtra's main teaching — the sixteen contemplations and nine grades of rebirth — waits for Fascicles 3 and 4. Notably, Shandao also flags that the sūtra as we have it records not one occasion of teaching but two: the direct address to Vaidehī in the royal palace, and Ānanda's own retelling of the same events afterward to the larger assembly gathered at Vulture Peak — each with its own preface, main teaching, and transmission in miniature. The sūtra's narrative frame, in other words, is doubled from the very start: what Vaidehī heard privately becomes, almost immediately, a public teaching Ānanda carries to everyone else.

"Thus Have I Heard": Two Words for the Teacher, Two for the Listener

Chinese Source

初言證信者即有二義：一謂如是二字，即總標教主，能說之人。二謂我聞兩字，即別指阿難，能聽之人。故言如是我聞。此即雙釋二意也。

又言如是者，即指法定散兩門也。是即定辭。機行必益。此明如來所說，言無錯謬，故名如是。又言如者，如眾生意也。隨心所樂，佛即度之。機教相應，復稱為是。故言如是。又言如是者，欲明如來所說，說漸如漸，說頓如頓，說相如相，說空如空，說人法如人法，說天法如天法，說小如小，說大如大，說凡如凡，說聖如聖，說因如因，說果如果，說苦如苦，說樂如樂，說遠如遠，說近如近，說同如同，說別如別，說淨如淨，說穢如穢。說一切諸法千差萬別，如來觀知歷歷了然。隨心起行，各益不同。業果法然，眾無錯失，又稱為是。故言如是。

言我聞者。欲明阿難是佛侍者，常隨佛後多聞廣識，身臨座下，能聽能持，教旨親承，表無傳說之錯。故曰我聞也。

又言證信者。欲明阿難稟承佛教傳持末代，為對眾生故如是觀法我從佛聞，證誠可信。故名證信序。此就阿難解也。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, as for what establishes trustworthiness, there are two points here: first, the two words "thus" and "is," which together mark out the teacher, the one who speaks; and second, the two words "I" and "heard," which specifically indicate Ānanda, the one who listens. Hence it is said, "thus have I heard" — this is a single phrase that explains both of these two meanings at once.

And further, as for the meaning of "thus," this refers to the two gates of the Dharma, the contemplative and the dispersed. This is itself the fixed formula: whatever capacity engages this practice will certainly benefit from it. This makes plain that what the Tathāgata has spoken carries no error or mistake at all — hence it is called "thus." And further, "thus" also means "according with" — according with the very mind of sentient beings themselves. Wherever their own minds find joy, the Buddha thereby brings them across; capacity and teaching accord with one another, and this too is why it is called "is." Hence it is said, "thus is." And further, "thus is" also means to make plain that whatever the Tathāgata speaks — the gradual as it truly is gradual, the sudden as it truly is sudden, marks as they truly are marks, emptiness as it truly is emptiness, the teaching for persons as it truly is for persons, the teaching for heavenly beings as it truly is for heavenly beings, the lesser as it truly is lesser, the greater as it truly is greater, the ordinary as it truly is ordinary, the sagely as it truly is sagely, cause as it truly is cause, fruit as it truly is fruit, suffering as it truly is suffering, bliss as it truly is bliss, the distant as it truly is distant, the near as it truly is near, the same as it truly is the same, the different as it truly is different, the pure as it truly is pure, the defiled as it truly is defiled — speaking of every dharma in its thousand and ten thousand distinctions, the Tathāgata perceives and knows each one, perfectly clear and distinct. According with the mind, he gives rise to practice, and each brings its own particular benefit; karma and its fruit follow their own natural law, and the assembly is never once led astray — this, too, is why it is called "is." Hence it is said, "thus is."

As for "I heard": this makes plain that Ānanda, as the Buddha's own attendant, constantly followed close behind him, learning broadly and widely, present in person beneath his seat, able both to hear and to hold fast to what he heard, receiving the teaching's own intent directly and in person — showing that there is no possibility of any error in what has been transmitted. Hence it is said, "I heard."

And further, as for what is meant by "establishing trustworthiness": this makes plain that Ānanda, having received the Buddha's own teaching and carried it forward into the final age, on behalf of sentient beings, speaks in this way — "this method of contemplation, I myself heard directly from the Buddha" — a testimony that is honest and can be trusted. Hence it is called the preface establishing trustworthiness. This is explained specifically in terms of Ānanda himself.

A note on this exegesis

Shandao's unpacking of "thus have I heard" takes what most readers pass over as a formulaic opening and treats it as already making two separate, load-bearing claims. "Thus" (如) is read twice, in two directions at once: as "correct, without error" — a claim about the teaching's own accuracy — and as "according with" the listener's own mind — a claim about the teaching's own responsiveness. The long catalogue that follows, pairing off dozens of opposites (gradual and sudden, suffering and bliss, the same and the different, the pure and the defiled), is Shandao's own way of insisting that the Buddha's speech never distorts whatever it describes: when he speaks of the sudden, it truly is the sudden; when he speaks of the defiled, it truly is the defiled, without softening or exaggeration in either direction. And "heard" (聞), rather than being merely Ānanda's own biographical detail, becomes evidence for the sūtra's own reliability: Ānanda's proximity to the Buddha, his own trained memory, his role as the one disciple who received teachings directly and in person, all function here as the sūtra's own chain of custody, established before a single word of its actual content has been spoken.
