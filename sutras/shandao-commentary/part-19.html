A World Renounced, A Practice Requested

Vaidehī's grief resolves into something more deliberate: a formal renunciation of this entire world, offered without exception even for her own son, followed by confession and a direct request for a method. The register shifts from lament to petition — and it is this petition, not the grief that preceded it, that the rest of the sūtra actually answers.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 2 of 4: 序分義 (Meaning of the Preliminary Section)

A General Request, and the World Named Outright

Chinese Source

五、就欣淨緣中即有其八。

一、從唯願世尊為我廣說下，至濁惡世也已來，正明夫人通請所求。別標苦界，此明夫人遇自身苦覺世非常。六道同然，無有安心之地。此聞佛說淨土無生，願捨穢身證彼無為之樂。

二、從此濁惡處下，至不見惡人已來，正明夫人舉出所厭之境。此明閻浮總惡，未有一處可貪。但以幻惑愚夫，飲斯長苦。言此濁惡處者，正明苦界也。又明器世間，亦是眾生依報處，亦名眾生所依處也。言地獄等已下，三品惡果最重也。言盈滿者，此三苦聚非直獨指閻浮，娑婆亦皆遍有，故言盈滿。言多不善聚者，此明三界六道不同種類恒沙，隨心差別。經云：業能莊識，世世處處各趣隨緣受果報，對面不相知。言願我未來已下，此明夫人真心徹到，厭苦娑婆，欣樂無為，永歸常樂。但無為之境，不可輕爾即階。苦惱娑婆，無由輒然得離。自非發金剛之志，永絕生死之元。若不親從慈尊，何能勉斯長歎。然願我未來不聞惡聲惡人者，此明如闍王、調達殺父破僧及惡聲等，願亦不聞不見。但闍王既是親生之子，上於父母起於殺心，何況疎人而不相害？是故夫人不簡親疎，總皆頓捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, within the condition of joyful aspiration for purity, there are eight points.

First, from "I pray only that the World-Honored One might explain this to me in full" down through "this defiled and evil world," this makes plain the queen's own general request, stating what she sought. Naming specifically this realm of suffering, this makes plain that the queen, having met with suffering in her own person, came to realize how impermanent this world truly is. All six destinies alike offer no place for the mind to find rest. Hearing the Buddha teach of a pure land free of birth, she wished to cast off this defiled body and realize that unconditioned bliss.

Second, from "this defiled and evil place" down through "nor see any evil person," this makes plain the queen naming outright the realm she had come to despise. This makes plain that Jambudvīpa is, in its entirety, an evil place, with not a single spot in it worth coveting — yet foolish men, deceived by its illusions, go on drinking down this prolonged suffering all the same. As for "this defiled and evil place": this names directly the realm of suffering itself. It further makes plain that this material world is itself the very locus of sentient beings' own dependent retribution — that is, it is called the very place sentient beings depend upon. As for "the hells" and what follows: these are the three grades of evil retribution, the very heaviest of all. As for "filled full": this threefold mass of suffering is not confined to Jambudvīpa alone — this Sahā world entire is everywhere pervaded by it, hence it is said to be "filled full." As for "a great mass of what is not good": this makes plain that the various kinds of beings throughout the three realms and six destinies are as numberless as the sands of the river, each differing according to its own mind. As the sūtra says: karma alone adorns consciousness, and in life after life, place after place, each being follows its own conditions into its own destiny and receives its own retribution, passing one another face to face without ever knowing it. As for "I pray that in future" and what follows: this makes plain that the queen's own sincere heart had penetrated through entirely — wearied of the suffering of this Sahā world, joyfully longing for the unconditioned, wishing to return forever to constant bliss. Yet the unconditioned realm cannot be ascended lightly or all at once; nor can the suffering and vexation of this Sahā world be cast off so readily. Unless one gives rise to a resolve as unbreakable as vajra, one cannot forever sever the root of birth and death; and unless one turns in person to the compassionate honored one, how could one ever be spared this prolonged lament? As for her wish that in future she might neither hear evil words nor see evil persons: this makes plain that beings such as Ajātaśatru and Devadatta, who kill their own fathers and break apart the Sangha, along with their evil reputations and all the rest — she wished never to hear of them nor see them again. Yet since Ajātaśatru was her own natural-born son, and even against his own parents he had given rise to murderous intent, how much more so, then, toward strangers, would he fail to do harm? For this very reason, the queen made no distinction between kin and stranger, and renounced all of them at once.

A note on this renunciation

Shandao's own gloss makes explicit something the sūtra's own wording leaves implicit: Vaidehī's request to never again hear "evil words or see evil persons" is not a generic wish but a direct reference to her own son, named here without euphemism. What makes this remarkable is the reasoning Shandao supplies for why she includes strangers in the same renunciation — not equal concern for all beings in the abstract, but a bleak inference drawn from her own experience: if her own son could turn murderous against his own parents, no stranger could be trusted either. Her renunciation of this entire world is thus not a serene detachment but a conclusion forced on her by grief, extended by logic to its full scope.

Confession, and a Request for a Method

Chinese Source

三、從今向世尊下，至懺悔已來，正明夫人淨土妙處非善不生。恐有餘愆，障不得往。是以求哀更須懺悔。

四、從唯願佛日下，至清淨業處已來，正明夫人通請去行。此明夫人上即通請生處，今亦通請得生之行。言佛日者，法喻雙標也。譬如日出，眾闇盡除。佛智輝光，無明之夜日朗。言教我觀於清淨已下，正明既能厭穢欣淨，若為安心注想，得生清淨處也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, from "now, before the World-Honored One" down through "repentance," this makes plain that the wondrous realm of the pure land cannot be reached except by the good, and the queen, fearing that some remaining offense of her own might yet obstruct her from going there, therefore begged for mercy and further sought to repent.

Fourth, from "I pray only that the Buddha's own sun" down through "a place of pure karma," this makes plain the queen's own general request for the practice that would let her go there. This makes plain that the queen, having above made her general request for the place of rebirth itself, now also makes her general request for the practice by which that rebirth is attained. As for "the Buddha's own sun": this names both the teaching and its analogy together. Just as when the sun rises, every darkness is entirely dispelled, so too does the Buddha's own radiant wisdom brighten the long night of ignorance like the sun itself. As for "teach me to contemplate that which is pure" and what follows: this makes plain that, now able both to weary of the defiled and to delight in the pure, she asks how she might settle her own mind and fix her own thought, so as to attain rebirth in that pure place.

A note on this request

Shandao is careful to distinguish two separate requests that a casual reading might collapse into one: first, in point one, a request for a place — somewhere free of this suffering; only now, in point four, a request for a practice — some actual method by which that place could be reached. Between the two comes something easy to skip past, confession, which Shandao frames not as ritual formality but as the queen's own honest fear that some remaining fault of her own might block the way even once a destination and a method are both in hand. The structure itself makes a doctrinal point before any doctrine is stated outright: wanting a pure land is not sufficient, and neither is wanting a method — something in the aspirant's own moral account must also be settled first.

Part 19 Complete

This closes Part 19. The queen has now renounced this defiled world entire, confessed her own remaining faults, and asked for a method. Part 20 answers her directly: the Buddha emits light from between his brows, and every pure land in the ten directions appears before her at once, closing "the condition of joyful aspiration for purity" in full.
