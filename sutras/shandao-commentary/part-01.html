Opening Verse, and the Name of the Sūtra

This page opens Ru-Yi's translation of Shandao's Commentary on the Contemplation Sūtra (觀無量壽佛經疏, commonly the Four-Fascicle Commentary, 觀經四帖疏) — the single most consequential work of exegesis in the entire history of Pure Land Buddhism, the text that directly gave rise to Hōnen's founding of Japan's Jōdo-shū school five centuries later. It carries Shandao's own opening verse of homage, and the first two of his seven analytical gates: marking out the sūtra's preface, and explaining its own name in full.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 1 of 4: 玄義分 (Profound Meaning)

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Taishō volume 37, no. 1753, the Guan Wuliangshou Fo Jing Shu (觀無量壽佛經疏), composed by Shandao (善導, 613–681), the third of the seven patriarchs of the Chinese Pure Land lineage as later tradition counts them, and — by most modern accounts — the most historically influential of all of them. This four-fascicle commentary on the Contemplation Sūtra already anchors the reading notes across this site's own translation of that sūtra; this new collection translates Shandao's commentary directly and in full, in his own right, rather than only as a secondary source cited elsewhere. Shandao's own historical significance is difficult to overstate: it was a single line from this very commentary — "if one single-mindedly holds fast to Amitābha's name... this is called the rightly established practice, because it accords with the Buddha's own vow" — that led the twelfth-century Japanese monk Hōnen to found Jōdo-shū, Japan's first independent Pure Land school, breaking from the earlier Tendai practice of treating nianfo as one contemplative technique among many. Hōnen's own disciple Shinran, whose hand-annotation of Tanluan's commentary already appears on this site's Rebirth Treatise pages, in turn built Jōdo Shinshū directly on Hōnen's own reading of Shandao.

Given the sheer scale of this work — 46,102 Chinese characters across four fascicles, roughly fifteen times the length of the Rebirth Treatise already translated here — this collection follows the same approach already used for this site's translation of Yuanying's commentary on the Śūraṅgama Sūtra: Shandao's own prose is translated directly and continuously, including both his citations of the base sūtra and his own extended argument, without a further layer of secondary "reading notes" from another commentator — since Shandao's own voice, arguing a genuinely contested question in his own right, is the primary content here, not commentary on commentary. This first fascicle, the Xuanyi Fen (玄義分, "Profound Meaning"), sets out Shandao's own sevenfold analytical framework before he turns to the sūtra's text itself in the fascicles that follow; it runs to roughly 8,100 characters and is translated here across five parts. Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right. See also this site's note on translation method.

Opening Verse: Taking Refuge

Chinese Source

先勸大眾發願歸三寶

道俗時眾等，　各發無上心，
生死甚難厭，　佛法復難欣，
共發金剛志，　橫超斷四流，
願入彌陀界，　歸依合掌禮。

世尊我一心，　歸命盡十方，
法性真如海，　報化等諸佛。

一一菩薩身，　眷屬等無量，
莊嚴及變化，　十地三賢海。

時劫滿未滿，　智行圓未圓，
正使盡未盡，　習氣亡未亡，
功用無功用，　證智未證智，
妙覺及等覺，　正受金剛心，
相應一念後，　果德涅槃者，
我等咸歸命，　三佛菩提尊。

無礙神通力，　冥加願攝受，
我等咸歸命，　三乘等賢聖。

學佛大悲心，　長時無退者，
請願遙加備，　念念見諸佛。

我等愚癡身，　曠劫來流轉，
今逢釋迦佛，　末法之遺跡，
彌陀本誓願，　極樂之要門，
定散等迴向，　速證無生身。

我依菩薩藏，　頓教一乘海，
說偈歸三寶，　與佛心相應。

十方恒沙佛，　六通照知我，
今乘二尊教，　廣開淨土門。

願以此功德，　平等施一切，
同發菩提心，　往生安樂國。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, Urging the Assembly to Give Rise to the Vow and Take Refuge in the Three Jewels

Monastics and laypeople gathered here, may each of you give rise to the unsurpassed mind. Birth and death are exceedingly hard to grow weary of; the Buddha-dharma, too, is hard to come to delight in. Together let us give rise to a resolve as hard as diamond, crossing over directly, cutting off the four streams; vowing to enter Amitābha's own realm, we take refuge, palms joined, and bow.

World-Honored One, with a single mind, I take refuge throughout the ten directions entire — in the sea of Dharma-nature and true suchness, in every buddha alike, whether reward-body or transformation-body.

Each and every bodhisattva's own body, with its retinue measureless in number, its adornments and its transformations — the whole sea of the ten grounds and the three worthy stages.

Whether their kalpas of time are full or not yet full, whether their wisdom and practice are complete or not yet complete, whether their fundamental afflictions are exhausted or not yet exhausted, whether their habitual tendencies are gone or not yet gone, whether their function is beyond function or not, whether their realized wisdom is yet realized or not — the wondrous enlightenment and the equal enlightenment, the diamond mind rightly received, and, after the single thought that accords with it, those who bear the fruit and virtue of nirvāṇa — to all of these we together take refuge: the honored ones of the bodhi of the three buddha-bodies.

Their unobstructed spiritual powers, secretly adding their strength, vowing to gather us in — to all of these we together take refuge: the worthies and sages of the three vehicles alike.

Those who study the Buddha's own mind of great compassion, never once retrogressing across a long span of time — we beg that they extend their aid to us from afar, that in thought after thought we may behold the buddhas.

We ourselves, foolish and deluded in body, have drifted and turned through kalpas without measure; now we have encountered Śākyamuni Buddha's own legacy, left behind in this age of the Dharma's decline — Amitābha's own original sworn vow, the essential gate to Ultimate Bliss. Dedicating both contemplative and dispersed practice alike, may we swiftly realize the unborn body.

Relying on the bodhisattva canon, the sudden teaching's sea of the single vehicle, I speak this verse, taking refuge in the Three Jewels, according with the Buddha's own mind.

May the buddhas of the ten directions, numerous as the sands of the Ganges, with their six penetrations, illuminate and know me — now, riding upon the teaching of these two honored ones, I open wide the gate of the Pure Land.

May this merit be given out equally to all, that together we may give rise to the mind of bodhi, and be reborn in the Land of Peace and Bliss.

A note on this opening verse

Shandao opens with an act of communal address rather than private meditation — "monastics and laypeople gathered here" — signaling from the very first line that this commentary is written for an assembly, not a solitary scholar's desk. The verse's own structure moves outward before it moves inward: refuge is taken first in the Dharma-nature itself, then in every buddha, then in bodhisattvas at every stage from the first ground to equal enlightenment, then in the worthies and sages of all three vehicles — a graduated sweep through the entire field of realized beings before the verse ever turns to its own petition. Only in its final third does it name what all of this refuge is actually for: "Amitābha's own original sworn vow, the essential gate to Ultimate Bliss," dedicating "both contemplative and dispersed practice alike" — the very two categories, 定 and 散, around which Shandao structures the rest of this entire commentary, already named here before a single line of the sūtra itself has been touched.

Gate One: Marking Out the Preface and Title

Chinese Source

此觀經一部之內，先作七門料簡，然後依文釋義。第一、先標序題，第二、次釋其名，第三、依文釋義並辨宗旨不同教之大小，第四、正顯說人差別，第五、料簡定散二善通別有異，第六、和會經論相違，廣施問答釋去疑情，第七、料簡韋提聞佛正說得益分齊。

第一、先標序題者。竊以真如廣大，五乘不測其邊，法性深高，十聖莫窮其際。真如之體量量性不出蠢蠢之心，法性無邊邊體則元來不動。無塵法界凡聖齊圓，兩垢如如則普該於含識。恒沙功德寂用湛然，但以垢障覆深，淨體無由顯照，故使大悲隱於西化，驚入火宅之門，灑甘露潤於群萌，輝智炬則朗重昏於永夜。三檀等備，四攝齊收，開示長劫之苦因，悟入永生之樂果。不謂群迷性隔樂欲不同，雖無一實之機，等有五乘之用，致使布慈雲於三界，注法雨於大悲。莫不等洽塵勞，普沾未聞之益，菩提種子藉此以抽心，正覺之芽念念因茲增長。依心起於勝行，門餘八萬四千，漸頓則各稱所宜，隨緣者則皆蒙解脫。然眾生障重，取悟之者難明，雖可教益多門，凡惑無由遍攬，遇因韋提致請，我今樂欲往生安樂，唯願如來教我思惟、教我正受。然娑婆化主因其請故，即廣開淨土之要門，安樂能人顯彰別意之弘願。其要門者，即此《觀經》定散二門是也。定即息慮以凝心，散即廢惡以修善，迴斯二行求願往生也。言弘願者，如《大經》說，一切善惡凡夫得生者，莫不皆乘阿彌陀佛大願業力為增上緣也。又佛密意弘深，教門難曉，三賢十聖，弗測所闚，況我信外輕毛敢知旨趣。仰惟釋迦此方發遣，彌陀即彼國來迎，彼喚此遣，豈容不去也。唯可勤心奉法，畢命為期，捨此穢身，即證彼法性之常樂。此即略標序題竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this one work, the Contemplation Sūtra, I will first make a sevenfold analysis, and only afterward follow the text to explain its meaning. First, marking out the preface and title; second, explaining its name; third, following the text to explain its meaning, and distinguishing its own governing purpose from the greater and lesser vehicles of other teachings; fourth, making plain the distinctions among those who speak such teachings; fifth, analyzing the difference between the contemplative and the dispersed forms of goodness, both general and specific; sixth, reconciling apparent contradictions among sūtras and treatises, setting out questions and answers at length to dispel doubt; and seventh, analyzing exactly what benefit Vaidehī received upon hearing the Buddha's own true teaching.

First, marking out the preface and title. I humbly reflect: true suchness is vast and expansive, and even the five vehicles cannot fathom its outer edge; the Dharma-nature is deep and lofty, and even the ten kinds of sages cannot exhaust its furthest limit. The very substance and measure of true suchness never departs from the sentient, stirring mind; the boundless substance of the Dharma-nature has, from the very beginning, never once moved. Within this dustless Dharma-realm, ordinary beings and sages alike are equally complete; the two kinds of defilement, seen as they truly are, pervade every sentient being without exception. Merit as numerous as the sands of the Ganges lies still and calm in its own function — but because the covering of defilement runs so deep, this pure substance has no way to shine forth and manifest. And so great compassion hides itself within a transformation toward the west, startling its way in through the gate of a house on fire, sprinkling the sweet dew of nectar to moisten every seedling, its torch of wisdom brightening the heavy darkness of an endless night. The three kinds of giving are equally provided; the four means of gathering beings in are equally taken up — opening and revealing the causes of suffering across long kalpas, awakening beings into the fruit of eternal life's own bliss. Yet it is said that deluded beings, differing in nature, are cut off from one another in what they take joy in and desire; though none of them carries a single, uniform capacity, all alike possess the function proper to one of the five vehicles — and so compassion's cloud is spread across the three realms, and the rain of the Dharma is poured out from great compassion. None fails to be equally soaked by it, however caught up in the dust of worldly toil; all alike are drenched in a benefit never before heard of. The seed of bodhi, relying on this, sends up its first shoot from the mind; the sprout of true awakening, thought after thought, grows because of it.

Relying on the mind, excellent practices arise — gates beyond counting, eighty-four thousand in number; gradual and sudden, each suited to its own occasion; and whoever follows the right condition is, without exception, brought to liberation. Yet sentient beings' own obstructions run so heavy that those who actually attain awakening are hard to find — for although the teaching that benefits them opens through many gates, an ordinary being's own confusion has no way to take hold of all of them at once. It was on this account that, through the occasion of Vaidehī's own request — "I now wish and desire to be reborn in the land of Peace and Bliss; I beg only that the Tathāgata teach me how to contemplate it, teach me to attain right reception of it" — that Śākyamuni, the teaching-lord of this Sahā world, on account of her very request, opened wide the essential gate to the Pure Land, and the one able to bring beings to that land of Peace and Bliss made manifest his own great vow of a different, further intent. This "essential gate" is exactly these two gates of the Contemplation Sūtra, the contemplative and the dispersed. The contemplative is stilling every distracting thought so as to fix the mind; the dispersed is setting aside evil so as to cultivate good — turning both of these practices toward the vow to seek rebirth there. As for what is called the "great vow," this is as the Larger Sūtra says: of all the good and evil ordinary beings who attain rebirth there, not one fails to ride upon the power of Amitābha Buddha's own great vow and karmic force as the predominant supporting condition.

And further, the Buddha's own hidden intent runs vast and deep, and the gate of his teaching is hard to fathom — even the three worthy stages and the ten kinds of sages cannot measure or peer into it fully, how much less myself, a mere feather of faith cast outside it, daring to claim I know its true purport. I can only look up in reverence: Śākyamuni, in this place, sends beings forth; Amitābha, from that land, comes to receive them. The one there calls, the one here sends off — how, then, could one refuse to go? One can only, with a diligent mind, uphold this teaching, taking the whole span of one's own life as the appointed term, and, casting off this defiled body, thereby realize that land's own constant bliss of Dharma-nature itself. This concludes the brief marking-out of the preface and title.

A note on this gate

Shandao's own image for how the Buddha's teaching reaches beings — "startling its way in through the gate of a house on fire" — draws directly on the burning-house parable of the Lotus Sūtra: children absorbed in play inside a burning house, unable to grasp that they are in danger, drawn out only by the promise of toys waiting outside. Applied here, the image casts ordinary rebirth in the three realms as exactly this fire, and the Pure Land teaching as exactly this kind of urgent, indirect summons — not a leisurely doctrinal option but a rescue pitched to whatever will actually move a distracted mind. Two terms introduced here recur throughout the rest of this fascicle and are worth fixing early: the "essential gate" (要門), which names the sūtra's own twofold practice of the contemplative and the dispersed, open to any practitioner's own effort; and the "great vow" (弘願), Amitābha's own forty-eight vows, which Shandao already states here — before any of his seven gates take up the question directly — is what "good and evil ordinary beings" alike actually ride upon to reach that land. The distinction between a gate a practitioner opens through his own effort and a vow that carries him regardless of that effort is, in miniature, the same self-power/other-power distinction already central to this site's Rebirth Treatise pages — introduced here in Shandao's very first analytical move, before the commentary proper has even begun.

Gate Two: Explaining the Name

Chinese Source

第二、次釋名者。經言《佛說無量壽觀經》一卷。言佛者，乃是西國正音。此土名覺，自覺覺他覺行窮滿，名之為佛。言自覺者，簡異凡夫，此由聲聞狹劣唯能自利、闕無利他大悲故。言覺他者，簡異二乘，此由菩薩有智故能自利，有悲故能利他，常能悲智雙行、不著有無也。言覺行窮滿者，簡異菩薩，此由如來智行已窮、時劫已滿，出過三位，故名為佛。言說者，口音陳唱故名為說。又如來對機說法多種不同，漸頓隨宜，隱彰有異，或六根通說，相好亦然，應念隨緣，皆蒙證益也。言無量壽者，乃是此地漢音。言南無阿彌陀佛者，又是西國正音。又南者是歸，無者是命，阿者是無，彌者是量，陀者是壽，佛者是覺，故言歸命無量壽覺。此乃梵漢相對，其義如此。今言無量壽者是法，覺者是人，人法並彰，故名阿彌陀佛。

又言人法者是所觀之境。即有其二：一者依報，二者正報。就依報中即有其三：一者地下莊嚴，即一切寶幢光明互相映發等是。二者地上莊嚴，即一切寶地池林寶樓宮閣等是。三者虛空莊嚴，即一切變化寶宮華網寶雲、化鳥風光動發聲樂等是。如前雖有三種差別，皆是彌陀淨國無漏真實之勝相。此即總結成依報莊嚴也。又言依報者，從日觀下至華座觀已來總明依報。就此依報中即有通有別。言別者，華座一觀是其別依，唯屬彌陀佛也。餘上六觀是其通依，即屬法界之凡聖，但使得生者共同受用故言通也。又就此六中即有真有假。言假者，即日想、水想、氷想等是其假依，由是此界中相似可見境相故。言真依者，即從瑠璃地下至寶樓觀已來是其真依。由是彼國真實無漏可見境相故。

二就正報中亦有其二：一者主莊嚴，即阿彌陀佛是。二者聖眾莊嚴，即現在彼眾及十方法界同生者是。又就此正報中亦有通有別。

言別者，即阿彌陀佛是也，即此別中亦有真有假。言假正報者，即第八像觀是也。觀音勢至等亦如是，此由眾生障重染惑處深，佛恐乍想真容無由顯現，故使假立真像以住心想，同彼佛以證境故言假正報也。言真正報者，即第九真身觀是也。此由前假正，漸以息於亂想、心眼得開，粗見彼方清淨二報種種莊嚴以除昏惑，由除障故得見彼真實之境相也。言通正報者，即觀音聖眾等已下是也。向來所言通別真假者，正明依正二報也。

言觀者照也，常以淨信心手以持智慧之輝，照彼彌陀正依等事。言經者經也，經能持緯，得成匹丈有其丈用，經能持法理事相應，定散隨機義不零落，能令修趣之者必藉教行之緣因，乘願往生證彼無為之法樂。既生彼國更無所畏，長時起行果極菩提，法身常住比若虛空，能招此益故曰為經。言一卷者，此《觀經》一部雖言兩會正說，總成斯一，故名一卷。故言《佛說無量壽觀經》一卷。此即釋其名義竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, explaining the name. The sūtra is called, in full, The Buddha Speaks the Sūtra on the Contemplation of Immeasurable Life, in one fascicle. As for the word "Buddha," this is the correct Sanskrit sound of the Western Land. In this land it is rendered "the awakened one" — one whose awakening of himself, awakening of others, and awakening in both realization and practice are all brought to their fullest completion is called a buddha. "Awakening of himself" distinguishes him from an ordinary being, since a śrāvaka, narrow and limited, can only benefit himself, lacking the great compassion that benefits others. "Awakening of others" distinguishes him from the two lesser vehicles, since a bodhisattva, possessing wisdom, is able to benefit himself, and, possessing compassion, is able to benefit others as well, constantly carrying out both compassion and wisdom together, attached to neither existence nor non-existence. "Awakening in both realization and practice, fully complete" distinguishes him from a bodhisattva, since the Tathāgata's own wisdom and practice have already been brought to their limit, his kalpas of time already fulfilled, surpassing all three of the preceding ranks — hence he alone is called a buddha.

"Speaks" means proclaiming with the sound of his own voice, hence called "speaking." And the Tathāgata, addressing different capacities, speaks the Dharma in many different ways — gradual or sudden, each suited to its occasion, sometimes hidden, sometimes revealed, each differently — sometimes speaking through all six sense-faculties at once, and his excellent marks do the same, responding to thought and according with condition, so that all alike receive the benefit of realization.

"Immeasurable Life" is the Chinese-language rendering used in this land. "Namo Amitābha Buddha" is, again, the correct sound of the Western Land. As for its parts: "na" means to take refuge, "mo" means life itself; "a" means without, "mi" means measure, "ta" means life; "buddha" means awakened — hence it is said, "I take refuge in the immeasurable life, the awakened one." This is how Sanskrit and Chinese correspond, and its meaning is exactly this. Here, "immeasurable life" is the Dharma, and "the awakened one" is the person — person and Dharma both made manifest together, hence it is called Amitābha Buddha.

And as for what is meant by "person" and "Dharma," these are the objects to be contemplated. There are two: the retributive support, and the direct retribution itself. Within the retributive support, there are three: first, the adornment beneath the ground — every jeweled banner and its light, each reflecting off the others, and the like; second, the adornment above the ground — every jeweled ground, pool, grove, jeweled tower, and palace hall, and the like; and third, the adornment of empty space — every transformed jeweled palace, flower-net, jeweled cloud, transformed bird, and the sound of music stirred by wind and light, and the like. Though these three differ as described above, all of them are the undefiled, true and excellent marks of Amitābha's own pure land. This, in sum, concludes the adornment of the retributive support. And as for what is meant by "retributive support": from the sun-contemplation down through the lotus-throne contemplation, all of this together makes plain the retributive support. Within this retributive support there is both the general and the specific. The specific is the lotus-throne contemplation alone, which belongs specifically to Amitābha Buddha himself. The remaining six contemplations above it are the general support, belonging to the ordinary beings and sages of the entire Dharma-realm — since whoever attains rebirth there shares equally in their use, they are called general. And within these six, there is further both the true and the provisional. The provisional are the contemplations of the sun, the water, and the ice, and the like — provisional supports, since they resemble visible objects found within this world of ours. The true supports are everything from the beryl-ground contemplation through the jeweled-tower contemplation — since these are the true, undefiled, visible objects actually found in that land.

Second, within direct retribution there are likewise two: the adornment of the lord, which is Amitābha Buddha himself; and the adornment of the assembly of sages, which is the assembly presently in that land, together with all who are born there alike from throughout the Dharma-realm. And within this direct retribution, there is likewise the general and the specific.

The specific is Amitābha Buddha himself — and within this specific category, there is likewise the true and the provisional. The provisional direct retribution is the eighth contemplation, the contemplation of the image. Avalokiteśvara, Mahāsthāmaprāpta, and the rest are the same. This is because sentient beings' own obstructions run so heavy, and their defiled confusion sits so deep, that the Buddha, fearing that suddenly contemplating his true form would give beings no way to make it appear at all, provisionally established a true image on which to settle their contemplative thought, that they might, through it, come to realize that same objective state as the Buddha himself — hence it is called the provisional direct retribution. The true direct retribution is the ninth contemplation, the contemplation of the true body. This is because, through the prior provisional direct retribution, one's scattered thoughts are gradually stilled, the mind's own eye opens, and one comes roughly to see that land's own pure, twofold retribution and its every kind of adornment, thereby dispelling confusion and darkness — and it is by dispelling this obstruction that one comes to see that land's own true and real objective state. The general direct retribution is Avalokiteśvara and the assembly of sages, from that point on. Everything said above, concerning the general and the specific, the true and the provisional, makes plain exactly these two kinds of retribution, the supportive and the direct.

"Contemplation" means illumination — constantly taking up, with the hand of a pure and faithful mind, the very radiance of wisdom, to illuminate Amitābha's own direct retribution and its support, and every such matter. "Sūtra" means what is constant — as a warp thread holds together the weft, so that a bolt of cloth may be completed and put to its own use, so a sūtra holds together the Dharma, principle and phenomena according with each other, contemplative and dispersed practice each fitting its own capacity without ever falling short — enabling one who takes up its practice to rely necessarily on the causal condition of its teaching and practice, riding upon the vow to be reborn there, and to realize that land's own unconditioned bliss of the Dharma. Once born in that land, one has nothing further to fear; across a long span of time one takes up practice, its fruit reaching the very summit of bodhi, one's own Dharma-body abiding forever, comparable to empty space itself — and because it is able to bring about this very benefit, it is called a sūtra. As for "one fascicle": although this one work, the Contemplation Sūtra, is said to have been spoken in two separate assemblies, it is gathered together as a single whole — hence it is called one fascicle. Hence it is said: The Buddha Speaks the Sūtra on the Contemplation of Immeasurable Life, in one fascicle. This concludes the explanation of its name and meaning.

A note on this gate

The distinction Shandao draws here between the "retributive support" (依報) — the land itself, its jeweled ground and towers and floating palaces — and "direct retribution" (正報) — the beings who actually inhabit it, Amitābha foremost among them — is the same distinction Tanluan draws, more than a century earlier, as "the purity of the receptacle-world" and "the purity of the sentient-being world" in his own commentary on the Rebirth Treatise. Shandao's own further subdivision — general versus specific, true versus provisional — maps this same distinction onto the sūtra's own sequence of sixteen contemplations directly: the first thirteen work through the land's own scenery before ever reaching Amitābha himself, and even then, the sūtra offers a provisional image (contemplation eight) before the true body (contemplation nine), on the explicit grounds that most practitioners' own minds cannot yet bear the true object without first rehearsing on a stand-in. The whole apparatus is worth holding onto going forward: nearly every argument in the fascicles that follow turns on exactly where, along this chain from provisional image to true body, a given practitioner or a given grade of rebirth actually stands.
