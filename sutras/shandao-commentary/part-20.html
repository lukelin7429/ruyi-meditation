Every Pure Land at Once

Rather than simply describing a single destination, the Buddha shows Vaidehī everything — every buddha-land in the ten directions, arrayed before her at once, each with its own distinct splendor. Her choice, made freely from this full field of options, is what Shandao reads as the deepest reason this particular sūtra exists at all.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 2 of 4: 序分義 (Meaning of the Preliminary Section)

Light From Between the Brows, and a Question of Hidden Intent

Chinese Source

五、從爾時世尊放眉間光下，至令韋提見已來，正明世尊廣現淨土酬前通請。此明世尊以見夫人廣求淨土，如來即放眉間光照十方國，以光攝國，還來頂上化作金臺，如須彌山。如之言似，似須彌山，此山腰細上闊，所有佛國並於中現。種種不同，莊嚴有異。佛神力故，了了分明，加備韋提，盡皆得見。

問曰：「韋提上請為我廣說無憂之處，佛今何故不為廣說？乃為金臺普現者有何意也？」

答曰：「此彰如來意密也。然韋提發言致請，即是廣開淨土之門，若為之總說，恐彼不見心猶致惑。是以一一顯現，對彼眼前，信彼所須，隨心自選。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, from "at that time, the World-Honored One emitted light from between his brows" down through "and let Vaidehī see them," this makes plain that the World-Honored One broadly revealed the pure lands, answering her earlier general request. This makes plain that the World-Honored One, seeing the queen's broad request for a pure land, thereupon emitted light from between his own brows, illuminating the lands of the ten directions; gathering these lands within that light, he drew it back to the crown of his own head, where it transformed into a platform of gold, resembling Mount Sumeru. As for "resembling": this means it was like Mount Sumeru — this mountain being narrow at its waist and broad above — and every buddha-land appeared arrayed within it, each differing in its own kind, each varying in its own splendor and adornment. Through the Buddha's own supernatural power, all of this appeared with perfect clarity, and, further assisted by his own power, Vaidehī was able to behold every one of them in full.

Question: "Vaidehī had earlier requested that the Buddha explain to her, in full, a place free of grief. Why, then, did the Buddha not explain it to her in full this way, but instead cause a platform of gold to broadly appear before her? What is the meaning of this?"

Answer: "This reveals the hidden intent of the Tathāgata. For when Vaidehī voiced her own request, this was itself a broad opening of the gate to the pure lands entire — had he simply given her a general explanation in words, it was feared she might not actually see for herself, and her mind might still remain in doubt. For this very reason, he caused each land to appear one by one, directly before her own eyes, trusting her to choose for herself, according to her own need and her own heart's inclination."

A note on this method

The Q&A Shandao inserts here makes an argument about pedagogy as much as about the narrative: a verbal description of "a place free of grief," however accurate, risks leaving the listener's own doubt unresolved, since she has not seen it for herself. Direct, sensory presentation — every land shown at once, in full — closes that gap in a way words alone cannot. What makes this significant for everything that follows is the phrase "trusting her to choose for herself, according to her own need and her own heart's inclination": the Buddha does not select Vaidehī's destination for her. He removes only the obstacle of not knowing what her actual choices are.

A Choice Made Freely, and Amitābha's Own Vows

Chinese Source

六、從時韋提白佛下，至皆有光明已來，正明夫人總領所現，感荷佛恩。此明夫人總見十方佛國，並悉精華。欲比極樂莊嚴，全非比況。故云我今樂生安樂國也。

問曰：「十方諸佛斷惑無殊，行畢果圓亦應無二。何以一種淨土，即有斯優劣也？」

答曰：「佛是法王，神通自在，優之與劣非凡惑所知。隱顯隨機，望存化益，或可故隱彼為優，獨顯西方為勝。」

七、從我今樂生彌陀已下，正明夫人別選所求。此明彌陀本國四十八願，願願皆發增上勝因。依因起於勝行，依行感於勝果，依果感成勝報，依報感成極樂，依樂顯通悲化，依於悲化，顯開智慧之門。然悲心無盡，智亦無窮。悲智雙行，即廣開甘露。因茲法潤，普攝群生也。諸餘經典勸處彌多，眾聖齊心皆同指讚，有此因緣，致使如來密遣夫人別選也。

八、從唯願世尊已下，正明夫人請求別行。此明韋提既選得生處，還修別行勵己注心，必望往益。言教我思惟者，即是定前方便，思想憶念彼國依正二報、四種莊嚴也。言教我正受者，此明因前思想漸漸微細覺想俱亡，唯有定心與前境合，名為正受。此中略已料簡，至下觀門更當廣辯。應知。

上來雖有八句不同，廣明欣淨緣竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth, from "at that time, Vaidehī addressed the Buddha" down through "each possessed of its own radiant light," this makes plain that the queen, taking in everything shown to her, felt deep gratitude for the Buddha's own grace. This makes plain that the queen, having beheld altogether the buddha-lands of the ten directions, saw that each was itself a place of the very finest splendor — and yet, when she sought to compare their adornments to those of the Land of Bliss, no comparison could be made at all. Hence she said: "I now wish to be reborn in the Land of Peace and Bliss."

Question: "The buddhas of the ten directions, having severed their own delusions without exception, and their own practice being complete and their own fruit perfected, ought equally to be without any difference between them. Why, then, should this one pure land alone stand superior to all the rest?"

Answer: "The Buddha is himself the king of the Dharma, his own supernatural powers entirely free and unobstructed — what is superior and what is lesser cannot be known by the deluded minds of ordinary beings. He conceals or reveals a land according to the capacity of each being, always intent on their own benefit and transformation — and so it may be that he deliberately concealed the others, revealing the Western Land alone as supreme."

Seventh, from "I now wish to be reborn in Amitābha's land" onward, this makes plain the queen's own particular choice, stating specifically what she sought. This makes plain that Amitābha's own original land arose from forty-eight vows, each and every vow giving rise to some surpassing, superior cause. From this cause arose surpassing practice; from this practice was felt a surpassing fruit; from this fruit was felt a surpassing retribution; from this retribution arose the Land of Ultimate Bliss itself; from this bliss was revealed the pervasive working of compassion; and from this compassionate working, the gate of wisdom itself was opened wide. Yet this compassionate heart is inexhaustible, and this wisdom likewise is boundless — compassion and wisdom proceeding together, the gate of sweet dew is thereby opened wide, and, moistened by this very Dharma, all beings without exception are gathered in. Among all the other sūtras, exhortations toward this land are exceedingly numerous, and the sages of every tradition, with one accord, alike point to it and praise it — and it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata, through hidden intent, caused the queen to make her own particular choice.

Eighth, from "I pray only, World-Honored One" onward, this makes plain the queen's own request for the particular practice that would take her there. This makes plain that the queen, having already chosen the place of her own rebirth, now wished further to cultivate the particular practice that would fix her own mind resolutely upon it, hoping without fail to gain the benefit of going there. As for "teach me to contemplate": this refers to the preliminary expedient means that precede meditative absorption itself — thinking upon and calling to mind that land's own dependent and direct retribution, and its four kinds of adornment. As for "teach me to attain right reception": this makes plain that, through this preceding contemplation, one's own thoughts gradually grow ever more subtle until both thought and awareness themselves fall away entirely, leaving only a settled mind united with the object before it — and this alone is called "right reception." This point has here been only briefly outlined; it will be explained further, and in full, below, within the gate of contemplation itself. This should be understood.

Though the above has unfolded across eight distinct points, this concludes the full explanation of the condition of joyful aspiration for purity.

A note on this choice

Shandao's own answer to why one pure land should be presented as superior to all others equally perfect buddhas — "he conceals or reveals a land according to the capacity of each being" — refuses to ground the claim in any objective hierarchy among buddhas themselves. The superiority belongs to the fit between this particular land and this particular listener, not to Amitābha's own attainment measured against others. What follows is the causal chain Shandao traces from the forty-eight vows down to "the gate of sweet dew opened wide" — vow, to practice, to fruit, to retribution, to bliss, to compassion, to wisdom — which functions as a compressed answer to a question the entire commentary has been building toward since Fascicle 1: not merely that Amitābha's land is worth choosing, but why a land built entirely from vows, rather than from karma alone, is structurally suited to receive beings as compromised as Vaidehī has just shown herself to be. Her own final request — for a method, not just a destination — is what the sūtra's sixteen contemplations, still to come, will spend their entire remaining length answering in full.

Part 20 Complete
