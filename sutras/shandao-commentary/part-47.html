The Lotus Throne Contemplation Begins

Śākyamuni's own thought toward the West and Amitābha's own reflection cast toward the East are described here as a single act of two teachers, not two separate events — and then Shandao asks the question a careful reader would already be forming: why does Amitābha appear standing, rather than seated with the dignity his own status would seem to demand?

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 3 of 4: 定善義 (Meaning of the Contemplative Good)

Permission to Speak, and an Exhortation to Spread This Teaching

Chinese Source

七、就華座觀中，亦先舉，次辨，後結。即有其十九。一、從佛告阿難下，至除苦惱法已來，正明勅聽許說。即有其三。一明告命二人。二明勅聽，令之諦受正念修行。三明佛為說華座觀法，但能住心緣念，罪苦得除。

二、從汝等憶持下，至解說已來，正明勸發流通。此明觀法深要，急救常沒眾生妄愛迷心漂流六道。汝持此觀處處勸修，普得知聞同昇解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, within the lotus throne contemplation, the text likewise first raises the matter, next distinguishes it in detail, and finally concludes it. There are nineteen points in all.

First, from "the Buddha told Ānanda" down through "the method that removes suffering and affliction," this makes plain the command to listen, and the granting of permission to speak. There are three points within it. First, this makes plain the issuing of this command to both these two people. Second, this makes plain the command to listen, causing them to receive it attentively and to practice with right mindfulness. Third, this makes plain that the Buddha, in expounding this method of the lotus throne contemplation, teaches that one need only settle the mind and hold this object in thought, and offense and suffering shall thereby be removed.

Second, from "you should bear this in mind and uphold it" down through "expound and explain it," this makes plain the exhortation to promulgate and transmit this teaching. This makes plain that this method of contemplation is profound and essential, urgently rescuing sentient beings forever sunk in delusion, their minds confused by false attachment, adrift throughout the six destinies. You should uphold this contemplation and exhort its cultivation everywhere, so that all who come to know and hear of it might rise together into liberation.

Two Buddhas Answering as One, and Why He Stands

Chinese Source

三、從說是語時下，至不得為比已來，正明娑婆化主為物故，住想西方；安樂慈尊知情故，則影臨東域。斯乃二尊許應無異，直以隱顯有殊。正由器朴之類萬差，致使互為郢匠。言說是語時者，正明就此意中即有其七。一明告勸二人時也。二明彌陀應聲即現，證得往生也。三明彌陀在空而立者，但使迴心正念願生我國，立即得生也。

問曰：「佛德尊高不可輒然輕舉，既能不捨本願來應大悲者，何故不端坐而赴機也？」

答曰：「此明如來別有密意。但以娑婆苦界，雜惡同居八苦相燒，動成違返，詐親含笑，六賊常隨，三惡火阬臨臨欲入。若不舉足以救迷，業繫之牢何由得勉。為斯義故，立撮即行，不及端坐以赴機也。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, from "as he spoke these words" down through "cannot be compared," this makes plain that Śākyamuni, the teaching-lord of this Sahā world, on behalf of all beings, fixed his own thought upon the West — while Amitābha, the compassionate honored one of the Land of Peace and Bliss, knowing this very intent, cast his own reflection into this Eastern realm. This is thus the two honored ones responding as one, entirely without difference between them, differing only in whether they conceal or reveal themselves — precisely because the countless different capacities of unrefined beings require that these two work as craftsmen together, each completing what the other begins. As for "as he spoke these words": this makes plain, within this very meaning, seven distinct points. First, this makes plain the moment this command and exhortation was addressed to these two people. Second, this makes plain that Amitābha, responding to this very voice, appeared at once, thereby bearing witness that rebirth is truly attained. Third, this makes plain that Amitābha appeared standing in the sky — for as soon as any being turns their own mind, holds right mindfulness, and vows to be reborn in his own land, they attain that rebirth at once, standing there just as he does.

Question: "The Buddha's own virtue is so revered and lofty that he ought never to bestir himself lightly and without cause — and yet, if he can indeed answer this call of great compassion without abandoning his own original vow, why does he not respond seated with proper dignity, rather than standing?"

Answer: "This makes plain that the Tathāgata carries here some further, hidden intent. It is simply that this Sahā world is a realm of suffering, where every manner of evil dwells intermingled, the eight sufferings burning against one another, movement itself becoming betrayal, false intimacy concealed behind a smile, the six thieves forever in pursuit, the fiery pit of the three evil destinies looming ever closer, ready to be entered at any moment. Were he not to raise his own foot at once to rescue the deluded, how could anyone ever be spared the prison of their own karmic bondage? It is for this very reason that he rises and moves at once, rather than waiting to respond with the leisure of one seated in dignity."

A note on this urgency

The image Shandao supplies to answer his own question — someone standing at the very edge of a fiery pit "looming ever closer, ready to be entered at any moment" — reframes Amitābha's standing posture from a matter of etiquette into a matter of triage. A seated Buddha, however dignified, implies time to spare; a standing Buddha implies none. This detail matters because it recasts the entire relationship between the two Buddhas described here: not simply Śākyamuni teaching about a distant Amitābha, but the two of them acting as "craftsmen together, each completing what the other begins" — one holding the thought, the other responding to it, in what Shandao insists is genuinely a single, undivided act rather than two separate events occurring in sequence.

Attendants, and a Radiance Beyond Comparison

Chinese Source

四明觀音勢至以為侍者，表無餘眾也。五明三尊身心圓淨，光明踰盛也。六明佛身光明朗照十方，垢障凡夫何能具覩。七明佛身無漏，光亦同然，豈將有漏之天金比方之也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, this makes plain that Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta serve as his own attendants, signifying that no other assembly is present. Fifth, this makes plain that these three honored ones' own bodies and minds are perfectly pure, their radiance blazing ever more brilliant. Sixth, this makes plain that the Buddha's own bodily light shines brightly throughout the ten directions — how could a defiled, obstructed ordinary being ever behold it in full? Seventh, this makes plain that the Buddha's own body is entirely free of defilement, and his light likewise so — how, then, could it ever be compared to the gold of the heavens, itself still subject to defilement?

A note on this radiance

The final comparison here, rejecting even "the gold of the heavens" as an adequate measure of the Buddha's own light, extends a distinction already made in the sun contemplation's own third reason for beginning with the sun: even the most refined material this world can offer remains "subject to defilement," and thus categorically different in kind, not merely in degree, from anything undefiled. This point closes the opening framing of the lotus throne contemplation and turns next to Vaidehī's own direct encounter with what has just appeared before her.
