Doctrinal Classification, and the Two Gates of Practice

Three more of Shandao's seven gates: a brisk classification of the sūtra's own doctrine and its speaker, followed by his own reinterpretation of "contemplative" and "dispersed" goodness — the two categories the whole commentary is organized around — against a reading he attributes to "other masters" and rejects outright. This is the first moment in the commentary where Shandao names a disagreement and argues his own case explicitly, a pattern the next three parts will return to at much greater length.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 1 of 4: 玄義分 (Profound Meaning)

Gate Three: Doctrinal Classification

Chinese Source

三、辯釋宗旨不同教之大小者。如《維摩經》以不思議解脫為宗。如《大品經》以空慧為宗。此例非一，今此《觀經》即以觀佛三昧為宗，亦以念佛三昧為宗。一心迴願往生淨土為體。言教之大小者，問曰：「此經二藏之中何藏攝？二教之中何教收？」答曰：「今此《觀經》菩薩藏收，頓教攝。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, distinguishing this sūtra's own governing purpose from the greater and lesser vehicles of other teachings. As the Vimalakīrti Sūtra takes the inconceivable liberation as its governing purpose, and the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra takes the wisdom of emptiness as its governing purpose — there are many such examples, not just one — so this Contemplation Sūtra takes the samādhi of contemplating the Buddha as its governing purpose, and also takes the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha as its governing purpose, with the single-minded dedication of the vow to be reborn in the Pure Land as its own substance. As for the greater and lesser vehicles of its teaching: someone asks, "Among the two canonical divisions, which does this sūtra belong to? Among the two categories of teaching, which does it fall under?" The answer: "This Contemplation Sūtra belongs to the bodhisattva canon, and falls under the sudden teaching."

A note on this gate

Shandao names two governing purposes for this sūtra rather than one — "the samādhi of contemplating the Buddha" (觀佛三昧) and "the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha" (念佛三昧) — and this doubling is not incidental. The first names the visualization practice the sūtra's sixteen contemplations describe in exhaustive detail; the second names the vocal, name-holding practice that will, by the end of this four-fascicle commentary, come to occupy the position of primary emphasis in Shandao's own reading. Both are already present here, in his very first formal statement of what the sūtra is actually about, before either has been argued for over the other. The classification of the sūtra as "sudden teaching" (頓教) — a single, complete path rather than one stage in a longer graduated sequence — is a claim this commentary will return to defend directly in the gates that follow, particularly against readers who assign the sūtra's nine grades of rebirth to bodhisattvas already partway up a long gradual path.

Gate Four: Who Spoke This Sūtra

Chinese Source

四、辯說人差別者。凡諸經起說，不過五種：一者佛說，二者聖弟子說，三者天仙說，四者鬼神說，五者變化說。今此《觀經》是佛自說。問曰：「佛在何處說？為何人說？」答曰：「佛在王宮為韋提等說。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, distinguishing among those who speak such teachings. In general, every sūtra's own teaching arises from no more than five sources: first, spoken by the Buddha; second, spoken by a noble disciple; third, spoken by a heavenly sage; fourth, spoken by a spirit; and fifth, spoken by a transformation-body. This Contemplation Sūtra is spoken by the Buddha himself. Someone asks: "Where did the Buddha speak it? For whom did he speak it?" The answer: "The Buddha spoke it in the royal palace, for Vaidehī and the others there."

A note on this gate

This gate is procedural rather than argumentative — a standard exegetical checklist item, settled in a single exchange, before Shandao turns to the questions that will actually occupy the rest of this fascicle. Its brevity is itself worth noting: unlike the doctrinal classification just given, or the two gates of practice about to follow, nothing here is contested. The sūtra's own narrative frame — the Buddha speaking directly, in the royal palace at Rājagṛha, to Queen Vaidehī — is simply reported as fact, with no rival reading to argue against.

Gate Five: The Two Gates of Practice, Contemplative and Dispersed

Chinese Source

五、料簡定散兩門，即有其六：一明能請者即是韋提。二明所請者即是世尊。三明能說者即是如來。四明所說即是定散二善十六觀門。五明能為即是如來。六明所為即韋提等是也。

問曰：「定散二善，因誰致請？」答曰：「定善一門韋提致請，散善一門是佛自說。」

問曰：「未審定散二善出在何文？今既教備不虛，何機得受？」

答曰：「解有二義。一者謗法與無信，八難及非人，此等不受也。斯乃朽林頑石，不可有生潤之期。此等眾生，必無受化之義。除斯已外，一心信樂求願往生，上盡一形，下收十念，乘佛願力，莫不皆往。此即答上何機得受義竟。

二出在何文者，即有通有別。言通者即有三義不同。何者？一、從『韋提白佛唯願為我廣說無憂惱處』者，即是韋提標心自為通請所求。二、從『唯願佛日教我觀於清淨業處』者，即是韋提自為通請去行。三、從『世尊光臺現國』，即是酬前通請為我廣說之言。雖有三義不同，答前通竟。言別者則有二義：一、從『韋提白佛我今樂生極樂世界彌陀佛所』者，即是韋提自為別選所求。二從『唯願教我思惟教我正受』者，即是韋提自為請修別行。雖有二義不同，答上別竟。」

從此已下次答定散兩門之義。

問曰：「云何名定善？云何名散善？」答曰：「從日觀下至十三觀已來名為定善。三福九品名為散善。」

問曰：「定善之中有何差別？出在何文？」

答曰：「出何文者，經言『教我思惟教我正受』，即是其文。言差別者，即有二義：一謂思惟，二謂正受。言思惟者，即是觀前方便，思想彼國依正二報總別相也。即地觀文中說言『如此想者名為粗見極樂國土』，即合上『教我思惟』一句。言正受者，想心都息緣慮並亡，三昧相應名為正受。即地觀文中說言『若得三昧見彼國地了了分明』，即合上『教我正受』一句。定散雖有二義不同，總答上問竟。

「又向來解者與諸師不同。諸師將思惟一句用合三福九品以為散善；正受一句用通合十六觀以為定善。如斯解者將謂不然。何者？

如《華嚴經》說：『思惟正受者但是三昧之異名』，與此地觀文同。以斯文證，豈得通於散善。又向來韋提上請但言『教我觀於清淨業處』，次下又請言『教我思惟正受』，雖有二請，唯是定善。

又散善之文都無請處，但是佛自開。次下散善緣中說云『亦令未來世一切凡夫』已下，即是其文。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, analyzing the two gates of the contemplative and the dispersed — there are six matters here: first, making plain who requested the teaching, which is Vaidehī; second, who was requested, which is the World-Honored One; third, who actually spoke it, which is the Tathāgata; fourth, what was spoken, which is the two forms of goodness, contemplative and dispersed, in the sixteen contemplations; fifth, who carried this out, which is the Tathāgata; and sixth, for whose sake it was carried out, which is Vaidehī and the others.

Question: "On whose account were these two forms of goodness, contemplative and dispersed, requested?" Answer: "The gate of contemplative goodness was requested by Vaidehī; the gate of dispersed goodness was spoken by the Buddha entirely on his own."

Question: "It is not yet clear where, in the text, these two forms of goodness actually appear. And now that this teaching is fully provided and not made in vain, what kind of capacity is actually able to receive it?"

Answer: "This resolves into two points. First: those who slander the Dharma, those without faith, those in the eight difficult conditions, and non-human beings — these do not receive it. They are like rotten timber or stubborn stone, with no season in which they could ever be brought to life or moistened. For beings such as these, there is simply no possibility of being transformed by this teaching. Apart from these, anyone at all who, with a single mind, believes in and delights in seeking the vow to be reborn there — whether it be the very fullest exertion of an entire lifetime, or, at the very least, ten thoughts alone — riding upon the power of the Buddha's own vow, without exception, all of them go there. This concludes the answer to the question of what capacity is able to receive this teaching.

"As for the second point, where in the text this appears: there is both a general and a specific request. As for the general, there are three distinct points. What are they? First, from where Vaidehī says to the Buddha, 'I beg only that you fully explain for me a place free of sorrow and vexation' — this is Vaidehī marking out, in her own mind, her general request for what she seeks. Second, from where she says, 'I beg only that the Buddha, the sun, teach me to contemplate a place of pure karma' — this is Vaidehī's own general request for a practice to carry out. Third, from where 'the World-Honored One's own radiant platform displays the lands' — this is the response to her prior general request, fully explaining it for her sake. Though these are three distinct points, this answers the general request in full. As for the specific, there are two points: first, from where Vaidehī says to the Buddha, 'I now wish to be born in the world of Ultimate Bliss, at Amitābha Buddha's own place' — this is Vaidehī's own specific selection of what she seeks; and second, from where she says, 'I beg only that you teach me to contemplate, teach me to attain right reception of it' — this is Vaidehī's own specific request to cultivate a particular practice. Though these are two distinct points, this answers the specific request in full."

From this point on, the meaning of these two gates, the contemplative and the dispersed, is answered in turn.

Question: "What is called contemplative goodness? What is called dispersed goodness?" Answer: "From the sun-contemplation down through the thirteenth contemplation, this is called contemplative goodness. The three blessings and the nine grades are called dispersed goodness."

Question: "Within contemplative goodness, what distinctions are there? Where in the text do they appear?"

Answer: "As for where in the text: the sūtra says, 'teach me to contemplate, teach me to attain right reception of it' — this is exactly the text. As for the distinction: there are two points, called contemplation and right reception. 'Contemplation' is exactly the preliminary skillful means before actual contemplation, the thinking-through of that land's general and specific marks, both its retributive support and its direct retribution. This is exactly what the text on the earth-contemplation means when it says, 'one who contemplates in this way is said to see the land of Ultimate Bliss only roughly' — this corresponds to the phrase above, 'teach me to contemplate.' 'Right reception' is when every thinking thought and every entangled concern is entirely stilled, and one accords with samādhi — this is called right reception. This is exactly what the text on the earth-contemplation means when it says, 'once one attains samādhi, one sees that land's own ground perfectly clear and distinct' — this corresponds to the phrase above, 'teach me to attain right reception of it.' Though the contemplative and the dispersed involve these two distinct points, this concludes the full answer to the question above.

"And further, my own reading here differs from that of other masters. Other masters take the single phrase 'contemplation' and apply it to the three blessings and the nine grades, treating this as dispersed goodness; and take the phrase 'right reception' and apply it generally to all sixteen contemplations, treating this as contemplative goodness. I take this reading to be mistaken. Why is this so?

"As the Avataṃsaka Sūtra says, 'contemplation and right reception are simply alternate names for samādhi' — this matches exactly what the text on the earth-contemplation says. Taking this text as proof, how could this phrase possibly extend generally to dispersed goodness at all? And further, in her request above, Vaidehī says only, 'teach me to contemplate a place of pure karma,' and then, further on, requests again, 'teach me to contemplate, and attain right reception of it' — though these are two separate requests, both concern contemplative goodness alone.

"And further, the text concerning dispersed goodness has no place where it is requested at all — it is opened entirely by the Buddha himself, on his own initiative. This is exactly the text found further on, in the passage on the causes of dispersed goodness, beginning: 'and also causing every ordinary being of future ages...'"

A note on this gate

Two moves in this gate carry weight far beyond their length. First, Shandao settles, against "other masters" he does not name, exactly which sixteen-fold division of the sūtra counts as "contemplative goodness" and which counts as "dispersed goodness" — a question that sounds merely organizational but is not: his own reading assigns all sixteen contemplations (the sun, water, ice, ground, trees, ponds, and so on, through to the nine grades of rebirth themselves) differently than the reading he rejects, and this reassignment is what makes the argument of Gate Six — that every one of the nine grades describes an ordinary being, not a bodhisattva already advanced along the path — possible to make at all. Second, and more quietly, this gate already states the thesis the rest of the commentary exists to defend: "anyone at all who, with a single mind, believes in and delights in seeking the vow to be reborn there — whether it be the very fullest exertion of an entire lifetime, or, at the very least, ten thoughts alone — riding upon the power of the Buddha's own vow, without exception, all of them go there." The only beings excluded are those who slander the Dharma, lack faith entirely, or are simply incapable of receiving any teaching at all — not those whose practice is thin, whose faith is late, or whose life has been spent badly until its very last thought. Gate Six exists to make this claim survive contact with readers who find it implausible.
