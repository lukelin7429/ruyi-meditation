The Highest Grade, and the Three Minds

The nine grades finally begin, opening with the very highest: an ordinary being, not a sage, who happens to have studied the Mahāyāna deeply. Before that grade's own practice can be described, its correct cause must first be named — the three minds — and Shandao pauses to explain something structurally significant: only the Buddha himself could have posed the question of what they are.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 4 of 4: 散善義 (Meaning of the Dispersed Good)

The Highest of the Nine Grades

Chinese Source

次下先就上品上生位中，亦先舉，次辨，後結。即有其十二。一、從佛告阿難已下，則雙標二意。一明告命。二明辨定其位。此即修學大乘上善凡夫人也。三、從若有眾生下，至即便往生已來，正明總舉有生之類。即有其四。一明能信之人。二明求願往生。三明發心多少。四明得生之益。

Ru-Yi Translation

Turning now below, within the position of the highest rebirth of the upper rank, the text likewise first raises the matter, next distinguishes it in detail, and finally concludes it. There are twelve points in all.

First, from "the Buddha told Ānanda" onward, this marks out two meanings together. First, this makes plain the issuing of this command. Second, this makes plain the distinguishing and fixing of this grade's own position — this is itself the ordinary being of superior goodness who has studied and cultivated the Mahāyāna.

Third, from "should there be sentient beings" down through "they attain rebirth at once," this makes plain the general raising up of the class of beings capable of this rebirth. There are four points within it. First, this makes plain the person capable of faith. Second, this makes plain the seeking and vowing of rebirth. Third, this makes plain the varying degree to which the mind is given rise to. Fourth, this makes plain the benefit of attaining rebirth.

A note on this identity

Shandao's own gloss — "this is itself the ordinary being of superior goodness who has studied and cultivated the Mahāyāna" — is a claim worth pausing on given everything Fascicle 1 already established about this sūtra's own primary audience. Even the very highest of the nine grades, occupied by someone who has studied the Mahāyāna deeply, remains explicitly an "ordinary being" rather than a sage — consistent with Shandao's own historically decisive argument, made across the opening fascicle, that the nine grades describe flawed, ordinary practitioners throughout their entire range, top to bottom, rather than advanced beings already secure on the path.

A Question Only the Buddha Could Ask

Chinese Source

四、從何等為三下，至必生彼國已來，正明辨定三心以為正因。即有其二。一明世尊隨機顯益意密難知，非佛自問自徵無由得解。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, from "what are these three" down through "shall certainly be reborn in that land," this makes plain the distinguishing and fixing of the three minds as the correct cause. There are two points within it. First, this makes plain that the World-Honored One, revealing this benefit according to each being's own capacity, carries a hidden intent difficult to know — and, were the Buddha not to raise the question himself and answer it himself, there would be no way at all to understand it.

A note on this question

The observation that the Buddha "raise[s] the question himself and answer[s] it himself" — rather than the question arising from Vaidehī or Ānanda, as with most of the sūtra's own earlier exchanges — signals to Shandao that what follows is of unusual doctrinal weight, something whose own hidden intent no ordinary interlocutor could have known enough to ask about. This structural observation sets up the passage that follows in the next part: the three minds themselves, opening with the first and most foundational of the three, the sincere mind.
