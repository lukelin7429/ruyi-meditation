Why the Eight Precepts, and a King Who Would Not Die

Shandao explains why a dying king chose these particular precepts over any other teaching the Buddha had to offer, quotes the precept-text itself in full, and closes "the condition of the father's imprisonment" with Maudgalyāyana's own daily, supernatural visits and Pūrṇa's teaching — the combination that keeps King Bimbisāra alive, and even at peace, far longer than anyone expected.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 2 of 4: 序分義 (Meaning of the Preliminary Section)

Why Maudgalyāyana, and Why the Eight Precepts

Chinese Source

問曰：「父王遙敬，先禮世尊。及其受戒，即請目連。有何意也？」

答曰：「凡聖極尊無過於佛，傾心發願即先禮大師。戒是小緣，是以唯請目連來授。然王意者，貴存得戒，即是義周，何勞迂屈世尊也。」

問曰：「如來戒法，乃有無量。父王唯請八戒，不請餘也。」

答曰：「餘戒稍寬，時節長遠，恐畏中間失念，流轉生死。其八戒者，如餘佛經說，在家人持出家戒。此戒持心極細極急。何意然者？但時節稍促，唯限一日一夜，作法即捨。云何知此戒用心行細？如戒文中具顯。云：佛子，從今旦至明旦，一日一夜，如諸佛不殺生，能持不？答言：能持。第二又云：佛子，從今旦至明旦，一日一夜，如諸佛不偷盜、不行婬、不妄語、不飲酒、不得脂粉塗身、不得歌舞唱伎及往觀聽、不得上高廣大床，此上八是戒非齋。

不得過中食，此一是齋非戒。此等諸戒皆引諸佛為證。何以故？唯佛與佛正習俱盡。除佛已還，惡習等由在。是故不引為證也。是以得知此戒用心起行極是細急。又此戒，佛說有八種勝法。若人一日一夜具持不犯，所得功德超過人天二乘境界。如經廣說，有斯益故，致使父王日日受之。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: "The king's own reverence from afar was directed first, in homage, toward the World-Honored One himself — and yet, when it came to actually receiving the precepts, he requested Maudgalyāyana instead. What is the reason for this?"

Answer: "Among ordinary beings and sages alike, none is more honored than the Buddha himself — and so, pouring out his own heart and making his vow, the king paid homage first to the great teacher. Receiving the precepts, however, is a lesser matter, and so he requested only that Maudgalyāyana come to grant them. The king's own intention, in truth, was simply to obtain the precepts themselves — this alone would fulfill the meaning of the request in full. Why should he trouble himself to inconvenience the World-Honored One over so small a matter?"

Question: "The Tathāgata's own precepts are measureless in number. Why did the king request only the eight precepts, and none of the others?"

Answer: "Other precepts are somewhat more lenient in scope, and extend over a long span of time — and there was a real fear that, somewhere in the midst of that long span, mindfulness might lapse, and one might drift back into birth and death regardless. As for the eight precepts: as other sūtras of the Buddha's own teaching say, these are precepts by which a layperson holds to the discipline of a monastic. Keeping this precept demands a mind extremely fine and extremely urgent in its own attention. Why is this so? Because its own span of time is quite brief, limited to only a single day and night, after which the observance is simply set aside. How do we know that keeping this precept demands such fine, careful attention? This is made fully plain in the precept-text itself, which says: 'Child of the Buddha, from this morning until tomorrow morning, for one day and one night, will you, as every buddha does, refrain from killing any living being — can you keep this?' And one answers: 'I can keep it.' And a second time it says: 'Child of the Buddha, from this morning until tomorrow morning, for one day and one night, will you, as every buddha does, refrain from stealing, from sexual conduct, from false speech, from intoxicating drink, from applying rouge or powder to your own body, from singing, dancing, or performing, or from going to watch or listen to such performances, and from sitting or lying upon a high and broad, luxurious bed?' These eight above are the precepts proper, not the fast.

"And will you refrain from eating past noon?" — this one alone is the fast, not a precept. Every one of these precepts, moreover, calls upon the buddhas themselves as its own witness. Why is this so? Because only a buddha, and no one but a buddha, has entirely exhausted every habitual tendency of affliction at all — below the rank of buddha, such habitual tendencies still remain in some measure, and so they are not called upon as witness in this way. From this alone, one can know that keeping this precept, in the very way one holds the mind and carries out the practice, demands the utmost fineness and urgency. And further, the Buddha himself taught that this precept carries eight kinds of excellent benefit. Should a person keep it fully, without a single violation, for even a single day and night, the merit thereby obtained surpasses the very state of gods, humans, and the two lesser vehicles alike. As the sūtras explain this at length, and given such benefit as this, it was precisely for this reason that the king took up this precept anew, day after day."

A note on this precept

Shandao's own explanation turns on scale rather than severity: the eight precepts are not chosen because they are the strictest available to a layperson, but because their brevity — a single day and night — matches exactly what a man with no certainty of tomorrow could actually promise to keep. A longer-term vow risks a lapse "somewhere in the midst of that long span"; a vow renewed daily never has to survive longer than the king himself expects to live. That every precept calls specifically upon a buddha, and only a buddha, as its own witness — since only a buddha has exhausted every habitual tendency completely — reads, in context, as its own quiet acknowledgment of what the king himself cannot yet claim: not sinlessness, but a discipline precise enough to be renewed, honestly, one day at a time.

Maudgalyāyana's Daily Visits, and a King Who Would Not Die

Chinese Source

六、從時大目連下，至為王說法已來，明其父王因請得蒙聖法。此明目連得他心智，遙知父王請意，即發神通，如彈指頃到於王所。

又恐人不識神通之相，故引快鷹為喻。然目連通力，一念之頃遶四天下百千之匝，豈得與鷹為類也。如是比校乃有眾多，不可具引，如《賢愚經》具說。言日日如是授王八戒者。此明父王延命，致使目連數來受戒。

問曰：「八戒既言勝者，一受即足。何須日日受之？」答曰：「山不厭高，海不厭深，刀不厭利，日不厭明，人不厭善，罪不厭除，賢不厭德，佛不厭聖。然王意者，既被囚禁，更不蒙進止。念念之中，畏人喚殺。為此晝夜傾心，仰憑八戒，望欲積善增高，擬資來業。」

言世尊亦遣富樓那為王說法者。此明世尊慈悲意重，愍念王身忽遇囚勞，恐生憂悴。然富樓那者，於聖弟子中最能說法，善有方便開發人心。為此因緣，如來發遣為王說法，以除憂惱。

七、從如是時間下，至顏色和悅已來，正明父王因食聞法，多日不死。此正明夫人多時奉食，以除飢渴。二聖又以戒法內資，善開王意。食能延命，戒法養神，失苦亡憂，致使顏容和悅也。

上來雖有七句不同，廣明禁父緣竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth, from "at that time the great Maudgalyāyana" down through "expounded the Dharma for the king," this makes plain that the king, through his own request, came to receive the sacred teaching. This makes plain that Maudgalyāyana, having attained the wisdom to know the minds of others, perceived from afar the very intent of the king's own request, and at once exercised his own spiritual power, arriving at the king's own place as swiftly as the snap of a finger.

And fearing that people might not recognize the true nature of such spiritual power, the sūtra offers the image of a swift hawk as a comparison. Yet Maudgalyāyana's own spiritual power could circle the four continents hundreds and thousands of times within the span of a single thought — how, then, could this possibly be classed together with a mere hawk? Comparisons of this kind are numerous, and cannot all be cited here in full — they are set out at length in the Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish. As for "granting the king the eight precepts, day after day, in this way": this makes plain that the king's own life was thereby prolonged, and it was for this reason that Maudgalyāyana came repeatedly to grant him the precepts anew.

Question: "If the eight precepts are indeed said to be so excellent, then receiving them once should be sufficient — why should the king need to receive them anew, day after day?"

Answer: "A mountain is never too high; the sea is never too deep; a blade is never too sharp; the sun is never too bright; a person is never too good; an offense is never too thoroughly removed; a worthy is never too virtuous; a buddha is never too sagely. As for the king's own intention: having been imprisoned, he no longer received any word at all of what might come next — in thought after thought, he feared that someone might be sent to kill him. It was for this very reason that, day and night, he poured out his whole heart, relying wholly on these eight precepts, hoping that his accumulated goodness might grow ever higher, to provide for whatever karma might come to him next."

As for "the World-Honored One also sent Pūrṇa to expound the Dharma for the king": this makes plain how deeply weighted with compassion the World-Honored One's own intent was, grieving that the king's body had so suddenly met with the toil of imprisonment, and fearing that this might give rise to grief and further suffering. Now, Pūrṇa, among all the noble disciples, was the most skilled in expounding the Dharma, adept in skillful means for opening and awakening the human mind. It was for this reason that the Tathāgata sent him to expound the Dharma for the king, so as to remove his grief and vexation.

Seventh, from "in this way, for some time" down through "his countenance grew calm and pleased," this makes plain that the king, through this food and this teaching, did not die for many days. This makes plain that the queen, over a long span of time, presented him with food, thereby relieving his hunger and thirst; and that these two sages, moreover, nourished him inwardly through the precepts and the Dharma, skillfully opening his own mind. Food is able to prolong life; the precepts and the Dharma nourish the spirit — and it was through the loss of his own suffering and grief that his countenance came to grow calm and pleased.

Though the above has unfolded across seven distinct points, this concludes the full explanation of the condition of the father's imprisonment.

A note on this closing

Shandao's closing summary of the entire "father's imprisonment" section names, with unusual directness, two entirely separate mechanisms working together: "food is able to prolong life; the precepts and the Dharma nourish the spirit." Vaidehī's own physical ingenuity keeps the king's body alive; Maudgalyāyana's daily visits and Pūrṇa's teaching keep something else alive alongside it. Shandao never suggests one could substitute for the other — a starving man cannot be taught into survival, and a merely-fed prisoner, isolated and awaiting execution, would have no reason for his countenance to "grow calm and pleased" rather than simply grow desperate. It is the combination itself, food answering the body's own crisis and teaching answering the mind's, that Shandao credits with the king's own unexpected endurance — and this same double structure, one register of care for the body and another for the mind, is about to recur, in an even more extreme form, when his own life comes under a second and far more direct threat.
