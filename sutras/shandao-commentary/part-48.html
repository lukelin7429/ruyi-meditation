A Question for Beings Yet to Come

Vaidehī's own gratitude at beholding Amitābha directly gives way almost immediately to a harder question, asked on behalf of everyone who will never share this same privilege: once the Buddha is gone, what becomes of them? Shandao then explains a detail easy to read past — why an answer addressed to Vaidehī alone was never intended for her alone.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 3 of 4: 定善義 (Meaning of the Contemplative Good)

A Defiled Woman, Beholding the Buddha Directly

Chinese Source

四、從時韋提希見無量下，至作禮已來，正明韋提實是垢凡女質，不足可言。但以聖力冥加，彼佛現時，得蒙稽首。斯乃序臨淨國，喜歎無以自勝。今乃正覩彌陀，更益心開悟忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, from "at that time, Vaidehī, beholding the Immeasurable" down through "made prostration," this makes plain that the queen, in truth still a defiled, ordinary woman, would hardly be worth mentioning on her own account — yet, through the hidden power of the sages, at the very moment that Buddha appeared, she was able to bow her own head before him. This is thus the sequence in which she came before the pure land, her own joy and wonder beyond what she could contain. Now, beholding Amitābha directly, her own mind opened further still, gaining insight and acceptance.

A Question Asked on Behalf of Everyone Not Yet Born

Chinese Source

五、從白佛言下，至及二菩薩已來，正明夫人領荷佛恩，為物陳疑，生於後問。此明夫人意者，佛今現在，蒙尊加念，得覩彌陀。佛滅後，眾生云何可見也。

六、從未來眾生下，至及二菩薩已來，明其夫人為物置請使同己見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, from "she addressed the Buddha, saying" down through "and the two bodhisattvas as well," this makes plain that the queen, receiving the Buddha's own grace with gratitude, on behalf of all beings laid out her own doubt, giving rise to the question that follows. This makes plain the queen's own intent: now, while the Buddha remains present, receiving his own mindful attention, she has been able to behold Amitābha directly. But once the Buddha has entered final nirvāṇa, how then could sentient beings ever behold him?

Sixth, from "sentient beings of future ages" down through "and the two bodhisattvas as well," this makes plain that the queen, on behalf of all beings, made this very request, so that they too might behold him just as she herself had.

A note on this question

What Vaidehī asks here is the same question already anticipated back in Part 26, but now sharpened by direct experience: having just beheld Amitābha with her own eyes, she immediately turns her thought to those who will never have that privilege. Her request is explicitly not for herself alone — "on behalf of all beings" — a small phrase that reorients the entire remaining teaching of this sūtra away from a private revelation granted to one fortunate queen, and toward a method meant to survive the Buddha's own absence.

An Answer Addressed to One, Meant for All

Chinese Source

七、從佛告韋提下，至當起想念已來，正明總告許說之言。

問曰：「夫人置請，通已為生。及至如來酬答，但指韋提不通生也？」

答曰：「佛身臨化，說法以逗機，不請尚自普弘，何論別指而不等備。但以文略故無，兼為之心必有也。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, from "the Buddha told Vaidehī" down through "you should now give rise to this contemplative thought," this makes plain the words of his own general command and granting of permission to speak.

Question: "The queen's own request was made generally, on behalf of beings still to come. Yet, when the Tathāgata comes to answer it, he addresses Vaidehī alone, without extending it generally to those future beings — why is this?"

Answer: "The Buddha's own body, arriving to teach, expounds the Dharma according to each occasion — even without being asked, he already extends it broadly to all; how much less, then, should addressing one person specifically be taken to mean it is not equally provided to all others? This is merely because the text itself is written concisely, and so this is not stated explicitly — but the intent to include them all is certainly present nonetheless."

A note on this address

Shandao's answer here supplies a small but important interpretive principle for reading the rest of this sūtra: a name in the text marks the narrative occasion of a teaching, not the boundary of who receives its benefit. His reasoning moves from the general to the specific — if the Buddha teaches broadly "even without being asked," then addressing a single named person can hardly be read as narrowing that scope, and the concise wording of the text is explained as an economy of style rather than a restriction of meaning. This principle matters directly for every reader of this translation as well: the sixteen contemplations that follow, though narrated as spoken to Vaidehī specifically, are understood by Shandao as intended for exactly the "beings of future ages" she herself requested them for.
