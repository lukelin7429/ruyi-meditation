Three Affinities: Why the Light Embraces

A question sharp enough to unsettle the entire teaching so far: if every virtuous practice, properly dedicated, brings rebirth equally, why does the Buddha's own light single out those who are mindful of him specifically? Shandao's answer — three affinities, intimate, close, and empowering — became one of the most influential passages in the entire history of Pure Land Buddhism.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 3 of 4: 定善義 (Meaning of the Contemplative Good)

A Body's Own Particular Marks, and a Light That Embraces

Chinese Source

五、從無量壽佛下，至攝取不捨已來，正明觀身別相光益有緣。即有其五。一明相多少。二明好多少。三明光多少。四明光照遠近。五明光所及處偏蒙攝益。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, from "Amitāyus Buddha" down through "embraces and never abandons," this makes plain the contemplation of this body's own particular marks, and the benefit its light brings to those with karmic affinity. There are five points within it. First, this makes plain the number of major marks. Second, this makes plain the number of minor excellences. Third, this makes plain the number of rays of light. Fourth, this makes plain the reach of this light, both near and far. Fifth, this makes plain that wherever this light reaches, that place alone receives this embracing benefit.

Why the Light Embraces Those Mindful of the Buddha

Chinese Source

問曰：「備修眾行但能迴向皆得往生，何以佛光普照唯攝念佛者，有何意也？」

答曰：「此有三義。一明親緣。眾生起行，口常稱佛，佛即聞之；身常禮敬佛，佛即見之；心常念佛，佛即知之。眾生憶念佛者，佛亦憶念眾生，彼此三業不相捨離，故名親緣也。二明近緣。眾生願見佛，佛即應念現在目前，故名近緣也。三明增上緣。眾生稱念，即除多劫罪。命欲終時，佛與聖眾自來迎接。諸邪業繫無能礙者，故名增上緣也。自餘眾行，雖名是善，若比念佛者，全非比校也，是故諸經中處處廣讚念佛功能。如《無量壽經》四十八願中，唯明專念彌陀名號得生。又如《彌陀經》中，一日七日專念彌陀名號得生。又十方恒沙諸佛證，誠不虛也。又此經定散文中，唯標專念名號得生。此例非一也。廣顯念佛三昧竟。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: "Cultivating every manner of practice in full, and then simply dedicating its merit, is likewise able to bring about rebirth there — why, then, does the Buddha's own light, shining everywhere, embrace only those who are mindful of the Buddha specifically? What is the meaning of this?"

Answer: "This carries three meanings. The first is what may be called the intimate affinity. When sentient beings undertake this very practice, their mouth constantly calling upon the Buddha's own name, the Buddha hears this at once; their body constantly bowing in reverence to the Buddha, the Buddha sees this at once; their mind constantly holding the Buddha in thought, the Buddha knows this at once. When sentient beings hold the Buddha in mindful recollection, the Buddha likewise holds these very beings in mindful recollection in return — the karma of body, speech, and mind on both sides never once forsaking the other. Hence this is called the intimate affinity.

"The second is what may be called the close affinity. When sentient beings wish to behold the Buddha, the Buddha, responding to that very thought, appears at once directly before their own eyes. Hence this is called the close affinity.

"The third is what may be called the empowering affinity. When sentient beings call upon the Buddha's own name, the offenses of many kalpas are thereby removed at once. And, when their own life is about to end, the Buddha and the assembly of sages come of their own accord to welcome and receive them, so that no deviant karma or entanglement whatsoever is able to obstruct them any longer. Hence this is called the empowering affinity.

"As for every other practice besides this one — though each may indeed be called good in its own right, when compared to mindfulness of the Buddha, none of them can be compared to it at all. It is for this very reason that, throughout the sūtras, everywhere, the power of buddha-recollection is extolled at length. Just as, within the forty-eight vows of the Sūtra of Immeasurable Life, it is made plain that single-minded recollection of Amitābha's own name alone brings about rebirth there; and just as, within the Amitābha Sūtra, single-minded recollection of Amitābha's own name, whether for one day or for seven, brings about rebirth there; and, further, the buddhas of the ten directions, as numerous as the sands of the river, bear witness to this, and it is certainly not without foundation. And, within this very sūtra's own text on the contemplative and dispersed practices alike, it is likewise marked out that single-minded recollection of this name alone brings about rebirth there. This pattern is not found merely once. This concludes, in full, the revealing of the samādhi of buddha-recollection."

A note on this passage

This is one of the single most consequential passages in the entire history of Pure Land Buddhism, and it is worth pausing to see exactly what argument it makes. The question is not rhetorical — it names a real tension the sixteen contemplations themselves might seem to create, since so much of this fascicle has described elaborate visualization practices (the sun, the ground, the trees, the ponds, the towers, the throne, the image, and now the true body itself) that would seem to stand alongside "every manner of practice" as merely one option among many equally capable of dedicating merit toward rebirth. Shandao's answer does not diminish those practices, but it does something more specific: it explains why mindfulness of the Buddha — calling his name, bowing to him, holding him in thought — creates a distinct kind of relationship unavailable through other virtuous acts. The intimate affinity describes mutual, reciprocal attention across all three modes of action; the close affinity describes an immediacy of response, appearing "directly before their own eyes" the moment wished for; the empowering affinity describes the specific promise of a welcoming escort at the moment of death, when karmic entanglement would otherwise have the most power to obstruct.

What makes this passage historically pivotal is its closing move: rather than treating buddha-recollection as one skillful means among equals, Shandao states outright that "none of them can be compared to it at all," and marshals the Sūtra of Immeasurable Life's forty-eight vows, the Amitābha Sūtra, and this very sūtra's own text as three independent scriptural witnesses to a single claim — that recollection of Amitābha's own name specifically, not merit in general, is what secures rebirth. This argument, first made here within a commentary on visualization practice, became the seed from which an entire later tradition — most famously Hōnen's founding of Japan's Jōdo-shū centuries later — grew a practice centered on the recitation of the Buddha's name above every other form of cultivation.
