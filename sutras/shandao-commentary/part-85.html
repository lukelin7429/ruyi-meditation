Three Practitioners, and a Mirror Held to Darkness

Having established the three minds as this grade's own correct cause, the text now names three distinct kinds of practitioner suited to it — each relying on a different capacity of their own — before the six mindfulnesses close on an unexpectedly personal note: a practitioner comparing their own drifting, ordinary life to bodhisattvas who never once begrudged their own body, and finding the comparison genuinely painful.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 4 of 4: 散善義 (Meaning of the Dispersed Good)

Non-Killing and Reciting the Mahāyāna

Chinese Source

五、從復有三種眾生已下，正明簡機，堪能奉法，依教修行。六、從何等為三下，至六念已來，正明受法不同。即有其三。一明慈心不殺。然殺業有多種，或有口殺，或有身殺，或有心殺。言口殺者，處分許可名為口殺。言身殺者，動身手等指授名為身殺。言心殺者，思念方便計校等名為心殺。若論殺業，不簡四生，皆能招罪障生淨土。但於一切生命起於慈心者，即是施一切眾生壽命安樂，亦是最上勝妙戒也。此即合上初福第三句云慈心不殺也。即有止行二善。自不殺故名止善，教他不殺故名行善。自他初斷名止善，畢竟永除名行善。雖有止持二善，總結成慈下行也。言具諸戒行者，若約人天二乘之器即名小戒，若約大心大行之人即名菩薩戒。此戒若以位約者，當此上輩三位者即名菩薩戒，正由人位定故自然轉成，即合上第二福戒分善根也。二、明讀誦大乘者。此明眾生性習不同，執法各異。前第一人但用修慈持戒為能，次第二人唯將讀誦大乘為是。然戒即能持五乘三佛之機，法即薰成三賢十地萬行之智慧。若以德用來比校者，各有一能。即合上第三福第三句云讀誦大乘也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, from "there are further three kinds of sentient beings" onward, this makes plain the distinguishing of capacity — those able to uphold this teaching and cultivate practice according to it. Sixth, from "what are these three" down through "the six mindfulnesses," this makes plain the differing ways in which this teaching is received. There are three points within it.

First, this makes plain a compassionate mind that refrains from killing. Now, the karma of killing is of many kinds — there is killing through speech, killing through the body, and killing through the mind. As for killing through speech: giving one's own order or permission is called killing through speech. As for killing through the body: moving one's own body or hand to direct or carry it out is called killing through the body. As for killing through the mind: thinking, planning, and calculating it is called killing through the mind. Whenever this karma of killing is discussed, it makes no distinction among the four modes of birth — every instance is able to bring about offense and obstruct rebirth in the pure land. But whoever gives rise to a compassionate mind toward every living being thereby bestows upon all sentient beings their own peace and longevity — this is itself the very highest and most excellent precept. This corresponds directly to the third clause of the first blessing described earlier, "a compassionate mind that refrains from killing." Within this, there are two kinds of goodness: restraint and active practice. Refraining from killing oneself is called the goodness of restraint; teaching others not to kill is called the goodness of active practice. Both oneself and others first ceasing this act is called the goodness of restraint; utterly and permanently eliminating it is called the goodness of active practice. Though there are these two kinds of goodness, restraint and its maintenance, together they are gathered into what was earlier called the practice of compassion toward those below oneself.

As for "possessing the full body of precepts": if measured against the capacity of gods, humans, and the two lesser vehicles, these are called the lesser precepts; if measured against a person of great mind and great practice, these are called the bodhisattva precepts. Measured according to grade, these precepts, belonging to these three positions of the upper rank, are themselves called the bodhisattva precepts — precisely because the very position of the person is already fixed at this level, they naturally transform into this. This corresponds to the root of goodness belonging to the precepts described in the second blessing above.

Second, this makes plain reciting the Mahāyāna. This makes plain that sentient beings' own natures and habits differ, each holding to the Dharma in their own distinct way. The first person described above relies only on cultivating compassion and upholding the precepts as their own capacity; this second person relies solely on reciting the Mahāyāna as their own capacity. Yet the precepts are themselves able to uphold the capacities belonging to the five vehicles and the three kinds of buddhas, while the Dharma itself cultivates and perfects the wisdom belonging to the three worthy stages, the ten grounds, and the myriad practices. Comparing their own respective virtue and function, each possesses its own distinct capability. This corresponds to the third clause of the third blessing described earlier, "reciting the Mahāyāna."

The Six Mindfulnesses, and a Mirror Held to Darkness

Chinese Source

三、明修行六念者。所謂念佛、法、僧，念戒、捨、天等。此亦通合上第三福大乘之意義也。言念佛者，即專念阿彌陀佛口業功德、身業功德、意業功德。一切諸佛亦如是。又一心專念諸佛所證之法，並諸眷屬菩薩僧。又念諸佛之戒，及念過去諸佛、現在菩薩等，難作能作、難捨能捨、內捨外捨、內外捨。此等菩薩，但欲念法不惜身財。行者等既念知此事，即須常作仰學前賢後聖捨身命意也。又念天者，即是最後身十地之菩薩，此等難行之行已過，三祇之劫已超，萬德之行已成，灌頂之位已證。行者等既念知已，即自思念：「我身無際已來，共他同時發願，斷惡行菩薩道，他盡不惜身命，行道進位，因圓果熟證聖者，踰於大地微塵。然我等凡夫，乃至今日虛然流浪，煩惱惡障轉轉增多，福慧微微，若對重昏之臨明鏡也。」忽思忖此事，不勝心驚悲歎者哉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, this makes plain cultivating the six mindfulnesses. These are mindfulness of the Buddha, of the Dharma, and of the Sangha; mindfulness of the precepts, of generosity, and of the heavens. This too corresponds broadly to the meaning of the Mahāyāna within the third blessing described above.

As for mindfulness of the Buddha: this means single-mindedly holding in mind Amitābha Buddha's own merit of speech, merit of body, and merit of mind — and likewise for every other buddha as well. Further, this means single-mindedly holding in mind the Dharma that every buddha has realized, together with their own retinue of bodhisattvas and the Sangha. Further, this means holding in mind every buddha's own precepts, and holding in mind the buddhas of the past and the bodhisattvas of the present, who accomplish what is difficult to accomplish, relinquish what is difficult to relinquish, relinquishing inwardly, relinquishing outwardly, relinquishing both within and without together. These very bodhisattvas wished only to hold the Dharma in mind, never begrudging their own body or wealth. Practitioners, having come to know this very matter through mindfulness, must constantly look up to and learn from these sages of the past and future, and their own resolve to relinquish body and life itself.

Further, as for mindfulness of the heavens: this refers to the bodhisattvas of their own final body, upon the tenth ground, who have already passed beyond these difficult practices, already transcended the three incalculable kalpas, already accomplished the practice of ten thousand virtues, and already realized the stage of consecration. Practitioners, having come to know this through mindfulness, should then reflect upon themselves, thinking: "My own self, since beginningless time, gave rise to this very vow together with these others, resolving to sever evil and practice the bodhisattva path — yet they, never begrudging their own body or life, advanced along the path and rose through its stages, their cause perfected and their fruit ripened, realizing sagehood in numbers exceeding the very dust motes of this great earth. Yet we ourselves, ordinary beings, have to this very day drifted about in vain, our own afflictions and evil obstructions only ever increasing further, our own merit and wisdom ever more slight — as though holding a bright mirror up before some heavy darkness." Suddenly reflecting upon this very matter, one cannot help but feel one's own heart startled, grieving, and lamenting.

A note on this grief

This passage breaks from Shandao's usual expository voice into something closer to confession, and it is worth noticing exactly where this happens: not while discussing the precepts or scriptural recitation, but at the close of mindfulness of the heavens — contemplating bodhisattvas who have already completed the entire path. The comparison Shandao stages here is deliberately unflattering to the practitioner: their own vow, made "since beginningless time," identical in kind to those advanced bodhisattvas' own vow, and yet followed by nothing but drift, while theirs was followed by "numbers exceeding the very dust motes of this great earth" who actually reached the goal. This grief is not incidental to the practice being described — it is itself part of what mindfulness of the heavens is meant to produce, an honest reckoning that, per everything already established in the deep mind, becomes the very ground from which resolute faith can then arise.
