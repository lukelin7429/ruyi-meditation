The Three Blessings, and a Monk's Three Robes

The teaching that will structure the rest of this fascicle finally opens: three blessings, offered as a path for beings of scattered, ordinary capacity rather than advanced meditators. The first is filial devotion — and Shandao anchors it not in abstract exhortation but in a story where the Buddha himself, starving, refuses a bowl of rice a monk nearly starved himself to buy him.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 2 of 4: 序分義 (Meaning of the Preliminary Section)

Opening the Three Blessings

Chinese Source

三、從亦令未來世下，至極樂國土已來，正明舉機勸修得益。此明夫人所請利益彌深，及未來迴心皆到。

四、從欲生彼國者下，至名為淨業已來，正明勸修三福之行。此明一切眾生機有二種：一者定，二者散。若依定行即攝生不盡，是以如來方便顯開三福，以應散動根機。言欲生彼國者，標指所歸也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, from "and also causing sentient beings of future ages" down through "the Land of Ultimate Bliss," this makes plain the raising up of this teaching, exhorting cultivation, so that its benefit might be gained. This makes plain that the benefit the queen had requested runs deeper still, extending even to future beings who turn their own minds toward it — every one of whom shall likewise arrive there.

Fourth, from "those who wish to be reborn in that land" down through "this is called pure karma," this makes plain the exhortation to cultivate the practice of the three blessings. This makes plain that all sentient beings' own capacities are of two kinds: the first, contemplative; the second, dispersed. Were the teaching to rely on contemplative practice alone, it could not gather in every being without exception — and so the Tathāgata, through skillful means, revealed and opened wide the three blessings, so as to accord with those of a more dispersed and scattered capacity. As for "those who wish to be reborn in that land": this marks out directly the destination itself.

Why Parents Are Owed So Much

Chinese Source

言當修三福者，總標行門也。云何名三？一者孝養父母。即有其四。一言孝養父母者，此明一切凡夫皆藉緣而生。云何藉緣？或有化生，或有濕生，或有卵生，或有胎生。此四生中各各復有四生，如經廣說，但是相因而生即有父母。既有父母，即有大恩，若無父者能生之因即闕，若無母者所生之緣即乖，若二人俱無即失託生之地，要須父母緣具方有受身之處。既欲受身，以自業識為內因，以父母精血為外緣，因緣和合故有此身，以斯義故父母恩重。母懷胎已經於十月，行住坐臥常生苦惱，復憂產時死難。若生已，經於三年恒常眠屎臥尿，床被衣服皆亦不淨。及其長大愛婦親兒，於父母處反生憎疾、不行恩孝者，即與畜生無異也。又父母者，世間福田之極也。佛者，即是出世福田之極也。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "you should now cultivate the three blessings": this generally marks out the very gate of practice itself. What are these three called? The first is filial devotion in nurturing one's own parents. Within this there are four points.

First, as for "filial devotion in nurturing one's own parents": this makes plain that every ordinary being is born only by relying on conditions. What is meant by relying on conditions? Some are born by transformation, some are born from moisture, some are born from eggs, some are born from a womb. Within each of these four modes of birth there are again four further modes, as the sūtras explain at length — but in every case, birth comes about through a mutual cause, and so there are parents. Once there are parents, there is a great debt of kindness owed to them: without a father, the very cause capable of engendering life would be lacking; without a mother, the very condition that gives rise to that life would be absent; and were both entirely absent, there would be no ground at all upon which to take rebirth — only when the conditions of both father and mother are complete together is there any place to receive a body at all. And once a body is to be received, one's own karmic consciousness serves as the inner cause, while the father's and mother's own seed and blood serve as the outer condition — it is through the union of this cause and this condition that this body comes to exist. For this very reason, the debt owed to one's parents is heavy indeed. A mother carries her child in the womb for a full ten months, and in walking, standing, sitting, and lying down alike, she continually suffers hardship, and further dreads the danger of death at the moment of birth. Once the child is born, for three full years she must constantly lie amid its urine and excrement, her bed, coverlets, and clothing all alike soiled. And yet, once the child has grown, and comes to love his own wife and cherish his own children, if he should instead give rise to resentment and enmity toward his own parents, and fail to repay them with gratitude and devotion, he is then no different at all from an animal. Further, one's parents are themselves the very highest field of merit within this mundane world; the Buddha is himself the very highest field of merit beyond this mundane world entirely.

A Bowl of Rice the Buddha Would Not Eat

Chinese Source

然佛在世時，遇值時年飢儉，人皆餓死白骨縱橫，諸比丘等乞食難得。於時世尊待比丘等去後，獨自入城乞食，從旦至中門門喚乞，無與食者，佛還空鉢而歸。明日復去又還不得，後日復去又亦不得。忽有一比丘道逢見佛，顏色異常似有飢相，即問佛言：「世尊！今已食竟也？」佛言：「比丘，我經三日已來乞食不得一匙，我今飢虛無力，能共汝語。」比丘聞佛語已，悲淚不能自勝，即自念言：「佛是無上福田、眾生覆護，我此三衣賣却，買取一鉢飯奉上於佛，今正是時也。」作是念已即買得一鉢飯，急將上佛。佛知而故問言：「比丘，時年飢儉人皆餓死，汝今何處得此一鉢純色飯來？」比丘如前具白世尊。佛又言：「比丘，三衣者即是三世諸佛之幢相，此衣因緣極尊極重極恩，汝今易得此飯與我者，大領汝好心，我不消此飯也。」比丘重白佛言：「佛是三界福田、聖中之極，尚言不消者，除佛已外誰能消也？」佛言：「比丘，汝有父母已不？」答言：「有。」「汝將供養父母去。」比丘言：「佛尚云不消，我父母豈能消也？」佛言：「得消。何以故？父母能生汝身，於汝有大重恩，為此得消。」佛又問比丘：「汝父母有信佛心不？」比丘言：「都無信心。」佛言：「今有信心。見汝與飯大生歡喜，因此即發信心。先教受三歸依，即能消此食也。」時比丘既受佛教愍仰而去。以此義故，大須孝養父母。又佛母摩耶生佛，經七日已即死，生忉利天。佛後成道，至四月十五日，即向忉利天一夏為母說法，為報十月懷胎之恩。佛尚自收恩孝養父母，何況凡夫而不孝養。故知父母恩深極重也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, when the Buddha was in the world, he once encountered a year of famine and dearth, when people everywhere starved to death, their bleached bones strewn across the ground, and the monks found it exceedingly difficult to obtain alms of any kind. At that time, the World-Honored One, once the monks had gone out ahead of him, entered the city alone to beg for food, going from door to door from morning until noon, calling out at each one — yet none gave him anything to eat, and the Buddha returned with his own bowl still empty. The following day he went out again, and again returned without success; the day after that he went out once more, and again received nothing at all. Then, quite suddenly, a certain monk, meeting the Buddha along the road, saw that his complexion looked unusual, as though he bore the marks of hunger, and asked him: "World-Honored One, have you eaten yet today?" The Buddha replied: "Monk, for three days now I have begged for alms and have not obtained so much as a single spoonful; I am now so weak from hunger that I can scarcely speak with you." Hearing the Buddha's own words, the monk was overcome with grief and could not hold back his own tears, and thought to himself: "The Buddha is the supreme field of merit, the protector and shelter of all sentient beings — I shall sell these three robes of mine, and buy a single bowl of rice to offer up to the Buddha; now is truly the time to do this." Having resolved upon this thought, he bought a single bowl of rice and hurried to bring it before the Buddha. The Buddha, though he already knew, asked him deliberately: "Monk, in this year of famine and dearth, when people everywhere are starving to death, where did you manage to obtain this single bowl of pure, unmixed rice?" The monk explained to the World-Honored One in full, just as before. The Buddha then said: "Monk, these three robes are themselves the very banner and emblem of the buddhas of the three times; the causes and conditions bound up in this robe are supremely honored, supremely weighty, and represent a debt of supreme kindness. Though you have now so readily traded it away for this bowl of rice to give to me, I deeply appreciate your own good intention — but I cannot accept and consume this rice." The monk addressed the Buddha once more, saying: "The Buddha is the field of merit for the three realms, the very highest among the sages — if even the Buddha says he cannot accept this offering, then who besides the Buddha could possibly accept it?" The Buddha replied: "Monk, do you have parents living or not?" He answered: "I do." "Then take this and offer it to your own parents instead." The monk said: "If even the Buddha says he cannot accept it, how could my own parents possibly accept it?" The Buddha said: "They can indeed accept it. Why is this so? Because your parents are themselves able to give birth to your own body, and they bear toward you a debt of great and weighty kindness — for this very reason, they are able to accept it." The Buddha then further asked the monk: "Do your parents have any faith in the Buddha, or not?" The monk replied: "They have no faith in him at all." The Buddha said: "They shall now have faith. Seeing you offer them this rice, they will feel great joy, and on account of this, faith itself will arise within them. First teach them to receive the three refuges, and only then will they truly be able to accept and consume this food." The monk, having received the Buddha's own teaching, departed in reverent gratitude. It is for this very reason that filial devotion in nurturing one's own parents is so greatly necessary.

Further, the Buddha's own mother, Māyā, gave birth to the Buddha, and seven days later she herself died, and was reborn in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven. After the Buddha had attained the path, on the fifteenth day of the fourth month, he went up to the Trāyastriṃśa heaven and, for the full duration of one summer retreat, taught the Dharma to his own mother there, in repayment of the debt owed for her ten months of carrying him in her own womb. If even the Buddha himself thus gathered in this debt of gratitude and showed filial devotion to his own parents, how much more, then, should ordinary beings fail to show such devotion? One should therefore know that the debt owed to one's own parents runs deep and weighs heavy indeed.

A note on this refusal

What makes this story turn on itself is the Buddha's own refusal, at the exact moment of his own hunger, to eat the rice offered him. Shandao does not read this as false modesty — the Buddha's own reasoning is explicit: the three robes given up to buy it were "supremely honored, supremely weighty," and a gift of that magnitude belongs, by right, to whoever bears the monk the greatest debt of kindness. That the Buddha redirects it to the monk's own parents, and then supplies the mechanism — their gratitude at receiving it becomes the very occasion for their own conversion — turns what began as a story about hunger into a demonstration of exactly what "filial devotion" is doing inside the three blessings at all: not sentiment, but the recognition of a debt so structurally basic that even the Buddha defers to it. The closing detail, that the Buddha himself ascended to teach his own mother in the heaven of the thirty-three, repaying her ten months of pregnancy even after his own full awakening, closes the point Shandao is building toward — if buddhahood itself does not exempt one from this debt, no ordinary being could ever claim to be exempt from it either.
