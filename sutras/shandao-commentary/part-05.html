Ten Proofs, and the "Deferred-Time" Objection

Shandao closes his refutation of "other masters" with ten direct citations from the sūtra's own text — each one addressed explicitly to "ordinary beings of future ages," never to sages. He then turns to defend a second, related claim under attack: that the ten recitations of Amitābha's name at the deathbed bring about rebirth immediately, not merely as a distant cause realized only much later — a reading he rejects in his own sharpest language anywhere in this fascicle.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 1 of 4: 玄義分 (Profound Meaning)

4. Ten Passages, Proving the Sūtra Is for Ordinary Beings

Chinese Source

第四、出文顯證者。

問曰：「上來返對之義，云何得知世尊定為凡夫，不為聖人者？未審直以人情準義為當，亦有聖教來證？」

答曰：「眾生垢重，智慧淺近。聖意弘深，豈寧自輒！今者一一悉取佛說以為明證。就此證中即有其十句。何者？第一、如《觀經》云：『佛告韋提：「我今為汝廣說眾譬，亦令未來世一切凡夫欲修淨業者，得生西方極樂國土」』者，是其一證也。二、言：『如來今者為未來世一切眾生為煩惱賊之所害者說清淨業』者，是其二證也。三、言：『如來今者教韋提希及未來世一切眾生觀於西方極樂世界』者，是其三證也。四、言：『韋提白佛：「我今因佛力故見彼國土。若佛滅後，諸眾生等濁惡不善五苦所逼，云何當見彼佛國土」』者，是其四證也。五、如日觀初云：『佛告韋提：「汝及眾生專念」已下乃至「一切眾生自非生盲，有目之徒見日」』已來者，是其五證也。六、如地觀中說言：『佛告阿難：「汝持佛語，為未來世一切眾生欲脫苦者，說是觀地法」』者，是其六證也。

七、如華座觀中說言：『韋提白佛：「我因佛力得見阿彌陀佛及二菩薩，未來眾生云何得見」』者，是其七證也。八、次下答請中說言：『佛告韋提：「汝及眾生欲觀彼佛者當起想念」』者，是其八證也。九、如像觀中說言：『佛告韋提：「諸佛如來入一切眾生心想中，是故汝等心想佛時」』者，是其九證也。十、如九品之中一一說言：『為諸眾生』者，是其十證也。上來雖有十句不同，證明如來說此十六觀法但為常沒眾生，不干大小聖也。以斯文證，豈是謬哉！」

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, bringing forward the text itself as clear proof.

Question: "As for the meaning of this reversed refutation given above, how can it be known for certain that the World-Honored One fixed this teaching for ordinary beings, and not for sages? It is not yet clear whether this is settled merely by taking human sentiment as one's standard, or whether there is, in fact, sacred teaching to prove it."

Answer: "Sentient beings' own defilements run heavy, and their wisdom is shallow and near-sighted. The sacred intent runs vast and deep — how could I possibly presume to decide this on my own authority! I will now take up, one by one, everything the Buddha himself said, as clear proof. Within this proof there are exactly ten passages. What are they? First, as the Contemplation Sūtra says: 'The Buddha said to Vaidehī: "I will now, for your sake, explain at length every kind of simile, and will also cause every ordinary being of future ages who wishes to cultivate pure karma to attain rebirth in the western land of Ultimate Bliss"' — this is the first proof. Second, it says: 'The Tathāgata now, for the sake of every sentient being of future ages harmed by the thief of affliction, expounds this pure karma' — this is the second proof. Third, it says: 'The Tathāgata now teaches Vaidehī, and every sentient being of future ages, to contemplate the western world of Ultimate Bliss' — this is the third proof. Fourth, it says: 'Vaidehī said to the Buddha: "I am now able, through the Buddha's own power, to see that land. But after the Buddha has entered extinction, sentient beings, turbid, evil, and unwholesome, pressed by the five sufferings — how will they be able to see that buddha's own land at all?"' — this is the fourth proof. Fifth, as the very opening of the sun-contemplation says: 'The Buddha said to Vaidehī: "You, and every sentient being, should single-mindedly hold this in thought"' — down through "every sentient being, provided only they are not blind from birth, anyone with eyes at all can see the sun" — this is the fifth proof. Sixth, as the text on the earth-contemplation says: 'The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Hold fast to the Buddha's own words, and, for the sake of every sentient being of future ages who wishes to be freed from suffering, teach this method of contemplating the ground"' — this is the sixth proof.

"Seventh, as the text on the lotus-throne contemplation says: 'Vaidehī said to the Buddha: "I am able, through the Buddha's own power, to see Amitābha Buddha and his two bodhisattvas — but how will sentient beings of future ages be able to see them?"' — this is the seventh proof. Eighth, in the answer to this request further on, it says: 'The Buddha said to Vaidehī: "You, and every sentient being who wishes to contemplate that buddha, should give rise to this same act of visualization"' — this is the eighth proof. Ninth, as the text on the image-contemplation says: 'The Buddha said to Vaidehī: "Every buddha, every Tathāgata, enters into the very mind and thought of every sentient being — and so, at the very moment your own mind holds the Buddha in thought..."' — this is the ninth proof. Tenth, as each and every one of the nine grades itself says: "for the sake of every sentient being" — this is the tenth proof. Though these ten passages differ from one another, taken together they prove that the Tathāgata, in speaking these sixteen contemplations, spoke them only for the sake of beings constantly sunk in suffering, and that this has nothing whatsoever to do with sages, whether of the greater or the lesser vehicle. Taking this text as proof — how could this possibly be a mistaken reading?"

A note on this proof

Shandao's own methodological confession, at the very opening of this section, is worth pausing over: "sentient beings' own defilements run heavy, and their wisdom is shallow and near-sighted... how could I possibly presume to decide this on my own authority!" Having just spent two entire sections refuting a rival reading by reasoning and by close citation, he still declines to let his own argument stand on reasoning alone — insisting instead on ten separate instances of the sūtra's own explicit language, each one naming "every sentient being of future ages" or "ordinary beings" as its addressee. The cumulative weight of the list is the point: no single citation could carry this claim on its own, but ten, drawn from across the entire sūtra — its opening frame, its middle contemplations, its closing nine grades — leave, in Shandao's own view, no room left for the reading he has spent this whole gate dismantling.

5. Reconciling the "Deferred-Time" Objection

Chinese Source

第五、會通別時意者。即有其二：一、論云：「如人念多寶佛，即於無上菩提得不退墮」者，凡言菩提乃是佛果之名，亦是正報道理成佛之法。要須萬行圓備，方乃剋成。豈將念佛一行即望成者，無有是處。雖言未證，萬行之中是其一行。何以得知？如《華嚴經》說：「功德雲比丘語善財言：『我於佛法三昧海中唯知一行，所謂念佛三昧。』」以此文證，豈非一行也。雖是一行，於生死中乃至成佛永不退沒，故名不墮。

問曰：「若爾者，《法華經》云：『一稱南無佛，皆已成佛道。』亦應成佛竟也。此之二文，有何差別？」

答曰：「論中稱佛，唯欲自成佛果。經中稱佛，為簡異九十五種外道。然外道之中，都無稱佛之人。但使稱佛一口，即在佛道中攝，故言已竟。」

二、論中說云：「如人唯由發願生安樂土」者，久來通論之家不會論意，錯引下品下生，十聲稱佛，與此相似，未即得生。如一金錢得成千者，多日乃得，非一日即得成千。十聲稱佛亦復如是，但與遠生作因，是故未即得生。噵佛直為當來凡夫，欲令捨惡稱佛，誑言噵生，實未得生，名作別時意者。何故《阿彌陀經》云：「佛告舍利弗：『若有善男子、善女人，聞說阿彌陀佛，即應執持名號，一日乃至七日，一心願生。命欲終時，阿彌陀佛與諸聖眾迎接往生。』次下十方各如恒河沙等諸佛各出廣長舌相，遍覆三千大千世界說誠實言：『汝等眾生，皆應信是一切諸佛所護念經。』」言護念者，即是上文一日乃至七日稱佛之名也。今既有斯聖教以為明證，未審今時一切行者，不知何意，凡小之論乃加信受，諸佛誠言返將妄語。苦哉奈劇，能出如此不忍之言。雖然，仰願一切欲往生知識等，善自思量。寧傷今世錯信佛語，不可執菩薩論以為指南。

若依此執者，即是自失誤他也。

問曰：「云何起行，而言不得往生？」

答曰：「若欲往生者，要須行願具足方可得生。今此論中但言發願不論有行。」

問曰：「何故不論？」答曰：「乃至一念曾未措心，是故不論。」

問曰：「願行之義有何差別？」

答曰：「如經中說：『但有其行，行即孤亦無所至，但有其願，願即虛亦無所至，要須願行相扶所為皆剋。』是故今此論中直言發願不論有行，是故未即得生與遠生作因者，其義實也。」

問曰：「願意云何乃言不生？」

答曰：「聞他說言西方快樂不可思議，即作願言：『我亦願生。』噵此語已更不相續，故名願也。今此《觀經》中十聲稱佛，即有十願，十行具足。云何具足？言南無者即是歸命，亦是發願迴向之義。言阿彌陀佛者即是其行。以斯義故必得往生。又來論中稱多寶佛為求佛果即是正報，下唯發願求生淨土即是依報。一正一依，豈得相似。然正報難期，一行雖精未剋。依報易求，所以一願之心未入。雖然，譬如邊方投化即易，為主即難。今時願往生者，並是一切投化眾生，豈非易也。但能上盡一形，下至十念，以佛願力莫不皆往，故名易也。斯乃不可以言定義，取信之者懷疑，要引聖教來明，欲使聞之者方能遣惑。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, reconciling the meaning of "deferred time." There are two points here.

First: the treatise says, "It is like a person who recollects the buddha Prabhūtaratna, and thereby attains non-retrogression in unsurpassed bodhi." Now, "bodhi," generally speaking, is the name for a buddha's own fruit — it is also the Dharma by which one accomplishes buddhahood through the principle of direct retribution. It requires the full and complete accomplishment of ten thousand practices before it can ever actually be realized. How, then, could the single practice of recollecting the Buddha alone be expected to accomplish it? There is no such possibility. Though it is said that this alone does not yet realize buddhahood, it is nonetheless one practice among the ten thousand. How is this known? As the Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "The bhikṣu Meghaśrī said to Sudhana: 'Within the ocean of samādhi in the Buddha-dharma, I know only a single practice — namely, the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha.'" Taking this text as proof, is this not indeed one practice among many? Though it is only one practice, within the whole of birth and death, all the way up to the attainment of buddhahood itself, it never once retrogresses or is lost — hence it is called "non-retrogression."

Question: "If this is so, then the Lotus Sūtra says: 'Even a single utterance of homage to the Buddha is already the accomplishment of the buddha-path.' This, too, should mean that buddhahood is already accomplished in full. What difference is there between these two texts?"

Answer: "In the treatise, calling upon the Buddha's name is intended solely to accomplish buddhahood for oneself. In the sūtra, calling upon the Buddha's name is meant to distinguish this from the ninety-five kinds of heterodox paths. For among these heterodox paths, there is no one at all who calls upon a buddha's name in the first place. So the moment anyone at all utters a buddha's name even once, they are already gathered into the buddha-path — hence it is said to be 'already accomplished.'"

Second, the treatise also says, "It is like a person who, merely by making a vow, is born in the land of peace and bliss" — and for a long time now, scholars given to general theorizing about this treatise have failed to grasp its actual intent, mistakenly citing the lowest of the lower grade, whose ten utterances of the Buddha's name resemble this passage, and concluding that such a person does not attain rebirth immediately. It is, they say, like a single gold coin that eventually grows into a thousand — but only after many days, not becoming a thousand in a single day. The ten utterances of the Buddha's name, they say, are the same: they serve only as a distant cause for eventual rebirth, and so rebirth is not attained immediately. They say the Buddha spoke this only for the sake of ordinary beings of the future, wishing to have them give up evil and call upon the Buddha's name, and that when he speaks of rebirth, this is a kind of expedient exaggeration — that rebirth is not, in fact, actually attained — and this is called the teaching of "deferred time."

Why, then, does the Amitābha Sūtra say: "The Buddha said to Śāriputra: 'If there is a good son or good daughter who, hearing Amitābha Buddha spoken of, holds fast to his name, whether for one day or up to seven days, with a single mind, wishing to be reborn there — when their life comes to its end, Amitābha Buddha, together with the assembly of sages, will come to welcome and receive them into rebirth'" — and, further on, buddhas of the ten directions, each as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, each extending their own broad and long tongue, covering an entire trichiliocosm, speak these true and honest words: "You, sentient beings, should all believe in this sūtra, which every buddha alike watches over and holds in mind." What is meant by "watches over and holds in mind" is exactly this same practice described above — holding fast to the Buddha's own name for one day, or up to seven. Given that there already exists this kind of sacred teaching as clear proof, it remains unclear why present-day practitioners, for whatever reason, place their trust in the theorizing of lesser scholars, while treating the true and honest words of every buddha as if they were mere falsehood instead. How bitter, how extreme, that anyone could utter words this unbearable! And yet, I can only look up and beg every teacher and every practitioner who wishes to attain rebirth there to reflect carefully on this for themselves: it is better to risk, in this present life, mistakenly placing one's trust in the Buddha's own words, than to cling to a bodhisattva's treatise as though it were one's own guiding compass.

Should one hold fast to this misreading, one would lead oneself astray, and mislead others besides.

Question: "How does this square with the claim that, unless one actually undertakes the practice, one cannot attain rebirth?"

Answer: "For one who wishes to attain rebirth, it is necessary that both vow and practice be complete together before rebirth can be attained at all. In this treatise, only the making of a vow is spoken of — the question of actual practice is simply not discussed there."

Question: "Why is it not discussed?" Answer: "Because not even a single thought has yet actually been put into action — this is exactly why it is not discussed."

Question: "What difference is there between the meaning of vow and the meaning of practice?"

Answer: "As the sūtra itself says: 'Practice alone, with no vow, is solitary, and arrives nowhere; vow alone, with no practice, is empty, and arrives nowhere as well — vow and practice must support each other before what one sets out to do can ever be accomplished.' This is exactly why this treatise, speaking only of the arising of a vow without discussing actual practice, means that rebirth is not attained immediately, but only serves as a cause for eventual, distant rebirth — and this much of its meaning is indeed true."

Question: "How, then, is it said that this vow alone does not lead to rebirth?"

Answer: "It is when someone hears another say that the west is blissful beyond conception, and simply makes the vow, saying, 'I too wish to be reborn there' — and, having said these words, carries this thought no further at all — that this is properly called mere 'vow' alone. But in this present Contemplation Sūtra, the ten utterances of the Buddha's name already contain, within them, ten vows and ten complete practices at once. How are they complete? The word 'namo' is itself taking refuge, and is itself the meaning of making a vow and dedicating it; the words 'Amitābha Buddha' are themselves the very practice. It is for this reason that rebirth is certainly attained. And further, in the treatise's own passage, calling upon the buddha Prabhūtaratna, seeking the fruit of buddhahood, is exactly direct retribution itself; while, below, merely making a vow to seek rebirth in the Pure Land is exactly retributive support. One is direct, the other is supportive — how could these two possibly be treated as the same thing? Direct retribution is difficult to expect quickly — a single practice, however diligent, has not yet accomplished it. Retributive support, however, is easy to seek — which is why a single moment's vow has not yet, on its own, been enough to enter it. And yet, it is like how, on the frontier, submitting to a ruler's authority is easy, while actually becoming that ruler oneself is hard. Those who now vow to be reborn there are, every one of them, simply submitting themselves to another's rule — how could this not be easy? So long as one is able, at the very fullest, to sustain this practice across an entire lifetime, or, at the very least, ten thoughts alone, riding upon the power of the Buddha's own vow, without exception, all such people go there — this is exactly why it is called easy. This cannot simply be settled by assertion alone; those who might otherwise place their trust in it are left instead in doubt — it is necessary to bring forward the sacred teaching itself to make this plain, so that whoever hears it may finally have their doubt dispelled."

A note on this reconciliation

This same "deferred-time" objection, and Shandao's own answer to it, will already be familiar to readers of this site's Rebirth Treatise pages, where Tanluan takes up the identical passage from the Compendium of the Great Vehicle a century and a half earlier, and this site's Ten Doubts treatise, whose eighth doubt runs an almost identical argument about ten deathbed thoughts outweighing a lifetime of evil. What distinguishes Shandao's own treatment is less the logic — vow and practice must support each other, and the ten recitations already contain both at once, "namo" itself already a vow, the name itself already the practice — than its tone. Where Tanluan and the Ten Doubts treatise argue this calmly, as one doctrinal position among others to be reasoned through, Shandao breaks into open alarm: "how bitter, how extreme, that anyone could utter words this unbearable" — treating the deferred-time reading not as a scholarly error but as something close to blasphemy, setting a bodhisattva's own treatise above the plainly stated words of the buddhas themselves. This is Shandao's most openly polemical moment in the fascicle so far, and it will not be his last: the same conviction — that a rigorist, gradualist reading of the sūtras does real harm to real people relying on this teaching at the hour of their own death — runs underneath every argument in this commentary, from here through its final fascicle.

Part 5 Complete
