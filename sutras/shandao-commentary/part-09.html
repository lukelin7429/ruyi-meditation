The Assembly, and Why Heterodox Teachers Are Named

The fourth and final point of "the preface before the teaching begins": the full assembly gathered to hear it, śrāvakas and bodhisattvas alike. Shandao pauses over a detail most readers pass by without a second thought — that so many of the Buddha's own listed disciples are introduced as former followers of heterodox paths — and finds in it a small but genuine act of compassion on both sides.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 2 of 4: 序分義 (Meaning of the Preliminary Section)

The Assembly of Śrāvakas: Nine Points of Greatness

Chinese Source

四、從與大比丘眾下，至而為上首已來，明佛徒眾。就此眾中，即分為二：一者聲聞眾，二者菩薩眾。就聲聞眾中，即有其九：初言與者，佛身兼眾，故名為與。二者總大。三者相大。四者眾大。五者耆年大。六者數大。七者尊宿大。八者內有實德大。九者果證大。

問曰：「一切經首，皆有此等聲聞以為猶置，有何所以？」

答曰：「此有別意。云何別意？此等聲聞多是外道。如《賢愚經》說：『優樓頻蠃迦葉，領五百弟子，修事邪法。伽耶迦葉，領二百五十弟子，修事邪法。那提迦葉，領二百五十弟子，修事邪法。總有一千。皆受佛化，得羅漢道。其二百五十者，即是舍利、目連弟子，共領一處，修事邪法。亦受佛化，皆得道果。此等四眾，合為一處。故有千二百五十人也。』」

問曰：「此眾中亦有非外道者，何故總標？」

答曰：「如經中說，此諸外道，常隨世尊，不相捨離。然結集之家，簡取外德，故有異名。是外道者多，非者少。」

又問曰：「未審此等外道，常隨佛後，有何意也？」

答曰：「解有二義。一、就佛解，二、就外道解。就佛解者，此諸外道，邪風久扇，非是一生。雖入真門，氣習由在。故使如來知覺，不令外化。畏損眾生正見根芽、惡業增長，此世後生，不收果實。為此因緣，攝令自近，不聽外益。此即就佛解竟。次就外道解者。迦葉等意，自唯曠劫久沈生死，循還六道，苦不可言。愚癡惡見，封執邪風，不值明師，永流於苦海。但以宿緣有遇，得會慈尊。法澤無私，我曹蒙潤。尋思佛之恩德，碎身之極惘然。致使親事靈儀，無由暫替。此即就外道解竟。」

又問曰：「此等尊宿，云何名眾所知識？」

答曰：「德高曰尊，耆年曰宿。一切凡聖知彼內德過人，識其外相殊異，故名眾所知識。」

上來雖有九句不同，解聲聞眾竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, from "together with a great assembly of bhikṣus" down through "as their foremost," this makes plain the Buddha's own body of disciples. Within this assembly, there are two divisions: the assembly of śrāvakas, and the assembly of bodhisattvas. Within the assembly of śrāvakas, there are nine points: first, as for the word "together," the Buddha's own body is here joined with the assembly, hence it is called "together." Second, its greatness in total number. Third, its greatness in outward appearance. Fourth, its greatness as an assembly. Fifth, its greatness in age and seniority. Sixth, its greatness in number. Seventh, its greatness in venerable seniority. Eighth, its greatness in genuine inner virtue. Ninth, its greatness in realized attainment.

Question: "At the head of every sūtra alike, these śrāvakas are set out and placed there — for what reason is this done?"

Answer: "There is a particular reason for this. What is this reason? Many of these śrāvakas were originally followers of heterodox paths. As the Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish says: 'Uruvilvā Kāśyapa led five hundred disciples, practicing and serving a heterodox teaching. Gayā Kāśyapa led two hundred and fifty disciples, practicing and serving a heterodox teaching. Nadī Kāśyapa led two hundred and fifty disciples, practicing and serving a heterodox teaching — one thousand in all. All of them received the Buddha's own transformation, and attained the path of arhatship. The remaining two hundred and fifty were the disciples of Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana, gathered together in a single place, likewise practicing and serving a heterodox teaching. They, too, received the Buddha's own transformation, and all attained the fruit of the path. These four assemblies, gathered together into one, are why there are, in all, one thousand two hundred and fifty people.'"

Question: "Within this assembly there are also those who were never followers of heterodox paths at all — why, then, are they all named together under this same heading?"

Answer: "As the sūtra itself says, all these former heterodox followers constantly stayed close to the World-Honored One, never once departing from him. Yet those who compiled the canon selected out this distinguishing feature, and so gave the whole assembly this particular name — for those who had been heterodox followers were the majority, and those who had not were few."

A further question: "It is not yet clear why these former heterodox followers would constantly stay so close behind the Buddha at all — what is the reason for this?"

Answer: "This resolves into two points: one from the Buddha's own side, and one from the heterodox followers' own side. From the Buddha's own side: these heterodox followers had been fanned by deviant winds for a very long time, not merely in a single lifetime — and though they have now entered the true gate, their old habitual tendencies still remain within them. This is exactly why the Tathāgata, aware of this, does not allow them to go teach outside his own presence — fearing that this might damage the very roots of right view in other sentient beings, and cause evil karma to grow further, so that neither this life nor lives to come would bear any wholesome fruit at all. For this very reason, he gathers them close to himself, and does not permit them to go out and teach independently. This concludes the explanation from the Buddha's own side. Next, from the heterodox followers' own side: Kāśyapa and the others reflected that they themselves had, since kalpas beyond measure, long been sunk in birth and death, circling endlessly through the six destinies, suffering beyond all telling — foolish, holding to evil views, sealed within their own deviant winds, never once meeting a wise teacher, forever adrift on the sea of suffering. It was only through some prior karmic connection that they finally came to meet the compassionate honored one — and the Dharma's own beneficial rain, showing no partiality at all, has now moistened even beings such as themselves. Reflecting on the Buddha's own kindness and virtue, they find themselves at a loss even should their bodies be shattered to repay it — and this is exactly why they stay so closely by his side, in person, never once willing to be parted from him even for a moment. This concludes the explanation from the heterodox followers' own side."

A further question: "Why are these venerable elders called 'well known to the assembly'?"

Answer: "One whose virtue is lofty is called venerable; one advanced in years is called an elder. Every ordinary being and sage alike knows that their inner virtue surpasses that of ordinary people, and recognizes how distinctive their outward appearance is — hence they are called 'well known to the assembly.'"

Though the above has unfolded across nine distinct points, this concludes the explanation of the assembly of śrāvakas.

A note on this exchange

Shandao's answer works in two directions at once, and both matter equally to him. From the Buddha's own side, keeping former heterodox followers close is a matter of ongoing protection: "old habitual tendencies still remain within them," and sending such disciples out to teach independently risks damaging other beings' own faith should those old tendencies resurface. But Shandao gives equal weight to how Kāśyapa and his followers themselves would have felt about it — not confined, but grateful past words, staying near the Buddha because "the Dharma's own beneficial rain, showing no partiality at all, has now moistened even beings such as themselves," and because repaying such a debt "even should their bodies be shattered" would still not feel like enough. What looks, at first glance, like a simple biographical detail in the sūtra's opening roll call becomes, in Shandao's reading, a small study in what genuine transformation actually looks like from both sides of the relationship.

The Assembly of Bodhisattvas

Chinese Source

次、解菩薩眾。就此眾中，即有其七：一者標相，二者標數，三者標位，四者標果，五者標德，六者別顯文殊高德之位，七者總結。

又此等菩薩，具無量行願，安住一切功德之法，遊步十方，行權方便。入佛法藏，究竟彼岸。於無量世界，化成等覺。光明顯曜，普照十方。無量佛土，六種震動。隨緣開示，即轉法輪，扣法鼓、執法劍、震法雷、雨法雨、演法施，常以法音覺諸世間。摑裂邪網，消滅諸見，散諸塵勞，壞諸欲塹。顯明清白，光融佛法，宣流正化，愍傷眾生。未曾慢恣，得平等法，具足無量百千三昧。於一念頃，無不周遍。荷負群生，愛之如子。一切善本，皆度彼岸。悉獲諸佛無量功德，智慧開朗不可思議。雖有七句不同，解菩薩眾訖。

上來雖有二眾不同。廣明化前序竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, explaining the assembly of bodhisattvas. Within this assembly, there are seven points: first, marking their own outward mark; second, marking their own number; third, marking their own rank; fourth, marking their own fruit; fifth, marking their own virtue; sixth, specifically making plain Mañjuśrī's own lofty rank of virtue; and seventh, a general summary.

And further, these bodhisattvas are complete with measureless practices and vows, abiding securely in every dharma of merit, roaming through the ten directions, carrying out skillful means. Entering the very treasury of the Buddha-dharma, they reach its ultimate shore. Throughout measureless worlds, they bring beings to equal enlightenment; their own light and radiance shines forth, universally illuminating the ten directions. Measureless buddha-lands shake in all six ways. According with each occasion, they open and reveal the teaching, turning the wheel of the Dharma, striking the drum of the Dharma, wielding the sword of the Dharma, sounding the thunder of the Dharma, raining down the rain of the Dharma, and bestowing the gift of the Dharma — constantly using the sound of the Dharma to awaken every world. They tear apart the net of wrong views, dissolve every mistaken opinion, scatter every worldly toil, and destroy every trench of desire. Making plain what is pure and undefiled, their light fuses with the Buddha-dharma itself, spreading and proclaiming its true transformation, grieving for the sake of every suffering being. Never once given to arrogance or self-indulgence, they attain the Dharma of perfect equality, complete with measureless hundreds and thousands of samādhis. In the space of a single thought, there is nowhere they do not reach. They bear the burden of every kind of being, loving them as their own children. Every wholesome root is brought, through them, to the further shore. They have all, without exception, attained the measureless merit of every buddha, their wisdom open and clear beyond all conception. Though this has unfolded across seven distinct points, this concludes the explanation of the assembly of bodhisattvas.

Though the above has unfolded across these two distinct assemblies, this concludes the full explanation of the preface before the teaching begins.

A note on this description

Shandao lets the bodhisattva assembly's own description run as a single, unbroken sweep of martial and natural imagery — a war-drum, a sword, thunder, rain — applied entirely to teaching rather than conflict: the Dharma is "struck," "wielded," and "sounded," but the net torn apart is a net of wrong views, and the trench destroyed is a trench of desire. This militant vocabulary for an entirely peaceable activity is common enough in Mahāyāna literature that Shandao does not pause to explain it, but its cumulative effect here is to close "the preface before the teaching begins" on a note of scale: after the roll call of former heterodox teachers now devoted entirely to the Buddha, an assembly capable of shaking measureless buddha-lands in six ways is what has gathered to hear a queen's private grief. The sūtra's actual drama — a son's rebellion, a mother's terror, a single imprisoned woman's request — is about to unfold in front of an audience this vast.
