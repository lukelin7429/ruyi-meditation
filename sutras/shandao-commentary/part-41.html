Nets, Palaces, and Countless Youths

Above the trees themselves, an entirely populated world hangs suspended: nets of pearl in many layers, palaces filled with youths, each adorned in their own strings of pearls whose light outshines every other color. Below, flowers and fruit unfold by a logic distinctly not of this world — never emerging from inside the tree at all.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1753, 觀無量壽佛經疏, by Shandao (善導, 613–681) Fascicle 3 of 4: 定善義 (Meaning of the Contemplative Good)

A Net of Pearls, Palaces, and Their Youths

Chinese Source

五、從妙真珠網下，至色中上者已來，正明樹上空裏莊嚴相。即有其七。一明珠網臨空覆樹。二明網有多重。三明宮殿多少。四明一一宮內多諸童子。五明童子身服珠瓔珞。六明瓔珞光照遠近。七明光超上色。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, from "a wondrous net of pearls" down through "foremost among all colors," this makes plain the adornment filling the sky above these trees. There are seven points within it. First, this makes plain that a net of pearls hangs suspended in the sky, covering the trees below. Second, this makes plain that this net has many layers. Third, this makes plain the number of palaces contained within. Fourth, this makes plain that within each and every palace are many youths. Fifth, this makes plain that these youths' own bodies are adorned with strings of pearls. Sixth, this makes plain the reach, both near and far, of the light cast by these very pearl-strings. Seventh, this makes plain that this light surpasses every other color, foremost among them all.

Fruit That Never Emerges From Within

Chinese Source

六、從此諸寶林下，至有七寶果已來，明其林樹雖多而無雜亂。華實開時，不從內出，斯乃法藏因深，致使自然而有。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth, from "these many jeweled groves" down through "bearing fruits of seven jewels," this makes plain that, though these groves and trees are many, they never once fall into confusion. When their flowers and fruit unfold, they do not emerge from within the tree itself — for this comes about through the very depth of the causal ground laid down within the Treasury of the Dharma, and thus arises entirely of its own accord.

A note on this order

Shandao's brief note that these countless groves and trees "never once fall into confusion" despite their sheer number is worth pausing on, given how much of this contemplation's own content is devoted to describing variation and abundance — different jewels, different colors, different combinations at every scale. Order here is not the absence of variety but a structural fact underlying it: profusion without chaos. The claim that fruit does not emerge "from within the tree itself" but arises directly from "the very depth of the causal ground" makes the same point about origin already established for the trees as a whole — nothing here grows in the ordinary biological sense; everything simply manifests, complete, as a direct expression of Amitābha's own accumulated vow and practice.

Leaves of Perfectly Uniform Measure

Chinese Source

七、從一一樹葉下，至婉轉葉間已來，正明華葉色相不同。即有其五。一明葉量大小等無差別。二明葉出光色多少。三明恐疑不識借喻以顯，如天瓔珞。四明葉有妙華，色比天金，相喻火輪。五明迭相顯照，婉轉葉間。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, from "each and every leaf of these trees" down through "weaving in and out among the leaves," this makes plain the differing colors and forms of these flowers and leaves. There are five points within it. First, this makes plain that these leaves are entirely uniform in their own size and measure, without the slightest difference. Second, this makes plain the number of colors of light these leaves emit. Third, this makes plain that, fearing beings might fail to recognize what is meant, the text borrows an analogy to make it plain, comparing them to heavenly strings of pearls. Fourth, this makes plain that these leaves bear wondrous flowers, whose color rivals heavenly gold, their form comparable to a wheel of fire. Fifth, this makes plain that these, in turn, illuminate one another, weaving in and out among the leaves.
