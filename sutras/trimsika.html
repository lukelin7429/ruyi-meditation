唯識三十論頌 · Triṃśikā · Vasubandhu, tr. Xuánzàng

Thirty Verses on Consciousness-Only

In thirty terse verses — barely more than a table of contents — Vasubandhu lays out the entire architecture of Consciousness-Only: the three transformations of consciousness that manufacture the appearance of a self and a world, the fifty-one mental factors that accompany them, the three natures and three naturelessnesses that explain how something can be both real and empty, and the five stages by which a bodhisattva actually awakens out of this whole structure. Vasubandhu offers almost no argument of his own here; the verses simply state the doctrine, verse by verse, leaving the case for it to be made elsewhere. That case is exactly what ten Indian masters supplied a generation later, and what Xuánzàng synthesized into the Cheng Weishi Lun, already translated in full on this site across ten fascicles — every one of these thirty verses is quoted there, verbatim, before being unpacked at whatever length its content demands. This page exists to let the skeleton be read on its own, the way a student would first encounter it, before turning to the flesh.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete Source: Taishō T31, no. 1586, 唯識三十論頌 (Vasubandhu, tr. Xuánzàng) Companion text: the Cheng Weishi Lun, ten Indian commentaries synthesized around these same thirty verses

How the Thirty Verses Are Organized

The treatise's own preface states its structure before a single verse is given: of the thirty verses, the first twenty-four and a half explain the characteristics of Consciousness-Only, the next single verse explains its nature, and the last five explain the stages of the path into it. This threefold division — characteristics, nature, path — is the map this whole page follows.

唯識三十論頌

世親菩薩造

大唐三藏法師玄奘奉　詔譯

Triṃśikā-vijñaptimātratāsiddhi — Thirty Verses Establishing Consciousness-Only

Composed by the bodhisattva Vasubandhu

Translated by imperial edict by the Tripiṭaka Master Xuánzàng of the great Táng

護法等菩薩約此三十頌造《成唯識》，今略標所以。謂此三十頌中，初二十四行頌明唯識相；次一行頌明唯識性；後五行頌明唯識行位。就二十四行頌中，初一行半略辯唯識相；次二十二行半廣辯唯識相。謂外問言：「若唯有識，云何世間及諸聖教說有我法？」舉頌詶答，頌曰：

Dharmapāla and the other bodhisattvas composed the Cheng Weishi Lun from these thirty verses; here only the reason for that undertaking is briefly indicated. Of these thirty verses: the first twenty-four and a half explain the characteristics of Consciousness-Only; the next single verse explains its nature; the last five explain the stages of the path into Consciousness-Only. Within the twenty-four and a half verses on characteristics, the first verse and a half briefly state them, and the remaining twenty-two and a half explain them at length. An outsider raises this objection: "If there is only consciousness, how can the world, and the sacred teachings themselves, speak of a self and of dharmas?" The verse is offered in reply:

Chinese Source

由假說我法，　有種種相轉。

彼依識所變，　此能變唯三：

謂異熟、思量，　及了別境識。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because self and dharmas are only provisionally spoken of,
all their various characteristics arise.

These depend on what consciousness transforms —
and this capacity for transformation is of only three kinds:

the ripening consciousness, the deliberating consciousness,
and the consciousnesses that discriminate objects.

Reading note: Everything the treatise will spend twenty-nine more verses unpacking is already compressed into this opening move: a self and dharmas are not simply illusions with nothing behind them, nor are they simply real — they are provisional labels applied to what consciousness itself transforms into. "Transformation" (轉變, pariṇāma) is the treatise's central technical term, and this verse names its three kinds at once, in the order the treatise will address them: the ripening consciousness (the ālayavijñāna, the eighth), the deliberating consciousness (manas, the seventh), and the six consciousnesses that discriminate sense-objects. Each gets its own section below, expanded from the Chinese as it is expanded in the Cheng Weishi Lun across fascicles 2 through 7 — this page states in one line what that commentary spends six fascicles proving.

The First Transformation: The Ripening Consciousness

Having named the three transformations, the treatise now addresses each in turn, starting with the deepest and least accessible to ordinary introspection: the ālayavijñāna, the storehouse consciousness that holds every seed and ripens every act of karma into its result.

次二十二行半廣辯唯識相者，由前頌文略標三能變，今廣明三變相。且初能變其相云何？頌曰：

As for the remaining twenty-two and a half verses that explain the characteristics of Consciousness-Only at length: the preceding verse briefly named the three transformations, and now their characteristics are explained in full. What, then, is the character of the first transformation? The verse states:

Chinese Source

初阿賴耶識，　異熟一切種，

不可知執受、處了，常與觸、

作意、受、想、思，　相應唯捨受。

是無覆無記，　觸等亦如是，

恒轉如瀑流，　阿羅漢位捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first is the ālayavijñāna —
ripening, holding all seeds.

What it grasps and sustains, and its own domain, are unknowable;
it is constantly joined with contact, attention,

feeling, perception, and volition,
and only ever with neutral feeling.

It is unobstructed and morally indeterminate;
contact and the rest that accompany it are likewise so.

It flows on constantly, like a rushing torrent,
and is relinquished at the stage of the arhat.

Reading note: This is one continuous flow of thirty Chinese characters describing eight distinct properties of the ālayavijñāna, and the Cheng Weishi Lun devotes the whole of its second fascicle and much of its third to establishing each one against rival Abhidharma schools who denied there was any eighth consciousness at all: that it ripens karma (異熟), that it holds every seed without loss, that its object and its mode of grasping the body are too subtle to be known by ordinary reflection, that it is accompanied only by the five most basic mental factors and only ever with neutral feeling (since a ripening result cannot itself be virtuous or unvirtuous), that it is morally neutral in a way that never obstructs the path, and that it flows on "like a rushing torrent" — neither a fixed, permanent self nor a series of unconnected moments, but a genuine continuity that is nonetheless always changing. See the Cheng Weishi Lun's fascicle 2 for the case that this consciousness must exist at all, and fascicle 3 for the argument, from this same "torrent" image, that it is the consciousness a arhat's final relinquishing of the afflictions actually acts upon.

The Second Transformation: Manas

The second transformation is the treatise's account of where the raw, prereflective sense of "I" actually comes from — not a philosophical conclusion anyone reasons their way into, but a seventh consciousness operating continuously and invisibly beneath ordinary awareness, mistaking the ālayavijñāna itself for a self.

已說初能變，第二能變其相云何？頌曰：

Having now explained the first transformation, what is the character of the second transformation? The verse states:

Chinese Source

次第二能變，　是識名末那，

依彼轉緣彼，　思量為性相。

四煩惱常俱，　謂我癡、我見，

并我慢、我愛，　及餘觸等俱。

有覆無記攝，　隨所生所繫，

阿羅漢滅定，　出世道無有。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the second transformation is the consciousness called manas.
It turns in dependence on that first consciousness and takes it as its object;

deliberation is its nature and characteristic.
Four afflictions constantly accompany it:

self-delusion and self-view,
together with self-conceit and self-love,

and other factors such as contact besides.
It belongs to the covered-yet-neutral moral category,

and is bound according to wherever it is born.
It is absent in arhats, in cessation-attainment,

and on the supramundane path.

Reading note: "Deliberation" (思量) is a deliberate pun the Chinese preserves from the Sanskrit manas itself — this consciousness is named for exactly the activity it performs, ceaselessly and without a break, of taking the ālayavijñāna as an object and mistaking it for "I." The four afflictions it is never without — self-delusion (not knowing there is no real self), self-view (grasping at one), self-conceit (comparing that self to others), and self-love (craving for its continuation) — are the root of every other affliction downstream, since every subsequent moment of anger, greed, or pride ultimately serves this one continuous, unnoticed act of self-grasping. That manas is absent precisely in an arhat, in the attainment of cessation, and on the supramundane path is the treatise's own definition of what liberation actually removes: not thoughts about a self, but the machinery that manufactures the sense of one. The Cheng Weishi Lun devotes the whole of fascicle 4 and the opening of fascicle 5 to manas, including the case for its existence against schools that recognized only six consciousnesses. Vasubandhu's own earlier Viṃśatikā never needs to name manas at all, since its whole argument runs at the coarser level of the six sense-consciousnesses — a useful measure of how much further this later, mature treatise reaches.

The Third Transformation, and Its Fifty-One Mental Factors

The third and final transformation is the six familiar consciousnesses — the five senses and the mental consciousness that thinks about their objects. Here the treatise pauses longest, since a full accounting of the third transformation means cataloguing every one of the fifty-one mental factors Yogācāra recognizes, sorted into six groups by how each one arises and what it does.

如是已說第二能變，第三能變其相云何？頌曰：

Having thus explained the second transformation, what is the character of the third transformation? The verse states:

Chinese Source

次第三能變，　差別有六種，

了境為性相，　善不善俱非。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the third transformation has, in its distinctions, six kinds;
discerning objects is its nature and characteristic;

it is wholesome, unwholesome, or neither.

此心所遍行、別境、善、煩惱、隨煩惱、不定，皆三受相應。

初遍行觸等，次別境謂欲、勝解、念、定、慧，所緣事不同。

善謂信、慚、愧、無貪等三根，勤、安、不放逸、行捨及不害。

煩惱謂貪、瞋、癡、慢、疑、惡見。

隨煩惱謂忿、恨、覆、惱、嫉、慳、誑、諂與害、憍、無慚及無愧、掉舉與惛沈、不信并懈怠、放逸及失念、散亂、不正知。

不定謂悔、眠，尋、伺二各二。

These mental factors — universal, particular, wholesome,
afflictive, secondary-afflictive, and indeterminate —

are all associated with the three feelings.
The first, universal factors, are contact and the rest;

next, the particular factors are aspiration, resolve,
recollection, concentration, and wisdom — their objects each differ.

The wholesome factors are: faith, sense of shame, sense of embarrassment,
the three wholesome roots beginning with non-greed,

diligence, pliancy, heedfulness, equanimity, and non-harming.
The afflictions are: craving, hatred, delusion, conceit, doubt, and wrong view.

The secondary afflictions are: wrath, resentment, concealment, vexation,
envy, stinginess, deceit, flattery, harmfulness, and arrogance;

lack of shame and lack of embarrassment; restlessness and torpor;
lack of faith and laziness; heedlessness and forgetfulness;

distraction and incorrect knowledge.
The indeterminates are regret and sleep, and coarse and subtle discursive thought — two, each two.

Reading note: These six verses are, character for character, the densest stretch of the entire treatise: fifty-one names and nothing else, no definitions, no examples, no argument for why the list has exactly this shape rather than some other. That silence is itself informative — Vasubandhu is assuming a reader who already has this taxonomy memorized from Abhidharma training, and is here only fixing which mental factors accompany the third transformation specifically (all fifty-one, as against the ālayavijñāna's five and manas's nine). The Cheng Weishi Lun spends fascicles 5 through 7 defining every single one of these terms in turn, with etymology, function, and the specific afflictions or virtues each one counteracts or supports — see fascicle 5 for the universal and particular factors, fascicle 6 for the wholesome factors and root afflictions, and fascicle 7 for the secondary afflictions and indeterminates. Readers who want the same fifty-one dharmas defined at three different depths, independent of this treatise's own compression, can turn to The Hundred Dharmas elsewhere on this site, which was built exactly for that purpose.

When the Six Consciousnesses Arise

Having listed what accompanies the third transformation, the treatise turns to a more practical question: the six consciousnesses are not all active at every moment, so under what conditions does each one actually arise, and when does it fall silent?

已說六識心所相應，云何應知現起分位？頌曰：

Having now explained the six consciousnesses and the mental factors associated with them, how should their actual moments of arising be understood? The verse states:

Chinese Source

依止根本識，　五識隨緣現，

或俱或不俱，　如濤波依水。

意識常現起，　除生無想天，

及無心二定，　睡眠與悶絕。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on the root consciousness,
the five sense-consciousnesses arise according to conditions —

together, or not together —
like waves resting upon water.

The mental consciousness constantly arises,
except when born in the heaven of no-conception,

in the two mindless concentrations,
in sleep, and in fainting.

Reading note: The "wave upon water" image sets up a precise dependency: the five sense-consciousnesses need not only their own specific conditions (a functioning eye and available light, for instance) but also the mental consciousness working alongside them, so any one of them, several together, or none at all may be active at a given moment, exactly as waves rise and fall on a water that itself persists underneath. The mental consciousness is by contrast almost always running — the four listed exceptions (rebirth in the heaven of no-conception, the two "mindless" meditative absorptions, dreamless sleep, and fainting) are the treatise's own exhaustive catalogue of every state in which it genuinely stops, discussed at length in the Cheng Weishi Lun's fascicle 7, which also takes up the further question these four states raise: if even the mental consciousness has stopped, what keeps a person who has merely fainted, rather than died, from actually dying?

Only Consciousness: Transformation, Discrimination, and the Chain of Rebirth

With all three transformations now described individually, the treatise turns to the claim they were introduced to support in the first place: that a self and dharmas, provisionally spoken of in dependence on what consciousness transforms into, have no separate reality of their own — everything, without remainder, is Consciousness-Only.

已廣分別三能變相為自所變二分所依，云何應知依識所變假說我、法非別實有，由斯一切唯有識耶？頌曰：

Having now distinguished at length the character of the three transformations — each serving as the basis for the two divisions it itself transforms into — how should it be understood that a self and dharmas, provisionally spoken of in dependence on what consciousness transforms, have no separate real existence, and that everything, on this basis, is Consciousness-Only alone? The verse states:

Chinese Source

是諸識轉變，　分別、所分別，

由此彼皆無，　故一切唯識。

Ru-Yi Translation

These consciousnesses undergo transformation
into what discriminates and what is discriminated;

because of this, both are without real, independent existence —
hence, everything is Consciousness-Only.

若唯有識，都無外緣，由何而生種種分別？頌曰：

If there is only consciousness, and no external condition whatsoever, then through what does the manifold variety of discrimination arise? The verse states:

由一切種識，　如是如是變，

以展轉力故，　彼彼分別生。

It is because consciousness possesses every kind of seed
that it transforms in this way and that;

through the power of mutual interaction,
this or that discrimination is thereby generated.

雖有內識而無外緣，由何有情生死相續？頌曰：

Although there is an inner consciousness but no external condition, through what, then, do sentient beings' birth and death continue in succession? The verse states:

由諸業習氣、　二取習氣俱，

前異熟既盡，　復生餘異熟。

It is through the habituation of karma,
together with the habituation of the two graspings,

that once the former ripening is exhausted,
a further ripening is again produced.

Reading note: These three verses answer, in sequence, the three objections any careful reader should already be forming. First: is "Consciousness-Only" simply a claim that nothing exists? No — every consciousness transforms into a perceiving aspect (分別, "the discriminating") and a perceived aspect (所分別, "the discriminated"), and it is the mistaken belief that the second half is something separate and external that is denied, not the arising of experience itself. Second: without an external world, why does experience show so much variety rather than a single undifferentiated blank? Because the ālayavijñāna holds "every kind of seed," and it is these seeds' own reciprocal interaction — habituation feeding back into habituation, across every consciousness in the system — that generates the specific texture of each moment's discrimination, developed at length as the doctrine of "mutual perfuming" in fascicle 2. Third: without an external world to die in or be reborn into, what actually keeps rebirth going? Two habituations working together: the habituation of karma (which supplies a new life's specific shape and duration) and the habituation of the two graspings — subject and object, discussed in fascicle 8 — which supplies the raw craving for continued existence that karma alone could never generate on its own. Vasubandhu's own Viṃśatikā answers a version of this same third objection through the case of hell and its hell-guardians, entirely without needing the ālayavijñāna machinery this later treatise now supplies directly.

The Three Natures

Having established that everything is Consciousness-Only, the treatise addresses an objection any well-read listener would raise at once: the sūtras themselves repeatedly speak of three natures. Are these, too, simply another name for something already reducible to consciousness?

若唯有識，何故世尊處處經中說有三性？應知三性亦不離識，所以者何？頌曰：

If there is only consciousness, why does the World-Honored One, in sūtra after sūtra, speak of there being three natures? It should be understood that the three natures, too, are not separate from consciousness. Why is this? The verse states:

Chinese Source

由彼彼遍計，　遍計種種物，

此遍計所執，　自性無所有。

依他起自性，　分別緣所生。

圓成實於彼，　常遠離前性。

故此與依他，　非異非不異，

如無常等性，　非不見此彼。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through this and that universal discrimination,
one universally discriminates all manner of things;

this discriminated, attached-to nature
has no essence of its own whatsoever.

The dependent nature is that
which discrimination, arising from conditions, produces.

The perfectly accomplished nature is what, within that dependent nature,
is forever free of the former, discriminated, attached-to nature.

Hence this and the dependent nature
are neither different nor not different —

like impermanence and similar characteristics,
nor is it that seeing one precludes seeing the other.

Reading note: Each of the three natures picks out the same field of experience under a different aspect, not three separate layers of reality stacked on top of one another. The discriminated, attached-to nature (遍計所執) is what our habitual overlay of fixed, independent "things" adds on top of experience — and it alone, of the three, is flatly unreal, "no essence of its own whatsoever." The dependent nature (依他起) is the actual arising, moment by moment, of dependently originated appearances — real, in the sense that it genuinely occurs, but never as the fixed things the first nature imagines it to be. The perfectly accomplished nature (圓成實) is simply the dependent nature seen correctly, once the false overlay has been permanently removed — which is why the verse insists it is "neither different nor not different" from the dependent nature: two ways of relating to a single field, not two different fields. This whole doctrine is developed at book length in Cheng Weishi Lun fascicle 8, and treated independently, with its own emphasis, in the Mahāyānasaṃgraha and the Madhyāntavibhāga — comparing all three accounts side by side is one of the more rewarding ways to read this site's Yogācāra corpus.

The Three Naturelessnesses

A further, sharper objection follows immediately from the last: if the three natures are genuinely established, how can the Buddha elsewhere say, just as flatly, that all dharmas are without any nature whatsoever? The treatise's answer is that "naturelessness" was never meant as the final word — it is a corrective aimed at a specific misunderstanding, spoken with hidden intent.

若有三性，如何世尊說一切法皆無自性？頌曰：

If there are three natures, how can the World-Honored One say that all dharmas are entirely without their own nature? The verse states:

Chinese Source

即依此三性，　立彼三無性，

故佛密意說，　一切法無性。

初即相無性，　次無自然性，

後由遠離前，　所執我、法性。

此諸法勝義，　亦即是真如，

常如其性故，　即唯識實性。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is precisely with reference to these three natures
that those three naturelessnesses are established;

hence the Buddha, with hidden intent,
said that all dharmas are without their own nature.

The first is precisely naturelessness of characteristic;
the next is the absence of any nature of spontaneous arising;

the last, through being forever free of the former,
[is free of] the attached-to nature of a self and of dharmas.

This, the dharmas' ultimate, is likewise suchness itself,
since it is permanently as its own nature is —

this is Consciousness-Only's genuine nature.

Reading note: Each naturelessness targets its corresponding nature in a logically distinct way, and this second verse pairs them off exactly as the first three-natures verse did: naturelessness of characteristic (相無性) corresponds to the discriminated, attached-to nature, since that nature simply has no characteristic to speak of at all; naturelessness of arising (生無性) corresponds to the dependent nature, since it lacks the kind of independent, self-caused arising naive attachment imagines, though it certainly arises through real conditions; and naturelessness in the ultimate sense (勝義無性) corresponds to the perfectly accomplished nature, which is called "natureless" only in the sense of being free from the first nature's false overlay, never in the sense of being nothing. The treatise's closing move in this section is its boldest identification: Consciousness-Only's own genuine nature simply is suchness (真如), the same ultimate reality named, in other texts on this site, the dharma-realm or emptiness itself. Note that this middle verse — on the three naturelessnesses — is the one place in the entire treatise where the Cheng Weishi Lun does not quote a formal root-verse couplet, instead folding its content directly into prose commentary; the translation above follows that same established vocabulary. See Cheng Weishi Lun fascicle 9 for the full argument, including the fourfold analysis of "ultimate truth" that explains exactly which of its four senses is meant here.

The Five Stages of the Path

With Consciousness-Only's characteristics and nature now fully established, the treatise's last five verses turn to genuinely new ground: not what is true, but how a bodhisattva actually comes to realize it, structured into five sequential stages from the first arousal of the resolve for awakening to buddhahood itself.

後五行頌明唯識行位者，論曰：「如是所成唯識性、相，誰依幾位？如何悟入？」謂具大乘二種種性：一、本性種性，謂無始來依附本識，法爾所得無漏法因；二、謂習所成種性，謂聞法界等流法已，聞所成等熏習所成。具此二性方能悟入。何謂五位？一、資糧位，謂修大乘順解脫分，依識性、相能深信解。其相云何？頌曰：

As for the last five verses, which explain the stages on the path into Consciousness-Only: the treatise itself asks, "Given that Consciousness-Only's nature and characteristics have thus been established, who relies on them, and at how many stages, and how does one awaken into them?" One who possesses the Mahāyāna's two kinds of spiritual lineage is able to do so: first, the innately abiding lineage, meaning the uncontaminated dharma-cause, naturally possessed since beginningless time, depending upon and adhering to the root consciousness; second, the lineage accomplished through cultivation, meaning what is brought about through habituation from hearing the dharma-realm's outflow teaching and the wisdom born from such hearing. Only one who possesses both of these lineages is capable of awakening into it. What are the five stages? First, the stage of provisioning, meaning the cultivation of the Mahāyāna's portion conducive to liberation, relying on which one is able to deeply believe in and understand Consciousness-Only's nature and characteristics. What is its character? The verse states:

The Stage of Provisioning 資糧位

Chinese Source

乃至未起識，　求住唯識性，

於二取隨眠，　猶未能伏滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Until one has not yet given rise to the consciousness
that seeks to abide in Consciousness-Only's nature,

one is still not yet able to suppress and eliminate
the underlying tendency of the two graspings.

The Stage of Applied Practice 加行位

二、加行位，謂修大乘順決擇分，在加行位能漸伏除所取、能取。其相云何？

Second, the stage of applied practice, meaning the cultivation of the Mahāyāna's portion conducive to decisive discernment; at the stage of applied practice, one is gradually able to suppress and remove the grasped object and the grasping subject. What is its character?

Chinese Source

現前立少物，　謂是唯識性，

以有所得故，　非實住唯識。

Ru-Yi Translation

Positing something as presently manifest,
calling it Consciousness-Only's own nature —

since this still involves something attained,
one is not yet genuinely abiding in Consciousness-Only.

The Stage of Realization 通達位

三、通達位，謂諸菩薩所住見道，在通達位如實通達。其相云何？

Third, the stage of realization, meaning the path of seeing upon which bodhisattvas abide; at the stage of realization, one genuinely and truly penetrates it. What is its character?

Chinese Source

若時於所緣，　智都無所得，

爾時住唯識，　離二取相故。

Ru-Yi Translation

When, with respect to the object taken,
wisdom has nothing at all attained —

at that time, one abides in Consciousness-Only,
free of the characteristics of both graspings.

The Stage of Cultivation 修習位

四、修習位，謂諸菩薩所住修道，修習位中如實見理，數數修習。其相云何？

Fourth, the stage of cultivation, meaning the path of cultivation upon which bodhisattvas abide; at the stage of cultivation, in accordance with the principle already genuinely seen, one cultivates it again and again. What is its character?

Chinese Source

無得不思議，　是出世間智，

捨二麁重故，　便證得轉依。

Ru-Yi Translation

Without attainment and inconceivable,
this is supramundane wisdom;

through relinquishing the two kinds of coarseness and heaviness,
one thereby realizes the transformation of the basis.

The Ultimate Stage 究竟位

五、究竟位，謂住無上正等菩提，出障圓明，能盡未來化有情類。其相云何？

Fifth, the ultimate stage, meaning abiding in unsurpassed, perfect awakening, free of every obstruction and perfectly luminous, capable, throughout all future time, of transforming sentient beings. What is its character?

Chinese Source

此即無漏界，　不思議善常，

安樂解脫身，　大牟尼名法。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is precisely the uncontaminated realm,
inconceivable, wholesome, and constant;

the body of bliss and liberation —
called the dharma in the great sage.

Reading note: The five stages trace a single continuous arc from confident belief to complete realization, and the treatise's own wording marks the hinge precisely: the stage of applied practice is explicitly still "positing something as presently manifest" and "involves something attained" — real spiritual attainment, but still operating through the very subject-object structure it is trying to see through. Only at the stage of realization does wisdom have "nothing at all attained," the treatise's own definition of a genuine, aspect-free breakthrough, elaborated as the path of seeing in Cheng Weishi Lun fascicle 9. "The transformation of the basis" (轉依), reached at the stage of cultivation, is this whole treatise's technical name for what ordinary language calls awakening: not the destruction of the ālayavijñāna, but its function turned over from generating affliction to generating wisdom — worked out across six distinct kinds of transformation in fascicle 10, which also closes both that fascicle and the entire ten-volume Cheng Weishi Lun with this same closing verse.

That the treatise's very last line names the Buddha "the great sage" (大牟尼) rather than by any more elaborate title is itself worth pausing on: after twenty-nine verses of the most technical vocabulary in the entire Yogācāra corpus, the destination the whole system points toward is described in the plainest possible terms — a body of bliss and liberation, wholesome and constant, uncontaminated by any of the afflictions this treatise has spent its length cataloguing. The treatise ends where it began, with "hidden intent" now made fully explicit: everything provisionally spoken of as self and dharmas across thirty verses turns out to have been, all along, a path toward exactly this.

Why This Page Exists Alongside the Cheng Weishi Lun

No independent classical commentary on the Triṃśikā alone survives in Chinese translation — Xuánzàng's own project was precisely to synthesize ten separate Indian commentaries on these thirty verses into the single, continuous Cheng Weishi Lun, and it is that synthesis, not any one master's individual gloss, that Chinese Buddhism has read for fourteen centuries since. The reading notes on this page are accordingly Ru-Yi Meditation Center's own, written to introduce a first-time reader to the verses' bare structure before they meet that far longer exposition — every technical term above is kept identical to its rendering across the ten fascicles of the Cheng Weishi Lun, so that moving from this page to that one, or back again, involves no change in vocabulary at all.
