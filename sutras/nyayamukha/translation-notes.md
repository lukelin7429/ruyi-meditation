# Nyāyamukha Translation Notes

These notes record terminology decisions for the Ru-Yi English translation of the *Nyāyamukha* (因明正理門論, *Yin Ming Zheng Li Men Lun*, "The Gate of Logic," Taishō T32, no. 1628), composed by Dignāga (陳那, styled 大域龍 in the Chinese sources) and translated into Chinese by Xuanzang.

This page shares its core vocabulary with the companion page, the *[Nyāyapraveśa](../nyayapravesa/translation-notes.md)* (因明入正理論) — Śaṅkarasvāmin's own introductory digest of this very treatise. All terms already fixed there (宗/因/喻, 同品/異品, 極成, 現量/比量, 能立/能破, 似能立/似能破, 九句因, etc.) carry over unchanged; this file only adds terms that are new to, or used more technically in, the *Nyāyamukha* itself.

## Source and independence from prior translations

- Base text: Taishō T1628, via the CBETA digital edition (public domain). We translate from this Chinese text directly.
- This is one of the hardest texts in the Buddhist logical corpus, and English-language Buddhology and Indology scholarship on it exists, some of it under copyright. We do not reproduce or paraphrase such scholarship. Difficult passages may be checked privately against it to confirm we have not misread the Chinese, but the English wording and structure on this page are our own.
- A second Chinese translation exists — Yijing's 因明正理門論本 (Taishō T1629) — which we do not use as the base text, for consistency with the rest of the site's practice of translating from Xuanzang's versions.

## New terminology (beyond the Nyāyapraveśa glossary)

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 生因 / 了因 | the generative cause / the informative cause | A distinction this treatise draws explicitly: a reason (因) does not *produce* its thesis the way a seed produces a sprout (生因); it only *reveals* or makes known a truth already the case (了因, lit. "the cause of coming-to-know"). Foundational to why a reason works through shared knowledge, not causal force |
| 五分 | the five-membered [syllogism] | The older Nyāya form: thesis, reason, example, application (合), and conclusion (結). Dignāga's three-part form (三支) is explicitly presented, in both this treatise and its postface tradition, as a deliberate simplification of the five-part form |
| 合 / 結 | application / conclusion | The fourth and fifth members of the older five-part syllogism, dispensed with in Dignāga's three-part form. The *Nyāyamukha* argues that a properly stated example, including its connective clause, already accomplishes what these two extra members were for |
| 最勝 | Prakṛti (Pradhāna) | The Sāṃkhya school's "primordial nature," a recurring target of critique in Buddhist logical texts. Left untranslated in Sanskrit on first appearance, per this site's convention for proper philosophical terms |
| 為自比量 / 為他比量 | inference for oneself / inference for others | svārthānumāna / parārthānumāna. The *Nyāyamukha* argues at length that these are the same reasoning process, differing only in whether it is carried out silently (for oneself) or stated fully in words (for others, i.e. proof, 能立) |
| 過類 | the sophistical types | jāti — a class of superficially plausible but logically illegitimate objections, adapted here (critically) from the Nyāya school's traditional list of twenty-four jātis. Consistently "the sophistical types" throughout, distinguished from genuine refutation (能破) |
| 相似 (as a suffix) | resemblance-by- | Each of the fifteen named types below is formed as "X相似" — "resembles [refutation] by way of X." Kept as a fixed compound, e.g. 同法相似, "resemblance-by-agreement," to preserve the pattern and let readers recognize the family at a glance |
| 同法相似 | resemblance-by-agreement | Arguing backwards by wrongly treating a heterologous instance as if it belonged to the example by agreement |
| 異法相似 | resemblance-by-difference | The mirror-image error, using the example by difference |
| 分別相似 | resemblance-by-distinction | Objecting via some further, irrelevant property distinguishing the example instance from the subject |
| 無異相似 | resemblance-by-non-difference | Forcing two things that share one property into total identity |
| 可得相似 | resemblance-by-attainability | Objecting that some other reason could equally establish the same conclusion |
| 猶豫相似 | resemblance-by-uncertainty | Manufacturing an ambiguity in the reason's own wording |
| 義准相似 | resemblance-by-implication | Drawing out an unintended, absurd-sounding implication for the heterologous class |
| 至不至相似 | resemblance-by-arrival-or-non-arrival | Objecting that a reason must either collapse into its thesis or never reach it at all |
| 無因相似 | resemblance-by-absence-of-cause | The "three times" objection: a cause supposedly cannot precede, follow, or coincide with its effect |
| 無說相似 / 無生相似 | resemblance-by-non-statement / resemblance-by-non-arising | Objecting that a thesis's property cannot have held true before the reason was ever stated, or before the subject itself existed |
| 所作相似 | resemblance-by-being-produced | Objecting that the specific way an example is "produced" differs from the specific way the subject is |
| 生過相似 | resemblance-by-manufactured-fault | Demanding proof of something already agreed upon by both sides |
| 常住相似 | resemblance-by-permanence | Objecting that impermanence, as a property, must itself permanently inhere in things |
| 足目 | Akṣapāda | The traditional name of the founding sage of the Nyāya school, to whom this treatise attributes most of the sophistical types just listed |

## Register and difficulty

- This treatise is genuinely one of the most difficult passages in the Buddhist canon to render in plain English — Dignāga is writing for readers who already have the *Nyāyapraveśa*'s vocabulary in hand, and he compresses arguments the *Nyāyapraveśa* spreads across whole sections into a few dense clauses, often via quoted mnemonic verses that only make sense once unpacked in prose.
- Where the Chinese is genuinely ambiguous or the subject of ongoing scholarly disagreement (for example, some of the more compressed grammatical justifications for why a given fallacy-type is grammatically gendered, or precise identifications of rival schools referenced only by epithet), the translation says so in a reading note rather than asserting false certainty.
- Plain English, close to the Chinese, not a loose paraphrase — matching the method used throughout the rest of this site. Given the density here, reading notes carry more of the explanatory weight than anywhere else on the site.
