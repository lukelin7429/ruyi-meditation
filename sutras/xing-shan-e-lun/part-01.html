Prefaces, the Confucian Debate, and the Ten Dharma-Realms

On Inherent Good and Evil was written in 1621 by Youxi Chuandeng (1554–1628), the Ming-dynasty restorer of the Tiantai school on Mount Tiantai, as a dialogue between himself and a Confucian guest. This first part contains two prefaces — one by the lay disciple Wang Ligu, one by Chuandeng himself — and the opening of the treatise proper. The guest objects that a teaching of "inherent evil" cannot lead people to goodness. Chuandeng answers first in terms of the worldly teaching: he reviews Confucius, Zisi, Mencius ("human nature is good"), Xunzi ("human nature is evil"), Yang Xiong ("a mixture of good and evil"), Han Yu, and Zhang Zai, and argues that since cultivation has good and evil, the nature that cultivation depends on must include both. He then turns to the world-transcending teaching, running through the Five Periods to show that only the perfect teaching speaks of the nature as including both good and evil, and announces eight "gates" for the rest of the work. The first gate presents unchanging true suchness, in which the ten realms lie hidden; the second shows the ten realms differentiated as true suchness follows conditions, illustrated by Ciyun Zunshi's descriptions of each of the ten dharma-realms and Chuandeng's comments on whether each can be "turned."

Ru-Yi Translation

Inherent evil is something the non-Buddhist classics avoid. Yet the Tiantai school brings to light what the true nature contains and puts it to wondrous use as its special province; this it takes as its family treasure. Unless one is versed in its teaching, even an elderly, seasoned śramaṇa, on hearing of it, would cover his ears and run. When I read Great Master Youmen's On Inherent Good and Evil, I sighed at how truly deep and weighty is the compassion of the bodhisattvas of the four reliances. Why? We sentient beings and the three poisons are like a cluster of akṣa seeds; through lifetimes over vast eons we have dwelt in cultivated evil, and in turning back toward good the hardships and errors are truly many. If restraint is too forceful, one becomes drunk on the wine of quiet meditation; if renunciation is extreme, one falls into the pit of the unconditioned — this is what is called error. Contemplating purity as something far off, so that the initial mind has no share in it; cultivating and realizing step by step, so that it is hard to achieve even over accumulated eons — this is what is called hardship. If we examine the cause, it is truly because evil is [held to be] different from good. Without the dharma-gate of inherent inclusion, which fuses both good and evil, how could one know that the ten realms are equally suchness, or awaken to sentient beings being identical to the Buddha? Everything is already complete, utterly perfect and utterly sudden — what could surpass this? Therefore, for those who discuss buddha-nature, it is not difficult to speak of good; what is difficult is to speak of inclusion.

Ru-Yi Translation

When the treatise was complete and [this preface] was to head it, Confucian scholars frequently used [its] criticism of Mencius to challenge me. I said: Yes, yes — and no, no. When the sages and worthies of the three teachings speak of the nature, they are all the same and yet different. Mencius once spoke of all things being complete [in us]; but complete with good and leaving out evil, how can it be called "all"? The sūtras say everywhere that [beings] originally have become buddhas; why should the Buddha's disciples not say that the nature is good? It is only that worldly teachings are strict in guarding against evil, while world-transcending teachings are wondrous in fusing good; fitting the times and suiting the capacities, each has what is appropriate. Even when Confucians speak of the nature, do they really say it is all good? "Near to one another" and "do not change" — is this not implicitly the one nature including [both]? Xuanni spoke of inclusion; Mencius of Zou spoke of good. But after Mencius of Zou spoke of good, Yaojiang further spoke of neither good nor evil, and the learning of the sages became ever clearer. The Tathāgata spoke of good; Tian[tai] and [Nan]heng spoke of inclusion. But after Tian[tai] and [Nan]heng spoke of inclusion, the patriarchs of Siming, and down to the Great Master, further spoke fully of good and evil, and the Buddha's mind was unfolded ever more freely. The Confucian lineage of Yaojiang is studied and savored without end even today. Now that the Master's treatise has appeared, in the five hundred years to come, what other than this will be called the ford and bridge to seeing the nature? The sages and worthies of the three teachings all prize having worthy descendants! They prize having worthy descendants!

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who speak of one-sided emptiness depart from the way of governing the world; those who take conditioned arising as their tenet are cut off from the road to nirvāṇa. Because of this, the knowable gathers like sand, and the river of views and craving runs deep. Falling into views and craving, one whirls in the waves of birth and death; obstructed by the knowable, one sinks into the pit of the unconditioned. How could one know that in the low, damp mire there always grows the puṇḍarīka that is auspicious for the world, while one finally sighs that the dry land of the high plateau never produces the utpala that blooms in its season? This dharma-gate of wondrous existence is why Śākyamuni Tathāgata, holding up dust, spoke of it again and again, and why the great bodhisattvas beat time and joined in harmony. Why? Once one speaks of its existence, the myriad practices are thereby able to flourish in fragrance, and the guiding transformation of the perfected ones goes forth; once one speaks of its wondrousness, suchness thereby becomes vast and open, and the birth and death of ordinary beings is cut off. Existing and yet wondrous, the wondrous is not wondrous of itself; wondrous and yet existing, existence does not exist of itself. Since existence does not exist of itself, though one practices and delivers [beings] all year long, the one path is pure and conditions are forgotten; since the wondrous is not wondrous of itself, though one dispels feelings all day long, the myriad dharmas stand arrayed and established. Being so, wondrous existence is always identical yet not identical, always separate yet not separate; how could I make the wondrous wondrous on its own, or make existence exist on its own? This is the great conduct and strategy of the buddhas and bodhisattvas, which certainly cannot be conceived or discussed.

Ru-Yi Translation

But existence is not existence through conditions — without inherent inclusion there is no way to establish its tenet; inclusion is not one-sided inclusion — without the ten realms there is no way to exhaust its purport. Cultivation and nature are distinguished from this, and good and evil are discerned through it. Therefore, borrowing the form of host and guest, I have composed a treatise on inherent good and evil. Yet since the path embraces both Confucian and Buddhist, and principle distinguishes the one-sided from the perfect, each has its proper place — how could they be lumped together? The purports of the worldly and the world-transcending must be divided into separate courts, as far apart as heaven and earth; the tenets of the great and the small, the sudden and the gradual, must be set apart like cloud and mud. In addition, cultivation and nature are raised side by side, dharma and simile are set forth again and again, events and persons are adduced, and sūtras and treatises are cited as proof. The gates are arranged in eight sections, expressed as the thought came, hoping only to accord in some measure with the wondrous, awakened, bright nature. I began writing in the middle ten days of the last month of summer in the first year of the Tianqi era of the august Ming, and the book was completed in mid-autumn, on the day the moon begins to shine. Laying down the brush, I therefore write this preface.

Ru-Yi Translation

The nature is principle. As principle, the nature is fundamentally neither good nor evil. When people past and present have set out their theories in terms of good and evil, have they not been relying on cultivation to speak of the nature, borrowing phenomena to name principle — like following the echo to seek the sound, or following the shadow to seek the form? The nature, as principle — how could good and evil suffice to describe it? A guest who wore a Confucian's cap but took the Buddha's mind as his mind passed by my Youxi [hermitage] and asked me: I hear that in your Tiantai teaching, Master, the dharma-gate of inherent evil is always taken as a great point of return. Is this so? I replied: It is. The guest said: To approve the good and hate the evil is the common feeling of humankind. When the sages of the three teachings put forth their words and set up their theories, they honored the worthy and detested the unworthy — not merely as one loves a lovely color, ever fearing to fall short, or as one hates a foul odor, ever fearing there is too much. Now the scholars of the Tiantai school deliberately establish their theory on this; where then is the way of leading beings to goodness and instructing people? I replied: The ultimate principle startles people; the ultimate words frighten the hearer. If one uses the petty instructions of the human world to doubt the deep and profound principle of world-transcendence, it confuses the ear and startles the mind — what is strange about that? Moreover, this school's thesis says only: "Just the one word 'include' makes our present school all the more evident. That the nature includes good, other masters also know; but that the conditioning and revealing [causes] include evil, none of them can fathom." When has it ever spoken definitively of evil and not of good?

Ru-Yi Translation

For when the Tiantai school speaks of the nature, good and evil are both included; only when it speaks of cultivation are good and evil divided. It takes the originally included buddha-realm as inherent good and the originally included nine realms as inherent evil; the buddha-realm accomplished through cultivation as cultivated good, and the nine realms accomplished through cultivation as cultivated evil. Since other schools know only that the nature includes good and do not know that the nature includes evil, the buddha-realm becomes something to grasp and the nine realms something to discard, and they cannot accord with the purport of equality and nonduality in the Vimalakīrti Sūtra: "Birth and death is identical to nirvāṇa; afflictions are identical to bodhi." Therefore [our school] establishes the perfect principle to refute the one-sided tenets. Moreover, it wishes to rescue people of cultivated evil in the nine realms: without needing to turn aside, they reach inherent evil, and "inherent evil fuses and penetrates, so that there is no dharma that does not tend toward it, and it effortlessly takes in the inherent good of the buddha-realm." This is taken as the wondrous gate of "pointing directly at the human mind, seeing the nature and becoming a buddha"; it is precisely the great way of leading beings to goodness and instructing people. That is why those who engage in this teaching put forth their words without fear.

Ru-Yi Translation

The guest said: How is it in the worldly teaching? I said: We observe that the sage Confucius, in his writings, rarely spoke of the nature; hence his disciples said of their teacher: "The Master's cultural displays can be heard; his words on the nature and the Way of Heaven cannot be heard." Yet he did once speak of it a little, saying: "By nature people are near to one another; by practice they grow far apart." And he never decided it as good or evil. Only when it came to the discourse of his grandson Zisi was it spoken of subtly: "What Heaven confers is called the nature; following the nature is called the Way; cultivating the Way is called teaching." He also said: "Before joy, anger, sorrow, and delight have arisen, it is called the mean; when they arise and all hit the proper measure, it is called harmony." The Explanation of the Dharma-Realm Diagram says: Suppose a person draws near wrong and evil friends, or by nature does evil himself, giving rise to a heightened mind, thought after thought in succession, and commits the ten evils of the highest grade — namely killing, stealing, lust, false speech, frivolous speech, divisive speech, harsh abuse, greed, anger, and wrong views — and the five heinous crimes; violates the four grave prohibitions, defiles those of pure conduct, sells liquor and causes drunken disorder; does not think of the kindness of ruler, father, teachers, and elders; wantonly gives rise to burning vexation, suppresses the worthy and able and sides with the unworthy; destroys stūpas and wrecks monasteries, burns and destroys scriptures and images, slanders the Mahāyāna, cuts off the study of prajñā, slanders [by saying] there are no buddhas, breaks the precepts while receiving offerings, uses the property of the Three Jewels, steals the property of the Saṅgha, gives rise to the non-Buddhist views of annihilation and permanence, destroys the correct dharma of cause and effect in the three times, and practices the twelve kinds of evil disciplines (first, butcher; second, executioner; third, raising pigs; fourth, raising chickens; fifth, catching fish; sixth, hunter; seventh, netting birds; eighth, catching snakes; ninth, charming dragons; tenth, prison guard; eleventh, thief; twelfth, catching thieves for the king, together with occupations such as raising silkworms). Beings such as these enter Avīci and the great hells of great heat and great cold. This is called the hell dharma-realm.

Chinese Source

釋曰：地獄法界，約方修與搆作二因論。轉不轉者，亦以一界見起九界，冥伏於一性之中，隨其造力強弱，性不可定。若其念念作此地獄之因，相續不斷，習以成性，則不可轉。或能慚愧改革，一念回心，即是善道。或照破此地獄惡念，為四諦，為十二因緣，為六度，為空、假、中三諦三觀，則轉為出世間眾生心行。因倒因起，轉與不轉，皆不可定。如阿闍世王殺父害母，身瘡腫熱，七日之後，當墮地獄。受耆婆大臣勸來佛所，蒙如來月愛三昧光明所照，頓獲清涼，身瘡痊愈。聞佛說法，懺悔心重，地獄相滅，得無根信。此已成之因，將墮之果，承佛法力，承懺悔力，承大信力，尚能轉地獄界為佛法界，況不滅罪生善界乎？

Ru-Yi Translation

Explanation: The hell dharma-realm is discussed in terms of the two causes, present cultivation and constructing. As for turning or not turning: here too one realm manifestly arises and the nine realms lie hidden within the one nature; according to the strength of its power of creating, the nature cannot be fixed. If thought after thought one creates this cause of hell, continuing without interruption, until practice becomes nature, then it cannot be turned. Or if one can, with shame and remorse, reform and change, turning the mind in a single thought, that is a good path. Or if one illuminates and breaks through this evil thought of hell, [seeing it] as the four truths, as the twelve causes and conditions, as the six perfections, as the threefold truth and threefold contemplation of empty, provisional, and the middle, then it turns into the mind and practice of world-transcending beings. As the cause falls, so the cause arises: turning and not turning cannot be fixed. For example, King Ajātaśatru killed his father and harmed his mother; sores broke out on his body, swollen and hot, and after seven days he was to fall into hell. Urged by the minister Jīvaka, he came to the Buddha; illuminated by the light of the Tathāgata's moon-love samādhi, he at once obtained coolness and the sores on his body healed. Hearing the Buddha teach the Dharma, his repentance was heavy, the marks of hell vanished, and he obtained rootless faith. This was a cause already accomplished and a fruit about to fall; yet relying on the power of the Buddha-dharma, the power of repentance, and the power of great faith, he could still turn the hell realm into the buddha dharma-realm — how much more could he not [reach] a realm of eradicating sins and producing good?

Chinese Source

又如楞嚴經佛告阿難：諸佛如來，語無虗妄。若復有人，身具四重十波羅夷，瞬息即經此方、他方、阿鼻地獄，乃至十方，窮書無間，靡不經歷。能以一念，將此法門，於末劫中，開示未學，是人罪障，應念消滅，變其所受地獄苦因，成安樂國。豈非地獄之因，因弘法力，亦可轉耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Again, as in the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, the Buddha told Ānanda: "The words of the buddhas, the tathāgatas, are not false. If there is a person whose body bears the four grave [offenses] and the ten pārājikas, in an instant he passes through the Avīci hells of this world and other worlds, and so on through the ten directions, to the end of the uninterrupted [hells], none not passed through. If he can, with a single thought, take this dharma-gate and open and show it to those not yet learned in the final eon, this person's sinful obstructions will be eradicated in response to that thought, and the causes of the hellish suffering he was to receive will become a land of peace and bliss." Is this not that the cause of hell can also be turned through the power of propagating the Dharma?

Chinese Source

約果報論定不定者，若尋常所論因果，既入地獄，決定受報，無不定理。若不思謙力、懺悔力，亦可迴其定果，得生善處。如仙豫國王，所殺五百婆羅門外道，竟墮阿鼻地獄，而興三念：一者知是地獄，二者知為仙豫所殺，三者知因邪見之罪，故墮地獄。由此生大慚愧，懺悔往業，即滅地獄，而得生天。故知阿鼻地獄，全處極聖之自心；毗盧身土，不逾下凡之一念。轉之變之，職由乎人。苟有重悔重悟，變地獄為天堂，在頃刻間也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Discussing in terms of retribution whether it is fixed or not fixed: if we speak of cause and effect as ordinarily discussed, once one has entered hell one must receive the retribution; there is no principle of its being unfixed. Yet [one should] not overlook that through the power of humility and the power of repentance one can also reverse a fixed fruit and be born in a good place. Like King Xianyu: the five hundred non-Buddhist brahmins whom he killed did fall into the Avīci hell, but they gave rise to three thoughts: first, they knew it was hell; second, they knew they had been killed by Xianyu; third, they knew they had fallen into hell because of the sin of wrong views. From this they gave rise to great shame and repented their past karma; the hell at once vanished, and they were born in the heavens. Hence we know that the Avīci hell lies wholly within the self-mind of the highest sage, and the body and land of Vairocana do not go beyond the single thought of the lowest ordinary being. Turning it and transforming it depend entirely on the person. If there is heavy repentance and deep awakening, transforming hell into a heavenly hall takes place within an instant.

Reading Notes for Part 1

Translator's notes

Authors and date: Preface A is by Wang Ligu (王立轂), lay name Xiangguang, a Tiantai disciple; "Youmen" (有門) is Chuandeng's courtesy name. Chuandeng's own preface (B) dates the writing to the sixth month of the first year of Tianqi (1621) and its completion to the third day of the eighth month ("the day the moon begins to shine," 哉生明). "Wujin" is Huisi; "Tian[tai] and [Nan]heng" are Zhiyi and Huisi; the Wondrous Purport is Zhili's Miaozong chao; "Yaojiang" is Wang Yangming. The "diagrams" (units 10, 12, 13, 17 …) are not reproduced in the CBETA text; only their titles remain.

"Just the one word 'include'" (units 7, 8): Chuandeng attributes this to Huaize of Huxi; it opens Huaize's Chuan foxin yin ji (T1938), which itself repeats Zhili's Record on the Profound Meaning of Guanyin. The English reuses the round-two draft of Chuan foxin yin ji p1, as does "inherent evil fuses and penetrates … takes in the inherent good of the buddha-realm."

Confucian sources (units 1, 7, 8): Analects 5.13 ("the Master's words on the nature and the Way of Heaven"), 17.2 ("near to one another … far apart"), 17.3 and 6.21 (the highest wisdom and the lowest folly; people of the middle); Doctrine of the Mean chs. 1, 2, 12; Great Learning ch. 6 (loving a lovely color, hating a foul odor); Mencius 7A4 (all things complete in us); Xunzi, "Contra Twelve Philosophers" (Zisi and Meng Ke bringing disorder) and "Human Nature Is Evil"; Yang Xiong, Fayan ("human nature is a mixture of good and evil"); Han Yu, "Yuan xing" (three grades of the nature); Zhang Zai's nature of heaven and earth vs. the nature of material endowment. All translated here; no published site English exists for them.

Su Shi (unit 8): much of the Confucian review — Mencius "making himself a target," Xunzi "obstinate and unyielding," "good and evil are what the nature can do, not what it can have," hunger and cold and the desires of male and female, the Odes and Spring and Autumn — closely follows Su Shi's essays on Zisi, Xun Qing, Yang Xiong, and the Odes. This was not checked against an edition; Chuandeng does not name him. "Mixes it with [some] particular aspect" (雜乎方): Su Shi's essay probably reads 雜乎才 "mixes it with capacity."

Avataṃsaka (unit 8): "It can follow defiled and pure conditions…" is not found in the sūtra (the round-two Chuan foxin yin ji notes locate it as a quoted line in T1885). "The mind is like a skilled painter" is in the sixty-fascicle (Jin) translation, fasc. 10; English from the round-two Guanyin xuanyi ji p2. "If people wish to know fully all the buddhas of the three times" is from the eighty-fascicle translation (T279, fasc. 19), not the Jin translation as stated.

Vimalakīrti and others (unit 8): the lines on lust, anger, and delusion and on the sixty-two views follow the site's Vimalakīrti (chapters "The Goddess" and "The Buddha-Path"). "Birth and death is identical to nirvāṇa; afflictions are identical to bodhi" and "the entrances of the eye, ear, nose, and tongue … are the pure land" are not in T475 (checked); source not located. Wuxing jing: English from the round-two Guanyin yishu p2. Laṅkāvatāra T670 confirmed ("the tathāgatagarbha is the cause of the good and the not-good").

Lotus quotations (unit 8): reuse the site's Lotus Sūtra, "Skillful Means" and "Faith and Understanding." Chuandeng writes 聲聞聞此法 where the sūtra has 菩薩聞是法 ("the bodhisattvas"); the English follows his wording. "One lowering of the head … one scattering of flowers" is a Tiantai summary of the "Skillful Means" verses, not a quotation. The Nirvāṇa line on the icchantika and the good person follows the round-two Chuan foxin yin ji.

Awakening of Faith and Perfect Awakening (unit 9): the Awakening of Faith passage follows the round-two Miaozong chao p1, adjusted for 常住一相 (Chuandeng) where Zhili has 法界一相. The Perfect Awakening passage (T842) is translated here; the text twice has 間 for 聞 ("hearing"). CBETA punctuation attaches 大圓鏡智 to the Awakening of Faith; it belongs to the Śūraṅgama list.

Ciyun's diagram descriptions (units 15–50): the same ten descriptions are quoted in Zhipan's Fozu tongji (T2035, fasc. 50), which reads 以五常立德 "establishes virtue on the five constants" and 慚愧是懷 "keeps shame and remorse in mind" where this text has 無常 "impermanence" and 是壞 (unit 34). The translation follows the text as written.

Stories (units 16–51): Śāriputra's eye — Great Treatise (T1509, fasc. 12); the sentence "retreated in the sixth mind" is not in that passage (source not located). Cutting flesh for a dove is King Śibi (Great Treatise, fasc. 4). Udraka Rāmaputra (unit 31) is usually said to have reached the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception; the text says 無想定. "Demotion through declining virtue" (降德貶墜) is the Śūraṅgama's phrase for asuras (fasc. 9); the four kinds of asuras and the ghosts "with little feeling and much thought" are also Śūraṅgama (fascs. 8–9). Ajātaśatru and the moon-love samādhi follow the site's Nirvāṇa. Pei Du returning the jade belts and Song Qi saving the ants are well-known Chinese tales of reversed fortune.

Apparent copying errors: 囿物為善 (unit 7) read as 誘物 "leading beings"; 歟大 (unit 8) for 歎大 "praised the great"; 化像 for 化儀; 苦通 (unit 15) for 苦道; 割內 for 割肉; 惜未 (unit 8) perhaps for 惜夫; 窮書 (unit 51) for 窮盡; 不惻孤貪 (unit 46) for 孤貧; 見起 (unit 51) for 現起. In unit 8 CBETA opens a parenthesis at 由（廢 and closes it after 亦非是也。）; this is not an interlinear note, and the English does not mark it.

Alternatively (unit 51): 若不思謙力 is translated literally ("the power of humility"); 謙 may be a slip for 議, giving "the inconceivable power [of the Buddha] and the power of repentance." Unit 51 「況不滅罪生善界乎」 is translated as a rhetorical question; the 不 may be intrusive.

Obscure passages in Preface A: 寧盡鳥唧 ("how could it all be birds' chirping?") and 蛣𧏙 ("wriggle like a larva") are rendered tentatively.
