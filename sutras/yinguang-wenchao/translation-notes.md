# Wenchao Jinghualu — Translation Notes

These notes record recurring translation decisions for the Ru-Yi English translation of selected chapters of the *Wenchao Jinghualu* (印光法師文鈔菁華錄), Li Jingtong's 1952 anthology of Master Yinguang's letters, prefaces, and occasional writings. They exist so that terminology stays consistent across the chapters translated so far, and so later chapters can inherit these renderings rather than re-coin them.

## The text and its author

Yinguang (印光, 1862–1940) is regarded as the thirteenth patriarch of Chinese Pure Land Buddhism, though — unusually among the patriarchs — he left behind no single treatise or systematic exposition of his own. His entire literary legacy consists of correspondence: thousands of letters written in reply to lay and monastic disciples across several decades, together with prefaces, epitaphs, and occasional pieces. These were gathered posthumously into the *Wenchao* ("Collected Writings"), published in three successive compilations — the *Zhengbian* (正編, "Correct Compilation," 1927 edition), the *Xubian* (續編, "Continued Compilation," 1939 edition), and the *Sanbian* (三編, "Third Compilation," edited from surviving manuscripts by the lay disciple Luo Hongtao in 1950, after Yinguang's own death). Together these run to well over a million characters, among the most widely circulated bodies of modern Chinese Buddhist prose.

In 1952, the lay disciple Li Jingtong distilled this vast correspondence into the *Jinghualu* ("Record of the Essential Radiance," 印光法師文鈔菁華錄) — 333 selected passages drawn from across all three compilations, organized into ten thematic chapters rather than by date or recipient, with a preface by Yuanying (圓瑛, 1878–1953), whose own commentary on the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* is separately translated on this site as the *Surangama Sutra Jiangyi*.

## Source and copyright

- Source text: the *Wenchao Jinghualu*, as transcribed on Chinese Wikisource, sourced from the 1952 first edition. Yinguang died in 1940 — his own words have been in the public domain for well over eighty years under any jurisdiction's copyright term. Li Jingtong's own editorial selection dates to 1952; the underlying content translated here is, in every case, Yinguang's own original writing, not any of Li Jingtong's own commentary.
- No prior complete English translation of the *Jinghualu* is known to exist. This is an original translation made directly from the Chinese.

## Scope and structure

The *Jinghualu* divides into ten chapters (see the [collection index](./) for the full list). This project translates selected chapters rather than the complete anthology, beginning with three chosen for their relevance to a reader new to Pure Land practice:

1. **Chapter 1** (讚淨土超勝, "Praising the Pure Land's Superiority") — 18 passages, no further subdivision.
2. **Chapter 2** (誡信願真切, "Admonishing True Faith and Sincere Vow") — 34 passages in three groups: 甲 (示真信切願, demonstrating genuine faith and vow), 乙 (勸祛疑生信, dispelling doubt), 丙 (勉具足信願, exhorting complete faith and vow).
3. **Chapter 4** (論生死事大, "Discussing the Magnitude of Life and Death") — 27 passages in three groups: 甲 (警人命無常, warning of impermanence), 乙 (教專仗佛力, teaching exclusive reliance on Buddha-power), 丙 (示臨終切要, instructing deathbed essentials), closing with the standalone "Three Great Essentials at the End of Life" (臨終三大要).

Chapters 3, 5–10 are not yet translated; see the collection index for their contents and scope, listed for reference so a later session can pick them up without re-researching the anthology's structure.

## Citation format

Each passage on this collection's pages carries its own citation line, giving the Chinese title of its source letter or preface, an English rendering of that title, and which of the three compilations it belongs to (正編/續編/三編, shown as a pill). Where the *Jinghualu* itself cites the same source twice within one chapter (for instance, two separate excerpts from the same letter), both citations are given in full rather than abbreviated, since the excerpts do not run consecutively in the original correspondence.

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 自力 / 他力 | self-power / other-power | The central distinction this anthology returns to constantly — cultivated realization through one's own effort versus reliance on Amitābha's own vow-power. | 淨業 | pure karma | The wholesome karma generated by Pure Land practice specifically, distinguished throughout from ordinary merit-making. |

## A note on citation accuracy

Several entries in this anthology quote or closely paraphrase other classical sources — the *Lotus Sūtra*, the *Contemplation Sūtra*, the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*'s closing chapter, and Ouyi Zhixu's own *Amituo Yaojie* among them. Where this site already has an established translation of the underlying passage (for instance, Ouyi's formula on faith, vow, and practice, quoted verbatim by Yinguang in Chapter 2), the translation here is checked against — though not mechanically copied from — the existing site translation, since Yinguang is citing Ouyi's own words as settled doctrine rather than paraphrasing loosely.
