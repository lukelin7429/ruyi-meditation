Admonishing True Faith and Sincere Vow

Thirty-four passages in three groups: demonstrating what genuine faith and vow actually consist of, dispelling the doubts that most commonly keep a reader from committing to the practice, and exhorting a complete, undivided aspiration. This is the chapter where Yinguang is most direct about what separates real faith from its counterfeits — and most patient in walking through objection after objection, one at a time.

所言信者，須信娑婆實實是苦，極樂實實是樂。婆娑之苦，無量無邊，總而言之，不出八苦。所謂生、老、病、死、愛別離、怨憎會、求不得、五陰熾盛。此八種苦，貴極一時，賤至乞丐，各皆有之。前七種是過去世所感之果，諦思自知，不須詳說，說則太費筆墨。第八五陰熾盛苦，乃現在起心動念，及動作云為，乃未來得苦之因。因果牽連，相續不斷；從劫至劫，莫能解脫。五陰者，即色、受、想、行、識也。色，即所感業報之身；受、想、行、識，即觸境所起幻妄之心。由此幻妄身心等法，於六塵境，起惑造業；如火熾然，不能止息，故名熾盛也。又陰者，蓋覆義，音義與蔭同。由此五法，蓋覆真性，不能顯現。如濃雲蔽日，雖杲日光輝，了無所損；而由雲蔽故，不蒙其照。凡夫未斷惑業，被此五法障蔽，性天慧日，不能顯現，亦復如是。此第八苦，乃一切諸苦之本。修道之人，禪定力深，於六塵境界，了無執著，不起憎愛；從此加功用行，進證無生，則惑業淨盡，斬斷生死根本矣。然此工夫，大不容易。末世之中，得者實難。故須專修淨業，求生極樂。仗佛慈力，往生西方。既得往生，則蓮花化生，無有生苦。純童男相，壽等虛空，身無災變；老病死等，名尚不聞，况有其實？追隨聖衆，親侍彌陀，水鳥樹林，皆演法音，隨己根性，由聞而證；親尚了不可得，何况有怨？思衣得衣，思食得食，樓閣堂舍，皆是七寶所成，不假人力，唯是化作；則翻娑婆之七苦，以成七樂。至於身則有大神通，有大威力；不離當處，便能於一念中，普於十方諸佛世界，作諸佛事，上求下化。心則有大智慧，有大辯才；於一法中，徧知諸法實相，隨機說法，無有錯謬；雖說世諦語言，皆契實相妙理。無五陰熾盛之苦，享身心寂滅之樂；故經云，無有衆苦，但受諸樂，故名極樂也。娑婆之苦，苦不可言；極樂之樂，樂莫能喻。深信佛言，了無疑惑，方名真信。切不可以凡夫外道知見，妄生猜度，謂淨土種種不思議勝妙莊嚴，皆屬寓言，譬喻心法，非有實境。若有此種邪知謬見，便失往生淨土實益；其害甚大，不可不知！

Ru-Yi Translation

As for what is meant by "faith": one must believe that the sahā world is truly, genuinely suffering, and that Utmost Bliss is truly, genuinely joy. The suffering of the sahā world is immeasurable and boundless, but, stated in sum, it does not exceed eight kinds: birth, old age, sickness, death, being parted from what one loves, being joined with what one hates, not obtaining what one seeks, and the blazing intensity of the five aggregates. These eight kinds of suffering, from the most exalted down to the humblest beggar, every single person possesses alike. The first seven are effects brought about by past lives; reflect carefully and you will know this for yourself — there is no need to explain them at length, for to do so would waste far too much ink. The eighth, the blazing intensity of the five aggregates, is the cause, arising from the thoughts stirring in one's mind right now and the actions one carries out, of the suffering one will obtain in the future. Cause and effect, bound together, continue on without break, from kalpa to kalpa, with no way to be freed from it. The five aggregates are form, sensation, perception, formation, and consciousness. Form is the body brought about by one's own karmic retribution; sensation, perception, formation, and consciousness are the illusory, false mind that arises upon contact with its objects. Through these illusory, false dharmas of body and mind, one gives rise to affliction and creates karma in relation to the objects of the six sense-fields — like a fire blazing without ever coming to rest, hence it is called "blazing intensity." Moreover, "aggregate" carries the sense of "covering over," its sound and meaning the same as "shade." Through these five dharmas, one's own true nature is covered over and cannot manifest itself — like thick clouds obscuring the sun: though the bright sun's own radiance suffers not the slightest diminishment, because the clouds obscure it, one does not receive its illumination. An ordinary being who has not yet severed affliction and karma, obstructed by these five dharmas, likewise cannot manifest the sun of wisdom in the sky of his own nature. This eighth suffering is the very root of every other suffering. A person cultivating the path, whose power of meditative concentration is deep, holds no attachment at all toward the objects of the six sense-fields, giving rise to neither hatred nor craving; advancing further in effort and practice from this point, realizing the unborn, affliction and karma are then purified and exhausted entirely, and the very root of birth-and-death is cut off. Yet this level of effort is by no means easy — in this final age, one who truly attains it is rare indeed. And so one must devote oneself exclusively to cultivating pure karma, seeking rebirth in Utmost Bliss, relying on the Buddha's own merciful power to be reborn in the West.

Once rebirth is attained, one is born by transformation from a lotus flower, without the suffering of ordinary birth. One takes on, purely, the appearance of a young boy; one's lifespan equals that of empty space itself; one's body undergoes no calamity or change; the very names of old age, sickness, and death are not so much as heard, how much less their actuality? Following the assembly of sages, personally attending upon Amitābha, the waters, birds, and forests all proclaim the sound of the Dharma, and, according to one's own capacity, one realizes it through hearing; even one's own loved ones are, in that place, no longer to be found as such, how much less any enemy? Think of clothing, and clothing arrives; think of food, and food arrives; towers, halls, and dwellings are all formed of the seven treasures, requiring no human labor, manifested purely by transformation — and thus the sahā world's own seven sufferings are each turned, in reverse, into seven joys. As for the body, one possesses great spiritual powers, great majestic authority; without leaving one's own place, one is able, within a single thought, to universally perform the works of every buddha throughout the buddha-worlds of the ten directions, seeking wisdom above and converting beings below. As for the mind, one possesses great wisdom, great eloquence; within a single dharma, one universally knows the true mark of every dharma, teaching according to capacity without ever erring, and though speaking in the language of worldly truth, one's every word already accords with the wondrous principle of the true mark. Free of the suffering of the five aggregates' blazing intensity, one enjoys the joy of body and mind utterly at rest — hence the sūtra says: "having no suffering of any kind, and experiencing only joy — that is why it is called Utmost Bliss." The suffering of the sahā world is suffering beyond all telling; the joy of Utmost Bliss is joy beyond all comparison. To believe the Buddha's own word deeply, with not the slightest doubt remaining, is what may properly be called true faith. One must never, on the basis of the views held by ordinary beings or outside paths, indulge in reckless speculation, claiming that the Pure Land's every inconceivable, supremely wondrous adornment is nothing but allegory, a metaphor for a state of mind, without any actual, real place behind it. Should one hold such deviant knowledge and mistaken views, one thereby loses the genuine benefit of rebirth in the Pure Land — the harm of this is very great, and must not go unknown!

彌陀為我發願立行，以期成佛。我違彌陀行願，以故長劫恆淪六道，永作衆生。了知彌陀乃我心中之佛，我乃彌陀心中之衆生。心既是一，而凡聖天殊者，由我一向迷背之所致也。如是信心，可為真信。從此信心上，發決定往生之願，行決定念佛之行，庶可深入淨宗法界，一生取辦。一超直入如來地，如母子相會，永樂天常矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Amitābha made his vow and established his practice for my own sake, in order that I might attain buddhahood. I, in turn, have gone against Amitābha's own practice and vow, and so have long revolved through the six destinies across endless kalpas, forever remaining a sentient being. Understanding fully that Amitābha is precisely the buddha within my own mind, and that I am precisely a sentient being within Amitābha's own mind — our minds already being one and the same, and yet the gulf between ordinary being and sage being as wide as it is — this is entirely the result of my own persistent delusion and turning away. Faith of this kind may be called true faith. Rising from this very faith, one should make the determined vow to be reborn there, and carry out the determined practice of recollecting the Buddha — and then one may hope to enter deeply into the Dharma-realm of the Pure Land tradition, settling this whole matter within a single life. Leaping over every stage at once, entering directly into the Tathāgata's own ground, one is reunited as mother and child, forever at peace in the constancy of heaven.

念佛一事，最要在了生死。既為了生死，則生死之苦，自生厭心；西方之樂，自生欣心。如此則信願二法，當念圓具。再加以志誠懇切，如子憶母而念，則佛力、法力、自心信願功德力，三法圓彰。猶如杲日當空，縱有濃霜層冰，不久即化。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the whole matter of buddha-recollection, the most essential thing is being done with birth-and-death. Once one truly practices for the sake of being done with birth-and-death, then a mind weary of the suffering of birth-and-death arises of itself, and a mind joyfully drawn to the joy of the West likewise arises of itself. In this way, the two dharmas of faith and vow are already fully present within that very thought. Add to this a sincere, earnest intent, recollecting the Buddha as a child recollects its mother, and then the three dharmas — the power of the Buddha, the power of the Dharma, and the power of one's own mind's merit in faith and vow — are all fully manifested together. It is like the bright sun standing in the sky: however thick the frost or layered the ice, before long it melts away entirely.

阿彌陀經云：從是西方，過十萬億佛土，有世界名曰極樂。其土有佛，號阿彌陀，今現在說法。又曰：彼土何故名為極樂？其國衆生，無有衆苦，但受諸樂，故名極樂。其無有衆苦，但受諸樂者，由阿彌陀佛福德智慧，神通道力，所莊嚴故。吾人所居之世界，則具足三苦八苦，無量諸苦，了無有樂，故名娑婆。梵語娑婆，此云堪忍；謂其中衆生，堪能忍受此諸苦故。然此世界，非無有樂，以所有樂事，多皆是苦；衆生迷昧，反以為樂。如嗜酒耽色，畋獵摴蒱等，何嘗是樂？一班愚夫，耽著不捨，樂以忘疲，誠堪憐愍。即屬真樂，亦難長久。如父母俱存，兄弟無故，此事何能常恆？故樂境一過，悲心續起，則謂了無有樂，非過論也。此世界苦，說不能盡；以三苦、八苦，包括無遺。三苦者：一、苦是苦苦，二、樂是壞苦，三、不苦不樂是行苦。苦苦者，謂此五陰身心，體性逼迫，故名為苦。又加以恆受生老病死等苦，故名苦苦。壞苦者，世間何事，能得久長？日中則昃，月盈則食。天道尚然，何况人事？樂境甫現，苦境即臨；當樂境壞滅之時，其苦有不堪言者，故名樂為壞苦也。行苦者，雖不苦不樂，似乎適宜；而其性遷流，何能常住？故名之為行苦也。舉此三苦，無苦不攝。八苦之義，書中備述。若知此界之苦，則厭離娑婆之心，自油然而生；若知彼界之樂，則欣求極樂之念，必勃然而起。由是諸惡莫作，衆善奉行，以培其基址；再加以至誠懇切，持佛名號，求生西方，則可出此娑婆，生彼極樂，為彌陀之真子，作海會之良朋矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Amitābha Sūtra says: "West from here, past a hundred thousand million buddha-lands, there is a world named Utmost Bliss. In that land there is a buddha called Amitābha, who is teaching the Dharma there right now." And again: "Why is that land called Utmost Bliss? Because the beings born in that country have no suffering of any kind and experience only joy — that is why it is called Utmost Bliss." That it has no suffering of any kind, and experiences only joy, is because it is adorned by Amitābha Buddha's own merit, virtue, wisdom, spiritual powers, and strength of the path. The world we ourselves inhabit, by contrast, is fully endowed with the three sufferings, the eight sufferings, and every immeasurable other suffering, utterly without any joy at all — hence it is called sahā. In Sanskrit, sahā means "endurable," meaning that the beings within it are able to endure these sufferings. Yet this does not mean this world has no joy at all — it is only that whatever passes for joy here is, in most cases, itself already suffering, and beings, lost in delusion, mistake it for joy instead. Take, for instance, addiction to drink or lust, hunting, or gambling — how could these ever truly be joy? And yet a whole class of foolish people, indulging in them without letting go, take pleasure in them to the point of forgetting their own exhaustion — a state truly to be pitied. Even were such things genuine joy, they could hardly last long: even when both parents are still living and one's siblings are free of misfortune, how could such a state remain constant forever? And so, once the state of joy has passed, sorrow follows in its wake — to say, therefore, that this world is utterly without joy is no overstatement at all.

The suffering of this world could never be spoken of exhaustively; the three sufferings and the eight sufferings together gather it in without remainder. The three sufferings are: first, suffering that is the suffering of suffering; second, joy that is the suffering of decay; third, neither suffering nor joy that is the suffering of process. As for the suffering of suffering: the very substance and nature of this body and mind of the five aggregates presses and constrains — hence it is called suffering; and further burdened by the constant experience of birth, old age, sickness, death, and the rest, it is called the suffering of suffering. As for the suffering of decay: what thing in this world can ever remain lasting? The sun at noon begins to decline; the moon at its fullest begins to wane. Even the way of heaven is thus — how much more so, then, human affairs? No sooner does a state of joy appear than a state of suffering already draws near; and when that state of joy comes to its own dissolution, the suffering that follows can hardly be put into words — hence joy itself is called the suffering of decay. As for the suffering of process: though it seems, at first, neither suffering nor joy, appearing suitable enough, its very nature is to flow and shift — how could it ever remain constant? Hence it is called the suffering of process. Raising up these three sufferings, no suffering at all is left ungathered. The meaning of the eight sufferings is set out fully elsewhere in this book.

If one truly understands the suffering of this world, then a mind weary of and forsaking the sahā world arises of itself, spontaneously; if one truly understands the joy of that other world, then a thought joyfully seeking Utmost Bliss must likewise arise, vigorously. From this, one refrains from every evil and upholds every good, cultivating thereby the very foundation for it; and adding to this an utterly sincere and earnest holding of the Buddha's name, seeking rebirth in the West, one may then escape this sahā world and be born in that Utmost Bliss — becoming Amitābha's own true child, a good companion within his own ocean-vast assembly.

肇法師云：天地之內，宇宙之間，中有一寶，秘在形山。此語，且約未悟未證者言；實則此寶包括太虛，豎窮橫徧，亙古亙今，時常顯露。正所謂：時時示時人，時人自不識。可不哀哉？唯我釋迦世尊一人，親得受用。餘諸衆生，經劫至劫，仗此寶威神之力，起惑造業，輪迴六道，了無出期；猶如盲人，親登寶山，不但不得受用，反更受彼所傷。由是世尊，隨順機宜，為之開示，俾彼各各就路還家；於彼六根、六塵、六識、七大中，隨於何境，諦審觀察，以期親見此寶。然具般若之智照，直下蘊空戹盡者，雖則大有其人，而非末世鈍根衆生所能希冀。於是遂開一特別法門，以期上中下根，同於現生，得其實益。令以深信切願，專念阿彌陀佛聖號，都攝六根，淨念相繼。久而久之，即衆生業識心，成如來秘密藏。則由三昧寶，證實相寶，方知此寶，徧滿法界。復以此寶，普施一切。以故自佛開此法門以來，一切菩薩、祖師、善知識，悉皆遵行此法。以其具足自他二力，校彼專仗自力者，其難易奚啻天淵之別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Master Sengzhao said: "Within heaven and earth, throughout the whole universe, there lies a single treasure, hidden away deep within the mountain of one's own form." These words, however, are spoken only in terms of one not yet awakened, not yet realized; in truth, this treasure encompasses the whole of great emptiness itself, exhausted vertically and pervading horizontally without limit, spanning past and present alike, ever manifest at every moment. This is precisely what is meant by: "constantly shown to the people of every age, and yet the people of every age fail, of themselves, to recognize it." How could one not grieve at this? Only our own Śākyamuni World-Honored One, alone among all, personally attained its full use. Every other sentient being, kalpa after kalpa, relying on the very majestic power of this treasure itself, has instead given rise to affliction and created karma, revolving through the six destinies with no way ever to escape — like a blind man who has personally climbed a mountain of treasure, and, far from making any use of it, is instead further injured by it. It was for this reason that the World-Honored One, according with each being's own capacity and occasion, opened up instruction for them, enabling each, by his own path, to return home — directing them, within the six sense-faculties, the six sense-objects, the six consciousnesses, and the seven great elements, to carefully observe and examine whatever object presented itself, in hopes of personally beholding this treasure for themselves.

Yet those endowed with the illuminating wisdom of prajñā, able directly to empty out the aggregates and exhaust every affliction on the spot, though there have certainly been such people, are not something the dull-natured beings of this final age could ever hope to be. And so he opened up one special Dharma-gate, in hopes that the upper, middle, and lower faculties alike might, together, in this very life, obtain its genuine benefit. He caused them, with deep faith and earnest vow, to recollect exclusively the sacred name of Amitābha Buddha, fully gathering in the six sense-faculties, pure thought following upon pure thought without interruption. In time, the very karmically-conditioned mind of a sentient being is thereby transformed into the Tathāgata's own secret treasury. From the treasure of samādhi, one thereby realizes the treasure of the true mark itself, and only then knows that this treasure fills the entire Dharma-realm — and, with this very treasure, universally bestows it upon all. This is why, ever since the Buddha first opened up this Dharma-gate, every bodhisattva, patriarch, and good spiritual friend since has, without exception, followed and practiced this very dharma. Because it is fully endowed with both self-power and other-power together, compared with those who rely exclusively on self-power alone, the difference in difficulty is nothing less than the gulf between heaven and earth.

吾人果能具真信切願，如子憶母，都攝六根，淨念相繼而念，即是以勢至反念念自性，觀音反聞聞自性，兩重工夫，融於一心，念如來萬德洪名。久而久之，則即衆生業識心，成如來秘密藏。所謂以果地覺為因地心，故得因該果海，果徹因源也。有緣遇者，幸勿忽諸。此是微塵佛，一路涅槃門；况我末法人，何敢不遵循？

Ru-Yi Translation

Should we ourselves truly be able to possess genuine faith and an earnest vow, recollecting the Buddha as a child recollects its mother, fully gathering in the six sense-faculties, pure thought following upon pure thought without interruption — this is precisely Mahāsthāmaprāpta's own method of turning recollection back to recollect one's own nature, together with Avalokiteśvara's own method of turning hearing back to hear one's own nature, two layers of effort fused into a single mind, recollecting the Tathāgata's own great name of ten thousand virtues. In time, the very karmically-conditioned mind of a sentient being is thereby transformed into the Tathāgata's own secret treasury — this is what is meant by taking the awakening of the fruit-ground as the very mind of the causal-ground, so that the cause already encompasses the ocean of the fruit, and the fruit already penetrates through to the source of the cause. Whoever has the good fortune to encounter this teaching, may you not treat it lightly. This is the single gate to nirvāṇa followed by buddhas as numerous as motes of dust — how much more so, then, should we, people of this degenerate age, dare not follow it as well?

世間所有，若根身、［即吾人之身］若世界，［即現在所住之天地］皆由衆生生滅心中，同業［世界］別業［根身］所感，皆有成壞，皆不久長。身則有生、老、病、死，界則有成、住、壞、空。所謂物極必反，樂極生悲者，此也。以因既是生滅，果亦不能不生滅也。極樂世界，乃阿彌陀佛徹證自心本具之佛性，隨心所現不思議稱性莊嚴之世界，故其樂無有窮盡之時期。譬如虛空，寬廓廣大，包含一切，森羅萬象。世界雖數數成、數數壞，而虛空畢竟無所增減。汝以世間之樂，難極樂之樂。極樂之樂，汝未能見。虛空汝雖未能全見，當天地之間之虛空，汝曾見過改變否？須知一切衆生，皆具佛性。故佛令人念佛求生西方，以仗阿彌陀佛之大慈悲願力，亦得受用此不生不滅之樂。以根身，則蓮花化生，無生老病死之苦；世界，則稱性功德所現，無成住壞空之變。雖聖人亦有所不知，况以世間生滅之法疑之乎？

Ru-Yi Translation

Everything in the world — whether one's own physical body, or the world itself, meaning the very heaven and earth we presently inhabit — is brought about, within the arising-and-perishing mind of sentient beings, by shared karma in the case of the world and individual karma in the case of the body; and every such thing has its arising and its dissolution, none of it lasting long. The body passes through birth, old age, sickness, and death; the world passes through formation, abiding, decay, and emptiness. This is precisely what is meant by "when a thing reaches its extreme it must reverse," and "when joy reaches its extreme, sorrow is born." For since the cause is itself arising-and-perishing, the effect, too, cannot fail to be arising-and-perishing as well. The World of Utmost Bliss, however, is the inconceivable world, adorned fully in accord with his own nature, manifested by Amitābha Buddha's own mind out of the buddha-nature he has thoroughly realized as inherently his own — and so its joy has no time at which it will ever come to an end. It is like empty space, vast and expansive, containing everything within it, every phenomenon arrayed throughout it: though worlds arise again and again, and dissolve again and again, empty space itself, in the end, is neither increased nor diminished by any of it. You would measure the joy of Utmost Bliss against the joy of this world — but the joy of Utmost Bliss, you have never yet seen. Though you have never seen the whole of empty space either, have you ever once seen the empty space between heaven and earth actually change? One must understand that every sentient being, without exception, is fully endowed with buddha-nature. This is why the Buddha leads people to recollect the Buddha and seek rebirth in the West — so that, relying on Amitābha Buddha's own great compassionate vow-power, they too may obtain the enjoyment of this unborn, unceasing joy. As for the body: one is born by transformation from a lotus flower, free of the suffering of birth, old age, sickness, and death. As for the world: it is manifested by merit and virtue fully in accord with one's own nature, free of the changes of formation, abiding, decay, and emptiness. Even a sage may not know everything about this — how much less, then, should one doubt it merely on the basis of this world's own arising-and-perishing dharmas?

Ru-Yi Translation

Every other Dharma-gate is such that a lesser dharma need not be cultivated by one of great capacity, while a greater dharma cannot be cultivated by one of lesser capacity. Only this single gate of the Pure Land reaches universally across all three levels of capacity, gathering in the sharp and the dull alike. At its highest, not even Avalokiteśvara, Mahāsthāmaprāpta, Mañjuśrī, or Samantabhadra stand outside its scope; at its lowest, even one guilty of the five heinous crimes or the ten evils, of the very seed-nature bound for the Avīci hell, may still be admitted into it beforehand. Had the Tathāgata not opened up this dharma, then beings of this final age, wishing to be done with birth-and-death within this very life, would have absolutely no hope at all. And yet, vast as this Dharma-gate is, its own method of cultivation is exceedingly simple — and for this very reason, one without roots of goodness for the Pure Land already planted in past lives finds it difficult to believe fully, without any doubt remaining. Not only do ordinary beings fail to believe it; even followers of the two lesser vehicles often still doubt it. Not only do the two lesser vehicles fail to believe it; even bodhisattvas of provisional rank still, in some cases, doubt it. Only a bodhisattva of the Mahāyāna's own deep stages is able to fully grasp it, believing it thoroughly, without any doubt remaining. [This is truly because this Dharma-gate takes the awakening of the fruit as the very mind of the cause, its whole substance already the very realm of a buddha — only buddha and buddha together are able to fathom it fully, something that lies beyond the wisdom of anyone else to know. See the preface to the Longshu Jingtu Wen in the Correct Compilation.]

One able to give rise to deep faith in this dharma, though still an ordinary being fully bound by affliction, has a seed-nature that has already surpassed that of the two lesser vehicles. By means of faith, vow, and holding the Buddha's name, one is thereby able to cast one's own ordinary mind directly into the ocean of the Buddha's own awakening, and so secretly penetrates the Buddha's own wisdom, silently according with the wondrous nature of the path. Whenever one wishes to expound the method of Pure Land cultivation, if one does not briefly set forth both the difficulty of relying on self-power to attain liberation through every other dharma, and the ease of relying on Buddha-power to attain rebirth through this dharma, then one is left either doubting the dharma itself, or doubting one's own capacity for it. Should there be even the slightest trace of doubt remaining, that doubt itself becomes an obstruction — never mind failing to practice at all; even if one does practice, one still fails to obtain the ultimate, genuine benefit of it. Considered from this, is the single dharma of faith not something to be urgently sought out and thoroughly investigated, in hopes of penetrating it to its very utmost?

或曰：阿彌陀佛，安居極樂。十方世界，無量無邊；一世界中念佛衆生，亦復無量無邊。阿彌陀佛，何能以一身，一時普徧接引十方無量無邊世界之一切念佛衆生乎？答：汝何得以凡夫知見，推測佛境？姑以喻明，使汝惑滅。一月麗天，萬川影現，月何容心哉。夫天只一月，而大海大江，大河小溪，悉現全月。即小而一勺一滴水中，無不各現全月。且江河之月，一人看之，則有一月當乎其人；百千萬億人，於百千萬億處看之，則無不各有一月當乎其人。若百千萬億人，各向東西南北而行，則月亦於所行之處，常當其人；相去之處，了無遠近。若百千萬億人，安住不動，則月亦安住不動，常當其人也。唯水清而靜則現，水濁而動則隱。月固無取捨，其不現者，由水昏濁奔騰，無由受其影現耳。衆生之心如水，阿彌陀佛如月；衆生信願具足，至誠感佛，則佛應之，如水清月現也。若心不清淨，不至誠，與貪瞋癡相應，與佛相背，如水濁而動，月雖不遺照臨，而不能昭彰影現也。月乃世間色法，尚有如此之妙，况阿彌陀佛，煩惑淨盡，福慧具足，心包太虛，量周法界者乎？故華嚴經云：佛身充滿於法界，普現一切羣生前；隨緣赴感靡不周，而恆處此菩提座。故知徧法界感，徧法界應。佛實未曾起心動念，有來去相，而能令緣熟衆生，見其來此接引以往西方也。懷此疑者，固非一二；因示大意，令生正信。

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone may say: "Amitābha Buddha dwells at ease in Utmost Bliss. The worlds of the ten directions are immeasurable and boundless; and within any one world alone, the beings who recollect the Buddha are likewise immeasurable and boundless. How could Amitābha Buddha, with a single body, universally receive and guide, all at the same time, every single being throughout the immeasurable, boundless worlds of the ten directions who recollects him?" Answer: How could you presume to measure the Buddha's own realm by an ordinary being's own limited views? Let me offer, for the moment, a comparison to make this clear, and dissolve your doubt. A single moon graces the sky, and yet its reflection appears in ten thousand streams at once — what deliberate intent does the moon exert in this? Heaven holds but a single moon, and yet the great ocean, the great rivers, the broad streams, and the small brooks alike, all manifest the whole moon within them; even down to the smallest ladle or single drop of water, there is not one that fails to manifest the whole moon as well. And of the moon reflected in a river: when a single person looks at it, one moon appears directly before that person; and when a hundred thousand million people look at it from a hundred thousand million different places, there is not one of them who fails to have, directly before himself, his own single moon. Should these hundred thousand million people each walk in a different direction, east, west, south, or north, the moon likewise remains, wherever each of them walks, always directly before that person — the distance between them making not the slightest difference near or far. Should these hundred thousand million people instead remain still, unmoving, the moon likewise remains still, unmoving, always directly before each of them. It is only that where the water is clear and calm, the reflection appears; where the water is turbid and turbulent, it remains hidden. The moon itself holds no preference at all — its failure to appear is entirely because the water, dark, turbid, and surging, has no way to receive its reflected image. The mind of a sentient being is like the water; Amitābha Buddha is like the moon. When a sentient being's faith and vow are complete, and he moves the Buddha with utmost sincerity, the Buddha responds to him — just as, when the water is clear, the moon appears within it. If the mind is not pure, not utterly sincere, but instead in accord with greed, hatred, and delusion, turned away from the Buddha — this is like water that is turbid and disturbed: though the moon never withholds its own illumination, it cannot manifest its reflection clearly within it.

If even the moon, a mere physical dharma of this world, already possesses such wondrous capacity as this, how much more so, then, Amitābha Buddha himself, whose affliction is purified and exhausted entirely, whose merit and wisdom are fully complete, whose mind encompasses the whole of great emptiness, whose measure fills the entire Dharma-realm? Hence the Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "The Buddha's body fills the entire Dharma-realm, universally manifesting before every group of beings; according with conditions, responding wherever moved, missing nothing at all, while forever abiding upon this very seat of awakening." From this one knows: wherever the Dharma-realm is moved, wherever the Dharma-realm is responded to. The Buddha himself, in truth, never gives rise to any thought or stirring, never bears the mark of coming or going at all — and yet is still able to cause a sentient being whose conditions have ripened to see him come there to receive and guide him onward to the West. Those who hold this very doubt are certainly not one or two alone; I offer this general sense of the matter here, in order to give rise to correct faith.

Ru-Yi Translation

Phenomena and principle, nature and characteristic, emptiness and existence, cause and effect — mixed together and hard to distinguish. It is better, instead, to learn from the simple, uncomplicated devotion of the most foolish man or woman, single-mindedly recollecting the Buddha. One must show him the utmost reverence and respect, with nothing but sincerity, nothing but earnestness, and, in time, karma dissolves and wisdom brightens, obstruction is exhausted and blessing flourishes — and this very kind of doubting mind falls away entirely of itself. Then whether the Buddha exists or not, whether one's own self exists or not, the very gateway into the Buddha's own path, the sure evidence of the farther shore — why would any of this still need to be asked of another? If, instead of single-mindedly devoting oneself to buddha-recollection, one goes seeking clarity from someone else's mouth, this is no different from reading the Diamond Sūtra without understanding the true mark, or reading Pure Land texts such as the Jingtu Wen or the Xigui Zhizhi without giving rise to faith at all. Because karmic obstruction blocks the mind, one cannot truly comprehend it — like a blind man beholding the sun: the sun is certainly there in the sky, and the eye certainly there to see it, and yet his failure to see its light is no different from before he ever tried to behold it at all. Should his sight be restored, then the very moment he looks, he beholds its light. The single method of buddha-recollection is precisely the most direct, most essential method for restoring this very sight. Should one wish to behold the mark of the true mark itself, one should devote oneself utterly and sincerely to this method — and there will certainly come a moment of great, satisfying joy.

To personally behold the true self, nothing less than great, thorough awakening will do; to realize it, nothing less than severing affliction and realizing the true will do; to realize it fully and completely, nothing less than the three delusions entirely purified and both kinds of death forever ended will do. But as for where the true self actually resides: your own long revolving through kalpa after kalpa of transmigration, and even your very questioning of this matter right now, are both themselves already carried out entirely through the power of that true self. It is only because you have turned your back on awakening and joined yourself to the defiling dust of the world that you fail to obtain its genuine enjoyment. It is like Yajñadatta's own head, or the jewel sewn within one's own robe: neither was ever, in fact, actually lost — the fear and terror, the poverty and hardship endured, were all groundless from the very start.

復顧顯微書 · Reply to Gu Xianwei 正編

Translator's note

"Yajñadatta's own head" (演若之頭) is a well-known parable from the Śūraṅgama Sūtra: a man named Yajñadatta, seeing his own reflection in a mirror one morning, becomes convinced that his real head has vanished and runs through the streets in terror — never realizing his head was on his own shoulders the whole time. Yinguang pairs it here with the parable of the jewel sewn into a sleeping man's robe, already cited in this collection's Part 1, Entry 14 — both images for a wholeness never actually lost, only forgotten.

Ru-Yi Translation

The habitual tendencies of sentient beings each incline in their own particular direction: the foolish incline toward the mediocre and the base; the wise incline toward the lofty and the elevated. Should the foolish be content in their own foolishness, applying their minds to nothing else, but devoting themselves exclusively to cultivating pure karma, they are certain, in this very life, to attain rebirth — one may well say their very foolishness is beyond anyone's reach to match. Should the wise refrain from relying on their own wisdom alone, and still devote themselves to this single gate of relying on the Buddha's own merciful power to seek rebirth in the Pure Land, this may be called true, great wisdom. But should one instead rely on one's own understanding and look down upon the Pure Land, one will find oneself, kalpa after kalpa, sinking ever deeper into the lower paths, wishing then to catch up again even with today's own foolish men and women — and finding it utterly beyond reach.

As for those who have thoroughly penetrated the doctrinal schools of nature and characteristic — I truly love and admire them, and yet dare not follow their own path myself. Why is this? Because a short rope cannot draw water from a deep well, and a small sheet of paper cannot wrap something large. This is not to say that everyone must imitate what I myself have done. But if someone, base and lowly just as I am, should nonetheless wish to imitate the conduct of a great, thoroughly accomplished master, directly seeking a wondrous awakening to his own mind, overturning the whole ocean of doctrine — I fear he will fail to become such a great, accomplished master, and will instead be pitied even by the simple, honest men and women who plainly recollect the Buddha and attain rebirth in the West. Would this not be a case of a clever scheme turning into the greatest clumsiness, of soaring into the sky only to plunge instead into the deepest abyss? To sum it up in a single phrase: one need only honestly examine one's own capacity.

And since birth-and-death remains unresolved, the greater one's blessing, the greater, too, one's karma — and in a further future life, one can hardly avoid falling into one of the three lower paths of hell, hungry ghost, or animal. Should one then wish to recover a human body once more, and once more encounter this very Dharma-gate of the Pure Land that resolves birth-and-death within this life, it would be as difficult as climbing to heaven. The Buddha taught people to recollect the Buddha and seek rebirth in the West precisely so that they might be done with birth-and-death within this very life; to seek instead the blessed reward of a future human or heavenly rebirth is itself already a betrayal of the Buddha's own teaching. It is like taking a priceless jewel, the finest in the entire world, and trading it away for a single piece of candy — is this not a tragic waste?
