The Wenchao Jinghualu.

Yinguang, the thirteenth patriarch of Chinese Pure Land Buddhism, left behind no formal treatise of his own — only a vast correspondence of letters, prefaces, and occasional writings, spanning several decades and gathered, after his death, into the Wenchao ("Collected Writings"), one of the most widely circulated bodies of modern Chinese Buddhist prose. In 1952, the lay disciple Li Jingtong distilled this correspondence into the Jinghualu ("Record of the Essential Radiance") — 333 passages across ten thematic chapters, prefaced by Yuanying, whose own commentary on the Śūraṅgama Sūtra is elsewhere on this site. This collection translates selected chapters of that anthology into English, each quoted passage matched to its original letter or preface.

How to read this collection

Unlike a sūtra or a single commentary, the Wenchao has no continuous argument of its own — it is a lifetime of individual letters, each written to answer one particular correspondent's own question or doubt. The Jinghualu's own achievement was to select, from across the three compilations of that correspondence (正編, 續編, 三編— the Correct, Continued, and Third Compilations), the passages that speak most directly to a general reader, organizing them by theme rather than by date. Each entry on this collection's pages carries its own citation, naming the specific letter or preface it is drawn from and which compilation it belongs to, so a reader can trace any passage back to its original occasion.

This is not yet the complete Jinghualu — only three of its ten chapters are translated so far, chosen to open with the chapters most directly relevant to a reader new to Pure Land practice: why the tradition holds this path in such high regard, what genuine faith and vow actually require, and how to face death itself, both one's own and a loved one's.

What each page includes

  • Every passage's Chinese source text, beside a complete English translation.
  • A citation for each passage, naming its source letter or preface and which of the three compilations it belongs to.

The Anthology's Ten Chapters

Li Jingtong's own organization, carried across from the original 1952 edition. Chapters already translated are linked below; the remaining seven are listed for reference and may be added to this collection later.

Chapter 1 · 讚淨土超勝
Praising the Pure Land's Superiority
Eighteen passages arguing that the Pure Land is the destination every school of Buddhism ultimately returns to, closing with a compressed history of Pure Land practice in China from Huiyuan's Lotus Society onward.
Translated · 18 passages
Chapter 2 · 誡信願真切
Admonishing True Faith and Sincere Vow
Thirty-four passages in three groups: demonstrating what genuine faith and vow require, dispelling common doubts, and exhorting a complete, undivided aspiration.
Translated · 34 passages
Chapter 3 · 示修持方法
Demonstrating Methods of Practice
Six sub-sections on buddha-recollection technique, Avalokiteśvara practice, correcting habitual faults, cultivating virtue, comparative method, and encouragement.
Not yet translated
Chapter 4 · 論生死事大
Discussing the Magnitude of Life and Death
Twenty-seven passages on impermanence, exclusive reliance on the Buddha's own power, and the full practical instruction for deathbed care — closing with the "Three Great Essentials at the End of Life."
Translated · 27 passages
Chapter 5 · 勉居心誠敬
Encouraging Sincerity and Reverence
On the disposition of mind Yinguang held as prerequisite to any genuine practice at all.
Not yet translated
Chapter 6 · 勸注重因果
Urging Attention to Cause and Effect
Two sub-sections on karmic principle and the importance of abstaining from killing.
Not yet translated
Chapter 7 · 分禪淨界限
Distinguishing the Boundary Between Chan and Pure Land
Where the two traditions genuinely differ, and where their apparent conflict dissolves on closer inspection.
Not yet translated
Chapter 8 · 釋普通疑惑
Resolving Common Doubts
A further set of frequently raised objections, addressed in Yinguang's characteristic question-and-answer style.
Not yet translated
Chapter 9 · 諭在家善信
Guidance for Lay Practitioners
Instruction addressed specifically to householders balancing family and worldly duty with practice.
Not yet translated
Chapter 10 · 標應讀典籍
Recommending Essential Texts
Yinguang's own reading list for students of the Pure Land tradition, with an appendix on the merit of printing and distributing scripture.
Not yet translated

Translation Notes

Recurring terms, kept consistent across the chapters translated so far, and cross-referenced with this site's existing Pure Land vocabulary. See the full translation notes for source details and the complete list of figures and texts cited, and also this site's note on translation method.

信願行 — faith, vow, practiceOuyi Zhixu's own framework, which Yinguang quotes directly and treats as settled doctrine — see the Amituo Yaojie.
帶業往生 — reborn carrying karma alongRebirth in the common-dwelling land while affliction remains unsevered — the promise that makes the practice accessible to ordinary practitioners.
自力 / 他力 — self-power / other-powerThe central distinction this anthology returns to constantly: paths depending on one's own cultivated realization versus reliance on Amitābha's vow-power.
正編 / 續編 / 三編 — the Correct / Continued / Third CompilationsThe three successive published collections of Yinguang's correspondence the Jinghualu draws from, cited on every entry.
臨終三大要 — the Three Great Essentials at the End of LifeYinguang's own standalone deathbed-care instruction, closing Chapter 4 of this collection.
