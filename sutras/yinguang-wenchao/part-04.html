Demonstrating Methods of Practice

Chapter 3 is the anthology's largest and most practical, running to six sections; this page covers the first two — the actual mechanics of buddha-recollection (daily liturgy, the "ten recollections" method for the busy, and Yinguang's own signature "ten-count" technique for gathering the mind) and the case for reciting Avalokiteśvara's name alongside Amitābha's, especially in acute distress.

Chinese Source

既有真信切願，當修念佛正行；以信願為先導，念佛為正行；信願行三，乃念佛法門宗要。有行無信願，不能往生；有信願無行，亦不能往生；信願行三，具足無缺，決定往生。得生與否，全由信願之有無；品位高下，全由持名之深淺。言念佛正行者，各隨自己身分而立，不可定執一法。如其身無事累，固當從朝至暮，從暮至朝，行住坐臥，語默動靜，穿衣吃飯，大小便利，一切時，一切處，令此一句洪名聖號，不離心口。若盥漱清淨，衣冠整齊，及地方清潔，則或聲或默，皆無不可。若睡眠及裸露澡浴大小便時，及至穢污不潔之處，只可默念，不宜出聲。默念功德一樣，出聲便不恭敬。勿謂此等時處，念不得佛，須知此等時處，出不得聲耳。又睡若出聲，非唯不恭，且致傷氣，不可不知。雖則長時念佛，無有間斷，須於晨朝向佛禮拜畢，先念阿彌陀經一遍，往生咒三徧畢，即念讚佛偈，即阿彌陀佛身金色偈。念偈畢，念南無西方極樂世界大慈大悲阿彌陀佛，隨即但念南無阿彌陀佛六字，或一千聲，或五百聲，當圍繞念。若不便繞，或跪或坐或立皆可。念至將畢，歸位念觀音、勢至、清淨大海眾菩薩，各三稱，然後念淨土文，發願回向往生。念淨土文者，令依文義而發心也。若心不依文而發，則成徒設虛文，不得實益矣。淨土文畢，念三歸依，禮拜而退。此為朝時功課，暮亦如之。若欲多多禮拜者，或在念佛歸位之時，則拜佛若干拜。九稱菩薩，即作九禮，禮畢即發願回向。或在功課念畢禮拜，隨己之便，皆無不可。但須懇切至誠，不可潦草粗率。蒲團不可過高，高則便不恭敬。若或事務多端，略無閒暇，當於晨朝盥漱畢，有佛則禮佛三拜，正身合掌念南無阿彌陀佛，盡一口氣為一念，念至十口氣，即念小淨土文；或但念願生西方淨土中四句偈，念畢禮佛三拜而退。若無佛，即向西問訊，照上念法而念。此名十念法門，乃宋慈雲懺主，為王臣政務繁劇，無暇修持者所立也。何以令盡一口氣念？以眾生心散，又無暇專念，如此念時，借氣攝心，心自不散。然須隨氣長短，不可強使多念，強則傷氣。又止可十念，不可二十　三十，多亦傷氣。以散心念佛，難得往生；此法能令心歸一處。一心念佛，決定往生。念數雖少，功德頗深。極閒極忙，既各有法，則半閑半忙者，自可斟酌其間，而為修持法則也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once one already possesses genuine faith and an earnest vow, one should cultivate the proper practice of buddha-recollection: faith and vow serve as the guiding forerunner, buddha-recollection as the proper practice itself. Faith, vow, and practice, these three, are the essential purport of the whole Dharma-gate of buddha-recollection. Having practice without faith and vow, one cannot attain rebirth; having faith and vow without practice, one likewise cannot attain rebirth. Only when faith, vow, and practice, these three, are fully complete, with nothing lacking, is rebirth secured. Whether one is reborn at all depends entirely on whether faith and vow are present; the grade and rank one attains depends entirely on the depth of one's holding the name.

As for the proper practice of buddha-recollection: this should be established according to each person's own circumstances, and one should not rigidly insist on a single fixed method. Should one's own body be free of pressing obligations, one should, from morning to evening and from evening to morning, whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, whether speaking or silent, moving or still, whether dressing or eating, or even attending to bodily necessities — at all times, in all places — never let this single great, sacred name depart from one's own mind or mouth. When one has washed and rinsed cleanly, one's robes and cap properly arranged, and the surroundings clean, then either reciting aloud or silently is entirely acceptable. But while sleeping, or unclothed while bathing, or attending to bodily necessities, or in any filthy, unclean place, one should recite silently only, and never aloud. Silent recitation carries the very same merit — reciting aloud in such circumstances would simply be disrespectful. Do not suppose that the Buddha cannot be recollected at such times and in such places — one must understand only that the voice cannot properly be raised there. And should one recite aloud while asleep, this is not only disrespectful, but may also injure one's own breath — this should not go unknown.

Although one recollects the Buddha continuously, without interruption, throughout the day, one should still, each morning, upon bowing before the Buddha, first recite the Amitābha Sūtra once through, and the Rebirth Dhāraṇī three times, then recite the verse of praise to the Buddha — the verse beginning "Amitābha Buddha's body is the color of gold." Having recited the verse, one recites "Homage to the greatly compassionate, greatly merciful Amitābha Buddha of the Western Land of Utmost Bliss," and immediately thereafter recites only the six characters "Namo Amitābha Buddha," either a thousand times or five hundred times, walking in circumambulation while doing so. Should circumambulation be inconvenient, kneeling, sitting, or standing are all equally acceptable. As the recitation nears its end, one returns to one's place and recites the names of Avalokiteśvara, Mahāsthāmaprāpta, and the pure, ocean-vast assembly of bodhisattvas, three times each, and then recites the Pure Land liturgical text, making the vow and dedicating merit toward rebirth. Reciting this liturgical text is meant to let one's own vow arise in accordance with its own wording and meaning — should the mind not arise from the text itself, this becomes nothing but an empty formality, and no genuine benefit is obtained. Once the liturgical text is finished, one recites the Threefold Refuge, bows, and withdraws. This constitutes the morning practice; the evening practice follows the very same pattern.

Should one wish to prostrate more extensively, one may either do so at the point of returning to one's place after buddha-recollection, bowing to the Buddha a certain number of times, or, at the nine invocations of the bodhisattvas, perform nine full prostrations, and then, once the prostrations are complete, make the vow and dedicate merit. Or one may instead prostrate once the whole liturgy has been recited — whichever suits one's own convenience is entirely acceptable. What matters is that it be carried out with earnest sincerity, never carelessly or roughly. One's own cushion should not be too high, since a cushion too high itself already shows a lack of respect.

Should one's own affairs be numerous and complex, leaving scarcely any free time at all, one should, each morning, upon finishing washing and rinsing, bow three times before a Buddha-image if one has one, then stand upright, join the palms, and recite "Namo Amitābha Buddha," treating a single full breath as one recitation-unit, continuing for ten such breaths, and then recite the short Pure Land liturgical text — or simply recite the four-line verse "vowing to be born within the Pure Land of the West" — and, once finished, bow three times before the Buddha and withdraw. Should one have no Buddha-image, one should simply bow in the direction of the west and follow the very same method of recitation described above.

This is called the "ten recollections method," established by the Song-dynasty Repentance Master Ciyun for the sake of kings, ministers, and officials whose governmental duties left them no free time for cultivation. Why should one treat a single full breath as a single recitation-unit? Because the minds of sentient beings are scattered and distracted, and, having no leisure to recite single-mindedly, this method borrows the breath itself to gather in the mind, so that the mind naturally ceases to scatter. Yet one must follow the natural length of one's own breath, and never force oneself to recite more within it than is natural, since forcing it injures the breath. And one should limit oneself to ten such recitation-units, never twenty or thirty — too many likewise injure the breath. Reciting the Buddha's name with a scattered mind makes rebirth difficult to secure; this method, by contrast, is able to gather the mind back into a single point. Recollecting the Buddha with a single, unified mind secures rebirth without fail — even though the actual count of recitations is small, its merit runs quite deep.

Since methods already exist for the utterly idle and the utterly busy alike, one who is half-idle and half-busy may simply weigh the two against each other, adjusting a method for oneself somewhere between them.

與陳錫周書 · Letter to Chen Xizhou 正編

Translator's note

Repentance Master Ciyun (慈雲懺主, i.e. Zunshi, 964–1032) was a Song-dynasty Tiantai monk especially known for repentance liturgy — the "ten recollections method" (十念法門) attributed to him here is distinct from the "ten recollections" (十念) discussed in the sūtra itself and in Ouyi's own commentary (see the Amituo Yaojie, Part 9): this is a specific breath-counting technique for daily practice, not the deathbed recitation the sūtra describes.

Ru-Yi Translation

During a period of solitary retreat devoted exclusively to pure karma, buddha-recollection should be one's own proper practice. For the morning liturgy, one should still recite, as usual, the Śūraṅgama Mantra, the Great Compassion Dhāraṇī, and the Ten Lesser Dhāraṇīs. Should the Śūraṅgama Mantra not yet be memorized, there is no harm in reading it daily from the text itself, and once thoroughly familiar with it, reciting it from memory instead. For the evening liturgy, the Amitābha Sūtra, the Great Repentance, and the Mengshan rite should likewise be recited daily without fail. Beyond this, buddha-recollection itself should be carried on constantly from morning to evening, whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down.

One should further establish a fixed routine: reciting once in the morning, and, before beginning, performing a set number of prostrations — first three prostrations to our own founding teacher Śākyamuni Buddha, then a certain number of prostrations to Amitābha Buddha, then three prostrations each to Avalokiteśvara, Mahāsthāmaprāpta, and the pure, ocean-vast assembly, and then three further prostrations to every buddha of the ten directions who ever abides, every venerable dharma, and every worthy and sagely member of the Saṅgha. One then recollects the Buddha, whether a thousand times or more or fewer, and, once finished, performs a further set number of prostrations. This is repeated once before noon and once after noon, and, after a further brief rest, the evening liturgy is performed. At the first watch of the night, one recites the Mengshan rite, followed by a certain number of recollections of the Buddha, a certain number of prostrations, the making of the vow and dedication of merit, and the Threefold Refuge — after which one settles the mind by silently recollecting the Buddha's name until sleep.

While lying down to sleep, only silent recollection is permitted — one must never recite aloud, since reciting aloud injures the breath, and in time leads to illness. Even while asleep, the mind should still constantly hold a sense of reverence, seeking only that the mind not wander outward, each thought held in accord with the Buddha's own name. Should scattered thoughts arise in the mind, one should at once gather the mind back and recite reverently, and the scattered thoughts will thereby dissolve.

One must never, under any circumstance, indulge in reckless fantasy — wishing to attain spiritual powers, to attain favorable karmic conditions, to attain fame and reputation, or to wish for the flourishing of some grand monastery. Should thoughts of this kind arise, they will, in time, certainly lead to demonic possession. And even should the mind become pure and delusive thought subdued, one must still not give rise to a self-satisfied joy over it, boasting to others of having attained a single tenth of realization by claiming ten-tenths — this too is a root from which demonic possession may take hold.

念佛之時，必須攝耳諦聽，一字一句，勿令空過，久而久之，身心歸一。聽之一法，實念佛要法，無論何人，均有利無弊，功德甚深。不比觀想等法，知法者則得益，不知法者多受損也。

Ru-Yi Translation

While recollecting the Buddha, one must gather in the ear and listen attentively, letting not a single character or phrase pass by unnoticed — and, in time, body and mind become unified. This method of listening is, in truth, an essential method of buddha-recollection: no matter who practices it, it carries benefit and no drawback whatsoever, its merit running quite deep. This is unlike methods such as visualization, where one who understands the method benefits from it, while one who does not understand it more often suffers harm.

汝不知淨土宗旨，當依一函徧復所說，生真信，發切願，志誠懇切，念佛名號，勿用觀心念法，當用攝心念法。楞嚴經大勢至菩薩說，都攝六根，淨念相繼，得三摩地，斯為第一。念佛時，心中要念得清清楚楚，口中要念得清清楚楚，耳中要聽得清清楚楚。意舌耳三根，一一攝於佛號，則眼也不會東張西望，鼻也不會齅別種氣味，身也不會懶惰懈怠，名為都攝六根。都攝六根而念，雖不能全無妄念，校彼不攝者，則心中清淨多矣，故名淨念。淨念若能常常相繼，無有間斷，自可心歸一處；淺之則得一心，深之則得三昧。三摩地，亦三昧之別名，此云正定，亦云正受。正定者，心安住於佛號中，不復外馳之謂。正受者，心所納受，唯佛號功德之境緣，一切境緣，皆不可得也。能真都攝六根而念，決定業障消除，善根增長。不須觀心，而心自清淨明了，又何致心火上炎之病乎？汝以極重之業力凡夫，妄用觀心之法，故致如此。觀心之法，乃教家修觀之法，念佛之人，不甚合機。都攝六根，淨念相繼，乃普被上中下，若聖若凡，一切機之無上妙法也。須知都攝，注重在聽；即心中默念，也要聽。以心中起念，即有聲相；自己耳，聽自己心中之聲，仍是明明了了。果能字字句句，聽得清楚，則六根通歸於一。校彼修別種觀法，為最穩當，最省力，最契理契機也。

Ru-Yi Translation

You do not yet understand the essential purport of the Pure Land tradition. You should, in accord with what is set forth in "A Letter to All," give rise to genuine faith, arouse an earnest vow, and, with sincere and earnest intent, recite the Buddha's name — you should not use the method of contemplating the mind while reciting, but rather the method of gathering in the mind while reciting. In the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, the bodhisattva Mahāsthāmaprāpta says: "Fully gathering in the six sense-faculties, pure thought following upon pure thought without interruption, one attains samādhi — this is foremost of all." While recollecting the Buddha, one's own mind should recite with utter clarity, one's own mouth should recite with utter clarity, and one's own ear should listen with utter clarity. Once the mind, tongue, and ear, these three faculties, are each gathered fully into the Buddha's name, then the eye, too, will no longer glance about here and there, the nose will no longer smell after other scents, and the body will no longer grow lazy or slack — this is what is called "fully gathering in the six sense-faculties." Reciting with the six sense-faculties thus fully gathered in, though one may not be entirely free of every scattered thought, compared with one who has not gathered them in at all, the mind is far purer — hence it is called "pure thought." Should this pure thought be able to continue on and on, without interruption, the mind will naturally come to rest in a single point — at a shallower level, this yields one-pointedness of mind; at a deeper level, it yields samādhi itself.

"Samādhi" is likewise another name for this same samādhi, translated here as "correct concentration," or also as "correct reception." "Correct concentration" means the mind abides settled within the Buddha's own name, no longer wandering outward. "Correct reception" means that what the mind receives and takes in is nothing but the very circumstance of the Buddha-name's own merit and virtue — every other circumstance becomes entirely unobtainable. One who is truly able to fully gather in the six sense-faculties while reciting is certain to dissolve away karmic obstruction and increase roots of goodness. There is no need to contemplate the mind separately at all — the mind naturally becomes pure and clear of itself. How, then, could the illness of the mind's fire rising upward ever arise from this?

You yourself, an ordinary being weighed down by extremely heavy karmic force, have recklessly applied the method of contemplating the mind, and this is precisely what has brought about your present condition. The method of contemplating the mind is a method of cultivating contemplation proper to the doctrinal schools, and is not especially well suited to one who recollects the Buddha. Fully gathering in the six sense-faculties, pure thought following upon pure thought without interruption, is instead the unsurpassed, wondrous dharma that universally covers the upper, middle, and lower faculties alike, sage and ordinary being together, every capacity without exception.

One must understand that "fully gathering in" places its weight on listening — even silent recollection within the mind must still be listened to. For the moment a thought arises within the mind, it already carries the mark of sound; one's own ear, listening to the sound within one's own mind, still hears it with utter clarity all the same. Should one truly be able to listen with utter clarity to every single character, every single phrase, then all six sense-faculties are gathered together and returned to a single point. Compared with cultivating any other kind of contemplation method, this is by far the most secure, the most effortless, and the most fully in accord with both principle and capacity.

復楊煒章書 · Reply to Yang Weizhang 續編

Translator's note

Mahāsthāmaprāpta's "fully gathering in the six sense-faculties, pure thought following upon pure thought without interruption" (都攝六根，淨念相繼) is the single most-quoted line in this entire anthology — see the [Mahāsthāmaprāpta Chapter]({{ '/sutras/mahasthamaprapta-chapter.html' | relative_url }}) for the full sūtra passage it comes from. Yinguang's warning against "the method of contemplating the mind" (觀心) reflects a real pastoral concern running throughout this section: that visualization and contemplation techniques borrowed from other schools, attempted without proper guidance, can produce physical and psychological harm (see Entry 4's own diagnosis, "the mind's fire rising upward," addressed at length later in this section).

念佛之人，當恭敬至誠，字字句句，心裏念得清清楚楚，口裏念得清清楚楚。果能如是，縱不能完全了無妄念，然亦不至過甚。多有只圖快、圖多，隨口滑讀，故無效也；若能攝心，方可謂為真念佛人。大勢至菩薩，以如子憶母為喻，子心中只念其母，其餘之境，皆非己心中事，故能感應道交。

Ru-Yi Translation

A person who recollects the Buddha should do so with reverence and utmost sincerity, each character and each phrase recited with utter clarity in the mind, and with utter clarity in the mouth. Should one truly be able to do this, even if one cannot be entirely free of every scattered thought, it will at least not become excessive. Many people seek only speed or quantity, reciting glibly without real attention, and so obtain no effect at all; only one who is able to gather in the mind may truly be called a genuine practitioner of buddha-recollection. The bodhisattva Mahāsthāmaprāpta offers the analogy of a child recollecting its mother: the child's mind holds nothing but its mother, every other circumstance no longer a matter for its own mind at all — and it is precisely for this reason that response and resonance can meet along the path.

修習淨土，隨分隨力，豈必屏除萬緣，方能修持乎？譬如孝子思慈親，淫人思美女，雖日用百忙中，此一念固無時或忘也。修淨土人，亦復如是；任憑日用紛繁，決不許忘其佛念，則得其要矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

In cultivating the Pure Land, one proceeds according to one's own portion and one's own strength — how could it possibly require setting aside every worldly condition before cultivation becomes possible at all? It is like a filial son thinking of his loving parent, or a lustful man thinking of a beautiful woman: even amid the busiest round of daily affairs, this single thought is never, for even a moment, forgotten. One who cultivates the Pure Land should be exactly the same — no matter how numerous and complex one's daily affairs may be, one must never allow oneself to forget the recollection of the Buddha, and one will thereby have grasped the very essential point of the practice.

When reciting aloud, one may recite the full six characters; when recollecting silently in the mind, the greater number of characters becomes difficult to sustain, and so one should recite the four-character form instead. Treat buddha-recollection as one's own very life-root — never setting it down, even for a moment — and one may thereby hope to transcend the ordinary and enter sagehood, be done with birth-and-death, and attain rebirth in the West.

Ru-Yi Translation

Every sentient being, since beginningless time, has created every kind of karma throughout the six destinies without exception. Should one have no mind to cultivate practice, one fails, on the contrary, to notice these strange, unsettling evil thoughts of one's own at all; but should one arouse the resolve to cultivate practice, such thoughts, on the contrary, seem to multiply all the more. At such a moment, one should picture Amitābha Buddha standing directly before oneself, and dare not allow a single scattered thought or delusive fantasy to arise, but recite the Buddha's own sacred name with utmost sincerity and earnestness. One must recite every character, every phrase, with utter clarity in the mind, with utter clarity in the mouth, and listen to it with utter clarity in the ear. Should one be able to recollect constantly in this manner, every scattered thought will naturally dissolve away of itself.

Whenever scattered thought arises, one should raise up one's own full spirit all the more, reciting the Buddha's name, refusing to let it work its mischief within one's own mind. Should one truly be able to recollect constantly in this manner, the ground of one's own intent naturally becomes pure. The moment scattered thought first arises, it is like a single man contending against ten thousand enemies — one must not allow oneself even the slightest indulgence toward it, or else it will become one's own master, and one will suffer harm at its hands instead. But should one resist with all one's might, it will instead be turned about according to one's own will — this is precisely what is meant by "turning affliction into bodhi." Should you be able constantly to resist with all your strength, using the Tathāgata's own great name of ten thousand virtues, then, in time, the mind naturally becomes pure. And once the mind has become pure, one should still continue reciting without any relaxation of effort, and karmic obstruction will thereby dissolve while wisdom opens.

One must never, under any circumstance, give rise to an impatient, hasty mind. Whether at home or in a hermitage, one must show respect to those above and harmony to those below, endure what others cannot endure, and do what others cannot do; take on others' own toil, and help complete others' own good deeds; sitting quietly, constantly reflect on one's own faults, and, in idle conversation, never discuss others' own wrongs. Whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, whether dressing or eating, from morning to evening and from evening to morning, let the single phrase of the Buddha's name never be interrupted — whether recited softly aloud, or silently. Apart from recollecting the Buddha, allow no other thought to arise; and should some delusive thought arise all the same, one must, on the spot, cause it to dissolve at once. Constantly give rise to a heart of shame, and a heart of repentance. Even should one already possess some measure of practice, always feel that one's own effort remains quite shallow, and never boast of it to oneself. Attend only to one's own affairs, not to those of others; look only at good examples, never at bad ones. Regard every other person as a bodhisattva, and oneself alone as the true ordinary being. Should you truly be able to act in accordance with everything I have told you, you are certain to be able to be born in the Western World of Utmost Bliss.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for buddha-recollection, when the mind proves difficult to unify, one should gather in the mind and recite earnestly, and it will naturally come to unity of itself. Of the methods for gathering in the mind, none comes before utmost sincerity and earnestness — without a sincere mind, there is no way at all to gather it in. Even once one is already sincere, should the mind still not yet be fully unified, one should gather in the ear and listen attentively. Whether reciting aloud or silently, in every case the recitation must arise from the mind, the sound issue from the mouth, and the tone enter through the ear. Should the mind and mouth recite with utter clarity, and the ear-faculty listen with utter clarity, gathering in the mind in this way, delusive thought naturally comes to rest of itself.

Should delusive waves still surge up regardless, one should use the "ten-count" method: the mind's entire strength is thereby directed toward a single recitation of the Buddha's name, and, though delusive thought might wish to arise, it finds no opportunity to do so. This is the ultimate, wondrous method for gathering in the mind while recollecting the Buddha — one that earlier propagators of the Pure Land had not yet spoken of, since the capacities of people in earlier ages were still sharp enough that this was unnecessary, and unity could be reached without it. It was only because I myself found the mind so difficult to subdue that I came to recognize the wonder of this method. I have tested it again and again, with proven results each time — this is no reckless conjecture on my part — and I wish to share it with every dull-natured practitioner throughout the world, in ages present and to come, so that ten thousand who cultivate it may see ten thousand attain rebirth.

As for what is meant by "the ten-count method": while recollecting the Buddha, from the first phrase to the tenth, one must recite each with clarity, and likewise keep count of each with clarity. Once the tenth phrase is reached, one begins again from the first through the tenth, never counting up to twenty or thirty. Count as you recite, without using prayer beads, relying on the mind alone to keep the count. Should counting straight through ten phrases prove difficult, one may divide it into two breaths, from one to five and from six to ten. Should even this prove effortful, one may divide it into three breaths, from one to three, four to six, and seven to ten. Reciting with clarity, counting with clarity, listening with clarity, delusive thought finds nowhere at all to gain a foothold, and one-pointedness of mind, undistracted, will in time naturally be attained.

One must understand that this "ten-count" method and the "ten recollections" of the morning liturgy, though alike in gathering in delusive thought, differ greatly in their actual technique. The morning "ten recollections" treats a single full breath as one recollection-unit, regardless of how many recitations of the Buddha's name fit within it; this present method treats a single recitation of the Buddha's name itself as one count. The former is suitable only for the ten morning recollections — should one extend it to twenty or thirty, it injures the breath and leads to illness. In this method, by contrast, one recites a single phrase of the Buddha's name, and the mind knows it as "one"; one recites ten phrases, and the mind knows them as "ten" — from one to ten, and again from one to ten — even should one recite tens of thousands of times in a single day, one keeps count in exactly this same way throughout. Not only does this dispel delusive thought — it is, moreover, exceptionally effective at nurturing one's own vital spirit. Whether recited quickly or slowly, it presents no obstruction whatsoever; from morning to evening, it suits every occasion without exception. Compared with counting on prayer beads, its benefit is vastly superior — the latter tires the body and stirs the spirit, while this method leaves the body at ease and the mind settled.

Should counting prove difficult while one is occupied with some task, one may simply recite earnestly and directly instead; once the task is finished, one resumes gathering in the mind and counting — so that even the mind's own restless comings and goings are gradually drawn together into single-pointed focus on the Buddha's name. Mahāsthāmaprāpta says: "Fully gathering in the six sense-faculties, pure thought following upon pure thought without interruption, one attains samādhi — this is foremost of all." For one of sharp faculties, this need not even be discussed; but for those of us with dull faculties, apart from this method of counting to ten, attempting to fully gather in the six sense-faculties with pure thought following pure thought without interruption is exceedingly, exceedingly difficult.

One must further understand that this method of gathering in the mind while recollecting the Buddha is a truly inconceivable dharma — shallow and yet deep at once, small and yet great at once. One need only look up to and trust in the Buddha's own word, and never, on the grounds that one's own understanding falls short, give rise to doubt about it, thereby causing roots of goodness accumulated across many kalpas to be lost midway, unable in the end to personally obtain any genuine benefit at all — a truly lamentable outcome. Counting on prayer beads is suitable only while walking or standing; should one sit quietly to nurture the spirit, the movement of the hand prevents the spirit from settling, and, in time, this leads to illness. This "ten-count" method, by contrast, suits walking, standing, sitting, and lying down alike, without exception.

Translator's note

This is Yinguang's single most famous practical contribution to Pure Land technique — the "ten-count method" (十念記數法), still taught today as one of his signature innovations, distinct from the "ten recollections" (十念) breath-counting method described in Entry 1 of this section. Note his own candor about its origin: not received tradition, but something he arrived at through his own difficulty concentrating the mind, tested repeatedly before being offered to others.
