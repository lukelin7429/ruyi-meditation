# Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen Translation Notes

These notes record recurring translation decisions for the Ru-Yi English translation of the *Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen* (釋禪波羅蜜次第法門, "Explaining the Sequential Dharma-Gates of Dhyāna-Pāramitā," Taishō T46, no. 1916). They exist so that terminology stays consistent across all ten fascicles, the way the running glossary already works for [`sutras/cheng-weishi-lun/translation-notes.md`](../cheng-weishi-lun/translation-notes.md) and the two Śūraṅgama projects.

## The text and its author

Zhiyi (智顗, 538–597 CE), posthumously honored as the Great Master of Tiantai (天台智者大師), is the founder of the Tiantai school and one of the most important systematizers in Chinese Buddhist history. This treatise is one of his three major works on meditation practice, delivered orally as lectures, recorded by his disciple Fashen (法慎), and later revised by his other major disciple Guanding (灌頂) — the same Guanding responsible for recording Zhiyi's other great meditation treatises. It runs to 10 fascicles and lays out the practice of dhyāna (禪, meditative absorption) as a *pāramitā* — a perfection to be walked through in careful, graduated stages, from the preliminary conditions for practice through to the deepest attainments and their fruits.

## Source and copyright

- Base text: Taishō T1916, via the CBETA digital edition. Zhiyi died in 597 CE — the text itself has been in the public domain for well over a millennium, under any jurisdiction's copyright term. CBETA's own transcription is distributed under a non-commercial-use license (full header preserved), consistent with how this non-profit site already uses CBETA-sourced text across every other translation project here.
- No complete English translation of this treatise is known to exist. A 2021 academic article by an Italian scholar translates a portion of fascicle 1 for scholarly analysis, not as a complete rendering of the work. If this project is completed, it will likely be the first full English translation.
- Zhiyi wrote two other, considerably shorter meditation manuals — the *Six Dharma Gates to the Sublime* (六妙法門) and the *Concise Śamatha-Vipaśyanā* / *童蒙止觀* (also known as the *Xiao Zhiguan* 小止觀) — both already available in published English translation by Bhikshu Dharmamitra (Kalavinka Press). This treatise is a distinct, much longer work, not a variant of either of those two. Because Dharmamitra's translations of Zhiyi's related shorter texts already exist and are well known, we take particular care here to translate independently from the Chinese source and not to consult, echo, or fall into the phrasing of those published translations — this project's English wording, sentence structure, and section framing must be its own throughout.

## Structure

- CBETA's edition of this treatise splits several of the ten fascicles into "upper" and "lower" halves (之上 / 之下) for print-volume reasons; both halves belong to the same fascicle and are treated as one continuous unit on this site, matching how the fascicle is actually organized by content.
- Zhiyi structures the entire treatise around ten framing topics (十意), stated at the very opening of fascicle 1: (1) the great intent of cultivating dhyāna-pāramitā, (2) explaining its name, (3) its gates, (4) the sequence of its exposition, (5) the states of mind proper to its method, (6) its preliminary expedient means — including the famous twenty-five preliminary conditions (二十五方便) — (7) cultivation and realization, (8) fruits and rewards, (9) the teachings arising from it, and (10) where it all ultimately leads. We keep this outline as the page structure, the way Kuiji's 科判 organizes the *Cheng Weishi Lun* pages.
- Confirmed from the source text: fascicle 1 (both its upper and lower halves) contains only topics 1 through 5 in full. Topics 6 through 10 — including the twenty-five preliminary conditions — belong to fascicle 2 and beyond; do not assume the "十意" outline maps one-to-one onto fascicle boundaries when planning future fascicle pages.

| 假名 | provisional name | prajñapti. Topic 5's conclusion about how dharma and mind can still be meaningfully distinguished even though neither is, under analysis, a fixed, findable thing. |
| 摩訶止觀 / 止觀 | the *Great Calming and Contemplation* | *Mo-ho Chih-kuan*, Zhiyi's other, much longer treatise on meditation — cited three times in topic 5 as the place where the "provisional realities" analysis is given in full. ### Fascicle 2 onward — the twenty-five preliminary conditions

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 前方便 | the preliminary expedient means | Topic 6's own subject; opens into external (外方便) and internal (內方便) categories. |
| 外方便 / 內方便 | external expedient means / internal expedient means | External: methods of using the mind outside concentration. Internal: methods of using the mind within concentration. |
| 二十五方便 | the twenty-five preliminary conditions | The treatise's celebrated five-groups-of-five structure: 具五緣 (being complete in the five conditions), 訶五欲 (rejecting the five desires), 棄五蓋 (abandoning the five hindrances), 調五法 (harmonizing the five faculties), 行五法 (practicing the five dharmas). Fascicle 2 begins with the first group. |
| 具五緣 | being complete in the five conditions | The first group of five: pure moral discipline, being complete in robes and food, dwelling in a quiet place, setting worldly affairs to rest, and obtaining a good spiritual friend. Condition 1 (moral discipline) alone occupies the whole of fascicle 2 so far. |
| 持戒清淨 | pure moral discipline | Condition 1 of the five conditions; itself opens into three parts — 明有戒無戒 (the presence or absence of precepts), 持犯 (upholding and violation), and 懺悔 (repentance). |
| 有戒 / 無戒 | having precepts / lacking precepts | The first of pure moral discipline's three parts. |
| 十種得戒 | the ten ways precepts are received | Ten classical Vinaya precedents, from the Buddha's own spontaneous attainment through ordination-by-messenger and the five/ten-person quorum rules for border versus central regions. |
| 無作（戒） | non-action | avijñapti. The Hīnayāna account of what precepts fundamentally are: a wholesome quality, brought into being at ordination, that continues functioning spontaneously without further deliberate mental effort — contested across schools (Sarvāstivādins: unmanifest form; Dharmaguptakas: a third category, neither form nor mind). |
| 戒體 | the essence of the precepts | The technical question of what precepts actually consist in; topic 6 contrasts the Hīnayāna's "non-action" account with the Mahāyāna's identification of the precepts with the wholesome mind itself. |
| 七眾 | the seven assemblies | The seven ordination categories: upāsaka, upāsikā (lay man, lay woman), śrāmaṇera, śrāmaṇerī (male, female novice), śikṣamāṇā (female probationer), bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī (fully ordained monk, nun). |
| 羯磨 | the formal procedure | karman. The ritual/legal act of conferring ordination or other saṅgha business; left as "the formal procedure" in translated prose rather than transliterated, since the text uses it as a functional term rather than discussing it technically (contrast terms like "dhyāna" or "pāramitā" that the treatise itself explains). |
| 持犯 | upholding and violation | The second of condition 1's three parts: the mark of properly maintaining the precepts (持) versus transgressing them (犯), extensively distinguished into ten grades from beginner to buddhahood. |
| 十種持戒 | the ten kinds of upholding | Condition 1's ladder of precept-observance, from the four grave precepts through the bodhisattva's mastery-free-of-self-grasping; grouped as worldly (the first four), world-transcending (the middle two), and world-transcending-and-beyond (the last four). |
| 覆發 | concealment and manifestation | The relationship between precept-observance and whether dhyāna is "brought forth" or "concealed"; qualified by a fourfold classification acknowledging that present-life observance and present-life dhyāna experience do not always track each other exactly, owing to causes from past lives. |
| 懺悔 | repentance | The third of condition 1's three parts, and the treatise's richest passage so far: setting the mind of repentance in motion, three distinct methods of increasing depth, grades of severance, and whether restoration to the original state is complete. |
| 作法懺悔 | repentance through formal procedure | The Hīnayāna-associated method: a defined communal ritual (羯磨) that purifies the offense obstructing the path, without requiring any visionary confirmation. |
| 觀相懺悔 | repentance through the contemplation of signs | The method supported by concentration: repentance confirmed by seeing a favorable sign (the Buddha touching the crown of the head, light, flowers, etc.) within a stilled mind; the text is notably cautious about distinguishing genuine signs from demonic interference. |
| 觀無生懺悔 | repentance through the contemplation of no-arising | The method supported by wisdom, and "the most honored, the most sublime" of the three: a sustained analysis of where "mind" (and therefore offense) could possibly be located — past, future, present, inside, outside, in between — concluding that offense's root-nature is empty and ever pure. Closely parallels fascicle 1 topic 5's tetralemma against "contaminated dharma." |
| 普賢觀經 | the *Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra* | 觀普賢菩薩行法經, quoted extensively in the repentance-through-no-arising passage; source of the treatise's most-quoted verse in this section ("The whole ocean of every karmic obstruction arises entirely from deluded thought..."). |
| 三品罪 | the three grades of offense | Procedural (違無作), essential-nature (體性), and root-ignorance (無明煩惱根本) — mapped one-to-one onto the three methods of repentance, each cutting one layer deeper than the last. |
| 訶五欲 | rejecting the five desires | The second group of the twenty-five preliminary conditions: form, sound, smell, taste, and touch (色/聲/香/味/觸), each illustrated with a cautionary story. |
| 棄五蓋 | abandoning the five hindrances | The third group: the hindrances of desire (貪欲蓋), anger (瞋恚蓋), sleep (睡眠蓋), restlessness-and-regret (掉悔蓋), and doubt (疑蓋) — nīvaraṇa, foundational across nearly every later Buddhist meditation tradition. Mapped onto the three poisons plus the "equal-portion" affliction, explaining the traditional count of 84,000 afflictions (21,000 × 4). |
| 三疑 | the three doubts | Doubt about oneself, about one's teacher, and about the Dharma (疑自/疑師/疑法) — the specific subset of doubt (out of doubt in general) that the treatise identifies as actually obstructing concentration. |
| 調五法 | harmonizing the five things | The fourth group, and the treatise's most concrete practical instruction so far: regulating food (調食), sleep (調睡眠), and — treated together — body, breath, and mind (調身、調息、調心) across the three phases of entering (入), abiding in (住), and emerging from (出) meditation. |
| 風 / 喘 / 氣 / 息 | wind / panting / breath-with-effort / true breath | The four grades of breath given in the body-breath-mind instruction, the first three unharmonized and the last (息) the harmonized mark — a foundational fourfold scheme for breath-based meditation instruction generally. |
| 半跏 / 全跏 | the half-lotus / the full-lotus posture | Given with specific, practical instructions for leg and hand placement in the "entering meditation" passage. |
| 沈 / 浮 | sinking / floating | The two basic failure-modes of an unharmonized meditating mind, each paired with its own remedy (fixing attention at the tip of the nose for sinking; directing the mind downward for floating). |
| 行五法 | practicing the five dharmas | The fifth and final group: aspiration (欲), diligence (精進), mindfulness (念), skillful wisdom (巧慧), and one-pointedness of mind (一心) — closing topic 6 in its entirety. |
| 十二頭陀 | the twelve dhutanga practices | Ascetic observances (forest-dwelling, alms-begging, a single daily meal, discarded robes, dwelling among graves, never lying down, and the rest) cited under "diligence" as the paradigm of complete bodily and mental effort. |
| 六行觀 | the six contemplations | A repurposed Abhidharma scheme (growing weary of the lower realm's suffering/coarseness/obstruction; reaching for the higher realm's superiority/subtlety/transcendence) applied here to moving from the desire realm into the first dhyāna, explained under "mindfulness" in both fruit-terms and cause-terms. |

### Fascicle 3 onward — the internal expedient means

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 內方便 | the internal expedient means | Topic 6's second half (fascicle 3 onward), opening into five layers: the gate of stopping (止門), verifying wholesome/unwholesome capacity (驗善惡根性), methods for settling the mind (安心法), treating illness (治病患), and recognizing demonic affairs (覺魔事). |
| 止門 | the gate of stopping | Śamatha; taken as the first of the five internal-expedient-means layers because, per the treatise, every dhyāna's merit depends on restraining the mind. Has four parts: distinguishing its kinds, its great intent, its method of cultivation, and the marks that verify it. |
| 繫緣止 | fixed-object stopping | The first, coarsest of the three kinds of stopping (in terms of practice): fixing the mind on a physical point (the nose, navel, etc.) to prevent it from wandering. |
| 制心止 | restraining-the-mind stopping | The second, middle kind: restraining discursive thought directly at its arising, rather than via an external object. |
| 體真止 | embodying-truth stopping | The third, subtlest kind: embodying the emptiness of all dharmas directly, described as "a stopping that stops nothing" — the deepest of the three, each breaking through the one before it as antidote. |

### Fascicle 4 — verifying unwholesome capacity, settling the mind, illness, and demonic affairs

Confirmed from the source text: fascicle 4 covers the whole of topic 6's remainder in one continuous sweep — verifying unwholesome capacity (明驗惡根性, the second half of the layer fascicle 3 began), settling the mind (明安心法), treating illness (明治病患), and recognizing demonic affairs (明覺魔事) — closing the internal expedient means, topic 6, and the twenty-five preliminary conditions entirely. ### Fascicle 5 — cultivation and realization: the four dhyānas

Confirmed from the source text: fascicle 5 opens topic 7 (修證, cultivation and realization), itself framed in four parts (mundane dhyāna; both mundane and world-transcending dhyāna; world-transcending dhyāna; neither mundane nor world-transcending dhyāna). Fascicle 5 covers only the first part's first section: the four dhyānas in full. The four immeasurable minds and four formless concentrations (mundane dhyāna's remaining two sections) are not yet reached. Fascicle 5 does not split into upper/lower halves.

### Fascicle 6 — the four immeasurable minds and the four formless concentrations

Confirmed from the source text: fascicle 6 covers mundane dhyāna's remaining two sections in full — the four immeasurable minds (四無量心) and the four formless concentrations (四無色定) — closing mundane dhyāna (世間禪), topic 7's first of four major divisions, entirely. A brief aside on the non-Buddhist concentration of no-thought (無想定) sits between the two. Fascicle 6 does not split into upper/lower halves.

|---|---|---|
| 亦有漏亦無漏禪 | dhyāna that is both contaminated and uncontaminated | Topic 7's second of four major divisions (after mundane dhyāna, fascicles 5–6). Taught through three dharma-gates matched to practitioner capacity: the six wondrous gates (wisdom-heavy), the sixteen special excellences (concentration-heavy), and the thorough-and-bright contemplation (balanced). |
| 六妙門 | the six wondrous gates | Counting, following, stilling, contemplating, returning, and purifying (數隨止觀還淨) — already named as Zhiyi's own separate, shorter treatise (see source-and-copyright note) and used as fascicle 5's own breath-cultivation method for the first dhyāna; here given full treatment for the first time, opened into twelve gates (cultivating and corresponding-with, for each of the six). The first three are concentration-dharmas, the last three wisdom-dharmas. Their own sequence of stages is explicitly not fixed — able to bring forth full uncontaminated realization already within the desire realm. Identified as what the Buddha himself used beneath the bodhi tree, and as "the Mahāyāna itself" when fully realized. |
| 三種觀 | the three kinds of contemplation | Introduced at 六妙門's own 修觀 stage: 慧行觀 (wisdom-conduct contemplation, direct insight into the true), 得解觀 (contemplation of understanding attained, i.e. guided visualization — retroactively naming what fascicle 3's nine contemplations and liberations actually are), and 實觀 (true contemplation, seeing what is actually present without imaginative construction). All three named methods in this fascicle rely on true contemplation first, adding wisdom-conduct contemplation only afterward to reach principle. |
| 先尼梵志 | the wandering ascetic Senika | Cited for his own formula ("not through inward contemplation... not through outward contemplation... and yet not without any contemplation either") as a compact parallel to the treatise's own tetralemma-style analysis of contemplation itself. |
| 十六特勝 | the sixteen special excellences | Sixteen items (knowing the breath entering/leaving/length-shortness/pervading-the-body, removing bodily formation, receiving joy/happiness/mental formations, the mind making joy/restraint/liberation, and contemplating impermanence/dispersal/freedom-from-desire/cessation/relinquishment). Taught, per the Vinaya, as a safer replacement for the contemplation of impurity for practitioners whose craving is slight but self-directed disgust runs deep enough to risk self-harm. Possesses both concentration and contemplation together, avoiding both the fault of self-harm and attachment to any single dhyāna. |
| 竪對諸禪 | the vertical correspondence to the various dhyānas | The treatise's preferred of two competing schemes for the sixteen special excellences (over a rival scheme mapping them onto the four foundations of mindfulness): each of the sixteen items corresponds to one specific stage, in sequence, from counting the breath through neither perception nor non-perception — items 1–2 to counting the breath, 3 to desire-realm concentration, 4 to the "not-yet-arrived" state, 5–8 to the first dhyāna's five factors, 9–10 to the second dhyāna, 11 to the third, 12 to the fourth, and 13–16 to the four formless concentrations in turn. |
| 根本禪 / 淨禪 | the root dhyāna / the pure dhyāna | Distinguished at the special excellences' fifth item: the same five-factor structure as an ordinary, dhyāna, or as a "pure" dhyāna once contemplative wisdom is layered onto each factor (perceiving its own emptiness rather than receiving it with attachment) — the technical basis for how the special excellences make each dhyāna "both contaminated and uncontaminated" at once. |
| 須跋陀羅 | Subhadra | Traditionally the Buddha's last personal convert before his parinirvāṇa, cited as the example of awakening through the sixteenth special excellence (contemplating relinquishment) — attaining arhatship by contemplating the subtle perception within neither-perception-nor-non-perception. The text notes awakening need not require all sixteen items in sequence; some awaken through only two or three. |

### Fascicle 8 — the thorough-and-bright contemplation

Confirmed from the source text: fascicle 8 covers the third and final dharma-gate of dhyāna both mundane and world-transcending (亦有漏亦無漏禪) in full — the thorough-and-bright contemplation (通明觀) — closing that entire second division of topic 7. It opens with the gate's naming and sequence of stages, then works the first dhyāna through three "worlds" in exhaustive detail (root, meaning, and witnessed phenomena) before treating the second through fourth dhyānas and the four formless attainments more briefly by direct sutra citation. Fascicle 8 does not split into upper/lower halves.

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 通明觀 | the thorough-and-bright contemplation | The third of three dharma-gates for dhyāna both mundane and world-transcending, matched to practitioners whose concentration and wisdom are equally balanced. Named by northern dhyāna masters (not found as a fixed term in the sūtras themselves) since it fit no prior category: "thorough" for its simultaneous contemplation of breath, form, and mind together; "bright" for the three insights it yields. Distinguished from the sixteen special excellences by contemplating all three objects at once from the very start, rather than the breath alone. |
| 三事 / 三性 (息色心) | the three matters / the three natures — breath, form, mind | The thorough-and-bright contemplation's own object throughout: breath, form (the body), and mind, observed together as a single field rather than in sequence, each in turn found to be, in the treatise's own term, "suchness" (如). |
| 根本世間 / 義世間 / 事世間 | the root world / the world of meaning / the world of witnessed phenomena | The three-part framework structuring this fascicle's entire treatment of the first dhyāna: the root world is the meditator's own physical body, contemplated directly (pores, the thirty-six-substance list, internal organs, vessels); the world of meaning maps that same body onto both the Buddhadharma's own doctrinal categories and indigenous Chinese correlative cosmology (five phases, trigrams, the body as royal court); the world of witnessed phenomena is what becomes directly visible through the five supernormal powers, obtained either spontaneously or through cultivation. Each of the three is worked through the same three-grade (lower/middle/upper) 覺-and-大覺 ("initial thought" and "great initial thought") pattern. |
| 八相 | the eight marks (of conditioned existence) | Applied in this fascicle to the root world's own factor of initial thought: birth, dwelling, and their respective subtle counterparts, together with the four marks of decay/death paired at each grade — used as the basis for a tetralemma-style refutation of whether mind is identical to or separate from the eight marks (via a fire-and-firewood simile). |
| 提謂經 | the Sūtra of Trapusa | Cited twice in this fascicle's world-of-meaning section as scriptural source for the body-as-cosmos and body-as-royal-court correspondences (the five organs mapped onto planets, sacred peaks, the four directional guardian creatures, and the royal court's own ranked officials) — anticipating, via a citation from the Vimalakīrti Sūtra, the objection that this resembles a non-Buddhist soul doctrine. |
| 法行比丘 | the dharma-practicing monk | The exemplary figure in the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra's own account (quoted at length in this fascicle) of obtaining each of the five supernormal powers in turn — body, eye, ear, others'-minds, and past-lives — simply by continuing to watch the breath entering and leaving, all the way from the first dhyāna through the fourth. |
| 空處 / 識處 / 少識處 / 非想非非想 | the sphere of space / the sphere of consciousness / the lesser sphere of consciousness / neither-perception-nor-non-perception | The four formless attainments as treated in this fascicle via direct Mahāsaṃnipāta citation: each stage releases the previous stage's own object as still "coarse," down to perception itself in the fourth — with the fourth alone said to be unable to be exhausted by the mundane path, hence an ordinary person can mistake its stillness for nirvāṇa. |
| 摩訶衍 | the Mahāyāna | The fascicle's own closing frame, echoing fascicle 7's identical move for the six wondrous gates: a bodhisattva who masters the thorough-and-bright contemplation does not stop at its own "final proof," but turns the resulting six supernormal powers outward, toward delivering other beings — the same single dharma-gate yielding a different fruit depending on the vehicle of the one who practices it. |

### Fascicle 9 — the nine contemplations, eight recollections, and ten perceptions

Confirmed from the source text: fascicle 9 covers the destructive path (壞法道) in full — the first of two paths belonging to dhyāna that is purely world-transcending (出世間禪), topic 7's third major division. It opens with the two-fold distinction between the undefiled that counteracts affliction (對治無漏) and the undefiled that takes principle as its object (緣理無漏), then translates all three dharma-gates of the destructive path: the nine contemplations, the eight recollections, and the ten perceptions. Fascicle 9 does not split into upper/lower halves.

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 對治無漏 / 緣理無漏 | the undefiled that counteracts / the undefiled that takes principle as its object | The two kinds of undefiled cultivation opening topic 7's third major division, attributed to the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra's own distinction between "practice-practice" (行行, taking phenomena as its object) and "wisdom-practice" (慧行, taking principle as its object and directly contemplating the Four Noble Truths and dependent origination). Fascicle 9 covers only the former, via nine dharma-gates. |
| 壞法道 / 不壞法道 | the destructive path / the non-destructive path | The two paths within the undefiled that counteracts. The destructive path (nine contemplations, eight recollections, ten perceptions — fascicle 9 in full) destroys the mark of the desire-realm body so thoroughly that the practitioner cannot go on to master the three realms' own contemplation-refinement-permeation-cultivation, three insights, or eight liberations — a narrower, faster route to arhatship. The non-destructive path (eight forms of detachment, eight stations of mastery, ten totalities, nine sequential concentrations, lion's-frolic samādhi, samādhi of transcendence) preserves that capacity and is reserved for fascicle 10. |
| 九想 | the nine contemplations | The charnel-ground meditation on a corpse's decay: swelling, decay, being smeared with blood, festering, livid discoloration, devouring, scattering, bones, and burning (脹/壞/血塗/膿爛/青瘀/噉/散/骨/燒). Preceded by a preliminary death-contemplation with two framing verses, and instructed to be practiced specifically on "a person one loves" for maximum effect. Matched item-by-item to six specific desires it counteracts (color, form, demeanor, speech, touch, and attachment to "the human mark" itself). Classified under the desire realm and first/second dhyānas (bones extending through the fourth). Closes with a citation from the Mahāprajñāpāramitā Sūtra naming the nine contemplations "the bodhisattva's own Mahāyāna." |
| 六欲 | the six desires | Desire for color, form and appearance, demeanor and bearing, speech and voice, smooth touch, and the human mark itself (色欲/形貌欲/威儀姿態欲/言語音聲欲/細滑欲/人相欲) — the taxonomy of attachment the nine contemplations are designed to counteract, with attachment to "the human mark" (a specific person, regardless of their other qualities) singled out as the most stubborn of the six. |
| 八念 | the eight recollections | Recollection of the Buddha, Dharma, Saṃgha, the precepts, relinquishment, the heavens, the breath (ānāpāna), and death (念佛/念法/念僧/念戒/念捨/念天/念入出息/念死). Taught as the direct sequel to the nine contemplations, specifically to remove the fear (including demonic interference) that following the charnel-ground meditation tends to provoke. Organized via a sustained medical simile (Buddha as physician, Dharma as medicine, Saṃgha as caregiver, and so on) for sequential cultivation, but each recollection is also said to independently open the path on its own. |
| 十想 | the ten perceptions | Impermanence, suffering, no-self, the impurity of food, that nothing in the world is to be delighted in, death, impurity, cessation, detachment, and exhaustion (無常想/苦想/無我想/食不淨想/一切世間不可樂想/死想/不淨想/斷想/離想/盡想). Mapped onto the three paths of seeing, cultivation, and no-more-learning respectively (first three / next four / last three), with three separate approaches to cultivating them (strictly sequential, front-loaded from the very start, or any single perception alone). Completing all ten, for a practitioner of the destructive path, yields arhatship complete with both nirvāṇa-with-remainder and nirvāṇa-without-remainder. |
| 三種道 | the three paths | The path of seeing, the path of cultivation, and the path of no-more-learning (見道/修道/無學道) — the sequence-of-stages framework used to distinguish the ten perceptions' own three groups, reused throughout Abhidharma-style analysis across the treatise. |
| 三相 | the three marks | Arising, abiding, and ceasing (生/住/滅) — the marks cited as constituting impermanence, then, in the same passage, argued to be themselves unfindable (arising cannot simultaneously possess abiding-and-ceasing without contradiction) — a compact tetralemma-style argument that impermanence itself is not to be reified as any kind of "real" ground. |
| 死想 (佛說死想義) | the Buddha's own teaching on the perception of death | The dialogue in which the Buddha rejects monks' claims to cultivate death-awareness at intervals of a year down through a single meal as "negligent," accepting only the monk who does not presume upon the next in-breath following an out-breath — reusing ānāpāna, the breath-counting method anchoring the treatise since fascicle 3, as the standard for genuine mindfulness of death. |

### Fascicle 10 — the non-destructive path; the treatise's final extant fascicle

Confirmed from the source text: fascicle 10 covers the non-destructive path (不壞法道) in full, closing dhyāna that is purely world-transcending (出世間禪), topic 7's third major division. This is the final fascicle of the treatise as it has survived — Zhiyi's own ten-topic outline, announced in fascicle 1, is never completed in the received Taishō text (T46, no. 1916), which ends here with its own closing line acknowledging the dharma-gates are without limit. Fascicle 10 does not split into upper/lower halves.

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 觀練熏修 | contemplation, refinement, permeation, cultivation | The four-part framework organizing this fascicle's six dharma-gates: 觀禪 (contemplative dhyāna — detachment, the stations of mastery, the totalities), 鍊禪 (refining dhyāna — the nine sequential concentrations), 熏禪 (permeating dhyāna — the lion's-frolic samādhi), 修禪 (cultivating dhyāna — the samādhi of transcendence). A metallurgical logic distinct from the destructive path's outright breaking of the body's mark: each stage works the same material more thoroughly than the last, like successive tempering of a single piece of metal. |
| 八背捨 | the eight forms of detachment | Form within contemplating form without / no form within contemplating form without / the pure detachment realized in the body / the detachments of space, consciousness, nothingness, neither-perception-nor-non-perception, and cessation of sensation and perception. Reprises the nine contemplations' charnel-ground imagery before extending it: the skeleton itself is refined until it radiates eight colors of light (earth, water, fire, wind, blue-green, yellow, red, white), mapped onto the four elements and four hues. Distinguished from "liberation" (解脫) — the same eight stages, but renamed only once the fetters are entirely exhausted, not merely weakened. |
| 四義分別淨不淨 | the four-fold impure/pure analysis | 不淨不淨 (impure-impure), 不淨淨 (impure-pure), 淨不淨 (pure-impure), 淨淨 (pure-pure) — the framework distinguishing why the third detachment alone earns the name "pure," despite pure light being present from the first detachment onward: the light's origin, object, and degree of refinement must each, in turn, be purified before the name is earned. |
| 十種細心數法 | the ten subtle mental factors | Sensation, perception, formation, contact, intention, desire, resolve, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom (受/想/行/觸/思/欲/解/念/定/慧) — present even within neither-perception-nor-non-perception despite its name, and each, in turn, extinguished in the eighth detachment (the cessation of sensation and perception), including, at the very end, the concentration and wisdom that were doing the contemplating. |
| 八勝處 | the eight stations of mastery | Form within/without, little/much, good/ill (four stations), plus the four color stations (blue-green, yellow, red, white) at the fourth dhyāna. Adds mastering the contemplating mind itself to the eight forms of detachment's own breaking of attachment — "like a rider on horseback striking at a bandit," who must also master the horse. Second station of mastery extends the visualization to a world filled with corpses; an alternate master's account pushes this further still (the "great contemplation of impurity," extending to animals, food, wealth, land — a deliberate, acknowledged distortion used as a corrective, not a claim about literal reality). |
| 十一切處 / 十一切入 | the ten totalities / the ten totality-entries | Blue-green, yellow, red, white, earth, water, fire, wind, space, consciousness (青/黃/赤/白/地/水/火/風/空/識) — a single color or element expanded, starting from an object the size of a blade of grass, until it fills the entire visualized world without moving. Totality-entries are the further refinement of causing multiple totalities to interpenetrate (e.g. seeing yellow, red, and white all present within blue-green) without destroying each other's own mark. The one dharma-gate in this fascicle not given a separate "approach to the path" treatment, since śrāvaka tradition treats it chiefly as a method for supernormal powers. |
| 六神通 | the six supernormal powers | The heavenly eye, heavenly ear, knowledge of others' minds, knowledge of past lives, mastery over the body at will, and the power of exhausting the contaminants (天眼/天耳/他心/宿命/如意/漏盡通). Obtained through one of three causes: retribution (past merit), spontaneous arising (through deep concentration alone), or deliberate cultivation (directly severing the specific ignorance blocking each power). |
| 十四變化 | the fourteen transformations | Each dhyāna stage (first through fourth) transforming into itself and into every stage below it, but never upward — yielding 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 14 transformations in total. The treatise explicitly declines to explain the mechanism further, calling the matter too subtle for "mere written words." |
| 九次第定 | the nine sequential concentrations | The four dhyānas, four formless concentrations, and the cessation of sensation and perception, entered in sequence "mind following mind without any gap." Also named the refining dhyāna (鍊禪), explicitly distinguished from the Abhidharma tradition's narrower version (which refines only the fourth dhyāna, to prevent regression) — here, every stage from the first dhyāna through neither-perception-nor-non-perception is refined, "like the refining of gold." The two-wheeled-cart and unbalanced-blade similes explain why concentration and wisdom must be equally matched for entry without gaps. |
| 三三昧 | the three samadhis | The samādhi possessing initial thought and sustained reflection / without initial thought but with sustained reflection / without either (有覺有觀/無覺有觀/無覺無觀三昧) — not a separate practice but the entire preceding catalogue of dhyānas sorted into three bins by which of these two factors is present. Etymology given for samādhi itself: "the place where the mind moves correctly," a naturally crooked mind straightened the way a snake is straightened by entering a bamboo tube. |
| 師子奮迅三昧 | the lion's-frolic samādhi | Ascending through all nine sequential concentrations and then reversing direction at the peak, descending back through the same sequence without a gap — named for the lion's unique capacity to leap backward as fluidly as forward. Also named the permeating dhyāna (熏禪), likened to an ox-hide worked until pliant enough to be shaped into anything. |
| 超越三昧 | the samādhi of transcendence | Leaping directly between non-adjacent stages (e.g. first dhyāna straight to neither-perception-nor-non-perception) and alternating with "the scattered mind" (ordinary waking consciousness) as one more station in the sequence. Also named the cultivating dhyāna (修禪). The yellow-lion/white-lion simile distinguishes śrāvaka attainment (able to leap only from the first to the third dhyāna) from bodhisattva attainment (able to leap all the way to the cessation of sensation and perception in a single bound) of the identical samādhi. |
| 大力阿羅漢 | the great-strength arhat | The non-destructive path's own culminating fruit: liberation-in-both-respects (phenomena and principle both complete), complete with the three insights, six supernormal powers, and eight liberations — contrasted with the destructive path's arhat of fascicle 9, complete instead with the two kinds of nirvāṇa alone. |
| 二十五三昧 / 禪波羅蜜滿 | the twenty-five samadhis / the fulfillment of the dhyāna-pāramitā | The bodhisattva's own culminating attainment, breaking through the twenty-five states of existence, abiding in the king of samādhis — the treatise's own closing image for what the entire ten-fascicle project has been building toward. |

## Register

- Plain English, close to the Chinese, not a loose paraphrase — matching the method already used for the *Cheng Weishi Lun* and the two Śūraṅgama projects on this site.
- Zhiyi's prose in this fascicle moves through tightly reasoned enumerations (the ten wrong motivations, the ten pāramitās argument) — keep the enumerated structure explicit in translation (numbered or clearly parallel clauses) rather than smoothing it into continuous narrative prose.
- Mark every fascicle's status honestly. Fascicle 1 runs to roughly 14,000 Chinese characters across its upper and lower halves and contains topics 1 through 5 of Zhiyi's ten-topic outline in full (topics 6–10 belong to later fascicles) — a fascicle page may still cover only part of a fascicle at a time for texts further ahead. Label each page clearly with exactly how much of the fascicle it covers, rather than implying more is finished than actually is.

Fascicles 1 through 10 are complete — the whole of the treatise as it survives. Fascicle 1: the front matter and all five of its topics (修禪波羅蜜大意, 釋禪波羅蜜名, 明禪波羅蜜門, 辨禪波羅蜜詮次, and 簡禪波羅蜜法心), spanning both its upper and lower halves (卷第一之上 and 卷第一之下).
