Cultivation and Realization: The Four Dhyānas

This page presents Ru-Yi's own English translation of fascicle 5 of Zhiyi's Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen (釋禪波羅蜜次第法門). Having finished the preliminary expedient means in fascicle 4, the treatise turns to topic 7 — cultivation and realization — and opens with its largest and most concrete section yet: a full account of the four dhyānas themselves, from the breath-counting method that opens the first dhyāna through the marks, factors, and merits of all four, in the kind of hands-on detail the preliminary chapters were building toward.

Explaining Dhyāna-Pāramitā's Cultivation and Realization, Topic 7, Part 1

Cultivation and Realization 修證

Chinese Source

上已廣明內外方便，行者若能專心修習，繫念禪門，必有證驗，是故第七、廣明修證。故經言：「修我法者，證乃自知。」今明修證中，自開為四：第一、修證世間禪相；第二、修證亦世間亦出世間禪相；第三、修證出世間禪相；第四、修證非世間非出世間禪相。今第一、釋修證世間禪者，則為三：一、四禪；二、四無量心；三、四無色定。今前釋修證四禪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Above, the external and internal expedient means have already been clarified at length; if the practitioner is able to apply the mind single-mindedly in cultivation, holding the dhyāna-gates in mind, realization is bound to follow — hence topic 7 now clarifies cultivation and realization at length. Hence the sūtra says: "One who cultivates my Dharma comes to know its realization for oneself."

Here, within clarifying cultivation and realization, this opens of itself into four parts: first, the marks of cultivating and realizing mundane dhyāna; second, the marks of cultivating and realizing dhyāna that is both mundane and world-transcending; third, the marks of cultivating and realizing world-transcending dhyāna; and fourth, the marks of cultivating and realizing dhyāna that is neither mundane nor world-transcending.

Here, the first is now explained: clarifying the cultivation and realization of mundane dhyāna. This itself has three parts: the four dhyānas; the four immeasurable minds; and the four formless concentrations. Here, the first is now explained: the cultivation and realization of the four dhyānas.

四禪者：一、初禪；二、二禪；三、三禪；四、四禪。今論色界根本正定，但說有四。若通方便中間，是則不定。若薩婆多人，說有未到地及中間禪，足四禪為六地定。若曇無德人，例不說有未來禪，而說有欲界定、中間禪，以為六地定。若《摩訶衍》及瞿沙所明，則具有欲界、未到地、中間禪，足四禪為七地定。此中融會，以義推之。今據正禪而論，但說有四：

The four dhyānas are: the first dhyāna; the second dhyāna; the third dhyāna; and the fourth dhyāna. Here, discussing the root, correct concentrations of the form realm, only four are given. If the preliminary expedient means and the intermediate dhyāna are included as well, the count is not fixed. The Sarvāstivādins hold that there is, in addition, the "not-yet-arrived" state and the intermediate dhyāna, making six stages of concentration together with the four dhyānas. The Dharmaguptakas, by contrast, do not speak of a "not-yet-arrived" state at all, but instead speak of desire-realm concentration and the intermediate dhyāna, likewise making six stages. The Mahāyāna treatise and the master Ghoṣa, however, clarify that desire-realm concentration, the "not-yet-arrived" state, and the intermediate dhyāna are all three present together, making seven stages together with the four dhyānas. These accounts may be reconciled by reasoning through their sense. Here, discussing strictly the correct dhyānas themselves, only four are given.

Reading note: This opening is a methodological throat-clearing before the real work begins: different Abhidharma schools (Sarvāstivādin, Dharmaguptaka) and the Mahāyāna treatise count the preliminary stages before the "real" four dhyānas differently — six stages, or seven — purely as a matter of whether the "not-yet-arrived" state and the intermediate dhyāna between the first and second are counted as their own stages. The treatise sets this scholastic dispute aside deliberately, choosing to organize its own account strictly around the four classical dhyānas themselves, which is what the rest of this fascicle, and the most concrete, hands-on material in the entire treatise so far, is actually going to walk through in full.

The First Dhyāna: Its Name 初禪 · 釋名

Chinese Source

第一、釋初禪修證，如經偈說：
離欲及惡法，有覺并有觀，
離生及喜樂，是人入初禪。
已得離婬火，則獲清涼定，
如人大熱悶，入冷池則樂。
如貧得寶藏，大喜覺動心。
分別則為觀，入初禪亦然。
佛此偈中具明修證初禪之相，但意難見，今當分別。就明初禪中，開為三別：第一、釋名；第二、明修習；第三、明證相。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, clarifying the cultivation and realization of the first dhyāna, as the sūtra verse says:
"Free of desire and unwholesome dharmas, possessing initial thought and sustained reflection,
Free of birth [in the desire realm], with joy and happiness — such a person enters the first dhyāna.
Having already gained freedom from the fire of lust, one obtains a cool, refreshing concentration,
Like a person greatly overheated and distressed, entering a cool pool and finding joy.
Like a poor person finding a buried treasure, great joy stirs and moves the mind.
Discrimination is what is called sustained reflection; entering the first dhyāna is exactly the same."
The Buddha, within this verse, clarifies in full the marks of cultivating and realizing the first dhyāna, though its intent is difficult to see at first; this shall now be distinguished. In clarifying the first dhyāna, this opens into three distinct parts: explaining the name; clarifying its cultivation; and clarifying the marks of its realization.

第一、釋名者，所言初禪者，禪名支林。行者初得支林之法，故名初禪。復次，覺觀等法，名之為支。行者修初禪覺觀之法，必於前發，故說覺觀名為初禪。問曰：若言在前發得，故為初禪者，欲界、未到地最在前發，何故不得受初禪之名？答：禪名功德叢林，欲界、未到等未有支林功德之法，雖復前發，不名初禪。復次，《摩訶衍》說欲界、未到、中間，智多而定少，是處非樂。既非正地，是故不得受初禪之名。

First, explaining the name: as for what is called the first dhyāna, "dhyāna" here means a grove of factors. The practitioner, first attaining this dharma of a grove of factors, is thus said to attain the first dhyāna. Furthermore, dharmas such as initial thought and sustained reflection are what is called a "factor." The practitioner's cultivation of the first dhyāna's dharma of initial thought and sustained reflection necessarily arises first, before the others — hence initial thought and sustained reflection are said to give the first dhyāna its name.

Question: if it is said that whatever arises first is thereby named "first dhyāna," then desire-realm concentration and the "not-yet-arrived" state arise earliest of all — why, then, do they not receive the name "first dhyāna"? Answer: "dhyāna" means a thicket of merit; desire-realm concentration and the "not-yet-arrived" state do not yet have the dharma of a grove of factors' merit — although they do arise first, they are not named "first dhyāna." Furthermore, the Mahāyāna treatise says that desire-realm concentration, the "not-yet-arrived" state, and the intermediate dhyāna have much wisdom but little concentration, and this state is not one of true happiness; since it is not yet the correct stage, it therefore does not receive the name "first dhyāna."

復次，言初禪者，亦名有覺有觀三昧，為有人疑言覺觀心中無定，是故佛說覺觀三昧。《地持論》說：「名覺觀俱禪。」此禪發時，必與覺觀俱發，亦名聖說法定。此定內有覺觀語言道未斷故，與說法之名，如是等種種名字不同。

Furthermore, what is called the first dhyāna is also named the samādhi possessing initial thought and sustained reflection — because some might doubt that concentration could be present at all within a mind of initial thought and sustained reflection, the Buddha therefore spoke of the samādhi of initial thought and sustained reflection. The Bodhisattva-bhūmi Treatise calls it "the dhyāna joined with initial thought and sustained reflection": when this dhyāna arises, it necessarily arises together with initial thought and sustained reflection. It is also called the noble concentration of expounding the Dharma, since, within this concentration, the path of language through initial thought and sustained reflection has not yet been cut off — hence it is given the name of expounding the Dharma. Such are its various differing names.

Reading note: Initial thought and sustained reflection (覺 and 觀) are the very same two characters this treatise has, up to now, always translated together as "discursive thought" — the restless mental chatter that restraining-the-mind stopping is meant to restrain, and one of the fifteen unwholesome roots in fascicle 4. Here the treatise addresses that tension directly: someone might reasonably doubt that concentration could coexist with discursive thought at all, which is exactly why the first dhyāna is also named "the samādhi possessing initial thought and sustained reflection" — a name that exists specifically to reassure the doubtful. The same mental activity that scatters an untrained mind becomes, in refined form, the very thing that first admits a practitioner into dhyāna; only from the second dhyāna onward is it finally left behind entirely. From here on, this translation renders 覺 and 觀 as "initial thought" and "sustained reflection" when they appear as this pair of technical factors, to keep them distinguishable from their more general, unwholesome-root sense.

The First Dhyāna: Its Cultivation 初禪 · 明修習

Chinese Source

第二、明修習，復開為二：前明所修之法，後辨能修之心。第一、明所修法者，即是阿那波那，為修習根本初禪之法。就中即有三意：一、釋息名；二、辨息相；三、明用息不同。第一、所言阿那波那者，此是外國語，秦言阿那為入息，波那為出息。《安般守意三昧經》言：「安之言生，般之言滅。」若約息生滅明，義如上說；若約心生滅為語，是則不定。今用入出息為正番。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying cultivation: this, in turn, opens into two parts — first clarifying the method to be cultivated, and afterward distinguishing the mind that does the cultivating.

First, clarifying the method to be cultivated: this is precisely mindfulness of the breath, the method for cultivating the root first dhyāna. Within this, there are three parts: explaining the terms for the breath; distinguishing the marks of the breath; and clarifying the different ways the breath may be used.

First, as for what is called ānāpāna: this is a foreign term; in the Qin-dynasty translation, āna is the in-breath and apāna is the out-breath. The Sūtra on the Samādhi of Mindfulness of the Breath says: "'Ān' means arising, and 'apān' means ceasing." If explained in terms of the breath's own arising and ceasing, the sense is as given above; if spoken of in terms of the mind's arising and ceasing, this is not fixed. Here, in-breath and out-breath are taken as the correct rendering.

二、辨息相中有四：一、風；二、喘；三、氣；四、息。分別四種之相，具如調息中說。但數風則散，數喘則結，數氣則勞，數息則定。行者應當捨三存息，善取不聲不結，綿綿若存若亡之相而用之。三、明用息不同者，一師教繫心數出息。所以者何？數出息則氣不急，身不脹滿，身心輕利，易入三昧。有師教數入息，何故爾？數入息：一者、易入定，隨息內歛故；二、斷外境故；三、易見內三十六物故；四、身力輕盛故；五、內實息貪恚故。有如是等勝利非一，應數入息。有師教數入出無在，但取所便而數。無的偏用，隨人心安，入定無過，即用三師所論，皆不許出入一時俱數。何以故？以有息遮病，生在喉中，猶如草葉，吐則不出，咽則不入，此患生故。又師依四時用數，今所未詳。

Second, distinguishing the marks of the breath: there are four — wind; panting; breath-with-effort; and true breath. The marks of these four kinds are distinguished in full within the discussion of regulating the breath. Counting the wind leads only to scattering; counting the panting leads only to constriction; counting the breath-with-effort leads only to fatigue; counting the true breath alone leads to concentration. The practitioner should set aside the first three and retain only the true breath, skillfully taking up its mark — soundless, unconstricted, continuous, as though present and yet almost imperceptible — and using this alone.

Third, clarifying the different ways the breath may be used: one master teaches fixing the mind on counting the out-breath. Why is this? Counting the out-breath keeps the breath from becoming urgent, the body from becoming distended and full, so that body and mind grow light and agile, easily entering samādhi. Another master teaches counting the in-breath instead — why is this? Counting the in-breath: first, makes concentration easier to enter, since the breath is thereby drawn inward and gathered in; second, cuts off external objects; third, makes it easier to see the thirty-six substances within the body; fourth, makes the body's own strength light and vigorous; fifth, genuinely settles craving and anger from within. There are such benefits as these, not of one kind only, and so one should count the in-breath. Yet another master teaches that it makes no difference whether one counts the in-breath or the out-breath, and that one should simply count whichever is more convenient, with no fixed preference either way — whichever puts the practitioner's own mind at ease and allows concentration to be entered without fault is fine. But all three masters, whatever they otherwise say, agree in not permitting the in-breath and out-breath to be counted together at the same time. Why is this? Because this gives rise to the ailment of breath-obstruction, arising in the throat like a blade of grass caught there — exhaling, it will not come out, and swallowing, it will not go in; this affliction arises from counting both at once. There is also a master who teaches counting according to the four seasons, though this is not examined in detail here.

第二、明能數之心，亦為三意：一、明能數之心；二、明轉緣；三、料揀。一、明能數之心者，以細念之心攝心對息，從一至十，令心不散，故名數息。若數不滿十，名數減；若至十一，名數增。然增減之數，並非得定之道。若從一至十，恒具十，無有間一之失，故名數法成就。若於中間心竊異緣，數法則亂，是故心覺散亂義強。若以一為數者，一則無間。若有異緣，便不時覺。是以但緣一息，不能除亂。若過十者，更一法起，一心緣二，即有亂生，故名為增。夫數息者，但細心約息記數而已，不得多取數相。若息多，則氣滿腹脹體急，坐欲不安。

Second, clarifying the mind that does the counting: this, too, has three parts — clarifying the counting mind itself; clarifying the shift in what it takes as its object; and a concluding distinction.

First, clarifying the counting mind itself: with a fine, attentive mind, gathering the mind in and fixing it on the breath, counting from one to ten so that the mind does not scatter — this is what is called counting the breath. If the count falls short of ten, this is called a deficient count; if it reaches eleven, this is called an excessive count. Yet neither an excessive nor a deficient count is truly the path to attaining concentration. If one counts from one to ten, always completing the full ten without ever missing a single count, this is called the accomplishment of the counting method. But if, partway through, the mind secretly wanders to some other object, the counting method falls into disorder — this is precisely how one comes to recognize that the mind has scattered. If one takes "one" alone as the count for each breath, there is then no interval left open for anything else to intrude; but should some other object arise, one may fail to notice it in time. Hence, taking a single breath alone as the object is not sufficient by itself to remove disorder. If the count exceeds ten, some further factor has arisen — the mind is then taking two things as its object within a single mind, and disorder arises from just this — hence this is called an excessive count. As for counting the breath: one should simply keep count with a fine, attentive mind fixed on the breath itself, and not fuss over the mark of the counting too much. If one dwells on the breath excessively, the qi becomes overfull, the abdomen distends, and the body grows tense, so that sitting itself becomes uneasy.

二、轉緣者，初數於息，覺息微微，當置數息，便隨於息，任運出入。若心欲靜，便捨隨，凝心止住。心若闇忽，即便靜照色息。心若浮動，即便捨觀歸數及隨止也，是故名還。心不馳蕩，凝神寂慮，故名為淨。行者若能如是善巧攝錄，心則易定。第三、料揀息為初門者。問曰：一切法門悉可為初，何故但說阿那波那以為初門？答曰：不然。今依佛教，如經說：「阿那波那是三世諸佛入道初門。」是故釋迦初詣道樹，欲習佛法，內思安般，一數，二隨，乃至還、淨，具如《瑞應經》所說。

Second, as for the shift in what the mind takes as its object: at first, counting the breath, one becomes aware of the breath growing faint and subtle; at this point, one should set counting aside and simply follow the breath instead, letting it go out and come in of its own accord. If the mind then wishes to grow still, one should set following aside as well, fixing the mind and settling it in stillness — this is called stilling. If the mind then grows dark and dull, one should instead calmly illuminate form and breath together — this is called contemplating. If the mind grows restless and unsettled instead, one should set contemplating aside and return to counting, following, or stilling — hence this is called returning. Once the mind no longer runs off and scatters, the spirit gathered in and every deliberation stilled, this is called purifying. If the practitioner is able to skillfully gather the mind in this way, concentration is thereby easily attained.

Third, distinguishing why the breath serves as the first gate: Question: every dharma-gate whatsoever could in principle serve as a starting point — why, then, is only mindfulness of the breath spoken of as the first gate? Answer: this does not follow. Here, in accordance with the Buddha's own teaching, the sūtra says: "Mindfulness of the breath is the first gate by which the buddhas of the three periods of time have entered the path." Hence, when Śākyamuni first arrived beneath the bodhi tree, wishing to practice the Buddhadharma, he reflected inwardly on mindfulness of the breath — first counting, second following, and on through stilling, contemplating, returning, and purifying — exactly as given in full in the Sūtra of Auspicious Manifestations.

Reading note: The six-stage sequence named here in passing — counting, following, stilling, contemplating, returning, purifying — is the "six wondrous gates" (六妙門), the subject of Zhiyi's own separate, shorter treatise. Its appearance here confirms that this whole breath-counting method is the seed the six wondrous gates grow out of, worked out here in the specific service of reaching the first dhyāna rather than as its own free-standing path. The three masters' disagreement over in-breath versus out-breath versus either is characteristic of how this treatise handles real disagreements among teachers: rather than picking a winner, it reports each position's own reasoning and lets the practitioner's own ease of practice be the deciding factor — with only one point held non-negotiable, never counting both breaths at once, on pain of a genuine physical ailment.

Why the Breath Comes First: Āryadeva's Teaching 初禪 · 明修習 · 料揀

Chinese Source

復次，提婆初出世時，伏外道已，諸人信敬，度人出家，不可稱數。於是大集在家、出家七眾弟子及剎利、婆羅門等，大眾之中，昇師子座，淚下如雨。爾時，大眾皆悉默念：「將非佛法欲滅，外道復興邪？將非國大擾亂，疫病流行邪？」菩薩爾時知大眾心念，以白㲲巾拭淚，更整容服，舉右手而言：「亦非佛法欲滅，外道將興，非國不安，疫病流行，但傷佛日潛輝，賢聖月沒，袈裟之中空無所有耳。」于時大眾聞此語已，各自感傷，發聲大哭。爾時，飛鳥雜類在虛空中，繽紛亂墜，皆悉悲鳴。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: when Āryadeva first appeared in the world, having already subdued the non-Buddhist teachers, people everywhere came to believe in and revere him, and those he brought across into the monastic life were beyond counting. At one point, a great assembly gathered — lay and ordained disciples of the seven assemblies, together with kṣatriyas, brāhmaṇas, and others — and, in the midst of this great assembly, he ascended the lion's seat, and tears fell from him like rain. At that moment, the whole assembly silently wondered: "Could it be that the Buddhadharma is about to be extinguished, and the non-Buddhist paths about to flourish again? Or could it be that the kingdom is about to fall into great turmoil, plague and pestilence about to spread?"

The bodhisattva, knowing at once what was in the assembly's mind, wiped away his tears with a white cotton cloth, straightened his robes and bearing, raised his right hand, and said: "Neither is the Buddhadharma about to be extinguished, nor are the non-Buddhist paths about to flourish; neither is the kingdom about to fall into unrest, nor is plague about to spread. I grieve only that the sun of the Buddha has already set beneath the horizon, that the moon of the sages and noble ones has already sunk from view, and that, within these very monastic robes gathered here, there is, in truth, nothing left within them at all."

Upon hearing these words, the whole assembly, each moved to sorrow, wept aloud together. At that moment, the birds and various creatures in the open sky scattered and fell in confusion, all crying out in grief as well.

爾時，菩薩以慈軟音安慰大眾，而說偈言：
佛日常在世，無目不見耳，
賢聖月不沒，障礙故不見。
若能淨膚翳，當自得覩見，
何為沒憂海，癡醉如嬰兒？
爾時，大眾聞菩薩慈音，心各醒悟，攝心安坐，寂然無聲，諦觀菩薩，咸欲聞法。爾時，菩薩普告大眾而說偈言：
佛說甘露門，名阿那波那，
於諸法門中，第一安隱道。
因緣次第起，不雜諸妄想，
譬如種石榴，芽莖次第生。
華實及色味，自然非可作，
時至時自證，非如脂粉色。
汝等調熟地，惠汝石榴種，
令心入甘露，道法次第生。

The bodhisattva then, with a voice gentle and full of compassion, consoled the assembly, speaking this verse:
"The sun of the Buddha forever remains in the world; it is only the eyeless who fail to see it.
The moon of the sages and noble ones has never truly set; it is only obstruction that keeps it from view.
Should you cleanse away the film clouding your own eyes, you would see it for yourselves at once —
Why, then, sink into an ocean of grief, deluded and drunk like a newborn child?"
At that, the assembly, hearing the bodhisattva's compassionate voice, each awakened in mind, gathered themselves, and sat down in peace, still and silent, gazing intently at the bodhisattva, all wishing to hear the Dharma. The bodhisattva then addressed the whole assembly, speaking this verse:
"The Buddha spoke of the gate to the deathless, named ānāpāna —
Among every dharma-gate whatsoever, it is the foremost path to peace and stability.
It arises through causes and conditions in due sequence, unmixed with any deluded thought,
Just as a planted pomegranate seed sends up its sprout and stem in due sequence.
Its flowers, fruit, and their color and flavor arise naturally, not something that can be forced —
When the time comes, one bears witness to it oneself; it is not like applying rouge or powder to the skin.
You who have properly cultivated this ground — I now bestow on you the pomegranate's own seed,
That your minds may enter the gate to the deathless, the dharma of the path arising in due sequence."

從此以來，西國法師相傳不絕，多以此法為學道之初。若四依大士、六通菩薩說法度人，此為首唱，豈非入道初門？末代相承，說法教授自不修禪，既無內道，出言即便破人修定，若觀提婆之說，乃以禪定為要。世人顛倒，實可哀哉！復有人言：「禪法一向不得處眾說之。」敬尋提婆在大眾中廣說禪定，今時豈頓杜口？但不得言：「我證是法，某證是法，及禪祕密微妙境界。」向人說此，獲罪不輕。

From this time onward, the teacher-masters of the Western Lands have transmitted this without interruption, for the most part taking this very method as the starting point of study on the path. If even the four kinds of reliance and the great beings, the bodhisattvas possessing the six supernormal powers, take this as their own opening proclamation when expounding the Dharma to liberate others, how could it not be the very first gate into the path?

In this latter age, those who inherit the transmission and teach the Dharma to others, without cultivating dhyāna themselves, having no inward path of their own, speak out and thereby only destroy others' own cultivation of concentration instead. Looking at what Āryadeva himself taught, dhyāna-concentration is precisely what is essential — the inverted state of people in this world is truly something to lament! There are, further, those who say: "The dharma of dhyāna should, without exception, never be expounded before an assembly at all." But respectfully examining Āryadeva's own example — expounding dhyāna-concentration at length before a great assembly — how, then, could one simply close one's mouth entirely in the present age? One should simply avoid saying: "I myself have realized this dharma," or "So-and-so has realized this dharma," or speaking of dhyāna's own secret and subtle states to others — to speak of these things to another incurs no small offense.

Reading note: This story of Āryadeva's grief — mourning not any external catastrophe but the hollowness beneath the robes of a Saṅgha that has stopped actually practicing — reframes everything the treatise has spent four fascicles building as urgent rather than merely thorough. The pomegranate simile is Zhiyi's own chosen image for something this whole treatise has insisted on from its opening pages: realization cannot be forced or faked (nor described in advance, "not like applying rouge or powder to the skin") — it can only be cultivated in proper sequence and witnessed for oneself when its own time comes. The closing rule about never claiming personal attainment, sandwiched between two passages defending the public teaching of dhyāna, draws a precise line: teach the method as fully and publicly as Āryadeva did, but never claim the result — a distinction the treatise clearly regards as being violated more in its own era than observed.

The First Dhyāna: Marks of Realization 初禪 · 明證相

Chinese Source

第三、明證禪相，通方便論證自有三階：一、證欲界定相；二、證未到定相；三、正明證初禪相。一、明證欲界定，自有二意：一、正明證相；二、明得失。今說欲界中自有三：一、麁住心；二、細住心；三、證欲界定。一、麁住相者，因前息道諸方便修習，心漸虛凝，不復緣慮，名為麁住。細住相者，於後其心泯泯轉細，即是細住心。當得此麁細住時，或將得時，必有持身法起。此法發時，身心自然正直，坐不疲倦，如物持身，若好持身，但微微扶助身力而已。若是麁持身者，堅急勁強，來則苦急堅強，去則寬緩困人。此非好法。心既細已，於覺心自然明淨，與定相應，定法持心，任運不動，從淺入深，或經一坐，無分散意，所以說此名欲界定。入此定時，欲界報身相未盡故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying the marks of realizing dhyāna: discussing realization broadly, together with the expedient means, this itself has three stages — the mark of realizing desire-realm concentration; the mark of realizing the "not-yet-arrived" concentration; and directly clarifying the mark of realizing the first dhyāna.

First, clarifying the realization of desire-realm concentration: this itself has two parts — directly clarifying its mark, and clarifying its gain and loss. Here, within the desire realm, three stages are given: the mind coarsely settling; the mind finely settling; and the realization of desire-realm concentration proper.

As for the mark of coarse settling: through the various expedient means of the path of the breath already cultivated, the mind gradually grows empty and fixed, no longer chasing after objects of thought — this is called coarse settling. As for the mark of fine settling: afterward, the mind grows ever more indistinct and refined — this is precisely the fine-settling mind. Upon reaching this coarse or fine settling, or as one is about to reach it, a body-sustaining phenomenon is bound to arise. When this phenomenon arises, body and mind become naturally upright, sitting without fatigue, as though something were sustaining the body — and, when this sustaining is proper, it merely, faintly, lends support to the body's own strength. But if it is a coarse form of body-sustaining instead, it is rigid, tense, and forceful — arriving with a strained, forced rigidity, and departing leaving the body slack and exhausted; this is not the proper phenomenon.

Once the mind has grown fine, the awakened mind itself becomes naturally bright and pure, according with concentration; the dharma of concentration then sustains the mind, unmoved of its own accord, proceeding from the shallow into the deep — sometimes throughout an entire sitting, with no thought of it scattering at all. This is why this state is called desire-realm concentration: upon entering this concentration, the marks of the desire realm's own retributive body have not yet been entirely exhausted.

二、明得失者，入欲界定，法心既淺，未有支持，難得易失。易失因緣，是事須識。失定有二種：一、從外緣失，謂得定時，不善用心，內外方便，中途違犯，則退失禪定。復次，若行者當得定時，或向人說，或現定相，令他知覺，或卒有事緣相壞，如是等種種外事，於中不覺不識，障法既生，則便失定。若能將護，本得不失，障不得生，故名為得。二者、約內論得失者，有六種法能失禪定：一、希望心；二、疑心；三、驚怖；四、大喜；五、重愛；六、憂悔。未得禪有一，謂希望心。入禪有四，謂疑、怖、喜、愛。出禪多有憂悔，此則能破定心令退失。若通論此六，皆得在未入住出中。俱有此六法，能退失定；若能離此六法，即易得定。以不失故，名得也。此雖近事，若不說者，則人不知。若善取其意，則知遮障。

Second, clarifying gain and loss: upon entering desire-realm concentration, the mind of this dharma is still shallow, not yet sustained by any factor, and so is difficult to attain and easy to lose. The causes and conditions of this easy loss are matters one must recognize. Loss of concentration is of two kinds.

First, loss through an external condition: this means that, at the very time concentration is attained, the mind is applied improperly, some fault is committed partway through in the external or internal expedient means, and dhyāna-concentration thereby regresses and is lost. Furthermore: if the practitioner, at the very moment of attaining concentration, either speaks of it to others, or displays some outward sign of concentration so that others become aware of it, or some sudden matter or condition arises and disrupts it — such various external matters as these, if not recognized and understood within, allow the obstructing dharma to arise, and concentration is thereupon lost. If one is able to guard and protect it properly, what has already been attained is not lost, and no obstruction is allowed to arise — hence this is called "attaining" it.

Second, discussing gain and loss in terms of internal causes: there are six kinds of dharma able to cause the loss of dhyāna-concentration — a mind of expectation; a mind of doubt; fright; great joy; excessive attachment; and worry and regret. Before dhyāna is attained, there is one of these: a mind of expectation. Upon entering dhyāna, there are four: doubt, fright, joy, and attachment. Upon emerging from dhyāna, worry and regret are frequently present — and this alone is enough to break apart the concentrated mind, causing it to regress and be lost. Speaking of these six generally, they may all occur before entering, while abiding within, or upon emerging from concentration alike; wherever any of these six dharmas is present, concentration is liable to regress and be lost; and if one is able to remain free of these six dharmas, concentration is thereby easily attained. Because it is thus not lost, this is called "attaining" it. Although this may seem a minor matter, if it were not explained, people would have no way of knowing it; properly grasping its sense, one comes to recognize the obstruction itself.

二、明證未到地定相，因此欲界定後，身心泯然虛豁，失於欲界之身，坐中不見頭手床敷，猶若虛空，此是未到地定。所言未到地者，此地能生初禪故，即是初禪方便定，亦名未來禪，亦名忽然湛心。證此定時，不無淺深之相，今不具明。復次，此等定中，或有邪偽，行者應證，其相非一，略出二事：一、定心過明；二者、過暗。並是邪定。明者，入定時見外境界青、黃、赤、白，或見日月星辰、宮殿等事，或一時日乃至七日不出禪定，見一切事，如得神通，此為邪，當急去之。二者、若入此定，暗忽無所覺知，如眠熟不異，即是無心想法，能令行人生顛倒心，當急却之。此則略說邪定之相，是中妨難，非可具以文傳。復次，若依《成論》、《毘曇》分別二定，為不便也。今依尊者瞿沙所明分別二定有異，亦應無失。具如前引《摩訶衍》中釋，而多見坐人證定之時，實有兩種定相不同，是故今說欲界、未到二定各異。

Second, clarifying the mark of realizing the "not-yet-arrived" concentration: following this desire-realm concentration, body and mind become indistinct and vacantly open, the sense of the desire-realm body itself lost — while sitting, one no longer sees one's own head, hands, or the seat beneath, as though it were all empty space. This is the "not-yet-arrived" concentration. As for what is meant by "not-yet-arrived": this stage is able to give rise to the first dhyāna, and so is precisely the first dhyāna's own preliminary concentration — also called the future dhyāna, and also called the suddenly deepened mind. When this concentration is realized, it is not without its own marks of shallowness and depth, though these are not given in full detail here.

Furthermore: within concentrations such as these, there may also be deviance and falsity present, which the practitioner should be able to recognize; its marks are not of one kind only, though, in brief, two matters are given: the concentrated mind being excessively bright; and being excessively dark — both of these are deviant concentration. As for excessive brightness: upon entering concentration, one sees external states of blue, yellow, red, and white, or sees the sun, moon, and stars, or palaces and other such things, or, for a full day or even as long as seven days, fails to emerge from dhyāna-concentration, seeing all manner of things as though one had attained supernormal powers — this is deviant, and one should urgently rid oneself of it. As for excessive darkness: if, upon entering this concentration, one becomes dark and dull, aware of nothing at all, no different from being deeply asleep — this is precisely a state without any mental activity at all, able to cause the practitioner to give rise to an inverted mind; one should urgently repel this as well. This, in brief, describes the marks of deviant concentration; the difficulties involved cannot be conveyed in full through writing.

Furthermore: were one to follow the Satyasiddhi-śāstra or the Abhidharma in distinguishing these two concentrations, this would not prove convenient here. Here, instead, the distinction between these two concentrations as clarified by the Venerable Ghoṣa is followed, which should likewise be without fault — exactly as already cited above from the Mahāyāna treatise's own explanation. And, having observed many practitioners at the actual moment of realizing concentration, there are, in truth, two genuinely differing marks of concentration present — hence desire-realm concentration and the "not-yet-arrived" concentration are here said to be each distinct from the other.

Reading note: The body-sustaining phenomenon and the coarse/fine settling distinction give a concrete physical criterion for a state that could otherwise only be described introspectively — a genuinely settled sitting posture feels sustained rather than held, "as though something were sustaining the body," and a forced, rigid posture is diagnosed here as itself a sign the mind has not actually settled yet. The six causes of losing concentration (expectation before, doubt/fright/joy/attachment during, worry and regret after) are worth comparing with the ten pairs of wrong marks from fascicle 3: both are diagnostic lists built from ordinary, recognizable mental events rather than exotic experiences, on the theory that what derails practice is usually mundane. The excessive-brightness and excessive-darkness deviant marks also echo fascicle 3's own "明闇" pair from the ten wrong marks almost exactly — the same diagnostic returning at a new stage of practice.

Third, clarifying the marks of realizing the first dhyāna: this itself has six kinds — naming the first dhyāna's own mark of arising; clarifying its factors; clarifying its cause, effect, substance, and function; clarifying its depth; clarifying its progress and regression; and clarifying its merits.

First, directly clarifying the mark of the first dhyāna's own arising: this, in turn, has four parts — directly clarifying the mark of the first dhyāna arising; distinguishing dharmas that are not truly dhyāna; explaining the causes and conditions of its arising; and distinguishing the deviant from the correct.

First, as for the mark of the first dhyāna's arising: the practitioner, within the "not-yet-arrived" state, realizes the sixteen tactile sensations brought to completion — this is precisely the mark of the first dhyāna's own arising. What is meant by "realizing" this? If the practitioner, within the "not-yet-arrived" state, enters concentration ever more deeply, body and mind empty and still, seeing neither what is within nor without — whether for a single day or as long as seven, whether for a month or as long as a year — and, should the concentrated mind remain unbroken, guarded and protected, growing ever further, then, within this very concentration, one suddenly feels body and mind grow fixed and still, and then set into motion, stirring gradually; and, at the very moment of this stirring, one becomes aware, little by little, of having a body once again, moving and arising like a cloud or a shadow — arising, perhaps, from above, or from below, or from the waist, gradually spreading through the whole body. Arising from above tends, for the most part, toward regression; arising from below tends, for the most part, toward progress. At the very moment this sensation of movement arises, its merit is measureless. In brief, ten kinds of wholesome dharma arise together with it, as its own retinue. What are these ten? Concentration; emptiness; brightness and purity; joy and delight; happiness; the arising of a wholesome mind; clear knowledge and vision; freedom from entanglement and liberation; states manifesting directly before one; and a mind grown pliant and soft. These ten dharmas, arising together with this sensation of movement, are called the retinue of movement — the wondrous, excellent merit that adorns the dharma of movement. Were this to be distinguished in full, it could hardly be exhausted; this, in brief, describes the initial mark of the tactile sensation of movement.

如是或經一日，或經十日，或一月四月，如是一年，此事既過，復有餘觸次第而發，故名初禪。餘觸發者，謂八觸也：一、動；二、痒；三、涼；四、暖；五、輕；六、重；七、澁；八、滑。復有八觸，謂：一、掉；二、猗；三、冷；四、熱；五、浮；六、沈；七、堅；八、軟。此八觸與前相雖同，而細分別不無小異，更別出名目，足前合為十六觸。此十六種觸發時，悉有善法功德眷屬，如前動觸中說。行者因未到地，發如是等種種諸觸功德善法，故名初禪初發。並是色界清淨四大，依欲界身中而發，故《摩訶衍》云色界四大造色，著欲界身中。

In this way, whether over the course of a single day, or ten days, or a month, or four months, or even a full year, once this matter has passed, further tactile sensations arise in turn, one after another — hence this is named the first dhyāna. These further sensations that arise are the eight tactile sensations: movement; itching; coolness; warmth; lightness; heaviness; roughness; and smoothness. There are, further, eight more tactile sensations: agitation; pliancy; cold; heat; floating; sinking; firmness; and softness. Although these eight resemble the previous eight in their marks, upon fine distinction they are not without their own small differences, and so are given separate names — together with the first eight, these make sixteen tactile sensations in all.

Whenever these sixteen kinds of tactile sensation arise, each is accompanied by wholesome dharmas and merit as its own retinue, exactly as described above for the sensation of movement. The practitioner, through the "not-yet-arrived" state, brings forth such various tactile sensations, merit, and wholesome dharmas as these — hence this is called the first arising of the first dhyāna. All of this belongs to the pure four elements of the form realm, arising within the body of the desire realm itself — hence the Mahāyāna treatise says that the form derived from the four elements of the form realm attaches itself within the body of the desire realm.

Reading note: The eight tactile sensations named here in passing — already used as a shorthand term all the way back in fascicle 3's account of the counting-the-breath wholesome root — turn out to be only half the full picture: a second set of eight (agitation, pliancy, cold, heat, floating, sinking, firmness, softness), close cousins of the first eight but distinguishable on careful attention, complete the full sixteen this section is actually about. Second, distinguishing dharmas that are not truly dhyāna. Question: a practitioner, within the very first sitting, before the concentrated mind is even attained, may likewise bring forth such sensations as cold, warmth, or movement, without any of the merit described above being present at all — some say: "This is simply the arising of illness." Why is this? Sensations such as heaviness and roughness arise from an illness of the earth element; sensations such as lightness and movement arise from an illness of the wind element; sensations such as heat and itching arise from an illness of the fire element; and sensations such as cold and smoothness arise from an illness of the water element. Furthermore: warmth, heat, and itching give rise to the hindrance of craving; heaviness, smoothness, and sinking give rise to the hindrance of drowsiness; movement, floating, and cold give rise to the hindrance of restlessness and regret; rigidity and roughness give rise to the hindrance of doubt; and heaviness, firmness, and roughness give rise to the hindrance of anger. One should know that, when such sensations arise, they are able to cause illness to arise within the four elements, and to give rise to the obstructing dharma of the five hindrances as well.

Others say this is the work of a demon: whenever the sensation of movement arises, exactly as described above and even beyond what was said there, all of it is a demon's own sensation arising — how, then, could this possibly be taken as the first dhyāna? Answer: this does not follow. If the mark of the sensation's arising is exactly as you have just described, this is a sensation that gives rise to illness and to the hindrances. If it is exactly as described above, and even intensifies beyond it, this too is the mark of a demon's sensation arising. What is being described here, however, is not this: if, before the "not-yet-arrived" concentration is even attained, a sensation arises first, this is, for the most part, an illness-sensation, or one that gives rise to the hindrances, or one that is the demon's own work. And if, when a sensation arises, none of the ten kinds of merit described above is present as its retinue, this too is an illness-sensation, hindrance-arising, or demon-sensation. What is described here as the sensation's arising, however, necessarily arises through the "not-yet-arrived" concentration, and is necessarily accompanied by every one of these merits as its retinue, arising together with it — hence this alone is taken as the mark of the first dhyāna's own arising. What room, then, is there for doubt?

問曰：未到地前發觸，但是生病生蓋及魔觸，亦有治病除蓋非魔觸不？答曰：亦有此義。問曰：若爾，與初禪觸復云何異？答曰：有異。欲界雖有治病除蓋及非魔觸，而非初禪觸者，此猶是欲界中四大色法，不能發定，無諸功德支林善法，故不名初禪。此則略出欲界善、不善觸相，但行人初坐，或有證此一兩，或都不證。然既有此法，故略出之耳。問曰：未到地中亦發欲界善、不善觸不？答曰：非無此義。

Answer: yes, this sense holds as well. Question: if this is so, how does it differ from the first dhyāna's own sensation? Answer: it does differ. Although the desire realm may have sensations that treat illness and remove the hindrances without being the work of any demon, these are still not the first dhyāna's own sensations — they remain simply the four elements' own material dharma within the desire realm, unable to bring forth concentration, without any of the wholesome dharma of a grove of factors present — hence they are not named the first dhyāna. This, in brief, sets out the marks of wholesome and unwholesome sensation within the desire realm; it is simply that, in a practitioner's very first sitting, one or two of these may sometimes be realized, or none at all may be realized. Since this dharma does exist, however, it is given here in brief. Question: does the "not-yet-arrived" state, too, give rise to the desire realm's own wholesome and unwholesome sensations? Answer: yes, this sense is not absent there either.

Reading note: This Q&A sharpens the diagnostic criterion from fascicle 3 into something almost clinical: the same eight sensations (cold, warmth, movement, and the rest) can indicate illness, demonic interference, ordinary desire-realm sensation, or genuine dhyāna, and the deciding factor is never the sensation's own quality but strictly its context — whether it arises before or only after the "not-yet-arrived" concentration is actually attained, and whether the full ten-fold retinue of merit accompanies it. A sensation identical in every describable respect can be either meaningless or the mark of a major attainment depending entirely on what came before it — a caution against ever diagnosing a state from its content in isolation.

Causes and Conditions of Dhyāna's Arising 初禪 · 釋發因緣

三、明禪發因緣有二：一者、從初修禪以來，不計勤苦，既有善心，功力成就，自然感報，如《法華》中說：「隨功賞賜，乃至禪定、根、力等事。」復次，有師言：「是十善相應。」此意難見。二者、色界五陰住在欲界身中，麁細相違，故有掉、動八觸等事。譬如世人憂愁煩惱，內起結滯，壅塞不通，令四大受諸熱惱，從心而生，乃至得病至死，不從外來而有苦也。今此禪中有觸樂之事，亦從心有，由數息故，使心軟細，修諸定法，色界定法住在欲界身中，色定之法與欲界報身相觸，故有十六觸次第而生，亦不從外來而能覺知，故名為觸。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying that dhyāna's arising has two causes and conditions.

First: from the very outset of cultivating dhyāna, without regard for toil or hardship, once a wholesome mind and the power of one's own effort come to completion, the result is naturally brought about of itself — as the Lotus Sūtra says: "Rewards bestowed according to merit, extending even to dhyāna-concentration, the faculties, the powers, and such matters." Furthermore, one master says: "This corresponds to the ten wholesome actions" — though this sense is difficult to see clearly.

Second: the five aggregates of the form realm come to dwell within the body of the desire realm, coarse and fine at odds with one another, and so such matters as the sensations of agitation, movement, and the rest arise. It is like a person in the world whose sorrow and vexation give rise to an inward knot or blockage, obstructed and unable to flow freely, causing the four elements to suffer their own feverish distress — arising from the mind itself, even to the point of falling ill and dying from it, a suffering that never comes from outside at all. Here, in this dhyāna, the matter of tactile joy is exactly the same, arising from the mind as well: through counting the breath, the mind is made soft and fine, and, as the various dharmas of concentration are cultivated, the concentration-dharma of the form realm, dwelling within the body of the desire realm, comes into contact with the desire realm's own retributive body — hence the sixteen tactile sensations arise, one after another, in sequence, likewise never coming from outside, yet still able to be felt; hence they are named "tactile sensations."

此八雖有十六，並約四大而發，因四大生：地中四者，重、沈、堅、澁；水中四者，涼、冷、軟、滑；火中四者，暖、熱、猗、痒；風中四者，動、掉、輕、浮。故《金光明》云：「地水二蛇，其性沈下；風火二蛇，性輕上昇。」問：若因四大，但應有四，何得十六？答曰：相兼故得爾。如熱是火體，兼水故有暖，兼風故有痒，兼地故有猗，兼三之時，失本熱相，故說有四。餘三大各兼三義，類此可知。復次，此十六觸各有十種功德善法，合則有一百六十法，而初坐發法之人未必發盡，或發三五，故略出之。問曰：此八觸為當發有次第？為無次第？諸觸之中，先發何等？答曰：若論其次第，亦無定前後。雖四大因緣合時，強者先發，而多見有人從動而發，事如前釋。四者、辨邪正之相，具如前內方便中驗善惡根性相明虛實中說，是中應廣分別。

Although these are eight in one counting and sixteen in another, all of them arise with reference to the four elements, born from the four elements themselves: within earth, four — heaviness, sinking, firmness, and roughness; within water, four — coolness, cold, softness, and smoothness; within fire, four — warmth, heat, pliancy, and itching; within wind, four — movement, agitation, lightness, and floating. Hence the Golden Light Sūtra says: "The two serpents of earth and water are sinking by nature; the two serpents of wind and fire are light and rising by nature."

Question: if these arise from the four elements, there should be only four of them — how can there be sixteen? Answer: this comes about through their combining with one another. Heat, for instance, is the very substance of fire; combined with water, it gives warmth; combined with wind, it gives itching; combined with earth, it gives pliancy — and, once combined with all three of the others, it loses its own original mark of heat, hence four are spoken of. Each of the remaining three elements likewise combines with the other three in the same way, and may be understood by this same pattern. Furthermore: each of these sixteen tactile sensations carries its own ten kinds of wholesome, meritorious dharma, which, combined together, come to one hundred sixty dharmas in all — though a person, in the very first sitting where this dharma arises, need not bring forth every one of them; some bring forth only three or five. This, in brief, is set out here.

Question: do these eight — or sixteen — tactile sensations arise in a fixed sequence, or not? Among the various sensations, which arises first? Answer: discussing their sequence, there is, in fact, no fixed order beforehand. Although, when the causes and conditions of the four elements come together, whichever is strongest tends to arise first, it is nonetheless most commonly observed that people's sensations arise beginning with movement, exactly as already explained above. Fourth, distinguishing the marks of the deviant and the correct: this is given in full already, within the internal expedient means, in the discussion of verifying wholesome and unwholesome capacity's clarification of the genuine and the false; this should be distinguished at length there.

Reading note: The comparison to psychosomatic illness is a genuinely striking move — the treatise explicitly likens the tactile sensations of dhyāna to how ordinary grief and vexation can produce real bodily suffering "arising from the mind itself... never coming from outside at all," using a recognizable everyday phenomenon to make a subtle claim about dhyāna's own sensations plausible. The combinatorial explanation of how four elements produce sixteen sensations (each element's own quality plus its three possible combinations with the others) is a small piece of genuine systematic reasoning embedded in what could otherwise read as simple enumeration — heat combined with water gives warmth, with wind gives itching, with earth gives pliancy, precisely because combining with a third element causes the original quality to lose its own pure mark.

The Five Factors 初禪 · 明支義

Chinese Source

第二、明支義，亦開為三：一、釋支名；二、釋支義；三、辨支相。第一、釋支名者，初禪有五支：一、覺支；二、觀支；三、喜支；四、樂支；五、一心支。覺者初心覺悟，名為覺。觀者，後細心分別，名為觀。慶悅之心，名為喜。恬澹之心，名為樂。寂然不散，名一心。所以制五支者，若對不善，即為破五欲、五蓋；若對善法，即對行五法。故《釋論》云：「離五蓋，行五法，具五支，入初禪。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the meaning of the factors: this, too, opens into three parts — explaining the name "factor"; explaining the meaning of "factor"; and distinguishing the marks of the factors.

First, explaining the name "factor": the first dhyāna has five factors — the factor of initial thought; the factor of sustained reflection; the factor of joy; the factor of happiness; and the factor of one-pointedness of mind. Initial thought is the initial mind's own awakening, and is called "initial thought." Sustained reflection is the subsequent fine mind's own discrimination, and is called "sustained reflection." A mind of celebration and delight is called "joy." A mind of tranquil ease is called "happiness." Being still and undispersed is called "one-pointedness of mind." As for why these five factors are established: with reference to the unwholesome, they serve to break through the five desires and the five hindrances; with reference to the wholesome dharmas, they correspond to the practice of the five dharmas. Hence the Exegetical Treatise says: "Leaving behind the five hindrances, practicing the five dharmas, complete in the five factors — one enters the first dhyāna."

第二、釋支義者，如《纓絡經》說：「禪名支林。」此即據總別之明義也。言支者，支離為義。如因樹根莖，則有枝條。根莖是一，枝條有異。禪中支義亦爾，從一定心出生五支，此是總中別義。所言林者，如林因眾多樹，得有林名。禪義亦爾，五支和合，總受禪稱。此即據別中之總，故知若說禪，即知有五支，如聞林名，必知有樹及以枝條。復次，有人言：「枝持為義。」如欲界、未到地中雖有單靜定心，未有覺觀等五支共相枝持，則定心淺薄易失。若得初禪，即有覺觀等法，則定心安隱，牢固難壞。

Second, explaining the meaning of "factor": as the Yingluo Sūtra says, "dhyāna is named a grove of factors" — this is precisely a statement made with reference to both the general and the particular senses of the term together. As for "factor": its meaning is that of branching apart. It is like a tree: because of its root and trunk, it has branches and twigs; the root and trunk are one, while the branches and twigs are many and varied. The meaning of "factor" within dhyāna is exactly the same — from a single concentrated mind, five factors come forth; this is the particular sense within the general. As for "grove": it is like a grove, which, because of its many trees together, comes to be called a grove. The meaning of "dhyāna" is exactly the same: the five factors combined together, taken as a whole, receive the name "dhyāna." This is precisely the general sense within the particular. Hence it is known that, whenever "dhyāna" is spoken of, the five factors are thereby already implied, just as, upon hearing the name "grove," one already knows there must be trees, and branches and twigs as well.

Furthermore, some say: "the meaning is that of branches sustaining the trunk." Just as, within desire-realm concentration and the "not-yet-arrived" state, although a single, still, concentrated mind is present, the five factors — initial thought, sustained reflection, and the rest — are not yet present together to sustain it like branches, and so the concentrated mind remains shallow and slight, easily lost; but once the first dhyāna is attained, the dharmas of initial thought, sustained reflection, and the rest are all present, and the concentrated mind is thereby made secure and firm, difficult to break apart.

三、辨支相。若數人辨相，正約二十二心數去取辨五支相，具出彼義。今家所明，略為二：一者、別；二者、通。一、別釋五支相者。云何名覺？覺名觸覺，有二種：一、成禪覺；二、壞禪覺。如有風，能成雨，有風能壞雨，如上所說。十六觸中，一觸有十種善法眷屬安隱莊嚴者，是成禪覺，如上說。一觸有二十惡法，是壞禪覺。復次，覺者，覺屬身根，為身有情，異乎木石，所以對觸故生覺。如經說見聞覺知義。見屬於眼，聞屬於耳，鼻、觸覺屬於身，知屬於意，亦對舌也，有增用故。

Third, distinguishing the marks of the factors: if the Ābhidharma masters distinguish these marks, they do so strictly with reference to the twenty-two mental factors, taking up and setting aside among them to distinguish the marks of the five factors — their own sense is given in full elsewhere. What this school clarifies is, in brief, of two kinds: the particular; and the general.

First, explaining the marks of the five factors particularly. What is meant by "initial thought"? Initial thought means tactile awareness, and is of two kinds: initial thought that accomplishes dhyāna; and initial thought that ruins dhyāna. It is like wind: there is a wind able to bring rain, and a wind able to ruin rain, exactly as already described above. Within the sixteen tactile sensations, whichever sensation carries the ten kinds of wholesome dharma as its retinue, peacefully adorning it, is initial thought that accomplishes dhyāna, as already described above; whichever sensation carries the twenty unwholesome dharmas instead is initial thought that ruins dhyāna. Furthermore, "initial thought" also means to be startled awake. This faculty belongs to the body-faculty, since a body possessed of sentience differs from wood or stone, and so, in response to a tactile sensation, an awareness arises. As the sūtra speaks of the meaning of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing: seeing belongs to the eye; hearing belongs to the ear; smell and tactile sensing belong to the body; and knowing belongs to the mind — though it also applies to the tongue, since it has an additional function there as well.

問曰：如經中說六觸因緣生受，何得覺觸但屬於身耶？答曰：此對通說。若通時，見中亦說聞，餘義類爾。今就別義論覺支者，正對身也。於未到定中發十六觸，觸於身根生識，覺前觸相，故名覺支。復次，覺名驚悟。行者得初禪，未曾所得善法諸功德故，心大驚悟。昔常為欲火所燒，得初禪時，如人入清涼池。但此覺生時，與欲界身根生覺有異。何以故？與定等善法一時俱發。是以偈言：「如貧得寶藏，大喜覺動心。」故言初心麁念名為覺。此與數人明義，應有小異。料簡。

Question: as the sūtra says that the six kinds of contact, through causes and conditions, give rise to sensation — how, then, can "sensing contact" belong only to the body? Answer: this is spoken of in general terms. When spoken of generally, "seeing" is also said to include "hearing," and the same holds for the rest by the same pattern; here, however, discussing "initial thought" in its particular sense, it corresponds precisely to the body. Within the "not-yet-arrived" concentration, the sixteen tactile sensations arise, coming into contact with the body-faculty and giving rise to consciousness, aware of the mark of the sensation that has just occurred — hence this is named the factor of initial thought.

Furthermore, "initial thought" also means being startled into awakening: the practitioner, attaining the first dhyāna, having never before attained such wholesome dharma and merit, the mind is greatly startled into awakening. Having long been burned by the fire of desire, upon attaining the first dhyāna, it is like a person entering a cool, refreshing pool. Yet this awakening, when it arises, differs from the awakening that arises through the body-faculty within the desire realm — why is this? Because it arises together, at one and the same time, with concentration and the other wholesome dharmas. Hence the verse says: "Like a poor person finding a buried treasure, great joy stirs and moves the mind." Hence it is said that the initial mind's coarse thought is what is called "initial thought." This differs slightly from how the Ābhidharma masters clarify the same sense — a distinction worth noting.

二、釋觀支者，後細心分別，名為觀。既分別觸發已，正念之心思量分別，向觸生時，與欲界中善法及未到等法大有異。所以者何？於此觸中有種種善法珍寶，與觸俱發，欲界所無。復次，分別者，分別十六觸中法寶之相亦不同，知麁則離，知善則修，此細心分別，故名觀支，故經說：「分別則為觀。」問曰：若爾，覺有何等異？答曰：如論說：「麁心在緣，名為覺；細心分別，名為觀。」又問：如《毘曇》中說：「覺觀在一心中。」今云何為二？答曰：二法雖在一心，二相不俱，謂覺時觀不明了，觀時覺不明了。譬如撞鐘，鐘聲雖一，而麁細有異。一心中覺觀亦如是。復次，身根、身識相應，名為覺；意根、意識相應，名為觀。身識是外鈍，故名麁；意識是內利，故能分別名細。此雖同緣一觸，而二相不俱，故為觀支。

Second, explaining the factor of sustained reflection: the subsequent fine mind's own discrimination is called "sustained reflection." Having already discriminated the sensation's own arising, the mind of right mindfulness deliberates and discriminates further, recognizing that, the moment the sensation first arose, it was already vastly different from any wholesome dharma of the desire realm or the "not-yet-arrived" state. Why is this? Because, within this very sensation, various treasures of wholesome dharma are present, arising together with it — treasures the desire realm itself does not possess at all. Furthermore, "discrimination" here means distinguishing that the marks of the dharma-treasures within the sixteen tactile sensations are themselves not all the same — recognizing the coarse, one leaves it behind; recognizing the wholesome, one cultivates it further — and this fine mind's own discrimination is what is named the factor of sustained reflection. Hence the sūtra says: "Discrimination is what is called sustained reflection."

Question: if this is so, how does this differ from initial thought? Answer: as the treatise says: "A coarse mind resting on its object is called initial thought; a fine mind discriminating is called sustained reflection." A further question: the Abhidharma says that initial thought and sustained reflection occur within a single mind — how, then, can they now be said to be two? Answer: although the two dharmas occur within a single mind, their two marks are not present simultaneously — that is, when initial thought is present, sustained reflection is not yet clear, and when sustained reflection is present, initial thought is no longer clear. It is like striking a bell: although the bell's sound is a single sound, it nonetheless has a coarse phase and a fine phase, differing from one another. Initial thought and sustained reflection within a single mind are exactly the same. Furthermore: correspondence with the body-faculty and body-consciousness is called initial thought; correspondence with the mind-faculty and mind-consciousness is called sustained reflection. Body-consciousness is outwardly dull, and so is called coarse; mind-consciousness is inwardly sharp, and so is able to discriminate, and is called fine. Although these two take the very same single sensation as their common object, their two marks are not present together — hence this is the factor of sustained reflection.

三、明喜支者，見細心分別思量，覺知十六觸等微妙珍寶，昔所未逢，是以心喜慶悅。又知所失欲樂甚少，今得初禪功德，其樂甚多。如是覺觀，利我不少，深心慶悅，踊躍無量，故名喜支。四、樂支者，行者於歡喜已後，其心恬然，受於觸中之樂，樂法娛心，安隱恬愉，故名樂支。問曰：喜樂有何異？答曰：如上覺觀分別，今喜樂亦爾。麁樂名喜，細樂名樂，亦可言麁喜為喜，細喜為樂。復次，喜樂雖俱是歡悅之相，而二相有異。喜根相應，故名喜；樂根相應，故名樂。踊躍心中，故名喜；恬靜心中，故名樂。復次，行者初緣得樂，心生歡喜，未及受樂，名喜；後緣喜情既息，以樂自娛，故名樂。譬如饑人得食，初得歡喜，未及受其味，故名喜；後得食之，方受味中之樂，故名樂。又如三禪有樂而無喜，故知二根有異。五、一心支者，經久受樂心息，雖有覺觸等事，而心不緣。既無分散，定住寂靜，故名一心支。此則略說初禪五支，次第而發，並據成就，立於支義。

Third, clarifying the factor of joy: seeing, through the fine mind's own discrimination and deliberation, that one has come to be aware of the subtle treasures within the sixteen tactile sensations, never before encountered — the mind is thereby joyful and delighted. Knowing, further, that what was lost, the pleasure of desire, was exceedingly slight, while what is now gained, the merit of the first dhyāna, is a pleasure exceedingly great: such initial thought and sustained reflection benefit oneself no small amount, and a deeply celebratory mind, leaping with joy beyond measure, is what is named the factor of joy.

Fourth, the factor of happiness: the practitioner, after this joy, the mind grown tranquil, receives the happiness present within the sensation itself — the dharma of happiness delighting the mind, at peace and content — this is named the factor of happiness. Question: how do joy and happiness differ? Answer: exactly as initial thought and sustained reflection were distinguished above, joy and happiness follow the same pattern: coarse pleasure is named joy, and fine pleasure is named happiness; or, alternatively, it may be said that coarse joy is joy, and fine joy is happiness. Furthermore, although joy and happiness are both marks of delight, the two marks nonetheless differ: correspondence with the joy-faculty is named joy; correspondence with the happiness-faculty is named happiness. A mind leaping with excitement is named joy; a mind at tranquil rest is named happiness. Furthermore: when the practitioner first takes happiness as an object, the mind gives rise to joy but has not yet actually received the happiness itself — this is named joy; afterward, once the sense of joy has come to rest, delighting in the happiness itself — this is named happiness. It is like a hungry person obtaining food: at first obtaining it, joy arises, but its taste has not yet actually been received — this is named joy; afterward, actually eating it, one then receives the happiness within its taste — this is named happiness. And, further still, the third dhyāna has happiness but no joy at all — hence it is known that these two faculties genuinely differ from one another.

Fifth, the factor of one-pointedness of mind: after happiness has long been received, the mind of happiness comes to rest; although the events of initial thought and sensation are still present, the mind no longer takes them as its object. Being now free of any scattering, concentration abides in stillness — this is named the factor of one-pointedness of mind. This, in brief, describes the first dhyāna's five factors, arising in sequence, and, with reference to their completion, establishes the meaning of "factor."

Reading note: The root-and-branch / grove-and-trees double image for 支 (factor) captures something genuinely subtle: dhyāna is one thing named for its parts (general-in-particular, like "grove" implying trees) and simultaneously many parts unified as one thing (particular-in-general, like "dhyāna" implying its five factors) — neither reduction fully applies. The bell simile for initial thought and sustained reflection solves a real problem the Abhidharma tradition itself debates: how can two supposedly simultaneous mental factors both occur "within a single mind" without being literally concurrent? By making them phases of one continuous event (a bell's sound is one sound with a coarse onset and fine decay) rather than two competing events, the treatise dissolves the puzzle rather than resolving it by fiat. The hungry-person simile for joy versus happiness is worth remembering going forward: joy is the anticipatory delight of first securing something good, happiness is the settled savoring that follows — and the text flags in advance that the third dhyāna will have the second without the first, evidence that these really are separate faculties and not just intensity-grades of the same thing.

Cause, Effect, Substance, and Function 初禪 · 明因果體用

Chinese Source

第三、明體用，即為二意：一者、明因果；二、明體用。一、因果者，遠而論之，行內外方便及入未到地等為因，感得初禪為果。今就近釋，但據初禪，自有因果。有人言：「四支為因，後一心支為果。」此即無文。今依《纓絡》解禪支，五支為因，第六默然心為定體，即以體為果。若通論因果，支支相因，悉得辨因果也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying substance and function: this itself has two parts — clarifying cause and effect; and clarifying substance and function.

First, as for cause and effect: discussed broadly, cultivating the external and internal expedient means and entering the "not-yet-arrived" state and the rest serve as the cause, and attaining the first dhyāna as its result serves as the effect. Discussed narrowly, however, the first dhyāna alone has its own cause and effect. Some say: "the four factors serve as the cause, and the subsequent factor of one-pointedness of mind serves as the effect" — but this has no textual basis. Here, following the Yingluo Sūtra's own explanation of the dhyāna-factors, the five factors serve as the cause, and the sixth, the mind of noble silence, serves as the substance of concentration — taking this substance itself as the effect. Discussed generally, cause and effect may be found in every factor causing the next in turn.

二、明體用，還以默然心為定體，從默然觸更動發起五支，此則為用。何以故？從體起用，用則在後，因則據前。問曰：因用體果，即無分別。答曰：不然。雖同據五支明因用，就默然為體果，然義意有異。所以者何？因中五支，為感默然之果。因默然之果，起五支之法。此就默然為體，五支為用。例如三十七品，道前為因，道後為用。問曰：有時從默然體發勝品五支，後得增勝默然，此義云何？答曰：若爾，即還應說因果，若無勝品，但是體用。

Second, clarifying substance and function: taking the mind of noble silence once again as the substance of concentration, from this silent contact the five factors are further stirred and brought forth — this, then, is the function. Why is this? Function arises from substance, and so function comes afterward while cause is what precedes it. Question: if cause and function, substance and effect, are thus paired, is there then no distinction between them at all? Answer: this does not follow. Although the five factors are used, in both cases, to clarify cause and function, and noble silence to clarify substance and effect, their sense nonetheless differs. Why is this? Within the sense of cause, the five factors serve to bring about the effect of noble silence. Within the sense of function, it is the effect of noble silence that gives rise to the dharma of the five factors — here, noble silence serves as substance, and the five factors serve as function. This is comparable to the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment: what precedes the path serves as cause, and what follows the path serves as function. Question: at times, from the substance of noble silence, a superior grade of the five factors is brought forth, and afterward an even more excellent noble silence is attained — what is the sense of this? Answer: if this is so, then cause and effect should indeed be spoken of once again; but where there is no superior grade involved, it is simply a matter of substance and function.

Reading note: This section's real subtlety is that the same five factors get assigned opposite roles depending on the frame: as cause, they bring about the substance of noble silence as their effect; as function, that very same substance of noble silence is what gives rise to them. Nothing here is inconsistent — it is the same relationship viewed forward (cause producing effect) and backward (substance expressing itself as function) — but the treatise is careful to flag exactly when a second, genuine cause-and-effect relationship reappears within what would otherwise be a single substance-and-function cycle: whenever a superior grade of the five factors leads to a genuinely more excellent noble silence than before, rather than simply re-expressing the same one.

Fourth, clarifying depth: when the first dhyāna arises, the five factors and the mind of noble silence are not without their own differences of coarseness and fineness from one moment to the next, and so there is shallowness and depth, which must be properly distinguished. Why is this? As the treatise says: "When the Buddha's disciples cultivate the various dhyānas, there is a lower, a middle, and an upper grade, named the three grades; and, separating out each of these three grades into three further grades in turn, there are nine grades of shallowness and depth in all. Examined finely, there should be measureless grades beyond even this." Non-Buddhist practitioners, too, attain concentrations with their own shallowness and depth, though this is not spoken of in terms of grades for them, since their minds are coarse, unable to perceive it within concentration itself, and, not cultivating the uncontaminated wisdom that illuminates it, the mind fails to become aware of it at all.

Within establishing these grades to clarify depth, there are, in turn, two senses: with reference to the same kind; and with reference to differing kinds. As for the same kind: as when the single sensation of movement arises, one becomes aware of it growing gradually deeper, all the way through nine grades. As for differing kinds: as when, once the sensation of movement has passed, some other sensation then arises in turn — although the marks of the sensations differ, the sense of concentration nonetheless grows gradually deeper, surpassing what came before. Furthermore, with reference to the five factors, depth may likewise be clarified in two senses: with reference to the same kind, as when the five factors arise together with a given sensation, there is already a mark of shallowness and depth present; and with reference to differing kinds, as when the five factors grow and increase one after another in sequence, each single factor, in turn, also has its own mark of shallowness and depth.

問曰：為當要發十六觸等具足，方名初禪，為當亦發一一觸亦名初禪？答曰：初禪有二種：一、具足；二、不具足。若具發十六觸，此即是具足初禪為勝；若發一兩觸等，亦得名初禪。何以故？以一觸具有十種定法，眷屬五支成就故，但此初禪不名具足。

Answer: the first dhyāna is of two kinds: complete; and incomplete. If all sixteen sensations arise together in full, this is precisely the complete first dhyāna, and is superior; but if only one or two sensations arise, this too may be named the first dhyāna. Why is this? Because even a single sensation is already complete with the ten dharmas of concentration, its retinue of five factors already accomplished — it is simply that this form of the first dhyāna is not called "complete."

第五、明進退者，證初禪時，有四種人根性不同：一者、退分；二者、住分；三者、進分；四者、達分。一、退分者，若人得初禪時，或有因緣，或無因緣，而便退失。失有二種：一者、更修還得；二者、更修不得。所謂過去今世障法起故，末世之中，此退分多。二、住分者，有人得初禪已，即不退失，定心安隱。住分亦有二種：一者、任運自住；二者、守護乃住。三、進分者，有人得初禪時，即便進得勝品，乃至進得上地。進有二種：一者、不加功力，任運自進；二者、勤修乃進。四、達分者，有人得初禪時，於此定中即發見思無漏，達到涅槃。達亦有二種：一者、任運自達；二者、修觀乃達。

Fifth, clarifying progress and regression: at the time the first dhyāna is realized, there are four kinds of practitioner, differing in capacity — those prone to regression; those who abide; those who progress; and those who penetrate through.

First, as for those prone to regression: some people, upon attaining the first dhyāna, whether through some cause and condition or through none at all, regress and lose it. This loss is of two kinds: regaining it through further cultivation; or failing to regain it even through further cultivation, owing to some obstructing dharma from the past or the present life. In this latter age, those prone to regression of this kind are many.

Second, as for those who abide: some people, having attained the first dhyāna, do not regress or lose it at all, the concentrated mind remaining secure. Abiding, too, is of two kinds: abiding spontaneously, of its own accord; and abiding only through guarding and protecting it.

Third, as for those who progress: some people, upon attaining the first dhyāna, immediately progress further to a superior grade, and even progress all the way to a higher stage. Progress, too, is of two kinds: progressing spontaneously, without any application of effort; and progressing only through diligent cultivation.

Fourth, as for those who penetrate through: some people, upon attaining the first dhyāna, within this very concentration, at once bring forth the uncontaminated attainment of the path of vision and the path of cultivation, penetrating through all the way to nirvāṇa. This too is of two kinds: penetrating through spontaneously, of its own accord; and penetrating through only through cultivating contemplation.

復次，此四分定中，復有四種人根性不同，如退分中四者：一、自有退退得，九品漸退，乃至併失；二、自有退住得，九品退至八品、七品，便住不失；三、自有退進得，九品退至八品、七品，乃至一品，從一品還進；四者、自有退達得，九品已退，還八、七等品，乃至一品，於其中間忽然發真無漏。餘住分、進分、達分各有四義亦如是。是中或有因放逸障故退，或因懺悔清淨故住、進、達，此義眾多，不可具辨。

Furthermore, within these four divisions of concentration, there are, in turn, four further kinds of practitioner differing in capacity, exactly as the four given within the "prone to regression" division: some regress and, having regressed, remain merely at regression — the nine grades gradually regressing, all the way to total loss; some regress and, having regressed, come to abide — the nine grades regressing to the eighth or seventh grade, and then abiding there without further loss; some regress and, having regressed, come to progress — the nine grades regressing to the eighth or seventh grade, or even to the first grade, and then progressing forward again from that first grade; and some regress and, having regressed, come to penetrate through — the nine grades having already regressed back to the eighth, seventh, or even the first grade, and then, in the midst of this very interval, suddenly bringing forth the true uncontaminated attainment. The remaining divisions — abiding, progressing, and penetrating through — each likewise have these same four further senses. Among these, regression may occur due to the obstruction of heedlessness, or abiding, progress, and penetration may occur due to the purity brought about through repentance — this sense is itself manifold, and cannot be distinguished here in full.

第六、明初禪功德者，如前偈說：「已得離婬火，則獲清涼定。」此偈自可為二功德：一者、離過德；二者、善心德。此對止、行二善，亦可類於智、斷二德，故《大集經》云：「初禪者，亦名為離，亦名為具。所言離者，謂離五蓋；所言具者，謂具五支。」今釋所以得初禪時離貪欲蓋者，欲界之樂麁淺，今得初禪之樂細妙，以勝奪輕，故能離五欲。離瞋者，欲界苦緣逼迫故生瞋，得初禪時，無有諸逼迫，樂境在心，故無瞋。能離睡眠者，得初禪時，身心明淨，定法所持，心不昏亂，觸樂自娛，故不睡也。所以能離掉悔者，禪定持心，任運不動，故能離掉。由掉故有悔，無掉即無悔。離疑者，未得初禪時，疑有定、無定，今親證定，疑心即除，故得離疑，是故得初禪時具有離過之德。

Sixth, clarifying the merit of the first dhyāna: as the verse already cited above says, "Having already gained freedom from the fire of lust, one obtains a cool, refreshing concentration." This verse may itself be understood as two kinds of merit: the merit of freedom from fault; and the merit of a wholesome mind. This corresponds to the two forms of wholesomeness, stopping and practice, and may likewise be compared to the two merits of wisdom and severance. Hence the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "The first dhyāna is named both 'freedom' and 'completeness.' As for 'freedom': this means freedom from the five hindrances. As for 'completeness': this means completeness in the five factors."

Here, it is explained why, upon attaining the first dhyāna, one gains freedom from the hindrance of craving: the pleasure of the desire realm is coarse and shallow, while the pleasure now attained through the first dhyāna is fine and subtle — the superior displacing the inferior, and so one is able to gain freedom from the five desires. As for gaining freedom from anger: within the desire realm, the oppressive conditions of suffering give rise to anger; upon attaining the first dhyāna, there is no such oppression present at all, and, with a state of happiness held in the mind, there is no anger. As for being able to gain freedom from drowsiness: upon attaining the first dhyāna, body and mind grow bright and pure, sustained by the dharma of concentration, the mind no longer dull or disordered, delighting of itself in the joy of the tactile sensation — hence there is no drowsiness. As for being able to gain freedom from restlessness and regret: dhyāna-concentration sustains the mind, unmoved of its own accord, and so one is able to gain freedom from restlessness; and since regret arises on account of restlessness, where there is no restlessness, there is no regret either. As for gaining freedom from doubt: before attaining the first dhyāna, one doubts whether concentration exists or not; now, having personally realized concentration, the doubting mind is thereby removed — hence one gains freedom from doubt as well. Hence, upon attaining the first dhyāna, one is complete in every one of these merits of freedom from fault.

得初禪時具足善心功德者，約五支明功德善法，義如前說。復次，若得初禪，即具信、戒、捨、定、聞、慧等善心也。

As for being complete in the merit of a wholesome mind upon attaining the first dhyāna: this is clarified with reference to the five factors' own merit and wholesome dharma, the sense of which is as already given above. Furthermore, upon attaining the first dhyāna, one is also complete in such wholesome states of mind as faith, moral discipline, generosity, concentration, learning, and wisdom.

Reading note: The four-by-four grid of practitioner types (regress/abide/progress/penetrate, each itself capable of regressing, abiding, progressing, or penetrating from wherever it lands) is the same combinatorial move fascicle 3's closing section made with unpredictable dhyāna-arising — demonstrating, by simply continuing to subdivide, that attainment is not a ladder climbed once and kept, but a genuinely unstable state whose trajectory depends on ongoing diligence or heedlessness. That the text flags "in this latter age, those prone to regression are many" is a rare moment of the treatise commenting directly on the difficulty of its own historical moment rather than describing the dharma in the abstract. The merits close on the same two-fold pattern used for verifying wholesome capacity back in fascicle 3 — freedom from fault paired with positive wholesome qualities — confirming that the first dhyāna is being assessed by exactly the same diagnostic apparatus already built for wholesome roots generally, not some separate standard. This closes the whole of the first dhyāna.

次明第二禪者，如偈說：知二法亂心，雖善而應離。如大水澄靜，波蕩亦無見。譬如人大極，安隱睡眠時，若有喚呼聲，其心大惱亂。攝心入禪時，以覺觀為惱，是故除覺觀，得入一識處。內心清淨故，定生得喜樂，得入此二禪，喜勇心大悅。佛以此偈中，廣明中間禪、二禪相。今明二禪有三義：一者、釋名；二、明修行；三、明證相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying the second dhyāna: as the verse says: "Knowing that these two dharmas disturb the mind, though wholesome, they should be left behind. Like a great body of water, still and clear, once its waves have settled, nothing further is seen stirring it. It is like a person, utterly exhausted, resting in peaceful sleep — should a shout suddenly call out, the mind is greatly disturbed and troubled. Gathering the mind to enter dhyāna, initial thought and sustained reflection are themselves felt as a disturbance; hence, removing initial thought and sustained reflection, one is able to enter the abode of a single consciousness. Because the inner mind becomes pure, concentration arises and joy and happiness are attained; entering this second dhyāna, joy leaps up and the mind is greatly delighted." The Buddha, within this verse, clarifies at length the marks of both the intermediate dhyāna and the second dhyāna. Here, the second dhyāna is clarified in three parts: explaining the name; clarifying its cultivation; and clarifying the marks of its realization.

第一、釋名者，次初禪後，故說二禪。既離覺觀，於第二心得勝支功德，故名二禪，亦名無覺無觀三昧。所以者何？得中間禪，斷覺，二禪內淨發，故斷觀，亦名聖默然定。以覺觀語言滅故，故名默然。若得無漏正慧，入此定故，即名聖默然，《地持論》中說名「喜俱禪」。此定生時，與喜俱發故。

First, explaining the name: coming after the first dhyāna, it is thus called the second dhyāna. Having left behind initial thought and sustained reflection, at this second stage of mind one attains a superior factor of merit — hence it is named the second dhyāna, also called the samādhi without initial thought or sustained reflection. Why is this? Upon attaining the intermediate dhyāna, initial thought is cut off; and, as the second dhyāna's own inner purity arises, sustained reflection is cut off as well — hence it is also called the noble concentration of silence, since the path of language through initial thought and sustained reflection has been extinguished, and so it is named "silence." Should one attain uncontaminated right wisdom and enter this concentration, it is then named noble silence in the fullest sense. The Bodhisattva-bhūmi Treatise names it "the dhyāna joined with joy," since, when this concentration arises, it arises together with joy.

第二、修習，即為二：一者、明修習方法；二、明證中間禪。今明修二禪者，若凡夫人，亦當先修六行，佛弟子多修八聖種。聖種義如前說。六行者，謂於初禪第六默然心中厭離覺觀，觀初禪為下苦，知二法動亂，逼惱定心，故為苦。從覺觀生喜樂定等，故為麁。此覺觀法障二禪內淨，故名障。攀上勝者，二禪內淨安隱，勝初禪覺觀動亂之定。妙者，喜定因內淨而發，是為微妙。出者，若得二禪，即心得出離覺觀等障。

Second, cultivation: this itself has two parts — clarifying the method of cultivation; and clarifying the realization of the intermediate dhyāna.

Here, clarifying the cultivation of the second dhyāna: an ordinary person should first cultivate the six practices; a disciple of the Buddha, for the most part, cultivates the eight noble lineages instead — the sense of the noble lineages has already been given above. As for the six practices: within the first dhyāna's sixth factor, the mind of noble silence, one grows weary of and turns away from initial thought and sustained reflection, contemplating the first dhyāna as inferior and as suffering — knowing that these two dharmas disturb and trouble the concentrated mind, and so are suffering; that, arising from initial thought and sustained reflection, joy, happiness, and concentration itself are thereby coarse; that this dharma of initial thought and sustained reflection obstructs the second dhyāna's own inner purity, and so is an obstruction; that reaching for what is superior, the second dhyāna's own inner purity and peace surpasses the first dhyāna's disturbed concentration troubled by initial thought and sustained reflection, and so is excellent; that the joy and concentration born through inner purity is subtle and wondrous; and that, upon attaining the second dhyāna, the mind gains release from the obstruction of initial thought and sustained reflection, and so is an exit.

復次，行者既知初禪之過障於二禪，今欲遠離，當依三種方便：一、不受不著故得離；二、訶責故得離；三、觀析故得離。譬如世人共事後，見其過失，心欲令去，亦用三法：一者、上人利智不與顏色，前人自去；二者、若不去，應須數責，彼即自去；三者、若不去，當與杖加之，自便去也。若得此三意，可以離初禪覺觀之過。

Furthermore: the practitioner, already knowing that the fault of the first dhyāna obstructs the second, and now wishing to leave it far behind, should rely on three expedient means: gaining freedom through neither receiving nor becoming attached to it; gaining freedom through reproach; and gaining freedom through analytic contemplation. It is like people in the world who, having worked together with someone and then seen their faults, wish to see them go — they, too, use three methods: first, a person of superior wisdom simply withholds any warm expression, and the other departs of their own accord; second, if they do not leave, one must reproach them repeatedly, and they will then leave of their own accord; and third, if they still do not leave, one must apply the rod, and they will then be made to leave. If one grasps these same three senses, one is able to leave behind the fault of the first dhyāna's initial thought and sustained reflection.

二者、明中間禪發相。行者既能深心訶責初禪覺觀，覺觀既滅，五支及默然悉謝。以離初禪，二禪未生，於其中間亦有定法，亦得名禪，但不牢固，無支等扶助之法，所以其心蔑蔑屑屑。然諸師多說為轉寂，心轉初禪默然也。《釋論》說名觀相應，此定以六行觀為體，住此定中。若離六行觀者，則多生憂悔。憂悔心生，則永不發二禪，乃至轉寂亦失，或時還更發初禪，或時合初禪亦失。因是無法自居，到此定時，為山之功而少一簣，當善自慎。經中說為無覺有觀三昧，初禪及默然已謝，但住觀相應心中，修二禪故。

Second, clarifying the mark of the intermediate dhyāna's arising: the practitioner, now able to reproach the first dhyāna's initial thought and sustained reflection with a deep and thorough mind, initial thought and sustained reflection thereby extinguished, the five factors and noble silence both pass away as well. Having left the first dhyāna behind, with the second dhyāna not yet arisen, there is, in the interval between them, a further concentration-dharma present as well, which may also be named dhyāna — though it is not yet secure or firm, without any factor to sustain and support it, and so the mind feels faint and fragmentary. The various masters, for the most part, speak of this as the "shifting stillness," the mind shifting away from the first dhyāna's own noble silence. The Exegetical Treatise names it "correspondence with contemplation," this concentration taking the contemplation of the six practices as its own substance, abiding within this very concentration.

Should one depart from the contemplation of the six practices, worry and regret arise in abundance instead. Once a mind of worry and regret has arisen, the second dhyāna will never be brought forth at all — and even this very "shifting stillness" will itself be lost; at times, the first dhyāna is instead brought forth once again, and, at other times, even the first dhyāna itself is lost along with it. Left, through this, with no proper footing at all, reaching this concentration and yet falling short of completion for the lack of a single basketful of earth — one must be exceedingly careful here. The sūtra speaks of this as the samādhi without initial thought but with sustained reflection alone: the first dhyāna and its noble silence having already passed away, one simply abides within the mind that corresponds with contemplation, cultivating the second dhyāna from there.

Reading note: The three methods for leaving the first dhyāna behind (withholding approval, reproach, force) are the same three-tier escalation already used against demons and against wrong marks in earlier fascicles, now applied to something wholesome rather than harmful — a reminder that even a genuine attainment must eventually be let go of using the same disciplined methods used against outright faults. The intermediate dhyāna's own warning is one of the treatise's more vivid images: reaching this unstable, unsupported halfway state and abandoning the six practices out of worry or impatience is "like a mountain of accomplished work left short for the lack of a single basketful of earth" — the point being that the very last stretch before the second dhyāna is also the most precarious, since neither the first dhyāna's own five factors nor the second dhyāna's own inner purity is yet available to stabilize the mind.

Third, clarifying the mark of the second dhyāna's own arising: this, too, opens into six parts — clarifying dhyāna's own arising; clarifying the meaning of its factors; clarifying its cause, effect, substance, and function; clarifying its depth; clarifying its progress and regression; and clarifying its merits.

First, clarifying the mark of the second dhyāna's own arising: the practitioner, within the intermediate dhyāna, the mind free of worry and regret, applies effort single-mindedly, devoted and unceasing; afterward, the mind grows calm and clear, without any scattering at all — this is named the "not-yet-arrived" state. Hence the treatise verse says, "one is able to enter the abode of a single consciousness" — this is precisely the arising of the second dhyāna's own preliminary concentration.

Question: the treatise speaks of a "not-yet-arrived" state only before the first dhyāna — why, then, is a "not-yet-arrived" state now spoken of before the second dhyāna as well? Answer: the treatise speaks in general terms, and so speaks of only a single such state. The Śāriputra-abhidharma-śāstra, however, speaks of four "not-yet-arrived" states and four intermediate dhyānas — here, following this same sense, a "not-yet-arrived" state and an intermediate state are spoken of at every stage.

經久不失不退，專心不止，於後其心豁然明淨皎潔，定心與喜俱發。亦如人從暗室中出，見外日月光明，其心豁然明亮。內淨十種功德眷屬俱發之義，具如初禪發相，但以從內淨定俱發為異耳。復次，二禪喜樂等發，不從外來，一心澄淨大喜，美妙清淨，勝初禪故，論云：「內心清淨故，定生得喜樂，得入此二禪，喜勇心大悅。」

Once this state has persisted for a long while without loss or regression, the mind applied single-mindedly and without ceasing, afterward the mind opens out, bright, pure, and radiant, the concentrated mind arising together with joy — just like a person emerging from a darkened room and seeing the sun and moon's own outer light, the mind opening out, bright and clear. The sense of the ten kinds of merit arising together as its retinue, through this very inner purity, is exactly as already given for the first dhyāna's own arising — differing only in that here they arise together through inner-purity concentration specifically. Furthermore, the second dhyāna's joy and happiness arise not from anything external at all, but from a single mind of pure clarity and great joy, wondrous and pure, surpassing the first dhyāna — hence the treatise says: "Because the inner mind becomes pure, concentration arises and joy and happiness are attained; entering this second dhyāna, joy leaps up and the mind is greatly delighted."

云何名為內淨？遠而言之，對外塵故，說內淨；近而言之，對內垢故，說內淨。所以者何？如初禪中得觸樂時，身即明淨，兼令心淨。觸是身識相應，故名外淨。今待初禪外淨，故說二禪心識相應為內淨，亦令身淨，淨身故，名外淨。內淨是心淨，淨從心出，令身亦淨，故言內淨。今言待內垢，故說內淨者，初禪之中，心為覺觀所動，故名為內垢。今得二禪，內心無有覺觀之垢，故名為內淨。

What is meant by "inner purity"? Discussed broadly, it is spoken of in contrast to external sense-objects; discussed narrowly, it is spoken of in contrast to inward defilement. Why is this? Just as, within the first dhyāna, when tactile happiness is attained, the body itself becomes bright and pure, and this, in turn, causes the mind to become pure as well — since tactile sensation corresponds to body-consciousness, this is named outer purity. Here, in relation to the first dhyāna's own outer purity, the second dhyāna's correspondence with mind-consciousness is said to be inner purity — and this, in turn, also causes the body to become pure; because the body is thereby purified, this is named outer purity in its own right. Inner purity is purity of mind — purity issuing from the mind causes the body, too, to become pure — hence it is called "inner" purity. As for saying it is spoken of in contrast to inward defilement: within the first dhyāna, the mind is stirred by initial thought and sustained reflection, and so this is named inward defilement; now, having attained the second dhyāna, the inner mind is free of the defilement of initial thought and sustained reflection, and so is named inner purity.

言定生得喜樂者，上於初禪說離生，今此說定生，義意云何？正言初禪離欲界，生色界定法故，二禪既無此義，但說定生。問曰：若爾，虛空定亦應說離生邪？答曰：不然。前已受名，故不應重說。又且虛空離色界，但發定之時而無支林等法生，故不說離生。問曰：初禪亦有喜樂，與此何異？答曰：彼從覺觀生喜樂，與身識相應。此中喜樂從內心生，還與意識相應，以此為異。

As for the phrase "concentration arises, and joy and happiness are attained": above, in the case of the first dhyāna, "birth" was spoken of; here, "concentration arises" is spoken of instead — what is the sense of this difference? Properly speaking, the first dhyāna is spoken of as "birth" because it leaves the desire realm behind and gives rise to the dharma of form-realm concentration; the second dhyāna, having no such sense of a further such birth, is instead spoken of simply as "concentration arising."

Question: if this is so, should the concentration of boundless space not likewise be spoken of as "birth"? Answer: this does not follow. The name has already been given once before, and so should not be spoken of again in the same way; and, further, the concentration of boundless space, though it leaves the form realm behind, has no grove-of-factors dharma arising at the moment concentration is brought forth, and so is not spoken of as "birth" either.

Question: the first dhyāna, too, has joy and happiness — how does this differ from the second dhyāna's own? Answer: there, joy and happiness arise from initial thought and sustained reflection, and correspond with body-consciousness. Here, joy and happiness arise from the inner mind itself, and correspond instead with mind-consciousness — this is how they differ.

Reading note: "Inner purity" gets the same double-angle treatment "factor" received in the first dhyāna — broad sense (versus external objects) and narrow sense (versus the internal defilement of initial thought and sustained reflection), plus a further inner/outer distinction where the second dhyāna's own "inner" purity of mind causes a secondary "outer" purity of body, mirroring but surpassing the first dhyāna's purity, which ran the other direction (body to mind). The precise vocabulary distinction between "birth" (reserved for the first dhyāna's crossing from the desire realm into the form realm) and "concentration arising" (used from the second dhyāna onward, since no further such crossing occurs) is a good example of how carefully this treatise tracks its own terminology — a difference in wording here is never incidental, always cashing out to a specific doctrinal point when questioned.

Second, clarifying the meaning of the factors: the second dhyāna has four factors — inner purity; joy; happiness; and one-pointedness of mind. Here, the meaning of "factor" is clarified along the same pattern, having both a general and a particular sense — the meanings of "sustaining" and "branching apart" are as already given above.

First, as for the factor of inner purity: having left initial thought and sustained reflection behind, concentration is brought forth in reliance on the inwardly pure mind, radiant and distinct, free of any defilement — hence this is named the factor of inner purity. Second, the factor of joy: concentration arises together with joy, the practitioner deeply celebrating within, the inner mind giving rise to joy, concentration, and the other ten kinds of meritorious, wholesome dharma — delight beyond measure — hence this is named the factor of joy. Third, the factor of happiness: the practitioner receives the happiness present within this joy, tranquil, contented, and continuously delightful — hence this is named the factor of happiness. Question: how is it that the Yingluo Sūtra establishes a factor of pliancy before one-pointedness of mind? Answer: this is still inner purity, simply given a different name after joy and happiness. Why is this? "Pliancy" means release, and release means ease — since there is, within, no defiling entanglement left at all, one is at ease and free, no longer troubled by sound's own sting or drawn along by initial thought and sustained reflection — hence it is called "pliancy." Question: why does the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra establish only three factors, without inner purity at all? Answer: that sūtra simply retains the essentials and omits the rest. Since the second dhyāna is named the concentration joined with joy, and joy is spoken of precisely because initial thought and sustained reflection have been left behind, the presence of inner-purity concentration is thereby already implied. The sense of the general and particular establishing of factors follows the same pattern as before, and may be understood accordingly.

Third, clarifying substance, function, cause, and effect: as the Yingluo Sūtra says, the second dhyāna's four factors serve as the cause, and the fifth, the mind of noble silence, serves as the substance of concentration — turning this back and forth to clarify the sense of cause, effect, substance, and function is no different from the first dhyāna. Fourth, clarifying depth: following the same pattern as the first dhyāna, proceeding from the initial grade in sequence to ever more excellent grades — this is the mark of shallowness and depth, which may be readily seen, and is not separately clarified here. Fifth, the sense of progress and regression follows the same pattern as the first dhyāna as well.

Sixth, clarifying merit: this itself again has two parts — the merit of freedom from fault; and the merit of a wholesome mind. Hence the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "The second dhyāna is likewise named both 'freedom' and 'completeness.' As for 'freedom': this means freedom from the five hindrances. As for 'completeness': this means completeness in the four factors." As for freedom from fault: this means freedom from the fault of initial thought and sustained reflection. As for completeness: from the inner-purity, joyful mind, complete in itself, faith, reverence, shame, remorse, and the other six wholesome dharmas are brought forth.

Reading note: "Pliancy" (猗) turning out to be inner purity under another name — introduced late in the sequence by one sūtra but absent from another's list entirely — is a good illustration of how this treatise handles cross-textual variation generally: rather than picking one canonical enumeration as correct, it shows how each source's apparent addition or omission still resolves back to the same underlying structure once properly understood. This closes the whole of the second dhyāna.

When the concentration of happiness first arises, it is not yet spread through the whole body, and, in the interval, there are commonly three faults present: first, the concentration of happiness being still shallow, the mind sinks down, with little use of wisdom present; second, the concentration of happiness being still slight, the mind and intellect become excitedly stirred, and so it is not yet at peace; and third, the mind of this concentration of happiness, together with the power of wisdom, being continuous and wondrous, gives rise, for the most part, to craving and attachment, the mind growing intoxicated by it. Hence the sūtra says: "This happiness is something the noble ones are able to relinquish; for everyone else, relinquishing it is difficult." As the third dhyāna is about to arise, these three faults are present, and so the concentration of happiness is unable to grow and spread through the whole body — the practitioner should properly regulate and adjust it.

One should use three methods: first, if the mind sinks down, one should rouse it up using mindfulness, diligence, wisdom, and other such dharmas; second, if the mind becomes excitedly stirred, one should hold it in check by recollecting the dharma of samādhi-concentration; and third, if the mind grows intoxicated, one should recollect the happiness yet to come and the other excellent, wondrous dharma-gates, to awaken oneself, so that the mind does not become attached. If the practitioner is able to skillfully cultivate these three methods, regulating and adjusting the concentration of happiness, one should know that the dharma of happiness is bound to grow and spread throughout the entire body — hence the sūtra says: "The third dhyāna receives happiness pervading the whole body."

Answer: when happiness pervades the whole body, every pore of the body is filled with delight. At that time, although the five faculties of feeling give rise to no consciousness through any external sense-object, the dharma of happiness issues forth from within, filling every faculty completely — within each of the five faculties, delight and happiness are present throughout; it is simply that, with no external object present to correspond to, none of the five sense-consciousnesses is thereby brought forth. The faculties of feeling depend upon the body, and, the body's happiness now complete, the faculties of feeling, too, become thoroughly delighted, this happiness corresponding with mind-consciousness — and, because consciousness itself is filled from within, happiness is thus received throughout the whole body; hence the Buddha says that the third dhyāna's happiness is received throughout the entire body.

Furthermore: the first dhyāna's happiness arises from without, corresponding with the outer consciousnesses but not with mind-consciousness, and so its inner happiness is not yet complete. The second dhyāna's happiness, although it arises from within, nonetheless arises from joy, corresponding with the joy-faculty but not with the happiness-faculty — happiness there still depends upon joy, and, since even joy itself is not yet pervasive, how much less could happiness be? Here, the third dhyāna's happiness arises from within, with happiness itself as the ruling factor, no excited joy present within it at all; through the causes and conditions of mindfulness and wisdom, happiness is made to grow and spread throughout the whole body, filling both within and without, tranquil and delightful — the very foremost and highest of every worldly happiness. Hence the Buddha says that the karmic result of practicing loving-kindness lies within the ground of universal purity.

問曰：佛說三禪有二時樂：一、受樂；二、快樂。約何義說邪？答曰：實爾。快樂樂者，樂定初發，未遍身也。受樂樂者，樂既增長，遍身受。譬如石中之泉，從內涌出，盈流於外，遍滿溝渠。三禪之樂亦復如是。

Question: the Buddha speaks of the third dhyāna as having happiness at two distinct times: received happiness; and immediate happiness — with reference to what sense is this spoken of? Answer: this is indeed so. As for immediate happiness: this is the concentration of happiness at its first arising, not yet spread throughout the body. As for received happiness: this is once happiness has already grown and is received throughout the whole body. It is like a spring within rock, gushing forth from within and overflowing outward, filling every channel and ditch completely — the happiness of the third dhyāna is exactly the same.

Reading note: The three faults during happiness's initial, unstable phase (sinking, excited overshoot, intoxicated attachment) map onto exactly the three regulatory tools given as their antidotes — rousing energy for the first, calming recollection for the second, and reflective perspective for the third — the same "matched antidote" logic from fascicle 4's treatment of unwholesome roots, now applied to managing a wholesome state rather than curing an unwholesome one. The claim that third-dhyāna happiness reaches all five sense-faculties without any of the five sense-consciousnesses actually firing is a genuinely careful psychological distinction: bodily delight can saturate the sensory system directly, from within, without needing any external stimulus to trigger it — which is precisely what makes it categorically different from ordinary sensory pleasure.

就此支中，約義自有四意：一者、三為方便支，二為證支。用念、慧、智三支調適樂定，令速得增長遍身，故說為方便支。受身樂、一心二支為證。此二一時發，是三禪之正主也。二者、四是自地立支。一望下地，立慧、念、樂，一心約自地立，捨支約捨下地喜不悔立也。三者、五支通得說作方便支。三如上說，下二何故亦得名方便邪？正言修樂增長，能感後樂故。一心亦爾，復望第六默然定體，五支例得名，因例得名方便。四者、五支通得說為證支。所證樂定時，自然生捨愛念樂定，如母護子，不由人勸，等智自發，籌量調適，五支俱皆屬證。問曰：若爾，前說並是方便，後說是證得，此意豈不碩相違反邪？答曰：並有其義，細尋自見。

Within these factors, there are, in terms of their sense, four further points to make.

First: three serve as expedient-means factors, and two serve as realization-factors. Using the three factors of mindfulness, wisdom, and equanimity to regulate and adjust the concentration of happiness, causing it to grow and spread throughout the body all the more quickly, these are called expedient-means factors; receiving bodily happiness and one-pointedness of mind, these two factors, serve as the realization — these two arise together at one and the same time, and are the true, principal factors of the third dhyāna.

Second: all four are established as factors with reference to this very stage itself. Looking toward the lower stage, wisdom, mindfulness, and happiness are established; one-pointedness of mind is established with reference to this stage on its own; and the factor of equanimity is established with reference to relinquishing the lower stage's own joy without regret.

Third: all five factors may, in common, be called expedient-means factors. The first three are as already described above — why, then, can the remaining two also be named "expedient means"? Properly speaking, because the cultivation of happiness's own growth is able to bring about further happiness to come. One-pointedness of mind is exactly the same: looking further still toward the sixth, the substance of noble-silence concentration, the five factors may, by the same pattern, all be named "expedient means" as its cause.

Fourth: all five factors may, in common, be called realization-factors. At the very moment this concentration of happiness is realized, equanimity, affection, mindfulness, and the concentration of happiness all arise naturally of themselves — like a mother protecting her child, needing no urging from anyone else — equanimous wisdom arising spontaneously, deliberating and adjusting of its own accord; all five factors belong, in this sense, to realization. Question: if this is so, does this not directly contradict what was said just before — that some are expedient means and others are realization? Answer: both senses hold their own validity; examined closely, this can be seen for oneself.

第二、明支前後不同者，諸經及論各異。立次第，如《成實論》，明五支次第者：捨、念、智、受樂、一心。《阿毘曇》明次第：慧、念、樂、捨、一心。《大集》所出次第者：念、捨、慧、安、定。《纓絡經》中明次第：樂、護、念、智、一心。《釋論》明次第，文則不定，或與《成論》同，或與《纓絡》同。問曰：何獨此明支次第不定，餘禪不然邪？答曰：初禪等唯有一樂故，今三禪有二種樂故。由此二樂前後異故，中間迴互不定，是故諸經次第各立不同，而悉有意，必須得所以。

Second, clarifying the differing sequence of the factors: the various sūtras and treatises each differ in the sequence they establish. As the Satyasiddhi-śāstra clarifies the sequence of the five factors: equanimity, mindfulness, wisdom, received happiness, one-pointedness of mind. The Abhidharma clarifies the sequence as: wisdom, mindfulness, happiness, equanimity, one-pointedness of mind. The sequence given in the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra is: mindfulness, equanimity, wisdom, ease, concentration. The sequence clarified in the Yingluo Sūtra is: happiness, protection, mindfulness, wisdom, one-pointedness of mind. The sequence clarified in the Exegetical Treatise is not fixed, at times agreeing with the Satyasiddhi-śāstra, and at times agreeing with the Yingluo Sūtra.

Question: why is it only here that the sequence of the factors is unfixed, while it is not so for the other dhyānas? Answer: the first dhyāna and the others each have only a single kind of happiness; here, the third dhyāna has two kinds of happiness. Because these two kinds of happiness differ in their own before-and-after order, the sequence shifts back and forth without a single fixed order — hence the various sūtras each establish a differing sequence, though each carries its own sense, which must be properly grasped in turn.

第三、明體用。如《纓絡經》云：「五支為因，第六默然心為體。」第四、明淺深。五、明進退，並如前釋。第六、明功德者，具有離過、善心二德，如《大集經》說：「所言離者，謂離五蓋；具者，謂具五支。」據別，則但三禪獨有離喜過之德，餘義類上可知。

Third, clarifying substance and function: as the Yingluo Sūtra says, the five factors serve as the cause, and the sixth, the mind of noble silence, serves as the substance. Fourth, clarifying depth. Fifth, clarifying progress and regression: both follow the same pattern as already explained above. Sixth, clarifying merit: this is complete in both the merit of freedom from fault and the merit of a wholesome mind, as the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "As for freedom: this means freedom from the five hindrances. As for completeness: this means completeness in the five factors." Discussed in its own particular sense, it is the third dhyāna alone that carries the distinct merit of freedom from the fault of joy; the remaining senses may be understood by the same pattern as above.

Reading note: The treatise's explanation for why only the third dhyāna's factor-sequence varies across sources — because it alone has two distinct kinds of happiness whose relative ordering different texts weigh differently — is a good example of its general method: rather than treating textual disagreement as a problem to explain away, it treats the disagreement itself as evidence pointing to a genuine structural feature of the third dhyāna that the other three don't share. The fourfold analysis of the same five factors (three as means versus two as realization; all four latecomers relative to the stage below; all five as means toward what follows; all five as realization in their own right) is the treatise demonstrating that a single list can be sliced along several different valid axes at once — a caution against assuming any one of its own enumerations is the only correct way to see the material. This closes the whole of the third dhyāna.

The Fourth Dhyāna: Its Name, Cultivation, and Arising 四禪 · 釋名、明修習、明發相

Next, explaining the mark of the fourth dhyāna, as the sūtra verse says:
"The noble ones are able to relinquish it; for everyone else, relinquishing is difficult.
If one is able to see happiness as a trouble, one sees the great peace of the unmoving.
Sorrow and joy have already been removed; suffering and happiness are now cut off as well;

The happiness within the third dhyāna, being impermanent and unsettled, is itself suffering;
Sorrow was cut off within the desire realm; suffering was removed by the first and second dhyānas.
Hence the World-Honored Buddha, within the fourth dhyāna, says:
Sorrow and suffering have already been cut off; and now suffering and happiness are removed as well."
This four-line verse clarifies in full the marks of cultivating and realizing the fourth dhyāna. Here, explaining the fourth dhyāna, this opens into three parts: explaining the name; clarifying the method of cultivation; and clarifying the mark of its arising.

第一、釋四禪名者，禪名支林，四禪攬四支成定，於第四心中證得，故名四禪，猶是無覺無觀三昧聖默然攝，亦名不動定。《地持經》說：「名捨俱禪。」此定發時，體無苦樂，與微妙捨受俱發。此定與捨根相應，故名捨俱禪。

First, explaining the name of the fourth dhyāna: "dhyāna" means a grove of factors, and the fourth dhyāna gathers together four factors to form its own concentration, realized at this fourth stage of mind — hence it is named the fourth dhyāna. It, too, still belongs to the samādhi without initial thought or sustained reflection, the concentration of noble silence, and is also named the unmoving concentration. The Bodhisattva-bhūmi Sūtra calls it "the dhyāna joined with equanimity": when this concentration arises, its own substance is free of both suffering and happiness, arising together with the subtle and wondrous feeling of equanimity. This concentration corresponds with the equanimity-faculty — hence it is named the dhyāna joined with equanimity.

第二、明修習方法者，如上一行偈說，是樂「聖人得能捨，餘人捨為難。若能知樂患，見不動大安」。佛此偈具明修四禪方便。所以者何？行者欲得四禪，當應深見三禪過患。云何見過？初欲得樂，一心勤求，大為辛苦，既得守護愛著，是亦為苦。一旦失壞，則復受苦，是故經說：「第三禪中樂，無常動故苦。」又此樂法覆念，令不清淨，行者既深見三禪樂有大苦之患，應一心厭離，求四禪種不動定，爾時於三禪邊地當修六行方法，例如前說，亦應用於三法除遣：一、不著；二、訶責；三、觀析。行此三法，即三禪謝滅，而四禪未到中間，必有定前發，與觀相應等相貌，並如上說，不同憂、喜。過如前說。

Second, clarifying the method of cultivation: as the single line of verse already given above says, this happiness is such that "the noble ones are able to relinquish it; for everyone else, relinquishing is difficult. If one is able to see happiness as a trouble, one sees the great peace of the unmoving." The Buddha, within this verse, clarifies in full the expedient means for cultivating the fourth dhyāna. Why is this? The practitioner wishing to attain the fourth dhyāna should deeply perceive the trouble present within the third dhyāna.

How does one perceive this trouble? At first, wishing to attain happiness, one seeks it single-mindedly, with great toil and hardship; and, having attained it, guarding and cherishing it with attachment, this too is a form of suffering; and, should it ever be lost or broken, one suffers all over again from this as well — hence the sūtra says: "The happiness within the third dhyāna, being impermanent and unsettled, is itself suffering." Furthermore, this dharma of happiness clouds over mindfulness, keeping it from being pure. The practitioner, having deeply perceived that the third dhyāna's happiness carries within it this great trouble of suffering, should single-mindedly grow weary of it and turn away, seeking instead the fourth dhyāna's own seed of unmoving concentration. At this point, at the border of the third dhyāna, one should cultivate the method of the six practices, following the same pattern as already described, and should likewise apply the same three methods to dispel it: non-attachment; reproach; and analytic contemplation. Practicing these three methods, the third dhyāna thereby passes away and is extinguished; and, with the fourth dhyāna not yet reached, in the interval between them, a preliminary concentration necessarily arises, corresponding with contemplation, in the same manner as already described above — differing only in no longer involving sorrow or joy. Its own fault is as already described above as well.

第三、釋四禪發相，此如上三行偈說證，例前開為六意：一、正明證四禪；二、明支義；三、明體用；四、明淺深；五、明進退；六、明功德。第一、明四禪發相。行者因中間禪修行不止，得入未到地，心無動散，即四禪方便定。於後其心豁然開發，定心安隱，出入息斷，定發之時，與捨俱生，無苦無樂，空明寂靜，善法眷屬類如前說，但無事用喜樂動轉之異。爾時心如明鏡不動，亦如淨水無波，絕諸亂想，正念堅固，猶如虛空，是名世間真實禪定，無諸垢染。

Third, explaining the mark of the fourth dhyāna's own arising: this is realized exactly as the three lines of verse already given above describe, and, following the same pattern as before, opens into six parts — directly clarifying the realization of the fourth dhyāna; clarifying the meaning of its factors; clarifying its substance and function; clarifying its depth; clarifying its progress and regression; and clarifying its merits.

First, clarifying the mark of the fourth dhyāna's own arising: the practitioner, through the intermediate dhyāna's own unceasing practice, comes to enter the "not-yet-arrived" state, the mind free of agitation or scattering — this is precisely the fourth dhyāna's own preliminary concentration. Afterward, the mind opens out and is brought forth, the concentrated mind at peace, the in-breath and out-breath both cut off; at the very moment concentration arises, it arises together with equanimity, free of both suffering and happiness, empty, luminous, and still, its wholesome dharma and retinue following the same pattern already described — differing only in having no excited stirring of joy or happiness present at all. At that point, the mind is like a bright mirror, unmoving, and like clear water free of any ripple, every scattered thought cut off entirely, right mindfulness firm and secure, just like empty space itself — this is named the true, genuine dhyāna-concentration of the mundane world, free of any defiling stain whatsoever.

行者住是定中，心不依善，亦不附惡，無所依倚，無形無質，亦無若干種種色相，而內成就淨色之法。何以得知？若無淨色根本，則不應於定中對因緣時，發種種色，如通四無量心、勝處、一切處變化等色，並依四禪。若以不見諸色謂言無色者，應如虛空處定三種色滅，一切色法悉不得現。今一切色法自在得現，而於定法無所損減者，當知是真色定。譬如明鏡，體是淨色，故隨對諸色，一切得現；若無淨色為本者，終不於虛空中現諸色像。復次，此四禪種智定一心故，念常清淨，亦名不動定，亦名不動智慧。於此禪中，若欲轉緣學一切事，隨意成就，一切神通變化，霔雨說法，莫不從此定出。如經說：「佛於四禪為根本。」

The practitioner, abiding within this concentration, the mind no longer relies on the wholesome, nor clings to the unwholesome, with nothing at all to depend upon or lean against — without shape, without substance, and without any particular kind of form or mark whatsoever — and yet, within, accomplishes the dharma of pure form. How is this known? Were there no root of pure form present at all, then, within concentration, in response to the proper causes and conditions, it ought not be possible for various forms to be brought forth at all — as with the transformative forms brought forth in common through the four immeasurable minds, the stations of mastery, and the totality-bases, all of which depend upon the fourth dhyāna. If one were to conclude, simply because no forms are seen, that there is therefore no form present at all, then, as in the concentration of the sphere of boundless space, where the three kinds of form are extinguished, no form-dharma whatsoever ought to be capable of manifesting there either. Here, however, every form-dharma is able to manifest freely, with no diminishment at all to the dharma of concentration itself — hence one should know that this is a concentration of true form. It is like a bright mirror: its own substance is pure form, and so, whatever is placed before it, every form is able to appear within it; were there no pure form as its basis, it could never manifest any image of form within empty space at all.

Furthermore: because this fourth dhyāna's own wise concentration is one-pointed in mind, mindfulness is forever pure — hence it is also named the unmoving concentration, and also named unmoving wisdom. Within this dhyāna, should one wish to shift one's object and study any matter whatsoever, it is accomplished exactly as intended — every supernormal power and transformation, every raining down of the Dharma in teaching, all issue from this very concentration without exception. As the sūtra says: "The Buddha takes the fourth dhyāna as his own root and foundation."

Reading note: The fourth dhyāna's "true form" argument is a genuinely careful piece of reasoning against a natural misreading: a concentration with "no shape, no substance, no particular mark" might seem to be a formless attainment, indistinguishable from the boundless-space sphere that actually does extinguish form — but the treatise's test is functional rather than descriptive. If the fourth dhyāna can still give rise to visible transformations (as the basis for the four immeasurable minds, the stations of mastery, and the totality-bases' own display of forms), it must retain "pure form" as its own hidden root, the way a mirror's own clarity — itself colorless — is precisely what allows any color to appear in it. This is the treatise's own criterion for distinguishing dhyāna from formlessness by what it can still do, not by what it superficially resembles.

The Fourth Dhyāna: Its Factors, Substance, and Merits 四禪 · 明支義、體用、淺深、進退、功德

Chinese Source

第二、明支義。四禪有四支：一、不苦不樂支；二、捨支；三、念清淨支；四、一心支。不苦不樂支者，此禪初發與捨受俱發。捨受心數不與苦樂相應，故言不苦不樂支。二、捨支者，既得不苦不樂定，捨下勝樂，不生厭悔。復次，真定以發，未得成就，若心進勝定，則便隨念動轉，不名無動定，是故定發，心不念著，自能捨離，故名捨支。禪定分明，等智照了，故名念清淨支。定心寂靜，雖對眾緣，心無動念，名一心支。若次第明支義，如今說，通而為論，於初一支即具四支。問曰：何故《大集》明不苦不樂支為第三，此中云何為初？答曰：前後皆有所以。今約發說，彼據成就而立，例如三禪立樂支，前後不定。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the meaning of the factors: the fourth dhyāna has four factors — the factor of neither suffering nor happiness; the factor of equanimity; the factor of purity of mindfulness; and the factor of one-pointedness of mind.

As for the factor of neither suffering nor happiness: this dhyāna, at its first arising, arises together with the feeling of equanimity; and, since the mental factor of equanimity-feeling corresponds with neither suffering nor happiness, it is called the factor of neither suffering nor happiness. Second, the factor of equanimity: having attained the concentration of neither suffering nor happiness, one relinquishes the superior happiness of the stage below without any regret arising. Furthermore: true concentration, though it has arisen, is not yet brought to completion — should the mind advance toward some superior concentration, it would then simply follow along with this thought and shift accordingly, and could not be named the unmoving concentration; hence, when this concentration arises, the mind does not dwell on or cling to it, and is able of itself to relinquish and leave it behind — hence this is named the factor of equanimity. Dhyāna-concentration here being clear and distinct, equanimous wisdom illuminating it fully, this is named the factor of purity of mindfulness. The concentrated mind being still, although it meets with every kind of condition, the mind gives rise to no agitated thought at all — this is named the factor of one-pointedness of mind.

If the meaning of the factors is explained in sequence, it is exactly as just given; discussed generally, however, the very first single factor already contains all four factors within it. Question: why does the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra place the factor of neither suffering nor happiness third, while here it is placed first? Answer: both orderings have their own reason. Here, it is spoken of with reference to its own arising; that source, by contrast, establishes it with reference to its completion — comparable to how the third dhyāna's own factor of happiness was placed differently in different sources; the ordering is not fixed.

第三、體用者，如《纓絡經》說：「四禪四支為用，第五默然為體。」第四、淺深。第五、進退，例上可解。第六、功德者，四禪亦具離過、善心二種功德，如《大集經》說者：「離五蓋，具四支。」而獨四禪有離憂、喜、苦、樂之過，善心：敬、信、慚、愧等及六善法，悉從不動定四禪而發。功德善根深厚，倍勝於上，類前可解。

Third, as for substance and function: as the Yingluo Sūtra says, the fourth dhyāna's four factors serve as the function, and the fifth, noble silence, serves as the substance. Fourth, depth. Fifth, progress and regression: both may be understood following the same pattern as above. Sixth, merit: the fourth dhyāna, too, is complete in both kinds of merit, freedom from fault and a wholesome mind, as the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "Freedom from the five hindrances; completeness in the four factors." And it is the fourth dhyāna alone that carries the distinct merit of freedom from the faults of sorrow, joy, suffering, and happiness; its wholesome mind — reverence, faith, shame, remorse, and the other six wholesome dharmas — all arise from this very unmoving concentration, the fourth dhyāna. The depth of its merit and wholesome roots is doubled in excellence over what came before, and may be understood by the same pattern as already given.

Reading note: The fourth dhyāna's factors close out a progression worth seeing across all four dhyānas together: the first dhyāna's factors (initial thought, sustained reflection, joy, happiness, one-pointedness) are the most numerous and the coarsest; each subsequent dhyāna sheds one factor pair while refining what remains, until the fourth dhyāna arrives at bare equanimity, purity of mindfulness, and one-pointedness — feeling itself reduced to neither suffering nor happiness. This closes the whole of the fourth dhyāna.

Question: here, in clarifying the bodhisattva path, one ought properly to speak of the true mark of all dharmas, the exceedingly profound concentration of emptiness, and such matters — why, then, speak instead of the ordinary person's own four dhyānas, a contaminated, deluded dharma of birth-and-death within the mundane world? Answer: this does not follow.

As the Exegetical Treatise poses this very question and answers it: "Within the discourse of the perfection of wisdom, only the empty mark of all dharmas is spoken of — how, then, can a bodhisattva bring forth dhyāna-concentration at all within such an empty dharma? Answer: the bodhisattva knows that the five desires and the five hindrances all arise from causes and conditions, without any nature of their own, empty and without anything to be found in them at all, and so relinquishing them is exceedingly easy. Sentient beings, however, through inverted causes and conditions, cling to these very matters of desire, craving a slight and inferior pleasure, and thereby remain far from the deep, subtle pleasure of concentration found within dhyāna. The bodhisattva, for the sake of these very sentient beings, gives rise to a mind of loving-kindness and compassion, and cultivates dhyāna-concentration: fixing the mind upon its object, leaving the five desires behind, removing the five hindrances, entering the joyful first dhyāna; extinguishing initial thought and sustained reflection, gathering the mind and entering deeply into inner purity, attaining subtle and wondrous joy, entering the second dhyāna; through this deep joy dispersing concentration, leaving all joy behind, attaining happiness pervading the whole body, entering the third dhyāna; leaving behind all suffering and happiness, all sorrow and joy, the in-breath and out-breath cut off of themselves, adorning oneself with pure, subtle equanimity, entering the fourth dhyāna.

是菩薩雖知諸法空無相，以眾生不知故，以禪相教化眾生。若有諸法空，是不名為空，亦不應捨五欲而得禪，無捨無得故。今諸法空相亦不可得，不應作是難言：若諸法空，何能得禪？復次，若菩薩不以取相愛著故行禪，如人服藥，欲以除病，不以為美。為戒清淨、智慧成就故行禪。菩薩一一禪中行大慈觀空，於禪無所依止，以五欲麁誑顛倒故，以細微妙虛空法治之，譬如毒能治諸毒。」

"This bodhisattva, although already knowing that all dharmas are empty and without mark, teaches sentient beings through the marks of dhyāna precisely because sentient beings themselves do not yet know this. If the emptiness of all dharmas were itself something that could be grasped as 'empty,' it could not properly be called emptiness at all, nor, in that case, ought one to have to relinquish the five desires in order to attain dhyāna, since there would then be nothing to relinquish and nothing to attain in the first place. But here, the very mark of all dharmas' emptiness cannot itself be found either — hence one should not raise the objection: 'If all dharmas are empty, how could dhyāna possibly be attained at all?' Furthermore: the bodhisattva does not practice dhyāna out of any grasping at its marks or attachment to it — it is like a person taking medicine in order to cure an illness, not because the medicine itself is pleasant to the taste. The bodhisattva practices dhyāna for the sake of pure moral discipline and the accomplishment of wisdom. Within every single dhyāna, the bodhisattva practices great loving-kindness and the contemplation of emptiness, with nothing at all depended upon within dhyāna itself; and, because the five desires are coarse, deceptive, and inverted, they are treated instead through this fine, subtle, empty dharma — just as a poison may be used to treat other poisons."

復次，《釋論》又說：「譬如國王見子從高崖墜落，恐必定死，即以軟物接之，不令身命損毀。菩薩亦爾，見眾生遠離波若，顛倒墜落，故說四禪空法以接眾生，不令損失法身慧命。」是故今辨行菩薩道，略明四禪，於義無過也。
釋禪波羅蜜次第法門卷第五

Furthermore, the Exegetical Treatise also says: "It is like a king who, seeing his own son falling from a high cliff, and fearing he is certain to die, catches him with something soft, so that his body and life are not destroyed. The bodhisattva is exactly the same: seeing sentient beings falling away, far removed from prajñā, inverted and plummeting downward, the bodhisattva therefore teaches the empty dharma of the four dhyānas in order to catch them, so that the very life of their dharma-body and wisdom is not lost."

Hence, in now discussing the practice of the bodhisattva path, this brief clarification of the four dhyānas is, in its own sense, without any fault at all.
The Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen, Fascicle Five, ends here.

Reading note: This closing apologetic answers a question the whole fascicle has been implicitly raising: why would a Mahāyāna treatise on the perfection of wisdom spend an entire fascicle on what is, technically, a contaminated, mundane attainment shared with non-Buddhist practitioners and even animals reborn in the form realm? The answer reframes everything just translated: the four dhyānas are not the destination but a rescue apparatus, deployed compassionately for beings who cannot yet grasp emptiness directly, "like a poison that treats other poisons" — using the desire realm's own logic of graduated pleasure to lead beings out of the desire realm's actual trap. The falling-son parable is the treatise's most tender image yet: dhyāna is not opposed to the bodhisattva's realization of emptiness but is precisely the soft thing a compassionate king throws beneath a child already falling, whether or not that child yet understands what is happening to them. This closes fascicle 5 in its entirety — the four dhyānas complete, the first of mundane dhyāna's three sections (four dhyānas, four immeasurable minds, four formless concentrations) now finished, with the latter two still to come.
