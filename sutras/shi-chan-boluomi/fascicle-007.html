Fascicle 7 · 釋禪波羅蜜次第法門卷第七

The Six Wondrous Gates and the Sixteen Special Excellences

This page presents Ru-Yi's own English translation of fascicle 7 of Zhiyi's Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen (釋禪波羅蜜次第法門). Having completed mundane dhyāna in fascicle 6, the treatise turns to topic 7's second division — dhyāna that is both mundane and world-transcending — and gives full, hands-on treatment to two of its three named practices: the six wondrous gates (already referenced since fascicle 5's breath-counting method) and the sixteen special excellences, each matched to a different kind of practitioner's own balance of concentration and wisdom.

Opening Fascicle Seven 卷第七

Chinese Source

卷第七
隋天台智者大師說
弟子法慎記
弟子灌頂再治
釋禪波羅蜜修證第七之三
今約三種法門以辯亦有漏亦無漏禪：一者、六妙門；二者、十六特勝；三者、通明觀。此三法門亦得說為淨禪。此中明淨禪與《阿毘曇》有小異，淺深、位次，並如前第五卷中說，但教門分一息道，立三種禪，為化眾生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fascicle Seven
Spoken by the Great Master Zhiyi of Tiantai, of the Sui dynasty
Recorded by his disciple Fashen
Revised and edited by his disciple Guanding
Explaining Dhyāna-Pāramitā's Cultivation and Realization, Topic 7, Part 3

Here, with reference to three dharma-gates, dhyāna that is both contaminated and uncontaminated is now distinguished: the six wondrous gates; the sixteen special excellences; and the thorough-and-bright contemplation. These three dharma-gates may also be spoken of as the pure dhyānas. What is clarified here regarding the pure dhyānas differs slightly from the Abhidharma's own account; their shallowness, depth, and sequence of stages are all as already given in fascicle 5 above — it is simply that the teaching divides the single path of the breath, establishing three kinds of dhyāna, for the sake of transforming sentient beings.

今須略推此教意，多是對三種人根性不同：一者、自有眾生慧性多而定性少，為說六妙門。六妙門中慧性多故，於欲界初禪中即能發無漏，此未必至上地諸禪也。二者、自有眾生定根多而慧性少，為說十六特勝。慧根性少故，下地不即發無漏；定性多故，以具上地諸禪，方得修道。三者、自有眾生定慧根性等，為說通明。通明者，亦具根本禪，而觀慧巧細，從於下地乃至上地皆能發無漏也。此是隨機之說。若隨對治，則與此相違，如前五門中意可解。

Here, the intent behind this teaching should be briefly reasoned through: it is, for the most part, matched to three kinds of person, differing in capacity. First, there are sentient beings whose wisdom-nature is abundant while their concentration-nature is slight — for these, the six wondrous gates are taught. Within the six wondrous gates, because wisdom-nature is abundant, one is able to bring forth the uncontaminated already within the desire realm or the first dhyāna — this need not wait until reaching the dhyānas of the higher stages at all.

Second, there are sentient beings whose faculty of concentration is abundant while their wisdom-nature is slight — for these, the sixteen special excellences are taught. Because the faculty of wisdom is slight, the lower stages do not immediately bring forth the uncontaminated; because the nature of concentration is abundant, only once complete in the dhyānas of the higher stages is the path actually cultivated.

Third, there are sentient beings whose faculties of concentration and wisdom are equal — for these, the thorough-and-bright contemplation is taught. As for the thorough-and-bright contemplation: this, too, is complete with the root dhyānas, but its contemplative wisdom is skillful and fine, able to bring forth the uncontaminated from the lower stage all the way through the higher stage alike. This is a teaching given according to capacity. Were it instead given according to the antidote, it would run contrary to this — the sense of this may be understood from the five gates already discussed above.

Reading note: This opening finally cashes out a promise this treatise has been making since fascicle 1 — the six wondrous gates, the sixteen special excellences, and the thorough-and-bright contemplation have all been named repeatedly as attainments along the way, but never given their own full treatment until now. The three-way matching to practitioner capacity (wisdom-heavy, concentration-heavy, or balanced) is a genuinely practical piece of pedagogy: rather than one universal curriculum, the treatise offers three different entry points into the identical underlying territory, chosen by diagnosing which of a student's two faculties needs the other's support.

The Six Wondrous Gates: Name and Sequence 六妙門 · 釋名、辨位次

Chinese Source

初釋六妙門，為三：一者、釋名；二、辨位次；三、明修證。第一、釋名者，所言六妙門者：一、數；二、隨；三、止；四、觀；五、還；六、淨。通稱六妙門者，妙名涅槃，此之妙法能通至涅槃，故名妙門，亦名六妙門。此六妙門，三是定法，三是慧法。定愛慧策，亦有漏亦無漏，義在於此。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, explaining the six wondrous gates: this is given in three parts — explaining the name; distinguishing their sequence of stages; and clarifying their cultivation and realization.

First, explaining the name: as for what is called the six wondrous gates, these are: counting; following; stilling; contemplating; returning; and purifying. As for why they are called, in general, the six wondrous gates: "wondrous" names nirvāṇa — this wondrous dharma is able to lead through all the way to nirvāṇa, and so is named the wondrous gate, also called the six wondrous gates. Of these six wondrous gates, three are dharmas of concentration, and three are dharmas of wisdom. Concentration cherishes and wisdom spurs onward — this is precisely the sense in which they are both contaminated and uncontaminated at once.

二、辨次位者，此六妙門位即無定。所以者何？若於欲界、未到地中巧行六法，第六淨心成就，即發三乘無漏，況復進得上地諸禪而不疾證道。雖此與前有異，是以《瑞應經》云：「因此六法，遊止三四，出生十二。」此而推之，故知此六妙門位則不必定耳。

Second, distinguishing their sequence of stages: the stages of these six wondrous gates are not fixed at all. Why is this? If, within the desire realm or the "not-yet-arrived" state, these six dharmas are skillfully practiced, and the sixth, purity of mind, is brought to completion, the uncontaminated attainment of the three vehicles is thereby brought forth directly — how much more, then, could one who goes on to attain the dhyānas of the higher stages fail to realize the path all the more quickly? Although this differs somewhat from what was given before, the Sūtra of Auspicious Manifestations accordingly says: "Through these six dharmas, roaming and abiding through the third and fourth dhyānas, twelve are brought forth." Reasoning this through, it is thus known that the sequence of stages for these six wondrous gates is not fixed at all.

Reading note: The three-and-three split (counting, following, and stilling as concentration; contemplating, returning, and purifying as wisdom) gives structural shape to a sequence that otherwise reads as a single continuous refinement — the first half quiets the mind, the second half turns that quiet mind reflectively on itself. That the six gates' own stages are explicitly "not fixed," able to bring forth full uncontaminated realization already within the desire realm without ever needing to climb through the higher dhyānas, is the payoff for the "wisdom-heavy" practitioner this method was matched to at the fascicle's opening — depth of insight substituting for depth of concentration.

The Six Wondrous Gates: Cultivation and Realization 六妙門 · 明修證

Chinese Source

三、明修證者，若廣明此六法修證，則諸禪皆屬六妙門攝。今但取次第相生入道之正要，以明六妙修證之相。今明修證六妙門，開為十二門也。所以者何？如數有二種：一者、修數；二者、數相應。乃至修淨與相應亦如是。今約修證分別，有十二門：一、修數；二、與數相應。一者、修數。行者調和氣息，不澁不滑，安詳徐數，從一至十，攝心在數，不令馳散，是名修數。二、與數相應者，覺心任運，從一至十，不加功力，心息自住。息既虛凝，心相漸細，患數為麁，意不欲數，爾時行者應當放數修隨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying their cultivation and realization: were the cultivation and realization of these six dharmas explained in full, every dhyāna whatsoever would fall under the six wondrous gates' own scope. Here, only the proper essentials of entering the path through their own sequential arising, one from another, are taken up, in order to clarify the mark of the six wondrous gates' cultivation and realization. Here, clarifying the cultivation and realization of the six wondrous gates, this opens into twelve gates. Why is this? Just as counting itself is of two kinds — cultivating counting, and correspondence with counting — the same holds all the way through cultivating purifying and correspondence with it as well. Here, distinguished with reference to cultivation and realization, there are twelve gates:

First, cultivating counting: the practitioner regulates and harmonizes the breath, neither too rough nor too smooth, counting calmly and slowly, from one to ten, the mind gathered in upon the count, not letting it run off and scatter — this is named cultivating counting.

Second, correspondence with counting: the awakened mind now abides of its own accord, from one to ten, without any further application of effort, mind and breath naturally settled. The breath having grown faint and fixed, the mark of the mind grows gradually finer, troubled now by counting itself as too coarse, the mind no longer wishing to count — at this point, the practitioner should set counting aside and cultivate following instead.

隨亦有二種：一者、修隨；二者、與隨相應。修隨者，捨數法，一心依隨息之出入，心住息緣，無分散意，是名修隨。二者、與隨相應，心既漸細，覺息長短，遍身入出息，任運相依，意慮怡然凝靜，是名與隨相應。覺隨為麁，心厭欲捨，如人疲極欲眠，不樂眾務，爾時行者應當捨隨修止。

Following, too, is of two kinds: cultivating following; and correspondence with following. Cultivating following: relinquishing the method of counting, fixing the mind single-mindedly on the breath's own going out and coming in, the mind abiding on the breath as its object, with no thought of scattering at all — this is named cultivating following. Second, correspondence with following: the mind now grows gradually finer, aware of the breath's own length and shortness, the in-breath and out-breath pervading the whole body, following it spontaneously of its own accord, thought and deliberation content and fixed in stillness — this is named correspondence with following. Aware now that following itself is too coarse, the mind grows weary of it and wishes to relinquish it, like a person utterly exhausted, wishing only to sleep, taking no delight in any further activity — at this point, the practitioner should relinquish following and cultivate stilling instead.

止有二種：一者、修止；二、與止相應。修止者，三止之中，但用制心止也。制心息諸緣慮，不念數隨，凝靜其心，是名修止。二、與止相應者，自覺身心泯然入定，不見內外相貌，如欲界、未到地等。定法持心，任運不動。行者爾時即作是念：「今此三昧雖復寂靜，而無慧方便，不能破壞生死。」復作是念：「今此定者，皆屬因緣，陰、入、界法和合而有，虛誑不實。我今不覺，應須照了。」作是念已，即不著止。

Stilling, too, is of two kinds: cultivating stilling; and correspondence with stilling. Cultivating stilling: among the three kinds of stopping, only restraining-the-mind stopping is used here. Restraining the mind brings every object of deliberation to rest, no longer thinking of counting or following, the mind fixed and still — this is named cultivating stilling. Second, correspondence with stilling: one becomes aware, of oneself, that body and mind have grown indistinct, entering concentration, seeing no mark of either what is within or without, just as in the desire realm or the "not-yet-arrived" state. The dharma of concentration sustains the mind, unmoved of its own accord. The practitioner, at this point, reflects: "Although this samādhi is indeed still, it lacks any skillful means of wisdom, and so is unable to break through birth-and-death." One reflects further: "This very concentration is itself entirely a matter of causes and conditions, the dharma of the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements combining together to produce it — deceptive and unreal. I, not yet aware of this, must now illuminate it clearly." Having reflected in this way, one no longer clings to stilling.

Reading note: Each transition in this sequence follows an identical pattern worth naming explicitly: cultivation of a given gate leads to correspondence with it (the practice becoming spontaneous), and this very spontaneity is what reveals the next gate's own coarseness by comparison — the practitioner does not decide to move on so much as discover that the current gate has already become too coarse to hold the mind's own refinement. The reflection that breaks attachment to stilling is significant: it is the exact moment concentration alone is recognized as insufficient without wisdom, "unable to break through birth-and-death" no matter how deep — the hinge on which this whole method turns from the three concentration-gates into the three wisdom-gates.

Three Kinds of Contemplation, and Cultivating Contemplation Itself 六妙門 · 修觀

Chinese Source

起觀分別亦有二種：一者、修觀；二者、與觀相應。一、修觀者，觀有三種：一者、慧行觀，觀真之慧；二者、得解觀，即假想觀；三者、實觀，如事而觀也。今此六妙門、十六特勝、通明等，並正用實觀成就，然後用慧行觀觀理入道。所以者何？名實者，如眾生一期果報，實有四大，不淨三十六物所成，但以無明覆蔽，心眼不開明，則不依實而見。若能審諦觀察，心眼開明，依實而見，故名實慧行觀。及得解觀，在下四諦、十二因緣、九想、背捨等中，當廣分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Giving rise to contemplation and discrimination is likewise of two kinds: cultivating contemplation; and correspondence with contemplation.

First, cultivating contemplation: contemplation itself is of three kinds — the contemplation of wisdom-conduct, the wisdom that contemplates the true; the contemplation of understanding attained, that is, contemplation through mental visualization; and true contemplation, contemplating things exactly as they actually are. Here, the six wondrous gates, the sixteen special excellences, the thorough-and-bright contemplation, and the rest, all properly use true contemplation to reach their own accomplishment, and only afterward use the contemplation of wisdom-conduct to contemplate principle and enter the path.

Why is this? As for what is meant by "true": it is like the fact that a sentient being's own single life-span's karmic result is, in truth, made up of the four elements, and of the thirty-six impure substances — it is simply that, covered over by ignorance, the mind's own eye fails to open into clarity, and so one does not see it as it truly is. If one is able to closely and carefully contemplate it, the mind's own eye opens into clarity, and one sees it exactly as it truly is — hence this is named true contemplation, the contemplation of wisdom-conduct. As for the contemplation of understanding attained, this will be distinguished at length below, within the Four Noble Truths, the twelve links of dependent origination, the nine contemplations, the liberations, and the rest.

云何修習實觀？行者於定心中以心眼諦觀此身中細微入出息，想如空中風，皮筋骨肉、三十六物如芭蕉不實，內外不淨，甚可厭惡。復觀定中喜樂等受，悉有破壞之相，是苦非樂。又觀定中心識無常生滅，剎那不住，無可著處。復觀定中善惡等法，悉屬因緣，皆無自性。如是觀時，能破四倒，不得人我，定何所依？是名修觀。

How does one cultivate true contemplation? The practitioner, within the concentrated mind, closely contemplates, with the mind's own eye, the fine, subtle in-breath and out-breath within this very body, conceiving of it as though it were wind within empty space; the skin, sinews, bones, and flesh, the thirty-six substances, as though a plantain trunk, without any solidity at all, impure both within and without, deeply repugnant. One further contemplates that the sensations of joy, happiness, and the rest present within concentration all carry within them a mark of their own eventual destruction — this is suffering, not happiness. One further contemplates that mind and consciousness, within concentration, are impermanent, arising and ceasing, never remaining still for a single instant, with nothing at all in them to cling to. One further contemplates that the wholesome and unwholesome dharmas present within concentration are, in every case, entirely a matter of causes and conditions, without any nature of their own whatsoever. Contemplating in this way, one is able to break through the four inversions, finding no self or person present at all — on what, then, could concentration possibly depend? This is named cultivating contemplation.

Reading note: The three-kind taxonomy of contemplation — wisdom-conduct (direct insight into the true), understanding-attained (guided visualization), and true contemplation (seeing what is actually there) — retroactively clarifies a distinction this treatise has used constantly without naming: the nine contemplations and liberations from fascicle 3 are "understanding-attained" contemplation (deliberately constructed mental images), while what is happening here, seeing the breath and body exactly as they present themselves without any imaginative construction at all, is "true contemplation." The explicit statement that all three of this fascicle's named methods rely on true contemplation first, only later adding wisdom-conduct contemplation to reach principle, gives a methodological key for reading everything that follows in both the six wondrous gates and the sixteen special excellences.

Correspondence with Contemplation, Then Returning 六妙門 · 與觀相應、修還

Chinese Source

二、與觀相應者，如是觀時，覺息入出，遍諸毛孔，心眼開明，徹見內三十六物及諸蟲戶內外不淨，眾苦逼迫，剎那變易，一切諸法悉無自性，心生悲喜，無所依倚，得四念處，破四顛倒，是名與觀相應。觀解既發，心緣觀境，分別破析，覺念流動，非真實道，爾時應當捨觀修還。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, correspondence with contemplation: contemplating in this way, one becomes aware of the breath going out and coming in, pervading every pore of the body, the mind's own eye opening into clarity, seeing through entirely to the thirty-six substances within and the various parasites there, impure both within and without, every kind of suffering pressing in, changing moment to moment, every dharma whatsoever without any nature of its own — the mind gives rise to both sorrow and joy, with nothing at all to depend upon or lean against, attaining the four foundations of mindfulness, breaking through the four inversions — this is named correspondence with contemplation. Once this contemplative understanding has arisen, the mind takes the very object of contemplation itself as its object, discriminating and analyzing it further — but becoming aware that this thought itself is still in flux, not yet the true path — at this point, one should relinquish contemplation and cultivate returning instead.

還亦有二：一者、修習還；二者、與還相應。一、修習還者，既知觀從心發，若隨析境，此則不會本源，應當返觀此心者從何而生：為從觀心生，為從非觀心生？若從觀心生，則先已有觀，今實不爾。所以者何？數、隨、止等三法之中，未有觀故。若非觀心生，不觀心為滅生，為不滅生？若不滅生，即二心並；若是滅法已謝，不能生現在；若言亦滅亦不滅生，乃至非滅非不滅生，皆不可得。當知觀心本自不生，不生故不有，不有故即空，空無觀心。若無觀心，豈有觀境？境智雙忘，還源之要，是名修還。

Returning, too, is of two kinds: cultivating returning; and correspondence with returning.

First, cultivating returning: already knowing that contemplation arises from the mind itself, if one simply follows along analyzing its object, this fails to meet the original source directly — one should instead turn the contemplation back upon this very mind, asking from what it itself arises: does it arise from a contemplating mind, or from a non-contemplating mind? If it arises from a contemplating mind, then contemplation would already have to be present beforehand — but this is, in truth, not so at all. Why is this? Because, within the three prior dharmas of counting, following, and stilling, no contemplation is yet present. If it arises instead from a non-contemplating mind, does this non-contemplating mind arise through cessation, or without cessation? If it arises without cessation, then two minds would exist together at once; if it is a ceased dharma that has already passed away, it cannot give rise to anything present now; and if it is said to arise through both cessation and non-cessation together, or, further still, through neither cessation nor non-cessation, none of these can be found to hold up either. One should know, then, that the contemplating mind is, in itself, originally unarisen — and, being unarisen, it does not exist; and, not existing, it is thus empty — and this very emptiness has no contemplating mind present within it at all. If there is no contemplating mind, how, then, could there be any object of contemplation? Object and wisdom both forgotten together — this is the very essential of returning to the source — this is named cultivating returning.

二、與還相應者，心慧開發，不加功力，任運自能破析，返本還源，是名與還相應。既相應已，行者當知，若離境智，欲歸無境智，不離境智，縛心隨二邊故，爾時當捨還。

Second, correspondence with returning: mind and wisdom now open forth, without any further application of effort, spontaneously able of themselves to analyze and break through, turning back to the very root and returning to the source — this is named correspondence with returning. Once this correspondence has arisen, the practitioner should know that, wishing to leave both object and wisdom behind and return to a state with neither object nor wisdom at all, while still not yet actually free of object and wisdom, the mind remains bound, following one or the other of these two extremes — at this point, one should relinquish returning as well.

Reading note: The tetralemma against a findable "contemplating mind" (arising from a contemplating mind, a non-contemplating mind, both, or neither) is the same four-alternative analysis this treatise has run repeatedly since fascicle 1 — against "contaminated dharma," against "offense," against "karma" — now turned reflexively on contemplation itself, the very faculty doing the analyzing in every prior instance. This is a genuinely elegant closing of the loop: having used contemplative analysis to dissolve object after object, returning finally asks whether the analyzing mind itself can survive its own method, and finds that it cannot — "object and wisdom both forgotten together."

Cultivating Purity, and Realizing It 六妙門 · 修淨、證淨

Chinese Source

安心淨道亦有二：一者、修淨；二者、與淨相應。一、修淨者，知色淨故，不起妄想分別，受、想、行、識亦復如是。息妄想垢，息分別垢，息取我垢，是名修淨。舉要言之，若能心如本淨，名為修淨；亦不得能所修及淨、不淨，是名修淨。二、與淨相應者，作是修時，豁然心慧相應，無礙方便，任運開發，三昧正受，心無依倚。

Ru-Yi Translation

Settling the mind upon the path of purity is likewise of two kinds: cultivating purity; and correspondence with purity.

First, cultivating purity: knowing that form is itself pure, one no longer gives rise to deluded thought or discrimination regarding it — and the same holds for sensation, perception, formation, and consciousness as well. Bringing to rest the defilement of deluded thought, the defilement of discrimination, and the defilement of grasping at a self — this is named cultivating purity. Stated in essence: if the mind is able to be exactly as originally pure, this is named cultivating purity; and, finding no subject that cultivates and no object cultivated, no purity and no impurity either, this too is named cultivating purity.

Second, correspondence with purity: practicing in this way, the mind and wisdom suddenly correspond with one another, unobstructed and skillful, opening forth spontaneously of their own accord, the samādhi properly received, the mind with nothing at all to depend upon or lean against.

證淨亦有二：一者、相似證，五方便相似無漏慧發；二者、真實證，苦法忍乃至第九無礙道等三乘真無漏慧發也。三界垢盡，故名證淨。

Realizing purity is likewise of two kinds: approximate realization, where an approximate, uncontaminated wisdom is brought forth through the five expedient means; and true realization, where the true, uncontaminated wisdom of the three vehicles is brought forth, from the patient acceptance of the truth of suffering all the way through the ninth unobstructed path. The defilement of the three realms entirely exhausted — this is named realizing purity.

The Six Wondrous Gates Are the Mahāyāna Itself 六妙門 · 結歎

Chinese Source

復次，觀眾生空，故名為觀；觀實法空，故名為還；觀平等空，故名為淨。復次，空三昧相應，故名為觀；無相三昧相應，故名為還；無作三昧相應，故名為淨。復次，一切外觀名為觀，一切內觀名為還，一切非內非外觀名為淨。故先尼梵志言：「非內觀故，得是智慧；非外觀故，得是智慧；亦不無觀故，得是智慧。」復次，菩薩從假入空觀，故名為觀；從空入假觀，故名為還；空假一心觀，故名為淨。若能如是修者，當知六妙門即是摩訶衍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: contemplating the emptiness of sentient beings is named contemplating; contemplating the emptiness of real dharmas is named returning; and contemplating the emptiness of equality itself is named purifying.

Furthermore: correspondence with the samādhi of emptiness is named contemplating; correspondence with the samādhi of signlessness is named returning; and correspondence with the samādhi of wishlessness is named purifying.

Furthermore: every outward contemplation is named contemplating; every inward contemplation is named returning; and every contemplation that is neither inward nor outward is named purifying. Hence the wandering ascetic Senika says: "Not through inward contemplation is this wisdom attained; not through outward contemplation is this wisdom attained; and yet not without any contemplation at all is this wisdom attained either."

Furthermore: the bodhisattva's contemplation proceeding from the provisional into emptiness is named contemplating; the contemplation proceeding from emptiness back into the provisional is named returning; and the single-minded contemplation of emptiness and the provisional together is named purifying. If one is able to cultivate in this way, one should know that the six wondrous gates are themselves precisely the Mahāyāna.

復次，三世諸佛入道之初，先以六妙門為本，如釋迦初詣道樹，即內思安般：一、數；二、隨；三、止；四、觀；五、還；六、淨。遊止三四，出生十二，因此證一切法門，降魔成道。當知菩薩善入六妙門，即能具一切佛法，故六妙門即是菩薩摩訶衍。今欲更論餘事，故略說不具足也。

Furthermore: at the very outset of entering the path, the buddhas of the three periods of time all take the six wondrous gates as their own foundation — just as, when Śākyamuni first arrived beneath the bodhi tree, he reflected inwardly on mindfulness of the breath: counting; following; stilling; contemplating; returning; and purifying. Roaming and abiding through the third and fourth dhyānas, twelve are brought forth — and, through this, he realized every dharma-gate whatsoever, subdued Māra, and accomplished the path.

One should know, then, that a bodhisattva who properly enters the six wondrous gates is thereby able to be complete in the whole of the Buddhadharma — hence the six wondrous gates are themselves precisely the bodhisattva's own Mahāyāna. Wishing to discuss further matters still, this is given here only in brief, not in full.

Reading note: The four successive re-descriptions of contemplating/returning/purifying (as objects of contemplation, as the three samādhi-gates, as inward/outward/neither, and as the bodhisattva's own two-truths movement between emptiness and the provisional) is the treatise's characteristic layering technique taken to its fullest extent — the same three-step structure viewed through four entirely different doctrinal vocabularies, each illuminating a different facet without displacing the others. Senika's own formula ("not inward, not outward, and not without any contemplation either") is a compact restatement of the very tetralemma logic just used against the contemplating mind itself. That the six wondrous gates are explicitly identified as what the Buddha himself used beneath the bodhi tree — the exact same claim fascicle 5 made for ānāpāna generally — confirms these gates are not a separate, lesser technique but the full elaboration of that same original method. This closes the six wondrous gates in their entirety.

The Sixteen Special Excellences: Their Name 十六特勝 · 釋名

Chinese Source

次釋十六特勝，即開為三意：一、釋名；二、明觀門制立不同；三、明修證。第一、釋名者，所言十六特勝者：一、知息入；二、知息出；三、知息長短；四、知息遍身；五、除諸身行；六、受喜；七、受樂；八、受諸心行；九、心作喜；十、心作攝；十一、心作解脫；十二、觀無常；十三、觀出散；十四、觀欲；十五、觀滅；十六、觀棄捨。所以通名十六特勝者，十六即是數法。特勝者，從因緣得名。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, explaining the sixteen special excellences: this opens into three parts — explaining the name; clarifying the two differing schemes for establishing the contemplation-gates; and clarifying their cultivation and realization.

First, explaining the name: as for what is called the sixteen special excellences, these are: knowing the breath entering; knowing the breath leaving; knowing the breath's length and shortness; knowing the breath pervading the whole body; removing every bodily formation; receiving joy; receiving happiness; receiving every mental formation; the mind making joy; the mind making restraint; the mind making liberation; contemplating impermanence; contemplating dispersal; contemplating desirelessness; contemplating cessation; and contemplating relinquishment. As for why they are named, in general, the sixteen special excellences: "sixteen" is simply the number of dharmas involved. "Special excellence" takes its name from the very causes and conditions behind their teaching.

如佛未出世時，外道等並已修得四禪、四空，而無對治觀行，故不出生死。如來成道，初為拘隣及舍利弗等利根弟子說四真諦，即得道跡。復為摩訶迦葉、絺那等直聞四諦真理不悟，更說不淨觀法對治，破諸煩惱。因此初明九想、背捨等諸不淨觀禪。爾時修此觀者，得道無量。復有一機眾生，貪欲既薄，若厭惡心重，作不淨觀，即大生厭患，便增惡此身，無漏未發，即顧人自害，此事如律文所明，佛因此告諸比丘捨不淨觀，更修勝法，法名十六特勝，修之可以得道。

When the Buddha had not yet appeared in the world, the non-Buddhist teachers had already attained the four dhyānas and the four formless concentrations on their own, but, lacking any antidote-contemplation, they never left birth-and-death behind. When the Tathāgata accomplished the path, he first taught the Four Noble Truths to disciples of sharp faculty such as Kauṇḍinya and Śāriputra, and they attained the fruit of the path at once. He then taught Mahākāśyapa, Kaṭyāyana, and others, who, hearing the true principle of the Four Noble Truths directly, failed to awaken through it — and so he further taught the contemplation of impurity as its antidote, breaking through their afflictions. Through this, the nine contemplations, the liberations, and the other contemplations of impurity were first clarified. At that time, those who cultivated this contemplation attained the path in immeasurable numbers.

There was, however, a further kind of disposition among sentient beings: those whose craving was already slight, but whose disgust and aversion ran deep — practicing the contemplation of impurity, they grew greatly disgusted and troubled, coming to loathe this very body all the more, and, the uncontaminated attainment not yet having arisen, they would turn on themselves and cause themselves harm. This matter is clarified in the Vinaya texts; the Buddha, because of this, told the monks to set the contemplation of impurity aside, and instead cultivate a more excellent dharma, named the sixteen special excellences — cultivating this, the path could still be attained.

此十六特勝有定有觀，是中具足諸禪，以喜、樂等法愛養故，則無自害之過，而有實觀觀察，不著諸禪，所以能發無漏。既進退從容，不隨二邊，亦能得道，故名特勝。問：若爾，應在觀禪後說淨禪。何以故？若取教門，即在觀禪之後；若論行法，既勝二邊，亦應在後。答：今明禪定力用淺深之相，非是對緣利物之時。所以者何？背捨、勝處悉是得解之觀，觀力既能轉想轉心，於斷結義強。今此特勝唯是實觀，能見身內三十六物，力用劣弱，不能疾斷結使，功德淺薄，故應前說。

These sixteen special excellences possess both concentration and contemplation together, complete, within them, with every dhyāna — and, being nourished by the dharmas of joy, happiness, and the rest, are free of the fault of self-harm; and, possessing true contemplation's own careful observation, they do not cling to any dhyāna, and so are able to bring forth the uncontaminated. Since one may thus proceed with ease, advancing or holding back as needed, following neither of the two extremes, and is nonetheless still able to attain the path through them — hence they are named the "special excellences."

Question: if this is so, then this dharma ought properly to be spoken of after the contemplation-dhyānas, being itself the "pure dhyāna." Why is this? If one follows the order of the teaching's own presentation, it comes after the contemplation-dhyānas; and if one discusses the practice itself, since it surpasses both extremes, it, too, ought properly to come afterward.

Answer: what is clarified here is the shallowness and depth of dhyāna-concentration's own power and function, not the timing of when it is actually taught in response to a given situation to benefit beings. Why is this? The liberations and the stations of mastery are all contemplations of understanding attained — their contemplative power is already able to transform both conception and mind, and so their sense of severing the fetters is strong. These special excellences, by contrast, are simply true contemplation, able only to see the thirty-six substances within the body — their power and function are comparatively weak, unable to quickly sever the fetters, their merit comparatively shallow — hence they are properly spoken of first here.

復次，若不淨觀散滅骨人，則不能得更觀身毛孔息出入相。若於實觀後轉作九想、背捨等，則具足成就，於義無失。復次《大品經．廣乘品》觀於十六特勝。復次，說九想、背捨等諸觀禪，此為明證，不應生疑也。

Furthermore: if the contemplation of impurity has already dissolved the skeleton entirely away, one would no longer be able to go on to contemplate the breath entering and leaving through the body's own pores. If, instead, one proceeds from true contemplation and only afterward turns to the nine contemplations, the liberations, and the rest, then both are fully accomplished, without any loss of sense. Furthermore, the Larger Sūtra's own chapter on the vast vehicle discusses the sixteen special excellences directly. Furthermore, the nine contemplations, the liberations, and the other contemplation-dhyānas are spoken of elsewhere as clear evidence of this — there should be no doubt raised about the matter.

Reading note: The origin story here gives the sixteen special excellences a genuinely different rationale than anything offered for the contemplation of impurity earlier in the treatise: not a more advanced technique, but a safer one for a specific, dangerous failure mode — practitioners whose craving is already slight but whose self-directed disgust runs so deep that contemplating the body's impurity tips them toward self-harm before the antidote has actually taken hold. That the treatise places these "special excellences" before the very contemplations they were designed to make safer, despite conceding the special excellences are weaker and slower on their own terms, is a clear statement of priorities: sequencing here follows what keeps a practitioner from being harmed, not what most efficiently severs the fetters.

Two Schemes: Against the Foundations of Mindfulness 十六特勝 · 明觀門制立不同 · 對四念處

Chinese Source

第二、明觀門制立不同者，解有二：一者、有人云：「此阿那波那等十六法對四念處。」若約四念處而明，當知但在欲界、未到地乃至初禪則具足也，欲至上地，非為不得，但觀法式少不具足。如四禪，既無出入息及喜、樂等，若約息及喜、樂明念處，則不便也。上下類而可知。亦明對四念處，復有二解不同：一師解云：「前五對身，中三對受，次二對心，後五對法。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the two differing schemes for establishing the contemplation-gates: there are two interpretations. First, some say: "This method of mindfulness of the breath and the other sixteen dharmas correspond to the four foundations of mindfulness." Discussed with reference to the four foundations of mindfulness, one should know that this is complete only within the desire realm, the "not-yet-arrived" state, and the first dhyāna; wishing to reach the higher stages, it is not that this cannot be attained there, only that the manner of contemplation is somewhat incomplete. As in the fourth dhyāna, which has no in-breath or out-breath, nor joy or happiness present at all — were the foundations of mindfulness to be explained with reference to the breath, joy, and happiness specifically, this would not fit properly there. The stages above and below may be understood by the same pattern.

Also, in explaining their correspondence to the four foundations of mindfulness, there are, in turn, two differing interpretations. One master explains: "The first five correspond to the body; the middle three correspond to sensation; the next two correspond to mind; and the last five correspond to dharma."

此師明十六特勝。自云依《禪經》中說：一、觀入息，至於氣滅。二、觀出息，止至於鼻端。三、觀息長短，若身不安，心多散亂，則出入息俱短；若身安心靜，則出入息俱長。四、息遍身者，形心既安，則氣道無壅，如似飲氣，既統遍身中。五、除諸身行者，想、受為心行，覺觀為口行，出入息為身行。既息遍身中，患彼覺動麁念，除諸麁故，名除諸身行。此五屬身念處。

This master clarifies the sixteen special excellences, stating that this follows what the Chan Sūtra itself says: first, observing the in-breath, all the way to the breath's own cessation. Second, observing the out-breath, stopping right at the tip of the nose. Third, observing the breath's length and shortness: if the body is not at ease, the mind for the most part scattered and disordered, both the in-breath and out-breath are short; if the body is at ease and the mind still, both the in-breath and out-breath are long. Fourth, the breath pervading the whole body: once form and mind are at ease, the channel of the breath's own energy is no longer obstructed, as though drinking in the breath, its flow now governing the entire body throughout. Fifth, removing every bodily formation: conception and sensation are mental formations; initial thought and sustained reflection are verbal formations; and the in-breath and out-breath are bodily formations. Once the breath pervades the whole body, one becomes troubled by the coarse thought stirred by initial thought's own movement, and, removing this coarseness, this is named removing every bodily formation. These first five belong to the foundation of mindfulness of the body.

受念處有三，謂麁息除故，身心安隱故。六、受喜；七、受樂者，雖有微喜，樂能遍滿身識，既滿，內心喜悅，故名樂；八、受諸心行者，既受樂在懷，必有數法相隨，倚心樂境，故名受諸心行。心念處有三：九、心作喜者，既止心一境，未有慧解，必為沈心所覆沒，以喜舉之，令不沈沒，故名作喜；十、心作攝者，喜心動散，則發越過，常攝之令還，不使馳散諸緣，故作攝；十一、心作解脫者，心不掉散，均等無累，故名解脫。

The foundation of mindfulness of sensation has three, since the coarse breath has now been removed, body and mind at peace. Sixth, receiving joy; seventh, receiving happiness: though there is only a faint joy present, happiness is able to fill the bodily consciousness completely, and, once full, the inward mind delights in it — hence it is named happiness. Eighth, receiving every mental formation: happiness now held within the mind, various mental factors are bound to accompany it in turn, the mind leaning upon this pleasant object — hence it is named receiving every mental formation.

The foundation of mindfulness of mind has three. Ninth, the mind making joy: the mind now stilled upon a single object, but not yet possessing wisdom's own understanding, is bound to be overwhelmed and submerged by a sinking mind — raising it up with joy keeps it from sinking; hence this is named making joy. Tenth, the mind making restraint: a mind of joy, once stirred and scattered, tends to leap and overshoot its own bounds — constantly restraining it, bringing it back, not letting it run off and scatter after various objects — hence this is named making restraint. Eleventh, the mind making liberation: the mind, no longer agitated or scattered, evenly balanced and free of entanglement — hence this is named liberation.

法念處有五：十二、觀無常者，已得自在，不為沈浮所敗，故能觀諸法無常，念念生滅，不可樂也；十三、觀散壞者，此身不久當散壞，磨滅之法，非真實有；十四、觀離欲者，此身唯是苦本，心欲離之，故名離欲；十五、觀滅者，是心住滅，多諸過患，不欲住故；十六、觀棄捨者，觀此諸法皆是過患，故名棄捨。此阿那波那等十六行是慧性，無一息入出而不覺，故彼師自云：「依經明十六特勝。」今既未見經文，但述而不作，亦未敢治定。

The foundation of mindfulness of dharma has five. Twelfth, contemplating impermanence: having now attained freedom, no longer defeated by either sinking or floating, one is thereby able to contemplate that all dharmas are impermanent, arising and ceasing moment to moment, nothing in them worth delighting in. Thirteenth, contemplating dispersal and decay: this very body will, before long, disperse and decay — a dharma of dissolution, without any true, real existence. Fourteenth, contemplating freedom from desire: this body is nothing but the very root of suffering, and the mind wishes to be free of it — hence this is named freedom from desire. Fifteenth, contemplating cessation: this mind now abides in cessation, free of the many faults and troubles it no longer wishes to abide in. Sixteenth, contemplating relinquishment: contemplating that all these dharmas are themselves nothing but fault and trouble — hence this is named relinquishment.

This mindfulness of the breath and the other sixteen practices are of the nature of wisdom, with not a single breath entering or leaving that is not fully perceived — hence this master states, of his own accord, that this follows what the sūtra itself clarifies as the sixteen special excellences. Since the actual sūtra text itself has not been seen here, this is only related, not independently composed, and so it is not yet asserted here as settled and fixed.

The Second Master's Correction, and the Vertical Scheme 十六特勝 · 明觀門制立不同 · 竪對諸禪

Chinese Source

次師別解云：「若對四念處起十六行，無往不爾。」但分之不調，如無漏十六行，約四諦中，一諦下有四，四四十六，有漏亦復應爾。然約四念處中說一念中有四，四四十六，義亦然。向言身念處有四，以除身行屬身者，此義不然。何以故？若心息為身行者，如《大集經》說：「息乃通於三行，非止屬身行。」今正明身行者，如《摩訶衍》說：「行名身業。」今明善惡諸業皆從心生。身息是無知之法，不能造善惡，但為行作緣。今身以心來受身，令身有所造作，名為身。今明行破於受心，即是破行也。故知此屬受念處，當知受中亦具四法也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The next master gives a different explanation: "If the sixteen practices are matched against the four foundations of mindfulness, this must hold true throughout without exception" — but the division just given does not properly balance. Just as the sixteen uncontaminated practices, discussed with reference to the Four Noble Truths, have four beneath each single truth, four times four making sixteen, the contaminated version ought properly to follow the same pattern. And, discussed with reference to the four foundations of mindfulness, it should likewise be said that each single foundation of mindfulness contains four, four times four making sixteen — the same sense holding here as well.

As for the claim just given, that the foundation of mindfulness of the body has four items, with "removing bodily formations" belonging to the body: this sense does not hold. Why is this? If mind and breath together were "bodily formation," then, as the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "The breath in fact extends through all three kinds of formation, not belonging solely to bodily formation." What is properly meant by "bodily formation" is, rather, as the Mahāyāna treatise says: "'Formation' means bodily karma." Here, it is clarified that every wholesome or unwholesome karmic action whatsoever arises from the mind. The body and the breath are themselves an unknowing dharma, unable to create anything wholesome or unwholesome on their own — they serve only as the condition through which formation acts. Here, it is the body, taken up by the mind and thereby received as a body, that causes the body to have anything actually done through it at all — this is what is properly named "body." What is clarified here as "formation," then, breaks through the very mind that receives — this is precisely what is meant by breaking through formation. Hence it is known that this properly belongs to the foundation of mindfulness of sensation instead — one should know that sensation, too, is thereby complete with its own four items.

向言法中有五，觀無常屬法念處者，此亦不然。何以故？經中皆說「觀心無常，觀法無我」，今明觀無常，正是心念處也。此則一中各說有四，四四十六，於義為便也。次第二師云：「此十六法應須竪對諸禪，八觀法相關故。」所以者何？一、知息入；二、知息出者，此則對於數息；三、知息長短者，對欲界定；四、知息遍身者，對未到地定；五、除諸身行者，對初覺支；六、受喜，對初禪喜支；七、心受樂，對初禪樂支；八、受諸心行者，對初禪一心支；九、心作喜，對二禪內淨喜支；十、心作攝，對二禪一心支；十一、心住解脫，對三禪樂支；十二、觀無常，對四禪不動定；十三、觀出散，對空處；十四、觀離欲，對識處；十五、觀滅，對不用處；十六、觀棄捨，對非想非非想處。此則從初調心乃至發諸禪定明觀行具足，此解為勝也。

As for the claim just given, that the dharma-category has five items, with "contemplating impermanence" belonging to the foundation of mindfulness of dharma: this, too, does not hold. Why is this? The sūtras all uniformly say: "contemplate mind as impermanent, contemplate dharmas as without self" — what is clarified here as "contemplating impermanence," then, properly belongs to the foundation of mindfulness of mind instead. This, then, gives each of the four foundations its own four items, four times four, which is more convenient in its own sense.

The second master, next, says: "These sixteen dharmas should properly correspond vertically to the various dhyānas in sequence, since the eight contemplation-dharmas are mutually related to them." Why is this?

First, knowing the breath entering; second, knowing the breath leaving — these correspond to counting the breath. Third, knowing the breath's length and shortness — this corresponds to desire-realm concentration. Fourth, knowing the breath pervading the whole body — this corresponds to the "not-yet-arrived" concentration. Fifth, removing every bodily formation — this corresponds to the first dhyāna's own factor of initial thought. Sixth, receiving joy — this corresponds to the first dhyāna's own factor of joy. Seventh, the mind receiving happiness — this corresponds to the first dhyāna's own factor of happiness. Eighth, receiving every mental formation — this corresponds to the first dhyāna's own factor of one-pointedness of mind. Ninth, the mind making joy — this corresponds to the second dhyāna's own factor of inner-purity joy. Tenth, the mind making restraint — this corresponds to the second dhyāna's own factor of one-pointedness of mind. Eleventh, the mind abiding in liberation — this corresponds to the third dhyāna's own factor of happiness. Twelfth, contemplating impermanence — this corresponds to the fourth dhyāna's own unmoving concentration. Thirteenth, contemplating dispersal — this corresponds to the sphere of space. Fourteenth, contemplating freedom from desire — this corresponds to the sphere of consciousness. Fifteenth, contemplating cessation — this corresponds to the sphere of nothingness. Sixteenth, contemplating relinquishment — this corresponds to the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception.

This scheme, then, runs from the very outset of regulating the mind all the way through bringing forth every dhyāna-concentration, its own contemplative practice complete throughout — this interpretation is the superior one.

Reading note: The first master's scheme sorts the sixteen items by which of the four foundations of mindfulness (body, sensation, mind, dharma) each belongs to; the second master keeps the same sixteen items but re-sorts two of them, and then proposes an entirely different organizing axis — mapping each item directly onto a specific stage of concentration, from counting the breath through the summit of the formless attainments. The treatise's own preference for the second, "vertical" scheme is significant: it turns the sixteen special excellences into a single continuous itinerary through every prior stage this whole treatise has covered — a compressed retrospective of fascicles 5 and 6 that will now be walked back through once more, this time with contemplative wisdom attached at every step rather than concentration alone.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying their cultivation and realization: as for why the mind is spoken of as "cultivating and realizing," this refers precisely to the mind's own deliberate cultivation and practice. Where the mind has not yet corresponded with realization, this refers to it opening forth spontaneously, the mind coming to correspond with it. Since there are, as already given, differing schemes for establishing the contemplation-method among these two masters, cultivation and realization likewise differ accordingly here as well — though the first master says: "Within the desire realm, the 'not-yet-arrived' state, and the first dhyāna, the sixteen special excellences are clarified with reference to the four foundations of mindfulness." The general sense of this contemplation-method does not differ from the contemplation-method already given within the six wondrous gates, though it is not entirely without small differences — the terms used differ, but, properly sought out and matched accordingly, the sense may be readily understood, and so this need not be separately explained here in terms of cultivation and realization.

Here, then, the second master's own scheme is directly clarified: the true special excellences, corresponding vertically through the three realms. Since the method of this contemplative wisdom has not yet been explained above, its mark of cultivation and realization shall now be set forth.

竪明修十六特勝者：一、知息入；二、知息出。此對代數息也。調息方法，事事如前數息中說。行者既調息綿綿，一心依隨於息，息若入時，知從鼻端入至臍；若出時，知從臍出至鼻。如是一心照息，依隨不亂，爾時知息麁細之相。麁者，知風喘氣為麁；細者，知息相為細。若入息麁時，即調之令細，是名知麁細相。譬如守門之人，知門人出入，亦知好人、惡人，知好則進，知惡則遮。復次，知麁細者，入息則麁，出息則細。何故爾？入氣利急故相麁，出息澁遲故細。復次，知輕重，知入息時輕，出息時重。何以故？入息既在身內，即令體輕；出息時，身無風氣，則覺身重。復次，知澁滑：入息時滑，出息時澁。何以故？息從外來，風氣利故，則滑；從內出吹，內滓穢，塞諸毛孔，則澁。復次，知冷暖：知入息時冷，知出息時暖。何以故？息從外來，冷氣而入，故冷；息從內出，吹內熱氣而出，故暖。復次，知久近：入息時近，出息時久。何以故？息入既利，則易盡，故近；息出澁，則難盡，故久。復須覺知因出入息故，則有一切眾苦煩惱，生死往來，輪轉不息，心知驚畏。行者隨息之時，知息有如是等法相非一，故云知息入出也。

Explaining the vertical cultivation of the sixteen special excellences: first, knowing the breath entering; second, knowing the breath leaving. These correspond to, and take the place of, counting the breath. The method for regulating the breath is, in every particular, exactly as already given within counting the breath. The practitioner, the breath now regulated and continuous, fixes the mind single-mindedly on following the breath: when it enters, one knows it as entering from the tip of the nose all the way to the navel; when it leaves, one knows it as leaving from the navel all the way to the nose. Illuminating the breath single-mindedly in this way, following it without disorder, one becomes aware, at this point, of the coarse and fine marks of the breath. As for the coarse: knowing the wind and panting breath as coarse. As for the fine: knowing the true breath's own mark as fine. If the in-breath is coarse, one regulates it, making it fine — this is named knowing the marks of coarse and fine.

It is like a gatekeeper, aware of everyone entering and leaving through the gate, likewise able to recognize the good person from the bad — recognizing the good, allowing them to pass; recognizing the bad, barring their way. Furthermore, as for knowing coarse and fine: the in-breath is coarse, and the out-breath is fine. Why is this? The in-breath's own energy is sharp and urgent, and so its mark is coarse; the out-breath is sluggish and slow, and so is fine. Furthermore, knowing light and heavy: knowing the in-breath as light, and the out-breath as heavy. Why is this? The in-breath, now present within the body, causes the body to feel light; at the moment of the out-breath, the body has no wind-energy present within it, and so is felt as heavy. Furthermore, knowing rough and smooth: the in-breath is smooth, and the out-breath is rough. Why is this? The breath comes from outside, its wind-energy sharp, and so is smooth; going out from within, blowing out the body's own inward filth, obstructing the various pores, it is thus rough. Furthermore, knowing cold and warm: knowing the in-breath as cold, and the out-breath as warm. Why is this? The breath comes from outside, cold air entering, and so is cold; the breath goes out from within, blowing out the body's own inward heat, and so is warm. Furthermore, knowing long-lasting and brief: the in-breath is brief, and the out-breath is long-lasting. Why is this? The in-breath, being sharp, is quickly exhausted, and so is brief; the out-breath, being rough, is not easily exhausted, and so is long-lasting.

One must further become aware that, because of this very in-breath and out-breath, every kind of suffering and affliction exists, coming and going through birth-and-death, turning without cease — the mind knowing this, and growing alarmed and fearful. The practitioner, at the time of following the breath, comes to know that the breath carries such marks and dharmas as these, not of one kind only — hence this is called knowing the breath entering and leaving.

問：何故以此代數息？答：若是數息，直闇心數，無有觀行，修證時多生愛、見、慢等諸煩惱病也。愛者，愛著此數息；見者，謂見我能數；慢者，謂我能數，以此慢他。今以隨代數者，隨息之時，即覺知此息無常，命依於息，以息為命，一息不還，即便無命。既覺息無常，知身命危脆。知息無常，即不生愛；知息非我，即不生見；悟無常，即不生慢。此則從初方便已能破諸結使，不同數息。復次，行者一心依息，令心不散，得入禪定，故名亦愛；覺悟無常，故名亦策；與定相應，名亦有漏；觀行不著，名亦無漏。復次，若數息時冥闇心而數，既無照了，後證定時，則心無所見。今隨息者既明心照息，後證定時，則心眼開明，見身三十六物，破愛、見、慢。此即是特勝勝於數息也。

Question: why is this used in place of counting the breath? Answer: were one simply counting the breath, this counting is done with a mind still in the dark, without any contemplative practice present at all, and so, at the time of cultivation and realization, tends to give rise to such afflictive ailments as craving, wrong views, and pride. Craving: growing attached to this very counting of the breath. Views: taking the view that "I" am the one able to count. Pride: taking pride that "I" am able to count, and, through this, feeling superior to others.

Here, following the breath is used in place of counting it: at the time of following the breath, one becomes aware directly that this very breath is impermanent — life itself depends upon the breath, the breath being taken as one's very life — and, should a single breath fail to return, life itself would be gone at once. Becoming aware of the breath's own impermanence, one thereby knows the body and its life to be perilous and frail. Knowing the breath as impermanent, craving no longer arises; knowing the breath as not-self, wrong views no longer arise; and awakening to impermanence, pride no longer arises. This, then, is already able, from the very first expedient means onward, to break through the fetters — unlike counting the breath alone.

Furthermore: the practitioner, fixing the mind single-mindedly on the breath, keeping the mind from scattering, is able to enter dhyāna-concentration through this — hence it, too, may be named a form of attachment; becoming aware of impermanence — hence it, too, may be named a spur; corresponding with concentration — hence it, too, is contaminated; and its contemplative practice not clinging to anything — hence it, too, is uncontaminated. Furthermore: were one counting the breath with a mind still in the dark, there being no illumination present at all, then, later, at the time concentration is actually realized, the mind would have nothing to see. Here, however, one who follows the breath already illuminates the mind, contemplating the breath directly, so that, later, at the time concentration is actually realized, the mind's own eye opens into clarity, seeing the body's thirty-six substances, breaking through craving, views, and pride. This is precisely how the special excellences surpass mere counting of the breath.

Reading note: The first two items already demonstrate the whole method's guiding logic: they occupy the exact functional slot of counting the breath, but replace bare mechanical repetition with an awareness of the breath's own impermanence built in from the very first step — the same craving/views/pride triad the six wondrous gates' own counting-stage was vulnerable to is here preempted before it can take hold, rather than corrected for later. The gatekeeper simile — recognizing good visitors from bad, admitting one and barring the other — gives a concrete image for what "knowing" the breath's marks actually means in practice: not passive awareness but active, discriminating attention.

Breath's Length and Shortness, and Pervading the Body 十六特勝 · 明修證 · 知息長短、遍身

Chinese Source

三、知息長短者，此對欲界定。若證欲界定時，宜是定明淨都不覺知息中相貌。今此中初得定時，即覺息中長短之相。云何為覺？若心定時，覺入息長，出息短。何以故？心既靜住於內，息隨心入故，入則知長。既心不緣外，故出則知短。復次，覺息長，則心細；覺息短，則心麁。何以故？心細則息細，息細則入從鼻至臍，微緩而長，出息從臍至鼻亦爾。心麁則息麁，息麁則入從鼻至臍，急疾短，出從臍至鼻亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, knowing the breath's length and shortness: this corresponds to desire-realm concentration. When desire-realm concentration is realized, the concentration itself is properly bright and pure, without any perception at all of the breath's own mark. Here, however, at the very moment this concentration is first attained, one becomes aware directly of the mark of the breath's own length and shortness.

How is this perceived? When the mind is concentrated, one perceives the in-breath as long and the out-breath as short. Why is this? The mind, now stilled and abiding within, the breath follows the mind inward, and so the in-breath is known as long; and, the mind no longer taking any external object, the out-breath is thus known as short. Furthermore: perceiving the breath as long corresponds to a fine mind; perceiving the breath as short corresponds to a coarse mind. Why is this? A fine mind produces a fine breath, and a fine breath, entering from the nose to the navel, is faint, gentle, and long, the out-breath from navel to nose being the same. A coarse mind produces a coarse breath, and a coarse breath, entering from the nose to the navel, is urgent and short, the out-breath from navel to nose being the same.

復次，息短故，覺心細；息長故，覺心麁。何以故？如心既轉靜，出息從臍至胸即盡，入息從鼻至咽間即知盡。此則心靜故，覺息短。覺長故心麁者，如行者心麁故，覺息從臍至鼻，從鼻至臍，道里長遠，此則心麁故，覺息長。復次，短中覺長則定細，長中知短則是麁。何故爾？如息從鼻至胸則盡，此行處雖短，而時若大，久久方至，臍。此則行處短而時節長也。若就此而論，短中覺長則定細。覺長中而短是麁者，如心麁故，息從鼻至臍，道里極長，而時節短，欻然之間，即出至鼻。何以故？心麁氣息行疾故。此雖長而短，然此息短則是心麁也，故云短中長而細，長中短而麁也。如此覺長短時節，知無常由心，生滅不定故。今息長短相貌非一，得此定時，覺悟無常，轉更分明。證欲界定，故名亦愛；觀行覺無常，故名亦策。此略說第三、知息長短，破欲界定也。

Furthermore: because the breath is short, the mind is perceived as fine; because the breath is long, the mind is perceived as coarse. Why is this? As the mind grows ever more still, the out-breath, from navel to chest, is exhausted at once, and the in-breath, from nose to throat, is likewise known to be exhausted at once — this, then, is the mind's own stillness causing the breath to be perceived as short. As for perceiving it as long, corresponding to a coarse mind: the practitioner's mind being coarse, the breath is perceived as traveling the long distance from navel to nose, and nose to navel — this, then, is the mind's own coarseness causing the breath to be perceived as long.

Furthermore: perceiving length within shortness corresponds to a fine concentration; perceiving shortness within length is instead coarse. Why is this? As when the breath, from nose to chest, is exhausted — this course, though short in distance, nonetheless takes a comparatively long time, only gradually reaching the navel; this, then, is a short course but a long duration. Discussed on this basis, perceiving length within shortness corresponds to a fine concentration. As for perceiving shortness within length being coarse: as when, the mind being coarse, the breath's course, from nose to navel, is an exceedingly long distance, and yet its duration is short, passing through to the nose again in a sudden instant. Why is this? Because a coarse mind causes the breath's own movement to be swift. Though this course is long, its duration is short — and this very shortness of duration is itself what marks the mind as coarse. Hence it is said: shortness containing length corresponds to fineness, and length containing shortness corresponds to coarseness.

Perceiving length and shortness of duration in this way, one comes to know that impermanence arises from the mind itself, its arising and ceasing never fixed. Now, the mark of the breath's own length and shortness being manifold, at the very moment this concentration is attained, awakening to impermanence grows all the more distinct. Realizing desire-realm concentration — hence it, too, may be named a form of attachment; its contemplative practice perceiving impermanence — hence it, too, may be named a spur. This, in brief, describes the third item, knowing the breath's length and shortness, breaking through desire-realm concentration.

第四、知息遍身者，對未到地定。若根本未到地，直覺身相泯然如虛空，爾時實有身息，但以眼不開故，不覺不見。今特勝中發未到地時，亦泯然入定，即覺漸漸有身如雲影，覺出入息遍身毛孔，爾時亦知息長短相等。見息入無積聚，出無分散，無常生滅，覺身空假不實，亦知生滅剎那不住，三事和合，故有定生。三事既空，則定無所依，知空亦空，於定中不著，即破根本未到地，愛、策之義已在其中。

Fourth, knowing the breath pervading the whole body: this corresponds to the "not-yet-arrived" concentration. Within the root "not-yet-arrived" state, one perceives only that the mark of the body has grown indistinct, as though it were empty space — at that point, the body and breath are, in truth, still genuinely present, only that, the eye not yet open, they are neither perceived nor seen.

Here, within the special excellences, when the "not-yet-arrived" state is brought forth, one likewise enters concentration indistinctly, but then becomes aware, gradually, of a body present once again, like a cloud or shadow, perceiving the in-breath and out-breath pervading every pore of the body — and, at this point, one also knows the mark of the breath's own length and shortness alike. Seeing the in-breath as gathering nowhere, and the out-breath as scattering nowhere, impermanent, arising and ceasing, one perceives the body as empty, provisional, and unreal, and also knows that its arising and ceasing never remain still for a single instant, these three matters combining together to produce concentration. These three matters being themselves empty, concentration thereby has nothing at all to depend upon — and, knowing even this very emptiness as itself empty, one no longer clings to anything within concentration at all. This, then, breaks through the root "not-yet-arrived" state, the sense of both attachment and spur already contained within it.

問：《摩訶衍》中及諸經多說觀息入出，何以故言知息入出？答：此說知為觀，而觀法實未具足，故今說為知。《大品．廣乘品》中明十六特勝相，皆言知息入出長短，以是文為證說知，則不乖文義。觀慧在下當說。

Question: the Mahāyāna treatise and the various sūtras, for the most part, speak of "observing" the breath entering and leaving — why, then, is it spoken of here as "knowing" the breath entering and leaving? Answer: this speaks of "knowing" as itself a form of observing, though the method of observation is not yet fully complete — hence it is spoken of here as "knowing" instead. The Larger Sūtra's own chapter on the vast vehicle, in clarifying the mark of the sixteen special excellences, likewise speaks throughout of "knowing" the breath entering, leaving, long, and short — taking this very text as evidence, speaking of "knowing" here does not run contrary to the text's own sense. Contemplative wisdom itself will be discussed below.

Reading note: The recurring short-within-long / long-within-short paradox (a short physical course taking a long time, a long course passing in an instant) is a genuinely careful phenomenological observation: length and shortness turn out not to be single, simple properties of the breath at all, but at least two independent measures — spatial distance and felt duration — that can point in opposite directions at once, and the treatise uses exactly this mismatch as the empirical hook for realizing that "impermanence arises from the mind itself." The "knowing versus observing" clarification is a small but careful piece of terminological housekeeping, anticipating a reader who might otherwise think the treatise's own vocabulary contradicts the sūtras it is built on.

Removing Bodily Formation: The First Dhyāna 十六特勝 · 明修證 · 除諸身行

Chinese Source

第五、除諸身行者，對初禪覺觀支。就中二：一、明身行；二、明除身。身行者，欲界身中發得初禪，色界四大造色，觸欲界身，欲界身根生身識，覺此色觸，二界色相依共住，故名身。身行者，即觀支。此觀支從身分生，知身中之法有所造作，故名身行。次明除身行者，因覺息遍身，發得初禪，心眼開明，見身三十六物臭穢可惡，爾時即知三十六物由四大有頭等六分，一一非身，四大之中，各各非身，此即是除欲界身也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, removing every bodily formation: this corresponds to the first dhyāna's own factor of initial thought and sustained reflection. Within this, there are two parts: clarifying bodily formation; and clarifying the removal of body.

As for bodily formation: the first dhyāna is brought forth within the body of the desire realm, the form realm's own four elements producing form that comes into contact with the desire-realm body — the desire-realm body's own faculty gives rise to body-consciousness, aware of this very tactile contact of form, the two realms' own form dwelling together, dependent on one another — hence this is named "body." "Bodily formation" is precisely the factor of initial thought: this factor of initial thought arises from the body itself, aware that the dharma within the body has something actually done through it — hence this is named "bodily formation."

Next, clarifying the removal of bodily formation: through perceiving the breath pervading the whole body, the first dhyāna is brought forth, the mind's own eye opening into clarity, seeing the body's thirty-six substances as foul, filthy, and repugnant — at this point, one comes to know that these thirty-six substances, arising from the four elements, are made up of six parts such as the head and the rest, no single one of them being the "body," nor is any single one of the four elements, in itself, the "body" either — this, then, is removing the desire-realm's own body.

除初禪身者，於欲界身中求色界四大不可得，名除初禪身。所以者何？若言有色界造色者，是為從外來，為從內出，為在中間住？如是觀時，畢竟不可得，但以顛倒憶想，故言受色界觸。諦觀不得，即是除初禪身，身除故，身行即滅。復次，未得初禪時，於欲界身中起種種善惡行，今見身不淨，則不造善惡諸結業，故名除身行。

As for removing the first dhyāna's own body: seeking, within the desire-realm body, for the form realm's own four elements, none can be found at all — this is named removing the first dhyāna's own body. Why is this? Were it said that there is form produced by the form realm present, does it come from outside, arise from within, or abide somewhere in between? Contemplating in this way, it can, in the end, never be found at all — it is simply through inverted, deluded thought that one speaks of "receiving the form realm's own tactile contact." Closely contemplated, it cannot be found — this is precisely removing the first dhyāna's own body; the body thus removed, bodily formation is thereby extinguished as well. Furthermore: before the first dhyāna is attained, within the desire-realm body, every kind of wholesome and unwholesome action is given rise to; now, seeing the body as impure, one no longer creates any wholesome or unwholesome karmic bond at all — hence this is named removing bodily formation.

今明此定亦有二種：一者、根本五支，如前說。二者、淨禪五支者，覺身三十六物虛假不實，名覺；分別此禪與欲界及根本功德大有優劣，名為觀。既得法喜，心大慶悅，名為喜；於無垢受恬憺之樂，名為樂。正定持心，令不動搖，名為一心。此中支除成就勝妙喜樂而心無染著，故名為淨禪也。復次，如《阿毗曇》中說隱沒無記有垢、不隱沒有記無垢等義，約此二種禪中，應廣分別。

Here, it is clarified that this concentration is itself likewise of two kinds: the root dhyāna's own five factors, as already described above; and the pure dhyāna's own five factors — perceiving the body's thirty-six substances as empty, provisional, and unreal, this is named initial thought; distinguishing that this dhyāna is, in its merit, vastly superior to the desire realm and to the root dhyāna alike, this is named sustained reflection; having attained the joy of the Dharma, the mind greatly celebrating and delighted, this is named joy; the tranquil happiness present within this undefiled sensation, this is named happiness; and right concentration sustaining the mind, keeping it from any agitation whatsoever, this is named one-pointedness of mind. Within this dhyāna, its factors having removed and accomplished excellent, wondrous joy and happiness while the mind remains free of any defiling attachment — hence this is named the pure dhyāna. Furthermore, as the Abhidharma speaks of the sense of the concealed-and-indeterminate, defiled, versus the unconcealed, determinate, undefiled — with reference to these two kinds of dhyāna, this should be distinguished at length.

Reading note: "Removing the body" happens in two distinct stages worth keeping separate: first the desire-realm body (dissolved by seeing it as parts, none of which is itself "the body"), then the first dhyāna's own subtler body (dissolved by searching for the form realm's four elements anywhere at all — outside, inside, or in between — and finding nothing). The resulting distinction between a "root" dhyāna and a "pure" dhyāna, sharing the same five factors but differing in whether contemplative wisdom accompanies them, gives a precise technical answer to what has been implicit throughout fascicle 5: the very same factor-structure can be either an ordinary, defiled attainment or a pure one, depending entirely on whether insight is layered on top of it.

Receiving Joy, Happiness, and Mental Formations 十六特勝 · 明修證 · 受喜、受樂、受諸心行

六、受喜者，即是對破初禪喜支。根本禪中喜支從隱沒有垢，覺觀後生，既無觀慧照了，多生煩惱，故不應受。今明受喜者，於淨禪覺觀支中生，以有觀行破析，達覺觀性空，當知從覺觀生喜亦空，即於喜中不著，無諸過罪，故說受喜。如羅漢人不著一切供養，故名應供。復次，如真實知見，得真法喜，故說受喜。七、受樂者，對根本禪樂支。彼禪既無觀慧，樂中多染，故不應受。今言受樂者，受無樂，知樂性空，於樂中不著。既無樂過罪，上無別證無為之樂，故說受樂。

Sixth, receiving joy: this corresponds to, and breaks through, the first dhyāna's own factor of joy. Within the root dhyāna, the factor of joy arises from the concealed, defiled factors of initial thought and sustained reflection, arising afterward — and, there being no contemplative wisdom to illuminate it, this, for the most part, gives rise to affliction, and so ought not to be received. Here, what is clarified as receiving joy arises instead from the pure dhyāna's own factor of initial thought and sustained reflection, and, through contemplative practice analyzing and breaking through it, one penetrates the very emptiness of initial thought and sustained reflection's own nature — one should thereby know that the joy arising from initial thought and sustained reflection is likewise empty, and so is no longer clung to within this very joy, free of every fault — hence this is called receiving joy. It is like an arhat who no longer clings to any offering whatsoever, and so is named "worthy of offerings." Furthermore: through this true knowledge and vision, one attains the true joy of the Dharma itself — hence this is called receiving joy.

Seventh, receiving happiness: this corresponds to the root dhyāna's own factor of happiness. That dhyāna, lacking any contemplative wisdom at all, its happiness, for the most part, defiled — and so ought not to be received. Here, what is spoken of as receiving happiness means receiving a happiness that is itself without happiness in the ordinary, graspable sense — knowing happiness's own nature to be empty, and so no longer clinging to it within happiness itself. Free of happiness's own fault in this way, and yet without any further, separately realized unconditioned happiness above it either — hence this is called receiving happiness.

八、受諸心行者，此對破根本一心支。今明能通諸法，故名諸心行。心行有二種：一者、動行；二者、不動行。有人解云：從初禪乃至三禪猶是動行，四禪已去，名不動行。今略說不動行者，覺等四支是動行，後一心支是不動行。亦名諸心行者，即是一心支不動之行。若根本禪入一心時，心生染著，此一心不應受。今明受諸心行者，知此一心虛誑不實，一心非心，即不取著，既無過罪，即是三昧正受，故說受諸心行。

Eighth, receiving every mental formation: this corresponds to, and breaks through, the root dhyāna's own factor of one-pointedness of mind. Here, what is clarified as being able to pervade every dharma is named "every mental formation." Mental formation is itself of two kinds: moving formation; and unmoving formation. Some explain: from the first dhyāna all the way through the third, this is still moving formation; from the fourth dhyāna onward, it is named unmoving formation. Here, briefly speaking of unmoving formation: the preceding four factors, initial thought and the rest, are moving formation, while the last, the factor of one-pointedness of mind, is unmoving formation. This, too, is what is meant by "every mental formation": precisely the unmoving formation of the factor of one-pointedness of mind itself.

Within the root dhyāna, at the moment one-pointedness of mind is entered, a mind of defiling attachment arises — this one-pointedness of mind ought not to be received. Here, what is clarified as receiving every mental formation means knowing this very one-pointedness of mind to be deceptive and unreal, this one-pointedness of mind itself not truly "mind" at all, and so no longer grasping or clinging to it — and, being thereby free of any fault, this is itself the true reception of samādhi — hence this is called receiving every mental formation.

Reading note: Items six through eight complete the first dhyāna's own five-factor structure (initial thought and sustained reflection already handled by item five) by showing that "receiving" joy, happiness, and one-pointedness of mind in the special-excellences sense is almost the opposite of ordinary reception — it means seeing each factor's own emptiness clearly enough that clinging becomes impossible, "a happiness without happiness in the ordinary, graspable sense." The arhat simile (no longer clinging to any offering, and so uniquely "worthy" of them) captures the paradox precisely: freedom from attachment to a good thing is what actually allows it to be received purely.

The Second and Third Dhyānas: Joy, Restraint, and Liberation 十六特勝 · 明修證 · 心作喜、心作攝、心住解脫

Chinese Source

九、心作喜者，此對二禪內淨喜。所以者何？二禪喜從內淨發，以無智慧照了多受也。今觀此喜，即知虛誑不生受著，如真實知生法喜，亦名喜覺分。既從正觀，心生真法喜，故名心作喜。十、心作攝者，此對二禪一心支。何以故？為二禪喜動經攝，故說心作攝。今明攝者，正以破前偽喜，生喜覺喜，此喜雖正，而不無湧動之過，即應返觀喜性。既知空寂畢竟，定心不亂，不隨喜動，故云作攝。是以《大集經》云：「動至心。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Ninth, the mind making joy: this corresponds to the second dhyāna's own inner-purity joy. Why is this? The second dhyāna's own joy arises from inner purity, and, lacking any wisdom to illuminate it, is, for the most part, received with attachment. Here, contemplating this very joy, one comes to know it as deceptive and false, and so does not give rise to any attached reception of it — and, through this true knowledge, one gives rise instead to the true joy of the Dharma, also named the factor of enlightenment that is joy. Arising, in this way, from right contemplation, the mind gives rise to the true joy of the Dharma — hence this is named the mind making joy.

Tenth, the mind making restraint: this corresponds to the second dhyāna's own factor of one-pointedness of mind. Why is this? Because the second dhyāna's own joy, once stirred, must be gathered back in through restraint — hence this is called the mind making restraint. Here, what is clarified as "restraint" is precisely breaking through the previous, false joy, giving rise instead to the joy of the factor of enlightenment. Though this joy is itself correct, it is nonetheless not entirely without the fault of an excited, surging movement — one should therefore turn the contemplation back upon the very nature of this joy. Knowing it, in the end, to be empty and still, the concentrated mind remains undisturbed, no longer following this excited movement of joy — hence this is called making restraint. Hence the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "Movement, brought all the way to the very mind itself."

十一、心住解脫者，此對破三禪樂。所以者何？三禪有遍身之樂，凡夫得之，多生染愛，為之所縛，不得解脫。今言解脫者，以觀慧破析，證遍身樂時，即知此樂從因緣生，空無自性，虛誑不實，觀樂不著，心得自在，故名心作解脫。

Eleventh, the mind abiding in liberation: this corresponds to, and breaks through, the third dhyāna's own happiness. Why is this? The third dhyāna has happiness pervading the whole body present within it; an ordinary person, attaining it, for the most part gives rise to defiled attachment, bound by it, unable to attain liberation. Here, what is spoken of as liberation means that, through contemplative wisdom analyzing and breaking through it, at the very moment this pervasive happiness is realized, one knows this happiness to arise from causes and conditions, empty, without any nature of its own, deceptive and unreal — contemplating happiness without clinging to it, the mind attains freedom — hence this is named the mind making liberation.

Reading note: Item ten's "restraint" is a subtle refinement worth flagging: it is not restraint of the second dhyāna's original, unwholesome joy (already broken through by item nine's "making joy"), but restraint of the correct, wisdom-born joy that item nine itself produced — the treatise catching an excess in its own antidote before it can become a new attachment in turn. This is the same pattern already seen with the six wondrous gates' own escalating dissatisfaction (each stage revealing the next as too coarse by comparison), now applied one level deeper, to a state that is already correct rather than merely unwholesome.

The Fourth Dhyāna: Contemplating Impermanence 十六特勝 · 明修證 · 觀無常

Chinese Source

十二、觀無常，此對破四禪不動。所以者何？如世間中有動、有不動法，三種為樂所動，猶名動法。今此四禪名不動定，凡夫得此定時，多生常想，心生愛取，今若觀此定生滅代謝，三相所遷，知是破壞不安之相，故經云：「一切世間動、不動法皆敗壞不安之相。」故名觀無常。

Ru-Yi Translation

Twelfth, contemplating impermanence: this corresponds to, and breaks through, the fourth dhyāna's own unmoving concentration. Why is this? Just as, within the mundane world, there are dharmas both moving and unmoving — the three lower dhyānas, moved by happiness, are still named "moving" dharmas. This fourth dhyāna, by contrast, is named the unmoving concentration; an ordinary person, attaining this concentration, for the most part gives rise to a notion of permanence, the mind giving rise to craving and grasping. Here, however, contemplating that this very concentration arises, ceases, and passes through change, shifted by the three marks of conditioned existence, one knows it to bear the very mark of destruction and instability — hence the sūtra says: "Every dharma in the mundane world, whether moving or unmoving, bears the mark of destruction and instability alike." Hence this is named contemplating impermanence.

Reading note: This item's whole force rests on the fourth dhyāna's own name — "unmoving" — being exactly the trap that catches an ordinary practitioner: mistaking the absence of gross agitation for the absence of impermanence itself. The sūtra citation makes the correction precise: "moving" and "unmoving" describe only a dharma's own internal turbulence, not whether it is subject to arising and ceasing at all — even the most stable-feeling attainment in the entire mundane hierarchy is still, at bottom, "shifted by the three marks of conditioned existence."

The Four Formless Attainments 十六特勝 · 明修證 · 觀出散、觀離欲、觀滅

Chinese Source

十三、觀出散者，此對破空處。所以者何？出者，即是出離色界；散者，即是散三種色。復次，出散者，謂出離色心，依虛空消散自在，不為色法所縛，故名出散。凡夫得此定時，謂是真空安隱，心生取著。今言觀出散者，行人初入虛空處時，即知四陰和合故有，無自性，不可取著。所以者何？若言有出散者，為虛空是出散，為心是出散？若心出散，心為三相所遷，過去已謝，未來未至，現在無住，何能出耶？若空是出散者，空是無知，無知之法，有何出散？既不得空定，則心無受著，是名觀出散。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thirteenth, contemplating dispersal: this corresponds to, and breaks through, the sphere of space. Why is this? "Dispersal" here means leaving the form realm behind; and it also means dispersing the three kinds of form. Furthermore, "dispersal" means leaving both form and mind behind, relying instead on empty space's own dissolution and freedom, no longer bound by the dharma of form — hence this is named dispersal. An ordinary person, attaining this concentration, takes it to be a true, secure emptiness, the mind giving rise to grasping and attachment.

Here, what is spoken of as contemplating dispersal means that the practitioner, upon first entering the sphere of space, already knows that this exists only through the combining together of the four aggregates, without any nature of its own, and so cannot properly be grasped or clung to. Why is this? Were it said that there is genuinely something that "disperses" here, is it empty space itself that disperses, or is it the mind that disperses? If it is the mind that disperses, the mind is itself shifted by the three marks of conditioned existence — the past already gone, the future not yet arrived, and the present never remaining still — how, then, could it possibly "disperse" at all? And if it is space itself that disperses, space is itself without any knowing at all — how could a dharma without any knowing possibly "disperse"? Since the concentration of emptiness can thus never actually be found, the mind is thereby free of any attached reception of it — this is named contemplating dispersal.

十四、觀離欲者，此對識處。所以者何？一切愛著外境，皆名為欲。從欲界乃至空處，皆是心外之境。若虛空為外境，識來領受此空，即以空為所欲。今識處定緣於內識，能離外空欲故離欲。若凡夫得此定，無慧眼照了，謂言心與識法相應，真實安隱，即生染著。今言觀離欲者得此定時，即觀破析。若言以心緣識，心與識相應，得入定者，此實不然。何以故？過去、未來、現在三世識皆不與現在心相應故，云何言心與三世識相應？定法持心，名為識定。故知此識定但有名字，虛誑不實，故名離欲也。

Fourteenth, contemplating freedom from desire: this corresponds to the sphere of consciousness. Why is this? Every attachment to an external object whatsoever is named "desire." From the desire realm all the way through the sphere of space, all of it is an object external to the mind. If empty space itself is taken as an external object, and consciousness comes to receive this very emptiness, then emptiness itself is taken as an object of desire. Here, the concentration of the sphere of consciousness takes internal consciousness as its object, and is thereby able to leave the desire for external emptiness behind — hence it is named freedom from desire.

An ordinary person, attaining this concentration, lacking the wisdom-eye to illuminate it, takes it that the mind corresponds with the dharma of consciousness itself, truly and genuinely secure, and so gives rise to defiled attachment. Here, what is spoken of as contemplating freedom from desire means that, upon attaining this concentration, one contemplates it, analyzing and breaking through it directly. Were it said that the mind takes consciousness as its object, the mind corresponding with consciousness, and thereby enters concentration — this is, in truth, not so at all. Why is this? Because the consciousness of the past, future, and present, across all three periods of time, never actually corresponds with the present mind at all — how, then, could it be said that the mind corresponds with the consciousness of the three periods of time? The dharma of concentration sustains the mind — this alone is named the concentration of consciousness. Hence it is known that this concentration of consciousness has only a name, deceptive and unreal — hence it is named freedom from desire.

十五、觀滅者，此對無所有處。所以者何？此定緣無為法塵，心與無為相應。對無為法塵發少識故，凡夫得之，謂之心滅，深生愛著，不能捨，為之所縛。今言觀滅者，得此定時，即覺有少識。此識雖少，亦有四陰和合，無常、無我、虛誑，譬如糞穢，多少俱臭，不可染著，是名觀滅。

Fifteenth, contemplating cessation: this corresponds to the sphere of nothingness. Why is this? This concentration takes the unconditioned dharma-object as its object, the mind corresponding with the unconditioned. In response to this unconditioned dharma-object, only a slight consciousness is brought forth — an ordinary person, attaining this, takes it that the mind itself has ceased, giving rise to deep attachment, unable to relinquish it, bound by it. Here, what is spoken of as contemplating cessation means that, upon attaining this concentration, one becomes aware directly that a slight consciousness is nonetheless still present. Though this consciousness is slight, it, too, is made up of the four aggregates combining together, impermanent, without self, deceptive — like filth and excrement, whether much or little, equally foul — not to be attached to or clung to at all. This is named contemplating cessation.

Reading note: Items thirteen through fifteen repeat, at the formless level, the exact critique fascicle 6 already made of each of these same three spheres — but the special excellences add a distinctive analytic move at item thirteen worth isolating: asking not just "is this attainment empty?" but "what, grammatically, would even be doing the dispersing?" and finding neither space (which has no knowing to disperse) nor mind (which, being already in flux across the three time-periods, has no fixed state to disperse from) can coherently fill that role. This is a sharper, more explicitly logical register than the parallel passages in fascicle 6, consistent with these items being contemplative wisdom retracing concentration's own prior ground.

Relinquishment, Subhadra, and the Closing Summary 十六特勝 · 明修證 · 觀棄捨、結歎

Chinese Source

十六、觀棄捨者，此對非想。所以者何？非想是兩捨之對治，從初禪以來，但有遍捨，無有兩捨，故未與棄捨之名。今此非想既有雙捨有無，故名棄捨。亦以此定是捨中之極，故最後受名。若凡夫得此定時，謂為涅槃，無有觀慧覺了，不能捨離。今明棄捨者，得此定時，即知四陰、十二入、三界及十種細心數等和合所成。當知此定無常、苦、空、無我，虛誑不實，不應計為涅槃，生安樂想。既知空寂，即不受著，是名觀棄捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixteenth, contemplating relinquishment: this corresponds to neither perception nor non-perception. Why is this? Neither-perception-nor-non-perception is the antidote to a double relinquishment; from the first dhyāna onward, there has only been a single, general relinquishment at each stage, never a double relinquishment — and so the name "relinquishment" has not yet been given until now. Here, this very state of neither perception nor non-perception involves relinquishing both existence and nonexistence together — hence it is named relinquishment. It is also because this concentration is the very utmost point of relinquishment that it receives this name last of all.

An ordinary person, attaining this concentration, takes it to be nirvāṇa itself, lacking any contemplative wisdom to illuminate it, unable to relinquish and leave it behind. Here, what is clarified as relinquishment means that, upon attaining this concentration, one knows directly that it is made up of the combining together of the four aggregates, the twelve sense-bases, the three realms, and the ten kinds of subtle mental factors. One should know that this concentration is impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, deceptive and unreal, not to be reckoned as nirvāṇa, nor should any notion of peace and happiness be given rise to regarding it. Knowing it to be empty and still in this way, one no longer receives or clings to it — this is named contemplating relinquishment.

雖求定相，而亦成就此定，爾時即具二種棄捨：一者、根本棄捨；二者、涅槃棄捨。永棄生死，故云觀棄捨。行者爾時深觀棄捨，即便得悟三乘涅槃。此事如須跋陀羅，佛令觀非想中細想，即便獲得阿羅漢果。今明悟道未必應須具十六，或得三二特勝，即便得悟。亦利根者，初隨息時，覺悟無常，即便悟道。此隨人不定也。從初以來，俱發根本定，故名亦有漏；於中觀行，破析不著，名亦無漏。故云特勝是亦有漏亦無漏禪。此竪對三界諸禪者，則一一觀法相，至義可見也。

Although one seeks the mark of this very concentration, and does indeed accomplish it, at this point one is complete with two kinds of relinquishment at once: the root relinquishment; and the relinquishment of nirvāṇa itself. Forever relinquishing birth-and-death — hence this is called contemplating relinquishment.

The practitioner, at this point, deeply contemplating relinquishment, thereby comes to awaken to the nirvāṇa of the three vehicles directly. This matter is exactly as with Subhadra: the Buddha had him contemplate the subtle perception present within neither-perception-nor-non-perception, and he thereby attained the fruit of arhatship at once.

Here, it is clarified that awakening to the path need not necessarily require all sixteen items in full — one may attain awakening through only two or three of the special excellences alone. Likewise, a practitioner of sharp faculty may, already at the very first stage of following the breath, awaken to impermanence and thereby awaken to the path directly — this differs, and is not fixed, from person to person.

From the very outset, these all bring forth the root concentrations alike — hence they, too, are named contaminated; and, within them, the contemplative practice analyzing and breaking through, without clinging to anything — hence they, too, are named uncontaminated. Hence it is said that the special excellences are dhyāna that is both contaminated and uncontaminated at once. As for this vertical correspondence to the various dhyānas across the three realms, the mark of each single contemplative method, down to its very sense, may be seen throughout.

Reading note: Subhadra was traditionally the Buddha's very last personal convert before his parinirvāṇa — invoking him here, at the close of the sixteenth and final special excellence, gives this whole method a kind of bookend authority: the same contemplation that opened with the Buddha's own practice beneath the bodhi tree (echoed throughout this fascicle's account of the six wondrous gates) closes with the very last person the Buddha personally taught. The explicit statement that awakening "need not necessarily require all sixteen items in full" is worth holding onto against the sheer length of this fascicle's own exposition — the sixteen-fold structure is a complete map of the territory, not sixteen mandatory checkpoints every practitioner must pass through in sequence. This closes the sixteen special excellences, and with them, fascicle 7 in its entirety — dhyāna both mundane and world-transcending's first two named practices now complete, with the thorough-and-bright contemplation, the third, still to come.
