The Gate of Stopping

This page continues Ru-Yi's own English translation of Zhiyi's Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen (釋禪波羅蜜次第法門). Having completed the external expedient means in fascicle 2, the treatise turns to the internal expedient means — five further layers of practice, beginning with the gate of stopping (止, śamatha), the foundation the treatise says every other dhyāna depends on.

弟子法慎記

Ru-Yi Translation

Fascicle Three, Upper Part
Spoken by the Great Master Zhiyi of Tiantai, of the Sui dynasty
Recorded by his disciple Fashen

The Internal Expedient Means 分別禪波羅蜜前方便第六之二

Fascicle 2 completed the twenty-five external expedient means. Topic 6 now turns to its second half: the internal expedient means, five further layers of practice for the moment concentration actually begins to take hold. Rather than external circumstances, these concern the direct handling of the mind itself — starting with what the treatise calls the foundation every other gate of practice depends on: stopping.

Chinese Source

第二、明修禪波羅蜜內方便，開為五重：一、先明止門；二、明驗善惡根性；三、明安心法；四、明治病患；五、明覺魔事。此五通稱內方便者，並據初發定時，靜細心中善巧運用，取捨不失其宜，因此必證深禪定，故名方便。今於內方便中，以止為初門者，一切禪定功德皆因制心息亂而發，故經云：「制之一處，無事不辦。」止為初門，則意在此也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the internal expedient means for cultivating dhyāna-pāramitā: this opens into five layers — first, clarifying the gate of stopping; second, clarifying how to verify wholesome and unwholesome capacity; third, clarifying the methods for settling the mind; fourth, clarifying the treatment of illness; fifth, clarifying the recognition of demonic affairs. All five of these are called, in general, the internal expedient means, because they concern the skillful application of a still, refined mind at the very moment concentration first begins to arise — taking up and setting aside without ever losing what is proper to the moment — and it is through this that deep dhyāna-concentration is certain to be realized; hence they are called expedient means.

Here, within the internal expedient means, stopping is taken as the first gate, because every merit of dhyāna-concentration is brought forth only through restraining the mind and stilling its confusion — hence the sūtra says: "Restrain it to a single place, and there is nothing that cannot be accomplished." This is exactly the intent behind taking stopping as the first gate.

問曰：上來明外方便行五法中，已辨一心，何故重說？答曰：不然。上但通論一心，未是具足分別微細止門之法，此中為令行者善知安心之本，廣明修止淺深麁細入定之相，重說無咎。問曰：經中說二為甘露門：一者、不淨觀門；二者、阿那波那門。不說止為初門，今云何言止為初門？答曰：不然。於諸禪中，止為通門，通攝於別，別不攝通，故先教止。若止後入餘禪，則有通益；若依餘門，則有乖違之過。治煩惱亦爾。

Question: Above, in explaining the external expedient means, within the practice of the five dharmas, one-pointedness of mind has already been distinguished — why explain it again here? Answer: This does not follow. What was given above only discussed one-pointedness of mind in a general way; it was not yet a complete, detailed distinguishing of the fine and subtle method of the gate of stopping. Here, in order to let the practitioner properly know the very root of settling the mind, the marks of cultivating stopping — shallow and deep, coarse and fine, entering concentration — are explained fully and at length; there is no fault in explaining it once more.

Question: The sūtras speak of two gates to the deathless — the gate of the contemplation of impurity, and the gate of mindfulness of the breath — they do not speak of stopping as the first gate. How, then, can it now be said that stopping is the first gate? Answer: This does not follow. Among the various dhyānas, stopping is the general gate, and the general encompasses the specific, while the specific does not encompass the general — hence stopping is taught first. If, after stopping, one then enters any other dhyāna, there is a general benefit gained; but if one relies on some other gate alone, there is the fault of a mismatch. The same holds for treating affliction.

復次，今明師有二種：第一師者，已得道眼，觀機授法，必扶本習，善識對治。不如舍利弗為二弟子說法，不知機故，金師之子，教不淨觀，浣衣之子，教令數息，違本所習，法則不起，遂生邪見。佛為轉觀，即悟道迹。第二師者，無他心智，不得道眼，不識機根，其有來學坐者，唯當先教止門，心在定故，即發善惡根性。若因靜心發諸禪定，師即應教扶本而修。若都不發法門，或貪、瞋、癡等諸結使發，隨其多者，即教對治破之，遮道法滅，禪定則發。今止門為先者，即是第二師授法之正意。若異此說，則善惡根緣難可分別，妄授他法，必有差機之過。

Furthermore, teachers here are clarified as being of two kinds. The first kind of teacher has already attained the eye of the path, observes each student's capacity, and bestows the teaching accordingly — necessarily matching it to that student's own prior habituation, skillfully recognizing the proper antidote. Not to do so is like Śāriputra teaching his two disciples: not knowing their capacity, he taught the contemplation of impurity to the son of a goldsmith, and taught mindfulness of the breath to the son of a launderer — going against their own prior habituation, so that the practice failed to take hold, and wrong views arose instead. The Buddha, redirecting their contemplations, brought them at once to realize the fruit of the path.

The second kind of teacher lacks the wisdom to know another's mind, has not attained the eye of the path, and cannot recognize a student's capacity. For such a teacher, whoever comes to learn sitting meditation should simply be taught the gate of stopping first — since, as the mind settles into concentration, wholesome and unwholesome capacities are thereby brought forth of themselves. If, through a stilled mind, the various dhyānas are brought forth, the teacher should then teach in accordance with, and building upon, that student's own foundation. If no dharma-gate is brought forth at all, or if craving, anger, delusion, or other fetters are instead brought forth, the teacher should, according to whichever predominates, teach the corresponding antidote to break it — removing what obstructs the path, so that dhyāna-concentration can then be brought forth.

That the gate of stopping is given first here is precisely the proper intent behind how the second kind of teacher bestows the teaching. If this order were otherwise, the causes and conditions of wholesome and unwholesome capacity would be hard to distinguish, and bestowing some other teaching at random would necessarily result in a mismatch with the student's actual capacity.

Reading note: This opening is a candid piece of pedagogical realism: Zhiyi acknowledges that the ideal teacher — one with genuine insight into a student's capacity, like the Buddha correcting Śāriputra's mismatched assignments — is rare, and designs the treatise's own default sequence for the more common case, a teacher without that direct insight. Starting everyone with stopping isn't presented as the single best method in the abstract; it's presented as the safest default precisely because it reveals what a student actually needs next, functioning as a diagnostic as much as a practice in its own right.

Four Parts of the Gate of Stopping 止門

Chinese Source

就止門中，自有四意：一者、分別止門不同；二者、立止大意；三、明修止方法；四、辯證止之相也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the gate of stopping, there are four parts in itself: first, distinguishing the different kinds of the gate of stopping; second, establishing the great intent of stopping; third, clarifying the method of cultivating stopping; fourth, distinguishing the marks that verify stopping.

Three Kinds of Stopping, in Practice 分別止門不同 · 約行論止

Chinese Source

第一、分別止門不同，即為二意：一、約行論止；二、約義論止。初、約行明止，乃有多途，今略出三意：一、繫緣止；二、制心止；三、體真止。所以通言止者，止名制止，亦名止息。心起制之，不令流動，故名制。專心定志，息諸亂想，故名止。今言繫緣止者，繫心鼻柱、臍間等處，不令馳蕩，故名繫緣止。制心止者，心若覺觀，即制令不起，故名制心止。體真止者，體諸法空，息諸妄慮，故名體真止。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, distinguishing the different kinds of the gate of stopping: this has two parts — stopping discussed in terms of practice, and stopping discussed in terms of principle.

First, stopping explained in terms of practice has many approaches; here, in brief, three are given: fixed-object stopping; restraining-the-mind stopping; and embodying-truth stopping. The reason "stopping" is used as the general term for all three: "stopping" means restraining, and also means bringing to rest. When the mind arises, restraining it and not letting it flow off — hence it is called restraining. Applying the mind single-mindedly with settled intent, bringing every scattered thought to rest — hence it is called stopping.

Now, as for fixed-object stopping: fixing the mind on the bridge of the nose, the navel, or some other such place, not letting it run off and wander — hence it is called fixed-object stopping. As for restraining-the-mind stopping: whenever coarse or subtle discursive thought arises in the mind, restraining it at once so that it does not arise — hence it is called restraining-the-mind stopping. As for embodying-truth stopping: embodying the emptiness of all dharmas, bringing every deluded deliberation to rest — hence it is called embodying-truth stopping.

Three Kinds of Stopping, in Principle 分別止門不同 · 約義論止

Chinese Source

二、約義論止，亦有多途，今略出三意：一、隨緣止；二、入定止；三、真性止。隨緣止者，隨心起時，悉有三摩提數，故《涅槃經》云：「十大地中定，名為下定。」入定止者，證定之時，定法持心，心息止住，是入定止。真性止者，心性之理，常自不動，故名為止，故《思益經》云：「一切眾生即是滅盡定。」今用此三義，成上三止：約隨緣任性有定故，說繫緣止；約果有定法，說制心止；由具性不動，說體真止。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, stopping explained in terms of principle likewise has many approaches; here, in brief, three are given: stopping that follows conditions; stopping that has entered concentration; and stopping of the true nature.

Stopping that follows conditions: whenever the mind arises, a factor of samādhi is already present within it — hence the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, "The concentration among the ten mental factors of the great ground is called the lower concentration." Stopping that has entered concentration: at the very moment concentration is realized, the dharma of concentration itself sustains the mind, and the mind and breath come to rest and abide — this is stopping that has entered concentration. Stopping of the true nature: the principle of the very nature of mind is, of itself, forever unmoving — hence it is called stopping; and so the Sūtra of Contemplating the Increase of Goodness says, "All sentient beings are, of themselves, already the concentration of cessation."

These three senses now serve to complete the three kinds of stopping given above: in terms of a concentration already present by following conditions and according with one's own nature, fixed-object stopping is spoken of; in terms of the dharma of concentration present in the fruit, restraining-the-mind stopping is spoken of; and owing to the unmoving nature already fully possessed, embodying-truth stopping is spoken of.

Reading note: The two three-part schemes are not two different lists but the same three kinds of stopping seen from two angles — one describing what a practitioner actually does (fix attention on an object; restrain the mind directly; embody the emptiness of all dharmas), the other describing the principle each practice ultimately rests on. Notice, too, the progression baked into all three kinds: from managing attention externally (an object) to managing it internally (the mind itself) to dissolving the very distinction that made "managing" necessary in the first place — the same coarse-to-fine movement already seen in fascicle 2's body-breath-mind sequence, now applied to the whole of practice.

The Great Intent of Establishing Stopping 立止大意

Chinese Source

第二、明立止大意者，自為四：一、明淺深；二、對治相破；三、隨樂欲；四、隨機宜。一、簡別三種止淺深之相不同者，因麁入細，則有淺深之義：繫緣及制心既是事，故麁淺；體真入細，故為深細。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the great intent of establishing stopping: this itself has four parts — first, clarifying the shallow and the deep; second, mutual antidoting; third, following inclination; fourth, matching capacity.

First, distinguishing that the marks of shallowness and depth differ among the three kinds of stopping: since one proceeds from the coarse into the fine, there is necessarily a sense of shallow and deep. Fixed-object stopping and restraining-the-mind stopping are both matters of practical technique, and so are coarse and shallow; embodying-truth stopping enters into the fine, and so is deep and subtle.

二、明三止對治相破，有二種：一者、以深破淺；二者、迴互相破。以深破淺者，為破緣外之散心故，立繫緣止。制心止者，即破繫緣止。心非色法，豈可繫在鼻膈等處？若欲靜之，但當息諸攀緣，故令制心守一。體真止者，即破前制心止。心無形相，性不可得，云何可制？了心非心，不起妄念，無止之止，止無所止，乃名為止。有止之止，由依妄想，不名為止。此則以深破淺，反本還源，故立三止。二、迴互相破者，隨修止時，若有見生，即互取一止對治破之，細尋可解。

Second, clarifying that the three kinds of stopping mutually break through one another as antidotes: this is of two kinds — first, the deep breaking through the shallow; second, each breaking through the others in turn.

As for the deep breaking through the shallow: fixed-object stopping is established in order to break through the scattered mind that takes external objects. Restraining-the-mind stopping then breaks through fixed-object stopping in turn: the mind is not a material thing — how could it truly be fixed at the nose or the diaphragm or any such place? If one wishes to still it, one need only bring every chasing after objects to rest, restraining the mind instead to hold to the one. Embodying-truth stopping then breaks through the prior restraining-the-mind stopping in turn: the mind has no fixed shape or form, and its very nature cannot be found — how, then, could it be restrained at all? Once it is understood that mind is not fixed as mind, and no deluded thought arises, then a stopping that stops nothing, a stopping with nothing at all left to stop, is what is truly called stopping. A stopping that still has something to stop still depends on deluded thought, and so cannot properly be called stopping. This, then, is the deep breaking through the shallow, turning back to the very root and returning to the source — hence the three kinds of stopping are established in this order.

Second, as for each breaking through the others in turn: whenever, in the course of cultivating stopping, some wrong view arises, whichever of the three kinds of stopping serves as its antidote should be taken up in turn to break through it — this can be understood upon careful examination.

三、隨樂欲者，自有人樂安心境界，自有但樂制心、體真亦爾。若隨所樂以法教之，則歡喜奉行；若乖其情，則心不願樂。四、對機宜者，未必隨樂。如有人樂欲體真，而不入定。若暫繫心守境，即發諸禪。此應隨便宜而授法。

Third, as for following inclination: some people delight in settling the mind upon an object, others delight simply in restraining the mind, and others again in embodying truth. If one is taught according to what one delights in, the teaching is joyfully received and put into practice; but if it goes against one's own inclination, the mind has no wish to delight in it.

Fourth, as for matching capacity: this need not always follow mere inclination. There are cases where a person delights in and wishes for embodying truth, and yet does not thereby enter concentration; whereas if the mind is temporarily fixed upon holding to an object instead, the various dhyānas are brought forth. In such a case, the teaching should be given according to whatever is actually suited to the moment.

Reading note: "Mutual antidoting" gives the three kinds of stopping their internal logic — deep replacing shallow in a fixed progressive order — while "following inclination" and "matching capacity" immediately qualify that order with real pedagogical flexibility: a student's own temperament matters, but so does what actually works for them, and the two do not always coincide. The example given is pointed — someone who longs for the most advanced practice, embodying-truth stopping, may simply fail to enter concentration through it, while a more basic fixed-object practice succeeds — a reminder that spiritual aspiration and present readiness are not the same thing. This closes the second of the gate of stopping's four parts.
