The Thorough-and-Bright Contemplation

This page presents Ru-Yi's own English translation of fascicle 8 of Zhiyi's Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen (釋禪波羅蜜次第法門). Having covered the six wondrous gates and the sixteen special excellences in fascicle 7, the treatise completes dhyāna both mundane and world-transcending with its third and final named practice: the thorough-and-bright contemplation, matched to practitioners whose concentration and wisdom are equally balanced. This fascicle contains some of the treatise's most extraordinary material — including a body-as-cosmos, body-as-royal-court correspondence unlike anything found elsewhere in the work.

弟子法慎記

今辨此禪，大意為三：一者、釋名；二者、辨次位；三者、明修證。一、釋名者，所以此禪名為通明觀者，此觀方法出《大集》經文，無別名目。北國諸禪師修得此禪，欲以授人，既不知名字，正欲安根本禪裡，而法相逈殊。若對十六特勝，則名目全不相關；若安之背捨、勝處，觀行方法條然別異。既進退並不相應，所以諸師別作名目，名為通明觀禪。或有說言：「《華嚴經》有此名目。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Fascicle Eight
Spoken by the Great Master Zhiyi of Tiantai, of the Sui dynasty
Recorded by his disciple Fashen
Revised and edited by his disciple Guanding
Explaining Dhyāna-Pāramitā's Cultivation and Realization, Topic 7, Part 4

Here, this dhyāna is distinguished, in its general sense, in three parts: explaining the name; distinguishing its sequence of stages; and clarifying its cultivation and realization.

First, explaining the name: as for why this dhyāna is named the thorough-and-bright contemplation, this contemplative method is given in the text of the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra, without any separate name of its own there. The various dhyāna masters of the northern lands, having cultivated and attained this dhyāna and wishing to transmit it to others, not knowing its proper name, at first wished simply to place it among the root dhyānas — but its own dharma-characteristics differ vastly from those. Matched against the sixteen special excellences, its own terms bear no relation to them at all; placed alongside the liberations or the stations of mastery, its own contemplative method and practice differ entirely as well. Since it corresponds properly with neither, in either direction, the various masters accordingly gave it a separate name of its own, calling it the dhyāna of thorough-and-bright contemplation. Some, further, say that this very name is given in the Avataṃsaka Sūtra.

所言通者，謂從初修習即通觀三事。若觀息時，即通照色心；若觀色，乃至心亦如是。此法明淨，能開心眼，無諸暗蔽，既觀一達三，徹見無閡，故名通明。復次，善修此禪，必定能發六通、三明故。《大集經》明法行比丘修此禪時，欲得神通，即能得之。今言通者，即是能得六通；明者，即是能生三明。此因中說果，故言通明觀。

As for what is meant by "thorough": this means that, from the very outset of its cultivation, one thoroughly contemplates all three matters — the breath, form, and mind — together. When contemplating the breath, one thereby illuminates form and mind together as well; and when contemplating form, the same holds all the way through to mind as well. This dharma is bright and pure, able to open the mind's own eye, free of every obscuring darkness — contemplating one, all three are reached at once, seeing through them without any obstruction whatsoever — hence it is named "thorough."

Furthermore: skillfully cultivating this dhyāna is certain to bring forth the six supernormal powers and the three insights. The Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra clarifies that, when the dharma-practicing monk cultivates this dhyāna, wishing to attain supernormal powers, he is thereby able to attain them directly. Here, "thorough" refers precisely to being able to attain the six supernormal powers; and "bright" refers precisely to being able to give rise to the three insights. This speaks of the result already within the cause — hence it is called the thorough-and-bright contemplation.

問曰：餘禪亦能發六通、三明，何故獨此禪說為通明？答曰：餘禪乃有發通明之義，不如此禪利疾，故名通明。問曰：如《大集經》亦有別釋此禪名義，故經言：「所言禪者，疾故名禪。疾大疾，住大住，靜寂靜，觀滅遠離，是名為禪。」今何故別立名耶？答曰：彼經雖有此釋，於義乃顯，而名猶漫，既不的有名目，故復更立通明之名。

Question: the other dhyānas, too, are able to bring forth the six supernormal powers and the three insights — why, then, is only this dhyāna spoken of as "thorough and bright"? Answer: the other dhyānas do indeed carry this same sense of bringing forth thoroughness and brightness, but not so swiftly and sharply as this dhyāna — hence this alone is named thorough-and-bright.

Question: the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra itself gives a separate explanation of this dhyāna's own name and meaning, the sūtra saying: "As for what is called dhyāna: it is swift, and so is named dhyāna — swift, greatly swift; abiding, greatly abiding; still, utterly still; contemplating cessation, far removed — this is what is named dhyāna." Why, then, is a separate name established here instead? Answer: although that sūtra does give this explanation, its sense, though made clear there, still leaves the name itself somewhat diffuse — since no definite, specific name is actually given there, a further name, "thorough-and-bright," is established here instead.

Reading note: The origin story here is unusually candid about how the name itself came about — not from a single scriptural source, but from northern dhyāna masters who had already cultivated this practice and needed some name for it once they discovered it fit neatly into none of the treatise's existing categories. "Thorough" and "bright" turn out to be independently justified: thoroughness names the method's own starting move (any one of breath, form, or mind illuminates all three at once), while brightness names its fruit (the three insights), with the six supernormal powers folded into "thorough" as well — "speaking of the result already within the cause," as the text itself puts it.

Its Sequence of Stages 通明觀 · 辨次位

Chinese Source

第二、明次位者，此禪無別次位，猶是約根本四禪、四空立次位。但於一一禪內更有增勝出世間觀定之法，能發無漏及三明、六通疾利，亦於非想後心滅諸心數，入滅受想定，故不同根本暗證取著，無有神智功能。是故雖復次位同於根本，而觀慧殊別，恐人謬解，故立別名。雖名有異，而次位無差。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying its sequence of stages: this dhyāna has no separate sequence of stages of its own — it, too, is established with reference to the sequence of stages of the root four dhyānas and the four formless concentrations. It is simply that, within each single dhyāna, there is, further, a superior, world-transcending dharma of contemplation and concentration present, able to bring forth the uncontaminated, along with the three insights and the six supernormal powers, swiftly and sharply — and, following neither-perception-nor-non-perception, the mind's various mental factors are extinguished, entering the concentration of the cessation of sensation and perception. Hence it differs from the root dhyānas' own dark realization and clinging attachment, which lack any such spiritual, wisdom-bearing capacity at all. Hence, although its sequence of stages is the same as the root dhyānas', its contemplative wisdom differs entirely — and, fearing this might be misunderstood, a separate name is established for it. Though the name differs, the sequence of stages itself shows no difference at all.

問曰：若此禪得入滅定，與九次第定有何異耶？答曰：修此定時，心心無間，亦得說為九次第定，然終非是具足九次第定法。是事在下自當可見。若比准《成實論》解九定、八解，亦是具足。

Question: if this dhyāna is able to enter the concentration of cessation, how does it differ from the nine sequential concentrations? Answer: at the time this concentration is cultivated, mind follows mind without interruption, and so it, too, may be spoken of as the nine sequential concentrations — yet, in the end, it is not the same as the nine sequential concentrations' own complete dharma. This matter will be seen for itself below. Compared against the Satyasiddhi-śāstra's own explanation of the nine concentrations and the eight liberations, it, too, is complete in this same respect.

Reading note: This is a precise statement of what actually distinguishes the thorough-and-bright contemplation from the ordinary root dhyānas — not a different ladder of attainments, but the identical ladder climbed with contemplative wisdom attached at every rung, all the way to a genuine cessation of sensation and perception rather than the "dark realization and clinging attachment" of an ordinary practitioner reaching the same formal stage. The separate name exists precisely to prevent this crucial difference from being missed by someone who sees only the shared stage-sequence.

The First Dhyāna: Cultivating Suchness-Thought 通明觀 · 明修證 · 修習如心

Chinese Source

第三、明修證，此禪既無別次位，還約根本次位辨修證也。第一、先明修證初禪之相，如《大集經》說：「言初禪者，亦名為具，亦名為離。離者，謂離五蓋；具者，謂具五支。五支者，謂覺、觀、喜、安、定。云何為覺？如心覺、大覺，思惟、大思惟，觀於心性，是名為覺。云何為觀？觀心行、大行、遍行、隨意，是名為觀。云何為喜？如真實知、大知，心動至心，是名為喜。云何為安？謂心安、身安、受安，受於樂觸，是名為安。云何為定？謂心住、大住，不亂於緣，不謬，無有顛倒，是名為定。」即是彼經略釋修證通明初禪之相。推此經文所明五支，則與餘經論所明大異，故須別釋。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying its cultivation and realization: since this dhyāna has no separate sequence of stages of its own, its cultivation and realization, too, are distinguished with reference to the root dhyānas' own sequence of stages.

First, clarifying beforehand the mark of cultivating and realizing the first dhyāna, as the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "As for the first dhyāna: it is named both 'complete' and 'free.' As for 'free': this means free of the five hindrances. As for 'complete': this means complete in the five factors. The five factors are: initial thought, sustained reflection, joy, ease, and concentration. What is meant by initial thought? Suchness-thought, great-thought; deliberation, great deliberation; contemplating the nature of mind — this is named initial thought. What is meant by sustained reflection? Contemplating the conduct of mind, great conduct, pervasive conduct, and conduct at will — this is named sustained reflection. What is meant by joy? Suchness-knowledge, great knowledge; the mind moved all the way to the very heart of mind itself — this is named joy. What is meant by ease? This means ease of mind, ease of body, and ease of sensation, receiving a blissful touch — this is named ease. What is meant by concentration? This means the mind abiding, greatly abiding, undisturbed by any object, unmistaken, free of any inversion whatsoever — this is named concentration." This is precisely that sūtra's own brief explanation of the mark of cultivating and realizing the thorough-and-bright contemplation's first dhyāna. Reasoning through what this sūtra passage clarifies regarding the five factors, this differs vastly from what the other sūtras and treatises clarify — hence it must be explained separately here.

今先釋如心。如心者，即是初禪前方便定發也。亦即是未到地，但證不孤發，要由修習。云何修習？行者從初安心即觀於息、色、心三事俱無分別。觀三事者，必須先觀息道。云何觀息？謂攝心靜坐，調和氣息，一心諦觀息想遍身出入。若慧心明利，即覺息入無積聚，出無分散，來無所經由，去無所履涉。雖復明覺息入出遍身，如空中風，性無所有。是則略說觀息如心相。

Here, "suchness-thought" is first explained. Suchness-thought is precisely the preliminary expedient concentration that arises before the first dhyāna itself — it is, in fact, the "not-yet-arrived" state, though its realization does not arise on its own, in isolation, but necessarily through cultivation.

How is it cultivated? The practitioner, from the very outset of settling the mind, contemplates that the three matters of breath, form, and mind are all alike without any distinct discrimination between them. To contemplate these three matters, one must first contemplate the path of the breath. How does one contemplate the breath? By gathering the mind and sitting still, regulating and harmonizing the breath, closely contemplating single-mindedly the very conception of the breath going out and coming in throughout the body. If the wisdom-mind is bright and sharp, one perceives the in-breath as gathering nowhere, and the out-breath as scattering nowhere, coming from no particular course and going by no particular path. Although one clearly perceives the breath entering and leaving throughout the body in this way, it is like wind within empty space, its very nature having nothing at all present within it. This, in brief, describes contemplating the breath as suchness-thought.

次觀色如。行者既知息依於身，離身無息，即應諦觀身色如。此色本自不有，皆是先世妄想因緣，招感今世四大、造色圍虛空故，假名為身。一心諦觀頭等六分、三十六物及四大、四微，一一非身，四微、四大亦各非實，尚不自有，何能生六分之身、三十六物？無身色可得，爾時心無分別，即達色如。次觀心如。行者當知由有心故，則有身色、去來、動轉。若無此心，誰分別色？色因誰生？諦觀此心藉緣而有，生滅迅速，不見住處，亦無相貌，但有名字，名字亦空，即達心如。行者若不得三性別異，名為如心。

Next, contemplating form as suchness. The practitioner, already knowing that the breath depends upon the body, and that, apart from the body, there is no breath at all, should then closely contemplate the body's own form as suchness. This form is, in itself, originally without existence at all — it is entirely a matter of past-life deluded thought and causal conditions, drawing together the present life's own four elements, whose form encircles empty space, and is thereby only provisionally named "body." Closely contemplating, single-mindedly, the six parts such as the head and the rest, the thirty-six substances, the four elements, and the four subtle elements, none of these, one by one, is itself the "body" — and even the four subtle elements and the four elements themselves are, each in turn, not truly real, not even existing on their own — how, then, could they possibly give rise to a six-part body, or to the thirty-six substances? No bodily form whatsoever can be found — at this point, the mind is free of any discrimination, and thus penetrates form as suchness.

Next, contemplating mind as suchness. The practitioner should know that it is because mind exists at all that bodily form, coming and going, movement and change, exist as well. Were this mind not present, who would discriminate form? From what would form itself arise? Closely contemplating this very mind, it, too, is found to exist only in dependence on conditions, arising and ceasing with great swiftness, no dwelling-place to be found, and no mark or appearance either — it has only a name, and even this name is itself empty — this, then, penetrates mind as suchness. The practitioner, no longer finding these three natures to be distinctly different from one another, this is named suchness-thought.

復次，行者若觀息時，既不得息，即達色、心空寂。何以故？三法不相離故。色心亦爾。若不得色心三事，即不得一切法。所以者何？由此三事和合，能生一切陰、入、界眾苦煩惱，善惡行業，往來五道，流轉不息。若了三事無生，則一切諸法本來空寂。是則略說修習如心之相。第二、明證相。此亦具有證欲界、未到地相。行者如上觀察三性，悉不可得，其心任運自住真如，其心泯然明淨，名欲界定。於此定後，心依真如法，心泯然入定，與如相應，如法持心，心定不動，泯然不見身、息、心三法異相，一往猶如虛空，故名如心，即是通明未到地也。

Furthermore: the practitioner, contemplating the breath, once the breath itself can no longer be found, thereby penetrates form and mind as empty and still as well. Why is this? Because these three dharmas are never actually separate from one another — form and mind are exactly the same. If these three matters, form, mind, and the rest, cannot be found, then no dharma whatsoever can be found either. Why is this? Because it is precisely through the combining together of these three matters that every aggregate, sense-base, and element, every kind of suffering and affliction, every wholesome and unwholesome karmic action, coming and going through the five realms, turning without cease, is able to arise at all. Once one understands that these three matters are themselves unarisen, then every dharma whatsoever is, in itself, originally empty and still. This, in brief, describes the mark of cultivating suchness-thought.

Second, clarifying the mark of realization: this, too, is complete with the mark of realizing both desire-realm concentration and the "not-yet-arrived" state. The practitioner, contemplating and observing these three natures as already described above, finding none of them at all, the mind now abides spontaneously, of its own accord, in true suchness itself, the mind grown indistinct, bright, and pure — this is named desire-realm concentration. Following this concentration, the mind relies on the dharma of true suchness, the mind entering concentration indistinctly, corresponding with suchness itself, the dharma of suchness sustaining the mind, the concentrated mind unmoving, indistinctly no longer seeing any distinct mark among the three dharmas of body, breath, and mind at all, altogether just like empty space — hence this is named suchness-thought, which is precisely the thorough-and-bright contemplation's own "not-yet-arrived" state.

Reading note: "Suchness-thought" gives the thorough-and-bright contemplation its own distinctive starting move, distinct from every other method in this treatise: rather than beginning with the breath alone and adding contemplation of form and mind later, it treats all three as inseparable from the very first sitting — "these three dharmas are never actually separate from one another." The regress this section runs through (form depends on mind, but searching for mind finds only something "existing only in dependence on conditions... its name itself empty") is the same reflexive move fascicle 7's own "returning" gate made against the contemplating mind — confirmation that this fascicle's method, despite its unfamiliar name, shares its deepest logic with everything already covered.

The First Dhyāna: Its Threefold World and Marks of Arising 通明觀 · 初禪發相 · 三種世間

Chinese Source

次釋初禪發相，如前引經說，此應具釋五支證相。今先據覺支為本，覺義既成，釋餘四支則從可見。所以經言「覺、大覺」。覺者，覺根本禪，覺觸發相，故名為覺。此事如前說，但輕重有異。大覺者，豁然心目開明，明見三事發相，名為大覺。此傍釋，未是正意。復次，今當分別覺、大覺義。所言覺者，覺世間相也；大覺，出世間也。此即對真、俗二諦釋之，亦有漏、無漏，義意在此。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, explaining the mark of the first dhyāna's own arising, as already cited above from the sūtra: this should be explained in full, the mark of realizing all five factors. Here, the factor of initial thought is first taken as the basis — once its sense is established, the remaining four factors may then be readily seen by the same pattern.

Hence the sūtra speaks of "initial thought, great initial thought." As for "initial thought": awakening to the root dhyāna, aware of the mark of tactile sensation's own arising — this is named initial thought. This matter is as already described above, differing only in its degree, light or heavy. As for "great initial thought": the mind's own eye opening suddenly into clarity, clearly seeing the mark of these three matters' own arising — this is named great initial thought. This, however, is only a peripheral explanation, not yet the proper intent.

Furthermore, the sense of initial thought and great initial thought shall now be properly distinguished. As for what is meant by "initial thought": awakening to the mark of the mundane world. "Great initial thought" means awakening to the world-transcending. This corresponds, respectively, to the two truths of the ultimate and the conventional, and to the contaminated and the uncontaminated — the sense intended here lies precisely in this.

今明世間則有三種：一、根本世間，一期正報五陰是也；二、義世間者，知根本之法，與外一切法義理相關也；三、事世間者，發五通時，悉見一切眾生種類及世間事也。世間既有三種，出世間對世間亦為三。所以者何？眾生根有下、中、上、利、鈍不等，是故雖同證此初禪，境界淺深，其實有異，故須約三義分別證初禪不同。第一、先釋約根本世間、出世間明覺大覺五支成初禪之相，即為二意：第一、先明初禪發相；第二、即釋成覺大覺五支差別之相。一、先明初禪發之相，即為三意，品次不同：一者、初發；二、次；三、後。

Here, "the mundane world" is itself clarified as being of three kinds: first, the root world, which is precisely the five aggregates of one's own present life's karmic retribution; second, the world of meaning, which means knowing that the dharma of the root world is itself interrelated, in principle, with every external dharma whatsoever; and third, the world of phenomena, which means, once the five supernormal powers are brought forth, seeing directly every kind of sentient being and every mundane matter whatsoever. Since the mundane world is thus of three kinds, the world-transcending, too, is correspondingly distinguished into three, set against the mundane. Why is this? Sentient beings' own faculties differ — lower, middle, upper, sharp, and dull, not all the same — and so, although they alike realize this same first dhyāna, the depth of their own state actually differs. Hence realizing the first dhyāna must be distinguished according to these three senses.

First, explaining, with reference to the root world and the world-transcending, how initial thought and great initial thought, together with the five factors, form the mark of the first dhyāna: this itself has two parts — first, clarifying beforehand the mark of the first dhyāna's own arising; and second, explaining how this constitutes the differentiated mark of initial thought, great initial thought, and the five factors. First, clarifying beforehand the mark of the first dhyāna's own arising: this itself has three parts, differing in grade and sequence — the initial arising; the middle; and the final.

Reading note: The three-world scheme (root, meaning, phenomena) is this fascicle's own organizing structure and worth holding in mind for everything that follows: the same five factors of the first dhyāna will be explained three separate times, once through each world, because a sharp-faculty practitioner's realization genuinely reaches further than a dull-faculty practitioner's, even though both are said to attain "the same first dhyāna." What follows next — the marks of the first dhyāna's own arising — belongs to the root world, the most basic and universal of the three.

Marks of Arising: The Initial Stage 通明觀 · 初禪發相 · 初發相

Chinese Source

一、初發相。行者發初禪時，即豁然見自身九萬九千毛孔空疎，氣息遍身毛孔出入。雖心眼明見遍身出入，而入無積聚，出無分散，來無所經由，去無所履涉，即見身內三十六物一一分明。三十六物者：諸髮、毛、爪、齒、薄皮、厚皮、筋、肉、骨、髓、脾、腎、心、肝、肺、小腸、大腸、胃、胞、膽、屎、尿、垢、汗、淚、涕、唾、膿、血、脈、黃痰、白痰、癊、肪、𦙽、腦膜。此三十六物，十是外物，二十六是內物；二十二是地物，十四是水物。已見風、水、地相分明，復覺諸物各有熱氣煎煮，火相分明。觀此四大猶如四蛇同處一篋，四大蚖蛇，其性各異；亦如屠牛之人，分肉為四分，諦觀四分，各不相關。行者亦爾，心大驚悟。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the mark of initial arising: the practitioner, at the very moment the first dhyāna arises, suddenly sees the ninety-nine thousand pores of one's own body opening loosely, the breath's own energy going out and entering through every pore of the body. Although the mind's own eye clearly sees this going out and entering throughout the whole body, the in-breath gathers nowhere, and the out-breath scatters nowhere, coming by no particular course and going by no particular path — and one thereby sees, within the body, each of the thirty-six substances clearly and distinctly.

The thirty-six substances are: head-hair, body-hair, nails, teeth, thin skin, thick skin, sinews, flesh, bones, marrow, spleen, kidneys, heart, liver, lungs, small intestine, large intestine, stomach, bladder, gall, feces, urine, grime, sweat, tears, mucus, saliva, pus, blood, vessels, yellow phlegm, white phlegm, lymph, fat, membrane-fat, and brain-membrane. Of these thirty-six substances, ten are external, and twenty-six are internal; twenty-two belong to the earth element, and fourteen belong to the water element.

Having already seen the marks of wind, water, and earth clearly, one further perceives that each of these substances carries its own heat, simmering and boiling — the mark of fire, too, now clear and distinct. Contemplating these four elements, it is as though four snakes shared a single basket together — the four elements as four venomous serpents, each of a differing nature; or it is like a butcher dividing an ox's meat into four portions, closely observing these four portions as entirely unrelated to one another. The practitioner is exactly the same, the mind greatly startled into awakening.

復次，行者非但見身三十六物四大假合，不淨可惡，亦覺知五種不淨之相。何等為五？一者、見外十物相不淨，心生厭患，是名自相不淨；二者、見身內二十六物內性不淨，是名自性不淨；三者、自覺此身從歌羅邏時，父母精血和合以為身種，是名種子不淨；四者、此身處胎之時，在生、熟二臟之間，是名生處不淨；五者、及其此身死後，捐棄塚間，壞爛臭穢，是名究竟不淨。當知此身從始至終，不淨所成，無一可樂，甚可厭惡，我為無目，忽於昔來，著此不淨臭爛之身，造生死業於無量劫，今始覺悟，悲喜交懷。五種不淨，如《摩訶衍論》廣說。復覺定內心識，緣諸境界，念念不停，諸心數法相續而起，所念相異，亦復非一，是名初禪初證之相。

Furthermore: the practitioner, seeing not only that the body's thirty-six substances are the four elements only provisionally combined, impure and repugnant, also becomes aware of five distinct marks of impurity. What are these five? First, seeing the mark of the ten external substances as impure, the mind gives rise to weariness and disgust — this is named the impurity of external mark. Second, seeing the twenty-six internal substances' own inward nature as impure — this is named the impurity of inward nature. Third, becoming aware, of oneself, that this very body, from the moment of kalala onward, is formed from the combined seed and blood of one's parents — this is named the impurity of the seed. Fourth, this body, at the time it dwells within the womb, resides between the two organs of the raw and the cooked — this is named the impurity of the place of birth. And fifth, once this body has died, it is discarded within the graveyard, decaying and rotting, foul and putrid — this is named the ultimate impurity.

One should know that this body, from beginning to end, is made up entirely of impurity, with nothing in it worth delighting in, deeply repugnant — I, having been without eyes, have, since time immemorial, clung to this impure, rotting body, creating the karma of birth-and-death across measureless kalpas, and only now am I first awakening to this, sorrow and joy mingling together in my heart. These five kinds of impurity are given at length within the Mahāyāna treatise. One further perceives that, within this very concentration, mind and consciousness, taking various states as their object, never remain still for a single moment, the various mental factors arising in unbroken succession, the object of each thought differing from the last, not of one kind only — this is named the mark of the first dhyāna's initial realization.

Reading note: The four-snakes-in-one-basket and butcher's-four-portions similes are among this treatise's most visceral images, chosen precisely because they refuse any comforting unity: the four elements composing "the body" are shown as fundamentally hostile to one another, sharing only forced proximity, not a genuine whole. The five kinds of impurity extend fascicle 3's earlier three-part account (from the internal expedient means) into a fuller five-part scheme spanning outward appearance, inward substance, conception, gestation, and death — the same basic content this treatise has returned to repeatedly, now anchored to an intensely detailed, first-person visionary experience rather than a doctrinal list.

Marks of Arising: The Middle and Final Stages 通明觀 · 初禪發相 · 中發相、後發相

次明中證相。行者住此定內，三昧漸深，覺息後五臟內生息相各異，所謂青、黃、赤、白、黑等，隨臟色別，出至毛孔。若從根入色，相亦不同。如是分別，氣相非一。復見此身薄皮、厚皮、膜、肉各有九十九重，大骨、小骨三百六十，及髓各有九十八重。於此骨肉之間有諸蟲，四頭、四口、九十九尾，如是形相非一，乃至出入、來去、音聲、言語亦悉覺知。唯腦有四分，分有十四重。身內五臟，葉葉相覆，猶如蓮華，孔竅空疎，內外相通，亦各有九十九重。諸物之間亦各有八十戶蟲，於內住止，互相使役。

Next, clarifying the mark of the middle realization. The practitioner, abiding within this very concentration, samādhi growing gradually deeper, perceives that, behind the breath, the five internal organs each give rise to their own differing breath-mark — namely, blue, yellow, red, white, and black, each differing according to its own organ's color, issuing out through the pores. Depending on which faculty it enters through, its color, too, differs. Distinguishing in this way, the marks of this energy are not of one kind only.

One further sees that this body's thin skin, thick skin, membranes, and flesh each have ninety-nine layers; the large and small bones number three hundred sixty; and the marrow, too, has ninety-eight layers each. Between this flesh and bone are various parasites, each with four heads, four mouths, and ninety-nine tails, their forms not of one kind only — and one becomes fully aware, further, of their own movement, coming and going, sounds, and speech as well. The brain alone has four sections, each section having fourteen layers. Within the body, the five internal organs, leaf upon leaf covering one another, are just like a lotus flower, their apertures open and porous, inward and outward freely interpenetrating, each likewise having ninety-nine layers. Between these various substances, there are also, in each case, eighty households of parasites dwelling within, each set to their own task, mutually employing one another.

若行者心靜細時，亦於定內聞諸蟲語言音聲，或時因此發解眾生言語三昧。身內諸脈，心脈為主，復從心脈內生四大之脈，一大各十脈，十脈之內一一復各九脈，合成四百脈。從頭至足，四百四脈內悉有風氣血流相注。此脈血之內亦有諸細微之蟲依脈而住。行者如是知，是知身內外不實，猶如芭蕉。復觀心數，隨所緣時，悉有受、想、行、識四心差別不同。

If the practitioner's mind grows still and fine, one may also hear, within this very concentration, the sound of these various parasites' own speech, and, at times, through this, come to understand the samādhi that comprehends the speech of sentient beings. Among the body's various vessels, the heart-vessel serves as the ruling one, and, from within the heart-vessel, the vessels of the four elements arise in turn, each element having ten vessels, and, within each of these ten vessels, nine further vessels each, combining to make four hundred vessels in all. From head to foot, within these four hundred and four vessels, wind, energy, and the flow of blood all pour through and irrigate one another. Within this very blood of the vessels, there are also various fine, subtle parasites dwelling, depending on the vessels for their own existence. The practitioner, knowing all this, thereby knows the body, within and without, to be unreal, just like a plantain trunk. One further contemplates the mental factors, and, whatever object is taken up, sensation, perception, formation, and consciousness, these four states of mind, are each found to differ from one another.

三、明後證之相。行者三昧智慧轉深淨明利。復見氣息調和，同為一相，如瑠璃器，非青、黃、赤、白。亦見息之出入無常生滅，悉皆空寂，復見身相新新，無常代謝。所以者何？飲食是外四大，入腹資身時，新四大既生，當知故身隨滅，譬如草木，新葉既生，故葉便落，身亦如是。愚夫不了，謂是惜身；智者於三昧內覺此身相無常所遷，新新生滅，空無自性，色不可得。復各一念心生之時，即有六十剎那生滅。或有人言：「六百剎那，生滅迅速，空無自性，心不可得。」

Third, clarifying the mark of the final realization. The practitioner's samādhi and wisdom grow ever deeper, purer, and sharper. One further sees the breath's own energy grown harmonious, become a single, unified mark, like a vessel of lapis lazuli, no longer distinctly blue, yellow, red, or white. One also sees the breath's own entering and leaving as impermanent, arising and ceasing, entirely empty and still, and further sees the body's own mark as ever-new, impermanent, constantly replacing itself.

Why is this? Food and drink are external instances of the four elements; entering the belly and nourishing the body, once this new instance of the four elements has arisen, one should know that the old body correspondingly perishes — just as, with grass and trees, once a new leaf has grown, the old leaf accordingly falls; the body is exactly the same. The foolish, not understanding this, cherish and cling to the body; the wise, within this very samādhi, perceive this bodily mark as shifted by impermanence, ever newly arising and ceasing, empty, without any nature of its own, its form nowhere to be found. Further still, within the arising of a single thought-moment, sixty instants of arising and ceasing are already present. Some, further, say: six hundred instants, arising and ceasing with such swiftness that it, too, is empty, without any nature of its own, the mind itself nowhere to be found.

Reading note: The escalation across these three stages traces a single arc from multiplicity to unity: the initial stage sees the body's parts as radically distinct and even hostile to one another (four warring snakes); the middle stage multiplies this detail even further, down to parasites with names for their own body parts and audible speech; and the final stage collapses everything — the four colors, the countless layers and vessels — into "a single, unified mark, like a vessel of lapis lazuli," where impermanence itself, not the body's parts, becomes the object of contemplation. The sixty-versus-six-hundred-instants disagreement, left unresolved, is a rare case of the treatise citing a numerical dispute without adjudicating it — the exact count matters less than the shared conclusion that thought-moments are far too swift for any fixed "mind" to be found within them.

The Root World: Initial Thought Through the Eight Marks 通明觀 · 根本世間 · 覺支

Chinese Source

第二、明釋成覺觀五支之相，即為五：第一、釋覺支，經說覺支云：「覺、大覺，思惟、大思惟，觀於心性。」約此五句以明覺相。今先釋「覺、大覺」二句，此約世間、出世間境界分別，故有此二覺之異。世間境，即是異相；出世間境，即是如相。此之如、異，即是真、俗二諦之別名也。今約觀門，淺深易見。今當具依《摩訶衍》分別。論云：「有三種：上、中、下。」如、異既有三種覺，大覺亦應為三也。論意分別假名為異。分別四大實法同體，名為下如。分別地大異餘三大，名為異。同一無常，生滅不異，名次如。無常生滅，名為異。生滅即空無異，名上如。今即約禪為下、中、上品，明觀門淺深之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, explaining and completing the mark of the factors of initial thought and sustained reflection: this itself has five parts. First, explaining the factor of initial thought — the sūtra, in speaking of the factor of initial thought, says: "Initial thought, great initial thought; deliberation, great deliberation; contemplating the nature of mind." With reference to these five phrases, the mark of initial thought is now clarified.

Here, the first two phrases, "initial thought, great initial thought," are first explained — these are distinguished with reference to the mundane and the world-transcending states, and so this twofold distinction of initial thought and great initial thought arises accordingly. The mundane state is precisely the mark of difference; the world-transcending state is precisely the mark of suchness. This very pair, suchness and difference, is itself simply another name for the two truths, ultimate and conventional. Discussed with reference to the contemplation-gate, this may be readily seen in its shallowness and depth. This shall now be distinguished in full accordance with the Mahāyāna treatise.

The treatise says: "There are three grades: upper, middle, and lower." Since suchness and difference are thus of three grades, initial thought and great initial thought, too, should correspondingly be of three grades. The treatise's own intent distinguishes: taking the provisional name as difference, and distinguishing the four elements' own real dharma as sharing a single substance, is named the lower grade of suchness; distinguishing the earth element as different from the other three elements is named difference. Recognizing them as alike impermanent, their arising and ceasing not different from one another, is named the middle grade of suchness; impermanent arising and ceasing itself is named difference. Recognizing that this very arising and ceasing is itself empty, without any difference at all, is named the upper grade of suchness. Here, then, with reference to dhyāna, the lower, middle, and upper grades are distinguished, clarifying the mark of the contemplation-gate's own shallowness and depth.

第一、先明下品覺相。覺氣息入出，青、黃、赤、白諸色隔別，名為覺；覺此諸息同一風大無異，名大覺。次覺三十六物隔別，名為覺；覺餘三大無有別異，名大覺。覺於心數非一，名為覺；同是四心無異，名大覺。第二、明中品覺者，息是風大，名為覺；覺息生滅無常，名大覺。覺餘三大各別，名為覺；覺同一無常，生滅不異，名大覺。覺四心差別不同，名為覺；覺無常生滅不異，名大覺。

First, clarifying beforehand the mark of the lower grade of initial thought: perceiving the breath entering and leaving, its various colors, blue, yellow, red, white, each distinct from the other, this is named initial thought; perceiving all these breaths as sharing, without difference, the single element of wind, this is named great initial thought. Next, perceiving the thirty-six substances as each distinct from the other, this is named initial thought; perceiving the remaining three elements as without any distinction between them, this is named great initial thought. Perceiving the mental factors as not of one kind only, this is named initial thought; perceiving them as, alike, these same four states of mind, without difference, this is named great initial thought.

Second, clarifying the mark of the middle grade of initial thought: recognizing the breath as the element of wind, this is named initial thought; perceiving the breath's own arising and ceasing as impermanent, this is named great initial thought. Perceiving the remaining three elements as each distinct, this is named initial thought; perceiving them as alike impermanent, their arising and ceasing not different from one another, this is named great initial thought. Perceiving the four states of mind as differing from one another, this is named initial thought; perceiving their impermanent arising and ceasing as not different from one another, this is named great initial thought.

第三、明上品覺者，覺息無常為異者，此息為八相所遷，故無常。何等為八相？一、生；二、住；三、異；四、滅；五、生生；六、住住；七、異異；八、滅滅。此八種相遷法體別異非一，名為覺；覺息本空寂，無八相之異，名大覺。覺餘三大各有八相別異，名為覺；覺餘三大本來空寂，無八相之異，名大覺。覺心八相所遷，別異非一，名為覺；覺心本來空寂，無八相之異，名大覺。

Third, clarifying the mark of the upper grade of initial thought: perceiving the breath's own impermanence as difference — this breath is shifted by eight marks, and so is impermanent. What are these eight marks? Arising; abiding; changing; ceasing; the arising of arising; the abiding of abiding; the changing of changing; and the ceasing of ceasing. These eight kinds of mark, shifting the dharma's own substance, distinct from one another, not of one kind only, this is named initial thought; perceiving the breath as originally empty and still, without any of these eight marks' own difference at all, this is named great initial thought.

Perceiving the remaining three elements as each having these eight marks' own distinction, this is named initial thought; perceiving the remaining three elements as originally empty and still, without any of these eight marks' own difference at all, this is named great initial thought. Perceiving the mind as shifted by these eight marks, distinct from one another, not of one kind only, this is named initial thought; perceiving the mind as originally empty and still, without any of these eight marks' own difference at all, this is named great initial thought.

所以者何？若心即是八相，八相即是心者，則壞有餘相。所以者何？今息色亦即是八相，八相亦即是息色，八相無異故，息、色、心三事亦應無異。若爾，說心時，即應是說息、色。今實不爾，壞亂世諦相故。如人喚火，應得水來。說心一向即是息、色，過同於此。復次，若離心有八相，離八相有心者，此則心非八相，八相非心。若心非八相，則心但有名無相，無相之法，是不名心。若八相離心，八相則無所遷，即不名八相，八相無所相故。如是審諦求之，則心與八相本自不有，亦不依他有性，性如虛空，無一異相，故名大覺。覺前息、色一一亦當如是分別。此則略說上品覺、大覺之相。

Why is this? Were the mind itself identical to these eight marks, and these eight marks identical to the mind, this would destroy what remains of their distinction. Why is this? Here, the breath and form, too, would then be identical to these eight marks, and these eight marks identical to the breath and form — and, the eight marks being without difference, the breath, form, and mind, these three matters, ought then to be without difference from one another as well. If this were so, then, whenever mind is spoken of, one ought equally to be speaking of breath and form — but this is, in truth, not so at all, since it would destroy the very mark of conventional truth. It is like calling for fire and expecting water to come instead. To say that mind is, without exception, identical to breath and form suffers from exactly this same fault.

Furthermore: were the eight marks present apart from mind, and mind present apart from the eight marks, then mind would not be the eight marks, and the eight marks would not be mind. If mind is not the eight marks, then mind would have only a name, without any mark at all, and a dharma without any mark cannot properly be named "mind" at all. If the eight marks are apart from mind, then the eight marks would have nothing to shift, and so could not properly be named "the eight marks" either, since the eight marks would then have nothing that they mark at all. Examined and sought after closely in this way, mind and the eight marks are, in themselves, originally without existence at all, nor do they possess any nature dependent on anything else either — their nature is like empty space itself, without any single mark of difference — hence this is named great initial thought. The breath and form already discussed should likewise, each in turn, be distinguished in this same way. This, in brief, describes the mark of the upper grade of initial thought and great initial thought.

Reading note: The eight marks of conditioned existence (arising, abiding, changing, ceasing, and each of these four applied a second time, to itself) is a classical Abhidharma scheme for how a moment of existence is not simply born and destroyed but shepherded through its own life-cycle by four further, second-order marks — here used to give the upper grade of initial thought a genuinely rigorous object. The refutation of "mind simply is the eight marks" versus "mind is entirely separate from the eight marks" is another instance of this treatise's recurring four-cornered logic, this time protecting a middle position: mind and its impermanence-marks are neither identical (which would collapse mind into breath and form, "calling for fire and expecting water") nor separate (which would leave both mind and the marks without any coherent referent at all), but empty of fixed existence in exactly the same way, "like empty space itself."

Next, explaining the two phrases "deliberation, great deliberation." These, too, are explained with reference to the initial thought and great initial thought already given. Why is this? The initial mind's own awakening to the mark of the ultimate and conventional is named initial thought and great initial thought; the later mind's own repeated deliberation and observation of this is named deliberation and great deliberation. Deliberation is spoken of following the lesser initial thought, and great deliberation following the great initial thought — this sense is readily seen, and need not be explained further here.

Next, explaining "contemplating the nature of mind": this is precisely turning the contemplation back upon the very mind that does this deliberating and great deliberating. Why is this? The practitioner, though able to fully understand the object before them, if unable to turn back and directly contemplate the contemplating mind itself, will fail to meet the true path. Here, then, one turns the contemplation back upon the very mind doing the observing: does it arise from a contemplating mind, or from a non-contemplating mind? Whether it arises from a contemplating mind or from a non-contemplating mind, both alternatives carry their own fault — one should know, then, that the contemplating mind is, in the end, empty and still. This concludes the five phrases explaining the factor of initial thought.

第二、次釋觀支。經云：「若觀心行、大行、遍行、隨意。」觀心者，即是前觀於心性也。行、大行者，聲聞之人以四諦為大行，當觀心時，即具四諦正觀。所以者何？若人不了心故無明，不了造諸結業，名為集諦。集諦因緣，必招未來名色苦果，是名苦諦。若觀心性，即是具足戒、定、智慧，行三十七品，故名道諦。若有正道，則現在煩惱不生，未來苦果亦滅，名為滅諦。是名聲聞大行。若緣覺人，以十二因緣為大行。若是菩薩，即入無生正道正觀，證於寂定瑠璃三昧，毛孔見佛，入菩薩位也。則略明三乘大行之道相也。

Second, next explaining the factor of sustained reflection. The sūtra says: "Observing the conduct of mind, great conduct, pervasive conduct, and conduct at will." "Observing mind" is precisely the same as "contemplating the nature of mind" already given above.

As for "conduct" and "great conduct": the śrāvaka takes the Four Noble Truths as great conduct — at the very moment mind is observed, one is already complete with the right contemplation of the Four Noble Truths. Why is this? If a person, failing to understand mind, thereby remains in ignorance, and, not understanding this, creates every kind of fettering karma, this is named the truth of accumulation. The causes and conditions of this accumulation are bound to bring about the future's own painful result of name-and-form — this is named the truth of suffering. Contemplating the nature of mind is itself already complete with moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom, practicing the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment — hence this is named the truth of the path. Where there is this right path, present affliction no longer arises, and the future's own painful result is thereby extinguished as well — this is named the truth of cessation. This is the śrāvaka's own great conduct. The pratyekabuddha, by contrast, takes the twelve links of dependent origination as great conduct. And the bodhisattva enters directly into the right path and right contemplation of the unarisen, realizing the still concentration of the lapis-lazuli samādhi, seeing the Buddha through every pore, entering the bodhisattva's own stage. This, in brief, clarifies the mark of the three vehicles' own path of great conduct.

遍行者，觀行未利，亦並約心而觀四諦，名為大行。今觀道稍利，能遍歷諸緣，觀於四諦，出十六行觀，故名遍行。隨意者，若是遍行，雖在定內，得見諸緣，出禪定時，則觀不相應。今隨意者，隨出入定，觀一切法，任運自成，不由作意，是名隨意。此則略釋觀支相也。

As for "pervasive conduct": when one's contemplative practice is not yet sharp, one still, alike, contemplates the Four Noble Truths with reference to mind alone — this is named great conduct. Here, once the path of contemplation has grown somewhat sharper, one is able to range pervasively through every condition, contemplating the Four Noble Truths, bringing forth the sixteen aspects of contemplation — hence this is named pervasive conduct. As for "conduct at will": within pervasive conduct, although one is able to see every condition while still within concentration, upon emerging from dhyāna-concentration, this contemplation no longer corresponds. Here, "at will" means that, whether entering or emerging from concentration, one contemplates every dharma whatsoever, spontaneously accomplished of its own accord, not dependent on any deliberate intention at all — this is named conduct at will. This, in brief, explains the mark of the factor of sustained reflection.

第三、明喜支。喜支者，經言：「如真實知、大知，心動至心，是名為喜。」如真實知者，即是上來觀於心性四諦真理也。大知者，如上觀行。若心審諦，停住緣內，稱觀而知，故言如真實知。若豁然開悟，稱理而知，心生法喜，故名大知。心動至心者，既得法喜心動，若隨此喜，則為顛倒。今了此喜無，即得喜性。即得喜性故，名至心，是名為喜。

Third, clarifying the factor of joy. The sūtra says: "Suchness-knowledge, great knowledge; the mind moved all the way to the very heart of mind itself — this is named joy." "Suchness-knowledge" is precisely the true principle of the Four Noble Truths within contemplating the nature of mind, already given above. "Great knowledge" is as within the contemplative practice already given above. If the mind closely examines and dwells within its object, knowing in accordance with contemplation, this is called suchness-knowledge. If one suddenly awakens, knowing in accordance with principle, the mind giving rise to the joy of the Dharma, this is named great knowledge. "The mind moved all the way to the very heart of mind itself" means that, having attained the joy of the Dharma, the mind is moved by it, and, were one to simply follow along with this joy, this would be a form of inversion. Here, understanding that this very joy has no fixed existence, one thereby attains joy's own true nature, and, having attained joy's own true nature, this is named "reaching the very heart of mind" — this is named joy.

第四、次明安支。安支者，經言：「若身安、心安、受安，受於樂觸，是名為安。」身安者，了達身性故，不為身業所動，即得身安，故名身安。心安者，了達心性故，不為心業所動，即得心樂，故名心安。受安者，能觀之心，名之為受，知受非受，斷諸受故，名之為樂，故名受安。受於樂觸者，世間、出世間二種樂法成就，樂法對心，故受於樂。第五、次明定支者，經言：「若心住、大住，不亂於緣，不謬，無有顛倒，是名為定。」心住者，住世間定法，持心不散，故名住。大住者，住真如定法，持心不散故。

Fourth, next clarifying the factor of ease. The sūtra says: "Ease of body, ease of mind, and ease of sensation, receiving a blissful touch — this is named ease." "Ease of body" means that, having fully understood the body's own nature, one is no longer moved by any bodily karma, and thereby attains bodily ease — hence this is named ease of body. "Ease of mind" means that, having fully understood the mind's own nature, one is no longer moved by any mental karma, and thereby attains mental happiness — hence this is named ease of mind. "Ease of sensation" means that the very mind able to observe is itself named "sensation" — knowing sensation as not truly "sensation" at all, severing every such sensation, this is named "happiness" — hence this is named ease of sensation. "Receiving a blissful touch" means that both the mundane and world-transcending kinds of blissful dharma have been accomplished, this blissful dharma meeting the mind directly — hence one receives this very bliss.

Fifth, next clarifying the factor of concentration. The sūtra says: "The mind abiding, greatly abiding, undisturbed by any object, unmistaken, free of any inversion whatsoever — this is named concentration." "The mind abiding" means abiding in the mundane dharma of concentration, sustaining the mind without scattering — hence this is named abiding. "Greatly abiding" means abiding in the dharma of concentration that is true suchness itself, sustaining the mind without scattering.

不亂於緣者，雖住一心，而分別世間之相不亂也。不謬者，謬名妄謬，諦了真如，妄取不起，故言不謬。不顛倒者，若心偏取世間相，即隨有見沈沒生死，不得解脫。若心偏取如相，即隨空見，破世間因果，不修善法，是大可畏處。行者善達真、俗，離此二種邪命，名不顛倒。復次，若二乘之人得此心，破四倒，名不顛倒。若是菩薩得此一心，能破八倒，名不顛倒。行者初得覺支成就，即覺身息不實，猶如芭蕉。今得住此一心，定支成就。心既寂靜，於後泯然微細，即覺身息之相不實，猶如聚沫。是則略明下根行者證通明初禪之相。

"Undisturbed by any object" means that, although abiding single-mindedly, one's discrimination of the mundane world's own marks remains undisturbed. "Unmistaken" means free of the fault of deluded mistake — closely understanding true suchness, deluded grasping no longer arises — hence this is called unmistaken. "Free of any inversion" means: were the mind to lean one-sidedly toward grasping the mundane world's own mark, one would then follow the view of existence, sinking into birth-and-death, unable to attain liberation; and were the mind to lean one-sidedly toward grasping the mark of suchness instead, one would then follow the view of emptiness, breaking apart the mundane world's own cause and effect, no longer cultivating any wholesome dharma at all — this is a place of great peril. The practitioner, properly penetrating both the ultimate and the conventional, leaving behind these two deviant modes of livelihood, this is named free of inversion. Furthermore: should a practitioner of the two lesser vehicles attain this very mind, breaking through the four inversions, this, too, is named free of inversion; and should a bodhisattva attain this same single mind, able to break through the eight inversions, this, too, is named free of inversion.

The practitioner, first attaining the accomplishment of the factor of initial thought, perceives the body and breath as unreal, just like a plantain trunk. Now, abiding in this very single-pointed mind, the factor of concentration, too, is accomplished. The mind now still, afterward growing indistinct and fine, one perceives the mark of body and breath as unreal, just like a mass of foam. This, in brief, clarifies the mark of a practitioner of lower faculty realizing the thorough-and-bright contemplation's first dhyāna.

Reading note: The five factors here map onto a familiar structure (initial thought, sustained reflection, joy, ease, concentration parallel the first dhyāna's own覺觀喜樂一心 from fascicle 5) but each is redefined around insight rather than raw absorption — sustained reflection becomes an explicit walk through the Four Noble Truths for each of the three vehicles, joy becomes the recognition that joy itself "has no fixed existence," and concentration's own "freedom from inversion" is calibrated by vehicle, four inversions broken for the two lesser vehicles and eight for the bodhisattva. This entire elaborate five-factor, eight-marks analysis, spanning several pages of source text, is explicitly said to describe only "a practitioner of lower faculty" — the treatise is about to walk through this same first dhyāna two more times, at increasing depth, for the middle and upper faculties.

If the mind practices the correct dharma, the multitude below all follow accordingly, and governance is thereby correct and at peace — hence the five organs are harmonized, the six repositories function smoothly, the four elements are at ease, free of every affliction and trouble, and one's own years and life are thereby preserved to their fullest. But if the mind practices what is not the dharma, then the assembled officials fall into disorder, harming one another in turn — hence the four elements grow unregulated, the various faculties darken and become obstructed, and, because of this, illness is contracted and life comes to its end — all of this arising from the mind's own practice of unwholesome dharma. Hence the sūtra says: "Losing the cloud-soul brings disorder; losing the animal-soul brings madness; losing intention brings confusion; losing the will brings forgetfulness; and losing the spirit brings death. One should know that the royal way of governance established outwardly is, in every case, simply this same dharma present within the body itself." Such senses as these are all given in full within the Sūtra of Trapusa.

Reading note: This political allegory of the body — mind as sovereign, organs as ministers of state, limbs as subjects, the whole apparatus of Han-dynasty bureaucratic cosmology (the Great Unity, the nine ministers, the altars of soil and grain) mapped directly onto physiology — completes the "world of meaning" the way the previous section's cosmology began it: the body is not only a small copy of the physical universe but a small copy of the political order as well. One should know, then, that the three realms, too, correspond to the five organs, their sense mutually interrelated.

Every kind of egg-birth is, for the most part, of the nature of the wind element, since the body is able to rise up lightly; every kind of moisture-birth is, for the most part, of the nature of the water element, since it arises through moisture; every kind of womb-birth belongs, for the most part, to the nature of the earth element, since its body is heavy and dull; and every kind of transformation-birth belongs, for the most part, to the nature of the fire element, since fire's own substance is absent and then suddenly present, and also possesses its own radiance.

The Tathāgata, in order to transform the three realms and the four kinds of birth, teaches the Four Noble Truths, the twelve links of dependent origination, and the six pāramitās — one should know that these three dharma-medicines, this very spirit-elixir, are, in every case, taught precisely as an antidote for sentient beings' own five organs, five faculties, and aggregates.

Why is this? As the Buddha teaches the sense of the Four Noble Truths within a single mind, one should know: the truth of accumulation corresponds to the liver, since its cause belongs to what first arises; the truth of suffering corresponds to the heart, since its result is what comes to completion; the truth of the path corresponds to the lungs, since metal is able to sever and cut through; and the truth of cessation corresponds to the kidneys, the dharma of winter's own storing away, what once existed now returned to nothing. This single mind already corresponds to the spleen, since it opens up and penetrates all Four Noble Truths at once. And, further still, the twelve links of dependent origination and the six pāramitās may likewise be understood by this same pattern.

These three kinds of dharma-treasury thereby broadly encompass every one of the Tathāgata's own teaching-gates — hence the practitioner, should the mind be bright and sharp, closely contemplating the bodily mark, thereby comes to understand the name and sense of the whole Buddhadharma directly. Hence the Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "One who clearly understands this very body has thereby penetrated everything whatsoever." This, then, explains the mark of how the internal world of meaning is interrelated with the world of meaning generally — its intent lies in the deeply hidden and subtle, not to be spoken of by anyone lacking realization.

第二、次釋成覺觀五支義。今先釋覺，亦為三義：一、下；二、中；三、上。下覺、大覺者，用天眼通徹見諸色分別，眾生種類非一，國土所有差別不同，名字亦異，故名為覺也。大覺者，即覺世間所有但假施設，諦觀四大，即不見有世間差別之異，了了分明，故名大覺。餘四通亦爾。第二、明次品覺、大覺者，用天眼通見四大色，即知其性各異，故名為覺。知四大無常生滅，性無差別，故名大覺。餘四通亦爾。第三、明上品覺、大覺者，用天眼通明見無常之法八相有異，是名為覺。覺知八相之法本來空寂，一相無相，故名大覺。餘四通亦爾。此則略說用五神通見事世間覺、大覺相。思惟、大思惟，觀於心性，成就覺支之相，類如前說，餘觀支、喜、安、定等，亦當如是一一分別。行者當知，若聲聞、緣覺得此禪故，依定獲得不壞解脫、無礙解脫、三明、六通，故名通明觀。若菩薩大士住此禪時，即得無礙大陀羅尼，乃至四禪亦復如是。

Second, next explaining how the five factors of initial thought and the rest come to completion. Here, initial thought is first explained, likewise having three senses: lower; middle; and upper.

Lower initial thought and great initial thought: through the heavenly eye's own power, seeing through and distinguishing every form directly, the kinds of sentient beings not being of a single sort, the features possessed by every land differing one from another, their very names likewise distinct — this is named initial thought. Great initial thought: perceiving that everything the world possesses is merely a provisional designation, closely contemplating the four elements, one thereby no longer sees any differentiated mark belonging to the world at all, this utterly clear and distinct — this is named great initial thought. The remaining four powers follow this same pattern.

Second, clarifying the middle grade of initial thought and great initial thought: through the heavenly eye's own power, seeing the form of the four elements, one thereby knows that their own natures each differ from one another — this is named initial thought. Knowing that the four elements are impermanent, arising and ceasing, their nature without any differentiation at all — this is named great initial thought. The remaining four powers follow this same pattern.

Third, clarifying the upper grade of initial thought and great initial thought: through the heavenly eye's own power, clearly seeing that the impermanent dharma bears the eight marks, each distinct from the rest — this is named initial thought. Perceiving that this very dharma of the eight marks is originally empty and still, a single mark that is no mark at all — this is named great initial thought. The remaining four powers follow this same pattern. This, then, briefly explains, through the five supernormal powers, the mark of initial thought and great initial thought within the world of witnessed phenomena. Deliberation and great deliberation, contemplating the nature of mind, and the accomplishment of the mark of the factor of initial thought all follow the same pattern as already given before; the remaining factors — sustained reflection, joy, ease, and concentration — should likewise, each in turn, be distinguished in this same way.

The practitioner should know: should a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha obtain this dhyāna, then, based upon this concentration, one obtains the indestructible liberation, the unobstructed liberation, the three insights, and the six supernormal powers — hence this is named the thorough-and-bright contemplation. Should a bodhisattva-mahāsattva abide in this dhyāna, one thereupon obtains the unobstructed great dhāraṇī — and, further still, this holds true all the way through the fourth dhyāna as well.

Reading note: The two roads to the five supernormal powers — arising spontaneously out of deepened samādhi, versus being cultivated deliberately through the breath-body-mind method — are given equal standing, and the quoted passage traces the same dharma-practicing monk (法行比丘) attaining each power in turn simply by continuing to watch the breath, all the way through the fourth dhyāna. The closing line is the payoff for the entire "thorough-and-bright contemplation" section: this is the dhyāna that gives śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas the three insights and six powers, and gives bodhisattvas the unobstructed great dhāraṇī — the same single method yielding a different fruit depending on the vehicle of the one who practices it.

The Second, Third, and Fourth Dhyānas 二禪 · 三禪 · 四禪

Chinese Source

次明二禪。自此已下，乃至非想滅定禪門，轉復深妙，事相非一，寧可具辯？今但別出經文，略釋正意而已。所言二禪者，經云：「二禪者，亦為名離，亦名為具。離者，同離五蓋；具者，具足三支，謂喜、安、定。」釋曰：行者於初禪後，心患初禪覺觀動散，攝心在定，不受覺觀，亦知上地不實，諦觀息、色、心三性，一心緣內，覺觀即滅，則發內淨大喜三昧，於定內見身如泡，具二禪行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying the second dhyāna. From here on, all the way through the dharma-gate of the concentration of cessation following neither-perception-nor-non-perception, the matter grows ever more deep and subtle, its phenomena no longer singular — how could it be treated in full? Here, only the sutra's own text shall be set out separately, its main sense explained in brief.

As for the second dhyāna, the sutra says: "The second dhyāna is likewise named departure, and likewise named completeness. Departure means departing equally from the five hindrances; completeness means being complete with three factors, namely joy, ease, and concentration." Explanation: the practitioner, after the first dhyāna, whose mind grows troubled by the first dhyāna's own initial thought and sustained reflection, agitated and scattered, gathers the mind within concentration, no longer accepting initial thought and sustained reflection, and also knows that the higher stage is not yet real. Closely contemplating the three natures of breath, form, and mind, the mind one-pointedly fixed within, initial thought and sustained reflection thereupon cease, and internal purity's own great joy-samādhi thereupon arises — within concentration, one sees the body as a bubble, complete with the second dhyāna's own practice.

次明三禪，經云：「三禪者，亦名為離，亦名為具。離者，同離五蓋；具者，具足五支，謂念、捨、慧、安、定。」釋曰：行者於二禪後，心患厭大喜，動散攝心不受，亦知上地不實，攝心諦觀，喜法即謝，發身樂，即於定內見身如雲，成三禪行。次明四禪相，經云：「四禪者，亦名為離，亦名為具。離者，謂同離五蓋；具者，具足四支，謂念、捨、不苦不樂、定。」釋曰：行者於三禪後，心厭患樂法，一心不受，亦知四禪非實，諦觀三性，即豁然明淨，三昧、智慧與捨俱發，心不依善，亦不附惡，正住其中，即於定內見身如影，具四禪行。

Next, clarifying the third dhyāna, the sutra says: "The third dhyāna is likewise named departure, and likewise named completeness. Departure means departing equally from the five hindrances; completeness means being complete with five factors, namely mindfulness, equanimity, wisdom, ease, and concentration." Explanation: the practitioner, after the second dhyāna, whose mind grows weary of the great joy, agitated and scattered, gathers the mind and no longer accepts it, and also knows that the higher stage is not yet real. Gathering the mind and closely contemplating, the dharma of joy thereupon withdraws, and bodily happiness arises — within concentration, one sees the body as a cloud, accomplishing the third dhyāna's own practice.

Next, clarifying the mark of the fourth dhyāna, the sutra says: "The fourth dhyāna is likewise named departure, and likewise named completeness. Departure means departing equally from the five hindrances; completeness means being complete with four factors, namely mindfulness, equanimity, neither-suffering-nor-happiness, and concentration." Explanation: the practitioner, after the third dhyāna, whose mind grows weary of the dharma of happiness, one-pointedly no longer accepting it, and also knows that the fourth dhyāna is not yet real. Closely contemplating the three natures, the mind thereupon opens out, bright and pure — samādhi and wisdom arise together with equanimity, the mind no longer relying upon good nor attaching to evil, abiding correctly within — within concentration, one sees the body as a shadow, complete with the fourth dhyāna's own practice.

Reading note: Having spent the entire chapter so far on the first dhyāna alone — root world, world of meaning, and world of witnessed phenomena all worked out in exhaustive detail — the text now moves quickly, treating the second through fourth dhyānas with a single sutra quotation and a brief gloss apiece. The four similes for the meditator's own body across the four dhyānas form a quiet, deliberate progression toward transparency: bubble, cloud, shadow — each stage's own self-image growing less substantial than the last, anticipating the formless attainments to come, where even the shadow is left behind.

The Four Formless Attainments 空處 · 識處 · 少識處 · 非想非非想

Chinese Source

次明空處，經言：「觀身厭患，遠離身相一切身觸、喜觸、樂觸，分別色相，遠離色陰，一心觀無量空處，是名比丘得空處定。」釋曰：此可為二義：一者、通觀上下；二者、但約自地及以上。通上下者，經「觀身厭患，遠離身相」者，深知欲界之身，過罪非一，身分皆不可得也。身等三觸，對初禪、二禪、三禪，對可見。「分別色相」者，分別欲界色身，乃至四禪色一一別異不實，亦知空處未離色法也。「遠離色陰」及「觀無量空處」者，並如前根本禪內滅三種色法，與虛空相應也。二、並約自地釋者，「觀身厭患，遠離身相」者，厭患如影之色覆蔽於心，觀此影色亦不可得也。「身等三觸」者，別喜根前已壞，此是四禪色起觸，心生三觸也。「分別色相」者，分別四禪喜樂及如影之色皆虛誑也。「遠離色陰」及「觀無量空處」，不異前說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying the sphere of infinite space, the sutra says: "Contemplating the body with weariness and aversion, growing distant from every mark of bodily contact — the touch of the body, the touch of joy, and the touch of happiness — distinguishing the mark of form, growing distant from the aggregate of form, and one-pointedly contemplating the sphere of infinite space: this is named a monk's attainment of the concentration of the sphere of space." Explanation: this may be given two senses — first, a comprehensive contemplation spanning both the lower and higher stages; second, a reading confined to one's own stage and above alone.

As for the comprehensive reading spanning both stages: the sutra's "contemplating the body with weariness and aversion, growing distant from the mark of the body" means deeply knowing that the body of the desire realm bears faults too numerous to count, and that no part of the body can be found at all. The three touches of the body and the rest correspond, respectively, to the first, second, and third dhyānas — this may be seen for itself. "Distinguishing the mark of form" means distinguishing that the form-body of the desire realm, and, further still, the form belonging to the fourth dhyāna, are each and every one distinct from one another and not real, and also knowing that the sphere of space has not yet departed from the dharma of form. "Growing distant from the aggregate of form" and "contemplating the sphere of infinite space" both correspond, as before, to the extinguishing of the three kinds of form-dharma within the root dhyānas already given above, in correspondence with empty space itself.

Second, as for the reading confined to one's own stage alone: "contemplating the body with weariness and aversion, growing distant from the mark of the body" means growing weary and averse to the shadow-like form that covers over the mind, contemplating that this shadow-like form, too, cannot be found at all. "The three touches of the body and the rest" means that, the faculty of joy having already been broken off earlier, this is the fourth dhyāna's own form giving rise to touch, the mind thereby generating these three kinds of touch. "Distinguishing the mark of form" means distinguishing that the fourth dhyāna's own joy and happiness, and the shadow-like form, are all empty and illusory. "Growing distant from the aggregate of form" and "contemplating the sphere of infinite space" do not differ from what was already explained above.

次明識處定相者，經言：「若有比丘修奢摩他、毘婆舍那，觀心、意、識，自知此身不受三受，以得遠離是三種受，是名比丘得識處定。」釋曰：「心、意、識」者，心者，即是捨空定，緣三性入識處定。行者用三昧攝智慧，雖知三性不實，為免空難，一心緣識，即入識處定也。「自知此身不受三受」者，緣色四句空處雖離初句，而猶受後三句。今識處緣識入定，則逈離色界四句，所有四受悉屬於識，故云自知此身不受三受，亦得言不受苦樂等三受也。已得遠離是三種受，名識處定相。

Next, clarifying the mark of the concentration of the sphere of consciousness, the sutra says: "Should a monk cultivate calming and insight, contemplating mind, intellect, and consciousness, he knows of himself that this body no longer accepts the three kinds of sensation, having thereby obtained distance from these three kinds of sensation: this is named a monk's attainment of the concentration of the sphere of consciousness." Explanation: as for "mind, intellect, and consciousness" — "mind" here means precisely relinquishing the concentration on emptiness, taking the three natures as its object, and entering the concentration of the sphere of consciousness. The practitioner, using samādhi to gather in wisdom, although knowing that the three natures are not real, in order to avoid the difficulty of emptiness, one-pointedly takes consciousness as the object and thereupon enters the concentration of the sphere of consciousness.

As for "knowing of himself that this body no longer accepts the three kinds of sensation": taking form as the object, the four propositions regarding the sphere of space, although departing from the first proposition, still accept the remaining three. Now, the sphere of consciousness takes consciousness as its object and enters concentration, thereby departing entirely from the four propositions of the form realm — the four kinds of sensation possessed all belong, instead, to consciousness — hence it is said that one knows of himself that this body no longer accepts the three kinds of sensation, which may also be said as no longer accepting the three sensations of suffering, happiness, and the rest. Having already obtained distance from these three kinds of sensation: this is the mark of the concentration of the sphere of consciousness.

次明少識處定相者，經言：「若有比丘觀三世空，知一切行亦生亦滅，空處、識處亦生亦滅。作是觀已，次第觀識，我今此識亦非識非非識。若非識者，是名寂靜，我今云何求斷此識？是名得少識處定。」釋曰：「觀三世空，一切諸行亦生亦滅」者，深觀自地及上下心數悉是有為之相，虛誑不實。「次第觀識」者，是觀識處亦識。「非識非非識」者，即通知所有法不可得也。「若非識者，是名寂靜，我今云何求斷此識」者，即是念滅識之方便，緣非識之法，入少識處定也。

Next, clarifying the mark of the concentration of the lesser sphere of consciousness, the sutra says: "Should a monk contemplate the emptiness of the three times, knowing that every conditioned formation both arises and ceases, and that the sphere of space and the sphere of consciousness likewise both arise and cease — having contemplated in this way, he next, in due order, contemplates consciousness itself: this consciousness of mine now is itself neither consciousness nor non-consciousness. If it is not consciousness, this is named stillness — why, then, should I now seek to sever this consciousness? This is named the attainment of the concentration of the lesser sphere of consciousness." Explanation: "contemplating the emptiness of the three times, every conditioned formation both arising and ceasing" means deeply contemplating that the mental factors of one's own stage, and of the stages both above and below, are, in every case, the mark of the conditioned — empty and illusory, not real. "In due order, contemplating consciousness" means contemplating that the sphere of consciousness, too, is itself consciousness. "Neither consciousness nor non-consciousness" means thoroughly knowing that no dharma whatsoever can be found. "If it is not consciousness, this is named stillness — why, then, should I now seek to sever this consciousness" is precisely the skillful means of extinguishing the thought of consciousness, taking the dharma of non-consciousness as the object, and thereby entering the concentration of the lesser sphere of consciousness.

次明非想定者，經言：「若有比丘有非心想，作是思惟：『我今此想是苦是漏，是瘡是癰，是不寂靜。若我能斷如是非想及非非想，是名寂靜。』若有比丘能斷如是非想非非想者，是名獲得無想解脫門。何以故？法行比丘作是思惟：『若有受想，若有識想，若有觸想，若有空想，若非想非非想，是等皆名麁想。我今若修無想三昧，則能永斷如是等想，是故見於非想非非想，為寂靜處。』如是見已，入非非想定已，不受不著，即破無明；破無明已，名獲阿羅漢果。」釋曰：「有非心想」者，即無想定也。「是苦是漏」等，即是觀無想定過罪也。若我能斷如是非想及非非想，是名寂靜。「非想」者，即是無想定也。「及非非想」者，已逆見上地之過，應斷除。「是寂靜」者，破非想定故，獲涅槃之寂靜也。「若有比丘能斷如是非想，獲得無想解脫門」者，一切三界之定，皆名為想。今斷此想，獲得無想三昧，即能於非想定破無明，發無漏，得阿羅漢果，證涅槃也。「法行比丘若有受想」已下，即是重釋出上意，義可見也。

Next, clarifying the concentration of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, the sutra says: "Should a monk possess the perception that is not-mind, he reflects thus: 'This perception of mine now is suffering, is defilement, is a wound, is an abscess, is not stillness itself. If I am able to sever both this not-perception and non-not-perception, this is named stillness.' Should a monk be able to sever this not-perception and non-not-perception, this is named the attainment of the liberation-gate of no-perception. Why is this? The dharma-practicing monk reflects thus: 'Whether there is the perception of sensation, or the perception of consciousness, or the perception of touch, or the perception of space, or the perception of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — all of these alike are named coarse perception. If I now cultivate the samādhi of no-perception, I will thereby be able to forever sever every perception of this kind — hence I see, with respect to neither-perception-nor-non-perception, that it is itself a place of stillness.' Having seen in this way, and having entered the concentration of non-not-perception, no longer accepting it and no longer attached to it, one thereupon breaks through ignorance; having broken through ignorance, this is named the attainment of the fruit of arhatship."

Explanation: "possessing the perception that is not-mind" means precisely the concentration of no-perception. "Is suffering, is defilement," and the rest, is precisely contemplating the faults of the concentration of no-perception. "If I am able to sever both this not-perception and non-not-perception, this is named stillness" — "not-perception" here means precisely the concentration of no-perception; "and non-not-perception" means that, having already anticipated the fault belonging to the higher stage, one ought to sever it as well; "this is stillness" means that, through breaking the concentration of not-perception, one obtains the stillness of nirvāṇa itself. "Should a monk be able to sever this not-perception, obtaining the liberation-gate of no-perception" means that every concentration belonging to the three realms is, in every case, named perception — now, severing this perception, one obtains the samādhi of no-perception, and is thereby able, within the concentration of not-perception, to break through ignorance, give rise to the undefiled, obtain the fruit of arhatship, and realize nirvāṇa itself. From "the dharma-practicing monk, whether there is the perception of sensation" onward, this is precisely a repeated explanation drawing out the sense already given above — its meaning may be seen for itself.

又經言：「前三種定，二道所斷；後第四定，終不可以世俗道斷。凡夫於非想處雖離麁煩惱，而亦具有十種細法。以其無麁煩惱故，一切凡夫謂是涅槃。」廣說如經。釋曰：此明凡夫等智於非想不能發無漏也。次經云「憍陳如！若比丘修習聖道，厭離四禪、四空處，觀於滅莊嚴之道」者，釋曰：此明通觀，於想後得入滅盡也。此義下背捨中當具說。行者入此法門，不取實際作證，具足大悲方便一切佛法，起六神通，度脫眾生，即是約一種法門明摩訶衍也。

The sutra further says: "The first three concentrations may be severed by either of the two paths; the fourth and final concentration, however, can never be severed by the mundane path alone. Although the ordinary person, within the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, has departed from the coarse afflictions, they nonetheless still possess, in full, the ten kinds of subtle dharmas. Because they lack the coarse afflictions, every ordinary person supposes this to be nirvāṇa itself." This is explained at length, as in the sutra. Explanation: this clarifies that the ordinary person's own worldly wisdom is, within the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, unable to give rise to the undefiled.

Next, the sutra says: "Kauṇḍinya! Should a monk cultivate and practice the noble path, growing weary of and departing from the four dhyānas and the four formless spheres, contemplating the path adorned with cessation —" Explanation: this clarifies the comprehensive contemplation, by which, after neither-perception-nor-non-perception, one is able to enter the attainment of cessation itself. This sense will be given in full below, within the section on the eight forms of detachment.

The practitioner who enters this dharma-gate, without grasping at the ultimate so as to make it one's own final proof, complete with great compassion's skillful means with respect to the whole Buddhadharma, gives rise to the six supernormal powers and delivers sentient beings across — this, then, is clarifying the Mahāyāna itself with reference to a single dharma-gate alone.

Reading note: The four formless attainments trace a single method — release the previous object, then discover the release itself needs releasing. Space replaces form; consciousness replaces space; then, in the "lesser sphere of consciousness," even consciousness turns out to be "neither consciousness nor non-consciousness"; and finally perception itself, having survived every prior renunciation, is recognized as still "coarse" and let go. The sutra's own closing distinction is worth marking: the first three formless attainments can be cut through by either the mundane or the world-transcending path, but the fourth — neither-perception-nor-non-perception — cannot be exhausted by mundane means alone, which is exactly why an ordinary person can rest there mistaking its stillness for nirvāṇa itself, "because they lack the coarse afflictions." The chapter's closing line reframes everything just covered as Mahāyāna practice: the bodhisattva who masters this dharma-gate does not stop at the "final proof" available within it, but turns the six supernormal powers outward, toward delivering others.
