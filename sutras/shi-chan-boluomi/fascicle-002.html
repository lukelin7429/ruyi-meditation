VI. Distinguishing the Preliminary Expedient Means of Dhyāna-Pāramitā 分別禪波羅蜜前方便第六之一

Fascicle 1 closed with a philosophical clearing of the ground — what dharma and mind even are, and why neither can be pinned down as a fixed thing. Fascicle 2 turns fully practical: this is the topic containing the treatise's celebrated twenty-five preliminary conditions (二十五方便), the concrete preparations a practitioner must have in place before dhyāna can even be attempted. It opens by naming the two broad categories of expedient means, then the five-times-five structure of the external ones, before diving into the first and, as it turns out, by far the most extensively treated: pure moral discipline.

External and Internal Expedient Means 外方便 · 內方便

Chinese Source

行人若能通達如前所辯五種，明諸禪相，則內信開發。若欲安心習學，必須善知方便。今明修禪方便，大開為二：一者、外方便，即是定外用心之法；二者、內方便，即是定內用心之法。此二通言方便者，善巧修學之異名。行者於初緣中善巧修習，故名方便。若細論，外方便亦有通定內用，內方便亦有得定外用。今一往從多為論，應如上分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a practitioner can penetrate the five categories just distinguished above, clarifying the marks of the various dhyānas, then inner faith is opened up. But to actually settle the mind and take up the practice, one must skillfully know the expedient means. Here, the expedient means for cultivating dhyāna are opened broadly into two: first, the external expedient means, which are methods of using the mind outside of concentration itself; second, the internal expedient means, which are methods of using the mind within concentration itself. Both are called, in general, "expedient means" — another name for skillful and well-crafted cultivation, since the practitioner cultivates skillfully amid the initial conditions. Examined closely, the external expedient means also extend into use within concentration, and the internal expedient means also extend into use gained outside concentration — but here, for the most part, the distinction is drawn broadly as above.

The Twenty-Five Preliminary Conditions, Named 外方便 · 二十五法

Chinese Source

就明外方便，自有五種：第一、具五緣；第二、訶五欲；第三、棄五蓋；第四、調五法；第五、行五法。此五五凡有二十五法，並是未得禪時初修心方便之相。第一、具五緣者：一、持戒清淨；二、衣食具足；三、閑居靜處；四、息諸緣務；五、得善知識。此是修禪五緣也。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for clarifying the external expedient means, these are themselves of five kinds: first, being complete in the five conditions; second, rejecting the five desires; third, abandoning the five hindrances; fourth, harmonizing the five faculties; fifth, practicing the five dharmas. These five sets of five make twenty-five dharmas in all, together forming the mark of the mind's initial cultivation through expedient means, prior to attaining dhyāna.

First, being complete in the five conditions: first, pure moral discipline; second, being complete in robes and food; third, dwelling in a quiet, secluded place; fourth, setting worldly affairs and involvements to rest; fifth, obtaining a good spiritual friend. These are the five conditions for cultivating dhyāna.

Reading note: This is the treatise's own table of contents for the famous "twenty-five expedient means" — five groups of five, each group its own topic. This fascicle begins with the first group, the five conditions, and within that group, its first and by far largest member: pure moral discipline, which alone will occupy the rest of what is translated here. The remaining four conditions, and the four further groups (rejecting the five desires, abandoning the five hindrances, harmonizing the five faculties, and practicing the five dharmas), are not yet reached in translation.

Pure Moral Discipline, and Ten Ways Precepts Are Received 持戒清淨 · 明有戒無戒（一）

Chinese Source

第一、持戒清淨者，開為三意：一、明有戒、無戒；二、明持犯；三、明懺悔。第一、明有戒、無戒者。出家受得禁戒，故名有戒，即為三意：一、明發戒機緣不同，凡有十種。何等為十？

Ru-Yi Translation

First, pure moral discipline opens into three parts: first, clarifying the presence or absence of precepts; second, clarifying upholding and violation; third, clarifying repentance.

First, clarifying the presence or absence of precepts. One who has gone forth from home and received the restraining precepts is called one who has precepts; this too has three parts: first, clarifying the different occasions on which precepts are brought forth, of which there are, in all, ten kinds. What are these ten?

一、自然得戒，即佛是其人，無師自發。二、自誓得戒，即迦葉，是本辟支根性，值佛出世，墮聲聞數中。其答佛言：「佛是我師，我是佛弟子。」作是言已，即便發戒。三、見諦得戒，即拘隣五人，佛為轉四諦法輪，即悟初果，因而發戒。四者、三歸得戒，于時未有羯磨，聞佛三說，即發戒品，以其根利故。五、八敬得戒，即佛姨母。佛意不欲度女人出家，姨母苦求，佛令遙授八敬，即發具戒。

First, receiving precepts spontaneously — this refers to the Buddha himself, who, without a teacher, brought forth the precepts of himself. Second, receiving precepts through one's own vow — this refers to Kāśyapa, who was originally of pratyekabuddha capacity; when he encountered the Buddha's appearance in the world, he fell among the ranks of the śrāvakas. He answered the Buddha, saying: "The Buddha is my teacher; I am the Buddha's disciple." Having spoken these words, the precepts were thereupon brought forth in him. Third, receiving precepts through the seeing of the truths — this refers to the group of five including Kauṇḍinya; when the Buddha turned the wheel of the Dharma on the Four Noble Truths for them, they awakened at once to the first fruit, and through this the precepts were brought forth. Fourth, receiving precepts through the three refuges — at that time the formal procedure did not yet exist; upon hearing the Buddha state the three refuges three times, the precepts were brought forth at once, owing to their sharp faculties. Fifth, receiving precepts through the eight special rules of respect — this refers to the Buddha's own aunt. The Buddha's own intention was not to let women go forth from home, but when his aunt earnestly and persistently entreated him, the Buddha had the eight special rules of respect conferred on her from a distance, and the full precepts were thereupon brought forth in her.

六者、論議得戒，即須陀耶沙彌與佛論義。佛問其無常等義，事事能答。後佛問：「汝家在何處？」答佛言：「三界皆空，世尊云何乃問我家處？」佛語阿難：「將還僧中，為受具戒。」于時年始七歲。七者、善來得戒。道機時熟，佛呼：「善來！」即便得戒。八者、遣使得戒，即半迦尸女，有好善容，評堪半迦尸國，為人欲抄斷故，令遣使僧中代受戒，後還尼寺為其受戒。九、邊地如法人少，聽五人受得戒。十者、中國人多，十人受具戒。此為十種得戒相。今時多用十人羯磨得戒。此辯有戒相。

Sixth, receiving precepts through debate — this refers to the novice Sudāya, who debated doctrine with the Buddha. The Buddha questioned him on the meaning of impermanence and the like, and he was able to answer every point. Afterward the Buddha asked him: "Where is your home?" He answered the Buddha, saying: "The three realms are all empty — World-Honored One, why do you still ask me where my home is?" The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Take him back among the assembly, and let him receive the full precepts." At that time he was only seven years old. Seventh, receiving precepts through the words "welcome, come forth" — when the occasion for realizing the path had ripened, the Buddha called out, "Welcome!" and the precepts were thereupon received. Eighth, receiving precepts through a sent messenger — this refers to a woman of surpassing, virtuous beauty, judged fit to be queen of her country; because someone wished to abduct and seize her, she was allowed to receive the precepts by proxy through a messenger sent among the assembly, and only later returned to a convent to formally receive them in person. Ninth, in border regions where those versed in the Dharma are few, it is permitted that five people alone may confer the precepts. Tenth, in the central lands, where people are numerous, ten people confer the full precepts. These are the ten marks of receiving the precepts. In the present age, the formal procedure with ten people is most commonly used to confer the precepts. This concludes the distinguishing of the mark of having precepts.

Reading note: These ten are classical Vinaya precedents — the recognized exceptions and special cases in the history of how ordination was actually conferred, cited here to establish that "having precepts" was never a single uniform procedure even in the earliest sangha. Most are famous: Kāśyapa's declaration of discipleship, the group of five ascetics awakening at the first turning of the Dharma-wheel, the Buddha's aunt Mahāprajāpatī's ordination (the origin of the bhikṣuṇī order), the seven-year-old novice Sudāya's precocious debate. The eighth case — the unnamed woman ordained by proxy for her own safety — is a Vinaya precedent whose exact identity we have not been able to confirm with certainty; we render it cautiously rather than assert a specific name.

What the Precepts Actually Are 持戒清淨 · 明有戒無戒（二）· 戒體

Chinese Source

第二、正明戒之體相者，有二種教門不同：若小乘教，辯戒是無作善法，受戒因緣具足，若發得無作戒，爾後睡眠入定，此善任運自生，不須身口意造作，以無作正為戒體。若薩婆多人，解無作戒，是無表色，不可見無對。若曇無德人，明無作戒，是第三聚，非色非心法。諸部既異，雖不可偏執，約小乘教門，終是無作為戒體，其義不差。若大乘教門中，說戒從心起，即以善心為戒體。此義如《纓絡經》說。有師言：摩訶僧祇部人云：「無作戒是心法。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, rightly clarifying the essential mark of the precepts: there are two differing gates of teaching. According to the Hīnayāna teaching, the precepts are explained as a wholesome dharma of non-action: once the causes and conditions for receiving the precepts are complete, if the precept of non-action is brought forth, then afterward, even in sleep or in concentration, this wholesome quality arises spontaneously of itself, requiring no further deliberate act of body, speech, or mind — it is this non-action that is taken as the very essence of the precepts. According to the Sarvāstivādins, the precept of non-action is explained as unmanifest form — invisible and without resistance. According to the Dharmaguptakas, the precept of non-action is explained as belonging to a third category of dharma, neither form nor mind. Since the various schools differ, one should not cling one-sidedly to any single view — but according to the Hīnayāna gate of teaching in general, the essence of the precepts is, in the end, non-action, and on this point their meaning does not differ.

According to the Mahāyāna gate of teaching, the precepts are said to arise from the mind, and it is the wholesome mind itself that is taken as the essence of the precepts. This is the meaning explained in the Yingluo Sūtra. One master states that, according to the Mahāsāṃghikas, "the precept of non-action is itself a mental dharma."

Reading note: This is a real doctrinal fault line, not a minor technicality: what actually makes someone "a person who holds precepts" between the moments they consciously choose to act well? The Hīnayāna answer is a peculiar category, "non-action" (無作, avijñapti) — something brought into being at ordination that then keeps functioning on its own, even in sleep, without any further mental effort — and the schools disagree sharply about what kind of thing this "non-action" even is (a subtle, invisible form? a third category, neither mind nor matter?). The Mahāyāna side sidesteps the puzzle differently: the precepts simply are the wholesome mind itself, not some separate entity the mind produces. Zhiyi records the disagreement without forcing a resolution.

Precept-Holding across the Seven Assemblies 持戒清淨 · 明有戒無戒（三）

Chinese Source

第三、明有戒相不同，即有二意：一者、若約小乘，七眾發心，受戒作法不同，故得戒亦有優劣，如優婆塞、優婆夷，在家有五戒相。若本未入佛法男子、女人，不殺父害母，不作逆罪，遇好良師，教歸依三寶，為受五戒。作法成就，即五戒無作起，名得五戒，從此名清信士、女。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying that the mark of having precepts is not uniform: this has two aspects. First, according to the Hīnayāna: among the seven assemblies, the resolve with which precepts are taken up, and the formal procedures for receiving them, all differ — hence the precepts received also differ in degree. Take, for instance, the upāsaka and upāsikā, the lay man and lay woman, who hold the five precepts while remaining at home: if a man or woman who has not yet entered the Buddhadharma has not killed their father or mother, has not committed any of the five heinous offenses, and, encountering a good and virtuous teacher, is taught to take refuge in the Three Jewels and receive the five precepts — once the formal procedure is accomplished, the non-action of the five precepts is brought forth, and this is called receiving the five precepts; from this point, such a person is called a pure believing man or woman.

復次，明沙彌有十戒相。若和尚、阿闍梨二師如法受人清淨歸依三寶，隨佛出家，若二師作法成就，即發無作，名得沙彌戒。次明大僧有戒相。若作沙彌時不犯重過，清淨十師和尚、阿闍梨作羯磨，如法成就，是名得大比丘具足戒。若沙彌尼、式叉摩尼、大戒尼有戒相亦爾。七人本雖犯重，若遇良緣，謂作大乘方等懺悔，得相成就，後受戒亦得無作善發。異於上說，名不得戒，亦名無戒。

Next, clarifying that the śrāmaṇera holds ten precepts: if the two preceptors — the ordaining master and the instructing teacher — properly receive a person who purely takes refuge in the Three Jewels and follows the Buddha in going forth from home, and if the two preceptors carry out the formal procedure to completion, then the non-action is brought forth, and this is called receiving the precepts of a śrāmaṇera. Next, clarifying the mark of a full monk's precepts: if, during the time as a śrāmaṇera, no grave offense was committed, and ten pure preceptors — the ordaining master and the instructing teachers — carry out the formal procedure lawfully to completion, this is called receiving the full precepts of a great bhikṣu. The same holds for the śrāmaṇerī, the śikṣamāṇā, and the fully ordained bhikṣuṇī.

As for these seven kinds of persons: even one who originally committed a grave offense, if they encounter a good condition — namely, undergoing the Mahāyāna's extensive repentance practice — and this is brought to completion, may, on later receiving the precepts, still have the non-action wholesomely brought forth in them. Different from what has just been described, without such repentance, this is called not receiving the precepts, also called having no precepts.

二者、若菩薩行人有戒、無戒則不可知。所以者何？菩薩世世已來，或初發心時，值遇良緣，受得戒故。

Second, as for the bodhisattva-practitioner, whether they have precepts or not cannot be known from outward form alone. Why is this? Because the bodhisattva, life after life, from the very moment the mind first arises, may already have encountered a good condition and received the precepts.

Reading note: The "seven assemblies" (七眾) are the seven traditional categories of Buddhist practitioner by ordination status: lay man and lay woman (upāsaka, upāsikā), male and female novice (śrāmaṇera, śrāmaṇerī), the female probationer (śikṣamāṇā), and fully ordained monk and nun (bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī). What's notable here is the closing move: even someone who broke a grave precept is not permanently barred — the Mahāyāna's own extensive repentance practice can restore the ground for genuinely receiving the precepts afterward. And the bodhisattva path escapes the whole framework of outward verification: since a bodhisattva's vow may span countless past lives, whether they "have precepts" in this technical, procedural sense is simply not something an outside observer can determine. This closes the first of condition 1's three parts (有戒無戒). Upholding and violation (持犯), and repentance (懺悔), are not yet reached in translation.
