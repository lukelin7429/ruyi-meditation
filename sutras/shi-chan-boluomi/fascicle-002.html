VI. Distinguishing the Preliminary Expedient Means of Dhyāna-Pāramitā 分別禪波羅蜜前方便第六之一

Fascicle 1 closed with a philosophical clearing of the ground — what dharma and mind even are, and why neither can be pinned down as a fixed thing. Fascicle 2 turns fully practical: this is the topic containing the treatise's celebrated twenty-five preliminary conditions (二十五方便), the concrete preparations a practitioner must have in place before dhyāna can even be attempted. It opens by naming the two broad categories of expedient means, then the five-times-five structure of the external ones, before diving into the first and, as it turns out, by far the most extensively treated: pure moral discipline.

External and Internal Expedient Means 外方便 · 內方便

Chinese Source

行人若能通達如前所辯五種，明諸禪相，則內信開發。若欲安心習學，必須善知方便。今明修禪方便，大開為二：一者、外方便，即是定外用心之法；二者、內方便，即是定內用心之法。此二通言方便者，善巧修學之異名。行者於初緣中善巧修習，故名方便。若細論，外方便亦有通定內用，內方便亦有得定外用。今一往從多為論，應如上分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a practitioner can penetrate the five categories just distinguished above, clarifying the marks of the various dhyānas, then inner faith is opened up. But to actually settle the mind and take up the practice, one must skillfully know the expedient means. Here, the expedient means for cultivating dhyāna are opened broadly into two: first, the external expedient means, which are methods of using the mind outside of concentration itself; second, the internal expedient means, which are methods of using the mind within concentration itself. Both are called, in general, "expedient means" — another name for skillful and well-crafted cultivation, since the practitioner cultivates skillfully amid the initial conditions. Examined closely, the external expedient means also extend into use within concentration, and the internal expedient means also extend into use gained outside concentration — but here, for the most part, the distinction is drawn broadly as above.

The Twenty-Five Preliminary Conditions, Named 外方便 · 二十五法

Chinese Source

就明外方便，自有五種：第一、具五緣；第二、訶五欲；第三、棄五蓋；第四、調五法；第五、行五法。此五五凡有二十五法，並是未得禪時初修心方便之相。第一、具五緣者：一、持戒清淨；二、衣食具足；三、閑居靜處；四、息諸緣務；五、得善知識。此是修禪五緣也。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for clarifying the external expedient means, these are themselves of five kinds: first, being complete in the five conditions; second, rejecting the five desires; third, abandoning the five hindrances; fourth, harmonizing the five faculties; fifth, practicing the five dharmas. These five sets of five make twenty-five dharmas in all, together forming the mark of the mind's initial cultivation through expedient means, prior to attaining dhyāna.

First, being complete in the five conditions: first, pure moral discipline; second, being complete in robes and food; third, dwelling in a quiet, secluded place; fourth, setting worldly affairs and involvements to rest; fifth, obtaining a good spiritual friend. These are the five conditions for cultivating dhyāna.

Reading note: This is the treatise's own table of contents for the famous "twenty-five expedient means" — five groups of five, each group its own topic. This fascicle begins with the first group, the five conditions, and within that group, its first and by far largest member: pure moral discipline, which alone will occupy the rest of what is translated here. The remaining four conditions, and the four further groups (rejecting the five desires, abandoning the five hindrances, harmonizing the five faculties, and practicing the five dharmas), are not yet reached in translation.

Pure Moral Discipline, and Ten Ways Precepts Are Received 持戒清淨 · 明有戒無戒（一）

Chinese Source

第一、持戒清淨者，開為三意：一、明有戒、無戒；二、明持犯；三、明懺悔。第一、明有戒、無戒者。出家受得禁戒，故名有戒，即為三意：一、明發戒機緣不同，凡有十種。何等為十？

Ru-Yi Translation

First, pure moral discipline opens into three parts: first, clarifying the presence or absence of precepts; second, clarifying upholding and violation; third, clarifying repentance.

First, clarifying the presence or absence of precepts. One who has gone forth from home and received the restraining precepts is called one who has precepts; this too has three parts: first, clarifying the different occasions on which precepts are brought forth, of which there are, in all, ten kinds. What are these ten?

一、自然得戒，即佛是其人，無師自發。二、自誓得戒，即迦葉，是本辟支根性，值佛出世，墮聲聞數中。其答佛言：「佛是我師，我是佛弟子。」作是言已，即便發戒。三、見諦得戒，即拘隣五人，佛為轉四諦法輪，即悟初果，因而發戒。四者、三歸得戒，于時未有羯磨，聞佛三說，即發戒品，以其根利故。五、八敬得戒，即佛姨母。佛意不欲度女人出家，姨母苦求，佛令遙授八敬，即發具戒。

First, receiving precepts spontaneously — this refers to the Buddha himself, who, without a teacher, brought forth the precepts of himself. Second, receiving precepts through one's own vow — this refers to Kāśyapa, who was originally of pratyekabuddha capacity; when he encountered the Buddha's appearance in the world, he fell among the ranks of the śrāvakas. He answered the Buddha, saying: "The Buddha is my teacher; I am the Buddha's disciple." Having spoken these words, the precepts were thereupon brought forth in him. Third, receiving precepts through the seeing of the truths — this refers to the group of five including Kauṇḍinya; when the Buddha turned the wheel of the Dharma on the Four Noble Truths for them, they awakened at once to the first fruit, and through this the precepts were brought forth. Fourth, receiving precepts through the three refuges — at that time the formal procedure did not yet exist; upon hearing the Buddha state the three refuges three times, the precepts were brought forth at once, owing to their sharp faculties. Fifth, receiving precepts through the eight special rules of respect — this refers to the Buddha's own aunt. The Buddha's own intention was not to let women go forth from home, but when his aunt earnestly and persistently entreated him, the Buddha had the eight special rules of respect conferred on her from a distance, and the full precepts were thereupon brought forth in her.

六者、論議得戒，即須陀耶沙彌與佛論義。佛問其無常等義，事事能答。後佛問：「汝家在何處？」答佛言：「三界皆空，世尊云何乃問我家處？」佛語阿難：「將還僧中，為受具戒。」于時年始七歲。七者、善來得戒。道機時熟，佛呼：「善來！」即便得戒。八者、遣使得戒，即半迦尸女，有好善容，評堪半迦尸國，為人欲抄斷故，令遣使僧中代受戒，後還尼寺為其受戒。九、邊地如法人少，聽五人受得戒。十者、中國人多，十人受具戒。此為十種得戒相。今時多用十人羯磨得戒。此辯有戒相。

Sixth, receiving precepts through debate — this refers to the novice Sudāya, who debated doctrine with the Buddha. The Buddha questioned him on the meaning of impermanence and the like, and he was able to answer every point. Afterward the Buddha asked him: "Where is your home?" He answered the Buddha, saying: "The three realms are all empty — World-Honored One, why do you still ask me where my home is?" The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Take him back among the assembly, and let him receive the full precepts." At that time he was only seven years old. Seventh, receiving precepts through the words "welcome, come forth" — when the occasion for realizing the path had ripened, the Buddha called out, "Welcome!" and the precepts were thereupon received. Eighth, receiving precepts through a sent messenger — this refers to a woman of surpassing, virtuous beauty, judged fit to be queen of her country; because someone wished to abduct and seize her, she was allowed to receive the precepts by proxy through a messenger sent among the assembly, and only later returned to a convent to formally receive them in person. Ninth, in border regions where those versed in the Dharma are few, it is permitted that five people alone may confer the precepts. Tenth, in the central lands, where people are numerous, ten people confer the full precepts. These are the ten marks of receiving the precepts. In the present age, the formal procedure with ten people is most commonly used to confer the precepts. This concludes the distinguishing of the mark of having precepts.

Reading note: These ten are classical Vinaya precedents — the recognized exceptions and special cases in the history of how ordination was actually conferred, cited here to establish that "having precepts" was never a single uniform procedure even in the earliest sangha. Most are famous: Kāśyapa's declaration of discipleship, the group of five ascetics awakening at the first turning of the Dharma-wheel, the Buddha's aunt Mahāprajāpatī's ordination (the origin of the bhikṣuṇī order), the seven-year-old novice Sudāya's precocious debate. The eighth case — the unnamed woman ordained by proxy for her own safety — is a Vinaya precedent whose exact identity we have not been able to confirm with certainty; we render it cautiously rather than assert a specific name.

What the Precepts Actually Are 持戒清淨 · 明有戒無戒（二）· 戒體

Chinese Source

第二、正明戒之體相者，有二種教門不同：若小乘教，辯戒是無作善法，受戒因緣具足，若發得無作戒，爾後睡眠入定，此善任運自生，不須身口意造作，以無作正為戒體。若薩婆多人，解無作戒，是無表色，不可見無對。若曇無德人，明無作戒，是第三聚，非色非心法。諸部既異，雖不可偏執，約小乘教門，終是無作為戒體，其義不差。若大乘教門中，說戒從心起，即以善心為戒體。此義如《纓絡經》說。有師言：摩訶僧祇部人云：「無作戒是心法。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, rightly clarifying the essential mark of the precepts: there are two differing gates of teaching. According to the Hīnayāna teaching, the precepts are explained as a wholesome dharma of non-action: once the causes and conditions for receiving the precepts are complete, if the precept of non-action is brought forth, then afterward, even in sleep or in concentration, this wholesome quality arises spontaneously of itself, requiring no further deliberate act of body, speech, or mind — it is this non-action that is taken as the very essence of the precepts. According to the Sarvāstivādins, the precept of non-action is explained as unmanifest form — invisible and without resistance. According to the Dharmaguptakas, the precept of non-action is explained as belonging to a third category of dharma, neither form nor mind. Since the various schools differ, one should not cling one-sidedly to any single view — but according to the Hīnayāna gate of teaching in general, the essence of the precepts is, in the end, non-action, and on this point their meaning does not differ.

According to the Mahāyāna gate of teaching, the precepts are said to arise from the mind, and it is the wholesome mind itself that is taken as the essence of the precepts. This is the meaning explained in the Yingluo Sūtra. One master states that, according to the Mahāsāṃghikas, "the precept of non-action is itself a mental dharma."

Reading note: This is a real doctrinal fault line, not a minor technicality: what actually makes someone "a person who holds precepts" between the moments they consciously choose to act well? The Hīnayāna answer is a peculiar category, "non-action" (無作, avijñapti) — something brought into being at ordination that then keeps functioning on its own, even in sleep, without any further mental effort — and the schools disagree sharply about what kind of thing this "non-action" even is (a subtle, invisible form? a third category, neither mind nor matter?). The Mahāyāna side sidesteps the puzzle differently: the precepts simply are the wholesome mind itself, not some separate entity the mind produces. Zhiyi records the disagreement without forcing a resolution.

Precept-Holding across the Seven Assemblies 持戒清淨 · 明有戒無戒（三）

Chinese Source

第三、明有戒相不同，即有二意：一者、若約小乘，七眾發心，受戒作法不同，故得戒亦有優劣，如優婆塞、優婆夷，在家有五戒相。若本未入佛法男子、女人，不殺父害母，不作逆罪，遇好良師，教歸依三寶，為受五戒。作法成就，即五戒無作起，名得五戒，從此名清信士、女。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying that the mark of having precepts is not uniform: this has two aspects. First, according to the Hīnayāna: among the seven assemblies, the resolve with which precepts are taken up, and the formal procedures for receiving them, all differ — hence the precepts received also differ in degree. Take, for instance, the upāsaka and upāsikā, the lay man and lay woman, who hold the five precepts while remaining at home: if a man or woman who has not yet entered the Buddhadharma has not killed their father or mother, has not committed any of the five heinous offenses, and, encountering a good and virtuous teacher, is taught to take refuge in the Three Jewels and receive the five precepts — once the formal procedure is accomplished, the non-action of the five precepts is brought forth, and this is called receiving the five precepts; from this point, such a person is called a pure believing man or woman.

復次，明沙彌有十戒相。若和尚、阿闍梨二師如法受人清淨歸依三寶，隨佛出家，若二師作法成就，即發無作，名得沙彌戒。次明大僧有戒相。若作沙彌時不犯重過，清淨十師和尚、阿闍梨作羯磨，如法成就，是名得大比丘具足戒。若沙彌尼、式叉摩尼、大戒尼有戒相亦爾。七人本雖犯重，若遇良緣，謂作大乘方等懺悔，得相成就，後受戒亦得無作善發。異於上說，名不得戒，亦名無戒。

Next, clarifying that the śrāmaṇera holds ten precepts: if the two preceptors — the ordaining master and the instructing teacher — properly receive a person who purely takes refuge in the Three Jewels and follows the Buddha in going forth from home, and if the two preceptors carry out the formal procedure to completion, then the non-action is brought forth, and this is called receiving the precepts of a śrāmaṇera. Next, clarifying the mark of a full monk's precepts: if, during the time as a śrāmaṇera, no grave offense was committed, and ten pure preceptors — the ordaining master and the instructing teachers — carry out the formal procedure lawfully to completion, this is called receiving the full precepts of a great bhikṣu. The same holds for the śrāmaṇerī, the śikṣamāṇā, and the fully ordained bhikṣuṇī.

As for these seven kinds of persons: even one who originally committed a grave offense, if they encounter a good condition — namely, undergoing the Mahāyāna's extensive repentance practice — and this is brought to completion, may, on later receiving the precepts, still have the non-action wholesomely brought forth in them. Different from what has just been described, without such repentance, this is called not receiving the precepts, also called having no precepts.

二者、若菩薩行人有戒、無戒則不可知。所以者何？菩薩世世已來，或初發心時，值遇良緣，受得戒故。

Second, as for the bodhisattva-practitioner, whether they have precepts or not cannot be known from outward form alone. Why is this? Because the bodhisattva, life after life, from the very moment the mind first arises, may already have encountered a good condition and received the precepts.

Reading note: The "seven assemblies" (七眾) are the seven traditional categories of Buddhist practitioner by ordination status: lay man and lay woman (upāsaka, upāsikā), male and female novice (śrāmaṇera, śrāmaṇerī), the female probationer (śikṣamāṇā), and fully ordained monk and nun (bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī). What's notable here is the closing move: even someone who broke a grave precept is not permanently barred — the Mahāyāna's own extensive repentance practice can restore the ground for genuinely receiving the precepts afterward. And the bodhisattva path escapes the whole framework of outward verification: since a bodhisattva's vow may span countless past lives, whether they "have precepts" in this technical, procedural sense is simply not something an outside observer can determine. This closes the first of condition 1's three parts (有戒無戒).

Upholding and Violation 持戒清淨 · 明持犯（一）

Chinese Source

第二、明持犯，自有三意：一、略明持犯；二、歷別廣明持犯；三、明覆發。就初總明持犯，有二：一者、持相；二者、犯相。一、持相者，持者，護持。如上所說七種之人受佛禁戒，為十利故，護持無犯。十利者，如毘尼中說：「一、攝僧故；二、極好攝故；三、僧安樂住故；四、折伏高心人故；五、慚愧得安樂住故；六、不信令信故；七、已信增長信故；八、遮今世漏故；九、斷後世惡故；十、令梵行人久住故。」行者一心敬慎，不敢侵毀，如護浮囊，微塵不棄，故名護持，亦名秉持，如持油鉢之喻，是名持相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying upholding and violation: this has three parts in itself — first, briefly clarifying upholding and violation; second, extensively clarifying upholding and violation through each successive stage; third, clarifying concealment and manifestation. As to the first, clarifying upholding and violation as a whole, there are two: first, the mark of upholding; second, the mark of violation.

First, the mark of upholding: "upholding" means guarding and maintaining. As stated above, the seven kinds of persons who receive the Buddha's restraining precepts guard and maintain them without violation for the sake of ten benefits. These ten benefits, as the Vinaya states, are: "First, so as to gather in the saṅgha; second, so as to gather it in most excellently; third, so that the saṅgha may dwell at ease and in joy; fourth, so as to subdue those of arrogant mind; fifth, so that those with a sense of shame may dwell at ease; sixth, so as to bring faith to those without it; seventh, so as to increase the faith of those who already have it; eighth, so as to check the contaminants of the present life; ninth, so as to sever the unwholesome deeds of lives to come; tenth, so that those of pure conduct may long abide." The practitioner, single-mindedly reverent and cautious, dares not encroach upon or damage the precepts — like guarding an inflated float used to cross water, not discarding it even to the smallest degree — hence this is called guarding and maintaining, also called firmly upholding, as in the simile of carrying a bowl brimful of oil. This is the mark of upholding.

二、明犯相者，犯名違犯。本受佛戒，欲出生死，願求解脫，今遇惡緣，不能自制其心，中途違返，若重若輕，故名違犯。復次，犯名犯觸。猶如服藥，誡忌斷食，不隨醫教，而食惡食，犯觸藥勢，非唯不能愈病，翻致更增，或時至死。犯戒之相亦復如之，故名為犯。

Second, clarifying the mark of violation: "violation" means going against and transgressing. One originally receives the Buddha's precepts wishing to leave birth-and-death behind, vowing to seek liberation — but now, encountering an unwholesome condition, being unable to master one's own mind, one turns back midway, whether in a grave or a lesser matter, and this is called going against and transgressing. Furthermore, "violation" also means coming into contact with what harms. It is like someone taking medicine who has been instructed to abstain from certain foods; if they do not follow the physician's instruction and eat something harmful, they come into contact with and undermine the medicine's own power — not only failing to be cured of their illness, but on the contrary making it worse, and sometimes even bringing about death. The mark of violating the precepts is exactly the same — hence it is called violation.

Ten Kinds of Upholding, from Beginner to Buddhahood 持戒清淨 · 明持犯（二）

Chinese Source

第二、廣明持犯者，從初心至佛果，以明持犯有十種：一、持不缺戒，謂持初四重不犯。二、持不破戒，謂對僧殘不犯。三、持不穿戒，謂對下三篇不犯。四、持無瑕戒，亦名不雜戒，謂不起諂心及諸惱覺觀雜念，亦名定共戒。五、持隨道戒，即是心行十六行觀，發苦忍智慧，亦名道共戒。六、持無著戒，即阿那含人若斷欲界九品思惟盡，名斷律儀戒，乃至色愛、無色愛等諸結使盡，皆名無著戒。七、持智所讚戒，發菩提心，為令一切眾生得涅槃故持戒。如是持戒，則為智所讚歎，亦可言持菩薩十重四十八輕戒。此戒能至佛果，故為智所讚歎。八、持自在戒，菩薩持戒，於種種破戒緣中而得自在，亦可言菩薩知罪、不罪不可得故，但隨利益眾生而持戒，心無所執，故名自在戒。九、持具足戒，菩薩能具一切眾生戒法及上地戒。十、持隨定戒，不起滅定，現種種威儀戒法，以度眾生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, extensively clarifying upholding and violation: from the first arising of the mind through to buddhahood, upholding the precepts is distinguished into ten kinds. First, upholding the precepts without deficiency — meaning upholding the initial four grave precepts without violating them. Second, upholding the precepts without breach — meaning not violating the saṅgha-remainder offenses. Third, upholding the precepts without perforation — meaning not violating the lower three categories of offense. Fourth, upholding the precepts without blemish, also called upholding them without mixture — meaning not giving rise to a flattering mind or to the scattered, vexing thoughts of coarse and subtle discursive thinking; also called the precepts held together with concentration. Fifth, upholding the precepts in accordance with the path — this is the mind's practice of the sixteen aspects of contemplation, bringing forth the wisdom of forbearance regarding suffering; also called the precepts held together with the path. Sixth, upholding the precepts without attachment — this refers to the non-returner who has completely severed the nine grades of discursive affliction of the desire realm, called severing the precepts of restraint, up through the exhaustion of every fetter such as craving for form and craving for the formless — all of these are called precepts without attachment. Seventh, upholding the precepts praised by wisdom — giving rise to the bodhi-mind, upholding the precepts so that all sentient beings may attain nirvāṇa. Precepts upheld in this way are praised by wisdom; this may also be spoken of as upholding the bodhisattva's ten major and forty-eight minor precepts. These precepts are able to lead all the way to buddhahood, and are therefore praised by wisdom. Eighth, upholding the precepts with mastery — the bodhisattva, upholding the precepts, attains mastery amid every kind of condition that might break them; this may also be spoken of as: since the bodhisattva knows that offense and non-offense cannot ultimately be found, they uphold the precepts solely in accordance with benefiting sentient beings, with a mind that grasps at nothing — hence this is called the precepts of mastery. Ninth, upholding the precepts in their completeness — the bodhisattva is able to be complete in the precept-dharmas of all sentient beings, together with the precepts proper to the higher grounds. Tenth, upholding the precepts in accordance with concentration — without rising from the concentration of cessation, manifesting every kind of dignified precept-conduct in order to bring sentient beings across.

前四即是世間戒淨，亦得出世間戒，義具如前說，善應分別；中二是出世間戒淨；後四是出世間上上戒淨。若能如上所說受持，是持戒相；異上所說，即是犯相。是名從初心至佛果，淺深論持戒及犯戒相。故經言：「唯佛一人具淨戒，餘人皆名破戒者。」復次，今明持戒者但隨分隨力而修習，令增進漸漸清淨；若不爾者，不能生諸禪定。復次，頓行菩薩能以慧方便，從初發心一念之中即具持十種戒，是故經言：「發心畢竟二不別。」

The first four of these are the purity of the precepts belonging to the worldly, though they may also give rise to world-transcending precepts — this meaning is complete as already explained above, and should be carefully distinguished. The middle two are the purity of the world-transcending precepts. The last four are the purity of the precepts transcending the world and beyond. If one is able to receive and uphold the precepts as described above, this is the mark of upholding; whatever differs from what is described above is the mark of violation. This is what is meant by discussing the shallow and the deep marks of upholding and violating the precepts, from the first arising of the mind all the way to buddhahood. Hence the sūtra says: "Only the Buddha alone is complete in pure precepts; everyone else is called one who has broken the precepts."

Furthermore, here it is clarified that upholding the precepts is cultivated only according to one's own portion and one's own power, allowing for gradual advancement toward purity; if this were not so, one would be unable to give rise to any dhyāna-concentration whatsoever. Furthermore, the bodhisattva of sudden practice, by means of wisdom and skillful means, is able, within a single thought-moment at the very first arising of the mind, to already be complete in upholding all ten kinds of precepts — hence the sūtra says: "The arising of the mind and its ultimate completion are, in the end, not two different things."

Reading note: This ten-stage ladder is the treatise's own answer to a question implicit in "the presence or absence of precepts": having precepts at all is binary, but upholding them well is a matter of degree, spanning the entire path. The first four stages sort ordinary monastic offenses by severity (the four grave offenses, the saṅgha-remainder offenses, and two lower categories); the middle two belong to genuine meditative and path attainment; the last four are the bodhisattva's own, culminating in a precept-holding so free of self-grasping that "offense and non-offense cannot ultimately be found." The closing citation — "the arising of the mind and its ultimate completion are, in the end, not two different things" — is the same "identity of beginning and end" move already seen at the close of fascicle 1's topic 2.

Concealment and Manifestation 持戒清淨 · 明持犯（三）· 明覆發

Chinese Source

第三、明覆發。就中自有二意：一者、正明覆發；二者、簡定。云何名覆發相？行者持戒，能發禪定破戒，即覆禪定。行者持世間戒淨故，即發世間禪；若持世間戒不淨，即覆世間禪。出世間戒及出世間上上戒，持則發禪，毀則覆禪，類如是分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying concealment and manifestation. This too has two parts in itself: first, directly clarifying concealment and manifestation; second, settling points of doubt. What is called the mark of concealment and manifestation? When a practitioner upholds the precepts, dhyāna-concentration can be brought forth; when the precepts are broken, dhyāna-concentration is concealed. Because the practitioner upholds the purity of the worldly precepts, worldly dhyāna is brought forth; if the worldly precepts are held impurely, worldly dhyāna is concealed. The same distinction applies, by the same kind of reasoning, to the world-transcending precepts and the precepts transcending the world and beyond: upheld, dhyāna is brought forth; violated, dhyāna is concealed.

二者、簡定覆發者，為眾生現在修禪不定故，應作四句分別：一、自有雖犯戒而發定，持而不發；二、自有破戒而不發定，持戒而發；三、自有持戒、犯戒二俱發；四、自有持、犯俱不發。初一、犯戒之人修禪定而發者，是過去習因，善根深厚，今雖有罪，過去善根力強故，亦以現前修禪定，重慚愧為緣故，譬如負債，強者先牽，所以得有發定之義。二、次持戒而不發定者，是人過去不種深禪定之因，今生雖復持戒修定，而不即發。三、俱發，四、俱不發，悉可類釋，故有四種不同。尋其根源，要因持戒而發，犯戒終為遮障。何以故？若過去經得禪定，即知過去以曾持戒發定故，成今世之習因；今生復以慚愧懺悔清淨為緣，是故得發宿世善根也。

Second, as to settling points of doubt about concealment and manifestation: because sentient beings, cultivating dhyāna in the present, are not uniform in this regard, the matter should be sorted into a fourfold classification: first, there are those who, though violating the precepts, still give rise to concentration, while others who uphold them do not give rise to it; second, there are those who break the precepts and do not give rise to concentration, while others who uphold the precepts do give rise to it; third, there are those in whom both upholding the precepts and violating them alike give rise to concentration; fourth, there are those in whom neither upholding nor violating gives rise to it.

As to the first — a person who violates the precepts yet gives rise to concentration through cultivating dhyāna — this is because of a habituated cause from a past life: the wholesome roots are deep and strong, and though there is an offense in the present, the power of the past wholesome roots is strong; and further, because present cultivation of dhyāna together with deep shame and remorse serve as conditions — it is like a debt, where the stronger creditor is paid first — this is how the meaning of "giving rise to concentration" comes about in such a case. Second, as to one who upholds the precepts yet does not give rise to concentration: such a person did not, in the past, plant the cause of deep dhyāna-concentration, and so, though upholding the precepts and cultivating concentration in this present life, it is not immediately brought forth. Third, both giving rise to it, and fourth, neither giving rise to it, may both be explained by the same kind of reasoning — hence these four distinct kinds. Tracing this to its root, it is essentially through upholding the precepts that concentration is brought forth, while violating the precepts is, in the end, an obstruction to it. Why is this? If dhyāna-concentration was attained in a past life, then it is known that, in the past, concentration was brought forth precisely through upholding the precepts, becoming the habituated cause of the present life; and in this present life, shame, remorse, repentance, and purity again serve as the condition through which the wholesome roots of past lives are brought forth once more.

Reading note: This fourfold classification is a piece of pastoral realism embedded in a technical treatise: Zhiyi has just spent several paragraphs establishing that upholding precepts is what properly gives rise to dhyāna — and then immediately acknowledges the obvious counter-cases, someone who breaks precepts yet still experiences concentration, or upholds them scrupulously yet experiences nothing. The explanation reaches back into past-life causation rather than abandoning the general rule: current dhyāna experience is read as the ripening of past habituated causes, with present moral conduct (and repentance where needed) serving as the condition that allows those causes to bear fruit now. This closes 持犯 (upholding and violation), the second of condition 1's three parts.

Repentance 持戒清淨 · 明懺悔

The third and final part of condition 1 turns to what happens when the precepts have already been broken. This is one of the richest passages translated from this treatise so far — moving from a plain account of why repentance matters, through three distinct methods of increasing depth, to a sustained meditation on the emptiness of offense itself that stands alongside fascicle 1's tetralemma analysis as some of the treatise's most purely philosophical writing.

Setting the Mind of Repentance in Motion 運懺悔心

Chinese Source

第三、明懺悔中，自有二意：一者、先明運懺悔心；二者、正明懺悔方法。第一、云何名運懺悔之心？若人性自不作惡，則無罪可悔。行人既不能決定持戒，或於中間值遇惡緣，即便破毀，若輕若重，以戒破故，則尸羅不淨，三昧不生，譬如衣有垢膩，不受染色，是故宜須懺悔。以懺悔故，則戒品清淨，三昧可生，如衣垢污，若浣清潔，染之可著。行者如是思惟，若戒不清淨，決須懺悔。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying repentance: this too has two parts in itself — first, clarifying how to set the mind of repentance in motion; second, rightly clarifying the method of repentance.

First, what is meant by setting the mind of repentance in motion? If a person's own nature simply never commits any evil, there is no offense to repent of. But since a practitioner is not always able to uphold the precepts with unwavering resolve, and may, at some point, encounter an unwholesome condition and thereby break them — whether gravely or lightly — once the precepts are broken, moral discipline is no longer pure, and samādhi cannot arise, just as cloth stained with grime will not properly take a dye. This is why repentance is necessary. Through repentance, the precepts become pure once more and samādhi can arise — just as cloth stained with grime, once washed clean, can properly take a dye. Reflecting in this way, the practitioner comes to know: if the precepts are not pure, repentance is absolutely necessary.

是故經云：「佛法之中有二種健兒：一、性不作惡；二、作已能悔。」今造過知悔，名健人也。夫懺悔者，懺名懺謝三寶及一切眾生，悔名慚愧，改過求哀。我今此罪，若得滅者，於將來時，寧失身命，終不更造如斯苦業。如比丘白佛：「我寧抱是熾然大火，終不敢毀犯如來淨戒。」生如是心，唯願三寶證明攝受，是名懺悔。復次，懺名外不覆藏，悔則內心剋責；懺名知罪為惡，悔則恐受其報。如是眾多，今不廣說，舉要言之，若能知法虛妄，永息惡業，修行善道，是名懺悔。

Hence the sūtra says: "Within the Buddhadharma there are two kinds of courageous person: first, one whose nature simply never commits evil; second, one who, having done wrong, is able to repent of it." To commit a transgression and yet know to repent of it is what makes one such a courageous person.

As for what repentance itself is: "confession" means confessing before, and expressing gratitude toward, the Three Jewels and all sentient beings; "repentance" means a sense of shame and remorse, correcting one's faults and beseeching compassion. One reflects: "If this offense of mine can now be extinguished, then in time to come, though I should lose my very body and life, I will never again commit such a painful karmic deed." As a monk once said to the Buddha: "I would rather embrace a raging, blazing fire than dare to violate and break the Tathāgata's pure precepts." Giving rise to a mind such as this, wishing only that the Three Jewels bear witness and receive one in compassion — this is called repentance. Furthermore: "confession" means not concealing one's fault outwardly, while "repentance" means holding oneself strictly to account inwardly; "confession" means knowing that the offense was evil, while "repentance" means fearing to receive its retribution. There is much more that could be said of this kind, which will not be given in full here — taking the essential point: if one is able to know that dharmas are illusory and unreal, forever setting unwholesome karma to rest and cultivating the wholesome path, this is what is called repentance.

Three Methods of Repentance 正明懺悔方法

Chinese Source

第二、明懺悔方法，即為三意：一、正明懺悔法不同；二、明罪滅階降；三、明復不復相。第一、正明懺悔法不同者，滅罪之由，各有其法，如衣垢膩，若直以水浣，終不可脫，皂莢灰汁，則能去之。滅罪之法亦復如是。今明懺悔方法，教門乃復眾多，取要論之，不過三種：一、作法懺悔，此扶戒律，以明懺悔；二、觀相懺悔，此扶定法，以明懺悔；三、觀無生懺悔，此扶慧法，以明懺悔。此三種懺悔法，義通三藏摩訶衍，但從多為說：前一法多，是小乘懺悔法；後二法多，是大乘懺悔法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the method of repentance: this has three parts — first, rightly clarifying that the methods of repentance differ; second, clarifying the different grades by which offenses are extinguished; third, clarifying whether restoration is complete or not.

First, rightly clarifying that the methods of repentance differ: the means by which an offense is extinguished each has its own particular method — just as cloth stained with grime cannot be freed of the stain by plain water alone, but soap-pod ash-lye is able to remove it. The methods for extinguishing an offense are exactly the same. Here, in clarifying the methods of repentance, the gates of teaching are indeed numerous, but taking the essentials, they number no more than three: first, repentance through formal procedure, which supports and clarifies repentance by means of the moral precepts; second, repentance through the contemplation of signs, which supports and clarifies repentance by means of concentration; third, repentance through the contemplation of no-arising, which supports and clarifies repentance by means of wisdom. These three methods of repentance, in their meaning, extend across both the Tripiṭaka and the Mahāyāna alike — but, speaking in terms of which predominates: the first method is, for the most part, the Hīnayāna's own method of repentance; the latter two are, for the most part, the Mahāyāna's own methods.

初明作法懺悔者，以作善事反惡事故，故名懺悔。如《毘尼》中，一向用此法滅罪。何以故？如懺第二篇：「二十眾作別住、下意、出罪等羯磨作法成就，即名為滅。」此不論見種種相貌，亦不論智慧觀空，故知但是作法懺悔。羯磨，此翻作法，如是乃至下三篇，並是作法。此事易知，義如律中廣明。但未明懺悔四重法，別有《最妙初教經》出懺悔四重法。彼經云：「當請三十清淨比丘僧，於大眾中，犯罪比丘當自發露。僧為作羯磨成就，又於三寶前作諸行法，及誦戒千遍，即得清淨。」亦云：「令取得相為證，而說罪滅清淨。」當知律中雖不出，經中有此羯磨明文，作法相貌如彼經中廣說。

First, clarifying repentance through formal procedure: because a wholesome act is undertaken to turn back an unwholesome one, this is called repentance. As in the Vinaya, this method alone is used to extinguish offenses. Why is this? As in repenting a second-category offense: "Once the assembly of twenty carries out to completion the formal procedures of probation, humbling of the mind, and restoration from expulsion, this is called extinguished." This makes no mention of seeing various signs, nor of wisdom's contemplation of emptiness — hence it is known that this is repentance through formal procedure alone. "Karman," rendered here as "formal procedure," applies in the same way all the way down to the lower three categories of offense — all are matters of formal procedure. This matter is easily understood, and its meaning is explained in full in the Vinaya.

As for the method of repentance for the four grave offenses, which the Vinaya itself does not set out — there is a separate work, the Sūtra of the Most Sublime Initial Teaching, which gives the method of repenting the four grave offenses. That sūtra states: "One should invite thirty pure bhikṣus; before the great assembly, the offending bhikṣu should openly disclose the offense. Once the assembly carries out the formal procedure to completion, and further, before the Three Jewels, carries out the various practices and recites the precepts a thousand times, purity is thereby attained." It also states: "Let a sign be obtained as confirmation, and let the extinguishing and purification of the offense then be declared." One should know, then, that although the Vinaya itself does not set this out, the sūtras do contain this explicit statement of a formal procedure — the manner of carrying it out is explained fully in that sūtra.

Reading note: The three methods track the three preliminary conditions the treatise keeps circling back to — precepts, concentration, wisdom — and each supports a progressively deeper kind of purification. Formal-procedure repentance is closest to what a Western reader might expect from "confession": a defined communal ritual with a clear beginning and end, requiring nothing more than the ritual's own correct performance.

Repentance through the Contemplation of Signs 觀相懺悔

Chinese Source

二、明觀相懺悔者，行人依諸經中懺悔方法，專心用意，於靜心中見種種諸相，如菩薩戒中所說。若懺十重，要須見好相乃滅相者，佛來摩頂，見光、華種種瑞相已，罪即得滅。若不見相，雖懺無益。諸大乘方等陀羅尼行法中，多有此觀相懺法。三藏及《雜阿含》中亦說觀相懺悔方法，謂作地獄、毒蛇、白毫等觀相成就，即說罪滅。此悉就定心中作故，觀相懺悔多依修定法說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying repentance through the contemplation of signs: the practitioner, following the methods of repentance found across the various sūtras, applies the mind single-mindedly and, within a stilled mind, sees various signs, as described in the bodhisattva precepts. If one is repenting the ten major precepts, a favorable sign must be seen before the offense can be said to be extinguished — the Buddha coming to rub the crown of one's head, or seeing light, flowers, and other auspicious signs — once such a sign is seen, the offense is thereupon extinguished; if no sign is seen, then even repentance brings no benefit. Among the various Mahāyāna Vaipulya dhāraṇī practice-methods, this kind of sign-contemplation repentance is found extensively. The Tripiṭaka and the Saṃyukta Āgama likewise speak of the method of sign-contemplation repentance — describing, for instance, the contemplation of hell, of poisonous snakes, or of the white curl of hair between the Buddha's brows, brought to completion, at which point the offense is declared extinguished. All of this is carried out within a concentrated mind, and so sign-contemplation repentance is, for the most part, explained in terms of the cultivation of concentration.

問曰：見種種相，云何知罪滅？答曰：經說不同，罪法輕重有異，不可定判。今但舉要而明，相不出四種：一、夢中見相；二、於行道時聞空中聲，或見異相及諸靈瑞；三、坐中覩見善、惡、破戒、持戒等相；四、以內證種種法門道心開發等為相。此隨輕重判之，不可定說，在下至驗善惡根性，更當略出。問曰：魔羅亦能作此等相，云何可別？答曰：實爾，邪正難別，不可定取。若相現時，良師乃識，事須面決，非可文載，是故行者初懺悔時，必須近善知識，別邪正之人。復次，夫見相者，忽然而覩，尚邪正難知，若逐文作心求之，多著魔也。問曰：若爾者，不應名觀相懺悔。答曰：言觀相者，但用心行道，功成相現，取此判之，便知罪滅、不滅。非謂行道之時，心存相事，而生取著。若如此用心，必定多來魔事。問曰：觀相懺悔行法云何？答曰：方法出在諸大乘方等修多羅中，行者當自尋經，依文而行。

Question: When these various signs are seen, how does one know that the offense is truly extinguished? Answer: The sūtras do not speak with one voice on this, since offenses differ in gravity, and no single fixed judgment can be given. Here, taking only the essentials, the signs do not exceed four kinds: first, seeing a sign in a dream; second, hearing a voice from empty space while walking the path, or seeing an extraordinary sign or other numinous, auspicious mark; third, beholding, while sitting, a sign of good, of evil, of having broken the precepts, or of having upheld them; fourth, taking as a sign the opening up of the mind of the path through various inwardly realized dharma-gates. These are judged according to gravity, and no fixed statement can be given — this will be given in brief again further below, where the marks that verify wholesome and unwholesome capacity are examined.

Question: Māra, too, is able to produce signs of this kind — how, then, can they be distinguished? Answer: This is indeed so; the deviant and the correct are hard to distinguish, and no fixed rule can be given. When such a sign appears, only a good teacher can properly recognize it — this is a matter that must be settled face to face, and cannot be conveyed through writing alone. This is why a practitioner, when first taking up repentance, must draw near to a good spiritual friend, someone able to distinguish the deviant from the correct. Furthermore, as for seeing a sign at all: it is beheld quite suddenly, and even then the deviant and the correct are hard to know — if one chases after the written description and forces the mind to seek such a sign out deliberately, one will, in most cases, fall prey to demonic interference.

Question: If this is so, then this should not be called repentance through the contemplation of signs at all. Answer: What is meant by "contemplation of signs" is only this: applying the mind to walking the path, and, once this effort bears fruit, a sign appears of itself — one then takes this sign as a basis for judgment, and so comes to know whether the offense is extinguished or not. It does not mean that, while walking the path, the mind should dwell on the matter of a sign and give rise to grasping after it. If the mind is used in that way, demonic interference is certain to follow in abundance. Question: What is the actual method of practice for repentance through the contemplation of signs? Answer: The method is set out across the various Mahāyāna Vaipulya sūtras; the practitioner should seek out these sūtras directly and practice in accordance with their text.

Reading note: This is a notably cautious, almost clinical treatment of visionary experience for a meditation manual: signs of purification are real and expected, but the same signs can be produced by demonic interference, and the text is explicit that "the deviant and the correct are hard to distinguish" even for the practitioner having the experience — hence the repeated insistence on finding a qualified teacher rather than relying on textual checklists alone. The warning against "chasing after the written description" is aimed squarely at readers of exactly this kind of text: naming the four categories of sign is meant to orient understanding, not to hand a practitioner a target to manufacture.

Repentance through the Contemplation of No-Arising 觀無生懺悔

Chinese Source

三、明觀無生懺悔者，如《普賢觀經》中偈說：
一切業障海，皆由妄想生，
若欲懺悔者，端坐念實相。
眾罪如霜露，慧日能消除，
是故至誠心，懺悔六情根。
夫行人欲行大懺悔者，應當起大悲心，憐愍一切，深達罪源。所以者何？一切諸法本來空寂，尚無有福，況復罪耶？但眾生不善思惟，妄執有為而起無明及與愛恚，從此三毒廣作無量無邊一切重罪，皆從一念不了心生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying repentance through the contemplation of no-arising: as the Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra says in verse:
"The whole ocean of every karmic obstruction arises entirely from deluded thought;
If one wishes to repent of it, sit upright and be mindful of true reality.
Every offense is like frost or dew, which the sun of wisdom can dissolve away;
Hence, with a mind of utmost sincerity, repent at the very root of the six faculties of feeling."
A practitioner wishing to carry out this great repentance should give rise to a mind of great compassion, feeling for all beings, and penetrate deeply to the very source of offense. Why is this? All dharmas whatsoever are, from the very beginning, empty and still — there is not even merit to be found there, how much less offense? It is only that sentient beings, failing to reflect rightly, wrongly cling to conditioned existence and thereby give rise to ignorance, along with craving and aversion; and from these three poisons, every measureless and boundless grave offense is committed at length — all of it arising from a single thought-moment of unenlightened mind.

若欲除滅，但當反觀如此心者從何處起？若在過去，過去已滅，已滅之法則無所有，無所有法不名為心；若在未來，未來未至，未至之法即是不有，不有之法亦無此心；若在現在，現在之中，剎那不住，無住相中，心不可得。復次，若言現在，現在者，為在內、外、兩中間耶？若言在內，則不待外，內自有故；若言在外，於我無過。復次，外塵無知，豈得有心？既無內、外，豈有中間？若無中間，則無停處。如是觀之，不見相貌，不在方所，當知此心畢竟空寂。既不見心，不見非心，尚無所觀，況有能觀？無能無所，顛倒想斷。既顛倒斷，則無無明及以愛恚。無此三毒，罪從何生？

If one wishes to remove and extinguish this, one need only turn contemplation back and ask: from where does this mind arise? If it is in the past, the past has already ceased, and a dharma already ceased possesses nothing at all — and what possesses nothing at all cannot be called mind. If it is in the future, the future has not yet arrived, and what has not yet arrived simply does not exist — and what does not exist likewise has no mind in it. If it is in the present, then within the present itself, not a single moment remains still — and within a mark that never remains still, mind cannot be found.

Furthermore, if it is said to be in the present, is the present located inside, outside, or in between the two? If it is said to be inside, then it does not depend on the outside, since it would already exist within of itself; if it is said to be outside, then it has nothing to do with me. Furthermore, an external object has no awareness of its own — how, then, could it possess mind? Since there is neither inside nor outside, how could there be any "in between" at all? And if there is no "in between," there is nowhere for it to come to rest.

Contemplated in this way, no mark or appearance is seen anywhere, in any location whatsoever — one should know, then, that this mind is, in the end, empty and still. Since mind itself cannot be found, nor can "not-mind" be found either — there is not even anything to be contemplated at all, how much less anyone who contemplates? With neither a contemplating subject nor a contemplated object, every inverted thought is cut off. And once inverted thought is cut off, there is no more ignorance, nor craving and aversion. Without these three poisons, from where could offense possibly arise?

復次，一切萬法，悉屬於心，心性尚空，何況萬法？若無萬法，誰是罪業？若不得罪，不得不罪，觀罪無生，破一切罪，以一切諸罪根本性空，常清淨故。故維摩羅詰謂優波離：「彼自無罪，勿增其過，當直爾除滅，勿擾其心。」又如《普賢觀經》中說：「觀心無心，法不住法，我心自空，罪福無主，一切諸法皆悉如是，無住無壞。作是懺悔，名大懺悔，名莊嚴懺悔，名破壞心識懺悔，名無罪相懺悔。行此悔者，心如流水，念念之中見普賢菩薩及十方佛。」故知深觀無生，名大懺悔，於懺悔中最尊最妙。一切大乘經中明懺悔法，悉以此觀為主。若離此觀，則不得名大方等懺也。

Furthermore: the ten thousand dharmas all belong to mind — and if the very nature of mind is empty, how much more so the ten thousand dharmas? If there are no ten thousand dharmas, who is it that commits karmic offense? If offense cannot be found, then non-offense cannot be found either — contemplating offense as unarisen dissolves every offense whatsoever, since the very root-nature of every offense is empty, and ever pure.

Hence Vimalakīrti said to Upāli: "That offense is, of itself, without any offense at all — do not increase its fault. It should simply be removed and extinguished directly, without disturbing the mind over it." And likewise the Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra says: "Contemplating mind, no mind is found; dharmas do not abide in dharmas; my own mind is, of itself, empty, and offense and merit have no master over them — every dharma whatsoever is exactly the same, neither abiding nor perishing. Repentance carried out in this way is called great repentance, called adorned repentance, called the repentance that breaks apart mind and consciousness, called the repentance in which no mark of offense remains. One who practices this repentance has a mind like flowing water, beholding the Bodhisattva Samantabhadra and the buddhas of the ten directions in every single thought-moment." Hence it is known that the deep contemplation of no-arising is called great repentance — the most honored, the most sublime of every form of repentance. In every Mahāyāna sūtra that explains the method of repentance, this contemplation is taken as the essential core. Apart from this contemplation, none can rightly be called the great Vaipulya repentance.

問曰：觀無生懺悔，云何知罪滅相？答曰：如是用心，於念念中即諸罪業念念自滅。若欲知障道法轉者，精勤不已，諸相亦當自現，觀此可知。如前觀相中所說，善夢靈瑞、定慧開發等相，此中應具明。復次，若行者觀心與理相應，即是罪滅之相，不勞餘求，故《普賢觀經》中言：「令此空慧與心相應，當知於一念中能滅百萬億阿僧祇劫生死重罪。」以此為證，若得無生忍慧，則便究盡罪源，此則尸羅清淨，可修禪定。

Question: In repentance through the contemplation of no-arising, how does one know the mark of the offense's extinguishing? Answer: Using the mind in this way, every karmic offense is, moment by moment, extinguished of itself, moment by moment. If one wishes to know that the dharmas obstructing the path are being transformed, then, through unceasing diligence, the various signs will also naturally appear of themselves — this contemplation itself makes it known. As already stated above in the discussion of sign-contemplation — favorable dreams, numinous and auspicious marks, the opening of concentration and wisdom, and the like — the same signs should be understood to apply fully here as well.

Furthermore, if a practitioner's contemplating mind comes into accord with principle itself, this is precisely the mark of the offense's extinguishing, with no further confirmation needed elsewhere. Hence the Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra says: "Let this wisdom of emptiness come into accord with the mind, and know that, within a single thought-moment, it is able to extinguish the grave offenses of birth-and-death accumulated across a hundred million koṭis of incalculable eons." Taking this as confirmation: if the wisdom of the forbearance of no-arising is attained, then the very source of offense is thereby exhausted to its root — and this is precisely when moral discipline becomes pure, and dhyāna-concentration may be cultivated.

Reading note: This is the treatise's second sustained excursion into explicit emptiness-analysis, and it runs a close parallel to fascicle 1's tetralemma against "contaminated dharma": here the target is offense itself, sought and never found across past, future, present, inside, outside, and in between. The Vimalakīrti citation is worth pausing on — Upāli was the Buddha's own foremost expert in monastic discipline, and the scene invoked (from the Vimalakīrti Sūtra) has the layman Vimalakīrti correcting the Vinaya specialist on the deepest nature of an offense he was trying to help a guilt-stricken monk resolve through ordinary procedure. Note carefully what this section does not claim: it never says moral discipline stops mattering once emptiness is realized — the very next section still treats formal-procedure and sign-contemplation repentance as real and necessary for those who need them. Emptiness completes the picture; it does not replace conduct.

Grades of Severance, and Whether Restoration Is Complete 明罪滅階降 · 明復不復相

Chinese Source

第二、明罪滅階降不同者，懺法既有別異，當知滅罪亦復不同。所以者何？罪有三品：一者、違無作起障道罪；二者、體性罪；三者、無明煩惱根本罪。通稱罪者，摧也。現則摧損行人功德智慧，未來之世三塗受報，則能摧折行者色心，故名為罪。一、明作法懺悔者，破違無作障道罪。二、明觀相懺者，破除體性惡業罪，故《摩訶衍論》云：「若比丘犯殺生戒，雖復懺悔，得戒清淨，障道罪滅，而殺報不滅。」此可以證前釋後，當知觀相懺悔，用功既大，能除體性之罪。三、觀無生懺悔罪滅者，破除無明一切煩惱習因之罪，此則究竟除罪源本。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying that the different grades by which offense is extinguished are not uniform: since the methods of repentance themselves differ, the extinguishing of offense likewise differs accordingly. Why is this? Offense is of three grades: first, the offense of violating the non-action precept, which obstructs the path; second, offense in its own essential nature; third, the root offense of ignorance and affliction. What is generally called "offense" means, literally, "that which crushes": in the present it crushes and diminishes the practitioner's own merit and wisdom, and in lives to come brings retribution in the three lower destinies, able to crush and break the practitioner's own body and mind — hence it is called offense.

First, as to repentance through formal procedure: this breaks the offense of violating the non-action precept that obstructs the path. Second, as to repentance through the contemplation of signs: this breaks and removes offense in its essential nature — hence the Mahāyāna treatise says: "If a monk violates the precept against taking life, then, though he may repent and have the precept restored to purity, and the offense obstructing the path be extinguished, the karmic retribution of killing itself is not thereby extinguished." This may be taken as confirming what came before by way of what follows: one should know that repentance through the contemplation of signs, since it requires greater effort, is able to remove offense in its essential nature as well. Third, as to the extinguishing of offense through repentance by the contemplation of no-arising: this breaks and removes the root offense of ignorance and every habituated cause of affliction — this alone is the ultimate removal of offense at its very source.

第三、明復本、不復本相者。問曰；懺悔清淨，得復本不？答曰；解者不同。有言：不復。如衣破更補，雖完，終不如不破。有言：得復。如衣不淨，更浣淨，與本無異。有言：有復，有不復。如律中所明，初二篇不復，後三篇可復。初教經所明，作羯磨懺悔，四重悉復。今言不必定爾。應當對前三種懺法還為三義：一者、復義；二者、過本義；三、增上過本義。今當借譬顯之：一者、作法懺悔，罪滅或復、不復，如冷病人服於薑桂，所患除差，身有復、不復。二者、觀相懺悔，非唯罪滅，能發禪定。此則過本。何以故？本無禪定故。如冷病人服石散等，非但冷除，亦復肥壯過本。三者、觀無生懺悔，非唯罪滅，發諸禪定，乃得成道。此為增上過本。如病服於仙藥，非直病除，乃得仙通，神變自在。此而推之，豈得一類？

Third, clarifying whether the mark of restoration to the original state is complete or not. Question: Once repentance brings purity, is restoration to the original state attained? Answer: Interpretations here differ. Some say: there is no restoration — like torn cloth that has been mended: though whole again, it is never quite the same as cloth that was never torn. Some say: restoration is attained — like soiled cloth that, once washed clean, is no different from what it was originally. Some say: there is both restoration and non-restoration — as explained in the Vinaya, the first two categories of offense admit no restoration, while the latter three categories can be restored; and as explained in the Sūtra of the Initial Teaching, once the formal-procedure repentance is carried out, even the four grave offenses are entirely restored. Here it is said that this need not be fixed in only one way. It should instead be matched against the three methods of repentance already given, yielding three distinct senses in turn: first, the sense of restoration; second, the sense of exceeding the original state; third, the sense of exceeding the original state to an even greater degree.

Let us illustrate this now with a comparison. First, repentance through formal procedure: the offense is extinguished, but restoration may or may not follow — like someone suffering from a cold-type illness who takes ginger and cinnamon: the ailment is cured, but the body may or may not be fully restored to its former state. Second, repentance through the contemplation of signs: not only is the offense extinguished, but dhyāna-concentration is also brought forth — this exceeds the original state, since there was originally no dhyāna-concentration at all. It is like someone suffering from a cold-type illness who takes mineral powder and the like: not only is the coldness removed, but the body also grows more robust than it originally was. Third, repentance through the contemplation of no-arising: not only is the offense extinguished and every dhyāna brought forth, but the path itself is thereby accomplished — this exceeds the original state to an even greater degree. It is like someone who, being ill, takes an elixir of immortality: not only is the illness removed, but immortal powers and self-mastered spiritual transformation are also attained. Reasoned through in this way, how could these three possibly be treated as all the same kind?

Reading note: The three-grade taxonomy of offense (procedural, essential-nature, and root-ignorance) maps exactly onto the three methods of repentance, each cutting one layer deeper than the last — a structural echo of the precepts/concentration/wisdom triad running through this whole condition. The three illustrative similes (ginger tea for a common cold, mineral tonic for lasting vigor, an elixir for outright immortality) are Zhiyi's own way of making a fairly abstract distinction — mere restoration versus exceeding the original state versus exceeding it "to an even greater degree" — immediately intuitive.

Without Precepts, and Whether Repentance Is Always Required 問答

Chinese Source

問曰：有戒者可然，其無戒者云何？答曰：無戒者，當更受戒。或有因懺發戒，此如《普賢觀經》中所說。復次，若菩薩戒者，眾生世世以來，或已遇善知識，發菩提心，受菩薩戒，但於生死中顛倒造罪，妄失違犯，因今歸依三寶，重更練之，兼復懺悔清淨，用此本戒，亦發禪定。是故雖無事戒，菩提本戒或已有之。復次，如《摩訶衍論》說：「尸羅，秦言好善，好行善道，不自放逸，是名尸羅。或受戒行善，或不受戒行善，皆名尸羅。」若不受戒行善名尸羅者，既有尸羅，豈不得發諸禪三昧耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: This holds for one who has precepts — but what of one who lacks them? Answer: One without precepts should receive them anew. There are also cases where the precepts are brought forth through repentance itself, as described in the Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra.

Furthermore, as for the bodhisattva precepts: sentient beings, life after life, may already have encountered a good spiritual friend, given rise to the bodhi-mind, and received the bodhisattva precepts — only to fall, amid the confusion of birth-and-death, into committing offenses, wrongly losing or violating them. If, in the present, such a person takes refuge in the Three Jewels once more, renews this training, and also carries out repentance to purity, then, by means of this original precept, dhyāna-concentration can likewise be brought forth. Hence, even without the outward, formal precepts, the fundamental precept of bodhi may already be present.

Furthermore, as the Mahāyāna treatise says: "Śīla, in the language of the Qin dynasty, means 'skilled at what is good' — delighting in walking the wholesome path, never given over to laxity: this is called śīla. Whether one practices what is good having received the precepts, or practices what is good without having received them, both alike are called śīla." If practicing what is good without having received the precepts can still be called śīla, then, once śīla is present, how could dhyāna and samādhi fail to be brought forth?

問曰：若爾者，何用受戒為？答曰：不然。一為助道，二定佛法外相，豈可不依？問曰：上來所說，初坐禪者必須懺悔，亦有不然？答曰：不必一向。如《妙勝定經》所明，但能直心坐禪，即是第一懺悔。若於坐中有難轉多，不得用心者，必須懺悔。

Question: If this is so, then what use is there in receiving the precepts at all? Answer: This does not follow. First, they assist the path; second, they establish the outward mark of the Buddhadharma — how could one not rely on them?

Question: According to what has been said above, must a beginner necessarily undergo repentance before sitting in meditation — or is this not always so? Answer: It need not always be so. As the Sūtra of the Sublime Supreme Concentration explains, simply being able to sit in meditation with a straightforward mind is itself the foremost repentance. But if, while sitting, obstacles arise in great number, such that the mind cannot be properly applied, then repentance is indeed necessary.

Reading note: The closing exchange keeps the whole discussion from tipping into either extreme. Against the worry that formal ordination might seem unnecessary once śīla is redefined as simply "skilled at what is good": Zhiyi insists the outward precepts still matter, both as practical support and as the visible mark that distinguishes the Buddhadharma from any other path. And against the opposite worry — that this entire elaborate repentance apparatus might be a mandatory prerequisite before anyone can sit down to meditate at all — the very last line relieves the pressure: a straightforward mind sitting in meditation is already "the foremost repentance" in itself; the more elaborate methods are for when genuine obstacles actually arise, not a checklist to complete beforehand. This closes 懺悔 (repentance), and with it, all three parts of condition 1, 持戒清淨 (pure moral discipline) — the largest of the five conditions.

The Remaining Four Conditions 具五緣（二～五）

With moral discipline fully treated, the remaining four of the five conditions move quickly by comparison.

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying being complete in robes and food. Here, the practice of robes is clarified as being of three kinds: first, as with the great being of the Snowy Mountains and others who cultivate the path, keeping only a single robe is sufficient, since they do not travel among people and their power of forbearance is already accomplished — this is the practice of the superior person. Second, as with Kāśyapa and others who constantly undertake the dhutanga practices, keeping only the three robes of discarded rags, needing nothing further — this is the practice of the middling person. Third, in lands of great cold, or for the lesser practitioner who cannot bear such hardship, the Tathāgata further permits keeping a hundred and one requisite items — but this still requires the proper procedure for declaring them pure, knowing moderation and contentment; if one goes beyond this into excessive craving, it becomes an obstruction to the path.

具足食法者，食有四種：若上人大士，深山絕人，果菜隨得，趣以支命。二者、常行頭陀，受乞食法，是乞食法能破四種邪命，依正命自活，能生聖道，故名聖種。四邪命自活者：一、下口食；二、仰口食；三、四維口食；四、方口食。此是邪命之相。如舍利弗為青木女說，是中應廣分別。三者、阿蘭若，受檀越送食。四者、於僧中結淨食，有此等食，名緣具足，是名衣食具足。若無此資身因緣，則心不寧，於道有妨。

As for being complete in the practice of food, food is of four kinds: first, the superior person, dwelling deep in the mountains apart from other people, takes whatever fruit and vegetables can be found, simply enough to sustain life. Second, one who constantly practices the dhutanga austerities undertakes the practice of alms-begging — this practice of alms-begging is able to break the four kinds of wrong livelihood, sustaining oneself in reliance on right livelihood, able to give rise to the noble path, and is therefore called one of the noble lineages. The four kinds of wrong livelihood are: first, eating by "lowered mouth"; second, eating by "raised mouth"; third, eating by "the four intermediate-direction mouth"; fourth, eating by "the directional mouth." These are the marks of wrong livelihood, as Śāriputra explained for a certain woman — this should be distinguished in full elsewhere. Third, dwelling in a forest hermitage, receiving food sent by a lay donor. Fourth, having food formally designated as pure within the saṅgha. Wherever such food is present, this is called being complete in the condition — this is what is meant by being complete in robes and food. Without this sustaining condition for the body, the mind cannot be at peace, and this becomes an obstruction to the path.

第三、得閑居靜處。閑者，不作眾事，名之為閑。無憒鬧故，名之為靜。此有三處可修禪定：一者、深山絕人之處；二者、頭陀蘭若之處，離於聚落極近二里，此放牧聲絕，無諸憒鬧；三者、遠白衣舍處，清淨伽藍之中，皆是閑居靜處也。

Third, obtaining a quiet, secluded dwelling place. "Quiet" means not engaged in the many affairs of ordinary life — hence it is called quiet. Being free of clamor and disturbance, it is called secluded. There are three kinds of place suitable for cultivating dhyāna-concentration here: first, a place deep in the mountains, apart from other people; second, a dhutanga hermitage, at least two li removed from any village, where the sounds of herding and grazing cannot be heard and there is no clamor of any kind; third, a place removed from the households of lay people, within a pure monastery — all of these are quiet, secluded dwelling places.

第四、息諸緣務者。緣務眾多，略說有四：一、息生活緣務，所謂不作一切有為事業；二、息人事緣務，所謂不追尋俗人、朋友、親識，斷絕往還；三、息工巧技術緣務，所謂不作世間工匠、醫方、藥呪、卜相、書數、算計等事；四、息學問緣務，所謂讀誦、聽學、義論等，悉皆棄捨，此為息諸緣務。所以者何？若多緣務，則於修定有廢，心亂難攝，不得定也。

Fourth, setting every worldly affair and involvement to rest. Such affairs and involvements are numerous; in brief, they may be given as four: first, setting to rest the affairs of livelihood — meaning not engaging in any conditioned occupation whatsoever; second, setting to rest personal affairs — meaning not seeking out worldly people, friends, and acquaintances, cutting off all coming and going with them; third, setting to rest the affairs of craft and technical skill — meaning not engaging in worldly trades, medicine, incantations, divination and physiognomy, calligraphy and arithmetic, calculation, and the like; fourth, setting to rest scholarly affairs — meaning reading and reciting, listening and studying, debating doctrine, and the like, all of which are to be entirely set aside. This is what is meant by setting every worldly affair and involvement to rest. Why is this? If one has many affairs and involvements, the cultivation of concentration is neglected, the mind becomes scattered and hard to gather in, and concentration cannot be attained.

第五、近善知識，有三種：一、外護善知識，經營供養，善能將護行人，不相惱亂；二者、同行善知識，共修一道，互相勸發，不相擾亂；三者、教授善知識，以內外方便禪定法門示教利喜。是則略明五緣具足。

Fifth, drawing near to a good spiritual friend — this is of three kinds: first, the good spiritual friend of outer protection, who manages and provides support, skillfully looking after the practitioner without causing any disturbance; second, the good spiritual friend of shared practice, who cultivates the same path together, mutually encouraging and inspiring one another without any mutual disruption; third, the good spiritual friend of instruction, who demonstrates and teaches the gates of dhyāna-concentration, both the external and internal expedient means, bringing benefit and joy. This, then, briefly clarifies being complete in the five conditions.

Reading note: This closes 具五緣 (being complete in the five conditions) — the first of the twenty-five preliminary conditions' five groups — in full. Three kinds of spiritual friend recur throughout later Chinese Buddhist writing: the protector who handles practical support, the peer who practices alongside you, and the teacher who actually instructs — a useful three-way distinction, since a good relationship in one role doesn't guarantee competence in the others.

Rejecting the Five Desires 訶五欲

The second of the twenty-five preliminary conditions' five groups turns from external circumstances to the mind's own relationship with sense-pleasure — the classical five desires bound to form, sound, smell, taste, and touch.

Chinese Source

Second, rejecting the five desires and abandoning the five hindrances. The sūtras speak of "being free of desire and of unwholesome dharmas," together with "possessing both coarse and subtle discursive thought." "Being free of desire" is precisely rejecting the five desires. "Unwholesome dharmas" is precisely abandoning the five hindrances. As for the five desires: these are the supremely fine forms, sounds, smells, tastes, and tactile sensations of the world, which constantly deceive and delude every ordinary person, ruining what is wholesome. If one does not clearly recognize their fault and reject them with weariness and disenchantment, no dhyāna or samādhi can ever be obtained.

一、訶色欲者，所謂男子、女人形貌端嚴，修目高眉，朱脣素齒，及世間寶物，青、黃、赤、白、紅、紫、縹、綠，種種妙色，能令愚人見即生愛，作諸惡業。如頻婆娑羅王以色欲故，身入敵國，獨在婬女房中。優填王以色染故，截五百仙人手足。如是等種種因緣知色過罪，如《摩訶衍》中廣說。二、訶聲欲者，所謂箜、篌、箏、笛、絲竹、金石音樂之聲，及男女歌詠、讚頌等聲，能令凡夫聞即染著，起諸惡業。如五百仙人雪山中住，聞歌聲，即失禪定，心醉狂亂。如是等種種因緣，知聲過罪，如《摩訶衍》中廣說。

First, rejecting the desire for form: this refers to the handsome, dignified appearance of men and women — fine eyes, high brows, red lips, white teeth — and the treasures of the world, blue, yellow, red, white, crimson, violet, azure, green, and every kind of fine color, which cause the foolish, upon seeing them, to give rise at once to craving and commit every kind of unwholesome deed. A certain king, on account of desire for form, entered an enemy country alone and stayed in a courtesan's chamber. Another king, stained by desire for form, cut off the hands and feet of five hundred sages. Through causes and conditions such as these, the fault of form is to be known, as the Mahāyāna treatise explains in full.

Second, rejecting the desire for sound: this refers to the sounds of the harp, the lute, the zither, the flute, string and wind instruments, bronze and stone chimes, and the singing and chanting of men and women, which cause ordinary people, upon hearing them, to become at once attached and give rise to unwholesome deeds. Five hundred sages, dwelling in the Snowy Mountains, upon hearing a certain singing voice, at once lost their dhyāna-concentration, their minds intoxicated and thrown into disorder. Through causes and conditions such as these, the fault of sound is to be known, as the Mahāyāna treatise explains in full.

三、訶香欲者，所謂男女身香、世間飲食馨香及一切熏香等，愚人不了香相，聞即愛著，開結使門。如一比丘在蓮華池邊，聞華香氣，心生愛樂，池神即大訶責：「何故偷我香氣？」以著香故，令諸結使臥者皆起。如是種種因緣知香過惡，如《摩訶衍》中廣說。四、訶味欲者，所謂苦、酸、甘、辛、鹹、淡等種種飲食餚饍美味，能令凡夫心生染著，起不善業。如一沙彌染著酪味，命終後即生酪中，受於蟲身。如是等種種知味過罪，如《摩訶衍》中廣說。五、訶觸欲者，男女身分，柔軟細滑，寒時體溫，熱時體涼，及諸好觸，愚人無智，為之沈沒，起障道業。如獨角仙人，因觸欲故，退失神通，為婬女騎頸。如是等種種觸欲過罪，如《摩訶衍》中廣說。

Third, rejecting the desire for smell: this refers to the fragrance of men's and women's bodies, the fragrant aroma of worldly food and drink, and every kind of scented incense, which cause the foolish, failing to understand the true mark of fragrance, to become attached upon smelling it, opening the gate to the fetters. A certain monk, standing by a lotus pond, smelled the fragrance of the flowers and gave rise to a mind of craving and delight; the spirit of the pond sternly rebuked him, saying: "Why do you steal my fragrance?" Because of this attachment to fragrance, every dormant fetter was thereby aroused. Through causes and conditions such as these, the fault of smell is to be known, as the Mahāyāna treatise explains in full.

Fourth, rejecting the desire for taste: this refers to the bitter, sour, sweet, pungent, salty, bland, and every other delicious flavor of food and drink, which cause ordinary people to give rise to a mind of craving and attachment and commit unwholesome karma. A certain novice, attached to the taste of cream, was reborn, after death, within a jar of cream itself, receiving the body of a worm there. Through causes and conditions such as these, the fault of taste is to be known, as the Mahāyāna treatise explains in full.

Fifth, rejecting the desire for touch: this refers to the softness and smoothness of the bodies of men and women, their warmth in cold weather and coolness in hot weather, and every other pleasing sensation of touch, by which the foolish, lacking wisdom, are utterly overcome, giving rise to karma that obstructs the path. A certain sage, on account of the desire for touch, lost his supernormal powers and was made to be ridden upon the neck by a courtesan. Through causes and conditions such as these, the faults of the desire for touch are to be known, as the Mahāyāna treatise explains in full.

問曰：云何訶五欲？答曰：訶欲之法，如《摩訶衍》中：「哀哉！眾生常為五欲所惱，而猶求之不已。此五欲者，得之轉劇，如火益薪，其焰轉熾。五欲無益，如狗齧枯骨；五欲增諍，如鳥競肉；五欲燒人，如逆風執炬；五欲害人，如踐惡蛇；五欲無實，如夢所得；五欲不久，亦如假借須臾。世人愚惑，貪著五欲，至死不捨，為之後世受無量苦。」此五欲法與眾生同有，一切眾生常為五欲所使，名欲奴僕。坐此弊欲，墜墮三塗。我今修禪，復為障蔽，此為大賊，當急遠之。如《禪經》中說偈：
生死不斷絕，貪欲嗜味故，
養怨入丘塚，廣受諸辛苦。
身臭如死屍，九孔流不淨，
如廁蟲樂糞，愚貪身無異。
智者應觀身，不貪染世間，
無累無所欲，是名真涅槃。
如諸佛所說，一心一意行，
數息在禪定，是名行頭陀。
如是等種種因緣知五欲過罪，心不親近，如離怨賊。以遠離故，心無熱惱，欲想不生，此為修禪之要。訶五欲相，如《摩訶衍》廣說。

Question: How, then, should the five desires be rejected? Answer: The method of rejecting desire is as the Mahāyāna treatise says: "How pitiable! Sentient beings are constantly afflicted by the five desires, and yet they seek them without ceasing. These five desires, the more they are obtained, grow only more intense — like fire fed with fuel, its flame growing ever fiercer. The five desires bring no true benefit, like a dog gnawing on a dry bone; the five desires increase strife, like birds contending over a piece of flesh; the five desires burn a person, like grasping a torch against the wind; the five desires harm a person, like stepping on a venomous snake; the five desires have no substance, like what is obtained in a dream; the five desires do not last, like something merely borrowed for a brief moment. Yet the people of the world, foolish and deluded, cling to the five desires, unable to let go even unto death, and for this reason undergo measureless suffering in lives to come." These five desires are shared alike by all sentient beings; every sentient being is constantly driven by the five desires, and so is called a slave of desire. Sitting bound by this ruinous desire, one falls into the three lower destinies. Now, as I cultivate dhyāna, this desire once again becomes an obstruction and a veil — it is a great thief, and must be swiftly kept at a distance. As the Dhyāna Sūtra says in verse:
"Birth-and-death never comes to an end, on account of craving desire and relishing flavor;
Nurturing an enemy, one enters the burial mound, undergoing every bitter suffering at length.
The body reeks like a corpse, its nine openings ceaselessly flowing with what is unclean;
Like a maggot delighting in a latrine, the foolish craving for the body is no different.
The wise should contemplate the body thus, and not crave attachment to the world;
Free of entanglement, free of desire — this alone is called true nirvāṇa.
As every buddha has taught, practice with one mind and single intent,
Counting the breath within dhyāna-concentration — this is called practicing the dhutanga way."
Through causes and conditions such as these, the fault of the five desires is to be known, and the mind should not draw near to them, as one would keep distance from an enemy or a thief. Through this distance, the mind is free of feverish affliction, and no thought of desire arises — this is the essential point of cultivating dhyāna. The mark of rejecting the five desires is explained in full in the Mahāyāna treatise.

Reading note: The five cautionary vignettes are classical exempla — each names a specific figure who came to grief through exactly one of the five senses, functioning the way parables do in any oral teaching tradition: memorable, slightly exaggerated, and easy to recall precisely because they're vivid. This closes 訶五欲 (rejecting the five desires), the second of the twenty-five preliminary conditions' five groups.

Abandoning the Five Hindrances 棄五蓋

The third group turns from sense-desire itself to five specific mental states that cover over ("hinder") a mind trying to enter concentration — a taxonomy that becomes foundational across the whole later tradition of Buddhist meditation instruction.

第三、棄五蓋者：一者、貪欲蓋；二、瞋恚蓋；三、睡眠蓋；四、掉悔蓋；五、疑蓋。第一、棄貪欲者，前說外五塵中生欲，今約內意根生欲。所謂行者端坐修禪，心生欲覺，念念相續，覆蓋善心，令不生長，覺已應棄。所以者何？如術婆伽欲心內發，尚能燒身，況復心生欲火而不燒諸善法？復次，貪欲之人，去道甚遠。所以者何？欲為種種惱亂住處，若心著欲，無由近道，如除蓋偈說：
入道慚愧人，持鉢福眾生。
云何縱塵欲，沈沒於五情？
已捨於五欲，棄之而不顧，
如何還欲得，如愚自食吐？
諸欲求時苦，得時多怖畏，
失時懷悲惱，一切無樂處。
諸欲患如是，已訶能捨之，

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, abandoning the five hindrances: first, the hindrance of desire; second, the hindrance of anger; third, the hindrance of sleep; fourth, the hindrance of restlessness and regret; fifth, the hindrance of doubt.

First, abandoning the hindrance of desire: what was described earlier concerns desire arising from the five external objects; here, desire arising from the internal faculty of intention is addressed. This refers to a practitioner sitting upright in the cultivation of dhyāna, giving rise to a mind of desire that continues moment after moment, covering over the wholesome mind and preventing it from growing — once this is recognized, it should be abandoned. Why is this? Even a hermit consumed by lustful desire was able to burn his own body through desire arising internally — how much more should the fire of desire arising in the mind be feared, for it burns away every wholesome dharma? Furthermore, a person given over to desire is very far from the path. Why is this? Desire is the very dwelling-place of every kind of vexation and disturbance; if the mind clings to desire, there is no way to draw near the path — as the verse on removing this hindrance says:
"One who has entered the path with a sense of shame, holding the bowl to bring merit to sentient beings —
How could such a person indulge in worldly desire, sinking into the five faculties of feeling?
Having already renounced the five desires, cast aside and given no further thought —
How could one wish to have them back again, like a fool who eats their own vomit?
Every desire brings suffering while it is sought, and, once obtained, brings much fear;
And when it is lost, sorrow and vexation follow — nowhere in it is there any true joy.
Since the affliction of every desire is like this, having rejected it, one is able to let it go,
And, gaining the joy of merit and dhyāna-concentration, one is no longer deceived by it."
Through causes and conditions such as these, the hindrance of desire is rejected, as the verse rejecting desire in the Mahāyāna treatise explains.

第二、棄瞋恚蓋者，瞋是生諸不善法之根本，墜諸惡道之因緣，法樂之怨家，善心之大賊，種種惡口之府藏。復次，行者於坐時思惟：「此人惱我及惱我親，讚歎我怨。」思惟過去、未來亦如是，是為九惱。惱故生瞋，瞋故生恨，恨故生怨，怨故欲加報惱彼。瞋、恨、怨、惱覺觀覆心，故名為蓋。當急棄之，無令增長。如釋提婆那以偈問佛：
何物殺安隱？何物殺無憂？
何物毒之根，吞滅一切善？
佛以偈答言：
殺瞋則安隱，殺瞋則無憂。
瞋為毒之根，瞋滅一切善。
如是知已，當修慈忍，以除滅之，令心清淨，如《摩訶衍》中佛教弟子訶瞋偈，是中應廣說。

Second, abandoning the hindrance of anger: anger is the very root from which every unwholesome dharma arises, the condition that causes one to fall into the evil destinies, the enemy of the joy of the Dharma, the great thief of the wholesome mind, the storehouse of every kind of evil speech. Furthermore, a practitioner, while sitting, may reflect: "This person has vexed me, and has vexed those dear to me, and has praised those I resent." Reflecting in the same way about the past and the future gives nine grounds for vexation in all. From vexation, anger arises; from anger, resentment arises; from resentment, enmity arises; and from enmity comes the wish to retaliate and vex the other in turn. Anger, resentment, enmity, and vexation, together with coarse and subtle discursive thought about them, cover over the mind — hence this is called a hindrance. It must be swiftly abandoned, and not allowed to grow. As Śakra, Lord of the Devas, once asked the Buddha in verse:
"What thing, once slain, brings peace? What thing, once slain, brings freedom from sorrow?
What is the very root of poison, that swallows up and destroys every wholesome thing?"
The Buddha answered in verse:
"Slay anger, and peace is attained; slay anger, and freedom from sorrow is attained.
Anger is the very root of poison; anger destroys every wholesome thing."
Knowing this, one should cultivate loving-kindness and patience in order to remove and extinguish it, purifying the mind, as the verse in which the Buddha teaches his disciples to reject anger, in the Mahāyāna treatise, explains in full.

Reading note: The "nine grounds for vexation" (九惱) generalize a single grievance across three tenses (past, present, future) and three targets (harm to me, harm to those I love, praise of those I resent) — a compact but exhaustive map of how one triggering thought multiplies into sustained anger. Notice the chain it produces: vexation → anger → resentment → enmity → the wish to retaliate, each step compounding the last.

第三、訶睡眠蓋者，內心惛暗，名為睡；放恣支節，委臥垂熟，名為眠。復次，意識惛冥，名為睡；五情闇蔽惛熟，名為眠。以是因緣，名為睡眠蓋。《阿毘曇》中說：「為增心數法，能破今世三事，謂樂、利樂、福德；又能破今世、後世實樂。」如此惡法，最為不善。何以故？餘蓋情覺可除，眠如死人，無所覺識。以不覺故，難可除滅。如有菩薩教睡眠弟子言：
汝起勿抱死屍臥，種種不淨假名人，
如得重病箭入體，諸苦痛集安可眠？
如人被縛將去殺，災害垂至安可眠？
結賊未滅害未除，如共毒蛇同室居，
亦如臨陣白刃間，爾時云何而可眠？
眠為大暗無所見，日日欺誑奪人明。
以眠覆心無所見，如是大失安可眠？
如是等種種訶眠蓋，警覺無常，滅損睡眠，令無惛覆。若睡眠心重，當用禪鎮、禪杖等却之也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, rejecting the hindrance of sleep: dullness and darkness within the mind is called drowsiness; the limbs going slack, the body sinking into deep repose, is called slumber. Furthermore, dullness and obscurity in mental consciousness is called drowsiness; darkness and obscuration covering the five faculties of feeling, sunk into deep repose, is called slumber. For these reasons, this is called the hindrance of sleep. As the Abhidharma says: "As a mental factor that grows stronger the more it is indulged, it is able to destroy three things in the present life — happiness, benefit and joy, and merit — and is further able to destroy the true happiness of both this life and lives to come." Such an unwholesome dharma as this is the most unwholesome of all. Why is this? The remaining hindrances can still be recognized and removed by feeling and awareness, but sleep is like a dead person, possessing no awareness or consciousness whatsoever. Because there is no awareness of it, it is hard to remove and extinguish.

There was once a bodhisattva who taught a sleeping disciple, saying:
"Rise — do not lie there embracing a corpse, this provisionally-named person made of every impurity;
As one struck by a grave illness, an arrow lodged in the body, every pain and suffering gathered — how can you sleep?
As one who is bound and being led away to execution, disaster imminent — how can you sleep?
The thief of the fetters not yet slain, the harm not yet removed — it is like dwelling in the same room as a venomous snake,
Or standing amid drawn blades on the very edge of battle — how, at such a time, could you possibly sleep?
Sleep is a great darkness in which nothing can be seen, day after day deceiving and robbing a person of clarity.
With sleep covering the mind, nothing at all can be seen — with such a great loss as this, how can you sleep?"
Through various means such as these, the hindrance of sleep is rejected, awakening one to impermanence and diminishing sleep, so that no dullness remains to cover the mind. If the mind is heavily weighed down by sleep, a meditation-weight or meditation-staff and the like should be used to drive it off.

第四、棄掉悔者，掉有三種：一、身；二、口；三、心。身掉者，身好遊走諸雜戲謔，坐不暫安。口掉者，好喜吟詠，諍競是非，無益談論及世俗言話等。心掉者，心情放蕩，縱意攀緣，思惟文藝、世間才技、諸惡覺觀等，名為心掉。掉之為法，破出家心，如人攝心猶不得定，何況掉散。掉散之人，如無鉤醉象、穴鼻駱駝，不可禁制，如偈說：
汝已剃頭著染衣，執持瓦鉢行乞食，
云何樂著戲掉法，放逸縱情失法利？
既無法利，又失世樂，覺其過已，當急棄之。悔者若掉，無悔則不成蓋。何以故？掉時未在緣中，故後欲入定時，大悔前所作，憂惱覆心，故名為蓋。復次，悔有二種：一者、因掉後生悔，如前說；二者、如大重罪人，常懷怖畏，悔箭入心，堅不可拔。如偈說：
不應作而作，應作而不作，
煩惱火所燒，後世墮惡道。
若人罪能悔，悔已莫復憂，
如是心安樂，不應常念著。
若有二種悔，若應作不作，
不應作而作，是則愚人相。
不以心悔故，不作而能作，
諸惡事已作，不能令不作。
如是種種因緣訶掉悔蓋，心神清淨，無有覆蓋，常在善心，則寂然安樂。以是因緣，心得法喜。

Fourth, abandoning restlessness and regret. Restlessness is of three kinds: bodily, verbal, and mental. Bodily restlessness is a fondness for wandering about and engaging in every kind of frivolous jesting, unable to sit still even briefly. Verbal restlessness is a fondness for chanting and reciting, contending over right and wrong, and idle conversation and worldly talk of no benefit. Mental restlessness is a mind given over to dissipation, indulging the intention to chase after objects, occupying itself with literary arts, worldly skills and talents, and every kind of unwholesome discursive thought — this is called mental restlessness. Restlessness as a phenomenon breaks down the very mind of one who has gone forth from home; even one who gathers the mind carefully still fails to attain concentration — how much more so one who is restless and scattered? A person who is restless and scattered is like an intoxicated elephant with no hook to guide it, or a camel with a pierced nose that cannot be controlled — as the verse says:
"You have already shaved your head and put on the dyed robe, taking up the clay bowl to walk for alms —
Why, then, delight in attachment to frivolous, restless ways, indulging laxly and losing the benefit of the Dharma?"
Having thus lost both the benefit of the Dharma and worldly happiness alike, once this fault is recognized, it must be swiftly abandoned.

As for regret, if it follows restlessness, then regret alone does not constitute a hindrance. Why is this? At the very moment of restlessness, the mind is not yet fixed on any object — but afterward, when one wishes to enter concentration, great regret arises over what was done before, and worry and vexation cover the mind; hence this is called a hindrance. Furthermore, regret is of two kinds: first, regret arising afterward because of restlessness, as just described; second, regret like that of a person who has committed a grave and serious offense, constantly harboring fear, the arrow of regret lodged firmly in the heart and unable to be pulled out. As the verse says:
"What should not be done, is done; what should be done, is left undone —
Burned by the fire of affliction, one falls, in lives to come, into an evil destiny.
If a person is able to repent of an offense, once repented, let there be no further worry —
With a mind thus at ease and joyful, one should not dwell on it constantly.
But if there are these two kinds of regret — over what should have been done and was not,
Or over what should not have been done and was — this is the mark of the foolish.
It is not that, through regretting in the mind, what was left undone can now be done,
Nor that whatever evil has already been done can be made undone."
Through causes and conditions such as these, the hindrance of restlessness and regret is rejected; the mind and spirit become pure, with no covering hindrance remaining, constantly abiding in a wholesome mind, and thus becoming still and at ease. Through this, the mind attains the joy of the Dharma.

第五、棄疑蓋者，以疑覆故，於諸法中不得定心。定心無故，於佛法中空無所獲，譬如人入寶山，若無有手，無所能取。復次，通疑甚多，未必障定。今正障定疑者，謂三種疑：一者、疑自；二者、疑師；三者、疑法。疑自者，若人作是念：「我諸根暗鈍，罪垢深重，非其器乎！」作此自疑，定法終不發也。欲去之者，無得自輕，以宿世善根難測故。二、疑師者：「彼人威儀相貌如是，自尚無道，何能教我？」作是疑慢，即為障定。欲除之法，如《摩訶衍》中說：「如臭皮囊中金，以貪金故，不可棄臭皮囊。行者亦爾，師雖不清淨，亦應生佛想。」此事如《摩訶衍》中釋薩陀波淪求善知識具明，是中應廣說。三、疑法者，世人多執本心，於所受之法不能即信，故不敬心受行。若心生猶豫，即法不染神。何以故？如訶疑偈中說：
如人在岐道，疑惑無所取，
諸法實相中，疑亦復如是。
疑故不勤求，諸法之實相，
是疑從癡生，惡中之惡者。
善不善法中，生死及涅槃，
定實真有法，於中莫生疑。
汝若懷疑惑，死生獄吏縛，
如師子搏鹿，不能得解脫。
在世雖有疑，當隨妙善法，
譬如觀岐道，利好者應逐。
復次，佛法之中，信為能入；若無信者，雖在佛法，終無所獲。如是等種種因緣覺知疑過，當急棄之。

Fifth, abandoning the hindrance of doubt: because doubt covers the mind, a concentrated mind cannot be attained with regard to any dharma whatsoever. And because a concentrated mind is absent, one gains nothing at all within the Buddhadharma — like a person entering a mountain of treasure who, having no hands, is unable to take anything at all. Furthermore, doubt in general is of very many kinds, and not all of it necessarily obstructs concentration. The doubt that truly obstructs concentration here refers to three specific kinds: doubt about oneself; doubt about one's teacher; and doubt about the Dharma.

Doubt about oneself: if a person thinks, "My faculties are dull and clouded, my offenses deep and grave — am I truly a fit vessel for this?" — giving rise to this self-doubt, the dharma of concentration will, in the end, never be brought forth. To remove this, one must not belittle oneself, since the depth of one's wholesome roots from past lives is difficult to measure.

Doubt about one's teacher: "That person's dignified bearing and outward appearance are of such a kind — they themselves lack the path, so how could they possibly teach me?" Giving rise to this kind of doubt and arrogance directly obstructs concentration. The method for removing it is as the Mahāyāna treatise says: "It is like gold within a foul-smelling leather sack — because one desires the gold, one does not discard the foul sack on that account. The practitioner should be exactly the same: even though a teacher may not be entirely pure, one should still give rise to the perception of them as a buddha." This matter is explained fully where the Mahāyāna treatise recounts Sadāprarudita's own seeking of a good spiritual friend — this should be explained in full there.

Doubt about the Dharma: people of the world mostly cling to their own original mind, unable to place immediate faith in the Dharma they have received, and so do not receive and practice it with a reverent mind. If the mind gives rise to hesitation and vacillation, the Dharma cannot color the spirit. Why is this? As the verse rejecting doubt says:
"Like a person standing at a forked road, doubtful and unsure, grasping at nothing —
So it is, exactly the same, with doubt regarding the true reality of all dharmas.
Because of doubt, one fails to diligently seek the true reality of all dharmas;
This doubt itself is born from delusion, and is the very worst among evils.
Amid wholesome and unwholesome dharmas, amid birth-and-death and nirvāṇa,
There is a fixed, real, and true dharma present — do not give rise to doubt regarding it.
If you harbor doubt and confusion, you are bound as though by the jailer of death and rebirth,
Like a deer seized in a lion's grip, unable to attain liberation.
While still in the world, though doubt may arise, one should follow the sublime and wholesome Dharma —
Just as, observing a forked road, one should follow whichever path is favorable and good."
Furthermore, within the Buddhadharma, faith alone is what allows one to enter; without faith, though one may remain within the Buddhadharma, nothing at all is ultimately gained. Through causes and conditions such as these, the fault of doubt is to be recognized, and it must be swiftly abandoned.

問曰：不善法塵無量，何故但棄五法？答曰：此五蓋中即有三毒等分為根本，亦得攝八萬四千塵勞門。所以者何？貪欲蓋即貪毒，瞋恚蓋即瞋毒，睡及疑此二蓋共為癡毒，當知即具三毒。掉悔蓋通從三毒起，即等分攝，合為四分煩惱。一中即有二萬一千，四中合有八萬四千，是故除此五蓋，即是除一切不善之法。行者如是等種種因緣棄於五蓋，譬如負債得脫，重病得差，如饑餓之人得至豐國，如於怨賊中得自免濟，安隱無患。行者亦如是，除此五蓋，其心安隱，清淨快樂。譬如日月以五事覆翳：煙、雲、塵、霧、羅睺阿修羅手障，則不能照。人亦如是。

Question: The unwholesome dharmas and defiling objects are measureless in number — why abandon only these five? Answer: Within these five hindrances are contained the three poisons together with the equal-portion affliction as their very root, and they are also able to encompass the eighty-four thousand gates of worldly affliction. Why is this? The hindrance of desire is precisely the poison of craving; the hindrance of anger is precisely the poison of aversion; the hindrances of sleep and doubt together constitute the poison of delusion — hence it can be known that the three poisons are already contained within them. The hindrance of restlessness and regret arises generally from all three poisons at once, and so belongs to the equal-portion affliction — together making four divisions of affliction in all. Within each one of these there are twenty-one thousand, and across all four together there are eighty-four thousand — hence removing these five hindrances is precisely removing every unwholesome dharma whatsoever.

The practitioner who abandons the five hindrances through causes and conditions such as these is like one freed from debt, or cured of a grave illness, or a starving person who has reached a land of plenty, or one who has escaped and been rescued from among enemies and thieves — at ease, in peace, and free of affliction. The practitioner is exactly the same: once these five hindrances are removed, the mind is at ease, pure, and joyful. It is like the sun and moon obscured by five things — smoke, cloud, dust, mist, and the hand of the asura Rāhu — unable to shine forth; a person is exactly the same.

Reading note: This is the treatise's canonical account of the five hindrances (五蓋, nīvaraṇa) — sensory desire, ill will, sloth-and-torpor, restlessness-and-remorse, and doubt — a list so foundational to Buddhist meditation instruction generally that it recurs across nearly every tradition's practice manuals to this day. Two details are worth flagging: the fourfold mapping onto the three poisons plus "equal-portion" affliction (with the arithmetic 21,000 × 4 = 84,000 explaining the traditional count of "eighty-four thousand afflictions" found throughout Chinese Buddhist literature), and the specifically Buddhist analysis of "restlessness and regret" as two related but distinguishable problems — restlessness during the act itself, regret arising only afterward — rather than a single undifferentiated agitation. This closes 棄五蓋 (abandoning the five hindrances), the third of the twenty-five preliminary conditions' five groups.

Harmonizing the Five Things 調五法

The fourth group is where this treatise becomes, unmistakably, a practical meditation manual — food, sleep, posture, breath, and mind, worked through in the kind of hands-on detail a beginning practitioner could actually follow. This is likely the single most quoted and most practically useful passage in the whole work.

Chinese Source

第四、調五法者：一者、調節飲食；二者、調節眠睡；三者、調身；四者、調氣息；五者、調心。所以者何？今借近譬以況斯法，如世陶師，欲造眾器，先須善巧調泥，令使不強不軟，然後可就輪繩。亦如彈琴，先應調絃，令寬急得所，方可入哢，出諸妙曲。行者修心亦復如是，善調五事，必使和適，則三昧易生；若有所不調，多諸妨難，善根難發。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, harmonizing the five things: first, regulating food and drink; second, regulating sleep; third, harmonizing the body; fourth, harmonizing the breath; fifth, harmonizing the mind. Why is this? Here, a familiar comparison may be borrowed to illustrate this method. It is like a potter who, wishing to fashion various vessels, must first skillfully knead the clay, making it neither too hard nor too soft, before it can be set upon the turning wheel and cord. It is also like tuning a lute: the strings must first be tuned, neither too slack nor too taut, before one can begin to play and produce every wondrous melody from it. The practitioner cultivating the mind is exactly the same: by skillfully harmonizing these five things, ensuring they are properly attuned, samādhi arises with ease; but if any of them is left unharmonized, many obstacles and difficulties arise, and wholesome roots are hard to bring forth.

第一、調食者，夫食之為法本，欲資身進道，食若過飽，則氣急身滿，百脈不通，令心閉塞，坐念不安。若食過少，則身羸心懸，意慮不固。此皆非得定之道。復次，若食穢濁之物，令人心識惛迷；若食不宜身物，則動宿疾，使四大違反。此為修定之初，深須慎之，故云身安則道隆。經云：「飯食知節量，常樂在閑處，心靜樂精進，是名諸佛教。」第二、調睡眠者，夫眠是無明惑覆之法，雖不可縱之，若都不眠，則心神虛恍；若其眠寐過多，非唯廢修聖法，亦復空喪功夫，令心暗晦，善根沈沒。當覺悟無常，調伏睡眠，令神道清白，念心明淨，如是乃可棲心聖境，三昧現前。故經云：「初夜、後夜亦勿有廢，無以睡眠因緣，令一生空過，無所得也。當念無常之火燒諸世間，早求自度，勿睡眠也。」

First, regulating food: food is the very basis of the dharma of sustaining the body in order to advance on the path. If one eats to excess, the breath becomes constricted and the body feels full, the hundred channels fail to circulate, the mind becomes stopped up, and sitting meditation is left unsettled. If one eats too little, the body grows weak and the mind restless, unable to settle its thoughts firmly. Neither of these is the way to attain concentration. Furthermore, if one eats impure and turbid things, the mind and consciousness become dull and confused; if one eats things unsuited to one's own constitution, dormant illnesses are stirred up and the four elements thrown into disorder. This is the very beginning of cultivating concentration, and must be attended to with the greatest care — hence it is said, "when the body is at ease, the path flourishes." As the sūtra says: "Eating with a knowledge of proper measure, constantly delighting in dwelling in a quiet place, with a still mind delighting in diligence — this is what every buddha has taught."

Second, regulating sleep: sleep is a dharma covered over by the delusion of ignorance; though it should not be indulged, if one does not sleep at all, the mind and spirit become empty and vacant; and if one sleeps to excess, this not only neglects the cultivation of the noble Dharma, but also wastes one's effort in vain, darkening and clouding the mind and causing wholesome roots to sink beneath the surface. One should awaken to impermanence and subdue sleep, so that the pathway of the spirit becomes pure and bright, and the mindful heart becomes clear and undefiled — only in this way can the mind come to dwell in the noble realm, with samādhi manifesting before it. Hence the sūtra says: "Do not neglect the first and last watches of the night either — do not let the cause and condition of sleep cause an entire life to pass by in vain, gaining nothing at all. One should be mindful that the fire of impermanence burns all the worlds, and swiftly seek one's own liberation, rather than sleeping."

Body, Breath, and Mind: Entering Meditation 調身息心 · 入

Chinese Source

第三、調身；第四、調息；第五、調心。此應合用，不得別說，但有初、中、後方法不同，是則入住出相有異。第一、入禪調三事者，行人欲入三昧，調身之宜，若在定外，行住進止，動靜運為，悉須詳審。若所作麁獷，則氣息隨麁。以氣麁故，則心散難錄。兼復坐時煩憒，心不恬怡，是以雖在定外，亦須用心逆作方便。後入禪時，須善安身得所。初至繩床，即前安坐處，每令安隱，久久無妨。次當正脚，若半跏坐，以左脚置右髀上，牽來近身，令左脚指與右髀齊，右脚指與左髀齊。若欲全跏，即上下右脚䟤置左脚上。次解寬衣帶周正，不令坐時脫落。次當安手，以左掌置右手上，重累手相對，頓置左脚上，牽近身，當心而安，正身，先當挺動其身，并諸支節，作七八反，如自按摩法，勿令手足差異。竟即正身端直，令脊相對，勿曲勿聳。次正頭頸，令鼻與臍相對，不偏不邪，不低不昂，平面正住。次開口吐胸中穢氣。吐法：開口放氣，自恣而出，想身分中百脈不通處，教悉隨氣而出盡；閉口，鼻中內清氣。如是至三。若身息調和，但一亦足。次當閉口，脣齒纔相拄著，舌向上齶。次當閉眼，纔令斷外光而已。當端身正坐，猶如奠石，無得身首四支竊爾搔動。是為初入禪定調身之法。舉要言之，不寬不急，是身調相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, harmonizing the body; fourth, harmonizing the breath; fifth, harmonizing the mind. These three should be applied together, and cannot properly be explained separately — though the methods do differ across the beginning, middle, and end, corresponding to the different marks of entering, abiding in, and emerging from meditation.

First, entering dhyāna and harmonizing the three things — beginning with the body. When a practitioner wishes to enter samādhi, the harmonizing of the body should begin even before formal concentration: walking, standing, moving forward, and coming to rest, every motion and stillness of the body, must all be carried out with careful attention. If one's actions are coarse and unrefined, the breath will follow suit and become coarse; and because the breath is coarse, the mind becomes scattered and hard to gather in. Moreover, if one is agitated and disturbed at the time of sitting, the mind will not be calm and at ease — hence, even while still outside formal concentration, one must already apply the mind in anticipation, preparing the way with skillful means.

When one then actually enters dhyāna, the body must be skillfully settled into its proper place. Upon first reaching the meditation seat, at the place where one is to sit, one should always make it comfortable and secure, so that no difficulty arises even after a long time. Next, the legs should be properly arranged: in the half-lotus posture, place the left foot upon the right thigh, drawing it close to the body, so that the toes of the left foot align with the right thigh and the toes of the right foot align with the left thigh. If the full lotus posture is desired, then further draw the right foot up and place it upon the left foot as well. Next, loosen and properly adjust the belt of one's robe, so that it does not come loose while sitting. Next, the hands should be settled: place the left palm upon the right hand, one resting upon the other, facing each other, set upon the left foot, drawn close to the body, and settled directly before the heart. Straighten the body: first sway and stretch the body and every limb and joint, doing this seven or eight times, as in a self-massage, taking care that the hands and feet are not left out of alignment. Once this is done, straighten the body directly upright, so that the spine is properly aligned, neither bent nor thrust upward. Next, straighten the head and neck, so that the nose is aligned directly with the navel, neither tilted nor crooked, neither lowered nor raised, the face level and settled straight ahead. Next, open the mouth and exhale the impure air from within the chest. The method of exhaling: open the mouth and release the breath, letting it flow out freely, imagining that, at every place within the body where the hundred channels are obstructed, the breath carries this obstruction entirely out with it as it leaves; then close the mouth and draw pure air in through the nose. Do this up to three times; if the body and breath are already well harmonized, even a single time is sufficient. Next, the mouth should be closed, the lips and teeth lightly touching, the tongue resting against the upper palate. Next, the eyes should be closed, allowing only enough light to be shut out and no more. The body should then be held upright and sitting straight, as steady as a placed stone, with no stealthy shifting or scratching of the body, head, or any of the four limbs. This is the method of harmonizing the body upon first entering dhyāna-concentration. Taking the essential point: neither too slack nor too tense — this is the mark of a properly harmonized body.

第二、初入禪調息法者。息調凡有四相：一、風；二、喘；三、氣；四、息。前三為不調相，後一為調相。云何風相？坐時鼻中息出入覺有聲。云何喘相？坐時雖無聲，而出入結滯不通，是喘相。云何氣相？坐時雖無聲，亦不結滯，而出入不細，是名氣相。息相者，不聲、不結、不麁，出入綿綿，若存若亡，資神安隱，情抱悅豫，此是息相。守風則散，守喘則結，守氣則勞，守息則定。復次，坐時有風、氣等三相，是名不調，而用心者則為患也，心亦難定。若欲調之，當依三法：一者、下著安心；二者、寬身體；三者、想氣遍毛孔，出入通同無障。若細其心，令息微微然，息調則眾患不生，其心易定。是名行者初入定時調息方法。舉要言之，不澁不滑，是息調相。第三、初入定調心者。調心有二義：一者、調伏亂念，不令越逸；二者、當令沈浮寬急得所。何等為沈相？若坐時心中昏暗，無所記錄，頭好低垂，是為沈相。爾時當繫念鼻端，令心住在緣中，無令散意，此可治沈。何等為浮相？若坐時心神飄動，身亦不安，念在異緣，此是浮相。爾時宜安心向下係緣，制諸亂念，心則定住，此則心易安靜。舉要言之，不沈不浮，是心調相。問曰：心得有寬急相不？答曰：亦有此事。心急相者，由坐中撮心，用念望得，因此入定，是故氣上向，胸臆急痛。當寬放其心，想氣流下，患自差矣。若心寬相者，覺心志遊漫，身好萎蛇，或口涎流，或時暗晦，爾時應當歛身急念，令心住在緣中，身體相持，以此為治。心有澁滑之相，推之可知。是為初入定時調心方法。欲入定時，本是從麁入細，是以身既為麁，息居其中，心最為細，以善方便調麁就細，令心安靜，此則入定初方便也。

Second, the method of harmonizing the breath upon first entering dhyāna. The harmonizing of the breath has, in all, four marks: wind, panting, breath-with-effort, and true breath. The first three are unharmonized marks; the last alone is the harmonized mark. What is the mark of wind? While sitting, the breath going in and out through the nose is felt to make a sound. What is the mark of panting? While sitting, though there is no sound, the breath going in and out is obstructed and does not flow smoothly — this is the mark of panting. What is the mark of breath-with-effort? While sitting, though there is no sound and no obstruction, the breath going in and out is not fine and subtle — this is called the mark of breath-with-effort. As for the mark of true breath: no sound, no obstruction, no coarseness, going in and out continuously and finely, as though it were present yet almost imperceptible, sustaining the spirit at ease, the feelings held in quiet contentment — this is the mark of true breath. Dwelling on wind leads to scatteredness; dwelling on panting leads to obstruction; dwelling on breath-with-effort leads to fatigue; dwelling on true breath leads to concentration.

Furthermore, while sitting, if the marks of wind, panting, or breath-with-effort are present, this is called unharmonized, and applying the mind while in this state becomes itself a source of trouble, making the mind hard to concentrate. To harmonize it, one should rely on three methods: first, settling the mind low and at ease; second, loosening and relaxing the body; third, imagining the breath pervading every pore, going in and out uniformly and without obstruction. If the mind is made fine and subtle in this way, the breath will naturally become faint and gentle; once the breath is harmonized, every trouble ceases to arise, and the mind concentrates with ease. This is the method of harmonizing the breath for a practitioner first entering concentration. Taking the essential point: neither rough nor slippery — this is the mark of a properly harmonized breath.

Third, harmonizing the mind upon first entering concentration. Harmonizing the mind has two aspects: first, subduing scattered thoughts, not allowing the mind to run wild and escape; second, ensuring that sinking and floating, slackness and tension, are each brought to their proper measure. What is the mark of sinking? If, while sitting, the mind is dark and dull, retaining nothing, the head inclined to droop — this is the mark of sinking. At such a time, one should fix the mind at the tip of the nose, holding it steady upon this object, not allowing the intention to scatter — this can remedy sinking. What is the mark of floating? If, while sitting, the mind and spirit drift and stir restlessly, the body also unsettled, thoughts wandering off to other objects — this is the mark of floating. At such a time, one should settle the mind, directing it downward and fixing it upon its object, restraining every scattered thought, and the mind will then come to rest in concentration — in this way the mind is easily made calm and still. Taking the essential point: neither sinking nor floating — this is the mark of a properly harmonized mind.

Question: Can the mind also have marks of slackness and tension? Answer: This too occurs. The mark of a tense mind arises from gathering the mind too forcefully during sitting, applying thought in eager anticipation of results; because of this, when entering concentration, the breath rises upward and the chest feels tight and pained. One should loosen and relax the mind, imagining the breath flowing downward, and the trouble will naturally subside. As for the mark of a slack mind: one becomes aware that the mind's intent has grown loose and wandering, the body inclined to slump and sag, perhaps drooling from the mouth, or at times growing dull and clouded; at such a time, one should draw the body in and apply thought more firmly, holding the mind steady upon its object, body and mind mutually sustaining one another — this is the remedy. The mind may also have marks of roughness or slipperiness, which can be understood by the same reasoning. This is the method of harmonizing the mind upon first entering concentration.

When one wishes to enter concentration, the movement is, by its very nature, from the coarse into the fine: the body, being coarsest, comes first; the breath, situated between, comes next; the mind, being finest of all, comes last. By skillful means, harmonizing the coarse until it becomes fine, the mind is settled and stilled — this is the initial expedient means for entering concentration.

Reading note: This is the treatise's fullest, most concrete instruction so far — full-lotus and half-lotus posture, the specific sequence of settling hands and spine, the four grades of breath from audible "wind" down to true, imperceptible breath, and a genuinely practical taxonomy of what can go wrong with the meditating mind (sinking versus floating, tense versus slack) paired with a specific remedy for each. The overall logic — body first, then breath, then mind, moving from coarse to fine — recurs as the organizing principle for the rest of this section: the same three things, and the same coarse-to-fine movement, are worked through again for abiding in meditation and for emerging from it.

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, harmonizing the three things while abiding in sitting meditation. Throughout the course of a single sitting, however long or short it may last, the mind should be gathered and applied with continuous care. Here, one should skillfully recognize the harmonized and unharmonized marks of the three things — body, breath, and mind.

While sitting, even if the body was properly harmonized at the outset, it may come to be too slack or too tense, tilted or bent, uneven in its posture — once this is noticed, it should be corrected at once, always kept comfortable and secure, neither slack nor tense throughout, level and upright.

Furthermore, throughout the sitting, even if the body remains harmonized, the breath may become unharmonized — its unharmonized marks are as described above: wind or panting, or breath-with-effort, with the body feeling swollen and full. This should be remedied at once using the methods already given, always keeping the breath's path continuous and fine, as though present and yet almost imperceptible.

Furthermore, throughout a single sitting, even if body and breath remain harmonized, the mind may come to sink or float, become too slack or too tense. If this is noticed at such a time, it should be adjusted back to its proper measure using the methods already given.

These three things have no fixed order among them — whichever becomes unharmonized should be adjusted accordingly, so that, throughout the course of a single sitting, body, breath, and mind remain harmonized together, without contradiction, blended and undivided. In this way, longstanding troubles can be removed, no obstacle or difficulty arises, and the achievement of concentration can be attained.

進止有次第，麁細不相違，
譬如善調馬，欲去而欲住。

Third, when sitting meditation is about to conclude and one wishes to emerge from concentration, one should first release the mind toward other objects, open the mouth and release the breath, imagining it dispersing at will through the hundred channels of the body; then gently move the body, next the shoulders and neck, then the two feet, making all of them supple; then use the hands to massage every pore of the skin, next rub the hands until they are warm and cover the two eyes with them, then withdraw the hands and open the eyes, waiting until the body's warmth and perspiration have gradually subsided before moving and resting freely as one wishes. If this is not done — if, for instance, the mind was in the midst of settled concentration and one emerges too abruptly — the subtle qualities of that state will not yet have dispersed and will remain lodged within the body, causing headaches and stiffness throughout the hundred joints, much like a rheumatic disorder, resulting in restlessness and unease in later sittings, so that the mind becomes reluctant to sit at all. This point must always be kept in mind — this is the method for harmonizing body, breath, and mind upon emerging from concentration, moving from the fine back out into the coarse. This is what is meant by skillfully entering, abiding in, and emerging from concentration. As the verse says:
"Advancing and resting each have their own proper sequence, the coarse and the fine never at odds with one another —
Just as with a well-trained horse, whether it is made to go or made to stay."

Reading note: The warning about emerging from meditation too abruptly — headaches, joint stiffness, a rheumatic-like malaise, and a resulting reluctance ever to sit again — is notably specific and clinical for a text this old, and matches lived experience reported across many later meditation traditions: how a session ends matters nearly as much as how it begins. This closes 調五法 (harmonizing the five things), the fourth of the twenty-five preliminary conditions' five groups, and the treatise's fullest practical instruction so far.

Practicing the Five Dharmas 行五法

The fifth and final group of the twenty-five preliminary conditions closes topic 6 — five qualities of mind a practitioner actively cultivates, rather than external circumstances arranged or internal obstacles removed.

Chinese Source

第五、行五法者：一、欲；二、精進；三、念；四、巧慧；五、一心。欲者，行人初修禪時，欲從欲界中出，欲得初禪，故亦名為志，亦名為願，亦名為樂，是人內心志願好樂諸禪定故。問曰：悕望心生，於修禪中則為妨礙，云何以此為方便耶？答曰：夫欲者，秖是大志成就願樂之心，故名為欲，不應於用心時起悕望憶想之念。若悕望心起，則不澄靜；若心不澄靜，則諸三昧無由得發矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, practicing the five dharmas: first, aspiration; second, diligence; third, mindfulness; fourth, skillful wisdom; fifth, one-pointedness of mind.

Aspiration: when a practitioner first cultivates dhyāna, wishing to emerge from the desire realm and attain the first dhyāna, this is also called resolve, also called vow, also called delight — since the practitioner's inner mind is resolved upon and delights in the various dhyānas. Question: If a mind of longing and expectation arises, does this not become an obstacle to cultivating dhyāna — how, then, can this be taken as an expedient means? Answer: "Aspiration" here means simply the mind of great resolve accomplishing its vowed delight — hence it is called aspiration; it does not mean that, while actually applying the mind in practice, one should give rise to thoughts of longing and expectant recollection. If a mind of longing and expectation arises, the mind will not be clear and still; and if the mind is not clear and still, no samādhi whatsoever can ever be brought forth.

二、精進者，有二種：一、身精進；二、心精進。行者若能修十二頭陀，即是具足身心精進。如佛告迦葉：「阿蘭若比丘，遠離二著，形心清淨，行頭陀。頭陀者，有十二事：一、阿蘭若處；二、常行乞食；三、次第乞食；四、受一食法；五、節量食；六、中後不飲漿；七、著弊衣；八、但三衣；九、塚間住；十、樹下止；十一、露地坐；十二、常坐不臥。是名十二頭陀。」如《頭陀經》中所明，是中應廣說。頭陀者，名抖擻，抖擻身心諸不善法故。若修禪時，行此等法，是名不放逸行，具足身心精進。當知此人能得三乘聖果，何況世間禪定。復次，行者為修禪故，持戒清淨，棄捨五蓋，初夜、後夜專精不廢，譬如鑽火未然，終不休息，是名精進。如佛告阿難：「諸佛一心勤精進，故得三菩提。」何況餘善道法？

Second, diligence: this is of two kinds — bodily diligence and mental diligence. If a practitioner is able to cultivate the twelve dhutanga practices, this is precisely being complete in both bodily and mental diligence. As the Buddha told Kāśyapa: "A forest-dwelling monk, far removed from the two kinds of attachment, pure in body and mind, practices the dhutanga austerities. The dhutanga practices are twelve in number: first, dwelling in a forest hermitage; second, constantly practicing alms-begging; third, begging for alms in sequence without discrimination; fourth, receiving only a single meal a day; fifth, eating with moderation; sixth, not drinking any liquid after midday; seventh, wearing discarded, worn-out robes; eighth, keeping only the three robes; ninth, dwelling among graves; tenth, resting beneath trees; eleventh, sitting in the open air; twelfth, constantly sitting and never lying down. These are called the twelve dhutanga practices." This is explained in full in the Dhutanga Sūtra, and should be given in full detail there.

"Dhutanga" means "shaking off" — shaking off every unwholesome dharma of body and mind. If, while cultivating dhyāna, one practices these methods, this is called conduct free of laxity, complete in both bodily and mental diligence. One should know that such a person is able to attain the noble fruits of all three vehicles — how much more so the dhyāna of the ordinary world?

Furthermore, a practitioner who, for the sake of cultivating dhyāna, upholds the precepts purely, abandons the five hindrances, and applies undivided effort without neglect through the first and last watches of the night — like drilling for fire without ever pausing until the fire catches — this is called diligence. As the Buddha told Ānanda: "Every buddha, with a single-pointed mind, diligently applies effort, and therefore attains the three kinds of bodhi." How much more so, then, every other wholesome path?

三、念者，如《摩訶衍》中說：「念欲界不淨，欺誑可賤，念初禪為尊重可貴。」此與六行意同，但立名異。六行觀者：一、厭下苦、麁、障為三，即是觀欲不淨，欺誑可賤；攀上勝、妙、出為三，即是觀初禪為尊重可貴。今釋六法，自可為二意：一、約果明；二約因明。先約欲界果明，言「厭下苦、麁、障」者，厭患欲界底下色心麁重故。行者思惟：今感欲界報身，饑渴、寒熱、病痛、刀杖等種種所逼，故名苦。麁者，此身為三十六物屎尿臭穢之所成，故名為麁。麁者，醜陋故。障者，此身質礙，不得自在，為山河石壁所隔礙，故名為障。次約色界果明「攀上勝」者，行者思惟，知色界樂為上勝故。如欲界樂為苦，色界樂為勝，得樂勝苦，故名上勝。「妙」者，受得色界之身，如鏡中像，雖有形色，無有質礙，故名為妙。「出」者，獲得五通，徹見障外等事，山壁無礙，故名為出。二、明因中六行者，先約欲界因，明厭下苦、麁、障者。行者思惟：若於報身中所起心數緣於貪欲，不能出離，如經說「一切眾生為愛奴僕」，故名為苦。麁者，緣欲界五塵散動起惡，故名為麁。障者，為煩惱蓋覆，故名為障。次約色界因，明攀上勝、妙、出者。行者思惟：初禪上勝之樂從樂內發，故名為上勝。貪欲樂從外五塵生，惱熱怨結，以為下劣，不如妙者禪定之樂，心定不動，而樂法成就，故名為妙。貪欲之樂，心亂馳動，故名為麁。出者，心得出離蓋障，至初禪，故名為出，亦如石泉不從外來，內自涌出。今因此六行釋於念義，意在可見。問曰：今說佛弟子修禪，何用說凡夫六行觀法？答曰：既說三界共禪，亦應知其所行之因。若佛弟子用八聖種，起十六行觀，離欲為念，入初禪，則無過失。在下明無漏禪中當廣分別。

Third, mindfulness: as the Mahāyāna treatise says: "Be mindful that the desire realm is impure, deceptive, and contemptible; be mindful that the first dhyāna is to be honored and cherished." This shares the same sense as the six contemplations, differing only in name. The six contemplations are: growing weary of the lower realm's suffering, coarseness, and obstruction — three in number, which is precisely contemplating desire as impure, deceptive, and contemptible; and reaching for the superiority, subtlety, and transcendence of the higher realm — three more, which is precisely contemplating the first dhyāna as honorable and cherished.

Now, in explaining these six items, there are, in themselves, two aspects: explained in terms of the fruit, and explained in terms of the cause. First, explained in terms of the fruit within the desire realm: as for "growing weary of the lower realm's suffering, coarseness, and obstruction" — this means growing weary of the coarse, heavy body and mind of the lower desire realm. The practitioner reflects: the retributive body received within the desire realm now is pressed upon by hunger and thirst, cold and heat, sickness and pain, blade and rod, and every other kind of affliction — hence it is called suffering. "Coarseness" means that this body is made up of the thirty-six foul, unclean things such as excrement and urine — hence it is called coarse, coarse because it is ugly and repulsive. "Obstruction" means that this body, being made of solid matter, cannot move freely, blocked and hindered by mountains, rivers, and stone walls — hence it is called obstruction.

Next, explained in terms of the fruit within the form realm: as for "reaching for what is superior" — the practitioner reflects and comes to know that the bliss of the form realm is superior. Just as the bliss of the desire realm is suffering, the bliss of the form realm is superior — attaining bliss that surpasses suffering, and so it is called superior. As for "subtlety" — the body received in the form realm is like a reflection in a mirror: though it has form and color, it possesses no material obstruction, and so it is called subtle. As for "transcendence" — attaining the five supernormal powers, one sees clearly through obstructions to what lies beyond, unhindered by mountains and walls, and so it is called transcendence.

Second, clarifying the six contemplations in terms of the cause: first, in terms of the cause within the desire realm, clarifying "growing weary of suffering, coarseness, and obstruction." The practitioner reflects: if, within this retributive body, the mental factors that arise take craving desire as their object and one cannot escape it, then, as the sūtra says, "all sentient beings are slaves to craving" — hence it is called suffering. "Coarseness" means that, taking the five defiling objects of the desire realm as its object, the mind stirs and scatters, giving rise to unwholesome deeds — hence it is called coarse. "Obstruction" means being covered over by the hindrance of affliction — hence it is called obstruction. Next, in terms of the cause within the form realm, clarifying "reaching for what is superior, subtle, and transcendent." The practitioner reflects: the superior bliss of the first dhyāna arises from within the bliss itself — hence it is called superior. The bliss of craving desire arises from the five external defiling objects, bound up with vexation and heat and enmity, and is thus inferior; it is not like the subtle bliss of dhyāna-concentration, in which the mind is settled and unmoving while the dharma of bliss is accomplished — hence it is called subtle. The bliss of craving desire, by contrast, leaves the mind scattered and racing about — hence it is called coarse. "Transcendence" means that the mind attains freedom from the covering hindrances and reaches the first dhyāna — hence it is called transcendence, just as a spring welling up from rock does not come from outside but wells up of itself from within.

This, then, explains the meaning of mindfulness in terms of these six contemplations — the intent should now be clear. Question: Since this passage is describing how a disciple of the Buddha cultivates dhyāna, what use is there in explaining the six contemplations practiced by ordinary, unenlightened people? Answer: Since it has already been said that dhyāna is shared in common across the three realms, one should also know the causes behind whatever is practiced there. If a disciple of the Buddha makes use of the eight noble lineages, gives rise to the sixteen aspects of contemplation, and takes freedom from desire as the object of mindfulness in order to enter the first dhyāna, then there is no fault in this at all. This will be distinguished fully further below, where the uncontaminated dhyānas are clarified.

四、巧慧者，籌量欲界樂、初禪樂得失輕重之相。今翻覆作二釋：言籌量者，即是用智慧思度之名。得失者，欲界樂為失，初禪樂為得。初禪樂無過失，故為得；欲界樂過失，故名為失。亦可言初禪為失，欲界樂為得者，欲界樂麁故，計以為實，生重得心；初禪為失者，覺身空寂，受於細樂，似若無故，不可定取，失樂相貌，故名為失。言輕重者，欲界為輕，初禪為重。欲界輕者，五識相應所得樂迅速，淺故為輕；初禪所得樂重，意識相應久住，緣深故名重。重者，可貴寶重。亦得言欲界為重，初禪為輕者，欲界樂與煩惱俱心累重故，故名重；初禪樂心累少，故名為輕。次有師言巧慧者，行人初修禪時，善識內外方便，巧而用之，不失其宜，疾得禪定，故名巧慧也。五、一心者，行人已善能巧慧籌量，用心無謬，今但應專心守一而行，故名一心。如人欲行善，須識道路通塞之相，決定知已，即一心而去，故說非智不禪，非禪不智，義在此也。

Fourth, skillful wisdom: weighing the marks of gain and loss, light and heavy, between the bliss of the desire realm and the bliss of the first dhyāna. Here two explanations are given, turned over from opposite sides. As to "weighing": this is simply the name for using wisdom to deliberate and measure. As to "gain and loss": the bliss of the desire realm is a loss, and the bliss of the first dhyāna is a gain — since the bliss of the first dhyāna has no fault, it is called a gain, and since the bliss of the desire realm has fault, it is called a loss. It may also be said, conversely, that the first dhyāna is a loss and the bliss of the desire realm is a gain: because the bliss of the desire realm is coarse, it is taken to be real, and gives rise to a strong sense of gain; the first dhyāna, by contrast, is felt as loss because the body is perceived as empty and still, experiencing a subtle bliss that seems almost like nothing at all, unable to be fixed and grasped — losing, in this way, the very appearance of bliss, and so it is called a loss.

As to "light and heavy": the desire realm is light, and the first dhyāna is heavy. The desire realm is light because the bliss obtained there, associated with the five sense-consciousnesses, is swift and shallow. The bliss obtained in the first dhyāna is heavy because it is associated with the mind-consciousness, abides for a long time, and takes a deep object — hence it is called heavy, "heavy" here meaning precious and weighty. It may also be said, conversely, that the desire realm is heavy and the first dhyāna is light: the bliss of the desire realm is heavy because it comes bound up together with affliction, weighing down the mind; the bliss of the first dhyāna is light because the mind is only lightly encumbered. There is also a further teaching that skillful wisdom means this: when a practitioner first cultivates dhyāna, they skillfully recognize the internal and external expedient means and apply them with skill, never losing what is proper to the moment, and thereby swiftly attain dhyāna-concentration — hence this too is called skillful wisdom.

Fifth, one-pointedness of mind: once a practitioner is already able to skillfully weigh matters through wisdom, applying the mind without error, they should now simply devote the mind single-mindedly, holding to the one and proceeding accordingly — hence this is called one-pointedness of mind. It is like a person wishing to travel a good road, who must first know which paths are open and which are blocked, and, having determined this with certainty, then proceeds with a single, undivided mind. Hence it is said: "without wisdom, there is no dhyāna; without dhyāna, there is no wisdom" — the meaning lies exactly here.
