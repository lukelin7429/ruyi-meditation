Fascicle 9 · 釋禪波羅蜜次第法門卷第九

The Nine Contemplations, Eight Recollections, and Ten Perceptions

This page presents Ru-Yi's own English translation of fascicle 9 of Zhiyi's Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen (釋禪波羅蜜次第法門). Having completed dhyāna both mundane and world-transcending in fascicle 8, the treatise now opens its third major division: dhyāna that is purely world-transcending (出世間禪), cultivated through nine traditional dharma-gates split into two paths — the destructive path (壞法道), translated here in full, and the non-destructive path (不壞法道), reserved for fascicle 10. This fascicle contains some of the treatise's starkest material: unflinching, verse-punctuated contemplations of a corpse's decay, composed to sever attachment to the body at its root.

Opening Fascicle Nine 卷第九

Chinese Source

卷第九
隋天台智者大師說
弟子法慎記
弟子灌頂再治
釋禪波羅蜜修證第七之五
明修證無漏禪。今明無漏有二種：一者、對治無漏；二者、緣理無漏。故《大集經》云：「有二種行：一者、慧行；二者、行行。」行行者，即是九想、背捨等對治無漏也，緣事起行，對治破諸煩惱，故名行行無漏行也。二、慧行者，即是四諦、十二因緣真空正觀，緣理斷惑，故名慧行無漏行也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fascicle Nine
Spoken by the Great Master Zhiyi of Tiantai, of the Sui dynasty
Recorded by his disciple Fashen
Revised and edited by his disciple Guanding
Explaining Dhyāna-Pāramitā's Cultivation and Realization, Topic 7, Part 5

Clarifying the cultivation and realization of the undefiled dhyānas. Here, the undefiled is clarified as being of two kinds: first, the undefiled that counteracts affliction; second, the undefiled that takes principle as its object. Hence the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "There are two kinds of practice: first, wisdom-practice; second, practice-practice." Practice-practice is precisely the nine contemplations, the eight forms of detachment, and the rest — the undefiled that counteracts, taking phenomena as its object and giving rise to practice, counteracting and breaking through the afflictions; hence it is named the undefiled practice of practice-practice. Second, wisdom-practice is precisely the true, empty, correct contemplation of the Four Noble Truths and the twelve links of dependent origination — taking principle as its object and severing delusion; hence it is named the undefiled practice of wisdom-practice.

第一、前釋對治無漏，此約九種法門明也。一、九想；二、八念；三、十想；四、八背捨；五、八勝處；六、十一切處；七、九次第定；八、師子奮迅三昧；九、超越三昧。今此九種禪通說為對治無漏及次第淺深之義，皆如前第一卷中說。今就此九種法門中，即有二種對治無漏道：一者、壞法道；二者、不壞法道。壞法道者，即是九想、八念、十想是也。善修此三，若發真無漏，即成壞法阿羅漢也。二、不壞法道，即是背捨、勝處、一切處、九次第定、師子奮迅、超越等三昧。具足此禪，發真無漏，成不壞法大阿羅漢也。

First, explaining the undefiled that counteracts, as clarified with reference to nine dharma-gates: first, the nine contemplations; second, the eight recollections; third, the ten perceptions; fourth, the eight forms of detachment; fifth, the eight stations of mastery; sixth, the ten totalities; seventh, the nine sequential concentrations; eighth, the lion's-frolic samādhi; ninth, the samādhi of transcendence. These nine dhyānas are, in general, explained as the undefiled that counteracts, together with the sense of their own sequence and relative depth — all of which has already been given above, in the treatise's first fascicle.

Now, within these nine dharma-gates, there are precisely two paths belonging to the undefiled that counteracts: first, the destructive path; second, the non-destructive path. The destructive path is precisely the nine contemplations, the eight recollections, and the ten perceptions. Skillfully cultivating these three, should true undefilement arise, one thereby becomes an arhat of the destructive path. Second, the non-destructive path is precisely the eight forms of detachment, the eight stations of mastery, the ten totalities, the nine sequential concentrations, the lion's-frolic samādhi, and the samādhi of transcendence. Complete with these dhyānas, should true undefilement arise, one thereby becomes a great arhat of the non-destructive path.

今通釋第一、壞法觀中三種法門。所以此三法門名壞法觀者，行人心厭六欲，猶如怨賊，故修九想以為對治。作此觀時，雖破壞六欲，而多生恐怖，若修八種正念，恐怖即除，既貪欲心薄，又無怖畏。爾時欲斷三界結使，即應進修十想，十想成就，即便殺諸結賊，成阿羅漢。是人既壞滅欲界身相，不能具足三界觀、練、熏、修、三明、八解，故名壞法也。問曰：九想與十想有何異耶？答曰：有異、不異。異者，九想如縛賊，十想如殺賊；九想為初學，十想為成就；九想為因，十想為果。故經云：「二為甘露門：一者、不淨觀門；二者、阿那波那門。」不異者，善修九想，即具足十想。此義在下當明之。

Here, the three dharma-gates within the destructive contemplation are explained together. As for why these three dharma-gates are named the destructive contemplation: the practitioner's mind, wearied by the six desires as though by a hateful bandit, thereupon cultivates the nine contemplations as their antidote. In performing this contemplation, although the six desires are broken through, considerable fear tends to arise as well; should one then cultivate the eight kinds of correct recollection, this fear is thereupon removed, and, the mind of craving already thinned, one is free of fear as well. At that time, wishing to sever the fetters of the three realms, one should then advance to cultivating the ten perceptions; once the ten perceptions are accomplished, one thereupon slays every bandit of the fetters and becomes an arhat. This person, having thus destroyed the very mark of the desire-realm body, is unable to become complete with the threefold contemplation-refinement-permeation-cultivation of the three realms, the three insights, and the eight liberations — hence this is named the destructive path.

Question: What is the difference between the nine contemplations and the ten perceptions? Answer: there is both difference and no difference. As for the difference: the nine contemplations are like binding the bandit, while the ten perceptions are like slaying the bandit; the nine contemplations belong to the beginning student, while the ten perceptions belong to the one who has already accomplished; the nine contemplations are the cause, while the ten perceptions are the fruit. Hence the sutra says: "There are two gates of ambrosia: first, the gate of the contemplation of impurity; second, the gate of mindfulness of the breath." As for there being no difference: skillfully cultivating the nine contemplations, one is already complete with the ten perceptions as well — this sense will be clarified further below.

Reading note: "Destructive" here does not mean destructive in a pejorative sense — it names a specific trade-off. The practitioner of the destructive path breaks the mark of the desire-realm body so thoroughly, through the nine contemplations' relentless imagery of decay, that the refined multi-realm observational skills covered elsewhere in this treatise (the threefold contemplation-refinement-permeation-cultivation, the three insights, the eight liberations) are foreclosed as a side effect — a narrower but faster route to arhatship, reserved for practitioners for whom aversion to the body is the more urgent lever than aversion mastered gently. The nine contemplations, eight recollections, and ten perceptions are then presented as a single three-stage arc: bind the bandit (see it clearly), calm the resulting fear (steady the mind against what was just seen), then kill the bandit outright (sever the fetters for good) — cause and fruit, in the text's own words.

The Nine Contemplations: Preliminary Death-Contemplation 九想 · 死想前方便

Chinese Source

初釋九想觀門者：一、脹想；二、壞想；三、血塗想；四、膿爛想；五、青瘀想；六、噉想；七、散想；八、骨想；九、燒想。此九種法門通稱想者，能轉心轉想，所謂能轉不淨中淨顛倒想，故名為想。今釋九想，即開為四意：一、明修證；二、明對治；三、明攝法；四、明趣道。一、明修證者，行人先持戒清淨，令心不悔，易受觀法，能破婬欲諸煩惱賊故。爾時，當先觀人初死之時，辭談言語，息出不反，忽已死亡，氣滅身冷，無所覺知。室家驚慟，號天叫地，言說方爾，奄便何去？此為大畏，無可免者，譬如劫盡火燒，無有遺脫，如偈說：

Ru-Yi Translation

First, explaining the dharma-gate of the nine contemplations: first, the perception of swelling; second, the perception of decay; third, the perception of being smeared with blood; fourth, the perception of festering; fifth, the perception of livid discoloration; sixth, the perception of devouring; seventh, the perception of scattering; eighth, the perception of bones; ninth, the perception of burning. These nine dharma-gates are, in general, named perception because they are able to turn the mind and turn perception itself — that is, able to turn the inverted perception of purity found within impurity — hence they are named perception.

Explaining the nine contemplations now opens into four senses: first, clarifying their cultivation and realization; second, clarifying what they counteract; third, clarifying their classification; fourth, clarifying how they lead toward the path.

First, clarifying cultivation and realization: the practitioner first keeps the precepts in purity, so that the mind is free of remorse and readily receives the contemplative method, able thereby to break through the bandit-like afflictions of lustful desire. At that time, one should first contemplate a person at the very moment of death — words and speech taking their leave, the breath going out and not returning, suddenly already dead, the vital energy extinguished and the body cold, without any awareness left at all. The household, stricken with grief, cries out to heaven and wails to the earth: just now speaking, and now suddenly gone — where? This is the great terror, from which there is no escape, like the fire that burns at the eon's own end, leaving nothing behind unconsumed. As the verse says:

死至無貧富，無勤修善法，
無貴亦無賤，老少無免者。
無祈請可救，亦無欺誑處，
無捍格得脫，一切無免者。

死法，名永離恩愛之處，一切有生之所惡，雖知可惡，甚無得免者。我身不久必當如是，同於木石，無所別知，我今不應貪著五欲，不覺死至，同於牛羊。牛羊禽獸雖見死者，跳騰哮吼，不自覺悟。我既已得人身，識別好醜，當求甘露不死之法，如偈說：

"Death arrives regardless of poverty or wealth, regardless of diligent cultivation of wholesome dharmas;
regardless of nobility or lowliness, neither the old nor the young escapes it.
There is no prayer or plea that can save one, nor any place where deceit avails;
there is no resistance by which one might break free — none whatsoever escapes it."

The dharma of death is named the place of eternal separation from love and affection, that which every living thing abhors — and though known to be abhorrent, there is truly no escaping it. This body of mine, too, before long, must certainly come to just this state, no different from wood or stone, without any distinguishing awareness left at all. I ought not, then, to crave and cling to the five desires, unaware that death is approaching, no different from cattle or sheep. Cattle, sheep, and every beast, though they see death before them, still leap about and bellow, never awakening to it themselves. I, having already obtained a human body, am able to distinguish good from ill — I ought, then, to seek the deathless dharma of ambrosia. As the verse says:

六情根完具，智鑒亦明利，
而不求道法，唐受身智慧。
禽獸皆亦智，欲樂以自恣，
而不知方便，為道修善事。
既已得人身，而但自放恣，
不知修善行，與彼復何異？
三惡道眾生，不得修道業，
已得此人身，當勉自益利。

行者思惟是已，即取我所愛人，若男若女，脫衣露體，臥置地上，於前如死尸想，一心三昧觀此死尸，心甚驚畏，破愛著心。此則略說死想以為九想前方便也。復次，九想有二種：一者、利根；二者、鈍根。若利根之人，懸心存想死脹等事，悉得成就。若鈍根之人，懸作不成，必須見人初死至尸，所取是相已，繫心修習。既見相分明，心想成就，即發三昧，於後雖離死尸，隨想即見。

"The six faculties fully complete, wisdom's own discernment bright and sharp as well,
and yet not seeking the dharma of the path — one thereby squanders this body's own wisdom in vain.
Every beast, too, has its own intelligence, indulging itself freely in pleasure,
and yet, not knowing the skillful means, cultivating no wholesome deed for the sake of the path.
Having already obtained a human body, and yet only indulging oneself freely,
not knowing how to cultivate wholesome conduct — how, then, does one differ from a beast?
Beings of the three evil destinies are unable to cultivate the path's own karma;
having already obtained this human body, one ought to strive to benefit oneself while one still can."

Having reflected in this way, the practitioner then takes a person he loves, whether man or woman, and — clothing removed, the body bared — lays them upon the ground, and, treating them as a corpse would appear, one-pointedly and in samādhi contemplates this very corpse, the mind thoroughly startled and afraid, thereby breaking the mind of loving attachment. This, in brief, is the perception of death, serving as the preliminary expedient means for the nine contemplations themselves.

Furthermore, the nine contemplations are of two kinds: first, sharp faculties; second, dull faculties. Should one be a person of sharp faculties, simply holding the mind fixed on swelling and the rest of these matters at will, one is able to accomplish them all. Should one be a person of dull faculties, holding the mind fixed at will does not succeed — one must instead actually witness a person at the moment of death, all the way through to the corpse itself, and, having taken up this very mark, fix the mind upon it and cultivate accordingly. Once the mark is seen clearly and the mind's own perception is accomplished, samādhi thereupon arises — thereafter, though one may be apart from the corpse itself, the mark is seen the moment it is called to mind.

Reading note: The two framing verses are worth reading as a pair: the first is a pure memento mori, flattening every worldly distinction (rich/poor, noble/low, old/young) before death's own indifference; the second turns that same fact into an argument specifically against wasted human potential, contrasting humans unfavorably with animals who at least indulge without the excuse of knowing better. The instruction to use "a person one loves" as the object, rather than a stranger, is a deliberately sharpened version of the practice — the whole method is designed to sever attachment precisely where attachment is strongest.

The Nine Contemplations, One by One 脹想 · 壞想 · 血塗想 · 膿爛想 · 青瘀想 · 噉想 · 散想 · 骨想 · 燒想

Chinese Source

一、脹想者，行者對死尸邊見胮脹，如韋囊盛風，異於本相。此身中無主，妄識役御，視聽言語，以此自誑，今何所趣？但見空舍、胮脹、項直。此身姿容、妖媚、細膚、朱脣、素齒、長眼、直鼻、平額、高眉，如是好身，令人心惑。今但見胮脹，好在何處？男女之相亦不可識。即取此相以觀我所愛人，作此訶責欲心，臭屎囊胮脹可惡，何足貪著，為此沈沒？自念我身未脫此法，一心三昧除世貪愛。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the perception of swelling: the practitioner, beside the corpse, sees it swollen, like a leather bag filled with wind, no longer resembling its original appearance. This body has no master within it — false consciousness alone drove and directed it, its seeing, hearing, and speech all merely deceiving oneself thereby — and now, where has it gone? One sees only an empty dwelling, swollen and rigid-necked. This body's own posture and countenance, its bewitching charm, its fine skin, vermilion lips, white teeth, long eyes, straight nose, broad forehead, and high brows — such a fine body once bewildered the onlooker's own mind. Now one sees only its swelling — where, then, is that fineness to be found? Even the mark of male or female can no longer be told apart. Taking up this very mark, one contemplates the person one loves in just this way, thereby reproaching the mind of desire: this stinking bag of filth, swollen and abhorrent — how could it be worth craving, that one should sink and drown for its sake? One reflects to oneself: my own body has not yet escaped this same dharma. One-pointedly, in samādhi, one thereby removes worldly craving and attachment.

二、壞想，行者復觀死尸風吹日曝轉大，烈壞在地，六分破碎，五臟屎尿臭穢盈流，惡露已現。我所著者，以此觀之，無可愛樂。我為癡惑，為此屎囊薄皮所誑，如燈蛾投火，但貪明色，不顧燒身之禍。自念我身亦爾，未脫此法，一心三昧除世貪愛。三、血塗漫想，行者復觀死尸，既見破壞，處處膿血流溢，從頭至足，點污不淨，臭穢、腥臊、胮脹，不可親近。我所愛者，以此觀之，無可愛樂。我為癡惑，坐是沈淪，污穢不淨，好在何處？自念我身未脫此法，一心三昧除世貪愛。

Second, the perception of decay: the practitioner further contemplates the corpse, blown by wind and exposed to sun, growing ever more swollen, then splitting apart violently upon the ground, its six sections broken and scattered, the five organs, excrement, and urine overflowing in a stinking, filthy stream — its foulness now fully exposed. That which I was once attached to, contemplated in just this way, holds nothing whatsoever worth loving or delighting in. I was deceived by folly, taken in by this thin skin over a bag of filth, like a moth that throws itself into the flame, craving only the brightness of its color, heedless of the disaster of its own burning body. One reflects to oneself: my own body, too, is just the same, not yet escaped this same dharma. One-pointedly, in samādhi, one thereby removes worldly craving and attachment.

Third, the perception of being smeared with blood: the practitioner further contemplates the corpse, its decay already seen, pus and blood overflowing everywhere, spattering and staining from head to foot, foul and impure, reeking and rank, swollen — impossible to draw near. That which I loved, contemplated in just this way, holds nothing whatsoever worth loving or delighting in. I was deceived by folly, thereby sinking into ruin for its sake — this filth and impurity, where, then, is that fineness to be found? One reflects to oneself: my own body has not yet escaped this same dharma. One-pointedly, in samādhi, one thereby removes worldly craving and attachment.

四、膿爛想，行者觀死尸風熱水漬，日漸經久，身上九孔蟲膿流出，皮肉處處膿爛，滂沱在地，臭氣轉增。我所愛者，以此觀之，好容美貌，為此昏迷。今見臭爛甚於糞穢，何可貪著？自念我身未脫此法，一心三昧除世貪愛。五、青瘀想，行者復觀死尸膿血稍盡，風日所變，皮肉黃赤，瘀黑青𪒠，臭氣轉增。我所愛者，以此觀之，桃華之色，誑惑於我，今何所在？自念我身未脫此法，一心三昧除世貪愛。

Fourth, the perception of festering: the practitioner contemplates the corpse, soaked by wind, heat, and moisture, day by day growing further along, worms and pus flowing out from the body's nine openings, the skin and flesh festering everywhere, pouring down upon the ground, the stench ever increasing. That which I loved — this fine countenance and beautiful appearance — was the very thing by which I was once bewildered. Now, seeing it stinking and festering, worse even than excrement, how could it be worth craving? One reflects to oneself: my own body has not yet escaped this same dharma. One-pointedly, in samādhi, one thereby removes worldly craving and attachment.

Fifth, the perception of livid discoloration: the practitioner further contemplates the corpse, its pus and blood now gradually exhausted, transformed by wind and sun, the skin and flesh turning yellow and red, then bruised black and livid blue-green, the stench ever increasing. That which I loved — that peach-blossom complexion — once deceived and bewildered me; where, now, has it gone? One reflects to oneself: my own body has not yet escaped this same dharma. One-pointedly, in samādhi, one thereby removes worldly craving and attachment.

六、噉想，行者復觀死尸蟲蛆唼食，烏挑其眼，狐狗咀嚼，虎狼爴裂，身殘缺駁，脫落可惡。我所愛人，以此觀之，本時形體清潔，服飾莊嚴，嬌態自惑。今見破壞，本相皆失，甚可厭惡。自念我身未脫此法，一心三昧除世貪愛。七、散想，行者復觀死尸禽獸分裂，身形破散，風吹日曝，筋斷骨離，頭首交橫。我所愛人，以此觀之，人相何在？自念我身未脫是法，一心三昧除世貪愛。

Sixth, the perception of devouring: the practitioner further contemplates the corpse, maggots gnawing and devouring it, crows plucking out its eyes, foxes and dogs chewing at it, tigers and wolves clawing it apart, the body left ragged, patchy, and falling away — utterly abhorrent. This person I loved, once, when the body was clean and pure, adorned in fine dress and ornament, its coquettish charm bewildering me of itself. Now, seeing it broken apart, every original mark entirely lost, it is utterly loathsome. One reflects to oneself: my own body has not yet escaped this same dharma. One-pointedly, in samādhi, one thereby removes worldly craving and attachment.

Seventh, the perception of scattering: the practitioner further contemplates the corpse, torn apart by birds and beasts, its bodily form broken and scattered, blown by wind and exposed to sun, tendons severed and bones separated, the head and skull lying crosswise and every which way. This person I loved, contemplated in just this way — where, now, is any human mark to be found? One reflects to oneself: my own body has not yet escaped this same dharma. One-pointedly, in samādhi, one thereby removes worldly craving and attachment.

八、骨想，行者復觀死尸皮肉等已盡，但見白骨。見骨有二種：一者、見筋相連；二者、筋盡骨離。復有二種：一則餘血膏膩染污，二則骨白如珂如貝。我所愛人，以此觀之，髑髏可畏，堅強之相甚於瓦石，柔軟細觸一旦皆失。自念我身未免此法，一心三昧除世貪愛。

Eighth, the perception of bones: the practitioner further contemplates the corpse, its skin and flesh now entirely gone, seeing only the white bones remaining. Seeing the bones, too, has two kinds: first, seeing the tendons still connecting them; second, the tendons entirely gone and the bones separated. And this, further, has two kinds: first, still stained with the grease and residue of blood; second, the bones white as agate or as shell. This person I loved, contemplated in just this way — the skull itself now fearsome, its hard, rigid mark harsher even than tile or stone, every trace of soft and delicate touch lost in a single moment. One reflects to oneself: my own body has not yet escaped this same dharma. One-pointedly, in samādhi, one thereby removes worldly craving and attachment.

九、燒想，行者復到死尸林中，或見𧂐多草木，焚燒死尸，腹破肥出，爆裂煙臭，甚可驚畏；或見但燒白骨，煙焰洞然，薪盡火滅，形同灰土。假令不燒不埋，亦歸磨滅。我所愛人，以此觀之，身相皆盡，甚於兵刃，沐浴香熏，華粉嚴飾，軟肥細體，清溫諂佞，以此惑人，今皆磨滅，竟何所在？自念我身未脫此法，一心三昧除世貪愛。

Ninth, the perception of burning: the practitioner further goes to the charnel grove, and there sees, perhaps, grass and wood piled high, the corpse set ablaze, its belly bursting open and fat pouring out, crackling and popping amid smoke and stench — utterly startling and fearsome; or sees, perhaps, only the white bones themselves burning, smoke and flame blazing through, until the firewood is spent and the fire dies out, the form now reduced to ash and dust alike. Even should it be neither burned nor buried, it, too, returns in the end to dissolution. This person I loved, contemplated in just this way — the bodily mark entirely gone, more thoroughly than by any blade — the bathing in fragrant incense, the adorning with powder and flowers, the soft and full delicate body, the cool warmth and flattering charm, by which one was once bewildered — all of it now dissolved away, where, in the end, does it remain? One reflects to oneself: my own body has not yet escaped this same dharma. One-pointedly, in samādhi, one thereby removes worldly craving and attachment.

Reading note: Each of the nine items closes with the identical refrain — "my own body has not yet escaped this same dharma" — turning what could read as external, almost clinical observation back onto the observer at every single step. The sequence itself follows an actual, observable timeline of decomposition (swelling, splitting, blood, festering, discoloration, scavenging, scattering, skeletonization, and finally cremation or natural dissolution), which is presumably why the text allows sharp-faculty practitioners to visualize it from memory while dull-faculty practitioners are instructed to witness an actual corpse first. The traditional charnel-ground contemplation practice that underlies this entire section predates Buddhism and appears across many contemplative traditions; this treatise's own contribution is folding it into the larger destructive-path/non-destructive-path framework as the first rung of a specific ladder toward arhatship.

Second, clarifying what the nine contemplations counteract: the practitioner, having already penetrated the nine contemplations, must further add to and repeatedly cultivate this perception, so that the practice of contemplation grows mature and sharp. Whenever one contemplates in this way, the mind thereupon corresponds with concentration, the dharma of perception holding the mind fast, without any scattering of intent — this, then, is able to break through the six desires and remove worldly craving and attachment.

The six desires are: first, desire for color; second, desire for form and appearance; third, desire for demeanor and bearing; fourth, desire for speech and voice; fifth, desire for smooth touch; sixth, desire for the human mark itself. Within these six desires, six kinds of attachment are able to arise.

As for desire for color: some people are stained with attachment to red, or to reddish-white, or to yellowish-white, or to black, or to reddish-black, or to blue-green, or to bluish-white, or to a peach-blossom hue. The unwise and foolish, seeing colors of this kind, sink and drown, bewildered and intoxicated. As for desire for form and appearance: some people are attached only to form and appearance — a face like the full moon, long eyes, high brows, a slender waist, delicate fingers, features fine and upright — the mind is thereupon bewildered and attached. As for desire for demeanor: some people are attached to demeanor and bearing — a gait broad and graceful, brows raised, cheeks dimpling, a smile held with coy allure — this thereupon gives rise to love and attachment.

言語欲者，有人但愛語聲，若聞巧言華說，應意承旨，音詞清雅，歌詠讚歎，悅動人心，愚夫淺識，為之迷惑。細滑欲者，有人但愛身形柔軟，肥膚光悅，猶若兜羅之綿，寒時體溫，熱時體涼，按摩接待，身服熏香，凡情沒溺，為此危喪。雜欲者，有人皆著五事。人相欲者，有人皆不著五事，但著人相。若男若女，雖見上五事，若不得所愛之人，猶不染著；若遇適意之人，則能捨世所重，頓亡軀命。如是六欲，世世誑惑眾生，沈淪生死，沒溺三塗，不得解脫。若能善修九想對治除滅，則六欲賊破散，疾證涅槃。

As for desire for speech: some people love only the sound of speech — hearing clever words and flowery phrases, attuned to one's own intent and following one's own wishes, the tone and diction clear and elegant, songs of praise and admiration, stirring and delighting the heart — the foolish and shallow of understanding are thereby bewildered by it. As for desire for smooth touch: some people love only a body soft and smooth to the touch, its flesh full and radiantly glossy, like the softest down — warm to the touch in the cold, cool to the touch in the heat, welcoming to massage and embrace, the body scented with fragrant perfume — the ordinary sentiment sinks and drowns in this, and is thereby endangered and ruined. As for the mixed desire: some people are attached to all five of these matters together. As for desire for the human mark itself: some people are attached to none of these five matters, but are attached only to the human mark as such — whether male or female — such that, though they may perceive the above five matters in someone, if that person is not the one they love, they still remain unattached; but should they encounter a person who suits their own fancy, they are able to abandon everything the world holds dear, and cast away body and life in an instant.

Desires of this kind, life after life, deceive and bewilder sentient beings, causing them to sink into birth-and-death, drown in the three evil destinies, and never obtain liberation. Should one be able to skillfully cultivate the nine contemplations as their antidote, thereby dissolving them away, the bandit of the six desires is scattered and broken, and one swiftly realizes nirvāṇa.

所以者何？初死想，破威儀、語言二欲；次脹想、壞想、噉想，破形貌欲；次血塗漫想、青瘀想、膿爛想，多破色欲；次骨想、燒想，多除細滑欲；九想，除雜欲及所著人相欲；噉想、散想、骨想，偏除人相欲。殘噉離散白骨中，不見有人可著故，以是九想觀能破欲結，瞋、癡亦薄。三毒薄故，九十八使山皆動，漸漸增進其道，以金剛三昧摧破結使山，得三乘道。九想雖是不淨觀，因是能成大事，譬如大海中死尸，溺人依之，即得度也。三、明攝法者，是九想法緣欲界身色、想陰攝，亦身念處少分，或欲界攝，或初禪、二禪攝。未離欲散心人得欲界繫，離欲人得色界繫，胮脹等八想欲界初禪、二禪中攝，淨骨想欲界初禪、二禪、四禪中攝。三禪中樂多，故無是想。

Why is this? The initial perception of death breaks the two desires of demeanor and speech; next, the perceptions of swelling, decay, and devouring break the desire for form and appearance; next, the perceptions of being smeared with blood, livid discoloration, and festering, for the most part, break the desire for color; next, the perceptions of bones and burning, for the most part, remove the desire for smooth touch; the nine contemplations as a whole remove the mixed desire and the attachment to the human mark itself; and the perceptions of devouring, scattering, and bones especially remove the desire for the human mark, since, amid the remains — devoured, scattered, and reduced to white bone — no human mark whatsoever is left to be found for attachment to fix upon.

Through these nine contemplations, one is thereby able to break through the fetter of desire, and anger and delusion, too, grow thin. As the three poisons grow thin, the very mountain of the ninety-eight proclivities is shaken, and one gradually advances further along the path, until, with the vajra samādhi, one shatters the mountain of the fetters outright and obtains the path of the three vehicles. Though the nine contemplations are themselves a contemplation of impurity, through them one is able to accomplish this great matter — like a corpse floating in the great ocean, upon which a drowning person may yet depend, and thereby be delivered across.

Third, clarifying their classification: this dharma of the nine contemplations takes the desire realm's own bodily form as its object, and is classified under the aggregate of perception, also forming a small portion of the mindfulness of the body. It is classified, variously, under the desire realm, or under the first and second dhyānas. A person of scattered mind not yet free of desire obtains the fetter belonging to the desire realm; a person already free of desire obtains the fetter belonging to the form realm. The eight perceptions — swelling and the rest — are classified under the desire realm and the first and second dhyānas; the pure perception of bones is classified under the desire realm and the first, second, and fourth dhyānas. Within the third dhyāna, happiness is abundant, hence this perception has no place there.

四、明九想趣道者，修九想有二種：若按事而修，此則但能伏欲界結，後別修十想以斷見思，成無學道。二者、若善修九想，即具十想，從事入理，此則不煩別約餘門修十想。所以者何？如行者觀人死時，動轉言語，須臾間忽然已滅，身體胮脹、爛壞、分散，各各變異，是則無常。若著此身，無常壞時，是即為苦。若無常苦不得自在者，是則無我。不淨、無常、苦、無我故，則世間不可樂著，觀身如是。食雖在口，腦涎流下，與唾和合成味，而咽與吐無異，下人腹中即為糞穢，即是食不淨想。以此九想觀觀身無常變易，念念皆滅，即是死想。

Fourth, clarifying how the nine contemplations lead toward the path: cultivating the nine contemplations is of two kinds. Should one cultivate them by their own concrete matter alone, this is able only to subdue the fetters of the desire realm — one must afterward separately cultivate the ten perceptions in order to sever the afflictions of view and thought, and accomplish the path of no-more-learning. Second, should one skillfully cultivate the nine contemplations, one is already complete with the ten perceptions as well, passing from concrete matter into principle itself — in which case there is no need, separately, through some other gate, to cultivate the ten perceptions as well.

Why is this? As the practitioner contemplates a person at the moment of death — moving and speaking one moment, and then, in an instant, suddenly gone, the body swelling, decaying, and scattering apart, each stage transforming into the next — this is precisely impermanence. Clinging to this body as its impermanence brings it to ruin is precisely suffering. Should this impermanence and suffering leave one without any freedom of one's own, this is precisely no-self. Through impurity, impermanence, suffering, and no-self, the world is thereby seen as nothing to delight in or cling to — the body contemplated in just this way.

Though food is taken into the mouth, the brain's own fluid flows down and mingles with saliva to form its taste, and swallowing differs not at all from vomiting — once it descends into a person's belly, it becomes excrement itself: this is precisely the perception of the impurity of food. Through these nine contemplations, contemplating the body's impermanent transformation, ceasing moment after moment: this is precisely the perception of death.

以是九想厭世間樂，知煩惱斷，即安隱寂滅，即是斷想。以是九想遮諸煩惱，即離想。以九想厭世間故，知五陰滅，更不須生，是處安隱，即是盡想。若能如是善修九想，即具十想，斷見、思惑，當知是人必定趣三乘道。復次，《摩訶衍》中說，若善修九想，開身念處門，身念處開三念處門，四念處開三十七品門，三十七品開涅槃門，入涅槃故，則滅一切憂苦。菩薩憐愍眾生故，雖於九想能入涅槃，而亦不取實際作證。

Through these nine contemplations, growing weary of worldly pleasure and knowing the affliction's own cessation, one attains peace and quiescence: this is precisely the perception of cessation. Through these nine contemplations, blocking every affliction: this is precisely the perception of detachment. Through these nine contemplations growing weary of the world, knowing that the five aggregates cease and need never arise again, this place itself being at peace: this is precisely the perception of exhaustion.

Should one be able to skillfully cultivate the nine contemplations in this way, one is already complete with the ten perceptions as well, severing the afflictions of view and of thought — one should know that this person is certain to advance toward the path of the three vehicles.

Furthermore, the Mahāyāna scriptures say: should one skillfully cultivate the nine contemplations, this opens the gate of the mindfulness of the body; the mindfulness of the body opens the gate of the three remaining foundations of mindfulness; the four foundations of mindfulness open the gate of the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment; the thirty-seven factors open the gate of nirvāṇa; and, entering nirvāṇa thereby, every sorrow and suffering is extinguished. The bodhisattva, out of compassion for sentient beings, although able, even through the nine contemplations, to enter nirvāṇa, nonetheless does not grasp at the ultimate so as to make it one's own final proof.

所以者何？若色中無味相，眾生即不應著於色；若色中無離相，今亦不應從色得解脫。以色中有味故，眾生則著於色；色中有離相故，眾生從色得解脫。而味不即離，離不即味，離味處無脫處，離脫處無味處，當知色即非縛非脫。爾時不隨生死，不證涅槃，但以大悲憐憫一切眾生，於此不淨觀中成就一切佛法。《大品經》云：「九想即是菩薩摩訶衍。」

Why is this? Should form possess no mark of savor at all, sentient beings ought not to become attached to form in the first place; should form possess no mark of detachment at all, one likewise ought not to obtain liberation from form either. It is precisely because form possesses the mark of savor that sentient beings become attached to form, and precisely because form possesses the mark of detachment that sentient beings obtain liberation from form. Yet savor is not itself detachment, nor is detachment itself savor — apart from the place of savor there is no place of liberation, and apart from the place of liberation there is no place of savor; one should know, then, that form itself is neither bondage nor liberation. At that time, one neither follows along with birth-and-death, nor grasps at the proof of nirvāṇa, but, solely through great compassion for every sentient being, accomplishes the whole of the Buddhadharma within this very contemplation of impurity. The Mahāprajñāpāramitā Sūtra says: "The nine contemplations are themselves precisely the bodhisattva's own Mahāyāna."

Reading note: The six desires are worth noting as a taxonomy in their own right — color, form, demeanor, speech, touch, and "the human mark itself" (attachment to a specific person regardless of their qualities, the most stubborn of the six since it survives even when every other desirable quality is absent). The precise mapping of which contemplation counteracts which desire is a good example of this treatise's habitual move: turning what looks like a single undifferentiated practice into a diagnostic instrument, matched item-for-item to specific afflictions. The closing citation from the Mahāprajñāpāramitā Sūtra performs the same reframing move fascicles 7 and 8 already used for the six wondrous gates and the thorough-and-bright contemplation: even the starkest, most viscerally "Hinayana"-coded practice in the entire treatise is, in the end, folded into the Mahāyāna, on the strength of the argument that form is "neither bondage nor liberation" — attachment and freedom from attachment both depend on the same object, so neither the object nor its contemplation is ultimately more real than the other.

The Eight Recollections: Purpose, and the Buddha, Dharma, Saṃgha, Precepts 八念 · 教門所為 · 念佛念法念僧念戒

次釋八念法門。所言八念者：一、念佛；二、念法；三、念僧；四、念戒；五、念捨；六、念天；七、念入出息；八、念死。此八通稱念者，一心緣中，憶持不忘失故，名之為念。今釋八念，即為三意：一、明教門所為；二、明修證；三、明趣道之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, explaining the dharma-gate of the eight recollections. The eight recollections are: first, recollection of the Buddha; second, recollection of the Dharma; third, recollection of the Saṃgha; fourth, recollection of the precepts; fifth, recollection of relinquishment; sixth, recollection of the heavens; seventh, recollection of the breath entering and leaving; eighth, recollection of death. These eight are, in general, named recollection because, the mind fixed one-pointedly upon its object, one holds it in mind without forgetting or losing it — hence they are named recollection.

Explaining the eight recollections now has three senses: first, clarifying the purpose of this teaching-gate; second, clarifying their cultivation and realization; third, clarifying the mark of how they lead toward the path.

一、明教門所為者，佛弟子於阿蘭若處：空舍、塚間、山林、曠野，善修九想外不淨，厭患其身，而作是念：「我云何將是底下不淨臭屎尿囊以自隨逐？」爾時㱇然驚怖，舉身毛豎；及為惡魔作種種形色來恐怖之，欲令其道退沒。以是故念佛。次九想後，說八念，以除怖畏。如經中說：「佛告諸比丘：若於阿蘭若處有驚怖心，爾時應當念佛，恐怖即滅；若不念佛，應當念法，恐怖即除；若不念法，應當念僧，恐怖即除。」故知三念為除怖畏說也。

First, clarifying the purpose of this teaching-gate: the Buddha's disciple, dwelling in a forest hermitage — an empty dwelling, a charnel ground, mountain forest, or open wilderness — skillfully cultivates the nine contemplations' own external impurity, growing weary of and averse to his own body, and reflects thus: "How is it that I go about dragging along this very bag of base impurity, this stinking filth of excrement and urine, wherever I go?" At that moment, he is suddenly seized with terror, every hair on his body standing on end; and evil māras, too, taking on all manner of forms, come to terrify him further still, wishing thereby to make him retreat and fall back from the path. It is for this reason that recollection of the Buddha is taught. Following the nine contemplations, the eight recollections are thus taught, in order to remove this very fear. As the sutra says: "The Buddha told the monks: should fear and terror arise in the forest hermitage, one should, at that time, recollect the Buddha, and the fear is thereupon extinguished; should one not recollect the Buddha, one should recollect the Dharma, and the fear is thereupon removed; should one not recollect the Dharma, one should recollect the Saṃgha, and the fear is thereupon removed." Hence one knows that these first three recollections are taught specifically to remove fear and terror.

問曰：經說三念因緣為除怖畏，後五念復云何？答曰：是比丘自布施、持戒，怖畏即除。所以者何？若彼破戒，畏墮地獄；若慳貪，畏墮餓鬼及貧窮中，自念：「我有是淨戒、布施，則歡喜。上諸天皆是布施、持戒果報，我亦有是福德。」是故念天亦能令怖畏不生。十六行中，念阿那波那時，細心覺尚滅，何況怖畏麁覺？念死者，念五陰身念念生滅，從生已來，恒與死俱，今何以畏死？是五念，佛雖不別說，當知是為深除怖畏。所以者何？念他功德以除恐怖則難，念自功德以除怖畏則易。以是義故，不別說。

Question: the sutra explains that these three recollections' own causes and conditions are for removing fear and terror — what, then, of the latter five recollections? Answer: this is because the monk, through his own giving and precept-keeping, has his fear thereupon removed as well. Why is this? Should he have broken the precepts, he fears falling into hell; should he be stingy and greedy, he fears falling among the hungry ghosts or into poverty. Reflecting to himself: "I possess this very purity of precepts and giving" — he is thereupon filled with joy. Every heavenly being above is likewise the fruit of giving and precept-keeping, and I, too, possess this same merit. Hence recollection of the heavens, too, is able to keep fear from arising. Within the sixteen practices, at the moment of recollecting the breath entering and leaving, even the subtlest initial thought is extinguished — how much more so, then, the coarse initial thought of fear? As for recollection of death: recollecting that the body of the five aggregates arises and ceases moment after moment, and that, ever since birth, one has always been accompanied by death — why, then, should one fear death now? These five recollections, though the Buddha did not explain them separately, should be understood as likewise deeply removing fear. Why is this? Recollecting another's merit in order to remove fear is difficult, while recollecting one's own merit in order to remove fear is easy — for this reason, they were not explained separately.

二、明修證八念。念佛者，若行者於阿蘭若中，心有怖畏，應當念佛，佛是多陀阿伽度、阿羅訶、三藐三佛陀乃至婆伽婆，十號具足，三十二相、八十種好、大慈大悲、十力、四無所畏、十八不共法，智慧光明，神通無量，能度無量十方眾生，是我大師，救護一切，我當何畏？一心憶念，恐怖即除。

Second, clarifying the cultivation and realization of the eight recollections. Recollection of the Buddha: should the practitioner, within the forest hermitage, find fear arising in the mind, he should recollect the Buddha — the Buddha is the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the Fully and Perfectly Awakened One, all the way through to the World-Honored One, complete with the ten epithets; possessed of the thirty-two marks and eighty minor characteristics, great loving-kindness and great compassion, the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, and the eighteen exclusive qualities; his wisdom's own light and supernormal powers beyond measure, able to deliver across countless beings throughout the ten directions. He is my own great teacher, the one who rescues and protects all — of what, then, should I be afraid? Recollecting one-pointedly in this way, fear is thereupon removed.

二、念法者，行者應念是法巧出，得今世果，無諸熱惱，不待時，能到善處，通達無礙。巧出者，善說二諦，不相違故，是法能出二邊，故名巧出。得今世果者，諸外道法皆無今世果，唯佛法中因緣展轉生。所謂持戒清淨故，得心不悔；得心不悔故，生法歡喜；生法歡喜故，得心樂；得心樂故，則能攝心；攝心故，得如實智；得如實智故，得厭離；得厭離故，得離欲；離欲故，得解脫；解脫果報故，得涅槃。是名得今世果報。無熱惱者，無三毒、生死熱惱也。不待時者，諸外道受法要須待時節，佛法不爾，譬如薪遇火即然，不待時。到善處者，若行佛法，必至人天樂果，三乘涅槃之處。通達無礙者，得三法印故，通達無礙也。我修如是等法，當何所畏？一心憶念，恐怖即除。

Second, recollection of the Dharma: the practitioner should recollect that this Dharma is skillfully liberating, yields its fruit already in this very life, is free of every feverish affliction, requires no waiting upon time, is able to arrive at a good place, and penetrates without obstruction.

"Skillfully liberating" means that it skillfully expounds the two truths without their contradicting each other, this very Dharma thereby able to transcend both extremes — hence it is named skillfully liberating. "Yields its fruit already in this very life" means that every non-Buddhist teaching lacks any fruit in this very life, while only within the Buddhadharma do causes and conditions unfold one from another in succession: that is, through the purity of upholding the precepts, one obtains freedom from remorse of mind; through freedom from remorse of mind, one gives rise to joy in the Dharma; through joy in the Dharma, one obtains happiness of mind; through happiness of mind, one is thereby able to gather in the mind; through gathering in the mind, one obtains wisdom that accords with reality; through wisdom that accords with reality, one obtains weariness and detachment; through weariness and detachment, one obtains freedom from desire; through freedom from desire, one obtains liberation; and through the fruit of liberation, one obtains nirvāṇa. This is named obtaining the fruit already in this very life.

"Free of every feverish affliction" means free of the three poisons and the feverish affliction of birth-and-death. "Requires no waiting upon time" means that every non-Buddhist teaching requires waiting upon its own proper season to receive its result, while the Buddhadharma is not so — like firewood, which, meeting fire, ignites at once, without waiting upon any season. "Able to arrive at a good place" means that, practicing the Buddhadharma, one is certain to arrive at the happy fruit of the human and heavenly realms, and at the place of the three vehicles' own nirvāṇa. "Penetrates without obstruction" means that, having obtained the three dharma-seals, one penetrates without any obstruction whatsoever. I cultivate dharmas such as these — of what, then, should I be afraid? Recollecting one-pointedly in this way, fear is thereupon removed.

三、念僧者，行者應念僧，僧是佛弟子眾，具五分法身。是中有四雙八輩、二十七人，應受供養禮事，世間無上福田。所謂若聲聞僧、若辟支佛僧、若菩薩僧，神智無量，能救苦難，度脫眾生，如是聖眾，是我真伴，當何所畏？一心憶念，恐怖即除。四、念戒者，行者應念：是戒能遮諸惡，安隱住處。是中戒有二種，所謂有漏戒、無漏戒。復有二種：一、律儀戒；二、定共戒。律儀戒，能遮諸惡，身得安隱；定共戒，能遮諸煩惱，心得內樂；無漏戒，能破無明諸惡根本，得解脫樂。我修如是之法，當何所畏？一心憶念，恐怖即除。

Third, recollection of the Saṃgha: the practitioner should recollect the Saṃgha — the Saṃgha is the assembly of the Buddha's own disciples, complete with the five-part dharma-body. Among them are the four pairs and eight kinds of noble persons, twenty-seven in number, worthy of receiving offerings and reverent service, the world's own unsurpassed field of merit — whether the saṃgha of śrāvakas, the saṃgha of pratyekabuddhas, or the saṃgha of bodhisattvas, their spiritual wisdom beyond measure, able to rescue beings from suffering and hardship and deliver them across. Noble assemblies such as these are my own true companions — of what, then, should I be afraid? Recollecting one-pointedly in this way, fear is thereupon removed.

Fourth, recollection of the precepts: the practitioner should recollect that these precepts are able to block every evil, a place of peace and security to abide within. Among these precepts there are two kinds, namely the contaminated precepts and the undefiled precepts. There are, further, two more kinds: first, the precepts of moral restraint; second, the precepts joined with concentration. The precepts of moral restraint are able to block every evil, the body thereby obtaining peace; the precepts joined with concentration are able to block every affliction, the mind thereby obtaining inner happiness; the undefiled precepts are able to break through ignorance, the root of every evil, obtaining thereby the happiness of liberation. I cultivate a dharma such as this — of what, then, should I be afraid? Recollecting one-pointedly in this way, fear is thereupon removed.

Reading note: The eight recollections are presented as a direct sequel to the nine contemplations' own psychological aftermath — having deliberately induced revulsion toward the body, the text is candid that fear, not just detachment, is a predictable side effect, and even names evil māras seizing the moment to exploit that fear. The explanation for why only the first three recollections (Buddha, Dharma, Saṃgha) are explicitly framed around fear-removal in the source sutra, while the remaining five are not, is a small window into this treatise's own interpretive method: rather than simply reporting the canonical list, it reasons out why the sutra's own silence about the latter five still implies the same function — recollecting one's own merit is "easy," recollecting another's is "difficult," so the fear-removing five needed no separate explanation.

Fifth, recollection of relinquishment: the practitioner should recollect that relinquishment is of two kinds: first, the relinquishment of giving; second, the relinquishment of every affliction. The relinquishment of giving is, itself, of two kinds: first, the giving of wealth; second, the giving of the Dharma. Both of these two kinds of giving alike are named relinquishment, and are themselves the root of every wholesome dharma. The practitioner reflects to himself: ever since I have had this body, I have likewise possessed this very merit of relinquishment and giving — of what, then, should I be afraid? Recollecting one-pointedly in this way, fear is thereupon removed.

Sixth, recollection of the heavens: the practitioner should recollect that the Heaven of the Four Great Kings, all the way through to the Heaven of Free Enjoyment of Others' Transformations, and every heavenly being within them, alike obtained rebirth in these places entirely through their own wholesome root of precept-keeping and giving in past lives — enjoying happiness through the long night of saṃsāra, their wholesome dharmas watching over and mindful of beings such as ourselves. Heavens such as these bear a pure fruit of retribution — should I, too, possess this very goodness of precept-keeping and giving, then, at the moment life is relinquished, I am certain to be born in a place such as these — of what, then, should I be afraid? Recollecting one-pointedly in this way, fear is thereupon removed.

七、念阿那波那者，如前六十特勝初門中說。行者若心驚怖，即當調息，緣息出入，覺知滿十，即當發言，念阿那波那，如是至十，六神即歸。一心念息，恐怖即除。次念死者，死有二種：一者、自死；二、他因緣死。是二種死，常隨此身，若他不殺，自亦當死，何足生怖？譬如勇士入陣，以死往遮，則心安無懼。如是一心念死，怖畏即除。是則略說八念對治恐怖。是中法相，並如《摩訶衍》廣分別。

Seventh, recollection of ānāpāna, the breath entering and leaving: as already explained in the opening gate of the sixteen special excellences above. Should the practitioner's own mind be seized with fear and terror, he should straightaway regulate the breath, taking the breath's own entering and leaving as his object, and, once aware of having counted a full ten breaths, should straightaway declare: "I recollect ānāpāna" — continuing thus up through ten, the six faculties thereupon return to their proper place. Recollecting the breath one-pointedly in this way, fear is thereupon removed.

Next, recollection of death: death is of two kinds: first, death of natural causes; second, death through others' causes and conditions. These two kinds of death, alike, are the constant companion of this very body — should others not kill me, I myself, too, must still die regardless: why, then, should this give rise to fear? It is like the brave warrior entering the field of battle, who, going forth to meet death itself head-on, thereby finds the mind at peace, without any fear at all. Recollecting death one-pointedly in just this way, fear is thereupon removed. This, then, briefly explains how the eight recollections counteract fear and terror; the dharma-characteristics involved here are all explained at greater length in the Mahāyāna scriptures.

三、明八念趣道之相者，若如前說，止是權除怖畏及諸障難，今明善修八念，即是一途入道法門。釋八念入道有二意：一者、次第修行入道之相；二者、一一念各得入道。次第修行入道者，行者欲求解脫煩惱之病，先當念佛如醫王，念法如良藥，念僧如瞻病，念戒如禁忌飲食，念捨如將養，念天如身病少差，念阿那波那使發禪定，念死即悟無常四諦。若三界病盡，即得聖道。

Third, clarifying the mark of how the eight recollections lead toward the path: should one proceed as already explained above, this is merely a skillful means for removing fear and terror and every obstruction and difficulty; here, it is further clarified that skillfully cultivating the eight recollections is, in itself, a single path's own dharma-gate for entering the path.

Explaining how the eight recollections enter the path has two senses: first, the mark of entering the path through sequential cultivation; second, each single recollection, on its own, being able to lead to entering the path.

As for entering the path through sequential cultivation: the practitioner, wishing to seek liberation from the illness of affliction, should first recollect the Buddha as the king of physicians, recollect the Dharma as the fine medicine, recollect the Saṃgha as the one who attends the sick, recollect the precepts as the forbidden foods to be avoided, recollect relinquishment as the nourishing care that follows, recollect the heavens as the body's illness beginning to improve, recollect ānāpāna as that which brings forth dhyāna concentration itself, and recollect death as awakening to the Four Noble Truths' own impermanence. Once the illness belonging to the three realms is entirely exhausted, one thereupon obtains the noble path.

二者、明一一念各是入道方法者，念佛，即是念佛三昧入道之相，如《文殊般若》及諸經中說。念法者，如經說：「諸佛所師，所謂法也。」若四諦、十二因緣、六波羅蜜、中道、實相，如是等法，皆是入道之法。念僧者，如《觀世音》、《三昧》、《藥上》等經中說。念戒，如前十種戒中說。念施，如《摩訶衍》中檀波羅蜜入道相中說。餘三念者，若念天及第一義天，即入道；若念阿那波那入道之相，具如通明中說；念死，如下死想義中說。當知八念，隨修一念即得入道，不須餘習。菩薩為求佛道故，行是八念，心無依倚，大悲方便，廣習法門以化眾生。當知八念即是菩薩摩訶衍也。

Second, clarifying how each single recollection, on its own, is itself a method for entering the path: recollection of the Buddha is precisely the mark of entering the path through the samādhi of buddha-recollection, as explained in the Mañjuśrī-prajñāpāramitā-sūtra and throughout the other scriptures. Recollection of the Dharma is as the sutra says: "That which every buddha takes as teacher is precisely the Dharma itself" — the Four Noble Truths, the twelve links of dependent origination, the six pāramitās, the middle way, and true reality: dharmas such as these are, every one, a method of entering the path. Recollection of the Saṃgha is as explained in the Avalokiteśvara Sūtra, the Samādhi Sūtra, the Bhaiṣajyarāja Sūtra, and the rest. Recollection of the precepts is as already explained above, among the ten kinds of precepts. Recollection of giving is as explained in the Mahāyāna scriptures, within the mark of entering the path through the dāna-pāramitā.

As for the remaining three recollections: recollection of the heavens — that is, of the heaven of ultimate meaning itself — is likewise a path of entry; the mark of entering the path through recollection of ānāpāna is given in full within the thorough-and-bright contemplation, already explained above; and recollection of death is as explained below, within the sense of the perception of death.

One should know, then, that, among the eight recollections, cultivating even a single one of them is already sufficient to enter the path, without any need for further practice besides. The bodhisattva, seeking buddhahood, practices these eight recollections with a mind that rests upon and clings to nothing, employing great compassion's own skillful means, broadly cultivating every dharma-gate in order to transform sentient beings. One should know, then, that the eight recollections are themselves precisely the bodhisattva's own Mahāyāna.

Reading note: The medical simile organizing the sequential path — Buddha as physician, Dharma as medicine, Saṃgha as caregiver, precepts as dietary restriction, relinquishment as convalescent care, the heavens as early recovery, the breath as what actually induces the cure, death as the diagnosis of impermanence itself — gives the eight recollections a narrative arc the nine contemplations didn't have: a full course of treatment, not just a repeated antidote. The second framing, that any single recollection independently opens the same door, is the more radical claim, and the text backs it with citations across a wide range of other scriptures (the Mañjuśrī-prajñāpāramitā, the Avalokiteśvara and Bhaiṣajyarāja sūtras) — suggesting each of the eight recollections already had its own, separately developed devotional tradition that this treatise is here folding into a single unified scheme.

Next, explaining the dharma-gate of the ten perceptions. The ten perceptions are: first, the perception of impermanence; second, the perception of suffering; third, the perception of no-self; fourth, the perception of the impurity of food; fifth, the perception that nothing in the whole world is to be delighted in; sixth, the perception of death; seventh, the perception of impurity; eighth, the perception of cessation; ninth, the perception of detachment; tenth, the perception of exhaustion. First, as for their sequence of stages: among the dharmas explained within the Buddha's teaching, there are three kinds of path — first, the path of seeing; second, the path of cultivation; third, the path of no-more-learning. These ten perceptions here are clarified in their sequence of stages precisely with reference to these three paths.

Why is this? The practitioner of the destructive path, already at the stage of dry wisdom, has already become complete with the nine contemplations and subdued the fetters; now, cultivating the first three perceptions — impermanence and the rest — this is precisely the general-mark contemplation, breaking through the sixty-two views and every inverted dharma, and entering the path of seeing, thereby obtaining the first fruit. Next come the four perceptions beginning with the impurity of food; these are for the stream-enterer and the once-returner, entering the path of cultivation, wishing to sever the five lower fetters and realize the fruit of non-returning — hence these four kinds of particular-mark contemplation of concrete matters are explained, assisting the correct contemplation to come to completion and severing the afflictions of thought. Last come the three perceptions of cessation, detachment, and exhaustion; these are for the non-returner, proceeding toward arhatship, cultivating the path of no-more-learning, wishing to sever and depart from craving for the form and formless realms, and realize arhatship — hence these are explained here. One should know, then, that the ten perceptions are distinguished in their sequence of stages with reference to these three paths; in a general sense, this may be seen for itself.

第二、明修證。一、無常想者，觀一切有為法無常，智慧相應故，名無常想。所以者何？一切有為法新新生滅故，屬因緣故，不增積故，生時無所從來，滅時無所去處，故名無常。是中無常有二種：一者、眾生無常；二者、世界無常。眾生無常者，行者觀我及一切眾生從歌羅邏來，色心生滅變異，乃至老死無暫停時。所以者何？一切有為悉屬生、住、滅三相遷變，故知無常。所謂欲生異生，欲住異住，欲滅異滅，如是變易無常，剎那迅速，無暫停息，故知一切眾生悉皆無常。世界無常者，如偈說：

Second, clarifying cultivation and realization. First, the perception of impermanence: contemplating that every conditioned dharma is impermanent, wisdom corresponding to this very contemplation — hence it is named the perception of impermanence. Why is this? Every conditioned dharma arises and ceases ever anew, belongs to causes and conditions, and does not accumulate or increase; at the moment of its arising, it comes from nowhere at all, and at the moment of its ceasing, it goes nowhere at all — hence it is named impermanence.

Within this, impermanence is of two kinds: first, the impermanence of sentient beings; second, the impermanence of the world. As for the impermanence of sentient beings: the practitioner contemplates that both oneself and every sentient being, ever since the kalala-stage, undergo form and mind arising, ceasing, and transforming, all the way through old age and death, without a single moment's pause. Why is this? Every conditioned thing belongs, entirely, to the three marks of arising, abiding, and ceasing, shifting and changing — hence one knows it to be impermanent. That is: wishing to arise, it instead arises as something already different; wishing to abide, it instead abides as something already different; wishing to cease, it instead ceases as something already different — change and impermanence of this kind, swift as an instant, without a single moment's rest — hence one knows that every sentient being, without exception, is impermanent. As for the impermanence of the world, the verse says:

大地草木皆磨滅，須彌巨海亦崩竭，
諸天住處皆燒盡，爾時世界何處常？

復次，如佛說無常觀有二種：一者、有餘；二者、無餘。一切人物皆盡，唯有名在，是名有餘；若人物滅盡，是名無餘。所以者何？若言以三相故，一切有為法為無常者，三相自不可得，云何有無常？如生時無住滅，離生時亦無住滅。若生時即有住滅，即壞生相，以生滅相違故。若言離生有滅住，亦壞三相義。若離生則滅，無所滅故。當知三相不可得。若無有三相，云何言無常？若不得無常相，即見聖道，是名無常想。

"The great earth, the grasses and trees, all alike are ground away; Mount Sumeru and the great ocean, too, collapse and run dry;
every heavenly dwelling-place is burned entirely away — at such a time, where, then, does the world remain constant?"

Furthermore, as the Buddha teaches, the contemplation of impermanence is of two kinds: first, impermanence with remainder; second, impermanence without remainder. Every person and thing entirely exhausted, only the name remaining — this is named with remainder; should person and thing alike be utterly extinguished — this is named without remainder.

Why is this? Should one say that, on account of the three marks, every conditioned dharma is impermanent, then the three marks themselves cannot be found at all — how, then, could there be impermanence? For, at the moment of arising, there is no abiding or ceasing; and apart from the moment of arising, too, there is no abiding or ceasing. Should arising itself already possess abiding and ceasing, this would destroy the very mark of arising, since arising and ceasing contradict one another. Should one say that, apart from arising, there is ceasing and abiding, this, too, destroys the sense of the three marks. Should there be ceasing apart from arising, there would be nothing there to cease at all. One should know, then, that the three marks cannot be found. If there are no three marks at all, how, then, can one speak of impermanence? Should one fail to find any mark of impermanence at all, this is precisely seeing the noble path — this is named the perception of impermanence.

問曰：若爾，佛何故說無常為聖諦？答曰：為對治破著常顛倒故，是中不應求實，若心計無常為實者，即墮斷見。復次，有餘無常想，如上特勝、通明中說；無餘者，在下慧行中當廣說。問曰：何以聖行初門先說無常想？答曰：一切凡夫未見道時，各貴所行，或言持戒為重，或言多聞為重，或言十二頭陀為重，或言禪定為重。如是各各所行為貴，更不復勤求涅槃。佛言：「是諸功德皆是趣涅槃道分，若觀諸法無常，是為真涅槃道。」如是種種因緣故，諸法雖空，而說是無常想。

Question: if this is so, why does the Buddha teach impermanence as a noble truth? Answer: this is in order to counteract and break through the inverted view of clinging to permanence — within this, one ought not to seek any reality at all; should the mind reckon impermanence itself to be real, one thereby falls into the view of annihilation. Furthermore, the perception of impermanence with remainder is as already explained above, within the special excellences and the thorough-and-bright contemplation; impermanence without remainder will be explained at greater length below, within the section on wisdom-practice.

Question: why does the very first gate of noble practice speak of the perception of impermanence first? Answer: every ordinary person, before seeing the path, prizes their own particular practice above the rest — some say upholding the precepts is what matters most, some say extensive learning is what matters most, some say the twelve austere practices are what matter most, some say dhyāna concentration is what matters most. Each, prizing their own particular practice in this way, no longer diligently seeks nirvāṇa itself. The Buddha says: "All of these merits alike are but a portion of the path leading toward nirvāṇa; contemplating that every dharma is impermanent — this alone is the true path to nirvāṇa." For causes and conditions of this kind, though every dharma is empty, the perception of impermanence is nonetheless taught.

二、苦想者，行者應作是思惟：若一切有餘法無常遷變，即是苦想。所以者何？從內六情、外六塵和合故，生六種識。六種識中生三種受，謂苦受、樂受、捨受。是三種受中生、老、病、死、恩愛別離、求不得、怨憎會、五陰盛等八苦之所逼切，故名為苦。復次，是苦受以事即是苦故，一切眾生所不欲；是樂受以為順情故，一切眾生所愛。若生貪著，無常敗壞，即現受眾苦，後受地獄、畜生、餓鬼等苦。如是等種種諸苦，皆從求樂生，故知樂即是苦。捨受雖復情中不覺苦樂，不取不棄，理實無常遷逼，亦為大苦。如是觀時，於三界中不見樂相可生貪著，心生厭畏，是名苦想。問曰：若無常即是苦者，道聖諦有為無常亦應是苦。答曰：道聖諦雖無常，而能滅苦，不生諸著，又與空、無我等諸智慧和合故，但是無常，而非苦也。

Second, the perception of suffering: the practitioner should reflect thus: that every remaining dharma's own impermanent shifting and changing is itself precisely the perception of suffering. Why is this? From the union of the internal six faculties and the external six sense-objects, the six kinds of consciousness arise; within these six kinds of consciousness, three kinds of sensation arise, namely painful sensation, pleasant sensation, and neutral sensation. Within these three kinds of sensation, one is pressed and afflicted by the eight sufferings — birth, old age, illness, death, separation from what one loves, failing to obtain what one seeks, union with what one hates, and the flourishing of the five aggregates of clinging themselves — hence this is named suffering.

Furthermore, painful sensation, being in itself a matter of suffering, is something no sentient being desires; pleasant sensation, being agreeable to one's own feeling, is something every sentient being loves. Should craving and attachment arise toward it, its impermanent ruin thereupon brings the immediate experience of manifold sufferings, and, afterward, the suffering of hell, of the animal realm, and of the hungry ghosts as well. Every kind of suffering such as this arises entirely from the very seeking of pleasure — hence one knows that pleasure is itself suffering. Neutral sensation, though it is, within one's own feeling, aware of neither pain nor pleasure, neither grasped nor cast away, is nonetheless, in truth and in principle, impermanent and ever shifting and pressing upon one — this, too, is a great suffering.

Contemplating in this way, one sees, throughout the three realms, no mark of pleasure whatsoever capable of giving rise to craving and attachment, and the mind thereby gives rise to weariness and aversion — this is named the perception of suffering. Question: if impermanence is itself suffering, then the noble truth of the path, too, being conditioned and impermanent, ought likewise to be suffering. Answer: though the noble truth of the path is impermanent, it is nonetheless able to extinguish suffering, giving rise to no attachment whatsoever, and, moreover, is joined together with the wisdom of emptiness, no-self, and the rest — hence it is merely impermanent, and not suffering.

三、無我想者，行者當深思惟：若有為法悉是苦者，苦即是無我。所以者何？五受陰中悉皆是苦。若是苦者，即不自在；若不自在，是則無我。何以故？若有我自在者，則不應為苦所逼，知苦即是無我。復次，五陰、十二入、十八界中，諸法從緣生，則無自性故。若即陰離陰，更求我等十六知見，皆不可得。既不得我，則捨一切諸見執著，心無所取，便得解脫，是名無我想。是無常、苦、無我三想，觀行深細，在下釋苦諦中更當廣說。

Third, the perception of no-self: the practitioner should deeply reflect thus: should every conditioned dharma be, entirely, suffering, then suffering is itself precisely no-self. Why is this? Within the five aggregates of clinging, all alike are suffering; being suffering, one is thereby without freedom of one's own; being without freedom of one's own, this is precisely no-self. Why is this? Should there be a self possessing freedom of its own, it ought not to be pressed and afflicted by suffering at all — hence one knows that suffering is itself precisely no-self.

Furthermore, within the five aggregates, the twelve sense-bases, and the eighteen elements, every dharma arises from conditions, and thus possesses no nature of its own. Whether identical to the aggregates or separate from the aggregates, seeking further for a self through the sixteen forms of knowledge and view, none whatsoever can be found. A self being nowhere found, one thereupon relinquishes every view and every attachment, the mind grasping at nothing at all, and thereby obtains liberation — this is named the perception of no-self. These three perceptions of impermanence, suffering, and no-self involve a contemplative practice both deep and subtle, and will be explained at still greater length below, within the explanation of the truth of suffering.

問曰：無常、苦、無我，為是一事，為是三事？若是一事，一事不應說三；若是三事，佛何故說無常即苦，苦即無我？答：三是一事，所謂受有漏法。觀門分別故，有三種異：無常行相應，是無常想；苦行相應，是苦想；無我行相應，是無我想。無常不令入三界，苦令知三界過罪，無我則捨世間。復次，無常生厭，苦生怖畏，無我拔出，令得解脫。復次，無常者遮常見，苦遮令世涅槃見，無我者遮著處見。無常者，世間所可著常法是；苦者，世間計樂處是；無我者，世間所可計我牢固者是。如是等種種分別，並如《摩訶衍》中廣說也。

Question: are impermanence, suffering, and no-self a single matter, or are they three separate matters? Should they be a single matter, a single matter ought not to be spoken of as three; should they be three separate matters, why does the Buddha teach that impermanence is itself suffering, and suffering is itself no-self? Answer: the three are a single matter, namely the experiencing of a contaminated dharma. Through the distinctions made by the gate of contemplation, there come to be three differences: correspondence with the practice of impermanence is the perception of impermanence; correspondence with the practice of suffering is the perception of suffering; correspondence with the practice of no-self is the perception of no-self. Impermanence keeps one from entering the three realms; suffering causes one to know the fault belonging to the three realms; no-self, then, causes one to relinquish the world entirely.

Furthermore, impermanence gives rise to weariness; suffering gives rise to fear; no-self draws one out, causing one to obtain liberation. Furthermore, impermanence blocks the view of permanence; suffering blocks the view that takes the world itself for nirvāṇa; no-self blocks the view of attachment to any fixed place at all. Impermanence is precisely that permanent dharma which the world clings to; suffering is precisely that place of pleasure which the world reckons upon; no-self is precisely that solid, fixed self which the world reckons upon. Distinctions of every such kind are all explained at greater length in the Mahāyāna scriptures.

Reading note: The chain-argument linking impermanence, suffering, and no-self — impermanent, therefore not under one's own control, therefore not a self — is one of the oldest formal arguments in Buddhist philosophy, and this treatise's own treatment of it is notably austere: it explicitly rejects taking impermanence itself as a metaphysically "real" ground (that would just be a subtler permanence-view, or worse, nihilism), insisting instead that finding no fixed mark of impermanence at all is what actually counts as seeing the path. The staged-question format — question, then answer, repeatedly — is doing real philosophical work here rather than serving as mere rhetorical decoration, anticipating the two most natural objections a careful reader would raise (why call the noble path itself, which is also conditioned, "impermanent" but not "suffering"; and whether these three are really one thing or three) before they're asked.

The Ten Perceptions: The Impurity of Food, and a World With No Delight 十想 · 食不淨想 · 一切世間不可樂想

Chinese Source

四、食不淨想者，行者雖知無常、苦、空、無我，若於飲食猶生貪著，當修食不淨想以為對治。諦觀此食皆是不淨因緣故有，如肉是精血水道中生，是為膿蟲住處；如酥乳酪，血變所成，與爛膿無異；飯似白蟲，羹如糞汁。一切飯食，厨人執作，污垢不淨，若著口中，腦有爛涎，二道流下，與唾和合，然後成味，其狀如吐，從腹門入，地持，水爛，風動，火煮。如釜熟糜，滓濁下沈，清者在上。譬如釀酒，滓濁者為屎，清者為尿。腰有三孔，風吹膿汁，散入百脈，先與血和合，凝變為肉；從新肉生脂、骨、髓，以是因緣，故生身根。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, the perception of the impurity of food: the practitioner, though already knowing impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, may still give rise to craving and attachment toward food and drink — one should therefore cultivate the perception of the impurity of food as its own antidote. Closely contemplate that this food exists, in every case, through causes and conditions that are themselves impure: meat, for instance, arises within the channels of semen and blood, itself a dwelling-place for pus and worms; ghee and dairy curds, transformed from blood, differ not at all from festering pus; cooked rice resembles white maggots, and broth resembles the liquid of excrement. Every meal, prepared and handled by the kitchen worker, is filthy and impure; once placed within the mouth, the brain's own decaying fluid flows down through its two channels, mingling with saliva, and only then does it form a taste — its very appearance resembling vomit. Descending through the body's gate into the belly, it is held by earth, decomposed by water, stirred by wind, and cooked by fire — like porridge simmering in a pot, its dregs and sediment sinking down while the clear part rises above; or like the brewing of wine, whose dregs and sediment become excrement, and whose clear part becomes urine.

The waist has three openings, through which wind blows the resulting fluid, scattering it into the hundred vessels, where it first mingles with blood and congeals, transforming into flesh; from this fresh flesh, fat, bone, and marrow are then generated — and, through these very causes and conditions, the physical faculty of the body itself is produced.

從新舊肉令生五情根，從此五根則生五識，次第生意識，分別取相，籌量好醜，然後生我、我所心等諸煩惱及諸罪業。觀食如是本末因緣，種種不淨，知內四大與外四大則無有異，但以我見力故，強計為我有。行者如是思惟，知食罪過，若我貪著，當墮地獄、餓鬼，吞熱鐵丸，或墮畜生猪狗之中，噉食糞穢。如是觀食，則生厭想；因厭食故，五欲亦薄，即是食不淨想。

From this fresh and older flesh together, the five faculties of feeling are then generated; from these five faculties, the five sense-consciousnesses then arise, followed in turn by mind-consciousness, which distinguishes and grasps at marks, weighing and judging good and ill — and only afterward does the mind of self and what-belongs-to-self arise, together with every affliction and every unwholesome deed that follows from it. Contemplating food in this way, from its very origin through to its end, its causes and conditions manifold and impure throughout, one knows that the four elements within the body and the four elements outside it are, in truth, no different from one another — it is only through the sheer force of the view of self that one insists on reckoning it as belonging to oneself.

The practitioner, reflecting in this way, comes to know the fault inherent in food — should I still crave and cling to it, I am bound to fall into hell or among the hungry ghosts, swallowing balls of molten iron, or to fall among the animals, among pigs and dogs, devouring filth and excrement. Contemplating food in this way, weariness thereupon arises; and, through this very weariness toward food, the five desires, too, grow thin — this is precisely the perception of the impurity of food.

五、一切世間不可樂想者，行者若念世間色欲、滋味、眷屬、親里、服飾、園觀、國土、人事等，則生樂想，惡覺不息，障離欲道故。行者應當深心諦觀世間過罪之相。過罪有二種：一者、眾生；二者、國土。眾生過罪者，一切眾生皆有八苦之患，無可貪著。復觀眾生貪欲多故，不擇好醜，猶如禽獸；瞋恚重故，乃至不受佛語，不敬聞法，不畏惡道；愚癡多故，所求不以道理，不識尊卑；或慳貪、憍慢、嫉妬、很戾、諂誑、讒賊、邪見、無信、不識恩義；或罪業多故，造作五逆，不敬三寶，輕蔑善人。世間眾生善者甚少，弊惡者多，深觀如是煩惱過罪，應生厭離，如是不可親厚。

Fifth, the perception that nothing in the whole world is to be delighted in: should the practitioner call to mind the world's own sensual desire, its flavors, one's own family and kin, adornment and dress, gardens and parks, one's own homeland, and worldly affairs, a perception of pleasure thereupon arises, unwholesome thought never ceasing, obstructing the very path of freedom from desire. The practitioner should therefore, with a deep and earnest mind, closely contemplate the mark of the world's own manifold faults.

These faults are of two kinds: first, those belonging to sentient beings; second, those belonging to the land itself. As for the faults belonging to sentient beings: every sentient being is beset by the affliction of the eight sufferings, nothing in them worth craving or clinging to. One further contemplates that, since sentient beings are so given to craving and desire, they choose neither good nor ill, no different from beasts; since they are so given to heavy anger and hatred, they refuse even to accept the Buddha's own words, show no reverence in hearing the Dharma, and hold no fear of the evil destinies; since they are so given to heavy folly, what they seek follows no principle at all, unable even to distinguish the honored from the lowly; some are stingy and greedy, arrogant and conceited, envious and jealous, perverse and contrary, flattering and deceitful, slanderous and treacherous, holding wrong views, without faith, unable to recognize a debt of kindness; some, so given to heavy unwholesome deeds, commit the five heinous acts, show no reverence for the Three Jewels, and hold the virtuous in contempt. Among the sentient beings of this world, those who are good are exceedingly few, while the corrupt and evil are many — deeply contemplating afflictions and faults of this kind, one ought to give rise to weariness and detachment, recognizing that such beings are not to be drawn close to or cherished.

國土過罪者，如偈說：

或有國多寒，或有國多熱，
有國無救護，或有國多惡，
有國多饑餓，或有國多病，
有國不修福，如是無樂處。

行者深觀欲界惡事如是，無有樂處，乃至上二界果報破壞時，憂苦甚於下界，譬如極高處墮落，摧碎爛壞。經言：「三界無安，猶如火宅，眾苦充滿，甚可怖畏。」若常觀是相，則深生厭離，愛覺不生，是名世間不可樂想。

As for the faults belonging to the land itself, the verse says:

"In some lands there is great cold, in some lands there is great heat;
some lands offer no refuge or protection, some lands are rife with evil;
some lands suffer great famine, some lands are rife with sickness;
some lands cultivate no merit at all — lands such as these hold no place of joy."

The practitioner, deeply contemplating the desire realm's own evils in this way, finds no place of joy whatsoever within it — and, further still, when the retribution belonging to the two higher realms themselves comes to ruin, its sorrow and suffering exceed even that of the lower realm, like a fall from the very greatest height, shattering and crushing all the more upon landing. The sutra says: "The three realms hold no peace at all, like a house already ablaze, filled everywhere with manifold sufferings, utterly to be feared." Should one constantly contemplate this mark, deep weariness and detachment thereby arise, and the thought of love no longer arises at all — this is named the perception that nothing in the whole world is to be delighted in.

Reading note: The digestion narrative — food traced from raw ingredient through mouth, stomach, and the body's own internal alchemy of the four elements into flesh, fat, bone, and marrow — mirrors the embryology and organ-anatomy passages from fascicle 8's root world, but redirects the same clinical, unflinching attentiveness toward an object most practitioners would never think to examine this closely: dinner. The fifth perception's own two-part structure (faults of beings, then faults of the land) reframes the nine contemplations' single corpse into something civilizational in scope — not one body's decay, but every body's shared moral failure, and every land's shared hardship — building toward the "burning house" image that becomes one of the most famous metaphors in later Mahāyāna literature (most notably the parable in the Lotus Sūtra).

The Ten Perceptions: Death, Impurity, Cessation, Detachment, Exhaustion 十想 · 死想不淨想斷想離想盡想

Chinese Source

六、死想者，行者若修上來諸想，多少懈怠心生，不能疾斷漏，是時應須深修死想。如佛說死想義：有一比丘偏袒白佛：「我能修死想。」佛言：「汝云何修？」比丘言：「我不望一歲活。」佛言：「汝為放逸修死想者。」復有比丘言：「我不望七月活。」有比丘言七日、六日、五日、四日、三日、二日活。佛言：「汝等皆是放逸修死想者。」有比丘言：「從旦至食。」有一比丘言：「一食頃。」佛言：「汝等皆是放逸修死想者。」有一比丘偏袒白佛言：「我於出息不保入息，入息不保出息。」佛言：「善哉！善哉！是真修死想者，是真不放逸行。」若能如是修死想者，當知是人破懈怠賊，一切善法恒得現前，是名修死想也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth, the perception of death: should the practitioner, having cultivated all of the perceptions given above, find some measure of laxity arising in the mind, unable to swiftly sever the contaminants, at such a time one should deeply cultivate the perception of death. As the Buddha explains the sense of the perception of death: there was a monk who, baring one shoulder, addressed the Buddha: "I am able to cultivate the perception of death." The Buddha said: "How do you cultivate it?" The monk said: "I do not expect to live out the year." The Buddha said: "You are one who cultivates the perception of death negligently." There was another monk who said: "I do not expect to live out seven months." Other monks said seven days, six days, five days, four days, three days, two days. The Buddha said: "All of you are cultivating the perception of death negligently." A monk said: "From dawn until the morning meal." Another monk said: "The space of a single meal." The Buddha said: "All of you, too, are cultivating the perception of death negligently." Then there was a monk who, baring one shoulder, addressed the Buddha, saying: "For my part, upon breathing out, I do not presume upon breathing in again; upon breathing in, I do not presume upon breathing out again." The Buddha said: "Excellent! Excellent! This is the true cultivation of the perception of death — this is true, unfailing diligence." Should one be able to cultivate the perception of death in just this way, one should know that this person breaks the bandit of laxity, and every wholesome dharma comes constantly to manifest before them — this is named cultivating the perception of death.

七、不淨想者，如前通明觀，見身三十六物五種不淨，是中應廣說。八斷想、九離想、十盡想者，緣涅槃，斷煩惱結使，故名斷想；離結使故，故名為離想；盡諸結使，故名為盡想。

Seventh, the perception of impurity: as already given above, within the thorough-and-bright contemplation, seeing the body's thirty-six substances and five kinds of impurity — the matter should be explained at length there.

Eighth, the perception of cessation; ninth, the perception of detachment; tenth, the perception of exhaustion: taking nirvāṇa as the object, severing the fetters of affliction, this is named the perception of cessation; being free of the fetters, this is named the perception of detachment; exhausting every one of the fetters entirely, this is named the perception of exhaustion.

問曰：若爾者，一想便足，何故說三？答曰：如前一法三說，無常即苦，苦即無我，此想亦如是。斷想，有餘涅槃；盡想，無餘涅槃；離想，二涅槃方便門。當知壞法人成就十想即成阿羅漢，具足二種涅槃，故說九想、十想為壞法道也。十想義種種分別，具如《摩訶衍》中廣說。

Question: if this is so, a single perception alone ought to suffice — why, then, are three spoken of? Answer: just as the single dharma spoken of before as three — impermanence is itself suffering, suffering is itself no-self — this perception, too, is just the same. The perception of cessation corresponds to nirvāṇa with remainder; the perception of exhaustion corresponds to nirvāṇa without remainder; the perception of detachment is the skillful gateway common to both kinds of nirvāṇa. One should know, then, that the practitioner of the destructive path, accomplishing the ten perceptions, thereby becomes an arhat, complete with both kinds of nirvāṇa — hence the nine contemplations and the ten perceptions together are spoken of as the destructive path. The sense of the ten perceptions, distinguished in every such way, is explained at greater length in the Mahāyāna scriptures.

Reading note: The Buddha's own dialogue with the monks is a small masterpiece of escalating standards — each answer sounds, on its face, like genuine urgency (who plans on living only a week?), and each is dismissed as "negligent" all the same, until the final monk names the only interval that isn't, in principle, presumptuous: the gap between one breath and the next. It's the same breath-based method (ānāpāna) that has anchored nearly every practice in this treatise since fascicle 3, now repurposed as the measuring stick for how seriously one takes death itself. The three-way split of cessation/detachment/exhaustion mapping onto nirvāṇa-with-remainder, nirvāṇa-without-remainder, and the gateway between them closes the ten perceptions on the same doctrinal point that closed the nine contemplations: a threefold analysis of what is, at bottom, a single realization.

The Ten Perceptions and the Path — Closing Fascicle Nine 十想 · 趣道 · 卷第九竟

Chinese Source

第三、明趣道相者，即有三種：一者、漸次入壞法道，具如前說。二者、非次第壞法道。從初發心即具修十想，斷諸結使，得阿羅漢果，具足二種涅槃，故《摩訶衍》云：「若於煖、頂、忍、世間第一法，正智慧觀，離諸煩惱，是離想；得無漏道，斷結使，是斷想；入涅槃時，滅五受陰，不復相續，是名盡想。」當知從初乾慧地來，即說離想等，此則異前所說。三想併在後無學道中也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying the mark of how the ten perceptions lead toward the path: this, too, is of three kinds.

First, gradually entering the destructive path in sequence — this is entirely as already explained above.

Second, the destructive path entered out of sequence: from the very first arousal of the mind, one already cultivates the ten perceptions complete, severing every fetter, obtaining the fruit of arhatship, and becoming complete with both kinds of nirvāṇa. Hence the Mahāyāna scriptures say: "Within the stages of heat, summit, forbearance, and the supreme mundane dharma, correct wisdom's own contemplation, freeing one from every affliction — this is the perception of detachment; obtaining the undefiled path and severing the fetters — this is the perception of cessation; and, upon entering nirvāṇa, extinguishing the five aggregates of clinging so that they never again continue — this is named the perception of exhaustion." One should know, then, that, from the very stage of dry wisdom onward, the perception of detachment and the rest are already spoken of here — this differs from what was explained before, where the three perceptions were placed together within the later path of no-more-learning alone.

三者、隨分入道。若於十想之中隨修一想，善得成就，即能斷三界結使，得阿羅漢，證二種涅槃。故經云：「善修無常，能斷一切欲愛、色愛、無色愛，掉、慢、無明三界結使永盡無餘。」當知無常即是具足入道不煩惱想，下九想亦當如是一一分別趣道之相。復次，菩薩摩訶薩行菩薩道時，心廣大故，欲為一切眾生習甘露法藥，雖知諸法畢竟空寂，而亦具足成就十想。是菩薩於一一想中，次第入一切法門，旋轉無閡，為眾生說，當知十想即是菩薩摩訶衍也。

釋禪波羅蜜次第法門卷第九

Third, entering the path according to one's own portion: should one, among the ten perceptions, cultivate even a single one, skillfully bringing it to accomplishment, one is thereby able to sever the fetters of the three realms, obtain arhatship, and realize both kinds of nirvāṇa. Hence the sutra says: "Skillfully cultivating the perception of impermanence alone, one is able to sever every craving for the desire, form, and formless realms, and the fetters of agitation, conceit, and ignorance belonging to the three realms are extinguished forever, without remainder." One should know, then, that the perception of impermanence alone is already complete and sufficient for entering the path, free of affliction; the remaining nine perceptions, too, should each in turn be distinguished in this same way, with respect to the mark of how they lead toward the path.

Furthermore, when the bodhisattva-mahāsattva practices the bodhisattva path, the mind being vast and expansive, wishing to prepare the ambrosial dharma-medicine for every sentient being, although knowing that every dharma is, in the end, empty and still, nonetheless becomes complete with the accomplishment of all ten perceptions as well. This bodhisattva, within each and every perception, enters every dharma-gate in turn, circling through them without any obstruction whatsoever, expounding them for the sake of sentient beings. One should know, then, that the ten perceptions are themselves precisely the bodhisattva's own Mahāyāna.

Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen, Fascicle Nine, ends here.

Reading note: The three approaches to the path — strictly sequential, front-loaded (all ten perceptions cultivated from the very start), and partial (any single perception, skillfully accomplished, sufficing on its own) — mirror the "difference and no difference" framing that opened the whole fascicle's treatment of the nine contemplations versus the ten perceptions: the treatise consistently refuses to present a single practice ladder as the only valid one, instead mapping out several legitimate routes to the identical destination. The fascicle's closing gesture is now a familiar one, echoing the nine contemplations, the eight recollections, the six wondrous gates, and the thorough-and-bright contemplation before it: every dharma-gate in this treatise, however narrow or visceral its starting point, turns out to be "the bodhisattva's own Mahāyāna" once fully realized.
