Vaidehī's Imprisonment, and the Three Blessings

序分：禁父母緣、厭苦欣淨、三福淨業

A prince imprisons his own parents to seize the throne. His mother, shut away and grieving, begs the Buddha for a world without grief — and is shown, in a single vision, every buddha-land in the universe, before choosing one.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T365 · Kālayaśas's translation, 5th century CE Commentary guide: Shandao's 觀經四帖疏 (7th c.)
This part covers
Prince Ajātaśatru's imprisonment of his father, King Bimbisāra, and then of his own mother, Queen Vaidehī; Vaidehī's grief and her plea to the Buddha for a world without suffering; the Buddha's vision of every buddha-land in the universe, and Vaidehī's choice of Amitābha's Sukhāvatī; the Buddha's teaching of the Three Blessings, the karmic ground shared by all buddhas.
Why it matters
This is the frame that gives the whole sūtra its occasion: a very ordinary catastrophe — a family destroyed by ambition — becomes the reason the sixteen contemplations that follow are ever taught at all.

1. The Assembly, and a Prince's Betrayal

Chinese Source

如是我聞：一時，佛在王舍城耆闍崛山中，與大比丘眾千二百五十人俱；菩薩三萬二千，文殊師利法王子而為上首。爾時王舍大城有一太子，名阿闍世，隨順調達惡友之教，收執父王頻婆娑羅，幽閉置於七重室內，制諸群臣，一不得往。國大夫人，名韋提希，恭敬大王，澡浴清淨，以酥蜜和麨，用塗其身，諸瓔珞中盛葡萄漿，密以上王。爾時大王，食麨飲漿，求水漱口；漱口畢已，合掌恭敬，向耆闍崛山，遙禮世尊，而作是言：「大目乾連是吾親友，願興慈悲，授我八戒。」時目乾連如鷹隼飛，疾至王所；日日如是，授王八戒。世尊亦遣尊者富樓那，為王說法。如是時間經三七日，王食麨蜜得聞法故，顏色和悅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard. At one time the Buddha was staying on Vulture Peak, at Rājagṛha, together with a great assembly of twelve hundred and fifty monks, and thirty-two thousand bodhisattvas, foremost among them the Dharma Prince Mañjuśrī. At that time, in the great city of Rājagṛha, there was a crown prince named Ajātaśatru. Following the counsel of his wicked friend Devadatta, he seized his father, King Bimbisāra, and shut him away within seven walls, forbidding every minister to go near him. The chief consort of the realm, Queen Vaidehī, in her devotion to the king, bathed herself clean, kneaded wheat flour together with honey and ghee and smeared it over her own body, and in her jeweled necklaces concealed grape juice — and in this way secretly brought food to the king. The king ate the flour and drank the juice, then asked for water to rinse his mouth. Once he had rinsed his mouth, he pressed his palms together in reverence, faced Vulture Peak, and bowed from afar to the World-Honored One, saying: "Mahāmaudgalyāyana is my old friend — may he, out of compassion, come and confer on me the eight precepts." Then Mahāmaudgalyāyana, swift as a hawk in flight, came at once to where the king was, and conferred the eight precepts on him, day after day. The World-Honored One also sent the Venerable Pūrṇa to teach the king the Dharma. In this way three full weeks passed, and because the king had eaten the flour and honey, and had been able to hear the Dharma, his countenance remained calm and at ease.

Reading note — after Shandao

Shandao opens his commentary by noting something easy to read past: Maudgalyāyana is called the king's "old friend" (親友) because the two relations are literally distinct in Chinese — as a layman he was the king's kinsman (親); once ordained, that bond becomes friendship (友) instead, since a monk stands outside the ordinary ties of family and rank. The king, cut off from every other approach to the Buddha, reaches for the one relationship that can still cross the wall around him.

On why an imprisoned man asks specifically for the eight precepts rather than any other vow, Shandao's answer is practical: the eight precepts are a lay discipline taken for a single day and night only, renewed each morning — the most searching form of practice available to someone who does not know whether he will live to see the next sunrise. That the king takes them fresh every day, for three full weeks, is itself the content of the scene: a man facing death, spending each day he is given as carefully as if it were his last.

2. The Threat Against the Queen

Chinese Source

時阿闍世問守門人：「父王今者猶存在耶？」時守門者白言：「大王！國大夫人身塗麨蜜，瓔珞盛漿，持用上王；沙門目連及富樓那，從空而來，為王說法，不可禁制。」時阿闍世聞此語已，怒其母曰：「我母是賊，與賊為伴；沙門惡人，幻惑呪術，令此惡王多日不死。」即執利劍欲害其母。 時有一臣，名曰月光，聰明多智，及與耆婆，為王作禮。白言：「大王！臣聞毘陀論經說：『劫初已來，有諸惡王貪國位故，殺害其父一萬八千。』未曾聞有無道害母。王今為此殺逆之事，污剎利種，臣不忍聞！是栴陀羅，我等不宜復住於此。」時二大臣說此語竟，以手按劍，却行而退。時阿闍世驚怖惶懼，告耆婆言：「汝不為我耶？」耆婆白言：「大王！慎莫害母。」王聞此語，懺悔求救，即便捨劍，止不害母，勅語內官：「閉置深宮，不令復出。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Ajātaśatru asked the gatekeeper: "Is my father the king still alive?" The gatekeeper answered: "Great king — the chief consort has been smearing her body with flour and honey, and carrying juice hidden in her necklaces, to give to the king; and the monks Maudgalyāyana and Pūrṇa come flying through the air to teach him the Dharma. There is no way to stop it." Hearing this, Ajātaśatru turned in fury on his mother: "My mother is a thief, in league with thieves! And these wicked monks, with their deceiving spells, have kept this evil king alive so many days!" He seized a sharp sword, meaning to kill his mother. At that time there was a minister named Candraprabha, wise and deeply learned, who together with Jīvaka bowed before the king and said: "Great king — we have heard the Vaidalya scriptures say that since the beginning of the aeon there have been evil kings who, coveting the throne, have killed their own fathers — eighteen thousand of them. But we have never heard of one so lawless as to kill his mother. If you now commit this act of matricide, you will defile the very lineage of kings; we cannot bear to hear of it. Such a deed belongs to an outcaste — and we could no longer remain in this land." Having said this, the two ministers pressed their hands to their swords and withdrew, stepping backward. Ajātaśatru, seized with terror, said to Jīvaka: "Will you not stand by me?" Jīvaka answered: "Great king — whatever you do, do not kill your mother." Hearing this, the king repented and begged to be delivered from this act; he cast down his sword and stopped himself from killing his mother. But he still commanded his attendants: "Shut her away deep within the palace, and do not let her out again."

Reading note — after Shandao

Shandao pauses here to explain a detail the sūtra itself assumes its audience already knows: the name Ajātaśatru — literally "unborn enemy" — refers to an episode before the prince was even born. A soothsayer had told Bimbisāra that a certain hermit would soon die and be reborn as his son; impatient for an heir, the king had the hermit killed to hasten the rebirth, and was later told the coming child would harm him. Bimbisāra and Vaidehī tried to kill the infant at birth by dropping him from a high tower; he survived, with only a broken finger, giving him a second name as well — "Broken Finger." It is Devadatta, according to Shandao, who tells the grown prince this story for the first time, precisely in order to detonate it: Ajātaśatru's rage at his mother is not simply about being deceived over his father's food. It is the discovery that the parents who raised him had once tried to kill him themselves.

Shandao also notes a small but telling asymmetry in the ministers' argument: history offers eighteen thousand precedents for a king killing his father over the throne, and not one for a king killing his mother — because a mother holds no throne to be coveted. Their point is not that matricide is worse in the abstract, but that it cannot even be explained by the ordinary logic of ambition; it is a different order of act, which is exactly why they say they can no longer remain in the land if it happens.

3. Vaidehī's Lament

Chinese Source

時韋提希被幽閉已，愁憂憔悴；遙向耆闍崛山，為佛作禮，而作是言：「如來世尊在昔之時，恒遣阿難來慰問我；我今愁憂，世尊威重，無由得見；願遣目連、尊者阿難，與我相見。」作是語已，悲泣雨淚，遙向佛禮。未舉頭頃，爾時世尊在耆闍崛山，知韋提希心之所念，即勅大目揵連及以阿難，從空而來。佛從耆闍崛山沒，於王宮出。 時韋提希禮已舉頭，見世尊釋迦牟尼佛，身紫金色坐百寶蓮華。目連侍左，阿難在右，釋梵護世諸天在虛空中，普雨天華，持用供養。時韋提希見佛世尊，自絕瓔珞，舉身投地，號泣向佛。白言：「世尊！我宿何罪，生此惡子？世尊復有何等因緣，與提婆達多共為眷屬？唯願世尊，為我廣說無憂惱處，我當往生，不樂閻浮提濁惡世也。此濁惡處，地獄、餓鬼、畜生盈滿，多不善聚。願我未來不聞惡聲，不見惡人。今向世尊五體投地，求哀懺悔。唯願佛日教我觀於清淨業處。」

Ru-Yi Translation

When Vaidehī had been shut away in this manner, she grew grief-stricken and gaunt. Facing Vulture Peak from afar, she bowed toward the Buddha and said: "Tathāgata, World-Honored One — in the past you always sent Ānanda to comfort and inquire after me. Now I am overcome with grief, and the World-Honored One's majesty is too great for me to hope to see you myself. I ask only that you send Maudgalyāyana and the Venerable Ānanda, that I may see them." Having said this, she wept, her tears falling like rain, and bowed toward the Buddha from afar. Before she had even raised her head again, the World-Honored One, on Vulture Peak, knowing what was in Vaidehī's heart, at once commanded Mahāmaudgalyāyana and Ānanda to come to her through the air, while the Buddha himself vanished from Vulture Peak and appeared within the royal palace. When Vaidehī, having finished her bow, raised her head, she saw the World-Honored One Śākyamuni Buddha, his body the color of purple gold, seated upon a lotus of a hundred jewels, Maudgalyāyana attending at his left and Ānanda at his right, while Śakra, Brahmā, and the guardian devas of the world filled the sky above, raining down heavenly flowers as an offering. When Vaidehī saw the World-Honored One, she tore off her own necklaces of her own accord, flung her whole body to the ground, and wept aloud before the Buddha, saying: "World-Honored One — what sin did I commit in a former life, that I should bear such a wicked son? And what karmic bond does the World-Honored One himself have, to be kin to Devadatta? I ask only that the World-Honored One set forth for me, at length, a place free of grief and affliction, where I may go to be reborn — I no longer wish to remain in this defiled and evil world of Jambudvīpa. This defiled and evil place is filled to overflowing with hells, hungry ghosts, and animals, and heaped with so much that is not good. I vow that in the future I will neither hear evil words nor see evil people. Now, before the World-Honored One, I throw all five limbs of my body to the ground and beg, in sorrow, for your forgiveness. I ask only that the Buddha, like the sun, teach me to contemplate a place of pure karma."

Reading note — after Shandao

Shandao is careful about one point that a modern reader might rush past: Vaidehī's opening question — what karma binds the Buddha himself to Devadatta, his own cousin and betrayer — is not idle curiosity. He reads it as the queen extending her own bewilderment about her son onto the Buddha's situation, as if to say: even you, who have ended all affliction, still carry a troubled kinsman at your side; so how am I, an ordinary woman, to make sense of mine? The Buddha's answer to this particular question is never given directly in the sūtra — what follows instead is an entirely different kind of answer, a vision of somewhere else to go.

On Vaidehī tearing off her own necklaces before throwing herself to the ground, Shandao reads this as more than grief — a woman of her rank would ordinarily never appear in such disarray, attended as she always was; stripping herself of ornament in front of the Buddha is itself an act of confession, a visible renunciation of the very rank and comfort that had insulated her from ever imagining a scene like this.

4. The Buddha Shows Every Pure Land, and Vaidehī Chooses

Chinese Source

爾時世尊放眉間光，其光金色，遍照十方無量世界；還住佛頂，化為金臺，如須彌山。十方諸佛淨妙國土，皆於中現。或有國土七寶合成；復有國土純是蓮花；復有國土如自在天宮；復有國土如頗梨鏡；十方國土皆於中現。有如是等無量諸佛國土嚴顯可觀，令韋提希見。 時韋提希白佛言：「世尊！是諸佛土，雖復清淨，皆有光明；我今樂生極樂世界阿彌陀佛所。唯願世尊，教我思惟，教我正受。」 爾時世尊即便微笑，有五色光，從佛口出，一一光照頻婆娑羅王頂。爾時大王雖在幽閉，心眼無障，遙見世尊，頭面作禮；自然增進，成阿那含。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then the World-Honored One released light from between his eyebrows, light the color of gold, illuminating the immeasurable worlds of the ten directions; the light returned and settled upon the Buddha's own crown, transformed into a golden platform shaped like Mount Sumeru, and upon it appeared the pure and wondrous lands of every buddha of the ten directions. Some lands were formed wholly of the seven treasures; others were made entirely of lotus flowers; others resembled the palaces of the heaven of free enjoyment; still others were like mirrors of crystal — the lands of all ten directions appeared there at once. There were immeasurable buddha-lands of this kind, adorned and radiant and fit to behold, and the Buddha let Vaidehī see them all. Then Vaidehī said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One — these buddha-lands, pure as they all are, are each filled with light. But it is Sukhāvatī, the land of Amitābha Buddha, where I now wish to be reborn. I ask only that the World-Honored One teach me how to hold that land in contemplation, teach me how to enter right absorption upon it." Then the World-Honored One smiled, and five-colored light streamed out from the Buddha's mouth, each ray of it shining upon the crown of King Bimbisāra's head. And the great king, though still shut away in confinement, found the eye of his mind unobstructed; he beheld the World-Honored One from afar and bowed his head to him — and of himself, without further teaching, he advanced to the fruit of Anāgāmin, the Non-Returner.

Reading note — after Shandao

Shandao raises a question worth sitting with: Vaidehī had asked the Buddha to "explain fully" a place free of grief — so why does he answer not with an explanation, but by making an entire panorama of buddha-lands appear before her all at once, with no land yet singled out as superior? His reading is that this is deliberate: had the Buddha simply told her the one best place to go, she would be taking it on faith; by showing her the comparative range of what exists first, her eventual choice of Sukhāvatī becomes her own act of discernment, made with the evidence in front of her, not a conclusion handed to her from outside.

He is equally direct that all buddha-lands are, in the ultimate sense, equally the fruit of ended affliction — so the various lands' apparent differences in splendor are not differences in the buddhas' attainment, but differences suited to what beings need to see. And he treats Bimbisāra's spontaneous advancement to the third fruit, reached the instant light from the Buddha's own mouth touches his crown, as this section's real point: a man in a locked room, at the mercy of his own son, is not left worse off by circumstance than someone free to practice at leisure — the light that reaches him is exactly as effective shut inside seven walls as anywhere else.

5. The Three Blessings

Chinese Source

爾時世尊告韋提希：「汝今知不？阿彌陀佛去此不遠；汝當繫念，諦觀彼國淨業成者。我今為汝廣說眾譬，亦令未來世一切凡夫欲修淨業者，得生西方極樂國土。欲生彼國者，當修三福：一者、孝養父母，奉事師長，慈心不殺，修十善業。二者、受持三歸，具足眾戒，不犯威儀。三者、發菩提心，深信因果，讀誦大乘，勸進行者。如此三事名為淨業。」 佛告韋提希：「汝今知不？此三種業，乃是過去、未來、現在三世諸佛淨業正因。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Then the World-Honored One said to Vaidehī: "Do you know this now? Amitābha Buddha is not far from here. You should fix your thoughts, and contemplate carefully that land where pure karma has come to fruition. I shall now set forth many similes for you at length, and shall also enable all ordinary beings of the future who wish to cultivate pure karma to attain rebirth in the western land of Sukhāvatī. Anyone who wishes to be born in that land should cultivate the Three Blessings. The first: to care for and nourish one's father and mother, to serve one's teachers and elders, to hold a compassionate mind that does no killing, and to cultivate the ten wholesome actions. The second: to take up and hold the Three Refuges, to keep every precept in full, and to commit no breach of proper conduct. The third: to give rise to the mind of awakening, to believe deeply in cause and effect, to read and recite the Mahāyāna scriptures, and to encourage others who practice. These three things together are called pure karma." The Buddha said to Vaidehī: "Do you know this now? These three kinds of karmic action are the true and proper cause of the pure karma of every buddha of the past, the future, and the present."

Reading note — after Shandao

Shandao treats this passage as the hinge of the entire sūtra's method, not a minor preamble to the sixteen contemplations that follow. His analysis divides all practice taught in this sūtra into two kinds: the "meditative good" (定善, ding shan) of the sixteen contemplations — stilling scattered thought until it can hold a single image — and the "dispersed good" (散善, san shan) of the Three Blessings, ordinary ethical and devotional conduct carried out amid an unstilled, everyday mind. He is explicit that the Buddha teaches both because no single method reaches every capacity: the contemplations that follow are for those who can still their minds; the Three Blessings are for everyone else, which in practice means nearly everyone. Nothing in the sixteen contemplations to come replaces this passage — it is this sūtra's own statement that the entire path it is about to describe rests on an ethical foundation shared by every buddha who has ever existed, not on visualization technique alone.

He also draws out something easy to miss in the phrasing: the Buddha does not say these three blessings are merely useful for reaching Sukhāvatī — he says they are "the true and proper cause of the pure karma of every buddha of the past, future, and present." On Shandao's reading, that claim is deliberately larger than this sūtra's own occasion: filial care, ethical discipline, and the resolve for awakening are not a Pure Land technique at all, but the shared ground beneath every buddha's path, of which the contemplations about to be taught are one particular application.

6. The Promise to Teach the Sixteen Contemplations

Chinese Source

佛告阿難及韋提希：「諦聽，諦聽！善思念之。如來今者，為未來世一切眾生為煩惱賊之所害者，說清淨業。善哉韋提希！快問此事。阿難！汝當受持，廣為多眾宣說佛語。如來今者，教韋提希及未來世一切眾生觀於西方極樂世界。以佛力故，當得見彼清淨國土，如執明鏡自見面像。見彼國土極妙樂事，心歡喜故，應時即得無生法忍。」 佛告韋提希：「汝是凡夫，心想羸劣，未得天眼，不能遠觀；諸佛如來有異方便，令汝得見。」 時韋提希白佛言：「世尊！如我今者，以佛力故見彼國土。若佛滅後，諸眾生等，濁惡不善，五苦所逼，云何當見阿彌陀佛極樂世界？」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Ānanda and Vaidehī: "Listen closely, listen closely, and reflect well on this. The Tathāgata is now, for the sake of all beings of future ages who are harmed by the thief of affliction, about to teach pure karma. Well done, Vaidehī — this is an excellent question. Ānanda, you must uphold this teaching, and proclaim the Buddha's words widely to the many. The Tathāgata will now teach Vaidehī, and all beings of future ages, to contemplate the western land of Sukhāvatī. Through the Buddha's power, one will come to see that pure land exactly as one sees one's own face reflected in a bright mirror; and on seeing the utterly wondrous and joyful things of that land, one's mind will fill with joy, and one will thereupon at once attain the acceptance of the non-arising of all dharmas." The Buddha said to Vaidehī: "You are an ordinary being, whose power of mind is weak; you have not yet attained the heavenly eye, and cannot see things far away. But the buddhas and tathāgatas have a special skillful means that will enable you to see it nonetheless." Then Vaidehī said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One — as for myself, I am able to see that land now only through the Buddha's own power. But after the Buddha has passed into extinction, how will the multitude of beings — defiled, unwholesome, and pressed upon by the five sufferings — ever manage to see Amitābha's land of Sukhāvatī?"

Reading note — after Shandao

Shandao reads Vaidehī's closing question as the real turning point of the entire preface: up to now, everything shown to her — the panorama of buddha-lands, the light that reaches her imprisoned husband — has depended on being in the Buddha's own living presence. Her question forces the teaching to address everyone who will never have that: every reader of this sūtra after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, living in what she names outright as a defiled and unwholesome age. The sixteen contemplations that occupy the rest of this sūtra are, on Shandao's reading, the Buddha's direct answer to exactly this anxiety — a method that does not require his physical presence to work, because it works through the practitioner's own trained contemplation reaching a land that is "not far from here," as he told her a few lines earlier.

He also flags something the sūtra states almost in passing and should not be read past too quickly: Vaidehī herself, though she appears here as an ordinary grieving queen, is understood in the tradition to already be a bodhisattva who has taken on an ordinary woman's suffering deliberately, precisely so that the Buddha's answer to her can double as an answer for every genuinely ordinary being who reads this sūtra after her. The Buddha says so explicitly a few lines on: "you are an ordinary being" is addressed to her role in the story, not a claim about her actual attainment — and Shandao takes care that readers not mistake her for someone whose situation does not apply to their own.
