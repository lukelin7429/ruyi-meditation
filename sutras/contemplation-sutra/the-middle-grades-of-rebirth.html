The Middle Grades of Rebirth

中輩生想：中品上生、中品中生、中品下生

The bar drops sharply: a single day and night of pure precept-keeping, or nothing more than ordinary filial devotion met, at the very end of life, by the right person saying the right thing at the right moment.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T365 · Kālayaśas's translation, 5th century CE Commentary guide: Shandao's 觀經四帖疏 (7th c.)
This part covers
The fifteenth contemplation: the three middle grades of rebirth — middle-upper, middle-middle, and middle-lower — continuing the nine grades begun in Part 5.
Shandao vs. Huiyuan, briefly
As with the upper grades, Jingying Huiyuan reads these middle grades as describing practitioners who have already attained specific stages of the śrāvaka path (stream-enterer through non-returner) before death; Shandao reads all three as ordinary beings who attain nothing until arriving in Sukhāvatī. See Part 5 for the fuller discussion of this disagreement.

24. Middle-Upper Birth

Chinese Source

佛告阿難及韋提希：「中品上生者，若有眾生受持五戒，持八戒齋，修行諸戒，不造五逆，無眾過惡；以此善根，迴向願求生於西方極樂世界。行者臨命終時，阿彌陀佛與諸比丘，眷屬圍繞，放金色光至其人所，演說苦、空、無常、無我，讚歎出家得離眾苦。行者見已，心大歡喜。自見己身坐蓮花臺，長跪合掌為佛作禮。未舉頭頃即得往生極樂世界，蓮花尋開。當華敷時，聞眾音聲讚歎四諦，應時即得阿羅漢道，三明、六通、具八解脫；是名中品上生者。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Ānanda and Vaidehī: "As for middle-upper birth: if there are beings who receive and keep the five precepts, or the eight precepts and fast, or who practice every precept, who commit none of the five heinous transgressions and hold no accumulation of faults — with this wholesome root, they dedicate it and vow to be born in the western land of Sukhāvatī. When such a practitioner's life is about to end, Amitābha Buddha, surrounded by an assembly of monks, sends forth golden light to where that person is, proclaiming suffering, emptiness, impermanence, and no-self, and praising the going-forth into monastic life that frees one from every suffering. Seeing this, the practitioner's heart fills with great joy, and they see themselves seated upon a lotus throne, kneeling with palms joined in reverence to the Buddha. Before they have even raised their head again, they attain rebirth in Sukhāvatī, and the lotus flower opens at once. As the flower opens, they hear the assembled voices praising the four noble truths, and at once attain the fruit of arhatship, complete with the three insights, the six supernatural powers, and the eight liberations. This is called middle-upper birth."

Reading note — after Shandao

As with every grade in this sūtra, Shandao reads the qualifying practice here plainly: five or eight precepts, kept without the five heinous transgressions or any accumulation of serious fault — an ordinary layperson's ethical discipline, nothing more specialized. He glosses the resulting attainment in technical terms worth spelling out, since the sūtra names them without explanation: the "three insights" are knowledge of past lives, the heavenly eye that sees where beings are reborn, and the knowledge that one's own afflictions are exhausted; the "eight liberations" are a graded sequence of meditative masteries over form and formlessness, culminating in the cessation of perception and feeling altogether. All of this, on his reading, is attained only once the lotus opens in Sukhāvatī — not brought with the practitioner from this life.

This is also where his disagreement with Jingying Huiyuan continues in miniature. Huiyuan's commentary identifies middle-upper birth with practitioners who have already reached one of the first three fruits of the śrāvaka path — stream-enterer, once-returner, or non-returner — since the passage states they attain arhatship immediately on arrival, which he reads as the final step of a path already well underway. Shandao's response, consistent with his reading of the upper grades in Part 5, is that a person who had already reached even the first of these fruits would have no further danger of falling into the lower realms and little apparent need of Vaidehī's request on their behalf at all — he reads the passage instead as describing an ordinary keeper of precepts who becomes an arhat only in Sukhāvatī, after arriving there empty-handed but for a clean ethical record.

25. Middle-Middle Birth

Chinese Source

「中品中生者，若有眾生，若一日一夜持八戒齋，若一日一夜持沙彌戒，若一日一夜持具足戒，威儀無缺。以此功德，迴向願求生極樂國。戒香薰修，如此行者命欲終時，見阿彌陀佛與諸眷屬放金色光，持七寶蓮花至行者前，行者自聞空中有聲，讚言：『善男子！如汝善人，隨順三世諸佛教故，我來迎汝。』行者自見坐蓮花上，蓮花即合，生於西方極樂世界。在寶池中，經於七日蓮花乃敷。花既敷已，開目合掌，讚歎世尊。聞法歡喜，得須陀洹，經半劫已成阿羅漢；是名中品中生者。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"As for middle-middle birth: if there are beings who, for a single day and night, keep the eight precepts and fast, or for a single day and night keep the novice precepts, or for a single day and night keep the full monastic precepts, with no lapse in proper conduct — with this merit, they dedicate it and vow to be born in Sukhāvatī, the fragrance of precept-keeping suffusing their practice. When such a practitioner's life is about to end, they see Amitābha Buddha, together with his retinue, sending forth golden light and carrying a lotus flower of the seven treasures to before them, and the practitioner hears, of themselves, a voice from the sky, saying in praise: 'Good man, because you, a good person, have followed the teaching of the buddhas of the three times, I have come to welcome you.' The practitioner sees themselves seated upon the lotus flower; the flower then closes, and they are born in the western land of Sukhāvatī. Within the jeweled pond, after seven days, the lotus flower opens. Once it has opened, they open their eyes, press their palms together, and praise the World-Honored One. Hearing the Dharma, they rejoice, and attain the fruit of stream-entry; after half an aeon has passed, they become an arhat. This is called middle-middle birth."

Reading note — after Shandao

The single day and night of precept-keeping described here is the same discipline King Bimbisāra took up daily from inside his locked cell in Part 1 — a lay discipline renewed each morning, demanding enough that it functions almost as a monastic vow compressed into a single day. Shandao treats the three options given here — the eight precepts, the novice precepts, or the full monastic precepts, all held for the same single day and night — as functionally equivalent for this purpose: what matters is not which set of precepts, but that whichever set is chosen is kept completely, without a single lapse, for the full stretch of time committed to. Jingying Huiyuan, continuing his reading of the grade above this one, identifies middle-middle birth with beings still short of the śrāvaka fruits — "outer and inner ordinary people prior to the path of seeing" in his terms — closer to Shandao's own reading here than in the grades above, though Huiyuan still frames the distinction in terms of formal stages on the śrāvaka path that Shandao's commentary does not adopt.

26. Middle-Lower Birth

Chinese Source

「中品下生者，若有善男子、善女人，孝養父母，行世仁義，此人命欲終時，遇善知識為其廣說阿彌陀佛國土樂事，亦說法藏比丘四十八大願。聞此事已，尋即命終。譬如壯士屈伸臂頃，即生西方極樂世界。生經七日，遇觀世音及大勢至，聞法歡喜得須陀洹。過一小劫，成阿羅漢。是名中品下生者。是名中輩生想，名第十五觀。作是觀者，名為正觀。若他觀者，名為邪觀。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"As for middle-lower birth: if there is a good son or good daughter who cares for and nourishes their father and mother, and practices ordinary worldly benevolence and righteousness, and when this person's life is about to end, they meet a good friend who explains for them, at length, the joyful things of Amitābha's land, and also relates the forty-eight great vows of the monk Dharmākara — on hearing this, they die soon after, and, as quickly as a strong man might bend and stretch his arm, they are reborn in the western land of Sukhāvatī. Having been reborn there, after seven days they meet Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta, and, hearing the Dharma, rejoice and attain the fruit of stream-entry. After one small aeon has passed, they become an arhat. This is called middle-lower birth. This is called the contemplation of the middle grades of rebirth, the fifteenth contemplation. To contemplate in this way is called right contemplation; to contemplate otherwise is called wrong contemplation."

Reading note — after Shandao

Shandao points out something distinctive about this grade that separates it from every other grade in the nine: this person has no religious practice at all, Buddhist or otherwise. They are, in his words, someone who "naturally practiced filial piety while in the world, not for the sake of seeking liberation" — their care for their parents and their ordinary decency toward others were never aimed at rebirth in any pure land, or at any spiritual goal whatsoever. What changes their fate is entirely external to anything they did: at the very end of life, a good friend happens to explain Sukhāvatī's joys and Dharmākara's vows to them, and hearing this account for the first time is enough, on its own, to carry them there.

This grade sits at a hinge point in the nine grades as a whole. Every grade above it, in Shandao's reading, involves at least some degree of deliberate practice undertaken with rebirth already in mind — precepts kept, scriptures read, faith cultivated. Middle-lower birth is the first grade built entirely on an encounter that could not have been planned for: ordinary secular virtue, present for its own sake, met by chance with the right teaching at the last possible moment. It is, in this sense, a hinge toward the three lower grades that follow in Part 7, where the sūtra goes further still, and asks what becomes of people who, at the end of life, have nothing to their name but wrongdoing.
