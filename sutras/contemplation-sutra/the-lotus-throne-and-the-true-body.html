The Lotus Throne, and the True Body

華座觀、像觀、真身觀

Amitāyus Buddha appears standing in the open sky. Vaidehī is taught to build his lotus throne in her mind, then his image, then — in this sūtra's most quoted line — is told that the mind contemplating the Buddha is itself already the Buddha it beholds.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T365 · Kālayaśas's translation, 5th century CE Commentary guide: Shandao's 觀經四帖疏 (7th c.)
This part covers
Amitāyus Buddha's sudden appearance, standing, with Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta at his sides (introducing the seventh through ninth contemplations); the jeweled lotus throne (第七觀); the image contemplation and the line "this mind makes the buddha, this mind is the buddha" (第八觀); and the true body of Amitāyus, whose light embraces without abandoning anyone mindful of him (第九觀).
Why it matters
The ninth contemplation's account of the Buddha's embracing light became, through Shandao's threefold explanation translated below, one of the single most influential passages in the entire East Asian and Japanese Pure Land tradition — the doctrinal seed of exclusive reliance on mindfulness of the Buddha's name.

13. Amitāyus Appears, Standing in the Sky

Chinese Source

佛告阿難及韋提希：「諦聽，諦聽！善思念之。吾當為汝分別解說除苦惱法，汝等憶持，廣為大眾分別解說。」說是語時，無量壽佛住立空中，觀世音、大勢至，是二大士侍立左右。光明熾盛不可具見，百千閻浮檀金色不可為比。 時韋提希見無量壽佛已，接足作禮，白佛言：「世尊！我今因佛力故，得見無量壽佛及二菩薩，未來眾生，當云何觀無量壽佛及二菩薩？」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Ānanda and Vaidehī: "Listen closely, listen closely, and reflect well on this. I shall now explain for you, point by point, the method for removing suffering and affliction; hold this in mind, and explain it, point by point, to the great assembly." As he spoke these words, Amitāyus Buddha appeared, standing in the open sky, with Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta, the two great bodhisattvas, attending at his left and right. His light blazed with such brilliance that it could not be fully beheld, outshining a hundred thousand measures of Jāmbūnada gold, beyond any comparison. When Vaidehī saw Amitāyus Buddha, she touched her head to his feet in reverence, and said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One — through the Buddha's power I have now been able to see Amitāyus Buddha and the two bodhisattvas. But beings of the future — how are they to contemplate Amitāyus Buddha and the two bodhisattvas?"

Reading note — after Shandao

Shandao asks a question a reader might not think to ask: why does a buddha of such majesty appear here standing, rather than seated in the dignified posture used everywhere else in this sūtra? His answer is that the posture itself is the teaching. This world, he writes, is a burning house — its inhabitants surrounded by affliction on every side, false friendships smiling while six thieves follow close behind, the fires of the three evil destinies already close enough to feel. A buddha who remained seated, waiting to be approached in the ordinary way, would not suit a situation this urgent. Amitāyus stands because the moment any being turns their mind toward his land with sincere intent, he is already on his feet to come to them — the same posture, Shandao notes, that reappears later in this sūtra at the moment of death, when Amitāyus and his assembly come in person to receive the dying. Standing, here, is a promise of speed, not merely a variation in etiquette.

14. The Seventh Contemplation: The Lotus Throne

Chinese Source

佛告韋提希：「欲觀彼佛者，當起想念，於七寶地上作蓮花想，令其蓮花一一葉作百寶色。有八萬四千脈，猶如天畫；一一脈有八萬四千光，了了分明，皆令得見。華葉小者，縱廣二百五十由旬；如是蓮華有八萬四千大葉，一一葉間，有百億摩尼珠王以為映飾。一一摩尼珠放千光明，其光如蓋，七寶合成，遍覆地上。釋迦毘楞伽摩尼寶以為其臺；此蓮花臺，八萬金剛甄叔迦寶，梵摩尼寶，妙真珠網，以為交飾。於其臺上，自然而有四柱寶幢，一一寶幢如百千萬億須彌山；幢上寶縵如夜摩天宮，復有五百億微妙寶珠，以為映飾。一一寶珠有八萬四千光，一一光作八萬四千異種金色，一一金色遍其寶土，處處變化，各作異相；或為金剛臺，或作真珠網，或作雜花雲，於十方面隨意變現，施作佛事，是為花座想，名第七觀。」 佛告阿難：「如此妙花，是本法藏比丘願力所成，若欲念彼佛者，當先作此妙花座想。作此想時不得雜觀；皆應一一觀之，一一葉，一一珠，一一光，一一臺，一一幢，皆令分明，如於鏡中自見面像。此想成者，滅除五百億劫生死之罪，必定當生極樂世界。作是觀者，名為正觀。若他觀者，名為邪觀。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Vaidehī: "To contemplate that Buddha, give rise to this thought: upon the ground of the seven treasures, form a contemplation of a lotus flower, its every petal a hundred jeweled colors. It has eighty-four thousand veins, like a painting drawn by the devas, and each vein has eighty-four thousand rays of light, utterly clear and distinct, all of them visible at once. Its smallest petals measure two hundred and fifty yojanas across; this lotus has eighty-four thousand such great petals, and between each petal are a hundred koṭis of maṇi jewel-kings, adding further radiance. Each maṇi jewel radiates a thousand rays of light, and this light forms canopies of the seven treasures, covering the ground entirely. Śakra-abhilagna maṇi jewel forms the flower's central platform; this lotus platform is further adorned, woven together, with eighty thousand vajra kiṃśuka jewels, brahma-maṇi jewels, and wondrous pearl netting. Upon this platform there arise, of themselves, four jeweled banner-pillars, each banner like a hundred thousand koṭis of Mount Sumerus; and upon these banners hang jeweled curtains like the palaces of the Yāma heaven, further adorned with five hundred koṭis of wondrous jewels. Each of these jewels has eighty-four thousand rays of light, and each ray forms eighty-four thousand different shades of gold, and each shade of gold, spreading throughout that jeweled land, transforms everywhere it touches, taking on ever-different forms — now a vajra platform, now a net of pearls, now a cloud of mingled flowers — manifesting freely in every one of the ten directions, and everywhere performing a buddha's work. This is the contemplation of the lotus throne, called the seventh contemplation." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "This wondrous flower came to be through the power of the vow of the monk Dharmākara himself, in his causal ground. If you wish to hold that Buddha in mindfulness, you should first form this contemplation of the wondrous lotus throne. When forming this contemplation, do not observe it all mixed together — each petal, each jewel, each ray of light, each platform, each banner should be contemplated one at a time, until each is as distinct as your own face seen in a mirror. Whoever completes this contemplation extinguishes the karmic offenses of five hundred koṭis of aeons of birth and death, and will certainly be born in Sukhāvatī. To contemplate in this way is called right contemplation; to contemplate otherwise is called wrong contemplation."

Reading note — after Shandao

Shandao is explicit about who "the monk Dharmākara" is: the same bodhisattva-monk whose forty-eight vows, related in full in the larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra, became the causal ground for everything Sukhāvatī now contains — this lotus throne, like every other feature of that land described in this sūtra, is presented here as one further, concrete fruit of those vows, not a separate creation. Readers of this site's page on Dharmākara's Resolve are meeting, in this single jeweled flower, the outcome of the five aeons of contemplation described there.

On the instruction not to "observe all mixed together," Shandao's point is procedural and meant to be taken literally: petal, then jewel, then light, then platform, then banner, each held in the mind one at a time in sequence, never attempted all at once. He treats this as the method behind every contemplation in this sūtra, not unique to the lotus throne — the difference between a practitioner who succeeds and one who does not, on his account, usually comes down to whether they respected this ordering or tried to force the entire scene into view simultaneously.

15. The Eighth Contemplation: The Image, and "This Mind Is the Buddha"

Chinese Source

佛告阿難及韋提希：「見此事已，次當想佛。所以者何？諸佛如來是法界身，遍入一切眾生心想中；是故汝等心想佛時，是心即是三十二相、八十隨形好。是心作佛，是心是佛。諸佛正遍知海，從心想生，是故應當一心繫念，諦觀彼佛、多陀阿伽度、阿羅呵、三藐三佛陀。想彼佛者，先當想像。閉目開目，見一寶像，如閻浮檀金色，坐彼華上。像既坐已，心眼得開，了了分明，見極樂國七寶莊嚴，寶地、寶池、寶樹行列，諸天寶縵彌覆樹上，眾寶羅網滿虛空中。見如此事，極令明了，如觀掌中。見此事已，復當更作一大蓮華在佛左邊，如前蓮華等無有異。復作一大蓮華在佛右邊，想一觀世音菩薩像坐左華座，亦放金光如前無異。想一大勢至菩薩像坐右華座。此想成時，佛菩薩像皆放妙光；其光金色，照諸寶樹。一一樹下亦有三蓮華，諸蓮華上各有一佛二菩薩像，遍滿彼國。此想成時，行者當聞水流、光明及諸寶樹、鳧鴈、鴛鴦皆說妙法。出定、入定恒聞妙法。行者所聞，出定之時憶持不捨，令與修多羅合。若不合者，名為妄想，若與合者，名為麁想見極樂世界。是為想像，名第八觀。作是觀者，除無量億劫生死之罪，於現身中得念佛三昧。作是觀者，名為正觀。若他觀者，名為邪觀。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Ānanda and Vaidehī: "Having seen this, next contemplate the Buddha himself. Why is this so? Every buddha tathāgata is a body of the dharma-realm, entering fully into the mind and thought of every being; therefore, when your own mind contemplates the Buddha, this very mind is itself the thirty-two marks and the eighty subsidiary excellent features. This mind makes the buddha; this mind is the buddha. The ocean of perfect and universal knowing of all buddhas arises from the mind's own contemplation — therefore you should hold your thought single-pointed, and contemplate carefully that Buddha, the Tathāgata, the Worthy One, the Perfectly and Fully Awakened One. To contemplate that Buddha, first contemplate his image. Whether your eyes are open or closed, see a single jeweled image, the color of Jāmbūnada gold, seated upon that lotus. Once the image is seated, the eye of your mind opens, and you see, utterly clear and distinct, the land of Sukhāvatī adorned with the seven treasures — its jeweled ground, its jeweled ponds, its rows of jeweled trees, heavenly jeweled curtains covering the trees, and jeweled netting filling the whole sky. See all this until it is utterly clear, as if seen in the palm of your own hand. Having seen this, next form a second great lotus flower to the Buddha's left, exactly like the first, and another great lotus flower to his right; picture an image of Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva seated on the left throne, radiating golden light exactly as before, and an image of Mahāsthāmaprāpta Bodhisattva seated on the right throne. When this contemplation is complete, the images of the Buddha and the bodhisattvas will all radiate wondrous light, golden in color, illuminating the jeweled trees; and beneath each tree there will likewise be three lotus flowers, and upon each of these flowers, images of one buddha and two bodhisattvas, filling that entire land. When this contemplation is complete, the practitioner will hear the flowing water, the light, the jeweled trees, and the wild geese and mandarin ducks, all proclaiming the wondrous Dharma; whether in meditative absorption or out of it, this wondrous Dharma will always be heard. What the practitioner hears, on emerging from absorption, should be held in mind and not let go, and checked against the sūtras: if it does not accord with them, it is to be called a delusion; if it does accord, it is to be called a rough sighting of Sukhāvatī. This is the contemplation of the image, called the eighth contemplation. Whoever makes this contemplation removes the karmic offenses of immeasurable koṭis of aeons of birth and death, and attains, in this very body, the samādhi of buddha-recollection. To contemplate in this way is called right contemplation; to contemplate otherwise is called wrong contemplation."

Reading note — after Shandao

This is the most quoted line in the sūtra — "this mind makes the buddha, this mind is the buddha" (是心作佛，是心是佛) — and Shandao is careful to explain it in a way that heads off a specific misreading. He first unpacks "dharma-realm body": a buddha's mind is unobstructed and pervades everywhere (心遍法界); a buddha's body likewise pervades everywhere (身遍法界); and nothing obstructs either — so when a being's mind forms the wish to see a buddha, that buddha's unobstructed knowing already knows it, and manifests within that very thought, exactly as wished. "This mind makes the buddha" means the practitioner's own believing mind, taking up the Buddha's marks one by one, forms the image as an act of construction; "this mind is the buddha" means that the mind doing the contemplating and the buddha being contemplated are, in that moment, not two separate things — there is no other buddha to be found somewhere outside this same contemplating mind.

Then comes his warning, stated as sharply as anywhere in his commentary: some practitioners, he writes, take this passage as teaching a formless "mind-only dharma-body" contemplation, or a contemplation of one's own inherently pure buddha-nature — and this, he says, is a serious misunderstanding, "not resembling the sūtra's meaning in the slightest." The dharma-body has no color and cannot be met with the eye or grasped as an object — which is exactly why this whole section of the sūtra teaches practitioners to fix on a concrete image with definite marks (立相住心) rather than to seek something formless. His reasoning is severely practical: the Buddha, foreseeing that ordinary beings of this degenerate age can barely achieve concentration even while holding onto a visible object, judged that abandoning form altogether and seeking realization directly would be hopeless for them — "like someone with no magical power trying to build a house floating in empty space." This passage, on Shandao's reading, is not an invitation to formless contemplation dressed in devotional language; it is a concrete visualization method, deliberately kept concrete because the audience it was designed for needs exactly that.

16. The Ninth Contemplation: The True Body, and the Light That Embraces

Chinese Source

佛告阿難及韋提希：「此想成已，次當更觀無量壽佛身相光明。阿難當知！無量壽佛身，如百千萬億夜摩天閻浮檀金色。佛身高六十萬億那由他恒河沙由旬；眉間白毫右旋宛轉，如五須彌山。佛眼清淨如四大海水，清白分明。身諸毛孔演出光明，如須彌山。彼佛圓光如百億三千大千世界；於圓光中，有百萬億那由他恒河沙化佛；一一化佛，亦有眾多無數化菩薩，以為侍者。無量壽佛有八萬四千相；一一相中，各有八萬四千隨形好；一一好中復有八萬四千光明；一一光明遍照十方世界，念佛眾生攝取不捨。其光相好及與化佛，不可具說；但當憶想令心明見。見此事者，即見十方一切諸佛，以見諸佛故名念佛三昧。作是觀者，名觀一切佛身，以觀佛身故亦見佛心。諸佛心者大慈悲是，以無緣慈攝諸眾生。作此觀者，捨身他世，生諸佛前，得無生忍。是故智者應當繫心諦觀無量壽佛。觀無量壽佛者，從一相好入，但觀眉間白毫，極令明了。見眉間白毫相者，八萬四千相好自然當見。見無量壽佛者，即見十方無量諸佛；得見無量諸佛故，諸佛現前受記。是為遍觀一切色想，名第九觀。作是觀者，名為正觀。若他觀者，名為邪觀。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Ānanda and Vaidehī: "When this contemplation is complete, next contemplate the bodily form and light of Amitāyus Buddha himself. Ānanda, you should know: Amitāyus Buddha's body is the color of a hundred thousand koṭis of Yāma-heaven Jāmbūnada gold. That Buddha's body is sixty koṭi nayutas of Ganges-sands of yojanas in height; the white tuft of hair between his brows, coiling to the right, is as large as five Mount Sumerus. The Buddha's eyes are pure as the water of the four great oceans, their black and white utterly distinct. Every pore of his body sends forth light, each as vast as Mount Sumeru. That Buddha's halo is as wide as a hundred koṭis of great-thousand-worlds, and within this halo appear a hundred koṭi nayutas of Ganges-sands of transformation-buddhas, and each transformation-buddha has, in turn, countless transformation-bodhisattvas attending him. Amitāyus Buddha has eighty-four thousand marks; within each mark are eighty-four thousand subsidiary excellent features; within each feature are eighty-four thousand rays of light; and each ray of light shines throughout the worlds of the ten directions, embracing and never abandoning any being who is mindful of the Buddha. His light, his marks and features, and his transformation-buddhas cannot be fully described in words; simply hold them in mind until your mind sees them clearly. Whoever sees this thereby sees all buddhas of the ten directions — and because they see all buddhas, this is called the samādhi of buddha-recollection. To make this contemplation is called contemplating the body of every buddha; and because one contemplates a buddha's body, one thereby also sees a buddha's mind. A buddha's mind is great compassion itself, embracing all beings with a compassion that seeks no cause outside itself. Whoever makes this contemplation, on setting this body aside in a future life, will be born before the buddhas, and will attain the acceptance of the non-arising of all dharmas. For this reason, the wise should hold their minds fixed and carefully contemplate Amitāyus Buddha. To contemplate Amitāyus Buddha, enter through a single mark: simply contemplate the white tuft between his brows, until it is utterly clear. Once you see the mark of the white tuft, all eighty-four thousand marks and features will naturally appear as well. Whoever sees Amitāyus Buddha thereby sees the immeasurable buddhas of the ten directions; and because they see the immeasurable buddhas, the buddhas appear before them and confer on them their prediction of awakening. This is the contemplation of every visible form at once, called the ninth contemplation. To contemplate in this way is called right contemplation; to contemplate otherwise is called wrong contemplation."

Reading note — after Shandao

The phrase "embracing and never abandoning" (攝取不捨) any being mindful of the Buddha is, for Shandao, the single most consequential clause in this entire sūtra, and he devotes some of his most quoted commentary to explaining why the Buddha's light singles out this one form of practice. His answer has three parts. First, the intimate condition (親緣): a being who constantly calls the Buddha's name is heard by the Buddha; who constantly bows before him is seen by him; who constantly holds him in mind is known by him — body, speech, and mind never part from each other in either direction, exactly as with any two people bound by real intimacy. Second, the proximate condition (近緣): a being who wishes to see the Buddha finds the Buddha responding at once, appearing before their eyes without delay. Third, the condition of superior power (增上緣): reciting the Buddha's name removes the karmic offenses of many aeons, and at the moment of death the Buddha and his assembly come in person to receive the dying, so that no accumulated evil karma can then stand in the way.

Shandao's conclusion, stated without qualification, is that every other form of practice, genuinely good as it is, is simply not comparable to mindfulness of the Buddha — which is why, he notes, sūtra after sūtra praises this one practice at such length: the larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra's forty-eight vows single out the recitation of Amitāyus's name as the path to rebirth, and the shorter Amitābha Sūtra promises the same to anyone who holds his name in mind for as little as a single day. This passage of this sūtra, on Shandao's reading, is the same claim seen from a different angle — the light of the ninth contemplation reaching out to embrace, specifically, whoever remembers the one whose light it is. It was this exact argument, transmitted through Shandao's commentary, that the Japanese teacher Hōnen later took as one of the central grounds for founding an entire school of practice on exclusive recitation of Amitāyus's name — a case where a single explanatory passage in a commentary changed the shape of Buddhist practice across an entire later tradition. He adds one practical simplification worth keeping in mind for anyone who finds eighty-four thousand light-rays within eighty-four thousand features within eighty-four thousand marks impossible to hold in view at once: the sūtra itself says a practitioner need only enter through the white tuft between the brows — see that one mark clearly, and every other mark, it promises, will follow of itself.
