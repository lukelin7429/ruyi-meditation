The Poor Woman's Gold, and the Wrestler's Jewel

This fascicle answers Kāśyapa's opening question from Part 6 in full: ten paired statements, on everything from the Buddha's birth to which monastic possessions are permitted, teach how to recognize Māra's counterfeit scripture set directly against the Buddha's own. The Four Noble Truths and the four inversions are then restated entirely around the Tathāgata's permanence, before the fascicle turns to its two central images: a destitute woman who owns unknowing gold buried beneath her own floor, and a wrestler whose opponent's blow drives a jewel invisibly into his own forehead — both figures for buddha-nature, present in every being, obscured only by affliction, never in truth absent at all.

Ten Ways to Tell Māra's Teaching from the Buddha's

Chinese Source

爾時迦葉白佛言：「世尊！如上所說四種人等，應當依耶？」佛言：「如是，如是。善男子！如我所說應當依止。何以故？有四魔故。」迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如佛所說有四種魔，若魔所說及佛所說，我當云何而得分別？」

佛告迦葉：「我般涅槃七百歲後，是魔波旬，漸當沮壞我之正法。譬如獵師身服法衣，魔王波旬亦復如是，作比丘像、比丘尼像、優婆塞像、優婆夷像，亦復化作須陀洹身，乃至化作阿羅漢身及佛色身。魔王以此有漏之形，作無漏身，壞我正法。是魔波旬壞正法時，當作是言：『菩薩昔於兜率天上沒，來在此迦毘羅城白淨王宮，依因父母愛欲和合，生育是身。』若言有人生於人中，為諸世間天人、大眾所恭敬者，無有是處。又復說言：『往昔苦行，種種布施頭目、髓腦、國城、妻子，是故今者得成佛道。』若有經律作是說者，當知悉是魔之所說。」

「善男子！若有經律作如是言：『如來正覺久已成佛，今方示現成佛道者，為欲度脫諸眾生故，示有父母依因愛欲和合而生，隨順世間作是示現。』如是經律，當知真是如來所說。若有隨順魔所說者，是魔眷屬；若能隨順佛說經律，即是菩薩。」

「若有說言：『如來生時，於十方面各行七步，不可信。』者，是魔所說。若復有說：『如來出世於十方面，各行七步，此是如來方便示現。』是名如來所說經律。」

「若有說言：『菩薩生已，父王使人將詣天祠，諸天見已悉下禮敬，是故名佛。』復有難言：『天者先出，佛在於後，云何諸天禮敬於佛？』作是難者，當知即是波旬所說。若有經言：『佛到天祠，是諸天等、摩醯首羅、大梵天王、釋提桓因，皆悉合掌敬禮其足。』如是經律是佛所說。」

「若有經律說言：『菩薩為太子時，以貪心故，四方娉妻，處在深宮，五欲自娛，歡悅受樂。』如是經律波旬所說。若有說言：『菩薩久已捨離貪心、妻息之屬，乃至不受三十三天上妙五欲，如棄涕唾，何況人欲？剃除鬚髮，出家修道。』如是經律是佛所說。」

「若有說言：『佛在舍衛祇陀精舍，聽諸比丘受畜奴婢、僕使、牛羊象馬……佛大慈故，憐愍眾生皆聽畜之。』如是經律，悉是魔說。若有說言：『佛在舍衛祇陀精舍……如來說言，比丘不應受畜金銀琉璃……如是等種種不淨之物。……如是之人我今不聽在比丘中，應當休道，還俗役使。譬如稗䅎，悉滅無餘。』當知是等經律所制，悉是如來之所說也。」

「若有說言：『菩薩為欲供養天神，故入天祠……若言不爾，無有是處。若言菩薩不能入於外道邪論……又復如來於怨親中其心平等，如以刀割及香塗身，於此二人不生增益、損減之心，唯能處中，故名如來。』如是經律，當知是魔之所說也。若有說言：『菩薩如是示入天祠，外學法中出家修道，示現知其威儀禮節、能解一切文章伎藝……雖處諸見，不生愛心，猶如蓮花不受塵垢，為度一切諸眾生故，善行如是種種方便，隨順世法。』如是經律當知即是如來所說。」

「若有說言：『如來為我解說經律，若惡法中輕重之罪及偷蘭遮，其性皆重，我等律中終不為之……汝所有律是魔所說，我等經律是佛所制。如來先說九部法印，如是九印印我經律，初不聞有方等經典……如其有者，當知必定調達所作，調達惡人以滅善法，造方等經，我等不信。』如是等經是魔所說。何以故？破壞佛法相是非故。」

「若有說言：『如來為欲度眾生故，說方等經。』當知是人真我弟子。若有不受方等經者，當知是人非我弟子，不為佛法而出家也，即是邪見外道弟子。」

「復次善男子！若有說言：『如來不為無量功德之所成就，無常變異。以得空法，宣說無我，不順世間。』如是經律名魔所說。若有人言：『如來正覺不可思議，亦為無量阿僧祇等功德所成，是故常住、無有變異。』如是經律是佛所說。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, should one truly rely on these four kinds of people described above?" The Buddha said: "It is so, it is so, good son — one should rely on them just as I have taught. Why is this? Because there are four kinds of māra." The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the Buddha has spoken of four kinds of māra — how am I to distinguish what māra teaches from what the Buddha teaches?"

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "Seven hundred years after my parinirvāṇa, Māra Pāpīyas will gradually set about corrupting my true Dharma. It is like a hunter who dresses himself in a monk's robe: Māra will likewise take on the appearance of a monk, a nun, an upāsaka, or an upāsikā, and will even transform himself into the body of a stream-enterer, up through the body of an arhat, and even the Buddha's own golden form. Using these outwardly undefiled forms to conceal his own tainted nature, Māra will corrupt my true Dharma. When Māra Pāpīyas sets about corrupting the true Dharma, he will say: 'The bodhisattva once passed away from the Tuṣita Heaven and came to be born in this palace of King Śuddhodana at Kapilavastu, this very body produced through his parents' union of desire.' It could never possibly be so that a person merely born among humans would be revered by every god and human, by the whole great assembly, in this way. He will say further: 'It was through the ascetic practices of ages past, giving away his own head, eyes, marrow, and brain, his kingdom, his wife and children, that he thereby came to accomplish buddhahood now.' Whatever scripture or Vinaya text teaches in this way should be understood to be entirely Māra's own teaching."

"Good son, whatever scripture or Vinaya text teaches instead: 'The Tathāgata, the Perfectly Awakened One, became a buddha long ago; his present manifestation of attaining buddhahood now is only, for the sake of bringing beings across, a manifestation of having parents and being born through their union of desire, shown only in accordance with the ways of the world' — such a scripture or Vinaya text should be understood to be truly the Tathāgata's own teaching. Whoever follows what Māra teaches is Māra's own follower; whoever is able to follow what the Buddha teaches is a bodhisattva."

"Whoever teaches that it cannot be believed that the Tathāgata, at his birth, took seven steps in each of the ten directions, is teaching what Māra teaches. Whoever teaches instead that the Tathāgata's taking seven steps in each of the ten directions at his birth is itself a skillful manifestation of the Tathāgata's own is teaching what the Tathāgata himself has taught."

"Whoever teaches that, once the bodhisattva was born, his father the king sent attendants to bring him to the shrine of the gods, and that the gods, on seeing him, all bowed down in reverence, and that this alone is why he is called 'Buddha' — and whoever then objects, 'The gods appeared first, and the Buddha came later — how could the gods bow in reverence to the Buddha?' — such an objection should be understood to be exactly what Pāpīyas teaches. Whatever scripture teaches instead that, on the Buddha's arrival at the shrine of the gods, every one of those gods — Maheśvara, the Great Brahmā King, and Śakra himself — joined their palms and bowed in reverence at his feet, is teaching what the Buddha himself has taught."

"Whatever scripture or Vinaya text teaches that the bodhisattva, as crown prince, out of craving, took wives from every direction, dwelt deep within the palace, and delighted in the pleasures of the five senses, is teaching what Pāpīyas teaches. Whoever teaches instead that the bodhisattva had long ago abandoned all craving for wives and children, that he would not accept even the finest pleasures of the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven's five senses, casting them aside like spit, let alone human pleasures, and so shaved his head and beard and went forth to cultivate the path, is teaching what the Buddha himself has taught."

"Whoever teaches that the Buddha, at the Jetavana monastery in Śrāvastī, permitted monks to keep servants, attendants, cattle, sheep, elephants, and horses… out of the Buddha's great compassion and pity for beings, permitting them to keep all such things, is teaching what Māra teaches. Whoever teaches instead that the Buddha, at the Jetavana monastery… taught that monks should not keep gold, silver, lapis lazuli… every such improper possession… and that such a person should no longer be counted among the monks at all, but should give up the path and be sent back to lay life and labor, entirely rooted out like barnyard grass — such teaching in scripture or Vinaya should be understood, in every case, to be exactly what the Tathāgata himself has taught."

"Whoever teaches that the bodhisattva entered the shrines of the gods in order to make offerings to them… when in truth it could never possibly be so that this was anything but a skillful means for taming those very gods and humans; and whoever teaches that the bodhisattva could not master the treatises of heterodox schools, their forms of conduct, arts, and learning… and further, that the Tathāgata holds an equal mind toward friend and foe alike — as though indifferent to being struck with a blade or anointed with perfume, feeling neither benefit nor harm from either, merely remaining neutral, and that this alone is why he is called Tathāgata — such teaching in scripture or Vinaya should be understood to be exactly what Māra teaches. Whoever teaches instead that the bodhisattva, in manifesting his entry into the shrines of the gods and his going forth within the schools of external learning, manifests a full mastery of every form of conduct, every art and skill… and, though moving among every kind of view, gives rise to no attachment at all, like a lotus untouched by the mud around it, employing every kind of skillful means for the sake of bringing every being across, according with the ways of the world — such teaching in scripture or Vinaya should be understood to be exactly what the Tathāgata himself has taught."

"Whoever teaches: 'The Tathāgata has explained to us, in scripture and Vinaya, that the offenses within the category of gross wrongdoing (thullaccaya) are all of a grave nature; in our own Vinaya, we would never commit such acts… what you call scripture and Vinaya is what Māra teaches; what we call scripture and Vinaya is what the Buddha himself has established. The Tathāgata taught nine categories of scripture as his own seal, and it is by these nine seals that our scripture and Vinaya are sealed as genuine — we have never once heard of any Vaipulya scripture among them… and should such a scripture exist, know that it must surely be the work of Devadatta, that wicked man who, to destroy the wholesome Dharma, fabricated these Vaipulya scriptures, which we do not believe in at all' — teaching of this kind is exactly what Māra teaches. Why is this? Because it destroys the very mark of the Buddha's own teaching, mistaking right for wrong."

"Whoever teaches: 'The Tathāgata, for the sake of bringing beings across, taught the Vaipulya scriptures,' should be understood to be truly my own disciple. Whoever does not accept the Vaipulya scriptures should be understood not to be my disciple at all, not to have gone forth for the sake of the Buddha's own Dharma, but to be a disciple of some heterodox school of wrong view instead."

"Furthermore, good son, whoever teaches: 'The Tathāgata is not accomplished through immeasurable virtue, but is impermanent and subject to change; having attained the dharma of emptiness, he teaches no-self, contrary to the ways of the world' — such teaching in scripture or Vinaya is what Māra teaches. Whoever teaches instead: 'The Tathāgata, the Perfectly Awakened One, is beyond conceptual thought, accomplished through immeasurable, countless virtues, and is therefore permanently abiding, without any change' — such teaching in scripture or Vinaya is what the Buddha himself has taught."

「復有人言：『或有比丘實不毀犯波羅夷罪，眾人皆謂犯波羅夷，如斷多羅樹。』而是比丘實無所犯。……所謂若有比丘少欲知足，持戒清淨，住空閑處。若王大臣見是比丘，生心念言謂得羅漢，即前讚歎恭敬禮拜，復作是言：『如是大師，捨是身已，當得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』比丘聞已即答王言：『我實未得沙門道果，王莫稱我已得道果。……我今若當默然受者，當為諸佛之所呵責。知足之行，諸佛所讚，是故我欲終身歡樂，奉修知足。……王稱我得，我今不受，故名知足。』時王答言：『大師實得阿羅漢果，如佛無異。』……而是比丘實不毀犯波羅夷罪。何以故？前人自生歡喜之心，讚歎供養故，如是比丘當有何罪？若有說言是人得罪，當知是經是魔所說。」

「復有比丘說：『佛祕藏甚深經典，一切眾生皆有佛性，以是性故，斷無量億諸煩惱結，即得成於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，除一闡提。』若王大臣作如是言：『比丘汝當作佛、不作佛耶？有佛性不？』比丘答言：『我今身中定有佛性，成以不成未能審之。』王言：『大德！如其不作一闡提者，必成無疑。』比丘言：『爾，實如王言。』是人雖言定有佛性，亦復不犯波羅夷也。復有比丘，即出家時作是思惟：『我今必定成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』如是之人，雖未得成無上道果，已為得福無量無邊不可稱計。假使有人當言是人犯波羅夷，一切比丘無不犯者。何以故？我於往昔八十億劫，常離一切不淨之物，少欲知足，威儀成就，善修如來無上法藏，亦自定知身有佛性，是故我今得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，得名為佛，有大慈悲。如是經律是佛所說。若有不能隨順是者，是魔眷屬；若能隨順，是大菩薩。」

「復有說言：『無四波羅夷、十三僧殘……及一闡提。若有比丘犯如是等墮地獄者，外道之人悉應生天。何以故？諸外道等無戒可犯，是故如來示現怖人，故說斯戒。』若言佛說：『我諸比丘若欲行婬，應捨法服著俗衣裳，然後行婬。復應生念，婬欲因緣，非我過咎；如來在世，亦有比丘，習行婬欲得正解脫……或犯四重、或犯五戒、或行一切不淨律儀，猶故而得真正解脫。……言波羅夷至突吉羅輕重無差，是諸律師，妄作此言，言是佛制，畢定當知非佛所說。』如是言說是魔經律。」

「若復說言：『於諸戒中若犯小戒，乃至微細，當受苦報，無有齊限。如是知已，防護自身，如龜藏六。』……我於經中亦說，有犯四波羅夷乃至微細突吉羅等，應當苦治。眾生若不護持禁戒，云何當得見於佛性？『一切眾生雖有佛性，要因持戒然後乃見，因見佛性得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。九部經中無方等經，是故不說有佛性也。經雖不說，當知實有。』若作是說，當知是人真我弟子。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"There is a further teaching that says: 'There may be a monk who has not truly committed any of the gravest offenses, though everyone believes he has, as surely as a palm tree once cut down' — and yet this monk has, in truth, committed no such offense at all… For example: there is a monk of few desires and easy contentment, keeping the precepts purely, dwelling in solitude. Should a king or minister see this monk and, believing he has attained arhatship, come forward to praise him, revere him, and bow before him, saying: 'Great teacher, once you set aside this body, you will surely attain unsurpassed, complete awakening' — the monk, hearing this, answers the king: 'I have not, in truth, attained the fruit of the śramaṇa's path; do not, great king, proclaim that I have already attained it… Were I to accept this in silence now, I would surely be reproached by every buddha. Contentment is what every buddha praises, and so I wish, for the whole of my life, to delight in and uphold contentment… The king declares that I have attained it, yet I do not accept this — this is what is called contentment.' The king then answers: 'This great teacher has truly attained the fruit of arhatship, no different from the Buddha himself'… and yet this monk has, in truth, committed no such gravest offense at all. Why is this? Because it was the other person who, of his own accord, gave rise to a joyful heart and offered his praise and reverence — what fault could such a monk possibly bear? Whoever teaches that such a person is guilty of an offense should be understood to be teaching exactly what Māra teaches."

"There is a further teaching, spoken by a certain monk: 'The Buddha's own profound, secret scripture teaches that every being without exception possesses buddha-nature, and, because of this nature, is able to cut off countless millions of afflictions and attain unsurpassed, complete awakening — every being, that is, except the icchantika.' Should a king or minister then say to him: 'Monk, will you become a buddha, or not? Do you possess buddha-nature?' — and the monk answers: 'I now hold, with certainty, that buddha-nature exists within my own body; whether I will actually accomplish buddhahood or not, I cannot yet say for certain' — and the king says: 'Venerable one, so long as you do not become an icchantika, you will surely accomplish it, beyond any doubt' — and the monk answers: 'Just so, exactly as the king has said' — such a person, though he has spoken of possessing buddha-nature with certainty, has still committed no such offense at all. There is, further, a monk who, at the very moment of going forth, thinks to himself: 'I shall surely, without doubt, accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening.' Such a person, though not yet having attained the peerless fruit of the path, has already attained merit immeasurable, boundless, beyond all reckoning. Should anyone claim that such a person has committed the gravest offense, then every monk without exception would be equally guilty. Why is this? I myself, throughout eighty billion kalpas in ages past, was forever free of every improper possession, of few desires and easy contentment, complete in my own bearing, well cultivating the Tathāgata's own peerless treasury of the Dharma, and knew with certainty, myself, that buddha-nature existed within my own body — and it is for this very reason that I have now accomplished unsurpassed, complete awakening, come to be called Buddha, possessed of great compassion. Such teaching in scripture or Vinaya is what the Buddha himself has taught. Whoever is unable to accept this is Māra's own follower; whoever is able to accept it is a great bodhisattva."

"There is a further teaching that says: 'There are, in truth, no such things as the four gravest offenses, the thirteen offenses requiring temporary suspension… nor the icchantika at all. Were any monk to fall into hell for committing such offenses, then every heterodox practitioner, having no precepts at all to transgress, should by rights be reborn in the heavens instead — this is why the Tathāgata merely manifested these precepts to frighten people, without their being real.' And should anyone claim the Buddha taught: 'My own monks, wishing to engage in sexual conduct, should first set aside their monastic robes and put on lay clothing before doing so; and they should further reflect that the cause and condition of desire is no fault of their own — for even while the Tathāgata still lived, there were monks who indulged in sexual conduct and yet still attained true liberation… whether committing one of the four gravest offenses, or breaking the five precepts, or engaging in every kind of improper conduct, they nonetheless still attained true and genuine liberation… and that there is, in truth, no real difference in gravity from the most serious offense down to the most minor transgression, and that the Vinaya masters who claim otherwise, attributing such distinctions to the Buddha's own teaching, must certainly be mistaken' — teaching of this kind is exactly what Māra's own scripture and Vinaya proclaim."

"Whoever instead teaches: 'Whoever transgresses even the smallest, most minor precept will surely receive a bitter result without any limit — knowing this, one should guard oneself carefully, as a tortoise draws in its six limbs'… I have likewise taught, within my own scriptures, that whoever commits any of the four gravest offenses, down to the most minor transgression, should be disciplined severely. If beings do not uphold the moral precepts, how could they ever come to see their own buddha-nature? 'Though every being possesses buddha-nature, it can only be seen through the keeping of the precepts, and it is by seeing buddha-nature that one comes to accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening. The ninefold scriptures contain no Vaipulya scripture, and this is why they do not speak of buddha-nature — but though the scriptures do not speak of it, one should understand that it truly exists.' Whoever teaches in this way should be understood to be truly my own disciple."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads these ten paired contrasts as organized around a single recurring logic rather than ten unrelated disputes: in every pair, the teaching attributed to Māra treats some feature of the Buddha's ordinary, historical-seeming life or conduct as a plain, unqualified fact, while the teaching attributed to the Buddha himself reframes that same feature as a skillful manifestation staged for the sake of beings — continuous with the "boundless manifestations" catalog earlier in this chapter, now turned into a diagnostic test a listener can actually apply. He singles out the sequence's most philosophically difficult stretch — the paired teachings on whether an outwardly humble monk who is praised as an arhat and does not correct the praise, or a monk who openly declares "I possess buddha-nature," has thereby committed one of the four gravest offenses (a false claim to a superhuman attainment, traditionally grounds for permanent expulsion) — and reads the Buddha's resolution as resting on a single distinction: culpability tracks whether the *speaker* asserts a completed attainment for personal gain, not whether an attainment is *believed* by someone else, or whether a general capacity (buddha-nature itself, which every being possesses) is honestly distinguished from its actual realization (which cultivation alone brings about). A monk who lets a king's mistaken praise stand, while privately correcting the record the moment he is asked directly, and a monk who says "I have buddha-nature, though I do not yet know whether I will succeed" have both, on this reading, kept the distinction; only the monk who collapses "I possess the cause" into "I have already reached the result" has actually lied about attainment.

And if the Tathāgata is not suffering, how could he relinquish his body and attain cessation?' — this too is giving rise to a perception of suffering within what is bliss, and is called inversion. This is the first inversion."

"Taking the impermanent as permanent, and the permanent as impermanent, is called inversion. The impermanent here means failing to cultivate emptiness — and, failing to cultivate emptiness, one's life is cut short. Whoever teaches that a long life can be attained without cultivating empty stillness is engaged in inversion — this is the second inversion."

"Taking no-self as self, and self as no-self, is called inversion. Ordinary people in the world also speak of a self; the Buddha's own teaching also speaks of a self. Yet when ordinary people speak of a self, they mean something without buddha-nature at all — this is precisely giving rise to a perception of self where there is, in truth, no self, and is called inversion. When the Buddha's teaching speaks of a self, it means precisely buddha-nature; when ordinary people say the Buddha's teaching holds there is no self at all, this is giving rise to a perception of no-self within what is truly self. To say that the Buddha's teaching holds, without qualification, that there is no self at all, and that the Tathāgata therefore instructs his disciples to cultivate no-self without exception, is itself called inversion. This is the third inversion."

"Taking what is pure as impure, and what is impure as pure, is called inversion. What is pure is the Tathāgata's own permanent abiding — not a body that eats mixed food, not a body of affliction, not a body of mere flesh, not a body bound together by sinew and bone. Whoever teaches: 'The Tathāgata is impermanent, a body that eats mixed food, even a body bound by sinew and bone; the Dharma, the Saṅgha, and liberation are themselves things utterly extinguished' — this is called inversion. As for taking what is impure as pure, being called inversion: whoever teaches: 'There is not a single impure thing within this body of mine; and, having nothing impure within it, I am certain to attain a place of purity — the Tathāgata's own teaching on cultivating the contemplation of impurity is itself a false teaching' — this too is called inversion. This is the fourth inversion."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan is precise about how this restatement differs from the four inversions this sūtra already taught back in fascicle 2: there, the target was a listener who applied the ordinary contemplations of impermanence, suffering, and no-self correctly to the conditioned world but wrongly extended them to nirvāṇa itself; here, the target is more specific and more dangerous — someone who has already accepted, in the abstract, that the Tathāgata is "permanence, bliss, self, and purity," but who then reasons backward from features of nirvāṇa's own description (that it involves relinquishing a body, that it is reached through emptiness, that it teaches no-self, that it requires contemplating the body's impurity) to conclude that the Tathāgata must, after all, be impermanent, and thereby talks himself back into the very inversion the doctrine was meant to correct. Huiyuan reads each of the four as blocking one specific such backward inference: the first blocks inferring impermanence from the fact of entering nirvāṇa; the second blocks inferring that cultivating emptiness shortens rather than lengthens the life that matters; the third blocks inferring that "the Buddha teaches no-self" means the Buddha denies any self whatsoever, rather than denying only the deluded, ordinary notion of self while affirming buddha-nature as the real referent of "self"; and the fourth blocks inferring that the sūtra's own doctrine of purity cancels the entirely separate, provisional teaching on contemplating bodily impurity, which addresses attachment to the five aggregates and was never making a claim about the Tathāgata's own nature at all.

The Buddha said: "Good son, the self here means precisely the tathāgatagarbha. That every being without exception possesses buddha-nature is precisely what is meant by this self. This meaning of self has, from the very beginning, always been covered over by countless afflictions, and this is why beings are unable to see it. Good son, it is like a poor woman whose house contains a great treasury of true gold, of which not a single member of her household, young or old, has any knowledge at all. He further shows that the self ordinary people conceive of in the world is itself false and unreal, and so teaches the cultivation of no-self, to purify the body — just as that woman, for her child's own sake, smeared her breast with something bitter. And just as that woman, having washed her breast clean, calls her child back to drink again, I am just the same: I now teach the tathāgatagarbha, and so you monks should not be afraid, just as that child, hearing his mother call, gradually returned to drink again. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan maps the poor-woman parable onto three earlier moments in this same fascicle rather than treating it as a freestanding illustration: the household that does not know its own buried gold corresponds to the ordinary being who has never once encountered the teaching of buddha-nature at all; the woman's own disbelief when first told the gold can be shown to her ("not even my own household knows of it — how could you?") corresponds to a listener who has heard the teaching but still cannot credit it; and the digging-up of the gold itself corresponds to this fascicle's own act of teaching plainly, for the first time, what earlier fascicles' talk of a permanent Dharma-body had only gestured toward. He reads the milk-and-bitter-medicine parable that follows immediately as answering a specific worry the gold parable leaves open: if buddha-nature is real and always present, why did the Buddha spend so much of his teaching career insisting on no-self at all? His answer, mapped clause by clause onto the parable, is temporal rather than doctrinal — the bitterness on the breast was never a permanent feature of the milk, only a temporary measure to keep a child from interfering with a necessary treatment already underway, and the calling-back is not a reversal of the earlier prohibition but its planned completion, timed to when the underlying illness (attachment to a false, ordinary self) has actually been cured.

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, there is, in truth, no self at all. Why is this? An infant, at birth, has no awareness of anything — if a self truly existed, awareness should already be present on the very day of birth; for this reason, one can be certain that there is no self. And if a self truly existed, it should never come to an end once a life has begun. If every being possessed buddha-nature, and this were something permanently abiding, it should have no mark of destruction at all — but if it has no mark of destruction, how could there be such differences as kṣatriya, brāhmaṇa, vaiśya, śūdra, caṇḍāla, and animal? If buddha-nature is said to be permanent with certainty, why, then, do we still speak of killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, divisive speech, harsh speech, false speech, idle chatter, greed, anger, and wrong view at all? If the nature of self were permanent, why would a person become confused and disoriented after drinking wine? If the nature of self were permanent, the blind should be able to see forms, the deaf should be able to hear sounds, the mute should be able to speak, and the lame should be able to walk. If the nature of self were permanent, one should have no need to avoid a pit of fire, a great flood, poison, a blade, a wicked person, or a wild beast. If the self were permanent, whatever one has once experienced should never be forgotten — yet if nothing is ever forgotten, why do we still say, 'Where have I seen this person before?' If the self were permanent, there should be no such thing as youth, maturity, and old age, no waxing and waning of strength, and no need to recall past events at all. If the self were permanent, where, exactly, does it reside? Is it found in spit and mucus, or among the colors blue, yellow, red, and white? A wound appears in its place, and he summons a skilled physician to treat it himself. This wise physician, well versed in remedies, recognizes at once that the wound was caused by the jewel sinking into the body, and that the jewel, having entered beneath the skin, now remains lodged there. The physician then comforts and reassures the wrestler: 'You should not now give way to such great sorrow. When you were wrestling, the jewel sank into your own body, and now lies beneath your skin, its image still visible from without. My own disciples are just the same: failing to draw near a true spiritual friend, they cultivate the teaching of no-self without knowing even where this no-self actually applies — not knowing the true nature of no-self itself, how much less could they know the true nature of self? Good son, the Tathāgata's teaching that every being possesses buddha-nature is just like that skilled physician showing the wrestler his own jewel of vajra. These beings, covered over by countless millions of afflictions, fail to recognize their own buddha-nature; only once every affliction is exhausted are they able to realize it with utter clarity, just as that wrestler saw his own jewel clearly reflected in the mirror. Good son, the Tathāgata's own secret treasury is boundless and beyond conceptual thought in just this way."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Kāśyapa's twelve-part challenge as splitting cleanly into two distinct arguments rather than one long list of objections, and notes that the Buddha answers each with a different simile because each targets a different claim. The first two objections (an infant's lack of awareness at birth; the fact that beings eventually die) attack whether a self exists at all, and the wrestler's jewel answers this one directly: the jewel is genuinely, continuously present in the wrestler's body the entire time he believes it lost, which is exactly Huiyuan's model for how buddha-nature can be real and permanently present even in a newborn infant with no yet-developed awareness of it, and even through the very changes ordinarily called death. The remaining ten objections (caste and species differences, moral evil, drunkenness, blindness and deafness, danger, forgetting, aging, unlocatable in the body, discontinuity at death) all attack whether such a self, if it existed, could be permanent given how obviously variable and destructible everything about an actual person's life is — and Huiyuan reads the wrestler simile as addressing this cluster too, on a second pass: everything Kāśyapa lists as evidence of change (the wound, the missing jewel, the wrestler's own grief and disbelief) belongs entirely to the *body* and the deluded belief built on top of it, while the jewel itself never changes at all — it only appears to have vanished because affliction (here, the very rage of the match that drove it under the skin) has obscured it from view. Buddha-nature's permanence, on this reading, was never a claim that the aggregates, the caste one is born into, or the vicissitudes of an ordinary life are themselves unchanging — only that something real and constant persists beneath all of that change, visible again the instant a mirror (this sūtra's own teaching) is finally held up to it.

「復次善男子！譬如雪山有一味藥，名曰樂味。其味極甜，在深叢下，人無能見。有人聞香，即知其地當有是藥。過去往世有轉輪王，於此雪山為此藥故，在在處處造作木筒，以接是藥，是藥熟時，從地流出，集木筒中，其味真正。王既歿已，其後是藥，或醋、或醎、或甜、或苦、或辛、或淡，如是一味，隨其流處有種種異，是藥真味停留在山，猶如滿月。凡人薄福，雖以钁斸加功困苦，而不能得。復有聖王，出現於世，以福因緣，即得是藥真正之味。善男子！如來祕藏，其味亦爾，為諸煩惱叢林所覆，無明眾生不能得見。一味者喻如佛性，以煩惱故出種種味，所謂地獄、畜生、餓鬼、天人，男女、非男非女、剎利、婆羅門、毘舍、首陀。佛性雄猛難可沮壞，是故無有能殺害者，若有殺者則斷佛性，如是佛性終不可斷，性若可斷，無有是處。如我性者，即是如來祕密之藏，如是祕藏，一切無能沮壞燒滅，雖不可壞，然不可見，若得成就阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，爾乃證知，以是因緣，無能殺者。」

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！若無殺者，應當無有不善之業。」佛告迦葉：「實有殺生。何以故？善男子！眾生佛性住五陰中，若壞五陰，名曰殺生，若有殺生，即墮惡趣，以業因緣，而有剎利、婆羅門等、毘舍、首陀及旃陀羅，若男、若女、非男非女，二十五有差別之相，流轉生死。非聖之人，橫計於我大小諸相，猶如稗子、或如米豆，乃至母指，如是種種妄生憶想，妄想之想，無有真實。出世我相名為佛性，如是計我，是名最善。」

「復次善男子！譬如有人善知伏藏，即取利钁斸地直下，磐石沙礫直過無難，唯至金剛不能穿徹。夫金剛者，所有刀斧，不能沮壞。善男子！眾生佛性亦復如是，一切論者、天魔波旬及諸人天，所不能壞。五陰之相，即是起作，起作之相，喻如石沙，可穿可壞。佛性者喻如金剛，不可沮壞。以是義故，壞五陰者名為殺生。善男子！必定當知佛法如是不可思議。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a single-flavored medicine found in the Himalayas, called the medicine of delight. Its flavor is exceedingly sweet, hidden deep beneath thick undergrowth, where no one can see it — yet whoever catches its scent knows at once that this medicine must be found in that place. In ages past there was a wheel-turning king who, for the sake of this medicine, had wooden channels built throughout the Himalayas to collect it; when the medicine ripens, it flows out from the earth and gathers in these wooden channels, its flavor pure and true. Once that king had passed away, this medicine came to taste, in various places, sometimes sour, sometimes salty, sometimes sweet, sometimes bitter, sometimes pungent, sometimes bland — this single flavor taking on every kind of difference depending on where it flowed. Yet the medicine's own true flavor remains, unchanged, within the mountain itself, like the moon at its fullest. Ordinary people, of scant merit, though they dig and labor at great cost, are unable to obtain it; but should a sage-king appear in the world, through the power of his own merit, he is able to obtain the medicine's true, unmixed flavor at once. Good son, the Tathāgata's own secret treasury is just the same in its flavor, covered over by the dense thicket of every affliction, so that beings clouded by ignorance are unable to see it at all. This single flavor is a likeness for buddha-nature itself; because of affliction, it takes on every kind of different flavor — namely, the flavors of hell, of the animal realm, of hungry ghosts, of gods and humans, of male and female and neither, of kṣatriya, brāhmaṇa, vaiśya, and śūdra. Buddha-nature is fierce and mighty, exceedingly hard to destroy, and this is why nothing can ever truly kill it; were anyone able to kill it, buddha-nature itself would thereby be severed — but buddha-nature can never, in the end, be severed at all, and for it to be severable could not possibly be so. What I call my own nature is precisely the Tathāgata's own secret treasury, and this secret treasury cannot be destroyed or burned away by anything at all; though it cannot be destroyed, it likewise cannot be seen, and only once one has accomplished unsurpassed, complete awakening does one come to realize it fully — and it is for this very reason that nothing can ever kill it."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if nothing can be killed, then there should be no such thing as an unwholesome act of killing at all." The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "There is, in truth, real killing. Why is this? Good son, the buddha-nature of beings abides within the five aggregates; to destroy the five aggregates is what is called killing, and whoever kills falls into an evil destiny, and, through this karmic cause, comes to be born as a kṣatriya, a brāhmaṇa, a vaiśya, a śūdra, a caṇḍāla, male, female, or neither, turning through birth and death amid every mark of difference within the twenty-five states of existence. Those who are not noble ones wrongly conceive of a self with every kind of size and mark — as small as a mustard seed, or a grain of rice or bean, or as large as a thumb — giving rise to every such false imagining, none of which holds any truth at all. The mark of self that transcends the world is precisely buddha-nature — to conceive of self in this way is the most excellent conception of all."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a person skilled in locating a buried treasure, who digs straight down into the earth with a sharp mattock, passing easily through solid rock and gravel alike, until he reaches vajra itself, which he cannot penetrate at all — for vajra cannot be destroyed by any blade or axe. Good son, the buddha-nature of beings is just the same: it cannot be destroyed by any philosopher, by the heavenly Māra Pāpīyas, or by any god or human. The marks of the five aggregates are themselves things that arise through activity, and, being marks that arise through activity, are like that rock and gravel, able to be penetrated and destroyed; buddha-nature, however, is like that vajra, unable to be destroyed at all. It is for this very reason that to destroy the five aggregates is what is called killing. Good son, you should be certain to understand that the Buddha's own teaching is, in this way, beyond conceptual thought."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the mountain-medicine simile as adding a claim the poor-woman and wrestler similes had not yet made explicit: not only is buddha-nature present and unseen, its single underlying flavor is what actually produces the entire apparent diversity of destinies and social categories the previous simile's own list of castes and species enumerated — hell-being, animal, human, kṣatriya, śūdra — the way one true medicinal source, once it leaves the mountain and mixes with the terrain it flows through, comes to taste sour in one place and bitter in another while never actually changing in itself. This sets up Kāśyapa's closing challenge with unusual precision: if buddha-nature truly cannot be killed by anything, has the doctrine of karma and killing not been quietly abolished? Huiyuan reads the Buddha's answer as resting entirely on the distinction the wrestler simile already established between the jewel and the wound around it — buddha-nature is not the five aggregates, and killing destroys only the aggregates (which are, like rock and gravel, always penetrable and destructible), never buddha-nature itself (which, like vajra, is categorically a different kind of thing from what any blade can reach). This is, on Huiyuan's reading, the fascicle's real answer to the very last of Kāśyapa's twelve objections several pages earlier — "if the self were permanent, it should be severed when the body is severed" — now made fully explicit: nothing about a knife passing through flesh ever touches buddha-nature at all, which is exactly why the act remains a real, karmically consequential act of killing rather than either an illusion or something rendered harmless by buddha-nature's own indestructibility.
