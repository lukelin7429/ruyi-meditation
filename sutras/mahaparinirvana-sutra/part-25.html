The Monkey Caught in Glue

The seventh merit names the four near-causes of nirvāṇa — a good friend, listening, reflection, and right practice — each unfolded through an extended run of similes before Bright Virtuous King presses a genuinely difficult historical objection: if severing affliction simply is nirvāṇa, why did the Buddha refuse Māra's own invitation to enter it decades before he actually did? The eighth merit then turns to what a bodhisattva must eliminate, avoid, accomplish, and protect, closing on Bright Virtuous King's rigorous logical challenge to the very coherence of calling a mind "liberated" from desire, answered through the middle way and the famous parable of a monkey caught, one limb at a time, in a hunter's glue.

The Good Friend

Chinese Source

「復次善男子！云何菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃微妙經典，具足成就第七功德？善男子！菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃微妙經典，作是思惟：『何法能為大般涅槃而作近因？』菩薩即知有四種法，為大涅槃而作近因。……何等為四？一者親近善友，二者專心聽法，三者繫念思惟，四者如法修行。善男子！譬如有人身遇眾病，若熱若冷、虛勞下瘧、眾邪鬼毒，到良醫所，良醫即為隨病說藥。是人至心善受醫教，隨教合藥，如法服之，服已病愈，身得安樂。有病之人，喻諸菩薩，大良醫者喻善知識，良醫所說喻方等經，善受醫教喻善思惟方等經義，隨教合藥喻如法修行三十七助道之法，病除愈者喻滅煩惱，得安樂者喻得涅槃、常、樂、我、淨。……善男子！善知識者，所謂菩薩、佛、辟支佛、聲聞、人中信方等者。何故名為善知識耶？善知識者能教眾生遠離十惡，修行十善，以是義故，名善知識。復次善知識者，如法而說、如說而行。……自能修行信、戒、布施、多聞、智慧，亦能教人信、戒、布施、多聞、智慧，復以是義名善知識。善知識者，有善法故。何等善法？所作之事不求自樂，常為眾生而求於樂，見他有過不訟其短，口常宣說純善之事，以是義故，名善知識。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, how does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who cultivates this subtle and wondrous scripture of the great nirvāṇa accomplish and fully complete the seventh merit? Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who cultivates this subtle and wondrous scripture of the great nirvāṇa reflects, thus: 'What dharma is able to serve as the near cause of this great nirvāṇa?' The bodhisattva then knows that there are four dharmas that serve as the near cause of this great nirvāṇa… What are these four? First, drawing near to a good and wise friend; second, listening to the Dharma with a single-pointed mind; third, fixing the mind in reflection; and fourth, practicing in accordance with the Dharma. Good son, it is like a man afflicted with every kind of illness — whether heat or cold, exhaustion, or dysentery, or possessed by every kind of evil spirit or poison — who goes to a good physician, and the good physician, according to his illness, prescribes him medicine. This man, with a sincere mind, well receives the physician's own instruction, compounds the medicine exactly as instructed, and takes it in accordance with the Dharma — and, having taken it, his illness is cured, and his body attains peace and ease. The sick man is a likeness for every bodhisattva; the great, good physician is a likeness for the good and wise friend; what the good physician says is a likeness for the Vaipulya scriptures; well receiving the physician's own instruction is a likeness for skillfully reflecting on the meaning of the Vaipulya scriptures; compounding the medicine exactly as instructed is a likeness for practicing, in accordance with the Dharma, the thirty-seven factors that assist the path; the illness being cured is a likeness for extinguishing affliction; and attaining peace and ease is a likeness for attaining nirvāṇa, permanence, bliss, self, and purity… Good son, a good and wise friend means the bodhisattva, the buddha, the pratyekabuddha, the śrāvaka, and, among humans, one who has faith in the Vaipulya teachings. Why is such a person named a good and wise friend? A good and wise friend is one able to teach beings to keep far from the ten evils and practice the ten wholesome deeds — it is for this very reason that he is named a good and wise friend. Furthermore, a good and wise friend is one who speaks in accordance with the Dharma and acts in accordance with what he speaks… He himself practices faith, moral discipline, giving, vast learning, and wisdom, and is, further, able to teach others faith, moral discipline, giving, vast learning, and wisdom — it is on account of this, too, that he is named a good and wise friend. A good and wise friend possesses wholesome dharmas of his own. What are these wholesome dharmas? Whatever he does, he does not seek pleasure for himself, but forever seeks pleasure for the sake of beings; seeing another's own fault, he does not broadcast his shortcoming; his mouth forever proclaims only what is purely wholesome — it is for this very reason that he is named a good and wise friend."

Chinese Source

「善男子！譬如有人遇惡癩病，有善知識而語之言：『汝若能到須彌山邊，病可得差。所以者何？彼有良藥，味如甘露，若能服者，病無不愈。』其人至心信是事已，即往彼山採服甘露，其病除愈，身得安樂。惡癩病者喻諸凡夫，善知識者喻諸菩薩摩訶薩等，至心信受喻四無量心，須彌山者喻八聖道，甘露味者喻於佛性，癩病除愈喻離煩惱，得安樂者喻得涅槃常樂我淨。……善男子！如空中月，從初一日至十五日漸漸增長。善知識者亦復如是，令諸學人漸遠惡法，增長善法。善男子！若有親近善知識者，本未有戒、定、慧、解脫、解脫知見，即便有之，未具足者，則得增廣。何以故？以其親近善知識故，因是親近，復得了達十二部經甚深之義。若能聽是十二部經甚深義者，名為聽法，聽法者則是大乘方等經典，聽方等經名真聽法，真聽法者即是聽受大涅槃經，大涅槃中聞有佛性、如來畢竟不般涅槃，是故名為專心聽法，專心聽法名八聖道，以八聖道能斷貪欲、瞋恚愚癡，故名聽法。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, it is like a man afflicted with a grave, leprous disease, whom a good and wise friend addresses, saying: 'Should you be able to reach the edge of Mount Sumeru, your illness can be cured — for there is a fine medicine there, with the flavor of ambrosia, and, should you be able to consume it, your illness will, without exception, be healed.' This man, with a sincere mind, believes this, and goes at once to that mountain, gathers and consumes this very ambrosia, and his illness is cured, his body attaining peace and ease. The grave, leprous disease is a likeness for every ordinary being; the good and wise friend is a likeness for the bodhisattva-mahāsattva; believing with a sincere mind is a likeness for the four immeasurable minds; Mount Sumeru is a likeness for the eightfold noble path; the flavor of ambrosia is a likeness for buddha-nature; the healing of the leprous disease is a likeness for leaving affliction behind; and attaining peace and ease is a likeness for attaining nirvāṇa's own permanence, bliss, self, and purity… Good son, it is like the moon in the sky, which, from the first day of the month to the fifteenth, grows gradually greater — a good and wise friend is exactly the same, causing every student to gradually keep far from unwholesome dharmas and to grow and increase in wholesome ones. Good son, should one draw near to a good and wise friend, whatever moral discipline, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and knowledge and vision of liberation he did not originally possess, he thereupon comes to possess, and whatever he has not yet fully completed comes to grow and expand. Why is this? Because, through this very drawing near to a good and wise friend, he comes, further, to penetrate the very deep meaning of the twelve divisions of scripture. Should he be able to hear this very deep meaning of the twelve divisions of scripture, this is named listening to the Dharma; and this listening to the Dharma is itself the Mahāyāna's own Vaipulya scriptures; listening to the Vaipulya scriptures is named true listening to the Dharma; and true listening to the Dharma is itself listening to and receiving this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra — hearing, within this great nirvāṇa, that buddha-nature exists, and that the Tathāgata, in the end, never actually enters parinirvāṇa at all — it is for this very reason that this is named listening to the Dharma with a single-pointed mind; and listening to the Dharma with a single-pointed mind is itself named the eightfold noble path, since the eightfold noble path is able to sever craving, anger, and delusion — it is for this reason that this, too, is named listening to the Dharma."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the seventh merit's opening question — what actually functions as nirvāṇa's own near cause — as deliberately correcting a natural but mistaken assumption: one might think grueling ascetic practice is the direct cause of liberation, but the sūtra names instead four specifically relational and cognitive conditions (a good friend, listening, reflection, right practice), reserving the thirty-seven factors and the rest as what such conditions eventually make possible, rather than as the immediate cause itself. He traces the sūtra's own escalating chain of definitions for "listening to the Dharma" — hearing the twelve divisions, which collapses into hearing the Mahāyāna Vaipulya scriptures specifically, which collapses further into hearing this very sūtra, which collapses finally into the eightfold noble path itself — as a rhetorical technique this collection has seen before (compressing an entire hierarchy of practice into a single term through successive redefinition), used here to establish that "listening" in the fullest sense is never passive reception but is already identical with the very practice it is said to precede.

On the two similes distinguishing types of aid (a physician curing an ordinary illness, and a leprosy so severe it requires traveling to Mount Sumeru itself for the one cure that exists), Huiyuan reads the second as marking out a qualitatively different scale of affliction from the first — ordinary affliction can be treated locally, by a competent physician nearby, but the deepest affliction (an ordinary being's own fundamental ignorance) requires a specific, singular remedy (buddha-nature itself, reached only via the eightfold noble path) that cannot be found or improvised anywhere closer to hand, which is why the second simile insists on the arduous journey the first does not require.

More Reasons to Listen

Chinese Source

「云何菩薩聽法因緣而得近於大般涅槃？一切眾生以聽法故，則具信根，得信根故，樂行布施、持戒、忍辱、精進、禪定、智慧，得須陀洹果乃至佛果，是故當知得諸善法，皆是聽法因緣勢力。善男子！譬如長者惟有一子，遣至他國市易所須，示其道路通塞之處，而復誡之：『若遇婬女，慎莫親愛，若親愛者，喪身殞命及以財寶。弊惡之人，亦莫交遊。』其子敬順父之教勅，身心安樂，多獲寶貨。菩薩摩訶薩為諸眾生敷演法要亦復如是，示諸眾生及四部眾諸道通塞，是諸眾生以聞法故，遠離諸惡，具足善法。……善男子！譬如明鏡照人面像，無不明了。聽法明鏡亦復如是，有人照之，則見善惡明了無翳。……善男子！譬如估客，欲至寶渚，不知道路，有人示之，其人隨語，即至寶渚，多獲諸珍不可稱計。一切眾生亦復如是，欲至善處採取道寶，不知其路通塞之處，菩薩示之，眾生隨已得至善處，獲得無上大涅槃寶。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"How is it that the cause and condition of listening to the Dharma brings a bodhisattva near to this great nirvāṇa? Every being, through listening to the Dharma, thereby becomes endowed with the root of faith, and, having attained this root of faith, delights in practicing giving, moral discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom, attaining the fruit of stream-entry, up through the fruit of buddhahood itself — you should therefore know that the attainment of every wholesome dharma is entirely the power of the cause and condition of listening to the Dharma. Good son, it is like an elder who has but a single son, whom he sends to another kingdom to trade for what is needed, showing him which roads are passable and which are not, and further warning him: 'Should you encounter a woman of pleasure, be careful never to become attached to her — for, should you become attached, you shall lose your very life and your wealth alike; nor should you keep company with wicked people.' The son, reverently obeying his father's own instruction, attains peace and ease of body and mind, and obtains a great deal of wealth. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, expounding the essentials of the Dharma for the sake of beings, is exactly the same: showing beings and the fourfold assembly which roads are passable and which are not, these very beings, through hearing the Dharma, keep far from every evil and become fully endowed with wholesome dharmas… Good son, it is like a bright mirror reflecting a person's own face, leaving nothing at all unclear — the bright mirror of listening to the Dharma is exactly the same: whoever is reflected in it sees good and evil with perfect clarity, without the slightest obscurity… Good son, it is like a merchant who wishes to reach an island of treasure, but does not know the way — someone shows him the road, and, following these directions, he reaches the island of treasure, and obtains every kind of precious thing beyond all reckoning. Every being is exactly the same: wishing to reach a wholesome place and gather the treasure of the path, yet not knowing which roads are passable and which are not, the bodhisattva shows them the way, and these beings, following him, reach this wholesome place, and obtain the unsurpassed treasure of this great nirvāṇa."

Chinese Source

「善男子！譬如醉象，狂騃暴惡，多欲殺害。有調象師，以大鐵鈎，鈎斲其頂，即時調順，惡心都盡。一切眾生亦復如是，貪欲、瞋恚、愚癡、醉故，欲多造惡，諸菩薩等，以聞法鈎，斲之令住，更不得起造諸惡心。……是故我於處處經中說：『我弟子專心聽受十二部經，則離五蓋、修七覺分，以是修習七覺分故，則得近於大般涅槃。』以聽法故須陀洹人離諸恐怖。所以者何？須達長者身遇重病，心大愁怖，聞舍利弗說須陀洹有四功德、十種慰喻，聞是事已，恐怖即除。……何以故？開法眼故。世有三人：一者無目，二者一目，三者二目。言無目者常不聞法，一目之人雖暫聞法其心不住，二目之人專心聽受如聞而行。以聽法故，得知世間如是三人。……善男子！如我昔於拘尸那城，時舍利弗身遇病苦，我時顧命阿難比丘廣為說法。時舍利弗聞是事已，告四弟子：『汝舁我床往至佛所，我欲聽法。』時四弟子即共舁往。既得聞法，聞法力故，所苦除差，身得安隱。以是義故，聽法因緣則得近於大般涅槃。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, it is like a drunken elephant, frenzied, wild, and violent, wishing to kill and harm many — an elephant-trainer, with a great iron hook, strikes it upon its crown, and it is at once tamed and made compliant, every evil thought within it entirely exhausted. Every being is exactly the same: drunk on craving, anger, and delusion, wishing to commit every kind of evil, the bodhisattva, with the hook of hearing the Dharma, strikes and restrains him, so that he can no longer give rise to any evil thought at all… It is for this very reason that I say, throughout every scripture: 'My own disciples, listening to and receiving the twelve divisions of scripture with a single-pointed mind, thereby keep far from the five hindrances, and cultivate the seven factors of awakening — and, through cultivating these seven factors of awakening, they thereby draw near to this great nirvāṇa.' It is through listening to the Dharma that the stream-enterer keeps far from every terror. Why is this? The elder Sudatta, once gravely ill, his mind filled with great sorrow and dread, heard Śāriputra speak of the stream-enterer's own four merits, offering him ten kinds of consolation — and, hearing this, his own terror was at once removed… Why is this? Because it opens the Dharma-eye. There are, in this world, three kinds of people: those without eyes, those with a single eye, and those with two eyes. Those without eyes are those who never hear the Dharma at all; those with a single eye, though they may hear the Dharma for a moment, do not let it settle in their own minds; and those with two eyes listen to and receive it with a single-pointed mind, and practice exactly as they have heard it. It is through listening to the Dharma that one comes to know that the world contains three kinds of people such as this… Good son, it was just as when, in the past, at the city of Kuśinagara, Śāriputra himself fell gravely ill, and I, at that time, entrusted the monk Ānanda to expound the Dharma broadly for him. Śāriputra, hearing of this, said to his four disciples: 'Carry my own bed to where the Buddha is — I wish to hear the Dharma.' His four disciples then carried him there together, and, having heard the Dharma, through the very power of this hearing, his suffering was removed and cured, his body attaining peace and comfort. It is for this very reason that the cause and condition of listening to the Dharma brings one near to this great nirvāṇa."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the merchant's father's warning to his son (avoid entanglement with a courtesan, avoid wicked company) as a somewhat unusual choice of simile for a scripture ordinarily working in more abstract registers, and reads its practical, almost mundane specificity as deliberate: the Dharma's own guidance functions exactly like a father's plain, unromantic warning about real-world dangers, not only like an elevated philosophical instruction — listening to the Dharma protects a being from concrete, ordinary ruin as much as from subtle metaphysical error. He reads the three-kinds-of-people taxonomy (no eyes, one eye, two eyes) as a compact restatement of the whole section's cumulative argument: mere exposure to teaching (one eye) accomplishes nothing lasting without the further step of letting it settle and be acted upon (two eyes), which is why hearing alone was never sufficient on its own, in any of the similes offered across both this and the preceding section.

On Śāriputra's own illness eased by the mere hearing of the Dharma, and Sudatta's terror removed by learning of the stream-enterer's four merits, Huiyuan reads these two closing historical examples as grounding the entire preceding sequence of similes in the sūtra's own narrated history rather than leaving them as pure metaphor — even for two of the Buddha's own most advanced disciples, hearing the Dharma at the moment of crisis produced an effect described in exactly the same terms (relief from suffering, removal of terror) the similes had already promised in figurative form.

Reflection, Right Practice, and Knowing Seven Things

Chinese Source

「云何菩薩思惟因緣而得近於大般涅槃？因是思惟，心得解脫。何以故？一切眾生常為五欲之所繫縛，以思惟故悉得解脫。……復次善男子！一切眾生常為常、樂、我、淨四法之所顛倒，以思惟故，得見諸法無常、無樂、無我、無淨，如是見已四倒即斷。……復次善男子！一切諸法有四種相。何等為四？一者生相，二者老相，三者病相，四者滅相。以是四相，能令一切凡夫眾生至須陀洹生大苦惱；若能繫念善思惟者，雖遇此四，不生於苦。……復次善男子！一切善法無不因於思惟而得。何以故？有人雖於無量無邊阿僧祇劫專心聽法，若不思惟，終不能得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。」

「云何菩薩如法修行？善男子！斷諸惡法，修習善法，是名菩薩如法修行。復次云何如法修行？見一切法空無所有，無常、無樂、無我、無淨，以是見故，寧捨身命，不犯禁戒，是名菩薩如法修行。復次云何如法修行？修有二種：一者真實，二者不實。不實者，不知涅槃、佛性、如來、法、僧、實相、虛空等相，是名不實。云何真實？能知涅槃、佛性、如來、法、僧、實相、虛空等相，是名真實。云何名為知涅槃相？涅槃之相凡有八事。何等為八？一者盡、二善性、三實、四真、五常、六樂、七我、八淨，是名涅槃。復有八事……一者解脫、二者善性、三者不實、四者不真、五者無常、六者無樂、七者無我、八者無淨。復有六相：一者解脫、二者善性、三者不實、四者不真、五者安樂、六者清淨。若有眾生依世俗道，斷煩惱者，如是涅槃，則有八事解脫不實。何以故？以不常故。以無常故則無有實，無有實故則無有真，雖斷煩惱，以還起故，無常、無我、無樂、無淨，是名涅槃解脫八事。云何六相？聲聞緣覺斷煩惱故，名為解脫，而未能得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，故名為不實。以不實故，名為不真。未來之世當得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，故名無常。以得無漏八聖道故，名為淨樂。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"How is it that the cause and condition of reflection brings a bodhisattva near to this great nirvāṇa? Through this very reflection, the mind attains liberation. Why is this? Every being is forever bound by the five desires — and, through reflection, he becomes entirely liberated from them… Furthermore, good son, every being is forever inverted by these four dharmas — permanence, bliss, self, and purity — and, through reflection, comes to see that every dharma is impermanent, without bliss, without self, and without purity; and, having seen this, these four inversions are at once severed… Furthermore, good son, every dharma bears four kinds of mark: the mark of birth, the mark of aging, the mark of illness, and the mark of cessation. It is these four marks that cause every ordinary being, up through the stream-enterer, to give rise to great suffering and anguish — but one who is able to fix his mind and reflect well, though he encounters these same four, gives rise to no suffering at all on account of them… Furthermore, good son, there is not a single wholesome dharma that is not attained through reflection. Why is this? There is a person who, though he listens to the Dharma with a single-pointed mind throughout immeasurable, boundless, incalculable kalpas, if he does not reflect upon it, shall, in the end, never attain unsurpassed, complete awakening."

"What is meant by 'the bodhisattva practicing in accordance with the Dharma'? Good son, severing every unwholesome dharma and cultivating every wholesome dharma — this is what is meant by the bodhisattva practicing in accordance with the Dharma. What else, further, is meant by practicing in accordance with the Dharma? Seeing that every dharma is empty, without anything at all, impermanent, without bliss, without self, and without purity, and, through this very seeing, would rather cast off his own body and life than transgress a single restraining precept — this, too, is what is meant by the bodhisattva practicing in accordance with the Dharma. What else, further, is meant by practicing in accordance with the Dharma? Practice is of two kinds: true, and untrue. What is untrue is not knowing the marks of nirvāṇa, buddha-nature, the Tathāgata, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, true reality, and empty space — this is what is meant by untrue. What is true? Being able to know the marks of nirvāṇa, buddha-nature, the Tathāgata, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, true reality, and empty space — this is what is meant by true. What is meant by knowing the mark of nirvāṇa? The mark of nirvāṇa consists, in all, of eight matters. What are these eight? Exhaustion, wholesome nature, reality, truth, permanence, bliss, self, and purity — this is what is named nirvāṇa. There are, further, eight matters… liberation, wholesome nature, unreality, untruth, impermanence, no bliss, no self, and no purity. There are, further, six marks: liberation, wholesome nature, unreality, untruth, peace and ease, and purity. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the reflection-cause's own four sub-points (liberation from the five desires, correction of the four inversions, equanimity in the face of the four universal marks of birth-aging-illness-cessation, and reflection as the necessary complement to mere listening) as together establishing that hearing the Dharma, however extensive, produces no lasting result without the further, active work of turning it over in the mind — the same point the three-kinds-of-eyes taxonomy made in the preceding section, now given its own dedicated treatment as a distinct cause in its own right rather than a mere qualifier on listening.

His sharpest technical point concerns the "eight matters" versus "six marks" comparison closing this section: an ordinary, worldly severing of affliction yields eight qualities that sound, on the surface, like genuine liberation (exhaustion, wholesome nature, and the rest) yet remain unreal precisely because affliction, once merely suppressed by worldly means, can arise again — impermanence at the root disqualifies every other quality built upon it. The śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha's own attainment, by contrast, earns six marks that include real liberation and real purity, yet still falls short of nirvāṇa proper because it has not yet attained unsurpassed awakening — which is why it, too, is named "impermanent," not because affliction returns, but because a further, greater attainment still lies ahead of it. Huiyuan reads the closing seven-fold taxonomy (nirvāṇa, buddha-nature, the Tathāgata, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, true reality, and space, each defined through its own list of paired or multiple qualities) as this fascicle's own comprehensive vocabulary test: a bodhisattva who has genuinely completed the fourth cause, right practice, is one who can distinguish these seven terms precisely rather than treating them as loosely interchangeable synonyms for "the sacred" in general — a distinction the following section will use immediately in its own treatment of space.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this section's own treatment of space as a deliberately destabilizing move right after the previous section's careful sevenfold taxonomy: having just distinguished nirvāṇa, buddha-nature, the Tathāgata, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, true reality, and space as seven genuinely distinct terms, the sūtra now turns and questions whether "space" names anything real at all — not a physical void waiting to be perceived, but simply the name given to an absence, produced by nothing and requiring no cause, since something genuinely produced would, by that very fact, be impermanent and therefore disqualified from the very permanence "empty space" is supposed to exemplify. His point is that this is not a retraction of the prior taxonomy but its logical completion: space earns a place in the list of seven terms precisely as the one item whose own "reality" consists entirely in demonstrating what a name without an underlying nature looks like — a template the sūtra immediately applies to nirvāṇa itself, which likewise "has no fixed dwelling place," being simply the name for the place where affliction is severed rather than a location or substance in its own right.

On the closing distinction between bliss the Buddha possesses (quiescence and awareness, but never mere sensory bliss), the three blisses of true reality (adding sensory bliss to the same pair), and buddha-nature's own single bliss (bliss as something "destined to be seen," realized fully only at the moment of awakening), Huiyuan reads these three distinct enumerations as calibrated to what each term actually names: reality as such encompasses the full range of experience, including ordinary felt pleasure, since reality is present even in unenlightened experience; the Buddha's own bliss necessarily excludes felt sensation, since sensation implies a subject still bound by feeling; and buddha-nature's bliss is prospective rather than currently realized, since buddha-nature, present in every being, is not yet seen by most of them — a final, careful discrimination completing the sūtra's own insistence, running throughout this entire chapter, that terms which sound interchangeable in ordinary religious language in fact name precisely different things.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Bright Virtuous King's challenge as pressing a genuine historical and logical inconsistency the sūtra's own narrative creates: if severing affliction simply constitutes nirvāṇa, the Buddha should have entered it the moment he attained awakening under the bodhi tree, making his own later three-month countdown, and his declaration to Vaiśālī's Māra decades afterward, either redundant or an outright falsehood — and if it does not constitute nirvāṇa, the sūtra's own repeated equation of the two elsewhere becomes equally puzzling. His gloss on the resolution turns on distinguishing two senses of "nirvāṇa" that the challenge itself conflates: an ordinary severing of affliction that merely stops active affliction without penetrating to buddha-nature (which the sūtra now names "nirvāṇa, not great nirvāṇa"), and the deeper attainment reached only through actually seeing buddha-nature, which alone yields the full quartet of permanence, bliss, self, and purity together — the Buddha's awakening under the bodhi tree already accomplished the first, but the specific delay Bright Virtuous King is asking about concerns something else entirely: a deliberate choice to remain active in the world for the sake of specific disciples not yet ready, regardless of which sense of nirvāṇa his own attainment had already reached.

He reads the detailed roll call of specific groups (the five ascetics at Vārāṇasī, the elder Ugra's fifty companions, King Bimbisāra's court, Uruvilvā-Kāśyapa's five hundred followers, the Kāśyapa brothers, Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana's own circle) as this fascicle's own way of making the Buddha's stated reason to Māra concrete and falsifiable rather than a vague appeal to "compassion in general" — each name corresponds to an actual episode elsewhere in the canon of the Buddha's own early teaching career, converting what could have remained an abstract theological point into a specific historical accounting of exactly whose conversion the Buddha judged still incomplete at the moment Māra first made his request. On Māra's own mistaken theory of nirvāṇa (silence and cessation of teaching, mistaken for death by ordinary onlookers), Huiyuan reads this as the sūtra's own diagnosis of a category error running through this entire chapter's argument: Māra assumes nirvāṇa is definable by external behavior (activity versus stillness) rather than by an internal, permanent quality of mind — exactly the same mistake this chapter has spent five fascicles correcting through its repeated insistence that nothing about the Tathāgata's true nature is exhausted by what can be externally observed.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this closing etymological catalogue not as a scholarly digression but as the sūtra's own final proof, offered through the very structure of language itself, of the argument this fascicle has built across its entire treatment of the seventh merit: every proposed root meaning of the syllable "vāṇa" names some form of limitation, activity, or dependency (weaving, covering, coming and going, grasping, instability, temporal change, obstruction, having a mark, existing, combination, suffering), and nirvāṇa is defined, in every single case, purely as its negation — never as a positive state added on top of ordinary experience, but as the systematic absence of every constraint an ordinary, conditioned existence is subject to. He includes the citation of a specific rival school's own etymology (the followers of Uraka Kapila, glossing "vāṇa" as "mark") as evidence the sūtra is engaging a real, contemporary debate about the term's own derivation rather than inventing the whole exercise from scratch.

His closing gloss on the final five-fold definition — no arising affliction is nirvāṇa; unobstructed wisdom is the Tathāgata; being none of the four ordinary categories of being is buddha-nature; pervading every land without obstruction is space; and forever unchanging is true reality — ties the whole of this long seventh merit back to the sevenfold vocabulary test from the "reflection" and "right practice" sections earlier in this fascicle, now given final, compressed definitions rather than lists of qualities: each of the seven terms this merit set out to distinguish carefully receives, at the very close of the merit, its own single, defining sentence, ending on the same claim that opened the whole Māra episode — that the Tathāgata, understood correctly through any of these seven lenses, never actually undergoes the ending his own śrāvaka disciples will mistakenly announce.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the eighth merit's own eight-fold list as organized around a clear progression from what must be eliminated, through what must be actively cultivated, to the final liberated state that results — the five aggregates and five views come first because they represent the most fundamental sources of bondage to be uprooted before any further cultivation can take hold; the six recollections, five concentrations, and single protected bodhi-mind represent the positive cultivation that fills the space the eliminated afflictions leave behind; the four immeasurable minds and trust in the one vehicle represent the outward-facing dimension of this same cultivation, extended toward other beings and toward the Buddha's own teaching as a whole; and the mind well liberated and wisdom well liberated name the resulting state once every prior item has been accomplished.

On the single protected bodhi-mind specifically, he reads the three similes (an only child, a blind man's remaining eye, a wilderness traveler's guide) as calibrated to increasing stakes: losing an only child is an ordinary, devastating loss; losing one's last remaining eye is total, irreversible loss of a specific faculty; and losing one's guide in a wilderness is loss that endangers survival itself — together conveying that the bodhi-mind, once given rise to, is not merely valuable but existentially decisive for the entire remainder of the bodhisattva path, deserving a vigilance that scales with what would actually be lost were it abandoned. This closing definition of "wisdom well liberated" as knowledge of every dharma without obstruction — explicitly credited as the source of hearing, seeing, and arriving at what was never heard, seen, or arrived at before — deliberately loops back to the second merit at the start of this whole sequence in Part 24, showing the ten merits are not simply sequential but cumulative, each later one drawing on and re-explaining what an earlier one had already established.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Bright Virtuous King's challenge as constructing a genuine dilemma with no easy escape: the first horn argues that if the mind's own nature is originally free of craving, craving could never actually arise within it at all (illustrated through five parallel impossibility-similes — milking a horn, a barren woman bearing a child, fire from damp wood, oil from pressed sand, a stake planted in empty space, each showing that nothing can be produced from a substrate that categorically lacks the potential for it) — while the second horn, argued at equal length, insists that craving obviously does arise and does produce real consequences (a real woman's form, or even a mere painting of one, visibly triggers craving and its resulting crime), which would seem to require that craving be a real, fixed property of the mind after all, making "liberation from craving" impossible to achieve by definition, since one cannot remove a nature the mind never had, nor coherently claim to have removed a nature the mind essentially possesses.

He reads the closing move — invoking the classical Buddhist analysis of the three time-periods (past, future, present) to argue that "liberation" cannot be located in any of them, since a past mind is already gone, a future mind does not yet exist, and a present mind, by the very fact of arising, is not yet in the state the path aims at — as the sharpest and most technical point in the whole challenge, deliberately parallel to the lamp-and-darkness analogy this collection has seen the sūtra itself deploy approvingly elsewhere: light and darkness cannot coexist in the same moment, so a mind cannot simultaneously be both bound and being liberated, which seems to make the very process of liberation, described as a transition, logically impossible to locate at any single moment in time. His framing note for what follows: the Buddha's coming answer, invoking the middle way between fixed existence and fixed non-existence, is offered as directly dismantling the entire dilemma's own shared premise — that craving and mind must each be either fixed-present or fixed-absent, with no coherent middle position available.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Buddha's opening move as dismantling Bright Virtuous King's entire dilemma at its shared root: both horns of the challenge assumed craving and mind must each be a fixed, determinate reality — either fixedly present in the mind's own nature, or fixedly absent from it — and the Buddha's answer, "neither bound nor unbound, neither existent nor non-existent," refuses this shared assumption rather than choosing between its two branches. He reads the sūtra's own catalogue of three rival, mistaken positions (a fixed dual nature of craving-and-liberation coexisting in the mind; a subtle-versus-coarse causal chain where only the coarse, impermanent fruit results; and pure temporal coincidence, with mind and craving arising for no reason at all beyond "the season") as together exhausting the space of naive answers to the causal puzzle Bright Virtuous King raised, each rejected because each still treats causation itself as a fixed relation between fixed terms.

The middle way's own technical content, on Huiyuan's reading, rests on the analysis of consciousness arising from eye, form, light, mind, and attention together: consciousness is not fixed as residing in any single one of these conditions, nor in some determinate combination of them, and yet it does genuinely arise — dependent origination itself is the answer to how something can be "not fixed" without being simply nonexistent, which is exactly the structure craving shares with consciousness. His gloss on the four-fold combination of craving's arising and ceasing (the ordinary being trapped in both; the śrāvaka or the bodhisattva at the immovable ground moving from craving to freedom; the advanced bodhisattva deliberately manifesting craving's appearance for pedagogical purposes while inwardly already free of it; and the fully liberated being, free at both ends) is that this fourfold matrix itself demonstrates the mind's own lack of fixed nature in the most concrete possible terms: the very same "craving," observed across different practitioners at different stages, behaves in four logically distinct ways, which could not be so if craving or mind possessed the single, fixed nature either horn of Bright Virtuous King's original dilemma required.

Should there be one who accepts and keeps none of these impure objects at all, but, for the sake of this great nirvāṇa, upholds, recites, copies out, and expounds the twelve divisions of scripture, know that a person of this kind is truly my own disciple, one who does not walk within the evil demon Māra's own domain at all — this is itself the cultivation of the thirty-seven factors, and, through this very cultivation, his mind arises apart from craving and ceases apart from craving. This is what is meant by the bodhisattva who cultivates this subtle and wondrous scripture of the great nirvāṇa accomplishing and fully completing the eighth merit."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the sun-and-moon simile as the Buddha's own direct answer to the deepest layer of Bright Virtuous King's original challenge: the mind's own true nature is never actually mixed together with craving the way two substances combine, exactly as the sun and moon's own light is never actually mixed with the smoke, cloud, or eclipse that merely obscures it from view — craving is a temporary, condition-dependent obscuration, not an ingredient blended into the mind's own substance, which is why it can be removed entirely without requiring that the mind ever have "contained" it as a fixed component to begin with. This directly resolves the original horn-milking and barren-woman similes: those similes assumed liberation would require producing something (freedom) from a substrate that categorically lacked it, but obscuration and its removal, unlike production, require no such shared substance at all.

He reads the famous monkey-and-glue parable as adding the missing account of exactly how an ordinary being becomes bound by craving despite the mind's own essential purity — not through the mind's own nature changing, but through successive, self-compounding contact: the monkey's own single touch of curiosity becomes, through its own foolish attempts to free itself, an ever-worsening entanglement of all five limbs, precisely mirroring how craving's initial contact, resisted incorrectly, only deepens its grip rather than loosening it. The three-tiered terrain (impassable to both person and monkey, passable to the monkey alone, passable to both) maps a corresponding three-tiered vulnerability: the fully liberated path Māra himself cannot reach at all; a middle ground reachable by wise non-Buddhist practitioners whom the five desires cannot bind, though Māra himself still can in principle enter; and ordinary compromised territory where both ordinary beings and Māra freely operate together. His closing point on the extended catalogue of corrupt monks (worldly dealings, appropriated Saṅgha property, gambling, courier work for foreign courts) is that this fascicle's entire abstract argument about the mind's true nature culminates, deliberately, in a wholly concrete ethical test: whichever position one takes on the metaphysics of craving, the sūtra's own final criterion for "my disciple" versus "Māra's own retinue" is behavioral and verifiable, not a matter that could be settled by philosophical argument alone.

Part 25 Complete

This closes Part 25 (fascicle 25 of 40) and continues 光明遍照高貴德王菩薩品. The seventh merit's four near-causes of nirvāṇa — a good friend, listening, reflection, and right practice — unfold through an extended run of similes before Bright Virtuous King presses the sūtra's own historical narrative against itself: if severing affliction simply is nirvāṇa, why did the Buddha refuse Māra's invitation at the river decades before actually entering parinirvāṇa? The answer distinguishes an ordinary severing of affliction from the deeper seeing of buddha-nature that alone constitutes this great nirvāṇa, and names the specific disciples the Buddha still needed to teach, before the fascicle closes its seventh merit with a sustained etymology of "nirvāṇa" itself as the negation of every form of bondage. The eighth merit then catalogues what a bodhisattva eliminates, avoids, cultivates, and protects, closing on Bright Virtuous King's rigorous dilemma about whether a mind can coherently be called "liberated" from desire at all — resolved through the middle way of dependent origination and the parable of a monkey caught, one limb at a time, in a hunter's glue.
