The Vajra Samādhi

Having spent three entire fascicles on the first of ten merits, the sūtra now moves briskly through the second, third, fourth, fifth, and sixth: powers and perceptions a bodhisattva never had before, mind and body no longer bound to each other, a compassion that arises without any cause at all, ten vows so complete they purify an entire future buddha-land, and the famous vajra samādhi — a concentration that shatters every dharma without diminishing itself in the slightest, exactly as a diamond can cut anything and never be cut.

Powers Never Had Before

Chinese Source

「復次善男子！云何菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃，成就具足第二功德？善男子！菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃，昔所不得而今得之，昔所不見而今見之，昔所不聞而今聞之，昔所不到而今得到，昔所不知而今知之。云何名為昔所不得而今得之？所謂神通，昔所不得而今乃得。通有二種：一者內，二者外。所言外者與外道共。內復有二：一者二乘，二者菩薩。菩薩修行大涅槃經所得神通，不與聲聞辟支佛共。云何名為不與聲聞辟支佛共？二乘所作神通變化，一心作一，不得眾多。菩薩不爾，於一心中，則能具足現五趣身。所以者何？以得如是大涅槃經之勢力故，是則名為昔所不得而今得之。又復云何昔所不得而今得之？所謂身得自在、心得自在。何以故？一切凡夫所有身心不得自在，或心隨身，或身隨心。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, how does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who cultivates this great nirvāṇa accomplish and fully complete the second merit? Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who cultivates this great nirvāṇa comes to attain what he had never attained before, to see what he had never seen before, to hear what he had never heard before, to arrive at what he had never arrived at before, and to know what he had never known before. What is meant by 'attaining what was never attained before'? Namely, the supernormal powers, never attained before and now finally attained. These powers are of two kinds: external, and internal. What is called 'external' is shared with the followers of other paths. The internal, further, is of two kinds: that of the two lesser vehicles, and that of the bodhisattva. The supernormal power the bodhisattva attains through practicing this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra is shared with no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha at all. What is meant by 'shared with no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha'? The supernormal transformations performed by the two lesser vehicles allow a single mind to produce only a single result, never many at once. The bodhisattva is not like this: within a single mind, he is able to manifest bodies in all five destinies simultaneously, fully complete. Why is this? Because he has attained the very power of this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra — this is what is meant by attaining what was never attained before. And, further, what else is meant by 'attaining what was never attained before'? Namely, attaining mastery of the body, and mastery of the mind. Why is this? Because every ordinary being's own body and mind lack any such mastery at all — either the mind follows the body, or the body follows the mind."

Chinese Source

「云何名為心隨於身？譬如醉人，酒在身中，爾時身動，心亦隨動，亦如身懶，心亦隨懶，是則名為心隨於身。又如嬰兒，其身稚小，心亦隨小，大人身大，心亦隨大。又如有人，身體麁澁，心常思念，欲得膏油潤漬令軟，是則名為心隨於身。云何名為身隨於心？所謂去來坐臥，修行施、戒、忍辱、精進，愁惱之人身則羸悴，歡喜之人身則肥鮮，恐怖之人身體戰動，專心聽法身則怡悅，悲泣之人涕淚橫流，是則名為身隨於心。菩薩不爾，於身心中俱得自在，是則名為昔所不得而今得之。復次善男子！菩薩摩訶薩所現身相，猶如微塵，以此微身，悉能遍至無量無邊恒河沙等諸佛世界，無所障礙，而心常定，初不移動，是則名為心不隨身。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"What is meant by 'the mind following the body'? It is like a drunken man, wine coursing through his own body — at that time, his body sways, and his mind sways along with it; and it is likewise when the body grows lazy, the mind, too, grows lazy along with it — this is what is meant by the mind following the body. It is also like an infant, whose body is small and immature, and whose mind, too, is correspondingly small; while a grown adult's body is large, and his mind, too, is correspondingly large. It is also like a person whose body is coarse and rough, whose mind forever longs to obtain oil and ointment to soften and moisten it — this, too, is what is meant by the mind following the body. What is meant by 'the body following the mind'? Namely, going, coming, sitting, and lying down, and practicing giving, moral discipline, patience, and diligence — a sorrowful person's body grows gaunt and haggard; a joyful person's body grows plump and radiant; a fearful person's body trembles and shakes; one who listens to the Dharma with a single-pointed mind finds his body at ease and content; and a weeping person's own tears stream freely down his face — this is what is meant by the body following the mind. The bodhisattva is not like this: within both body and mind alike, he attains complete mastery — this is what is meant by attaining what was never attained before. Furthermore, good son, the bodily form the bodhisattva manifests may be as small as a single mote of dust, and yet, with this very subtle body, he is able to reach every immeasurable, boundless buddha-world, as numerous as the sands of the Gaṅgā, entirely without obstruction, while his mind remains forever settled, never stirring in the slightest — this is what is meant by the mind not following the body."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the second merit's own five-part structure ("attaining, seeing, hearing, arriving, knowing what was never attained, seen, heard, arrived at, or known before") as a deliberate escalation through the traditional six supernormal powers, treated here not as a checklist of magic tricks but as evidence of a specifically Mahāyāna capacity distinguished from the two lesser vehicles' own attainment of the identical powers by name: where a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha's own mind can produce only one transformation at a time, the bodhisattva's single mind produces simultaneous manifestation across all five destinies at once — a difference of kind, on his reading, not merely of degree, since it reflects the bodhisattva's freedom from the very body-mind interdependence the sūtra spends the bulk of this section illustrating through six ordinary examples (a drunken man, an infant, a person craving softening oil, and the correlations between emotional state and physical bearing).

He reads the closing image — a body the size of a mote of dust reaching every buddha-world in the universe while the mind remains utterly unmoved — as the section's own precise definition of "mastery": not merely doing something impossible by ordinary physical standards, but doing so without the mind being dragged along by the action the way an ordinary being's mind is dragged by his own body's fatigue or growth. This distinction between an action's scale and the mind's own stability during it becomes the technical thread the fascicle's remaining sections on the second merit will keep pulling — first through hearing, then through sight, then through knowledge itself.

Sounds Without Fixed Nature

Chinese Source

「復次善男子！云何菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃，昔所不聞而今得聞？菩薩摩訶薩先取聲相，所謂象聲馬聲、車聲人聲、貝聲鼓聲、簫笛等聲、歌聲哭聲而修習之，以修習故，能聞無量三千大千世界所有地獄音聲。復轉修習得異耳根，異於聲聞緣覺天耳。何以故？二乘所得清淨耳通，若依初禪淨妙四大，唯聞初禪不聞二禪，乃至四禪亦復如是，雖可一時得聞三千大千世界所有音聲，而不能聞無量無邊恒河沙等世界音聲。以是義故，菩薩所得異於聲聞緣覺耳根，以是異故，昔所不聞而今得聞。雖聞音聲，而心初無聞聲之相，不作有相、常相、樂相、我相、淨相、主相依相、作相因相、定相果相，以是義故，諸菩薩等昔所不聞而今得聞。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, how does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who cultivates this great nirvāṇa come to hear what he had never heard before? The bodhisattva-mahāsattva first takes hold of every kind of sound-mark — the sound of an elephant, of a horse, of a chariot, of a person, of a conch, of a drum, of a flute or pipe, of song, and of weeping — and cultivates himself upon them, and, through this very cultivation, becomes able to hear the sounds of hell throughout an immeasurable trichiliocosm. He then cultivates further still, and attains an ear-faculty entirely distinct from that of the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha's own heavenly ear. Why is this? The pure ear-power attained by the two lesser vehicles, should it depend on the pure, subtle four elements of the first meditative absorption, hears only the first absorption's own sounds, not the second's; and it is exactly the same, up through the fourth absorption — though it may, at one time, come to hear every sound throughout an entire trichiliocosm, it is unable to hear the sounds of worlds as numerous and boundless as the sands of the Gaṅgā. It is for this very reason that the power the bodhisattva attains is distinct from the ear-faculty of the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha — and it is on account of this very distinction that he comes to hear what he had never heard before. Though he hears these sounds, his mind never, in the end, holds any mark of 'hearing a sound' at all — giving rise to no mark of existence, no mark of permanence, no mark of pleasure, no mark of self, no mark of purity, no mark of a governing subject or its dependent, no mark of an agent, no mark of a cause, no mark of anything fixed, and no mark of any fruit. It is for this very reason that every bodhisattva comes to hear what he had never heard before."

爾時光明遍照高貴德王菩薩摩訶薩言：「若佛所說，不作定相、不作果相，是義不然。何以故？如來先說，若人聞是大涅槃經一句一字，必定得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提；如來於今，云何復言無定、無果？若得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，即是定相，即是果相；云何而言無定、無果？聞惡聲故則生惡心，生惡心故則至三塗，若至三塗則是定果；云何而言無定、無果？」爾時如來讚言：「善哉，善哉！善男子！能作是問。若使諸佛說諸音聲有定果相者，則非諸佛世尊之相，是魔王相、生死之相、遠涅槃相。何以故？一切諸佛凡所演說無定果相。善男子！譬如刀中見人面像，竪則見長，橫則見闊，若有定相，云何而得竪則見長、橫則見闊？以是義故，諸佛世尊凡所演說無定果相。善男子！夫涅槃者實非聲果，若使涅槃是聲果者，當知涅槃非是常法。善男子！譬如世間從因生法，有因則有果，無因則無果。因無常故，果亦無常。所以者何？因亦作果，果亦作因，以是義故，一切諸法無有定相。若使涅槃從因生者，因無常故，果亦無常；而是涅槃不從因生，體非是果，是故為常。善男子！以是義故，涅槃之體無定無果。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva Bright Virtuous King of Universal Illumination said: "Should the Buddha say that no fixed mark and no fruit-mark are formed at all, this does not seem correct. Why is this? The Tathāgata has said before that should a person hear even a single phrase or a single word of this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, he shall most certainly attain unsurpassed, complete awakening — how, then, can the Tathāgata now say there is no fixed mark and no fruit at all? Should one attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, this is itself a fixed mark, itself a fruit-mark — how, then, can it be said there is no fixed mark and no fruit? And should hearing an evil sound give rise to an evil mind, and this evil mind lead to the three evil destinies, arriving at the three evil destinies is itself a fixed fruit — how, then, can it be said there is no fixed mark and no fruit?" At that time, the Tathāgata praised him: "Excellent, excellent, good son, that you are able to ask such a question! Should any buddha teach that sounds possess a fixed fruit-mark, this would not be the mark of any buddha, any world-honored one, at all — it would be the mark of the demon king, the mark of birth-and-death, a mark far removed from nirvāṇa. Why is this? Whatever every buddha expounds is entirely without any fixed fruit-mark. Good son, it is like seeing a person's own reflected face in a blade — held upright, it appears elongated; held sideways, it appears widened. Were there a fixed mark to it, how could it appear elongated when held upright and widened when held sideways? It is for this very reason that whatever every buddha, every world-honored one, expounds is entirely without any fixed fruit-mark. Good son, nirvāṇa, in truth, is not the fruit of any sound at all — should nirvāṇa be the fruit of a sound, you should know that nirvāṇa would then not be an ever-abiding dharma at all. Good son, it is like how, in this world, a dharma arising from a cause has a fruit only where it has a cause, and no fruit where it has no cause — and, the cause being impermanent, the fruit, too, is impermanent. Why is this? Because a cause can itself become a fruit, and a fruit can itself become a cause — it is for this very reason that not a single dharma has any fixed mark at all. Should nirvāṇa arise from a cause, then, the cause being impermanent, its fruit, too, would be impermanent — but this nirvāṇa does not arise from any cause at all; its very substance is not a fruit, and it is for this reason that it is permanent. Good son, it is for this very reason that the substance of nirvāṇa is without any fixed mark and without any fruit at all."

「善男子！夫涅槃者，亦可言定，亦可言果。云何為定？一切諸佛所有涅槃常樂我淨，是故為定，無生老壞是故為定，一闡提等、犯四重禁、誹謗方等、作五逆罪，捨除本心，必定得故，是故為定。善男子！如汝所言，若人聞我說大涅槃一字一句，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者。汝於是義，猶未了了。汝當諦聽，吾當為汝更分別之。善男子！若有善男子、善女人，聞大涅槃一字一句，不作字相、不作句相、不作聞相、不作佛相、不作說相，如是義者名無相相，以無相相故，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。善男子！如汝所言，聞惡聲故到三塗者，是義不然。何以故？非以惡聲而至三塗，當知是果乃是惡心。所以者何？有善男子、善女人等，雖聞惡聲心不生惡，是故當知，非因惡聲生三塗中。而諸眾生因煩惱結，惡心滋多，生三惡趣，非因惡聲。若聲有定相，諸有聞者一切悉應生於惡心。或有生者，有不生者，是故當知聲無定相。以無定故，雖復因之，不生惡心。」「世尊！聲若無定，云何菩薩昔所不聞而今得聞？」「善男子！聲無定相，昔所不聞，令諸菩薩而今得聞，以是義故，我作是說，昔所不聞而今得聞。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, nirvāṇa can, indeed, also be spoken of as fixed, and can, indeed, also be spoken of as a fruit. What is meant by this fixedness? Every buddha's own nirvāṇa possesses permanence, bliss, self, and purity — it is for this reason that it is fixed; it is free of birth, aging, and decay — it is for this reason that it is fixed; and, should the icchantika and the rest, guilty of the four gravest offenses, of slandering the Vaipulya teachings, or of the five gravest offenses, cast off their own original mind, they most certainly attain it — it is for this reason, too, that it is fixed. Good son, as for what you have said — that should a person hear a single word or phrase of my own teaching of this great nirvāṇa, he attains unsurpassed, complete awakening — you have not yet fully grasped the meaning of this. Listen closely, and I shall distinguish it further for your sake. Good son, should any good son or good daughter hear a single word or phrase of this great nirvāṇa, giving rise to no mark of a word, no mark of a phrase, no mark of hearing, no mark of a buddha, and no mark of speaking at all, a meaning of this kind is named the markless mark — and it is through this very markless mark that he attains unsurpassed, complete awakening. Good son, as for what you have said, that hearing an evil sound leads to the three evil destinies — this is not quite correct. Why is this? It is not on account of the evil sound itself that one arrives at the three evil destinies; you should know that this fruit is, in fact, produced by an evil mind. Why is this? There are good sons and good daughters who, though they hear an evil sound, give rise to no evil mind on that account — you should therefore know that it is not the evil sound itself that produces rebirth among the three evil destinies. Rather, it is on account of the fetter of affliction that beings' own evil minds proliferate, producing rebirth among the three evil destinies — not on account of the evil sound. Were sound to possess a fixed mark, then everyone who hears it should, without exception, give rise to an evil mind — and yet some do, and some do not; you should therefore know that sound has no fixed mark at all. It is precisely because it has no fixed mark that, though one may depend upon it, one need not give rise to any evil mind on that account." "World-Honored One, if sound has no fixed nature, how, then, does the bodhisattva come to hear what he had never heard before?" "Good son, it is precisely because sound has no fixed mark that what was never heard before can now be heard by every bodhisattva — it is for this very reason that I speak of hearing what was never heard before."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Bright Virtuous King's objection as pressing a real tension the sūtra has created for itself: having just said sounds bear no fixed fruit-mark at all, the Buddha appears to contradict his own earlier promise, stated elsewhere in this very sūtra, that hearing even a single word of this teaching guarantees eventual awakening. His gloss on the Buddha's answer distinguishes two senses of "fixed" that must not be conflated: sound as such (any sound, taken as a bare physical or auditory phenomenon) bears no fixed, automatic causal power to produce any particular result — the same evil word spoken to two different listeners produces an evil mind in one and nothing at all in the other, since the actual cause of what follows is the listener's own mental state, not the sound itself, which is why the mirror-in-a-blade simile works: the same object of reflection genuinely does look different depending on how the blade is held, without this proving the reflected face itself is unstable or unreal.

Yet nirvāṇa, and the specific teaching of this sūtra, are "fixed" in an entirely different sense — not as an automatic causal guarantee attached to the bare act of hearing sound, but as an unconditioned reality that does not arise from a cause and therefore cannot decay the way anything caused eventually must. The reconciling move, on Huiyuan's reading, is the "markless mark": one who hears this sūtra's words without clinging to any fixed notion of a word, a phrase, a speaker, or an act of hearing at all — precisely the same non-clinging quality this whole second merit has been describing through the bodhisattva's freedom from body-mind interdependence — is the one for whom hearing genuinely does guarantee awakening, not because the sound itself possesses magical fixed power, but because a mind free of every fixed mark is already the condition awakening requires. The guarantee was never about the sound; it was always about the quality of the hearing.

Seeing the Buddhas of Every World

Chinese Source

「善男子！云何昔所不見而今得見？善男子！菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃微妙經典，先取明相，所謂日月星宿、燈燎燈燭、珠火之明、藥草等光，以修習故得異眼根，異於聲聞緣覺所得。云何為異？二乘所得清淨天眼，若依欲界四大眼根，不見初禪，若依初禪不見上地，乃至自眼猶不能見，若欲多見，極至三千大千世界。菩薩摩訶薩不修天眼，見妙色身悉是骨相，雖見他方恒河沙等世界色相，不作色相、不作常相、有相、物相、名字等相，作因緣相，不作見相。不言是眼微妙淨相，唯見因緣、非因緣相。……以是義故，菩薩所得清淨天眼，異於聲聞緣覺所得。以是異故，一時遍見十方世界現在諸佛，是名菩薩昔所不見而今得見。以是異故，能見微塵，聲聞緣覺所不能見。以是異故，雖見自眼，初無見相，見無常相。見凡夫身三十六物不淨充滿，如於掌中觀阿摩勒果，以是義故，昔所不見而今得見。若見眾生所有色相，則知其人大小乘根，一觸衣故，亦知是人善惡諸根差別之相，以是義故，昔所不知而今得知。以一見故，昔所不知而今得知，以此知故，昔所不見而今得見。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, what is meant by 'seeing what he had never seen before'? Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who cultivates this subtle and wondrous scripture of the great nirvāṇa first takes hold of every kind of luminous mark — the sun, moon, and stars, torch, lamp, and candle, the light of gems and fire, and the glow of medicinal herbs — and, through cultivating himself upon them, attains an eye-faculty entirely distinct from that of the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha. How is it distinct? The pure heavenly eye attained by the two lesser vehicles, should it depend on the four elements of the eye-faculty in the desire realm, cannot see the first meditative absorption; should it depend on the first absorption, it cannot see any higher stage; and, in the end, it is even unable to see its own eye — and, should it wish to see as much as possible, it can reach, at the very most, an entire trichiliocosm. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva does not cultivate the heavenly eye in this way at all: he sees even the finest, most excellent form as nothing but a skeleton's own mark, and, though he sees the very forms of worlds as numerous as the sands of the Gaṅgā in every other direction, he gives rise to no mark of form, no mark of permanence, no mark of existence, no mark of a thing, and no mark of a name — he forms only the mark of a cause and condition, never the mark of an act of seeing itself. He does not say that this eye possesses any subtle, pure mark of its own; he sees only the mark of what is a cause and condition, and what is not… It is for this very reason that the pure heavenly eye the bodhisattva attains is distinct from that attained by the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha. On account of this very distinction, he sees, in a single moment, every present buddha throughout the worlds of the ten directions — this is what is meant by seeing what he had never seen before. On account of this very distinction, he is able to see even the finest mote of dust, which the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha are unable to see. On account of this very distinction, though he sees his own eye, he never, in the end, holds any mark of 'seeing' at all, seeing only the mark of impermanence. He sees an ordinary being's own body as filled entirely with its thirty-six impure substances, as clearly as viewing a myrobalan fruit held in the palm of his own hand — it is for this very reason that he comes to see what he had never seen before. Should he see any being's own bodily form, he thereby knows whether that person's faculties belong to the greater or the lesser vehicle, and, merely by touching that person's own clothing, he likewise knows the distinguishing marks of that person's own wholesome and unwholesome faculties — it is for this very reason that he comes to know what he had never known before. Through this single act of seeing, he comes to know what he had never known before; and through this very knowing, he comes to see what he had never seen before."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads "casting off compassion to attain compassion" as describing a single continuous transformation rather than two separate acts performed in sequence: the compassion cast off is defined by its dependence on a specific, worldly object (a particular class of person judged especially deserving or undeserving of pity — the icchantika, the grave offender, but also, on the list's more uncomfortable second half, eunuchs, women, and those in stigmatized trades), while the compassion attained arises independent of any such classification at all, which is precisely why it "does not arise from any cause or condition" — it is not compassion caused by encountering a pitiable object, but an unconditioned disposition that simply is, prior to and regardless of any particular being's own circumstances.

He reads the section's closing four "non-seeings" (no compassion of one's own, no compassion of another's, no precept-keeper, no precept-breaker; and, further, perceiving suffering yet seeing no being, feeling pain yet seeing no one who feels it) as this merit's own version of the same emptiness-of-fixed-marks principle running through the second merit's treatment of hearing, seeing, and knowing: genuine, unconditioned compassion is not compassion that has been purified of feeling anything, but compassion that no longer superimposes a fixed self, a fixed sufferer, or a fixed moral category onto what it responds to — which is why it can extend, without discrimination or exception, to every being the ordinary, worldly compassion this section catalogues would instinctively withhold it from.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the field-of-merit teaching as extending the third merit's own "no fixed compassion" principle into the domain of giving specifically: the sūtra's own four-part classification of giving (pure giver/impure receiver, and the reverse, and both pure, and both impure) is offered precisely to demonstrate that purity of the resulting merit never depends on correctly sorting beings in advance into "worthy" and "unworthy" recipients — a bodhisattva who has genuinely internalized this teaching stops performing that sorting altogether, not out of indifference to moral distinctions, but because clinging to any fixed category of "true field of merit" is itself, on his reading, a subtler form of the same discrimination this whole chapter has been dismantling since Part 22's opening argument that nothing is fixed.

He reads the ten vows purifying a future buddha-land as mapped with exact precision onto the ten traditional wholesome courses of action (freedom from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, divisive speech, harsh speech, idle speech, craving, malice, and wrong view), each vow taking the specific quality of restraint practiced now and projecting it forward as a specific, concrete feature of an entire future world — long life and strength from non-killing, material abundance from non-stealing, freedom from affliction from sexual restraint, beautiful sound from truthful speech, social harmony from non-divisive speech, a land literally free of every obstacle from gentle speech, freedom from suffering from meaningful speech, freedom from envy from non-craving, universal loving-kindness from non-malice, and universal wisdom from right view. His point in tracing this precise one-to-one correspondence is that a pure land, on this sūtra's own account, is never a supernatural reward bestowed independently of conduct, but the direct, world-scale expression of exactly which ordinary ethical restraints a bodhisattva actually practiced on the way there — a systematic causal structure this collection has not seen laid out with this degree of precision since the parallel-world narrative of Acala in Part 21.

Good son, you should not now think that any buddha appears within an impure world at all — know that a thought of this kind is unwholesome and narrow. You should now know that I, in reality, have never actually appeared within this very Jambudvīpa at all. It is like someone who says that only this world possesses the sun and moon, and that no other world at all possesses them — words of this kind have no basis in reason whatsoever. Should any bodhisattva say that this present buddha-world is foul and impure, while some other buddha-land is pure and resplendent, this is exactly the same kind of error. Good son, to the west of this Sahā world, past buddha-lands as numerous as thirty-two times the sands of the Gaṅgā, there is a world named Unsurpassed. Why is that land named Unsurpassed? Every resplendent feature of that land is entirely equal, without the slightest difference between them, just like the western Land of Peace and Bliss, and just like the eastern world of Full-Moon Light. I myself have appeared in the world from that very land — and, for the sake of transforming beings, I now manifest the turning of the Dharma-wheel here, within this Jambudvīpa. And it is not I alone who manifests the turning of the Dharma-wheel here — every buddha, likewise, turns the Dharma-wheel here as well. It is for this very reason that every buddha, every world-honored one, has, without exception, cultivated all ten of these matters. Good son, the bodhisattva Maitreya, through the power of his own vow, shall, in an age to come, cause this very world to become pure and resplendent — it is for this very reason that not a single one of every buddha's own worlds fails to be resplendent and pure."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the three kinds of "what still remains" as forming a hierarchy of increasingly subtle residue: the residual fruit of affliction describes karmic consequences that persist across lifetimes even after the original unwholesome mental state has passed, mapped precisely onto four specific afflictions (craving, anger, delusion, arrogance) each yielding its own distinct catalogue of animal rebirths, human deformities, and, for a renunciant, a specific one of the four gravest precepts he becomes prone to transgressing; residual karma describes what remains binding even a stream-enterer, once-returner, or non-returner to a fixed, if diminishing, number of further rebirths; and residual existence describes the subtlest remainder of all — an arhat or pratyekabuddha who has genuinely exhausted every karmic cause and fetter, and yet still "turns" within the fixed, static fruit of his own attainment, unable to progress further toward the greater completion a bodhisattva pursues. Only the bodhisattva who cultivates this sūtra, on his reading, eliminates residue at all three levels simultaneously.

On Bright Virtuous King's closing question — why does the Buddha, having cultivated all ten items himself, appear to have skipped the fourth (purifying a buddha-land), teaching instead within this manifestly impure Sahā world — Huiyuan reads the Buddha's answer as resting on a single point already established across this whole chapter: the very premise of the question (that this present world is simply, straightforwardly impure) mistakes a skillful, deliberately chosen manifestation for the Buddha's own actual, underlying reality, exactly as Part 22's extended catalogue of paired negations warned against fixing the Tathāgata's nature to any single observable feature. The Buddha's own true land of attainment, the world Unsurpassed, is every bit as pure as any other buddha's own land; what appears here in Jambudvīpa is a deliberate manifestation for the specific purpose of teaching beings suited to exactly this kind of world — with Maitreya's own future vow to purify this same world eventually demonstrating, within the narrative's own terms, that its impurity was never a fixed, permanent feature to begin with, but simply the present stage of a trajectory every buddha's own teaching activity is already steering toward its opposite.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Bright Virtuous King's question as pressing a genuine puzzle: the five outcomes named here (complete faculties, freedom from birth in a border region, the gods' own affection, universal reverence, knowledge of past lives) are, in fact, classical, well-attested fruits of ordinary generosity elsewhere in the canon, so crediting them instead to this sūtra specifically requires some real distinction to be drawn. His gloss on the Buddha's answer traces five paired contrasts, each identical in outward description yet different in kind: giving's own version of each fruit remains contingent, temporary, mixed with residual impurity, unremarkable relative to a buddha's own attainment, indistinguishable from an ordinary being's fortune, still subject to outflow, and incapable of extending genuine benefit to all beings universally — while the same five fruits, attained through this sūtra, are each secured absolutely, permanently, purely, supremely, distinctively, free of outflow, and universally beneficial.

The closing formula — giving alone relieves hunger, but only this teaching relieves the craving underlying all twenty-five states of existence; giving alone continues the ordinary cycle of birth-and-death, but only this teaching severs it — is, on his reading, this fascicle's own most economical restatement of a distinction it has been building since the very first merit in Part 21: ordinary virtuous practice, however genuinely wholesome, still operates within the categories of cause and effect, portion and fruit, that this sūtra's own teaching transcends entirely, which is why identical-looking outcomes can nonetheless differ in what actually produces and sustains them.

The Vajra Samādhi

Chinese Source

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the vajra samādhi's own extended catalogue of similes (an indestructible jewel able to cut anything, the supreme treasure to which every lesser samādhi "belongs" as a lesser king belongs to a wheel-turning sovereign, a mountain from whose summit every direction is seen with perfect clarity, spring rain so dense no gap remains uncovered, seven suns burning away an entire mountain's own forest) as together illustrating one central claim: the vajra samādhi's own destructive power — shattering the fixed mark of every single dharma, without exception — is total and unlimited, and yet, remarkably, this totality never diminishes the samādhi itself, exactly as a diamond used to cut every other substance never grows blunt through the cutting. His sharpest point concerns the recurring refrain — the bodhisattva multiplies bodies, travels to countless worlds, severs the affliction of innumerable beings, and yet, at every step, "never gives rise to the thought that he has done so" — which he reads as this fascicle's own final, most technically precise statement of the non-attainment principle first introduced through the eight kinds of mastery in Part 23: an accomplishment performed without the accompanying thought "I have accomplished this" is not a lesser or incomplete accomplishment, but the very thing that keeps the vajra samādhi's own destructive power from generating a new, subtler form of clinging (pride in one's own power to destroy clinging) in its place.

On the closing three etymological similes for "vajra" itself (color unfixed in sunlight, value beyond appraisal, a poor man's fortune transformed), Huiyuan reads these as returning, one final time, to the "nothing is fixed" argument that has organized this entire chapter since Part 22 — even the name of the sūtra's own most powerful named samādhi is chosen specifically because vajra itself, among ordinary objects, is the one whose very appearance famously refuses to hold a single fixed color under changing light, making it the perfect image for a concentration whose entire function is to reveal that no dharma anywhere possesses the fixed mark ordinary perception assigns to it. This closes the sixth merit, and with it, the fascicle's own extended run through five of the chapter's ten merits in relatively rapid succession, compared to the three full fascicles the first merit alone required.
