The Buddha's Last Announcement, and the World's Final Offering

The sūtra opens at Kuśinagara, among the twin sāla trees, on the day the Buddha will enter parinirvāṇa. A great voice goes out to every realm, inviting last questions; a healing light goes out after it, and the whole of existence — monks, nuns, bodhisattvas, kings, nāgas, gods up through the Brahmā heavens, even Māra himself — converges on the sāla forest bearing offerings without number. Every single offering is silently refused. The chapter closes with the arrival of a bodhisattva from a buddha-land in the east, Mañjuśrī's reassurance to a frightened assembly, and the Buddha's light withdrawing back into his own mouth — the last and most ominous of six signs that fascicles ahead will draw to their conclusion in Cunda's own offering, the one gift this sūtra's opening was already refusing every other in order to receive.

The Buddha's Final Announcement

Chinese Source

如是我聞：一時佛在拘尸那國力士生地阿利羅跋提河邊娑羅雙樹間。爾時世尊與大比丘八十億百千人俱，前後圍遶。二月十五日臨涅槃時，以佛神力出大音聲，其聲遍滿乃至有頂，隨其類音普告眾生：「今日如來、應、正遍知，憐愍眾生，覆護眾生，等視眾生如羅睺羅，為作歸依屋舍室宅。大覺世尊將欲涅槃，一切眾生若有所疑，今悉可問，為最後問。」爾時世尊於晨朝時，從其面門放種種光，其明雜色，青、黃、赤、白、頗梨、馬瑙，光遍照此三千大千佛之世界，乃至十方亦復如是。其中所有六趣眾生遇斯光者，罪垢煩惱一切消除。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard. At one time the Buddha was dwelling among the twin sāla trees on the bank of the Ajitavatī River, in the land of the Mallas at Kuśinagara, together with a great assembly of eight hundred thousand million monks surrounding him on every side. On the fifteenth day of the second month, as he drew near his parinirvāṇa, the Buddha, by his own spiritual power, sent forth a great voice that filled every realm up to the summit of existence, proclaiming to beings of every kind, each in the voice proper to its kind: "Today the Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly Awakened One, out of pity for all beings and to shelter and protect them — regarding every being as he regards his own son Rāhula — becomes for them a refuge, a house, a home. The Great Awakened World-Honored One is about to enter parinirvāṇa; let any being who still has a doubt ask it now, for this is the last chance to ask." Then, in the early morning, the World-Honored One sent forth from his face rays of many colors — blue, yellow, red, white, crystal, and agate — light that shone throughout this trichiliocosm, this three-thousandfold great-thousandfold Buddha-world, and likewise through all the ten directions. Every being of the six destinies touched by that light had every sin, defilement, and affliction wiped away.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's commentary reads what follows as six successive signs the Buddha gives of his own parinirvāṇa — sound, light, the earthquake, the whitening forest, the appearance of a pure land, and finally the light's withdrawal back into his mouth — and groups them in pairs: the first three summon the assembly, the last three press the teaching home. The voice tells beings when and who is dying, so that they will come out of longing for him; the light shows them where to come, and burns away the very afflictions that would keep them from making the journey — "the text says beings touched by this light have every sin and defilement removed," Huiyuan notes, precisely because grief-stricken beings setting out to see a dying teacher need that obstruction cleared first. Two further signs are still to come in this fascicle, and Huiyuan is explicit that all six point at the same single fact — parinirvāṇa — from different sides, not that each carries some separate private meaning; he singles out and refutes, one by one, several rival readings that try to find a distinct hidden message in each individual sign.

Earthquake, and the First Wave of Grief

Chinese Source

是諸眾生見聞是已，心大憂愁，同時舉聲悲啼號哭：「嗚呼慈父，痛哉苦哉。」舉手拍頭搥胸叫喚，其中或有身體戰慄、涕泣、哽咽。

爾時大地諸山大海，皆悉震動，時諸眾生共相謂言：「且各裁抑，莫大愁苦。當疾往詣拘尸那城力士生處，至如來所，頭面禮敬，勸請如來莫般涅槃，住世一劫若減一劫。」互相執手復作是言：「世間空虛，眾生福盡，不善諸業，增長出世。仁等今當速往速往，如來不久必入涅槃。」復作是言：「世間空虛，世間空虛，我等從今無有救護，無所宗仰，貧窮孤露。一旦遠離無上世尊，設有疑惑當復問誰？」

時有無量諸大弟子，尊者摩訶迦旃延、尊者薄俱羅、尊者優波難陀，如是等諸大比丘，遇佛光者，其身戰掉，乃至大動不能自持，心濁迷悶，發聲大喚，生如是等種種苦惱。

爾時復有八十百千諸比丘等，皆阿羅漢，心得自在，所作已辦，離諸煩惱，調伏諸根，如大龍王有大威德，成就空慧，逮得己利，如栴檀林栴檀圍遶，如師子王師子圍遶，成就如是無量功德，一切皆是佛之真子。於其晨朝，日始初出，離常住處，嚼楊枝時，遇佛光明，并相謂言：「仁等，速疾漱口澡手。」作是言已，舉身毛竪，遍體血現如波羅奢花，涕泣盈目生大苦惱。為欲利益安樂眾生，成就大乘第一空行，顯發如來方便密教，為不斷絕種種說法，為諸眾生調伏因緣故，疾至佛所稽首佛足，繞百千匝，合掌恭敬，却坐一面。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seeing and hearing this, all these beings were overcome with grief, and together raised their voices in weeping and lamentation: "Ah, our compassionate father — how painful, how bitter!" Some struck their own heads and beat their breasts, crying aloud; others trembled in their bodies, weeping and choking on their own sobs.

Then the whole earth, every mountain, and the great ocean all quaked and shook. The beings said to one another: "Let each of us restrain ourselves a little, and not give way to excessive grief. Let us go quickly to the birthplace of the Mallas in the city of Kuśinagara, to where the Tathāgata is, bow our heads to his feet in reverence, and beg him not to enter parinirvāṇa, but to remain in the world for a kalpa, or at least for less than a kalpa." Taking one another's hands, they said further: "The world is empty, empty. The merit of beings is exhausted, and unwholesome deeds are increasing and spreading through the world. All of you, go now, go quickly — the Tathāgata will surely enter parinirvāṇa before long." And they said again: "The world is empty, the world is empty. From this day we shall have no one to save or protect us, nothing to rely on or revere; we shall be poor, destitute, and alone. Once we are parted from the Peerless World-Honored One, if any doubt should arise in us, whom shall we ask?"

At that time there were countless great disciples — the venerable Mahākātyāyana, the venerable Bakkula, the venerable Upananda, and other great monks like them — who, touched by the Buddha's light, trembled so violently in their bodies that they could not compose themselves; their minds grew clouded and distressed, and they cried out, giving rise to every kind of anguish.

At that time there were also eighty hundred-thousand monks, every one an arhat, minds that had attained mastery, their work already done, free of every affliction, their faculties well-tamed — like great nāga kings of great majestic virtue, they had accomplished the wisdom of emptiness and attained their own true benefit; like a sandalwood forest surrounded by sandalwood, like a lion king surrounded by lions, they had accomplished immeasurable virtues of this kind, and every one of them was a true child of the Buddha. In the early morning, just as the sun was rising, while they were still at their dwelling places chewing their tooth-cleaning twigs, they were touched by the Buddha's light, and said to one another: "Friends, quickly rinse your mouths and wash your hands." No sooner had they said this than the hair on their bodies stood on end, and blood appeared all over their skin like the palāśa flower; tears filled their eyes, and they were overcome with great anguish. Wishing to benefit and bring peace to beings, to accomplish the Great Vehicle's foremost practice of emptiness, to reveal the Tathāgata's skillful and hidden teaching, and, for the sake of beings still to be tamed, to keep the teaching of the Dharma from ever ceasing, they hastened to where the Buddha was, bowed their heads to his feet, circled him a hundred thousand times, joined their palms in reverence, and withdrew to sit to one side.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan is struck by a question a casual reading would pass over: these eighty hundred-thousand monks are already described as arhats — beings who, by definition, have finished the work that produces grief. Why do they weep and tremble at all? His answer draws a line between two kinds of sorrow: the ordinary suffering born of unresolved craving, which an arhat has genuinely extinguished, and the specific anguish of losing a teacher, which even a mind free of affliction can still feel — "an arhat has cut off suffering with respect to worldly things, but before the Buddha's own presence he cannot fully compose himself." Huiyuan reaches for a well-known image to make the same point: Mahākāśyapa and Ānanda were famously immovable before the ordinary five desires, and yet when the bodhisattva Mahākinnara played his lute of lapis lazuli, Mahākāśyapa found himself dancing without noticing it, and Ānanda singing along — a pleasure pitched at a register only bodhisattvas can fully bear, before which even the steadiest arhat's composure gives way. Grief at a teacher's parinirvāṇa, on this reading, belongs to the same register: not a lapse back into affliction, but a response scaled to what is actually being lost.

The Nuns, and a Ganges-Sand of Bodhisattvas

Chinese Source

爾時復有拘陀羅女、善賢比丘尼、優波難陀比丘尼、海意比丘尼，與六十億比丘尼等，一切亦是大阿羅漢，諸漏已盡，心得自在，所作已辦，離諸煩惱，調伏諸根，猶如大龍有大威德，成就空慧。亦於晨朝，日初出時，舉身毛竪，遍體血現如波羅奢花，涕泣盈目生大苦惱。亦欲利益安樂眾生，成就大乘第一空行，顯發如來方便密教，為不斷絕種種說法，為諸眾生調伏因緣故，疾至佛所稽首佛足，遶百千匝，合掌恭敬，却坐一面。

於比丘尼眾中復有諸比丘尼，皆是菩薩，人中之龍，位階十地安住不動，為化眾生現受女身，而常修集四無量心，得自在力，能化作佛。

爾時復有一恒河沙菩薩摩訶薩，人中之龍，位階十地安住不動，方便現身。其名曰：海德菩薩、無盡意菩薩，如是等菩薩摩訶薩而為上首。其心皆悉敬重大乘，安住大乘，深解大乘，愛樂大乘，守護大乘，善能隨順一切世間，作是誓言：「諸未度者，當令得度。」已於過世無數劫中，修持淨戒，善持所行，解未解者，紹三寶種使不斷絕，於未來世當轉法輪，以大莊嚴而自莊嚴，成就如是無量功德，等觀眾生如視一子。亦於晨朝，日初出時遇佛光明，舉身毛竪，遍體血現如波羅奢花，涕泣盈目生大苦惱。亦為利益安樂眾生，成就大乘第一空行，顯發如來方便密教，為不斷絕種種說法，為諸眾生調伏因緣故，疾至佛所稽首佛足，繞百千匝，合掌恭敬，却坐一面。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time there were also the woman Kuṇḍalā, the nun Bhadrā, the nun Upananda, the nun Sāgaramati, and sixty million other nuns, every one of them likewise a great arhat, every affliction already exhausted, minds that had attained mastery, their work already done, free of every affliction, their faculties well-tamed — like great nāgas of great majestic virtue, they had accomplished the wisdom of emptiness. They too, in the early morning as the sun was rising, felt the hair on their bodies stand on end, blood appearing all over their skin like the palāśa flower, tears filling their eyes, overcome with great anguish. Wishing likewise to benefit and bring peace to beings, to accomplish the Great Vehicle's foremost practice of emptiness, to reveal the Tathāgata's skillful and hidden teaching, and, for the sake of beings still to be tamed, to keep the teaching of the Dharma from ever ceasing, they hastened to where the Buddha was, bowed their heads to his feet, circled him a hundred thousand times, joined their palms in reverence, and withdrew to sit to one side.

Among these nuns there were others who were in truth bodhisattvas, dragons among humankind, abiding unshaken at the tenth ground, who had taken on a woman's body only to teach beings, while constantly cultivating the four immeasurable minds, and who had gained the power to manifest as buddhas at will.

At that time there was also a bodhisattva-mahāsattva assembly numbering a Ganges-sand, dragons among humankind, abiding unshaken at the tenth ground, present there by their own skillful manifestation. Among them the bodhisattva Sāgaraguṇa and the bodhisattva Akṣayamati were foremost. Their minds all revered the Great Vehicle, abided in the Great Vehicle, deeply understood the Great Vehicle, delighted in the Great Vehicle, and guarded the Great Vehicle; skillfully according with every kind of being in the world, they had made this vow: "Whoever has not yet crossed over, we will bring across." Through countless past kalpas they had upheld pure precepts and kept well to their practice, freeing those not yet freed, so that the seed of the Three Jewels would never be cut off; in ages to come they would turn the wheel of the Dharma, adorning themselves with great adornment, and had accomplished immeasurable virtues of this kind, regarding every being with the same care a parent gives an only child. They too, in the early morning as the sun was rising, were touched by the Buddha's light, and felt the hair on their bodies stand on end, blood appearing all over their skin like the palāśa flower, tears filling their eyes, overcome with great anguish. Wishing likewise to benefit and bring peace to beings, to accomplish the Great Vehicle's foremost practice of emptiness, to reveal the Tathāgata's skillful and hidden teaching, and, for the sake of beings still to be tamed, to keep the teaching of the Dharma from ever ceasing, they hastened to where the Buddha was, bowed their heads to his feet, circled him a hundred thousand times, joined their palms in reverence, and withdrew to sit to one side.

The Upāsakas' Funeral Offerings

Chinese Source

爾時復有二恒河沙諸優婆塞，受持五戒，威儀具足，其名曰：威德無垢稱王優婆塞、善德優婆塞等而為上首，深樂觀察諸對治門，所謂苦樂、常無常、淨不淨、我無我、實不實、歸依非歸依、眾生非眾生、恒非恒、安非安、為無為、斷不斷、涅槃非涅槃、增上非增上，常樂觀察如是等法對治之門，亦欲樂聞無上大乘，如所聞已，能為他說。善持淨戒，渴仰大乘，既自充足，復能充足餘渴仰者。善能攝取無上智慧，愛樂大乘，守護大乘。善能隨順一切世間，度未度者，解未解者。紹三寶種使不斷絕，於未來世當轉法輪，以大莊嚴而自莊嚴，心常深味清淨戒行，悉能成就如是功德，於諸眾生生大悲心，平等無二如視一子。

亦於晨朝日初出時，為欲闍毘如來身故，人人各取香木萬束、栴檀沈水、牛頭栴檀、天木香等，是一一木，文理及附，皆有七寶微妙光明，譬如種種雜彩畫飾，以佛力故有是妙色，青黃赤白，為諸眾生之所樂見。諸木皆以種種香塗，欝金沈水及膠香等，散以諸花而為莊嚴，優鉢羅花、拘物頭花、波頭摩花、分陀利花。諸香木上懸五色幡，柔軟微妙猶如天衣，憍奢耶衣芻摩繒綵。是諸香木載以寶車，是諸寶車出種種光，青黃赤白。轅轢皆以七寶廁填，是一一車駕以四馬，是一一馬駿疾如風。一一車前竪立五十七寶妙幢，真金羅網彌覆其上，一一寶車復有五十微妙寶蓋，一一車上垂諸花鬘，優鉢羅花、拘物頭花、波頭摩花、分陀利花，其花純以真金為葉，金剛為臺。是花臺中多有黑蜂，遊集其中歡娛受樂，又出妙音，所謂無常、苦、空、無我，是音聲中，復說菩薩本所行道。復有種種歌舞伎樂、箏笛、箜篌、簫瑟、鼓吹，是樂音中復出是言：「苦哉，苦哉，世間空虛。」一一車前，有優婆塞擎四寶案，是諸案上有種種花，優鉢羅花、拘物頭花、波頭摩花、分陀利花，欝金諸香及餘薰香，微妙第一。

諸優婆塞為佛及僧，辦諸食具，種種備足，皆是栴檀沈水香薪，八功德水之所成熟，其食甘美有六種味：一苦、二醋、三甘、四辛、五醎、六淡。復有三德：一者輕軟、二者淨潔、三者如法，作如是等種種莊嚴。至力士生處娑羅雙樹間，復以金沙遍布其地，以加陵伽衣、欽婆羅衣及繒綵衣而覆沙上，周匝遍滿十二由旬。為佛及僧敷置七寶師子之座，其座高大如須彌山，是諸座上皆有寶帳，垂諸瓔珞。諸娑羅樹，悉懸種種微妙幡蓋，種種好香以塗樹身，種種名花以散樹間。諸優婆塞各作是念：「一切眾生若有所乏，須食與食、須飲與飲、須頭與頭、須目與目，隨諸眾生所須之物，皆悉給與。」作是施時，離欲瞋恚、穢濁毒心，無餘思惟求世福樂，唯期無上清淨菩提。

是優婆塞等，皆已安住於菩薩道，復作是念：「如來今者受我食已，當入涅槃。」作是念已，身毛皆竪，遍體血現如波羅奢花，涕泣盈目生大苦惱，各各齎持供養之具，載以寶車、香木、幢幡、寶蓋、飲食，疾至佛所稽首佛足，以其所持供養之具，供養如來，遶百千匝，舉聲號泣，哀動天地，搥胸大叫，淚下如雨，復相謂言：「苦哉，仁者！世間空虛，世間空虛。」便自舉身，投如來前，而白佛言：「唯願如來，哀受我等最後供養。」世尊知時默然不受。

如是三請，悉皆不許。諸優婆塞不果所願，心懷悲惱，默然而住。

猶如慈父唯有一子，卒病喪亡，送其屍骸置於塚間，歸還悵悢，愁憂苦惱。諸優婆塞憂愁苦惱亦復如是，以諸供具安置一處，却在一面，默然而坐。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time there were also two Ganges-sands of upāsakas, holding the five precepts, their conduct fully accomplished, among whom the upāsaka King Vimalakīrti of Majestic Virtue and the upāsaka Sudatta were foremost. They delighted in examining the gateways that counter attachment — suffering and happiness, permanence and impermanence, purity and impurity, self and no-self, the real and the unreal, refuge and its absence, being a sentient being and not being one, the constant and the inconstant, safety and its absence, the conditioned and the unconditioned, what is to be cut off and what is not, nirvāṇa and its absence, the surpassing and the unsurpassing — constantly delighting in examining these dharma-gateways that counter attachment, and wishing also to hear the peerless Great Vehicle, so that, once having heard it, they could teach it to others. Well upholding pure precepts, thirsting after the Great Vehicle, having satisfied themselves they could satisfy others still thirsting. Skillfully gathering peerless wisdom, delighting in the Great Vehicle, guarding the Great Vehicle. Skillfully according with every kind of being in the world, freeing those not yet freed, releasing those not yet released. So that the seed of the Three Jewels would never be cut off, in ages to come they would turn the wheel of the Dharma, adorning themselves with great adornment, their minds forever savoring pure conduct, having accomplished virtues of this kind, giving rise to great compassion for every being, regarding each one equally, without exception, as an only child.

Also in the early morning, as the sun was rising, wishing to cremate the Tathāgata's body, each of them gathered ten thousand bundles of fragrant wood — sandalwood, aloeswood, ox-head sandalwood, and heavenly wood-incense — every single piece of which, in its very grain, carried the fine luster of the seven treasures, as though painted over in every color, made so by the Buddha's own power, blue, yellow, red, and white, delightful for every being to see. Every piece of wood was smeared with every kind of fragrance — saffron, aloeswood, resin-incense — and adorned further with scattered flowers: blue lotus, red lotus, white lotus, and the great white lotus. Banners of five colors, soft and fine as a garment of the gods, silk brocade of every weave, were hung above the fragrant wood. This fragrant wood was loaded onto jeweled carriages, and these jeweled carriages sent out light of every color, blue, yellow, red, and white; their shafts and wheel-rims were inlaid throughout with the seven treasures, and each carriage was drawn by four horses, each horse as swift as the wind. Before each carriage stood fifty-seven exquisite jeweled banner-poles, draped over in a net of true gold; each jeweled carriage also carried fifty fine jeweled canopies, and from each carriage hung garlands of flowers — blue lotus, red lotus, white lotus, and the great white lotus — their petals wrought purely of true gold, their calyxes of diamond. Within these flower-calyxes gathered many black bees, roaming there in delight, and sending out a wondrous sound — the sound of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self — a sound in which, further, the bodhisattva path once practiced was proclaimed. There was also every kind of song and dance and music — zithers, flutes, harps, panpipes, and drums — and within this music, further, came the words: "How bitter, how bitter — the world is empty." Before each carriage stood an upāsaka bearing a stand of the four treasures, laden with every kind of flower — blue lotus, red lotus, white lotus, and the great white lotus — with saffron and every other fragrance, of the finest and rarest kind.

And in giving this way, they were free of desire, of anger, and of any defiled or poisonous thought, seeking nothing further for worldly happiness, aspiring only to the peerless, pure awakening.

These upāsakas, all of them already established on the bodhisattva path, thought further: "Once the Tathāgata has accepted our food, he will enter parinirvāṇa." The world is empty, the world is empty." The World-Honored One, knowing that the time had not come, remained silent and did not accept it.

Though they asked three times in this way, he refused every time. The upāsakas, their wish ungranted, were filled with sorrow, and fell silent.

It was like a loving father whose only son has suddenly sickened and died: having carried the body to the burial ground and returned home, he is left disconsolate, sunk in grief and anguish. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan pauses over a detail easy to read past: why do black bees, of all creatures, gather in the golden flower-calyxes on these funeral carriages? He records that some readers take the color itself as a deliberate sign — black standing for beings still clouded in ignorance, drawn even here to the flowers of an offering they cannot yet understand — but declines to settle the question, since the sūtra itself never glosses the detail, and he will not assert a symbolism the text does not state outright. He is considerably more confident, a few lines earlier, about a structural point: this whole assembly is being listed in a deliberate order — renunciants before laypeople, because the ordained carry less worldly rank but are fewer in number and thus listed first; śrāvakas before bodhisattvas, for reasons he traces through four different rationales (proximity to the Buddha, dignity of outward bearing, doctrinal register, and sheer numbers) — so that what can look like an arbitrary parade of donors is in fact organized throughout by consistent, statable principles, the same care Huiyuan brings to nearly every verse of this text.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan takes care to separate this passage from a simple denunciation of women as such. His gloss on "taking on a woman's body precisely to reproach the life of the household" (現女身呵責家法) reads these upāsikās as bodhisattvas who have deliberately chosen female rebirth as the more effective platform from which to deliver a teaching aimed at the household life in general, using their own bodies as the example precisely because a body already burdened by a culture's low regard is the sharpest possible text for a meditation everyone, regardless of their own body, is meant to draw the same conclusion from. He organizes the discourse itself into three components matched to three faults he finds named in the sūtra's own language — the body's suffering (compared to four vipers and countless devouring worms), its impurity (traced through five classical sources of impurity: the impure origin in the parents' fluids, the impure dwelling-place in the womb, the impure substance of its thirty-six parts, the impure marks of its nine ever-flowing openings, and its impure end in decay), and its lack of any fixed self (unpacked through the fortress-city simile, mapped organ by organ) — and notes that the sūtra states only three of these five classical categories of impurity explicitly, leaving the enumeration itself, not just its content, as something the careful reader has to reconstruct.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan raises, and answers, the plainest objection a reader could bring to this whole scene: if the whitened forest and the jeweled pure land are meant as further signs of the Buddha's approaching death, why does the sūtra later go on to call this same forest "delightful," rather than a mark of dying and decay? He rejects the reading that the forest's white color signifies death by comparison to a corpse's pallor — noting that the text itself calls the whitened forest "delightful, like a flock of cranes," language no one would use of a death-mark — and rejects equally the claim that the jeweled pure land is unrelated to the parinirvāṇa signs because its beauty is produced by the purified minds of the assembled beings rather than by the Buddha himself: the very next line, he points out, states plainly that "by the Buddha's own spiritual power the ground became soft," crediting the transformation to the Buddha, not the crowd. His resolution is that a forest changing its very appearance, and a whole land turning into something like Amitābha's own pure land, serve exactly the same rhetorical purpose as the sound and the light that opened the chapter: this-worldly beings, still hoping their teacher has not really left them, need to be shown unmistakably that departure is imminent — and a landscape transforming under their own eyes accomplishes that more forcefully than any further announcement could.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this entire escalating sequence of refusals as building toward a single deferred payoff still several fascicles away. He gives four distinct reasons the Buddha declines every offering here, none of them a matter of the food or flowers themselves being unworthy: first, accepting any one group's gift now would signal to every other group waiting its turn that further offering is pointless, cutting the assembly's collective generosity short; second, and most pointed, accepting anyone's offering before Cunda's own would rob Cunda specifically of the chance to be the one whose gift the Buddha finally receives, and this sūtra's own theology of the Buddha's constant, eternal body is going to be taught precisely through that acceptance; third, refusal keeps every donor's mind focused on the weight of what they are trying to give, rather than settling into satisfaction once some other party's gift succeeds; and fourth — the reason Huiyuan calls decisive — the Buddha has not yet, at this point in the text, actually taught Cunda the doctrine of the permanent Dharma-body, and accepting an ordinary offering now, before that teaching is given, would let the assembly mistake the acceptance for confirmation of an ordinary, impermanent death rather than what the sūtra is building toward. Every refusal in this fascicle, on Huiyuan's reading, is holding a place open for one specific meal still to come.

On Māra's appearance in this scene — an episode many readers find startling on its own terms, since Māra is elsewhere in the canon this sūtra's assembly's most reliable adversary — Huiyuan reads him here as one of the "inconceivable bodhisattvas" (不思議菩薩) who periodically take on Māra's own form and function as a kind of expedient teaching device, citing this same principle to explain how Maheśvara, similarly, can appear both as an ordinary god paying homage and, per Nāgārjuna's testimony that he elsewhere cites, as a tenth-ground bodhisattva residing among the pure abodes. Nothing about the sūtra's plain language forces this reading, Huiyuan concedes, but it resolves what would otherwise be a jarring inconsistency: a figure defined throughout the tradition by opposition to the Dharma, shown here freeing beings from hell and composing a protective spell explicitly to safeguard the Great Vehicle he supposedly despises.

At that time the auspicious ground between the twin sāla trees, thirty-two yojanas across, was filled entirely with the assembly, without a single empty space remaining.

At that time the places where the bodhisattvas Boundless Body from the four directions and their retinues had taken their seats were pressed so close together they were like the point of an awl, the tip of a needle, a mote of dust — and yet every great bodhisattva from buddha-worlds as numerous as motes of dust throughout the ten directions had come and gathered there, together with the whole assembly of Jambudvīpa, all likewise gathered — save only the venerable Mahākāśyapa and Ānanda, and King Ajātaśatru and his retinue. Even venomous snakes, dung beetles whose gaze alone can kill, vipers and scorpions, and beings who commit the sixteen kinds of evil deed — every one of them had come and gathered there. The dānava spirits and the asuras had all abandoned their malice and given rise to loving-kindness, regarding one another as father, mother, elder sister, younger sister; and every being throughout the trichiliocosm turned toward every other with this same loving-kindness — every being, that is, except the icchantika.

At that time, throughout the trichiliocosm, by the Buddha's own spiritual power, the ground everywhere became soft, free of hills, dust, gravel, thorns, and poisonous plants, adorned throughout with every kind of jewel, just like Amitāyus's own Pure Land of Ultimate Bliss in the west. At that moment the whole assembly could see every buddha-world throughout the ten directions, as numerous as motes of dust, as clearly as seeing their own reflection in a bright mirror, and saw that every one of these buddha-lands appeared in just this same way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This single clause — "every being throughout the trichiliocosm turned toward every other with this same loving-kindness, except the icchantika" — is the first appearance in this sūtra of a term that will become one of its most consequential and most contested. An icchantika, in the tradition this sūtra inherits, is a being whose roots of goodness are said to be so thoroughly severed that liberation is permanently closed to them; here, at the very threshold of the text, that exclusion is total enough to reach even venomous snakes and beings guilty of the most severe transgressions, all of whom are folded into the assembly's universal tenderness, while the icchantika alone stands outside it. Readers who know where this sūtra is headed will recognize the tension this single word is quietly setting up: it is precisely this text, in the fascicles ahead treating the tathāgatagarbha and buddha-nature, that will become the classical source for the claim — associated above all with the fifth-century monk Zhu Daosheng, who defended it against the consensus of his own contemporaries before a more complete version of this sūtra vindicated him — that even the icchantika possesses buddha-nature and will one day attain buddhahood. Bao Liang's sixth-century compilation of commentary on this sūtra, the Compilation of Explanations of the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra (T1763), preserves some of the only surviving traces of Daosheng's own reasoning on this very point, and this collection will return to it when the text itself takes up the question directly.

The Buddha's Light Returns

Chinese Source

爾時如來面門所出五色光明，其光明曜覆諸大會，令彼身光，悉不復現；所應作已，還從口入。時諸天人及諸會眾，阿修羅等，見佛光明，還從口入，皆大恐怖，身毛為竪，復作是言：「如來光明出已還入，非無因緣，必於十方所作已辦，將是最後涅槃之相。何期苦哉，何期苦哉！如何？世尊一旦捨離四無量心，不受人天所奉供養，聖慧日光從今永滅，無上法船於斯沈沒。嗚呼痛哉，世間大苦。」舉手搥胸，悲號啼哭，支節戰動，不能自持，身諸毛孔流血灑地。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the five-colored light streaming from the Tathāgata's face shone so brightly that it eclipsed the light of every being in the whole assembly, until, its work accomplished, it withdrew back into his mouth. When the devas, humans, and every being in the assembly, the asuras among them, saw the Buddha's light return into his mouth, they were seized with great terror, the hair standing up on their bodies, and they said to one another: "The Tathāgata's light, having gone out, has now returned — this cannot be without a reason. He must have accomplished everything he needed to throughout the ten directions, and this must be the last sign before his final parinirvāṇa. How bitter this is, how bitter! What can we do? The World-Honored One is about to cast aside the four immeasurable minds all at once, and will no longer accept the offerings of humans and gods; the sun of holy wisdom is about to be extinguished forever, and the peerless ship of the Dharma is about to sink beneath the waves. Ah, how painful — this is the great suffering of the world." Raising their hands, they beat their breasts, wailing and weeping aloud, their limbs shaking so violently they could not steady themselves, and blood flowed from every pore of their bodies, spattering the ground.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

This is the sixth and last of the signs Huiyuan traces across the whole of this opening scene, and he pairs it deliberately with the very first: the great voice at the chapter's opening told the assembly when the Buddha would die and summoned them to come; this closing sign, the light's withdrawal, tells them the summons is now complete and presses the same urgency home a final time. Huiyuan reads the assembly's terror here as itself diagnostic — despite everything already shown them, the earthquake, the transfigured forest, the pure land, the arrival of bodhisattvas from every direction, this frightened crowd still has not fully absorbed that the Buddha is actually about to die, and needs one unmistakable, wordless sign to finally drive the fact home before they can receive, with anything like readiness, the teaching this sūtra exists to deliver. That teaching — beginning with the offering this whole fascicle's chain of refusals has been holding open a place for — is where Part 2 will pick up the story.
