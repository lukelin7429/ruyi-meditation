Seven Kinds of Beings in the River

The great river simile from the Lion's Roar chapter returns, but turned to a new purpose: not the general shape of saṃsāra, but exactly how a real audience receives the very sūtra now being spoken — who sinks on first hearing that the Tathāgata is permanent and every being has buddha-nature, who rises briefly on faith and sinks again for reasons the text catalogues in exhausting, almost clinical detail, and who never sinks at all. From there the fascicle turns technical: what the warmth stage actually is, why a stream-enterer is defined by severing only three fetters out of countless afflictions, the full taxonomy of the four fruits, and a precise account of what does and does not count as slandering the three jewels when speaking of an icchantika's own buddha-nature.

Every Pair, Present and Absent

Chinese Source

「◎善男子！如來復有隨自意語。如來佛性則有二種：一者有、二者無。有者，所謂三十二相、八十種好、十力、四無所畏、三念處、大慈大悲、首楞嚴等無量三昧、金剛等無量三昧、方便等無量三昧、五智印等無量三昧，是名為有。無者，所謂如來、過去諸善、不善、無記業因果報、煩惱、五陰、十二因緣，是名為無。善男子！如有、無，善、不善，有漏、無漏，世間、非世間，聖、非聖，有為、無為，實、不實，寂靜、非寂靜，諍、非諍，界、非界，煩惱、非煩惱，取、非取，受記、非受記，有、非有，三世、非三世，時、非時，常、無常，我、無我，樂、無樂，淨、無淨，色受想行識、非色受想行識，內入、非內入，外入、非外入，十二因緣、非十二因緣，是名如來佛性有無。乃至一闡提佛性有無，亦復如是。 「善男子！我雖說言一切眾生悉有佛性，眾生不解佛如是等隨自意語。善男子！如是語者，後身菩薩尚不能解，況於二乘、其餘菩薩？善男子！我往一時在耆闍崛山，與彌勒菩薩共論世諦。舍利弗等五百聲聞，於是事中都不識知，何況出世第一義諦？ 「善男子！或有佛性一闡提有，善根人無。或有佛性善根人有，一闡提無。或有佛性，二人俱有。或有佛性，二人俱無。善男子！我諸弟子若解如是四句義者，不應難言，一闡提人定有佛性、定無佛性。若言眾生悉有佛性，是名如來隨自意語。如來如是隨自意語，眾生云何一向作解？」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, the Tathāgata has, further, this teaching according to his own intent. The Tathāgata's own buddha-nature is of two kinds: one that exists, and one that does not. What exists is the thirty-two marks, the eighty minor characteristics, the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, the three foundations of mindfulness, great loving-kindness and great compassion, the countless samādhis such as the Śūraṅgama, the countless vajra samādhis, the countless samādhis of skillful means, and the countless samādhis such as the five wisdom-seals — this is called what exists. What does not exist is the Tathāgata's own past wholesome, unwholesome, and morally neutral karma, its causes and fruits, affliction, the five aggregates, and the twelve links — this is called what does not exist. Good son, exactly as with existent and non-existent, so too with wholesome and unwholesome, defiled and undefiled, worldly and beyond the world, noble and not noble, conditioned and unconditioned, real and unreal, still and not still, contentious and not contentious, bounded and unbounded, afflicted and unafflicted, grasped and not grasped, prophesied and not prophesied, being and non-being, within the three times and outside the three times, temporal and not temporal, permanent and impermanent, self and no-self, bliss and its absence, purity and its absence, the five aggregates and what is not the five aggregates, the internal sense-bases and what is not the internal sense-bases, the external sense-bases and what is not the external sense-bases, and the twelve links and what is not the twelve links — this is what is meant by the existence and non-existence of the Tathāgata's own buddha-nature. And, all the way through to an icchantika's own buddha-nature, existence and non-existence are exactly the same. "Good son, though I teach that every being entirely possesses buddha-nature, beings do not understand that the Buddha's teaching of this kind is spoken according to his own intent. Good son, teaching of this kind, even a bodhisattva in his final body is unable to fully understand — how much less, then, the two vehicles or the remaining bodhisattvas? Good son, I was once, at Vulture Peak, discussing conventional truth together with the bodhisattva Maitreya, and Śāriputra and the five hundred śrāvakas present had no understanding whatsoever of this matter — how much less, then, the ultimate truth that transcends the world? "Good son, there is a buddha-nature the icchantika possesses and the person of wholesome roots lacks; there is a buddha-nature the person of wholesome roots possesses and the icchantika lacks; there is a buddha-nature both possess; and there is a buddha-nature neither possesses. Good son, should my own disciples understand the meaning of these four statements, they ought never to object, flatly, that the icchantika definitely possesses buddha-nature or definitely lacks it. To say that every being entirely possesses buddha-nature is itself teaching according to the Tathāgata's own intent — and, the Tathāgata teaching according to his own intent in this way, how could beings possibly settle on a single, fixed understanding of it?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

This continues directly from Part 35's closing "teaching according to my own intent," and Huiyuan reads the long list of paired opposites as making one point through sheer accumulation: buddha-nature cannot be pinned to either side of any single dichotomy, since the Tathāgata's own buddha-nature genuinely has a "has" side (the thirty-two marks, the ten powers, every visible perfection) and a "does not have" side (any past karma, affliction, or the twelve links themselves) at once — and every other pair in the list works the identical way (T1764, lines 21403–21431). The four-cornered icchantika/wholesome-person schema is the same point applied to the hardest single case: since "buddha-nature" names several genuinely distinct things (as cause, as fruit, as the not-yet-severed capacity, as the already-manifest quality), it is simply false that any flat yes/no answer about "the icchantika's buddha-nature" could ever be adequate — which is exactly why Kāśyapa is warned, in the fascicle's very next lines, not to settle on one understanding.

Seven Kinds of Beings in the River, and What "Always Sinking" Really Means

Chinese Source

「善男子！如恒河中有七眾生：一者常沒、二者暫出還沒、三者出已則住、四者出已遍觀四方、五者遍觀已行、六者行已復住、七者水陸俱行。言常沒者，所謂大魚，受大惡業，身重處深，是故常沒。暫出還沒者，如是大魚，受惡業故，身重處淺，暫見光明，因光故出，重故還沒。出已住者，謂坻彌魚，身處淺水，樂見光明，故出已住。遍觀方者，所謂䱜魚，為求食故，遍觀四方，是故觀方。觀已行者，謂是䱜魚，遙見餘物，謂是可食，疾行趣之，故觀已行。行已復住者，是魚趣已，既得可食，即便停住，故行已復住。水陸俱行者，即是龜也。 「善男子！如是微妙大涅槃河，其中亦有七種眾生，從初常沒乃至第七，或入或出。所言沒者，有人聞是大涅槃經，如來常住、無有變易、常樂我淨、終不畢竟入於涅槃；一切眾生悉有佛性，一闡提人謗方等經作五逆罪犯四重禁，必當得成菩提之道；須陀洹人、斯陀含人、阿那含人、阿羅漢人、辟支佛等必當得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。聞是語已，生不信心，即作是念，作是念已，便作是言：『是涅槃典，即外道書，非是佛經。』是人爾時遠離善友，不聞正法，雖時得聞，不能思惟，雖復思惟，不思惟善，不思善故，如惡法住。惡法住者，則有六種：一者惡、二者無善、三者污法、四者增有、五者惱熱、六者受惡果。是名為沒。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, in the Ganges river there are seven kinds of beings: first, those that always sink; second, those that briefly rise and sink again; third, those that rise and then remain; fourth, those that rise and survey the four directions; fifth, those that, having surveyed, then move; sixth, those that, having moved, then remain once more; and seventh, those that travel equally by water and by land. Those that always sink are the great fish that, bearing great evil karma, are heavy of body and dwell in the depths, and so always sink. Those that briefly rise and sink again are these same great fish, who, bearing evil karma, are heavy of body yet dwell in shallower water, briefly glimpsing the light, rising on account of this light, and, being heavy, sinking once more. Those that rise and then remain are the katimira fish, whose bodies dwell in shallow water, delighting in the sight of light, and so, having risen, they remain. Those that survey the four directions are the rohita fish, who, seeking food, survey the four directions, and so are called those that survey the directions. Those that, having surveyed, then move are these same rohita fish, who, spotting something else in the distance and taking it for food, swiftly move toward it, and so are called those that, having surveyed, then move. Those that, having moved, then remain once more are these same fish who, having reached what they sought and obtained their food, then come to rest, and so are called those that, having moved, then remain once more. Those that travel equally by water and by land are simply the turtle. "Good son, in this same subtle and wondrous river of the great nirvāṇa, there are likewise seven kinds of beings, from the first that always sinks through to the seventh, some entering, some emerging. As for what is called 'sinking': there is a person who, hearing this great nirvāṇa sūtra teach that the Tathāgata abides forever without any change at all, that he is permanence, bliss, self, and purity, and never, in the end, finally enters nirvāṇa; that every being entirely possesses buddha-nature, so that even an icchantika who slanders the Vaipulya sūtras, commits the five heinous crimes, and violates the four gravest precepts is certain, in the end, to attain the path to awakening; and that the stream-enterer, the once-returner, the non-returner, the arhat, and the pratyekabuddha are all certain, in the end, to attain supreme, perfect awakening — hearing teaching of this kind, this person gives rise to a mind of disbelief, and, having given rise to this thought, then says: 'This nirvāṇa scripture is simply a non-Buddhist text, not a genuine Buddhist sūtra at all.' This person, at that very moment, departs from good spiritual friends, and no longer hears the true Dharma; though he may, at times, still hear it, he is unable to reflect upon it; and, though he may reflect upon it, he does not reflect wholesomely — and, failing to reflect wholesomely, he comes to abide in an unwholesome state. This unwholesome abiding is, in turn, of six kinds: first, evil; second, absence of good; third, defiling the Dharma; fourth, increasing states of becoming; fifth, affliction and heat; and sixth, receiving an evil fruit. This is called sinking."

「何故名沒？無善心故、常行惡故、不修對治故，是名為沒。所言惡者，聖人呵責故、心生怖畏故、善人遠離故、不益眾生故，是名為惡。言無善者，能生無量惡果報故、常為無明所纏繞故、樂與惡人為等侶故、無有修善諸方便故、其心顛倒常錯謬故，是名無善。言污法者，常污身口故、污淨眾生故、增不善業故、遠離善法故，是名污法。言增有者，如上三人所行之法，能增地獄、畜生、餓鬼，不能修習解脫之法，身、口、意業，不厭諸有，是名增有。言惱熱者，是人具行如上四事，能令身、心二事惱熱，遠離寂靜，則名為熱，受地獄報故名為熱，燒諸眾生故名為熱，燒諸善法故名為熱。善男子！信心、清涼，是人不具，是故名熱。言受惡果者，是人具足行上五事，死墮地獄、餓鬼、畜生。善男子！有三惡事復名惡果，一者煩惱惡、二者業惡、三者報惡，是名受惡果報。善男子！是人具足如上六事，能斷善根、作五逆罪、能犯四重、能謗三寶、用僧鬘物、能作種種非法之事，是因緣故，沈沒在於阿鼻地獄，所受身形縱廣八萬四千由旬，是人身、口、心業重故，不能得出。何以故？其心不能生善法故。雖有無量諸佛出世，不聞不見，是故名常沒，如恒河中大魚。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Why is this called sinking? Because there is no wholesome mind, because unwholesome conduct is constant, and because no antidote is ever cultivated — this is called sinking. As for what is called evil: because the noble ones rebuke it, because it gives rise to terror in the mind, because good people keep their distance from it, and because it never benefits any being — this is called evil. As for what is called absence of good: because it is able to give rise to boundless evil fruits, because it is forever entangled by ignorance, because it delights in the company of evil people, because it possesses no skillful means for cultivating good, and because its mind is forever inverted and mistaken — this is called absence of good. As for what is called defiling the Dharma: because it constantly defiles body and speech, defiles beings who would otherwise be pure, increases unwholesome karma, and departs from every wholesome dharma — this is called defiling the Dharma. As for what is called increasing states of becoming: because the dharma practiced by these three kinds of people above is able only to increase hell, the animal realm, and the realm of hungry ghosts, is never able to cultivate the dharma of liberation, and, in body, speech, and mind, never grows weary of any state of becoming — this is called increasing states of becoming. As for what is called affliction and heat: because this person, fully practicing these four things above, brings affliction and heat upon both body and mind, departing from stillness — it is called heat because it yields the fruit of hell, heat because it burns every being, and heat because it burns every wholesome dharma; good son, faith and its own cooling relief this person entirely lacks, and it is for this reason that it is called heat. As for what is called receiving an evil fruit: because this person, fully practicing these five things above, at death falls into hell, into the realm of hungry ghosts, or into the animal realm. Good son, there are, further, three evil things also called an evil fruit: evil affliction, evil karma, and evil retribution — this is called receiving the fruit of evil. Good son, this person, fully possessing these six things above, is able to sever his own wholesome roots, commit the five heinous crimes, violate the four gravest precepts, slander the three jewels, misappropriate the saṅgha's own garlands and offerings, and commit every kind of unlawful act — and it is on account of exactly this that he sinks down into the Avīci hell, receiving there a body eighty-four thousand yojanas in breadth and length; and, because this person's own bodily, verbal, and mental karma is so heavy, he is unable ever to emerge from it. Why is this? Because his mind is unable to give rise to any wholesome dharma at all. Though countless buddhas may appear in the world, he neither hears of them nor sees them — and it is for this very reason that he is called one who always sinks, exactly like the great fish in the Ganges river."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan lays out three distinct ways the sūtra's own text applies this sevenfold river simile, which is worth knowing before reading what follows: a "separate practice" reading, where each of the three vehicles (śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, bodhisattva) and the Buddha each have their own complete set of seven; a "shared practice" reading, where ordinary beings of both vehicles are lumped together as "always sinking" and the six higher stages are shared across arhat, pratyekabuddha, bodhisattva, and Buddha; and the "pervasive practice" reading used here, which maps eighteen distinct stages — from the icchantika through the five expedient-stage practices, the four fruits, the pratyekabuddha, the bodhisattva, and the Buddha himself — onto these same seven categories, so that several adjacent stages can share a single category like "abiding" or "surveying the directions" (T1764, lines 21441–21476). This fascicle's own explicit application (from the icchantika reader of this very sūtra who disbelieves it, through the "briefly rising" and "abiding" readers to come) is doing the "pervasive practice" reading, which is why Huiyuan reads it as continuous with — not a repetition of — the magnet and elephant similes already used for buddha-nature in Part 32: there the point was that buddha-nature's presence never guarantees present attainment; here the same architecture is turned specifically onto how a listener actually receives this sūtra's own most difficult claims.

Briefly Rising, Then Sinking Again

Chinese Source

「善男子！我雖復說一闡提等，名為常沒。復有常沒，非一闡提。何者是耶？如人為有，修施、戒、善，是名常沒。善男子！有四善事獲得惡果。何等為四？一者為勝他故讀誦經典、二者為利養故受持禁戒、三者為他屬故而行布施、四者為於非想非非想處故繫念思惟，是四善事得惡果報。若人修習如是四事，是名沒已還出，出已還沒。何故名沒？樂三有故。何故名出？以見明故，明者即是聞戒、施、定。何故還沒？增長邪見生憍慢故。是故我於經中說偈： 「『若有眾生樂諸有，　為有造作善惡業， 是人迷失涅槃道，　是名暫出還復沒。 行於黑闇生死海，　雖得解脫雜煩惱， 是人還受惡果報，　是名暫出還復沒。』 「善男子！如彼大魚，因見光故，暫得出水。其身重故，還復沈沒。如上二人亦復如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, though I have taught that the icchantika is called one who always sinks, there is, further, another who always sinks who is not an icchantika at all. Who is this? It is a person who, for the sake of continued becoming, cultivates giving, precepts, and other wholesome deeds — this, too, is called always sinking. Good son, there are four wholesome deeds that yield an evil fruit. What are these four? First, reciting the scriptures for the sake of surpassing others; second, upholding the precepts for the sake of one's own material gain; third, practicing giving for the sake of a dependent's benefit; and fourth, focusing the mind in meditation for the sake of reaching the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — these four wholesome deeds yield an evil fruit. Should a person cultivate these four things, this is called sinking, then emerging, and, having emerged, sinking once more. Why is it called sinking? Because it delights in the three states of becoming. Why is it called emerging? Because it comes to see the light — this light being simply the hearing of the precepts, of giving, and of concentration. Why does it sink once more? Because it increases wrong view and gives rise to arrogance. It is for this very reason that I taught, in the sūtras, this verse: 'Should any being delight in every state of becoming, And, for the sake of becoming, perform both wholesome and unwholesome karma, This person has lost his way, straying from the path to nirvāṇa — This is called briefly rising, then sinking once more. Traveling through the dark ocean of birth and death, Though attaining a liberation still mixed with affliction, This person still receives an evil fruit in the end — This is called briefly rising, then sinking once more.' "Good son, it is exactly like that great fish, who, on account of glimpsing the light, briefly manages to rise from the water, yet, being heavy of body, sinks back down once more — the two kinds of people described above are exactly the same."

「善男子！或復有人樂著三有，是名為沒。得聞如是大涅槃經生於信心，是名為出。何因緣故名之為出？聞是經已，遠離惡法，修習善法，是名為出。是人雖信，亦不具足。何因緣故信不具足？是人雖信大般涅槃、常樂我淨，言如來身無常、無我、無樂、無淨。如來則有二種涅槃：一者有為、二者無為。有為涅槃無常樂我淨，無為涅槃有常樂我淨。雖信佛性是眾生有，不必一切皆悉有之，是故名為信不具足。善男子！信有二種：一者信、二者求。如是之人雖復有信，不能推求，是故名為信不具足。信復有二：一從聞生、二從思生。是人信心從聞而生，不從思生，是故名為信不具足。復有二種：一信有道、二信得者。是人信心唯信有道，都不信有得道之人，是故名為信不具足。復有二種：一者信正、二者信邪。言有因果、有佛法僧，是名信正。言無因果、三寶性異、信諸邪語、富蘭那等，是名信邪。是人雖信佛、法、僧寶，不信三寶同一性相。雖信因果，不信得者。是故名為信不具足。是人成就不具足信，所受禁戒亦不具足。何因緣故名不具足？因不具故，所得禁戒亦不具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, there is, further, a person who delights in and clings to the three states of becoming — this is called sinking. Hearing this great nirvāṇa sūtra and giving rise to a mind of faith — this is called emerging. For what reason is this called emerging? Because, having heard this sūtra, he departs from unwholesome dharmas and cultivates wholesome dharmas — this is called emerging. Though this person has faith, it is not yet complete. For what reason is his faith incomplete? Though this person has faith in the great nirvāṇa's own permanence, bliss, self, and purity, he still says that the Tathāgata's own body is impermanent, without self, without bliss, and without purity. The Tathāgata's own nirvāṇa is, in truth, of two kinds: the conditioned, and the unconditioned. The conditioned nirvāṇa lacks permanence, bliss, self, and purity; the unconditioned nirvāṇa possesses permanence, bliss, self, and purity. Though this person believes that buddha-nature is possessed by beings, he does not believe that it is necessarily possessed by every single one of them — and it is for this reason that his faith is called incomplete. Good son, faith is of two kinds: belief, and inquiry. A person of this kind, though he has belief, is unable to inquire further — and it is for this reason that his faith is called incomplete. Faith is, further, of two kinds: that born from hearing, and that born from reflection. This person's own faith is born from hearing alone, not from reflection — and it is for this reason that his faith is called incomplete. There are, further, two kinds: believing that the path exists, and believing in those who have attained it. This person's own faith believes only that the path exists, without believing at all in anyone who has actually attained it — and it is for this reason that his faith is called incomplete. There are, further, two kinds: correct faith, and mistaken faith. Holding that there is cause and fruit, and that the Buddha, the Dharma, and the saṅgha exist — this is correct faith. Holding that there is no cause and fruit, that the three jewels each have a different nature, and believing every kind of mistaken teaching, such as that of Pūraṇa Kāśyapa — this is mistaken faith. This person, though he has faith in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the saṅgha as jewels, does not believe that the three jewels share one and the same nature; and, though he has faith in cause and fruit, he does not believe in those who have actually attained it — and it is for this reason that his faith is called incomplete. This person, having only this incomplete faith, likewise receives the precepts incompletely. For what reason is this called incomplete? Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's structural point here is worth making explicit: this whole section describes a single person's trajectory through five compounding layers of incompleteness — faith, precept, learning, giving, and wisdom, each one's incompleteness explained as a direct consequence of the incompleteness just before it (T1764, lines 21519–21565). The listed incompletenesses in faith are unusually precise: believing in the unconditioned nirvāṇa's own permanence while still doubting the Tathāgata's own body shares it, believing buddha-nature exists without believing it is universal, believing only from having heard rather than from having inquired — each is a real, subtler failure mode than flat disbelief, which is exactly Huiyuan's point in placing this whole category between "always sinking" and "abiding": this is a believer, not a denier, who nonetheless keeps sinking back down. The long named list closing the section — Devadatta, Kokālika, and Sunakṣatra chief among them, familiar from Parts 30 and 33 — gives the abstract taxonomy real faces, exactly as the sūtra's own method throughout Parts 27–35 keeps pairing doctrine with named case studies rather than leaving it purely conceptual.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan raises and answers a real puzzle here: Śāriputra and the other arhats named in this list already belong, elsewhere in the sūtra, to the seventh and highest category, "traveling equally by water and by land" — so why are they placed here under mere "abiding," the third of seven? His answer is that the sūtra is naming them by what first made them abide, not by where they eventually arrived: in the small-vehicle framework they possess the complete range of practice from first faith through final attainment, so they can rightly be called "abiding," "surveying the directions," or "traveling equally by water and by land" depending on which stage of their own development is being pointed to (T1764, lines 21585–21592). It is a small but important reminder that this whole sevenfold taxonomy tracks a trajectory, not a fixed label — the same person can correctly be named by several of its categories at once, depending on which moment in their own practice is under discussion.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan resolves the seeming conflict at the heart of this section — a conditioned dharma somehow marking the unconditioned path — through the horse-and-rider simile taken quite literally: the warmth-mind is "cherished" the way a rider loves his horse (its own pleasant, generative quality gives rise to further birth), yet it is also "driven" the way a rider goads a horse forward (weariness with that very birth spurs further contemplation), so a single conditioned state does double duty as both a thing still caught in becoming and the very thing propelling the practitioner out of becoming (T1764, lines 21680–21694). He also carefully distinguishes the "warmth" that composes every living being together with lifespan and consciousness — mere bodily heat, present even in an icchantika — from this technical "warmth stage," which requires the specific cultivation of the sixteen practices and belongs only to the form realm, never to the desire realm, and, among form-realm practitioners, only to those following the Buddhist path rather than the six practices of non-Buddhist ascetics (lines 21636–21651). The seventy-three-fold count of warmth-stage practitioners works exactly like the "not-cultivating body/precept/mind/wisdom" ladders from Part 31 and the buddha-nature-by-stage ladder from Part 35: ten grades in the desire realm (one entirely unbound by any prior severing, plus nine further grades corresponding to how much of that realm's affliction has already been cut), repeated across each of the seven remaining stages up through the sphere of nothingness, for seventy-three in total (lines 21696–21706).

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's gloss on "the king comes, the king goes" is precise: the three fetters are singled out not because they exhaust what a stream-enterer severs, but because of three specific properties — they arise constantly in every being and are hard to recognize, they are the direct root-cause from which every other affliction grows, and each is the specific adversary of one of the three trainings (self-view opposing precept, since disbelief in karmic consequence licenses indulgence; mistaking a wrong cause for the true cause opposing concentration, since clinging to ascetic non-Buddhist practices as the path blocks genuine meditative cultivation; and doubt opposing wisdom, since doubt itself prevents right understanding from ever taking hold) (T1764, lines 21763–21778). On the "old and guest" naming convention, Huiyuan adds a further subtlety worth preserving: within the technical apparatus of the four fruits, a practitioner who advances through the levels in strict sequence is properly called "stream-enterer" only upon completing the sixteenth mind-moment of the path of seeing, while one who has already severed most of the desire realm's affliction beforehand can leap straight past the stream-enterer designation to "once-returner" or "non-returner" at that very same sixteenth moment — meaning the name "stream-enterer" tracks a structural position (having just completed one's very first fruit) rather than a fixed quantity of severed affliction (lines 21840–21920). The Śroṇa story closing this section is not incidental: a demon impersonating the Buddha's own image and offering a plausible-sounding but structurally nonsensical alternative teaching (five truths where there should be four, mismatched aggregate and element counts) is exactly the kind of test the "holding firmly" quality of the rohita fish is meant to withstand — recognizing a counterfeit not by authority but by checking whether the teaching's own internal structure actually holds together.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's answer to why the first fruit alone is called "stream-enterer" turns on a precise technical distinction the sūtra states but does not fully unpack: within the sixteen practices, the "shared" set is observed twice over across the sixteen registers of the four truths at both the desire and higher realms (thirty-two total observations), while the "unshared" set observes each register only once, in an increasingly condensed sequence culminating in a single moment of attention at the summit stage — a practitioner moving from ordinary being to noble one relinquishes the doubled, shared version of contemplation for the single, unshared version, and relinquishes the eight "approaching" wisdoms (observed but not yet realized) for the eight "attained" wisdoms actually realized at the completion of the path of seeing (T1764, lines 21867–21904). A non-returner who arrives by the direct, sequential route never makes this particular exchange in the same way stream-entry does, which is why the name belongs to the first fruit specifically. Huiyuan also flags, without fully resolving, a genuine puzzle in the "in-between" nirvāṇa's four-minds count: two of the non-returner's four possible mental states qualify as nirvāṇa and two do not, which is precisely why this variety needs the qualifier "in between" rather than being classed simply as ordinary parinirvāṇa (line 21536-ff.) — a reminder that even Huiyuan's own systematic commentary sometimes only restates the sūtra's distinction rather than fully deriving it.

The monk who demanded a jewel-adorned cell before he could finally attain arhatship is a small, almost comic story doing real doctrinal work: it answers, in narrative form, the abstract question of why intermediate-state nirvāṇa's own "sharp faculty" does not guarantee present-life attainment — sharpness of faculty is necessary but not sufficient, since the supporting conditions of the four great elements' own health and basic material sufficiency must also be in place, exactly as the text states just before telling the story. On why the desire realm alone has intermediate-state nirvāṇa, Huiyuan's gloss on "internal and external" causes clarifies what could otherwise look like an odd asymmetry: it is not that the desire realm's affliction is somehow worse, but that it alone has an external component (external objects of desire and craving for form encountered through the senses) in addition to the internal component every realm shares — and it is precisely this added, external layer that the intermediate state gives a practitioner one further opportunity to reject, immediately after leaving the body, before form-realm rebirth would otherwise settle the matter.

「善男子！到彼岸者，喻阿羅漢、辟支佛、菩薩、佛，猶如神龜水陸俱行。何因緣故，喻之以龜？善藏五根故。阿羅漢乃至諸佛亦復如是，善覆五根，是故喻龜。言水陸者，水喻世間，陸喻出世。是諸聖等亦復如是，能觀一切惡煩惱故，到於彼岸，是故喻以水陸俱行。 「善男子！如恒河中七種眾生，雖有魚龜之名，不離於水。如是微妙大涅槃中，從一闡提上至諸佛雖有異名，然亦不離於佛性水。善男子！是七眾生，若善法、若不善法，若方便道、若解脫道、若次第道，若因、若果，悉是佛性，是名如來隨自意語。」 迦葉菩薩言：「世尊！若有因則有果，若無因則無果。涅槃名果，常故無因。若無因者云何名果？而是涅槃，亦名沙門，名沙門果。云何沙門？云何沙門果？」 「善男子！一切世間有七種果：一者方便果、二者報恩果、三者親近果、四者餘殘果、五者平等果、六者果報果、七者遠離果。方便果者，如世間人秋多收穀，咸相謂言得方便果。方便果者，名業行果。如是果者有二種因：一者近因、二者遠因。近因者，所謂種子。遠因者，謂水、糞、人功。是名方便果。 「報恩果者，如世間人供養父母，父母咸言：『我今已得恩養之果。』子能報恩名之為果。如是果者因亦二種：一者近因、二者遠因。近因者，即是父母過去純善之業。遠因者，即是所生孝子。是名報恩果。 「親近果者，譬如有人親近善友，或得須陀洹果至阿羅漢果，是人唱言：『我今已得親近果報。』如是果者，因有二種：一者近因、二者遠因。近者信心，遠者善友。是名親近果。 「餘殘果者，如因不殺得第三身，延年益壽，是名殘果。如是果者有二種因：一者近因、二者遠因。近者，即是身、口、意淨。遠者，即是延年益壽。是名殘果。 「平等果者，謂世界器。如是果者亦二種因：一者近因、二者遠因。近因者，所謂眾生修十善業。遠因者，所謂三災。是名平等果。 「果報果者，如人獲得清淨身已，修身、口、意清淨三業，是人便說：『我得報果。』如是果者因有二種：一者近因、二者遠因。近因者，所謂現在身、口、意淨。遠因者，所謂過去身、口、意淨。是名果報果。 「遠離果者，即是涅槃，離諸煩惱一切善業。是涅槃因復有二種：一者近因、二者遠因。近因者，即是三解脫門。遠因者，即無量世所修善法。 「善男子！如世間法，或說生因、或說了因；出世之法亦復如是，亦說生因、亦說了因。善男子！三解脫門、三十七品，能為一切煩惱作不生生因，亦為涅槃而作了因。善男子！遠離煩惱，則得了了見於涅槃，是故涅槃唯有了因，無有生因。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, reaching the far shore is a simile for the arhat, the pratyekabuddha, the bodhisattva, and the Buddha, exactly like the sacred turtle traveling equally by water and by land. For what reason is this compared to the turtle? Because it well conceals its own five limbs. The arhat, and, all the way through, every buddha, is exactly the same, well concealing his own five faculties — and it is for this reason that he is compared to the turtle. As for water and land: water represents the mundane world, land represents what transcends it — and these very noble ones are exactly the same, able to contemplate every evil affliction and thereby reach the far shore, and it is for this reason that they are compared to traveling equally by water and by land. "Good son, exactly as the seven kinds of beings in the Ganges, though bearing the different names of fish and turtle, never depart from the water — so too, in this subtle and wondrous great nirvāṇa, from the icchantika up through every buddha, though bearing different names, none ever depart from the water of buddha-nature. Good son, of these seven kinds of beings, whether wholesome dharma or unwholesome dharma, whether the path of skillful means, the path of liberation, or the path of sequential progress, whether cause or fruit — all of it is buddha-nature. This is called teaching according to the Tathāgata's own intent." Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said: "World-Honored One, where there is a cause, there is a fruit; where there is no cause, there is no fruit. Nirvāṇa is called a fruit, yet, being permanent, it has no cause — how, then, if it has no cause, can it possibly be called a fruit? And this very nirvāṇa is also called śramaṇa-hood, and called the fruit of the śramaṇa — what, then, is śramaṇa-hood, and what is the fruit of the śramaṇa?" "Good son, in the world generally there are seven kinds of fruit: first, the fruit of skillful means; second, the fruit of gratitude; third, the fruit of intimacy; fourth, the residual fruit; fifth, the fruit of equality; sixth, the fruit of retribution; and seventh, the fruit of departure. The fruit of skillful means is exactly like when people in the world, harvesting an abundant crop in autumn, all say together that they have obtained the fruit of skillful means. The fruit of skillful means is called the fruit of karmic action. A fruit of this kind has two kinds of cause: the near cause, and the far cause. The near cause is simply the seed itself. The far cause is water, fertilizer, and human labor. This is called the fruit of skillful means. "The fruit of gratitude is exactly like when people in the world make offerings to their own father and mother, and the father and mother together say: 'We have now obtained the fruit of our own loving care.' A child's ability to repay this kindness is called this very fruit. A fruit of this kind likewise has two kinds of cause: the near cause is simply the father and mother's own past, purely wholesome karma; the far cause is simply the filial child thereby produced. This is called the fruit of gratitude. "The fruit of intimacy is exactly like when a person, drawing close to a good spiritual friend, attains anything from the fruit of stream-entry up through the fruit of arhatship, and this person then proclaims: 'I have now obtained the fruit of this very intimacy.' A fruit of this kind has two kinds of cause: the near cause is faith itself; the far cause is the good friend. This is called the fruit of intimacy. "The residual fruit is exactly like when, through the cause of not killing, one obtains a third rebirth, prolonging one's own years and increasing one's own lifespan — this is called the residual fruit. A fruit of this kind has two kinds of cause: the near cause is simply the purity of one's own body, speech, and mind; the far cause is simply the prolonging of one's own years and the increasing of one's own lifespan. This is called the residual fruit. "The fruit of equality refers to the very vessel of the world itself. A fruit of this kind, likewise, has two kinds of cause: the near cause is simply beings' own cultivation of the ten wholesome deeds; the far cause is simply the three great calamities. This is called the fruit of equality. "The fruit of retribution is exactly like when a person, having attained a pure body, cultivates the three pure karmas of body, speech, and mind, and this person then says: 'I have obtained the fruit of retribution.' A fruit of this kind has two kinds of cause: the near cause is simply the present purity of body, speech, and mind; the far cause is simply the past purity of body, speech, and mind. This is called the fruit of retribution. "The fruit of departure is simply nirvāṇa itself, departed from every affliction and every wholesome karma alike. This nirvāṇa's own cause is, further, of two kinds: the near cause, and the far cause. The near cause is simply the three gates of liberation. The far cause is simply the wholesome dharmas cultivated across countless ages. "Good son, exactly as, in the ways of the world, one speaks of both a generative cause and a revealing cause, so too, in the dharmas that transcend the world, one speaks of both a generative cause and a revealing cause. Good son, the three gates of liberation and the thirty-seven factors of the path are able to serve as the generative cause preventing every affliction from arising, and are also able to serve as the revealing cause of nirvāṇa itself. Good son, once affliction is departed from entirely, one then attains a perfectly clear vision of nirvāṇa — and it is for this reason that nirvāṇa has only a revealing cause, and never a generative cause."

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, as you have asked — what, exactly, is śramaṇa-hood, and what is the fruit of the śramaṇa? Good son, śramaṇa-hood is simply the noble eightfold path itself; the fruit of the śramaṇa is the complete and final severing, through this very path, of every craving, hatred, delusion, and the rest — this is called śramaṇa-hood, and the fruit of the śramaṇa." Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said: "World-Honored One, for what reason is the noble eightfold path called śramaṇa-hood?" "Good son, in worldly speech, 'śramaṇa' [沙門] means 'lack,' and 'na' [那] means 'path' — a path of this kind severs every lack, and severs every path leading to it. It is on account of this very reasoning that the noble eightfold path is called śramaṇa-hood; and, since a fruit is obtained through this very path, it is called the fruit of the śramaṇa. Good son, further, śramaṇa-hood is exactly like when people in the world who delight in stillness are also called 'śramaṇa' — this path, too, is exactly the same, able to bring the practitioner to depart from evil, wrong livelihood in body, speech, and mind, and to attain a delight in stillness, and it is for this reason that it is called śramaṇa-hood. Good son, exactly as, in the world, a lowly person may become a noble one, and this too is called 'śramaṇa' — this path, too, is exactly the same, able to make a lowly person into a noble one, and it is for this reason that it, too, is called śramaṇa-hood. Good son, the arhat, cultivating this very path, attains the fruit of the śramaṇa, and it is for this reason that he is said to have reached the far shore. The fruit of arhatship is simply the fivefold Dharma-body beyond training — precept, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge-and-vision of liberation — and it is through these very five factors that one reaches the far shore, and so is called one who has reached the far shore. Having reached the far shore, he then declares of himself: 'My own birth is ended; the holy life has been established; what had to be done has been done; I shall not receive any further becoming.' Good son, this arhat, having forever severed the very causes and conditions of birth across all three times, thereby declares of himself: 'My own birth is ended.' He has, likewise, severed the very fruit of the five aggregates across the three realms, and, for this reason, too, declares: 'My own birth is ended.' Having brought his own cultivation of the holy life to its complete fulfillment, he thereby proclaims: 'The holy life has been established.' And, having relinquished the path of one still in training, this too is called 'established.' Exactly as he originally sought, he has now obtained it, and thereby proclaims: 'What had to be done has been done.' Having cultivated the path and attained its fruit, this too is called 'having been done.' Having attained the exhaustion-of-outflows wisdom and the wisdom of non-arising, he proclaims: 'My own birth is ended,' exhausting every bond of becoming. On account of this very reasoning, the arhat is said to have reached the far shore — and, exactly as with the arhat, so too with the pratyekabuddha. The bodhisattva and the Buddha, fully accomplishing the six perfections, are said to have reached the far shore; and it is precisely upon attaining supreme, perfect awakening that the Buddha and the bodhisattva are said to be fully endowed with the six perfections. Why is this? Because it is through attaining the very fruit of the six perfections that one is said to be fully endowed with them."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the seven-fruit taxonomy as building deliberately from the most ordinary worldly examples (a harvest, filial gratitude, a good friend's benefit) toward the most rarefied (nirvāṇa itself), so that the reader accepts the near-cause/far-cause structure on familiar ground before it is applied to the hardest case — and his three-part gloss on the nirvāṇa passage specifically (T1764, lines 22098–22110) distinguishes a general claim (every wholesome dharma is, in some sense, nirvāṇa's cause), a near/far distinction (the three gates of liberation as near, countless ages of cultivated goodness as far), and the generative/revealing distinction that finally answers Kāśyapa's challenge: the three gates of liberation and the thirty-seven factors do function as a genuine generative cause — but only with respect to preventing affliction's own arising, which is itself a conditioned, produced effect. With respect to nirvāṇa's own intrinsic purity, which was never produced and cannot be manufactured, that same cultivation functions only as a revealing cause, uncovering what was there all along rather than making it exist — precisely the same generative/revealing distinction the milk-curd debate turned on back in Part 28, now applied to close the causation puzzle nirvāṇa itself poses.

On the arhat's fourfold declaration, Huiyuan notes that the Vaibhāṣika tradition and the *Śrīmālādevī Siṃhanāda Sūtra* pair each phrase with a different one of the four wisdoms (the wisdom of ending suffering, the wisdom of ending its arising, the wisdom of realizing its cessation, the wisdom of cultivating the path) in reversed order from each other — the sūtra's own gloss here, giving each phrase two separate readings (one from the causal side, one from the resultant side), effectively honors both traditions at once rather than forcing a single correct pairing (lines 22132–22144). The śramaṇa etymology itself is presented as threefold — cutting off every "lack," delighting in stillness the way any quiet ascetic is loosely called śramaṇa in ordinary speech, and elevating a lowly person into a noble one — with only the first count actually deriving the technical sense of the term; the other two, Huiyuan implies, are the sūtra meeting popular usage partway, exactly the "ordinary worldly language" register named back in Part 35.

What Actually Counts as Slandering the Three Jewels

Chinese Source

「善男子！是七眾生不修身、不修戒、不修心、不修慧、不能修習如是四事，則能造作五逆重罪，能斷善根、犯四重禁、謗佛法僧，是故得名為常沈沒。善男子！是七人中，有能親近善知識者，至心聽受如來正法，內善思惟，如法而住，精勤修習身、戒、心、慧，是故得名渡生死河到於彼岸。若有說言一闡提人得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者，是名染著；若言不得，是名虛妄。 「善男子！是七種人，或有一人具七，或有七人各一。善男子！若有心口異、想異，說言一闡提得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者，當知是人謗佛法僧；若人心口異、想異，說言一闡提不得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是人亦名謗佛法僧。善男子！若有說言八聖道分，凡夫所得，是人亦名謗佛法僧；若有說言八聖道分，非凡夫得，是人亦名謗佛法僧。善男子！若有說言一切眾生定有佛性、定無佛性，是人亦名謗佛法僧。善男子！是故我於契經中說，有二種人謗佛法僧，一者不信瞋恚心故、二者雖信不解義故。善男子！若人信心，無有智慧，是人則能增長無明。若有智慧，無有信心，是人則能增長邪見。善男子！不信之人瞋恚心故，說言無有佛、法、僧寶。信者無慧，顛倒解義，令聞法者謗佛、法、僧。善男子！是故我說不信之人瞋恚心故、有信之人無智慧故，是人能謗佛法僧寶。善男子！若有說言一闡提等未生善法，便得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是人亦名謗佛法僧；若復有言一闡提人捨一闡提，於異身中得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是人亦名謗佛法僧。若復說言一闡提人能生善根，生善根已，相續不斷，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，故言一闡提得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，當知是人不謗三寶。善男子！若有人言一切眾生定有佛性，常、樂、我、淨，不作不生煩惱因緣，故不可見，當知是人謗佛法僧；若有說言一切眾生都無佛性，猶如兔角，從方便生，本無今有，已有還無，當知是人謗佛法僧；若有說言眾生佛性，非有如虛空，非無如兔角。何以故？虛空常故、兔角無故，是故得言亦有亦無。有故破兔角，無故破虛空，如是說者不謗三寶。」

"Good son, these seven kinds of beings — should they fail to cultivate body, precept, mind, and wisdom, unable to cultivate these very four things — are thereby able to commit the five heinous crimes, sever their own wholesome roots, violate the four gravest precepts, and slander the Buddha, the Dharma, and the saṅgha, and it is for this reason that they are called ones who always sink. Good son, should anyone say that every being definitely possesses buddha-nature, possesses permanence, bliss, self, and purity, yet, because it is never made and never arises through any cause or condition, can therefore never be seen — one should know that this person slanders the Buddha, the Dharma, and the saṅgha; and should anyone say that every being entirely lacks buddha-nature, exactly like a rabbit's horn, arising only through skillful means, originally non-existent and now come to exist, and, having come to exist, returning again to non-existence — one should know that this person, too, slanders the Buddha, the Dharma, and the saṅgha. But should anyone say that beings' own buddha-nature is not existent in the manner of space, and is not non-existent in the manner of a rabbit's horn — why is this? because space is permanent, while a rabbit's horn is simply non-existent, and it is for this reason that one may say buddha-nature is both existent and non-existent, existent so as to refute the rabbit's horn, non-existent so as to refute space — one who speaks in this way does not slander the three jewels at all."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's precise gloss on "mind and speech at odds, and thought at odds with both" is the key to this entire passage, and easy to miss on a first read: what makes a flat claim into slander is not which side of the icchantika debate one takes, but whether one's own inner conviction, spoken words, and underlying intention actually agree with each other (T1764, lines 22166–22170). Saying "the icchantika attains awakening" is slander when spoken as a settled, final claim about a fixed present fact he does not yet possess; but the very same sentence is not slander when it is shorthand for the sūtra's own careful teaching — that the icchantika can give rise to fresh wholesome roots and, sustaining them without a further break, eventually attain awakening on that basis. The distinction Huiyuan draws out is between denying a real future possibility (slander) and describing that same possibility honestly, with its actual causal mechanism intact, using compressed language (not slander) — exactly the "teaching according to one's own intent" register from earlier in this very fascicle, now given its sharpest practical test case. The closing formula — buddha-nature is "existent" only to refute the rabbit's horn, "non-existent" only to refute space, and never asserted as either in some further, freestanding sense — closes the whole existence/non-existence debate carried from Part 35 into this fascicle with the same non-attachment the opening exchange on doubt was already recommending back in Part 35's first lines.

Buddha-Nature Is Not One Dharma

Chinese Source

「善男子！夫佛性者，不名一法、不名十法、不名百法、不名千法、不名萬法，未得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提時，一切善、不善、無記，盡名佛性。如來或時因中說果，果中說因，是名如來隨自意語。隨意語故名為如來，隨意語故名阿羅呵，隨意語故名三藐三佛陀。」◎

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, buddha-nature itself cannot be called a single dharma, cannot be called ten dharmas, cannot be called a hundred dharmas, cannot be called a thousand dharmas, and cannot be called ten thousand dharmas — before one has attained supreme, perfect awakening, every wholesome, unwholesome, and morally neutral dharma alike is called buddha-nature. The Tathāgata, at times, teaches the fruit in terms of the cause, and, at other times, teaches the cause in terms of the fruit — this is called teaching according to the Tathāgata's own intent. It is on account of this very teaching according to his own intent that he is called Tathāgata; on account of this very teaching according to his own intent that he is called Arhat; and on account of this very teaching according to his own intent that he is called Samyaksambuddha, the perfectly, truly awakened one."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this closing statement as the deliberate capstone of the entire "teaching according to one's own intent" section that has run continuously from the end of Part 35 through this whole fascicle (T1764, lines 22208–22212): buddha-nature is not countable at all, in any number of dharmas, because before awakening every dharma whatsoever — wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral — already counts as buddha-nature in the sense of cause, and the Tathāgata's own habit of teaching fruit in the language of cause and cause in the language of fruit is precisely what has generated, across three fascicles now, every one of Kāśyapa's difficult questions. The final sentence closes the loop in a way easy to overlook: the very three names by which the Buddha himself is known — Tathāgata, Arhat, Samyaksambuddha — are said to derive from this same habit of speaking according to his own intent, meaning the entire method this section of the sūtra has been demonstrating is not an incidental teaching device but definitional of what a buddha actually is.
