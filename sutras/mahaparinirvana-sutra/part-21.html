Hearing What Cannot Be Heard

A new chapter opens, and a new bodhisattva, Bright Virtuous King, takes the floor. The Buddha names ten kinds of merit unique to this sūtra — starting with "hearing what was never heard" — and Bright Virtuous King immediately presses the paradox at its center: how can anything coherently be called both unheard and heard at once? A sudden, unexplained light then passes silently from bodhisattva to bodhisattva, none of them willing to answer, until Mañjuśrī names it as wisdom itself and a visiting bodhisattva from a distant pure world arrives to ask what it could even mean to "come" at all.

Ten Kinds of Merit, Shared With No Lesser Vehicle

Chinese Source

爾時世尊告光明遍照高貴德王菩薩摩訶薩言：「善男子！若有菩薩摩訶薩修行如是大涅槃經，得十事功德，不與聲聞辟支佛共，不可思議，聞者驚怪，非內、非外、非難、非易、非相、非非相、非是世法、無有相貌、世間所無。何等為十？一者有五，何等為五？一者所不聞者而能得聞，二者聞已能為利益，三者能斷疑惑之心，四者慧心正直無曲，五者能知如來密藏，是為五事。何等不聞而能得聞？所謂甚深微密之藏，一切眾生悉有佛性，佛、法、眾僧無有差別，三寶性相常樂我淨，一切諸佛無有畢竟入涅槃者，常住無變。如來涅槃，非有非無、非有為非無為、非有漏非無漏、非色非不色、非名非不名、非相非不相、非有非不有、非物非不物、非因非果、非待非不待、非明非闇、非出非不出、非常非不常、非斷非不斷、非始非終、非過去、非未來、非現在、非陰非不陰、非入非不入、非界非不界、非十二因緣非不十二因緣，如是等法甚深微密，昔所不聞而能得聞。復有不聞，所謂一切外道經書、四毘陀論、毘伽羅論、衛世師論、迦毘羅論，一切呪術、醫方、伎藝，日月博蝕、星宿運變、圖書讖記。如是等經初未曾聞祕密之義，今於此經而得知之。復有十一部經，除毘佛略，亦無如是深密之義，今因此經而得知之。善男子！是名不聞而能得聞。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the World-Honored One said to the bodhisattva-mahāsattva Bright Virtuous King of Universal Illumination: "Good son, should any bodhisattva-mahāsattva practice this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, he attains ten kinds of merit shared with no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, inconceivable, astonishing to whoever hears of them, neither internal nor external, neither difficult nor easy, neither a mark nor the absence of a mark, not a worldly dharma at all, without any describable appearance, and found nowhere in this world. What are these ten? The first consists of five matters. What are these five? First, being able to hear what one has never heard; second, gaining benefit once one has heard it; third, being able to sever every mind of doubt; fourth, a mind of wisdom, upright and free of any distortion; and fifth, being able to know the Tathāgata's own secret treasury. These are the five matters. What is meant by 'hearing what one has never heard'? Namely, the very deep and subtle treasury: that every being possesses buddha-nature; that the Buddha, the Dharma, and the holy Saṅgha are without any distinction between them; that the Three Jewels' own nature and mark are permanence, bliss, self, and purity; and that not a single buddha ever, in the end, enters nirvāṇa at all, but abides forever, without change. The Tathāgata's own nirvāṇa is neither existent nor non-existent, neither conditioned nor unconditioned, neither subject to outflow nor free of it, neither form nor non-form, neither name nor non-name, neither marked nor unmarked, neither existing nor not existing, neither a thing nor not a thing, neither cause nor effect, neither dependent nor independent, neither illumination nor darkness, neither emergent nor non-emergent, neither permanent nor impermanent, neither severed nor unsevered, neither having a beginning nor an end, neither past, future, nor present, neither the aggregates nor their absence, neither the sense-fields nor their absence, neither the elements nor their absence, and neither the twelve links of dependent origination nor their absence — dharmas of this kind, so very deep and subtle, that one has never before heard, and yet is now able to hear. There is, further, what one has never heard: namely, every scripture of every other school — the four Vedas, the Vyākaraṇa treatise, the Vaiśeṣika treatise, the Kapila treatise — every incantation, medical text, and craft, the eclipses of sun and moon, the movements and changes of the stars, and every chart, diagram, and prophetic record. Texts of this kind, one has never before heard their own secret meaning, and yet now, through this very sūtra, comes to know it. There are, further, the eleven other divisions of scripture, apart from the Vaipulya division, which likewise possess no meaning as deep and secret as this — and yet now, through this very sūtra, one comes to know it as well. Good son, this is what is meant by 'hearing what one has never heard.'"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan glosses the chapter's own title as shorthand for its actual subject, "the chapter on ten kinds of merit" (十功德品) — the bodhisattva's own name, Bright Virtuous King, is used as the chapter's title only because he is the one who requested this teaching, his name itself compounding the two forms of adornment a bodhisattva accumulates: wisdom (光明遍照, illumination reaching everywhere) and merit (高貴德王, virtue that makes one worthy of kingship). Structurally, he reads the ten as falling into two groups of six and four: the first six are qualities any advanced practitioner cultivates in some measure, while the final four mark a genuine advance beyond them (faith, moral discipline, drawing near to good friends, being fully equipped, and vast learning, culminating in the thirty-seven factors of awakening) — with today's fascicle covering only the first of these ten in full: the five-part unfolding of "hearing what was never heard" (itself glossed as a compressed form of hearing, reflecting, cultivating, and realizing).

On the long catena of paired negations describing the Tathāgata's own nirvāṇa, Huiyuan reads each pair as denying two opposite errors rather than staking out some middle position between them: "neither existent nor non-existent" denies both that nirvāṇa is bound to saṃsāra's own existence and that it lacks every virtue and quality; "neither conditioned nor unconditioned" denies both that it arises and ceases like an ordinary phenomenon and that it is inertly static, without any capacity for skillful, responsive activity; "neither form nor non-form" denies both that it is impermanent, corruptible form and that it is denied the Tathāgata's own vajra body. The device recurs across all twenty-one pairs, and Huiyuan's broader point is that the entire list exists to forestall exactly the kind of either/or question Bright Virtuous King is about to ask in the very next passage: if nothing here can be pinned to one fixed pole, the coherence of "hearing" and "not-hearing" as mutually exclusive categories was already undermined before he ever objected to it.

What Studying This Sūtra Gives You

Chinese Source

「聞已利益者，若能聽受是大涅槃經，悉能具知一切方等大乘經典甚深義味。譬如男女，於明淨鏡，見其色像，了了分明。大涅槃鏡亦復如是，菩薩執之，悉得明見大乘經典甚深之義。亦如有人在闇室中執大炬火，悉見諸物。大涅槃炬亦復如是，菩薩執之，得見大乘深奧之義。亦如日出，有千光明，悉能照了諸山幽闇，令一切人遠見諸物。是大涅槃清淨慧日亦復如是，照了大乘深邃之處，令二乘人遠見佛道。」

「斷疑心者，疑有二種：一者疑名，二者疑義。聽是經者，斷疑名心；思惟義者，斷疑義心。復次善男子！疑有五種：一者疑佛定涅槃不？二者疑佛是常住不？三者疑佛是真樂不？四者疑佛是真淨不？五者疑佛是實我不？聽是經者，疑佛涅槃則得永斷；書寫讀誦、為他廣說、思惟其義，四疑永斷。復次善男子！疑有三種：一疑聲聞為有、為無？二疑緣覺為有、為無？三疑佛乘為有、為無？聽是經者，如是三疑永滅無餘。」

「慧心正直無邪曲者，心若有疑，則所見不正。一切凡夫若不得聞是大涅槃微妙經典，所見邪曲，乃至聲聞辟支佛人，所見亦曲。云何名為一切凡夫所見邪曲？於有漏中見常樂我淨；於如來所，見無常、苦、不淨、無我；見有眾生、壽命、知見；計非有想非無想處，以為涅槃；見自在天有八聖道；有見、斷見；如是等見，名為邪曲。菩薩摩訶薩若得聞是大涅槃經，修行聖行，則得斷除如是邪曲。云何名為聲聞緣覺邪曲見耶？見於菩薩從兜率下，化乘白象，降神母胎……乃至於此拘尸那城入般涅槃。如是等見，是名聲聞緣覺曲見。善男子！菩薩摩訶薩聽受如是大涅槃經，悉得斷除如是等見。」

「能知如來深密義者，所謂即是大般涅槃，一切眾生悉有佛性，懺四重禁、除謗法心、盡五逆罪、滅一闡提，然後得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是名甚深祕密之義。復次善男子！云何復名甚深之義？雖知眾生實無有我，而於未來不失業果；雖知五陰於此滅盡，善惡之業終不敗亡；雖有諸業不得作者；雖有至處無有去者；雖有繫縛無受縛者；雖有涅槃亦無滅者；是名甚深祕密之義。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"As for 'gaining benefit once one has heard it': should one be able to listen to and receive this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, one comes to know fully the very deep meaning and flavor of every Vaipulya Mahāyāna scripture. It is like a man or woman who, in a bright and clear mirror, sees his or her own reflected form, entirely clear and distinct — the mirror of this great nirvāṇa is exactly the same: a bodhisattva who holds it comes to see clearly the very deep meaning of every Mahāyāna scripture. It is also like a person in a dark room, holding a great torch, who sees every object within it — the torch of this great nirvāṇa is exactly the same: a bodhisattva who holds it comes to see the profound and deep meaning of the Mahāyāna. It is also like the sun rising, its thousand rays of light illuminating every mountain's own hidden darkness, causing everyone to see every object from afar — the pure sun of wisdom that is this great nirvāṇa is exactly the same, illuminating the Mahāyāna's own deep and hidden places, causing those of the two lesser vehicles to see the buddha-path from afar."

"As for 'severing every mind of doubt': doubt is of two kinds — doubt about a name, and doubt about a meaning. One who listens to this sūtra severs the mind that doubts a name; one who reflects on its meaning severs the mind that doubts a meaning. Furthermore, good son, doubt is of five kinds: first, doubting whether the Buddha certainly enters nirvāṇa; second, doubting whether the Buddha is ever-abiding; third, doubting whether the Buddha possesses true bliss; fourth, doubting whether the Buddha possesses true purity; and fifth, doubting whether the Buddha possesses a real self. One who listens to this sūtra has his doubt about the Buddha's nirvāṇa forever severed; one who copies it out, recites it, expounds it broadly for others, and reflects on its meaning, has all four remaining doubts forever severed. Furthermore, good son, doubt is of three kinds: first, doubting whether the śrāvaka vehicle exists or not; second, doubting whether the pratyekabuddha vehicle exists or not; and third, doubting whether the buddha vehicle exists or not. One who listens to this sūtra has these three doubts forever extinguished, without any remainder."

"As for 'a mind of wisdom, upright and free of any distortion': should the mind harbor any doubt, what it sees is thereby not correct. Every ordinary being who has not yet heard this subtle and wondrous Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra sees things in a distorted way, and even the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha, too, see things in a distorted way. What is meant by every ordinary being's own distorted view? Seeing permanence, bliss, self, and purity within what is subject to outflow; seeing impermanence, suffering, impurity, and non-self with regard to the Tathāgata; holding to the notion of a being, a lifespan, and a knower; taking the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception to be nirvāṇa; seeing that Īśvara possesses the eightfold noble path; and holding to the view of existence or the view of annihilation — views of this kind are named distorted. Should a bodhisattva-mahāsattva come to hear this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra and practice noble conduct, he thereby comes to sever and remove every one of these distorted views. What is meant by the distorted view of the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha? Seeing the bodhisattva descend from Tuṣita, riding a white elephant in transformation, and enter his own mother's womb… and so on, up through his entering parinirvāṇa here at this city of Kuśinagara. Views of this kind are named the distorted view of the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha. Good son, should a bodhisattva-mahāsattva listen to and receive this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, he comes to sever and remove every one of these views entirely."

"As for 'being able to know the Tathāgata's own deep and secret meaning': this means precisely this great nirvāṇa itself, that every being possesses buddha-nature, and that, having confessed the four gravest offenses, removed a slandering mind toward the Dharma, exhausted the five gravest offenses, and extinguished the icchantika within oneself, one then attains unsurpassed, complete awakening — this is what is meant by the very deep and secret meaning. Furthermore, good son, what, again, is meant by this very deep meaning? Though one knows that beings, in reality, have no self at all, the fruit of karma is not lost with regard to the future; though one knows that the five aggregates are, in this very place, entirely extinguished, wholesome and unwholesome karma is never, in the end, lost or destroyed; though there is action, there is no one who performs it; though there is a destination reached, there is no one who goes to it; though there is bondage, there is no one bound by it; though there is nirvāṇa, there is no one who is extinguished into it. This is what is meant by the very deep and secret meaning."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the five doubts about the Buddha (whether he truly enters nirvāṇa, and whether he possesses permanence, bliss, self, and purity) as splitting on a single hinge: the first doubt is resolved by hearing alone, since it concerns whether a fact obtains at all, while the remaining four require reflection on meaning as well, since they concern qualities whose reality is not settled merely by knowing the right vocabulary. He reads the ordinary being's eight-fold distorted view (seeing permanence etc. in the conditioned, and their opposites in the Tathāgata) as inverted precisely, and reads the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha's own distortion differently in kind: not a moral inversion but a category error — mistaking the entire narrated life of the Buddha, from descent through parinirvāṇa, as literally, exhaustively real, rather than as skillful display consistent with a Dharma-body that never actually arose or ceased at all.

On the six-clause "secret meaning" closing this section — no self, yet karma's fruit persists; the aggregates extinguished, yet good and evil never lost; action without an actor, arrival without a goer, bondage without one bound, nirvāṇa without one extinguished — Huiyuan pairs these into three couplets (denying a fixed effect without a cause; denying a fixed agent behind an action; denying a fixed subject behind bondage and its release), and reads the whole passage as this sūtra's most compressed statement yet of a principle this collection has tracked since the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā and Kātyāyana lineage of texts elsewhere on this site: that emptiness of a fixed self does not erase moral continuity, but is the very condition that makes it coherent — a point Part 20's dismantling of "the fixed father, the fixed killer" already demonstrated in narrative form, and which this passage now states as bare doctrine.

A Question About Hearing What Cannot Be Heard

Chinese Source

爾時光明遍照高貴德王菩薩摩訶薩白佛言：「世尊！如我解佛所說，聞不聞義，是義不然。何以故？法若有者便應定有，法若無者便應定無，無不應生、有不應滅。如其聞者是則為聞，若不聞者則為不聞，云何而言聞所不聞？世尊！若不可聞是為不聞，若已聞者則更不聞。何以故？已得聞故。云何而言聞所不聞？譬如去者，到則不去，去則不到。亦如生已不生、不生不生。得已不得、不得不得。聞已不聞、不聞不聞亦復如是。世尊！若不聞聞者，一切眾生未有菩提即應有之，未得涅槃亦應得之，未見佛性應見佛性，云何復言十住菩薩雖見佛性未得明了？世尊！若不聞聞者，如來往昔從誰得聞？若言得聞，何故如來於《阿含》中復言無師？若不聞不聞，如來得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者，一切眾生不聞不聞，亦應得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。如來若當不聞如是大涅槃經見佛性者，一切眾生不聞是經亦應得見。世尊！凡是色者，或有可見，或不可見。聲亦如是，或是可聞，或不可聞。是大涅槃非色非聲，云何而言可得見聞？世尊！過去已滅則不可聞，未來未至亦不可聞，現在聽時則不名聞，聞已聲滅更不可聞。是大涅槃亦非過去、未來、現在，若非三世，則不可說，若不可說則不可聞，云何而言菩薩修是大涅槃經，聞所不聞？」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva Bright Virtuous King of Universal Illumination said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as I understand the meaning of what the Buddha has taught, this teaching on 'hearing what was not heard' does not seem correct. Why is this? Should a dharma exist, it should exist as a fixed fact; should a dharma not exist, it should not exist as a fixed fact — what does not exist should not arise, and what exists should not perish. If one has heard something, this is simply 'hearing'; if one has not heard it, this is simply 'not hearing' — how, then, can it be said that one hears what one has not heard? World-Honored One, should something be inherently unable to be heard, this is 'not hearing'; and should it already have been heard, it can no longer be 'not heard.' Why is this? Because it has already been heard. How, then, can it be said that one hears what one has not heard? It is like one who goes — once arrived, he no longer goes; while going, he has not yet arrived. It is likewise like something already born no longer being unborn, and what is unborn remaining unborn; like something already attained no longer being unattained, and what is unattained remaining unattained. 'Having heard, no longer unheard' and 'unheard, remaining unheard' are exactly the same. World-Honored One, should 'not hearing' truly be 'hearing,' then every being, not yet possessing bodhi, should already possess it; not yet having attained nirvāṇa, should already have attained it; and not yet having seen buddha-nature, should already see it — how, then, can it still be said that the bodhisattva of the ten abidings, though he sees buddha-nature, has not yet seen it with full clarity? World-Honored One, should 'not hearing' truly be 'hearing,' then from whom, in ages past, did the Tathāgata himself hear this? And, should you say he did hear it from someone, why does the Tathāgata, in the Āgamas, further say that he had no teacher at all? And should 'not hearing' remain simply 'not hearing,' and the Tathāgata nonetheless attained unsurpassed, complete awakening in this way, then every being, likewise not hearing and remaining not hearing, should equally attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. Should the Tathāgata have come to see buddha-nature without ever hearing this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, then every being, too, without ever hearing this sūtra, should likewise come to see it. World-Honored One, whatever is form, in general, is either visible or invisible; and sound, likewise, is either audible or inaudible — but this great nirvāṇa is neither form nor sound at all, so how can it be said that it can be seen or heard? World-Honored One, what is past is already gone, and so cannot be heard; what is future has not yet arrived, and so likewise cannot be heard; what is present, at the very moment of listening, cannot yet be named 'heard'; and, once heard, the sound has already ceased, and so can no longer be heard. This great nirvāṇa, too, is neither past, future, nor present — and, being none of the three times, it cannot be spoken of at all; and, being unable to be spoken of, it cannot be heard at all. How, then, can it be said that a bodhisattva who cultivates this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra hears what he has not heard?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Bright Virtuous King's objection as three distinct lines of attack, each targeting a different weak point in the phrase "hearing what one has not heard." The first, built from three parallel pairs (existence/non-existence, hearing/not-hearing itself, and the going/arriving, born/unborn, attained/unattained similes), argues on purely logical grounds that any two mutually exclusive categories cannot both apply to the same case at once — one either has heard something or has not, with no coherent third position. The second raises three escalating counterexamples specifically targeting "not-hearing is hearing": if true, every unenlightened being should already be enlightened; the Tathāgata himself, prior to his own awakening, must have heard the teaching from someone, directly contradicting the Āgama's own insistence that he awakened without a teacher; and, if instead "not-hearing remains not-hearing," his awakening becomes inexplicable by his own account, since it was reached without ever having heard the teaching that supposedly produces it. The third argument shifts entirely to nirvāṇa's own nature, arguing from its lack of any form or sound, and from the impossibility of locating any single moment across past, present, or future at which "hearing" could actually occur, that it is unhearable in principle, not merely difficult to hear.

Huiyuan is explicit that this objection is not staged as a straw man to be easily dismissed — he treats it as genuinely rigorous, philosophically serious criticism, and reads the Buddha's response across the remainder of this fascicle (through the next) as engaging it point by point rather than sidestepping it. His own framing note for what follows: the objection assumes that "hearing" and "not-hearing" must be two mutually exclusive, fixed states, and the Buddha's coming answer will not defend "hearing what one has not heard" as a literal, simultaneous contradiction, but will instead reframe what "hearing" itself means when its object is something — nirvāṇa, buddha-nature — that has no fixed nature of its own to be either heard or not heard as a settled fact.

Like Illusion, Like a Flame

Chinese Source

爾時世尊讚光明遍照高貴德王菩薩摩訶薩言：「善哉，善哉！善男子！汝今善知一切諸法，如幻如焰、如乾闥婆城、畫水之跡、亦如泡沫、芭蕉之樹空無有實，非命非我，無有苦樂，如十住菩薩之所知見。」時大眾中忽然之頃有大光明，非青見青、非黃見黃、非赤見赤、非白見白，非色見色、非明見明、非見而見。爾時大眾遇斯光已，身心快樂，譬如比丘入師子王定。爾時文殊師利菩薩摩訶薩白佛言：「世尊！今此光明誰之所放？」爾時如來默然不說。迦葉菩薩復問文殊師利：「何因緣故，有此光明照於大眾？」文殊師利默然不答。爾時無邊身菩薩復問迦葉菩薩：「今此光明誰之所有？」迦葉菩薩默然不說。淨住王子菩薩復問無邊身菩薩：「何因緣故，是大眾中有此光明？」無邊身菩薩默然不說。如是五百菩薩皆亦如是，雖相諮問然無答者。

Rather than explaining the light through abstract causal analysis, the passage tells a story: a bodhisattva elsewhere asked the identical question, was told of a matching bodhisattva asking it here, and set out; the light was the sign of his approach. This is, on Huiyuan's reading, a genuine causal account (the light has a real, tellable cause — Vaidūryaprabha's own approaching journey) that nonetheless remains compatible with Mañjuśrī's prior claim that the light, considered as wisdom or nirvāṇa itself, has no cause at all — two truths about the same event, operating at different levels, exactly as the Buddha's redirection anticipated.

He glosses the closing formula — "a cause and condition, and yet not a cause and condition" — as the passage's own explicit reconciliation of Mañjuśrī's and the Buddha's seemingly opposed positions: worldly truth supplies the causal story (Vaidūryaprabha's journey), while ultimate truth still holds that the light itself, as an expression of wisdom and nirvāṇa, is not something any cause produces or exhausts. Huiyuan notes this device — mirroring one world's question and answer in another, symmetrically, east and west — recurs nowhere else in the sūtra with this degree of narrative elaboration, and reads it as this fascicle's own way of dramatizing, rather than merely asserting, that identical doubts arise identically wherever beings inquire into the same ultimate truth.

Huiyuan reads Vaidūryaprabha's "coming that is not coming" as building the same kind of list Bright Virtuous King's own opening objection resisted — a series of paired conditions (arrogance/no arrogance, grasping/non-grasping, seeing the Tathāgata's parinirvāṇa as final or not, hearing of buddha-nature or not) each showing that "coming and going" is seen only by a mind that already holds some fixed notion (a determinate nature in beings, permanence or impermanence taken as settled facts) — remove the fixed notion, and the question of whether he "came" dissolves rather than resolving to either answer. The listening-etiquette teaching that follows, Huiyuan treats as the sūtra's actual, direct answer to Bright Virtuous King's entire opening objection — not a further deflection, but the resolution itself, arriving in a form easy to read past because it looks, on the surface, like ordinary advice about how to behave as a listener. His point: "hearing what was not heard" was never a claim that the same fixed mental event both did and did not occur — it names the transformation a mind undergoes when it listens correctly (with reverence, without seeking personal gain, treating self and other equally, holding the Mahāyāna's own view rather than the two lesser vehicles') — a transformation that is genuinely new (hence "hearing what was not heard") without requiring that some strange contradiction in the ordinary meaning of "hearing" be accepted. The entire apparatus of paradox — coming/not-coming, hearing/not-hearing — exists, on this reading, only to force the listener past a naively literal reading of the sūtra's own language, toward exactly this kind of practical transformation.

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva-mahāsattva Vaidūryaprabha said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, should a dharma subject to outflow possess birth at all, is this birth permanent, or is it impermanent? Should birth itself be permanent, then a dharma subject to outflow would have no birth at all; and should birth itself be impermanent, then what is subject to outflow would, in that case, be permanent. World-Honored One, should birth be able to give rise to itself, then birth itself has no fixed nature of its own; and should it be able to give rise to something else, then for what cause and condition does it fail to give rise to what is free of outflow? World-Honored One, should birth already exist before something has arisen, how, then, can it now be named 'arising' at all? And should birth not exist before something has arisen, why, then, is empty space not likewise said to be 'arising'?" The Buddha said: "Excellent, excellent, good son! 'Unborn-born' cannot be spoken of; 'born-born,' too, cannot be spoken of; 'born-unborn,' too, cannot be spoken of; 'unborn-unborn,' too, cannot be spoken of; 'born' alone cannot be spoken of; 'unborn' alone cannot be spoken of — and yet, because there is a cause and condition, it can, indeed, be spoken of after all… Good son, do not now enter into the very deep concentration on emptiness — why is this? Because the great assembly is dull of faculty. Good son, the arising of a conditioned dharma is itself permanent — and yet, since its abiding is impermanent, its arising, too, is impermanent; its abiding is itself permanent — and yet, since it arises from arising, its abiding, too, is impermanent; its changing is itself permanent — and yet, since the dharma itself is impermanent, its changing, too, is impermanent; its perishing is itself permanent — and yet, since it was originally absent and now exists, its perishing, too, is impermanent. Good son, by their own nature, arising, abiding, changing, and perishing are all, indeed, permanent — and yet, because they perish, thought after thought, they cannot be spoken of as permanent; it is this great nirvāṇa alone, able to sever and extinguish them entirely, that is named impermanent. Good son, before a dharma subject to outflow has arisen, it already possesses the nature of arising, and so arising is able to give rise to it; a dharma free of outflow, however, has originally no nature of arising at all, and so arising is unable to give rise to it. It is like fire, which possesses its own inherent nature, and, upon meeting the right conditions, bursts forth; or the eye, which possesses the nature of seeing, and sees on account of form, of light, and of mind. A being's own arising is exactly the same: on account of a nature already inherently present, upon meeting the causes and conditions of karma, and the union of father and mother, birth thereupon occurs."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this exchange as extending "hearing/not-hearing" into a full family of four-part structures — born/unborn, arrived/unarrived — each admitting the same four combinations, and reads the internal-dharma set (applied to a being's own birth and death) against the external-dharma set (applied to a seed's own germination) as deliberately parallel rather than redundant: the internal set shows the pattern in a conscious being's own life-cycle (ordinary beings caught in unbroken birth-after-birth, versus a fourth-stage bodhisattva who has mastered his own arising), while the external set shows the identical structure in a plant, stripped of any question about consciousness, to prove the pattern is a general feature of conditioned existence rather than something peculiar to sentient life.

On Vaidūryaprabha's sharp follow-up (is arising itself permanent or impermanent — either answer seeming to generate an absurdity), Huiyuan reads the Buddha's response as refusing the question's own premise that "permanent" and "impermanent" must be exclusive, settled predicates applying to arising as a whole: arising, abiding, changing, and perishing are each, by their own essential nature, "permanent" (a universal, ever-recurring feature of conditioned existence, present in every conditioned thing without exception) and yet each, considered moment by moment, "impermanent" (perishing, thought after thought, in any given instance) — two senses of "permanent," not a contradiction, and it is only nirvāṇa, which actually terminates this cycle rather than merely instantiating it, that Huiyuan reads as properly named "impermanent" here, in the sense of decisively bringing arising-abiding-changing-perishing itself to an end. The closing point about inherent nature (fire bursting forth only because it already possesses a nature able to burst forth; a being born only because a nature already present meets the right karmic conditions) answers Vaidūryaprabha's causal puzzle directly: what is free of outflow is never produced by "arising" at all, not because arising fails to reach it, but because it never possessed the prior nature arising requires to act upon in the first place — which is why liberation is not one more thing that could, in principle, be caused into being by cultivation, but the removal of what obstructed a nature that needed no producing to begin with.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this hymn as organized around the classical scheme of moral discipline, giving, and Dharma-devotion in ascending order: the opening verses trace the five basic precepts and their extensions (non-killing, non-stealing, sexual restraint, right speech in its four traditional forms — falsehood, harsh speech, divisive speech, idle speech — and freedom from ill-will and wrong view), the middle verses turn to giving in its ordinary and its more difficult, self-sacrificing registers (wells and orchards for strangers, care for the sick), and the closing verses turn specifically to devotion toward this sūtra itself, in a sequence he reads as deliberately including even impure motives (fear, profit, mere merit-seeking) alongside pure ones — the point being that the karmic weight of engaging this scripture at all outweighs the purity of the motive behind it, a threshold considerably lower than the exacting standard set for pāramitā-level giving that the fascicle is about to introduce in its closing section.

He flags one deliberate practical concession worth noting: the sūtra explicitly counts even a thumb-sized image or stūpa, or a single flower or lamp offered, as sufficient — not because the scale of the offering is irrelevant to merit generally, but because this particular list is calibrated to the condition for rebirth in a pure land specifically, which Huiyuan reads as requiring sincerity and direction of the mind toward the Three Jewels rather than any particular magnitude of material offering.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this passage as the sūtra's own long-deferred, direct answer to Bright Virtuous King's opening objection, now finally delivered by mapping "hearing/not-hearing" onto the structurally identical "arriving/not-arriving" framework already built through Vaidūryaprabha's exchange — proving, by exact parallel, that the four-part structure is not a special exception invented to rescue "hearing what was not heard" from incoherence, but a general pattern that also governs how a stream-enterer, an arhat, or a bodhisattva "arrives" at nirvāṇa. His gloss on the four arrival-categories: ordinary beings simply have not arrived (the plain case); the various levels of attainment count as "arrived" only in the qualified sense that what was never actually reachable by degrees (nirvāṇa itself, being unconditioned) is approached asymptotically through a fixed span of kalpas, which Huiyuan reads as measuring the practitioner's own remaining defilement rather than any literal distance nirvāṇa itself must be traversed; the third category (arrived at the twenty-five states of existence, yet not arrived, in the sense of no longer being bound by them) describes liberated beings who nonetheless manifest within saṃsāra for others' sake; and the fourth (ordinary beings and those below full liberation) names straightforward bondage.

The final application to hearing — nirvāṇa itself never heard, because it is neither conditioned, nor sound, nor sayable, and yet its name is heard, under the words "permanence, bliss, self, and purity" — resolves the paradox, on Huiyuan's reading, by distinguishing what is heard (a name, a designation, genuinely audible and speakable) from what that name refers to (a reality that is neither of these things). Bright Virtuous King's original objection assumed "hearing" must mean hearing the thing itself, in its own unconditioned nature; the sūtra's actual claim was always more modest and, once seen this way, no longer paradoxical at all: one hears the name, and, through hearing the name correctly taught and correctly practiced, comes to what the name points toward, which was never itself an object any sound could reach.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Bright Virtuous King's second objection as running five distinct arguments for nirvāṇa's impermanence, each independently plausible: from its being "originally absent, now existing" (like an ordinary object); from being accomplished through a cause (the thirty-seven factors and the rest); from simply existing at all (since "existence" is elsewhere paired with impermanence); from being visible or knowable; and from its apparent inequality among beings (contrasted with truly universal, unconditioned things like space, which admit no differential attainment). His reading of the Buddha's answer treats the darkened-well, blind-man, and released-prisoner similes as together dismantling only the first of these five: nirvāṇa was never absent and newly produced — it was always present but obscured, so that cultivation reveals rather than manufactures it, exactly as a lamp reveals treasure that was already there, and a cure restores sight to eyes that were never actually missing their object.

On causation specifically, Huiyuan distinguishes the sūtra's ordinary five-fold analysis of cause (generative, combinatory, sustaining, augmenting, remote — the standard causal categories governing conditioned phenomena generally) from the sharper productive/revelatory pair introduced to handle nirvāṇa's own special case: a potter's wheel is a productive cause, genuinely bringing a pot into existence that did not exist before, while a lamp is merely a revelatory cause, making visible an object that was already fully there in the dark. Practice, on this reading, functions only as a lamp with respect to nirvāṇa itself — never as a wheel that manufactures it — which is why the thirty-seven factors and the six perfections can be genuinely necessary to nirvāṇa's being seen at all, without this making nirvāṇa itself something produced, and therefore impermanent, in the way its own opponent assumed any cause-dependent thing must be. The closing distinction between "a cause of nirvāṇa" (giving, the thirty-seven factors in their ordinary, incomplete cultivation) and "a cause of this great nirvāṇa" (giving perfected as a pāramitā, the same factors cultivated to their immeasurable completion) sets up precisely the question the fascicle's own final section takes up: what, exactly, turns an ordinary practice into its perfected, pāramitā form?

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Buddha's opening move — that a bodhisattva who has genuinely realized this teaching neither "hears of" nor "sees" giving, wisdom, or nirvāṇa at all — as answering Bright Virtuous King's question at the level of realization before any of the practical distinctions that follow: a pāramitā is not, first and foremost, a more careful or more generous version of an ordinary practice, but the same practice performed from within a mind that has stopped treating giver, gift, and receiver as independently real objects to begin with, which is why the passage lists nine explicit "non-seeings" (no giver, no receiver, no wealth, no season, no field of merit, no cause, no fruit, no more or less, no purity or impurity) before returning to the ordinary-language distinctions Bright Virtuous King actually asked about (spontaneous versus solicited giving, constant versus occasional, without regret, without hope of reward, undertaken even under threat).

On the five closing similes — clinging to a corpse to survive drowning, escaping prison through a latrine, a noble person relying on an outcaste in crisis, a sick person eating what is normally unclean, a starving brahmin eating dog meat — Huiyuan reads these as making one shared, deliberately uncomfortable point: a bodhisattva's giving sometimes requires accepting or performing what would, under ordinary circumstances, be judged repugnant or beneath one's own dignity, precisely because clinging to purity of form over the actual saving of a being (or, by extension, over the actual completion of the practice) is itself a subtler form of the very self-regard the pāramitā is meant to dissolve. He notes that all five images share the same underlying logic — an ordinarily undesirable object or association becomes, under the right extremity, the very thing that saves — and reads this as the fascicle's closing answer to its own opening question: perfection is measured not by how clean an act looks from the outside, but by whether it is performed free of the very attachments (to purity, to reputation, to reciprocity) whose absence this entire fascicle has been defining, one paradox at a time, since Bright Virtuous King's first objection to "hearing what cannot be heard."

Part 21 Complete

This closes Part 21 (fascicle 21 of 40) and opens 光明遍照高貴德王菩薩品, a new chapter that will run six fascicles as a single extended dialogue. Bright Virtuous King's sharp opening challenge — that "hearing what was never heard" is a plain logical contradiction — sets this fascicle's entire agenda: a sudden light no bodhisattva will explain, a visiting bodhisattva from a distant pure world whose "coming that is not coming" dissolves the same false dichotomy from another angle, a full family of four-part structures (born/unborn, arrived/unarrived, heard/unheard) that resolve the paradox by distinguishing a name that can genuinely be heard from the unconditioned reality that name points toward, and a second challenge — that nirvāṇa's dependence on cultivated causes makes it impermanent — met by distinguishing a cause that produces from a cause that merely reveals what was there all along. The fascicle closes by asking what turns an ordinary virtuous act into its perfected, pāramitā form, answered through the difficult image of a bodhisattva's giving sometimes looking, from the outside, like clinging to a corpse or eating what should not be eaten.
