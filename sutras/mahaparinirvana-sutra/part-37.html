The Wondrous Medicine King in the Poisonous Body

Kāśyapa presses on the space simile from Part 35 until it nearly breaks: if buddha-nature is like space, and space itself turns out, on eleven separate lines of argument, not to be a real existing thing at all, does buddha-nature vanish along with it? The Buddha's answer holds the simile to exactly three points of resemblance and no further, then turns to a different, more practical question — how a mind actually traces affliction back through its causes, through feeling and craving and karma, to find that even a body built entirely from poison already carries within it the one wondrous medicine capable of curing it.

Why Buddha-Nature Is Like Space

Chinese Source

◎迦葉菩薩言：「世尊！如佛所說，眾生佛性猶如虛空，云何名為如虛空耶？」 「善男子！虛空之性，非過去、非未來、非現在，佛性亦爾。善男子！虛空非過去。何以故？無現在故。法若現在，可說過去，以無現在故，無過去亦無現在。何以故？無未來故。法若未來，可說現在，以無未來故，無現在亦無未來。何以故？無現在過去故。若有現在過去，則有未來，以無現在過去故，則無未來。以是義故，虛空之性非三世攝。善男子！以虛空無故，無有三世，不以有故無三世也。如虛空花，非是有故，無有三世。虛空亦爾，非是有故，無有三世。善男子！無物者，即是虛空，佛性亦爾。善男子！虛空無故，非三世攝；佛性常故，非三世攝。善男子！如來已得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，所有佛性、一切佛法，常無變易。以是義故，無有三世，猶如虛空。善男子！虛空無故，非內、非外；佛性常故，非內、非外；故說佛性猶如虛空。善男子！如世間中無罣礙處，名為虛空。如來得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，已於一切佛法無有罣礙，故言佛性猶如虛空。以是因緣，我說佛性猶如虛空。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught, beings' own buddha-nature is exactly like space — what, exactly, is meant by this likeness?" "Good son, space's own nature is neither past, nor future, nor present, and buddha-nature is exactly the same. Good son, space is not past. Why is this? Because it has no present. Were a dharma present, it could then be said to have become past — but, since space has no present, it has neither a past nor a present. Why is this? Because it has no future. Were a dharma future, it could then be said to become present — but, since space has no future, it has neither a present nor a future. Why is this? Because it has no present or past. Were there a present and a past, there would then be a future — but, since it has no present or past, it has no future either. On account of this very reasoning, space's own nature is not encompassed by the three times. Good son, it is because space does not exist at all that it has no three times — it is not because it exists that it has no three times. Exactly as a flower in the sky, not being an existent thing, has no three times, space, too, is exactly the same: not being an existent thing, it has no three times. Good son, what is meant by 'no thing at all' is simply space itself, and buddha-nature is exactly the same. Good son, space, because it does not exist, is not encompassed by the three times; buddha-nature, because it is permanent, is not encompassed by the three times. Good son, the Tathāgata, having already attained supreme, perfect awakening, possesses a buddha-nature and every buddha-dharma that are forever unchanging — and it is on account of this very reasoning that they have no three times, exactly like space. Good son, space, because it does not exist, is neither internal nor external; buddha-nature, because it is permanent, is neither internal nor external — and it is for this reason that buddha-nature is said to be exactly like space. Good son, exactly as, in the world, a place free of any obstruction at all is called space, the Tathāgata, having attained supreme, perfect awakening, is already free of any obstruction at all with regard to every buddha-dharma — and it is for this reason that buddha-nature is said to be exactly like space. It is on account of exactly this reasoning that I teach that buddha-nature is like space."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan carefully limits this simile to exactly three specific points of resemblance, no more: buddha-nature is like space in falling outside the three times, in being neither internal nor external, and in being free of every obstruction — and each point rests on a slightly different logical basis (T1764, lines 22219–22248). Space falls outside the three times because it simply does not exist at all — nothing can be said to have become past, be present, or be yet to come if there is no thing there in the first place. Buddha-nature falls outside the three times for the opposite reason: because it is permanent, and a genuinely permanent thing cannot be said to move from future to present to past the way a conditioned thing does. The two arrive at the identical conclusion — "outside the three times" — from opposite premises, one from non-existence, one from permanence, which is exactly the asymmetry Kāśyapa will press on in the next exchange.

Is Space Truly "Non-Existent"? The Long Refutation

Chinese Source

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如來、佛性、涅槃，非三世攝，而名為有。虛空亦非三世所攝，何故不得名為有耶？」 佛言：「善男子！為非涅槃名為涅槃，為非如來名為如來，為非佛性名為佛性。云何名為非涅槃耶？所謂一切煩惱有為之法，為破如是有為煩惱，是名涅槃。非如來者，謂一闡提至辟支佛，為破如是一闡提等至辟支佛，是名如來。非佛性者，所謂一切牆壁、瓦石、無情之物，離如是等無情之物，是名佛性。善男子！一切世間，無非虛空對於虛空。」 迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！世間亦無非四大對，而猶得名四大是有。虛空無對，何故不得名之為有？」 佛言：「善男子！若言涅槃非三世攝，虛空亦爾者，是義不然。何以故？涅槃是有，可見可證，是色、足跡、章句，是有、是相、是緣、是歸依處，寂靜、光明、安隱、彼岸，是故得名非三世攝。虛空之性無如是法，是故名無。若有離於如是等法更有法者，應三世攝。虛空若同是有法者，不得非是三世所攝。善男子！如世人說，虛空名為無色、無對、不可覩見，若無色、無對、不可見者，即心數法。虛空若同心數法者，不得不是三世所攝，若三世攝即是四陰，是故離四陰已，無有虛空。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the Tathāgata, buddha-nature, and nirvāṇa, though not encompassed by the three times, are called existent. Space, too, is not encompassed by the three times — why, then, can it not likewise be called existent?" The Buddha said: "Good son, 'nirvāṇa' is named for what is not nirvāṇa; 'Tathāgata' is named for what is not the Tathāgata; and 'buddha-nature' is named for what is not buddha-nature. What is meant by 'what is not nirvāṇa'? It is simply every conditioned dharma of affliction — and it is precisely to break through this very conditioned affliction that the name 'nirvāṇa' is given. 'What is not the Tathāgata' refers to everyone from the icchantika up through the pratyekabuddha — and it is precisely to break through this range from icchantika to pratyekabuddha that the name 'Tathāgata' is given. 'What is not buddha-nature' refers to every wall, tile, and stone, every insentient thing — and it is precisely by departing from insentient things of this kind that the name 'buddha-nature' is given. Good son, there is no place at all in the entire world that is not space, set against space itself." Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, there is likewise no place in the world that is not set against the four great elements, and yet the four great elements are still called existent. Why, then, can space, having no counterpart at all, not likewise be called existent?" The Buddha said: "Good son, should it be said that, since nirvāṇa is not encompassed by the three times, space must be the same, this reasoning does not hold. Why is this? Nirvāṇa is existent — it can be seen, it can be realized, it is form, it is a footprint, it is a passage of teaching, it is existence, it is a mark, it is an object of the mind, it is a place of refuge; it is stillness, light, peace, and the far shore — and it is for this very reason that it can be said to fall outside the three times. Space's own nature possesses none of these qualities at all, and it is for this reason that it is called non-existent. Were there some further thing, apart from qualities of this kind, that still deserved the name 'existent,' it ought to be encompassed by the three times. Should space share the same status as an existent dharma, it could not fail to be encompassed by the three times. Good son, exactly as people in the world say, space is called formless, without any counterpart, and impossible to behold — and, being formless, without counterpart, and impossible to behold, it would then simply be a mental factor. Should space share the same status as a mental factor, it could not fail to be encompassed by the three times — and, being encompassed by the three times, it would then simply be one of the four aggregates. Therefore, apart from the four aggregates, there is no such thing as space at all."

「復次善男子！諸外道言，夫虛空者即是光明，若是光明即是色法。虛空若爾是色法者，即是無常，是無常故，三世所攝。云何外道說非三世？若三世攝則非虛空，亦可說言虛空是常。善男子！復有人言，虛空者即是住處，若有住處即是色法。而一切處皆是無常三世所攝，虛空亦常非三世攝，若說處者知無虛空。復有說言，虛空者即是次第，若是次第即是數法，若是可數即三世攝，若三世攝云何言常？善男子！若復說言，夫虛空者不離三法：一者空、二者實、三者空實。若言空是，當知虛空是無常法。何以故？實處無故。若言實是，當知虛空亦是無常。何以故？空處無故。若空實是，當知虛空亦是無常。何以故？二處無故。是故虛空名之為無。 「善男子！如說虛空是可作法，如說去樹去舍而作虛空、平作虛空、覆於虛空、上於虛空，畫虛空色，如大海水，是故虛空是可作法。一切作法皆是無常，猶如瓦瓶，虛空若爾，應是無常。 「善男子！世間人說，一切法中無罣礙處名虛空者。是無礙處於一切法所，為具足有？為分有耶？若具足有，當知餘處則無虛空。若分有者，則是彼此可數之法，若是可數當知無常。善男子！若有人說虛空無礙與有并合，又復說言虛空在物如器中果，二俱不然。何以故？若言并合，則有三種：一異業合，如飛鳥集樹；二共業合，如兩羊相觸；三已合共合，如二雙指合在一處。若言異業共合，異則有二：一是物業、二虛空業。若空業合物，空則無常。若物業合空，物則不遍，如其不遍是亦無常。若言虛空是常，其性不動與動物合者，是義不然。何以故？虛空若常，物亦應常。物若無常，空亦無常。若言虛空亦常、無常，無有是處。若共業合，是義不然。何以故？虛空名遍。若與業合，業亦應遍，若是遍者應一切遍，若一切遍應一切合，不應說有合與不合。若言已合共合如二雙指合，是義不然。何以故？先無有合，後方合故。先無後有，是無常法，是故不得說言虛空已合共合。如世間法先無後有，是物無常。虛空若爾，亦應無常。若言虛空在物，如器中果，是義不然。何以故？如是虛空，先無器時，在何處住？若有住處，虛空則多。如其多者，云何言常、言一、言遍？若使虛空離空有住，有物亦應離虛空住，是故當知無有虛空。善男子！若有說言，指住之處名為虛空，當知虛空是無常法。何以故？指有四方，若有四方，當知虛空亦有四方。一切常法，都無方所，以有方故，虛空無常。若是無常，不離五陰，要離五陰，是無所有。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Further, good son, various non-Buddhist schools say that space is simply light itself — and, being light, it would then be a material dharma. Were space, being a material dharma in this way, then it would be impermanent, and, being impermanent, it would be encompassed by the three times — so how, then, can these non-Buddhist schools claim it falls outside the three times? Should it be encompassed by the three times, it would then not be space at all — or else one might equally say that space is permanent. Good son, there are, further, those who say that space is simply a location — and, having a location, it would then be a material dharma; yet every location is impermanent, encompassed by the three times, while space, too, is said to be permanent, outside the three times — and, should one speak of it as a 'location,' one thereby knows there is no such thing as space at all. There are, further, those who say that space is simply sequence itself — and, being a sequence, it would then be a countable thing; and, being countable, it would be encompassed by the three times — and, being encompassed by the three times, how could it possibly be called permanent? Good son, there are, further, those who say that space cannot be separated from three states: emptiness, solidity, and both emptiness and solidity together. Should it be said to be emptiness, one should know that space is thereby an impermanent dharma — why is this? because, wherever space is, solidity is absent. Should it be said to be solidity, one should know that space, too, is impermanent — why is this? because, wherever space is, emptiness is absent. Should it be said to be both emptiness and solidity together, one should know that space, too, is impermanent — why is this? because both are absent in the very place the other is present. It is for this very reason that space is called non-existent. "Good son, exactly as it is said that space is something that can be constructed — as when one speaks of removing a tree or a house to 'make' space, of leveling ground to 'make' space, of covering space, of building above space, or of painting the color of space, as with the great ocean — space is, on this account, something that can be constructed. Yet everything constructed is impermanent, exactly like a clay pot — and space, being constructed in this way, ought likewise to be impermanent. "Good son, people in the world say that, among every dharma, the place free of any obstruction at all is called space. Is this unobstructed place present, then, in its entirety at every single location, or only in scattered portions? Were it present in its entirety at every location, one should then know that no other location could possibly lack space. And were it present only in scattered portions, it would then be a countable thing, divided between this place and that — and, being countable, one should know it to be impermanent. Good son, should anyone say that space, being unobstructed, is somehow joined together with existent things, or, further, say that space exists within a thing the way a fruit exists within a container, neither of these holds. Why is this? Should one speak of 'joining together,' this is, in turn, of three kinds: joining through distinct actions, as a flying bird alights upon a tree; joining through shared action, as two rams butt against one another; and joining that is already joined and jointly joined, as when two fingers of a pair are pressed together side by side. Should one speak of joining through distinct actions, this 'distinctness' is, in turn, of two kinds: the action of the thing itself, and the action of space itself. Should space's own action join with a thing, space would thereby become impermanent. Should the thing's own action join with space, the thing would then fail to be all-pervading — and, failing to be all-pervading, it, too, would be impermanent. Should it be said that space is permanent, its own nature unmoving, and yet joins with a moving thing, this reasoning does not hold. Why is this? Were space permanent, the thing, too, ought to be permanent; and were the thing impermanent, space, too, would be impermanent. To say that space is both permanent and impermanent at once — there is no such possibility. Should one speak, instead, of joining through shared action, this reasoning, too, does not hold. Why is this? Space is called all-pervading — and, should it join with an action, this action, too, ought to be all-pervading; and, being all-pervading, it ought to pervade everything equally; and, pervading everything equally, it ought to be joined to everything equally, with no room left to speak of joining here but not joining there. Should one speak, instead, of joining that is already joined and jointly joined, as with two fingers pressed together, this reasoning, too, does not hold. Why is this? Because there was, at first, no joining, and only afterward did the joining occur — and, being originally absent and now present, this is an impermanent dharma; and it is for this reason that one cannot say space is a joining that is already joined and jointly joined. Exactly as, in the ways of the world, whatever is originally absent and now present is thereby an impermanent thing, space, being exactly the same, ought likewise to be impermanent. Should it be said that space exists within a thing, the way a fruit exists within a container, this reasoning, too, does not hold. Why is this? Before this very container existed, where, exactly, did this space dwell? Were there some place it dwelled, space would then be manifold. And, being manifold, how could it possibly be called permanent, called one, called all-pervading? And should space, apart from space itself, have some other dwelling-place, then an existent thing, too, ought to be able to dwell apart from space — and therefore one should know that there is no such thing as space at all. Good son, should anyone say that the place a pointing finger indicates is called space, one should know that space is thereby an impermanent dharma. Why is this? A finger has four directions, and, having four directions, one should know that space, too, would have four directions — yet every permanent dharma is entirely without any directional location at all, and, having a directional location, space would thereby be impermanent. And, being impermanent, it could not be separate from the five aggregates — yet, once truly separated from the five aggregates, there is nothing there at all."

「善男子！有法若從因緣住者，當知是法名為無常。善男子！譬如一切眾生樹木因地而住，地無常故，因地之物次第無常。善男子！如地因水，水無常故，地亦無常。如水因風，風無常故，水亦無常。風依虛空，虛空無常故，風亦無常。若無常者，云何說言虛空是常、遍一切處？虛空無故，非是過去、未來、現在，亦如兔角是無物故，非是過去、未來、現在。是故我說佛性常故，非三世攝。虛空無故，非三世攝。善男子！我終不與世間共諍。何以故？世智說有，我亦說有，世智說無，我亦說無。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, should any dharma abide dependent upon causes and conditions, one should know that this dharma is thereby impermanent. Good son, it is exactly like how every being and every tree abides dependent upon the earth — and, the earth itself being impermanent, whatever depends upon the earth is, in due sequence, impermanent as well. Good son, exactly as the earth depends upon water, and, water being impermanent, the earth, too, is impermanent; and as water depends upon wind, and, wind being impermanent, water, too, is impermanent; wind, in turn, depends upon space — and, were space impermanent, wind, too, would be impermanent. Were it truly impermanent, how, then, could it be said that space is permanent, pervading every location equally? It is precisely because space does not exist at all that it is neither past, future, nor present — exactly as a rabbit's horn, being no thing at all, is neither past, future, nor present. It is for this very reason that I teach that buddha-nature, because it is permanent, falls outside the three times, while space, because it does not exist, likewise falls outside the three times. Good son, I never, in the end, dispute with the world at all. Why is this? Wherever the wise of the world say a thing exists, I, too, say that it exists; and wherever the wise of the world say a thing does not exist, I, too, say that it does not exist."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan counts eleven distinct arguments in this refutation and groups them by target (T1764, lines 22293–22407): one argument shows that "space" cannot be some further thing standing apart from nirvāṇa's own positive qualities without itself falling back under the three times; one argument shows that, defined as formless and imperceptible, space would simply collapse into a mental factor, which is itself one of the four aggregates; and nine further arguments attack, one after another, every popular non-Buddhist account of what space actually is — as light, as a location, as a sequence, as a threefold emptiness-solidity structure, as something literally constructed, as mere absence of obstruction, as something physically joined to matter, as something a pointing finger indicates, and as something matter itself depends upon the way earth depends on water. Every single one of the eleven ends the identical way: whatever "space" is finally said to be, it turns out either to be countable, locatable, constructed, or dependent on further conditions — and anything with even one of those features is, by the sūtra's own working definition, impermanent and therefore encompassed by the three times, which is precisely the fate space was supposed to have escaped. The closing declaration — "I never dispute with the world" — is not a retreat from this whole demolition; it simply marks the boundary of what the argument was for: refusing to grant that space is a special standing counter-example to how conditioned things work, not refusing to speak in the ordinary way people speak about the sky.

The Ten Dharmas That Keep a Bodhisattva From Conflict With the World

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan matches this list of ten against the nearly identical list given in the Lion's Roar chapter (Part 27), noting that eight of the ten items are the same practice under a different name — "drawing close to a good friend" and "faith, precept" carry over directly, "wise reflection within" corresponds to what was earlier called "stillness," and "compassion for beings" corresponds to what was earlier called "generosity" — while wisdom shifts from tenth place to seventh (T1764, lines 22425–22428). Huiyuan's gloss on the impermanent/permanent resolution is exact: an ordinary being's five aggregates arise from affliction and so are correctly called impermanent by any worldly-wise observer, the Buddha included, when speaking of ordinary beings; the Tathāgata's own aggregates, having no affliction as their cause at all, are correctly called permanent by that very same worldly-wise standard, once the standard is applied to what actually produced them — so there is no dispute with the world's own wisdom anywhere in this exchange, only a difference in which body is under discussion.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this section as a single, continuous method laid out in five steps — observing affliction's own fault, observing its cause, observing its fruit, observing its relative weight, and cultivating the path to sever it — with the blind-man-in-the-thorns simile illustrating the very first step alone (T1764, lines 22447–22468): the blind man represents an ordinary being led by delusion straight into affliction's own thicket and then abandoned there, unable to find his own way back out, and, even in the rare case he does escape, permanently scarred by the passage. The four-person taxonomy of heavy and light karma and retribution is Huiyuan's own bridge back to Part 31's teaching on how wisdom turns heavy karma light: seeing the fault clearly enough to feel genuine revulsion is itself what shrinks a karmic act's own eventual weight, before the antidote of path-cultivation is even applied. The sequence "sever the heaviest affliction first, and the lighter ones depart on their own" reads as a practical corollary of Part 36's "the king comes, the king goes" principle: exactly as the three fetters were singled out not because they exhaust what a stream-enterer severs but because their sheer weight already implies the rest, the heaviest affliction in any given mind carries enough of the lighter ones' own momentum that removing it collapses much of the remaining structure at once.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's gloss precisely locates the desire-leak's internal/external split: the "internal, unwholesome train of thought" is craving's own arising within the mind, while the "external condition" is whatever sense-object actually triggers it — and he reads the Ānanda anecdote as illustrating exactly this split, since the danger the Buddha is naming there is not the woman's verse itself but the internal train of thought a monk's own mind might build around it (T1764, lines 22506–22508). The becoming-leak and ignorance-leak definitions are constructed by careful subtraction: the becoming-leak covers everything the desire-leak does not (the subtler afflictions of the form and formless realms, where external sense-objects no longer operate the same way), while the ignorance-leak is defined purely relationally, as the failure to distinguish self from what belongs to self, internal from external — making it, as the text says explicitly, the root beneath both other leaks rather than a third leak standing beside them. The mutual-causation puzzle Kāśyapa raises (does unwise reflection cause ignorance, or ignorance cause unwise reflection?) gets the same answer the sūtra has given to similar puzzles throughout: both directions are true, exactly as a seed is the near cause of a sprout while the four great elements are its far cause — two different causal registers, not two rival claims competing for the single correct answer.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the seed-and-container simile as answering a real puzzle rather than a merely decorative image: a single body's capacity to generate craving, hatred, delusion, pride, and every other affliction in turn is not evidence of several distinct underlying selves or natures, only of several distinct latent seeds sharing one and the same vessel, each needing its own particular condition — its own "rain" — before it germinates. The "undefiled fruit" puzzle Kāśyapa raises here is a direct continuation of the cause/fruit teaching convention introduced back in Part 34 (the fruit-in-cause/cause-in-fruit examples of clay-as-pot and thread-as-garment): read literally, "the noble ones possess an undefiled fruit" sounds like affliction's own fruit surviving path-cultivation intact, merely relabeled — but the Buddha's resolution is that there is, in the strictest sense, no such fruit at all; "undefiled fruit" is simply a name of convenience for the state of no longer producing any further leak, borrowed from the vocabulary of ordinary conditioned fruits because no other vocabulary exists to describe an absence of this kind. This is the identical move used for nirvāṇa's own causation in Part 36 — a revealing cause uncovering what was never itself manufactured — now applied to the specific case of what an arhat's own "attainment" actually consists of.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's resolution of Kāśyapa's paradox turns on a single, careful distinction the fruit/seed simile makes explicit: not every fruit is itself capable of becoming a further seed, and it is precisely this distinction — a fruit that terminates the chain rather than perpetuating it — that defines "pure, holy conduct" within a being who otherwise arose entirely from affliction (T1764's general method of pairing every abstract claim with a concrete image, consistent throughout this fascicle). Affliction genuinely is the being's own cause, and the being genuinely is affliction's own fruit — Kāśyapa's syllogism is not wrong — but a fruit's capacity to keep producing further fruits of the same kind is a separate, additional fact about it, not guaranteed simply by being a fruit at all, and it is exactly this separate fact that holy conduct interrupts. The feeling-craving-contact chain that follows is the sūtra's own detailed mechanism for exactly how that interruption actually happens: contact is graded into three kinds by whether wisdom or ignorance accompanies it, and severing only the two ignorance-tainted kinds of contact — never contact itself, which cannot be eliminated while a body remains — is sufficient to stop feeling from generating the kind of craving that in turn produces further becoming. The ten-fold list of what feeling is not caused by (not Īśvara, not a primordial person, not atoms, not mere timing, and so on) is the same anti-nihilist, anti-eternalist argumentative pattern used against the twenty-nine severed-wholesome-roots arguments in Part 35, here turned to affirmatively establish dependent origination as the sole correct account of feeling's own arising.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's key distinction here — "worldly-convention perception" versus "the perception of attachment" — is what allows the sūtra to say, without contradiction, that a noble one still correctly perceives a cow as a cow while an ordinary being's identical-sounding perception is called inverted: the noble one's perception simply registers what convention has agreed to call things, with no further grasping laid on top of it, while the ordinary being's perception takes that same conventional label and mistakes it for something permanent, satisfying, and substantial in a way convention never claimed. This is the same worldly/ultimate register distinction that has run through the sūtra since Part 34's "teaching according to worldly consensus," now applied specifically to perception itself rather than to doctrine. The two teaching modes Huiyuan glosses — "by way of beings" and "by way of the Dharma" — resolve the apparent narrowness of "cessation of perception and feeling equals liberation" by showing it is simply a compressed way of naming the same total cessation described elsewhere in full: since perception is the root cause from which feeling, craving, and every subsequent link grows, stating that these two specifically cease is already, by strict implication, a statement that everything downstream of them ceases as well — not a narrower claim standing in tension with "the cessation of every dharma," but the identical claim compressed to its structural root.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

The four-fold karmic-fruit taxonomy resolves its own apparent tension the same way the earlier "undefiled fruit" puzzle in this fascicle was resolved: "fruit" and "retribution" are not synonyms, but two distinct properties a karmic result may or may not both have. Black, white, and mixed karma each produce a result that is itself both a fruit (arising from a prior cause) and a retribution (capable, in turn, of generating a further cause) — which is exactly what keeps ordinary karma cycling. Undefiled karma produces a result that is a fruit but never a retribution, since, having arisen from a cause, it generates no further cause of its own kind at all — which is precisely why it is called neither black nor white rather than white: "white" specifically names a result that still participates in the cycle of retribution, just on its pure side, while the undefiled result has stepped outside that cycle altogether and so needs its own separate name, "stillness."

The closing suffering-cycle passage — affliction generating karma, karma generating suffering, suffering generating further affliction, in an unbroken loop — is this fascicle's own restatement of the twelve links, and Huiyuan's method throughout this entire section has been to insist that observing this chain accurately is itself the mechanism of liberation, not merely preparation for it: a person who genuinely sees the loop for what it is thereby "creates no new karma," since new karma requires exactly the inverted perception this contemplation has already dismantled. The wondrous-medicine-king refrain, repeated after nearly every stage of this analysis — feeling, perception, desire, karma, and now the full cycle of suffering itself — is the fascicle's own way of insisting that this capacity was never something added to an otherwise poisonous body from outside: correct contemplation is simply what the medicine king already growing there, unnoticed, actually consists of.
