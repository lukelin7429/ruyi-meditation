The Lute That Makes No Sound by Itself

The ninth merit opens five accomplishments — faith, an upright mind, moral discipline, drawing near to good friends, and vast learning — before Bright Virtuous King mounts his sharpest challenge yet to the icchantika teaching, met by the famous parable of a king who dismantles an entire lute searching for the sound he loved, and never finds it. The tenth and final merit closes the whole chapter with a warning: corrupt monks to come will trade this sūtra's own true teaching for a counterfeit, sandalwood for common wood, gold for brass — and yet even half a verse of genuine faith in it is enough to see a portion of buddha-nature.

Faith

Chinese Source

「復次善男子！云何菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃微妙經典，具足成就第九功德？善男子！菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃微妙經典，初發五事悉得成就。何等為五？一者信、二者直心、三者戒、四者親近善友、五者多聞。云何為信？菩薩摩訶薩信於三寶、施有果報，信於二諦，一乘之道更無異趣，為諸眾生速得解脫，諸佛菩薩分別為三。信第一義諦，信善方便，是名為信。如是信者，若諸沙門、若婆羅門、若天、魔、梵，一切眾生所不能壞。因是信故，得聖人性。修行布施，若多、若少，悉得近於大般涅槃，不墮生死。戒、聞、智慧亦復如是。是名為信。雖有是信，而亦不見，是為菩薩修大涅槃成就初事。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, how does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who cultivates this subtle and wondrous scripture of the great nirvāṇa accomplish and fully complete the ninth merit? Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who cultivates this subtle and wondrous scripture of the great nirvāṇa fully accomplishes five matters at the very outset. What are these five? Faith, an upright mind, moral discipline, drawing near to a good friend, and vast learning. What is meant by faith? The bodhisattva-mahāsattva has faith in the Three Jewels, that giving bears its fruit, and in the two truths; he has faith that the one vehicle's own path leads to no other destination at all, and that, for the sake of beings' swift liberation, every buddha and bodhisattva divides it into three; he has faith in the ultimate meaning, and faith in skillful means — this is what is meant by faith. Faith of this kind cannot be destroyed by any śramaṇa, any brahmin, any god, Māra, or Brahmā, nor by any being at all. Through this very faith, he attains the nature of a noble one. Whether he practices giving greatly or slightly, he draws near, in every case, to this great nirvāṇa, and does not fall into birth-and-death. It is exactly the same with moral discipline, learning, and wisdom. This is what is meant by faith. And yet, though he possesses this faith, he does not, on that account, see buddha-nature directly — this is what is meant by the bodhisattva who cultivates this great nirvāṇa accomplishing the very first of these matters."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the ninth merit's own opening list of five as marking a return, after the abstract philosophical density of the seventh and eighth merits, to concrete, foundational qualities of practice — faith itself is subdivided here into faith in the Three Jewels and karmic causation (ordinary religious faith), faith in the two truths and the ultimate unity of the three vehicles (doctrinal faith specific to the Mahāyāna), and faith in skillful means (faith that the Buddha's own varied teachings are themselves purposeful rather than inconsistent). His point in flagging the closing qualification — faith of this kind, however unshakeable, does not by itself constitute seeing buddha-nature — is that it sets up the entire remainder of this fascicle's central drama: faith secures a bodhisattva's own progress, but the far harder question of whether faith alone can ever be sufficient for an icchantika, who by definition lacks even this starting faith, is exactly what Bright Virtuous King is about to press with unprecedented rigor.

Why Can't Praising Buddha-Nature Save Everyone?

Chinese Source

「云何直心？菩薩摩訶薩於諸眾生作質直心。一切眾生若遇因緣則生諂曲，菩薩不爾。何以故？善解諸法悉因緣故。菩薩摩訶薩雖見眾生諸惡過咎，終不說之。何以故？恐生煩惱，若生煩惱則墮惡趣。如是菩薩若見眾生有少善事，則讚歎之。云何為善？所謂佛性。讚佛性故，令諸眾生發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。」爾時光明遍照高貴德王菩薩摩訶薩白佛言：「世尊！如佛所說菩薩摩訶薩讚歎佛性，令無量眾生發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，是義不然。何以故？如來初開涅槃經時，說有三種：一者若有病人得良醫藥及瞻病者，病則易差，如其不得則不可愈；二者若得、不得，悉不可差；三者若得不得，悉皆可差。一切眾生亦復如是，若遇善友、諸佛菩薩，聞說妙法，則得發於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，如其不遇，則不能發，所謂須陀洹、斯陀含、阿那含、呵羅漢、辟支佛。二者雖遇善友、諸佛菩薩，聞說妙法，亦不能發，若其不遇亦不能發，謂一闡提。三者若遇、不遇，一切悉能發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，所謂菩薩。若言遇與不遇，悉發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心者，如來今者云何說言，因讚佛性，令諸眾生發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心？」

Ru-Yi Translation

"What is meant by an upright mind? The bodhisattva-mahāsattva holds a mind of genuine, unaffected uprightness toward every being. Every being, upon encountering the right cause and condition, gives rise to flattery and deceit — the bodhisattva is not like this. Why is this? Because he understands well that everything arises from cause and condition. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, though he sees every kind of fault and error in beings, never, in the end, speaks of it. Why is this? For fear of giving rise to affliction — should affliction arise, he would fall into an evil destiny. And should this same bodhisattva see even the slightest wholesome deed in a being, he praises it at once. What is meant by 'wholesome' here? Namely, buddha-nature itself. Through praising this very buddha-nature, he causes beings to give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening." At that time, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva Bright Virtuous King of Universal Illumination said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, by praising buddha-nature, causes immeasurable beings to give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening — this does not seem correct. Why is this? When the Tathāgata first opened this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, he taught that there are three kinds of patients: first, one who, whether he obtains a good physician's medicine and an attendant or not, is easily cured if he obtains them, and cannot be healed at all if he does not; second, one who, whether he obtains them or not, cannot be cured in either case; and third, one who, whether he obtains them or not, can be cured in either case. Every being is exactly the same: should he encounter a good friend, a buddha, or a bodhisattva, and hear a wondrous teaching expounded, he thereby gives rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening; and, should he not encounter this, he is unable to give rise to it — this describes the stream-enterer, the once-returner, the non-returner, the arhat, and the pratyekabuddha. As for the second kind: though he encounters a good friend, a buddha, or a bodhisattva, and hears a wondrous teaching expounded, he is, likewise, unable to give rise to this mind; and, should he not encounter this, he is, likewise, unable to give rise to it — this describes the icchantika. And, as for the third kind: whether he encounters this or not, he is, in every case, able to give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening — this describes the bodhisattva. Should it be said that whether one encounters this or not, one gives rise, in every case, to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening, how, then, can the Tathāgata now say that it is through praising buddha-nature that beings are caused to give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening?"

Chinese Source

「世尊！若遇善友、諸佛菩薩，聞說妙法，及以不遇，悉不能發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，當知是義亦復不然。何以故？如是之人當得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提故。一闡提輩以佛性故，若聞、不聞悉亦當得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提故。世尊！如佛所說，何等名為一闡提耶？謂斷善根。如是之義，亦復不然。何以故？不斷佛性故。如是佛性理不可斷，云何佛說斷諸善根？……世尊！若因佛性發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，何故如來廣為眾生說十二部經？世尊！譬如四河，從阿那婆踏多池出。若有天、人、諸佛世尊說言，是河不入大海，當還本源，無有是處。菩提之心亦復如是，有佛性者，若聞、不聞，若戒、非戒，若施、非施，若修、不修，若智、非智，悉皆應得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。世尊！如優陀延山，日從中出，至于正南。日若念言，我不至西，還東方者，無有是處。佛性亦爾，若不聞、不戒、不施、不修、不智、不得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者，無有是處。世尊！諸佛如來說因果性非有非無，如是之義，是亦不然。何以故？如其乳中無酪性者，則無有酪。尼拘陀子無五丈性者，則不能生五丈之質。若佛性中無阿耨多羅三藐三菩提樹者，云何能生阿耨多羅三藐三菩提樹？以是義故，所說因果，非有非無，如是之義，云何相應？」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One, should it be that, whether one encounters a good friend, a buddha, or a bodhisattva, and hears a wondrous teaching, or does not encounter this, one is, in either case, unable to give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening — know that this, too, does not seem correct. Why is this? Because a person of this kind must, in the end, attain unsurpassed, complete awakening all the same. The icchantika, on account of buddha-nature, whether he hears the teaching or not, must likewise, in the end, attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught, what, exactly, is meant by 'icchantika'? It is said to mean one who has severed his own wholesome roots. This, too, does not seem correct. Why is this? Because buddha-nature itself is never severed. Since buddha-nature is, by its very principle, incapable of being severed, how, then, can the Buddha say that every wholesome root is severed?… World-Honored One, should the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening arise on account of buddha-nature, why, then, does the Tathāgata expound the twelve divisions of scripture so extensively for the sake of beings? World-Honored One, it is like the four great rivers that flow forth from Lake Anavatapta — should any god, any human, any buddha, any world-honored one say that these rivers do not flow into the great ocean, but instead return to their own source, this could not possibly be so. The bodhi-mind is exactly the same: whoever possesses buddha-nature, whether he hears the teaching or not, whether he keeps the precepts or not, whether he gives or not, whether he cultivates or not, whether he is wise or not, must, in every case, attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. World-Honored One, it is like Mount Udayagiri, from which the sun rises and travels to due south — should the sun think to itself, 'I shall not reach the west, but shall return instead to the east,' this could not possibly be so. Buddha-nature is exactly the same: should it be that one who does not hear the teaching, does not keep the precepts, does not give, does not cultivate, and is not wise, fails to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, this, too, could not possibly be so. World-Honored One, every buddha, every tathāgata, teaches that the nature of cause and effect is neither existent nor non-existent — this, too, does not seem correct. Why is this? Just as, should there be no nature of curds within milk, there could be no curds at all; and just as, should a banyan seed possess no nature capable of a tree eighty feet tall, it could never produce a form eighty feet tall — should there be no tree of unsurpassed, complete awakening within buddha-nature itself, how, then, could it ever produce this very tree of unsurpassed, complete awakening? It is for this very reason that this teaching of cause and effect being neither existent nor non-existent does not seem to hold together at all — how, then, could this very teaching possibly be correct?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Bright Virtuous King's challenge here as the most rigorous and comprehensive version yet of the icchantika objection this collection has tracked since Part 5, now organized as a genuine dilemma with three exhaustive branches drawn from the sūtra's own earlier "three kinds of patients" teaching: either praising buddha-nature actually changes an icchantika's outcome (in which case the icchantika belongs to the first category, whose salvation depends on encountering the right conditions — but this contradicts the icchantika's own defining feature, that nothing changes his fate); or it changes nothing regardless of whether he hears it (the second category — but then buddha-nature's presence in him becomes pointless to mention at all, since it produces no different outcome than its absence would); or — and this is the branch Bright Virtuous King actually presses hardest — the icchantika belongs to the third category alongside the bodhisattva, guaranteed to attain awakening regardless of circumstance, precisely because buddha-nature's own nature (like the four rivers destined for the ocean, or the sun destined to travel to the west, or curds already latent within milk) makes the outcome causally fixed and inevitable from the start, which would make both the Buddha's own extensive teaching career and the very term "icchantika" a source of confusion rather than clarification.

His framing note for what follows: three separate images anchor this final branch of the argument — a river's destined flow to the ocean, the sun's destined path across the sky, and milk's already-present nature of curds — each proposing that buddha-nature functions as a fixed, determinate cause guaranteeing its effect with the same reliability as a natural law, which is precisely the metaphysical picture the Buddha's coming answer will need to complicate rather than simply affirm or deny, since this collection has already established, across the whole of this chapter since Part 22, that nothing about buddha-nature or nirvāṇa is ever quite this straightforwardly fixed.

The Lute That Makes No Sound by Itself

Chinese Source

爾時世尊讚言：「善哉，善哉！善男子！世有二人，甚為希有，如優曇花：一者不行惡法，二者有罪能悔，如是之人甚為希有。……善男子！因是善問，即得轉于無上法輪，能枯十二因緣大樹，能度無邊生死大河，能與魔王波旬共戰，能摧波旬所立勝幢。善男子！如我先說，三種病人值遇良醫、瞻病好藥，及以不遇，病悉得差，是義云何？若得、不得，謂定壽命。所以者何？是人已於無量世中修三種善，謂上、中、下，以修如是三種善故，得定壽命。如欝單越人，壽命千年。有遇病者，若得良醫好藥瞻病，及以不得，悉皆得差。何以故？得定命故。善男子！如我所說，若有病人得遇良醫好藥瞻病，病得除差，若不遇者則不得差，是義云何？善男子！如是之人壽命不定。命雖不盡，有九因緣，能夭其壽。……以是緣故，我說病者若遇醫藥病則可差，若不遇者則不可愈。善男子！如我先說，若遇不遇俱不差者，是義云何？有人命盡，若遇不遇悉不可差。何以故？以命盡故。以是義故，我說病人若遇醫藥及以不遇悉不得差。眾生亦爾，發菩提心者，若遇善友、諸佛菩薩，諮受深法，若不遇之，皆悉當成。何以故？以其能發菩提心故，如欝單越人得定壽命。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the World-Honored One praised him: "Excellent, excellent, good son! There are, in this world, two kinds of people who are exceedingly rare, like the udumbara flower: one who commits no evil act at all, and one who, having committed a crime, is able to repent it — people of these two kinds are exceedingly rare… Good son, it is through a good question of exactly this kind that the unsurpassed Dharma-wheel is turned, the great tree of the twelve links of dependent origination is withered, the boundless great river of birth-and-death is crossed, war is waged against the demon king Māra himself, and the victory banner Māra has raised is thrown down. Good son, as I said before, that the three kinds of patients, whether they encounter a good physician, fine medicine, and an attendant or not, are, in every case, cured of their illness — what is the meaning of this? As for those who are cured whether they obtain these or not, this describes a fixed lifespan. Why is this? Such a person has, throughout immeasurable past lives, cultivated three kinds of wholesome deed — upper, middling, and lower — and, through cultivating these three kinds of wholesome deed, has thereby attained a fixed lifespan, just as the people of Uttarakuru live for a full thousand years — should such a person fall ill, whether he obtains a good physician, fine medicine, and an attendant or not, he is, in every case, cured. Why is this? Because he has attained a fixed lifespan. Good son, as I said, that a patient who encounters a good physician, fine medicine, and an attendant is thereby cured, and, failing to encounter these, is not cured — what is the meaning of this? Good son, a person of this kind has an unfixed lifespan. Though his own allotted life is not yet exhausted, there are nine causes and conditions able to cut it short prematurely… It is on account of causes such as these that I say a patient who encounters medicine can be cured, and, failing to encounter it, cannot be healed at all. Good son, as I said before, that some, whether they encounter these or not, cannot be cured in either case — what is the meaning of this? There are those whose own allotted life is already exhausted, and who, whether they encounter these or not, cannot be cured in either case. Why is this? Because their life is already exhausted. It is for this very reason that I say a patient of this kind, whether he encounters medicine or not, cannot be cured in either case. Beings are exactly the same: one who gives rise to the bodhi-mind, whether he encounters a good friend, a buddha, or a bodhisattva, and consults and receives the profound teaching, or fails to encounter this, shall, in every case, come to accomplish it. Why is this? Because he is able to give rise to the bodhi-mind, just as the people of Uttarakuru have attained a fixed lifespan."

「如我先說，若遇善友、諸佛菩薩，聞說深法，若不值遇，俱不能發，是義云何？善男子！一闡提輩，若遇善友諸佛菩薩聞說深法，及以不遇，俱不得離一闡提心。何以故？斷善法故。一闡提輩亦得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。所以者何？若能發於菩提之心，則不復名一闡提也。……善男子！一闡名信，提名不具，不具信故名一闡提。佛性非信，眾生非具，以不具故，云何可斷？一闡名善方便，提名不具，修善方便不具足故，名一闡提。佛性非是修善方便，眾生非具，以不具故，云何可斷？一闡名進，提名不具，進不具故名一闡提。佛性非進，眾生非具，以不具故云何可斷？一闡名念，提名不具，念不具故名一闡提。佛性非念，眾生非具，以不具故云何可斷？一闡名定，提名不具，定不具故，名一闡提。佛性非定，眾生非具，以不具故云何可斷？一闡名慧，提名不具，慧不具故，名一闡提。佛性非慧，眾生非具，以不具故，云何可斷？一闡名無常善，提名不具。以無常善不具足故，名一闡提。佛性是常、非善非不善。何以故，善法要從方便而得。而是佛性，非方便得，是故非善。何故復名非不善耶？能得善果故，善果即是阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。又善法者生已得故，而是佛性非生已得，是故非善。以斷生得諸善法故，名一闡提。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"As I said before, that whether one encounters a good friend, a buddha, or a bodhisattva, and hears the profound teaching, or fails to encounter it, one is, in either case, unable to give rise to this mind — what is the meaning of this? Good son, the icchantika, whether he encounters a good friend, a buddha, or a bodhisattva, and hears the profound teaching, or fails to encounter it, is, in either case, unable to leave behind his own icchantika-mind. Why is this? Because his wholesome dharmas are severed. And yet the icchantika, too, shall attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. Why is this? Because, should he be able to give rise to the bodhi-mind, he is no longer, on that very account, named an icchantika at all… Good son, 'icchan' means faith, and 'tika' means incomplete — being incomplete in faith, he is named an icchantika. Buddha-nature is not faith, and beings are not the completion of it — and, this incompleteness pertaining only to faith, how, then, could buddha-nature itself be severed? 'Icchan' means skillful means, and 'tika' means incomplete — being incomplete in cultivating skillful means, he is named an icchantika. Buddha-nature is not the cultivation of skillful means, and beings are not its completion — how, then, could it be severed? 'Icchan' means diligence, and 'tika' means incomplete — being incomplete in diligence, he is named an icchantika. Buddha-nature is not diligence, and beings are not its completion — how, then, could it be severed? 'Icchan' means mindfulness, and 'tika' means incomplete — being incomplete in mindfulness, he is named an icchantika. Buddha-nature is not mindfulness, and beings are not its completion — how, then, could it be severed? 'Icchan' means concentration, and 'tika' means incomplete — being incomplete in concentration, he is named an icchantika. Buddha-nature is not concentration, and beings are not its completion — how, then, could it be severed? 'Icchan' means wisdom, and 'tika' means incomplete — being incomplete in wisdom, he is named an icchantika. Buddha-nature is not wisdom, and beings are not its completion — how, then, could it be severed? 'Icchan' means impermanent goodness, and 'tika' means incomplete — being incomplete in impermanent goodness, he is named an icchantika. Buddha-nature is permanent, neither wholesome nor unwholesome. Why is this? Because a wholesome dharma must be attained through skillful means, and buddha-nature is not attained through skillful means — it is for this reason that it is not wholesome. And why, further, is it named 'not unwholesome'? Because it is able to yield a wholesome fruit, and this wholesome fruit is itself unsurpassed, complete awakening. And, further, a wholesome dharma is one attained only after having first arisen, while buddha-nature is not attained only after having first arisen — it is for this reason, too, that it is not wholesome. It is on account of severing every wholesome dharma that is attained only after having first arisen that one is named an icchantika."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Buddha's own extended answer as dismantling Bright Virtuous King's dilemma at exactly the point his framing note flagged: the "fixed" and "unfixed" lifespan distinction, mapped onto the three kinds of patients, shows that the icchantika's own membership in the "unaffected by circumstance" category does not mean his outcome is guaranteed like the fixed-lifespan patient's recovery — it means the opposite, that his own wholesome roots are so thoroughly severed that no external circumstance, favorable or unfavorable, can touch them at all, which is a description of total present incapacity, not of destiny. This directly answers the river-and-sun analogies from the prior challenge: those images pictured buddha-nature as a fixed cause mechanically guaranteeing its effect, but the icchantika category names precisely the case where the causal chain has been broken, not one where an alternate fixed mechanism guarantees success regardless.

His gloss on the sevenfold etymology of "icchantika" (incomplete faith, incomplete skillful means, incomplete diligence, incomplete mindfulness, incomplete concentration, incomplete wisdom, and incomplete impermanent goodness) reads each pairing as isolating a specific practical capacity the icchantika lacks, while buddha-nature itself, defined as belonging to none of these seven categories (neither faith nor its absence, neither wholesome nor unwholesome in the ordinary sense), remains logically untouched by their incompleteness — the icchantika's own deficiency is real and total with respect to practice, but says nothing about buddha-nature's own continued presence, since the two operate at entirely different levels. The lute parable, on his reading, is the fascicle's own central image for why this does not contradict buddha-nature's genuine presence in the icchantika: the king's own methodological error — dismantling the instrument piece by piece, expecting to locate "the sound" as a fixed, isolable object — exactly parallels demanding to locate buddha-nature as a fixed thing findable through direct inspection of a being's own current, severed condition; the sound was never absent from the lute, only unmanifest without the combination of conditions (a player, a plucked string, resonant wood) that alone bring it forth, and buddha-nature is exactly the same, requiring precisely the practice the icchantika has, for now, entirely abandoned. The milk-curds simile closing this section makes the identical point in causal rather than musical terms: dependency on conditions is not a mark of a fixed, pre-existing nature waiting passively to emerge, but is itself proof that no such fixed nature was ever there to begin with — which is exactly what allows something new (awakening) to be genuinely accomplished rather than merely uncovered.

「復次善男子！云何菩薩質直心也？菩薩摩訶薩常不犯惡，設有過失即時懺悔，於師同學終不覆藏，慚愧自責不敢復作，於輕罪中生極重想。若人詰問，答言實犯。復問是罪為好不好？答言不好。復問是罪為善不善？答言不善。復問是罪是善果耶不善果耶？答言是罪，實非善果。又問是罪誰之所造？將非諸佛、法、僧所作？答言非佛、法、僧，我所作也，乃是煩惱之所搆集。以直心故信有佛性，信佛性故則不得名一闡提也。以直心故名佛弟子，若受眾生衣服飲食、臥具醫藥、種各千萬，不足為多，是名菩薩質直心也。云何菩薩修治於戒？菩薩摩訶薩受持禁戒，不為生天、不為恐怖，乃至不受狗戒、鷄戒、牛戒、雉戒，不作破戒、不作缺戒、不作瑕戒、不作雜戒、不作聲聞戒，受持菩薩摩訶薩戒、尸羅波羅蜜戒、得具足戒，不生憍慢，是名菩薩修大涅槃具足第三戒。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, what is meant by the bodhisattva's own upright mind? The bodhisattva-mahāsattva forever commits no evil, and, should any fault arise, confesses it at once, never concealing it from his own teacher or fellow students, feeling shame and remorse, reproaching himself, and never daring to commit it again, holding even the lightest transgression to be exceedingly grave. Should anyone question him about it, he answers that he has indeed transgressed. Should he be asked further whether this transgression is good or not good, he answers that it is not good. Should he be asked further whether this transgression is wholesome or unwholesome, he answers that it is unwholesome. Should he be asked further whether this transgression yields a wholesome fruit or not, he answers that this transgression, in truth, yields no wholesome fruit at all. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the confession dialogue's own repeated question-and-answer structure (is it good, is it wholesome, does it bear a wholesome fruit, who is responsible) as a deliberately thorough model of genuine self-accounting, each question closing off a different avenue of self-deception a less honest confession might leave open — minimizing the act's own moral quality, attributing it to some external or sacred cause, or simply naming the fact without acknowledging its actual character. His point in connecting this uprightness directly to faith in buddha-nature ("through this very uprightness of mind, he has faith that he possesses buddha-nature") is that honesty about one's own faults is not incidental to the buddha-nature teaching but structurally necessary to it: the icchantika's own defining trait, on this collection's running account since Part 19's discussion of shame and remorse, is precisely the absence of exactly this capacity for honest self-acknowledgment — so an upright mind of the kind described here is itself already evidence that one has not fallen into that category, regardless of what specific faults remain to be confessed.

On the "dog, rooster, ox, and pheasant precepts" — ascetic vows, current among certain contemporary Indian ascetic movements, that involved imitating an animal's own behavior as a form of self-mortification — Huiyuan reads their explicit rejection here as continuing this sūtra's own consistent distinction, first drawn in earlier fascicles, between genuine moral discipline aimed at ending affliction and superficially rigorous asceticism that produces only the appearance of discipline without touching its actual cause.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Śāriputra episode as a carefully chosen counterexample to the very idea that advanced spiritual attainment alone qualifies someone as a "true good friend": Śāriputra, wisest among the Buddha's own disciples, nonetheless mismatched two meditation objects to two students' own temperaments (giving the breath-counting practice suited to a naturally scattered mind to a goldsmith already accustomed to fine, exacting work, and the coarser skeleton-contemplation suited to a naturally coarse mind to a launderer doing rough manual labor) — the specific error being one of pedagogical fit rather than doctrinal error, which is precisely why even Śāriputra's own considerable wisdom did not, on this occasion, prevent real harm to his own students. The Buddha's own diagnosis and correction demonstrates the specific quality — perfect attunement to each individual's own actual nature — that Huiyuan reads as this fascicle's own working definition of a "true" good friend, distinguished from a merely well-intentioned or highly attained one.

He reads the extended catalogue that follows (Nanda's lust, Aṅgulimāla's anger, Ajātaśatru's delusion, Vākkali's accumulated affliction, Śrīgupta's wrong view, and the rest) as illustrating this same quality of perfect individual attunement across the full range of afflictions this sūtra has organized its teaching around throughout — desire, anger, and delusion specifically, plus wrong view, terminal illness, madness, habitual evil, and fear of breaking a vow — each resolved through an intervention exactly fitted to that specific person's own condition, several of which this collection has already encountered in earlier fascicles (Aṅgulimāla and Ajātaśatru both appearing in Jīvaka's own catalogue in Part 19). His closing gloss on the correction of Ānanda's own famous claim — that "having good friends is half of the holy life," a formula recorded in the Pāli canon itself — reads the Buddha's insistence that it is the entirety, not half, of holy conduct as this fascicle's own final, emphatic statement of just how central this fourth item is to the whole of practice, rather than a minor supporting condition alongside it.

Huiyuan reads the fifth item's own cascading definition of "vast learning" — collapsing from all twelve divisions, to the Vaipulya alone, to this sūtra alone, to a single four-line verse of it, to simply knowing the Tathāgata's own permanence, to finally knowing that the Tathāgata never actually speaks any dharma at all — as this collection's now-familiar rhetorical device (already seen applied to "listening" in Part 25) applied here to its logical limit: true vast learning is not measured by the sheer volume of text mastered, but by depth of insight into a single point, so that grasping the deepest truth in its most compressed form counts, paradoxically, as the fullest possible learning rather than the least.

On the three "difficult yet done" categories, he reads the specific closing qualification — that the bodhisattva who accomplishes acts of this magnitude never thinks "I have done this" — as directly continuing the vajra samādhi's own non-attainment principle from Part 24 and the eight masteries from Part 23, now applied to ordinary heroic virtue rather than supernormal power: the absence of self-congratulation is not incidental modesty but the very thing that keeps extreme sacrifice from curdling into a subtler form of pride. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Bright Virtuous King's opening question here as a genuinely sophisticated meta-level puzzle distinct from the icchantika challenge just resolved: if emptiness is dharmas' own inherent nature, cultivating a concentration to "see" it seems redundant (one cannot cultivate one's way into a fact already true regardless); but if dharmas are not inherently empty, no amount of meditative cultivation could make them so, since cultivation cannot alter a thing's actual nature. His gloss on the Buddha's answer distinguishes two senses of "seeing" that resolve the puzzle: emptiness is indeed dharmas' own inherent, already-true nature (form is neither identical to nor separate from the four elements or the four colors, and can never be pinned to any fixed nature under analysis) — but ordinary beings, misled by the mere appearance of continuity across moments, fail to see this already-present emptiness, so that cultivation does not create emptiness but removes the specific cognitive obstruction (mistaking apparent continuity for a fixed underlying nature) that had prevented it from being seen.

On Bright Virtuous King's sharp follow-up — comparing the concentration on emptiness to a magical property that would turn salt itself sweet, which he rightly rejects as an inverted, impossible operation — Huiyuan reads the Buddha's answer as clarifying exactly what this concentration does and does not do: it does not convert an existing thing (craving, form, a woman's own appearance) into a different, nonexistent thing, but reveals that what appeared to be a fixed, existing thing was never fixed in the first place — craving genuinely produces real karmic consequences and is therefore not simply nonexistent, but a bodhisattva's own freedom from the superimposed mark ("woman," triggering craving) that ordinary perception adds on top of bare sensory data is what emptiness actually removes, not the sensory data itself. His point on the ninth-ground bodhisattva still "seeing a fixed nature in dharmas" is that this fascicle locates the very last, subtlest obstruction to buddha-nature precisely here — not in gross affliction, long since overcome by this advanced a stage, but in a residual, philosophically sophisticated belief in dharmas' own fixed characteristics that only the direct seeing of buddha-nature itself finally dissolves.

He reads the closing Kapilavastu episode — Ānanda's grief at his own clan's massacre, met by the Buddha's own undiminished radiance — as this fascicle's own most emotionally direct demonstration of what "seeing emptiness" actually accomplishes in practice: not indifference achieved by suppressing feeling, but a genuinely different perception of the very same event, in which the "kin" and "city" Ānanda sees as real, substantial losses are, for the Buddha, never fixed, substantial objects to begin with — closing the ninth merit by returning, one final time, to the same non-attainment, non-clinging principle this entire chapter has pursued since Bright Virtuous King's opening challenge back in Part 22, now demonstrated not as abstract argument but as a difference in how grief itself is or is not generated by the very same catastrophic loss.

Ru-Yi Translation

"On account of evil monks of exactly this kind, this subtle and wondrous scripture of the great nirvāṇa shall nonetheless spread widely throughout Jambudvīpa. At that very time, there shall be disciples who uphold, recite, copy out, and broadly expound and spread this very sūtra, and they shall be killed by these evil monks on that very account. These evil monks, gathering together, shall establish a strict decree: 'Should anyone uphold, copy out, recite, or distinctly expound this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, no one at all is to dwell together, sit together, or converse with him. Why is this? Because this Nirvāṇa Sūtra was never taught by the Buddha at all, but fabricated by wrong view, and those of wrong view are none other than the six teachers themselves — the scriptures of the six teachers are not the Buddha's own scriptures. Why is this? Every buddha teaches that every dharma is impermanent, without self, without bliss, and without purity — should it be taught, instead, that every dharma possesses permanence, bliss, self, and purity, how, then, could this possibly be a scripture the Buddha himself taught? Every buddha and bodhisattva permits monks to keep various possessions, while the six teachers do not permit their own disciples to keep anything at all — how, then, could a teaching of this kind possibly be what the Buddha himself taught? Every buddha and bodhisattva does not forbid disciples the five products of the cow, nor the eating of meat, while the six teachers do not permit the eating of the five kinds of salt, the five products of the cow, fat, or blood — should this sūtra forbid these very things, how, then, could it possibly be the Buddha's own true scripture? Every buddha and bodhisattva teaches the three vehicles, while this very sūtra teaches only the one vehicle alone, namely, this great nirvāṇa — how, then, could words of this kind possibly be the Buddha's own true scripture? Every buddha, in the end, enters nirvāṇa, while this very sūtra says that the Buddha possesses permanence, bliss, self, and purity, and does not enter nirvāṇa at all, and this sūtra is not counted among the twelve divisions of scripture at all — it is, in fact, Māra's own teaching, not the Buddha's own teaching.' Good son, people of this kind, though they are my own disciples, are unable to believe and follow this very Nirvāṇa Sūtra. Good son, at that very time, should any being believe even so much as half a verse of this sūtra, know that this very person is truly my own disciple — through this very faith, he thereby sees buddha-nature, and enters nirvāṇa." At that time, Bright Virtuous King of Universal Illumination said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, excellent, excellent! The Tathāgata has, this very day, skillfully revealed this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra. World-Honored One, through this very teaching, I have come to awaken to and understand a single verse, even half a verse, of this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra — and, through understanding this single verse, this half verse, I have seen a portion of buddha-nature. As the Buddha has taught, I, too, shall come to enter this great nirvāṇa." Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the five trade-similes (sandalwood for common wood, gold for brass, silver for pewter, silk for coarse wool, ambrosia for poison) as organized around a single shared structure worth naming explicitly: in every pairing, the counterfeit item is not merely inferior but is actively, knowingly substituted for something genuinely precious by someone who ought to know the difference — an indolent congregation preferring comfort to substance, a monk trading discipline for sensory gratification, wholesome conduct exchanged for its ten-fold opposite, honest self-accounting replaced by shamelessness, and false claims of attainment substituted for the real freedom from outflow such claims falsely announce. He reads the specific detail of lay believers seated above a teaching monk, provided with fine food yet still unwilling to listen, as this fascicle's own concrete, almost satirical image of exactly the corruption Part 22's earlier catalogue of "evil monks" described only abstractly — here dramatized as a single scene rather than a list of behaviors.

On the six heterodox teachers' own elaborate case against this sūtra's authenticity — that a scripture teaching permanence, bliss, self, and purity, permitting possessions and meat, and not requiring the Buddha's own final extinction cannot possibly be genuine — Huiyuan reads this as the sūtra's own deliberate, self-aware acknowledgment of every serious doctrinal objection a reader steeped in more conventional Buddhist teaching might raise against this text specifically, voiced not by the sūtra's own narrator but attributed to hostile monks precisely so that it can be named and set aside as originating from exactly the same six-teacher lineage this collection has tracked as the sūtra's own recurring image of wrong view since Part 19's account of King Ajātaśatru's six ministers. His closing point concerns the resolution offered — not a further doctrinal rebuttal, but simply the promise that faith in even half a verse already constitutes discipleship and a partial seeing of buddha-nature — which he reads as this entire, six-fascicle chapter's own final answer to the persistent challenge of the icchantika and the corrupt monk alike: the criterion for genuine practice was never flawless doctrinal argument, but the capacity for faith itself, however small its initial object, closing the whole of 光明遍照高貴德王菩薩品 on the same note struck at its very opening in Part 21 — hearing what was never heard before, now completed in Bright Virtuous King's own explicit, first-person testimony that he has done exactly this.

The ninth merit's five accomplishments — faith, an upright mind, moral discipline, drawing near to a good friend, and vast learning — culminate in Bright Virtuous King's sharpest challenge yet to the icchantika teaching, resolved through the fixed-versus-unfixed-lifespan distinction, a sevenfold etymology of "icchantika" as incompleteness rather than fixed damnation, and the famous parable of a king dismantling an entire lute in search of a sound that was never a fixed, isolable thing to begin with. The tenth and final merit closes the whole chapter with a warning about corrupt monks who will trade this sūtra's own truth for convenient counterfeits, and a promise that faith in even half a verse is already enough.
