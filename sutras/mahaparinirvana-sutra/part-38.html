Everything Is Pure, Holy Conduct

Kāśyapa's opening question closes the loop on six fascicles of increasingly technical inquiry: what, in the end, is pure, holy conduct? The Buddha's answer — "every dharma is" — triggers a magnificent catalogue of similes for the sūtra itself, before narrowing to a precise, working answer: the thirty-seven factors of the path, known nine distinct ways at once. From there the fascicle turns to the ten perceptions that actually lead to nirvāṇa, observed with the same exhaustive, step-by-step method used throughout this whole chapter — and closes with Kāśyapa's own hymn of praise, the first extended verse offering from any bodhisattva in the entire twelfth chapter, marking the end of six fascicles of questioning.

What Is Pure, Holy Conduct? Everything.

Chinese Source

◎迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！云何名為清淨梵行？」 佛言：「善男子！一切法是。」 迦葉菩薩言：「世尊！一切法者，義不決定。何以故？如來或說是善不善、或時說為四念處觀、或說是十二入、或說是善知識、或說是十二因緣、或說是眾生、或說是正見邪見、或說十二部經、或說即是二諦，如來今乃說，一切法為淨梵行，悉是何等一切法耶？」 佛言：「善哉，善哉！善男子！如是微妙大涅槃經，乃是一切善法寶藏。譬如大海是眾寶藏，是涅槃經亦復如是，即是一切字義祕藏。善男子！如須彌山眾藥根本，是經亦爾，即是菩薩戒之根本。善男子！譬如虛空是一切物之所住處，是經亦爾，即是一切善法住處。善男子！譬如猛風無能繫縛一切菩薩行，是經者亦復如是，不為一切煩惱惡法之所繫縛。善男子！譬如金剛無能壞者，是經亦爾，雖有外道惡邪之人，不能破壞。善男子！如恒河沙無能數者，如是經義亦復如是，無能數者。善男子！是經典者為諸菩薩而作法幢，如帝釋幢。善男子！是經即是趣涅槃城之商主也，如大導師引諸商人趣向大海。善男子！是經能為諸菩薩等作法光明，如世日月能破諸闇。善男子！是經能為病苦眾生作大良藥，如香山中微妙藥王能治眾病。善男子！是經能為一闡提杖，猶如羸人因之得起。善男子！是經能為一切惡人而作橋梁，猶如世橋能渡一切。善男子！是經能為行二十五有者，遇煩惱熱，而作陰涼，如世間蓋，遮覆暑熱。善男子！是經即是大無畏王，能壞一切煩惱惡魔，如師子王降伏眾獸。善男子！是經即是大神呪師，能壞一切煩惱惡鬼，如世呪師能去魍魎。善男子！是經即是無上霜雹，能壞一切生死果報，如世雹雨壞諸果實。善男子！是經能為壞戒目者作大良藥，猶如世間安闍那藥善療眼痛。善男子！是經能住一切善法，如世間地能住眾物。善男子！是經即是毀戒眾生之明鏡也，如世明鏡見諸色像。善男子！是經能為無慚愧者而作衣服，如世衣裳障蔽形體。善男子！是經能為貧善法者作大財寶，如功德天利益貧者。善男子！是經能為渴法眾生作甘露漿，如八味水充足渴者。善男子！是經能為煩惱之人而作法床，如世之人遇安隱床。善男子！是經能為初地菩薩至十住菩薩，而作瓔珞、香花塗香、末香燒香，清淨種性具足之乘，過於一切六波羅蜜受妙樂處，如忉利天波利質多羅樹。 「善男子！是經即是剛利智斧能伐一切煩惱大樹、即是利刀能割習氣、即是勇健能摧魔怨、即是智火焚煩惱薪、即因緣藏出辟支佛、即是聞藏生聲聞人、即是一切諸天之眼、即是一切人之正道、即是一切畜生依處、即是餓鬼解脫之處、即是地獄無上之尊、即是一切十方眾生無上之器、即是十方過去未來現在諸佛之父母也。善男子！是故此經攝一切法。如我先說，此經雖攝一切諸法，我說梵行即是三十七助道法。善男子！若離如是三十七品，終不能得聲聞正果乃至阿耨多羅三藐三菩提果，不見佛性及佛性果，以是因緣梵行即是三十七品。何以故？三十七品性非顛倒，能壞顛倒；性非惡見，能壞惡見；性非怖畏，能壞怖畏；性是淨行，能令眾生畢竟造作清淨梵行。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what is meant by pure, holy conduct?" The Buddha said: "Good son, every dharma is." Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said: "World-Honored One, 'every dharma' is not a fixed meaning. Why is this? The Tathāgata, at times, teaches it as wholesome and unwholesome; at times teaches it as the contemplation of the four foundations of mindfulness; at times teaches it as the twelve sense-bases; at times teaches it as a good spiritual friend; at times teaches it as the twelve links; at times teaches it as beings themselves; at times teaches it as right view and wrong view; at times teaches it as the twelve divisions of the scriptures; and at times teaches it as the two truths — the Tathāgata now says that every dharma is pure, holy conduct, but which 'every dharma,' exactly, is meant?" The Buddha said: "Excellent, excellent, good son! This subtle and wondrous great nirvāṇa sūtra is itself the very treasury of every wholesome dharma. Exactly as the great ocean is a treasury of every jewel, this nirvāṇa sūtra is exactly the same, the very secret treasury of every letter and every meaning. Good son, exactly as Mount Sumeru is the very root of every medicinal herb, this sūtra is exactly the same, the very root of the bodhisattva precepts. Good son, exactly as space is the very dwelling-place of every existent thing, this sūtra is exactly the same, the very dwelling-place of every wholesome dharma. Good son, exactly as a fierce wind cannot be bound by anything at all, this sūtra is exactly the same, never bound by any afflicted, evil dharma. Good son, exactly as vajra cannot be destroyed by anything, this sūtra is exactly the same — though non-Buddhist and evil people exist, they can never destroy it. Good son, exactly as the sands of the Ganges cannot be counted, this sūtra's own meaning is exactly the same, impossible to fully count. Good son, this scripture serves every bodhisattva as a banner of the Dharma, exactly like Śakra's own banner. Good son, this sūtra is the very caravan-leader guiding beings toward the city of nirvāṇa, exactly like a great guide leading merchants toward the great ocean. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve as the very light of the Dharma for every bodhisattva, exactly as the sun and moon of the world dispel every darkness. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve as a great medicine for beings afflicted by sickness and suffering, exactly as the wondrous medicine king in the fragrant mountains cures every illness. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve as a staff for the icchantika, exactly as a frail person is able to rise again by leaning upon one. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve as a bridge for every evil person, exactly as a bridge in the world allows everyone to cross. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve as cool shade for those traveling through the twenty-five states of existence who encounter affliction's own heat, exactly as a canopy in the world shields against the summer's heat. Good son, this sūtra is itself the great, fearless king, able to destroy every afflicted, evil demon, exactly as the lion king subdues every beast. Good son, this sūtra is itself the great master of spells, able to destroy every afflicted, evil spirit, exactly as a spell-master in the world is able to drive away every goblin. Good son, this sūtra is itself the supreme frost and hail, able to destroy every fruit of birth and death's own retribution, exactly as hail in the world destroys every crop. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve as a great medicine for those whose precept-eye has been damaged, exactly as the world's own añjana ointment skillfully cures a diseased eye. Good son, this sūtra is able to sustain every wholesome dharma, exactly as the earth of the world sustains every existent thing. Good son, this sūtra is itself a bright mirror for beings who have broken the precepts, exactly as a bright mirror in the world reveals every form and image. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve as clothing for those without any sense of shame, exactly as clothing in the world shields the body from view. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve as great wealth for those impoverished in wholesome dharmas, exactly as the goddess of merit benefits the poor. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve as a stream of ambrosia for beings thirsting for the Dharma, exactly as water of the eight qualities satisfies the thirsty. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve as a bed of the Dharma for those afflicted, exactly as a person in the world finds peace upon a comfortable bed. Good son, this sūtra is able to serve, for bodhisattvas from the first ground up through the tenth abiding, as a necklace, as fragrant flowers and perfumed unguent, as powdered and burning incense, as the complete vehicle of the pure lineage, surpassing every place of subtle pleasure found even in the six perfections, exactly like the Trāyastriṃśa heaven's own pārijāta tree. "Good son, this sūtra is itself the sharp, keen axe of wisdom able to fell the great tree of every affliction; is itself the sharp knife able to cut away habitual tendencies; is itself the bold courage able to crush every demon and enemy; is itself the fire of wisdom that burns affliction's own kindling; is itself the storehouse of causes and conditions from which the pratyekabuddha emerges; is itself the storehouse of hearing from which the śrāvaka is born; is itself the very eye of every god; is itself the true path of every person; is itself the very refuge of every animal; is itself the very place of liberation for every hungry ghost; is itself the supreme lord of hell itself; is itself the supreme vessel for every being of the ten directions; and is itself the very father and mother of every buddha of the ten directions, past, present, and future. Good son, it is for this reason that this sūtra encompasses every dharma. As I have previously said, though this sūtra encompasses every dharma, I teach that pure, holy conduct is itself the thirty-seven factors that assist the path. Good son, apart from these thirty-seven factors, one can never attain the true fruit of the śrāvaka, nor, all the way through, the fruit of supreme, perfect awakening, nor behold buddha-nature or its fruit — and it is on account of exactly this that pure, holy conduct is itself these thirty-seven factors. Why is this? Because the nature of these thirty-seven factors is itself free of inversion, and is able to destroy inversion; is itself free of evil view, and is able to destroy evil view; is itself free of fear, and is able to destroy fear; and, its very nature being pure conduct, is able to cause beings, in the end, to bring about pure, holy conduct."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan counts forty-two separate phrases of praise in this catalogue before the sūtra narrows to its working definition (T1764, lines 22798–22804), and reads the entire structure as a two-step answer: first, an expansive, almost overwhelming demonstration that this sūtra genuinely does encompass every dharma — treasury, root, dwelling-place, weapon, medicine, mirror, food, clothing, wealth, bed, ornament, axe, knife, storehouse, eye, refuge, father and mother — and only then, having established the breadth of the claim beyond doubt, a deliberate narrowing to the single, practical answer a practitioner can actually act on: the thirty-seven factors of the path. The catalogue's own function is rhetorical rather than merely decorative — it forecloses the objection that naming "the thirty-seven factors" as pure conduct somehow diminishes or contradicts the earlier, sweeping claim that "every dharma" is pure conduct, since the thirty-seven factors are shown to already contain, in compressed form, everything the forty-two similes separately praised.

Why the Contaminated Path Can Never Be Called Pure Conduct

Chinese Source

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！有漏之法，亦復能作無漏法因，如來何故不說有漏為淨梵行？」 「善男子！一切有漏即是顛倒，是故有漏不得名為清淨梵行。」 迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！世第一法，為是有漏、是無漏耶？」 佛言：「善男子！是有漏也。」 「世尊！雖是有漏，性非顛倒，何故不名清淨梵行？」 「善男子！世第一法，無漏因故似於無漏，向無漏故不名顛倒。善男子！清淨梵行發心相續乃至畢竟，世第一法唯是一念，是故不得名淨梵行。」 迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！眾生五識亦是有漏，非是顛倒，復非一念，何故不名清淨梵行？」 「善男子！眾生五識雖非一念，然是有漏，復是顛倒，增諸漏故，名為有漏。體非真實，著想故倒。云何名為體非真實，著想故倒？非男女中生男女想，乃至舍宅車乘瓶衣亦復如是，是名顛倒。善男子！三十七品性無顛倒，是故得名清淨梵行。善男子！若有菩薩於三十七品，知根、知因、知攝、知增、知主、知導、知勝、知實、知畢竟者，如是菩薩則得名為清淨梵行。」◎

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, a defiled dharma, too, is able to serve as the cause of an undefiled dharma — why, then, does the Tathāgata not teach that the defiled path is itself pure, holy conduct?" "Good son, every defiled dharma is itself inversion — and it is for this reason that the defiled can never be called pure, holy conduct." Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, is the supreme worldly dharma itself defiled, or is it undefiled?" The Buddha said: "Good son, it is defiled." "World-Honored One, though it is defiled, its own nature is free of inversion — why, then, is it not called pure, holy conduct?" "Good son, the supreme worldly dharma, being the very cause of the undefiled, resembles the undefiled, and, tending toward the undefiled, is not called inversion. Good son, pure, holy conduct extends continuously from the very arising of the mind all the way to its final completion, while the supreme worldly dharma lasts but a single moment of thought — and it is for this reason that it cannot be called pure, holy conduct." Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, beings' own five sense-consciousnesses are likewise defiled, yet are free of inversion, and are, further, not confined to a single moment — why, then, are they not called pure, holy conduct?" "Good son, beings' own five sense-consciousnesses, though not confined to a single moment, are nonetheless defiled, and are, further, inverted — increasing every defilement, they are called defiled. Their own essence lacking reality, and clinging to a false perception, they are called inverted. What is meant by their essence lacking reality, and clinging to a false perception producing inversion? Giving rise to the perception of male and female where there is, in truth, no male or female at all, and, all the way through, giving rise to the perception of dwelling, vehicle, vessel, and clothing in exactly the same way — this is called inversion. Good son, the very nature of the thirty-seven factors is free of inversion, and it is for this reason that they alone are properly called pure, holy conduct. Good son, should any bodhisattva, with regard to these thirty-seven factors, know the root, know the cause, know the inclusion, know the increase, know the lord, know the guide, know the supreme, know the real, and know the ultimate, a bodhisattva of exactly this kind is then called one who possesses pure, holy conduct."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan tracks this as four successive rounds of Kāśyapa narrowing the same objection — a defiled dharma can serve as an undefiled dharma's cause, so why exclude it from "pure conduct"? — against four increasingly specific candidates (T1764, lines 22806–22819): defiled dharmas generally (excluded because inversion is definitional to the defiled), the supreme worldly dharma specifically (excluded not for inversion, which it lacks, but for lasting only a single moment of thought, whereas pure conduct names a sustained continuity from first arising to final completion), and the five sense-consciousnesses (excluded because, though not confined to a single moment, they still qualify as inverted in a narrower, technical sense — clinging to a superimposed perception, such as "male" or "female," laid over what is, in reality, simply the aggregates). Huiyuan flags a genuine cross-reference puzzle here worth preserving: strictly speaking, "perception" (想) and "consciousness" (識) are distinct mental categories in the Abhidharma systems this sūtra draws on, so calling the five sense-consciousnesses themselves "inverted through clinging to perception" blends two technically separate categories — a blending Huiyuan attributes to this sūtra following the *Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā*'s looser usage rather than the stricter *Abhidharmakośa* distinction, since in ordinary experience perception and its associated sense-consciousness always arise together as inseparable companions.

Nine Ways of Knowing the Thirty-Seven Factors

Chinese Source

◎迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！云何名為知根乃至知畢竟耶？」 佛言：「善男子！善哉，善哉。菩薩發問為於二事：一者為自知故、二者為他知故。汝今已知，但為無量眾生未解，啟請是事，是故我今重讚歎汝。善哉，善哉！善男子！三十七品，根本是欲，因名明觸，攝取名受，增名善思，主名為念，導名為定，勝名智慧，實名解脫，畢竟名為大般涅槃。善男子！善欲即是初發道心，乃至阿耨多羅三藐三菩提之根本也，是故我說欲為根本。善男子！如世間說，一切苦惱，愛為根本；一切疹病，宿食為本；一切斷事，鬪諍為本。一切惡事，虛妄為本。」 迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如來先於此經中說，一切善法不放逸為本，今乃說欲，是義云何？」 佛言：「善男子！若言生因，善欲是也；若言了因，不放逸是。如世間說，一切果者子為其因。或復有說，子為生因，地為了因，是義亦爾。」 迦葉菩薩言：「世尊！如來先於餘經中說，三十七品佛是根本，是義云何？」 「善男子！如來先說，眾生初知三十七品，佛是根本。若自證得，欲為根本。」 「世尊！云何明觸，名之為因？」 「善男子！如來或時說明為慧、或說為信。善男子！信因緣故，親近善友，是名為觸。親近因緣，得聞正法，是名為觸。因聞正法，身口意淨，是名為觸。因三業淨，獲得正命，是名為觸。因正命故，得淨根戒；因淨根戒，樂寂靜處；因樂寂靜，能善思惟；因善思惟，得如法住；因如法住，得三十七品，能壞無量諸惡煩惱，是名為觸。善男子！受名攝取，眾生受時能作善惡，是故名受為攝取也。善男子！受因緣故生諸煩惱，三十七品能破壞之，是故以受為攝取也。因善思惟能破煩惱，是故名增。何以故？勤修習故。得如是等三十七品，若觀能破諸惡煩惱，要賴專念，是故以念為主。如世間中一切四兵隨主將意，三十七品亦復如是，皆隨念主。善男子！既入定已，三十七品能善分別一切法相，是故以定為導。是三十七品分別法相，智為最勝，是故以慧為勝。如是智慧知煩惱已，智慧力故，煩惱消滅，如世間中四兵壞怨，或一、或二勇健者能。三十七品亦復如是，智慧力故能壞煩惱，是故以慧為勝。善男子！雖因修習三十七品，獲得四禪神通安樂，亦不名實。若壞煩惱證解脫時，乃名為實。是三十七品發心修道，雖得世樂及出世樂、四沙門果及以解脫，亦不得名為畢竟也。若能斷除三十七品所行之事，是名涅槃，是故我說畢竟者即大涅槃。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what is meant by knowing the root, all the way through to knowing the ultimate?" The Buddha said: "Good son, excellent, excellent! A bodhisattva raises a question for the sake of two things: knowing it for himself, and having others come to know it. You yourself already know this, and yet, for the sake of countless beings who do not yet understand, you raise this very request — and it is for this reason that I now praise you once again. Excellent, excellent! Good son, of these thirty-seven factors, the root is desire; the cause is called the contact of wisdom; the inclusion is called feeling; the increase is called wholesome reflection; the lord is called mindfulness; the guide is called concentration; the supreme is called wisdom; the real is called liberation; and the ultimate is called the great nirvāṇa. Good son, wholesome desire is itself the very root, from the first arising of the mind bent on the path, all the way through to supreme, perfect awakening — and it is for this reason that I teach desire as the root. Good son, exactly as it is said in the world that craving is the root of every suffering and affliction, that old, undigested food is the root of every illness, that quarreling is the root of every ended relationship, and that falsehood is the root of every evil deed." Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the Tathāgata has previously taught, in this very sūtra, that heedfulness is the root of every wholesome dharma — how, then, can it now be taught that desire is the root instead?" The Buddha said: "Good son, spoken of as a generative cause, wholesome desire is indeed the root; spoken of as a revealing cause, heedfulness is the root. Exactly as it is said in the world that, of every fruit, the seed is its cause — yet one might equally say that the seed is the generative cause, while the earth is the revealing cause — the meaning here is exactly the same." Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said: "World-Honored One, the Tathāgata has previously taught, in other sūtras, that the Buddha himself is the root of the thirty-seven factors — what is the meaning of this?" "Good son, the Tathāgata previously taught that, for a being first coming to know the thirty-seven factors, the Buddha is their root; but, once one has personally realized them for oneself, desire is the root." "World-Honored One, in what sense is the contact of wisdom called the cause?" "Good son, the Tathāgata, at times, speaks of wisdom as 'wisdom' itself, and, at other times, speaks of it as 'faith.' Good son, through the cause and condition of faith, one draws close to a good spiritual friend — this is called contact. Through the cause and condition of drawing close to such a friend, one comes to hear the true Dharma — this is called contact. Through hearing the true Dharma, body, speech, and mind become pure — this is called contact. Through the purity of these three karmas, one attains right livelihood — this is called contact. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's technical identification is precise and worth preserving: eight of the nine terms in this taxonomy map directly onto eight of the ten universal mental factors recognized across the Abhidharma systems (desire, contact, feeling, volition, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom, and liberation), with only perception and recollection excluded — perception because its role lies mainly in the distant approach to the path rather than the path itself, recollection because it contributes no great independent function of its own (T1764, lines 22826–22836). The ninth term, "the ultimate," stands apart as the fruit these eight factors-as-causes together produce, rather than a ninth factor alongside them. Huiyuan reads the three parallel restatements that follow — desire-through-ultimate mapped onto going-forth-through-supreme-awakening, and then onto the twelve links themselves, from consciousness through birth-old-age-death — not as three competing systems but as the identical ninefold structure viewed through three different lenses: the general cause-and-effect vocabulary of ordinary mental factors, the concrete monastic path from ordination to buddhahood, and the twelve links of dependent origination, each equally valid because each names the same underlying progression from a first stirring to its ultimate, exhaustive fruit. The closing distinction between root, cause, and increase — first arising, continuous similarity, and the productive transformation of that similarity into something new — gives the entire ninefold schema its own internal logic: not nine static labels, but nine stages of a single process caught at nine different moments of its own unfolding.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan organizes the coarse-impermanence contemplation into a careful progression from what is directly witnessed to what is only known by report: first internal change across a single lifetime (birth through death), then external change in the natural world (seed through fruit), then what is seen but not personally lived through (the varying fortunes of other beings), and finally what is never seen at all but only heard of — the gods' own decline, the first beings of this eon losing their self-luminous bodies, a wheel-turning king's power ultimately failing, and the earth's own fertility diminishing through the general decline of merit (T1764, lines 22897–22916). Each register extends the contemplation's reach further from direct experience, so that impermanence is established not merely as a personal observation but as a truth holding at every possible scale, witnessed or not. The subtle-impermanence argument then makes a stronger, more technical claim: the ten-stage sequence of bodily development (from a mere membrane through infancy to old age) could only unfold gradually, stage by stage, if something were already perishing and renewing at every single moment beneath the surface of that visible sequence — otherwise, lacking any moment-by-moment mechanism for change, the whole developmental sequence would have to appear complete all at once, which plainly does not happen. This is the same argument used, from a different angle, against the milk-curd debate's own naive theories of causation in earlier fascicles: visible, gradual sequences of change are themselves the proof of an invisible, momentary process underlying them, not evidence against it.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the no-self argument here as running through five distinct approaches in sequence rather than repeating a single point: suffering and impermanence are shown to exclude a self directly (since neither can coherently be identified with anything permanent enough to count as a "self"); every dharma's own status as a mere combination, lacking any fixed nature of its own, excludes a self residing anywhere within that combination; the fact that no single dharma, nor any mere aggregate of dharmas, is capable of independent agency excludes a self acting as their controller; the fact that all arising and ceasing already happens through combination alone, with no further agent needed to explain it, excludes positing a self as an unnecessary addition; and finally the direct dilemma — a self would have to be either exactly one, shared identically by every being, or else many, one per being — closes off both remaining possibilities, since a single universal self cannot explain the plain differences between beings, while a plural self cannot be called truly "one, all-pervading, without boundary" the way self-theories typically require. This fivefold structure mirrors the twenty-nine severed-wholesome-roots arguments from Part 35 in form — a battery of independent proofs converging on one conclusion — but reversed in direction, building a positive case for no-self rather than a case for nihilism, showing that the same rigorous, multi-angled argumentative method serves opposite ends depending on what it is actually establishing.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the four-food analysis (edible food, contact, intention, and consciousness — the standard fourfold classification of what sustains a being) as escalating deliberately from the most familiar to the most abstract, so that revulsion cultivated toward ordinary eating carries over to the subtler forms of "feeding" a mind performs at every moment through sense-contact, volitional intention, and the mere continuity of consciousness itself — each illustrated by an image more visceral than the last, from eating one's own child's flesh to being pierced by three hundred spears. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's arithmetic here is worth spelling out, since the text moves quickly between counts: six perceptions have been fully treated by this point (impermanence, suffering, no-self, weariness toward food, the world's own lack of delight, and death), and these six together become the "cause" of seven further perceptions the text lists but does not individually unpack in detail — constant cultivation, joyful cultivation, freedom from anger, freedom from envy, wholesome vows, freedom from pride, and mastery in samādhi (T1764, lines 22970–22975). Huiyuan reads this as a genuine structural claim, not mere praise: cultivating the six base perceptions to completion does not simply add up to seven more virtues sitting alongside them, but actually generates these seven as their natural consequence, the way a cause generates its own proper fruit. The remaining four perceptions in the original list of ten (many past faults, departure, cessation, and freedom from craving) are subsumed within this same structure rather than treated as wholly separate stages — Huiyuan notes the *Daśabhūmika Sūtra*'s own parallel fourfold sequence of weariness, departure, cessation, and dedication as the likely source for this compression, with the sūtra here choosing to develop the first six in exhaustive step-by-step detail while gathering the remaining four into a single, summarizing close. The lord-of-death similes — the tree over the riverbank, the condemned criminal, the ravenous lion, the poisonous serpent, the thirsty horse, the enraged spirit — each capture a different quality of death's own imminence (structural precariousness, moral finality, appetite, force, desperation, and sudden violence) rather than repeating a single image six times, giving the contemplation genuine range rather than mere repetition.

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, Bodhisattva Kāśyapa, in the Buddha's own presence, praised the Buddha with these verses: "Compassionate toward the world, the great physician-king, His body and his wisdom both perfectly still — Within the very dharma of no-self, a true self is found; It is for this that I bow in reverence to the unsurpassed one. Between the mind first arising and the mind at final completion, there is no difference, Yet, of these two minds, it is the first that is the harder; Not yet crossed over himself, he crosses others over first — It is for this that I bow before the mind's very first arising. Once this mind has first arisen, he already becomes the teacher of gods and humans, Surpassing even the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha — A mind arisen in this way already transcends the three realms; It is for this that it is called the very most unsurpassed. The saviors of the world require a request before they act, Yet the Tathāgata, unasked, becomes the teacher of all — The Buddha follows the world exactly as a calf follows its mother; It is for this that he is called the great, compassionate ox. The Tathāgata's own merit fills the ten directions, Yet the ignorant and lowly are unable to praise it at all — I now praise only his mind of loving-kindness and compassion, In repayment for his own bodily and verbal deeds. The world forever seeks pleasure for its own benefit alone, Yet the Tathāgata never once acts for this reason — He is able to sever beings' own worldly retribution; It is for this that I bow before one who benefits both self and other. The world follows kinship in granting its generosity, Yet the Tathāgata's own benefit knows neither friend nor foe — The Buddha bears no such distinction, unlike people of the world; It is for this that his mind remains, without exception, undivided. The world speaks one way and acts another, Yet the Tathāgata's actions never differ from his own words — Whatever he cultivates, he thereby severs every conditioned activity; It is for this that he is called the Tathāgata. Having already fully understood affliction's own faults, He manifests himself dwelling among them for beings' own sake — Long ago already liberated from this very world, He delights in dwelling amid birth and death, out of compassion alone. Though he manifests the body of a god, and the body of a human, His compassion follows after beings exactly as a calf follows its mother — The Tathāgata is himself the very mother of all beings; His mind of loving-kindness is himself the small calf. Receiving every suffering upon himself for the sake of remembering beings, At the very moment of this compassionate remembrance, his mind never regrets or falters — So full is his compassion that he does not even notice the suffering; It is for this that I bow my head before the one who draws out suffering. Though the Tathāgata performs boundless merit, His bodily, verbal, and mental karma remain forever pure — Always acting for the sake of beings, never for himself; It is for this that I bow before this very pure karma. The Tathāgata receives suffering yet does not notice it as suffering, Seeing beings' own suffering exactly as though it were his own — Though he dwells in hell itself for beings' own sake, He gives rise to no perception of suffering, nor any thought of regret. Every being's own different suffering Is, in every case, this single Tathāgata's own suffering alone — Realizing this, his mind grows ever more resolute, And so he is able to diligently cultivate the unsurpassed path. The Buddha possesses a single, undivided mind of great loving-kindness, Holding every being in compassionate thought exactly as his own child — Yet beings, not knowing that the Buddha is able to save them, Thereby slander the Tathāgata, and the Dharma, and the saṅgha as well. Though the world is filled with every affliction, And possesses boundless faults and evils besides, Bonds and faults of exactly this kind The Buddha was already able to destroy from the very first arising of his own mind. Only the buddhas themselves are truly able to praise a buddha; Apart from a buddha, no one else can offer this praise in full — I now offer my praise through but a single dharma alone: That of a mind of loving-kindness, wandering freely through the world. The Tathāgata's own loving-kindness is itself the great gathering of every dharma; This very loving-kindness is also able to deliver every being across — It is itself the unsurpassed, true liberation, And this very liberation is itself the great nirvāṇa."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

This hymn is the first extended verse offering from Kāśyapa himself across the entire twelfth chapter, and its placement here, closing six fascicles of relentless technical questioning, gives it the weight of a formal conclusion rather than a mere interlude. The praise moves through a deliberate sequence: first the paradox of true self found within no-self, then the harder-than-completion difficulty of the very first arising of the awakening-mind (a theme reaching back to the sūtra's own teaching that a beginning bodhisattva's resolve, though incomplete, already surpasses śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas in kind), then a series of contrasts between ordinary worldly conduct and the Buddha's own — acting unbidden, benefiting without partiality, matching word to deed exactly — before turning to the central image of the calf and its mother, repeated twice, casting the Buddha's own compassion as instinctive and inseparable rather than a discipline cultivated at a distance from the one who feels it. The verse "every being's own different suffering is, in every case, this single Tathāgata's own suffering alone" is the hymn's own emotional center, and gives concrete, felt form to what Parts 27 through 38 have spent twelve fascicles arguing in technical terms: that buddha-nature, the identity of beings and Buddha, and the reality of a suffering shared rather than merely observed from outside are not separate doctrines but different expressions of a single truth. The chapter closes exactly where its central argument always pointed — loving-kindness itself named as liberation, and liberation named, in turn, as the great nirvāṇa the entire twelfth chapter has been circling from its very first fascicle.

Part 38 Complete — 迦葉菩薩品 Concluded

With Kāśyapa's own hymn of praise, the twelfth chapter, 迦葉菩薩品, comes to its close — six fascicles (Parts 33–38) that opened with Sunakṣatra's fall into hell before the Buddha's own eyes, worked through Kāśyapa's catalogue of twenty apparent contradictions and the Buddha's own point-by-point resolution of each, built the full technical vocabulary of four kinds of answer, seven kinds of language, and buddha-nature traced stage by stage, reapplied the river simile to the sūtra's own reception, and closed with an exhaustive, first-principles map of affliction, karma, and suffering culminating in the ten perceptions that lead to nirvāṇa. This is the second complete six-fascicle chapter of this translation, following 師子吼菩薩品 (Parts 27–32) — and, exactly as that chapter closed with Lion's Roar's own hymn, this one closes with Kāśyapa's, the questioner across six fascicles taking up his own voice in praise rather than inquiry.
