The Four Marks, and the Buddha's Boundless Manifestations

This fascicle opens 如來性品 (The Chapter on the Tathāgata's Nature), the sūtra's longest single chapter, spanning seven fascicles. It sets out the four qualifications for teaching this sūtra to others, gives the sūtra's own reasoning for prohibiting meat entirely, and explains why the Buddha did not set out every precept and every teaching all at once. Then, in one of this sūtra's most theologically consequential passages, the Buddha explains that his birth, his marriage, his son Rāhula, his austerities, and his awakening were all skillful manifestations of a body that never truly entered the womb at all — and extends the same claim to appearing as an icchantika, a schismatic, Māra himself, and a woman who attains buddhahood, none of which he actually is.

Self-Correction

Chinese Source

佛復告迦葉：「善男子！菩薩摩訶薩分別開示大般涅槃，有四相義。何等為四？一者自正，二者正他，三者能隨問答，四者善解因緣義。云何自正？若佛如來，見諸因緣而有所說，譬如比丘見大火聚，便作是言：『我寧抱是熾燃火聚，終不敢於如來所說十二部經及祕密藏，謗言云是波旬所說，若言如來、法、僧無常；如是說者，為自侵欺，亦欺於人。寧以利刀，自斷其舌，終不說言，如來、法、僧，是無常也。若聞他說，亦不信受，於此說者，應生憐愍，如來、法、僧不可思議。』應如是持，自觀己身猶如火聚，是名自正。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said further to Kāśyapa: "Good son, a bodhisattva-mahāsattva who would distinguish and expound this great parinirvāṇa to others must have four marks. What are these four? First, self-correction; second, correcting others; third, being able to answer whatever is asked; and fourth, understanding well the meaning of causes and conditions. What is self-correction? When the Buddha, the Tathāgata, sees a particular cause and condition and speaks accordingly — it is like a monk who, seeing a great blazing fire, says to himself: 'I would sooner embrace this raging fire than ever dare to slander the twelvefold scriptures and secret treasury the Tathāgata has taught, saying they were spoken by Pāpīyas, or saying that the Tathāgata, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha are impermanent — for to speak this way is to injure and deceive oneself, and to deceive others as well. I would sooner cut out my own tongue with a sharp knife than ever say that the Tathāgata, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha are impermanent. And should I hear another say such a thing, I would not believe or accept it, but would instead give rise to compassion for the one who says it — for the Tathāgata, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha are beyond conceptual thought.' One should hold to this resolve, regarding one's own self as though it stood before that very fire — this is called self-correction."

Correcting Others: The Nursing Mother

Chinese Source

「云何正他？」佛說法時，有一女人，乳養嬰兒，來詣佛所，稽首佛足，有所顧念，心自思惟，便坐一面。爾時世尊知而故問：「汝以愛念多唅兒酥，不知籌量消與不消？」爾時女人即白佛言：「甚奇，世尊！善能知我心中所念，唯願如來教我多少。世尊！我於今朝多與兒蘇，恐不能消，將無夭壽？唯願如來為我解說。」佛言：「汝兒所食，尋即消化，增益壽命。」女人聞已，心大踊躍，復作是言：「如來實說，故我歡喜。」

「世尊如是，為欲調伏諸眾生故，善能分別，說消、不消。亦說諸法無我、無常。若佛世尊先說常者，受化之徒，當言此法與外道同，即便捨去。」

復告女人：「若兒長大，能自行來，凡所食噉，能消難消，本所與蘇，則不供足。我之所有聲聞弟子，亦復如是，如汝嬰兒，不能消是常住之法，是故我先說苦、無常。若我聲聞諸弟子等，功德已備，堪任修習大乘經典，我於是經，為說六味。云何六味？說苦醋味、無常醎味、無我苦味、樂如甜味、我如辛味、常如淡味。彼世間中有三種味，所謂無常、無我、無樂。煩惱為薪，智慧為火，以是因緣成涅槃飯，謂常、樂、我，令諸弟子悉皆甘嗜。」

復告女人：「汝若有緣欲至他處，應驅惡子，令出其舍，悉以寶藏付示善子。」女人白佛：「實如聖教，珍寶之藏應示善子，不示惡子。」

「姊！我亦如是，般涅槃時，如來微密無上法藏，不與聲聞諸弟子等，如汝寶藏不示惡子；要當付囑諸菩薩等，如汝寶藏委付善子。何以故？聲聞弟子生變異想，謂佛如來真實滅度，然我真實不滅度也。如汝遠行未還之頃，汝之惡子便言汝死，汝實不死。諸菩薩等，說言如來常、不變易，如汝善子，不言汝死。以是義故，我以無上祕密之藏付諸菩薩。」

「善男子，若有眾生，謂佛常住、不變異者，當知是家，則為有佛，是名正他。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"What is correcting others? While the Buddha was teaching the Dharma, a woman nursing an infant came to where the Buddha was, bowed her head to the Buddha's feet, and, with some concern on her mind, sat quietly to one side, absorbed in her own thoughts. The World-Honored One, knowing her thought, asked her anyway: "Out of fondness for your child, have you fed him too much ghee, without judging whether he could digest it?" The woman then said to the Buddha: "How wondrous, World-Honored One! You know exactly what is on my mind. I beg only that the Tathāgata teach me the proper measure. World-Honored One, this morning I gave my child a great deal of ghee, and I fear he may not be able to digest it — could this shorten his life? I beg only that the Tathāgata explain this to me." The Buddha said: "What your child has eaten will digest completely, and will lengthen his life." Hearing this, the woman was filled with great joy, and said further: "Because the Tathāgata has spoken the truth, I now rejoice."

"The World-Honored One acts in just this way: in order to tame and guide beings, he skillfully distinguishes what can be digested from what cannot. He also teaches that all dharmas are without self and impermanent — for if the Buddha, the World-Honored One, were to teach permanence first, those still being taught would say this teaching is no different from the heterodox schools, and would simply leave."

He said further to the woman: "Once your child has grown, and can walk and move about on his own, able to digest even what is hard to digest, the ghee you once gave him will no longer be sufficient. My own śrāvaka disciples are just the same: like your infant, unable to digest this permanent Dharma, and so I first teach suffering and impermanence. But once my śrāvaka disciples have completed their virtues, and are able to bear the cultivation of the Great Vehicle's own scriptures, I then teach them, within this very scripture, the six flavors. What are these six flavors? Suffering, like a sour taste; impermanence, like a salty taste; no-self, like a bitter taste; bliss, like a sweet taste; self, like a pungent taste; permanence, like a bland taste. Within the ordinary world there are only three of these flavors — namely impermanence, no-self, and the absence of bliss. Affliction becomes the firewood, and wisdom the fire, and through this very cause and condition the meal of nirvāṇa is prepared — namely permanence, bliss, and self — and every disciple comes to relish it."

He said further to the woman: "Should you have some errand that takes you elsewhere, you would drive your wayward son out of the house, and entrust your treasury entirely to your worthy son." The woman said to the Buddha: "It is truly as your holy teaching says — a treasury of jewels should be shown to a worthy son, not to a wayward one."

"Sister, I am just the same. At my parinirvāṇa, the Tathāgata's subtle and secret, peerless treasury of the Dharma is not given to the śrāvaka disciples — just as your treasure is not shown to your wayward son — but must instead be entrusted to the bodhisattvas, just as your treasure is entrusted to your worthy son. Why is this? Because the śrāvaka disciples give rise to the notion of change, thinking that the Tathāgata has truly and utterly passed away — when in truth I have not passed away at all. It is like when you go on a long journey and have not yet returned: your wayward son says you have died, though you have in truth not died. The bodhisattvas, however, say that the Tathāgata is permanent and unchanging — like your worthy son, who does not say you have died. For this reason, I entrust the peerless secret treasury to the bodhisattvas."

"Good son, if there are beings who understand that the Buddha abides permanently, without change, know that in that household there is truly a buddha — this is called correcting others."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the nursing mother's dilemma as tracking the exact structure of the Buddha's own teaching career across three distinct stages, not merely illustrating a general principle about pacing. Infancy, matched to milk alone, corresponds to the earliest teaching of suffering and impermanence to disciples not yet able to digest anything sturdier; the child grown enough to eat solid but still-limited food corresponds to the intermediate teaching of the six flavors, already naming permanence, bliss, and self but pairing them symmetrically against their negations rather than asserting them outright; and the grown child no longer served milk at all corresponds to this very sūtra's own direct teaching of permanence, bliss, self, and purity without qualification. He is careful to note what the parable does not license: an interpreter who used it to argue the Buddha's earlier teachings of no-self and impermanence were therefore false, rather than simply incomplete for their intended audience, would be repeating the very error the milk-and-poison parable two fascicles earlier was built to warn against — mistaking a remedy correctly calibrated to one stage of development for a false statement now being corrected, rather than a true statement now being completed.

Answering Whatever Is Asked: The Prohibition of Meat

Chinese Source

「能隨問答者，若有人來問佛世尊：『我當云何不捨錢財，而得名為大施檀越？』佛言：『若有沙門、婆羅門等，少欲知足，不受、不畜不淨物者，當施其人奴婢、僕使。修梵行者，施與女人。斷酒肉者，施以酒肉。不過中食，施過中食。不著花香，施以花香。如是施者，施名流布，遍至他方，財寶之費不失毫釐。』是則名為能隨問答。」

爾時迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！食肉之人不應施肉。何以故？我見不食肉者，有大功德。」

佛讚迦葉：「善哉，善哉！汝今乃能善知我意，護法菩薩應當如是。善男子！從今日始，不聽聲聞弟子食肉，若受檀越信施之時，應觀是食如子肉想。」

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！云何如來不聽食肉？」「善男子！夫食肉者，斷大慈種。」

迦葉又言：「如來何故，先聽比丘食三種淨肉？」「迦葉！是三種淨肉，隨事漸制。」

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！何因緣故，十種不淨乃至九種清淨而復不聽？」佛告迦葉：「亦是因事漸次而制，當知即是現斷肉義。」

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「云何如來稱讚魚肉為美食耶？」「善男子！我亦不說魚肉之屬為美食也，我說甘蔗、粳米、石蜜、一切穀麥及黑石蜜、乳酪、蘇油，以為美食。雖說應畜種種衣服，所應畜者要是壞色，何況貪著是魚肉味。」

迦葉復言：「如來若制不食肉者，彼五種味，乳酪、酪漿、生酥、熟酥、胡麻油等，及諸衣服、憍奢耶衣、珂貝、皮革、金銀盂器，如是等物亦不應受。」

「善男子！不應同彼尼乾所見，如來所制一切禁戒，各有異意。異意故，聽食三種淨肉。異想故，斷十種肉。異想故，一切悉斷，及自死者。迦葉！我從今日制諸弟子，不得復食一切肉也。迦葉！其食肉者，若行、若住、若坐、若臥，一切眾生聞其肉氣，悉生恐怖。譬如有人近師子已，眾人見之，聞師子臭，亦生恐怖。」

「善男子！如人噉蒜，臭穢可惡，餘人見之，聞臭捨去。設遠見者猶不欲視，況當近之？諸食肉者亦復如是，一切眾生聞其肉氣，悉皆恐怖，生畏死想；水陸空行有命之類，悉捨之走，咸言此人，是我等怨。是故菩薩不習食肉，為度眾生，示現食肉，雖現食之，其實不食。善男子！如是菩薩清淨之食猶尚不食，況當食肉？」

爾時迦葉復白佛言：「世尊！諸比丘、比丘尼、優婆塞、優婆夷，因他而活。若乞食時，得雜肉食，云何得食應清淨法？」佛言：「迦葉！當以水洗，令與肉別，然後乃食。若其食器，為肉所污，但使無味，聽用無罪。若見食中多有肉者，則不應受。一切現肉，悉不應食，食者得罪。我今唱是斷肉之制，若廣說者，則不可盡。涅槃時到，是故略說。是則名為能隨問答。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"What is being able to answer whatever is asked? If someone comes to ask the Buddha, the World-Honored One: 'How can I become a great donor without giving away my own wealth?' the Buddha answers: 'If there are śramaṇas or brāhmaṇas of few desires and easy satisfaction, who neither accept nor keep impure possessions, one should give such people servants and attendants. To those who practice pure conduct, give women in marriage instead. To those who abstain from wine and meat, offer wine and meat. To those who do not eat past noon, offer food eaten past noon. To those who do not wear flowers or perfume, offer flowers and perfume.' Giving in this way, one's generosity becomes known and spreads to every land, without the true cost of one's wealth being diminished by so much as a hair's breadth.' This is what is called being able to answer whatever is asked."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa then said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, one should not give meat to a meat-eater. Why is this? Because I have seen that those who do not eat meat possess great merit."

The Buddha praised Kāśyapa: "Excellent, excellent! You have now grasped my own intent well — a bodhisattva who protects the Dharma should be just like this. Good son, from this day forward, I do not permit my śrāvaka disciples to eat meat at all; whenever they receive food offered in faith by a donor, they should regard that food as though it were the flesh of their own child."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, why does the Tathāgata not permit the eating of meat?" "Good son, whoever eats meat cuts off the seed of great loving-kindness."

Kāśyapa said further: "Then why did the Tathāgata previously permit monks to eat the three kinds of pure meat?" "Kāśyapa, these three kinds of pure meat were permitted as a gradual restriction, suited to the circumstances of the time."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, for what reason are the ten kinds of impure meat, and even the nine kinds of pure meat, now also forbidden?" The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "This too was restricted gradually according to circumstance — understand that it points toward the eventual complete prohibition of meat."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "Then why has the Tathāgata praised fish and meat as fine food?" "Good son, I have never said that fish or meat are fine food. I have said that sugarcane, rice, rock sugar, every kind of grain and wheat, dark rock candy, curds, and ghee are fine foods. And though I have said monks may keep various kinds of robes, what may be kept must be of a subdued, undyed color — how much less, then, should one covet the taste of fish or meat?"

Kāśyapa said further: "If the Tathāgata now forbids the eating of meat, then should not the five flavors — milk, whey, fresh butter, clarified butter, and sesame oil — and also silk garments, shells, leather, and vessels of gold and silver, likewise not be accepted?"

"Good son, one should not adopt the view of the Nirgrantha ascetics on this matter. Every precept the Tathāgata has set has its own distinct intent behind it. It was with one intent that I permitted the three kinds of pure meat; with a different intent that I then forbade the ten kinds of meat; and with yet another intent that I now forbid all meat entirely, including that from an animal already dead of natural causes. Kāśyapa, from this day I forbid my disciples to eat any meat whatsoever. Kāśyapa, whoever eats meat — whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down — will cause every being who catches the scent of that meat on him to be filled with fear, just as when someone who has been near a lion passes by, and people, catching the lion's scent on him, are filled with fear as well."

"Good son, it is like a person who has eaten garlic — the smell is foul and repugnant, and others, catching that smell, avoid him and turn away; even seen from a distance, one has no wish to look at him, let alone draw near. Those who eat meat are just the same: every being who catches the scent of meat on them is filled with fear, giving rise to the thought of death — every living creature of water, land, and air flees from them, saying: 'This person is our enemy.' For this reason, a bodhisattva does not practice eating meat; when he does appear to eat it, for the sake of bringing beings across, though he appears to eat it, he does not, in truth, eat it at all. Good son, if a bodhisattva does not even eat food that is genuinely pure in this way, how much less would he eat meat?"

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, monks, nuns, upāsakas, and upāsikās depend on others for their food. If, while begging for alms, they receive food mixed with meat, how can they eat in accordance with the pure Dharma?" The Buddha said: "Kāśyapa, one should wash it with water, separating the meat from the rest, and only then eat it. If the vessel itself has been tainted by meat, so long as no flavor of it remains, one may use it without fault. But if one sees that the food contains a great deal of meat, one should not accept it at all. No meat that is plainly visible as such should ever be eaten; whoever eats it incurs a fault. I have now proclaimed this prohibition of meat; were I to explain it fully, it could not be exhausted. The time of my parinirvāṇa has come, and so I speak of it only briefly. This is what is called being able to answer whatever is asked."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the sequence from permitting the "three kinds of pure meat" (from an animal the monk did not see, hear, or suspect was killed specifically for him) through forbidding ten named kinds of meat, then nine further categories, and finally all meat without exception, as a single continuous line rather than three separate, unrelated rulings — each stage narrowing what came before it, moving in one direction only, toward the final prohibition this fascicle now states outright. He reads Kāśyapa's objection about milk, butter, and other animal products as a serious test of the rule's own coherence, not a rhetorical foil: if meat is forbidden because eating it endangers loving-kindness, why not everything else that comes from an animal? The Buddha's answer — that each precept carries "its own distinct intent" rather than a single mechanically applied principle — is, on Huiyuan's reading, doing real philosophical work: it blocks a slippery-slope argument from meat to milk by insisting that a precept's proper scope is set by the specific harm it addresses (here, killing, and the terror an animal-eater carries with him), not by any generic feature ("comes from an animal") that milk and meat happen to share.

Understanding Causes and Conditions: Why the Precepts Came Gradually

Chinese Source

「迦葉！云何善解因緣義？如有四部之眾來問我言：『世尊！如是之義，如來初出，何故不為波斯匿王，說是法門深妙之義？或時說深、或時說淺、或名為犯、或名不犯？云何名墮？云何名律？云何名波羅提木叉義？』佛言：『波羅提木叉者，名為知足；成就威儀，無所受畜，亦名淨命，墮者名四惡趣。……律者，入戒威儀，深經善義，遮受一切不淨之物及不淨因緣，亦遮四重、十三僧殘、二不定法、三十捨墮、九十一墮、四悔過法、眾多學法、七滅諍等。或復有人盡破一切戒。云何一切？謂四重法乃至七滅諍法。或復有人誹謗正法甚深經典，及一闡提，具足成就盡一切相無有因緣。如是等人，自言我是聰明利智，輕重之罪悉皆覆藏，覆藏諸惡如龜藏六；如是眾罪，長夜不悔，以不悔故，日夜增長。是諸比丘，所犯眾罪，終不發露，是使所犯，遂復滋蔓。是故如來知是事已，漸次而制，不得一時。』」

爾時有善男子、善女人白佛言：『世尊，如來久知如是之事，何不先制？將無世尊欲令眾生入阿鼻獄？譬如多人，欲至他方，迷失正路，隨逐邪道，是諸人等不知迷故，皆謂是道，復不見人可問是非。眾生如是迷於佛法，不見正真，如來應為先說正道，勅諸比丘，此是犯戒，此是持戒，當如是制。何以故？如來正覺是真實者，知見正道，惟有如來天中之天，能說十善增上功德及其義味，是故啟請應先制戒。』」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Kāśyapa, what is understanding well the meaning of causes and conditions? Suppose the fourfold assembly comes to ask me: 'World-Honored One, regarding this very teaching — why, when the Tathāgata first appeared, did he not immediately teach King Prasenajit this profound and subtle meaning? Why is it sometimes taught deeply, and sometimes only shallowly? Why is something at one time called an offense, and at another time not an offense? What does it mean to "fall"? What is meant by the Vinaya? What is meant by the prātimokṣa?' The Buddha answers: 'The prātimokṣa means knowing contentment; being complete in one's bearing and keeping nothing improper; it is also called pure livelihood. To "fall" means falling into the four evil destinies… The Vinaya is that which accords with the precepts and proper bearing, and with the profound and excellent meaning of the scriptures, forbidding the acceptance of every impure object and every impure circumstance, and forbidding as well the four grave offenses, the thirteen saṅghāvaśeṣa offenses, the two indeterminate offenses, the thirty offenses requiring forfeiture, the ninety-one offenses requiring confession, the four offenses requiring acknowledgment, the many rules of training, and the seven ways of settling disputes. There are some who break every one of these precepts entirely — what is meant by "every one"? The four grave offenses, down through the seven ways of settling disputes. And there are some who slander the profound scriptures of the true Dharma, becoming icchantikas, fully embodying the view that every mark whatsoever arises without any cause or condition at all. Such people, calling themselves clever and sharp-witted, conceal every offense, whether light or grave, hiding their every evil deed the way a tortoise draws in its six limbs; such offenses, through a long night of never being confessed, only grow and multiply day and night. These monks, never bringing their own many transgressions into the open, allow what they have transgressed to spread and proliferate further still. This is why the Tathāgata, knowing all this, set the precepts gradually, one by one, rather than all at once.'"

At that time a certain good son and good daughter said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, since the Tathāgata has long known of these things, why did he not set out the precepts beforehand? Could it be that the World-Honored One actually wishes beings to fall into the Avīci hell? It is like a great crowd of people traveling toward some other place, who lose the true road and follow instead a false path, not realizing they have gone astray, each supposing this false path to be the right one, with no one else in sight to ask whether it is right or wrong. Beings are just the same, lost within the Buddha-dharma, unable to see what is true and correct — the Tathāgata should have taught the true path first, instructing the monks in advance: 'This is a transgression of the precepts, this is keeping them,' setting out the rule beforehand. Why is this? Because the Tathāgata, the Perfectly Awakened One, alone knows and sees the true path as it really is; only the Tathāgata, a god among gods, is able to teach the ten wholesome deeds and their surpassing virtue, along with their true meaning — and this is why we now ask that the precepts should have been set beforehand."

The Buddha said: "Good son, if it is said that the Tathāgata is able to proclaim to beings the ten wholesome deeds and their surpassing virtue, then the Tathāgata already regards every being as he regards Rāhula — how, then, can it be objected that the World-Honored One might actually wish beings to fall into hell? Seeing even a single person with the cause and condition for falling into the Avīci hell, I would remain in the world for that person's sake for a whole kalpa, or at least for less than a kalpa. I hold great compassion for beings — for what reason would I ever deceive those I regard as my own children, and lead them into hell? Good son, it is like a tailor in a king's own realm, who mends a garment only once he sees where it has a hole. The Tathāgata is just the same: on seeing that beings have the cause and condition for falling into the Avīci hell, he then mends that very fault with the good of the precepts. Good son, it is like a wheel-turning sage-king who first teaches beings the ten wholesome deeds; only afterward, as some gradually begin to do evil, does the king, according to each circumstance, gradually forbid it, and only once every evil has been forbidden does he himself then practice the sage-king's own Dharma. Good son, I am just the same: though I have already taught much, I could not set out every precept beforehand — it was only as the monks gradually engaged in improper conduct that I then set out each precept according to the circumstance. Beings who delight in the Dharma, practicing in accordance with this teaching, are the ones who are able to see the Tathāgata's own Dharma-body." Just as the wheel-treasure possessed by a wheel-turning king is beyond conceptual thought, the Tathāgata is likewise beyond conceptual thought; the two treasures of the Dharma and the Saṅgha are likewise beyond conceptual thought; both the one who teaches the Dharma and the one who hears it are beyond conceptual thought — this is what is called understanding well the meaning of causes and conditions. A bodhisattva who can distinguish and expound these four marks in this way is said to have understood the meaning of causes and conditions within the Great Vehicle's own great parinirvāṇa."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the objection voiced here — that a truly all-knowing teacher should have announced every rule in advance, sparing the community the harm caused by transgressions committed before any rule against them existed — as the strongest available challenge to the entire practice of gradually promulgated Vinaya law, and credits the Buddha's answer with meeting it on its own terms rather than deflecting it. His gloss on the tailor simile is precise about the logic: a tailor who patched every possible future tear in a garment before any tear appeared would not be demonstrating foresight but wasting the very cloth meant to serve as the patch — a rule against a transgression no one has yet conceived of teaches nothing, since it has no failure to correct and no living case to make its point vivid. The wheel-turning king simile adds the further claim that this pacing is not particular to the Buddha's own limitation but the general shape moral authority takes whenever it is meant to be lived rather than merely recited: the king teaches the ten wholesome deeds first, in the affirmative and without qualification, and only carves out specific prohibitions once specific violations show him exactly what needs forbidding — a sequence Huiyuan reads as inseparable from how any community actually comes to internalize a rule as its own, rather than as an external imposition handed down complete before there was ever a reason to feel its weight.

Is the Tathāgata Extinguished Like Scattered Sparks?

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "Suppose a good son or good daughter says: 'The Tathāgata is impermanent. How can this be known? For the Buddha has himself said: "The extinguishing of every affliction is called nirvāṇa, just as when a fire goes out, nothing at all remains — the extinguishing of every affliction is just the same, and this is why it is called nirvāṇa." How, then, can the Tathāgata be the permanently abiding, unchanging Dharma?… As the Buddha has also said: "It is like a piece of heated iron, struck so that sparks fly out — once scattered, they vanish at once, and no one knows where they have gone. True liberation is just the same: having crossed beyond the mire of lust and every state of existence, one attains the unmoving place, and no one knows where one has gone." How, then, can the Tathāgata be the permanently abiding, unchanging Dharma?' Kāśyapa, whoever raises such an objection is raising a false objection. Kāśyapa, you too should not imagine that the Tathāgata's own nature is utter extinction. Kāśyapa, the extinguishing of affliction is not itself a thing at all. Why is this? Because it is ultimate and final — this is precisely why it is called permanent; this very phrase means stillness, and has nothing higher than it. The extinguishing of every mark, leaving nothing whatsoever behind, is a phrase meaning purity itself, permanently abiding and never regressing. This is why nirvāṇa is called permanent abiding, and the Tathāgata is just the same, permanently abiding, without change. The 'flying sparks' spoken of here refer to affliction. 'Once scattered, they vanish at once, and no one knows where they have gone' means that once every buddha's affliction is extinguished, it no longer exists within any of the five destinies. This is why the Tathāgata is the permanently abiding Dharma, without any change. Furthermore, Kāśyapa, that which every buddha takes as teacher is the Dharma itself; this is why the Tathāgata reveres and honors it — and because the Dharma is permanent, every buddha is likewise permanent."

The Tathāgata's own affliction would be just the same, extinguished and going nowhere at all. And just as that iron's heat and red glow, once extinguished, no longer exist, the Tathāgata would be just the same: once extinguished, he becomes impermanent — extinguishing the fire of affliction, he simply enters nirvāṇa. The Tathāgata is not like this: once extinguished, it never arises again in him — this is why he is called permanent."

Kāśyapa said further: "But once iron's red glow is extinguished, if it is placed back into the fire, that red glow arises again. If the Tathāgata is like this, his own afflictions should likewise arise again — and if they arise again, this is precisely impermanence." The Buddha said: "Kāśyapa, you should not now speak this way, that the Tathāgata is impermanent. Why is this? Because the Tathāgata is permanent. Good son, it is like burning a piece of wood: once it is consumed, ash remains. Once affliction is extinguished, nirvāṇa comes to be. The similes of the torn garment, the severed head, and the shattered vessel are all just the same — each of these things, once it happens, is given its own name: a torn garment, a severed head, a shattered vessel. Kāśyapa, iron, once cooled, can be made hot again — but the Tathāgata is not like this: having cut off affliction, he is forever, finally cool, and the blazing fire of affliction never arises in him again. Kāśyapa, you should know that countless beings are like that iron, and it is with the blazing fire of undefiled wisdom that I burn away the knots of affliction in those beings."

Kāśyapa said further: "Excellent, excellent! I now truly understand what the Tathāgata has taught — that every buddha is permanent." The Buddha said: "Kāśyapa, it is like a sage-king who ordinarily dwells within his inner palace, but at times goes out to wander in his rear garden; though the king is then not among his court ladies, one still cannot say that the sage-king has died. Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same: though he does not appear within the land of Jambudvīpa, entering instead into nirvāṇa, he should not be called impermanent for this. The Tathāgata, having emerged from immeasurable affliction, enters the place of ease and bliss that is nirvāṇa, wandering among flowers of awakening, delighting in joy."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan traces Kāśyapa's three successive attempts to press the hot-iron simile toward impermanence, and the Buddha's answer to each, as a single argument advancing in careful stages rather than the Buddha simply repeating himself under pressure. Kāśyapa's first challenge treats the extinguished spark itself as the vanishing Tathāgata; the Buddha answers by relocating what the simile actually names — the spark stands for affliction being extinguished, not for the Tathāgata's own being. Kāśyapa's second challenge grants this but presses further: an ordinary flame, once out, can always be relit, so why should the Buddha's own "extinguishing" be any different in kind? Huiyuan reads the Buddha's answer here as the passage's real hinge: the iron itself stands only for ordinary beings, whose afflictions return because their root cause has not been removed, while what has happened in a buddha is a different order of event entirely — burnt wood does not un-burn back into wood, a severed head does not reattach, a shattered vessel does not un-shatter, precisely because each of these names a genuine, non-reversible transformation into something new rather than a temporary, reversible state like heat in metal. Kāśyapa's third exchange, where he finally accepts the teaching, sets up the king-in-the-garden simile that follows immediately: having secured agreement that the Buddha's own cessation of affliction cannot recur, the text pivots from arguing that the Tathāgata does not un-become permanent to arguing that entering nirvāṇa was never really a departure from anywhere in the first place.

Good son, there is likewise a bodhisattva-mahāsattva who abides in this great nirvāṇa and is able to sever and lift up every immeasurable buddha-world throughout all ten directions, drawing them entirely within his own body, without the beings inside feeling any confinement, or any sense of having moved or of dwelling anywhere different at all; only those ready to be brought across can perceive it, and the same is true when he restores them again… A bodhisattva-mahāsattva who abides in this great nirvāṇa is thus able to manifest every kind of immeasurable spiritual transformation — this is why it is called the great parinirvāṇa. No being at all is able to measure such immeasurable spiritual transformations as these bodhisattva-mahāsattvas can manifest — how, then, could you presume to know that the Tathāgata engaged in sexual desire, and thereby fathered Rāhula?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the cascade of cosmic manifestations here — Sumeru fit into a mustard-seed husk, the trichiliocosm likewise, whole buddha-worlds strung on a needle's point, an entire universe drawn into a single body, each accomplished without the beings inside noticing any change at all — as making a single deliberate point about scale before the argument turns to Rāhula: these bodhisattvas can rearrange the fundamental furniture of the cosmos without disturbance to anyone inside it, precisely because the samādhi power involved acts without needing to move anything the way an ordinary force would. If a bodhisattva who has merely attained a share of this state can already do this, Huiyuan asks rhetorically, what warrant does anyone have to assume the Buddha's own conduct in the palace at Kapilavastu — his marriage, Rāhula's birth — must have proceeded by the same causal mechanics an ordinary person's would? The argument is not that the historical events did not occur in some observable sense (the text does not deny Rāhula was born), but that inferring "the Buddha genuinely experienced desire" from "the Buddha appeared to marry and father a son" commits exactly the error the mustard-seed and Sumeru images were just built to rule out — assuming that what is observed from outside must match, mechanism for mechanism, what is actually happening in a being whose capacities have already been shown to exceed ordinary causal expectation entirely.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this catalog as organized around a single recurring grammatical shape — "I manifest X; everyone supposes I am X; yet in truth I am not X" — applied first to an ascending sequence of ordinary biographical milestones (birth, the seven steps, hair-shaving, schooling, princely pleasure, renunciation, the fruits up through arhatship, the defeat of Māra beneath the Bodhi tree) and then, more startlingly, to a descending sequence of the very worst things a Buddhist figure could be accused of being: a precept-breaker, an icchantika, a schismatic who splits the saṅgha, Māra himself, a visitor to a brothel. He treats the second sequence as making the passage's most important point, one the first sequence alone could not: if the principle "manifesting X does not entail being X" held only for admirable milestones, a skeptical reader could suspect the whole passage of simply flattering the Buddha's biography by relabeling ordinary human experiences as performances. Placing the four grave offenses, the icchantika, and Devadatta-style schism inside the very same grammatical frame as the seven steps at birth forces the principle to bear its full theoretical weight — and, on Huiyuan's reading, is doing double duty for this fascicle's central concerns: the icchantika clause in particular, following directly on Part 1's quiet exclusion of the icchantika from the assembly's universal loving-kindness, is the sūtra's first move toward its later, more systematic argument that the icchantika designation names a temporary condition rather than a fixed, unchangeable nature — since here it is applied, and then immediately retracted, even in the case of the Buddha's own manifested conduct.

Kāśyapa said further: "How, then, can the Tathāgata be called permanently abiding? For the Buddha has said: 'Just as a lamp, once extinguished, has no place it has gone to, the Tathāgata is just the same — once he has entered parinirvāṇa, he too has no place he has gone to.'"

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "Good son, you should not now say: 'Just as a lamp, once utterly extinguished, has no place it has gone to, the Tathāgata is just the same — once he has entered parinirvāṇa, he has no place he has gone to.' That going-out of the light is a likeness for the extinguishing of affliction — the light may be entirely extinguished, but the lamp's own vessel still remains. The Tathāgata is just the same: though affliction is extinguished, the Dharma-body remains, permanently. Good son, what do you think — do the light and the lamp's vessel go out together, or not?" Kāśyapa answered: "No, World-Honored One. Though they do not go out together, the vessel is still impermanent. If the Dharma-body is likened to the lamp's vessel, then, since the lamp's vessel is impermanent, the Dharma-body should likewise be impermanent."

"Good son, you should not raise this objection. Just as one speaks of an ordinary 'vessel' in the world, the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, is a peerless vessel of the Dharma — but it is the vessel that is impermanent, not the Tathāgata himself. Among all dharmas, nirvāṇa alone is permanent, and it is because the Tathāgata embodies it that he is called permanent. Furthermore, good son, when I spoke of the lamp going out, I meant the nirvāṇa realized by an arhat, likened to a lamp going out because craving and every affliction have been extinguished in him. A non-returner (anāgāmin), however, is said still to have some trace of craving remaining, and so cannot properly be said to be like a lamp going out. This is why, in the past, I spoke only obliquely, using the likeness of a lamp going out — it was never the great nirvāṇa itself that I meant to liken to a lamp going out. A non-returner is one who no longer returns again and again to the desire realm, and does not return to any of the twenty-five states of existence, no longer taking on a foul body, a worm-ridden body, a body that eats, or a poisoned body — this is what is called a non-returner. Whoever takes on a body again is called a 'returner'; whoever does not take on a body again is called a 'non-returner'; whoever still goes and comes is called a 'returner'; whoever no longer goes or comes is called a 'non-returner.'"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the lamp simile's resolution as turning on a distinction the Sanskrit-to-Chinese translation itself risks flattening: "the light going out" and "the lamp going out" are not the same event, even though the original teaching used a single word, "lamp," loosely enough to seem to identify them. Once the vessel and its flame are separated, Kāśyapa's own objection — that the vessel is impermanent, so the Dharma-body it illustrates should be too — is answered by pointing out that the vessel was never doing the illustrating in the first place; it was there only to hold the light steady long enough to show that the light's own extinguishing leaves something behind rather than nothing. Huiyuan reads the passage's closing distinction between the arhat (to whom the lamp simile properly applies, since craving is genuinely and fully extinguished) and the non-returner (who still carries some remainder, and so was never a clean fit for the simile even in its original, more limited teaching context) as a quiet correction of an imprecision in how the Buddha's own earlier teaching had been received: "I spoke only obliquely" (覆相說) concedes that the earlier phrasing invited exactly the misreading Kāśyapa has just voiced, without withdrawing the earlier teaching's real point — that the extinguishing described was always meant to apply narrowly, to the destruction of a specific affliction, never as a claim about the Tathāgata's own being ceasing to exist.
