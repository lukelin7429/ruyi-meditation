The King Who Could Not Sleep

梵行品's sixth and final practice, sick conduct, opens with the sūtra's central illustration of it: King Ajātaśatru, having murdered his own father to seize the throne, breaks out in sores that no physician's medicine can reach, because the wound is remorse itself. Six ministers each bring him a different heterodox teacher's promise that the killing carries no consequence at all — no karma, no self to be harmed, no law above a king. The king's own physician, Jīvaka, answers not with a seventh denial but with an argument for facing the guilt honestly: two white dharmas, shame and remorse, that can save even this; a catalogue of everyone the Buddha has ever healed, however far gone; and a case, built one broken person at a time, that the true physician is still waiting twelve leagues away. The fascicle closes as a voice from the sky, warning that the Dharma itself is dying, reveals that it belongs to the king's own murdered father.

The Wound That Medicine Cannot Heal

Chinese Source

◎爾時王舍大城阿闍世王，其性弊惡，憙行殺戮，具口四惡，貪恚愚癡，其心熾盛，唯見現在、不見未來，純以惡人而為眷屬，貪著現世五欲樂故，父王無辜橫加逆害。因害父已，心生悔熱，身諸瓔珞伎樂不御，心悔熱故遍體生瘡，其瘡臭穢不可附近。尋自念言：「我今此身已受花報，地獄果報將近不遠。」爾時其母字韋提希，以種種藥而為傅之，其瘡遂增，無有降損。王即白母：「如是瘡者，從心而生，非四大起。若言眾生有能治者，無有是處。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, King Ajātaśatru of the great city of Rājagṛha was, by nature, vicious, delighting in killing and slaughter, possessed of all four verbal evils, and consumed by greed, hatred, and delusion, his mind so ablaze that he saw only the present and never the future; he kept company solely with evil men, and, craving the pleasures of the five desires in this present life, he wrongfully turned against his own father, the king, who was entirely innocent, and had him killed. Having killed his father, a feverish remorse arose in his mind; he no longer wore his jeweled ornaments or called for music; and, because his mind burned with this remorse, sores broke out over his entire body, foul and stinking, so that no one could bear to come near him. He thought to himself: "This body of mine has already received its flowering retribution — the fruiting retribution of hell itself cannot now be far off." His mother, whose name was Vaidehī, applied every kind of medicine to the sores, and yet they only worsened, never lessening at all. The king said to his mother: "A sore of this kind arises from the mind itself, not from the four elements. Should anyone claim that any being is able to cure it, this could not possibly be so."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this fascicle as an extended illustration of "sick conduct" (病行), the sixth and last of the five practices this collection first listed back in Part 11's opening of 聖行品 — and notes, pointedly, that the sūtra itself never labels any passage here with that name, nor closes with a summary saying so, the way it does for the other practices. His answer to why: some teachings are stated directly, and some are "shown through an event" (寄事表法) — and where a teaching works by narrative demonstration rather than direct assertion, the sūtra simply lets the story do its work and moves on, without framing it. The clue that this is what is happening, on his reading, is that the entire passage is, quite literally, the treatment of an illness — Ajātaśatru's own psychosomatic wound, which no ordinary medicine reaches because its cause is not physical at all.

He divides the king's opening condition into two layers, worth keeping distinct: first, the three underlying evils already present in his character — bodily viciousness, the four verbal faults, and a mind ablaze with greed, hatred, and delusion; and second, the specific act built from these, the patricide itself, which he traces to the same three roots (a mind "ablaze" names the hatred; seeing only the present and keeping evil company names the delusion; craving the pleasures of the present names the greed). The king's own diagnosis — "this arises from the mind, not from the four elements" — Huiyuan reads as evidence that the king already possesses real insight into his own condition, even before any of the six ministers or Jīvaka arrives to counsel him; what he lacks is not understanding that the wound is spiritual, but any correct account of what to do about it, which is exactly what the following ministers, one after another, fail to supply.

Three Ministers, Three Denials of Consequence

Chinese Source

時有大臣名曰月稱，往至王所，在一面立，白言：「大王！何故愁悴，顏容不悅？為身痛耶？為心痛乎？」王即答言：「我今身心，豈得不痛？我父無辜，橫加逆害。我從智者曾聞是義，世有五人，不脫地獄，謂五逆罪。我今已有無量無邊阿僧祇罪，云何身心而得不痛？又無良醫治我身心。」臣言：「大王！莫大愁苦。」即說偈言：「若常愁苦，愁遂增長。如人憙眠，眠則滋多。貪婬嗜酒，亦復如是。「如王所言，世有五人，不脫地獄；誰往見之，來語王耶？言地獄者，即是世間，多智者說。如王所言，世無良醫治身心者，今有大醫名富蘭那，一切知見，得自在定，畢竟修習清淨梵行，常為無量無邊眾生，演說無上涅槃之道。為諸弟子說如是法，無有黑業、無黑業報，無有白業、無白業報，無黑白業、無黑白業報，無有上業及以下業。是師今在王舍城中，唯願大王。屈駕往彼，可令是師，療治身心。」時王答言：「審能如是滅除我罪，我當歸依。」◎

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, there was a great minister named Candraprabha, who went to where the king was, stood to one side, and said: "Great king, why are you so downcast and unwell in appearance? Is it your body that pains you, or your mind?" The king answered: "How could my body and mind not pain me? My father, entirely innocent, I wrongfully turned against and had killed. I have heard from the wise that there are, in this world, five kinds of persons who cannot escape hell — those guilty of the five gravest offenses. I have now committed immeasurable, boundless, incalculable such crimes — how could my body and mind not pain me? And I have no good physician to heal my body and mind." The minister said: "Great king, do not give yourself over to such great sorrow." And he spoke this verse: "Should one forever give way to sorrow, / that sorrow only increases and grows. / It is like a person who delights in sleep — / the more he sleeps, the more sleep he craves. / Craving lust and delighting in wine / are both exactly the same. As for what the great king has said, that there are five kinds of persons in this world who cannot escape hell — who has gone there and seen it, and come back to tell the king so? To speak of 'hell' is simply something that clever talkers among the world say. As for what the great king has said, that the world has no good physician to heal body and mind — there is now a great physician named Pūraṇa, who possesses all knowledge and vision, has attained mastery in meditative concentration, has, in the end, cultivated a pure and holy conduct, and forever expounds, for immeasurable, boundless beings, the path of unsurpassed nirvāṇa. He teaches his disciples a dharma of this kind: there is no dark karma and no fruit of dark karma; there is no bright karma and no fruit of bright karma; there is no karma both dark and bright and no fruit of karma both dark and bright; there is no superior karma and no inferior karma at all. This teacher now dwells within the city of Rājagṛha — I beg the great king to bend his own journey and go to him, so that this teacher may heal your body and mind." The king answered: "Should he truly be able to destroy my crime in this way, I shall then take refuge in him."

Ru-Yi Translation

There was another minister named Treasury-Virtue, who likewise went to the king, and spoke thus: "Great king, why is your face so haggard, your lips so parched, your voice so faint, your very complexion cracked and drawn, as though a timid man had seen a great and mortal enemy? What is it that troubles you so — is it your body, or your mind?" The king answered: "How could my body and mind not pain me? Blind with folly, lacking any eye of wisdom, I drew near to evil companions and made them my intimates, and, following the words of the wicked man Devadatta, wrongfully turned against the king of the true Dharma and had him killed. I once heard a wise man recite this verse: 'Should one give rise to an unwholesome thought toward one's parents, toward the Buddha, or toward his disciples, and carry out an evil act on account of it, the fruit of such a deed lies in the Avīci hell.' It is on account of this very matter that my mind is filled with terror and great anguish, and I have no good physician to come to my rescue." The great minister said further: "I beg the great king, for now, not to give way to sorrow and terror. There are two kinds of law: the law of the renunciant, and the law of the king. As for the law of the king — killing one's own father in order to rule the kingdom, though it is called a transgression, in truth carries no crime at all; it is like the kalalā insect, which must destroy its mother's own womb before it can be born — such is simply the nature of its birth, and, though it breaks its mother's body, in truth this, too, carries no crime. It is exactly the same with a mule's own pregnancy. The law that governs a kingdom is, by its very nature, of this kind — though one kills his own father or brother, in truth there is no crime in it at all. It is only the law of the renunciant where killing even a mosquito or an ant carries a crime. I beg the great king to ease his mind and not give way to sorrow. Why is this? 'Should one forever give way to sorrow, / that sorrow only increases and grows. / It is like a person who delights in sleep — / the more he sleeps, the more sleep he craves. / Craving lust and delighting in wine / are both exactly the same.' As for what the great king has said, that the world has no good physician to heal body and mind — there is now a great teacher named Maskarī Gośālīputra, who possesses all knowledge and vision, who has compassion for beings as though they were his own newborn children, who has already left affliction behind, and who is able to draw out the three poisons' own sharp arrows from beings. Not a single being possesses any knowledge, vision, or awareness with regard to any dharma at all — he alone possesses this knowledge, vision, and awareness. A teacher of this kind forever teaches his disciples a dharma of this kind: 'Every being's own body consists of seven parts. What are these seven? Earth, water, fire, wind, suffering, pleasure, and life. These seven dharmas are neither conjured nor made, and cannot be destroyed or harmed at all. Like the īṣīkā grass, they abide, unmoving; like Mount Sumeru, they are neither abandoned nor produced; like curds and whey, each remains apart from the others, without contention. Whether in suffering or in pleasure, whether wholesome or unwholesome, should a sharp blade be thrust into them, nothing at all is harmed. Why is this? Because these seven parts, being empty like space, admit of no obstruction. Life, too, cannot be harmed. Why is this? Because there exists no one who harms, and no one who dies. There is no action and no receiving of its fruit, no speaking and no hearing, no one who thinks and no one who teaches. He forever teaches this dharma, and is able to cause beings to destroy and remove every immeasurable, grave crime.' This teacher now dwells within the great city of Rājagṛha — I beg the great king to go to where he is; should the king see him, every crime shall be extinguished." The king answered: "Should he truly be able to remove my crime in this way, I shall then take refuge in him."

There was another minister, named True-Attainment, who likewise came to the king, and spoke this verse: "Great king, why have you cast off your jeweled ornaments, and let your hair fall into such disarray, to such a degree as this? Why does the king's own body tremble so, unable to find peace, like a fierce wind shaking the blossoming trees? Why is the king's own complexion so sorrowful and drawn today — as sorrowful as a farmer's, after he has sown his seed, when the sky will not send down rain? Is it your mind that pains you, or your body?" The king answered: "How could my body and mind not pain me? My father, the former king, in his compassion and affection, showed me particular tenderness and love, and was, in truth, entirely without fault — and yet, when he once went to ask a physiognomist, the physiognomist answered him: 'This child, once born, is fated certainly to kill his own father.' Though he heard these words, he still raised and cared for me all the same. I once heard a wise man speak in this way: 'Whoever violates his own mother, or a nun, or steals property belonging to the Saṅgha, or kills one who has given rise to the unsurpassed bodhi-mind, or kills his own father — such a person will, in the end, most certainly fall into the Avīci hell.' How could my body and mind not pain me now?" The great minister said further: "I beg the great king, for now, not to give way to sorrow. Should one's father have been a cultivator of liberation, killing him would indeed carry a crime; but under the law that governs a kingdom, such a killing carries no crime at all. Great king — what is unlawful is called unlawful, and what has no dharma at all is called dharma-less. It is like calling a man 'sonless' when he has no son, and likewise calling a man 'sonless' when he has only a wicked son — though he is called sonless, in truth he is not without a son at all. It is like calling food 'saltless' when it lacks salt, and calling it 'saltless,' too, when it has only a little salt. It is like calling a river 'waterless' when it lacks water, and calling it 'waterless,' too, when it has only a little water. It is like calling something impermanent because it perishes, thought after thought, and calling it impermanent, too, even should it endure for an entire kalpa. It is like calling a person 'joyless' when he undergoes suffering, and calling him 'joyless,' too, even when he experiences a little joy. It is like calling something 'selfless' when it lacks mastery over itself, and calling it 'selfless,' too, even when it has a little mastery. It is like calling it 'sunless' in the darkness of night, and calling it 'sunless,' too, at a time of cloud and mist. Great king, though something is said to have but a little of a dharma, and is therefore called dharma-less, in truth it is not without dharma at all. I beg the king to attend closely to what his servant is about to say: every being possesses residual karma of every kind, and, on account of the conditions of this karma, undergoes birth and death again and again. Should your former father, the king, have possessed residual karma of his own, what crime at all could there be in the present king's having killed him? I beg the great king to ease his mind and not give way to sorrow. Why is this? 'Should one forever give way to sorrow, / that sorrow only increases and grows. / It is like a person who delights in sleep — / the more he sleeps, the more sleep he craves. / Craving lust and delighting in wine / are both exactly the same.' As for what the great king has said, that the world has no good physician to heal body and mind — there is now a great teacher named Sañjaya Vairaṭīputra, who possesses all knowledge and vision, whose wisdom is as deep and profound as the great ocean itself, who possesses great and awesome virtue, is endowed with great spiritual powers, and is able to free beings from every net of doubt. Not a single being possesses knowledge, vision, or awareness — he alone possesses this knowledge, vision, and awareness. He now dwells nearby, within the city of Rājagṛha, and teaches his disciples a dharma of this kind: 'Among all beings, whoever is a king acts entirely as he pleases, freely doing good or evil — and, though he commits every evil, none of it carries any crime whatsoever. It is like fire, which, in burning something, makes no distinction between the pure and the impure — the king is exactly the same, sharing fire's own nature. It is like the great earth, which bears the pure and the impure alike, and, in doing so, feels neither anger nor joy from the start — the king is exactly the same, sharing the earth's own nature. It is like the nature of water, which washes the pure and the impure both alike, and, in doing so, feels neither sorrow nor joy — the king is exactly the same, sharing water's own nature. It is like the nature of wind, which blows equally upon the pure and the impure, and, in doing so, feels neither sorrow nor joy — the king is exactly the same, sharing wind's own nature. It is like a tree stripped bare in autumn, which grows again once spring returns — though it was stripped and cut, in truth this carries no crime at all. Every being is exactly the same: dying here in this place, it is reborn again in this very same place — and, since it is reborn again, what crime at all could there be in this? The fruits of suffering and pleasure that every being undergoes are none of them caused by karma of the present life at all; their cause lies in the past, and it is in the present that their fruit is received. The present has no cause, and the future has no fruit. It is on account of this present fruit that beings uphold the precepts and diligently practice with vigor, so as to block off the evil fruit of the present; and, through upholding the precepts, they attain what is free of outflow, and, having attained what is free of outflow, they exhaust every karma subject to outflow, and, through exhausting this karma, every suffering comes to its end, and, through the ending of every suffering, they attain liberation.' I beg the great king to hasten to where he dwells, and let him heal the suffering of your body and mind — should the king see him, every crime shall then be removed." The king answered: "Should there truly be such a teacher, able to remove my crime, I shall then take refuge in him."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan identifies the six ministers as disciples, one apiece, of the historical "six heterodox teachers" (六師) — the non-Buddhist teachers the canon repeatedly names as the Buddha's own contemporaries — and gives each a one-phrase doctrinal label that clarifies what is actually being rejected in each case. Candraprabha's teacher, Pūraṇa Kāśyapa, holds the "view of emptiness" (空見): no karma is dark or bright, no act bears any fruit at all, which is why his teaching answers the king's fear of the five gravest offenses by denying that moral cause-and-effect exists in the first place. Treasury-Virtue's teacher, Maskarī Gośālīputra, holds the "view of eternalism" (常見): beings are built from seven indestructible, uncreated elements — including, notably, suffering and pleasure and life itself, listed as elements alongside earth, water, fire, and wind — so that no blade can harm what is already eternal and unchanging, which is a different route to the same practical conclusion (no real killer, no real killed). True-Attainment's teacher, Sañjaya Vairaṭīputra, holds "the view of a single cause" (一因見): whatever a person suffers now was caused entirely in the past, so a present act carries no present fruit, which lets the minister argue, alongside his separate claim that royal law and renunciant law simply differ, that the king's father must already have had residual karma calling for this very death.

Structurally, Huiyuan notes that all six ministerial visits follow an identical four-part pattern he traces through each one: the minister asks after the king's health, the king answers with his pain and names his lack of a physician, the minister opens the king's heart with an argument and then names his recommended teacher's teaching and location, and the king answers with the same tentative, conditional refrain — "should he truly be able to remove my crime, I shall take refuge in him" — a hedge, not an assent, that Huiyuan reads as evidence the king already senses something is wrong even as he cannot yet say what. On the fourth minister's argument specifically — the long chain of examples ("sonless," "saltless," "waterless," "impermanent," "joyless," "selfless," "sunless") used to prove that "a small amount of X is still properly called 'no X'" — Huiyuan treats this as sound reasoning applied to a false case: the logic itself is unobjectionable, but it is being used to argue that the king's father's supposed "residual karma," however slight, licenses calling the killing blameless, which is exactly the kind of argument this fascicle exists to expose as false comfort rather than refute point by point.

The remaining three teachers, per Huiyuan, complete the same six-fold catalogue: Knower-of-Meaning's teacher, Ajita Keśakambalī, holds the "view of naturalism" (自然見) — good and evil are illusory distinctions a mind of true equanimity sees through entirely, which is why his teaching is framed as radical non-discrimination (gold and dirt, friend and foe, cutting flesh and anointing it with sandalwood, all literally indistinguishable) rather than as a denial of consequence through some other mechanism; Auspicious-Virtue's teacher, Kakuda Kātyāyana, holds the "view that Īśvara is the cause" (自在天因見) — every being's fate is authored entirely by a controlling deity, reducing beings to a craftsman's puppets with no moral agency of their own to be praised or blamed; and Fearless's teacher, Nirgrantha Jñātiputra, holds "the view that no cultivation is required" (不須修道外道) — liberation arrives automatically after a fixed span of time (eighty thousand kalpas) regardless of what anyone does or fails to do, which is arguably the most complete denial of the whole structure of practice this sūtra has spent eighteen fascicles building, since it makes even correct effort pointless rather than merely making incorrect effort harmless.

Huiyuan flags one line for special caution: "north of the Gaṅgā, giving to beings brings no merit; south of the Gaṅgā, killing beings brings no crime," attributed to Ajita's teaching — he explicitly warns the reader not to strain for a hidden meaning here ("蓋且言耳，不勞異釋"), treating it as simply an arbitrary illustration of "no consequence attaches to any act at all, regardless of geography," rather than a claim requiring doctrinal unpacking. He also notes that the fifth minister's argument contains an internal contradiction worth registering rather than resolving: it first argues that killing carries no crime because a kṣatriya king may act freely for the good of the realm, and then argues, on a wholly different basis, that killing carries no crime because "life" is merely a name for the motion of wind and cannot be killed at all — two arguments from two different metaphysical premises, stacked together as though they were one, which is itself part of what these six ministers' teachings share: each borrows freely from whatever argument is most convenient in the moment, with no concern for whether it coheres with the argument offered just before it.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan parses this poem into an eleven-and-a-half-verse account of who sleeps well, followed by six verses on who does not, and reads the first half as itself structured in two tiers: four verses on how a buddha sleeps (severing affliction, attaining nirvāṇa and expounding it, keeping the three actions pure, and being free of both feverish affliction and its resulting torment), followed by four-and-a-half verses on how a bodhisattva sleeps (freedom from the three karmic actions across three verses, plus one further verse combining freedom from remaining affliction and suffering, then a half-verse of altruistic action turning through saṃsāra for others' sake) — with the closing two verses ("who attains a peaceful sleep? it is every buddha... it is one of loving-kindness and compassion") functioning as the poem's own explicit summary, naming outright the two figures the preceding verses had only described.

On the second half, he splits the six verses evenly: the first three name states of active evil that prevent sleep (delusion producing wrongdoing, greed producing wrongdoing, evil companionship producing wrongdoing), and the last three name states of unresolved suffering that do the same, working through a schema of seven kinds of suffering the sūtra has used before — illness, the fear of death implicit in "transgression against the king," and five distinct varieties of the suffering of not getting what one wants (unripe precepts, an unsucceeded throne, unclaimed plunder, and the like), each an image of a desire still held in suspension rather than of pain already inflicted. Huiyuan's broader point in laying out this structure is that the king's own recitation — offered, ironically, in direct answer to Jīvaka's simple question about sleep — is itself proof that the king already knows, in exhaustive and organized detail, exactly what peace would require and exactly why he does not have it; the six ministers gave him permission to stop asking the question, and this poem shows that permission never actually worked.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the king's own six-part confession here — the fish on dry land, the snared deer, the man who knows his own death is near, the exiled king, the man told his illness is incurable, the precept-breaker hearing his fault named aloud — as the final piece of evidence, before Jīvaka speaks at all, that the king's condition is not actually severe in the way he believes: a person genuinely beyond reach does not produce an inventory of similes this precise about his own suffering. This is exactly the diagnostic distinction Jīvaka goes on to make explicit: shame and remorse are not signs that the king's crime is worse than he thinks, but proof that his case is treatable at all, "翻上初段" (the reverse of what came before) — where the six ministers had each tried to convince him that no crime existed, Jīvaka reverses course entirely, letting the crime stand exactly as grave as the king believes it to be, and locating the cure not in denying the wound but in how one responds to it.

On the icchantika definition itself, Huiyuan is careful to specify what is doing the actual work: it is not the greatness of a crime that makes someone incurable — the king has committed patricide, one of the five gravest offenses — but the five-part refusal that constitutes the icchantika as such (no belief in cause and effect, no shame, no belief in karmic fruit, no regard for either this world or the next, no willingness to approach a good friend or follow any buddha's teaching). Because the king retains shame, remorse, and the capacity to be moved by an argument at all, he fails this definition on every count, which is precisely why Huiyuan reads Jīvaka's closing line — "the great king, at this moment, is no icchantika" — as the passage's actual thesis, delivered only after twenty-two verses and two long chains of simile have made the reader feel, alongside the king, that he might be one.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this entire catalogue as Jīvaka's own answer to a structural problem: having just told the king that shame and remorse alone can save him, he must now show that the Buddha specifically, and not merely some abstract capacity for repentance, is the one physician equal to the case — so the passage moves from naming the Buddha (his epithets, his qualities, his location, then a long list of philosophical pairs the sūtra elsewhere treats as its own worldly/ultimate framework) into an escalating sequence of proof: first Śakra, a god, saved from his own fear of death through a chain of causal questioning (envy traced back through ignorance, heedlessness, inversion, all the way to doubt) that Huiyuan reads as a miniature of the twelve links of dependent origination applied to an emotional rather than cosmological chain; then Aṅgulimāla and Prince Suvīra, two humans already in the depths of active violence or injury; then hungry ghosts, whose very perception of reality is warped by their own karma (seeing fire where water flows); then blinded bandits, restored by voice alone before any teaching is even given; then a caṇḍāla, a incest-patricide-matricide-guru-murderer, a maddened elephant, and Māra himself — the sequence moving deliberately from the merely afflicted toward cases that look, at each step, less recoverable than the last, until it reaches Devadatta, the Buddha's own cousin, guilty of three of the five gravest offenses, the same category of crime as the king's own.

The long paired list beginning "he does not teach only the noble... but also the lowly" Huiyuan reads as the passage's own explicit thesis stated in propositional form, rather than through further narrative: the Buddha's compassion is defined by symmetry across every axis a hearer might imagine excludes them — rank, wealth, sharpness of mind, temperament, degree of affliction, sex, age, spiritual readiness, and social station — so that the king, hearing story after story of criminals more violent than himself being received without hesitation, cannot locate any criterion by which his own case might reasonably be the exception. The closing comparison of merits (a single step toward the Buddha outweighing a month of universal almsgiving, a hundred chariots of treasure, offerings to a trichiliocosm) Huiyuan treats as the same argument restated one final time in a different register — not that formal giving lacks value, but that it cannot substitute for the one thing this fascicle has been building toward since its opening line: the king must actually go.

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the great king answered: "Jīvaka, the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, is by nature already gentle and tamed, and it is for this very reason that he has gathered a retinue that is, itself, gentle and tamed — just as a forest of sandalwood is surrounded only by sandalwood trees. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the king's own refusal, structurally, as the mirror image of Jīvaka's preceding catalogue: where Jīvaka built his case entirely from the Buddha's own impartiality — that no category of person is excluded from his compassion — the king answers not by disputing a single fact in that catalogue, but by reasoning from an entirely different premise: that like gathers with like, and that his own present impurity is therefore, by its very nature, incompatible with approaching someone pure, regardless of whether that person would, in principle, receive him. Read this way, the king's five similes (sandalwood, purity, the dragon's retinue, tranquility, freedom from greed and affliction) are not a counter-argument to what Jīvaka has just said — they do not deny that the Buddha heals bandits, incest-murderers, and demons — but a separate, self-referential judgment about his own present fitness to appear at all, which is exactly the gap the coming voice from the sky is required to close, since no further argument about the Buddha's compassion could touch a refusal built on this particular foundation.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the sky-voice's opening litany — river running dry, lamp going out, mountain crumbling, ship sinking, bridge collapsing, banner toppling, tree snapping — as a deliberate change of register from everything said before it: the six ministers argued philosophy, and Jīvaka argued through narrative example, but this voice argues through urgency alone, piling up image after image of irreversible loss to produce dread rather than to persuade, because its actual function, on his reading, is not to add one more argument to the pile but to break the king's specific refusal from the previous section — a refusal grounded not in disbelief but in a settled conviction that he is simply too impure to approach the Buddha at all, a position no further reasoning about the Buddha's compassion could touch, but which raw terror at total, final loss can override where argument cannot.

On the Avīci description itself, Huiyuan glosses "無間" (without interval) as bearing four distinct senses worth holding apart, though the passage itself develops only the last: the retributive process is without interval (death and rebirth into the hell follow one another instantly, with no interval of any other state between them); the body itself is without interval (it fills the entire hell, however many beings enter, with no empty space remaining, exactly as the passage describes); the lifespan there is without interval (one full kalpa, unbroken); and the suffering itself is without interval (pain follows pain with no interval of relief at all) — this fourth sense, he notes, is the one this passage actually elaborates, and he adds that the Abhidharma tradition assigns a separate kalpa to each of the five gravest offenses individually, whereas this sūtra (in agreement, he notes, with the Āgamas) has all five offenses share a single kalpa, their severity registering as intensified suffering within it rather than as additional time.

On the voice's identity, Huiyuan reads the king's own three distinct reasons for fainting as worth separating rather than treated as one undifferentiated shock: first, terror that the Buddha might indeed pass away with his own crime still untreated; second, dread of the hell just described now confirmed as his own certain destination; and third, and perhaps sharpest, sheer grief at hearing his own murdered father's voice address him with continued tenderness rather than condemnation — urging him toward the very physician who could save him, rather than toward the retribution he has just spent an entire fascicle being told he deserves. This closes the fascicle exactly where it opened: with a wound that will not heal, though now for a different reason — not because no cure exists, but because the king has not yet been able to bring himself to seek it.

Part 19 Complete

This closes Part 19 (fascicle 19 of 40), the whole of which stages the sūtra's central illustration of sick conduct: King Ajātaśatru's patricide and the psychosomatic wound of remorse it opens in him, six ministers each offering a different heterodox teacher's denial that the act carries any consequence at all, and the physician Jīvaka's counter-case — built from shame and remorse as two saving white dharmas, a careful distinction between the king's own treatable guilt and the icchantika's true incurability, and an escalating catalogue of everyone the Buddha has ever healed, from a god fearing death to his own cousin guilty of the same category of crime as the king himself. The fascicle ends unresolved: the king, still convinced his own impurity disqualifies him from approaching the Buddha at all, refuses to go, until a voice from the sky, warning that the Dharma itself is dying, reveals itself as his own murdered father, urging him toward Jīvaka's counsel rather than the six ministers' comfort — and the king, overwhelmed, only faints, his wound worse than before.
