The King Who Went to the Buddha

King Ajātaśatru's story reaches its resolution: the Buddha's moon-love light heals the wound of remorse before the king has taken a single step, Jīvaka answers his last hesitations with a long parable about a physician who reads every unfavorable omen and still refuses to give up on a patient, and the king finally makes the journey — only to have the Buddha dismantle, argument by argument, the very idea that there was ever a fixed father, a fixed killer, or a fixed crime to be punished. This closes 梵行品 entirely, and opens 嬰兒行品, the ninth chapter, on why a buddha sometimes teaches as artlessly as an infant behaves.

Why Ajātaśatru, Specifically

Chinese Source

爾時世尊，在雙樹間見阿闍世悶絕躄地，即告大眾：「我今當為是王住世至無量劫，不入涅槃。」迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如來當為無量眾生不入涅槃，何故獨為阿闍世王？」佛言：「善男子！是大眾中，無有一人謂我畢定入於涅槃，阿闍世王定謂我當畢竟永滅，是故悶絕，自投於地。善男子！如我所言，為阿闍世不入涅槃。如是密義，汝未能解。何以故？我言為者，一切凡夫。阿闍世者，普及一切造五逆者。又復為者，即是一切有為眾生。我終不為無為眾生而住於世。何以故？夫無為者，非眾生也。阿闍世者，即是具足煩惱等者。又復為者，即是不見佛性眾生。若見佛性，我終不為久住於世。何以故？見佛性者，非眾生也。阿闍世者，即是一切未發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心者。又復為者，即是阿難、迦葉二眾。阿闍世者，即是阿闍世王、後宮妃后，及王舍城一切婦女。又復為者，名為佛性。言阿闍者，名為不生，世者名怨，以不生佛性故，則煩惱怨生，煩惱怨生故，不見佛性；以不生煩惱故，則見佛性，以見佛性故，則得安住大般涅槃。是名不生，是故名為為阿闍世。善男子！阿闍者名不生，不生者名涅槃，世名世法，為者名不污，以世八法所不污故，無量無邊阿僧祇劫不入涅槃。是故我言，為阿闍世，無量億劫不入涅槃。善男子！如來密語不可思議，佛、法、眾僧亦不可思議，菩薩摩訶薩亦不可思議，大涅槃經亦不可思議。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the World-Honored One, between the twin sāla trees, saw Ajātaśatru faint and fall to the ground, and said at once to the great assembly: "I shall now remain in this world, for the sake of this king, for immeasurable kalpas, and shall not enter nirvāṇa." The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, should the Tathāgata remain, and not enter nirvāṇa, for the sake of immeasurable beings, why do you speak of doing so for Ajātaśatru alone?" The Buddha said: "Good son, within this great assembly, there is not a single person who believes that I shall, in the end, most certainly enter nirvāṇa — but Ajātaśatru is entirely certain that I am about to be extinguished forever, and it is for this very reason that he fainted and threw himself to the ground. Good son, when I say that I do this for Ajātaśatru's sake, and shall not enter nirvāṇa, this is a secret meaning you are not yet able to grasp. Why is this? When I say 'for the sake of,' I mean every ordinary being; 'Ajātaśatru' takes in universally everyone who has committed one of the five gravest offenses. And, further, 'for the sake of' means every conditioned being — I shall never remain in this world for the sake of any unconditioned being at all. Why is this? What is unconditioned is not a being at all. 'Ajātaśatru' means one fully endowed with affliction. And, further, 'for the sake of' means beings who do not yet see buddha-nature — should they see buddha-nature, I would never, in the end, remain long in this world for their sake. Why is this? One who sees buddha-nature is not a being at all. 'Ajātaśatru' means everyone who has not yet given rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening. And, further, 'for the sake of' means the two assemblies of Ānanda and Kāśyapa; 'Ajātaśatru' means King Ajātaśatru himself, the women of his own harem, and every woman throughout the city of Rājagṛha. And, further, 'for the sake of' names buddha-nature itself. 'Ajā' means unborn, and 'śatru' means enemy; because buddha-nature is not born, the enemy of affliction is born, and, the enemy of affliction being born, buddha-nature is not seen; and because affliction is not born, buddha-nature is seen, and, buddha-nature being seen, one abides securely in this great nirvāṇa. This is what is meant by unborn — it is for this very reason that this is called 'for the sake of Ajātaśatru.' Good son, 'ajā' means unborn, and unborn means nirvāṇa; 'śatru' means worldly dharma, and 'for the sake of' means undefiled; because he is undefiled by the eight worldly dharmas, he does not enter nirvāṇa for immeasurable, boundless, incalculable kalpas. It is for this very reason that I say I do this for Ajātaśatru's sake, and shall not enter nirvāṇa for immeasurable koṭis of kalpas. Good son, the Tathāgata's secret speech is inconceivable; the Buddha, the Dharma, and the holy Saṅgha, too, are inconceivable; the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, too, is inconceivable; and this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, too, is inconceivable."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this passage as answering Kāśyapa's question through six successive re-readings of the single word "for" (為) and the name "Ajātaśatru," the first four organized around a person and a class he generalizes to, the last two around a wordplay on the name's own Sanskrit meaning. First register: "for" means every ordinary being, and "Ajātaśatru" stands in for everyone guilty of one of the five gravest offenses. Second: "for" means every conditioned being generally (ordinary beings and the two lesser vehicles alike), and "Ajātaśatru" stands for everyone still fully possessed of affliction. Third: "for" means everyone who has not yet seen buddha-nature (which Huiyuan notes may include bodhisattvas through the ninth ground, who have not yet seen it with direct vision), and "Ajātaśatru" stands for everyone who has not yet given rise to the bodhi-mind. Fourth: "for" means, quite literally, only Ānanda's and Kāśyapa's own two assemblies, and "Ajātaśatru" means, quite literally, only the king's own harem and the women of Rājagṛha — the narrowest and most literal of the six readings. The fifth and sixth registers shift from generalizing the name to etymologizing it: "ajā" as unborn and "śatru" as enemy, so that "for the sake of Ajātaśatru" becomes "for the sake of causing the enemy of affliction to remain unborn" — read one way by someone still deluded (the enemy, affliction, is born, and buddha-nature accordingly stays hidden) and the opposite way by someone awakened (affliction stays unborn, and buddha-nature is accordingly seen); and finally "ajā" as unborn-meaning-nirvāṇa itself, "śatru" as worldly dharma, and "for" as undefiled, yielding "remaining undefiled by the eight worldly dharmas for the sake of nirvāṇa."

His broader point is that none of these six readings competes with the others for being the "real" one — the sūtra's own closing line, calling this secret speech itself inconceivable, is the actual answer to Kāśyapa's question: a single sentence can carry an entire stack of true meanings at once, each valid at its own register, which is precisely the same rhetorical move this collection has already tracked under 「密語」 and the "worldly sense plus revealed sense" pattern running throughout the ten epithets in Part 18 — except here compounded six times over in a single utterance, rather than once per epithet.

The Moon-Love Light

Chinese Source

爾時世尊，大悲導師，為阿闍世王，入月愛三昧。入三昧已，放大光明，其光清涼，往照王身，身瘡即愈，欝蒸除滅。王覺瘡愈，身體清涼，語耆婆言：「曾聞人說，劫將欲盡三月竝現，當是之時一切眾生患苦悉除。時既未至，此光何來照觸吾身，瘡苦除愈，身得安樂？」耆婆答言：「此非劫盡三月竝照，亦非火日、星宿、藥草、寶珠天光。」王又問言：「此光若非三月竝照、寶珠明者，為是誰光？」「大王當知，是天中天所放光明，是光無根無有邊際，非熱非冷、非常非滅、非色非無色、非相非無相、非青非黃、非赤非白。欲度眾生，故使可見、有相可說，有根、有邊、有熱有冷、青黃赤白。大王！是光雖爾，實不可說、不可覩見，乃至無有青黃赤白。」王言：「耆婆！彼天中天，以何因緣放斯光明？」「大王！今是瑞相將為大王。以王先言，世無良醫療治身心，故放此光，先治王身，然後治心。」王言：「耆婆！如來世尊亦見念耶？」耆婆答言：「譬如一人而有七子，是七子中一子遇病，父母之心非不平等，然於病子心則偏多。大王！如來亦爾，於諸眾生非不平等，然於罪者心則偏重，於放逸者佛則慈念，不放逸者心則放捨。何等名為不放逸者？謂六住菩薩。大王！諸佛世尊於諸眾生，不觀種姓、老少中年、貧富、時節、日月星宿、工巧、下賤、僮僕婢使，惟觀眾生有善心者，若有善心則便慈念。大王當知，如是瑞相，即是如來入月愛三昧所放光明。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the World-Honored One, the great, compassionate guide, entered the moon-love samādhi for the sake of King Ajātaśatru. Having entered this samādhi, he emitted a great light, cool and refreshing, which reached out and shone upon the king's own body, and, at once, his sores were healed, their feverish inflammation entirely removed. The king, feeling his sores healed and his body cool and at ease, said to Jīvaka: "I once heard it said that, when a kalpa is about to end, three moons appear together in the sky, and, at that time, every being's own affliction and suffering is entirely removed. That time has not yet come — from where, then, has this light come, to touch my own body, remove the suffering of my sores, and grant my body such peace and ease?" Jīvaka answered: "This is not the light of three moons appearing together at a kalpa's end, nor is it the light of fire, sun, star, herb, jewel, or any heavenly light." The king asked further: "If this light is not the light of three moons together, nor the brightness of a jewel, then whose light is it?" "Great king, you should know that this is a light emitted by the god beyond gods himself; this light has no root and no boundary at all — neither hot nor cold, neither permanent nor extinguished, neither form nor formless, neither marked nor unmarked, neither blue, yellow, red, nor white. It is only for the sake of saving beings that it is made visible, given a describable mark, a root, a boundary, heat and cold, and the colors blue, yellow, red, and white. Great king, though this light is such, in truth it cannot be described at all, nor beheld at all, nor does it possess, in the end, any blue, yellow, red, or white whatsoever." The king said: "Jīvaka, for what cause and condition has that god beyond gods emitted this light?" "Great king, this very auspicious sign has now appeared for the great king's own sake. Because the king earlier said that the world holds no good physician to heal his body and mind, this light was emitted first to heal the king's own body, and only afterward to heal his mind." The king said: "Jīvaka, does the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, truly take notice of me and think of me?" Jīvaka answered: "It is like a man who has seven sons; should one of these seven sons fall ill, it is not that the parents' own mind lacks impartiality toward all seven — and yet their mind naturally inclines more toward the sick child. Great king, the Tathāgata is exactly the same: it is not that his mind lacks impartiality toward every being — and yet his mind naturally inclines more heavily toward one who has committed a crime. Toward the heedless, the Buddha holds a compassionate thought; toward the heedful, his mind lets go and does not dwell on them at all. Who is meant by 'the heedful'? This means the bodhisattva of the sixth abiding stage. Great king, every buddha, every world-honored one, regarding every being, gives no regard at all to caste, to youth, old age, or middle age, to wealth or poverty, to season, to sun, moon, or star, to skill or craft, to low station, or to servant or slave — he regards only whether a being possesses a wholesome mind, and, should such a wholesome mind be present, he holds it in compassionate regard at once. The great king should know that this very auspicious sign is precisely the light emitted by the Tathāgata upon entering the moon-love samādhi."

王即問言：「何等名為月愛三昧？」耆婆答言：「譬如月光，能令一切優鉢羅花開敷鮮明；月愛三昧亦復如是，能令眾生善心開敷，是故名為月愛三昧。大王！譬如月光能令一切行路之人心生歡喜；月愛三昧亦復如是，能令修習涅槃道者心生歡喜，是故復名月愛三昧。大王！譬如月光，從初一日至十五日，形色光明，漸漸增長；月愛三昧亦復如是，令初發心諸善根本漸漸增長，乃至具足大般涅槃，是故復名月愛三昧。大王！譬如月光，從十六日至三十日，形色光明漸漸損減；月愛三昧亦復如是，光所照處，所有煩惱能令漸滅，是故復名月愛三昧。大王！譬如盛熱之時，一切眾生常思月光，月光既照，欝熱即除；月愛三昧亦復如是，能令眾生除貪惱熱。大王！譬如滿月，眾星中王、為甘露味，一切眾生之所愛樂；月愛三昧亦復如是，諸善中王為甘露味，一切眾生之所愛樂，是故復名月愛三昧。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The king then asked: "What is meant by the moon-love samādhi?" Jīvaka answered: "It is like the moon's own light, which is able to cause every blue lotus to unfold in bright and fresh bloom — the moon-love samādhi is exactly the same, able to cause the wholesome mind of every being to unfold in just this way; it is for this reason that it is named the moon-love samādhi. Great king, it is like the moon's own light, which is able to cause every traveler upon the road to feel joy in his heart — the moon-love samādhi is exactly the same, able to cause one who cultivates the path of nirvāṇa to feel joy in his heart; it is for this reason, too, that it is named the moon-love samādhi. Great king, it is like the moon's own light, which, from the first day of the month to the fifteenth, grows gradually greater in its form and brightness — the moon-love samādhi is exactly the same, causing the wholesome roots of one who has just given rise to the bodhi-mind to grow gradually greater, until they become fully complete in this great nirvāṇa; it is for this reason, too, that it is named the moon-love samādhi. Great king, it is like the moon's own light, which, from the sixteenth day of the month to the thirtieth, diminishes gradually in its form and brightness — the moon-love samādhi is exactly the same, causing every affliction, wherever this light shines, to be gradually extinguished; it is for this reason, too, that it is named the moon-love samādhi. Great king, it is like how, in the season of great heat, every being forever longs for the moon's own light, and, once the moon shines forth, the sweltering heat is at once removed — the moon-love samādhi is exactly the same, able to remove the feverish heat of craving and affliction from every being. Great king, it is like the full moon, king among every star, possessed of the flavor of ambrosia, beloved and cherished by every being — the moon-love samādhi is exactly the same, king among every wholesome thing, possessed of the flavor of ambrosia, beloved and cherished by every being; it is for this reason, too, that it is named the moon-love samādhi."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the physician's-favoritism simile (a parent's love for all seven sons undiminished, yet naturally concentrated on the sick one) as this fascicle's own resolution of a tension the king raised explicitly in Part 19 — that the Buddha's impartiality, insisted on at such length in Jīvaka's earlier catalogue, might seem to leave no room for anything like being singled out for help. His answer distinguishes impartiality of basic regard from an entirely compatible asymmetry of active attention: the Buddha holds no being in higher or lower regard by nature, caste, wealth, or any other worldly marker, and yet compassion itself, functioning correctly, concentrates wherever suffering is greatest — which is not a departure from equanimity but what equanimity actually produces in practice, once a being in front of it happens to be in crisis.

On the six similes of the moon-love samādhi itself, Huiyuan groups them by function rather than treating them as six independent points: the first two (blooming lotuses, the traveler's joy) describe the samādhi's immediate effect on contact; the next two (waxing moon, waning moon) describe its effect over time, in two complementary directions — growing wholesome roots and shrinking affliction, which he notes are simply two descriptions of the same single process rather than two separate accomplishments; and the last two (relief from summer heat, the singular preeminence of the full moon among stars) describe its felt quality and its rank among every other wholesome practice. He flags one deliberate asymmetry worth preserving in translation: the sūtra names the samādhi for love (愛) rather than for compassion (悲) or wisdom (慧), and reads this as pointed — the passage that follows is not primarily an argument, but an experience of being cared for, arriving before Jīvaka has said another persuasive word, and healing the wound the entire preceding fascicle of argument could not touch.

A King Among the Impure

Chinese Source

王言：「我聞如來不與惡人同止坐起、語言談論，猶如大海不宿死屍、如鴛鴦鳥不住圊廁、釋提桓因不與鬼住、鳩翅羅鳥不棲枯樹；如來亦爾，我當云何而得往見？設其見者，我身將不陷入地耶？我觀如來，寧近醉象、師子虎狼、猛火絕焰，終不近於重惡之人。是故我今思忖是已，當有何心往見如來？」耆婆答言：「大王！譬如渴人，速赴清泉，飢者求食，怖者求救，病求良醫，熱求蔭涼，寒者求火，王今求佛亦應如是。大王！如來尚為一闡提等演說法要，何況大王非一闡提，而當不蒙慈悲救濟？」王言：「耆婆！我昔曾聞，一闡提者不信不聞、不能觀察、不得義理，何故如來而為說法？」

Ru-Yi Translation

The king said: "I have heard that the Tathāgata does not dwell, sit, rise, or converse together with evil people — just as the great ocean does not harbor any dead corpse within it, just as the mandarin duck does not dwell in a latrine, just as Śakra, lord of the gods, does not dwell together with spirits, and just as the kokila bird does not perch upon a withered tree. The Tathāgata is exactly the same — how, then, could I possibly go before him? And, should I see him, would my own body not sink straight into the earth? As I consider the Tathāgata, I think he would sooner draw near a drunken elephant, a lion, a tiger, a wolf, or a raging, blazing fire than ever draw near a person of such grave evil as myself. Having pondered this well, what state of mind at all could I possibly have, going to see the Tathāgata?" Jīvaka answered: "Great king, it is like a man parched with thirst, who hastens swiftly to a clear spring; one starving, who seeks food; one afraid, who seeks rescue; one ill, who seeks a good physician; one overheated, who seeks the cool shade; and one who is cold, who seeks fire — the king's own seeking of the Buddha should be exactly the same. Great king, the Tathāgata even expounds the essentials of the Dharma for the icchantika himself — how much more so should the great king, who is no icchantika at all, fail to receive his compassionate rescue?" Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this long parable, first introduced back in Part 18 in outline and now given in full, as structured in five sequential stages he numbers explicitly: the patient's own unfavorable dream (his good qualities inverting into their opposites, itself the deepest-lying and most genuinely dire sign of the five); a messenger sent who bears every unfavorable mark; the physician's own repeated divinations along the way (day, star, season, each one unfavorable); the physician's direct observation of the patient's own body through sight, smell, and touch, confirming every prior sign; and finally the physician's own considered choice not to declare the patient's death outright, but to promise a return, provide for the patient's needs in the meantime, and delay. He stresses that every one of these signs, taken individually or together, points to certain death — and that this is exactly the point: the physician's continued engagement is not a failure to read the signs correctly, but a deliberate refusal to let a correct diagnosis settle the question of what to do.

The parable's application, on Huiyuan's reading, is only loosely mapped rather than one-to-one at every point — he warns against over-fitting the correspondence — but its center is clear: the icchantika's own five wholesome faculties (faith, and so on) already lost, or a person so far gone that not even divination offers comfort, is precisely the case this whole story exists to address, and the physician's refusal to give up is the model for why the Buddha teaches the icchantika at all, distinguished sharply by Huiyuan's own added gloss into two practical categories the passage does not fully spell out on its own: an icchantika of sharp faculties, for whom teaching bears fruit in this very life, and one of middling faculties, for whom the same teaching plants a cause maturing only later — with the sūtra's own point being that neither case licenses silence, since "the Buddha does not speak in vain" holds even when a fruit is centuries away.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the vast retinue numbers (twelve thousand chariots, fifty thousand elephants, five hundred and eighty thousand people) not as incidental scene-setting but as the narrative's own way of dramatizing the merit-comparison Jīvaka made explicit back in Part 19 — a single step toward the Buddha outweighs any quantity of worldly display — by having the king finally take that step surrounded by precisely the kind of overwhelming material display the sūtra has just finished ranking as worth less than the step itself. The two rumors the king hears en route (Virūḍhaka's fiery death at sea, Kokālika's living descent into hell) versus the third (Sunakṣatra's crimes extinguished by reaching the Buddha) are, on his reading, a final compressed rehearsal of Part 19's entire argument, now stripped to bare contrast rather than developed at length — which is why the king's continued hesitation here (wanting to ride with Jīvaka in case he falls to hell regardless) reads less as fresh doubt than as the same anxiety cycling one last time before resolution.

The exchange with the bodhisattva Sarvadhara, Huiyuan treats as a genuine philosophical objection rather than a rhetorical setup: if the sūtra's own teaching is that no dharma has any fixed nature, how can the Buddha coherently promise to fix the king's mind? His reading of the Buddha's answer turns on a distinction between two senses of "fixed" operating in the same sentence: the king's doubt is itself an unfixed, changeable mental state (which is precisely why it can be resolved at all — a truly fixed, unchangeable doubt could never be altered by anything, including the Buddha's own intervention), and this same absence of fixed nature is what allows the king's crime, too, to be broken through rather than standing as an immovable, determinate fact. The apparent paradox dissolves once "making the mind resolute" is read not as imposing a new fixed state from outside, but as removing the false belief that a fixed state (irreversible guilt, certain damnation) was ever there to begin with — the same operation, Huiyuan notes, that the entire remainder of this fascicle's dialogue with the king is about to perform in far greater technical detail.

Great King

爾時大王即到娑羅雙樹間，至於佛所，仰瞻如來三十二相、八十種好，猶如微妙真金之山。爾時世尊出八種聲告言：「大王。」時阿闍世左右顧視，「此大眾中誰是大王？我既罪戾，又無福德，如來不應稱為大王。」爾時如來即復喚言：「阿闍世大王。」時王聞已，心大歡喜，即作是言：「如來今日顧命語言，真知如來於諸眾生，大悲憐愍，等無差別。」白佛言：「世尊！我今疑心永無遺餘，定知如來真是眾生無上大師。」爾時迦葉菩薩語持一切菩薩言：「如來已為阿闍世王作決定心。」爾時阿闍世王即白佛言：「世尊！假使我今得與梵王釋提桓因坐起飲食，猶不欣悅；得遇如來一言顧命，深以欣慶。」爾時阿闍世王，即以所持幡蓋、香花、伎樂供養，前禮佛足，右遶三匝，禮敬畢已，却坐一面。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the great king arrived among the twin sāla trees, coming before the Buddha, and, looking up, beheld the Tathāgata's thirty-two marks and eighty subsidiary signs, like a mountain of subtle and wondrous true gold. At that time, the World-Honored One, in one of his eight kinds of voice, called out: "Great king." Ajātaśatru looked about him to the left and right: "Who, within this great assembly, is the great king? I myself am guilty of transgression, and possess no merit at all — the Tathāgata could not possibly be addressing me as 'great king.'" At that time, the Tathāgata called out to him once more: "Great King Ajātaśatru." Hearing this, the king felt great joy in his heart, and said: "That the Tathāgata should today turn his attention to me and address me by name — this truly shows that the Tathāgata's own great compassion and pity extends equally to every being, without any distinction at all." He said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, my own mind of doubt is now entirely gone, without the slightest remainder — I know for certain that the Tathāgata is truly the unsurpassed teacher of every being." At that time, the bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the bodhisattva Sarvadhara: "The Tathāgata has already made King Ajātaśatru's own mind resolute." At that time, King Ajātaśatru said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, were I now able to sit, rise, and take food together with Brahmā or with Śakra, lord of the gods, I would still feel no such joy as this — but, having received the Tathāgata's own single word, turning his attention to me by name, I feel the deepest joy and delight." At that time, King Ajātaśatru at once made offering with the banners, canopies, incense, flowers, and music he had brought, bowed before the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, and, having finished paying his respects, withdrew to sit to one side.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan glosses the "eight kinds of voice" from the classical list this collection first noted in Part 19's commentary on the Buddha's own teaching qualities: most excellent, easily understood, harmonious, gentle, free of error, unfeminine, dignified, and deep and far-reaching — and reads the Buddha's own hesitation to name the king outright on the first call as deliberate, not incidental: a direct, immediate naming might have overwhelmed the king with joy before he was ready to receive it, so the address comes in two stages, "great king" alone first, allowing the king's own self-disqualifying reflex ("I am guilty, I have no merit, this cannot be meant for me") to surface and be witnessed, before the second, fuller call — "Great King Ajātaśatru" — answers that very reflex directly, using his name specifically rather than the plain title.

He reads the king's own closing comparison — greater joy at a single word from the Buddha than at feasting alongside Brahmā and Śakra themselves — as the narrative payoff of everything Jīvaka argued at length across Part 19's merit comparisons (a single step toward the Buddha outweighing worldly wealth and status), now delivered not as an abstract ranking of merits but as the king's own felt experience, confirming in the first person what had, until this moment, been offered to him only as argument.

Twenty Things to Observe

Chinese Source

爾時佛告阿闍世王言：「大王！今當為汝說正法要，汝當一心，諦聽！諦聽！凡夫常當繫心觀身，有二十事：一、所謂我，此身中空，無無漏，二、無諸善根本，三、我此生死未得調順，四、墮墜深坑無處不畏，五、以何方便得見佛性，六、云何修定得見佛性，七、生死常苦無常我淨，八、八難之難難得遠離，九、恒為怨家之所追逐，十、無有一法能遮諸有，十一、於三惡趣未得解脫，十二、具足種種諸惡邪見，十三、亦未造立度五逆津，十四、生死無際未得其邊，十五、不作諸業不得果報，十六、無有我作他人受果，十七、不作樂因終無樂果，十八、若有造業果終不失，十九、因無明生亦因而死，二十、去來現在常行放逸。大王！凡夫之人常於此身，當作如是二十種觀。作是觀已不樂生死，不樂生死則得正觀。爾時次第觀心生相、住相、滅相；次第觀心生住滅相，定、慧、進、戒，亦復如是；觀生住滅已，知心相乃至戒相，終不作惡，無有死畏、三惡道畏。若不繫心觀察如是二十事者，心則放逸，無惡不造。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the Buddha said to King Ajātaśatru: "Great king, I shall now expound for you the essentials of the true Dharma — attend with a single mind, and listen closely, listen closely! An ordinary being should forever fix his mind upon observing his own body, through twenty matters: first, that, so-called, within this very body of mine, there is emptiness, with nothing free of outflow at all; second, that it is without any wholesome root whatsoever; third, that I have not yet tamed and subdued this very birth-and-death of mine; fourth, that I have fallen into a deep pit, with nowhere at all free of fear; fifth, by what skillful means might I attain the sight of buddha-nature; sixth, how might I cultivate concentration so as to attain the sight of buddha-nature; seventh, that birth-and-death is forever suffering, impermanence, non-self, and impurity; eighth, that the difficulty of the eight difficulties is hard indeed to escape; ninth, that I am forever pursued by my own enemy; tenth, that there is not a single dharma able to block off any of the realms of existence; eleventh, that I have not yet attained liberation from the three evil destinies; twelfth, that I am fully endowed with every kind of evil, wrong view; thirteenth, that I have not yet built the ford by which to cross beyond the five gravest offenses; fourteenth, that birth-and-death is without limit, its very boundary not yet attained; fifteenth, that, were no action performed, no fruit at all would be received; sixteenth, that there is no such thing as one person acting and another receiving the fruit of it; seventeenth, that, were no cause of happiness performed, there would, in the end, be no fruit of happiness at all; eighteenth, that, should any action be performed, its fruit is never, in the end, lost; nineteenth, that one is born through the cause of ignorance, and dies through this same cause as well; and twentieth, that, in the past, future, and present, one forever behaves with heedlessness. Great king, an ordinary being should forever make these twenty kinds of observation, exactly as given, upon this very body. Having made these observations, he no longer takes pleasure in birth-and-death, and, no longer taking pleasure in birth-and-death, he attains right contemplation. He then observes, in turn, the mark of the mind's own arising, the mark of its abiding, and the mark of its ceasing; and, having observed in turn the mark of the mind's arising, abiding, and ceasing, he observes concentration, wisdom, diligence, and moral discipline in exactly the same way. Having observed arising, abiding, and ceasing, and having come to know the mark of the mind, and, further, the mark of moral discipline, he never, in the end, commits any evil at all, and is free of the fear of death and the fear of the three evil destinies. Should he fail to fix his mind and observe these twenty matters in this way, his mind becomes heedless, and there is no evil at all that he will not commit."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan divides the twenty items into two groups of fourteen and six: the first fourteen concern the absence, in an ordinary being's own body, of ultimate bliss and the presence, instead, of every kind of suffering, and the last six concern one's own confusion and inverted thinking about action and its fruit. Within the first fourteen, he finds a further pattern of pairs — two on the absence of ultimate bliss (no outflow-free quality, no wholesome root), two on the presence of suffering already at hand (unsubdued birth-and-death, the deep pit of fear), two that pivot from this suffering toward a wish to see buddha-nature (by what means, and through what cultivation), and eight further items detailing the specific varieties of this suffering (the four marks of birth-and-death, the eight difficulties, being pursued by one's own enemies — namely the five aggregates themselves, since they guarantee further rebirth — the absence of any dharma able to block off future existence, non-liberation from the three evil destinies, wrong views, the unbuilt ford past the five gravest offenses, and the boundlessness of birth-and-death itself). The last six items, by contrast, lay out four general principles about action and its fruit (action is never without effect, its fruit is never received by someone else, happiness always requires its own cause, and a performed action's fruit is never simply lost) before naming, in the final two items, the ordinary being's own actual confusion about these very principles — born through ignorance and dying through the same cause, forever heedless across all three times.

Huiyuan's point in laying out this structure so carefully is that the twenty-item teaching, offered here as a general instruction for "an ordinary being," is not yet aimed at the king's specific crime at all — it is preparatory groundwork, cultivating in him the capacity for right contemplation in general, before the Buddha turns, in the section that follows, to dismantle the king's own particular conviction that he has committed a fixed, determinate act of patricide deserving a fixed, determinate punishment. The general teaching on impermanence and non-fixed action comes first; the specific application to the king's own guilt comes only once this foundation is in place.

No Father, No Killer, No Fixed Crime

Chinese Source

阿闍世言：「如我解佛所說義者，我從昔來，初未曾觀是二十事，故造眾惡，造眾惡故，則有死畏、三惡道畏。世尊！自我招殃，造茲重惡，父王無辜，橫加逆害。是二十事設觀不觀，必定當墮阿鼻地獄。」佛告大王：「一切諸法性相無常、無有決定，王云何言，必定當墮阿鼻地獄？」阿闍世王白佛言：「世尊！若一切法無定相者，我之殺罪亦應不定；若殺定者，一切諸法則非不定。」佛言：「大王！善哉，善哉！諸佛世尊說一切法悉無定相，王復能知殺亦不定，是故當知殺無定相。大王！如汝所言，先父無辜橫加逆害者，何者是父？但於假名眾生五陰，妄生父想。於十二入、十八界中，何者是父？若色是父，四陰應非；若四是父，色亦應非。若色非色合為父者，無有是處。何以故？色與非色，性無合故。大王！凡夫眾生於是色陰妄生父想，如是色陰亦不可害。何以故？色有十種，是十種中，唯色一種，可見可持、可稱可量、可牽可縛。雖可見縛，其性不住，以不住故不可得見、不可捉持、不可稱量、不可牽縛。色相如是，云何可殺？若色是父，可殺可害、獲罪報者；餘九應非。若九非者，則應無罪。大王！色有三種：過去、未來、現在。過去、現在則不可害。何以故？過去過去故，現在念念滅故；遮未來故名之為殺。如是一色，或有可殺，或不可殺，有殺不殺，色則不定。若色不定殺亦不定，殺不定故報亦不定。云何說言定入地獄？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Ajātaśatru said: "As I understand the meaning of what the Buddha has taught, I, from the past until now, never once made these twenty observations, and it was for this very reason that I committed every kind of evil — and, having committed every kind of evil, I now have the fear of death and the fear of the three evil destinies. World-Honored One, I myself invited this disaster, committing this very grave evil, wrongfully turning against my innocent father, the king, and having him killed. Whether or not I make these twenty observations now, I must certainly, in the end, fall into the Avīci hell." The Buddha said to the great king: "Every single dharma's own nature and mark is impermanent, without any fixed or determinate quality at all — how, then, can the king say that he must certainly, in the end, fall into the Avīci hell?" King Ajātaśatru said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, should every dharma be without any fixed mark, then my own crime of killing should likewise be without any fixed mark; but should this killing be fixed and determinate, then not every dharma at all is without a fixed mark." The Buddha said: "Great king, excellent, excellent! Every buddha, every world-honored one, teaches that every dharma is, indeed, without any fixed mark at all, and the king is further able to know that this killing, too, is without any fixed mark — you should therefore know that this killing has no fixed mark at all. Great king, as you yourself have said, that your innocent father, the king, was wrongfully turned against and killed — but who, exactly, is this 'father'? It is only with regard to the five aggregates of a being conventionally so named that the false notion of 'father' arises at all. Among the twelve sense-fields and eighteen elements, which one, exactly, is this 'father'? Should form be the father, the four other aggregates should not be; and should these four be the father, form, too, should not be. Should you say that form combined with what is not form together constitute the father, this could not possibly be so. Why is this? Because form and what is not form, by their very natures, admit of no combination at all. Great king, it is only with regard to this aggregate of form that ordinary beings falsely give rise to the notion of 'father' — and yet this very aggregate of form, too, cannot be harmed at all. Why is this? Form is of ten kinds, and, among these ten kinds, only a single kind of form itself can be seen, grasped, weighed, measured, drawn, and bound. Though it can be seen and bound, its own nature does not abide even for a moment, and, because it does not abide, it cannot, in truth, be seen, cannot be grasped, cannot be weighed or measured, and cannot be drawn or bound at all. Since the mark of form is exactly this, how, then, could it possibly be killed? Should form be the father, capable of being killed and harmed, and thereby incurring the fruit of a crime, then the remaining nine kinds of form should not be so; and, should these nine not be so, then, indeed, there should be no crime at all. Great king, form is of three kinds: past, future, and present. What is past and what is present cannot be harmed at all. Why is this? Because the past is already past, and the present perishes, thought after thought; it is only the blocking off of the future that is named 'killing.' Since this single aggregate of form is, in this way, in one sense capable of being killed and, in another sense, incapable of being killed — sometimes killed, sometimes not — form itself is therefore without any fixed nature at all. And, should form be without fixed nature, killing, too, is without fixed nature; and, killing being without fixed nature, its fruit, too, is without fixed nature. How, then, can it be said that you must certainly, in the end, fall into hell?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Buddha's argument here as attacking the king's conviction along three separate lines, each targeting a different fixed idea buried in his single sentence "my innocent father, I wrongfully turned against and killed": first, that there was a determinate person, "father," to be killed at all; second, granting a person, that this person's own aggregate of form was itself something a blade could actually reach; and third, granting that it could be reached, that the very act of killing has a single, fixed timing (past, present, or future) rather than a shifting relation to time that changes what "killing" could even mean. On the first point, he traces the argument through the aggregates (five), sense-fields (twelve), and elements (eighteen) methodically, showing that no single one of these categories, taken alone or combined with any other, yields anything that could be pointed to as "the father" without arbitrarily privileging one aggregate over the rest.

On the ten kinds of form, Huiyuan notes the sūtra's own count (the five sense faculties and five sense objects) differs from both major Abhidharma traditions available to him — the Sarvāstivāda's eleven (adding "unmanifest form," 無作色) and the Satyasiddhi's fourteen (adding the four great elements as their own category) — and reads the sūtra's own choice as deliberate rather than an oversight: the four great elements are already subsumed here under the "tangible" sense-object, and unmanifest form, lacking any perceptible mark of its own, simply has no work to do in an argument specifically about what can be seen, touched, and thereby killed. His sharpest point concerns the three-times argument at the end: killing is defined here as specifically blocking off a future arising, which means the very same aggregate of form is, at different moments relative to the act, sometimes killable and sometimes not — a form already past cannot be killed (it is already gone), a form presently perishing thought-by-thought cannot be killed (it is already ceasing on its own), and only a form's future continuation can be blocked — so that "killing," far from naming one fixed relation between an agent and a victim, names something that shifts depending on which of three temporal positions the same aggregate happens to occupy, which is exactly what "without fixed nature" means in concrete rather than abstract terms.

Light and Heavy Karma, and the Blameless Sun

Chinese Source

「大王！一切眾生所作罪業凡有二種：一者輕，二者重。若心口作，則名為輕；身口心作，則名為重。大王！心念口說，身不作者，所得報輕。大王！昔日口不勅殺，但言削足。大王！若勅侍臣立斬王首，坐時乃斬猶不得罪，況王不勅云何得罪？王若得罪，諸佛世尊亦應得罪。何以故？汝父先王頻婆娑羅，常於諸佛種諸善根，是故今日得居王位。諸佛若不受其供養則不為王，若不為王，汝則不得為國生害。若汝殺父當有罪者，我等諸佛亦應有罪。若諸佛世尊無有罪者，汝獨云何而得罪耶？大王！頻婆娑羅往有惡心，於毘富羅山遊行獵鹿，周遍壙野悉無所得，唯見一仙五通具足，見已即生瞋恚惡心：『我今遊獵所以不得，正坐此人。』驅逐令去，即勅左右而令殺之。其人臨終，生瞋惡心，退失神通，而作誓言：『我實無辜，汝以心口，橫加戮害，我於來世亦當如是，還以心口而害於汝。』時王聞已，即生悔心，供養死屍。是王如是尚得輕受，不墮地獄；況王不爾，而當地獄受果報耶？先王自作還自受之，云何令王而得殺罪？如王所言，父王無辜者。大王！云何言無？夫有罪者則有罪報，無惡業者則無罪報。汝父先王若無辜罪，云何有報？頻婆娑羅於現世中，亦得善果及以惡果，是故先王亦復不定，以不定故殺亦不定，殺不定故，云何而言定入地獄？」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Great king, the crimes that every being commits are, in all, of two kinds: light, and grave. Should it be committed by mind and speech alone, it is named light; should it be committed by body, speech, and mind together, it is named grave. Great king, when one merely thinks it in the mind and speaks it aloud, without the body itself acting, the fruit received is light. Great king, on that former day, your own mouth did not order him killed — you said only to cut off his feet. Great king, should you have ordered your own attendant minister to stand and behead the king outright, and he sat there and beheaded him, this would still not incur any crime — how much less, then, should any crime be incurred when you gave no such order at all? Should the king truly incur a crime here, then every buddha, every world-honored one, should incur a crime as well. Why is this? Your own father, the former king Bimbisāra, forever planted every kind of wholesome root under every buddha, and it was for this very reason that he came to occupy the throne in the first place. Had every buddha refused to accept his offerings, he would never have become king at all; and, had he never become king, you would never have been able to bring harm upon him for the sake of the kingdom. Should you incur a crime for killing your own father, then we buddhas, too, should incur a crime in just the same way. And should every buddha, every world-honored one, be free of any crime in this, how, then, could you alone incur one? Great king, Bimbisāra, in ages past, once harbored an evil mind: wandering Mount Vipula in pursuit of deer, he searched the whole wilderness and found nothing at all, until he came upon a single seer fully endowed with the five supernormal powers. Seeing him, an angry, evil thought at once arose in him: 'The very reason I have found nothing on this hunt is because of this very man' — and he drove the seer away, and then ordered his own attendants to kill him. That seer, as he was dying, gave rise to an angry, evil thought of his own, and, his supernormal powers thereby lost, made this vow: 'I am, in truth, entirely innocent, and yet you have wrongfully had me killed through your own mind and speech — in a life to come, I, too, shall do just the same, and shall likewise harm you through my own mind and speech.' The king, hearing this, at once gave rise to a remorseful mind, and made offering to the seer's own corpse. This king, having done as he did, still received only a light retribution, and did not fall into hell — how much less, then, should the present king, who has done nothing of the sort, receive his fruit in hell? Your former father, the king, brought this about himself, and received its fruit himself in turn — how, then, could the present king incur any crime of killing at all? As for what the king has said, that his father, the king, was innocent — great king, how can you say he was without fault? Where there is a crime, there is a fruit of that crime; and where there is no evil act, there is no fruit of any crime. Should your own father, the king, have truly been entirely without fault, how, then, could he have received such a fruit? Bimbisāra, in this very present life, received both a wholesome fruit and an unwholesome one — your own former father, the king, is therefore likewise without any fixed nature, and, being without fixed nature, this killing, too, is without fixed nature; and, this killing being without fixed nature, how can it be said that you must certainly, in the end, fall into hell?"

Chinese Source

「大王！眾生狂惑凡有四種：一者貪狂，二者藥狂，三者呪狂，四者本業緣狂。大王！我弟子中有是四狂，雖多作惡，我終不記是人犯戒。是人所作，不至三惡，若還得心，亦不言犯。王本貪國，逆害父王，貪狂心作，云何得罪？大王！如人酒醉，逆害其母，既醒寤已，心生悔恨，當知是業亦不得報。王今貪醉，非本心作，若非本心，云何得罪？大王！譬如幻師，四衢道頭，幻作種種男女、象馬、瓔珞、衣服，愚癡之人謂為真實，有智之人知非真有。殺亦如是，凡夫謂實，諸佛世尊知其非真。大王！譬如山間響聲，愚癡之人謂之實聲，有智之人知其非真。殺亦如是，凡夫謂實，諸佛世尊知其非真。大王！如人有怨，詐來親附，愚癡之人謂為實親，智者了達乃知虛詐。殺亦如是，凡夫謂實，諸佛世尊知其非真。大王！如人執鏡，自見面像，愚癡之人謂為真面，智者了達知其非真。殺亦如是，凡夫謂實，諸佛世尊知其非真。大王！如熱時炎，愚癡之人謂之是水，智者了達知其非水。殺亦如是，凡夫謂實，諸佛世尊知其非真。大王！如乾闥婆城，愚癡之人謂為真實，智者了達知其非真。殺亦如是，凡夫謂實，諸佛世尊知其非真。大王！如人夢中受五欲樂，愚癡之人謂之為實，智者了達知其非真。殺亦如是，凡夫謂實，諸佛世尊知其非真。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Great king, the delusion of beings is, in all, of four kinds: the delusion of craving, the delusion of drugs, the delusion of incantation, and the delusion of one's own past karmic conditions. Great king, among my own disciples there are those afflicted with these four kinds of delusion, and, though they commit a great deal of evil, I never, in the end, record such a person as having broken the precepts. What such a person does does not lead him to the three evil destinies, and, should his own mind return to him, he is likewise not said to have transgressed. The king originally coveted the kingdom, and turned against his own father, the king, and had him killed — this was done through a mind deluded by craving; how, then, could this incur a crime? Great king, it is like a man drunk on wine, who turns against his own mother and kills her; once he has sobered and awakened, remorse arises in his mind — you should know that a deed of this kind, too, incurs no fruit at all. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the light-versus-grave karma distinction as doing real, technical work rather than merely softening the king's guilt: an act committed by mind and speech alone, without the body itself carrying it out, is light — and the king, in historical fact, ordered only that his father's feet be cut off, not that he be killed outright, which places his own culpability, on strict classification, below even that of a minister ordered to behead someone and who then hesitates before acting. The argument that follows — that the Buddha himself would share in any crime here, since it was the buddhas' own acceptance of Bimbisāra's offerings that made him king, and kingship the precondition for the son's crime against him — Huiyuan is explicit is not meant to actually implicate the Buddha (a conclusion the passage clearly intends as absurd), but to run a reductio: if the causal chain leading to the king's crime is followed back far enough, it implicates everyone and everything, including the Buddha, which shows the very notion of a single, isolable culprit to be incoherent rather than merely mitigated.

The Bimbisāra backstory — his own past killing of an innocent seer, met with only a light retribution despite mind, speech, and body all being involved — Huiyuan reads on two parallel tracks he keeps distinct: read as an argument from cause, it shows Bimbisāra's death was already set in motion by his own prior grave act, so the actual weight of guilt for the present event does not fall where the king assumes; read as an argument from result, it shows that even a fully embodied, premeditated killing, met with genuine remorse, receives a light fruit — so the king's own lesser act (feet only, mind and speech alone) should receive an even lighter one still. On the seven similes of unreality (illusionist, echo, false friend, mirror, mirage, gandharva city, dream), Huiyuan notes these split three ways: the first three (illusionist, false friend, and by extension the dream) show that what is taken as real is, in fact, constructed to deceive; the mirror and mirage show that what is taken as an independent object is actually a mere reflection or an appearance with no substance of its own; and the echo and gandharva city show that what seems to have a persisting location or source in fact has none at all — three distinct ways an ordinary being mistakes appearance for reality, all converging on the same conclusion applied to killing itself.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan traces three successive "gates" of argument through this dense passage, each subordinate to the next in depth: first, a gate that simply affirms killing has real existence (the wine-master and sun/moon similes, arguing that mere knowledge of an act, or being the occasion for another's act, does not itself constitute committing it — the wine merchant is not drunk merely by trading in wine, the sun is not guilty merely by illuminating a thief's crime, and the king, similarly, is not the true agent behind an act driven by craving rather than by his own free will); second, a gate that breaks through this naive affirmation into emptiness (the nirvāṇa-as-neither-existent-nor-non-existent analogy, showing that "killing," like nirvāṇa itself, resists being pinned to either existence or non-existence as a simple, univocal fact — its status shifts entirely according to the mental state of whoever regards it: existent to one without shame, non-existent to one with it; existent to one who holds views, non-existent to the view of emptiness); and third, a gate that moves past this emptiness to reveal the underlying reality (the closing argument from impermanence, where "killing" impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self themselves is reinterpreted as attaining permanence, bliss, reality, and true self — the very language of "killing" repurposed to describe liberation itself, rather than an act performed on another being).

On the closing argument's final rhetorical turn — "I, too, kill impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self, and do not fall into hell; why would you?" — Huiyuan reads this as the passage's actual destination rather than merely its most provocative line: the entire chain of arguments has not been building toward proving the king committed no crime in any ordinary sense (he clearly did order violence against his father), but toward relocating what "killing" fundamentally means, from an irreversible act fixing one's identity forever as "a patricide," to a description that, properly understood, points at liberation from exactly the kind of fixed, self-punishing identity the king has been carrying since the fascicle's very first line. The eraṇḍa-seed-into-sandalwood-tree simile the king offers in response, Huiyuan notes, is his own spontaneous elaboration, not prompted by any specific instruction from the Buddha — a sign, on his reading, that the king has genuinely internalized the teaching rather than merely accepted an argument, since he immediately reaches for an image of transformation rather than mere pardon.

What Is Meant by Infant-Like Conduct

Chinese Source

「善男子！云何名嬰兒行？善男子！不能起住、來去、語言，是名嬰兒。如來亦爾，不能起者，如來終不起諸法相；不能住者，如來不著一切諸法；不能來者，如來身行無有動搖；不能去者，如來已到大般涅槃；不能語者，如來雖為一切眾生演說諸法，實無所說。何以故？有所說者，名有為法。如來世尊非是有為，是故無說。又無語者，猶如嬰兒，語言未了，雖復有語，實亦無語。如來亦爾，語未了者，即是諸佛祕密之言，雖有所說，眾生不解，故名無語。又嬰兒者，名物不一、未知正語，雖名物不一、未知正語，非不因此而得識物。如來亦爾，一切眾生方類各異，所言不同，如來方便隨而說之，亦令一切因而得解。又嬰兒者，能說大字。如來亦爾，說於大字，所謂婆啝，啝者有為，婆者無為，是名嬰兒。啝者名為無常，婆者名為有常，如來說常，眾生聞已，為常法故，斷於無常，是名嬰兒行。又嬰兒者，不知苦樂、晝夜、父母。菩薩摩訶薩亦復如是，為眾生故，不知苦樂、無晝夜想，於諸眾生其心平等，故無父母親踈等相。又嬰兒者，不能造作大小諸事。菩薩摩訶薩亦復如是，菩薩不造生死作業，是名不作。大事者即五逆也，菩薩摩訶薩終不造作五逆重罪；小事者即二乘心，菩薩終不退菩提心，而作聲聞辟支佛乘。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, what is meant by infant-like conduct? Good son, one who is unable to rise, to abide, to come, to go, or to speak is named an infant. The Tathāgata is exactly the same: 'unable to rise' means that the Tathāgata never, in the end, gives rise to the mark of any dharma at all; 'unable to abide' means that the Tathāgata clings to no dharma whatsoever; 'unable to come' means that the Tathāgata's own bodily conduct is never stirred or moved; 'unable to go' means that the Tathāgata has already arrived at this great nirvāṇa; and 'unable to speak' means that, though the Tathāgata expounds every dharma for the sake of every being, he has, in truth, nothing at all that he speaks. Why is this? Whatever is spoken is named a conditioned dharma; the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, is not conditioned at all — it is for this very reason that he has nothing that he speaks. And, further, 'without speech' is like an infant, whose speech is not yet fully formed — though he does speak after a fashion, in truth he has no speech at all. The Tathāgata is exactly the same: what is 'not yet fully formed' in his speech is itself every buddha's own secret utterance — though something is indeed spoken, beings do not understand it, and it is for this reason that it is named 'without speech.' And, further, an infant is one for whom things are not yet distinguished as one from another, and who does not yet know correct speech — and yet, though things are not yet distinguished and correct speech not yet known, it is not that he fails, on this very account, to come to recognize things. The Tathāgata is exactly the same: every being differs in its own class and kind, and what is said to each differs accordingly — the Tathāgata, through skillful means, speaks in accordance with each, and causes every one of them, through this very means, to attain understanding. And, further, an infant is able to speak great syllables. The Tathāgata is exactly the same, speaking a great syllable, namely, 'bhava' — 'va' means conditioned, and 'bha' means unconditioned; this is what is meant by 'infant.' 'Va' means impermanence, and 'bha' means permanence; the Tathāgata speaks of permanence, and beings, hearing this, on account of this very permanent dharma, sever impermanence — this is what is meant by infant-like conduct. And, further, an infant does not know suffering and pleasure, day and night, or father and mother. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva is exactly the same: for the sake of beings, he does not distinguish suffering from pleasure, nor hold any notion of day and night; his mind is equal and impartial toward every being, and so he holds no mark at all of father, mother, kin, or stranger. And, further, an infant is unable to carry out any great or small undertaking. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva is exactly the same: the bodhisattva does not carry out the deeds that produce birth-and-death — this is what is meant by 'not carrying out.' 'Great undertakings' means the five gravest offenses; the bodhisattva-mahāsattva never, in the end, commits the grave crime of any of the five gravest offenses. 'Small undertakings' means the mind of the two lesser vehicles; the bodhisattva never, in the end, turns back from the bodhi-mind to take up the vehicle of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha."

「又嬰兒行者，如彼嬰兒啼哭之時，父母即以楊樹黃葉，而語之言：『莫啼莫啼，我與汝金。』嬰兒見已生真金想，便止不啼，然此楊葉實非金也。木牛木馬、木男木女，嬰兒見已，亦復生於男女等想，即止不啼，實非男女。以作如是男女想故，名曰嬰兒。如來亦爾，若有眾生欲造眾惡，如來為說三十三天常樂我淨、端正自恣、於妙宮殿受五欲樂、六根所對無非是樂。眾生聞有如是樂故，心生貪樂，止不為惡，勤作三十三天善業。實是生死無常、無樂、無我、無淨，為度眾生，方便說言常樂我淨。又嬰兒者，若有眾生厭生死時，如來則為說於二乘，然實無有二乘之實，以二乘故知生死過，見涅槃樂。以是見故，則能自知，有斷、不斷，有真、不真，有修、不修，有得、不得。「善男子！如彼嬰兒於非金中而生金想，如來亦爾，於不淨中而為說淨，如來已得第一義故則無虛妄。如彼嬰兒，於非牛馬作牛馬想，若有眾生於非道中作真道想，如來亦說非道為道，非道之中實無有道，以能生道微因緣故，說非道為道。如彼嬰兒於木男女生男女想，如來亦爾，知非眾生說眾生想，而實無有眾生相也。若佛如來說無眾生，一切眾生則墮邪見，是故如來說有眾生。於眾生中作眾生想者，則不能破眾生相也；若於眾生破眾生相者，是則能得大般涅槃，以得如是大涅槃故，止不啼哭，是名嬰兒行。善男子！若有男女受持讀誦、書寫解說是五行者，當知是人必定當得如是五行。」迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如我解佛所說義者，我亦定當得是五行。」佛言：「善男子！不獨汝得如是五行，今此會中九十三萬人亦同於汝，得是五行。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"And, further, as for infant-like conduct: it is like how, when an infant cries, his parents take a willow tree's own yellow leaf and say to him: 'Do not cry, do not cry — I shall give you gold.' The infant, seeing this, gives rise to the notion of real gold, and at once stops crying — and yet this very willow leaf is, in truth, not gold at all. It is exactly the same with a wooden ox, a wooden horse, a wooden boy, or a wooden girl: the infant, seeing these, likewise gives rise to the notion of a boy or a girl, and at once stops crying — though, in truth, these are no boy or girl at all. It is on account of giving rise to just such a notion of a boy or a girl that this is named 'infant.' The Tathāgata is exactly the same: should any being wish to commit every kind of evil, the Tathāgata teaches him of the permanence, bliss, self, and purity of the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, its beings upright and free to do as they please, receiving the pleasures of the five desires within its wondrous palaces, every one of the six senses meeting with nothing but pleasure itself. Beings, hearing that such pleasure exists, give rise in their minds to a craving for it, and, on account of this, cease from evil, and diligently perform the wholesome deeds that lead to the Trāyastriṃśa heaven. Yet, in truth, this is nothing but birth-and-death — impermanence, without pleasure, without self, without purity — and it is only for the sake of saving beings, through skillful means, that this is spoken of as permanence, bliss, self, and purity. And, further, as for the infant: should any being, at some time, come to loathe birth-and-death, the Tathāgata then teaches him of the two lesser vehicles — and yet these two lesser vehicles, in truth, have no reality of their own at all; it is only through these two vehicles that one comes to know the fault of birth-and-death, and to see the bliss of nirvāṇa. Through this very seeing, one is then able to know for himself what is severed and what is not yet severed, what is true and what is not yet true, what is cultivated and what is not yet cultivated, and what is attained and what is not yet attained.

"Good son, just as that infant gives rise to the notion of gold where there is, in truth, no gold at all, the Tathāgata is exactly the same: he teaches purity where there is, in truth, impurity — and yet, because the Tathāgata has already attained the ultimate meaning, there is no falsehood at all in this. Just as that infant gives rise to the notion of an ox or a horse where there is neither ox nor horse at all — should any being give rise to the notion of a true path where there is, in truth, no path at all, the Tathāgata, too, speaks of what is not the path as the path; though there is, in truth, no path at all within what is not the path, it is because it is able to give rise, through a subtle cause and condition, to the true path itself that the Tathāgata speaks of what is not the path as the path. Just as that infant gives rise to the notion of a boy or a girl at a wooden boy or girl, the Tathāgata is exactly the same: knowing there is no being at all, he yet speaks of the notion of a being, though there is, in truth, no mark of any being whatsoever. Should the Buddha, the Tathāgata, teach that there is no being at all, every being would then fall into wrong view — it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata teaches that beings exist. One who, among beings, holds fast to the very notion of a being is thereby unable to break through the mark of any being at all; but one who, among beings, breaks through the mark of a being is thereby able to attain this great nirvāṇa — and it is on account of attaining this great nirvāṇa that he ceases his own weeping. This is what is meant by infant-like conduct. Good son, should any man or woman uphold, recite, copy out, and expound these five practices, know that this very person shall most certainly come to attain these same five practices himself." The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as I understand the meaning of what the Buddha has taught, I, too, shall most certainly attain these five practices." The Buddha said: "Good son, it is not you alone who shall attain these five practices in this way — the nine hundred and thirty thousand people gathered in this very assembly today shall attain these same five practices, exactly as you shall."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan divides this closing chapter into the Buddha's own inherent "infant" qualities, discussed first, and the bodhisattva's "infant-like" conduct toward others, discussed second — a distinction, he stresses, that matters because the two halves answer entirely different questions: the first describes what a buddha already is (beyond arising, abiding, coming, going, or speaking, in the technical sense that none of these apply to an unconditioned reality), while the second describes a deliberate pedagogical strategy a bodhisattva or buddha adopts toward beings who are not yet ready for the whole truth. He reads the opening fourfold analysis (unable to rise, abide, come, go) as covering the Tathāgata's three types of action at once — mind, body, and speech — with the final three sub-points on speech alone receiving separate, extended treatment because speech is where an infant's real limitation is felt most concretely: not silence, but words not yet fully formed, which Huiyuan reads as the sūtra's own image for its own "secret speech" running throughout this collection — communication genuinely occurring, yet not yet grasped by its audience, exactly paralleling an infant who does, in fact, produce recognizable sounds long before those sounds carry an adult's full meaning.

On the "bhava" syllable, Huiyuan glosses the two components straightforwardly (va as conditioned/impermanent, bha as unconditioned/permanent) and reads the whole example as the sūtra's own model for its entire teaching method: an infant's single utterance carries, within it, a distinction adults would need a whole sentence to make, exactly as this sūtra's own compressed language regularly carries multiple registers of meaning in what looks, on the surface, like an artlessly simple statement. On the three parables of skillful deception (willow-leaf gold, wooden animals, wooden figures), he traces a clear escalation: the willow leaf calms an ordinary being's own destructive impulse by dangling a heavenly reward that is, in ultimate truth, still just birth-and-death dressed up as permanence, bliss, self, and purity; the wooden animals draw a being disillusioned with saṃsāra toward the two lesser vehicles, themselves ultimately without independent reality, useful only as a stepping stone toward seeing nirvāṇa's own bliss; and the wooden figures address the single hardest case — how to teach at all once a being has, correctly, seen through the notion of "self" and "other" as illusory, where the Tathāgata's own paradoxical answer (teaching that beings exist, while knowing they do not, precisely to prevent the more dangerous error of nihilism) closes 梵行品's entire five-practice structure on the same note this collection has tracked since Part 11: skillful means is not concealment of the truth, but the only form in which the truth can actually reach a mind not yet ready to receive it directly.

Part 20 Complete

This closes Part 20 (fascicle 20 of 40) and, with it, the entire second major division of the sūtra's practice teachings, running from fascicle 11's opening of 聖行品 through this fascicle's close of 嬰兒行品. King Ajātaśatru's story concludes here: the Buddha's moon-love light heals the wound before the king takes a single step, Jīvaka's long parable of the physician who reads every unfavorable sign yet never abandons the patient answers the king's last hesitation, and the Buddha's own extended teaching — dismantling the fixed father, the fixed killer, the fixed timing of the act, the fixed weight of light versus grave karma, and finally the very language of "killing" itself, redirected to name liberation rather than harm — resolves what six ministers' denials of consequence and Jīvaka's catalogue of the healed could not. The king departs not merely forgiven but revealed to have held the bodhi-mind since the buddha Vipaśyin, ages before this life's crime. 嬰兒行品 then closes 聖行品's entire five-practice structure, on the note that skillful means — teaching what is not quite true so that a mind not yet ready can be led toward what is — is not a departure from the sūtra's honesty, but the very shape that honesty must take to reach an unready listener at all.
