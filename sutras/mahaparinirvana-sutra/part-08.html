The Golden Probe, and the Vanished Sword

This fascicle opens with verses comparing the Vaipulya scriptures to nectar and poison at once, and dissolves the ordinary distinction among the three refuges into a single ground in buddha-nature, before turning to the sūtra's most demanding argument yet: that self and no-self, permanence and impermanence, bear no duality at all, worked out through the vexed question of whether curds already exist within milk. A grass hidden in the Himalayas and a flower that blooms on an elephant's tusk only at the sound of thunder extend the buddha-nature simile further, before the fascicle's two most vivid narratives: a hundred blind men whose eyes are lanced open by a golden probe, seeing buddha-nature only by degrees even at the tenth bodhisattva ground; and a king's vanished sword, remembered differently by four generations of ministers who never once saw it truly. An extended meditation on the mystical meanings hidden in the fourteen sounds of the Sanskrit syllabary follows, before the fascicle closes on two birds who never travel apart, and the sūtra's still-unresolved question of whether the Tathāgata himself, having entered nirvāṇa, can be said to grieve.

是道佛所讚，　正進安止處，
亦名正遍見，　故為佛所稱。

我亦趣善逝，　所讚無上道，

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, the Vaipulya scriptures are like nectar, and yet also like poison." The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha again: "For what reason does the Tathāgata say that the Vaipulya scriptures are like nectar, and yet also like poison?" The Buddha said: "Good son, do you now wish to know the true meaning of the Tathāgata's own secret treasury?" Kāśyapa said: "I do indeed now wish to know the meaning of the Tathāgata's own secret treasury." The World-Honored One then spoke these verses:

"There are those who drink nectar, and yet perish, their lives cut tragically short; there are others who drink that same nectar, and their lives are thereby prolonged. There are those who take poison, through some cause, and live; others, through that same cause, take poison and die. The nectar of unobstructed wisdom is what is called the scripture of the Great Vehicle.

This same scripture of the Great Vehicle is likewise called a poison mixed in — like ghee, like clarified butter, like rock candy: taken and properly digested, it becomes medicine; taken and left undigested, it becomes poison.

The Vaipulya scriptures are just the same: to the wise, they are nectar; to the fool who does not know buddha-nature, taking them in becomes poison instead.

For the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha, the Great Vehicle is nectar — just as, among every flavor, milk is foremost of all.

Just so, whoever strives with diligence, relying on the cause of the Great Vehicle, comes to reach nirvāṇa, and becomes the elephant-king among all humankind.

Beings who know buddha-nature are just like Kāśyapa here — tasting the unsurpassed flavor of nectar, they are neither born nor die.

Kāśyapa, you should now rightly discern the three refuges — for the nature of these three refuges is precisely my own nature.

Whoever is able to truly observe that this nature of self is buddha-nature itself should be understood to have thereby entered the secret treasury; knowing both this self and what belongs to this self, such a person has already gone beyond the world.

The nature of the Three Jewels — Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha — is unsurpassed, foremost, and most honored; the meaning of this nature is just as I have spoken in these verses."

Kāśyapa then spoke these verses in reply: "I do not, even now, know where the three refuges truly lie — how, then, should I take refuge in what is unsurpassed and free of every fear?

Not knowing where the Three Jewels truly lie, how could I practice no-self?

How does one who takes refuge in the Buddha come thereby to find comfort?

How does one take refuge in the Dharma? I ask only that you explain this to me.

How does one attain mastery, and how does one fail to attain it?

How does one take refuge in the Saṅgha, and thereby gain the unsurpassed benefit?

How can it be truly said that buddhahood will be accomplished in the future?

If it will not be accomplished in the future, how could I take refuge in the Three Jewels at all?

I have, even now, no foreknowledge of this — I can only proceed by following each refuge in turn.

How can one, before even having conceived, form the thought of already having a son?

If the child is certainly already in the womb, then it may rightly be called having a son — for a child once in the womb is certain to be born before long; this is what is meant by the meaning of a son, and the karma of beings is just the same.

As the Buddha has taught it, the foolish are unable to understand it, and, because of this not-understanding, turn through the prison of birth and death, upāsakas in name only, not knowing its true meaning.

I ask only that you explain this at length, and remove the net of my own doubt; with the Tathāgata's own great wisdom, I ask only that you take pity and explain it fully, and speak to me of the Tathāgata's own secret and precious treasury."

"Kāśyapa, you should know this — I shall now, for your sake, rightly open up this subtle and secret meaning, so that your doubt may be cut off.

Listen now with utmost sincerity: among the bodhisattvas, you share one and the same name with the seventh buddha.

Whoever takes refuge in the Buddha is truly called an upāsaka, and will never again take refuge in any other god or spirit.

Whoever takes refuge in the Dharma thereby departs from killing and harming; whoever takes refuge in the noble Saṅgha no longer seeks anything from the heterodox schools. Taking refuge in the Three Jewels in this way, one attains freedom from every fear."

"Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: I too now take refuge in the Three Jewels — this is called the true path, the very domain of every buddha.

The mark of equality among the Three Jewels is that they forever possess the nature of great wisdom; the nature of self and buddha-nature are without duality, without any difference.

This is the path the Buddha himself praises, the true forward place of rest, also called true and universal seeing — and is for this reason praised by the Buddha.

I too now go toward what the Well-Gone One himself has praised, the unsurpassed path — this is the very greatest nectar, found nowhere among the states of existence at all."

The Buddha then said to the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Good son, you should not now distinguish the Three Jewels the way śrāvakas and ordinary people do; within this Great Vehicle, there is no mark distinguishing the three refuges at all. Why is this? Because the Dharma and the Saṅgha are already present within buddha-nature itself; it is only for the sake of converting and bringing across śrāvakas and ordinary people that the three refuges are taught as separate, distinct marks. Good son, whoever wishes to accord with the ways of the world should indeed distinguish the three refuges as separate. Good son, a bodhisattva should reflect in this way: 'I now take refuge, in this very body, in the Buddha; and should this very body come to accomplish buddhahood, then, once buddhahood is accomplished, I should no longer revere, bow before, or make offerings to any other world-honored one. Why is this? Because every buddha is equal, equally serving as a refuge for beings. Should I wish to honor the relics of the Dharma-body, I should then bow in reverence before the stūpas and shrines of every buddha. Why is this? For the sake of converting and bringing across every being. And I shall also cause beings to form the thought of a stūpa or shrine within my own body, bowing before it and making offerings to it — beings, in this way, take my own Dharma-body as their place of refuge. Every being relies upon teachings that are not true, false and counterfeit; I shall, in due order, teach them the true Dharma for their sake. And there are those who take refuge in a saṅgha that is not the true one; I shall become, for them, a place of refuge in the true Saṅgha. Whoever distinguishes the three refuges as separate, I shall make into a single place of refuge, with no threefold difference at all. For those born blind, I shall become their very eyes; and for śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas alike, I shall become their true place of refuge.' Good son, a bodhisattva of this kind performs the Buddha's own work for the sake of countless wicked beings and every wise one alike.

"Good son, it is like a person who, on facing battle, gives rise to this very thought: 'Among all those here, I am the very foremost — every soldier here relies upon and depends on me.' It is likewise like a crown prince who reflects in this way: 'I shall bring the other princes under my own command, carry forward the great king's own kingly undertaking, and attain mastery over it; I shall make every prince see me as the one to whom they owe allegiance — and so I should not give rise to any inferior state of mind.' As it is with a king and his princes, so it is likewise with his ministers. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva is just the same, reflecting in this way: 'How are these three things one single body with me?' Good son, the three things I have shown are nirvāṇa itself. The Tathāgata is called the peerless one — it is like the human body, in which the head is uppermost, not the other limbs, the hands and feet. The Buddha is likewise uppermost and most honored, not the Dharma or the Saṅgha — and it is only for the sake of converting and bringing across the whole world that he manifests every kind of differentiated mark, like the rungs of a ladder. This is why you should not now hold to the marks of the three refuges as distinct, the way ordinary, foolish people understand them. In the Great Vehicle, your own resolve should be fierce and decisive, like a blade of vajra."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I ask this knowing the answer already, not out of ignorance. I ask, as one of great courage among the bodhisattvas, about the undefiled, pure place of conduct, wishing the Tathāgata to explain and distinguish, at length, this extraordinary matter for the sake of every bodhisattva, extolling the Vaipulya scriptures of the Great Vehicle — the Tathāgata's own great compassion has now taught this well, and I too now abide within it just so. What is taught here as the bodhisattva's pure place of conduct is precisely the teaching of this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra itself. World-Honored One, I too shall now proclaim this Tathāgata's own secret treasury at length for the sake of beings, and shall myself come to realize the true place of the three refuges. Should any being be able to have faith in this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, that person will thereby naturally come to fully understand the place of the three refuges. Why is this? Because buddha-nature exists within the Tathāgata's own secret treasury. Whoever proclaims this scripture teaches that buddha-nature exists in full within every body — and such a person need not seek the place of the three refuges far away at all. Why is this? Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the opening nectar-and-poison verses as making a point about the listener, not about two different substances: the very same Vaipulya teaching benefits whoever receives it with faith and harms whoever meets it with slander, exactly as one substance digested becomes medicine and undigested becomes poison. He reads the "one nature, three refuges" teaching that follows as resting on a distinction the fascicle draws explicitly: the three refuges have both a separate aspect (別相), proper to worldly teaching for śrāvakas and ordinary people, and a single essential nature (一體), which is buddha-nature itself — and the Buddha is urging Kāśyapa to set aside the separate aspect for the single nature. On the general-and-prince parable, Huiyuan reads the point as a corrective to false humility rather than an endorsement of pride: a bodhisattva who has, in principle, all three jewels within his own body should hold himself with the same "vajra-sharp resolve" as a prince who does not doubt his own claim to the throne — not because the Buddha ranks above Dharma and Saṅgha in worth, but the way a head ranks above the limbs of a single body, each necessary, one naturally foremost.

Self and No-Self, Without Duality

若我住者即是常法，不離於苦；若無我者，修行淨行，無所利益。若言諸法皆無有我，是即斷見；若言我住即是常見。若言一切行無常者，即是斷見；諸行常者復是常見。若言苦者即是斷見；若言樂者復是常見。修一切法常者墮於斷見，修一切法斷者墮於常見。如步屈虫，要因前脚得移後足，修常斷者，亦復如是，要因斷常。以是義故，修餘法苦者，皆名不善；修餘法樂者，則名為善。修餘法無我者是諸煩惱分；修餘法常者是則名曰如來祕藏，所謂涅槃，無有窟宅。修餘無常法者，即是財物；修餘常法者，謂佛、法、僧及正解脫。當知如是佛法中道，遠離二邊而說真法。凡夫愚人於中無疑，如羸病人服食酥已，氣力輕便。有無之法，體性不定，譬如四大，其性不同，各相違反。良醫善知，隨其偏發而消息之。善男子！如來亦爾，於諸眾生猶如良醫，知諸煩惱體相差別而為除斷，開示如來祕密之藏，清淨佛性常住不變。若言有者，智不應染；若言無者，即是妄語。若言有者，不應默然，亦復不應戲論諍訟，但求了知諸法真性，凡夫之人戲論諍訟，不解如來微密藏故。若說於苦，愚人便謂身是無常，說一切苦，復不能知身有樂性。若說無常者，凡夫之人計一切身皆是無常，譬如瓦坏；有智之人，應當分別，不應盡言一切無常。何以故？我身即有佛性種子。若說無我，凡夫當謂一切佛法悉無有我；智者應當分別無我假名不實。如是知已，不應生疑。若言如來祕藏空寂，凡夫聞之，生斷滅見；有智之人應當分別，如來是常、無有變易。若言解脫喻如幻化，凡夫當謂得解脫者即是摩滅；有智之人應當分別，人中師子雖有去來，常住無變。若言無明因緣諸行，凡夫之人聞已分別，生二法想，明與無明；智者了達其性無二，無二之性即是實性。若言諸行因緣識者，凡夫謂二，行之與識；智者了達其性無二，無二之性即是實性。若言十善十惡、可作不可作、善道惡道、白法黑法，凡夫謂二；智者了達，其性無二，無二之性即是實性。若言應修一切法苦，凡夫謂二；智者了達，其性無二，無二之性即是實性。若言一切行無常者，如來祕藏亦是無常，凡夫謂二；智者了達，其性無二，無二之性即是實性。若言一切法無我，如來祕藏亦無有我，凡夫謂二；智者了達其性無二，無二之性即是實性，我與無我，性無有二。如來祕藏其義如是，不可稱計無量無邊諸佛所讚，我今於是一切功德成就，經中皆悉說已。

Ru-Yi Translation

"If a self were to abide within the aggregates, this would itself be an eternalist teaching, and one could never depart from suffering; if there were no self at all, then cultivating pure conduct would bring no benefit whatsoever. To say that every dharma is entirely without self is itself an annihilationist view; to say that a self abides within the aggregates is itself an eternalist view. To say that all conditioned activity is impermanent is itself an annihilationist view; to say that all conditioned activity is permanent is, again, an eternalist view. To say suffering alone, without qualification, is itself an annihilationist view; to say bliss alone, without qualification, is, again, an eternalist view. Whoever cultivates the view that all dharmas are permanent falls into an annihilationist view; whoever cultivates the view that all dharmas are annihilated falls into an eternalist view. It is like an inchworm, which can only move its hind foot by first fixing its front foot — cultivating the permanent and the annihilated is just the same, each depending upon the other in turn. For this very reason, to cultivate the view that every remaining dharma is suffering is called unwholesome; to cultivate the view that every remaining dharma is bliss is called wholesome. To cultivate the view that every remaining dharma is without self belongs to the portion of affliction; to cultivate the view that every remaining dharma is permanent is precisely what is called the Tathāgata's own secret treasury, namely nirvāṇa, which has no den or lair at all. To cultivate the view that every remaining dharma is impermanent concerns mere possessions; to cultivate the view that every remaining dharma is permanent concerns the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, and true liberation itself. Know that this is the Buddha's own middle way, departing far from both extremes to teach the true Dharma. Ordinary, foolish people harbor no doubt at all about this — like a weak and sickly person who, having eaten ghee, feels his strength grow light and nimble. The dharmas of being and non-being have no fixed essential nature, just as the four great elements each have a different nature, each contrary to the others; a skilled physician knows this well, and treats each according to which one has become excessive. Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same: toward all beings, he is like a skilled physician, knowing the differing characteristics of every affliction and removing and cutting them off, opening up and revealing the Tathāgata's own secret treasury — buddha-nature, pure, permanently abiding, without change. Should it be said to exist, the wise should not become attached to it as such; should it be said not to exist, this is itself a false statement. Should it be said to exist, one should not remain silent about it, and yet one should also not engage in idle debate and contention over it, but should only seek to fully understand the true nature of every dharma — ordinary people engage in idle debate and contention over it precisely because they do not understand the Tathāgata's own subtle and secret treasury. When suffering is taught, the fool takes this to mean that the body is impermanent, teaches that everything is suffering, and remains unable to know that the body also possesses the nature of bliss. When impermanence is taught, ordinary people suppose that every body without exception is impermanent, like an unfired clay vessel; the wise, however, should discern this rightly, and should not simply declare that everything is impermanent without qualification. Why is this? Because this very body of mine already contains the seed of buddha-nature. When no-self is taught, ordinary people suppose that the whole of the Buddha's own teaching holds there to be no self whatsoever; the wise should discern rightly that this "no-self" is only a provisional name, not something ultimately real. Knowing this, one should give rise to no doubt at all. Should it be taught that the Tathāgata's own secret treasury is empty and still, ordinary people, hearing this, give rise to a view of utter annihilation; the wise should discern rightly that the Tathāgata is permanent, without any change. Should it be taught that liberation is like an illusion or a conjured apparition, ordinary people suppose that whoever attains liberation is thereby simply erased; the wise should discern rightly that the lion among humankind, though he comes and goes, abides permanently, without change. Should it be taught that conditioned activity arises through the condition of ignorance, ordinary people, hearing this, distinguish it and give rise to the notion of two separate dharmas, ignorance and wisdom; the wise fully understand that their nature is without duality, and this nature without duality is itself the true nature. Should it be taught that consciousness arises through the condition of conditioned activity, ordinary people suppose these two, activity and consciousness, to be separate; the wise fully understand that their nature is without duality, and this nature without duality is itself the true nature. Should it be taught that there are ten wholesome and ten unwholesome acts, what may and may not be done, the wholesome destiny and the unwholesome destiny, the pure dharma and the impure dharma, ordinary people suppose these to be two; the wise fully understand that their nature is without duality, and this nature without duality is itself the true nature. Should it be taught that one should cultivate the view that every dharma is suffering, ordinary people suppose this to be two, distinct from bliss; the wise fully understand that their nature is without duality, and this nature without duality is itself the true nature. Should it be taught that all conditioned activity is impermanent, and that the Tathāgata's own secret treasury is likewise impermanent, ordinary people suppose these to be two; the wise fully understand that their nature is without duality, and this nature without duality is itself the true nature. Should it be taught that every dharma is without self, and that the Tathāgata's own secret treasury is likewise without self, ordinary people suppose these to be two; the wise fully understand that their nature is without duality, and this nature without duality is itself the true nature — self and no-self are, in their nature, without any duality at all. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan structures this dense argument in three passes rather than reading it as one undifferentiated list. The first pass corrects ordinary people's own stubborn extremes — five paired terms (self/no-self, permanent/impermanent, suffering/bliss, and so on) each shown to collapse into either eternalism or annihilationism the moment it is asserted without qualification, with the two extremes shown to depend on each other the way an inchworm's front and hind foot depend on each other to move at all. The second pass corrects five specific teachings the two lesser vehicles took as final rather than provisional — that the body is only suffering, only impermanent, only without self, that the Tathāgata's own treasury is only empty, that liberation is only illusion-like — each corrected by adding a further truth rather than retracting the first, since each earlier teaching was genuine medicine for its own context, not an error to be discarded. The third pass, Huiyuan notes, answers a specific worry the other two leave open: if wisdom and ignorance, action and consciousness, good and evil are each "without duality," has the sūtra collapsed every moral and causal distinction into one undifferentiated blur? His answer, carried directly into the milk simile that follows, is that "without duality" never meant "identical" — it means one true mind-nature (真識心) taking on different appearances under different conditions, the way a single water's nature remains one even as it turns clear or turbid by turns.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the milk-and-curds argument as deliberately refusing all three tidy answers Kāśyapa's own question offers — not already fully present (which cannot explain why substance and flavor differ so completely), not simply absent (which cannot explain why poisoned milk yields lethal curds, or why grass, which is genuinely absent from milk, never emerges from it the way curds do), and not produced by anything wholly other (which cannot explain why curds never arise from water). What remains is "arising through cause and condition" — neither identity nor separation — and this, on his reading, is exactly the sūtra's model for how buddha-nature relates to the affliction that covers it: neither already fully manifest nor manufactured from nothing, but real and present, awaiting the right condition to become visible. He reads the thunder-and-tusk-flower simile as answering a question the milk simile leaves open: if buddha-nature was always there, why does hearing this particular sūtra matter at all? His answer is that presence and visibility are not the same thing — the capacity for the flower is already latent in the ivory, but thunder alone triggers its actual blooming, exactly as this sūtra's own teaching is what triggers buddha-nature's passage from merely present to actually known.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the golden-probe parable's four parts as tracking a precise sequence: the hundred blind men correspond to bodhisattvas at each of the ten abiding-stages, each still working with only a partial share of the pāramitās and so still unable to see reality; seeking out a physician corresponds to petitioning the Buddha for the teaching; the golden probe itself is this sūtra's own teaching, piercing the film of ignorance; and the one-finger, two-finger, three-finger exchange tracks the gradual deepening of realization from a first hazy impression to fuller but still incomplete clarity. He is explicit that the nine similes which follow — geese overhead, the drunken traveler, the thirsty man mistaking cranes and trees for water, the distant ship's towers, the tired prince at dawn, the official startled by lightning, the monk peering at water, the figure glimpsed in darkness, the painted image seen by night — do not each carry a separate doctrinal payload to be decoded individually; their point is cumulative, piling up one variation after another on a single structure (a real object, a genuine obscuring condition, and a certainty that grows but never quite arrives) to drive home just how persistent partial vision is, reaching all the way up to the tenth ground. If even bodhisattvas that advanced see buddha-nature only "as through a haze," Huiyuan reads the following exchange on the physical eye as closing off any reading of the golden probe as literal ophthalmology: what actually opens is not the eye but faith placed in this scripture, the one vantage from which śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas — who cannot see buddha-nature by direct realization at all — nonetheless come to know it truly.

Should anyone be able to rightly discern and proclaim this teaching in accordance with the truth, know that this is itself the very mark of a bodhisattva. Good son, every kind of differing treatise, mantra, language, and script there is — all of it is what the Buddha himself has taught, not the teaching of any heterodox school."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan maps this parable's every detail with unusual precision. The sword itself is the true self, buddha-nature; the bodhisattva who first speaks of it, then departs, corresponds directly to the prince who owned the sword and fled with it to another country — an image, Huiyuan notes, that repeats exactly the logic of the milk-and-bitter-medicine parable earlier in this sūtra (Part 7): the true teaching does not vanish, but its plain, positive statement recedes behind a season of no-self teaching. The poor man who only ever glimpsed the sword once, from a respectful distance, and now mumbles about it in his sleep, is every ordinary person who has heard of self without ever having witnessed it truly. The four successive kings who interrogate their ministers are, on Huiyuan's identification, the four fruits of the śrāvaka path — stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, and arhat — each one genuinely further along the path than the last, and each one still offering only a different mistaken guess (a goat's horn, and nothing more) at what the true self actually looks like: attainment on the path, Huiyuan is careful to note, is not the same as having actually seen buddha-nature itself. Only when the original prince returns as king — this sūtra's own reintroduction of the language of true self, after the era of no-self teaching has done its necessary work of clearing away every wrong view — does anyone learn that every prior description, including the ministers' most recent and most confident ones, missed the sword's real appearance entirely.

The Fourteen Sounds

Kāśyapa then asks how the Tathāgata's teaching can be said to be the root of all writing and speech. What follows is an extended exercise in a genre found elsewhere in Indian Buddhist literature: each sound of the Siddham syllabary — the alphabet in which Sanskrit was written down — is given a Dharma-meaning of its own, so that the very letters used to write any teaching down are said to already encode this sūtra's teaching in miniature. Fourteen vowel-sounds are treated first, followed by the syllabary's consonants, and finally four sounds treated as a set. Because these are units of sound, not translatable words, this collection keeps each Chinese transliteration character as the entry's own headword, following it with the Buddha's gloss, rather than reconstructing a Sanskrit phonetic value this collection cannot verify with confidence.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Sounds drawn in through the breath, sounds formed at the root of the tongue, sounds following the nose, long sounds, short sounds, sounds beyond these — the meaning understood through each sound differs according to how the tongue and teeth are used, and letter-meanings of this kind are able to make the karma of beings' speech pure. Yet the buddha-nature of beings does not depend, in this same way, upon letters in order to become pure. Why is this? Because its nature is originally pure. Though it dwells within the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields, it is not the same as the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields themselves — and this is why every being should take refuge in the bodhisattvas, who, because of buddha-nature, view every being with complete equality, without any difference at all. This is why the half-letter serves as the root of every scripture, treatise, and text. And the meaning of the half-letter is entirely the root of speech born of affliction — this is why it is called the half-letter. The full letter, by contrast, is the very root of speech born of every wholesome dharma — just as, in the world, one who does evil is called a half-person, and one who cultivates good is called a full person. So it is that every scripture, treatise, and text has the half-letter as its root. Should it be said that the Tathāgata, and true liberation itself, enter into the half-letter, this could not possibly be so. Why is this? Because they are apart from letters altogether — and it is for this reason that the Tathāgata, unobstructed and unattached with regard to every dharma, has truly attained liberation. What, then, is meant by fully understanding the meaning of letters? Whoever knows that the Tathāgata, appearing in the world, is able to extinguish the half-letter — this is what is meant by fully understanding the meaning of letters. Whoever instead follows after the meaning of the half-letter does not know the Tathāgata's own nature. And what is meant by 'no letter' at all? Whoever draws near to and cultivates unwholesome dharmas is said to have no letter. And further, one may draw near to and cultivate wholesome dharmas, and yet still not know the Tathāgata as permanent as against impermanent, constant as against inconstant, the two jewels of Dharma and Saṅgha, what is proper Vinaya as against what is not, what is true scripture as against what is not, what Māra teaches as against what the Buddha teaches — whoever is unable to discern these is likewise said to follow after the meaning of 'no letter.' I have now finished explaining this meaning of following after 'no letter.' Good son, this is why you should now depart from the half-letter, and rightly understand the full letter."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, we should indeed rightly study the number of these letters — I have now encountered the unsurpassed teacher, and have received the Tathāgata's own earnest instruction." The Buddha praised Kāśyapa: "Excellent, excellent! Whoever delights in the true Dharma should study it in just this way."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Siddham syllabary's role in this fascicle as a genuine paradox against the sūtra's own usual vocabulary. Elsewhere the sūtra sorts teachings into "half-letter" (provisional, lesser-vehicle) and "full-letter" (complete, Mahāyāna) — but here the "half-letter" named is the basic syllabary itself, the raw phonetic root beneath every text whatsoever, and this most basic thing is treated as already containing the sūtra's full meaning implicitly, a case, in Huiyuan's phrase, where "the half is also the great" (半亦是大). His resolution is that the usual half/full hierarchy sorts doctrinal content, while this passage sorts something prior to content altogether — the letters that make any teaching sayable at all — so the two schemes are not actually in tension, only easy to conflate at first glance. He is emphatic, though, that the entire exercise remains skillful means rather than literal metaphysics: the passage's own closing lines state plainly that buddha-nature's purity does not depend on letters to become pure, since its nature is pure from the start — the elaborate correspondence between phonemes and doctrines exists for beings who need language to approach the Dharma, not because buddha-nature is itself made of Sanskrit sound.

「善男子！譬如空中，舍宅微塵不可住立，若言舍宅不因空住無有是處，以是義故，不可說舍住於虛空、不住虛空。凡夫之人，雖復說言舍住虛空，而是虛空實無所住。何以故？性無住故。善男子！心亦如是，不可說言住陰界入及以不住，無想天壽亦復如是，如來憂悲亦復如是，若無憂悲，云何說言等視眾生如羅睺羅？若言有者，復云何言性同虛空？善男子！譬如幻師，雖復化作種種宮殿，殺生長養繫縛放捨，及作金銀、琉璃寶物、叢林樹木，都無實性。如來亦爾，隨順世間，示現憂悲，無有真實。善男子！如來已入於般涅槃，云何當有憂悲苦惱？若謂如來入於涅槃是無常者，當知是人則有憂悲。若謂如來不入涅槃常住不變，當知是人無有憂悲。如來有愁及以無愁，無能知者。

「復次善男子！譬如下人，能知下法，不知中上；中者知中，不知於上；上者知上，及知中下。聲聞緣覺亦復如是，齊知自地。如來不爾，悉知自地及以他地，是故如來名無礙智。示現幻化，隨順世間，凡夫肉眼謂是真實；而欲盡知如來無礙無上智者，無有是處。有愁無愁，唯佛能知，以是因緣，異法有我，異法無我；是名鴛鴦、迦隣提鳥性。

「復次善男子！佛法猶如鴛鴦共行。是迦隣提及鴛鴦鳥，盛夏水涱，選擇高原，安處其子，為長養故，然後隨本，安隱而遊。如來出世亦復如是，化無量眾令住正法，如彼鴛鴦迦隣提鳥，選擇高原安置其子。如來亦爾，令諸眾生所作已辦，即便入於大般涅槃。善男子！是名異法是苦，異法是樂，諸行是苦，涅槃是樂，第一微妙壞諸行故。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the Tathāgata has already departed from the poisoned arrow of grief and sorrow. Now, grief and sorrow belong to the gods — and the Tathāgata is not a god. Grief and sorrow belong to human beings — and the Tathāgata is not a human being. Grief and sorrow belong to the twenty-five states of existence — and the Tathāgata does not belong to the twenty-five states of existence. This being so, the Tathāgata has no grief or sorrow at all — why, then, is it said that the Tathāgata grieves?"

"Good son, the heaven of no-perception is called 'no-perception' — but if there were truly no perception at all, there would be no life-span either; and if there were no life-span, how could the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields exist there at all? For this reason, one cannot say that the life-span of beings in the heaven of no-perception has any fixed dwelling-place at all. Good son, it is like a tree-spirit dwelling within its tree — one cannot say with certainty that it dwells in this branch, this node, this trunk, or that leaf; and though it has no fixed location, one cannot say it does not exist at all. The life-span of the heaven of no-perception is just the same. Good son, buddha-nature is just the same — exceedingly profound, hard to fathom. The Tathāgata truly has no grief, sorrow, or affliction at all, and yet, out of great compassion toward beings, manifests the appearance of grief and sorrow, viewing every being just as he views Rāhula. Furthermore, good son, the life-span within the heaven of no-perception can be fully known only by the Buddha, beyond the reach of anyone else — and it is just the same for the heaven of neither-perception-nor-non-perception as well. Kāśyapa, the Tathāgata's own nature is pure and undefiled, just like a transformation-body — where, then, could there be any grief, sorrow, or affliction at all? Yet if it were said the Tathāgata has no grief or sorrow whatsoever, how could he benefit every being and spread the Buddha's own Dharma so widely? And if it were said he has none at all, how could it also be said that he views every being with the same regard as he views Rāhula? Were he not to view them so, this statement itself would be false. For this very reason, good son: the Buddha is beyond conceptual thought, the Dharma is beyond conceptual thought, the buddha-nature of beings is beyond conceptual thought, and the life-span of the heaven of no-perception is beyond conceptual thought. That the Tathāgata both has grief and has no grief is the Buddha's own domain, something no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha can ever know.

"Good son, it is like empty space: no house, no dust-mote, can truly stand or dwell within it — yet to say that a house does not, in some sense, rest upon that very emptiness could not possibly be so either. For this reason, one cannot say that a house dwells within empty space, nor that it does not dwell there. Ordinary people, though they say a house 'dwells' within empty space, yet this empty space itself truly dwells nowhere at all. Why is this? Because its nature is without any dwelling. Good son, the mind is just the same — one cannot say it dwells within the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields, nor that it does not; the life-span of the heaven of no-perception is just the same; the Tathāgata's own grief and sorrow are just the same. Were there no grief or sorrow at all, how could it be said that he views every being with the same regard as he views Rāhula? And if it is said that grief exists, how, then, could it also be said that his nature is the same as empty space? Good son, it is like a conjurer who, though he may magically fashion every kind of palace, killing, nurturing, binding, and releasing, and fashion gold, silver, lapis lazuli, treasures, groves, and trees, has produced nothing with any real substance at all. The Tathāgata is just the same: in according with the ways of the world, he manifests grief and sorrow, though there is nothing real in it at all. Good son, the Tathāgata has already entered parinirvāṇa — how, then, could there be any grief, sorrow, or affliction? Whoever holds that the Tathāgata's entering nirvāṇa means he is impermanent should be understood to be one who has, himself, grief and sorrow. Whoever holds that the Tathāgata does not enter nirvāṇa at all, abiding permanently without change, should be understood to be one without any grief or sorrow. Whether the Tathāgata sorrows or does not sorrow is something no one is able to know.

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a person of the lowest rank, who knows only the lowest things, and knows nothing of the middle or higher; a person of the middle rank knows the middle, but not the higher; a person of the higher rank knows the higher, and also knows the middle and lower. Śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas are just the same, knowing only their own level fully. The Tathāgata is not so — he fully knows his own level, and every other level besides, and this is why the Tathāgata is called one of unobstructed wisdom. He manifests illusory transformation, according with the ways of the world, and ordinary people, with their fleshly eyes, take this to be truly real — but to wish to fully know the Tathāgata's own unobstructed, unsurpassed wisdom could never possibly be so. Whether he sorrows or does not sorrow can be fully known only by the Buddha. It is for this very reason that one dharma has self, and a different dharma has no self — this is what is meant by the nature of the mandarin duck and the kāraṇḍava bird.

"Furthermore, good son, the Buddha's own Dharma is likewise like the mandarin ducks who travel always together. In the height of summer, when the waters rise, the kāraṇḍava and the mandarin duck choose out high ground and settle their young there, for the sake of raising them, before returning again to their own accustomed place, roaming there in peace. The Tathāgata's appearing in the world is just the same: he converts countless beings, establishing them in the true Dharma, just as those mandarin ducks and kāraṇḍava birds choose out high ground and settle their young there. The Tathāgata is just the same: once he has caused every being's own task to be accomplished, he then enters Mahāparinirvāṇa. Good son, this is what is meant by one dharma being suffering and a different dharma being bliss — conditioned activity is suffering, nirvāṇa is bliss, supremely subtle and wondrous, because it destroys all conditioned activity."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, how does a being who attains nirvāṇa come to have the very foremost bliss?" The Buddha said: "Good son, as I have taught, the coming-together of every conditioned activity is what is called old age and death.

"Careful watchfulness, without heedlessness — this is what is called the place of nectar;
heedlessness, without care — this is called the road of death.
Whoever is without heedlessness attains the place free of death;
whoever is heedless forever hastens along the road of death.

"Heedlessness belongs to the conditioned, and the conditioned is the very foremost suffering. Freedom from heedlessness is called nirvāṇa, and this nirvāṇa is called nectar, the very foremost, greatest bliss. To hasten toward conditioned activity is called the place of death, receiving the very foremost suffering; to arrive at nirvāṇa is called freedom from death, receiving the most wondrous bliss. Yet one who is without heedlessness, though he gathers conditioned activities around him still, may likewise be called permanent, blissful, deathless, and unbroken in body. What, then, is heedlessness, and what is its absence? For the non-noble, ordinary person, this is called heedlessness, the ever-dying dharma; for the one who has gone beyond the world, the noble one, this is being without heedlessness, free of old age and death. Why is this? Because he enters the very foremost, permanent, blissful nirvāṇa. For this very reason, one dharma is suffering, and a different dharma is bliss; one dharma is self, and a different dharma is no-self. It is like a person standing on the ground, gazing up at the empty sky, unable to see the tracks left by a bird's flight. Good son, beings are just the same: without the heavenly eye, dwelling amid affliction, they are unable to see for themselves that the Tathāgata's own nature exists within them — and this is why I have taught the secret teaching of no-self. Why is this? Whoever lacks the heavenly eye does not know the true self, and wrongly conceives of a self instead, produced through the conditioned activity that affliction itself constructs — and this is itself impermanence. This is why I say that one dharma is permanent, and a different dharma is impermanent.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan flags a caution the fascicle itself builds directly into Kāśyapa's own question: the five similes of rice, the āmra tree, gold ore, sesame, and rivers reaching the sea are not claims that ripened, ordinary things share the Tathāgata's own uncompounded permanence. Kāśyapa asks this exact question outright — "if these things are permanent, are they the same as the Tathāgata?" — and the Buddha's answer is a flat no: excepting nirvāṇa alone, nothing is truly permanent, and calling ripe fruit "permanent" is only worldly convention, describing something functionally settled and useful, not the absolute permanence unique to nirvāṇa itself. On the grief question that follows, Huiyuan reads the seven similes (the heaven of no-perception, the tree-spirit, the heaven of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, empty space and a house, the magician's illusion, and the ranked knowledge of lower, middle, and higher persons) as building toward a single resolution operating on two levels at once, not a contradiction to be smoothed away: in his own true nature the Tathāgata has no affliction whatsoever, which is the self and bliss side of this sūtra's central fourfold teaching; and in his compassionate responsiveness to suffering beings, he manifests what looks like grief, a skillful appearance rather than a falsehood, exactly as a conjurer's palace is fully vivid and yet has no substance of its own. True to this fascicle's recurring rhetorical move, the text does not fully collapse the paradox even at the end — Kāśyapa's final challenge, that grief and complete awakening cannot coexist, is met not with a synthesis but with the same move that closed the previous fascicle: whether the Tathāgata sorrows or does not sorrow remains, in the text's own words, "the Buddha's own domain," known to no one else.
