The Rākṣasī Wife, and the Water With a Thousand Names

This fascicle completes the Four Noble Truths within 聖行品: nine similes exposing the many faces of craving as the true cause of suffering, from a rākṣasī demoness who devours her own children to a comet's plague across the land; six similes — a physician's medicine, a single word for water spoken in a dozen tongues, a goldsmith's one gold reshaped into every ornament — showing the path itself to be one thing endlessly renamed; the sūtra's own parable of a handful of leaves held up against every leaf in the forest, distinguishing what the Buddha has fully realized from what he has actually taught; and an extended dialogue with Mañjuśrī pressing on what makes any truth "real" at all, closing with the fullest case any rival tradition in this sūtra ever mounts for its own claim to permanence, bliss, self, and purity — a case the Buddha here diagnoses rather than yet refutes point by point, saving that argument for the fascicle to come.

Nine Faces of Craving

Chinese Source

「◎善男子！云何菩薩摩訶薩住於大乘大般涅槃，觀察集諦？善男子！菩薩摩訶薩觀此集諦是陰因緣，所謂集者還愛於有。愛有二種：一愛己身，二愛所須。復有二種：未得五欲，繫心專求；既求得已，堪忍專著。復有三種：欲愛、色愛、無色愛。復有三種：業因緣愛、煩惱因緣愛、苦因緣愛。出家之人有四種愛：何等為四？衣服、飲食、臥具、湯藥。復有五種：貪著五陰，隨諸所須一切愛著，分別挍計無量無邊。善男子！愛有二種：一者善愛，二不善愛。不善愛者，惟愚求之。善法愛者，諸菩薩求。善法愛者復有二種：不善與善。求二乘者名為不善，求大乘者是名為善。善男子！凡夫愛者名之為集，不名為諦。菩薩愛者，名之實諦不名為集。何以故？為度眾生，所以受生，不以愛故而受生也。」

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如佛世尊於餘經中，為諸眾生說業為因緣、或說憍慢、或說六觸、或說無明，為五盛陰而作因緣，今以何義說四聖諦，獨以愛性為五陰因？」

佛讚迦葉菩薩：「善哉，善哉！善男子！如汝所說，諸因緣者非為非因，但是五陰要因於愛。善男子！譬如大王若出遊巡，大臣、眷屬悉皆隨從。愛亦如是，隨愛行處，是諸結等亦復隨行。譬如膩衣，隨有塵著，著則隨住。愛亦如是，隨所愛處，業結亦住。復次善男子！譬如濕地則能生牙，愛亦如是，能生一切業煩惱牙。善男子！菩薩摩訶薩住是大乘大般涅槃，深觀此愛，凡有九種：一如債有餘，二如羅剎女婦，三如妙花莖有毒蛇纏之，四如惡食性所不便而強食之，五如婬女，六如摩樓迦子，七如瘡中息肉，八如暴風，九如彗星。

云何名為如債有餘？善男子！譬如窮人，負他錢財，雖償欲畢，餘未畢故，猶繫在獄，而不得脫。聲聞緣覺亦復如是，以有愛習之餘氣故，不能得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。善男子！是名如債有餘。善男子！云何如羅剎女婦？善男子！譬如有人，以羅剎女而為婦妾，是羅剎女隨所生子，生已便噉，子既盡已復噉其夫。善男子！愛羅剎女亦復如是，隨諸眾生，生善根子，隨生隨食。善子既盡，復噉眾生，令墮地獄、畜生、餓鬼，唯除菩薩，是名如羅剎女婦。善男子！云何如妙花莖毒蛇纏之？譬如有人性愛好花，不見花莖毒蛇過患，即便前捉，捉已蛇螫，螫已命終。一切凡夫亦復如是，貪五欲花，不見是愛毒蛇過患，而便受取，即為愛毒之所蠍螫，命終墮於三惡道中，唯除菩薩，是名如妙花莖毒蛇纏之。善男子！云何所不便食而強食之？譬如有人，所不便食而強食之，食已腹痛，患下而死。愛食亦爾，五道眾生，強食貪著，以是因緣墮三惡道，唯除菩薩，是名所不便食而強食之。

善男子！云何婬女？譬如愚人與婬女通，而彼婬女巧作種種諂媚現親，悉奪是人所有錢財，錢財既盡便復驅逐。愛之婬女亦復如是，愚人無智與之交通，而是愛女奪其所有一切善法，善法既盡，驅逐令墮三惡道中，唯除菩薩，是名婬女。善男子！云何摩樓迦子？譬如摩樓迦子，若鳥食已，隨糞墮地，或因風吹，來在樹下即便生長，纏繞縛束尼拘陀樹，令不增長，遂至枯死。愛摩樓迦子亦復如是，纏縛凡夫所有善法，不令增長遂至枯滅，既枯滅已，命終之後墮三惡道，唯除菩薩，是名摩樓迦子。善男子！云何瘡中息肉？如人久瘡，中生息肉，其人要當勤心療治，莫生捨心，若生捨心，瘡息增長，虫疽復生，以是因緣即便命終。凡夫愚人五陰瘡痍亦復如是，愛於其中而為息肉，應當勤心療治愛息，若不治者，命終即墮三惡道中，唯除菩薩，是名瘡中息肉。善男子！云何暴風？譬如暴風，能偃山移岳，拔深根栽。愛暴大風，亦復如是，於父母所而生惡心，能拔大智舍利弗等無上深固菩提根栽，唯除菩薩，是名暴風。善男子！云何如彗星？譬如彗星出現天下，一切人民飢饉病瘦、癭諸苦惱。愛之彗星，亦復如是，能斷一切善根種子，令凡夫人孤窮飢饉，生煩惱病，流轉生死受種種苦，唯除菩薩，是名彗星。善男子！菩薩摩訶薩住於大乘大般涅槃，觀察愛結，如是九種。

「善男子！以是義故，諸凡夫人有苦無諦，聲聞緣覺有苦有苦諦而無真實，諸菩薩等解苦無苦，是故無苦而有真實。諸凡夫人有集無諦，聲聞緣覺有集有集諦，諸菩薩等解集無集，是故無集而有真諦。聲聞緣覺有滅非真，菩薩摩訶薩有滅有真諦。聲聞緣覺有道非真，菩薩摩訶薩有道有真諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, how does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding within this Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle, contemplate the noble truth of the origin of suffering? Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva observes that this truth of origination is itself the cause and condition of the five aggregates — namely, that what is called 'origin' is nothing other than craving turned back toward existence. Craving is of two kinds: craving for one's own body, and craving for what one requires. It is, further, of two kinds: fixing the mind and pursuing single-mindedly what one has not yet obtained among the five desires, and, having obtained it, clinging to it with settled attachment. It is, further, of three kinds: craving for the realm of desire, craving for the realm of form, and craving for the formless realm. It is, further, of three kinds: craving as the cause and condition of karma, craving as the cause and condition of affliction, and craving as the cause and condition of suffering. For one who has left the household life, craving is of four kinds — what are these four? Craving for robes, for food and drink, for bedding, and for medicine. It is, further, of five kinds: craving and clinging to the five aggregates, and, following whatever one requires, every manner of craving and attachment, distinguished and reckoned without limit or bound. Good son, craving is of two kinds: wholesome craving, and unwholesome craving. Unwholesome craving is sought only by the foolish. Wholesome craving is what every bodhisattva seeks. Wholesome craving is, further, of two kinds: unwholesome and wholesome — seeking the two lesser vehicles is called unwholesome, while seeking the Great Vehicle is called wholesome. Good son, the craving of an ordinary person is called 'origination,' and is not called 'truth.' The craving of a bodhisattva is called the 'real truth,' and is not called 'origination.' Why is this? Because he takes on birth for the sake of saving beings, and does not take on birth on account of craving."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, in other scriptures the World-Honored One has taught beings that karma is their cause and condition, or, at other times, taught that it is pride, or the six kinds of contact, or ignorance that serves as the cause and condition for the five aggregates in their full intensity — by what meaning, then, does the teaching on the Four Noble Truths now say that craving alone is the cause of the five aggregates?"

The Buddha praised the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Excellent, excellent, good son! Just as you have said — none of these other causes and conditions is untrue, yet the five aggregates require craving above all as their cause. Good son, it is like a great king who, when he goes out on royal progress, is followed everywhere by his ministers and retinue; craving is just the same — wherever craving goes, these very fetters follow along with it. It is like a greasy garment: wherever dust touches it, the dust clings and stays. Craving is just the same: wherever it attaches, the fetters of karma likewise remain fixed there. Furthermore, good son, it is like damp ground, which alone is able to sprout a shoot; craving is just the same, able to sprout every shoot of karma and affliction. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding within this Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle, contemplating this craving deeply, finds it to be of nine kinds altogether: like a debt not yet fully repaid; like a rākṣasī wife; like a fine flower's stem, coiled about by a poisonous snake; like food unsuited to one, forced down all the same; like a harlot; like a māluta seed; like proud flesh within a wound; like a fierce gale; and like a comet.

"What is meant by 'like a debt not yet fully repaid'? Good son, it is like a poor man who owes another his money — though he is on the verge of finishing his repayment, because some remainder is still unpaid, he remains bound in prison and cannot gain his release. The śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha are just the same: because some residual scent of craving's habitual imprint still remains in them, they are unable to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. Good son, this is called 'like a debt not yet fully repaid.' Good son, what is meant by 'like a rākṣasī wife'? Good son, it is like a man who takes a rākṣasī demoness as his wife: whatever children this rākṣasī bears, she devours them as soon as they are born, and, once she has devoured every child, she devours her own husband as well. Good son, the rākṣasī of craving is just the same: whatever wholesome-root children beings bring forth, she devours them as fast as they are born; and once every wholesome child is devoured, she then devours the beings themselves, casting them down into hell, among the animals, or among the hungry ghosts — the bodhisattva alone excepted. This is called 'like a rākṣasī wife.' Good son, what is meant by 'like a fine flower's stem, coiled about by a poisonous snake'? It is like a man who, by nature, loves fine flowers, and, not seeing the danger of a poisonous snake coiled about the flower's stem, reaches out at once to seize it — and, having seized it, is bitten by the snake, and, once bitten, dies. Every ordinary being is just the same: craving the flower of the five desires, and not seeing the danger of this poisonous snake of craving, he reaches out and takes it, and is at once stung by the poison of craving, and, upon death, falls into the three evil destinies — the bodhisattva alone excepted. This is called 'like a fine flower's stem, coiled about by a poisonous snake.' Good son, what is meant by 'food unsuited to one, forced down all the same'? It is like a man who, though this food does not agree with him, forces it down all the same, and, having eaten it, suffers pain in the belly, and dies of dysentery. The food of craving is just the same: beings throughout the five destinies force it down and cling to it, and, through this very cause and condition, fall into the three evil destinies — the bodhisattva alone excepted. This is called 'food unsuited to one, forced down all the same.'

"Good son, what is meant by 'the harlot'? It is like a foolish man who consorts with a harlot, while she, skilled in every manner of flattery and false intimacy, seizes every last coin of his money, and, once his money is exhausted, drives him away. The harlot of craving is just the same: the foolish man, lacking wisdom, consorts with her, and this woman of craving seizes every one of his wholesome dharmas, and, once his wholesome dharmas are exhausted, drives him out to fall into the three evil destinies — the bodhisattva alone excepted. This is called 'the harlot.' Good son, what is meant by 'the māluta seed'? It is like the seed of the māluta vine: once a bird has eaten it, the seed falls to the ground within its droppings, or is carried by the wind, and, coming to rest beneath a nyagrodha tree, at once takes root and grows, coiling about and binding fast the nyagrodha tree, so that it can no longer grow, until at last it withers and dies. The māluta seed of craving is just the same: it coils about and binds fast every wholesome dharma an ordinary person possesses, so that it can no longer grow, until at last it withers away entirely, and, once withered away, upon death he falls into the three evil destinies — the bodhisattva alone excepted. This is called 'the māluta seed.' Good son, what is meant by 'proud flesh within a wound'? It is like a man with a long-standing wound, in which proud flesh has grown: he must diligently apply his mind to treating it, and must never give rise to a mind of neglect — for should he give rise to such neglect, the proud flesh within the wound will grow further, and infection will arise again, and, through this very cause and condition, he will die. The wound of the five aggregates in the foolish, ordinary person is just the same: craving grows within it as proud flesh, and he must diligently apply his mind to treating and removing this craving — for should he fail to treat it, upon death he will fall at once into the three evil destinies — the bodhisattva alone excepted. This is called 'proud flesh within a wound.' Good son, what is meant by 'a fierce gale'? It is like a fierce gale, able to level mountains and shift great peaks, uprooting even the deepest-set roots. The fierce, great gale of craving is just the same: giving rise to malice even toward one's own parents, it is able to uproot the deep-set root of unsurpassed bodhi in one as great in wisdom as Śāriputra himself — the bodhisattva alone excepted. This is called 'a fierce gale.' Good son, what is meant by 'a comet'? It is like a comet appearing over the world, bringing famine, sickness, wasting, and goiter, and every kind of suffering, upon all its people. The comet of craving is just the same: it is able to sever the very seed of every wholesome root, leaving the ordinary person destitute and starving, giving rise in him to the sickness of affliction, so that he wanders on through birth and death, undergoing suffering of every kind — the bodhisattva alone excepted. This is called 'a comet.' Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding within this Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle, contemplating the fetter of craving, finds it to be of these nine kinds.

"Good son, it is for this very reason that every ordinary person has suffering but no truth of it; the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha have both suffering and the truth of suffering, but no reality beneath it; the bodhisattva, understanding suffering, sees no suffering at all, and it is for this very reason that, having no suffering, he has this very reality. Every ordinary person has origination but no truth of it; the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha have both origination and the truth of origination; the bodhisattva, understanding origination, sees no origination at all, and it is for this very reason that, having no origination, he has this very real truth. The śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha have cessation, but it is not real; the bodhisattva-mahāsattva has cessation and has the real truth of it. The śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha have the path, but it is not real; the bodhisattva-mahāsattva has the path and has the real truth of it.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Kāśyapa's opening question as a real puzzle, not a rhetorical setup: elsewhere the Buddha names karma, pride, the six contacts, or ignorance as what causes the five aggregates, so why does this passage single out craving alone? His resolution is that none of those other causes is false — the Buddha's answer literally says so — but craving is the load-bearing one among them, the way a king's ministers and retinue follow wherever the king himself goes. Name the king and you have, in effect, named his whole procession; name craving and every other fetter it drags along comes with it. The greasy-garment and damp-ground images make the same point from a different angle: craving is not one cause competing with others so much as the medium in which every other cause takes hold and settles.

On the nine similes themselves, Huiyuan notes a structure easy to miss on a first read: they are not nine interchangeable illustrations of the same point, but fall into four distinct groups by whom they strike. The first (debt not fully repaid) binds even the noble — the arhat and pratyekabuddha, who have severed the coarse afflictions but still carry a residual scent of craving's old habit. The next six (the rākṣasī wife through proud flesh in a wound) harm the ordinary person specifically, each one closing on the same phrase, "the bodhisattva alone excepted." The eighth (the fierce gale uprooting Śāriputra's own root of bodhi) is the one simile in the set that reaches even a figure as advanced as Śāriputra — the sudden animosity craving can provoke even toward one's own parents does not respect rank the way the other similes' harms do. The ninth (the comet) returns to afflicting the ordinary person generally. Reading the nine as this four-part structure rather than a flat list clarifies why the fascicle needed nine similes rather than stopping at three or four: craving's danger has to be shown at every level the path passes through, not only at the bottom.

The Water With a Thousand Names

Chinese Source

「善男子！云何菩薩摩訶薩住於大乘大般涅槃，見滅、見滅諦？所謂斷除一切煩惱，若煩惱斷，則名為常；滅煩惱火，則名寂滅，煩惱滅故則得受樂；諸佛菩薩求因緣故，故名為淨；更不復受二十五有故名出世，以出世故名為我常；於色、聲、香、味、觸、男、女、生、住、滅、苦、樂、不苦、不樂，不取相貌，故名畢竟寂滅真諦。善男子！菩薩如是住於大乘大般涅槃，觀滅聖諦。

「善男子！云何菩薩摩訶薩住於大乘大般涅槃，觀道聖諦？善男子！譬如闇中，因燈得見麁細之物。菩薩摩訶薩亦復如是，住於大乘大般涅槃，因八聖道見一切法，所謂常、無常、有為、無為、有眾生、非眾生、物、非物、苦、樂、我、無我、淨、不淨、煩惱、非煩惱、業、非業、實、不實、乘、非乘、知、不知、陀羅驃、非陀羅驃、求那、非求那、見、非見、色、非色、道、非道、解、非解。善男子！菩薩如是住於大乘大般涅槃，觀道聖諦。」

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！若八聖道是道聖諦，義不相應。何以故？如來或說信心為道，能度諸漏。或時說道不放逸是，諸佛世尊不放逸故，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，亦是菩薩助道之法。或時說言精進是道，如告阿難：『若有人能勤修精進，則得成於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』或時說言觀身念處，若有繫心，精勤修習是身念處，則得成於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。或時說言正定為道，如告大德摩訶迦葉：『夫正定者真實是道，非不正定而是道也；若入正定，乃能思惟五陰生滅，非不入定能思惟也。』或說一法，若人修習，能淨眾生，滅除一切憂愁苦惱，逮得正法，所謂念佛三昧。或復說言修無常想，是名為道，如告比丘：『有能多修無常想者，能得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』或說空寂阿蘭若處，獨坐思惟，能得速成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。或時說言，為人演法，是名為道，若聞法已疑網即斷，疑網斷已則得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。或時說言持戒是道，如告阿難：『若有精勤修持禁戒，是人則度生死大苦。』或時說言親近善友是名為道，如告阿難：『若有親近善知識者，則具淨戒，若有眾生能親近我，則得發於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。』或時說言，修慈是道，修學慈者，斷諸煩惱得不動處。或時說言智慧是道，如佛昔為波闍波提比丘尼說：『姊妹！如諸聲聞，以智慧刀，能斷諸流、諸漏煩惱。』或時如來說施是道，如佛往昔告波斯匿王：『大王！當知我於往昔多行惠施，以是因緣，今日得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』世尊，若八聖道是道諦者，如是等經，豈非虛妄？若彼諸經非虛妄者，彼中何緣不說八道為道聖諦？若彼不說，如來往昔何故錯謬？然我定知諸佛如來，久離錯謬。」

爾時世尊讚迦葉菩薩：「善哉，善哉！善男子！汝今欲知菩薩大乘微妙經典所有祕密，故作是問。善男子！如是諸經，悉入道諦。善男子！如我所說，若有信道，如是信道，是信根本，是能佐助菩提之道，是故我說無有錯謬。善男子！如來善知無量方便，欲化眾生，故作如是種種說法。善男子！譬如良醫，識諸眾生種種病原，隨其所患而為合藥、并藥所禁，唯水一種不在禁例。或服薑水、或甘草水、或細辛水、或黑石蜜水、或阿摩勒水、或尼婆羅水、或鉢晝羅水、或服冷水、或服熱水、或蒲萄水、或安石榴水。善男子！而是良醫，善知眾生所患種種，藥雖多禁，水不在例。如來亦爾，善知方便，於一法相，隨諸眾生，分別廣說種種名相，彼諸眾生隨所說受，受已修習，除斷煩惱，如彼病人，隨良醫教，所患得除。

「復次善男子！如有一人，善解雜語，在大眾中，是諸大眾，熱渴所逼，咸發聲言：『我欲飲水！我欲飲水！』是人即時以清冷水，隨其種類說言是水，或言波尼、或言欝持、或言紗利藍、或言紗利、或言婆耶、或言甘露、或言牛乳，以如是等無量水名，為大眾說。善男子！如來亦爾，以一聖道，為諸聲聞種種演說，從信根等至八聖道。

「復次善男子！譬如金師，以一種金隨意造作種種瓔珞，所謂鉗鎖、環釧釵璫、天冠臂印，雖有如是差別不同，然不離金。善男子！如來亦爾，以一佛道隨諸眾生種種分別，而為說之。或說一種，所謂諸佛一道無二。復說二種，所謂定、慧。復說三種，謂見、慧、智。復說四種，所謂見道、修道、無學道、佛道。復說五種，所謂信行道、法行道、信解脫道、見到道、身證道。復說六種，所謂須陀洹道、斯陀含道、阿那含道、阿羅漢道、辟支佛道、佛道。復說七種，所謂念覺分、擇法覺分、精進覺分、喜覺分、除覺分、定覺分、捨覺分。復說八種，所謂正見、正思惟、正語、正業、正命、正精進、正念、正定。復說九種，所謂八聖道及信。復說十種，所謂十力。復說十一種，所謂十力、大慈。復說十二種，所謂十力、大慈、大悲。復說十三種，所謂十力、大慈、大悲、念佛三昧。復說十六種，所謂十力、大慈、大悲、念佛三昧及佛所得三正念處。復說二十道，所謂十力、四無所畏、大慈、大悲、念佛三昧、三正念處。善男子！是道一體，如來昔日為眾生故種種分別。

「復次善男子！譬如一火，因所然故，得種種名，所謂木火、草火、糠火、䴬火、牛馬糞火。善男子！佛道亦爾，一而無二，為眾生故種種分別。復次善男子！譬如一識，分別說六，若至於眼則名眼識，乃至意識亦復如是。善男子！道亦如是，一而無二，如來為化諸眾生故種種分別。復次善男子！譬如一色，眼所見者，則名為色。耳所聞者，則名為聲。鼻所嗅者，則名為香。舌所甞者，則名為味。身所覺者，則名為觸。善男子！道亦如是，一而無二，如來為欲化眾生故種種分別。善男子！以是義故，以八聖道分，名道聖諦。善男子！是四聖諦，諸佛世尊次第說之，以是因緣，無量眾生得度生死。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, how does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding within this Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle, see cessation, and see the truth of cessation? It is the severing and elimination of every affliction: once affliction is severed, this is called permanence; once the fire of affliction is extinguished, this is called still extinction, and, affliction extinguished, one thereby attains bliss; because every buddha and bodhisattva seeks this cause and condition, it is called purity; because one no longer takes on any of the twenty-five states of existence, it is called transcending the world, and, by transcending the world, it is called the permanent self. With regard to form, sound, scent, taste, touch, male, female, arising, abiding, ceasing, suffering, bliss, and neither suffering nor bliss — grasping at none of these marks — it is therefore called the ultimate, still-extinct real truth. Good son, the bodhisattva, abiding thus within this Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle, contemplates the noble truth of cessation.

"Good son, how does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding within this Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle, contemplate the noble truth of the path? Good son, it is like a lamp within darkness, by which one comes to see things both coarse and subtle. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva is just the same: abiding within this Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle, he sees, by means of the eightfold noble path, every dharma without exception — namely, permanence and impermanence, the conditioned and the unconditioned, sentient being and non-being, thing and non-thing, suffering and bliss, self and no-self, purity and impurity, affliction and non-affliction, karma and non-karma, real and unreal, vehicle and non-vehicle, knowledge and non-knowledge, substance and non-substance, quality and non-quality, view and non-view, form and formlessness, path and non-path, understanding and non-understanding. Good son, the bodhisattva, abiding thus within this Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle, contemplates the noble truth of the path."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if the eightfold noble path alone is the noble truth of the path, the meaning does not fit together. Why is this? At times the Tathāgata has taught that faith itself is the path, able to carry one across every outflow. At times he has taught that heedfulness is the path — since every buddha, every world-honored one, attains unsurpassed, complete awakening precisely through heedfulness, and this, too, is a dharma that aids the bodhisattva's path. At times he has taught that diligent effort is the path, as he told Ānanda: 'Should anyone be able diligently to cultivate effort, he will thereby attain unsurpassed, complete awakening.' At times he has taught the foundation of mindfulness that contemplates the body, saying that should anyone fix his mind and diligently cultivate this foundation of mindfulness, he will thereby attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. At times he has taught that right concentration is the path, as he told the venerable Mahākāśyapa: 'Right concentration alone is truly the path — it is not that what lacks right concentration is also the path; only by entering right concentration is one able to contemplate the arising and ceasing of the five aggregates — it is not that one can contemplate this without entering concentration at all.' At times he has taught a single dharma which, should anyone cultivate it, is able to purify beings, extinguish every sorrow and distress, and attain the true Dharma — namely, the samādhi of mindfulness of the Buddha. At times, again, he has taught that cultivating the notion of impermanence is called the path, as he told the monks: 'Whoever is able extensively to cultivate the notion of impermanence is able to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening.' At times he has taught that sitting alone in empty, quiet forest dwelling and contemplating is able swiftly to bring about unsurpassed, complete awakening. At times he has taught that expounding the Dharma for others is called the path — for, once one has heard the Dharma, the net of doubt is at once severed, and, this net of doubt severed, one then attains unsurpassed, complete awakening. At times he has taught that upholding the precepts is the path, as he told Ānanda: 'Should anyone diligently uphold the restraining precepts, this person will thereby cross over the great suffering of birth and death.' At times he has taught that drawing near to good friends is called the path, as he told Ānanda: 'Should anyone draw near to a good and wise friend, he will thereby be furnished with pure precepts; should any being be able to draw near to me, he will thereby give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening.' At times he has taught that cultivating loving-kindness is the path — one who cultivates and learns loving-kindness severs every affliction and attains the unshakable place. At times he has taught that wisdom is the path, as the Buddha once told the bhikṣuṇī Mahāprajāpatī: 'Sister, it is just as with every śrāvaka: with the very blade of wisdom, one is able to sever every stream and every outflow and affliction.' At times the Tathāgata has taught that giving is the path, as the Buddha once told King Prasenajit: 'Great King, you should know that, in ages past, I practiced giving and charity extensively, and it is through this very cause and condition that I have today attained unsurpassed, complete awakening.' World-Honored One, if the eightfold noble path alone is the truth of the path, are not scriptures such as these, then, false? And if those scriptures are not false, then for what reason do they fail to name the eightfold path as the noble truth of the path? And if they fail to name it, then why did the Tathāgata, in ages past, make such an error? And yet I know for certain that every buddha, every tathāgata, has, for a very long time now, been entirely free of error."

At that time, the World-Honored One praised the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Excellent, excellent, good son! You now wish to know every secret held within the Great Vehicle's own subtle and wondrous scriptures, and this is why you ask this question. Good son, every one of these scriptures is included within the truth of the path. Good son, as I have taught it: wherever there is faith in the path, this very faith in the path is itself the root of faith, and is itself a dharma able to assist the path of bodhi — this is why I say there is no error in it at all. Good son, the Tathāgata, skillfully knowing immeasurable skillful means, wishing to transform beings, therefore teaches in these many different ways. Good son, it is like a skilled physician who, knowing the various origins of the various illnesses of beings, compounds medicine to suit what each patient suffers, together with what each medicine forbids — water alone, of every substance, falling under no such prohibition. He may prescribe water infused with ginger, or with licorice, or with asarum, or with dark rock-sugar, or with āmalaka, or with nimba, or with pāṭala, or plain cold water, or plain hot water, or grape-water, or pomegranate-water. Good son, this skilled physician, well knowing the various afflictions of beings, forbids many things among his medicines, yet never forbids water. The Tathāgata is just the same: skillfully knowing expedient means, with regard to a single dharma-mark, he distinguishes it and expounds it at length, according to each being, in many different names and marks; and these beings, each receiving what is taught to him, and, having received it, cultivating it, thereby remove and sever their afflictions — just as the sick person, following the skilled physician's instruction, is thereby cured of what afflicts him.

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a man, skilled in many languages, standing amid a great assembly — this assembly, pressed by heat and thirst, cries out together: 'I wish to drink water! I wish to drink water!' This man, at once bringing them clear, cool water, calls it by its name according to each group's own tongue — calling it, in turn, pānīya, or udaka, or salila, or sāra, or vāri, or nectar, or simply cow's milk — expounding it to the assembly under these countless names for water. Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same: with a single noble path, he expounds it to the various śrāvakas in many different ways, from the faculty of faith through to the eightfold noble path.

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a goldsmith who, from a single kind of gold, fashions at will every manner of ornament — clasps and chains, bracelets and armlets, earrings and pendants, a crown fit for a god, an armband — and though these differ from one another in this way, none of them ever departs from the gold itself. Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same: with a single buddha-path, he distinguishes it in many different ways, according to each being, and teaches it accordingly. At times he teaches it as a single kind — namely, that every buddha has this one path, without a second. At times he teaches it as two kinds — namely, concentration and wisdom. At times he teaches it as three kinds — namely, view, discernment, and wisdom. At times he teaches it as four kinds — namely, the path of seeing, the path of cultivation, the path beyond further training, and the path of buddhahood. At times he teaches it as five kinds — namely, the path of one who follows through faith, the path of one who follows through the Dharma, the path of liberation through faith, the path of one who has reached the view, and the path of one who has attained bodily realization. At times he teaches it as six kinds — namely, the path of stream-entry, of once-returning, of non-returning, of arhatship, of pratyekabuddhahood, and of buddhahood. At times he teaches it as seven kinds — namely, the awakening-factors of mindfulness, investigation of dharmas, energy, joy, tranquility, concentration, and equanimity. At times he teaches it as eight kinds — namely, right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. At times he teaches it as nine kinds — namely, the eightfold noble path together with faith. At times he teaches it as ten kinds — namely, the ten powers. At times he teaches it as eleven kinds — namely, the ten powers together with great loving-kindness. At times he teaches it as twelve kinds — namely, the ten powers, great loving-kindness, and great compassion. At times he teaches it as thirteen kinds — namely, the ten powers, great loving-kindness, great compassion, and the samādhi of mindfulness of the Buddha. At times he teaches it as sixteen kinds — namely, the ten powers, great loving-kindness, great compassion, the samādhi of mindfulness of the Buddha, and the three foundations of right mindfulness attained by a buddha. At times he teaches it as twenty paths — namely, the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, great loving-kindness, great compassion, the samādhi of mindfulness of the Buddha, and the three foundations of right mindfulness. Good son, this path is one single essence — it is only that the Tathāgata, in ages past, distinguished it in many different ways for the sake of beings.

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a single fire, which, according to what feeds it, takes on many different names — a wood-fire, a grass-fire, a chaff-fire, a bran-fire, a fire of cattle- or horse-dung. Good son, the path of buddhahood is just the same: one, without a second, yet distinguished in many different ways for the sake of beings. Furthermore, good son, it is like a single consciousness, distinguished and spoken of as six: when it reaches the eye, it is called eye-consciousness, and so forth up through mind-consciousness, just the same. Good son, the path is just the same: one, without a second, yet the Tathāgata, in order to transform the various beings, distinguishes it in many different ways. Furthermore, good son, it is like a single object: what the eye sees is called form; what the ear hears is called sound; what the nose smells is called scent; what the tongue tastes is called flavor; what the body senses is called touch. Good son, the path is just the same: one, without a second, yet the Tathāgata, wishing to transform beings, distinguishes it in many different ways. Good son, it is for this very reason that the eightfold noble path is named the noble truth of the path. Good son, these Four Noble Truths are taught by every buddha, every world-honored one, in this same sequence, and it is through this very cause and condition that immeasurable beings attain liberation from birth and death."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

On the truth of cessation, Huiyuan reads the closing list — form, sound, scent, taste, touch, male, female, arising, abiding, ceasing, suffering, bliss, neither-suffering-nor-bliss — as thirteen marks whose absence defines "the ultimate, still-extinct real truth": the first ten recur often elsewhere in the sūtra, but the final three (arising, abiding, ceasing) are, on his count, treated this precisely only here. He also flags 陀羪驃 and 求那 in the path-list as transliterations of technical terms from outside the Buddhist schools proper — dravya (substance, that which underlies a quality) and guṇa (an attribute inhering in a substance) — categories the sūtra invokes and then declares the eightfold path sees through, rather than adopting as its own analytic scheme.

On Kāśyapa's challenge, Huiyuan reads all fourteen alternative "paths" he lists as a single rhetorical move: each was spoken to a specific audience about a specific dharma the Buddha judged that audience needed most, not offered as a rival, complete definition of the path meant to compete with the eightfold path itself. The six similes that follow build the same point from six different angles rather than repeating it: the physician's water shows the path as one thing suited to endless application (隨治為多); the many names for water spoken to a thirsty crowd show it as one thing named according to what a given audience wants to hear (隨欲分多); the goldsmith's gold shows it as one thing counted out according to the practitioner's own capacity and rank (隨根為多) — and it is this third simile's numbered list that carries the most technical freight. Huiyuan notes that in the threefold division ("view, discernment, wisdom"), 見 (view) is not yet settled — it moves together with doubt, still searching — while 智 (wisdom) is a settled cognition that has already reached its object and no longer searches; and that on the fivefold division, two distinct scholastic traditions disagree about exactly where 信行/法行 and 信解脫/見到 fall relative to the path of seeing and the path of cultivation — the *Satyasiddhi* places the first pair before the path of seeing and the second pair after it, while the *Madhyama Āgama* places both pairs within the path of cultivation itself. The last three similes (fire, consciousness, and the sense-object list) return to reinforce the first three in turn — fire again shows one thing serving many applications, consciousness again shows one thing counted by capacity, and the sense-objects again show one thing named according to the audience's own faculty — closing a six-part argument whose real target, on Huiyuan's reading, is not the number eight at all, but the mistake of treating any single enumeration as though it exhausted what the path actually is.

The water-names themselves — 波尼, 欝持, 紗利藍, 紗利, 婆耶 — Huiyuan himself treats as likely regional or dialectal variants for "water" rather than confidently identified Sanskrit terms, and this collection follows his own hedge rather than reconstructing more precision than the commentary claims; the two names the sūtra gives that are not transliterations at all — 甘露 ("nectar," a term this collection has kept as an English word since Part 8) and 牛乳 ("cow's milk") — are translated rather than transliterated accordingly.

Meanings of this kind, too, I have never taught within those other scriptures. Good son, knowing the extinguishing of affliction is called middling wisdom; distinguishing affliction as beyond number and reckoning, and its extinguishing likewise beyond number and reckoning — this is not known by any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, and is called superior wisdom. Meanings of this kind, too, I have never taught within those other scriptures. Meanings of this kind, too, I have never taught within those other scriptures. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the eleven parallel refrains in the Buddha's answer as one deliberately repetitive structure: eight of them break down the five aggregates one by one (a general opening on the aggregates as a set, then form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness each taken singly, for a total of eight clauses, since form alone is subdivided differently from the rest), with one clause each for the sense-fields, the elements, craving, cessation, the path, worldly truth, and the ultimate truth. In every single case the pattern is identical: the middling wisdom of the two lesser vehicles grasps a thing under one general aspect (aggregates are suffering, feeling has a felt quality, and so on), while the superior wisdom of a buddha or bodhisattva sees the same thing resolved into an unlimited number of particular instances — and it is this unlimited resolution, not some undisclosed additional fact, that the Buddha means when he says he has "never taught" these meanings in other scriptures. Nothing new is being withheld; the same four truths are being known at an incomparably finer grain.

Huiyuan glosses "有量" (limited) and "無量" (unlimited) — his terms for middling and superior wisdom respectively — along three distinct axes, not one: by scope (the two lesser vehicles' truths of suffering and origination cover only conditioned rebirth and its karmic history, while the Great Vehicle's cover both that and the further "changeable" rebirth a bodhisattva still undergoes even after leaving conditioned existence behind); by depth (the *Abhidharma* schools treat suffering as simply, flatly suffering, and the *Satyasiddhi* stops at the truths' bare conventional existence, while the Great Vehicle presses on to the underlying tathāgatagarbha itself, citing the *Śrīmālā Sūtra*'s "the tathāgatagarbha is spoken of at the very place of the noble truths"); and by grain (a śrāvaka knows suffering only as a single general category, while a bodhisattva can resolve one instance of suffering into countless finer particulars within it). The passage's own repeated phrase "無量無邊不可稱計" (immeasurable, boundless, beyond number and reckoning) draws on this third sense most directly, on his reading — depth and scope explain why the Great Vehicle's truths reach further, but it is this fine-grained resolution that explains why the very same four truths, taught to the very same assembly, can still be called, in one breath, both fully spoken and never yet spoken at all.

"Furthermore, good son, whatever is burned, or cut, or dies, or is destroyed — this is called worldly truth; the absence of burning, cutting, dying, or destruction is called the ultimate truth. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan places this whole dialogue against a fourfold map of how Buddhist teaching in general handles the two truths, from shallow to deep: a school that affirms each dharma's own fixed nature (立性宗, the coarsest reading), a school that negates that fixed nature while still granting conventional existence (破性宗), a school that negates the very mark of causal arising itself (破相宗), and a school that affirms an underlying real nature beneath all negation (顯實宗, the deepest). Every one of these four schools, on his account, still operates with some version of "two truths" — the sūtra's own answer to Mañjuśrī does not pick one school and discard the rest, but works through the first three registers in turn (seven distinct sub-analyses within them alone) before letting the deepest reading absorb and re-interpret everything said at the shallower levels, which is why the passage can seem to shift definitions of "worldly truth" several times without contradicting itself: each definition is correct at its own depth.

The closing "one person, many names" simile belongs, on Huiyuan's reading, specifically to the deepest of these four registers. He traces "arising through cause and condition" itself through six increasingly subtle senses across the wider commentarial tradition — arising as a bare Abhidharma event, as impermanent, momentary dharmas, as conventionally-existing-but-essence-less phenomena per the *Satyasiddhi*, as illusion-like appearance, as dream-like projection, and finally as what he calls "true arising" — buddha-nature itself, likened to water giving rise to waves without ceasing to be water. The runner-reaper-cook-smith simile illustrates only this sixth and final sense: one and the same underlying reality, never becoming many things, merely called by different names as it takes on different functions — which is why the passage pairs it directly with dependent origination itself being named the ultimate truth, rather than with any of the five shallower senses of arising the same phrase could otherwise carry.

Good son, what is called cessation is named the extinguishing of affliction, and is both permanent and impermanent: what the two lesser vehicles attain is called impermanent, while what every buddha attains is called permanent, and is also called a dharma realized — this is the real truth of it. Good son, the nature of the Tathāgata is not called cessation, but is able to extinguish affliction; it is neither permanent nor impermanent, is not called something realized, but abides forever, unchanging — it is, for this very reason, reality; empty space and buddha-nature are just the same. Good son, the path is able to sever affliction, and is both permanent and impermanent, a dharma that can be cultivated — this is called the real truth of it. The Tathāgata is not the path, yet is able to sever affliction; he is neither permanent nor impermanent, nor a dharma that can be cultivated, but abides forever, unchanging — it is, for this very reason, reality; empty space and buddha-nature are just the same.

What is truly real is itself empty space, and empty space is itself what is truly real. What is truly real is itself buddha-nature, and buddha-nature is itself what is truly real. Mañjuśrī, there is suffering, the cause of suffering, the ending of suffering, and the counter-agent to suffering; the Tathāgata is neither suffering nor, indeed, this counter-agent — he is, for this very reason, reality, and is not called a truth; empty space and buddha-nature are just the same. Suffering is conditioned, is subject to outflow, and is without bliss; the Tathāgata is neither conditioned nor subject to outflow, but is tranquil and at ease — he is reality, and not a truth."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan draws out a three-tier vocabulary this passage depends on but never defines outright: suffering (a bare, coarse fact — pressing distress), truth (that same fact plus the causal pattern by which it does or does not arise, stated without error), and reality (the underlying nature the fact and its pattern both rest on — for suffering specifically, the tathāgatagarbha itself, which "never arose and so will never cease, its own nature forever still"). Every one of the four noble truths, on his reading, carries all three tiers at once; what makes the Tathāgata, empty space, and buddha-nature different is that none of them has the first two tiers at all — they are reality without ever being the coarse fact or its causal truth in the first place, which is exactly why the text can say, in the same breath, that they are "not suffering, not its truth" and yet "are its reality."

He also flags a distinction easy to flatten in translation: Mañjuśrī's own question treats the Tathāgata, empty space, and buddha-nature as apparently identical, and the Buddha's answer, read carefully, agrees they share one reality without quite making them synonyms. The Tathāgata named here is specifically the Dharma-body, already arrived at ultimate purity, with no residue of birth-and-death defilement left to speak of — this is "result." Buddha-nature is the very same purity considered from where an ordinary being still stands, still covered over by the confusion of birth and death, its full manifestation yet to come — this is "cause." Empty space is neither cause nor result, but the bare fact of this reality's own equality, its being apart from every determinate nature whatsoever — the ground on which cause and result alike stand, without itself ripening into either. The five-part pattern applied identically to suffering, origination, cessation, and the path (state the ordinary mark, name its shallower reality, deny the Tathāgata bears that mark, conclude he is only its reality, then extend the same conclusion to empty space and buddha-nature) is, on Huiyuan's reading, this same fascicle's most concentrated statement of how a single underlying reality can be spoken of under three names without becoming three different things.

Mañjuśrī said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught, what is free of inversion is called the real truth. If this is so, then do the four truths themselves contain the four inversions? If they do, how can it be said that what is free of inversion is called the real truth, and that whatever is inverted is not called real?"

The Buddha told Mañjuśrī: "Every inversion is subsumed within the truth of suffering — as when any being holds an inverted mind, this is called inversion. Good son, it is like a person who does not accept the instruction of his parents and elders, or who, having accepted it, is unable to act in accordance with it and practice it — a person of this kind is called inverted, and this inversion is nothing other than suffering — it is not that it is not suffering, but exactly suffering itself."

Mañjuśrī said: "As the Buddha has taught, what is free of falsehood is itself the real truth; if this is so, one should know that falsehood, then, is not the real truth."

The Buddha said: "Good son, every falsehood is subsumed within the truth of suffering — as when a being deceives another, and, through this very cause and condition, falls into hell, among the animals, or among the hungry ghosts; a dharma of this kind is called falsehood, and this falsehood is nothing other than suffering — it is not that it is not suffering, but exactly suffering itself. The śrāvaka, the pratyekabuddha, and every buddha, every world-honored one, keep far from it and do not engage in it, and it is for this reason that it is called falsehood. Falsehood of this kind is severed and eliminated by every buddha and by the two lesser vehicles, and it is for this reason that it is called the real truth."

Mañjuśrī said: "As the Buddha has taught, if the Great Vehicle is the real truth, one should know that the vehicles of the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha are, then, not real."

The Buddha said: "Mañjuśrī, those two vehicles are both real and unreal. The śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha sever every affliction, and this is called real. Yet they are impermanent, do not abide, and are subject to change — this is called unreal."

Mañjuśrī said: "As the Buddha has taught, if what the Buddha teaches is called real, one should know that what Māra teaches is, then, unreal. World-Honored One, is what Māra teaches subsumed within the noble truths, or not?"

The Buddha said: "Mañjuśrī, what Māra teaches is subsumed within two of the truths, namely suffering and its origin. Whatever is entirely without Dharma, without discipline, and is unable to bring any benefit to anyone — though taught the whole day through, no one thereby ever sees suffering, severs its origin, realizes cessation, or cultivates the path — this is called falsehood; falsehood of this kind is called the teaching of Māra."

Mañjuśrī said: "As the Buddha has taught, the one path is pure, without a second. Yet the followers of every other tenet likewise say: 'I, too, have one path, pure, without a second.' If this one path is said to be the real truth, how does it differ from what these other tenets hold? And if there is no difference, one should not say that this one path alone is pure."

The Buddha said: "Good son, the followers of these other tenets have the truths of suffering and of origination, but lack the truths of cessation and of the path. Amid what is not cessation, they give rise to the notion of cessation; amid what is not the path, they give rise to the notion of the path; amid what is not a true result, they give rise to the notion of a result; amid what is not a true cause, they give rise to the notion of a cause. It is for this very reason that they do not have this one path, pure, without a second."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Mañjuśrī's five short challenges here as one continuous line of attack rather than five separate questions: each takes a criterion the Buddha has just used to define the real truth — freedom from inversion, freedom from falsehood, being the Great Vehicle, being buddha-speech, being a single undivided path — and turns it back on the four truths, the two lesser vehicles, or the Buddha's own teaching to show it apparently failing its own test. The Buddha's answer follows the same move each time: what looked like a hidden defect in the "real truth" claim turns out to be already and correctly accounted for elsewhere. Inversion and falsehood are not exceptions smuggled into the four truths — they simply are the truth of suffering, correctly classified there rather than left as an embarrassment. The two lesser vehicles are not simply unreal — they are real in exactly the respect that they have severed affliction, and unreal in exactly the respect that impermanence and change still apply to them; both statements hold without contradiction because they answer to different aspects of the same thing. And when the challenge finally reaches other traditions' own claim to "one pure path," Huiyuan reads the Buddha's answer as turning on a structural gap rather than a value judgment: those traditions are not condemned for teaching something false about suffering and its origin — the sūtra grants they have real truths there — but for mistaking something else entirely for cessation and the path, the two truths their own systems never actually reach.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this entire catalogue as Mañjuśrī voicing the strongest form of an objection on the assembly's behalf, not as his own position — the sūtra lets the case for the opposing view run to its full length, eight arguments for permanence, five for bliss, three for purity, and eight for a self, precisely so that what follows can answer the case at its strongest rather than a caricature of it. He is careful to note what the Buddha's closing rebuke in this same fascicle does and does not accomplish. What follows immediately — the extended moral portrait of these thinkers as bound in craving, covered by ignorance, drinking salt water for thirst, refusing the physician who could cure them — diagnoses what is wrong with holding views of this kind and the state of mind that clings to them; it does not yet take apart any of the twenty-four individual arguments Mañjuśrī has just laid out one by one. That point-by-point dismantling — the killer-and-victim argument, the counting argument, the recitation argument, and all the rest, taken up in the same order they were raised here — is exactly what the next fascicle undertakes, continuing this same dialogue without a change of speaker or setting. A reader arriving at this fascicle's close should not mistake the moral rebuke for the logical one; the sūtra is quite deliberately holding the second in reserve.

On the individual arguments themselves, Huiyuan's own commentary (as it survives) moves fastest through the permanence arguments, since these receive the fullest treatment ahead: he flags that the killing argument and the memory arguments are answered first and together in the next fascicle, the five arguments concerning habituation, counting, recitation, and physical continuity are answered together as a block, and the property-list argument (vessels, carts, mountains, and the rest) is answered last and separately. For this fascicle's own purposes, he draws attention to what the whole list has in common structurally: nearly every argument, across all four categories, infers a permanent underlying entity from the mere fact of continuity, function, or desire — that recognition, counting, craving, or obstruction are possible at all is repeatedly taken to prove that something fixed must underlie them. This collection has kept the catalogue at its full length, following the sūtra's own choice not to abridge it, since the argument the next fascicle answers is precisely this catalogue, not a summary of it.
