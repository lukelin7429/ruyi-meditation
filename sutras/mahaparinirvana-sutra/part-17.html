What Was, What Wasn't

This fascicle names the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge a bodhisattva attains through the eightfold seeing and knowing of the previous fascicle, and argues at length that even Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, and Mahākauṣṭhila — foremost among the Buddha's own disciples in wisdom, supernatural power, and unimpeded knowledge respectively — never actually possess all four in full. It then turns to a teaching that a bodhisattva's own knowledge is itself best described as "non-attainment," before Kāśyapa presses the Buddha to explain, in full, the most celebrated verse he spoke to Cunda on the night of his parinirvāṇa: "what was is now not, what was not is now" — unpacked here through eight paired contrasts between the Buddha's own past and present. The fascicle closes on the three trainings of precepts, concentration, and wisdom, and a warning against reciting this very sūtra while breaking the precepts it teaches.

Four Kinds of Unimpeded Knowledge

Chinese Source

◎迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！菩薩摩訶薩能如是知，得何等利？」

佛言：「善男子！菩薩摩訶薩能如是知，得四無礙：法無礙、義無礙、辭無礙、樂說無礙。法無礙者，知一切法及法名字。義無礙者，知一切法所有諸義，能隨諸法所立名字而為作義。辭無礙者，隨字論、正音論、闡陀論、世辯論。樂說無礙者，所謂菩薩摩訶薩凡所演說，無有障礙、不可動轉、無所畏省、難可摧伏。善男子！是名菩薩能如是見知，即得如是四無礙智。

「復次善男子！法無礙者，菩薩摩訶薩，遍知聲聞、緣覺、菩薩、諸佛之法。義無礙者，乘雖有三，知其歸一，終不謂有差別之相。辭無礙者，菩薩摩訶薩於一法中，作種種名，經無量劫，說不可盡；聲聞緣覺能作是說，無有是處。樂說無礙者，菩薩摩訶薩於無量劫，為諸眾生演說諸法，若名、若義、種種異說，不可窮盡。

「復次善男子！法無礙者，菩薩摩訶薩雖知諸法，而不取著。義無礙者，菩薩摩訶薩雖知諸義，而亦不著。辭無礙者，菩薩摩訶薩雖知名字，亦不取著。樂說無礙者，菩薩摩訶薩雖知樂說，如是最上，而亦不著。何以故？善男子，若取著者，不名菩薩。」

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！若不取著，則不知法；若知法者，則是取著。若知不著，則無所知。云何如來說言知法而不取著？」

佛言：「善男子！夫取著者，不名無礙；無所取著，乃名無礙。善男子！是故一切諸菩薩等，有取著者，則無無礙；若無無礙，不名菩薩，當知是人名為凡夫。何故取著名為凡夫？一切凡夫，取著於色乃至著識。以著色故，則生貪心；生貪心故，為色繫縛，乃至為識之所繫縛；以繫縛故，則不得免生、老、病、死、憂悲、大苦一切煩惱，是故取著名為凡夫。以是義故，一切凡夫，無四無閡。善男子！菩薩摩訶薩已於無量阿僧祇劫，知見法相，以知見故，則知其義；以見法相及知義故，而於色中不生繫著，乃至識中亦復如是；以不著故，菩薩於色不生貪心，乃至識中亦不生貪；以無貪故，則不為色之所繫縛，乃至不為識之所縛；以不縛故，則得脫於生老病死、憂悲大苦、一切煩惱。以是義故，一切菩薩得四無礙。善男子！以是因緣，我為弟子十二部中說繫著者名為魔縛，若不著者則脫魔縛。譬如世間有罪之人，為王所縛，無罪之人王不能縛。菩薩摩訶薩亦復如是，有繫著者，為魔所縛，無繫著者魔不能縛。以是義故，菩薩摩訶薩而無所著。◎

「◎復次善男子！法無礙者，菩薩摩訶薩善知字持，而不忘失。所謂持者，如地、如山、如眼、如雲、如人、如母，一切諸法亦復如是。義無礙者，菩薩雖知諸法名字而不知義，得義無礙，則知於義。云何知義？謂地持者，如地普持一切眾生及非眾生，以是義故，名地為持。善男子！謂山持者，菩薩摩訶薩作是思惟：『何故名山而為持耶？山能持地，令無傾動，是故名持。何故復名眼為持耶？眼能持光，故名為持。何故復名雲為持耶？雲名龍氣，龍氣持水，故名雲持。何故復名人為持耶？人能持法及以非法，故名人持。何故復名母為持耶？母能持子，故名母持。』菩薩摩訶薩知一切法名字、句義，亦復如是。辭無礙者，菩薩摩訶薩以種種辭，演說一義，亦無有義，猶如男女、舍宅車乘、眾生等名。何故無義？善男子！夫義者，乃是菩薩諸佛境界，辭者凡夫境界。以知義故，得辭無礙。樂說無礙者，菩薩摩訶薩知辭、知義故，於無量阿僧祇劫，說辭、說義而不可盡，是名樂說無礙。

「善男子！菩薩摩訶薩於無量無邊阿僧祇劫，修行世諦，以修行故，知法無礙。復於無量阿僧祇劫，修第一義諦故，得義無礙。亦於無量阿僧祇劫，習毘伽羅那論故，得辭無礙。亦於無量阿僧祇劫，修習說世諦論故，得樂說無礙。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what benefit does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva obtain, being able to know in this way?"

The Buddha said: "Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, being able to know in this way, attains four kinds of unimpeded knowledge: unimpeded knowledge of the Dharma, unimpeded knowledge of meaning, unimpeded knowledge of expression, and unimpeded knowledge of eloquence. Unimpeded knowledge of the Dharma means knowing every dharma and every dharma's own name. Unimpeded knowledge of meaning means knowing every meaning possessed by every dharma, able to construct its meaning according to whatever name has been given to it. Unimpeded knowledge of expression means mastery of etymological analysis, of correct pronunciation, of poetic composition, and of worldly rhetoric. Unimpeded knowledge of eloquence means that whatever the bodhisattva-mahāsattva expounds is entirely without obstruction, cannot be shaken or turned aside, is fearless and unhesitating, and is difficult for anyone to overcome. Good son, this is what is meant by a bodhisattva able to see and know in this way, thereby attaining these four kinds of unimpeded knowledge.

"Furthermore, good son, unimpeded knowledge of the Dharma means that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva thoroughly knows the dharma of the śrāvaka, the pratyekabuddha, the bodhisattva, and every buddha. Unimpeded knowledge of meaning means that, though there are three vehicles, he knows that they return to a single one, and never once holds that they bear any mark of real difference. Unimpeded knowledge of expression means that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, with regard to a single dharma, is able to construct names of every kind, and could speak of it throughout immeasurable kalpas without ever exhausting them — that a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha could speak in this way is simply not possible. Unimpeded knowledge of eloquence means that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, throughout immeasurable kalpas, expounds every dharma for the sake of beings — whether its name or its meaning — in every kind of varied exposition, without ever exhausting it.

"Furthermore, good son, unimpeded knowledge of the Dharma means that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, though he knows every dharma, grasps at none of them. Unimpeded knowledge of meaning means that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, though he knows every meaning, likewise grasps at none of them. Unimpeded knowledge of expression means that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, though he knows every name, likewise grasps at none of them. Unimpeded knowledge of eloquence means that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, though he knows this eloquence to be the very finest, likewise grasps at none of it. Why is this? Good son, should one grasp at these things, one would not be called a bodhisattva at all."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said further to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if one grasps at nothing, then one does not know the Dharma; and if one does know the Dharma, then one must be grasping at it. If one knows without grasping, then one knows nothing at all. How, then, can the Tathāgata say that one knows the Dharma and yet grasps at none of it?"

The Buddha said: "Good son, whoever grasps at things is not called unimpeded; only one who grasps at nothing at all is called unimpeded. Good son, it is for this very reason that, among every bodhisattva, whoever grasps at things has no unimpeded knowledge at all; and, lacking unimpeded knowledge, he is not called a bodhisattva — one should know that such a person is called an ordinary being instead. Why is grasping called the mark of an ordinary being? Every ordinary being grasps at form, and so on, up through grasping at consciousness. Because he grasps at form, craving arises in him; and, craving having arisen, he becomes bound by form, and so on, up through being bound by consciousness; and, being so bound, he is unable to escape birth, old age, illness, death, sorrow and grief, great suffering, and every affliction — it is for this very reason that grasping is called the mark of an ordinary being. It is for this very reason that every ordinary being lacks the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, having already, throughout immeasurable incalculable kalpas, known and seen the mark of every dharma, thereby knows its meaning as well; and, having seen the mark of every dharma and known its meaning, he gives rise to no binding attachment toward form, and so on, up through consciousness, in just the same way; and, being free of attachment, the bodhisattva gives rise to no craving toward form, and so on, up through consciousness, likewise giving rise to no craving; and, being free of craving, he is not bound by form, and so on, up through not being bound by consciousness; and, being unbound, he thereby attains liberation from birth, old age, illness, death, sorrow and grief, great suffering, and every affliction. It is for this very reason that every bodhisattva attains the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge. Good son, it is through this very cause and condition that I have taught my disciples, within the twelve divisions of the scriptures, that binding attachment is called the bondage of Māra, and that whoever is free of attachment is thereby freed from the bondage of Māra. It is like a guilty man in the world, who is bound by the king, while an innocent man cannot be bound by the king at all. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva is just the same: whoever has binding attachment is bound by Māra, and whoever is free of binding attachment cannot be bound by Māra at all. It is for this very reason that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva grasps at nothing whatsoever.

"Furthermore, good son, unimpeded knowledge of the Dharma means that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva is well able to retain every letter, without ever forgetting it. What is meant by 'retaining' here is: as the earth retains, as a mountain retains, as the eye retains, as a cloud retains, as a person retains, as a mother retains — every dharma is just the same. Unimpeded knowledge of meaning means this: though a bodhisattva knows the names of every dharma, he might not yet know their meaning; attaining unimpeded knowledge of meaning, he then knows this meaning. How does he know this meaning? As for the earth's retaining, it is like the earth, which universally retains every sentient and insentient thing — it is for this reason that the earth is named 'retaining.' Good son, as for a mountain's retaining, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva reflects in this way: 'Why is a mountain, too, named "retaining"? A mountain is able to retain the earth, keeping it from collapsing — it is for this reason that it, too, is named "retaining." Why, further, is the eye named "retaining"? The eye is able to retain light — it is for this reason that it is named "retaining." Why, further, is a cloud named "retaining"? A cloud is called the breath of the nāga, and the nāga's breath retains water — it is for this reason that a cloud is named "retaining." Why, further, is a person named "retaining"? A person is able to retain the Dharma and non-Dharma alike — it is for this reason that a person is named "retaining." Why, further, is a mother named "retaining"? A mother is able to retain her own child — it is for this reason that a mother is named "retaining."' The bodhisattva-mahāsattva knows the names and the meanings of every dharma's phrases in just this same way. Unimpeded knowledge of expression means that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, using every kind of expression, expounds a single meaning — and yet there is no real meaning in the expression itself, just as with the names 'man' and 'woman,' 'house' and 'carriage,' or 'being.' Why is there no meaning in these? Good son, meaning itself is the domain of the bodhisattva and of every buddha, while expression is the domain of the ordinary being. It is through knowing meaning that one attains unimpeded knowledge of expression. Unimpeded knowledge of eloquence means that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, knowing both expression and meaning, is able, throughout immeasurable incalculable kalpas, to speak of expression and of meaning without ever exhausting them — this is called unimpeded knowledge of eloquence.

"Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, throughout immeasurable, boundless incalculable kalpas, cultivates worldly truth, and, through this very cultivation, attains unimpeded knowledge of the Dharma. He further, throughout immeasurable incalculable kalpas, cultivates the ultimate truth, and thereby attains unimpeded knowledge of meaning. He further, throughout immeasurable incalculable kalpas, studies the treatises on grammar, and thereby attains unimpeded knowledge of expression. He further, throughout immeasurable incalculable kalpas, cultivates the treatises expounding worldly truth, and thereby attains unimpeded knowledge of eloquence."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the sūtra as defining the same four terms three separate times, each pass isolating a different axis rather than repeating itself: the first pass defines them by their object (knowing dharmas and their names; knowing meaning; mastering four registers of language — etymology, correct pronunciation, poetic composition, and worldly argument; and unshakeable eloquence in delivery); the second pass defines them by scope, contrasting a bodhisattva's grasp of all three vehicles as ultimately one against the narrower reach of expression and eloquence a śrāvaka could never match; and the third pass defines them by the crucial qualifier of non-attachment — knowing without grasping — which Kāśyapa immediately seizes on as apparently self-contradictory (how can one know something one does not, in some sense, hold onto?). The Buddha's answer distinguishes ordinary grasping, which chains an unenlightened being to the five aggregates through the sequence craving → bondage → inability to escape suffering, from a bodhisattva's knowing, which has already passed through and beyond that same chain in reverse (seeing → knowing meaning → freedom from attachment → freedom from craving → freedom from bondage → liberation) — "grasping" and "knowing without grasping" are not two answers to the same question but two different relationships to the object entirely, the first constitutive of bondage and the second constitutive of its opposite.

On the third definition — retaining a letter as the earth, a mountain, an eye, a cloud, a person, or a mother each "retain" something in their own distinct way — Huiyuan reads this less as decoration than as itself a worked demonstration of unimpeded knowledge of meaning in action: the bodhisattva does not merely memorize that six things are called "retaining," he can supply, for each one, the specific mechanism that earns it the name (the earth's universal support, a mountain's structural stability, an eye's capture of light, a cloud's containment of moisture, a person's capacity to embody Dharma or its absence, a mother's care for her child) — which is precisely what distinguishes unimpeded knowledge of expression (knowing that a word applies) from unimpeded knowledge of meaning (knowing why it applies). The passage's closing claim that ordinary words like "man," "woman," or "house" carry "no real meaning" is not a claim that language is useless, on his reading, but the same point Part 13's two-truths discussion already established: such words are worldly truth, real at their own level, and it is precisely the bodhisattva's own unimpeded knowledge of the deeper meaning behind them — not the words themselves — that belongs to the buddha's domain rather than the ordinary person's.

Why the Two Vehicles Fall Short

Chinese Source

「善男子！聲聞緣覺若有得是四無閡者，無有是處。善男子！九部經中我說聲聞緣覺之人有四無礙，聲聞緣覺真實無有。何以故？菩薩摩訶薩為度眾生故，修如是四無礙智。緣覺之人修寂滅法，志樂獨處。若化眾生，但現神通，終日默然無所宣說，云何當有四無礙智？何故默然而無所說？緣覺不能說法度人。使得煖法、頂法、忍法、世第一法、須陀洹、斯陀含、阿那含、阿羅漢、辟支佛、菩薩摩訶薩，不能令人發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。何以故？善男子！緣覺出世，世間無有九部經典，是故緣覺無辭無礙、樂說無礙。善男子！緣覺之人雖知諸法，無法無礙。何以故？法無礙者，名為知字，緣覺之人雖知文字，無字無礙。何以故？不知常住二字故，是故緣覺不得法無礙。雖知於義，無義無礙。真知義者，知諸眾生悉有佛性，佛性義者，名為阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。以是義故，緣覺之人不得義無礙。是故緣覺，一切無有四無礙智。云何聲聞無四無礙？聲聞之人無有三種善巧方便。何等為三？一者必須軟語然後受法，二者必須麁語然後受化，三者不軟不麁然後受化。聲聞之人無此三故，無四無礙。復次聲聞緣覺不能畢竟知辭、知義，無自在智，知於境界。無有十力、四無所畏，不能畢竟度於十二因緣大河，不能善知眾生諸根利鈍差別，未能永斷二諦疑心，不知眾生種種諸心所緣境界，不能善說第一義空，是故二乘無四無礙。」

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！若諸聲聞緣覺之人，一切無有四無礙者，云何世尊說舍利弗智慧第一？大目犍連神通第一？摩訶拘絺羅四無礙第一？如其無者，如來何故作如是說？」

爾時世尊讚迦葉言：「善哉，善哉！善男子！譬如恒河有無量水，辛頭大河水亦無量，博叉大河水亦無量，悉陀大河水亦無量，阿耨達池水亦無量，大海之中水亦無量。如是諸水雖同無量，然其多少其實不等；聲聞緣覺及諸菩薩四無礙智，亦復如是，善男子！若說等者，無有是處。善男子！我為凡夫說，摩訶拘絺羅四無礙智為最第一，汝所問者其義如是。善男子！聲聞之人，或有得一，或有得二；若具足四，無有是處。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, that a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha could ever attain these four kinds of unimpeded knowledge is simply not possible. Good son, within the nine divisions of the scriptures, I have said that the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha possess the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge — yet, in truth, they possess none at all. Why is this? The bodhisattva-mahāsattva cultivates these four kinds of unimpeded knowledge for the very sake of saving beings. The pratyekabuddha, cultivating the dharma of still extinction, delights in dwelling alone, and, should he transform beings at all, does so only by manifesting supernatural power, remaining silent the whole day through, without expounding anything at all — how, then, could he possess the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge? Why does he remain silent, expounding nothing? Because the pratyekabuddha is unable to teach the Dharma to save others. Even should he cause a being to attain the dharmas of heat, of the summit, of forbearance, and of the highest worldly dharma, or the fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, arhatship, or pratyekabuddhahood, or even the state of a bodhisattva-mahāsattva, he is unable to cause anyone to give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening. Why is this? Good son, when a pratyekabuddha appears in the world, the world is entirely without the nine divisions of the scriptures — it is for this very reason that the pratyekabuddha has no unimpeded knowledge of expression, and no unimpeded knowledge of eloquence. Good son, the pratyekabuddha, though he knows every dharma, has no unimpeded knowledge of the Dharma at all. Why is this? Unimpeded knowledge of the Dharma is named knowing every letter, and the pratyekabuddha, though he knows the written letters, has no unimpeded knowledge of these letters. Why is this? Because he does not know the two characters for 'eternally abiding' — it is for this very reason that the pratyekabuddha does not attain unimpeded knowledge of the Dharma. Though he knows of meaning, he has no unimpeded knowledge of meaning. Truly knowing meaning means knowing that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature, and the meaning of buddha-nature is itself named unsurpassed, complete awakening. It is for this very reason that the pratyekabuddha does not attain unimpeded knowledge of meaning. It is for this very reason that the pratyekabuddha possesses none of the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge at all. How is it that the śrāvaka, too, lacks the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge? The śrāvaka lacks three kinds of skillful means. What are these three? First, one who must be approached with gentle words before he will accept the Dharma; second, one who must be approached with harsh words before he will accept being transformed; and third, one who must be approached with neither gentle nor harsh words before he will accept being transformed. The śrāvaka, lacking these three, therefore lacks the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge. Furthermore, the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha are unable, in the end, to fully know expression and meaning; they lack the mastery of wisdom that knows every domain; they lack the ten powers and the four kinds of fearlessness; they are unable, in the end, to cross the great river of the twelve links of dependent origination; they are unable to discern well the sharp and dull faculties of beings in all their difference; they have not yet forever severed all doubt regarding the two truths; they do not know the various objects upon which the minds of beings depend; and they are unable to expound well the emptiness of the ultimate meaning — it is for this very reason that the two lesser vehicles lack the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if every śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha, without exception, lacks the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge entirely, why, then, has the World-Honored One taught that Śāriputra is foremost in wisdom, that Mahāmaudgalyāyana is foremost in supernatural power, and that Mahākauṣṭhila is foremost in the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge? If they truly lack it, why did the Tathāgata teach in this way?"

At that time, the World-Honored One praised Kāśyapa, saying: "Excellent, excellent, good son! It is like this: the Gaṅgā River holds an immeasurable amount of water; the great Sindhu River, too, holds an immeasurable amount of water; the great Vakṣu River, too, holds an immeasurable amount of water; the great Sītā River, too, holds an immeasurable amount of water; Lake Anavatapta, too, holds an immeasurable amount of water; and the great ocean, too, holds an immeasurable amount of water. Though these waters are all alike called "immeasurable," their actual quantities are, in fact, not equal to one another; the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge possessed by the śrāvaka, the pratyekabuddha, and every bodhisattva are just the same, good son — to say that they are all equal is simply not possible. Good son, I taught, for the sake of ordinary beings, that Mahākauṣṭhila's own four kinds of unimpeded knowledge were the very foremost — the meaning of what you have asked is just this. Good son, a śrāvaka may attain one of these four, or he may attain two of them; but that he should be fully endowed with all four is simply not possible."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the sūtra's case against the pratyekabuddha and the case against the śrāvaka as built on different grounds, not the same complaint twice. The pratyekabuddha's own chosen mode of teaching — silent, relying purely on displays of supernatural power rather than speech — structurally forecloses unimpeded knowledge of expression and eloquence before any question of his inner attainment even arises, since these two kinds of unimpeded knowledge are, by definition, exercised through speech; and, on Huiyuan's reading, his ignorance of "the two characters for eternally abiding" points to a specific doctrinal blind spot rather than illiteracy — the pratyekabuddha's own path realizes impermanence and no-self but does not arrive at the sūtra's central claim that buddha-nature, and therefore something, is genuinely eternal, which is exactly why unimpeded knowledge of meaning is denied him on grounds distinct from the silence that costs him the other two. The śrāvaka's case, by contrast, turns on a lack of adaptive skillful means: needing to be approached in one of three fixed registers (gentle words, harsh words, or neither) rather than reading and responding to a listener's need directly, unlike a bodhisattva or buddha who can supply whichever approach a given being actually requires.

On the river simile answering Kāśyapa's challenge about Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, and Mahākauṣṭhila's own famous specialties, Huiyuan reads it as resolving an apparent contradiction rather than softening the sūtra's own claim: the Buddha's earlier teaching that these three disciples were each "foremost" in their respective quality was true relative to other śrāvakas, exactly as the Gaṅgā, the Sindhu, and the ocean are all genuinely called "immeasurable" without being equal in volume to one another — a claim of superlative rank among peers does not entail full possession of the quality being ranked. Mahākauṣṭhila's ranking as "foremost in the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge," on this reading, means he possessed more of this quality than any other śrāvaka, which the sūtra's closing line makes explicit is still, at most, one or two of the four in full — never all four together, a completeness reserved for a bodhisattva alone.

"Furthermore, good son, non-attainment is itself named the Great Vehicle, and the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding in no dharma at all, thereby attains the Great Vehicle — it is for this very reason that the bodhisattva is named 'one who attains nothing at all.' Attainment, on the other hand, is named impermanence, the absence of bliss, the absence of self, and the absence of purity, and the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, having severed this impermanence, absence of bliss, absence of self, and absence of purity — it is for this very reason that the bodhisattva is named 'one who attains nothing at all.'

"Furthermore, good son, non-attainment is itself named the emptiness of the ultimate meaning, and the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, contemplating this emptiness of the ultimate meaning, sees nothing at all — it is for this very reason that the bodhisattva is named 'one who attains nothing at all.' Attainment, on the other hand, is named the five wrong views, and the bodhisattva, having forever severed these five wrong views, thereby attains the emptiness of the ultimate meaning — it is for this very reason that the bodhisattva is named 'one who attains nothing at all.'

"Furthermore, good son, non-attainment is itself named unsurpassed, complete awakening, and the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, at the very moment of attaining unsurpassed, complete awakening, sees nothing at all — it is for this very reason that the bodhisattva is named 'one who attains nothing at all.' Attainment, on the other hand, is named the awakening of the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, and the bodhisattva, having forever severed the awakening of the two lesser vehicles — it is for this very reason that the bodhisattva is named 'one who attains nothing at all.'

"Good son, what you have now asked is itself non-attainment, and what I have now taught is itself non-attainment as well. Should there be anything actually attained here, this would be the retinue of Māra, and not my own disciple."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, when you teach me this very meaning of a bodhisattva's non-attainment, immeasurable beings sever their own mind that grasps at marks — it was for this very reason that I dared to inquire about the meaning of non-attainment, so that immeasurable beings such as these might leave behind the retinue of Māra and become the Buddha's own disciples."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads these nine paired definitions as one argument stated nine times rather than nine separate teachings, and divides them by function: the first names non-attainment directly against the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge themselves, and the remaining eight extend the same logic outward to eight neighboring topics this fascicle and its predecessors have already established (wisdom against ignorance, nirvāṇa against the twenty-five states of existence, the Great Vehicle against the two lesser vehicles, the Vaipulya scriptures against the other eleven divisions, empty space against the wheel of birth-and-death, permanence-bliss-self-purity against their four opposites, the emptiness of the ultimate meaning against the five wrong views, and unsurpassed awakening against the awakening of the two lesser vehicles). Each of the nine follows an identical two-part structure: first stating plainly what non-attainment *is* named, then explaining, by contrast, why what is genuinely *attained* in the ordinary sense — an inversion, a wrong view, saṃsāra itself — is exactly what a bodhisattva has severed. On Huiyuan's reading, the logic never actually claims a bodhisattva knows nothing; it claims that what a bodhisattva knows has no fixed mark for the mind to seize and hold as an object, and it is the seizing itself, not the knowing, that "attainment" names and "non-attainment" denies.

The tenth move, closing the teaching, works differently from the preceding nine: rather than offering a further paired definition, the Buddha turns the argument back onto the exchange itself — "what you have asked is itself non-attainment, and what I have taught is itself non-attainment as well" — using speech to dissolve any residual sense that "non-attainment" is one more thing that has just been successfully attained through this very conversation. Huiyuan reads Kāśyapa's closing remark, that immeasurable beings sever their own mark-grasping mind upon hearing this teaching, as confirming that the entire exchange was staged for the sake of the wider assembly rather than for Kāśyapa's own benefit — the same pattern already seen in Part 14's exchange with Mañjuśrī over "turning the wheel of the Dharma," where a bodhisattva's own question exists to draw out, for others' sake, an answer the questioner himself already understood.

Should any śramaṇa, any brahmin, any god, any demon, any Brahmā, or any person say that the Tathāgata, in the past, the future, or the present, has a body dependent on various kinds of food, this is simply not possible.

To say 'what was not' means this: I did not, in ages past, have the true meaning of the middle way, and it was on account of not having the true meaning of the middle way that I held a grasping mind toward every dharma. Should any śramaṇa, any brahmin, any god, any demon, any Brahmā, or any person say that the Tathāgata, in the past, the future, or the present, teaches every dharma as possessing a fixed mark, this is simply not possible.

"Furthermore, good son, to say 'what was' means this: at the very time I first attained unsurpassed, complete awakening, I had disciples among the śrāvakas of dull faculty, and it was on account of having these dull-faculty śrāvaka disciples that I was unable to expound the real truth of the one vehicle. To say 'what was not' means this: I did not, at that time, yet have disciples of sharp faculty, the elephant-kings among men, the bodhisattva Kāśyapa and others, and it was on account of not having sharp-faculty disciples like Kāśyapa that I opened up and revealed the three vehicles according to what was fitting. Should any śramaṇa, any brahmin, any god, any demon, any Brahmā, or any person say that the Tathāgata, in the past, the future, or the present, expounds only and ultimately the teaching of the three vehicles, this is simply not possible.

"Furthermore, good son, to say 'what was' means this: I originally taught that, three months hence, I would enter parinirvāṇa between the twin sāla trees, and it was for this reason that, in the present, I could not expound this great Vaipulya scripture, this Mahāparinirvāṇa. To say 'what was not' means this: I did not, in ages past, yet have a great bodhisattva such as Mañjuśrī, and it was on account of not having him that, in the present, it is said that the Tathāgata is impermanent. Should any śramaṇa, any brahmin, any god, any demon, any Brahmā, or any person say that the Tathāgata, in the past, the future, or the present, is impermanent, this is simply not possible.

"Good son, the Tathāgata, universally for the sake of every being, though he knows every dharma, at times says he does not know it; though he sees every dharma, at times says he does not see it; a dharma possessed of marks, he at times calls markless; a markless dharma, he at times calls possessed of marks; what is truly impermanent, he at times calls permanent; what is truly permanent, he at times calls impermanent — and it is just the same with self, bliss, and purity. A teaching of three vehicles, he at times calls a single vehicle; a teaching of a single vehicle, he at times, as is fitting, teaches as three. A brief teaching, he at times expands; an expanded teaching, he at times abbreviates. A gravest offense, he at times calls a lesser transgression; a lesser transgression, he at times calls a gravest offense. A transgression, he at times calls no transgression at all; what is no transgression, he at times calls a transgression. A light offense, he at times calls grave; a grave offense, he at times calls light. Why is this? Because the Tathāgata sees clearly into the faculties of beings. Good son, though the Tathāgata teaches in this way, there is, in the end, no falsehood in it at all. Why is this? False speech is itself a transgression, and the Tathāgata has severed every transgression entirely — how, then, could there be any false speech in him at all? Good son, though the Tathāgata never speaks falsely, should he know that a being is able to gain benefit from the Dharma through what appears, on its surface, to be a contrary statement, he will, according to whatever skillful means is fitting, teach in this way for his sake. Good son, every instance of worldly truth is, from the Tathāgata's own standpoint, itself the ultimate truth. Why is this? Every buddha, every world-honored one, teaches worldly truth for the sake of the ultimate truth, and does so in order to cause beings to attain this very ultimate truth — and, should beings be unable to attain this ultimate truth in this way, no buddha would ever, in the end, teach worldly truth at all. Good son, the Tathāgata, at times, expounds worldly truth, and beings suppose that the Buddha is teaching the ultimate truth; and, at times, he expounds the ultimate truth, and beings suppose that the Buddha is teaching worldly truth. This is, then, the profoundest domain of every buddha, not something known by any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha. Good son, it is for this very reason that you should not, as you did before, raise the objection that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva attains nothing at all. A bodhisattva constantly attains the ultimate truth — how, then, can you object that he attains nothing?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the eight paired explanations as answering the same underlying question from eight increasingly specific angles: what did the Tathāgata's own verse to Cunda actually mean, and why does it license "having," at the same instant, the very attainments the earlier "non-attainment" teaching seemed to deny? Each pair follows an identical logical form — the presence of some obstruction (affliction, an ordinary birth-body, the four inversions, an ordinary ascetic's mistaken confidence, a food-dependent body, grasping at fixed marks, an audience limited to dull-faculty disciples, an announced three-month deadline) is called "what was," and it is this very presence that explains the corresponding absence of a genuine attainment (nirvāṇa, the vajra Dharma-body, awakening, the destruction of the four demons, a boundless body, the concentration on ultimate emptiness, the one vehicle's own real teaching, the Mahāparinirvāṇa's own full exposition); the reverse absence (never having had the perfection of wisdom, the thirty-two marks, a vision of buddha-nature, the six perfections, the thirty-seven factors, the real meaning of the middle way, sharp-faculty disciples like Kāśyapa, a bodhisattva such as Mañjuśrī) is called "what was not," and it is this very absence that explains the corresponding presence of the obstruction just named. Read together, the eight pairs trace something closer to a spiritual biography in miniature than a static doctrinal formula — each one names a specific limitation now overcome and the specific attainment that overcoming it made possible, with the sūtra's own closing formula in each case ("that this should hold true across past, present, and future is simply not possible") ruling out treating any of these eight limitations as a permanent feature of what the Tathāgata is, rather than a stage since surpassed.

On the closing catalogue of deliberate reversals (teaching the impermanent as permanent, a single vehicle as three, a light offense as grave), Huiyuan reads this as the sūtra's own general theory of upāya stated in its most exposed form: none of these statements is false, because falsehood is itself defined as speech that fails to benefit the hearer, and the Tathāgata's own criterion for choosing which side of an apparent contradiction to state is always "what this particular being's own faculties can actually receive and act on," never a fixed, context-independent fact about the topic itself. His sharpest point concerns the closing claim that worldly truth, spoken by a buddha, "is itself the ultimate truth" — which he reads not as collapsing the distinction between the two truths outright, but as locating that distinction in the *listener's own uncertainty* about which one is being addressed at a given moment, rather than in any instability in the Tathāgata's own speech: a buddha may state worldly truth while a listener mistakes it for the ultimate, or state the ultimate while a listener mistakes it for the worldly, and it is exactly this asymmetry between the speaker's own settled clarity and the listener's own uncertainty that the sūtra calls "the profoundest domain of every buddha, not known by any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha" — closing the argument by turning it back onto Kāśyapa's own earlier challenge: a bodhisattva who "constantly attains the ultimate truth" was never, in fact, the being with nothing at all, only the being with nothing fixed enough to be grasped and held as an object.

Should any bodhisattva say that the path, bodhi, and nirvāṇa can be attained, this would itself make them impermanent. Why is this? Whatever is truly permanent cannot be attained at all — just as with empty space, who could ever attain it? World-Honored One, it is like a worldly thing, originally absent and now present, which is called impermanent; the path is just the same: should the path be something that can be attained, it would thereby be called impermanent. Whatever is truly permanent has no attainment and no arising at all — just as with buddha-nature, which has no attainment and no arising. World-Honored One, the path is neither form nor non-form; it is neither long nor short, neither high nor low, neither arisen nor ceased, neither red, white, blue, nor yellow; it is neither existent nor non-existent — how, then, can the Tathāgata say that it can be attained? Bodhi and nirvāṇa are just the same."

The Buddha said: "It is so, it is so. Good son, the path is of two kinds: one permanent, and one impermanent. The mark of bodhi, too, is of two kinds: one permanent, and one impermanent — and it is just the same for nirvāṇa. The path of those who follow other tenets is called impermanent; the path of those within the true teaching is called permanent. The bodhi possessed by the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha is called impermanent; the bodhi possessed by the bodhisattva and every buddha is called permanent. Liberation from without is called impermanent; liberation from within is called permanent. Good son, the path, bodhi, and nirvāṇa are all, in fact, named permanent. Every being is forever covered over by immeasurable affliction, and, lacking the eye of wisdom, is unable to see them. And yet beings, wishing to see them, cultivate moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom, and, through this very cultivation, come to see the path, bodhi, and nirvāṇa — this is what is meant by a bodhisattva's attaining the path, bodhi, and nirvāṇa. The very nature and mark of the path, in truth, neither arises nor ceases, and it is for this very reason that it cannot be grasped or held. Good son, the path, though it has no visible form to be seen, nor any measure to be known, truly has its own function nonetheless. Good son, it is like the mind of a being: though it is neither form, nor long, nor short, nor coarse, nor subtle, neither bound nor liberated, nor a dharma that can be seen, it, too, truly exists — and it is for this very reason that I once told the elder Sudatta: 'Elder, the mind is the ruler of the city; should you, elder, fail to guard your own mind, you will fail to guard your body and speech as well. It is because beings fail to guard their own body and speech well that they arrive at the three evil destinies; and it is by guarding their body and speech that beings attain rebirth among gods and men, and nirvāṇa itself. To attain this is called truly real; to fail to attain it is not called truly real.' Good son, the path, bodhi, and nirvāṇa are just the same: they, too, truly exist, and are, too, truly permanent. Were they truly non-existent, how could they ever sever every affliction? It is precisely because they truly exist that every bodhisattva sees and knows them fully and completely.

It is as when one sees smoke from afar and calls this seeing fire, though one does not, in truth, see the fire itself — yet, though one does not see the fire, this is not false either. One sees a crane in the sky and thereupon says one sees water — though one does not see the water, this is not false either. One sees a flower's leaves and thereupon says one sees its root — though one does not see the root, this is not false either. One sees a woman who is pregnant and thereupon says one sees an act of desire — though one does not see the act of desire itself, this is not false either. One sees a tree putting forth leaves and thereupon says one sees water — though one does not see the water, this is not false either. One sees clouds and thereupon says one sees rain — though one does not see the rain, this is not false either. One sees an act of the body or of speech and thereupon says one sees the mind — though one does not see the mind itself, this is not false either. This is called seeing by inference from a mark. What is seeing fully and completely? It is like the eye seeing form. Good son, it is as when a person whose own eye-faculty is pure and undamaged directly beholds an āmalaka fruit resting in the palm of his own hand. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva's own full and complete seeing of the path, of bodhi, and of nirvāṇa is just the same. Though he sees in this way, there is, from the very outset, no mark of seeing involved at all.

"Good son, it is through this very cause and condition that I once, in ages past, told Śāriputra: 'Whatever every world — every śramaṇa, every brahmin, every god, every demon, every Brahmā, and every person — does not know, does not see, and does not perceive, only the Tathāgata knows, sees, and perceives entirely, and every bodhisattva does likewise. Śāriputra, whatever the world does know, see, and perceive, I and every bodhisattva likewise know, see, and perceive. Beings of the world, with regard to what they do not know, do not see, and do not perceive, do not even know of themselves that they fail to know, see, or perceive it; and, with regard to what beings of the world do know, see, and perceive, they say of themselves, "I know, I see, I perceive." Śāriputra, the Tathāgata, though he knows, sees, and perceives every single thing entirely, never once says of himself, "I know, I see, I perceive" — and every bodhisattva is just the same. Why is this? Should the Tathāgata make of knowing, seeing, and perceiving some fixed mark to hold onto, one should know that he would not, in that case, be a buddha, a world-honored one, at all, but would be called an ordinary being — and it is just the same for a bodhisattva.'"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Kāśyapa's challenge as pressing a genuine logical difficulty rather than a rhetorical setup: if permanence and attainability are mutually exclusive concepts — one attains only what was previously absent, and what is genuinely permanent was never absent to begin with — then calling the path, bodhi, and nirvāṇa both "permanent" and "attained" looks like a plain contradiction, buddha-nature's own established permanence being the obvious counter-example of something that, by this same logic, cannot be "attained" either. The Buddha's answer does not deny the logic but relocates where impermanence actually sits: the path itself divides into a permanent and an impermanent kind, with the impermanent kind belonging to every rival tradition and to the two lesser vehicles (their own liberation still subject to relapse and change), and the permanent kind belonging to the bodhisattva and buddha alone — so that "attaining the path" never means bringing the permanent path itself into existence, only removing the affliction that had been obstructing a pre-existing view of it, on the model of guarding the mind as a city's own ruler: the city gates were never built by the guarding, only kept safe by it. Huiyuan reads the mind-simile Sudatta received as doing real philosophical work here, not mere illustration: mind has no color, length, or fixed shape, and is yet plainly efficacious, which is exactly the kind of "existing without being a graspable object" the path, bodhi, and nirvāṇa are being said to share.

On the two kinds of seeing, Huiyuan reads the eight similes of "seeing by inference from a mark" (smoke for fire, a crane for water, leaves for a root, a horn for a cow, pregnancy for an act of desire, leaves for water, clouds for rain, and bodily or verbal conduct for mind itself) as together making a single point about the entire eight-fold and four-fold epistemology this fascicle and its predecessor have built up: inferring one thing from a visible sign of it is genuine knowledge, not falsehood, even though the thing itself was never directly seen — which is exactly the status of a bodhisattva's "knowing without seeing" from Part 16. "Seeing fully and completely," by contrast, is not a sharper version of the same inferential seeing but a different kind of contact altogether, on the model of an eye directly beholding an āmalaka fruit already resting in one's own open hand — nothing stands between the seeing and its object, which is why the passage insists this seeing "from the very outset has no mark of seeing involved at all": there is no inferential gap left over that a "mark" could be a sign of. The closing citation of what the Buddha once told Śāriputra reads, per Huiyuan, as the sūtra's own summary of everything argued in this fascicle so far: a buddha's or bodhisattva's superiority over the ordinary world is not that they know more facts, but that they know without ever converting that knowing into a fixed self-concept ("I know") — the very move that would, paradoxically, demote them back into an ordinary being the instant they made it.

佛言：「善男子！言菩薩者，亦是世間，亦非世間。不知見覺者，名為世間，知見覺者，不名世間。汝言有何異者？我今當說。善男子！若男、若女，若有初聞是涅槃經，即生敬信，發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，是則名為世間。菩薩一切世間不知、見、覺，如是菩薩，亦同世間不知見覺。菩薩聞是涅槃經已，知有世間不知見覺，應是菩薩所知見覺，知是事已，即自思惟：『我當云何方便修習，得知見覺？』覆自念言：『惟當深心修持淨戒。』善男子！菩薩爾時以是因緣，於未來世，在在生處，戒常清淨。善男子！菩薩摩訶薩以戒淨故，在在生處，常無憍慢、邪見疑網，終不說言如來畢竟入於涅槃，是名菩薩修持淨戒。戒既清淨，次修禪定，以修定故，在在生處正念不忘，所謂一切眾生悉有佛性、十二部經、諸佛世尊常樂我淨。一切菩薩安住方等大涅槃經，悉見佛性，如是等事，憶而不忘，因修定故得十一空，是名菩薩修清淨定。戒定已備，次修淨慧，以修慧故，初不計著身中有我、我中有身、是身是我、非身非我，是名菩薩修習淨慧。以修慧故，所受持戒牢固不動。善男子！譬如須彌不為四風之所傾動，菩薩摩訶薩亦復如是，不為四倒之所傾動。善男子！菩薩爾時自知見覺，所受持戒無有傾動，是名菩薩所知見覺，非世間也。善男子！菩薩見所持戒牢固不動，心無悔恨，無悔恨故心得歡喜，得歡喜故心得悅樂，得悅樂故心則安隱，心安隱故得無動定，得無動定故得實知見，實知見故厭離生死，厭離生死故便得解脫，得解脫故明見佛性，是名菩薩所知見覺，非世間也。善男子！是名世間不知見覺，而是菩薩所知見覺。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said: "As the Buddha, the World-Honored One, told Śāriputra — 'whatever the world knows, I, too, know; and whatever the world does not know, I, too, know entirely' — what is the meaning of this?"

"Good son, every world does not know, does not see, and does not perceive buddha-nature at all; and whoever does know, see, and perceive buddha-nature is not called 'the world,' but is called a bodhisattva. Beings of the world, likewise, do not know, do not see, and do not perceive the twelve divisions of the scriptures, the twelve links of dependent origination, the four inversions, the Four Noble Truths, the thirty-seven factors, unsurpassed complete awakening, or this Mahāparinirvāṇa; and whoever does know, see, and perceive these is not called 'the world,' but should be called a bodhisattva. Good son, this is what is meant by what the world does not know, see, or perceive. What, then, does the world know, see, and perceive? Namely, Brahmā, Maheśvara, the eight-armed god, the doctrines of primordial nature, time, atoms, and dharma and non-dharma as a creator-lord, and the two views of the world's own annihilation or eternal persistence, and the teaching that the first dhyāna up through the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception is itself nirvāṇa. Good son, this is what is meant by what the world does know, see, and perceive. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, with regard to matters of this kind too, both knows, sees, and perceives them; and, the bodhisattva having thus known, seen, and perceived them, should he say that he does not know, see, or perceive them, this would be a falsehood. A dharma of falsehood is itself a transgression, and, through this very transgression, one falls into hell. Good son, should any man or woman, any śramaṇa or brahmin, say that there is no path, no bodhi, and no nirvāṇa, one should know that people of this kind are named icchantikas, the retinue of Māra, and are said to slander the Dharma — and slandering the Dharma in this way is itself called slandering every buddha. A person of this kind is called neither 'the world' nor 'not the world.'"

At that time, Kāśyapa, having heard this teaching, thereupon praised the Buddha in verse:

"'Your great loving-kindness pities every being —
this alone has led me to take refuge in you.
Well do you draw out every poisoned arrow —
this alone earns you the name of the great king of physicians.
What a worldly physician cures may, though healed, arise once more;
but what the Tathāgata cures never, in the end, breaks out again.
The World-Honored One's own nectar-medicine is given to every being;

The Tathāgata now, for my own sake, expounds this Mahāparinirvāṇa;
and beings, hearing this secret treasury, at once attain what neither arises nor ceases.'

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa, having spoken this verse, said further to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught it, that the whole world does not know, see, or perceive, while a bodhisattva is entirely able to know, see, and perceive — if a bodhisattva is himself part of the world, one could not then say that the world does not know, see, or perceive while this same bodhisattva is able to know, see, and perceive. And if he is not part of the world, what mark, then, distinguishes him?"

The Buddha said: "Good son, what is called a bodhisattva is both part of the world and not part of the world. Whoever does not know, see, or perceive is called 'the world'; whoever does know, see, and perceive is not called 'the world.' You ask what distinguishes them — I shall now explain. Good son, should any man or woman, upon first hearing this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, at once give rise to reverent faith and to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening, this is, at that point, called 'the world.' A bodhisattva, with regard to whatever the whole world does not know, see, or perceive, is, at this stage, likewise the same as the world in not knowing, seeing, or perceiving it. The bodhisattva, having heard this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, knows that there is something the world does not know, see, or perceive, which ought to be known, seen, and perceived by a bodhisattva; and, knowing this, he thereupon reflects to himself: 'By what skillful means, then, should I cultivate practice, in order to attain this very knowing, seeing, and perceiving?' He then thinks further to himself: 'I should only cultivate and uphold pure precepts, with a profound and earnest mind.' Good son, the bodhisattva, at that time, through this very cause and condition, in every future place of his own rebirth, has precepts that are forever pure. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, through the very purity of his precepts, in every place of his rebirth, is forever free of arrogance and the net of wrong views and doubt, and never once says that the Tathāgata, in the ultimate sense, actually enters parinirvāṇa — this is what is meant by the bodhisattva's cultivating and upholding pure precepts. His precepts thus made pure, he next cultivates meditative concentration; and, through this cultivation of concentration, in every place of his rebirth, he holds right mindfulness without ever forgetting it — namely, that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature, and the twelve divisions of the scriptures, and that every buddha, every world-honored one, is permanence, bliss, self, and purity. Every bodhisattva, abiding firmly within this Vaipulya Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, sees buddha-nature entirely, and, holding matters of this kind in mind, never forgets them; and it is through this very cultivation of concentration that he attains the eleven kinds of emptiness — this is what is meant by the bodhisattva's cultivating pure concentration. His precepts and concentration thus complete, he next cultivates pure wisdom; and, through this cultivation of wisdom, he never once reckons that there is a self within the body, a body within the self, that the body is the self, or that the body is not the self — this is what is meant by the bodhisattva's cultivating and practicing pure wisdom. And, through this very cultivation of wisdom, the precepts he upholds become firm and unshakeable. Good son, it is like Mount Sumeru, which is never shaken by the four winds; the bodhisattva-mahāsattva is just the same, never shaken by the four inversions. Good son, the bodhisattva, at that time, knows and sees of himself that the precepts he upholds are utterly unshaken — this is what is meant by what a bodhisattva knows, sees, and perceives, and is not part of the world. Good son, the bodhisattva, seeing that the precepts he upholds are firm and unshaken, holds a mind free of any regret; and, being free of regret, his mind attains joy; and, attaining joy, his mind attains happiness; and, attaining happiness, his mind is thereby at peace; and, his mind being at peace, he attains an unshakeable concentration; and, attaining this unshakeable concentration, he attains true knowing and seeing; and, attaining true knowing and seeing, he becomes weary of and detached from birth-and-death; and, becoming weary of and detached from birth-and-death, he thereupon attains liberation; and, attaining liberation, he clearly sees buddha-nature — this is what is meant by what a bodhisattva knows, sees, and perceives, and is not part of the world. Good son, this is what is meant by what the world does not know, see, or perceive, and yet is what a bodhisattva does know, see, and perceive."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads "the world" here as a technical term for a specific epistemic position rather than a place or a population: whoever has not yet come to know, see, and perceive buddha-nature and its neighboring doctrines simply is "the world," by definition, regardless of what else that person knows — which is why a philosophically sophisticated tradition (Brahmā, the eight-armed god, primordial nature, atoms, the doctrine of a creator, eternalism and annihilationism, dhyāna states mistaken for nirvāṇa) still counts as "worldly knowledge" no matter how elaborate it is, since none of it touches the one thing that would move a knower out of the category "the world" at all. He reads the list of what the world does know as making a specific point often missed: the sūtra does not deny these philosophical systems observe or reason correctly within their own terms, only that correct reasoning about the wrong object — mistaking a meditative absorption for liberation itself, for instance — still leaves the reasoner classified as "the world," since the classification tracks the object known, not the rigor of the knowing.

On the bodhisattva being "both world and not-world," Huiyuan reads the sūtra's own resolution as tracking a single moment of genuine transition rather than two permanently coexisting halves of one person: anyone who, on first hearing this sūtra, gives rise to faith is, at that very moment, still "the world" (since faith is not yet the knowing itself), and it is only through the specific three-stage cultivation that follows — pure precepts, then concentration, then wisdom — that this same person passes out of "the world" and into what the passage calls "the bodhisattva's own knowing." He traces the ten-step sequence this cultivation follows (four stages of precept-purity, two of concentration, four of wisdom, the tenth being liberation and the clear seeing of buddha-nature that crowns them) as a single causal chain in which each link is the direct condition for the next: pure precepts free the mind of the arrogance and doubt that would otherwise misread the sūtra's own claim that the Tathāgata "does not really enter parinirvāṇa"; concentration then holds this correct view in unwavering memory long enough for the eleven kinds of emptiness from Part 16 to be realized through it, rather than merely recited; and wisdom then dismantles the twenty possible permutations of self-and-body confusion (a full accounting of which Huiyuan lays out at sixty-five variants once each of the five aggregates is checked against the other four), stabilizing the very precepts that made the whole sequence possible in the first place — Mount Sumeru's steadiness against the four winds standing for exactly this closed loop, where the last stage reinforces the first rather than simply following it.

Kāśyapa said further: "How does a bodhisattva cultivate and uphold pure precepts, so that his mind holds no regret at all, and he comes, in the end, to see buddha-nature with full clarity?"

The Buddha said: "Good son, worldly precepts are not called pure. Why is this? Worldly precepts are held for the sake of existence itself, their own nature is unstable, and they are not ultimate — they are unable to benefit every being broadly, and it is for this very reason that they are called impure. Because of this impurity, one holds a mind of regret; because of this regret, the mind has no joy; without joy, there is no happiness; without happiness, there is no peace; without peace, there is no unshakeable concentration; without unshakeable concentration, there is no true knowing and seeing; without true knowing and seeing, there is no weariness or detachment; without weariness or detachment, there is no liberation; without liberation, one does not see buddha-nature; and, not seeing buddha-nature, one is, in the end, unable to attain this Mahāparinirvāṇa at all — this is what is meant by worldly precepts' being impure. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva's pure precepts are held without grasping even at the mark of a precept, are not held for the sake of existence, are truly ultimate, and are held for the sake of beings — this is what is meant by a bodhisattva's precepts being pure.

"Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, within these pure precepts, though he does not deliberately wish to give rise to a mind free of regret, this very mind free of regret arises of its own accord. Good son, it is like a person holding a bright mirror: without expecting to see his own face, his own reflected image appears of itself. It is, likewise, like a farmer who plants seed in fine soil: without expecting a shoot to sprout, the shoot sprouts of itself. It is, likewise, like the lighting of a lamp: without expecting the darkness to be dispelled, the darkness is dispelled of itself. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who firmly upholds pure precepts is just the same: a mind free of regret arises of its own accord, and, through these very pure precepts, his mind attains joy. Good son, it is like a person of fine appearance, who, seeing his own face, gives rise to joy in his own heart — one who upholds pure precepts is just the same. Good son, a person who breaks the precepts, seeing that his own precepts are impure, gives rise to no joy at all — it is like a person disfigured in body, who, seeing his own face, gives rise to no joy at all; one who breaks the precepts is just the same. Good son, it is like two women herding cattle, one carrying a jar of curds and the other a jar of whey, who go together to the city, wishing to sell them. Along the road, they stumble, and both jars break — and one of them is filled with joy, while the other is filled with sorrow and distress. Upholding the precepts and breaking them are just the same: one who upholds pure precepts has a mind filled with joy, and, his mind filled with joy, he thereupon reflects: 'Every buddha, every tathāgata, has taught, within this Mahāparinirvāṇa, that whoever is able to uphold pure precepts will thereby attain nirvāṇa. I now cultivate and practice pure precepts of this very kind, and I, too, should attain it.' Through this very cause and condition, his mind attains happiness."

Kāśyapa said further: "What difference is there between joy and happiness?"

"Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, at the moment he does not commit evil, is said to have joy; a mind purely upholding the precepts is called happiness. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva's own contemplation of birth-and-death is called joy; his seeing of this great nirvāṇa is called happiness. The lower is called joy; the higher is called happiness. Leaving behind what is shared in common with the world is called joy; attaining what is not shared in common with the world is called happiness. Through the very purity of his precepts, his body becomes light and supple, and his speech is free of coarse fault, and the bodhisattva, at that time, whether seeing, hearing, feeling lightly, tasting, touching, or knowing, is entirely free of every evil; and, being free of evil, his mind attains peace; and, attaining peace, he thereby attains still concentration; and, attaining still concentration, he attains true knowing and seeing; and, attaining true knowing and seeing, he becomes weary of and detached from birth-and-death; and, becoming weary of and detached from birth-and-death, he thereupon attains liberation; and, attaining liberation, he attains the seeing of buddha-nature; and, seeing buddha-nature, he attains this great nirvāṇa — this is what is meant by a bodhisattva's pure upholding of the precepts, and is not a worldly precept at all. Why is this? Good son, the pure precepts a bodhisattva-mahāsattva receives are assisted by five dharmas. What are these five? Faith, conscience, shame, good and wise friends, and reverence for the precepts — through these, he is free of the five hindrances. What he sees is pure, since he is free of the five wrong views. His mind holds no net of doubt, since he is free of five kinds of doubt: doubt in the Buddha, doubt in the Dharma, doubt in the Saṅgha, doubt in the precepts, and doubt in heedfulness. The bodhisattva, at that time, thereby attains the five faculties — namely, faith, mindfulness, diligence, concentration, and wisdom. Having attained these five faculties, he attains five kinds of liberation — namely, liberation from form, and so on, up through liberation from consciousness. This is what is meant by a bodhisattva's pure upholding of the precepts, and is not something worldly. Good son, this is what is meant by what the world does not know, does not see, and does not perceive, and yet is what a bodhisattva does know, see, and perceive.

If a person, upholding this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, breaks the restraining precepts, one should know that this sūtra has no power at all; and, having no power, though one recites it, it brings no benefit at all' — through this very disparagement of the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, immeasurable, boundless beings are further caused to fall into hell. One who upholds this sūtra while breaking the precepts is himself a harmful influence upon beings, and is not my own disciple, but is, rather, the retinue of Māra. I do not permit a person of this kind to uphold this scripture at all — it would be better for him not to receive it, not to uphold it, and not to cultivate it, than to receive, uphold, and cultivate it while breaking the precepts. Good son, should any of my disciples uphold, recite, copy, or expound this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, he should keep his own body and mind upright, taking great care never to engage in frivolous conduct or reckless, impulsive action — the body's frivolity is called reckless conduct, and the mind's frivolity is called impulsive craving. Should any of my disciples crave further existence and perform unwholesome acts, he should not uphold this scripture of the Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle at all. One who upholds this sūtra while craving further existence and performing unwholesome acts is himself a harmful influence upon beings, and is not my own disciple, but is, rather, the retinue of Māra.

Good son, should any of my disciples, upholding this sūtra, teach it at an improper time, or on up through teaching it merely to fan the flames of worldly fame, another person will reproach him, saying: 'If the Buddha's own secret treasury, this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, truly had power, how could it let you teach it at an improper time, or on up through fanning the flames of worldly fame? If one who upholds this sūtra teaches it in this way, one should know that this sūtra has no power at all; and, having no power, though one upholds it, it brings no benefit at all' — through this very disparagement of the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, immeasurable beings are caused to fall into hell. One who upholds this sūtra while teaching it at an improper time, or on up through fanning the flames of worldly fame, is himself a harmful influence upon beings, and is not my own disciple, but is, rather, the retinue of Māra. Good son, should one wish to uphold this sūtra, to teach this Mahāparinirvāṇa, to teach buddha-nature, to teach the Tathāgata's own secret treasury, to teach the Great Vehicle, to teach the Vaipulya scriptures, to teach the vehicle of the śrāvaka, to teach the vehicle of the pratyekabuddha, to teach liberation, or to see buddha-nature himself, he should first purify his own body; and, through the very purity of his body, no one will reproach him; and, no one reproaching him, immeasurable people will thereby give rise to pure faith in this Mahāparinirvāṇa; and, faith having arisen in them, they will revere this sūtra, and, upon hearing even a single verse, a single phrase, a single character of it, or hearing the one who teaches it, will thereby give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening. One should know that a person of this kind is truly a good and wise influence upon beings, and not a harmful one; he is my own disciple, and not the retinue of Māra — this is what is meant by a bodhisattva's not being part of the world. Good son, this is what is meant by what the world does not know, does not see, and does not perceive, and yet is what a bodhisattva does know, see, and perceive."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the two-women-with-milk simile as making a precise point often flattened in retelling: it is not that the broken jar of curds represents a total loss and the broken jar of whey a total gain, but that curds have not yet "produced" anything further (no butter, no ghee has yet been drawn from them), while whey (漿, already a step further along the same dairy sequence this collection has tracked since Part 14's six-stage ghee simile) represents value already realized — so the woman who loses her whey grieves a *smaller* loss than the one who feared losing something not yet actualized, exactly as one who breaks the precepts loses something never yet brought to fruition, whom the sūtra says feels no joy at the moment of loss precisely because there was nothing yet gained to lose, while one who keeps the precepts loses comparatively little because the value was already secured. On the joy/happiness distinction Kāśyapa presses for, Huiyuan lays out four separate criteria the Buddha's answer supplies rather than one: by cause (not committing evil versus a mind purely holding the precepts), by object (contemplating saṃsāra versus beholding nirvāṇa itself), by rank (the lower attainment versus the higher), and by scope (what is shared with ordinary worldly experience versus what belongs to the bodhisattva alone) — the same pair of words doing different work depending on which axis is in view, a pattern this collection has already met in the five-axis distinction between "seeing" and "knowing" earlier in this same fascicle.

On the closing three-part warning against misusing this very sūtra — reciting it while breaking precepts, reciting it with a frivolous or craving mind, and teaching it at the wrong time, place, or for the wrong motive — Huiyuan reads all three as answering a single anticipated objection: a critic who sees an unworthy reciter and concludes "this scripture has no real power, since it did nothing for him" has, on the sūtra's own account, drawn exactly the wrong conclusion and, in doing so, committed a worse harm than the original misconduct, since the disparagement itself now sends immeasurable further beings to hell who might otherwise have taken faith from the very same scripture. The remedy the passage proposes is not defending the reciter's misconduct but insisting that whoever intends to actually teach this sūtra "first purify his own body" — removing the occasion for this exact objection to arise at all, which Huiyuan reads as the practical, teaching-facing counterpart to the three-training sequence (precepts, concentration, wisdom) this same section has just laid out for the bodhisattva's own inward cultivation: the inward discipline and the outward conduct that makes the teaching credible to others are, on his reading, the same discipline viewed from two different directions.

Part 17 Complete

This closes Part 17 (fascicle 17 of 40), continuing 梵行品第八之三: the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge defined three separate ways, and an extended argument that even Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, and Mahākauṣṭhila — each ranked "foremost" among the Buddha's disciples — never possess all four in full, resolved through the simile of many rivers all genuinely called "immeasurable" without being equal in volume; a teaching that a bodhisattva's own knowledge is best named "non-attainment," stated nine parallel ways and closing on the paradox of using speech to dissolve the very idea that non-attainment is itself something attained; the sūtra's own explanation, in eight paired contrasts, of its most celebrated verse to Cunda — "what was is now not, what was not is now" — read here as a spiritual biography of specific limitations surpassed rather than a static formula; Kāśyapa's challenge that a permanent path cannot coherently be "attained," met by the path's own division into a permanent and an impermanent kind and by two distinct senses of "seeing"; and a bodhisattva's own path from being "the world" to knowing what the world cannot, traced through the three trainings of precepts, concentration, and wisdom, closing on a warning against reciting this very sūtra while breaking the precepts it teaches.
