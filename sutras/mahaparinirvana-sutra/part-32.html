The Elephant and the Blind Men

Lion's Roar presses his final and sharpest challenge to buddha-nature: if it draws a being toward awakening the way a magnet draws iron, why bother cultivating the path at all? The Buddha's answer runs through seven kinds of people crossing the great river of birth and death — some sinking, some barely surfacing, one reaching the far shore entirely — before turning to why a magnet, in fact, never simply pulls iron of its own accord, six blind men each mistaking a single part of an elephant for the whole animal, and a long list of what buddha-nature is truly called: great compassion, great joy, great faith, the single-child stage. The fascicle, and with it the entire six-part chapter opened in Part 27, closes with the sūtra's own eight wonders compared to the ocean's, an account of how a bodhisattva tests his own resolve through extreme austerity and voluntary rebirth as a starving beast, and Lion's Roar's own closing hymn of praise.

If Buddha-Nature Is Already There, Why Cultivate the Path?

Chinese Source

◎師子吼菩薩言：「世尊！若一切業不定得果，一切眾生悉有佛性，應當修習八聖道者，何因緣故，一切眾生悉不得是大般涅槃？世尊！若一切眾生有佛性者，即當定得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，何須修習八聖道耶？世尊！如此經中說有病人，若得醫藥及瞻病人，隨病飲食，若使不得，皆悉除差。一切眾生亦復如是，若遇聲聞及辟支佛、諸佛菩薩、諸善知識，若聞說法，修習聖道。若不遇、不聞、不修習道，悉當得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。何以故？以佛性故。世尊！譬如日月，無有能遮，令不得至頞多山邊、四大河水不至大海、一闡提等不至地獄。一切眾生亦復如是，無有能遮令不得至阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。何以故？以佛性故。世尊！以是義故，一切眾生不須修道，以佛性力故，應得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，不以修習聖道力故。世尊！若一闡提、犯四重禁、五逆罪等，不得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者，應須修習。以因佛性定當得故，非因修習，然後得也。世尊！譬如磁石，去鐵雖遠，以其力故，鐵則隨著。眾生佛性亦復如是，是故不須勤修習道。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar Bodhisattva said: "World-Honored One, if not every karmic action, without exception, is certain to yield its fruit, and every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature, then, should one still be obliged to cultivate the eightfold noble path, for what cause and condition does every being still fail to attain this very great nirvāṇa? World-Honored One, if every being truly possesses buddha-nature, he ought thereby to be certain to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening — why, then, would he need to cultivate the eightfold noble path at all? World-Honored One, as this very sūtra teaches, a sick person, should he obtain medicine, a physician's care, and food and drink suited to his own illness, is entirely cured — and, should he fail to obtain these, he remains uncured. Every being is exactly the same: should he encounter a śrāvaka, a pratyekabuddha, a buddha, a bodhisattva, or any good friend, and hear the Dharma taught, he cultivates the noble path — and, should he encounter none of these, hear nothing, and cultivate no path at all, he ought still to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening regardless. Why is this? Because of buddha-nature. World-Honored One, it is like the sun and moon, which nothing at all can prevent from reaching the peak of Mount Asta; like the four great rivers, which nothing can prevent from reaching the great ocean; and like the icchantika, whom nothing can prevent from reaching hell. Every being is exactly the same: nothing at all can prevent him from reaching unsurpassed, complete awakening. Why is this? Because of buddha-nature. World-Honored One, it is for this very reason that every being need not cultivate the path at all — it is through the very power of buddha-nature that he ought to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, and not through the power of cultivating the noble path. World-Honored One, should the icchantika, one who has violated the four gravest precepts, or one who has committed the five heinous crimes, truly fail to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, then cultivation would indeed be necessary — but, since it is through the cause of buddha-nature that one is certain to attain it, and not through the cause of cultivation, cultivation is not truly needed at all. World-Honored One, it is like a magnet: though the iron lies at some distance from it, through its own power, the iron is drawn to cling to it regardless. A being's own buddha-nature is exactly the same — it is for this very reason that there is no need to diligently cultivate the path at all."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lion's Roar's magnet argument is the sharpest possible formulation of a worry this entire chapter has circled since Part 27's opening question about how universal possession relates to actual practice: if buddha-nature's own causal power is sufficient on its own, cultivation becomes not merely optional but positively pointless — a redundant effort added on top of a process already guaranteed to complete itself. The sun/moon, river/ocean, and icchantika/hell similes are chosen precisely because each names an outcome that follows from a fixed, structural fact about the universe rather than from any effort on the part of the thing moving — the sun reaches its zenith and the river reaches the sea regardless of what either "does," and the icchantika's own fall into hell was itself established, back in Part 26, as a matter of severed roots rather than active pursuit. Lion's Roar's argument gains its force by asking why buddha-nature's own causal relationship to awakening should be any different in kind from these three cases.

Seven Kinds of People Crossing the River

Chinese Source

佛言：「善哉，善哉！善男子！如恒河邊有七種人，若為洗浴、恐畏寇賊、或為採花，則入河中。第一人者入水則沈。何以故？羸無勢力，不習浮故。第二人者雖沒還出，出已復沒。何以故？身力大故則能還出，不習浮故出已還沒。第三人者沒已即出，出更不沒。何以故？身重故沒，力大故出，先習浮故，出已即住。第四人者入已便沒，沒已還出，出已即住，遍觀四方。何以故？重故則沈，力大故還出，習浮則住，不知出處，故觀四方。第五人者入已即沈，沈已便出，出已即住，住已觀方，觀已即去。何以故？為怖畏故。第六人者入已即去，淺處則住。何以故？觀賊近遠故。第七人者既至彼岸，登上大山，無復恐怖，離諸怨賊，受大快樂。「善男子！生死大河亦復如是，有七種人畏煩惱賊故，發意欲渡生死大河，出家剃髮，身被法服。既出家已，親近惡友，隨順其教，聽受邪法，所謂眾生身者即是五蔭，五蔭者即名五大，眾生若死永斷五大，斷五大故，何須修習善惡諸業，是故當知無有善惡及善惡報。如是則名一闡提也。一闡提者名斷善根，斷善根故沒生死河，不能得出。何以故？惡業重故、無信力故，如恒河邊第一人也。善男子，一闡提輩有六因緣，沒三惡道不能得出。何等為六？一者惡心熾盛故、二者不見後世故、三者樂習煩惱故、四者遠離善根故、五者惡業障隔故、六者親近惡知識故。復有五事，沒三惡道。何等為五？一者於比丘邊作非法故、二者比丘尼邊作非法故、三者自在用僧鬘物故、四者母邊作非法故、五者於五部僧互生是非故。復有五事，沒三惡道。何等為五？一者常說無善惡果故、二者殺發菩提心眾生故、三者憙說法師過失故、四者法說非法非法說法故、五者為求法過而聽受故。復有三事，沒三惡道。何等為三？一謂如來無常永滅、二謂正法無常遷變、三謂僧寶可滅壞故，是故常沒三惡道中。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said: "Excellent, excellent! Good son, it is like the bank of the Ganges River, along which there are seven kinds of people who enter the river, whether to bathe, to escape fear of bandits, or to gather flowers. The first person, upon entering the water, sinks at once. Why is this? Because he is weak, without any strength, and has never practiced swimming. The second person, though he sinks, comes back up, and, having come up, sinks once more. Why is this? Because his own bodily strength is great, he is able to rise again — yet, because he has never practiced swimming, once he has risen, he sinks once more. The third person sinks and then comes up, and, once up, sinks no more. Why is this? He sinks because his body is heavy, rises because his strength is great, and, having previously practiced swimming, remains afloat once he has risen. The fourth person, upon entering, sinks, and, having sunk, rises again, and, once risen, remains afloat, looking about in every direction. Why is this? He sinks because he is heavy, rises again because his strength is great, remains afloat because he has practiced swimming, and, not knowing where to find the shore, looks about in every direction. The fifth person, upon entering, sinks at once, and, having sunk, rises, and, once risen, remains afloat, and, having looked about, sets off at once. Why is this? Because of his own fear. The sixth person, upon entering, sets off at once, and stops once he reaches a shallow place. Why is this? Because he watches to see whether the bandits are near or far. The seventh person has already reached the far shore, and climbs a great mountain there, free of all further fear, departed from every hostile bandit, and receiving great happiness. "Good son, the great river of birth-and-death is exactly the same. There are seven kinds of people who, fearing the bandit of affliction, resolve to cross this great river of birth-and-death, becoming renunciants, shaving their heads, and wearing the very robes of the Dharma. Having become renunciants, they draw close to evil friends, follow their teaching, and accept and receive wrong teachings — namely, that a being's own body is nothing but the five aggregates, and the five aggregates are nothing but the five great elements, and, should a being die, these five great elements are forever severed — and, since the five great elements are severed, what need would there be to cultivate wholesome or unwholesome karmic action at all? — and, on this account, one should know that there is neither wholesome nor unwholesome action, nor any fruit of wholesome or unwholesome action at all. A person of exactly this kind is named an icchantika. The icchantika is named one who has severed his own wholesome roots — and, because he has severed his wholesome roots, he sinks within the river of birth-and-death, unable ever to come up. Why is this? Because his evil karma is heavy, and he lacks the very power of faith — he is exactly like the first person at the bank of the Ganges. Good son, the icchantika sinks into the three evil destinies, unable to come up, through any of six causes and conditions. What are these six? First, an evil mind blazing fiercely; second, failing to see any future life at all; third, delighting in the habitual practice of affliction; fourth, keeping distant from every wholesome root; fifth, being obstructed by the very weight of his own evil karma; and sixth, drawing close to evil companions. There are, further, five things that cause one to sink into the three evil destinies. What are these five? First, committing an unlawful act with a monk; second, committing an unlawful act with a nun; third, freely making use of the Saṅgha's own communal property; fourth, committing an unlawful act with one's own mother; and fifth, stirring up dispute and discord among the five divisions of the Saṅgha. There are, further, five things that cause one to sink into the three evil destinies. What are these five? First, forever teaching that there is no wholesome or unwholesome fruit at all; second, killing a being who has given rise to the bodhi-mind; third, delighting in speaking of a Dharma-teacher's own faults; fourth, calling the Dharma non-Dharma, and calling non-Dharma the Dharma; and fifth, listening to and receiving teachings for the sake of finding fault with the Dharma. There are, further, three things that cause one to sink into the three evil destinies. What are these three? First, holding that the Tathāgata is impermanent and forever extinguished; second, holding that the true Dharma is impermanent and subject to change; and third, holding that the jewel of the Saṅgha can be destroyed — it is for reasons of exactly this kind that one forever sinks within the three evil destinies."

Chinese Source

「第二人者，發意欲渡生死大河，斷善根故，沒不能出。所言出者，親近善友則得信心。是信心者，信施、施果，信善、善果，信惡、惡果，信生、死、苦、無常、敗壞，是名為信。以得信心修習淨戒，受持讀誦書寫解說，常樂惠施，善修智慧。以鈍根故，復遇惡友，不能修習身、戒、心、慧，聽受邪法；或值惡時，處惡國土，斷諸善根。斷善根故，常沒生死，如恒河邊第二人也。「第三人者，發意欲渡生死大河，斷善根故於中沈沒，親近善友得名為出，信於如來是一切智、常恒無變、為眾生故說無上道，一切眾生悉有佛性，如來非滅，法、僧亦爾，無有滅壞。一闡提等不斷其法，終不能得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，要當遠離然後乃得。以信心故修習淨戒，修習戒已，受持讀誦書寫解說十二部經，為諸眾生廣宣流布，樂於惠施，修習智慧，以利根故，堅住信慧，心無退轉，如恒河邊第三人也。「第四人者，發意欲渡生死大河，斷善根故於中沈沒，親近善友故得信心，是名為出，得信心故，受持讀誦書寫解說十二部經，為眾生故廣宣流布，樂於惠施，修習智慧，以利根故堅住信慧，心無退轉，遍觀四方，觀四方者四沙門果，如恒河邊第四人也。「第五人者，發意欲渡生死大河，斷善根故於中沈沒，親近善友故得信心，是名為出，以信心故，受持讀誦書寫解說十二部經，為眾生故廣宣流布，樂於慧施修習智慧，以利根故，堅住信慧，心無退轉，無退轉已，即便前進，前進者謂辟支佛，雖能自渡，不及眾生，是名為去，如恒河邊第五人也。「第六人者，發意欲渡生死大河，斷善根故於中沈沒，親近善友獲得信心，得信心故名之為出，以信心故，受持讀誦書寫解說十二部經，為眾生故廣宣流布，樂於惠施，修習智慧，以利根故，堅住信慧，心無退轉，無退轉已即復前進遂到淺處，到淺處已即住不去，住不去者所謂菩薩，為欲度脫諸眾生故，住觀煩惱，如恒河邊第六人也。「第七人者，發意欲渡生死大河，斷善根故於中沈沒，親近善友獲得信心，得信心已是名為出，以信心故，受持讀誦書寫解說十二部經，為眾生故廣宣流布，樂於惠施，修習智慧，以利根故，堅住信慧，心無退轉，無退轉已即便前進，既前進已得到彼岸，登大高山，離諸恐怖，多受安樂。善男子！彼岸山者喻於如來，受安樂者喻佛常住，大高山者喻大涅槃。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"The second person resolves to cross this great river of birth-and-death, yet, having severed his own wholesome roots, sinks and is unable to rise. To 'rise,' here, means this: through drawing close to a good friend, one attains faith. This very faith means believing in giving and in the fruit of giving; believing in wholesome deeds and their wholesome fruit; believing in unwholesome deeds and their unwholesome fruit; and believing in birth, death, suffering, impermanence, and decay — this is named faith. Having attained faith, one cultivates pure precepts, upholding, reciting, copying out, and expounding scripture, forever delighting in generous giving, and skillfully cultivating wisdom. Yet, because his own faculties are dull, he encounters an evil friend once more, and, unable to cultivate body, precept, mind, and wisdom, he accepts and receives wrong teachings instead; or he happens upon an evil age, dwelling in an evil land, and severs every wholesome root. Because he has severed his own wholesome roots, he forever sinks within birth-and-death — he is exactly like the second person at the bank of the Ganges. "The third person resolves to cross this great river of birth-and-death, and, having severed his own wholesome roots, sinks within it — yet, drawing close to a good friend, he is thereby named as one who rises. He believes that the Tathāgata is himself omniscient, ever-abiding and without change, teaching the unsurpassed path for the sake of every being; that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature; that the Tathāgata does not truly become extinct, and that the Dharma and the Saṅgha are exactly the same, never destroyed. He believes that the icchantika, so long as he fails to sever this very icchantika-nature, can never attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, and that only by departing from it entirely can he finally attain it. Through faith, he cultivates pure precepts, and, having cultivated these precepts, upholds, recites, copies out, and expounds the twelve divisions of scripture, broadly proclaiming and spreading them for the sake of every being, delighting in generous giving, and cultivating wisdom — and, because his faculties are sharp, he remains firmly grounded in faith and wisdom, his mind never retrogressing — he is exactly like the third person at the bank of the Ganges. "The fourth person resolves to cross this great river of birth-and-death, and, having severed his own wholesome roots, sinks within it — yet, drawing close to a good friend, he attains faith, and this is named rising. Through this very faith, he upholds, recites, copies out, and expounds the twelve divisions of scripture, broadly proclaiming and spreading them for the sake of beings, delighting in generous giving, and cultivating wisdom — and, because his faculties are sharp, he remains firmly grounded in faith and wisdom, his mind never retrogressing, and he looks about in every direction — this looking about in every direction is a likeness for the four śramaṇa fruits — he is exactly like the fourth person at the bank of the Ganges. "The fifth person resolves to cross this great river of birth-and-death, and, having severed his own wholesome roots, sinks within it — yet, drawing close to a good friend, he attains faith, and this is named rising. Through this very faith, he upholds, recites, copies out, and expounds the twelve divisions of scripture, broadly proclaiming and spreading them for the sake of beings, delighting in generous giving, and cultivating wisdom — and, because his faculties are sharp, he remains firmly grounded in faith and wisdom, his mind never retrogressing, and, having never retrogressed, he then advances — this advancing is a likeness for the pratyekabuddha, who, though able to cross over himself, does not reach out to save others — this is named setting off — he is exactly like the fifth person at the bank of the Ganges. "The sixth person resolves to cross this great river of birth-and-death, and, having severed his own wholesome roots, sinks within it — yet, drawing close to a good friend, attains faith, and, through this attainment, is named as one who rises. Through this very faith, he upholds, recites, copies out, and expounds the twelve divisions of scripture, broadly proclaiming and spreading them for the sake of beings, delighting in generous giving, and cultivating wisdom — and, because his faculties are sharp, he remains firmly grounded in faith and wisdom, his mind never retrogressing, and, having never retrogressed, he advances further still, and reaches a shallow place — and, having reached this shallow place, he stops there and goes no further. This stopping without going further is a likeness for the bodhisattva, who, wishing to deliver every being, remains there, contemplating their own affliction — he is exactly like the sixth person at the bank of the Ganges. "The seventh person resolves to cross this great river of birth-and-death, and, having severed his own wholesome roots, sinks within it — yet, drawing close to a good friend, attains faith, and, having attained it, is named as one who rises. Through this very faith, he upholds, recites, copies out, and expounds the twelve divisions of scripture, broadly proclaiming and spreading them for the sake of beings, delighting in generous giving, and cultivating wisdom — and, because his faculties are sharp, he remains firmly grounded in faith and wisdom, his mind never retrogressing, and, having never retrogressed, he advances further still, and, having advanced, reaches the very far shore, climbing a great, high mountain, departed from every fear, and receiving great happiness in abundance. Good son, the mountain upon the far shore is a likeness for the Tathāgata; receiving this happiness is a likeness for the Buddha's own permanence; and this great, high mountain is a likeness for the great nirvāṇa."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seven river-crossers form a single continuum from total failure to complete success, and the sūtra's own mapping is precise about what actually distinguishes each stage: the first two people (the icchantika, and the ordinary being who gains faith but loses it again to bad company or a hostile environment) both fail for the same underlying reason — impermanent wholesome roots, easily severed by circumstance — while people three through seven all share the same foundation (faith, precept-keeping, scriptural study, generosity, wisdom) and differ only in how far that same foundation actually carries them: the stream-enterer through arhat's four fruits looking about for the shore, the pratyekabuddha advancing but only far enough to save himself, the bodhisattva stopping deliberately in the shallows to keep contemplating and helping others still struggling, and only the Tathāgata reaching the mountain itself. Read this way, the parable answers Lion's Roar's magnet challenge before the Buddha even states his argument explicitly: nothing in this entire spectrum happens automatically, and the single largest variable determining outcome — never named directly, but implied by exactly what separates person one from person three — is whether a good friend and genuine cultivation actually arrive, which is the entire function this fascicle's argument goes on to assign to the eightfold path.

The sixteen-item breakdown of exactly how an icchantika sinks (six general causes, five clerical-specific transgressions, five doctrinal corruptions, three denials of the three jewels' permanence) is this collection's most granular taxonomy yet of the icchantika condition, tracked since Part 19's earliest treatment — worth reading against Part 26's "sevenfold etymology" and "three kinds of patients" frameworks as a third, complementary lens: where those earlier passages explained why the icchantika's condition is not permanently fixed at the level of buddha-nature, this passage catalogues, in exhaustive practical detail, exactly what specific acts and attitudes constitute the condition in the first place.

Why the Path Is Still Needed

Chinese Source

「善男子！是恒河邊如是諸人，悉具手足而不能渡。一切眾生亦復如是，實有佛寶、法寶、僧寶，如來常說諸法要義，有八聖道大般涅槃，而諸眾生悉不能得。此非我咎，亦非聖道、眾生等過。當知悉是煩惱過惡，以是義故，一切眾生不得涅槃。善男子！譬如良醫知病說藥，病者不服，非醫咎也。善男子！如有施主，以其所有施一切人，有不受者非施主咎。善男子！譬如日出，幽冥皆明，盲瞽之人不見道路，非日過也。善男子！如恒河水能除渴乏，渴者不飲，非水咎也。善男子！譬如大地，普生果實，平等無二，農夫不種，非地過也。善男子！如來普為一切眾生廣開分別十二部經，眾生不受，非如來咎。善男子！若修道者，即得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, every one of these people at the bank of the Ganges was fully endowed with hands and feet, and yet was unable to cross. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the five similes answering "why doesn't the path's own fault, or the sūtra's own fault, explain universal failure" — the physician, the donor, the sun, the river, the earth — as together isolating a single variable: in every case, the provision is genuinely complete and freely available, and the only thing that varies is the recipient's own response, which is exactly the diagnosis the sūtra gives directly afterward (affliction alone, not any deficiency in the Buddha, the Dharma, the path, or beings' own basic capacity, explains the failure). He then reads the magnet refutation as unfolding in two matched moves: first, a direct denial that a magnet attracts iron through any intention or action of its own at all (it has no mind, hence no "attracting" in the sense Lion's Roar's argument requires); and second, a positive account of what actually happens instead — "a different thing is present, and a different thing arises; a different thing is absent, and a different thing perishes" — where the fire-and-firewood pairing supplies the clearest illustration: fire doesn't "act on" firewood so much as fire's own arising and firewood's own destruction are simply two names for a single event with two conditions present together.

The five botanical examples that follow (the sunflower turning without any reverence, the plantain tree responding to thunder without any ear to hear it, the aśoka blooming at a touch it cannot feel, the orange and pomegranate fruiting through corpse-remains and bone-meal without any mind registering nourishment) are chosen specifically because each names a genuinely mindless object producing a response that looks, from outside, purposeful or reactive — exactly analogous to the mindless magnet's own "attraction" of iron, and, by direct extension, to buddha-nature's own dependence on cultivation without buddha-nature itself performing any act of drawing anything toward it. The chain of similes culminates in an explicit statement that even the twelve links of dependent origination — the paradigm case of one thing conditioning another — involve no fixed producer at all ("ignorance is not itself capable of 'attracting' formations... and yet it may still be said that ignorance conditions formations"), extending this fascicle's central point about buddha-nature into a claim about causation generally: dependent arising was never a story about one thing reaching out and pulling another into being, but about two things' mutual presence and absence tracking each other exactly.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The elephant parable's own closing qualification — the blind men "did not describe the elephant's own actual body, and yet cannot be said to have described nothing at all" — is the interpretive key to everything that follows: each of the six identifications of buddha-nature with form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness, or a self apart from the aggregates is partially right and partially wrong in exactly the same way a hand on the trunk is partially right about the elephant. Each identification follows an identical logical structure worth naming explicitly: some element of ordinary experience is impermanent in its everyday instances, yet exhibits a kind of continuity across change (form's succession of moments still yielding the thirty-two marks, a clan-name persisting across a thousand generations, a scripture outlasting every reader and speaker, heat remaining constant while the fire that carries it flickers) — and each identification then leaps from this "continuity within impermanence" to the further claim that this very continuity simply is buddha-nature, which is precisely the leap Part 27's fourteen-fold catechism already ruled out with its own insistence that buddha-nature is form and not-form, self and not-self, at once.

The sixth identification (self apart from the aggregates) receives the sūtra's most extended treatment because it is the position closest to the truth while still missing it: the seven similes of composite wholes (the lotus needing stem and leaves, the house needing wall and wood, the forest needing its four named tree species, the army needing its four divisions, brocade needing its five colors, the populace needing its four castes) all make the same point — there is no separate self floating free of the five aggregates, exactly as there is no separate flower floating free of stem and petal — while the Tathāgata's own self, defined instead as the boundless, unarising, unceasing Dharma-body possessed of the eight masteries, occupies an entirely different logical category from the composite self these similes rule out. The passage's own closing move — buddha-nature named not because beings already possess this permanent self now, but "because they are certain, in the end, to attain ultimate emptiness" — is this fascicle's own restatement, in the register of self-theory, of the same future-certainty framework Part 27 used to resolve the icchantika's claim to buddha-nature through the three times.

This catalogue of positive identifications (great compassion, great joy and equanimity, great faith, the single-child stage, the fourth power, the twelve links, the four unobstructed knowledges, the crown samādhi) shares a single justifying formula repeated word for word each time — "because every being is certain, in the end, to attain it" — which quietly resolves the tension between the elephant parable's own six rejected identifications and this passage's own seemingly parallel list: the earlier six failed because they identified buddha-nature with something merely continuous across change (a color, a name, a heat), while this list identifies buddha-nature with qualities the Buddha explicitly possesses now and every being is certain to possess eventually, making future attainment rather than persistent continuity the actual warrant. The closing exchange about the eighty-five thousand koṭis of unconvinced listeners, still present within the very assembly hearing this teaching, gives the fascicle's abstract inconceivability-language its most concrete demonstration: the sūtra does not merely assert that faith in itself is remarkable, it points to visible proof of the claim's difficulty, sitting in the audience at that very moment — and the Buddha's own answer, that these same people will certainly come to believe in a future age, extends this entire chapter's future-certainty framework for buddha-nature (already used for the icchantika in Part 27 and for self in this fascicle's own elephant-parable close) to something as ordinary and human as an audience's own present skepticism.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The graduated series of self-tests (the seven-fold week-by-week starvation fast, willingness to relinquish wealth and body "like grass," the lamp-body austerity, and finally offering head, eyes, marrow, and flesh outright) is structured as an ascending scale of stakes, each one explicitly framed not as an end in itself but as a diagnostic — the bodhisattva does not undergo suffering to earn merit directly, but to discover empirically whether his own resolve holds under pressure, with the discovery itself ("my mind did not retrogress") serving as the actual proof of non-retrogression. The animal-body vows extend the "freely chosen, not karmically earned" framework from Part 31 into an even more startling register: not merely accepting suffering personally, but offering one's own body as literal food while feigning death so the recipient feels no guilt — the passage is explicit that "though the bodhisattva undergoes an animal's own body, he never commits an animal's own karmic action," making the physical form and the moral status of the act come entirely apart.

The catalogue of "inconceivable" bodhisattva qualities that closes this section is worth reading as a systematic inventory of paradoxes this entire chapter has built toward: compassion without any prior relationship to justify it, kindness given with no expectation of return, austerity performed for others' benefit named as one's own true self-interest, gentleness and apparent arrogance both deployed as skillful means without either touching the bodhisattva's actual inner state, and — in the final, most philosophically loaded item — a bodhisattva who has already seen with complete clarity that there is no fixed being, no real affliction to be cleansed, and no genuine path apart from affliction to walk, and who nonetheless continues to perform the very conduct that "destroys suffering" as though all of this were real. This last paradox directly answers, in the register of the bodhisattva's own lived practice, the theoretical question the entire chapter opened with in Part 27: buddha-nature and ultimate emptiness were identified as the middle way from the very start, and here that identification cashes out as a mode of action that operates fully within apparent reality while never mistaking that reality for something fixed or final.

Should any of my own disciples be able to uphold, recite, copy out, expound, and distinctly explain this very subtle and wondrous scripture of the great nirvāṇa, he ought rather to lose his own body and life than ever once violate this rule — this is named 'the tide never exceeding its own limit.' "Fifth, it contains every kind of treasure. This very sūtra is itself an immeasurable treasury. What is meant by this treasure is the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right exertions, the four bases of supernatural power, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of awakening, the eightfold noble path, infant-like conduct, noble conduct, pure conduct, and heavenly conduct, every kind of skillful means, every being's own buddha-nature, the merit of the bodhisattva, the merit of the Tathāgata, the merit of the śrāvaka, the merit of the pratyekabuddha, the six perfections, immeasurable samādhis, and immeasurable wisdom — this is named its treasury. Beings of great size are the buddhas and bodhisattvas — named great beings on account of their great wisdom, their great body, their great mind, their great adornment, their great taming power, their great skillful means, their great teaching of the Dharma, their great strength, their great retinue, their great supernatural power, their great loving-kindness and compassion, their forever unchanging nature, their freedom from obstruction toward every being, and their capacity to embrace every being at once — this is named the dwelling-place of beings of great size. "Seventh, it never harbors a dead body. A dead body means the icchantika; one who has violated the four gravest precepts, or the five crimes of immediate retribution; one who has slandered the Vaipulya scriptures; one who calls non-Dharma the Dharma, and the Dharma non-Dharma; one who accepts and keeps the eight kinds of impure object, freely using property belonging to the Buddha or the Saṅgha as he wishes; or one who commits an unlawful act with a monk or a nun — this is named a dead body. This Nirvāṇa Sūtra is free of every one of these, and it is for this very reason that it is named 'never harboring a dead body.' "Eighth, it neither increases nor decreases. Because it is without any boundary at all, without beginning or end, not form, not a produced thing, ever-abiding, neither arising nor ceasing, because every being, without exception, is equal within it, and because the nature of every dharma shares one and the same nature — it is for these very reasons that this is named 'neither increasing nor decreasing.' It is for this very reason that this sūtra, exactly like that great ocean, possesses these eight inconceivable qualities."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Tuṣita passage extends the previous section's catalogue of bodhisattva paradoxes into its most concrete instance yet: a being who philosophically censures every state of existence as inherently flawed nonetheless takes rebirth in exactly the finest heaven the desire realm offers, and does so without the desire that would ordinarily be required to produce such a birth — the sūtra's own explanation (he can teach and transform beings from any state of existence equally well, so the choice of Tuṣita reflects no craving at all) resolves the paradox the same way Part 31 resolved the bodhisattva's voluntary hell-rebirth: an outcome ordinarily produced by karma is instead produced by vow, leaving the karmic mechanism itself untouched.

The point-by-point correspondence between the ocean's eight wonders and the sūtra's own eight — gradual deepening mapped onto the graduated precepts and fruits, the unreachable bottom mapped onto the Buddha's own paradoxical non-action (neither arising, ceasing, eating, nor teaching, and yet somehow "teaching" this very sūtra), one uniform taste mapped onto every being's shared buddha-nature, the tide's fixed limit mapped onto monastic discipline that will not be transgressed even at the cost of life, the ocean's treasures mapped onto the sūtra's own inventory of practices, its great inhabitants mapped onto buddhas and bodhisattvas, its rejection of corpses mapped onto its own exclusion of the icchantika and precept-breaker, and its constancy under every river's inflow mapped onto the sūtra's own permanence — is this collection's most extended single instance of the "pervasive simile" method Part 29 catalogued among its eight kinds of simile, mapping an entire natural phenomenon onto an entire text feature by feature rather than illustrating one point at a time. The second wonder's own aside — that the sūtra teaches self and no-self, permanence and impermanence, emptiness and non-emptiness, by turns, "and yet no single, fixed formulation of buddha-nature is ever given as final" — is this fascicle's own explicit acknowledgment of the very method the whole chapter has practiced since Part 27's fourteen-fold catechism: depth, here, is defined precisely as resistance to any single settled formula.

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar said: "World-Honored One, if it is said that the Tathāgata's own being neither born nor ceasing is what makes this teaching 'deep' — every being has four kinds of birth: egg-birth, womb-birth, moisture-birth, and transformation-birth, and all four kinds of birth are found among human beings. Moisture-birth is exemplified, as the Buddha himself has taught, by my own past life as a bodhisattva, when I became King Mūrdhaja and King Hastigarbha; and, as is now taught, by the amra-tree woman and the kapittha-tree woman — one should therefore know that moisture-birth, too, is found among human beings. At the very beginning of the kalpa, every being, without exception, was born by transformation. The Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, having attained the eight kinds of mastery — for what cause and condition did he not choose transformation-birth for himself?" Good son, at the very beginning of the kalpa, every being was born by transformation, and, at exactly that time, no buddha had yet appeared in the world. Good son, it is like a being who, encountering the suffering of illness, requires a physician and medicine — at the very beginning of the kalpa, beings, born by transformation, though they possessed affliction, had not yet had their own illness manifest, and it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata did not appear in the world at that time. At the very beginning of the kalpa, beings' own bodies and minds were not yet fit vessels for the Dharma, and it is for this very reason, too, that the Tathāgata did not appear in the world then. Good son, the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, possesses affairs and undertakings that surpass every other being — namely, his own lineage, his own retinue, and his own parents. Because these are so excellent, whatever Dharma he teaches, every person believes and accepts it — it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata does not take on a transformation-body. Good son, every being has a father who performs the deeds of a father, and a son who performs the deeds of a son. Should the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, take on a transformation-body, he would then have no father or mother at all — and, without a father or mother, how could he possibly cause every being to perform every kind of wholesome deed? It is for this very reason that the Tathāgata does not take on a transformation-body. Good son, within the Buddha's own true Dharma, there are two kinds of protection: internal, and external. Internal protection is the precepts themselves; external protection is one's own clan and kin. Should the Buddha, the Tathāgata, take on a transformation-body, he would then have no external protection at all — it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata does not take on a transformation-body. Good son, there are people who, relying upon their own lineage, give rise to arrogance — and the Tathāgata, wishing to destroy arrogance of exactly this kind, is born instead into a noble lineage, rather than taking on a transformation-body. Good son, the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, has genuine parents — his father named Śuddhodana, and his mother named Māyā — and yet beings still say that this is all mere illusion. How, then, could he possibly take on a transformation-body instead? And, were he to take on a transformation-body, how could there possibly be relics from his own shattered body afterward? It is for the sake of increasing beings' own merit that the Tathāgata allows his own body to be shattered and offered for veneration — it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata does not take on a transformation-body. Every buddha, without exception, is entirely without transformation-birth — how, then, could I alone be made to take on a transformation-body?"

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lion's Roar's question presses a genuine inconsistency: if the Buddha possesses the eight kinds of mastery, transformation-birth (arising instantaneously, without gestation or biological parents) would seem the most fitting form for one already free of every ordinary constraint, and its absence needs explaining rather than assuming. The Buddha's six reasons divide cleanly into two groups: three concern what the audience needs to actually accept the teaching (an unimpeachable lineage and parentage that lends his words automatic credibility, the biological "protection" of real kin the way precepts furnish inward protection, and the deliberate humbling of caste-pride by being born into it rather than appearing above it) and three concern what the physical fact of an ordinary birth and body make possible afterward (real parents who can perform their own parental role reciprocally, a genuine body capable of being cremated and enshrined as relics, and the simple consistency of matching every other buddha's own historical pattern). Read together, the reasons make a case that has run through this collection since Part 27: the Buddha's own extraordinary freedom is not expressed by escaping ordinary categories altogether, but by inhabiting them so completely and so skillfully that even something as constrained as a physical human birth becomes an instrument of teaching rather than a limitation on it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lion's Roar's closing hymn functions as a summary in verse of nearly every argument this six-fascicle chapter has made in prose, now compressed into a sequence of epithets each justified by a single line of reasoning: "great compassion" earned by delivering beings from ignorance, "the guide" earned by revealing the true road, "the great ship's captain" earned by having crossed the river himself before bringing others across, "the great king of physicians" earned by knowing cause and cessation together. The mother-and-sick-child and father-and-only-child similes recall the "single-child stage" identification of buddha-nature from earlier in this very fascicle, now given emotional rather than doctrinal expression — and the image of hell endured without any felt pain restates, in verse, the same freely-chosen-not-karmically-earned suffering Part 31 and this fascicle's own bodhisattva catalogue established in prose.

The verse's own philosophical center sits in its account of the Buddha's teaching on suffering as "not made by oneself, nor by another, nor by both, nor without any cause at all" — a fourfold rejection of every possible account of causation a rival philosophy might offer, closing the same causation debate this fascicle opened with the magnet argument, and doing so in the same register Nāgārjuna's own tetralemma would later make famous. The hymn's final image — a mind that holds neither friend nor foe, remaining forever equal — returns to the very quality this collection has tracked since the sūtra's opening fascicles as the clearest outward sign of what "seeing buddha-nature" actually looks like in practice, closing the entire Lion's Roar chapter on the same note it opened with in Part 27: a roar is only truly a lion's roar once uttered before an audience capable of recognizing it, and Lion's Roar's own hymn is offered as exactly that recognition, given back to the one who first taught him to give it.

Part 32 Complete

This closes Part 32 (fascicle 32 of 40) and, with it, the entire six-fascicle 師子吼菩薩品第十一, begun in Part 27 with Lion's Roar's opening question about why he alone, among the assembly, could be said to give a genuine lion's roar. Across these six fascicles, buddha-nature has been identified in turn with ultimate emptiness, the middle way, the twelve links of dependent origination, the Śūraṅgama samādhi, and the middle path between every extreme — tested against a poor man's hidden treasure, a milk-curd debate spanning an entire fascicle, an eightfold catalogue of scriptural simile, Cunda's own hardest challenge that karma might never permit awakening, and, in this final fascicle, seven kinds of people crossing the river of birth and death, a magnet that never actually pulls iron of its own accord, and six blind men each grasping only a part of an elephant they could never fully describe. The chapter closes on the same insistence that opened it: buddha-nature is real, universal, and certain to be seen — and yet cultivation remains entirely necessary, since nothing about universal possession was ever a claim that the path could be skipped.
