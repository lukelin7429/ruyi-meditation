The Poor Man's Hidden Treasure

A new chapter opens as the bodhisattva Lion's Roar earns his name through a genuine roar of a question: what is buddha-nature, why is it called that, why can't all beings see it, and what exactly do the ten-stage bodhisattva and the Buddha each see that the other does not? The answer identifies buddha-nature in turn with ultimate emptiness, the middle way, and the twelve links of dependent origination, illustrated through a poor man who owns a buried treasure of gold he has never seen, and closing on a famous catalogue of fourteen paradoxical descriptions — form and not-form, one and not-one, existent and non-existent — none of which quite captures it, and all of which, together, come close.

The Lion's Roar

爾時佛告一切大眾：「諸善男子！汝等若疑，有佛、無佛，有法、無法，有僧、無僧，有苦、無苦，有集、無集，有滅、無滅，有道、無道，有實、無實，有我、無我，有樂、無樂，有淨、無淨，有常、無常，有乘、無乘，有性、無性，有眾生、無眾生，有有、無有，有真、無真，有因、無因，有果、無果，有作、無作，有業、無業，有報、無報者，今恣汝所問吾，當為汝分別解說。善男子！我實不見，若天、若人、若魔、若梵、若沙門、若婆羅門，有來問我不能答者。」爾時會中有一菩薩名師子吼，即從座起，斂容整服，前禮佛足，長跪叉手，白佛言：「世尊！我適欲問，如來大慈復垂聽許？」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the Buddha said to the whole great assembly: "Good sons, should you harbor any doubt at all — whether the Buddha exists or not, the Dharma exists or not, the Saṅgha exists or not, suffering exists or not, its arising exists or not, its cessation exists or not, the path exists or not, reality exists or not, self exists or not, bliss exists or not, purity exists or not, permanence exists or not, a vehicle exists or not, a nature exists or not, a being exists or not, existence itself exists or not, truth exists or not, cause exists or not, fruit exists or not, action exists or not, karma exists or not, or its fruit exists or not — you may now ask me freely of anything at all, and I shall explain and distinguish it fully for you. Good son, I truly see no one at all — whether god, human, Māra, Brahmā, śramaṇa, or brahmin — who has ever come to question me and found me unable to answer." At that time, within the assembly, there was a bodhisattva named Lion's Roar, who at once rose from his seat, composed his own bearing, arranged his robes, bowed before the Buddha's feet, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I now wish to ask a question — might the Tathāgata's own great compassion grant me permission to do so?"

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the Buddha said to the great assembly: "Good sons, you should now hold this very bodhisattva in deep reverence, respect, and praise, and should make offering to him with every kind of incense, flower, music, garland, banner, canopy, clothing, food, bedding, medicine, dwelling, and hall, welcoming him and seeing him off. Why is this? This person has already, under every buddha of the past, deeply planted wholesome roots, and, his own merit fully accomplished, wishes now, before me, to roar this very lion's roar. Good son, it is like a lion-king, who, knowing his own bodily strength, his fangs and claws sharp as blades, his four feet planted firmly on the ground, abiding securely within his own cave, raises his tail and lets out his own roar — should any being possess every one of these marks in full, know that he is thereby able to roar a true lion's roar. The true lion-king, emerging from his cave at dawn, stretches and yawns, gazes about in all four directions, and lets out a roar that shakes the earth, for eleven reasons. What are these eleven? First, to destroy those who are not truly lions yet falsely pretend to be; second, to test his own strength; third, to purify the place where he dwells; fourth, so that his own cubs may know where he is; fifth, so that the herd may hold no fear; sixth, so that those asleep may awaken; seventh, so that every heedless beast may become heedful; eighth, so that other beasts may come to rely upon him; ninth, to tame the great, fragrant elephant; tenth, to instruct his own offspring; and eleventh, to adorn his own retinue. Every bird and beast, hearing the lion's roar, responds accordingly: those of watery nature sink deep beneath the water; those that walk on land hide within their own dens; those that fly fall from the sky; and every great, fragrant elephant flees in terror, voiding its own bowels. Good sons, it is like the jackal, which, though it follows the lion for a full hundred years, is never, in the end, able to roar a lion's own roar at all — while a lion's own cub, having reached but three years of age, is already able to roar exactly as the lion-king himself does."

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, the Tathāgata's own complete and correct awakening is his own fangs and claws of wisdom; the four bases of psychic power and the body fully complete with the six perfections are his own body; the ten powers, fierce and mighty, are his own strength; great compassion is his own tail; and, abiding securely within the pure cave of the four meditative absorptions, he roars this very lion's roar for the sake of every being, shattering the army of Māra. He reveals to the assembly the ten powers, opens the very ground the Buddha himself walks upon, becomes a place of refuge for every wrong view, comforts the multitude fearful of birth-and-death, awakens beings asleep in ignorance, and brings a mind of remorse to those who practice evil dharmas. He reveals to every being holding wrong view that the six teachers' own roar is no lion's roar at all, breaks the arrogant mind of Pūraṇa and the rest, brings a mind of remorse to the two lesser vehicles, teaches the bodhisattvas of the five abidings to give rise to a mind of great strength, and causes the fourfold assembly of right view to hold no fear at all before the fourfold assembly of wrong view. Emerging from the cave of noble conduct, pure conduct, and heavenly conduct, he stretches and yawns to break the arrogance of every being; gazes about in every direction to cause every being to give rise to wholesome dharmas; plants his four feet firmly on the ground to cause beings to attain the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge; and roars this lion's roar so that beings may become fully established in the perfection of moral discipline. A lion's roar is itself named a resolute, decisive teaching: that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature, and that the Tathāgata is ever-abiding, without any change at all. Good son, though the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha follow the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, throughout immeasurable hundreds of thousands of incalculable kalpas, they are, even so, never able to roar this lion's roar. Should the bodhisattva of the ten abidings be able to practice these three conducts, know that he is thereby able to roar this very lion's roar. Good sons, this very bodhisattva-mahāsattva Lion's Roar now wishes to roar precisely this great lion's roar — it is for this very reason that you should hold him in the deepest reverence, respect, and praise."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the chapter's own title as taken from the questioner rather than the topic, exactly as the previous chapter was named for Bright Virtuous King — this bodhisattva's own name, and the fact that he alone among the assembly is able to ask a question worthy of the name "lion's roar," itself signals the shift this new chapter marks: the previous chapter examined "conduct freely undertaken according to circumstance" (the ten merits, each a practice a bodhisattva engages), while this one turns to "conduct that abandons every mark to enter direct realization" — buddha-nature itself as the object actually verified, rather than a further practice leading toward it. He reads the extended lion-roar simile's eleven reasons for roaring as falling into two functional groups: benefiting the rightly-practicing (the four-legged land creatures and the assembly of cubs, corresponding to monastics and lay disciples securely established in the true teaching) and vanquishing the wrong (creatures that fly or flee, corresponding to Māra's own forces and the followers of other paths, who scatter at the sound of genuine teaching) — with the elephant specifically representing external, non-Buddhist ascetic teachers powerful enough to require deliberate taming rather than mere dispersal.

He glosses the correspondence between the lion's own physical attributes and the Buddha's own qualities precisely: the four feet map onto the four bases of psychic power (aspiration, diligence, mind, and investigation), the body onto the six perfections' own completion, the roar's power onto the ten powers, and the tail — chosen, on Huiyuan's reading, because a lion's tail is the last part to disappear from view and the part that signals the whole animal's continued presence — onto great compassion, which likewise remains active even where the Buddha's other qualities might seem, to an unaware onlooker, to have withdrawn from view. His point in tracing the "three conducts" a tenth-stage bodhisattva must master to roar this same roar (noble conduct, pure conduct, and heavenly conduct, the very first three of the five practices this collection tracked from Part 11 onward) is that this fascicle's entire treatment of buddha-nature, however abstract it becomes in the pages that follow, is framed from its very first line as something that must actually be practiced into, not merely reasoned toward.

Two Kinds of Adornment, and the Middle Way

Chinese Source

爾時世尊告師子吼菩薩摩訶薩言：「善男子！汝若欲問今可隨意。」師子吼菩薩摩訶薩白佛言：「世尊！云何為佛性？以何義故名為佛性？何故復名常樂我淨？若一切眾生有佛性者，何故不見一切眾生所有佛性？十住菩薩住何等法，不了了見？佛住何等法，而了了見？十住菩薩以何等眼，不了了見？佛以何眼而了了見？」佛言：「善男子！善哉，善哉！若有人能為法諮啟，則為具足二種莊嚴：一者智慧，二者福德。若有菩薩具足如是二莊嚴者，則知佛性，亦復解知名為佛性，乃至能知十住菩薩以何眼見，諸佛世尊以何眼見。」……「善男子！慧莊嚴者，謂從一地乃至十地，是名慧莊嚴。福德莊嚴者，謂檀波羅蜜乃至般若非般若波羅蜜。復次善男子！慧莊嚴者，所謂諸佛菩薩。福德莊嚴者，謂聲聞緣覺九住菩薩。復次善男子！福德莊嚴者，有為、有漏、有有、有果報、有礙、非常，是凡夫法。慧莊嚴者，無為、無漏、無無、無果報、無礙、常住。善男子！汝今具足是二莊嚴，是故能問甚深妙義，我亦具足是二莊嚴，能答是義。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the World-Honored One said to the bodhisattva-mahāsattva Lion's Roar: "Good son, should you wish to ask a question, you may now do so freely." The bodhisattva-mahāsattva Lion's Roar said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what is buddha-nature? For what meaning is it named buddha-nature? Why, further, is it named permanence, bliss, self, and purity? Should every being possess buddha-nature, why is it that not every being sees this very buddha-nature they possess? In what dharma does the bodhisattva of the ten abidings abide, such that he does not see it with complete clarity? In what dharma does the Buddha abide, such that he sees it with complete clarity? With what eye does the bodhisattva of the ten abidings fail to see it with complete clarity? And with what eye does the Buddha see it with complete clarity?" The Buddha said: "Good son, excellent, excellent! Should anyone be able to inquire into the Dharma in this way, he is thereby fully endowed with two kinds of adornment: wisdom, and merit. Should any bodhisattva be fully endowed with these two adornments, he thereby knows buddha-nature, and further understands what is named buddha-nature, and is even able to know with what eye the bodhisattva of the ten abidings sees it, and with what eye every buddha, every world-honored one, sees it."… "Good son, the adornment of wisdom means progressing from the first ground up through the tenth ground — this is what is named the adornment of wisdom. The adornment of merit means the perfection of giving, up through the perfection of wisdom that is not yet the true perfection of wisdom. Furthermore, good son, the adornment of wisdom belongs to every buddha and bodhisattva; the adornment of merit belongs to the śrāvaka, the pratyekabuddha, and the bodhisattva of the ninth abiding. Furthermore, good son, the adornment of merit is conditioned, subject to outflow, bound up with existence, bearing a karmic fruit, obstructed, and impermanent — this is the dharma of the ordinary being. The adornment of wisdom is unconditioned, free of outflow, free of existence, free of karmic fruit, unobstructed, and ever-abiding. Good son, you are now fully endowed with these two adornments, and it is for this very reason that you are able to ask a question of such very deep and subtle meaning — and I, too, am fully endowed with these two adornments, and am therefore able to answer this very question."

「善男子！汝問云何為佛性者？諦聽，諦聽！吾當為汝分別解說。善男子！佛性者名第一義空，第一義空名為智慧。所言空者，不見空與不空。智者見空及與不空、常與無常、苦之與樂、我與無我。空者一切生死，不空者謂大涅槃；乃至無我者即是生死，我者謂大涅槃。見一切空，不見不空，不名中道；乃至見一切無我，不見我者，不名中道，中道者名為佛性。以是義故，佛性常恒、無有變易，無明覆故，令諸眾生不能得見。聲聞緣覺見一切空，不見不空；乃至見一切無我，不見於我。以是義故，不得第一義空，不得第一義空故，不行中道，無中道故，不見佛性。善男子！不見中道者，凡有三種：一者定樂行，二者定苦行，三者苦樂行。定樂行者，所謂菩薩摩訶薩憐愍一切諸眾生故，雖復處在阿鼻地獄，如三禪樂。定苦行者，謂諸凡夫。苦樂行者，謂聲聞、緣覺，聲聞緣覺行於苦樂，作中道想。以是義故，雖有佛性而不能見。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, you ask what buddha-nature is — listen closely, listen closely, and I shall explain and distinguish this fully for your sake. Good son, buddha-nature is named the ultimate emptiness, and this ultimate emptiness is itself named wisdom. What is meant by 'emptiness' here does not mean failing to see either emptiness or what is not empty — a wise person sees both emptiness and what is not empty, both permanence and impermanence, both suffering and bliss, both self and non-self. Emptiness is birth-and-death entirely; what is not empty is this great nirvāṇa; and so on, up through: non-self is birth-and-death, and self is this great nirvāṇa. Seeing only emptiness everywhere, and never what is not empty, is not named the middle way; and so on, up through: seeing only non-self everywhere, and never the self, is not named the middle way. The middle way is what is named buddha-nature. It is for this very reason that buddha-nature is ever-abiding, without any change at all — and it is only because it is covered over by ignorance that beings are unable to see it. The śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha see only emptiness everywhere, and never what is not empty; and so on, up through: they see only non-self everywhere, and never the self. It is for this very reason that they fail to attain the ultimate emptiness, and, failing to attain the ultimate emptiness, fail to practice the middle way, and, lacking the middle way, fail to see buddha-nature. Good son, those who fail to see the middle way are, in all, of three kinds: those fixed in blissful practice, those fixed in painful practice, and those practicing a mixture of both. Fixed in blissful practice describes the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, who, out of compassion for every being, though he dwells within the Avīci hell itself, experiences it as though it were the bliss of the third meditative absorption. Fixed in painful practice describes every ordinary being. And practicing a mixture of pain and pleasure describes the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha, who, practicing amid both suffering and pleasure, form the notion that this itself constitutes the middle way. It is for this very reason that, though they, too, possess buddha-nature, they are unable to see it."

「如汝所問，以何義故名佛性者？善男子！佛性者，即是一切諸佛阿耨多羅三藐三菩提中道種子。復次善男子！道有三種，謂下、上、中。下者——梵天無常，謬見是常。上者——生死無常，謬見是常；三寶是常，橫計無常。何故名上？能得最上阿耨多羅三藐三菩提故。中者名第一義空，無常見無常，常見於常。第一義空，不名為下。何以故？一切凡夫所不得故。不名為上。何以故？即是上故。諸佛菩薩所修之道，不上、不下，以是義故，名為中道。復次善男子！生死本際，凡有二種：一者無明，二者有愛。是二中間，則有生老病死之苦，是名中道，如是中道能破生死，故名為中。以是義故，中道之法名為佛性，是故佛性常樂我淨。以諸眾生不能見故，無常無樂、無我無淨，佛性實非無常無樂、無我無淨。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"As you have asked, for what meaning is it named buddha-nature? Good son, buddha-nature is precisely the middle-way seed of unsurpassed, complete awakening for every buddha there is. Furthermore, good son, the path is of three kinds: lower, upper, and middle. The lower path is exemplified by taking Brahmā, who is impermanent, to be mistakenly seen as permanent. The upper path is exemplified by taking birth-and-death, which is impermanent, to be mistakenly seen as permanent, while the Three Jewels, which are permanent, are mistakenly calculated to be impermanent. Why is this named 'upper'? Because it is able to attain the very highest, unsurpassed, complete awakening. The middle path is named the ultimate emptiness: seeing the impermanent as impermanent, and the permanent as permanent. This ultimate emptiness is not named 'lower' — why is this? Because it is something every ordinary being fails to attain. Nor is it named 'upper' — why is this? Because it is itself the very meaning of 'upper.' The path that every buddha and bodhisattva cultivates is neither upper nor lower — it is for this very reason that it is named the middle way. Furthermore, good son, the very origin of birth-and-death is, in all, of two kinds: ignorance, and the craving for existence. Between these two lies the very suffering of birth, old age, illness, and death — this is what is named the middle way, since this middle way alone is able to break through birth-and-death, and it is for this reason that it is named 'middle.' It is for this very reason that the dharma of the middle way is named buddha-nature — and it is for this reason, too, that buddha-nature is permanence, bliss, self, and purity. It is only because beings are unable to see it that it appears as impermanence, suffering, non-self, and impurity — buddha-nature, in truth, is not impermanence, suffering, non-self, and impurity at all."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Lion's Roar's opening eight-part question as splitting evenly into two halves — the first four questions asking about the very substance of what is verified (buddha-nature's own reality, its name, why it bears the four attributes of permanence-bliss-self-purity, and why it remains unseen despite being universally present) and the last four asking about the practice and vision that verifies it (what a tenth-stage bodhisattva and a buddha each abide in, and what each sees with) — and reads the requirement of "two adornments," wisdom and merit, as the sūtra's own answer to why such a question can be asked or answered at all: genuine inquiry into buddha-nature is not a matter of intellectual capacity alone, but requires the accumulated merit of the ten perfections practiced to completion, alongside the wisdom that has actually progressed through all ten grounds — which is why Lion's Roar's own question is treated, from its very first line, as evidence of an already-advanced attainment rather than mere curiosity.

He reads the identification of buddha-nature with "the middle way" as resolving what could otherwise seem an odd equation (emptiness, usually a purely negative term denying fixed existence, identified with something positively described as permanent, blissful, and pure) by insisting the middle way is not a compromise position located between two extremes, but the correct, complete seeing of both truths at once — emptiness with regard to birth-and-death, and non-emptiness with regard to nirvāṇa — where the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha's own failure is not seeing too little, but seeing only half: emptiness everywhere, permanence nowhere. His gloss on the three kinds of practice that fail to reach this middle (the bodhisattva fixed in bliss even amid hell's own suffering, the ordinary being fixed in suffering throughout, and the two lesser vehicles oscillating between both while mistaking this oscillation itself for balance) reads the third case as the subtlest error of the three, precisely because it looks, superficially, like the very moderation the middle way might seem to recommend — which is exactly why the sūtra needs to specify that the middle way is a matter of correct vision, not psychological equanimity between extremes.

The Twelve Links, and the Poor Man's Hidden Treasure

「善男子！譬如貧人，家有寶藏，是人不見，以不見故，無常無樂、無我無淨。有善知識而語之言：『汝舍宅中，有金寶藏，何故如是貧窮困苦、無常無樂、無我無淨？』即以方便令彼得見。以得見故，是人即得常樂我淨。佛性亦爾，眾生不見，以不見故，無常無樂、無我無淨。有善知識諸佛菩薩，以方便力，種種教告，令彼得見，以得見故，眾生即得常樂我淨。復次善男子！眾生起見，凡有二種：一者常見，二者斷見。如是二見不名中道，無常無斷乃名中道。無常無斷，即是觀照十二因緣智，如是觀智是名佛性。二乘之人雖觀因緣，猶亦不得名為佛性。佛性雖常，以諸眾生無明覆故，不能得見。又未能渡十二因緣河，猶如兔馬。何以故？不見佛性故。善男子！是觀十二因緣智慧，即是阿耨多羅三藐三菩提種子，以是義故，十二因緣名為佛性。善男子！譬如胡瓜名為熱病。何以故？能為熱病作因緣故。十二因緣亦復如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, it is like a poor man whose own house holds a hidden treasure, which this man does not see — and, because he does not see it, he remains impermanent, without bliss, without self, and without purity. There is a good and wise friend who says to him: 'Within your own house, there is a treasure of gold — why, then, do you remain so poverty-stricken and afflicted, impermanent, without bliss, without self, and without purity?' And, through skillful means, he causes this man to see it at last — and, through seeing it, this man thereupon attains permanence, bliss, self, and purity. Buddha-nature is exactly the same: beings do not see it, and, because they do not see it, remain impermanent, without bliss, without self, and without purity. There is a good and wise friend — every buddha and bodhisattva — who, through the power of skillful means, teaches and instructs them in every kind of way, causing them to see it at last — and, through seeing it, beings thereupon attain permanence, bliss, self, and purity. Furthermore, good son, the views beings give rise to are, in all, of two kinds: the view of permanence, and the view of annihilation. Neither of these two views is named the middle way — it is freedom from both permanence and annihilation that is named the middle way. This very freedom from permanence and annihilation is itself the wisdom that contemplates the twelve links of dependent origination — and this contemplative wisdom is itself named buddha-nature. The practitioners of the two lesser vehicles, though they, too, contemplate this dependent origination, still cannot, on that account, be said to have attained the name of buddha-nature. Though buddha-nature is permanent, beings, covered over by their own ignorance, are unable to see it — and, further, they have not yet crossed the river of the twelve links, remaining like a rabbit or a horse attempting to cross it. Why is this? Because they do not see buddha-nature. Good son, this very wisdom that contemplates the twelve links is itself the seed of unsurpassed, complete awakening — it is for this very reason that the twelve links are themselves named buddha-nature. Good son, it is like how a bitter melon is named 'fever' — why is this? Because it is able to serve as the cause and condition of fever. The twelve links are exactly the same."

「善男子！佛性者，有因有因因，有果有果果。有因者即十二因緣，因因者即是智慧，有果者即是阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，果果者即是無上大般涅槃。善男子！譬如無明為因，諸行為果，行因識果，以是義故，彼無明體亦因、亦因因，識亦果、亦果果。佛性亦爾。善男子！以是義故，十二因緣不出不滅、不常不斷、非一非二、不來不去、非因非果。善男子！是因非果如佛性，是果非因如大涅槃。是因是果，如十二因緣所生之法。非因非果名為佛性。非因果故，常恒無變。以是義故，我經中說十二因緣其義甚深，無知無見，不可思惟，乃是諸佛菩薩境界，非諸聲聞緣覺所及。以何義故甚深甚深？眾生業行不常不斷而得果報，雖念念滅而無所失，雖無作者而有作業，雖無受者而有果報，受者雖滅果不敗亡，無有慮知和合而有。一切眾生雖與十二因緣共行而不見知，不見知故無有終始，十住菩薩惟見其終不見其始，諸佛世尊見始見終。以是義故，諸佛了了得見佛性。善男子！一切眾生不能見於十二因緣，是故輪轉。善男子！如蠶作繭，自生自死。一切眾生亦復如是，不見佛性故，自造結業，流轉生死，猶如拍毬。善男子！是故我於諸經中說：『若有人見十二緣者，即是見法。見法者，即是見佛，佛者即是佛性。何以故？一切諸佛以此為性。』」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, buddha-nature possesses a cause and a cause of the cause, and possesses a fruit and a fruit of the fruit. The cause is precisely the twelve links of dependent origination; the cause of the cause is precisely wisdom itself; the fruit is precisely unsurpassed, complete awakening; and the fruit of the fruit is precisely this unsurpassed great nirvāṇa. Good son, it is like how ignorance is the cause and mental formations are its fruit, while formations are, in turn, the cause and consciousness their fruit — and, in this very sense, ignorance's own substance is both a cause and a cause of a cause, while consciousness is both a fruit and a fruit of a fruit. Buddha-nature is exactly the same. Good son, it is for this very reason that the twelve links neither emerge nor cease, are neither permanent nor annihilated, are neither one nor two, neither come nor go, and are neither cause nor fruit. Good son, that which is cause but not fruit is buddha-nature itself; that which is fruit but not cause is this great nirvāṇa itself; that which is both cause and fruit is the very dharma produced by the twelve links; and that which is neither cause nor fruit is named buddha-nature. Because it is neither cause nor fruit, it is forever unchanging. It is for this very reason that I teach, throughout my own scriptures, that the meaning of the twelve links is exceedingly profound, beyond ordinary knowledge or vision, inconceivable, the very domain of every buddha and bodhisattva, and not something the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha can reach at all. For what reason is it so exceedingly, exceedingly profound? Because beings' own karmic action is neither permanent nor annihilated, and yet bears its own fruit; though it perishes, thought after thought, nothing is ever lost; though there is no fixed agent, there is genuine action performed; though there is no fixed one who receives the fruit, there is a genuine fruit received; and though the one who receives it perishes, the fruit itself is never destroyed — arising through a combination beyond any deliberate calculation at all. Every being, though he acts together with these very twelve links, fails to see or know them, and, failing to see or know them, finds neither a beginning nor an end to them; the bodhisattva of the ten abidings sees only their end, but not their beginning; and every buddha, every world-honored one, sees both their beginning and their end. It is for this very reason that every buddha sees buddha-nature with complete clarity. Good son, every being, unable to see these twelve links, therefore turns endlessly within them. Good son, it is like a silkworm spinning its own cocoon, giving rise to itself and dying within it by its own hand — every being is exactly the same: failing to see buddha-nature, he creates his own binding karma by himself, and turns through birth-and-death exactly like a bouncing ball. Good son, it is for this very reason that I teach, throughout my own scriptures: 'Should any person see these twelve links, this is itself seeing the Dharma; and seeing the Dharma is itself seeing the Buddha; and the Buddha is himself buddha-nature. Why is this? Because every buddha takes this very thing as his own nature.'"

「善男子！觀十二緣智，凡有四種：一者下，二者中，三者上，四者上上。下智觀者不見佛性，以不見故得聲聞道。中智觀者不見佛性，以不見故得緣覺道。上智觀者見不了了，不了了故住十住地。上上智觀者見了了故，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提道。以是義故，十二因緣名為佛性。佛性者即第一義空，第一義空名為中道，中道者即名為佛，佛者名為涅槃。」爾時師子吼菩薩摩訶薩白佛言：「世尊！若佛與佛性無差別者，一切眾生何用修道？」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, the wisdom that contemplates the twelve links is, in all, of four kinds: lower, middle, upper, and upper-upper. One who contemplates with lower wisdom does not see buddha-nature, and, failing to see it, attains the path of the śrāvaka. One who contemplates with middle wisdom does not see buddha-nature, and, failing to see it, attains the path of the pratyekabuddha. One who contemplates with upper wisdom sees it, but not with complete clarity, and, lacking this complete clarity, abides at the ground of the ten abidings. One who contemplates with upper-upper wisdom sees it with complete clarity, and thereby attains the path of unsurpassed, complete awakening. It is for this very reason that the twelve links are named buddha-nature. Buddha-nature is itself the ultimate emptiness; the ultimate emptiness is named the middle way; the middle way is itself named the Buddha; and the Buddha is named nirvāṇa." At that time, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva Lion's Roar said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, should the Buddha and buddha-nature be without any distinction at all, what use would there be for any being at all to cultivate the path?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the poor man's hidden treasure as answering the question left open by the previous section's identification of buddha-nature with the middle way: why does something already permanently present remain unseen at all? His answer, on Huiyuan's reading, is that the treasure was never absent, only unknown — the poor man's own poverty was never a fact about the house, but a fact about his own ignorance of what the house already contained, and the good friend's intervention does not create wealth but reveals it, which is precisely why buddha-nature can be genuinely, truly present in every being while still requiring a teacher, a teaching, and skillful means to actually be seen.

He reads the cause/cause-of-cause/fruit/fruit-of-fruit framework as this sūtra's own technical apparatus for holding together two claims that could otherwise seem contradictory: buddha-nature is genuinely connected to ordinary causal process (the twelve links, contemplated correctly, function as its cause, exactly as ignorance functions as the cause of mental formations within ordinary dependent origination) and yet buddha-nature itself, considered in its own final nature, is "neither cause nor fruit" — permanent, unchanging, outside the causal chain altogether. The ignorance-formations-consciousness illustration makes this concrete: within the ordinary twelve links, ignorance is simultaneously a cause (of formations) and itself something caused (a "cause of a cause," produced by still earlier conditions), while consciousness is simultaneously a fruit (of formations) and itself something that causes further effects (a "fruit of a fruit") — and buddha-nature occupies the position outside this entire nested structure, related to it without being reducible to any single link within it.

On the four levels of contemplating the twelve links, he reads the progression (śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, tenth-stage bodhisattva, buddha) as calibrated precisely to how much of the causal chain each is able to see: the two lesser vehicles fail to see buddha-nature at all because their own contemplation stops at recognizing impermanence and dependent arising without penetrating to what lies beyond the chain itself; the bodhisattva sees it, but "not with complete clarity," since he sees only the chain's own ending (its cessation in nirvāṇa) without yet seeing its ultimate beginning; and only the buddha sees both beginning and end simultaneously, which is why complete clarity about buddha-nature and complete awakening are, on this fascicle's own account, the very same attainment described twice.

Why Bother Practicing the Path?

Chinese Source

佛言：「善男子！如汝所問，是義不然。佛與佛性雖無差別，然諸眾生悉未具足。善男子！譬如有人惡心害母，害已生悔，三業雖善，是人故名地獄人也。何以故？是人定當墮地獄故。是人雖無地獄陰界諸入，猶故得名為地獄人。善男子！是故我於諸經中說：『若見有人修行善者，名見天人。修行惡者，名見地獄。何以故？定受報故。』善男子！一切眾生定得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提故，是故我說一切眾生悉有佛性；一切眾生真實未有三十二相、八十種好，以是義故，我於此經而說是偈：『本有今無，本無今有。三世有法，無有是處。』」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said: "Good son, as you have asked, this does not seem correct. Though the Buddha and buddha-nature are without any distinction, every being has, in fact, not yet fully accomplished it. Good son, it is like a man who, with an evil mind, kills his own mother, and, having killed her, gives rise to remorse — though his own three actions have now turned wholesome, this man is nonetheless named a 'man of hell.' Why is this? Because this man must, in the end, certainly fall into hell. Though this man does not yet possess the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields of hell itself, he is, even so, already named a man of hell. Good son, it is for this very reason that I teach, throughout my own scriptures: 'Should one see a person practicing what is wholesome, this is named seeing a god or a human; should one see a person practicing what is unwholesome, this is named seeing a denizen of hell. Why is this? Because the fruit is thereby certainly determined.' Good son, because every being certainly attains unsurpassed, complete awakening, I therefore teach that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature — and yet every being, in genuine truth, does not yet possess the thirty-two marks or the eighty subsidiary signs. It is for this very reason that I speak, in this very sūtra, this verse: 'What was, is now absent; / what was not, is now present. / That a dharma exists across all three time-periods at once / could not possibly be so.'"

Chinese Source

「善男子！有者凡有三種：一未來有，二現在有，三過去有。一切眾生未來之世，當有阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是名佛性。一切眾生現在悉有煩惱諸結，是故現在無有三十二相、八十種好。一切眾生過去之世有斷煩惱，是故現在得見佛性。以是義故，我常宣說一切眾生悉有佛性，乃至一闡提等亦有佛性。一闡提等無有善法，佛性亦善，以未來有故，一闡提等悉有佛性。何以故？一闡提等定當得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提故。善男子！譬如有人家有乳酪，有人問言：『汝有蘇耶？』答言：『我有酪，實非蘇，以巧方便定當得故，故言有蘇。』眾生亦爾，悉皆有心，凡有心者，定當得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。以是義故，我常宣說一切眾生悉有佛性。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, 'existence' is, in all, of three kinds: future existence, present existence, and past existence. Every being, in a world to come, shall possess unsurpassed, complete awakening — this is what is named buddha-nature. Every being, in the present, is fully possessed of affliction and its fetters — and it is for this very reason that, in the present, he does not yet possess the thirty-two marks or the eighty subsidiary signs. Every being, in the past, has, at some point, severed affliction — and it is for this very reason that, in the present, he is able to come to see buddha-nature. It is for this very reason that I forever proclaim that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature, and that even the icchantika, too, possesses buddha-nature. The icchantika possesses no wholesome dharma at all, and yet buddha-nature is itself wholesome — and, because it exists as a future existence, the icchantika, too, possesses buddha-nature in full. Why is this? Because the icchantika, too, shall certainly, in the end, come to accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening. Good son, it is like a man whose own house contains milk and curds, and someone asks him: 'Do you have ghee?' He answers: 'I have curds, which are, in truth, not yet ghee — but, because I shall certainly, through skillful means, obtain it, I say that I have ghee.' Beings are exactly the same: every one of them possesses a mind, and whoever possesses a mind shall certainly, in the end, come to accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening. It is for this very reason that I forever proclaim that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the matricide-and-remorse simile as the sūtra's own precise answer to Lion's Roar's challenge: identity of destination does not collapse the distinction between having already arrived and being certainly bound to arrive — the murderer is genuinely, truthfully named "a man of hell" the instant his crime is committed, even though every element of hell itself (its own aggregates, elements, and sense-fields) remains, for now, entirely absent from his actual present experience, because the label tracks a fixed future certainty rather than a present possession. Buddha-nature and the Buddha share exactly this relationship: calling every being's own buddha-nature identical to the Buddha's own attainment does not mean the thirty-two marks are already secretly present in an ordinary being's own body, only that the outcome is as fixed and certain as the murderer's own future rebirth in hell — which is why practicing the path remains entirely necessary, since it is the actual mechanism by which this already-certain future outcome comes to be realized in the present.

His gloss on the three kinds of existence (future, present, past) reads the famous "本有今無，本無今有" verse — already encountered by this collection in Part 17's treatment of the sūtra's own self-glossed puzzle about the Buddha's past teachings — as receiving here its most complete technical resolution: buddha-nature genuinely "was not, and now is" with respect to the present tense (an ordinary being does not currently possess the thirty-two marks), while simultaneously "was, and is now absent" with respect to a past tense (every being has, at some point across beginningless past lives, already severed some affliction, which is why buddha-nature can be spoken of as already glimpsed) — and the verse's own closing line, that no dharma can exist identically across all three times at once, rules out the naive reading that would make buddha-nature simply, flatly, and changelessly present regardless of tense. The milk-and-ghee exchange closing this section, on his reading, restates the same point through ordinary commercial honesty rather than technical grammar: saying "I have ghee" when one currently has only curds is not a lie, provided the transformation from curds to ghee is genuinely certain to occur — exactly the same structure by which the icchantika, currently possessing no wholesome dharma at all, can nonetheless be truthfully said to possess buddha-nature, since the eventual attainment is equally certain.

The Śūraṅgama Samādhi

Chinese Source

「善男子！畢竟有二種：一者莊嚴畢竟，二者究竟畢竟。一者世間畢竟，二者出世畢竟。莊嚴畢竟者，六波羅蜜。究竟畢竟者，一切眾生所得一乘，一乘者名為佛性，以是義故，我說一切眾生悉有佛性，一切眾生悉有一乘，以無明覆故不能得見。善男子！如欝單越、三十三天，果報覆故，此間眾生不能得見。佛性亦爾，諸結覆故，眾生不見。復次善男子！佛性者即首楞嚴三昧，性如醍醐，即是一切諸佛之母。以首楞嚴三昧力故，而令諸佛常樂我淨。一切眾生悉有首楞嚴三昧，以不修行故不得見，是故不能得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。善男子！首楞嚴三昧者，有五種名：一者首楞嚴三昧，二者般若波羅蜜，三者金剛三昧，四者師子吼三昧，五者佛性；隨其所作，處處得名。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, ultimate completion is of two kinds: the ultimate completion of adornment, and the ultimate completion of final attainment. Alternately: worldly ultimate completion, and transcendent ultimate completion. The ultimate completion of adornment is the six perfections. The ultimate completion of final attainment is the one vehicle that every being attains — and this one vehicle is itself named buddha-nature. It is for this very reason that I teach that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature, and that every being, without exception, possesses this one vehicle — and it is only because it is covered over by ignorance that it cannot be seen. Good son, it is like Uttarakuru or the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, which beings in this present world are unable to see, covered over as they are by their own karmic fruit — buddha-nature is exactly the same: it is because it is covered over by every fetter that beings fail to see it. Furthermore, good son, buddha-nature is itself the Śūraṅgama samādhi, its own nature like ghee itself, the very mother of every buddha there is. It is through the power of this very Śūraṅgama samādhi that every buddha attains permanence, bliss, self, and purity. Every being, without exception, possesses this very Śūraṅgama samādhi — and it is only because he fails to cultivate it that he fails to see it, and it is for this very reason that he is unable to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. Good son, this Śūraṅgama samādhi bears five distinct names: the Śūraṅgama samādhi, the perfection of wisdom, the vajra samādhi, the lion's roar samādhi, and buddha-nature — each name given according to what function it performs, in whatever context it appears."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the two kinds of "ultimate completion" as distinguishing the accumulated practice that adorns the path (the six perfections, cultivated deed by deed) from the single, final attainment that practice leads toward (the one vehicle, identical to buddha-nature itself) — a distinction he reads as clarifying why buddha-nature can be called both something every being already possesses and something that requires the entire six-perfection path to actually reach: the goal and the process leading to it are related but not identical, exactly as a treasure already buried in the ground is not the same thing as the digging that uncovers it. His gloss on the five names for a single samādhi (Śūraṅgama, the perfection of wisdom, the vajra samādhi, the lion's roar samādhi, and buddha-nature) reads these as functional descriptions of the very same reality rather than five distinct attainments, comparable to how a single concentrated mind is variously named a "faculty," a "power," or a "factor of awakening" depending on which of its functions is being emphasized at a given point in the path.

On the three-tiered concentration (buddha-nature as upper, the first meditative absorption as middle, and the bare mental factor of concentration present in every mental event as lower), he reads this hierarchy as demonstrating that "concentration" itself names a graded continuum rather than a single fixed capacity — every being already possesses some form of concentration at the very lowest, most basic level of mental functioning, which is part of what licenses the sūtra's own confident claim that buddha-nature, too, in its own way, is universally present, without this collapsing the very real distance between the lowest and highest points on that same continuum.

The Nairañjanā episode, on Huiyuan's reading, resolves what could otherwise seem the sharpest possible contradiction in this entire fascicle: the doctrine of no-self, taught throughout the whole of the earlier canon and reaffirmed by this very sūtra elsewhere, appears to directly contradict the buddha-nature teaching's own insistence on a permanent, self-like reality within every being. His gloss on the Buddha's resolution treats "self" and "no-self" here not as a single univocal term applied inconsistently, but as two distinct technical uses distinguished by what each denies: "no-self" denies the ordinary, mistaken self conventionally imagined as a fixed, independent controller of the five aggregates (a denial this sūtra never actually retracts), while "self," applied to buddha-nature, affirms something else entirely — permanence and freedom from every constraint, which is precisely why the Buddha can, without contradiction, speak of no-self as self, and self as no-self, in the same breath, since each usage targets an entirely different referent and neither statement is false with respect to what it actually denies or affirms.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The night-seeing/day-seeing image gives this fascicle's central epistemic claim its sharpest form yet: the bodhisattva of the ten abidings and the Buddha are not seeing two different things, nor is one seeing correctly while the other sees an illusion — they are seeing the very same buddha-nature under two different conditions of light, exactly as the same landscape looks utterly different by moonlight and by sun. The tenfold "ten parts, one part seen" formula makes this quantitative rather than merely qualitative: what separates a ten-abiding bodhisattva from a buddha is not a different kind of vision but an incomplete instance of the identical vision, which is also why the crescent-moon simile immediately following insists that invisibility on any given night is never evidence of nonexistence.

The twelve links passage extends the causal analysis begun earlier in this fascicle (the ignorance-formations-consciousness chain and the cause/cause-of-cause/fruit/fruit-of-fruit framework) into a claim about universal possession: every being holds all twelve links "by determination" even when a given link's actual unfolding is cut short by an early death or bypassed by rebirth in a realm lacking certain aggregates. This is the same logical move the sūtra makes with buddha-nature itself — present possession secured not by what is currently manifest, but by what is certain to unfold — making the twelve links a small-scale rehearsal of the very argument the icchantika debate has been making at the largest scale all fascicle long.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The herb simile makes an epistemic rather than metaphysical point: absence from a particular record (the twelve divisions of scripture as ordinarily read, or a cow's own particular digestion) is never evidence of absence in the thing itself. The fourteen-fold catechism that follows then systematically closes off every fixed predicate a reader might reach for — form, mark, oneness, permanence, existence, exhaustion, causation, meaning, language, self, emptiness — always in the same three-step pattern: yes, no, and neither, each justified by a different, specific reason rather than by a single blanket denial. Read together, the reasons form a small map of why buddha-nature resists description: it takes a body (the vajra body) yet exceeds every material property; it is universal yet is taught through the compromise of three vehicles; it is the unconditioned goal (empty, self-less, permanent) yet remains the very "seed" from which every wholesome, conditioned action must still grow.

The black-iron and seed-sprout similes then answer the natural follow-up question — if buddha-nature is neither straightforwardly present nor straightforwardly absent, how does causation touch it at all? Their shared answer is that a color, or a sprout's nature, is never simply "inside" what produces it nor simply "outside," imported from elsewhere — it arises only relationally, through conditions, which is exactly the register in which buddha-nature itself is said to become visible once the fire of affliction has gone out.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This passage's five successive redefinitions of the same ten headings are not repetition for its own sake but a deliberate escalation through five distinct registers of practice. The first pass sets a baseline via the taxonomy of three corrupt desires (status, spiritual reputation, and future power) and defines nirvāṇa itself through the fourfold dwelling/refuge/island/final-refuge imagery. The second pass reframes the same ten headings around the ascetic's four-disease/four-medicine structure and the three conducts introduced earlier in this collection (聖行/梵行/天行), tying "few desires" to specific physical disciplines (rag-robes, begging, dwelling under trees) rather than to an inner attitude alone. The third pass locates the same ten headings in the single, concrete act of studying this very sūtra with honest self-assessment — the practitioner's own private admission that he has not yet attained what the world credits him with. The fourth pass shifts to advanced solitary contemplative accomplishment (the eight liberations, the four unobstructed knowledges, freedom from the seven outflows), describing a practitioner far along the path. The fifth and final pass then closes the loop deliberately: where the very first pass condemned proximity to kings and donors as evil desire, this last pass shows that the identical external circumstance — moving among the powerful, receiving honor — is no longer any obstacle at all once the underlying desire for gain has been uprooted, so that what looked like the clearest case of corruption in round one becomes, in round five, compatible with genuine practice. The same ten words name ten different depths of the same underlying orientation, which is precisely why even the most accomplished bodhisattva described here still falls short of the Buddha's own complete clarity — there remains, even at the fifth and subtlest level, some structure of practice still being performed, and it is exactly the absence of any such remaining structure that the next section identifies as what finally distinguishes the Buddha's own seeing.

「善男子！如汝所言，十住菩薩以何眼故，雖見佛性而不了了？諸佛世尊以何眼故，見於佛性而得了了？善男子！慧眼見故，不得明了；佛眼見故，故得明了。為菩提行故，則不了了；若無行故，則得了了。住十住故雖見不了；住不住故則得了了。菩薩摩訶薩智慧因故，見不了了；諸佛世尊斷因果故，見則了了。一切覺者名為佛性，十住菩薩不得名為一切覺故，是故雖見而不明了。善男子！見有二種：一者眼見，二者聞見。諸佛世尊眼見佛性，如於掌中觀阿摩勒果。十住菩薩聞見佛性故不了了，十住菩薩唯能自知定得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，而不能知一切眾生悉有佛性。善男子！復有眼見，諸佛如來、十住菩薩眼見佛性。復有聞見，一切眾生乃至九地聞見佛性。菩薩若聞一切眾生悉有佛性，心不生信，不名聞見。善男子！若有善男子、善女人，欲見如來，應當修習十二部經，受持讀誦、書寫解說。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, as you have said — through what eye does the bodhisattva of the ten abidings, though he sees buddha-nature, fail to see it with complete clarity? And through what eye does a buddha, a world-honored one, see buddha-nature and attain complete clarity? Good son, it is through seeing with the eye of wisdom that he fails to attain complete clarity; it is through seeing with the eye of a buddha that he attains complete clarity. Because he still engages in the practice leading toward bodhi, he does not yet see it with complete clarity; only where there is no more such practice does he attain complete clarity. Because he still abides within the ten abidings, though he sees it, he does not see it clearly; only where he no longer abides anywhere at all does he attain clarity. Because the bodhisattva mahāsattva's seeing still has wisdom itself as its cause, his seeing is not yet complete; because the buddhas, the world-honored ones, have severed cause and effect entirely, their seeing is complete. To be one who is awakened to everything without exception is what is named buddha-nature — the bodhisattva of the ten abidings cannot yet be named one who is awakened to everything without exception, and it is for this very reason that, though he sees it, he does not see it with complete clarity. Good son, seeing is of two kinds: seeing with the eye, and seeing through hearing. The buddhas, the world-honored ones, see buddha-nature with the eye, exactly as one views an āmalaka fruit held in the palm of one's own hand. The bodhisattva of the ten abidings sees buddha-nature only through hearing, and so does not see it with complete clarity — the bodhisattva of the ten abidings is able only to know, of himself, that he is certain to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, but is not able to know that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature. Good son, there is, further, seeing with the eye: the tathāgatas and the bodhisattva of the ten abidings see buddha-nature with the eye. There is, further, seeing through hearing: every being, up through the bodhisattva of the ninth ground, sees buddha-nature through hearing. Should a bodhisattva hear that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature, and his mind give rise to no faith in it at all, this cannot be named seeing through hearing. Good son, should any good son or good daughter wish to see the Tathāgata, he ought to cultivate the twelve divisions of the scriptures, upholding, reciting, copying out, and expounding them."

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar Bodhisattva Mahāsattva said: "World-Honored One, since no being is able to know the Tathāgata's own state of mind, how, then, should one observe him so as to come to know him?" "Good son, in truth, no being is able to know the Tathāgata's own state of mind — yet, if one wishes to observe and come to know him, there are two causes and conditions: seeing with the eye, and seeing through hearing. If one sees all of the Tathāgata's actions of body, one should know that this is indeed the Tathāgata — this is named seeing with the eye; if one observes all of the Tathāgata's actions of speech, one should know that this is indeed the Tathāgata — this is named seeing through hearing. If one sees a bodily form and countenance that no being at all can equal, one should know that this is indeed the Tathāgata — this is named seeing with the eye; if one hears a voice of the most subtle and supreme excellence, unlike any voice possessed by any other being, one should know that this is indeed the Tathāgata — this is named seeing through hearing. If one observes the miraculous powers the Tathāgata performs, and asks whether they are for the sake of beings or for the sake of gain — if they are for the sake of beings, and not for the sake of gain, one should know that this is indeed the Tathāgata — this is named seeing with the eye; if one observes, when the Tathāgata uses the knowledge of others' minds to look upon beings, whether he speaks for the sake of gain or for the sake of beings — if it is for the sake of beings, and not for the sake of gain, one should know that this is indeed the Tathāgata — this is named seeing through hearing. Asking how the Tathāgata comes to take on this body, why he takes on a body, and for whose sake he takes on a body, is named seeing with the eye; observing how the Tathāgata teaches the Dharma, why he teaches the Dharma, and for whose sake he teaches the Dharma, is named seeing through hearing. When met with an evil action of body, and yet showing no anger, one should know that this is indeed the Tathāgata — this is named seeing with the eye; when met with an evil action of speech, and yet showing no resentment, one should know that this is indeed the Tathāgata — this is named seeing through hearing."

Ru-Yi Translation

"If one sees that, at the moment of the bodhisattva's birth, he took seven steps in each of the ten directions; that the great demon generals Māṇibhadra and Pūrṇabhadra held aloft banners and canopies; that immeasurable, boundless world-systems shook; that a golden light blazed forth, filling the whole of empty space; that the nāga kings Nanda and Upananda, through their supernatural power, bathed the bodhisattva's body; that the very forms of the gods came to greet and pay homage; that the seer Asita joined his palms in reverence; that, in the prime of his life, he cast off desire as one casts off spittle, unseduced by the pleasures of the world; that, having become a renunciant to cultivate the path, he delighted in solitude and stillness; that, in order to refute wrong views, he practiced six years of austerities; that toward every being he remained equal and without discrimination; that his mind remained forever in concentration, from the very first never scattered; that his marks and physical beauty adorned his body in splendor; that wherever he wandered, every mound and hollow of the ground became level; that his robes, though four inches from his body, never fell; that, when walking, he gazed straight ahead, never glancing left or right; that whatever food he ate left nothing whole uneaten; and that, wherever he sat or rose, not a single blade of grass was disturbed — and that, for the sake of taming beings, he went forth to teach the Dharma, his mind free of all pride — this is named seeing with the eye. If one hears that, having taken these seven steps, the bodhisattva proclaimed these very words: 'This body of mine, now, is my very last' — that the seer Asita, joining his palms, said: 'Great king, you should know that Prince Siddhārtha will most certainly attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, and will never remain at home to become a wheel-turning king. Why is this? Because his marks are perfectly manifest, while the marks of a wheel-turning sage-king are not perfectly manifest. Prince Siddhārtha's bodily marks blaze forth in splendor, and it is for this very reason that he must attain unsurpassed, complete awakening' — that, upon seeing old age, sickness, and death, he further said: 'Every being is deeply to be pitied, forever following after birth, old age, sickness, and death in just this way, and yet unable to observe that he forever dwells in suffering — I shall now put an end to this' — that he received the concentration of no-perception from the five-fold-clairvoyant seer Ārāḍa Kālāma, and, having accomplished it, afterward declared it inadequate — that he received the concentration of neither-perception-nor-non-perception from the seer Udraka Rāmaputra, and, having accomplished it, declared that this, too, was not nirvāṇa but still a dharma of birth-and-death — and that, after six years of austerities yielding no attainment at all, he said these very words: 'To practice austerities of this kind is empty and without any attainment at all — were this the true path, I ought to have attained something through it; because it is false, I have attained nothing at all through it — this, then, is a wrong technique, and not the true path' — this is named seeing through hearing."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The wisdom-eye/buddha-eye distinction closes this fascicle's long argument with the same causal logic that has run through it from the start: a bodhisattva's seeing "still has wisdom itself as its cause," while a buddha has "severed cause and effect entirely" — the same cause/fruit vocabulary applied earlier to the twelve links and to buddha-nature itself now applied to the very act of seeing buddha-nature, so that complete clarity turns out to mean the exhaustion of causation rather than the accumulation of a superior cause. The eye-seeing/hearing-seeing framework that follows solves a genuinely practical problem this raises: since no being can read the Tathāgata's mind directly, and since even the ten-abiding bodhisattva's own seeing of buddha-nature is admitted to fall short of full clarity, some method short of direct mind-reading is needed for recognizing a buddha at all — and the sūtra's answer is to infer interior motive (for beings' sake, or for gain?) from an assembled pattern of exterior conduct across an entire lifetime, rather than from any single decisive sign.

The long paired catalogue that follows is itself a demonstration of the method it describes: the physically witnessed marvels of the Buddha's own biography (the birth wonders, the six years of austerity, the undisturbed grass beneath his seat) supply the eye-seeing evidence, while the reported declarations threaded through the same biography (Asita's prophecy, the rejection of his two teachers' formless attainments, the verdict on the six years of futile austerity) supply the hearing-seeing evidence — so that a reader assembling this very portrait from the sūtra's own text is, in effect, performing the second kind of seeing right now, in the act of reading. The catalogue's closing turn, redefining "middle way" through a run of paired negations describing not metaphysical claims but the Buddha's own manner of teaching — never calling himself a teacher, never teaching for gain yet never failing to bear fruit, speech that is always timely and never wasted — extends the fascicle's earlier metaphysical identification of buddha-nature with the middle way into an ethical register: knowing the Tathāgata, in the end, means recognizing this particular style of speech and conduct wherever it appears.
