The Six Teachers' Long Defeat

Slandered before King Prasenajit as a fraud born of no father or mother, the Buddha answers not with argument but with fifteen days of wonders — and the six rival teachers flee from city to city, each flight ending the same way: a courtesan converted, a heedless youth reformed, a corpse in a charnel ground triggering awakening, a poisoned mango failing to stop a miraculous birth, until they arrive, defeated and humiliated, at Kuśinagara itself to make one final accusation — that the Buddha is nothing but an illusionist. What follows is the sūtra's own most direct engagement with rival theories of a fixed self, before the fascicle turns to why the Buddha chose the twin sāla trees, the second month, and the fifteenth day of the month for his final teaching, which of his own disciples could be said to adorn those trees, and why nothing in any of the categories language offers can be said to describe where — or whether — the Tathāgata truly "abides" at all.

The Six Teachers' Challenge

Chinese Source

「◎時諸六師心生嫉妬，悉共集詣波斯匿王，作如是言：『大王當知，王之土境，清夷閑靜，真是出家住止之處，是故我等為斯事故而來至此。大王以法正治，為民除患。沙門瞿曇，年既幼稚，學日又淺，道術無施；此國先有耆舊宿德，自怙王種，不生恭敬。若是王種，法應治民，如其出家，應敬宿德。大王善聽，沙門瞿曇，真實不生王種之中，瞿曇沙門若有父母，何由劫奪他之父母。大王！我經中說，過千歲已，有一妖祥幻化物出，所謂沙門瞿曇是也。是故當知，沙門瞿曇無父無母，若有父母，云何說言諸法無常、苦、空、無我、無作、無受。以幻術故誑惑眾生，愚者信受智者捨之。大王！夫人王者，天下父母，如秤、如地、如風、如火、如道、如河、如橋、如燈、如日、如月，如法斷事，不擇怨親。沙門瞿曇不聽我活，隨我去處，追逐不捨。惟願大王，聽我等輩，與彼瞿曇，較其道力。若彼勝我，我當屬彼；我若勝彼，彼當屬我。』王言：『大德！汝等各各自有行法，止住之處亦各不同。我今定知，如來世尊於汝無妨。』六師答言：『云何無妨？沙門瞿曇以幻術法，誘誑諸人及婆羅門，歸伏已盡。王若聽我與較道力，王之善名流布八方；如其不者，惡聲盈路。』王言：『大德！汝以未知如來道力威神巍巍，故求較試，若定知者，恐不能也。』『大王！汝今已受瞿曇幻耶？唯願大王，留神聽察，莫輕我等。搆之虛言，不如驗之以實。』王言：『善哉，善哉。』六師之徒歡喜而出。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"At that time, the six teachers, their minds filled with envy, gathered together and went before King Prasenajit, saying: 'Great king, know that your own realm is pure, level, and tranquil — truly a fitting place for renunciants to dwell — and it is for exactly this reason that we have come here. Great king, you govern rightly by the Dharma, removing every affliction from your people. This śramaṇa Gautama, still young in years and shallow in learning, has no true skill in the path at all — and yet, though this kingdom already has its own venerable elders of long standing, he shows them no reverence at all, relying instead on his own royal lineage. Were he truly of royal lineage, the Dharma would have him govern the people; and, being instead a renunciant, he ought to show reverence to his elders. Great king, listen well: this śramaṇa Gautama, in truth, was never born of any royal lineage at all — for, had Gautama truly had a father and mother, by what right would he go about seizing others' own fathers and mothers from them? Great king, our own scriptures teach that, after a thousand years have passed, some strange and ominous illusory creature shall appear — and this śramaṇa Gautama is none other than that very creature. One should therefore know that this śramaṇa Gautama has no father and no mother at all — for, had he truly had a father and mother, how could he teach that every dharma is impermanent, suffering, empty, without self, without action, and without any experiencer at all? It is through illusory arts that he deceives and beguiles beings — the foolish believe and accept him, while the wise cast him aside. Great king, a ruler of men is father and mother to the whole world, like a scale, like the earth, like the wind, like fire, like a road, like a river, like a bridge, like a lamp, like the sun, like the moon — settling every matter according to the Dharma, showing no preference between friend and foe. This śramaṇa Gautama does not allow us to live in peace, pursuing us relentlessly wherever we go, never once releasing us. We ask only that the great king permit us to measure our own power in the path against this very Gautama — should he defeat us, we shall submit to him; and should we defeat him, he shall submit to us.' The king said: 'Venerable ones, each of you already has your own practice, and your own separate dwelling places besides. I know for certain that the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, poses no obstruction to you at all.' The six teachers answered: 'How could he pose no obstruction? This śramaṇa Gautama, through illusory arts, seduces and deceives every person and every brahmin, until all have submitted to him entirely. Should the great king permit us to measure our power in the path against him, the great king's own good name shall spread throughout the eight directions; and, should he not permit it, an evil reputation shall fill every road instead.' The king said: 'Venerable ones, it is only because you do not yet know the majestic, towering power of the Tathāgata's own path that you seek this very contest — were you to know it for certain, I fear you would not be able to withstand it at all.' 'Great king, have you, too, already fallen under Gautama's own illusion? We ask only that the great king retain his own discernment and listen with care, and not make light of us. Idle words settle nothing — better to put the matter to the actual test.' The king said: 'Excellent, excellent.' The followers of the six teachers then departed, rejoicing."

Ru-Yi Translation

"At that time, King Prasenajit at once commanded his own carriage to be prepared and came to where I was, bowing his head to the ground in reverence, circling me three times to the right, and, withdrawing to sit to one side, said to me: 'World-Honored One, the six teachers have just now sought to measure their own power in the path against you — without properly weighing the matter, I have already dared to grant them this.' The Buddha said: 'Great king, excellent, excellent! You need only, further, build monasteries throughout every part of this kingdom — why is this? Should I measure my own supernatural power against theirs, many among their own followers shall be converted and transformed, and this present place is far too small and narrow to accommodate them all.' Good son, I, at that time, for the sake of these six teachers, from the very first day all the way to the fifteenth day, manifested great and rare supernatural transformations. At that very time, immeasurable beings gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening; immeasurable beings gave rise to unwavering faith in the three jewels; the followers of the six teachers, numbering beyond count, had their own wrong views destroyed, and became renunciants within the true Dharma; immeasurable beings attained a mind of non-retrogression with respect to bodhi; immeasurable beings attained the dhāraṇīs and every gate of samādhi; and immeasurable beings attained the fruit of stream-entry, all the way up to the fruit of arhatship."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The six teachers' slander is carefully constructed to attack on two fronts at once: a social charge (Gautama shows no reverence to elders, and improperly claims royal lineage) and a metaphysical charge (he must have no real parents at all, since a truly born, ordinary person could never teach that everything is impermanent and without self) — the second charge cleverly weaponizing the Buddha's own core teaching against him, as if consistency required him to deny his own biography. The king's own initial reluctance ("I know the Tathāgata poses no obstruction to you") and his eventual capitulation to public pressure ("your good name shall spread... or an evil reputation shall fill every road") stages a recognizable political dynamic — a ruler pressured into legitimizing a contest he suspects is pointless — before the Buddha's own response quietly refuses to treat it as a contest of raw power at all: rather than a duel, fifteen days of wonders become an occasion for conversion, and the chapter's own scorecard (immeasurable beings gaining faith, non-retrogression, the dhāraṇīs, and every fruit from stream-entry to arhatship) reframes what looked like a test of strength as, in substance, a teaching.

The Mother in Heaven, and the Thirteen Fruits of Heedlessness

Chinese Source

「爾時六師內心慚愧，相與圍繞至婆枳多城，教彼人民信受邪法，瞿曇沙門但說空事。善男子！我時為母，處忉利天波利質多樹安居說法。是時六師心大歡喜唱言：『善哉！瞿曇幻術今已滅沒。』復教無量無數眾生增長邪見。爾時頻婆娑羅王波斯匿王，及四部眾，白目連言：『大德！此閻浮提邪見增長，眾生可愍，行大黑闇。惟願大德，至彼天上，稽首世尊，如我言曰：「譬如犢子，其生未久，若不得乳，必死無疑。我等眾生亦復如是，惟願如來，哀愍眾生，還來住此。」』爾時目連默然而許，如大力士屈伸臂頃，往彼天上，至世尊所，白佛言：『閻浮提中所有四眾渴仰如來，思見聞法。頻婆娑羅王波斯匿王及四眾等，稽首足下。此閻浮提所有眾生邪見增長，行大黑闇，甚可憐愍。譬如犢子其生未久，若不得乳必死不疑。我等亦爾。惟願如來，為眾生故，還來在此閻浮提中。』佛告目連：『汝今速還至閻浮提，告諸國王及四部眾，却後七日我當還下。為六師故，復當至彼婆枳多城。』過七日已，我與釋天、梵天、魔天無量天子，及首陀會一切天人，前後圍繞至婆枳多城，大師子吼，作如是言：『惟我法中，獨有沙門及婆羅門，一切諸法無常無我、涅槃寂靜、離諸過惡。若言他法亦有沙門及婆羅門，有常有我有涅槃者，無有是處。』爾時無量無邊眾生發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。是時六師各相謂言：『若我法中實無沙門婆羅門者，云何而得世間供養？』於是六師，復相集聚詣毘舍離。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"At that time, the six teachers, their hearts filled with shame, gathered together and went to the city of Sāketa, teaching its people to believe and accept wrong teachings, saying that this śramaṇa Gautama taught nothing but empty words. Good son, I, at that time, was dwelling in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, beneath the pārijāta tree, teaching the Dharma there in retreat for my own mother's sake. At that time, the six teachers, their hearts filled with great joy, proclaimed: 'Excellent! Gautama's own illusory arts have now vanished entirely' — and went on to teach immeasurable, countless beings to increase their own wrong views. At that time, King Bimbisāra, King Prasenajit, and the four assemblies said to Maudgalyāyana: 'Venerable one, wrong views are increasing throughout this Jambudvīpa, and beings are truly to be pitied, walking in great darkness. We ask only that you, venerable one, go up to that very heaven, and, bowing at the World-Honored One's own feet, speak these very words on our behalf: "It is like a newborn calf, not long born — should it fail to receive its mother's milk, it is certain to die without doubt. We beings are exactly the same — we ask only that the Tathāgata, in his own pity for beings, return once more to dwell among us."' At that time, Maudgalyāyana silently consented, and, in the time it takes a strong man to bend and extend his own arm, went up to that very heaven, arrived at where the World-Honored One was, and said to the Buddha: 'Every one of the four assemblies throughout Jambudvīpa thirsts after the Tathāgata, longing to see him and hear the Dharma. King Bimbisāra, King Prasenajit, and the four assemblies bow at your very feet. Every being throughout this Jambudvīpa has wrong views increasing within them, walking in great darkness, and is truly to be pitied. It is like a newborn calf, not long born — should it fail to receive milk, it is certain to die without doubt. We, too, are exactly the same. We ask only that the Tathāgata, for the sake of beings, return once more to dwell here in this Jambudvīpa.' The Buddha said to Maudgalyāyana: 'Return now swiftly to Jambudvīpa, and tell every king and the four assemblies that, after seven days have passed, I shall descend once more. It is for the sake of these six teachers that I shall, further, go to that very city of Sāketa.' After seven days had passed, I, together with Indra, Brahmā, the deity of the Māra heaven, and immeasurable sons of the gods, along with every deity and human of the Śuddhāvāsa heavens, surrounded before and behind, arrived at the city of Sāketa, and there gave forth this very great lion's roar: 'Only within my own Dharma are there true śramaṇas and brahmins at all — every dharma is impermanent and without self, nirvāṇa is tranquil and still, and free of every fault and evil. Should it be said that, within any other teaching, there are, likewise, true śramaṇas and brahmins, holding a permanent self and possessing nirvāṇa, this could never be so.' At that time, immeasurable, boundless beings gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening. At this, the six teachers said among themselves: 'If, within our own teaching, there are truly no genuine śramaṇas or brahmins at all, how, then, could we possibly continue receiving worldly offerings?' — and the six teachers, gathering together once more, made their way to the city of Vaiśālī."

「善男子！我於一時住毘舍離菴羅林間，時菴羅女知我在中，欲來我所。我於爾時告諸比丘：『當觀念處，善修智慧，隨所修習，心莫放逸。云何名為觀於念處？若有比丘觀察內身，不見於我及以我所。觀察外身及內外身，不見於我及以我所。觀受心法亦復如是，是名念處。云何名為修習智慧？若有比丘真實而見苦集滅道，是名比丘修習智慧。云何名為心不放逸？若有比丘念佛、念法、念僧、念戒、念捨、念天，是名比丘心不放逸。』時菴羅女即至我所，頭面作禮，右繞三匝，修敬已畢，却坐一面。善男子！我於爾時為菴羅女如應說法，是女聞已，發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。時彼城中有梨車子其數五百來至我所，頭面作禮，右繞三匝，修敬已畢，却坐一面。我時復為諸梨車子如應說法：『諸善男子，夫放逸者有五事果。何等為五？一者不得自在財利、二者惡名流布於外、三者不樂慧施窮乏、四者不樂見於四眾、五者不得諸天之身。諸善男子，因不放逸，能生世法、出世間法。若有欲得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者，應當勤修不放逸法。夫放逸者，復有十三果報。何等十三？一者樂為世間作業、二者樂說無益之言、三者常樂久寢睡眠、四者樂說世間之事、五者常樂親近惡友、六者常懈怠懶惰、七者常為他人所輕、八者雖有所聞尋復忘失、九者樂處邊地、十者不能調伏諸根、十一者食不知足、十二者不樂空寂、十三者所見不正，是名十三。善男子！夫放逸者，雖得近佛及佛弟子，猶故為遠。』諸梨車子言：『我等自知是放逸人。何以故？如其我等不放逸者，如來法王當出我土。』時大會中有婆羅門子，名曰無勝，語諸梨車子：『善哉，善哉！如汝所言，頻婆娑羅王已獲大利。如來世尊出其國土，猶如大池生妙蓮花、雖生在水，水不能污。諸梨車子！佛亦如是，雖生彼國，不為世法之所滯礙。諸佛世尊無出無入，為眾生故出現於世，不為世法之所滯礙。仁等自迷耽荒五欲，不知親近往如來所，是故名為放逸之人。非佛出於摩伽陀國名放逸也。何以故？如來世尊猶彼日月，非為一人、二人出世。』時諸梨車聞是語已，尋發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，復作是言：『善哉，善哉！無勝童子！快說如是善妙之言。』時諸梨車，各各脫身所著一衣，以施無勝。無勝受已，轉以奉我，復作是言：『世尊！我從梨車得是衣物，惟願如來，哀愍眾生，受我所獻。』我於爾時，愍彼無勝，即為納受。時諸梨車同時合掌，作如是言：『惟願如來，於此土地一時安居，受我微供。』我時默然受梨車請。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, I once dwelt within a mango grove at Vaiśālī. At that time, the courtesan Āmrapālī, knowing that I was there, wished to come to where I was. I, at that time, said to the monks: 'You ought to contemplate the foundations of mindfulness, cultivate wisdom well, and, whatever you cultivate, never let the mind grow heedless. What, then, is meant by contemplating the foundations of mindfulness? Should a monk observe his own inner body, and see within it neither a self nor anything belonging to a self; and observe an outer body, and both inner and outer bodies together, and see within them, likewise, neither a self nor anything belonging to a self; and observe feeling, mind, and mental phenomena in exactly the same way — this is named the foundations of mindfulness. What, then, is meant by cultivating wisdom? Should a monk truly and genuinely see suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path — this is named a monk who cultivates wisdom. What, then, is meant by a mind free of heedlessness? Should a monk be mindful of the Buddha, mindful of the Dharma, mindful of the Saṅgha, mindful of the precepts, mindful of generosity, and mindful of the heavens — this is named a monk whose mind is free of heedlessness.' At that very time, the courtesan Āmrapālī arrived at where I was, bowed her head to the ground in reverence, circled me three times to the right, and, having paid her respects, withdrew to sit to one side. Good son, I, at that time, taught the Dharma for Āmrapālī exactly as was fitting, and this woman, upon hearing it, gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening. At that time, within that city, there were five hundred young Licchavis who came to where I was, bowed their heads to the ground in reverence, circled me three times to the right, and, having paid their respects, withdrew to sit to one side. I then, further, taught the Dharma for these young Licchavis, exactly as was fitting: 'Good sons, heedlessness bears five kinds of fruit. What are these five? First, one fails to gain mastery over one's own wealth and profit; second, an evil reputation spreads abroad; third, one takes no delight in generously giving to the poor and needy; fourth, one takes no delight in meeting with the four assemblies; and fifth, one fails to attain a heavenly body. Good sons, freedom from heedlessness is able to give rise to both worldly and transcendent dharmas alike. Should anyone wish to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, he ought diligently to cultivate the dharma of freedom from heedlessness. Heedlessness, further, bears thirteen kinds of consequence. What are these thirteen? First, delighting in worldly business; second, delighting in speaking useless words; third, forever delighting in prolonged sleep and slumber; fourth, delighting in speaking of worldly affairs; fifth, forever delighting in keeping close company with evil friends; sixth, forever indolent and lazy; seventh, forever held in contempt by others; eighth, forgetting again whatever one has heard, no sooner than one has heard it; ninth, delighting in dwelling in remote places; tenth, being unable to tame one's own sense-faculties; eleventh, never knowing contentment in eating; twelfth, taking no delight in emptiness and stillness; and thirteenth, holding wrong views — these are named the thirteen. Good son, one who is heedless, though he may draw physically close to the Buddha and to the Buddha's own disciples, nevertheless remains, in truth, far from them.' The young Licchavis said: 'We ourselves already know that we are heedless people. Why is this? For, were we truly not heedless, the Tathāgata, this Dharma-king, would surely have already appeared within our own land.' At that time, within the great assembly, there was a brahmin youth named Aprameya, who said to the young Licchavis: 'Excellent, excellent! Exactly as you have said, King Bimbisāra has already gained great benefit. The Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, appearing within his own kingdom, is exactly like a wondrous lotus flower born within a great pond — though it grows within the water, the water is never able to stain it. Young Licchavis, the Buddha is exactly the same: though he is born within that kingdom, he is never obstructed at all by any worldly dharma. Every buddha, every world-honored one, neither truly departs nor truly arrives — he appears in the world only for the sake of beings, and is never obstructed at all by any worldly dharma. It is you yourselves who, lost and indulging in the five desires, fail to know how to draw close and go to where the Tathāgata is — it is for this very reason that you are named heedless people. It is not the Buddha's appearing within the kingdom of Magadha that is named heedlessness. Why is this? The Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, is exactly like the sun and moon, appearing in the world not merely for the sake of one person, or two.' Upon hearing this, the young Licchavis at once gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening, and further said: 'Excellent, excellent, young Aprameya! You have spoken words as fine and wondrous as these!' The young Licchavis then, each removing a single garment from his own body, gave it as an offering to Aprameya. Aprameya, having received these garments, in turn offered them to me, further saying: 'World-Honored One, I have received these very garments from the Licchavis — I ask only that the Tathāgata, in his own pity for beings, accept this offering of mine.' I, at that time, out of compassion for Aprameya, accepted them. The Licchavis then, joining their palms together at the very same moment, said: 'We ask only that the Tathāgata dwell for a single retreat within this very land, and accept our own humble offerings.' I, at that time, silently accepted the Licchavis' own invitation."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The calf-and-milk plea sent up to the Trāyastriṃśa heaven gives the six teachers' premature victory lap its dramatic irony: their claim that "Gautama's illusion has vanished" depends entirely on reading his temporary absence as permanent defeat, exactly the same error King Prasenajit warned against — mistaking silence or absence for weakness rather than for a teacher's own freely chosen priorities (here, tending to his own mother). The Buddha's declaration at Sāketa — that only his own Dharma contains true śramaṇas and brahmins at all — is deliberately provocative in a way this fascicle's earlier arguments were not, functioning less as a philosophical claim to be defended step by step than as a direct challenge staged for the six teachers' own followers to judge for themselves, which is exactly what drives them to flee to Vaiśālī rather than to argue back.

The Vaiśālī episode pairs two very different kinds of conversion in immediate succession: Āmrapālī's own approach is met first with a teaching to the monks about mindfulness and heedlessness, before she ever arrives, suggesting the teaching itself matters more than any special accommodation for her famous profession — and the young Licchavis' extended catalogue of heedlessness (five fruits, then thirteen further consequences) is answered not directly by the Buddha but by one of their own peers, Aprameya, whose lotus-in-the-pond simile makes the point that a buddha's appearance in one particular kingdom was never itself a form of favoritism the Licchavis' own kingdom had somehow been denied — the real heedlessness was always theirs, in failing to seek him out, precisely because a buddha's arising, like the sun, is for no one person or place exclusively.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ratnakīrti's spontaneous skeleton-vision, triggered by nothing more than the Buddha's own arrival nearby, offers this collection's most vivid single image of what several earlier fascicles have argued abstractly — that seeing impermanence correctly is less a doctrine memorized than a genuine perceptual shift, here dramatized as literally seeing through beautiful bodies to the bones beneath, terrifying precisely because it is involuntary rather than reasoned toward.

The Campā story is built as a chain of escalating vindications, each answer to a challenge becoming the setup for a harder one: the friend's own argument for the Buddha's reliability rests entirely on precedent (naming disciples the six teachers' own followers would recognize as credible witnesses) rather than any independent proof; the Buddha's prophecy about the child's sex is then tested by outright murder when the six teachers poison the mother rather than let the prediction stand; and the six teachers' own logic — "if this is illusion, perform it yourselves" — is turned back on them by the elder himself once the fire fails to consume the newborn child. Jīvaka's own willingness to enter the flames, contrasted with the lay disciples' anxious reluctance even to approach the charnel ground, distinguishes two very different responses to a teaching whose logic exceeds ordinary expectation: hesitant, embarrassed accommodation on one side, and total confidence acted upon literally on the other. The child's own name — Jyotiṣka, "born of fire" — closes the episode by making the miracle itself into a permanent, spoken reminder, exactly as Aṅgulimāla's own name (invoked earlier in the same passage as one the Buddha tamed) already carries its own history within it.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the six teachers' final accusation as their most sophisticated attack yet, precisely because it takes the Buddha's own most distinctive teaching — that he manifests different bodies and teaches different, seemingly contradictory doctrines according to what beings need to hear (impermanence to some, permanence to others; self to some, no-self to others) — and recasts this very skillful-means flexibility as proof of fraudulence rather than of compassion. The charge only lands, on this reading, if adaptability and consistency are assumed to be incompatible; the sūtra's own answer, given directly afterward, is to define the lion's roar not as a claim requiring a single fixed content in every context, but as whatever consistently exposes the difference between conditioned, saṃsāric dharmas (always impermanent, suffering, selfless, impure) and the Buddha's own unconditioned nature (permanent, blissful, self, and pure) — a distinction that remains completely stable beneath every skillful variation of language and form.

Huiyuan's structural note on the phrase "a lion's roar requires a gathering of the wise" is worth preserving: he reads this as the sūtra's own explicit correction of a natural misunderstanding — that a lion's roar is impressive merely for being loud or bold — replacing it with a relational definition: a roar becomes a lion's roar only in front of an audience capable of recognizing what is actually at stake in it, which is why summoning bodhisattvas from the ten directions is not incidental staging but constitutive of the act itself, echoing this whole chapter's own name and its bodhisattva Lion's Roar, who has spent thirty fascicles' worth of pressing exactly the kind of hard questions this discerning audience is now gathered to hear answered in full.

「爾時六師復作是言：『若瞿曇有我，我亦有我。所言我者，見者名我。瞿曇！譬如有人向中見物，我亦如是。向喻於眼，見者喻我。』佛告六師：『若言見者名我，是義不然。何以故？汝所引喻，因向見者，人在一向，六根俱用。若定有我，因眼見者，何不如彼一根之中俱伺諸塵？若一根中不能一時聞見六塵，當知無我。所引向喻，雖經百年，見者因之所見無異。眼根若爾，年邁根熟亦應無異，人向異故見內見外。眼根若爾，亦應內外一時俱見。若不見者，云何有我？』六師復言：『瞿曇！若無我者，誰能見耶？』佛言：『有色有明，有心有眼，是四和合，故名為見。是中實無見者、受者。眾生顛倒，言有見者，及以受者。以是義故，一切眾生所見顛倒，諸佛菩薩所見真實。六師！若言色是我者，是亦不然。何以故？色實非我。色若是我，不應而得醜陋形貌，何故復有四姓差別，悉不一種婆羅門耶？何故屬他，不得自在？諸根缺陋，生不具足？何故不作諸天之身？而受地獄畜生餓鬼種種諸身？若不能得，隨意作者，當知必定無有我也。以無我故名為無常，無常故苦，苦故為空，空故顛倒，以顛倒故一切眾生輪轉生死，受想行識亦復如是。六師！如來世尊永斷色縛乃至識縛，是故名為常樂我淨。復次色者即是因緣，若因緣者則名無我，若無我者名為苦空。如來之身非是因緣，非因緣故則名有我，若有我者即常樂淨。』

Ru-Yi Translation

"At that time, the six teachers said, further: 'Should Gautama truly possess a self, then we, too, possess a self. What we mean by "self" is this: that which sees is named the self. Gautama, it is like a person who, facing toward something, sees an object — we mean exactly the same by "self": "facing toward" is a likeness for the eye, and "that which sees" is a likeness for the self.' The Buddha said to the six teachers: 'To say that "that which sees" is named the self is, I think, not correct. Why is this? In the very simile you have drawn upon — a person facing toward an object and seeing it — a person faces in but a single direction at any one time, while all six of his own sense-faculties are, nonetheless, functioning together at once. If a self truly exists, and it is through the eye that it sees, why, then, is it not also, at that very same moment, attending to every other sense-object through every other single faculty as well? If a single sense-faculty is unable to hear and see all six kinds of sense-object at one and the same time, one should therefore know that there is no self at all. And, in the very simile of "facing toward" that you have drawn upon, though a hundred years may pass, what the one who sees perceives, by means of this very faculty, remains unchanged throughout. If the eye-faculty were truly like this, then, even as one grows old and this faculty matures and ages, it, too, ought to remain unchanged — and yet, because a person turns to face in different directions, he sees, at one time, what lies within, and, at another, what lies without. If the eye-faculty were truly a fixed self, it ought to see both what lies within and what lies without at one and the same moment. If it is unable to see them both at once, how, then, could there possibly be a self at all?' The six teachers said, further: 'Gautama, if there is truly no self at all, who, then, is it that is able to see?' The Buddha said: 'There is form, there is light, there is mind, and there is the eye — it is through the coming together of these four conditions that seeing is named. Within this process, there is, in truth, no one at all who sees, and no one at all who experiences. It is only through their own inverted perception that beings speak of a seer and an experiencer at all. It is for this very reason that every being's own seeing is inverted, while every buddha's and bodhisattva's own seeing is genuinely true. Six teachers, should you say that form itself is the self, this, too, is not correct. Why is this? Form, in truth, is not the self at all. Were form truly the self, one ought never to obtain an ugly or unpleasant form at all — why, then, are there the differences among the four castes, with not even every single brahmin sharing one and the same form? Why does one's own form remain subject to others, never fully within one's own control? Why are the sense-faculties sometimes deficient, or one born without them fully complete? Why does one fail to take on the very body of a god at will, and instead receive one body after another among the hells, the animals, and the hungry ghosts? If one is unable to produce whatever form he wishes at will, one should know for certain that there is, in truth, no self at all. Because there is no self, this is named impermanence; because it is impermanent, it is suffering; because it is suffering, it is empty; and because it is empty, it is subject to inversion — and it is on account of this very inversion that every being turns endlessly through birth-and-death; and feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness are exactly the same. Six teachers, the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, has forever severed the bondage of form, all the way through to the bondage of consciousness — it is for this very reason that he is named permanence, bliss, self, and purity. Furthermore, form is itself a product of causes and conditions — and, being a product of causes and conditions, it is thereby named without self; and, being without self, it is named suffering and emptiness. The Tathāgata's own body is not a product of causes and conditions at all — and, not being a product of causes and conditions, it is thereby named possessing a self; and, possessing a self, it is thereby permanence, bliss, and purity.'"

"The six teachers said, further: 'Gautama, form, too, is not the self, and consciousness, too, is not the self. The self pervades every place, exactly like empty space.' The Buddha said: 'If it truly, pervasively exists everywhere, one ought never to say that it was, at first, unseen at all. And, if it truly was, at first, unseen, one should know that this very seeing was originally absent and is now present — and, if it is originally absent and now present, this is named impermanence. And, if it is impermanent, how, then, can it be called all-pervading at all? And, if it truly pervades everywhere, then, within every one of the five destinies, there ought to be a body fully endowed with it — and, should there be such a body, each ought to receive its own karmic fruit accordingly — and, if each receives its own karmic fruit accordingly, how, then, can it be said that one turns from one destiny to another, becoming, in turn, a human or a god? You speak of this self as "pervading everywhere" — but is it one single thing, or many? If it is a single thing, there could then be no distinction at all between father and son, or between friend and foe; and, if it is many things, then every being's own five sense-faculties ought to be entirely equal to one another, and his own karma and wisdom, too, ought to be exactly the same. And, if this is so, how, then, can it be said that some faculties are complete while others are deficient, or that karma is wholesome in one case and unwholesome in another, or that some are wise and others foolish?' 'Gautama, a being's own self is without any boundary or limit at all. It is dharma and non-dharma that possess their own distinct measure. A being who cultivates dharma thereby attains a fine body, while one who practices non-dharma thereby attains a poor one — it is for this very reason that a being's own karmic fruit cannot fail to differ from one being to the next.' The Buddha said: 'Good son, if dharma and non-dharma are truly like this, then the self cannot be all-pervading at all. And, were the self truly all-pervading, it ought to reach everywhere at once — and, were it to reach everywhere at once, then one who cultivates what is wholesome ought also to possess what is unwholesome, and one who practices what is unwholesome ought also to possess what is wholesome. If this is not so, how, then, can it be called all-pervading at all?' A being's own self is exactly the same: the cultivation of good and the practice of evil never mix or blend together at all.' A lamp's own light arises only in dependence on conditions — as the lamp itself grows, its light, too, grows correspondingly. A being's own self is not like this at all. Furthermore, a lamp's own light emerges from the lamp, and dwells in a place separate from it; a being's own self cannot be like this either, emerging from the body and dwelling in some separate place. A lamp's own light, further, coexists together with darkness — why is this? It is like a single lamp lit within a dark room: its illumination is not yet complete, and it is only once many lamps are lit together that full illumination is achieved. If the very first lamp already dispelled the darkness entirely, there would be no need at all for any further lamp — and, since a further lamp is indeed needed, one should know that the first lamp's own light coexists together with darkness after all.' "The Buddha said: 'If the self is itself the one who acts, how, then, could it be called permanent at all? And, should you say that it performs good and evil at different times, how, then, could it also be said that the self is without any boundary at all? Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this debate as proceeding through the six teachers' own three successive fallback positions on the self, each refuted before the next is proposed. Against "the seer is the self" (illustrated by the eye "facing toward" an object), the Buddha's refutation runs on three points: the six senses operate simultaneously while a person faces only one direction, so a self fixed to a single sense-organ could not explain multi-sense awareness; a lifetime of stable seeing through an aging, changing organ would require the organ itself to remain unchanged, which it plainly does not; and a fixed self ought to see inside and outside at once rather than one at a time. Against "form is the self," five separate reasons are given in sequence — ugly and unpleasant forms would be impossible, the four-caste hierarchy could not produce such variety from one self, the body remains subject to conditions outside its own control, birth defects would be inexplicable, and no one is able to choose a divine body simply by willing it — collectively showing that form lacks the freedom a genuine self would need.

Against "the self pervades everywhere like space," Huiyuan tracks three separate counter-arguments: the seeing-argument (an all-pervading self, being always present, could never have been "at first unseen," yet it plainly was, which marks it as something newly arisen rather than eternal); the five-destinies argument (a truly pervasive self ought to inhabit every realm's body simultaneously and receive every realm's karmic fruit at once, which would make rebirth from one destiny to another unintelligible); and the one-or-many argument (a single universal self would erase every distinction between individuals, while many pervasive selves would erase every difference in ability, fortune, and wisdom between beings) — met by the teachers' own two rescue attempts (a separate "measure" belonging to dharma and non-dharma, and the many-lamps-in-one-room simile), both dismantled in turn: the first concedes that the self isn't truly pervasive after all if good and evil results stay properly sorted, and the second fails because an ordinary lamp's light is itself conditioned, spatially separate from its source, and never fully dispels the very darkness it coexists with — none of which a proponent of a fixed, pervading self can actually want to grant. The self-as-agent challenge is met with a final fourfold argument: a genuine agent could not also be permanent (since acting implies change over time), could not be spoken of as bounded to specific acts if truly boundless, could not remain a mystery to itself if it were both agent and knower, and so — the sūtra's own striking final move — the word "self," rightly used, refers to none of these positions at all, but only to the Tathāgata himself, whose freedom from action, birth, affliction, and every fixed mark is exactly what "permanence, bliss, self, and purity" was always meant to name.

Why the Second Month, Why the Fifteenth Day

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar said: "World-Honored One, why does the Tathāgata enter parinirvāṇa in the second month?" "Good son, the second month is named spring — in this month of spring sunshine, every living thing grows and flourishes: seeds and roots are planted, flowers and fruit bloom and thrive, rivers and streams run full, and every kind of animal bears its young. At this very season, beings are most inclined to give rise to the thought of permanence — and, in order to overcome exactly this thought of permanence in beings, I teach that every dharma, without exception, is impermanent, while teaching solely that the Tathāgata abides forever, unchanging. Good son, of the six seasons, the depths of early winter bring withering and decay, which beings take no delight in at all; while the warm, gentle moisture of spring is what people crave and love. It is in order to overcome beings' own worldly delight that I proclaim permanence and bliss in exactly this season. Self and purity are exactly the same: it is in order to overcome the world's own false notion of self and purity that the Tathāgata teaches the Tathāgata's own true self and purity. To speak of 'the second month' is itself a likeness for the Tathāgata's own two kinds of Dharma-body. Not delighting in winter is a likeness for the wise taking no delight in the Tathāgata's entering an impermanent nirvāṇa; delighting in the second month is a likeness for the wise delighting in the Tathāgata's own permanence, bliss, self, and purity. The planting of seeds is a likeness for beings, upon hearing the Dharma, rejoicing and giving rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening, planting every kind of wholesome root. The rivers are a likeness for the great bodhisattvas of the ten directions coming to where I am, to inquire into and receive this very scripture of the great nirvāṇa. Every kind of animal bearing its young is a likeness for my own disciples giving rise to every kind of wholesome root. The flower is a likeness for the seven factors of awakening, and the fruit is a likeness for the four fruits of the path. It is for this very reason that I enter the great nirvāṇa in the second month."

Huiyuan reads the second-month symbolism as working through four parallel points at once: beings, seeing spring's own flourishing, are most tempted toward the thought of permanence, which the Buddha counters by teaching universal impermanence precisely in this season, reserving the teaching of his own true permanence for a different register entirely; the season itself doubles as an image for his two Dharma-bodies (the true and the responsive), with winter's unloved bleakness standing for what the wise do not delight in (an impermanent, final extinction) and spring's welcomed warmth standing for what they do delight in (the Buddha's own actual permanence, bliss, self, and purity); and every other element of the spring scene — planted seeds, rivers running full, animals bearing young, flowers and fruit — maps onto a specific element of the audience and the teaching itself, from beings newly resolving on awakening to the ten-direction bodhisattvas gathering to receive this very sūtra.

On the fifteenth day, Huiyuan reads the full moon's own freedom from waxing and waning as the entire point of the choice: every other major event in the Buddha's life (birth, renunciation, awakening, first turning of the wheel) occurred on the eighth day, a day of the moon still visibly incomplete, appropriate to events still unfolding within an ordinary life; only the parinirvāṇa, which the sūtra insists is not really an ending at all, is matched to the one day of the month when the moon is already whole and no longer changing. The eleven benefits of the full moon and the eleven parallel benefits of "the Tathāgata's own full moon," read side by side, are not incidental doubling but a deliberate correspondence built term by term (worldly darkness and the darkness of ignorance; fear of wild beasts and fear of the five hindrances; the traveler's resolve and the practitioner's own resolve to advance toward nirvāṇa), teaching the reader to find, in an entirely ordinary natural phenomenon witnessed every month, the very structure of the teaching itself. The closing image of the traveling mother, mistakenly presumed dead by children too young to understand her true situation, gives this whole discussion — otherwise built from cosmic-scale symbolism — its most intimate and human register.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this parallel structure — Buddha names a quality, Lion's Roar identifies the disciple who embodies it, six times over — as itself a kind of teaching through portraiture: rather than defining textual mastery, clairvoyance, contentment, freedom from contention, supernatural power, or wisdom in the abstract, the sūtra fixes each quality to a specific, recognizable person (Ānanda's own transparent transmission of what he heard, likened to water poured cleanly between vessels; Aniruddha's blind eyes compensated by a heavenly eye reaching every world-system and even the intermediate state; Kāśyapa's austerity; Subhūti's peaceableness; Maudgalyāyana's supernatural range), so that the reader has a living model rather than only a definition. On Śāriputra's own ninefold catalogue of wisdom, Huiyuan glosses each term individually — quickness in penetrating principle names "sharp wisdom," quickness in resolving practical matters names "swift wisdom," untroubled mastery over every obstruction names "liberating wisdom," and so on — and connects it to this sūtra's own separate declaration elsewhere that no being besides the Buddha himself could match even a sixteenth part of Śāriputra's wisdom and learning, which is exactly why Śāriputra's own equanimity toward the Buddha's teaching of impermanence and permanence alike (neither grieving nor rejoicing, since both are known to be provisional framings rather than final claims) becomes, in Huiyuan's reading, the wisdom-quality's own clearest demonstration.

The list's final, deliberately unreachable rung — Lion's Roar naming the Tathāgata himself as the only one who fully embodies teaching buddha-nature, the vajra body, and the eight masteries together — completes the pattern by making explicit what the previous six examples already implied: each named disciple adorns the sāla trees only partially, excelling in one dimension while the Buddha alone integrates all of them simultaneously, which is precisely the request Lion's Roar makes explicit in asking the Buddha to remain — a request the following section immediately takes up and, at length, declines to grant in the terms it was asked.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lion's Roar's request that the Buddha "dwell" in the sāla grove sounds like the most modest possible ask — simply stay a while longer — which is exactly why the Buddha's refusal has to work so hard to show that the request is not modest at all but self-contradictory. His method is not to give one knockdown argument but to run the same move through a long list of registers, each one showing that "dwelling" already presupposes something the Tathāgata has specifically eliminated: dwelling belongs to conditioned form, and he has severed the bondage of form; dwelling is a symptom of arrogance, and he has severed all arrogance; dwelling would make his body finite, and a finite body would be impermanent, which he is not. The most pointed reversals turn ordinary virtues into further evidence against "dwelling" rather than for it — the perfection of giving, taken as a fixed resting place, would block progress to every perfection after it, and the four foundations of mindfulness, if clung to as a final destination, would themselves prevent full awakening — the sūtra's own reminder that even wholesome practices become obstacles the moment they are treated as places to remain rather than stages to pass through.

The closing chain (no dwelling-house → non-existence → unborn → deathless → markless → unbound → unattached → undefiled → wholesome → unconditioned → great nirvāṇa → permanence → self → purity → bliss) mirrors the same technique this fascicle has already used for "cause" and "existence": rather than defining nirvāṇa positively in one step, the text walks a long chain of negations that each imply the next, arriving at the four positive qualities only at the very end, as what remains once nothing further can be taken away. The list of ten "impossible things" that follows then applies this entire discussion practically: entering samādhi is not evasion but a deliberate teaching device, addressed in its own closing catalogue of reasons to every kind of listener at once — the beginner who has not yet planted wholesome roots, the advanced practitioner ready to hear of awakening, the heedless disciple in need of reproach, and Mañjuśrī himself, worthy of genuine debate.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ten-marks catalogue completes the sūtra's own causal chain (marks → delusion → craving → bondage → rebirth → death → impermanence, and its reverse) in its most compressed form yet, tracing every step of saṃsāra back to the single root habit of clinging to marks at all — which is why the antidote is not additional doctrine but the cultivation of three specific marks (concentration, wisdom, equanimity) capable of dissolving the other ten. Lion's Roar's challenge to this — if samādhi is a mind fixed on one object, doesn't every shift to a new object break it, and if every being already has some form of one-pointed attention, why call it something to be "cultivated"? — forces a precise clarification: what must be cultivated is not attention as such but wholesome samādhi specifically, and the object-fixation worry dissolves once any object, including a changing sequence of objects, is recognized as itself "one object" relative to the attending mind at that moment.

The charioteer simile is this passage's clearest statement of what "equanimity" actually names: not a third, separate faculty alongside concentration and wisdom, but the skill of keeping the other two in the right proportion for a given practitioner — which is also the sūtra's own diagnosis of exactly why the ten-abiding bodhisattva and the śrāvaka each fail to see buddha-nature clearly, one having too much wisdom relative to concentration and the other the reverse, while only the Buddha holds both in perfect balance. The extended numerical taxonomy that follows (samādhi classified by twos, threes, all the way to tens, then declared to run to innumerable further kinds among the buddhas and bodhisattvas) functions less as an exhaustive inventory to memorize than as a demonstration that no single scheme could ever capture the whole of concentration practice — which is itself a small, structural echo of this entire fascicle's ending point: no fixed description, however elaborate, exhausts what is actually being pointed at.
