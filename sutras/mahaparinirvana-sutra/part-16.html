The Ground of an Only Child

This fascicle answers Kāśyapa's challenge that loving-kindness brings no real benefit with five miracles — a grieving mother restored to her senses, a laywoman's own flesh healing a dying monk, a poisoned prince cured from a distant city, blinded bandits given back their sight, mutilated women made whole — each worked, the Buddha insists, purely through the power of loving-kindness, without his body ever actually leaving where he sat. It then names the ground a bodhisattva reaches through this practice "the ground of an only child," and meets Kāśyapa's sharpest challenge yet: if a bodhisattva at this ground loves every being as an only child, how does this square with the sūtra's own account of a past life in which the Buddha, then a king, put brahmins to death, and of his harsh words to Devadatta in this very life? The fascicle closes with an extended teaching on eleven kinds of emptiness, culminating in the ground of empty equality Subhūti himself is said to have reached.

Five Miracles of Loving-Kindness

Chinese Source

「◎復次善男子！舍衛城中有婆羅門女，姓婆私吒，唯有一子，愛之甚重，遇病命終。爾時女人，愁毒入心，狂亂失性，裸身無恥，遊行四衢，啼哭失聲，唱言：『子子，汝何處去？』周遍城邑，無有疲已。而是女人，已於先佛殖眾德本。善男子！我於是女起慈愍心。是時女人即得見我，便生子想，還得本心，前抱我身，嗚唼我口。我時即告侍者阿難：『汝可持衣與是女人。』既與衣已，便為種種說諸法要。是女聞法，歡喜踊躍，發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。善男子！我於爾時實非彼子，彼非我母，亦無抱持。善男子！當知皆是慈善根力，令彼女人見如是事。

「復次善男子！波羅㮈城有優婆夷，字曰摩訶斯那達多，已於過去無量先佛種諸善根。是優婆夷，夏九十日，請命眾僧，奉施醫藥。是時眾中有一比丘，身嬰重病。良醫診之，當須肉藥，若得肉者，病則可除，若不得肉，命將不全。時優婆夷聞醫此言，尋持黃金，遍至市[厂@黑]，唱如是言：『誰有肉賣，吾以金買，若有肉者，當等與金。』周遍城市，求不能得。是優婆夷尋自取刀割其髀肉，切以為臛，下種種香，送病比丘。比丘服已，病即得差。是優婆夷患瘡苦惱，不能堪忍，即發聲言：『南無佛陀南無佛陀。』我於爾時在舍衛城，聞其音聲，於是女人起大慈心，是女尋見，我持良藥，塗其瘡上，還合如本。我即為其種種說法，聞法歡喜，發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。善男子！我於爾時，實不往至波羅㮈城，持藥塗是優婆夷瘡。善男子！當知皆是慈善根力，令彼女人見如是事。

「復次善男子！調達惡人，貪不知足，多服穌故，頭痛腹痛，受大苦惱，不能堪忍，發如是言：『南無佛陀南無佛陀。』我時住在優禪尼城，聞其音聲，即生慈心。爾時調達尋便見我，往至其所，手摩頭腹，授與鹽湯，而令服之，服已平復。善男子！我實不往調婆達所，摩其頭腹授湯令服。善男子！當知皆是慈善根力，令調婆達見如是事。

「復次善男子！憍薩羅國有諸群賊，其數五百，群黨抄劫，為害滋甚。波斯匿王患其縱暴，遣兵伺捕，得已挑目，遂著黑闇叢林之下。是諸群賊，已於先佛殖眾德本，既失目已，受大苦惱，各作是言：『南無佛陀南無佛陀，我等今者無有救護。』啼哭號咷。我時住在祇洹精舍，聞其音聲，即生慈心，時有涼風，吹香山中種種香藥，滿其眼眶，尋還得眼，如本不異。諸賊開眼，即見如來，住立其前而為說法。賊聞法已，發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。善男子！我於爾時實不作風，吹香山中種種香藥，住其人前而為說法。善男子！當知皆是慈善根力，令彼群賊見如是事。

「復次善男子！琉璃太子以愚癡故，廢其父王，自立為主。復念宿嫌，多害釋種，取萬二千釋種諸女，刵劓耳鼻，斷截手足，推之坑塹。時諸女人，身受苦惱，作如是言：『南無佛陀南無佛陀，我等今者無有救護。』復大號咷。是諸女人，已於先佛種諸善根，我於爾時在竹林中，聞其音聲，即起慈心。諸女爾時，見我來至迦毘羅城，以水洗瘡，以藥傅之，苦痛尋除，耳鼻手足，還復如本。我時即為略說法要，悉令俱發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，即於大愛道比丘尼所，出家受具足戒。善男子！如來爾時實不往至迦毘羅城，以水洗瘡傅藥止苦。善男子！當知皆是慈善根力，令彼女人得如是事，悲喜之心亦復如是。善男子！以是義故，菩薩摩訶薩修慈思惟，即是真實，非虛妄也。善男子！夫無量者不可思議，菩薩所行不可思議，諸佛所行亦不可思議，是大乘典大涅槃經亦不可思議。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, there was, in the city of Śrāvastī, a brahmin woman of the Vāsiṣṭha clan, who had but a single son, whom she loved most dearly, and who fell ill and died. This woman, at that time, her heart pierced with grief, became deranged and lost her own mind, wandering naked and without shame through the city's crossroads, weeping and crying out until her voice failed, calling out: 'My child, my child, where have you gone?' — roaming throughout the whole city without ever tiring. This woman, however, had already, under buddhas of the past, planted many roots of virtue. Good son, I gave rise to a mind of compassionate love toward this woman. This woman, at that moment, caught sight of me, and at once took me for her own child, her original mind restored, and came forward to embrace my body, kissing my mouth. I then told my attendant Ānanda: 'Bring some clothing for this woman.' Once the clothing had been given to her, I then expounded the essentials of the Dharma for her at length. This woman, hearing the Dharma, was filled with joy, leaping with delight, and gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening. Good son, I, at that time, was not, in reality, her son at all, nor was she my mother, nor was there any actual embrace. Good son, one should know that it was entirely through the power of loving-kindness's own wholesome root that this woman came to see things in this way.

"Furthermore, good son, there was, in the city of Vārāṇasī, a laywoman named Mahāsenadattā, who had already, under immeasurable buddhas of the past, planted every wholesome root. This laywoman, during the ninety days of the rains retreat, invited the whole assembly of monks and offered them medicine. At that time, there was, within the assembly, a monk afflicted with a grave illness. A skilled physician, examining him, determined that he required medicinal meat — should he obtain meat, his illness could be cured, but should he fail to obtain it, his life could not be preserved. When the laywoman heard the physician say this, she at once took gold in hand and went throughout every market, calling out: 'Whoever has meat to sell, I shall buy it with this gold — should anyone have meat, I shall pay its equal weight in gold.' She searched throughout the entire city, yet was unable to obtain any. This laywoman then took a knife herself and cut flesh from her own thigh, sliced it, and prepared it as a broth, seasoned with every kind of spice, and sent it to the sick monk. The monk, having taken it, was at once cured of his illness. This laywoman, suffering the pain of her own wound, unable to bear it, at once cried out: 'Homage to the Buddha! Homage to the Buddha!' I, at that time, was in the city of Śrāvastī, and, hearing her voice, gave rise, toward this woman, to a mind of great loving-kindness. This woman, at once, saw me bring good medicine and apply it to her wound, which healed at once, restored as it was before. I then expounded the Dharma for her at length, and, hearing the Dharma, she was filled with joy, and gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening. Good son, I, at that time, did not, in reality, go to the city of Vārāṇasī and apply medicine to this laywoman's wound. Good son, one should know that it was entirely through the power of loving-kindness's own wholesome root that this woman came to see things in this way.

"Furthermore, good son, the evil man Devadatta, in his craving, never satisfied, consumed a great quantity of ghee, and, suffering a severe pain in his head and stomach, unable to bear it, cried out: 'Homage to the Buddha! Homage to the Buddha!' I, at that time, was dwelling in the city of Ujjayinī, and, hearing his voice, at once gave rise to a mind of loving-kindness. Devadatta, at that moment, at once saw me come to where he was, place my hand upon his head and stomach, and give him salted water to drink, whereupon he recovered at once. Good son, I did not, in reality, go to Devadatta's own place, place my hand upon his head and stomach, and give him water to drink. Good son, one should know that it was entirely through the power of loving-kindness's own wholesome root that Devadatta came to see things in this way.

"Furthermore, good son, there were, in the kingdom of Kosala, a band of five hundred bandits, who, banding together, plundered and robbed, causing ever greater harm. King Prasenajit, troubled by their unchecked violence, sent soldiers to lie in wait and capture them, and, having captured them, gouged out their eyes, and cast them beneath a dark and gloomy forest thicket. These bandits, however, had already, under buddhas of the past, planted many roots of virtue, and, having lost their eyes, suffering great agony, each cried out: 'Homage to the Buddha! Homage to the Buddha! We have no one now to save and protect us!' — weeping and wailing aloud. I, at that time, was dwelling in the Jetavana monastery, and, hearing their voices, at once gave rise to a mind of loving-kindness. At that moment, a cool breeze arose, carrying fragrant medicinal herbs of every kind from a mountain of fragrance, filling their eye sockets, and, before long, their eyes were restored, exactly as they had been before. The bandits, opening their eyes, at once saw the Tathāgata standing before them, teaching the Dharma. The bandits, having heard the Dharma, gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening. Good son, I, at that time, did not, in reality, raise a wind to carry fragrant medicinal herbs of every kind from a mountain of fragrance, nor did I stand before these men and teach the Dharma. Good son, one should know that it was entirely through the power of loving-kindness's own wholesome root that these bandits came to see things in this way.

"Furthermore, good son, Prince Virūḍhaka, out of his own folly, deposed his father the king and set himself up as ruler in his place. Harboring, further, a long-held grudge, he did great harm to the Śākya clan, seizing twelve thousand women of the Śākyas, cutting off their ears and noses, severing their hands and feet, and casting them into a pit. These women, their bodies wracked with agony, cried out: 'Homage to the Buddha! Homage to the Buddha! We have no one now to save and protect us!' — wailing aloud once more. These women, however, had already, under buddhas of the past, planted every wholesome root, and I, at that time, dwelling within the bamboo grove, hearing their voices, at once gave rise to a mind of loving-kindness. These women, at that moment, saw me come to the city of Kapilavastu, wash their wounds with water, and apply medicine to them, and their pain and suffering was at once removed, their ears, noses, hands, and feet restored exactly as they had been before. I then expounded the essentials of the Dharma for them in brief, causing every one of them to give rise together to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening, and they thereupon went forth from the household life under the nun Mahāprajāpatī, receiving the full monastic precepts. Good son, the Tathāgata, at that time, did not, in reality, go to the city of Kapilavastu, wash their wounds with water, and apply medicine to relieve their suffering. Good son, one should know that it was entirely through the power of loving-kindness's own wholesome root that these women came to attain things in this way — and it is just the same for a mind of compassion and of sympathetic joy. Good son, it is for this very reason that a bodhisattva-mahāsattva's contemplation cultivated through loving-kindness is truly real, and not empty at all. Good son, what is immeasurable is inconceivable; what a bodhisattva practices is inconceivable; what every buddha practices, too, is inconceivable; and this scripture of the Great Vehicle, the Mahāparinirvāṇa, too, is inconceivable."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads these five stories as the direct answer to the challenge that closed Part 15: if wishing beings well through loving-kindness doesn't visibly deliver happiness, why call the contemplation real? Here, unlike the abstract argument given there, the sūtra answers with events an audience could in principle verify — the Buddha never leaves where he sits, and yet each sufferer, in a different city, perceives him arriving in person to help. He reads the pattern as illustrating "無量門" (the immeasurable gateway) directly: loving-kindness is not one power among others that happens to also work at a distance, but a gateway containing, in a single mental state, the full range of a buddha's own qualities — which is why the sūtra can call the very same capacity "real thought," "the Great Vehicle," and "bodhi" almost interchangeably, as the closing section of this fascicle's predecessor already did at length. He also notes, briefly, why only loving-kindness and compassion get illustrated with named case-histories here while sympathetic joy and equanimity are only mentioned in passing at the end ("it is just the same for a mind of compassion and of sympathetic joy"): equanimity's own effect is to make giving and receiving equal and unremarkable, which does not lend itself to a dramatic story the way rescuing someone from visible suffering does.

On the five stories individually, Huiyuan flags what each was chosen to demonstrate rather than treating them as interchangeable: the grieving mother (a real figure elsewhere identified with the arhat nun Vāsiṣṭhī) shows loving-kindness restoring sanity itself, not merely physical healing; the laywoman's self-mutilation for a dying monk's medicine shows an ordinary person's compassion, prior to any miracle, already real enough to found one — the miracle only completes what her own sacrifice began; Devadatta's healing, given to the sūtra's most notorious antagonist, shows the reach of loving-kindness extending even to declared enemies; the bandits blinded by royal punishment show it extending even to those a legitimate worldly authority has justly condemned; and the mutilated Śākya women — kinswomen of the Buddha himself, harmed by Virūḍhaka's revenge for an old insult — anchor the whole sequence back to the Buddha's own clan, closing the set with the case closest to home before the fascicle turns, in its very next section, to defining exactly this quality of universal, unconditional care as "the ground of an only child."

The Ground of an Only Child

Chinese Source

「復次善男子！菩薩摩訶薩修慈悲喜已，得住極愛一子之地。善男子！云何是地名曰極愛？復名一子？善男子！譬如父母，見子安隱，心大歡喜。菩薩摩訶薩住是地中，亦復如是，視諸眾生同於一子，見修善者生大歡喜，是故此地名曰極愛。善男子！譬如父母見子遇患，心生苦惱，愍之愁毒，初無捨離。菩薩摩訶薩住是地中亦復如是，見諸眾生，為煩惱病之所纏切，心生愁惱，憂念如子，身諸毛孔，血皆流出，是故此地，名為一子。善男子！如人小時，拾取土塊、糞穢、瓦石、枯骨、木枝，置於口中，父母見已，恐為其患，左手捉頭，右手挑出。菩薩摩訶薩住是地中，亦復如是，見諸眾生法身未增，或行身口意業不善，菩薩見已，則以智手拔之令出，不欲令彼流轉生死受諸苦惱，是故此地，復名一子。善男子！譬如父母，所愛之子捨而終亡，父母愁惱，願與併命。菩薩亦爾，見一闡提墮於地獄，亦願與俱生地獄中。何以故？是一闡提若受苦時，或生一念改悔之心，我即當為說種種法，令彼得生一念善根。是故此地，復名一子。善男子！譬如父母，唯有一子，其子睡寤、行住、坐臥，心常念之；若有罪咎，善言誘喻，不加其惡。菩薩摩訶薩亦復如是，見諸眾生若墮地獄、畜生、餓鬼，或人、天中，造作善惡，心常念之，初不放捨。若行諸惡，終不生瞋，以惡加之，是故此地，復名一子。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, having cultivated loving-kindness, compassion, and sympathetic joy, thereby comes to abide at the ground of utmost love, as an only child. Good son, why is this ground named 'utmost love,' and, further, named 'an only child'? Good son, it is like a parent who, seeing a child safe and at peace, is filled with great joy in his own heart. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding at this ground, is just the same: regarding every being as though he were his own only child, and, seeing one who cultivates wholesome conduct, being filled with great joy — it is for this very reason that this ground is named 'utmost love.' Good son, it is like a parent who, seeing a child fall ill, is filled with pain and distress in his own heart, grieving over him with anguish, and never once abandoning him. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding at this ground, is just the same: seeing every being bound and afflicted by the sickness of their own affliction, his heart is filled with sorrow and distress, grieving over them as though they were his own children, blood flowing forth from every pore of his own body — it is for this very reason that this ground is named 'an only child.' Good son, it is as when a small child picks up a clod of earth, some filth, a shard of tile, a dried bone, or a piece of wood, and puts it into his own mouth, and his parent, seeing this, fearing it will harm him, grasps his head with the left hand and pries it out with the right. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding at this ground, is just the same: seeing that beings have not yet increased their own Dharma-body, or that they engage in unwholesome conduct of body, speech, or mind, the bodhisattva, upon seeing this, uses the hand of wisdom to draw it out of them, not wishing them to wander on through birth-and-death, undergoing every kind of suffering — it is for this very reason that this ground is, further, named 'an only child.' Good son, it is like a parent whose beloved child is abandoned and comes to die, and the parent, grieving and distressed, wishes to perish together with him. The bodhisattva is just the same: seeing the icchantika fall into hell, he, too, wishes to be reborn together with him within that very hell. Why is this? Because, should this icchantika, at the moment of undergoing suffering, give rise to even a single thought of repentance and change, the bodhisattva would then at once teach him the Dharma in every way, causing him to give rise to even a single thought of a wholesome root. It is for this very reason that this ground is, further, named 'an only child.' Good son, it is like a parent who has but a single child, and whose mind is forever fixed upon that child, whether he sleeps or wakes, walks, stands, sits, or lies down; and, should the child commit some fault, the parent guides and instructs him with kind words, never inflicting any harm upon him. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva is just the same: seeing every being, whether fallen into hell, among the animals, among the hungry ghosts, or among humans or gods, engaged in wholesome or unwholesome conduct, his mind is forever fixed upon them, never once abandoning or forsaking them; and, should they engage in every kind of evil, he never once gives rise to anger, nor ever repays their evil with evil — it is for this very reason that this ground is, further, named 'an only child.'"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads these five similes as splitting naturally into two groups by what kind of parental feeling they illustrate: the first three describe compassion felt on beholding a being's condition — joy at a child's wellbeing (explaining "utmost love"), grief at a child's illness, and the impulse to remove something harmful from a child's own mouth (both grouped under "an only child," since both spring from the same protective anguish) — while the fourth and fifth describe compassion sustained across time regardless of what is beheld: a parent's willingness to die alongside a doomed child, and a parent's unbroken attention to a child through every ordinary moment, sleeping or waking, at fault or not. He reads the fourth simile — the bodhisattva wishing to be reborn in hell alongside the icchantika — as the sharpest test case in the whole set, precisely because it names the one being this same fascicle's Part 5 discussion called "incurable": the wish to accompany him there is not a claim that the bodhisattva can force his liberation, only that no circumstance, including the icchantika's own hell, is severe enough to make the bodhisattva stop wishing for the single thought of repentance that would change everything, should it ever arise. This ground, on his reading, is explicitly identified with the first bodhisattva ground — the same ground Part 15 named as the threshold where compassion first becomes properly "great" — and this fivefold analysis of "only child" love is this fascicle's own elaboration of what that first ground's compassion actually consists in, moment by moment, rather than a separate teaching.

Did the Buddha Ever Truly Kill?

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what the Buddha has taught is secret in its meaning, and my own wisdom, being shallow, cannot fully understand it. If a bodhisattva, abiding at the ground of an only child, is able to be as the Buddha has described, then how is it that the Tathāgata, in ages past, as a king practicing the bodhisattva path, put an end to the lives of so many brahmins? If he had attained this ground at that time, he should have guarded and cherished them; and if he had not attained it, then for what reason did he not fall into hell? And if he truly regards every being with equal love, as his own only child, as he regards Rāhula himself, then why does he say to Devadatta: 'You foolish, shameless man, eater of another's spittle,' causing him, upon hearing this, to give rise to hatred and anger, and to give rise to an unwholesome mind, drawing blood from the Buddha's own body? And, once Devadatta had committed this evil deed, the Tathāgata further predicted that he would fall into hell and undergo suffering there for a full kalpa. World-Honored One, how can words such as these not contradict one another in meaning? World-Honored One, Subhūti, abiding at the ground of emptiness, whenever he wished to enter a city to beg for food, would first observe the people there, and, should any one of them harbor resentment or jealousy toward him, would stop and not go to him at all, even to the point of enduring extreme hunger rather than begging from him. Why is this? Because this Subhūti constantly thought to himself: 'I recall that, in ages past, I gave rise to a single unwholesome thought toward a field of merit, and, through this very cause and condition, fell into the great hell, undergoing suffering of every kind. I would now rather go hungry, fasting the whole day through, than ever cause another to give rise to resentment toward me, and thereby fall into hell to undergo suffering.' He thought further: 'Should any being resent my standing, I shall remain seated the whole day through, without rising at all. Should any being resent my sitting, I shall remain standing the whole day through, without moving from my place at all' — and likewise for walking and lying down. This Subhūti, for the sake of protecting beings, gave rise to a mind such as this — how much more so, then, should a bodhisattva? If a bodhisattva has truly attained the ground of an only child, for what reason did the Tathāgata utter such coarse words, causing beings to give rise to a mind of grave hatred?"

"Good son, you should not now raise an objection such as this, saying that the Tathāgata acts as a cause and condition of affliction for beings. Good son, even should a mosquito's proboscis be able to drain the ocean to its very bottom, the Tathāgata would never, in the end, act as a cause and condition of affliction for beings. Good son, even should the whole great earth become entirely formless, water take on the mark of solidity, fire take on the mark of coldness, wind take on the mark of fixed stillness, and the Three Jewels, buddha-nature, and empty space itself take on the mark of impermanence, the Tathāgata would never, in the end, act as a cause and condition of affliction for beings. Good son, even should one who has transgressed one of the four gravest offenses, or an icchantika, or one who slanders the true Dharma, attain, in this very body, the ten powers, the fearlessnesses, the thirty-two marks, and the eighty subsidiary signs, the Tathāgata would never, in the end, act as a cause and condition of affliction for beings. Good son, even should the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha abide forever, unchanging, the Tathāgata would never, in the end, act as a cause and condition of affliction for beings. Good son, even should every bodhisattva of the ten abiding-stages transgress one of the four gravest offenses, become an icchantika, and slander the true Dharma, the Tathāgata would never, in the end, act as a cause and condition of affliction for beings. Good son, even should every immeasurable being lose and destroy their own buddha-nature, and the Tathāgata enter his own final parinirvāṇa in the ultimate sense, the Tathāgata would never, in the end, act as a cause and condition of affliction for beings. Good son, even should a thrown noose be able to bind the wind itself, teeth be able to shatter iron, and fingernails be able to destroy Mount Sumeru, the Tathāgata would never, in the end, act as a cause and condition of affliction for beings. I would rather dwell in the same place as a venomous serpent, thrust both my hands into the mouth of a starving lion, or bathe my own body in the burning coals of the khadira tree, than ever say that the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, acts as a cause and condition of affliction for beings. Good son, the Tathāgata is truly able to sever and remove affliction for the sake of beings — he could never, in the end, act as its cause.

"Good son, as for what you have said, that the Tathāgata, in ages past, killed brahmins — good son, a bodhisattva-mahāsattva would not deliberately kill so much as an ant, how much less a brahmin? A bodhisattva constantly employs skillful means of every kind to bestow immeasurable life upon beings. Good son, to give food is itself to give life, and the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, when practicing the perfection of giving, constantly gives immeasurable life to beings. Good son, cultivating the precept against killing brings a long life, and the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, when practicing the perfection of moral discipline, thereby gives immeasurable life to every being. Good son, guarding one's speech from fault brings a long life, and the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, when practicing the perfection of patience, constantly encourages beings not to give rise to any thought of resentment, attributing what is right to another and taking what is wrong upon himself, engaging in no dispute at all, and thereby attaining a long life — and it is for this very reason that a bodhisattva, when practicing the perfection of patience, has already given immeasurable life to beings. Good son, diligently cultivating wholesome conduct brings a long life, and the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, when practicing the perfection of diligence, constantly encourages beings to cultivate wholesome dharmas diligently; and beings, once they practice this, attain immeasurable life — and it is for this very reason that a bodhisattva, when practicing the perfection of diligence, has already given immeasurable life to beings. Good son, cultivating a controlled mind brings a long life, and the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, when practicing the perfection of meditative concentration, encourages beings to cultivate a mind of equanimity; and beings, once they practice this, attain a long life — and it is for this very reason that a bodhisattva, when practicing the perfection of meditative concentration, has already given immeasurable life to beings. Good son, being free of heedlessness with regard to every wholesome dharma brings a long life, and the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, when practicing the perfection of wisdom, encourages beings to give rise to no heedlessness with regard to every wholesome dharma; and beings, once they practice this, through this very cause and condition, attain a long life — and it is for this very reason that a bodhisattva, when practicing the perfection of wisdom, has already given immeasurable life to beings. Good son, it is for this very reason that a bodhisattva-mahāsattva never, in the end, takes life from any being at all.

"Good son, as for what you have just asked, whether I had already attained this ground at the time I killed those brahmins — good son, I had, at that time, already attained it. It was out of loving affection that I ended their lives — it was not done with an evil mind. Good son, it is like a parent who has but a single child, whom he loves most dearly, and who transgresses the ruler's own law; the parent, out of fear for him, either has him banished or has him put to death — yet, though he does banish or put him to death, he does so with no evil mind at all. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, in protecting the true Dharma, is just the same: should any being slander the Great Vehicle, he at once has him flogged and punished with suffering, or even takes his life, wishing to cause him to change his ways and practice wholesome conduct instead. A bodhisattva should constantly reflect in this way: 'Through what cause and condition am I able to cause beings to give rise to a mind of faith?' — and, according to whatever skillful means this requires, he must certainly act. Once these brahmins had died and were reborn in the Avīci hell, they were certain to have three successive thoughts: first, they thought to themselves, 'From where have I come, to be born in this place?' and at once knew of themselves that they had come from the human realm; second, they thought to themselves, 'Where, exactly, have I now been born?' and at once knew of themselves that this was the Avīci hell; and third, they thought to themselves, 'Through what karmic cause and condition have I come to be born in this place?' and at once knew of themselves that it was through slandering the Vaipulya scriptures of the Great Vehicle, disbelieving their cause and condition, and being put to death by the ruler of the country, that they had come to be born here. Having reflected on this matter, they at once gave rise to a mind of faith and reverence toward the Vaipulya scriptures of the Great Vehicle, and, before long, at the end of their lives there, were reborn within the world-system of the tathāgata Amṛtadundubhi ("the Drum of Nectar"), where their lifespan there was fully ten kalpas. Good son, it is for this very reason that I, in ages past, in fact gave this very person ten kalpas of life — how, then, could this be called killing? Good son, if a person digs the earth, cuts grass, fells a tree, chops apart a corpse, or reviles and flogs something — does he, through this very karmic cause and condition, fall into hell?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Kāśyapa's challenge as raising two distinct historical difficulties, not one: the past-life killing of brahmins (as the ascetic-king Sunetra, or in the account given here) and the present-life harshness toward Devadatta, with the Subhūti comparison inserted specifically to sharpen the second — if even an arhat as advanced as Subhūti will forgo food all day rather than risk provoking a single unwholesome thought in anyone, how could a buddha, of all beings, speak words calculated to provoke exactly that in Devadatta? The nine "even should..." hypotheticals the Buddha opens with, on Huiyuan's reading, function as a single rhetorical wall built from impossibilities of increasing absurdity (a mosquito draining the ocean, water becoming solid, an icchantika attaining the marks of buddhahood, the Tathāgata's own final extinction) — the point being that every one of these impossible things would have to happen before the plain claim "the Tathāgata causes beings affliction" could be true, which is another way of saying the claim itself cannot be entertained as a live possibility at all, prior to any specific defense of the brahmin story.

Only after establishing this does the Buddha take up the specific charge, and Huiyuan reads the argument in two stages. First, a general defense: a bodhisattva who practices the six perfections is, in the very act of doing so, engaged in bestowing life, since each perfection — from the plain fact that offered food sustains a life, through moral discipline's freedom from killing, patience's freedom from provoking mortal conflict, diligence's cultivation of long-lived virtue, concentration's calm that preserves the body, and wisdom's freedom from the heedlessness that shortens life — structurally gives life to whoever practices it under a bodhisattva's guidance; a being so occupied with the work of extending others' lives could not, in the same act, be the one who takes them. Second, a specific defense of this particular killing: the Buddha states plainly that he had already attained the ground of an only child at the time, and explains the killing itself through the model of a loving parent who has an unruly child executed under the law — the act causes death without the mind behind it being murderous, since its entire motive is the child's own correction, which the brahmins' own posthumous experience confirms: their own sequence of three successive realizations in hell (where they came from, where they now were, and why) led them to faith in the Great Vehicle precisely because of, not despite, the manner of their death, resulting in ten kalpas of life in a pure buddha-field that they could not otherwise have earned. Huiyuan does not treat this outcome as incidental narrative detail but as the argument's actual proof: a bodhisattva's killing, unlike an ordinary killing, is measured by its result for the one killed, and here the measured result is a vastly extended life, not a shortened one — which is what licenses the sūtra's closing rhetorical question, "how, then, could this be called killing?"

「善男子！我亦以木打護法鬼。又於一時，在一山上，推羊頭鬼，令墮山下。復於樹頭，撲護獼猴鬼；令護財象，見五師子；使金剛神怖薩遮尼犍；亦以針刺箭毛鬼身。雖作如是，亦不令彼諸鬼神等有滅沒者，直欲令彼，安住正法，故示如是種種方便。善男子！我於爾時，實不罵辱提婆達多；提婆達多亦不愚癡食人㖒唾，亦不生於惡趣之中，阿鼻地獄受罪一劫，亦不壞僧出佛身血，亦不違犯四重之罪、誹謗正法大乘經典，非一闡提，亦非聲聞、辟支佛也。善男子！提婆達多者，實非聲聞緣覺境界，唯是諸佛之所知見。善男子！是故汝今不應難言，如來何緣呵責罵辱提婆達多？汝於諸佛所有境界，不應如是生於疑網。」

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！譬如甘蔗，數數煎煮，得種種味；我亦如是，從佛數聞，多得法味，所謂出家味、離欲味、寂滅味、道味。世尊！譬如真金，數數燒打，融消鍊治，轉更明淨，調和柔軟，光色微妙，其價難量，然後乃為人天寶重。世尊！如來亦爾，鄭重諮問，則得聞見甚深之義，令深行者受持奉修，無量眾生發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，然後為諸人天所宗，恭敬供養。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as I understand the meaning of what the Buddha has taught, one should fall into hell. Why is this? For the Buddha has, in the past, taught the śrāvakas: 'You monks, toward even a burnt and withered log, you should give rise to no unwholesome mind at all. Why is this? Because every being, on account of an unwholesome mind, falls into hell.'"

At that time, the Buddha praised the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Excellent, excellent! As you have said, this should be well upheld and remembered. Good son, if one falls into hell on account of an unwholesome mind, then the bodhisattva, at that time, in truth, had no unwholesome mind at all. Why is this? Because the bodhisattva-mahāsattva gives rise to a mind of compassion and benefit toward every being, even down to a worm or an ant. Why is this so? Because, well knowing every cause and condition and every skillful means, he uses the power of skillful means, wishing to cause beings to plant every kind of wholesome root. Good son, it is for this very reason that I, at that time, through skillful and wholesome means, though I took their lives, did so with no unwholesome mind at all. Good son, according to the law of the brahmins, should one kill enough ants to fill ten cartloads, there is no karmic retribution for it. Killing enough mosquitoes, gadflies, fleas, lice, wildcats, lions, tigers, wolves, bears, every evil creature and beast, and every other thing capable of harming beings, to fill ten cartloads, likewise carries no retribution; and taking the life of a spirit, a rākṣasa, a kumbhāṇḍa, a piśāca, a pūtana, or any other deranged and withered spirit capable of afflicting and harming beings, likewise carries no retribution at all; but should one kill an evil person, there is karmic retribution for it — and, having killed him, should one feel no remorse, one falls among the hungry ghosts; yet, should one be able to repent, fasting for three days, this transgression is entirely dissolved, with nothing left over. But should one kill one's own preceptor, or harm one's own parents, a woman, or a cow, one remains in hell for countless thousands of years. Good son, the Buddha and every bodhisattva know that killing is of three grades — namely, the lowest, the middling, and the highest.

"The lowest is an ant, on up through every animal — the bodhisattva alone excepted, when he manifests himself as being born as one of them. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, through the cause and condition of his own vow, manifests himself as receiving an animal's body — killing such a being is called the lowest killing; and, through the cause and condition of this lowest killing, one falls into hell, among the animals, or among the hungry ghosts, undergoing the lowest grade of suffering in full. Why is this? Because these animals possess some slight wholesome root, and it is for this reason that whoever kills them undergoes karmic retribution for it in full — this is called the lowest killing. The middling killing is that of anyone from an ordinary person up through a non-returner — this is called the middling grade — and, through this karmic cause, one falls into hell, among the animals, or among the hungry ghosts, undergoing the middling grade of suffering in full — this is called the middling killing. The highest killing is that of one's own parents, on up through an arhat, a pratyekabuddha, or a bodhisattva certain of buddhahood — this is called the highest grade — and, through this very karmic cause and condition, one falls into the great Avīci hell, undergoing the highest grade of suffering in full — this is called the highest killing. Good son, should there be one able to kill an icchantika, this does not fall within these three kinds of killing at all. Good son, every one of these brahmins was, in fact, an icchantika. It is just as with digging the earth, cutting grass, felling a tree, chopping apart a corpse, or reviling and flogging something, which carry no karmic retribution — killing an icchantika is just the same, carrying no karmic retribution at all. Why is this? Because these brahmins were entirely without even the five faculties, beginning with faith — and it is for this very reason that, though killed, they do not cause one to fall into hell.

"Good son, as for what you have said before, asking why the Tathāgata reviled Devadatta, calling him a foolish man who eats another's spittle — you should not, in fact, raise a question of this kind at all. Why is this? Whatever every buddha, every world-honored one, has to say is inconceivable. Good son, there is speech that is true, and loved by the world, and yet, being untimely and improper, brings no benefit — speech of this kind I would never, in the end, utter. Good son, there is, further, speech that is coarse and false, untimely and improper, unloved by the one who hears it, and unable to bring benefit — this, too, I would never utter. Good son, but should there be speech that, though coarse, is true and not false, and, being timely and proper, is able to bring benefit to every being — even should the one who hears it feel no pleasure, I would certainly speak it. Why is this? Because every buddha, every world-honored one, the worthy one, the fully and rightly awakened one, knows how to employ skillful means. Good son, it is as when, on one occasion, I was traveling through a wilderness, a hamlet with scattered trees, and, resting beneath its forest, there was a spirit there, named 'the Wilderness,' who fed purely on flesh and blood, killing beings in great numbers, and who, further, within that very hamlet, devoured one person every day. Good son, I, at that time, expounded the essentials of the Dharma at length for this spirit; yet, being violent and evil, foolish and without wisdom, he would not accept this teaching, and so I transformed my own body into a spirit of great strength, and shook his very palace, so that he could find no peace within it. This spirit, at that time, gathered his own retinue, came forth from his palace, and sought to resist and oppose me. This spirit, upon seeing me, at once lost his own presence of mind, and, terrified, collapsed to the ground, falling into confusion and unconsciousness, exactly like a dead man. I, moved by compassion, placed my hand upon his body, whereupon he at once rose and sat up, and spoke these words: 'How wonderful — I have, today, regained my very life! This great spirit-king, endowed with great awesome virtue and a compassionate heart, has forgiven my own transgression.' He thereupon gave rise, toward me, to a mind of wholesome faith. I then returned to my own form as the Tathāgata, and expounded the essentials of the Dharma for him at length once more, causing this spirit to receive the precept against killing. On that very same day, there was, within the wilderness village, an elder whose turn it was to die, and the villagers had already delivered him over to this spirit. The spirit, having received him, at once offered him to me; and I, having accepted him, thereupon gave this elder a new name, calling him 'Elder Hasta' ('the Hand'). At that time, this spirit said to me: 'World-Honored One, I and my retinue depend entirely upon flesh and blood in order to survive — now, on account of this precept, how are we to live?' I answered him at once: 'From this time forward, I shall instruct my śrāvaka disciples that, wherever the Buddha-dharma is practiced, they should always give you food and drink there.' Good son, it is through this very cause and condition that I established this very precept for the monks: that you should, from now on, always give food to this wilderness spirit — and should there be any dwelling place unable to provide this offering, you should know that those dwelling there are not my disciples at all, but are, rather, followers and retinue of the heavenly demon Māra. Good son, the Tathāgata, wishing to tame and subdue beings, manifests skillful means of every kind such as this — it is not done in order to cause them fear for its own sake.

"Good son, I have, likewise, struck a Dharma-protecting spirit with a piece of wood. And, on another occasion, upon a certain mountain, I pushed a spirit with the head of a sheep, causing it to fall down the mountainside. I have, further, cast down from a treetop a spirit that protected monkeys; I caused an elephant that guarded treasure to see five lions; I caused the vajra-wielding spirit to terrify the naked ascetic Satyaka; and I have, likewise, pierced with a needle the body of a spirit covered in arrow-like feathers. Though I have done things of this kind, I have never once caused any of these spirits to be destroyed or annihilated — I wished only to cause them to abide firmly within the true Dharma, and it is for this reason that I manifest skillful means of every kind such as this. Good son, I, at that time, did not, in reality, revile or insult Devadatta at all; nor is Devadatta, in reality, a foolish man who eats another's spittle; nor is he, in reality, reborn among the evil destinies, undergoing suffering in the Avīci hell for a full kalpa; nor did he, in reality, disrupt the saṅgha or draw blood from the Buddha's own body; nor did he, in reality, transgress one of the four gravest offenses or slander the true Dharma of the Great Vehicle scriptures; he is neither an icchantika, nor a śrāvaka, nor a pratyekabuddha. Good son, Devadatta is, in truth, not within the domain of any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha at all — he is known and seen only by every buddha. Good son, it is for this very reason that you should not now raise the objection, asking why the Tathāgata rebukes and reviles Devadatta — you should not give rise, in this way, to a net of doubt regarding the very domain that belongs to every buddha."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, it is like sugarcane, which, boiled and pressed again and again, yields flavor of every kind — I am just the same: hearing the Buddha's teaching again and again, I obtain the flavor of the Dharma in ever greater measure — namely, the flavor of renunciation, the flavor of freedom from desire, the flavor of still extinction, and the flavor of the path. World-Honored One, it is like true gold, which, smelted and hammered again and again, melted down and refined, becomes ever more lustrous and pure, pliant and soft, its light and color subtle and wondrous, its value beyond all reckoning, and only then does it become a treasure prized among gods and men. World-Honored One, the Tathāgata is just the same: through earnest and repeated questioning, one comes to hear and see meanings of the profoundest kind, causing those of profound practice to uphold and cultivate them, and immeasurable beings to give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening — and only then does he become revered by every god and every human, honored and given offerings."

At that time, the Buddha praised the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Excellent, excellent! A bodhisattva-mahāsattva, wishing to bring benefit to every being, questions and consults the Tathāgata about profound meanings such as this. Good son, it is for this very reason that, according with your own wish, I have taught this profound and secret dharma of the Great Vehicle's own Vaipulya teaching — namely, the ground of utmost love, as an only child."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the three-grade schema of killing (the lowest, animals; the middling, anyone from an ordinary person through a non-returner; the highest, one's parents, an arhat, a pratyekabuddha, or a bodhisattva assured of buddhahood) as tracking the victim's own residual wholesome capacity — an animal still carries some slight wholesome root, which is exactly why killing one carries retribution at all, not despite it. The icchantika's exclusion from all three grades follows the same logic taken to its limit: the sūtra states plainly that the brahmins killed in the earlier story were themselves icchantikas, entirely without even the five faculties beginning with faith, so that killing one is compared not to killing an animal at the bottom of the graded scale but to non-acts altogether — digging earth, cutting grass, chopping a corpse — which carry no moral weight because there is no capacity present to be harmed. Huiyuan flags, and resolves, an apparent tension worth carrying forward: elsewhere this sūtra teaches that giving to an icchantika earns a *thousandfold* greater merit than giving to an animal, which looks backwards next to killing an icchantika earning *less* retribution than killing an animal. His resolution is that the two acts are measured on different axes — giving is measured by the giver's own state of mind (compared against how deserving the recipient appears, and an icchantika's evident wretchedness intensifies the giver's own generosity of heart, hence more merit), while killing is measured by the victim's own remaining capacity to be harmed (which the icchantika, by definition, lacks, hence less retribution) — so the two rulings do not actually contradict each other despite pointing in opposite directions.

On the Devadatta question, Huiyuan reads the Buddha's eightfold classification of speech (true or false, timely-and-beneficial or not, gentle or coarse, in every combination) as narrowing, in this passage, to just the one combination that licenses the harsh words to Devadatta: coarse in tone, yet true, timely, and beneficial — the fourth criterion is what does the actual work, since the sūtra explicitly rules out coarse-and-false speech in the same breath. The wilderness-demon story that follows, on his reading, is offered as the clearest illustration of the same principle in a starker register: the Buddha does not reason gently with a violence too entrenched to hear reason, but manifests as a still more fearsome spirit to break through it first, and only afterward returns to his own form to teach — with the demon's own later question ("how are we to live, now that we cannot kill?") answered by instituting the standing precept that monks feed such spirits, which Huiyuan reads as the story's real point: fear is used only to open a door reason alone could not, never as an end that leaves the frightened party worse off once it closes. Devadatta's case, the Buddha insists at the end, is not actually analogous even to this — he denies every specific claim made about Devadatta (that he disrupted the saṅgha, drew blood, will suffer a kalpa in hell, or is an icchantika at all), placing the whole matter outside what any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, and by extension any ordinary reader, is positioned to judge.

「善男子！我今於是大眾之中說如是等諸空義時，有十恒河沙等菩薩摩訶薩，即得住於虛空等地。善男子！菩薩摩訶薩住是地已，於一切法中，無有滯礙、繫縛、拘執，心無迷悶，以是義故，名虛空等地。善男子！譬如虛空，於可愛色，不生貪著；不愛色中，不生瞋恚。菩薩摩訶薩住是地中，亦復如是，於好惡色，心無貪、恚。善男子！譬如虛空，廣大無對，悉能容受一切諸法。菩薩摩訶薩住是地中亦復如是，廣大無對，悉能容受一切諸法，以是義故，復得名為虛空等地。」

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if a bodhisattva who cultivates loving-kindness, compassion, and sympathetic joy attains the ground of an only child, what ground, then, does he attain when he cultivates a mind of equanimity?"

The Buddha said: "Excellent, excellent, good son! You know well the proper time, and, knowing what I wish to teach, you now ask this question. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, when he cultivates a mind of equanimity, thereby comes to abide at the ground of empty equality, just as Subhūti did. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding at the ground of empty equality, no longer sees any father, mother, brother, sister, child, relative, friend, enemy, or one in between — nor does he even see the aggregates, sense-fields, or elements, a being, or a lifespan. Good son, it is like empty space, which has no father, mother, brother, wife, or child, nor even any being or lifespan. Every dharma is just the same: it has no father, mother, and so on, up through no lifespan. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, seeing every dharma in just this way, holds a mind of perfect equality, just like that empty space. Why is this? Because he is well able to cultivate every one of the empty dharmas."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what is meant by emptiness?"

"Good son, emptiness means the following: internal emptiness, external emptiness, internal-and-external emptiness, the emptiness of the conditioned, the emptiness of the unconditioned, the emptiness without beginning, the emptiness of intrinsic nature, the emptiness of what has no existence at all, the emptiness of the ultimate meaning, the emptiness of emptiness itself, and the great emptiness. How does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplate internal emptiness? This bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplates the emptiness of internal dharmas — this emptiness of internal dharmas means that there is no father, mother, enemy, loved one, one in between, being, lifespan, permanence, bliss, self, purity, Tathāgata, Dharma, Saṅgha, or any possession at all. Within these internal dharmas, though buddha-nature is indeed present, this buddha-nature is neither internal nor external. Why is this? Because buddha-nature abides forever, without change — this is called the bodhisattva-mahāsattva's contemplation of internal emptiness. External emptiness is just the same, containing no internal dharma at all. Internal-and-external emptiness is just the same as well. Good son, only the Tathāgata, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, and buddha-nature are not included within these two emptinesses. Why is this? Because these four dharmas are permanence, bliss, self, and purity, and it is for this very reason that these four dharmas are not called empty. This is called the combined emptiness of the internal and the external. Good son, the emptiness of the conditioned means that every conditioned dharma, without exception, is empty — namely, internal emptiness, external emptiness, internal-and-external emptiness, and the emptiness of permanence, bliss, self, purity, a being, a lifespan, the Tathāgata, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, and the ultimate meaning. Within this, buddha-nature is not a conditioned dharma, and it is for this reason that buddha-nature is not emptied by the emptiness of the conditioned. This is called the emptiness of the conditioned. Good son, how does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplate the emptiness of the unconditioned? Every unconditioned dharma, without exception, is empty — namely, impermanence, suffering, impurity, no-self, the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements, the mark of a being and a lifespan, the conditioned, what is subject to outflow, internal dharmas, and external dharmas. Within the unconditioned, the four dharmas of the Buddha and the rest are neither conditioned nor unconditioned: their nature is wholesome, and so they are not unconditioned; their nature abides forever, and so they are not conditioned. This is called the bodhisattva's contemplation of the emptiness of the unconditioned. How does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplate the emptiness without beginning? This bodhisattva-mahāsattva sees that birth-and-death, without any beginning, is, in its entirety, empty and still. What is empty is this: permanence, bliss, self, and purity are, in their entirety, empty and still, without any change; a being, a lifespan, the Three Jewels, buddha-nature, and every unconditioned dharma are likewise so — this is called the bodhisattva's contemplation of the emptiness without beginning. How does the bodhisattva contemplate the emptiness of intrinsic nature? This bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplates every dharma as, in its own intrinsic nature, entirely empty — namely, the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements, permanence and impermanence, suffering and bliss, purity and impurity, self and no-self — and, contemplating every dharma of this kind, sees no intrinsic nature in any of them at all. This is called the bodhisattva-mahāsattva's contemplation of the emptiness of intrinsic nature. How does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplate the emptiness of what has no existence at all? It is as when a man without a son says that his own house is empty, contemplating it to its very limit as empty, with no one there to love; or as when a foolish person says that every direction is empty; or as when a destitute person says that everything, without exception, is empty. Reckonings of this kind are, in some cases, truly empty, and, in other cases, not truly empty at all. When a bodhisattva contemplates in this way, he does so as the destitute person does — everything, without exception, is truly empty. This is called the bodhisattva-mahāsattva's contemplation of the emptiness of what has no existence at all. How does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplate the emptiness of the ultimate meaning? Good son, when the bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplates the ultimate meaning, he sees that, at the moment the eye arises, it comes from nowhere at all, and, at the moment it ceases, it goes to nowhere at all; originally absent, it now exists, and, having existed, it returns to absence once more; seeking its own real nature, there is no eye there, and no master of it either. Just as the eye has no intrinsic nature, every dharma is just the same. What, then, is called the emptiness of the ultimate meaning? There is karmic action, and there is its fruit, and yet no agent who performs it is to be found — an empty dharma of this kind is called the emptiness of the ultimate meaning. This is called the bodhisattva-mahāsattva's contemplation of the emptiness of the ultimate meaning. How does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplate the emptiness of emptiness itself? This emptiness of emptiness is, in fact, the very place where the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha become lost and confused. Good son, 'this is existence, this is non-existence' — this is what is called the emptiness of emptiness; 'this is thus, this is not thus' — this, too, is called the emptiness of emptiness. Good son, even a bodhisattva of the ten abiding-stages penetrates only some small portion of this, no more than a mote of dust — how much more so, then, for anyone else? Good son, this emptiness of emptiness is, likewise, not the same as the concentration on the emptiness of emptiness attained by a śrāvaka. This is called the bodhisattva's contemplation of the emptiness of emptiness. Good son, how does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplate the great emptiness? Good son, what is called the great emptiness is the perfection of wisdom itself — this is called the great emptiness. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, having attained gateways of emptiness such as these, thereby comes to abide at the ground of equality with empty space."

"Good son, when I now, within this great assembly, taught these very meanings of emptiness, bodhisattva-mahāsattvas as numerous as the sands of ten Gaṅgā rivers at once came to abide at the ground of equality with empty space. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, having abided at this ground, encounters, with regard to every dharma, no obstruction, no bondage, and no attachment at all, his mind free of any confusion — it is for this very reason that this is named the ground of equality with empty space. Good son, it is like empty space, which gives rise to no craving or attachment toward a pleasing form, and gives rise to no anger toward an unpleasing one. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding at this ground, is just the same: toward both a pleasing and an unpleasing form, his mind holds neither craving nor anger. Good son, it is like empty space, vast and boundless, able to contain and receive every dharma without exception. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, abiding at this ground, is just the same: vast and boundless, able to contain and receive every dharma without exception — and it is for this very reason that this, too, is named the ground of equality with empty space."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan divides these eleven emptinesses into ten that clear away a mistaken grasping and one, the last, that names what is left standing once the clearing is done — the first ten operate on the level of "characteristics" (相空, emptying what deluded thought projects onto things), while the eleventh operates on the level of "true mind" (真空, describing reality itself rather than negating a projection onto it). Within the first ten, he traces a further nested structure: the first seven concern the emptiness of a *person* (no fixed self behind the aggregates), the eighth concerns the emptiness of *dharmas themselves* (intrinsic nature); the first eight together use absence to refute presence ("no such thing as a self is found"), the ninth uses presence to refute a mistaken absence (karma and its fruit really do occur, refuting nihilism), and the tenth refutes both existence and non-existence at once, which is why it alone is called "the emptiness of emptiness" rather than simply another kind of emptiness. He is explicit that this eleven-fold scheme closely parallels, without exactly duplicating, the ten kinds of equality taught in the *Daśabhūmika Sūtra*.

Two individual entries carry technical weight worth flagging. On "the emptiness of intrinsic nature" (性空, the seventh), Huiyuan notes it does not yet clear away the conventional, causally-constructed *appearance* of a thing — only its claim to an intrinsic, self-standing nature — which is what distinguishes it from "the emptiness of what has no existence at all" (無所有空, the eighth), illustrated by the childless man's empty house: there, even the conventional appearance itself (a "son-shaped" absence, not merely an absence of a permanent self called "son") is what is being denied, a stronger and more total claim than the seventh entry makes. On "the great emptiness" closing the list, Huiyuan quotes Nāgārjuna's own distinction between the eighteen emptinesses as "the gateway of lesser wisdom" and this contemplation of prajñā itself as "the gateway of great wisdom" — the great emptiness is not a twelfth item added to a finite list but the vantage from which the entire list of ten was only ever a set of remedies for particular delusions, prajñā itself being what the *Daśabhūmika Sūtra* calls, in its own vocabulary, the ālayavijñāna's own intrinsic emptiness, viewed here from the "revealed" rather than the "hidden" side of the same reality.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan opens this closing section by distinguishing "seeing" from "knowing" along five separable axes rather than treating them as loose synonyms: by faculty (wisdom discerning a dharma is called knowing, the heavenly eye directly perceiving an object is called seeing); by the stage an object of contemplation has reached (an initial grasp is knowing, a fully realized, verified grasp is seeing); by certainty (that a path leads nowhere is knowing, that it certainly leads to hell is seeing, since the second has been settled beyond doubt); by coarseness or subtlety (an unwholesome cause and its result are coarse and easily discerned, hence seen; a wholesome cause and its subtler result are seen only with difficulty, hence merely known); and by depth (worldly truth, being shallow, is seen clearly; the one real truth, being profound, is only known). None of these five is meant to replace the others — Huiyuan offers them as five compatible lenses on the same two-word technical pair, each one illuminating a different pass through the eight cases that follow.

On the eight cases themselves, he reads the sequence as deliberately building from the coarsest to the most refined, and closes by mapping the whole set onto the traditional five eyes: the physical eye accounts for ordinary worldly perception (people, carts, houses); the heavenly eye accounts for seeing beings pass between destinies through karma; the wisdom eye accounts for what the noble ones' own secret language points to, beyond the reach of ordinary description; the Dharma eye accounts for the broader analytic knowledge of causes, conditions, minds, and vehicles running through several of the middle cases; and the buddha eye alone accounts for knowing the Tathāgata's own indestructible body and every being's buddha-nature — the passage's own closing line, "this is itself what is known through the Tathāgata's own five eyes," is not a decorative flourish but the chapter's own key for sorting everything that came before it. The long catalogue of heterodox austerities in the first case — self-starvation, the cow-vow, the dog-vow, fire-worship — Huiyuan treats less as ethnographic detail than as an exhaustive demonstration that "knowing without seeing" can still be complete knowledge: the bodhisattva need never have witnessed a single one of these practices actually deliver liberation (since none of them ever has) to know with total certainty that none of them ever will.
