Nothing Is Fixed

Bright Virtuous King presses the sūtra's own claim that even the icchantika possesses buddha-nature: if so, why do they still fall to hell? The Buddha's answer becomes this fascicle's governing principle — nothing is ever fixed, not an icchantika's fate, not a crime, not even the Tathāgata's own nature, unpacked through a vast catalogue of what the Buddha both is and is not. The fascicle then turns practical, teaching a bodhisattva how to guard the five senses against every distraction, temptation, and evil companionship that could sever this hard-won understanding.

What the Two Lesser Vehicles Never Heard

Chinese Source

◎善男子！云何菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃不聞而聞？十二部經其義深邃，昔來不聞，今因是經，得具足聞。先雖得聞，唯聞名字，而今於此大涅槃經，乃得聞義。聲聞緣覺唯聞十二部經名字，不聞其義，今於此經具足得聞，是名不聞而聞。善男子！一切聲聞緣覺經中，不曾聞佛有常樂我淨、不畢竟滅，三寶佛性無差別相，犯四重罪、謗方等經、作五逆罪及一闡提悉有佛性，今於此經而得聞之，是名不聞而聞。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, how does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who cultivates this great nirvāṇa hear what he has not heard? The twelve divisions of scripture are, in their meaning, very deep and profound — never heard before, and now, through this very sūtra, one comes to hear them in full. Though one may have heard them before, one heard only their names, and it is only now, in this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, that one comes to hear their actual meaning. The śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha have heard only the names of the twelve divisions of scripture, never their meaning — and now, in this very sūtra, they come to hear it in full. This is what is meant by hearing what one has not heard. Good son, in every scripture of the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha, one has never heard that the Buddha possesses permanence, bliss, self, and purity, and never, in the end, is extinguished; that the Three Jewels and buddha-nature bear no mark of difference between them; and that one who transgresses the four gravest offenses, slanders the Vaipulya teachings, commits one of the five gravest offenses, or is an icchantika, still possesses buddha-nature all the same — and now, in this very sūtra, one comes to hear this. This is what is meant by hearing what one has not heard."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this short passage as completing the "hearing what was not heard" merit from Part 21 by applying it specifically to buddha-nature and the icchantika question, distinguishing three progressively deeper senses in which something can be "unheard": never encountered at all, encountered only as a bare name without its meaning, and — the deepest case — encountered as a name whose meaning the two lesser vehicles' own scriptures never actually disclose. It is this third, most provocative claim (that even the icchantika possesses buddha-nature) that immediately draws Bright Virtuous King's sharpest challenge yet, opening the fascicle's central argument.

If They Have Buddha-Nature, Why Hell?

Chinese Source

光明遍照高貴德王菩薩摩訶薩白佛言：「世尊！若犯重禁、謗方等經、作五逆罪、一闡提等，有佛性者，是等云何復墮地獄？世尊！若使是等有佛性者，云何復言無常樂我淨？世尊！若斷善根名一闡提者，斷善根時所有佛性云何不斷？佛性若斷，云何復言常樂我淨？如其不斷，何故名為一闡提耶？世尊！犯四重禁名為不定，謗方等經、作五逆罪及一闡提悉名不定，如是等輩若決定者，云何得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提？得須陀洹乃至辟支佛亦名不定，若須陀洹至辟支佛是決定者，亦不應成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提？世尊！若犯四重不決定者，須陀洹乃至辟支佛亦不決定，如是不定，諸佛如來亦復不定；若佛不定，涅槃體性亦復不定，至一切法亦復不定。云何不定？若一闡提除一闡提，則成佛道。諸佛如來亦應如是，入涅槃已，亦應還出，不入涅槃。若如是者，涅槃之性則為不定，不決定故當知無有常樂我淨，云何說言一闡提等當得涅槃？」

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva-mahāsattva Bright Virtuous King of Universal Illumination said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, should one who transgresses the gravest precepts, slanders the Vaipulya teachings, commits one of the five gravest offenses, or is an icchantika, still possess buddha-nature, how, then, could such a person still fall into hell? World-Honored One, should such a person truly possess buddha-nature, how, then, can it still be said that he lacks permanence, bliss, self, and purity? World-Honored One, should one who severs his own wholesome roots be named an icchantika, why, then, at the very moment his wholesome roots are severed, is his own buddha-nature not severed along with them? Should buddha-nature itself be severed, how, then, can it still be said to be permanence, bliss, self, and purity? And should it not be severed, why, then, is he named an icchantika at all? World-Honored One, transgressing the four gravest offenses is said to be indeterminate; slandering the Vaipulya teachings, committing one of the five gravest offenses, and being an icchantika are all likewise said to be indeterminate — but should people of this kind be fixed and determinate, how could they ever attain unsurpassed, complete awakening? And the attainment of stream-entry, up through the path of the pratyekabuddha, is likewise said to be indeterminate — but should the stream-enterer, up through the pratyekabuddha, be fixed and determinate, then they, too, should be unable to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. World-Honored One, should transgressing the four gravest offenses be indeterminate, then the stream-enterer, up through the pratyekabuddha, must likewise be indeterminate — and, being indeterminate in this way, every buddha, every tathāgata, must likewise be indeterminate; and, should the Buddha himself be indeterminate, the very substance and nature of nirvāṇa, too, must be indeterminate, and, indeed, every single dharma must be indeterminate as well. How is this indeterminacy to be understood? Should an icchantika be able to remove his own icchantika-hood, he thereby accomplishes the buddha-path — and every buddha, every tathāgata, should likewise, having entered nirvāṇa, be able to emerge from it once again, rather than remaining permanently within it. Should this be so, then the very nature of nirvāṇa is indeterminate — and, being indeterminate, one should know that it possesses no permanence, bliss, self, or purity at all. How, then, can it be said that the icchantika and the rest shall ever attain nirvāṇa?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan isolates three distinct arguments compressed into Bright Virtuous King's challenge, each attacking the icchantika-buddha-nature teaching from a different angle: first, an argument from observed consequence — buddha-nature, being wholesome, should logically block rebirth in hell, so continued rebirth there proves its absence; second, an argument from incompatible qualities — buddha-nature is defined as permanence, bliss, self, and purity, so a being visibly lacking all four cannot coherently be said to possess it; and third, and most technical, an argument from the very definition of "icchantika" itself — since severing wholesome roots is what constitutes an icchantika, and buddha-nature counts as a wholesome root, either it is severed along with the rest (making the "eternal" buddha-nature not eternal after all) or it is not severed (making the very term "icchantika," which implies total severance, incoherent). He then reads the objection's second half — deploying "indeterminacy," a term the sūtra itself has already used approvingly elsewhere, as ammunition against the sūtra's own teaching — as a genuinely sophisticated move: if nothing is fixed, this cuts both ways, threatening to make even the Buddha's own nirvāṇa an unstable, reversible state rather than a secure attainment.

Huiyuan flags that the Buddha's coming answer addresses only the third of the three opening arguments directly (the severing-of-wholesome-roots puzzle) — the first (rebirth in hell) he treats as already answered by material this collection covered in Part 16's treatment of the icchantika's total absence of the five spiritual faculties, and the second (incompatible qualities) as properly belonging to the later 師子吼菩薩品, where the sūtra takes up buddha-nature's own precise relationship to visible qualities at far greater length. He also flags, before Bright Virtuous King's own closing acknowledgment later in this fascicle, that the Buddha praises the king lavishly before answering — noting his own long history of having asked this identical question of countless past buddhas already, framing the coming answer as a teaching whose depth matches an inquiry no one else present is equipped to have raised.

Nothing Is Fixed, Except in Nirvāṇa

Chinese Source

爾時世尊告光明遍照高貴德王菩薩摩訶薩言：「善哉，善哉！善男子！為欲利益無量眾生令得安樂，憐愍慈念諸世間故，為欲增長發菩提心諸菩薩故，作如是問。……善男子！一闡提者亦不決定，若決定者，是一闡提終不能得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提；以不決定，是故能得。如汝所言，佛性不斷，云何一闡提斷善根者？善男子！善根有二種：一者內，二者外。佛性非內非外，以是義故，佛性不斷。復有二種：一者有漏，二者無漏。佛性非有漏，非無漏，是故不斷。復有二種：一者常，二者無常。佛性非常，非無常，是故不斷。若是斷者，則應還得，若不還得，則名不斷，若斷已得，名一闡提。犯四重者亦是不定，若決定者，犯四重禁終不能得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。謗方等經亦復不定，若決定者，謗正法人終不能得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。作五逆罪亦復不定，若決定者，五逆之人終不能得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。色與色相二俱不定，香、味、觸相，生相、至無明相，陰入界相、二十五有相、四生，乃至一切諸法，皆亦不定。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the World-Honored One said to the bodhisattva-mahāsattva Bright Virtuous King of Universal Illumination: "Excellent, excellent, good son! You have asked this question for the sake of benefiting immeasurable beings and bringing them peace, out of compassion and loving concern for every world, and for the sake of strengthening every bodhisattva who has given rise to the bodhi-mind… Good son, the icchantika, too, is not fixed and determinate — should he be fixed and determinate, this icchantika could never, in the end, attain unsurpassed, complete awakening; it is precisely because he is not fixed and determinate that he is able to attain it. As you yourself have said, buddha-nature is not severed — how, then, can an icchantika be one who severs his own wholesome roots? Good son, wholesome roots are of two kinds: internal, and external. Buddha-nature is neither internal nor external — it is for this very reason that it is not severed. There are, further, two kinds: subject to outflow, and free of outflow. Buddha-nature is neither subject to outflow nor free of it — it is for this reason that it is not severed. There are, further, two kinds: permanent, and impermanent. Buddha-nature is neither permanent nor impermanent — it is for this reason that it is not severed. Should something truly be severed, it must be capable of being regained; and should it not be regained, this shows that it was never, in fact, severed at all — it is only once what was severed has been regained that one is named an icchantika. Transgressing the four gravest offenses, too, is indeterminate — should it be fixed and determinate, one who transgresses these four gravest offenses could never, in the end, attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. Slandering the Vaipulya teachings, too, is indeterminate — should it be fixed and determinate, one who slanders the true Dharma could never, in the end, attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. Committing one of the five gravest offenses, too, is indeterminate — should it be fixed and determinate, one guilty of one of the five gravest offenses could never, in the end, attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. Form and the mark of form are both, likewise, indeterminate; and so, too, are the marks of fragrance, flavor, and touch; the marks running from birth to ignorance; the marks of the aggregates, the sense-fields, and the elements; the mark of the twenty-five states of existence; the four modes of birth; and so on, up through every single dharma — every one of these, likewise, is indeterminate."

Chinese Source

「善男子！譬如幻師，在大眾中化作四兵，車、步、象、馬，作諸瓔珞嚴身之具，城邑聚落、山林樹木、泉池河井。而彼眾中，有諸小兒，無有智慧，覩見之時，悉以為實。其中智人知其虛誑，以幻力故，惑人眼目。善男子！一切凡夫乃至聲聞辟支佛等，於一切法，見有定相亦復如是。諸佛菩薩於一切法不見定相。善男子！譬如小兒於盛夏月，見熱時焰，謂之為水。有智之人，於此熱焰，終不生於實水之想，但是虛焰誑人眼目，非實是水。一切凡夫聲聞緣覺見一切法，亦復如是，悉謂是實。諸佛菩薩於一切法不見定相。善男子！譬如山澗因聲有響，小兒聞之謂是實聲。有智之人解無定實，但有聲相，誑於耳識。善男子！一切凡夫聲聞緣覺於一切法，亦復如是，見有定相。諸菩薩等解了諸法，悉無定相，見無常相、空寂等相、無生滅相，以是義故，菩薩摩訶薩見一切法是無常相。善男子！亦有定相。云何為定？常樂我淨。在何處耶？所謂涅槃。善男子！須陀洹果亦復不定，不決定故，經八萬劫，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。斯陀含果亦復不定，不決定故，經六萬劫得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。阿那含果亦復不定，不決定故，經四萬劫得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。阿羅漢果亦復不定，不決定故，經二萬劫得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。辟支佛道亦復不定，不決定故，經十千劫得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, it is like an illusionist who, within a great assembly, conjures four kinds of troops — chariots, foot soldiers, elephants, and cavalry — and every kind of ornament for adorning the body, and cities, towns, and villages, mountains, forests, and trees, and springs, ponds, rivers, and wells. Among that very assembly, there are small children, lacking any wisdom, who, upon seeing all this, take every bit of it to be real; while the wise among them know it to be an empty deception, the illusionist's own power merely confusing the eyes of onlookers. Good son, every ordinary being, and even the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha, is exactly the same, seeing a fixed mark in every dharma — but every buddha and bodhisattva sees no fixed mark in any dharma at all. Good son, it is like a small child who, in the height of summer, sees a mirage in the heat and takes it to be water; a person of wisdom, seeing this same mirage, never gives rise to the notion of real water in it, knowing it to be nothing but an empty shimmer, deceiving the eyes, and not truly water at all. Every ordinary being, śrāvaka, and pratyekabuddha, seeing every dharma, is exactly the same, taking every one of them to be real — but every buddha and bodhisattva sees no fixed mark in any dharma at all. Good son, it is like a mountain ravine, whose echo arises on account of a sound — a small child, hearing it, takes it to be a real sound; a person of wisdom understands that it has no fixed reality at all, being nothing but the mark of a sound, deceiving the faculty of hearing. Good son, every ordinary being, śrāvaka, and pratyekabuddha, with regard to every dharma, is exactly the same, seeing a fixed mark within it — but every bodhisattva understands every dharma fully, and sees that none of them has any fixed mark at all, seeing instead the mark of impermanence, the mark of emptiness and stillness, and the mark of neither arising nor ceasing; it is for this very reason that the bodhisattva-mahāsattva sees every dharma as bearing the mark of impermanence. Good son, and yet there is, indeed, a fixed mark. What is this fixed mark? Permanence, bliss, self, and purity. And where is it found? Namely, in nirvāṇa. Good son, the fruit of stream-entry, too, is indeterminate — and, being indeterminate, after eighty thousand kalpas, one comes to give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening. The fruit of once-returning, too, is indeterminate — and, being indeterminate, after sixty thousand kalpas, one comes to give rise to this same mind. The fruit of non-returning, too, is indeterminate — and, being indeterminate, after forty thousand kalpas, one comes to give rise to this same mind. The fruit of arhatship, too, is indeterminate — and, being indeterminate, after twenty thousand kalpas, one comes to give rise to this same mind. And the path of the pratyekabuddha, too, is indeterminate — and, being indeterminate, after ten thousand kalpas, one comes to give rise to this same mind."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Buddha's core answer as resolving Bright Virtuous King's severing-of-wholesome-roots puzzle through three successive pairs, each showing buddha-nature to fall on neither side of a dichotomy that does apply to ordinary wholesome roots: wholesome roots are internal (mental) or external (bodily/verbal) — buddha-nature is neither, and so shares neither category's vulnerability to severance; wholesome roots are tainted or untainted — buddha-nature is neither; wholesome roots are conditioned-permanent or impermanent in the ordinary sense — buddha-nature is neither. His sharpest gloss is on the closing test for "severed": anything genuinely severable must, in principle, be capable of being regained later (since severance is itself a temporary event, reversible by further cultivation) — and it is precisely this capacity for eventual recovery that "icchantika" names, on his reading, not an actual, permanent loss of buddha-nature but the temporary absence of every other wholesome root that would otherwise let it manifest. The term "icchantika" describes the interval before recovery, not a different metaphysical category of being.

On the three similes of illusion (conjured armies, a summer mirage, a mountain echo), Huiyuan reads these as illustrating three distinct ways ordinary beings mistake appearance for fixed reality — an illusionist's own deliberate craft deceiving the eye, a natural phenomenon (heat distortion) deceiving the eye without any deceiver's intent behind it, and a purely auditory case (an echo) deceiving the ear rather than the eye — together covering the full range of sensory misapprehension the sūtra wants to indict as "seeing a fixed mark" where none exists. His point in flagging the passage's own concession — "and yet there is, indeed, a fixed mark: permanence, bliss, self, and purity, found in nirvāṇa" — is that "nothing is fixed" was never meant as a universal solvent dissolving every claim whatsoever; it is precisely because everything else is indeterminate that nirvāṇa's own fixed, unconditioned nature stands out as the one genuine exception, which is also, not coincidentally, the fixed point Bright Virtuous King's own closing argument tried to threaten by extending "indeterminacy" to the Buddha's nirvāṇa itself — a move the fascicle's next section answers directly.

Is the Tathāgata a God?

Chinese Source

「善男子！如來今於拘尸那城娑羅雙樹間，示現倚臥師子之床，欲入涅槃，令諸未得阿羅漢果眾弟子等，及諸力士，生大憂苦，亦令天、人、阿修羅、乾闥婆、迦樓羅、緊那羅、摩睺羅伽等大設供養，欲使諸人以千端疊纏裹其身，七寶為棺盛滿香油，積諸香木以火焚之，唯除二端不可得燒：一者儭身，二最在外。為諸眾生分散舍利，以為八分。一切所有聲聞弟子，咸言如來入於涅槃，當知如來亦不畢定入於涅槃。何以故？如來常住不變易故，以是義故，如來涅槃亦復不定。善男子！當知如來亦復不定。如來非天。何以故？有四種天：一者世間天、二者生天、三者淨天、四者義天。世間天者如諸國王；生天者從四天王乃至非有想非無想天；淨天者從須陀洹至辟支佛；義天者十住菩薩摩訶薩等。以何義故，十住菩薩名為義天？以能善解諸法義故。云何為義？見一切法是空義故。善男子！如來非王，亦非四天乃至非有想非無想天、從須陀洹至辟支佛、十住菩薩，以是義故，如來非天。然諸眾生亦復稱佛為天中天，是故如來非天、非非天，非人、非非人，非鬼、非非鬼，非地獄畜生餓鬼、非非地獄畜生餓鬼，非眾生、非非眾生，非法、非非法，非色、非非色，非長、非非長，非短、非非短，非相、非非相，非心、非非心，非有漏、非無漏，非有為、非無為，非常、非無常，非幻、非非幻，非名、非非名，非定、非非定，非有、非無，非說、非非說，非如來、非不如來，以是義故，如來不定。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, the Tathāgata now, among the twin sāla trees at the city of Kuśinagara, manifests himself reclining upon a lion's own couch, about to enter nirvāṇa — causing every one of his disciples who has not yet attained arhatship, and every one of the strongmen present, to give rise to great grief and suffering, and causing every god, human, asura, gandharva, garuḍa, kinnara, and mahoraga to prepare great offerings; and about to cause everyone to wrap his own body in a thousand lengths of fine cloth, place it within a coffin of the seven treasures filled with fragrant oil, pile up fragrant wood, and cremate it with fire — with only two of these thousand lengths of cloth left unburned: the innermost, next to his own body, and the very outermost — and, for the sake of every being, his relics divided into eight portions. Every one of his śrāvaka disciples will together say that the Tathāgata has entered nirvāṇa — and yet you should know that the Tathāgata does not, in the end, actually enter nirvāṇa at all. Why is this? Because the Tathāgata is forever ever-abiding, unchanging — and it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata's own nirvāṇa, too, is indeterminate. Good son, you should know that the Tathāgata, too, is indeterminate. The Tathāgata is not a god. Why is this? There are four kinds of gods: the worldly god, the born god, the pure god, and the meaning god. The worldly god is like any king of a realm; the born god runs from the Four Heavenly Kings up through the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception; the pure god runs from the stream-enterer up through the pratyekabuddha; and the meaning god is the bodhisattva-mahāsattva of the ten abidings. For what reason is the bodhisattva of the ten abidings named the meaning god? Because he is able to understand well the meaning of every dharma. And what is this meaning? Seeing that every dharma bears the meaning of emptiness. Good son, the Tathāgata is not a king, nor is he any of the four kinds of gods — not the born god up through the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception, not the pure god from the stream-enterer up through the pratyekabuddha, and not the meaning god, the bodhisattva of the ten abidings — it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata is not a god. And yet every being, too, calls the Buddha 'the god beyond gods' — it is for this reason that the Tathāgata is neither a god nor not a god, neither a human nor not a human, neither a spirit nor not a spirit, neither a denizen of hell, an animal, nor a hungry ghost, nor not one of these, neither a being nor not a being, neither a dharma nor not a dharma, neither form nor not form, neither long nor not long, neither short nor not short, neither marked nor unmarked, neither mind nor not mind, neither subject to outflow nor free of it, neither conditioned nor unconditioned, neither permanent nor impermanent, neither an illusion nor not an illusion, neither a name nor not a name, neither fixed nor unfixed, neither existent nor non-existent, neither spoken of nor unspoken of, and neither the Tathāgata nor not the Tathāgata — it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata is indeterminate."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the cremation scene's own careful detail — a thousand lengths of cloth burned entirely except the innermost and outermost — as bearing a double meaning worth preserving rather than passed over as ritual color: read literally, the inner cloth survives to keep the relics clean and the outer to shield the ash from the surrounding dirt; read symbolically, the pair signals that the Tathāgata remains, at once, untouched by any outer defilement and inwardly ever-pure — or, on a further reading, that his true body (the Dharma-body) and his manifested body (the form seen by the assembly) both persist even through what looks, to an unenlightened onlooker, like total extinction. This entire elaborate death-scene, staged here as something about to happen, functions as the concrete referent for "the Tathāgata's own nirvāṇa is indeterminate" — the assembled śrāvakas will call this event "nirvāṇa," and, at one level, they will be right; and yet, because the Tathāgata is ever-abiding and unchanging, they will also, at a deeper level, be describing something that never actually occurred as an ending at all.

On the four kinds of heaven, Huiyuan cross-references the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra's own terminology: the "worldly god" (kings and rulers, godlike only by social convention), and what that same text groups together as simply "pure gods" (a category Huiyuan's own source splits further into the "pure god" proper — the noble ones from stream-entry through pratyekabuddhahood, pure through their own attainment — and the "meaning god" — bodhisattvas of the ten abidings, distinguished not by purity of conduct alone but by penetrating insight into the meaning, specifically the emptiness, of every dharma). The Tathāgata is none of these four, and yet beings rightly call him "god beyond gods" — which is exactly the move repeated, Huiyuan notes, across every one of the roughly twenty pairs that follow: each pair denies two opposite, equally mistaken ways of fixing the Tathāgata's nature to a single category, whether that category seems to elevate him (a god) or merely describe him factually (a being, a mind, a name).

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan notes that Huiyuan's own commentary leaves several of these pairs unglossed individually, on the explicit ground that they are already covered by the fuller treatment of "neither permanent nor impermanent" that follows in the next section: "neither existent nor non-existent," "neither a name nor not a name," and "neither the Tathāgata nor not the Tathāgata" all share the same underlying logic already spelled out there in greater technical detail, so repeating the analysis pair by pair would be redundant. His running gloss for every pair that is treated individually follows one consistent template: the negative half of each pair denies that the Tathāgata is exhausted by some category true of ordinary, conditioned things (a king bound to a throne, a being bound by a fixed nature, form bound by the ten sense-fields, long or short measured against an ordinary body), while the positive half denies the opposite extreme — that he therefore has no worldly presence, engagement, or describable feature at all (he was, after all, born a prince, does appear with a body bearing every mark, does manifest a short body for Gośāla's sake, and does come, go, sit, and lie down like anyone else).

He singles out "neither conditioned nor unconditioned" as carrying particular weight for the fascicle's larger argument: denying that the Tathāgata is merely conditioned protects his permanence, bliss, self, and purity from the same critique Bright Virtuous King leveled against nirvāṇa in Part 21 (that whatever depends on cultivated causes must be impermanent); but denying that he is simply, inertly unconditioned is equally necessary, since an entirely static, non-responsive being could never actually appear, teach, or manifest a parinirvāṇa at all — the entire elaborate scene of the sāla trees, the couch, and the coming cremation this fascicle opened with. Huiyuan's broader point across this whole catalogue: each pair models, in miniature, the exact structure the sūtra needs for its own central claim — a reality that is neither destroyed by conditions nor cut off from all activity within them.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan counts seven distinct reasons offered for "the Tathāgata is not permanent" (a body with parts, possessing knowledge, possessing speech, possessing a clan-name, possessing parents, possessing the four postures, occupying a fixed place) and eight for "nor is he not permanent" (freedom from birth, from fixed nature, universal presence, freedom from location-bound existence, freedom from time-bound existence, namelessness and formlessness, freedom from cause and effect, and transcendence of the three time-periods) — each list measured consistently against empty space as the standard image of true permanence, but reaching opposite verdicts because the Tathāgata, unlike space, does visibly speak, teach, and appear with a body and a lineage, while also, unlike any ordinary conditioned thing, sharing space's own freedom from birth, location, and temporal restriction. He reads this seven-versus-eight asymmetry as unremarkable — the pattern is illustrative rather than a rigid, matched taxonomy — and treats "neither permanent nor impermanent" as this fascicle's technical centerpiece precisely because it is the pair Bright Virtuous King's own challenge most directly threatened by extending "indeterminacy" to nirvāṇa itself.

On the closing image of the Tathāgata's own transformations (multiplying bodies, passing through mountains, walking on water, becoming a city or a tree), Huiyuan reads this not as displaying illusion-like unreality but as displaying the opposite: only a being who has fully severed every deceptive mind is capable of manifestations this total and unconstrained, since an ordinary illusionist's tricks (recalled from this fascicle's own earlier simile) work only by deceiving another's perception, whereas the Tathāgata's transformations involve no deception of anyone at all — a real capacity, not a false appearance, which is exactly why he is "not an illusion" and yet, in the very same breath, "not not an illusion" either. The three-outflow definition closing this section (desire, existence, ignorance) sets up the technical vocabulary — outflow, 漏 — that the fascicle's remaining sections will use to organize an entirely practical turn: having established what the Tathāgata has already severed completely, the sūtra now teaches the bodhisattva still on the path how to sever these same outflows himself.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this catalogue of unanswerable questions — deliberately overwhelming in its exhaustiveness — as illustrating the first two of the seven kinds of outflow the Satyasiddhi tradition catalogues in detail: the outflow of view (見漏, afflictions tied to holding fixed philosophical positions, exemplified here by the doubts about self, body, time, and causation) and, by extension, the outflow of cultivation (修漏, afflictions that persist even after such views are abandoned, addressed only implicitly here). His point in cataloguing the sheer range of the questions — biological origin, caste in past and future lives, whether the self resides in the mind or the eye or the whole body, whether killing or drinking carries a fixed moral weight regardless of intent — is that an ordinary being's mind is not troubled by one or two philosophical puzzles but by an entire, self-perpetuating field of unresolved uncertainty, out of which arise the six specific kinds of wrong view the passage names (a fixed self, a fixed no-self, and four further permutations of a self seeing or not seeing itself) — a taxonomy he reads as covering every logically possible stance one could take on the self's own reality without ever actually resolving the underlying doubt that generated the question.

Having established this deep root of affliction (the outflow of view, the first and most fundamental of the seven), the sūtra turns, in the fascicle's remaining sections, to the practical outflows a bodhisattva must actively guard against in daily conduct — the outflow of the faculties (根漏), of evil companionship (惡漏), and of reliance on the four requisites (親近漏) — which Huiyuan reads as a deliberate movement from the deepest, most abstract root of affliction to its most concrete, everyday expressions, ending the fascicle on practical instruction rather than further doctrinal analysis.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan identifies this section as beginning the sūtra's treatment of the third of the seven kinds of outflow, the outflow of the faculties (根漏) — afflictions that arise specifically because the five sense-faculties, left unguarded, generate craving toward their objects — and reads the five similes (vicious horse, untamed elephant, flattering minister, wayward son, and bandit) as covering five distinct failure-modes rather than five decorative repetitions of one point: the horse and elephant show unguarded faculties actively carrying the practitioner somewhere he did not choose to go; the flattering minister shows them actively counseling wrong action, rather than merely failing to resist it; the wayward son shows a failure to receive correction that would otherwise be available; and the bandit shows the faculties as an external threat exploited by hostile forces (hell, the animal realm, hungry ghosts) rather than an internal failing alone.

On the king-and-kingdom analogy, Huiyuan reads the reciprocal relationship (guarding the king guards the kingdom; guarding the kingdom guards the king) as making a precise technical point about the relationship between mind and the five faculties: neither is more fundamental than the other in practice, so that mindfulness born of wisdom (念慧), cultivated by hearing and reflecting on this very sūtra, disciplines the faculties indirectly by holding the mind steady, exactly as a skilled herdsman restrains cattle not by chasing each one individually but by maintaining a single, attentive watch. The closing insight — that a bodhisattva possessed of this mindfulness sees no fixed self, no fixed being, only conditions combining the way stray materials combine into a house — is, on Huiyuan's reading, the actual mechanism by which craving is prevented in the first place: not by suppressing desire through willpower directed at the five faculties themselves, but by removing the false perception of a fixed, graspable object that desire could ever actually attach to.

Chinese Source

「復次菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃經者，不著眾生相，作種種法相。善男子！譬如畫師，以眾雜彩，畫作眾像，若男若女、若牛若馬。凡夫無智，見之則生男女等相，畫師了知無有男女。菩薩摩訶薩亦復如是，於法異相觀於一相，終不生於眾生之相。何以故？有念慧故。菩薩摩訶薩修大涅槃，或時覩見端正女人，終不生於貪著之相。何以故？善觀相故。善男子！菩薩摩訶薩知五欲法無有歡樂，不得暫停，如犬囓枯骨、如人持火逆風而行、如篋毒蛇夢中所得、路首有果樹多人所擲、亦如段肉眾鳥競逐、如水上泡畫水之迹、如織經盡、如囚趣市，猶如假借勢不得久，觀欲如是多諸過惡。」

"Furthermore, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who cultivates this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra does not cling to the mark of any being, though he still works with every kind of dharma's own mark. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the painter simile as extending the previous section's "no fixed self, no fixed being" insight from abstract analysis into active engagement with the world: a bodhisattva does not withdraw from working with the conventional marks of things (form, gender, category) any more than a painter refuses to paint recognizable figures — the difference lies entirely in whether the one perceiving knows the figure to be a construction of colors on a surface, or mistakes it for the real thing. This is why the bodhisattva can behold a beautiful woman and feel no craving: not through suppressing the sense-perception itself, but through "skillfully observing her mark" — seeing the conventional appearance without the superimposed belief in a fixed, inherently desirable object underneath it.

On the ten similes for the five desires, Huiyuan reads these as covering distinct facets of why desire fails to deliver what it promises: the dry bone and the fire against the wind show pursuit that harms the pursuer more than it satisfies him; the snake in the basket and the dream-object show danger and unreality masquerading as safety and substance; the roadside fruit tree and the contested meat show how what looks desirable actually invites attack and competition from others; and foam, a mark on water, an unraveling thread, and a prisoner en route to execution together show simple transience — nothing to be held onto even briefly. He reads the accumulation of so many similes for a single point as deliberate, matching this sūtra's own established rhetorical habit (already seen in earlier fascicles' extended lists) of overwhelming the reader with images until the point is felt rather than merely understood.

A Mountain of Bones

Chinese Source

「復次善男子！菩薩摩訶薩觀諸眾生，為色、香、味、觸因緣故，從昔無數無量劫來，常受苦惱。一一眾生，一劫之中所積身骨，如王舍城毘富羅山，所飲乳汁如四海水，身所出血多四海水，父母兄弟妻子眷屬命終哭泣所出目淚多四大海，盡地草木為四寸籌以數父母亦不能盡，無量劫來或在地獄畜生餓鬼所受行苦不可稱計，揣此大地猶如棗等易可窮極，生死難盡。菩薩摩訶薩如是深觀一切眾生，以是欲因緣故受苦無量，菩薩以是生死行苦故不失念慧。善男子！譬如世間有諸大眾滿二十五里，王勅一臣：『持一油鉢經由中過莫令傾覆，若棄一渧，當斷汝命。』復遣一人，拔刀在後，隨而怖之。臣受王教，盡心堅持，經歷爾所大眾之中，雖見可意五邪欲等，心常念言：『我若放逸，著彼邪欲，當棄所持，命不全濟。』是人以是怖因緣故，乃至不棄一渧之油。菩薩摩訶薩亦復如是，於生死中不失念慧，以不失故，雖見五欲心不貪著，若見淨色，不生色相，唯觀苦相，乃至識相亦復如是，不作生相、不作滅相、不作因相、觀和合相，菩薩爾時五根清淨，根清淨故護根戒具。一切凡夫五根不淨，不能善持，名曰根漏。菩薩永斷是故無漏，如來拔出永斷根本，是故非漏。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva contemplates that every being, on account of the causes and conditions of form, fragrance, flavor, and touch, has forever undergone suffering and affliction from immeasurable, incalculable kalpas past. Each and every being's own accumulated bones, over the course of a single kalpa alone, would rise as high as Mount Vipula at Rājagṛha; the milk each has drunk would equal the water of the four great oceans; the blood each has shed would exceed the water of the four great oceans; and the tears each has wept, mourning the deaths of parents, brothers, wife, children, and kin, would likewise exceed the four great oceans. Grasping the whole of this great earth is, by comparison, as easy as holding a single date in one's hand — but birth-and-death is inexhaustible. Good son, it is like a great multitude of people, filling twenty-five leagues, among whom the king commands one minister: 'Carry this single bowl of oil through the entire crowd, and let none of it spill — should you spill even a single drop, I shall take your life.' It is on account of this very terror that this man does not spill even a single drop of the oil. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva is exactly the same: amid birth-and-death, he never loses his own mindfulness and wisdom, and, because he does not lose it, though he sees the five desires, his mind gives rise to no craving or attachment toward them. The bodhisattva, at this time, has purified his own five faculties; and, his faculties being pure, he thereby guards them and is fully endowed with their discipline. Every ordinary being's five faculties are impure, unable to be well maintained — this is named the outflow of the faculties. The bodhisattva has forever severed this, and is therefore free of outflow; and the Tathāgata has uprooted and forever severed its very root, and is therefore, likewise, free of outflow."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this vast accounting of accumulated suffering — bones piled higher than a mountain, tears and blood exceeding the oceans, parents too numerous to count even with the entire earth's vegetation converted into tally sticks — as a deliberate escalation beyond any single lifetime's own scale, designed to make the true magnitude of saṃsāra's suffering register emotionally rather than merely conceptually, closing on the striking comparison that even the whole physical earth is, by contrast, graspable and finite where birth-and-death is not. He reads this contemplation as the specific antidote to desire's own seduction: having just cultivated non-attachment through recognizing desire's inherent instability (the previous section's ten similes), the bodhisattva here adds recognition of its actual cost, measured across the full span of his own past lives.

On the oil-bowl parable, Huiyuan reads the two threats facing the carrier — losing the king's oil, and the executioner's blade close behind — as together modeling the specific quality of a bodhisattva's own vigilance: not casual mindfulness maintained at leisure, but attention sustained under a felt stake genuinely equal to the reward, since the bodhisattva's own "spilled drop" (a lapse into craving amid the five desires) carries a consequence just as serious, in his own reckoning, as sudden death. This closes the sūtra's treatment of the outflow of the faculties on the same note it opened: purification of the five faculties comes not from avoiding sense-objects altogether, but from perceiving them correctly, through mindfulness and wisdom, at the very moment of contact — the "mark of suffering" and "the mark of a combination of conditions" replacing the ordinary, craving-generating mark of a fixed, desirable form.

Good son, such a person may, in a dream while sleeping at night, dream of an image of the Buddha, or of a god, of a śramaṇa, of a king, of a sage-king, of a lion-king, of a lotus flower, or of an udumbara flower; or he may see a great mountain, or the waters of a great ocean, or the sun and moon, or a white elephant or a white horse; or he may see his own father and mother, or obtain flowers or fruit, or gold, silver, lapis lazuli, crystal, and other treasures, or the five products of the cow — at such a time, he should know that this is precisely the Tathāgata receiving his own offering. Waking, filled with joy, he soon obtains every kind of thing he needs, his mind gives rise to no evil thought, and he takes delight in cultivating every wholesome dharma. Good son, this great nirvāṇa is able to accomplish immeasurable, incalculable, inconceivable, and boundless merit of exactly this kind."

"Good son, you should now believe and accept my own words: should any good son or good daughter wish to see me, wish to revere me, wish to see me by sharing in the very nature of dharmas, wish to attain the concentration on emptiness, wish to see true reality, wish to cultivate the Śūraṅgama samādhi or the lion-king samādhi, wish to break through the eight demons — these eight demons being the four demons, together with impermanence, the absence of bliss, the absence of self, and the absence of purity — or wish to attain the happiness of humans and gods, and should he see anyone who upholds this great Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, copying it out, reciting it, expounding it for others, and reflecting on its meaning, he should go to draw near to and rely upon such a person, consulting and receiving his teaching, making offerings, showing reverence, respect, and praise, washing his hands and feet, arranging his bedding, and providing him with the four requisites so that he lacks nothing at all. Should this teacher come from far away, he should go out ten leagues along the road to welcome him, and, for the sake of this very sūtra, offer up whatever he holds most precious — and, should he possess nothing at all to offer, he should sell his own body. Why is this? This sūtra is harder to encounter even than the udumbara flower."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this section as opening the fourth of the seven kinds of outflow, the outflow of evil (惡漏), through the same three-fold structure the fascicle has used consistently — a bodhisattva's own practice (studying this sūtra), a set of similes illustrating its power (a good teacher's own restraining influence, a protective incantation), and the sūtra's own explicit self-praise, which he reads not as mere rhetorical inflation but as functionally necessary: since this outflow specifically concerns exposure to corrupting external influences, the sūtra must establish, before anything else, that engaging with this particular text is itself protective rather than merely neutral, which is why the incantation simile is chosen deliberately — an incantation heard even once continues to protect for years afterward, exactly paralleling how a single encounter with this sūtra is said to protect for seven kalpas.

On the promise that the Buddha "dwells always" wherever someone upholds this sūtra, appearing to receive offerings even in the guise of a beggar, Huiyuan reads the list of auspicious dream-signs (buddha images, mountains, oceans, white elephants, one's own parents, the five products of the cow used in traditional offerings) as a pastoral concession rather than a doctrinal claim requiring literal belief: an ordinary practitioner, uncertain whether devotion offered to an absent teacher actually "reaches" anyone, is given a concrete, checkable sign of confirmation, addressing a real psychological need for reassurance that the sūtra's more abstract teachings on the Tathāgata's own indeterminate nature do not, by themselves, satisfy. The closing exhortation — travel ten leagues to welcome such a teacher, give what is most precious, sell your own body if you have nothing else — Huiyuan reads as setting up, quite deliberately, the story the Buddha tells next: his own account of having done exactly this, in a past life, for the sake of hearing a single verse.

Ru-Yi Translation

Along the road, I saw a man, and said to him: 'I wish to sell my own body — are you able to buy it?' The man answered: 'There is a task in my own household that no one is able to perform — should you be able to do it, I shall buy you.' I asked at once: 'What task is this, that no one is able to perform?' The man answered me: 'I suffer from a grave illness, and a good physician has prescribed a medicine for it — I must eat three ounces of human flesh each day. Should you be able to supply me with three ounces of your own flesh, day after day, I shall give you five pieces of gold in return.' Hearing this, my heart filled with joy, and I said to him further: 'Give me the money now, and grant me seven days — once my own affairs are settled, I shall come and fulfill my task.' The man answered me: 'Seven days is not possible — should you truly be able to do this, I shall grant you only a single day.' Having received this verse, I returned at once to that sick man's own house. Good son, though I gave him three ounces of my own flesh, day after day, on account of my own recollection of this very verse, I did not feel it as pain at all, and continued without fail, day after day, for a full month. Good son, on account of this very cause and condition, his illness was cured, and my own body, too, was restored, entirely without any wound or scar. Seeing my own body fully whole and complete once more, I at once gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening. The power of a single verse alone was able to accomplish this much — how much more so, then, one who upholds, recites, and fully receives the entire sūtra? Seeing that this sūtra held benefit of this very kind, my resolve redoubled, and I vowed that, in a future age, I would accomplish the buddha-path, and be named Śākyamuni. Good son, it is through the very power of the causes and conditions of this single verse that I am now able, in the midst of this great assembly, to expound it fully for the sake of every god and human present. Good son, it is on account of this very cause and condition that this great nirvāṇa is inconceivable, accomplishing immeasurable, boundless merit, and is indeed the very deep, secret treasury of every buddha, every tathāgata — and it is for this very reason that whoever is able to uphold it severs and separates himself from the outflow of evil."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this as a distinct past-life story from the more famous "half-verse" account of the Snow Mountain boy this collection already translated in Part 14, and cautions against conflating the two: there, the bodhisattva traded his own life outright for half a verse from a transformed demon, illustrating total, unconditional sacrifice; here, the bodhisattva trades a month of his own flesh, in fixed daily increments, for the far more modest opportunity merely to attend a teaching and retain a single verse of it — a smaller sacrifice matched to a smaller, though still substantial, gain, and Huiyuan reads the pairing of these two stories across the sūtra as showing that this kind of self-sacrifice for the Dharma is not limited to one dramatic, unrepeatable case, but recurs across the bodhisattva's own path in proportion to what is actually being sought.

He reads the detail of the bodhisattva's own request for seven days, refused in favor of a single day, as adding a note of real, unglamorized negotiation to what could otherwise read as pure hagiography — this is a story about someone poor enough that even his own body has to be haggled over, and desperate enough to accept far worse terms than he asked for. The verse itself, retained despite the bodhisattva's own admitted dullness at the time, becomes the entire content of his month-long ordeal, sustaining him against pain the same way the oil-bowl carrier's fear of death sustained his own attention two sections earlier — a deliberate echo, on Huiyuan's reading, tying this story back to the fascicle's larger argument that mindfulness genuinely fixed on the Dharma changes what a body can actually endure. That this single verse's power was sufficient to heal the wounds entirely and prompt the bodhi-resolve that would eventually make him the very Buddha now telling the story, Huiyuan reads as the sūtra's own closing argument for its opening claim in this section: engaging this teaching, even in the smallest possible dose, already separates a being from the outflow of evil.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the bodhisattva's own conditional avoidance principle — separating from what actually functions as a cause of outflow, and only from that, rather than from things by category regardless of their actual effect — as the section's key methodological point: a bodhisattva does not treat "elephant," "thorny ground," or "companion" as inherently defiling categories to be shunned on sight, but assesses each encounter by its actual causal contribution to affliction, wholesome or otherwise, and adjusts accordingly — the same discernment already modeled by the painter and the mindful sense-contact of earlier sections, now applied to the choice of external circumstances rather than internal perception. His gloss on tending an admittedly impure body (compared to illness, a sore, a boil, an enemy, an arrow-wound) as nonetheless worth preserving turns on a sharp means-end distinction: the body is never valued for its own sake, but solely as the instrument through which the Dharma can be practiced and eventually a wholly different, marked body (the thirty-two marks) attained — value derived entirely from function, not from any inherent worth in the flesh itself.

On the elephant-versus-companion comparison, Huiyuan traces the six-fold escalation explicitly: mind versus body-only harm; scope (one being versus countless); the kind of body harmed (impure flesh versus a purified body and mind); the kind of harm (physical body versus Dharma-body); the destination after death (not necessarily the three evil destinies versus certainly the three evil destinies); and the nature of the enmity itself (personal, bodily enemy versus enemy to the Dharma as such) — six distinct reasons, not mere repetition, for why an outwardly far more frightening threat (a rampaging elephant) merits less actual fear than an ordinary human companion whose influence corrupts from within. The closing treatment of the four requisites, framed through the extended wound-dressing analogy, closes the fascicle's entire sequence of the seven outflows on a note of practical moderation rather than asceticism for its own sake: the bodhisattva's own body is a wound requiring ongoing care, and refusing the four requisites out of some misguided purity would itself produce the very failure of endurance — and thus the very inability to practice — that this whole fascicle's teaching on outflow was meant to prevent.

Part 22 Complete

This closes Part 22 (fascicle 22 of 40) and continues 光明遍照高貴德王菩薩品. Bright Virtuous King's challenge — that buddha-nature and hell cannot coexist, that indeterminacy threatens even the Buddha's own nirvāṇa — is answered by making indeterminacy the fascicle's own governing principle: nothing is fixed except nirvāṇa itself, buddha-nature is severed by nothing because it falls outside every category that could sever it, and even the Tathāgata's own nature resists every fixed description offered for it, catalogued at exhaustive length through paired negations covering divinity, humanity, form, permanence, and outflow itself. The fascicle then turns from doctrine to discipline, working through the seven kinds of outflow — view, cultivation, the five faculties, evil companionship, and reliance on the four requisites — each addressed through vivid similes: a vicious horse and untamed elephant for unguarded senses, a painter who sees no man or woman in his own painted figures, a mountain of accumulated bone and an oil-bowl carried through a crowd under threat of death, an incantation whose protection outlasts its hearing, and the Buddha's own past-life account of trading a month of his flesh for a single verse.
