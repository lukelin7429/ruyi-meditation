The Moon That Never Sets, and the Word That Means Four Things

This fascicle opens with an extended cluster of similes — the moon that only appears to rise, wane, and set; three suns for three lengths of life; stars invisible by daylight — each teaching that the Tathāgata's manifest births and deaths never touch his own unchanging nature. It then turns to this sūtra's single most demanding claim: that its own teaching, entering every being's pores, becomes a cause for buddhahood in everyone but the icchantika, defended through more than a dozen similes for what makes this one exception different from every other kind of grave wrongdoing. A famous parable anchors the fascicle's turn toward its close — a king's word that means salt, a vessel, water, and a horse, understood correctly only by the minister who grasps his intent — before the fascicle looks forward to this teaching's own eventual dilution, and closes on buddha-nature as the true mark distinguishing a man from a woman, regardless of the body either is born into.

The Moon That Never Sets

Chinese Source

「復次善男子！譬如有人，見月不現，皆言月沒，而作沒想，而此月性，實無沒也；轉現他方，彼處眾生，復謂月出，而此月性，實無出也。何以故？以須彌山障故不現，其月常生，性無出沒。如來、應、正遍知亦復如是，出於三千大千世界，或閻浮提示有父母，眾生皆謂如來生於閻浮提內。或閻浮提示現涅槃，而如來性實無涅槃，而諸眾生皆謂如來實般涅槃，喻如月沒。善男子！如來之性實無生滅，為化眾生故示生滅。

「善男子！如此滿月，餘方見半；此方半月，餘方見滿。閻浮提人若見月初，皆謂一日，起初月想。見月盛滿，謂十五日，生盛滿想。而此月性，實無虧盈，因須彌山而有增減。善男子！如來亦爾，於閻浮提或現初生，或現涅槃。現始生時，猶如初月，一切皆謂童子初生，行於七步如二日月，或復示現入於書堂，如三日月，示現出家，如八日月，放大智慧微妙光明，能破無量眾生魔眾，如十五日盛滿之月。或復示現三十二相、八十種好，以自莊嚴而現涅槃，喻如月蝕。如是眾生所見不同，或見半月，或見滿月，或見月蝕，而此月性實無增減、蝕噉之者，常是滿月。如來之身亦復如是，是故名為常住不變。」

「復次善男子！喻如滿月一切悉現，在在處處、城邑聚落、山澤水中、若井若池、若瓫若鍑、一切皆現，有諸眾生，行百由旬，百千由旬見月常隨，凡夫愚人妄生憶想，言：『我本於城邑屋宅，見如是月，今復於此空澤而見，為是本月？為異於本？』各作是念，月形大小或如鍑口，或復有言大如車輪，或言猶如四十九由旬。一切皆見月之光明，或見團圓喻如金盤。是月性一，種種眾生，各見異相。善男子！如來亦爾，出現於世，或有人天而作是念：『如來今者在我前住。』復有眾生亦生是念：『如來今者在我前住。』或有聾瘂，亦見如來有聾瘂相。眾生雜類，言音各異，皆謂如來悉同己語，亦各生念：『在我舍宅受我供養。』或有眾生，見如來身廣大無量。有見微小，或有見佛是聲聞像，或復有見為緣覺像。有諸外道復各念言：『如來今者，在我法中出家學道。』或有眾生復作是念：『如來今者獨為我故出現於世。』如來實性喻如彼月，即是法身，是無生身。方便之身隨順於世，示現無量本業因緣，在在處處示現有生，猶如彼月。以是義故，如來常住無有變易。」

「復次善男子！如羅睺羅阿修羅王以手遮月，世間諸人咸謂月蝕，阿修羅王實不能蝕。以阿修羅障其明故，是月團圓無有虧損，但以手障，故使不現。若攝手時，世間咸謂月已還生，皆言是月多受苦惱。假使百千阿修羅王，不能惱之。如來亦爾，示有眾生於如來所，生麁惡心，出佛身血，起五逆罪，至一闡提，為未來世諸眾生故，如是示現壞僧斷法而作留難。假使百千無量諸魔，不能侵出如來身血。所以者何？如來之身無有肉血、筋脈、骨髓。如來真實實無惱壞，眾生皆謂法、僧毀壞，如來滅盡。而如來性真實無變，無有破壞，隨順世間，如是示現。復次善男子！如二人鬪，若以刀杖傷身出血，雖至於死，不起殺想。如是業相，輕而不重。於如來所，本無殺心，雖出身血，是業亦爾，輕而不重。如來如是，於未來世為化眾生示現業報。」

「復次善男子！猶如良醫，勤教其子，醫方根本，此是根藥、此是莖藥、此是色藥，種種相貌汝當善知。其子敬奉父之所勅，精勤習學，善解諸藥。是醫後時壽盡命終，其子號咷而作是言：『父本教我，根藥如是，莖藥如是，花藥如是，色相如是。』如來亦爾，為化眾生示現制戒，應當如是，受持莫犯作五逆罪、誹謗正法及一闡提。為未來世起是事者，是故示現，欲令比丘，於佛滅後作如是知，此是契經甚深之義，此是戒律輕重之相，此是阿毘曇分別法句，如彼醫子。」

「復次善男子！如人知月，六月一蝕，而上諸天，須臾之間已見月蝕。何以故？彼天日長，人間短故。善男子！如來亦爾，天人咸謂如來壽短，如彼天人，須臾之間頻見月蝕。如來又於須臾之間，示現百千萬億涅槃，斷煩惱魔、陰魔、死魔，是故百千萬億天魔，悉知如來入般涅槃。又復示現無量百千先業因緣，隨順世間種種性故，示現如是無量無邊不可思議，是故如來常住無變。復次善男子！譬如明月，眾生樂見，是故稱月號為樂見。眾生若有貪恚愚癡，則不得稱為樂見也。如來如是，其性純善，清淨無垢，是最可稱為樂見也。樂法眾生，視之無厭，惡心之人，不憙瞻覩，以是義故，故言如來喻如明月。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, it is like the moon: when a person no longer sees it, everyone says the moon has set, and forms the notion that it has set — yet this nature of the moon has, in truth, never set at all. When it then appears in some other region, the beings there suppose that the moon has risen — yet this nature of the moon has, in truth, never risen at all. Why is this? Because it is merely obscured by Mount Sumeru, and so does not appear; the moon itself is forever present, its own nature knowing neither rising nor setting. The Tathāgata, the Worthy One, the Perfectly and Fully Awakened One, is just the same: appearing throughout this billion-world universe, he manifests, within Jambudvīpa, the appearance of having parents, and beings all suppose that the Tathāgata was born within Jambudvīpa itself; and he manifests, within Jambudvīpa, the appearance of entering parinirvāṇa, yet the Tathāgata's own nature truly never enters nirvāṇa at all — and every being supposes that the Tathāgata has truly entered final nirvāṇa, exactly as they suppose the moon has set. Good son, the Tathāgata's own nature truly knows neither birth nor death; it is only for the sake of converting beings that he manifests birth and death.

"Good son, just as, when the full moon appears here, it is seen as a half-moon elsewhere, and, when a half-moon appears here, it is seen as full elsewhere. Should the people of Jambudvīpa see the moon at its first appearance, they all suppose it is the first day, forming the notion of a new moon; should they see the moon at its fullest, they suppose it is the fifteenth day, forming the notion of fullness. Yet this nature of the moon truly waxes and wanes not at all — it is only on account of Mount Sumeru that it appears to increase and decrease. Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same: within Jambudvīpa, he manifests, at one time, his own first birth, and, at another, his own parinirvāṇa. When he manifests his first taking birth, it is like the new moon; when he is seen to take his seven steps, it is like the moon on its second day; when he manifests entering the schoolroom, it is like the moon on its third day; when he manifests going forth from the household life, it is like the moon on its eighth day; when he sends forth the subtle light of great wisdom, able to destroy the countless hosts of Māra among beings, it is like the full moon on the fifteenth day. And when he manifests the thirty-two marks and eighty minor characteristics adorning himself, and then manifests entering parinirvāṇa, it is like an eclipse of the moon. Beings, in this way, see differently — some see a half-moon, some a full moon, some an eclipsed moon — yet this nature of the moon truly neither increases nor decreases, is never truly eclipsed or devoured at all, and remains forever the full moon. The Tathāgata's own body is just the same — and this is why he is called ever-abiding, without change."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like the full moon, appearing everywhere at once — in every place, every city and village, every mountain and marsh and body of water, in a well, in a pond, in a basin, in a pot, appearing in every one of them; and beings who travel a hundred leagues, or a hundred thousand leagues, see the moon always following them, and, in their confusion, ordinary and foolish people give rise to this false thought: 'I first saw this moon at my own house in the city — now I see it again here, in this empty marsh; is this the very same moon, or a different one?' Each forms his own idea of the moon's size and shape — some say it is as small as the mouth of a pot; others say it is as large as a cart-wheel; others say it is as vast as forty-nine leagues across. All of them see the moon's own light; some see it as a perfect circle, likened to a golden disc. This one nature of the moon is seen with every kind of different mark, by every kind of different being. Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same, appearing in the world: some gods and humans think to themselves, 'The Tathāgata now dwells right in front of me'; other beings likewise give rise to this same thought; some who are deaf or mute even see the Tathāgata as bearing the mark of deafness or muteness. Beings of every different kind, speaking every different language, all suppose that the Tathāgata speaks exactly their own language, and each likewise thinks, 'He dwells in my own house, receiving my own offerings.' Some beings see the Tathāgata's body as vast and boundless; others see it as small. Some see the Buddha in the form of a śrāvaka; others see him in the form of a pratyekabuddha. Every heterodox school likewise thinks, 'The Tathāgata now goes forth and studies the path within my own teaching.' And some beings think, 'The Tathāgata appears in the world now for my sake alone.' The Tathāgata's own true nature is likened to that moon — it is the Dharma-body itself, the unborn body. The body of skillful means, according with the world, manifests countless karmic causes and conditions from its own past deeds, manifesting the appearance of birth in every place, exactly like that moon. It is for this very reason that the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like Rāhu, the asura king, covering the moon with his hand: every person in the world says the moon is eclipsed, yet the asura king has, in truth, never actually eclipsed it at all. Because the asura merely blocks its light, the moon itself remains a perfect circle, suffering no loss whatsoever — it is merely his hand that blocks it, causing it not to appear. Once his hand is withdrawn, the world says the moon has been restored, and everyone says the moon has suffered greatly. Yet even a hundred thousand asura kings could never actually trouble it at all. The Tathāgata is just the same: he manifests a being who, in the Tathāgata's own presence, gives rise to a coarse and vicious mind, draws the Buddha's own blood, commits one of the five heinous crimes, and becomes an icchantika — manifesting, for the sake of beings in future ages, this destruction of the Saṅgha and severing of the Dharma, and this obstruction. Yet even a hundred thousand, countless māras could never truly draw the Tathāgata's own blood at all. Why is this? Because the Tathāgata's own body contains no flesh, no blood, no sinew, no marrow. The Tathāgata is, in truth, never actually harmed or destroyed at all, though every being supposes that the Dharma and the Saṅgha have been destroyed, and that the Tathāgata has been utterly extinguished. Yet the Tathāgata's own nature truly never changes, is never truly destroyed at all — he merely manifests in this way, according with the world. Furthermore, good son, it is like two people fighting: should one wound the other with a blade or staff, drawing blood, even to the point of death, without the thought of killing ever arising, such a karmic act is light, not grave. In the Tathāgata's own presence, there is likewise fundamentally no thought of killing at all — and though his blood is drawn, this karmic act is just the same, light and not grave. The Tathāgata, in this way, manifests the ripening of karma for the sake of converting beings in future ages."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a skilled physician who diligently teaches his son the very root of the medical art: 'This is a root-medicine, this is a stem-medicine, this is a medicine known by its color — you should learn well every kind of appearance there is.' His son, honoring his father's own instruction, studies diligently and comes to understand every medicine well. This physician, in time, reaches the end of his own life-span and dies, and his son, weeping and wailing, says: 'My father once taught me: the root-medicines are like this, the stem-medicines like this, the flower-medicines like this, their colors and forms like this.' The Tathāgata is just the same: for the sake of converting beings, he manifests the establishment of the precepts, teaching that one should uphold them and never transgress, committing none of the five heinous crimes, slandering neither the true Dharma nor becoming an icchantika. It is for the sake of those who would raise such matters in future ages that he manifests in this way, wishing to cause his monks, after the Buddha's own parinirvāṇa, to come to this understanding: 'This is the profound meaning of the scriptures; this is the mark of what is grave and what is light within the Vinaya; this is the Abhidharma's own analysis of the Dharma's every phrase' — just like that physician's own son."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like the moon, known by people to be eclipsed once every six months — yet the gods above see the moon eclipsed within the space of a single instant. Why is this? Because a single day in that heaven is so much longer, and a day among humans so much shorter. Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same: gods and humans alike suppose the Tathāgata's own life-span to be short, exactly as those gods see the moon eclipsed again and again within a single instant. And the Tathāgata, within a single instant, manifests hundreds of thousands, millions of parinirvāṇas, cutting off the māra of affliction, the māra of the aggregates, and the māra of death — and this is why hundreds of thousands, millions of heavenly māras all believe that the Tathāgata has entered parinirvāṇa. And he further manifests countless hundreds of thousands of causes and conditions from his own past deeds, according with the world's every different nature — manifesting in this way, boundlessly, immeasurably, beyond conceptual thought. This is why the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change. Furthermore, good son, it is like the bright moon, which beings delight to see — this is why the moon is named 'delighted-in.' Should beings themselves be full of craving, anger, and delusion, they could never be called 'delighted-in' in this way. The Tathāgata is just the same: his own nature is purely wholesome, pure and undefiled, and is for this reason most rightly called 'delighted-in.' Beings who delight in the Dharma gaze upon him without ever tiring; those of a wicked mind take no pleasure in beholding him at all. It is for this very reason that the Tathāgata is likened to the bright moon."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan sorts this cluster into six distinct points rather than one repeated idea: the moon's rising and setting (the Tathāgata's own body knows neither beginning nor ending, though he shows birth and death); its waxing and waning (his own body knows no increase or decrease, though he shows growth from infancy to enlightenment); its single light seen everywhere at once, differently by every viewer (his own body admits no real difference, though beings encounter him however their own capacity allows); Rāhu's hand across its face (his own body suffers no real harm, though he permits the appearance of injury); the physician's death and his son's grief (his own body knows no real loss, though the precepts he leaves behind function, after his parinirvāṇa, exactly as a father's teaching does for an orphaned son); and the mismatch between heavenly and human time (his own body knows no real shortening, though beings whose own lives are short perceive him as living only briefly). On the asura simile specifically, Huiyuan is precise that the Buddha's body is said to contain no flesh, blood, sinew, or marrow at all — the point is not that the wound was faked, but that a body of this kind was never, in the first place, the kind of thing that blood could actually be drawn from; what beings interpret as grave harm to the Saṅgha and the Dharma is, on his reading, no more able to reach the Tathāgata's own nature than a hand blocking moonlight is able to touch the moon.

Three Suns, and the Light in Every Pore

Chinese Source

「復次善男子，譬如日出有三時異，謂春夏冬，冬日則短，春日處中，夏日極長。如來亦爾，於此三千大千世界為短壽者，及諸聲聞示現壽短，斯等見已，咸謂如來壽命短促，喻如冬日。為諸菩薩示現中壽，若至一劫若減一劫，喻如春日。唯佛覩佛，其壽無量，喻如夏日。善男子！如來所說方等大乘微密之教，示現世間，雨大法雨，於未來世，若有人能護持是典，開示分別利益眾生，當知是輩真是菩薩，喻如盛夏，天降甘雨。若有聲聞緣覺之人，聞佛如來微密之教，喻如冬日多遇冷患。菩薩之人，若聞如是微密教誨，如來常住性無變易，喻如春日，萌牙開敷。而如來性，實無長短，為世間故，示現如是，即是諸佛真實法性。」

「復次善男子！譬如眾星，晝則不現，而人皆謂晝星滅沒，其實不沒，所以不現，日光映故。如來亦爾，聲聞緣覺不能得見，喻如世人不見晝星。復次善男子！譬如陰闇，日月不現，愚夫謂言日月失沒，而是日月實不失沒。如來正法滅盡之時，三寶現沒亦復如是，非為永滅，是故當知如來常住無有變易。何以故？三寶真性，不為諸垢之所染故。」

「復次善男子！譬如黑月，彗星夜現，其明炎熾暫出還沒，眾生見已，生不祥想。諸辟支佛亦復如是，出無佛世，眾生見已，皆謂如來真實滅度，生憂悲想；而如來身實不滅度，如彼日月無有滅沒。」

「復次善男子！譬如日出，眾霧悉除。此大涅槃微妙經典亦復如是，出興於世，若有眾生一經耳者，悉能滅除一切諸惡、無間罪業。是大涅槃甚深境界不可思議，善說如來微密之性，以是義故，諸善男子、善女人等，應於如來生常住心，無有變易，正法不斷，僧寶不滅，是故應當多修方便，勤學是典，是人不久當得成於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。是故此經名為無量功德所成，亦名菩提不可窮盡，以不盡故，故得稱為大般涅槃；有善光故，猶如夏日，身無邊故，名大涅槃。」

「復次善男子！如日月光，諸明中最，一切諸明，所不能及。大涅槃光，亦復如是，於諸契經，三昧光明最為殊勝，諸經三昧所有光明，所不能及。何以故？大涅槃光，能入眾生諸毛孔故。眾生雖無菩提之心，而能為作菩提因緣，是故復名大般涅槃。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, it is like the sun's rising, which differs across three seasons — spring, summer, and winter: the winter sun is short, the spring sun middling, the summer sun exceedingly long. The Tathāgata is just the same: within this billion-world universe, for those of short life-span, and for śrāvakas, he manifests a short life-span — and, seeing this, they all suppose that the Tathāgata's own life-span is brief, like the winter sun. For bodhisattvas, he manifests a middling life-span, reaching a full kalpa, or something less than a kalpa, like the spring sun. Only a buddha beholds a buddha's own life-span as truly boundless, like the summer sun. Good son, the profound and subtle teaching of the Vaipulya Great Vehicle that the Tathāgata teaches, manifesting in the world, rains down the great rain of the Dharma; in future ages, should there be anyone able to protect and uphold this scripture, opening it up and expounding it for the benefit of beings, know that such people are truly bodhisattvas, like the sweet rain the heavens send down in the height of summer. Should any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha hear the Tathāgata's own profound and subtle teaching, it is like the winter sun, bringing on a season of cold and affliction. Should any bodhisattva hear this profound and subtle instruction — that the Tathāgata abides permanently, his own nature without change — it is like the spring sun, causing the sprouts to open and bloom. Yet the Tathāgata's own nature truly knows neither long nor short at all; it is only for the sake of the world that he manifests in this way — this alone is the true Dharma-nature of every buddha."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like the stars in the sky, invisible by day, though people all say the daytime stars have vanished — yet, in truth, they have not vanished at all; it is only that they cannot be seen, being outshone by the sun's own light. The Tathāgata is just the same: śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas are unable to see him, exactly as ordinary people cannot see the stars by day. Furthermore, good son, it is like the sun and moon, invisible in a time of darkness — the foolish say the sun and moon have been lost, yet the sun and moon have, in truth, never been lost at all. When the Tathāgata's own true Dharma finally comes to be extinguished, the Three Jewels' own apparent disappearance is just the same — never truly extinguished at all. One should therefore know that the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change. Why is this? Because the true nature of the Three Jewels is never stained by any defilement at all."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a comet appearing by night, on a moonless night, its brilliant blaze appearing only briefly before vanishing again — beings, seeing this, form the notion of an ill omen. Pratyekabuddhas are just the same: appearing in an age without a buddha, beings, seeing this, suppose that the Tathāgata has truly and finally passed away, giving rise to grief and sorrow — yet the Tathāgata's own body has, in truth, never truly passed away at all, exactly as the sun and moon are never truly extinguished."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like the sun rising, dispelling every mist entirely. This Mahāparinirvāṇa, this subtle and wondrous scripture, is just the same: appearing and arising in the world, should any being simply let it pass once through his own ear, it is able to remove entirely every evil, every act of unremitting karma. This Mahāparinirvāṇa's own profound domain is beyond conceptual thought, teaching well the Tathāgata's own profound and subtle nature — and it is for this very reason that every good son and good daughter should give rise to the thought that the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change, that the true Dharma is never severed, that the jewel of the Saṅgha is never extinguished; and should therefore diligently cultivate skillful means, studying this scripture with all diligence — such a person will, before long, come to accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening. This is why this scripture is called the accomplishment of immeasurable virtue, and is also called the inexhaustibility of bodhi itself — and, because it is inexhaustible, it is rightly called Mahāparinirvāṇa; because it possesses this wholesome light, like the summer sun, and its own body is boundless, it is called Mahāparinirvāṇa."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like the light of the sun and moon, foremost among every light, a light no other light can ever match. The light of Mahāparinirvāṇa is just the same: among every scripture, its own light of samādhi is most excellent of all, a light no other scripture's own samādhi-light can ever match. Why is this? Because the light of Mahāparinirvāṇa is able to enter every one of a being's own pores. Though a being may be entirely without the mind of bodhi, this light is nonetheless able to become, for him, a cause and condition for bodhi — and this is why it is called Mahāparinirvāṇa once again."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the sun-simile's three seasons as tracking exactly which audience is present, not any real change in the sun itself: a short manifest life-span for short-lived beings and śrāvakas, a middling one (a kalpa, or nearly one) for bodhisattvas, and only a buddha beholding a buddha's own truly boundless span — the same body of teaching read as cold and threatening by the two lesser vehicles and as spring's own first bloom by bodhisattvas hearing the identical words. He treats the stars-by-day and sun-and-moon-in-darkness similes as making one continuous point about two different audiences of the same disappearance: śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas cannot see the Tathāgata for the same reason the stars cannot be seen at noon — not absence, but being outshone; and ordinary people at the time the true Dharma seems to vanish are in the position of someone in total darkness who cannot find the sun and moon, mistaking invisibility for destruction in both cases. The section's real hinge, on his reading, is the closing move from "this scripture's own light surpasses every other light" to "this light enters every being's pores and becomes a cause for bodhi even without bodhicitta already present" — a claim so sweeping that the fascicle immediately has Kāśyapa press on whether it can really be unconditional.

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught, that the light of Mahāparinirvāṇa enters every being's own pores, and that this light is able to become, for a being entirely without the mind of bodhi, a cause for bodhi — this does not seem right. Why is this? World-Honored One, if this light, entering the body of one who has committed one of the four gravest offenses, or one of the five heinous crimes, or an icchantika, becomes for them a cause of bodhi in just the same way, what difference could there then be between such people and those who purely uphold the precepts and cultivate every wholesome practice? And if there is no difference, why does the Tathāgata teach the meaning of the four reliances at all? World-Honored One, and further, as the Buddha has said, should any being simply let this Mahāparinirvāṇa pass once through his own ear, he thereby comes to cut off every affliction — yet how is it that the Tathāgata has earlier taught that there are those who, having given rise to the resolve for bodhi in the presence of buddhas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, still hear this Mahāparinirvāṇa without understanding its meaning? And if they do not understand its meaning, how could they possibly cut off every affliction at all?"

The Buddha said: "Good son, excepting the icchantika alone, every other being, having heard this scripture, is entirely able to make of it a cause and condition for bodhi. Whoever has this scripture's own sound and light enter his own pores will, without fail, in time, attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. Why is this? Only one who is able to make offerings and show reverence to countless buddhas comes to hear this Mahāparinirvāṇa at all; a person of scant merit does not come to hear it. Why is this? Only a person of great virtue is able to hear so great a matter as this; a lowly, petty person does not come to hear it. What, then, is meant by 'great'? It refers to the profound secret treasury of every buddha, namely buddha-nature itself — and it is for this very reason that it is called a great matter."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, how does one who has not yet given rise to the mind of bodhi come to attain a cause for bodhi?" The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "Should anyone, having heard this Mahāparinirvāṇa, say, 'I have no use for giving rise to the mind of bodhi,' slandering the true Dharma, such a person will, in a dream, see the form of a rākṣasa, and, terrified at heart, the rākṣasa will say to him: 'Now then, good son — if you do not give rise to the mind of bodhi this instant, I shall take your life.' Terrified, this person, upon waking, at once gives rise to the mind of bodhi. Should this person then die, and be reborn in one of the three evil destinies, or among humans or gods, he will, in time, come to recall this mind of bodhi once again — know that such a person is a great bodhisattva-mahāsattva. It is for this very reason, through the awesome, divine power of this Mahāparinirvāṇa, that it is able to cause even one who has not yet given rise to the mind of bodhi to attain a cause for bodhi. Good son, this is what is meant by the causes and conditions of a bodhisattva's own resolve — it is never without cause and condition at all, and it is for this very reason that this wondrous scripture of the Great Vehicle is truly what the Buddha himself has taught."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a great mass of clouds rising in the sky and raining down upon the earth: withered trees, stony mountains, high plateaus, and mounds cannot hold this water, which flows down instead into the low-lying fields, filling every pond and reservoir, benefiting countless beings. This Mahāparinirvāṇa, this subtle and wondrous scripture, is just the same: raining down the great rain of the Dharma, moistening every being universally — only that the icchantika alone could never possibly give rise to the mind of bodhi through it. Furthermore, good son, it is like a scorched seed: though it meets with sweet rain for hundreds of thousands of millions of kalpas, it will never sprout at all — for a sprout to grow from it could not possibly be so. The icchantika is just the same: though he hears this Mahāparinirvāṇa, this subtle and wondrous scripture, he could never possibly make the sprout of bodhicitta grow at all — for him to give rise to it could not possibly be so. Why is this? Because this person has entirely destroyed every wholesome root, like that scorched seed, unable ever again to grow the sprout of bodhi's own root. Furthermore, good son, it is like a bright pearl placed in muddy water: through the pearl's own awesome power, the water is thereby made clear — yet cast into mud itself, it cannot make the mud clear. This Mahāparinirvāṇa, this subtle and wondrous scripture, is just the same: placed within the muddy water of any other being's five heinous crimes or four gravest offenses, it can still bring clarity, still give rise to the mind of bodhi; but cast into the mud of the icchantika, not for hundreds of thousands of millions of years could it ever bring clarity, or give rise to the mind of bodhi. Why is this? Because this icchantika has destroyed every wholesome root, and is simply not a vessel fit for it at all. Even were such a person to listen to and receive this Mahāparinirvāṇa for hundreds of thousands of millions of years, he could never give rise to the mind of bodhi at all. Why is this? Because he is without any wholesome mind whatsoever."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a medicinal tree called the Medicine King, most excellent among every medicine. Whether mixed with whey, or honey, or ghee, or water, or milk, whether taken as powder or as a pill, whether applied to a wound, or used to fumigate the body, or applied to the eyes, whether merely seen or merely smelled, it is able to remove every one of a being's illnesses. And this medicinal tree never thinks to itself: 'Should anyone take my root, he should not also take my leaves; should he take my leaves, he should not also take my root; should he take my trunk, he should not also take my bark; should he take my bark, he should not also take my trunk.' Though this tree never gives rise to any such thought, it is nonetheless able to remove every illness and suffering. Good son, this Mahāparinirvāṇa, this subtle and wondrous scripture, is just the same: able to remove every being's unwholesome karma, the four pārājika offenses, the five heinous crimes, every evil there is, whether inward or outward — and whoever has not yet given rise to the mind of bodhi is thereby enabled to give rise to it. Why is this? Because this wondrous scripture is the king among every scripture, just as that medicinal tree is the king among every medicine. Whether one cultivates this Mahāparinirvāṇa or does not, should one merely hear this scripture's own name, and, hearing it, revere it with faith, every grave illness of affliction is thereby entirely removed — only that it is unable to establish the icchantika securely in unsurpassed, complete awakening. Just as that wondrous medicine, though able to cure every kind of grave illness, cannot cure one who is certainly about to die."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a person with a wound on his hand who then handles poison — the poison thereupon enters him; but should there be no wound, the poison could not enter at all. The icchantika is just the same: lacking any cause for bodhi, like one with no wound, the poison cannot enter him at all. What is meant here by the wound is precisely the cause and condition for unsurpassed bodhi; what is meant by the poison is precisely this foremost, wondrous medicine; and one whose skin is entirely unwounded is the icchantika. Furthermore, good son, it is like vajra, which nothing can destroy, and which is itself able to destroy every other thing — excepting only a tortoise's shell and a white ram's horn. This Mahāparinirvāṇa, this subtle and wondrous scripture, is just the same: entirely able to establish countless beings securely upon the path of bodhi — only unable to make the icchantika establish a cause for bodhi."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like purslane, the sāla-winged tree, and the nikura tree, which, though their branches and stems are cut, continue to grow back just as before — unlike the tāla palm, which, once cut, never grows again. Beings are just the same: should they come to hear this Mahāparinirvāṇa, then, even having committed one of the four gravest offenses or one of the five heinous crimes, they are nonetheless still able to give rise to a cause and condition for bodhi. The icchantika is not so: though he may come to listen to and receive this wondrous scripture, he is unable to give rise to any cause for the path of bodhi at all. Furthermore, good son, it is like the khadira tree, or the tinduka tree, which, once cut, never grow again, and like every scorched seed. The icchantika is just the same: though he may come to hear this Mahāparinirvāṇa, he is unable to give rise to a cause and condition for bodhi, exactly like a scorched seed. Furthermore, good son, it is like a great rain, which never remains suspended in the sky at all. This Mahāparinirvāṇa, this subtle and wondrous scripture, is just the same: raining down the Dharma universally, it is simply unable to remain within the icchantika — for this icchantika's entire being is sealed shut, dense and impenetrable throughout, like vajra itself, admitting nothing from outside at all."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan counts ten similes here but reads them as eight distinct points arranged in two mirrored waves rather than ten independent illustrations. The first four — the rain that cannot collect on bare stone, the scorched seed, the pearl that clears muddy water but not mud itself, the medicine-king tree that cures everything but a fatal illness — state the exclusion from four different angles. The last four then restate those same four points in reverse order: the wound-and-poison simile mirrors the medicine tree (both turn on having a receptive opening at all, not on the poison's or medicine's own strength); the vajra mirrors the pearl (both turn on something too dense and resistant to be worked on, tortoise shell and goat horn standing in for the icchantika's own mud); the regrowing grasses against the un-regrowing tāla palm mirror the scorched seed (a wholesome root either can or categorically cannot regrow); and the rain that never lingers in open sky mirrors the opening cloud simile itself. The structure carries a claim easy to miss on a first read: nowhere does this list actually rank the icchantika as more severely sinful than the four gravest offenses or the five heinous crimes — those offenders are repeatedly named as still reachable, still able to become "muddy water" the pearl can clear. The icchantika's exclusion, on Huiyuan's reading, is never a matter of degree at all; every simile turns on a structural absence of any receiving capacity whatsoever — a seed with nothing left inside to sprout, a body with no wound for the medicine to enter through — not on committing a worse class of wrong.

The One Who Cannot See What He Has Done

Chinese Source

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如佛說偈：

「『不見善不作，　唯見惡可作。
是處可怖畏，　猶如險惡道。』

「世尊！如是所說，有何等義？」

佛言：「善男子！不見者，謂不見佛性。善者，即是阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。不作者，所謂不能親近善友。唯見者，見無因果。惡者，謂謗方等大乘經典。可作者，謂一闡提說無方等。以是義故，一闡提輩，無心趣向清淨善法。何等善法？謂涅槃也。趣涅槃者，謂能修習賢善之行；而一闡提無賢善行，是故不能趣向涅槃。是處可畏，謂謗正法。誰應怖畏？所謂智者。何以故？以謗法者，無有善心及方便故，險惡道者謂諸行也。」

迦葉復言：「如佛所說：

「『云何見所作？　云何得善法？
何處不怖畏，　如王夷坦道？』

「是義何謂？」

佛言：「善男子！見所作者，發露諸惡，從生死際所作諸惡悉皆發露，至無至處，以是義故，是處無畏。喻如人王，所遊正路，其中盜賊，悉皆逃走。如是發露一切諸惡，悉滅無餘。復次不見所作者，謂一闡提，所作眾惡而不自見，是一闡提憍慢心故，雖多作惡，於是事中初無怖畏，以是義故不得涅槃，喻如獼猴捉水中月。

善男子！假使一切無量眾生，一時成於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提已，此諸如來亦復不見彼一闡提成於菩提，以是義故名不見所作。又復不見誰之所作，所謂不見如來所作。佛為眾生說有佛性，一闡提輩流轉生死，不能知見，以是義故，名為不見如來所作。又一闡提見於如來畢竟涅槃，謂真無常，猶如燈滅，膏油俱盡。何以故？是人惡業不虧損故。若有菩薩所作善業，迴向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提時，一闡提輩雖復毀呰，破壞不信，然諸菩薩猶故施與，欲共成於無上之道。何以故？諸佛法爾。

「作惡不即受，　如乳即成酪。
猶灰覆火上，　愚者輕蹈之。

「一闡提者名為無目，是故不見阿羅漢道。如阿羅漢不行生死險惡之道，以無目故誹謗方等，不欲修習。如阿羅漢勤修慈心，一闡提輩不修方等，亦復如是。若人說言：『我今不信聲聞經典，信受大乘，讀誦解說，是故我今即是菩薩。一切眾生悉有佛性，以佛性故，眾生身中即有十力、三十二相、八十種好。我之所說，不異佛說。汝今與我俱破無量諸惡煩惱，如破水瓶，以破結故，即得見於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』是人雖作如是演說，其心實不信有佛性，為利養故隨文而說，如是說者名為惡人。如是惡人，不速受果，如乳成酪。譬如王使，善能談論，巧於方便，奉命他國，寧喪身命，終不匿王所說言教。智者亦爾，於凡夫中不惜身命，要必宣說大乘方等如來祕藏，一切眾生皆有佛性。善男子！有一闡提作羅漢像，住於空處，誹謗方等大乘經典。諸凡夫人見已皆謂，真阿羅漢是大菩薩摩訶薩。是一闡提、惡比丘輩，住阿蘭若處，壞阿蘭若法，見他得利，心生嫉妬，作如是言：『所有方等大乘經典悉是天魔波旬所說。』亦說如來是無常法，毀滅正法，破壞眾僧。復作是言：『波旬所說，非善順說。』作是宣說邪惡之法，是人作惡不即受報如乳成酪。灰覆火上，愚輕蹈之。如是人者謂一闡提。是故當知大乘方等微妙經典，必定清淨，如摩尼珠投之濁水，水即為清，大乘經典亦復如是。」

「復次善男子！譬如蓮花為日所照，無不開敷。一切眾生亦復如是，若得見聞大涅槃日，未發心者皆悉發心為菩提因，是故我說大涅槃光所入毛孔，必為妙因。彼一闡提雖有佛性，而為無量罪垢所纏，不能得出，如蠶處繭，以是業緣，不能生於菩提妙因，流轉生死無有窮已。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught in verse:

'Seeing no good to be done, seeing only evil that can be done —
this is a fearful place, like a steep and evil road.'

World-Honored One, what is the meaning of this teaching?"

The Buddha said: "Good son, 'seeing no good' means not seeing buddha-nature. 'The good' is precisely unsurpassed, complete awakening. 'Nothing to be done' means being unable to draw near to a good spiritual friend. 'Seeing only' means seeing that there is no cause and no result at all. 'Evil' means slandering the Vaipulya scriptures of the Great Vehicle. 'That can be done' means the icchantika's own claim that there are no Vaipulya scriptures at all. For this very reason, the icchantika has no mind inclined toward the pure and wholesome Dharma. What is this wholesome Dharma? It is nirvāṇa itself. To incline toward nirvāṇa means being able to cultivate virtuous and wholesome conduct — and the icchantika, having no virtuous and wholesome conduct, is thereby unable to incline toward nirvāṇa at all. 'This is a fearful place' means slandering the true Dharma. Who should fear this? The wise. Why is this? Because whoever slanders the Dharma is without any wholesome mind or skillful means at all. 'The steep and evil road' means conditioned activity itself."

Kāśyapa said further: "As the Buddha has taught:

'How does one see what has been done? How does one attain the wholesome Dharma?
Where is the place free of fear, like a king's own level, open road?'

What is the meaning of this?"

The Buddha said: "Good son, 'seeing what has been done' means confessing every evil openly — every evil act committed from the very edge of birth and death is thereby confessed in full, arriving at the place of no arrival at all, and it is for this reason that this is a place free of fear. It is like a king's own level road, upon which every bandit flees at once. Confessing every evil in this way, it is entirely extinguished, with nothing left behind. Furthermore, 'not seeing what has been done' refers to the icchantika, who does not see for himself the many evils he has done; because of this icchantika's own arrogant mind, though he commits much evil, he never once feels any fear over it at all — and it is for this very reason that he cannot attain nirvāṇa, exactly like a monkey grasping at the moon reflected in water.

Good son, even should every one of countless beings, without exception, at one and the same moment, accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening, not one of these very tathāgatas would see that icchantika accomplish bodhi along with them — and it is for this very reason that this is called 'not seeing what has been done.' And there is a further sense of 'not seeing whose doing' — namely, not seeing the Tathāgata's own doing. The Buddha teaches beings that buddha-nature exists, yet the icchantika, turning through birth and death, is unable to know or see this at all — and it is for this reason that this is called 'not seeing the Tathāgata's own doing.' And further, the icchantika, seeing the Tathāgata's own final nirvāṇa, takes it to be truly impermanent, like a lamp going out once its oil is entirely spent. Why is this? Because this person's own unwholesome karma remains undiminished. Should any bodhisattva's own wholesome deeds be dedicated toward unsurpassed, complete awakening, the icchantika, though he reviles it, destroys faith in it, and does not believe in it, is nonetheless still given a share by the bodhisattvas, who wish to accomplish the unsurpassed path together with him. Why is this? Because this is simply how every buddha's own Dharma works.

'Committing evil does not bring immediate result, no more than milk becomes curds at once —
like fire covered over by ash, which the fool carelessly treads upon.'

The icchantika is called one without eyes, and this is why he cannot see the path of the arhat. Just as the arhat does not walk the steep and evil road of birth and death, so, being without eyes, the icchantika slanders the Vaipulya scriptures, having no wish to cultivate them. Just as the arhat diligently cultivates a mind of loving-kindness, the icchantika does not cultivate the Vaipulya scriptures — this too is just the same. Should anyone say: 'I now place no faith in the śrāvaka scriptures, but believe in and receive the Great Vehicle, reading, reciting, and explaining it — and so I am now a bodhisattva. Every being without exception possesses buddha-nature, and, because of this buddha-nature, the ten powers, the thirty-two marks, and the eighty minor characteristics already exist within every being's own body. What I teach is no different from what the Buddha teaches. You and I, together, now break apart countless evil afflictions, as one shatters a water-jar — and, through breaking these fetters, one thereby comes to see unsurpassed, complete awakening' — though such a person speaks in this way, his heart, in truth, does not believe that buddha-nature exists at all; he speaks the words only for the sake of personal gain, following the text without believing it. Such a speaker is called a wicked person. Such a wicked person does not receive his result swiftly, no more than milk becomes curds at once. It is like a king's own envoy, skilled at speech, adept in every skillful means, sent on a mission to another country, who would rather lose his own life than ever conceal the king's own message. The wise are just the same: among ordinary people, sparing not even their own lives, they must proclaim the Vaipulya scriptures of the Great Vehicle, the Tathāgata's own secret treasury, that every being without exception possesses buddha-nature. Good son, there is an icchantika who takes on the form of an arhat, dwelling in solitude, slandering the Vaipulya scriptures of the Great Vehicle. Every ordinary person, seeing him, supposes him a true arhat, a great bodhisattva-mahāsattva. This icchantika, this wicked monk, dwelling in a forest hermitage, corrupts the very practice of the forest hermitage; seeing another gain some benefit, his heart gives rise to envy, and he says: 'Every one of these Vaipulya scriptures of the Great Vehicle is nothing but what the heavenly Māra Pāpīyas himself has taught.' He also teaches that the Tathāgata is an impermanent dharma, destroying the true Dharma, corrupting the assembled Saṅgha. He says further: 'What Pāpīyas teaches is not a wholesome, compliant teaching' — proclaiming, in this way, a wicked and evil teaching. Such a person, committing evil, does not receive his punishment at once, no more than milk becomes curds at once, like fire covered over by ash, which the fool carelessly treads upon. A person of this kind is called an icchantika. One should therefore know that the Vaipulya scriptures of the Great Vehicle are, without fail, pure — just as a maṇi jewel cast into muddy water at once makes the water clear, the scriptures of the Great Vehicle are just the same."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a lotus flower touched by the sun's light — there is not one that fails to open and bloom. Every being is just the same: should he come to see and hear the sun of this Mahāparinirvāṇa, whoever has not yet given rise to the mind of bodhi will, without exception, give rise to it as a cause for bodhi — and this is why I have taught that the light of Mahāparinirvāṇa, entering a being's pores, must always become a wondrous cause. Though that icchantika possesses buddha-nature, he remains bound by countless defilements of wrongdoing, unable to break free of them, like a silkworm trapped within its own cocoon — and, through this very karmic condition, he is unable to give rise to the wondrous cause of bodhi, turning through birth and death without end."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the first verse as answered word by word rather than as a single mood to be paraphrased: "seeing no good" is specifically not seeing buddha-nature, "the good" is specifically unsurpassed awakening, "nothing to be done" is specifically the inability to approach a good spiritual friend, and so through each phrase — a technique that keeps the icchantika's condition from collapsing into vague wickedness and ties it instead to this fascicle's own running concerns. He then reads "not seeing what has been done" as carrying two distinct senses rather than one: first, the icchantika's own inability to see and feel remorse for his own wrongdoing (the direct cause of his fearlessness, illustrated by a monkey clutching at the moon's reflection instead of the moon itself); second, and more strikingly, that not even the buddhas of some future moment when every other being has become enlightened together would see the icchantika among them — a claim about the shape of the future, not a further insult. He also flags the verse recycled from Part 8's milk-and-curds causality argument ("committing evil does not bring immediate result, no more than milk becomes curds at once") as doing real work here: it explains why the slandering hypocrite — Huiyuan notes the text gives two mirror-image cases, one posing as a quiet forest arhat while secretly denouncing the Vaipulya teachings, the other loudly proclaiming universal buddha-nature while privately believing none of it — seems to suffer no immediate consequence; the ripening is simply delayed, not absent. Perhaps most striking, on Huiyuan's reading, is the detail that bodhisattvas keep dedicating their own merit toward the icchantika's eventual awakening regardless, "because this is simply how every buddha's own Dharma works" — a line that keeps the tension this collection has tracked since Part 5, between the icchantika's structural incurability and the sūtra's own insistence that no being is finally excluded from compassion, open rather than resolved.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, it is like the utpala lotus, the padma lotus, the kumuda lotus, and the puṇḍarīka lotus, all growing out of the mud, yet never in the end stained by that very mud. Should any being cultivate this Mahāparinirvāṇa, this subtle and wondrous scripture, he is just the same: though affliction remains, he is never in the end stained by this affliction at all. Why is this? Because he knows the power of the Tathāgata's own nature and marks. Good son, it is like a certain country, rich in cool, clear wind, which, touching every one of a being's own pores, is able to remove every torment of stifling heat. This scripture of the Great Vehicle, this Mahāparinirvāṇa, is just the same: entering universally into every being's own pores, becoming a wondrous cause and condition for bodhi — excepting only the icchantika. Why is this? Because he is simply not a vessel fit for the Dharma.

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a skilled physician who understands eight kinds of medicine, curing every illness — excepting only one who is certainly about to die. Every scripture, every dhyāna, every samādhi is just the same: able to treat every illness of craving, anger, and delusion, every affliction, able to draw out the poisoned thorn, the very arrow of affliction — yet unable to treat one who has committed one of the four gravest offenses or one of the five heinous crimes. Good son, there is, further, a skilled physician who surpasses these eight kinds of technique, able to remove every illness and suffering a being has — only unable to treat one who is certainly about to die. This Mahāparinirvāṇa, this scripture of the Great Vehicle, is just the same: able to remove every affliction a being has, establishing him securely in the Tathāgata's own pure and wondrous cause, causing whoever has not yet given rise to the mind of bodhi to give rise to it — excepting only the icchantika, who is certainly about to die.

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a skilled physician who is able, with a wondrous medicine, to treat the blind, causing them to see the sun and moon, the stars, and every form and color there is — only unable to treat one who has been blind from birth. This scripture of the Great Vehicle, this Mahāparinirvāṇa, is just the same: able to open the eye of wisdom for śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, establishing them securely amid the boundless, immeasurable scriptures of the Great Vehicle; whoever has not yet given rise to the mind of bodhi, even one who has committed one of the four gravest offenses or one of the five heinous crimes, is entirely able to be caused to give rise to the mind of bodhi — excepting only the icchantika, blind from birth. Should a poor and foolish person be unwilling to take them, the skilled physician, out of compassion, brings this person back to his own house and forces him to take the medicine — and, through the medicine's own power, his affliction is thereby removed. When a woman is in labor and the afterbirth will not come out, he gives her medicine to take, and, once taken, it comes out at once, causing the infant, too, to be safe and free of harm. This scripture of the Great Vehicle, this Mahāparinirvāṇa, is just the same: wherever it reaches, even reaching into a single house, it is able to remove a being's countless afflictions, and whoever has not yet given rise to the mind of bodhi, even one who has committed one of the four gravest offenses or one of the five heinous crimes, is entirely caused to give rise to it — excepting only the icchantika."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, committing one of the four gravest offenses or one of the five heinous crimes is called the very gravest of wrongs, like cutting off the crown of a tāla palm, which never grows back again — how could beings of this kind, who have not yet given rise to the mind of bodhi, ever come to attain a cause for bodhi?" The Buddha said: "Good son, should such beings, in a dream, dream of falling into hell and receiving every suffering there, they at once give rise to a remorseful mind: 'How pitiful — we have brought this very crime upon ourselves; should I now be freed of this crime, I shall surely give rise to the mind of bodhi; what I now see is the very worst of evils.' Upon waking from this, he thereby comes to know that the true Dharma bears great fruit — just as that infant, gradually growing up, constantly thinks to himself: 'This physician is most excellent, well understanding every remedy. While I was still in the womb, he gave medicine to my mother, and, through this medicine, her body found ease — and it is through this very cause that my own life was preserved. How extraordinary, that my mother endured such great suffering, carrying me in her womb for a full ten months, and, once I was born, cleared away what was wet, removed what was unclean, nursed and raised me, and watched over my body with such care. For this very reason, I should repay this kindness, caring for her with a warm countenance, attending upon her, and making offerings in accordance with her wishes.' One who has committed one of the four gravest offenses or one of the five heinous crimes, at the moment of death, calling to mind this Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle, though he then falls into hell, among the animals, the hungry ghosts, or is reborn among the gods or humans, will still find that this scripture becomes, for him too, a cause for bodhi — excepting only the icchantika."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a skilled physician, and his physician son, whose knowledge is so profound it surpasses every other physician, well understanding the unsurpassed mantras for removing poison — for wicked poisonous snakes, for nāgas, for vipers alike — using these mantras and medicines to render them harmless, and further using this same medicine to treat leather sandals, so that these sandals, touching any poisonous creature, dissolve its poison at once — excepting only one poison, called the Great Nāga. This scripture of the Great Vehicle, this Mahāparinirvāṇa, is just the same: should any being have committed one of the four gravest offenses or one of the five heinous crimes, it is entirely able to dissolve this, establishing him in bodhi, exactly as the medicine-treated sandal is able to dissolve every poison; whoever has not yet given rise to the mind of bodhi is thereby able to give rise to it, established securely upon the path of bodhi. It is through this Mahāparinirvāṇa's own awesome, divine medicine that beings are caused to find peace and ease — excepting only the Great Nāga, the icchantika."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a person who smears a great drum with every kind of poison, and strikes it before a great assembly so that its sound resounds — and though no one there has any wish to hear it, all who hear it die at once, excepting only the one person who does not die an untimely death. This scripture of the Great Vehicle, this Mahāparinirvāṇa, is just the same: wherever it is heard, among any assembly engaged in any practice, every craving, anger, and delusion there is entirely extinguished; and though there may be those among them with no thought of attending to it at all, through this Mahāparinirvāṇa's own causal power, affliction is nonetheless dissolved, and its fetters extinguished of themselves. Whoever has committed one of the four gravest offenses or one of the five heinous crimes, having heard this scripture, likewise makes of it a cause and condition for unsurpassed bodhi, gradually cutting off affliction — excepting only the icchantika, who alone does not die this untimely death."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan groups this cluster of ten similes into four paired contrasts rather than reading them as one long, repetitive list. A first pair contrasts a lesser physician's eight remedies (the lesser vehicles' own scriptures and meditations, unable to reach the four gravest offenses or five heinous crimes) against a greater physician who has gone beyond those eight (this sūtra, which reaches even those, though still not the icchantika) — small measured against great. A second pair contrasts generating understanding (the physician curing blindness) against generating actual practice (the physician forcing medicine on an unwilling patient, and easing a difficult birth) — insight against action taken on it. A third pair contrasts the teaching empowering realization (the antidote-treated sandal) against realization empowering the teaching itself (the poisoned drum, whose sound kills — that is, converts — even someone with no wish at all to hear it, purely through the sūtra's own causal power). A fourth, unlisted here, will contrast those easy to convert against those hard to convert. On Kāśyapa's challenge specifically — how anyone guilty of a tāla-palm-severing crime could still find a cause for bodhi — Huiyuan reads the Buddha's answer as offering two different timescales rather than one argument: the hell-dream supplies an immediate benefit, fear tipping directly into remorse and insight into the Dharma's own power, while the growing infant's gratitude toward a mother and physician he could not have understood at the time supplies a benefit that only becomes legible in retrospect, once maturity allows the debt to be recognized and repaid.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan notes a quiet shift of register across this closing stretch of the physician cluster: the first eight similes cast the sūtra's own teaching itself as the physician, but the final two switch to casting the buddhas and bodhisattvas who transmit it as the physician instead — a reminder, on his reading, that the Dharma's power to heal is never independent of the people who actually carry and speak it. He reads the water-land-mountain-stream simile as encoding this sūtra's own standing teaching order rather than a random trio of images: water (the body as a bubble) teaches impermanence, land (the body as a plantain tree, all husk and no solid core) teaches its lack of substance, and the mountain stream's own empty hollow teaches no-self — the very sequence of preliminary contemplations a teacher gives disciples before ever mentioning the Tathāgata's own secret treasury, exactly matching the milk-and-bitter-medicine logic from Part 7: the negative teaching comes first, by design, and only once it has done its work does the positive teaching of permanence follow. The purging-king simile, read alongside the poisoned drum from the previous section, completes a matched pair for Huiyuan: one shows the teaching converting someone who does not even wish to listen, the other shows it converting someone who has already actively refused it — between them, covering every degree of resistance this sūtra's own power is said to overcome, short of the icchantika's total absence of any capacity to be reached at all.

「復次善男子！如蛇脫皮，為死滅耶？」「不也。世尊！」「善男子，如來亦爾，方便示現，棄捨毒身，可言如來無常滅耶？」「不也。世尊！如來於此閻浮提中方便捨身，如彼毒蛇，捨於故皮，是故如來名為常住。」「復次善男子！譬如金師，得好真金，隨意造作種種諸器。如來亦爾，於二十五有，悉能示現種種色身，為化眾生拔生死故。是故如來名無邊身，雖復示現種種諸身，亦名常住無有變易。」

「復次善男子！如菴羅樹及閻浮樹一年三變，有時生花，光色敷榮，有時生葉，滋茂蓊欝，有時彫落，狀似枯死。善男子！於意云何？是樹實為枯滅不耶？」「不也。世尊！」「善男子！如來亦爾，於三界中示三種身，有時初生，有時長大，有時涅槃，而如來身實非無常。」迦葉菩薩讚言：「善哉，誠如聖教，如來常住無有變易。」

「善男子！如來密語甚深難解，譬如大王告諸群臣：『先陀婆來。』先陀婆者，一名四實：一者鹽，二者器，三者水，四者馬。如是四法，皆同此名。有智之臣善知此名，若王洗時索先陀婆，即便奉水。若王食時索先陀婆，即便奉鹽。若王食已將欲飲漿索先陀婆，即便奉器。若王欲遊索先陀婆，即便奉馬。如是智臣善解大王四種密語。是大乘經亦復如是，有四無常，大乘智臣，應當善知。

若佛出世為眾生說如來涅槃，智臣當知此是如來為計常者說無常相，欲令比丘修無常想。或復說言正法當滅，智臣應知此是如來為計樂者說於苦相，欲令比丘多修苦想。或復說言我今病苦，眾僧破壞，智臣當知此是如來為計我者說無我相，欲令比丘修無我想。或復說言所謂空者是正解脫，智臣當知此是如來說正解脫無二十五有，欲令比丘修學空想。以是義故，是正解脫則名為空，亦名不動。謂不動者，是解脫中無有苦故，是故不動。是正解脫為無有相，謂無相者，無有色、聲、香、味、觸等，故名無相。是正解脫常不變易，是解脫中無有無常、熱惱變易，是故解脫名曰常住、不變、清涼。或復說言一切眾生有如來性，智臣當知此是如來說於常法，欲令比丘修正常法。是諸比丘若能如是隨順學者，當知是人真我弟子，善知如來微密之藏，如彼大王智慧之臣，善知王意。善男子！如是大王亦有如是密語之法，何況如來而當無耶？善男子！是故如來微密之教，難可得知，唯有智者乃能解我甚深佛法，非是世間凡夫品類，所能信也。」

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a great ship, sailing from this shore of the sea to the far shore, and then sailing back again from the far shore to this shore. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan distinguishes the boat from the wind with unusual precision: within nirvāṇa there is both realization and teaching — realization is what actually carries a being across, and so is likened to the ship itself, while teaching is what sets a being in motion toward it, and so is likened to the wind that fills the sail. The two similes are not decorative variation on a single idea but a division of labor: one names what nirvāṇa is, the other names what hearing about it does. He reads the snake's shed skin and the goldsmith's gold as answering a different question than the boat and wind — not whether the Tathāgata benefits others, but whether the Tathāgata himself, in manifesting death or manifesting many different bodies, actually undergoes any loss or change; a snake sheds skin without dying, and gold remains gold through every vessel it is shaped into. The mango-and-jambu-tree simile opens a new movement in Huiyuan's own outline, turning from the power of the teaching itself toward the power of contemplating the Three Jewels directly — the tree simile observing the buddha-jewel, and the saindhava parable that follows observing the dharma- and saṅgha-jewels together, since the health of one tracks the health of the other. On the saindhava parable itself, Huiyuan reads its structure as significant in its own right: four riddles, each requiring the same act of interpretive care (impermanence for those clinging to permanence, suffering for those clinging to bliss, no-self for those clinging to self, emptiness for release from the twenty-five states of existence), followed by a fifth statement — that every being possesses the Tathāgata's own nature — given plainly, with no riddle to decode at all. The four disguised teachings and the one undisguised one are not, on his reading, of equal doctrinal weight: the disguises were always provisional care for a listener's particular attachment, while the fifth statement is this sūtra's own direct and final word.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the drought-tree simile and the milk-dilution parable as naming two distinct causes of the same eventual decline, not one repeated warning: the drought trees fail through simple neglect, beings who no longer study the scripture at all; the diluted milk fails through active interference, editors who keep transmitting the text while quietly rearranging and adulterating it for their own convenience, each successive hand adding a little more water. His sharpest observation concerns the ordering itself: even after four dilutions, the resulting porridge, though it "has no flavor of milk at all," is still said to surpass every bitter alternative a thousandfold — the parable is not a warning that corruption will destroy the teaching's value, but a claim that even a badly mishandled transmission of it remains worth having.

On the passage equating buddha-nature with "the mark of a true man," Huiyuan is careful to specify which of three possible readings of "male" and "female" is actually in play here: physical form, temperament (resolute counting as male, yielding as female), or understanding (grasping the truth counting as male, failing to grasp it as female) — and the text's own argument, he notes, runs entirely on the third basis, not the first. He lays out four paired cases the passage implies: a man in body who does not know buddha-nature has, in this technical sense, no mark of a man at all, and is called "woman"; and a woman who does know buddha-nature has the mark of a true man, and the label "woman" simply drops away. The label tracks insight, not anatomy, and changes the instant insight does — which is also, on Huiyuan's reading, exactly what Kāśyapa's own exchange with the Buddha is doing in miniature: the Buddha first observes that Kāśyapa has spoken "according with the ways of the world" (using the conventional language of manhood at all), and Kāśyapa immediately clarifies that he does not mean it in the ordinary, literal sense — a correction the Buddha praises rather than overrides, confirming that the passage's own vocabulary was never meant to be taken as a claim about bodies.
