Twenty Answers to Twenty Doubts

Having ended Part 33 with Kāśyapa's own catalogue of twenty contradictions later disciples would draw from the Buddha's teaching, the Buddha now takes up each one in turn — not to declare a single winning side, but to name the exact person, the exact moment, and the exact need each statement was made for. Fifty-three thousand sages at a fragrant mountain, three hundred thousand wrestlers moved by a floating stone, a king who murders his own father and finds no peace even in the throne he killed for, a prince who loses his sight of buddha-nature the moment he insists it must be identical to himself — every apparent contradiction turns out to be two true things said to two different people, at two different moments, for two entirely different reasons.

Why the Buddha Said Both "Three Months Hence" and "I Am Ever-Abiding"

Chinese Source

「◎善男子！若言如來畢竟涅槃、不畢竟涅槃，是人不解如來意故，作如是說。善男子！是香山中有諸仙人五萬三千，皆於過去迦葉佛所，修諸功德未得正道，親近諸佛聽受正法。如來欲為如是人故，告阿難言：『過三月已，吾當涅槃。』諸天聞已，其聲展轉乃至香山。諸仙聞已，即生悔心，作如是言：『云何我等得生人中，不親近佛？諸佛如來出世甚難，如優曇花，我今當往至世尊所聽受正法。』善男子！爾時五萬三千諸仙即來我所，我時即為如應說法：『諸大士！色是無常。何以故？色之因緣是無常故，無常因生，色云何常？乃至識亦如是。』爾時諸仙聞是法已，即時獲得阿羅漢果。「善男子！拘尸那竭有諸力士三十萬人，無所繫屬，自恃憍恣、色力命財，狂醉亂心。善男子！我為調伏諸力士故，告目連言：『汝當調伏如是力士。』時目犍連敬順我教，於五年中種種教化，乃至不能令一力士受法調伏。是故我復為彼力士告阿難言：『過三月已，吾當涅槃。』善男子！時諸力士聞是語已，相與集聚，平治道路。過三月已，我時便從毘舍離國，至拘尸那城，中路遙見諸力士輩，即自化身為沙門像，往力士所作如是言：『諸童子！作何事耶？』力士聞已，皆生瞋恨，作如是言：『沙門！汝今云何謂我等輩為童子耶？』我時語言：『汝今大眾三十萬人，盡其身力，不能移此微末小石，云何不名為童子乎？』諸力士言：『汝若謂我為童子者，當知汝即是大人也。』善男子！我於爾時以足二指，掘出此石。是諸力士見是事已，即於己身生輕劣想，復作是言：『沙門！汝今復能移徙此石，令出道不？』我言童子：『何因緣故，嚴治此道？』諸力士言：『沙門！汝不知耶？釋迦如來當由此路至娑羅林，入於涅槃。以是因緣，我等平治。』我時讚言：『善哉，善哉！童子！汝等已發如是善心，吾當為汝除去此石。』我時以手舉擲大石，高至阿迦尼吒。時諸力士見石在空，皆生驚怖，尋欲四散。我復告言：『諸力士等，汝今不應生恐怖心，各欲散去。』諸力士言：『沙門！若能救護我者，我當安住。』爾時我復以手接石，置之右掌。力士見已，心生歡喜，復作是言：『沙門！是石常耶？是無常乎？』我於爾時，以口吹之，石即散壞，猶如微塵。力士見已唱言：『沙門！是石無常。』即生愧心而自考責：『云何我等，恃怙自在色力命財，而生憍慢？』我知其心，即捨化身，還復本形而為說法。力士見已，一切皆發菩提之心。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, should anyone say that the Tathāgata truly, finally enters nirvāṇa, or that he does not finally enter nirvāṇa at all, this person simply fails to understand the Tathāgata's own intention, and so speaks in this way. Good son, in the fragrant mountain there dwelt fifty-three thousand seers, every one of whom had, in the age of the past buddha Kāśyapa, cultivated every kind of merit without yet attaining the true path, drawing close to the buddhas and receiving the true Dharma. Wishing to teach these very seers, the Tathāgata said to Ānanda: 'After three months have passed, I shall enter nirvāṇa.' The gods, hearing of this, passed the word on, until it reached, in turn, all the way to the fragrant mountain. These seers, hearing it, at once gave rise to a mind of regret, saying: 'How could we possibly have been born among humans, and yet never once drawn close to the Buddha? A buddha's own appearance in the world is exceedingly rare, like the udumbara flower — we ought now to go to where the World-Honored One is, and receive the true Dharma.' Good son, these fifty-three thousand seers then came to where I was, and I taught the Dharma for them exactly as was fitting: 'Great beings, form is impermanent — why is this? Because the very cause and condition of form is itself impermanent, and, arising from an impermanent cause, how could form possibly be permanent? And, all the way through, consciousness is exactly the same.' Upon hearing this teaching, these seers at once attained the fruit of arhatship. "Good son, at Kuśinagara there dwelt three hundred thousand wrestlers, belonging to no one, relying arrogantly and self-indulgently upon their own beauty, strength, life, and wealth, their minds wild and confused. Good son, wishing to tame these very wrestlers, I said to Maudgalyāyana: 'You ought to tame wrestlers such as these.' Maudgalyāyana, reverently following my own instruction, taught and transformed them in every kind of way across five years, and yet was unable to bring even a single one of these wrestlers to accept the Dharma and be tamed. It was for this very reason that I, for the sake of these wrestlers, said to Ānanda: 'After three months have passed, I shall enter nirvāṇa.' Good son, these wrestlers, hearing this, gathered together and set about leveling the road. Once three months had passed, I set out from the kingdom of Vaiśālī toward the city of Kuśinagara, and, along the way, saw these wrestlers from a distance, and at once transformed my own body into the likeness of a śramaṇa, and, going to where they were, said: 'Young men, what are you doing here?' The wrestlers, hearing this, all gave rise to resentment, saying: 'Śramaṇa, how dare you call us "young men"?' I then said: 'Here you are, three hundred thousand strong, and yet, exerting the whole of your own bodily strength, you are unable to move even this small, insignificant stone — how, then, could you not be called mere young men?' The wrestlers said: 'Should you call us young men on this account, know that you yourself must then be a truly great man indeed.' Good son, I, at that very moment, dug this very stone out using only two toes of my own foot. These wrestlers, seeing this, at once gave rise to a low estimation of their own selves, and said, further: 'Śramaṇa, are you also able to move this stone entirely off the road?' I said to these young men: 'For what cause and condition are you leveling this very road?' The wrestlers said: 'Śramaṇa, do you not know? The Tathāgata Śākyamuni shall pass along this very road to the sāla grove, to enter nirvāṇa there — it is for this very reason that we are leveling it.' I then praised them, saying: 'Excellent, excellent, young men! Since you have already given rise to a wholesome mind such as this, I shall now remove this stone for your sake.' I then lifted this great stone with my own hand and cast it up, as high as the Akaniṣṭha heaven. The wrestlers, seeing this stone suspended in the sky, all gave rise to terror, and sought at once to scatter in every direction. I said to them, further: 'Wrestlers, you ought not to give rise to any fear at all, nor should you each seek to flee.' The wrestlers said: 'Śramaṇa, should you be able to protect us, we shall remain here in peace.' I then, once more, caught this stone with my own hand, and placed it upon my own right palm. The wrestlers, seeing this, gave rise to joy, and said, further: 'Śramaṇa, is this stone permanent, or is it impermanent?' I then, at that very moment, blew upon it with my own breath, and the stone at once scattered and crumbled, exactly like fine dust. The wrestlers, seeing this, proclaimed: 'Śramaṇa, this stone is impermanent!' — and at once gave rise to a mind of shame, reproaching themselves, saying: 'How could we possibly have relied so arrogantly upon our own beauty, strength, life, and wealth, giving rise to such pride?' Knowing their own minds had changed, I then relinquished my own transformed body, returned to my own true form, and taught the Dharma for them — and these wrestlers, seeing this, every one of them gave rise to the bodhi-mind."

「善男子！拘尸那竭有一工巧，名曰純陀，是人先於迦葉佛所，發大誓願：『釋迦如來入涅槃時，我當最後奉施飲食。』是故我於毘舍離國，顧命比丘優波摩那：『善男子！過三月已，吾當於彼拘尸那竭娑羅雙樹，入般涅槃。汝可往告純陀令知。』善男子！王舍城中有五通仙，名須跋陀，年百二十，常自稱是一切智人，生大憍慢，已於過去無量佛所，種諸善根，我亦為欲調伏彼故，告阿難言：『過三月已，吾當涅槃。須跋聞已，當來我所，生信敬心，我當為彼說種種法，其人聞已，當得盡漏。』「善男子！羅閱耆王頻婆娑羅，其王太子名曰善見，業因緣故，生惡逆心，欲害其父，而不得便。爾時惡人提婆達多，亦因過去業因緣故，復於我所生不善心，欲害於我，即修五通，不久獲得，與善見太子共為親厚。為太子故，現作種種神通之事，從非門出、從門而入，從門而出、非門而入，或時示現象馬牛羊、男女之身。善見太子見已，即生愛心、喜心、敬信之心，為是事故，嚴設種種供養之具而供養之，又復白言：『大師，聖人。我今欲見曼陀羅花。』時調婆達多即便往至三十三天，從彼天人而求索之，其福盡故，都無與者。既不得花，作是思惟：『曼陀羅樹，無我、我所，我若自取，當有何罪？』即前欲取，便失神通，還見己身，在王舍城。心生慚愧，不能復見善見太子，復作是念：『我今當往至如來所，求索大眾。佛若聽者，我當隨意教詔勅使舍利弗等。』爾時提婆達多便來我所，作如是言：『唯願如來，以此大眾付囑於我，我當種種說法教化，令其調伏。』我言癡人：『舍利弗等聰明大智，世所信伏。我猶不以大眾付囑，況汝癡人，食唾者乎？』時提婆達，復於我所倍生惡心，作如是言：『瞿曇！汝今雖復調伏大眾，勢亦不久，當見磨滅。』作是語已，大地即時六返震動，提婆達多尋時躄地，於其身邊，出大暴風吹諸塵土，而污坌之。提婆達多見惡相已，復作是言：『若我此身現世必入阿鼻地獄，我要當報如是大怨。』時提婆達多，尋起往至善見太子所，善見見已即問：『聖人！何故顏容憔悴，有憂色耶？』提婆達言：『我常如是，汝不知乎？』善見答言：『願說其意何因緣爾？』提婆達言：『我今與汝極成親愛，外人罵汝以為非理，我聞是事，豈得不憂？』善見太子復作是言：『國人云何罵辱於我？』提婆達言：『國人罵汝為未生怨。』善見復言：『何故名我為未生怨？誰作此名？』提婆達言：『汝未生時，一切相師皆作是言，是兒生已當殺其父。是故外人皆悉號汝為未生怨。一切內人，護汝心故，謂為善見。韋提夫人聞是語已，既生汝身於高樓上，棄之於地，壞汝一指。以是因緣，人復號汝為婆羅留枝。我聞是已，心生愁憒，而復不能向汝說之。』提婆達多以如是等種種惡事，教令殺父：『若汝殺父，我亦能殺瞿曇沙門。』善見太子問一大臣，名曰雨行：『大王何故為我立字作未生怨？』大臣即為說其本末，如提婆達所說無異。善見聞已，即與大臣收其父王，閉之城外，以四種兵而守衛之。韋提夫人聞是事已，即至王所，諸守王人，遮不聽入。爾時夫人生瞋恚心，便呵罵之。時諸守人即告太子：『大王！夫人欲得往見父王，不審聽不？』善見聞已，復生瞋嫌，即往母所，前牽母髮，拔刀欲斫。爾時耆婆白言：『大王！有國已來，罪雖極重，不及女人，況所生母？』善見太子聞是語已，為耆婆故，即便放捨。遮斷父王衣服臥具、飲食湯藥，過七日已，王命便終。善見太子見父喪已，方生悔心，雨行大臣復以種種惡邪之法而為說之：『大王！一切業行都無有罪，何故今者而生悔心？』耆婆復言：『大王！當知如是業者，罪兼二種：一者殺害父王，二者殺須陀洹。如是罪者，除佛更無能除滅者。』善見王言：『如來清淨，無有穢濁，我等罪人，云何得見？』善男子！我知是事，故告阿難：『過三月已，吾當涅槃。善見聞已，即來我所，我為說法，重罪得薄，獲無根信。』善男子！我諸弟子聞是說已，不解我意，故作是言：『如來定說畢竟涅槃。』」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, at Kuśinagara there was a skilled craftsman named Cunda, who had, in ages past, at the buddha Kāśyapa's own feet, made a great vow: 'When the Tathāgata Śākyamuni enters nirvāṇa, may I be the very last to offer him food and drink.' It was for this very reason that, while in the kingdom of Vaiśālī, I instructed the monk Upamāna: 'Good son, after three months have passed, I shall enter nirvāṇa there, at Kuśinagara, among the twin sāla trees — go and inform Cunda, so that he may know of this.' Good son, in the city of Rājagṛha, there was a seer possessed of the five supernatural powers, named Subhadra, one hundred and twenty years of age, who forever proclaimed himself an omniscient being, filled with great arrogance, though he had, in ages past, planted every kind of wholesome root at the feet of countless buddhas — and it was likewise in order to tame this very man that I said to Ānanda: 'After three months have passed, I shall enter nirvāṇa. Subhadra, hearing this, shall come to where I am, giving rise to a mind of faith and reverence, and I shall teach every kind of Dharma for him — and this man, upon hearing it, shall attain the exhaustion of every outflow.' "Good son, King Bimbisāra of Rājagṛha had a crown prince named Ajātaśatru, who, on account of his own past karma, gave rise to a murderous, rebellious mind, wishing to kill his own father, yet finding no opportunity to do so. At that time, the evil man Devadatta, likewise on account of his own past karma, gave rise to an unwholesome mind toward me, wishing to harm me, and so cultivated the five supernatural powers, which he soon attained, and became closely intimate with Prince Ajātaśatru. For the prince's sake, he manifested every kind of supernatural feat — emerging where there was no door and entering by the door, emerging by the door and entering where there was no door, and, at times, manifesting the body of an elephant, a horse, an ox, a sheep, or a man or a woman. Prince Ajātaśatru, seeing this, gave rise to affection, delight, and a mind of reverent faith, and, on this account, prepared and offered him every kind of lavish gift, further saying: 'Great teacher, holy one, I now wish to see the coral-tree flower.' Devadatta then went to the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, and sought this flower from the gods there — yet, because his own merit was already exhausted, no one at all would give it to him. Failing to obtain the flower, he reflected: 'The coral tree has no self, and nothing belonging to a self — should I simply take it myself, what fault could there possibly be in that?' He then stepped forward to take it, and at once lost his own supernatural power, finding himself back in the city of Rājagṛha. Filled with shame, and unable to face Prince Ajātaśatru again, he further reflected: 'I ought now to go to where the Tathāgata is, and ask him for his own assembly of disciples — should the Buddha grant this, I shall then be free to instruct and command Śāriputra and the rest as I wish.' Devadatta then came to where I was, and said: 'I ask only that the Tathāgata entrust this very assembly to me — I shall teach and transform them in every kind of way, and bring them under my own control.' I said to this fool: 'Śāriputra and the others are wise and greatly intelligent, trusted and respected throughout the world — I would not entrust the assembly even to them, how much less, then, to a fool such as you, who feeds upon mere spittle?' Devadatta, at this, gave rise to an even greater evil mind toward me, saying: 'Gautama, though you now hold this assembly under your own control, your own power, too, shall not last long, and you shall soon see it wither away.' Having spoken these words, the great earth at once shook in six ways, and Devadatta at once fell prostrate to the ground, a great violent wind arising beside his own body, blowing dust and dirt over him, defiling him entirely. Devadatta, seeing this evil omen, said, further: 'Should this very body of mine, in this present life, certainly enter the Avīci hell, I must still avenge this very great enmity of mine.' Devadatta then rose and went to where Prince Ajātaśatru was. Ajātaśatru, seeing him, asked: 'Holy one, why does your countenance appear so haggard, so filled with sorrow?' Devadatta said: 'I am always like this — did you not know?' Ajātaśatru answered: 'I beg you, tell me the reason why.' Devadatta said: 'You and I are now the closest of friends — outsiders slander you unjustly, and, hearing of this, how could I not be troubled?' Prince Ajātaśatru said, further: 'How, exactly, do the people of this land slander and insult me?' Devadatta said: 'The people of this land call you Ajātaśatru, "the enemy before birth."' Ajātaśatru said, further: 'Why am I named "the enemy before birth"? Who gave me this name?' Devadatta said: 'Before you were born, every physiognomist in the land said that this child, once born, would kill his own father — and it is for this very reason that outsiders all call you "the enemy before birth." Those close to you, wishing to protect your own feelings, call you instead "Ajātaśatru, the well-seen one." Queen Vaidehī, upon hearing this prophecy, once you were born, threw you down from a high tower to the ground, breaking one of your own fingers — and it is for this very reason that people, further, call you "the one with the broken finger." I, hearing of this, was filled with sorrow and distress, and yet was unable to tell you of it.' Devadatta, through evil deeds of exactly this kind, incited him to kill his own father, saying: 'Should you kill your father, I, too, shall be able to kill the śramaṇa Gautama.' Prince Ajātaśatru then asked one of his own great ministers, named Varṣākāra: 'Why, minister, was I given the name "the enemy before birth"?' This minister then told him the whole story, exactly as Devadatta had told it, without any difference at all. Ajātaśatru, hearing this, at once, together with this minister, seized his own father, the king, imprisoned him outside the city, and guarded him with all four divisions of his own army. Queen Vaidehī, hearing of this, went at once to where the king was, but the guards there blocked her, refusing to let her enter. The queen, at this, gave rise to a mind of anger, and rebuked them harshly. The guards then reported to the prince: 'Great king, the queen wishes to see the king, her husband — are we to permit this or not?' Ajātaśatru, hearing this, gave rise, further, to anger and suspicion, and went at once to his own mother, seizing her by the hair and drawing his sword to strike her. At this, Jīvaka said to him: 'Great king, since the founding of this kingdom, however grave a crime may be, it has never extended to a woman — how much less, then, to one's own birth mother?' Prince Ajātaśatru, hearing these words, for Jīvaka's own sake, at once released her. He then cut off his own father's clothing, bedding, food and drink, and medicine — and, after seven days had passed, the king's own life came to an end. Prince Ajātaśatru, seeing that his own father had died, only then gave rise to a mind of regret — yet the minister Varṣākāra, further, spoke to him with every kind of evil, wrong teaching, saying: 'Great king, no karmic action at all bears any fault whatsoever — why, then, do you now give rise to a mind of regret?' Jīvaka said, further: 'Great king, you should know that a deed of exactly this kind carries a twofold crime: first, the killing of one's own father, the king; and second, the killing of a stream-enterer. A crime of this kind, none but the Buddha himself is able to remove.' King Ajātaśatru said: 'The Tathāgata is pure, without any defilement or turbidity at all — how could a sinner such as I possibly go to see him?' Good son, knowing of this very matter, I said to Ānanda: 'After three months have passed, I shall enter nirvāṇa.' Ajātaśatru, hearing this, came at once to where I was, and I taught the Dharma for him, and his own grave sin was thereby lightened, and he attained rootless faith. Good son, my own disciples, hearing this teaching, failed to understand my own intention, and so said: 'The Tathāgata definitively teaches that he shall finally enter nirvāṇa.'"

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Each of these four episodes shows the exact same three-month announcement producing an entirely different, calibrated effect on an entirely different audience: the sages need only to hear that a buddha's presence is finite to be moved to seek him out before it is too late; the arrogant wrestlers need a demonstration of impermanence made physically undeniable (a stone lifted, suspended, then dissolved to dust) before pride can even begin to loosen its grip; Subhadra needs the announcement as a summons to come at all; and Ajātaśatru — whose crime is treated as the gravest possible case, a double murder of father and stream-enterer that "none but the Buddha can remove" — needs it as his own last, narrow window in which approach might still be conceivable. Read together, the four cases make the passage's own argument better than any abstract statement could: "three months hence I shall enter nirvāṇa" was never a metaphysical claim about the nature of the Tathāgata's own body at all, but a single skillful device recalibrated four separate times for four entirely different kinds of listener, each one needing a different degree of urgency to actually move.

The Ajātaśatru narrative — Devadatta's own failed theft of the coral flower, his manipulation of the prince through the "enemy before birth" name, and the parricide's own devastating aftermath — is one of the most extensively told individual stories in the sūtra, and its detail is not incidental: the passage needs the reader to feel the full weight of what "the gravest possible crime" actually means before the very next line can show that even this crime yields to the same compassionate device used for far lesser cases. This is the sūtra's own answer, in narrative rather than doctrinal form, to the question animating this entire fascicle: whether an apparently rigid teaching (the Buddha "will" enter nirvāṇa) can still function as a flexible, life-saving instrument once its true audience and purpose are correctly identified.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The self/no-self pair works the same way as the three-month/ever-abiding pair in the previous section: it is not two metaphysical claims left standing in contradiction, but two antidotes given to two different diseases. Against the nihilist who thinks nothing survives to receive karma's fruit, the King Sudarśana story — an eighty-four-thousand-year sequence of lives, culminating in the very body now speaking — establishes continuity of consequence. Against the essentialist who wants a self standing behind or apart from the aggregates, the two-hands-clapping simile reduces "self" to nothing more than the meeting-point of being, karma, and craving, with no independent thing left over once those three are removed. Both teachings serve the same buddha-nature discussion the fascicle keeps circling back to: a continuity strong enough to carry karmic fruit across immeasurable ages, yet without a fixed, independently existing self anywhere within it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Gotika's story lands with particular weight precisely because it resists being folded neatly into either the "retrogression exists" or "retrogression does not exist" camp: he does retrogress, six times over, yet each fall is followed by renewed effort rather than final collapse, until the seventh attempt succeeds — and the fear of losing it a seventh time proves fatal in a different way than the falls themselves. The negative account of the three unconditioned dharmas and the positive account given to Maudgalyāyana are not, on this reading, simply contradictory teachings averaged out to a middle position — they answer two different questions. Chinese Source

「善男子！我又復說：『眾生者即是佛性。何以故？若離眾生，不得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。是故我與波斯匿王說於象喻，如盲說象，雖不得象，然不離象。眾生說色乃至說識是佛性者，亦復如是，雖非佛性，非不佛性。如我為王說箜篌喻，佛性亦爾。』善男子！我諸弟子聞是說已，不解我意，作種種說。如盲問乳，佛性亦爾，以是因緣，或有說言犯四重禁、謗方等經、作五逆罪、一闡提等，悉有佛性，或說言無。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, I have also further said: 'Beings themselves are simply buddha-nature — why is this? Because, apart from beings, supreme, perfect awakening cannot be attained. It is for this very reason that I taught King Prasenajit the simile of the elephant: just as the blind men, describing the elephant, do not grasp the elephant as it truly is, and yet are not apart from the elephant either — so too, when beings describe form, and, all the way through, consciousness itself, as buddha-nature, it is exactly the same: though this is not truly buddha-nature, it is not entirely apart from buddha-nature either. Just as I taught the king the simile of the lute, buddha-nature, too, is exactly like this.' Good son, my own disciples, hearing this teaching, failed to understand my own intention, and spoke of it in every kind of different way — exactly like the blind men each asking about milk, buddha-nature, too, is exactly like this. On account of this very matter, some say that one who has violated the four gravest precepts, slandered the Vaipulya sūtras, committed the five heinous crimes, or is an icchantika, entirely possesses buddha-nature, while others say that he does not."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the fascicle's own capstone, and it names its own method openly: the blind-men-and-the-elephant simile, first given in Part 32 to explain why beings misdescribe buddha-nature when they identify it with any single aggregate, is here explicitly reused — by the text itself, not by this translation's own commentary — to explain the entire twenty-dispute structure of Parts 33 and 34 together. Every doctrine so far in this fascicle has been one hand on one part of the elephant: true as far as it goes, wrong only if mistaken for the whole animal. And the "beings apart from buddha-nature" / "beings are buddha-nature" pair is not resolved by picking a side — it is resolved by pointing out that both descriptions are the whole elephant felt from different hands, exactly as the sūtra's own closing line insists: some rightly say the gravest offenders possess buddha-nature, some rightly say they do not, and neither is describing something other than the single elephant everyone is touching.

Are There Buddhas of the Ten Directions?

Chinese Source

「善男子，我於處處經中說言：『一人出世多人利益，一國土中二轉輪王，一世界中二佛出世，無有是處。一四天下，八四天王乃至二他化自在天，亦無是處。』然我乃說：『從閻浮提、阿鼻地獄，上至阿迦膩吒天。』我諸弟子聞是說已，不解我意，唱言佛說，無十方佛。我亦於諸大乘經中說有十方佛。◎」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, in sūtras throughout, I have taught: 'When a single person appears in the world, many people benefit thereby — yet that a single land should have two wheel-turning sage-kings, or that a single world-system should have two buddhas appear within it, there is no such thing at all. That a single four-continent world should have eight sets of four heavenly kings, or two Paranirmita-vaśavartin heavens, there is likewise no such thing at all.' And yet I have also spoken of a single such reach extending from Jambudvīpa and the Avīci hell, all the way up to the Akaniṣṭha heaven. My own disciples, hearing this teaching, failed to understand my own intention, and so proclaimed: 'The Buddha teaches that there are no buddhas of the ten directions at all.' Yet I have also, in the various Mahāyāna sūtras, taught that there are buddhas of the ten directions."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fascicle closes on the widest-scoped of all twenty disputes, and leaves it standing exactly where it began — one world-system, one buddha at a time, and yet the Mahāyāna sūtras' own testimony of buddhas without number filling the ten directions. No further reconciling clause is supplied here, unlike almost every prior dispute in this fascicle; the text simply states both teachings side by side and stops. Read against everything that has come before, this reads less like an oversight than like a final instance of the same lesson pressed to its outer edge: some questions do not need to be — perhaps cannot be — closed by a single further sentence, and the willingness to let two true statements stand unreconciled is itself part of what "the fifth power, the power of liberating interpretation," named at the very end of Part 33, actually means in practice.

Part 34 Complete

Every one of Kāśyapa's twenty apparent contradictions from the end of Part 33 has now been answered — not by declaring a winner in each pair, but by restoring the specific person, moment, and need each half of the pair was spoken for. Self and no-self, the intermediate state affirmed and denied, retrogression taught both ways, the Buddha's two bodies, the twelve links conditioned and unconditioned, the mind permanent and impermanent, the five desires permitted and forbidden, the three unconditioned dharmas denied and defended, form as bare elements and form as derived quality, precepts lost and precepts merely polluted, one vehicle and five distinct fruits, and, at the center of it all, buddha-nature apart from beings and buddha-nature as beings themselves — every pair turns out to be two hands on one elephant.
