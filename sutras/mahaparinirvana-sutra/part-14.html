Half a Verse

This fascicle closes 聖行品 by answering, one by one, the twenty-four arguments the previous fascicle's rival teachers raised for permanence and for a self, before Mañjuśrī presses a startling objection — that the Buddha, on the very night of his parinirvāṇa, seems at last to be teaching something new — and the Buddha answers with five similes explaining why he has, in the deepest sense, never once "turned the wheel of the Dharma" at all. The fascicle then catalogs the twenty-five concentrations that break the twenty-five states of existence, and closes on one of this sūtra's most celebrated passages: the Buddha's own account of a former life as a Himalayan ascetic who heard half a verse of the Dharma from a flesh-eating demon, and gave up his body to hear the other half.

Nothing Escapes Its Cause

Chinese Source

「◎善男子！我觀諸行悉皆無常。云何知耶？以因緣故。若有諸法從緣生者，則知無常。是諸外道無有一法不從緣生。善男子！佛性無生、無滅、無去、無來，非過去、非未來、非現在，非因、所作、非無因作，非作、非作者，非相、非無相，非有名、非無名，非名、非色、非長、非短、非陰界入之所攝持，是故名常。善男子！佛性即是如來，如來即是法，法即是常。善男子！常者即是如來，如來即是僧，僧即是常。以是義故，從因生法，不名為常。是諸外道無有一法，不從因生。善男子！是諸外道不見佛性、如來及法，是故外道所可言說，悉是妄語，無有真諦。諸凡夫人，先見瓶衣、車乘舍宅、城郭河水、山林男女、象馬牛羊，後見相似，便言是常。當知其實，非是常也。善男子！一切有為，皆是無常；虛空無為是故為常，佛性無為是故為常。虛空者即是佛性，佛性者即是如來，如來者即是無為，無為者即是常。常者即是法，法者即是僧，僧即無為，無為者即是常。善男子！有為之法，凡有二種：色法、非色法。非色法者，心心數法。色法者，地、水、火、風。善男子！心名無常。何以故？性是攀緣相，應分別故。善男子！眼識性異，乃至意識性異，是故無常。善男子！色境界異，乃至法境界異，是故無常。善男子！眼識相應異，乃至意識相應異，是故無常。善男子！心若常者，眼識應獨緣一切法。善男子！若眼識異乃至意識異，則知無常。以法相似，念念生滅，凡夫見已，計之為常。善男子！諸因緣相，可破壞故，亦名無常。所謂因眼、因色、因明、因思惟、生於眼識，耳識生時，所因各異，非眼識因緣，乃至意識異亦如是。◎

「◎復次善男子！壞諸行因緣異故，心名無常。所謂修無常心異，修苦、空、無我心異。心若常者，應常修無常，尚不得觀苦、空、無我，況復得觀常、樂、我淨？以是義故，外道法中，不能攝取常樂我淨。善男子！當知心法必定無常。復次善男子！心性異故，名為無常。所謂聲聞心性異，緣覺心性異，諸佛心性異。一切外道心有三種：一者出家心，二者在家心，三者在家遠離心。樂相應心異，苦相應心異，不苦不樂相應心異，貪欲相應心異，瞋恚相應心異，愚癡相應心異。一切外道心相亦異，所謂愚癡相應心異，疑惑相應心異，邪見相應心異，進止威儀其心亦異。善男子！心若常者，亦復不能分別諸色，所謂青黃赤白紫色。善男子！心若常者，諸憶念法不應忘失。善男子！心若常者，凡所讀誦不應增長。復次善男子！心若常者，不應說言已作、今作、當作，若有已作、今作、當作，當知是心必定無常。善男子！心若常者，則無怨、親、非怨、非親。心若常者，則不應言我物、他物，若死、若生。心若常者，雖有所作，不應增長。善男子！以是義故，當知心性各各別異，有別異故當知無常。

「善男子！我今於此非色法中，演說無常，其義已顯。復當為汝說色無常。是色無常，本無有生，生已滅故。內身處胎歌羅邏時，本無有生，生已變故；外諸牙莖，本亦無生，生已變故；是故當知一切色法，悉皆無常。善男子！所有內色，隨時而變，歌羅邏時異、安浮陀時異、伽那時異、閉手時異、諸疱時異、初生時異、嬰孩時異、童子時異、乃至老時各各變異。所謂外色亦復如是，牙異、莖異、枝異、葉異、花異、果異。復次善男子！內味亦異，歌羅邏時乃至老時，各各變異。外味亦爾，牙、莖、枝、葉、花、果味異。歌羅邏時力異，乃至老時力異。歌羅邏時狀貌異，乃至老時狀貌亦異。歌羅邏時果報異，乃至老時果報亦異。歌羅邏時名字異，乃至老時名字亦異。所謂內色，壞已還合，故知無常。外諸樹木，亦壞已還合，故知無常。次第漸生，故知無常。次第生歌羅邏時乃至老時，次第生牙乃至果子，故知無常。諸色可滅，故知無常。歌羅邏滅時異，乃至老滅時異。牙滅時異乃至果滅時異，故知無常。凡夫無知，見相似生，計以為常。以是義故，名曰無常。若無常即是苦，若苦即是不淨。◎

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, I observe that conditioned phenomena, every one of them, are impermanent. How do I know this? Because of cause and condition. Whatever dharma arises from conditions, one thereby knows to be impermanent. Not a single dharma among what these followers of other tenets hold fails to arise from conditions. Good son, buddha-nature is without arising, without ceasing, without departing, without coming; it is neither past, nor future, nor present; it is neither caused, nor made, nor uncaused and unmade; it is neither an agent nor the one who acts; it is neither a mark nor the absence of a mark; it has neither a name nor the absence of a name; it is neither name nor form, neither long nor short, and is not held within the aggregates, sense-fields, or elements — it is, for this very reason, called permanent. Good son, buddha-nature is itself the Tathāgata; the Tathāgata is itself the Dharma; the Dharma is itself permanence. Good son, permanence is itself the Tathāgata; the Tathāgata is itself the Saṅgha; the Saṅgha is itself permanence. It is for this very reason that whatever dharma arises from a cause is not called permanent. Not a single dharma among what these followers of other tenets hold fails to arise from a cause. Good son, these followers of other tenets do not see buddha-nature, the Tathāgata, or the Dharma, and it is for this reason that whatever they have to say is entirely false speech, without any real truth in it. Every ordinary person, having previously seen a vessel, a garment, a cart, a house, a city wall, a river, a mountain forest, a man, a woman, an elephant, a horse, cattle, or sheep, and later seeing something resembling it, thereupon says that it is permanent. One should know that, in reality, it is not permanent at all. Good son, everything conditioned is impermanent; empty space, being unconditioned, is for this reason permanent, and buddha-nature, being unconditioned, is for this reason permanent. Empty space is itself buddha-nature; buddha-nature is itself the Tathāgata; the Tathāgata is itself the unconditioned; the unconditioned is itself permanence. Permanence is itself the Dharma; the Dharma is itself the Saṅgha; the Saṅgha is itself the unconditioned; the unconditioned is itself permanence. Good son, conditioned dharmas are, in every case, of two kinds: form dharmas, and non-form dharmas. Non-form dharmas are the mind and mental factors. Form dharmas are earth, water, fire, and wind. Good son, mind is called impermanent. Why is this? Because its very nature is the mark of grasping at an object, and it must be distinguished accordingly. Good son, the nature of eye-consciousness differs from that of the other consciousnesses, up through mind-consciousness, and this is why it is impermanent. Good son, the objects of form differ from the objects of the other senses, up through mental objects, and this is why it is impermanent. Good son, the concomitants associated with eye-consciousness differ from those associated with the other consciousnesses, up through mind-consciousness, and this is why it is impermanent. Good son, were mind permanent, eye-consciousness alone would have to grasp every dharma without exception. Good son, since eye-consciousness differs from the other consciousnesses, up through mind-consciousness, one thereby knows it to be impermanent — it is only because these dharmas resemble one another, arising and ceasing moment after moment, that ordinary people, upon seeing this, take it to be permanent. Good son, the marks of every cause and condition, since they can be broken apart, are likewise called impermanent — namely, eye-consciousness arises through the eye, through form, through light, and through attention as its causes, while, when ear-consciousness arises, each of its own causes differs from these; the causes and conditions differ from those of eye-consciousness, and it is just the same up through mind-consciousness.

"Furthermore, good son, since the causes and conditions that break down conditioned phenomena themselves differ, mind is called impermanent — namely, the mind that cultivates the notion of impermanence differs from the mind that cultivates suffering, emptiness, and no-self. Were mind permanent, one would forever be cultivating impermanence alone, unable even to contemplate suffering, emptiness, and no-self — how much less able to contemplate permanence, bliss, self, and purity? It is for this very reason that the teachings of other tenets are unable to lay hold of permanence, bliss, self, and purity at all. Good son, one should know that the dharma of mind is, without exception, impermanent. Furthermore, good son, because the very nature of mind differs, it is called impermanent — namely, the nature of a śrāvaka's mind differs, the nature of a pratyekabuddha's mind differs, and the nature of every buddha's mind differs. Every follower of another tenet has one of three kinds of mind: the mind of one who has left the household life, the mind of one who remains at home, and the mind of one who remains at home yet keeps apart from it. The mind associated with pleasure differs, the mind associated with suffering differs, the mind associated with neither suffering nor pleasure differs, the mind associated with craving differs, the mind associated with anger differs, the mind associated with delusion differs. The mental characteristics of every follower of another tenet, too, differ from one another — namely, the mind associated with delusion differs, the mind associated with doubt differs, the mind associated with wrong view differs, and the mind differs even in its outward bearing and demeanor. Good son, were mind permanent, it would, moreover, be unable to distinguish among colors — blue-black, yellow, red, white, and purple. Good son, were mind permanent, whatever is held in memory should never be forgotten. Good son, were mind permanent, whatever one recites should never increase in one's mastery of it. Furthermore, good son, were mind permanent, one should never speak of what was done, what is being done, and what will be done — and since there is indeed what was done, what is being done, and what will be done, one should know that this mind is, without exception, impermanent. Good son, were mind permanent, there would be no enemy, no relation, no non-enemy, and no non-relation. Were mind permanent, one should not be able to speak of 'my own thing' and 'another's thing,' or of dying and being born. Were mind permanent, though it performed some action, it should never increase in mastery of it. Good son, it is for this very reason that one should know the nature of mind to differ in every single instance, and, because it differs in this way, one should know it to be impermanent.

"Good son, I have now, within these non-form dharmas, expounded impermanence, and its meaning is already clear. I shall now further explain to you the impermanence of form. This impermanence of form is such that it was originally without any arising, and, having arisen, then ceases. The inner body, at the kalala stage within the womb, was originally without any arising, and, having arisen, then changes; the sprout and stem outside the body, likewise, were originally without any arising, and, having arisen, then change — one should therefore know that every form dharma, without exception, is impermanent. Good son, whatever inner form there is changes according to its time: it differs at the kalala stage, differs at the arbuda stage, differs at the ghana stage, differs at the closed-hand stage, differs at the stage of budding limbs, differs at the stage of first birth, differs in infancy, differs in childhood, and, on up through old age, differs at every single stage. Outer form, too, is just the same: the sprout differs, the stem differs, the branch differs, the leaf differs, the flower differs, the fruit differs. Furthermore, good son, inner flavor, too, differs: from the kalala stage on up through old age, it differs at every stage. Outer flavor is just the same: the sprout, stem, branch, leaf, flower, and fruit each differ in flavor. Strength differs at the kalala stage, on up through old age. Appearance differs at the kalala stage, on up through old age. Karmic result differs at the kalala stage, on up through old age. Name differs at the kalala stage, on up through old age. Inner form, once it has broken down, comes together again — one thereby knows it to be impermanent. Every outer tree and plant, too, once broken down, comes together again — one thereby knows it to be impermanent. Arising in due sequence, one thereby knows it to be impermanent — arising in due sequence from the kalala stage on up through old age, and arising in due sequence from the sprout on up through the fruit, one thereby knows it to be impermanent. Every form can be extinguished — one thereby knows it to be impermanent: the manner of its extinguishing differs at the kalala stage, on up through old age; the manner of its extinguishing differs at the sprout stage, on up through the fruit — one thereby knows it to be impermanent. It is only because the ignorant, ordinary person sees a succession of resembling things arise that he takes this to be permanence. It is for this very reason that this is called impermanence. And if it is impermanent, it is thereby suffering; and if it is suffering, it is thereby impure.

"Good son, this matter regarding what is bliss I have already answered earlier, through Kāśyapa's own question.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this whole fascicle's opening as the Buddha finally taking apart, in order, the twenty-four arguments Mañjuśrī voiced on the heretics' behalf at the close of Part 13 — eight for permanence, five for bliss, three for purity, eight for a self — but not in their original sequence, and not with equal weight given to each. The eight permanence arguments (pleasant/unpleasant karmic retribution, the consequence of killing, fixed recollection, recognizing a previously-seen face or hand, skill improving with practice, counting, reciting successive scriptures, and the durability of ordinary property) are answered in three uneven batches: the first two — retribution and killing — fall together to the single, general point that opens this section, since both arguments assume an unchanging agent must persist to receive a karmic fruit, and "whatever arises from cause and condition is impermanent" removes that assumption at the root; buddha-nature's own ten-fold definition of permanence (unborn, unceasing, without going or coming, and so on) is inserted here specifically to make clear that denying the heretics' permanence is not the same as denying permanence altogether — something is genuinely permanent, just not what they point to. The eighth argument (that vessels, carts, mountains, and the rest persist unchanged) is dispatched almost in passing, with the observation that ordinary people mistake a *resemblance* between successive things for the persistence of one and the same thing. The middle five arguments (recollection, recognition, practice, counting, recitation) are then handled together, split across two different targets: recollection, practiced skill, counting, and recitation are shown to be impermanent because mind itself is impermanent (the sūtra's long analysis of consciousness differing by faculty, object, association, and momentary arising); recognizing a face or hand seen before is shown to be impermanent through the parallel analysis of form — the body changing at every embryonic and developmental stage in shape, flavor, strength, and even name — so that recognizing something as "the same" turns out to rest on resemblance across constant change, exactly the same error the eighth argument makes about ordinary objects.

On the bliss and purity arguments, Huiyuan is explicit that they receive no comparable point-by-point treatment here at all: "impermanent, and therefore suffering; suffering, and therefore impure" answers both in a single breath, by simple entailment from what has just been established about permanence, and the sūtra's own closing line — "I have already answered this matter regarding bliss through Kāśyapa's own question" — points the reader back to Part 12's resolution of the three feelings and three sufferings (the passage Huiyuan calls "the milk-cow simile" for its context), where the Buddha had already shown at length that no rival system's "bliss" survives close analysis. Readers expecting a five-fold and three-fold rebuttal to mirror the eight-fold ones for permanence and self will not find them; the sūtra treats the case for bliss and purity as already closed by argument made elsewhere, and spends its remaining space on the two claims — permanence and self — it judges to need a direct answer.

What the Puppet Cannot Prove

Chinese Source

「復次善男子！諸行無我。善男子！總一切法，謂色、非色。色非我也。何以故？可破、可壞、可裂、可打、生增長故。我者不可破壞、裂打、生長，以是義故，知色非我。非色之法亦復非我。何以故？因緣生故。善男子！若諸外道以專念故，知有我者，專念之性實非我也。若以專念為我性者，過去之事則有忘失，有忘失故定知無我。善男子！若諸外道以憶想故知有我者，無憶想故定知無我。如說見人手有六指，即便問言：『我先何處共相見耶？』若有我者，不應復問；以相問故，定知無我。善男子！若諸外道以有遮故，知有我者，善男子！以有遮故，定知無我。如言調達，終不發言非調達也。我亦如是，若定是我，終不遮我；以遮我故，定知無我。若以遮故，知有我者，汝今不遮，定應無我。善男子！若諸外道，以伴非伴知有我者，以無伴故，應無有我。有法無伴，所謂如來、虛空、佛性，我亦如是實無有伴，以是義故，定知無我。復次善男子！若諸外道以名字故，知有我者，無我法中，亦有我名，如貧賤人名字富貴。如言我死，若我死者，我則殺我，而我實不可殺，假名殺我；亦如矬人，名為長者。以是義故，定知無我。

「復次善男子！若諸外道，以生已求乳知有我者，善男子！若有我者，一切嬰兒不應執持糞穢、火蛇、毒藥，以是義故，定知無我。復次善男子！一切眾生於三法中，悉有等智，所謂婬欲、飲食恐怖，是故無我。復次善男子！若諸外道以相貌故，知有我者，善男子！相故無我，無相故亦無我。若人睡時，不能進止、俯仰視眴、不覺苦樂，不應有我。若以進止、俯仰視眴，知有我者，機關木人亦應有我。善男子！如來亦爾，不進、不止、不俯、不仰、不視、不眴，不苦、不樂、不貪、不恚、不癡、不行，如來如是真實有我。復次善男子！若諸外道以見他食果口中生涎，知有我者，善男子！以憶念故，見則生涎，涎非我也。我亦非涎、非喜非悲、非欠非笑，非臥非起、非飢非飽，以是義故，定知無我。

「善男子！是諸外道癡如小兒，無慧方便，不能了達常與無常、苦樂、淨不淨、我無我、壽命非壽命、眾生非眾生、實非實、有非有，於佛法中取少許分，虛妄計有常、樂、我、淨，而實不知常樂我淨。如生盲人不識乳色，便問他言：『乳色何似？』他人答言：『色白如貝。』盲人復問：『是乳色者如貝聲耶？』答言：『不也。』復問：『貝色為何似耶？』答言：『猶稻米末。』盲人復問：『乳色柔軟如稻米末耶？稻米末者復何所似？』答言：『猶如雨雪。』盲人復言：『彼稻米末冷如雪耶？雪復何似？』答言：『猶如白鶴。』是生盲人雖聞如是四種譬喻，終不能得識乳真色。是諸外道亦復如是，終不能識常樂我淨。善男子！以是義故，我佛法中有真實諦，非於外道。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, conditioned phenomena are without self. Good son, taking every dharma together, there is form and there is non-form. Form is not the self. Why is this? Because it can be broken, destroyed, torn apart, and struck, and because it arises and grows. The self cannot be broken, destroyed, torn apart, struck, or made to arise and grow — it is for this very reason that one knows form is not the self. Non-form dharmas, too, are likewise not the self. Why is this? Because they arise through cause and condition. Good son, should the followers of other tenets know there is a self through the fact of fixed recollection, this very nature of fixed recollection is, in reality, not the self. Were fixed recollection taken as the nature of the self, then whatever happened in the past would sometimes be forgotten — and, since it is indeed forgotten, one thereby knows for certain there is no self. Good son, should the followers of other tenets know there is a self through the fact of memory, then, since there are things one does not remember, one thereby knows for certain there is no self. It is as when a man sees another person whose hand has six fingers, and at once asks: 'Where have I met you before?' — were there truly a self, he should never need to ask such a question at all; and it is precisely because he does ask that one knows for certain there is no self. Good son, should the followers of other tenets know there is a self through the fact of denial, good son, it is precisely because of this denial that one knows for certain there is no self. It is as when one speaks of Devadatta — one would never say 'this is not Devadatta.' The self should be just the same: were it truly the self, one would never deny it — and it is precisely because the self is denied that one knows for certain there is no self. If it is through denial that one knows there is a self, then, since you yourselves do not deny it, there should certainly be no self. Good son, should the followers of other tenets know there is a self through the fact of companionship and its absence, then, since there is no companion, there should be no self at all. There are dharmas without any companion — namely, the Tathāgata, empty space, and buddha-nature — and the self is just the same, in reality without any companion at all, and it is for this very reason that one knows for certain there is no self. Furthermore, good son, should the followers of other tenets know there is a self through the fact of a name, then, within a dharma that has no self, there can likewise be a name of "self" — just as a poor and lowly person may be given the name "Wealthy and Noble." It is as when one speaks of "my own death" — were there truly a self that dies, then this self would be killing itself, and yet the self, in reality, cannot be killed at all; it is only in name that one speaks of "killing myself" — just as a short man may be given the name "Tall One." It is for this very reason that one knows for certain there is no self.

"Furthermore, good son, should the followers of other tenets know there is a self through the fact that, immediately after birth, an infant already seeks the breast, good son, were there truly a self, then no infant should ever grasp at feces, fire, a snake, or poison — and it is for this very reason that one knows for certain there is no self. Furthermore, good son, every being alike has an equal instinctive knowledge with regard to three things — namely, sexual desire, food and drink, and fear — and this, too, is why there is no self. Furthermore, good son, should the followers of other tenets know there is a self through the fact of outward marks, good son, there is no self through the presence of a mark, and there is likewise no self through the absence of a mark. When a person is asleep, unable to move or stop, to bend or stretch, to gaze or blink, unaware of suffering or pleasure, there should, on their reckoning, be no self at all. And should one know there is a self through moving and stopping, bending and stretching, gazing and blinking, then a wooden puppet worked by machinery should likewise have a self. Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same: he neither moves nor stops, neither bends nor stretches, neither gazes nor blinks, is neither in suffering nor in pleasure, neither craves nor is angry nor is deluded, and yet does act — the Tathāgata, in just this way, truly has a self. Furthermore, good son, should the followers of other tenets know there is a self through seeing another eat fruit and thereupon feeling saliva rise in one's own mouth, good son, it is through memory that, upon seeing this, saliva rises — this saliva is not the self. The self, likewise, is neither this saliva, nor joy, nor sorrow, neither a yawn nor a laugh, neither lying down nor rising up, neither hunger nor satiety — and it is for this very reason that one knows for certain there is no self.

"Good son, these followers of other tenets are as foolish as little children, without wisdom or skillful means, unable to fathom permanence and impermanence, suffering and bliss, purity and impurity, self and no-self, a lifespan and the absence of one, a being and the absence of one, what is real and what is not, what exists and what does not — and, taking hold of some small fragment within the Buddha's own Dharma, they falsely reckon there to be permanence, bliss, self, and purity, while, in reality, knowing nothing at all of true permanence, bliss, self, and purity. It is like a man blind from birth, who, not knowing the color of milk, asks another: 'What does the color of milk resemble?' The other person answers: 'Its color is as white as a conch shell.' The blind man asks further: 'Is this color of milk, then, like the sound a conch shell makes?' The answer is: 'No, it is not.' He asks further: 'What, then, does the color of a conch shell resemble?' The answer is: 'It is like ground rice-flour.' The blind man asks further: 'Is the color of milk, then, as soft to the touch as ground rice-flour? And what, in turn, does ground rice-flour resemble?' The answer is: 'It is like falling snow.' The blind man says further: 'Is that ground rice-flour, then, as cold as snow? And what, in turn, does snow resemble?' The answer is: 'It is like a white crane.' This man, blind from birth, though he has heard these four similes in turn, is still, in the end, utterly unable to know the true color of milk. These followers of other tenets are just the same: they are, in the end, utterly unable to know true permanence, bliss, self, and purity. Good son, it is for this very reason that the real truth exists within my own Buddha-dharma, and not among these other tenets."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the eight arguments for a self as answered in four deliberately reordered pairs rather than in their original sequence. The first pair answers argument one (that the six sense faculties are the instruments through which a self must act, illustrated in the previous fascicle by the potter's wheel that implies a potter) together with argument four (that grasping and performing actions — reaping with a sickle, driving a cart — proves an agent exists): the opening form/non-form division answers the first directly (the six faculties are simply the five bodily sense organs plus mind, all impermanent, arising through cause and condition, and thus no evidence of an agent behind them), while the fixed-recollection and memory arguments — borrowed back from the previous section's refutation of permanence, since holding something in mind is itself a mental "action" — answer the fourth. The second pair answers argument seven (that denial itself proves a self, since one only denies what is real) together with argument eight (that companionship and its absence proves a self): denial is turned back on itself — the very fact that a self can be denied at all is what proves there is none to deny, exactly as no one ever needs to say "this is not Devadatta" of something that plainly is; and companionship fails for the opposite reason — the Tathāgata, empty space, and buddha-nature all have no companion at all, and the true self is no different. The third pair answers argument six (that things combining to bring mutual benefit, as a cart's parts benefit each other, proves a self benefits from the aggregates' combination) together with argument five (that an infant's inborn urge to nurse proves an inherited self): the "benefit" argument is dissolved by exposing "self" as a bare label with no referent behind it, on the model of a poor man nicknamed "the wealthy one" or a short man nicknamed "the tall one" — a name that can be applied to something is no proof that the thing it purports to name exists; and the infant argument is undercut by the same infant's equally inborn tendency to grasp at excrement, fire, and poison, which no theory of a wise, self-preserving inner agent can explain. The fourth pair answers argument two (that involuntary marks — breathing, blinking, a directed will — prove a self) together with argument three (that involuntary salivation at the sight of food eaten by another proves a taster-self): a sleeping person shows none of these marks and yet is not thereby proven selfless, while a wooden puppet worked by machinery shows every one of them and is not thereby proven to have a self — and the Tathāgata, who shows none of these ordinary marks at all, is named here as the one case where a true self is nonetheless present, precisely because that self was never what the outward marks were tracking in the first place. Salivation, likewise, turns out to trace back to memory and anticipation rather than to any taster standing behind the tongue.

On the closing blind-man simile, Huiyuan reads its four failed comparisons (white like a conch, like ground rice-flour, soft like falling snow, cold like a white crane) as deliberately compounding rather than converging: each new comparison answers the previous question but immediately raises a new one the blind man cannot resist asking, so that the four similes together never once arrive at milk's actual color — they only ever describe some other single property (whiteness, texture, softness, coldness) borrowed from something else the blind man has touched or heard, none of which is color itself. His point in drawing this out is that a heretic seizing on one true fragment of the Buddha's teaching — permanence here, self there — makes exactly this mistake: each fragment, examined closely, resembles the real virtue in some one respect, and none of the four, however patiently multiplied, ever adds up to actually seeing it. This is also, per Huiyuan, precisely why the fascicle needed to walk through all twenty-four arguments individually rather than dismissing the heretics' position at a stroke — every fragment they seize on has to be shown, one at a time, not to be what it is mistaken for.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Mañjuśrī's opening praise as a deliberate rhetorical trap rather than an innocent compliment: to call this fascicle's teaching "the Tathāgata turning the wheel anew, on the very night of his parinirvāṇa" packs in three separate claims at once — that the Tathāgata is about to enter final extinction, that he "turns" anything at all, and that whatever he now teaches is somehow "more" than before — and the Buddha's answer takes the three apart one at a time. The first is dismissed outright: the Tathāgata is ever-abiding, so there is no parinirvāṇa to have "approached" in the first place. The second occupies most of this section, in two stages: first, a direct denial that the Tathāgata ever thinks "I am the one turning the wheel" (with the parallel, more startling denial that he ever thinks "I am the eye," "I am faith," "I am the six perfections," extending the same point from the person to the eighteen elements to the path itself); then five similes — eye-consciousness, fire, ghee, a sprout, and a drum's sound — that make the same point about causation generally: in every one, several conditions come together to produce something, and not one component of the process, examined individually, can be said to have "done" it. Huiyuan reads the pattern uniting all five as a doubled negation held together — "if it does not do X, this not-doing is itself the true turning of the wheel, and the true turning of the wheel is itself the Tathāgata" — meaning "turning the wheel" and "the Tathāgata" name the very same not-acting from two different angles, not two things linked by inference. The final comparison to empty space and buddha-nature (neither arisen, produced, made, nor conditioned) generalizes the same conclusion one register higher, closing the second denial by placing "turning the wheel" in the same category as the other things this sūtra has already shown cannot, strictly speaking, act at all.

On the third denial — the claim that tonight's teaching is somehow "more" — Huiyuan finds a genuinely different structure at work: rather than deny the distinction outright, the sūtra grants it and then insists it does not license the word "more." His count finds eight paired contrasts between the first turning at Vārāṇasī and this one at Kuśinagara: the audience's own path (Lesser Vehicle then, Great Vehicle now), the hearers' faculty (middling then, superior — men like the bodhisattva Kāśyapa — now, with the icchantika excluded from both), their diligence, the result attained (eighty thousand stream-enterers then, eight hundred thousand million now made irreversible), who made the request (Brahmā then, Kāśyapa now), the content taught (impermanence-suffering-emptiness-no-self then, permanence-bliss-self-purity now), and how far the teaching's sound reached (as far as the Brahmā heaven then, across twenty Gaṅgā-sands of buddha-worlds in every direction now). Every one of these differences is real and is not denied; what is denied is only the inference from "this teaching differs from that one" to "the Tathāgata is doing something now he was not always doing" — the cakravartin's wheel-treasure closing simile makes the point directly, since one and the same wheel-treasure subdues what is unsubdued and stabilizes what is already subdued, moves beings both up and down, without the wheel itself ever being two different wheels. Mañjuśrī's closing acknowledgment — that he already knew all this, and asked only "for the benefit of beings" — confirms, on Huiyuan's reading, that the whole exchange was staged from the start: a bodhisattva of Mañjuśrī's own stated understanding does not need to be corrected, but the assembly listening in does need to hear the correction made in full.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads "noble conduct" (聖行) itself as carrying two distinct justifications for its name, not one: it is what every buddha alone has fully realized and taught (the "noble" ones proper), and it is, at the very same time, what the śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, and bodhisattva are each able to hear and put into practice — Kāśyapa's own question exists precisely to draw out this second sense, since the first sense alone would make the name misleading for anyone but a buddha. The six fears this ground removes, he notes, track five categories shared with the *Daśabhūmika Sūtra*'s own account of the first bodhisattva ground (fear of not surviving, of an unfavorable rebirth, of death, of an evil destiny, and of losing standing before an assembly) plus one addition unique to this sūtra — freedom from fearing the twenty-five states of existence themselves, which the *Daśabhūmika* does not name as a distinct fear in its own sevenfold or fivefold lists.

On the twenty-five concentrations, Huiyuan raises and answers a natural objection: since the bodhisattva at the first ground has not yet exhausted birth-and-death, how can he already "break" every one of the twenty-five states of existence? His answer distinguishes two kinds of birth-and-death — segmented birth-and-death (the ordinary kind, ending in a fixed death after a fixed lifespan) and transformational birth-and-death (the subtler kind a bodhisattva continues even after leaving the first behind) — and, within segmented birth-and-death, a further split between its coarse residue (exhausted already at the first ground) and its subtle residue (not exhausted until buddhahood). "Breaking the twenty-five states of existence" at the first ground means breaking only the coarse residue of segmented existence in each of the twenty-five realms, not their subtle residue nor the transformational cycle a bodhisattva still knowingly enters thereafter — which is exactly what the next paragraph's account of a bodhisattva being reborn in hell "not as the fruit of his own past karma, but through the power of his own mastery" goes on to describe.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the six-stage dairy simile (cow, milk, curds, fresh butter, clarified butter, ghee) as tracking six real stages in the Buddha's own teaching career, not an arbitrary count chosen only for its five transformations: the cow is the Buddha's own physical, response-body presence; milk is the twelve divisions of the śrāvaka-vehicle scriptures as a whole; curds are the sūtra genre specifically, opened out from the smaller collections ("opening the small to reveal the great" is why this step is called an "emergence" rather than a repetition); fresh butter is the Vaipulya scriptures, which expound the emptiness of all characteristics by way of specific topics — giving, receiving, the ungraspability of wealth — deriving principle from particular cases; clarified butter is the perfection of wisdom, which reaches the ultimate realization of emptiness directly, deriving practice from principle rather than the reverse; and ghee is this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra itself, where wisdom's own "known-through" (了因) finally discloses the beginningless dharma-nature-nirvāṇa it was always oriented toward. He is careful to specify exactly which "Tathāgata" the simile identifies with ghee: the Dharma-body specifically, not the response-body that appeared as "the cow" at the simile's other end — the merit of a buddha's response-body, however great, does not equal this sūtra's own merit, precisely because what this sūtra teaches is the deeper reality the response-body itself only points toward.

On Kāśyapa's vow, Huiyuan reads the escalating list of six approaches to different kinds of listeners — winning over the greedy with a gift first, the proud with flattery, the ordinary with authority, the arrogant by becoming their servant, the hostile by first defeating them in argument, and the already-devoted with reverence and honor — as a structured application of the four means of attracting and guiding beings (四攝法: giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and cooperation), with beneficial conduct alone stretched across four of the six cases and the fourth means, "acting in common with others," left unused here. The Buddha's five-fold description of Kāśyapa's love for the sūtra that follows — loving it, craving it, receiving it, savoring it, revering it — is, on Huiyuan's reading, a deliberate escalation from an initial fondness through active desire, genuine reception, sustained absorption in its flavor, and finally settled faith and devoted practice, each word marking a further stage of how thoroughly the teaching has been taken in.

I shall now ask him.' I then went forward to where this rākṣasa stood, and spoke these words: 'Excellent, great being! Where did you obtain this half-verse, spoken by one of the buddhas of ages past who is free of every fear? Great being, where, further, did you obtain a jewel such as this, granting half of every wish? Great being, the meaning of this half-verse is itself the true path of every buddha, every world-honored one, past, future, and present. Every being throughout the world, immeasurable in number, forever covered over by the net of wrong views, has, throughout this entire life, dwelt among these other tenets, never once having heard a teaching of emptiness such as this, spoken by one who has transcended the world, possessed of the ten powers, the hero of the world.'

"Good son, having asked this, he at once answered me, saying: 'Great brahmin, you should not now ask me about this meaning. Why is this? I have gone many days now without food, searching everywhere, yet utterly unable to find any. Tormented by hunger and thirst, my mind disordered, I spoke deranged words — they were not something known by my own original mind at all. Even were I now able to fly through empty space all the way to Uttarakuru, or even to the heavens themselves, searching everywhere for food, I would still be unable to find any — it is for this very reason that I spoke as I did.' Good son, I then said further to the rākṣasa: 'Great being, should you be able to finish speaking this verse for me, I shall become your disciple for the rest of my life. Great being, what you have spoken has neither finished its own words nor exhausted its own meaning — for what reason, then, do you not wish to finish speaking it? The giving of wealth has its limit and comes to an end, but the cause and condition of giving the Dharma can never be exhausted — and, though it is never exhausted, it brings great benefit all the same. I, having now heard this Dharma of half a verse, feel a startled uncertainty arise within my own mind — might you now, mercifully, resolve and remove this uncertainty for me, by finishing this verse? I shall become your disciple for the rest of my life.' The rākṣasa answered: 'Your own wisdom goes too far, concerned only with your own body, giving no thought to mine at all — I am, right now, truly pressed by the suffering of hunger, and, in truth, unable to speak it.' I then asked him: 'What is it that you eat?' The rākṣasa answered: 'This is not worth your asking — were I to tell you, it would terrify a great many people.' I asked further: 'Here, in this solitary place, there is no one else at all — I myself am not afraid of you; for what reason, then, will you not tell me?' The rākṣasa answered: 'What I eat is nothing but the warm flesh of a human being. What I drink is nothing but the hot blood of a human being. Owing to my own scant merit, I am reduced to eating only this food, and, searching everywhere for it, am still hard-pressed and unable to obtain it. Though there are many people in the world, they all possess merit and virtue, and are, besides, protected and guarded by the gods — I myself lack the power to be able to kill any of them.' Good son, I then said further: 'Only finish speaking this half-verse in full, and, once I have heard it, I shall offer up this very body of mine to you as an offering. Great being, should I simply come to the end of my life, this body of mine would be of no further use at all — it would only become food for tigers, wolves, kites, owls, eagles, and vultures, and I would not gain from it even a hair's breadth of merit. I now, for the sake of seeking unsurpassed, complete awakening, would give up this impermanent body in exchange for the imperishable body.' The rākṣasa answered: 'Who would ever believe words such as these of you — that you would give up your own beloved body for the sake of a mere eight characters?' Good son, I then answered him: 'You are truly without wisdom. It is like a man who gives another an ordinary vessel, and receives in exchange a vessel made of the seven treasures. I am just the same: I give up this impermanent body, and receive in exchange the vajra body. You ask who would ever believe this? I have witnesses even now — the great lord Brahmā, Śakra, and the Four Heavenly Kings are all able to bear witness to this very thing. There are, further, bodhisattvas possessed of the heavenly eye, who, wishing to benefit immeasurable beings, cultivate the Great Vehicle and are fully endowed with the six perfections — they, too, are able to bear witness and know this. And there are, further, every buddha, every world-honored one, throughout the ten directions, who bring benefit to beings — they, too, are able to bear witness that I, for the sake of a mere eight characters, would give up my own body and life.' The rākṣasa then said: 'If you are truly able to give up your body in this way, then listen closely, listen closely — I shall now speak for you the remaining half of the verse.'

"Good son, I, upon hearing this, felt my own heart fill with joy, and at once undid the deerskin I wore, and spread it out as a Dharma-seat for this rākṣasa, saying: 'Preceptor, please be seated upon this seat.' I then, before him, joined my hands, knelt, and spoke these words: 'I ask only that you, my preceptor, kindly speak for me the remaining half of this verse, that it may be complete.' The rākṣasa then spoke:

"'Arising and ceasing themselves extinguished,
this very extinction is bliss.'

"Having spoken this verse, the rākṣasa said further: 'Bodhisattva-mahāsattva, you have now heard the meaning of this verse in full, and everything you wished for has been entirely fulfilled. If you truly wish to bring benefit to every being, then give me your body now.' Good son, I, at that time, deeply reflecting on this meaning, then wrote this verse everywhere I could — upon rocks, upon walls, upon trees, and along the road — and, having done so, further fastened tight the garment I wore, fearing that, once I was dead, my body might lie exposed, and at once climbed a tall tree. At that time, the spirit of the tree asked me: 'Excellent, worthy one! What is it that you intend to do?' Good son, I answered at that time: 'I wish to give up my body, to repay the price of this verse.' The spirit of the tree asked: 'What benefit is there in a verse of this kind?' I answered at that time: 'A verse-phrase of this kind is itself the path of the Dharma, opening onto emptiness, spoken by every buddha, past, future, and present — it is for the sake of this Dharma that I give up my body and my life. It is not for the sake of gain, fame, wealth, becoming a cakravartin king, the Four Heavenly Kings, Śakra, the great lord Brahmā, or any pleasure among gods or men — it is for the sake of bringing benefit to every being, without exception, that I give up this body.' Good son, as I gave up my body, I spoke these words further: 'May every miserly and grasping person come to see me giving up and forsaking this body; and should there be any who, having given some small gift, become filled with pride because of it, may they, too, come to see this — that I, for the sake of a single verse, give up this body and this life, casting it aside as one would a blade of grass or a piece of wood.' I, at that time, having spoken these words, at once let go of my body and cast myself down from the tree. Before I had yet reached the ground, sounds of every kind rang out from within empty space, their sound reaching all the way to Akaniṣṭha heaven itself. At that time, the rākṣasa returned to his true form, and, in the midst of the sky, caught hold of my body, and set it gently down upon level ground.

"At that time, Śakra, together with the other gods and men and the great lord Brahmā, bowed their heads and prostrated themselves at my feet, praising me, saying: 'Excellent, excellent! You are truly a bodhisattva, able to bring great benefit to immeasurable beings, wishing to kindle the great torch of the Dharma within the black darkness of ignorance. It was only because I so cherished the Tathāgata's own great Dharma that I troubled and vexed you in this way — I ask only that you allow me to confess and repent this transgression. You shall, in a future age, most certainly attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, and I ask only that you look upon me and grant me deliverance.' Śakra and the assembly of gods, having thus bowed at my feet, then took their leave, and vanished suddenly from sight. Good son, it was through my having, in ages past, given up this body for the sake of half a verse that I was able, through this very cause and condition, to surpass fully twelve kalpas, attaining unsurpassed, complete awakening even before Maitreya. Good son, I attained merit and virtue of this immeasurable kind entirely through making offering to the Tathāgata's own true Dharma. Good son, you, too, are just the same now: having given rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening, you have already surpassed immeasurable, boundless bodhisattvas as numerous as the sands of the Gaṅgā. Good son, this is called the bodhisattva's cultivating noble conduct while abiding within this Mahāparinirvāṇa of the Great Vehicle."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the whole account in three movements: what the bodhisattva did (his austerities, and the test he underwent), what he thereby attained, and the closing application to Kāśyapa himself. The testing itself he further divides into three stages — Śakra's own astonishment and decision to investigate, the test itself, and Śakra's return to his true form to confess and depart — and finds the middle stage, the test proper, built from five deliberate moves on Śakra's side answered by six exchanges on the ascetic's: Śakra first assumes a fearsome form; then speaks an attractive half-truth (the first half-verse); then, when questioned, claims a fearsome hunger for human flesh; then, once the ascetic offers his body, speaks the appealing second half-verse; and finally, having spoken it, demands the body be surrendered at once. Against this, the ascetic's six responses run: hearing the half-verse and asking who spoke it; searching and finding only the rākṣasa; questioning him directly; offering his own body when the rākṣasa pleads hunger; when the rākṣasa doubts his sincerity, naming Brahmā, Śakra, the Four Heavenly Kings, and the buddhas of the ten directions as his witnesses; and finally, once the rākṣasa agrees, spreading his own deerskin as a seat and renewing his request. Huiyuan reads Śakra's own doubting speech — the moon in water, the painted image, soldiers routed at the first sign of battle, the mother fish and the mango tree — as deliberately echoing the "three trials of gold" image Śakra names in the same breath: burning, hammering, and grinding are three separate tests because a single test can be faked, and the sūtra's own narrative supplies exactly three matching provocations (a fearsome appearance, a claim of cannibalism, and a demand for the body itself) before Śakra is satisfied.

On the closing claim of surpassing "twelve kalpas" before Maitreya, Huiyuan flags a real discrepancy with other scriptures, which speak instead of surpassing "nine kalpas," and resolves it rather than smoothing it over: one tradition holds that Śākyamuni's own first resolve for awakening came twelve kalpas behind Maitreya's, that diligent practice through the three incalculable eons closed three of those kalpas, and that a further nine were closed only later, at the start of the third incalculable eon, through the praise of the buddha Puṣya and further practice besides — so that "nine kalpas" names the later portion of the gap closed, and "twelve kalpas" names the whole gap closed from the very beginning, this sūtra simply speaking of the total rather than the increment other texts describe. He adds, without softening it, a further doctrinal qualification worth carrying forward: within the Abhidharma schools, this kind of "surpassing so many kalpas" is treated as a literal fact about a bodhisattva's career; within the teaching of the Great Vehicle, it is treated as a skillful expedient rather than a literal one, since, in reality, once a bodhisattva has entered the fixed lineage, every bodhisattva at that point cultivates practice equally, with no true "earlier" or "later" between them at all — the count of kalpas is a teaching device for beings who think in terms of sequence, not a claim about how awakening itself actually unfolds.

Part 14 Complete

This closes Part 14 (fascicle 14 of 40) and, with it, the whole of 聖行品第七: the point-by-point demolition of every argument Mañjuśrī voiced on the heretics' behalf in Part 13 — eight for permanence, answered through the impermanence of both mind and form; eight for a self, answered in four reordered pairs; bliss and purity dismissed by simple entailment and a pointer back to Part 12 — closing on the parable of a blind man who can never quite piece together the true color of milk from four true but partial comparisons. Mañjuśrī then presses the Buddha on whether tonight's teaching is itself something new, drawing five similes — an eye's seeing, a struck fire, ghee churned from curds, a sprout from a seed, a drum's sound — that show "turning the wheel of the Dharma" to be, in the deepest sense, an instance of the very not-acting this whole sūtra has been describing all along. The fascicle then catalogs the twenty-five concentrations that break the twenty-five states of existence, praises this sūtra itself as the ghee at the summit of a six-stage dairy simile for the Buddha's own teaching career, records Kāśyapa's vow to give his own skin as paper and blood as ink to preserve it, and closes on one of this sūtra's most celebrated passages: the Buddha's account of a former life as a Himalayan ascetic who heard half a verse of the Dharma from a demon and gave up his body to hear the rest.
