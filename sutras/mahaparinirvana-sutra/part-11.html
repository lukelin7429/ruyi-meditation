The Man and the Rākṣasa's Floating Bag

This fascicle closes 現病品, the sūtra's chapter on the illness the Buddha has just manifested, with Kāśyapa's full case for why it cannot be real: an elaborate catalogue of what genuine sickness looks like, none of it applicable to a buddha, met by the Buddha rising, sending light from every pore of his body, and emptying the hells and hungry-ghost and animal realms of every being but the icchantika. The Buddha's own answer catalogues a string of paradoxical statements — that he is a lion, and not a lion; a parent, and not a parent — as this sūtra's own secret teaching, before naming the three conditions truly beyond any cure. The fascicle then opens 聖行品, the sūtra's chapter on noble conduct, with its most famous parable: a man crossing the ocean who will not surrender even a grain's worth of his own floating bag to a starving rākṣasa, however much is asked of him.

The Illness That Was Never There

Chinese Source

爾時迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如來已免一切疾病，患苦悉除，無復怖畏。世尊！一切眾生有四毒箭，則為病因。何等為四？貪欲、瞋恚、愚癡、憍慢。若有病因，則有病生，所謂愛熱肺病、上氣吐逆、膚體㿇㿇、其心悶亂、下痢噦噎、小便淋瀝、眼耳疼痛、背滿腹脹、顛狂乾消鬼魅所著。如是種種身心諸病，諸佛世尊悉無復有。今日如來何緣顧命文殊師利，而作是言：『我今背痛，汝等當為大眾說法？』有二因緣，則無病苦。何等為二？一者憐愍一切眾生，二者給施病者醫藥。如來往昔已於無量萬億劫中修菩薩道，常行愛語，利益眾生不令苦惱，施疾病者種種醫藥，何緣於今自言有病？

「世尊！世有病人，或坐或臥不安處所，或索飲食，勅誡家屬，修治產業，何故如來默然而臥，不教弟子聲聞人等，尸波羅蜜、諸禪解脫、三摩跋提、修諸正勤？何緣不說如是甚深大乘經典？如來何故不以無量方便，教大迦葉、人中象王、諸大人等，令不退於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提？何故不治諸惡比丘受畜一切不淨物者？世尊實無有病，云何默然，右脇而臥？

「諸菩薩等，凡所給施病者醫藥，所得善根悉施眾生，而共迴向一切種智，為除眾生諸煩惱障、業障、報障。煩惱障者，貪欲、瞋恚、愚癡，忿怒、纏蓋、焦惱、嫉妬、慳悋、奸詐、諛諂、無慚無愧，慢慢慢、不如慢、增上慢、我慢、邪慢、憍慢，放逸貢高、懟恨諍訟、邪命諂媚、詐現異相、以利求利、惡求多求，無有恭敬、不隨教誨、親近惡友、貪利無厭、纏縛難解，欲於惡欲、貪於惡貪、身見有見及以無見、頻申憙睡、欠呿不樂、貪嗜飲食、其心瞢瞢、心緣異想、不善思惟、身口多惡、好憙多語，諸根闇鈍、發言多虛，常為欲覺、恚覺、害覺之所覆蓋，是名煩惱障。業障者，五無間罪，重惡之病。報障者，生在地獄、畜生、餓鬼，誹謗正法及一闡提，是名報障。如是三障名為大病。而諸菩薩於無量劫修菩提時，給施一切疾病醫藥，常作是願，令諸眾生永斷如是三障重病。

「復次世尊！菩薩摩訶薩修菩提時，給施一切病者醫藥，常作是願，願令眾生永斷諸病，得成如來金剛之身。又願一切無量眾生作妙藥王，斷除一切諸惡重病，願諸眾生得阿伽陀藥，以是藥力能除一切無量惡毒。又願眾生於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提無有退轉，速得成就無上佛藥，消除一切煩惱毒箭。又願眾生勤修精進，成就如來金剛之心，作微妙藥療治眾病，不令有人生諍訟想。亦願眾生作大藥樹，療治一切諸惡重病。又願眾生拔出毒箭，得成如來無上光明。又願眾生得入如來智慧大藥微密法藏。世尊！菩薩如是，已於無量百千萬億那由他劫發是誓願，令諸眾生悉無復病，何緣如來乃於今日唱言有病？

「復次世尊！世有病人，不能坐起、俯仰、進止，飲食不御，漿水不下，亦復不能教戒諸子，修治家業。爾時父母、妻子、兄弟、親屬、知識，各於是人生必死想。世尊！如來今日亦復如是，右脇而臥，無所論說，此閻浮提有諸愚人，當作是念：『如來正覺必當涅槃。』生滅盡想。而如來性實不畢竟入於涅槃。何以故？如來常住無變易故，以是因緣，不應說言我今背痛。

「復次世尊！世有病者，身體羸損，若偃若側，臥著床褥，爾時家室心生惡賤，起必死想。如來今者亦復如是，當為外道九十五種之所輕慢，生無常想。彼諸外道當作是言：『不如我等，以我性常，自在時節、微塵等法而為常住，無有變易。沙門瞿曇無常所遷，是變易法。』以是義故，世尊！今日不應默然，右脇而臥。

「復次世尊！世有病者，四大增損，互不調適，羸瘦乏極，是故不能隨意坐起，臥著床褥。如來四大無不和適，身力具足亦無羸損。世尊！如十小牛力不如一大牛力，十大牛力不如一青牛力，十青牛力不如一凡象力，十凡象力不如一野象力，十野象力不如一二牙象力，十二牙象力不如一四牙象力，十四牙象力不如雪山一白象力，十雪山白象力不如一香象力，十香象力不如一青象力，十青象力不如一黃象力，十黃象力不如一赤象力，十赤象力不如一白象力，十白象力不如一山象力，十山象力不如一優鉢羅象力，十優鉢羅象力不如一拘物頭象力，十拘物頭象力不如一分陀利象力，十分陀利象力不如人中一力士力，十人中力士力不如一鉢健提力，十鉢健提力不如一八臂那羅延力，十那羅延力不如一十住菩薩一節之力。一切凡夫，身中諸節，節不相到。人中力士，節頭相到。鉢健提身，諸節相接。那羅延身，節頭相拘。十住菩薩諸節骨解，盤龍相結，是故菩薩其力最大。世界成時，從金剛際起金剛座，上至道場菩提樹下，菩薩坐已，其心即時逮得十力。如來今者不應如彼嬰孩小兒，嬰孩小兒愚癡無智，無所能說，以是義故，隨意偃側，無人譏訶。

如來世尊有大智慧，照明一切；人中之龍，具大威德；成就神通無上仙人，永斷疑網，已拔毒箭，進止安詳，威儀具足，得無所畏。今者何故右脇而臥，令諸人天悲愁苦惱？」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the Tathāgata has already been freed from every illness, every affliction and suffering entirely removed, with nothing left to fear. World-Honored One, every being carries four poisoned arrows, which are the very cause of illness. What are these four? Craving, anger, delusion, and pride. Where there is a cause of illness, illness arises from it — feverish lung disease from craving, choking and vomiting from rising breath, skin eruptions, a mind thrown into confusion, dysentery and hiccupping, difficulty urinating, pain in the eyes and ears, a swollen back and distended belly, madness, wasting away, possession by evil spirits. Every buddha, every world-honored one, is entirely free of every such illness of body and mind. For what reason, then, does the Tathāgata today turn to instruct Mañjuśrī, saying: 'My back aches now — you should teach the Dharma for the great assembly in my place'? There are two causes and conditions through which one comes to be free of illness. What are these two? First, compassion for every being; second, giving medicine to the sick. The Tathāgata, in ages past, has already, throughout countless millions of kalpas, cultivated the bodhisattva path, forever speaking words of loving-kindness, benefiting beings so that they need not suffer, giving every kind of medicine to the sick — for what reason, then, does he now say of himself that he is ill?

"World-Honored One, when there is a sick person in the world, he sits or lies restlessly, unable to settle anywhere; he may call for food and drink, or instruct his household on the management of their affairs. Why, then, does the Tathāgata lie silent, teaching his disciples, the śrāvakas, none of the pāramitā of moral discipline, none of the dhyānas and liberations, none of the samāpattis, none of the right endeavors? For what reason does he not teach this very profound scripture of the Great Vehicle? Why does the Tathāgata not use his own countless skillful means to teach the great Kāśyapa, this elephant-king among humankind, and every great being, so that they never turn back from unsurpassed, complete awakening? Why does he not correct those wicked monks who keep and hoard every kind of improper possession? The World-Honored One, in truth, has no illness at all — why, then, does he lie silent, upon his right side?

"Every bodhisattva, whenever he gives medicine to the sick, gives entirely of the wholesome root thereby obtained to beings, dedicating it together toward the wisdom of all modes, for the sake of removing beings' own afflictive hindrance, karmic hindrance, and retributive hindrance. The afflictive hindrance is craving, anger, delusion, wrath, entanglement and covering, agitation, envy, stinginess, deceit, flattery, shamelessness and lack of conscience, the pride of pride, the pride of the inferior, overweening pride, the pride of self, wrong pride, arrogance, heedlessness and haughtiness, resentment and contentiousness, a crooked livelihood and obsequiousness, feigning what one is not, seeking gain through gain, seeking wrongfully and seeking too much, showing no reverence, refusing instruction, keeping company with wicked friends, an insatiable craving for gain, entanglements hard to loosen, desire for what is wrongfully desired, craving for what is wrongfully craved, the view of a fixed self, the view of existence and the view of non-existence, constant yawning and drowsiness, restless discontent, a craving appetite for food and drink, a mind clouded and confused, a mind given over to distracting notions, unwholesome reflection, much wrongdoing in body and speech, a fondness for excessive talk, faculties dull and clouded, speech given largely to falsehood, and being forever covered over by thoughts of desire, of malice, and of harm — this is called the afflictive hindrance. The karmic hindrance is the five heinous crimes, the gravest illness of wrongdoing. The retributive hindrance is being born in hell, among the animals, or among the hungry ghosts, slandering the true Dharma, or becoming an icchantika — this is called the retributive hindrance. These three hindrances together are called the great illness. And every bodhisattva, cultivating bodhi throughout countless kalpas, gives medicine to the sick of every kind, forever making this vow: that beings may forever cut off this grave illness of the three hindrances.

"Furthermore, World-Honored One, when the bodhisattva-mahāsattva cultivates bodhi, giving medicine of every kind to the sick, he forever makes this vow: that beings may forever cut off every illness, and come to accomplish the Tathāgata's own body of vajra. He further vows that every one of the countless beings may become a king among wondrous medicines, cutting off every grave illness of wrongdoing; he vows that beings may obtain the agada medicine, whose power is able to remove every one of the countless poisons there is. He further vows that beings, never turning back from unsurpassed, complete awakening, may swiftly come to accomplish the unsurpassed medicine of buddhahood, dissolving the very poisoned arrow of every affliction. He further vows that beings, diligently cultivating with all energy, may accomplish the Tathāgata's own vajra mind, becoming a subtle and wondrous medicine that heals every illness, never giving rise, in anyone, to a thought of contention. He also vows that beings may become a great medicinal tree, healing every grave illness of wrongdoing. He further vows that beings, drawing out the poisoned arrow, may come to accomplish the Tathāgata's own unsurpassed light. He further vows that beings may enter the Tathāgata's own great medicine of wisdom, the subtle and secret treasury of the Dharma. World-Honored One, the bodhisattva, in this way, has already, throughout countless hundreds of thousands of millions of nayutas of kalpas, made these very vows, that beings may be entirely free of illness — for what reason, then, does the Tathāgata today proclaim that he himself is ill?

"Furthermore, World-Honored One, when there is a sick person in the world, unable to sit up or rise, to bend or straighten, to move about, unable to take food or drink, unable even to swallow water, and unable, further, to instruct and admonish his own children, or manage his household affairs — at such a time, his parents, wife and children, brothers, relatives, and friends all form, of this person, the thought that he must surely die. World-Honored One, the Tathāgata today is just the same, lying upon his right side, saying nothing at all — and the foolish people of this Jambudvīpa will surely think to themselves: 'The Tathāgata, the Perfectly Awakened One, must now be about to enter nirvāṇa,' giving rise to a thought of his utter extinction. Yet the Tathāgata's own nature, in truth, never finally enters nirvāṇa at all. Why is this? Because the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change — and it is for this very reason that he should not say, 'My back now aches.'

"Furthermore, World-Honored One, when there is a sick person in the world whose body has grown emaciated and weak, lying flat or on his side, confined to his own bed — at such a time, his own household comes to regard him with contempt and disdain, giving rise to the thought that he must surely die. The Tathāgata today is just the same: he will surely be treated with contempt by every one of the ninety-five heterodox schools, who will give rise to the thought that he is impermanent. Those heterodox teachers will surely say: 'He is no match for us — for our own nature is permanent, whether we speak of mastery, of time, or of the atom, each of these abides permanently, without any change; but the ascetic Gautama is swept along by impermanence, a thing subject to change.' It is for this very reason, World-Honored One, that you should not today lie silent, upon your right side.

"Furthermore, World-Honored One, when there is a sick person in the world whose four great elements grow excessive or deficient, thrown out of balance with one another, exhausted and utterly spent, he is thereby unable to sit up or rise as he wishes, confined to his own bed. The Tathāgata's own four great elements are never anything but perfectly balanced, his bodily strength complete, never weak or exhausted at all. World-Honored One, the strength of ten small oxen does not match that of one large ox; the strength of ten large oxen does not match that of one blue-black ox; the strength of ten blue-black oxen does not match that of one ordinary elephant; the strength of ten ordinary elephants does not match that of one wild elephant; the strength of ten wild elephants does not match that of one two-tusked elephant; the strength of ten two-tusked elephants does not match that of one four-tusked elephant; the strength of ten four-tusked elephants does not match that of one white elephant of the Himalayas; the strength of ten Himalayan white elephants does not match that of one fragrant elephant; the strength of ten fragrant elephants does not match that of one blue-black elephant; the strength of ten blue-black elephants does not match that of one yellow elephant; the strength of ten yellow elephants does not match that of one red elephant; the strength of ten red elephants does not match that of one white elephant; the strength of ten white elephants does not match that of one mountain elephant; the strength of ten mountain elephants does not match that of one utpala elephant; the strength of ten utpala elephants does not match that of one kumuda elephant; the strength of ten kumuda elephants does not match that of one puṇḍarīka elephant; the strength of ten puṇḍarīka elephants does not match that of one human wrestler; the strength of ten human wrestlers does not match that of one prākhaṇḍin; the strength of ten prākhaṇḍins does not match that of one eight-armed Nārāyaṇa; and the strength of ten Nārāyaṇas does not match the strength of a single joint of one bodhisattva at the tenth abiding-stage. In every ordinary person, the body's own joints do not quite meet one another. In a human wrestler, the ends of the joints meet fully. In the body of a prākhaṇḍin, every joint connects. In the body of Nārāyaṇa, the ends of every joint interlock. In a bodhisattva at the tenth abiding-stage, every joint and bone is bound together like coiled dragons — and this is why a bodhisattva's own strength is the very greatest there is. When the world first comes into being, from the very edge of the vajra foundation there arises a vajra seat, reaching up to the bodhi tree at the seat of enlightenment; and once the bodhisattva is seated there, his mind at once attains the ten powers. The Tathāgata should not now be like a small infant — a small infant, foolish and without wisdom, unable to say anything at all, is for this very reason free to lie however he wishes, without anyone reproaching him for it.

"The Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, possesses great wisdom, illuminating everything; he is the very dragon among humankind, possessed of great, majestic virtue; he has accomplished the unsurpassed sage's own supernormal powers, forever cutting through the net of every doubt, having already drawn out the poisoned arrow, moving and resting with perfect composure, complete in every dignified bearing, having attained fearlessness in every respect. Why, then, does he now lie upon his right side, causing every god and human to grieve and suffer?"

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

No surviving portion of Huiyuan's commentary treats this opening speech line by line, though his later summary confirms that he read it as building seven distinct arguments, each answered in turn once the Buddha finally speaks — a structure this collection's next several reading notes will trace as each argument is taken up. Two features of the speech itself are worth flagging on their own terms. First, the three-hindrance taxonomy — an extended catalogue of affliction, then the single karmic hindrance of the five heinous crimes, then the retributive hindrance of hell, the animal realm, hungry ghosts, slandering the Dharma, and the icchantika — quietly folds the icchantika question this collection has tracked since Part 5 into the definition of illness itself: to be an icchantika just is, on this taxonomy, to suffer from an illness in its most acute, retributive form. Second, the escalating ladder of strength — nineteen doublings from a small ox up through nine grades of elephant to a human wrestler, a prākhaṇḍin, an eight-armed Nārāyaṇa, and finally a single joint of a bodhisattva at the tenth abiding-stage — is not really an argument about physical strength at all: its rhetorical function is to make Kāśyapa's underlying claim vivid by sheer accumulation, each rung making the next barely conceivable, so that a genuine buddha's own condition lands as something categorically off this entire scale rather than merely at its top.

The Buddha Rises

Chinese Source

爾時迦葉菩薩即於佛前而說偈言：

「瞿曇大聖德，　願起演妙法，
不應如小兒，　病者臥床席。

調御天人師，　倚臥雙樹間，
下愚凡夫見，　當言必涅槃。

不知方等典，　甚深佛所行，
不見微密藏，　猶盲不見道。

惟有諸菩薩，　文殊師利等，
能解是甚深，　譬如善射者。

三世諸世尊，　大悲為根本，
如是大慈悲，　今為何所在？

若無大悲者，　是則不名佛，
佛若必涅槃，　是則不名常。

惟願無上尊，　哀受我等請，
利益於眾生，　摧伏諸外道。」

爾時世尊，大悲熏心，知諸眾生各各所念，將欲隨順畢竟利益，即從臥起，結跏趺坐，顏貌熈怡，如融金聚，面目端嚴，猶月盛滿，形容清淨，無諸垢穢，放大光明充遍虛空。其光大盛，過百千日，照于東方、南西北方、四維上下諸佛世界，惠施眾生大智之炬，悉令得滅無明黑闇，令百千億那由他眾生，安止不退菩提之心。爾時世尊，心無疑慮，如師子王，以三十二大人之相、八十種好莊嚴其身。於其身上一切毛孔，一一毛孔出一蓮花，其花微妙各具千葉，純真金色，琉璃為莖，金剛為鬚，玫瑰為臺，形大團圓猶如車輪，是諸蓮花，各出種種雜色光明，青黃赤白紫頗梨色。

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this entire display as the sūtra's own answer to the assembly's unspoken inference — if the Buddha shows illness, does that mean his own power is beginning to fail? — answered not by argument but by demonstration: at the very moment he is supposedly too weak to rise, he sends forth a light that empties every hell, every realm of hungry ghosts, and the whole animal world at once. He structures the rescue in a consistent three-step pattern repeated for each realm — the light arrives, the immediate physical suffering particular to that realm is relieved, and only then is the tathāgatagarbha teaching itself spoken, the beings dying into a fortunate rebirth on hearing it — a sequence that keeps easing suffering and teaching liberation as two distinct, sequential acts of compassion rather than collapsing them into one. He is also careful to note that each realm empties with its own exception: the icchantika alone remains in hell, slanderers of the Vaipulya scriptures alone remain among the hungry ghosts, slanderers of the true Dharma alone remain among the animals — a variation in language, Huiyuan suggests, that maps roughly onto how directly each group has rejected the specific teaching that would otherwise have reached them, rather than naming three unrelated exceptions by accident.

"Kāśyapa, it is as I have already taught before: 'Should any good son or good daughter be well able to cultivate and discipline his own deeds of body, speech, and mind, then, at the moment he gives up his own life, though his relatives may take up his own corpse, whether burning it with fire, casting it into a great body of water, or abandoning it among the graves, where foxes, wolves, and birds of prey compete to devour it, his own mind, thought, and consciousness will nonetheless at once be reborn in a wholesome destiny. A statement of this kind is itself the Tathāgata's own secret teaching. Kāśyapa, my speaking of illness now is likewise just the same — this too is the Tathāgata's own secret teaching. This is why I turned to instruct Mañjuśrī: 'My back aches now — you should teach the Dharma for the fourfold assembly in my place.' Kāśyapa, the Tathāgata, the Perfectly Awakened One, in truth has no illness at all; his lying upon his right side is likewise no final entering into nirvāṇa at all. Kāśyapa, this Mahāparinirvāṇa is itself the exceedingly profound meditative absorption of every buddha — a meditative absorption of this kind is not within the domain of any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha at all.

"Kāśyapa, as for what you asked earlier — why does the Tathāgata recline without rising, without calling for food or drink, without instructing his household on the management of their affairs? Kāśyapa, the very nature of empty space likewise has no sitting or rising, no calling for food or drink, no instructing a household on the management of its affairs; it likewise has no coming or going, no birth or death, no youth or age, no emerging or vanishing, no injury or breaking, no bondage or liberation; it neither speaks of itself nor of another, neither understands itself nor another; it is neither at ease nor ill. Good son, every buddha, every world-honored one, is just the same, exactly like empty space — how, then, could there ever be any illness or suffering in him at all?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Buddha's past-life account — a śrāvaka under the buddha Unsurpassed Victor, studying this very sūtra there — as answering, point for point, Kāśyapa's earlier claim that a bodhisattva's vows over countless kalpas should already have secured him a body free of illness: this is the Buddha's own record of exactly that vow already fulfilled. On the catalogue of paradoxical statements that follows — ten items naming the Buddha (a lion who is not a lion, a dragon who has set that identity aside, neither human nor god nor any of the eight classes of spirit, an ocean and a Sumeru without salt or stone, a lotus that is not a lotus, a parent, a ship's captain, a caravan's leader, a subduer of Māra with no hostile intent, a healer of boils who is no physician) followed by a final, eleventh item about beings generally — Huiyuan reads all eleven as working by the same mechanism. Ordinary logic holds that body and mind alike perish entirely at death, yet the Buddha has also taught, elsewhere, that a virtuous person's own mind "goes" somewhere at the moment of death — a statement that does not fully accord with ultimate logic either, since nothing, strictly speaking, comes or goes at all; there is only a succession of resembling states, each following the last without any real transit between them. This is why Huiyuan reads the rebirth teaching as belonging to the very same category as "the Tathāgata is a lion, and yet not a lion": both are useful, practical descriptions that serve their purpose without being literally true in the way they sound, and "I am ill" belongs to this same family of secret teaching, spoken for Kāśyapa's own sake and for beings in ages to come, never a report of the Tathāgata's actual condition.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Kāśyapa, it is like a sick person who, having an attendant to nurse him and the right medicine given, may be cured — but who, lacking any of these three things, cannot be cured. Śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas are just the same: having heard the Dharma from a buddha or a bodhisattva, they are thereby able to give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening — it is not that they could give rise to this mind without ever hearing the Dharma at all. Kāśyapa, it is likewise like a sick person who may be cured whether or not he has an attendant to nurse him, whether or not he is given the right medicine. Kāśyapa, the second person has cut off the bonds of the three fetters, and thinned out craving, anger, and delusion, attaining the fruit of once-returning, called 'one coming and going,' forever cutting off every suffering and entering nirvāṇa. Kāśyapa, this is called the second person's place of sickness-conduct — such a person will, in a future age, after sixty thousand kalpas, come to accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening. Kāśyapa, the third person has cut off the five lower fetters, attaining the fruit of non-returning, never coming back to this world again, forever cutting off every suffering and entering nirvāṇa — this is called the third person's place of sickness-conduct. Such a person will, in a future age, after forty thousand kalpas, come to accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening. Kāśyapa, the fourth person has forever cut off craving, anger, and delusion, attaining the fruit of arhatship, his affliction entirely exhausted, entering nirvāṇa — though not, like the solitary rhinoceros, walking a path entirely alone — this is called the fourth person's place of sickness-conduct. Such a person will, in a future age, after ten thousand kalpas, come to accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening. Kāśyapa, this is called the fifth person's place of sickness-conduct — unlike the Tathāgata at all."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this closing stretch of 現病品 as sorting three broad classes of person — ordinary beings, the two lesser vehicles, and bodhisattvas — and then, having named the three truly incurable conditions among ordinary beings, deliberately singling out the middle class, the two lesser vehicles, for the fascicle's most detailed treatment. His reasoning: the two lesser vehicles are the Great Vehicle's own natural point of comparison, so spelling out exactly where they stand illuminates, by contrast, where the Great Vehicle itself stands — small measured against great makes great legible in a way it would not be on its own. On the descending kalpa-counts attached to each of the five kinds of person — eighty thousand, sixty thousand, forty thousand, twenty thousand, ten thousand — he notes these track how many fetters remain uncut at each stage, and flags a technical point worth carrying forward: even a stream-enterer, barely past the first fetters, is already said to be moving toward "bodhi," which sounds premature until one recalls that this sūtra uses "bodhi" in five distinct senses elsewhere (the arising of the resolve, its subduing, its clarification, its full emergence, and its unsurpassed completion) — the first of these already present the moment a being enters the fortunate destinies, long before anything resembling full awakening. What every one of these five kinds of person shares, and what finally distinguishes every one of them from the Tathāgata, is simply that they still have a place where sickness-conduct — some remaining fetter, however fine — can be said to apply at all; the Tathāgata, this whole chapter has argued, does not.

Having gone forth, he upholds the precepts, his conduct never lacking, moving and resting with perfect composure, transgressing in nothing at all — and, even toward the smallest offense, his heart gives rise to dread, his mind guarding the precepts as firmly as vajra.

"Good son, it is like a person carrying an inflated floating bag, wishing to cross the great ocean. There is, at that time, within the ocean, a rākṣasa demon, who at once begs this person for his own floating bag. Hearing this, the person thinks to himself: 'Should I give this to him now, I would surely sink and die.' He answers: 'Rākṣasa, you may as well kill me — you shall not have this floating bag.' The rākṣasa says further: 'If you cannot give me the whole of it, then grant me at least half.' The person still refuses to give it. The rākṣasa says further: 'If you will not grant me half, I beg you, then, to give me a third of it.' The person refuses. The rākṣasa says further: 'If you cannot even do this, then give me as much as would fit in the palm of a hand.' The person refuses. The rākṣasa says further: 'If you cannot even give me that much, then I, now starving and desperate, pressed by every suffering, beg you to save me with even as much as a single mote of dust.' The person answers: 'What you now ask is, in truth, not very much at all — yet I am only now about to cross this ocean, and do not know how near or far the way ahead may be. Should I give even this much to you, the air would gradually leak out, and how, then, could I ever survive the peril of this great ocean? I might well, midway, sink into the water and die.' Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, upholding the precepts, is just the same, guarding his own floating bag exactly as that man crossing the ocean did.

"When a bodhisattva guards the precepts in this way, the many wicked rākṣasas of affliction constantly say to him: 'You should trust me — I would never deceive you. Only break the four gravest precepts, and keep the rest — through this very cause and condition, you shall find peace and ease, and attain nirvāṇa.' The bodhisattva should, at such a time, say this in reply: 'I would rather uphold these very precepts and fall into the Avīci hell than ever transgress them and be reborn in heaven instead.' The rākṣasa of affliction says further: 'If you cannot break the four gravest precepts, then break one of the offenses requiring temporary suspension — through this very cause and condition, you shall find peace and ease, and attain nirvāṇa.' The bodhisattva should likewise not follow these words. The rākṣasa says further: 'If you cannot commit an offense requiring temporary suspension, then you may at least deliberately commit an offense of gross wrongdoing — through this very cause and condition, you shall find peace and ease, and attain nirvāṇa.' The bodhisattva, at such a time, likewise does not follow this. The rākṣasa says further: 'If you cannot commit an offense of gross wrongdoing, then you may commit an offense requiring forfeiture — through this very cause and condition, you may find peace and ease, and enter nirvāṇa.' The bodhisattva, at such a time, likewise does not follow this. The rākṣasa says further: 'If you cannot commit an offense requiring forfeiture, then break an offense of simple expiation — through this very cause and condition, you shall find peace and ease, and attain nirvāṇa.' The bodhisattva, at such a time, likewise does not follow this. The rākṣasa says further: 'If you cannot commit an offense of simple expiation, then I beg you, at least, to transgress an offense of wrongful conduct — through this very cause and condition, you may find peace and ease, and enter nirvāṇa.' The bodhisattva, at such a time, thinks to himself: 'Should I now commit even an offense of wrongful conduct, and fail to confess it openly, I would then be unable to cross to the far shore of birth and death, and attain nirvāṇa at all.' The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, with regard to even the smallest of these disciplinary rules, guards them with a mind as firm as vajra. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva upholds the four gravest precepts and the offense of wrongful conduct with equal reverence and firmness, without any difference at all. Should a bodhisattva be able to hold to this so firmly, he thereby fully possesses the five roots of every precept — namely, the precept of purity in the very root of a bodhisattva's own karmic action, the precept of purity in every accompanying circumstance before and after, the precept of purity in awareness that guards against every wicked thought, the precept of purity in mindfulness that protects right recollection, and the precept of dedication toward unsurpassed, complete awakening.

"Kāśyapa, this bodhisattva-mahāsattva has, further, two kinds of precept: first, the precept received through worldly instruction; second, the precept attained through the true Dharma. Should a bodhisattva attain the precept of the true Dharma, he will never commit evil at all; the precept received through worldly instruction is obtained only through the formal procedure of announcement and threefold motion. Furthermore, good son, there are two kinds of precept: first, the precepts grave by their own nature; second, the precepts that avoid what the world finds shameful. The precepts grave by their own nature are the four gravest precepts."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan glosses each of the five practices by how its own name is formed: "noble conduct" is named for its practitioners, since buddhas and bodhisattvas are together called noble; "pure conduct" (梵行) and "heavenly conduct" (天行) are both named for the quality they share, purity, at two different registers the Sanskrit distinguishes and Chinese collapses into one word; "sick conduct" is named by function, conduct that treats illness; "infant-like conduct" is named by simile, conduct as free of calculation as an infant who understands nothing at all. Of the five, this sūtra treats noble conduct in this chapter, pure conduct in the next, infant-like conduct later still, and heavenly conduct not at all here — Huiyuan points the reader to the Avataṃsaka Sūtra for it instead. Sick conduct's own textual home is more contested: some readers, he notes, take the five kinds of "sickness-conduct" person just described in 現病品 as fulfilling this fifth category — but Huiyuan disagrees, since those five are śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, not bodhisattvas, and the conduct described there is itself the disease, not the treatment of it. He locates true "sick conduct" further ahead, in 梵行品's account of King Ajātaśatru's repentance, where the Buddha treats both the king's psychological illness of guilt and his physical illness of boils at once — conduct that heals sickness, matching the category's own name, rather than conduct that merely exhibits it. He also notes that this same chapter names a sixth item, "the Tathāgata's own conduct," identified simply as this Mahāparinirvāṇa itself — not a sixth practice alongside the other five, but the already-accomplished ground the five practices are cultivated toward and depend upon.

On the floating-bag parable, Huiyuan's mapping is direct: the man is the bodhisattva, the floating bag is the precepts, the ocean is birth and death, and the rākṣasa is affliction itself, wanting nothing more than to talk the bodhisattva into surrendering some fraction of his own discipline. He flags one detail worth not over-reading: the rākṣasa's five successive demands (the whole bag, then half, a third, a handful, a single mote of dust) do not map one-to-one onto the six categories of precept the parallel dialogue that follows walks through (the four gravest offenses, offenses requiring temporary suspension, gross wrongdoing, forfeiture, simple expiation, and wrongful conduct) — the numbers were never meant to line up exactly. The point of both passages together is simply that grave and minor precepts alike are guarded with the very same unbending resolve, all the way down to the smallest rule a monastic keeps.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this long catalogue as organized in four movements rather than as one undifferentiated list: not wrongfully taking (trade, false weights and measures, exploiting another's influence, malicious dealing); not wrongfully spending (idling in bed past dawn, unnecessary household enterprise); not wrongfully hoarding, by far the longest movement, itself running through eight further categories in sequence — land and cultivation, livestock and beasts, servants, precious materials, food stores, surplus clothing, the five kinds of propagating seed, and every article of luxury; and, finally, not wrongfully acting — watching contests and military displays, listening to music, playing games of chance, practicing divination, running messages for a royal household, and gossiping about kings, war, or famine. The four movements are not, on Huiyuan's reading, an arbitrary sequence: what a monastic must never wrongfully acquire, spend, keep, or do covers the whole cycle of how worldly entanglement actually accumulates, one stage feeding the next. The precepts grave by their own nature (the four gravest offenses) and these precepts against giving the world cause for scorn are held, the text insists explicitly, with exactly the same undivided reverence — a claim this collection has already seen made about the floating bag, now spelled out item by item across the whole of a monastic's ordinary life.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan sorts the twelve vows into three groups: a first, on its own, against the root precept the whole sequence orbits (chastity); six tied to the six things a donor might offer — robe, food, bedding, medicine, housing, reverence — each vowing self-destruction sooner than receiving that gift with an impure body; and five tied to the five sense faculties — eyes, ears, nose, tongue, and body — each vowing self-mutilation sooner than letting desire enter through that particular gate. He then maps the nine qualities of the precept dedicated to all beings at the passage's close onto the bodhisattva grounds: the pure and wholesome precepts as the basic pair (restraint from wrong, active cultivation of right); the unbroken and unshattered precepts specifying purity further; the precept of the Great Vehicle proper to the lineage stage and above; the precept that never turns back proper to the first ground; the precept in accord proper to the second ground; the ultimate precept proper to the eighth ground; and the precept complete in the pāramitā proper to buddhahood itself — a single list quietly tracing the entire path from a new monastic's basic vow to a buddha's own completion.

On the "unshakable ground" itself, Huiyuan flags a disambiguation worth keeping in mind: standard bodhisattva-ground doctrine names two distinct "unshakable" grounds, an initial one at the first ground and a later, more advanced one at the eighth. What precept-keeping alone secures here is explicitly the first, initial unshakability, not the eighth-ground version reached only much further along the path — a bodhisattva at this stage is unmoved by ordinary temptation and heterodox pressure, not yet at the point where even subtle effort itself falls away. The chapter's own closing definition of "noble" gives five grounds for the term, one turning on constant contemplation of every dharma's own empty, still nature and four turning on practice — moral discipline, concentration joined with wisdom, the seven noble treasures, and the seven factors of awakening — closing 聖行品's opening movement on the same note it began: nobility, in this sūtra's own vocabulary, is something earned through sustained practice, never a rank inherited or assumed.

Part 11 Complete

This closes Part 11 (fascicle 11 of 40), completing 現病品第六 and opening 聖行品第七之一: Kāśyapa's full case that the illness the Buddha has just manifested cannot be real, a light from every pore emptying the hells, the hungry-ghost realm, and the animal world of every being but the icchantika, a catalogue of paradoxical statements the sūtra calls the Tathāgata's own secret teaching, and the three conditions — slandering the Great Vehicle, the five heinous crimes, the icchantika — that remain beyond any cure. The fascicle then opens the sūtra's chapter on noble conduct with a bodhisattva's motivation for renunciation, the famous parable of a man crossing the ocean who will not surrender his floating bag to a starving rākṣasa however little is asked, the precepts that avoid what the world finds shameful, and a string of vows to suffer bodily destruction sooner than receive an offering, or indulge a sense, with an impure mind.
