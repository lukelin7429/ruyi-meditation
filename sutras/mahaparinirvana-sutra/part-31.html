The Bodhisattva Who Vowed to Suffer Every Fate

Lion's Roar presses a hard technical question about meditation — does insight alone destroy affliction, or does it need concentration's help? — before the fascicle turns to Cunda's own sharper challenge: if giving to the Buddha yields an inexhaustible reward, and karma reliably ripens into its fruit, how could Cunda's own single act of generosity ever stop paying out long enough for him to actually attain awakening? The Buddha's answer distinguishes fixed karma from unfixed karma, and unfolds into an extraordinary sequence of his own past lives — as a hell-being, an animal, a hungry ghost, an outcaste executioner, a border-dweller, a eunuch, even a follower of a rival ascetic sect — each one freely chosen rather than actually earned, followed by the story of a shipwrecked merchant blinded by his own partner's greed, and a king who mutilates a forbearing ascetic limb by limb, each restored by nothing more than the truth of an untroubled mind.

Does Wisdom Alone Destroy Affliction?

Chinese Source

◎師子吼言：「世尊！如經中說，若毘婆舍那能破煩惱，何故復修奢摩他耶？」佛言：「善男子！汝言毘婆舍那破煩惱者，是義不然。何以故？有智慧時則無煩惱，有煩惱時則無智慧，云何而言毘婆舍那能破煩惱？善男子！譬如明時無闇，闇時無明。若有說言，明能破闇，無有是處。善男子！誰有智慧？誰有煩惱？而言智慧能破煩惱；如其無者則無所破。善男子！若言智慧能破煩惱，為到故破？不到故破？若不到破者，凡夫眾生則應能破。若到故破者，初念應破，若初念不破，後亦不破。若初到便破，是則不到，云何說言智慧能破？若言到與不到而能破者，是義不然。復次毘婆舍那破煩惱者，為獨能破？為伴故破？若獨能破，菩薩何故修八正道？若伴故破，當知獨則不能破也。若獨不能、伴亦不能，如一盲人不能見色，雖伴眾盲，亦不能見；毘婆舍那亦復如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar said: "World-Honored One, as taught within scripture, if vipaśyanā alone is able to destroy affliction, why, then, must śamatha also be cultivated?" The Buddha said: "Good son, to say that vipaśyanā destroys affliction is, I think, not correct. Why is this? When wisdom is present, affliction is absent; when affliction is present, wisdom is absent — how, then, can it be said that vipaśyanā is able to destroy affliction at all? Good son, it is like light and darkness: when light is present, darkness is absent, and when darkness is present, light is absent — should anyone say that light destroys darkness, this could never be so. Good son, who, exactly, possesses wisdom, and who, exactly, possesses affliction, such that one could say wisdom destroys affliction? Were there truly no such possessor at all, there would be nothing at all for it to destroy. Good son, should you say that wisdom is able to destroy affliction, does it destroy it by reaching it, or by not reaching it? If it destroys it without reaching it, then every ordinary being ought already to be able to destroy it. If it destroys it by reaching it, then this destruction ought to occur in the very first moment of thought — and, if it does not occur in the first moment, it will not occur in any later moment either. And, if it is destroyed the very instant it is reached, this is then no longer a case of 'reaching' at all — how, then, can one say that wisdom destroys it by reaching it? To say that it destroys affliction either by reaching it or by not reaching it is, I think, not correct either way. Furthermore, does vipaśyanā destroy affliction alone, by itself, or only with the aid of a companion? If it is able to destroy affliction alone, why, then, does the bodhisattva also cultivate the eightfold noble path? And, if it requires the aid of a companion, one should know that it is unable to destroy affliction alone. And, if it is unable to do so either alone or with a companion, this is exactly like a single blind man who is unable to see form — even joined by a great many other blind men, he would still be unable to see. Vipaśyanā is exactly the same."

Chinese Source

「善男子！如地堅性、火熱性、水濕性、風動性。而地堅性乃至風動性，非因緣作，其性自爾。如四大性，煩惱亦爾，性自是斷。若是斷者，云何而言智慧能斷？以是義故，毘婆舍那決定不能破諸煩惱。善男子！如鹽性醎，令異物醎；蜜本性甘，令異物甘；水本性濕，令異物濕；智慧性滅，令法滅者。是義不然。何以故？若法無滅，云何智慧強能令滅？若言鹽醎令異物醎，慧滅亦爾，能令異法滅者，是亦不然。何以故？智慧之性，念念滅故。若念念滅，云何而言能滅他法？以是義故，智慧之性不破煩惱。「善男子！一切諸法有二種滅：一者性滅，二者畢竟滅。若性滅者，云何而言智慧能滅？若言智慧能燒煩惱，如火燒物，是義不然。何以故？如火燒物，則有遺燼；智慧若爾，應有餘燼。如斧伐樹，斫處可見；智慧若爾，有何可見？慧若能令煩惱離者，如是煩惱應餘處現，如諸外道離六大城，拘尸那現；若是煩惱不餘處現，則知智慧不能令離。善男子！一切諸法性若自空，誰能令生？誰能令滅？異生異滅無造作者。善男子！若修習定，則得如是正知、正見，以是義故，我經中說：『若有比丘修習定者，能見五陰生滅之相。』善男子！若不修定，世間之事尚不能了，況於出世？若無定者，平處顛墜，心緣異法，口宣異言，耳聞異語，心解異義，欲造異字，手書異文，欲行異路，身涉異徑。若有修習三昧定者，則大利益，乃至阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, it is like earth's own nature to be solid, fire's own nature to be hot, water's own nature to be moist, and wind's own nature to be in motion — and this solidity, heat, moisture, and motion are not produced by any cause or condition at all, but are simply the very nature of these elements themselves. Affliction is exactly the same: its own extinction is simply its own nature. And, if this is so, how, then, can it be said that wisdom is what extinguishes it? It is for this very reason that vipaśyanā, most certainly, is not what destroys affliction at all. Good son, salt's own nature is to be salty, and it makes other things salty; honey's own nature is to be sweet, and it makes other things sweet; water's own nature is to be moist, and it makes other things moist — should you say that wisdom's own nature is to extinguish, and it thereby makes other dharmas become extinguished, this, I think, is not correct. Why is this? If a dharma has no capacity for extinction at all, how could wisdom possibly force it into extinction? And, should you say that, just as salt's saltiness makes other things salty, wisdom's own extinction likewise makes other dharmas become extinguished, this, too, is not correct. Why is this? Because wisdom's own nature perishes moment by moment — and, if it perishes moment by moment, how could it possibly extinguish any other dharma at all? It is for this very reason that wisdom's own nature does not destroy affliction. "Good son, every dharma, without exception, has two kinds of cessation: cessation by its own nature, and ultimate cessation. If it is cessation by its own nature, how, then, can it be said that wisdom brings about this cessation? Should you say that wisdom burns away affliction, exactly as fire burns an object, this, I think, is not correct. Why is this? When fire burns an object, ash and cinders remain — were wisdom truly like this, some remainder ought likewise to be left behind. When an axe fells a tree, the very mark of its cutting can be seen — were wisdom truly like this, what mark at all could possibly be seen? Should wisdom truly be able to cause affliction to depart, this very affliction ought then to reappear somewhere else — exactly as the six teachers, departing the six great cities, reappeared at Kuśinagara — and, since this affliction never reappears anywhere else at all, one should know that wisdom is not what causes it to depart. Good son, if every dharma, by its own nature, is already empty, who, then, could possibly cause anything to arise, and who could possibly cause anything to cease? Arising and ceasing are each, in their own way, without any agent who produces them at all. Good son, should one cultivate concentration, one thereby attains right knowledge and right view of exactly this kind — it is for this very reason that I teach, within my own scriptures: 'Should a monk cultivate concentration, he is thereby able to see the very mark of the five aggregates' own arising and ceasing.' Good son, without cultivating concentration, one cannot even fully grasp the affairs of the ordinary world — how much less, then, anything transcendent? Without concentration, one stumbles even on level ground; one's mind dwells on one thing while one's mouth speaks of another; one's ear hears one thing while one's mind grasps a different meaning entirely; one intends to write one character yet writes another with one's own hand; one intends to walk one road yet finds one's own body wandering down another path entirely. Should one cultivate the concentration of samādhi, one thereby gains great benefit, all the way up to unsurpassed, complete awakening."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lion's Roar's opening challenge exploits a genuine tension in ordinary Buddhist teaching: if wisdom and affliction are simply incompatible states that cannot coexist (as light and darkness cannot), then speaking of wisdom "destroying" affliction already smuggles in a causal relationship that the light/darkness pairing doesn't actually license — darkness isn't destroyed by light so much as simply absent wherever light is present. The dilemma that follows (does wisdom destroy affliction by reaching it or without reaching it? alone or with help?) systematically closes off every way of cashing out "destruction" as an actual causal transaction between two things, leaving affliction's cessation looking less like an event wisdom performs and more like affliction's own built-in impermanence finally showing itself — which is exactly the elements analogy's point: solidity, heat, and motion aren't imposed on earth, fire, and wind from outside, and affliction's own extinguishability may likewise just be what it already is.

The fire-and-axe counter-examples then test what a genuine act of "destruction" ought to look like — a remainder, a visible mark, a trace left behind — and affliction's cessation leaves none of these, which the passage reads not as proof wisdom fails to do anything, but as proof that whatever wisdom does is not analogous to ordinary physical destruction at all. Having spent the whole exchange undermining any naive picture of wisdom "destroying" affliction through force, the passage pivots to a wholly practical answer to the section's original question (why cultivate śamatha at all, if vipaśyanā destroys affliction): concentration is defended not as the metaphysical agent of destruction either, but as the precondition for right knowledge and functional competence in general — the person without concentration who stumbles on level ground and writes the wrong character is offered as evidence that whatever wisdom is, it cannot operate without concentration's own stabilizing support, regardless of how the metaphysics of "destruction" ultimately gets resolved.

Concentration and Wisdom, Working Together

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩摩訶薩具足二法能大利益：一者定，二者智。善男子！如刈菅草，執急則斷；菩薩摩訶薩修是二法，亦復如是。善男子！如拔堅木，先以手動後則易出；菩薩定慧亦復如是，先以定動，後以智拔。善男子！如浣垢衣，先以灰汁，後以清水，衣則鮮潔；菩薩定慧，亦復如是。善男子！如先讀誦，後則解義；菩薩定慧亦復如是。善男子！譬如勇人，先以鎧仗牢自莊嚴，然後御陣，能壞怨賊；菩薩定慧亦復如是。善男子！譬如巧匠，鉗鐹盛金，自在隨意撓攪融消；菩薩定慧亦復如是。善男子！譬如明鏡照了面像；菩薩定慧亦復如是。善男子！如先平地，然後下種，先從師受，後思惟義；菩薩定慧亦復如是。以是義故，菩薩摩訶薩修是二法能大利益。「善男子！菩薩摩訶薩修是二法，調攝五根，堪忍眾苦，所謂飢渴寒熱、打擲罵辱、惡獸所嚙、蚊虻所螫，常攝其心不令放逸，不為利養行於非法，客塵煩惱所不能污，不為諸邪異見所惑，常能遠離諸惡覺觀，不久成就阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，為欲成就利眾生故。善男子！菩薩摩訶薩修是二法，四倒暴風不能吹動，如須彌山雖為四風之所吹鼓不能令動；不為外道邪師所拔，如帝釋幢不可移轉；眾邪異術不能誑惑，常受微妙第一安樂，能解如來深祕密義，受樂不欣、逢苦不戚，諸天世人恭敬讚歎，明見生死及非生死，善能了知法界法性，身有常樂我淨之法，是則名為大涅槃樂。「善男子！定相者名空三昧，慧相者名無願三昧，捨相者名無相三昧。善男子！若有菩薩摩訶薩善知定時、慧時、捨時及知非時，是名菩薩摩訶薩行菩提道。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, a bodhisattva mahāsattva who is fully endowed with these two dharmas — concentration, and wisdom — is able to gain great benefit. Good son, it is like cutting reed-grass: gripped tightly, it is easily severed — a bodhisattva mahāsattva's cultivation of these two dharmas is exactly the same. Good son, it is like uprooting a firmly planted tree: one first loosens it by hand, and only then is it easily drawn out — a bodhisattva's concentration and wisdom are exactly the same, first loosening through concentration, and then drawing out through wisdom. Good son, it is like washing a soiled garment: one first applies lye, and only then clean water, and the garment becomes fresh and clean — a bodhisattva's concentration and wisdom are exactly the same. Good son, it is like first reciting a text, and only afterward coming to understand its meaning — a bodhisattva's concentration and wisdom are exactly the same. Good son, it is like a brave warrior who first arms himself securely with armor and weapons, and only then goes forth to battle, able to defeat the enemy — a bodhisattva's concentration and wisdom are exactly the same. Good son, it is like a skilled craftsman who holds gold with tongs over a crucible, freely stirring and melting it at will — a bodhisattva's concentration and wisdom are exactly the same. Good son, it is like a bright mirror that clearly reflects a face — a bodhisattva's concentration and wisdom are exactly the same. Good son, it is like leveling the ground before planting a seed, or first receiving instruction from a teacher before reflecting on its meaning — a bodhisattva's concentration and wisdom are exactly the same. It is for this very reason that a bodhisattva mahāsattva's cultivation of these two dharmas is able to yield great benefit. "Good son, a bodhisattva mahāsattva who cultivates these two dharmas thereby tames and governs his own five sense-faculties, able to endure every kind of suffering — hunger, thirst, cold, heat, beating, abuse, the bites of fierce beasts, the stings of mosquitoes and gadflies — forever governing his own mind so that it never grows heedless, never acting against the Dharma for the sake of personal gain, never stained by any adventitious affliction, never deceived by any wrong or heterodox view, forever able to depart from every evil thought and reflection, and, before long, accomplishing unsurpassed, complete awakening, for the sake of benefiting every being. Good son, a bodhisattva mahāsattva who cultivates these two dharmas cannot be shaken by the raging wind of the four inversions, exactly as Mount Sumeru, though buffeted by the four winds, cannot be made to move at all; he cannot be uprooted by any heterodox teacher, exactly as Indra's own banner cannot be dislodged; he cannot be deceived by any wrong or heterodox art; he forever receives the most subtle, supreme happiness; he is able to understand the Tathāgata's own profound, secret meaning; he feels no elation upon receiving happiness, nor any sorrow upon meeting with suffering; he is honored and praised by every god and every person in the world; he clearly sees both birth-and-death and what is not birth-and-death; he is well able to understand the Dharma-realm and the Dharma-nature; and his own body possesses the dharma of permanence, bliss, self, and purity — this is what is named the bliss of the great nirvāṇa. "Good son, the mark of concentration is named the emptiness samādhi; the mark of wisdom is named the wishlessness samādhi; and the mark of equanimity is named the signlessness samādhi. Good son, should a bodhisattva mahāsattva be well able to know the proper time for concentration, the proper time for wisdom, the proper time for equanimity, and the proper time for none of these, this is named a bodhisattva mahāsattva who truly practices the path to bodhi."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The eight similes for concentration-then-wisdom (grass gripped before it is cut, a tree loosened before it is pulled, a garment treated with lye before clean water, a text recited before it is understood, armor donned before battle, gold held steady before it is worked, a mirror's own stillness before it reflects, ground leveled before a seed is planted) all share one structural insight: in every case, the second action's success is not merely helped but actually made possible by the first, and the two are never interchangeable or reversible in order. Read together, they answer this fascicle's opening question from the opposite direction — rather than arguing abstractly about whether wisdom alone destroys affliction, they simply show, through eight ordinary crafts, what it looks like for a stabilizing prior condition to make a subsequent action effective, without needing to settle the metaphysics of "destruction" at all.

The passage's closing identification of concentration, wisdom, and equanimity with the three samādhis of emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness draws together threads running through this entire fascicle and the two before it: emptiness names what concentration reveals about the object contemplated, wishlessness names the freedom from craving that genuine wisdom produces, and signlessness names the equanimity that neither elevates happiness nor resists suffering — the same "ten marks" whose absence this fascicle's earlier section identified with nirvāṇa itself. Knowing "the proper time for none of these" — a fourth category alongside knowing when to cultivate each of the three — quietly acknowledges that even this threefold framework is itself a tool to be picked up and set down appropriately, not a rule to apply indiscriminately at every moment, which is exactly the discernment the next section goes on to specify in concrete, diagnostic detail.

Knowing the Right Time

Chinese Source

師子吼言：「世尊！云何菩薩知時非時？」「善男子！菩薩摩訶薩因於受樂生大憍慢，或因說法而生憍慢，或因精勤而生憍慢，或因解義善問答時而生憍慢，或因親近惡知識故而生憍慢，或因布施所重之物而生憍慢，或因世間善法功德而生憍慢，或因世間豪貴之人所恭敬故而生憍慢，當知爾時，不宜修智，宜應修定，是名菩薩知時非時。若有菩薩勤修精進，未得利益涅槃之樂，以不得故生於悔心，以鈍根故不能調伏五情諸根，諸垢煩惱勢力盛故，自疑戒律有羸損故，當知爾時，不宜修定，宜應修智，是名菩薩知時非時。善男子！若有菩薩定、慧二法不平等者，當知爾時不宜修捨，二法若等則宜修之，是名菩薩知時非時。善男子！若有菩薩修習定慧起煩惱者，當知爾時不宜修捨，宜應讀誦、書寫解說十二部經。念佛、念法、念僧、念戒、念天、念捨，是名修捨。善男子！若有菩薩修習如是三法相者，以是因緣得無相涅槃。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar said: "World-Honored One, how does a bodhisattva know the proper time and the improper time?" "Good son, should a bodhisattva give rise to great arrogance on account of receiving happiness, or on account of teaching the Dharma, or on account of diligent exertion, or on account of skillfully understanding meaning in questions and answers, or on account of drawing close to evil companions, or on account of some cherished object he has given away, or on account of some worldly wholesome dharma or merit, or on account of being honored by some wealthy or noble person in the world — know that, at exactly such a time, it is not fitting to cultivate wisdom, but fitting instead to cultivate concentration; this is named a bodhisattva who knows the proper time and the improper time. Should a bodhisattva, diligently cultivating exertion, fail to attain the benefit and bliss of nirvāṇa, and, because of this failure, give rise to a mind of regret; or, because of dull faculties, be unable to tame his own five sense-faculties; or, because the power of every defiling affliction is strong within him; or, doubting himself, suspect that his own moral discipline has grown weak — know that, at exactly such a time, it is not fitting to cultivate concentration, but fitting instead to cultivate wisdom; this, too, is named a bodhisattva who knows the proper time and the improper time. Good son, should a bodhisattva's own concentration and wisdom be unequal to one another, know that, at exactly such a time, it is not fitting to cultivate equanimity — only once these two dharmas are equal is it fitting to cultivate it; this, too, is named a bodhisattva who knows the proper time and the improper time. Good son, should a bodhisattva, in cultivating concentration and wisdom, give rise to affliction, know that, at exactly such a time, it is not fitting to cultivate equanimity, but fitting instead to recite, copy out, and expound the twelve divisions of scripture. Being mindful of the Buddha, mindful of the Dharma, mindful of the Saṅgha, mindful of the precepts, mindful of the heavens, and mindful of relinquishment — this is named the cultivation of equanimity. Good son, should a bodhisattva cultivate these three marks in exactly this way, it is through this very cause and condition that he attains the signless nirvāṇa."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This diagnostic catalogue turns the abstract concentration/wisdom/equanimity framework of the previous section into something a practitioner could actually use in the moment: arrogance, whatever its specific trigger (success in teaching, being honored by the powerful, even pride in one's own generosity), calls for concentration's own sobering stillness rather than more analytical wisdom, since wisdom applied to an already self-satisfied mind risks only sharpening the very self-congratulation that needs dissolving. The opposite condition — discouragement, doubt, weak faculties, a sense of moral backsliding — calls for wisdom instead, since concentration applied to a mind already prone to self-doubt risks deepening rumination rather than clarifying it. The prescription is symmetrical by design: each imbalance is treated with its own opposite, and equanimity itself is reserved for the one condition where concentration and wisdom are already roughly matched, since applying equanimity to a genuinely unequal pair would simply paper over an imbalance that first needs correcting on its own terms.

The final case — concentration and wisdom themselves, properly balanced, nonetheless giving rise to affliction — is the passage's most practically important admission: even correct meditative technique can misfire, and the remedy offered is not more meditation at all, but a return to the concrete, textual, and devotional (reciting scripture, mindfulness of the Buddha, Dharma, Saṅgha, precepts, the heavens, and relinquishment) — a reminder that the three-marks framework, however refined, was never meant to replace ordinary devotional practice as a fallback when subtler techniques stall.

What Nirvāṇa's Names Mean, and the Ten Dharmas for Seeing It

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The twelve names for nirvāṇa continue this fascicle's preferred method — definition by negation traced to its own specific cause — but read together they sort into two families: negations of ordinary causal process (unborn, unemergent, unproduced, since it involves no cause, no conditioning, no karmic action) and negations of ordinary danger (island, refuge, tranquility, final extinction, since it involves freedom from the four torrents, every fetter, and every fire of affliction). The pairing suggests nirvāṇa answers two different anxieties at once — the philosophical puzzle of what escapes causation, and the practical, felt need for actual safety — without collapsing one into the other.

The full completion of faith's own paradoxical content — believing the Tathāgata is permanent while specifically not believing the literal narrative details of his birth, sickness, austerities, and death, nor that Devadatta truly wounded him, nor that the true Dharma truly perishes — directly recalls this fascicle's own earlier distinction between the Buddha's two bodies, the responsive body that manifests within ordinary causal history and the true body that never actually enters it; genuine faith, on this account, means holding both without confusing which one the ordinary biographical narrative is actually describing. The pure-precepts section's own four escalating failure-cases (open flirtation, attraction to a mere sound, vicarious desire watching others pursue each other, and precept-keeping aimed only at a heavenly reward) trace desire's persistence through increasingly subtle disguises, none of which requires physical contact at all — each one still counts as "the dharma of desire" fully accomplished, since the sūtra locates the violation in the mind's own orientation rather than in any external act. The list's own closing item — wisdom as the capacity to hold two aspects of a dharma together (empty and not-empty, permanent and impermanent) rather than collapsing into either alone — restates, in the register of a practical checklist, the same both-truths-at-once structure this entire chapter has been building toward since Part 27's identification of buddha-nature with the middle way.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Cunda's challenge as marshaling every piece of the sūtra's own prior teaching on karma into a single, tightly constructed dilemma: if giving to the Buddha yields inexhaustible reward, and deliberate wholesome action must certainly ripen, then Cunda's own single act of generosity ought to generate an unending sequence of pleasant fruits that could never actually terminate in awakening — and, by the same logic, an icchantika's own crimes ought likewise never be exhaustible, making buddha-nature's eventual manifestation in anyone equally impossible. He tracks the Buddha's answer as unfolding in four stages: first, a direct denial that karma is a single, uniform phenomenon at all — it is light or heavy, and, more importantly, decisive or indecisive, a distinction introduced specifically to correct two opposite errors (some deny karma has any fixed fruit at all, citing Aṅgulimāla's own liberation as proof that "evil" acts can be undone, while the sūtra's blanket teaching that "every action yields its fruit" was itself a corrective aimed at that very denial, not a claim that every action's fruit is rigidly fixed).

Huiyuan's second stage tracks the wise/foolish distinction as the mechanism making both truths compatible: karmic weight is not a fixed physical property but something a cultivated mind can actually alter, the wise turning heavy hell-bound karma light and the foolish turning light karma heavy — which directly answers Lion's Roar's own follow-up worry (if karma can shift like this, why bother with pure conduct at all?) by pointing out that this very shifting power is exactly what pure conduct and the cultivation of the path exist to develop. The third stage's reductio — if every action rigidly, mechanically yielded a proportional, unalterable fruit, a human would always remain human, a brahmin always a brahmin, evil committed in old age could not be experienced before old age recurred, and no cultivation could ever alter anything — demonstrates that rigid karmic determinism, taken seriously, would abolish caste mobility, rebirth across life stages, and the very possibility of practice, none of which anyone (including karma's staunchest defenders) actually wants to grant. The bodhisattva's own vow to be reborn in hell without possessing any hell-bound karma at all then demonstrates the positive side of this same flexibility: karmic destiny is genuinely alterable enough that a being with no such karma can still choose to experience its fruit voluntarily, for the sake of others — a mirror image of the wise turning heavy karma light.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The catalogue of freely chosen bodies — animal, hungry ghost, outcaste executioner, border-dweller, woman, eunuch, follower of a rival ascetic sect — is this fascicle's most direct answer to Cunda's own dilemma from the previous section: karma is not what actually explains any of these bodies at all, since the bodhisattva possesses none of the karma that would ordinarily produce them, yet the fruit is voluntarily received anyway, for the sole purpose of gaining access to beings otherwise unreachable. The repeated refrain — "the bodhisattva, in truth, possesses no such karma at all... this is named not actually receiving this evil karma in a present, future, or later life" — is a careful piece of doctrinal precision: appearing to undergo suffering and actually incurring its karmic debt are shown to be entirely separable, which is also this fascicle's own resolution to why the Buddha himself can be born as a butcher's son or an adherent of a heretical creed without either fact touching his own moral or ontological status.

The two truth-act stories that follow test this same principle under maximum pressure, since here the suffering is not merely apparent but genuinely, physically inflicted by another's real malice — Devadatta's greed for a second pearl, and King Kalābu's escalating cruelty testing whether forbearance is real. Both stories share an identical structure: injury, an outward test of whether inner equanimity is genuine, and healing accomplished not through any external remedy but through the simple, spoken truth of an unafflicted mind — "should I hold any ill will at all, let this affliction remain; should I hold none, let it be healed" — a device this collection has used before (Part 27's śūraṅgama-samādhi episode) but nowhere at such length or with such graphic physical stakes. Read against the immediately preceding section's account of karma as genuinely alterable by a wise mind, these stories make the doctrine vivid rather than abstract: a mind entirely free of anger and craving is shown, quite literally, to be strong enough to reverse a mutilation that any ordinary victim's own resentment would have left as permanent, physical proof of injury.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-times classification of karmic fruit (present-life, next-life, later-life retribution) completes the "decisive/indecisive" distinction from the previous section with a concrete taxonomy, and its own example for present-life retribution — a man who betrays a bear's den or a rare deer to a hunter and has his own hand fall off — deliberately mirrors the very structure of the truth-act stories just told: an act's consequence made visible immediately, rather than deferred. The teaching that a buddha's own awakening causes every remaining karmic action to ripen at once is this fascicle's own explanation for why the Buddha's biography includes real physical suffering (Devadatta's own attack, the mutilation by King Kalābu) despite his complete freedom from any binding karma: whatever residual karmic pattern still touches an awakened being's own physical continuity collapses into the present, with nothing left over to be deferred.

The ten similes for how a store of wholesome deeds turns heavy karma light (the fat man surviving deep mud, the antidote neutralizing poison, strong digestion handling a large drink, a wise prisoner securing release, sight avoiding a hidden pit, a full stomach absorbing wine without harm, armor turning back an enemy, prompt washing keeping a garment clean, a spare axle letting a cart keep moving, a hidden reserve softening a robbery's loss) are worth reading as a matched set rather than individually: each pairs a passive endowment (fat, strong digestion, sight) with an acquired resource (an antidote, armor, a spare axle, a hidden store) - suggesting that "wisdom," in this fascicle's own sense, functions less like a single skill and more like an accumulated margin of safety that absorbs shocks a thinner margin cannot, which is exactly the sense in which prior practice (the "wholesome roots" this whole section keeps returning to) determines how the very same objective event — a heavy transgression, a serious illness, a theft — lands differently on two people who appear, from outside, to be in identical circumstances.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This closing section's five successive redefinitions of "not cultivating body, precept, mind, wisdom" mirror the same escalating-rounds structure Part 27 used for the ten things a bodhisattva must accomplish: an initial baseline (governing the senses, upholding precepts, taming the mind, noble conduct), then progressively subtler failures (clinging to a permanent self, self-serving precept-keeping, an ungoverned mind amid temptation, failure to distinguish wholesome from unwholesome), culminating in a final round conducted entirely in ultimate-truth vocabulary, where even *perceiving* body, precept, mind, or wisdom as objects with a cause, a fruit, an aggregation, or a fixed nature at all is itself named a failure to cultivate them — the same "no mark, no cultivator" formula this fascicle has used since its very first section on concentration and wisdom. The extended catalogue of body-as-enemy similes (the untended fire that burns the very hand that fed it, the basket of four venomous serpents standing for the four elements, the unfired clay pot, the boil not yet ripe, the caṇḍāla's hereditary trade standing for the body's own impure origin) builds a case not for despising the body out of simple revulsion, but for the very specific claim that this body's own fragility and impurity are permanent structural facts rather than temporary conditions to be improved upon — which is exactly why even the form realm's own comparatively refined bodies are said to be abandoned by every buddha and bodhisattva, purely on account of still having a body at all.

The closing exchange returns, after this long detour through what genuine cultivation actually requires, to the fascicle's own governing question about karma's malleability, now answered with three final similes of proportion rather than substitution: cotton outweighed by gold despite its greater bulk, a measure of salt lost in the Ganges' own vastness, and an elephant snapping a chain that would hold a smaller creature fast. None of these images claims that evil karma simply vanishes — the salt is still there, dissolved in the river — but each shows how an accumulated store of wholesome deeds can change karma's own practical weight without denying its reality, which is this entire fascicle's answer, finally made vivid, to the question Cunda's challenge and Lion's Roar's own follow-ups have been circling since its opening pages: karma is real, and yet never so rigid that a cultivated mind cannot alter how it actually lands.
