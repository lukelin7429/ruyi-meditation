Cunda's Offering, and the Secret Treasury

Fascicle 1 ended with every offering refused. This fascicle opens with the one offering the whole assembly turns out to have been waiting for: an artisan's son named Cunda, whose gift the Buddha finally accepts — not because it is worthy where the others were not, but to teach, through the act of accepting it, that the Tathāgata's body was never a body that eats at all. Cunda presses the point in a real philosophical exchange, Mañjuśrī takes up the argument on the Buddha's behalf, and the fascicle closes with the sūtra's most famous simile — the secret treasury of the three-dot letter i — the parable of the two physicians on milk as poison and cure, and the entrustment of the Dharma to Mahākāśyapa.

Cunda's Final Offering

Chinese Source

爾時會中有優婆塞，是拘尸那城工巧之子，名曰純陀，與其同類十五人俱。為令世間得善果故，捨身威儀，從座而起，偏袒右肩，右膝著地，合掌向佛，悲泣墮淚，頂禮佛足，而白佛言：「唯願世尊及比丘僧，哀受我等最後供養，為度無量諸眾生故。世尊！我等從今無主、無親、無救、無護、無歸、無趣、貧窮飢困，欲從如來求將來食，唯願哀愍，受我微供，然後乃入於般涅槃。世尊！譬如剎利、若婆羅門、毘舍、首陀，以貧窮故，遠至他國，役力農作，得好調牛，良田平正，無諸沙鹵、惡草、株杌，唯悕天雨。言調牛者，喻身口七。良田平正，喻於智慧。除去沙鹵、惡草、株杌，喻除煩惱。世尊！我今身有調牛、良田、除去株杌，唯悕如來甘露法雨。貧四姓者，即我身是，貧於無上法之財寶。唯願哀愍，除斷我等貧窮困苦，拯及無量苦惱眾生。我今所供，雖復微少，冀得充足如來大眾。我今無主、無親、無歸，願垂矜愍，如羅睺羅。」

爾時世尊、一切種智、無上調御，告純陀曰：「善哉，善哉！我今為汝除斷貧窮，無上法雨雨汝身田，令生法芽。汝今於我，欲求壽命、色力、安、辯，我當施汝常命、色力、安、無礙辯。何以故？純陀！施食有二，果報無差。何等為二？一者受已，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提；二者受已，入於涅槃。我今受汝最後供養，令汝具足檀波羅蜜。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time there was, among the assembly, an upāsaka — the son of an artisan of the city of Kuśinagara — named Cunda, together with fifteen others of his own kind. Wishing that the world might obtain good fruit, he rose from his seat, casting off his own composed bearing, bared his right shoulder, set his right knee upon the ground, joined his palms toward the Buddha, weeping and shedding tears, bowed his head to the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "I beg only that the World-Honored One and the assembly of monks, out of compassion, accept our final offering, for the sake of saving countless beings. World-Honored One, from this day we shall have no lord, no kin, no one to save us, no one to protect us, no refuge, no destination; we are poor and famished, and we wish to beg from the Tathāgata the food that is yet to come. We beg only that, out of compassion, you accept our humble offering, and only then enter parinirvāṇa. World-Honored One, it is as when a kṣatriya, a brāhmaṇa, a vaiśya, or a śūdra, being poor, travels far to another country and labors there at farming, obtaining a well-trained ox and fertile, level fields free of salt-flats, weeds, and stumps, and hopes only for rain to fall from heaven. The well-trained ox is a likeness for the seven virtues of body and speech; the fertile, level fields are a likeness for wisdom; the clearing away of salt-flats, weeds, and stumps is a likeness for the removal of affliction. World-Honored One, I now have in myself this trained ox, these fertile fields, this ground already cleared of stumps, and I hope only for the sweet rain of the Tathāgata's Dharma. It is I who am the poor one among the four classes, poor in the wealth of the peerless Dharma. I beg only that you, out of compassion, put an end to our poverty and hardship, and rescue the countless beings still in anguish. Though what I now offer is meager, I hope it may be enough to satisfy the Tathāgata and the whole assembly. I now have no lord, no kin, no refuge; I beg only that you look on me with the same tenderness you show Rāhula."

Then the World-Honored One, possessed of the knowledge of all modes, the peerless tamer of beings, said to Cunda: "Excellent, excellent! I will now put an end to your poverty; the peerless rain of the Dharma will fall upon the field of your body, and cause the sprout of the Dharma to grow within it. You now wish, through me, to obtain long life, radiance, strength, ease, and eloquence; I shall grant you constant life, radiance, strength, ease, and unobstructed eloquence. Why is this? Cunda, there are two kinds of food-offering whose fruit is exactly the same. What are the two? The first is the offering received just before attaining unsurpassed, complete awakening; the second is the offering received just before entering nirvāṇa. I am now accepting your final offering, so that you may fulfill the perfection of giving."

Cunda's Challenge, and the Buddha's Answer

Chinese Source

爾時純陀即白佛言：「如佛所說，二施果報無差別者，是義不然。何以故？先受施者，煩惱未盡，未得成就一切種智，亦未能令眾生具足檀波羅蜜；後受施者，煩惱已盡，已得成就一切種智，能令眾生普得具足檀波羅蜜。先受施者，直是眾生；後受施者，是天中天。先受施者，是雜食身、煩惱之身、是後邊身、是無常身；後受施者，無煩惱身、金剛之身、法身、常身、無邊之身。云何而言，二施果報等無差別？先受施者，未能具足檀波羅蜜乃至般若波羅蜜，唯得肉眼，未得佛眼乃至慧眼；後受施者，已得具足檀波羅蜜乃至般若波羅蜜，具足佛眼乃至慧眼。云何而言二施果報等無差別？世尊！先受施者，受已食噉，入腹消化，得命、得色、得力、得安、得無礙辯；後受施者，不食、不消、無五事果；云何而言二施果報等無差別？」

佛言：「善男子！如來已於無量無邊阿僧祇劫，無有食身、煩惱之身，無後邊身，常身、法身、金剛之身。善男子，未見佛性者，名煩惱身、雜食之身，是後邊身。菩薩爾時受飲食已，入金剛三昧，此食消已，即見佛性，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是故我言，二施果報等無差別。菩薩爾時破壞四魔，今入涅槃，亦破四魔，是故我言二施果報等無差別。菩薩爾時雖不廣說十二部經，先已通達，今入涅槃，廣為眾生分別演說，是故我言二施果報等無差別。善男子！如來之身，已於無量阿僧祇劫不受飲食。為諸聲聞說言，先受難陀、難陀波羅二牧牛女所奉乳糜，然後乃得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提；我實不食。我今為於此會大眾，是故受汝最後所奉；實亦不食。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Cunda then said to the Buddha: "What the Buddha has said — that the fruit of these two offerings is exactly the same — this cannot be right. Why is this? The one who received the first offering had not yet exhausted affliction, had not yet accomplished the knowledge of all modes, and could not yet cause beings to fulfill the perfection of giving; the one who receives the last offering has already exhausted affliction, has already accomplished the knowledge of all modes, and can cause beings everywhere to fulfill the perfection of giving. The one who received the first offering was simply a sentient being; the one who receives the last offering is a god among gods. The one who received the first offering had a body that eats mixed food, a body of affliction, a final body, an impermanent body; the one who receives the last offering has a body free of affliction, a body of vajra, a Dharma-body, a permanent body, a boundless body. How, then, can it be said that the fruit of these two offerings is exactly the same? The one who received the first offering had not yet fulfilled the perfections from giving up through wisdom, and had only the fleshly eye, not yet the buddha-eye or the wisdom-eye; the one who receives the last offering has already fulfilled the perfections from giving up through wisdom, complete with the buddha-eye and the wisdom-eye. How, then, can it be said that the fruit of these two offerings is exactly the same? World-Honored One, the one who received the first offering ate it, took it into his stomach, and digested it, gaining life, radiance, strength, ease, and unobstructed eloquence; the one who receives the last offering will not eat it, will not digest it, and gains none of these five results. How, then, can it be said that the fruit of these two offerings is exactly the same?"

The Buddha said: "Good son, the Tathāgata has, throughout immeasurable, boundless asaṃkhyeya kalpas, never had a body that eats food, a body of affliction, or a final body — only a permanent body, a Dharma-body, a body of vajra. Good son, it is only one who has not yet seen buddha-nature who is said to have a body of affliction, a body that eats mixed food, a final body. When the bodhisattva received that first food, he entered the vajra samādhi, and once that food was digested, he immediately saw buddha-nature and attained unsurpassed, complete awakening; this is why I say the fruit of these two offerings is exactly the same. At that time the bodhisattva destroyed the four māras; now, entering nirvāṇa, I likewise destroy the four māras; this is why I say the fruit of these two offerings is exactly the same. At that time the bodhisattva, though he had not yet expounded the twelvefold scriptures at length, had already penetrated them fully; now, entering nirvāṇa, I will expound them at length, distinguishing them for the sake of beings; this is why I say the fruit of these two offerings is exactly the same. Good son, the Tathāgata's body has, throughout immeasurable asaṃkhyeya kalpas, never taken any food or drink at all. For the sake of the śrāvakas it is said that he first received the rice-milk offered by the two cowherd girls Nandā and Nandabalā, and only then attained unsurpassed, complete awakening — but in truth I did not eat it. I am now accepting your final offering for the sake of this whole assembly gathered here; but in truth I am not eating it either."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Cunda's objection as proceeding through five distinct grounds in turn — affliction, spiritual rank, the body's own nature, accomplishment in the six perfections, and the ordinary digestive fact of eating — and finds that the Buddha's answer does not actually rebut all five point for point. Instead it answers by a different route entirely: rather than conceding that a first, lesser offering and a last, greater one differ and then arguing the difference washes out, the Buddha denies the premise underneath Cunda's whole comparison — that there ever was an earlier, ordinary, food-dependent body to compare against the later one. Both meals, on this account, were received by the identical permanent, unconditioned body; the appearance of eating at Bodh Gayā was already a concession to how śrāvakas needed the story told, not a report of what actually happened. Huiyuan is explicit that this passage is doing the same work, in miniature, that the rest of the fascicle will do at far greater length: teaching the doctrine of the Tathāgata's permanent Dharma-body by first letting a listener voice, in full and unanswered force, the ordinary assumption that a buddha's body is just another impermanent thing that eats, ages, and dies.

The Assembly's Praise, and Cunda's Joy

Chinese Source

爾時大眾聞佛世尊，普為大會受於純陀最後供養，歡喜踊躍同聲讚言：「善哉，善哉！希有純陀！汝今立字，名不虛稱。言純陀者，名解妙義。汝今建立如是大義，是故依實從義立名，故名純陀。汝今現世得大名利，德願滿足。甚奇純陀！生在人中，復得難得無上之利。善哉純陀！如優曇花世間希有，佛出於世亦復甚難，值佛生信聞法復難，佛臨涅槃最後供養，能辦是事復難於是。南無純陀，南無純陀！汝今已具檀波羅蜜，猶如秋月，十五日夜，清淨圓滿，無諸雲翳，一切眾生無不瞻仰；汝亦如是，而為我等之所瞻仰。佛已受汝最後供養，令汝具足檀波羅蜜。南無純陀！是故說汝如月盛滿，一切眾生無不瞻仰。南無純陀！雖受人身，心如佛心。汝今純陀，真是佛子，如羅睺羅，等無有異。」爾時大眾即說偈言：「汝雖生人道，　已超第六天，我及一切眾，　今故稽首請。人中最勝尊，　今當入涅槃，汝應愍我等，　唯願速請佛，久住於世間，　利益無量眾，演說智所讚，　無上甘露法。汝若不請佛，　我命將不全，是故應見為，　稽請調御師。」

爾時純陀歡喜踊躍，譬如有人，父母卒喪忽然還活，純陀歡喜亦復如是，復起禮佛，而說偈言：「快哉獲己利，　善得於人身，蠲除貪恚等，　永離三惡道。快哉獲己利，　遇得金寶聚，值遇調御師，　不懼墮畜生。佛如優曇花，　值遇生信難，遇已種善根，　永離餓鬼苦，亦復能損減，　阿修羅種類。芥子投針鋒，　佛出難於是，我以具足檀，　度人天生死。佛不染世法，　如蓮花處水，善斷有頂種，　永度生死流。生世為人難，　值佛世亦難，猶如大海中，　盲龜遇浮孔。我今所奉食，　願得無上報，一切煩惱結，　摧破不堅牢。我今於此處，　不求天人身，設使得之者，　心亦不甘樂。如來受我供，　歡喜無有量，猶如伊蘭花，　出於栴檀香。我身如伊蘭，　如來受我供，如出栴檀香，　是故我歡喜。我今得現報，　最勝上妙處，釋梵諸天等，　悉來供養我。一切諸世間，　悉生諸苦惱，以知佛世尊，　欲入於涅槃。高聲唱是言，　世間無調御，不應捨眾生，　應視如一子。如來在僧中，　演說無上法，如須彌寶山，　安處于大海。佛智能善斷，　我等無明闇，猶如虛空中，　起雲得清涼。如來能善除，　一切諸煩惱，猶如日出時，　除雲光普照。是諸眾生等，　啼泣面目腫，悉皆為生死，　苦水之所漂。以是故世尊，　應長眾生信，為斷生死苦，　久住於世間。」

Ru-Yi Translation

When the assembly heard that the World-Honored One had, on behalf of the whole gathering, accepted Cunda's final offering, they rejoiced and leapt for joy, and praised him together with one voice: "Excellent, excellent! How rare you are, Cunda! The name you bear is no empty title — the name Cunda means one who understands the subtle meaning, and you have now established just such a great meaning; in accordance with the truth, your name was given for its meaning, and hence you are called Cunda. In this very life you have gained great renown and benefit, and your virtuous aspiration is now fulfilled. How wondrous, Cunda! Born among humankind, you have obtained a peerless benefit exceedingly hard to obtain. Excellent, Cunda! Just as the udumbara flower is rare in this world, so a buddha's appearance in the world is exceedingly rare; to encounter a buddha and give rise to faith and hear the Dharma is rarer still; and to accomplish the very last offering as the Buddha draws near his parinirvāṇa is rarer even than that. Homage to you, Cunda! Homage to you, Cunda! You have now fulfilled the perfection of giving; you are like the autumn moon on the fifteenth night, pure and full, free of every cloud, looked up to by every being without exception — you are just the same now, looked up to by all of us. The Buddha has accepted your final offering, and you have fulfilled the perfection of giving. Homage to you, Cunda! And so we say you are like the moon at its very fullest, looked up to by every being without exception. Homage to you, Cunda! Though you bear a human body, your mind is like a buddha's mind. You, Cunda, are now truly a child of the Buddha, no different at all from Rāhula." Then the assembly spoke this verse:

"Though you were born into the human path,
you have already surpassed the sixth heaven —
we, and this whole assembly,
now bow our heads and beg you.

The most excellent one among humankind
is now about to enter parinirvāṇa;
you should have pity on us —
we beg you, quickly entreat the Buddha

to remain long in the world,
bringing benefit to countless beings,
proclaiming the sweet Dharma of nectar
that wisdom itself has praised.

If you do not entreat the Buddha,
our own lives will not remain whole;
for this reason you should act for us,
and bow to entreat the Tamer of beings."

Then Cunda rejoiced and leapt for joy, as a person might whose father and mother, suddenly dead, had just as suddenly come back to life — Cunda's joy was just the same. He rose again, bowed to the Buddha, and spoke this verse:

"How joyful, to have gained my own true benefit,
to have obtained so well a human body,
to have swept away craving, anger, and the rest,
and forever left the three evil destinies behind.

How joyful, to have gained my own true benefit,
to have come upon a heap of gold and jewels,
to have encountered the Tamer of beings,
and no longer fear falling among the animals.

The Buddha is like the udumbara flower —
to encounter him and give rise to faith is hard;
having encountered him, I have planted roots of goodness,
and left the suffering of the hungry ghosts forever behind,
and lessened, too, the ranks of the asuras.

A mustard seed thrown to land on a needle's point —
a buddha's appearing is harder even than this;
and by this act of fulfilling the perfection of giving
I cross beyond the round of birth and death for gods and humans alike.

The Buddha is untouched by worldly things,
as a lotus flower stands untouched by the water it grows in;
he has thoroughly cut off the seed of the summit of existence,
and forever crossed beyond the stream of birth and death.

To be born into the world as a human is hard;
to encounter a buddha's era is hard as well —
it is like a blind turtle in the great ocean
chancing upon a single floating log with a hole in it.

May the food I now offer
bring a peerless reward —
may every knot of affliction
be broken, and hold together no more.

I do not seek, here in this place,
rebirth as a god or a human;
and even were such a thing given to me,
my heart would take no pleasure in it.

That the Tathāgata has accepted my offering
fills me with joy beyond measure —
it is like the eraṇḍa flower
giving off the fragrance of sandalwood.

My own self is like the eraṇḍa,
and the Tathāgata's acceptance of my offering
is like sandalwood's fragrance issuing from it —
this is why I am filled with joy.

I have now obtained a reward in this very life,
the most excellent, wondrous place there is —
Śakra, Brahmā, and every god
have all come to make offering to me.

Every being throughout every world
has now given rise to anguish,
knowing that the World-Honored One
is about to enter parinirvāṇa.

They cry out in loud voices, saying:
"The world has no one left to tame it" —
you should not abandon sentient beings,
but look on them as your own only child.

The Tathāgata, here among the saṅgha,
proclaims the peerless Dharma,
like a mountain of jewels, Sumeru,
set at rest within the great ocean.

The Buddha's wisdom can rightly cut through
the darkness of our own ignorance,
like clouds arising in open sky
that bring, in the end, a cooling relief.

The Tathāgata can rightly remove
every kind of affliction,
like the sun when it rises
dispersing the clouds, its light shining everywhere.

All these beings here
weep until their faces swell,
every one of them swept along
by the flood-waters of birth and death.

For this reason, World-Honored One,
you should lengthen the faith of beings,
and, to put an end to the suffering of birth and death,
remain long in the world."

The Buddha's Teaching on Impermanence

Chinese Source

佛告純陀：「如是如是，如汝所說。佛出世難如優曇花，值佛生信亦復甚難，佛臨涅槃最後施食，能具足檀復倍甚難。汝今純陀！莫大愁苦，應生踊躍，喜自慶幸，得值最後供養如來，成就具足檀波羅蜜，不應請佛久住於世。汝今當觀諸佛境界，悉皆無常，諸行性相，亦復如是。」即為純陀而說偈言：「一切諸世間，　生者皆歸死，壽命雖無量，　要必當有盡。夫盛必有衰，　合會有別離，壯年不久停，　盛色病所侵，命為死所吞，　無有法常者。諸王得自在，　勢力無等雙，一切皆遷動，　壽命亦如是。眾苦輪無際，　流轉無休息，三界皆無常，　諸有無有樂。有道本性相，　一切皆空無，可壞法流轉，　常有憂患等。恐怖諸過惡，　老病死衰惱，是諸無有邊，　易壞怨所侵。煩惱所纏裹，　猶如蠶處繭，何有智慧者，　而當樂是處？此身苦所集，　一切皆不淨，扼縛癰瘡等，　根本無義利。上至諸天身，　皆亦復如是，諸欲皆無常，　故我不貪著。離欲善思惟，　而證於真實，究竟斷有者，　今日當涅槃。我度有彼岸，　已得過諸苦，是故於今者，　純受上妙樂。以是因緣故，　證無戲論邊，永斷諸纏縛，　今日入涅槃。我無老病死，　壽命不可盡，我今入涅槃，　猶如大火滅。純陀汝不應，　思量如來義，當觀如來住，　猶如須彌山。我今入涅槃，　受於第一樂，諸佛法如是，　不應復啼哭。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Cunda: "It is so, it is so, just as you have said. A buddha's appearance in the world is as rare as the udumbara flower; to encounter a buddha and give rise to faith is rarer still; and to accomplish, as the Buddha draws near his parinirvāṇa, the very last food-offering and fulfill the perfection of giving through it is rarer even than that. But you, Cunda, should not now give way to excessive grief; you should instead rejoice and leap for joy, glad and fortunate to have had the chance to make this final offering to the Tathāgata and accomplish the perfection of giving in full. You should not ask the Buddha to remain long in the world. 爾時文殊師利法王子告純陀言：「純陀！汝今不應發如是言，欲使如來常住於世，不般涅槃，如彼飢人無所變吐。汝今當觀諸行性相，如是觀行具空三昧，欲求正法應如是學。」

純陀問言：「文殊師利！夫如來者，天上人中最尊最勝，如是如來，豈是行耶？若是行者，為生滅法。譬如水泡，速起速滅，往來流轉，猶如車輪，一切諸行亦復如是。我聞諸天，壽命極長，云何世尊是天中天，壽命更促不滿百年？如聚落主，勢得自在，以自在力，能制他人，是人福盡，其後貧賤，人所輕蔑，為他策使。所以者何？失勢力故。世尊亦爾，同於諸行，同諸行者，則不得稱為天中天。何以故？諸行即是生死法故。是故文殊！勿觀如來同於諸行。復次文殊！為知而說、不知而說，而言如來同於諸行？設使如來同諸行者，則不得言於三界中，為天中天、自在法王。譬如人王，有大力士，其力當千，更無有能降伏之者，故稱此人一人當千。如是力士，王所愛念，偏賜爵祿，封賞自然。所以得稱當千人者，是人未必力敵於千，但以種種伎藝所能能勝千故，故稱當千。如來亦爾，降煩惱魔、陰魔、天魔、死魔，是故如來名三界尊，如彼力士一人當千。以是因緣，成就具足種種無量真實功德，故稱如來、應、正遍知。文殊師利！汝今不應憶想分別，以如來法同於諸行。」

「譬如巨富長者生子，相師占之有短壽相。父母聞已，知其不任紹繼家嗣，不復愛重，視如芻草。夫短壽者，不為沙門、婆羅門等男女大小之所敬念。若使如來同諸行者，亦復不為一切世間人天眾生之所奉敬，如來所說不變、不異、真實之法亦無受者。是故文殊！不應說言如來同於一切諸行。」

「復次文殊！譬如貧女，無有居家救護之者，加復病苦飢渴所逼，遊行乞丐，止他客舍，寄生一子，是客舍主驅逐令去。其產未久，携抱是兒，欲至他國。於其中路，遇惡風雨，寒苦並至，多為蚊虻、蜂螫、毒蟲之所唼食，經由恒河，抱兒而度，其水漂疾而不放捨，於是母子遂共俱沒。如是女人慈念功德，命終之後，生於梵天。文殊師利！若有善男子欲護正法，勿說如來同於諸行、不同諸行！唯當自責，我今愚癡，未有慧眼，如來正法不可思議。是故不應宣說，如來定是有為、定是無為。若正見者，應說如來定是無為。何以故？能為眾生生善法故、生憐愍故。如彼貧女在於恒河，為愛念子而捨身命。善男子！護法菩薩亦應如是，寧捨身命，不說如來同於有為，當言如來同於無為。以說如來同無為故，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，如彼女人得生梵天。何以故？以護法故。云何護法？所謂說言如來同於無為。善男子！如是之人雖不求解脫，解脫自至；如彼貧女，不求梵天，梵天自至。」

「文殊師利！如人遠行，中路疲極，寄止他舍，臥寐之中，其室忽然大火卒起，即時驚寤，尋自思惟：『我於今者定死不疑。』具慚愧故，以衣纏身，即便命終，生忉利天。從是已後，滿八十返作大梵王，滿百千世生於人中為轉輪王，是人不復生三惡趣，展轉常生安樂之處。以是緣故，文殊師利！若善男子有慚愧者，不應觀佛同於諸行。文殊師利！外道邪見可說如來同於有為，持戒比丘不應如是，於如來所生有為想。若言如來是有為者，即是妄語；當知是人死入地獄，如人自處於己舍宅。文殊師利！如來真實是無為法，不應復言是有為也。汝從今日於生死中，應捨無智，求於正智，當知如來即是無為。若能如是觀如來者，具足當得三十二相，速疾成就阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。」

爾時文殊師利法王子讚純陀言：「善哉，善哉！善男子！汝今已作長壽因緣，能知如來是常住法、不變異法、無為之法。汝今如是善覆如來有為之相，如彼火人，為慚愧故以衣覆身，以是善心生忉利天，復為梵王、轉輪聖王，不至惡趣，常受安樂。汝亦如是，善覆如來有為相故，於未來世必定當得三十二相、八十種好、十八不共法、無量壽命，不在生死、常受安樂，不久得成應、正遍知。純陀！如來次後自當廣說，我之與汝，俱亦當覆如來有為。有為、無為且共置之，汝可隨時速施飯食，如是施者，諸施中最。若比丘、比丘尼、優婆塞、優婆夷，遠行疲極，所須之物，應當清淨，隨時給與。如是速施，即是具足檀波羅蜜根本種子。純陀！若有最後施佛及僧，若多、若少、若足、不足，宜速及時，如來正爾當般涅槃。」

純陀答言：「文殊師利！汝今何故，貪為此食，而言多少、足與不足，令我時施？文殊師利！如來昔日苦行六年，尚自支持，況於今日須臾間耶？文殊師利！汝今實謂如來正覺受斯食耶？然我定知，如來身者，即是法身，非為食身。」

爾時佛告文殊師利：「如是如是，如純陀言。善哉，純陀！汝已成就微妙大智，善入甚深大乘經典。」

文殊師利語純陀言：「汝謂如來是無為者，如來之身即是長壽；若作是知，佛所悅可。」純陀答言：「如來非獨悅可於我，亦復悅可一切眾生。」文殊師利言：「如來於汝及以於我、一切眾生皆悉悅可。」純陀答言：「汝不應言如來悅可。夫悅可者，則是倒想。若有倒想，則是生死。有生死者，即有為法。是故文殊！勿謂如來是有為也。若言如來是有為者，我與仁者俱行顛倒。文殊師利！如來無有愛念之想。夫愛念者，如彼母牛愛念其子，雖復飢渴行求水草，若足、不足，忽然還歸。諸佛世尊無有是念，等視一切如羅睺羅。如是念者，即是諸佛智慧境界。文殊師利！譬如國王，調御駕駟，欲令驢車而及之者，無有是處。我與仁者亦復如是，欲盡如來微密深奧，亦無是處。文殊師利！如金翅鳥飛昇虛空無量由旬，下觀大海悉見水性魚鼈、黿鼉、龜龍之屬，及見己影，如於明鏡見諸色像。凡夫少智，不能籌量如是所見，我與仁者亦復如是，不能籌量如來智慧。」文殊師利語純陀言：「如是如是，如汝所說。我於此事，非為不達，直欲試汝諸菩薩事。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Cunda then said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, it is so, it is so, truly as your holy teaching says. My own wisdom is as slight as a mosquito's or a gnat's — how could I possibly fathom the profound meaning of the Tathāgata's parinirvāṇa? World-Honored One, I have now, together with great nāga-like bodhisattva-mahāsattvas who have cut off every taint, including the Dharma-prince Mañjuśrī — World-Honored One, it is as when a young child newly gone forth into homelessness, though not yet fully ordained, is already counted among the saṅgha. I am just the same: by the spiritual power of the Buddha and the bodhisattvas, I have come to be counted among these great bodhisattvas. This is why I now wish the Tathāgata to remain long in the world, and not enter parinirvāṇa. It is like a starving person who could never bring himself to vomit up food once eaten — I wish the World-Honored One to be just the same, abiding forever in the world, never entering parinirvāṇa."

The Dharma-prince Mañjuśrī then said to Cunda: "Cunda, you should not now speak this way, wishing the Tathāgata to abide forever in the world and never enter parinirvāṇa, like that starving man who could never bring himself to vomit. You should now observe the nature and mark of every conditioned thing; observing them in this way, you will attain the samādhi of emptiness. If you wish to seek the true Dharma, you should train in just this way."

Cunda asked: "Mañjuśrī, the Tathāgata is the most honored and most excellent being among gods and humans — how could such a Tathāgata be a conditioned thing? If he were a conditioned thing, he would be a thing subject to arising and ceasing, like a bubble on water, arising and vanishing in an instant, coming and going, turning like a wheel — and every conditioned thing is just the same. I have heard that the lifespan of the gods is exceedingly long — how is it, then, that the World-Honored One, a god among gods, has so short a lifespan, not even a hundred years? It is like the headman of a village who holds sovereign power, able through that power to command other people; once his merit is exhausted, he afterward becomes poor and lowly, held in contempt by others, and made to serve at their command. Why is this? Because he has lost his power. The World-Honored One would be just the same, were he identical with conditioned things — and one identical with conditioned things cannot be called a god among gods. Why is this? Because every conditioned thing is itself the very stuff of birth and death. For this reason, Mañjuśrī, do not regard the Tathāgata as identical with conditioned things. Furthermore, Mañjuśrī, do you say the Tathāgata is identical with conditioned things knowingly, or without knowing? Even granting that the Tathāgata were identical with conditioned things, one could not then call him, throughout the three realms, a god among gods, a sovereign lord of the Dharma. It is like a human king who has a great strongman, whose strength is a match for a thousand men, whom no one else is able to overpower, so that this one man is said to be worth a thousand. Such a strongman, beloved by the king, is granted rank and stipend, and rewards come to him as a matter of course. The reason he is called a match for a thousand is not necessarily that his strength alone can equal a thousand men, but that his various skills together are enough to overcome a thousand — and so he is called their match. The Tathāgata is just the same: he has subdued the māra of affliction, the māra of the aggregates, the māra of the heavens, and the māra of death, and this is why the Tathāgata is called the honored one of the three realms, just as that strongman is called the match for a thousand. It is by this cause and condition that he has accomplished, in full, every kind of immeasurable, genuine virtue, and is called Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly Awakened One. Mañjuśrī, you should not now imagine or discriminate the Tathāgata's own nature as identical with conditioned things.

"It is like a wealthy elder whose son is born, and a fortune-teller reads in him the mark of a short life. On hearing this, the parents, knowing he will not be fit to carry on the family line, no longer cherish or value him, and look on him as though he were mere straw. One who is fated to a short life is not held in reverence by monks, brāhmaṇas, men or women, young or old. If the Tathāgata were identical with conditioned things, he too would not be revered by any being throughout the world, among gods or humans, and the unchanging, undiffering, true Dharma he teaches would find no one to receive it. For this reason, Mañjuśrī, one should not say that the Tathāgata is identical with any conditioned thing.

"Furthermore, Mañjuśrī, it is like a poor woman with no household and no one to protect her, further beset by sickness, hunger, and thirst, wandering about begging, who lodges for a night at another's inn and there gives birth to a child, only to be driven out by the innkeeper. Not long after giving birth, she carries the child in her arms, wishing to reach another country. Along the way she meets a violent storm, cold and hardship assailing her together, and is bitten again and again by mosquitoes, gnats, wasps, and venomous insects; coming to the Ganges, she carries the child in her arms to cross it, but the current is too swift and will not release her, and mother and child are swept away together and drown. Because of the merit of that woman's loving-kindness, she is reborn, after death, in the Brahmā heaven. Mañjuśrī, if a good son wishes to protect the true Dharma, he should not say the Tathāgata is identical with conditioned things, nor that he is not identical with them — he should instead only reproach himself, saying: 'I am foolish and ignorant, and do not yet have the wisdom-eye; the Tathāgata's true Dharma is beyond conceptual thought.' For this reason one should not assert outright that the Tathāgata is definitely conditioned, or definitely unconditioned. If one has correct view, one should say that the Tathāgata is definitely unconditioned. Why is this? Because doing so gives rise to wholesome states in beings, and gives rise to compassion. It is like that poor woman at the Ganges who gave up her own body and life out of love for her child. Good son, a bodhisattva who protects the Dharma should be just the same: rather give up body and life than say the Tathāgata is identical with the conditioned — one should say instead that the Tathāgata is identical with the unconditioned. By saying the Tathāgata is identical with the unconditioned, one attains unsurpassed, complete awakening, just as that woman attained rebirth in the Brahmā heaven. Why is this? Because of protecting the Dharma. What is meant by protecting the Dharma? It means saying that the Tathāgata is identical with the unconditioned. Good son, such a person, though not seeking liberation, will have liberation come to them of itself — just as that poor woman, without seeking the Brahmā heaven, had the Brahmā heaven come to her of itself.

"Mañjuśrī, it is like a person traveling far, exhausted along the way, who lodges for the night at another's house; while he is asleep, a great fire suddenly breaks out in the room, and he wakes with a start, and thinks to himself: 'I am certainly going to die now, beyond doubt.' Out of a sense of shame, he wraps his body in his own robe, and dies; he is then reborn in the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven. From that time on, he becomes the Great Brahmā King eighty times over, and is reborn as a wheel-turning king among humans for a hundred thousand ages, never again falling into the three evil destinies, and is reborn again and again only in places of ease and happiness. For this reason, Mañjuśrī, if a good son has any sense of shame, he should not regard the Buddha as identical with conditioned things. Mañjuśrī, it is heterodox teachers of wrong view who may say the Tathāgata is identical with the conditioned; a monk who keeps the precepts should not give rise to such a conditioned notion regarding the Tathāgata. Whoever says the Tathāgata is conditioned speaks falsely, and it should be known that such a person, upon death, falls into hell, just as a person settles into his own home. Mañjuśrī, the Tathāgata is truly and genuinely the unconditioned Dharma, and one should not say that he is conditioned. From this day on, amid birth and death, you should abandon ignorance and seek right knowledge, and understand that the Tathāgata is precisely the unconditioned. If you are able to view the Tathāgata in this way, you will attain, in full, the thirty-two marks, and swiftly accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening."

The Dharma-prince Mañjuśrī then praised Cunda, saying: "Excellent, excellent, good son! You have now built the very cause of long life, knowing the Tathāgata to be the permanent Dharma, the unchanging Dharma, the unconditioned Dharma. In thus skillfully concealing the Tathāgata's conditioned appearance, you are like that man in the fire who, out of shame, covered his body with his own robe — and by that very wholesome intention was reborn in the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven, and became a Brahmā king and a wheel-turning sage-king, never falling into any evil destiny, forever receiving ease and happiness. You are just the same: because you have skillfully concealed the Tathāgata's conditioned appearance, in ages to come you will surely obtain the thirty-two marks, the eighty minor characteristics, the eighteen unique qualities, and immeasurable life, will not remain in birth and death, will forever receive ease and happiness, and will, before long, accomplish the state of the Worthy One, the Perfectly Awakened One. Cunda, the Tathāgata will himself, in what follows, expound this at length; you and I should both, for now, go on concealing the Tathāgata's conditioned appearance. Let us set aside, for the moment, the question of conditioned and unconditioned — you should now quickly, in good time, offer the meal; such a swift offering is the very best among offerings. If any monk, nun, upāsaka, or upāsikā, exhausted from a long journey, needs anything at all, one should give it to them promptly and without impurity. An offering given this swiftly is itself the very root and seed of the perfection of giving fulfilled in full. Cunda, if one is to make a final offering to the Buddha and the saṅgha, whether much or little, whether sufficient or not, one should act swiftly, without delay — the Tathāgata is on the very point of entering parinirvāṇa."

Cunda replied: "Mañjuśrī, why is it that you now covet this meal so, speaking of much or little, sufficient or not, urging me to offer it at once? Mañjuśrī, the Tathāgata once endured six years of ascetic practice and sustained himself through it — how much more so, then, through this brief moment today? Mañjuśrī, do you truly believe that the Tathāgata, the Perfectly Awakened One, will actually eat this food? But I am certain: the Tathāgata's body is itself the Dharma-body, not a body that eats."

The Buddha then said to Mañjuśrī: "It is so, it is so, just as Cunda has said. Excellent, Cunda! You have already accomplished a subtle, great wisdom, and have skillfully entered the profound scriptures of the Great Vehicle."

Mañjuśrī said to Cunda: "If you hold that the Tathāgata is unconditioned, then the Tathāgata's own body is itself long life; if you understand it this way, it pleases the Buddha." Cunda replied: "The Tathāgata is not pleased by me alone — he is equally pleased by every being." Mañjuśrī said: "The Tathāgata is equally pleased with you, and with me, and with every being." Cunda replied: "You should not say the Tathāgata is 'pleased.' To be pleased is itself an inverted notion; where there is an inverted notion, there is birth and death; where there is birth and death, there is a conditioned thing. For this reason, Mañjuśrī, do not think the Tathāgata is conditioned. If you say the Tathāgata is conditioned, then you and I are both caught in inversion together. Mañjuśrī, the Tathāgata has no notion of fond attachment at all. Fond attachment is like a mother cow's love for her calf: though hungry and thirsty, she goes in search of water and grass, but whether she finds enough or not, she suddenly turns back to her calf all the same. The buddhas, the World-Honored Ones, have no such notion — they regard every being equally, as they regard Rāhula. Such regard is itself the very domain of the buddhas' own wisdom. Mañjuśrī, it is like a king skilled at driving a four-horse chariot who wishes a donkey-cart to keep pace with it — there is no such possibility. You and I are just the same: to wish to fathom the Tathāgata's subtle and profound depths is likewise no such possibility. Mañjuśrī, it is like a garuḍa soaring immeasurable yojanas into the sky, who looks down upon the great ocean and sees clearly every creature of the water — fish, turtles, crocodiles, tortoises, dragons — and sees also his own reflection, as clearly as forms seen in a bright mirror. Ordinary people of little wisdom cannot fathom what such a bird sees; you and I are just the same, unable to fathom the Tathāgata's own wisdom." Mañjuśrī said to Cunda: "It is so, it is so, just as you have said. It is not that I failed to understand this matter myself — I only wished to test you, and your standing among the bodhisattvas."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this whole exchange as a deliberately staged pedagogical performance rather than a real disagreement between Mañjuśrī and Cunda — Mañjuśrī, he stresses, was never actually confused about the Tathāgata's unconditioned nature, and says so plainly at the very end ("I only wished to test you"). The staging matters because of who is actually meant to overhear it: the śrāvaka-disciples still present, whose confidence in this doctrine cannot simply be asserted to them directly, but has to be built by watching one bodhisattva press the hardest available objection (impermanence, the analogy to any other conditioned thing that ages and dies, the villager who loses his power) and watching another meet it point for point. Huiyuan traces the argument's actual turn to Cunda's series of counter-parables: the fortune-teller's short-lived heir, the poor woman who dies protecting her child at the Ganges, the exhausted traveler who covers himself with his own robe against a fire he cannot escape. Now that you have encountered this jeweled city of the Buddha-dharma, you should not take hold of what is worthless and false instead — it would be like a merchant leader who, having come upon a true city of treasure, gathers up mere tiles and rubble and returns home with those instead. You are just the same, having encountered a city of treasure and taking hold of what is worthless. You monks, do not settle for a lesser state of mind and call it sufficient. Though you have now gone forth from the household life, you have not yet given rise to a genuine longing for the Great Vehicle. You monks, though your bodies now wear the dyed robes of a monastic, your minds have not yet been dyed by the pure Dharma of the Great Vehicle. You monks, I must now teach you the plain truth: at this very moment, while I am still present and the whole assembly is gathered together in harmony, the Tathāgata's own Dharma-nature is true and free of any inversion — and so you should strive diligently, gathering your minds and, with courage, break apart your mental fetters, for once the sun of the ten powers has set, you will be covered over by ignorance."

"Monks, it is like the great earth, the mountains, and the medicinal herbs, all put to use for the sake of beings; my own Dharma is just the same, bringing forth the fine flavor of the nectar of the Dharma, and becoming, for beings, the true medicine for every kind of affliction and sickness. I shall now cause all beings, together with my own children, the fourfold assembly, to abide securely within the secret treasury, and I myself shall likewise abide within it as I enter parinirvāṇa. What is meant by this secret treasury? It is like the three dots of the letter i: if they are joined together, they do not form the letter i; if they are simply set side by side in a row, they likewise do not form it — only when they are placed as the three eyes on Maheśvara's face do they come to form the letter i; if they remain entirely separate from one another, they likewise cannot form it. I am just the same: the Dharma of liberation is not, by itself, nirvāṇa; the Tathāgata's own body is not, by itself, nirvāṇa; the great prajñā is not, by itself, nirvāṇa; and these three dharmas, each remaining entirely distinct from the others, are not nirvāṇa either. It is only now, abiding within these three dharmas together, that I am, for the sake of beings, said to enter nirvāṇa — just as with the letter i in this world."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan works through the letter-i simile's logic in three separate registers, each answering a slightly different question about why three things that are each, on their own, "not nirvāṇa" can nonetheless together be nirvāṇa. Taken as a contrast between the provisional and the real: the three dots joined into a solid mass, or set merely side by side, stand for expedient, conditioned versions of liberation, the Dharma-body, and prajñā that cannot constitute true nirvāṇa precisely because they remain impermanent constructions; only their genuine, unconditioned counterparts, united as Maheśvara's three eyes are united in a single face, actually form it. Taken instead as a contrast between expedient and intrinsically pure attainment: the three dots joined or scattered belong to the "gate of bodhi" — results still produced by cultivation — while nirvāṇa proper belongs to what is intrinsically, already pure, needing no further production at all. And taken as a contrast between the parts and the whole: each dot, examined separately, plainly fails to be the letter, exactly as each of the three dharmas, examined in isolation, fails to be nirvāṇa — yet what fails distributively can still succeed collectively, the way the three separate strokes, gathered into one particular configuration, succeed as a single character where no single stroke by itself ever could. Huiyuan does not choose among these three readings as though only one could be correct; he presents them as three complementary angles on the same non-negotiable point the simile is built to make unmistakable — that liberation, the Dharma-body, and wisdom are not three candidate definitions of nirvāṇa to pick among, nor three separate accomplishments to rank, but a single reality nameable in three ways at once, inseparable from one another the moment any one of them is genuinely present.

Ru-Yi Translation

Though they give rise to these perceptions, they have not grasped their true meaning — just like that drunken man, who, where there is no spinning at all, gives rise to a perception of spinning. Self here means the meaning of buddha; permanence means the meaning of the Dharma-body; bliss means the meaning of nirvāṇa; purity means the meaning of the Dharma. You monks, how can you speak of a perception of self as though it were itself arrogance and pride that keeps one turning through birth and death? To reckon the impermanent as permanent, and the permanent as impermanent, is an inverted teaching. To reckon no-self as self, and self as no-self, is an inverted teaching. To reckon the impure as pure, and the pure as impure, is an inverted teaching. Having these four inverted teachings, such a person does not know how to rightly cultivate the dharmas at all."

"You monks give rise to a perception of bliss within what is suffering, a perception of permanence within what is impermanent, a perception of self within what is without self, and a perception of purity within what is impure. But there is also, in truth, a permanence, bliss, self, and purity belonging to the world, and a permanence, bliss, self, and purity belonging to what transcends the world. The dharmas of the world have the word but not the meaning; those that transcend the world have both the word and the meaning. Why is this? Because the dharmas of the world are subject to four inversions, they do not know the true meaning. Why is this? Because there is inversion of perception, inversion of mind, and inversion of view. Because of these three inversions, worldly people see suffering within what is bliss, see impermanence within what is permanent, see no-self within what is self, and see impurity within what is pure — this is called inversion. Because of this inversion, the world knows the word but not the meaning. What, then, is the true meaning? No-self is the name for birth and death; self is the name for the Tathāgata. Impermanence belongs to the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas; permanence is the Tathāgata's own Dharma-body. Suffering belongs to every heterodox teacher; bliss is itself nirvāṇa. Impurity is every conditioned dharma; purity is the true Dharma possessed by every buddha and bodhisattva. This is called freedom from inversion. Because it is free of inversion, it knows both the word and the meaning. If you wish to be free of the four inversions, you should understand permanence, bliss, self, and purity in just this way."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this passage as staging a precise reversal rather than a simple correction. The monks are not wrong that the four contemplations of impermanence, suffering, and no-self are valuable — they have cultivated them faithfully, and Huiyuan credits their objection as sincere and well-informed, not merely confused. Their actual error, on his reading, is a scope error: they have taken teachings the Buddha gave specifically to break attachment to a false, imagined self within the impermanent five aggregates, and extended them to a domain — the Tathāgata's own Dharma-body and nirvāṇa itself — where the very same words point to their exact opposites. The drunken man does not merely see something that isn't there; he applies a perceptual habit formed under one condition (motion of his own head and eyes) to a case where it produces the opposite of truth (the actually still sun and moon). Ordinary "no-self, impermanence, suffering, impurity" contemplation, Huiyuan stresses, remains entirely correct and necessary as applied to the conditioned world of birth and death — the text is explicit that "the dharmas of the world" still possess their own four terms, "with the word but not the meaning" only when that same fourfold analysis is wrongly carried over, unmodified, onto nirvāṇa. This passage supplies the doctrinal key — self is a name for the Tathāgata, permanence for the Dharma-body, bliss for nirvāṇa, purity for the true Dharma — that the rest of this sūtra will spend its remaining thirty-eight fascicles working out in full, and this collection's translation notes record it as a core terminology anchor for that reason.

The Parable of the Two Physicians

爾時諸比丘白佛言：「世尊！如佛先說：『諸法無我，汝當修學！修學是已，則離我想；離我想者，則離憍慢；離憍慢者，得入涅槃。』是義云何？」

佛告諸比丘：「善哉，善哉！汝今善能諮問是義，為自斷疑。譬如國王，闇鈍少智，有一醫師，性復頑嚚，而王不別，厚賜俸祿，療治眾病純以乳藥，亦復不知病起根原，雖知乳藥復不善解，或有風病、冷病、熱病、一切諸病，悉教服乳。是王不別，是醫知乳好醜、善惡。復有明醫曉八種術，善療眾病，知諸方藥，從遠方來。是時舊醫不知諮受，反生貢高輕慢之心。彼時明醫，即便依附，請以為師，諮受醫方祕奧之法，語舊醫言：『我今請仁以為師範，唯願為我宣暢解說。』舊醫答言：『卿今若能為我給使四十八年，然後乃當教汝醫法。』時彼明醫即受其教：『我當如是，我當如是，隨我所能，當給走使。』是時舊醫，即將客醫共入見王。是時客醫，即為王說種種醫方及餘伎藝：『大王當知，應善分別，此法如是，可以治國；此法如是，可以療病。』爾時國王聞是語已，方知舊醫癡騃無智，即便驅逐，令出國界，然後倍復恭敬客醫。是時客醫作是念言：『欲教王者，今正是時。』即語王言：『大王於我實愛念者，當求一願。』王即答言：『從此右臂及餘身分，隨意所求，一切相與。』彼客醫言：『王雖許我一切身分，然我不敢多有所求。今所求者，願王宣令，一切國內，從今已往，不得復服舊醫乳藥。所以者何？是藥毒害，多傷損故。若故服者當斬其首。斷乳藥已，終更無有橫死之人，常處安樂，故求是願。』時王答言：『汝之所求，蓋不足言。』尋為宣令：『一切國內，有病之人，皆悉不聽以乳為藥，若為藥者，當斬其首。』爾時客醫以種種味和合眾藥，謂辛、苦、醎、甜、醋等味，以療眾病，無不得差。其後不久，王復得病，即命是醫：『我今病重，困苦欲死，當云何治？』醫占王病，應用乳藥，尋白王言：『如王所患，應當服乳。我於先時，所斷乳藥，是大妄語。今若服者，最能除病，王今患熱，正應服乳。』時王語醫：『汝今狂耶？為熱病乎？而言服乳，能除此病。汝先言毒，今云何服？欲欺我耶？先醫所讚，汝言是毒，令我驅遣。今復言好，最能除病。如汝所言，我本舊醫，定為勝汝。』是時客醫復語王言：『王今不應作如是語，如虫食木，有成字者，此虫不知是字非字；智人見之，終不唱言是虫解字，亦不驚怪。大王當知，舊醫亦爾，不別諸病，悉與乳藥，如彼虫道，偶成於字。是先舊醫，不解乳藥好醜、善惡。』時王問言：『云何不解？』客醫答王：『是乳藥者，亦是毒害，亦是甘露。云何是乳復名甘露？若是牸牛，不食酒糟、滑草、麥䴬，其犢調善，放牧之處不在高原，亦不下濕，飲以清流，不令馳走，不與特牛同共一群，飲餧調適，行住得所。如是乳者，能除諸病，是則名為甘露妙藥。除是乳已，其餘一切皆名毒害。』爾時大王聞是語已，讚言大醫：『善哉，善哉！我從今日，始知乳藥善惡、好醜。』即便服之，病得除愈。尋時宣令一切國內，從今已往當服乳藥。國人聞之皆生瞋恨，咸相謂言：『大王今者為鬼所持，為狂顛耶？而誑我等，復令服乳。』一切人民，皆懷瞋恨，悉集王所。王言：『汝等不應於我而生瞋恨，而此乳藥服與不服，悉是醫教，非是我咎。』爾時大王及諸人民，踊躍歡喜，倍共恭敬，供養是醫。一切病者皆服乳藥，病悉除愈。」

「汝等比丘！當知如來、應、正遍知、明行足、善逝、世間解、無上士、調御丈夫、天人師、佛、世尊，亦復如是，為大醫王，出現於世，降伏一切外道邪醫，諸王眾中唱如是言：『我為醫王。』欲伏外道，故唱是言：『無我、無人、眾生、壽命、養育、知見、作者、受者。』比丘當知，是諸外道所言我者，如虫食木，偶成字耳。是故如來於佛法中，唱言無我，為調眾生故、為知時故，說是無我。有因緣故，亦說有我。如彼良醫，善知於乳是藥非藥，非如凡夫所計吾我。凡夫愚人所計我者，或言大如拇指、或如芥子、或如微塵；如來說我悉不如是，是故說言，諸法無我，實非無我。何者是我？若法是實、是真、是常、是主、是依、性不變易者，是名為我。如彼大醫善解乳藥，如來亦爾，為眾生故，說諸法中真實有我。汝等四眾，應當如是修習是法。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The monks then said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha taught before: 'All dharmas are without self — you should cultivate and train in this. Having cultivated it, you will be free of the perception of self; being free of the perception of self, you will be free of arrogance; being free of arrogance, you will attain nirvāṇa.' What does this mean now?"

The Buddha said to the monks: "Excellent, excellent! You have asked about this meaning well, so as to resolve your own doubt yourselves. It is like a king, dull-witted and of little intelligence, who has a physician of a similarly stubborn and foolish disposition, whom the king, unable to tell the difference, rewards generously with rank and stipend. This physician treats every illness with nothing but milk, not even understanding the root cause of any disease; and though he claims to know the remedy of milk, he does not truly understand it at all — whether the illness is caused by wind, by cold, or by heat, whatever the ailment, he teaches the patient to drink milk in every case. The king cannot tell that this physician does not actually know good milk from bad, or whether it helps or harms. Then a brilliant physician arrives from a distant land, skilled in the eight branches of medicine, able to treat every illness well and versed in every kind of remedy. The old physician, unwilling to consult or learn from him, instead grows arrogant and contemptuous. The brilliant physician, for his part, attaches himself to the old physician, asking to become his student, seeking to learn the secret and profound art of medicine from him, and says to the old physician: 'I now ask you to be my teacher; I beg only that you explain and teach me fully.' The old physician answers: 'If you can serve me faithfully for forty-eight years, I will then teach you the art of medicine.' The brilliant physician accepts this condition: 'I shall do just as you say, I shall do just as you say — I will serve you to the very limit of my ability.' The old physician then brings this visiting physician together with him to an audience with the king. There the visiting physician explains to the king every kind of medical remedy and skill besides, saying: 'Great king, you should know how to discern well: this remedy of this kind can govern the state; this remedy of that kind can cure disease.' Hearing this, the king at once recognizes that the old physician is foolish and without wisdom, and has him driven out beyond the borders of the kingdom, and afterward shows the visiting physician all the more reverence. The visiting physician then thinks to himself: 'Now is exactly the time to teach the king.' He says to the king: 'Great king, if you truly cherish me, grant me one wish.' The king replies at once: 'Whatever you ask, even my own right arm or any other part of my body, I will grant it all to you.' The visiting physician says: 'Though the king would grant me his very body, I dare not ask for so much. What I ask instead is only this: that the king proclaim throughout the whole kingdom that, from this day forward, no one may take the old physician's remedy of milk. Why is this? Because that remedy is poisonous, and does great harm; whoever still takes it should be beheaded. Once the remedy of milk is forbidden, there will never again be anyone who dies wrongfully from it, and everyone will dwell always in ease and happiness — this is why I ask for this wish.' The king answers: 'What you ask is a small thing indeed,' and at once proclaims throughout the kingdom: 'No one who is ill within this kingdom is permitted to use milk as medicine; whoever does so shall be beheaded.' The visiting physician then compounds every kind of remedy from a blend of flavors — pungent, bitter, salty, sweet, and sour — and treats every illness with them, curing every one without exception. Not long afterward, the king himself falls ill, and summons this physician, saying: 'I am now gravely ill, suffering to the point of death — how should I be treated?' The physician examines the king's illness and determines that milk is exactly what is needed, and says to the king: 'For the illness you now suffer, you should drink milk. What I said before, forbidding milk, was a great falsehood. If you drink it now, it will cure your illness perfectly — your illness now is one of heat, and milk is exactly what is called for.' The king says to the physician: 'Have you gone mad? Or is it I who am feverish, that you should say milk can cure this illness? You once called it poison — why do you now tell me to drink it? Are you trying to deceive me? The physician I praised before, you called poison and had me drive away; now you call it good, and say it can cure illness best of all. By what you yourself have said, my original physician must surely have been better than you.' The visiting physician then says to the king: 'Great king, you should not speak this way. It is like a worm that eats through wood and happens to form what looks like a written character — the worm itself has no idea whether it is a character or not; a wise person, seeing it, would never claim the worm actually understands writing, nor be astonished by it. Great king, you should understand: that old physician was just the same — unable to distinguish one illness from another, he gave milk for every one of them, exactly like that worm's path happening to form a character. That former, old physician never truly understood whether milk was good or bad, helpful or harmful.' The king asks: 'In what way did he not understand it?' The visiting physician answers the king: 'This remedy of milk is at once a poison and a nectar. How can milk be called both poison and nectar? If the cow is one that has not eaten distiller's grain, slippery grass, or wheat husks, if her calf is gentle and well cared for, if she is pastured neither on high, dry ground nor in low, damp ground, if she drinks clear, flowing water, is not made to run about, is not kept in the same herd as a bull, and is fed and watered in proper measure, moving and resting as is fitting — then milk from such a cow can cure every illness, and is rightly called the wondrous nectar-medicine. Apart from milk of this kind, every other kind should be called a poison.' Hearing this, the great king praised the physician, saying: 'Excellent, excellent! Only from this day do I finally understand the good and the bad, the helpful and the harmful, in the remedy of milk.' He then drank it, and his illness was cured. He at once proclaimed throughout the whole kingdom that, from this day forward, everyone should drink the remedy of milk. On hearing this, the people of the kingdom were filled with resentment, saying to one another: 'Is the great king now possessed by some demon, or has he gone mad, that he should deceive us and now make us drink milk again?' Every one of the people, filled with resentment, gathered together at the king's palace. The king said to them: 'You should not be resentful toward me over this — whether milk is to be taken or not has always been entirely the physician's own teaching, and no fault of mine.' The great king and all the people then rejoiced and leapt for joy, and together showed the physician all the greater reverence, making offerings to him. Every one of the sick then drank the remedy of milk, and every illness was cured."

"You monks should know that the Tathāgata, the Worthy One, the Perfectly Awakened One, Perfect in Wisdom and Conduct, the Well-Gone One, Knower of the World, the Peerless Guide, Tamer of Beings, Teacher of Gods and Humans, Buddha, World-Honored One, is just the same: he is the great king of physicians, appearing in the world to subdue every heterodox teacher's own false remedy, and, standing among all the kings of the earth, proclaims: 'I am the king of physicians.' In order to subdue the heterodox teachers, he proclaims: 'There is no self, no person, no sentient being, no life, no rearing, no knower, no seer, no doer, no receiver.' Monks, you should understand that when these heterodox teachers speak of a self, it is exactly like that worm eating through wood and happening to form a character. This is why the Tathāgata, within the Buddha's own teaching, proclaims 'no-self' — he teaches this no-self for the sake of taming beings, and because he knows the right time to teach it. And when there is cause and condition for it, he equally teaches that there is a self. It is just like that skilled physician, who knew well when milk was medicine and when it was not — not at all like the self that ordinary, foolish people imagine for themselves. The self that ordinary, foolish people imagine is said to be as large as a thumb, or as small as a mustard seed, or as fine as a mote of dust; but the self the Tathāgata teaches is nothing at all like this — this is why it is said that all dharmas are without self, though this is not, in truth, an absolute absence of self. What, then, is this self? Whatever dharma is real, true, permanent, sovereign, a foundation, and unchanging in its own nature — this is called the self. Just as that great physician came to understand milk well, the Tathāgata is just the same: for the sake of beings, he teaches that there is, in truth, a real self among all dharmas. You, the fourfold assembly, should cultivate and train in this teaching just so."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the two physicians as tracking a real historical sequence rather than an abstract logical point: the "old physician" who prescribes milk indiscriminately, is later exposed as a fraud, and has milk banned kingdom-wide stands for the Buddha's own earlier teaching of no-self, taught without qualification to break the ordinary person's grasping at a fixed, substantial self; the "visiting physician" who cures every illness with a blend of flavors, then astonishingly reverses course and prescribes the very milk he had outlawed, stands for this sūtra's own teaching that beings possess a true self after all. The king's outrage at the apparent contradiction is deliberately left unresolved by any claim that the first physician was simply wrong — Huiyuan is careful to note the text's own moral: the milk itself was never simply poison or simply medicine, only correctly or incorrectly prescribed, exactly as "no-self" is neither simply true nor simply false but a remedy fitted to a particular condition (attachment to a false, substantial self) that becomes actively harmful once that attachment has been cured and a different, deeper misunderstanding — that the Tathāgata and buddha-nature themselves are nothing at all — has taken its place. This same passage carries the sūtra's formal entrustment of the Dharma to Mahākāśyapa, and Huiyuan reads the two events as deliberately paired: the teaching that will need a living steward to carry it forward correctly, distinguishing when "no-self" is the medicine and when "self" is, is handed at the very moment that distinction is first drawn out in full.
