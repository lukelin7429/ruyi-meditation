Does Milk Contain Curd?

Lion's Roar presses his hardest line of questioning yet: is buddha-nature already, secretly present in every being — the way curd is said to already be present in milk — or does it come to exist only through cultivation, the way curd comes to exist only through the dairyman's own work? Neither answer alone will do, and the ensuing debate ranges from a knife's reflection to an unhatched chick, from a king's forgotten curse to a nyagrodha seed no eye can find a tree inside, before turning to the reasons a bodhisattva falls back from the path, the extraordinary vow of one who never will, and the karmic history behind each of the thirty-two marks.

The Amra Fruit, and Two Kinds of Precept-Keeping

Chinese Source

◎爾時師子吼菩薩摩訶薩白佛言：「世尊！如先所說，菴羅果喻四種人等。有人行細，心不正實。有人心細，行不正實。有人心細，行亦正實。有人心不細，行不正實。是初二種，云何可知？如佛所說，雖依是二，不可得知。」佛言：「善哉，善哉！善男子！菴羅果喻二種人等，實難可知。以難知故，我經中說，當與共住；住若不知，當與久處；久處不知，當以智慧；智若不知，當深觀察；以觀察故，則知持戒及以破戒。善男子！具是四事，共住、久處、智慧、觀察，然後得知持戒破戒。善男子！戒有二種，持戒亦二：一究竟戒、二不究竟。有人以因緣故受持禁戒，智者當觀是人持戒，為為利養？為究竟持？善男子！如來戒者無有因緣，是故得名為究竟戒。以是義故，菩薩雖為諸惡眾生之所傷害，不生恚礙，是故如來得名成就畢竟持戒、究竟持戒。善男子！我昔一時，與舍利弗及五百弟子，俱共止住摩伽陀國瞻婆大城。時有獵師追逐一鴿，是鴿惶怖，至舍利弗影，猶故戰慄如芭蕉樹，至我影中身心安隱，恐怖得除。是故當知如來世尊畢竟持戒，乃至身影猶有是力。善男子！不究竟戒，尚不能得聲聞緣覺，何況能得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提？

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, Lion's Roar Bodhisattva Mahāsattva said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as was said before, the amra fruit gives a simile for four kinds of people: one whose conduct is refined, yet whose mind is not upright and true; one whose mind is refined, yet whose conduct is not upright and true; one whose mind is refined, and whose conduct, too, is upright and true; and one whose mind is not refined, and whose conduct, too, is not upright and true. How, then, can one come to know which of the first two kinds a given person is? As the Buddha himself has said, though one relies upon these two categories, one still cannot come to know for certain." The Buddha said: "Excellent, excellent! Good son, the amra fruit's two harder kinds of people are, in truth, difficult indeed to know. Because they are so difficult to know, I teach, within my own scriptures: one ought to dwell together with such a person; should dwelling together not reveal it, one ought to remain with him for a long time; should remaining with him for a long time not reveal it, one ought to apply wisdom; should wisdom not reveal it, one ought to observe deeply — and it is through this deep observation that one comes to know whether he upholds the precepts or breaks them. Good son, it is only once these four things — dwelling together, remaining long, applying wisdom, and deep observation — are all complete that one comes to know whether the precepts are upheld or broken. Good son, the precepts are of two kinds, and precept-keeping, likewise, is of two kinds: final, and not yet final. There is a person who upholds the precepts on account of some particular cause and condition — the wise ought to observe of this person whether he upholds them for the sake of gain, or upholds them all the way to their final completion. Good son, the Tathāgata's own precept-keeping has no cause or condition behind it at all, and it is for this very reason that it may be named final precept-keeping. It is for this very reason that, though a bodhisattva is harmed by every kind of evil being, he gives rise to no resentment or obstruction at all, and it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata may be named one who has accomplished ultimate precept-keeping, final precept-keeping. Good son, I once dwelt together with Śāriputra and five hundred disciples in the great city of Campā, in the land of Magadha. At that time, there was a hunter pursuing a single dove — the dove, terrified, fled into Śāriputra's own shadow, and yet still trembled there like a plantain tree; only upon entering my own shadow did its body and mind find peace, and its terror was entirely removed. One should therefore know that the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, possesses final precept-keeping to such a degree that even the mere shadow of his body carries this very power. Good son, precept-keeping that is not yet final cannot even attain the fruit of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha — how much less could it attain unsurpassed, complete awakening?

「復有二種：一為利養，二為正法。為利養故，受持禁戒，當知是戒不見佛性及以如來，雖聞佛性及如來名，猶不得名為聞見也。若為正法受持禁戒，當知是戒能見佛性及以如來，是名眼見，亦名聞見。復有二種：一者根深難拔，二者根淺易動。若能修習空無相願，是名根深難拔。若不修習是三三昧，雖復修習為二十五有，是名根淺易動。復有二種：一為自身，二為眾生。為眾生者，能見佛性及以如來。持戒之人復有二種：一者性自能持，二者須他教勅。若受戒已，經無量世初不漏失，或值惡國、遇惡知識、惡時、惡世，聞邪惡法、邪見同止。爾時雖無受戒之法，修持如本，無所毀犯，是名性自能持。若遇師僧白四羯磨，然後得戒，雖得戒已，要憑和上、諸師、同學、善友誨喻，乃知進止，聽法說法備諸威儀，是名須他教勅。善男子！性能持者，眼見佛性及以如來，亦名聞見。戒復有二：一聲聞戒，二菩薩戒。從初發心乃至得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是名菩薩戒。若觀白骨乃至證得阿羅漢果，是名聲聞戒。若有受持聲聞戒者，當知是人不見佛性及以如來。若有受持菩薩戒者，當知是人得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，能見佛性、如來、涅槃。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"There are, further, two kinds: for the sake of gain, and for the sake of the true Dharma. Should one uphold the precepts for the sake of gain, know that this precept-keeping does not see buddha-nature or the Tathāgata — though he may hear the name of buddha-nature and of the Tathāgata, this still cannot be named seeing through hearing. Should one uphold the precepts for the sake of the true Dharma, know that this precept-keeping is able to see buddha-nature and the Tathāgata — this is named seeing with the eye, and also named seeing through hearing. There are, further, two kinds: roots deep and hard to uproot, and roots shallow and easily shaken. One who is able to cultivate the three samādhis of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness has roots deep and hard to uproot. One who does not cultivate these three samādhis, though he may still cultivate the twenty-five states of existence, has roots shallow and easily shaken. There are, further, two kinds: for one's own sake, and for the sake of beings. One who upholds them for the sake of beings is able to see buddha-nature and the Tathāgata. One who upholds the precepts is, further, of two kinds: one whose very nature is able to uphold them of itself, and one who requires the instruction of others. Should one, having received the precepts, pass through countless lifetimes without ever once lapsing — even encountering an evil land, an evil companion, an evil time, an evil age, hearing wicked and evil teachings, and dwelling among those of wrong view — and yet, at such a time, though there be no formal precept to hold him to it, still cultivate and uphold them exactly as before, violating nothing at all, this is named one whose very nature is able to uphold them of itself. Should one instead receive the precepts only through a formal assembly of monks and the fourfold proclamation, and, having received them, still need to rely upon the guidance of his preceptor, his teachers, his fellow students, and good friends in order to know how to proceed, and to be fully equipped with proper bearing in listening to and teaching the Dharma, this is named one who requires the instruction of others. Good son, one whose very nature is able to uphold the precepts sees buddha-nature and the Tathāgata with the eye, and this is also named seeing through hearing. The precepts are, further, of two kinds: the śrāvaka's precepts, and the bodhisattva's precepts. From the very first arising of the mind of awakening all the way through the attainment of unsurpassed, complete awakening is named the bodhisattva's precepts. Contemplating the bare skeleton all the way through to the attainment of the fruit of arhatship is named the śrāvaka's precepts. Should one uphold the śrāvaka's precepts, know that this person does not see buddha-nature or the Tathāgata. Should one uphold the bodhisattva's precepts, know that this person attains unsurpassed, complete awakening, and is able to see buddha-nature, the Tathāgata, and nirvāṇa."

師子吼菩薩言：「世尊！何因緣故，受持禁戒？」佛言：「善男子！為心不悔故。何故不悔？為受樂故。何故受樂？為遠離故。何故遠離？為安隱故。何故安隱？為禪定故。何故禪定？為實知見故。何故為實知見？為見生死諸過患故。何故為見於生死過患？為心不貪著故。何故為心不貪著？為得解脫故。何故為得解脫？為得無上大涅槃故。何故為得大般涅槃？為得常樂我淨法故。何故為得常樂我淨？為得不生不滅故。何故為得不生不滅？為見佛性故。是故菩薩性自能持究竟淨戒。善男子！持戒比丘雖不發願求不悔心，不悔之心自然而得。何以故？法性爾故。雖不求樂、遠離安隱、真實知見，見生死過，心不貪著。解脫涅槃、常樂我淨、不生不滅，見於佛性而自然得。何以故？法性爾故。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar Bodhisattva said: "World-Honored One, for what cause and condition does one uphold the precepts?" The Buddha said: "Good son, for the sake of the mind's own freedom from regret. Why freedom from regret? For the sake of receiving bliss. Why receiving bliss? For the sake of departure. Why departure? For the sake of tranquility. Why tranquility? For the sake of meditative concentration. Why meditative concentration? For the sake of true seeing and knowing. Why true seeing and knowing? For the sake of seeing every fault of birth-and-death. Why seeing the faults of birth-and-death? For the sake of the mind's own freedom from craving and attachment. Why the mind's freedom from craving and attachment? For the sake of attaining liberation. Why attaining liberation? For the sake of attaining the unsurpassed great nirvāṇa. Why attaining the great nirvāṇa? For the sake of attaining the dharma of permanence, bliss, self, and purity. Why attaining permanence, bliss, self, and purity? For the sake of attaining the unborn and the unceasing. Why attaining the unborn and the unceasing? For the sake of seeing buddha-nature. It is for this very reason that the bodhisattva's very nature is able, of itself, to uphold the final, pure precepts. Good son, though a precept-keeping monk does not vow or seek a mind free of regret, this freedom from regret is attained naturally, of itself. Why is this? Because the very nature of dharmas is exactly so. Though he does not seek bliss, departure, tranquility, or true and real seeing and knowing, seeing the fault of birth-and-death, his mind is free of craving and attachment. Liberation, nirvāṇa, permanence, bliss, self, purity, the unborn and the unceasing, and the seeing of buddha-nature are all attained naturally, of themselves. Why is this? Because the very nature of dharmas is exactly so."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The amra-fruit typology (refined mind, refined conduct, both, or neither) sets up a genuinely practical epistemic problem this section then solves procedurally rather than doctrinally: since the two hardest cases cannot be told apart by any single observation, the sūtra prescribes an escalating four-step protocol — cohabitation, then duration, then applied wisdom, then deep observation — treating the discernment of genuine virtue as itself a discipline requiring method, not just insight. The pigeon fleeing to Śāriputra's shadow yet trembling, only to find peace in the Buddha's own shadow, gives this abstract "final versus not-yet-final" precept-keeping a strikingly concrete demonstration: the difference between an arhat's own genuine attainment and a buddha's own complete attainment registers even in something as passive and unwilled as a shadow.

The closing fifteen-step chain of "why" questions (precept-keeping → freedom from regret → bliss → departure → tranquility → concentration → true seeing → seeing birth-and-death's faults → freedom from craving → liberation → great nirvāṇa → permanence-bliss-self-purity → the unborn-unceasing → seeing buddha-nature) is this fascicle's own restatement, in a single unbroken chain, of the causal architecture Parts 25 and 27 built separately (the four near-causes of nirvāṇa, and the twelve links' cause/fruit framework) — and its closing claim, that a bodhisattva of genuine nature attains every one of these without ever having sought them as separate goals, previews the section immediately following, where Lion's Roar presses exactly this claim into a formal challenge about causation itself.

Is Nirvāṇa a Result Without a Cause?

Chinese Source

師子吼菩薩言：「世尊！若因持戒得不悔果，因於解脫得涅槃果者，戒則無因，涅槃無果。戒若無因，則名為常。涅槃有因，則是無常。若爾者，涅槃則為本無今有。若本無今有，是為無常，猶如然燈。涅槃若爾，云何得名我樂淨耶？」佛言：「善男子！善哉，善哉！汝以曾於無量佛所種諸善根，能問如來如是深義。善男子！不失本念，乃如是問也。我憶往昔過無量劫，波羅㮏城有佛出世，號曰善得。爾時彼佛三億歲中演說如是大涅槃經，我時與汝，俱在彼會，我以是事諮問彼佛。爾時如來為眾生故，三昧正受，未答此義。善哉，大士！乃能憶念如是本事。諦聽，諦聽，當為汝說。戒亦有因，謂聽正法。聽正法者是亦有因，謂近善友。近善友者是亦有因，所謂信心。信心者是亦有因，因有二種：一者聽法，二思惟義。善男子！信心者因於聽法，聽法者因於信心。如是二法，亦因、亦因因，亦果、亦果果。善男子！譬如尼乾立拒舉瓶，互為因果不得相離。善男子！如無明緣行，行緣無明。是無明行，亦因、亦因因，亦果、亦果果。乃至生緣老死，老死緣生，是生老死，亦因、亦因因，亦果、亦果果。善男子！生能生法，不能自生，不自生故，由生生生；生生不自生，復賴生故生。是故二生，亦因，亦因因，亦果、亦果果。善男子！信心、聽法，亦復如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar Bodhisattva said: "World-Honored One, if the fruit of freedom from regret is attained through the cause of precept-keeping, and the fruit of nirvāṇa is attained through the cause of liberation, then the precepts have no cause of their own, while nirvāṇa has a fruit. If the precepts have no cause, they should be named permanent; if nirvāṇa has a cause, it should be impermanent. If this is so, then nirvāṇa would be something originally absent and now present — and, if it is originally absent and now present, it is thereby impermanent, exactly like a kindled lamp. If nirvāṇa is like this, how could it possibly be named self, bliss, and purity?" The Buddha said: "Good son, excellent, excellent! Because you have, in ages past, planted every kind of wholesome root at the feet of countless buddhas, you are able to ask the Tathāgata a question of such profound meaning as this. Good son, it is because you have not lost your original recollection that you are able to ask in just this way. I recall that, in ages past, beyond countless kalpas, there appeared in the world, in the city of Vārāṇasī, a buddha named Well-Attained. At that time, that buddha, across three hundred million years, proclaimed this very Great Nirvāṇa Sūtra, and I, together with you, was present in that very assembly, and I put this very question to that buddha. At that time, the Tathāgata, for the sake of beings, entered proper absorption in samādhi, and did not yet answer this question. Excellent, great being, that you are able to recall this very event from so long ago! Listen closely, listen closely, and I shall now explain it for you. The precepts, too, have a cause — namely, listening to the true Dharma. Listening to the true Dharma, too, has a cause — namely, drawing near to a good friend. Drawing near to a good friend, too, has a cause — namely, faith. Faith, too, has a cause, and this cause is of two kinds: listening to the Dharma, and reflecting on its meaning. Good son, faith has listening to the Dharma as its cause, and listening to the Dharma has faith as its cause — these two dharmas are thus both cause and cause-of-cause, both fruit and fruit-of-fruit. Good son, it is like two jars propped up leaning against one another, each holding the other up as both cause and effect, unable to be separated from one another. Good son, it is just as ignorance conditions formations, and formations condition ignorance — this pairing of ignorance and formations is thus both cause and cause-of-cause, both fruit and fruit-of-fruit; and, all the way up to birth conditioning old age and death, and old age and death conditioning birth, this pairing of birth and old-age-and-death is thus, likewise, both cause and cause-of-cause, both fruit and fruit-of-fruit. Good son, that which is able to produce birth cannot produce itself — because it cannot produce itself, it is through a further birth that this birth is itself produced; yet this further birth, too, cannot produce itself, and must, in turn, rely upon a birth to be produced. It is for this very reason that these two births are thus both cause and cause-of-cause, both fruit and fruit-of-fruit. Good son, faith and listening to the Dharma are exactly the same.

「善男子！是果非因謂大涅槃。何故名果？是上果故、沙門果故、婆羅門果故、斷生死故、破煩惱故，是故名果。為諸煩惱之所呵責，是故涅槃名果，煩惱者名為過過。善男子！涅槃無因，而體是果。何以故？無生滅故、無所作故、非有為故、是無為故、常不變故、無處所故、無始終故。善男子！若涅槃有因，則不得稱為涅槃也。槃者言因，般涅言無，無有因故，故稱涅槃。」師子吼菩薩言：「如佛所說，涅槃無因，是義不然。若言無者，則合六義：一者畢竟無故，故名為無，如一切法無我、無我所。二者有時無故，故名為無，如世人言，河池無水、無有日月。三者少故，故名為無，如世人言，食中少醎，名為無醎，甘漿少甜，名為無甜。四者無受故，故名為無，如旃陀羅不能受持婆羅門法，是故名為無婆羅門。五者受惡法故，故名為無，如世人言，受惡法者不名沙門及婆羅門，是故名為無有沙門及婆羅門。六者不對故，故名為無，譬如無白，名之為黑，無有明故，名之無明。世尊！涅槃亦爾，有時無因，故名涅槃。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, that which is a fruit and not a cause is named the great nirvāṇa. Why is it named a fruit? Because it is the supreme fruit, the fruit of the śramaṇa, the fruit of the brahmin, the severing of birth-and-death, and the destruction of affliction — it is for these reasons that it is named a fruit. Because it is what every affliction is censured for the sake of, nirvāṇa is named a fruit, and affliction is named the fault-of-faults. Good son, nirvāṇa has no cause at all, and yet its own essence is a fruit. Why is this? Because it is without arising or ceasing, without any activity, not a conditioned thing, but the unconditioned itself; because it is permanent and unchanging, without any location, and without beginning or end. Good son, if nirvāṇa had a cause, it could not be named nirvāṇa at all — 'vāṇa' means cause, and 'nir' means absence; because it is without any cause at all, it is therefore named nirvāṇa." Lion's Roar Bodhisattva said: "As the Buddha has taught, that nirvāṇa is without cause is not, I think, correct. Should one speak of 'absence,' this must accord with one of six meanings: first, absence named for what is, in the end, utterly nonexistent — as when every dharma is said to be without self or without anything belonging to a self; second, absence named for what is merely absent at a given time — as when people say a river or pond is 'without water,' or that there is 'no sun or moon'; third, absence named for scarcity — as when people say that food with little salt is 'without salt,' or that a sweet drink with little sugar is 'without sweetness'; fourth, absence named for the failure to receive something — as when a caṇḍāla, unable to receive and uphold the brahmin's own teaching, is therefore said to be 'without brahminhood'; fifth, absence named for having received an evil teaching — as when people say that one who has received an evil teaching cannot be named a śramaṇa or a brahmin, and is therefore said to be 'without any śramaṇa or brahmin' in him; sixth, absence named for the lack of a contrasting opposite — as when the absence of white is named black, and the absence of light is named darkness. World-Honored One, nirvāṇa is exactly the same: it is merely absent of a cause at a given time, and it is for this reason that it is named nirvāṇa."

佛言：「善男子！汝今所說如是六義，何故不引畢竟無者以喻涅槃？乃取有時無耶？善男子！涅槃之體畢竟無因，猶如無我及無我所。善男子！世法、涅槃終不相對，是故六事不得為喻。善男子！一切諸法悉無有我，而此涅槃真實有我，以是義故，涅槃無因，而體是果，是因非果，名為佛性。非因生故，是因非果。非沙門果，故名非果。何故名因？以了因故。善男子！因有二種：一者生因，二者了因。能生法者，是名生因；燈能了物，故名了因。煩惱諸結，是名生因；眾生父母，是名了因。如穀子等是名生因；地水糞等是名了因。復有生因，謂六波羅蜜、阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。復有了因，謂佛性、阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。復有了因，謂六波羅蜜、佛性。復有生因，謂首楞嚴三昧、阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。復有了因，謂八正道、阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。復有生因，所謂信心、六波羅蜜。」

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lion's Roar's opening challenge presses a genuine logical worry: if every step in a causal chain (precepts → freedom from regret → ... → nirvāṇa) is, in fact, a cause producing a fruit, then nirvāṇa itself, sitting at the chain's far end, is a produced fruit — and a produced fruit, like a kindled lamp, is precisely the kind of thing that can also go out. The Buddha's answer reframes the entire chain rather than defending nirvāṇa's place within it: faith and hearing the Dharma, examined closely, are not a simple one-directional sequence at all but a genuinely reciprocal pair (the "two jars leaning against each other" and the ignorance/formations pairing from the twelve links), each requiring the other to arise — and this reciprocity is offered as the general pattern of causation within saṃsāra, against which nirvāṇa is then declared the one exception: a fruit that is not the product of any cause at all.

The generative-cause/revealing-cause (生因/了因) distinction introduced here — a seed generates a sprout it did not contain, while a lamp merely reveals an object that was already there in the dark — becomes this fascicle's central analytical tool for the entire milk-and-curd debate that follows: whether buddha-nature is "produced" by practice (as a sprout is produced by a seed) or merely "revealed" by it (as an object is revealed by a lamp) is exactly the question Lion's Roar is about to press through the curd analogy, and the answer given here — that buddha-nature is a revealing cause for awakening, while the six perfections are a generative cause for the same awakening, and a revealing cause for buddha-nature itself — previews the two-causes (正因/緣因) framework that will resolve that debate.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lion's Roar's question here presses the same worry that opened the previous fascicle's fourteen-fold catechism — how can something without any of form's defining properties (length, color, location) be an object of seeing at all? — but the Buddha's answer takes a different route than the earlier apophatic catalogue: rather than negating every predicate, he sorts buddha-nature (and the buddha-body itself) into paired categories — permanent/impermanent, visible/invisible, form/not-form — each pairing tracking a different practical distinction (whether skillful manifestation or unconditioned essence is meant; whether the seer has reached the ten abidings or beyond) rather than a single univocal answer. This sets up the chapter's next and longest debate directly: if buddha-nature can be called "form" at all under any of these three pairings, in what sense is it already present in a being the way curd is said to be already present in milk — a question Lion's Roar raises in the very next section and pursues at greater length than any other topic in this fascicle.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Lion's Roar's opening claim ("I now know for certain that milk possesses the nature of curd") and the Buddha's reply as opening a structured four-part refutation, each part disproving the fixed presence of curd in milk by appeal to a different piece of ordinary human behavior: people take up a blade to see their own face (yet the reflected face is visibly distorted — long when held upright, wide when held sideways — which no fixed image inside the metal could explain); a milk-seller charges only for milk, never separately for the curd it might become; a man does not call his pregnant wife "mother," on pain of an absurd regress into grandchildren and siblings; and no one sees all five flavors, or a nyagrodha tree's full sprout-to-fruit sequence, present all at once in a single seed. He further divides the knife-face exchange's own middle section — Lion's Roar's fallback claim that the eye's own light reaches the blade to explain the distortion — into six distinct counter-arguments (near and far objects seen at once; nothing seen in between; fire not burning the eye that sees it; doubt about a distant white object if the light truly reached and resolved it; sight of objects embedded in crystal or underwater; sight of crystal's interior yet not of what lies past an opaque wall), reading this unusually granular breakdown as evidence the debate engages a real, contemporary theory of vision (that seeing occurs via a ray extending from the eye to its object) rather than a strawman built only to be knocked down.

On the closing string of similes — brush-and-paper forming characters neither the paper nor the ink previously contained, blue and yellow combining into a green neither singly possessed, food producing a life it does not itself possess — Huiyuan reads these as jointly establishing the chapter's key point about the "primary cause" (正因) and "conditional cause" (緣因) distinction: a result's dependence on assembled conditions is not weak evidence for some fainter, pre-existing version of that result already lying latent within one of those conditions, but is, if anything, the very opposite — proof that the result is genuinely new, brought about only by the meeting of conditions, exactly as buddha-nature itself is said to require conditions (the six perfections) without this making it any less real, present, or universally possessed once seen. The virtual-space simile closing the passage — a space every being possesses yet none directly perceives, evidenced only indirectly by the very fact that movement and growth remain possible at all — gives this abstract principle its clearest illustration: absence-from-perception, once again, is never the same claim as absence-from-being.

師子吼菩薩言：「世尊！一切眾生有佛性性，如乳中酪性。若乳無酪性，云何佛說有二種因，一者正因、二者緣因？緣因者，一醪、二煖。虛空無性，故無緣因。」佛言：「善男子！若使乳中定有酪性者，何須緣因？」師子吼菩薩言：「世尊！以有性故，故須緣因。何以故？欲明見故，緣因者即是了因。世尊！譬如闇中先有諸物，為欲見故，以燈照了。若本無者，燈何所照？如泥中有瓶，故須人、水、輪、繩、杖等而為了因。如尼拘陀子，須地、水、糞而作了因。乳中醪煖，亦復如是，須作了因。是故雖先有性，要假了因，然後得見，以是義故，定知乳中先有酪性。」「善男子！若使乳中定有酪性者，即是了因。若是了因，復何須了？善男子！若是了因，性是了者，常應自了；若自不了，何能了他？若言了因有二種性：一者自了、二者了他，是義不然。何以故？了因一法，云何有二？若有二者，乳亦應二。若使乳中無二相者，云何了因而獨有二？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar Bodhisattva said: "World-Honored One, every being possesses this very buddha-nature-ness, just as milk possesses the nature of curd. If milk has no nature of curd at all, why does the Buddha teach that there are two kinds of cause — the primary cause and the conditional cause? The conditional cause is, first, the curdling agent, and, second, warmth — and empty space, having no such nature at all, therefore has no conditional cause of its own." The Buddha said: "Good son, if milk truly, fixedly possessed the nature of curd, what need would there be for any conditional cause at all?" Lion's Roar Bodhisattva said: "World-Honored One, it is precisely because this nature is already present that a conditional cause is needed — why is this? Because it is needed only to make it manifest and seen — the conditional cause is itself none other than the revealing cause. World-Honored One, it is like objects already present in the dark: it is only in order to see them that a lamp is lit to reveal them — were they not originally present at all, what would the lamp's light possibly be revealing? It is like a pot already present in clay, which therefore requires a potter, water, a wheel, cord, and a rod to serve as its revealing cause; or a nyagrodha tree already present in its seed, which requires soil, water, and manure to serve as its revealing cause. The curdling agent and warmth within milk are exactly the same, requiring only a revealing cause to manifest what is already there. It is for this reason that, though the nature is already present beforehand, it still requires the aid of a revealing cause before it can be seen — and it is for this very reason that one may know for certain that milk already possesses the nature of curd beforehand." "Good son, if milk truly, fixedly possessed the nature of curd, this would itself already be a revealing cause. And, if it is itself already a revealing cause, what further need would there be to reveal it at all? Good son, if this is a revealing cause, and its very nature is to reveal, it ought forever to reveal itself; and, if it cannot reveal itself, how could it possibly reveal anything else? Should you say that a revealing cause is of two natures — one that reveals itself, and one that reveals another — this, I think, is not correct. Why is this? A revealing cause is a single thing — how, then, could it be of two kinds? Were it truly of two kinds, milk, too, ought to be of two kinds. And, if milk itself bears no such twofold characteristic at all, how, then, could a revealing cause alone possess these two?"

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar said: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught, this, I think, is not correct. The past has already ceased, and the future has not yet arrived — how, then, can either be named 'existent'? Should you say that what is certain to come into being can already be named 'existent,' this, too, is not correct — for, in the world, when people see that a couple has no children, they simply say they have no child. If every being, in truth, has no buddha-nature at all, how, then, can it be taught that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature?" The Buddha said: "Good son, the past is named 'existent' in this way: it is like planting an orange seed — the sprout arises and the seed itself ceases, and this sprout, too, is sweet, all the way through to the fruit it eventually bears, which is likewise sweet in taste, until it fully ripens and only then turns sour. Good son, this very sour taste was entirely absent in the seed, the sprout, and even the newly formed fruit — it is only once the fruit reaches its own proper ripening that this form, color, and appearance gives rise to this sour taste; and, though this sour taste was originally absent and is now present, it does not arise apart from its own original seed. This original seed, though it has already passed away, may therefore still be named 'existent' — and it is for this very reason that the past may be named 'existent.' How, then, may the future also be named 'existent'? It is like a man who plants sesame — someone asks him: 'Why do you plant this?' He answers: 'Because there is oil in it.' Though there is, in truth, no oil present yet, once the sesame has ripened, its seeds are gathered, steamed, pounded, and pressed, and only then is the oil actually extracted — one should know that this man has spoken no falsehood at all. It is for this very reason that this is named 'future existence.' How, then, may the past also be named 'existent' in another sense? Good son, it is like a man who, in private, curses the king — years pass, and only then does the king come to hear of it. Having heard of it, he asks: 'Why did you curse me?' The man answers: 'Great king, I did not curse you at all — why is this? Because the one who cursed you has long since ceased to exist.' The king says: 'The one who cursed me — his self, his very body, both still exist even now — how could you say he has ceased to exist?' — and, on account of this very affair, the man loses his own life. Good son, though this self and this body, in the philosophical sense the man intended, are, in truth, absent, the fruit of his own past action does not thereby cease at all — and this, too, is what is named 'past existence.' How, then, may the future also be named 'existent' in this further sense? It is like a man who goes to a potter's workshop and asks whether a pot is to be had there — the potter answers that a pot is indeed to be had, though, in truth, no pot has yet been made at all; it is only because clay is present that he says a pot is to be had, and one should know that this potter, too, has spoken no falsehood at all. Milk's possession of curd, and a being's own possession of buddha-nature, are exactly the same. One who wishes to see buddha-nature ought to observe its own proper season and its own form and color — it is for this very reason that I teach that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature, and this is, in truth, no falsehood at all."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Lion's Roar's fallback position — that the "conditional cause" is really a "revealing cause," uncovering a pre-existing curd-nature the way a lamp uncovers an object already sitting in the dark — as the debate's most sophisticated move, since it seems to preserve both universal possession and the genuine need for practice at once. He tracks the Buddha's refutation as four successive blows against this single position: first, an already-existing revealing cause would need no further revealing itself, an infinite regress Lion's Roar tries to escape by proposing a cause that reveals both itself and other things; second, this proposed self-revealing capacity would have to be single (a "revealing cause" is one thing, not two), yet self-revealing and other-revealing are clearly two different functions, so the singular cause cannot coherently perform both; third, the "I, together with seven others, make eight" counting simile fails because a counted color or form never counts itself — the counting capacity belongs to intelligence, not to the counted object — so a "revealing cause" modeled on this analogy still cannot reveal itself; and fourth, if the effect (buddha-nature fully attained) already sat fixed within the cause (a being's present, unawakened state), moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom could never actually increase through training, which plainly contradicts what is observed of students under a teacher.

On the three-times debate that follows, Huiyuan's broader point — echoed by every one of the four similes given (the orange's sourness absent from seed and sprout yet inseparable from them; the sesame oil not yet pressed yet truthfully promised; the king's curse whose speaker "no longer exists" yet whose consequence still lands; the potter's pot not yet thrown yet truthfully answered for) — is that ordinary language already uses "existence" flexibly across all three times without anyone taking this as dishonesty, so the sūtra is not inventing a special exception for buddha-nature so much as making explicit a distinction ordinary speech already relies on: a thing can be truthfully called "existent" in virtue of what it is certain to become, without this collapsing into the false claim that it already, fully is that thing right now.

The Nyagrodha Seed, and Lion's Roar's Hardest Challenge

Chinese Source

師子吼言：「一切眾生無有佛性者，云何而得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提？以正因故，故令眾生得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。何等正因？所謂佛性。世尊！若尼拘陀子，無尼拘陀樹者，何故名為尼拘陀子，而不名為佉陀羅子？世尊！如瞿曇姓不得稱為阿坻耶姓，阿坻耶姓亦復不得稱瞿曇姓。尼拘陀子亦復如是，不得稱為佉陀羅尼子，佉陀羅尼子不得稱為尼拘陀子。猶如世尊不得捨離瞿曇種姓，眾生佛性亦復如是，以是義故，當知眾生悉有佛性。」

Lion's Roar Bodhisattva said: "World-Honored One, if milk has no nature of curd at all, sesame has no nature of oil at all, a nyagrodha seed has no nature of a tree at all, and clay has no nature of a pot at all, then, likewise, no being at all has buddha-nature — and, if this is so, then the Buddha's earlier teaching that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature, and therefore ought to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, is, I think, not correct. Why is this? Human beings and heavenly beings possess no such fixed nature at all — it is precisely because they possess no fixed nature that a human being can become a heavenly being, and a heavenly being can become a human being, through the cause and condition of karma, and not through any fixed nature at all. A bodhisattva mahāsattva, too, attains unsurpassed, complete awakening through the cause and condition of karma. If every being truly possesses buddha-nature, then for what cause and condition does the icchantika sever every wholesome root and fall into hell? If the bodhi-mind is itself buddha-nature, then the icchantika ought never to be able to sever it at all; and, if it can indeed be severed, how, then, can buddha-nature possibly be called permanent? And, if it is not permanent, it cannot be named buddha-nature at all. If every being truly possesses buddha-nature, why, then, is there any such thing as 'the first arising of the mind' of awakening at all? And how, then, can one speak of a bodhisattva as either retrogressing or non-retrogressing? One who retrogresses, one should know, is a person entirely without buddha-nature. World-Honored One, a bodhisattva mahāsattva who single-mindedly turns toward unsurpassed, complete awakening, who possesses great loving-kindness and great compassion, who sees the faults and calamities of birth, old age, sickness, death, and affliction, who contemplates the great nirvāṇa as free of every such fault, who has faith in the three jewels and in the fruit of karmic action, and who upholds the precepts — dharmas of exactly this kind are what should be named buddha-nature. And, if buddha-nature exists apart from these very dharmas, what need would there be for these dharmas to serve as its cause and condition at all? World-Honored One, milk, without needing any further condition at all, is bound to become curd — yet fresh butter is not like this, and must instead depend upon causes and conditions, namely human effort, a water-vessel, and a churning cord. A being is exactly the same: if he already possesses buddha-nature, he ought to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening entirely apart from any cause or condition at all. And, if it is truly fixed and already present, why, then, does the practitioner, upon seeing the suffering of the three evil destinies, and of birth, old age, sickness, and death, give rise to a mind of retreat at all? Nor, in that case, would he need to cultivate the six perfections at all — he ought simply to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening directly, exactly as milk becomes curd without any further condition. And yet it is certainly not the case that unsurpassed, complete awakening is attained without depending on the six perfections at all — it is for this very reason that one should know that no being at all truly possesses buddha-nature. Furthermore, as the Buddha has earlier taught, the jewel of the Saṅgha is permanent — and, if it is truly permanent, it cannot be impermanent; and, if it is not impermanent, how, then, could it possibly attain unsurpassed, complete awakening at all? And, if the Saṅgha is truly permanent, how, then, can it also be said that every being, without exception, possesses buddha-nature? World-Honored One, if beings, from the very beginning, originally had no bodhi-mind at all, nor any mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening, and only came to possess it afterward, then a being's own buddha-nature ought to be exactly the same — originally absent, and only present afterward. It is for this very reason that every being, without exception, ought to have no buddha-nature at all."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the eight-reasons-for-non-seeing catalogue as Lion's Roar's own strongest possible move on behalf of hidden presence — if a nyagrodha tree fails to be seen in its seed only because of some ordinary perceptual limitation (too distant, too subtle, too obstructed, and so on), then non-seeing would indeed be compatible with the tree's genuine presence. The Buddha's refutation, on Huiyuan's reading, does not deny that things can be unseen for such reasons in general — it denies only that a nyagrodha seed's own case fits any of the eight, since a tree's characteristic form is precisely coarse, not subtle or easily obstructed, so the seed's own visible coarseness (root, stem, leaf, and fruit already discernible in gross outline) rules out exactly the excuses that would let a fine-grained, hidden tree escape detection. The burning argument that follows then closes off a different escape route: were "burnability" and "growability" both fixed properties already present in the seed from the start, both ought to be equally actual at every moment, yet a seed that is burned never also produces a tree, which is only intelligible if neither property was ever a fixed, pre-existing feature of the seed to begin with, but something that comes to be only through whichever set of conditions the seed actually meets.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lion's Roar's culminating rebuttal here is the sharpest and most comprehensive challenge to the buddha-nature teaching raised anywhere in the sūtra so far, gathering nearly every thread of this fascicle's debate into a single, coordinated attack: if human and heavenly rebirth already show that karma, not fixed nature, explains transformation, why posit a fixed nature for awakening specifically? If buddha-nature is truly present and permanent, how can the icchantika sever it, and why does any bodhisattva ever retrogress at all? And if it is genuinely a cause requiring conditions — precepts, compassion, the six perfections — in what sense was it ever "already there" rather than simply "not yet produced"? Note that this challenge does not get a dedicated point-by-point rebuttal in the verses immediately following it — the Buddha's actual response, taken up in the next section, works instead by relocating the entire debate: rather than defending buddha-nature's fixed presence against each objection in turn, he distinguishes the impermanent bodhi-mind (which genuinely can retrogress, and is not itself buddha-nature) from buddha-nature proper (which the icchantika's severing never actually touches, precisely because it operates at an entirely different level than mental states or karmic causation) — a distinction that, in effect, concedes much of Lion's Roar's logical pressure on "the bodhi-mind as buddha-nature" while preserving the deeper claim intact.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Buddha's answer here not as a point-by-point rebuttal of Lion's Roar's preceding challenge but as a reframing that dissolves much of its force at once: the bodhi-mind and buddha-nature are quietly separated into two different things sharing a confusingly similar vocabulary. Huiyuan reads the ore-and-gold simile as this section's positive account of how a "primary cause" can require a "conditional cause" without either collapsing into the other: gold, silver, copper, and iron all share the name and substance "ore," yet only the right combination of a smith's furnace and human effort draws out gold specifically from ore that could equally have yielded another metal — establishing, against Lion's Roar's charge that conditioned production disproves prior existence, that a substance's dependence on conditions to become manifest is compatible with its already being the specific kind of substance it is. He reads the concluding restatement — buddha-nature as primary cause (正因), the arising of the bodhi-mind as conditional cause (緣因) — as the fascicle's own final settlement of the entire causal debate opened at the very start of this part: the two-causes framework survives every objection because it was never claiming that the effect (buddhahood) already, fully exists within the cause, only that the specific cause capable of yielding it (buddha-nature, as opposed to some other substance) is already, genuinely present.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four escalating lists of retrogression-causes (thirteen, then six, then five, then two) work like the ten-things passage of Part 27: not redundant repetition but successive tightenings, each list naming failure at a different grain — the thirteen catalogue interior psychological states (doubt, dread, self-contempt), the six name relational and behavioral failures (miserliness, bad company, worldly entanglement), and the closing five and two narrow to the barest essentials (non-Buddhist company, craving, irreverence). Read together, they make the same point from four angles: retrogression is never one single failure but a whole ecology of smaller ones, any single strand of which can unravel the whole.

The vow that follows is the longest sustained first-person statement in the sūtra so far, and its scale is itself the argument: rather than defining "non-retrogression" abstractly, the text simply lets a bodhisattva speak in his own voice at enough length that the reader feels the sheer comprehensiveness of what a genuinely unshakeable resolve would have to cover — bodily harm met with gratitude rather than resentment, worldly competence (languages, crafts, medicine, statecraft) placed entirely in service of others, austerity chosen rather than feared, and even birth among non-Buddhist teachers accepted as a deliberate strategy for undermining wrong view from within. Its closing line — that even this vow, sustained to its end, is still not itself buddha-nature — completes the same maneuver as the previous section's separation of the bodhi-mind from buddha-nature proper: however total a practitioner's resolve becomes, resolve itself remains an act of mind, and buddha-nature, by this fascicle's own repeated insistence, is what no act of mind, however vast, can be identical to.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, you cannot, merely because there is such a thing as a retrogressing mind, say that beings therefore have no buddha-nature at all. It is like two men who both hear that, in a distant land, there is a mountain of the seven treasures, upon which flows a clear spring whose water is sweet — whoever is able to reach it will forever put an end to poverty, and whoever drinks of its water will have his lifespan increased by ten thousand years; only the road there is impossibly distant, steep, and beset with every kind of danger. At that time, these two men both wished to make the journey together — one equipped himself with every kind of provision, while the other set out with nothing at all. As they journeyed on together, they met, along the road, a man richly laden with treasure and all seven kinds of jewels. The two men came forward and asked him: 'Good sir, does that land truly contain a mountain of the seven treasures?' The man answered: 'It truly exists, without falsehood — I have already obtained its treasure, and drunk of its water; only the road is perilous, beset with many bandits, its sands and salt-flats and thorns leaving it bare of water and grass — those who set out for it number in the thousands and tens of thousands, yet those who actually arrive are very few indeed.' Upon hearing this, one of the two men at once repented, saying: 'Since the road is so impossibly distant, and its hardships so many, and those who set out number beyond count while those who arrive are almost none — how, then, could I possibly reach it? My own household affairs, as things stand, roughly provide for themselves already — should I set out upon this road, I might well lose my very life, and, my body and life no longer whole, what use would a long lifespan even be to me then?' The other man said, further: 'Since another has been able to cross it, I, too, am able to cross it. Should I succeed in arriving, I shall attain exactly what I wished — gathering its treasure, and drinking its sweet water. And, should I fail to arrive, then death itself shall simply be my appointed end.' At that time, of these two men, one repented and turned back, while the other pressed onward and reached that very mountain, where he gathered great wealth, drank the water exactly as he had wished, and, laden with all he had gained, returned to his own home, where he supported his parents and provided generously for his own kin. The man who had repented and turned back, upon seeing this, felt his own heart burn within him, thinking: 'He who went has now returned — why, then, do I remain here?' — and at once equipped himself and set out upon that very road. The mountain of the seven treasures is a likeness for the great nirvāṇa; its sweet, delicious water is a likeness for buddha-nature; these two men are a likeness for two bodhisattvas who have both newly given rise to the mind of the path; the perilous, evil road is a likeness for birth-and-death; the man they encountered is a likeness for the Buddha, the World-Honored One; the bandits present along the way are a likeness for the four Māras; the sands, salt-flats, and thorns are a likeness for every affliction; the absence of water and grass is a likeness for the failure to cultivate the path to bodhi; the man who turned back is a likeness for the retrogressing bodhisattva; and the man who pressed straight onward is a likeness for the non-retrogressing bodhisattva. Good son, a being's own buddha-nature abides forever, unchanging, exactly like that perilous road itself — one cannot say that, merely because a man repented and turned back, the road itself thereby became impermanent; buddha-nature is exactly the same. Good son, along the path to bodhi, there is, in the end, no such thing as true retrogression at all. Good son, it is exactly as with the man who repented: seeing his former companion return with treasure in hand, possessed of power and ease, supporting his parents and providing amply for his kin, enjoying great happiness in abundance — upon seeing this, his own heart burned within him, and he, too, at once equipped himself and set out once more upon that very road, sparing not his own body or life, enduring every hardship, until he, too, finally reached that very mountain of the seven treasures. The retrogressing bodhisattva is exactly the same. Good son, every being, without exception, is certain to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening — it is for this very reason that, within my own scriptures, I teach that every being, without exception — even one who has committed the five heinous crimes, violated the four gravest precepts, or is an icchantika — possesses buddha-nature."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seven-jewel mountain simile resolves the whole retrogression debate through a shift of reference, not a denial of the facts: what turns back is never the road, the mountain, or the water — only, temporarily, one traveler's own resolve — and the man who turns back is shown, in the story's own telling, eventually completing the journey after all, spurred on by seeing his companion succeed. This structurally answers Lion's Roar's sharpest objection several sections earlier (if buddha-nature is fixed and permanent, why does anyone ever retrogress at all?): retrogression describes a temporary interruption in the traveler, describable and real, without implying anything at all about the permanence of the destination itself, which is precisely the distinction the sūtra has been building since separating buddha-nature from the impermanent bodhi-mind two sections earlier. The story's insistence that the road "never becomes impermanent" merely because a traveler turns back translates directly: a bodhisattva's retrogression is a fact about bodhisattvas, not a fact about buddha-nature, and the two must never be confused despite how naturally the debate keeps sliding between them.

The closing formula — that even the one who committed the five heinous crimes, violated the four gravest precepts, or is an icchantika possesses buddha-nature — closes the entire causation debate opened at this fascicle's very start on the same note this whole chapter has struck since Part 26's lute simile: universal possession was never a claim about universal, guaranteed, effortless success, but about what remains permanently, truly present beneath even total, if temporary, incapacity.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, every being is inconceivable, and the domain of every buddha, the fruit of karma, and buddha-nature are, likewise, inconceivable. Why is this? Because these four dharmas are all, without exception, permanent, and it is precisely because they are permanent that they are inconceivable. Every being is covered over by affliction, and it is for this reason that he is named permanent; and it is through severing this very affliction that he is named impermanent. Should one say that every being is simply permanent, why, then, does he cultivate the eightfold noble path in order to sever every suffering at all? Once suffering is severed, it is then named impermanent, while the bliss he thereby receives is then named permanent. It is for this very reason that I say: it is only because every being is covered over by affliction that he fails to see buddha-nature, and it is only because he fails to see it that he fails to attain nirvāṇa."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The thirty-two marks list deliberately answers Lion's Roar's causation debate on its own terms rather than by appeal to buddha-nature: each mark is traced to a specific, ordinary virtuous act — nursing sick parents, sweeping the road for others, never disputing another's faults — with no hidden metaphysical claim that the mark was somehow already latent in the bodhisattva beforehand. This is the fascicle's own worked demonstration that "non-retrogression" can be discussed entirely in the vocabulary of karmic cause and effect, without needing buddha-nature to do any explanatory work at all — which is exactly the register in which the earlier debate showed the bodhi-mind, unlike buddha-nature, actually operates.

The closing paradox — that beings, the buddha-domain, karmic fruit, and buddha-nature are all "permanent," and inconceivable precisely because they are permanent, and yet beings are also correctly called "impermanent" once the eightfold path severs their affliction — completes this fascicle's running theme (already raised through the six-meanings-of-'without' debate and the three-times analysis of past, present, and future existence) that a single word can carry two legitimate but distinct senses depending on what, exactly, is being tracked: a being covered by affliction is "permanent" only in the sense that this covering has always applied and buddha-nature beneath it is untouched by time, while the very same being is "impermanent" in the sense that his suffering itself, once severed, does not return. Fixing which sense is meant, rather than picking one usage as simply correct, is this entire fascicle's method from its opening precept debate onward.

Part 28 Complete

This closes Part 28 (fascicle 28 of 40), the sūtra's longest and most sustained debate over buddha-nature's mode of existence. Lion's Roar's amra-fruit question on discerning genuine precept-keeping opens onto a challenge that nirvāṇa, as a caused fruit, ought to be impermanent — resolved through the reciprocal, "cause and cause-of-cause" structure of faith and hearing, and the distinction between a generative cause and a merely revealing one. The chapter's central debate then asks, at exhausting and exhilarating length, whether milk already secretly contains curd: a knife's distorted reflection, a pregnant woman not yet called "mother," a nyagrodha seed no eye can find a tree inside, and the etymology of "existence" across all three times each test whether buddha-nature is already, fully present or merely certain to be revealed — resolved through the two-causes framework (buddha-nature as primary cause, the six perfections and the bodhi-mind as conditional cause) and the sharp separation of the impermanent, retrogression-capable bodhi-mind from buddha-nature proper, which no icchantika's severing can ever actually touch. The fascicle closes with the seven-jewel mountain's answer to why anyone ever turns back from a permanent goal, the extraordinary vow of a bodhisattva who never will, and the karmic history behind each of the thirty-two marks.
