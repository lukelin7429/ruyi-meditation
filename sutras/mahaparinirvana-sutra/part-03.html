The Vajra Body, and the Virtues of This Sūtra's Name

This fascicle closes 壽命品 (The Chapter on the Buddha's Lifespan) and moves through two short chapters named for their subject: 金剛身品 (The Vajra Body) and 名字功德品 (Virtues of This Sūtra's Name). The young bodhisattva Kāśyapa opens with a long chain of questions in verse, and the Buddha answers with a teaching on equal regard that has to reckon honestly with a monk killed by a vajra-spirit and an icchantika who breaks every precept; the parable of a poor man's stolen milk explains why other traditions' claims to permanence fall short of the Tathāgata's own; and the fascicle closes with the sūtra's own explanation of what its own name means, down to the eight flavors folded into the single word "nirvāṇa."

Which Disciples Should Ask?

Chinese Source

佛復告諸比丘：「汝於戒律，有所疑者，今恣汝問，我當解說，令汝心喜。我已修學一切諸法本性空寂，了了通達。汝等比丘，莫謂如來唯修諸法本性空寂。」復告諸比丘：「若於戒律有所疑者，今可致問。」

時諸比丘即白佛言：「世尊！我等無有智慧，能問如來、應、正遍知。所以者何？如來境界不可思議，所有諸定不可思議，所演教誨不可思議，是故我等無有智慧，能問如來。世尊！譬如老人，年百二十，身嬰長病，寢臥床席，不能起居，氣力虛劣，餘命無幾。有一富人，緣事欲行，當至他方，以百斤金寄是老人，而作是言：『我今他行，以是寶物，持用相寄，或十年還，二十年還，汝當還我。』是時老人，即便受之。而此老人復無繼嗣，其後不久，病篤命終，所寄之物悉皆散失。財主行還，債索無所。如是癡人，不知籌量可寄不可寄？是故行還，債索無所，以是因緣喪失財寶。世尊！我等聲聞亦復如是，雖聞如來慇懃教誡，不能受持，令法久住，如彼老人受他寄付。我今無智於諸戒律，當何所問？」

佛告諸比丘：「汝等今者，若問於我，則能利益一切眾生。是故告汝，聽隨所疑，恣意而問。」

爾時諸比丘白佛言：「世尊！譬如有人，年二十五，盛壯端正，多有財寶金銀琉璃，父母、妻子、眷屬、宗親，悉皆存在，亦有人來寄其寶物，語其人言：『我有緣事，欲至他處，事訖當還，汝當還我。』是時壯人，守護是物，如自己有。其人遇病，即命家屬：『如是金寶是他所寄，彼若來索悉皆還之。』智者如是，善知籌量，行還索物，皆悉得之，無所亡失。世尊亦爾，若以法寶付囑阿難及諸比丘，不得久住。何以故？一切聲聞及大迦葉悉當無常，如彼老人受他寄物。是故應以無上佛法，付諸菩薩，以諸菩薩善能問答如是法寶，則得久住無量千世，增益熾盛，利安眾生，如彼壯人受他寄物。以是義故，諸大菩薩乃能問耳，我等智慧猶如蚊虻，何能諮請如來深法？」時諸聲聞默然而住。

爾時佛讚諸比丘言：「善哉，善哉！汝等善得無漏之心、阿羅漢心。我亦曾念以此二緣，應以大乘付諸菩薩，令是妙法久住於世。」爾時佛告一切大眾：「善男子、善女人！我之壽命不可稱量，樂說之辯亦不可盡，汝等宜可隨意諮問，若戒、若歸。」第二、第三亦復如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said further to the monks: "If you have any doubt regarding the moral precepts, ask it now, without reserve, and I will explain it, so that your minds may rejoice. I have already trained fully in the empty, quiescent nature of every dharma, and understand it completely — you monks should not think the Tathāgata has cultivated only the empty, quiescent nature of dharmas." He said again to the monks: "If you have any doubt regarding the moral precepts, you may raise it now."

The monks then said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, we lack the wisdom to question the Tathāgata, the Worthy One, the Perfectly Awakened One. Why is this? The Tathāgata's own domain is beyond conceptual thought, every one of his meditative absorptions is beyond conceptual thought, and the teachings he proclaims are beyond conceptual thought — this is why we lack the wisdom to question the Tathāgata. World-Honored One, it is like an old man of a hundred and twenty years, long bedridden with illness, unable to rise or move about, his strength utterly spent, with little life left in him. A wealthy man, about to travel abroad on some errand, entrusts a hundred catties of gold to this old man, saying: 'I am now going abroad; I entrust this treasure to you, and will return for it in ten years, or twenty years — you are to return it to me then.' The old man accepts it. But this old man has no heir, and not long afterward, his illness grows severe and he dies, and the treasure entrusted to him is entirely scattered and lost. When the wealthy man returns and asks for his gold, there is nothing left to be found. Such a foolish man did not know how to judge whether this old man was fit to be entrusted with such a thing — and so, on his return, he found nothing left to reclaim, and through this very cause lost his treasure. World-Honored One, we śrāvakas are just the same: though we have heard the Tathāgata's earnest teaching and admonition, we are unable to uphold it so that the Dharma may long abide in the world, just like that old man who accepted another's treasure. Lacking wisdom regarding the moral precepts as we do, what could we possibly ask?"

The Buddha said to the monks: "If you question me now, you will be able to benefit all beings. This is why I tell you: whatever doubt you have, ask it freely, without reserve."

The monks then said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, it is like a man of twenty-five, in the prime of vigorous, handsome youth, possessed of much wealth — gold, silver, and lapis lazuli — whose parents, wife and children, and whole extended family are all still living. Someone comes to entrust his own treasure to this man, saying: 'I have some business and must go elsewhere; when my affairs are settled I will return, and you are to give it back to me then.' This vigorous young man guards the treasure as though it were his very own. Should he fall ill, he at once instructs his household: 'This gold and treasure was entrusted to me by another — should he come to reclaim it, return it to him in full.' A wise person acts in just this way, judging well, so that when the owner returns to reclaim his property, he receives it all, with nothing lost. The World-Honored One is just the same: if he entrusts the treasure of the Dharma to Ānanda and the other monks, it will not long abide — why is this? Because every śrāvaka, and Mahākāśyapa himself, are all subject to impermanence, just like that old man who accepted another's entrusted treasure. This is why the peerless Buddha-dharma should instead be entrusted to the bodhisattvas — for the bodhisattvas, being skillfully able to question and answer regarding this treasure of the Dharma, will cause it to abide for countless thousands of ages, growing ever more vigorous, bringing benefit and ease to beings, just like that vigorous young man who accepted another's entrusted treasure. For this reason, it is the great bodhisattvas alone who are fit to question you now — our own wisdom is like that of a mosquito or a gnat; how could we possibly inquire into the Tathāgata's profound Dharma?" The śrāvakas then fell silent.

The Buddha then praised the monks, saying: "Excellent, excellent! You have rightly attained the mind free of all outflows, the mind of an arhat. I too have long considered, for these same two reasons, that the Great Vehicle should be entrusted to the bodhisattvas, so that this wondrous Dharma may long abide in the world." The Buddha then said to the whole assembly: "Good sons, good daughters, my lifespan cannot be measured, nor can my eloquence in teaching ever be exhausted — you should now ask freely of whatever you wish, whether it concerns the precepts or the refuges." He said this a second time, and a third time as well.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The two parables the monks offer here — the dying centenarian who loses the entrusted gold, and the vigorous twenty-five-year-old who returns it intact — quietly do more work than a simple compliment to the bodhisattvas' greater capacity. The monks are volunteering their own disqualification: they diagnose themselves, unprompted, as structurally unfit custodians of a teaching meant to outlast them, not because of any personal failing but because of what a śrāvaka's own attainment simply is — a path that ends, for each individual, in that individual's own final nirvāṇa, rather than in centuries of continued teaching activity. This sets up the scene the rest of the fascicle plays out: it is the young bodhisattva Kāśyapa, not one of the elder arhats present, who steps forward next to ask the sūtra's opening chain of questions — a casting choice the monks have just spent a whole parable explaining in advance.

Kāśyapa's Questions

Chinese Source

爾時眾中有一菩薩摩訶薩，本是多羅聚落人也，姓大迦葉，婆羅門種，年在幼稚，以佛神力即從座起，偏袒右臂，遶百千匝，右膝著地，合掌向佛，而白佛言：「世尊！我於今者欲少諮問，若佛聽者，乃敢發言。」佛告迦葉：「如來、應、正遍知，恣汝所問，當為汝說，斷汝所疑，令汝歡喜。」爾時迦葉菩薩摩訶薩白佛言：「世尊！如來哀愍，已垂聽許，今當問之，然我所有智慧，微少猶如蚊虻。如來世尊道德巍巍，純以栴檀，師子難伏，不可壞眾而為眷屬。如來之身，猶真金剛，色如琉璃，真實難壞，復為如是大智慧海之所圍遶。是眾會中，諸大菩薩摩訶薩等，皆悉成就無量無邊深妙功德，猶如香象。於如是等大眾之前，豈敢發問？為當承佛神通之力及因大眾善根威德，少發問耳。」即於佛前說偈問曰：

「云何得長壽，　金剛不壞身？復以何因緣，　得大堅固力？云何於此經，　究竟到彼岸？願佛開微密，　廣為眾生說。云何得廣大，　為眾作依止，實非阿羅漢，　而與羅漢等？云何知天魔，　為眾作留難？佛說波旬說，　云何分別知？云何諸調御，　心喜說真諦，正善具成就，　演說四顛倒？云何作善業？　大仙今當說。云何諸菩薩，　能見難見性？云何解滿字，　及與半字義？云何共聖行，　如娑羅娑鳥，迦隣提日月，　太白與歲星？云何未發心，　而名為菩薩？云何於大眾，　而得無所畏，猶如閻浮金，　無能說其過？云何處濁世，　不污如蓮華？云何處煩惱，　煩惱不能染，如醫療眾病，　不為病所污？生死大海中，　云何作船師？云何捨生死，　如蛇脫故皮？云何觀三寶，　猶如天意樹？三乘若無性，　云何而得說；猶如樂未生，　云何名受樂？云何諸菩薩，　而得不壞眾？云何為生盲，　而作眼目導？云何示多頭？　唯願大仙說。云何說法者，　增長如月初？云何復示現，　究竟於涅槃？云何勇進者，　示人天魔道？云何知法性，　而受於法樂？云何諸菩薩，　遠離一切病？云何為眾生，　演說於祕密？云何說畢竟，　及與不畢竟？如其斷疑網，　云何不定說？云何而得近，　最勝無上道？我今請如來，　為諸菩薩故，願為說甚深，　微妙諸行等。一切諸法中，　悉有安樂性，唯願大仙尊，　為我分別說。眾生大依止，　兩足尊妙藥，今欲問諸陰，　而我無智慧。精進諸菩薩，　亦復不能知，如是等甚深，　諸佛之境界。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time there was, among the assembly, a bodhisattva-mahāsattva, originally from the village of Tāla, of the brahmin clan, surnamed Mahākāśyapa, still young in years, who, by the Buddha's own spiritual power, rose from his seat, bared his right arm, circled the Buddha a hundred thousand times, set his right knee upon the ground, joined his palms toward the Buddha, and said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I now wish to ask a small question; if the Buddha permits it, I will venture to speak." The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "The Tathāgata, the Worthy One, the Perfectly Awakened One, grants you leave to ask whatever you wish; I will explain it for you, resolve your doubt, and bring joy to your heart." The bodhisattva-mahāsattva Kāśyapa then said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, since the Tathāgata, out of compassion, has already granted me leave, I shall now ask my question — though my own wisdom is as slight as a mosquito's or a gnat's. The World-Honored One's own virtue towers like a mountain of pure sandalwood, a lion hard to subdue, surrounded by an unbreakable assembly as his own retinue. The Tathāgata's own body is like true vajra, its color like lapis lazuli, genuinely indestructible, further encircled by an ocean of great wisdom such as this. Among this very assembly, the great bodhisattva-mahāsattvas have each accomplished immeasurable, boundless, profound and wondrous virtues, like fragrant elephants. Before such a great assembly as this, how could I dare to speak? It is only by relying on the Buddha's own spiritual power, and on the roots of goodness and majestic virtue of this whole assembly, that I venture to raise this small question." He then spoke this verse in question before the Buddha:

"How does one attain long life,
a body of vajra, indestructible?
By what further cause and condition
does one gain great, unshakable strength?

How does one, regarding this very sūtra,
reach all the way to the far shore?
I beg the Buddha to open what is subtle and hidden,
and expound it fully for the sake of beings.

How does one become vast enough
to be a support for the whole assembly —
not truly an arhat,
and yet equal to one?

How does one recognize the heavenly māra
when he sets obstacles before the assembly?
When the Buddha speaks, and when Pāpīyas speaks,
how does one tell the two apart?

How does every tamer of beings
proclaim the ultimate truth with a joyful heart,
rightly and fully accomplished,
expounding the four inversions?

How does one perform wholesome deeds?
I beg the great sage to speak of this now.

How do the bodhisattvas
come to see a nature so hard to see?

How does one understand the meaning
of the full letters, and of the partial letters?

How does one share in the noble conduct
of the sārasa bird,
of the kalandaka bird, of the sun and moon,
of Venus and of Jupiter?

How can one not yet have given rise to the resolve
and still be called a bodhisattva?

How does one, before the whole assembly,
attain fearlessness,
like gold of the Jambū river,
whose flaws no one can name?

How does one dwell in this turbid world
unstained, like a lotus flower?

How does one dwell amid affliction
untouched by that very affliction,
like a physician who treats every illness
without being tainted by any of them?

In the great ocean of birth and death,
how does one become its ferryman?

How does one cast off birth and death,
as a snake sheds its old skin?

How does one regard the Three Jewels
as though they were the wish-fulfilling tree of the gods?

If the three vehicles have no fixed nature of their own,
how can they still be taught?
It is like bliss not yet arisen —
how can it then be called something received?

How do the bodhisattvas
come to hold an unbreakable assembly?

How does one become an eye and a guide
for those born blind?

How does one show many heads?
I beg only that the great sage speak of this.

How does a teacher of the Dharma
grow like the moon in its first days?

How does one further manifest
the ultimate reach of nirvāṇa?

How does one who presses courageously forward
show others the path of gods and of māras alike?

How does one know the Dharma's own nature,
and thereby receive the joy of the Dharma?

How do the bodhisattvas
keep themselves far from every illness?

How does one, for the sake of beings,
proclaim what is secret and hidden?

How does one speak of what is final,
and of what is not yet final?

If one is to cut through the net of doubt,
how can one still speak without settling the matter?

How does one draw near
to the most excellent, peerless path?

I now ask this of the Tathāgata,
for the sake of the bodhisattvas —
I beg you to speak of the profound
and subtle practices, and all that is like them.

Within every dharma
there lies a nature of ease and joy —
I beg only the great sage, the honored one,
to distinguish and explain this for me.

The great support of all beings,
the wondrous medicine, most honored among the two-legged —
I now wish to ask about the aggregates,
though I myself have no wisdom for it.

Not even the most diligent bodhisattvas
are able to fully know
such profound matters as these,
the very domain of the buddhas themselves."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this long chain of questions as setting the entire agenda for the sūtra's central doctrinal core, not as a loose miscellany — he notes explicitly that the Buddha's own reply calls it equal in depth to a question bodhisattvas asked him at the very moment of his awakening under the Bodhi tree, and treats that comparison as license to expect the fascicles ahead to answer these questions systematically rather than in passing. He flags one recurring feature of Kāśyapa's phrasing worth watching for across the rest of the sūtra: several questions ask how one can be something "not truly" one thing while still functioning as it — "not truly an arhat, and yet equal to one," bliss "not yet arisen" yet somehow already received, a bodhisattva before the resolve to become one has even been made. Huiyuan treats these as genuine paradoxes the text intends to resolve rather than loose rhetorical flourishes, each one pointing toward a later passage where the sūtra will explain precisely how a designation can properly apply before, or independently of, the condition that would ordinarily be required to earn it.

Equal Regard, Even for Those Who Break the Precepts

Chinese Source

爾時佛讚迦葉菩薩：「善哉，善哉！善男子！汝今未得一切種智，我已得之。然汝所問甚深密藏，如一切智之所諮問等無有異。……」爾時佛告迦葉菩薩：「善男子！諦聽，諦聽，當為汝說如來所得長壽之業。……善男子！譬如王子，犯罪繫獄，王甚憐愍，愛念子故，躬自迴駕至其繫所。菩薩亦爾，欲得長壽，應當護念一切眾生同於子想，生大慈大悲、大喜大捨，授不殺戒，教修善法。亦當安止一切眾生於五戒十善，復入地獄、餓鬼、畜生、阿修羅等一切諸趣，拔濟是中苦惱眾生，脫未脫者，度未度者，未涅槃者令得涅槃，安慰一切諸恐怖者，以如是等業因緣故，菩薩則得壽命長遠，於諸智慧而得自在，隨所壽終生於天上。」

爾時迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！菩薩摩訶薩等視眾生，同於子想，是義深隱，我未能解。世尊！如來不應說言菩薩於諸眾生修平等心，同於子想。所以者何？於佛法中有破戒者、作逆罪者、毀正法者，云何當於如是等人同子想耶？」佛告迦葉：「如是，如是，我於眾生實作子想，如羅睺羅。」

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！昔十五日僧布薩時，曾於受具清淨眾中，有一童子，不善修習身、口、意業，在屏隈處盜聽說戒。密迹力士承佛神力，以金剛杵碎之如塵。世尊！是金剛神，極成暴惡，乃能斷是童子命根。云何如來視諸眾生同於子想，如羅睺羅？」

佛告迦葉：「汝今不應作如是言，是童子者，即是化人，非真實也，為欲驅遣破戒毀法令出眾故；金剛密迹亦是化耳。迦葉！毀謗正法及一闡提，或有殺生乃至邪見及故犯禁，我於是等，悉生悲心，同於子想，如羅睺羅。善男子！譬如國王，諸群臣等有犯王法，隨罪誅戮而不捨置。如來世尊不如是也，於毀法者，與驅遣羯磨、訶責羯磨、置羯磨、舉罪羯磨、不可見羯磨、滅羯磨、未捨惡見羯磨。善男子！如來所以與謗法者作如是等降伏羯磨，為欲示諸行惡之人有果報故。……善男子，未可見法，汝欲見者，今當為汝說其相貌。我涅槃已，隨其方面，有持戒比丘，威儀具足，護持正法，見壞法者，即能驅遣，呵責徵治，當知是人，得福無量不可稱計。……」

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！如佛所言，則不等視一切眾生同於子想，如羅睺羅。世尊！若有一人以刀害佛，復有一人持栴檀塗佛，佛於此二若生等心，云何復言，當治毀禁？若治毀禁，是言則失。」

佛告迦葉菩薩：「善男子！譬如國王、大臣、宰相產育諸子，顏貌端正，聰明黠慧，若二、三、四，將付嚴師，而作是言：『君可為我教詔諸子，威儀、禮節、伎藝、書䟽、校計、算數，悉令成就。我今四子，就君受學，假使三子病杖而死，餘有一子必當苦治，要令成就。雖喪三子，我終不恨。』迦葉！是父及師得殺罪不？」「不也。世尊！何以故？以愛念故，為欲成就，無有惡心，如是教誨，得福無量。」「善男子！如來亦爾，視壞法者等如一子。如來今以無上正法，付囑諸王、大臣、宰相、比丘、比丘尼、優婆塞、優婆夷。是諸國王及四部眾，應當勸勵諸學人等，令得增上戒定智慧。若有不學是三品法，懈怠、破戒，毀正法者，王者、大臣、四部之眾應當苦治。善男子！是諸國王及四部眾，當有罪不？」「不也。世尊！」「善男子！是諸國王及四部眾尚無有罪，何況如來。善男子！如來善修如是平等，於諸眾生同一子想。如是修者，是名菩薩修平等心，於諸眾生同一子想。」

The Buddha then praised the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Excellent, excellent, good son! You have not yet attained the knowledge of all modes, which I have already attained; yet the profound, secret treasury you have asked about is no different from what one possessing the knowledge of all modes would ask…" The Buddha then said to the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Good son, listen closely, listen closely; I shall now explain to you the karma by which the Tathāgata attained long life… Good son, it is like a prince who, having committed a crime, is bound in prison; the king, out of deep pity and love for his son, personally turns his own carriage and goes to where the prince is imprisoned. A bodhisattva is just the same: wishing to attain long life, he should hold every being in mind, regarding them as his own children, giving rise to great loving-kindness, great compassion, great joy, and great equanimity, bestowing the precept against killing, and teaching the cultivation of wholesome dharmas. He should also settle every being in the five precepts and the ten wholesome deeds, and further enter hell, the realm of hungry ghosts, the animal realm, and the realm of asuras and every other destiny, rescuing the suffering beings found there, freeing those not yet freed, bringing across those not yet brought across, and causing those not yet in nirvāṇa to attain it, comforting all who are afraid. By karmic causes and conditions of this kind, a bodhisattva attains a long and far-reaching life, gains mastery over every kind of wisdom, and, when his life at last comes to its end, is reborn among the gods."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa then said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the meaning of a bodhisattva-mahāsattva regarding beings equally, as his own children, is profound and hidden, and I have not yet been able to grasp it. World-Honored One, the Tathāgata should not say that a bodhisattva cultivates an equal mind toward all beings, regarding them as his own children. Why is this? Within the Buddha's own teaching there are those who break the precepts, those who commit the gravest transgressions, and those who slander the true Dharma — how could one regard such people equally, as one's own children?" The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "It is so, it is so — I truly regard beings as my own children, just as I regard Rāhula."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, once, at a fortnightly poṣadha assembly, among the pure assembly of fully ordained monks, there was a young novice who had not cultivated wholesome conduct of body, speech, or mind, and who hid himself away to eavesdrop on the recitation of the precepts. The vajra-wielding spirit Guhyapāda, sustained by the Buddha's own spiritual power, crushed him to dust with his vajra mace. World-Honored One, this vajra spirit acted with the utmost violence, taking the very life of this young novice — how, then, can the Tathāgata be said to regard every being equally, as his own children, as he regards Rāhula?"

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "You should not speak this way. That young novice was himself a manifested apparition, not a real person, conjured precisely in order to drive out and expel those who break the precepts and destroy the Dharma from the assembly; the vajra spirit Guhyapāda was likewise a manifested apparition. Kāśyapa, toward those who slander the true Dharma, toward the icchantika, toward those who kill living beings or hold wrong views or deliberately violate the precepts, I give rise, in every case, to a mind of compassion, regarding them equally as my own children, as I regard Rāhula. Good son, it is like a king whose various ministers, having broken the king's own law, are put to death according to their crime, without exception. The Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, is not like this: toward those who destroy the Dharma, he applies instead the acts of expulsion, of reproach, of probation, of public censure, of suspension pending acknowledgment, of suspension pending repentance, and of suspension for an unrelinquished wrong view. Good son, the reason the Tathāgata applies acts of subduing such as these to those who slander the Dharma is to show that every evildoer receives a due result… Good son, as for the unmanifest Dharma you wish to see, I shall now describe its form for you. After my parinirvāṇa, wherever there is a precept-keeping monk, complete in his bearing, upholding the true Dharma, who, on seeing one who destroys the Dharma, is able to drive that person out, reproaching and disciplining them — know that such a person gains merit beyond measure, beyond all reckoning…"

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has just said, he does not, after all, regard every being equally, as his own children, as he regards Rāhula. World-Honored One, if one person were to strike the Buddha with a knife, and another were to anoint the Buddha with sandalwood, and the Buddha held an equal mind toward these two, how could it then also be said that those who break the precepts should be disciplined? If those who break the precepts are to be disciplined, then that earlier statement no longer holds."

The Buddha said to the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Good son, it is like a king, a great minister, or a chancellor who raises several sons, handsome and intelligent — say two, three, or four — and entrusts them to a strict teacher, saying: 'Please teach my sons for me — their bearing, their manners, their skills, their reading and writing, their reckoning and mathematics — see that they master it all. I now give you these four sons to instruct; even should three of them die from the discipline of the rod, the one remaining son must still be disciplined thoroughly, so that he is made to succeed. Though I lose three sons, I will hold no resentment for it.' Kāśyapa, would this father, or this teacher, be guilty of murder?" "No, World-Honored One. Why is this? Because it is done out of love, for the sake of the sons' own success, with no malicious intent at all — such instruction, given this way, gains merit beyond measure." "Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same: he regards those who destroy the Dharma equally, as his own single child. The Tathāgata has now entrusted the peerless true Dharma to kings, great ministers, chancellors, monks, nuns, upāsakas, and upāsikās. These kings and the fourfold assembly should encourage and exhort all who are training in the Dharma to attain the higher precepts, concentration, and wisdom. If there are those who fail to train in these three, who grow heedless, break the precepts, or destroy the true Dharma, the kings, ministers, and the fourfold assembly should discipline them severely. Good son, would these kings and this fourfold assembly then be guilty of any wrong?" "No, World-Honored One." "Good son, if these kings and this fourfold assembly are not guilty of any wrong, how much less the Tathāgata. Good son, the Tathāgata has skillfully cultivated just this kind of equality, regarding every being as his own single child. To cultivate in this way is what is called a bodhisattva's cultivation of an equal mind, regarding every being as his own single child."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Guhyapāda episode as resolved entirely by a single move: both the offending novice and the vajra spirit who kills him are declared, retroactively, to have been manifested apparitions rather than real beings — a device, Huiyuan notes, aimed specifically at driving those who break the precepts and destroy the Dharma out of the assembly, not a report of an actual killing the Buddha's equal regard would otherwise have to explain away. He then reads the king's-sons parable as doing the harder remaining work: explaining why real, non-apparitional discipline of real wrongdoers is still compatible with equal regard. His gloss is precise about what changes and what doesn't: love for the child is constant across every case — the father grieves for all four sons alike — but love expressed as protection from consequence and love expressed as correction through discipline are not the same expression of the same love, and choosing the harsher one for a given case is not evidence the love was ever unequal. Huiyuan extends this reading immediately to the sūtra's own list of seven graduated disciplinary acts (from simple expulsion up through suspension for an unrelinquished wrong view), reading their calibrated severity as itself proof of equal regard rather than its absence — a king who punished every offense identically, with no proportion to the offense at all, would be the one actually failing to treat his subjects as individuals worth correcting rather than merely eliminating.

The Ocean of the Tathāgata's Lifespan

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！如佛所說，菩薩若有修平等心，視諸眾生同於子想，得壽命長。如來不應作如是說。何以故？如知法人，能說種種孝順之法，還至家中，以諸瓦石打擲父母。而是父母是良福田，多所利益，難遭難遇，應好供養，反生惱害，是知法人言行相違。如來所言，亦復如是，菩薩修習等心，眾生同子想者，應得長壽，善知宿命，常住於世，無有變易。今者世尊，以何因緣，壽命極短，同人間耶？如來將無於諸眾生生怨憎想？世尊昔日作何惡業，斷幾命根，得是短壽不滿百年？」

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught, a bodhisattva who cultivates an equal mind, regarding beings as his own children, attains a long life — but the Tathāgata should not speak this way. Why is this? It is like a person learned in the teachings on filial devotion, able to expound every kind of teaching on treating one's parents well, who then returns home and strikes his own parents with tiles and stones. His parents are a true field of merit, a source of great benefit, rare and hard to encounter, and should be well cared for — yet instead he brings them harm; such a learned person's words and deeds are at odds with each other. What the Tathāgata has said is just the same: if a bodhisattva who cultivates an equal mind, regarding beings as his own children, should attain long life, understand his own past lives well, and abide constantly in the world without change — then why is it, World-Honored One, that you yourself have so short a lifespan, no different from an ordinary human? Could it be that the Tathāgata harbors some resentment toward beings after all? What evil deed did the World-Honored One once commit, and how many lives did he take, to deserve so short a life, not even a hundred years?"

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "Good son, for what reason do you now speak such coarse words before the Tathāgata? The Tathāgata's own life is, among every kind of life, the very highest and most excellent; the permanence he has attained is, among every kind of permanence, the very foremost."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, how, then, does the Tathāgata attain a long life?"

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "Good son, it is like the eight great rivers — the Ganges, the Yamunā, the Sarasvatī, the Ajitavatī, the Mahī, the Sindhu, the Vakṣu, and the Sītā — these eight great rivers, together with every lesser river, all flow into the great ocean. Kāśyapa, in just this way, the great river of every lifespan — among humans, among gods, on the earth, and through open space — flows entirely into the ocean of the Tathāgata's own life; this is why the Tathāgata's life is immeasurable. Furthermore, Kāśyapa, it is like Lake Anavatapta, from which four great rivers flow forth; the Tathāgata is just the same, the source from which every life flows forth. Kāśyapa, it is like how, among every permanent thing, space itself is foremost; the Tathāgata is just the same, foremost among every kind of permanence. Kāśyapa, it is like how, among every medicine, ghee is foremost; the Tathāgata is just the same, foremost in lifespan among all beings."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if the Tathāgata's life is truly such as this, he should remain for a kalpa, or at least for less than a kalpa, forever proclaiming the wondrous Dharma, like a great rain pouring down."

"Kāśyapa, you should not now give rise to a notion of the Tathāgata's utter extinction. Kāśyapa, if even a monk, a nun, an upāsaka, an upāsikā, or even a heterodox practitioner who has attained the five superknowledges and mastery over them, can, remaining for a kalpa or less than a kalpa, walk through open space, sit and lie down at will, send forth fire from the left side of his body and water from the right, his whole body streaming with smoke and flame like a mass of fire, and, wishing to prolong his life, do so exactly as he wishes, lengthening or shortening his own lifespan at will — if such a person, through these five superknowledges alone, can gain spiritual power of this kind, exercised entirely at will, how much more the Tathāgata, who has gained mastery over every dharma — how could he possibly be unable to sustain his life for half a kalpa, or a whole kalpa, or a hundred kalpas, or a hundred thousand kalpas, or immeasurable kalpas? For this reason, you should know that the Tathāgata is the permanent Dharma, the unchanging Dharma. This body of the Tathāgata's is a manifested, transformation body, not a body that eats mixed food; for the sake of bringing beings across, he shows himself as though subject to the same tree of poison as they are, and for this reason manifests the appearance of relinquishing it and entering parinirvāṇa. Kāśyapa, you should know that the Buddha is the permanent Dharma, the unchanging Dharma. In this ultimate truth, you should all strive diligently, cultivating it single-mindedly, and, having cultivated it, expound it widely for the sake of others."

The Parable of the Stolen Milk

Chinese Source

爾時迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！出世之法與世間法，有何差別？如佛言曰：『佛是常法、不變易法。』世間亦說：『梵天是常，自在天常，無有變易。我常、性常、微塵亦常。』若言如來是常法者，如來何故不常現耶？若不常現，有何差別？何以故？梵天乃至微塵、世性亦不現故。」

佛告迦葉：「譬如長者，多有諸牛，色雖種種，同共一群，付放牧人，令逐水草，但為醍醐，不求乳酪。彼牧牛者，搆已自食。長者命終，所有諸牛悉為群賊之所抄掠。賊得牛已，無有婦女。即自搆捋，得已而食。爾時群賊各相謂言：『彼大長者，畜養此牛，不期乳酪，但為醍醐。我等今者，當設何方而得之耶？夫醍醐者，名為世間第一上味。我等無器，設使得乳，無安置處。』復共相謂：『唯有皮囊，可以盛之。』雖有盛處，不知攢搖，漿猶難得，況復生酥。爾時諸賊，以醍醐故，加之以水，以水多故，乳酪醍醐，一切俱失。凡夫亦爾，雖有善法，皆是如來正法之餘。何以故？如來世尊入涅槃後，盜竊如來遺餘善法，若戒、定、慧，如彼諸賊劫掠群牛。諸凡夫人，雖復得是戒、定、智慧，無有方便，不能解說。以是義故，不能獲得常戒、常定、常慧、解脫；如彼群賊，不知方便，喪失醍醐。亦如群賊，為醍醐故，加之以水。凡夫亦爾，為解脫故，說我、眾生、壽命、士夫、梵天、自在天、微塵世性、戒定智慧及與解脫，非想非非想天即是涅槃，實亦不得解脫涅槃，如彼群賊不得醍醐。是諸凡夫，有少梵行，供養父母，以是因緣，得生天上，受少安樂，如彼群賊加水之乳。而是凡夫，實不知因修少梵行，供養父母，得生天上。又不能知戒、定、智慧，歸依三寶，以不知故，說常、樂、我、淨，雖復說之，而實不知。是故如來出世之後，乃為演說常、樂、我、淨。如轉輪王出現於世，福德力故，群賊退散，牛無損命。時轉輪王，即以諸牛付一牧人，多巧便者。是人方便，即得醍醐。以醍醐故，一切眾生，無有患苦。法輪聖王出現世時，諸凡夫人不能演說戒定慧者，即便退散，如賊退散。爾時如來善說世法及出世法，為眾生故，令諸菩薩隨而演說。菩薩摩訶薩既得醍醐，復令無量無邊眾生獲得無上甘露法味，所謂如來常、樂、我、淨。以是義故，善男子！如來是常、不變易法。非如世間、凡夫、愚人，謂梵天等是常法也。此常法稱要是如來，非是餘法。迦葉！應當如是知如來身。迦葉！諸善男子、善女人，常當繫心修此二字，佛是常住。迦葉！若有善男子、善女人修此二字，當知是人，隨我所行，至我至處。善男子！若有修習如是二字為滅相者，當知如來則於其人為般涅槃。善男子！涅槃義者，即是諸佛之法性也。」

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！佛法性者，其義云何？世尊！我今欲知法性之義，唯願如來哀愍廣說。夫法性者即是捨身，捨身者名無所有，若無所有，身云何存？身若存者，云何而言身有法性？身有法性云何得存？我今云何當知是義？」

佛告迦葉菩薩：「善男子！汝今不應作如是說，滅是法性。夫法性者，無有滅也。善男子！譬如無想天，成就色陰而無色想；不應問言，是諸天等，云何而住？歡娛受樂？云何行想？云何見聞？善男子，如來境界，非諸聲聞緣覺所知。善男子，不應說言，如來身者是滅法也。善男子，如是滅法是佛境界，非諸聲聞緣覺所及。善男子，汝今不應思量如來何處住？何處行？何處見？何處樂？善男子，如是之義，亦非汝等之所知及，諸佛法身，種種方便，不可思議。」

「復次善男子！應當修習佛法及僧而作常想。是三法者，無有異想、無無常想、無變異想。若於三法修異想者，當知是輩，清淨三歸則無依處，所有禁戒皆不具足，終不能證聲聞緣覺菩提之果。若能於是不可思議，修常想者，則有歸處。善男子！譬如因樹，則有樹影。如來亦爾，有常法故，則有歸依，非是無常。若言如來是無常者，如來則非諸天、世人所歸依處。」

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！譬如闇中，有樹無影。」「迦葉！汝不應言有樹無影，但非肉眼之所見耳。善男子！如來亦爾，其性常住，是不變異。無智慧眼，不能得見，如彼闇中，不見樹影。凡夫之人，於佛滅後，說言如來是無常法，亦復如是。若言如來異法、僧者，則不能成三歸依處，如汝父母各各異故，故使無常。」

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！我從今始，當以佛、法、眾僧三事常住，啟悟父母乃至七世，皆令奉持。甚奇，世尊！我今當學如來、法、僧不可思議，既自學已，亦當為人廣說是義。若有諸人不能信受，當知是輩久修無常，如是之人，我當為其而作霜雹。」爾時佛讚迦葉菩薩：「善哉，善哉！汝今善能護持正法，如是護法，不欺於人。以不欺人善業緣故，而得長壽，善知宿命。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what difference is there between what transcends the world and what belongs to the world? The Buddha has said: 'The Buddha is the permanent Dharma, the unchanging Dharma.' Yet the world also says: 'Brahmā is permanent; Maheśvara is permanent, without change; the self is permanent, own-nature is permanent, and even the atom is permanent.' If it is said that the Tathāgata is the permanent Dharma, then why does the Tathāgata not always manifest himself? And if he does not always manifest himself, what difference is there at all — since Brahmā, and the atom, and own-nature likewise do not always manifest themselves?"

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "It is like an elder who owns many cattle, of every color, kept together in a single herd, entrusted to a herdsman to graze on water and grass, kept only for the sake of their ghee, not for milk or curds. This herdsman milks them and eats the milk himself. When the elder dies, all his cattle are seized and carried off by a band of robbers. Once the robbers have the cattle, having no women among them, they milk the cows themselves, and eat what they get. The robbers then say to one another: 'That great elder kept these cattle not for milk or curds, but for ghee alone. What method should we now devise to obtain it? Ghee is said to be the finest flavor in the whole world.' But they say further: 'We have no proper vessel — even if we obtain milk, we have nowhere to put it.' They say again: 'We have only leather sacks; we could hold it in those.' Though they now have something to hold it in, they do not know how to churn it, and even curdled milk is hard for them to produce, let alone fresh butter. The robbers, in their eagerness for ghee, then add water to the milk, and because of the excess water, they lose the milk, the curds, and the ghee all together. Ordinary people are just the same: whatever wholesome dharmas they possess are all merely leftover fragments of the Tathāgata's own true Dharma. Why is this? Because after the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, enters parinirvāṇa, they steal the wholesome dharmas he left behind — precepts, concentration, and wisdom — just as those robbers plundered the herd of cattle. Though ordinary people obtain these — precepts, concentration, and wisdom — they have no skillful means, and cannot properly explain them. For this reason they are unable to attain permanent precepts, permanent concentration, permanent wisdom, or liberation — just as those robbers, lacking the proper method, lost the ghee. And just as those robbers, in their eagerness for ghee, added water to the milk, ordinary people are just the same: in their eagerness for liberation, they speak of a self, of a sentient being, of a life-force, of a person, of Brahmā, of Maheśvara, of the atom, of own-nature, of precepts, concentration, and wisdom, and of liberation, and say that the heaven of neither-perception-nor-non-perception is itself nirvāṇa — yet in truth they attain no liberation, no nirvāṇa at all, just as those robbers never obtained the ghee. These ordinary people, having practiced a little pure conduct and made some offering to their parents, are, through this cause, reborn among the gods, and receive a small measure of ease — just like the milk those robbers diluted with water. Yet these ordinary people do not truly understand that it was through cultivating a little pure conduct and making offering to their parents that they were reborn among the gods; nor are they able to truly understand precepts, concentration, and wisdom, or to take refuge in the Three Jewels. Because they do not understand this, when they speak of permanence, bliss, self, and purity, though they speak these very words, they do not truly understand them. This is why, only after the Tathāgata appears in the world, are permanence, bliss, self, and purity finally taught in their true meaning. It is like when a wheel-turning king appears in the world: by the power of his own merit, the bands of robbers scatter and flee, and not one of the cattle is harmed. The wheel-turning king then entrusts the cattle to a single herdsman, one skilled in method, and this person, through skillful means, obtains the ghee at once — and because of this ghee, every being is freed from affliction. In just this way, when the Dharma-wheel-turning sage-king appears in the world, those ordinary people unable to properly explain precepts, concentration, and wisdom scatter and flee, just as the robbers scattered. The Tathāgata then skillfully teaches both worldly and transcendent dharmas, and, for the sake of beings, causes the bodhisattvas to expound them in turn. The bodhisattva-mahāsattvas, having obtained the ghee themselves, then cause countless, boundless beings to obtain the peerless flavor of the nectar of the Dharma — namely, the Tathāgata's own permanence, bliss, self, and purity. For this reason, good son, the Tathāgata is the permanent, unchanging Dharma — not at all like what the foolish and ignorant people of the world call permanent, such as Brahmā and the rest. This permanent Dharma is properly and exclusively the Tathāgata, and no other dharma. Kāśyapa, you should understand the Tathāgata's body in just this way. Kāśyapa, every good son and good daughter should constantly fix their minds on cultivating these two words: the Buddha abides permanently. Kāśyapa, if any good son or good daughter cultivates these two words, know that such a person follows in my own conduct, and arrives where I myself arrive. Good son, if one cultivates these two words while still holding a notion of their eventual extinction, know that the Tathāgata will, for that very person, appear to enter parinirvāṇa. Good son, the meaning of nirvāṇa is precisely the Dharma-nature of every buddha."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what is the meaning of the Buddha's own Dharma-nature? World-Honored One, I now wish to know the meaning of Dharma-nature; I beg only that the Tathāgata, out of compassion, explain it fully. If Dharma-nature is itself the relinquishing of the body, and relinquishing the body means having nothing at all — then if there is nothing at all, how can the body still exist? And if the body still exists, how can it then be said that the body has a Dharma-nature? And if the body has a Dharma-nature, how can it come to exist at all? How am I now to understand this meaning?"

The Buddha said to the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Good son, you should not now speak of this Dharma-nature as something extinguished. Dharma-nature has no extinction at all. Good son, it is like the heaven of no-perception, where beings accomplish the aggregate of form but are without any perception of form — one should not ask how such gods abide there, how they experience joy and pleasure, how they engage in mental formations, or how they see and hear. Good son, the Tathāgata's own domain is not something known by śrāvakas or pratyekabuddhas. Good son, one should not say that the Tathāgata's body is a dharma subject to extinction. Good son, this very matter of extinction is itself the Buddha's own domain, beyond the reach of any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha. Good son, you should not now try to reckon where the Tathāgata abides, where he moves, where he sees, or where he finds joy. Good son, such a matter as this is likewise beyond what you yourselves are able to know — the Dharma-body of every buddha, and its every kind of skillful means, is beyond conceptual thought."

"Furthermore, good son, you should cultivate the perception of permanence with regard to the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha. These three things should never be given a perception of difference, a perception of impermanence, or a perception of change. Whoever cultivates a perception of difference regarding these three things should be understood to have, for the pure threefold refuge, no true foundation at all; their moral precepts will remain incomplete in every case, and they will never be able to realize the fruit of awakening, whether of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha. But whoever is able to cultivate the perception of permanence with regard to this inconceivable matter will have a true foundation. Good son, it is like how, because of a tree, there is a tree's shadow; the Tathāgata is just the same: because there is the permanent Dharma, there is a refuge to be taken — it is not something impermanent. If one says the Tathāgata is impermanent, then the Tathāgata could not be a refuge for any god or any person in the world."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, it is like a tree in darkness, which casts no shadow." "Kāśyapa, you should not say the tree casts no shadow — it is only that the shadow cannot be seen by the fleshly eye. Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same: his nature abides permanently, and is without change; it simply cannot be seen without the eye of wisdom, just as a tree's shadow cannot be seen in the dark. Ordinary people, after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, who say the Tathāgata is an impermanent thing, are doing just the same. If one says the Tathāgata is different from the Dharma and the Saṅgha, then the threefold refuge cannot be established at all — it would be like saying your own parents are each entirely different people, and so must be impermanent in relation to one another."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, from this day forward I shall take the permanent abiding of these three things — the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha — and awaken my own parents, and their ancestors down through seven generations, so that all of them may uphold this teaching. How wondrous, World-Honored One! I shall now study this inconceivable matter of the Tathāgata, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, and, having studied it myself, shall expound this teaching widely to others as well. Should there be any who cannot believe and accept it, know that such people have long cultivated the notion of impermanence — for such people, I myself will become as frost and hail." The Buddha then praised the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Excellent, excellent! You are now well able to protect and uphold the true Dharma; protecting the Dharma in this way, you deceive no one. Through the wholesome karma of deceiving no one, you shall attain long life, and understand your own past lives well."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan works through the stolen-cattle parable's seven distinct beats — the elder's original herd kept only for ghee, the herdsman's competent milking, the robbers' theft, their possession of the cattle without knowing how to churn the milk, their attempt anyway, the dilution with water, and the resulting loss of milk, curds, and ghee all at once — mapping each onto a specific claim about why the world's own philosophical schools fail to reach genuine permanence even when they use the same words this sūtra uses. The robbers are not without genuine milk in their hands; ordinary people, on Huiyuan's reading, likewise are not without genuine wholesome dharmas — the precepts, concentration, and wisdom that survive as fragments of the Buddha's own teaching after his parinirvāṇa. Their failure is specifically a failure of method: lacking the skillful means to process what they hold, they dilute it with speculative additions — a permanent Brahmā, a permanent self, a permanent atom — that seem to strengthen the claim to permanence but in fact destroy the very thing they were trying to preserve, exactly as adding water to milk one cannot properly churn ruins the milk, the curds, and the ghee together rather than salvaging any of them. The point of contrast is not that non-Buddhist claims to permanence are simply false where the Buddha's is simply true, but that unprocessed philosophical borrowing of a genuine partial truth, without the "skillful means" (方便) proper cultivation supplies, actively spoils what it touches — a warning Huiyuan clearly intends to apply as much to careless readers of this very sūtra as to the outside schools the parable is nominally about.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan cautions against trying to parse this long chain of negations clause by clause, as though each individual "neither X nor not-X" pinpointed a separate, isolable feature of the Tathāgata's body. He reads the whole passage as making one sustained point through relentless repetition of a single grammatical pattern: every ordinary category available to describe anything at all — form, cause, duration, place, number, sameness and difference, presence and absence — is being tried against the Tathāgata's body in turn and found equally unable to capture it, precisely because that body is not one more item classifiable within any of those categories, but their common ground. He singles out the passage's own closing admission — that this is "the single mark of the Dharma" beyond counting or numbering, which does not truly enter parinirvāṇa even in the act of entering it — as the passage's real payload: everything preceding it is negative preparation, clearing away every available misconception, so that this one positive (if still paradoxical) claim can land without being reabsorbed into any of the categories just ruled out.

佛告迦葉：「莫作是語，言禿居士。……善男子！過去之世無量無邊阿僧祇劫，於此拘尸那城，有佛出世，號歡喜增益如來、應、正遍知……彼佛世尊住世無量，化眾生已，然後乃於娑羅雙樹入般涅槃。佛涅槃後，正法住世無量億歲，餘四十年佛法未滅。爾時有一持戒比丘，名曰覺德，多有徒眾、眷屬圍遶，能師子吼，頒宣廣說九部經典，制諸比丘不得畜養奴婢、牛羊、非法之物。爾時多有破戒比丘，聞作是說，皆生惡心，執持刀杖，逼是法師。是時國王，名曰有德，聞是事已，為護法故，即便往至說法者所，與是破戒諸惡比丘極共戰鬪，令說法者得免危害。王於爾時，身被刀劍、箭槊之瘡，體無完處如芥子許。爾時覺德尋讚王言：『善哉，善哉！王今真是護正法者，當來之世，此身當為無量法器。』王於是時，得聞法已，心大歡喜，尋即命終，生阿閦佛國，而為彼佛作第一弟子。其王將從人民眷屬，有戰鬪者、有隨喜者，一切不退菩提之心，命終悉生阿閦佛國。覺德比丘却後壽終，亦得往生阿閦佛國，而為彼佛作聲聞眾中第二弟子。若有正法欲滅盡時，應當如是受持擁護。迦葉！爾時王者，則我身是；說法比丘，迦葉佛是。迦葉！護正法者，得如是等無量果報。以是因緣，我於今日得種種相，以自莊嚴，成就法身、不可壞身。」

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！如來常身，猶如畫石。」

佛告迦葉菩薩：「善男子！以是因緣故，比丘、比丘尼、優婆塞、優婆夷，應當勤加護持正法，護法果報廣大無量。善男子！是故護法優婆塞等，應執刀杖，擁護如是持法比丘。若有受持五戒之者，不得名為大乘人也；不受五戒為護正法，乃名大乘。護正法者，應當執持刀劍、器仗，侍說法者。」

迦葉白佛言：「世尊！若諸比丘，與如是等諸優婆塞持刀杖者共為伴侶，為有師耶？為無師乎？為是持戒？為是破戒？」

佛告迦葉：「莫謂是等為破戒人。善男子！我涅槃後，濁惡之世國土荒亂，互相抄掠，人民飢餓。爾時多有為飢餓故發心出家，如是之人名為禿人。是禿人輩，見有持戒、威儀具足、清淨比丘護持正法，驅逐令出，若殺若害。……善男子！是故我今聽持戒人，依諸白衣持刀杖者，以為伴侶。若諸國王、大臣長者、優婆塞等，為護法故，雖持刀杖，我說是等名為持戒。雖持刀杖，不應斷命。若能如是，即得名為第一持戒。」

「迦葉！言護法者，謂具正見，能廣宣說大乘經典，終不捉持王者寶蓋、油瓶穀米、種種果蓏，不為利養親近國王、大臣長者，於諸檀越，心無諂曲，具足威儀，摧伏破戒諸惡人等，是名持戒護法之師，能為眾生真善知識，其心弘廣譬如大海。迦葉！若有比丘，以利養故為他說法，是人所有徒眾眷屬，亦効是師貪求利養，是人如是，便自壞眾。迦葉！眾有三種：一者犯戒雜僧，二者愚癡僧，三者清淨僧。……如是，善男子！佛法無量不可思議，如來亦爾不可思議。」

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如是，如是，誠如聖教。佛法無量不可思議，如來亦爾不可思議，故知如來常住不壞、無有變異。我今善學，亦當為人廣宣是義。」

爾時佛讚迦葉菩薩：「善哉，善哉！如來身者，即是金剛、不可壞身，菩薩應當如是善學，正見正知。若能如是了了知見，即是見佛金剛之身、不可壞身，如於鏡中見諸色像。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said: "Kāśyapa, it is through the cause and condition of being able to protect and uphold the true Dharma that this vajra body is accomplished. Kāśyapa, it is through the causes and conditions of protecting the Dharma in ages past that I have now accomplished this vajra body, permanently abiding and indestructible. Good son, one who protects and upholds the true Dharma need not receive the five precepts or cultivate the ordinary monastic bearing — such a person should instead carry swords, bows and arrows, and spears, to guard and protect the precept-keeping, pure monks."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if there is a monk who abandons such protection and dwells alone in solitude, among graveyards or beneath trees, such a person should be called a true monk; but if there is one who instead follows after these armed protectors, such a person should be understood as a mere shaven-headed householder."

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "Do not speak of them as shaven-headed householders… Good son, in ages past, throughout immeasurable, boundless asaṃkhyeya kalpas, there appeared, in this very city of Kuśinagara, a buddha named Nandivardhana Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly Awakened One… That buddha, the World-Honored One, abided in the world for an immeasurable time, and, having taught beings fully, at last entered parinirvāṇa among twin sāla trees. After that buddha's parinirvāṇa, the true Dharma abided in the world for immeasurable millions of years, with forty years still remaining before the Dharma was to be extinguished. At that time there was a precept-keeping monk named Kakuvimarda, surrounded by many followers and disciples, who was able to roar the lion's roar, proclaiming and expounding the ninefold scriptures at length, and forbidding monks from keeping servants, cattle, sheep, or any other improper possessions. At that time there were many monks who had broken the precepts, and, hearing him teach this way, all gave rise to malicious intent, took up swords and staves, and pressed in upon this teacher of the Dharma. At that time there was a king named Yǒudé — Possessed of Virtue — who, hearing of this, went at once, for the sake of protecting the Dharma, to where the teacher of the Dharma was, and fought fiercely against these evil, precept-breaking monks, so that the teacher of the Dharma escaped danger and harm. In the course of this battle, the king's own body was wounded by swords and arrows, until there was no part of him left unmarked, down to the size of a mustard seed. Kakuvimarda then praised the king, saying: 'Excellent, excellent! You, great king, are now a true protector of the true Dharma; in the age to come, this very body of yours will become a vessel for the Dharma beyond measure.' The king, having heard this teaching, was filled with great joy, and, dying soon after, was reborn in the buddha-land of Akṣobhya, becoming that buddha's own foremost disciple. Among the king's own soldiers and followers, some had fought in the battle and some had merely rejoiced in it, but every one of them, never regressing from the resolve for awakening, was, upon death, reborn likewise in the buddha-land of Akṣobhya. The monk Kakuvimarda, when his own life later came to its end, was likewise reborn in the buddha-land of Akṣobhya, becoming that buddha's second disciple among the śrāvakas. Whenever the true Dharma is about to be utterly extinguished, it should be upheld and protected in just this way. Kāśyapa, the king in that story was myself; the teacher of the Dharma was the buddha Kāśyapa. Kāśyapa, one who protects the true Dharma attains rewards immeasurable such as these. It is through this very cause that I have today attained every kind of mark, adorning myself with them, and have accomplished the Dharma-body, the indestructible body."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the Tathāgata's permanent body is like a painting carved into stone."

The Buddha said to the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Good son, for this very reason, monks, nuns, upāsakas, and upāsikās should diligently protect and uphold the true Dharma; the reward for protecting the Dharma is vast and immeasurable. Good son, this is why upāsakas who protect the Dharma should take up swords and staves to guard and protect monks who uphold the Dharma in this way. Whoever holds only the five precepts cannot be called a follower of the Great Vehicle; one who does not hold the five precepts but instead protects the true Dharma is the one properly called a follower of the Great Vehicle. One who protects the true Dharma should take up swords, blades, and weapons to attend the teacher of the Dharma."

Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if monks keep company with such upāsakas who carry swords and staves, do such monks then have a proper teacher, or not? Are they keeping the precepts, or breaking them?"

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "Do not regard such people as having broken the precepts. Good son, after my parinirvāṇa, in an age of turbidity and evil, kingdoms will fall into chaos, people will plunder one another, and the population will go hungry. At that time many will go forth into homelessness simply out of hunger, and such people will be called mere shaven-headed men. These shaven-headed men, on seeing a precept-keeping monk, complete in bearing, pure, upholding the true Dharma, will drive him out, or even kill or harm him… Good son, this is why I now permit precept-keeping monks to keep company with lay followers who carry swords and staves. If kings, ministers, elders, or upāsakas take up swords and staves for the sake of protecting the Dharma, I say that they too are keeping the precepts — though they carry swords and staves, they should not take a life. If they can act in this way, they are rightly called the very foremost among those who keep the precepts."

"Kāśyapa, one who protects the Dharma is one who possesses right view, who is able to proclaim the scriptures of the Great Vehicle widely, who never takes hold of a king's own jeweled canopy, oil, grain, or fruit for his own use, who does not draw close to kings, ministers, or elders for the sake of personal gain, whose mind harbors no flattery or deceit toward any donor, who is complete in bearing, and who subdues those who break the precepts and do evil — such a person is called a teacher who keeps the precepts and protects the Dharma, a true and good spiritual friend to beings, whose mind is as vast and expansive as the great ocean. Kāśyapa, if there is a monk who teaches the Dharma to others for the sake of personal gain, then his own followers and disciples will likewise imitate their teacher in greedily seeking personal gain, and such a person thereby destroys his own assembly. Kāśyapa, there are three kinds of assembly: the mixed assembly of those who break the precepts, the assembly of the foolish, and the pure assembly… It is just so, good son: the Buddha-dharma is immeasurable, beyond conceptual thought, and the Tathāgata is likewise beyond conceptual thought."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, it is so, it is so, truly as your holy teaching says. The Buddha-dharma is immeasurable, beyond conceptual thought, and the Tathāgata is likewise beyond conceptual thought — and so I know the Tathāgata abides permanently, indestructible, without any change. I shall now study this well, and shall also proclaim this meaning widely to others."

The Buddha then praised the bodhisattva Kāśyapa: "Excellent, excellent! The Tathāgata's body is itself the vajra, the indestructible body; bodhisattvas should study this well, with right view and right knowledge. If one can know and see this with utter clarity, this is itself seeing the Buddha's own vajra body, the indestructible body, as clearly as seeing every form reflected in a mirror."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the King Yǒudé story as the sūtra's own answer to an objection it has just staged through Kāśyapa's blunt question: if a true monk lives apart in solitude, doesn't a monk who travels with armed lay protectors thereby fail as a monk? Huiyuan's gloss distinguishes three tiers of monastic conduct rather than two — a lower tier content merely to keep the precepts without ever preaching against wrongdoing, a middle tier that preaches boldly but stops short of accepting armed protection, and Kakuvimarda's own tier, which preaches the ninefold scriptures at full volume against entrenched, armed, precept-breaking monks and accepts a king's soldiers to make that preaching survivable — and reads the sūtra as ranking the third highest specifically because it alone preserves the Dharma against violent suppression rather than merely preserving the preacher's own personal purity. He is careful to note what King Yǒudé's soldiers are and are not permitted: the text's repeated insistence that they "should not take a life" even while carrying swords is not a loophole quietly abandoned, but the entire point of the distinction between protecting the Dharma and merely fighting for it — a distinction Huiyuan reads as continuous with the very same "equal regard, calibrated by proportionate response" logic worked out earlier in this fascicle regarding the seven disciplinary acts against those who break the precepts.

The Virtues of This Sūtra's Name

Chinese Source

爾時如來復告迦葉：「善男子！汝今應當善持是經文字、章句、所有功德。若有善男子、善女人，聞是經名，生四趣者，無有是處。何以故？如是經典，乃是無量無邊諸佛之所修習，所得功德我今當說。」

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！當何名此經？菩薩摩訶薩云何奉持？」

佛告迦葉：「是經名為『大般涅槃』，上語亦善，中語亦善，下語亦善。義味深邃，其文亦善，純備具足清淨梵行，金剛寶藏滿足無缺。汝今善聽，我今當說。善男子，所言大者，名之為常，如八大河悉歸大海。此經如是，降伏一切諸結、煩惱及諸魔性，然後要於大般涅槃，放捨身命，是故名曰大般涅槃。善男子！又如醫師有一祕方，悉攝一切所有醫方。善男子！如來亦爾，所說種種妙法祕密深奧藏門，悉皆入於大般涅槃，是故名為大般涅槃。善男子！譬如農夫，春月下種，常有悕望，既收果實，眾望都息。善男子！一切眾生亦復如是，修學餘經，常悕滋味，若得聞是大般涅槃，悕望諸經所有滋味悉皆永斷。是大涅槃，能令眾生度諸有流。善男子！如諸跡中，象跡為最；此經如是，於諸經三昧最為第一。善男子！譬如耕田，秋耕為勝；此經如是，諸經中勝。善男子！如諸藥中，醍醐第一；善治眾生熱惱亂心，是大涅槃為最第一。善男子！譬如甜酥，八味具足；大般涅槃亦復如是，八味具足。云何為八？一者常，二者恒，三者安，四者清涼，五者不老，六者不死，七者無垢，八者快樂，是為八味具足。具是八味，是故名為大般涅槃。若諸菩薩摩訶薩等安住是中，復能處處示現涅槃，是故名為大般涅槃。迦葉！善男子、善女人，若欲於此大般涅槃而涅槃者，當如是學如來常住，法僧亦然。」

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「甚奇，世尊！如來功德不可思議，法僧亦爾不可思議，是大涅槃亦不可思議。若有修學是經典者，得正法門，能為良醫。若未學者，當知是人，盲無慧眼，無明所覆。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Tathāgata then said further to Kāśyapa: "Good son, you should now well uphold this sūtra's every letter, chapter, and verse, and all the virtues they contain. If any good son or good daughter, on hearing the very name of this sūtra, should still be reborn into one of the four evil destinies — there is no such possibility. Why is this? Because this scripture is precisely what immeasurable, boundless buddhas have themselves cultivated; I shall now explain the virtues it contains."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what should this sūtra be named? And how should a bodhisattva-mahāsattva uphold it?"

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "This sūtra is named 'Mahāparinirvāṇa' — excellent in its opening words, excellent in its middle words, and excellent in its closing words. Its meaning and flavor run deep, and its very language is excellent as well, complete and replete with pure conduct, a treasury of vajra fulfilled without any lack. Listen well now, and I shall explain it. Good son, the word 'great' here means permanent, just as the eight great rivers all flow into the great ocean; this sūtra is just the same, subduing every fetter, every affliction, and every nature of māra, and, only then, in the great parinirvāṇa, relinquishing body and life — and this is why it is named Mahāparinirvāṇa. Good son, it is also like a physician who has a single secret formula that encompasses every other medical remedy there is; the Tathāgata is just the same: every wondrous dharma he has taught, every secret and profound treasury-gate, is gathered entirely into the great parinirvāṇa, and this is why it is named Mahāparinirvāṇa. Good son, it is like a farmer who, sowing seed in the spring months, holds constant hope for the harvest, and, once the fruit is gathered in, finds every such hope finally at rest. Good son, every being is just the same: in studying other scriptures, they constantly hope for some flavor of truth, but once they hear this Mahāparinirvāṇa, their hope for whatever flavor other scriptures might hold is forever put to rest. This great nirvāṇa is able to bring beings across every stream of existence. Good son, it is like how, among the tracks of every creature, the elephant's track is supreme; this sūtra is just the same, foremost among the samādhis of every other sūtra. Good son, it is like how, in ploughing a field, autumn ploughing is finest; this sūtra is just the same, finest among every other sūtra. Good son, it is like how, among every medicine, ghee is foremost, well able to cure the fevered, disturbed minds of beings; this great nirvāṇa is likewise foremost among all things. Good son, it is like sweet cream, which is complete with eight flavors; this Mahāparinirvāṇa is just the same, complete with eight flavors. What are these eight? First, permanence; second, constancy; third, security; fourth, coolness; fifth, freedom from old age; sixth, freedom from death; seventh, freedom from defilement; eighth, true happiness — these are its eight complete flavors. Being complete with these eight flavors, it is named Mahāparinirvāṇa. And because the bodhisattva-mahāsattvas who abide within it are then able to manifest the appearance of nirvāṇa everywhere, in every place, it is likewise named Mahāparinirvāṇa. Kāśyapa, if any good son or good daughter wishes to attain nirvāṇa within this Mahāparinirvāṇa, they should study, in just this way, that the Tathāgata abides permanently, and that the Dharma and the Saṅgha do just the same."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "How wondrous, World-Honored One! The Tathāgata's own virtue is beyond conceptual thought, the Dharma and Saṅgha are likewise beyond conceptual thought, and this great nirvāṇa is beyond conceptual thought as well. Whoever studies and cultivates this scripture attains the gateway to the true Dharma, and can become a true and skillful physician; but whoever has not yet studied it should be understood to be blind, without the eye of wisdom, and covered over by ignorance."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this chapter's eight similes as falling into three distinct registers rather than one long list of equivalent comparisons: the first explains why the title itself means "great" (the eight rivers converging into a single ocean, matching this sūtra's own earlier image for the Tathāgata's lifespan in this same fascicle); the next four explain the sūtra's virtue as a teaching — its completeness, its finality, its power to surpass every other scripture's partial truths, and its supremacy among every kind of contemplative absorption; and the final three explain the virtue of what it actually points to — nirvāṇa's own essence and function. He reads the eight flavors themselves as an expansion, not a coincidence of number, of the six ordinary tastes (bitter, sour, sweet, pungent, salty, and bland) already invoked earlier in this same fascicle for Cunda's offered food, since "sweet" itself divides three ways — not-bitter, not-sour, not-pungent — yielding eight from what is ordinarily counted as six. The recurrence of paired similes across this whole fascicle (the eight rivers for the Tathāgata's lifespan, and now again for his sūtra's very name; ghee as the finest medicine, and now again as the finest flavor) is, on Huiyuan's reading, not repetition for its own sake but this sūtra's own way of insisting that its title, its teacher, and its teaching are ultimately naming the same single reality from three different angles — precisely the same move the secret-treasury simile of the three-dot letter i made two fascicles earlier for liberation, the Dharma-body, and prajñā.
