The Monk Who Fell Into Hell Before the Buddha's Own Eyes

A new chapter opens as the bodhisattva Kāśyapa presses the Buddha's own hardest case: Sunakṣatra, once his own son in a past life, mastered the twelve divisions of scripture and attained the four meditative absorptions — and yet fell, through a slow accumulation of contempt and bad company, into the fixed view that nothing has any cause or effect at all, and sank into the Avīci hell before the Buddha's own eyes. What follows is the sūtra's most sustained meditation yet on how a mind already this far along the path can still fall, why buddha-nature's presence never guarantees present rescue, why the Buddha ordained a man he already knew would sever his own roots, and why the sūtra itself speaks in as many different registers as there are different minds to reach — one name given a thousand meanings, and one meaning given a thousand names.

Six Similes for Three Kinds of Disciple

Chinese Source

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如來憐愍一切眾生，不調能調，不淨能淨，無歸依者能作歸依，未解脫者能令解脫，得八自在，為大醫師，作大藥王。善星比丘是佛菩薩時子，出家之後，受持讀誦、分別解說十二部經，壞欲界結，獲得四禪。云何如來記說善星，是一闡提，廝下之人，地獄劫住，不可治人？如來何故不先為其演說正法，後為菩薩？如來世尊若不能救善星比丘，云何得名有大慈愍、有大方便？」佛言：「善男子！譬如父母，唯有三子。其一子者有信順心，恭敬父母，利根智慧，於世間事能速了知。其第二子不敬父母，無信順心，利根智慧，於世間事能速了知。其第三子不敬父母，無信順心，鈍根無智。父母若欲教告之時，應先教誰？先親愛誰？當先教誰知世間事？」迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！應先教授有信順心，恭敬父母，利根智慧知世事者；其次第二乃及第三，而彼二子，雖無信心、恭敬之心，為愍念故，次復教之。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Kāśyapa Bodhisattva said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the Tathāgata, in his own pity for every being, tames the untamed, purifies the impure, becomes a refuge for those without any refuge, and brings liberation to those not yet liberated — having attained the eight kinds of mastery, he is a great physician, a great king of medicine. The monk Sunakṣatra was your own son during your own bodhisattva life, and, having become a renunciant, upheld, recited, and distinctly expounded the twelve divisions of scripture, destroyed the fetters of the desire realm, and attained the four meditative absorptions. Why, then, has the Tathāgata prophesied of Sunakṣatra that he is an icchantika, a base and lowly person, doomed to dwell in hell for a full kalpa, a person beyond all cure? Why did the Tathāgata not first teach him the true Dharma, and only afterward treat him as a bodhisattva? If the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, is truly unable to save the monk Sunakṣatra, how could he possibly be named one possessed of great compassion and great skillful means?" The Buddha said: "Good son, it is like a father and mother who have but three sons. The first son has a mind of faith and obedience, reveres his parents, is sharp in his own faculties and wisdom, and is quick to understand every worldly affair. The second son shows his parents no reverence, has no mind of faith or obedience, and yet is likewise sharp in his own faculties and wisdom, and quick to understand every worldly affair. The third son shows his parents no reverence, has no mind of faith or obedience, and is, further, dull in his own faculties, without any wisdom at all. Should these parents wish to instruct them, which ought they to teach first? Which ought they to love and cherish first? And which ought they first to teach the affairs of the world?" Kāśyapa Bodhisattva said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, they ought first to instruct the one who has a mind of faith and obedience, reveres his parents, and is sharp in his own faculties and wisdom, understanding the affairs of the world; and only then the second, and, after him, the third — and, though these latter two sons have neither faith nor reverence, still, out of pity for them, their parents go on to teach them as well, each in turn."

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, the Tathāgata is exactly the same. Of these three sons, the first is a likeness for the bodhisattva, the middle for the śrāvaka, and the last for the icchantika. The subtle and profound meaning found within the twelve divisions of scripture, I have already taught first for the bodhisattvas; its shallower, more accessible meaning I teach for the śrāvakas; and its merely worldly meaning I teach for the icchantika and for those who have committed the five heinous crimes — though this brings no benefit in the present life, it is, out of pity, meant to plant wholesome seeds for a future life. Good son, it is like three kinds of field: the first has an easy, convenient irrigation channel, free of alkaline soil, rubble, or thorns, and yields a hundredfold return for every seed planted; the second, though likewise free of alkaline soil, rubble, or thorns, has a difficult, perilous irrigation channel, and yields only half its proper harvest; and the third has both a perilous irrigation channel and much alkaline soil, rubble, and thorns, yielding only as much as was planted, its produce fit only for straw. Good son, in the month of spring, which field does the farmer plant first?" "World-Honored One, he plants the first field first, then the second field, and, last of all, the third." "The first is a likeness for the bodhisattva, the second for the śrāvaka, and the last for the icchantika. Good son, it is like three jars — one whole, one leaking, and one broken — should one wish to fill them with curds, water, or ghee, which does one use first?" "World-Honored One, one ought to use the whole one first, then the leaking one, and, last of all, the broken one." "The whole, pure jar is a likeness for the bodhisattva saṅgha; the leaking jar is a likeness for the śrāvaka; and the broken jar is a likeness for the icchantika. Good son, it is like three sick people who all come together to a physician — one easily cured, one cured only with difficulty, and one beyond all cure. Good son, whom should the physician treat first, should he treat them at all?" "World-Honored One, he ought to treat the one easily cured first, then the second, and, last of all, the third — why is this? Because they are all, in the end, his own kin." "The one easily cured is a likeness for the bodhisattva saṅgha; the one cured only with difficulty is a likeness for the śrāvaka saṅgha; and the one beyond all cure is a likeness for the icchantika — who, though he bears no wholesome fruit in the present life, is nonetheless still treated, out of pity, so as to plant wholesome seeds for a future life. Good son, it is like a great king who possesses three kinds of horse: one well-trained, robust, and strong; one untrained, yet of mature teeth and great strength; and one untrained, aged, weak, and without strength at all. Should the king wish to ride, which does he ride first?" "World-Honored One, he ought to ride the well-trained, robust, strong horse first, then the second, and, last of all, the third." "Good son, the well-trained, robust, strong horse is a likeness for the bodhisattva saṅgha; the second is a likeness for the śrāvaka saṅgha; and the third is a likeness for the icchantika — who, though he brings no benefit in the present life, is nonetheless still attended to, out of pity, so as to plant wholesome seeds for a future life. Good son, it is like a great act of giving, to which three people come: one of noble lineage, intelligent, and precept-keeping; one of middling lineage, dull-witted, yet still precept-keeping; and one of low lineage, dull-witted, and precept-breaking. Good son, to whom ought this great donor give first?" "World-Honored One, he ought to give first to the son of noble lineage, sharp-witted and precept-keeping, then to the second, and, last of all, to the third." "The first is a likeness for the bodhisattva saṅgha, the second for the śrāvaka saṅgha, and the third for the icchantika. Good son, it is like a great lion who, in killing a fragrant elephant, exerts the whole of his own strength — and, in killing even a mere hare, does exactly the same, giving rise to no thought of contempt at all. Every buddha, every tathāgata, is exactly the same: in teaching the Dharma for the sake of the bodhisattva and the icchantika alike, his own effort and exertion are never divided into two."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Kāśyapa's opening question as carrying two distinct charges at once, cleverly disguised as a single compliment: praising the Buddha's compassion and skillful means first, only to use Sunakṣatra's own case to argue the Buddha demonstrated neither — if the Buddha truly foresaw Sunakṣatra's fall, why ordain him at all, and why, having ordained him, address him only in terms suited to a bodhisattva rather than starting with teachings he might actually have absorbed? He reads the Buddha's answer as reframing the entire premise: the same teacher does not fail three different sons by teaching the same lesson to all of them in the same order, and the six similes (sons, fields, jars, patients, horses, donors) are offered not as six separate arguments but as the same single point restated across six ordinary domains of life, so that no reader could mistake the pattern for special pleading confined to religious teaching alone — every domain of ordinary practical wisdom already treats capable, willing recipients first without this constituting neglect of the rest.

The closing lion-and-hare image sharpens this into its final form: unequal treatment in the order of teaching (advanced disciples first, the icchantika last) is compatible with, and indeed requires, equal exertion once teaching does reach each one — the Buddha's effort teaching the icchantika is not diminished merely because the icchantika is taught last, exactly as a lion does not become halfhearted when its prey is small. This directly answers Kāśyapa's own sharpest charge (that unequal timing proves unequal compassion) by separating sequence from intensity, a distinction the rest of this fascicle's account of Sunakṣatra's own fall will test to its very limit.

Sunakṣatra's Long Fall

Chinese Source

「善男子！我於一時住王舍城，善星比丘為我給使。我於初夜，為天帝釋演說法要。弟子法應後師眠臥，爾時善星以我久坐心生惡念。時王舍城小男、小女若啼不止，父母則語：『汝若不止，當將汝付薄拘羅鬼。』爾時善星反被拘執，而語我言：『速入禪室，薄拘羅來。』我言：『癡人，汝常不聞如來世尊無所畏耶？』爾時帝釋即語我言：『世尊！如是人等，亦復得入佛法中耶？』我即語言：『憍尸迦！如是人者，得入佛法，亦有佛性，當得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』我雖為是善星說法，而彼都無信受之心。「善男子！我於一時在迦尸國尸婆富羅城，善星比丘為我給使。我時欲入彼城乞食，無量眾生，虛心渴仰，欲見我跡。善星比丘尋隨我後，而毀滅之；既不能滅，而令眾生生不善心。我入城已，於酒家舍，見一尼乾，踡脊蹲地，飡食酒糟。善星比丘見已而言：『世尊！世間若有阿羅漢者，是人最勝。何以故？是人所說無因無果。』我言：『癡人，汝常不聞阿羅漢者，不飲酒、不害人、不欺誑、不偷盜、不婬妷。是人殺害父母，食噉酒糟，云何而言是阿羅漢？是人捨身，必定當墮阿鼻地獄。阿羅漢者永斷三惡，云何而言是阿羅漢？』善星即言：『四大之性，猶可轉易，欲令是人必墮阿鼻，無有是處。』我言：『癡人，汝常不聞諸佛如來誠言無二。』我雖為是善星說法，而彼絕無信受之心。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, I once dwelt in the city of Rājagṛha, where the monk Sunakṣatra served as my own attendant. In the first watch of the night, I was teaching the essential Dharma to Indra, the lord of the gods. According to the proper conduct of a disciple, he ought to have gone to sleep only after his own teacher — and, at that time, Sunakṣatra, resenting how long I remained seated, gave rise to an evil thought. At that time, in the city of Rājagṛha, whenever a small boy or girl would not stop crying, the parents would say to the child: 'Should you not stop, I shall hand you over to the demon Vakkula.' Sunakṣatra, at that very moment, himself seized with a kind of panic, said to me: 'Enter your meditation chamber quickly — Vakkula is coming!' I said: 'Fool, have you never heard that the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, is without any fear at all?' Indra, at that very moment, said to me: 'World-Honored One, can a person such as this truly still enter the Buddha's own Dharma?' I answered him: 'Kauśika, a person such as this is indeed able to enter the Buddha's own Dharma, and he, too, possesses buddha-nature, and shall attain unsurpassed, complete awakening.' Though I taught the Dharma for Sunakṣatra in this way, he gave rise to no faith or acceptance of it at all. "Good son, I once dwelt in the city of Śrāvastī, in the kingdom of Kāśī, where the monk Sunakṣatra served as my own attendant. I wished, at that time, to enter that city to beg for alms, and immeasurable beings, their minds open and thirsting, wished to see the very trace of my own footprints. The monk Sunakṣatra, following close behind me, tried to erase them — and, unable to erase them, caused beings instead to give rise to an unwholesome mind. Having entered the city, I saw, in a tavern, a Nirgrantha ascetic, hunched over, crouching on the ground, eating the dregs left over from wine. The monk Sunakṣatra, seeing this, said: 'World-Honored One, should there be any arhat at all in this world, this very man is the finest of them — why is this? Because what he teaches admits of neither cause nor effect at all.' I said: 'Fool, have you never heard that an arhat does not drink wine, does not harm others, does not deceive, does not steal, and does not commit sexual misconduct? This man has killed his own father and mother, and now eats the dregs of wine — how could he possibly be called an arhat? Once this man casts off his own body, he shall certainly fall into the Avīci hell. An arhat has forever severed the three evil destinies — how could he possibly be called an arhat?' Sunakṣatra then said: 'The very nature of the four great elements can still be transformed and changed — to say that this man must certainly fall into the Avīci hell could never be so.' I said: 'Fool, have you never heard that every buddha, every tathāgata, speaks the truth without any contradiction at all?' Though I taught the Dharma for Sunakṣatra in this way, he gave rise to absolutely no faith or acceptance of it at all.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, I once dwelt together with the monk Sunakṣatra in the city of Rājagṛha, at a time when there was, within that city, a Nirgrantha ascetic named Kokālika, who forever said: 'Beings' own affliction has neither cause nor condition at all, and beings' own liberation, likewise, has neither cause nor condition.' The monk Sunakṣatra said, further: 'World-Honored One, should there be any arhat at all in this world, Kokālika is the very foremost of them.' I said: 'Fool, this Nirgrantha Kokālika is, in truth, no arhat at all, and is unable even to understand the arhat's own path.' Sunakṣatra said, further: 'For what cause and condition would one arhat feel envy toward another arhat?' I said: 'Fool, I feel no envy at all toward any arhat — it is you yourself who have given rise to this very evil, wrong view. Should you say that Kokālika is truly an arhat, then, after seven days have passed, he shall suffer from undigested food, his belly aching, and die; and, having died, he shall be reborn among the vomit-eating hungry ghosts; and his own fellow students shall carry his corpse and lay it out in the charnel grove.' At this, Sunakṣatra at once went to where the Nirgrantha Kokālika was, and said to him: 'Elder, do you know this? The śramaṇa Gautama has prophesied that, after seven days, you shall suffer from undigested food, your belly aching, and die, and, having died, shall be reborn among the vomit-eating hungry ghosts, and that your own fellow students and teacher shall carry your corpse and lay it out in the charnel grove. Elder, reflect well upon this, and devise some skillful means, so that Gautama might be made to fall into false speech.' Kokālika, upon hearing these words, at once ceased eating — from the first day all the way to the sixth — and, once the full seven days had passed, ate some black honey; and, having eaten this black honey, drank cold water afterward; and, having drunk this cold water, his belly ached, and he died. Once he had died, his fellow students carried his corpse and laid it out in the charnel grove, and he at once took on the very form of a vomit-eating hungry ghost, remaining there beside his own corpse. The monk Sunakṣatra, hearing of this, went to the charnel grove, and saw Kokālika's own body, now in the form of a vomit-eating ghost, hunched over, crouching beside his own corpse. Sunakṣatra said to him: 'Venerable one, are you dead?' Kokālika answered: 'I have already died.' 'How did you die?' He answered: 'I died from a pain in my belly.' 'Who carried out your corpse?' He answered: 'My own fellow students.' 'Where did they lay it out?' He answered: 'Fool, do you not now recognize this very charnel grove?' 'What kind of body have you now received?' He answered: 'I have received the body of a vomit-eating ghost. Sunakṣatra, listen closely: the Tathāgata's speech is good speech, true speech, timely speech, meaningful speech, and lawful speech. Sunakṣatra, the Tathāgata's own mouth uttered exactly this true speech — why, at that time, did you not believe it? Should any being fail to believe the Tathāgata's own true speech, he, too, shall receive a body exactly like this one of mine.' At this, Sunakṣatra at once returned to where I was, and said: 'World-Honored One, the Nirgrantha Kokālika, after his own death, was reborn in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven.' I said: 'Fool, an arhat has no further place of rebirth at all — how could you possibly say that Kokālika was reborn in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven?' 'World-Honored One, it is truly exactly as you have said — the Nirgrantha Kokālika was, in truth, not reborn in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven at all, but has now received the very body of a vomit-eating hungry ghost.' Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three episodes are arranged as an escalating sequence of the very same underlying error — mistaking a superficial resemblance for the real thing — moving from the trivial to the doctrinal: fearing a bogeyman's name when he ought to know his own teacher's fearlessness better than anyone; mistaking a degraded ascetic's nihilism ("no cause, no effect") for the marks of genuine arhatship simply because both reject the ordinary rules; and finally staking his own credibility on Kokālika's supposed arhatship against the Buddha's own direct prophecy, only to become, unwittingly, the very witness who confirms that prophecy's truth in the most vivid way possible — interviewing a hungry ghost at its own corpse, and still finding a way not to believe what he has just seen with his own eyes. This last episode's structure is worth noting: Sunakṣatra does not merely fail to update on new evidence, he actively supplies Kokālika with a countermeasure ("devise some skillful means to make Gautama fall into falsehood"), and Kokālika's own hunger strike is itself a superstitious attempt to defeat the prophecy that instead fulfills it exactly as foretold — the same self-defeating logic showing up twice, once in the disciple's advice and once in the ascetic's own actions.

The closing loss of the four dhyānas is the passage's own diagnosis of what actually went wrong beneath these three individual incidents: genuine meditative attainment (the four absorptions are not trivial; they are real, advanced accomplishments) proved entirely separable from genuine understanding, since Sunakṣatra "never once understood the meaning of even a single verse" of everything he had memorized — attainment without comprehension, sustained only by discipline rather than insight, collapsed the moment bad company gave him an alternative framework to explain his own experience. His final fall, triggered by nothing more than the sight of the Buddha and a single evil thought, dramatizes how thin the remaining barrier had become by that point — not a sudden catastrophe but the visible conclusion of a process this section has just spent three full episodes tracing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The wealth-lost-versus-never-had comparison gives this collection's recurring theme of "the further one falls, the greater the pity" its clearest statement yet, and it answers Kāśyapa's implicit charge from this fascicle's opening question directly: the Buddha's harsh-sounding prophecy is not a withdrawal of compassion but its most acute expression, since describing Sunakṣatra's heedlessness in such stark terms is itself only possible because the Buddha has tracked, in detail, exactly how much was actually lost. The latrine-pit simile, appearing twice in this section — once for the search that finally gives up, and again for the single hair unable to bear a body's weight — supplies two distinct but related points: the first shows the Buddha's own diagnostic method (searching exhaustively before concluding, rather than writing someone off in advance), and the second shows why a genuinely present but vanishingly small wholesome root still fails to rescue someone from an immediate crisis, even though it guarantees eventual rescue in principle.

Kāśyapa's own follow-up question — since beings possess wholesome dharmas across all three times, how can severing merely present and future wholesome roots justify calling someone entirely without any wholesome dharma at all? — receives an answer built on a distinction this collection has used before in different registers: severing in the present, and obstructing the future from the present, are treated as jointly sufficient for the label "icchantika" even though the icchantika's own past wholesome deeds remain, in some sense, unerased. The passage's closing move — buddha-nature alone can save the icchantika, precisely because buddha-nature belongs to none of the three times and therefore cannot be severed by anything that operates within them — restates, in the vocabulary of this specific and painfully concrete case, the very distinction Part 32 drew abstractly between the impermanent, severable bodhi-mind and buddha-nature proper: whatever Sunakṣatra actually destroyed in himself, it was never the one thing this entire sūtra insists cannot be destroyed at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Kāśyapa's objection is genuinely sharp: an icchantika who loves his own family, however imperfectly, seems to exhibit a wholesome mental state by any ordinary measure — and if buddha-nature is permanent, like space, calling it "future" seems to contradict permanence outright, since what is truly permanent should already be fully present now. The Buddha's answer separates two things Kāśyapa's question runs together: buddha-nature's own permanent, atemporal status (which is why it resists being assigned even to the present, since assignment to any one of the three times would make it impermanent by definition) and the language used to point toward it, which borrows the future tense purely because that is where a being's own purified body will finally see it — the "cause taught as fruit, fruit taught as cause" comment generalizes this into an explicit acknowledgment that much of the sūtra's own vocabulary works by convenient substitution rather than literal description, continuing the same method Part 33 and its predecessors have used throughout for "existence," "self," and "cause."

The answer to the family-love objection is more severe, and worth reading carefully: the Buddha does not deny that the icchantika's affection for his own kin looks, superficially, like love — he denies that it counts as a wholesome dharma at all, on the grounds that every action the icchantika performs, including this one, is directed at nothing beyond itself, seeking "no true cause or fruit," and is therefore corrupted at the root the same way every part of the myrobalan fruit shares the same bitterness. This is a stronger and more totalizing claim than simply saying the icchantika lacks wholesome roots in general — it specifically closes off the one exception (ordinary human affection) that might seem to survive even total severance, extending the completeness of the icchantika's own condition to cover every visible behavior, not only the doctrinal proclamations that triggered the diagnosis in the first place.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's answer to "why ordain someone doomed to fall" turns out to rest on two entirely different justifications, offered in sequence rather than as alternatives — worth distinguishing carefully, since conflating them would weaken both. The first is frankly political and pre-emptive: leaving Sunakṣatra in the royal succession risked a ruler hostile to the Dharma with the actual power to suppress it, making ordination a defensive measure regardless of Sunakṣatra's own future spiritual trajectory. The second is a genuine cost-benefit accounting of the ordination's effect on Sunakṣatra himself: even a life that ends in severed roots was, for twenty years, a life of precept-keeping, reverence, and real meditative attainment — dharmas which, however temporary, are still named "a wholesome cause," and the sūtra's own point is that a wasted opportunity is not the same as a purely negative one. The faculties-are-never-fixed teaching that opens this section is the doctrinal foundation making both justifications coherent at all: were a being's own capacity for wholesome or unwholesome character fixed in advance, foreknowledge of an eventual fall would make intervention simply pointless, but since faculties genuinely move between superior, middling, and inferior across a lifetime and across lives, the Buddha's own foreknowledge of Sunakṣatra's fall was never foreknowledge of an unavoidable, unalterable outcome — only of what would in fact happen given the causes and conditions that did, in fact, obtain.

The fingernail-dirt simile closes the section with the sūtra's own starkest image yet for the sheer improbability of the conditions this whole discussion has been assuming as background: a human rebirth with complete faculties, born where the Dharma can be heard, is compared to a speck of dirt beneath a single fingernail against the entire earth of every world-system in the ten directions — and genuine faith in this very Nirvāṇa Sūtra specifically is measured by the same ratio. Read against the immediately preceding argument, the simile reframes what might look like an indictment of Sunakṣatra's own personal failure into a demonstration of exactly how rare his own twenty years of genuine, if ultimately unsustained, practice actually were — the sūtra does not soften how badly Sunakṣatra failed, but it does insist that the conditions making failure or success possible at all were themselves already remarkable.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Kāśyapa's catalogue reads, to a historically informed eye, less like a hypothetical list than a fairly precise preview of the real doctrinal controversies that would in fact divide the various Abhidharma schools for centuries after this sūtra's own composition — the reality of unmanifest form (avijñapti-rūpa), whether the twelve links are conditioned or unconditioned, whether three unconditioned dharmas exist, whether a precept survives its own violation, and whether the mind is momentary or enduring were all live disputes among named schools of Buddhist philosophy. Read this way, the sūtra is not inventing a fictional future confusion to make a rhetorical point but naming, with unusual directness, the actual scholastic fragmentation its own compilers were living through or anticipating, and putting the question of why the Buddha permits this fragmentation directly into the mouth of a senior bodhisattva rather than leaving it to be asked from outside the tradition.

The question's own rhetorical structure — every item paired as a flat contradiction, "some say X, others say not-X" — sets up a genuine puzzle about omniscience and pedagogy that the next section answers at length: if the Buddha truly knows every future faculty in advance, his failure to simply state, once and for all, which side of each dispute is correct looks like either a failure of knowledge or a failure of compassion, exactly the same dilemma structuring this fascicle's opening question about Sunakṣatra. The parallel is almost certainly deliberate — both questions ask why a being of complete knowledge tolerates an outcome (a disciple's damnation, a tradition's fragmentation) that foreknowledge seems to make preventable, and the sūtra's answer to both turns out to hinge on the same insight: uniform treatment of non-uniform minds is not compassion at all, but its opposite.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, for the sake of beings, teaches briefly within what is broad, and broadly within what is brief; teaches the ultimate truth as though it were conventional truth, and teaches conventional truth as though it were the ultimate truth. What is meant by teaching briefly within what is broad? It is like telling the monks: 'I shall now proclaim the twelve links of dependent origination.' What, then, is meant by the twelve links of dependent origination? Simply cause and fruit. What is meant by teaching broadly within what is brief? It is like telling the monks: 'I shall now proclaim suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path.' Suffering means immeasurable kinds of suffering; its arising means immeasurable kinds of affliction; its cessation means immeasurable kinds of liberation; and the path means immeasurable kinds of skillful means. What is meant by teaching the ultimate truth as though it were conventional truth? It is like telling the monks: 'This very body of mine now has old age, sickness, and death.' What is meant by teaching conventional truth as though it were the ultimate truth? It is like telling Kauṇḍinya: 'Because you have attained the Dharma, you are now named Ājñāta-Kauṇḍinya.' It is for this very reason that, in accord with the person, the intention, and the occasion, the Tathāgata is named one who knows every faculty's own power. Good son, were I to give a single, fixed, decisive teaching on matters of exactly this kind, I could not then be called a tathāgata fully endowed with the knowledge of every faculty's own power at all. "Good son, a person of wisdom should know that what a fragrant elephant is able to carry, a donkey could never bear. Every being's own conduct is immeasurably varied, and it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata teaches immeasurable dharmas in every kind of way. Why is this? Because beings possess a great many different kinds of affliction. Were the Tathāgata to teach but a single, uniform practice for all, he could not be called a tathāgata fully endowed with and accomplished in the knowledge of every faculty's own power. It is for this very reason that I have taught, within other scriptures, that five kinds of beings ought never to be taught the five corresponding teachings in return: one without faith ought not to be praised for right faith; one who breaks the precepts ought not to be praised for precept-keeping; one who is stingy and greedy ought not to be praised for generous giving; one who is indolent ought not to be praised for extensive learning; and one who is foolish ought not to be praised for wisdom. Why is this? Should a wise person teach these very five things to these five kinds of people, one should know that this teacher does not fully possess the knowledge of every faculty's own power, nor can he be named one who truly has compassion for beings. Why is this? These five kinds of people, upon hearing such teaching, would give rise to a mind of disbelief, an evil mind, and an angry mind — and, through this very cause and condition, would undergo suffering as their own fruit across immeasurable ages. It is for this very reason that this cannot be named having compassion for beings, or truly possessing the knowledge of every faculty's own power. It is for this very reason that I once told Śāriputra, within another scripture: 'Take care never to teach at great length to one of sharp faculties, nor to teach only briefly to one of dull faculties.' Śāriputra said: 'World-Honored One, I taught only out of my own compassion, not because I fully possessed the knowledge of every faculty's own power.' Good son, teaching at length or in brief, as the occasion demands, is the Buddha's own domain, and is not known by any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha. "Good son, as you yourself have said, after the Buddha's own parinirvāṇa, his disciples shall each hold differing views, every one of them through the cause and condition of their own inverted thinking, unable to attain right view, and thereby unable to benefit either themselves or others. Good son, these very beings are not of a single nature, a single conduct, a single faculty, a single kind of land, or a single good friend — and it is for this very reason that the Tathāgata proclaims the essential Dharma for them in every kind of different way. It is through this very cause and condition that every buddha, every tathāgata, throughout the ten directions and the three times, reveals and proclaims the twelve divisions of scripture for the sake of beings. Good son, the Tathāgata teaches these twelve divisions of scripture not for his own benefit at all, but solely for the benefit of others. It is for this very reason that the Tathāgata's own fifth power is named the power of liberating interpretation. It is through these two powers that the Tathāgata knows, in profound detail, that this person is able, in the present, to sever his own wholesome roots, that person is able, in a future life, to sever his own wholesome roots, this person is able, in the present, to attain liberation, and that person is able, in a future life, to attain liberation. It is for this very reason that the Tathāgata is named the unsurpassed mighty one."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this section's own fourfold taxonomy of naming (one name/many names, one meaning/many names, many meanings/many names, and — restated once more for the aggregates — one meaning/many names again) as the sūtra's own direct answer to Kāśyapa's charge of inconsistency from the previous section: what looks, from outside, like the Buddha contradicting himself across different discourses is, on inspection, one of these four regular patterns of naming at work, each with its own logic. Twenty-five names for nirvāṇa, or ten for Indra, are not competing definitions to be reconciled but a single referent approached from twenty-five or ten different angles — and the sūtra's own closing example, the aggregates renamed as inversion, the four foundations of mindfulness, or the twelve links depending on context, shows that even a psychologically basic term shifts its effective meaning entirely with the framework surrounding it, without this constituting any actual inconsistency in what is being pointed at.

The elephant-load/donkey-load simile and the explicit warning against praising faith to the faithless, precepts to the precept-breaker, or wisdom to the foolish directly answers the deeper worry beneath Kāśyapa's twenty disputes: a single, uniform, once-and-for-all doctrinal pronouncement would function like loading an elephant's burden onto a donkey — not more efficient, but simply impossible to carry, and liable to provoke exactly the disbelief and anger the passage predicts. The anecdote about Śāriputra — rebuked for over-simplifying to a sharp-minded listener and under-simplifying to a dull one, and answering only that he acted from compassion rather than full mastery of this very skill — is a rare moment in the sūtra where even an arhat is shown getting a teaching decision wrong, used to mark the precise boundary between ordinary compassionate teaching (available even to disciples) and the Buddha's own "fifth power," the specific, comprehensive skill of matching teaching to listener without error — the sūtra's own final answer to why Sunakṣatra's fall, and the future fragmentation of the tradition Kāśyapa fears, are never treated as failures of the Buddha's own knowledge, but as necessary consequences of teaching real, non-uniform beings honestly rather than uniformly.
