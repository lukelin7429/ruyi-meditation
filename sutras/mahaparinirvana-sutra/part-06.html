The Caṇḍāla King, and the Four Reliances

Four kinds of people, from an ordinary person still full of affliction up through the arhat, are named as fit protectors and interpreters of this sūtra — but Kāśyapa immediately presses the hardest available objection: if Māra can counterfeit the Buddha's own form, why trust these four at all? The fascicle then turns to two of this sūtra's most vivid narrative set-pieces — a corrupt monk's private glee at the news of the Buddha's death, and a disguised bodhisattva's poisoning of a usurper caṇḍāla king to restore the rightful heir — before closing with the four reliances that have shaped how this whole tradition reads scripture ever since: on the Dharma rather than the person who speaks it, on the meaning rather than the words, on wisdom rather than ordinary consciousness, and on the definitive teaching rather than the provisional one.

The Four Kinds of People Who Protect the Dharma

Chinese Source

「善男子！是大涅槃微妙經中，有四種人，能護正法、建立正法、憶念正法，能多利益憐愍世間，為世間依安樂人天。何等為四？有人出世具煩惱性是名第一，須陀洹人、斯陀含人是名第二，阿那含人是名第三，阿羅漢人是名第四。是四種人出現於世，能多利益憐愍世間，為世間依安樂人天。」

「云何名為具煩惱性？若有人能奉持禁戒，威儀具足，建立正法，從佛所聞，解其文義，轉為他人分別宣說，所謂少欲是道，多欲非道，廣說如是八大人覺，有犯罪者教令發露，懺悔滅除，善知菩薩方便所行祕密之法，是名凡夫，非第八人。第八人者，不名凡夫，名為菩薩，不名為佛。」

「第二人者名須陀洹、斯陀含，若得正法，受持正法，從佛聞法，如其所聞，聞已書寫，受持讀誦，轉為他說。若聞法已，不寫不受、不持不說，而言奴婢不淨之物佛聽畜者，無有是處，是名第二人。如是之人未得第二、第三住處，名為菩薩，已得受記。」

「第三人者名阿那含，阿那含者，誹謗正法若言聽畜奴婢、僕使、不淨之物，受持外道典籍、書論，及為客塵煩惱所障，諸舊煩惱之所覆蓋，若藏如來真實舍利，及為外病之所惱害，或為四大毒蛇所侵，論說我者，悉無是處；若說無我，斯有是處。說著世法，無有是處；若說大乘相續不絕，斯有是處。若所受身有八萬虫，亦無是處；永離婬欲乃至夢中不失不淨，斯有是處。臨終之日生怖畏者，亦無是處。阿那含者為何謂也？是人不還。如上所說所有過患，永不能污往返周旋。名為菩薩，已得受記，不久得成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是則名為第三人也。」

「第四人者名阿羅漢，阿羅漢者，斷諸煩惱，捨於重擔，逮得己利，所作已辦，住第十地，得自在智；隨人所樂種種色像，悉能示現；如所莊嚴欲成佛道，即能得成。能成如是無量功德，名阿羅漢。是名四人出現於世，能多利益憐愍世間，為世間依安樂人天，於人天中最尊最勝，猶如如來，名人中勝，為歸依處。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, within this subtle and profound scripture of the great nirvāṇa, there are four kinds of people able to protect the true Dharma, establish the true Dharma, and hold the true Dharma in mind, bringing great benefit and compassion to the world, becoming a support and a source of ease for gods and humans alike. What are these four? One who appears in the world still possessing the nature of affliction is the first; a stream-enterer or a once-returner is the second; a non-returner is the third; and an arhat is the fourth. These four kinds of people, appearing in the world, are able to bring great benefit and compassion to the world, becoming a support and a source of ease for gods and humans alike."

"What is meant by still possessing the nature of affliction? If there is a person able to uphold the moral precepts, complete in bearing, establishing the true Dharma, who, having heard it from the Buddha, understands its words and meaning, and expounds it in turn for others — teaching, for instance, that few desires are the path and many desires are not the path, expounding at length the eight awakenings of a great person, instructing whoever has transgressed to confess openly, repent, and be cleansed of the offense, and who understands well the skillful, secret conduct of the bodhisattvas — such a person is called an ordinary being, not the eighth kind of person. The eighth kind of person is not called an ordinary being, but is called a bodhisattva, though not yet called a buddha."

"The second kind of person is called a stream-enterer or a once-returner: one who has attained the true Dharma, upholds the true Dharma, and, having heard the Dharma from the Buddha exactly as it was taught, writes it down, upholds it, reads and recites it, and expounds it in turn for others. Should such a person, having heard the Dharma, fail to write it down, fail to receive it, fail to uphold or expound it, and instead claim that the Buddha permits monks to keep servants and other improper possessions — this could not possibly be so. This is the second kind of person. Such a person has not yet attained the second or third abode, and is called a bodhisattva who has already received a prediction of future buddhahood."

"The third kind of person is called a non-returner. As for the non-returner: it could not possibly be so that such a person slanders the true Dharma or claims the Buddha permits keeping servants and other improper possessions, upholds the treatises and writings of heterodox schools, is obstructed by newly arisen, adventitious afflictions, or is covered over by long-standing afflictions, conceals the Tathāgata's own true relics, is afflicted by outward illness, or is assailed by the four great elements as though by poisonous serpents, or asserts the existence of a fixed self — none of this could possibly be so. It could be so, however, that such a person teaches no-self; it could not possibly be so that such a person remains attached to worldly things, though it could be so that such a person continues, without interruption, to expound the Great Vehicle. It could not possibly be so that the body such a person has received harbors eighty thousand kinds of worm; it could be so, however, that such a person is forever free of sexual desire, never once losing purity even in dreams. It could not possibly be so that such a person, on the point of death, gives rise to fear. What, then, is meant by non-returner? Such a person does not return. None of the faults described above can ever taint such a person's own coming and going. This is called a bodhisattva who has already received a prediction of future buddhahood, and who will, before long, accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening — this is what is meant by the third kind of person."

"The fourth kind of person is called an arhat. An arhat has cut off every affliction, laid down every heavy burden, attained his own true benefit, and completed what needed to be done; abiding on the tenth ground, he has gained mastery of wisdom, and is able to manifest every kind of form according to what beings delight in; whatever he sets his mind to accomplishing toward buddhahood, he is able to accomplish. Having accomplished immeasurable virtues such as these, he is called an arhat. These, then, are the four kinds of people who, appearing in the world, are able to bring great benefit and compassion to the world, becoming a support and a source of ease for gods and humans, the most honored and excellent among gods and humans, just like the Tathāgata himself, foremost among people, a true refuge."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan is careful to note what this fourfold scheme is not: none of these four figures is, strictly speaking, the ordinary śrāvaka path's stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, or arhat that the same names denote elsewhere in the canon. He maps each onto specific bodhisattva grounds instead — the first roughly to the stages before the ten grounds proper, the second and third pairs spanning clusters of grounds from the first through the ninth, the fourth to the tenth ground and its approach to buddhahood itself — and raises, then answers, the obvious question this creates: if these are all bodhisattvas at various stages, why borrow the śrāvaka path's own familiar vocabulary to name them? His answer is pedagogical rather than doctrinal: readers still thinking in śrāvaka categories can use the rough correspondence in maturity and effort between (for instance) a non-returner's freedom from sensual craving and a given bodhisattva ground's own corresponding freedom, as a ladder into a scheme that would otherwise mean nothing to them — the borrowed names are training wheels, not a claim that fourth-ground bodhisattvas and śrāvaka arhats occupy the same rank.

Can Māra Impersonate These Four?

Chinese Source

迦葉白佛言：「世尊！我今不依是四種人。何以故？如《瞿師羅經》中，佛為瞿師羅說：『若天魔梵為欲破壞，變為佛像，具足莊嚴三十二相、八十種好、圓光一尋，面部圓滿猶月盛明，眉間毫相白踰珂雪，如是莊嚴來向汝者，汝當撿校定其虛實，既覺知已，應當降伏。』世尊！魔等尚能變作佛身，況當不能作羅漢等四種之身，坐臥空中，左脇出水，右脇出火，身出烟炎，猶如火聚？以是因緣，我於是中，心不生信。或有所說，不能稟受，亦無敬念，而作依止。」

佛言：「善男子！於我所說若生疑者，尚不應受，況如是等？是故應當善分別知，是善、不善，可作、不可作，如是作已，長夜受樂。善男子！譬如偷狗，夜入人舍，其家婢使若覺知者，即應驅罵：『汝疾出去，若不出者，當奪汝命。』偷狗聞之，即去不還。汝等從今，亦應如是降伏波旬，應作是言：『波旬！汝今不應作如是像，若故作者，當以五繫，繫縛於汝。』魔聞是已，便當還去，如彼偷狗，更不復還。」

迦葉白佛言：「世尊！如佛為瞿師羅長者說，若能如是降伏魔者，亦可得近大般涅槃，如來何必說是四人為依止處？如是四人，所可言說，未必可信。」

佛告迦葉：「善男子！如我所說，亦復如是，非為不爾。善男子！我為聲聞、有肉眼者，說言降魔，不為修學大乘人說。聲聞之人雖有天眼，故名肉眼。學大乘者，雖有肉眼乃名佛眼。何以故？是大乘經名為佛乘，而此佛乘最上最勝。善男子！譬如有人勇健威猛，有怯弱者常來依附。其勇健人常教怯者，汝當如是持弓執箭，修學矟道、長鉤、羂索。又復告言：『夫鬪戰者，雖如履刃，不應生於怖畏之想，當視人天生輕弱想，應自生心作勇健想。或時有人素無膽勇，詐作健相，執持弓刀、種種器仗，以自莊嚴，來至陣中，唱呼大喚。汝於是人，亦復不應生於憂怖。如是輩人，若見汝時不怖畏者，當知是人不久散壞，如彼偷狗。』善男子！如來亦爾，告諸聲聞：『汝等不應畏魔波旬，若魔波旬化作佛身，至汝所者，汝當精勤堅固其心，降伏於魔。時魔即當愁憂不樂，復道而去。』善男子！如彼健人不從他習，學大乘者亦復如是，得聞種種深密經典，其心欣樂不生驚怖。何以故？如是修學大乘之人，已曾供養、恭敬、禮拜過去無量萬億佛故，雖有無量億千魔眾欲來侵嬈，於是事中終不驚畏。善男子！譬如有人得阿竭陀藥，不畏一切。毒蛇等畏是藥力故，亦能消除一切毒等。是大乘經亦復如是，如彼藥力不畏一切諸魔毒等，亦能降伏令更不起。」

「復次善男子！譬如有龍，性甚妬憋，欲害人時，或以眼視，或以氣噓，是故一切師子、虎豹、豺狼、狗犬皆生怖畏，是等惡獸，或聞聲見形，或觸其身，無不喪命。有善呪者以呪力故，能令如是諸惡毒龍、金翅鳥等、惡象師子、虎豹豺狼，皆悉調善，任為御乘。如是等獸，見彼善呪，即便調伏。聲聞緣覺亦復如是，見魔波旬皆生恐怖，而魔波旬亦復不生畏懼之心，猶行魔業。學大乘者亦復如是，見諸聲聞怖畏魔事，於此大乘不生信樂，先以方便降伏諸魔，悉令調善堪任為乘，因為廣說種種妙法。聲聞緣覺見調魔已，不生怖畏，於此大乘無上正法，方生信樂，作如是言：『我等從今不應於此正法之中而作障礙。』」

「復次善男子！聲聞緣覺於諸煩惱而生怖畏，學大乘者都無恐懼，修學大乘有如是力。以是因緣先所說者，為欲令彼聲聞緣覺調伏諸魔，非為大乘。是大涅槃微妙經典，不可消伏，甚奇甚特。若有聞者，聞已信受，能信如來是常住法，如是之人甚為希有，如優曇花。我涅槃後，若有得聞如是大乘微妙經典，生信敬心，當知是等於未來世百千億劫，不墮惡道。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I do not now rely on these four kinds of people. Why is this? In the Gośāla Sūtra, the Buddha taught the elder Gośāla: 'Should the heavenly Māra or Brahmā, wishing to cause harm, transform himself into an image of the Buddha, complete with the thirty-two marks and eighty minor characteristics, a halo a fathom wide, a face full and radiant as the moon at its fullest, and the white, snow-surpassing curl of hair between the brows, and come toward you adorned in this way, you should examine him closely to determine whether he is genuine or false, and, once you have recognized him, subdue him.' World-Honored One, if Māra can even transform himself into the Buddha's own body, how could he not also be able to take on the bodies of these four kinds of people — sitting or lying in open space, water flowing from his left side, fire from his right, smoke and flame pouring from his whole body like a blazing mass? For this very reason, I cannot give rise to faith in this teaching; whatever is taught in this way, I am unable to accept, and hold no reverence for it as something to rely upon."

The Buddha said: "Good son, if you can doubt even what I myself have taught, how much more should you doubt something like this? For this very reason you should skillfully distinguish and know what is wholesome from what is not, what should be done from what should not be done, and, having acted accordingly, you will find lasting ease through the long night ahead. Good son, it is like a thieving dog that slips into someone's house at night; if the household maid notices it, she should at once drive it out, scolding it: 'Get out at once — if you do not leave, I will take your life.' Hearing this, the thieving dog flees at once and never returns. From this day forward, you should subdue Pāpīyas in just the same way, saying to him: 'Pāpīyas, you should not take on such a form; should you persist in doing so, I will bind you with the five bonds.' Hearing this, Māra will turn back at once, just like that thieving dog, never to return again."

Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if, as the Buddha taught the elder Gośāla, one who is able to subdue Māra in this way can thereby draw near this great parinirvāṇa on his own, why does the Tathāgata still need to name these four kinds of people as a refuge to rely upon at all? What these four kinds of people say cannot necessarily be trusted."

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "Good son, what I taught there is just the same as what I teach now — it is not otherwise. Good son, I taught the subduing of Māra for the sake of śrāvakas, those possessing only the fleshly eye, not for the sake of those who study the Great Vehicle. Though a śrāvaka may possess the heavenly eye, it is still called only the fleshly eye; but one who studies the Great Vehicle, though possessing only the fleshly eye, may still be said to possess the buddha-eye. Why is this? Because the scriptures of the Great Vehicle are themselves called the buddha-vehicle, and this buddha-vehicle is the highest and most excellent of all. Good son, it is like a person of great courage and might, whom the timid constantly come to rely upon; this courageous person constantly teaches the timid, saying: 'You should learn to draw the bow and loose the arrow in this way, and train in the use of the spear, the long hook, and the snare.' And he says further: 'In battle, though it may feel like walking on a blade's edge, you should not give rise to any thought of fear — you should regard both gods and humans as insubstantial and weak, and give rise instead to a mind of your own courage. Sometimes a person who has, at heart, no real courage at all will falsely put on a show of strength, arming himself with bow, sword, and every kind of weapon, and come shouting loudly onto the field of battle — but toward such a person too, you should not give rise to any anxiety or fear. If such a person sees that you show no fear before him, know that he will soon fall apart, just like that thieving dog.' Good son, the Tathāgata is just the same: I say to the śrāvakas, 'You should not fear Māra Pāpīyas; should Māra Pāpīyas transform himself into the Buddha's own body and come to you, you should diligently steady your own mind and subdue him — Māra will then grow sorrowful and displeased, and turn back the way he came.' Good son, just as that courageous person needs no further instruction from anyone else, one who studies the Great Vehicle is just the same: on hearing every kind of profound and secret scripture, his heart delights in it and gives rise to no alarm at all. Why is this? Because one who studies the Great Vehicle in this way has already made offerings to, revered, and paid homage to countless millions of buddhas in ages past; though countless millions of hosts of māras might come to assail him, he remains, in every such matter, entirely without alarm or fear. Good son, it is like a person who has obtained the agada medicine, and fears nothing at all — every venomous serpent fears the power of this medicine, which is also able to dissolve every poison entirely. The scriptures of the Great Vehicle are just the same: like the power of that medicine, they fear no poison of any māra at all, and are able to subdue every māra so that it never rises again."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a dragon of an exceedingly jealous, malicious nature, who, wishing to harm a person, need only look at him or breathe upon him — and every lion, tiger, leopard, wolf, and dog is filled with terror at this dragon, such fierce beasts losing their lives the moment they hear its voice, see its form, or are touched by it. Yet one skilled in mantra, through the power of that mantra, is able to render even such venomous dragons, garuḍas, fierce elephants, lions, tigers, leopards, and wolves entirely tame, fit to be ridden and driven at will — such beasts, on encountering that skilled mantra, are subdued at once. The śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas are just the same: on seeing Māra Pāpīyas, they are all filled with terror, while Māra Pāpīyas himself feels no fear at all, and continues on with the business of Māra undisturbed. One who studies the Great Vehicle is just the same as that mantra-adept: seeing the śrāvakas terrified of Māra's own doings, and unable to give rise to faith and delight in the Great Vehicle because of it, he first subdues every māra through skillful means, rendering them all tame and fit to be ridden, and only then expounds every kind of wondrous Dharma at length. Once the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas see that Māra has been subdued, and no longer feel any fear, they at last give rise to faith and delight in this peerless true Dharma of the Great Vehicle, saying to themselves: 'From this day forward, we should not become an obstacle within this true Dharma.'"

"Furthermore, good son, the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas are filled with fear before every affliction, while one who studies the Great Vehicle has no fear at all — such is the power that studying the Great Vehicle brings. It was for this very reason that what I taught before was meant to help the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas subdue māras, and was not meant for the Great Vehicle at all. This subtle and profound scripture of the great nirvāṇa cannot be dissolved or subdued by anything — it is exceedingly rare and wondrous. Whoever hears it, and, having heard it, believes and accepts it, coming to believe that the Tathāgata is the permanently abiding Dharma, is a person exceedingly rare, like the udumbara flower. Should anyone, after my parinirvāṇa, come to hear this subtle and profound scripture of the Great Vehicle and give rise to a mind of faith and reverence, know that such a person will not fall into any evil destiny for a hundred thousand million kalpas to come."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Kāśyapa's objection as pressing a genuine inconsistency the Buddha's own earlier teaching seems to invite: if the Gośāla Sūtra already taught that even the Buddha's own appearance can be counterfeited by Māra, and that the remedy is simply for the practitioner to examine and subdue the impostor directly, why does this sūtra now ask listeners to place their trust in four intermediary figures at all, rather than simply teaching everyone the same self-reliant test? Huiyuan's gloss on the Buddha's answer turns on a distinction between audiences rather than a retraction: the earlier teaching to Gośāla was pitched at śrāvakas — "those possessing only the fleshly eye" — for whom the danger of Māra's counterfeit is real and confidence has to be built through direct confrontation and testing, while students of the Great Vehicle operate from an entirely different footing, one where fear of Māra has already been outgrown through the accumulated merit of longstanding devotion, so that the four reliances are not a hedge against uncertainty for this audience but simply a mark of accomplished trust already secured elsewhere. The dragon-tamer simile Huiyuan reads as clarifying the actual sequence implied: the Great Vehicle practitioner is not exempt from ever facing Māra, but subdues him first, before ever needing the śrāvaka's confidence-building test at all — which is why the śrāvakas' own fear, witnessed and then resolved by watching the bodhisattva succeed, becomes itself the bridge that finally lets them trust the Great Vehicle teaching they had been unable to accept on its own terms.

Forty Years, Then Hidden in the Earth

Chinese Source

爾時佛告迦葉菩薩：「善男子！我涅槃後當有百千無量眾生，誹謗不信是大涅槃微妙經典。」迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！是諸眾生於佛滅後，久近便當誹謗是經？世尊！復有何等純善眾生，當能拔濟是謗法者？」

「爾時有一婆羅門子，年在弱冠，修治淨行，長髮為相，善知呪術，往至王所，白言：『大王！王所勅使，我悉能為。』爾時大王心生歡喜，受此童子作灌頂師。諸婆羅門聞是事已，皆生瞋恚，責此童子：『汝婆羅門，云何乃作旃陀羅師？』爾時其王，即分半國與是童子，因共治國，經歷多時。爾時童子語其王言：『我捨家法，來作王師，然教大王微密呪術，而今大王，猶不見親。』時王答言：『我今云何不親汝耶？』童子答言：『先王所有不死之藥，猶未共食。』王言：『善哉，善哉！大師！我實不知。師若須者，唯願持去。』是時童子，聞王語已，即取歸家，請諸大臣而共食之。諸臣食已，即共白王：『快哉，大師！有是甘露不死之藥。』王既知已，語其師言：『云何大師獨與諸臣服食甘露，而不見分？』爾時童子，更以其餘雜毒之藥，與王令服。王既服已，須臾藥發，悶亂躄地，無所覺知，猶如死人。爾時童子立本儲君，還以為王，作如是言：『師子御座，法不應令旃陀羅昇。我從昔來，未曾聞見旃陀羅種而為王也，若旃陀羅治國理民，無有是處。汝今應還，紹繼先王，正法治國。』爾時童子經理是已，復以解藥與旃陀羅，令其醒寤，既醒寤已，驅令出國。是時童子雖為是事，猶故不失婆羅門法，其餘居士、婆羅門等，聞其所作歎未曾有，讚言：『善哉，善哉！仁者！善能驅遣旃陀羅王。』」

「善男子！我涅槃後，護持正法諸菩薩等，亦復如是，以方便力，與彼破戒假名、受畜一切不淨物僧，同其事業。爾時菩薩若見有人雖多犯戒，能治毀禁諸惡比丘，即往其所，恭敬禮拜，四事供養，經書什物悉以奉上；如其自無，要當方便，從諸檀越求覓而與。為是事故，應畜八種不淨之物。何以故？是人為治諸惡比丘，如彼童子驅旃陀羅。爾時菩薩雖復恭敬禮拜是人，受畜八種不淨之物，悉無有罪。何以故？以是菩薩為欲擯治諸惡比丘，令清淨僧得安隱住，流布方等大乘經典，利益一切諸天人故。善男子！以是因緣，我於經中說是二偈，令諸菩薩皆共讚嘆護法之人，如彼居士婆羅門等稱讚童子：『善哉，善哉。』護法菩薩正應如是，若有人見護法之人，與破戒者同其事業，說有罪者，當知其人自受其殃。是護法者實無有罪。善男子！若有比丘犯禁戒已，憍慢心故，覆藏不悔，當知是人名真破戒。菩薩摩訶薩為護法故，雖有所犯不名破戒。何以故？以無憍慢，發露悔故。」

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如是等菩薩摩訶薩，於戒極緩，本所受戒為具在不？」佛言：「善男子！汝今不應作如是說。何以故？本所受戒，如本不失；設有所犯，即應懺悔，悔已清淨。善男子！如故堤塘，穿穴有孔，水則淋漏。何以故？無人治故。若有人治，水則不出。菩薩亦爾，雖與破戒共作布薩，受戒自恣，同其僧事。所有戒律，不如堤塘穿穴淋漏。何以故？若無清淨持戒之人，僧則損減，慢緩懈怠日有增長。若有清淨持戒之人，即能具足不失本戒。善男子！於乘緩者乃名為緩，於戒緩者不名為緩。菩薩摩訶薩於此大乘，心不懈慢，是名本戒。為護正法，以大乘水而自澡浴，是故菩薩雖現破戒，不名為緩。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, offering to one's teachers and elders should indeed be as the Buddha has taught. But I now have a doubt, and beg only that you explain it at length. If a senior monk who has upheld the precepts learns something he has not heard before from a junior monk, should he then bow to that junior monk? If he should, then he could hardly be called one who keeps the precepts. And if a junior monk who upholds the precepts learns something he has not heard before from a senior monk who has broken the precepts, should he then bow to him? And if a renunciant learns something he has not heard before from a layperson, should the renunciant then bow to the layperson? Yet a renunciant should not bow to a layperson at all. And within the Buddha's own teaching, the young should revere the old and senior, since these elders received the full precepts first and are complete in their bearing, and so should be honored and revered. Yet the Buddha has said: 'One who breaks the precepts has no place at all within the Buddha's own teaching, just as a fine field can still be overrun with weeds' — and yet the Buddha has also said: 'Whoever knows the Dharma, whether old or young, should for this reason be offered to, as one honors Śakra.' What is the meaning of these two statements together? Could the Tathāgata be speaking falsely? The Buddha has also said: 'Even a precept-keeping monk may still commit some transgression' — why does the Tathāgata say this? The World-Honored One has also, in other scriptures, spoken of permitting the correction of those who break the precepts — the meaning of all this remains unclear to me."

The Buddha said to Kāśyapa: "Good son, I spoke that verse for the sake of future bodhisattvas who study the Great Vehicle, not for the sake of śrāvaka disciples. Good son, as I have said before, at the time when the true Dharma is being destroyed, when the true precepts are being violated, when the breaking of precepts is on the rise, when what is contrary to the Dharma flourishes, when every true sage has withdrawn from view, and when the keeping of servants and other improper possessions has become common — at just such a time, one among these four kinds of people will appear in the world, shaving his head and beard, going forth to cultivate the path. Seeing that the monks everywhere keep servants, attendants, and other improper possessions, unable to tell pure from impure or to distinguish what accords with the Vinaya from what does not, this person, wishing to bring these monks under control, blends his own light with theirs without mixing with their dust — knowing well, within himself, the difference between his own true conduct and the Buddha's own conduct. Though he sees others commit even the gravest offenses, he remains silent and does not expose them. Why is this? Because he has appeared in the world in order to establish and protect the true Dharma, and it is for this reason that he remains silent and does not bring such charges. Good son, though such a person may appear to transgress for the sake of protecting the Dharma, this cannot be called breaking the precepts."

"Good son, it is like a king who falls ill and dies, leaving behind only a young, still immature crown prince, not yet fit to succeed him. There is a caṇḍāla, immensely wealthy, possessed of countless riches and a great retinue, who, relying on his own strength, waits for the kingdom to grow weak and then usurps the throne for himself. Before he has ruled for long, the people of the kingdom, the householders, and the brāhmaṇas flee in rebellion, escaping far into other lands; even those who remain can hardly bear even to look upon this king. Some elders and brāhmaṇas, unable to leave their own native soil, remain — like trees that, once rooted in a place, must also die there. Realizing that so many of his people have fled in rebellion, the caṇḍāla king at once dispatches his own caṇḍāla kinsmen to guard every road, and, after seven days, has drums beaten and a proclamation made: 'Whichever brāhmaṇa is able to serve as my own consecrating priest shall be rewarded with half the kingdom.' On hearing this proclamation, not a single brāhmaṇa comes forward, each saying to himself: 'What brāhmaṇa anywhere could possibly do such a thing?' The caṇḍāla king then proclaims further: 'If not one among the brāhmaṇas will come to serve as my priest, I will see to it that every brāhmaṇa is made to dwell, eat, and sleep together with caṇḍālas, sharing in their very trade; but whoever comes to consecrate me, I promise, without fail, half the kingdom as his reward — and we shall share together, too, in the deathless nectar of the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven that my own spells can summon.'"

"At that time there was a young brahmin, still barely of age, of pure conduct, his hair grown long as a mark of his practice, well versed in the mantric arts, who came before the king and said: 'Great king, whatever you command, I am able to do.' The king, filled with joy, accepted this youth as his consecrating priest. On hearing of this, the brāhmaṇas were filled with fury, and rebuked the youth: 'You are a brāhmaṇa — how could you become a caṇḍāla's own priest?' The king then divided half his kingdom and gave it to the youth, and the two of them ruled the kingdom together for a long time after. The youth then said to the king: 'I gave up the brahmanical way of life to become your royal priest, teaching you the subtle and secret mantric arts — and yet, even now, you still do not treat me as someone close to you.' The king answered: 'How could I not treat you as someone close to me?' The youth replied: 'The deathless medicine the former king possessed — we have still never shared it together.' The king said: 'Excellent, excellent, great teacher! I truly did not know of this. If you need it, please, take it for yourself.' On hearing the king's words, the youth took the medicine home at once, and invited the king's own ministers to share it with him. Once the ministers had eaten it, they said together to the king: 'How wonderful, great teacher, to have this nectar of deathlessness!' Hearing this, the king said to his teacher: 'Why does the great teacher share this nectar only with the ministers, and not with me?' The youth then gave the king a different medicine instead, mixed with poison, and had him take it. No sooner had the king swallowed it than the poison took effect, and he collapsed to the ground in confusion, unconscious, as though dead. The youth then installed the former crown prince as king once more, saying: 'The lion's own throne should never, by rights, be mounted by a caṇḍāla. Never before, from ancient times to now, have I heard of any caṇḍāla becoming a king — for a caṇḍāla to govern a kingdom and rule its people could not possibly be right. You should now return to your rightful place, succeeding your former king, and rule the kingdom with the true Dharma.' Having set this right, the youth then gave the caṇḍāla an antidote, reviving him, and, once revived, had him driven out of the kingdom entirely. Though the youth had done all this, he never once lost his own brahmanical conduct throughout; the rest of the householders and brāhmaṇas, hearing of what he had done, marveled at what they had never seen before, and praised him, saying: 'Excellent, excellent, noble one! How skillfully you have driven out the caṇḍāla king!'"

"Good son, the bodhisattvas who protect the true Dharma after my parinirvāṇa are just the same: through skillful means, they take part in the very same affairs as those monks who, in name only, have broken the precepts and keep every kind of improper possession. Should such a bodhisattva see someone who, though he has broken many precepts himself, is nonetheless able to bring wicked, precept-breaking monks under control, that bodhisattva will go to him, bow in reverence, and make the four kinds of offering, presenting him with scriptures, books, and every useful implement; and should the bodhisattva himself have none of these to give, he will use skillful means to seek them out from donors and provide them all the same. For this very purpose he may keep the eight kinds of improper possession. Why is this? Because this person is working to bring wicked monks under control, just as that youth drove out the caṇḍāla. Though such a bodhisattva bows in reverence to this person, and keeps and uses the eight kinds of improper possession, he incurs no fault at all in doing so. Why is this? Because this bodhisattva does so in order to expel and correct wicked monks, so that the pure saṅgha may dwell in peace, so that the scriptures of the Great Vehicle may circulate widely, and for the benefit of every god and human being. Good son, it is for this very reason that I spoke, within this sūtra, the two verses causing every bodhisattva to praise together those who protect the Dharma, just as those householders and brāhmaṇas praised the youth, saying: 'Excellent, excellent.' A bodhisattva who protects the Dharma should act in just this way. Should anyone see a protector of the Dharma taking part in the same affairs as those who have broken the precepts, and declare that person guilty of some fault, know that it is that very accuser who brings misfortune upon himself — the true protector of the Dharma is, in fact, entirely without fault. Good son, if a monk breaks the precepts and, out of arrogance, conceals it without repenting, know that such a person truly breaks the precepts. A bodhisattva-mahāsattva who, for the sake of protecting the Dharma, appears to transgress cannot be said to break the precepts at all — why is this? Because he holds no arrogance, and confesses and repents openly."

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, are such bodhisattva-mahāsattvas, so extremely lax in their observance of the precepts, still complete in the precepts they originally received?" The Buddha said: "Good son, you should not speak this way. Why is this? The precepts one has originally received remain just as they were from the start; should any transgression occur, one need only confess and repent, and, having repented, become pure once more. Good son, it is like an old embankment, worn through with holes, through which water leaks and drains away — this is only because no one repairs it; were someone to repair it, the water would no longer escape. A bodhisattva is just the same: though he joins in the poṣadha, the receiving and confession of precepts, and the general business of the saṅgha together with those who have broken the precepts, his own moral conduct is not thereby worn through and leaking away like that embankment. Why is this? Because if there were no one of pure precept-keeping present at all, the saṅgha would decline, and laxity and heedlessness would only grow day by day; but where there is a person of pure precept-keeping present, the precepts remain complete and unlost. Good son, laxity toward the Vehicle is properly called laxity; laxity toward the precepts, in this case, is not properly called laxity at all. A bodhisattva-mahāsattva whose mind holds no laxity or heedlessness toward this Great Vehicle is said to keep his original precepts intact. Bathing himself, for the sake of protecting the true Dharma, in the very waters of the Great Vehicle, such a bodhisattva, though he may appear to break the precepts, cannot be called lax at all."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the caṇḍāla-king parable through a point-by-point correspondence he lays out with unusual precision: the dying king and his too-young heir stand for the Buddha's own passing and the still-undeveloped state of ordinary practitioners left behind; the caṇḍāla usurper stands for corrupt monks who exploit the resulting vacuum to seize communal authority for themselves; the young brahmin who apparently defects to serve the usurper, accepts half the kingdom, and shares in every privilege of that court, stands for the bodhisattva-protector who "blends his light with theirs" precisely so as to gain the position from which he alone can act; and the poisoned nectar — offered as a shared delicacy, withheld once, and then substituted for a fatal dose the moment the king insists on equal treatment — stands for the precise, calculated act of discipline the bodhisattva has been working the entire time to make possible. Huiyuan's sharpest observation concerns what the parable withholds until its very end: nothing in the youth's outward conduct up to the moment of poisoning would let an observer distinguish him from a genuine collaborator, and it is only the final restoration of the rightful heir that retroactively reveals every earlier act of apparent complicity as instrumental rather than sincere. This is, on his reading, the parable's entire point about the bodhisattva's own precept-breaking: judged action by action, in real time, apparent transgression for the sake of the Dharma is indistinguishable from actual transgression, and can only be told apart by its outcome and by the confessed, unashamed intent behind it — which is exactly why Kāśyapa's own next question, whether such a bodhisattva's original precepts remain intact, cannot be settled by pointing to any single observable act, and has to be settled instead by the embankment simile's own distinction between a structure that leaks because no one repairs it and one that never needed repairing in the first place.

「復次善男子！如迦羅迦林，其樹眾多，於是林中唯有一樹，名鎮頭迦。是迦羅迦樹、鎮頭迦樹，二果相似，不可分別。其果熟時，有一女人，悉皆拾取，鎮頭迦果纔有一分，迦羅迦果乃有十分。是女不識，齎來詣市而衒賣之。凡愚小兒，復不別故，買迦羅迦果，噉已命終。有智人輩，聞是事已，即問女人：『姊於何處持是果來？』是時女人即示方所，諸人即言：『如是方所，多有無量迦羅迦樹，唯有一根鎮頭迦樹。』諸人知已，笑而捨去。」

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "Among the assembly of monks there are these four kinds of people, hard to tell apart, like a mango fruit whose ripeness cannot be judged from outside — how, then, can one tell precept-breaking from precept-keeping?" The Buddha said: "Good son, this can be known easily by relying on this subtle and profound scripture of the great nirvāṇa. How can it be known through this great nirvāṇa sūtra? It is like a farmer who sows rice and other grain, and weeds out the barnyard grass — to the ordinary eye, the field looks clean and pure, but once the crop has ripened, the true grain and the weeds show themselves as entirely distinct. It is the same with these eight practices that defile the saṅgha: were they removed, the ordinary eye would see purity at once. Whether a monk keeps or breaks the precepts is hard to tell by the ordinary eye so long as no wrongdoing has come to light — but once wrongdoing is exposed, it becomes easy to recognize, just as the barnyard grass becomes easy to distinguish once it has grown. It is the same within the saṅgha: whoever is able to keep far from these eight impure, poisonous-serpent practices is called a pure and holy field of merit, worthy of offerings from gods and humans alike — but this pure result cannot be discerned by the ordinary eye."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a forest of kalaka trees, among which stands a single tinduka tree. The fruit of the kalaka and the fruit of the tinduka closely resemble each other and cannot be told apart. When the fruit ripens, a woman gathers it all together — for every portion of tinduka fruit there are ten portions of kalaka fruit — and, unable to tell the two apart, carries it to market to sell. Some foolish, ignorant children, likewise unable to distinguish them, buy the kalaka fruit, eat it, and die. On hearing of this, some wise people ask the woman: 'Sister, where did you gather this fruit?' The woman shows them the place, and the people say to one another: 'In that place there are countless kalaka trees, and only a single tinduka tree.' Having learned this, they laugh and walk away."

"Good son, the eight impure practices within the great assembly are just the same: within this assembly there are many who indulge in these eight practices, and only a single person of pure precept-keeping who refuses these eight impure practices, though he knows well that the others keep improper possessions, and yet does not separate himself from their company — just like that single tinduka tree standing amid the kalaka forest. Suppose there is an upāsaka who, seeing that many in this assembly engage in improper conduct, refuses to show reverence or make offerings to any of them; before making any offering, he should first ask: 'Venerable ones, do you keep and use these eight kinds of possession? Did the Buddha permit this? And if the Buddha did permit it, are such people still permitted to join in the poṣadha and the confession ceremony together with the rest of the saṅgha?' Once this upāsaka has asked this, suppose the whole assembly answers: 'The Tathāgata, out of compassion, permits all eight of these practices.' The upāsaka should then say: 'At the Jetavana monastery there were monks who said the Buddha permitted keeping gold and silver, and others who said he did not — and those who said it was permitted were not allowed to dwell together with those who said it was not, nor to join them in the recitation of precepts or the confession ceremony, not even to drink from the same river, nor to share in any of the same offerings. How, then, can you all now say the Buddha permitted this?' Even were the Buddha, that god among gods, himself to accept such things, you, the ordinary saṅgha, should still not keep them. Whoever does keep them should not be allowed to join in the recitation of precepts, the confession ceremony, or any business of the saṅgha at all; and whoever does join with them in these things will, upon death, fall straight into hell — just like those people who ate the kalaka fruit and died."

"Furthermore, good son, it is like a city marketplace where a medicine-seller offers a fine, sweet medicine that comes from the Himalayas, but also sells, alongside it, many other, inferior medicines whose taste closely resembles it. People wishing to buy this fine medicine, unable to tell it apart from the rest, go to the seller and ask: 'Do you have the medicine from the Himalayas?' The seller answers that he does — but, being a cheat, hands the buyer some other, inferior medicine instead, saying: 'This is the fine, sweet medicine from the Himalayas.' The buyer, unable to judge for himself by the ordinary eye, buys it and takes it away, thinking to himself: 'I have now obtained the fine medicine from the Himalayas.' Kāśyapa, it is the same within the śrāvaka saṅgha: there is the saṅgha in name only, the truly genuine saṅgha, and the saṅgha as an undifferentiated whole — whether keeping or breaking the precepts, all should equally receive offerings, reverence, and homage from an upāsaka unable to tell them apart by the ordinary eye, just as that buyer could not distinguish the true Himalayan medicine from the rest. Who truly keeps the precepts, and who breaks them? Which is the true saṅgha, and which only bears its name? Only one possessed of the heavenly eye is able to tell them apart. Kāśyapa, if an upāsaka knows for certain that a given monk has broken the precepts, he should not give offerings, homage, or reverence to that monk; and if he knows that this person keeps and uses the eight kinds of improper possession, he should likewise not provide for his needs or show him homage or reverence. Wherever there is one who has broken the precepts within the saṅgha, one should not show him reverence or homage merely because he wears the monastic robe."

The Four Reliances

Chinese Source

迦葉菩薩復白佛言：「世尊！善哉，善哉。如來所說真實不虛，我當頂受。譬如金剛，珍寶異物，如佛所說，是諸比丘當依四法。何等為四？依法不依人，依義不依語，依智不依識，依了義經不依不了義經。如是四法，應當證知非四種人。」

佛言：「善男子！依法者，即是如來大般涅槃，一切佛法即是法性，是法性者即是如來，是故如來常住不變。若復有言如來無常，是人不知、不見法性。若不知見是法性者，不應依止。如上所說，四人出世護持法者，應當證知而為依止。何以故？是人善解如來微密深奧藏故，能知如來常住不變，若言如來無常變易，無有是處。如是四人即名如來。何以故？是人能解如來密語及能說故。若有人能了知如來甚深密藏，及知如來常住不變，如是之人，若為利養說言如來是無常者，無有是處。如是之人尚可依止，何況不依是四人也？依法者即是法性，不依人者即是聲聞，法性者即是如來，聲聞者即是有為，如來者，即是常住，有為者即是無常。善男子！若人破戒為利養故，說言如來無常變易，如是之人所不應依。善男子！是名定義。」

「依義不依語者，義者名曰覺了，覺了義者名不羸劣，不羸劣者名曰滿足，滿足義者名曰如來常住不變、如來常住不變義者即是法常，法常義者即是僧常，是名依義不依語也。何等語言所不應依？所謂諸論、綺飾文辭。如佛所說無量諸經，貪求無厭，多姦諛諂，詐現親附，現相求利，經理白衣為其執役，又復唱言：『佛聽比丘畜諸奴婢、不淨之物，金銀珍寶、穀米倉庫、牛羊象馬，販賣求利，於飢饉世憐愍子故，聽諸比丘儲貯陳宿，手自作食，不受而噉。』如是等語所不應依。」

「依智不依識者，所言智者即是如來。若有聲聞，不能善知如來功德，如是之識不應依止。若知如來即是法身，如是真智所應依止。若見如來方便之身，言是陰界諸入所攝，食所長養，亦不應依，是故知識不可依止。若復有人作是說者，及其經書亦不應依。」

「依了義經，不依不了義經。不了義經者，謂聲聞乘，聞佛如來深密藏處，悉生疑怪，不知是藏出大智海，猶如嬰兒無所別知，是則名為不了義也。了義者名為菩薩，真實智慧隨於自心，無礙大智，猶如大人無所不知，是名了義。又聲聞乘名不了義；無上大乘乃名了義。若言如來無常變易名不了義；若言如來常住不變是名了義。聲聞所說應證知者名不了義；菩薩所說應證知者名為了義。若言如來食所長養，是不了義；若言常住不變易者，是名了義。若言如來入於涅槃，如薪盡火滅，名不了義；若言如來入法性者，是名了義。聲聞乘法則不應依。何以故？如來為欲度眾生故，以方便力說聲聞乘，猶如長者教子半字。善男子！聲聞乘者猶如初耕，未得果實，如是名為不了義也，是故不應依聲聞乘。大乘之法則應依止。何以故？如來為欲度眾生故，以方便力說於大乘，是故應依，是名了義。如是四依應當證知。」

「復次依義者，義名質直，質直者名曰光明，光明者名不羸劣，不羸劣者名曰如來。又光明者名為智慧，質直者名為常住，如來常者名為依法，法者名常，亦名無邊、不可思議、不可執持、不可繫縛而亦可見；若有說言不可見者，如是之人所不應依，是故依法不依於人。若有人以微妙之語，宣說無常，如是之言所不應依，是故依義不依於語。依智者，眾僧是常，無為不變，不畜八種不淨之物，是故依智不依於識。若有說言，識作識受，無和合僧。何以故？夫和合者名無所有，無所有者云何言常？是故此識不可依止。依了義者，了義者名為知足，終不詐現威儀清白、憍慢自高，貪求利養，亦於如來隨宜方便所說法中不生執著，是名了義。若有能住如是等中，當知是人，則為已得住第一義，是故名為依了義經。」

「不依不了義，不了義者如經中說，一切燒燃、一切無常、一切皆苦、一切皆空、一切無我，是名不了義。何以故？以不能了如是義故，令諸眾生墮阿鼻獄。所以者何？以取著故，於義不了。一切燒者，謂如來說涅槃亦燒；一切無常者，涅槃亦無常；苦空無我，亦復如是。是故名為不了義經，不應依止。善男子！若有人言如來憐愍一切眾生，善知時宜，以知時故，說輕為重，說重為輕。如來觀知所有弟子，有諸檀越供給所須、令無所乏，如是之人，佛則不聽受畜奴婢、金銀財寶、販賣市易、不淨物等。若諸弟子無有檀越供給所須，時世飢饉，飲食難得，為欲建立護持正法，我聽弟子受畜奴婢、金銀車乘、田宅穀米、賣易所須，雖聽受畜如是等物，要當淨施篤信檀越。如是四法所應依止。若有戒律、阿毘曇、修多羅不違是四，亦應依止。若有說言，有時非時，有能護法不能護法，如來悉聽一切比丘，受畜如是不淨物者，如是之言不應依止。若有戒律、阿毘曇、修多羅中，有同是說，如是三分亦不應依。我為肉眼諸眾生等，說是四依，終不為於有慧眼者。是故我今說是四依，法者即是法性，義者即是如來常住不變，智者了知一切眾生悉有佛性，了義者了達一切大乘經典。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said again to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, excellent, excellent! What the Tathāgata has taught is true and without falsehood — I shall receive it with my whole heart. It is like a treasure of vajra, a jewel beyond any other. As the Buddha has taught, monks should rely on these four things. What are the four? Rely on the Dharma, not on the person; rely on the meaning, not on the words; rely on wisdom, not on ordinary consciousness; rely on the definitive teaching, not on the provisional teaching. One should verify these four principles, and not simply rely on any of the four kinds of people directly."

The Buddha said: "Good son, relying on the Dharma means relying on the Tathāgata's own great parinirvāṇa. Every dharma taught by every buddha is itself the Dharma-nature, and this Dharma-nature is itself the Tathāgata — this is why the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change. Whoever says the Tathāgata is impermanent neither knows nor sees this Dharma-nature; and whoever neither knows nor sees this Dharma-nature should not be relied upon. As I have said above, the four kinds of people who appear in the world to protect the Dharma should be recognized and relied upon — why is this? Because such a person well understands the Tathāgata's own subtle and profound treasury, and is able to know that the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change; for such a person to say the Tathāgata is impermanent or subject to change could not possibly be so. These four kinds of people are themselves rightly called the Tathāgata — why is this? Because such a person is able both to understand the Tathāgata's own hidden meaning and to teach it in turn. Whoever is truly able to understand the Tathāgata's own profound and secret treasury, and to know that the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change, could never, for the sake of personal gain, say that the Tathāgata is impermanent. Such a person can indeed be relied upon — how much more, then, should these four kinds of people be relied upon? Relying on the Dharma means relying on the Dharma-nature; not relying on the person means not relying on the śrāvaka. The Dharma-nature is itself the Tathāgata; the śrāvaka is himself a conditioned thing. The Tathāgata is permanently abiding; the conditioned is impermanent. Good son, whoever breaks the precepts and, for the sake of personal gain, says the Tathāgata is impermanent and subject to change, should not be relied upon at all. Good son, this is what is meant by the settled meaning of reliance on the Dharma."

"Relying on the meaning, not on the words, means this: meaning here means full comprehension; full comprehension means being free of weakness; being free of weakness means being complete; being complete means that the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change; the Tathāgata's permanent abiding, without change, means that the Dharma is permanent; and the Dharma's permanence means that the Saṅgha is permanent — this is what is meant by relying on the meaning, not on the words. What kind of words, then, should not be relied upon? Namely, elaborate treatises and ornately decorated language. It is like when someone, having heard the Buddha's own countless scriptures, becomes insatiably greedy, deceitful, and flattering, falsely feigning closeness with others, cultivating appearances in order to seek personal gain, managing lay affairs and putting laypeople to work for himself, and further proclaiming: 'The Buddha permits monks to keep servants and other improper possessions, gold, silver, and treasures, granaries of grain, cattle, sheep, elephants, and horses, buying and selling for profit; and, out of compassion for his disciples in times of famine, the Buddha permits monks to stockpile provisions and cook for themselves, eating food not formally offered to them.' Words such as these should not be relied upon at all."

"Relying on wisdom, not on ordinary consciousness, means this: what is called wisdom here is itself the Tathāgata. If there is a śrāvaka who is unable to properly understand the Tathāgata's own virtues, that person's own ordinary consciousness should not be relied upon. Whoever understands that the Tathāgata is himself the Dharma-body — that person's true wisdom should be relied upon. Whoever, seeing only the Tathāgata's own skillfully manifested body, says that it is subsumed under the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields, and nourished by food, should likewise not be relied upon — this is why ordinary consciousness cannot be relied upon. And should anyone teach in this way, their scriptures and writings should likewise not be relied upon."

"Relying on the definitive teaching, not on the provisional teaching, means this: the provisional teaching refers to the śrāvaka vehicle, which, on hearing of the Tathāgata's own profound and secret treasury, gives rise only to doubt and bewilderment, unaware that this treasury issues from the great ocean of wisdom itself — like an infant, unable to discern anything at all. This is what is meant by the provisional teaching. The definitive teaching belongs to the bodhisattva, whose true wisdom follows freely from his own mind, an unobstructed great wisdom, like a fully grown person who lacks nothing in understanding — this is what is meant by the definitive teaching. The śrāvaka vehicle, again, is called the provisional teaching; the peerless Great Vehicle alone is called the definitive teaching. To say the Tathāgata is impermanent and subject to change is the provisional teaching; to say the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change, is the definitive teaching. What the śrāvakas teach still needs to be further verified — this is the provisional teaching; what the bodhisattvas teach needs no further verification — this is the definitive teaching. To say the Tathāgata is nourished by food is the provisional teaching; to say he abides permanently, without change, is the definitive teaching. To say the Tathāgata's entering nirvāṇa is like firewood burning out and a flame going out is the provisional teaching; to say the Tathāgata enters the Dharma-nature is the definitive teaching. The teaching of the śrāvaka vehicle should not be relied upon — why is this? Because the Tathāgata, for the sake of bringing beings across, taught the śrāvaka vehicle only as a skillful means, just as an elder teaches his son the half-letters first. Good son, the śrāvaka vehicle is like a field only just ploughed, not yet bearing fruit — this is why it is called the provisional teaching, and why it should not be relied upon. The teaching of the Great Vehicle, however, should be relied upon — why is this? Because the Tathāgata, for the sake of bringing beings across, taught the Great Vehicle through skillful means as well, and this is why it should be relied upon, and is called the definitive teaching. These four reliances should be well understood."

"Furthermore, relying on the meaning means this: meaning here means what is genuine and upright; what is genuine and upright means what is luminous; what is luminous means what is free of weakness; what is free of weakness means the Tathāgata himself. Further, what is luminous means wisdom; what is genuine and upright means permanent abiding. The Tathāgata's own permanence means relying on the Dharma; the Dharma means what is permanent, and is also called boundless, beyond conceptual thought, unable to be grasped, unable to be bound — yet also able to be seen. Whoever says it cannot be seen at all should not be relied upon — this is why one relies on the Dharma, not on the person. Whoever, in the most subtle and beautiful language, proclaims impermanence, should not be relied upon — this is why one relies on the meaning, not on the words. Relying on wisdom means this: the saṅgha is permanent, unconditioned, and unchanging, keeping none of the eight kinds of improper possession — this is why one relies on wisdom, not on ordinary consciousness. Should anyone say that consciousness itself acts and receives, and that there is no such thing as a harmonious saṅgha at all — why is this wrong? Because harmony itself means having no fixed substance of its own; and how could something with no fixed substance of its own be called permanent? This is why such a consciousness cannot be relied upon. Relying on the definitive teaching means this: the definitive teaching means contentment, never falsely feigning a pure, dignified bearing while secretly harboring arrogance and pride and greedily seeking personal gain, and never clinging to any of the Tathāgata's own teachings, given as they are through skillful and appropriate means — this is what is meant by the definitive teaching. Whoever is able to abide within such things should be understood to have already come to abide in the ultimate truth — this is why it is called relying on the definitive teaching."

"Not relying on the provisional teaching: the provisional teaching is as stated elsewhere in the scriptures — that everything is burning, everything is impermanent, everything is suffering, everything is empty, everything is without self. This is called the provisional teaching. Why is this? Because failing to understand this teaching in its true meaning causes beings to fall into the Avīci hell. Why is this so? Because of clinging and attachment, the true meaning is not grasped. 'Everything is burning' means that even nirvāṇa, as the Tathāgata has taught it, could be said to burn; 'everything is impermanent' means that even nirvāṇa could be said to be impermanent; and it is the same for suffering, emptiness, and no-self. This is why such teaching is called provisional, and should not be relied upon. Good son, it may also be said that the Tathāgata, out of compassion for all beings, skillfully knows the right time for each teaching, and, knowing the time, sometimes teaches what is light as though it were grave, and what is grave as though it were light. When the Tathāgata sees that his disciples have donors providing for their every need, leaving them wanting for nothing, the Buddha does not permit such disciples to keep servants, gold, silver, treasures, or engage in buying and selling improper goods. But when his disciples have no such donors to provide for their needs, and famine makes food and drink hard to come by, then, for the sake of establishing and protecting the true Dharma, I permit my disciples to keep servants, gold, silver, carriages, land, houses, and grain, and to trade for what they need — though I permit them to keep such things, they must always be donated onward, in the end, to some sincere and faithful benefactor. These four principles should be relied upon. Whatever Vinaya text, Abhidharma treatise, or sūtra does not contradict these four should likewise be relied upon. But should anyone say that there is a right time and a wrong time, that some are able to protect the Dharma and others are not, and that the Tathāgata permits every monk without exception to keep such improper possessions regardless — such words should not be relied upon. And should any Vinaya text, Abhidharma treatise, or sūtra agree with such words, none of these three should be relied upon either. I have taught these four reliances for the sake of beings possessing only the fleshly eye, never for those who already possess the eye of wisdom. This is why I now teach these four reliances: the Dharma is itself the Dharma-nature; the meaning is that the Tathāgata abides permanently, without change; wisdom is fully knowing that every being without exception possesses buddha-nature; and the definitive teaching is fully mastering every scripture of the Great Vehicle."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the four reliances as unfolding in a single deliberate order rather than four independent rules listed side by side: reliance on the Dharma names the object that grounds everything else (the Dharma-nature itself, identified with the Tathāgata); reliance on meaning over words follows from it, since the Dharma-nature's own virtues — the Dharma-body, liberation, and prajñā together — are what the meaning of any teaching about it has to track, regardless of how skillfully or clumsily that teaching happens to be phrased; reliance on wisdom over ordinary consciousness follows in turn, since it is precisely the discernment born of that same wisdom, applied to concrete situations like the saṅgha's own composition, that lets a listener judge whether a given teacher's words are tracking the Dharma-nature or merely dressing up a provisional half-truth; and reliance on the definitive teaching over the provisional closes the sequence by naming, in scriptural terms, which body of teaching — the Great Vehicle rather than the śrāvaka vehicle — actually delivers what the first three reliances have been oriented toward all along. Read this way, the four are less a checklist for filtering out bad teachers than a single method applied at four successive depths, from the deepest metaphysical commitment down to the most concrete textual judgment call — which is why Huiyuan reads the sūtra's closing gloss, collapsing all four back into "the Dharma-nature," "the Tathāgata's permanence," "buddha-nature in every being," and "the whole of the Great Vehicle's teaching," as a genuine restatement of one single point in four registers, continuous with this fascicle's own earlier insistence that four apparently distinct kinds of people are, at bottom, protecting one and the same Dharma.
