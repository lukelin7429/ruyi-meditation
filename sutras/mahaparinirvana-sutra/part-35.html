The Stump in the Dark

Kāśyapa opens with a puzzle about doubt itself: doubt normally requires having seen two things and being unable to tell them apart, so how can anyone doubt nirvāṇa, which no one has ever seen at all? The Buddha's answer — that ordinary suffering and its absence are what a being has already seen, and it is these that make nirvāṇa a live question — opens onto the fascicle's real subject: the person who reasons his way, argument by careful argument, into denying cause and fruit altogether, and whether such a person still possesses buddha-nature. The Buddha's four kinds of answer, a full accounting of buddha-nature's own visible qualities at every stage from stream-enterer to Tathāgata, and seven kinds of language follow.

The Stump in the Dark, and the Muddy Water Nobody Has Seen

Chinese Source

「◎善男子！如是諍訟是佛境界，非諸聲聞、緣覺所知。若人於是生疑心者，猶能摧壞無量煩惱如須彌山，若於是中生決定者，是名執著。」 迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！云何執著？」 佛言：「善男子！如是之人，若從他聞、若自尋經、若他故教，於所著事不能放捨，是名執著。」 迦葉復言：「世尊！如是執著，為是善耶？是不善乎？」 「善男子！如是執著，不名為善。何以故？不能摧壞諸疑網故。」 迦葉復言：「世尊！如是人者，本自不疑，云何說言不壞疑網？」 「善男子！夫不疑者，即是疑也。」 「世尊！若有人謂須陀洹人不墮三惡，是人亦當名著、名疑？」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, disputes of this kind belong to the Buddha's own domain, not something known by any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha. Should a person give rise to doubt regarding this, he is thereby able to shatter countless afflictions, vast as Mount Sumeru; but should he give rise to a fixed conclusion about it, this is called attachment." Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what is meant by attachment?" The Buddha said: "Good son, a person of this kind — whether hearing it from another, seeking it out in the scriptures himself, or being deliberately taught it — is unable to let go of the very thing he clings to; this is called attachment." Kāśyapa said, further: "World-Honored One, is attachment of this kind wholesome, or unwholesome?" "Good son, attachment of this kind is not called wholesome. Why is this? Because it is unable to shatter the net of doubt." Kāśyapa said, further: "World-Honored One, a person of this kind is, from the very start, free of doubt himself — how, then, can it be said that he fails to shatter the net of doubt?" "Good son, to be free of doubt in this way is itself simply doubt." "World-Honored One, should a person hold that a stream-enterer never falls into the three evil destinies, is such a person, too, to be called attached, to be called doubting?"

「善男子！是可名定，不得名疑。何以故？善男子！譬如有人先見人、樹，後時夜行，遙見杌根，便生疑想，人耶？樹耶？善男子！如人先見比丘梵志，後時於路遙見比丘，即生疑想，是沙門耶？是梵志乎？善男子！如人先見牛與水牛，後遙見牛，便生疑想，彼是牛耶？是水牛乎？善男子！一切眾生先見二物，後便生疑。何以故？心不了故。我亦不說須陀洹人有墮三惡、不墮三惡，是人何故生於疑心？」 迦葉言：「世尊！如佛所說，要先見已，然後疑者。有人未見二種物時，亦復生疑。何等是耶？所謂涅槃。世尊！譬如有人路遇濁水，然未曾見而亦生疑，如是水者深耶？淺耶？是人未見，云何生疑？」 「善男子！夫涅槃者，即是斷苦。非涅槃者，即是苦也。一切眾生見有二種，見苦、非苦。苦非苦者，即是飢渴、寒熱、瞋喜，病瘦安隱、老壯生死，繫縛解脫、恩愛別離、怨憎聚會。眾生見已即便生疑，當有畢竟遠離如是苦惱事不？是故眾生於涅槃中而生疑也。汝意若謂是人先來未見濁水，云何疑者？是義不然。何以故？是人先於餘處見已，是故於此未曾到處，而復生疑。」 「世尊！是人先見深淺處時，已不生疑，於今何故而復生疑？」 佛言：「善男子！本未行故，所以生疑。是故我言，不了故疑。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, this may be called a fixed conclusion, not doubt. Why is this? Good son, it is exactly like a person who, having previously seen both a man and a tree, later, walking at night, sees a stump from a distance and gives rise to doubt: is it a man, or is it a tree? Good son, it is like a person who, having previously seen both a monk and a brahmin ascetic, later sees, from a distance on the road, a monk, and gives rise to doubt: is this a śramaṇa, or is this a brahmin ascetic? Good son, it is like a person who, having previously seen both an ox and a water-buffalo, later sees, from a distance, an ox, and gives rise to doubt: is this an ox, or is this a water-buffalo? Good son, every being, having previously seen two things, later gives rise to doubt — why is this? Because the mind has not fully grasped the distinction. I, too, have never taught that a stream-enterer both falls into the three evil destinies and does not fall into them — for what reason, then, should this person give rise to a doubting mind at all?" Kāśyapa said: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught, doubt necessarily requires having first seen two things. Yet there is a case where a person gives rise to doubt without having seen either of two things at all — what is this case? It is nirvāṇa itself. World-Honored One, it is exactly like a person who, encountering muddy water along the road, though he has never seen it before, still gives rise to doubt: is this water deep, or is it shallow? This person has not seen it — how, then, does doubt arise?" "Good son, nirvāṇa itself is simply the severing of suffering; what is not nirvāṇa is simply suffering itself. Every being sees these two things: suffering, and the absence of suffering. Suffering and its absence are hunger and thirst, cold and heat, anger and joy, sickness and ease, old age and youth, birth and death, bondage and liberation, separation from those one loves, and encounter with those one hates. Having seen these, beings then give rise to doubt: will there ever truly, finally, be a departure from every such affliction? It is for this very reason that beings give rise to doubt regarding nirvāṇa. Should you think that, since this person has never before seen muddy water, how could doubt arise at all — this reasoning does not hold. Why is this? Because this person has already seen it elsewhere, and it is for this very reason that, upon reaching this place he has never before been, doubt arises once more." "World-Honored One, this person, having previously seen both deep and shallow water, no longer gives rise to doubt regarding those — why, then, does doubt arise once more now?" The Buddha said: "Good son, it is precisely because he has not yet actually traveled this particular water himself that doubt arises. It is for this very reason that I say: doubt arises from a lack of full comprehension."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this entire exchange as the first of nine question-and-answer pairs running through the fascicle's opening (T1764, lines 20870–20977): the first eight concern the severing of wholesome roots, the ninth their regeneration. He also distinguishes two kinds of doubt operating here — a doubt that precedes and merely reinforces a wrong view, which only deepens confusion, and a doubt that precedes genuine understanding, which is powerful enough to shatter a settled wrong view "vast as Mount Sumeru." The stream-enterer exchange is doing quiet, careful work: since the Buddha has never taught two contradictory things about the stream-enterer's fate, a person who settles firmly on one side has not resolved a real doubt at all — he has simply manufactured a false certainty, which is why it still counts as attachment.

How a Person Severs His Own Wholesome Roots

Chinese Source

迦葉菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如佛所說，疑即是著，著即是疑。為是誰耶？」 「善男子！斷善根者。」 迦葉言：「世尊！何等人輩，能斷善根？」 「善男子！若有聰明黠慧利根，能善分別。遠離善友，不聽正法，不善思惟，不如法住，如是之人，能斷善根。離是四事，心自思惟：『無有施物。何以故？施者即是捨於財物，若施有報，當知施主常應貧窮。何以故？子果相似故，是故說言無因無果。』若如是說無因無果，是則名為斷善根也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught, doubt is itself attachment, and attachment is itself doubt — to whom, then, does this actually apply?" "Good son, to one who has severed his own wholesome roots." Kāśyapa said: "World-Honored One, what kind of person is able to sever his own wholesome roots?" "Good son, one who is intelligent, clever, and sharp of faculty, well able to draw fine distinctions, yet who has departed from good spiritual friends, does not listen to the true Dharma, reflects unwisely, and does not abide by the Dharma — a person of exactly this kind is able to sever his own wholesome roots. Departed from these four supports, such a person reflects to himself: 'There is no such thing as a genuine gift. Why is this? Because giving is simply the relinquishing of one's own wealth — and, were giving truly to bear fruit, the donor ought always to end up poor, since a seed's own fruit resembles the seed itself; it is for this very reason that I say there is no cause and no fruit at all.' Should anyone reason in this way, that there is no cause and no fruit, this is exactly what is called severing one's own wholesome roots.

「復作是念：『施主、受者及以財物，三事無常、無有停住，若無停住，云何說言，此是施主、受者、財物？若無受者，云何得果？以是義故，無因無果。』若如是說無因無果，當知是人能斷善根。 「復作是念：『施者施時有五事施，受者受已，或時作善、或作不善，而是施主亦復不得善、不善果。如世間法，從子生果，果還作子。因即施主果即受者，而是受者不能以此善、不善法，令施主得，以是義故，無因無果。』若如是說無因無果，當知是人能斷善根。 「復作是念：『無有施物。何以故？施物無記。若是無記，云何而得善果報耶？無善惡果，即是無記。財若無記，當知即無善惡果報，是故無施、無因、無果。』若如是說無因無果，當知是人能斷善根。 「復作是念：『施者即意。若是意者，無見無對，非是色法。若非是色，云何可施？是故無施、無因、無果。』若如是說無因無果，當知是人能斷善根。 「復作是念：『施主若為佛像、天像，命過父母而行施者，即無受者，若無受者，應無果報。若無果報，是為無因，若無因者是為無果。』若如是說無因無果，當知是人能斷善根。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"He further reflects: 'The donor, the recipient, and the wealth given — these three things are all impermanent, never remaining fixed. If nothing remains fixed, how can one even say "this is the donor," "this is the recipient," "this is the wealth"? And, if there is no recipient at all, how could any fruit be obtained? For this very reason, there is no cause and no fruit.' Should anyone reason in this way, that there is no cause and no fruit, one should know that this person is able to sever his own wholesome roots. "He further reflects: 'One who gives, in the act of giving, gives by way of five factors — yet the recipient, having received them, may then act either wholesomely or unwholesomely, and the donor, for his part, receives neither the wholesome nor the unwholesome fruit of this. Just as, in the way of the world, a fruit is produced from a seed, and this fruit, in turn, becomes a seed — the cause here is the donor, the fruit is the recipient, and yet this recipient is unable to make the donor himself receive this wholesome or unwholesome result. For this very reason, there is no cause and no fruit.' Should anyone reason in this way, that there is no cause and no fruit, one should know that this person is able to sever his own wholesome roots. "He further reflects: 'There is no such thing as a genuine gift-object. Why is this? Because the object given is itself morally neutral. If it is morally neutral, how could it possibly yield a wholesome fruit? Whatever yields neither a wholesome nor an unwholesome fruit is simply morally neutral — and, if wealth itself is morally neutral, one should know that it yields no wholesome or unwholesome fruit whatsoever; therefore there is no giving, no cause, and no fruit at all.' Should anyone reason in this way, that there is no cause and no fruit, one should know that this person is able to sever his own wholesome roots. "He further reflects: 'The one who gives is simply his own intention. If it is intention, it cannot be seen, has no physical counterpart, and is not a material dharma at all — and, if it is not material, how could it possibly be given? Therefore there is no giving, no cause, and no fruit at all.' Should anyone reason in this way, that there is no cause and no fruit, one should know that this person is able to sever his own wholesome roots. "He further reflects: 'Should a donor give an offering for the sake of an image of the Buddha, an image of a god, or a deceased father or mother, there is, in truth, no recipient present at all — and, if there is no recipient, there ought to be no fruit at all; and, if there is no fruit, there is no cause; and, if there is no cause, there is no fruit.' Should anyone reason in this way, that there is no cause and no fruit, one should know that this person is able to sever his own wholesome roots."

Ru-Yi Translation

"He further reflects: 'There is no father, no mother. Were father and mother truly the cause of a being's birth, they ought, by rights, to always be producing beings without ever ceasing — since the cause is always present. And yet they do not always produce a being; therefore one should know that there is, in truth, no such thing as father or mother.' "He further reflects: 'There is no father, no mother. Why is this? Were a being's own body truly caused by its father and mother, any single person ought to possess both the male and the female faculty at once — and yet no one possesses both; therefore one should know that beings are not caused by father and mother at all.' "He further reflects: 'Beings are not produced by father and mother as their cause. Why is this? We see with our own eyes that beings do not resemble their own father and mother — in body, in complexion, in mind, in bearing, in manner of movement — and therefore father and mother are not the cause of beings.' "He further reflects: 'In the world generally there are four kinds of "non-existence": first, "non-existence" before something has arisen, as when a lump of clay has not yet become a usable pot; second, "non-existence" once something has already ceased, as once a pot has already shattered, this too is called non-existence; third, mutual "non-existence" between distinct things, as an ox does not exist within a horse, nor a horse within an ox; and fourth, absolute "non-existence," as with a rabbit's horn or a tortoise's hair. The relation between beings and their father and mother is exactly the same, falling under one of these four kinds of "non-existence." Were father and mother truly the cause of a being, then, when the parents die, the child ought necessarily to die as well — and yet this is not so; therefore father and mother are not the cause of beings.' "He further reflects: 'Were father and mother truly the cause of beings, then beings ought always to be produced by father and mother alone. And yet there are, further, beings born by transformation and beings born from moisture; therefore one should know that beings are not produced by the cause of father and mother at all.' "He further reflects: 'There are, indeed, beings that grow and develop without any father or mother as their cause at all — exactly as the peahen, hearing the sound of thunder, thereby conceives; or exactly as the blue sparrow, drinking the male sparrow's urine, thereby conceives; or exactly as the jīvañjīva bird, seeing the male's own dance, thereby conceives.' Reflecting in this way, should such a person fail to encounter a true spiritual friend, one should know that he is able to sever his own wholesome roots."

Ru-Yi Translation

"He further reflects: 'In the world generally there is no such thing as a wholesome or unwholesome fruit at all. Why is this? There are beings who fully possess the ten wholesome dharmas, who delight in generous giving, and who diligently cultivate merit — and yet these very people also fall ill with disease, die prematurely in their prime, lose their own wealth, and suffer every kind of sorrow and affliction. And there are those who practice the ten unwholesome deeds, who are stingy, greedy, envious, idle, and lazy, cultivating no wholesome dharma at all — and yet these very people enjoy bodily ease, freedom from illness, a life lived out to its natural end, abundant wealth, and no sorrow at all. Therefore one should know that there is no such thing as a wholesome or unwholesome fruit.' "He further reflects: 'I have, moreover, once heard the sages themselves say that a certain person, having cultivated wholesome deeds, upon death fell straight into the three evil destinies, while another, having done unwholesome deeds, upon death was born among humans and gods; therefore one should know that there is no such thing as a wholesome or unwholesome fruit.' "He further reflects: 'The sages themselves offer two different teachings — some say that killing yields a wholesome fruit, others say that killing yields an unwholesome fruit; therefore one should know that the sages' own teaching is itself not fixed. If even the sages are not fixed in what they teach, how could I possibly be fixed in anything? Therefore one should know that there is no such thing as a wholesome or unwholesome fruit.' "He further reflects: 'In the world generally there is no such thing as a sage at all. Why is this? Were it said that a sage attains the true path, then, for any being who cultivates the true path while still possessing affliction, one should know that both the true path and affliction are present in this person at one and the same time — and, if both are present at once, one should know that the true path is simply unable to destroy affliction at all. And should someone cultivate the path while having no affliction at all, then what, exactly, would such a path even accomplish? Therefore, for one who still possesses affliction, the path cannot destroy it; and for one who no longer possesses affliction, the path is of no use at all. Therefore one should know that, in the world generally, there is no such thing as a sage.'"

「復作是念：『無明緣行，乃至生緣老死，是十二因緣，一切眾生等共有之。八聖道者，其性平等，亦應如是一人得時，一切應得；一人修時，應一切苦滅。何以故？煩惱等故。而今不得，是故當知無有正道。』 「復作是念：『聖人皆有同凡夫法，所謂飲食、行住、坐臥、睡眠、喜笑、飢渴、寒熱、憂愁、恐怖。若同凡夫如是事者，當知聖人不得聖道；若得聖道，應當永斷如是等事。如是等事如其不斷，當知無道。』 「復作是念：『聖人有身，受五欲樂，亦復罵辱撾打，於人嫉妬憍慢，受於苦樂，作善惡業，是因緣故，知無聖人。若有道者，應斷是事，是事不斷，當知無道。』 「復作是念：『多憐愍者，名為聖人。何因緣故，名為聖人？道因緣故，名為聖人。若道性憐愍，便應愍念一切眾生，不待修已然後方得。如其無愍，何故聖人因得聖道，能憐愍耶？是故當知世無聖道。』 「復作是念：『一切四大，不從因生，眾生等有是四大性，不觀眾生是邊應到、彼不應到。若有聖道，性應如是。然今不爾，是故當知，世無聖人。』」

Ru-Yi Translation

"He further reflects: 'Ignorance conditions formations, and so on, all the way through to birth conditioning old age and death — these twelve links are possessed equally by every being alike. The noble eightfold path, too, has a nature that is equally shared, and so, exactly as with the twelve links, when a single person attains it, everyone ought to attain it at once; when a single person cultivates it, the suffering of everyone ought to be extinguished at once — why is this? Because affliction, too, is shared equally by all. And yet this is plainly not what happens; therefore one should know that there is no such thing as a true path at all.' "He further reflects: 'The sages themselves possess every dharma an ordinary being possesses — that is to say, eating and drinking, walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, sleeping, laughing with joy, hunger and thirst, cold and heat, sorrow, and fear. If the sages share exactly these very things with ordinary beings, one should know that the sages have not truly attained the noble path at all; for, had they truly attained the noble path, they ought to have forever severed every one of these very things. And, since these very things are plainly not severed, one should know that there is no such thing as the path at all.' Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan organizes this entire catalogue into exactly four slanders, each with its own set of sub-arguments (T1764, lines 20909–20976): slandering giving itself (six arguments, from the seed-fruit-resemblance point through the unanswerable image/deceased-parent offering), slandering father and mother as any real cause of a being (six arguments, from the always-producing objection through the bird-conception examples), slandering cause and fruit generally (three arguments, each an empirical-seeming counter-example), and slandering the very existence of a sage (nine arguments, escalating from "the path and affliction can't coexist" through the sharpest and most abstract — that a single, permanent nirvāṇa shared by every sage is not obtainable, while many separate nirvāṇas would make it impermanent, so a sage in either case cannot exist). Huiyuan's point in laying the whole architecture out this carefully is not to admire its cleverness: every single one of these twenty-nine reflections is grammatically valid and depends on a real, unresolved technical question about how causation actually works — which is precisely why the text insists, twice, that severing wholesome roots is not the mark of a "foolish, dull-witted person," but is well within reach of exactly the "intelligent, clever, sharp-of-faculty" mind the passage opens by naming.

Two Moments for Wholesome Roots to Return, and Kāśyapa's Sharpest Question Yet

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattva Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if the severing of the causes belonging to all three times is what is called severing one's wholesome roots, then a person who has severed his wholesome roots must still possess buddha-nature. This very buddha-nature — is it past, is it present, is it future, or does it pervade all three times equally? If it is future, how could it possibly be called permanent — and why, then, does the Buddha teach that every being is certain, in the end, to attain it? If it is certain to be attained, how could it possibly be said to be 'severed'? If it is present, how, again, could it possibly be permanent — and why, further, does the Buddha say that it is certain to be seen? The Tathāgata has, moreover, taught that buddha-nature possesses six qualities: first, permanence; second, truth; third, reality; fourth, goodness; fifth, purity; and sixth, visibility. If one who has severed his own wholesome roots still possesses buddha-nature, he ought not, then, to be called one who has severed his wholesome roots at all. And if he does not possess buddha-nature, how, then, can it still be said that every being entirely possesses buddha-nature? And should it be said that buddha-nature both exists and is severed at once, how, then, can the Tathāgata still teach that it is permanent?"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan glosses the "two moments" precisely: dread felt at the moment of first falling into hell, and the exhaustion of the offense at the moment of emerging from it, are each occasions on which wholesome roots can begin to regrow (T1764, line 20977). The severing formula gets a similarly exact gloss: what is severed is "the arising of a present result from a past cause" and "the capacity to give rise to a future result" — but the past cause's own bare existence and the future result's own eventual arising are never actually severed, since neither could be severed even in principle (line 20982, "斷三世因者...唯斷此因，不斷過家當來之果，故善得生"). Kāśyapa's question then applies the exact four-cornered timing analysis used for buddha-nature throughout Parts 33–34 to this specific hard case — a severed person who must, by the sūtra's own six-quality definition of buddha-nature, still possess something permanent — setting up the elaborate answer that follows.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's gloss on the seven-then-six-then-five-fold buddha-nature schema is precise about what is actually happening: the qualities are not different buddha-natures but the same buddha-nature becoming visible one quality at a time (T1764, lines 21054–21103). "Self" and "bliss" belong properly to the fruit — self meaning full mastery, bliss meaning nirvāṇa's own peace through the complete cutting-off of affliction — so neither is named until the Tathāgata's own sevenfold list; a final-body bodhisattva already shares permanence, purity, truth, reality, and goodness, but sees the whole only "one part in ten," hence "little seen"; a ninth-abiding bodhisattva loses "little seen" but keeps the same five plus visibility, since hearing of it clearly is not yet beholding it directly; from the sixth through eighth abidings permanence itself drops away, because Huiyuan explains permanence here specifically names the Dharma-body, which only the final stages truly share; and the earliest bodhisattvas, fifth abiding and below, still carry a "wholesome-or-unwholesome" quality instead of full goodness, since at this stage the pāramitā of wisdom is not yet clear enough to have finished uprooting subtle pride. Read this way, the schema is a diagnostic ladder rather than a list of exceptions — it tells you exactly how much of the same single buddha-nature any given practitioner, including one who has severed his own wholesome roots, can be said to already "have."

On the milk-mixed-with-blood simile and the milk-to-ghee sequence, Huiyuan notes a small but real inconsistency with the schema used back in Part 32, where pratyekabuddhas were grouped with śrāvakas as "curds" rather than with bodhisattvas as "refined butter" — he resolves it by observing that a pratyekabuddha's narrow aspiration groups him with śrāvakas in most contexts, but his freedom from ever retrogressing groups him with bodhisattvas in this one, and the sūtra simply emphasizes whichever kinship serves the point at hand (line 21150–21154). On the "set-aside answer," Huiyuan's own two-part gloss matches the text precisely: "rejecting" (遮止) blocks the flat claim that a severed person definitely lacks buddha-nature, while "not clinging" (莫著) blocks the flat claim that he definitely possesses it as a settled, already-manifest fact — together enacting, in the form of an answer, the very non-attachment the fascicle's opening exchange on doubt was already recommending.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan counts exactly seven rounds of this same maneuver (T1764, lines 21186–21190): the five aggregates, the twelve sense-bases, the non-Buddhist and Buddhist paths, the Buddha's own physical marks against his mental qualities, inner reflection against hearing the Dharma from another, the giving-perfection against the remaining five perfections, and finally the two similes of the forehead jewel and the hidden treasure. He groups them by which kind of five aggregates is doing the work in each: the first, second, and seventh rounds run on the unwholesome aggregates, the third, fifth, and sixth rounds run on the wholesome aggregates, and the fourth round alone runs on the Buddha's own resultant aggregates — three different registers of the same argument, repeated until the reader stops expecting a winning side and starts recognizing the pattern itself as the teaching. Every round follows the identical shape: state one extreme, state its opposite, and then block both by naming the union as the middle way — which is why Huiyuan reads the whole section as one continuous demonstration rather than seven separate debates.

Therefore one knows that salt, in truth, possesses neither of these two natures inherently — and, exactly as with salt, so too with everything not salty. Should it be said that the power of the external four great elements is able to increase the internal four great elements, this reasoning, too, does not hold. Why is this? Because a definite sequence is always taught: curd is never obtained from milk, nor butter produced from curd, and so on through every dharma, apart from the necessary skillful means — and the four great elements, too, are exactly the same. Should it be said that the internal four great elements are increased from the external four great elements, one would then expect, in turn, to see the external four great elements increased from the internal — yet this is never observed. It is exactly like the śirīṣa fruit, which originally has no form or substance at all, and yet, the moment the Pleiades appear, this very fruit springs into being, growing to a full five inches in length — a fruit of this kind, in truth, is not increased by reliance upon any external four great elements at all."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads these three arguments — the seed and its fruit, milk transforming into curd, and salt making the unsalty salty — as three distinct causal models deployed against the same pair of extremes, and names the overall maneuver "refuting the attachment to reveal the principle" (破情顯理, T1764, line 21221): denying that buddha-nature is a settled, already-present fact refutes fixed existence, and denying that it is simply absent refutes fixed non-existence. The seed-fruit argument works through identity-across-time (the seed and its fruit are one continuous substance seen at different moments); the milk-curd argument works through causal asymmetry (if curd's nature were already latent in milk, the fixed sequence milk→curd→butter→ghee would have no explanation, and water and grass — which also produce milk — would have to carry the identical latent nature, which is absurd); and the salt argument works through the impossibility of a "hidden," undetectable nature doing any explanatory work at all (if unsalted things secretly already had salt's nature, salt itself would need a matching hidden non-salty nature, and then nothing would explain why salt cannot season anything by itself). Huiyuan reads all three as building toward the same conclusion the milk-curd exchange states outright: fixed possession is attachment, fixed absence is falsehood, and only "both exists and does not exist" survives every one of these three tests. The śirīṣa fruit — appearing complete at five inches the instant the Pleiades rise, with no visible material buildup beforehand — caps the whole argument by showing that even ordinary nature furnishes cases where an effect cannot be explained by any prior accumulation of matter, which is exactly the kind of arising the sūtra is asking the reader to accept for buddha-nature itself.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan's gloss makes explicit why "teaching according to one's own intent" is singled out, earlier in the fascicle, as belonging to the Buddha's own domain that even a bodhisattva of the ten abidings cannot fully fathom: it is the register in which every listener's own honest but partial answer can be simultaneously affirmed and yet subordinated to a single unstated intent the Buddha alone holds, as when all five hundred monks are told they answered "correctly" while the Buddha's own actual point — that he teaches according to conventional, desire-realm understanding — was never on their list at all (T1764, lines 21340–21344). "According to another's understanding," by contrast, is teaching that borrows the other person's own framework and turns it back on him — the illusion exchange with Pāṭalika works entirely inside the householder's own concept of "knowing X" without becoming X, never asserting anything the Buddha would affirm as his own final view. And "according to both" is reserved for claims where the Buddha's own teaching and ordinary worldly consensus already agree, so that no translation or reframing is needed at all. Read together with the immediately following point — that countless dharmas taught as "buddha-nature" all still resolve into the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements — these three modes explain how the sūtra can sound, across forty fascicles, like it is contradicting itself: most of its apparent inconsistency is simply a shift between these three different registers of address, exactly the same insight the whole of Parts 33–34 was built to demonstrate case by case.

Seven Kinds of Language the Tathāgata Uses

Chinese Source

「善男子！如來說法為眾生故，有七種語：一者因語、二者果語、三者因果語、四者喻語、五者不應說語、六者世流布語、七者如意語。 「云何名因語？現在因中，說未來果，如我所說。善男子！汝見眾生，樂殺乃至樂行邪見，當觀是人即地獄人。善男子！若有眾生，不樂殺生乃至邪見，當觀是人即是天人。是名因語。 「云何果語？現在果中說過去因，如經中說。善男子！如汝所見貧窮眾生，顏貌醜陋、不得自在，當知是人定有破戒、妬心、瞋心、無慚愧心。若見眾生多財巨富、諸根完具、威德自在，當知是人定有戒、施、精勤、慚愧，無有妬瞋，是名果語。 「云何因果語？如經中說。善男子！眾生現在六入觸因，是名過去業果，如來亦說名之為業，是業因緣得未來果。是名因果語。 「云何喻語？如說師子王者，即喻我身。大象王、大龍王、波利質多羅樹、七寶聚、大海、須彌山、大地、大雨、船師、導師、調御丈夫、力士、牛王、婆羅門、沙門、大城、多羅樹，如是喻經，名為喻語。 「云何不應語？我經中說，天地可合，河不入海。如為波斯匿王說四方山來。如為鹿母優婆夷說，若娑羅樹能受八戒，則得受於人天之樂。如說十住菩薩有退轉心，不說如來有二種語。寧說須陀洹人墮三惡道，不說十住有退轉心。是名不應語。 「云何世流布語？如佛所說，男女、大小、去來、坐臥、車乘、房舍、瓶衣，眾生、常樂我淨，軍林、城邑、僧幻、合散，是名世流布語。 「云何如意語？如我呵責毀禁之人，令彼自責，護持禁戒。如我讚歎須陀洹人，令諸凡夫，生於善心。讚歎菩薩，為令眾生發菩提心。說三惡道所有苦惱，為令修習諸善法故。說一切燒，唯為一切有為法故，無我亦爾。說諸眾生悉有佛性，為令一切不放逸故。是名如意語。◎

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, the Tathāgata, in teaching the Dharma, uses, for the sake of beings, seven kinds of language: first, cause-language; second, fruit-language; third, cause-and-fruit-language; fourth, simile-language; fifth, not-to-be-taken-literally language; sixth, ordinary worldly language; and seventh, as-intended language. "What is meant by cause-language? It is teaching a future fruit by way of a present cause, exactly as I have taught: good son, when you see a being who delights in killing, and, all the way through, delights in wrong view, you should regard this person as already a being of hell; good son, should there be a being who does not delight in killing, and, all the way through, does not delight in wrong view, you should regard this person as already a being of the heavens. This is called cause-language. "What is meant by fruit-language? It is teaching a past cause by way of a present fruit, exactly as taught in the sūtras: good son, when you see a poor and destitute being, ugly in countenance and lacking any freedom, you should know that this person certainly once broke the precepts, harbored envy and anger, and lacked all sense of shame; when you see a being of great wealth and abundance, complete in every faculty, dignified and free, you should know that this person certainly once kept the precepts, practiced giving, exerted diligent effort, and possessed a sense of shame, free of envy and anger. This is called fruit-language. "What is meant by cause-and-fruit-language? It is exactly as taught in the sūtras: good son, a being's own present six sense-bases and the contact arising from them are themselves the fruit of past karma — and the Tathāgata, too, teaches that this very fruit is itself further called karma, since it is, in turn, the cause and condition by which a future fruit is obtained. This is called cause-and-fruit-language. "What is meant by simile-language? It is exactly as when I speak of the lion king as a simile for my own body — the great elephant king, the great nāga king, the pārijāta tree, the mass of seven jewels, the great ocean, Mount Sumeru, the great earth, the great rain, the ship's captain, the guide, the tamer of men, the mighty wrestler, the bull among oxen, the brahmin, the śramaṇa, the great city, the tāla tree — scriptures using similes of exactly this kind are called simile-language. "What is meant by not-to-be-taken-literally language? It is exactly as when, in the sūtras, I speak of heaven and earth being able to merge, or of rivers never entering the sea; or as when I spoke to King Prasenajit of mountains approaching from the four directions; or as when I taught the laywoman Mṛgāra's mother that, should a sāla tree be able to receive the eight precepts, it too would attain the pleasures of humans and gods; or as when I speak of a bodhisattva of the ten abidings possessing a retrogressing mind — I do not thereby teach that the Tathāgata speaks with two contradictory voices; rather, I would sooner say that a stream-enterer falls into the three evil destinies than say that a bodhisattva of the ten abidings possesses a mind of retrogression. This is called not-to-be-taken-literally language. "What is meant by ordinary worldly language? It is exactly as when the Buddha speaks of man and woman, great and small, coming and going, sitting and lying down, carriages, dwellings, vessels, and clothing, of beings, of permanence, bliss, self, and purity, of armies and forests, cities and towns, of the saṅgha, of illusion, of union and dispersal — language of exactly this kind is called ordinary worldly language. "What is meant by as-intended language? It is exactly as when I rebuke one who has violated the precepts, so that he may reproach himself and uphold the precepts once more; or as when I praise the stream-enterer, so that ordinary beings may give rise to a wholesome mind; or as when I praise the bodhisattva, so that beings may give rise to the bodhi-mind; or as when I describe the suffering and affliction found in the three evil destinies, so that beings may cultivate every wholesome dharma; or as when I teach that all things are burning, solely for the sake of every conditioned dharma, and the very same holds for no-self; or as when I teach that every being entirely possesses buddha-nature, so that all may cease to be heedless. This is called as-intended language."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads all seven kinds of language as building toward the seventh alone, since the point of naming the first six is to isolate what makes the last one distinct: teaching designed, case by case, to produce a specific transformation in the listener rather than to state a literal fact (T1764, lines 21370–21392). He treats "not-to-be-taken-literally language" as the clearest proof that the sūtra itself authorizes reading some of its own statements non-literally — rivers merging with the sea and mountains walking are named, in the very same breath, alongside the claim that a bodhisattva of the ten abidings "retrogresses," which the text insists is spoken this same protective way precisely because the alternative, admitting real retrogression at that stage, would be worse than the admittedly false-sounding claim that even a stream-enterer might fall into the evil destinies. On why buddha-nature above all is taught as "as-intended language," Huiyuan imports a fivefold rationale from the *Ratnagotravibhāstra*: it prevents beings from despairing of their own capacity, prevents contempt for other beings who might yet awaken, corrects a mistaken clinging to a fixed self, corrects clinging to falsity generally, and corrects the opposite error of denying the tathāgatagarbha as mere emptiness (lines 21394–21400) — of these five, Huiyuan says the sūtra's own stated purpose here, preventing heedlessness, corresponds to the first: a being who believes awakening is impossible for him has no reason left to make any effort at all.

Part 35 Complete

From a doubt no one should be able to feel about something never seen, through the full architecture of how a mind reasons its way into denying cause and fruit altogether, to a precise accounting of buddha-nature's own visible qualities at every single stage from stream-enterer to Tathāgata, and finally to the seven kinds of language that make the whole of the Buddha's teaching legible as skillful address rather than a single flat statement — this fascicle supplies the working vocabulary for everything Parts 33 and 34 already demonstrated case by case. Milk neither simply contains curd nor simply lacks it; a severed wholesome root neither simply has buddha-nature nor simply lacks it; and the Buddha's own words are answered in four ways, spoken in three registers, and phrased in seven kinds of language — never one flat kind of statement, always calibrated to what a particular question, at a particular moment, actually needs.
