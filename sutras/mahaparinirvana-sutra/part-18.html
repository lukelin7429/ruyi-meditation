The Monks of Kauśāmbī

This fascicle closes 梵行品 with the sixth and final set of practices, the six recollections — of the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, moral discipline, giving, and the gods — anchored by the sūtra's own extended etymology of the Buddha's ten traditional epithets, each unpacked through multiple layers of meaning. It then argues at length for why a bodhisattva is genuinely inconceivable, through a string of impossible feats made ordinary, before turning to a question every reader of a religion's scripture eventually asks: how long will this teaching actually last, and what determines whether it survives or perishes? The sūtra answers with unusual candor — eight paired conditions, a frank list of the doctrinal disputes that will splinter the community after the Buddha's death, and the notorious incident at Kauśāmbī, where a majority of five hundred precept-breaking monks killed a minority of a hundred arhats, and yet, the sūtra insists, the true Dharma did not actually perish.

Ten Names for the Buddha

Chinese Source

「◎復次善男子！云何復名一切世間所不知見覺，而是菩薩所知見覺？所謂六念處。何等為六？念佛、念法、念僧、念戒、念施、念天。善男子！云何念佛？如來、應、正遍知、明行足、善逝、世間解、無上士、調御丈夫、天人師、佛、世尊，常不變易，具足十力、四無所畏、大師子吼，名大沙門、大婆羅門、大淨畢竟到於彼岸、無能勝者、無見頂者、無有怖畏不驚不動、獨一無侶、無師自悟、疾智、大智、利智、深智、解脫智、不共智、廣普智、畢竟智、智寶成就、人中象王、人中牛王、人中龍王、人中丈夫、人中蓮花、分陀利花、調御人師、為大施主大法之師，以知法故名大法師，以知義故名大法師，以知時故名大法師，以知足故名大法師，以知我故名大法師，知大眾故名大法師，以知眾生種種性故名大法師，以知諸根利鈍中故名大法師，說中道故名大法師。

「云何名如來？如過去諸佛所說不變。云何不變？過去諸佛，為度眾生說十二部經，如來亦爾，故名如來。諸佛世尊從六波羅蜜、三十七品、十一空來至大涅槃，如來亦爾，是故號佛為如來也。諸佛世尊為眾生故，隨宜方便開示三乘，壽命無量不可稱計，如來亦爾，是故號佛為如來也。

「云何為應？世間之法悉名怨家，佛應害故，故名為應。夫四魔者是菩薩怨，諸佛如來為菩薩時，能以智慧破壞四魔，是故名應。復次應者名為遠離，為菩薩時，應當遠離無量煩惱，故名為應。復次應者名樂，過去諸佛為菩薩時，雖於無量阿僧祇劫為眾生故受諸苦惱，終無不樂，而常樂之，如來亦爾，是故名應。又復應者，一切人天，應以種種香花、瓔珞、幢幡、伎樂而供養之，是故名應。

「云何正遍知？正者名不顛倒，遍知者於四顛倒無不通達。又復正者名為苦行，遍知者，知因苦行，定有苦果。又復正者名世間中，遍知者畢竟定知，修習中道得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。又復正者，名為可數、可量、可稱，遍知者，不可數、不可量、不可稱，是故號佛為正遍知。善男子！聲聞緣覺亦有遍知、亦不遍知。何以故？遍知者，名五陰、十二入、十八界，聲聞緣覺亦得遍知，是名遍知。云何不遍知？善男子！假使二乘於無量劫，觀一色陰，不能盡知，以是義故，聲聞緣覺無有遍知。

「云何明行足？明者名得無量善果，行名脚足，善果者名阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。脚足者名為戒、慧，乘戒慧足，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是故名為明行足也。又復明者名呪，行者名吉，足者名果，善男子！是名世間義；呪者名為解脫，吉者名為阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，果者名為大般涅槃，是故名為明行足也。又復明者名光，行者名業，足者名果，善男子！是名世間義；光者名不放逸，業者名六波羅蜜，果者名為阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。又復明者名為三明：一菩薩明，二諸佛明，三無明明；菩薩明者即是般若波羅蜜，諸佛明者即是佛眼，無明明者即畢竟空，行者於無量劫為眾生故修諸善業，足者明見佛性，以是義故名明行足。

「云何善逝？善者名高，逝名不高，善男子！是名世間義；高者名為阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，不高者即如來心也。善男子！心若高者不名如來，是故如來名為善逝。又復善者名為善知識，逝者善知識果，善男子！是名世間義；善知識者即初發心，果者名為大般涅槃，如來不捨最初發心得大涅槃，是故如來名為善逝。又復善者名好，逝者名有，善男子！是名世間義；好者名見佛性，有者名大涅槃。善男子！涅槃之性實非有也，諸佛世尊因世間故，說言是有。善男子！譬如世人，實無有子說言有子，實無有道說言有道，涅槃亦爾，因世間故說言為有，諸佛世尊成大涅槃，故名善逝。◎

「◎善男子！云何世間解？善男子！世間者名為五陰，解者名知，諸佛世尊善知五陰，故名世間解。又世間者名為五欲，解名不著，不著五欲故，名世間解。又世間解者，東方無量阿僧祇世界，一切聲聞緣覺不知、不見、不解，諸佛悉知、悉見、悉解，南西北方、四維上下，亦復如是，是故號佛為世間解。又世間者一切凡夫，解者知諸凡夫善惡因果，非是聲聞緣覺所知，惟佛能知，是故號佛為世間解。又世間者名曰蓮花，解名不污，善男子！是名世間義；蓮花者即是如來，不污者，如來不為世間八法之所染污，是故號佛為世間解。又世間解者，諸佛菩薩名世間解。何以故？諸佛菩薩見世間故，故名世間解。善男子！如因食得命，名食為命；諸佛菩薩亦復如是，見世間故，故名世間解。

「云何無上士？上士者名之為斷，無所斷者，名無上士，諸佛世尊無有煩惱，故無所斷，是故號佛為無上士。又上士者名為諍訟，無上士者無有諍訟，如來無諍，是故號佛為無上士。又上士者名語可壞，無上士者語不可壞，如來所言，一切眾生所不能壞，是故號佛為無上士。又上士者名為上座，無上士者名無上座，三世諸佛更無過者，是故號佛為無上士。上者名新，士者名故，諸佛世尊體大涅槃，無新無故，是故號佛為無上士。

「云何調御丈夫？自既丈夫，復調丈夫。善男子！言如來者，實非丈夫、非不丈夫，因調丈夫，故名如來為丈夫也。善男子！一切男女，若具四法，則名丈夫。何等為四？一善知識，二能聽法，三思惟義，四如說修行。善男子！若男若女具是四法，則名丈夫。善男子！若有男子，無此四法，則不得名為丈夫也，何以故？身雖丈夫，行同畜生。如來調伏若男若女，是故號佛調御丈夫。復次善男子！如御馬者，凡有四種：一者觸毛，二者觸皮，三者觸肉，四者觸骨，隨其所觸，稱御者意。如來亦爾，以四種法，調伏眾生：一為說生，令受佛語，如觸其毛，隨御者意。二說生老，便受佛語，如觸毛皮，隨御者意。三者說生及以老病，便受佛語，如觸毛皮肉，隨御者意。四者說生及老病死，便受佛語，如觸毛皮肉骨，隨御者意。善男子！御者調馬，無有決定，如來世尊調伏眾生，必定不虛，是故號佛為調御丈夫。

「云何天人師？師有二種：一者善教，二者惡教。諸佛菩薩常以善法教諸眾生。何等善法？謂身、口、意善。諸佛菩薩教諸眾生，作如是言：『善男子，汝當遠離身不善業。何以故？以身惡業，是可遠離，得解脫故，是故我以此法教汝。若是惡業不可遠離，得解脫者，終不教汝令遠離也。若諸眾生離惡業已，墮三惡者無有是處，以遠離故，成阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，得大涅槃。』是故諸佛菩薩常以此法教化眾生，口、意亦爾，是故號佛為無上師。復次昔未得道，今已得之，以所得道，為眾生說；從本已來未修梵行，今已修竟，以己所修，為眾生說；自破無明，復為眾生破壞無明；自得淨目，復為眾生破除盲冥，令得淨眼；自知二諦，復為眾生演說二諦。既自解脫，復為眾生說解脫法；自度無邊生死大河，復令眾生皆悉得度；自得無畏，復教眾生令無怖畏；自既涅槃，復為眾生演大涅槃；是故號佛為無上師。天者名晝，天上晝長夜短，是故名天。又復天者名無愁惱，常受快樂是故名天。又復天者名為燈明，能破黑闇而為大明，是故名天。亦以能破惡業黑闇，得於善業，而生天上，是故名天。又復天者名吉，以吉祥故，得名為天。又復天者名曰日，有光明故，名曰為天。以是義故，名為天也。人者名曰能多恩義，又復人者身口柔軟，又復人者名有憍慢，又復人者能破憍慢。善男子！諸佛雖為一切眾生無上大師，然經中說為天人師。何以故？善男子！諸眾生中惟天與人，能發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，能修十善業道，能得須陀洹果、斯陀含果、阿那含果、阿羅漢果、辟支佛道、得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是故號佛為天人師。

「云何為佛？佛者名覺，既自覺悟，復能覺他。善男子！譬如有人，覺知有賊，賊無能為。菩薩摩訶薩能覺一切無量煩惱，既覺了已，令諸煩惱無所能為，是故名佛。以是覺故，不生、不老、不病、不死，是故名佛。

「婆伽婆者，婆伽名破，婆名煩惱，能破煩惱故，名婆伽婆。又能成就諸善法故，又能善解諸法義故，有大功德無能勝故，有大名聞遍十方故，又能種種大慧施故，又於無量阿僧祇劫吐女根故。

「善男子！若男、若女，能如是念佛者，若行、若住、若坐、若臥、若晝、若夜、若明、若闇，常得不離見佛世尊。

「善男子！何故名為如來、應、正遍知乃至婆伽婆，而有如是無量功德大名稱耶？善男子！菩薩摩訶薩於昔無量阿僧祇劫，恭敬父母、和上、諸師、上座、長老，於無量劫常為眾生而行布施，堅持禁戒，修集忍辱，勤行精進、禪定、智慧，大慈、大悲、大喜、大捨，是故今得三十二相、八十種好、金剛之身。又復菩薩於昔無量阿僧祇劫，修集信、念、進、定、慧根，於諸師長，恭敬供養，常為法利，不為食利。菩薩若持十二部經，若讀、若誦，常為眾生令得解脫、安隱、快樂，終不自為。何以故？菩薩常修出世間心及出家心、無為之心、無諍訟心、無垢穢心、無繫縛心、無取著心、無覆蓋心、無無記心、無生死心、無疑網心、無貪欲心、無瞋恚心、無愚癡心、無憍慢心、無穢濁心、無煩惱心、無苦心、無量心、廣大心、虛空心、無心、無無心、調心、不護心、無覆藏心、無世間心、常定心、常修心、常解脫心、無報心、無願心、善願心、無誤心、柔軟心、不住心、自在心、無漏心、第一義心、不退心、無常心、正直心、無諂曲心、純善心、無多少心、無堅硬心、無凡夫心、無聲聞心、無緣覺心，善知心界、知心生界、知心住界、知心自在界心，是故今得十力、四無所畏、大悲、三念處、常樂我淨，是故得稱如來乃至婆伽婆，是名菩薩摩訶薩念佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, what is meant, again, by what the whole world does not know, see, or perceive, and yet is what a bodhisattva does know, see, and perceive? Namely, the six recollections. What are these six? Recollection of the Buddha, of the Dharma, of the Saṅgha, of moral discipline, of giving, and of the gods. Good son, what is recollection of the Buddha? The Tathāgata, the worthy one, the fully and rightly awakened one, the one perfect in wisdom and conduct, the well-gone one, the knower of the world, the unsurpassed one, the tamer who guides the true man, the teacher of gods and men, the buddha, the world-honored one — forever unchanging, fully endowed with the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, and the great lion's roar; named the great śramaṇa, the great brahmin, the greatly pure one who has reached, in the end, the far shore; the one none can overcome; the one whose crown no one can see; the one without fear, unstartled and unmoved; the one alone, without companion; the one awakened by himself, without a teacher; of swift wisdom, of great wisdom, of sharp wisdom, of profound wisdom, of the wisdom of liberation, of wisdom shared by none other, of vast and universal wisdom, of ultimate wisdom, accomplished in the treasure of wisdom; the elephant-king among men, the bull-king among men, the dragon-king among men, the true man among men, the lotus among men, the white lotus; the master who tames people, the great donor, the great master of the Dharma — named the great Dharma-master because he knows the Dharma, named the great Dharma-master because he knows its meaning, named the great Dharma-master because he knows the proper time, named the great Dharma-master because he knows sufficiency, named the great Dharma-master because he knows himself, named the great Dharma-master because he knows the assembly, named the great Dharma-master because he knows the various natures of beings, named the great Dharma-master because he knows the sharpness and dullness of every faculty, and named the great Dharma-master because he teaches the middle way.

"What is meant by 'Tathāgata'? It means unchanging, just as every buddha of the past has taught. What is meant by unchanging? Every buddha of the past, for the sake of saving beings, taught the twelve divisions of the scriptures, and the Tathāgata is just the same — it is for this reason that he is named Tathāgata. Every buddha, every world-honored one, arrives at this great nirvāṇa by way of the six perfections, the thirty-seven factors, and the eleven emptinesses, and the Tathāgata is just the same — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named Tathāgata. Every buddha, every world-honored one, for the sake of beings, opens up and reveals the three vehicles according to what is fitting, his own lifespan immeasurable, beyond all reckoning, and the Tathāgata is just the same — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named Tathāgata.

"What is meant by 'worthy'? Every worldly dharma is itself named an enemy, and, because the Buddha should destroy it, he is named worthy. The four demons are a bodhisattva's own enemies, and every buddha, every tathāgata, when he was a bodhisattva, was able, through wisdom, to destroy these four demons — it is for this reason that he is named worthy. Furthermore, "worthy" means keeping far away: when he was a bodhisattva, he should keep far away from immeasurable affliction — it is for this reason that he is named worthy. Furthermore, "worthy" means bliss: every buddha of the past, when he was a bodhisattva, though he underwent every kind of suffering for the sake of beings throughout immeasurable incalculable kalpas, never once lacked bliss, but forever held to it, and the Tathāgata is just the same — it is for this reason that he is named worthy. And, further, "worthy" means that every god and every human should make offering to him with every kind of fragrant flower, garland, banner, and musical performance — it is for this reason that he is named worthy.

"What is meant by 'fully and rightly awakened'? 'Rightly' means free of inversion; 'fully awakened' means having penetrated the four inversions without exception. Furthermore, 'rightly' means austerity; 'fully awakened' means knowing that austerity, as a cause, certainly bears a fruit of suffering. Furthermore, 'rightly' means the middle of the world; 'fully awakened' means knowing with ultimate certainty that cultivating the middle way attains unsurpassed, complete awakening. Furthermore, 'rightly' means what can be counted, measured, and reckoned; 'fully awakened' means what cannot be counted, measured, or reckoned — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the fully and rightly awakened one. Good son, the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha, too, both fully know and do not fully know. Why is this? 'Fully knowing' means knowing the five aggregates, the twelve sense-fields, and the eighteen elements, and the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha, too, are able fully to know these — this is what is meant by fully knowing. What, then, is not fully knowing? Good son, even should the two lesser vehicles, throughout immeasurable kalpas, contemplate a single aggregate of form, they would be unable to know it completely — it is for this very reason that the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha do not fully know at all.

"What is meant by 'perfect in wisdom and conduct'? 'Wisdom' means attaining an immeasurable wholesome fruit; 'conduct' means the feet themselves; the wholesome fruit is unsurpassed, complete awakening. The feet are moral discipline and wisdom, and, riding upon the feet of discipline and wisdom, one attains unsurpassed, complete awakening — it is for this reason that this is named 'perfect in wisdom and conduct.' Furthermore, 'wisdom' names an incantation, 'conduct' names an auspicious sign, and 'perfection' names a fruit — good son, this is the worldly sense of the terms; in the deeper sense, the incantation is liberation, the auspicious sign is unsurpassed, complete awakening, and the fruit is this great nirvāṇa — it is for this reason that this is named 'perfect in wisdom and conduct.' Furthermore, 'wisdom' names light, 'conduct' names karma, and 'perfection' names a fruit — good son, this is the worldly sense of the terms; light is heedfulness, karma is the six perfections, and the fruit is unsurpassed, complete awakening. Furthermore, 'wisdom' names the three illuminations: the bodhisattva's illumination, every buddha's illumination, and the illumination of the unillumined itself; the bodhisattva's illumination is the perfection of wisdom itself, every buddha's illumination is the buddha eye itself, and the illumination of the unillumined is ultimate emptiness itself; 'conduct' is cultivating every wholesome act, throughout immeasurable kalpas, for the sake of beings; 'perfection' is clearly seeing buddha-nature — it is for this very reason that this is named 'perfect in wisdom and conduct.'

"What is meant by 'well-gone'? 'Well' means lofty, and 'gone' means not lofty — good son, this is the worldly sense of the terms; the lofty is unsurpassed, complete awakening, and the not-lofty is the Tathāgata's own mind itself. Good son, were his mind lofty, he would not be called the Tathāgata — it is for this reason that the Tathāgata is named 'well-gone.' Furthermore, 'well' names a good and wise friend, and 'gone' names the fruit of a good and wise friend — good son, this is the worldly sense of the terms; the good and wise friend is the very first arising of the bodhi-mind, and the fruit is this great nirvāṇa; the Tathāgata never abandons this first arising of the bodhi-mind even as he attains this great nirvāṇa — it is for this reason that the Tathāgata is named 'well-gone.' Furthermore, 'well' names what is fine, and 'gone' names existence itself — good son, this is the worldly sense of the terms; what is fine is seeing buddha-nature, and existence is this great nirvāṇa. Good son, the very nature of nirvāṇa is not, in truth, existence at all; every buddha, every world-honored one, on account of the world, says that it exists. Good son, it is like a worldly person who, having no son in reality, says he has a son, or who, having no path in reality, says he has a path — nirvāṇa is just the same: on account of the world, it is said to exist. Every buddha, every world-honored one, accomplishes this great nirvāṇa, and is therefore named "well-gone."

"Good son, what is meant by 'knower of the world'? Good son, 'world' means the five aggregates, and 'knower' means one who knows; every buddha, every world-honored one, knows the five aggregates well — it is for this reason that he is named knower of the world. Furthermore, 'world' means the five desires, and 'knower' means one who does not grasp them; because he does not grasp the five desires, he is named knower of the world. Furthermore, as for being the knower of the world: throughout immeasurable incalculable worlds to the east, whatever every śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha does not know, does not see, and does not understand, every buddha knows entirely, sees entirely, and understands entirely — and it is just the same to the south, west, and north, and in the four intermediate directions, above and below — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named knower of the world. Furthermore, 'world' means every ordinary being, and 'knower' means knowing the wholesome and unwholesome causes and their fruits of every ordinary being — something not known by any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, known by the Buddha alone — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named knower of the world. Furthermore, 'world' means the lotus flower, and 'knower' means undefiled — good son, this is the worldly sense of the terms; the lotus flower is the Tathāgata himself, and undefiled means that the Tathāgata is never stained by any of the eight worldly dharmas — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named knower of the world. Furthermore, as for being the knower of the world: every buddha and bodhisattva is named knower of the world. Why is this? Every buddha and bodhisattva sees the world — it is for this reason that he is named knower of the world. Good son, it is like how, because life is sustained through food, food itself is named "life"; every buddha and bodhisattva is just the same: because he sees the world, he is named knower of the world.

"What is meant by 'unsurpassed one'? 'Superior man' means one who cuts something off; 'one with nothing left to cut off' is named the unsurpassed one — every buddha, every world-honored one, is free of affliction entirely, and so has nothing left to cut off — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the unsurpassed one. Furthermore, 'superior man' means dispute and contention; 'the unsurpassed one' is free of dispute and contention — the Tathāgata engages in no dispute at all — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the unsurpassed one. Furthermore, 'superior man' means speech that can be refuted; 'the unsurpassed one's' speech cannot be refuted — whatever the Tathāgata says, no being is able to refute it — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the unsurpassed one. Furthermore, 'superior man' names the highest seat; 'the unsurpassed one' names the seat beyond which there is no higher — no buddha, past, present, or future, ever surpasses him — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the unsurpassed one. 'Superior' means new, and 'man' means old; every buddha, every world-honored one, embodies this great nirvāṇa, which is neither new nor old — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the unsurpassed one.

"What is meant by 'the tamer who guides the true man'? He is himself a true man, and, further, tames true men. Good son, the Tathāgata is, in reality, neither a true man nor not a true man; it is on account of taming true men that the Tathāgata is named a true man. Good son, every man and woman, should they be endowed with four dharmas, are named a true man. What are these four? A good and wise friend, the ability to listen to the Dharma, reflecting on its meaning, and practicing in accordance with what is taught. Good son, should any man or woman be endowed with these four dharmas, he or she is named a true man. Good son, should any man lack these four dharmas, he cannot be named a true man at all — why is this? Though his body is that of a true man, his conduct is the same as an animal's. The Tathāgata tames both man and woman alike — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the tamer who guides the true man. Furthermore, good son, it is like one who trains a horse: there are, in all, four kinds — touching its mane, touching its hide, touching its flesh, and touching its bone — and, according to whichever it responds to, the trainer's own will is thereby fulfilled. The Tathāgata is just the same, taming beings through four kinds of dharma: first, teaching birth, so that they accept the Buddha's own word, like touching the mane, fulfilling the trainer's will; second, teaching birth and old age, so that they accept the Buddha's own word, like touching the mane and hide, fulfilling the trainer's will; third, teaching birth, old age, and illness, so that they accept the Buddha's own word, like touching the mane, hide, and flesh, fulfilling the trainer's will; and fourth, teaching birth, old age, illness, and death, so that they accept the Buddha's own word, like touching the mane, hide, flesh, and bone, fulfilling the trainer's will. Good son, a horse-trainer's taming of a horse is not always certain to succeed; the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, taming beings, is, however, certainly never in vain — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the tamer who guides the true man.

"What is meant by 'teacher of gods and men'? A teacher is of two kinds: one who teaches wholesome things, and one who teaches unwholesome things. Every buddha and bodhisattva forever teaches beings with wholesome dharmas. What are these wholesome dharmas? They are wholesome conduct of body, speech, and mind. Every buddha and bodhisattva teaches beings, speaking in this way: 'Good son, you should keep far away from every unwholesome act of the body. Why is this? Because an unwholesome act of the body is something that can indeed be kept far away from, and, being kept far from, one attains liberation — it is for this very reason that I teach you this dharma. Were an unwholesome act something that could not be kept far away from, and liberation thereby attained, I would never, in the end, teach you to keep far from it at all. Should beings, having kept far from unwholesome acts, still fall among the three evil destinies, this could not possibly be so — it is on account of keeping far from them that they accomplish unsurpassed, complete awakening and attain this great nirvāṇa.' It is for this very reason that every buddha and bodhisattva forever teaches and transforms beings with this dharma — and it is just the same for speech and mind — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the unsurpassed teacher. Furthermore: in ages past, not yet having attained the path, he now has attained it, and teaches beings by means of the very path he has attained; from the very beginning, not yet having cultivated pure conduct, he has now completed its cultivation, and teaches beings by means of what he himself has cultivated; having himself broken through ignorance, he further breaks through ignorance for the sake of beings; having himself attained a pure eye, he further removes the blindness and darkness of beings, causing them to attain a pure eye; having himself known the two truths, he further expounds the two truths for the sake of beings; having himself attained liberation, he further teaches the dharma of liberation for the sake of beings; having himself crossed the boundless great river of birth-and-death, he further causes beings, every one of them, to cross it as well; having himself attained fearlessness, he further teaches beings to be free of fear; having himself attained nirvāṇa, he further expounds this great nirvāṇa for the sake of beings — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the unsurpassed teacher. "Gods" are named for daytime, since, in the heavens, the day is long and the night short — it is for this reason that they are named gods. Furthermore, "gods" are named for being free of sorrow and distress, forever receiving happiness — it is for this reason that they are named gods. Furthermore, "gods" are named for being like a lamp's light, able to dispel darkness and become a great illumination — it is for this reason that they are named gods; and also because, by breaking through the darkness of unwholesome karma, one attains wholesome karma and is thereby born among the gods above — it is for this reason that they are named gods. Furthermore, "gods" are named for being auspicious, and, on account of this auspiciousness, they are named gods. Furthermore, "gods" are named for the sun, since they possess light — it is for this reason that they are named gods. It is for these reasons that they are named gods. "Men" are named for being able to show great kindness and righteousness; and, further, men are named for having a gentle body and speech; and, further, men are named for holding arrogance; and, further, men are named for being able to break through arrogance. Good son, though every buddha is the unsurpassed great teacher of every being, the scriptures speak of him as the teacher of gods and men. Why is this? Good son, among all beings, only gods and men are able to give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, complete awakening, are able to cultivate the ten wholesome courses of action, and are able to attain the fruit of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, arhatship, the path of pratyekabuddhahood, and unsurpassed, complete awakening — it is for this reason that the Buddha is named the teacher of gods and men.

"What is meant by 'buddha'? 'Buddha' means one who has awakened, both awakening himself and, further, being able to awaken others. Good son, it is like a person who, becoming aware of a thief's presence, renders the thief unable to do anything at all. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva is able to awaken to every immeasurable affliction, and, having awakened to it fully, renders every affliction unable to do anything at all — it is for this reason that he is named buddha. And it is through this very awakening that he is unborn, unaging, unailing, and undying — it is for this reason that he is named buddha.

"As for 'Bhagavān': 'bhaga' means to destroy, and 'vān' means affliction — being able to destroy affliction, he is named Bhagavān. Further, because he is able to accomplish every wholesome dharma; further, because he is able to understand well the meaning of every dharma; because he possesses great merit that none can surpass; because he possesses great renown, spreading throughout the ten directions; further, because he is able to give the great gift of wisdom of every kind; and, further, because, throughout immeasurable incalculable kalpas, he has cast off the female faculty entirely.

"Good son, should any man or woman be able to recollect the Buddha in this way, then, whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, whether by day or by night, whether in light or in darkness, he forever attains an unbroken vision of the Buddha, the World-Honored One.

"Good son, for what reason is he named the Tathāgata, the worthy one, the fully and rightly awakened one, and so on up through Bhagavān, and possesses immeasurable merit and such great renown? Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva, throughout immeasurable incalculable kalpas in ages past, revered his own parents, his preceptor, his teachers, his elders, and the senior monks, and, throughout immeasurable kalpas, forever practiced giving for the sake of beings, firmly upheld the restraining precepts, cultivated and accumulated patience, diligently practiced diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom, and great loving-kindness, great compassion, great sympathetic joy, and great equanimity — it is for this very reason that he now attains the thirty-two marks, the eighty subsidiary signs, and the vajra body. And, further, the bodhisattva, throughout immeasurable incalculable kalpas in ages past, cultivated and accumulated the faculties of faith, mindfulness, diligence, concentration, and wisdom, revering and making offerings to his teachers and elders, forever seeking the benefit of the Dharma, and never seeking the benefit of food. Whenever the bodhisattva upholds the twelve divisions of the scriptures, whether reading or reciting them, he does so forever for the sake of causing beings to attain liberation, peace, and happiness, and never, in the end, for his own sake. Why is this? The bodhisattva forever cultivates a mind that transcends the world, a mind of renunciation, a mind of the unconditioned, a mind free of contention, a mind free of defilement, a mind free of bondage, a mind free of grasping, a mind free of concealment, a mind free of what is morally indeterminate, a mind free of birth-and-death, a mind free of the net of doubt, a mind free of craving, a mind free of anger, a mind free of delusion, a mind free of arrogance, a mind free of impurity, a mind free of affliction, a mind free of suffering, an immeasurable mind, a vast mind, a mind like empty space, a mind of no-mind, a mind free even of no-mind, a tamed mind, a mind that guards nothing, a mind free of concealment, a mind free of worldliness, a mind of constant concentration, a mind of constant cultivation, a mind of constant liberation, a mind free of seeking any return, a mind free of any personal wish, a mind of wholesome aspiration, a mind free of error, a gentle mind, a mind that abides in nothing, a mind of mastery, a mind free of outflow, a mind fixed on the ultimate meaning, a mind that never turns back, an ever-fresh mind, an upright mind, a mind free of flattery and deceit, a mind of pure goodness, a mind free of reckoning more or less, a mind free of hardness, a mind free of the ordinary being's own mind, a mind free of the śrāvaka's own mind, and a mind free of the pratyekabuddha's own mind; well knowing the realm of mind, knowing the realm where mind arises, knowing the realm where mind abides, and knowing the realm of mind's own mastery — it is for this very reason that he now attains the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, great compassion, the three foundations of mindfulness, and permanence, bliss, self, and purity, and it is for this reason that he comes to be called the Tathāgata, and so on up through Bhagavān. This is what is meant by the bodhisattva-mahāsattva's recollection of the Buddha.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the ten traditional epithets as splitting into two groups of five: the first five (Tathāgata, worthy one, fully and rightly awakened one, perfect in wisdom and conduct, well-gone) name the Buddha's own inward attainment, while the last five (knower of the world, unsurpassed one, tamer who guides the true man, teacher of gods and men, buddha) name his outward benefit to others, with "world-honored one" standing outside the count as a summary term covering every quality at once. Within the first group he traces a further internal pairing: "Tathāgata" and "worthy one" together name a completed path and its final extinction of affliction; "fully and rightly awakened one" and "perfect in wisdom and conduct" together name the perfected cause (right understanding, then right practice) that produces that completion; and "well-gone" names the perfected result on its own. He is explicit that every one of the sūtra's own glosses on these names operates on two registers at once — a plain "worldly sense" (世間義) using the words' everyday meanings, followed immediately by a "revealed sense" (約顯法) reinterpreting the same syllables to point at a specific doctrinal referent — and that a reader who stops at the worldly gloss (for instance, taking "well-gone" to mean merely "modest, not self-important") will miss that the sūtra's own deeper point is being made through, not despite, the everyday meaning of the word.

Two entries carry particular technical weight in Huiyuan's reading. On "fully and rightly awakened one," he distinguishes the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha's own genuine, but bounded, knowledge of the five aggregates from a buddha's own knowledge of the same aggregates: both are said to "fully know" them in one sense, yet the two lesser vehicles could contemplate a single aggregate of form for immeasurable kalpas and never exhaust it, which is the same distinction between middling and superior wisdom this collection has already tracked since Part 13's "leaf in the hand" parable — knowing a category as one thing versus knowing it resolved into unlimited particulars. On "perfect in wisdom and conduct," he reads the passage's own "three illuminations" (the bodhisattva's illumination as the perfection of wisdom, a buddha's illumination as the buddha eye, and "the illumination of the unillumined" as ultimate emptiness itself) as a deliberately paradoxical third term: emptiness is not itself a cognitive faculty the way the first two illuminations are, yet is called an "illumination" all the same because it is what a buddha's and a bodhisattva's illumination are both illuminations *of* — the object generating a name normally reserved for the seeing subject. The closing catalogue of some sixty distinct qualities of mind that a bodhisattva cultivates on the way to these ten names, Huiyuan notes, is not meant to be memorized as a checklist but read as evidence that no single formula captures what freedom from every possible attachment actually requires in practice — hence the sheer accumulated length of the list itself.

"How does the bodhisattva-mahāsattva recollect the Dharma? Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva reflects that whatever every buddha teaches is the very finest, the very highest, and, on account of this Dharma, is able to cause beings to attain a fruit even in this present life. Only this true Dharma is without any fixed season; it is seen by the Dharma-eye, not by the fleshly eye; and yet it cannot be compared to anything through any simile at all. It neither arises nor issues forth, neither abides nor ceases, has neither a beginning nor an end; it is unconditioned, beyond all number. For one without a dwelling, it becomes a dwelling; for one without refuge, it becomes a refuge; for one without light, it becomes light; for one who has not yet reached the far shore, it causes him to reach it; where there is no fragrance at all, it becomes an unobstructed fragrance; it cannot be seen, and yet is seen; it neither moves nor turns; it is neither long nor short; forever severing every ordinary pleasure, it is itself peaceful bliss, ultimate and subtle beyond measure. It is not form, and severs form, and yet is itself form; and so on, up through: it is not consciousness, and severs consciousness, and yet is itself consciousness. It is not karma, and severs karma; it is not a fetter, and severs every fetter; it is not a thing, and severs every thing, and yet is itself a thing; it is not an element, and severs every element, and yet is itself an element; it is not existence, and severs existence, and yet is itself existence; it is not a sense-field, and severs every sense-field, and yet is itself a sense-field; it is not a cause, and severs every cause, and yet is itself a cause; it is not a fruit, and severs every fruit, and yet is itself a fruit; it is neither unreal nor real, and severs everything real, and yet is itself real; it is neither arisen nor ceased, and forever leaves behind arising and ceasing, and yet is itself cessation; it is neither a mark nor the absence of a mark, and severs every mark, and yet is itself a mark; it is neither a teaching nor not a teaching, and yet is itself a teacher; it is neither fearsome nor peaceful, and severs every fear, and yet is itself peace; it is neither patience nor impatience, and forever severs impatience, and yet is itself patience; it is neither cessation nor non-cessation, and severs every cessation, and yet is itself cessation. "How does one recollect the Saṅgha? The holy Saṅgha of every buddha abides in accordance with the Dharma, receiving and following its true and upright teaching, cultivating and practicing in accordance with it; it cannot be beheld, cannot be grasped, cannot be destroyed, cannot be harmed by anyone, and is inconceivable — a true and beneficial field of merit for every being. This is what is meant by recollection of the Saṅgha.

"How does one recollect moral discipline? The bodhisattva reflects that there is a moral discipline that is unbroken, unleaking, unbreached, and undefiled, and, though it has no form or color at all, can be guarded and upheld; and, though it has no tangible contact at all, can, through skillful cultivation, be made fully complete, without any fault whatsoever — praised by every buddha and bodhisattva, and itself the very cause of this great Vaipulya Mahāparinirvāṇa. Good son, it is like the great earth, a ship, a garland, a great clan, the great ocean, lye, a dwelling, a sword, a bridge, a skilled physician's own fine medicine, the universal antidote, the wish-fulfilling gem, one's own feet and eyes, or the cool shade of one's own parents — something that cannot be robbed or stolen, cannot be harmed, that fire cannot burn and water cannot sweep away; a great mountain's own staircase, the finest treasure-banner of every buddha and bodhisattva. Should I abide in this precept, I, too, have a share in attaining the fruit of stream-entry — and yet I do not seek it. Why is this? Were I to attain this fruit of stream-entry, I would be unable to save every being broadly. Should I abide in this precept, I attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, and I, too, have a share in this — and this is what I truly desire. Why is this? Were I to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening, I would then be able to expound this wondrous Dharma broadly for the sake of beings, and become their true protector. This is what is meant by the bodhisattva-mahāsattva's recollection of moral discipline.

"How does one recollect giving? The bodhisattva-mahāsattva deeply contemplates that this very act of giving is itself the cause of unsurpassed, complete awakening; every buddha and bodhisattva has drawn near to and cultivated giving of this kind, and I, too, draw near to and cultivate it in just this way — for, without giving generously, one is unable to adorn the fourfold assembly at all. Though giving is unable, in the end, to sever every fetter, it is able to remove and break through the affliction present here and now. Through the very cause and condition of giving, one is forever praised by beings throughout worlds as numerous as the sands of the Gaṅgā, immeasurable and boundless, in every direction. The bodhisattva-mahāsattva, giving beings food, thereby gives them life, and, through this very karmic fruit, remains forever unchanging at the moment he becomes a buddha. Through giving happiness, he attains peace and happiness at the moment he becomes a buddha. When the bodhisattva gives, he seeks wealth in accordance with the Dharma, never encroaching on one person to give to another — and it is for this reason that, becoming a buddha, he attains a pure nirvāṇa. When the bodhisattva gives, he causes beings to obtain what they need without even having to ask for it — and it is for this reason that, becoming a buddha, he attains mastery. Through the cause and condition of my own giving, I cause others to gain strength — and it is for this reason that, becoming a buddha, I obtain the ten powers. Through the cause and condition of giving, I cause others to gain speech — and it is for this reason that, becoming a buddha, I attain the four kinds of unimpeded knowledge. Every buddha and bodhisattva cultivates and accumulates this very giving as the cause of nirvāṇa, and I, too, cultivate and accumulate giving in just this way, as the cause of nirvāṇa — expounded at length within the Avataṃsaka.

"How does one recollect the gods? There are the realms of the Four Heavenly Kings, and so on, up through the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception. Should I have faith, I, too, have a share in attaining the realm of the Four Heavenly Kings. The foolish are every ordinary being — and I am not the same as an ordinary, foolish person. Should I have faith, and, further, have wisdom, I shall then be able to attain this very heaven of the ultimate meaning, and shall then broadly distinguish and expound this heaven of the ultimate meaning for the sake of beings. This is what is meant by the bodhisattva-mahāsattva's recollection of the gods.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the recollection of the Dharma's long cascade of paradoxes ("not form, and severs form, and yet is itself form," repeated across form through consciousness, cause, fruit, mark, and more) as structurally identical to the fivefold pattern already used for suffering, origination, cessation, and the path back in Part 13's "what is real, what is true" dialogue: each triplet states what the Dharma is not (a given category), what it does to that category (severs it), and what it nonetheless is (that category's own reality, once its ordinary appearance is removed) — the Dharma is called "form" not because it is one more form among others, but because it is form's own underlying reality, exactly as the Tathāgata was earlier called neither suffering nor its truth and yet its reality. On recollection of moral discipline, he reads the long list of similes (earth, a ship, a bridge, a wish-fulfilling gem, a parent's cool shade) as each isolating a distinct protective function — bearing weight, carrying across danger, spanning a gap, granting a wish, shielding from harm — rather than one repeated image, and reads the bodhisattva's explicit refusal of the stream-entry fruit his own precepts would earn him as this fascicle's clearest statement yet of why a bodhisattva's practice differs in kind, not merely in degree, from a śrāvaka's: the very same discipline that qualifies one person for personal liberation is, for a bodhisattva, held back and redirected toward unsurpassed awakening specifically because personal liberation would end his own ability to save others.

On recollection of the gods, Huiyuan reads the sūtra's rejection of the ordinary heavens (even the highest, the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception, still impermanent and still ending in birth, old age, illness, and death) as directly continuing the critique Part 9 already made of mistaking a meditative absorption for liberation itself — the same error this fascicle's earlier "what the world knows" section named as characteristic of "the world" generally. "The heaven of the ultimate meaning" (第一義天) is, on his reading, not a seventh heaven added to the list of six desire-realm heavens and the form/formless realms, but a deliberate category shift: it names the permanent nature of every buddha and bodhisattva itself, likened to a wish-fulfilling tree specifically because, unlike the impermanent heavens that merely postpone suffering, it removes affliction at its root — which is why the bodhisattva's own closing vow is not to attain this heaven for himself alone, but to "broadly distinguish and expound" it for the sake of every being, closing the recollection on the same outward-facing note every one of the six recollections has struck in turn.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this long catalogue of reasons for a bodhisattva's inconceivability as organized around three distinct kinds of achievement, corresponding to a bodhisattva still on the path before full awakening: the achievement of genuinely seeking bodhi (holding nothing back of body or life before ever having seen the goal itself, which he reads as the more remarkable case precisely because it precedes any confirming experience), the achievement of benefiting beings despite seeing saṃsāra's faults with total clarity (the burning-house parable making the point that clear-eyed knowledge of danger and willingness to re-enter it are not in tension but the very definition of parental, and bodhisattva, love), and the achievement of remaining unshaken by conditioned existence while still fully immersed in it (entering the womb with full awareness, unlike the three grades of ordinary rebirth-consciousness the sūtra catalogues, none of which retain any clear knowledge of the process at all). He reads the ocean-crossing and thread-suspended-Sumeru exchanges as a matched pair testing the very edge of "conceivable": Kāśyapa's own careful qualification (an asura crossing the ocean is conceivable; a human doing so is not, unless that human is himself noble rather than ordinary) shows the concept "inconceivable" tracking a being's actual category, not the physical difficulty of the feat itself — which is exactly why a bodhisattva crossing the ocean of birth-and-death, an ordinary being by rank but not by function, earns the label.

On the closing analysis of the "three kinds of withdrawal" (bodily, verbal, and the withdrawal that is neither), Huiyuan reads the sūtra's own careful denial that any single dharma is "the body, the act, or the one who withdraws" as forestalling a subtle but real error: if withdrawal from unwholesome conduct required identifying a real agent who withdraws, this would reintroduce exactly the fixed self the whole of this sūtra's teaching on no-self has worked to dismantle. His resolution is that withdrawal is real as an event without requiring a substantial withdrawer behind it — the same logic, on his reading, that lets the bodhisattva say "I destroy affliction" without this being either false (since the destroying genuinely happens) or a lapse into self-view (since no fixed self is asserted to be doing the destroying), because every conditioned dharma, wisdom and affliction alike, "arises differently and ceases differently" and so was never a stable enough entity for one to destroy the other in the naive sense the sentence's grammar suggests. It is this same insight — that ordinary language about agency survives being taken literally, provided one does not mistake grammatical subject for metaphysical substance — that Huiyuan reads as tying together every item on this list, from the ocean-crossing bodhisattva to the fire-cured monk, under the single header "inconceivable."

"Good son, so long as this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, together with its five practices — namely, noble conduct, pure conduct, heavenly conduct, sick conduct, and infant-like conduct — so long as my disciples are able to uphold, recite, copy, and expound its meaning, and are revered, honored, praised, and given every kind of offering by beings, one should know that, at that time, the Buddha-dharma has not yet perished. The bodhisattva Kāśyapa said further to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I have personally heard from the Buddha this very teaching, that the Dharma of the buddha Kāśyapa abided in the world for only seven days, and then perished entirely. World-Honored One, did the tathāgata Kāśyapa possess this very sūtra, or not? If he did possess it, how can it be said to have perished? And if he did not possess it, how can it be said that this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra is the secret treasury of every tathāgata?"

The Buddha said: "Good son, I have already said that only Mañjuśrī fully understands this meaning. I shall now explain it once more — listen closely, with an earnest mind. Good son, every buddha, every world-honored one, has two kinds of dharma: worldly dharma, and the dharma of the ultimate meaning. Worldly dharma is subject to destruction and perishing; the dharma of the ultimate meaning is not subject to destruction. There is, further, a twofold division: impermanence, no-self, no bliss, and no purity, and permanence, bliss, self, and purity. Impermanence, no-self, no bliss, and no purity are subject to destruction; permanence, bliss, self, and purity are not subject to destruction. There is, further, a twofold division: what the two lesser vehicles uphold, and what a bodhisattva upholds. What the two lesser vehicles uphold is subject to destruction; what a bodhisattva upholds is not subject to destruction. There is, further, a twofold division: the external, and the internal. External dharma is subject to destruction; internal dharma is not subject to destruction. There is, further, a twofold division: the conditioned, and the unconditioned. Conditioned dharma is subject to destruction; unconditioned dharma is not subject to destruction. There is, further, a twofold division: what can be attained, and what cannot be attained. What can be attained is subject to destruction; what cannot be attained is not subject to destruction. There is, further, a twofold division: what is shared in common, and what is not shared in common. What is shared in common is subject to destruction; what is not shared in common is not subject to destruction. There is, further, a twofold division: what pertains to humans, and what pertains to the gods. What pertains to humans is subject to destruction; what pertains to the gods is not subject to destruction. There is, further, a twofold division: the eleven other divisions of the scriptures, and the Vaipulya scriptures. The eleven other divisions of the scriptures are subject to destruction; the Vaipulya scriptures are not subject to destruction. Good son, should my disciples uphold, recite, copy, and explain the Vaipulya scriptures, revering them, making offerings to them, honoring and praising them, one should know that, at that time, the Buddha-dharma does not perish.

"Good son, as for what you have just now asked, whether the tathāgata Kāśyapa possessed this very sūtra — good son, this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra is, in every case, the secret treasury of every buddha. Why is this? Though every buddha possesses the eleven other divisions of the scriptures, these do not teach buddha-nature, do not teach that the Tathāgata is permanence, bliss, self, and purity, and do not teach that every buddha, every world-honored one, never, in the ultimate sense, actually enters parinirvāṇa at all — and it is for this very reason that this sūtra is named the Tathāgata's own secret treasury. Because the eleven other divisions of the scriptures do not teach this, it is therefore named a "treasury." It is like a person's seven treasures, which are never brought out for ordinary use, and are therefore called a "treasury." Good son, the reason such a person stores and accumulates these possessions is for the sake of a future need. What kind of future need? Namely: when grain becomes costly, when bandits come to invade the country, when one encounters an evil king, in order to ransom one's own life, or when the roads become difficult and impassable, and wealth becomes hard to obtain — at such a time, these treasures are finally brought out and used. Good son, the secret treasury of every buddha, every tathāgata, is just the same: for the sake of a future age, when evil monks hoard impure possessions, teach the fourfold assembly that the Tathāgata, in the ultimate sense, actually enters parinirvāṇa, recite worldly texts while showing no reverence for the Buddha's own scriptures — when evils of this kind appear in the world, the Tathāgata, wishing to eliminate these very evils and cause beings to keep far from wrongful livelihood and improper gain, then expounds this very sūtra. Should this scripture, this secret treasury, perish and no longer appear in the world, one should know that, at that time, the Buddha-dharma has indeed perished.

"Good son, this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra is forever unchanging — how, then, can you object by asking whether the buddha Kāśyapa possessed this very sūtra? Good son, at the time of the buddha Kāśyapa, every being's own craving was slight, and wisdom flourished; every bodhisattva-mahāsattva was gentle and pliant, easy to transform, possessed of great awesome virtue, retaining every teaching without forgetting it, like a great elephant-king, the whole world entirely pure. Good son, the Dharma of the buddha Kāśyapa, in truth, likewise never perished at all. Why is this? Because it is forever unchanging. Good son, should there be beings who see self where there is no self, and no-self where there is self; who see permanence where there is impermanence, and impermanence where there is permanence; who see bliss where there is no bliss, and no bliss where there is bliss; who see purity where there is impurity, and impurity where there is purity; who see cessation where there is no cessation, and no cessation where there is cessation; who see a transgression where there is none, and no transgression where there is one; who see a light offense as grave, and a grave offense as light; who see a non-vehicle as a vehicle, and a vehicle as a non-vehicle; who see a non-path as the path, and the path as a non-path; who see what is truly bodhi as not bodhi, and mistakenly see what is truly not bodhi as bodhi; who see suffering as not suffering, origination as not origination, cessation as not cessation, and the real as not real; who see what is truly worldly truth as the ultimate truth, and the ultimate truth as worldly truth; who see refuge as not refuge, and non-refuge as refuge; who name the Buddha's own true word the word of Māra, and take what is truly the word of Māra to be the word of the Buddha — it is at a time such as this that every buddha then teaches this Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra. Good son, one might more readily say that a mosquito's proboscis could drain the great ocean to its very bottom than say that the Tathāgata's own Dharma has perished. One might more readily say that a mere breath could blow Mount Sumeru apart and scatter it than say that the Tathāgata's own Dharma has perished. One might more readily say that a rope could bind a raging wind than say that the Tathāgata's own Dharma has perished. One might more readily say that a lotus flower could grow within the fire of the khadira tree than say that the Tathāgata's own Dharma has perished. One might more readily say that the universal antidote itself could become a poison than say that the Tathāgata's own Dharma has perished. One might more readily say that the moon could be made hot, or the sun made cold, than say that the Tathāgata's own Dharma has perished. One might more readily say that each of the four great elements could abandon its own nature than say that the Tathāgata's own Dharma has perished.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the nine-fold division into "what perishes" and "what does not perish" as mapping directly onto the same eight-part list this fascicle's predecessor already used to define "non-attainment" (Part 17), here inverted to answer a different question: not what a bodhisattva attains, but what actually survives a buddha's own parinirvāṇa. Worldly dharma, impermanence and its companions, what the two lesser vehicles uphold, the external, the conditioned, what can be attained, what is shared in common, and what pertains to humans — all of these perish, because all of them are, by their own nature, already in the business of arising and ceasing; the ultimate meaning, permanence-bliss-self-purity, what a bodhisattva upholds, the internal, the unconditioned, the unattainable, the uncommon, and what pertains to the gods do not perish, for exactly the same reason in reverse — and the Vaipulya scriptures, the ninth and final pairing, are where the sūtra's own answer to Kāśyapa's specific question actually lands: they alone, among every division of scripture, teach buddha-nature and the Tathāgata's own permanence, and it is this teaching, not any particular set of physical scrolls, that "does not perish."

On why the buddha Kāśyapa's own Dharma is said to have lasted only seven days and yet, in the very same breath, is said never to have perished at all, Huiyuan reads the sūtra's resolution as turning entirely on the distinction between a teaching's *content* and its *occasion for being spoken*. At Kāśyapa Buddha's own time, beings already lived the truth this sūtra teaches without needing to hear it stated — craving was slight, wisdom abundant, and no one doubted the Tathāgata's own permanence — so the sūtra's own content, though never spoken aloud, was never absent either; the "seven days" names only how long the occasion for silence about it lasted before something else (Huiyuan does not specify what) made restating it unnecessary again. What is unusual, on his reading, about the present age is not that the content has changed but that circumstances now require its explicit restatement — the long catalogue of inverted perceptions (self for no-self, worldly truth for ultimate, Māra's word for the Buddha's own) names exactly the condition under which a truth that once needed no defense suddenly needs an entire sūtra's worth of one. The seven closing "sooner would X happen" hyperboles (a mosquito draining the ocean, a breath scattering Mount Sumeru, a rope binding the wind) are, on his reading, not decorative escalation but a precise rhetorical device already used earlier in this sūtra (Part 16's defense of the Devadatta episode uses an identical structure) for ruling a proposition entirely outside the space of live possibilities, rather than merely asserting it is false.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads these six conditions as arranged in a strict logical sequence, each presupposing that the previous one has already been satisfied: without at least one disciple who understands the Dharma's own most profound meaning (the first condition), nothing downstream can matter at all, since there is no one left to transmit anything after the Buddha's own parinirvāṇa; given such a disciple, the second condition asks whether the lay community actually supports him materially, since a teaching with no sustained means of survival, however well understood, cannot outlast a single generation; given both, the third asks the disciple's own motive for teaching (personal gain versus nirvāṇa itself); the fourth asks whether multiple such disciples can coexist without destructive rivalry; the fifth asks whether they observe the monastic discipline honestly, without hoarding forbidden possessions or falsely claiming attainments they have not reached; and the sixth and final condition — on Huiyuan's reading, the most subtle of the six — asks whether disciples who have passed every other test still resist the temptation to claim private, unverifiable authority over what the Buddha "really meant," setting their own personal transmission above the actual text of the twelve divisions of scripture everyone can check.

On this sixth condition specifically, Huiyuan is careful to note what the sūtra does and does not condemn: the physician's-medicine argument the failing disciple makes (that the Buddha adapted his teaching to different times, places, and capacities) is not itself false — this collection has already seen the Buddha make exactly this argument himself, both about "opening or restricting" (Part 8's precept-adjustment discussions) and about apparently contradictory teachings serving different listeners (Part 17's "what was, what wasn't"). What condemns the argument here is not its content but its use: invoked to place the speaker's own word beyond his fellow monks' ability to check it ("I know; you cannot"), the same true doctrine that elsewhere licenses the Buddha's own flexibility becomes, in a disciple's mouth, a claim to unaccountable authority. The surviving version of the sixth condition answers this not by denying that scripture requires interpretation, but by keeping the check external and shared: "should this meaning accord with the twelve divisions of the scriptures, I shall uphold it; and should it not, I shall set it aside" — a standard any listener can apply for himself, rather than a credential only the speaker possesses.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the long catalogue of future doctrinal disputes as falling into two distinct kinds, worth keeping apart: a first set of purely philosophical disagreements (whether a soul exists, whether an intermediate state between lives exists, whether the three time-periods are real, whether dependent origination is conditioned or not) that any tradition might debate honestly, and a second set of disciplinary disputes (which meats, trades, dwellings, fabrics, and possessions are permitted) where the sūtra's own point is not that the underlying questions are illegitimate but that the community's answers to them will fracture precisely when authority to answer is no longer settled. He reads the Kauśāmbī incident that follows as the sūtra's own worked example of exactly this second kind of fracture reaching its most extreme possible outcome: a numerical majority (five hundred) claiming Vinaya laxity is justified by the Buddha's own supposed relaxation of the rules near his death, opposed by a numerical minority (one hundred) insisting the rules never changed at all — and it is worth noting, on his reading, that the sūtra does not resolve this dispute by having the majority turn out to be wrong about doctrine in some subtle way; it has them commit murder, which settles nothing about who was doctrinally correct and everything about what actually happens to a community once genuine authority is gone.

The sūtra's own resolution of "the Dharma did not perish, despite every visible monk in Jambudvīpa dying or apostatizing," Huiyuan reads as depending entirely on the twelve hundred thousand bodhisattvas the passage names almost in passing — a claim easy to read past, since the narrative's own emotional weight sits with the murdered arhats and the grieving crowd, not with an invisible bodhisattva population the text never shows doing anything. His point in drawing attention to it is that the sūtra's working definition of "the Dharma has not perished," established already in this same fascicle's "two kinds of dharma" teaching, was never about the survival of any particular monk, scripture, or institution — external forms, all of them, subject by nature to destruction — but about whether anyone, anywhere, still holds the content itself, which the bodhisattvas' continued, unremarked presence quietly guarantees even as every visible marker of the Saṅgha's survival is burned, stolen, or slaughtered in the same breath. The assembly's own cry, "the world is empty," and Kāśyapa's answering reassurance that close this fascicle, restate, in the register of raw grief rather than doctrine, the very distinction the whole fascicle has been building toward: what looks, from the outside, like total loss is not the same as what the sūtra means by loss at all.
