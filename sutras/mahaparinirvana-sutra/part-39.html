Six Brahmins Come to Debate the Buddha

The sutra's final chapter opens on the eve of the parinirvāṇa itself, with a crowd of non-Buddhist philosophers, provoked by the Buddha's own teaching that every being possesses buddha-nature, marching to King Ajātaśatru to demand an audience — and being sent, instead, straight to the Buddha. Six of them, one after another, each press their own sharpest objection: that a caused nirvāṇa cannot be permanent, that nirvāṇa is a mere absence, that a self must pervade everywhere, that body and life-force must be either identical or distinct, and that fourteen classic metaphysical questions deserve a straight answer. Each is answered in turn — and each, defeated, asks on the spot to be ordained.

Kauṇḍinya's Teaching, and the Heretics' Fury

Chinese Source

爾時世尊告憍陳如：「色是無常，因滅是色，獲得解脫常住之色，受想行識亦是無常，因滅是識，獲得解脫常住之識。憍陳如！色即是苦，因滅是色，獲得解脫安樂之色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色即是空，因滅空色，獲得解脫非空之色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色是無我，因滅是色，獲得解脫真我之色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色是不淨，因滅是色，獲得解脫清淨之色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色是生老病死之相，因滅是色，獲得解脫非生老病死相之色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色是無明因，因滅是色，獲得解脫非無明因色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！乃至色是生因，因滅是色，獲得解脫非生因色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色者即是四顛倒因，因滅倒色，獲得解脫非四倒因色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色是無量惡法之因，所謂男女等身、食愛、欲愛、貪瞋、嫉妬惡心、慳心、揣食識食思食觸食、卵生胎生濕生化生、五欲五蓋，如是等法皆因於色，因滅色故，獲得解脫無如是等無量惡色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色即是縛，因滅縛色，獲得解脫無縛之色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色即是流，因滅流色，獲得解脫非流之色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色非歸依，因滅是色，獲得解脫歸依之色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色是瘡疣，因滅是色，獲得解脫無瘡疣色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！色非寂靜，因滅是色，獲得涅槃寂靜之色，受想行識亦復如是。憍陳如！若有人能如是知者，是名沙門、名婆羅門，具足沙門、婆羅門法。憍陳如！若離佛法，無有沙門，無婆羅門，亦無沙門、婆羅門法。一切外道，虛假詐稱，都無實行，雖復作相，言有是二，實無是處。何以故？若無沙門、婆羅門法，云何而言有沙門、婆羅門？我常於此大眾之中作師子吼，汝等亦當在大眾中作師子吼。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the World-Honored One said to Kauṇḍinya: "Form is impermanent — through the cessation of this very cause, this form is liberated, and one attains a permanently abiding form; and feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness are impermanent in exactly the same way, and, through the cessation of this consciousness, one attains a permanently abiding consciousness. Kauṇḍinya, form is itself suffering — through the cessation of this cause, one attains a liberated, blissful form, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is itself empty — through the cessation of this empty form as cause, one attains a liberated form that is not empty, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is without self — through the cessation of this cause, one attains a liberated form possessing the true self, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is impure — through the cessation of this cause, one attains a liberated, pure form, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form bears the mark of birth, old age, sickness, and death — through the cessation of this cause, one attains a liberated form free of this mark, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is the very cause of ignorance — through the cessation of this cause, one attains a liberated form no longer the cause of ignorance, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, all the way through, form is the cause of birth itself — through the cessation of this cause, one attains a liberated form no longer the cause of birth, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is itself the cause of the four inversions — through the cessation of this inverted form as cause, one attains a liberated form free of these four inversions as its cause, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is the cause of boundless evil dharmas — that is, the male and female body, craving for food, craving for desire, greed, hatred, envy, an unwholesome mind, a stingy mind, the four kinds of food (edible, contact, intention, and consciousness), birth from an egg, from a womb, from moisture, or by transformation, the five desires, and the five hindrances — dharmas of exactly this kind all have form as their cause, and, through the cessation of form as this cause, one attains liberation from boundless evil forms of exactly this kind, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is itself bondage — through the cessation of this bound form as cause, one attains a liberated form free of bondage, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is itself a current — through the cessation of this flowing form as cause, one attains a liberated form that is not a current, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is no true refuge — through the cessation of this cause, one attains a liberated form that is itself a true refuge, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is itself a wound and a growth — through the cessation of this cause, one attains a liberated form free of any wound or growth, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, form is not still — through the cessation of this cause, one attains the still form of nirvāṇa, and the rest are exactly the same. Kauṇḍinya, should anyone be able to know in this way, he is called a śramaṇa, called a brahmin, fully endowed with the very dharma of the śramaṇa and the brahmin. Kauṇḍinya, apart from the Buddha's own Dharma, there is no such thing as a true śramaṇa, no true brahmin, and no true dharma of the śramaṇa or the brahmin at all. Every non-Buddhist school makes a false and deceptive claim, possessing no genuine practice whatsoever — though they put on the outward appearance and claim to possess these two things, there is, in reality, no such thing there at all. Why is this? If there is no true dharma of the śramaṇa or the brahmin, how could there possibly be a true śramaṇa or brahmin at all? I have always, before this very assembly, roared the lion's roar — you, too, ought now to roar this same lion's roar before the assembly."

爾時外道有無量人，聞是語已，心生瞋惡：「瞿曇今說我等眾中無有沙門及婆羅門，亦無沙門、婆羅門法。我當云何廣設方便語瞿曇言，我等眾中亦有沙門、有沙門法，有婆羅門、有婆羅門法。」 時彼眾中有一梵志，唱如是言：「諸仁者！瞿曇之言，如狂無異。何可撿校？世間狂人，或歌、或舞、或哭、或笑、或罵、或讚，於怨親所不能分別。沙門瞿曇亦復如是，或說我生淨飯王家、或言不生，或說生已行至七步、或說不行，或說從小習學世事、或說我是一切智人，或時處宮受樂生子、或時厭患呵責惡賤，或時親修苦行六年、或時呵責外道苦行，或言從彼欝頭藍弗、阿羅邏等稟承未聞、或時說其無所知曉，或時說言菩提樹下得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提、或時說言我不至樹無所剋獲，或時說言我今此身即是涅槃、或言身滅乃是涅槃。瞿曇所說如狂無異，何故以此而愁憂耶？」 諸婆羅門即便答言：「大士！我等今者何得不愁？沙門瞿曇先出家已，說無常、苦、空、無我等法，我諸弟子聞生恐怖，云何眾生無常、苦、空、無我、不淨？不受其語。今者瞿曇復來至此娑羅林中，為諸大眾說有常樂我淨之法，我諸弟子聞是語已，悉捨我去，受瞿曇語，以是因緣生大愁苦。」 爾時復有一婆羅門作如是言：「諸仁者！諦聽，諦聽。瞿曇沙門名修慈悲，是名虛妄，非真實也。若有慈悲，云何教我諸弟子等自受其法？慈悲果有隨順他意，今違我願，云何言有？若有說言沙門瞿曇不為世間八法所染，是亦虛妄。若言瞿曇少欲知足，今者云何奪我等利？若言種姓是上族者，是亦虛妄，何以故？從昔已來，不見不聞大師子王殘害小鼠。若使瞿曇是上種姓，如何今者惱亂我等？若言瞿曇具大勢力，是亦虛妄。何以故？從昔已來亦不見聞金翅鳥王與烏共諍，若言力大，復以何事與我共鬪？若言瞿曇具他心智，是亦虛妄。何以故？若具此智，以何因緣不知我心？諸仁者！我昔曾從先舊智人聞說是事，過百年已，世間當有一妖幻出。即是瞿曇。如是妖惑，今於此處娑羅林中將滅不久，汝等今者不應愁惱。」 爾時復有一尼犍子答言：「仁者！我今愁苦，不為自身弟子供養，但為世間癡闇無眼，不識福田及非福田，棄捨先舊智婆羅門，供養年少，以為愁耳。瞿曇沙門大知呪術，因呪術力，能令一身作無量身，令無量身還作一身，或以自身作男女像、牛羊象馬。我力能滅如是呪術，瞿曇沙門呪術既滅，汝等當還多得供養，受於安樂。」 爾時復有一婆羅門作如是言：「諸仁者！瞿曇沙門成就具足無量功德，是故汝等不應與諍。」 大眾答言：「癡人，云何說言沙門瞿曇具大功德？其生七日，母便命終，是可得名福德相耶？」 婆羅門言：「罵時不瞋，打時不報，當知即是大福德相。其身具足三十二相、八十種好、無量神通，是故當知是福德相。心無憍慢，先意問訊，言語柔軟，初無麁獷，年志俱盛心不卒暴，王國多財無所愛戀，捨之出家如棄涕唾，是故我說沙門瞿曇成就具足無量功德。」 大眾答言：「善哉，仁者！瞿曇沙門實如所說，成就無量神通變化，我不與彼捔試是事。瞿曇沙門受性柔軟，不堪苦行，生長深宮，不綜外事，唯可軟語。不知伎藝、書籍、論議，請共詳辯正法之要。彼若勝我，我當給事。我若勝彼，彼當事我。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, countless non-Buddhist practitioners, hearing this teaching, gave rise to anger and resentment in their hearts: "Gautama now says that our own assembly contains no true śramaṇa or brahmin, nor any true dharma of the śramaṇa or the brahmin at all — how, then, ought we to devise some broad skillful means to tell Gautama that our own assembly does indeed possess the śramaṇa, and the śramaṇa's own dharma, the brahmin, and the brahmin's own dharma?" At that time, among this very assembly, a certain brahmin ascetic proclaimed: "Gentlemen, Gautama's own words are no different from the ravings of a madman — why examine them at all? Madmen of the world, at times, sing, at times dance, at times weep, at times laugh, at times curse, at times praise, unable to distinguish friend from foe. The śramaṇa Gautama is exactly the same: at times he says he was born in King Śuddhodana's own household, at times says he was not born at all; at times says that, once born, he walked seven steps, at times says he did not walk at all; at times says he studied worldly matters from his own youth, at times says he is himself the all-knowing one; at times he dwelt in the palace enjoying pleasure and fathering a son, at times grew weary of it and denounced it as base and contemptible; at times personally practiced austerities for six years, at times denounced the very austerities of non-Buddhist practitioners; at times says he received his own teaching, unprecedented, from Udraka Rāmaputra and Ārāḍa Kālāma, at times says these teachers knew nothing at all; at times says he attained supreme, perfect awakening beneath the bodhi tree, at times says he never reached the tree and attained nothing whatsoever; at times says this very body of his is itself nirvāṇa, at times says that only the body's own cessation is nirvāṇa. Gautama's own words are no different from the ravings of a madman — why, then, ought we to be troubled by them at all?" The brahmins then answered together: "Great one, how could we possibly not be troubled? The śramaṇa Gautama, having first left the household life, taught the dharmas of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self — our own disciples, hearing this, were struck with terror, saying: 'How could beings be impermanent, suffering, empty, without self, and impure?' — and would not accept his teaching. Yet now Gautama has come once more, to this very sāla grove, and teaches the great assembly the dharma of permanence, bliss, self, and purity — and our own disciples, hearing this teaching, have entirely abandoned us and gone over to him, accepting Gautama's own words instead. It is on account of exactly this that we are struck with such great sorrow and suffering." At that time, a certain brahmin, further, said: "Gentlemen, listen closely, listen closely. The śramaṇa Gautama is said to cultivate loving-kindness and compassion — this is itself false, not true at all. Were he truly compassionate, why would he teach our own disciples to abandon their own dharma and take up his? True compassion's own fruit accords with another's own wishes — yet he now goes against our own wishes entirely; how, then, can it be said that he possesses it? Should anyone say that the śramaṇa Gautama is untainted by the eight worldly conditions, this too is false. Should it be said that Gautama has few desires and knows contentment, why, then, does he now rob us of our own benefit? Should it be said that his lineage is of the highest clan, this too is false — why is this? Since ancient times, no one has ever seen or heard of a great lion king harming a small mouse; were Gautama truly of the highest lineage, how could he now trouble and disturb us in this way? Should it be said that Gautama possesses great power, this too is false — why is this? Since ancient times, no one has ever seen or heard of the garuḍa king contending with a mere crow; were his power truly great, what business would he have contending with us at all? Should it be said that Gautama possesses the wisdom to know another's own mind, this too is false — why is this? Were he truly endowed with this wisdom, for what reason does he fail to know our own minds? Gentlemen, I myself once heard, from the wise elders of old, that, after a hundred years had passed, a certain deceptive apparition would appear in the world — and this is none other than Gautama himself. This very deceptive apparition, here in this sāla grove, shall soon perish of its own accord — you gentlemen ought not, therefore, to trouble yourselves with sorrow over this at all." At that time, a certain Nirgrantha disciple, further, answered: "Gentlemen, my own sorrow is not for the sake of my own disciples' offerings — it is only that the world, blind and benighted with ignorance, unable to distinguish a true field of merit from a false one, has abandoned the wise brahmin elders of old and now makes offerings to the young instead — this alone is the cause of my own sorrow. The śramaṇa Gautama is greatly skilled in magical spells, and, through the very power of these spells, is able to make a single body into countless bodies, and countless bodies once more into a single body, or to transform his own body into the likeness of a man or a woman, an ox, a sheep, an elephant, or a horse. My own power is able to destroy spells of exactly this kind — and, once the śramaṇa Gautama's own spells are destroyed, you gentlemen shall once again receive abundant offerings, and enjoy peace and ease." At that time, a certain brahmin, further, said: "Gentlemen, the śramaṇa Gautama has, in truth, fully accomplished boundless merit — you ought not, therefore, to dispute with him at all." The assembly answered: "Foolish man, how can you say that the śramaṇa Gautama possesses great merit? He was but seven days old when his own mother died — could this possibly be called the mark of great merit?" The brahmin said: "Never angered when cursed, never retaliating when struck — one should know that this itself is the mark of great merit. His own body is fully endowed with the thirty-two marks, the eighty minor characteristics, and boundless supernormal powers — one should therefore know that this, too, is the mark of great merit. His mind is free of arrogance; he greets others first with kind inquiry; his speech is gentle, never coarse or rough from the very start; though still in the fullness of his own youth and vigor, his mind is never rash or impulsive; possessing a kingdom's own great wealth, he holds no attachment to it at all, relinquishing it to leave the household life exactly as one discards spittle or mucus — it is for this very reason that I say the śramaṇa Gautama has fully accomplished boundless merit." The assembly answered: "Excellent, sir! The śramaṇa Gautama has indeed, exactly as you say, accomplished boundless supernormal powers and transformations — we do not intend to test him on this point at all. The śramaṇa Gautama, however, is by nature soft and delicate, unable to endure real austerity, raised deep within the palace, unfamiliar with worldly affairs, capable only of gentle speech; he knows nothing of craft, of scripture, or of formal debate — let us, then, request a detailed disputation on the essential points of the true Dharma. Should he defeat us, we shall serve him; should we defeat him, he shall serve us instead."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Kauṇḍinya's opening teaching as a single formula applied to sixteen different registers in succession — form (and, by extension, every aggregate) is impermanent/suffering/empty/selfless/impure/marked by birth-old-age-sickness-death/the cause of ignorance/the cause of birth/the cause of the four inversions/the cause of boundless evil/bondage/a current/no refuge/a wound/not still — with liberation, each time, simply being this same form once its causal role has ceased, never a separate thing added from outside (T1764's consistent method of treating "liberated form" as the same form purified of function, not a different substance). The narrative that follows — heretics conferring, marching to the king, being rebuffed and then permitted — is read as three deliberate stages building toward the confrontation: first mutual reassurance among the heretics themselves, then a failed attempt to enlist royal authority against the Buddha, and finally King Ajātaśatru's own shrewd redirection, refusing to adjudicate a debate he has no standing to judge and sending the heretics to the only party actually qualified to answer them. Huiyuan classifies the ten visitors who appear across this fascicle and the next into three broad types by their underlying wrong view: Jātiśroṇa and Vātsīputra hold the wrong view that nirvāṇa itself must be impermanent; Senika and the Kāśyapa-gotra brahmin hold a wrong view of self; and Pūrṇa and Śuddha hold an extremist view built on the fourteen unanswerable questions — a taxonomy worth keeping in mind as each debate unfolds in turn.

Huiyuan identifies Jātiśroṇa's underlying system as Kapila's own Sāṃkhya cosmology, and glosses the technical sequence precisely (T1764, lines 23063–23087): from an unseen primordial nature (postulated because the seer Kapila, having attained worldly clairvoyance reaching back eighty thousand eons, could see no further and inferred an eternal ground beneath his own vision's limit) arises the "great principle" (the first stirring of awareness), then the sense of self, then twenty-five categories in total — the five elements, five subtle sense-objects, five sense-faculties, five action-faculties, the equalizing mind, and the three root qualities of taint, coarseness, and darkness (craving, hatred, and ignorance). The Buddha's own refutation does not attack this cosmology directly but instead borrows Jātiśroṇa's own generative-cause/revealing-cause distinction — already present in the brahmin's own system — and uses it to answer the objection on its own terms: exactly as the brahmin admits that a lamp reveals an object without itself producing that object as its "child," the Buddha's own path reveals nirvāṇa's own already-permanent purity without generating it as a mere impermanent effect, so an impermanent cause and a permanent fruit are not the contradiction they first appear to be. This is the identical generative/revealing distinction used against the milk-curd debate in Part 28 and reprised for nirvāṇa's own causation in Part 36, here turned outward for the first time against a rival system's own vocabulary rather than developed from within the sūtra's own framework — a fitting move for the sūtra's final chapter, closing the argument by showing it was never parochial to begin with.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Vātsīputra's objection as a natural sequel to Jātiśroṇa's: having conceded that nirvāṇa's cause need not share its own impermanence, Vātsīputra presses the harder-seeming problem that nirvāṇa, defined negatively as "the absence of affliction," must then be a mere non-existence, and non-existence carries none of the positive qualities the sūtra claims for it. The Buddha's resolution locates nirvāṇa specifically within the third of the sūtra's own standard four-part taxonomy of non-existence — carried over verbatim from the same fourfold framework used in Parts 32 and 34 — mutual, reciprocal absence between two distinct things (an ox lacking horse-nature, nirvāṇa lacking affliction-nature), which alone, among the four kinds, does not itself entail impermanence or absolute nothingness the way the other three do. The three-medicine simile then does the same work in reverse, showing by direct analogy why "absence" is not automatically privative: the medicine that cures craving contains no craving within it, precisely because it is medicine and not the disease — an absence that constitutes health rather than a mere gap. Vātsīputra's death by self-immolation immediately after ordination, and his own dying wish to atone for having used the Buddha's clan-name too familiarly, gives this exchange an emotional register distinct from Jātiśroṇa's purely intellectual concession — the heretics' own final, ironic misreading of his supernormal feats at the pyre as proof that he has "learned Gautama's magic" closes the episode on the same note of willful misunderstanding that opened this fascicle's whole confrontation.

「善男子！汝亦說言，當受地獄、當受餓鬼、當受畜生、當受人天。我若先遍五道中者，云何方言當受諸趣？汝亦說言，父母和合然後生子。若子先有，云何復言和合已有？是故一人有五趣身，若是五處先有身者，何因緣故為身造業？是故不平。善男子！汝意若謂我是作者，是義不然。何以故？若我作者，何因緣故自作苦事？然今眾生實有受苦，是故當知我非作者。若言是苦非我所作，不從因生，一切諸法亦當如是，不從因生，何因緣故說我作耶？善男子！眾生苦樂實從因緣，如是苦樂能作憂喜，憂時無喜，喜時無憂。或喜、或憂，智人云何說是常耶？善男子！汝說我常，若是常者，云何說有十時別異？常法不應有歌羅邏時乃至老時，虛空常法，尚無一時，況有十時。善男子！我者非是歌羅邏時乃至老時，云何說有十時別異？善男子！若我作者，是我亦有盛時衰時，眾生亦有盛時衰時。若我爾者，云何是常？善男子！我若作者，云何一人有利有鈍？善男子！我若作者，是我能作身業、口業。口業若是我所作者，云何口說無有我耶？云何自疑有耶、無耶？善男子！汝意若謂離眼有見，是義不然。何以故？若離眼已別有見者，何須此眼，乃至身根亦復如是。汝意若謂我雖能見，要因眼見，是亦不然。何以故？如有人言須曼那花能燒大村。云何能燒？因火能燒。汝立我見，亦復如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, there was, further, within the assembly, a brahmin ascetic named Senika, who said: "Gautama, does a self exist?" The Tathāgata remained silent. "Gautama, does no self exist?" The Tathāgata remained silent. He asked a second and a third time in exactly the same way, and the Buddha remained silent each time. Senika said: "Gautama, if every being possesses a self — pervading every place, single, and an agent — why, then, does Gautama remain silent and refuse to answer?" The Buddha said: "Senika, do you yourself teach that this self pervades every place?" Senika answered: "Gautama, it is not I alone who teach this — every wise person teaches it in exactly this way." The Buddha said: "Good son, were this self truly to pervade every place, it ought to receive the retribution of all five destinies at one and the same moment — and, were this so, for what reason, then, do you brahmins refrain from committing every evil, to avoid falling into hell, and cultivate every wholesome dharma, to receive a heavenly body instead?" Senika said: "Gautama, in my own system, the self is, in fact, of two kinds: the acting-body self, and the eternal-body self. It is for the sake of the acting-body self that one cultivates departure from evil dharmas, so as not to enter hell, and cultivates every wholesome dharma, so as to be born in heaven." The Buddha said: "Good son, as you yourself say that this self pervades every place — should this self exist within the acting body, one should know it to be impermanent; and should it not exist within the acting body, how, then, could it be said to pervade everywhere at all?" "Gautama, the very self I posit exists within the acting body, and is also an eternal dharma. Gautama, it is exactly like a person whose house catches fire — when the house burns, its owner escapes, and one cannot then say that, because the house was burned, its owner, too, was burned. My own system is exactly the same: though this acting body is indeed impermanent, at the very moment of its impermanence, the self simply departs from it — and it is for this reason that this self is both pervading and eternal at once." The Buddha said: "Good son, your claim that the self is both pervading and eternal at once does not hold. Why is this? 'Pervading' is, in fact, of two kinds: the permanent, and the impermanent; and, further, of two kinds: the material, and the immaterial. Therefore, should you say that a thing pervades everything, it would then be both permanent and impermanent at once, both material and immaterial at once. And should you say that the house's own owner is able to escape and is therefore not called impermanent, this reasoning does not hold either. Why is this? Because the house is not the owner, and the owner is not the house — one burns while the other escapes, and it is precisely because they are distinct that this can happen. Your self, however, is not like this at all. Why is this? Because, on your own account, the self is itself form, and form is itself the self — the formless is itself the self, and the self is itself the formless. How, then, could you possibly say that, at the very moment form becomes impermanent, the self simply escapes from it? Good son, should your own intention be that every being shares one and the same self, this would directly contradict both worldly and transcendent dharma alike. Why is this? Because worldly convention speaks of father, son, mother, and daughter — yet, were the self truly one and the same for all, the father would be the son, and the son the father; the mother would be the daughter, and the daughter the mother; the enemy would be the friend, and the friend the enemy; this very thing would be that thing, and that thing this very thing. Therefore, should it be said that every being shares one and the same self, this directly contradicts both worldly and transcendent dharma." Senika said: "I, too, do not teach that every being shares one and the same self — I teach, rather, that each single person possesses his own individual self." The Buddha said: "Good son, should it be said that each single person possesses his own individual self, this would then amount to many selves, and this reasoning, too, does not hold. Why is this? As you yourself previously said, the self pervades everything — and, were it to pervade everything, every being's own karmic faculties ought to be identical: when a god sees something, a buddha ought likewise to see it; when a god acts, a buddha ought likewise to act; when a god hears something, a buddha ought likewise to hear it — and every other dharma ought to be exactly the same. And, should what a god sees not be what a buddha sees, one ought not to say that the self pervades every place at all — and, should it not pervade everywhere, it is thereby impermanent." Senika said: "Gautama, every being's own self does indeed pervade everything — yet dharma and non-dharma themselves do not pervade everything, and it is on account of this very reasoning that a buddha's own action differs from a god's own action. It is for this reason, Gautama, that one ought not to say that, when a buddha sees something, a god ought likewise to see it, or that, when a buddha hears something, a god ought likewise to hear it." The Buddha said: "Good son, are dharma and non-dharma not themselves produced by karma?" Senika said: "Gautama, they are indeed produced by karma." The Buddha said: "Good son, should dharma and non-dharma be produced by karma, they would then be the very same kind of thing — how, then, can they be called different? Why is this? Wherever a buddha's own karma operates, a god's own self is likewise present there; and wherever a god's own karma operates, a buddha's own self is likewise present there — and so, when a buddha acts, a god ought likewise to act, and dharma and non-dharma ought to be exactly the same. Good son, therefore, if every being, and dharma and non-dharma alike, are as you describe, the fruit each obtains, too, ought to be no different from another's. Good son, it is exactly like how, from a seed, a fruit is produced — yet this very seed never once reflects or distinguishes, saying: 'I shall produce only the fruit of a brahmin, and shall never produce a fruit for a kṣatriya, a vaiśya, or a śūdra.' Why is this? Because, in producing a fruit from a seed, there is never any such obstruction between these four castes at all — dharma and non-dharma, too, are exactly the same, unable to distinguish and say: 'I shall produce a fruit only for a buddha's own self, and not for a god's own self,' or 'I shall produce a fruit for a god's own self, and not for a buddha's own self.' Why is this? Because karma is impartial and equal for all." Senika said: "Gautama, it is exactly like a single room containing a hundred thousand lamps — though their wicks each differ, their light is entirely undifferentiated. The distinctness of the lamp-wicks is a simile for dharma and non-dharma; the undifferentiated light is a simile for beings' own shared self." The Buddha said: "Good son, your use of lamplight as a simile for the self does not hold. Why is this? A room is one thing, a lamp another. This very lamp's own light exists both beside its own wick and pervading the entire room at once. Were your own self truly like this, then a self ought to exist on the side of both dharma and non-dharma alike, and dharma and non-dharma, too, ought to exist within the self. Should dharma and non-dharma possess no self at all, one could not then say that the self pervades every place; and should both truly possess it, how, then, could you still use the wick and its light as your simile at all? Good son, should your own intention be that the wick and its light are truly, fundamentally distinct from one another, then for what reason does the light grow brighter as the wick grows, and grow dim as the wick withers? It is for this very reason that you ought not to use the lamp-wick as a simile for dharma and non-dharma, and the undifferentiated light as a simile for the self. Why is this? Because dharma, non-dharma, and the self are, in fact, one and the same single thing." Senika said: "Gautama, this lamp-simile you have offered is itself inauspicious. Why is this? Were the lamp-simile auspicious, I myself would already have offered it first — and, since it is inauspicious, why do you offer it at all?" "Good son, the simile I have offered is neither made auspicious nor inauspicious by me at all — speak of it however you yourself see fit. This very simile can equally well be stated as 'light exists apart from the wick,' or as 'light exists only together with the wick.' Since your own mind is not impartial, you speak of the wick and the light as a simile for dharma and non-dharma on one side, and for the self on the other. It is for this very reason that I now correct you: the wick is itself the light, and light exists apart from the wick as well; dharma is itself the self, and the self is itself dharma; non-dharma is itself the self, and the self is itself non-dharma. Why, then, do you now accept only one side of this and refuse the other? A simile handled in this way is inauspicious for you, not for me — and it is for this very reason that I now use it, in turn, to refute you. Good son, a simile of this kind is, in truth, no genuine simile at all — and, being no genuine simile, it is auspicious for me, and inauspicious for you. Good son, should your own intention be that, if it is inauspicious for me, it must equally be inauspicious for you as well, this reasoning does not hold. Why is this? One sees, in the world, a person cutting himself with his own blade, using on himself what was meant for another — the very simile you yourself have offered is exactly the same, auspicious for me, and inauspicious for you." Senika said: "Gautama, you previously accused me of an unequal, partial mind — yet what you yourself have just said is equally partial. Why is this? Gautama now turns what is auspicious toward himself, and what is inauspicious toward me — and, reasoning from this, it is you who are truly partial." The Buddha said: "Good son, exactly as my own partiality is able to refute your own partiality, and thereby brings you to impartiality, this very partiality of mine is itself auspicious. My own partiality refutes your partiality and brings you to impartiality — and this is itself my own impartiality. Why is this? Because it thereby accords with the impartiality every noble one has already attained." Senika said: "Gautama, I myself am always impartial — how can you say that you have destroyed my own impartiality? Every being equally possesses this self in perfect impartiality — how, then, can you say that I am partial at all?" "Good son, you yourself also say that one is destined to receive the retribution of hell, of hungry ghosts, of animals, and of humans and gods — yet, were this self of yours already pervading all five destinies from the very start, how, then, could you still speak of being 'destined' to receive any of these states at all? You yourself also say that a child is born only once father and mother have come together — yet, if the child already existed beforehand, how can it then be said that it only comes to exist once they have come together? Therefore, should a single person already possess bodies in all five destinies at once, for what reason, then, would he still need to create karma for the sake of any one particular body? It is for this very reason that your view is not impartial at all. Good son, should your own intention be that the self is itself an agent, this reasoning does not hold. Why is this? Were the self truly an agent, for what reason would it inflict suffering upon itself? Yet beings do, in fact, genuinely experience suffering — and therefore one should know that the self is not an agent at all. And should it be said that this suffering is not caused by the self, and does not arise from any cause at all, then every other dharma, too, ought to be exactly the same, arising from no cause whatsoever — for what reason, then, would you still say that the self is the agent of anything at all? Good son, beings' own suffering and pleasure genuinely arise from causes and conditions — and suffering and pleasure of this kind are able to produce sorrow and joy in turn, with no joy present at the moment of sorrow, and no sorrow present at the moment of joy. Being, at times, joyful, and, at other times, sorrowful — how could the wise possibly call this permanent? Good son, you yourself say that the self is permanent — yet, if it is truly permanent, how can it be said to pass through ten distinct stages? A permanent dharma ought never to pass through the kalala stage, and, all the way through, the stage of old age — even space itself, a permanent dharma, has not even a single such stage, how much less, then, ten stages? Good son, the self, if it is not itself the kalala stage, and, all the way through, not itself the stage of old age, how, then, can it be said to pass through these ten distinct stages at all? Good son, were the self truly an agent, this self, too, ought to have periods of flourishing and periods of decline, exactly as beings themselves have periods of flourishing and decline — and, were this truly so, how could it possibly be called permanent? Good son, were the self truly an agent, why, then, does one single person possess sharp faculties while another possesses dull ones? Good son, were the self truly an agent, this self would be able to produce bodily karma and verbal karma — and, were verbal karma truly produced by the self, why, then, does the mouth itself say that no self exists at all, and why does it doubt, of its own accord, whether the self exists or not? Good son, should your own intention be that seeing exists apart from the eye, this reasoning does not hold. Why is this? Were there truly a separate seeing apart from the eye, what need would there be for this very eye at all — and, all the way through, exactly the same holds for the body's own faculty of touch. And should your own intention be that, though the self itself is able to see, it necessarily sees only through the eye, this, too, does not hold. Why is this? It is exactly like someone saying that the campaka flower is able to burn down an entire village — in what sense could it possibly burn it down? Only through fire is it able to burn. Your own claim that the self sees is exactly the same."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Senika's initial silence-provoking questions as a deliberate trap the Buddha refuses to walk into: answering either "a self exists" or "no self exists" as a flat, unqualified statement would concede Senika's own framing before the actual debate even begins, so the Buddha's silence forces Senika to commit to his own position first (T1764, lines 23173–23174). Huiyuan classifies Senika's core doctrine as positing a self both "identical to" the acting body (the house-fire simile, self as the escaping owner) and "distinct from" it — an unstable middle position the Buddha's refutation systematically collapses by showing that "pervading everywhere" itself splits into permanent/impermanent and material/immaterial senses that cannot be held together consistently, that a self identical with form cannot coherently be said to "escape" form's own impermanence, and that a self genuinely shared by every being would erase the very kinship distinctions (father is not son, friend is not enemy) that make ordinary language possible at all. The lamp-and-many-wicks simile receives special attention: Huiyuan notes that Senika's own comparison secretly proves the opposite of what he intends, since wick and light are observably interdependent (the light visibly brightens and dims with the wick's own condition), which is exactly the entanglement between self and its "acting body" that Senika's whole argument needs to deny — the Buddha's "inauspicious for you, not for me" rejoinder turns the borrowed simile back on its own author with exactly the same reversal-through-borrowed-vocabulary technique used against Jātiśroṇa's generative/revealing cause distinction two debates earlier.

Senika said: "Gautama, it is exactly like a person grasping a sickle, thereby able to cut grass — my own self, through the five faculties, seeing, hearing, and all the way through to touch, is exactly the same." "Good son, a person and a sickle are two distinct things, and it is precisely for this reason that grasping the sickle accomplishes something. Apart from the faculties themselves, there exists no further, separate self at all — how, then, can you say that the self accomplishes something through the faculties? Good son, should your own intention be that grasping the sickle is able to cut, and the self is exactly the same — does this self have hands, or does it not? If it has hands, why does it not grasp the sickle itself? And if it has no hands, how can it be called an agent at all? Good son, that which is actually able to cut the grass is simply the sickle itself, not the self, and not the person. Were the self and the person themselves capable of cutting, what need would there be for the sickle at all? Good son, a person performs two actions: grasping the grass, and grasping the sickle — and it is the sickle alone that possesses the power to cut. Beings' own act of seeing is exactly the same: the eye is able to see form, arising from a coming-together of factors. If seeing arises from the very coming-together of causes and conditions, how could the wise possibly say that a self exists there at all? Good son, should your own intention be that the body acts while the self receives the result, this reasoning does not hold. Why is this? One never sees, in the world, a god's own karma being performed while a buddha's own self receives its fruit. And should it be said that it is not the body that acts, and that the self does not receive through any causal process at all, then for what reason do you yourselves seek liberation through causes and conditions at all? If this very body of yours was not originally produced by causes and conditions, then, once liberation is attained, a further body ought likewise to arise again without any cause at all — and, exactly as with the body, every affliction, too, ought to be exactly the same." Senika said: "Gautama, my own self is, in fact, of two kinds: the knowing self, and the unknowing self. The unknowing self is able to acquire a body; the knowing self is able to relinquish the body entirely. It is exactly like an unfired clay pot — once it has been fired, it loses its original raw color and never returns to it again. A wise person's own affliction is exactly the same: once it has been extinguished and destroyed, it never arises again." The Buddha said: "Good son, as for this thing you call 'knowing' — is it wisdom itself that knows, or is it the self that knows? If it is wisdom that knows, why, then, do you say that the self is the knower? And if it is the self that knows, why, then, does it still need to seek out wisdom through skillful means at all? Should your own intention be that the self knows by means of wisdom, this is exactly the same flawed logic as the flower-simile already refuted. Good son, it is exactly like a thorn tree, whose own nature is itself to be thorny — one would never say that the tree 'grasps' its own thorns in order to be thorny. Wisdom, too, is exactly the same: wisdom itself is simply what knows — how, then, can you say that the self 'grasps' wisdom in order to know? Good son, in your own system, when the self attains liberation, does an unknowing self attain it, or does a knowing self attain it? If an unknowing self attains it, one should then know that it still possesses affliction in full; and if a knowing self attains it, one should then know that it still possesses the five faculties and every sense-organ intact — why is this? Because, apart from the faculties themselves, there exists no separate knowing at all. And, possessing every faculty intact, how, then, could it still be called attaining liberation at all? Good son, it is exactly like a person trying to pull a thorn out of empty space — you are exactly the same. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this second half of the Senika debate as systematically dismantling four successive refuges the self-view can retreat to once "pervading everywhere" has already collapsed (T1764, lines 23285–23350): the self as a mere passive instrument the body wields (refuted by the sickle simile — a sickle cuts, not the hand that grasps it, and positing a self "using" the faculties the way a hand uses a sickle only pushes the question back one step, since the self would then need its own hands to grasp anything at all); the self as split into a knowing and unknowing aspect to explain how liberation removes affliction without removing the self itself (refuted by asking whether it is wisdom or the self that actually knows, since either answer undermines the self's own claimed role); the self as what remembers (refuted by pointing out that a truly abiding, unchanging self ought never to forget anything, whereas memory is plainly patchy and involuntary); and the self as what sees and hears (refuted by the six-consciousness analysis, showing that "seeing" is fully accounted for by the coming-together of eye, form, light, and attention, with nothing left over for a self to explain). The wind-through-a-bell and heated-iron-in-water similes make the same point from a different angle: a sound clearly produced by natural causes still gets described in agent-language ("the bell rings," "the iron hisses") without anyone supposing a genuine agent stands behind it, and ordinary speech like "I see" or "I act" is exactly this same kind of grammatically convenient shorthand for a process with no single owner. Senika's own confession — that his initial hostility traced back to a mind still full of pride — closes the debate on the same practical note as Vātsīputra's dying apology: for Huiyuan, the metaphysical argument and the moral posture underlying it were never fully separable, and Senika's own conversion completes only once both are addressed together.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan classifies this brahmin, together with Senika, as holding a self-view — but where Senika's self was positive and pervasive, this ascetic's question presupposes a self-view built on strict identity or strict difference between body and life-force, with no middle position available (T1764, line 23166). The Buddha's silence here works the same way it did with Senika: any flat answer, "identical" or "distinct," would already concede the false dichotomy the question smuggles in. The detached-flame simile is the debate's own turning point — the ascetic thinks he has found a genuinely uncaused event (a flame traveling without fuel), and the Buddha's correction (the flame's continuation still depends causally on the wind, simply a different cause than firewood) directly answers his real question about the intermediate state between death and rebirth: body and life-force are "identical" in the sense that both depend on ignorance and craving as their shared sustaining cause, and "distinct" in the sense that the body itself can cease while that causal continuity — like the detached flame riding the wind — carries on without it. The closing fire-and-smoke exchange, where the ascetic supplies his own doctrinal gloss (fire as affliction, smoke as affliction's own foul retribution) before the Buddha even asks, mirrors the same collaborative-realization pattern seen with Jātiśroṇa's four-noble-truths conclusion — each of these converts does not merely accept a stated answer but demonstrates having internalized the method well enough to complete the analysis in his own words.

The Buddha said: "Good son, clinging to a view of this kind is itself called the dharma of birth and death — and, the Tathāgata already being departed from the dharma of birth and death, he does not cling to any such view at all. Good son, the Tathāgata is called one who is able to see and able to teach, yet is never called one who clings." "Gautama, in what way are you able to see, and in what way are you able to teach?" The Buddha said: "Good son, I am able to clearly see suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path, and to distinguish and proclaim these four noble truths. Seeing in this way, I am thereby able to depart far from every view, every craving, every current, and every arrogance — and it is for this very reason that I am fully endowed with pure, holy conduct, the unsurpassed stillness, and have attained a permanent body — a body that is likewise neither east, west, south, nor north." Pūrṇa said: "Gautama, for what cause and condition is this permanent body neither east, west, south, nor north?" The Buddha said: "Good son, I shall now question you in turn — answer as you see fit. What do you think, good son? Suppose a great mass of fire were burning before you — at the very moment it burns, do you know that it is burning?" "Just so, Gautama." "And, at the moment this fire goes out, do you know that it has gone out?" "Just so, Gautama." "Pūrṇa, should someone ask you, of this fire that burned before you: from where did it come when it burned, and to where did it go when it was extinguished — how would you answer?" "Gautama, should someone ask me this, I would answer: this fire, at the moment of its arising, depended upon a gathering of many conditions; and, once its original conditions were exhausted and no new conditions had yet arrived, this fire was thereby extinguished." "And should someone further ask: once this fire was extinguished, to which direction did it go — how, then, would you answer?" "Gautama, I would answer: it was extinguished because its conditions were exhausted, and it went to no direction at all." "Good son, the Tathāgata is exactly the same. There is a sense in which impermanent form, and, all the way through, impermanent consciousness, burns on account of craving — and, so burning, one receives the twenty-five states of existence; and it is in this very sense, while burning, that this fire may be spoken of as east, west, south, or north. Yet, once present craving is extinguished, the fruit of these twenty-five states of existence no longer burns at all — and, no longer burning, one cannot speak of it as having any direction, east, west, south, or north. Good son, the Tathāgata has already extinguished impermanent form, all the way through to impermanent consciousness, and it is for this reason that his own body is permanent — and, this body being permanent, one cannot speak of it as having any direction, east, west, south, or north, at all." Pūrṇa said: "Please allow me to offer a simile — I ask only that you accept it." The Buddha said: "Excellent, excellent! Speak as you wish." "World-Honored One, it is exactly like a sāla grove outside a great village, within which stands a single tree, having grown there a full hundred years before the grove itself arose around it. The keeper of this grove waters it and tends to it according to the season, and this tree, grown old and decayed, sheds its own bark, branches, and leaves entirely, so that only its true, solid heartwood remains. The Tathāgata is exactly the same: everything old and decayed within him has already been entirely removed, and only the true, real Dharma remains. World-Honored One, I now greatly wish to leave the household life and cultivate the path." The Buddha said: "Welcome, monk." Having spoken these words, Pūrṇa left the household life at that very moment, and, his outflows exhausted, realized the fruit of arhatship.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan identifies Pūrṇa's fourteen questions as the classic set of metaphysical undecidables attributed to non-Buddhist śramaṇas across the wider canon — four on whether the self and the world are permanent, four on whether they are bounded, four on the Tathāgata's own state after death, plus two more on whether body and life-force are one or distinct — and notes that Nāgārjuna treats this same fourteenfold set at length elsewhere, so the sūtra here assumes rather than re-derives their classic formulation (T1764, lines 23386–23396). Huiyuan's own gloss on why the Buddha refuses to answer any of them directly is precise: it is not that the questions have no true answer, but that answering any one of them as a flat, fixed position — "the world is permanent, full stop" — is itself already the disease being diagnosed, since fixation on any single view, whichever one, breeds the same entanglement, attachment, and suffering regardless of which side is chosen. This is the sūtra's own most extreme instance of the principle running through the whole sūtra since Parts 33–36: a settled, unqualified answer is not a stronger position than an unsettled one, but a narrower and more dangerous one. The extinguished-fire simile answers Pūrṇa's real underlying worry — what happens to the Tathāgata after parinirvāṇa — not by picking one of the fourteen fixed options but by dissolving the question's own presupposition that "going somewhere" or "not going somewhere" are the only two possibilities: a fire needing no further direction to have gone once its fuel is exhausted, because being extinguished was never a form of travel in the first place.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan notes that Śuddha, though classified alongside Pūrṇa as holding the same extremist view built on the fourteen unanswerable questions, does not repeat Pūrṇa's own challenge outright — having just watched it refuted, he instead asks the more tractable question standing behind it: not "which of the fourteen positions is correct?" but "what is it that beings fail to know that makes any of the fourteen positions seem urgent in the first place?" (T1764, lines 23424–23429). The Buddha's answer collapses the entire fourteenfold apparatus into a single diagnosis: not knowing the five aggregates for what they truly are is the single root generating every one of Pūrṇa's fourteen fixed positions, and knowing them is what dissolves the impulse to ask any of these questions at all — old ignorance-and-craving relinquished, no new grasping-and-becoming produced. Read together, this sixth and final debate of the fascicle functions as a coda to the entire sequence: where Jātiśroṇa needed the generative/revealing distinction, Vātsīputra needed the fourfold non-existence taxonomy, and Senika needed the six-consciousness analysis, Śuddha needs only the recognition that ignorance of the aggregates is itself what every other question in this fascicle has, in its own way, been circling.

Part 39 Complete

Six brahmin philosophers, sent by King Ajātaśatru to the sāla grove itself, each brought the sharpest objection their own system could produce — a caused nirvāṇa, a merely negative nirvāṇa, a self pervading everywhere, a self split into knowing and unknowing halves, body and life-force forced into strict identity or difference, and fourteen classic metaphysical questions demanding a fixed answer — and each objection dissolved the same way: not by choosing a side, but by showing that the very frame forcing a choice was itself the error. Every one of the six, defeated, asked on the spot to be ordained, and five attained arhatship within days of taking robes.
