Subhadra, the Last Disciple

The final fascicle closes four more debates from Part 39's sequence — Vātsīputra's own quiet passing, a naturalist ascetic's case that everything has its own fixed nature apart from any cause, and a hidden bodhisattva's mind read before he even speaks — before the narrative itself intervenes: Ānanda is discovered missing, held captive twelve leagues away by an army of demons, and must be freed by a dhāraṇī before the parinirvāṇa can proceed. The sūtra's very last teaching goes to Subhadra, a hundred-and-twenty-year-old ascetic and the Buddha's own final direct disciple, who receives, in his last hours, the teaching of the markless mark — and, with his own arhatship, the entire Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra reaches its close.

Vātsīputra: Good and Evil, and His Own Passing

犢子梵志復作是言：「瞿曇！我今欲問，能見聽不？」如來默然。第二、第三亦復如是。犢子復言：「瞿曇！我久與汝共為親友，汝之與我義無有二，我欲諮問，何故默然？」爾時世尊作是思惟：「如是梵志，其性儒雅，純善質直，常為知故而來諮啟，不為惱亂。彼若問者，當隨意答。」佛言：「犢子！善哉，善哉！隨所疑問，吾當答之。」 犢子言：「瞿曇！世有善耶？」 「如是。梵志！」 「有不善耶？」 「如是。梵志！」 「瞿曇！願為我說，令我得知善、不善法。」 佛言：「善男子！我能分別廣說其義，今當為汝簡略說之。善男子！欲名不善，解脫欲者，名之為善，瞋恚愚癡亦復如是。殺名不善，不殺名善，乃至邪見亦復如是。善男子！我今為汝已說三種善不善法，及說十種善不善法。若我弟子能作如是分別三種善不善法，乃至十種善不善法，當知是人，能盡貪欲、瞋恚、愚癡、一切諸漏，斷一切有。」 梵志言：「瞿曇！是佛法中頗有一比丘能盡如是貪欲瞋癡、一切諸漏、一切有不？」 佛言：「善男子！是佛法中非一二三乃至五百，乃有無量諸比丘等，能盡如是貪欲恚癡、一切諸漏、一切諸有。」 「瞿曇！置一比丘，是佛法中頗有一比丘尼能盡如是貪欲瞋癡、一切諸漏、一切有不？」 佛言：「善男子！是佛法中非一二三乃至五百，乃有無量諸比丘尼，能斷如是貪欲瞋癡、一切諸漏、一切諸有。」 犢子言：「瞿曇！置一比丘、一比丘尼，是佛法中頗有一優婆塞，持戒精勤，梵行清淨，度疑彼岸，斷於疑網不？」 佛言：「善男子！我佛法中非一二三乃至五百，乃有無量諸優婆塞，持戒精勤梵行清淨，斷五下結，得阿那含，度疑彼岸，斷於疑網。」 犢子言：「瞿曇！置一比丘、一比丘尼、一優婆塞，是佛法中頗有一優婆夷，持戒精勤梵行清淨度疑彼岸斷疑網不？」 佛言：「善男子！我佛法中非一二三乃至五百，乃有無量諸優婆夷，持戒精勤梵行清淨斷五下結，得阿那含，度疑彼岸，斷於疑網。」 犢子言：「瞿曇！置一比丘、一比丘尼盡一切漏，一優婆塞、一優婆夷持戒精勤梵行清淨斷於疑網。是佛法中頗有優婆塞受五欲樂，心無疑網不？」 佛言：「善男子！是佛法中，非一二三乃至五百，乃有無量諸優婆塞斷於三結，得須陀洹；薄貪恚癡，得斯陀含。如優婆塞，優婆夷亦如是。」 「世尊！我於今者樂說譬喻。」 佛言：「善哉！樂說便說。」 「世尊！譬如難陀、婆難陀龍王等降大雨，如來法雨亦復如是，平等雨於優婆塞、優婆夷。世尊！若諸外道欲來出家，不審如來幾月試之？」 佛言：「善男子！皆四月試，不必一種。」 「世尊！若不一種，唯願大慈聽我出家。」 爾時世尊告憍陳如：「聽是犢子出家受戒。」時憍陳如受佛勅已，立眾僧中為作羯磨，於出家後滿十五日，得須陀洹果。既得果已，復作是念：「若有智慧從學得者，我今已得，堪任見佛。」即往佛所，頭面作禮，修敬已畢，却住一面白佛言：「世尊！諸有智慧從學得者，我今已得。唯願為我重分別說，令我獲得無學智慧。」 佛言：「善男子！汝勤精進修習二法：一奢摩他、二毘婆舍那。善男子！若有比丘欲得須陀洹果，亦當勤修如是二法。若復欲得斯陀含果、阿那含果、阿羅漢果，亦當修習如是二法。善男子！若有比丘欲得四禪、四無量心、六神通、八背捨、八勝處、無諍智、頂智、畢竟智、四無礙智、金剛三昧盡智、無生智，亦當修習如是二法。善男子！若欲得十住地、無生法忍、無相法忍、不可思議法忍、聖行、梵行、天行、菩薩行、虛空三昧、智印三昧、空無相無作三昧、地三昧、不退三昧、首楞嚴三昧；金剛三昧，阿耨多羅三藐三菩提、佛行，亦當修習如是二法。」犢子聞已，禮拜而出，在娑羅林中修是二法，不久即得阿羅漢果。

Ru-Yi Translation

The brahmin ascetic Vātsīputra said, further: "Gautama, I now wish to ask something — may I do so?" The Tathāgata remained silent. He asked a second and a third time in exactly the same way. Vātsīputra said, further: "Gautama, you and I have long been the closest of friends, with no difference at all between us — I wish to ask you something, so why do you remain silent?" At that time, the World-Honored One reflected: "This ascetic's own nature is gentle and refined, purely wholesome and sincere — he has always come to inquire for the sake of true understanding, never to cause trouble. Should he ask something, I ought to answer as he wishes." The Buddha said: "Vātsīputra, excellent, excellent! Ask whatever you doubt, and I shall answer it." Vātsīputra said: "Gautama, does good exist in the world?" "Just so, ascetic." "Does evil exist?" "Just so, ascetic." "Gautama, please explain this for me, so that I may come to know what is good and what is evil." The Buddha said: "Good son, I am able to distinguish and explain this meaning at great length, but shall now explain it for you briefly. Good son, desire is called unwholesome; liberation from desire is called wholesome — and hatred and delusion are exactly the same. Killing is called unwholesome; not killing is called wholesome — and, all the way through, wrong view is exactly the same. Good son, I have now taught you three kinds of wholesome and unwholesome dharmas, and, further, ten kinds of wholesome and unwholesome dharmas. Should any of my own disciples be able to distinguish these three kinds of wholesome and unwholesome dharmas, and, all the way through, these ten kinds, one should know that this person is thereby able to exhaust craving, hatred, delusion, and every leak, severing every state of becoming entirely." The ascetic said: "Gautama, is there, within this very Buddhist teaching, even a single monk able to exhaust craving, hatred, and delusion, every leak, and every state of becoming in this way?" The Buddha said: "Good son, within this very Buddhist teaching, there are not merely one, two, three, or even five hundred, but countless monks able to exhaust craving, hatred, and delusion, every leak, and every state of becoming." "Gautama, setting aside a single monk — is there, within this very Buddhist teaching, even a single nun able to exhaust craving, hatred, and delusion, every leak, and every state of becoming?" The Buddha said: "Good son, within this very Buddhist teaching, there are not merely one, two, three, or even five hundred, but countless nuns able to sever craving, hatred, and delusion, every leak, and every state of becoming." Vātsīputra said: "Gautama, setting aside a single monk and a single nun — is there, within this very Buddhist teaching, even a single layman, upholding the precepts with diligence, of pure holy conduct, who has crossed to the far shore of doubt, severing the very net of doubt?" The Buddha said: "Good son, within my own Buddhist teaching, there are not merely one, two, three, or even five hundred, but countless laymen, upholding the precepts with diligence, of pure holy conduct, who have severed the five lower fetters, attained non-returning, crossed to the far shore of doubt, severing the very net of doubt." Vātsīputra said: "Gautama, setting aside a single monk, a single nun, and a single layman — is there, within this very Buddhist teaching, even a single laywoman, upholding the precepts with diligence, of pure holy conduct, who has crossed to the far shore of doubt, severing the net of doubt?" The Buddha said: "Good son, within my own Buddhist teaching, there are not merely one, two, three, or even five hundred, but countless laywomen, upholding the precepts with diligence, of pure holy conduct, who have severed the five lower fetters, attained non-returning, crossed to the far shore of doubt, severing the net of doubt." Vātsīputra said: "Gautama, setting aside a single monk and a single nun who have exhausted every leak entirely, and a single layman and a single laywoman, upholding the precepts with diligence, of pure holy conduct, who have severed the net of doubt — is there, within this very Buddhist teaching, even a single layman who still receives the pleasures of the five desires, and yet whose mind is already free of the net of doubt?" The Buddha said: "Good son, within this very Buddhist teaching, there are not merely one, two, three, or even five hundred, but countless laymen who have severed the three fetters, attaining stream-entry, and whose craving, hatred, and delusion have grown thin, attaining once-returning. And, exactly as with the laymen, the laywomen are exactly the same." "World-Honored One, I now wish to offer a simile, if I may." The Buddha said: "Excellent! Speak, if you wish to." "World-Honored One, it is exactly like when the nāga kings Nanda and Upananda send down a great rain — the Tathāgata's own rain of Dharma is exactly the same, falling equally upon laymen and laywomen alike. World-Honored One, should a non-Buddhist practitioner wish to come and leave the household life, I am not sure — for how many months does the Tathāgata test him?" The Buddha said: "Good son, all are tested for four months, though this need not always be the case." "World-Honored One, if it need not always be the case, I ask only, in your great compassion, that you permit me to leave the household life." The World-Honored One then said to Kauṇḍinya: "Permit this Vātsīputra to leave the household life and receive the precepts." Kauṇḍinya, having received the Buddha's own instruction, stood him before the assembled saṅgha and performed the formal act for him — and, after fifteen days had passed since leaving the household life, he attained the fruit of stream-entry. Having attained this fruit, he reflected, further: "Should there be any wisdom still attainable through further training, I have now attained it, and am fit to see the Buddha." He then went to where the Buddha was, bowed his head to the ground, and, his reverence complete, withdrew to one side, saying to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, whatever wisdom is attainable through further training, I have now attained it in full — I ask only that you explain and distinguish further for me, so that I may attain the wisdom of one beyond all further training." The Buddha said: "Good son, diligently cultivate these two dharmas: calming meditation, and insight meditation. Good son, should any monk wish to attain the fruit of stream-entry, he too ought diligently to cultivate these very two dharmas; and should he further wish to attain the fruit of once-returning, of non-returning, or of arhatship, he ought likewise to cultivate these very two dharmas. Good son, should any monk wish to attain the four dhyānas, the four immeasurable minds, the six supernormal powers, the eight liberations, the eight bases of mastery, the wisdom free of contention, the summit wisdom, the ultimate wisdom, the four unobstructed knowledges, the vajra samādhi, the exhaustion-wisdom, and the wisdom of non-arising, he ought likewise to cultivate these very two dharmas. Good son, should he wish to attain the ten abidings, the patience of non-arising, the patience of signlessness, the inconceivable patience, the noble practice, the pure holy conduct, the heavenly practice, the bodhisattva practice, the space samādhi, the wisdom-seal samādhi, the samādhi of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, the ground samādhi, the non-retrogressing samādhi, the Śūraṅgama samādhi, the vajra samādhi, supreme, perfect awakening, and the very conduct of a buddha, he ought likewise to cultivate these very two dharmas." Vātsīputra, hearing this, bowed and departed, and, cultivating these two dharmas within the sāla grove, before long attained the fruit of arhatship.

At that time, there were, further, countless monks about to go to where the Buddha was. Vātsīputra, seeing them, asked: "Venerable ones, where are you going?" The monks said: "We are going to where the Buddha is." Vātsīputra said, further: "Venerable ones, should you reach the Buddha, please convey this message on my behalf: the ascetic Vātsīputra, having cultivated these two dharmas, has attained the wisdom of one beyond further training, and now, in repayment of the Buddha's own kindness, shall enter parinirvāṇa." Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan classifies Vātsīputra as the doubt-heretic among the ten visitors spanning Parts 39–40, doubting both whether the path exists and whether anyone has actually attained it — which is exactly why his own escalating series of questions (is there even one monk, one nun, one layman, one laywoman, even one layman still enjoying the five desires, who has genuinely attained something?) reads less like hostility than like a methodical, almost desperate search for a single confirmed case (T1764, line 23028). The Buddha's repeated answer — not one, or five hundred, but countless — directly answers the doubt with sheer numerical weight rather than argument. Huiyuan reads Vātsīputra's own request for calming and insight meditation, immediately after his first fruit, as significant: unlike the previous six converts, who each received a single doctrinal key unlocking full arhatship at once, Vātsīputra needs the standard, gradual monastic training in full, suggesting the sūtra deliberately varies how quickly and by what means each of these ten converts actually completes the path, resisting a single formulaic pattern for what conversion from without looks like.

The Naturalist Ascetic: Does Everything Have Its Own Fixed Nature?

Chinese Source

◎納衣梵志復作是言：「瞿曇！如瞿曇所說無量世中作善、不善，未來還得善不善身。是義不然。何以故？如瞿曇說，因煩惱故，獲得是身。若因煩惱獲得身者，身為在先？煩惱在先？若煩惱在先，誰之所作？住在何處？若身在先，云何說言因煩惱得？是故若言煩惱在先，是亦不可；若身在先，是亦不可；若言一時，是亦不可。先、後、一時，義俱不可，是故我說一切諸法皆有自性，不從因緣。 「復次瞿曇！堅是地性、濕是水性、熱是火性、動是風性、無所罣礙是虛空性，是五大性非因緣有，若使世間有一法性非因緣有，一切法性亦應如是非因緣有。若有一法從於因緣，何因緣故，五大之性不從因緣？瞿曇！眾生善身及不善身獲得解脫，皆是自性，不從因緣。是故我說一切諸法自性故有，非因緣生。 「復次瞿曇！世間之法有定用處。譬如工匠，云如是木任作車輿，如是任作門戶、床机。亦如金師，所可造作，在額上者名之為鬘，在頸下者名之為瓔，在臂上者名之為釧，在指上者名之為環，用處定故名為定性。瞿曇！一切眾生亦復如是，有五道性故，有地獄、餓鬼、畜生、人、天，若如是者，云何說言從於因緣？ 「復次瞿曇！一切眾生其性各異，是故名為一切自性。瞿曇！如龜陸生，自能入水。犢子生已，能自飲乳。魚見鉤餌，自然吞食。毒蛇生已，自然食土。如是等事，誰有教者？如刺生已，自然頭尖，飛鳥毛羽自然色別。世間眾生亦復如是，有利有鈍、有富有貧、有好有醜、有得解脫、有不得解脫，是故當知一切法中各有自性。 「復次瞿曇！如瞿曇說，貪欲瞋癡從因緣生，如是三毒，因緣五塵，是義不然。何以故？眾生睡時遠離五塵，亦復生於貪欲瞋癡。在胎亦爾，初出胎時未能分別五塵、好醜，亦復生於貪欲瞋癡。諸仙賢聖，處在寂處，無有五塵，亦能生於貪欲瞋癡。亦復有人因於五塵，生於不貪、不瞋、不癡，是故不必從於因緣，生一切法，以自性故。 「復次瞿曇！我見世人五根不具，多饒財寶，得大自在。有根具足，貧窮下賤，不得自在，為人僕使。若有因緣，何故如是？是故諸法各有自性，不由因緣。瞿曇！世間小兒，亦復未能分別五塵，或笑、或啼，笑時知喜，啼時知愁，是故當知一切諸法各有自性。復次瞿曇！世法有二：一者有、二者無。有即虛空，無即兔角。如是二法，一是有故不從因緣，二是無故亦非因緣，是故諸法有自性故，不從因緣。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The kāṣāya-clad ascetic said, further: "Gautama, as you yourself teach, across countless ages beings perform wholesome and unwholesome deeds, and, in the future, receive a wholesome or unwholesome body accordingly. This reasoning does not hold. Why is this? As Gautama teaches, it is on account of affliction that this very body is obtained. If the body is obtained on account of affliction, which comes first — the body, or the affliction? If affliction comes first, who, exactly, produced it, and where, exactly, did it dwell beforehand? And if the body comes first, how, then, can it be said to be obtained on account of affliction? Therefore, should it be said that affliction comes first, this cannot be so; should it be said that the body comes first, this too cannot be so; and should it be said that they arise simultaneously, this too cannot be so. First, later, or simultaneous — none of these three possibilities holds — and it is for this very reason that I teach that every dharma possesses its own fixed nature, and does not arise from causes and conditions at all. "Further, Gautama, hardness is earth's own nature; moisture is water's own nature; heat is fire's own nature; movement is wind's own nature; and freedom from all obstruction is space's own nature — these natures of the five great elements do not exist through causes and conditions at all. Should there exist, in the world, even a single dharma whose nature does not arise from causes and conditions, then every dharma's own nature ought likewise not to arise from causes and conditions. And should there be some further dharma that does arise from causes and conditions, for what reason, then, would the nature of these five great elements alone fail to arise from causes and conditions? Gautama, beings' own wholesome and unwholesome bodies, and their attainment of liberation, are all simply their own fixed nature, and do not arise from causes and conditions at all. It is for this very reason that I teach that every dharma exists through its own fixed nature, and is not produced by causes and conditions. "Further, Gautama, dharmas in the world each have their own fixed function. It is exactly like a craftsman, who says that such-and-such wood is fit to be made into a cart, and such-and-such wood is fit to be made into a door or a bed; or exactly like a goldsmith, whose creations, worn upon the forehead, are called a garland, worn beneath the neck are called a necklace, worn upon the arm are called a bracelet, and worn upon the finger are called a ring — and it is precisely because their function is fixed that their own nature is said to be fixed. Gautama, every being is exactly the same: it is because there exist five fixed natures of destiny that there are hell, the realm of hungry ghosts, the animal realm, the human realm, and the heavens — and, this being so, how, then, can it be said that these arise from causes and conditions at all? "Further, Gautama, every being's own nature differs from every other's, and it is for this very reason that this is called each thing's own fixed nature. Gautama, exactly as a tortoise, though born on land, is naturally able to enter the water; as a calf, once born, is naturally able to drink milk; as a fish, seeing bait upon a hook, naturally swallows it; and as a venomous serpent, once born, naturally eats earth — in matters of exactly this kind, who, exactly, has taught them to do so? Exactly as a thorn, once grown, is naturally sharp at its own tip, and a bird's own feathers are naturally distinct in color — beings of the world are exactly the same, some sharp of faculty, some dull, some wealthy, some poor, some beautiful, some ugly, some attaining liberation, some never attaining it — and therefore one should know that every dharma possesses its own fixed nature. "Further, Gautama, as Gautama teaches, craving, hatred, and delusion arise from causes and conditions, these three poisons arising from the cause and condition of the five sense-objects — this reasoning does not hold. Why is this? Beings, even while asleep, departed entirely from the five sense-objects, still give rise to craving, hatred, and delusion. The same holds true within the womb: at the very moment of emerging from the womb, before one is even able to distinguish the five sense-objects, or beauty from ugliness, craving, hatred, and delusion still arise. Even sages and seers, dwelling in a place of utter stillness, apart from the five sense-objects entirely, are still able to give rise to craving, hatred, and delusion. And there are, further, those who, in the very presence of the five sense-objects, give rise to no craving, no hatred, and no delusion at all — and therefore it is not necessarily the case that every dharma arises from causes and conditions; it is, rather, on account of each thing's own fixed nature. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan classifies this ascetic as the sūtra's representative naturalist — holding that every dharma possesses its own inherent, fixed nature entirely apart from causal production — and organizes his nine arguments into a clear escalation: a dilemma about which of body and affliction comes first (with all three logical options, prior, posterior, and simultaneous, seemingly ruled out at once); an appeal to the four uncaused elements as proof that at least some natures are uncaused, generalized to all; an appeal to fixed function (a craftsman's wood, a goldsmith's gold) as proof of inherent, unchosen nature; an appeal to unlearned animal behavior (a tortoise instinctively swimming, a serpent instinctively eating earth); an appeal to afflictions arising without any sense-contact at all (in sleep, in the womb, in the sage's own solitude); an appeal to unequal fortune among people with equally complete or incomplete faculties; an appeal to an infant's unlearned laughter and tears; and, finally, a metaphysical appeal to space and the rabbit's horn as two limiting cases — existence and non-existence — that seem equally uncaused (T1764, lines 23025–23029 classify him among the "自然外道," naturalist heretics, alongside the wrong-view, self-view, and extremist-view types already met in Part 39). This is the most philosophically serious challenge in either fascicle, since it does not merely propose an alternative metaphysics but marshals genuine empirical evidence — infant behavior, animal instinct, and moral luck — that any causal account of the world needs to actually explain rather than dismiss.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Buddha's refutation as answering the naturalist's nine arguments roughly in the order they were raised, with two moves doing most of the real work (T1764, lines 23075–23145): first, showing that the four elements the naturalist treats as paradigm cases of fixed, uncaused nature are themselves observably unfixed and interconvertible (water freezing yet remaining "water" rather than becoming "earth," metals melting into flowing "fire" that behaves like water) — if even the supposedly bedrock elements shift under different conditions, they cannot serve as proof that anything has a truly fixed nature; and second, turning the naturalist's own reliance on simile back on him, since a genuine, inherent, self-identical nature could never be illuminated by comparison to anything else, and the naturalist constantly reaches for exactly such comparisons (a craftsman's wood, a goldsmith's gold) to make his own case. The body/affliction priority puzzle that opened the whole debate receives a genuinely careful answer rather than a dismissal: body and affliction arise simultaneously, like a wick and its flame, yet remain asymmetrically dependent even so, since the flame needs the wick in a way the wick does not need the flame — simultaneity does not entail mutual independence, dissolving the naturalist's original three-way dilemma (prior, posterior, or simultaneous) by showing that "simultaneous but asymmetrically dependent" was never on his own original list of options. The ascetic's own two-extremes-and-the-middle formula, arrived at instantly once given the framework, closes the sequence exactly the way Jātiśroṇa's four-noble-truths realization did in Part 39 — genuine philosophical opposition dissolving the moment its own hidden assumption is named.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads Hongguang's opening challenge — "do you know what I'm thinking?" — as a genuine test the Buddha passes in an unexpected way: rather than reciting Hongguang's actual private thought verbatim, the Buddha reframes it entirely, replacing the brahmin's own crude image (a private meal invitation as the "crooked path," alms-begging as the "straight") with the true doctrinal content that thought was only groping toward (T1764, line 23163 and surrounding). Reading the Buddha's own mind, in other words, is demonstrated not by mimicry but by correction — showing he has seen past the surface of Hongguang's thought to what it was actually reaching for. The Buddha's silence on whether the eightfold path saves everyone repeats the exact same refusal used against Pūrṇa's fourteen questions in Part 39, and Kauṇḍinya's own city-with-one-gate simile supplies the reason without requiring the Buddha to break that silence: the gatekeeper can guarantee that every entrant uses the one gate without being able to guarantee, or even needing to know, how many will ultimately choose to enter — the path's own necessity is certain even where the extent of its actual uptake remains genuinely open. The revelation that Hongguang is a bodhisattva already committed to buddhahood, deliberately playing the heretic to serve beings, recontextualizes every debate in this fascicle and the last: some of these ten "converts" may never have needed converting at all, and the entire confrontation may be understood, from the Buddha's own vantage, as staged pedagogy rather than a real contest of ideas.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Buddha's question — "where is Ānanda?" — as serving three purposes at once, despite the Buddha's own evident foreknowledge: to formally entrust the sūtra's own future transmission before witnesses, to prompt Mañjuśrī's actual mission to summon Subhadra afterward, and to create the occasion for teaching the dhāraṇī that subdues Māra, thereby demonstrating the true Dharma's power openly (T1764, lines 23594–23595). The lengthy flashback to how Ānanda first became the Buddha's attendant — Kauṇḍinya and the five hundred arhats all initially rejected as too old, Maudgalyāyana's samādhi-vision revealing the Buddha's true preference, and Ānanda's own three carefully calculated conditions — is read as establishing Ānanda's own foresight and integrity before his eight inconceivable qualities and eight sustaining dharmas are catalogued: refusing special food and robes preempts the accusation that his own service was ever mercenary, and refusing a fixed schedule preserves his capacity to serve the entire fourfold assembly rather than the Buddha alone. Huiyuan notes the demons' own strategy is aimed precisely at Ānanda's specific role as the future carrier of the teaching — since he alone is slated to transmit what the Buddha has taught after the parinirvāṇa, his capture and confusion (surrounded by countless false Buddha-images each teaching something different) directly threatens the Dharma's own future transmission, which is exactly why only a dhāraṇī taught by every buddha across ten Ganges-sands of world-systems, rather than an ordinary argument, is powerful enough to resolve it — a demonstration of the true teaching's own reach that argument alone could never accomplish.

What is the meaning of this?" The Buddha said: "Good son, should any śramaṇa or brahmin make a claim of this kind, I, out of compassion, would always go to visit such a person, and, having arrived, would ask him: 'Sir, do you truly make this very claim?' Should he answer: 'I do indeed say this' — I would ask: 'Why is this?' — and he might answer: 'Gautama, I observe beings who habitually practice every evil, and yet possess great wealth and enjoy freedom of every kind; and I observe others who cultivate goodness, and yet remain poor and lacking, without any freedom at all. I observe some who, out of loving-kindness, refrain from killing, and yet die prematurely; and others who delight in killing, and yet live out their full years. I observe some who purely cultivate holy conduct, diligently upholding the precepts, and yet some attain liberation while others do not. It is for this very reason that I say every being's own receiving of suffering and pleasure as retribution follows entirely from the very cause and condition of his own past karma.' Subhadra, I would then ask him, further: 'Sir, do you actually perceive this past karma directly? And, if such karma exists, how much of it is there? How much of it is your present austerity actually able to destroy? Are you able to know whether this karma is already exhausted, or not yet exhausted? And, once this karma is exhausted, is everything thereby exhausted?' Should he answer: 'I, in truth, do not know' — I would then offer him this simile: 'It is exactly like a person struck by a poisoned arrow, whose own family summons a physician to remove this arrow — and, once the arrow is removed, his body attains ease and comfort. Yet you, sir, not knowing your own past karma at all, how, then, can you possibly know that your present austerity is truly able to destroy this past karma?' Should he then say, further: 'Gautama, you yourself, too, possess past karma — why, then, do you alone hold me to account for my own past karma? I would then answer, further: 'Sir, knowledge of this kind is called inferential knowledge, not called true, direct knowledge. Within my own Buddhist teaching, there is knowing the fruit from the cause, and knowing the cause from the fruit. Within my own Buddhist teaching, there is past karma, and there is present karma. Your own system is not like this — it recognizes only past karma, and no present karma at all. Your own system does not sever karma through any skillful means; my own system is not like this, severing karma precisely through skillful means. In your own system, once karma is exhausted, suffering is thereby exhausted; in my own system, this is not so — once affliction is exhausted, karma and suffering are thereby exhausted together — and it is for this very reason that I now hold you to account regarding your own past karma.' Should this person then say: 'Gautama, I, in truth, do not know this myself — I have received it from my own teacher, who taught it this way, and I myself bear no fault in the matter' — I would ask: 'Sir, who, exactly, is your own teacher?' Should he answer: 'It is Pūraṇa Kāśyapa' — I would then say, further: 'Why, then, did you not, in the past, inquire of him one point at a time — does the great teacher truly know past karma directly, or not? Should your own teacher say he does not know it, how, then, could you still accept his own teaching at all? And should he say that he does know it, you ought, further, to have asked him: does a lower degree of suffering as cause and condition yield a middling or an upper degree of suffering as its result? Does a middling degree of suffering as cause yield a lower or an upper degree as its result? Does an upper degree of suffering as cause yield a middling or a lower degree as its result? And, should he answer that it does not, you ought, further, to have asked him: why, then, does the teacher claim that the fruit of suffering and pleasure comes solely from past karma, and not at all from the present? You ought, further, to have asked him: does this very present suffering also exist in the past? If it exists in the past, then this past karma is already, in every case, exhausted — and, if it is already entirely exhausted, how, then, could one still receive this very present body at all? And if it does not exist in the past, but exists only in the present, how, then, can it still be said that beings' own suffering and pleasure come entirely from past karma? Sir, should you know that present austerity is able to destroy past karma, by what, then, is this present austerity itself, in turn, destroyed? Should it not be destroyed at all, suffering would then be permanent — and, were suffering permanent, how could it be said that liberation from suffering is even possible? And should there be some further practice that destroys this very austerity, then, the past karma already exhausted, how could there still be any suffering left at all? Sir, is this very austerity able to cause karma that yields pleasure to instead yield the fruit of suffering? Is it able to cause karma that yields suffering to instead yield the fruit of pleasure? Is it able to cause karma yielding neither suffering nor pleasure to yield no fruit at all? Is it able to cause a retribution due in this very life to become one due in a future life instead? Is it able to cause a retribution due in a future life to become one due in this very life instead? Is it able to cause these two kinds of retribution to become no retribution at all? Is it able to cause a fixed retribution to become no retribution at all? And is it able to cause what carries no retribution at all to become a fixed retribution instead?' Should he then answer, further: 'Gautama, it is not able to do any of this' — I would then say, further: 'Sir, if it is unable to do any of this, for what reason, then, do you undergo this very austerity at all? Sir, you should know that there truly exists both past karma and present causes and conditions, and it is for this very reason that I say affliction gives rise to karma, and karma, in turn, yields retribution. Sir, you should know that every being possesses both past karma and present causes — for, though a being's own past karma determines his lifespan, he still necessarily depends on the present cause and condition of food and drink. Sir, should it be said that beings' own receiving of suffering and pleasure comes entirely and solely from the cause and condition of their own past karma, this is simply not so. Why is this? Sir, it is exactly like a person who removes an enemy on the king's own behalf, and, on account of this very deed, receives great wealth, and, through this very wealth, enjoys pleasure in the present — a person of this kind creates, in the present, the cause of pleasure, and receives, in the present, the fruit of pleasure. Or it is exactly like a person who kills the king's own beloved son, and, on account of this very deed, loses his own body and life — a person of this kind creates, in the present, the cause of suffering, and receives, in the present, the fruit of suffering. Sir, every being, in the present, receives suffering and pleasure on account of the four great elements, the season, the land, and other people — and it is for this very reason that I say it is not necessarily the case that every being's own suffering and pleasure come entirely from past karma. Sir, should liberation truly be attained solely through the power of severing karma, no noble one at all could ever attain liberation. Why is this? Because every being's own past karma has neither beginning nor end — and it is for this very reason that I teach that, in cultivating the noble path, this very path is able to block karma that has neither beginning nor end. Sir, should undergoing austerity alone be sufficient to attain the path, every animal, too, ought to attain the path. It is for this reason that one ought first to tame the mind, not merely the body — and it is on account of exactly this that I have taught, in my own sūtras: "Cut down this very forest — do not cut down a single tree." Why is this? Because fear arises from the forest, not from any single tree. Wishing to tame the body, one ought first to tame the mind — the mind is likened to the forest, and the body to a single tree within it.'"

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads this as the sūtra's own fullest, most systematic refutation of pure karma-determinism — the view, attributed to Pūraṇa Kāśyapa's own school, that all present suffering traces exclusively to past karma and can therefore be exhausted through austerity alone (T1764, lines 23638–23708). The refutation proceeds in two connected movements: first exposing that the doctrine's own proponents cannot actually verify the very claims their practice depends on (they cannot directly perceive their own past karma, cannot measure how much of it remains, and cannot confirm that austerity is actually depleting it — reducing their supposed knowledge to mere "inferential" guesswork dressed as certainty), and second, granting the doctrine's own best case (that a teacher genuinely does know past karma) only to show that even this concession collapses under a cascade of unanswerable follow-up questions about proportionality, timing, and what could possibly destroy the destroying-practice itself once the original karma is gone. The forest-and-tree simile closes the debate with the sūtra's own positive alternative: suffering's true root is the mind's own untamed condition (the forest, which produces the fear), not the body's own specific, isolated acts (the individual tree) — austerity attacks single trees one at a time while leaving the forest that keeps producing them intact, which is exactly why animals, capable of extraordinary physical endurance, never attain liberation through suffering alone.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the Buddha's rejection of Subhadra's own attainment as precise and merciless in its precision: neither-perception-nor-non-perception is still, definitionally, a perception, and any perception whatsoever remains subject to arising and ceasing, and is therefore still one of the "boil, wound, poison, arrow" images used throughout the sūtra for the subtlest remaining objects of clinging — the fact that Subhadra's own teacher Udraka Rāmaputra, sharper of faculty than Subhadra himself, likewise mistook this very state for liberation and died still bound by it, closes off any appeal to spiritual attainment as its own guarantee of correctness (T1764, lines 23710–23716). The teaching that follows — the markless mark, defined by an exhaustive negation running through roughly forty distinct categories (self and other, cause and fruit, agent and recipient, seeing and seer, karma and its lord) before naming the single place all these negations converge as the true mark — is read as the sūtra's own final, most complete statement of what every technical apparatus across all forty fascicles was ultimately pointing toward: not a further, subtler thing to attain, but the falling-away of the very grammar of marks that made "attainment" itself seem to require a subject, an object, and a distance between them. Huiyuan glosses the five synonymous names given to this single teaching — the true mark, the Dharma-realm, the ultimate wisdom, the truth of ultimate meaning, and the emptiness of ultimate meaning — as five names for one identical realization, each drawn from a different technical register, in the exact same "one meaning, many names" pattern the sūtra itself named and used deliberately as far back as Part 33's own taxonomy of naming.

Subhadra's own conversion completes the sūtra's own carefully built cast of the twelfth and thirteenth chapters: the same man who arrived clinging to the subtlest possible misidentification of liberation, tested against the exact same teacher-lineage failure that doomed Udraka Rāmaputra himself, becomes the Buddha's own final ordained disciple, attaining arhatship in the very hours before the parinirvāṇa the sūtra itself never narrates directly, closing on the note struck at the very start of this fascicle's sequence: the markless mark was never a new destination, only what remained once every mark obscuring it had, at last, fallen away.

Part 40 Complete — The Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra Concluded

With Subhadra's own arhatship, the last of ten philosophers converted across Parts 39 and 40, this bilingual translation of the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, Northern Edition (大般涅槃經, T0374, Dharmakṣema's 421 CE translation), reaches the end of its base text — forty fascicles, thirteen chapters, translated in full and without abridgment. From the opening announcement of the parinirvāṇa in Part 1 through the Lion's Roar chapter's demonstration that buddha-nature is inseparable from the middle way, the Kāśyapa chapter's patient resolution of twenty apparent contradictions and its exhaustive map of affliction, karma, and the ten perceptions that lead to nirvāṇa, and, finally, this closing chapter's sequence of philosophers each dismantled by the very tools of their own systems — the sūtra's own argument has never really changed: that permanence, bliss, self, and purity are not denied by the earlier teaching of impermanence, suffering, no-self, and impurity, but are what remains once affliction's own grip on the aggregates has genuinely fallen away, for every being without exception, icchantika included. The final teaching, given to the very last disciple the Buddha himself ordained, is fittingly the sūtra's most complete statement of what every other teaching pointed toward: the markless mark, true reality named five ways, one truth reached from three sides depending on which vehicle approaches it.
