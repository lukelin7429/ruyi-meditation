The Candle-Wax Seal

If the five aggregates arise and perish moment by moment, what exactly is bound by affliction, and what exactly is freed? Lion's Roar's question sends the Buddha through a candle's wax seal pressed into clay, a catalogue of eight kinds of scriptural simile, a stream-enterer whose precepts survive even rebirth in an evil land, and a debate over whether buddha-nature — shared by every being at once, yet never divided or used up — is one thing or many, like a single path walked by countless travelers. The fascicle closes with the Buddha's own account of why he chose humble Kuśinagara, rather than any of the six great cities, for his final teaching, and the story of the merchant Sudatta covering a garden floor with gold to build the Buddha a place to stay.

Bondage Without a Self to Bind

Chinese Source

◎師子吼言：「世尊！如佛所說，一切諸法有二種因：一者正因，二者緣因。以是二因，應無縛解。是五陰者，念念生滅，如其生滅，誰有縛解？世尊！因此五陰，生後五陰，此陰自滅，不至彼陰，雖不至彼，能生彼陰。如因子生芽，子不至芽，雖不至芽，而能生芽。眾生亦爾，云何縛解？」「善男子！諦聽，諦聽！我當為汝分別解說。善男子！如人捨命，受大苦時，宗親圍遶，號哭懊惱，其人惶怖，莫知依救。雖有五情，無所知覺，肢節戰動，不能自持，身體虛冷，煖氣欲盡，見先所修善惡報相。善男子！如日垂沒，山陵堆阜影現東移，理無西逝。眾生業果亦復如是，此陰滅時，彼陰續生，如燈生闇滅，燈滅闇生。善男子！如蠟印印泥，印與泥合，印滅文成，而是蠟印，不變在泥，文非泥出，不餘處來，以印因緣，而生是文。現在陰滅，中陰陰生，是現在陰，終不變為中陰五陰。中陰五陰，亦非自生，不從餘來。因現陰故，生中陰陰，如印印泥，印壞文成。名雖無差，而時節各異。是故我說中陰五陰，非肉眼見，天眼所見。是中陰中有三種食：一者思食，二者觸食，三者意食。中陰二種：一善業果，二惡業果。因善業故，得善覺觀；因惡業故，得惡覺觀。父母交會牉合之時，隨業因緣向受生處，於母生愛，於父生瞋。父精出時謂是己有，見已心悅，而生歡喜。以是三種煩惱因緣，中陰陰壞，生後五陰，如印印泥，印壞文成。生時諸根有具、不具，具者見色，則生於貪，生於貪故則名為愛。狂故生貪，是名無明。貪愛、無明二因緣故，所見境界皆悉顛倒，無常見常、無我見我、無樂見樂、無淨見淨。以四倒故，作善惡行，煩惱作業，業作煩惱，是名繫縛。以是義故，名五陰生。是人若得親近於佛及佛弟子、諸善知識，便得聞受十二部經，以聞法故觀善境界，觀善境界故得大智慧，大智慧者名正知見，得知見故於生死中而生悔心，生悔心故不生歡樂，不生歡樂故能破貪心，破貪心故修八聖道，修八聖道故得無生死，無生死故名得解脫。如火不遇薪，名之為滅，滅生死故，名為滅度。以是義故，名五陰滅。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar said: "World-Honored One, as the Buddha has taught, every dharma has two kinds of cause — the primary cause, and the conditional cause. Given these two causes, there ought to be no such thing as bondage or liberation at all. These five aggregates arise and perish moment by moment — given this constant arising and perishing, who, exactly, is bound, and who, exactly, is liberated? World-Honored One, it is in dependence on these very five aggregates that a later set of five aggregates arises — this aggregate ceases of itself, and never reaches that later one, and yet, though it never reaches it, it is able to produce it. It is like a seed producing a sprout: the seed never reaches the sprout, and yet, though it never reaches it, it is able to produce it. A being is exactly the same — how, then, could there be any bondage or liberation at all?" "Good son, listen closely, listen closely, and I shall distinguish and explain this fully for you. Good son, it is like a person casting off his own life, undergoing great suffering, surrounded by his own kin, who wail and grieve — this person, terrified, knows of nowhere to turn for refuge. Though his five senses remain, he has no awareness left at all; his limbs and joints tremble, unable to hold themselves steady; his body grows hollow and cold, its warmth nearly spent, and he sees before him the fruit of every wholesome and unwholesome deed he has ever performed. Good son, it is like the sun about to set: the shadow cast by a mountain ridge appears to move eastward, though, in truth, nothing at all has actually traveled westward. A being's own karmic fruit is exactly the same: as this aggregate ceases, that aggregate continues on and arises, exactly as a lamp being lit causes darkness to vanish, and a lamp going out causes darkness to arise once more. Good son, it is like a wax seal pressed into clay: the seal meets the clay, and, as the seal is lifted away, its impression comes to be formed — and yet this very wax seal itself does not remain, unchanged, within the clay, nor does the impression emerge out of the clay itself, nor does it come from anywhere else at all; it is only through the cause and condition of the seal that this very impression comes to arise. The present aggregates cease, and the intermediate-state aggregates arise — these present aggregates never themselves transform into the intermediate-state aggregates, and yet the intermediate-state aggregates, too, are not self-produced, nor do they come from anywhere else at all. It is only in dependence on the present aggregates that the intermediate-state aggregates arise, exactly as a seal is pressed into clay, and, as the seal is removed, its impression comes to be formed. Though the name remains without distinction, the moment of each is entirely different. It is for this very reason that I teach that the intermediate-state aggregates are not seen by the fleshly eye, but only by the heavenly eye. Within this intermediate state there are three kinds of nourishment: nourishment through intention, nourishment through contact, and nourishment through mental will. The intermediate state is of two kinds: the fruit of wholesome karma, and the fruit of unwholesome karma. Through wholesome karma, one attains wholesome thoughts and reflections; through unwholesome karma, one attains unwholesome thoughts and reflections. At the very moment a father and mother come together in union, this being, following the cause and condition of his own karma, moves toward the place of his own coming rebirth, giving rise to craving toward the mother and to resentment toward the father. At the moment the father's own seed is released, this being takes it to be his own, and, upon perceiving it, his mind delights, and joy arises within him. Through the cause and condition of these very three kinds of affliction, the intermediate-state aggregates disintegrate, and a later set of five aggregates arises, exactly as a seal is pressed into clay, and, as the seal is removed, its impression comes to be formed. At the moment of birth, the sense-faculties may be complete or incomplete — one whose faculties are complete, upon seeing a form, gives rise to craving, and, because craving has arisen, this is named attachment. Because of this delusion, craving arises, and this is named ignorance. Through the cause and condition of these two, craving-attachment and ignorance, every realm he perceives becomes entirely inverted: he sees the impermanent as permanent, the selfless as possessing a self, the joyless as joyful, and the impure as pure. On account of these four inversions, he performs wholesome and unwholesome deeds; affliction gives rise to karmic action, and karmic action gives rise to further affliction — this is what is named bondage. It is for this very reason that this is named the arising of the five aggregates. Should this person come to draw close to the Buddha, to the Buddha's own disciples, and to every kind of good friend, he thereby comes to hear and receive the twelve divisions of scripture; and, because he has heard the Dharma, he contemplates wholesome objects; and, because he contemplates wholesome objects, he attains great wisdom; and this great wisdom is named right view and right knowledge; and, because he has attained this right view and knowledge, he gives rise, within birth-and-death, to a mind of remorse; and, because he has given rise to this mind of remorse, he no longer gives rise to any delight in it; and, because he no longer gives rise to delight, he is able to destroy his own craving mind; and, because he has destroyed his craving mind, he cultivates the eightfold noble path; and, because he cultivates the eightfold noble path, he attains freedom from birth-and-death; and, because he attains freedom from birth-and-death, this is named the attainment of liberation. It is exactly as when a fire, meeting no further fuel, is named extinguished — and, because birth-and-death is thereby extinguished, this is named final extinction. It is for this very reason that this is named the cessation of the five aggregates."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lion's Roar's challenge here presses the two-causes framework from the previous fascicle to its logical breaking point: if nothing genuinely persists from one moment's aggregates to the next — a seed never literally becomes the sprout it produces — then there seems to be no continuous subject left over for either bondage or liberation to happen to. The candle-wax seal simile is this section's answer, and it is worth reading carefully against the two more familiar images (the sunset shadow, the lamp and darkness) it sits between: unlike those two, which illustrate mere succession, the seal-and-clay image adds a crucial further element — a genuine, non-arbitrary causal relation between what came before and what comes after (the impression's shape is not random; it is entirely determined by the seal, even though the seal itself is never "in" the clay). This gives the sūtra a middle position between two unwanted extremes: not a persisting self carried forward unchanged (which momentary arising-and-ceasing rules out), and not mere unrelated succession either (which would make karmic continuity arbitrary) — but a strict, shaped dependency of the next moment on the last, without any single thing traveling between them.

The extended account of the intermediate state (中陰) — its three kinds of nourishment, its origin in an inverted perception of the parents at the moment of conception, and its own dissolution into a next life's aggregates through the very same three afflictions (craving toward the mother, resentment toward the father, and the misappropriation of the father's seed as "one's own") — completes this picture by showing exactly how the shaping actually happens: it is not an impersonal mechanical process but is driven, at each transition, by a specific structure of desire and aversion, which is why the closing twelve-step chain (hearing the Dharma → contemplating what is wholesome → wisdom → right view → remorse → freedom from delight → freedom from craving → the eightfold path → freedom from birth-and-death → liberation) can intervene in this same chain and redirect it, exactly where the earlier chain of ignorance and craving once ran unchecked.

Is There Anything There to Be Bound?

Chinese Source

師子吼言：「空中無刺，云何言拔？陰無繫者，云何繫縛？」佛言：「善男子！以煩惱鎖，繫縛五陰，離五陰已無別煩惱，離煩惱已無別五陰。善男子！如柱持屋，離屋無柱，離柱無屋。眾生五陰亦復如是，有煩惱故名為繫縛，無煩惱故名為解脫。善男子！如拳、合掌、繫結等三，合散生滅更無別法。眾生五陰亦復如是，有煩惱故名為繫縛，無煩惱故名為解脫。善男子！如說名色、繫縛、眾生，名色若滅則無眾生，離名色已無別眾生，離眾生已無別名色，亦名名色繫縛眾生，亦名眾生繫縛名色。」師子吼言：「世尊！如眼不自見，指不自觸，刀不自割，受不自受，云何如來說言名色繫縛名色？何以故言名色者即是眾生？言眾生者即是名色？若言名色繫縛眾生，即是名色繫縛名色。」佛言：「善男子！如二手合時，更無異法而來合也。名之與色亦復如是，以是義故，我言名色繫縛眾生。若離名色則得解脫，是故我言眾生解脫。」師子吼言：「世尊！若有名色是繫縛者，諸阿羅漢未離名色，亦應繫縛。」「善男子！解脫二種：一者子斷，二者果斷。言子斷者，名斷煩惱，阿羅漢等已斷煩惱，眾結爛壞，是故子結不能繫縛。未斷果故，名果繫縛，諸阿羅漢不見佛性，以不見故，不得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。以是義故，可言果繫，不得說言名色繫縛。善男子！譬如然燈，油未盡時，明則不滅，若油盡者，滅則無疑。善男子！所言油者喻諸煩惱，燈喻眾生。一切眾生，煩惱油故，不入涅槃，若得斷者，則入涅槃。」師子吼言：「世尊！燈之與油，二性各異。眾生煩惱則不如是，眾生即是煩惱，煩惱即是眾生。眾生名五陰，五陰名眾生。五陰名煩惱，煩惱名五陰，云何如來喻之於燈？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Lion's Roar said: "There is no thorn in empty space at all — how, then, could one speak of 'pulling it out'? If the aggregates have nothing binding them, how, then, could there be any bondage at all?" The Buddha said: "Good son, it is through the chain of affliction that the five aggregates are bound — apart from the five aggregates, there is no separate affliction, and apart from affliction, there is no separate set of five aggregates. Good son, it is like a pillar supporting a house: apart from the house, there is no pillar, and apart from the pillar, there is no house. A being's own five aggregates are exactly the same: because affliction is present, this is named bondage; because affliction is absent, this is named liberation. Good son, it is like a fist, a joined palm, and a knot: their coming together and their coming apart, their arising and their ceasing, are never some further, separate thing beyond the coming together and coming apart itself. A being's own five aggregates are exactly the same: because affliction is present, this is named bondage; because affliction is absent, this is named liberation. Good son, it is as one speaks of name-and-form binding a being: should name-and-form cease, there is then no being at all — apart from name-and-form, there is no separate being, and apart from a being, there is no separate name-and-form. It may equally be said that name-and-form binds a being, or that a being binds name-and-form." Lion's Roar said: "World-Honored One, just as an eye cannot see itself, a finger cannot touch itself, a knife cannot cut itself, and feeling cannot feel itself — how, then, can the Tathāgata teach that name-and-form binds name-and-form? Why is it said that name-and-form is itself nothing other than a being, and that a being is itself nothing other than name-and-form? If it is said that name-and-form binds a being, this is itself already saying that name-and-form binds name-and-form." The Buddha said: "Good son, it is like the joining of two hands: no further, separate thing at all comes along to bring about this joining. Name and form are exactly the same — it is for this very reason that I say name-and-form binds a being. And, should one depart from name-and-form, one thereby attains liberation — it is for this very reason that I say a being attains liberation." Lion's Roar said: "World-Honored One, if it is name-and-form that constitutes bondage, then every arhat, not yet having departed from name-and-form, ought likewise still to be bound." "Good son, liberation is of two kinds: the severing of the seed, and the severing of the fruit. The severing of the seed is named the severing of affliction — the arhat has already severed affliction, and every fetter has rotted away entirely, and it is for this reason that the seed-fetter is no longer able to bind him. Yet, because he has not yet severed the fruit, this is named being bound by the fruit — every arhat fails to see buddha-nature, and it is only because he fails to see it that he fails to attain unsurpassed, complete awakening. It is for this very reason that one may speak of being bound by the fruit, though one cannot say that name-and-form itself still binds him. Good son, it is like a lit lamp: while its oil is not yet exhausted, its light does not go out — and, once its oil is exhausted, it is beyond doubt that it will go out. Good son, this very oil is a likeness for affliction, and the lamp is a likeness for a being. Every being, on account of the oil of affliction, fails to enter nirvāṇa — and, should he attain its severing, he thereupon enters nirvāṇa." Lion's Roar said: "World-Honored One, a lamp and its oil are two entirely distinct natures — a being and his own affliction are not like this at all. A being is himself nothing other than affliction, and affliction is itself nothing other than a being. A being is named the five aggregates, and the five aggregates are named a being; the five aggregates are named affliction, and affliction is named the five aggregates — how, then, can the Tathāgata illustrate this through the likeness of a lamp?"

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lion's Roar's opening image — no thorn in empty space, nothing to pull out — presses a mereological worry the previous section's causal account did not fully settle: even granting a genuine, shaped dependency between successive moments, if the five aggregates themselves just are what gets called a "being," there seems to be no further, separate thing left over to serve as either the binder or the bound. The Buddha's answer, through the pillar-and-house, fist-and-hand, and name-and-form pairings, embraces this mutual dependency rather than resisting it: bondage is not one thing (affliction) acting upon a separate other thing (the aggregates) any more than a house is one thing propped up by a separate pillar — the two terms are mutually defining, each meaningless without the other, exactly as a closed hand simply is what "fist" names, with no further fist-substance added to plain fingers.

The arhat objection sharpens this into the fascicle's own version of a puzzle raised already in Part 26 (the difference between "nirvāṇa" and "great nirvāṇa"): if bondage just is the aggregates-plus-affliction pairing, and the arhat still possesses aggregates, how is he not still bound? The seed/fruit distinction answers by splitting "liberation" itself into two grades — severing the seed (affliction itself, which the arhat genuinely accomplishes) and severing the fruit (the still-unseen buddha-nature this liberation does not yet reach) — which is also why Lion's Roar's final challenge to the lamp-and-oil simile lands so well: a lamp and its oil really are separable in a way a being and his own affliction are not, and the section closes without fully answering this last objection, setting up the very reframing — through a formal typology of how similes are meant to be read — that opens the next section.

Eight Kinds of Simile

Chinese Source

佛言：「善男子！喻有八種：一者順喻，二者逆喻，三者現喻，四者非喻，五者先喻，六者後喻，七者先後喻，八者遍喻。云何順喻？如經中說，天降大雨，溝瀆皆滿，溝瀆滿故小坑滿，小坑滿故大坑滿，大坑滿故小泉滿，小泉滿故大泉滿，大泉滿故小池滿，小池滿故大池滿，大池滿故小河滿，小河滿故大河滿，大河滿故大海滿。如來法雨亦復如是，眾生戒滿，戒滿足故不悔心滿，不悔心滿故歡喜滿，歡喜滿故遠離滿，遠離滿故安隱滿，安隱滿故三昧滿，三昧滿故正知見滿，正知見滿故厭離滿，厭離滿故呵責滿，呵責滿故解脫滿，解脫滿故涅槃滿，是名順喻。「云何逆喻？大海有本所謂大河，大河有本所謂小河，小河有本所謂大池，大池有本所謂小池，小池有本所謂大泉，大泉有本所謂小泉，小泉有本所謂大坑，大坑有本所謂小坑，小坑有本所謂溝瀆，溝瀆有本所謂大雨。涅槃有本所謂解脫，解脫有本所謂呵責，呵責有本所謂厭離，厭離有本所謂正知見，正知見有本所謂三昧，三昧有本所謂安隱，安隱有本所謂遠離，遠離有本所謂喜心，喜心有本所謂不悔，不悔有本所謂持戒，持戒有本所謂法雨，是名逆喻。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said: "Good son, simile is of eight kinds: the forward simile, the reverse simile, the direct simile, the non-simile, the prior simile, the posterior simile, the prior-and-posterior simile, and the pervasive simile. What is the forward simile? It is as taught within scripture: heaven sends down a great rain, and every ditch and channel becomes full; because the ditches and channels are full, the small pits become full; because the small pits are full, the great pits become full; because the great pits are full, the small springs become full; because the small springs are full, the great springs become full; because the great springs are full, the small pools become full; because the small pools are full, the great pools become full; because the great pools are full, the small rivers become full; because the small rivers are full, the great rivers become full; and because the great rivers are full, the great ocean becomes full. The Tathāgata's own rain of Dharma is exactly the same: as a being's own precept-keeping becomes full, because his precept-keeping is fully complete, a mind free of regret becomes full; because this mind free of regret is full, joy becomes full; because joy is full, departure becomes full; because departure is full, tranquility becomes full; because tranquility is full, samādhi becomes full; because samādhi is full, right view and right knowledge become full; because right view and right knowledge are full, disenchantment becomes full; because disenchantment is full, self-censure becomes full; because self-censure is full, liberation becomes full; and because liberation is full, nirvāṇa becomes full. This is named the forward simile. "What is the reverse simile? The great ocean has its own origin, namely the great rivers; the great rivers have their own origin, namely the small rivers; the small rivers have their own origin, namely the great pools; the great pools have their own origin, namely the small pools; the small pools have their own origin, namely the great springs; the great springs have their own origin, namely the small springs; the small springs have their own origin, namely the great pits; the great pits have their own origin, namely the small pits; the small pits have their own origin, namely the ditches and channels; and the ditches and channels have their own origin, namely the great rain. Nirvāṇa has its own origin, namely liberation; liberation has its own origin, namely self-censure; self-censure has its own origin, namely disenchantment; disenchantment has its own origin, namely right view and right knowledge; right view and right knowledge have their own origin, namely samādhi; samādhi has its own origin, namely tranquility; tranquility has its own origin, namely departure; departure has its own origin, namely a joyful mind; a joyful mind has its own origin, namely freedom from regret; and freedom from regret has its own origin, namely precept-keeping; and precept-keeping has its own origin, namely the rain of the Dharma. This is named the reverse simile."

Ru-Yi Translation

"What is the direct simile? It is as taught within scripture: a being's own mind and nature are just like a monkey — a monkey's own nature is to release one thing the moment it grasps another. A being's own mind and nature are exactly the same, grasping after and clinging to form, sound, fragrance, flavor, touch, and mental objects, never resting still for even a single moment. Reading note — Ru-Yi

This eightfold catalogue is a rare moment of explicit metalinguistic reflection within the sūtra itself — rather than simply deploying similes, the text pauses to classify how similes work, and the classification is itself doing real philosophical labor. The forward and reverse similes (water filling every vessel from ditch to ocean, then traced back from ocean to rain) model the same causal chain read in two directions — cultivation building toward nirvāṇa, and nirvāṇa's own conditions traced back to their root — showing that a single causal chain supports both a constructive and an analytical reading without contradiction. The "non-simile" is a pointed inclusion: King Prasenajit's exchange about the nyagrodha tree demonstrates that a simile can fail to transfer (a tree cannot keep precepts) precisely in order to mark the limit of a *different*, successful comparison (the four mountains as birth, old age, sickness, and death) — teaching the reader to ask, of any given simile, exactly how far its correspondence is meant to reach.

This last question is answered explicitly in the closing passage, which directly resolves the objection Lion's Roar raised against the lamp-and-oil simile at the end of the previous section: a being and his affliction are not, in fact, as separable as a lamp and its oil, but the sūtra's own theory of partial similes says this was never the point — "the Tathāgata's face is like the full moon" borrows only roundness and radiance, not the moon's own coldness or distance, and the milk-description-by-three-blind-men image (wetness, sweetness, whiteness — none of them milk itself) shows that even three similes together need not fully capture their target. Read this way, the lamp-and-oil simile was never claiming that a being and affliction are two separable substances; the corrected mapping given here — wick as the twenty-five states of existence, oil as craving, light as wisdom, warmth as the noble path — borrows only the specific relation of exhaustion-leading-to-extinction, which is exactly the relation the earlier bondage-and-liberation debate needed all along.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nine similes answering "how can what's always perishing still be a path" (the lamp's dispelling light, food's nourishment, a text's gradual mastery, a goldsmith's accumulated skill, a seed's spontaneous sprouting, counting's arrival at large numbers, one lamp-flame never instructing the next, a calf's untaught instinct) share a single structural point: none of them requires any single, persisting substance carried across the successive moments — a flame does not hand anything to the flame that replaces it, any more than the number "one" hands anything to "two" — and yet each still produces a real, cumulative, and irreversible result. This is the fascicle's positive answer to the puzzle the very first section of this part raised through the candle-wax seal: cultivation is possible without a self to do the cultivating, because cumulative causal shaping across a series never actually required a persisting substance in the first place, only a reliable pattern of dependency from one moment to the next.

The stream-enterer objection then tests this account against a hard case: if the aggregates cultivated here never literally reach the aggregates reborn there, in what sense does the stream-enterer's own precept-keeping travel with him into an evil land at all? The resolution — through the lion-king whose mere reputation clears a mountain he no longer occupies, the digested ambrosia whose effect outlasts the substance itself, and the grandson who inherits wealth he never personally earned — locates the answer not in any substance that persists, but in an effect, a "power," that outlives its own cause without needing to remain identical with it. This is the same move the goldsmith and reciting-student similes already made about ordinary skill; applied now to a stream-enterer's precept-keeping and, by clear extension, to a bodhisattva's own accumulated practice toward buddha-nature, it explains how genuine spiritual attainment can be real, effective, and inheritable without collapsing into the very fixed, persisting self this whole fascicle's opening question was designed to rule out.

師子吼言：「如佛說偈：『比丘若修習，戒定及智慧。當知是不退，親近大涅槃。』世尊，云何修戒？云何修定？云何修慧？」佛言：「善男子！若有人受持禁戒，但為自利人天受樂，不為度脫一切眾生，不為擁護無上正法，但為利養，畏三惡道，為命、色力、安、無礙辯，畏懼王法、惡名穢稱、為世事業，如是護戒則不得名修習戒也。善男子！云何名為真修習戒？受持戒時，若為度脫一切眾生為護正法，度不度故、解未解故、歸無歸故、未入涅槃令得入故，如是修時，不見戒、不見戒相、不見持者、不見果報、不觀毀犯。善男子！若能如是，是則名為修習戒也。「云何復名修習三昧？修三昧時為自度脫、為於利養，不為眾生、不為護法，為見貪欲、穢食等過，男女等根九孔不淨，鬪訟打刺、互相殺害，若為此事修三昧者，是則不名修習三昧。善男子！云何復名真修三昧？若為眾生修習三昧，於眾生中得平等心、為令眾生得不退法、為令眾生得聖心故、為令眾生得大乘故、為欲護持無上法故、為令眾生不退菩提故、為令眾生得首楞嚴故、為令眾生得金剛三昧故、為令眾生得陀羅尼故、為令眾生得四無礙故、為令眾生見佛性故，作是行時，不見三昧，不見三昧相，不見修者，不見果報。善男子！若能如是，是則名為修習三昧。「云何復名修於智慧？若有修者作是思惟：『我若修習如是智慧，則得解脫，度三惡道。誰能利益一切眾生？誰能度人於生死道？佛出世難如優曇鉢花，我今能斷諸煩惱結，必得解脫果，是故我當勤修智慧，速斷煩惱，早得度脫。』如是修者，不得名為修習智慧。云何名為真修習者？智者若觀生、老、死、苦，一切眾生無明所覆，不知修習無上正道。『願我此身悉代眾生受大苦惱，眾生所有貧窮、下賤、破戒之心，貪瞋癡業，願皆悉來集于我身。願諸眾生不生貪取，不為名色之所繫縛，願諸眾生早度生死，令我一身處之不厭，願令一切皆得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』如是修時，不見智慧，不見智慧相，不見修者，不見果報，是則名為修習智慧。善男子！修習如是戒、定、智慧，是名菩薩。不能如是修戒定慧，是名聲聞。」

Lion's Roar said: "As the Buddha has spoken in verse: 'Should a monk cultivate moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom, know that this is one who never retrogresses, drawing ever nearer to the great nirvāṇa.' World-Honored One, how, then, does one cultivate moral discipline? How does one cultivate concentration? And how does one cultivate wisdom?" The Buddha said: "Good son, should a person uphold the precepts merely for his own benefit, so as to receive happiness among gods and humans, and not for the sake of delivering every being, nor for the sake of upholding and protecting the unsurpassed true Dharma; should he uphold them merely for the sake of personal gain, out of fear of the three evil destinies, for the sake of his own life, his own beauty and strength, his own comfort, or his own unobstructed eloquence, or out of fear of the king's own law, of ill repute, or merely for the sake of worldly business — precept-keeping guarded in this way cannot be named the true cultivation of moral discipline. Good son, what, then, may be named the true cultivation of moral discipline? When one upholds the precepts for the sake of delivering every being, and for the sake of protecting the true Dharma; for the sake of those not yet delivered, that they be delivered; for the sake of those not yet liberated, that they be liberated; for the sake of those without refuge, that they gain refuge; and for the sake of those not yet entered into nirvāṇa, that they come to enter it — when one cultivates in this way, one sees no precept, no mark of the precept, no one who upholds it, and no fruit to be gained, nor does one dwell upon any transgression at all. Good son, should one be able to cultivate in exactly this way, this is then what is named the true cultivation of moral discipline. "What, then, may be named the true cultivation of samādhi? Should one cultivate samādhi merely for his own liberation, or for the sake of personal gain, and not for the sake of beings, nor for the sake of protecting the Dharma; should he cultivate it merely in order to perceive the faults of greed and desire, of impure food, of the nine impure orifices of the male and female body, or of contention, litigation, beating, stabbing, and mutual slaughter — should one cultivate samādhi for the sake of matters such as these, this cannot be named the true cultivation of samādhi. Good son, what, then, may be named the true cultivation of samādhi? Should one cultivate samādhi for the sake of beings — attaining an equal mind toward every being; causing beings to attain the dharma of non-retrogression; causing beings to attain the mind of a noble one; causing beings to attain the Great Vehicle; wishing to uphold and protect the unsurpassed Dharma; causing beings never to retrogress from bodhi; causing beings to attain the Śūraṅgama samādhi; causing beings to attain the vajra samādhi; causing beings to attain the dhāraṇīs; causing beings to attain the four unobstructed knowledges; and causing beings to see buddha-nature — when one practices in this way, one sees no samādhi, no mark of samādhi, no one who cultivates it, and no fruit to be gained. Good son, should one be able to cultivate in exactly this way, this is then what is named the true cultivation of samādhi. "What, then, may be named the true cultivation of wisdom? Should a practitioner reflect in this way: 'Should I cultivate wisdom of this kind, I shall attain liberation, and shall cross beyond the three evil destinies. Who else is able to benefit every being? Who else is able to deliver others from the path of birth-and-death? A buddha's appearance in the world is as rare as the udumbara flower — I shall now be able to sever every affliction and fetter, and shall certainly attain the fruit of liberation; it is for this very reason that I shall diligently cultivate wisdom, swiftly sever every affliction, and be delivered all the sooner' — a practitioner of this kind cannot be named one who truly cultivates wisdom. What, then, may be named one who truly cultivates it? Should the wise contemplate birth, old age, death, and suffering, and see that every being is covered over by ignorance, unable to know how to cultivate the unsurpassed true path, and thereupon vow: 'May this very body of mine take upon itself every great affliction and suffering in place of every being; may every being's own poverty, lowliness, precept-breaking mind, and every karmic action born of greed, hatred, and delusion, all come, without exception, to gather within this very body of mine instead; may every being give rise to no craving or grasping at all, and never be bound by name-and-form; may every being swiftly cross beyond birth-and-death, and may I myself dwell within it without ever tiring; and may every being, without exception, attain unsurpassed, complete awakening' — when one cultivates in this way, one sees no wisdom, no mark of wisdom, no one who cultivates it, and no fruit to be gained — this is then what is named the true cultivation of wisdom. Good son, one who cultivates moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom in exactly this way is named a bodhisattva. One who is unable to cultivate moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom in this way is named a śrāvaka."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The first pass through precept, concentration, and wisdom draws the bodhisattva/śrāvaka line entirely on intention and on a shared closing formula — "sees no [precept/samādhi/wisdom], no mark of it, no one who practices it, no fruit to be gained" — making the genuinely decisive factor not what is practiced but whether the practice is performed for every being's liberation or only one's own, and whether it is performed without the subtle self-congratulation of watching oneself practice. The bodhisattva's wisdom-vow in particular (offering to personally absorb every being's poverty, precept-breaking, and affliction) reads as this fascicle's own culmination of the substitution-logic threaded through Parts 23 and 26's "hard to do, hard to endure, hard to give" heroic virtues, now folded into the definition of wisdom itself rather than treated as a separate practice.

The second pass then grounds the same three headings in concrete, specifiable content rather than intention alone: the sixteen evil livelihoods name entire categories of trade built on raising creatures only to profit from their slaughter (the fatten-then-sell, buy-then-slaughter pattern repeated four times across lambs, pigs, calves, and chickens is itself a small, damning taxonomy of the livestock economy); the list of wrong samādhis catalogues meditative states genuinely capable of producing profound absorption yet still mistaken for nirvāṇa itself; and the list of wrong views on self and form catalogues nearly every logically possible relation a philosopher could propose between self, aggregates, and agency (self as form, form as vehicle for self, self as agent, form as agent, no agent at all, time as sole cause, and so on) — read together, this second pass shows that "true cultivation" is not a purely private, motivational achievement but also requires actually getting the ethical and philosophical content right, closing with the fifteen-step chain (precept → concentration → wisdom → freedom from doubt → the path → buddha-nature → awakening → nirvāṇa → severing of birth-and-death) that ties this section's entire discussion back to the fascicle's own opening question about what, exactly, cultivation accomplishes.

Why Isn't Saṃsāra Also Nirvāṇa?

師子吼言：「世尊！如佛所說，若不生不滅名大涅槃者，生亦如是不生不滅，何故不得名為涅槃。」「善男子！如是，如是。如汝所言。是生雖復不生不滅，而有始終。」「世尊！是生死法亦無始終，若無始終則名為常，常即涅槃，何故不名生死為涅槃耶？」「善男子！是生死法悉有因果，有因果故，不得名之為涅槃也。何以故？涅槃之體無因果故。」師子吼言：「世尊！夫涅槃者亦有因果。如佛所說：『從因故生天，從因墮惡道。從因故涅槃，是故皆有因。』如佛往昔告諸比丘：『我今當說沙門道果。言沙門者，謂能具修戒、定、智慧。道者謂八聖道，沙門果者所謂涅槃。』世尊！涅槃如是，豈非果耶？云何說言涅槃之體無因無果？」佛言：「善男子！我所宣說涅槃因者，所謂佛性，佛性之性不生涅槃，是故我言涅槃無因。能破煩惱故名大果，不從道生故名無果，是故涅槃無因無果。」

Lion's Roar said: "World-Honored One, is a being's own buddha-nature something possessed in common by all, or something possessed individually by each? Should it be possessed in common, then, at the very moment one person attains unsurpassed, complete awakening, every being, without exception, ought likewise to attain it at the very same time. Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the saṃsāra-nirvāṇa exchange as turning on a distinction easy to miss: both are, in a sense, "unborn and unceasing," yet birth-and-death still has a beginning and an end for any given being (it starts, and it stops, upon liberation), while nirvāṇa's own essence has neither — and the deeper reason birth-and-death cannot be named nirvāṇa is that it possesses cause and fruit (karmic causation running all the way through), while nirvāṇa's own essence has neither, its "cause" (buddha-nature) not itself producing it and its status as "fruit" being only the destruction of affliction, not a further conditioned production. This restates, in more technical language, the distinction Part 28 drew between the impermanent bodhi-mind and buddha-nature proper: what has cause and fruit running through it in the ordinary sense is thereby marked as belonging to saṃsāra's own causal order, however permanent-seeming it looks from outside.

On the shared/individual buddha-nature debate, Huiyuan reads the Buddha's answer as asserting a middle position the sūtra states but does not immediately defend — "neither one nor two" — and then reads the whole remainder of the passage as the working-out of exactly how this is possible: the herb of forbearance, unlike a countable, exhaustible object, stands for something that is not used up by being "eaten," which Lion's Roar rightly presses by asking whether the noble path itself, likewise said to be single, gets exhausted once one person walks it. The Buddha's road-and-shade, gate, bridge, and physician similes are, on Huiyuan's reading, each partial illustrations of non-exhaustive sharing, honestly conceded to be imperfect the moment Lion's Roar points out that an ordinary road does, in fact, produce real congestion — the decisive correction being the shift from generative cause to revealing cause: the path does not produce buddha-nature the way each traveler's passage would use up a shared resource, but merely reveals what was already, non-competitively, present in each being, exactly as the twelve links of dependent origination are said to apply "equally" to every being without one being's ignorance depleting anyone else's share of it. The poison-in-milk simile closing the debate then answers Lion's Roar's final objection (many kinds of bodies, one buddha-nature) the same way the milk-curd debate resolved presence-through-transformation in the previous fascicle: a single underlying property can persist unchanged through every change of name and form the five flavors undergo, exactly as buddha-nature persists, one and unvarying, through birth after birth in different bodies and destinies.

Huiyuan reads Lion's Roar's question here as carrying three distinct edges at once — why leave the six great cities at all, why choose to enter nirvāṇa here specifically, and why this particular wretched place — and reads the Buddha's threefold counter-simile (the lowly dwelling a king visits, the foul medicine that cures, the corpse that saves a drowning man) as together making a single point: worth is established by function and by what actually occurs there, not by a place's own outward appearance, so that Kuśinagara's humility is simply irrelevant to the question of its merit once "every buddha and bodhisattva has walked here" is granted as the actual standard. On his reading, the string of past-life narratives that follows is not merely decorative biography but the positive argument this negative point was clearing space for: the site's own merit is not asserted in the abstract but demonstrated through an accumulating chain of concrete precedent, stretching back through kalpa after kalpa, each story adding a further layer of reason this exact location was, in fact, already sacred long before this final visit.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story is structured as a chain of escalating conversions, each one preparing the next: Sudatta's own fright at merely hearing the word "Buddha" gives way, through the guiding deity's testimony, to a concrete measure of value (a single step taken toward the Tathāgata outweighing the most extravagant material gift imaginable, repeated across the whole of Jambudvīpa) that only makes sense once he has already met the Buddha and attained the stream-enterer's fruit for himself. The gate-of-light episode — radiant while he holds the Buddha's virtues in mind, extinguished the instant fear intrudes and he bows to a lesser shrine instead — dramatizes a point this whole fascicle's discussion of momentary, non-persisting mental states has been building toward: even a single interruption of the right kind of attention is enough to lose, for a moment, an accomplishment that must then be regained by the deity's own timely encouragement.

The garden purchase itself is this collection's most famous instance of a generosity contest resolved not by legal victory but by mutual escalation: Jeta's flippant, seemingly impossible price (gold covering the entire ground) is met literally rather than argued down, and it is precisely Sudatta's willingness to actually attempt this absurd bargain — pouring out cart after cart of gold rather than negotiating — that converts Jeta from a reluctant seller into a co-donor who gives the remaining, uncovered ground outright. The story closes, fittingly, on the Buddha's own instantaneous arrival "in the time it takes a strong man to bend and extend his own arm" — a small, understated miracle that lets the human drama of Sudatta and Jeta's competing generosity remain the story's actual center, with the Buddha's own supernatural power appearing only once every human effort has already been spent.
