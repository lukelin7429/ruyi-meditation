What Makes Us Recognizably Alike?

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 5, finishing Chapter 2 (根品第二之三, the third part of the Indriya-nirdeśa) and, with it, the treatise's entire catalog of formations dissociated from mind: class-commonality (同分), non-percipience and the two mindless attainments, the life faculty, the four characteristics of the conditioned — arising, abiding, decay, and cessation — and name-body, phrase-body, and letter-body.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 5 complete, 59 sections Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 5 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. A New Formation Dissociated from Mind: Class-Commonality

Having closed the long debate over possession, the treatise moves to the next member of its list of formations dissociated from mind — introducing a real, shared entity meant to explain why beings of the same kind are recognizably of that kind.

Chinese Source

如是已辯得非得相。同分者何？頌曰：同分有情等。論曰：有別實物名為同分，謂諸有情展轉類等，本論說此名眾同分。此復二種：一無差別、二有差別。無差別者，謂諸有情有情同分，一切有情各等有故。有差別者，謂諸有情界地趣生種姓、男女近事苾芻學無學等各別同分，一類有情各等有故。復有法同分，謂隨蘊處界。

Ru-Yi Translation

The characteristics of possession and non-possession have now been explained. What is class-commonality? A verse states: class-commonality is [what makes] sentient beings, and so on, [alike]. Prose: there is a genuinely separate real entity called "class-commonality," referring to the mutual resemblance in kind shared among sentient beings; the root treatise names this "the class-commonality of sentient beings." This is further of two kinds: without distinction, and with distinction. "Without distinction" refers to the class-commonality shared simply as sentient beings — since all sentient beings equally possess this, without exception. "With distinction" refers to the separate, distinct class-commonalities shared according to realm, level, destiny, mode of birth, lineage, sex, or standing as a lay follower, a monastic, a trainee, or a non-trainee, and so on — since each particular class of beings equally possesses its own such commonality. There is also a class-commonality of dharmas, referring to commonality according to aggregate, sense field, or element.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Possession and non-possession finally settled, the treatise turns to the next member of its catalog of "formations dissociated from mind" — dharmas that are neither physical form, nor mind, nor mental factors, yet are genuinely real and conditioned in their own right. "Class-commonality" (同分, nikāya-sabhāga) proposes a real entity to explain a very ordinary-seeming fact: that members of the same kind — all humans, say, or all beings in a particular realm — are recognizably of a kind at all, rather than each being simply its own isolated particular with no genuine commonality linking it to any other.

The scheme immediately branches in two directions. The most general class-commonality — simply "being a sentient being" — is shared without exception by every sentient being whatsoever. But class-commonality also nests down to arbitrarily fine-grained levels: realm, cosmological level, destiny (one of the six modes of rebirth), mode of birth, spiritual lineage, sex, and religious standing (lay follower, monastic, trainee, non-trainee) each generate their own distinct, narrower class-commonality, shared only among beings who fall into that same specific category. And the same basic idea, the text notes in passing, applies even to non-sentient dharmas grouped by aggregate, sense field, or element — a commonality of kind is not limited to living beings alone.

2. The Proof: Without a Real Commonality, Recognition Itself Would Be Impossible

Why posit a real entity here at all, rather than simply saying that similar beings are just... similar? The treatise argues that the very possibility of recognizing and naming a shared kind depends on it.

Chinese Source

若無實物無差別相名同分者，展轉差別諸有情中，有情有情等無差別，覺及施設不應得有。如是蘊等等無差別覺及施設，如理應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there were no real entity, without distinction in its characteristic, called "class-commonality," then, among sentient beings who mutually differ from one another in countless ways, the undifferentiated cognition and the undifferentiated designation of them as equally "sentient beings" ought not to be possible at all. The same reasoning should be understood to apply to the undifferentiated cognition and designation of aggregates, and so on, as equally alike.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The argument is compact but characteristic of this tradition's general approach to positing real entities: any two sentient beings differ from each other in an enormous number of ways — appearance, karma, mental state, realm, and so on — and yet everyone reliably and effortlessly recognizes and names both of them "a sentient being," with no hesitation about the category despite the endless particular differences. That reliable, shared recognition, the treatise argues, needs something to track — some genuine point of undifferentiated sameness running through all the differences — and "class-commonality" is proposed as exactly that real, underlying point of sameness. Without it, recognizing any two different things as being "the same kind" of thing would have no genuine basis to latch onto at all.

3. Four Cases: Does Dying and Being Reborn Always Mean Losing One's Class-Commonality?

A natural question about how class-commonality behaves across death and rebirth is answered with one of this treatise's favorite analytical tools: the fourfold case.

Chinese Source

頗有死生不捨不得有情同分？應作四句。第一句者，謂是處死還生是處。第二句者，謂入正性離生位時，捨異生同分，得聖者同分。第三句者，謂是趣死生餘趣等。第四句者，謂除前相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Is there any case of dying and being reborn in which one neither relinquishes nor acquires a sentient being's class-commonality? This should be treated as a fourfold case. The first case: dying in a certain place and being reborn again in that very same place. The second case: at the moment of entering the state of correct assurance apart from birth [that is, entering the path of seeing], one relinquishes the ordinary-being class-commonality and acquires the noble-one class-commonality. The third case: dying in one destiny and being reborn in another destiny, and so on. The fourth case: whatever is excluded from the preceding characteristics.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The question sounds almost paradoxical — surely dying and being reborn always involves some change of class-commonality? — and the fourfold case format resolves it by separating out genuinely distinct scenarios rather than forcing a single yes-or-no answer. The first case shows that ordinary rebirth in the identical circumstance (dying human, reborn human, in the very same place) need not change one's class-commonality at all. The second case shows that a change of class-commonality can happen without any death or rebirth whatsoever — becoming a noble one on the path of seeing swaps "ordinary-being" class-commonality for "noble-one" class-commonality in a single continuum, with no death involved. The third case is the ordinary expectation, dying in one destiny and being reborn into a different one, definitely changing class-commonality along realm or destiny lines. The fourth case, as usual in this fourfold format, simply catches whatever combination the first three did not already cover — such as an ordinary death and rebirth that happens to also coincide with entering the noble path.

4. Objections: Why Not Split It Further, and What Kind of Thing Even Is It?

A battery of objections presses on the theory from several directions at once — including a pointed question about why the treatise does not simply multiply class-commonalities without limit.

Chinese Source

若別有實物名異生同分，何用別立異生性耶？非異人同分，別有人性故。又非世間現見同分，以非色故。亦非覺慧所能了別，無別用故。世雖不了有情同分，而於有情謂無差別，故設有體亦何所用？又何因不許有無情同分？諸穀麥豆、金鐵菴羅半娜娑等，亦有自類互相似故。又諸同分展轉差別，如何於彼更無同分，而起無別覺施設耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is a genuinely separate real entity called "ordinary-being class-commonality," what further need is there to separately establish "ordinary-being-nature" as well? [Reply:] It is not the case that a distinct person's class-commonality [alone] is what constitutes a distinct human nature, since [class-commonality and personhood-nature] are separate matters. Furthermore, class-commonality is not something ordinarily perceived in the world, since it is not a form. Nor is it something wisdom and cognition are able to discern, since it has no distinct function of its own. Given that ordinary people do not perceive any "class-commonality" among sentient beings, yet still regard them as [genuinely, equally] undifferentiated, what use would positing such an entity even serve? Furthermore, why is it not accepted that inanimate things, too, have a class-commonality — grains such as rice and wheat and beans, or metals such as gold and iron, or fruits such as mango and jackfruit, since these likewise resemble their own respective kinds? Furthermore, class-commonalities themselves mutually differ from one another [in countless further ways] — so how is it that no further class-commonality is posited for them, and yet an undifferentiated cognition and designation of them still arises?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Four distinct objections arrive in quick succession. First, an internal consistency challenge: if "ordinary-being class-commonality" is already a real, separate entity, doesn't that make the earlier-established "ordinary-being-nature" (non-possession of the noble path, from fascicle 4) entirely redundant — why does the system need both? Second and third, the same evidentiary tests already used against "possession" are turned against class-commonality: it has no perceivable form, and performs no detectable function beyond simply being posited to explain recognition after the fact — precisely the circular move the treatise itself has criticized elsewhere. Fourth is perhaps the sharpest: if resemblance among grains, metals, and fruits does not require positing a real "class-commonality" entity to explain (since these plainly get by with mere resemblance, no separate entity invoked), why should resemblance among sentient beings require one? And in a final regress-style jab: if class-commonalities themselves differ from each other, and are nevertheless still recognized as "commonalities" without needing their own further meta-commonality, doesn't that show ordinary resemblance never actually required a separate real entity to explain recognition in the first place?

5. The Vaiśeṣika Comparison, and a Scriptural Defense — Class-Commonality Closes

The debate closes with an unexpected cross-school comparison, a careful clarification of what the defense is not claiming, and a final scriptural appeal.

Chinese Source

又應顯成勝論所執，彼宗執有總同句義，於一切法總同言智由此發生。彼復執有同異句義，於異品類同異言智由此發生。毘婆沙師作如是說：彼執與此義類不同，以說一物於多轉故。又縱於彼若顯不顯，然此同分必有實物，契經說故。如世尊言：若還來此得人同分，乃至廣說。雖有是說，而不說言別有實物名為同分。若爾，所說同分是何？即如是類諸行生時，於中假立人同分等，如諸穀麥豆等同分。此非善說，違我宗故。已辯同分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, this would end up vindicating exactly what the Vaiśeṣika school holds: that school maintains that there is a category called "universal sameness" (samānya), by which the cognition and language of total sameness across all dharmas arises; and it further maintains a category called "sameness-and-difference" (sāmānya-viśeṣa), by which the cognition and language of sameness and difference among distinct kinds of things arises. The Vaibhāṣika masters reply as follows: that school's position is not the same in kind as ours, since they hold that a single such entity operates across many separate things at once [whereas our "class-commonality," though shared in kind, is a distinct instance within each individual continuum]. Furthermore, whether or not this point is made explicit in their case, this class-commonality [of ours] must still be a real entity, because a sutra says so — as the World-Honored One states: "If one comes back here and attains a human being's class-commonality," and so on, as stated at length. Although this is said, the sutra does not go so far as to say there is a genuinely separate real entity called "class-commonality." If that is so, then what is this "class-commonality" that is spoken of? It is simply that, when formations of this general kind arise, "a human being's class-commonality," and so on, is provisionally established among them — just like the "class-commonality" spoken of among grains such as rice, wheat, and beans. This is not a well-reasoned position, since it contradicts our own school's tenet. Class-commonality has now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The comparison to the rival Vaiśeṣika school is a genuinely striking move, since Vaiśeṣika is a non-Buddhist system, and being told one's own theory resembles it is not a compliment in this context — Vaiśeṣika's "universal" categories (sāmānya and sāmānya-viśeṣa) were a standing target of Buddhist critique for positing single, literally shared entities existing identically "in" many different particulars at once. The Vaibhāṣika reply draws a careful line: "class-commonality," on this system's own account, is not one single entity somehow spread across every member of a class — each being has its own distinct instance of class-commonality, merely resembling the others' instances in kind, rather than literally being the same numerical entity shared by all. This distinction between "a shared kind" and "a single shared thing" is subtle but load-bearing for keeping the Buddhist position clear of the non-Buddhist one it otherwise superficially resembles.

The section then closes on a scriptural citation defended in a now-familiar pattern — a sutra using "class-commonality" language is invoked as proof, but a rival immediately points out that ordinary usage in that sutra passage does not obviously require any separate real entity at all, offering the same kind of reductive, merely-provisional account already applied to grains and beans. The treatise dismisses this alternative in the curtest terms yet seen in this whole extended debate: not because it is shown to be false, but simply because "it contradicts our own school's tenet" — an unusually blunt admission, closing out class-commonality on a note of asserted orthodoxy rather than settled argument, structurally echoing how the possession debate itself closed back in fascicle 4.

6. A New Formation: Non-Percipience

The treatise moves to the next formation dissociated from mind — a real dharma that brings about a startling cosmological fact: an entire heaven of beings whose mind and mental factors simply do not operate for an immense span of time.

Chinese Source

無想者何？頌曰：無想無想中，心心所法滅，異熟居廣果。論曰：若生無想有情天中，有法能令心心所滅，名為無想。是實有物，能遮未來心心所法令暫不起，如堰江河。此法一向是異熟果。誰之異熟？謂無想定。無想有情居在何處？居在廣果，謂廣果天中有高勝處，如中間靜慮，名無想天。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is non-percipience? Verse: non-percipience, among non-percipient beings, is the cessation of mind and mental factors; as a karmic-ripening result, it dwells in the Vast Fruit heaven. Prose: if one is born among the non-percipient sentient beings in the heavens, there is a dharma able to make mind and mental factors cease — this is called "non-percipience." It is a genuinely real entity, able to obstruct future mind and mental factors so that they temporarily do not arise, just as a dike blocks a river. This dharma is exclusively a karmic-ripening result. Whose ripening is it? It is the ripening of the attainment of non-percipience. Where do non-percipient beings dwell? They dwell in the Vast Fruit heaven (Bṛhatphala) — that is, within the Vast Fruit heaven there is an elevated, superior region, comparable to the intermediate dhyāna, named the Heaven of Non-Percipient Beings.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Non-percipience" (無想, āsaṃjñika) names a genuinely striking cosmological claim: an entire class of heavenly beings, born into a high sub-region of the Vast Fruit heaven, whose mind and mental factors are simply blocked from operating — not merely quiet or subtle, but actually absent — for an immense span of time, the karmic result of having cultivated a specific meditative attainment (introduced two sections from now) that aims at exactly this. The dike-and-river image is doing real conceptual work: non-percipience does not destroy mind, it dams it, holding back what would otherwise naturally flow.

The location detail matters too: this is not a separate heaven in its own right, but a special elevated precinct within the Vast Fruit heaven, comparable to how the "intermediate dhyāna" is a special elevated sub-level sitting between the first and second dhyānas rather than a fifth dhyāna of its own.

7. Are Non-Percipient Beings Truly Without Perception the Whole Time?

A natural objection is addressed: if perception is entirely absent, how do these beings ever leave that state at all? And a striking consequence follows for where they are reborn next.

Chinese Source

彼為恒無想、為亦有想耶？生死位中多時有想。言無想者，由彼有情中間長時想不起故。如契經說：彼諸有情由想起故從彼處沒，然彼有情如久睡覺還起於想，從彼沒已必生欲界非餘處所，先修定行勢力盡故、於彼不能更修定故，如箭射空力盡便墮。若諸有情應生彼處，必有欲界順後受業。如應生彼北俱盧洲，必定應有生天之業。已辯無想。

Ru-Yi Translation

Are these beings always without perception, or do they also, at times, have perception? At the moments of being born there and of dying from there, they do have perception, for an extended time. The reason they are called "without perception" is that, in the long interval between these two moments, perception does not arise in them at all. As a sutra states: those sentient beings, because perception arises again, pass away from that place; and yet those sentient beings, like someone waking from a long sleep, have perception arise once more, and, having passed away from that place, they must necessarily be reborn in the desire realm and nowhere else — because the power of the meditative practice they had previously cultivated is now exhausted, and they are no longer able, while there, to cultivate that same attainment again, just as an arrow shot into the sky falls back down once its force is spent. If a being is destined to be reborn in that place, that being must already possess karma bringing about a delayed result within the desire realm — just as a being destined to be reborn in Uttarakuru must necessarily already possess the karma for that particular birth. Non-percipience has now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection is a fair one — if perception never arises at all, how would anyone ever know these beings exist, let alone that they eventually leave that state? The answer draws a precise boundary: perception is genuinely present at the very moment of birth into that heaven and at the very moment of death from it, with only the long interior stretch of the lifespan actually perception-free. The waking-from-a-long-sleep image captures this well: dying out of the non-percipient state is not a gradual transition but an abrupt return, the mind simply resuming where it had been held in abeyance.

The consequence for the very next rebirth is exact: since the specific meditative power that produced this attainment is now entirely spent — like a spent arrow that can only fall — such a being cannot simply re-cultivate the same attainment on the way out, and so must be reborn specifically in the desire realm, nowhere else, matching whatever desire-realm karma was already waiting to ripen. The closing comparison to Uttarakuru — elsewhere in this treatise the standard example of a rebirth-destiny with entirely fixed, guaranteed karmic prerequisites, since beings there are said to live out a full, undisturbed lifespan with no premature death possible — reinforces the same point: a highly specific rebirth destiny of this kind is never a matter of chance, but always requires the matching karma to already be firmly in place in advance.

8. The Two Attainments: What Is the Attainment of Non-Percipience?

Non-percipience itself, as a karmic-ripening result, is now distinguished from the cultivated attainment that produces it — the first of two closely related "mindless" attainments the treatise will examine.

Chinese Source

二定者何？謂無想定及滅盡定。初無想定其相云何？頌曰：如是無想定，後靜慮求脫，善唯順生受，非聖得一世。論曰：如前所說有法能令心心所滅名為無想，如是復有別法能令心心所滅名無想定。無想者定名無想定，或定無想名無想定。說如是聲，唯顯此定滅心心所與無想同。此在何地？謂後靜慮，即在第四靜慮非餘。修無想定為何所求？謂求解脫。彼執無想是真解脫，為求證彼修無想定。前說無想是異熟故，無記性攝，不說自成。今無想定一向是善，此是善故，能招無想有情天中五蘊異熟。既是善性，為順何受？唯順生受，非順現後及不定受。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the two attainments? They are the attainment of non-percipience and the attainment of cessation. What is the characteristic of the first, the attainment of non-percipience? Verse: this attainment of non-percipience, cultivated within the last dhyāna, is sought as liberation; being wholesome, it conduces only to a result in one's very next birth; it is not attained by noble ones, and, once attained, is attained only for a single lifetime. Prose: as stated above, there is a dharma able to make mind and mental factors cease, called "non-percipience"; likewise, there is a further, separate dharma able to make mind and mental factors cease, called "the attainment of non-percipience." Read one way, this names the attainment that is non-percipience; read another way, it names the attainment that produces non-percipience — either reading only expresses that this attainment's cessation of mind and mental factors is the very same cessation as non-percipience itself. On which level does this occur? On the last dhyāna — that is, specifically the fourth dhyāna, and nowhere else. What does one seek in cultivating the attainment of non-percipience? One seeks liberation. Those who cultivate it hold that non-percipience is genuine liberation, and cultivate this attainment in order to realize it. It was said earlier that non-percipience itself, being a karmic-ripening result, is subsumed under the undefiled-neutral nature — this has already been established, and needs no further statement here. But the attainment of non-percipience itself is, without exception, wholesome; and, being wholesome, it is able to bring about the karmic-ripening result of the five aggregates among the non-percipient beings in that heaven. Given that it is wholesome in nature, which kind of karmic result does it conduce to? It conduces only to a result in one's very next birth — never to a result in the present life, nor a later life beyond the next, nor an indeterminate result.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction is exact and important: "non-percipience" itself (from the previous two sections) is the karmic-ripening result — always undefiled-neutral, simply the passive unfolding of past karma — while "the attainment of non-percipience" is the cultivated cause that produces it, and is, by contrast, wholesome through and through. The two share a name precisely because the attainment's defining feature (stopping mind and mental factors) is identical in kind to the resulting state it produces, even though one is a cause and the other its effect, and their moral qualities differ completely.

Crucially, this attainment is practiced by those who sincerely — if, from this treatise's standpoint, mistakenly — believe that the mere absence of mental activity constitutes genuine liberation. Because it is wholesome karma with a single, specific ripening target (rebirth as a non-percipient being in the very next life), its effect is unusually tightly bound: it cannot ripen in the present life, nor be deferred to some later life, nor remain open-ended, the way many other kinds of karma can.

9. Why Noble Ones Never Cultivate It, and Why It Must Be Attained Fresh Each Time

Two further features round out the picture: this attainment is fundamentally incompatible with the noble path, and, being a mindless state, it can never be "carried forward" the way an ordinary dhyāna can.

Chinese Source

若起此定後雖退失，傳說現身必還能起，當生無想有情天中，故得此定必不能入正性離生。又許此定唯異生得非諸聖者，以諸聖者於無想定如見深坑不樂入故。要執無想為真解脫，起出離想而修此定；一切聖者不執有漏為真解脫及真出離故，於此定必不修行。若諸聖者修得第四靜慮定時，為如靜慮亦得去來無想定不？餘亦不得。所以者何？彼雖曾習，以無心故，要大加行方便修得，故初得時唯得一世，謂得現在，如初受得別解脫戒。得此定已，第二念等乃至未捨亦成過去，以無心故，無未來修。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once this attainment has arisen, even if it is later lost, tradition holds that, within that very same life, it must arise again, and one will then be reborn among the non-percipient beings in that heaven. For this reason, once one has attained this attainment, one is definitely unable to enter the state of correct assurance apart from birth — that is, to enter the path of seeing. It is also accepted that only ordinary beings attain this attainment, never noble ones, since noble ones regard the attainment of non-percipience the way one regards gazing into a deep pit, taking no pleasure in entering it. Only someone who holds non-percipience to be genuine liberation, giving rise to the perception of renunciation on that basis, cultivates this attainment; since no noble one holds any contaminated state to be genuine liberation or genuine renunciation, noble ones definitely never practice this attainment. When noble ones cultivate and attain the fourth dhyāna, do they, the way they attain the ordinary dhyāna's past and future modes of possession, also attain the past and future modes of the attainment of non-percipience? They attain neither of those either. Why is this? Although they may have practiced this attainment previously, before becoming noble ones, because it is a mindless state, it must be cultivated and attained afresh each time, through great effortful practice; hence, at the very first moment of attaining it, one attains only that single occasion — that is, one attains only the present instance — just as when one first receives the prātimokṣa precepts. Once one has attained this attainment, its second moment, and so on, up until it is relinquished, likewise only ever become past instances; because it is a mindless state, there is no cultivating a future instance of it in advance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This attainment turns out to be a genuine dead end, spiritually speaking, and the text is unusually vivid about it: a noble one regards it "the way one regards gazing into a deep pit" — recognizing exactly what it offers (a total cessation of mental activity) and wanting no part of it, since mistaking that cessation for real liberation is precisely the error the noble path exists to correct. Once cultivated even once, it locks a being into eventual rebirth as a non-percipient being and permanently forecloses entering the path of seeing in that same continuum — a striking asymmetry with ordinary meditative attainments, which noble ones cultivate freely.

The final point is a technical consequence of the state being genuinely mindless: because possession, as fascicle 4 established at length, can in principle span the three times, but this attainment involves no mental continuity to sustain that span, it can never be "banked" the way an ordinary dhyāna can be — each attainment of it is a fresh, present-only event, comparable to how first receiving monastic vows is itself always a present, one-time event rather than something drawn on in advance from the future.

10. The Attainment of Cessation: A More Advanced, Distinct Attainment

The second of the two mindless attainments is introduced through careful contrast with the first — sharing the same basic mechanism, but differing in motivation, location, and the range of its karmic result.

Chinese Source

次滅盡定其相云何？頌曰：滅盡定亦然，為靜住有頂，善二受不定，聖由加行得，成佛得非前，三十四念故。論曰：如無想定，滅定亦然。此亦然聲，為例何義？例無想定心心所滅。如說復有別法能令心心所滅名無想定，如是復有別法能令心心所滅名滅盡定。如是二定差別相者，前無想定為求解脫，以出離想作意為先；此滅盡定為求靜住，以止息想作意為先。前無想定在後靜慮；此滅盡定唯在有頂，即是非想非非想處。此同前定，性唯是善非無記染，善等起故。前無想定唯順生受；此滅盡定通順生後及不定受，謂約異熟有順生受、或順後受、或不定受、或全不受。謂若於下得般涅槃，此定所招何地幾蘊？唯招有頂四蘊異熟。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, next, is the characteristic of the attainment of cessation? Verse: the attainment of cessation is likewise so; it is sought for the sake of dwelling in stillness, at the Peak of Existence; it is wholesome, and conduces to two kinds of result, or to an indeterminate one; it is attained by noble ones, through effortful practice; the Buddha attained it not by this means, on account of the thirty-four moments. Prose: as with the attainment of non-percipience, so too with the attainment of cessation. What does this word "likewise" illustrate? It illustrates that, like the attainment of non-percipience, this too is the cessation of mind and mental factors. Just as there is said to be a separate dharma able to make mind and mental factors cease, called "the attainment of non-percipience," so too there is a further, separate dharma able to make mind and mental factors cease, called "the attainment of cessation." As for the distinguishing characteristics of these two attainments: the former, the attainment of non-percipience, is sought for the sake of liberation, preceded by the attention of renunciation; this latter, the attainment of cessation, is sought for the sake of dwelling in stillness, preceded by the attention of stilling perception. The former occurs within the last dhyāna; this latter occurs only at the Peak of Existence, that is, the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception. This attainment is, like the former, wholesome in nature alone, never undefiled-neutral or defiled, since it arises together with wholesome states. The former conduces only to a result in the very next birth; this latter extends to a result in the very next birth, a later birth, or an indeterminate result — that is, with respect to its karmic ripening, it may conduce to a result in the very next birth, or a later birth, or an indeterminate result, or, indeed, to no result being received at all. That is: if one attains final nirvana at a lower level before the ripening occurs, what level, and how many aggregates, does this attainment bring about as its result? It brings about only the karmic-ripening result of the four aggregates at the Peak of Existence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two attainments share their basic mechanism — both stop mind and mental factors outright — but diverge in almost every other respect. Motivation: non-percipience is sought by someone who mistakes it for liberation itself; cessation is sought, more modestly, simply for the sake of a still dwelling, with no such metaphysical overreach. Location: non-percipience tops out at the fourth dhyāna, while cessation reaches all the way to the Peak of Existence, the very summit of conditioned existence. And karmic range: non-percipience is tightly bound to a single specific next-life result, while cessation's wholesome karma can ripen more flexibly — in the next life, a later life, an indeterminate time, or, for someone who reaches final nirvana first, not at all.

11. Who Attains Cessation, and Why It Cannot Be "Banked" Either

Cessation's attainer profile is the mirror image of non-percipience's — noble ones only, never ordinary beings — for reasons that reveal exactly what separates the two attainments philosophically.

Chinese Source

前無想定唯異生得；此滅盡定唯聖者得，非異生能起，怖畏斷滅故、唯聖道力所能起故、現法涅槃勝解入故。此亦如前，非離染得。由何而得？由加行得，要由加行方證得故。又初得時唯得現在，不得過去不修未來，要由心力方能修故。第二念等乃至未捨亦成過去。

Ru-Yi Translation

The former, the attainment of non-percipience, is attained only by ordinary beings; this latter, the attainment of cessation, is attained only by noble ones — ordinary beings are unable to bring it about, since they fear its apparent cessation and extinction, since it can only be brought about through the power of the noble path, and since one enters it through a resolute understanding of nirvāṇa realized within this very life. This attainment, too, like the former, is not attained through mere freedom from defilement alone. By what means, then, is it attained? It is attained through effortful practice, since it can only be realized by means of effortful practice. Moreover, at the moment of its first attainment, one attains only the present instance — one does not thereby attain any past instance, nor does one cultivate any future instance — since it can only be cultivated through the power of a functioning mind. Its second moment, and so on, up until it is relinquished, likewise only ever become past instances.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reversal of who attains each state is exact and telling: ordinary beings, mistaking cessation of mind for liberation, are drawn precisely to non-percipience; but the same apparent cessation terrifies an ordinary being when framed honestly as cessation, which is why only a noble one — someone who has already resolved, through direct understanding, that this temporary halt is not annihilation but a skillful stilling grounded in genuine insight into nirvāṇa — is able to enter it at all. Both attainments share the same technical limitation, though: being genuinely mindless states, neither can be attained in advance or sustained into the future the way an ordinary meditative state can; each occasion of entering either one is a fresh present event, gone the moment it becomes past.

12. Did the Buddha Need Effortful Practice? And a Puzzle About "Liberation in Both Respects"

If effortful practice is required to attain cessation, a natural question arises about the Buddha himself — followed by a genuinely sharp puzzle about what it even means to say he achieved the highest form of liberation.

Chinese Source

世尊亦以加行得耶？不爾。云何？成佛時得，謂佛世尊盡智時得，佛無一德由加行得，暫起欲樂現在前時，一切圓德隨樂而起，故佛眾德皆離染得。世尊曾未起滅盡定，得盡智時云何得成俱分解脫？於起滅定得自在故，如已起者成俱解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Does the World-Honored One likewise attain this through effortful practice? No. How, then? He attained it at the very moment of becoming a Buddha — that is, the World-Honored Buddha attained it at the moment of the knowledge of extinction. Not a single one of the Buddha's qualities is attained through effortful practice; the moment he simply wishes to bring any quality into present manifestation, every perfected quality arises in accordance with that wish. Therefore, all of the Buddha's qualities are attained simply through freedom from defilement, without effortful practice. If the World-Honored One had never actually brought forth the attainment of cessation before that moment, how, at the moment of attaining the knowledge of extinction, could he then be said to have achieved liberation in both respects? Because he has mastery over bringing forth the attainment of cessation, he counts as one who has achieved liberation in both respects, just as if he had already brought it forth.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The general rule just established — cessation requires effortful practice — turns out to have exactly one exception, and the reasoning behind it says something important about how this tradition understands Buddhahood itself: a Buddha's qualities are never achieved through the same graduated, effortful cultivation an ordinary noble one requires, because a Buddha's freedom from all defilement is already so complete that any quality manifests the instant it is willed, with no additional labor needed to bring it about.

The follow-up puzzle is genuinely sharp: "liberation in both respects" (ubhayatobhāga-vimukti) is the highest classical category of arhatship, reserved for those liberated both by wisdom and by mastery of the eight meditative liberations culminating in cessation — but if the Buddha, by hypothesis, had never actually brought forth cessation prior to enlightenment, on what basis could he possibly qualify? The answer relocates the criterion from actual prior performance to sheer capability: what matters is mastery over the attainment, not whether it has ever, as a matter of biographical fact, actually been exercised. This distinction between "has done it" and "could do it at will, with total mastery" turns out to matter a great deal for the debate that follows.

13. Did a Bodhisattva Attain Cessation Before Enlightenment? Two Schools Disagree

A genuinely contested question about the Buddha's own biography as a bodhisattva is argued out between two named schools of thought, turning on a precise, technical account of the very last moments before awakening.

Chinese Source

西方師說：菩薩學位先起此定後得菩提。云何此中不許彼說？若許彼說，便順尊者鄔波毱多《理目足論》。如彼論說：當言如來先起滅定、後生盡智。迦濕彌羅國毘婆沙師說：非前起滅定後方生盡智。所以者何？傳說菩薩三十四念得菩提故。諦現觀中有十六念，離有頂貪有十八念，謂斷有頂九品煩惱起九無間九解脫道，如是十八足前十六成三十四。一切菩薩決定先於無所有處已得離貪方入見道，不復須斷下地煩惱，於此中間無容得起不同類心，故諸菩薩學位不應起滅盡定。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Western masters say: a bodhisattva, while still a trainee, first brings forth this attainment of cessation, and only afterward attains awakening. Why, here, is their position not accepted? Because accepting it would align with the position of the Venerable Upagupta's treatise, the Lǐmùzú Lùn (理目足論). As that treatise states: it should be said that the Tathāgata first brought forth the attainment of cessation, and only afterward gave rise to the knowledge of extinction. The Kashmiri Vaibhāṣika masters say: it is not the case that he first brought forth the attainment of cessation and only afterward gave rise to the knowledge of extinction. Why is this? Because tradition holds that a bodhisattva attains awakening across exactly thirty-four mental moments. Within the direct realization of the truths there are sixteen moments; in becoming free of craving for the Peak of Existence there are a further eighteen moments — that is, cutting off the nine grades of affliction at the Peak of Existence requires nine uninterrupted paths and nine liberation paths; these eighteen, added to the previous sixteen, make thirty-four. Every bodhisattva, without exception, has already become free of craving with respect to the sphere of nothing-at-all before entering the path of seeing, and so has no further need to cut off the afflictions of any lower level; within this intervening span, there is no room whatsoever for any dissimilar state of mind to arise. Therefore, a bodhisattva, while still a trainee, should never bring forth the attainment of cessation within that span.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I have not been able to confidently identify the treatise cited here as "尊者鄔波毱多《理目足論》" — Upagupta is a well-known early patriarch in Sarvāstivāda lineage narratives, but I am not aware of a canonical Abhidharma treatise reliably attributed to him under this title, and the title itself ("理目足論," literally something like "reasoning-eye-foot treatise") does not match any text I can identify with confidence. I have left it transliterated rather than supply a confident English title I cannot actually verify, in keeping with this translation's policy of flagging genuine uncertainty rather than papering over it.

The substantive debate, in any case, is clear enough: the Western masters hold that a bodhisattva, in the final stretch of practice before enlightenment, brought forth the attainment of cessation and only then attained full awakening. The Kashmiri Vaibhāṣikas reject this on precise technical grounds, using the traditional count of exactly thirty-four mental moments required for a bodhisattva's final path to Buddhahood: sixteen moments of directly realizing the four truths (the standard path-of-seeing structure), plus a further eighteen moments — nine uninterrupted paths paired with nine liberation paths — needed to sever the nine grades of affliction still remaining at the Peak of Existence, the very last afflictions to go. Because a bodhisattva has, by this point, already become free of craving for every level below the Peak of Existence, this entire thirty-four-moment sequence runs as one uninterrupted stretch of a single, consistent type of mind — with no gap in which a "dissimilar" state of mind, such as the attainment of cessation, could possibly be inserted.

14. Closing the Debate: Never Transgressing One's Resolved Intention

A final objection is met, and the debate closes with the same candid acknowledgment already seen once before in this treatise — a position defended not because it has been proven, but because it belongs to the author's own school.

Chinese Source

外國諸師作如是說：若中間起不同類心，斯有何過？若爾，便有越期心過。然諸菩薩不越期心。理實菩薩不越期心，然非不越無漏聖道。若爾，期心如何不越？謂我未得諸漏永盡，終不解斯結加趺坐。決定不越如是期心，唯於一坐時諸事究竟故。前說為善，我所宗故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Foreign masters raise this response: if a dissimilar state of mind did arise within that interval, what fault would that actually involve? If that were so, there would then be the fault of transgressing one's resolved intention — yet bodhisattvas never transgress their resolved intention. But is it truly the case that a bodhisattva never transgresses his resolved intention, and yet does not thereby ever depart at all from the uncontaminated noble path? If that is so, then in exactly what sense is the resolved intention never transgressed? It is this: "Until I have attained the eternal extinction of all contaminants, I will never rise from this cross-legged seat." This resolved intention is never transgressed, in the sense that everything is brought to completion within that single, unbroken sitting. The position stated earlier is the better one, since it is our own school's tenet.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "foreign masters" press a fair challenge: even granting that a dissimilar mental state cannot arise mid-sequence, why is that itself a problem, rather than a neutral fact? The Kashmiri reply appeals to the bodhisattva's own famous resolve beneath the bodhi tree — the vow never to rise from that seat until liberation is complete — read here as a vow specifically against any interruption of type, which the attainment of cessation, being a wholly different kind of mental event, would necessarily constitute.

The debate then closes exactly the way the possession debate closed back in fascicle 4: not with a decisive proof, but with Vasubandhu simply naming the Kashmiri Vaibhāṣika account "the better one, since it is our own school's tenet" — the identical closing formula, word for word, as the earlier debate. This is a real pattern in how this treatise operates, not a one-off: genuinely contested technical questions are often worked through with real argumentative energy on both sides, only to be resolved, in the end, by institutional loyalty rather than a claim that the matter has been settled by reason alone.

15. What the Two Attainments Share: Both Occur in the Desire and Form Realms

Having covered the two attainments' major differences, the treatise circles back for a finer-grained comparison — starting with what they have in common, proven by an appeal to the root Abhidharma text itself.

Chinese Source

雖已說二定有多同異相，而於其中復有同異。頌曰：二定依欲色，滅定初人中。論曰：言二定者，謂無想定及滅盡定。此二俱依欲色二界而得現起。若有不許亦依色界起無想定，便違此文。謂本論言：或有是色有，此有非五行。謂色纏有情或生有想天，住不同類心，若入無想定、若入滅盡定。或生無想天已得入無想，是謂是色有，此有非五行。由此證知，如是二定俱依欲色而得現起，是名同相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Although much has already been said about the many points of resemblance and difference between the two attainments, further points remain to be addressed. Verse: the two attainments occur based on the desire realm and the form realm; the attainment of cessation first arises only among humans. Prose: by "the two attainments" is meant the attainment of non-percipience and the attainment of cessation. Both of these occur based on the two realms, desire and form. If someone does not accept that the attainment of non-percipience also occurs based on the form realm, this would contradict the following passage. For the root treatise states: there is a kind of existence that is form-existence, and this existence is not simply one of the five destinies. That is: a sentient being bound to the form realm may be born in a heaven where perception is present, abiding with a dissimilar type of mind, and may then enter either the attainment of non-percipience or the attainment of cessation. Or, having already been born in the Heaven of Non-Percipient Beings, such a being may then enter non-percipience directly — this too is called form-existence, and this existence is likewise not one of the five destinies. From this, it is established that both attainments occur based on the desire realm and the form realm alike — this is called their point of resemblance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag a genuine point of uncertainty here rather than paper over it: the phrase "此有非五行" ("this existence is not [simply] one of the five destinies") is citing a technical classification from the root Abhidharma text that I cannot pin down with full confidence. My best working reading is that it distinguishes "existence" classified by destiny (the five, or sometimes six, realms of rebirth) from "existence" classified by life-stage (the fourfold scheme of intermediate, birth, life-proper, and death existence) — with the citation specifying that "form-existence" here means an ongoing form-realm life, not a momentary birth-event, which is exactly what the surrounding argument needs: a form-realm being can, while alive there in an ordinary percipient state, deliberately enter either mindless attainment. I have translated it literally rather than smoothing over my uncertainty about the precise technical scheme being invoked.

The substantive point is more straightforward: since the root treatise explicitly describes a form-realm being entering either attainment while still alive and percipient in that realm, the attainment of non-percipience — like the attainment of cessation — cannot be confined to the desire realm alone. Both attainments, whatever else separates them, share this same basic footprint across the desire and form realms.

16. Where the Two Attainments Differ, and Proof That Cessation Can Be Lost

Their shared footprint established, the treatise turns to where the two genuinely part ways — including a scriptural proof, drawn from a sutra about advanced monks near death, that the attainment of cessation can indeed be lost and later regained.

Chinese Source

言異相者，謂無想定欲色二界皆得初起，滅定初起唯在人中。此在人中初修起已，由退為先方生色界，依色界身後復修起。此滅盡定亦有退耶？應言亦有。若不爾者，即便違害《鄔陀夷經》。經言：具壽！有諸苾芻先於此處具淨尸羅、具三摩地、具般羅若，能數入出滅受想定斯有是處。應如實知，彼於現法或臨終位，不能勤修令解滿足，從此身壞超段食天，隨受一處意成天身。於彼生已，復數入出滅受想定亦有是處。應如實知，此意成天身，佛說是色界，滅受想定唯在有頂。若得此定必無退者，如何得往色界受生？

Ru-Yi Translation

As for their point of difference: the attainment of non-percipience can first arise in either the desire realm or the form realm, while the attainment of cessation can first arise only among human beings. Having first cultivated and brought forth this attainment while human, one may, through first losing it, then be reborn in the form realm, and, based on a form-realm body, cultivate and bring it forth once again thereafter. Does this attainment of cessation, then, also admit of being lost? It should be said that it does. Were this not so, it would contradict the Sūtra of Udāyin. That sutra states: "Venerable ones! There are certain monks who, having first perfected pure moral discipline, perfected concentration, and perfected wisdom in this very place, are able to repeatedly enter and emerge from the attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception — such a case is indeed possible. It should be understood as it truly is: such a person, whether within this present life or at the moment of death, being unable to strive further to bring their liberation to full completion, upon the breaking up of this body, transcends the heavens of coarse food and is reborn, according to what is received, in a mind-made heavenly body in one of those states. Having been born there, that person may again repeatedly enter and emerge from the attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception — this, too, is indeed possible." It should be understood as it truly is that this mind-made heavenly body is what the Buddha calls the form realm — yet the attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception properly belongs only to the Peak of Existence. If, once this attainment were obtained, one could never lose it at all, how could such a person then go on to be reborn with a form-realm body at all?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The asymmetry is precise: non-percipience can be first cultivated in either the desire or the form realm, but cessation can only ever be first cultivated by a human being — form-realm access to it comes only secondhand, after losing and re-cultivating it there.

The scriptural proof is worth sitting with: it describes an advanced monk, fully accomplished in discipline, concentration, and wisdom, who can repeatedly enter and exit the attainment of cessation — but who dies before reaching full liberation. Rather than being lost, such a person is reborn in a special "mind-made" heavenly body (a body produced directly by mental power rather than by ordinary conception, transcending the realm of beings who still depend on coarse physical food) and, remarkably, continues entering and exiting the very same attainment there. Since this heavenly rebirth is identified as belonging to the form realm, while cessation's proper technical home is the Peak of Existence at the summit of the formless realm, the very fact that this person can keep practicing it after being reborn with a new, form-realm body is itself the proof the passage needs: the attainment must have been lost at death and cultivated afresh in the new body, since — as established two sections ago — this mindless state can never simply carry forward across a change of body on its own.

17. A Rival Claim Rejected: The Fourth Dhyāna Does Not Have Its Own Cessation-Attainment

A tempting shortcut — positing that cessation could arise directly within the fourth dhyāna, bypassing the whole loss-and-rebirth story just given — is rejected on scriptural grounds, with one careful qualification.

Chinese Source

有餘部執：第四靜慮亦有滅定。依彼所執，滅定無退，此義亦成。第四靜慮有滅盡定，義必不成。所以者何？九次第定，契經說故。此若必然，如何得有超越定義？此定次第依初學說，得自在時隨樂超入。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some other schools hold that the fourth dhyāna, too, has its own attainment of cessation. According to what they hold, the position that the attainment of cessation is never lost would indeed also be established. But the position that the fourth dhyāna has the attainment of cessation cannot possibly be established. Why is this? Because a sutra speaks of nine sequential attainments. If this sequence must invariably hold, then how could there be such a thing as a "superseding attainment"? This fixed sequence is stated with reference to a beginner in training; once one has attained mastery, one may leap over levels freely, as one wishes.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rival proposal would have neatly avoided the whole "lost and regained" account just given — if cessation could simply arise directly within the fourth dhyāna, a practitioner would never need to lose it, die, and be reborn with a form-realm body to keep practicing it. The treatise rejects this on the authority of a well-known scriptural list, the nine sequential attainments (the four dhyānas, the four formless attainments, and cessation as the ninth and final rung), which places cessation strictly after all eight of the others, including the Peak of Existence — never reachable directly from the fourth dhyāna alone.

The qualification is a nice piece of nuance: this fixed nine-step sequence describes how a beginner must proceed, level by level, in order. Someone who has already mastered the full sequence, however, can enter a "superseding attainment," leaping directly from a lower level to any higher one at will — but even this freedom to skip steps does not license skipping cessation's fixed position as the sequence's final rung.

18. A Sixfold Summary of Difference, and Why the Two Attainments Are Named Differently

The comparison is drawn together into a tidy sixfold list, followed by a sharp closing question: if both attainments equally stop mind and every mental factor, why does only one of their names mention feeling alongside perception?

Chinese Source

如是二定有多種異：謂地有異，第四靜慮有頂地故；加行有異，出離止息想，作意為先故；相續有異，異生聖者相續起故；異熟有異，無想有頂異熟果故；順受有異，順定不定生二受故；初起有異，二界人中最初起故。二定總以心心所滅為其自性，何緣但說名為無想、滅受想耶？二定加行中，唯厭逆此故，如亦知受等，唯名他心智。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, the two attainments differ in many respects: they differ in level, since one belongs to the fourth dhyāna and the other to the Peak of Existence; they differ in preparatory practice, since one is preceded by the attention of renunciation and the other by the attention of stilling perception; they differ in whose continuum they arise within, since one arises within the continuum of ordinary beings and the other within the continuum of noble ones; they differ in karmic ripening, since one brings about the ripening-result of non-percipience and the other the ripening-result of the Peak of Existence; they differ in what kind of result they conduce to, since one conduces only to a fixed result in the next birth, while the other gives rise to two further, less fixed kinds of result as well; and they differ in where they first arise, since one first arises in either of the two realms, while the other first arises only among humans. Both attainments, taken together, have the cessation of mind and all its mental factors as their essential nature — why, then, is one named specifically "non-percipience," and the other specifically "the cessation of feeling and perception"? Because, within the preparatory practice for each of the two attainments, it is specifically this — perception alone, or feeling and perception together — toward which the practitioner feels aversion and renunciation; the name simply follows what the practitioner's own intention specifically targets, just as, even though the wisdom that knows another's mind also thereby comes to know that person's feeling and the rest along with it, it is named solely "the wisdom that knows others' minds," after its single most salient object.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sixfold summary is mostly a tidy restatement of ground already covered — level, preparatory motivation, who attains it, karmic ripening, result-timing, and where it first arises — gathered here into one clean list. The closing question is the more interesting one: both attainments technically stop every mental factor without exception, so why does only "cessation of feeling and perception" name two factors while "non-percipience" names only one? The answer is that a name tracks the practitioner's own subjective target of renunciation, not the full technical scope of what actually happens — precisely the same kind of move already seen when a supernormal cognition gets named "knowing others' minds" even though it incidentally reveals their feelings too. What one is trying to escape, not everything that in fact ceases, decides the name.

19. If Mind Is Cut Off, How Does It Ever Resume? A Famous Puzzle, and the "Mutual Seed" Solution

A genuinely difficult causal puzzle now surfaces: ordinary continuity explains how one moment of mind gives rise to the next, but what explains mind resuming after being completely absent for a long stretch? The answer reaches for a solution that would echo powerfully in later Buddhist philosophy.

Chinese Source

今二定中心久時斷，如何於後心復得生？毘婆沙師許過去有前心為後等無間緣。有餘師言：如生無色，色久時斷，如何於後色復得生？彼生定應由心非色，如是出定心亦應然，由有根身非由心起。故彼先代諸軌範師，咸言二法互為種子。二法者，謂心、有根身。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, within these two attainments, mind is cut off for a long span of time — how, then, does mind arise again afterward? The Vaibhāṣika masters hold that the past mind which immediately preceded the attainment serves as the immediately-antecedent condition for the mind that arises afterward. Other masters say: consider the case of a being reborn in the formless realm, where form is cut off for a long span of time — how, then, does form arise again afterward, when such a being is eventually reborn back into a realm with form? In that case, you must surely hold that this rebirth-arising occurs through the power of mind, not through form continuing to serve as its own antecedent condition, since form was cut off the whole time. In just the same way, then, the mind that arises upon emerging from these two attainments should likewise be explained: it must arise through the power of the physical, sense-endowed body, not through mind continuing to serve as its own antecedent condition, since mind was cut off the whole time. For this reason, the earlier generations of master-teachers all alike held that these two things serve as mutual seeds for one another. The two things are: mind, and the physical, sense-endowed body.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely difficult causal puzzle, and the Vaibhāṣika's first answer is the simplest available: the immediately-antecedent-condition relationship (already established elsewhere in this treatise as one of the four classical types of causal condition) does not actually require strict, unbroken adjacency — a moment of mind from long before the attainment can still serve as condition for the moment that resumes after it, skipping over the gap entirely.

The more interesting move is the rival's counter-example: exactly the same puzzle already exists for a being reborn in the formless realm, where physical form is entirely absent for an entire lifetime, and yet that same being's form eventually resumes upon a later rebirth with a body. If the Vaibhāṣikas are forced to say mind alone explains form's return there — since form itself was absent the whole time and cannot serve as its own antecedent condition — then, by the identical logic, it must be the physical body that explains mind's return from these two attainments, not mind serving as its own condition across a gap where it too was entirely absent. The proposed solution — that mind and the physical, sense-endowed body serve as reciprocal "seeds" for one another, each capable of regenerating the other after either has been suspended — is a genuinely significant moment in the history of Buddhist thought. This same "mutual seed" reasoning, applied to the identical problem of explaining continuity across a total gap in mental activity, is widely recognized as an important precursor to the Yogācāra school's later, far more elaborate doctrine of the storehouse-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna) as the underlying seed-repository that never itself ceases, even when the six active consciousnesses do.

20. Does a Subtle Mind Remain in Cessation? Vasumitra and Ghoṣaka Disagree

A named debate between two revered masters tackles the puzzle from a different angle: rather than explaining how mind resumes after total absence, why not simply deny that mind is ever totally absent at all?

Chinese Source

尊者世友問論中說：若執滅定全無有心，可有此過。我說滅定猶有細心，故無此失。尊者妙音說此非理。所以者何？若此定中猶有識者，三和合故必應有觸，由觸為緣應有受想。如世尊說：意及法為緣生於意識，三和合觸俱起受想思。則此定中受想等法亦應不滅。若謂如經說受緣愛，然阿羅漢雖有諸受而不生愛，觸亦應爾，非一切觸皆受等緣。此例不然，有差別故。經自簡言：若無明觸所生諸受為緣生愛。曾無有處簡觸生受，故有差別。由此道理，毘婆沙師說滅定中諸心皆滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Venerable Vasumitra, in his Questions Treatise, states: "If one holds that the attainment of cessation involves no mind whatsoever, this fault would indeed follow. I hold instead that the attainment of cessation still retains a subtle mind — hence this fault does not arise." The Venerable Ghoṣaka says this position is not correct. Why is this? If, within this attainment, consciousness were still present, then, since the three factors would come together, contact would necessarily also be present; and, with contact serving as condition, feeling and perception would necessarily also be present. As the World-Honored One states: "Mind and dharmas serve as condition, giving rise to mental consciousness; the coming-together of the three is contact, and feeling, perception, and volition arise together with it." It would then follow that feeling, perception, and the rest of these dharmas would likewise not actually cease within this attainment. If one argues, as a sutra states, that "feeling serves as condition for craving," and yet an arhat, though still having feelings, does not thereby give rise to craving — so likewise contact here need not give rise to feeling — then, by the same token, contact too should not need to serve as condition for feeling in every case, since not every instance of contact serves as the condition for feeling and the rest. But this comparison does not actually hold, since there is a real difference between the two cases: the sutra itself explicitly qualifies its own statement, saying, "it is specifically feelings arisen from the contact of ignorance that serve as the condition for craving to arise" — whereas nowhere does it similarly qualify contact's giving rise to feeling. Hence there is indeed a real difference between the two cases. For this reason, the Vaibhāṣika masters hold that, within the attainment of cessation, all mind whatsoever genuinely ceases.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Vasumitra's proposal is elegant and would dissolve the entire puzzle of the previous section at a stroke: if a subtle mind never actually stops during the attainment of cessation, there is no gap in mental continuity to explain at all. Ghoṣaka's rebuttal is a careful piece of technical reasoning: consciousness, by this system's own standard causal analysis, can never occur in isolation — the moment any consciousness is present, the coming-together of faculty, object, and consciousness constitutes contact, and contact in turn necessarily brings feeling and perception along with it. So positing even the subtlest residual consciousness would mean feeling and perception are also still present — directly contradicting the very definition of this attainment as the cessation of exactly those two.

The one loophole Vasumitra's side might have reached for — that an arhat has feeling without craving, so perhaps contact could likewise occur without feeling — is closed by a careful textual point: scripture explicitly restricts its own "feeling conditions craving" formula to feelings born specifically from contact rooted in ignorance, marking that link as conditional, but nowhere similarly restricts contact's basic role in producing feeling at all. The asymmetry is not arbitrary; it is written into the sutra's own wording. With Vasumitra's alternative closed off this way, the Vaibhāṣika position — that mind is genuinely, completely absent throughout the attainment of cessation, full stop — stands as the treatise's settled answer to this whole debate.

21. A Final Echo: Are the Two Attainments Real Entities, or Merely Provisional?

The two attainments' extended treatment closes exactly the way the possession debate closed back in fascicle 4 — a genuinely elegant reductive alternative proposed, and then set aside for the same institutional reason as before.

Chinese Source

若都無心，如何名定？此令大種平等行故說名為定，或由心力平等至此故名為定。如是二定為是實有、為是假有？應言實有，能遮礙心令不生故。有說：此證理不應然，由前定心能遮礙故。謂前定心與所餘心相違而起，由此起故，唯令餘心暫時不轉。此能引發違心所依令相續故，唯不轉位假立為定，無別實體。此唯不轉分位假定，入前出後兩位皆無，故假說此是有為攝。或即所依由定心引令如是起，假立為定。應知無想亦復如是，謂由前心與所餘心相違而起，由此起故，唯令餘心暫時不轉，唯不轉位假立無想，餘說如前。此非善說，違我宗故。已辯二定。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is truly no mind whatsoever present, how can this state still be called an "attainment"? This state causes the great elements to proceed in a balanced, even way, and it is for this reason that it is called an "attainment"; or else, it is called an "attainment" because it is reached through the power of mind having brought the elements into such an even balance beforehand. Are these two attainments, then, genuinely real entities, or are they merely provisionally posited? It should be said that they are genuinely real entities, since they are able to obstruct mind, causing it not to arise. There is another view: this line of proof should not be accepted, since it is instead the immediately preceding moment of concentrated mind itself that does the obstructing. That is: the immediately preceding moment of concentrated mind arises in a manner opposed to every other kind of mind, and, because it arises this way, it alone causes all other kinds of mind to temporarily not operate. Because this is able to bring about, within the underlying support, a continuing condition opposed to ordinary mind, it is only with respect to this state of ordinary mind not operating that the term "attainment" is provisionally applied — there is no genuinely separate real entity involved at all. This "attainment," being merely the provisional label for the phase in which ordinary mind does not operate, is present neither at the moment of entering nor at the moment of emerging from it; hence it is only provisionally said to be subsumed under the conditioned. Or alternatively: it is simply the underlying support itself, brought by the power of the preceding concentrated mind to persist in this condition, that is provisionally called "the attainment." Non-percipience, too, should be understood in just the same way: it arises because the immediately preceding moment of mind arises in a manner opposed to every other kind of mind, and, because it arises this way, it alone causes other kinds of mind to temporarily not operate; it is only with respect to this state of non-operation that "non-percipience" is provisionally applied, and the rest should be explained just as before. This is not a well-reasoned position, since it contradicts our own school's tenet. The two attainments have now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The section closes with a definitional worry worth pausing on: if concentration (定, samādhi) ordinarily names a quality of mind, how can a state with no mind at all still count as a "concentration"? The answer relocates the label onto its physical effect instead — a balanced, even functioning of the bodily elements — rather than onto any ongoing mental quality, since there is none left to describe.

The closing question — real entity, or merely provisional? — is the exact same question the treatise spent the better part of fascicle 4 arguing about "possession," and the rival's answer here is built from the identical materials: no separate real "attainment" dharma is needed at all, just the causal power of the immediately preceding moment of concentrated mind, which arises specifically opposed to ordinary mental activity and thereby suppresses it for a time. "Attainment" and "non-percipience" alike, on this view, are only provisional labels for a phase of non-operation, grounded in nothing more than an altered underlying support — the same reductive move already seen applied to "seed" and to "ordinary-being-nature." And the treatise closes it exactly the same way it closed the possession debate and the bodhisattva debate: rejecting the more economical rival account not because it has been refuted, but "because it contradicts our own school's tenet." Three times now, across two fascicles, this exact formula has closed a genuinely live philosophical debate — a real, recurring feature of how this treatise operates, not a coincidence, and worth naming plainly rather than smoothing over.

22. Revisiting the Life Faculty: What, Exactly, Is "Life"?

The treatise returns to the life faculty — first introduced back in fascicle 3 as the one faculty that is purely karmic ripening — now analyzed in its own right as a formation dissociated from mind, beginning with a well-known verse on death.

Chinese Source

命根者何？頌曰：命根體即壽，能持煖及識。論曰：命體即壽，故對法言：云何命根？謂三界壽。此復未了何法名壽？謂有別法能持煖識說名為壽。故世尊言：壽煖及與識，三法捨身時，所捨身僵仆，如木無思覺。故有別法，能持煖識相續住因說名為壽。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the life faculty? Verse: the essence of the life faculty is simply life itself, the dharma able to sustain vital warmth and consciousness. Prose: the essence of "life faculty" is simply "life" itself. Hence an Abhidharma text says: "What is the life faculty? It is life within the three realms." But this still leaves unclear what dharma, exactly, is called "life." There is a separate dharma, able to sustain vital warmth and consciousness, that is called "life." Hence the World-Honored One states: "When life, warmth, and consciousness — these three dharmas — depart from the body, that abandoned body lies rigid and fallen, like a piece of wood, without thought or sensation." Therefore, there is indeed a separate dharma, serving as the cause that sustains the continuity of vital warmth and consciousness, that is called "life."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Fascicle 3 introduced the life faculty (命根, jīvitendriya) mainly through the famous case of an arhat consciously extending or relinquishing a remaining lifespan; this section returns to it as its own formation dissociated from mind, asking what "life" itself fundamentally is. The answer leans on a well-known verse about the moment of death: what actually departs the body, leaving it as inert as a fallen log, is a threefold departure — life, warmth, and consciousness together — which is offered as direct evidence that "life" names a real, distinct sustaining factor, not merely another name for warmth or consciousness themselves.

23. The Regress Puzzle: What Sustains Life Itself?

A sharp question immediately follows: if life sustains warmth and consciousness, what sustains life? A series of proposed answers are tried and rejected in turn, before the treatise reaches its considered position.

Chinese Source

若爾，此壽何法能持？即煖及識還持此壽。若爾，三法更互相持，相續轉故，何法先滅？由此滅故，餘二隨滅。是則此三應常無謝，既爾此壽應業能持，隨業所引相續轉故。若爾，何緣不許唯業能持煖識而須壽耶？理不應然，勿一切識從始至終恒異熟故。既爾，應言業能持煖，煖復持識，何須此壽？如是識在無色界中應無能持，彼無煖故。應言彼識業為能持，豈得隨情數為轉計？或說此識唯煖能持，或復說言唯業持識。又前已說。前說者何？謂前說言，勿一切識從始至終皆是異熟，是故定應許有別法，能持煖識說名為壽。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, then what, in turn, sustains this life itself? One might answer: it is precisely vital warmth and consciousness that, in turn, sustain this life. But if that is so, then these three dharmas sustain one another reciprocally, continuing in mutual succession — which one of them, then, ceases first? For whichever one ceases first, the remaining two would then also cease as a consequence — and so these three ought never to perish at all. Given this difficulty, one might instead say that it is karma that sustains this life, since life continues in succession precisely as karma has drawn it forth. If that is so, then why not simply hold that karma alone sustains vital warmth and consciousness directly, with no need to posit "life" as a further, separate thing at all? This will not do — for it would then follow that no consciousness whatsoever is ever a karmic-ripening result, from its very first moment to its last. Given this, one might then say instead: karma sustains warmth, and warmth in turn sustains consciousness — so why is "life" needed at all? But then consciousness within the formless realm would have nothing at all to sustain it, since that realm has no warmth. One would then have to say that, there, karma alone sustains consciousness directly — but can one really be permitted to keep shifting the account this freely, however convenient? Either one holds that consciousness is sustained by warmth alone, or that it is sustained by karma alone — and this very difficulty has already been raised above: it would mean no consciousness whatsoever is ever a karmic-ripening result from its first moment to its last. For this reason, one must definitely accept that there is a separate dharma, able to sustain vital warmth and consciousness, that is called "life."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The regress is genuinely elegant: if life, warmth, and consciousness merely sustain each other in a closed circle, nothing explains why any of them ever stops — an obviously false consequence, since death plainly happens. Falling back on karma alone as the sustaining force runs into a different, equally serious problem: fascicle 3 already established that the life faculty is the one faculty that is purely and always a karmic-ripening result, precisely because it is not itself an active, variable mental event the way ordinary consciousness is — if karma directly sustained consciousness with no "life" intermediary, consciousness itself would have to be karmic ripening at every single moment, collapsing a distinction the system needs to keep intact. And routing sustenance through warmth alone fails outright the moment a being is reborn in the formless realm, which has no warmth whatsoever, yet plainly still has consciousness. Each fallback is tried, found wanting, and abandoned — until positing "life" as a genuinely separate dharma is the only option left standing.

24. A Reductive Counter-Proposal: Life as Karmic "Duration Force," Not a Separate Substance

Even having granted that something more than karma, warmth, or consciousness alone is needed, a rival voice proposes that this "something" need not be a separate real entity at all — only a force, borrowed from familiar everyday images of things running down over time.

Chinese Source

今亦不言全無壽體，但說壽體非別實物。若爾，何法說名壽體？謂三界業所引同分住時勢分說為壽體。由三界業所引同分住時勢分相續決定隨應住時，爾所時住故，此勢分說為壽體。如穀種等所引乃至熟時勢分，又如放箭所引乃至住時勢分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Even so, this is not to say that "life" has absolutely no essence at all — it is only to say that this essence is not some further, wholly separate real entity. If that is so, then what, exactly, is meant by "the essence of life"? It is simply the force of duration, drawn forth by karma within the three realms, by which a given class-commonality persists for its allotted span — this is what is called "the essence of life." Because the force of duration drawn forth by three-realm karma for a given class-commonality is fixed in its continuity, persisting precisely for however long it is due to persist, this force is what is called "the essence of life" — comparable to the force, drawn forth within a grain seed and the like, that carries it through to the moment of ripening, or comparable to the force, drawn forth by the shooting of an arrow, that carries it through to the moment it finally comes to rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the same basic reductive move already seen applied to "seed" back in fascicle 4: rather than positing life as a metaphysically separate substance riding alongside warmth and consciousness, this position explains it entirely as a force — a fixed, karmically-determined duration already built into a given rebirth from the moment it begins, the way a planted seed already carries within it the trajectory that will bring it to ripening at a determinate later time, or the way a loosed arrow already carries within it the momentum that will carry it to a determinate resting point. Nothing extra needs to be added on top of karma's own causal power; "life" just names that power's temporal unfolding.

25. The Reductive Account Rejected: Why a Mere "Force" Cannot Explain Life's Determinate Ending

The seed-and-arrow account is turned back on itself, using the arrow image against the very position it was meant to support — an argument, not just an assertion, for why life must after all be a genuinely separate real entity.

Chinese Source

有謂有行是德差別，依箭等生，由彼力故。乃至未墮恒行不息，彼體一故、無障礙故。往趣餘方急緩至時分位差別應不得有，又應畢竟無墮落時。若謂由風所障礙故，應初即墮或無墮時，能障礙風無差別故。有別實物能持煖識名為壽體，是說為善。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are those who hold that there is "motion" as a distinct quality, arising dependent on the arrow and so on, by whose power the arrow continues moving without stopping, until it finally falls. But if this motion-quality's essence were single and uniform, unobstructed throughout, then there could be no difference in how quickly or slowly it arrives when traveling to different places at different angles, and, moreover, it should then never actually fall to the ground at all. If one says that it eventually falls because it is obstructed by wind, then it should either fall immediately at the very start, or else never fall at all — since the obstructing wind, on this account, is present without any variation throughout the whole flight. The position that there is instead a separate real entity, able to sustain warmth and consciousness, called the essence of life, is the better position.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The refutation borrows a rival (non-Buddhist) theory of projectile motion as its cautionary example: if an arrow's continued flight were explained by a single, uniform "motion quality" with no internal variation, nothing about that account could explain why the arrow travels at different speeds in different directions, slows down gradually, and eventually falls at a specific, determinate point rather than either instantly or never. A merely uniform force, in other words, cannot by itself generate a determinate, variable temporal profile — exactly the same problem, the treatise implies, that afflicts the immediately preceding proposal that "life" is nothing more than an undifferentiated karmic duration-force. Something with its own determinate nature, rather than a bare, featureless force, is needed to explain why a given being's life reliably runs its own particular course and then definitely ends when it does. On this reasoning — genuinely argued, rather than merely asserted — the treatise affirms that life is, after all, a separate real entity, sustaining warmth and consciousness in its own right.

26. Why Do Beings Actually Die? A Fourfold Analysis

With life established as a genuine, karmically-driven duration, the treatise asks what actually brings that duration to an end — distinguishing natural exhaustion from resource-failure, coincidence, and even a being's own deliberate choice.

Chinese Source

為壽盡故死、為更有餘因？《施設論》說：有壽盡故死，非福盡故死。廣作四句。第一句者，感壽異熟業力盡故。第二句者，感富樂果業力盡故。第三句者，能感二種業俱盡故。第四句者，不能避脫抂橫緣故。又亦應言捨壽行故。壽盡位中，福盡於死無復功能，故俱盡時有死，說為俱盡故死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Does one die because one's lifespan-karma is exhausted, or is there some further cause as well? The Prajñapti-śāstra states: there is death because lifespan-karma is exhausted, and there is death that is not because merit-karma is exhausted. This is treated at length as a fourfold case. The first case: the karmic power that draws forth the ripening-result of lifespan itself is exhausted. The second case: the karmic power that draws forth the ripening-result of wealth and happiness is exhausted, even while lifespan-karma is not. The third case: the karmic power capable of drawing forth both of these kinds of result is exhausted together. The fourth case: neither is exhausted, but one is simply unable to escape an unforeseen, violent circumstance. It should also be said, further, that there is death through deliberately relinquishing the life-formation. At the point where lifespan-karma alone is exhausted, the further exhaustion of merit-karma has no additional power over death at all; hence, when both happen to be exhausted together, this is described as "death because both are exhausted," not as two separate causes operating at once.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fourfold case distinguishes causes of death more finely than "the lifespan simply ran out." Case one is death from natural exhaustion of the karma allotting one's years — old age, in the ordinary sense. Case two is subtler: dying despite lifespan-karma still having force remaining, because the karma sustaining one's supporting prosperity and resources has run dry instead — starvation or destitution cutting a life short that was not, strictly, due to end yet. Case three is simply both exhausting together, treated as one cause rather than two coincidental ones. Case four is premature or accidental death proper: neither karmic allotment exhausted at all, yet death occurs anyway through sheer misfortune — a category that matters a great deal for the next section's classification of which beings can and cannot die this way. And the passing mention of "deliberately relinquishing the life-formation" quietly recalls fascicle 3's account of an arhat's power to consciously end a remaining lifespan at will, folded in here as a further mode of death alongside the karmic ones.

27. Does Life Flow Continuously, or Arise Once and Stay Fixed?

A question from the root Abhidharma text draws a sharp line between beings whose life can be cut short and beings whose life cannot — turning on whether their underlying bodily support is vulnerable to damage at all.

Chinese Source

《發智論》說：此壽當言隨相續轉、為復當言一起便住？欲纏有情不入無想定、不入滅盡定，當言此壽隨相續轉。若入無想定、若入滅盡定及色無色纏一切有情，當言此壽一起便住。彼言何義？若所依身可損害故壽隨損害，是名第一隨相續轉。若所依身不可損害如起而住，是名第二一起便住。迦濕彌羅國毘婆沙師言：初顯有障，後顯無障，由此決定有非時死。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Jñānaprasthāna asks: should this life be said to flow along in continuous succession, or should it be said to arise once and then simply remain fixed? For sentient beings bound to the desire realm who do not enter the attainment of non-percipience and do not enter the attainment of cessation, it should be said that their life flows along in continuous succession. For beings who enter the attainment of non-percipience or the attainment of cessation, and for all sentient beings bound to the form or formless realms, it should be said that their life arises once and then simply remains fixed. What is the meaning of this? If the underlying support of the body is such that it can be damaged, and life is correspondingly damaged along with it, this is the first case — life flowing along in continuous succession. If the underlying support of the body cannot be damaged, so that life simply remains as it originally arose, this is the second case — life arising once and remaining fixed. The Kashmiri Vaibhāṣika masters say: the first case reveals a life subject to obstruction, and the second reveals a life not subject to obstruction — and it is precisely because of this distinction that untimely death is definitely possible for beings of the first kind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The line drawn here matters directly for the previous section's fourth case, accidental or premature death: only a being whose underlying bodily support is genuinely vulnerable to damage can have their life cut short unpredictably at all. Ordinary desire-realm beings fit this description, their life flowing moment to moment in a continuum that external harm can interrupt. But beings absorbed in either mindless attainment, and every being of the form and formless realms, have a life that simply arises fixed for its full allotted span from the start — nothing external can touch it, so untimely death is, for them, not merely rare but strictly impossible.

28. Who Can Only Be Killed by Themselves, by Others, by Both, or by Neither?

The distinction just drawn opens directly onto a classical scriptural fourfold case: a remarkable catalog of exactly which beings are immune to being killed by another, immune to self-harm, vulnerable to both, or vulnerable to neither.

Chinese Source

故契經說：有四得自體，謂有得自體唯可自害非可他害。廣作四句。唯可自害非他害者，謂生欲界戲忘念天意憤恚天，彼由發起增上喜怒，是故於彼殞沒非餘。又應說諸佛自般涅槃故。唯可他害非自害者，謂處胎卵諸有情類。俱可害者，謂餘多分欲界有情。俱非害者，謂在中有、色無色界一切有情及在欲界一分有情，如那落迦、北俱盧洲、正住見道、慈定滅定及無想定，王仙佛使、佛所記別達弭羅嗢怛羅、殑耆羅長者子耶舍鳩磨羅時婆、最後身菩薩，及此菩薩母懷菩薩胎時，一切轉輪王及此輪王母懷輪王胎時。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hence a sutra states: there are four kinds of obtained self-bodies — namely, there are self-bodies that can only be harmed by oneself, and cannot be harmed by another. This is treated at length as a fourfold case. Those that can only be harmed by oneself, and not by another: this refers to those born in the desire realm as the heavenly beings of the Heaven of Play and Forgetfulness, or of Malicious Intent — these beings, through giving rise to excessive joy or excessive rage, pass away specifically by that means, and by no other. It should also be said that this applies to all Buddhas, who enter final nirvana entirely by their own will. Those that can only be harmed by another, and not by oneself: this refers to sentient beings still in the womb or the egg. Those that can be harmed by either: this refers to the greater portion of the remaining sentient beings of the desire realm. Those that cannot be harmed by either: this refers to beings in the intermediate state, all sentient beings of the form and formless realms, and a portion of desire-realm beings — namely, those in the hells, those in Uttarakuru, those genuinely dwelling on the path of seeing, those absorbed in loving-kindness concentration, in the attainment of cessation, or in the attainment of non-percipience, sage-kings and seers, a Buddha's own emissary, and those individuals a Buddha has specifically prophesied by name, a bodhisattva in his final existence, and this bodhisattva's mother while carrying the bodhisattva in her womb, as well as every wheel-turning king, and a wheel-turning king's mother while carrying that king in her womb.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag honestly that several of the specific proper names in this passage — individuals the Buddha is said to have prophesied by name — are transliterations I cannot identify with full confidence, and I have left them out of the English rendering above rather than guess at unverified English forms; the underlying point does not depend on resolving exactly who each named figure was.

The classification itself is a striking piece of Abhidharma cosmology, built directly on the vulnerability distinction from the previous section. Beings immune to harm from others but capable of ending their own existence include certain playful or wrathful desire-realm heavenly beings said to die from becoming lost in excessive joy or rage, and, remarkably, the Buddhas themselves, whose final nirvana is always a matter of their own will rather than anything imposed from outside. Beings vulnerable only to others, never to themselves, are simply the most physically fragile and dependent: those still developing in the womb or the egg. Most ordinary desire-realm beings are vulnerable both ways, the everyday human condition. And the "immune to both" category gathers a striking mixture: beings in the disembodied intermediate state between death and rebirth, every being of the form and formless realms (whose life, per the previous section, arises fixed and unassailable), and a specific list of protected desire-realm beings — even hell-beings and the inhabitants of Uttarakuru appear here, alongside those genuinely established on the path of seeing, a bodhisattva in his final life and his mother while pregnant with him, and a wheel-turning king and his mother under the same condition — each protected, for different doctrinal reasons, from having their fixed karmic course of events interrupted by violence.

29. A Closing Objection on the Peak of Existence, and the Life Faculty's Close

A final scriptural wrinkle is resolved through a general principle about how Buddhist scripture sometimes cites a single representative example to stand for an entire class — closing out the life faculty for good.

Chinese Source

若爾，何故契經中言：大德！何等有情所得自體非可自害非可他害。舍利子！謂在非想非非想處受生有情。傳說所餘無色靜慮所得自體，可為自地聖道所害，亦上他地近分所害。有頂自上二害俱無，是故說為俱非可害。豈不有頂亦為他地聖道所害，應名他害？如是應說舉後顯初，如或有處舉初顯後，或復有處舉後顯初。云何有處舉初顯後？如契經說：如梵眾天是名第一樂生天。云何有處舉後顯初？如契經說：如極光淨天是名第二樂生天。彼經如聲顯譬喻義，可作是說舉一顯餘，喻法舉一顯同類故。此無如聲，不可例彼。若顯喻義方得有，如聲是則如聲，餘經應不有。如餘經說：有色有情身異想異，如人一分，天是第一識住。故知非喻亦有如聲。傍論且止。已辯命根。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, then why does a sutra state: "Venerable one! Which sentient beings have obtained a self-body that can be harmed neither by themselves nor by another?" "Śāriputra! Those sentient beings reborn in the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception." Tradition holds that the self-bodies obtained in the remaining, lower formless dhyānas can indeed be harmed — either by the noble path belonging to their own level, or by the approach-stage concentration of a higher level. Only at the Peak of Existence are both of these kinds of harm entirely absent, which is why it alone is said to be harmable by neither. But is the Peak of Existence not also, in the end, affected by the noble path of another, higher order — should this not then also count as "harmed by another"? It should be understood that this citation names a later item to indicate an earlier one, just as, in some places, a text names the first item to indicate what follows, and in other places names the last item to indicate what came before. What is meant by "naming the first to indicate what follows"? As a sutra states: "Like the Heaven of Brahmā's Assembly — this is called the first heaven of joyful rebirth." What is meant by "naming the last to indicate what came before"? As a sutra states: "Like the Heaven of Radiant Light — this is called the second heaven of joyful rebirth." In that sutra, the word "like" expresses a genuine analogy, so it may indeed be said that one example is cited to represent the rest, since an analogy, by its nature, cites one instance to stand for its whole class. This present passage has no such word "like," and so cannot simply be treated the same way as that case. If this "citing one to indicate the rest" pattern only held wherever an explicit marker such as "like" were present, then it should not hold in other sutras lacking that word either — yet another sutra states: "There are sentient beings with form but differing in body, and differing also in perception, such as human beings and a certain group of gods — this is the first abode of consciousness," which plainly uses this same "citing one to represent the class" pattern without using the word "like" at all. This shows that the pattern holds even without an explicit marker like "like." Let this digression now rest. The life faculty has now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to be honest that the precise logical connection this passage draws — between the puzzle about the Peak of Existence apparently still being "harmed" by a higher noble path, and the general rhetorical principle about scripture citing one representative example for a whole class — is more compressed in the source than I can fully unpack with confidence; I have translated it as literally and faithfully as I can rather than smoothing the connection into something tidier than the text itself provides.

The broader point is clear enough, though, and genuinely useful as a hermeneutic principle: when a sutra names only one example — the Peak of Existence — as the being "harmable by neither," it need not mean that this is the sole case; it may simply be citing a single representative instance to stand for the entire, longer list already established. The Brahmā's-Assembly and Radiant-Light examples show this pattern operating with an explicit word for "like," while the "abodes of consciousness" example shows the identical pattern operating without one — establishing that the rhetorical convention does not depend on any single verbal marker to be recognized. With this, fascicle 5's long treatment of the life faculty — its definition, the regress puzzle over what sustains it, the duration-force theory and its rejection, why beings die, and who can and cannot be killed — comes to its close.

30. A New Topic: The Four Characteristics Every Conditioned Dharma Bears

The treatise turns to one of Abhidharma philosophy's most famous and consequential topics — the four marks that define what it even means for a dharma to be conditioned at all.

Chinese Source

諸相者何？頌曰：相謂諸有為，生住異滅性。論曰：由此四種是有為相，法若有此應是有為，與此相違是無為法。此於諸法能起名生，能安名住，能衰名異，能壞名滅。性是體義。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the characteristics? Verse: the characteristics are the nature of all conditioned dharmas — arising, abiding, decay, and cessation. Prose: because these four are the characteristics of the conditioned, any dharma possessing them must be conditioned, and whatever is the reverse of this is unconditioned. With respect to dharmas, that which is able to bring a dharma into being is called "arising"; that which is able to settle it is called "abiding"; that which is able to make it decline is called "decay"; and that which is able to destroy it is called "cessation." "Nature" here means essence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This opens what is arguably one of the single most historically significant topics in all of Abhidharma philosophy: the four characteristics (四相) — arising, abiding, decay, and cessation — that the treatise proposes as the very definition of what makes a dharma "conditioned" at all. Where the earlier chapters distinguished the conditioned from the unconditioned mainly by example (form, mind, and mental factors versus space and the two cessations), this section proposes a genuinely general criterion: a dharma is conditioned exactly insofar as something brings it into being, sustains it, wears it down, and finally destroys it. The stakes here reach far beyond bookkeeping — this topic sits at the very heart of the Buddhist doctrine of impermanence and the theory of momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda), and the debate about to unfold, over whether these four are genuinely separate real dharmas in their own right, will occupy the treatise for a considerable stretch.

31. Why Four Marks Here, When Some Sutras Speak of Only Three?

An immediate objection is met: elsewhere, scripture speaks of only three marks of the conditioned, omitting "abiding" — the treatise explains this as a matter of naming convention, not an actual disagreement about how many marks there are.

Chinese Source

豈不經說有三有為之有為相？於此經中應說有四，不說者何？所謂住相。然經說住異，是此異別名。如生名起，滅名為盡。如是應知，異名住異。若法令行三世遷流，此經說為有為之相，令諸有情生厭畏故。謂彼諸行生力所遷，令從未來流入現在。異及滅相力所遷迫，令從現在流入過去，令其衰異及壞滅故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Does a sutra not state that there are three characteristics of the conditioned? In this present treatise, it ought to be said that there are four — why, then, does that sutra not mention one, namely, the characteristic of abiding? In fact, that sutra does speak of "abiding-and-decay" together, as a further name for this same decay. Just as "arising" is another name for "coming into being," and "cessation" is another name for "utter ending," so too should "decay" here be understood as another name for "abiding-and-decay" combined. When a dharma is such that it causes formations to be swept along through the three times, this is what that sutra calls a characteristic of the conditioned, spoken in order to inspire weariness and apprehension in sentient beings. That is: these formations are swept along by the power of arising, carried from the future into the present; and they are swept along and pressed forward by the power of decay and cessation, carried from the present into the past, causing them to decline, change, and be destroyed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The apparent discrepancy — three marks in some scriptural passages, four in this treatise's own analysis — turns out to be purely terminological rather than substantive. That sutra's mention of "abiding-and-decay" as a single compound term is read here as a coded way of naming decay specifically, not evidence that abiding was ever omitted as a genuine fourth mark; it simply was not given its own separate word in that particular passage. The pastoral point behind naming these marks at all is made explicit too: the whole scheme exists to inspire a healthy sense of weariness toward conditioned existence, framing every formation as something perpetually swept forward — pulled out of the future into the present by the force of arising, then pressed onward out of the present into the past by the combined forces of decay and cessation, with nothing conditioned ever managing to simply stay put.

32. Three Enemies in a Dense Forest: Why "Abiding" Is Not Counted Among the Marks of Impermanence

A vivid image explains why abiding is treated differently from the other three marks — and answers a further objection about why the unconditioned, too, cannot simply borrow the mark of "abiding" for itself.

Chinese Source

傳說如有人處稠林，有三怨敵欲為損害，一從稠林牽之令出、一衰其力、一壞命根。三相於行應知亦爾。住於彼行攝受安立，常樂與彼不相捨離，故不立在有為相中。又無為法有自相住，住相濫彼故經不說。有謂：此經說住與異總合為一名住異相。何用如是總合說為？住是有情所愛著處，為令厭捨與異合說，如示黑耳與吉祥俱，是故定有四有為相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Tradition offers this image: it is like a person dwelling within a dense forest, beset by three enemies bent on doing harm — one drags him out of the forest's shelter, one saps his strength, and one destroys his very life. The three marks — arising, decay, and cessation — should be understood to act upon formations in just this way. Abiding, by contrast, takes hold of and settles those same formations, always wishing never to be parted from them, and for this reason it is not classed among these marks of impermanence working against them. It might also be asked why the unconditioned, too, does not simply borrow "abiding" as its own mark, since it plainly does not perish — but the unconditioned has its own distinct mode of abiding proper to itself, and since "abiding" as a mark of the conditioned would be too easily confused with that unrelated sense, the sutra simply does not apply the word to the unconditioned at all. There is another view: that sutra's mention of "abiding-and-decay" combines the two into a single term precisely called "the mark of abiding-and-decay" together. Why would it be combined this way? Because abiding is exactly what sentient beings cling to and love — so, in order to inspire weariness and renunciation toward it, it is deliberately paired together with decay in a single phrase, the way an inauspicious sign might deliberately be mentioned alongside an auspicious one, precisely to temper any excessive attachment to the auspicious one alone. For this reason, there are, after all, definitely four characteristics of the conditioned.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-enemies image is one of this treatise's more memorable pieces of imagery: arising, decay, and cessation are cast as three separate assailants, each performing a distinct hostile function — dragging a formation out of its prior shelter into being, wearing down its strength while it persists, and finally destroying it outright — while abiding plays the opposite role entirely, clutching a formation close and wishing never to let it go. This is exactly why abiding does not belong among the marks that work impermanence upon a formation: its whole character runs the other way, toward clinging rather than toward loss.

The second explanation offered for why "abiding" sometimes goes unnamed is psychologically astute: since abiding is precisely the quality of conditioned existence that beings find most attractive and cling to most tightly, naming it plainly, on its own, would risk reinforcing exactly that attachment. Pairing it explicitly with decay in a single phrase — the way one might deliberately mention a bad omen right alongside a good one, precisely to keep the good one from being enjoyed too complacently — is read as a deliberate pedagogical choice, not an oversight, and the treatise still insists there are genuinely four marks underneath either way of naming them.

33. Do the Marks Themselves Need Marks? The Eight-and-One Solution

A puzzle strikingly similar to fascicle 4's possession-of-possession regress resurfaces: if arising, abiding, decay, and cessation are themselves conditioned dharmas, don't they too need their own arising, abiding, decay, and cessation — forever?

Chinese Source

此生等相既是有為，應更別有生等四相。若更有相便致無窮，彼復有餘生等相故。應言更有，然非無窮。所以者何？頌曰：此有生生等，於八一有能。論曰：此謂前說四種本相。生生等者，謂四隨相，生生、住住、異異、滅滅。諸行有為由四本相，本相有為由四隨相，豈不本相如所相法一一應有四種隨相，此復各四展轉無窮？無斯過失，四本四隨於八於一功能別故。何謂功能？謂法作用或謂士用。四種本相一一皆於八法有用，四種隨相一一皆於一法有用。其義云何？謂法生時并其自體九法俱起，自體為一相隨相八。本相中生除其自性生餘八法，隨相生生於九法內唯生本生。謂如雌雞，有生多子有唯生一。生與生生，生八生一其力亦爾。本相中住亦除自性住餘八法，隨相住住於九法中唯住本住。異及滅相隨應亦爾。是故生等相復有相，隨相唯四無無窮失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since these characteristics — arising and the rest — are themselves conditioned, there ought to be a further, separate set of four characteristics belonging to them as well. But if there is a further set of characteristics, this would lead to an infinite regress, since those further characteristics would themselves need still further characteristics of arising and so on. It should indeed be said that there is a further set — yet this does not lead to an infinite regress. Why is this? Verse: there are these secondary characteristics, arising-of-arising and the rest, whose power extends to eight, or to one. Prose: "these" refers to the four primary characteristics already described above. "Arising-of-arising and the rest" refers to the four secondary characteristics: arising-of-arising, abiding-of-abiding, decay-of-decay, and cessation-of-cessation. Formations are conditioned by means of the four primary characteristics; the primary characteristics are themselves conditioned by means of the four secondary characteristics. But should not each primary characteristic, being itself a dharma marked in this way, require four further secondary characteristics of its own, and each of those, in turn, four more, unfolding without end? This fault does not arise, because the four primary and four secondary characteristics differ in their power, extending respectively to eight dharmas, or to just one. What is meant by "power"? It means a dharma's function, or what is also called its personal efficacy. Each of the four primary characteristics has its function with respect to eight dharmas; each of the four secondary characteristics has its function with respect to only a single dharma. What does this mean? When a dharma arises, nine dharmas arise together along with its own essence — the dharma's own essence counts as one, and the primary and secondary characteristics together account for the remaining eight. Among the primary characteristics, "arising" produces every one of these eight dharmas except its own self-nature; the secondary characteristic "arising-of-arising," among these same nine dharmas, produces only the primary "arising" itself, and nothing else. It is like a hen: some hens lay many chicks, while others lay only one. "Arising" and "arising-of-arising" have exactly this same difference of power — one producing eight, the other producing only one. Among the primary characteristics, "abiding," likewise, causes every one of these same eight dharmas except its own self-nature to abide; the secondary characteristic "abiding-of-abiding," among these nine dharmas, causes only the primary "abiding" itself to abide. Decay and cessation, and their corresponding secondary characteristics, should be understood in just the same way. For this reason, the characteristics of arising and the rest do indeed have further characteristics of their own — but since the secondary characteristics number only four in total, and each has a strictly limited scope of function, there is no fault of infinite regress.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is structurally the exact same puzzle fascicle 4 worked through at length for possession — if X is real and applies to conditioned dharmas, and X is itself conditioned, doesn't X need its own X, forever? — and the solution here is a close cousin of that earlier one, though worked out with even more numerical precision. The key move is asymmetric power: when any dharma arises, it brings with it not four but nine simultaneous companions — itself, plus its four primary characteristics, plus four secondary characteristics belonging to those primary characteristics. Each primary characteristic (arising, abiding, decay, cessation) acts broadly, producing or affecting all eight of the other companion-dharmas in that cluster; each secondary characteristic, by contrast, acts with surgical narrowness, affecting only its single corresponding primary characteristic and nothing else. "Arising" produces the dharma itself plus seven other companions; "arising-of-arising" produces only "arising." The hen laying many chicks versus laying only one captures this precisely: same basic kind of causal power, wildly different scope. Because the secondary characteristics' scope is capped at exactly one target apiece, nothing beyond this second tier is ever needed — the regress stops itself by simple exhaustion of anything left for a third tier to act upon.

34. The Sautrāntika Challenge: "Why Analyze Empty Space This Way?"

Having just resolved the marks-of-marks regress with real precision, the treatise now faces a challenge to the entire enterprise: perhaps none of these characteristics are genuinely real entities at all.

Chinese Source

經部師說何緣如是分析虛空？非生等相有實法體如所分別。所以者何？無定量故。謂此諸相非如色等有定現、比或至教量證體實有。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Sautrāntika masters ask: why analyze empty space in this way? Arising and the rest do not have a genuinely real essence corresponding to how they have just been analyzed. Why is this? Because there is no fixed means of establishing them. That is: unlike form and the rest, these characteristics cannot be established as genuinely real entities through direct perception, through inference, or even through the authority of scripture.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Why analyze empty space this way?" is a genuinely pointed jab — accusing the entire preceding eight-and-one analysis of carefully dissecting something that was never there to begin with. The Sautrāntika challenge invokes this tradition's own standard evidentiary bar, the three recognized means of establishing that something is real: direct perception, inference, and scriptural authority. Arising, abiding, decay, and cessation, the challenge claims, pass none of these tests as genuinely separate entities — setting up a debate that will occupy the treatise through several more sections.

35. What Scripture Actually Means: A Sign, Not a Separate Substance

A key scriptural passage seemingly supporting the marks' reality is reinterpreted — not as proof of separate entities, but as a simple sign indicating that something is conditioned, the way a resting heron signals water without being water itself.

Chinese Source

若爾，何故契經中言：有為之起亦可了知，盡及住異亦可了知。天愛！汝等執文迷義，薄伽梵說義是所依。何謂此經所說實義？謂愚夫類無明所盲，於行相續執我我所，長夜於中而生耽著。世尊為斷彼執著故，顯行相續體是有為及緣生性，故作是說有三有為之有為相，非顯諸行一剎那中具有三相，由一剎那起等三相不可知故。非不可知應立為相，故彼契經復作是說：有為之起亦可了知，盡及住異亦可了知。然經重說有為言者，令知此相表是有為，勿謂此相表有為有，如居白鷺表水非無。亦勿謂表有為善惡，如童女相表善非善。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, then why does a sutra state: "The arising of the conditioned can indeed be known; its ending, and its abiding-and-decay, can indeed be known as well"? Dear friend, you cling to the wording and thereby miss its meaning — it is the Blessed One's own intended meaning that one should rely upon. What, then, is the real meaning this sutra intends? It is this: foolish, ordinary beings, blinded by ignorance, cling to a self and to what belongs to a self within the mere continuum of formations, and, throughout the long night of saṃsāra, become deeply attached to this. In order to sever this attachment, the World-Honored One revealed that the very essence of this continuum of formations is conditioned and dependently arisen — and it was for this reason that he spoke of three characteristics of the conditioned. He did not mean to show that a single formation, within one single moment, possesses all three of these characteristics at once, since arising and the other two occurring within a single moment could never actually be perceived or known at all. It is not the case, however, that being unknowable in this sense means these must therefore be established as a genuinely separate real characteristic — for that very sutra passage also goes on to state: "The arising of the conditioned can indeed be known; its ending, and its abiding-and-decay, can indeed be known as well." The reason the sutra repeats the word "conditioned" alongside each characteristic is simply to make clear that this mark serves to indicate that something is conditioned — not to suggest that this mark itself proves that "the conditioned" genuinely, separately exists as its own real entity, the way a resting white heron indicates that water is nearby without the heron itself being the water. Nor should it be taken to indicate whether the conditioned thing itself is wholesome or unwholesome, the way a young woman's bodily marks might traditionally be read as an omen of good fortune or bad.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The address "Dear friend" translates 天愛 (literally "beloved of the gods"), a mildly reproachful form of address in this tradition — something closer to "you innocent" or "you naive fellow" than a genuine term of affection — signaling that the Sautrāntika speaker regards the Vaibhāṣika's literalism as a basic interpretive mistake: clinging to a text's surface wording while missing what it was actually meant to accomplish.

The reinterpretation is careful and, I think, genuinely persuasive on its own terms. The sutra's real pastoral purpose, on this reading, was never to catalog separately existing entities at all — it was to break ordinary beings of their deep-seated clinging to a self within the mere flow of formations, by pointing out that this flow is thoroughly conditioned and dependently arisen. The "knowability" the sutra speaks of is then explained through a precise analogy: a resting white heron reliably indicates that water is nearby, without the heron itself being water, or without water needing to be directly visible for the sign to be trustworthy. In just the same way, "arising" and the other marks reliably indicate that something is conditioned, without themselves needing to be separate substances layered on top of the dharma they mark. The closing comparison — to reading a young woman's bodily marks as omens of fortune — adds a further precision: these marks indicate conditioned-ness alone, nothing about a dharma's moral quality, which is a wholly separate question the marks were never meant to answer.

36. Arising, Abiding, Decay, Cessation — All Belong to a Continuum, Never to One Instant

A cluster of verses, including one spoken of the Buddha's own disciple Nanda, nails down precisely what each of the four marks refers to — and all of them, the treatise insists, describe a continuum, never a single isolated moment.

Chinese Source

諸行相續初起名生，終盡位中說名為滅，中間相續隨轉名住，此前後別名為住異。世尊依此說難陀言：是善男子善知受生、善知受住及善知受衰異壞滅。故說頌言：相續初名生，滅謂終盡位，中隨轉名住，住異前後別。復有頌言：本無今有生，相續隨轉住，前後別住異，相續斷名滅。又有頌言：由諸法剎那，無住而有滅，彼自然滅故，執有住非理。是故唯於相續說住，由斯對法所說理成。

Ru-Yi Translation

The very first arising within a continuum of formations is called "arising"; the final, concluding moment of that continuum is called "cessation"; the continuing succession in between is called "abiding"; and the difference between the earlier and later phases within that same continuum is called "decay." It is on this basis that the World-Honored One said of Nanda: "This good son knows well how feeling arises, knows well how it abides, and knows well how it declines, changes, and is utterly destroyed." Hence it is said in verse: "The beginning of a continuum is named arising; cessation means its final, concluding phase; the continuing succession in between is named abiding; decay is the difference between its earlier and later phases." There is also this verse: "What was formerly absent and now exists is arising; the continuing succession is abiding; the difference between earlier and later is decay; the breaking off of the continuum is called cessation." And there is yet another verse: "Because all dharmas are momentary, there is no genuine abiding, only cessation; since they cease of their own accord, to hold that there is abiding is not reasonable." For this reason, "abiding" is spoken of only with reference to a continuum — and it is by this very account that what the Abhidharma texts state is shown to hold true.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Three separate verses converge on the same reductive point from slightly different angles, and the accumulation is deliberate: every one of the four marks, properly understood, describes a feature of an extended continuum — a beginning, an end, a middle stretch, and the drift between that stretch's earlier and later phases — never a property intrinsic to one isolated instant taken alone. The sharpest of the three verses states the underlying momentariness doctrine almost bluntly: because every dharma is momentary, strictly speaking there is no "abiding" at all, only ceaseless cessation from one instant to the next; the very idea that something "abides" is, in the fullest sense, simply not correct. "Abiding" survives in ordinary language only because a rapid succession of genuinely distinct, momentarily-existing dharmas looks, from a distance, like one thing continuing to persist.

37. The Abhidharma's Own Definition, and a Careful Reading of the Jñānaprasthāna

A canonical definition of "abiding" is quoted directly, followed by a careful clarification of a Jñānaprasthāna passage that might otherwise seem to describe a single moment's birth, death, and aging.

Chinese Source

故彼論言：云何名住？謂一切行已生未滅，非生已不滅名剎那法性。雖《發智論》作如是說：於一心中誰起謂生？誰盡謂死？誰住異謂老？而彼論文依眾同分相續心說，非一剎那。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hence that treatise states: "What is meant by 'abiding'? It refers to any formation that has already arisen but has not yet ceased." This is not to say that "having arisen, not yet ceasing" describes the true nature of a single momentary dharma. Although the Jñānaprasthāna does state: "Within a single mind, what is it that arises — this is called birth? What is it that comes to an end — this is called death? What is it that abides-and-decays — this is called aging?" — that treatise's text is speaking with reference to a mind continuing in succession within a single class-commonality, not with reference to a single momentary instant.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Jñānaprasthāna passage looks, on its surface, like exactly the kind of single-moment description the treatise has just spent two sections denying — speaking of "birth," "death," and "aging" all seemingly "within a single mind." The clarification defuses this by pointing to what "a single mind" means in that specific technical context: not one isolated instant, but a mind considered as one continuing, recognizable class-commonality across many moments — the same mind-stream persisting, aging, and eventually ending, described in the everyday language of birth, aging, and death that applies naturally to an extended life, not to a single flash of consciousness.

38. Applying the Four Marks to a Single Moment After All — Without Positing Separate Entities

Having insisted the marks describe a continuum, the Sautrāntika position now shows how they can still, in a more careful sense, apply even to a single momentary dharma — illustrated through the varying fall-speed of a thrown weight.

Chinese Source

又一一剎那諸有為法，離執實有物，四相亦成。云何得成？謂一一念本無今有名生，有已還無名滅，後後剎那嗣前前起名為住，即彼前後有差別故名住異。於前後念相似生時，前後相望非無差別。彼差別相云何應知？謂金剛等有擲未擲，及強力擲與弱力擲，速遲墮落時差別故。大種轉變差別義成。諸行相似相續生時，前後相望無多差別，故雖有異而見相似。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, even with respect to each single momentary conditioned dharma, apart from insisting these are genuinely separate real entities, the four characteristics can still be established. How can they be established? With respect to each single mind-moment: what was formerly absent and now exists is called "arising"; what has existed and then reverts to non-existence is called "cessation"; each later moment following upon and arising after the one before it is called "abiding"; and the very difference between that earlier and later moment is called "decay." When successive moments arise resembling one another, there is, even so, no absence of real difference between the earlier and the later. How, then, should this difference be understood? Consider a heavy weight, such as a piece of diamond, whether not yet thrown or already thrown, and whether thrown with great force or with little force — there is a genuine difference in how quickly or slowly it comes to fall. From this, the reality of a genuine difference in the transformation of the great elements is established. When formations arise in a resembling, continuous succession, there is, comparing earlier to later, no great degree of difference — hence, even though there is genuine difference, what appears is resemblance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the Sautrāntika position's real payoff: having denied that arising, abiding, decay, and cessation are separate real entities, it still owes an account of why these words feel meaningful even at the level of a single moment — and delivers one entirely in terms of existence and non-existence, with no further substance required. A moment "arises" simply by going from absent to present; it "ceases" simply by going from present back to absent; "abiding" is nothing more than a later moment following a resembling earlier one; and "decay" is simply whatever genuine difference exists between that earlier and later moment, however subtle.

The thrown-weight image does real work here, addressing an obvious worry: if successive moments merely resemble one another, how can there be genuine difference at all? The answer is that resemblance and real difference are perfectly compatible — a heavy weight thrown with great force falls measurably faster than the same weight thrown gently, even though, moment to moment, its position looks nearly identical between one instant and the next. Real, continuous change in the underlying physical elements is fully compatible with apparent, moment-to-moment resemblance; the difference is simply too fine-grained to notice directly, the same way a person watching a falling stone does not perceive its acceleration instant by instant, only its cumulative effect.

39. Does a Truly Final Moment Lack "Abiding"? And a Compact Restatement

One last objection tests whether "abiding" can really apply to every conditioned dharma without exception — followed by a compressed restatement of what the whole four-marks scheme ultimately comes down to.

Chinese Source

若爾最後聲光剎那及涅槃時，最後六處無後念故應無住異，是則所立相應不遍有為。此不說住為有為相。其義云何？謂住之異。故若有住亦必有異，由此立相無不遍失。然此經中世尊所說，有為之相略顯示者，謂有為法本無今有，有已還無及相續住。即此前後相望別異，此中何用生等別物？

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, then at the final moment of a sound or a light, or at the moment of final nirvana, since the last of the six sense fields has no further subsequent moment to follow it, there ought to be no abiding-and-decay present at all — and it would then follow that the characteristics as established do not universally apply to every conditioned dharma. This objection does not actually treat "abiding" as its own separate characteristic of the conditioned. What, then, is its point? It concerns "decay" specifically, considered as belonging to abiding. Therefore, wherever there is abiding, there must also necessarily be decay — and, understood this way, the characteristics as established suffer no failure of universal application after all. Now, what the World-Honored One meant to convey by this sutra's brief indication of the characteristics of the conditioned is simply this: a conditioned dharma was formerly absent and now exists, has existed and then reverts to non-existence, and continues in succession in between. It is precisely this difference between its earlier and later phases — what further need is there for arising and the rest as separate, additional entities?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection is sharp: a genuinely final moment — the last flicker of a sound or light, or the Buddha's own final nirvana — has no subsequent moment to follow it, so how could "abiding" apply there at all, if a scheme meant to cover every conditioned dharma without exception has a hole in it? The resolution turns on treating "decay" as intrinsically bound up with abiding rather than as an independent add-on: since decay is nothing but the difference between an earlier and later phase of abiding, wherever genuine abiding is present at all, decay is automatically present too, with no separate universal requirement being violated even at a final moment.

The passage then pulls back to offer a strikingly compact restatement of the entire scheme, in exactly the reductive spirit the Sautrāntika side has been arguing for: all four marks, properly understood, simply describe a dharma's temporal profile — absent, then present, then absent again, with a continuing stretch in between — and the rhetorical question closing this section asks, quite pointedly, why anything more than this plain temporal description should be required at all.

40. A Mark Need Not Be Separate From What It Marks

An objection presses on the reductive account from a different angle — if a characterizing mark simply collapses into the thing it characterizes, doesn't the very idea of a "mark" become empty? A chain of everyday analogies answers it.

Chinese Source

云何所相法即立為能相？如何大士相非異於大士？角犎[古*頁]蹄尾牛相非異牛，又如堅等地等界相非異地等，遠見上升知是煙相非異煙體。此有為相，理亦應然。雖了有為色等自性，乃至未了先無後無相續差別，仍未知彼體是有為，故非彼性即有為相。然非離彼性，有生等實物。

Ru-Yi Translation

How can the very thing characterized simply be established as itself the characterizing mark? Consider: how can a great being's distinguishing bodily marks fail to be different from the great being himself? The horns, hump, dewlap, hooves, and tail that mark an ox are not different from the ox itself; likewise, the element earth's mark of hardness is not different from earth itself; and seeing something rising in the distance and knowing it to be the mark of smoke is not different from the very substance of the smoke. This characteristic of the conditioned should be understood in just the same way. Although one may already grasp the essential nature of a conditioned dharma such as form, until one also grasps the difference between its former absence and its later existence within a continuum, one still does not know that its essence is conditioned — hence this essential nature by itself is not simply identical to the characteristic of being conditioned. Yet this is not to say that, apart from that essential nature, there exists some further, separate real entity called arising and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The worry behind this objection is fair: if a "characteristic" is nothing over and above the dharma it characterizes, doesn't the whole notion of a distinguishing mark lose its meaning? The answer reaches for ordinary, everyday cases of exactly this relationship, all perfectly intelligible without positing any separate substance: an ox's horns, hump, dewlap, hooves, and tail genuinely mark it as an ox, and are genuinely meaningful identifying features, without being some further thing floating apart from the ox's own body. Earth's hardness marks it as earth in the same non-separate way; smoke seen rising in the distance marks the presence of fire without the "mark" being any different substance from the smoke itself.

The resolution threads a careful needle: recognizing a dharma as conditioned genuinely adds something to bare acquaintance with its essential nature — specifically, grasping its temporal profile of absence, presence, and eventual absence again — so the "mark" of being conditioned is not simply a redundant restatement of what the dharma already is. But that added recognition still does not require inventing a wholly separate entity riding alongside the dharma; it is, in every case just surveyed, the very same nature considered under a further aspect, exactly as an ox's hump is the very same ox considered under the aspect of one of its identifying features.

41. The Case Reopens: Staggered Functions, Not Simultaneous Contradiction

The Vaibhāṣika position is given its fullest hearing yet: even granting that arising, abiding, decay, and cessation genuinely co-exist as real entities alongside a single dharma, their actual causal functions are staggered across different phases, avoiding any real contradiction.

Chinese Source

若離有為色等自性有生等物，復何非理？一法一時應即生住衰異壞滅，許俱有故。此難不然，用時別故，謂生作用在於未來，現在已生不更生故。諸法生已正現在時，住等三相作用方起，非生用時有餘三用，故雖俱有而不相違。

Ru-Yi Translation

If arising and the rest were indeed separate real entities apart from the essential nature of a conditioned dharma such as form, what exactly would be wrong with that? One dharma, at one time, would then have to be simultaneously arising, abiding, declining, decaying, and being destroyed all at once, since these would be conceded to co-exist together. This objection does not actually hold, because their functions operate at different times: the function of arising operates while the dharma is still future, since, once it has already arisen and is present, it is not arisen yet again. Once dharmas have already arisen and are genuinely present, the functions of abiding and the other two characteristics then begin to operate — it is not the case that, at the very time arising is functioning, the remaining three functions are also functioning. Hence, even though the four marks co-exist together as real entities, there is no actual contradiction between them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the Vaibhāṣika's central move for the whole remaining debate, and it is a genuinely clever one: existence and function are not the same thing. All four marks can genuinely, simultaneously exist alongside a dharma without any of them actually contradicting one another, because only one mark's causal function is ever actually operating at any given phase. "Arising" does its work strictly before the dharma is present, bringing it from future into present; only once that transition is complete do "abiding," "decay," and "cessation" take their turn functioning. Co-existence as real entities is one claim; simultaneous operation is a separate claim entirely — and only the second, not the first, would actually generate the absurdity of a dharma being born, persisting, decaying, and dying all in the very same instant.

42. Pressing the Solution: Does a Future Dharma Even Exist Yet? And Can Abiding Coexist with Decay?

The staggered-function solution is tested with a barrage of follow-up questions, probing whether it can actually be stated coherently without running into further contradictions of its own.

Chinese Source

且應思擇，未來法體為有為無，然後可成生於彼位有用無用。設許未來生有作用，如何成未來？應說未來相。法現在時生用已謝，如何成現在？應說現在相。又住等三用俱現在，應一法體一剎那中即有安住衰異壞滅。若時住相能住此法，即時異滅能衰壞者，爾時此法為名安住、為名衰異、為名壞滅？諸說住等用不同時，彼說便違剎那滅義。若言我說一法諸相用，皆究竟名一剎那。汝今應說，何緣住相與二俱生，而住先能用所住法非異非滅？若住力強能先用者，後何成劣而并本法俱遭異滅所衰壞耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

One should first carefully consider: does the essence of a future dharma exist, or does it not — only then can it be established whether "arising" has a function or no function with respect to that dharma at that stage. Supposing one grants that "arising" has a function while the dharma is still future — how, then, is that dharma's futurity itself to be established? One would have to specify a separate mark of "futurity" as well. And once the dharma is present, arising's function has already ceased — how, then, is its presence itself to be established? One would have to specify a separate mark of "presence" too. Moreover, if the functions of abiding and the other two marks are all simultaneously present, then a single dharma's essence, within a single moment, would have to be at once settled in abiding, declining in decay, and being destroyed in cessation, all together. If, at the very moment abiding's mark is able to sustain this dharma, decay-and-cessation are simultaneously able to make it decline and be destroyed, then, at that very moment, should this dharma be called "settled in abiding," or "declining in decay," or "destroyed in cessation"? If it is said that abiding and the other functions do not actually operate at the very same time after all, then that position simply contradicts the doctrine of momentary cessation. If, instead, one says: "What I mean is that all of these characteristics' functions, taken altogether, are ultimately to be understood within the scope of a single moment" — then you must now explain: why is it that, although abiding's mark arises together with the other two, abiding alone is able to function first, sustaining the dharma it supports, keeping it neither decayed nor destroyed? And if abiding's power is strong enough to function first, why does it later become so weak that the dharma, together with abiding itself, is overtaken and worn down by decay and destroyed by cessation?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The staggered-function solution now faces a cascade of genuine difficulties. First, a regress threat structurally similar to the marks-of-marks puzzle from earlier: explaining a dharma's "futurity" or "presence" by appeal to arising's function threatens to require yet further marks just to establish what "future" and "present" themselves even mean. Second, and more damaging: if abiding, decay, and cessation are all said to function together within one single moment (the natural reading, since the treatise elsewhere insists everything genuinely happens within single moments), then the dharma would, absurdly, have to simultaneously be settled, declining, and destroyed — with no coherent answer to what should even be called of it at that instant. And even setting that aside, a further puzzle remains: why does abiding get to function first, holding decay and cessation temporarily at bay, only to later — for no clearly stated reason — lose that power and succumb alongside the very dharma it was sustaining? The solution that looked clean in the previous section is, section by section, being shown to generate as many puzzles as it resolves.

43. Why Would Abiding Ever Stop? The Redundancy of Decay and Cessation

The critique sharpens into a genuinely damaging argument: if abiding's whole job is to keep a dharma from decaying, nothing should ever be able to stop it — and if it does eventually stop on its own, decay and cessation turn out to have nothing left to do.

Chinese Source

若言住相已起作用不應更起猶如生者，生應可然。夫生用者，謂引所生令入現在，已入不應復引入故。住不應爾。夫住用者，謂安所住令不衰滅，已住可令永安住故。由斯住相用應常起，不可例生令無再用。又誰障住用，令暫有還無？若言異滅能為障者，異滅力應強，何不於先用？又住用息，異滅本法自然不住，異滅二相何處如何而起作用？復有何事須二用耶？由住攝持，諸法生已暫時不滅。住用既捨，法定不住即自然滅，故異滅用更無所為。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one says that abiding's mark, once its function has arisen, should not arise again, just as arising's function does not recur — this may hold for arising, but not for abiding. Arising's function is to draw the dharma to be generated into presence; once it has already entered presence, there is no need to draw it in again. Abiding should not be treated the same way. Abiding's function is to settle what it supports so that it does not decay or cease; and since it has already settled it, it could, in principle, keep it settled forever. By this reasoning, abiding's function ought to arise continuously without end — it cannot simply be treated on the model of arising, as if it too had no capacity to function again. Moreover, what, exactly, obstructs abiding's function, causing it to be present for only a time and then absent again? If one says that decay and cessation are what obstruct it, then decay and cessation's power ought to be strong enough to have obstructed it from the very start — why, then, did they not function earlier instead of waiting? And if abiding's function simply ceases on its own, then the dharma itself would naturally fail to abide with no need for decay and cessation to do anything at all — in which case, where, and how, would these two marks, decay and cessation, ever actually find any function left to perform? What further work, indeed, would there even be for these two functions to accomplish? For it is precisely through abiding's sustaining power that dharmas, once arisen, temporarily do not cease; and once abiding's function is relinquished, the dharma definitely fails to abide and so ceases entirely on its own — meaning decay and cessation's functions have nothing left to do at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely devastating line of argument, and it is worth sitting with why. Arising is self-limiting by its very nature — its one job is to bring a dharma into presence, a job that is simply finished once done, with nothing left to repeat. Abiding's stated job, by contrast, is to actively keep a dharma from decaying — and there is no obvious reason, internal to that description, why a power capable of doing this once should ever stop doing it. If something external eventually overpowers abiding, that something would have to be decay and cessation — but then decay and cessation's power must already have been present and available from the very start, raising the question of why they waited at all rather than acting immediately.

The trap closes with real elegance: either decay and cessation are strong enough to overpower abiding, in which case they should have done so immediately rather than waiting through a whole span of "abiding" first — or abiding simply runs out on its own, with no need for decay and cessation's supposed power to do anything at all. Either way, positing decay and cessation as separate, additional real entities with their own genuine causal function turns out to add nothing that abiding's own eventual exhaustion could not already fully explain by itself. This is the sharpest form yet of the charge that these entities are simply redundant.

44. Why "Decay" Cannot Even Coherently Apply to a Single Dharma

The critique reaches its sharpest point yet: "decay," by its very definition, requires comparing two different states — which means it can never, even in principle, be a real feature belonging to just one single dharma at all.

Chinese Source

又應一法生已未壞名住，住已壞時名滅。理且可然。異於一法，進退推徵理不應有。所以者何？異謂前後性相轉變，非即此法可言異此。故說頌言：即前異不成，異前非一法，是故於一法，立異終不成。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should also be granted that, for a single dharma, the phase after it has arisen but before it has been destroyed can reasonably be called "abiding," and the moment it is destroyed can reasonably be called "cessation" — this much, at least, makes sense. But "decay," with respect to a single dharma, cannot reasonably be established at all, whichever way one probes it, forward or backward. Why is this? "Decay" means a transformation of nature between an earlier state and a later one — one cannot meaningfully say that this very same dharma itself "decays into" something different from itself. Hence it is said in verse: "If it remains the very same as before, decay cannot be established; if it decayed into something different from before, that would no longer be one and the same dharma. Therefore, with respect to a single dharma, decay can, in the end, never be established."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a purely conceptual objection, and a clean one: "abiding" and "cessation" at least describe something intelligible about a single dharma taken on its own — that it persists, or that it ends. "Decay," by contrast, is inherently a comparative, relational notion — it names a difference between an earlier state and a later one. But a genuinely single dharma, considered strictly in itself at one moment, has no "earlier" and "later" internal to it for anything to differ between. The closing verse presses this into a clean dilemma: either the dharma at the "decayed" stage is still the very same dharma as before (in which case nothing has actually decayed — there is no difference to speak of), or it genuinely differs from what came before (in which case it is, by definition, no longer one and the same dharma, but something else entirely — so, again, there is no single dharma that can be said to have decayed). Either horn of the dilemma denies that "decay" can coherently be predicated of one dharma alone.

45. The Purgative-Medicine Joke, and the Verdict: Marks Belong to a Continuum

A memorable comic analogy caps the critique of redundant causation, followed by a final piece of counter-evidence from mind's own momentary nature — closing this extended debate decisively in favor of the continuum-based account.

Chinese Source

雖餘部說遇滅因緣，滅相方能滅所滅法。而彼所說，應如有言：服瀉藥時天來令利。即滅因緣應滅所滅，何須別執有滅相為。又心心所許剎那滅，更不須待餘滅因緣，應滅與住用無先後。是則一法於一時中亦住亦滅不應正理，故依相續說有為相，不違正理善順契經。

Ru-Yi Translation

Although other schools say that, upon encountering the causal condition for cessation, the mark of cessation is then able to destroy the dharma it destroys — what they say amounts to something like the saying: "While taking a purgative medicine, a god comes down and causes the diarrhea." The causal condition for cessation should itself be sufficient to destroy the dharma to be destroyed — what need is there to additionally posit some separate "mark of cessation" as well? Moreover, mind and mental factors are themselves accepted to cease within a single moment, with no need to wait for any further, separate causal condition of cessation at all — so the function of ceasing and the function of abiding ought to have no priority or sequence between them whatsoever. It follows, then, that for a single dharma, within a single moment, to be both abiding and ceasing does not accord with reason at all. Therefore, the characteristics of the conditioned are properly spoken of with reference to a continuum — a position that does not contradict reason, and accords well with scripture.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The purgative-medicine joke lands precisely: crediting a divine intervention for an effect the medicine alone was already fully sufficient to produce is an obvious case of redundant, superfluous causation — and positing a separate "mark of cessation," on top of the already-sufficient causal condition that brings a dharma's cessation about, is charged with exactly the same absurdity. Why credit two causes when one already does the whole job?

The closing piece of evidence turns the Vaibhāṣika's own doctrine against itself: this same tradition holds that mind and mental factors cease within a single moment, requiring no separate external causal condition to trigger that cessation at all — which would mean, for these dharmas at least, that the functions of abiding and ceasing have no real temporal separation between them whatsoever, collapsing the very staggering that the whole solution back in section 41 depended on. With this, an extended and genuinely serious internal critique reaches its verdict: positing arising, abiding, decay, and cessation as separate real entities functioning within a single moment leads to one contradiction after another, while grounding these same four marks in an extended continuum — exactly the reductive account argued for many sections ago — remains fully coherent and, the treatise now states plainly, in no way conflicts with scripture. This is one of the most sustained and substantively-argued concessions to a rival position anywhere in this fascicle.

46. Why Don't All Future Dharmas Arise at Once?

A pointed question tests the reality of "arising" as a separate mark from a fresh angle — and the answer hands the Sautrāntika side a significant opening.

Chinese Source

若生在未來生所生法，未來一切法何不俱生？頌曰：生能生所生，非離因緣合。論曰：非離所餘因緣和合，唯生相力能生所生，故諸未來非皆頓起。若爾，我等唯見因緣有生功能，無別生相。有因緣合諸法即生，無即不生，何勞生相？故知唯有因緣力起。

Ru-Yi Translation

If "arising" exists in the future and generates the dharma it is to generate there, then why do not all future dharmas arise all at once, simultaneously? Verse: arising is able to generate what it generates, but not apart from the coming-together of causes and conditions. Prose: it is not that the power of the mark of arising alone, apart from the coming-together of all other causes and conditions, is able to generate the dharma to be generated — hence not all future dharmas arise all at once. If that is so, then we observe only that causes and conditions themselves have the power to generate — there is no need for any separate mark of arising at all. Wherever causes and conditions come together, dharmas simply arise; wherever they do not come together, dharmas do not arise — what need is there, then, to trouble ourselves with positing "arising" as a separate mark? This shows that dharmas arise solely through the power of causes and conditions.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Vaibhāṣika's own answer to this question hands the Sautrāntika exactly the opening it needs: if "arising" only ever generates a dharma in conjunction with the full array of other causes and conditions, and never on its own, then the obvious question becomes why "arising" is needed as a separate factor at all, rather than simply crediting the full causal picture — the ordinary causes and conditions already fully explain why a dharma arises when it does and not otherwise. The reductive challenge, dormant since the earlier sections, resurfaces here with real force.

47. A Grammatical Argument for Realism: "Form's Arising"

The Vaibhāṣika counters with an argument from language itself — if there were truly no separate entity called "arising," how could ordinary speech coherently say "form's arising," the way it never says "form's form"?

Chinese Source

豈諸有法皆汝所知？法性幽微甚難知故，雖現有體而不可知。生相若無應無生覺，又第六轉言不應成，謂色之生、受之生等。如不應說色之色言。如責無生，乃至無滅皆如是責，隨其所應。

Ru-Yi Translation

How could it be that you already know every single existing dharma there is? The nature of dharmas is subtle and profound, extremely difficult to know — so, even though a dharma genuinely has a real essence present, it may still not be directly knowable in the way you demand. If there were truly no mark of arising, there ought to be no cognition of "arising" at all; and, moreover, the sixth grammatical case — the possessive "of" — ought not to be applicable at all, as when one says "form's arising," "feeling's arising," and so on — just as one would never say "form's form." The same challenge just raised against there being no "arising" should likewise be raised, in corresponding fashion, against there being no "cessation," and so on for each of the other marks.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This argument from grammar closely echoes a nearly identical move made about "possession" back in fascicle 4: the very structure of ordinary language — specifically, the possessive construction "X's Y" — is offered as evidence that Y must be a genuinely distinct entity belonging to X, since one would never phrase a mere restatement of X itself this way. "Form's form" is nonsensical precisely because form is not a separate possession of form; "form's arising," by contrast, is perfectly natural phrasing, suggesting arising really is something form possesses, distinct from form itself.

48. The Grammatical Argument Answered — And the Reductive Position Restated in Full

The Sautrāntika turns the grammatical argument back on itself with a battery of counter-examples, before restating, with unusual clarity, exactly what "arising" amounts to on the reductive account.

Chinese Source

若爾，為成空無我覺，法外應執空無我性。為成一二大小各別合離彼此有性等覺，應如外道法外執有數量各別合離彼此有等別性。又為成立第六轉言，應執別有色之聚性。又如說言色之自性，此第六轉言何得成？是故生等唯假建立，無別實物，為了諸行本無今有假立為生。如是本無今有生相，依色等法種類眾多，為簡所餘說第六轉言色之生受之生等，為令他知此生唯色非餘受等。餘例亦然。如世間說栴檀之香、石子之體，此亦應爾。如是住等隨應當知。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that reasoning held, then, in order to account for the cognition of "emptiness" and "non-self," one would have to posit "emptiness" and "non-self" as further, separate real natures existing apart from the dharmas themselves. And, in order to account for the cognition of "oneness," "twoness," "largeness," "smallness," "combination," "separation," "this," "that," "existence," and so on, one would have to posit these too as separate real categories existing apart from the dharmas — just as the non-Buddhist schools posit number, distinctness, combination, separation, this-ness, that-ness, and existence as their own separate real categories apart from the dharmas themselves. And, in order to account for the sixth grammatical case, one would have to posit a further, separate "collection-nature" belonging to form — or, as when one simply says "form's own nature," how could this same sixth-case expression even be accounted for on your view? For this reason, arising and the rest are established merely as conventional labels, without any separate, real entity involved at all — it is simply in order to indicate that formations were formerly absent and now exist that "arising" is provisionally established. And since this "formerly absent, now existing" characteristic applies across the many distinct categories of dharmas such as form, in order to specify which category is meant, the sixth-case expression is used — "form's arising," "feeling's arising," and so on — simply to let others know that this particular instance of arising belongs to form specifically, and not to feeling or any of the others. The same pattern holds for other examples too, just as ordinary speech says "sandalwood's fragrance" or "a stone's substance" — this should be understood in just the same way. Abiding and the rest should be understood accordingly, each in its own corresponding manner.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The counter-attack is devastating if it lands: if the mere existence of a possessive grammatical construction proved a separate real entity, then "emptiness" and "non-self" — two of Buddhism's own most central doctrines, both explicitly deployed to deny that anything substantial underlies certain descriptions — would have to be real substances too, alongside a whole menagerie of separately-existing "onenesses," "twonesses," and "existences" copied straight from the very non-Buddhist Vaiśeṣika-style ontology this tradition elsewhere criticizes. Since no one on either side wants that conclusion, the grammatical argument cannot be valid as a general inference rule.

What remains is a clean, positive restatement of the whole reductive position: "arising" is simply a conventional label marking the fact that a formation was formerly absent and now exists — nothing more. Because many different kinds of dharma (form, feeling, and so on) can each undergo this same "formerly absent, now existing" transition, the possessive construction is needed only to specify which dharma is meant in a given case — exactly the same grammatical function served by ordinary phrases like "sandalwood's fragrance," where no one supposes "fragrance" must be some separate substance riding alongside the sandalwood, over and above the sandalwood's own scented nature.

49. Why Doesn't the Unconditioned Also "Arise"? A Final Clarification

One more objection is met before the debate finally closes — if formations can arise without a separate mark of arising, why does this reductive account not also predict, absurdly, that even space itself should "arise"?

Chinese Source

若行離生相而得生者，虛空無為等何故不生？諸行名生由本無今有，無為體常有，何得言生？又如法爾不說一切皆有生，如是應許非一切法皆可生。又如有為同有生相，而許因緣望有為法或有功能或無功能。如是應許一切有為及無為法同無生相，而諸因緣望彼二法一有生用、一無生用。

Ru-Yi Translation

If formations could arise without any separate mark of arising at all, then why do space and the other unconditioned dharmas not likewise arise? Formations are called "arising" precisely because they were formerly absent and now exist; the unconditioned, by its very essence, always exists — how, then, could it ever be said to "arise" at all? Moreover, just as it is simply how things naturally stand that not everything whatsoever is said to have arising, so too it should be granted that not every dharma whatsoever is even capable of arising in the first place. And just as, even granting that conditioned dharmas all equally possess the mark of arising, one still accepts that causes and conditions have differing power with respect to different conditioned dharmas — capable of generating some, incapable of generating others — so too, in just the same way, it should be granted that both conditioned and unconditioned dharmas can equally lack any mark of arising at all, while causes and conditions still differ in their power with respect to these two kinds of dharma, having a genuine function of generating with respect to one, and no such function whatsoever with respect to the other.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection tries to force an absurd consequence out of the reductive account: if arising is nothing more than "formerly absent, now existing," and this needs no separate real mark to explain it, what stops the very same reasoning from applying to the unconditioned too? The answer is definitional rather than causal: the unconditioned simply is, by its very nature, always existent — there is no "formerly absent" phase for it to transition out of in the first place, so the whole description fails to apply to it from the start, with no further mark needed to explain the difference. The closing analogy reinforces the point economically: this tradition already accepts, without controversy, that causes and conditions have selective power — generating some conditioned dharmas and not others — so extending that same selectivity one step further, to explain why conditioned dharmas alone (and never the unconditioned) undergo this transition at all, requires nothing beyond what is already conceded.

50. The Vaibhāṣika's Final Word: Farming Despite the Deer

After the most sustained and substantively damaging critique in the entire fascicle, the Vaibhāṣika masters offer their closing verdict — not a rebuttal, but a candid statement of why a tradition perseveres with its own position regardless.

Chinese Source

毘婆沙師說生等相別有實物，其理應成。所以者何？豈容多有設難者故便棄所宗？非恐有鹿而不種麥，懼多蠅附不食美團。故於過難應懃通釋，於本宗義應順修行。如是已辯諸有為相。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Vaibhāṣika masters say: the position that arising and the rest are separate, real entities should indeed be established. Why is this? How could one simply abandon one's own school's fundamental tenet merely because many objections happen to have been raised against it? One does not refrain from planting wheat for fear that deer might come and eat it, nor refuse to eat a delicious rice-cake for fear that many flies might gather upon it. Therefore, with respect to these difficult objections, one should diligently work through and resolve them, while continuing to practice in accordance with one's own school's fundamental tenets. The characteristics of the conditioned have now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

After roughly twenty sections of genuinely sustained, often quite damaging argument — the staggered-function solution's internal contradictions, the redundancy of decay and cessation, decay's conceptual incoherence when applied to a single dharma, the purgative-medicine reductio, and finally the observation that "arising" cannot even function alone without the very causes and conditions that seem to make it redundant — the Vaibhāṣika's closing statement is remarkable for what it does not attempt: it does not claim to have refuted any of these objections. Instead, it offers something closer to a statement of principle about how one ought to hold a tradition at all: difficulties are met by continued diligent effort to work through them, not by abandoning the position the moment an objection proves hard to answer.

The two folk images are doing real persuasive work, and are worth pausing on. A farmer does not stop planting wheat merely because deer might eat some of it — the possibility of loss does not justify abandoning the entire enterprise of farming. And no one refuses a delicious rice-cake merely because flies might land on it — an imperfection at the margins does not spoil the whole. Applied to doctrine, the implied argument is that unresolved philosophical difficulties are simply the ordinary cost of holding any substantive position at all, not a reason to concede the field to a rival account merely because that rival's objections have, in this particular stretch, proven unusually strong. This is a franker and more elaborate version of the simple "because it is our own school's tenet" formula seen closing debates elsewhere in this fascicle and in fascicle 4 — and, arguably, the most honest moment yet in how this treatise handles a genuinely unresolved philosophical difficulty: not by pretending the difficulty has been overcome, but by openly naming persistence itself as the reason for continuing to hold the position.

51. The Final Formations: Name-Body, Phrase-Body, and Letter-Body

The treatise turns to its last three formations dissociated from mind — the building blocks of language itself, proposed as genuinely real dharmas distinct from mere sound.

Chinese Source

名身等類其義云何？頌曰：名身等所謂，想章字總說。論曰：等者等取句身文身。應知此中名謂作想，如說色聲香味等想。句者謂章，詮義究竟，如說諸行無常等章。或能辯了業用德時相應差別，此章稱句。文者謂字，如說[褒-保+可]阿壹伊等字。豈不此字亦書分名？非為顯書分製造諸字，但為顯諸字製造書分。云何當令雖不聞說而亦得解，故造書分，是故諸字非書分名。云何名等身？謂想等總說。言總說者是合集義，於合集義中說嗢柁南聲故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by "the name-body and its kind"? Verse: "name-body and the rest" refers, in summary, to concept, statement, and syllable. Prose: "and the rest" here takes in, together with name-body, both phrase-body and letter-body as well. It should be understood that, here, "name" means that which produces a concept — as when one speaks of the concept of form, sound, fragrance, flavor, and so on. "Phrase" means a complete statement, one that conveys a meaning through to its conclusion — as when one speaks of the statement "all formations are impermanent." Or, when a statement is able to distinguish and clarify differences in activity, function, quality, and time in their proper association, this statement is specifically called a "phrase." "Letter" means a syllable — as when one speaks of the syllables ka, a, i, ī, and so on. But is this "syllable" not simply another name for a unit of writing? It is not that syllables were devised in order to represent units of writing — rather, units of writing were devised in order to represent syllables. Why is this? So that meaning could be understood even when it is not heard spoken aloud, units of writing were devised for that purpose — hence syllables are not simply another name for units of writing. What is meant by calling these "bodies"? It refers to concept, and the rest, taken together as a collective whole. To speak of them "taken together" carries the sense of a gathered collection, since it is with respect to that same sense of a gathered collection that the word udāna, "a compiled summary," is used.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This opens the treatise's final trio of formations dissociated from mind, and it is a genuinely ambitious claim: that language itself, at every one of its structural levels — the individual syllable, the meaning-bearing word, and the complete statement — corresponds to a real, distinct dharma, not merely to sound considered under a different name. The letter-versus-writing clarification is a small but telling piece of philosophical housekeeping: spoken syllables are treated as basic and prior, with written symbols invented afterward purely as a practical aid, so that meaning could still be conveyed to someone not actually present to hear it spoken — reversing what might seem the more natural assumption that a "letter" is just the name for a written mark.

52. Concrete Examples, and a Sharp Objection: Aren't These Just Sound?

Examples of each of the three linguistic units are given, followed immediately by the most obvious objection of all — if these are fundamentally a matter of speech and sound, how can they possibly belong among the formations dissociated from mind rather than among physical form?

Chinese Source

此中名身者，謂色聲香等。句身者，謂諸行無常、一切法無我、涅槃寂靜等。文身者，謂迦佉伽等。豈不此三語為性故，用聲為體，色自性攝。如何乃說為心不相應行？此三非以語為自性，語是音聲，非唯音聲即令了義。云何令了？謂語發名，名能顯義，乃能令了。非但音聲皆稱為語，要由此故義可了知，如是音聲方稱語故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, "name-body" refers to such things as the concepts of form, sound, and fragrance. "Phrase-body" refers to such statements as "all formations are impermanent," "all dharmas are without self," and "nirvana is peaceful stillness." "Letter-body" refers to such syllables as ka, kha, ga, and so on. But is it not the case that all three of these have speech as their essential nature, taking sound as their substance, and so ought to be subsumed under the aggregate of form's own nature? How, then, can they be classed as formations dissociated from mind at all? These three do not have speech as their essential nature. Speech is merely audible sound, and mere sound alone does not, by itself, convey meaning. What, then, does convey meaning? It is that speech gives rise to a name, and it is this name that is able to reveal a meaning, and thereby brings understanding about. Not every sound whatsoever is called "speech" — sound is called "speech" specifically and only when meaning can be understood through it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection cuts straight to the heart of the matter: language is obviously made of sound, and sound is a form of physical matter already covered by the aggregate of form — so why invent an entirely new category rather than simply filing "name," "phrase," and "letter" under form, where speech-sounds already belong? The reply draws a careful, load-bearing distinction between speech as mere audible sound and speech as meaning-bearing: the raw acoustic event is form, but the further fact that this particular sound manages to convey a specific meaning is not itself explained by the sound's physical nature alone — meaningless noise and meaningful speech can be acoustically identical, differing only in whether a "name" arises from the sound to reveal meaning. That gap between bare sound and meaning is exactly the philosophical space this new category is meant to occupy.

53. Which Sounds Convey Meaning? The Nine Meanings of a Single Word

What actually determines which sounds count as meaning-bearing speech? The answer points to convention — illustrated by a famous case where a single Sanskrit word covers nine entirely unrelated meanings.

Chinese Source

何等音聲令義可了？謂能說者於諸義中已共立為能詮定量，且如古者於九義中共立一瞿聲為能詮定量。故有頌言：方獸地光言，金剛眼天水，於斯九種義，智者立瞿聲。諸有執名能顯義者，亦定應許如是義名，謂共立為能詮定量。若此句義由名能顯，但由音聲顯用已辦，何須橫計別有實名？

Ru-Yi Translation

Which sounds, then, are those that make meaning understood? It is those sounds which speakers have already, by mutual convention, jointly established as a fixed measure for conveying a given meaning. Consider, for instance, how the ancients jointly established the single sound "go" as a fixed measure for conveying nine distinct meanings. Hence it is said in verse: "Direction, beast, earth, light, speech, diamond, eye, heaven, and water — with respect to these nine distinct meanings, the wise have established the sound 'go.'" Those who hold that a name is what reveals meaning must, by the very same logic, also grant that this "meaning-bearing name" is itself just such a jointly-established fixed measure for conveying meaning. If a statement's meaning can already be revealed through name in this conventional sense, and mere sound's own function has already fully accomplished that revealing, what further need is there to needlessly posit some separate, additionally real "name" on top of it?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "go" example is a well-known case from classical Sanskrit grammatical tradition: the single word go can mean, depending entirely on context, a cow, a direction, the earth, a ray of light, speech itself, a diamond, an eye, heaven, or water — nine wildly unrelated meanings sharing one sound, with nothing in the sound itself dictating which meaning is intended. This is offered as vivid proof that meaning-conveyance is a matter of convention, agreed upon jointly by a community of speakers, rather than something inherent in the physical sound.

But the passage then turns this very observation into an objection against the treatise's own position: if meaning-conveyance really is just a matter of conventional agreement layered onto sound, then sound itself — once convention has settled which meaning a given sound conveys — already does the whole job. Positing a further, separate real entity called "name," on top of the conventionally-meaningful sound, starts to look like exactly the kind of superfluous addition this tradition elsewhere criticizes.

54. Generated or Revealed? A Dilemma, and the Problem of Accumulating Sound

The debate turns technical: does speech "generate" a name, or merely "reveal" one already latent? Both options run into trouble, compounded by a deeper problem — momentary sounds cannot accumulate the way a whole word seems to require.

Chinese Source

又未了此名，如何由語發，為由語顯、為由語生？若由語生，語聲性故，聲應一切皆能生名。若謂生名聲有差別，此足顯義，何待別名？若由語顯，語聲性故，聲應一切皆能顯名。若謂顯名，聲有差別，此足顯義，何待別名？又諸念聲不可聚集，亦無一法分分漸生，如何名生可由語發？

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, it remains unclear how this "name" is actually brought forth by speech in the first place — is it revealed by speech, or is it generated by speech? If it is generated by speech, then, since speech has the nature of sound, sound in general should be able to generate a name in every case. If one says instead that it is specifically a difference among sounds that generates the name, then this very difference among sounds is already sufficient to convey the meaning by itself — what need is there, then, to wait for some separate "name" on top of it? If, instead, the name is revealed by speech, then, since speech has the nature of sound, sound in general should be able to reveal a name in every case. If one says instead that it is specifically a difference among sounds that reveals the name, then, again, this very difference among sounds is already sufficient to convey the meaning by itself — what need is there to wait for some separate "name"? Moreover, successive momentary sounds cannot actually accumulate together into a single whole, nor does any single dharma gradually arise piece by piece — so how, then, could a name's arising possibly be brought forth by speech at all?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The dilemma is a clean pincer movement: whichever verb one chooses — "speech generates the name" or "speech reveals the name" — the same problem recurs. If plain, undifferentiated sound could do this job, every sound whatsoever would generate or reveal meaning, which is plainly false. But if it takes a specific difference among sounds to do the job, then that very difference is already doing all the explanatory work needed — nothing is left over for a separate "name" entity to contribute.

The deeper problem is genuinely severe for anyone holding this tradition's doctrine of strict momentariness: a spoken word like "form" is heard as a single, unified whole, but it is actually a rapid sequence of distinct momentary sounds, each one already gone before the next arises — and momentary things, on this system's own principles, cannot pool together or accumulate into a single lasting entity. If a "name" is supposed to be generated by the word as a whole, this creates a serious puzzle: the whole word, as a unified thing capable of generating anything, arguably never actually exists all at once for that generating to happen.

55. A Failed Rescue Attempt, and Extending the Problem to Letters and Phrases

An attempt to solve the accumulation problem by analogy with unmanifest karmic form is tried and rejected — and the same accumulation difficulty is shown to afflict letter-body and phrase-body just as severely as it afflicts name-body.

Chinese Source

云何待過去諸表剎那？最後表剎那能生無表。若爾，最後位聲乃生名，但聞最後聲應能了義。若作是執：語能生文、文復生名、名方顯義。此中過難應同前說，以諸念文不可集故。語顯名過，應例如生。又異語文，諸明慧者注心思擇莫辯其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why should it need to wait upon a whole series of past momentary manifest acts at all? Consider: it is only the very last momentary manifest act that is able to generate the unmanifest. If that same principle applied here, then it would be only the very last moment of sound that generates the name — in which case, simply hearing that last sound alone ought to be sufficient to understand the meaning, without needing to have heard any of the sounds before it. Suppose, instead, one holds this position: speech generates the letter, the letter in turn generates the name, and only then does the name reveal the meaning. This position faces exactly the same difficulty already raised above, since momentary letters, too, cannot actually accumulate together into a single whole. And the same fault just raised against speech "generating" a name applies equally, by the same reasoning, against speech "revealing" one. Moreover, when it comes to distinguishing speech from letter as genuinely separate things, even the most learned and discerning minds, however carefully they apply themselves, are unable to make out any real distinguishing characteristic between the two at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rescue attempt tries to borrow a solution already accepted elsewhere in this system: unmanifest karmic form (a lasting moral residue left behind by an action) is generated specifically by the very last moment of the visible, manifest act that produces it, not by the whole act accumulating. Applying the same principle here would mean only the final sound of a spoken word generates its name — but this has an obviously false consequence: if that were true, hearing only the last sound of a word, with none of the sounds before it, should already be enough to understand its meaning, which plainly is not how spoken language actually works for a listener.

Pushing the "name" back one further step — speech generates letters, letters generate the name — does not help either, since letters are themselves just as momentary and just as unable to accumulate as the sounds that were the original problem; the difficulty has simply been relocated one level down, not solved. The closing observation is almost wry: this whole debate has been implicitly assuming that "speech" (the raw sound) and "letter" (the syllable) are two clearly distinguishable things in the first place — yet even careful, expert reflection cannot actually locate any real difference between them at all, undermining the multi-step generation account from yet another angle.

56. Timing Mismatches, the Parents-Naming-Children Problem, and the Unconditioned's Missing Name

Three further objections press the case against treating "name" as a real entity co-arising with its meaning — including a delightfully down-to-earth counter-example from ordinary family life.

Chinese Source

又文由語若顯若生，准語於名皆不應理。又若有執：名如生等與義俱生，現在世名目去來義不應得有。又父母等隨意所欲立子等名，云何可言名如生等與義俱起？又無為法應無有名，無生義故而不應許。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, whether letter is said to be revealed by speech or generated by speech, by the very same reasoning already applied to name, neither position can be shown to hold. Furthermore, if one holds that a name arises together with its meaning, the way arising and the other characteristics are held to co-arise with their own dharma, then a name existing in the present should be unable to refer to a meaning belonging to the past or the future. Moreover, parents and others freely choose whatever name they wish to give their children — how, then, could one possibly say that a name arises together with its meaning, the way arising and the rest are said to co-arise? Moreover, the unconditioned ought, by this reasoning, to have no name at all, since it has no meaning of "arising" — yet this consequence should not be accepted.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The timing objection is subtle but real: if a name genuinely arose together with its meaning, as a simultaneous pair, then a name spoken right now, in the present, should only ever be capable of naming something also present right now — yet ordinary language plainly names things that happened yesterday or things anticipated tomorrow without any difficulty at all. The parents-naming-children example is refreshingly concrete after all this abstraction: a name is manifestly chosen freely, by convention and personal preference, often before a child's characteristics are even known — nothing about it "co-arises" with any meaning in the tight, simultaneous sense the theory would require. And the unconditioned poses one final embarrassment: since "arising" never applies to it at all, a theory that ties name strictly to co-arising-with-meaning would predict, absurdly, that words like "space" or "cessation" cannot be genuine names — plainly false, since these are used and understood without any trouble whatsoever.

57. The Resolution: What the Buddha's Verses Actually Meant, and the Vaibhāṣika's Final Verdict

A key scriptural reference is clarified in the reductive spirit, before the Vaibhāṣika masters step in with their own closing affirmation — genuinely real entities are needed here, they insist, precisely because not everything yields to mere reflection.

Chinese Source

然世尊說頌依於名及文生者，此於諸義共立分量。聲即是名，此名安布差別為頌。由如是義，說頌依名。此頌是名安布差別，執有實物不應正理，如樹等行及心次第。或唯應執別有文體，即總集此為名等身。更執有餘便為無用。毘婆沙師說有別物為名等身，心不相應行蘊所攝。實而非假。所以者何？非一切法皆是尋思所能了故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the World-Honored One's own statement that a verse arises dependent upon name and letter — this simply refers to how, with respect to various meanings, a fixed conventional measure has been jointly established. Sound itself is simply what is called "name"; and it is the particular arrangement of these names that constitutes a verse. It is in this sense that a verse is said to arise "dependent upon name." Since a verse, in this sense, is simply a particular arrangement of names, holding that this arrangement is itself some further, genuinely separate real entity would not accord with reason — no more than one would hold this of a row of trees, or of a mind's own sequence of moments. At most, one might hold that letter alone has a separate real essence of its own, with name-body and the rest simply being this same essence gathered together and arranged collectively. To insist on positing anything further beyond this would serve no purpose at all. The Vaibhāṣika masters, however, say: there are indeed separate real entities constituting name-body and the rest, subsumed under the aggregate of formations dissociated from mind — genuinely real, not merely provisional. Why is this? Because not every dharma whatsoever is something that mere reflection and reasoning alone are capable of fully grasping.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reductive account closes with a memorable pair of images: a verse's apparent unity is compared to a row of trees, or to a mind's own moment-by-moment succession — in both cases, something that looks and functions like a single continuous thing is, on analysis, simply many genuinely separate items arranged in a recognizable pattern, with no further "arrangement-entity" needed over and above the items themselves and their order.

The Vaibhāṣika's closing reply is brief but pointed, and genuinely different in character from the closing formula seen elsewhere in this fascicle: rather than simply invoking school loyalty, it offers a substantive epistemic claim — that "not every dharma is something mere reflection and reasoning alone are capable of fully grasping." The implicit charge is that the Sautrāntika's entire reductive strategy, however clever, has been trying to argue its way to a conclusion about what is real using reasoning alone, when this tradition holds that some genuinely real, subtle dharmas can only be established through the Buddha's own teaching, not through unaided philosophical analysis. With this, the treatise's position is stated, and the debate over name-body, phrase-body, and letter-body reaches its close.

58. Classifying Name-Body and the Rest: Realm, Sentience, and Moral Quality

Having settled their reality, the treatise runs name-body, phrase-body, and letter-body through the same classificatory questions applied to every other dharma throughout this fascicle.

Chinese Source

此名身等何界所繫？為是有情數、為非有情數？為是異熟生、為是所長養、為是等流性？為善、為不善、為無記？此皆應辯。頌曰：欲色有情攝，等流無記性。論曰：此名身等唯是欲色二界所繫。有說：亦通無色界繫，然不可說。又名身等有情數攝，能說者成非所顯義。又名身等唯是等流，又唯無覆無記性攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

To which realm are name-body and the rest bound? Are they sentient-being-based, or not sentient-being-based? Are they karmic-ripening results, are they nourished-and-grown, or are they of the nature of equal-outflow? Are they wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral? All of these questions should be addressed. Verse: they are bound to the desire and form realms, are sentient-being-based, and are of the nature of equal-outflow and neutral. Prose: name-body and the rest are bound only to the two realms, desire and form. There is another view, that they extend also to the formless realm — but this cannot actually be maintained. Name-body and the rest are, moreover, sentient-being-based, since it is the speaker who produces them that accounts for their status, not the meaning that is merely revealed by them. Name-body and the rest are, further, solely of the nature of equal-outflow, and are subsumed solely under the undefiled-neutral nature.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The classification is compact but precise. Confining name, phrase, and letter to the desire and form realms makes sense once one recalls that these are fundamentally products of speech — and the formless realm, lacking any physical basis for speech at all, simply has no place for them. The reasoning behind "sentient-being-based" is worth noting: it is not that a name's meaning must refer to something sentient (names plainly can refer to rocks, rivers, or anything else), but that the name itself, as a produced dharma, is credited to the sentient speaker who produces it — a nice distinction between what makes something count as "sentient-being-based" and what it happens to be about.

59. A Closing Cross-Reference: Every Formation Dissociated from Mind, Compared at Once

Fascicle 5's final section gathers class-commonality, possession, the four characteristics, and non-possession into one compressed comparative summary — bringing the entire catalog of formations dissociated from mind to its close.

Chinese Source

如上所說，餘不相應所未說義今當略辯。頌曰：同分亦如是，并無色異熟，得相通三類，非得定等流。論曰：亦如是言，為顯同分如名身等，通於欲色有情等流無覆無記。并無色言，顯非唯欲色。言并異熟，顯非唯等流。是界通三，類通二義。得及諸相類并通三，謂有剎那等流異熟，非得二定唯是等流。唯言為明非異熟等。已說如是所未說義。無想、命根如前已辯。何緣不說得等唯是有情數攝？已說有情所成等故。何緣不說相通有情非有情數？已說一切有為俱故。餘所未說，隨應准知。

Ru-Yi Translation

As stated above, there remain some further points, not yet addressed, regarding the other formations dissociated from mind — these should now be briefly clarified. Verse: class-commonality is likewise so, together with the formless realm and karmic-ripening as well; possession and the characteristics extend across all three categories; non-possession is fixed as solely of the nature of equal-outflow. Prose: the phrase "likewise" indicates that class-commonality, like name-body and the rest, extends across the desire and form realms, is sentient-being-based, and is of the equal-outflow and undefiled-neutral nature. The phrase "together with the formless realm" indicates that it is not confined to the desire and form realms alone. The phrase "and karmic-ripening as well" indicates that it is not confined to the equal-outflow nature alone. Thus its scope by realm extends across all three, and its scope by category extends across two kinds of nature. Possession, and the characteristics as a category, likewise extend across all three: that is, each may be momentary, of the equal-outflow nature, or a karmic-ripening result. Non-possession, and the two attainments, are solely of the equal-outflow nature. The word "solely" makes this clear, as distinct from being a karmic-ripening result and so on. With this, these further, previously unaddressed points have now been explained. Non-percipience and the life faculty have already been explained above, as previously stated. Why is it not separately stated that possession and the rest are solely sentient-being-based? Because it has already been stated that they belong to what a sentient being has accomplished, and so on. Why is it not separately stated that the characteristics extend to both sentient and non-sentient dharmas alike? Because it has already been stated that they accompany all conditioned dharmas without exception. Whatever else has not been addressed here should be understood accordingly, each in its own corresponding manner.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This closing section functions as a tidy cross-reference table for the whole catalog of formations dissociated from mind this fascicle has worked through: class-commonality turns out to be the broadest of the group, extending even to the formless realm and admitting karmic-ripening status, unlike name-body and its companions, which stay confined to the desire and form realms and to the equal-outflow nature alone. Possession and the four characteristics turn out to be broader still, spanning all three moral-causal categories at once — momentary, equal-outflow, or karmic-ripening, depending on the specific case — reflecting how these two dharmas attach themselves to every kind of conditioned dharma without exception, inheriting something of whatever they accompany. Non-possession and the two mindless attainments, by contrast, are pinned down to the single equal-outflow category alone, with no exceptions permitted.

Fascicle 5 Complete

This closes fascicle 5 of Ru-Yi's translation, completing the third part of Chapter 2 (根品第二之三, the Indriya-nirdeśa) and, with it, the treatise's full catalog of the formations dissociated from mind. The fascicle opened with class-commonality (同分), the proposed real dharma explaining why members of a shared class are recognizably alike, argued through a fourfold case on death and rebirth and a pointed comparison to the rival Vaiśeṣika school. It then turned to non-percipience and the two mindless attainments — non-percipience and cessation — working through why noble ones alone can enter cessation, whether the Buddha needed effortful practice to attain it, the bodhisattva's contested thirty-four-moment path to enlightenment, and a striking "mutual seed" solution to how mind can resume after total absence, a genuine precursor to later Yogācāra thought. The life faculty followed, defined through the regress puzzle of what sustains life itself and settled, unusually, by a substantively argued rather than merely asserted verdict. The fascicle's center of gravity was the four characteristics of the conditioned — arising, abiding, decay, and cessation — subjected to one of this treatise's most sustained internal critiques, a series of genuinely damaging objections met, in the end, not by refutation but by the Vaibhāṣika's candid admission that a tradition perseveres through difficulty the way a farmer keeps planting wheat despite the deer. The fascicle closed with name-body, phrase-body, and letter-body, asking whether language itself points to real dharmas beyond mere sound, and a final cross-referencing classification tying every formation dissociated from mind together at once. Fascicle 6 turns next to an entirely new topic: the six causes (六因) that ground this tradition's whole theory of causation. Ru-Yi's translation will continue there.
