What Makes Us Recognizably Alike?

This page carries the opening of fascicle 5, continuing Chapter 2 (根品第二之三, the third part of the Indriya-nirdeśa) with the remaining formations dissociated from mind. It opens with class-commonality (同分, nikāya-sabhāga): the treatise's proposed real, shared dharma that accounts for why members of the same class of beings — or the same kind of thing — are recognizably alike.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 14 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 5 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. A New Formation Dissociated from Mind: Class-Commonality

Having closed the long debate over possession, the treatise moves to the next member of its list of formations dissociated from mind — introducing a real, shared entity meant to explain why beings of the same kind are recognizably of that kind.

Chinese Source

如是已辯得非得相。同分者何？頌曰：同分有情等。論曰：有別實物名為同分，謂諸有情展轉類等，本論說此名眾同分。此復二種：一無差別、二有差別。無差別者，謂諸有情有情同分，一切有情各等有故。有差別者，謂諸有情界地趣生種姓、男女近事苾芻學無學等各別同分，一類有情各等有故。復有法同分，謂隨蘊處界。

Ru-Yi Translation

The characteristics of possession and non-possession have now been explained. What is class-commonality? A verse states: class-commonality is [what makes] sentient beings, and so on, [alike]. Prose: there is a genuinely separate real entity called "class-commonality," referring to the mutual resemblance in kind shared among sentient beings; the root treatise names this "the class-commonality of sentient beings." This is further of two kinds: without distinction, and with distinction. "Without distinction" refers to the class-commonality shared simply as sentient beings — since all sentient beings equally possess this, without exception. "With distinction" refers to the separate, distinct class-commonalities shared according to realm, level, destiny, mode of birth, lineage, sex, or standing as a lay follower, a monastic, a trainee, or a non-trainee, and so on — since each particular class of beings equally possesses its own such commonality. There is also a class-commonality of dharmas, referring to commonality according to aggregate, sense field, or element.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Possession and non-possession finally settled, the treatise turns to the next member of its catalog of "formations dissociated from mind" — dharmas that are neither physical form, nor mind, nor mental factors, yet are genuinely real and conditioned in their own right. "Class-commonality" (同分, nikāya-sabhāga) proposes a real entity to explain a very ordinary-seeming fact: that members of the same kind — all humans, say, or all beings in a particular realm — are recognizably of a kind at all, rather than each being simply its own isolated particular with no genuine commonality linking it to any other.

The scheme immediately branches in two directions. The most general class-commonality — simply "being a sentient being" — is shared without exception by every sentient being whatsoever. But class-commonality also nests down to arbitrarily fine-grained levels: realm, cosmological level, destiny (one of the six modes of rebirth), mode of birth, spiritual lineage, sex, and religious standing (lay follower, monastic, trainee, non-trainee) each generate their own distinct, narrower class-commonality, shared only among beings who fall into that same specific category. And the same basic idea, the text notes in passing, applies even to non-sentient dharmas grouped by aggregate, sense field, or element — a commonality of kind is not limited to living beings alone.

2. The Proof: Without a Real Commonality, Recognition Itself Would Be Impossible

Why posit a real entity here at all, rather than simply saying that similar beings are just... similar? The treatise argues that the very possibility of recognizing and naming a shared kind depends on it.

Chinese Source

若無實物無差別相名同分者，展轉差別諸有情中，有情有情等無差別，覺及施設不應得有。如是蘊等等無差別覺及施設，如理應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there were no real entity, without distinction in its characteristic, called "class-commonality," then, among sentient beings who mutually differ from one another in countless ways, the undifferentiated cognition and the undifferentiated designation of them as equally "sentient beings" ought not to be possible at all. The same reasoning should be understood to apply to the undifferentiated cognition and designation of aggregates, and so on, as equally alike.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The argument is compact but characteristic of this tradition's general approach to positing real entities: any two sentient beings differ from each other in an enormous number of ways — appearance, karma, mental state, realm, and so on — and yet everyone reliably and effortlessly recognizes and names both of them "a sentient being," with no hesitation about the category despite the endless particular differences. That reliable, shared recognition, the treatise argues, needs something to track — some genuine point of undifferentiated sameness running through all the differences — and "class-commonality" is proposed as exactly that real, underlying point of sameness. Without it, recognizing any two different things as being "the same kind" of thing would have no genuine basis to latch onto at all.

3. Four Cases: Does Dying and Being Reborn Always Mean Losing One's Class-Commonality?

A natural question about how class-commonality behaves across death and rebirth is answered with one of this treatise's favorite analytical tools: the fourfold case.

Chinese Source

頗有死生不捨不得有情同分？應作四句。第一句者，謂是處死還生是處。第二句者，謂入正性離生位時，捨異生同分，得聖者同分。第三句者，謂是趣死生餘趣等。第四句者，謂除前相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Is there any case of dying and being reborn in which one neither relinquishes nor acquires a sentient being's class-commonality? This should be treated as a fourfold case. The first case: dying in a certain place and being reborn again in that very same place. The second case: at the moment of entering the state of correct assurance apart from birth [that is, entering the path of seeing], one relinquishes the ordinary-being class-commonality and acquires the noble-one class-commonality. The third case: dying in one destiny and being reborn in another destiny, and so on. The fourth case: whatever is excluded from the preceding characteristics.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The question sounds almost paradoxical — surely dying and being reborn always involves some change of class-commonality? — and the fourfold case format resolves it by separating out genuinely distinct scenarios rather than forcing a single yes-or-no answer. The first case shows that ordinary rebirth in the identical circumstance (dying human, reborn human, in the very same place) need not change one's class-commonality at all. The second case shows that a change of class-commonality can happen without any death or rebirth whatsoever — becoming a noble one on the path of seeing swaps "ordinary-being" class-commonality for "noble-one" class-commonality in a single continuum, with no death involved. The third case is the ordinary expectation, dying in one destiny and being reborn into a different one, definitely changing class-commonality along realm or destiny lines. The fourth case, as usual in this fourfold format, simply catches whatever combination the first three did not already cover — such as an ordinary death and rebirth that happens to also coincide with entering the noble path.

4. Objections: Why Not Split It Further, and What Kind of Thing Even Is It?

A battery of objections presses on the theory from several directions at once — including a pointed question about why the treatise does not simply multiply class-commonalities without limit.

Chinese Source

若別有實物名異生同分，何用別立異生性耶？非異人同分，別有人性故。又非世間現見同分，以非色故。亦非覺慧所能了別，無別用故。世雖不了有情同分，而於有情謂無差別，故設有體亦何所用？又何因不許有無情同分？諸穀麥豆、金鐵菴羅半娜娑等，亦有自類互相似故。又諸同分展轉差別，如何於彼更無同分，而起無別覺施設耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is a genuinely separate real entity called "ordinary-being class-commonality," what further need is there to separately establish "ordinary-being-nature" as well? [Reply:] It is not the case that a distinct person's class-commonality [alone] is what constitutes a distinct human nature, since [class-commonality and personhood-nature] are separate matters. Furthermore, class-commonality is not something ordinarily perceived in the world, since it is not a form. Nor is it something wisdom and cognition are able to discern, since it has no distinct function of its own. Given that ordinary people do not perceive any "class-commonality" among sentient beings, yet still regard them as [genuinely, equally] undifferentiated, what use would positing such an entity even serve? Furthermore, why is it not accepted that inanimate things, too, have a class-commonality — grains such as rice and wheat and beans, or metals such as gold and iron, or fruits such as mango and jackfruit, since these likewise resemble their own respective kinds? Furthermore, class-commonalities themselves mutually differ from one another [in countless further ways] — so how is it that no further class-commonality is posited for them, and yet an undifferentiated cognition and designation of them still arises?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Four distinct objections arrive in quick succession. First, an internal consistency challenge: if "ordinary-being class-commonality" is already a real, separate entity, doesn't that make the earlier-established "ordinary-being-nature" (non-possession of the noble path, from fascicle 4) entirely redundant — why does the system need both? Second and third, the same evidentiary tests already used against "possession" are turned against class-commonality: it has no perceivable form, and performs no detectable function beyond simply being posited to explain recognition after the fact — precisely the circular move the treatise itself has criticized elsewhere. Fourth is perhaps the sharpest: if resemblance among grains, metals, and fruits does not require positing a real "class-commonality" entity to explain (since these plainly get by with mere resemblance, no separate entity invoked), why should resemblance among sentient beings require one? And in a final regress-style jab: if class-commonalities themselves differ from each other, and are nevertheless still recognized as "commonalities" without needing their own further meta-commonality, doesn't that show ordinary resemblance never actually required a separate real entity to explain recognition in the first place?

5. The Vaiśeṣika Comparison, and a Scriptural Defense — Class-Commonality Closes

The debate closes with an unexpected cross-school comparison, a careful clarification of what the defense is not claiming, and a final scriptural appeal.

Chinese Source

又應顯成勝論所執，彼宗執有總同句義，於一切法總同言智由此發生。彼復執有同異句義，於異品類同異言智由此發生。毘婆沙師作如是說：彼執與此義類不同，以說一物於多轉故。又縱於彼若顯不顯，然此同分必有實物，契經說故。如世尊言：若還來此得人同分，乃至廣說。雖有是說，而不說言別有實物名為同分。若爾，所說同分是何？即如是類諸行生時，於中假立人同分等，如諸穀麥豆等同分。此非善說，違我宗故。已辯同分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, this would end up vindicating exactly what the Vaiśeṣika school holds: that school maintains that there is a category called "universal sameness" (samānya), by which the cognition and language of total sameness across all dharmas arises; and it further maintains a category called "sameness-and-difference" (sāmānya-viśeṣa), by which the cognition and language of sameness and difference among distinct kinds of things arises. The Vaibhāṣika masters reply as follows: that school's position is not the same in kind as ours, since they hold that a single such entity operates across many separate things at once [whereas our "class-commonality," though shared in kind, is a distinct instance within each individual continuum]. Furthermore, whether or not this point is made explicit in their case, this class-commonality [of ours] must still be a real entity, because a sutra says so — as the World-Honored One states: "If one comes back here and attains a human being's class-commonality," and so on, as stated at length. Although this is said, the sutra does not go so far as to say there is a genuinely separate real entity called "class-commonality." If that is so, then what is this "class-commonality" that is spoken of? It is simply that, when formations of this general kind arise, "a human being's class-commonality," and so on, is provisionally established among them — just like the "class-commonality" spoken of among grains such as rice, wheat, and beans. This is not a well-reasoned position, since it contradicts our own school's tenet. Class-commonality has now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The comparison to the rival Vaiśeṣika school is a genuinely striking move, since Vaiśeṣika is a non-Buddhist system, and being told one's own theory resembles it is not a compliment in this context — Vaiśeṣika's "universal" categories (sāmānya and sāmānya-viśeṣa) were a standing target of Buddhist critique for positing single, literally shared entities existing identically "in" many different particulars at once. The Vaibhāṣika reply draws a careful line: "class-commonality," on this system's own account, is not one single entity somehow spread across every member of a class — each being has its own distinct instance of class-commonality, merely resembling the others' instances in kind, rather than literally being the same numerical entity shared by all. This distinction between "a shared kind" and "a single shared thing" is subtle but load-bearing for keeping the Buddhist position clear of the non-Buddhist one it otherwise superficially resembles.

The section then closes on a scriptural citation defended in a now-familiar pattern — a sutra using "class-commonality" language is invoked as proof, but a rival immediately points out that ordinary usage in that sutra passage does not obviously require any separate real entity at all, offering the same kind of reductive, merely-provisional account already applied to grains and beans. The treatise dismisses this alternative in the curtest terms yet seen in this whole extended debate: not because it is shown to be false, but simply because "it contradicts our own school's tenet" — an unusually blunt admission, closing out class-commonality on a note of asserted orthodoxy rather than settled argument, structurally echoing how the possession debate itself closed back in fascicle 4.

6. A New Formation: Non-Percipience

The treatise moves to the next formation dissociated from mind — a real dharma that brings about a startling cosmological fact: an entire heaven of beings whose mind and mental factors simply do not operate for an immense span of time.

Chinese Source

無想者何？頌曰：無想無想中，心心所法滅，異熟居廣果。論曰：若生無想有情天中，有法能令心心所滅，名為無想。是實有物，能遮未來心心所法令暫不起，如堰江河。此法一向是異熟果。誰之異熟？謂無想定。無想有情居在何處？居在廣果，謂廣果天中有高勝處，如中間靜慮，名無想天。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is non-percipience? Verse: non-percipience, among non-percipient beings, is the cessation of mind and mental factors; as a karmic-ripening result, it dwells in the Vast Fruit heaven. Prose: if one is born among the non-percipient sentient beings in the heavens, there is a dharma able to make mind and mental factors cease — this is called "non-percipience." It is a genuinely real entity, able to obstruct future mind and mental factors so that they temporarily do not arise, just as a dike blocks a river. This dharma is exclusively a karmic-ripening result. Whose ripening is it? It is the ripening of the attainment of non-percipience. Where do non-percipient beings dwell? They dwell in the Vast Fruit heaven (Bṛhatphala) — that is, within the Vast Fruit heaven there is an elevated, superior region, comparable to the intermediate dhyāna, named the Heaven of Non-Percipient Beings.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Non-percipience" (無想, āsaṃjñika) names a genuinely striking cosmological claim: an entire class of heavenly beings, born into a high sub-region of the Vast Fruit heaven, whose mind and mental factors are simply blocked from operating — not merely quiet or subtle, but actually absent — for an immense span of time, the karmic result of having cultivated a specific meditative attainment (introduced two sections from now) that aims at exactly this. The dike-and-river image is doing real conceptual work: non-percipience does not destroy mind, it dams it, holding back what would otherwise naturally flow.

The location detail matters too: this is not a separate heaven in its own right, but a special elevated precinct within the Vast Fruit heaven, comparable to how the "intermediate dhyāna" is a special elevated sub-level sitting between the first and second dhyānas rather than a fifth dhyāna of its own.

7. Are Non-Percipient Beings Truly Without Perception the Whole Time?

A natural objection is addressed: if perception is entirely absent, how do these beings ever leave that state at all? And a striking consequence follows for where they are reborn next.

Chinese Source

彼為恒無想、為亦有想耶？生死位中多時有想。言無想者，由彼有情中間長時想不起故。如契經說：彼諸有情由想起故從彼處沒，然彼有情如久睡覺還起於想，從彼沒已必生欲界非餘處所，先修定行勢力盡故、於彼不能更修定故，如箭射空力盡便墮。若諸有情應生彼處，必有欲界順後受業。如應生彼北俱盧洲，必定應有生天之業。已辯無想。

Ru-Yi Translation

Are these beings always without perception, or do they also, at times, have perception? At the moments of being born there and of dying from there, they do have perception, for an extended time. The reason they are called "without perception" is that, in the long interval between these two moments, perception does not arise in them at all. As a sutra states: those sentient beings, because perception arises again, pass away from that place; and yet those sentient beings, like someone waking from a long sleep, have perception arise once more, and, having passed away from that place, they must necessarily be reborn in the desire realm and nowhere else — because the power of the meditative practice they had previously cultivated is now exhausted, and they are no longer able, while there, to cultivate that same attainment again, just as an arrow shot into the sky falls back down once its force is spent. If a being is destined to be reborn in that place, that being must already possess karma bringing about a delayed result within the desire realm — just as a being destined to be reborn in Uttarakuru must necessarily already possess the karma for that particular birth. Non-percipience has now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection is a fair one — if perception never arises at all, how would anyone ever know these beings exist, let alone that they eventually leave that state? The answer draws a precise boundary: perception is genuinely present at the very moment of birth into that heaven and at the very moment of death from it, with only the long interior stretch of the lifespan actually perception-free. The waking-from-a-long-sleep image captures this well: dying out of the non-percipient state is not a gradual transition but an abrupt return, the mind simply resuming where it had been held in abeyance.

The consequence for the very next rebirth is exact: since the specific meditative power that produced this attainment is now entirely spent — like a spent arrow that can only fall — such a being cannot simply re-cultivate the same attainment on the way out, and so must be reborn specifically in the desire realm, nowhere else, matching whatever desire-realm karma was already waiting to ripen. The closing comparison to Uttarakuru — elsewhere in this treatise the standard example of a rebirth-destiny with entirely fixed, guaranteed karmic prerequisites, since beings there are said to live out a full, undisturbed lifespan with no premature death possible — reinforces the same point: a highly specific rebirth destiny of this kind is never a matter of chance, but always requires the matching karma to already be firmly in place in advance.

8. The Two Attainments: What Is the Attainment of Non-Percipience?

Non-percipience itself, as a karmic-ripening result, is now distinguished from the cultivated attainment that produces it — the first of two closely related "mindless" attainments the treatise will examine.

Chinese Source

二定者何？謂無想定及滅盡定。初無想定其相云何？頌曰：如是無想定，後靜慮求脫，善唯順生受，非聖得一世。論曰：如前所說有法能令心心所滅名為無想，如是復有別法能令心心所滅名無想定。無想者定名無想定，或定無想名無想定。說如是聲，唯顯此定滅心心所與無想同。此在何地？謂後靜慮，即在第四靜慮非餘。修無想定為何所求？謂求解脫。彼執無想是真解脫，為求證彼修無想定。前說無想是異熟故，無記性攝，不說自成。今無想定一向是善，此是善故，能招無想有情天中五蘊異熟。既是善性，為順何受？唯順生受，非順現後及不定受。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the two attainments? They are the attainment of non-percipience and the attainment of cessation. What is the characteristic of the first, the attainment of non-percipience? Verse: this attainment of non-percipience, cultivated within the last dhyāna, is sought as liberation; being wholesome, it conduces only to a result in one's very next birth; it is not attained by noble ones, and, once attained, is attained only for a single lifetime. Prose: as stated above, there is a dharma able to make mind and mental factors cease, called "non-percipience"; likewise, there is a further, separate dharma able to make mind and mental factors cease, called "the attainment of non-percipience." Read one way, this names the attainment that is non-percipience; read another way, it names the attainment that produces non-percipience — either reading only expresses that this attainment's cessation of mind and mental factors is the very same cessation as non-percipience itself. On which level does this occur? On the last dhyāna — that is, specifically the fourth dhyāna, and nowhere else. What does one seek in cultivating the attainment of non-percipience? One seeks liberation. Those who cultivate it hold that non-percipience is genuine liberation, and cultivate this attainment in order to realize it. It was said earlier that non-percipience itself, being a karmic-ripening result, is subsumed under the undefiled-neutral nature — this has already been established, and needs no further statement here. But the attainment of non-percipience itself is, without exception, wholesome; and, being wholesome, it is able to bring about the karmic-ripening result of the five aggregates among the non-percipient beings in that heaven. Given that it is wholesome in nature, which kind of karmic result does it conduce to? It conduces only to a result in one's very next birth — never to a result in the present life, nor a later life beyond the next, nor an indeterminate result.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction is exact and important: "non-percipience" itself (from the previous two sections) is the karmic-ripening result — always undefiled-neutral, simply the passive unfolding of past karma — while "the attainment of non-percipience" is the cultivated cause that produces it, and is, by contrast, wholesome through and through. The two share a name precisely because the attainment's defining feature (stopping mind and mental factors) is identical in kind to the resulting state it produces, even though one is a cause and the other its effect, and their moral qualities differ completely.

Crucially, this attainment is practiced by those who sincerely — if, from this treatise's standpoint, mistakenly — believe that the mere absence of mental activity constitutes genuine liberation. Because it is wholesome karma with a single, specific ripening target (rebirth as a non-percipient being in the very next life), its effect is unusually tightly bound: it cannot ripen in the present life, nor be deferred to some later life, nor remain open-ended, the way many other kinds of karma can.

9. Why Noble Ones Never Cultivate It, and Why It Must Be Attained Fresh Each Time

Two further features round out the picture: this attainment is fundamentally incompatible with the noble path, and, being a mindless state, it can never be "carried forward" the way an ordinary dhyāna can.

Chinese Source

若起此定後雖退失，傳說現身必還能起，當生無想有情天中，故得此定必不能入正性離生。又許此定唯異生得非諸聖者，以諸聖者於無想定如見深坑不樂入故。要執無想為真解脫，起出離想而修此定；一切聖者不執有漏為真解脫及真出離故，於此定必不修行。若諸聖者修得第四靜慮定時，為如靜慮亦得去來無想定不？餘亦不得。所以者何？彼雖曾習，以無心故，要大加行方便修得，故初得時唯得一世，謂得現在，如初受得別解脫戒。得此定已，第二念等乃至未捨亦成過去，以無心故，無未來修。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once this attainment has arisen, even if it is later lost, tradition holds that, within that very same life, it must arise again, and one will then be reborn among the non-percipient beings in that heaven. For this reason, once one has attained this attainment, one is definitely unable to enter the state of correct assurance apart from birth — that is, to enter the path of seeing. It is also accepted that only ordinary beings attain this attainment, never noble ones, since noble ones regard the attainment of non-percipience the way one regards gazing into a deep pit, taking no pleasure in entering it. Only someone who holds non-percipience to be genuine liberation, giving rise to the perception of renunciation on that basis, cultivates this attainment; since no noble one holds any contaminated state to be genuine liberation or genuine renunciation, noble ones definitely never practice this attainment. When noble ones cultivate and attain the fourth dhyāna, do they, the way they attain the ordinary dhyāna's past and future modes of possession, also attain the past and future modes of the attainment of non-percipience? They attain neither of those either. Why is this? Although they may have practiced this attainment previously, before becoming noble ones, because it is a mindless state, it must be cultivated and attained afresh each time, through great effortful practice; hence, at the very first moment of attaining it, one attains only that single occasion — that is, one attains only the present instance — just as when one first receives the prātimokṣa precepts. Once one has attained this attainment, its second moment, and so on, up until it is relinquished, likewise only ever become past instances; because it is a mindless state, there is no cultivating a future instance of it in advance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This attainment turns out to be a genuine dead end, spiritually speaking, and the text is unusually vivid about it: a noble one regards it "the way one regards gazing into a deep pit" — recognizing exactly what it offers (a total cessation of mental activity) and wanting no part of it, since mistaking that cessation for real liberation is precisely the error the noble path exists to correct. Once cultivated even once, it locks a being into eventual rebirth as a non-percipient being and permanently forecloses entering the path of seeing in that same continuum — a striking asymmetry with ordinary meditative attainments, which noble ones cultivate freely.

The final point is a technical consequence of the state being genuinely mindless: because possession, as fascicle 4 established at length, can in principle span the three times, but this attainment involves no mental continuity to sustain that span, it can never be "banked" the way an ordinary dhyāna can be — each attainment of it is a fresh, present-only event, comparable to how first receiving monastic vows is itself always a present, one-time event rather than something drawn on in advance from the future.

10. The Attainment of Cessation: A More Advanced, Distinct Attainment

The second of the two mindless attainments is introduced through careful contrast with the first — sharing the same basic mechanism, but differing in motivation, location, and the range of its karmic result.

Chinese Source

次滅盡定其相云何？頌曰：滅盡定亦然，為靜住有頂，善二受不定，聖由加行得，成佛得非前，三十四念故。論曰：如無想定，滅定亦然。此亦然聲，為例何義？例無想定心心所滅。如說復有別法能令心心所滅名無想定，如是復有別法能令心心所滅名滅盡定。如是二定差別相者，前無想定為求解脫，以出離想作意為先；此滅盡定為求靜住，以止息想作意為先。前無想定在後靜慮；此滅盡定唯在有頂，即是非想非非想處。此同前定，性唯是善非無記染，善等起故。前無想定唯順生受；此滅盡定通順生後及不定受，謂約異熟有順生受、或順後受、或不定受、或全不受。謂若於下得般涅槃，此定所招何地幾蘊？唯招有頂四蘊異熟。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, next, is the characteristic of the attainment of cessation? Verse: the attainment of cessation is likewise so; it is sought for the sake of dwelling in stillness, at the Peak of Existence; it is wholesome, and conduces to two kinds of result, or to an indeterminate one; it is attained by noble ones, through effortful practice; the Buddha attained it not by this means, on account of the thirty-four moments. Prose: as with the attainment of non-percipience, so too with the attainment of cessation. What does this word "likewise" illustrate? It illustrates that, like the attainment of non-percipience, this too is the cessation of mind and mental factors. Just as there is said to be a separate dharma able to make mind and mental factors cease, called "the attainment of non-percipience," so too there is a further, separate dharma able to make mind and mental factors cease, called "the attainment of cessation." As for the distinguishing characteristics of these two attainments: the former, the attainment of non-percipience, is sought for the sake of liberation, preceded by the attention of renunciation; this latter, the attainment of cessation, is sought for the sake of dwelling in stillness, preceded by the attention of stilling perception. The former occurs within the last dhyāna; this latter occurs only at the Peak of Existence, that is, the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception. This attainment is, like the former, wholesome in nature alone, never undefiled-neutral or defiled, since it arises together with wholesome states. The former conduces only to a result in the very next birth; this latter extends to a result in the very next birth, a later birth, or an indeterminate result — that is, with respect to its karmic ripening, it may conduce to a result in the very next birth, or a later birth, or an indeterminate result, or, indeed, to no result being received at all. That is: if one attains final nirvana at a lower level before the ripening occurs, what level, and how many aggregates, does this attainment bring about as its result? It brings about only the karmic-ripening result of the four aggregates at the Peak of Existence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two attainments share their basic mechanism — both stop mind and mental factors outright — but diverge in almost every other respect. Motivation: non-percipience is sought by someone who mistakes it for liberation itself; cessation is sought, more modestly, simply for the sake of a still dwelling, with no such metaphysical overreach. Location: non-percipience tops out at the fourth dhyāna, while cessation reaches all the way to the Peak of Existence, the very summit of conditioned existence. And karmic range: non-percipience is tightly bound to a single specific next-life result, while cessation's wholesome karma can ripen more flexibly — in the next life, a later life, an indeterminate time, or, for someone who reaches final nirvana first, not at all.

11. Who Attains Cessation, and Why It Cannot Be "Banked" Either

Cessation's attainer profile is the mirror image of non-percipience's — noble ones only, never ordinary beings — for reasons that reveal exactly what separates the two attainments philosophically.

Chinese Source

前無想定唯異生得；此滅盡定唯聖者得，非異生能起，怖畏斷滅故、唯聖道力所能起故、現法涅槃勝解入故。此亦如前，非離染得。由何而得？由加行得，要由加行方證得故。又初得時唯得現在，不得過去不修未來，要由心力方能修故。第二念等乃至未捨亦成過去。

Ru-Yi Translation

The former, the attainment of non-percipience, is attained only by ordinary beings; this latter, the attainment of cessation, is attained only by noble ones — ordinary beings are unable to bring it about, since they fear its apparent cessation and extinction, since it can only be brought about through the power of the noble path, and since one enters it through a resolute understanding of nirvāṇa realized within this very life. This attainment, too, like the former, is not attained through mere freedom from defilement alone. By what means, then, is it attained? It is attained through effortful practice, since it can only be realized by means of effortful practice. Moreover, at the moment of its first attainment, one attains only the present instance — one does not thereby attain any past instance, nor does one cultivate any future instance — since it can only be cultivated through the power of a functioning mind. Its second moment, and so on, up until it is relinquished, likewise only ever become past instances.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reversal of who attains each state is exact and telling: ordinary beings, mistaking cessation of mind for liberation, are drawn precisely to non-percipience; but the same apparent cessation terrifies an ordinary being when framed honestly as cessation, which is why only a noble one — someone who has already resolved, through direct understanding, that this temporary halt is not annihilation but a skillful stilling grounded in genuine insight into nirvāṇa — is able to enter it at all. Both attainments share the same technical limitation, though: being genuinely mindless states, neither can be attained in advance or sustained into the future the way an ordinary meditative state can; each occasion of entering either one is a fresh present event, gone the moment it becomes past.

12. Did the Buddha Need Effortful Practice? And a Puzzle About "Liberation in Both Respects"

If effortful practice is required to attain cessation, a natural question arises about the Buddha himself — followed by a genuinely sharp puzzle about what it even means to say he achieved the highest form of liberation.

Chinese Source

世尊亦以加行得耶？不爾。云何？成佛時得，謂佛世尊盡智時得，佛無一德由加行得，暫起欲樂現在前時，一切圓德隨樂而起，故佛眾德皆離染得。世尊曾未起滅盡定，得盡智時云何得成俱分解脫？於起滅定得自在故，如已起者成俱解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Does the World-Honored One likewise attain this through effortful practice? No. How, then? He attained it at the very moment of becoming a Buddha — that is, the World-Honored Buddha attained it at the moment of the knowledge of extinction. Not a single one of the Buddha's qualities is attained through effortful practice; the moment he simply wishes to bring any quality into present manifestation, every perfected quality arises in accordance with that wish. Therefore, all of the Buddha's qualities are attained simply through freedom from defilement, without effortful practice. If the World-Honored One had never actually brought forth the attainment of cessation before that moment, how, at the moment of attaining the knowledge of extinction, could he then be said to have achieved liberation in both respects? Because he has mastery over bringing forth the attainment of cessation, he counts as one who has achieved liberation in both respects, just as if he had already brought it forth.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The general rule just established — cessation requires effortful practice — turns out to have exactly one exception, and the reasoning behind it says something important about how this tradition understands Buddhahood itself: a Buddha's qualities are never achieved through the same graduated, effortful cultivation an ordinary noble one requires, because a Buddha's freedom from all defilement is already so complete that any quality manifests the instant it is willed, with no additional labor needed to bring it about.

The follow-up puzzle is genuinely sharp: "liberation in both respects" (ubhayatobhāga-vimukti) is the highest classical category of arhatship, reserved for those liberated both by wisdom and by mastery of the eight meditative liberations culminating in cessation — but if the Buddha, by hypothesis, had never actually brought forth cessation prior to enlightenment, on what basis could he possibly qualify? The answer relocates the criterion from actual prior performance to sheer capability: what matters is mastery over the attainment, not whether it has ever, as a matter of biographical fact, actually been exercised. This distinction between "has done it" and "could do it at will, with total mastery" turns out to matter a great deal for the debate that follows.

13. Did a Bodhisattva Attain Cessation Before Enlightenment? Two Schools Disagree

A genuinely contested question about the Buddha's own biography as a bodhisattva is argued out between two named schools of thought, turning on a precise, technical account of the very last moments before awakening.

Chinese Source

西方師說：菩薩學位先起此定後得菩提。云何此中不許彼說？若許彼說，便順尊者鄔波毱多《理目足論》。如彼論說：當言如來先起滅定、後生盡智。迦濕彌羅國毘婆沙師說：非前起滅定後方生盡智。所以者何？傳說菩薩三十四念得菩提故。諦現觀中有十六念，離有頂貪有十八念，謂斷有頂九品煩惱起九無間九解脫道，如是十八足前十六成三十四。一切菩薩決定先於無所有處已得離貪方入見道，不復須斷下地煩惱，於此中間無容得起不同類心，故諸菩薩學位不應起滅盡定。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Western masters say: a bodhisattva, while still a trainee, first brings forth this attainment of cessation, and only afterward attains awakening. Why, here, is their position not accepted? Because accepting it would align with the position of the Venerable Upagupta's treatise, the Lǐmùzú Lùn (理目足論). As that treatise states: it should be said that the Tathāgata first brought forth the attainment of cessation, and only afterward gave rise to the knowledge of extinction. The Kashmiri Vaibhāṣika masters say: it is not the case that he first brought forth the attainment of cessation and only afterward gave rise to the knowledge of extinction. Why is this? Because tradition holds that a bodhisattva attains awakening across exactly thirty-four mental moments. Within the direct realization of the truths there are sixteen moments; in becoming free of craving for the Peak of Existence there are a further eighteen moments — that is, cutting off the nine grades of affliction at the Peak of Existence requires nine uninterrupted paths and nine liberation paths; these eighteen, added to the previous sixteen, make thirty-four. Every bodhisattva, without exception, has already become free of craving with respect to the sphere of nothing-at-all before entering the path of seeing, and so has no further need to cut off the afflictions of any lower level; within this intervening span, there is no room whatsoever for any dissimilar state of mind to arise. Therefore, a bodhisattva, while still a trainee, should never bring forth the attainment of cessation within that span.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I have not been able to confidently identify the treatise cited here as "尊者鄔波毱多《理目足論》" — Upagupta is a well-known early patriarch in Sarvāstivāda lineage narratives, but I am not aware of a canonical Abhidharma treatise reliably attributed to him under this title, and the title itself ("理目足論," literally something like "reasoning-eye-foot treatise") does not match any text I can identify with confidence. I have left it transliterated rather than supply a confident English title I cannot actually verify, in keeping with this translation's policy of flagging genuine uncertainty rather than papering over it.

The substantive debate, in any case, is clear enough: the Western masters hold that a bodhisattva, in the final stretch of practice before enlightenment, brought forth the attainment of cessation and only then attained full awakening. The Kashmiri Vaibhāṣikas reject this on precise technical grounds, using the traditional count of exactly thirty-four mental moments required for a bodhisattva's final path to Buddhahood: sixteen moments of directly realizing the four truths (the standard path-of-seeing structure), plus a further eighteen moments — nine uninterrupted paths paired with nine liberation paths — needed to sever the nine grades of affliction still remaining at the Peak of Existence, the very last afflictions to go. Because a bodhisattva has, by this point, already become free of craving for every level below the Peak of Existence, this entire thirty-four-moment sequence runs as one uninterrupted stretch of a single, consistent type of mind — with no gap in which a "dissimilar" state of mind, such as the attainment of cessation, could possibly be inserted.

14. Closing the Debate: Never Transgressing One's Resolved Intention

A final objection is met, and the debate closes with the same candid acknowledgment already seen once before in this treatise — a position defended not because it has been proven, but because it belongs to the author's own school.

Chinese Source

外國諸師作如是說：若中間起不同類心，斯有何過？若爾，便有越期心過。然諸菩薩不越期心。理實菩薩不越期心，然非不越無漏聖道。若爾，期心如何不越？謂我未得諸漏永盡，終不解斯結加趺坐。決定不越如是期心，唯於一坐時諸事究竟故。前說為善，我所宗故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Foreign masters raise this response: if a dissimilar state of mind did arise within that interval, what fault would that actually involve? If that were so, there would then be the fault of transgressing one's resolved intention — yet bodhisattvas never transgress their resolved intention. But is it truly the case that a bodhisattva never transgresses his resolved intention, and yet does not thereby ever depart at all from the uncontaminated noble path? If that is so, then in exactly what sense is the resolved intention never transgressed? It is this: "Until I have attained the eternal extinction of all contaminants, I will never rise from this cross-legged seat." This resolved intention is never transgressed, in the sense that everything is brought to completion within that single, unbroken sitting. The position stated earlier is the better one, since it is our own school's tenet.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "foreign masters" press a fair challenge: even granting that a dissimilar mental state cannot arise mid-sequence, why is that itself a problem, rather than a neutral fact? The Kashmiri reply appeals to the bodhisattva's own famous resolve beneath the bodhi tree — the vow never to rise from that seat until liberation is complete — read here as a vow specifically against any interruption of type, which the attainment of cessation, being a wholly different kind of mental event, would necessarily constitute.

The debate then closes exactly the way the possession debate closed back in fascicle 4: not with a decisive proof, but with Vasubandhu simply naming the Kashmiri Vaibhāṣika account "the better one, since it is our own school's tenet" — the identical closing formula, word for word, as the earlier debate. This is a real pattern in how this treatise operates, not a one-off: genuinely contested technical questions are often worked through with real argumentative energy on both sides, only to be resolved, in the end, by institutional loyalty rather than a claim that the matter has been settled by reason alone.

15. What the Two Attainments Share: Both Occur in the Desire and Form Realms

Having covered the two attainments' major differences, the treatise circles back for a finer-grained comparison — starting with what they have in common, proven by an appeal to the root Abhidharma text itself.

Chinese Source

雖已說二定有多同異相，而於其中復有同異。頌曰：二定依欲色，滅定初人中。論曰：言二定者，謂無想定及滅盡定。此二俱依欲色二界而得現起。若有不許亦依色界起無想定，便違此文。謂本論言：或有是色有，此有非五行。謂色纏有情或生有想天，住不同類心，若入無想定、若入滅盡定。或生無想天已得入無想，是謂是色有，此有非五行。由此證知，如是二定俱依欲色而得現起，是名同相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Although much has already been said about the many points of resemblance and difference between the two attainments, further points remain to be addressed. Verse: the two attainments occur based on the desire realm and the form realm; the attainment of cessation first arises only among humans. Prose: by "the two attainments" is meant the attainment of non-percipience and the attainment of cessation. Both of these occur based on the two realms, desire and form. If someone does not accept that the attainment of non-percipience also occurs based on the form realm, this would contradict the following passage. For the root treatise states: there is a kind of existence that is form-existence, and this existence is not simply one of the five destinies. That is: a sentient being bound to the form realm may be born in a heaven where perception is present, abiding with a dissimilar type of mind, and may then enter either the attainment of non-percipience or the attainment of cessation. Or, having already been born in the Heaven of Non-Percipient Beings, such a being may then enter non-percipience directly — this too is called form-existence, and this existence is likewise not one of the five destinies. From this, it is established that both attainments occur based on the desire realm and the form realm alike — this is called their point of resemblance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag a genuine point of uncertainty here rather than paper over it: the phrase "此有非五行" ("this existence is not [simply] one of the five destinies") is citing a technical classification from the root Abhidharma text that I cannot pin down with full confidence. My best working reading is that it distinguishes "existence" classified by destiny (the five, or sometimes six, realms of rebirth) from "existence" classified by life-stage (the fourfold scheme of intermediate, birth, life-proper, and death existence) — with the citation specifying that "form-existence" here means an ongoing form-realm life, not a momentary birth-event, which is exactly what the surrounding argument needs: a form-realm being can, while alive there in an ordinary percipient state, deliberately enter either mindless attainment. I have translated it literally rather than smoothing over my uncertainty about the precise technical scheme being invoked.

The substantive point is more straightforward: since the root treatise explicitly describes a form-realm being entering either attainment while still alive and percipient in that realm, the attainment of non-percipience — like the attainment of cessation — cannot be confined to the desire realm alone. Both attainments, whatever else separates them, share this same basic footprint across the desire and form realms.

16. Where the Two Attainments Differ, and Proof That Cessation Can Be Lost

Their shared footprint established, the treatise turns to where the two genuinely part ways — including a scriptural proof, drawn from a sutra about advanced monks near death, that the attainment of cessation can indeed be lost and later regained.

Chinese Source

言異相者，謂無想定欲色二界皆得初起，滅定初起唯在人中。此在人中初修起已，由退為先方生色界，依色界身後復修起。此滅盡定亦有退耶？應言亦有。若不爾者，即便違害《鄔陀夷經》。經言：具壽！有諸苾芻先於此處具淨尸羅、具三摩地、具般羅若，能數入出滅受想定斯有是處。應如實知，彼於現法或臨終位，不能勤修令解滿足，從此身壞超段食天，隨受一處意成天身。於彼生已，復數入出滅受想定亦有是處。應如實知，此意成天身，佛說是色界，滅受想定唯在有頂。若得此定必無退者，如何得往色界受生？

Ru-Yi Translation

As for their point of difference: the attainment of non-percipience can first arise in either the desire realm or the form realm, while the attainment of cessation can first arise only among human beings. Having first cultivated and brought forth this attainment while human, one may, through first losing it, then be reborn in the form realm, and, based on a form-realm body, cultivate and bring it forth once again thereafter. Does this attainment of cessation, then, also admit of being lost? It should be said that it does. Were this not so, it would contradict the Sūtra of Udāyin. That sutra states: "Venerable ones! There are certain monks who, having first perfected pure moral discipline, perfected concentration, and perfected wisdom in this very place, are able to repeatedly enter and emerge from the attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception — such a case is indeed possible. It should be understood as it truly is: such a person, whether within this present life or at the moment of death, being unable to strive further to bring their liberation to full completion, upon the breaking up of this body, transcends the heavens of coarse food and is reborn, according to what is received, in a mind-made heavenly body in one of those states. Having been born there, that person may again repeatedly enter and emerge from the attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception — this, too, is indeed possible." It should be understood as it truly is that this mind-made heavenly body is what the Buddha calls the form realm — yet the attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception properly belongs only to the Peak of Existence. If, once this attainment were obtained, one could never lose it at all, how could such a person then go on to be reborn with a form-realm body at all?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The asymmetry is precise: non-percipience can be first cultivated in either the desire or the form realm, but cessation can only ever be first cultivated by a human being — form-realm access to it comes only secondhand, after losing and re-cultivating it there.

The scriptural proof is worth sitting with: it describes an advanced monk, fully accomplished in discipline, concentration, and wisdom, who can repeatedly enter and exit the attainment of cessation — but who dies before reaching full liberation. Rather than being lost, such a person is reborn in a special "mind-made" heavenly body (a body produced directly by mental power rather than by ordinary conception, transcending the realm of beings who still depend on coarse physical food) and, remarkably, continues entering and exiting the very same attainment there. Since this heavenly rebirth is identified as belonging to the form realm, while cessation's proper technical home is the Peak of Existence at the summit of the formless realm, the very fact that this person can keep practicing it after being reborn with a new, form-realm body is itself the proof the passage needs: the attainment must have been lost at death and cultivated afresh in the new body, since — as established two sections ago — this mindless state can never simply carry forward across a change of body on its own.

17. A Rival Claim Rejected: The Fourth Dhyāna Does Not Have Its Own Cessation-Attainment

A tempting shortcut — positing that cessation could arise directly within the fourth dhyāna, bypassing the whole loss-and-rebirth story just given — is rejected on scriptural grounds, with one careful qualification.

Chinese Source

有餘部執：第四靜慮亦有滅定。依彼所執，滅定無退，此義亦成。第四靜慮有滅盡定，義必不成。所以者何？九次第定，契經說故。此若必然，如何得有超越定義？此定次第依初學說，得自在時隨樂超入。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some other schools hold that the fourth dhyāna, too, has its own attainment of cessation. According to what they hold, the position that the attainment of cessation is never lost would indeed also be established. But the position that the fourth dhyāna has the attainment of cessation cannot possibly be established. Why is this? Because a sutra speaks of nine sequential attainments. If this sequence must invariably hold, then how could there be such a thing as a "superseding attainment"? This fixed sequence is stated with reference to a beginner in training; once one has attained mastery, one may leap over levels freely, as one wishes.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rival proposal would have neatly avoided the whole "lost and regained" account just given — if cessation could simply arise directly within the fourth dhyāna, a practitioner would never need to lose it, die, and be reborn with a form-realm body to keep practicing it. The treatise rejects this on the authority of a well-known scriptural list, the nine sequential attainments (the four dhyānas, the four formless attainments, and cessation as the ninth and final rung), which places cessation strictly after all eight of the others, including the Peak of Existence — never reachable directly from the fourth dhyāna alone.

The qualification is a nice piece of nuance: this fixed nine-step sequence describes how a beginner must proceed, level by level, in order. Someone who has already mastered the full sequence, however, can enter a "superseding attainment," leaping directly from a lower level to any higher one at will — but even this freedom to skip steps does not license skipping cessation's fixed position as the sequence's final rung.

18. A Sixfold Summary of Difference, and Why the Two Attainments Are Named Differently

The comparison is drawn together into a tidy sixfold list, followed by a sharp closing question: if both attainments equally stop mind and every mental factor, why does only one of their names mention feeling alongside perception?

Chinese Source

如是二定有多種異：謂地有異，第四靜慮有頂地故；加行有異，出離止息想，作意為先故；相續有異，異生聖者相續起故；異熟有異，無想有頂異熟果故；順受有異，順定不定生二受故；初起有異，二界人中最初起故。二定總以心心所滅為其自性，何緣但說名為無想、滅受想耶？二定加行中，唯厭逆此故，如亦知受等，唯名他心智。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, the two attainments differ in many respects: they differ in level, since one belongs to the fourth dhyāna and the other to the Peak of Existence; they differ in preparatory practice, since one is preceded by the attention of renunciation and the other by the attention of stilling perception; they differ in whose continuum they arise within, since one arises within the continuum of ordinary beings and the other within the continuum of noble ones; they differ in karmic ripening, since one brings about the ripening-result of non-percipience and the other the ripening-result of the Peak of Existence; they differ in what kind of result they conduce to, since one conduces only to a fixed result in the next birth, while the other gives rise to two further, less fixed kinds of result as well; and they differ in where they first arise, since one first arises in either of the two realms, while the other first arises only among humans. Both attainments, taken together, have the cessation of mind and all its mental factors as their essential nature — why, then, is one named specifically "non-percipience," and the other specifically "the cessation of feeling and perception"? Because, within the preparatory practice for each of the two attainments, it is specifically this — perception alone, or feeling and perception together — toward which the practitioner feels aversion and renunciation; the name simply follows what the practitioner's own intention specifically targets, just as, even though the wisdom that knows another's mind also thereby comes to know that person's feeling and the rest along with it, it is named solely "the wisdom that knows others' minds," after its single most salient object.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sixfold summary is mostly a tidy restatement of ground already covered — level, preparatory motivation, who attains it, karmic ripening, result-timing, and where it first arises — gathered here into one clean list. The closing question is the more interesting one: both attainments technically stop every mental factor without exception, so why does only "cessation of feeling and perception" name two factors while "non-percipience" names only one? The answer is that a name tracks the practitioner's own subjective target of renunciation, not the full technical scope of what actually happens — precisely the same kind of move already seen when a supernormal cognition gets named "knowing others' minds" even though it incidentally reveals their feelings too. What one is trying to escape, not everything that in fact ceases, decides the name.

19. If Mind Is Cut Off, How Does It Ever Resume? A Famous Puzzle, and the "Mutual Seed" Solution

A genuinely difficult causal puzzle now surfaces: ordinary continuity explains how one moment of mind gives rise to the next, but what explains mind resuming after being completely absent for a long stretch? The answer reaches for a solution that would echo powerfully in later Buddhist philosophy.

Chinese Source

今二定中心久時斷，如何於後心復得生？毘婆沙師許過去有前心為後等無間緣。有餘師言：如生無色，色久時斷，如何於後色復得生？彼生定應由心非色，如是出定心亦應然，由有根身非由心起。故彼先代諸軌範師，咸言二法互為種子。二法者，謂心、有根身。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, within these two attainments, mind is cut off for a long span of time — how, then, does mind arise again afterward? The Vaibhāṣika masters hold that the past mind which immediately preceded the attainment serves as the immediately-antecedent condition for the mind that arises afterward. Other masters say: consider the case of a being reborn in the formless realm, where form is cut off for a long span of time — how, then, does form arise again afterward, when such a being is eventually reborn back into a realm with form? In that case, you must surely hold that this rebirth-arising occurs through the power of mind, not through form continuing to serve as its own antecedent condition, since form was cut off the whole time. In just the same way, then, the mind that arises upon emerging from these two attainments should likewise be explained: it must arise through the power of the physical, sense-endowed body, not through mind continuing to serve as its own antecedent condition, since mind was cut off the whole time. For this reason, the earlier generations of master-teachers all alike held that these two things serve as mutual seeds for one another. The two things are: mind, and the physical, sense-endowed body.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely difficult causal puzzle, and the Vaibhāṣika's first answer is the simplest available: the immediately-antecedent-condition relationship (already established elsewhere in this treatise as one of the four classical types of causal condition) does not actually require strict, unbroken adjacency — a moment of mind from long before the attainment can still serve as condition for the moment that resumes after it, skipping over the gap entirely.

The more interesting move is the rival's counter-example: exactly the same puzzle already exists for a being reborn in the formless realm, where physical form is entirely absent for an entire lifetime, and yet that same being's form eventually resumes upon a later rebirth with a body. If the Vaibhāṣikas are forced to say mind alone explains form's return there — since form itself was absent the whole time and cannot serve as its own antecedent condition — then, by the identical logic, it must be the physical body that explains mind's return from these two attainments, not mind serving as its own condition across a gap where it too was entirely absent. The proposed solution — that mind and the physical, sense-endowed body serve as reciprocal "seeds" for one another, each capable of regenerating the other after either has been suspended — is a genuinely significant moment in the history of Buddhist thought. This same "mutual seed" reasoning, applied to the identical problem of explaining continuity across a total gap in mental activity, is widely recognized as an important precursor to the Yogācāra school's later, far more elaborate doctrine of the storehouse-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna) as the underlying seed-repository that never itself ceases, even when the six active consciousnesses do.

20. Does a Subtle Mind Remain in Cessation? Vasumitra and Ghoṣaka Disagree

A named debate between two revered masters tackles the puzzle from a different angle: rather than explaining how mind resumes after total absence, why not simply deny that mind is ever totally absent at all?

Chinese Source

尊者世友問論中說：若執滅定全無有心，可有此過。我說滅定猶有細心，故無此失。尊者妙音說此非理。所以者何？若此定中猶有識者，三和合故必應有觸，由觸為緣應有受想。如世尊說：意及法為緣生於意識，三和合觸俱起受想思。則此定中受想等法亦應不滅。若謂如經說受緣愛，然阿羅漢雖有諸受而不生愛，觸亦應爾，非一切觸皆受等緣。此例不然，有差別故。經自簡言：若無明觸所生諸受為緣生愛。曾無有處簡觸生受，故有差別。由此道理，毘婆沙師說滅定中諸心皆滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Venerable Vasumitra, in his Questions Treatise, states: "If one holds that the attainment of cessation involves no mind whatsoever, this fault would indeed follow. I hold instead that the attainment of cessation still retains a subtle mind — hence this fault does not arise." The Venerable Ghoṣaka says this position is not correct. Why is this? If, within this attainment, consciousness were still present, then, since the three factors would come together, contact would necessarily also be present; and, with contact serving as condition, feeling and perception would necessarily also be present. As the World-Honored One states: "Mind and dharmas serve as condition, giving rise to mental consciousness; the coming-together of the three is contact, and feeling, perception, and volition arise together with it." It would then follow that feeling, perception, and the rest of these dharmas would likewise not actually cease within this attainment. If one argues, as a sutra states, that "feeling serves as condition for craving," and yet an arhat, though still having feelings, does not thereby give rise to craving — so likewise contact here need not give rise to feeling — then, by the same token, contact too should not need to serve as condition for feeling in every case, since not every instance of contact serves as the condition for feeling and the rest. But this comparison does not actually hold, since there is a real difference between the two cases: the sutra itself explicitly qualifies its own statement, saying, "it is specifically feelings arisen from the contact of ignorance that serve as the condition for craving to arise" — whereas nowhere does it similarly qualify contact's giving rise to feeling. Hence there is indeed a real difference between the two cases. For this reason, the Vaibhāṣika masters hold that, within the attainment of cessation, all mind whatsoever genuinely ceases.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Vasumitra's proposal is elegant and would dissolve the entire puzzle of the previous section at a stroke: if a subtle mind never actually stops during the attainment of cessation, there is no gap in mental continuity to explain at all. Ghoṣaka's rebuttal is a careful piece of technical reasoning: consciousness, by this system's own standard causal analysis, can never occur in isolation — the moment any consciousness is present, the coming-together of faculty, object, and consciousness constitutes contact, and contact in turn necessarily brings feeling and perception along with it. So positing even the subtlest residual consciousness would mean feeling and perception are also still present — directly contradicting the very definition of this attainment as the cessation of exactly those two.

The one loophole Vasumitra's side might have reached for — that an arhat has feeling without craving, so perhaps contact could likewise occur without feeling — is closed by a careful textual point: scripture explicitly restricts its own "feeling conditions craving" formula to feelings born specifically from contact rooted in ignorance, marking that link as conditional, but nowhere similarly restricts contact's basic role in producing feeling at all. The asymmetry is not arbitrary; it is written into the sutra's own wording. With Vasumitra's alternative closed off this way, the Vaibhāṣika position — that mind is genuinely, completely absent throughout the attainment of cessation, full stop — stands as the treatise's settled answer to this whole debate.

21. A Final Echo: Are the Two Attainments Real Entities, or Merely Provisional?

The two attainments' extended treatment closes exactly the way the possession debate closed back in fascicle 4 — a genuinely elegant reductive alternative proposed, and then set aside for the same institutional reason as before.

Chinese Source

若都無心，如何名定？此令大種平等行故說名為定，或由心力平等至此故名為定。如是二定為是實有、為是假有？應言實有，能遮礙心令不生故。有說：此證理不應然，由前定心能遮礙故。謂前定心與所餘心相違而起，由此起故，唯令餘心暫時不轉。此能引發違心所依令相續故，唯不轉位假立為定，無別實體。此唯不轉分位假定，入前出後兩位皆無，故假說此是有為攝。或即所依由定心引令如是起，假立為定。應知無想亦復如是，謂由前心與所餘心相違而起，由此起故，唯令餘心暫時不轉，唯不轉位假立無想，餘說如前。此非善說，違我宗故。已辯二定。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is truly no mind whatsoever present, how can this state still be called an "attainment"? This state causes the great elements to proceed in a balanced, even way, and it is for this reason that it is called an "attainment"; or else, it is called an "attainment" because it is reached through the power of mind having brought the elements into such an even balance beforehand. Are these two attainments, then, genuinely real entities, or are they merely provisionally posited? It should be said that they are genuinely real entities, since they are able to obstruct mind, causing it not to arise. There is another view: this line of proof should not be accepted, since it is instead the immediately preceding moment of concentrated mind itself that does the obstructing. That is: the immediately preceding moment of concentrated mind arises in a manner opposed to every other kind of mind, and, because it arises this way, it alone causes all other kinds of mind to temporarily not operate. Because this is able to bring about, within the underlying support, a continuing condition opposed to ordinary mind, it is only with respect to this state of ordinary mind not operating that the term "attainment" is provisionally applied — there is no genuinely separate real entity involved at all. This "attainment," being merely the provisional label for the phase in which ordinary mind does not operate, is present neither at the moment of entering nor at the moment of emerging from it; hence it is only provisionally said to be subsumed under the conditioned. Or alternatively: it is simply the underlying support itself, brought by the power of the preceding concentrated mind to persist in this condition, that is provisionally called "the attainment." Non-percipience, too, should be understood in just the same way: it arises because the immediately preceding moment of mind arises in a manner opposed to every other kind of mind, and, because it arises this way, it alone causes other kinds of mind to temporarily not operate; it is only with respect to this state of non-operation that "non-percipience" is provisionally applied, and the rest should be explained just as before. This is not a well-reasoned position, since it contradicts our own school's tenet. The two attainments have now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The section closes with a definitional worry worth pausing on: if concentration (定, samādhi) ordinarily names a quality of mind, how can a state with no mind at all still count as a "concentration"? The answer relocates the label onto its physical effect instead — a balanced, even functioning of the bodily elements — rather than onto any ongoing mental quality, since there is none left to describe.

The closing question — real entity, or merely provisional? — is the exact same question the treatise spent the better part of fascicle 4 arguing about "possession," and the rival's answer here is built from the identical materials: no separate real "attainment" dharma is needed at all, just the causal power of the immediately preceding moment of concentrated mind, which arises specifically opposed to ordinary mental activity and thereby suppresses it for a time. "Attainment" and "non-percipience" alike, on this view, are only provisional labels for a phase of non-operation, grounded in nothing more than an altered underlying support — the same reductive move already seen applied to "seed" and to "ordinary-being-nature." And the treatise closes it exactly the same way it closed the possession debate and the bodhisattva debate: rejecting the more economical rival account not because it has been refuted, but "because it contradicts our own school's tenet." Three times now, across two fascicles, this exact formula has closed a genuinely live philosophical debate — a real, recurring feature of how this treatise operates, not a coincidence, and worth naming plainly rather than smoothing over.
