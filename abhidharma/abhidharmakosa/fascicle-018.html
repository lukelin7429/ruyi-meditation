The Substance of the Five Ānantarya Offenses

Fascicle 18 completes the sixth part of Chapter 4 (分別業品第四之六, the Karma-nirdeśa), continuing directly from fascicle 17's closing account of the three grave obstructions. It opens with what the five ānantarya offenses actually consist of and what a schism in the monastic community truly is, moves through an extended digression on the bodhisattva path — when a bodhisattva becomes irreversibly settled, the thirty-two marks, the three incalculable eons, and the six pāramitās — and closes with a detailed treatment of the three grounds for meritorious deeds: giving, moral discipline, and meditative cultivation.

1. What the Five Ānantarya Offenses Consist Of, and What a Schism Actually Is

Continuing directly from fascicle 17's account of the karmic obstruction, the treatise now asks what the five ānantarya offenses actually consist of at the most basic level of bodily and verbal karma. Of the five, four are bodily karma and one is verbal: killing one's mother, killing one's father, and killing an arhat are three instances of the killing karma-path (all bodily karma); maliciously causing a buddha's body to bleed is a fourth bodily act, though one that only ever reaches the level of preparatory action and never the completed root act, since a buddha's body can never actually be destroyed by such violence; and causing a schism in the monastic community is, surprisingly, an instance of the false-speech karma-path, a verbal act, rather than any kind of bodily violence. The reasoning behind classifying schism as false speech is that a schism is, most fundamentally, brought about by convincing others of something untrue about the Dharma or the teacher — that is, presenting a false alternative teaching as though it were the genuine one — and this act of misrepresentation is what earns it the name "breaking the community" only by extension, since it is the effect (a broken community) that is being named after its cause (the deceptive speech that produced it), or because that same false speech simultaneously has the further capacity to break the community apart. This raises a further, subtler question: if a schism is at root an act of false speech, what then does "the community being broken" itself actually consist of, and who — the one causing the schism, or the community that ends up divided — actually comes to possess whatever it is that constitutes this brokenness? The treatise's answer is that the substance of a broken community is itself a case of non-concurrence — a formation dissociated from mind, belonging to the "morally neutral, non-obstructive" category — and, strikingly, it is possessed not by the person who instigates the schism, but by the members of the community who end up on the divided, "broken" side of it; the instigator, by contrast, comes to possess only the false-speech offense itself (the manifested and unmanifested verbal karma arising simultaneously with the schism), not the state of communal brokenness, since it is specifically those who are split off from the community, not the one who does the splitting, who count as being "broken."

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the three grave obstructions discussed earlier, the five ānantarya offenses were said to constitute the substance of the karmic obstruction. What is the substance of these five ānantarya karmas? Verse: among these five ānantarya, four are bodily karma, one is verbal karma; three are killing, one is false speech, and one is [merely] the preparatory action of the killing karma-path. Explanation: among the five ānantarya, four are bodily karma, one is verbal karma; three are killing, one is the root act of the false-speech karma-path, and one is the preparatory action of the killing karma-path — since the Tathāgata's body cannot actually be destroyed. The ānantarya offense of causing a schism is false speech. Since it is false speech, why is it named "breaking the community"? It is named after its effect by way of its cause, or because it also has the capacity to break [the community]. If so, what is the substance of the community's being broken? Who — the one who breaks it, or the one broken — comes to possess it? Verse: the community's brokenness is a state of non-concurrence, a formation dissociated from mind, of a neutral, non-obstructive nature; it is possessed by the broken community. Explanation: the substance of the community's brokenness is a state of non-concurrence, included within the aggregate of formations dissociated from mind, of a neutral, non-obstructive nature — how, then, does this constitute an ānantarya offense? It is because this communal brokenness arises from false speech that causing a schism is said to be the fruit of an ānantarya act. It is not the one who causes the schism who comes to possess this communal brokenness, but rather the community that is broken that comes to possess it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should highlight how unexpected this classification is on first encounter: causing a schism — arguably the ānantarya offense with the most obviously "communal" and social character of the five — turns out, in this tradition's precise technical analysis, to reduce to an act of false speech, a single verbal karma-path, rather than any distinct category of its own. The treatise's own explanation (naming the cause after its effect, "breaking the community" standing in for "the false speech that breaks the community") is presented as a straightforward point of Abhidharma technical precision, and we have rendered it as such rather than reading more into it.

The claim that the state of "brokenness" is possessed by the divided community rather than by the instigator is a genuinely subtle piece of the Vaibhāṣika "formations dissociated from mind" apparatus (already introduced in earlier fascicles for concepts like non-possession and shared class-membership) — we flag that readers unfamiliar with this category may find it counterintuitive that a schismatic's own guilt is analyzed as residing in a property of the community rather than in the instigator's own mental continuum; the instigator's culpability, in this technical framework, is fully accounted for instead through their possession of the underlying false-speech karma itself, which the following sections go on to describe as uniquely severe.

2. The Ripened Result of Schism, and Who Can Cause It

The treatise now specifies exactly which unwholesome karma the schismatic instigator personally possesses, and what its ripened result is. It is specifically the false-speech offense — the manifested and unmanifested verbal karma that arises together with the schism itself — that the instigator possesses; this offense inevitably brings rebirth in the deepest hell (Avīci), where the instigator undergoes extremely severe suffering for a full intermediate eon. Other grave ānantarya offenses do not necessarily result in rebirth specifically in Avīci; if a person commits several ānantarya offenses, all of them ripen together in the very same next rebirth — since one cannot undergo several separate rebirths simultaneously — but the suffering of that single rebirth intensifies in proportion to the number of offenses committed, meaning a being who committed multiple ānantarya offenses experiences an especially large, tormented body equipped with especially numerous and severe instruments of suffering, receiving two, three, four, or five times the intensity of pain compared to an ordinary Avīci rebirth. The treatise then narrows down exactly who is capable of causing this specific kind of schism, under what conditions, and for how long the schism actually lasts once caused. Only a fully ordained monk of substantial standing (not a nun, and not a layperson) can cause this kind of schism; furthermore, only a person of "view" temperament, not "craving" temperament, is capable of it, and only one who observes pure moral conduct, not one who has already broken the precepts, since a precept-breaker's words carry no persuasive authority. The schism must be caused at a location other than wherever the Teacher (the Buddha) himself is currently residing, since the Buddha's presence and the authoritative weight of his own words make him impossible to overshadow or contradict in this way. Only ordinary, unenlightened persons (not noble ones who have realized the nature of reality) can be led into such a schism, since noble ones have directly verified the Dharma's true character and cannot be talked out of it — though some hold that even one who has merely attained forbearance (a stage just short of full realization) also cannot be led into schism. The term "ordinary, unenlightened" here is meant to cover both senses: not only must those persuaded into the schism be ordinary, unenlightened beings, but the teaching that persuades them must present itself as differing even from the Buddha's own teaching, asserting that some other path to liberation exists as well. A schism, once it occurs, holds for only that single night: the very same night, without fail, the divided parties naturally return to unity, without the split extending overnight into a further day. This particular kind of division is specifically called "breaking the wheel of the Dharma," since it obstructs the wheel of the noble path and destroys the community's harmony. As for exactly which continents can produce this kind of schism, and how many monastics at minimum are required: schism against the Dharma-wheel itself (a rival teaching to the Buddha's own) can only occur in Jambudvīpa, and requires at minimum nine monastics (eight forming the two divided factions, with the ninth being the one who causes the split, since a faction requires at least four to be legitimately reckoned as such) — this specific number being required because a schism of this kind must involve the establishment of an actual, functioning rival community structure, something possible only where a buddha has appeared to begin with, since only then can there be a rival teacher to set up in opposition. A more limited kind of schism — merely a schism over how to conduct formal monastic procedures (羯磨), without setting up any full rival teaching — can occur across all three of the ordinary continents (not Uttarakuru), and requires at minimum only eight monastics, since this lesser schism simply requires a single monastic community, within one shared territorial boundary, to split into two factions for the purpose of separately conducting formal procedures.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should note that the claim "even one who has attained forbearance cannot be led into schism" is explicitly marked in the source as an additional, not universally shared, position ("some say") rather than the treatise's settled view — we have preserved it as an attributed alternative rather than folding it into the main account.

The detail that a schism "lasts only one night" before the community naturally reunites is a specific point of Vinaya (monastic discipline) procedure being assumed here rather than fully explained — this fascicle is drawing on background knowledge of how a formal schism and its resolution were understood to function procedurally within the monastic community, which we have translated as stated without attempting to reconstruct the fuller Vinaya context behind it.

3. When Schism Is Impossible, and Why Each Ānantarya Offense Is Grave

The treatise identifies six specific circumstances under which a schism of the Dharma-wheel type cannot occur. It cannot occur very early in a buddha's teaching career, shortly after the wheel of the Dharma has first been set in motion, since the community is still unified in a single flavor of understanding at that point; nor can it occur very late, when the Well-Gone One is on the verge of final nirvāṇa, for the same reason. It cannot occur before the "blisters" of doubt regarding correct precepts and correct view have even arisen within the community, since a schism requires these two kinds of doubt to already be present as a precondition. It cannot occur before the first pairing of tranquility (śamatha) and insight (vipaśyanā) meditation practices has been formally established as the community's foundation, since, prior to that point, any incipient division would, by its very nature, quickly and spontaneously reunite on its own. It cannot occur after a buddha has already passed into final nirvāṇa, since there is then no true, living Teacher left to serve as the rival's actual opponent. And it cannot occur before a formal territorial boundary (界) has been established for the community, since a schism, by definition, requires a single community sharing one boundary to split into two factions, which is impossible where no such shared boundary yet exists. The treatise notes that not every buddha's dispensation experiences a schism of the Dharma-wheel type; whether one occurs in any given case depends entirely on the specific karmic circumstances accumulated by that particular community over past lives. Having addressed this side topic, the treatise now returns to explain the deeper conditions behind why killing one's mother, father, and the others specifically count as ānantarya offenses, rather than other kinds of unwholesome acts. The underlying reason is that these offenses involve casting aside a "field" of gratitude, or destroying a "field" of virtue. Killing one's father or mother specifically constitutes casting aside a field of gratitude, since parents are the fundamental source of one's own bodily existence — one owes them gratitude precisely because they are one's origin, and killing them constitutes an act of casting aside that very debt of gratitude. Arhats, meanwhile, constitute a field of virtue, since they possess superior spiritual qualities and the capacity to generate further merit in others; killing an arhat destroys the very basis upon which such virtue depends, and this is what makes it a grave offense. An intriguing question arises here: does killing a parent whose sex has changed (through some karmic or physical transformation) still constitute an ānantarya offense? The treatise answers that it does, since the offense depends on the underlying continuum being one's actual parent, not on that parent's current bodily sex — hence there can be a case of causing a man to lose his life who is not, strictly speaking, one's "father" nor an arhat, yet still incurs an ānantarya offense, namely when one's mother has undergone a change of sex to male and one then kills that person; and, symmetrically, causing a woman to lose her life who is not one's "mother" nor an arhat can also incur an ānantarya offense, namely when one's father has changed to female form. The treatise then works through several further puzzles regarding intentionality and mistaken identity in these cases: even in the extreme case where a fetus has been miscarried and another woman raises the child as her own, later killing that adoptive mother does not incur the specific ānantarya offense of matricide, since gratitude in this context traces back to the blood-relation of one's actual bodily origin, and one's proper duty of gratitude is owed to whichever mother actually nursed and raised the child to maturity, regardless of biological origin — though the text notes some subtlety in exactly how these two considerations interact. If one sets out with the intention to kill one's actual father or mother but, by mistake, kills someone else instead, no ānantarya offense is incurred; conversely, if one sets out to kill someone other than one's parents but, by mistake, ends up killing one's actual father or mother, this likewise does not constitute the grave offense — as in the example of a son who swings a stick at a mosquito on his father's body but instead strikes and kills his father, or a mother who hides herself in a place her child mistakes for someone else and is killed unknowingly. However, if a single act of violence, through one continuous effort, ends up killing both one's mother and some other person simultaneously, two distinct instances of unmanifested karma arise, but only the manifested karma directed at the mother counts as the grave, ānantarya-level offense, since the power of an ānantarya act is understood to be especially strong and dominant. The venerable Ghoṣaka holds a different view: that two distinct instances of manifested karma also arise in such a case, since manifested karma, in his view, is constituted by an accumulation of subtle particles that can, in principle, be distinguished this way. If one kills someone believing them to be an ordinary being, without any specific awareness that the victim is actually an arhat, but the object one's killing-intention is actually fixed upon turns out to be that same arhat with certainty (i.e., there is no ambiguity about which specific person is being targeted, only ignorance of their spiritual attainment), an ānantarya offense of killing an arhat is still incurred, since intentional targeting of that specific person, not conscious awareness of the victim's spiritual status, is what matters here. If a person kills their own father, and that father happens to also be an arhat, only a single ānantarya offense (not two) is incurred, since there is only a single underlying physical basis (the one person) being killed — despite this, the treatise notes, the Buddha, addressing a specific individual, once said he had committed two ānantarya offenses (killing his father, and killing an arhat), which should be understood either as indicating that a single offense can arise from the confluence of two distinct conditions, or as the Buddha rebuking that person's fault by pointing to it from two separate angles for greater emphasis.

4. The Worst of All Offenses, and the Greatest Worldly Result

The treatise completes its account of the ānantarya conditions with two further points: maliciously causing a buddha's body to bleed only reaches the level of preparatory action, since a buddha's body can never actually be destroyed by such an attack — merely striking with malicious intent, rather than actually drawing blood, does not constitute the ānantarya offense at all. And if, at the time one undertakes the preparatory action of killing, the intended victim is not yet an arhat but attains arhatship only in the moment just before dying, the one who kills them does not incur the offense of killing an arhat, since there was no preparatory action of killing directed at someone already beyond training at the time that preparatory action was actually undertaken. A further important question follows: once a person has irreversibly set the preparatory action of an ānantarya offense in motion, is it still possible for them to become free from affliction or attain a noble result before the offense is completed? The treatise answers firmly that it is not: once the preparatory action of an ānantarya offense is certain to reach completion, there is absolutely no possibility, in the interval before it is completed, of becoming free from affliction or attaining any noble result — this stands in contrast to the preparatory action of other, lesser unwholesome karma-paths, where, if the noble path happens to arise in the interim, the karma-path itself simply fails to be completed, since the physical and mental basis of one on the noble path is fundamentally incompatible with completing such an act. The treatise then poses two capping questions: among all unwholesome acts including the ānantarya offenses, which single offense is the very gravest? And among all wholesome, worldly acts, which single one produces the greatest result? The answer to the first is unequivocal: causing a schism through false speech is the single gravest offense of all, worse even than the other four ānantarya offenses, since — even though the schismatic instigator may fully understand what is and is not the true Dharma — he deliberately gives rise to false, distorted speech for the specific purpose of breaking apart the community, thereby wounding the Buddha's own Dharma-body and obstructing the entire world's path to heavenly rebirth and liberation. For as long as the community remains broken and has not yet reconciled, the entire world is thereby blocked from entering the noble path, attaining its fruits, becoming free from affliction, or exhausting the contaminants; the practice of meditative concentration, recitation, and contemplative reflection all cease; the great trichiliocosm's wheel of the Dharma stops turning; and gods, humans, nāgas, and other beings all become mentally and physically agitated — this is why causing a schism specifically brings about a full intermediate eon's ripened suffering in Avīci, the single heaviest ripened result of any unwholesome act, with the other four ānantarya offenses graded successively lighter in the order of killing an arhat, killing one's father, killing one's mother, and drawing a buddha's blood, this last being the very lightest of the five since the debt of gratitude and similar factors involved are comparatively least in that case. An apparent tension arises here: elsewhere the Buddha states that, among the three kinds of harmful action (bodily, verbal, and mental), mental wrongdoing is the gravest, and elsewhere still that wrong view is the gravest among all wrong views — how can causing a schism through speech also be called gravest? The resolution is that each of these three claims is made from a different angle: among the five ānantarya offenses specifically, causing a schism is gravest; among the three kinds of harmful action generally, mental wrongdoing is gravest; and among the five specific varieties of distorted view, wrong view (the view denying karma altogether) is gravest — the different rankings simply reflect different criteria being applied, such as the sheer scale of the resulting harm, the number of beings affected, or whether wholesome roots are severed altogether. As for the greatest single worldly result, this belongs to the volition that leads to rebirth at the very peak of conditioned existence (the highest, most refined level of the formless realm), which produces the extraordinarily long and tranquil ripened result of a full eighty thousand great eons — this claim specifically concerns ripened results; if instead one considers the freedom-from-bondage fruit rather than ripened result, then it is the volition associated with the diamond-like concentration (the final meditative attainment immediately preceding full liberation) that produces the truly greatest result, since it is this volition's power that permanently severs every remaining fetter — the qualifying phrase "worldly" in the original verse is precisely what distinguishes this ripened-result claim from that further, supramundane case.

5. The Five Ānantarya "Counterparts," and the Three Critical Junctures

The treatise now identifies five further offenses which, while not technically counted among the five true ānantarya offenses, are close enough in kind and gravity to be called their "counterparts" (同類業): defiling one's own mother who is an arhat nun through non-chaste conduct; killing a bodhisattva who has already become "settled" on the definite path to buddhahood; killing a noble one still in training (below the level of arhat); destroying the specific conditions that maintain a monastic community's harmony (short of an actual full schism); and destroying a stūpa. Each of these mirrors one of the five true ānantarya offenses closely enough — defiling one's arhat mother parallels the shamefulness of matricide; killing a bodhisattva or a noble-in-training parallels killing an arhat; disrupting communal harmony parallels causing an actual schism; and destroying a stūpa parallels wounding a buddha's body — that the tradition groups them together as a secondary tier of especially grave offenses. The treatise then identifies three especially critical junctures in a practitioner's spiritual progress at which a ripening, obstructive karma poses its greatest danger. When a practitioner, having reached the summit stage of preparatory practice, is on the verge of attaining forbearance (the point after which falling back into a bad rebirth becomes permanently impossible), any karma capable of producing rebirth in a bad destiny becomes an extremely powerful obstruction at that exact moment, since forbearance would otherwise place the practitioner permanently beyond the reach of such karma's ripening stage — much like all of a person's creditors converging most urgently just as that person is about to leave the country where the debts were incurred, since this is their last opportunity to collect. Likewise, when a practitioner is on the verge of attaining the fruit of non-returner, any karma bound to the desire realm (apart from karma whose ripening is already fixed to occur within this very life) becomes an extremely powerful obstruction at that moment; and when a practitioner is on the verge of attaining the fruit of one beyond training (arhatship), any karma bound to the form or formless realms (again apart from karma already fixed to ripen in this present life) becomes an extremely powerful obstruction, for the same underlying reason in each case.

6. When a Bodhisattva Becomes Irreversibly "Settled"

Having mentioned the "settled bodhisattva" in the preceding section, the treatise now opens an extended digression to explain exactly what this term means. A bodhisattva earns the designation "settled" specifically from the point at which he begins cultivating the karma that will produce the marvelous thirty-two marks of a great being as its ripened result — from that moment onward, up through his eventual attainment of buddhahood, he is guaranteed to be reborn only in fortunate destinies (among humans or gods, these being called "fortunate" because their circumstances are excellent and praiseworthy) and, within those fortunate destinies, only into eminent families — brahmin or kṣatriya households, or the households of extremely wealthy elders and great brahmin clans. Within such eminent families, though ordinary members may have complete or deficient faculties, a settled bodhisattva always possesses superior, unimpaired faculties, and is always reborn in a male body, never again taking female form, still less the form of a paṇḍaka or similarly categorized person. In every successive rebirth, he retains unbroken memory of his past lives, and the wholesome deeds he undertakes never regress or falter — that is, in all matters of benefiting and bringing happiness to sentient beings, he is able to endure every hardship pressing upon his body, and even when others treat him with all manner of hostility and contrariety, his mind never grows weary or discouraged. As the world has a traditional saying about "the priceless serving-attendant," this saying is meant to describe precisely such a bodhisattva: even though this great being has already achieved every kind of supreme and perfect virtue, through his long cultivation of unconditioned great compassion he remains spontaneously and constantly bound in service to others, embracing all classes of sentient beings without a trace of arrogance, treating them all as his equals — indeed, he constantly regards himself as though he were their very own servant, and it is for this reason that he is able to patiently endure all manner of difficult demands, and to shoulder every burdensome task asked of him, without exception.

7. Cultivating the Thirty-Two Marks: A Hundred Eons, a Hundred Merits Each

The treatise now specifies the precise conditions under which the karma producing the thirty-two marks is actually cultivated. This cultivation can only take place in Jambudvīpa, since it is only in this continent that beings possess the sharpest, clearest wisdom needed for it. It can only be undertaken in a male body, never in a female or other body, since by the time a bodhisattva reaches this stage of practice he has already transcended the possibility of female or similarly categorized rebirths. It can only occur through volition arising directly in a buddha's actual presence, taking that living buddha as its object — this volition being specifically "cultivation consisting in thought" (a volition arising from direct, present contemplation) rather than the kind cultivated merely through hearing the teaching or through more general meditative practice. And this entire course of cultivation takes a full hundred eons to complete, no more and no less, since this is simply the fixed pattern by which every buddha's causal-stage practice unfolds. However, our own teacher, Śākyamuni, being an especially vigorous and energetic bodhisattva, was able to compress ninety-one eons of ordinary practice into the equivalent of only nine — completing the karma for the marvelous marks in the equivalent of only ninety-one eons of full duration, this being why the Tathāgata once told a village headman: "I recall that across ninety-one eons, I have never seen a single household suffer even the slightest harm on account of an offering of food they made to me — quite the opposite, they invariably received great benefit from it," since from that point onward he was constantly able to remember his past lives, and it is for this reason that the treatise speaks only of "ninety-one eons" rather than the full hundred. The elder teachers of the tradition additionally hold that, from the very beginning of his first of three incalculable eons of practice as a bodhisattva, Śākyamuni had already eliminated four specific faults and attained two specific virtues from that point onward. As already explained, each individual one of the thirty-two marvelous marks requires the completion of a hundred units of merit to bring about — but what, precisely, constitutes the measure of "a single unit of merit"? Various teachers offer different answers: some hold that it is the specific karma, cultivated by every other sentient being apart from a bodhisattva already nearing buddhahood, that brings about the ripened result of wealth and happiness — this quantity constitutes "one unit of merit." Others hold that it is, instead, the dominant-condition power of the karma that all sentient beings collectively generate when a world-system is about to come into formation, this collective karmic power constituting "one unit of merit." 9. The Six Pāramitās, Completed at Four Milestone Moments

The treatise now identifies exactly at which stage, and through which specific episodes, our bodhisattva Śākyamuni brought each of the six pāramitās (transcendent perfections) to full completion. The perfection of giving (dāna) was completed at the moment when the bodhisattva was able to give absolutely everything to everyone — extending even to his own eyes and bone-marrow — purely out of compassion, without any personal expectation of a superior future rebirth in return; once his giving reached this standard, the pāramitā of giving was fully perfected. The perfections of moral discipline (śīla) and patience (kṣānti) were completed together at the moment when the bodhisattva's body was cut apart, limb from limb, and even though he had not yet become free from desire and craving, not the slightest trace of anger arose in his mind — once his endurance reached this standard, both the discipline and patience pāramitās were fully perfected. The perfection of vigorous effort (vīrya) was completed during an episode of courageous, energetic striving: the bodhisattva, in the course of his practice, encountered the Tathāgata Tiṣya seated within a jeweled shrine, absorbed in the meditative state of the fire-element, his majestic radiance blazing forth unlike anything ordinary; the bodhisattva gazed up at him with such single-minded reverence that he forgot even to set down the one foot he had raised, remaining standing that way, without the slightest weariness, for seven full days and nights, his mind purified, praising that buddha with this verse of praise: "Among all things heard of in heaven and on this earth, in realms departed to and heavenly abodes throughout the ten directions, there is none equal to you, O foremost of men, O bull among men, great renunciant, sought throughout every land and mountain and forest." Having offered this praise, he thereby leapt ahead by nine entire eons in his practice — this episode alone completing the perfection of vigorous effort. Finally, the perfections of meditative concentration (dhyāna) and wisdom (prajñā) were completed together at the moment the bodhisattva was seated upon the diamond throne, on the very verge of attaining unsurpassed, perfect awakening, entering the diamond-like concentration immediately preceding that supreme realization — this being the point at which both the concentration and wisdom pāramitās reached full completion. Because these six perfections enable one to arrive at the far shore of one's own complete, perfected virtues, they are called "pāramitās" ("gone beyond," or "perfections").

10. Three Kinds of Meritorious Deeds, and What "Giving" Really Is

The treatise now turns to a sūtra teaching on three "grounds for meritorious deeds" (福業事): the ground of giving, the ground of moral discipline, and the ground of meditative cultivation. Why are these three called "grounds for meritorious deeds" specifically? All three categories are indeed meritorious, and each may count, depending on the specific case, as karma proper, as a "ground" or basis distinct from that karma, or as both together — this distinction working analogously to the earlier analysis of the ten karma-paths, where some elements counted as both karma and its path, some as path but not karma, and so on. Within the giving category specifically, bodily and verbal karma fully qualifies for all three labels together (merit, meritorious karma, and ground of merit), since giving is itself an activity expressed through body and speech; the volition that initiates such giving is called only "meritorious karma," while the mental factors that accompany that volition are called only "merit," without qualifying as a "ground." Since the moral-discipline category likewise consists essentially of bodily and verbal karma, it too qualifies fully for all three labels. Within the meditative-cultivation category, however, loving-kindness itself is called only a "ground of merit," being the object which that activity operates upon, whereas the volition associated with loving-kindness, taking loving-kindness as its vehicle for action, is called only "meritorious karma"; the remaining mental factors accompanying it are called only "merit." Some other teachers hold instead that only volition constitutes true "meritorious karma," while giving, moral discipline, and cultivation are simply the specific "grounds" through which meritorious karma operates, since it is by means of these three that meritorious karma unfolds and proceeds. Turning next to giving itself: what precisely constitutes an act of giving, and what result does it bring about? Although the object one relinquishes may loosely be called "the gift," in the strictest sense it is the specific act of relinquishing that properly deserves the name "giving," since it is only through this act of relinquishing that the transfer is actually accomplished — the act of relinquishing itself is thus the true substance of giving. However, an act of relinquishing may also be driven by fear, or by hope of some future advantage, or by simple greed, and such motives are specifically excluded from what the treatise means by "giving" here; the term "for the sake of honoring or benefiting" is included precisely to rule these out, since true giving is relinquishing something specifically in order to honor or benefit another. This "act of relinquishing" is itself constituted by bodily and verbal karma together with the volition that gives rise to them — that volition being, specifically, the cluster of virtuous states arisen together with non-greed, as expressed in a verse: "when a person, with a pure mind, sets something of their own aside and gives it away, this momentary cluster of wholesome aggregates is, in its entirety, established under the name of 'giving.'" Understood this way, the ground of merit constituted by giving is understood to bring about, as its result, great wealth in both this present life and future lives — the term "ground" here, denoting giving as the essential foundation or basis of the resulting merit, functions the same way one speaks of a "leaf-type" container or a "grass-type" dwelling, meaning a container made of leaves or a dwelling made of grass; moral discipline and meditative cultivation should be understood by the same explanatory pattern.

11. For Whose Benefit Is Giving Undertaken?

The treatise now asks, for whose benefit is an act of giving properly undertaken — one's own, another's, both, or neither? Four distinct cases emerge. When any ordinary being, whether still bound by desire and craving or already free from desire (but not yet a noble one), takes what belongs to them and offers it up to a caitya (a stūpa or shrine, which cannot itself receive or use the gift), this act of giving is said to benefit only oneself, since the giver alone accrues benefit from it, there being no other party who actually receives any benefit in return. When a noble one who has already become free from desire and craving gives to other sentient beings, apart from the specific kind of giving whose result is fixed to ripen within this very life, this act of giving is said to benefit only the other, since it is the recipient who obtains benefit from it, while the noble giver personally derives no further benefit for themselves, having already transcended the stage where such fruit could apply to them. When any ordinary being, whether bound by or free from desire and craving, gives what belongs to them to other sentient beings, this act of giving is said to benefit both self and other together. And when a noble one who has already become free from desire and craving offers something up to a caitya, apart from giving whose result ripens within this very life, this act of giving is said to benefit neither self nor other, since it is undertaken purely out of reverence and gratitude, with no expectation of benefit accruing to either party in the ordinary sense.

12. How the Giver's Own Character Shapes Giving's Result

Having established that giving generally brings about great wealth, the treatise now turns to what specifically accounts for the differences among the many varieties of wealth different givers eventually receive — explaining that these differences trace back to three distinct factors: differences in the giver, differences in the gift, and differences in the recipient field. Taking up the giver's role first: a giver who possesses distinguished qualities such as faith, moral discipline, and learning is said to differ from an ordinary giver in this respect, and it is this difference in the giver that produces a corresponding difference in the giving itself, which in turn produces a difference in the resulting fruit. A giver possessing such qualities, who properly carries out giving marked by reverence and the like, obtains four corresponding results in turn. If the giver gives with an attitude of genuine reverence and respect, this brings about being constantly held in high regard and respect by others in return. If the giver personally hands over the gift with his own hands (rather than delegating the act to someone else), this brings about the capacity to enjoy great wealth with genuine delight and ease. If the giver gives at the appropriate, timely occasion, this brings about receiving wealth precisely when needed, at the right time, without ever missing the proper moment. And if the giver gives without causing any harm or loss to others in the process, this brings about resulting wealth that remains secure — not seized by others, nor destroyed by fire or similar calamities.

13. How the Gift Itself, and the Recipient, Shape the Result

The treatise now turns to differences arising from the gift itself. Depending on whether the gift one offers is lacking or complete in color, fragrance, taste, and texture, one correspondingly obtains a lacking or complete result in beauty, reputation, popularity, and bodily comfort. That is, if the gift offered is complete in color, this brings about a beautiful bodily appearance as its result; if complete in fragrance, this brings about a good reputation that spreads in every direction, the way a fragrance itself spreads far and wide; if complete in taste, this brings about being loved and cherished by others, just as a delicious taste is loved by all who experience it; and if complete in texture, this brings about both a supple, comfortable body and a body capable of experiencing pleasurable sensations whenever desired, comparable to the legendary "jewel-woman" of a wheel-turning monarch's seven treasures. Where any of these four qualities is instead lacking in the gift, the corresponding result is diminished accordingly, since a deficient cause naturally yields a correspondingly deficient result — and it is likewise by the presence or absence of color, fragrance, and the other qualities that a gift comes to be classified as "different" from another in kind, this difference in the gift correspondingly bringing about a difference in both the substance of the giving itself and in its resulting fruit. Turning finally to differences arising from the recipient field: these differences trace to distinctions in the recipient's destiny, degree of suffering, and the debt of gratitude or virtue owed to them. Regarding destiny, the Blessed One himself states that giving to an animal yields a hundredfold return, while giving to an ordinary person who has broken the moral precepts yields a thousandfold return — showing that the destiny of the recipient itself scales the resulting merit dramatically. Regarding suffering, the sūtra on the seven kinds of giving grounded in ongoing conditions specifically names giving to a traveler, giving to one caring for the sick, giving to a monastery's garden and grove, giving one's regular provisions, and giving according to the season's cold, wind, or heat as especially meritorious, further stating that any faithful man or woman who fully carries out these seven kinds of giving grounded in ongoing conditions accumulates merit that cannot be measured or calculated. Regarding the debt of gratitude, giving to one's parents, teachers, or others to whom one owes gratitude yields especially great results, just as the Jātaka literature records of bears, deer, and other animals who repaid great kindness shown to them. And regarding virtue, a sūtra states that giving to one who upholds the moral precepts yields a hundred-million-fold return, and so on, scaling upward according to the recipient's degree of accomplished virtue.

14. The Best Kind of Giving, and Five Recipients Who Yield Boundless Fruit

The treatise now asks which single kind of giving is judged supreme among all forms of giving. The Blessed One states that when someone already free from affliction gives material resources to another who is likewise already free from affliction, this ranks as the very best among all gifts of material wealth. A bodhisattva's own giving, meanwhile, since it serves as the cause of universal benefit and happiness for all sentient beings, may not technically qualify as this specific "giving between the already-liberated," yet it too ranks as supreme among meritorious giving. Apart from these, there are eight further specific kinds of giving, the eighth of which is likewise judged supreme: (1) giving simply because someone close at hand happens to be asking, without particular reflection; (2) giving out of fear, seeing one's wealth about to be lost or destroyed and preferring to give it away rather than lose it entirely; (3) giving to repay a favor already received; (4) giving in the hope of receiving some favor in return; (5) giving simply because it is the customary practice handed down from one's ancestors; (6) giving in hope of being reborn in heaven; (7) giving in order to acquire a reputation for generosity; and (8) giving specifically to adorn one's own mind, to support one's own mind, to provide the resources for meditative practice, and to attain the supreme good — it is this eighth kind of giving, undertaken with such elevated spiritual purpose, that ranks as supreme among these eight. The treatise then identifies five further kinds of recipients for whom giving yields an immeasurable result even when the giver is not yet a noble one at all: giving to one's parents, giving to someone who is sick, giving to a teacher who expounds the Dharma, giving to a bodhisattva in his final rebirth before buddhahood, and — by clear extension, as the sūtra elsewhere similarly indicates — giving to a stream-enterer either on the verge of, or having already attained, that fruit yields boundless result as well. Among the four established categories of "field" (destiny-field, suffering-field, gratitude-field, and virtue-field), a Dharma-expounding teacher specifically belongs to the gratitude-field, since such a teacher serves as the great spiritual friend of the entire world, gives the eye of wisdom to those blinded by ignorance, reveals the world's true dangers and its true safety, and enables sentient beings to give rise to the uncontaminated Dharma-body within themselves — in short, an accomplished Dharma-teacher performs, to whatever degree, the very same work a buddha performs, and it is for this reason that giving to such a person brings about boundless result.

15. Six Factors of Karma's Weight, and "Increased" Versus Merely "Created" Karma

Wishing to understand the relative weight or lightness of different karmic acts, the treatise identifies six governing factors: the follow-up action, the field acted upon, the root act itself, the preparatory action, the volition that completes the karma-path, and the underlying disposition behind it — and whichever of these factors is comparatively weaker or stronger determines whether the resulting karma is of a correspondingly lower or higher grade. Some karma becomes especially heavy purely on account of its follow-up action, since it is this stage specifically that fixes the karma's eventual ripened result. Some karma becomes heavy on account of the field it is directed at. Some karma becomes heavy specifically through the power of the root act rather than any other factor — as when killing one's own father or mother is especially grave because of the field involved (one's parents), whereas theft and similar offenses become heavy through some other governing factor instead; this pattern should be reasoned through analogously case by case. When all six of these factors happen to be of the highest grade at once, the resulting karma is the very heaviest possible; conversely, when all six are of the lowest grade, the resulting karma is the very lightest; and where the six factors are mixed, falling somewhere between these extremes, the resulting karma is correspondingly of an intermediate weight, neither the heaviest nor the lightest. The treatise then explains a further important distinction found in the sūtras between two general kinds of karma: karma that is merely "created" and karma that has additionally become "increased" (that is, fully consummated and destined to ripen). What accounts for calling certain karma specifically "increased"? Five distinct conditions must all be met. First, the karma must have been carried out only after careful, deliberate reflection — not undertaken thoughtlessly or impulsively from the very start, nor carried out through hasty, unreflective intention. Second, the karma must have reached actual completion — since, among sentient beings generally, some fall into a bad rebirth through a single act of unwholesome conduct, while others require as many as ten such acts, or a single completed karma-path, or as many as ten; whichever specific measure of karma is sufficient to produce that rebirth, if that measure has not yet been reached, the karma counts merely as "created," not yet "increased," but once that threshold measure is reached, it does qualify for the name "increased." Third, the karma must be free from any remorse or corrective countermeasure applied afterward — that is, no regret and no deliberate antidote have been brought to bear against it. Fourth, the karma must have had accomplices — that is, it was carried out with unwholesome factors assisting and supporting it as companions. Fifth, the karma's ripening must be certain — that is, it is definitely destined to bring about its ripened result. Karma lacking these five specific conditions is called merely "created," in the sense already explained in earlier discussions of karma.

16. Giving Without a Receiver, and the Seed That Bears True Fruit Regardless of Soil

The treatise now returns to resolve a puzzle left open earlier in this fascicle: when one not yet free from desire and craving takes what belongs to oneself and offers it to a caitya (a stūpa or shrine), this act was said to benefit only oneself, since there is no other party present who actually receives the gift. If there is truly no recipient at all, how can merit possibly arise from such an act? The treatise explains that merit is actually of two distinct kinds: merit arising through relinquishing, and merit arising through receiving. "Merit through relinquishing" means merit that arises simply through a wholesome mind releasing material resources, requiring nothing further; "merit through receiving" means merit that arises only once the recipient field actually makes use of the gift offered. In offering material gifts to a caitya, although the "receiving" type of merit is absent, the "relinquishing" type of merit is still fully present. But if the caitya does not actually receive anything, from what source does this merit actually arise? And by what reasoning do we even know that merit's arising truly depends on receiving or not receiving in the first place? After all, giving something to someone incapable of benefiting from it at all would seem, on its face, to produce no benefit whatsoever. The treatise rejects this reasoning as inconclusive: if merit necessarily required benefiting another party to arise at all, then cultivating loving-kindness meditation, right view, and similar inwardly directed practices — none of which involve conferring any benefit on another being at all — would likewise have to fail to generate any merit, which is plainly false. Therefore, one must accept that venerating a caitya does indeed generate abundant merit, exactly as cultivating loving-kindness does: just as a person absorbed in loving-kindness meditation, even without any actual recipient present and without conferring benefit on anyone in particular, nonetheless generates immeasurable merit simply from their own mind, so too a person of genuine virtue, even though that virtuous being may have already passed into extinction in the past, still generates merit purely from their own mind when they express reverence and make offerings in remembrance. But is this offering and veneration not then simply wasted effort, given that no one is actually there to receive it? Not at all — since it is specifically the mind that initiates the karmic action that carries the decisive weight here, not whether a recipient happens to be present. Consider, by analogy, a person who wishes to harm an enemy: even if that enemy has already died, the aggressor, still harboring hostile intent, may nonetheless give rise to all manner of harmful bodily and verbal karma directed at the deceased, and this generates substantial demerit — not merely from the hostile intention alone, but through the actual bodily and verbal karma that intention gives rise to. In just the same way, even though a great teacher may have already passed away, one who expresses reverence and makes offerings in remembrance, giving rise to actual bodily and verbal karma in doing so, generates substantial merit — not merely from the reverential intention alone. A further question then arises: if one plants the seed of a virtuous act of giving in a wholesome recipient field, might one expect a corresponding desirable result, but if one gives, however virtuously intentioned, to an unwholesome or corrupt recipient field, ought the result then to be undesirable instead? The treatise firmly rejects this reasoning as well. Just as it is directly observed in the world that a seed's fruit always accords faithfully with the seed itself, regardless of the specific field it happens to be planted in — a bitter gourd seed always produces a bitter gourd, tasting extremely bitter, while a sugarcane seed always produces sugarcane, tasting extremely sweet, this outcome never being reversed by any influence of the soil itself — so too, even when a giver directs their generosity toward a corrupt recipient field, so long as their own mind genuinely intends to benefit that recipient, the seed of giving they plant will still bring about only a desirable result, never an undesirable one, though the specific fault or deficiency of that corrupt field may still cause the resulting fruit to be somewhat diminished in quantity, or in especially severe cases to fail to arise at all.

17. What Makes a Precept Genuinely Pure

Having completed its treatment of the giving-type ground for merit, the treatise turns to the precept-type ground. All unwholesome physical conduct is called a "root offense" (犯戒), and it is specifically root offenses of this kind — the inherent wrongs already discussed extensively in earlier fascicles — that are meant by this term here. "Proscribed" conduct, by contrast, refers to actions such as eating at an improper time, which, though not inherently wrong in themselves, are nonetheless restricted by the Buddha for the sake of protecting the Dharma and for specific reasons concerning particular beings; a precept-holder who violates such a restriction is likewise said to commit an "offense," though the term "proscribed" is specifically used to distinguish this from a genuine root offense. Refraining from both root offenses and proscribed conduct together is what is properly called "moral discipline" (戒), and each of these two categories exists in two forms — manifested and unmanifested — since both consist essentially of bodily and verbal karma. Having briefly explained the essential nature of moral discipline in this way, the treatise now specifies exactly what qualities make a set of precepts genuinely "pure" as opposed to impure. Four specific qualities must be present. First, the precepts must not be undermined by any root offense, "root offense" here referring to the unwholesome physical conduct already described. Second, the precepts must not be undermined by the causes of such offenses — namely, the afflictions of greed and the like, together with their secondary afflictions. Third, the precepts must be supported by an appropriate remedial practice, such as the four foundations of mindfulness, which is capable of counteracting both root offenses and their underlying causes. Fourth, the precepts must be grounded in and directed toward cessation — that is, oriented toward nirvāṇa itself, dedicating one's practice to that ultimate cessation rather than toward some merely superior future rebirth. The word "and the like" in the summary verse signals that other accounts of purity exist as well: some teachers hold that precepts are pure through five distinct causes instead — purity in the root itself, purity in one's supporting companions, freedom from harmful intent, being sustained by mindfulness, and dedication toward tranquil cessation. Other teachers offer yet another classification, dividing precepts into four kinds by the motivation that leads someone to take them up in the first place: precepts taken up out of fear — that is, out of fear of losing one's livelihood, of acquiring a bad reputation, of facing punishment, or of falling into a bad rebirth, and so receiving and guarding one's moral discipline defensively; precepts taken up out of aspiration — that is, out of craving for a superior worldly position, abundant wealth, honor, or renown, and so receiving and upholding pure precepts as a means to that end; precepts that accord with the factors of awakening — that is, precepts received and upheld specifically for the sake of seeking liberation and cultivating right view; and, finally, genuinely pure precepts — meaning specifically uncontaminated moral discipline, since this kind alone is permanently free from the defilement of karma and affliction altogether.

18. Meditative Cultivation, and Which Ground Leads to Heaven or to Liberation

The treatise now turns to the third and final ground for merit: meditative cultivation. What, precisely, constitutes wholesome concentration, the essential nature of this cultivation-type merit? It is samādhi itself, together with its concurrent mental factors. Why is this specifically called "cultivation"? Because it "perfumes" the mind — that is, wholesome concentration is exceptionally capable of perfuming the mental continuum, gradually transforming it into an accumulation of genuine virtue, in the same way a flower's fragrance perfumes sesame seeds pressed alongside it, leaving its scent behind long after the flower itself is gone. It is for this reason that this category alone, among the three grounds for merit, receives the specific name "cultivation." Having previously explained that giving generally brings about great wealth, the treatise now asks what result the remaining two grounds — moral discipline and cultivation — specifically bring about respectively. Moral discipline brings about rebirth in the heavens; cultivation brings about liberation itself. The word "supreme" in the verse indicates that this correspondence is being stated only in terms of each ground's most distinguished or paradigmatic result, not as an exclusive claim — that is, giving is also capable of bringing about heavenly rebirth, yet it is specifically with reference to moral discipline that heavenly rebirth is spoken of as the paradigmatic case; likewise, upholding moral discipline is also capable of bringing about the fruit of liberation, yet it is specifically with reference to cultivation that liberation is spoken of as the paradigmatic case. A sūtra further identifies four kinds of persons capable of generating what is called "great brahma-merit": first, one who, in a place where no such structure has ever previously existed, builds a stūpa to enshrine and venerate the Tathāgata's relics; second, one who constructs a monastery and provides its four requisites in support of the saṅgha of the four directions; third, a disciple of the Buddha who, having caused a schism, is then able to reconcile the divided community once more; and fourth, one who universally cultivates loving-kindness and the like toward all sentient beings without exception. As for the precise measure or quantity of this "brahma-merit," the elder teachers of the tradition explain it this way: whatever merit is capable of bringing about a full eon's rebirth in the heavens, experiencing all the pleasures of that realm, constitutes a single unit of this merit-measure, since the pleasure experienced during that eon corresponds exactly to a full eon's lifespan among the gods of the Brahma-attendant heaven. As a verse from another tradition's textual transmission likewise states: "a person of faith and right view, who cultivates the ten supreme practices, thereby generates brahma-merit, bringing about an eon's heavenly bliss." The Vaibhāṣika masters, meanwhile, explain this measure of merit as being identical to the measure of merit already discussed earlier, in the section on cultivating the karma of the marvelous marks — the word "and the like" in the verse being included specifically to indicate that these differing accounts exist side by side.

19. Dharma-Giving, and the Three Portions of Wholesome Dharmas

Having discussed material giving at length, the treatise now briefly addresses "Dharma-giving." What is Dharma-giving? It is teaching sūtras and other scriptures faithfully, exactly as they are, to sentient beings, with an uncorrupted mind, so that they come to a correct understanding — this alone is properly called Dharma-giving. Consequently, one who teaches the Dharma with a distorted mind, or with a mind corrupted by the desire for personal gain, reputation, or honor and praise, thereby harms both themselves and others rather than generating any great merit at all, since such teaching, whatever its outward form, no longer qualifies as genuine giving of the Dharma. Having already explained the three grounds for meritorious deeds separately, the treatise now turns to explain a related sūtra teaching on three "portions" of wholesome dharmas. The portion conducive to merit refers to wholesome dharmas capable of bringing about a desirable worldly result. The portion conducive to liberation refers to wholesome dharmas definitely capable of bringing about the fruit of nirvāṇa; once this specific kind of wholesome root has arisen within someone, that person is said to already possess, deep within their own being, "the dharma of nirvāṇa" — meaning that their eventual liberation has, in effect, already been set irreversibly in motion. A telling sign marks such a person: upon hearing that saṃsāra is fraught with fault, that all dharmas are without self, and that nirvāṇa possesses genuine virtue, their bodily hairs stand on end, and they weep tears of grief, moved to their very core — and one should understand, from these outward signs, that they have already planted the wholesome root conducive to liberation, in the same way one who sees new sprouts growing in a field freshly watered by rain knows with confidence that seeds must already have been present, hidden within the soil, before the rain ever fell. The portion conducive to penetrating insight, finally, refers to wholesome dharmas capable of drawing one, in the near term, toward the actual result of the noble path — this being specifically the four stages of the "aids to penetration" (warmth, summit, forbearance, and supreme worldly dharma), which the treatise will explain at greater length later on.

The physical description of someone who has planted the "liberation-conducive" wholesome root — hair standing on end, weeping upon hearing the Dharma's core teachings — is a vivid, recurring motif across Buddhist literature more broadly (often associated with the specific experience of hearing profound teaching for the first time); we have rendered it plainly rather than adding interpretive gloss, since the text's own rain-and-sprout analogy already makes its intended point (a visible sign indicating an invisible, prior cause) without needing further elaboration from us.

20. Five Foundational Activities, and a Closing Glossary — Fascicle 18 Complete

The treatise closes this extended discussion of karma with a brief technical side inquiry into five foundational human activities commonly spoken of in the world — writing, seal-making (or engraving), calculation, literary composition, and enumeration — asking exactly which aggregates constitute the essential nature of each. All five arise from activity performed correctly, in accordance with proper method, through one of the three doors of karma — body, speech, or mind — together with whatever mental factors initiate that karma, such as perception and the rest, arising as appropriate. Writing and seal-making have, as their essential nature, the aforementioned bodily karma together with the aggregates that initiate it. Calculation and literary composition have, as their essential nature, the aforementioned verbal karma together with the aggregates that initiate it. Enumeration, finally, has, as its essential nature, the aforementioned mental karma together with the aggregates that initiate it, since it is specifically through the deliberate operation of mental volition that counting and calculation of this kind are carried out. Having addressed this, the treatise offers a final, compact glossary of alternate names for several categories of dharmas already discussed at length. Wholesome, uncontaminated dharmas are also called "excellent," since they are free of all fault. Defiled dharmas are also called "possessing fault," "obstructive," and "inferior" — matching this pattern, wholesome dharmas are elsewhere similarly implied to be "faultless," "unobstructive," and "superior," though the verse, having already established this parallel, does not separately spell out each corresponding wholesome term. Conditioned wholesome dharmas are also called "worthy of cultivation," since repeated practice allows them to increase and grow — by the same parallel logic, dharmas other than these are, correspondingly, "not worthy of cultivation." An important question follows: why are unconditioned dharmas not themselves called "worthy of cultivation"? Because they cannot be repeatedly cultivated so as to increase or grow at all, cultivation properly referring only to the accumulation of a result through repeated practice, which by definition cannot apply to what is unconditioned and hence never arises or increases to begin with. Finally, liberation — nirvāṇa itself — is also called "unsurpassed," since there exists no single dharma anywhere capable of surpassing nirvāṇa, it being wholesome, permanent, and transcending every other dharma whatsoever; by this same parallel logic, it follows that every dharma other than nirvāṇa does, correspondingly, admit of something superior to it. With this closing glossary, Fascicle 18 of the Abhidharmakośa comes to an end.
