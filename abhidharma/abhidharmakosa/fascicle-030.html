The Burden-Bearer

Fascicle 30 — the last of the entire thirty-fascicle Abhidharmakośa — continues Chapter 9 (破執我品第九之二, Refuting the Self) from where fascicle 29 left off. It opens with a hard scriptural challenge: a sūtra in which the Buddha himself speaks of "the bearer of the heavy burden," language that seems to demand exactly the kind of persisting subject the treatise has spent the previous fascicle arguing against. From there the chapter moves through the Buddha's refusal to answer a whole class of ill-formed questions about the self and the world, the celebrated tigress-and-cub verses, and an extended, careful account of how memory, cognition, and karmic continuity all work without requiring any real, persisting self behind them — closing, at the very end, with the closing verses of the entire treatise.

1. The Burden-Bearer Sūtra: Doesn't "Bearer" Require a Real Person?

Chapter 9 continues directly from fascicle 29 with the hardest scriptural challenge yet: a sūtra in which the Buddha himself speaks of "the bearer of the heavy burden" — language that seems, on its face, to require exactly the kind of real, persisting subject the treatise has spent the entire previous fascicle arguing does not exist.

Chinese Source

破執我品第九之二 若唯五取蘊名補特伽羅，何故世尊作如是說：吾今為汝說諸重擔、取捨重擔、荷重擔者。何緣於此佛不應說？不應重擔即名能荷。所以者何？曾未見故。不可說事亦不應說。所以者何？亦未見故。又取重擔應非蘊攝，重擔自取，曾未見故。然經說愛名取擔者，既即蘊攝，荷者應然，即於諸蘊立數取趣。然恐謂此補特伽羅是不可說常住實有，故此經後佛自釋言：但隨世俗，說此具壽有如是名，乃至廣說。如上所引人經文句，為令了此補特伽羅，可說無常非實有性。即五取蘊自相逼害得重擔名。前前剎那引後後故名為荷者，故非實有補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Chapter 9, Refuting the Self, Part 2. If the pudgala is nothing but the five aggregates of clinging, why did the Blessed One speak thus: "I shall now expound for you the heavy burden, the taking up and laying down of the burden, and the bearer of the burden"? Why should the Buddha not have spoken in this way? The burden itself should not be called the very thing that bears it. Why is this? Because this has never been observed. Nor should the Buddha have spoken of it as an "inexpressible" matter. Why is this? Because this too has never been observed. Moreover, the taking up of the burden should then not be included within the aggregates, since the burden taking itself up has likewise never been observed. Yet the sūtra states that craving is what is called "the taking up of the burden" — and since craving is itself included within the aggregates, the bearer should likewise be so included, and it is precisely with reference to the aggregates that the term "repeated-taker-of-existences" is established. Yet, fearing that one might suppose this pudgala to be an inexpressible, permanently abiding, truly real entity, the Buddha himself, later in this very sūtra, explains: "this is stated only in accordance with worldly convention — this venerable one has such-and-such a name" — and so on, stated fully. As with the passage from the Sūtra on the Person cited earlier, this is meant to make clear that the pudgala can be said to be impermanent, and not of a genuinely real nature. It is precisely the five aggregates of clinging, oppressing and afflicting themselves by their own inherent nature, that receive the name "burden"; and it is because each earlier moment draws forth the one that follows that they receive the name "bearer" — hence there is no genuinely real pudgala.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the treatise's resolution here as depending entirely on a distinction already established across this project's earlier fascicles on the mental factors: craving (愛) is itself one of the aggregates — specifically, part of the formations aggregate — so when the sūtra names craving as "the taking up of the burden," it is not introducing some entity outside the aggregates to do the taking, but pointing to one process internal to the aggregates themselves. "Bearer," by the same logic, names nothing more than the aggregates' own self-perpetuating momentum, each moment's burden drawing forth the next moment's burden in turn — the very same causal succession this project has seen used throughout this chapter to explain apparent continuity without a persisting subject.

We should also flag the Buddha's own explanatory gloss, quoted here as decisive, as the same move the treatise relied on in the previous fascicle's citation of the Sūtra on the Person: whenever a sūtra's vivid, agent-implying language (a "bearer," someone who "sees," someone who "remembers") might be misread as evidence for a real self, the tradition holds that the Buddha's own later clarification — "this is stated only in accordance with worldly convention" — settles the matter, and should govern how the vivid language is read, rather than the vivid language being taken to override the explicit clarification.

2. Doesn't Denying "Spontaneously-Born Beings" Prove the Pudgala Is Real?

The Vātsīputrīya tries a new scriptural angle: a sūtra condemns denying the existence of spontaneously-born sentient beings as wrong view, so surely the pudgala must be real after all. The treatise turns this citation around, showing it actually supports the aggregate-continuum reading, then closes with a technical objection about which path could even abandon this supposed wrong view.

Chinese Source

補特伽羅定應實有，以契經說諸有撥無化生有情邪見攝故。誰言無有化生有情？如佛所言：我說有故。謂蘊相續能往後世，不由胎卵濕名化生有情。撥此為無，故邪見攝。化生諸蘊，理實有故。又許此邪見謗補特伽羅。汝等應言，是何所斷？見修所斷。理並不然，補特伽羅非諦攝故，邪見不應修所斷故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The pudgala must definitely be genuinely real, the Vātsīputrīya objects, since a sūtra states that whoever denies the existence of spontaneously-born sentient beings falls into the category of wrong view. Who says there are no spontaneously-born sentient beings? As the Buddha himself states, we affirm that they exist — namely, the aggregates' continuum, capable of proceeding on to a future life without being born from a womb, an egg, or moisture, is what is called a "spontaneously-born sentient being." Denying this is what falls into wrong view, since the aggregates involved in spontaneous birth are, in principle, genuinely real. Moreover, if this wrong view of denying spontaneous birth specifically slanders the pudgala, then you should tell us: by which path is it abandoned? Neither of the two answers actually works: since the pudgala is not included among the Four Truths, it cannot be abandoned by the path of seeing, which operates specifically by seeing the Truths; and wrong view is never something abandoned by the path of cultivation alone, as you yourselves hold elsewhere.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the treatise's handling of "spontaneously-born sentient being" as a clean demonstration of a strategy this project has now seen repeated many times across this chapter: rather than disputing the sūtra's authority or its content, the treatise simply supplies its own already-established reading of what the term actually refers to — the aggregate-continuum's capacity to proceed into a next life without the mediation of womb, egg, or moisture — which affirms everything the sūtra actually says while requiring nothing beyond what the treatise has already argued exists. The Vātsīputrīya's citation, meant to force a real pudgala into the picture, ends up doing no such work once its terms are properly unpacked.

We should also flag the closing technical objection as a smaller, more specialized point worth noting for readers tracking this project's earlier fascicles on the path structure: the path of seeing operates specifically by seeing each of the Four Truths for the first time, so anything it abandons must be classifiable under one of those Truths — and the pudgala, on the Vātsīputrīya's own telling, is explicitly not classifiable in this way, being "inexpressible." This leaves the Vātsīputrīya with no coherent path by which the supposed wrong view of denying the pudgala could ever actually be abandoned, a structural gap the treatise treats as symptomatic of the position's incoherence rather than a mere technicality.

3. Does "One Pudgala Is Born" Prove It's Conditioned? The Sūtra on Ultimate Emptiness

Two further scriptural wordings are tested: the numeral "one" applied to the pudgala, and the verb "born" applied to it. Both turn out to be ordinary figures of speech rather than evidence of a real, unified, newly-arising entity — and the section closes with two of the treatise's most decisive citations against any real "agent" behind karma at all.

Chinese Source

若謂經說有一補特伽羅生在世間，應非蘊者，亦不應理，此於總中假說一故，如世間說一麻一米一聚一言。或補特伽羅應許有為攝，以契經說生世間故。非此言生如蘊新起，依何義說生在世間？依此今時取別蘊義，如世間說能祠者生、記論者生，取明論故。又如世說有苾芻生、有外道生，取儀式故。或如世說有老者生，有病者生，取別位故。佛已遮故，此救不成。如勝義空契經中說：有業有異熟，作者不可得。謂能捨此蘊，及能續餘蘊，唯除法假。故佛已遮。頗勒具那契經亦說：我終不說有能取者。故定無一補特伽羅能於世間取捨諸蘊。

Ru-Yi Translation

If it is said that, since a sūtra states there is "one pudgala born into the world," the pudgala should therefore not simply be the aggregates — this too is unreasonable, since the word "one" is conventionally applied to a collective totality, just as ordinary speech says "one sesame," "one grain of rice," "one heap," or "one word." Or else, if you insist on taking "born" literally, the pudgala should then have to be granted as included among the conditioned dharmas, since a sūtra states it is "born into the world." This word "born" does not mean newly arising, the way the aggregates newly arise, they reply — on what basis, then, is it said to be "born into the world"? It is with reference to presently taking on a distinct set of aggregates that this is meant — just as ordinary speech says "a sacrificer is born" or "a grammarian is born," meaning that one has taken up the Vedic scriptures of sacrifice or grammar. And just as ordinary speech says "a monk is born" or "a heterodox ascetic is born," meaning that one has taken up a particular set of observances. Or just as ordinary speech says "an old person is born" or "a sick person is born," meaning that one has taken on a distinct stage or condition. This defense does not succeed, since the Buddha has already ruled it out. As the Sūtra on Ultimate Emptiness states: there is karma, there is karmic ripening, but no agent can be found — that is, no one can be found who lays down these aggregates and takes up other aggregates, except in the sense of a conventional designation applied to dharmas. Hence the Buddha has already ruled this out. The Sūtra to Phalguna likewise states: "I have never once said there is one who grasps." Hence it is certain that there is no single pudgala capable of taking up and laying down the aggregates within the world.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the Vātsīputrīya's proposed reading of "born" — modeled on everyday expressions like "a sacrificer is born" or "an old person is born," meaning one has simply taken on a new role or condition rather than newly come into existence — as a genuinely clever move, since it lets them accept the sūtra's language without conceding that the pudgala is conditioned and impermanent. The treatise does not dispute that this is a coherent way ordinary language works; it instead cuts the defense off at the root by citing the Sūtra on Ultimate Emptiness, which states directly and unconditionally that no agent can be found who lays down one set of aggregates and takes up another — foreclosing any reading, clever or otherwise, that would smuggle a real "taker-on" of new aggregates back into the picture.

We should also flag the Sūtra to Phalguna's one-line citation — "I have never once said there is one who grasps" — as functioning here almost as a capstone, closing off the whole line of argument this section and its predecessor have pursued: whether the Vātsīputrīya's evidence is the word "one," the word "born," or the general grammar of agency implicit in "bearer" and "taker," the Buddha is cited as having directly and explicitly disclaimed positing any such grasping agent, leaving the treatise's opponents without scriptural ground left to stand on for this particular line of defense.

4. Why the Buddha Refuses Ill-Formed Questions: The Tortoise's Fur

The treatise finishes dismantling the "sacrificer is born" analogies from the previous section, presses a technical parallel about derived form and the four great elements, and then arrives at one of this chapter's most quotable images: why the Buddha would never answer whether the "life-force" is identical to or different from the body — because the question itself presupposes something that does not exist, like asking whether a tortoise's fur is soft or coarse.

Chinese Source

又汝所引祠者等生，其體是何而能喻此？若執是我，彼不極成。若心心所，彼念念滅、新新生故，取捨不成。若許是身，亦如心等。又如明等與身有異，蘊亦應異補特伽羅。老病二身各與前別，數論轉變如前已遣，故彼所引為喻不成。又許蘊生非數取趣，則定許此異蘊及常。又此唯一，蘊體有五，寧不說此與蘊有異？大種有四，造色唯一，寧言造色不異大種？是彼宗過。何謂彼宗？諸計造色即大種論。設如彼見，應作是質：如諸造色即四大種，亦應即五蘊立補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, as for the examples you cited — "a sacrificer is born" and the rest — what exactly is their own substance, such that they could serve as a genuine analogy for this? If you hold that it refers to a self, this itself is not an established, mutually agreed premise. If it refers to mind and mental factors, then, since these perish moment by moment and arise anew each moment, the notion of "taking up and laying down" does not hold. If you grant it refers to the body, this is likewise just like the case of mind. Moreover, just as "knowledge of the Vedic scriptures" is different from the body itself that takes on this knowledge, so too the aggregates should be different from the pudgala. The bodies of "the old" and "the sick" are each different from what came before them; and the Sāṃkhya doctrine of transformation has already been refuted earlier. Hence the examples you have cited fail to serve as genuine analogies. Moreover, if you grant that it is merely the aggregates that are born, not the repeated-taker-of-existences itself, then you must definitely grant that this pudgala is different from the aggregates and permanent. Moreover, this pudgala is said to be single, while the substance of the aggregates numbers five — why, then, is it not said that this differs from the aggregates? The great elements number four, while derived form is single, yet it is not said that derived form is thereby different from the great elements — this is a fault specific to your own school's position. What is meant by "your own school's position" here? It refers to the theory, held by some, that derived form is simply identical with the great elements themselves. Granting that view for the sake of argument, one should press this challenge: just as derived form is said to be simply identical with the four great elements, then, by the same logic, the pudgala should likewise have to be simply identical with the five aggregates.

Chinese Source

若補特伽羅即諸蘊者，世尊何不記命者即身？觀能問者阿世耶故。問者執一內用士夫體實非虛名為命者，依此問佛與身一異。此都無故，一異不成，如何與身可記一異？如不可記龜毛䩕軟，古昔諸師已解斯結。

Ru-Yi Translation

If the pudgala is simply identical with the aggregates, why did the Blessed One not directly declare that the life-force is simply identical with the body? This is because he took into account the underlying intention of the one asking the question. The questioner held that there exists a single, genuinely real, inwardly-functioning personal agent — not a mere empty name — calling it the "life-force," and it was with this in mind that he asked the Buddha whether it was identical to or different from the body. Since this presupposed real life-force does not exist at all, neither "identical" nor "different" can be established — how, then, could the Buddha possibly declare it identical to or different from the body? This is just like how one cannot declare whether a tortoise's fur is soft or coarse — the ancient masters have already resolved this very knot.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "derived form is identical with the great elements" reference as a technical internal debate this passage assumes rather than explains — a minority position, held by some within the broader Sarvāstivāda tradition, that derived material qualities (color, smell, taste, tangibility) are not genuinely distinct from the four great elements (earth, water, fire, wind) that give rise to them, but simply identical with them. The treatise does not endorse this position itself; it uses it only as a hypothetical granted for the sake of argument, to show that even on this rival view's own terms, consistency would require treating the pudgala as flatly identical with the aggregates — not the "neither same nor different" the Vātsīputrīya actually wants.

We should also flag the "tortoise's fur" image as the clearest possible statement of this chapter's governing principle about the Buddha's silences: a question is not always answerable simply because it is grammatically well-formed. "Is the tortoise's fur soft or coarse?" presupposes that a tortoise has fur at all, and no answer to the soft-or-coarse question can be given until that false presupposition is corrected. "Is the life-force identical to or different from the body?" works the same way, presupposing a real, unified "life-force" that, on the treatise's analysis, simply does not exist — so the Buddha's refusal to answer is not an evasion or a mystery, but the only coherent response available once the question's own hidden premise is seen to be empty. This principle will recur across several of the sections immediately following, applied to a whole family of questions the Buddha traditionally declined to answer.

5. The Elder Nāgasena and King Piṇḍola: The Mango Trees That Weren't There

The treatise illustrates the tortoise-fur principle with a memorable narrative: an exchange between the elder Nāgasena and King Piṇḍola, in which the elder turns the king's own trick question back on him with a question about mango trees that don't exist — before addressing a final, natural objection: if there is truly no life-force, why didn't the Buddha simply say so outright?

Chinese Source

昔有大德名曰龍軍，三明六通具八解脫。于時有一畢隣陀王，至大德所作如是說：我今來意欲請所疑。然諸沙門性好多語，尊能直答，我當請問。大德受請。王即問言：命者與身為一為異？大德答言：此不應記。王言：豈不先有要耶？今何異言不答所問？大德質曰：我欲問疑。然諸國王性好多語，王能直答，我當發問。王便受教。大德問言：大王宮中諸菴羅樹所生果味為酢為甘。王言：宮中本無此樹。大德復責：先無要耶？今何異言不答所問。王言：宮內此樹既無，寧可答言果味甘酢？大德誨曰：命者亦無，如何可言與身一異？佛何不說命者都無？亦觀問者阿世耶故。問者或於諸蘊相續謂為命者，依之發問。世尊若答命者都無，彼墮邪見，故佛不說。彼未能了緣起理故，非受正法器，不為說假有。理必應爾，世尊說故。

Ru-Yi Translation

In ancient times, there was a great elder named Nāgasena, who possessed the three insights, the six supernormal powers, and the eight liberations in full. At that time, there was a King Piṇḍola who came to the great elder and spoke thus: "My intention in coming here is to ask about my doubts. But ascetics, by nature, are fond of much talk — if you, Venerable One, are able to answer directly, I shall pose my question." The great elder accepted this condition. The king then asked: "Is the life-force identical to or different from the body?" The great elder replied: "This should not be answered directly." The king said: "Was there not an agreement beforehand? Why do you now speak otherwise and fail to answer the question asked?" The great elder countered: "I too wish to ask about a doubt. But kings, by nature, are fond of much talk — if you, O King, are able to answer directly, I shall pose my question." The king accepted this in turn. The great elder asked: "The mango trees in the Great King's palace — is the fruit they produce sour or sweet?" The king replied: "There are no such trees in my palace at all." The great elder countered again: "Was there not an agreement beforehand? Why do you now speak otherwise and fail to answer the question asked?" The king said: "Since there is no such tree at all within my palace, how could I possibly answer whether its fruit's flavor is sweet or sour?" The great elder instructed him: "The life-force is likewise nonexistent — how, then, could one possibly say whether it is identical to or different from the body?" Why, then, did the Buddha not simply state that the life-force does not exist at all? This too is because he took into account the underlying intention of the one asking. The questioner may have been referring, by "life-force," to the aggregates' own continuum, asking his question with this in mind. If the Blessed One had answered that the life-force does not exist at all, without qualification, the questioner would have fallen into wrong view — hence the Buddha did not state this. Since such a questioner has not yet been able to understand the principle of dependent origination, and is not yet a fit vessel for receiving the true Dharma, the Buddha did not explain to him the doctrine of conventional existence. This must necessarily be the reason, since the Blessed One himself has stated as much elsewhere.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the elder Nāgasena's mango-tree counter-question as an elegant piece of Socratic pedagogy in its own right: rather than simply lecturing the king about why his question is malformed, Nāgasena engineers a situation in which the king discovers the principle himself — the king cannot answer whether a nonexistent tree's fruit is sweet or sour, and the moment he articulates why not ("since there is no such tree at all... how could I possibly answer?"), he has already supplied, in his own words, the exact reasoning that dissolves his own original question about the life-force. This is a considerably more vivid, memorable illustration of the tortoise-fur principle from the previous section than abstract argument alone could provide.

We should also flag the final point — that the Buddha declined to simply say "the life-force does not exist" outright — as adding a layer of pastoral sensitivity to what might otherwise read as pure logical technicality: the treatise's concern is that a listener who has not yet grasped dependent origination might hear a flat denial of "life-force" and mistake it for a denial of the conventionally real aggregate-continuum itself, tipping into the more dangerous wrong view of nihilism rather than correcting the more common wrong view of a real self. The Buddha's silence, on this reading, is calibrated not just to the truth of the matter but to what a given listener is actually capable of correctly hearing.
