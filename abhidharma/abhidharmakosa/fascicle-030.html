The Burden-Bearer

Fascicle 30 — the last of the entire thirty-fascicle Abhidharmakośa — continues Chapter 9 (破執我品第九之二, Refuting the Self) from where fascicle 29 left off. It opens with a hard scriptural challenge: a sūtra in which the Buddha himself speaks of "the bearer of the heavy burden," language that seems to demand exactly the kind of persisting subject the treatise has spent the previous fascicle arguing against. From there the chapter moves through the Buddha's refusal to answer a whole class of ill-formed questions about the self and the world, the celebrated tigress-and-cub verses, and an extended, careful account of how memory, cognition, and karmic continuity all work without requiring any real, persisting self behind them — closing, at the very end, with the closing verses of the entire treatise.

1. The Burden-Bearer Sūtra: Doesn't "Bearer" Require a Real Person?

Chapter 9 continues directly from fascicle 29 with the hardest scriptural challenge yet: a sūtra in which the Buddha himself speaks of "the bearer of the heavy burden" — language that seems, on its face, to require exactly the kind of real, persisting subject the treatise has spent the entire previous fascicle arguing does not exist.

Chinese Source

破執我品第九之二 若唯五取蘊名補特伽羅，何故世尊作如是說：吾今為汝說諸重擔、取捨重擔、荷重擔者。何緣於此佛不應說？不應重擔即名能荷。所以者何？曾未見故。不可說事亦不應說。所以者何？亦未見故。又取重擔應非蘊攝，重擔自取，曾未見故。然經說愛名取擔者，既即蘊攝，荷者應然，即於諸蘊立數取趣。然恐謂此補特伽羅是不可說常住實有，故此經後佛自釋言：但隨世俗，說此具壽有如是名，乃至廣說。如上所引人經文句，為令了此補特伽羅，可說無常非實有性。即五取蘊自相逼害得重擔名。前前剎那引後後故名為荷者，故非實有補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Chapter 9, Refuting the Self, Part 2. If the pudgala is nothing but the five aggregates of clinging, why did the Blessed One speak thus: "I shall now expound for you the heavy burden, the taking up and laying down of the burden, and the bearer of the burden"? Why should the Buddha not have spoken in this way? The burden itself should not be called the very thing that bears it. Why is this? Because this has never been observed. Nor should the Buddha have spoken of it as an "inexpressible" matter. Why is this? Because this too has never been observed. Moreover, the taking up of the burden should then not be included within the aggregates, since the burden taking itself up has likewise never been observed. Yet the sūtra states that craving is what is called "the taking up of the burden" — and since craving is itself included within the aggregates, the bearer should likewise be so included, and it is precisely with reference to the aggregates that the term "repeated-taker-of-existences" is established. Yet, fearing that one might suppose this pudgala to be an inexpressible, permanently abiding, truly real entity, the Buddha himself, later in this very sūtra, explains: "this is stated only in accordance with worldly convention — this venerable one has such-and-such a name" — and so on, stated fully. As with the passage from the Sūtra on the Person cited earlier, this is meant to make clear that the pudgala can be said to be impermanent, and not of a genuinely real nature. It is precisely the five aggregates of clinging, oppressing and afflicting themselves by their own inherent nature, that receive the name "burden"; and it is because each earlier moment draws forth the one that follows that they receive the name "bearer" — hence there is no genuinely real pudgala.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the treatise's resolution here as depending entirely on a distinction already established across this project's earlier fascicles on the mental factors: craving (愛) is itself one of the aggregates — specifically, part of the formations aggregate — so when the sūtra names craving as "the taking up of the burden," it is not introducing some entity outside the aggregates to do the taking, but pointing to one process internal to the aggregates themselves. "Bearer," by the same logic, names nothing more than the aggregates' own self-perpetuating momentum, each moment's burden drawing forth the next moment's burden in turn — the very same causal succession this project has seen used throughout this chapter to explain apparent continuity without a persisting subject.

We should also flag the Buddha's own explanatory gloss, quoted here as decisive, as the same move the treatise relied on in the previous fascicle's citation of the Sūtra on the Person: whenever a sūtra's vivid, agent-implying language (a "bearer," someone who "sees," someone who "remembers") might be misread as evidence for a real self, the tradition holds that the Buddha's own later clarification — "this is stated only in accordance with worldly convention" — settles the matter, and should govern how the vivid language is read, rather than the vivid language being taken to override the explicit clarification.
