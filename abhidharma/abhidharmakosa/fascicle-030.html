The Burden-Bearer

Fascicle 30 — the last of the entire thirty-fascicle Abhidharmakośa — continues Chapter 9 (破執我品第九之二, Refuting the Self) from where fascicle 29 left off. It opens with a hard scriptural challenge: a sūtra in which the Buddha himself speaks of "the bearer of the heavy burden," language that seems to demand exactly the kind of persisting subject the treatise has spent the previous fascicle arguing against. From there the chapter moves through the Buddha's refusal to answer a whole class of ill-formed questions about the self and the world, the celebrated tigress-and-cub verses, and an extended, careful account of how memory, cognition, and karmic continuity all work without requiring any real, persisting self behind them — closing, at the very end, with the closing verses of the entire treatise.

1. The Burden-Bearer Sūtra: Doesn't "Bearer" Require a Real Person?

Chapter 9 continues directly from fascicle 29 with the hardest scriptural challenge yet: a sūtra in which the Buddha himself speaks of "the bearer of the heavy burden" — language that seems, on its face, to require exactly the kind of real, persisting subject the treatise has spent the entire previous fascicle arguing does not exist.

Chinese Source

破執我品第九之二 若唯五取蘊名補特伽羅，何故世尊作如是說：吾今為汝說諸重擔、取捨重擔、荷重擔者。何緣於此佛不應說？不應重擔即名能荷。所以者何？曾未見故。不可說事亦不應說。所以者何？亦未見故。又取重擔應非蘊攝，重擔自取，曾未見故。然經說愛名取擔者，既即蘊攝，荷者應然，即於諸蘊立數取趣。然恐謂此補特伽羅是不可說常住實有，故此經後佛自釋言：但隨世俗，說此具壽有如是名，乃至廣說。如上所引人經文句，為令了此補特伽羅，可說無常非實有性。即五取蘊自相逼害得重擔名。前前剎那引後後故名為荷者，故非實有補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Chapter 9, Refuting the Self, Part 2. If the pudgala is nothing but the five aggregates of clinging, why did the Blessed One speak thus: "I shall now expound for you the heavy burden, the taking up and laying down of the burden, and the bearer of the burden"? Why should the Buddha not have spoken in this way? The burden itself should not be called the very thing that bears it. Why is this? Because this has never been observed. Nor should the Buddha have spoken of it as an "inexpressible" matter. Why is this? Because this too has never been observed. Moreover, the taking up of the burden should then not be included within the aggregates, since the burden taking itself up has likewise never been observed. Yet the sūtra states that craving is what is called "the taking up of the burden" — and since craving is itself included within the aggregates, the bearer should likewise be so included, and it is precisely with reference to the aggregates that the term "repeated-taker-of-existences" is established. Yet, fearing that one might suppose this pudgala to be an inexpressible, permanently abiding, truly real entity, the Buddha himself, later in this very sūtra, explains: "this is stated only in accordance with worldly convention — this venerable one has such-and-such a name" — and so on, stated fully. As with the passage from the Sūtra on the Person cited earlier, this is meant to make clear that the pudgala can be said to be impermanent, and not of a genuinely real nature. It is precisely the five aggregates of clinging, oppressing and afflicting themselves by their own inherent nature, that receive the name "burden"; and it is because each earlier moment draws forth the one that follows that they receive the name "bearer" — hence there is no genuinely real pudgala.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the treatise's resolution here as depending entirely on a distinction already established across this project's earlier fascicles on the mental factors: craving (愛) is itself one of the aggregates — specifically, part of the formations aggregate — so when the sūtra names craving as "the taking up of the burden," it is not introducing some entity outside the aggregates to do the taking, but pointing to one process internal to the aggregates themselves. "Bearer," by the same logic, names nothing more than the aggregates' own self-perpetuating momentum, each moment's burden drawing forth the next moment's burden in turn — the very same causal succession this project has seen used throughout this chapter to explain apparent continuity without a persisting subject.

We should also flag the Buddha's own explanatory gloss, quoted here as decisive, as the same move the treatise relied on in the previous fascicle's citation of the Sūtra on the Person: whenever a sūtra's vivid, agent-implying language (a "bearer," someone who "sees," someone who "remembers") might be misread as evidence for a real self, the tradition holds that the Buddha's own later clarification — "this is stated only in accordance with worldly convention" — settles the matter, and should govern how the vivid language is read, rather than the vivid language being taken to override the explicit clarification.

2. Doesn't Denying "Spontaneously-Born Beings" Prove the Pudgala Is Real?

The Vātsīputrīya tries a new scriptural angle: a sūtra condemns denying the existence of spontaneously-born sentient beings as wrong view, so surely the pudgala must be real after all. The treatise turns this citation around, showing it actually supports the aggregate-continuum reading, then closes with a technical objection about which path could even abandon this supposed wrong view.

Chinese Source

補特伽羅定應實有，以契經說諸有撥無化生有情邪見攝故。誰言無有化生有情？如佛所言：我說有故。謂蘊相續能往後世，不由胎卵濕名化生有情。撥此為無，故邪見攝。化生諸蘊，理實有故。又許此邪見謗補特伽羅。汝等應言，是何所斷？見修所斷。理並不然，補特伽羅非諦攝故，邪見不應修所斷故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The pudgala must definitely be genuinely real, the Vātsīputrīya objects, since a sūtra states that whoever denies the existence of spontaneously-born sentient beings falls into the category of wrong view. Who says there are no spontaneously-born sentient beings? As the Buddha himself states, we affirm that they exist — namely, the aggregates' continuum, capable of proceeding on to a future life without being born from a womb, an egg, or moisture, is what is called a "spontaneously-born sentient being." Denying this is what falls into wrong view, since the aggregates involved in spontaneous birth are, in principle, genuinely real. Moreover, if this wrong view of denying spontaneous birth specifically slanders the pudgala, then you should tell us: by which path is it abandoned? Neither of the two answers actually works: since the pudgala is not included among the Four Truths, it cannot be abandoned by the path of seeing, which operates specifically by seeing the Truths; and wrong view is never something abandoned by the path of cultivation alone, as you yourselves hold elsewhere.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the treatise's handling of "spontaneously-born sentient being" as a clean demonstration of a strategy this project has now seen repeated many times across this chapter: rather than disputing the sūtra's authority or its content, the treatise simply supplies its own already-established reading of what the term actually refers to — the aggregate-continuum's capacity to proceed into a next life without the mediation of womb, egg, or moisture — which affirms everything the sūtra actually says while requiring nothing beyond what the treatise has already argued exists. The Vātsīputrīya's citation, meant to force a real pudgala into the picture, ends up doing no such work once its terms are properly unpacked.

We should also flag the closing technical objection as a smaller, more specialized point worth noting for readers tracking this project's earlier fascicles on the path structure: the path of seeing operates specifically by seeing each of the Four Truths for the first time, so anything it abandons must be classifiable under one of those Truths — and the pudgala, on the Vātsīputrīya's own telling, is explicitly not classifiable in this way, being "inexpressible." This leaves the Vātsīputrīya with no coherent path by which the supposed wrong view of denying the pudgala could ever actually be abandoned, a structural gap the treatise treats as symptomatic of the position's incoherence rather than a mere technicality.

3. Does "One Pudgala Is Born" Prove It's Conditioned? The Sūtra on Ultimate Emptiness

Two further scriptural wordings are tested: the numeral "one" applied to the pudgala, and the verb "born" applied to it. Both turn out to be ordinary figures of speech rather than evidence of a real, unified, newly-arising entity — and the section closes with two of the treatise's most decisive citations against any real "agent" behind karma at all.

Chinese Source

若謂經說有一補特伽羅生在世間，應非蘊者，亦不應理，此於總中假說一故，如世間說一麻一米一聚一言。或補特伽羅應許有為攝，以契經說生世間故。非此言生如蘊新起，依何義說生在世間？依此今時取別蘊義，如世間說能祠者生、記論者生，取明論故。又如世說有苾芻生、有外道生，取儀式故。或如世說有老者生，有病者生，取別位故。佛已遮故，此救不成。如勝義空契經中說：有業有異熟，作者不可得。謂能捨此蘊，及能續餘蘊，唯除法假。故佛已遮。頗勒具那契經亦說：我終不說有能取者。故定無一補特伽羅能於世間取捨諸蘊。

Ru-Yi Translation

If it is said that, since a sūtra states there is "one pudgala born into the world," the pudgala should therefore not simply be the aggregates — this too is unreasonable, since the word "one" is conventionally applied to a collective totality, just as ordinary speech says "one sesame," "one grain of rice," "one heap," or "one word." Or else, if you insist on taking "born" literally, the pudgala should then have to be granted as included among the conditioned dharmas, since a sūtra states it is "born into the world." This word "born" does not mean newly arising, the way the aggregates newly arise, they reply — on what basis, then, is it said to be "born into the world"? It is with reference to presently taking on a distinct set of aggregates that this is meant — just as ordinary speech says "a sacrificer is born" or "a grammarian is born," meaning that one has taken up the Vedic scriptures of sacrifice or grammar. And just as ordinary speech says "a monk is born" or "a heterodox ascetic is born," meaning that one has taken up a particular set of observances. Or just as ordinary speech says "an old person is born" or "a sick person is born," meaning that one has taken on a distinct stage or condition. This defense does not succeed, since the Buddha has already ruled it out. As the Sūtra on Ultimate Emptiness states: there is karma, there is karmic ripening, but no agent can be found — that is, no one can be found who lays down these aggregates and takes up other aggregates, except in the sense of a conventional designation applied to dharmas. Hence the Buddha has already ruled this out. The Sūtra to Phalguna likewise states: "I have never once said there is one who grasps." Hence it is certain that there is no single pudgala capable of taking up and laying down the aggregates within the world.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the Vātsīputrīya's proposed reading of "born" — modeled on everyday expressions like "a sacrificer is born" or "an old person is born," meaning one has simply taken on a new role or condition rather than newly come into existence — as a genuinely clever move, since it lets them accept the sūtra's language without conceding that the pudgala is conditioned and impermanent. The treatise does not dispute that this is a coherent way ordinary language works; it instead cuts the defense off at the root by citing the Sūtra on Ultimate Emptiness, which states directly and unconditionally that no agent can be found who lays down one set of aggregates and takes up another — foreclosing any reading, clever or otherwise, that would smuggle a real "taker-on" of new aggregates back into the picture.

We should also flag the Sūtra to Phalguna's one-line citation — "I have never once said there is one who grasps" — as functioning here almost as a capstone, closing off the whole line of argument this section and its predecessor have pursued: whether the Vātsīputrīya's evidence is the word "one," the word "born," or the general grammar of agency implicit in "bearer" and "taker," the Buddha is cited as having directly and explicitly disclaimed positing any such grasping agent, leaving the treatise's opponents without scriptural ground left to stand on for this particular line of defense.

4. Why the Buddha Refuses Ill-Formed Questions: The Tortoise's Fur

The treatise finishes dismantling the "sacrificer is born" analogies from the previous section, presses a technical parallel about derived form and the four great elements, and then arrives at one of this chapter's most quotable images: why the Buddha would never answer whether the "life-force" is identical to or different from the body — because the question itself presupposes something that does not exist, like asking whether a tortoise's fur is soft or coarse.

Chinese Source

又汝所引祠者等生，其體是何而能喻此？若執是我，彼不極成。若心心所，彼念念滅、新新生故，取捨不成。若許是身，亦如心等。又如明等與身有異，蘊亦應異補特伽羅。老病二身各與前別，數論轉變如前已遣，故彼所引為喻不成。又許蘊生非數取趣，則定許此異蘊及常。又此唯一，蘊體有五，寧不說此與蘊有異？大種有四，造色唯一，寧言造色不異大種？是彼宗過。何謂彼宗？諸計造色即大種論。設如彼見，應作是質：如諸造色即四大種，亦應即五蘊立補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, as for the examples you cited — "a sacrificer is born" and the rest — what exactly is their own substance, such that they could serve as a genuine analogy for this? If you hold that it refers to a self, this itself is not an established, mutually agreed premise. If it refers to mind and mental factors, then, since these perish moment by moment and arise anew each moment, the notion of "taking up and laying down" does not hold. If you grant it refers to the body, this is likewise just like the case of mind. Moreover, just as "knowledge of the Vedic scriptures" is different from the body itself that takes on this knowledge, so too the aggregates should be different from the pudgala. The bodies of "the old" and "the sick" are each different from what came before them; and the Sāṃkhya doctrine of transformation has already been refuted earlier. Hence the examples you have cited fail to serve as genuine analogies. Moreover, if you grant that it is merely the aggregates that are born, not the repeated-taker-of-existences itself, then you must definitely grant that this pudgala is different from the aggregates and permanent. Moreover, this pudgala is said to be single, while the substance of the aggregates numbers five — why, then, is it not said that this differs from the aggregates? The great elements number four, while derived form is single, yet it is not said that derived form is thereby different from the great elements — this is a fault specific to your own school's position. What is meant by "your own school's position" here? It refers to the theory, held by some, that derived form is simply identical with the great elements themselves. Granting that view for the sake of argument, one should press this challenge: just as derived form is said to be simply identical with the four great elements, then, by the same logic, the pudgala should likewise have to be simply identical with the five aggregates.

Chinese Source

若補特伽羅即諸蘊者，世尊何不記命者即身？觀能問者阿世耶故。問者執一內用士夫體實非虛名為命者，依此問佛與身一異。此都無故，一異不成，如何與身可記一異？如不可記龜毛䩕軟，古昔諸師已解斯結。

Ru-Yi Translation

If the pudgala is simply identical with the aggregates, why did the Blessed One not directly declare that the life-force is simply identical with the body? This is because he took into account the underlying intention of the one asking the question. The questioner held that there exists a single, genuinely real, inwardly-functioning personal agent — not a mere empty name — calling it the "life-force," and it was with this in mind that he asked the Buddha whether it was identical to or different from the body. Since this presupposed real life-force does not exist at all, neither "identical" nor "different" can be established — how, then, could the Buddha possibly declare it identical to or different from the body? This is just like how one cannot declare whether a tortoise's fur is soft or coarse — the ancient masters have already resolved this very knot.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "derived form is identical with the great elements" reference as a technical internal debate this passage assumes rather than explains — a minority position, held by some within the broader Sarvāstivāda tradition, that derived material qualities (color, smell, taste, tangibility) are not genuinely distinct from the four great elements (earth, water, fire, wind) that give rise to them, but simply identical with them. The treatise does not endorse this position itself; it uses it only as a hypothetical granted for the sake of argument, to show that even on this rival view's own terms, consistency would require treating the pudgala as flatly identical with the aggregates — not the "neither same nor different" the Vātsīputrīya actually wants.

We should also flag the "tortoise's fur" image as the clearest possible statement of this chapter's governing principle about the Buddha's silences: a question is not always answerable simply because it is grammatically well-formed. "Is the tortoise's fur soft or coarse?" presupposes that a tortoise has fur at all, and no answer to the soft-or-coarse question can be given until that false presupposition is corrected. "Is the life-force identical to or different from the body?" works the same way, presupposing a real, unified "life-force" that, on the treatise's analysis, simply does not exist — so the Buddha's refusal to answer is not an evasion or a mystery, but the only coherent response available once the question's own hidden premise is seen to be empty. This principle will recur across several of the sections immediately following, applied to a whole family of questions the Buddha traditionally declined to answer.

5. The Elder Nāgasena and King Piṇḍola: The Mango Trees That Weren't There

The treatise illustrates the tortoise-fur principle with a memorable narrative: an exchange between the elder Nāgasena and King Piṇḍola, in which the elder turns the king's own trick question back on him with a question about mango trees that don't exist — before addressing a final, natural objection: if there is truly no life-force, why didn't the Buddha simply say so outright?

Chinese Source

昔有大德名曰龍軍，三明六通具八解脫。于時有一畢隣陀王，至大德所作如是說：我今來意欲請所疑。然諸沙門性好多語，尊能直答，我當請問。大德受請。王即問言：命者與身為一為異？大德答言：此不應記。王言：豈不先有要耶？今何異言不答所問？大德質曰：我欲問疑。然諸國王性好多語，王能直答，我當發問。王便受教。大德問言：大王宮中諸菴羅樹所生果味為酢為甘。王言：宮中本無此樹。大德復責：先無要耶？今何異言不答所問。王言：宮內此樹既無，寧可答言果味甘酢？大德誨曰：命者亦無，如何可言與身一異？佛何不說命者都無？亦觀問者阿世耶故。問者或於諸蘊相續謂為命者，依之發問。世尊若答命者都無，彼墮邪見，故佛不說。彼未能了緣起理故，非受正法器，不為說假有。理必應爾，世尊說故。

Ru-Yi Translation

In ancient times, there was a great elder named Nāgasena, who possessed the three insights, the six supernormal powers, and the eight liberations in full. At that time, there was a King Piṇḍola who came to the great elder and spoke thus: "My intention in coming here is to ask about my doubts. But ascetics, by nature, are fond of much talk — if you, Venerable One, are able to answer directly, I shall pose my question." The great elder accepted this condition. The king then asked: "Is the life-force identical to or different from the body?" The great elder replied: "This should not be answered directly." The king said: "Was there not an agreement beforehand? Why do you now speak otherwise and fail to answer the question asked?" The great elder countered: "I too wish to ask about a doubt. But kings, by nature, are fond of much talk — if you, O King, are able to answer directly, I shall pose my question." The king accepted this in turn. The great elder asked: "The mango trees in the Great King's palace — is the fruit they produce sour or sweet?" The king replied: "There are no such trees in my palace at all." The great elder countered again: "Was there not an agreement beforehand? Why do you now speak otherwise and fail to answer the question asked?" The king said: "Since there is no such tree at all within my palace, how could I possibly answer whether its fruit's flavor is sweet or sour?" The great elder instructed him: "The life-force is likewise nonexistent — how, then, could one possibly say whether it is identical to or different from the body?" Why, then, did the Buddha not simply state that the life-force does not exist at all? This too is because he took into account the underlying intention of the one asking. The questioner may have been referring, by "life-force," to the aggregates' own continuum, asking his question with this in mind. If the Blessed One had answered that the life-force does not exist at all, without qualification, the questioner would have fallen into wrong view — hence the Buddha did not state this. Since such a questioner has not yet been able to understand the principle of dependent origination, and is not yet a fit vessel for receiving the true Dharma, the Buddha did not explain to him the doctrine of conventional existence. This must necessarily be the reason, since the Blessed One himself has stated as much elsewhere.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the elder Nāgasena's mango-tree counter-question as an elegant piece of Socratic pedagogy in its own right: rather than simply lecturing the king about why his question is malformed, Nāgasena engineers a situation in which the king discovers the principle himself — the king cannot answer whether a nonexistent tree's fruit is sweet or sour, and the moment he articulates why not ("since there is no such tree at all... how could I possibly answer?"), he has already supplied, in his own words, the exact reasoning that dissolves his own original question about the life-force. This is a considerably more vivid, memorable illustration of the tortoise-fur principle from the previous section than abstract argument alone could provide.

We should also flag the final point — that the Buddha declined to simply say "the life-force does not exist" outright — as adding a layer of pastoral sensitivity to what might otherwise read as pure logical technicality: the treatise's concern is that a listener who has not yet grasped dependent origination might hear a flat denial of "life-force" and mistake it for a denial of the conventionally real aggregate-continuum itself, tipping into the more dangerous wrong view of nihilism rather than correcting the more common wrong view of a real self. The Buddha's silence, on this reading, is calibrated not just to the truth of the matter but to what a given listener is actually capable of correctly hearing.

6. The Wanderer of the Vatsa Clan, and the Tigress Carrying Her Cub

The treatise now presents one of the most celebrated passages in this entire chapter: the Buddha's own account, told to Ānanda, of why he refused to answer a wandering ascetic's blunt question — does the self exist, or does it not? — and the tigress-and-cub image the tradition uses to capture the precise, calibrated care this refusal requires.

Chinese Source

如世尊告阿難陀言：有姓筏蹉出家外道，來至我所作是問言：我於世間為有非有？我不為記。所以者何？若記為有，違法真理，以一切法皆無我故。若記為無，增彼愚惑，彼便謂我先有今無。對執有愚，此愚更甚，謂執有我則墮常邊，若執無我便墮斷邊。此二輕重，如經廣說。依如是義，故有頌曰： 觀為見所傷， 及壞諸善業， 故佛說正法， 如牝虎銜子。 執真我為有， 則為見牙傷， 撥俗我為無， 便壞善業子。 復說頌曰： 由實命者無， 佛不言一異， 恐撥無假我， 亦不說都無。 謂蘊相續中， 有業果命者， 若說無命者， 彼撥此為無。 不說諸蘊中， 有假名命者， 由觀發問者， 無力解真空。 如是觀筏蹉， 意樂差別故， 彼問有無我， 佛不答有無。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the Blessed One told Ānanda: there was a wandering ascetic of the Vatsa clan who came to me and asked: does the self exist in the world, or does it not exist? I did not answer him directly. Why is this? If I had answered that it exists, this would contradict the true principle of the Dharma, since all dharmas are without self. If I had answered that it does not exist, this would only increase his confusion, since he would then suppose: "I formerly existed, and now I no longer do." Compared to the confusion of grasping at existence, this confusion would be even worse — for holding that a self exists causes one to fall into the extreme of eternalism, while holding that no self exists causes one to fall into the extreme of annihilationism. The relative severity of these two extremes is explained at length in the sūtra. On the basis of this meaning, there is the following verse: seeing that an unqualified answer would be wounded by one view or the other, and would destroy one's wholesome deeds, the Buddha therefore taught the true Dharma the way a tigress carries her cub. Grasping at a genuinely real self as existing is to be wounded by the fang of wrong view; denying even the conventional self as non-existent is to destroy the cub of one's own wholesome deeds. A further verse states: because there is truly no life-force, the Buddha did not say it is identical to or different from the body; fearing that this would lead to denying even the conventional self, he likewise did not say it does not exist at all. That is: within the aggregates' continuum, there is karma, karmic result, and a "life-force" spoken of conventionally — if he had said there is no life-force at all, the questioner would have denied even this conventional referent as non-existent. Nor did he state that, within the aggregates, there is a life-force that is merely a conventional name, because he saw that the one asking the question lacked the capacity to understand true emptiness. It was by observing the Vatsa wanderer's particular disposition in just this way that, when he asked whether the self exists or does not exist, the Buddha answered neither that it exists nor that it does not exist.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the tigress-and-cub image as capturing something this project has now seen this chapter build toward across several sections: the danger the Buddha navigates is not symmetrical. Grasping at a real, permanent self ("eternalism") and denying the self entirely, including its conventional, aggregate-based reality ("annihilationism," or nihilism), are both wrong views — but the passage explicitly ranks the second as worse, since it threatens to "destroy the cub of one's own wholesome deeds" by undermining confidence in karma and its results altogether, not merely misdescribing what a person ultimately is. A tigress carrying her cub in her jaws must apply exactly enough pressure to hold it securely without crushing it — the Buddha's silence toward the Vatsa wanderer is presented as this same kind of precisely calibrated act, not evasion or uncertainty.

We should also flag this passage as drawing together several threads this chapter has developed piece by piece: the treatise has already established that there genuinely is no life-force or self in the ultimate sense (the tortoise-fur point, two sections earlier), that there is nonetheless a conventionally real aggregate-continuum which karma, result, and memory can all be meaningfully attributed to, and that a given listener's capacity to correctly receive either truth — full non-existence, or conventional existence — varies by their own understanding of dependent origination. The Vatsa wanderer's blunt existence-or-non-existence question forces exactly this calibration into view, and the Buddha's silence is the treatise's clearest illustration yet of what skillful means looks like when applied to metaphysics.

7. Why the Buddha Won't Say Whether the World Is Eternal

The treatise extends the same principle to another famous class of questions the Buddha traditionally declined to answer — whether the world is eternal, and whether it has an end — showing that every one of the four possible answers leads to an absurd consequence, and that a later, superficially different question from the ascetic Uttiya turns out, on inspection, to be the very same question in disguise.

Chinese Source

何緣不記世間常等？亦觀問者阿世耶故。問者若執我為世間，我體都無故，四記皆非理。若執生死皆名世間，佛四種記亦皆非理。謂若常者，無得涅槃。若是非常，便自斷滅，不由功力咸得涅槃。若記為常亦非常者，定應一分無得涅槃，一分有情自證圓寂。若記非常非非常者，則非得涅槃，非不得涅槃。決定相違，便成戲論。然依聖道可般涅槃，故四定記皆不應理。如離繫子問雀死生，佛知彼心不為定記。有邊等四亦不記者，以同常等皆有失故。寧知此四義同常等？以有外道名嗢底迦，先問世間有邊等四，復設方便矯問世尊：為諸世間皆由聖道能得出離、為一分耶？尊者阿難因告彼曰：汝以此事已問世尊，今復何緣改名重問？故知後四，義與前同。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why did the Buddha not answer questions such as whether the world is eternal? This too is because he took into account the underlying intention of the one asking. If the questioner takes "the world" to mean the self, then, since the self has no substance whatsoever, none of the four possible answers could be reasonable. If the questioner takes "the world" to mean saṃsāra as a whole, the Buddha's four possible answers would still all be unreasonable. That is: if it were answered that the world is eternal, then no one could ever attain nirvāṇa. If it were answered that it is non-eternal, then it would simply annihilate itself on its own, and everyone would attain nirvāṇa without any effort at all. If it were answered that it is both eternal and non-eternal, then it would necessarily follow that one portion never attains nirvāṇa while another portion of beings spontaneously realizes perfect quiescence on its own. If it were answered that it is neither eternal nor non-eternal, then it would be neither the case that one attains nirvāṇa nor that one does not attain it — a definite self-contradiction, amounting to nothing but idle speculation. Yet it is by relying on the noble path that one can attain final nirvāṇa — hence none of these four definite answers is reasonable. This is like when a Jain ascetic asked about a sparrow's death and rebirth — the Buddha, knowing that questioner's mind, likewise did not give a definite answer. The four questions about whether the world has an end and so forth were likewise not answered, since they involve the same faults as the eternal/non-eternal questions. How is it known that these four questions carry the same meaning as the eternal/non-eternal ones? Because there was a heterodox ascetic named Uttiya who first asked the four questions about whether the world has an end and so forth, and then, employing a stratagem, cunningly asked the Blessed One instead: "is it that all beings in the world are able to attain liberation by means of the noble path, or only a portion of them?" The Venerable Ānanda thereupon told him: "you have already asked the Blessed One about this very matter — why, then, do you now change the wording and ask it again?" Hence it is known that these latter four questions carry the same meaning as the earlier ones.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag this passage's exhaustive four-way analysis (eternal / non-eternal / both / neither) as the classic tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) structure this project has encountered before in Buddhist logic, here applied specifically to test whether "the world is eternal" could ever receive a coherent answer: each of the four positions is shown to entail an absurd consequence about who can and cannot attain nirvāṇa, from guaranteeing universal effortless liberation to guaranteeing that none is possible at all. The point is not that the correct answer is hiding among some fifth option, but that the entire question, taken as a question about "the world" as some single, independently existing subject, is malformed from the outset in a way structurally identical to the tortoise-fur and mango-tree cases from earlier in this fascicle.

We should also flag the exchange with the ascetic Uttiya as a nice narrative confirmation of the treatise's analytical claim: rather than the treatise merely asserting that "whether the world has an end" is equivalent to "whether the world is eternal," it cites an incident in which a questioner tried to sneak the same underlying question past the Buddha in disguised form, only to be caught out by Ānanda pointing out he had already asked this. This functions as textual evidence, drawn from within the canon itself, that the tradition already recognized these superficially distinct questions as one and the same at a deeper level — precisely the analysis the treatise has just walked through.

8. Why the Buddha Won't Say Whether the Tathāgata Exists After Death

The treatise takes up a final class of unanswered questions — whether the Tathāgata exists after death — and presses the Vātsīputrīya on an apparent inconsistency: the Buddha freely predicted specific future rebirths for Maitreya and his own disciples, so why would he refuse to say anything at all about a Tathāgata's own continued existence, unless there simply were no real pudgala there to begin with?

Chinese Source

復以何緣世尊不記如來死後有等四耶？亦觀問者阿世耶故，問者妄計已解脫我名為如來而發問故。今應詰問計有我者，佛何緣記有現補特伽羅，不記如來死後亦有？彼言恐有墮常失故。若爾，何緣佛記慈氏汝於來世當得作佛，及記弟子身壞命終，某甲今時已生某處。此豈非有墮常過失？若佛先見補特伽羅，彼涅槃已便不復見，以不知故不記有者，則撥大師具一切智。或應許不記，由我體都無。若謂世尊見而不說，則有離蘊及常住過。若見非見俱不可說，則應漸言，不可說佛是一切智非一切智。

Ru-Yi Translation

For what further reason did the Blessed One not answer the four questions about whether the Tathāgata exists after death and so forth? This too is because he took into account the underlying intention of the one asking, since the questioner falsely conceived of a liberated self, calling it "Tathāgata," and asked his question with this in mind. One should now press this challenge to those who hold there is a self: why did the Buddha state that there presently exists a pudgala, yet not state that it also exists after the Tathāgata's death? They reply: because he feared this would incur the fault of falling into eternalism. If this is so, then why did the Buddha declare to Maitreya, "you shall become a Buddha in a future life," and declare of his disciples, upon the dissolution of their bodies and the end of their lives, "so-and-so has now already been reborn in such-and-such a place"? Would this not equally incur the fault of falling into eternalism? If it is held that the Buddha previously saw the pudgala, but, once it entered nirvāṇa, he no longer saw it, and it was for this reason of not knowing that he did not affirm its continued existence — this would amount to denying that the great teacher possesses omniscience. One should instead grant that he gave no definite answer because the self's substance does not exist at all. If it is claimed that the Blessed One saw it but simply did not speak of it, this would incur the fault of positing something apart from the aggregates and permanently abiding. If it cannot be said whether he saw it or did not see it, then one would gradually have to say that it cannot even be said whether the Buddha is omniscient or not omniscient.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the contrast between the Buddha's willingness to predict Maitreya's future buddhahood or a specific disciple's specific rebirth, and his refusal to say anything about a Tathāgata's post-death existence, as the crux of this section's argument: both kinds of statement involve continuity across death, so if the fear of "falling into eternalism" genuinely explained the second silence, it should equally have prevented the first set of predictions — which it plainly did not. The treatise takes this asymmetry as proof that the Vātsīputrīya's proposed explanation cannot be the real one, leaving the treatise's own explanation (there is simply no real, findable pudgala for the question to be about) as the only account that fits every case consistently.

We should also flag the closing dilemma about whether the Buddha "saw" the pudgala after death as forcing the Vātsīputrīya into an exhaustive and uncomfortable set of options: if he saw it and stayed silent regardless, this concedes exactly the permanent, aggregate-independent entity their own position is committed to denying elsewhere; if he simply didn't know, this directly denies the Buddha's omniscience, a conclusion no Buddhist school could accept; and if one retreats to saying even "did he see it or not" is itself unanswerable, this absurdly extends the inexpressibility all the way to the question of the Buddha's own omniscience, which the treatise offers as a reductio no one could seriously maintain.

9. If There Is No Pudgala, Who Transmigrates? The Wildfire Simile

Having closed off the whole family of unanswered questions, the treatise turns to what may be the most intuitive objection of all: if there is truly no persisting self, who is it that actually transmigrates through birth and death? The answer draws on an image this project has now seen used in related forms before — a wildfire spreading across a field, never the same flame twice, yet genuinely, continuously spreading all the same.

Chinese Source

若謂實有補特伽羅，以契經言諦故住故，定執無我者墮惡見處故。此不成證，彼經亦說定執有我者墮惡見處故。阿毘達磨諸論師言：執我有無俱邊見攝，如次墮在常斷邊故。彼師所說深為應理，以執有我則墮常邊，若執無我便墮斷邊。前《筏蹉經》分明說故。若定無有補特伽羅，為說阿誰流轉生死？不應生死自流轉故。然薄伽梵於契經中，說諸有情無明所覆、貪愛所繫馳流生死，故應定有補特伽羅。此復如何流轉生死？由捨前蘊取後蘊故。如是義宗前已徵遣。如燎原火雖剎那滅，而由相續說有流轉。如是蘊聚假說有情，愛取為緣流轉生死。

Ru-Yi Translation

The pudgala must genuinely exist as real, the Vātsīputrīya objects, since a sūtra states, "because of truth, because of abiding" — and since it also states that whoever definitively holds there is no self falls into the domain of wrong view. This does not constitute proof, since that very same sūtra also states that whoever definitively holds there is a self likewise falls into the domain of wrong view. The Ābhidharmika masters state: grasping at either the existence or the non-existence of a self is included within the extreme views, since these two positions fall, respectively, into the extremes of eternalism and annihilationism. What these masters state accords deeply with reason: holding that a self exists causes one to fall into the extreme of eternalism, while holding that no self exists causes one to fall into the extreme of annihilationism — the Sūtra to Vatsa, cited earlier, states this plainly. If there is definitely no pudgala, then who is it that transmigrates through birth-and-death? Birth-and-death should not simply transmigrate all on its own, with no one doing the transmigrating. Yet the Blessed One, within a sūtra, states that sentient beings, covered over by ignorance and bound by craving, race and flow through birth-and-death — hence there must definitely be a pudgala. How, then, does this transmigration through birth-and-death actually occur? Through laying down the former aggregates and taking up later ones. This very position has already been examined and refuted earlier. It is like a wildfire spreading across a field: although each individual flame perishes moment by moment, one speaks of it as "spreading" with reference to its continuum. In just the same way, the aggregate-mass is conventionally called a "sentient being," and, with craving and clinging as its condition, it transmigrates through birth-and-death.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the Vātsīputrīya's citation — "because of truth, because of abiding" — as a scriptural phrase whose force the treatise dismantles with a move now familiar across this entire chapter: rather than disputing the sūtra, the treatise points out that the very same sūtra also condemns the opposite extreme, holding that a self definitely exists, as equally a form of wrong view. A single citation cannot establish the reality of a self while ignoring the same source's parallel condemnation of that very position — the sūtra, read in full, supports the treatise's own middle position (neither affirming nor denying a self in the ultimate sense) far better than it supports either extreme.

We should also flag the wildfire simile as a natural extension of images this project has now encountered in related but distinct forms across this chapter — the burning fuel of the fire-and-fuel analogy several fascicles earlier, and the burden-bearer's self-perpetuating momentum just a few sections back. What is new here is the emphasis on genuine, continuous spreading across a field despite no single flame lasting more than an instant: this directly answers "who transmigrates?" without requiring anything that persists — the transmigration is real, in exactly the sense that a wildfire's spread is real, without requiring a single, self-identical entity to carry it from one place, or one life, to the next.

10. Why Even a Real Self Would Be Dangerous, and the Memory Problem Opens

The treatise closes off the "Excellent Eye" past-life citation with the same continuum-reading used before, then makes an unexpected move: even setting the metaphysics aside, a genuinely real self would be actively dangerous, since perceiving it would necessarily breed self-love and bind one to affliction. Three wrong positions are surveyed and rejected together, before the treatise turns to the hardest remaining objection — how can a momentarily perishing mind remember anything at all?

Chinese Source

若唯有蘊，何故世尊作如是說：今我於昔為世導師名為妙眼。此說何咎？蘊各異故。若爾，是何物謂補特伽羅？昔我即今，體應常住，故說今我；昔為師言，顯昔與今是一相續，如言此火曾燒彼事。若謂決定有真實我，則應唯佛能明了觀，觀已應生堅固我執，從斯我執我所執生，從此應生我我所愛。故薄伽梵作如是言：若執有我便執我所，執我所故於諸蘊中便復發生我我所愛薩迦耶見，我愛所縛則為謗佛，去解脫遠。若謂於我不起我愛，此言無義。所以者何？於非我中橫計為我，容起我愛。非實我中，如是所言無理為證，故彼於佛真聖教中無有因緣起見瘡皰。如是一類執有不可說補特伽羅。復有一類總撥一切法體皆非有。外道執有別真我性，此等一切見不如理，皆不能免無解脫過。若一切類我體都無，剎那滅心於曾所受久相似境何能憶知？

Ru-Yi Translation

If there are only the aggregates, why did the Blessed One state: "I, in a past life, was a world-teacher named Excellent Eye"? What fault does this raise? Because the aggregates of that past life are each different from the present ones. If this is so, what exactly is the thing called "pudgala"? It does not mean the past self is simply identical with the present one, whose substance would then have to be permanently abiding, and thus called "the present self." Rather, saying "in the past, I was a teacher" is meant to show that the past and the present belong to a single continuum — just as one says "this fire once burned that thing." If it were held that there definitely is a genuinely real self, then only the Buddha should be able to clearly perceive it — and, having perceived it, this would give rise to a firm grasping at self, from which grasping at "self" and grasping at "what belongs to self" would arise, and from this, in turn, attachment to self and to what belongs to self would necessarily arise. Hence the Blessed One states thus: if one grasps at there being a self, one thereby grasps at what belongs to self; because of grasping at what belongs to self, one further gives rise, with respect to the aggregates, to attachment to self and to what belongs to self, and to the view of a substantial self; bound by this self-love, one thereby slanders the Buddha, and remains far removed from liberation. If it is claimed that one can grasp at a self without giving rise to self-love toward it, this statement is meaningless. Why is this? Because it is precisely in falsely conceiving of what is not-self as a self that self-love can arise — but not with respect to a genuinely real self, since no such thing exists for it to arise with respect to. Hence such a claim has no reasoning to serve as its proof, and so, within the Buddha's own true and noble teaching, there is no legitimate cause or condition for such people to develop the festering sore of this view. Thus, one type of view holds there is an inexpressible pudgala; another type entirely denies that any dharma's substance exists at all; and the heterodox teachers hold there is a separate, genuinely real self-nature. All of these views, in every case, are contrary to reason, and none of them can avoid the fault of being without liberation. If, across every type of view, the self's substance is entirely nonexistent, then how can a mind that perishes moment by moment come to remember and recognize an object experienced long ago, resembling what was previously experienced?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the argument that a real self would necessarily breed self-love as a genuinely distinctive move within this chapter, worth setting apart from the many purely logical or scriptural refutations surrounding it: even granting, purely hypothetically, that a real self could somehow be coherently established, the treatise argues this would still be a bad outcome rather than a neutral metaphysical fact, since directly perceiving one's own real self would inevitably generate exactly the self-love and self-grasping the entire path is designed to dismantle. This reframes the stakes of the whole debate — the treatise is not simply defending a preferred metaphysics, but arguing that the Vātsīputrīya's position, even if it somehow turned out to be true, would be soteriologically catastrophic.

We should also flag the closing three-way survey — the "inexpressible pudgala" (the Vātsīputrīya), total denial of all dharmas' existence (an unnamed nihilist position), and the heterodox teachers' fully real, separate self — as bracketing the entire debate this chapter has pursued between two failure modes on either side of the treatise's own position, echoing the tigress-and-cub verses from several sections earlier: too much affirmation and too much denial both lead away from liberation, and the treatise's actual position (conventionally real aggregates and their continuum, no separately real self or pudgala, but also no denial of karma, memory, or conventional personal continuity) is offered as the narrow path between them. The closing question about memory opens what will be an extended, detailed defense of exactly how that narrow path actually works in practice.

11. How Memory Actually Works Without a Self, and the Grammar of "Remembering"

The treatise now gives its most detailed account yet of the mechanics of memory: what specific mental conditions must jointly be present for an act of remembering to occur, why this differs entirely from one person somehow remembering what a different person saw, and — in one of this chapter's most intricate passages — a careful grammatical analysis of what the possessive case in "Caitra's memory" or "whose remembering is this?" is actually doing, once no real self is available to "own" it.

Chinese Source

如是憶知，從相續內念境想類心差別生。且初憶念為從何等心差別無間生？從有緣彼作意相似相屬想等，不為依止差別愁憂散亂等緣損壞功德心差別起。雖有如是作意等緣，若無彼類心差別者，則無堪能修此憶念。雖有彼類心差別因，若無如是緣，亦無能修理，要具二種方可能修。諸憶念生但由於此，不見離此有功能故。如何異心見，後異心能憶。非天授心曾所見境，後祠授心有憶念理。此難非理，不相屬故。謂彼二心互不相屬，非如一相續有因果性故。我等不言異心見境異心能憶，相續一故。然從過去緣彼境心，引起今時能憶念識。謂如前說相續轉變差別力故生念何失？由此憶念力，有後記知生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such remembering and recognizing arises from a specific kind of mental differentiation within the continuum — a mind bearing the perception of, and resembling, the object once experienced. From what specific kind of mental differentiation does an initial act of remembering arise immediately after? It arises from a mind bearing mental attention directed toward that object, a resembling and connected perception, and so on — one not damaged in its capacity by conditions such as a differing basis, grief, distraction, and the like. Even given such conditions as mental attention, if that specific kind of mental differentiation is absent, one lacks the capacity to bring about this act of remembering. Conversely, even given that specific kind of mental differentiation as a cause, if such conditions are absent, one likewise lacks the capacity to bring it about — both are required together before remembering becomes possible. Every instance of remembering arises solely on this basis, since no capacity for it is ever observed apart from these two. How can one mind see, and then a different mind remember what that first mind saw? It is not reasonable that an object once seen by Devadatta's mind could later be remembered by Yajñadatta's mind. This objection does not hold, since the two cases are not analogous. Devadatta's mind and Yajñadatta's mind are mutually unrelated to each other, not standing in a cause-and-effect relationship the way a single continuum's moments do. We do not claim that one mind sees an object and an entirely different, unrelated mind remembers it — remembering occurs because the continuum is one. Rather, it is from the past mind that took that object as its condition that the present mind capable of remembering is drawn forth — that is, as stated earlier, memory arises through the power of this specific transformational difference in the continuum. What fault could this possibly involve? It is through the power of this act of remembering that a subsequent recognition arises.

Chinese Source

我體既無，孰為能憶？能憶是何義？由念能取境。此取境豈異念？雖不異念，但由作者，作者即是前說念因，謂彼類心差別。然世間所言制怛羅能憶，此於蘊相續立制怛羅名。從先見心後憶念起，依如是理說彼能憶。我體若無，是誰之念？為依何義說第六聲？此第六聲依屬主義，如何物屬何主？此如牛等屬制怛羅。彼如何為牛主？謂依彼彼所乘所搆所役等中彼得自在。欲於何所驅役於念，而勤方便尋求念主？於所念境驅役於念。役念為何？謂令念起。奇哉自在起無理言，寧為此生而驅役此？又我於念如何驅役？為令念起、為令念行？念無行故，但應念起。則因名主，果名能屬。由因增上令果得生，故因名主。果於生時是因所有，故名能屬。即生念因足為念主，何勞立我為念主耶？即諸行聚一類相續，世共施設制怛羅牛，立制怛羅名為牛主。是牛相續於異方生，變異生因故名為主。此中無一實制怛羅亦無實牛，但假施設，故言牛主，亦不離因。憶念既爾，記知亦然。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since the self's substance does not exist, who is it that does the remembering? What does "the one who remembers" even mean? It means that mindfulness itself is capable of apprehending its object. Is this apprehending of the object then something different from mindfulness itself? Although it is not different from mindfulness, one may nonetheless speak of an "agent" — but this "agent" is precisely what was called, earlier, the cause of mindfulness: namely, that specific kind of mental differentiation. As for the ordinary expression "Caitra remembers" — the name "Caitra" here is simply applied to an aggregate-continuum. Because a later act of remembering arises from an earlier moment of seeing, it is on this basis that one speaks of "Caitra remembering." If the self does not exist, whose act of remembering is it? On what basis is the possessive grammatical case used here? This possessive case is used with reference to the sense of belonging to an owner — but what kind of thing belongs to what kind of owner? It is like saying "cattle belong to Caitra." How, then, is Caitra the "owner" of the cattle? It means that, with respect to riding them, milking them, putting them to labor, and so on, he has mastery and free command over them. In what sense, then, is one seeking to find an "owner of mindfulness," as though laboring to find something to direct and command mindfulness toward some task? The intended sense would be: directing mindfulness toward the object being remembered. What does "directing mindfulness" actually mean? It means causing mindfulness to arise. How strange — to speak of "mastery over its own arising" is simply unreasonable: how could something direct and command its own arising, generating itself? Moreover, how would a self even "direct and command" mindfulness? Would it be to cause mindfulness to arise, or to cause mindfulness to act? Since mindfulness has no further activity beyond its own arising, it could only mean causing mindfulness to arise. In that case, the cause is what should be called the "owner," and the effect is what should be called "belonging to it." Because the cause exerts a dominant influence in bringing the effect into being, the cause is called the "owner"; because the effect, at the moment of its arising, belongs to what the cause possesses, it is called "belonging." The very cause that gives rise to mindfulness is itself sufficient to serve as mindfulness's "owner" — what need is there to posit a self as mindfulness's owner at all? It is precisely with reference to a specific continuum, a collection of formations, that the world conventionally designates "Caitra's cattle," establishing the name "Caitra" as the cattle's "owner." It is because that cattle-continuum, when it arises transformed in another location, does so through a cause involving change specifically traceable to Caitra, that he is called their "owner." In this, there is no single real "Caitra" and no real, unified "cattle" either — it is merely a conventional designation; hence, in speaking of "the owner of the cattle," one likewise does not depart from the underlying causal relationship. Just as remembering works this way, so too does recognition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "Devadatta sees, Yajñadatta remembers" objection as testing exactly the right pressure point: if there is no persisting self linking the seeing-moment to the remembering-moment, what actually distinguishes legitimate memory (I remember what I saw) from the absurd case of one person remembering what an entirely different person once saw? The treatise's answer rests entirely on the causal continuum established across this project's many earlier fascicles: Devadatta's mind-stream and Yajñadatta's mind-stream never stand in cause-and-effect relation to each other, while every moment within a single stream does — memory is legitimate exactly where, and only where, this specific causal chain runs unbroken, with no need for anything beyond that chain to explain the difference.

We should also flag the cattle-and-Caitra grammatical analysis as this chapter's most sustained engagement with language itself as a source of the illusion of a self: the "sixth case" (roughly the genitive/possessive, as in Sanskrit grammar) makes "Caitra's memory" and "Caitra's cattle" look structurally identical, both implying a real owner standing behind and possessing something. The treatise dissolves this by showing that even the completely uncontroversial case — cattle belonging to a herder — reduces, on inspection, to a cause-and-effect relationship (the herder is simply whatever causally governs the cattle's activity and movement) with no need for the herder to be a separately real, unified substance beyond that causal role. Applied back to mindfulness, "memory's owner" becomes nothing more than a name for whatever mental cause gave rise to it — the same causal relationship the treatise has been describing all along, now shown to be exactly what ordinary grammar was already tracking, once its implicit metaphysical assumptions are set aside.

12. Cognition Without a Self: "Devadatta Walks"

The treatise extends the memory analysis to cognition itself, taking up a new objection: surely any activity requires an agent to perform it, just as "walking" requires a walker named Devadatta. The response applies the exact same continuum-based analysis already developed for memory — "Devadatta" is himself only a conventional name for a causal continuum, and "cognizing" works no differently from "walking" once this is understood.

Chinese Source

如辯憶知，熟為能了誰之識等，亦應例釋。且識因緣與前別者，謂根境等，如應當知。有作是言：決定有我，事用必待事用者故。謂諸事用待事用者，如天授行必待天授。行是事用天授名者，如是識等所有事用，必待所依能了等者。今應詰彼：天授謂何？若是實我，此如先破。若假士夫，體非一物，於諸行相續假立此名故。如天授能行，識能了亦爾。依何理說天授能行？謂於剎那生滅諸行不異相續立天授名，愚夫於中執為一體，為自相續異處生因。異處生名行，因即名行者。依此理說天授能行。如焰及聲異處相續，世依此說焰聲能行。如是天授身能為識因故，世間亦謂天授能了。然諸聖者為順世間言說理故，亦作是說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as remembering and recognizing have been explained, the questions of who is capable of cognizing, and whose consciousness this is, and so on, should likewise be explained by the same pattern. As for how the causes and conditions of consciousness differ from those discussed above — namely, the sense-faculty and its object and the rest — this should be understood as appropriate. There must definitely be a self, an objection runs, since any functional activity necessarily requires an agent who performs that activity. That is: every functional activity requires an agent to perform it — just as the activity of "walking" necessarily requires Devadatta as the one walking. Walking is the activity, and Devadatta is its agent's name — in just the same way, the various activities belonging to consciousness and the rest must likewise depend on some underlying agent capable of cognizing. One should now press this challenge: what exactly is "Devadatta"? If it refers to a real self, this has already been refuted earlier. If it refers to a conventionally designated person, whose substance is not a single, unified thing but merely a name conventionally applied to a continuum of formations, then, just as "Devadatta walks" works this way, so too does "consciousness cognizes." On what basis, then, is it said that "Devadatta walks"? It is with reference to a continuum of momentarily arising-and-perishing formations, not genuinely differing from one another, that the name "Devadatta" is established; the unlearned, with respect to this continuum, grasp at it as a single, unified substance, when in fact it is simply the cause for that same continuum's arising in a different location. Arising in a different location is what is called "walking"; the cause of this is what is called "the walker." It is on this basis that "Devadatta walks" is said. This is just like how a flame's or a sound's continuum arises successively in different locations, and, on this basis, the world speaks of "the flame traveling" or "the sound traveling." In just the same way, because Devadatta's body can serve as a cause for consciousness, the world likewise says "Devadatta cognizes." The noble ones, too, in order to accord with the conventions of worldly speech, likewise speak in this way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the flame-and-sound analogy as an elegant bridge between two images this project has now encountered in related but distinct forms: like the wildfire spreading across a field several sections earlier, a flame does not literally "travel" from one point to another — each moment's flame perishes as the next arises at an adjacent location, and "the flame travels" is simply the conventional way of describing this rapid succession. "Devadatta walks" is claimed to work by an identical mechanism: no single, unified Devadatta relocates from one spot to another; rather, a causal continuum conventionally called "Devadatta" gives rise to its own continuation at a new location, and this arising-elsewhere is what "walking" actually names.

We should also flag the closing note about "the noble ones" speaking this way "to accord with worldly convention" as an important qualification this project has seen the treatise make before, most explicitly around the Buddha's own use of ordinary self-referring language: adopting the grammar of agency ("Devadatta walks," "I remember," "consciousness cognizes") is not itself a concession that a real, unified agent exists behind these expressions — even those who have fully understood the underlying causal reality continue speaking in the same conventional idiom everyone else uses, simply because language built around agents and actions is how communication works, not because it commits the speaker to the metaphysics that idiom might seem, on its surface, to presuppose.

13. What Does Consciousness Actually Do to Its Object? Fruit, Lamp, and Gong

The treatise asks a genuinely precise question about what "cognizing" could even mean at the level of a single moment: does consciousness do something to its object, the way a hand might grasp a cup? The answer is no — consciousness does nothing to its object at all, but simply arises resembling it, in exactly the sense a fruit is said to "repay" its cause, or a lamp is said to "travel."

Chinese Source

經說諸識能了所緣，識於所緣為何所作？都無所作，但以境生。如果酬因，雖無所作而似因起，說名酬因。如是識生，雖無所作而似境，故說名了境。如何似境？謂帶彼相。是故諸識雖亦託根生，不名了根，但名為了境。或識於境相續生時，前識為因引後識起，說識能了亦無有失，世間於因說作者故。如世間說鍾鼓能鳴，或如燈能行，識能了亦爾。為依何理說燈能行？焰相續中假立燈號，燈於異處相續生時說為燈行，無別行者。如是心相續假立識名，於異境生時說名能了。或如色有色生色住，此中無別有生住者。說識能了，理亦應然。

Ru-Yi Translation

A sūtra states that consciousness is capable of cognizing its object — but what does consciousness actually do to its object? It does nothing to it at all; it simply arises by means of that object as its condition. This is like how a fruit is said to "repay" its cause: although it does nothing to its cause, it arises resembling its cause, and so is said to "repay" it. In just the same way, consciousness, when it arises, does nothing to its object, yet it arises resembling that object — hence it is said to "cognize the object." In what sense does it "resemble" the object? It means it bears that object's own aspect. This is why, although consciousness also arises in dependence on a sense-faculty, it is never said to "cognize the faculty," but only ever to "cognize the object." Or, alternatively: when consciousness arises in succession with respect to its object, an earlier consciousness serves as the cause drawing forth a later one — saying consciousness "cognizes" involves no fault under this reading either, since ordinary usage applies the term "agent" to a cause. Just as ordinary usage says "the bell and drum can sound," or "the lamp can travel" — consciousness "cognizing" works the very same way. On what basis is it said that "the lamp travels"? The name "lamp" is conventionally applied to a continuum of flame; when this continuum arises successively in different locations, it is said that "the lamp travels" — there is no separate "traveler" involved. In just the same way, the name "consciousness" is conventionally applied to a continuum of mind; when it arises with respect to different objects, it is said to "cognize." Or, just as form is said to "arise" and to "abide," without there being any separate agent of arising or abiding apart from the form itself — the same principle should apply to saying consciousness "cognizes."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag "bearing that object's own aspect" (帶彼相) as a technically precise, load-bearing phrase in this passage: consciousness "resembles" its object not in some vague, general sense, but by literally taking on that object's specific form or image as part of its own arising — which is also why consciousness is only ever said to "cognize" its object and never its enabling sense-faculty, even though it depends causally on both. The faculty makes the cognizing possible, but only the object supplies the aspect the resulting consciousness actually bears; the asymmetry in ordinary language ("I see the color," never "I see my eye") tracks this real, structural asymmetry rather than being an arbitrary quirk of speech.

We should also flag the fruit-repaying-its-cause analogy as reaching back to this project's earlier fascicles on karma: a ripened karmic fruit does not "do" anything to the karmic seed that produced it, any more than the seed reaches forward in time to "do" something to its future fruit — the relationship is simply that the fruit arises resembling, and thereby answering to, its cause. Applying this same non-agentive model to consciousness and its object lets the treatise affirm the sūtra's ordinary language ("consciousness cognizes its object") while emptying that language of any implication that cognition is some kind of action performed by one thing upon another — it is, structurally, the same causal resemblance relationship running throughout this entire chapter's account of memory, walking, and now cognition itself.

14. Why Later Minds Differ from Earlier Ones: Sequence Without a Self

The treatise now explains why consciousness arises from a prior consciousness rather than from a self: unlike a hypothetical unchanging self, the mind's continuum genuinely varies moment to moment, following patterned but not rigid sequences — illustrated with a memorable example of how a single woman's mind can give rise to several different possible follow-on states, and why the strongest-cultivated one tends to win out.

Chinese Source

若後識生，從識非我。何緣從識？不恒似前，及不定次生，如芽莖葉等，有為皆有住異相故。謂諸有為自性法爾，微細相續後必異前。若異此者，縱意入定，身心相續相似而生，後念與初無差別故，不應最後念自然從定出。諸心相續亦有定次，若此心次彼心應生，於此心後彼必生故。亦有少分行相等心方能相生，種性別故。如女心無間起嚴污身心，或起彼夫彼子心等。後時從此諸心相續轉變差別還生女心。如是女心於後所起嚴污心等有生功能，異此無功能，由種性別故。女心無間容起多心，然多心中若先數起、明了近起，先起非餘。由如是心修力強故，唯除將起位身外緣差別。諸有修力最強盛者，寧不恒時生於自果？由此心有住異相故，此住異相於別修果相續生中最隨順故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a later consciousness arises, it arises from a prior consciousness, not from a self. Why does it arise from consciousness? Because it does not always resemble what came before it, and does not arise in any fixed, single sequence — like a sprout, stem, and leaf, since all conditioned things possess the characteristics of abiding and change. That is, it is the very nature of all conditioned things that, within their subtle continuum, what comes later must necessarily differ from what came before. If it were otherwise, then even when the mind enters concentration, body and mind would continue arising in a manner exactly the same throughout — and since there would then be no difference between the final moment and the very first, one should never spontaneously emerge from concentration at that final moment. The mind's continuum also follows a fixed sequence in certain respects: if this mind is next to be followed by that mind, then that mind must necessarily arise after this one. There are also certain minds that, sharing only a small portion of their aspect, are nonetheless capable of generating one another, owing to differences in their underlying disposition. For example, a woman's mind may, immediately following, give rise to a mind of adorning and beautifying the body, or give rise to a mind directed toward her husband, or toward her child, and so on. Later, from these various minds' continuum, through transformation and differentiation, the woman's original mind arises once again. In this way, the woman's mind has the specific functional capacity to give rise to the subsequently arising mind of adorning the body and the rest; apart from this specific capacity, it has no such function, owing to the difference in underlying disposition. Immediately following the woman's mind, many different minds may possibly arise; yet, among these many possible minds, whichever has previously been most frequently cultivated, is clearest, and most recently active, is the one that actually arises first — not any of the others. This is because that mind's power of cultivation is strongest — except in cases where, at the very moment of arising, some external bodily condition intervenes and differs. For whichever mind's power of cultivation is strongest of all, how could it fail to consistently produce its own corresponding result? This is because the mind possesses the characteristics of abiding and change, and it is this same characteristic of abiding-and-change that most readily accommodates the arising of a distinctly cultivated result within the continuum.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the concentration-emergence argument as a clever piece of internal evidence: if the mind genuinely stayed identical from moment to moment while absorbed in concentration, there would be nothing to distinguish the final moment of that absorption from any other moment within it, and so no explanation for why a practitioner spontaneously emerges at one particular point rather than continuing indefinitely. The fact that meditators do reliably emerge from concentration is offered as evidence that the mind's continuum is subtly changing throughout, even during states that feel, subjectively, perfectly stable and unvarying.

We should also flag the woman's-mind example as a vivid, almost novelistic illustration of a genuinely technical point about probabilistic-but-patterned causation: a single mental state does not mechanically determine one single successor, since several different follow-on states (adorning herself, thinking of her husband, thinking of her child) are all genuinely possible from the same starting point. What actually determines which one arises is not randomness but relative strength of prior cultivation — whichever associated mental tendency has been most frequently reinforced, is currently clearest, and was most recently active will tend to win out over its rivals, barring some external interruption. This gives the treatise a way to explain both the patterned regularity of mental life (certain minds reliably follow certain others) and its genuine variability (the same starting state can lead to different outcomes) without needing to posit anything beyond the continuum's own accumulated habits and their relative strength.

15. The Peacock's Feather: Only Omniscience Grasps Every Causal Detail

Before moving on, the treatise pauses to acknowledge the limits of its own explanation, closing this stretch on mental causation with a striking image: if even the precise causal history behind a single eye-spot on a peacock's tail-feather lies beyond any but the Buddha's own complete knowledge, how much more so the far subtler causal differentiation running through mind and its factors.

Chinese Source

諸心品類次第相生因緣方隅我已略說，委悉了達唯在世尊，一切法中智自在故。依如是義故，有頌言： 於一孔雀輪， 一切種因相， 非餘智境界， 唯一切智知。 色差別因尚為難了，況心心所諸無色法因緣差別可易了知？

Ru-Yi Translation

I have now briefly explained the general direction of the causes and conditions by which the various categories of mind arise from one another in sequence; fully and exhaustively understanding this belongs to the Buddha alone, since he has complete mastery of knowledge with respect to all dharmas. On the basis of this meaning, there is a verse that states: with respect to a single peacock's tail-feather eye, the causes and characteristics of its every variation are not within the domain of any other knowledge — only omniscience can know them. If even the causal differentiation of something material is this difficult to fully understand, how much more so the causal differentiation of mind, mental factors, and the other formless dharmas — how could these possibly be easy to understand?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag this passage as a rare moment of explicit epistemic humility within a chapter otherwise built almost entirely from confident, methodical refutation: having just walked through a genuinely intricate account of how memory, cognition, and sequences of mental states can all be explained through causal continuity alone, the treatise stops to note that its own explanation is necessarily a sketch of the "general direction" only — the full, granular causal history behind any single moment of experience is explicitly placed beyond what the text itself, or any teacher short of a Buddha, could ever fully lay out.

We should also flag the choice of a peacock's feather specifically as a memorable, concrete image for combinatorial complexity: a single eye-spot's precise pattern of colors and their exact placement is the visible result of an enormous, largely invisible causal history, and the verse's point is that even this comparatively simple, purely material case already outstrips ordinary knowledge — a natural bridge into the far more complex domain of mind and mental factors, whose causal patterning this entire fascicle has been trying to describe only in outline. The image also serves as a graceful transition, signaling that the detailed sequence-by-sequence account of mental causation is now complete, and the discussion is about to turn to a different, related question.

16. A Heterodox Theory That All Minds Arise From a Self — and the Quack Doctor

The treatise turns to a specific heterodox position that every mind arises entirely from a self, and presses the same two objections raised earlier — that later minds neither always resemble nor reliably follow earlier ones — directly against it, working through every proposed way a self might "conjoin" with the mental faculty to produce this variation, and finding each one incoherent.

Chinese Source

一類外道作如是執，諸心生時皆從於我。前之二難於彼最切。若諸心生皆從我者，何緣後識不恒似前，及不定次生，如芽莖葉等。若謂由待意合差別有異識生，理定不然，我與餘合非極成故，又二物合有分限故。謂彼自類釋合相言，非至為先、後至名合。我與意合應有分限，意移轉故我應移轉，或應與意俱有壞滅。若謂一分合，理定不然，於一我體中無別分故。設許有合，我體既常，意無別異，合寧有別？若待別覺，為難亦同。謂覺因何得有差別？若待行別，我意合者，則應但心待行差別能生異識，何用我為？我於識生都無有用，而言諸識皆從我生。如藥事成能除痼疾，誑醫矯說普莎訶言。

Ru-Yi Translation

A certain group of heterodox teachers holds this view: whenever a mind arises, it arises entirely from a self. The two objections given above apply to this position most pointedly. If every arising mind comes entirely from a self, why does a later consciousness not always resemble the one before it, and why does it not arise in a fixed sequence, the way a sprout, stem, and leaf do? If it is said that a differing consciousness arises depending on the specific way the self conjoins with the mental faculty, this is certainly not correct in principle, since the very notion of a self conjoining with anything else is not an established, agreed premise, and moreover, because when two things conjoin, there must be some boundary or limit involved. That is, according to their own school's explanation of what "conjoining" means: not having yet arrived, and then subsequently arriving — this is what is called "conjoining." If the self conjoins with the mental faculty in this sense, it should have some boundary or limit; since the mental faculty moves and shifts, the self should likewise have to move and shift, or else it should have to perish together with the mental faculty. If it is said that only a part of the self conjoins, this too is certainly incorrect in principle, since there is no separate part within a single, unified self-substance. Even granting that conjoining occurs, since the self's substance is permanent and the mental faculty is presumably meant to remain without distinguishing variation, how could the conjoining itself possibly differ from one instance to the next? If it is said that this depends instead on a distinct cognition, the same objection applies equally: by what cause could this cognition itself come to differ? If it is said that the self conjoins with the mental faculty depending on differences among the formations, then it should simply be the mind, depending on differences among the formations, that is capable of generating a differing consciousness — what use is the self for this at all? The self serves no function whatsoever in the arising of consciousness, and yet it is claimed that all consciousness arises entirely from the self — this is like a quack doctor, once a medicine has actually worked to cure a chronic illness, falsely claiming credit by reciting some magic incantation over it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the systematic exhaustion of every proposed reading of "conjoining" here as following the same method this project has now seen applied to "depends on" (the fire-and-fuel analogy, several fascicles earlier): rather than disputing the heterodox position's conclusion directly, the treatise forces a precise definition of the key mechanism — what does "the self conjoins with the mind" actually mean? — and shows that every available answer (spatial arrival, partial conjoining, dependence on a distinct cognition, dependence on the formations) either contradicts something the position itself holds (a permanent, partless self cannot coherently "move" or have "parts" that conjoin) or quietly does all the real explanatory work without the self's involvement at all.

We should also flag the quack-doctor image as a sharp, almost comic capstone to this argument: the objection's final form is not that positing a self for consciousness's arising is merely unproven, but that it is explanatorily idle — once differences among the formations are already doing all the work of producing a differing consciousness, adding a self to the story changes nothing about the outcome, exactly as a chanted incantation changes nothing about whether a medicine that was always going to work does its job. The self, on this diagnosis, is not a false cause exactly — it is worse, a claimed cause that never needed to be there to begin with.

17. The Earth and Its Fragrance: An Opponent's Simile Backfires

Pressed to explain what "the self supports mind and the formations" could even mean, the heterodox opponent reaches for a seemingly innocent comparison — the self is like the earth, which supports fragrance and the other sensory qualities. The treatise seizes on this simile with evident delight, since earth's relationship to fragrance turns out to be exactly its own position, not the opponent's.

Chinese Source

若謂此二由我故有，此但有言，無理為證。若謂此二我為所依，如誰與誰為所依義？非心與行如畫如果，我為能持如壁如器，如是便有更相礙失，及有或時別住失故。非如壁器，我為彼依。若爾如何？此但如地，能為香等四物所依。彼如是言，證成無我，故我於此深生喜慰。如世間地不離香等，我亦應爾，非離心行。誰能了地離於香等？但於香等聚集差別，世俗流布立以地名。我亦應然，但於心等諸蘊差別，假立我名。若離香等無別有地，如何說言地有香等？為顯地體有香等別，故即於地說有香等，令他了達是此非餘，如世間言木像身等。

Ru-Yi Translation

If it is claimed that these two exist because of the self, this is merely an assertion, with no actual reasoning to prove it. If it is claimed that the self serves as the support for these two, then what exactly does "support" mean here — of what, for what? It cannot mean that mind and the formations are like a painting or like fruit, with the self serving as what supports them the way a wall supports a painting, or a container holds fruit — since this would incur the fault of the two mutually obstructing each other, and the further fault that, at certain times, they could exist apart from one another. The self is not like a wall or container serving as their support in that sense, the opponent concedes. What, then, is it like? It is simply like the earth, which serves as the support for the four things — fragrance and the rest. In saying this, they have actually proven that there is no self — and so I find myself deeply gladdened by this very point. Just as, in the world, earth is never apart from fragrance and the rest, the self, by this same logic, should likewise never be apart from mind and the formations. Who could ever perceive "earth" apart from fragrance and the rest? It is simply with reference to a specific collection of fragrance and the other qualities that the conventional, popularly current name "earth" is established. The self should be understood in exactly the same way: it is simply with reference to a specific collection of mind and the other aggregates that the conventional name "self" is applied. If there is no separate "earth" apart from fragrance and the rest, how can one still say "earth has fragrance" and so on? This is simply in order to indicate that the substance conventionally called "earth" possesses these distinct qualities — fragrance and the rest — that one speaks of "earth having fragrance," so as to help others understand that this specific quality belongs to this particular case and not some other, just as ordinary speech refers to "a wooden statue's body" and the like.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag "so I find myself deeply gladdened by this very point" as one of the few moments in this entire chapter, and arguably in the whole treatise as translated across this project, where Vasubandhu's own personal voice breaks through the otherwise impersonal, dialectical register — a small flash of evident satisfaction at watching an opponent's own chosen analogy do the treatise's work for it. The rhetorical move is genuinely elegant: the opponent reaches for "earth supports fragrance" specifically to avoid the wall-and-painting problem (separable, mutually obstructing things), not realizing that earth and fragrance are inseparable in exactly the way the treatise has been arguing mind and self-designation are — which means the analogy proves there is no separately existing "earth" behind its qualities, exactly paralleling the treatise's claim that there is no separately existing self behind the aggregates.

We should also flag the closing point about how "earth has fragrance" can still be meaningfully said, despite there being no separate earth apart from fragrance, as resolving a natural worry a careful reader might have at this stage: doesn't denying any separate "earth" behind its qualities make it incoherent to attribute those qualities to earth at all? The treatise's answer — that such language simply distinguishes this particular collection of qualities from others, the way "a wooden statue's body" specifies which body without implying the statue possesses some separate body beneath its wood — gives the whole chapter's conventional, aggregate-based account of personhood its clearest possible parallel: "the self has feelings," "the self remembers," and "the self acts" can all be true in exactly this same sense, without ever requiring a separately real self standing behind the aggregates they are said of.
