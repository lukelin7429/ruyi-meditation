Why "Karma-Path," and What Cuts Off the Wholesome Roots

Fascicle 17 opens the fifth part of Chapter 4 (分別業品第四之五, the Karma-nirdeśa), continuing directly from fascicle 16's completed survey of the ten karma-paths' precise definitions. It begins with a technical question the treatise has been assuming an answer to all along: why is the term "karma-path" applied differently to different members of the set of ten, and what does the term itself actually mean? From there it turns to a new and consequential topic — how the wholesome roots can be entirely cut off in a person's mental continuum, and by what process they are eventually restored.

1. Why These Are Called "Karma-Paths"

Having finished describing the character of all ten karma-paths, the treatise pauses to ask a technical question implicit in everything that has come before: by what meaning is the compound term "karma-path" (業道, literally "karma" plus "path") actually applied? Its answer distinguishes the ten into two groups. The last three — greed, hatred, and wrong view — are only "paths," not karma in their own right, since it is the associated volition that constitutes the actual karma, while greed, hatred, and wrong view merely provide the path or channel along which that volition runs its course and through whose power it acts. The first seven — killing, theft, sexual misconduct, and the four kinds of speech — are both karma in their own right, since they are bodily and verbal karma, and also "paths," since volition travels through them: the volition that initiates bodily and verbal karma proceeds by taking that very bodily or verbal karma as its object. This is why the single compound "karma-path" is used to express two rather different relationships at once — a term applied to two dissimilar cases under one label, which the treatise notes is common enough even in ordinary, non-Buddhist writings. The same reasoning, suitably adapted, explains why non-greed, non-hatred, and right view (the wholesome counterparts) also receive this name. An objection follows: if this is so, why are a karma-path's preparatory action and follow-up action not themselves called karma-paths, given that they too involve karma and volition? The reply is that preparatory and follow-up action operate only in dependence on the root act itself, and, as already explained in fascicle 16, they belong to a coarser, less precisely defined class of activity — it is specifically the root act, and only the root act, through which internal or external things are actually increased or decreased, that qualifies for the name. A further objection raises the Dārṣṭāntika position, discussed already in fascicle 16, that greed, hatred, and wrong view are themselves already a form of mental karma rather than merely a path for karma to travel — if so, by what reasoning could that school still call them "karma-paths" at all? The treatise's answer is candid: this question should properly be put to the Dārṣṭāntika masters themselves. It nonetheless offers two possible accounts on their behalf — either that these are called karma-paths because, as mental karma, they lead toward an unfortunate rebirth-destiny (a "path" in that sense), or else that karma and path here simply "ride upon" one another mutually, so that both labels can apply to both terms. With this technical point addressed, the treatise notes that all ten unwholesome karma-paths just described stand in direct opposition to the actual arising of wholesome states — setting up its next topic, the destruction of the wholesome roots themselves.

Ru-Yi Translation

We have now finished explaining the character of the ten karma-paths. By what meaning are they called "karma-paths"? Verse: among these, the [last] three are only "paths"; the [first] seven are karma and also "paths." Explanation: among the ten karma-paths, the last three are only "paths," since they establish a path along which karma travels, and thus receive the name "karma-path." The volition associated with them is what is called "karma"; because of them, it runs its course, and because of them, it acts, since it carries out its activity through their power. The first seven are karma, being bodily and verbal karma, and are also the "path" of karma, since volition travels through them — because the volition that initiates bodily and verbal karma proceeds by taking that bodily or verbal karma as its object. [Thus] both "karma" and "the path of karma" receive the name "karma-path"; hence, when we speak of "karma-path" here, this fully expresses the meaning both of "the path of karma" and of "karma that is also a path." Although these are not the same kind of thing, one term is applied to both, since this is also fully accepted even in ordinary [non-Buddhist] treatises. Apart from killing and the rest of the seven, and apart from non-greed and the rest of the three, the establishing of the name "karma-path" should be explained analogously. [Objection:] Why are this [root act's] preparatory action and follow-up action not themselves karma-paths? [Reply:] Because it is in dependence on this [root act] that those in turn operate. Moreover, as stated earlier, these are included within the coarser class. Further, it is specifically that through which there is decrease or increase, causing internal or external things to increase or decrease, that is established as the karma-path; apart from this, it is not so. [Objection:] The Dārṣṭāntika masters hold that greed, hatred, and the like are themselves mental karma — by what meaning, then, do they explain these as being called karma-paths? [Reply:] This should be asked of those masters. Still, one may also say: these are mental karma that lead to an unfortunate destiny, and are thus established under the name "karma-path"; or that [karma and path] mutually ride upon one another, and both receive the name "karma-path." The ten unwholesome karma-paths thus described are all contrary to the actual arising of wholesome dharmas.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should be transparent that "karma-path" (業道) is a slightly awkward compound in English, and this section is essentially the treatise's own explanation of why the compound holds together two different relationships: for greed/hatred/wrong-view, the "karma" is the associated volition and these three are merely its "path"; for the other seven, the act itself is both the karma and the path volition travels through. We have kept "karma-path" as a single consistent rendering throughout this project for readability, even though this section shows the term is doing double duty — readers should understand the technical distinction being made here rather than assume the phrase always means exactly the same thing in every occurrence.

The exchange about the Dārṣṭāntika masters is notable for its candor: rather than forcing a rival school's terminology into its own framework, the treatise explicitly says the question should be asked of that school directly, then offers two possible charitable reconstructions on their behalf without claiming either is certainly correct. We have preserved this open-endedness rather than picking one answer for the Dārṣṭāntika.

2. How the Wholesome Roots Are Cut Off

The treatise opens a substantial new inquiry with a two-verse summary: only complete wrong view cuts off the wholesome roots; what is cut off is desire-realm, born-of-maturation wholesome roots; the wrong view that accomplishes this denies both cause and effect entirely; the cutting-off may be gradual, and both [good and bad, in a sense] are relinquished together; it happens only in a human body, only in three of the four continents, only to males or females, and only to those of "view" temperament, its nature being non-possession; wholesome roots are restored through doubt or right view arising, either gradually or, in the case of those who have committed an ānantarya offense, all at once — except that even they cannot restore them in this very life. Working through this systematically: among the unwholesome karma-paths, only fully mature, "upper-grade" wrong view is capable of cutting off the wholesome roots — specifically the view that categorically denies any causal connection between good or bad conduct and their results. This wrong view is described as functioning like a thief who sets a fire that then burns down a village: the wrong view itself is what finally severs the roots, but the other unwholesome roots (which gave rise to it in the first place) are popularly said to be responsible for the "burning," much as the village is said to be burned by the thief who started the blaze rather than by the fire itself. What is cut off is specifically the desire-realm wholesome roots that arise through simple ripening (as opposed to those cultivated through meditative effort, which are said to have already retreated beforehand) — form-realm and formless-realm wholesome roots were never established to begin with in a being capable of this, so there is nothing there to cut. On the question of whether the cutting-off happens all at once or gradually, the treatise records a range of positions: some hold the nine grades of wholesome roots are cut off all at once, in a single instant of wrong view, just as the path of seeing abandons its afflictions all at once; others hold they are cut off gradually, grade by grade, the very weakest wrong view able to sever only the very strongest wholesome roots and so on in sequence, just as the path of cultivation gradually abandons its own afflictions — and the treatise notes that this gradual account better fits the root treatise's own wording, which states that "cutting off the wholesome roots" refers to the state in which even the last, most minute remaining wholesome root is finally relinquished, since as long as even one grade of wholesome root remains, it can still give rise to the others, and cutting is only complete once nothing at all is left. This cutting-off is possible only within a human body, and only in three of the four continents (excluding Uttarakuru, whose inhabitants lack the capacity for such an extremely evil underlying disposition), and only in a male or female body (not, according to one view, in the body of specific categories of sexually ambiguous or impotent persons, whose faculties are said to be too dull for this), and, crucially, only by those of "view" temperament rather than "craving" temperament, since a craving-natured person's evil disposition is too restless and shallow to sustain such a thoroughgoing severance, while a view-natured person's evil disposition runs extremely deep — the treatise adds that this is also why beings such as paṇḍakas (a category of sexually ambiguous persons) cannot cut off the wholesome roots, since they belong to the "craving" type. Finally, on the deeper metaphysical question of what this "cutting" actually consists of, the treatise gives its now-familiar Vaibhāṣika answer: it is non-possession that constitutes the substance of the cutting, since, at the moment the wholesome roots are cut, no further possession of wholesome dharmas arises, while non-possession continues to arise in an unbroken chain, taking the place where possession would otherwise have continued.

如是所說十惡業道，皆與善法現起相違。諸斷善根由何業道？斷續善相差別云何？頌曰：唯邪見斷善，所斷欲生得，撥因果一切，漸斷二俱捨。人三洲男女，見行斷非得，續善疑有見，頓現除逆者。論曰：惡業道中唯有上品圓滿邪見能斷善根。若爾，何緣本論中說云何上品諸不善根，謂諸不善根能斷善根者？或離欲位最初所除，由不善根能引邪見，故邪見事推在彼根。如火燒村，火由賊起，故世間說彼賊燒村。何等善根為此所斷？謂唯欲界生得善根，色無色善先不成故。《施設足論》當云何通？如彼論言：唯由此量是人已斷三界善根，依上善根得更遠說，令此相續非彼器故。何緣唯斷生得善根？加行善根先已退故。緣何邪見能斷善根？謂定撥無因果邪見。撥無因者，謂定撥無妙行惡行。撥無果者，謂定撥無彼果異熟。有餘師說：此二邪見猶如無間解脫道別。有餘師說：斷善邪見唯緣有漏非無漏緣，唯自界緣不緣他界。由彼唯作相應隨眠，境不隨增，勢力劣故。如是說者通一切緣，隨因亦增，有強力故。有餘師說：九品善根由一剎那邪見頓斷，如見道斷見所斷惑。如是說者漸斷善根，謂九品善根由九品邪見逆順相對漸次而斷。如修道斷修所斷惑，即下下邪見能斷上上善根，乃至下下善根上上邪見所斷。若作是說，符本論文。如本論言：云何名微俱行善根？謂斷善根時最後所捨者，由捨彼故名斷善根。若爾，彼文何理復說：云何上品諸不善根？謂諸不善根能斷善根者。彼依究竟密說此言，由此善根斷無餘故。謂若猶有一品善根，餘品善根因斯可起，未可說彼名斷善根；斷究竟時方名斷善，故唯說上品名能斷善根。有餘師言：斷九品善終無中出如見道中。如是說者，通出不出。有餘師說：先捨律儀後斷善根，未易捨故。如是說者，若彼律儀是此品心所等起果，此品心斷捨彼律儀，以果與因品類同故。為在何處能斷善根？人趣三洲非在惡趣亦非天趣。所以者何？以惡趣中染不染慧不堅牢故，以天趣中現見善惡諸業果故。言三洲者，除北俱盧，彼無極惡阿世耶故。有餘師說：唯贍部洲。若爾，便違本論所說。如本論說：贍部洲人極少成八根，東西洲亦爾。如是斷善，依何類身？唯男女身，志意定故。有餘師說：亦非女身，欲勤慧等皆昧鈍故。若爾，便違本論所說。如本論說：若成女根定成八根，男根亦爾。為何行者能斷善根？唯見行人非愛行者，諸愛行者惡阿世耶極躁動故，諸見行者惡阿世耶極堅深故。由斯理趣，非扇搋等能斷善根，愛行類故。又此類人如惡趣故。此善根斷其體是何？善斷應知非得為體，以斷善位善得不生非得續生，替善根得非得生位，名斷善根。故斷善根非得為體。

Ru-Yi Translation

The ten unwholesome karma-paths thus described are all contrary to the actual arising of wholesome dharmas. By which karma-path is the cutting-off of the wholesome roots accomplished? What is the difference between the characteristics of their cutting-off and their restoration? Verse: only wrong view cuts off wholesome roots; what is cut off is desire-realm, born-of-maturation [wholesome roots]; it denies cause and effect entirely; the cutting-off may be gradual, and both are relinquished together; [this occurs] in human [bodies], three continents, male and female, [those of] view-temperament; its nature is non-possession; restoration comes through doubt or [right] view arising, gradually or all at once, except for those who have committed an ānantarya offense. Explanation: among the unwholesome karma-paths, only upper-grade, fully complete wrong view is able to cut off the wholesome roots. [Objection:] If so, why does the root treatise state: what are the upper-grade unwholesome roots? Those unwholesome roots that are capable of cutting off the wholesome roots. [Reply:] This refers either to what is first eliminated at the stage of freedom from desire, or [is stated thus] because the unwholesome roots are what give rise to the wrong view, and so the matter of the wrong view is attributed back to those roots — just as a village is burned by fire that a thief set, yet the world says that thief burned the village. Which wholesome roots are cut off by this? Only the desire-realm, born-of-maturation wholesome roots, since form-realm and formless-realm wholesome roots were never established [in such a person] to begin with. [Objection:] How is this to be reconciled with the Prajñaptiśāstra? [Reply:] As that treatise states, it is only by this measure that a person is said to have cut off the wholesome roots of the three realms; with reference to the higher wholesome roots, it speaks further still, meaning that this continuum is no longer a vessel for them. [Objection:] Why are only the born-of-maturation wholesome roots cut off? [Reply:] Because the wholesome roots cultivated through effort have already retreated beforehand. [Objection:] In dependence on which wrong view can the wholesome roots be cut off? [Reply:] The wrong view that categorically denies cause and effect. Denying the cause means categorically denying good and bad conduct; denying the effect means categorically denying their ripened result. Other teachers say: these two wrong views are like the uninterrupted path and the path of liberation, [functioning as] distinct [phases]. Other teachers say: the wrong view that cuts off wholesome roots takes only contaminated objects, not uncontaminated ones, as its object, and takes only objects of its own realm, not another realm — since it only functions as an associated latent tendency, its object does not increase in strength [across realms], its power being weak. Those who say otherwise hold it takes all objects, and increases in accordance with its cause, having strong power. Other teachers say: the nine grades of wholesome roots are cut off all at once, in a single instant, by wrong view, just as the path of seeing abandons the afflictions to be abandoned by seeing. Those who say otherwise hold the wholesome roots are cut off gradually — the nine grades of wholesome roots are cut off gradually, in reverse-and-forward correspondence, by the nine grades of wrong view, just as the path of cultivation gradually abandons the afflictions to be abandoned by cultivation — that is, the very weakest wrong view is able to cut off the very strongest wholesome roots, on up to the very strongest wrong view cutting off the very weakest wholesome roots. If stated this way, it accords with the root treatise's text. As the root treatise states: what is meant by "the most minute accompanying wholesome root"? That which is relinquished last at the time the wholesome roots are cut off — it is by relinquishing this that one is said to have cut off the wholesome roots. [Objection:] If so, by what reasoning does that same text also state: what are the upper-grade unwholesome roots? Those unwholesome roots capable of cutting off the wholesome roots. [Reply:] That statement speaks with reference to the final completion, with hidden intent, since it is by this that the wholesome roots are cut off without remainder — that is, as long as even one grade of wholesome root still remains, the other grades of wholesome root can still arise on account of it, and one cannot yet say the wholesome roots have been cut off; only when the cutting is complete is it called "cutting off the wholesome roots," and so only the upper grade is said to be capable of cutting off the wholesome roots. Other teachers say: in cutting off the nine grades of wholesome roots, there is never any emerging partway through, as there can be in the path of seeing. Those who say otherwise hold that both emerging and not emerging partway through are possible. Other teachers say: one first relinquishes one's restraint-vow, and only afterward cuts off the wholesome roots, since the vow is not easily relinquished. Those who say otherwise hold that if that restraint-vow is the result initiated by this grade of mind, then when this grade of mind is cut off, that restraint-vow is relinquished as well, since result and cause belong to the same category. In what location can the wholesome roots be cut off? In the human destiny, in three continents — not in the bad destinies, nor in the heavens. Why is this? Because in the bad destinies, whether defiled or undefiled, wisdom is not stable enough; and because in the heavens, the results of good and bad karma are directly seen [making such denial impossible]. By "three continents" is meant excluding Uttarakuru, since its inhabitants lack an extremely evil underlying disposition. Other teachers say: only in Jambudvīpa. [Objection:] If so, this contradicts what the root treatise states — the root treatise states that the people of Jambudvīpa possess, at minimum, eight faculties, and the same holds for the eastern and western continents. In what kind of body does this cutting-off of wholesome roots occur? Only in a male or female body, since their resolve is fixed. Other teachers say: also not in a female body, since desire, diligence, wisdom, and the like are all too dull [in women]. [Objection:] If so, this contradicts what the root treatise states — the root treatise states that whoever possesses the female faculty definitely possesses [all] eight faculties, and likewise for the male faculty. Which type of practitioner is capable of cutting off the wholesome roots? Only the view-practitioner, not the craving-practitioner, since the craving-practitioner's evil underlying disposition is extremely restless, while the view-practitioner's evil underlying disposition is extremely deep and firm. By this reasoning, paṇḍakas and the like cannot cut off the wholesome roots, being of the craving type; moreover, such persons are like those in the bad destinies. What is the substance of this cutting-off of wholesome roots? It should be understood that this cutting-off has non-possession as its substance: at the stage of cutting off the wholesome roots, possession of wholesome dharmas no longer arises, while non-possession continues to arise in succession, taking the place where the arising of possession of the wholesome roots would otherwise have occurred — this is called cutting off the wholesome roots. Hence, the cutting-off of wholesome roots has non-possession as its substance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should be honest that this section preserves an unusually large number of unresolved "other teachers say" alternatives on nearly every sub-question — whether the two component wrong views (denying cause, denying effect) are simultaneous or sequential like the uninterrupted-and-liberation paths; whether the cutting-off is sudden or gradual (with the treatise leaning toward gradual as better fitting the root text, but not flatly rejecting the sudden view); whether emerging partway through the process is possible; whether the restraint-vow or the wholesome roots are relinquished first; and whether a female body is capable of this at all, a claim the treatise itself immediately flags as contradicting the root treatise's own statement that women can possess all eight faculties. We have translated this last, disputed claim faithfully as part of the historical text's own internal debate, without endorsing it — the treatise's own citation of a contradicting authority suggests this view was already controversial within the tradition itself, not a settled position.

The "craving-practitioner cannot cut off wholesome roots" reasoning, and its application to paṇḍakas specifically, reflects this treatise's broader (and, by modern standards, often unflattering) classification of certain persons as inherently spiritually limited by temperament or embodiment — a recurring feature of this scholastic tradition that we have flagged before (e.g. fascicle 14's discussion of who may take ordination) and flag again here rather than silently smoothing over.

3. How the Wholesome Roots Are Restored

Having explained how the wholesome roots are cut off, the treatise turns to their restoration. Once cut off, the wholesome roots are restored through the arising of either doubt or right view: when doubt arises regarding whether cause and effect actually exist after all ("perhaps there really is such a thing"), or when right view directly arises, settling that cause and effect definitely exist and are not non-existent, the possession of the wholesome roots begins to arise once more, and this is called their restoration. Some teachers hold this restoration happens gradually, across the nine grades, the way a serious illness first subsides before one's strength gradually returns. As for whether this restoration can happen within the very same lifetime in which the wholesome roots were cut off: it generally can, with one significant exception — a person who has committed one of the five ānantarya offenses (killing one's father, killing one's mother, killing an arhat, causing a schism in the monastic community, or maliciously wounding a buddha) can never restore the wholesome roots within that same life, according to the sūtras on this point; instead, such a person is capable of restoring them only when about to die and fall into hell, or precisely at the moment of being reborn there — that is, either during the intermediate state leading to that hellish rebirth, or at the very point of death immediately preceding it — and at no other point. Whether restoration occurs through the force of an internal cause (occurring near death) or through the force of an external condition (occurring near the moment of rebirth) determines which of these two junctures applies; the same reasoning holds whether the determining force comes from the person's own effort or from another's influence. A further distinction concerns exactly what was damaged when the wholesome roots were cut off: if only the person's underlying intention was corrupted, without any accompanying breakdown in outward conduct or precepts, restoration can occur within this very life; but if both the underlying intention and the outward conduct were corrupted together, restoration must wait until after this body has perished. The same distinction applies, correspondingly, to cases involving corrupted view versus corrupted precepts specifically. Finally, the treatise raises a related but distinct question: is everyone who has cut off the wholesome roots necessarily fixed in a wrong destiny (i.e., certain to be reborn in a state from which liberation becomes impossible), and is everyone fixed in a wrong destiny necessarily someone who has cut off the wholesome roots? A four-cornered analysis answers this precisely: some have cut off wholesome roots without being fixed in a wrong destiny — such as the historical figure Pūraṇa and others like him; some are fixed in a wrong destiny without having cut off wholesome roots — such as the historical figure Ajātaśatru and others like him (whose grave offense fixed his destiny through a different mechanism entirely, not through severing his wholesome roots); some are both — such as the historical figure Devadatta and others like him; and some are neither — everyone else, falling under none of the preceding three categories. With this, the discussion of cutting off and restoring the wholesome roots is complete, and the treatise returns to clarify a further point about the karma-paths themselves.

善根斷已由何復續？由疑有見。謂因果中有時生疑：此或應有？或生正見，定有非無。爾時善根得還續起，善得起故名續善根。有餘師言：九品漸續。如是說者，頓續善根然後後時漸漸現起，如頓除病氣力漸增。於現身中能續善不？亦有能續，除造逆人。經依彼人作如是說：彼定於現法，不能續善根。彼人定從地獄將歿、或即於彼將受生時能續善根，非餘位故。言將生位，謂中有中。將歿時言，謂彼將死。若由因力彼斷善根將死時續，若由緣力彼斷善根將生時續，由自他力應知亦爾。又意樂壞非加行壞斷善根者，是人現世能續善根。若意樂壞加行亦壞斷善根者，要身壞後方續善根。見壞戒不壞、見壞戒亦壞斷善根者，應知亦爾。有斷善根非墮邪定，應作四句。第一句者，謂布剌拏等。第二句者，謂未生怨等。第三句者，謂天授等。第四句者，謂除前相。已乘義便辯斷善根，今應復明本業道義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once the wholesome roots have been cut off, by what means are they restored? Through doubt or [right] view arising — that is, when, with respect to cause and effect, doubt sometimes arises: "perhaps this does exist after all"; or when right view arises, [settling that] it definitely exists and is not non-existent — at that time, the possession of the wholesome roots arises once more; because possession arises, this is called the restoration of the wholesome roots. Other teachers say: the nine grades are restored gradually. Those who hold this view say that the wholesome roots are restored all at once and thereafter gradually manifest further, just as when a serious illness is suddenly relieved, one's strength gradually increases thereafter. Can this restoration occur within the present body? It can, except for one who has committed an ānantarya offense. The sūtra, with reference to such a person, states: that person definitely cannot restore the wholesome roots within this present life. That person is only able to restore the wholesome roots when about to die from the hell-state, or precisely at the point of being reborn there — at no other stage. "The stage of being about to be reborn" refers to the intermediate state; "the time of being about to die" refers to when that person is about to die. If it is by the force of a cause, the cut-off wholesome roots are restored at the time of dying; if it is by the force of a condition, the cut-off wholesome roots are restored at the time of being reborn; the same should be understood with respect to the force of oneself or of another. Moreover, one whose underlying intention was corrupted but whose outward conduct was not corrupted, in cutting off the wholesome roots, is a person able to restore the wholesome roots within the present life. One whose underlying intention was corrupted and whose outward conduct was also corrupted, in cutting off the wholesome roots, must wait until after the body has perished before the wholesome roots are restored. The same should be understood for [the pair of] corrupted view without corrupted precepts, and corrupted view together with corrupted precepts. There are those who have cut off wholesome roots without being fixed in a wrong destiny; a four-cornered analysis should be made. The first case: Pūraṇa and others like him. The second case: Ajātaśatru and others like him. The third case: Devadatta and others like him. The fourth case: excluding the preceding characteristics. Having addressed this point in passing, we have finished discussing the cutting off of wholesome roots; we should now further clarify a point about the meaning of the root karma-paths.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three historical figures named here — Pūraṇa (布剌拏, likely Pūraṇa Kāśyapa, one of the six non-Buddhist teachers frequently criticized in Buddhist sūtras as a nihilist), Ajātaśatru (未生怨, "the one not yet born was an enemy," a well-known epithet — the king who imprisoned his own father), and Devadatta (天授, the Buddha's cousin traditionally said to have caused a schism and attempted to kill the Buddha) — are treated here as settled, well-known reference cases from within this tradition's own narrative tradition, rather than as claims we are independently verifying; we render their names as commonly known in English-language Buddhist studies rather than leaving them only in transliterated or literal form, for readability.

We should flag that "fixed in a wrong destiny" (邪定, niyata) is a significant technical status in this tradition, generally meaning a being whose future rebirth in a state that forecloses liberation is now guaranteed by their own karma — this section's point is that cutting off the wholesome roots and being fixed in this way are related but genuinely distinct mechanisms, with different beings arriving at either, both, or neither condition by different paths (Ajātaśatru's case in particular shows that committing an ānantarya offense fixes one's destiny by a route separate from cutting off the wholesome roots specifically).
