Why "Karma-Path," and What Cuts Off the Wholesome Roots

Fascicle 17 completes the fifth part of Chapter 4 (分別業品第四之五, the Karma-nirdeśa), continuing directly from fascicle 16's completed survey of the ten karma-paths' precise definitions. It begins with a technical question the treatise has been assuming an answer to all along — why the term "karma-path" applies differently to different members of the set of ten — then moves through how the wholesome roots can be entirely cut off and eventually restored, an extensive combinatorial treatment of how many karma-paths co-arise and what fruits each type of karma produces, and closes with the three grave obstructions, including a careful account of who can and cannot commit an ānantarya offense.

1. Why These Are Called "Karma-Paths"

Having finished describing the character of all ten karma-paths, the treatise pauses to ask a technical question implicit in everything that has come before: by what meaning is the compound term "karma-path" (業道, literally "karma" plus "path") actually applied? Its answer distinguishes the ten into two groups. The last three — greed, hatred, and wrong view — are only "paths," not karma in their own right, since it is the associated volition that constitutes the actual karma, while greed, hatred, and wrong view merely provide the path or channel along which that volition runs its course and through whose power it acts. The first seven — killing, theft, sexual misconduct, and the four kinds of speech — are both karma in their own right, since they are bodily and verbal karma, and also "paths," since volition travels through them: the volition that initiates bodily and verbal karma proceeds by taking that very bodily or verbal karma as its object. This is why the single compound "karma-path" is used to express two rather different relationships at once — a term applied to two dissimilar cases under one label, which the treatise notes is common enough even in ordinary, non-Buddhist writings. The same reasoning, suitably adapted, explains why non-greed, non-hatred, and right view (the wholesome counterparts) also receive this name. An objection follows: if this is so, why are a karma-path's preparatory action and follow-up action not themselves called karma-paths, given that they too involve karma and volition? The reply is that preparatory and follow-up action operate only in dependence on the root act itself, and, as already explained in fascicle 16, they belong to a coarser, less precisely defined class of activity — it is specifically the root act, and only the root act, through which internal or external things are actually increased or decreased, that qualifies for the name. A further objection raises the Dārṣṭāntika position, discussed already in fascicle 16, that greed, hatred, and wrong view are themselves already a form of mental karma rather than merely a path for karma to travel — if so, by what reasoning could that school still call them "karma-paths" at all? The treatise's answer is candid: this question should properly be put to the Dārṣṭāntika masters themselves. It nonetheless offers two possible accounts on their behalf — either that these are called karma-paths because, as mental karma, they lead toward an unfortunate rebirth-destiny (a "path" in that sense), or else that karma and path here simply "ride upon" one another mutually, so that both labels can apply to both terms. With this technical point addressed, the treatise notes that all ten unwholesome karma-paths just described stand in direct opposition to the actual arising of wholesome states — setting up its next topic, the destruction of the wholesome roots themselves.

Ru-Yi Translation

We have now finished explaining the character of the ten karma-paths. By what meaning are they called "karma-paths"? Verse: among these, the [last] three are only "paths"; the [first] seven are karma and also "paths." Explanation: among the ten karma-paths, the last three are only "paths," since they establish a path along which karma travels, and thus receive the name "karma-path." The volition associated with them is what is called "karma"; because of them, it runs its course, and because of them, it acts, since it carries out its activity through their power. The first seven are karma, being bodily and verbal karma, and are also the "path" of karma, since volition travels through them — because the volition that initiates bodily and verbal karma proceeds by taking that bodily or verbal karma as its object. [Thus] both "karma" and "the path of karma" receive the name "karma-path"; hence, when we speak of "karma-path" here, this fully expresses the meaning both of "the path of karma" and of "karma that is also a path." Although these are not the same kind of thing, one term is applied to both, since this is also fully accepted even in ordinary [non-Buddhist] treatises. Apart from killing and the rest of the seven, and apart from non-greed and the rest of the three, the establishing of the name "karma-path" should be explained analogously. [Objection:] Why are this [root act's] preparatory action and follow-up action not themselves karma-paths? [Reply:] Because it is in dependence on this [root act] that those in turn operate. Moreover, as stated earlier, these are included within the coarser class. Further, it is specifically that through which there is decrease or increase, causing internal or external things to increase or decrease, that is established as the karma-path; apart from this, it is not so. [Objection:] The Dārṣṭāntika masters hold that greed, hatred, and the like are themselves mental karma — by what meaning, then, do they explain these as being called karma-paths? [Reply:] This should be asked of those masters. Still, one may also say: these are mental karma that lead to an unfortunate destiny, and are thus established under the name "karma-path"; or that [karma and path] mutually ride upon one another, and both receive the name "karma-path." The ten unwholesome karma-paths thus described are all contrary to the actual arising of wholesome dharmas.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should be transparent that "karma-path" (業道) is a slightly awkward compound in English, and this section is essentially the treatise's own explanation of why the compound holds together two different relationships: for greed/hatred/wrong-view, the "karma" is the associated volition and these three are merely its "path"; for the other seven, the act itself is both the karma and the path volition travels through. We have kept "karma-path" as a single consistent rendering throughout this project for readability, even though this section shows the term is doing double duty — readers should understand the technical distinction being made here rather than assume the phrase always means exactly the same thing in every occurrence.

The exchange about the Dārṣṭāntika masters is notable for its candor: rather than forcing a rival school's terminology into its own framework, the treatise explicitly says the question should be asked of that school directly, then offers two possible charitable reconstructions on their behalf without claiming either is certainly correct. We have preserved this open-endedness rather than picking one answer for the Dārṣṭāntika.

2. How the Wholesome Roots Are Cut Off

The treatise opens a substantial new inquiry with a two-verse summary: only complete wrong view cuts off the wholesome roots; what is cut off is desire-realm, born-of-maturation wholesome roots; the wrong view that accomplishes this denies both cause and effect entirely; the cutting-off may be gradual, and both [good and bad, in a sense] are relinquished together; it happens only in a human body, only in three of the four continents, only to males or females, and only to those of "view" temperament, its nature being non-possession; wholesome roots are restored through doubt or right view arising, either gradually or, in the case of those who have committed an ānantarya offense, all at once — except that even they cannot restore them in this very life. Working through this systematically: among the unwholesome karma-paths, only fully mature, "upper-grade" wrong view is capable of cutting off the wholesome roots — specifically the view that categorically denies any causal connection between good or bad conduct and their results. This wrong view is described as functioning like a thief who sets a fire that then burns down a village: the wrong view itself is what finally severs the roots, but the other unwholesome roots (which gave rise to it in the first place) are popularly said to be responsible for the "burning," much as the village is said to be burned by the thief who started the blaze rather than by the fire itself. What is cut off is specifically the desire-realm wholesome roots that arise through simple ripening (as opposed to those cultivated through meditative effort, which are said to have already retreated beforehand) — form-realm and formless-realm wholesome roots were never established to begin with in a being capable of this, so there is nothing there to cut. On the question of whether the cutting-off happens all at once or gradually, the treatise records a range of positions: some hold the nine grades of wholesome roots are cut off all at once, in a single instant of wrong view, just as the path of seeing abandons its afflictions all at once; others hold they are cut off gradually, grade by grade, the very weakest wrong view able to sever only the very strongest wholesome roots and so on in sequence, just as the path of cultivation gradually abandons its own afflictions — and the treatise notes that this gradual account better fits the root treatise's own wording, which states that "cutting off the wholesome roots" refers to the state in which even the last, most minute remaining wholesome root is finally relinquished, since as long as even one grade of wholesome root remains, it can still give rise to the others, and cutting is only complete once nothing at all is left. This cutting-off is possible only within a human body, and only in three of the four continents (excluding Uttarakuru, whose inhabitants lack the capacity for such an extremely evil underlying disposition), and only in a male or female body (not, according to one view, in the body of specific categories of sexually ambiguous or impotent persons, whose faculties are said to be too dull for this), and, crucially, only by those of "view" temperament rather than "craving" temperament, since a craving-natured person's evil disposition is too restless and shallow to sustain such a thoroughgoing severance, while a view-natured person's evil disposition runs extremely deep — the treatise adds that this is also why beings such as paṇḍakas (a category of sexually ambiguous persons) cannot cut off the wholesome roots, since they belong to the "craving" type. Finally, on the deeper metaphysical question of what this "cutting" actually consists of, the treatise gives its now-familiar Vaibhāṣika answer: it is non-possession that constitutes the substance of the cutting, since, at the moment the wholesome roots are cut, no further possession of wholesome dharmas arises, while non-possession continues to arise in an unbroken chain, taking the place where possession would otherwise have continued.

Ru-Yi Translation

The ten unwholesome karma-paths thus described are all contrary to the actual arising of wholesome dharmas. By which karma-path is the cutting-off of the wholesome roots accomplished? What is the difference between the characteristics of their cutting-off and their restoration? Verse: only wrong view cuts off wholesome roots; what is cut off is desire-realm, born-of-maturation [wholesome roots]; it denies cause and effect entirely; the cutting-off may be gradual, and both are relinquished together; [this occurs] in human [bodies], three continents, male and female, [those of] view-temperament; its nature is non-possession; restoration comes through doubt or [right] view arising, gradually or all at once, except for those who have committed an ānantarya offense. Explanation: among the unwholesome karma-paths, only upper-grade, fully complete wrong view is able to cut off the wholesome roots. [Objection:] If so, why does the root treatise state: what are the upper-grade unwholesome roots? Those unwholesome roots that are capable of cutting off the wholesome roots. [Reply:] This refers either to what is first eliminated at the stage of freedom from desire, or [is stated thus] because the unwholesome roots are what give rise to the wrong view, and so the matter of the wrong view is attributed back to those roots — just as a village is burned by fire that a thief set, yet the world says that thief burned the village. Which wholesome roots are cut off by this? Only the desire-realm, born-of-maturation wholesome roots, since form-realm and formless-realm wholesome roots were never established [in such a person] to begin with. [Objection:] How is this to be reconciled with the Prajñaptiśāstra? [Reply:] As that treatise states, it is only by this measure that a person is said to have cut off the wholesome roots of the three realms; with reference to the higher wholesome roots, it speaks further still, meaning that this continuum is no longer a vessel for them. [Objection:] Why are only the born-of-maturation wholesome roots cut off? [Reply:] Because the wholesome roots cultivated through effort have already retreated beforehand. [Objection:] In dependence on which wrong view can the wholesome roots be cut off? [Reply:] The wrong view that categorically denies cause and effect. Denying the cause means categorically denying good and bad conduct; denying the effect means categorically denying their ripened result. Other teachers say: these two wrong views are like the uninterrupted path and the path of liberation, [functioning as] distinct [phases]. Other teachers say: the wrong view that cuts off wholesome roots takes only contaminated objects, not uncontaminated ones, as its object, and takes only objects of its own realm, not another realm — since it only functions as an associated latent tendency, its object does not increase in strength [across realms], its power being weak. Those who say otherwise hold it takes all objects, and increases in accordance with its cause, having strong power. Other teachers say: the nine grades of wholesome roots are cut off all at once, in a single instant, by wrong view, just as the path of seeing abandons the afflictions to be abandoned by seeing. Those who say otherwise hold the wholesome roots are cut off gradually — the nine grades of wholesome roots are cut off gradually, in reverse-and-forward correspondence, by the nine grades of wrong view, just as the path of cultivation gradually abandons the afflictions to be abandoned by cultivation — that is, the very weakest wrong view is able to cut off the very strongest wholesome roots, on up to the very strongest wrong view cutting off the very weakest wholesome roots. If stated this way, it accords with the root treatise's text. As the root treatise states: what is meant by "the most minute accompanying wholesome root"? That which is relinquished last at the time the wholesome roots are cut off — it is by relinquishing this that one is said to have cut off the wholesome roots. [Objection:] If so, by what reasoning does that same text also state: what are the upper-grade unwholesome roots? Those unwholesome roots capable of cutting off the wholesome roots. [Reply:] That statement speaks with reference to the final completion, with hidden intent, since it is by this that the wholesome roots are cut off without remainder — that is, as long as even one grade of wholesome root still remains, the other grades of wholesome root can still arise on account of it, and one cannot yet say the wholesome roots have been cut off; only when the cutting is complete is it called "cutting off the wholesome roots," and so only the upper grade is said to be capable of cutting off the wholesome roots. Other teachers say: in cutting off the nine grades of wholesome roots, there is never any emerging partway through, as there can be in the path of seeing. Those who say otherwise hold that both emerging and not emerging partway through are possible. Other teachers say: one first relinquishes one's restraint-vow, and only afterward cuts off the wholesome roots, since the vow is not easily relinquished. Those who say otherwise hold that if that restraint-vow is the result initiated by this grade of mind, then when this grade of mind is cut off, that restraint-vow is relinquished as well, since result and cause belong to the same category. In what location can the wholesome roots be cut off? In the human destiny, in three continents — not in the bad destinies, nor in the heavens. Why is this? Because in the bad destinies, whether defiled or undefiled, wisdom is not stable enough; and because in the heavens, the results of good and bad karma are directly seen [making such denial impossible]. By "three continents" is meant excluding Uttarakuru, since its inhabitants lack an extremely evil underlying disposition. Other teachers say: only in Jambudvīpa. [Objection:] If so, this contradicts what the root treatise states — the root treatise states that the people of Jambudvīpa possess, at minimum, eight faculties, and the same holds for the eastern and western continents. In what kind of body does this cutting-off of wholesome roots occur? Only in a male or female body, since their resolve is fixed. Other teachers say: also not in a female body, since desire, diligence, wisdom, and the like are all too dull [in women]. [Objection:] If so, this contradicts what the root treatise states — the root treatise states that whoever possesses the female faculty definitely possesses [all] eight faculties, and likewise for the male faculty. Which type of practitioner is capable of cutting off the wholesome roots? Only the view-practitioner, not the craving-practitioner, since the craving-practitioner's evil underlying disposition is extremely restless, while the view-practitioner's evil underlying disposition is extremely deep and firm. By this reasoning, paṇḍakas and the like cannot cut off the wholesome roots, being of the craving type; moreover, such persons are like those in the bad destinies. What is the substance of this cutting-off of wholesome roots? It should be understood that this cutting-off has non-possession as its substance: at the stage of cutting off the wholesome roots, possession of wholesome dharmas no longer arises, while non-possession continues to arise in succession, taking the place where the arising of possession of the wholesome roots would otherwise have occurred — this is called cutting off the wholesome roots. Hence, the cutting-off of wholesome roots has non-possession as its substance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should be honest that this section preserves an unusually large number of unresolved "other teachers say" alternatives on nearly every sub-question — whether the two component wrong views (denying cause, denying effect) are simultaneous or sequential like the uninterrupted-and-liberation paths; whether the cutting-off is sudden or gradual (with the treatise leaning toward gradual as better fitting the root text, but not flatly rejecting the sudden view); whether emerging partway through the process is possible; whether the restraint-vow or the wholesome roots are relinquished first; and whether a female body is capable of this at all, a claim the treatise itself immediately flags as contradicting the root treatise's own statement that women can possess all eight faculties. We have translated this last, disputed claim faithfully as part of the historical text's own internal debate, without endorsing it — the treatise's own citation of a contradicting authority suggests this view was already controversial within the tradition itself, not a settled position.

The "craving-practitioner cannot cut off wholesome roots" reasoning, and its application to paṇḍakas specifically, reflects this treatise's broader (and, by modern standards, often unflattering) classification of certain persons as inherently spiritually limited by temperament or embodiment — a recurring feature of this scholastic tradition that we have flagged before (e.g. fascicle 14's discussion of who may take ordination) and flag again here rather than silently smoothing over.

3. How the Wholesome Roots Are Restored

Having explained how the wholesome roots are cut off, the treatise turns to their restoration. Once cut off, the wholesome roots are restored through the arising of either doubt or right view: when doubt arises regarding whether cause and effect actually exist after all ("perhaps there really is such a thing"), or when right view directly arises, settling that cause and effect definitely exist and are not non-existent, the possession of the wholesome roots begins to arise once more, and this is called their restoration. Some teachers hold this restoration happens gradually, across the nine grades, the way a serious illness first subsides before one's strength gradually returns. As for whether this restoration can happen within the very same lifetime in which the wholesome roots were cut off: it generally can, with one significant exception — a person who has committed one of the five ānantarya offenses (killing one's father, killing one's mother, killing an arhat, causing a schism in the monastic community, or maliciously wounding a buddha) can never restore the wholesome roots within that same life, according to the sūtras on this point; instead, such a person is capable of restoring them only when about to die and fall into hell, or precisely at the moment of being reborn there — that is, either during the intermediate state leading to that hellish rebirth, or at the very point of death immediately preceding it — and at no other point. Whether restoration occurs through the force of an internal cause (occurring near death) or through the force of an external condition (occurring near the moment of rebirth) determines which of these two junctures applies; the same reasoning holds whether the determining force comes from the person's own effort or from another's influence. A further distinction concerns exactly what was damaged when the wholesome roots were cut off: if only the person's underlying intention was corrupted, without any accompanying breakdown in outward conduct or precepts, restoration can occur within this very life; but if both the underlying intention and the outward conduct were corrupted together, restoration must wait until after this body has perished. The same distinction applies, correspondingly, to cases involving corrupted view versus corrupted precepts specifically. Finally, the treatise raises a related but distinct question: is everyone who has cut off the wholesome roots necessarily fixed in a wrong destiny (i.e., certain to be reborn in a state from which liberation becomes impossible), and is everyone fixed in a wrong destiny necessarily someone who has cut off the wholesome roots? A four-cornered analysis answers this precisely: some have cut off wholesome roots without being fixed in a wrong destiny — such as the historical figure Pūraṇa and others like him; some are fixed in a wrong destiny without having cut off wholesome roots — such as the historical figure Ajātaśatru and others like him (whose grave offense fixed his destiny through a different mechanism entirely, not through severing his wholesome roots); some are both — such as the historical figure Devadatta and others like him; and some are neither — everyone else, falling under none of the preceding three categories. With this, the discussion of cutting off and restoring the wholesome roots is complete, and the treatise returns to clarify a further point about the karma-paths themselves.

4. How Many Karma-Paths Can Arise Together with One Volition

The treatise now asks how many of the ten karma-paths can be completed simultaneously, accompanying a single act of volition. Among the unwholesome karma-paths, the number ranges from just one up to eight. A single karma-path is completed when, for instance, only one of greed, hatred, or wrong view arises on its own, or when a bodily karma-path already set in motion by prior preparatory action reaches completion at an undefiled moment of mind. Two are completed together when killing is completed at a moment of hatred, or when theft, sexual misconduct, or frivolous speech is completed at a moment of greed. Three together when killing and theft are both completed at a single moment of hatred directed at another's life and belongings. Four together when a wish to harm another gives rise to both false speech and harsh speech (one mental karma-path plus three verbal ones, since false speech and harsh speech can be completed within the same utterance). Five, six, and seven follow the same reasoning, extended appropriately. Eight together — the maximum for unwholesome karma-paths — occurs when a prior preparatory action has set the other six bodily and verbal unwholesome acts in motion, and one's own greed and wrong view are completed at the very same moment; the last three mental karma-paths can never all arise from their own individual force simultaneously, which is why nine or ten unwholesome karma-paths co-arising is impossible. For the wholesome karma-paths, the count opens up to as many as all ten together, though certain configurations are specifically excluded: it is impossible for exactly one, eight, or five wholesome karma-paths alone to arise together with volition (a purely formal restriction the treatise works out precept-configuration by precept-configuration — for instance, two arise together when the five wholesome consciousnesses, or the uncontaminated knowledge of "extinction and non-arising" based on a formless absorption, are present without any of the seven undistracted wholesome bodily/verbal precepts also active; nine arise together when full monastic ordination is received at a moment when the five wholesome sense-consciousnesses, or an analogous absorption-based mental state, is present; and the full ten arise together when full ordination is received at a moment of wholesome mental consciousness accompanied by right view together with the karma of restraint proper). Considered from a fully comprehensive rather than a narrowly formal standpoint, however, no combination is actually excluded. As for the restraint of simply abstaining from unwholesome conduct outside any formal precept structure, the count is limited to one, five, or eight together — one when an undefiled or neutral state of mind gives rise to abstention from a single branch; five when a wholesome mental consciousness without any accompanying "following" physical action, associated with right view, gives rise to abstention from two branches together (and so on up to five); and eight when that same mental state gives rise to abstention from five or more branches at once.

5. Where the Karma-Paths Are Possessed Versus Actually Practiced

The treatise now surveys, across the realms and destinies, which of the ten karma-paths are merely possessed as a latent capacity and which are actually carried out in practice. In hell, only three unwholesome karma-paths are both possessed and actually practiced: harsh speech (since hell-beings constantly hurl abuse at one another), frivolous speech (since they wail and lament, their bodies and minds being coarse, violent, and unruly), and hatred (since they constantly harbor mutual malice). Greed and wrong view are possessed there but never actually practiced, since there is no desirable object to covet and since hell-beings directly witness the results of karma unfolding before them, leaving no room for the denial that wrong view requires. Killing does not occur there because death from karmic exhaustion already applies uniformly, leaving nothing further to kill, and because there is no property or women present to make theft or sexual misconduct possible; false speech does not occur because there is no purpose it could serve, and, for the same reason, and because hell-beings are perpetually separated from one another, divisive speech does not occur either. In Uttarakuru, greed, hatred, and wrong view are all definitely possessed but never actually practiced, since its inhabitants have no attachment to personal property, are gentle in body and mind, cause no harm to one another, and have no evil disposition; frivolous speech alone is both possessed and practiced there, since its inhabitants sometimes sing with a defiled mind, though without evil intent behind it. None of killing, theft, sexual misconduct, false speech, divisive speech, or harsh speech occur in Uttarakuru, for reasons following the same pattern already given — fixed lifespans, no attachment to property or women, gentle bodies and minds, and no practical purpose these acts could serve. An interesting question arises about how sexual relations occur there at all, given the absence of anything resembling misconduct: when a man and woman there develop mutual desire, they simply join hands and walk beneath a tree together, and if the tree's branches bend down to shelter them, they know this is a permissible union; if the branches do not bend down, the two, feeling ashamed, part ways instead. In every other part of the desire realm besides hell and Uttarakuru — that is, among the gods, hungry ghosts, animals, and humans of the three [ordinary] continents — all ten unwholesome karma-paths are both possessed and actually practiced, though with an important distinction: for gods, hungry ghosts, and animals, the first seven karma-paths exist only in their "neutral" (neither restrained nor unrestrained) form, since these beings lack the capacity for formal non-restraint (a fixed, ongoing commitment to unwholesome conduct), whereas among humans of the three continents both restraint and non-restraint genuinely exist. Although the gods as a class have no one who kills another god, they are said to sometimes kill beings of other destinies; other teachers hold that gods can indeed kill other gods too, since the fatal method there requires severing both head and waist before the victim's life is finally extinguished. Turning to the wholesome karma-paths: the three purely mental ones (non-greed, non-hatred, right view) are both possessed and actually practiced across all three realms and five destinies without restriction, but the seven bodily and verbal ones can only be possessed, never actually practiced, in the formless realm or by beings without perception, since noble ones reborn in the formless realm merely possess uncontaminated restraint carried over from their past and future lives (without any body or speech through which to exercise it in the present), and beings without perception likewise merely possess the fourth-meditation restraint carried over from before their entry into that state and after their eventual emergence from it. 6. The Three Fruits of the Karma-Paths, and Wrong Livelihood

The treatise now explains that each of the ten karma-paths, whether unwholesome or wholesome, produces three distinct kinds of fruit when habitually cultivated: a ripened fruit, an outflow fruit, and a dominant fruit. Cultivating the ten unwholesome karma-paths repeatedly and habitually produces, as ripened fruit, rebirth in hell; once that hell-rebirth is exhausted and one returns to be born again as a human, one experiences the corresponding outflow fruit within one's own person: the killer has a short lifespan, the thief lacks material resources, the sexual offender has an unfaithful spouse, the false speaker is frequently slandered, the divisive speaker loses friends and loved ones to estrangement, the harsh speaker constantly hears unpleasant things said, the frivolous speaker's own words carry no weight or authority, the greedy person's greed only intensifies, the hateful person's hatred only intensifies, and the wrong-viewed person's delusion only intensifies, since the type of ignorance corresponding to that karma-path grows stronger in each case. A short human lifespan is anticipated to be also, in a sense, a wholesome karmic result in some other framework — how, then, can it be called killing's "outflow" result specifically? The point is not that a short human lifespan simply is the fruit of killing outright, but rather that killing acts as an obstructing cause preventing the life-faculty from continuing for long, and this obstructing influence is what is meant by calling it killing's outflow result. As for the dominant fruit of these ten, this manifests in one's external environment and material circumstances: killing causes the things one relies on for sustenance to lose their luster and vitality; theft brings frequent frost and hail; sexual misconduct brings excessive dust; false speech brings foul, unpleasant surroundings; divisive speech brings a hazardous, difficult terrain to live in; harsh speech brings land full of thorns, gravel, and salty, infertile soil unsuited to cultivation; frivolous speech brings unpredictable, ever-shifting seasons; greed brings diminished harvests; hatred brings harsh, bitter-tasting produce; and wrong view brings scant or entirely absent harvests. A further question arises: does a single act of killing produce both the ripened fruit of hell-rebirth and the outflow fruit of a short human lifespan afterward, or is something more involved? Some teachers hold that it is indeed the very same act of killing that first brings about the hell-rebirth and, afterward, the shortened human lifespan; others hold that the two results actually trace back to two distinct causes — the ripened fruit stemming from the preparatory action, and the outflow fruit from the root act itself — noting that although the text speaks in general terms of "one act of killing," this actually encompasses both the root act and its accompanying preparatory and follow-up actions together; the term "outflow fruit" used here does not exclude the ripened or dominant fruits from also applying, but is simply a loosely applied label describing a fruit's mere resemblance to its cause. Why do each of these ten produce exactly three distinct kinds of fruit? Taking killing as the illustrative case: at the moment of killing, one causes the victim to suffer, one severs their life, and one diminishes their dignity and standing — causing suffering leads to rebirth in hell as the ripened fruit; severing life leads to a short human lifespan afterward as the outflow fruit; and diminishing another's dignity leads to a dulled, lackluster external environment as the dominant fruit. The remaining unwholesome karma-paths should be understood analogously. From this, the corresponding threefold fruit of the wholesome karma-paths can likewise be inferred: repeatedly and habitually cultivating abstention from killing and the rest produces, as ripened fruit, rebirth among the gods; upon returning from there to be reborn as a human, one experiences the corresponding outflow fruit — for instance, one who abstains from killing enjoys a long lifespan — with everything else understood analogously, in each case opposite to the unwholesome pattern already described.

Turning briefly to a related terminological question, a certain sūtra divides the noble eightfold path's bodily component into three separate branches — right speech, right action, and right livelihood. Since right livelihood, apart from right speech and right action, has no distinct content of its own, why does the sūtra list it as a separate item? The verse explains: bodily and verbal karma arising from hatred and delusion are called wrong speech and wrong action respectively, but bodily and verbal karma arising specifically from greed are singled out and separately named "wrong livelihood," precisely because greed-driven misconduct is unusually difficult to root out — greed has the power to seize hold of sentient beings' minds so thoroughly that the karma it gives rise to becomes especially hard to guard against and restrain, and so the Buddha, wishing practitioners to cultivate right livelihood with special earnestness and care, separated it out as its own distinct category rather than folding it into ordinary speech and action. As a verse on this point states: worldly wrong view is difficult to remove, since one clings constantly to a different [erroneous] view; on the path, wrong livelihood is difficult to guard against, since one's very means of subsistence depend on others. Other teachers hold a narrower position: only bodily and verbal karma arising from greed specifically directed at the material requisites of one's own livelihood should be called "wrong livelihood," and not greed-driven karma more broadly — since, for instance, singing and dancing purely for one's own amusement, though also arising from greed, are not undertaken for the sake of sustaining one's life and so would not qualify. The treatise rejects this narrower position as contradicting the Sūtra on the Aggregate of Precepts, which explicitly classifies watching contests such as elephant fights, among other forms of idle spectacle, as falling under wrong livelihood as well, since these too constitute a wrongful, self-indulgent use of external objects to artificially prolong one's enjoyment of life. Right speech and right livelihood should be understood as defined in the corresponding opposite manner.

7. The Five Kinds of Fruit, and How Many Each Type of Karma Produces

Having discussed the three fruits specific to the karma-paths, the treatise now situates this within a broader, previously introduced fivefold classification of fruit in general: ripened fruit, outflow fruit, freedom-from-bondage fruit (the cessation attained through the power of the path), personal-effort fruit (a result actively brought about through one's own exertion, as opposed to passively ripening), and dominant fruit (everything else not covered by the first four, produced simply by not obstructing). The question now is how many of these five fruits each type of karma is capable of producing. The uninterrupted path — the specific moment of the path that directly severs an affliction, as opposed to the path of liberation that immediately follows it — comes in two varieties, contaminated and uncontaminated, and a contaminated uninterrupted path is capable of producing all five fruits at once: a ripened fruit (a desirable ripened result in its own stage, brought about by this path), an outflow fruit (later similar or superior states resembling it within its own stage), a freedom-from-bondage fruit (the specific cessation-through-discernment that this path's power directly brings about), a personal-effort fruit (the liberation and cultivation that this path draws forth together with it), and a dominant fruit (all remaining conditioned dharmas other than its own intrinsic nature, excluding only what has already arisen in a prior life). An uncontaminated uninterrupted path produces only four of these five, lacking a ripened fruit, since uncontaminated karma by its nature does not ripen into a further conditioned result of that kind. Other contaminated wholesome or unwholesome karma, apart from this specific uninterrupted path, likewise produces only four fruits, but the one it lacks is different: it lacks the freedom-from-bondage fruit, since only the uninterrupted path's specific power can bring about that particular cessation. Everything else — remaining uncontaminated karma, together with morally neutral karma of any kind — produces only three fruits, lacking whichever of the previously excluded fruits already applied to that category. The treatise then works through several further cross-tabulations of this same fivefold fruit scheme, considered from different angles. Considered by moral nature (wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral) as cause and effect in every pairing: wholesome karma as cause, producing a wholesome effect, yields four fruits (excluding only the ripened fruit, since a wholesome cause does not itself ripen into another wholesome effect directly in this pairing); producing an unwholesome effect, yields two fruits (personal-effort and dominant only); producing a neutral effect, yields three fruits (excluding outflow and freedom-from-bondage). Unwholesome karma as cause, producing a wholesome effect, yields two fruits; producing an unwholesome effect, yields three fruits (excluding ripened and freedom-from-bondage) — its outflow fruit here consists specifically of neutral dharmas belonging to the same family as self-view and extreme-view, which unwholesome karma of the pervasive-affliction type, or the type abandoned through seeing suffering, gives rise to as its outflow; producing a neutral effect, yields four fruits (excluding only freedom-from-bondage). Neutral karma as cause, producing a wholesome effect, yields two fruits; its outflow fruit consists of the unwholesome dharmas belonging to the family of self-view and extreme-view that certain neutral karma gives rise to; producing an unwholesome effect, yields three fruits (excluding ripened and freedom-from-bondage); producing a neutral effect, yields three fruits (excluding ripened and freedom-from-bondage).

8. The Fruit Cross-Tabulation Continued: Time, Stage, Training-Status, and Abandonment

The treatise continues its fivefold-fruit cross-tabulation across four further dimensions. Considered by time (past, present, future) as cause and effect: past karma, with respect to any of the three times as effect, has four fruits (excluding only freedom-from-bondage in every case); present karma, with respect to future dharmas, has four fruits, as before; with respect to present dharmas, has two fruits (personal-effort and dominant only, since a present cause cannot ripen into or flow into another present effect simultaneously); future karma, with respect to future dharmas, has three fruits (excluding outflow and freedom-from-bondage) — and it is not said that a later-arising karma can produce an earlier fruit, since what comes earlier can never be the fruit of what comes later. Considered by meditative stage: karma occurring within the same stage as its effect has four fruits (excluding freedom-from-bondage); karma of one stage producing an effect in a different stage has only two fruits if contaminated (personal-effort and dominant), or three fruits if uncontaminated (excluding ripened and freedom-from-bondage), since uncontaminated karma, not being confined to any particular realm, is not barred from producing an outflow effect across stages the way contaminated karma is. Considered by training-status (a trainee still in training, an adept beyond further training, and one who is neither, meaning an ordinary being): trainee karma, with respect to the dharmas of a trainee or of one beyond training, has three fruits in each case (excluding ripened and freedom-from-bondage); with respect to the dharmas of neither, has three fruits (excluding ripened and outflow). Karma of one beyond training, with respect to the dharmas of a trainee, has only one fruit (the dominant fruit alone); with respect to the dharmas of one beyond training, has three fruits (excluding ripened and freedom-from-bondage); with respect to the dharmas of neither, has two fruits (personal-effort and dominant). Karma belonging to neither (an ordinary being's karma), with respect to the dharmas of a trainee, has two fruits; with respect to the dharmas of one beyond training, likewise has two fruits; with respect to the dharmas of neither, has all five fruits. Finally, considered by abandonment-status (karma abandoned through seeing, karma abandoned through cultivation, and karma that is not to be abandoned by either path, meaning uncontaminated karma): karma to be abandoned through seeing, with respect to dharmas of its own category, has three fruits (excluding ripened and freedom-from-bondage); with respect to dharmas abandoned through cultivation, has four fruits (excluding freedom-from-bondage); with respect to dharmas not to be abandoned, has only one fruit (the dominant fruit). Karma to be abandoned through cultivation, with respect to dharmas abandoned through seeing, has two fruits; with respect to dharmas abandoned through cultivation, has four fruits (excluding freedom-from-bondage); with respect to dharmas not to be abandoned, has three fruits (excluding ripened and outflow). Karma not to be abandoned, with respect to dharmas abandoned through seeing, has one fruit (dominant only); with respect to dharmas abandoned through cultivation, has two fruits; with respect to dharmas not to be abandoned, has four fruits (excluding only the ripened fruit). 9. "Should Be Done" Karma, and Whether One Karma Projects One Life

Having finished this extended treatment of karma's fruits, the treatise addresses a leftover technical question: the root treatise speaks of three kinds of karma — action that should be done, action that should not be done, and action that is neither — what do these actually mean? One position holds that "should not be done" simply refers to defiled karma, since it arises from improper attention; a further position broadens this to include any bodily, verbal, or mental karma that breaks with established norms — that is, whatever fails to conform to how one should properly walk, stand, speak, dress, or eat according to convention is "should not be done," since it does not accord with worldly propriety, while its opposite is "should be done." Others hold that "should be done" simply refers to wholesome karma, arising from proper attention, while karma conforming to established norms is likewise called "should be done" under the broader account; whatever contradicts both of the preceding two categories is the third, "neither" — with these two explanations differing only in how narrowly or broadly the categories are drawn. The treatise then turns to a genuinely interesting question: does a single act of karma project only a single rebirth, or can it project several: and, conversely, is a single rebirth produced by only a single act of karma, or can several acts of karma jointly produce it? According to this tradition's own principle, a single act of karma projects only a single rebirth — "a single rebirth" here meaning a single instance of shared class-membership (the specific configuration that makes a being count as belonging to a particular type of existence), since only upon obtaining this shared class-membership can one properly speak of "a rebirth" having occurred. An objection immediately follows, citing the venerable Aniruddha's own famous account: he recalled that once, in a distant past life, he made a single offering of food to an especially worthy field of merit, and from that single act experienced its ripening as seven rebirths among the gods of the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, then seven more rebirths among humans as a wheel-turning monarch, and finally was born into the great, wealthy household of the Śākya clan, abundant in treasures and enjoying great happiness — does this not show a single act of karma projecting many rebirths? The treatise resolves this by explaining that it was actually one single karmic act that first brought about that one great, wealthy, and fortunate rebirth, together with the wisdom carried over from past lives; riding on the power of that initial result, Aniruddha then went on to perform further meritorious deeds that in turn projected his subsequent rebirths, and so it was, ultimately, through this chain of successive acts that he arrived at his final, culminating rebirth of great wealth and honor — his own statement is simply emphasizing the initial cause's importance, the way a person who turns one gold coin into a thousand through successive trades might still say, "I became wealthy because of that one original coin." Other teachers offer a different resolution: that single act of giving food actually gave rise, at the time, to many distinct and powerful aspirations directed toward different specific results — some aimed at heavenly rebirth, others at human rebirth — which ripened at different moments, some sooner and some later, precisely because a single moment of volition cannot itself ripen at multiple distinct times; hence it was not, strictly speaking, one single act of karma projecting many rebirths, nor one single rebirth produced by many acts of karma. The treatise adds an important qualification: although a single rebirth is projected by only a single act of karma, its actual fulfillment and completion — the specific bodily configuration one ends up with, including whether one's limbs, faculties, physical proportions, coloring, and strength are complete or defective — is indeed permitted to come about through the joint contribution of many acts of karma, much as a painter first sketches a figure's outline in a single color before filling in its many different colors and details afterward; this is why, even among beings who equally share a human birth, some possess complete limbs, faculties, proportions, coloring, strength, and adornment, while others are markedly deficient in these respects compared to what came before — the "projecting" karma alone does not account for this variation.

10. The Three Grave Obstructions, and Who Can Commit an Ānantarya Offense — Fascicle 17 Complete

The treatise closes its extended treatment of karma by addressing a teaching found in a sūtra: the Blessed One speaks of three grave obstructions — the karmic obstruction, the afflictive obstruction, and the ripened-result obstruction. What is the actual substance of each? The karmic obstruction refers specifically to the five ānantarya offenses: killing one's mother, killing one's father, killing an arhat, causing a schism in a harmonious monastic community, and, with malicious intent, causing a buddha's body to bleed. These five are called the karmic obstruction. Affliction, meanwhile, comes in two varieties: persistently recurring affliction (affliction that arises again and again, habitually) and intensely sharp affliction (affliction of an especially severe, upper grade). Only the persistently recurring variety counts as the afflictive obstruction here — beings such as paṇḍakas, whose afflictions recur constantly and are difficult to subdue or remove, exemplify this, since habitual recurrence itself constitutes the obstruction, even without the affliction being especially intense; conversely, an especially intense affliction that does not recur habitually is comparatively easy to subdue, since it lacks this quality of relentless repetition. Even among weaker-grade afflictions, if they recur persistently, they remain hard to overcome, since their sheer habitual recurrence denies any opportunity for a remedial path to arise, weaker afflictions serving as conditions from which intermediate afflictions arise, and intermediate as conditions for superior afflictions, on and on, such that whatever grade an affliction may be, it is specifically its persistent recurrence, not its intensity, that qualifies it as the afflictive obstruction. As for the ripened-result obstruction, it consists of a complete rebirth in any of the three bad destinies (hell, hungry ghosts, animals), together with rebirth as a human in Uttarakuru, or rebirth among the gods without perception — this obstruction specifically obstructs the noble path and the wholesome roots that serve as its preparatory cultivation, since these states of rebirth are simply unconducive to spiritual practice by their very nature. On the question of exactly who is capable of committing an ānantarya offense specifically among the karmic obstructions: this obstruction, being reckoned among certain further "determinate" unwholesome karmas (which also include acts certain to result in rebirth as an animal, an insect, or as a woman, or the "eighth-existence" limit on how many more times a stream-enterer can be reborn), is said to be especially "easily seen and known" due to five distinguishing factors — the location where it is committed, the destiny it produces, the type of birth, its specific result, and the type of person capable of committing it — and it is only for this reason that the ānantarya offenses receive separate, explicit treatment while other equally determinate karmas do not. The ānantarya offenses can only be committed by human beings of the three continents excluding Uttarakuru — never by beings of other realms or destinies. Even within these three continents, only ordinary men and women, not paṇḍakas or similarly categorized persons, are capable of committing them, since — echoing the same reasoning already given regarding who can cut off the wholesome roots — such persons lack the underlying cause that would otherwise prevent them from cutting off their wholesome roots, which the treatise identifies as precisely the deeper reason they also cannot commit an ānantarya offense; moreover, such persons are said to feel comparatively little gratitude toward their parents and comparatively little sense of shame before them, since parents provide comparatively little of the specific increase in bodily faculties involved in conception that such persons would especially have needed, and they harbor comparatively little affection for such offspring in turn — and it is this diminished capacity for shame toward one's parents specifically, without any occasion for outwardly manifested, socially visible shame ever even arising to be violated, that the treatise identifies as the deeper reason such persons cannot incur the specific fault of matricide or patricide. This same reasoning explains why animals and hungry ghosts, though they may sometimes kill their own mothers, do not thereby commit an ānantarya offense. However, a great authority within the tradition holds that if the awareness at the moment of the act is sufficiently clear and deliberate, even an animal may indeed commit an ānantarya offense, citing the example of an unusually intelligent horse; killing a father or mother who is not actually one's biological parent, meanwhile, does not constitute this grave offense at all, since the mental object one is acting upon is of a lesser, inapplicable kind. Having thus explained that the karmic obstruction belongs only to humans of the three continents, the remaining two obstructions — afflictive and ripened-result — should be understood as occurring, by contrast, across all five destinies; within the human destiny specifically, the ripened-result obstruction is confined to Uttarakuru alone, and within the heavens, to the non-percipient heaven alone.

Upper-grade affliction, though intensely sharp, does not constantly arise, and so is comparatively easy to subdue and remove. Among lower-grade afflictions, though the persistently recurring kind is not intensely sharp, it is difficult to subdue and remove, since its constant activity gives no opportunity [for a remedy to take hold] — that is, from the lower grade as condition, the intermediate grade arises; from the intermediate grade as condition, the upper grade arises again, such that no opportunity arises for the path that subdues and removes it to come about. Hence, among afflictions, regardless of upper or lower grade, it is specifically the persistently recurring kind that is called the afflictive obstruction. The whole of the three bad destinies, the human destiny's Uttarakuru, and the non-percipient heaven are called the ripened-result obstruction. What does this obstruction obstruct? It obstructs the noble path, and the wholesome roots that are the preparatory cultivation for the noble path. Moreover, within the karmic obstruction, by rights, other determinate karmas should also be mentioned — namely, all other karma definitely leading to rebirth in a bad destiny, or as an egg-born or moisture-born being, or as a woman, or the karma fixing one's eighth [and final] existence [before liberation], and so on. However, if a karma is, by five causal factors, especially easy to see and know, it is on this basis that it is singled out for mention here — these five being the place, the destiny, the type of birth, the result, and the person. Among all karmas, it is only the five ānantarya karmas that possess all five of these easy-to-see-and-know factors; other karmas do not, and so are not mentioned here. The remaining obstructions' scope should be understood accordingly. Among these three obstructions, the afflictive and karmic obstructions are both grave, since one who has these cannot be remedied even within the very next rebirth. The Vaibhāṣika masters explain it this way: because the earlier can give rise to the later, the later is lighter than the earlier. What meaning does this term "ānantarya" [without interval] point to? With reference to the ripened result, it is certain that no further karma or further rebirth can serve as an interval — hence this term refers only to the meaning of "without interval." Alternatively, the person who commits this karma, upon dying, definitely falls into hell without any interval — hence it is called "without interval." That person possesses this "without interval" [quality], and thereby obtains the name "ānantarya" — being joined with the dharma of "without interval," one is named "ānantarya," just as, being joined with the state of a śramaṇa, one is named a śramaṇa. In which destinies should these three obstructions be understood to occur? Verse: the ānantarya [obstruction exists only] in the three continents, not elsewhere, not for paṇḍakas and the like, due to little gratitude and little shame; the remaining obstructions occur throughout the five destinies. Explanation: the ānantarya karmas belong only to humans of the three continents, not to Uttarakuru, nor to other destinies or realms. Even within the three continents, only ordinary women and men commit ānantarya karma, not paṇḍakas and the like. Why is this? It is for the very same reason already given as to why such persons lack the cause that would prevent them from cutting off the wholesome roots — this is also the reason there is no ānantarya offense here. Moreover, with respect to their parents and to themselves, in turn, there is little gratitude and little shame — that is, their parents feel little gratitude toward them, since they provided little of the increase specifically needed for such a person's bodily formation, and also because [the parents] feel little affection toward them in turn; such a person's sense of shame and conscience toward their parents is likewise faint, since there is no outwardly manifested, heightened sense of shame or conscience that could even be said to be violated, and so they do not incur the fault of an ānantarya offense. By this same reasoning, it is already explained why hungry ghosts and animals, though they may kill their own mothers and so forth, do not commit an ānantarya offense. Yet a great authority says: if the awareness [at the time] is clear and distinct, this can also constitute an ānantarya offense — as with an unusually intelligent horse. If a person kills a father or mother who is not their actual [biological] parent, this does not constitute the grave offense, since the mental object is of an inferior kind. Having explained that the karmic obstruction belongs only to humans of the three continents, the remaining obstructions should be understood to occur throughout all five destinies; however, within the human destiny, [the ripened-result obstruction] is found only in Uttarakuru, and within the heavenly destiny, only in the non-percipient [heaven]. Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag plainly that this closing section repeats, and leans further into, the same troubling reasoning about paṇḍakas already seen earlier in this fascicle and in fascicle 14 — here extended to explain why such persons supposedly cannot commit an ānantarya offense, on the grounds of "little gratitude" and "little shame" toward parents. We are translating this faithfully as part of the historical scholastic text's own argument, not endorsing its premises; readers should understand this as documenting how this particular tradition reasoned about categories of persons it viewed as spiritually and socially marginal, a recurring feature of the source material we have flagged consistently throughout fascicles 14, 15, 16, and now 17 rather than silently smoothing over.

The claim that an unusually intelligent horse or other animal could, in principle, commit an ānantarya offense if its awareness is "clear and distinct" is attributed to "a great authority" (大德, likely referring to a specific named master in the broader Vaibhāṣika/Sautrāntika debate tradition, though the text does not name him more specifically here) as a minority view against the more standard position that animals cannot commit these offenses — we have preserved this as a genuine, attributed alternative rather than smoothing it into the majority position.
