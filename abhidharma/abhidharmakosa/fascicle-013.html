No Creator, Only Karma

This page carries the opening of fascicle 13, beginning Chapter 4 (分別業品第四之一, the Karma-nirdeśa, "Discrimination of Karma") — one of the Abhidharmakośa's most important chapters. Everything distinctive about the world, from a being's own body to the physical cosmos surrounding it, is here attributed entirely to karma, with no creator god required, beginning from karma's most basic definition: volition itself, and what that volition produces through body and speech.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 3 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 13 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. The World's Differences Come From Karma, Not a Creator

Why does the world contain such stark contrasts — delightful things like saffron and sandalwood alongside a body that constantly discharges impurity through its own openings? The treatise's answer is direct: there is no single, prior, all-knowing creator behind any of it, only the distinctions among sentient beings' own karma, which can bring about both an unpleasant body and delightful possessions from a single mixed karmic cause. Karma itself is defined simply: it is volition, and it is what volition produces through body and speech.

Chinese Source

如前所說有情世間及器世間各多差別，如是差別由誰而生？頌曰：世別由業生，思及思所作，思即是意業，所作謂身語。論曰：非由一主先覺而生，但由有情業差別起。若爾，何故俱從業生？欝金栴檀等甚可愛樂，而內身形等與彼相違。以諸有情業類如是，若造雜業感內身形，於九瘡門常流不淨。為對治彼，感外具生色香味觸甚可愛樂。諸天眾等造純淨業，故彼所招二事俱妙。此所由業其體是何？謂心所思及思所作。故契經說：有二種業，一者思業、二思已業，思已業者謂思所作。如是二業分別為三，謂即有情身、語、意業。

Ru-Yi Translation

As previously discussed, the world of sentient beings and the container world each display many distinctions — by what are these distinctions produced? Verse: the world's distinctions arise from karma; karma is volition, and what volition produces; volition itself is mental karma; what is produced is bodily and verbal karma. Prose: the world does not arise from any single, prior, all-knowing lord, but arises solely through the distinctions among sentient beings' own karma. If this is so, why do both pleasant and unpleasant things all arise from karma alike? Saffron, sandalwood, and the like are exceedingly delightful, and yet the body's own inner form is the very opposite of this. This is because sentient beings' types of karma are of this kind: if one commits mixed karma, this brings about an inner bodily form that continuously discharges impurity through its nine openings; but, in order to counteract this, that same mixed karma also brings about external possessions — form, fragrance, flavor, and touch — that are exceedingly delightful. The various hosts of gods, having committed purely wholesome karma, therefore have both of these two things equally excellent. What is the essential nature of this karma that serves as the cause? It is the mental factor of volition, and what that volition produces. Hence a sūtra states: there are two kinds of karma — first, volitional karma; second, karma-after-volition; karma-after-volition refers to what volition produces. These two kinds of karma, further distinguished, become three: namely, sentient beings' bodily, verbal, and mental karma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The saffron-and-sandalwood example is doing careful work: rather than explaining pleasant and unpleasant circumstances as arising from entirely separate causes, the treatise attributes both the impurity of one's own body and the delight of one's external possessions to a single "mixed" karma — the same act can plant seeds for opposite kinds of result, depending on which aspect of the result is in question. This sets up the chapter's governing thesis in the most economical way possible: no creator, no dual cosmic principle of good and evil, just the internal structure of karma itself producing every contrast the world contains.

2. Three Karmas, Three Different Reasons for Being Three

The three kinds of karma could, in principle, be counted by their support, their own essential nature, or their arising-condition — but each of these criteria, applied uniformly, would actually collapse all three into just one kind. The Vaibhāṣikas resolve this by applying a different criterion to each: bodily karma by its support, verbal karma by its own nature, and mental karma by its arising-condition — meaning volition itself simply is mental karma, while its bodily and verbal effects are what that same volition gives rise to.

Chinese Source

如何建立此三業耶？為約所依、為據自性、為就等起？縱爾何違？若約所依應唯一業，以一切業並依身故。若據自性應唯語是業，以三種中唯語即業故。若就等起亦應唯一業，以一切業皆意等起故。毘婆沙師說立三業，如其次第由上三因。然心所思即是意業，思所作業分為身、語二業，是思所等起故。

Ru-Yi Translation

How are these three kinds of karma established? Is it on the basis of their support, their essential nature, or their arising-condition? Even granting any of these, what fault would follow? If established on the basis of support, there ought to be only a single kind of karma, since all karma alike depends upon the body. If established on the basis of essential nature, only verbal karma ought to count as karma, since, among the three, only speech is itself literally an act. If established on the basis of arising-condition, there ought likewise to be only a single kind of karma, since all karma alike arises from the condition of mind. The Vaibhāṣikas say that the three kinds of karma are established, in that same order, by the three reasons stated above. Thus, the mental factor of volition itself is precisely mental karma, while the karma produced by that volition divides into the two, bodily and verbal, since these are what that volition gives rise to as its arising-condition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rhetorical move here is worth noticing: rather than defending a single, uniform criterion for "what makes something a kind of karma," the Vaibhāṣikas openly apply three different criteria to the three different kinds — support for the bodily, essential nature for the verbal, arising-condition for the mental — and justify this by showing that any single uniform criterion would collapse the threefold division entirely. This is presented not as an ad hoc patch but as the only way to preserve a real, meaningful distinction that everyone already accepts must exist.

3. Is Bodily Action a Real Shape, or Just Motion? The Momentariness Argument

Bodily and verbal karma each turn out to have two aspects, manifest and unmanifest — and the treatise opens a famous debate over what bodily manifestation actually is. Against a rival school holding that bodily manifestation simply is motion, the treatise insists on a real, distinct shape instead, grounding the argument in this tradition's core doctrine of momentariness: since every conditioned thing ceases the very instant it arises, nothing ever literally travels from one place to another, so "motion" cannot be what bodily action fundamentally is.

Chinese Source

身語二業自性云何？頌曰：此身語二業，俱表無表性。論曰：應知如是所說諸業中，身語二業俱表無表性。且身語表其相云何？頌曰：身表許別形，非行動為體，以諸有為法，有剎那盡故。應無無因故，生因應能滅，形亦非實有，應二根取故，無別極微故，語表許言聲。論曰：由思力故，別起如是如是身形，名身表業。有餘部說：動名身表，以身動時由業動故。為破此故，說非行動，以一切有為皆有剎那故。剎那何謂？得體無間滅，有此剎那法，名有剎那，如有杖人名為有杖。諸有為法纔得自體，從此無間必滅歸無。若此處生即此處滅，無容從此轉至餘方，故不可言動名身表。若有為法皆有剎那，不至餘方義可成立。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the essential nature of bodily and verbal karma? Verse: these two, bodily and verbal karma, both have the nature of manifest and unmanifest forms. Prose: it should be understood that, among the karmas thus discussed, both bodily and verbal karma possess the nature of both manifest and unmanifest karma. What, then, is the character of bodily and verbal manifestation? Verse: bodily manifestation is accepted as a distinct shape, not motion, as its essential nature, since all conditioned dharmas are momentary. If motion were accepted as real, the very cause that produces something would, absurdly, also be capable of destroying it. Shape, too, is not truly real on the opposing view, since form would then have to be apprehended by two different faculties at once. Since there is no separate atom corresponding to shape as such, verbal manifestation is accepted as speech-sound instead. Prose: through the power of volition, one specific bodily configuration or another is brought about — this is called bodily manifestation-karma. There is a further school that holds: motion is what is called bodily manifestation, since, when the body moves, this is because karma itself moves. In order to refute this, it is stated that bodily manifestation is not motion, since everything conditioned is momentary. What does "momentary" mean? It means that, immediately upon attaining its own essential nature, a thing ceases without interruption — a dharma possessing this characteristic is called "having momentariness," just as a man carrying a staff is called "one who has a staff." Every conditioned dharma, the very instant it attains its own essential nature, immediately and necessarily ceases, returning to non-existence. If something arises in a given place, it likewise ceases in that very same place — there is no possibility of it then transferring to move to some other location; hence it cannot be said that motion is what constitutes bodily manifestation. If it is established that all conditioned dharmas are momentary, then the principle that nothing literally travels to another location can likewise be established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is one of this tradition's most consequential doctrines put to direct practical use: momentariness isn't just an abstract metaphysical claim about conditioned phenomena in general, but is here doing the specific work of ruling out an entire rival account of what bodily action even is. If nothing literally persists long enough to travel anywhere, then "motion" cannot be a basic, real category — what looks like a hand moving across space must actually be a rapid succession of distinct, momentary configurations, each arising and perishing in its own single location, never actually traveling at all. "Shape" survives this argument specifically because a shape is something that can be truly present in a single instant, without requiring any of the cross-instant persistence that "motion" would require.

The verse's opening also drops a striking related claim in passing — that the rival school's account of bodily action leads to the absurdity of "the cause that produces something also being capable of destroying it" — which will be unpacked in the argument that follows. This fascicle has now opened one of the Abhidharmakośa's longest sustained philosophical debates, and the full case for momentariness itself is still to come.
