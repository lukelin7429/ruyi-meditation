The Four Foundations of Mindfulness

Fascicle 23 continues the second part of Chapter 6 (分別賢聖品第六之二, Distinguishing the Noble Ones), taking up cultivation's next stage once the calm-abiding built through the two gates described in fascicle 22 (impurity-contemplation and mindfulness of breathing) is in place: insight, cultivated through the four foundations of mindfulness — body, feeling, mind, and dharmas — observed through both their own specific characteristics and the shared marks of impermanence, suffering, and non-self common to all conditioned things.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 9 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 23 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. The Four Foundations of Mindfulness: Structure, Naming, and Sequence

Having established the calm-abiding built through the two gates of cultivation, the treatise now turns to insight, cultivated through the four foundations of mindfulness: body, feeling, mind, and dharmas, each observed through both its own specific characteristic and the characteristics shared by all conditioned things — impermanence, suffering, and being empty of self. The body's own nature is derived matter composed of the four great elements; feeling and mind are as their names indicate; and "dharmas" here means whatever remains once body, feeling, and mind are set aside. Each of the four foundations has a threefold structure: an "own-nature" aspect, whose substance is wisdom itself (in its three forms born of hearing, reflection, and meditation); a "mixed-with-associates" aspect, whose substance is whatever else co-arises with that wisdom; and an "object-based" aspect, whose substance is whatever dharmas that wisdom takes as its object. That the own-nature aspect is specifically wisdom, and nothing else, is confirmed by scripture, which describes mindfulness of the body as "repeatedly observing" it — a function only wisdom, not mindfulness as such, is capable of performing.

Chinese Source

如是已說入修二門，由此二門心便得定。心得定已復何所修？頌曰：依已修成止，為觀修念住，以自相共相，觀身受心法。自性聞等慧，餘相雜所緣，說次第隨生，治倒故唯四。論曰：依已修成滿勝奢摩他，為毘鉢舍那修四念住。如何修習四念住耶？謂以自共相觀身、受、心、法。身受心法各別自性名為自相。一切有為皆非常性、一切有漏皆是苦性及一切法空非我性名為共相。身自性者，大種造色。受心自性，如自名顯。法自性者，除三餘法。傳說在定以極微剎那各別觀身，名身念住滿，餘三滿相如應當知。何等名為四念住體？此四念住體各有三，自性、相雜、所緣別故。自性念住以慧為體，此慧有三種，謂聞等所成，即此亦名三種念住。相雜念住以慧所餘俱有為體。所緣念住以慧所緣諸法為體。寧知自性是慧非餘？經說：於身住循身觀名身念住。餘三亦然。諸循觀名唯目慧體，非慧無有循觀用故。

Ru-Yi Translation

We have now explained the two gates of entering cultivation, through which the mind attains concentration. Once the mind has attained concentration, what is next to be cultivated? Verse: relying on the calm-abiding already accomplished through cultivation, one cultivates the foundations of mindfulness for the sake of insight, observing body, feeling, mind, and dharmas by way of both their own specific and their shared characteristics. Their essential nature is the wisdom born of hearing and the rest; the remaining aspects are mixed-with-associates and object-based. They are said to arise in this sequence; and, because they counter the inversions, there are exactly four. Explanation: relying on the fully accomplished, excellent calm-abiding already cultivated, one cultivates the four foundations of mindfulness for the sake of insight. How does one cultivate them? By observing body, feeling, mind, and dharmas through both their own specific and their shared characteristics. The distinct essential nature of each individually is called its specific characteristic. That all conditioned things are impermanent, that all contaminated things are suffering, and that all dharmas are empty and without self are called the shared characteristics. The essential nature of the body is the derived matter composed of the four great elements. The essential nature of feeling and of mind are as their own names indicate. The essential nature of dharmas is whatever dharmas remain, excluding the preceding three. It is said that, while in concentration, observing the body distinctly down to its ultimate particles and momentary instants constitutes the completion of body-mindfulness; the completion of the remaining three should be understood correspondingly. What constitutes the substance of the four foundations of mindfulness? Each has three distinct aspects: its own essential nature, its mixed-with-associates aspect, and its object-based aspect. The own-nature foundation has wisdom as its substance, this wisdom being of three kinds — born of hearing and the others — accordingly also called the three kinds of mindfulness-foundation. The mixed-with-associates foundation has as its substance whatever else co-arises with that wisdom. The object-based foundation has as its substance whatever dharmas that wisdom takes as its object. How is it known that the own-nature aspect is specifically wisdom, and not something else? Because a sūtra states: abiding in the body, repeatedly observing the body, is called body-mindfulness-foundation — and the same holds for the remaining three. The term "repeatedly observing" refers specifically to the substance of wisdom, since nothing other than wisdom has the function of repeated observation.

Why, then, is the name "mindfulness-foundation" given to what is essentially wisdom? The Vaibhāṣika masters explain that mindfulness is especially prominent in this category, sustaining wisdom's function much as a wedge, by its steady force, allows an axe to split wood. The treatise refines this: properly speaking, it is wisdom that causes mindfulness to remain firmly established on its object — which is precisely why the name applies, since mindfulness stays clearly fixed on whatever wisdom observes. The four foundations arise in a specific, motivated sequence, always beginning with the body, since coarser objects are naturally observed first and since desire and craving operate specifically with respect to the body. Craving for the body arises because one delights in the feeling it provides; delighting in feeling arises because the mind is not yet tamed; and the mind's untamed state arises because affliction has not yet been severed — hence feeling, mind, and dharmas follow the body in exactly this order. These four foundations respectively counteract the four inversions — mistaking the impure for pure, suffering for pleasurable, the impermanent for permanent, and non-self for self — and it is for this reason, tracking these four inversions precisely, that there are exactly four foundations, neither more nor fewer.

Chinese Source

何緣於慧立念住名？毘婆沙師說：此品念增故。是念力持慧得轉義，如斧破木由楔力持。理實應言慧令念住，是故於慧立念住名，隨慧所觀能明記故，由此無滅作如是言：若有能於身住循身觀，緣身念得住，乃至廣說。世尊亦說：若有於身住循身觀者，念便住不謬。然有經言：此四念住由何故集？由何故滅？食觸名色作意集故，如次令身受心法集。食觸名色作意滅故，如次令身受心法滅。應知彼說所緣念住，以念於彼得安住故。又念住別名隨所緣緣自他俱相續異故。一一念住各有三種，此四念住說次隨生。生復何緣？次第如是。隨境麁者應先觀故，或諸欲貪於身處轉，故四念住觀身在初。然貪於身由欣樂受，欣樂於受由心不調，心之不調由惑未斷，故觀受等如是次第。此四念住如次治彼淨、樂、常、我四種顛倒，故唯有四不增不減。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is the name mindfulness-foundation applied to wisdom? The Vaibhāṣika masters explain: because, in this category, mindfulness is especially prominent — wisdom is able to function precisely because it is sustained by the power of mindfulness, just as an axe splits wood because it is held steady by the force of a wedge. In truth, it should rather be said that wisdom is what causes mindfulness to remain established, and it is for this reason that the name mindfulness-foundation is applied to wisdom: because, in dependence on whatever wisdom observes, mindfulness remains clearly fixed upon it. On this basis, the Venerable Aniruddha states: whoever is able to abide in the body, repeatedly observing the body, has mindfulness of the body thereby established, and so on, at length. The Blessed One likewise states: whoever abides in the body, repeatedly observing the body, has mindfulness thereby established without error. There is, however, a further sūtra passage stating: by what are these four foundations of mindfulness assembled, and by what are they dissolved? By the assembling of food, contact, name-and-form, and mental application, respectively, body, feeling, mind, and dharmas are assembled; by their dissolving, respectively, body, feeling, mind, and dharmas are dissolved. It should be understood that this passage speaks of the object-based foundation of mindfulness, since it is with reference to that object that mindfulness comes to be established. The foundations of mindfulness are also distinguished by name according to whether their object is one's own continuum, another's, or both together. Each individual foundation thus has three kinds. These four foundations are said to arise in this specific sequence. Why does this sequence hold? Whichever object is coarser should logically be observed first, and since desire and craving operate specifically with respect to the body, its contemplation is placed first. Craving for the body arises because one delights in feeling; delighting in feeling arises because the mind is not yet tamed; and the mind's untamed state arises because affliction has not yet been severed — hence the sequence through feeling, mind, and dharmas. These four foundations respectively counteract the four inversions of purity, pleasure, permanence, and self, and it is for this reason that there are exactly four, neither more nor fewer.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that this section's naming discussion directly parallels the treatise's earlier point about mindfulness of breathing (fascicle 22): in both cases, a practice named "mindfulness" turns out to have wisdom, not mindfulness itself, as its actual essential nature — mindfulness being instead the sustaining force that keeps the object clearly and steadily in view. We have kept this recurring naming pattern visible, since the treatise itself explicitly draws the comparison ("as with the foundations of mindfulness" was used to explain breath-mindfulness's own naming) and revisits it here in fuller detail.

We should also note the elegant, tightly reasoned causal chain the treatise offers for why body is contemplated first, then feeling, then mind, then dharmas: each step names a specific psychological dependency (craving for the body depends on delighting in feeling; delighting in feeling depends on an untamed mind; an untamed mind depends on unsevered affliction) rather than simply asserting an arbitrary order — and the four foundations are explicitly correlated, one-to-one, with the four specific inversions (purity/pleasure/permanence/self) already discussed in fascicle 19, which is offered as the reason there are exactly four foundations and not some other number.

2. From Unmixed to Mixed: The Combined Dharma-Mindfulness Stage

The treatise now distinguishes how the four foundations of mindfulness take their objects. Among the four, three — body, feeling, and mind — take only an unmixed object, while the fourth, dharmas, can take either a mixed or an unmixed object. Observing dharmas alone is "unmixed"; observing two, three, or all four of body, feeling, mind, and dharmas together is "mixed." Once a practitioner has matured this mixed practice — observing body and the rest together within the dharma-foundation of mindfulness — what comes next? The practitioner now abides within a fully combined dharma-mindfulness-foundation, observing all four objects — body, feeling, mind, and dharmas — together as a single field, and cultivating with respect to that combined field four specific aspects: impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self.

Chinese Source

四中三種唯不雜緣，第四所緣通雜不雜。若唯觀法名不雜緣，若於身等二三或四總而觀察名為雜緣。如是熟修雜緣身等法念住已，復何所修？頌曰：彼居法念住，總觀四所緣，修非常及苦，空非我行相。論曰：彼觀行者居緣總雜法念住中，總觀所緣身等四境修四行相，所謂非常、苦、空、非我。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these four, three take only unmixed objects, while the fourth can take either a mixed or an unmixed object. Observing dharmas alone is called taking an unmixed object; observing two, three, or all four of body and the rest together is called taking a mixed object. Having thus matured the practice of the dharma-foundation of mindfulness with a mixed object encompassing body and the rest, what does one cultivate next? Verse: abiding in that dharma-mindfulness-foundation, one observes all four objects together, cultivating the aspects of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self. Explanation: that practitioner, abiding within the dharma-foundation of mindfulness that takes a totally mixed object, observes all four objects — body and the rest — together, cultivating four aspects: impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the clean architectural logic of this progression: the practitioner moves from observing each of the four objects separately (body alone, then feeling alone, then mind alone, then dharmas alone — each with its own specific characteristic), through progressively combining pairs and larger groupings of objects within the dharma-foundation specifically, and finally to a single, fully unified field of observation covering all four objects at once. This is the immediate structural preparation for the four wholesome roots the treatise introduces next, which build directly on this fully combined observation rather than on any of the earlier, narrower stages.

We should also note that only the dharma-foundation of mindfulness is capable of this expansion into mixed objects, while body, feeling, and mind each remain confined to their own single, unmixed object throughout — a structural asymmetry the text states without further explanation, though it fits naturally with dharmas being defined, earlier in this fascicle, as "whatever remains" once body, feeling, and mind are set aside, making it the natural category flexible enough to eventually reabsorb and unify all three of the others.

3. Warmth and Summit: The First Two Wholesome Roots, and the Sixteen Aspects

As the combined dharma-mindfulness-foundation gradually matures to its highest grade, it gives rise to the first wholesome root conducive to reaching the decisive distinction of the path of seeing — called "warmth" (ūṣmagata), named by analogy with actual warmth, since it is the preliminary sign of the fire of the noble path, the fire capable of burning up the firewood of affliction, just as warmth precedes an actual flame. Because this stage spans a comparatively long period, it is the first point at which a practitioner is capable of fully observing all four noble truths, and of fully cultivating all sixteen aspects that structure that observation — four aspects for each truth. In observing suffering, one cultivates impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self; in observing the origin, cause, origin, arising, and condition; in observing cessation, cessation, peace, excellence, and freedom; and in observing the path, path, suchness, practice, and deliverance. The precise meaning of each of these sixteen aspects is reserved for later discussion.

Chinese Source

修此觀已生何善根？頌曰：從此生煖法，具觀四聖諦，修十六行相，次生頂亦然，如是二善根，皆初法後四。論曰：修習總緣共相法念住，漸次成熟乃至上上品。從此念住後，有順決擇分初善根生，名為煖法。此法如煖立煖法名，是能燒惑薪聖道火前相，如火前相故名為煖。此煖善根分位長故，能具觀察四聖諦境，及能具修十六行相。觀苦聖諦修四行相：一非常、二苦、三空、四非我。觀集聖諦修四行相：一因、二集、三生、四緣。觀滅聖諦修四行相：一滅、二靜、三妙、四離。觀道聖諦修四行相：一道、二如、三行、四出。此相差別如後當辯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having cultivated this observation, what wholesome root does it give rise to? Verse: from this arises the dharma of warmth, at which stage one fully observes the four noble truths, cultivating the sixteen aspects; next arises the dharma of summit, likewise; for both of these two wholesome roots, mindfulness begins with the dharma foundation alone, and then encompasses all four afterward. Explanation: as one cultivates the totally-combined, shared-characteristic dharma-mindfulness-foundation, it gradually matures up to its highest-of-the-highest grade. Following this mindfulness-foundation, the first wholesome root conducive to reaching the decisive distinction arises — this is called the dharma of warmth. This dharma is named warmth by analogy with actual warmth, since it is the preliminary sign of the fire of the noble path, the fire capable of burning up the firewood of affliction, and, being like the warmth that precedes an actual fire, it is called warmth. Because this wholesome root of warmth spans a comparatively long stage, it is capable of fully observing the object of all four noble truths, and of fully cultivating all sixteen aspects. In observing the noble truth of suffering, one cultivates four aspects: impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self. In observing the noble truth of the origin, one cultivates four aspects: cause, origin, arising, and condition. In observing the noble truth of cessation, one cultivates four aspects: cessation, peace, excellence, and freedom. In observing the noble truth of the path, one cultivates four aspects: path, suchness, practice, and deliverance. The specific distinctions among these aspects will be discussed later.

As the wholesome root of warmth gradually increases through its lower, middle, and upper grades and reaches completion, a further wholesome root arises, called the dharma of "summit" (mūrdhan) — marking a further advance in excellence and so given its own distinct name. Among the "movable" wholesome roots (those still capable of being lost through retrogression), this dharma is the most excellent, comparable to the crown of a person's head; or, alternatively, it is named "summit" because this stage is the very turning point between advancing further and falling back, like the peak of a mountain, from which one may go either up or down. Summit likewise fully observes the four truths and fully cultivates the sixteen aspects, just as warmth does. For both warmth and summit, at the very moment of their initial attainment, only the dharma-foundation of mindfulness is present — the moment a given wholesome root, through the sixteen aspects, first treads upon the path of the four noble truths; only as it subsequently advances further do all four foundations of mindfulness become present again, since at that first moment one has not yet generated a comparable sense of regard toward the three foundations (body, feeling, mind) individually, compared to the newly unified dharma-foundation.

Chinese Source

此煖善根下中上品漸次增長至成滿時有善根生，名為頂法，此轉勝故更立異名。動善根中此法最勝，如人頂故，名為頂法。或由此是進退兩際，如山頂故，說名為頂。此亦如煖具觀四諦，及能具修十六行相。如是煖頂二種善根，初安足時唯法念住。以何義故名初安足？謂隨何善根以十六行相最初遊踐四聖諦迹，後增進時具四念住。諸先所得後不現前，於彼不生欽重心故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As this wholesome root of warmth gradually increases through its lower, middle, and upper grades, and reaches completion, a further wholesome root arises, called the dharma of summit — since this represents a further advance in excellence, it is given a distinct new name. Among the movable wholesome roots, this dharma is the most excellent, comparable to the crown of a person's head, hence it is called the dharma of summit. Or, alternatively, because this stage is the very turning point between advancing further and falling back, like the peak of a mountain, it is called summit. This likewise, just as with warmth, fully observes the four truths and is capable of fully cultivating the sixteen aspects. For both of these two wholesome roots, warmth and summit, at the moment of their initial setting foot, only the dharma-foundation of mindfulness is present. What is meant by initial setting foot? It refers to the very first moment at which a given wholesome root, through the sixteen aspects, first treads upon the path of the four noble truths; as it subsequently advances further, all four foundations of mindfulness become present. Whatever was previously attained does not yet manifest again at that first moment, since one has not yet generated a sense of high regard toward those foundations individually.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the sixteen aspects introduced here as a structurally central framework the treatise itself says will be explained more fully "later" — we have translated the sixteen terms (four per truth) as given, without expanding on their individual meanings beyond what this passage itself states, since the treatise explicitly defers that fuller explanation to a subsequent point we have not yet reached in this project's fascicle-by-fascicle progress. Readers should expect a more detailed gloss on terms like "suchness" or "deliverance" once the treatise itself provides one.

We should also note the vivid double meaning offered for why the second wholesome root is named "summit" — most excellent among a still-retrogressible class of practice (like a person's literal crown, their highest point), and simultaneously the precise turning point between further advance and falling back (like a mountain peak, from which the path runs in either direction) — the treatise presents these as complementary explanations for the same name rather than competing accounts, and we have kept both together as offered.

4. Patience and Supreme Mundane Dharma: Narrowing to the Threshold of the Path

As summit gradually increases through its own lower, middle, and upper grades and reaches completion, a further wholesome root arises: "patience" (kṣānti) — named for being the most excellent among the various capacities for patiently accepting the principle of the four truths, and also because, at this stage, one can never again fall back into an unfortunate rebirth. Unlike warmth and summit, patience is always the dharma-foundation of mindfulness alone, both at its initial attainment and as it advances. Patience itself has lower, middle, and upper grades: its lower and middle grades match summit exactly, fully observing all four truths through all sixteen aspects, but its upper grade narrows dramatically, observing only the desire realm's truth of suffering — since it stands immediately adjacent to the still narrower scope of supreme mundane dharma. This narrowing happens progressively: the practitioner reduces the object taken with respect to each aspect of each truth in turn, across the counteragent paths for the form and formless realms, until only two moments of mental application remain, focused solely on the desire realm's suffering — this whole progression is called "middle patience."

Chinese Source

此頂善根下中上品漸次增長，至成滿時有善根生名為忍法，於四諦理能忍可中此最勝故。又此位忍無退墮故名為忍法。此忍善根安足增進皆法念住，與前有別。然此忍法有下中上，下中二品與頂法同，謂具觀察四聖諦境及能具修十六行相；上品有異，唯觀欲苦，與世第一相隣接故。由此義准，煖等善根皆能具緣三界苦等義已成立，無簡別故。謂瑜伽師於色無色對治道等一一聖諦行相所緣漸減漸略，乃至但有二念作意思惟欲界苦聖諦境，齊此以前名中忍位。

Ru-Yi Translation

As this wholesome root of summit gradually increases through its lower, middle, and upper grades, and reaches completion, a further wholesome root arises, called the dharma of patience — since, among the various capacities for patiently accepting the principle of the four truths, this is the most excellent. Moreover, at this stage, patience no longer admits of falling back into an unfortunate rebirth, and so it is named the dharma of patience. This wholesome root of patience, both at its initial attainment and as it subsequently advances, is always the dharma-foundation of mindfulness alone, differing in this respect from the preceding two. This dharma of patience itself has lower, middle, and upper grades. Its lower and middle grades are the same as summit, fully observing all four noble truths and fully cultivating the sixteen aspects. Its upper grade, however, differs: it observes only the desire realm's truth of suffering, since it stands immediately adjacent to supreme mundane dharma. On this basis, it is established, without further distinction needed, that warmth and the other wholesome roots are each capable of fully taking as their object the suffering and so forth of all three realms. That is, the yoga practitioner, with respect to the counteragent paths for the form and formless realms and so on, progressively reduces and contracts the object taken with respect to each aspect of each noble truth in turn, until only two moments of mental application remain, contemplating the object of the desire realm's truth of suffering alone — everything up to this point is called the stage of middle patience.

Immediately following middle patience, without interruption, a superior wholesome root arises, lasting a single aspect for a single instant only — this is "upper-grade patience," so named because it arises without any continuity, occurring for exactly that one moment. Immediately following upper-grade patience, again without interruption, arises the fourth and final wholesome root: "supreme mundane dharma" (laukikāgra-dharma), which matches upper-grade patience's narrowed scope exactly — the desire realm's truth of suffering, a single aspect, a single instant. Because it remains contaminated, it is called "mundane"; because it is the most excellent among all contaminated, worldly dharmas, it is called "supreme." It possesses the power of a person's own effort and, standing apart from any homogeneous cause of its own kind (since nothing precedes it that is truly like it), is uniquely capable of drawing forth the arising of the noble path itself immediately after it — which is why it earns the name "most supreme." All four of these wholesome roots — warmth, summit, patience, and supreme mundane dharma — being mindfulness-foundations in nature, have wisdom as their essential substance, or, including their accompanying associates, the nature of all five aggregates; this excludes their subsequent "attainment" as a separate factor, since noble ones who have already passed beyond this stage have no need to manifest these wholesome roots again.

Chinese Source

從此位無間起勝善根一行一剎那名上品忍，此善根起不相續故。上品忍無間生世第一法。如上品忍緣欲苦諦修一行相唯一剎那，此有漏故名為世間，是最勝故名為第一。此有漏法世間中勝，是故名為世第一法。有士用力，離同類因，引聖道生，故名最勝。如是煖等四種善根，念住性故皆慧為體，若并助伴皆五蘊性。然除彼得，勿諸聖者煖等善根重現前故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Immediately following this stage, without interruption, a superior wholesome root arises, lasting a single aspect for a single instant — this is called upper-grade patience, since this wholesome root arises without any continuity. Immediately following upper-grade patience, supreme mundane dharma arises. Just as upper-grade patience takes the desire realm's truth of suffering as its object, cultivating a single aspect for a single instant only, so too does this: because it is contaminated, it is called mundane; because it is the most excellent, it is called supreme. This contaminated dharma is the most excellent among all worldly dharmas, and it is for this reason that it is called supreme mundane dharma. It possesses the power of a person's own effort and, apart from any homogeneous cause of its own kind, is capable of drawing forth the arising of the noble path — and it is for this reason that it is called most supreme. Thus, all four of these wholesome roots, being of the nature of mindfulness-foundations, all have wisdom as their essential substance; if their accompanying associates are included as well, they have the nature of all five aggregates. This excludes, however, their attainment, lest the wholesome roots of warmth and the rest manifest again for noble ones.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the elegant funnel structure of these four wholesome roots as one of the treatise's clearest architectural sequences: warmth and summit both range over the full breadth of all four truths and all sixteen aspects; patience's lower and middle grades match that same breadth, but its upper grade collapses down to a single truth (suffering), a single realm (desire); and supreme mundane dharma occupies that identical narrowest point for exactly one moment, immediately before the path of seeing itself opens onto the full scope of all three realms and all four truths again. We have kept this expand-then-radically-narrow-then-transform structure visible, since the progression itself is the point — each stage's scope is not arbitrary but tracks how close the practitioner stands to the irreversible threshold of the path.

We should also note the technical claim that supreme mundane dharma has "no homogeneous cause of its own kind" (離同類因) — meaning nothing exactly like it precedes it in the practitioner's continuum — which the treatise offers as part of the explanation for why it alone, among worldly dharmas, is capable of directly triggering the arising of the noble path. This is a fairly technical causal claim drawing on this tradition's broader theory of causes (the six-cause / four-condition scheme introduced in an earlier chapter this project has not yet reached), and we have presented it as stated rather than reconstructing the full causal theory behind it.

5. Present and Future Cultivation: A Technical Accounting

The treatise now tracks, with considerable precision, how much of each wholesome root is cultivated "in the present" versus simultaneously secured "for the future" at each stage — a doctrine holding that a single present act of meditative cultivation can, under the right conditions, also cultivate related future instances of the same practice, not merely the present moment alone. At warmth's initial attainment, with respect to three of the four truths, the dharma-foundation of mindfulness is cultivated as one in the present and four for the future, and likewise for any one aspect; with respect to cessation specifically, it is one in the present and only one for the future, since this type has not been attained before and only what shares its exact category can be secured this early. As warmth advances further, this expands: three truths now secure four in the future per mindfulness-foundation and sixteen per aspect, and cessation itself catches up to the same four and sixteen — because the type has by then already been attained once, even dissimilar categories can now be secured for the future as well.

Summit, from its very first attainment, already matches warmth's more advanced count across all four truths at once — four per mindfulness-foundation, sixteen per aspect — and holds that same count as it advances further, including with respect to cessation. Patience matches this same count too, at both its initial attainment and throughout its advancement, across all four truths; the one exception is that once its object begins to contract (in the progression toward middle and upper-grade patience described earlier), the aspects corresponding to whatever has been contracted away are simply no longer cultivated at all. Supreme mundane dharma, finally, narrows back down: taking only the desire realm's truth of suffering as its object, it cultivates the dharma-foundation of mindfulness as one in the present and four for the future, and likewise one aspect as one in the present and four for the future — a narrower count than patience's, because supreme mundane dharma admits of no differing category from what immediately follows it (the path of seeing itself), already sharing that path's own character.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that this section presupposes a specific technical doctrine — that a single act of present cultivation can, under the right conditions, simultaneously secure the cultivation of related future instances of the same practice, "banking" future progress rather than requiring each moment to be separately earned. This mechanism was not explained from first principles in this passage; it draws on a broader theory of "present cultivation and future cultivation" (現在修/未來修) that this project has encountered in more limited form in earlier fascicles' technical accounting sections (such as fascicle 21's six times of re-attainment), and a fuller grounding of the mechanism likely belongs to material on causes and conditions not yet reached in this project's progress.

We should also note the numbers themselves trace a clear narrative even without fully unpacking the underlying mechanism: they climb as a practitioner's attainment matures and generalizes (from as narrow as one-for-one at the very first moment of warmth, up to four-for-sixteen at full maturity), before collapsing sharply back down at supreme mundane dharma, whose deliberately narrow scope (a single truth, a single aspect, a single realm) mirrors the narrowing already described for upper-grade patience in the previous section. We have preserved every stated count exactly rather than summarizing the trend, since the precise numbers are what the passage is actually asserting.

6. "Favorable to the Decisive Distinction": Naming, Grade, and Where These Roots Arise

The treatise now steps back to classify warmth, summit, patience, and supreme mundane dharma together under a single collective name: "favorable to the decisive distinction" (nirvedha-bhāgīya). The name is built from its parts — "decisive" means cutting through conclusively, "distinction" means discerning discriminately, and what does both is the noble path itself, since only the noble path can sever doubt and distinguish the specific characteristics of the four truths. "Portion" indicates that what these four roots favor is specifically the decisive-distinction portion of the path of seeing — hence "favorable to" that portion, and so the full name. All four are accomplished exclusively through meditation, never through hearing or reflection alone, since they exist only within stages of balanced meditative absorption. Among them, warmth and summit belong to the lower grade, since both remain "movable" and capable of retrogression; patience belongs to the middle grade, surpassing the first two while still having supreme mundane dharma above it; and supreme mundane dharma alone occupies the upper grade.

7. How Each Wholesome Root Is Lost, and the Guarantee Each One Secures

The treatise now explains how these wholesome roots are actually relinquished. A noble one loses warmth or summit only by losing the meditative stage they depended on — meaning rebirth into a higher stage that supersedes it — whereas an ordinary worldling loses them simply through death itself, regardless of whether the meditative stage is otherwise lost. Only warmth and summit can be lost through outright retrogression, and this applies only to ordinary worldlings, never to noble ones; patience and supreme mundane dharma, once attained, are never retrogressed from even by ordinary worldlings. One who attains warmth and the rest by relying on a root (not merely preparatory) meditative stage will definitely attain the seeing of the truths within that very lifetime, given how intense their aversion to birth-and-death has become. If a wholesome root once relinquished is later attained again, what is attained is necessarily a genuinely new instance, not a resumption of the old one — just as retaking a relinquished monastic vow yields a new vow, since the earlier one was never brought to full maturity through sustained effort. If warmth and the rest are lost through an intervening rebirth, encountering a skilled teacher allows summit and beyond to arise again directly, without needing to be built up from scratch; without such a teacher, one must cultivate again entirely from the beginning.

Losing the stage here refers to being reborn into a higher stage. For an ordinary worldling, however, whether or not the meditative stage itself is lost, the wholesome root is necessarily relinquished simply through the loss of that being's shared class-membership. The first two wholesome roots can also be relinquished through retrogression. Relinquishment through death applies only to ordinary worldlings, not to noble ones; relinquishment through losing the meditative stage applies only to noble ones, not to ordinary worldlings. With respect to patience and supreme mundane dharma, even ordinary worldlings never retrogress from them. One who gives rise to warmth and the other wholesome roots by relying on a root meditative stage will, within that very lifetime, definitely attain the seeing of the truths, since their aversion toward birth-and-death is exceptionally intense. If, having previously relinquished a wholesome root, one later attains it again, what is thereby attained is necessarily not identical to what was previously relinquished, just as one who has relinquished the prātimokṣa precepts and later takes them up again receives a genuinely new instance, since the original was never brought to maturity through great, sustained effort. If one has previously attained warmth and the other wholesome roots, but then passes through a rebirth, causing them to be relinquished, and then encounters a skilled teacher of the Dharma who properly discerns their stage, summit and the rest can arise again directly; but if no such teacher is encountered, one must instead cultivate again entirely from the beginning. Both losing and retrogressing have as their essential nature a lack of continued attainment; however, retrogression necessarily involves the arising of some fault, whereas mere loss does not necessarily involve this.

What benefit does attaining each wholesome root confer? Warmth guarantees that one will eventually reach nirvāṇa, even if one still retrogresses, cuts off one's own wholesome roots, commits an ānantarya offense, or falls into an unfortunate rebirth in the meantime — since even so, one will not circulate in saṃsāra indefinitely. This distinguishes warmth from the earlier "liberation-conducive" wholesome roots from fascicle 18: warmth already shares the path of seeing's own mode of activity, standing close to it, whereas the liberation-conducive roots do not yet. Summit guarantees that one's wholesome roots, though retrogression remains possible, will never ultimately be cut off. Patience guarantees, even though it can still be relinquished through death while one remains an ordinary worldling, that one will never again retrogress from it, never commit an ānantarya offense, and never fall into an unfortunate rebirth — the latter two guarantees being logically linked, since anyone who commits such an offense necessarily falls into such a rebirth.

We have kept the two terms distinct rather than treating "lose" and "retrogress" as simple synonyms, since the text's own reasoning about who can and cannot experience each depends on this distinction.

8. Can a Wholesome Root Transfer Between Vehicles?

Since each of the four wholesome roots comes in three grades corresponding to the three spiritual lineages (śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, and bodhisattva/buddha), the treatise asks whether a root cultivated within one lineage can later transfer to another. For someone of the śrāvaka lineage, once warmth or summit has arisen, it remains genuinely possible to redirect one's practice toward unsurpassed buddhahood instead — but once patience has been attained, this possibility closes permanently, since patience guarantees one has already transcended unfortunate rebirths altogether, while a bodhisattva's very vocation requires a willingness to enter such rebirths in order to benefit and transform other beings. A śrāvaka's warmth, summit, and patience can all still transfer toward the pratyekabuddha vehicle instead, since that vehicle lies outside full buddhahood and so remains open even from patience onward.

Chinese Source

隨何種性善根已生，彼可移轉向餘乘不？頌曰：轉聲聞種性，二成佛三餘，麟角佛無轉，一坐成覺故。論曰：聲聞種性煖頂已生，容可轉成無上正覺，彼若得忍無成佛理，謂於惡趣已超越故。菩提薩埵利物為懷，為化有情必往惡趣，彼忍種性不可迴轉，是故定無得成佛義。聲聞種性煖頂忍三，皆有可轉成獨覺義，在佛乘外故說為餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever lineage a wholesome root has arisen within, can it transfer toward a different vehicle? Verse: among those of the śrāvaka lineage, the first two can transform into buddhahood; the first three can transform into the remaining vehicle; the rhinoceros-horn type and the buddha admit of no transformation, since each attains awakening in a single sitting. Explanation: for one of the śrāvaka lineage, once warmth or summit has arisen, it remains possible to transform toward unsurpassed, perfect awakening. But once one has attained patience, there is no longer any possibility of becoming a buddha, since, having attained patience, one has already transcended unfortunate rebirths. A bodhisattva, whose very heart is set on benefiting all beings, must, in order to transform sentient beings, be willing to go even into unfortunate rebirths — but one who has already attained the fixed lineage of patience can no longer turn back toward this willingness, and it is for this reason that such a person can definitely never attain buddhahood. For one of the śrāvaka lineage, all three of warmth, summit, and patience retain the possibility of transforming toward the pratyekabuddha vehicle instead, since that vehicle lies outside full buddhahood, and is therefore called "the remaining" vehicle.

Two paths, however, admit no transformation whatsoever once begun: the "rhinoceros-horn" solitary buddha (who practices entirely alone, without companions) and the fully-enlightened buddha. Both rely on the fourth dhyāna as their basis and both attain their respective awakening in a single, uninterrupted sitting — meaning, from the arising of warmth all the way through to awakening itself, the practitioner never rises from that seat (other teachers extend this even further back, to the very start of impurity-contemplation). The fourth dhyāna is capable of supporting this because it is an unshakeable, supremely clear and sharp concentration, and "awakening" here refers specifically to the knowledge of exhaustion and the knowledge of non-arising, the essential nature of bodhi itself, to be discussed later. A different kind of pratyekabuddha exists as well — one who practices within a community rather than entirely alone — and for this lineage, the first two wholesome roots can still transfer toward a different vehicle without obstruction, unlike the rhinoceros-horn type.

Chinese Source

麟角佛言顯麟角喻及無上覺。煖等善根並無移轉向餘乘義，皆以第四靜慮為依，一坐便成自乘覺故。第四靜慮是不傾動最極明利三摩地故，堪為麟角喻無上覺所依。此中覺言顯盡、無生智，後當辯，此是菩提性故。言一坐者，從煖善根乃至菩提，不起于座。有餘師說：從不淨觀不起于座乃至菩提。有餘獨覺異麟角喻，起彼種性初二善根，轉向餘乘，理無遮礙。

Ru-Yi Translation

The verse's phrase "rhinoceros-horn, buddha" refers to the rhinoceros-horn-simile type and to unsurpassed buddhahood. For those following either of these two paths, their wholesome roots admit of no possibility of transformation toward any other vehicle at all, since both rely on the fourth dhyāna as their basis, and both attain the awakening proper to their own vehicle in a single sitting. The fourth dhyāna is a concentration that is unshakeable and supremely clear and sharp, and it is for this reason that it is capable of serving as the basis for both the rhinoceros-horn type and unsurpassed awakening. Here, the term awakening refers to the knowledge of exhaustion and the knowledge of non-arising, since these constitute the essential nature of bodhi itself, to be discussed later. By a single sitting is meant that, from the arising of the wholesome root of warmth all the way through to the attainment of awakening, one never rises from one's seat. Other teachers say instead: one does not rise from one's seat from the very beginning of impurity-contemplation all the way through to awakening. There is another kind of pratyekabuddha, distinct from the rhinoceros-horn type; for one of this lineage, the first two wholesome roots can indeed still transform toward a different vehicle, there being no obstruction in principle to this.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the reasoning behind why patience forecloses the bodhisattva path as a genuinely striking piece of logic: it is not that patience is somehow "too advanced" for bodhisattvahood, but that patience specifically guarantees permanent freedom from unfortunate rebirths, while a bodhisattva's defining vocation requires the willingness to enter exactly such rebirths for the sake of helping other beings — the two paths become logically incompatible at precisely this point, not because one is superior to the other in some general sense, but because their specific commitments directly conflict.

We should also note the vivid image of "a single, uninterrupted sitting" spanning from the very first wholesome root all the way to full awakening, used to explain why the rhinoceros-horn pratyekabuddha and the buddha vehicles admit of absolutely no possibility of redirection — the entire progression happens in one unbroken meditative session, leaving no gap at which a change of direction could occur. We have preserved this image and the open disagreement about exactly how far back that "single sitting" extends (from warmth specifically, or all the way back to the start of impurity-contemplation) as the text presents both views, without indicating which the treatise itself favors.

9. Three Lifetimes to Liberation, and Planting the Liberation-Conducive Roots

Can the wholesome roots favorable to the decisive distinction arise in the very same lifetime a practitioner first takes up this practice? No — whoever gives rise to them in the present lifetime must have already given rise, in some earlier lifetime, to the more basic "liberation-conducive" roots first introduced back in fascicle 18. Whoever first plants those liberation-conducive roots reaches liberation, at the very fastest, only after three lifetimes: in the first lifetime, the liberation-conducive roots themselves arise; in the second, the roots favorable to the decisive distinction arise; and in the third, one finally enters the noble path and, in due course, attains liberation. This unfolds much like planting a seed, watching the sprout mature, and finally gathering the fruit — three genuinely distinct stages, just as a person's entry into the nature of the Dharma, their maturation, and their liberation are three distinct stages as well.

Chinese Source

頗有此生創修此行，即此生引起順決擇分耶？不爾。云何？頌曰：前順解脫分，速三生解脫，聞思成三業，殖在人三洲。論曰：順決擇分今生起者，必前生起順解脫分。諸有創殖順解脫分，極速三生方得解脫。謂初生起順解脫分，第二生起順決擇分，第三生入聖乃至得解脫。譬如下種、苗成、結實，三位不同。身入法性、成熟、解脫，三位亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Is it possible that, in this very lifetime one first begins to cultivate this practice, one gives rise to the roots favorable to the decisive distinction in that same lifetime? No. What, then, is the actual timeline? Verse: the earlier liberation-conducive roots must be planted first; at the very fastest, liberation is reached across three lifetimes; these roots are accomplished through the three karmas of hearing and reflection, and are planted only among humans, in the three continents. Explanation: whoever gives rise to the roots favorable to the decisive distinction in the present lifetime must have given rise, in some prior lifetime, to the liberation-conducive roots. Whoever first plants the liberation-conducive roots will, at the very earliest, attain liberation only after three lifetimes. That is, in the first lifetime, the liberation-conducive roots arise; in the second lifetime, the roots favorable to the decisive distinction arise; and in the third lifetime, one enters the noble path and, in due course, attains liberation. This is comparable to the three distinct stages of planting a seed, the sprout maturing, and the fruit finally forming. The three stages of a person entering into the nature of the Dharma, maturing, and attaining liberation are likewise analogous to these.

These liberation-conducive roots are accomplished exclusively through hearing and reflection, never through meditation at this early stage, and have as their essential substance all three kinds of karma together — bodily, verbal, and mental — even though, considered in its most essential aspect, this is really just the mental karma of aspiration itself; because that aspiration can also give rise to accompanying bodily and verbal acts, those acts too rightly count as part of the liberation-conducive roots. Even a modest act — giving a single meal, keeping a single precept — genuinely plants these roots if it is done while deeply delighting in the aspiration for liberation, sustained by the power of that vow. Such planting can occur only among humans, in the three continents (excluding the north), since other destinies lack the necessary aversion to saṃsāra and wisdom to make it possible — and it occurs specifically upon encountering the arising of a buddha in the world, though other teachers add that encountering a pratyekabuddha can serve the same purpose.

Chinese Source

傳說如是順解脫分唯聞思所成，通三業為體。雖就最勝唯是意業，而此思願攝起身語亦得名為順解脫分。有施一食持一戒等深樂解脫，願力所持便名種殖順解脫分。殖順解脫分唯人三洲，餘厭離般若，如應無故，遇佛出世殖此善根。有餘師言：亦遇獨覺。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is said that these liberation-conducive roots are accomplished exclusively through hearing and reflection, and have as their essential substance all three kinds of karma together. Although, considered in its most essential and primary aspect, this is exclusively mental karma, nevertheless, since this same aspiration-volition can also give rise to accompanying bodily and verbal karma, these too can rightly be called part of the liberation-conducive roots. One who, for instance, gives even a single meal or keeps even a single precept, while deeply delighting in the aspiration for liberation, sustained by the power of that aspiration-vow, can rightly be said to have thereby planted the liberation-conducive roots. The liberation-conducive roots can be planted only among humans, in the three continents, since the remaining destinies lack the necessary aversion and wisdom, as appropriate to each case, and they are planted only upon encountering the arising of a buddha in the world. Other teachers say: one may also plant them upon encountering a pratyekabuddha.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the reassuring, practical implication of this section: attaining liberation is presented not as requiring extraordinary or dramatic acts, but as something a genuinely modest deed — a single offered meal, a single kept precept — can set in motion, provided it is done with authentic aspiration toward liberation rather than mixed or worldly motives. We have kept the treatise's own modest examples exactly as given, since the point being made depends on their being ordinary, achievable acts, not extraordinary ones.

We should also note the three-stage seed/sprout/fruit and enter/mature/liberate analogies are offered as parallel structures for the same three-lifetime timeline, and we have kept both images side by side as the text presents them, since each captures a slightly different aspect of the progression (the agricultural image emphasizing patience and gradual growth, the Dharma-entry image emphasizing the practitioner's own internal transformation).
