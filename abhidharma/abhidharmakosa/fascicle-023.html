The Four Foundations of Mindfulness

Fascicle 23 continues the second part of Chapter 6 (分別賢聖品第六之二, Distinguishing the Noble Ones), taking up cultivation's next stage once the calm-abiding built through the two gates described in fascicle 22 (impurity-contemplation and mindfulness of breathing) is in place: insight, cultivated through the four foundations of mindfulness — body, feeling, mind, and dharmas — observed through both their own specific characteristics and the shared marks of impermanence, suffering, and non-self common to all conditioned things.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 3 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 23 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. The Four Foundations of Mindfulness: Structure, Naming, and Sequence

Having established the calm-abiding built through the two gates of cultivation, the treatise now turns to insight, cultivated through the four foundations of mindfulness: body, feeling, mind, and dharmas, each observed through both its own specific characteristic and the characteristics shared by all conditioned things — impermanence, suffering, and being empty of self. The body's own nature is derived matter composed of the four great elements; feeling and mind are as their names indicate; and "dharmas" here means whatever remains once body, feeling, and mind are set aside. Each of the four foundations has a threefold structure: an "own-nature" aspect, whose substance is wisdom itself (in its three forms born of hearing, reflection, and meditation); a "mixed-with-associates" aspect, whose substance is whatever else co-arises with that wisdom; and an "object-based" aspect, whose substance is whatever dharmas that wisdom takes as its object. That the own-nature aspect is specifically wisdom, and nothing else, is confirmed by scripture, which describes mindfulness of the body as "repeatedly observing" it — a function only wisdom, not mindfulness as such, is capable of performing.

Chinese Source

如是已說入修二門，由此二門心便得定。心得定已復何所修？頌曰：依已修成止，為觀修念住，以自相共相，觀身受心法。自性聞等慧，餘相雜所緣，說次第隨生，治倒故唯四。論曰：依已修成滿勝奢摩他，為毘鉢舍那修四念住。如何修習四念住耶？謂以自共相觀身、受、心、法。身受心法各別自性名為自相。一切有為皆非常性、一切有漏皆是苦性及一切法空非我性名為共相。身自性者，大種造色。受心自性，如自名顯。法自性者，除三餘法。傳說在定以極微剎那各別觀身，名身念住滿，餘三滿相如應當知。何等名為四念住體？此四念住體各有三，自性、相雜、所緣別故。自性念住以慧為體，此慧有三種，謂聞等所成，即此亦名三種念住。相雜念住以慧所餘俱有為體。所緣念住以慧所緣諸法為體。寧知自性是慧非餘？經說：於身住循身觀名身念住。餘三亦然。諸循觀名唯目慧體，非慧無有循觀用故。

Ru-Yi Translation

We have now explained the two gates of entering cultivation, through which the mind attains concentration. Once the mind has attained concentration, what is next to be cultivated? Verse: relying on the calm-abiding already accomplished through cultivation, one cultivates the foundations of mindfulness for the sake of insight, observing body, feeling, mind, and dharmas by way of both their own specific and their shared characteristics. Their essential nature is the wisdom born of hearing and the rest; the remaining aspects are mixed-with-associates and object-based. They are said to arise in this sequence; and, because they counter the inversions, there are exactly four. Explanation: relying on the fully accomplished, excellent calm-abiding already cultivated, one cultivates the four foundations of mindfulness for the sake of insight. How does one cultivate them? By observing body, feeling, mind, and dharmas through both their own specific and their shared characteristics. The distinct essential nature of each individually is called its specific characteristic. That all conditioned things are impermanent, that all contaminated things are suffering, and that all dharmas are empty and without self are called the shared characteristics. The essential nature of the body is the derived matter composed of the four great elements. The essential nature of feeling and of mind are as their own names indicate. The essential nature of dharmas is whatever dharmas remain, excluding the preceding three. It is said that, while in concentration, observing the body distinctly down to its ultimate particles and momentary instants constitutes the completion of body-mindfulness; the completion of the remaining three should be understood correspondingly. What constitutes the substance of the four foundations of mindfulness? Each has three distinct aspects: its own essential nature, its mixed-with-associates aspect, and its object-based aspect. The own-nature foundation has wisdom as its substance, this wisdom being of three kinds — born of hearing and the others — accordingly also called the three kinds of mindfulness-foundation. The mixed-with-associates foundation has as its substance whatever else co-arises with that wisdom. The object-based foundation has as its substance whatever dharmas that wisdom takes as its object. How is it known that the own-nature aspect is specifically wisdom, and not something else? Because a sūtra states: abiding in the body, repeatedly observing the body, is called body-mindfulness-foundation — and the same holds for the remaining three. The term "repeatedly observing" refers specifically to the substance of wisdom, since nothing other than wisdom has the function of repeated observation.

Why, then, is the name "mindfulness-foundation" given to what is essentially wisdom? The Vaibhāṣika masters explain that mindfulness is especially prominent in this category, sustaining wisdom's function much as a wedge, by its steady force, allows an axe to split wood. The treatise refines this: properly speaking, it is wisdom that causes mindfulness to remain firmly established on its object — which is precisely why the name applies, since mindfulness stays clearly fixed on whatever wisdom observes. The four foundations arise in a specific, motivated sequence, always beginning with the body, since coarser objects are naturally observed first and since desire and craving operate specifically with respect to the body. Craving for the body arises because one delights in the feeling it provides; delighting in feeling arises because the mind is not yet tamed; and the mind's untamed state arises because affliction has not yet been severed — hence feeling, mind, and dharmas follow the body in exactly this order. These four foundations respectively counteract the four inversions — mistaking the impure for pure, suffering for pleasurable, the impermanent for permanent, and non-self for self — and it is for this reason, tracking these four inversions precisely, that there are exactly four foundations, neither more nor fewer.

Chinese Source

何緣於慧立念住名？毘婆沙師說：此品念增故。是念力持慧得轉義，如斧破木由楔力持。理實應言慧令念住，是故於慧立念住名，隨慧所觀能明記故，由此無滅作如是言：若有能於身住循身觀，緣身念得住，乃至廣說。世尊亦說：若有於身住循身觀者，念便住不謬。然有經言：此四念住由何故集？由何故滅？食觸名色作意集故，如次令身受心法集。食觸名色作意滅故，如次令身受心法滅。應知彼說所緣念住，以念於彼得安住故。又念住別名隨所緣緣自他俱相續異故。一一念住各有三種，此四念住說次隨生。生復何緣？次第如是。隨境麁者應先觀故，或諸欲貪於身處轉，故四念住觀身在初。然貪於身由欣樂受，欣樂於受由心不調，心之不調由惑未斷，故觀受等如是次第。此四念住如次治彼淨、樂、常、我四種顛倒，故唯有四不增不減。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is the name mindfulness-foundation applied to wisdom? The Vaibhāṣika masters explain: because, in this category, mindfulness is especially prominent — wisdom is able to function precisely because it is sustained by the power of mindfulness, just as an axe splits wood because it is held steady by the force of a wedge. In truth, it should rather be said that wisdom is what causes mindfulness to remain established, and it is for this reason that the name mindfulness-foundation is applied to wisdom: because, in dependence on whatever wisdom observes, mindfulness remains clearly fixed upon it. On this basis, the Venerable Aniruddha states: whoever is able to abide in the body, repeatedly observing the body, has mindfulness of the body thereby established, and so on, at length. The Blessed One likewise states: whoever abides in the body, repeatedly observing the body, has mindfulness thereby established without error. There is, however, a further sūtra passage stating: by what are these four foundations of mindfulness assembled, and by what are they dissolved? By the assembling of food, contact, name-and-form, and mental application, respectively, body, feeling, mind, and dharmas are assembled; by their dissolving, respectively, body, feeling, mind, and dharmas are dissolved. It should be understood that this passage speaks of the object-based foundation of mindfulness, since it is with reference to that object that mindfulness comes to be established. The foundations of mindfulness are also distinguished by name according to whether their object is one's own continuum, another's, or both together. Each individual foundation thus has three kinds. These four foundations are said to arise in this specific sequence. Why does this sequence hold? Whichever object is coarser should logically be observed first, and since desire and craving operate specifically with respect to the body, its contemplation is placed first. Craving for the body arises because one delights in feeling; delighting in feeling arises because the mind is not yet tamed; and the mind's untamed state arises because affliction has not yet been severed — hence the sequence through feeling, mind, and dharmas. These four foundations respectively counteract the four inversions of purity, pleasure, permanence, and self, and it is for this reason that there are exactly four, neither more nor fewer.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that this section's naming discussion directly parallels the treatise's earlier point about mindfulness of breathing (fascicle 22): in both cases, a practice named "mindfulness" turns out to have wisdom, not mindfulness itself, as its actual essential nature — mindfulness being instead the sustaining force that keeps the object clearly and steadily in view. We have kept this recurring naming pattern visible, since the treatise itself explicitly draws the comparison ("as with the foundations of mindfulness" was used to explain breath-mindfulness's own naming) and revisits it here in fuller detail.

We should also note the elegant, tightly reasoned causal chain the treatise offers for why body is contemplated first, then feeling, then mind, then dharmas: each step names a specific psychological dependency (craving for the body depends on delighting in feeling; delighting in feeling depends on an untamed mind; an untamed mind depends on unsevered affliction) rather than simply asserting an arbitrary order — and the four foundations are explicitly correlated, one-to-one, with the four specific inversions (purity/pleasure/permanence/self) already discussed in fascicle 19, which is offered as the reason there are exactly four foundations and not some other number.

2. From Unmixed to Mixed: The Combined Dharma-Mindfulness Stage

The treatise now distinguishes how the four foundations of mindfulness take their objects. Among the four, three — body, feeling, and mind — take only an unmixed object, while the fourth, dharmas, can take either a mixed or an unmixed object. Observing dharmas alone is "unmixed"; observing two, three, or all four of body, feeling, mind, and dharmas together is "mixed." Once a practitioner has matured this mixed practice — observing body and the rest together within the dharma-foundation of mindfulness — what comes next? The practitioner now abides within a fully combined dharma-mindfulness-foundation, observing all four objects — body, feeling, mind, and dharmas — together as a single field, and cultivating with respect to that combined field four specific aspects: impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self.

Chinese Source

四中三種唯不雜緣，第四所緣通雜不雜。若唯觀法名不雜緣，若於身等二三或四總而觀察名為雜緣。如是熟修雜緣身等法念住已，復何所修？頌曰：彼居法念住，總觀四所緣，修非常及苦，空非我行相。論曰：彼觀行者居緣總雜法念住中，總觀所緣身等四境修四行相，所謂非常、苦、空、非我。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these four, three take only unmixed objects, while the fourth can take either a mixed or an unmixed object. Observing dharmas alone is called taking an unmixed object; observing two, three, or all four of body and the rest together is called taking a mixed object. Having thus matured the practice of the dharma-foundation of mindfulness with a mixed object encompassing body and the rest, what does one cultivate next? Verse: abiding in that dharma-mindfulness-foundation, one observes all four objects together, cultivating the aspects of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self. Explanation: that practitioner, abiding within the dharma-foundation of mindfulness that takes a totally mixed object, observes all four objects — body and the rest — together, cultivating four aspects: impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the clean architectural logic of this progression: the practitioner moves from observing each of the four objects separately (body alone, then feeling alone, then mind alone, then dharmas alone — each with its own specific characteristic), through progressively combining pairs and larger groupings of objects within the dharma-foundation specifically, and finally to a single, fully unified field of observation covering all four objects at once. This is the immediate structural preparation for the four wholesome roots the treatise introduces next, which build directly on this fully combined observation rather than on any of the earlier, narrower stages.

We should also note that only the dharma-foundation of mindfulness is capable of this expansion into mixed objects, while body, feeling, and mind each remain confined to their own single, unmixed object throughout — a structural asymmetry the text states without further explanation, though it fits naturally with dharmas being defined, earlier in this fascicle, as "whatever remains" once body, feeling, and mind are set aside, making it the natural category flexible enough to eventually reabsorb and unify all three of the others.

3. Warmth and Summit: The First Two Wholesome Roots, and the Sixteen Aspects

As the combined dharma-mindfulness-foundation gradually matures to its highest grade, it gives rise to the first wholesome root conducive to reaching the decisive distinction of the path of seeing — called "warmth" (ūṣmagata), named by analogy with actual warmth, since it is the preliminary sign of the fire of the noble path, the fire capable of burning up the firewood of affliction, just as warmth precedes an actual flame. Because this stage spans a comparatively long period, it is the first point at which a practitioner is capable of fully observing all four noble truths, and of fully cultivating all sixteen aspects that structure that observation — four aspects for each truth. In observing suffering, one cultivates impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self; in observing the origin, cause, origin, arising, and condition; in observing cessation, cessation, peace, excellence, and freedom; and in observing the path, path, suchness, practice, and deliverance. The precise meaning of each of these sixteen aspects is reserved for later discussion.

Chinese Source

修此觀已生何善根？頌曰：從此生煖法，具觀四聖諦，修十六行相，次生頂亦然，如是二善根，皆初法後四。論曰：修習總緣共相法念住，漸次成熟乃至上上品。從此念住後，有順決擇分初善根生，名為煖法。此法如煖立煖法名，是能燒惑薪聖道火前相，如火前相故名為煖。此煖善根分位長故，能具觀察四聖諦境，及能具修十六行相。觀苦聖諦修四行相：一非常、二苦、三空、四非我。觀集聖諦修四行相：一因、二集、三生、四緣。觀滅聖諦修四行相：一滅、二靜、三妙、四離。觀道聖諦修四行相：一道、二如、三行、四出。此相差別如後當辯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having cultivated this observation, what wholesome root does it give rise to? Verse: from this arises the dharma of warmth, at which stage one fully observes the four noble truths, cultivating the sixteen aspects; next arises the dharma of summit, likewise; for both of these two wholesome roots, mindfulness begins with the dharma foundation alone, and then encompasses all four afterward. Explanation: as one cultivates the totally-combined, shared-characteristic dharma-mindfulness-foundation, it gradually matures up to its highest-of-the-highest grade. Following this mindfulness-foundation, the first wholesome root conducive to reaching the decisive distinction arises — this is called the dharma of warmth. This dharma is named warmth by analogy with actual warmth, since it is the preliminary sign of the fire of the noble path, the fire capable of burning up the firewood of affliction, and, being like the warmth that precedes an actual fire, it is called warmth. Because this wholesome root of warmth spans a comparatively long stage, it is capable of fully observing the object of all four noble truths, and of fully cultivating all sixteen aspects. In observing the noble truth of suffering, one cultivates four aspects: impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self. In observing the noble truth of the origin, one cultivates four aspects: cause, origin, arising, and condition. In observing the noble truth of cessation, one cultivates four aspects: cessation, peace, excellence, and freedom. In observing the noble truth of the path, one cultivates four aspects: path, suchness, practice, and deliverance. The specific distinctions among these aspects will be discussed later.

As the wholesome root of warmth gradually increases through its lower, middle, and upper grades and reaches completion, a further wholesome root arises, called the dharma of "summit" (mūrdhan) — marking a further advance in excellence and so given its own distinct name. Among the "movable" wholesome roots (those still capable of being lost through retrogression), this dharma is the most excellent, comparable to the crown of a person's head; or, alternatively, it is named "summit" because this stage is the very turning point between advancing further and falling back, like the peak of a mountain, from which one may go either up or down. Summit likewise fully observes the four truths and fully cultivates the sixteen aspects, just as warmth does. For both warmth and summit, at the very moment of their initial attainment, only the dharma-foundation of mindfulness is present — the moment a given wholesome root, through the sixteen aspects, first treads upon the path of the four noble truths; only as it subsequently advances further do all four foundations of mindfulness become present again, since at that first moment one has not yet generated a comparable sense of regard toward the three foundations (body, feeling, mind) individually, compared to the newly unified dharma-foundation.

Chinese Source

此煖善根下中上品漸次增長至成滿時有善根生，名為頂法，此轉勝故更立異名。動善根中此法最勝，如人頂故，名為頂法。或由此是進退兩際，如山頂故，說名為頂。此亦如煖具觀四諦，及能具修十六行相。如是煖頂二種善根，初安足時唯法念住。以何義故名初安足？謂隨何善根以十六行相最初遊踐四聖諦迹，後增進時具四念住。諸先所得後不現前，於彼不生欽重心故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As this wholesome root of warmth gradually increases through its lower, middle, and upper grades, and reaches completion, a further wholesome root arises, called the dharma of summit — since this represents a further advance in excellence, it is given a distinct new name. Among the movable wholesome roots, this dharma is the most excellent, comparable to the crown of a person's head, hence it is called the dharma of summit. Or, alternatively, because this stage is the very turning point between advancing further and falling back, like the peak of a mountain, it is called summit. This likewise, just as with warmth, fully observes the four truths and is capable of fully cultivating the sixteen aspects. For both of these two wholesome roots, warmth and summit, at the moment of their initial setting foot, only the dharma-foundation of mindfulness is present. What is meant by initial setting foot? It refers to the very first moment at which a given wholesome root, through the sixteen aspects, first treads upon the path of the four noble truths; as it subsequently advances further, all four foundations of mindfulness become present. Whatever was previously attained does not yet manifest again at that first moment, since one has not yet generated a sense of high regard toward those foundations individually.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the sixteen aspects introduced here as a structurally central framework the treatise itself says will be explained more fully "later" — we have translated the sixteen terms (four per truth) as given, without expanding on their individual meanings beyond what this passage itself states, since the treatise explicitly defers that fuller explanation to a subsequent point we have not yet reached in this project's fascicle-by-fascicle progress. Readers should expect a more detailed gloss on terms like "suchness" or "deliverance" once the treatise itself provides one.

We should also note the vivid double meaning offered for why the second wholesome root is named "summit" — most excellent among a still-retrogressible class of practice (like a person's literal crown, their highest point), and simultaneously the precise turning point between further advance and falling back (like a mountain peak, from which the path runs in either direction) — the treatise presents these as complementary explanations for the same name rather than competing accounts, and we have kept both together as offered.
