What Abhidharma Really Means

This page carries the opening of fascicle 1: Vasubandhu's homage verse, and his definition of Abhidharma itself — first in the ultimate sense, then in the conventional sense that lets a written treatise like this one borrow the name. This is the beginning of a thirty-fascicle translation; the rest of fascicle 1 (the etymology of "kośa," the enumeration of the seventy-five dharmas, and the opening of the analysis of the eighteen elements) will be added in the sections that follow.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: opening section only Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 1 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharmakośa, preserved in the Taishō canon (T1558, vol. 29) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. Leo Pruden's English translation of Louis de La Vallée Poussin's French Abhidharmakośabhāṣyam, and Chinese commentaries such as Yen-pei's (演培) 俱舍論頌講記, are consulted privately to check difficult passages, but neither is reproduced or paraphrased here; the English wording is Ru-Yi's own.

Unlike Ru-Yi's translations of the Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra and the Cheng Weishi Lun, this treatise does not yet follow a single named teacher's lecture transcript for its reading notes. The notes below are Ru-Yi's own, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma scholarship rather than a specific oral commentary — marked plainly as such below, so readers know what they are getting. If a suitable public-domain or licensed commentary source becomes available later, later fascicles may adopt it the way the other treatises have.

Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right.

1. Homage to the Teacher

Like most Indian śāstras, the treatise opens with a verse of homage before its argument begins.

Chinese Source

諸一切種諸冥滅，拔眾生出生死泥，敬禮如是如理師，對法藏論我當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

He in whom every kind of darkness, of every sort, is extinguished; who draws sentient beings out of the mire of birth and death — to such a teacher, one whose teaching accords with reality, I pay homage. I shall now set forth the treatise that is the Treasury of Abhidharma.

論曰：今欲造論，為顯自師其體尊高超諸聖眾，故先讚德方申敬禮。諸言所表謂佛世尊，此能破闇故稱冥滅。

Prose: Now, wishing to compose this treatise, the author first praises his teacher's virtues before paying homage, in order to show that this teacher's very being is exalted, surpassing all the noble ones. What the verse's words point to is the Buddha, the World-Honored One. Because he is able to destroy darkness, he is called "the extinguisher of darkness."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Darkness" here is the classical gloss for avidyā, ignorance — the root affliction that the whole treatise's analysis of dharmas exists to dispel. Note what the verse actually praises: not power or majesty, but the fact that the Buddha's teaching "accords with reality" (如理). That is the treatise's basic claim in miniature — correct classification of what is real is itself what frees a being from the mire of birth and death, not a separate technique layered on top of it.

2. Abhidharma in the Ultimate Sense

Chapter 1 opens (分別界品第一) with the treatise's central question: what does the word "Abhidharma" actually mean? The answer comes in two stages — first the ultimate sense, then, in the next section, the conventional sense that allows an ordinary book to carry the name.

Chinese Source

淨慧隨行名對法，及能得此諸慧論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pure wisdom, together with what accompanies it, is called Abhidharma — as is the treatise able to bring about this wisdom.

論曰：慧謂擇法，淨謂無漏，淨慧眷屬名曰隨行。如是總說無漏五蘊名為對法，此則勝義阿毘達磨。

Prose: "Wisdom" means the discernment of dharmas. "Pure" means uncontaminated. Pure wisdom's retinue is called "what accompanies it." Thus, stated in general, the uncontaminated five aggregates are named Abhidharma — this is Abhidharma in the ultimate sense.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a striking claim to open on: in the strict sense, Abhidharma is not a book at all. It is a mental event — the direct, uncontaminated (anāsrava) insight of a noble one (ārya) into the true nature of dharmas, together with whatever mental factors necessarily arise alongside any single moment of consciousness (its "retinue," 隨行 — a term the treatise will return to in far more technical detail once it takes up mind and mental factors in later chapters). "The uncontaminated five aggregates" does not mean the ordinary aggregates of form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness that make up any experience; it means those same five categories as they occur within one particular kind of experience — a moment of liberating, undefiled seeing.

3. Abhidharma in the Conventional Sense

Having defined Abhidharma as a state of mind, the treatise now explains how the word can also apply to ordinary, contaminated wisdom, and to a written treatise like this one.

Chinese Source

若說世俗阿毘達磨，即能得此諸慧及論。慧謂得此有漏修慧、思聞生得慧及隨行。論謂傳生無漏慧教。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one speaks of conventional Abhidharma, it is the wisdom and the treatise able to bring about this pure wisdom. "Wisdom" here means the contaminated wisdom of cultivation that gives rise to it, together with the wisdom born of reflection and of learning, and what accompanies them. "Treatise" means the teaching that transmits and gives rise to uncontaminated wisdom.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This draws on the classical triad of wisdom the treatise assumes readers already know: wisdom born of learning (聞, hearing the teaching), wisdom born of reflection (思, thinking it through), and wisdom born of cultivation (修, meditative practice) — all three still contaminated (有漏), still short of the noble one's direct insight. "Conventional Abhidharma" covers both this preparatory wisdom and the verbal teachings — including this very treatise — that transmit it, because both function as provisions on the way to the ultimate, uncontaminated wisdom defined in the previous section. This is how a physical book can honestly be called "Abhidharma," even though Abhidharma, strictly speaking, is a state of mind rather than a text.

4. Why It Is Called "Abhidharma"

The treatise closes this opening movement by explaining the name itself, character by character.

Chinese Source

此諸慧論是彼資糧，故亦得名阿毘達磨。釋此名者，能持自相故名為法。若勝義法唯是涅槃，若法相法通四聖諦，此能對向或能對觀，故稱對法。

Ru-Yi Translation

These forms of wisdom and this treatise are provisions for that ultimate wisdom, and so they too may be called Abhidharma. To explain this name: because it holds its own particular mark, it is called "dharma." If "dharma" is meant in the ultimate sense, this refers only to nirvāṇa; if it is meant as dharma bearing a characteristic, it extends to the Four Noble Truths. Because this wisdom is able to face toward its object, or able to contemplate it directly, it is called Abhidharma — "facing the dharma."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Dharma" is defined here exactly as the treatise will use it throughout its remaining twenty-nine fascicles: whatever "holds its own particular mark" — whatever has a definite, distinguishable nature of its own, whether a physical element, a mental factor, or an unconditioned reality like space or cessation. The etymology then does real philosophical work: dharma in the strict, ultimate sense names nirvāṇa alone, since it is the one reality that needs no further characteristic to secure it; taken more broadly, as "dharma bearing a characteristic," it covers everything analyzed under the Four Noble Truths — suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path. Either way, this wisdom is called "Abhidharma" — facing toward the dharma — because it takes one or the other as its direct object of contemplation. This is the charter for the entire treatise: to enumerate, one by one, every dharma that bears its own mark.

Fascicle 1: More to Come

This opening movement — the homage, and the definition of Abhidharma in both the ultimate and the conventional sense — is complete. The Chinese text continues from here into the etymology of the treatise's own title, kośa ("treasury," so named for its relationship to the Mahāvibhāṣā), then into the classification of all dharmas as either contaminated or uncontaminated, and finally into the opening of Chapter 1 proper: the five aggregates, twelve sense fields, and eighteen elements.
