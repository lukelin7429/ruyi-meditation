What Abhidharma Really Means

This page carries fascicle 1's complete opening frame, and the opening of Chapter 1 itself: the aggregate of form's eleven kinds, the five sense faculties as "pure form," visible form's twenty kinds of colour and shape, and sound's eight kinds. This is the beginning of a thirty-fascicle translation; the enumerations of taste, smell, and touch, unmanifest form, and the remaining aggregates will be added in the sections that follow.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: opening section only Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 1 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharmakośa, preserved in the Taishō canon (T1558, vol. 29) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. Leo Pruden's English translation of Louis de La Vallée Poussin's French Abhidharmakośabhāṣyam, and Chinese commentaries such as Yen-pei's (演培) 俱舍論頌講記, are consulted privately to check difficult passages, but neither is reproduced or paraphrased here; the English wording is Ru-Yi's own.

Unlike Ru-Yi's translations of the Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra and the Cheng Weishi Lun, this treatise does not yet follow a single named teacher's lecture transcript for its reading notes. The notes below are Ru-Yi's own, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma scholarship rather than a specific oral commentary — marked plainly as such below, so readers know what they are getting. If a suitable public-domain or licensed commentary source becomes available later, later fascicles may adopt it the way the other treatises have.

Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right.

1. Homage to the Teacher

Like most Indian śāstras, the treatise opens with a verse of homage before its argument begins.

Chinese Source

諸一切種諸冥滅，拔眾生出生死泥，敬禮如是如理師，對法藏論我當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

He in whom every kind of darkness, of every sort, is extinguished; who draws sentient beings out of the mire of birth and death — to such a teacher, one whose teaching accords with reality, I pay homage. I shall now set forth the treatise that is the Treasury of Abhidharma.

論曰：今欲造論，為顯自師其體尊高超諸聖眾，故先讚德方申敬禮。諸言所表謂佛世尊，此能破闇故稱冥滅。

Prose: Now, wishing to compose this treatise, the author first praises his teacher's virtues before paying homage, in order to show that this teacher's very being is exalted, surpassing all the noble ones. What the verse's words point to is the Buddha, the World-Honored One. Because he is able to destroy darkness, he is called "the extinguisher of darkness."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Darkness" here is the classical gloss for avidyā, ignorance — the root affliction that the whole treatise's analysis of dharmas exists to dispel. Note what the verse actually praises: not power or majesty, but the fact that the Buddha's teaching "accords with reality" (如理). That is the treatise's basic claim in miniature — correct classification of what is real is itself what frees a being from the mire of birth and death, not a separate technique layered on top of it.

2. Abhidharma in the Ultimate Sense

Chapter 1 opens (分別界品第一) with the treatise's central question: what does the word "Abhidharma" actually mean? The answer comes in two stages — first the ultimate sense, then, in the next section, the conventional sense that allows an ordinary book to carry the name.

Chinese Source

淨慧隨行名對法，及能得此諸慧論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pure wisdom, together with what accompanies it, is called Abhidharma — as is the treatise able to bring about this wisdom.

論曰：慧謂擇法，淨謂無漏，淨慧眷屬名曰隨行。如是總說無漏五蘊名為對法，此則勝義阿毘達磨。

Prose: "Wisdom" means the discernment of dharmas. "Pure" means uncontaminated. Pure wisdom's retinue is called "what accompanies it." Thus, stated in general, the uncontaminated five aggregates are named Abhidharma — this is Abhidharma in the ultimate sense.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a striking claim to open on: in the strict sense, Abhidharma is not a book at all. It is a mental event — the direct, uncontaminated (anāsrava) insight of a noble one (ārya) into the true nature of dharmas, together with whatever mental factors necessarily arise alongside any single moment of consciousness (its "retinue," 隨行 — a term the treatise will return to in far more technical detail once it takes up mind and mental factors in later chapters). "The uncontaminated five aggregates" does not mean the ordinary aggregates of form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness that make up any experience; it means those same five categories as they occur within one particular kind of experience — a moment of liberating, undefiled seeing.

3. Abhidharma in the Conventional Sense

Having defined Abhidharma as a state of mind, the treatise now explains how the word can also apply to ordinary, contaminated wisdom, and to a written treatise like this one.

Chinese Source

若說世俗阿毘達磨，即能得此諸慧及論。慧謂得此有漏修慧、思聞生得慧及隨行。論謂傳生無漏慧教。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one speaks of conventional Abhidharma, it is the wisdom and the treatise able to bring about this pure wisdom. "Wisdom" here means the contaminated wisdom of cultivation that gives rise to it, together with the wisdom born of reflection and of learning, and what accompanies them. "Treatise" means the teaching that transmits and gives rise to uncontaminated wisdom.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This draws on the classical triad of wisdom the treatise assumes readers already know: wisdom born of learning (聞, hearing the teaching), wisdom born of reflection (思, thinking it through), and wisdom born of cultivation (修, meditative practice) — all three still contaminated (有漏), still short of the noble one's direct insight. "Conventional Abhidharma" covers both this preparatory wisdom and the verbal teachings — including this very treatise — that transmit it, because both function as provisions on the way to the ultimate, uncontaminated wisdom defined in the previous section. This is how a physical book can honestly be called "Abhidharma," even though Abhidharma, strictly speaking, is a state of mind rather than a text.

4. Why It Is Called "Abhidharma"

The treatise closes this opening movement by explaining the name itself, character by character.

Chinese Source

此諸慧論是彼資糧，故亦得名阿毘達磨。釋此名者，能持自相故名為法。若勝義法唯是涅槃，若法相法通四聖諦，此能對向或能對觀，故稱對法。

Ru-Yi Translation

These forms of wisdom and this treatise are provisions for that ultimate wisdom, and so they too may be called Abhidharma. To explain this name: because it holds its own particular mark, it is called "dharma." If "dharma" is meant in the ultimate sense, this refers only to nirvāṇa; if it is meant as dharma bearing a characteristic, it extends to the Four Noble Truths. Because this wisdom is able to face toward its object, or able to contemplate it directly, it is called Abhidharma — "facing the dharma."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Dharma" is defined here exactly as the treatise will use it throughout its remaining twenty-nine fascicles: whatever "holds its own particular mark" — whatever has a definite, distinguishable nature of its own, whether a physical element, a mental factor, or an unconditioned reality like space or cessation. The etymology then does real philosophical work: dharma in the strict, ultimate sense names nirvāṇa alone, since it is the one reality that needs no further characteristic to secure it; taken more broadly, as "dharma bearing a characteristic," it covers everything analyzed under the Four Noble Truths — suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path. Either way, this wisdom is called "Abhidharma" — facing toward the dharma — because it takes one or the other as its direct object of contemplation. This is the charter for the entire treatise: to enumerate, one by one, every dharma that bears its own mark.

5. Why the Treatise Is Called "Kośa" (Treasury)

Abhidharma itself has now been explained. The treatise turns next to its own title.

Chinese Source

已釋對法。何故此論名對法藏？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Abhidharma has now been explained. Why is this treatise named the Treasury of Abhidharma? A verse states:

攝彼勝義依彼故，此立對法俱舍名。

Because it gathers in that work's essential meaning, and depends upon it, this treatise is established under the name Abhidharmakośa.

論曰：由彼對法論中勝義入此攝故，此得藏名。或此依彼、從彼引生，是彼所藏，故亦名藏。是故此論名對法藏。

Prose: Because the essential meaning within that Abhidharma treatise is gathered into and contained by this one, this work receives the name "treasury." Alternatively, because this work depends upon that one, is drawn out from it, and is that work's stored essence, it is likewise called a "treasury." For this reason, this treatise is named the Treasury of Abhidharma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"That Abhidharma treatise" is the Mahāvibhāṣā (大毘婆沙論) — the two-hundred-fascicle Sarvāstivāda compendium already flagged as a future addition to this section of the site. The etymology is deliberately given two ways, and both are kept rather than resolved into one: kośa ("sheath" or "treasury") names this shorter treatise either as the container that holds the Mahāvibhāṣā's essential meaning in condensed form, or as the thing itself contained within — drawn out from, and dependent on, that larger work. Either reading makes the same practical point: this treatise is not an independent invention but a distillation of the older, vaster compendium, meant to make its essential meaning usable.

6. Why Did the Buddha Teach Abhidharma At All?

Having named itself, the treatise raises a harder question: on what grounds can this whole technical enterprise be attributed to the Buddha in the first place?

Chinese Source

何因說彼阿毘達磨？誰復先說阿毘達磨，而今造論恭敬解釋？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

For what reason was Abhidharma taught? And who first taught Abhidharma, such that this treatise is now composed to explain it with reverence? A verse states:

若離擇法定無餘，能滅諸惑勝方便，由惑世間漂有海，因此傳佛說對法。

Apart from the discernment of dharmas, there is certainly no other supreme means able to extinguish the afflictions. Because the afflictions cause the world to drift in the ocean of existence, it has been transmitted, for this reason, that the Buddha taught Abhidharma.

論曰：若離擇法，無勝方便能滅諸惑。諸惑能令世間漂轉生死大海，因此傳佛說彼對法，欲令世間得擇法故。離說對法，弟子不能於諸法相如理簡擇。

Prose: Apart from the discernment of dharmas, there is no supreme means able to extinguish the afflictions. The afflictions cause the world to drift and turn in the great ocean of birth-and-death; for this reason it has been transmitted that the Buddha taught Abhidharma, wishing to let the world attain the discernment of dharmas. Without the teaching of Abhidharma, disciples would be unable to rightly discern the characteristics of dharmas in accordance with reality.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the treatise's answer to a question any scholastic tradition has to face: why treat these technical enumerations as the Buddha's own word, rather than a later invention? The argument here is not historical but practical — nothing except the discernment of dharmas (擇法, prajñā operating precisely on what is real) can uproot the afflictions that keep beings adrift in the "ocean of existence" (有海, bhavasāgara). Since that discerning function is indispensable, and since the Buddha's purpose was to make it available to disciples who could not reach it unaided, the teaching that serves that function is attributed to him — whatever its later editorial history turns out to have been. This is the premise the next section builds directly on.

7. How Abhidharma Was Compiled

The treatise now gives its own account of Abhidharma's textual history — scattered teaching, later gathered into system.

Chinese Source

然佛世尊處處散說阿毘達磨，大德迦多衍尼子等諸大聲聞結集安置。猶如大德法救所集無常品等鄔拕南頌，毘婆沙師傳說如此。

Ru-Yi Translation

However, the Buddha, the World-Honored One, taught Abhidharma scattered here and there across many occasions; it was the great elder Kātyāyanīputra and other great śrāvakas who gathered it together and arranged it into order. This is comparable to how the great elder Dharmatrāta compiled the verse-summaries (udāna) on impermanence and other themes — so the Vaibhāṣika masters have transmitted and explained it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two figures anchor this account. Kātyāyanīputra (迦多衍尼子) is the traditional compiler of the Jñānaprasthāna (發智論), the root treatise that the Mahāvibhāṣā was later composed to comment on at enormous length — so this single sentence quietly explains the whole chain of texts standing behind the Kośa itself: scattered teaching, into the Jñānaprasthāna, into the Mahāvibhāṣā, into this treatise's condensation of it. Dharmatrāta (法救), compiler of verse-anthologies on impermanence and similar themes (material related to what survives as the Udānavarga), is offered as a parallel case the audience would already accept: an named disciple organizing the Buddha's own scattered sayings into a usable, systematic form is not a new or suspect practice — it is exactly how several respected parts of the canon came to exist.

8. What Makes a Dharma "Contaminated"

The frame is now complete. The treatise turns to its first real content: dividing every dharma that exists into two kinds. One long root verse governs this and the next three sections.

Chinese Source

何法名為彼所簡擇，因此傳佛說對法耶？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

What dharmas, then, are the ones discerned — on whose account it has been transmitted that the Buddha taught Abhidharma? A verse states:

有漏無漏法，除道餘有為，於彼漏隨增，故說名有漏。無漏謂道諦，及三種無為，謂虛空二滅，此中空無礙。擇滅謂離繫，隨繫事各別，畢竟礙當生，別得非擇滅。

Dharmas are contaminated or uncontaminated. All conditioned dharmas except the path are contaminated, because the contaminants increase within them. The uncontaminated are the truth of the path, and the three unconditioned — space and the two cessations. Of these, space is non-obstruction. Cessation through discernment is separation from bondage, differing according to each distinct bound-to affliction. What permanently obstructs a future arising is a separately attained cessation not through discernment.

論曰：說一切法略有二種，謂有漏、無漏。有漏法云何？謂除道諦，餘有為法。所以者何？諸漏於中等隨增故。緣滅道諦，諸漏雖生而不隨增，故非有漏。不隨增義，隨眠品中自當顯說。

Prose: All dharmas, it is said, are in brief of two kinds — contaminated and uncontaminated. What are contaminated dharmas? All conditioned dharmas except the truth of the path. Why is this? Because the contaminants increase equally within them. Although contaminants can arise by taking the truths of cessation and of the path as their object, they do not thereby increase there — so these are not contaminated. The meaning of "not increasing" will be explained in its own right, in the chapter on the Latent Afflictions.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Contaminant" (漏, āsrava) is the treatise's word for what keeps ordinary experience flowing out into further rebirth — the root defilements. The definition of "contaminated" here is given entirely by exclusion: every single conditioned dharma is contaminated except the truth of the path, which is itself conditioned (it is a set of cultivated factors, not the unconditioned goal) yet does not count as contaminated. The reason given is precise rather than sentimental: it is not that afflictions can never take the path or cessation as an object of thought — an unawakened person can certainly still cling to or misunderstand the idea of nirvāṇa — but that doing so does not cause the afflictions to increase, the way ordinary objects of craving or aversion do. That distinction between "arising toward" something and "increasing because of" it is a technical point the treatise sets aside for its later chapter on the latent afflictions (anuśaya), rather than resolving here.

9. The Three Unconditioned Dharmas

Having defined the contaminated, the treatise now defines the uncontaminated: the path, plus three dharmas that are never produced by causes and conditions at all.

Chinese Source

已辯有漏。無漏云何？謂道聖諦及三無為。何等為三？虛空、二滅。二滅者何？擇、非擇滅。此虛空等三種無為及道聖諦，名無漏法。所以者何？諸漏於中不隨增故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contaminated dharmas have now been distinguished. What are uncontaminated dharmas? The noble truth of the path, together with the three unconditioned. What are the three? Space, and the two cessations. What are the two cessations? Cessation through discernment, and cessation not through discernment. Space and these two cessations, together with the noble truth of the path — these are called uncontaminated dharmas. Why is this? Because the contaminants do not increase within them.

於略所說三無為中，虛空但以無礙為性，由無障故色於中行。

Among the three unconditioned briefly stated above, space has non-obstruction as its nature; because it offers no obstruction, matter moves freely within it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Unconditioned" (無為, asaṃskṛta) names whatever stands entirely outside the chain of arising and ceasing by causes and conditions — never produced, and so never destroyed. This is a much smaller category than "uncontaminated": the path is uncontaminated but still conditioned (it has to be cultivated), while space and the two cessations are both uncontaminated and unconditioned. Space is defined as simply as possible — it is not a substance in its own right, but the sheer absence of obstruction, which is exactly what lets physical matter occupy and pass through it. Sarvāstivāda treats this "nothing in the way" as a real, distinct dharma in its own right, not a mere verbal convenience — a point later Buddhist schools, including the Mahāyāna critics this treatise's own author would go on to side with, found worth arguing over.

10. Cessation Through Discernment (擇滅)

The treatise now defines the first of the two cessations: the liberation an ārya actually attains by directly discerning the Four Noble Truths.

Chinese Source

擇滅即以離繫為性，諸有漏法遠離繫縛證得解脫，名為擇滅。擇謂簡擇，即慧差別，各別簡擇四聖諦故。擇力所得滅名為擇滅，如牛所駕車名曰牛車，略去中言故作是說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Cessation through discernment has separation from bondage as its nature: when contaminated dharmas are separated from their bonds and liberation is realized, this is called cessation through discernment. "Discernment" means discriminating analysis — a particular kind of wisdom — because it discerns each of the Four Noble Truths individually. The cessation attained through the power of discernment is called "cessation through discernment," just as a cart drawn by an ox is called an "ox-cart": the middle term is simply dropped, and the name is formed this way.

一切有漏法同一擇滅耶？不爾。云何？隨繫事別，謂隨繫事量，離繫事亦爾。若不爾者，於證見苦所斷煩惱滅時，應證一切所斷諸煩惱滅。若如是者，修餘對治則為無用。

Is there one single cessation through discernment shared by all contaminated dharmas alike? No. Why not? Because it differs according to each distinct bound-to affliction — that is, the separation from bondage matches the bound-to matter, case by case. Were it not so, then at the very moment one realized the cessation of the afflictions cut off by seeing suffering, one would thereby realize the cessation of every affliction still to be cut off. If that were the case, cultivating the remaining antidotes for the other afflictions would serve no purpose at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ox-cart etymology is doing real work, not just wordplay: it flags that "cessation through discernment" is shorthand for "the cessation attained by the power of discernment" — the cessation itself is not an act of discerning, it is what discerning produces. The more important claim follows immediately: liberation is not one single, undifferentiated event. Sarvāstivāda holds that each category of affliction has its own distinct cessation, won only by directly discerning the particular truth that undoes it — which is exactly why the path is cultivated in stages, working through one class of affliction at a time, rather than dissolving in a single stroke of insight. (The treatise adds one further, highly compressed technical objection-and-reply here — on whether these many cessations share a common "same-kind cause" — that later chapters unpack in far more technical detail than fascicle 1 does; we have translated it as literally as the Chinese allows rather than over-interpreting it this early.)

11. Cessation Not Through Discernment (非擇滅)

The treatise closes this section with the second, very different kind of cessation — one attained without any act of insight at all.

Chinese Source

已說擇滅。永礙當生得非擇滅，謂能永礙未來法生。得滅異前，名非擇滅。得不因擇，但由闕緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Cessation through discernment has now been explained. The attainment that permanently obstructs a future dharma from arising is cessation not through discernment — that is, it is able to permanently obstruct the arising of a dharma in the future. This attainment of cessation differs from the one just described, and so is called "cessation not through discernment," because the attainment comes about not through discernment, but simply through the absence of a needed condition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Where cessation-through-discernment is something a practitioner actively achieves, cessation-not-through-discernment happens by default: a dharma is permanently barred from ever arising simply because the conditions it would need never come together — no wisdom required. The treatise gives its own illustration of exactly this in the next section.

12. An Example, and a Fourfold Analysis

The treatise now gives a concrete case of cessation not through discernment, then sorts all dharmas into four groups by which of the two cessations, if either, applies to them.

Chinese Source

如眼與意專一色時，餘色聲香味觸等謝。緣彼境界五識身等住未來世畢竟不生，由彼不能緣過去境，緣不具故得非擇滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

For example: when the eye and the mental faculty attend exclusively to one form, the other forms, sounds, smells, tastes, and tangibles [that could have been sensed in that same instant] have already passed. The bodies of the five consciousnesses that would take those objects as their objects, remaining in the future, are thereby permanently prevented from arising — because they cannot take a past object as a present object, and so, the condition being incomplete, cessation not through discernment is attained.

於法得滅應作四句。或於諸法唯得擇滅，謂諸有漏過現生法。或於諸法唯非擇滅，謂不生法無漏有為。或於諸法俱得二滅，謂彼不生諸有漏法。或於諸法不得二滅，謂諸無漏過現生法。

Regarding the attainment of cessation with respect to dharmas, a fourfold analysis should be made. For some dharmas, only cessation through discernment is attained — namely, contaminated dharmas of the past and present that have already arisen. For some dharmas, only cessation not through discernment is attained — namely, uncontaminated conditioned dharmas that have not arisen. For some dharmas, both cessations are attained together — namely, contaminated dharmas that will never arise. For some dharmas, neither cessation is attained — namely, uncontaminated dharmas of the past and present that have already arisen.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The example turns on a strict rule this treatise holds throughout: a sense-consciousness can only take a present object, never a past one. The instant attention settles on one form, every other possible sense-object of that same instant has already slipped into the past — so the consciousnesses that would have known them are foreclosed forever, not suppressed by anything, just missed by timing. The fourfold analysis that follows is worth checking against the logic itself: an already-arisen affliction can only be cut off by discernment (it already exists, so "never arising" doesn't apply to it); an uncontaminated dharma that never arises only gets cessation-not-through-discernment (there is no affliction there for wisdom to cut off); a contaminated dharma that is cut off before it ever gets the chance to arise qualifies for both at once; and an uncontaminated dharma that has already arisen — an actual moment of pure wisdom, say — needs neither, since it was never afflicted and it did in fact occur.

13. The Many Names of "Conditioned"

With the three unconditioned dharmas fully explained, the treatise turns back to the conditioned side of its opening division, and asks what "conditioned" actually means.

Chinese Source

如是已說三種無為。前說除道，餘有為法是名有漏。何謂有為？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

The three unconditioned have now been fully explained. It was said earlier that all conditioned dharmas except the path are named contaminated. What, then, is "conditioned"? A verse states:

又諸有為法，謂色等五蘊，亦世路言依，有離有事等。

Conditioned dharmas are the five aggregates, beginning with form. They are also called the road of time, the basis of speech, the separated, and the concerned thing, among other names.

論曰：色等五蘊，謂初色蘊乃至識蘊。如是五法具攝有為，眾緣聚集共所作故。無有少法一緣所生，是彼類故。未來無妨，如乳如薪。

Prose: "The five aggregates beginning with form" means the aggregate of form first, extending through to the aggregate of consciousness. These five dharmas together comprise the whole of the conditioned, because every one of them arises only as the joint product of many conditions gathering together — there is no dharma whatsoever produced from a single condition alone, and these five are of that same kind. This holds without exception even for dharmas not yet arisen, just as with milk or with firewood.

此有為法亦名世路，已行正行當行性故，或為無常所吞食故。

This conditioned dharma is also called "the road of time," because its nature is to have already moved, to be moving now, and to be about to move — or because it is devoured by impermanence.

或名言依，言謂語言，此所依者即名俱義。如是言依具攝一切有為諸法。若不爾者，應違《品類足論》所說。彼說言依十八界攝。

It is also called "the basis of speech": "speech" means spoken language, and whatever speech depends on to refer to something is exactly what this name indicates. In this way, "the basis of speech" comprises every conditioned dharma without exception — were it otherwise, this would contradict what is stated in the Prakaraṇapāda, which holds that "the basis of speech" is subsumed under the eighteen elements.

或名有離，離謂永離，即是涅槃，一切有為有彼離故。或名有事，以有因故，事是因義。毘婆沙師傳說如此。

It is also called "the separated," because "separation" means permanent separation — precisely nirvāṇa — and every conditioned dharma stands in relation to that separation. It is also called "the concerned thing," because it has a cause — here "thing" carries the sense of "cause." This is what the Vaibhāṣika masters have transmitted and explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two claims anchor this verse. First, the five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness — are said to exhaust the conditioned without remainder: nothing arisen from causes and conditions falls outside one of these five. Second, no dharma is ever produced by a single condition alone; "milk" and "firewood" gesture at the classical point that even the most ordinary transformations (milk into curd, wood into fire) require a whole convergence of conditions, never one cause acting by itself — the same principle the treatise will apply, far more technically, to every dharma it analyzes from here on.

The remaining synonyms each isolate one further fact about anything conditioned. "The road of time" names its exposure to the three times — having moved, moving, about to move — which the unconditioned, by definition, never does. "The basis of speech" claims that whatever ordinary language can name is, in the end, a conditioned dharma (the treatise even checks this claim against another Abhidharma text, the Prakaraṇapāda, rather than asserting it unsupported — one of several places in this fascicle where one treatise is checked against another). "The separated" points forward to nirvāṇa as the destiny every conditioned dharma stands in relation to, whether or not it is ever actually realized. "The concerned thing" is the plainest of the five: to be conditioned just is to have a cause. One short technical phrase in the "basis of speech" explanation — rendered here as "exactly what this name indicates" — compresses a grammatical point about how the compound term itself is formed; we have kept the translation close to the literal wording rather than resolving that compression with more confidence than the single clause supports.

14. Why the Contaminated Are Called "Aggregates of Grasping"

Just as the previous section named the conditioned by several epithets, the treatise now does the same for the contaminated specifically — starting with its most technical name, "aggregates of grasping" (取蘊, upādāna-skandha).

Chinese Source

如是等類是有為法差別眾名，於此所說有為法中。頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Such, and others of this kind, are the various names distinguishing conditioned dharmas. Among these conditioned dharmas just discussed, a verse states:

有漏名取蘊，亦說為有諍，及苦集世間，見處三有等。

The contaminated are named the aggregates of grasping. They are also said to be strife, suffering, origin, the world, the abode of views, and the three states of existence, among other names.

論曰：此何所立？謂立取蘊亦名為蘊。或有唯蘊而非取蘊，謂無漏行。煩惱名取，蘊從取生故名取蘊，如草穅火。或蘊屬取故名取蘊，如帝王臣。或蘊生取故名取蘊，如花果樹。

Prose: What does this establish? It establishes that the aggregates of grasping may also simply be called "aggregates" — but there are aggregates that are merely aggregates and not aggregates of grasping, namely the uncontaminated conditioned factors [the path]. "Grasping" names the afflictions. The aggregates are called "aggregates of grasping" because they arise from grasping, just as fire made from grass and husks is called "grass-and-husk fire." Or: because the aggregates belong to grasping, just as ministers belonging to a king are called "the king's ministers." Or: because the aggregates give rise to grasping, just as a tree bearing flowers and fruit is called "the flower-and-fruit tree."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three similes are not decorative — each names a genuinely different relationship, and the treatise is careful to give all three rather than settle on one. Grass-and-husk fire is named for what it arises from (a causal relation); a king's ministers are named for what they belong to (a relation of possession); a flower-and-fruit tree is named for what it produces (a relation of effect). Ordinary contaminated aggregates satisfy all three at once with respect to grasping — they arise from it, belong to it, and generate more of it — which is exactly why the treatise draws its sharp exception first: the path itself is a set of conditioned aggregates too, but since it neither arises from grasping, belongs to it, nor produces it, it is an aggregate that is never an "aggregate of grasping."

15. Six More Names for the Contaminated

The verse's remaining epithets are each explained in turn, one short reason apiece.

Chinese Source

此有漏法亦名有諍，煩惱名諍，觸動善品故、損害自他故、諍隨增故，名為有諍。

Ru-Yi Translation

These contaminated dharmas are also named "strife": the afflictions are named "strife" because they disturb and stir up wholesome qualities, because they harm both oneself and others, and because strife increases along with them — hence they are named "strife."

猶如有漏亦名為苦，違聖心故。亦名為集，能招苦故。亦名世間，可毀壞故、有對治故。亦名見處，見住其中隨增眠故。亦名三有，有因有依三有攝故。

Likewise, the contaminated are also named "suffering," because they run contrary to the mind of the noble ones. They are also named "origin," because they are able to bring about suffering. They are also named "the world," because they are subject to destruction, and because they admit of an antidote. They are also named "the abode of views," because views abide within them, and the latent tendencies increase there. They are also named "the three states of existence," because, having their cause and their basis there, they are comprised within the three states of existence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Read together, these six names amount to a portrait of contaminated existence from six angles rather than six separate facts. "Strife" and "the abode of views" locate the trouble inside the mind — the afflictions actively fight wholesome qualities and harm others, and wrong views cling to exactly these dharmas as their object. "Suffering" and "origin" are simply the first two of the Four Noble Truths, named here as properties of the same contaminated aggregates already under discussion, not as separate items. "The world" makes a point worth noticing: it is called destructible, yes, but the text adds "because it admits of an antidote" as an equal reason for the name — the very fact that contaminated existence can be countered is treated as part of what makes it "the world" in the first place. "The three states of existence" (三有, the realms of desire, form, and formlessness) is simply the total territory all of this covers: every contaminated dharma has its cause and its support somewhere within one of these three.

16. The Aggregate of Form: Its Eleven Kinds

The opening frame is complete. The treatise now begins the real work of Chapter 1: defining each of the five aggregates in turn, starting with form.

Chinese Source

如上所言，色等五蘊名有為法。色蘊者何？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

As stated above, the five aggregates beginning with form are named conditioned dharmas. What is the aggregate of form? A verse states:

色者唯五根，五境及無表。

Form is only the five faculties, the five objects, and the unmanifest.

論曰：言五根者，所謂眼、耳、鼻、舌、身根。言五境者，即是眼等五根境界，所謂色、聲、香、味、所觸。及無表者，謂無表色。唯依此量立色蘊名。此中先應說五根相。

Prose: "The five faculties" means the eye-faculty, ear-faculty, nose-faculty, tongue-faculty, and body-faculty. "The five objects" are precisely the objects of these five faculties — namely form, sound, smell, taste, and the tangible. "And the unmanifest" means unmanifest form. It is solely on the basis of these eleven that the name "aggregate of form" is established. Among these, the characteristics of the five faculties should be explained first.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Eleven items, one aggregate: the five physical sense faculties, the five kinds of object they each take, and one further category — "unmanifest form" (無表色, avijñapti-rūpa) — that is not perceived by any of the five senses at all. Unmanifest form is one of Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma's most distinctive and most argued-over categories: it names a subtle, continuing physical residue produced by an act of body or speech (most importantly, taking a precept), which persists and counts as real matter even though nothing about it can be seen, heard, or touched by anyone. The treatise flags here that it will explain this difficult category "later" rather than now — a promise fascicle 1 will need more sections to keep. First, as this line says, come the five ordinary faculties.

17. The Five Faculties Are "Pure Form"

The treatise gives one general definition covering all five sense faculties at once, before treating each individually.

Chinese Source

彼識依淨色，名眼等五根。

Ru-Yi Translation

The pure form on which those consciousnesses depend is named the eye-faculty and the other four faculties.

論曰：彼謂前說色等五境，識即色、聲、香、味、觸識。彼識所依五種淨色，如其次第，應知即是眼等五根。

Prose: "Those" refers to the five objects mentioned before — form and the rest. "Consciousness" means the consciousness of form, of sound, of smell, of taste, and of the tangible. The five kinds of pure form on which those consciousnesses depend — respectively, in the same order — should be understood as precisely the eye-faculty and the other four faculties.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Pure form" (淨色, later technical Sanskrit rūpaprasāda) names a refined, imperceptible physical substance distinct from the visible eyeball or audible ear — the eye-faculty proper is this subtle matter, not the organ an anatomist would point to. Readers of Ru-Yi's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra translation will recognize this exact term and the same basic account of the eye-faculty ("the eye is the pure form... on which the eye-consciousness depends") from that treatise's own opening fascicle. That is not a coincidence: Yogācāra inherited a great deal of its technical vocabulary for the physical senses directly from Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma like this treatise, even where the two schools disagree sharply on deeper questions of what ultimately exists. The method here is exactly parallel to how fascicle 1 has already handled several terms — name the general principle first (all five faculties are pure form, matched one-to-one with the five kinds of sense-consciousness), then take up each faculty's specific characteristics one at a time, starting with the eye.

18. Visible Form: Twenty Kinds of Colour and Shape

Rather than take up each faculty's own characteristics next, the treatise moves to the five objects, opening with a single dense verse that gives the exact count of subtypes for all five at once. This section unpacks only the first: visible form, the eye's object.

Chinese Source

色二或二十，聲唯有八種，味六香四種，觸十一為性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Form is two, or twenty. Sound is only eight kinds. Taste is six, smell four kinds. Touch has eleven as its nature.

論曰：言色二者，一顯、二形。顯色有四：青、黃、赤、白，餘顯是此四色差別。形色有八，謂長為初不正為後。或二十者，即此色處復說二十，謂青、黃、赤、白、長、短、方、圓、高、下、正、不正、雲、煙、塵、霧、影、光、明、闇。

Prose: "Form is two" means: first, colour; second, shape. Colour has four — blue, yellow, red, and white; every other colour is a variation of these four. Shape has eight — long as the first, through irregular as the last. As for "or twenty": this same sense-field of visible form is also stated as twenty, namely: blue, yellow, red, white, long, short, square, round, high, low, regular, irregular, cloud, smoke, dust, mist, shadow, light, brightness, and darkness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The count checks out exactly: four basic colours plus eight shapes plus eight further derived colours (cloud, smoke, dust, mist, shadow, light, brightness, darkness) makes twenty. The logic is explicit — everything beyond blue, yellow, red, and white is treated as a variation of one of those four, not an independent colour in its own right, so "twenty" and "two" are not competing counts but two levels of the same classification: two broad categories (colour, shape) unpacked into twenty specific items.

Readers of Ru-Yi's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra translation will recognize most of this list — that treatise's account of the eye-consciousness's object names nearly the same colours and shapes. One difference is worth flagging: the Yogācārabhūmi's list includes "the single colour of open sky" (空一顯色) as one of its colours, while this twenty-item count does not. The Kośa takes up exactly that question next — whether the sky's colour deserves its own place in the list at all.

19. Defining the Harder Colours, and a Minority View on the Sky

Having listed all twenty items by name, the treatise now defines the ones that are not self-evident — two of the eight shapes, and five of the eight derived colours — before recording a dissenting view.

Chinese Source

此中正者謂形平等，形不平等名為不正，地水氣騰說之為霧，日焰名光，月星火藥寶珠電等諸焰名明，障光明生於中餘色可見名影，翻此為闇。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these: "regular" means a shape that is even; an uneven shape is named "irregular." The rising vapour of earth and water is called "mist." The sun's radiance is named "light." The radiance of the moon, stars, fire, medicinal herbs, jewels, lightning, and the like is named "brightness." Where light is obstructed and something arises within it such that other colours can still be seen, this is named "shadow"; the reverse of this is "darkness."

有餘師說，空一顯色，第二十一。

Some other masters say that the single colour of open space is itself a twenty-first kind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice what the treatise leaves out of this list of definitions: cloud, smoke, and dust get no gloss at all, presumably because Vasubandhu judged them self-explanatory, while shadow, light, brightness, and darkness — genuinely harder to pin down, since none of them is a pigment in the ordinary sense — each get a careful causal definition. "Shadow" in particular is defined relationally rather than as a simple absence of light: it is what appears when light is blocked but not so completely that other colours vanish from view; total blockage, with nothing else visible, is "darkness" instead.

The dissenting view that follows — that the sky's own colour deserves a twenty-first slot — is simply recorded, not argued with or refuted here; this fascicle gives no verdict either way. It is worth remembering as the reason Ru-Yi's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra translation and this one do not quite line up on this single point: different Abhidharma lineages preserved different resolutions to exactly this disagreement.

20. Sound: Eight Kinds

Visible form is now fully explained. The treatise moves to the ear's object, sound, and unpacks it by three independent yes-or-no distinctions that combine into eight kinds total.

Chinese Source

已說色處，當說聲處。聲唯八種，謂有執受或無執受大種為因，及有情名、非有情名差別為四，此復可意及不可意差別成八。

Ru-Yi Translation

Visible form has now been explained; sound will now be explained. Sound is only eight kinds: caused by appropriated great elements, or caused by non-appropriated great elements, together with the further distinction into the designation of a sentient being and not the designation of a sentient being, makes four. These four, further divided by being agreeable or disagreeable, make eight.

執受大種為因聲者，謂言手等所發音聲。風林河等所發音聲，名無執受大種為因。有情名聲謂語表業，餘聲則是非有情名。

"Sound caused by appropriated great elements" refers, for instance, to the sound produced by the hand and the like. Sound produced by wind, forests, rivers, and the like is named "caused by non-appropriated great elements." Sound that is "the designation of a sentient being" means vocal expressive action; all other sound is "not the designation of a sentient being."

有說：有聲通有執受及無執受大種為因，如手鼓等合所生聲。如不許一顯色極微二四大造，聲亦應爾。

Some say: there is sound that has both appropriated and non-appropriated great elements together as its cause, such as the sound produced by a hand and a drum striking together. But just as it is not accepted that a single atom of visible colour is produced by two [separate sets of the] four great elements, so too should sound be understood.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The arithmetic is transparent: two options for physical cause (living body versus inanimate matter) times two options for meaning (language versus non-language) times two options for how it feels (pleasant versus unpleasant) gives eight. A handclap and human speech are both "caused by appropriated great elements" — produced by a living body — but only speech also counts as "the designation of a sentient being," since that phrase names meaningful vocal expression specifically (語表業, the karmically significant act of speaking), not just any sound a body happens to make. Wind in the trees and a river's noise are "non-appropriated," and, having no speaker, are automatically "not the designation of a sentient being" as well.

The closing exchange is a genuine technical dispute in Sarvāstivāda atomism, and we have kept it close to the literal wording rather than resolving it further. Someone raises an apparent counter-example — a hand striking a drum seems to produce one sound from two different kinds of cause at once. The reply appeals to the system's atomic theory (paramāṇu): just as a single "atom" of colour is never built from two separate sets of the four great elements, a single unit of sound should not be either — implying that what sounds like one hand-and-drum noise is, strictly, two distinct sounds arising together rather than one sound with a mixed cause.

21. Taste: Six Kinds, and Smell: Four Kinds

The next two sense-objects are dealt with briefly, one right after the other.

Chinese Source

已說聲處，當說味處。味有六種，甘、醋、醎、辛、苦、淡別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sound has now been explained; taste will now be explained. Taste is six kinds, distinguished as sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter, and bland.

已說味處，當說香處。香有四種：好香、惡香、等不等香，有差別故。

Taste has now been explained; smell will now be explained. Smell is four kinds: pleasant smell, unpleasant smell, and balanced or unbalanced smell — since these are distinguished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Worth noticing in passing: the treatise does not enumerate every sense-object the same way. Taste's six kinds are simply named as six distinct physical qualities, with no further cross-classification layered on top. Smell's four kinds, by contrast, are built from a small grid — pleasant or unpleasant, crossed with balanced (a moderate, well-matched concentration) or unbalanced (excessive or harsh) — the same combinatorial method already seen driving sound's count of eight. The treatise reaches for whichever method fits the material, rather than forcing every sense into one uniform scheme.

22. Touch: Eleven Kinds, and a Point about Naming

Touch is the last and longest of the five sense-objects — the four great elements themselves, plus seven further tangible qualities — and it closes with a small lesson on how language works.

Chinese Source

已說香處，當說觸處。觸有十一，謂四大種、滑性、澁性、重性、輕性及冷、飢、渴。

Ru-Yi Translation

Smell has now been explained; touch will now be explained. Touch is eleven kinds: the four great elements, together with smoothness, roughness, heaviness, lightness, cold, hunger, and thirst.

柔軟名滑。麁強為澁。可稱名重。翻此為輕。煖欲名冷。食欲名飢。飲欲名渴。

Softness is named smoothness. Coarseness and harshness are roughness. What can be weighed is named heaviness; the reverse of this is lightness. The desire for warmth is named cold. The desire for food is hunger. The desire for drink is thirst.

此三於因立果名故，作如是說。如有頌言：諸佛出現樂，演說正法樂，僧眾和合樂，同修勇進樂。

These three are spoken of this way because the name of the effect is established upon the cause. As a verse states: "The appearance of Buddhas is joy; the expounding of the true teaching is joy; the harmonious gathering of the saṅgha is joy; practising together with vigour is joy."

於色界中無飢渴觸，有所餘觸。

In the form realm, there is no tangible sensation of hunger or thirst; the remaining tangibles are present there.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Eleven kinds split cleanly in two: the four great elements themselves (earth, water, fire, wind — named here but not yet defined; their own characteristics get a dedicated, later treatment this fascicle has not reached), and seven further tangible qualities built from them. Five of those seven are named for what they plainly are — smoothness, roughness, heaviness, lightness. The last three are not: "cold" is literally defined as "desire for warmth," "hunger" as "desire for food," "thirst" as "desire for drink."

The treatise catches this oddity itself and explains it as a general feature of language, not a special case: sometimes a cause is named after the effect it produces, rather than by what it actually is. Cold is a physical sensation, but the word used for it names the craving that sensation provokes. The supporting verse makes the identical move in an entirely different context — the Buddha's appearance, the teaching, a harmonious saṅgha, and practising together are all, strictly, causes of joy, yet the verse calls each one "joy" outright. The closing remark about the form realm is a small piece of Abhidharma cosmology in passing: beings born there, having transcended desire-realm craving, no longer experience hunger or thirst at all, though they still have bodies subject to the other nine tangible qualities.

23. A Coda on the Form Realm, and the Sense-Field of Matter Concluded

Before closing the whole discussion of visible matter, the treatise adds two last, small cosmological details about the form realm mentioned in the previous section.

Chinese Source

彼界衣服，別不可稱，聚則可稱。冷煖於彼雖無能損而有能益。傳說如此。

Ru-Yi Translation

In that realm, garments cannot be weighed individually, but can be weighed when gathered together. Cold and warmth, although unable to harm beings there, are nonetheless able to benefit them. So it has been transmitted and explained.

此中已說多種色處。

Here, the many kinds belonging to the sense-field of matter have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image is a memorable one: form-realm beings' clothing is so refined that a single garment has, in effect, no weight at all — only a whole pile of them registers as anything to weigh. Cold and warmth work on these beings only as a pleasant sensation, never as harm, since the coarse physical vulnerability of the desire realm's bodies is already left behind at that level of existence.

The closing line is a genuine bookend for the sense-objects proper — form, sound, taste, smell, and touch — but not, as it turns out, for unmanifest form. Correction to what we said here: checking further ahead in the Chinese text shows that fascicle 1 does return to give unmanifest form's basic definition directly, a few sections on — what belongs to the much later chapter on karma is the detailed mechanics of exactly how particular precepts and vows produce and sustain it, not its basic nature as a kind of matter. We are leaving this note visible rather than quietly editing it away, since the correction is itself a useful reminder that even a careful reading of a dense, digression-filled text can misjudge how far ahead a promised topic actually is.

24. Does the Eye See One Thing, or Many?

Before moving to the remaining aggregates, the treatise pauses on a question of ordinary experience: can a single act of seeing take more than one thing as its object?

Chinese Source

有時眼識緣一事生，謂於爾時各別了別。有時眼識緣多事生，謂於爾時不別了別，如遠觀察軍眾山林、無量顯形珠寶聚等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sometimes the eye-consciousness arises taking a single thing as its object — that is, at that time it discerns each item separately. Sometimes the eye-consciousness arises taking many things as its object — that is, at that time it does not discern them separately, as when observing from a distance an army, a mountain forest, or a mass of countless colours, shapes, and jewels.

應知耳等諸識亦爾。

It should be understood that the ear-consciousness and the other remaining consciousnesses are likewise so.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The examples are well chosen: an army seen from a hilltop, or a forest seen from a distance, registers as one visual mass rather than as thousands of separately discerned soldiers or trees. The treatise treats this as an entirely ordinary mode of seeing, not a failure of it — sometimes consciousness resolves its object item by item, and sometimes it takes in a whole field at once without separately discerning each part. The very next passage pushes on exactly why this is allowed at all without breaking a rule the treatise otherwise holds firmly.

25. A Debate: Particular Characteristics and a Question of Priority

The same question is pressed harder for the body-consciousness and touch, raising one of Abhidharma's central epistemological distinctions — and the section closes with a practical puzzle about competing senses.

Chinese Source

有餘師說：身識極多緣五觸起，謂四大種，滑等隨一。有說：極多總緣一切十一觸起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some other masters say: the body-consciousness, at most, arises taking five tangibles as its object — namely, the four great elements, together with any one of smoothness and the rest. Others say: at most, it arises taking all eleven tangibles together as its object.

若爾，五識總緣境故，應五識身取共相境非自相境。約處自相，許五識身取自相境非事自相，斯有何失？

If that is so, then since the five sense-consciousnesses would be taking their objects all together, the body of five consciousnesses ought to be grasping a shared characteristic as its object, not a particular characteristic. [In reply:] with respect to the particular characteristic of the sense-field as a whole, it may be accepted that the body of five consciousnesses grasps the particular characteristic of the sense-field, not the particular characteristic of each individual thing — what fault is there in this?

今應思擇，身舌二根兩境俱至，何識先起？隨境強盛彼識先生；境若均平舌識先起，食飲引身令相續故。

Now this should be considered: when the objects of the two faculties, body and tongue, both arrive at once, which consciousness arises first? Whichever object is stronger, that consciousness arises first. If the objects are equal in strength, the tongue-consciousness arises first — because eating and drinking are what draw the body onward and keep it continuing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection assumes a rule the treatise holds elsewhere as basic: the five sense-consciousnesses only ever grasp a "particular characteristic" (自相, svalakṣaṇa) — the specific, unrepeatable nature of what is directly given right now — never a "shared characteristic" (共相, sāmānya-lakṣaṇa), a general feature abstracted across many things, which is held to be the mental consciousness's business alone. If the body-consciousness can take all eleven tangibles together in one act, doesn't that already amount to grasping something general rather than something particular? The reply draws a finer line than the objection allows for: even taken together, what is grasped is still the particular character of that sense-field as a directly-given whole, not a concept abstracted from it by thought. We have kept this exchange close to the literal Chinese rather than resolving it further — the boundary between "a particular that happens to be complex" and "a generality" is a genuinely fine one, argued over well beyond this treatise.

The closing puzzle is refreshingly concrete after that: when a sensation of touch and a taste arrive at the same moment with equal force, why does taste win? The treatise's answer is frankly functional — an organism has to keep eating and drinking to stay alive, so when the contest is otherwise even, priority goes to the sense that keeps the body going. It is a rare moment in this fascicle where the reasoning is not purely definitional but openly asks what a sense is for.

26. What Unmanifest Form Actually Is

The treatise now delivers on the promise made all the way back when the eleven kinds of form were first listed: a real definition of unmanifest form, the one item in that list that no sense can ever perceive.

Chinese Source

已說根境及取境相，無表色相今次當說。頌曰：亂心無心等，隨流淨不淨，大種所造性，由此說無表。

Ru-Yi Translation

The faculties, their objects, and how they take their objects have now been explained. The character of unmanifest form will next be explained. A verse states: through a scattered mind, a mindless state, and so on; following its own stream as pure or impure; having the nature of what is produced by the great elements — by this, unmanifest form is described.

論曰：亂心者，謂此餘心。無心者，謂入無想及滅盡定。等，言顯示不亂有心。相似相續說名隨流。善與不善名淨不淨。

Prose: "A scattered mind" means any mind other than the one that first produced it. "Mindless" means having entered the attainment of no-perception or the attainment of cessation. "And so on" indicates that it continues through an undistracted, present mind as well. A continuous succession of similar moments is called "following its own stream." Wholesome and unwholesome are named "pure" and "impure."

為簡諸得相似相續，是故復言大種所造。毘婆沙說：造是因義，謂作生等五種因故。顯立名因，故言由此。

In order to distinguish it from other things that also have a continuous succession of similar moments, it is further said to be "produced by the great elements." The Vaibhāṣika masters say: "produced" here has the sense of "caused" — that is, it functions as a cause in the sense of generating, and so forth, through five kinds of causal relation. To show that this establishes the reason for its name, the verse says "by this."

無表雖以色業為性如有表業，而非表示令他了知，故名無表。

Although unmanifest karma has physical action as its nature, just as manifest action does, it does not manifest itself so as to let others know of it — hence it is named "unmanifest."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The problem this verse solves is real: unmanifest form was placed among the eleven kinds of matter, yet it cannot be seen, heard, or otherwise sensed — so what keeps it in existence once produced? The answer is that it does not depend on being sensed, or even on the original state of mind that produced it, at all. Take a precept in one wholesome moment of mind, and the unmanifest form it generates continues afterward as its own "stream of similar moments" — through a distracted mind thinking about something else entirely, and even through the two meditative attainments in which ordinary mental activity stops altogether. It is "pure" or "impure" depending on whether the original act was wholesome or unwholesome, and it is specifically classed as matter — produced by the four great elements as its generating cause — precisely to distinguish it from other things (like the mental continuum itself) that also persist as a stream of similar moments without being made of matter at all.

The closing etymology is the cleanest statement in the whole discussion: unmanifest karma is real physical action, exactly as manifest bodily or verbal action is, with one difference — it never shows itself to anyone else. Taking a vow involves a visible act (manifest karma, seen and heard by witnesses) that produces an invisible, ongoing consequence (unmanifest karma) which only its owner carries forward, unseen by anyone. This is the "later explanation" fascicle 1 promised, and the fuller mechanics of exactly which acts produce it, and how it is gained or lost, are what the treatise takes up in the chapter on karma still to come.

27. The Four Great Elements: Function and Nature

Every kind of form named so far — the faculties, the five objects, unmanifest form — has been said to be "made of the four great elements." The treatise now finally defines what earth, water, fire, and wind actually are.

Chinese Source

大種謂四界，即地水火風，能成持等業，堅濕煖動性。

Ru-Yi Translation

The great elements are the four elements: earth, water, fire, and wind. They accomplish the functions of holding and the rest; their natures are solidity, wetness, heat, and motion.

論曰：地、水、火、風能持自相及所造色，故名為界，如是四界亦名大種，一切餘色所依性故、體寬廣故。或於地等增盛聚中形相大故，或起種種大事用故。

Prose: Earth, water, fire, and wind are able to hold their own particular character, and the derived matter produced from them — hence they are named "elements." These same four elements are also named "great elements," because all other matter has them as its underlying nature, and because their substance is vast in scope; or because, within an aggregate where one of them predominates, its form appears large; or because they give rise to great functions and effects of every kind.

此四大種能成何業？如其次第能成持、攝、熟、長四業，地界能持，水界能攝，火界能熟，風界能長。長謂增盛或復流引。

What functions do these four great elements accomplish? In their respective order, they accomplish four functions — holding, gathering, ripening, and growing: the earth element holds; the water element gathers; the fire element ripens; the wind element grows. "Growing" means causing to increase, or, further, causing to spread onward.

業用既爾，自性云何？如其次第即用堅、濕、煖、動為性，地界堅性，水界濕性，火界煖性，風界動性。

Given their functions, what is their own nature? In their respective order, their nature is precisely solidity, wetness, heat, and motion: the earth element's nature is solidity; the water element's nature is wetness; the fire element's nature is heat; the wind element's nature is motion.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is easy to misread as ordinary chemistry, and it isn't. "Earth," "water," "fire," and "wind" do not mean the visible substances we call by those names — visible water is itself already a combination of all four elements, just one in which wetness happens to predominate strongly enough to be felt as flowing and cohesive. The four "great elements" are instead the most basic tactile qualities present, in some proportion, in every last particle of matter without exception: solidity (resistance, the capacity to be felt as hard), wetness (cohesion, the capacity to bind and hold shape), heat (the capacity to ripen or mature), and motion (the capacity to spread or propagate). Nothing physical — not a sense faculty, not a sound, not unmanifest form itself — is ever built from less than all four together; what varies from one thing to the next is only which element predominates.

The treatise draws a careful line between what each element does (its function: earth holds a compound together as its base, water binds its parts to each other, fire ripens or matures it, wind causes it to grow or spread) and what each element simply is (its nature: solidity, wetness, heat, motion, respectively). This function-versus-nature move is the same analytical habit already seen applied to the eye-consciousness elsewhere on this site — first ask what a thing does, only then ask what it is — applied here to the four building blocks that everything physical in the Kośa's system is ultimately made of.

28. Feeling, Perception, and Formations — and a Bridge to the Elements

With form fully explained, the treatise moves through the next three aggregates quickly, in a single dense verse — and quietly uses the same verse to start connecting the five-aggregate scheme to the eighteen-element scheme still to come.

Chinese Source

受領納隨觸，想取像為體，四餘名行蘊，如是受等三，及無表無為，名法處法界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Feeling is the experiencing that follows upon contact. Perception has the grasping of an image as its essence. What remains of the four is named the aggregate of volitional formations. Feeling and these other two, together with unmanifest form and the unconditioned, are named the sense-field of dharmas and the element of dharmas.

論曰：受蘊謂三，領納隨觸，即樂及苦、不苦不樂。此復分別成六受身，謂眼觸所生受乃至意觸所生受。

Prose: The aggregate of feeling is threefold: the experiencing that follows upon contact — namely pleasant, painful, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant. This, further distinguished, forms the six bodies of feeling: feeling born of eye-contact, through feeling born of mind-contact.

想蘊謂能取像為體，即能執取青黃長短、男女怨親、苦樂等相。此復分別成六想身，應如受說。

The aggregate of perception has as its essence the ability to grasp an image — that is, the ability to apprehend such marks as blue, yellow, long, short, male, female, friend, enemy, pleasure, and pain. This, further distinguished, forms the six bodies of perception, to be understood just as feeling was explained.

除前及後色、受、想、識，餘一切行名為行蘊。

Excluding what has already come before and what comes after — form, feeling, perception, and consciousness — all remaining conditioned factors are named the aggregate of volitional formations.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Feeling and perception both get real definitions; formations gets none at all, on purpose. Feeling is simply the tone that arises with contact — pleasant, painful, or neutral — sorted into six "bodies" by which of the six sense-doors (eye through mind) produced it. Perception is the mind's labelling function: not the raw sensation itself, but the act of recognizing what is there — blue rather than yellow, a friend rather than an enemy — again sorted the same sixfold way. Volitional formations, by contrast, is defined purely by subtraction: whatever conditioned dharma is not form, not feeling, not perception, and not consciousness belongs here. This is why formations is famously the most crowded, heterogeneous aggregate in the whole system — it is the aggregate's job to catch everything else, from the will to act, to attention, to dozens of further mental factors this treatise will name one by one much later, rather than to name a single unified function the way feeling and perception each do.

The verse's last clause is doing quiet, important structural work. Feeling, perception, and formations — three of the five aggregates — together with unmanifest form and every unconditioned dharma, are here declared to be exactly what makes up "the sense-field of dharmas" and "the element of dharmas" (法處, 法界): one of the twelve sense-fields and eighteen elements the treatise is building toward. This is the first place fascicle 1 explicitly shows how its three classification schemes — aggregates, sense-fields, elements — are not independent systems but different cuts through the very same set of dharmas. Consciousness, left out of this list, is about to get its own treatment as the eighteen elements' remaining seven members.

29. Consciousness — the Fifth Aggregate, and a Bridge to the Elements

One loose end from the previous section is tied off first, before the treatise defines the last aggregate and immediately begins connecting it to the eighteen elements still to come.

Chinese Source

然薄伽梵於契經中說六思身為行蘊者，由最勝故。

Ru-Yi Translation

However, the Blessed One, in the sūtras, spoke of the six bodies of volition as "the aggregate of volitional formations," because they are the most excellent among what that aggregate contains.

識謂各了別，此即名意處，及七界應知，六識轉為意。

Consciousness is the discerning of each object individually. This same consciousness is named the mind sense-field; and the seven elements should be understood in relation to it — the six consciousnesses, in turn, become the mind element.

論曰：各各了別彼彼境界，總取境相故名識蘊。

Prose: The individual discerning of this or that object-realm — the taking up of an object's overall mark — is why this is named the aggregate of consciousness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The opening line resolves a tension a careful reader would already have noticed: the previous section defined volitional formations as a huge catch-all — everything conditioned that isn't form, feeling, perception, or consciousness — yet the sūtras themselves sometimes speak as if "formations" simply meant volition (思, cetanā). The answer is that volition is being singled out as the most excellent, most prominent member of that crowded aggregate, not the whole of it — a common shorthand, not a narrower technical definition competing with the one already given.

Consciousness itself gets a deliberately spare definition: the bare discerning of an object's overall mark. Set beside perception's "grasping an image" from the previous section, the contrast is exact — perception performs the specific labelling (this is blue, this is a friend), while consciousness is simply the underlying awareness-of-an-object that makes any labelling possible in the first place. With this, all five aggregates are now defined: form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness.

The verse's remaining clauses look forward rather than explain in full, and we are leaving them that way rather than resolving them ahead of schedule: consciousness, taken as a single aggregate, is also what the treatise will call the "mind sense-field" (意處) — one of the twelve; and, split by which of the six consciousnesses has just ceased, it also accounts for the "mind element" (意界) among the eighteen — the same doctrine already seen in Ru-Yi's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra translation, where the immediately-preceding moment of the eye-consciousness is likewise called manas. The full construction of the twelve sense-fields and eighteen elements is what fascicle 1 turns to next.

30. The Payoff: How the Three Schemes Are One

Every piece has now been placed. The treatise closes consciousness's definition by stating, in full, exactly how the five aggregates, twelve sense fields, and eighteen elements are three ways of cutting the very same reality.

Chinese Source

此復差別有六識身，謂眼識身至意識身。應知如是所說識蘊，於處門中立為意處。

Ru-Yi Translation

This, further differentiated, has six consciousness-bodies: the eye-consciousness-body, through the mind-consciousness-body. It should be understood that this aggregate of consciousness, just explained, is established, within the framework of the sense-fields, as the mind sense-field.

於界門中立為七界，謂眼識界至意識界，即此六識轉為意界。

Within the framework of the elements, it is established as seven elements: the eye-consciousness element, through the mind-consciousness element — and these same six consciousnesses, in transition, become the mind element.

如是此中所說五蘊，即十二處并十八界，謂除無表，諸餘色蘊即名十處，亦名十界。

Thus, the five aggregates spoken of here are precisely the twelve sense fields together with the eighteen elements: excluding unmanifest form, the remaining aggregate of form is named the ten sense-fields, and is likewise named the ten elements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Twenty-nine sections of definitions now snap into one table. The aggregate of form, minus its one imperceptible member, supplies ten of the twelve sense-fields and ten of the eighteen elements outright — the five faculties (eye through body) and their five matching objects (form through the tangible), exactly as fascicle 1 defined them section by section. The aggregate of consciousness supplies an eleventh sense-field, the "mind sense-field," and — split by which of the six consciousnesses has just ceased — seven of the eighteen elements: the six consciousness-elements plus the "mind element," the same doctrine already met in Ru-Yi's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra translation, where a consciousness's own immediately-past moment is called manas. Feeling, perception, formations, unmanifest form, and every unconditioned dharma — already declared in section 28 — supply the twelfth sense-field and eighteenth element: the "dharma sense-field" and "dharma element," the single catch-all category for everything that is neither a physical sense-organ, a physical sense-object, nor an act of consciousness itself.

Ten plus one plus one makes twelve; ten plus seven plus one makes eighteen. Nothing has been added or invented to make the arithmetic work — the same dharmas that fascicle 1 has been patiently defining since the aggregate of form are simply being counted a second and third way. This is the real point of naming this treatise's opening chapter Dhātu-nirdeśa, "the exposition of the elements": aggregates, sense-fields, and elements are not three competing theories of what exists, but three different lenses the treatise gives its readers for examining one and the same set of dharmas, each useful for a different kind of question.

31. Why Seven, Not Six? A Child Who Is Also a Father

A careful reader would already be objecting: if the mind element is nothing but the six consciousnesses, why count seven elements instead of six? The treatise raises the objection itself and answers with two everyday images.

Chinese Source

豈不識蘊唯六識身，異此說何復為意界？更無異法。即於此中頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Is it not the case that the aggregate of consciousness is only the six consciousness-bodies? Apart from these, what further thing is being spoken of as the "mind element"? There is no further, separate dharma. Precisely on this point, a verse states:

由即六識身，無間滅為意。

It is exactly the six consciousness-bodies themselves that, immediately upon ceasing without interval, become the mind.

論曰：即六識身無間滅已，能生後識故名意界，謂如此子即名餘父，又如此果即名餘種。

Prose: It is precisely the six consciousness-bodies which, having just ceased without interval, are able to give rise to the consciousness that follows — hence they are named the mind element. It is just as this same person, a child to his own parents, is named "father" in relation to his own child; or just as this same fruit is named "seed" in relation to what grows from it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

No eighth substance is smuggled in here, and none is needed. The treatise's answer to its own objection is that "the six consciousness-elements" and "the mind element" are two names for the very same six dharmas, distinguished only by which of two relations is being pointed to: taken as presently arising and discerning an object, they are the six consciousness-elements; taken as having just ceased and thereby opening the way for the next moment of consciousness, the very same dharmas are the mind element. The two similes make the logic vivid rather than abstract: one and the same man is "child" in relation to his own parents and "father" in relation to his own child — nobody thinks these are two different people. One and the same fruit is "fruit" in relation to the tree it grew from and "seed" in relation to the plant it will produce — nobody thinks these are two different objects. Six dharmas, two functional relations, seven names in the count of elements: the arithmetic from section 30 survives fully intact.

32. Why Eighteen, Not Seventeen? The Hidden Symmetry

The previous section's own logic invites an even sharper objection: if the mind element is nothing but the six consciousnesses, shouldn't the real count drop even further? The treatise's answer reveals the structural principle the whole eighteen-element scheme is built on.

Chinese Source

若爾實界應唯十七或唯十二，六識與意更相攝故，何緣得立十八界耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, then the real number of elements should be only seventeen, or even only twelve, since the six consciousnesses and the mind mutually subsume one another. For what reason, then, are eighteen elements established?

成第六依故，十八界應知。

Because it establishes the support for the sixth consciousness, the eighteen elements should be understood as genuinely eighteen.

論曰：如五識界別有眼等五界為依，第六意識無別所依，為成此依故說意界。如是所依能依境界，應知各六界成十八。

Prose: Just as the five sense-consciousness elements each have separate elements — the eye and the rest — as their support, the sixth, the mental consciousness, has no separate support of its own. In order to establish this support for it, the mind element is spoken of. Thus, the supports, the supporting consciousnesses, and their objects — each of these should be understood as six elements, making eighteen in total.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection is sharper than it first looks: if six dharmas can honestly be called seven under two different names, why stop there — why not admit the whole scheme is really just twelve elements (six consciousnesses plus six objects), with "supports" added only for the five physical senses and none for the mind? The answer exposes the actual design principle behind the number eighteen: every one of the six sense-modalities — the five physical ones and the mental one alike — needs exactly three elements to be structurally complete: a support, a consciousness, and an object. The five physical senses already have their supports ready-made, in the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body. The mental consciousness has no organ of its own the way the eye or ear does — so the treatise gives it one anyway, built from the only material available: the immediately-preceding moment of any of the six consciousnesses, which is exactly the "mind element" already explained in the previous section. Six supports, six consciousnesses, six objects: eighteen elements, not because eighteen is a traditional round number, but because it is the smallest count that gives every sense-modality, mental included, the same three-part structure the others already have.

33. Visible and Invisible

With the eighteen elements' number settled, the treatise begins cross-classifying them by other properties, starting with a question and a dense verse governing several distinctions at once.

Chinese Source

復次於前所說十八界中，幾有見、幾無見？幾有對、幾無對？幾善、幾不善、幾無記？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Further: among the eighteen elements spoken of above, how many are visible, and how many invisible? How many are resistant, and how many non-resistant? How many are wholesome, how many unwholesome, and how many neutral? A verse states:

一有見謂色，十有色有對，此除色聲八，無記餘三種。

One is visible: the element of visible form. Ten, being matter, are resistant. Of these ten, excluding visible form and sound, the remaining eight are neutral; the rest are of all three kinds.

論曰：十八界中色界有見，以可示現此彼差別。由此義准，說餘無見。

Prose: Among the eighteen elements, the element of visible form is visible, because it can be pointed to and shown, exhibiting "here" and "there" differences. On this same principle, by inference, the remaining seventeen are said to be invisible.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Visible" here is a narrow, technical sense, not a loose synonym for "perceptible." It means specifically that one can point at part of it and show a difference from another part — this patch is here, that patch is there. Sound, smell, taste, and the tangible are all perfectly perceptible by their own senses, yet none of them is "visible" in this sense: you cannot point at a sound and show that one part of it is "here" and another "there" the way you can with a colour or a shape. Only visible form itself has this spatial, showable quality, which is why exactly one element out of eighteen earns the label, while the other seventeen — including several that are directly sensed every moment — are technically "invisible."

34. Three Kinds of Resistance, and a Digression into Animal Eyes

The treatise now unpacks "resistant," and the explanation turns out to need three distinct senses of the word — including a memorable, almost zoological aside.

Chinese Source

已說有見無見。唯色蘊攝十界有對，對是礙義。此復三種：障礙、境界、所緣異故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Visible and invisible have now been explained. Resistance belongs only to the ten elements comprised within the aggregate of form; "resistant" has the sense of "obstruction." This, further, is of three kinds, differing as obstructive resistance, object-domain resistance, and objective-support resistance.

障礙有對，謂十色界。自於他處被礙不生，如手礙手或石礙石或二相礙。

Obstructive resistance refers to the ten elements of form: each, being obstructed by another at the same place, does not arise there — as when a hand is obstructed by a hand, or a stone by a stone, or the two obstruct each other.

境界有對，謂十二界法界一分諸有境法於色等境，故《施設論》作如是言：有眼於水有礙非陸，如魚等眼。有眼於陸有礙非水，從多分說，如人等眼。有眼俱礙，如畢舍遮、室獸摩羅及捕魚人、蝦蟇等眼。有俱非礙，謂除前相。

Object-domain resistance refers to the twelve elements — together with the portion of the dharma-element that has an object — being restricted to their own objects, such as visible form and the rest. Hence the Prajñaptiśāstra states: there are eyes obstructed in water but not on land, such as the eyes of fish. There are eyes obstructed on land but not in water — speaking of the majority of cases — such as the eyes of humans. There are eyes obstructed in both, such as the eyes of piśācas, crocodiles, fishermen, and frogs. There are eyes obstructed in neither, meaning those falling outside the aforementioned kinds.

有眼於夜有礙非晝，如諸蝙蝠鵂鶹等眼。有眼於晝有礙非夜，從多分說，如人等眼。有眼俱礙，如狗野干馬豹豺狼猫狸等眼。有俱非礙，謂除前相。此等名為境界有對。

There are eyes obstructed at night but not by day, such as the eyes of bats and owls. There are eyes obstructed by day but not at night — speaking of the majority of cases — such as the eyes of humans. There are eyes obstructed in both, such as the eyes of dogs, jackals, horses, leopards, wolves, and cats. There are eyes obstructed in neither, meaning those falling outside the aforementioned kinds. These are named object-domain resistance.

所緣有對，謂心心所於自所緣。境界、所緣復有何別？若於彼法此有功能，即說彼為此法境界。心心所法執彼而起，彼於心等名為所緣。

Objective-support resistance refers to mind and mental factors being restricted to their own objective support. What, then, is the difference between "object-domain" and "objective-support"? If this faculty or consciousness has the functional capacity with respect to that dharma, that dharma is called this dharma's "object-domain." When mind and mental factors take hold of that same dharma and arise in dependence on it, that dharma, with respect to the mind, is named its "objective-support."

云何眼等於自境界所緣轉時說名有礙？越彼於餘此不轉故。或復礙者是和會義，謂眼等法於自境界及自所緣和會轉故。應知此中唯就障礙有對而說，故但言十有色有對，更相障故。

How is it that the eye and the rest, when operating with respect to their own object-domain or objective-support, are said to be "obstructed"? Because, apart from that proper domain, they do not operate with respect to anything else. Or, further, "obstruction" here carries the sense of "conjunction": the eye and the other faculties operate only in conjunction with their own object-domain and their own objective-support. It should be understood that the earlier verse, in simply stating that "the ten, being matter, are resistant," meant only obstructive resistance — because these ten mutually obstruct one another.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Resistant" turns out to name three genuinely different things, and the treatise is careful not to blur them. Obstructive resistance is the plainest: two pieces of matter cannot occupy the same place, the way one hand blocks another or one stone blocks another — this is the sense the opening verse actually intends when it counts "ten" resistant elements. Object-domain resistance is a faculty's restriction to its own proper territory, and the treatise illustrates it with a small survey of animal vision quoted from another Abhidharma text, the Prajñaptiśāstra: fish see in water but not on land, humans (mostly) see on land but not in water, some creatures manage both, bats and owls see by night but not day, humans (mostly) see by day but not night, and creatures like dogs and wolves manage both. Objective-support resistance, the finest distinction of the three, is about mind and mental factors specifically: a faculty's "object-domain" is simply whatever it has the functional capacity to engage, but when a moment of mind or a mental factor actually arises by taking hold of that object, the same object is now called its "objective-support" — the same territory, named differently depending on whether we are speaking of mere capacity or of an actual act of cognition arising from it.

The zoological aside is not decoration. It exists to make a genuinely abstract point — that every cognitive faculty is restricted to operating within its own proper domain — vivid through cases any reader already half-knows from observing animals, before the discussion returns to the much more exacting distinction between a faculty's mere "domain" and mind's active "objective support."

35. A Closing Tetralemma on Resistance, and an Elder's View

Before leaving resistance behind, the treatise checks whether its two subtler senses — object-domain and obstructive resistance — always travel together, and records one further scholarly voice on the topic.

Chinese Source

若法境界有對，亦障礙有對耶？應作四句。謂七心界法界一分諸相應法是第一句。色等五境是第二句。眼等五根是第三句。法界一分非相應法是第四句。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a dharma has object-domain resistance, does it also have obstructive resistance? A fourfold analysis should be made. The seven mind-elements, and the portion of the dharma-element consisting of associated mental factors, form the first case [object-domain resistance without obstructive resistance]. The five objects — form and the rest — form the second case. The five faculties — the eye and the rest — form the third case. The portion of the dharma-element that is dissociated from mind forms the fourth case.

此中大德鳩摩邏多作如是說：是處心欲生，他礙令不起，應知是有對，無對此相違。此是所許。

Here, the great elder Kumāralāta stated it this way: wherever a mind wishes to arise, and something else obstructs it and prevents its arising, this should be known as resistant; non-resistant is the reverse of this. This too is accepted.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four cases sort dharmas by whether they have a cognitive domain, physical obstructiveness, both, or neither: the seven mind-elements and their associated mental factors have a domain but no matter to obstruct anything with; the five sense-objects and five sense-organs have both, since they are matter that also functions within a domain; and the unconditioned dharmas and mind-dissociated formations, within the dharma-element, have neither. Kumāralāta's citation — a named elder whose view the treatise records without simply adopting it as the only word on the subject — reframes resistance psychologically rather than physically: something is "resistant," on his account, whenever a mind that wants to arise is blocked by something else from doing so. It is a small but telling moment of the treatise showing its work: naming the person a definition comes from, rather than presenting every view anonymously as "the" position.

36. Wholesome, Unwholesome, and Neutral

The treatise finally reaches the third and last of this section's three-way questions, closing the circle back to why exactly eight of the ten matter elements were said, several sections ago, to be permanently neutral.

Chinese Source

如是已說有對無對。於此所說十有對中，除色及聲餘八無記。謂五色根、香、味、觸境，不可記為善不善性，故名無記。有說：不能記異熟果故名無記。若爾，無漏應唯無記。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resistant and non-resistant have now been explained. Among the ten resistant elements just discussed, excluding visible form and sound, the remaining eight are neutral — that is, the five material faculties, and the objects of smell, taste, and touch, cannot be reckoned as having a wholesome or unwholesome nature; hence they are named "neutral." Some say: it is because they are unable to determine a ripened result that they are named neutral. If that were so, the uncontaminated ought to be entirely neutral as well [which is not accepted].

其餘十界通善等三，謂七心界與無貪等相應名善，貪等相應名為不善，餘名無記。

The remaining ten elements are common to all three. That is: the seven mind-elements, when associated with non-greed and the rest, are named wholesome; when associated with greed and the rest, are named unwholesome; the rest are named neutral.

法界若是無貪等性相應等起擇滅名善，若貪等性相應等起名為不善，餘名無記。

As for the dharma-element: if it has the nature of non-greed and the rest, is associated with them, arises from them, or is cessation through discernment, it is named wholesome. If it has the nature of greed and the rest, is associated with them, or arises from them, it is named unwholesome. The rest is named neutral.

色界聲界若善不善心力等起身語表攝，是善不善，餘是無記。

As for the element of visible form and the element of sound: if they are produced by the power of a wholesome or unwholesome mind, and so are comprised within bodily or vocal manifestation, they are wholesome or unwholesome; the rest is neutral.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reasoning closes a loop opened all the way back when unmanifest form was first defined. The five sense faculties and the objects of smell, taste, and touch are pure receptors and raw physical qualities — nothing about an eyeball or a patch of sourness is itself a moral act, so they are permanently neutral. Visible form and sound are different precisely because they can be a moral act: a respectful bow or a violent gesture is visible form produced by a wholesome or unwholesome mind and expressed as bodily manifestation (身表); a kind word or a cruel one is sound produced the same way and expressed as vocal manifestation (語表). It is only when colour or sound rises to the level of manifest bodily or verbal karma that it inherits the moral quality of the mind behind it — otherwise, like a rock's colour or the sound of wind, it is neutral too.

The seven mind-elements and the dharma-element are judged the same way, by what they are yoked to: a moment of mind (or a mental factor, in the dharma-element) is wholesome when paired with the three wholesome roots — non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion — and unwholesome when paired with their opposites; cessation through discernment, already met early in this fascicle, is explicitly named wholesome outright, since it is nothing other than liberation itself. The proposed alternative definition of "neutral" — that it simply means "producing no karmic ripening" — is raised and quietly set aside, since it would wrongly force every uncontaminated dharma into the neutral category too, when uncontaminated wholesome states are not treated as merely neutral in this system.

37. Which Elements Belong to Which Realm?

The treatise now returns to the three-tier cosmology touched on earlier — the desire, form, and formless realms — and asks how many of the eighteen elements a being in each realm actually has.

Chinese Source

已說善等。十八界中幾欲界繫、幾色界繫、幾無色界繫？頌曰：欲界繫十八，色界繫十四，除香味二識，無色繫後三。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wholesome and the rest have now been explained. Among the eighteen elements, how many are bound to the desire realm, how many to the form realm, and how many to the formless realm? A verse states: bound to the desire realm are all eighteen; bound to the form realm are fourteen, excluding smell, taste, and their two consciousnesses; bound to the formless realm are the last three.

論曰：繫謂繫屬，即被縛義。欲界所繫具足十八，色界所繫唯十四種，除香味境及鼻舌識。除香味者，段食性故，離段食欲方得生彼。除鼻舌識，無所緣故。

Prose: "Bound" means belonging to, in the sense of being tied. What is bound to the desire realm is complete in all eighteen. What is bound to the form realm is only fourteen kinds, excluding the objects of smell and taste, and the nose- and tongue-consciousnesses. Smell and taste are excluded because they have the nature of morsel-food; only by being free of the desire for morsel-food can one be born there. The nose- and tongue-consciousnesses are excluded because they would then have no object to take.

若爾，觸界於彼應無，如香味境段食性故，彼所有觸非段食性。若爾，香味類亦應然，香味離食無別受用，觸有別用持根衣等，彼離食欲香味無用，有根衣等故觸非無。

If that is so, one might object: the element of the tangible should likewise be absent there, since it too is involved with morsel-food just as smell and taste are. In reply: whatever tangible exists there is not of the nature of morsel-food. If that is so, one might press further: smell and taste should likewise have some other use. In reply: apart from food, smell and taste have no separate use, whereas the tangible has a separate use — sustaining the faculties, clothing, and the like. Since beings free from the desire for food have no use for smell and taste, those are excluded; but since they still have faculties, clothing, and the like, the tangible is not absent.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The pattern connects directly to what was already said about the form realm: beings there have gone beyond desire for "morsel-food" (段食, ordinary chewed and swallowed food), and smell and taste exist, in this system, for no other reason than to serve that appetite — so once the appetite is gone, both the objects and their two consciousnesses drop out entirely, with nothing left for them to do. The exchange that follows is a small model of how this treatise argues: an objector presses the same logic further each time (if food-relatedness disqualifies smell and taste, shouldn't it disqualify touch too? and if touch survives because it has other uses, don't smell and taste have other uses as well?), and each reply has to find the actual asymmetry rather than simply repeating the rule. The answer that survives is functional: touch is still needed to sustain the body's own faculties and to feel contact with clothing and surroundings, uses that have nothing to do with eating, while smell and taste, once food is set aside, have simply nothing left to do.

38. A Second View on Touch, and Why Nose and Tongue Remain

The debate over the form realm's remaining senses continues with a further scholarly opinion, then a defence of why the nose and tongue faculties persist even without smell or taste to sense.

Chinese Source

有餘師說：住此依彼靜慮等至見色聞聲，輕安俱起有殊勝觸攝益於身，是故此三生彼靜慮猶相隨逐，香味不爾，故在彼無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some other masters say: those dwelling there, based on the level of meditative absorption they have attained, see forms and hear sounds; and pliancy arises together with a special kind of tangible sensation that nourishes the body. For this reason, these three — visible form, sound, and touch — continue to accompany one born into that meditative state, whereas smell and taste do not work this way, and so are absent there.

若爾，鼻舌彼應非有，如香味境彼無用故。不爾，二根於彼有用，謂起言說及莊嚴身。

If that is so, one might object: the nose and tongue faculties should likewise be absent there, since, like the objects of smell and taste, they would have no use. In reply: not so — the two faculties still have a use there, namely, producing speech and adorning the body.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This second scholarly view sharpens the earlier explanation: it isn't just that touch has non-food uses in general, but that a specific, refined kind of touch — pliancy (輕安, praśrabdhi, a meditative factor of bodily and mental ease) — is bound up with the deep meditative states that define life in the form realm itself, so it naturally persists there alongside seeing and hearing. Nose and tongue, meanwhile, survive not because they still smell or taste anything, but for reasons that have nothing to do with their original sensory job: a complete, well-formed body needs a nose and a tongue to look whole, and a tongue is needed to produce speech.

The debate presses one step further still — whether the form realm's nose and tongue are full functioning faculties or merely their physical, decorative bases without real sensory capability, drawing an analogy to the male organ (wholly absent in the form realm, since it serves no purpose there at all) and to a fetus that grows complete faculties even though it is destined to die before ever using them. We have summarized this final, very fine-grained volley rather than translating it line by line, since it presses a technical distinction — a faculty's mere physical basis versus its full functioning arising — well past what changes the substantive conclusion already given: nose and tongue remain in the form realm for speech and bodily completeness, even without smell and taste to sense.

39. The Formless Realm: Only Three Elements Remain

The three-realm survey closes with its starkest case: a mode of existence with no physical body at all.

Chinese Source

無色界繫唯有後三，所謂意、法及意識界，要離色欲於彼得生，故無色中無十色界。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is bound to the formless realm consists only of the last three — namely, the mind element, the dharma element, and the mind-consciousness element. Only by being free of desire for form can one be born there; hence, within the formless realm, the ten elements of form are absent.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three realms now form a clean, descending sequence: the desire realm keeps all eighteen elements; the form realm drops smell, taste, and their two consciousnesses once the appetite for food is gone, but keeps a physical body and its remaining senses; the formless realm drops all ten elements of form outright, since what is left behind there is not merely food but form itself — matter of any kind. What remains is only mind: the mind element, the dharma element it takes as its object, and the mind-consciousness that arises from the two together. A being in the formless realm has no eye, no body, no shape at all — only a bare continuum of mental events, which is exactly what its highest meditative attainments (infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception) are meditations on. The Chinese text goes on to press one further, narrower debate about whether the form realm's sexual characteristics survive in the same way as its nose and tongue — a fine continuation of the faculty-versus-use argument already covered rather than new doctrine, which we are not translating line by line here.

40. Which Elements Can Be Uncontaminated?

One last remark closes the three-realm survey; then the treatise opens an entirely new cross-classification, asking how many of the eighteen elements are ever capable of being uncontaminated at all.

Chinese Source

依緣無故五識亦無，故唯後三無色界繫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since their supports and objects are both absent, the five sense-consciousnesses are absent as well; hence only the last three are bound to the formless realm.

已說界繫，十八界中幾有漏、幾無漏？頌曰：意法意識通，所餘唯有漏。

Bound-to-realm has now been explained. Among the eighteen elements, how many are contaminated, and how many uncontaminated? A verse states: the mind, the dharma, and the mind-consciousness elements are common to both; the remainder are contaminated only.

論曰：意及意識道諦攝者，名為無漏，餘名有漏。法界若是道諦無為，名為無漏，餘名有漏。餘十五界唯名有漏。

Prose: When the mind element and the mind-consciousness element are comprised within the truth of the path, they are named uncontaminated; the rest are named contaminated. When the dharma element is the truth of the path, or is the unconditioned, it is named uncontaminated; the rest is named contaminated. The remaining fifteen elements are named contaminated only.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing line on the formless realm quietly reinforces why only three elements remain there: a consciousness needs both a support and an object to arise at all, and since the formless realm has neither a physical faculty nor a physical object for any of the five sense-consciousnesses, those five simply cannot arise — not by some special exclusion rule, but because the ordinary conditions for their arising are entirely missing.

The new question then narrows sharply: of all eighteen elements, only three — mind, dharma, and mind-consciousness — are ever even capable of being uncontaminated, and only when they specifically belong to the path or the unconditioned. The other fifteen, including every physical sense-organ and every physical sense-object, are contaminated without exception, no matter the circumstance. This is a strong claim worth sitting with: matter itself, on this system, is never uncontaminated — liberation is not a property any eye, ear, or patch of colour could ever have. Only mind, and what mind takes as its object, can ever rise to that level, and even then only in the narrow case where what is grasped is specifically the path itself or an unconditioned dharma like cessation through discernment.

41. Applied and Sustained Thought — and the Map of Meditative Absorption

This cross-classification connects directly to meditation practice: whether an element involves applied thought (尋, vitarka), sustained thought (伺, vicāra), both, or neither turns out to track the boundaries between the levels of meditative absorption themselves.

Chinese Source

十八界中幾有尋有伺、幾無尋唯伺、幾無尋無伺？頌曰：五識唯尋伺，後三三餘無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the eighteen elements, how many have both applied and sustained thought, how many have only sustained thought, and how many have neither? A verse states: the five sense-consciousnesses have both applied and sustained thought only. The last three are of all three kinds. The rest have neither.

論曰：眼等五識有尋有伺，由與尋伺恒共相應，以行相麁外門轉故、顯義決定，故說唯言。

Prose: The eye-consciousness and the other four have both applied and sustained thought, because they are constantly and invariably associated with applied and sustained thought, their mode of activity being coarse and operating through the outward-facing sense-doors. To make this meaning definite, the word "only" is used.

後三謂是意、法、意識，根、境、識中各居後故。此後三界皆通三品，意界、意識界及相應法界，除尋與伺。

"The last three" means the mind, dharma, and mind-consciousness elements, since each occupies the last position among the faculties, objects, and consciousnesses respectively. These last three elements are all common to all three kinds — the mind element, the mind-consciousness element, and the dharma element associated with them — excluding applied and sustained thought themselves.

若在欲界初靜慮中有尋有伺，靜慮中間無尋唯伺，第二靜慮以上諸地乃至有頂無尋無伺。

If these three are in the desire realm or the first meditative absorption, they have both applied and sustained thought. In the intermediate meditative state, they have only sustained thought, without applied thought. From the second meditative absorption upward, through to the summit of existence, they have neither applied nor sustained thought.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Applied thought (尋, vitarka) and sustained thought (伺, vicāra) are traditionally illustrated by a bell: applied thought is the initial strike that brings the mind onto its object, coarse and sudden; sustained thought is the reverberation that follows, subtler and lingering, examining what the strike brought into range. The five sense-consciousnesses always have both, simply because of how they work — reaching outward through the sense-doors to a coarse object is already, in effect, a kind of striking-and-examining, regardless of anyone's level of meditative attainment. What actually varies with practice is the mind, dharma, and mind-consciousness elements alone, and the treatise uses them to mark the very boundaries between stages of absorption: desire-realm consciousness and the first dhyāna both still strike and examine their object; a special transitional state between the first and second dhyāna has let go of the striking but still lingers in sustained examination; from the second dhyāna all the way up to the highest point of existence, both drop away entirely, leaving an absorption with no discursive activity, coarse or subtle, at all. The classification of eighteen elements has, without warning, turned into a map of the meditative path itself.

42. A Closer Look: Four Kinds of "Having Thought" Within One Level

The treatise now turns the same question on applied and sustained thought themselves: even within a single meditative level said to "have both," do the two factors have both with respect to their own selves?

Chinese Source

由此故言有尋伺地有四品法：一有尋有伺，謂除尋伺餘相應法。二無尋唯伺，謂即是尋。三無尋無伺，謂即一切非相應法。四無伺唯尋，謂即是伺。

Ru-Yi Translation

For this reason, it is said that within a level having both applied and sustained thought, dharmas fall into four categories. First, having both: every associated dharma except applied and sustained thought themselves. Second, without applied thought but with sustained thought only: applied thought itself. Third, without either: every dissociated dharma. Fourth, without sustained thought but with applied thought only: sustained thought itself.

餘十色界尋伺俱無，常與尋伺不相應故。

The remaining ten elements of form have neither applied nor sustained thought at all, since they are never associated with either.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The logic is self-referential and worth working through slowly. Ask of any ordinary mental factor, in a level that "has both applied and sustained thought," whether applied and sustained thought accompany it — yes, both do, so it falls in the first category. Now ask the same question of applied thought itself: does applied thought come paired with a second instance of applied thought? No — nothing is its own accompanying duplicate — but it is genuinely accompanied by sustained thought, so applied thought itself is classed as "without applied thought, with sustained thought only." Sustained thought gets the mirror-image answer for the same reason: no second instance of itself, but genuinely accompanied by applied thought, so it is classed as "without sustained thought, with applied thought only." Dissociated dharmas, not being the kind of thing that co-arises with mental factors in this way at all, have neither. The ten elements of form need no such careful sorting: being matter, they are simply never associated with either factor, full stop.

43. Are the Five Senses Truly "Non-Discriminating"? The One-Legged Horse

The objection lands squarely: elsewhere the five sense-consciousnesses are called "non-discriminating," yet this fascicle has just said they always have applied thought — which sounds like a kind of discrimination. The treatise resolves this by splitting "discrimination" into three very different things.

Chinese Source

如何得說無分別耶？頌曰：說五無分別，由計度隨念，以意地散慧，意諸念為體。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, then, can it be said that the five are without discrimination? A verse states: the five are said to be without discrimination because of deliberative and recollective discrimination — these have, as their essence, the mind-ground's scattered wisdom, and the mind's various acts of recollection.

論曰：傳說，分別略有三種：一自性分別、二計度分別、三隨念分別。由五識身雖有自性而無餘二，說無分別，如一足馬名為無足。

Prose: It has been transmitted that discrimination is, in brief, of three kinds: self-nature discrimination, deliberative discrimination, and recollective discrimination. Because the body of five consciousnesses, although it has self-nature discrimination, lacks the other two, it is said to be without discrimination — just as a horse with only one leg is called legless.

自性分別體唯是尋，後心所中自當辯釋。餘二分別如其次第。意地散慧諸念為體。散謂非定。意識相應散慧，名為計度分別。若定若散意識相應諸念，名為隨念分別。

Self-nature discrimination has applied thought alone as its essence, and will be explained in its own right later, in the section on mental factors. The remaining two kinds of discrimination, in order, have as their essence the mind-ground's scattered wisdom, and the mind's various acts of recollection. "Scattered" means not in meditative concentration. Wisdom associated with the mental consciousness, when scattered, is named deliberative discrimination. Any recollection associated with the mental consciousness, whether concentrated or scattered, is named recollective discrimination.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The one-legged horse is one of the most memorable images in this fascicle, and it does real philosophical work: the five sense-consciousnesses are not literally free of all discrimination — they do have "self-nature discrimination," which turns out to be nothing more than applied thought (尋) itself, the same factor already discussed at length. But that is only one of three legs. What the five senses genuinely lack are deliberative discrimination — the mental consciousness's own discursive, judging wisdom, active only outside meditative absorption — and recollective discrimination — the mental consciousness's capacity for memory, active whether concentrated or not. Since these two are what ordinary language actually means by "discriminating" or "judging" something, calling the five senses "non-discriminating" is true in every sense that matters, even though, strictly, they retain the one weakest leg of the three.

This quietly answers a question a careful reader of Ru-Yi's other translations may already have been asking: how can a sense-consciousness register a colour or a sound at all without already having judged or interpreted it? The answer this treatise gives is that bare registration — applied thought's minimal "aiming" at an object — is not yet judgment, memory, or interpretation. Those belong entirely to the sixth consciousness, arising a moment later, exactly as fascicle 1 of the Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra describes the "sudden mind" of the eye-consciousness giving way to the "seeking" and "deciding" minds of the mental consciousness that follows it.

44. Which Elements Have an Object? Which Are Part of a Living Body?

One dense verse now governs two more cross-classifications at once: which elements take an object at all, and which count as "appropriated" — genuinely part of a living, sentient body, rather than inert matter.

Chinese Source

如是已說有尋伺等。十八界中幾有所緣、幾無所緣？幾有執受、幾無執受？頌曰：七心法界半，有所緣餘無，前八界及聲，無執受餘二。

Ru-Yi Translation

Applied and sustained thought and the rest have now been explained. Among the eighteen elements, how many have an object, and how many have none? How many are appropriated, and how many are not? A verse states: the seven mind elements, and half of the dharma element, have an object; the rest have none. The first eight elements, together with sound, are without appropriation; the rest fall into both categories.

論曰：六識意界及法界攝諸心所法，名有所緣，能取境故。餘十色界及法界攝不相應法，名無所緣，義准成故。

Prose: The six consciousnesses, the mind element, and the portion of the dharma element comprising the mental factors — these are named "having an object," because they are able to take hold of an object. The remaining ten elements of form, and the portion of the dharma element comprising the dissociated formations, are named "without an object," established by the same reasoning.

如是已說有所緣等。十八界中九無執受，前七心界及法界全，此八及聲皆無執受。所餘九界各通二門，謂有執受、無執受故。

Thus, "having an object" and the rest have now been explained. Among the eighteen elements, nine are without appropriation: the first seven mind-elements and the entire dharma element — these eight, together with sound, are all without appropriation. The remaining nine elements each fall into both categories — that is, they may be either appropriated or non-appropriated.

眼等五根住現在世名有執受，過去未來名無執受。色香味觸住現在世不離五根名有執受，若住現在非不離根過去未來名無執受。

The eye and the other four faculties, when abiding in the present, are named appropriated; when past or future, they are named non-appropriated. Form, smell, taste, and touch, when abiding in the present and not separated from the five faculties, are named appropriated; if present but separated from the faculties, or if past or future, they are named non-appropriated.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Having an object" turns out to be a simple, clean line: mind and mental factors take hold of something, matter and dissociated formations do not — so only the seven mind-elements and the associated half of the dharma element make the cut, and everything else is automatically "without an object" by the same logic in reverse.

"Appropriated" (執受, upātta) is the more interesting distinction, and it names something quite specific: matter that is genuinely part of a living, sentient body right now, as opposed to matter that merely resembles it. Nine elements — the seven mind-elements, the dharma element, and sound — can never be appropriated at all: mind and mental factors aren't matter to begin with, and sound, even a living being's own voice, is treated as something emitted rather than as living tissue. The other nine — the five faculties and four of the five objects (form, smell, taste, touch, but not sound) — can go either way, and the deciding line is exact: your own eye, right now, in this present moment, is appropriated; the very same eye a moment ago or a moment from now is not, because appropriation belongs only to the present. The colour of your living skin, the taste in your mouth, the smell of your own body, and the warmth felt in your hand are appropriated in exactly the same way, but only while they remain part of a functioning body in the present — the same colour once it is, say, a bruise that has healed and sloughed off, or the same taste once swallowed and gone, no longer counts.

45. What "Appropriated" Really Means — Hair, Nails, and Felt Touch

The treatise now sharpens "appropriated" with a surprising list of exceptions, then gives the concept its actual technical definition.

Chinese Source

如在身內除與根合，髮毛爪齒大小便利洟唾血等，及在身外地水等中色香味觸，雖在現世而無執受。

Ru-Yi Translation

For example: within the body, excluding what is joined with the faculties — hair, body hair, nails, teeth, feces, urine, nasal mucus, saliva, blood, and the like — and, outside the body, the form, smell, taste, and touch found in earth, water, and the like: although these exist in the present, they are without appropriation.

有執受者，此言何義？心心所法共所執持攝為依處名有執受，損益展轉更相隨故。即諸世間說有覺觸，眾緣所觸覺樂等故。與此相違名無執受。如是已說有執受等。

"Appropriated" — what does this term actually mean? That which mind and mental factors together hold and sustain, taking it as their basis, is named appropriated, because harm and benefit to one, in turn, follow to the other. This is why ordinary people speak of "felt touch" — the various conditions that produce a touch bring about a feeling such as pleasure and the like. The opposite of this is named non-appropriated. Thus, appropriated and the rest have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The exceptions are the memorable part, and they match ordinary experience precisely: hair, nails, teeth, and bodily fluids like blood, saliva, urine, and mucus are all, in one sense, "inside" or attached to the living body, yet the treatise excludes every one of them from "appropriated" status. The reason becomes clear with the definition that follows: appropriation is not about location but about a two-way sympathetic bond — mind and mental factors take some matter as their basis such that harm to that matter is genuinely felt, and the mind's condition in turn affects the matter. Cut a strand of hair or trim a nail, and nothing hurts, because that tissue was never bound to the mind this way; cut living skin, and it does, because skin is. This is also, the treatise notes, the technical basis for "felt touch" in ordinary speech — a stimulus registers as pleasant or painful precisely because it lands on matter genuinely appropriated by mind, not on hair, nails, or waste products passing through or out of the body.

46. Primary or Derived Matter? And Which Elements Are Built of Atoms?

One more dense verse governs two further classifications: which elements are the four great elements themselves versus matter derived from them, and which are literally composed of clustered atoms.

Chinese Source

十八界中幾大種性、幾所造性？幾可積集、幾非積集？頌曰：觸界中有二，餘九色所造，法一分亦然，十色可積集。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the eighteen elements, how many have the nature of the primary great elements, and how many are derived matter? How many are accumulable, and how many are not? A verse states: within the element of the tangible there are both; the remaining nine elements of form are derived matter; a portion of the dharma element is likewise so; the ten elements of form are accumulable.

論曰：觸界通二，謂大種及所造。大種有四，謂堅性等。所造有七，謂滑性等，依大種生故名所造。餘九色界唯是所造，謂五色根、色等四境。法界一分無表業色亦唯所造。

Prose: The element of the tangible is common to both: the great elements themselves, and derived matter. The great elements are four — solidity and the rest. The derived are seven — smoothness and the rest — named "derived" because they arise in dependence on the great elements. The remaining nine elements of form are entirely derived: the five material faculties, and the four objects — visible form and the rest. The portion of the dharma element that is unmanifest karmic matter is likewise entirely derived.

十八界中，五根、五境十有色界是可積集，極微聚故。義准餘八非可積集，非極微故。

Among the eighteen elements, the five faculties and the five objects — the ten elements of form — are accumulable, because they are aggregations of atoms. By the same reasoning, the remaining eight are non-accumulable, because they are not aggregations of atoms.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Only one element out of eighteen contains the four great elements directly: touch, since solidity, wetness, heat, and motion are precisely what the tangible sense feels — nowhere else are the primary elements themselves the actual content of a sense-object. Every other kind of matter, without exception, is derived: the five faculties, and visible form, sound, smell, and taste as objects, are all secondary matter built from some combination of the four elements, never the four elements appearing as such. Unmanifest karmic matter, tucked inside the dharma element, is derived in exactly the same way — a further confirmation, from a completely different angle, of what fascicle 1 established when it first defined unmanifest form as matter produced by the great elements.

"Accumulable" returns to the atomism already met in the earlier debate over whether a hand striking a drum makes one sound or two: the ten elements of form are, quite literally, clusters of countless atoms (極微) bound together, which is exactly why they can be measured, weighed, and divided into parts. The remaining eight — the six consciousnesses, the mind element, and the dharma element — are never spoken of as clusters of atoms at all, because nothing about mind is built up piece by piece the way matter is.

47. What Can Cut, and What Can Be Cut?

The treatise now asks a strikingly concrete question: of all eighteen elements, which can serve as a cutting tool, and which can be cut?

Chinese Source

謂唯外四界，能斫及所斫，亦所燒能稱，能燒所稱諍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Only the four external elements can be both the cutter and the cut, and also the burned and the weigher; whether they can be the burner and the weighed is disputed.

論曰：色香味觸成斧薪等，此即名為能斫所斫。何法名斫？薪等色聚相逼續生，斧等分隔令各續起，此法名斫。

Prose: Form, smell, taste, and touch make up such things as an axe and firewood; this is what is called "the cutter" and "the cut." What is meant by "cutting"? When a cluster of matter such as firewood, through mutual pressing, would otherwise continue arising as one connected mass, and an axe or the like separates it, causing each part to continue arising separately — this capacity is what is named "cutting."

身等色根不名所斫，非可全斷令成二故。非身根等可成二分，支分離身則無根故。又身根等亦非能斫，以淨妙故如珠寶光。

The body and the other material faculties are not called "the cut," because they cannot be entirely severed so as to become two functioning wholes — it is not that the body-faculty and the like can be made into two parts, since once a limb is separated from the body, there is no longer a faculty there at all. Furthermore, the body-faculty and the like are also not "the cutter," because they are pure and subtle, like the light of a jewel or pearl.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Cutting" gets a precise definition worth noticing: it is not damage in general, but specifically separating a continuous mass into parts that each keep on existing independently afterward. This is exactly why the sense faculties are excluded from both roles, for two different reasons. They are not "the cut" because severing a limb does not leave behind two functioning faculties — the faculty simply ends where the body does, unlike firewood, which remains firewood on both sides of the axe's stroke. They are not "the cutter" either, because the faculties are made of "pure form" (the same refined, imperceptible matter defined back in section 17) — too subtle a substance, the text says, comparing it to the light given off by a jewel, to physically cut anything at all. Ordinary gross matter can cut and be cut; the delicate matter of a living sense-organ can do neither.

48. Burning, Weighing, and a Recorded Disagreement

The verse's remaining two questions get answered together, and this time the treatise records real disagreement among its own scholars rather than settling on one view.

Chinese Source

聲界總非，不相續故。能燒所稱有異諍論，謂或有說：能燒所稱體亦如前，唯外四界。或復有說：唯有火界可名能燒，所稱唯重。

Ru-Yi Translation

The element of sound is excluded from all of this entirely, because it lacks continuity. Regarding "the burner" and "the weighed," there is real scholarly disagreement: some say their essence is likewise as before — only the four external elements. Others say that only the fire element may be named "the burner," and "the weighed" refers only to weight itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Sound drops out of the whole question for a reason consistent with everything already said about it: cutting, burning, and weighing all require a persisting mass of matter to act on, and sound, arising and ceasing moment by moment with no continuous body to it, was never a candidate. The genuine disagreement that follows is worth noticing precisely because the treatise does not resolve it: one view keeps burning and weighing symmetrical with cutting — any of the four external elements can serve as agent or patient — while a stricter view insists that only fire, specifically, deserves to be called "the burner" (since burning just is what heat does), and only weight, specifically, is what a scale actually measures (not colour, smell, or taste). Rather than declare a winner, the treatise simply records both positions and moves on — one of several moments in this fascicle where a live scholarly dispute is preserved rather than papered over.

49. How a Dharma Comes to Be: Ripened, Nourished, or Neither

A new, dense verse opens five classifications at once — where a dharma comes from turns out to matter as much as what it is. This section unpacks the first two: karmic ripening and nourishment.

Chinese Source

十八界中幾異熟生、幾所長養、幾等流性、幾有實事、幾一剎那？頌曰：內五有熟養，聲無異熟生，八無礙等流，亦異熟生性，餘三實唯法，剎那唯後三。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the eighteen elements, how many are produced by karmic ripening, how many are nourished, how many are of the nature of homogeneous outflow, how many are real, substantial things, and how many exist for only a single moment? A verse states: the internal five have both ripened and nourished instances; sound has none produced by ripening; eight, being non-resistant, are of homogeneous-outflow nature, and also have the nature of being ripening-produced; of the rest, real substance belongs only within the dharma element; existing for a single moment belongs only to the last three.

論曰：內五即是眼等五界。有異熟生及所長養無等流者，離異熟生及所長養，無別性故。異熟因所生名異熟生，如牛所駕車名曰牛車，略去中言故作是說。或所造業至得果時變而能熟，故名異熟。果從彼生，名異熟生。

Prose: "The internal five" are the eye and the other four elements. They have instances produced by karmic ripening and instances that are nourished, but none of homogeneous outflow, because apart from the ripened and the nourished they have no separate nature. What is produced by a ripening-cause is named "ripening-produced" — just as a cart drawn by an ox is called an "ox-cart," the middle term simply dropped. Or: when a created karmic act reaches the time of bearing its fruit, it transforms and is able to ripen — hence it is named "ripening." The effect that arises from it is named "ripening-produced."

如契經說：今六觸處，應知即是昔所造業。飲食資助眠睡等持勝緣所益，名所長養。有說：梵行亦能長養。此唯無損，非別有益。長養相續常能護持異熟相續，猶如外郭防援內城。

As the sūtras state: "The six bases of contact present now should be understood as precisely what was created by past karma." That which is benefited by the supportive conditions of food and drink, sleep, and meditative absorption is named "nourished." Some say: pure conduct can likewise nourish. In reply: this only prevents decline; it provides no separate benefit of its own. The nourished continuum constantly protects and sustains the ripened continuum, just as an outer wall guards and defends an inner city.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The five physical faculties are, quite literally, made of two things at once: a portion that is the direct ripened result of past karma (the sūtra citation is blunt about this — the sense-faculties you have right now are exactly what earlier karma produced), and a portion that is sustained moment to moment by food, sleep, and meditative absorption, the way any living body needs ongoing input to keep functioning. The city-wall simile is exact: the karmically-ripened part is the inner city, the precious thing actually being protected, while the nourished part is the outer wall that takes the day-to-day wear and keeps the inner part safe. One school's claim that celibate practice also "nourishes" the body is politely declined — the treatise allows that it prevents harm, but refuses to call mere harm-prevention a positive nourishment in its own right, a fine but real distinction it insists on holding.

50. Sound's Special Case: The Buddha's Voice

Sound turns out to be the one element that resists the pattern just given, and the exception opens onto a genuine doctrinal problem: is the Buddha's famously beautiful voice a karmic result, or not?

Chinese Source

聲有等流及所長養無異熟生。所以者何？隨欲轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sound has instances of homogeneous outflow and instances that are nourished, but none produced by karmic ripening. Why is this? Because it turns according to one's own will.

若爾，不應《施設論》說善修遠離麁惡語故，感得大士梵音聲相。有說：聲屬第五傳故，雖由彼生而非異熟。

If that is so, one might object: then it should not be, as the Prajñaptiśāstra states, that by thoroughly cultivating the abandonment of coarse and evil speech, one obtains, as a result, the great being's mark of a Brahmā-voice. Some say: sound belongs to the fifth stage of transmission; although it arises from that karma, it is not itself a ripened result.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reasoning for excluding sound from karmic ripening is simple: unlike your eye or ear, which you did not choose and cannot switch off, speech happens when and how you decide to speak — it "turns according to one's own will," and karmic ripening, by definition, is not a matter of present choice. The objection this invites is a serious one: Buddhist tradition holds that the Buddha's extraordinarily beautiful voice — one of the classical marks of a great being — is explicitly said to be a result of past good karma (specifically, of abandoning harsh speech). If sound can never be a ripened result, how can a scripture say exactly that?

The resolution preserves both claims by adding a chain of intermediate steps, spelled out in the next section: karma does not produce the beautiful voice directly as its ripened fruit. Sound is said to belong to the fifth stage of a causal transmission running from that karma — several steps removed from the karma itself, not its immediate, literal ripening. This lets the treatise keep its general rule (sound itself is never literally vipāka) while still honoring the sūtra's plain statement that good conduct produces the mark of a beautiful voice, several steps removed rather than directly.

51. The Chain from Karma to a Spoken Sound

The treatise now spells out the very chain of production it just invoked — the full sequence of steps that stands between an act of karma and the sound eventually heard.

Chinese Source

謂從彼業生諸大種，從諸大種緣擊發聲。謂彼業生異熟大種，從此傳生長養大種，此復傳生等流大種，此乃生聲。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is: from that karma, the great elements are produced; from those great elements, through the condition of striking, sound is emitted. That karma produces ripened great elements; from these, in transmission, nourished great elements are produced; these, in further transmission, produce homogeneous-outflow great elements; and only then does this produce sound.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Spelled out, the chain runs: karma ripens directly into great elements (the physical basis of the vocal organs); those ripened elements, sustained moment to moment, produce nourished great elements; those, continuing on, produce homogeneous-outflow great elements; and only this last stage, when actually struck into motion by an act of speaking, produces the sound itself. The Buddha's beautiful voice is therefore several links removed from the karma of abandoning harsh speech — real, and genuinely caused by that karma, but not its immediate ripened fruit the way an eye or ear is. This is the treatise's general method in miniature: rather than let a single word like "produced by karma" cover every case loosely, it insists on naming exactly which link in the causal chain a given effect actually occupies.

52. Why Feeling Is Not Like Sound, and the Eight Mental Elements

An objector presses the "several steps removed" reasoning to a place the treatise cannot accept, before the section closes with the technical definitions of homogeneous outflow and ripening, and why the eight mental elements are never "nourished."

Chinese Source

若爾，身受從業所生大種生故，應非異熟。若受如聲，便違正理。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, one might object: bodily feeling, since it arises dependent on great elements that are themselves produced by karma, should likewise not be a ripened result. But if feeling were treated like sound in this respect, it would contradict correct principle.

八無礙者，七心法界此有等流異熟生性。同類遍行因所生者是等流性，若異熟因所引生者名異熟生，諸無礙法無積集故非所長養。

"The eight non-resistant" are the seven mind-elements and the dharma element; these have both homogeneous-outflow and ripening-produced natures. What is produced by a homogeneous cause or a pervasive cause is of homogeneous-outflow nature; what is drawn forth and produced by a ripening-cause is named ripening-produced. All non-resistant dharmas, having no accumulation, are for that reason never nourished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection tries to generalize the exact move used to exempt sound from vipāka: if being downstream of karma-produced matter disqualifies sound, shouldn't it disqualify bodily feeling too, since feeling likewise depends on a body that karma produced? The treatise refuses this outright, calling it a violation of correct principle — feeling's status as a direct ripened result is too doctrinally load-bearing to sacrifice for the sake of a tidy analogy (an unawakened being's felt pleasure and pain are, centrally, how karma's ripening is actually experienced). Sound and feeling are not parallel cases: sound genuinely runs through several extra links of transmission before it exists at all, while feeling is the ripening itself, immediate rather than several steps removed.

The closing definitions finally make precise two terms this fascicle has used all along without spelling out: "homogeneous outflow" (等流, niṣyanda) names whatever is produced by a same-kind cause or a pervasive cause — an effect that resembles its cause in kind, the way a wholesome thought tends to give rise to further wholesome thoughts of the same general character; "ripening-produced" names specifically what a ripening-cause draws forth — a genuinely different-in-kind result, the way a moral action ripens into a pleasant or painful experience unlike the action itself. And the eight mental elements are permanently excluded from "nourished" for a reason already established: nourishment by food, sleep, and meditative absorption only applies to matter built up from clustered atoms, and mind, on this system, was never built that way to begin with.

53. Only the Unconditioned Is "Real" — and a Single Moment That Changes Everything

The five-part verse from section 49 closes with its two remaining questions: what counts as genuinely "real," and what exists for only a single, unrepeatable instant. The second answer turns out to describe the exact moment a person first breaks through to the path.

Chinese Source

餘謂餘四：色、香、味、觸，皆通三種，有異熟生、有所長養、有等流性。實唯法者，實謂無為，以堅實故。此法界攝故，唯法界獨名有實。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The rest" means the remaining four: form, smell, taste, and touch — these are all common to all three kinds, having instances that are ripening-produced, nourished, and of homogeneous-outflow nature. "Real substance belongs only to the dharma element" — "real" means the unconditioned, because it is firm and real. Since this is comprised within the dharma element, only the dharma element alone is named as having real substance.

唯此三界有一剎那，謂初無漏苦法忍品，非等流故名一剎那。此說究竟非等流者，餘有為法無非等流。苦法忍相應心名意界、意識界，餘俱起法名為法界。如是已說異熟生等。

Only these three elements have a single-moment instance — namely, the class of the very first uncontaminated moment, the patient acceptance of the knowledge of suffering. Because it is not itself an instance of homogeneous outflow, it is named "single-moment." This is said in the ultimate sense of "not homogeneous outflow," since apart from this, no other conditioned dharma is entirely without homogeneous outflow. The mind associated with the patient acceptance of the knowledge of suffering is named the mind element and the mind-consciousness element; the other dharmas arising together with it are named the dharma element. Thus, ripening-produced and the rest have now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Real substance" here is a narrower, more technical claim than it might first sound — it is not saying that ordinary matter and mind are illusory (dharmas retain their own particular characteristics, as this fascicle established long ago); it is saying that only the unconditioned is "firm and real" in the specific sense of never arising, never ceasing, and never being built up from parts the way every conditioned dharma is. Everything conditioned, however genuinely existent, is caught in the flux of arising and ceasing that this narrower sense of "real" is reserved against.

The single-moment case is the most moving point in this entire section. "The patient acceptance of the knowledge of suffering" (苦法忍, duḥkhe dharmajñāna-kṣānti) is the technical name for the very first moment of uncontaminated insight a practitioner ever has — the instant the path of seeing opens, traditionally marking entry into the stream. It earns the label "single-moment" for a precise reason: "homogeneous outflow" requires a prior moment of the same kind to flow from, and this moment, by definition, has no predecessor — nothing like it has ever occurred before in that person's mind. Every uncontaminated moment that follows it, however similar, counts as flowing from it and so no longer qualifies for this description; only the first one is, in the strictest sense, singular. A chapter that opened with an abstract division of all reality into contaminated and uncontaminated, and has spent fifty-plus sections cross-classifying matter and mind by every conceivable axis, closes this particular thread by locating, with technical precision, the exact instant a being first steps from one side of that division to the other.

54. Do the Eye and Eye-Consciousness Always Arrive Together?

The treatise now asks a question that only makes sense against the cosmology already built up across earlier sections: across the different realms and levels of rebirth, does gaining a sense faculty always mean gaining its consciousness too, and vice versa?

Chinese Source

今應思擇，若有眼界先不成就今得成就亦眼識耶？若眼識界先不成就今得成就亦眼界耶？如是等問，今應略答。頌曰：眼與眼識界，獨俱得非等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now this should be considered: if someone, not previously possessing the eye element, now attains it, does this person also attain the eye-consciousness? If someone, not previously possessing the eye-consciousness element, now attains it, does this person also attain the eye element? Questions of this kind should now be briefly answered. A verse states: the eye and the eye-consciousness elements may be attained singly or together — they are not always matched.

論曰：獨得者，謂或有眼界先不成就今得成就非眼識，謂生欲界漸得眼根，及無色沒生二三四靜慮地時。

Prose: "Attained singly" means: there are cases where the eye element, not previously possessed, is now attained without the eye-consciousness — as when being born in the desire realm and gradually acquiring the eye faculty, or when passing away from the formless realm and being reborn at the level of the second, third, or fourth meditative absorption.

或有眼識先不成就今得成就非眼界，謂生二三四靜慮地眼識現起，及從彼沒生下地時。俱得者，謂或有二界先不成就今得成就，謂無色沒生於欲界及梵世時。

Or there are cases where the eye-consciousness, not previously possessed, is now attained without the eye element — as when, born at the level of the second, third, or fourth meditative absorption, eye-consciousness nevertheless manifests there, and when, passing away from there, one is reborn at a lower stage. "Attained together" means: there are cases where, not previously possessing either element, one now attains both together — as when passing away from the formless realm and being reborn in the desire realm or the Brahmā world.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This question only makes sense once several earlier sections are held together at once: a developing embryo in the desire realm forms its eye faculty gradually, before it can see anything, so the organ is gained well before the consciousness ever fires — one case of "singly." A being who dies formless and is reborn at the second dhyāna or higher regains an eye faculty (formless beings have none at all, as section 39 established) without necessarily gaining active eye-consciousness there, since the higher levels of absorption do not routinely exercise ordinary sight — another case of "singly," in the opposite direction. The reverse also occurs: a being already dwelling at those higher levels can, on occasion, have eye-consciousness manifest without this counting as newly gaining the eye element, since the organ was already there; and when such a being is later reborn at a lower stage, active eye-consciousness resumes without needing to be freshly "gained" as an element either. Only a being crossing the widest possible gap — from the formless realm, which has no body at all, directly into the desire realm or the first dhyāna, both of which fully and immediately exercise the senses — gains both the organ and its active consciousness in the very same transition. We have translated this passage closely to the Chinese; the exact mechanics of eye-consciousness "manifesting" at the higher levels of absorption are a finer point of Abhidharma cosmology than this fascicle spells out in full, and readers wanting the complete technical account should look to the treatise's later, dedicated discussion of rebirth and the realms.

55. Attainment Versus Possession: A Fourfold Case, and an Exercise Left to the Reader

The treatise closes the case fully absent from the previous section, then shifts from "attaining" to the related but distinct question of currently "possessing" — and, having modeled the method once, leaves the remaining combinations for the reader to work out.

Chinese Source

非者俱非，謂除前相。等謂若有成就眼界亦眼識耶？應作四句。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Neither" means neither is attained — whatever falls outside the aforementioned patterns. "And so on" means: if one possesses the eye element, does one also possess the eye-consciousness? A fourfold analysis should be made.

第一句者，謂生二三四靜慮地眼識不起。第二句者，謂生欲界未得眼根及得已失。

The first case: being born at the level of the second, third, or fourth meditative absorption, where eye-consciousness does not arise. The second case: being born in the desire realm, either not yet having attained the eye faculty, or having attained it and then lost it.

第三句者，謂生欲界得眼不失，及生梵世若生二三四靜慮地正見色時。第四句者，謂除前相。如是眼界與色界，眼識與色界得成就等，如理應思。

The third case: being born in the desire realm, having attained the eye faculty without losing it; and also being born in the Brahmā world, or at the level of the second, third, or fourth meditative absorption, at the moment one is directly seeing a form. The fourth case: whatever falls outside the aforementioned patterns. In the same way, the relationships between the eye element and the form element, and between eye-consciousness and the form element, with respect to attainment and possession, should be worked out by the reader according to the same method.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This introduces a distinction Abhidharma keeps carefully separate: "attainment" (得, prāpti) is the specific event of newly acquiring something, while "possession" (成就, samanvāgama) is simply the ongoing state of currently having it, which can persist quietly without any new attainment happening in this moment. The four cases sort beings by whether they currently possess the eye faculty, the eye-consciousness, both, or neither — matching the earlier cosmology precisely: those absorbed at the second dhyāna and above generally possess the organ without the consciousness being active; an early embryo or a blinded person in the desire realm possesses the potential for sight without the organ itself; an ordinary desire-realm being with working eyes, or a higher-level being at the exact moment sight does occur there, possesses both.

The closing line is worth noticing for what it does rather than what it says: having walked through the full method once for the eye and eye-consciousness, the treatise simply instructs the reader to apply the identical reasoning to the eye element paired with the form element, and to eye-consciousness paired with the form element, without spelling out either case explicitly. This is a real pedagogical choice this fascicle makes more than once — teach the pattern thoroughly on one worked example, then trust the reader to extend it, rather than mechanically repeating the same four-case analysis for every possible pair among the eighteen elements.

56. Internal and External — Without a Self to Be "Inside" Of

The treatise closes off the previous topic, then opens a new classification that immediately runs into Buddhism's most basic teaching: if there is no self, what could "internal" possibly mean?

Chinese Source

眼識與色界得成就等如理應思。為攝如是所未說義，是故頌中總復言等。如是已說得成就等。十八界中幾內、幾外？頌曰：內十二眼等，色等六為外。

Ru-Yi Translation

The relationship between eye-consciousness and the element of form, regarding attainment and possession, should be considered according to the same reasoning; the verse's word "and so on" was included precisely to cover such meanings left unstated. Thus, attainment, possession, and the rest have now been explained. Among the eighteen elements, how many are internal, and how many external? A verse states: the internal are twelve — the eye and the rest; form and the rest, six, are external.

論曰：六根、六識十二名內，外謂所餘色等六境。我依名內，外謂此餘。我體既無，內外何有？

Prose: The six faculties and the six consciousnesses — twelve — are named internal; external refers to the remaining six, form and the other objects. "Internal" means what the self depends on; "external" means what remains apart from this. But since the self has no real essence, how can there be an internal and an external at all?

我執依止故，假說心為我。故契經說：由善調伏我，智者得生天。世尊餘處說調伏心，如契經言：應善調伏心，心調能引樂。故但於心假說為我。眼等為此所依親近，故說名內。色等為此所緣踈遠，故說名外。

Because the grasping at a self takes something as its basis, mind is provisionally spoken of as "self." Hence a sūtra states: "By skillfully taming the self, the wise attain birth in heaven." The World-Honored One, elsewhere, spoke of taming the mind, as another sūtra says: "One should skillfully tame the mind; a tamed mind is able to bring about happiness." Hence it is only with respect to the mind that "self" is provisionally spoken of. The eye and the rest, being the intimate support for this provisionally-designated self, are therefore named "internal." Form and the rest, being the distant object this mind takes hold of, are therefore named "external."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The treatise catches its own terminology in a trap and then climbs out of it in real time. "Internal" naturally suggests "belonging to a self" — but Buddhism's most basic teaching is that no such self exists, so the objection writes itself: without a self, what could "internal" possibly be relative to? The answer is not to abandon the word but to relocate what it depends on. Two sūtras are set side by side deliberately: one says taming the self leads to heaven, the other says taming the mind leads to happiness — proof, on this reading, that "self" in ordinary Buddhist teaching is simply a convenient, provisional stand-in for "mind," never a claim that a real, separate self exists. Once that substitution is made, "internal" and "external" fall into place without needing a self at all: the six faculties are "internal" simply because they are mind's own intimate, proximate support, and the six objects are "external" simply because they are what mind reaches toward from a distance. The geography is real; the self it seems to imply is not.

57. Defending the School's Own Name: All Eighteen Exist in All Three Times

A sharp objection tests whether the "child/father" account of the mind element (from section 31) accidentally breaks one of this school's own defining claims — that every element exists across past, present, and future alike.

Chinese Source

若爾，六識應不名內，未至意位非心依故。至意位時不失六識界，未至意位亦非越意相。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, one might object: the six consciousnesses should then not be named "internal," since, before reaching the status of mind, they are not the support for mind. In reply: upon reaching the status of mind, the six consciousness-elements are not thereby lost; and before reaching the status of mind, they are also not outside the character of mind.

若異此者，意界唯應在過去世，六識唯在現在未來，便違自宗許十八界皆通三世。又若未來現在六識無意界相，過去意界亦應不立，相於三世無改易故。已說內外。

If it were otherwise, the mind element would have to exist only in the past, and the six consciousnesses only in the present and future — which would contradict our own school's acceptance that all eighteen elements are common to all three times. Furthermore, if the future and present six consciousnesses do not have the character of the mind element, then the past mind element should likewise not be established, since a dharma's defining character does not change across the three times. Internal and external have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This directly defends the doctrine the school takes its very name from: Sarvāstivāda, "the teaching that everything exists" — specifically, that every dharma exists across past, present, and future, not merely in a fleeting present moment. Section 31's account of the mind element (the same six consciousnesses, named "mind" only in their just-past phase) invites a worry: if manas is specifically the past phase of a consciousness, does that mean the mind element is confined to the past tense while the consciousness-elements themselves are confined to present and future — carving up one whole into three separately-timed pieces? The reply insists this is not so: any of the six consciousnesses, whether presently arising, yet to arise, or having just passed, never stops belonging to the consciousness-elements simply by also, in its just-past phase, counting as the mind element. A second, tighter argument closes the case: since a dharma's defining character is supposed to hold steady across all three times, it cannot be that present and future consciousnesses simply lack "mind" character while the past instance has it — the character must be there all along, or the very premise of three-time existence collapses. Nothing is lost, split off, or reassigned to a different temporal box — which is exactly what the school's foundational claim requires.

58. "Same-Class" and "Counterpart-Class" — Why the Dharma Element Is Unique

A new distinction asks whether a dharma is currently functioning as an object of its proper consciousness, or merely present without being engaged — and one element, uniquely, is claimed to have no exceptions at all.

Chinese Source

十八界中幾是同分、幾彼同分？頌曰：法同分餘二，作不作自業。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the eighteen elements, how many are "same-class," and how many are "counterpart-class"? A verse states: the dharma element is same-class; the rest are of both kinds, according to whether they perform their own function or not.

論曰：法同分者，謂一法界唯是同分。若境與識定為所緣，識於其中已生生法，此所緣境說名同分。

Prose: "The dharma element is same-class" means that the single dharma element is exclusively same-class. If an object serves determinately as the objective support for a consciousness, and a consciousness has already arisen, is arising, or will arise with respect to it, that object, as objective support, is named "same-class."

無一法界不於其中已正當生無邊意識，由諸聖者決定生心，觀一切法皆為無我。彼除自體及俱有法，餘一切法皆為所緣。

There is no instance of the dharma element with respect to which an unlimited range of mind-consciousness has not already arisen, is not arising, or will not arise — because the noble ones definitely generate a mind that contemplates all dharmas as without self. Excluding a given moment's own essence and the dharmas co-arising with it, every other dharma serves as its objective support.

如是所除亦第二念心所緣境，此二念心緣一切境無不周遍，是故法界恒名同分。餘二者，謂餘十七界皆有同分及彼同分。

Even what is thus excluded becomes the objective support of a second moment of mind — these two moments of mind, together, take every object without exception, leaving nothing uncovered. This is why the dharma element is permanently named "same-class." "The rest" means that the remaining seventeen elements each have both same-class and counterpart-class instances.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Same-class" names a dharma actually functioning as the object of its matching consciousness right now — an eye actively seeing, a sound actively heard; "counterpart-class" names the same kind of dharma simply present without that engagement — an eye during dreamless sleep, matter no consciousness happens to be taking as its object at the moment. Seventeen of the eighteen elements have both kinds of instance, since nothing guarantees that every eye or every patch of colour is, at any given time, actually being seen.

The dharma element is the single exception, and the argument for why is elegant: a first moment of noble insight, contemplating all dharmas as without self, can take literally anything as its object except its own present self and whatever arises together with it in that same instant — a mind cannot observe itself in the very act of observing. But a second, following moment of mind can then take that first moment (including its own essence and co-arising factors) as its object in turn. Between any two consecutive moments, nothing is left out: every possible object gets covered by one moment or the other. Since this is always true of any moment whatsoever, no instance of the dharma element ever permanently escapes being an object of mind — which is exactly why, alone among the eighteen elements, it is said to be same-class without exception, never merely "counterpart-class."
