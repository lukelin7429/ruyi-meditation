What Abhidharma Really Means

This page carries fascicle 1's complete opening frame and the start of Chapter 1: the definition of Abhidharma, the aggregate of form's eleven kinds, visible form's twenty kinds of colour and shape, sound's eight kinds, the five aggregates and twelve sense fields, the mind element as consciousness's own immediately-past moment, and why the elements are counted as eighteen rather than seventeen or twelve. Fascicle 1's own conclusion — cross-referencing other sutra enumerations against the five aggregates, twelve sense fields, and eighteen elements — is still being translated; it will be added in the sections that follow before this fascicle closes and fascicle 2 continues.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharmakośa, preserved in the Taishō canon (T1558, vol. 29) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. Leo Pruden's English translation of Louis de La Vallée Poussin's French Abhidharmakośabhāṣyam, and Chinese commentaries such as Yen-pei's (演培) 俱舍論頌講記, are consulted privately to check difficult passages, but neither is reproduced or paraphrased here; the English wording is Ru-Yi's own.

Unlike Ru-Yi's translations of the Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra and the Cheng Weishi Lun, this treatise does not yet follow a single named teacher's lecture transcript for its reading notes. The notes below are Ru-Yi's own, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma scholarship rather than a specific oral commentary — marked plainly as such below, so readers know what they are getting. If a suitable public-domain or licensed commentary source becomes available later, later fascicles may adopt it the way the other treatises have.

1. Homage to the Teacher

Like most Indian śāstras, the treatise opens with a verse of homage before its argument begins.

Chinese Source

諸一切種諸冥滅，拔眾生出生死泥，敬禮如是如理師，對法藏論我當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

He in whom every kind of darkness, of every sort, is extinguished; who draws sentient beings out of the mire of birth and death — to such a teacher, one whose teaching accords with reality, I pay homage. I shall now set forth the treatise that is the Treasury of Abhidharma.

論曰：今欲造論，為顯自師其體尊高超諸聖眾，故先讚德方申敬禮。諸言所表謂佛世尊，此能破闇故稱冥滅。

Prose: Now, wishing to compose this treatise, the author first praises his teacher's virtues before paying homage, in order to show that this teacher's very being is exalted, surpassing all the noble ones. What the verse's words point to is the Buddha, the World-Honored One. Because he is able to destroy darkness, he is called "the extinguisher of darkness."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Darkness" here is the classical gloss for avidyā, ignorance — the root affliction that the whole treatise's analysis of dharmas exists to dispel. Note what the verse actually praises: not power or majesty, but the fact that the Buddha's teaching "accords with reality" (如理). That is the treatise's basic claim in miniature — correct classification of what is real is itself what frees a being from the mire of birth and death, not a separate technique layered on top of it.

2. Abhidharma in the Ultimate Sense

Chapter 1 opens (分別界品第一) with the treatise's central question: what does the word "Abhidharma" actually mean? The answer comes in two stages — first the ultimate sense, then, in the next section, the conventional sense that allows an ordinary book to carry the name.

Chinese Source

淨慧隨行名對法，及能得此諸慧論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pure wisdom, together with what accompanies it, is called Abhidharma — as is the treatise able to bring about this wisdom.

論曰：慧謂擇法，淨謂無漏，淨慧眷屬名曰隨行。如是總說無漏五蘊名為對法，此則勝義阿毘達磨。

Prose: "Wisdom" means the discernment of dharmas. "Pure" means uncontaminated. Pure wisdom's retinue is called "what accompanies it." Thus, stated in general, the uncontaminated five aggregates are named Abhidharma — this is Abhidharma in the ultimate sense.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a striking claim to open on: in the strict sense, Abhidharma is not a book at all. It is a mental event — the direct, uncontaminated (anāsrava) insight of a noble one (ārya) into the true nature of dharmas, together with whatever mental factors necessarily arise alongside any single moment of consciousness (its "retinue," 隨行 — a term the treatise will return to in far more technical detail once it takes up mind and mental factors in later chapters). "The uncontaminated five aggregates" does not mean the ordinary aggregates of form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness that make up any experience; it means those same five categories as they occur within one particular kind of experience — a moment of liberating, undefiled seeing.

3. Abhidharma in the Conventional Sense

Having defined Abhidharma as a state of mind, the treatise now explains how the word can also apply to ordinary, contaminated wisdom, and to a written treatise like this one.

Chinese Source

若說世俗阿毘達磨，即能得此諸慧及論。慧謂得此有漏修慧、思聞生得慧及隨行。論謂傳生無漏慧教。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one speaks of conventional Abhidharma, it is the wisdom and the treatise able to bring about this pure wisdom. "Wisdom" here means the contaminated wisdom of cultivation that gives rise to it, together with the wisdom born of reflection and of learning, and what accompanies them. "Treatise" means the teaching that transmits and gives rise to uncontaminated wisdom.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This draws on the classical triad of wisdom the treatise assumes readers already know: wisdom born of learning (聞, hearing the teaching), wisdom born of reflection (思, thinking it through), and wisdom born of cultivation (修, meditative practice) — all three still contaminated (有漏), still short of the noble one's direct insight. "Conventional Abhidharma" covers both this preparatory wisdom and the verbal teachings — including this very treatise — that transmit it, because both function as provisions on the way to the ultimate, uncontaminated wisdom defined in the previous section. This is how a physical book can honestly be called "Abhidharma," even though Abhidharma, strictly speaking, is a state of mind rather than a text.

4. Why It Is Called "Abhidharma"

The treatise closes this opening movement by explaining the name itself, character by character.

Chinese Source

此諸慧論是彼資糧，故亦得名阿毘達磨。釋此名者，能持自相故名為法。若勝義法唯是涅槃，若法相法通四聖諦，此能對向或能對觀，故稱對法。

Ru-Yi Translation

These forms of wisdom and this treatise are provisions for that ultimate wisdom, and so they too may be called Abhidharma. To explain this name: because it holds its own particular mark, it is called "dharma." If "dharma" is meant in the ultimate sense, this refers only to nirvāṇa; if it is meant as dharma bearing a characteristic, it extends to the Four Noble Truths. Because this wisdom is able to face toward its object, or able to contemplate it directly, it is called Abhidharma — "facing the dharma."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Dharma" is defined here exactly as the treatise will use it throughout its remaining twenty-nine fascicles: whatever "holds its own particular mark" — whatever has a definite, distinguishable nature of its own, whether a physical element, a mental factor, or an unconditioned reality like space or cessation. The etymology then does real philosophical work: dharma in the strict, ultimate sense names nirvāṇa alone, since it is the one reality that needs no further characteristic to secure it; taken more broadly, as "dharma bearing a characteristic," it covers everything analyzed under the Four Noble Truths — suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path. Either way, this wisdom is called "Abhidharma" — facing toward the dharma — because it takes one or the other as its direct object of contemplation. This is the charter for the entire treatise: to enumerate, one by one, every dharma that bears its own mark.

5. Why the Treatise Is Called "Kośa" (Treasury)

Abhidharma itself has now been explained. The treatise turns next to its own title.

Chinese Source

已釋對法。何故此論名對法藏？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Abhidharma has now been explained. Why is this treatise named the Treasury of Abhidharma? A verse states:

攝彼勝義依彼故，此立對法俱舍名。

Because it gathers in that work's essential meaning, and depends upon it, this treatise is established under the name Abhidharmakośa.

論曰：由彼對法論中勝義入此攝故，此得藏名。或此依彼、從彼引生，是彼所藏，故亦名藏。是故此論名對法藏。

Prose: Because the essential meaning within that Abhidharma treatise is gathered into and contained by this one, this work receives the name "treasury." Alternatively, because this work depends upon that one, is drawn out from it, and is that work's stored essence, it is likewise called a "treasury." For this reason, this treatise is named the Treasury of Abhidharma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"That Abhidharma treatise" is the Mahāvibhāṣā (大毘婆沙論) — the two-hundred-fascicle Sarvāstivāda compendium already flagged as a future addition to this section of the site. The etymology is deliberately given two ways, and both are kept rather than resolved into one: kośa ("sheath" or "treasury") names this shorter treatise either as the container that holds the Mahāvibhāṣā's essential meaning in condensed form, or as the thing itself contained within — drawn out from, and dependent on, that larger work. Either reading makes the same practical point: this treatise is not an independent invention but a distillation of the older, vaster compendium, meant to make its essential meaning usable.

6. Why Did the Buddha Teach Abhidharma At All?

Having named itself, the treatise raises a harder question: on what grounds can this whole technical enterprise be attributed to the Buddha in the first place?

Chinese Source

何因說彼阿毘達磨？誰復先說阿毘達磨，而今造論恭敬解釋？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

For what reason was Abhidharma taught? And who first taught Abhidharma, such that this treatise is now composed to explain it with reverence? A verse states:

若離擇法定無餘，能滅諸惑勝方便，由惑世間漂有海，因此傳佛說對法。

Apart from the discernment of dharmas, there is certainly no other supreme means able to extinguish the afflictions. Because the afflictions cause the world to drift in the ocean of existence, it has been transmitted, for this reason, that the Buddha taught Abhidharma.

論曰：若離擇法，無勝方便能滅諸惑。諸惑能令世間漂轉生死大海，因此傳佛說彼對法，欲令世間得擇法故。離說對法，弟子不能於諸法相如理簡擇。

Prose: Apart from the discernment of dharmas, there is no supreme means able to extinguish the afflictions. The afflictions cause the world to drift and turn in the great ocean of birth-and-death; for this reason it has been transmitted that the Buddha taught Abhidharma, wishing to let the world attain the discernment of dharmas. Without the teaching of Abhidharma, disciples would be unable to rightly discern the characteristics of dharmas in accordance with reality.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the treatise's answer to a question any scholastic tradition has to face: why treat these technical enumerations as the Buddha's own word, rather than a later invention? The argument here is not historical but practical — nothing except the discernment of dharmas (擇法, prajñā operating precisely on what is real) can uproot the afflictions that keep beings adrift in the "ocean of existence" (有海, bhavasāgara). Since that discerning function is indispensable, and since the Buddha's purpose was to make it available to disciples who could not reach it unaided, the teaching that serves that function is attributed to him — whatever its later editorial history turns out to have been. This is the premise the next section builds directly on.

7. How Abhidharma Was Compiled

The treatise now gives its own account of Abhidharma's textual history — scattered teaching, later gathered into system.

Chinese Source

然佛世尊處處散說阿毘達磨，大德迦多衍尼子等諸大聲聞結集安置。猶如大德法救所集無常品等鄔拕南頌，毘婆沙師傳說如此。

Ru-Yi Translation

However, the Buddha, the World-Honored One, taught Abhidharma scattered here and there across many occasions; it was the great elder Kātyāyanīputra and other great śrāvakas who gathered it together and arranged it into order. This is comparable to how the great elder Dharmatrāta compiled the verse-summaries (udāna) on impermanence and other themes — so the Vaibhāṣika masters have transmitted and explained it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two figures anchor this account. Kātyāyanīputra (迦多衍尼子) is the traditional compiler of the Jñānaprasthāna (發智論), the root treatise that the Mahāvibhāṣā was later composed to comment on at enormous length — so this single sentence quietly explains the whole chain of texts standing behind the Kośa itself: scattered teaching, into the Jñānaprasthāna, into the Mahāvibhāṣā, into this treatise's condensation of it. Dharmatrāta (法救), compiler of verse-anthologies on impermanence and similar themes (material related to what survives as the Udānavarga), is offered as a parallel case the audience would already accept: an named disciple organizing the Buddha's own scattered sayings into a usable, systematic form is not a new or suspect practice — it is exactly how several respected parts of the canon came to exist.

8. What Makes a Dharma "Contaminated"

The frame is now complete. The treatise turns to its first real content: dividing every dharma that exists into two kinds. One long root verse governs this and the next three sections.

Chinese Source

何法名為彼所簡擇，因此傳佛說對法耶？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

What dharmas, then, are the ones discerned — on whose account it has been transmitted that the Buddha taught Abhidharma? A verse states:

有漏無漏法，除道餘有為，於彼漏隨增，故說名有漏。無漏謂道諦，及三種無為，謂虛空二滅，此中空無礙。擇滅謂離繫，隨繫事各別，畢竟礙當生，別得非擇滅。

Dharmas are contaminated or uncontaminated. All conditioned dharmas except the path are contaminated, because the contaminants increase within them. The uncontaminated are the truth of the path, and the three unconditioned — space and the two cessations. Of these, space is non-obstruction. Cessation through discernment is separation from bondage, differing according to each distinct bound-to affliction. What permanently obstructs a future arising is a separately attained cessation not through discernment.

論曰：說一切法略有二種，謂有漏、無漏。有漏法云何？謂除道諦，餘有為法。所以者何？諸漏於中等隨增故。緣滅道諦，諸漏雖生而不隨增，故非有漏。不隨增義，隨眠品中自當顯說。

Prose: All dharmas, it is said, are in brief of two kinds — contaminated and uncontaminated. What are contaminated dharmas? All conditioned dharmas except the truth of the path. Why is this? Because the contaminants increase equally within them. Although contaminants can arise by taking the truths of cessation and of the path as their object, they do not thereby increase there — so these are not contaminated. The meaning of "not increasing" will be explained in its own right, in the chapter on the Latent Afflictions.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Contaminant" (漏, āsrava) is the treatise's word for what keeps ordinary experience flowing out into further rebirth — the root defilements. The definition of "contaminated" here is given entirely by exclusion: every single conditioned dharma is contaminated except the truth of the path, which is itself conditioned (it is a set of cultivated factors, not the unconditioned goal) yet does not count as contaminated. The reason given is precise rather than sentimental: it is not that afflictions can never take the path or cessation as an object of thought — an unawakened person can certainly still cling to or misunderstand the idea of nirvāṇa — but that doing so does not cause the afflictions to increase, the way ordinary objects of craving or aversion do. That distinction between "arising toward" something and "increasing because of" it is a technical point the treatise sets aside for its later chapter on the latent afflictions (anuśaya), rather than resolving here.

9. The Three Unconditioned Dharmas

Having defined the contaminated, the treatise now defines the uncontaminated: the path, plus three dharmas that are never produced by causes and conditions at all.

Chinese Source

已辯有漏。無漏云何？謂道聖諦及三無為。何等為三？虛空、二滅。二滅者何？擇、非擇滅。此虛空等三種無為及道聖諦，名無漏法。所以者何？諸漏於中不隨增故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contaminated dharmas have now been distinguished. What are uncontaminated dharmas? The noble truth of the path, together with the three unconditioned. What are the three? Space, and the two cessations. What are the two cessations? Cessation through discernment, and cessation not through discernment. Space and these two cessations, together with the noble truth of the path — these are called uncontaminated dharmas. Why is this? Because the contaminants do not increase within them.

於略所說三無為中，虛空但以無礙為性，由無障故色於中行。

Among the three unconditioned briefly stated above, space has non-obstruction as its nature; because it offers no obstruction, matter moves freely within it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Unconditioned" (無為, asaṃskṛta) names whatever stands entirely outside the chain of arising and ceasing by causes and conditions — never produced, and so never destroyed. This is a much smaller category than "uncontaminated": the path is uncontaminated but still conditioned (it has to be cultivated), while space and the two cessations are both uncontaminated and unconditioned. Space is defined as simply as possible — it is not a substance in its own right, but the sheer absence of obstruction, which is exactly what lets physical matter occupy and pass through it. Sarvāstivāda treats this "nothing in the way" as a real, distinct dharma in its own right, not a mere verbal convenience — a point later Buddhist schools, including the Mahāyāna critics this treatise's own author would go on to side with, found worth arguing over.

10. Cessation Through Discernment (擇滅)

The treatise now defines the first of the two cessations: the liberation an ārya actually attains by directly discerning the Four Noble Truths.

Chinese Source

擇滅即以離繫為性，諸有漏法遠離繫縛證得解脫，名為擇滅。擇謂簡擇，即慧差別，各別簡擇四聖諦故。擇力所得滅名為擇滅，如牛所駕車名曰牛車，略去中言故作是說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Cessation through discernment has separation from bondage as its nature: when contaminated dharmas are separated from their bonds and liberation is realized, this is called cessation through discernment. "Discernment" means discriminating analysis — a particular kind of wisdom — because it discerns each of the Four Noble Truths individually. The cessation attained through the power of discernment is called "cessation through discernment," just as a cart drawn by an ox is called an "ox-cart": the middle term is simply dropped, and the name is formed this way.

一切有漏法同一擇滅耶？不爾。云何？隨繫事別，謂隨繫事量，離繫事亦爾。若不爾者，於證見苦所斷煩惱滅時，應證一切所斷諸煩惱滅。若如是者，修餘對治則為無用。

Is there one single cessation through discernment shared by all contaminated dharmas alike? No. Why not? Because it differs according to each distinct bound-to affliction — that is, the separation from bondage matches the bound-to matter, case by case. Were it not so, then at the very moment one realized the cessation of the afflictions cut off by seeing suffering, one would thereby realize the cessation of every affliction still to be cut off. If that were the case, cultivating the remaining antidotes for the other afflictions would serve no purpose at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ox-cart etymology is doing real work, not just wordplay: it flags that "cessation through discernment" is shorthand for "the cessation attained by the power of discernment" — the cessation itself is not an act of discerning, it is what discerning produces. The more important claim follows immediately: liberation is not one single, undifferentiated event. Sarvāstivāda holds that each category of affliction has its own distinct cessation, won only by directly discerning the particular truth that undoes it — which is exactly why the path is cultivated in stages, working through one class of affliction at a time, rather than dissolving in a single stroke of insight. (The treatise adds one further, highly compressed technical objection-and-reply here — on whether these many cessations share a common "same-kind cause" — that later chapters unpack in far more technical detail than fascicle 1 does; we have translated it as literally as the Chinese allows rather than over-interpreting it this early.)

11. Cessation Not Through Discernment (非擇滅)

The treatise closes this section with the second, very different kind of cessation — one attained without any act of insight at all.

Chinese Source

已說擇滅。永礙當生得非擇滅，謂能永礙未來法生。得滅異前，名非擇滅。得不因擇，但由闕緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Cessation through discernment has now been explained. The attainment that permanently obstructs a future dharma from arising is cessation not through discernment — that is, it is able to permanently obstruct the arising of a dharma in the future. This attainment of cessation differs from the one just described, and so is called "cessation not through discernment," because the attainment comes about not through discernment, but simply through the absence of a needed condition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Where cessation-through-discernment is something a practitioner actively achieves, cessation-not-through-discernment happens by default: a dharma is permanently barred from ever arising simply because the conditions it would need never come together — no wisdom required. The treatise gives its own illustration of exactly this in the next section.

12. An Example, and a Fourfold Analysis

The treatise now gives a concrete case of cessation not through discernment, then sorts all dharmas into four groups by which of the two cessations, if either, applies to them.

Chinese Source

如眼與意專一色時，餘色聲香味觸等謝。緣彼境界五識身等住未來世畢竟不生，由彼不能緣過去境，緣不具故得非擇滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

For example: when the eye and the mental faculty attend exclusively to one form, the other forms, sounds, smells, tastes, and tangibles [that could have been sensed in that same instant] have already passed. The bodies of the five consciousnesses that would take those objects as their objects, remaining in the future, are thereby permanently prevented from arising — because they cannot take a past object as a present object, and so, the condition being incomplete, cessation not through discernment is attained.

於法得滅應作四句。或於諸法唯得擇滅，謂諸有漏過現生法。或於諸法唯非擇滅，謂不生法無漏有為。或於諸法俱得二滅，謂彼不生諸有漏法。或於諸法不得二滅，謂諸無漏過現生法。

Regarding the attainment of cessation with respect to dharmas, a fourfold analysis should be made. For some dharmas, only cessation through discernment is attained — namely, contaminated dharmas of the past and present that have already arisen. For some dharmas, only cessation not through discernment is attained — namely, uncontaminated conditioned dharmas that have not arisen. For some dharmas, both cessations are attained together — namely, contaminated dharmas that will never arise. For some dharmas, neither cessation is attained — namely, uncontaminated dharmas of the past and present that have already arisen.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The example turns on a strict rule this treatise holds throughout: a sense-consciousness can only take a present object, never a past one. The instant attention settles on one form, every other possible sense-object of that same instant has already slipped into the past — so the consciousnesses that would have known them are foreclosed forever, not suppressed by anything, just missed by timing. The fourfold analysis that follows is worth checking against the logic itself: an already-arisen affliction can only be cut off by discernment (it already exists, so "never arising" doesn't apply to it); an uncontaminated dharma that never arises only gets cessation-not-through-discernment (there is no affliction there for wisdom to cut off); a contaminated dharma that is cut off before it ever gets the chance to arise qualifies for both at once; and an uncontaminated dharma that has already arisen — an actual moment of pure wisdom, say — needs neither, since it was never afflicted and it did in fact occur.

13. The Many Names of "Conditioned"

With the three unconditioned dharmas fully explained, the treatise turns back to the conditioned side of its opening division, and asks what "conditioned" actually means.

Chinese Source

如是已說三種無為。前說除道，餘有為法是名有漏。何謂有為？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

The three unconditioned have now been fully explained. It was said earlier that all conditioned dharmas except the path are named contaminated. What, then, is "conditioned"? A verse states:

又諸有為法，謂色等五蘊，亦世路言依，有離有事等。

Conditioned dharmas are the five aggregates, beginning with form. They are also called the road of time, the basis of speech, the separated, and the concerned thing, among other names.

論曰：色等五蘊，謂初色蘊乃至識蘊。如是五法具攝有為，眾緣聚集共所作故。無有少法一緣所生，是彼類故。未來無妨，如乳如薪。

Prose: "The five aggregates beginning with form" means the aggregate of form first, extending through to the aggregate of consciousness. These five dharmas together comprise the whole of the conditioned, because every one of them arises only as the joint product of many conditions gathering together — there is no dharma whatsoever produced from a single condition alone, and these five are of that same kind. This holds without exception even for dharmas not yet arisen, just as with milk or with firewood.

此有為法亦名世路，已行正行當行性故，或為無常所吞食故。

This conditioned dharma is also called "the road of time," because its nature is to have already moved, to be moving now, and to be about to move — or because it is devoured by impermanence.

或名言依，言謂語言，此所依者即名俱義。如是言依具攝一切有為諸法。若不爾者，應違《品類足論》所說。彼說言依十八界攝。

It is also called "the basis of speech": "speech" means spoken language, and whatever speech depends on to refer to something is exactly what this name indicates. In this way, "the basis of speech" comprises every conditioned dharma without exception — were it otherwise, this would contradict what is stated in the Prakaraṇapāda, which holds that "the basis of speech" is subsumed under the eighteen elements.

或名有離，離謂永離，即是涅槃，一切有為有彼離故。或名有事，以有因故，事是因義。毘婆沙師傳說如此。

It is also called "the separated," because "separation" means permanent separation — precisely nirvāṇa — and every conditioned dharma stands in relation to that separation. It is also called "the concerned thing," because it has a cause — here "thing" carries the sense of "cause." This is what the Vaibhāṣika masters have transmitted and explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two claims anchor this verse. First, the five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness — are said to exhaust the conditioned without remainder: nothing arisen from causes and conditions falls outside one of these five. Second, no dharma is ever produced by a single condition alone; "milk" and "firewood" gesture at the classical point that even the most ordinary transformations (milk into curd, wood into fire) require a whole convergence of conditions, never one cause acting by itself — the same principle the treatise will apply, far more technically, to every dharma it analyzes from here on.

The remaining synonyms each isolate one further fact about anything conditioned. "The road of time" names its exposure to the three times — having moved, moving, about to move — which the unconditioned, by definition, never does. "The basis of speech" claims that whatever ordinary language can name is, in the end, a conditioned dharma (the treatise even checks this claim against another Abhidharma text, the Prakaraṇapāda, rather than asserting it unsupported — one of several places in this fascicle where one treatise is checked against another). "The separated" points forward to nirvāṇa as the destiny every conditioned dharma stands in relation to, whether or not it is ever actually realized. "The concerned thing" is the plainest of the five: to be conditioned just is to have a cause. One short technical phrase in the "basis of speech" explanation — rendered here as "exactly what this name indicates" — compresses a grammatical point about how the compound term itself is formed; we have kept the translation close to the literal wording rather than resolving that compression with more confidence than the single clause supports.

14. Why the Contaminated Are Called "Aggregates of Grasping"

Just as the previous section named the conditioned by several epithets, the treatise now does the same for the contaminated specifically — starting with its most technical name, "aggregates of grasping" (取蘊, upādāna-skandha).

Chinese Source

如是等類是有為法差別眾名，於此所說有為法中。頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Such, and others of this kind, are the various names distinguishing conditioned dharmas. Among these conditioned dharmas just discussed, a verse states:

有漏名取蘊，亦說為有諍，及苦集世間，見處三有等。

The contaminated are named the aggregates of grasping. They are also said to be strife, suffering, origin, the world, the abode of views, and the three states of existence, among other names.

論曰：此何所立？謂立取蘊亦名為蘊。或有唯蘊而非取蘊，謂無漏行。煩惱名取，蘊從取生故名取蘊，如草穅火。或蘊屬取故名取蘊，如帝王臣。或蘊生取故名取蘊，如花果樹。

Prose: What does this establish? It establishes that the aggregates of grasping may also simply be called "aggregates" — but there are aggregates that are merely aggregates and not aggregates of grasping, namely the uncontaminated conditioned factors [the path]. "Grasping" names the afflictions. The aggregates are called "aggregates of grasping" because they arise from grasping, just as fire made from grass and husks is called "grass-and-husk fire." Or: because the aggregates belong to grasping, just as ministers belonging to a king are called "the king's ministers." Or: because the aggregates give rise to grasping, just as a tree bearing flowers and fruit is called "the flower-and-fruit tree."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three similes are not decorative — each names a genuinely different relationship, and the treatise is careful to give all three rather than settle on one. Grass-and-husk fire is named for what it arises from (a causal relation); a king's ministers are named for what they belong to (a relation of possession); a flower-and-fruit tree is named for what it produces (a relation of effect). Ordinary contaminated aggregates satisfy all three at once with respect to grasping — they arise from it, belong to it, and generate more of it — which is exactly why the treatise draws its sharp exception first: the path itself is a set of conditioned aggregates too, but since it neither arises from grasping, belongs to it, nor produces it, it is an aggregate that is never an "aggregate of grasping."

15. Six More Names for the Contaminated

The verse's remaining epithets are each explained in turn, one short reason apiece.

Chinese Source

此有漏法亦名有諍，煩惱名諍，觸動善品故、損害自他故、諍隨增故，名為有諍。

Ru-Yi Translation

These contaminated dharmas are also named "strife": the afflictions are named "strife" because they disturb and stir up wholesome qualities, because they harm both oneself and others, and because strife increases along with them — hence they are named "strife."

猶如有漏亦名為苦，違聖心故。亦名為集，能招苦故。亦名世間，可毀壞故、有對治故。亦名見處，見住其中隨增眠故。亦名三有，有因有依三有攝故。

Likewise, the contaminated are also named "suffering," because they run contrary to the mind of the noble ones. They are also named "origin," because they are able to bring about suffering. They are also named "the world," because they are subject to destruction, and because they admit of an antidote. They are also named "the abode of views," because views abide within them, and the latent tendencies increase there. They are also named "the three states of existence," because, having their cause and their basis there, they are comprised within the three states of existence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Read together, these six names amount to a portrait of contaminated existence from six angles rather than six separate facts. "Strife" and "the abode of views" locate the trouble inside the mind — the afflictions actively fight wholesome qualities and harm others, and wrong views cling to exactly these dharmas as their object. "Suffering" and "origin" are simply the first two of the Four Noble Truths, named here as properties of the same contaminated aggregates already under discussion, not as separate items. "The world" makes a point worth noticing: it is called destructible, yes, but the text adds "because it admits of an antidote" as an equal reason for the name — the very fact that contaminated existence can be countered is treated as part of what makes it "the world" in the first place. "The three states of existence" (三有, the realms of desire, form, and formlessness) is simply the total territory all of this covers: every contaminated dharma has its cause and its support somewhere within one of these three.

16. The Aggregate of Form: Its Eleven Kinds

The opening frame is complete. The treatise now begins the real work of Chapter 1: defining each of the five aggregates in turn, starting with form.

Chinese Source

如上所言，色等五蘊名有為法。色蘊者何？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

As stated above, the five aggregates beginning with form are named conditioned dharmas. What is the aggregate of form? A verse states:

色者唯五根，五境及無表。

Form is only the five faculties, the five objects, and the unmanifest.

論曰：言五根者，所謂眼、耳、鼻、舌、身根。言五境者，即是眼等五根境界，所謂色、聲、香、味、所觸。及無表者，謂無表色。唯依此量立色蘊名。此中先應說五根相。

Prose: "The five faculties" means the eye-faculty, ear-faculty, nose-faculty, tongue-faculty, and body-faculty. "The five objects" are precisely the objects of these five faculties — namely form, sound, smell, taste, and the tangible. "And the unmanifest" means unmanifest form. It is solely on the basis of these eleven that the name "aggregate of form" is established. Among these, the characteristics of the five faculties should be explained first.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Eleven items, one aggregate: the five physical sense faculties, the five kinds of object they each take, and one further category — "unmanifest form" (無表色, avijñapti-rūpa) — that is not perceived by any of the five senses at all. Unmanifest form is one of Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma's most distinctive and most argued-over categories: it names a subtle, continuing physical residue produced by an act of body or speech (most importantly, taking a precept), which persists and counts as real matter even though nothing about it can be seen, heard, or touched by anyone. The treatise flags here that it will explain this difficult category "later" rather than now — a promise fascicle 1 will need more sections to keep. First, as this line says, come the five ordinary faculties.

17. The Five Faculties Are "Pure Form"

The treatise gives one general definition covering all five sense faculties at once, before treating each individually.

Chinese Source

彼識依淨色，名眼等五根。

Ru-Yi Translation

The pure form on which those consciousnesses depend is named the eye-faculty and the other four faculties.

論曰：彼謂前說色等五境，識即色、聲、香、味、觸識。彼識所依五種淨色，如其次第，應知即是眼等五根。

Prose: "Those" refers to the five objects mentioned before — form and the rest. "Consciousness" means the consciousness of form, of sound, of smell, of taste, and of the tangible. The five kinds of pure form on which those consciousnesses depend — respectively, in the same order — should be understood as precisely the eye-faculty and the other four faculties.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Pure form" (淨色, later technical Sanskrit rūpaprasāda) names a refined, imperceptible physical substance distinct from the visible eyeball or audible ear — the eye-faculty proper is this subtle matter, not the organ an anatomist would point to. Readers of Ru-Yi's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra translation will recognize this exact term and the same basic account of the eye-faculty ("the eye is the pure form... on which the eye-consciousness depends") from that treatise's own opening fascicle. That is not a coincidence: Yogācāra inherited a great deal of its technical vocabulary for the physical senses directly from Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma like this treatise, even where the two schools disagree sharply on deeper questions of what ultimately exists. The method here is exactly parallel to how fascicle 1 has already handled several terms — name the general principle first (all five faculties are pure form, matched one-to-one with the five kinds of sense-consciousness), then take up each faculty's specific characteristics one at a time, starting with the eye.

18. Visible Form: Twenty Kinds of Colour and Shape

Rather than take up each faculty's own characteristics next, the treatise moves to the five objects, opening with a single dense verse that gives the exact count of subtypes for all five at once. This section unpacks only the first: visible form, the eye's object.

Chinese Source

色二或二十，聲唯有八種，味六香四種，觸十一為性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Form is two, or twenty. Sound is only eight kinds. Taste is six, smell four kinds. Touch has eleven as its nature.

論曰：言色二者，一顯、二形。顯色有四：青、黃、赤、白，餘顯是此四色差別。形色有八，謂長為初不正為後。或二十者，即此色處復說二十，謂青、黃、赤、白、長、短、方、圓、高、下、正、不正、雲、煙、塵、霧、影、光、明、闇。

Prose: "Form is two" means: first, colour; second, shape. Colour has four — blue, yellow, red, and white; every other colour is a variation of these four. Shape has eight — long as the first, through irregular as the last. As for "or twenty": this same sense-field of visible form is also stated as twenty, namely: blue, yellow, red, white, long, short, square, round, high, low, regular, irregular, cloud, smoke, dust, mist, shadow, light, brightness, and darkness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The count checks out exactly: four basic colours plus eight shapes plus eight further derived colours (cloud, smoke, dust, mist, shadow, light, brightness, darkness) makes twenty. The logic is explicit — everything beyond blue, yellow, red, and white is treated as a variation of one of those four, not an independent colour in its own right, so "twenty" and "two" are not competing counts but two levels of the same classification: two broad categories (colour, shape) unpacked into twenty specific items.

Readers of Ru-Yi's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra translation will recognize most of this list — that treatise's account of the eye-consciousness's object names nearly the same colours and shapes. One difference is worth flagging: the Yogācārabhūmi's list includes "the single colour of open sky" (空一顯色) as one of its colours, while this twenty-item count does not. The Kośa takes up exactly that question next — whether the sky's colour deserves its own place in the list at all.

19. Defining the Harder Colours, and a Minority View on the Sky

Having listed all twenty items by name, the treatise now defines the ones that are not self-evident — two of the eight shapes, and five of the eight derived colours — before recording a dissenting view.

Chinese Source

此中正者謂形平等，形不平等名為不正，地水氣騰說之為霧，日焰名光，月星火藥寶珠電等諸焰名明，障光明生於中餘色可見名影，翻此為闇。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these: "regular" means a shape that is even; an uneven shape is named "irregular." The rising vapour of earth and water is called "mist." The sun's radiance is named "light." The radiance of the moon, stars, fire, medicinal herbs, jewels, lightning, and the like is named "brightness." Where light is obstructed and something arises within it such that other colours can still be seen, this is named "shadow"; the reverse of this is "darkness."

有餘師說，空一顯色，第二十一。

Some other masters say that the single colour of open space is itself a twenty-first kind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice what the treatise leaves out of this list of definitions: cloud, smoke, and dust get no gloss at all, presumably because Vasubandhu judged them self-explanatory, while shadow, light, brightness, and darkness — genuinely harder to pin down, since none of them is a pigment in the ordinary sense — each get a careful causal definition. "Shadow" in particular is defined relationally rather than as a simple absence of light: it is what appears when light is blocked but not so completely that other colours vanish from view; total blockage, with nothing else visible, is "darkness" instead.

The dissenting view that follows — that the sky's own colour deserves a twenty-first slot — is simply recorded, not argued with or refuted here; this fascicle gives no verdict either way. It is worth remembering as the reason Ru-Yi's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra translation and this one do not quite line up on this single point: different Abhidharma lineages preserved different resolutions to exactly this disagreement.

20. Sound: Eight Kinds

Visible form is now fully explained. The treatise moves to the ear's object, sound, and unpacks it by three independent yes-or-no distinctions that combine into eight kinds total.

Chinese Source

已說色處，當說聲處。聲唯八種，謂有執受或無執受大種為因，及有情名、非有情名差別為四，此復可意及不可意差別成八。

Ru-Yi Translation

Visible form has now been explained; sound will now be explained. Sound is only eight kinds: caused by appropriated great elements, or caused by non-appropriated great elements, together with the further distinction into the designation of a sentient being and not the designation of a sentient being, makes four. These four, further divided by being agreeable or disagreeable, make eight.

執受大種為因聲者，謂言手等所發音聲。風林河等所發音聲，名無執受大種為因。有情名聲謂語表業，餘聲則是非有情名。

"Sound caused by appropriated great elements" refers, for instance, to the sound produced by the hand and the like. Sound produced by wind, forests, rivers, and the like is named "caused by non-appropriated great elements." Sound that is "the designation of a sentient being" means vocal expressive action; all other sound is "not the designation of a sentient being."

有說：有聲通有執受及無執受大種為因，如手鼓等合所生聲。如不許一顯色極微二四大造，聲亦應爾。

Some say: there is sound that has both appropriated and non-appropriated great elements together as its cause, such as the sound produced by a hand and a drum striking together. But just as it is not accepted that a single atom of visible colour is produced by two [separate sets of the] four great elements, so too should sound be understood.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The arithmetic is transparent: two options for physical cause (living body versus inanimate matter) times two options for meaning (language versus non-language) times two options for how it feels (pleasant versus unpleasant) gives eight. A handclap and human speech are both "caused by appropriated great elements" — produced by a living body — but only speech also counts as "the designation of a sentient being," since that phrase names meaningful vocal expression specifically (語表業, the karmically significant act of speaking), not just any sound a body happens to make. Wind in the trees and a river's noise are "non-appropriated," and, having no speaker, are automatically "not the designation of a sentient being" as well.

The closing exchange is a genuine technical dispute in Sarvāstivāda atomism, and we have kept it close to the literal wording rather than resolving it further. Someone raises an apparent counter-example — a hand striking a drum seems to produce one sound from two different kinds of cause at once. The reply appeals to the system's atomic theory (paramāṇu): just as a single "atom" of colour is never built from two separate sets of the four great elements, a single unit of sound should not be either — implying that what sounds like one hand-and-drum noise is, strictly, two distinct sounds arising together rather than one sound with a mixed cause.

21. Taste: Six Kinds, and Smell: Four Kinds

The next two sense-objects are dealt with briefly, one right after the other.

Chinese Source

已說聲處，當說味處。味有六種，甘、醋、醎、辛、苦、淡別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sound has now been explained; taste will now be explained. Taste is six kinds, distinguished as sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter, and bland.

已說味處，當說香處。香有四種：好香、惡香、等不等香，有差別故。

Taste has now been explained; smell will now be explained. Smell is four kinds: pleasant smell, unpleasant smell, and balanced or unbalanced smell — since these are distinguished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Worth noticing in passing: the treatise does not enumerate every sense-object the same way. Taste's six kinds are simply named as six distinct physical qualities, with no further cross-classification layered on top. Smell's four kinds, by contrast, are built from a small grid — pleasant or unpleasant, crossed with balanced (a moderate, well-matched concentration) or unbalanced (excessive or harsh) — the same combinatorial method already seen driving sound's count of eight. The treatise reaches for whichever method fits the material, rather than forcing every sense into one uniform scheme.

22. Touch: Eleven Kinds, and a Point about Naming

Touch is the last and longest of the five sense-objects — the four great elements themselves, plus seven further tangible qualities — and it closes with a small lesson on how language works.

Chinese Source

已說香處，當說觸處。觸有十一，謂四大種、滑性、澁性、重性、輕性及冷、飢、渴。

Ru-Yi Translation

Smell has now been explained; touch will now be explained. Touch is eleven kinds: the four great elements, together with smoothness, roughness, heaviness, lightness, cold, hunger, and thirst.

柔軟名滑。麁強為澁。可稱名重。翻此為輕。煖欲名冷。食欲名飢。飲欲名渴。

Softness is named smoothness. Coarseness and harshness are roughness. What can be weighed is named heaviness; the reverse of this is lightness. The desire for warmth is named cold. The desire for food is hunger. The desire for drink is thirst.

此三於因立果名故，作如是說。如有頌言：諸佛出現樂，演說正法樂，僧眾和合樂，同修勇進樂。

These three are spoken of this way because the name of the effect is established upon the cause. As a verse states: "The appearance of Buddhas is joy; the expounding of the true teaching is joy; the harmonious gathering of the saṅgha is joy; practising together with vigour is joy."

於色界中無飢渴觸，有所餘觸。

In the form realm, there is no tangible sensation of hunger or thirst; the remaining tangibles are present there.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Eleven kinds split cleanly in two: the four great elements themselves (earth, water, fire, wind — named here but not yet defined; their own characteristics get a dedicated, later treatment this fascicle has not reached), and seven further tangible qualities built from them. Five of those seven are named for what they plainly are — smoothness, roughness, heaviness, lightness. The last three are not: "cold" is literally defined as "desire for warmth," "hunger" as "desire for food," "thirst" as "desire for drink."

The treatise catches this oddity itself and explains it as a general feature of language, not a special case: sometimes a cause is named after the effect it produces, rather than by what it actually is. Cold is a physical sensation, but the word used for it names the craving that sensation provokes. The supporting verse makes the identical move in an entirely different context — the Buddha's appearance, the teaching, a harmonious saṅgha, and practising together are all, strictly, causes of joy, yet the verse calls each one "joy" outright. The closing remark about the form realm is a small piece of Abhidharma cosmology in passing: beings born there, having transcended desire-realm craving, no longer experience hunger or thirst at all, though they still have bodies subject to the other nine tangible qualities.

23. A Coda on the Form Realm, and the Sense-Field of Matter Concluded

Before closing the whole discussion of visible matter, the treatise adds two last, small cosmological details about the form realm mentioned in the previous section.

Chinese Source

彼界衣服，別不可稱，聚則可稱。冷煖於彼雖無能損而有能益。傳說如此。

Ru-Yi Translation

In that realm, garments cannot be weighed individually, but can be weighed when gathered together. Cold and warmth, although unable to harm beings there, are nonetheless able to benefit them. So it has been transmitted and explained.

此中已說多種色處。

Here, the many kinds belonging to the sense-field of matter have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image is a memorable one: form-realm beings' clothing is so refined that a single garment has, in effect, no weight at all — only a whole pile of them registers as anything to weigh. Cold and warmth work on these beings only as a pleasant sensation, never as harm, since the coarse physical vulnerability of the desire realm's bodies is already left behind at that level of existence.

The closing line is a genuine bookend for the sense-objects proper — form, sound, taste, smell, and touch — but not, as it turns out, for unmanifest form. Correction to what we said here: checking further ahead in the Chinese text shows that fascicle 1 does return to give unmanifest form's basic definition directly, a few sections on — what belongs to the much later chapter on karma is the detailed mechanics of exactly how particular precepts and vows produce and sustain it, not its basic nature as a kind of matter. We are leaving this note visible rather than quietly editing it away, since the correction is itself a useful reminder that even a careful reading of a dense, digression-filled text can misjudge how far ahead a promised topic actually is.

24. Does the Eye See One Thing, or Many?

Before moving to the remaining aggregates, the treatise pauses on a question of ordinary experience: can a single act of seeing take more than one thing as its object?

Chinese Source

有時眼識緣一事生，謂於爾時各別了別。有時眼識緣多事生，謂於爾時不別了別，如遠觀察軍眾山林、無量顯形珠寶聚等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sometimes the eye-consciousness arises taking a single thing as its object — that is, at that time it discerns each item separately. Sometimes the eye-consciousness arises taking many things as its object — that is, at that time it does not discern them separately, as when observing from a distance an army, a mountain forest, or a mass of countless colours, shapes, and jewels.

應知耳等諸識亦爾。

It should be understood that the ear-consciousness and the other remaining consciousnesses are likewise so.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The examples are well chosen: an army seen from a hilltop, or a forest seen from a distance, registers as one visual mass rather than as thousands of separately discerned soldiers or trees. The treatise treats this as an entirely ordinary mode of seeing, not a failure of it — sometimes consciousness resolves its object item by item, and sometimes it takes in a whole field at once without separately discerning each part. The very next passage pushes on exactly why this is allowed at all without breaking a rule the treatise otherwise holds firmly.

25. A Debate: Particular Characteristics and a Question of Priority

The same question is pressed harder for the body-consciousness and touch, raising one of Abhidharma's central epistemological distinctions — and the section closes with a practical puzzle about competing senses.

Chinese Source

有餘師說：身識極多緣五觸起，謂四大種，滑等隨一。有說：極多總緣一切十一觸起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some other masters say: the body-consciousness, at most, arises taking five tangibles as its object — namely, the four great elements, together with any one of smoothness and the rest. Others say: at most, it arises taking all eleven tangibles together as its object.

若爾，五識總緣境故，應五識身取共相境非自相境。約處自相，許五識身取自相境非事自相，斯有何失？

If that is so, then since the five sense-consciousnesses would be taking their objects all together, the body of five consciousnesses ought to be grasping a shared characteristic as its object, not a particular characteristic. [In reply:] with respect to the particular characteristic of the sense-field as a whole, it may be accepted that the body of five consciousnesses grasps the particular characteristic of the sense-field, not the particular characteristic of each individual thing — what fault is there in this?

今應思擇，身舌二根兩境俱至，何識先起？隨境強盛彼識先生；境若均平舌識先起，食飲引身令相續故。

Now this should be considered: when the objects of the two faculties, body and tongue, both arrive at once, which consciousness arises first? Whichever object is stronger, that consciousness arises first. If the objects are equal in strength, the tongue-consciousness arises first — because eating and drinking are what draw the body onward and keep it continuing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection assumes a rule the treatise holds elsewhere as basic: the five sense-consciousnesses only ever grasp a "particular characteristic" (自相, svalakṣaṇa) — the specific, unrepeatable nature of what is directly given right now — never a "shared characteristic" (共相, sāmānya-lakṣaṇa), a general feature abstracted across many things, which is held to be the mental consciousness's business alone. If the body-consciousness can take all eleven tangibles together in one act, doesn't that already amount to grasping something general rather than something particular? The reply draws a finer line than the objection allows for: even taken together, what is grasped is still the particular character of that sense-field as a directly-given whole, not a concept abstracted from it by thought. We have kept this exchange close to the literal Chinese rather than resolving it further — the boundary between "a particular that happens to be complex" and "a generality" is a genuinely fine one, argued over well beyond this treatise.

The closing puzzle is refreshingly concrete after that: when a sensation of touch and a taste arrive at the same moment with equal force, why does taste win? The treatise's answer is frankly functional — an organism has to keep eating and drinking to stay alive, so when the contest is otherwise even, priority goes to the sense that keeps the body going. It is a rare moment in this fascicle where the reasoning is not purely definitional but openly asks what a sense is for.

26. What Unmanifest Form Actually Is

The treatise now delivers on the promise made all the way back when the eleven kinds of form were first listed: a real definition of unmanifest form, the one item in that list that no sense can ever perceive.

Chinese Source

已說根境及取境相，無表色相今次當說。頌曰：亂心無心等，隨流淨不淨，大種所造性，由此說無表。

Ru-Yi Translation

The faculties, their objects, and how they take their objects have now been explained. The character of unmanifest form will next be explained. A verse states: through a scattered mind, a mindless state, and so on; following its own stream as pure or impure; having the nature of what is produced by the great elements — by this, unmanifest form is described.

論曰：亂心者，謂此餘心。無心者，謂入無想及滅盡定。等，言顯示不亂有心。相似相續說名隨流。善與不善名淨不淨。

Prose: "A scattered mind" means any mind other than the one that first produced it. "Mindless" means having entered the attainment of no-perception or the attainment of cessation. "And so on" indicates that it continues through an undistracted, present mind as well. A continuous succession of similar moments is called "following its own stream." Wholesome and unwholesome are named "pure" and "impure."

為簡諸得相似相續，是故復言大種所造。毘婆沙說：造是因義，謂作生等五種因故。顯立名因，故言由此。

In order to distinguish it from other things that also have a continuous succession of similar moments, it is further said to be "produced by the great elements." The Vaibhāṣika masters say: "produced" here has the sense of "caused" — that is, it functions as a cause in the sense of generating, and so forth, through five kinds of causal relation. To show that this establishes the reason for its name, the verse says "by this."

無表雖以色業為性如有表業，而非表示令他了知，故名無表。

Although unmanifest karma has physical action as its nature, just as manifest action does, it does not manifest itself so as to let others know of it — hence it is named "unmanifest."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The problem this verse solves is real: unmanifest form was placed among the eleven kinds of matter, yet it cannot be seen, heard, or otherwise sensed — so what keeps it in existence once produced? The answer is that it does not depend on being sensed, or even on the original state of mind that produced it, at all. Take a precept in one wholesome moment of mind, and the unmanifest form it generates continues afterward as its own "stream of similar moments" — through a distracted mind thinking about something else entirely, and even through the two meditative attainments in which ordinary mental activity stops altogether. It is "pure" or "impure" depending on whether the original act was wholesome or unwholesome, and it is specifically classed as matter — produced by the four great elements as its generating cause — precisely to distinguish it from other things (like the mental continuum itself) that also persist as a stream of similar moments without being made of matter at all.

The closing etymology is the cleanest statement in the whole discussion: unmanifest karma is real physical action, exactly as manifest bodily or verbal action is, with one difference — it never shows itself to anyone else. Taking a vow involves a visible act (manifest karma, seen and heard by witnesses) that produces an invisible, ongoing consequence (unmanifest karma) which only its owner carries forward, unseen by anyone. This is the "later explanation" fascicle 1 promised, and the fuller mechanics of exactly which acts produce it, and how it is gained or lost, are what the treatise takes up in the chapter on karma still to come.

27. The Four Great Elements: Function and Nature

Every kind of form named so far — the faculties, the five objects, unmanifest form — has been said to be "made of the four great elements." The treatise now finally defines what earth, water, fire, and wind actually are.

Chinese Source

大種謂四界，即地水火風，能成持等業，堅濕煖動性。

Ru-Yi Translation

The great elements are the four elements: earth, water, fire, and wind. They accomplish the functions of holding and the rest; their natures are solidity, wetness, heat, and motion.

論曰：地、水、火、風能持自相及所造色，故名為界，如是四界亦名大種，一切餘色所依性故、體寬廣故。或於地等增盛聚中形相大故，或起種種大事用故。

Prose: Earth, water, fire, and wind are able to hold their own particular character, and the derived matter produced from them — hence they are named "elements." These same four elements are also named "great elements," because all other matter has them as its underlying nature, and because their substance is vast in scope; or because, within an aggregate where one of them predominates, its form appears large; or because they give rise to great functions and effects of every kind.

此四大種能成何業？如其次第能成持、攝、熟、長四業，地界能持，水界能攝，火界能熟，風界能長。長謂增盛或復流引。

What functions do these four great elements accomplish? In their respective order, they accomplish four functions — holding, gathering, ripening, and growing: the earth element holds; the water element gathers; the fire element ripens; the wind element grows. "Growing" means causing to increase, or, further, causing to spread onward.

業用既爾，自性云何？如其次第即用堅、濕、煖、動為性，地界堅性，水界濕性，火界煖性，風界動性。

Given their functions, what is their own nature? In their respective order, their nature is precisely solidity, wetness, heat, and motion: the earth element's nature is solidity; the water element's nature is wetness; the fire element's nature is heat; the wind element's nature is motion.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is easy to misread as ordinary chemistry, and it isn't. "Earth," "water," "fire," and "wind" do not mean the visible substances we call by those names — visible water is itself already a combination of all four elements, just one in which wetness happens to predominate strongly enough to be felt as flowing and cohesive. The four "great elements" are instead the most basic tactile qualities present, in some proportion, in every last particle of matter without exception: solidity (resistance, the capacity to be felt as hard), wetness (cohesion, the capacity to bind and hold shape), heat (the capacity to ripen or mature), and motion (the capacity to spread or propagate). Nothing physical — not a sense faculty, not a sound, not unmanifest form itself — is ever built from less than all four together; what varies from one thing to the next is only which element predominates.

The treatise draws a careful line between what each element does (its function: earth holds a compound together as its base, water binds its parts to each other, fire ripens or matures it, wind causes it to grow or spread) and what each element simply is (its nature: solidity, wetness, heat, motion, respectively). This function-versus-nature move is the same analytical habit already seen applied to the eye-consciousness elsewhere on this site — first ask what a thing does, only then ask what it is — applied here to the four building blocks that everything physical in the Kośa's system is ultimately made of.

28. Feeling, Perception, and Formations — and a Bridge to the Elements

With form fully explained, the treatise moves through the next three aggregates quickly, in a single dense verse — and quietly uses the same verse to start connecting the five-aggregate scheme to the eighteen-element scheme still to come.

Chinese Source

受領納隨觸，想取像為體，四餘名行蘊，如是受等三，及無表無為，名法處法界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Feeling is the experiencing that follows upon contact. Perception has the grasping of an image as its essence. What remains of the four is named the aggregate of volitional formations. Feeling and these other two, together with unmanifest form and the unconditioned, are named the sense-field of dharmas and the element of dharmas.

論曰：受蘊謂三，領納隨觸，即樂及苦、不苦不樂。此復分別成六受身，謂眼觸所生受乃至意觸所生受。

Prose: The aggregate of feeling is threefold: the experiencing that follows upon contact — namely pleasant, painful, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant. This, further distinguished, forms the six bodies of feeling: feeling born of eye-contact, through feeling born of mind-contact.

想蘊謂能取像為體，即能執取青黃長短、男女怨親、苦樂等相。此復分別成六想身，應如受說。

The aggregate of perception has as its essence the ability to grasp an image — that is, the ability to apprehend such marks as blue, yellow, long, short, male, female, friend, enemy, pleasure, and pain. This, further distinguished, forms the six bodies of perception, to be understood just as feeling was explained.

除前及後色、受、想、識，餘一切行名為行蘊。

Excluding what has already come before and what comes after — form, feeling, perception, and consciousness — all remaining conditioned factors are named the aggregate of volitional formations.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Feeling and perception both get real definitions; formations gets none at all, on purpose. Feeling is simply the tone that arises with contact — pleasant, painful, or neutral — sorted into six "bodies" by which of the six sense-doors (eye through mind) produced it. Perception is the mind's labelling function: not the raw sensation itself, but the act of recognizing what is there — blue rather than yellow, a friend rather than an enemy — again sorted the same sixfold way. Volitional formations, by contrast, is defined purely by subtraction: whatever conditioned dharma is not form, not feeling, not perception, and not consciousness belongs here. This is why formations is famously the most crowded, heterogeneous aggregate in the whole system — it is the aggregate's job to catch everything else, from the will to act, to attention, to dozens of further mental factors this treatise will name one by one much later, rather than to name a single unified function the way feeling and perception each do.

The verse's last clause is doing quiet, important structural work. Feeling, perception, and formations — three of the five aggregates — together with unmanifest form and every unconditioned dharma, are here declared to be exactly what makes up "the sense-field of dharmas" and "the element of dharmas" (法處, 法界): one of the twelve sense-fields and eighteen elements the treatise is building toward. This is the first place fascicle 1 explicitly shows how its three classification schemes — aggregates, sense-fields, elements — are not independent systems but different cuts through the very same set of dharmas. Consciousness, left out of this list, is about to get its own treatment as the eighteen elements' remaining seven members.

29. Consciousness — the Fifth Aggregate, and a Bridge to the Elements

One loose end from the previous section is tied off first, before the treatise defines the last aggregate and immediately begins connecting it to the eighteen elements still to come.

Chinese Source

然薄伽梵於契經中說六思身為行蘊者，由最勝故。

Ru-Yi Translation

However, the Blessed One, in the sūtras, spoke of the six bodies of volition as "the aggregate of volitional formations," because they are the most excellent among what that aggregate contains.

識謂各了別，此即名意處，及七界應知，六識轉為意。

Consciousness is the discerning of each object individually. This same consciousness is named the mind sense-field; and the seven elements should be understood in relation to it — the six consciousnesses, in turn, become the mind element.

論曰：各各了別彼彼境界，總取境相故名識蘊。

Prose: The individual discerning of this or that object-realm — the taking up of an object's overall mark — is why this is named the aggregate of consciousness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The opening line resolves a tension a careful reader would already have noticed: the previous section defined volitional formations as a huge catch-all — everything conditioned that isn't form, feeling, perception, or consciousness — yet the sūtras themselves sometimes speak as if "formations" simply meant volition (思, cetanā). The answer is that volition is being singled out as the most excellent, most prominent member of that crowded aggregate, not the whole of it — a common shorthand, not a narrower technical definition competing with the one already given.

Consciousness itself gets a deliberately spare definition: the bare discerning of an object's overall mark. Set beside perception's "grasping an image" from the previous section, the contrast is exact — perception performs the specific labelling (this is blue, this is a friend), while consciousness is simply the underlying awareness-of-an-object that makes any labelling possible in the first place. With this, all five aggregates are now defined: form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness.

The verse's remaining clauses look forward rather than explain in full, and we are leaving them that way rather than resolving them ahead of schedule: consciousness, taken as a single aggregate, is also what the treatise will call the "mind sense-field" (意處) — one of the twelve; and, split by which of the six consciousnesses has just ceased, it also accounts for the "mind element" (意界) among the eighteen — the same doctrine already seen in Ru-Yi's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra translation, where the immediately-preceding moment of the eye-consciousness is likewise called manas. The full construction of the twelve sense-fields and eighteen elements is what fascicle 1 turns to next.

30. The Payoff: How the Three Schemes Are One

Every piece has now been placed. The treatise closes consciousness's definition by stating, in full, exactly how the five aggregates, twelve sense fields, and eighteen elements are three ways of cutting the very same reality.

Chinese Source

此復差別有六識身，謂眼識身至意識身。應知如是所說識蘊，於處門中立為意處。

Ru-Yi Translation

This, further differentiated, has six consciousness-bodies: the eye-consciousness-body, through the mind-consciousness-body. It should be understood that this aggregate of consciousness, just explained, is established, within the framework of the sense-fields, as the mind sense-field.

於界門中立為七界，謂眼識界至意識界，即此六識轉為意界。

Within the framework of the elements, it is established as seven elements: the eye-consciousness element, through the mind-consciousness element — and these same six consciousnesses, in transition, become the mind element.

如是此中所說五蘊，即十二處并十八界，謂除無表，諸餘色蘊即名十處，亦名十界。

Thus, the five aggregates spoken of here are precisely the twelve sense fields together with the eighteen elements: excluding unmanifest form, the remaining aggregate of form is named the ten sense-fields, and is likewise named the ten elements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Twenty-nine sections of definitions now snap into one table. The aggregate of form, minus its one imperceptible member, supplies ten of the twelve sense-fields and ten of the eighteen elements outright — the five faculties (eye through body) and their five matching objects (form through the tangible), exactly as fascicle 1 defined them section by section. The aggregate of consciousness supplies an eleventh sense-field, the "mind sense-field," and — split by which of the six consciousnesses has just ceased — seven of the eighteen elements: the six consciousness-elements plus the "mind element," the same doctrine already met in Ru-Yi's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra translation, where a consciousness's own immediately-past moment is called manas. Feeling, perception, formations, unmanifest form, and every unconditioned dharma — already declared in section 28 — supply the twelfth sense-field and eighteenth element: the "dharma sense-field" and "dharma element," the single catch-all category for everything that is neither a physical sense-organ, a physical sense-object, nor an act of consciousness itself.

Ten plus one plus one makes twelve; ten plus seven plus one makes eighteen. Nothing has been added or invented to make the arithmetic work — the same dharmas that fascicle 1 has been patiently defining since the aggregate of form are simply being counted a second and third way. This is the real point of naming this treatise's opening chapter Dhātu-nirdeśa, "the exposition of the elements": aggregates, sense-fields, and elements are not three competing theories of what exists, but three different lenses the treatise gives its readers for examining one and the same set of dharmas, each useful for a different kind of question.

31. Why Seven, Not Six? A Child Who Is Also a Father

A careful reader would already be objecting: if the mind element is nothing but the six consciousnesses, why count seven elements instead of six? The treatise raises the objection itself and answers with two everyday images.

Chinese Source

豈不識蘊唯六識身，異此說何復為意界？更無異法。即於此中頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Is it not the case that the aggregate of consciousness is only the six consciousness-bodies? Apart from these, what further thing is being spoken of as the "mind element"? There is no further, separate dharma. Precisely on this point, a verse states:

由即六識身，無間滅為意。

It is exactly the six consciousness-bodies themselves that, immediately upon ceasing without interval, become the mind.

論曰：即六識身無間滅已，能生後識故名意界，謂如此子即名餘父，又如此果即名餘種。

Prose: It is precisely the six consciousness-bodies which, having just ceased without interval, are able to give rise to the consciousness that follows — hence they are named the mind element. It is just as this same person, a child to his own parents, is named "father" in relation to his own child; or just as this same fruit is named "seed" in relation to what grows from it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

No eighth substance is smuggled in here, and none is needed. The treatise's answer to its own objection is that "the six consciousness-elements" and "the mind element" are two names for the very same six dharmas, distinguished only by which of two relations is being pointed to: taken as presently arising and discerning an object, they are the six consciousness-elements; taken as having just ceased and thereby opening the way for the next moment of consciousness, the very same dharmas are the mind element. The two similes make the logic vivid rather than abstract: one and the same man is "child" in relation to his own parents and "father" in relation to his own child — nobody thinks these are two different people. One and the same fruit is "fruit" in relation to the tree it grew from and "seed" in relation to the plant it will produce — nobody thinks these are two different objects. Six dharmas, two functional relations, seven names in the count of elements: the arithmetic from section 30 survives fully intact.

32. Why Eighteen, Not Seventeen? The Hidden Symmetry

The previous section's own logic invites an even sharper objection: if the mind element is nothing but the six consciousnesses, shouldn't the real count drop even further? The treatise's answer reveals the structural principle the whole eighteen-element scheme is built on.

Chinese Source

若爾實界應唯十七或唯十二，六識與意更相攝故，何緣得立十八界耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, then the real number of elements should be only seventeen, or even only twelve, since the six consciousnesses and the mind mutually subsume one another. For what reason, then, are eighteen elements established?

成第六依故，十八界應知。

Because it establishes the support for the sixth consciousness, the eighteen elements should be understood as genuinely eighteen.

論曰：如五識界別有眼等五界為依，第六意識無別所依，為成此依故說意界。如是所依能依境界，應知各六界成十八。

Prose: Just as the five sense-consciousness elements each have separate elements — the eye and the rest — as their support, the sixth, the mental consciousness, has no separate support of its own. In order to establish this support for it, the mind element is spoken of. Thus, the supports, the supporting consciousnesses, and their objects — each of these should be understood as six elements, making eighteen in total.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection is sharper than it first looks: if six dharmas can honestly be called seven under two different names, why stop there — why not admit the whole scheme is really just twelve elements (six consciousnesses plus six objects), with "supports" added only for the five physical senses and none for the mind? The answer exposes the actual design principle behind the number eighteen: every one of the six sense-modalities — the five physical ones and the mental one alike — needs exactly three elements to be structurally complete: a support, a consciousness, and an object. The five physical senses already have their supports ready-made, in the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body. The mental consciousness has no organ of its own the way the eye or ear does — so the treatise gives it one anyway, built from the only material available: the immediately-preceding moment of any of the six consciousnesses, which is exactly the "mind element" already explained in the previous section. Six supports, six consciousnesses, six objects: eighteen elements, not because eighteen is a traditional round number, but because it is the smallest count that gives every sense-modality, mental included, the same three-part structure the others already have.

33. The Arhat's Final Thought, and Two Modes of Subsumption

Section 32's account of the mind element invites a sharp test case before the treatise moves on to a new question: how the aggregates, sense fields, and elements each subsume the totality of dharmas.

Chinese Source

若爾，無學最後念心應非意界，此無間滅後識不生，非意界故。不爾，此已住意性故、闕餘緣故，後識不生。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, then an arhat's very last moment of mind should not count as the mind element, since after this immediately-ceasing consciousness no further consciousness arises — and so it would not be a mind element. Not so: this moment has already settled into the nature of mind; it is only because the other conditions are lacking that no further consciousness arises from it.

此中蘊攝一切有為，取蘊唯攝一切有漏，處界總攝一切法盡。別攝如是，總攝云何？

Among these three schemes: the aggregates subsume all conditioned dharmas; the aggregates-of-grasping subsume only all contaminated dharmas; the sense fields and the elements together subsume the entirety of all dharmas without exception. This is how they subsume individually — how, then, do they subsume collectively?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection takes the mind element's definition at its most literal: manas is whatever consciousness has just ceased, functioning as manas precisely because it supports a next moment of consciousness. An arhat's death-moment thought ceases but gives rise to nothing further — no rebirth-consciousness follows, since final nirvana intervenes — so a literal reading would disqualify it from ever counting as "mind element" at all. The resolution separates two things the objection has run together: having the nature of mind element is about a moment having already settled into the general role of "the immediately-past phase of consciousness," not about whether some further consciousness happens, in a given case, actually to arise from it. When nothing follows, that is because the other conditions a rebirth would need are simply absent — not because this moment ever failed to be mind element in the first place.

The pivot to "total subsumption" (總攝) versus the "individual subsumption" (別攝) already covered notes something easy to miss: the three schemes do not cover identical ground. The five aggregates subsume only conditioned dharmas; the five aggregates-of-grasping narrow this further to only contaminated conditioned dharmas; only the twelve sense fields and eighteen elements manage to subsume absolutely everything, conditioned and unconditioned alike. "Individually" names this scope-by-scope difference. "Collectively" asks a different question: within any one scheme, how does a single dharma relate to all the others it is said to "subsume"?

34. One Verse: How Each Category Subsumes All Dharmas at Once

The answer draws a careful line between two senses of "subsuming" — one strict, one loose — using the eye faculty as its worked example.

Chinese Source

頌曰：總攝一切法，由一蘊處界，攝自性非餘，以離他性故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: All dharmas are subsumed collectively by a single aggregate, sense field, or element; each subsumes only its own nature, never another's, because a dharma is forever separate from any other dharma's nature.

論曰：由一色蘊意處法界，應知總攝一切法盡。謂於諸處就勝義說，唯攝自性不攝他性。所以者何？法與他性恒相離故。此離於彼而言攝者，其理不然。且如眼根，唯攝色蘊眼處眼界苦集諦等，是彼性故不攝餘蘊餘處界等，離彼性故。若於諸處就世俗說，應知亦以餘法攝餘。如四攝事攝徒眾等。

Prose: By a single representative — the aggregate of form, the mental sense field, or the dharma element — one should know that all dharmas are, each within its own scheme, subsumed in their entirety. That is: speaking of the sense fields in the ultimate sense, each subsumes only its own nature, not another's. Why is this? Because a dharma is forever separated from what belongs to another dharma's nature. To speak of "subsuming" something on the grounds of being separate from it — this reasoning does not hold. Take the eye faculty: it subsumes only the aggregate of form, the eye sense field, the eye element, the truth of suffering, the truth of origin, and so on, because these share its own nature; it does not subsume the remaining aggregates, sense fields, or elements, because these are separate from its nature. If, however, one speaks of the sense fields in the conventional sense, a dharma may also be said to subsume others by what belongs to them — just as the four means of attraction are said to "gather in" a following.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Ultimate sense" subsumption turns out to be almost tautological: strictly speaking, a dharma "subsumes" only itself, since no two dharmas ever share a single nature — the eye faculty subsumes the eye faculty, and nothing else, full stop. "Conventional sense" subsumption is looser and more useful: a dharma can be said to gather in things it is merely associated with or productive of, the way the four means of attraction — giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and cooperation — are said to "gather a following," even though the followers themselves are separate people, not identical with the practices that drew them in. The distinction between strict metaphysical identity and loose functional grouping is not just a technicality here — it is exactly the tool the treatise needs for what comes next: checking whether various other sutra enumerations can be said to fit inside the aggregates, sense fields, and elements, when they plainly are not identical with them in the strict sense.

35. Two Eyes, One Element: Why Paired Organs Don't Double the Count

A concrete objection tests the eighteen-element count from an unexpected angle: the eye, ear, and nose each come as a pair of physical organs, so why isn't the true count twenty-one?

Chinese Source

眼耳鼻三處各有二，何緣界體非二十一？此難非理。所以者何？頌曰：類境識同故，雖二界體一。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eye, ear, and nose sense fields each come in a pair of organs — why, then, is the total count of elements not twenty-one instead of eighteen? This objection does not hold. Why not? A verse states: because the class, the object, and the consciousness are each the same, though there are two organs, the element's essence is one.

論曰：類同者，謂二處同是眼自性故。境同者，謂二處同用色為境故。識同者，謂二處同為眼識依故。由此眼界雖二而一。耳鼻亦應如是安立。

Prose: "Same class" means that the two organs share the very same nature of being "eye." "Same object" means that the two organs alike take form as their object. "Same consciousness" means that the two organs alike serve as support for eye-consciousness. Because of this, though the eye element exists in two places, it is a single element. The ear and the nose should be established in just the same way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The test for "how many elements" turns out not to be a headcount of physical organs at all, but a threefold sameness test: do the candidates share one nature, one kind of object, and one dependent consciousness? Two eyes pass on all three counts — both are eye-tissue, both take form as object, both support the same eye-consciousness — so despite being physically two, they count as a single element, the same way "the eye element" named back in the treatise's opening sections was always meant as a type, not a token. The same reasoning is simply carried over to the ear and the nose without needing to be re-argued from scratch.

36. Why Two, Not One? The Real Reason Is Clarity, Not Beauty

If one element can perfectly well occupy two organs, a further question follows naturally: why bother with two at all? The verse's first answer does not survive its own commentary.

Chinese Source

若爾，何緣生依二處？頌曰：然為令端嚴，眼等各生二。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, why do they arise attached to two locations at all? A verse states: rather, it is so that the body may be handsome that the eye and the rest each arise as two.

論曰：為所依身相端嚴故，界體雖一而兩處生。若眼耳根處唯生一，鼻無二穴身不端嚴。此釋不然。若本來爾，誰言醜陋？又猫鵄等雖生二處，有何端嚴？

Prose: It is so that the body which supports them may be handsome in appearance that, though the element's essence is one, it arises in two places. If the eye and ear faculties arose in only a single place each, and the nose had no two openings, the body would not be handsome. This explanation does not hold. If that were simply the natural state of things, who would call a single-organed form ugly? Moreover, cats, owls, and the like also have their faculties arising in two places — what handsomeness is achieved by that?

若爾，三根何緣生二？為所發識明了端嚴。現見世間閉一目等，了別色等便不分明，是故三根各生二處。已說諸蘊及處界攝，當說其義。

If that is so, then why do these three faculties arise as two? It is so that the consciousness they give rise to may be clear and refined. It is observed in the world that when one shuts a single eye, discrimination of form and the rest becomes correspondingly unclear — and so these three faculties each arise in two places. Having now explained how the aggregates, sense fields, and elements subsume all dharmas, their meanings should next be explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The verse's own proposed answer — symmetry for the sake of good looks — does not survive its own commentary's scrutiny: calling a single-organed form "ugly" quietly assumes the very symmetry it is supposed to explain, and animals plainly have paired eyes, ears, and nostrils without anyone crediting them with a concern for handsomeness. The real reason the prose lands on is functional rather than aesthetic: paired organs sharpen the resulting consciousness's clarity, which anyone can verify simply by closing one eye and noticing vision blur. Notice the wordplay holding the passage together — 端嚴 first names physical handsomeness (the explanation that fails), then gets reused to describe the refined clarity of cognition itself (the explanation that succeeds), the same two characters pivoting from an aesthetic claim to a functional one.

The closing line signals a shift in register: the treatise has finished explaining how the three schemes subsume dharmas, and turns next to explaining what their names themselves mean — the classic Abhidharma move of following an analysis with an etymology.

37. What Aggregate, Sense Field, and Element Each Mean

Having finished analyzing what the three schemes contain, the treatise turns to what their very names mean — the classic Abhidharma move of following an analysis with an etymology.

Chinese Source

此蘊處界別義云何？頌曰：聚生門種族，是蘊處界義。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the distinct meaning of aggregate, sense field, and element, respectively? A verse states: heap, growth-gate, and lineage — these are the meanings of aggregate, sense field, and element.

論曰：諸有為法和合聚義是蘊義，如契經言：諸所有色，若過去若未來若現在、若內若外、若麁若細、若劣若勝、若遠若近，如是一切略為一聚，說名色蘊。由此聚義，蘊義得成。

Prose: Among conditioned dharmas, the meaning of "combined heap" is the meaning of "aggregate," just as a sutra says: "Whatever form there is — past, future, or present; internal or external; coarse or subtle; inferior or superior; far or near — all of this, gathered together in brief as a single heap, is called the aggregate of form." By this meaning of "heap," the meaning of "aggregate" is established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The quoted sutra passage is one of the most recognizable formulas in all of Abhidharma literature, defining each of the five aggregates by the same eleven-fold set of pairs: temporal (past, future, present), spatial (internal, external), and by degree (coarse or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near). The treatise will spend the next section unpacking exactly what each of these paired terms technically means — they are not left to intuition.

38. Unpacking the Sutra's Own Pairs, and a Father Who Is Also a Son

Each pair from the aggregate formula gets its own precise definition, including a defense of "coarse" and "subtle" against the charge that relative terms can never be fixed.

Chinese Source

於此經中，無常已滅名過去、若未已生名未來、已生未謝名現在，自身名內、所餘名外。或約處辯，有對名麁、無對名細。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this sutra: what is impermanent and has already ceased is called "past"; what has not yet arisen is called "future"; what has arisen but not yet passed away is called "present." One's own body is called "internal"; everything else is called "external." Or, considered by sense field: what is resistant is called "coarse"; what is non-resistant is called "subtle."

或相待立。若言相待，麁細不成。此難不然，所待異故。待彼為麁未嘗為細、待彼為細未嘗為麁，猶如父子苦集諦等，染污名劣、不染名勝，去來名遠、現在名近，乃至識蘊應知亦然。

Or, "coarse" and "subtle" are established relatively. If they are established relatively, one might object, then "coarse" and "subtle" can never be fixed. This objection does not hold, because what each is relative to differs: what is coarse relative to one thing is never, in that same relation, subtle, and what is subtle relative to one thing is never, in that same relation, coarse — just as with a father and a son, or the truths of suffering and origin. What is defiled is called "inferior"; what is undefiled is called "superior." What is past or future is called "far"; what is present is called "near." This should be understood to hold all the way through to the aggregate of consciousness as well.

而有差別，謂依五根名麁，唯依意根名細。或約地辯，毘婆沙師所說如是。大德法救復作是言：五根所取名麁色，所餘名細色。非可意者名劣色，所餘名勝色。不可見處名遠色，在可見處名近色。過去等色如自名顯，受等亦然，隨所依力，應知遠近麁細同前。

But there is a distinction here: what depends on the five physical faculties is called "coarse"; what depends only on the mental faculty is called "subtle." Or, this may instead be considered by cosmological realm — this is what the Vaibhāṣika masters say. The Venerable Dharmatrāta, however, says otherwise: what is grasped by the five physical faculties is called "coarse form"; everything else is called "subtle form." What is disagreeable is called "inferior form"; everything else is called "superior form." What is in an invisible place is called "far form"; what is in a visible place is called "near form." Form's "past" and the rest are just as their own names already show. Feeling and the remaining aggregates should be understood the same way, each according to the strength of what it depends on, for their "far," "near," "coarse," and "subtle" alike, just as before.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The father-son image is a callback to the exact same relational logic used earlier in this fascicle to explain how one moment of mind can be simultaneously "child" of the consciousness before it and "parent" of the one after: a single man is unambiguously father relative to his son and unambiguously son relative to his own father, with no contradiction, because each relation is anchored to a specific counterpart. "Coarse" and "subtle" work the same way — nothing is coarse or subtle in a vacuum, but relative to a named comparandum the terms are perfectly fixed.

The two named readings that follow are a recurring pattern in this treatise: the mainstream Vaibhāṣika position, which sorts "coarse versus subtle" partly by which of the six faculties something depends on and partly by cosmological realm, against the alternate opinion of the Venerable Dharmatrāta (大德法救), a named Sarvāstivāda teacher the Kośa regularly cites as a dissenting voice. Here his version re-grounds every pair — coarse and subtle, inferior and superior, far and near — directly in ordinary sense experience: what the five senses can grasp, what feels agreeable, what can actually be seen, rather than in the more abstract, doctrinally loaded categories the mainstream reading relies on.

39. Sense Field: The "Growth-Gate," and Element: The Mountain's Many Ore-Veins

With the aggregate's etymology settled, the treatise turns to the sense field and the element, each explained through a vivid, literal image.

Chinese Source

心心所法，生長門義是處義。訓釋詞者，謂能生長心心所法故名為處，是能生長彼作用義。

Ru-Yi Translation

For mind and mental factors, the meaning of "growth-gate" is the meaning of "sense field." By etymological explanation: because it can generate and nourish the growth of mind and mental factors, it is called "sense field" — this is the functional meaning of being able to generate their growth.

法種族義是界義，如一山中有多銅鐵金銀等族，說名多界。如是一身或一相續，有十八類諸法種族，名十八界。

The meaning of "lineage of dharmas" is the meaning of "element," just as within a single mountain there are many lineages of copper, iron, gold, silver, and the rest, which are called its many "elements." In just the same way, within a single body, or a single continuum of existence, there are eighteen kinds of dharma-lineages — these are called the eighteen elements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both etymologies are meant literally, not just as homely illustrations. "Āyatana" (sense field) is glossed by its function as a gate or doorway through which mind and mental factors are generated and made to grow — the twelve sense fields are literally the twelve entry-points through which cognition arises at all. "Dhātu" (element) is glossed through its root sense of a mineral seam running through rock — the Sanskrit word genuinely means "layer" or "stratum" in exactly this geological sense, so the mountain is not a decorative metaphor but the very image the word was built from: a single person, like a single mountain, contains eighteen distinct veins of dharma running through it.

40. Is "Element" About Causing, or Just About Kind?

A technical difficulty follows immediately: if "element" means "causal root," how can the causally inert unconditioned dharmas still count as elements at all?

Chinese Source

此中種族是生本義。如是眼等誰之生本？謂自種類同類因故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, "lineage" carries the meaning of "root of arising." In that case, what is the eye and the rest the root of arising for? It is the root of arising for its own kind, by way of being a same-class cause for later instances of its own type.

若爾，無為應不名界，心心所法生之本故。有說：界聲表種類義，謂十八法種類自性各別不同，名十八界。

If that is so, then the unconditioned should not be called "element," since being an element in this sense requires being a root of arising for mind and mental factors. Some say instead: the word "element" expresses the meaning of "kind" — meaning that, since each of the eighteen dharmas differs in its own nature, each in its own kind, from all the others, they are called the eighteen elements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The difficulty is precise: if "dhātu" specifically means a causal root, tied to the technical notion of a "same-class cause" — a dharma serving as cause for later dharmas of its own type — then this etymology quietly assumes every element is causally active. But the unconditioned dharmas classified under the dharma element, such as space or the two kinds of cessation, are by definition causally inert in this system; they never serve as anyone's cause. The proposed fix drops the causal reading in favor of a purely classificatory one: "element" simply means "kind" — one of eighteen distinct natures — with no requirement that it actively cause anything at all, which quietly preserves the unconditioned dharmas' place among the eighteen elements without asking them to do any causal work.

41. Is an Aggregate Just a Conventional Heap? Three Rival Etymologies

The aggregate's etymology now comes under its own pressure: if "aggregate" really does mean "heap," does that make an aggregate no more real than a heap of grain?

Chinese Source

若言聚義是蘊義者，蘊應假有，多實積集共所成故，如聚如我。此難不然，一實極微亦名蘊故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one says that the meaning of "heap" is the meaning of "aggregate," then the aggregate should be merely conventionally existent, since it is formed jointly out of an accumulation of many real substances — like a heap of grain, like a conventionally posited self. This objection does not hold, because even a single real atom is also called an "aggregate."

若爾，不應言聚義是蘊義，非一實物有聚義故。有說：能荷重擔義是蘊義，由此世間說肩名蘊，物所聚故。

If that is so, then one should not say that the meaning of "heap" is the meaning of "aggregate," since a single real substance does not carry the meaning of "heap" at all. Some say instead: the meaning of "bearing a heavy load" is the meaning of "aggregate" — hence in ordinary usage, the shoulder is called by the same word, "skandha," because things are piled upon it.

或有說者：可分段義是蘊義，故世有言：汝三蘊還，我當與汝。此釋越經，經說聚義是蘊義故，

Still others say: the meaning of "divisible into installments" is the meaning of "aggregate" — hence it is said in the world, "Return my three installments, and I will repay you." This last explanation oversteps the sutra, since the sutra itself states that the meaning of "heap" is the meaning of "aggregate."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Skandha" genuinely carries several senses in Sanskrit — heap or mass, shoulder or tree-trunk, and, in ordinary transactional speech, a portion or installment — and the treatise entertains all three before anchoring its technical usage strictly to the sutra's own stated gloss, "heap." But that anchor comes with its own tension, one the treatise has not yet resolved: calling an aggregate a "heap" seems to imply it is a mere conventional composite, no more real than a heap of grain apart from the grains themselves — yet the very same tradition also calls a single, partless atom an "aggregate" in its own right, which a plain heap-reading struggles to explain, since one atom is not a heap of anything. Whether an aggregate is ultimately real or only conventionally so is exactly where the treatise turns next.

42. Does "Heap" Make the Sense Fields Conventional Too?

The tension flagged at the end of the last section comes to a head: does the sutra's own wording really force "aggregate" into being merely conventional — and if so, does the same logic threaten the sense fields as well?

Chinese Source

如契經言：諸所有色若過去等。廣說如前。若謂此經顯過去等一一色等各別名蘊，是故一切過去色等一一實物各各名蘊。此執非理，故彼經言：如是一切略為一聚說名蘊故，是故如聚，蘊定假有。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the sutra says: "Whatever form there is, whether past," and so on, stated in full as before. If one holds that this sutra shows each individual instance — past form and the rest, one by one — separately called an "aggregate," so that every past form and the rest, each individual real substance, is itself called an aggregate — this view does not hold. For that same sutra states: "all of this, gathered together in brief as a single heap, is called the aggregate" — therefore, just like a heap, an aggregate is indeed only conventionally existent.

若爾，應許諸有色處亦是假有，眼等極微要多積聚成生門故。此難非理，多積聚中一一極微有因用故。

If that is so, then one should also accept that the sense fields consisting of form are likewise only conventionally existent, since the eye and the other atoms, too, must accumulate as a multitude before they can constitute a growth-gate. This objection does not hold, because within that very accumulation, each individual atom still possesses its own causal function.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Whether something is ultimately real in this system hinges on whether it retains a genuine causal function down to its smallest parts, not merely on whether it happens to be composed of many parts. The sutra's own wording — "gathered as one heap" — settles that "aggregate," at least in the sense that formula uses the word, names a collective grouping rather than any single individual real thing, so aggregate-hood in that sense is conventional. But this threatens to prove too much: the sense fields, like the eye field, are also physically many atoms accumulated, so wouldn't the same "it's just a heap" reasoning make them conventional too — undermining the school's core commitment that the eighteen elements are all ultimately real dharmas? The answer draws the real dividing line: what matters is not accumulation as such, but whether each atom, even within the accumulation, keeps its own distinct causal role. The atoms making up the eye field each individually do real causal work toward producing eye-consciousness; the "aggregate" named by the sutra's collective formula does no comparable unified work over and above its members. That difference, not sheer plurality, is what separates the merely conventional from what remains ultimately real.

43. One Atom, Many Roles: The "Burning the Garment" Analogy

The Vaibhāṣika masters close the debate with a move that dissolves the apparent either/or into two equally valid descriptions of the very same atom.

Chinese Source

若不爾者，根境相助共生識等應非別處，是則應無十二處別。然毘婆沙作如是說。

Ru-Yi Translation

If it were not so, then the faculty and the object, mutually assisting each other to jointly produce a consciousness, would have no need to occupy separate domains, and it would follow that there would be no real distinction among the twelve sense fields at all. This, however, is what the Vaibhāṣikas say.

對法諸師若觀假蘊，彼說極微一界一處一蘊少分；若不觀者，彼說極微即是一界一處一蘊。此應於分，假謂有分，如燒少衣亦說燒衣。

The Ābhidharmika masters, when regarding things from the standpoint of the conventional aggregate, say that a single atom is only a portion of a single element, a single sense field, and a single aggregate; when not regarding things that way, they say that a single atom is itself a whole element, a whole sense field, and a whole aggregate. This should be understood as a matter of "the part": one conventionally speaks of a whole as "having parts" — just as burning only a small corner of a robe is also spoken of as "burning the robe."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Rather than forcing a single verdict on whether an atom "is" an aggregate, field, and element or merely "a piece of" one, the text simply notes that both descriptions are available depending on the frame in use: relative to the conventional whole, an atom is a fragment; taken on its own causal merits, it fully qualifies as one instance of the category in its own right. "Burning the garment" for scorching one corner of it is a classic synecdoche — naming the whole by a part — and it dissolves what looked like a strict either/or into simply two valid ways of describing the same atom, chosen according to context.

The opening line also supplies the deeper stakes of the whole aggregate-versus-heap argument: if atoms lost their individual causal role, the very reason faculty and object need to be functionally distinct domains, rather than one undifferentiated mass, would disappear — and the entire edifice of twelve and eighteen-fold classification this fascicle has spent over forty sections building would lose its rationale along with it.

44. Why Three Schemes? Delusion, Sharpness, and a Taste for Brevity

A final question closes this whole arc with a genuinely satisfying answer: why did the Buddha teach the very same reality three separate, overlapping ways at all?

Chinese Source

何故世尊於所知境，由蘊等門作三種說？頌曰：愚根樂三故，說蘊處界三。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why did the World-Honored One teach the objects of knowledge by three different approaches — the aggregate and the rest? A verse states: because delusion, faculty, and inclination are each of three kinds, the aggregates, sense fields, and elements are taught as three.

論曰：所化有情有三品故，世尊為說蘊等三門。傳說：有情愚有三種，或愚心所總執為我，或唯愚色、或愚色心。根亦有三，謂利、中、鈍。樂亦三種，謂樂、略、中，及廣文故。如其次第，世尊為說蘊處界三。

Prose: Because the beings to be taught fall into three kinds, the World-Honored One taught by way of the three approaches — the aggregate and the rest. It is said: beings' delusion is of three kinds — some are deluded in a way that seizes on the mental factors collectively as a self; some are deluded only about form; some are deluded about both form and mind together. Faculties are likewise of three kinds: sharp, middling, and dull. And inclination is also of three kinds: a preference for brevity, for a middling length, or for the fullest, most extensive exposition. Correspondingly, in just this order, the World-Honored One taught the aggregates, the sense fields, and the elements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-way matching here is genuinely elegant. The five aggregates — the coarsest scheme — suit beings deluded broadly about "self" as some lump of mental factors, with sharp faculties, who prefer the most concise teaching. The twelve sense fields — a middle level of detail — suit beings specifically confused about form, with middling faculties, preferring a middling amount of explanation. The eighteen elements — the finest-grained scheme, which alone splits "mind" into six separate consciousnesses — suit beings confused about both form and mind together, with the dullest faculties, who need everything spelled out and want the fullest, most extensive treatment.

This passage retroactively explains something that could easily look like redundancy — why teach the same reality three overlapping ways at all — as a deliberate pedagogical design matched to three different kinds of listener, not competing accounts where one must be more "correct" than the others. It also quietly closes the entire arc this fascicle opened many sections ago: the extended argument over why eighteen elements rather than seventeen or twelve turns out to have been building toward exactly this point all along — the finer granularity of eighteen exists because some listeners need it, not because five or twelve were somehow deficient.

45. Why Feeling and Perception Each Get Their Own Aggregate

A pointed question about the aggregates' own internal design: dozens of mental factors are bundled into one catch-all aggregate, so why do feeling and perception alone earn a dedicated aggregate each?

Chinese Source

何緣世尊說餘心所總置行蘊，別分受想為二蘊耶？頌曰：諍根生死因，及次第因故，於諸心所法，受想別為蘊。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why did the World-Honored One place the remaining mental factors together into a single aggregate of formations, while separating out feeling and perception as two distinct aggregates of their own? A verse states: because they are the root of contention, the cause of the cycle of birth-and-death, and the cause of teaching order, feeling and perception, among all the mental factors, are made into separate aggregates.

論曰：諍根有二，謂著諸欲及著諸見。此二受想，如其次第為最勝因，味受力故貪著諸欲，倒想力故貪著諸見。又生死法以受及想為最勝因，由耽著受起倒想故生死輪迴。由此二因及後當說次第因故，應知別立受想為蘊。其次第因，隣次當辯。

Prose: The root of contention is of two kinds — attachment to desires, and attachment to views. Feeling and perception are, respectively and in this order, the foremost cause of each: by the force of savoring feeling, one becomes attached to desires; by the force of distorted perception, one becomes attached to views. Moreover, feeling and perception are the foremost cause of the cycle of birth-and-death: it is by indulging in feeling that distorted perception arises, and so the wheel of birth-and-death turns. Because of these two causes, and because of the cause of teaching order to be explained next, one should understand that feeling and perception are established as separate aggregates. The cause of order will be explained in what follows immediately.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The asymmetry being justified is striking: volition, contact, attention, and dozens of other mental factors are all bundled into one catch-all aggregate (formations), yet feeling and perception each earn a dedicated aggregate of their own. The reason given is functional rather than metaphysical — these two are causally central to how beings get bound to saṃsāra in the first place. Attachment to sense pleasures is driven by the pull of savored feeling; attachment to wrong views is driven by perception's habit of mis-categorizing experience. The pairing is precise: feeling feeds desire, perception feeds views, mirroring the "two roots of contention" the verse names, and a second cause is added on top — feeling and perception together as the very engine of birth-and-death, since craving what is felt is what keeps the wheel turning. A third cause, "order," is explicitly promised for the section that follows.

46. Why the Unconditioned Isn't an Aggregate (Though It Is a Sense Field and an Element)

An asymmetry noted several sections ago now gets its own explicit justification: the sense fields and elements subsume the unconditioned, but the aggregates do not.

Chinese Source

何故無為說在處界，非蘊攝耶？頌曰：蘊不攝無為，義不相應故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is it that the unconditioned is said to be included among the sense fields and the elements, but is not subsumed under the aggregates? A verse states: the aggregates do not subsume the unconditioned, because its meaning is incompatible with theirs.

論曰：三無為法不可說在色等蘊中，與色等義不相應故。謂體非色乃至非識，亦不可說為第六蘊，彼與蘊義不相應故。聚義是蘊，如前具說。謂無為法，非如色等有過去等品類差別，可略一聚名無為蘊。又言取蘊為顯染依，染淨二依蘊言所顯。無為於此二義都無，義不相應故不立蘊。

Prose: The three unconditioned dharmas cannot be said to belong within the aggregate of form and the rest, because they are incompatible with the meaning of form and the other aggregates. That is: their essence is neither form nor any of the rest, up through consciousness — nor can they be said to constitute a sixth aggregate, since they are incompatible with the very meaning of "aggregate." "Heap" is the meaning of "aggregate," as fully explained before. The unconditioned dharmas, unlike form and the rest, do not have the differentiated categories of past, future, and present that could be briefly gathered into one heap and called "the aggregate of the unconditioned." Moreover, the term "aggregate-of-grasping" is used to indicate a support for defilement — the word "aggregate," used together with it, expresses both the defiled and undefiled supports together. The unconditioned has neither of these two meanings at all; its meaning being incompatible, it is not established as an aggregate.

有說：如瓶破非瓶，如是蘊息應非蘊，彼於處界例應成失。如是已說諸蘊廢立，當說次第。

Some say: just as a broken vase is no longer a vase, so too a ceased aggregate should no longer be an aggregate. But by this same logic, applied equally to the sense fields and the elements, the same reasoning would fail there too. Thus the establishment of the various aggregates has now been fully explained; their order should be explained next.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The deeper point being protected: the twelve sense fields and eighteen elements subsume everything, including the unconditioned (space and the two kinds of cessation), while the five aggregates subsume only conditioned dharmas — and this passage explains why that asymmetry is principled rather than arbitrary. "Aggregate" specifically means a heap gathered from things differentiated by past, present, and future, and the unconditioned, having no temporal differentiation at all, simply does not fit that definition — not through some ad hoc exclusion, but because the term's own meaning does not apply.

The "broken vase" alternative is floated and rejected for proving too much: if "a ceased X is no longer X" were the real principle at work, it would also wrongly exclude the unconditioned from the sense fields and elements — but those two schemes plainly do include it, so that reasoning cannot be what is actually doing the work. The "heap requires temporal differentiation" explanation is what survives.

47. The Aggregates' Order: Coarse to Subtle

With the aggregates fully established, the treatise turns to why they are always listed in exactly this sequence — form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness.

Chinese Source

頌曰：隨麁染器等，界別次第立。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: According to coarseness, defilement, being a vessel, and so on, the aggregates' distinct order is established.

論曰：色有對故諸蘊中麁，無色中麁唯受行相，故世說我手等痛言。待二想麁，男女等想易了知故。行麁過識，貪瞋等行易了知故。識最為細，總取境相難分別故。由此隨麁立蘊次第。

Prose: Form, being resistant, is the coarsest among the aggregates. Among the formless aggregates, feeling is relatively coarse in how it manifests — hence people commonly say things like "my hand hurts," treating the felt pain almost as tangibly as the hand itself. Relative to these two, perception is coarse, because notions like "male" and "female" are easily recognized. The formations are coarser than consciousness, because afflictions such as greed and hatred, being among the formations, are easily recognized. Consciousness is the subtlest of all, because grasping an object's overall character as a single, bare whole is difficult to differentiate any further. It is by this coarse-to-subtle ordering that the aggregates' sequence is established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Only the first of the verse's three stated criteria — coarseness — is explained here; "defilement" and "being a vessel" are named but their reasoning is still to come, and this translation marks that honestly rather than reading more into this passage than it yet says.

The coarse-to-subtle logic itself has a nice experiential ring to it: form is outright physical and resistant, the easiest thing to notice; feeling is nearly as directly graspable as the body itself, which is exactly why ordinary language conflates a felt pain with its physical location ("my hand hurts," not "a painful feeling arises in connection with my hand"); perception — recognizing "this is male," "this is female" — is a notch more abstract than raw feeling but still easy to catch oneself doing; the formations, especially emotionally charged ones like greed and hatred, are noticeable when they flare up, but are subtler than perceiving surface categories; and bare consciousness — the simple fact of cognizing an object at all, before any further mental commentary is layered on top — is hardest of all to isolate, since introspection almost always finds feeling, perception, or reaction already present by the time one looks.

48. Two More Reasons for the Aggregates' Order: A Causal Chain, and a Meal

Coarseness was only the first of several reasons the verse promised; the treatise now supplies a second and a third, each retelling the same five-aggregate sequence through a different lens.

Chinese Source

或從無始生死已來，男女於色更相愛樂，此由耽著樂受味故，耽受復因倒想生故，此倒想生由煩惱故，如是煩惱依識而生。此及前三皆染污識，由此隨染立蘊次第。

Ru-Yi Translation

Alternatively: from beginningless birth-and-death onward, male and female have delighted mutually in each other's form. This arises from indulging in the savor of pleasant feeling; that indulgence in feeling, in turn, arises because of distorted perception; that distorted perception arises because of the afflictions; and the afflictions arise in dependence on consciousness. These — the formations — together with the preceding three, all defile consciousness. It is by this chain of defilement that the aggregates' order is established.

或色如器、受類飲食、想同助味、行似厨人、識喻食者，故隨器等立蘊次第。

Or: form is like a vessel; feeling is like the food and drink within it; perception is like the seasoning that enhances its flavor; the formations are like the cook; and consciousness is like the one who eats. Hence, by this image of vessel and the rest, the aggregates' order is established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both reasons complement the coarse-to-subtle ordering from the previous section rather than replacing it. The causal-chain reading retraces the same five aggregates as a narrative of how craving actually operates: attraction begins with form, proceeds through the pleasure taken in feeling, which depends on perception's habit of mislabeling experience, which is itself powered by the afflictions grouped under "formations," all of which arise dependent on and defile consciousness — a chain running exactly form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness, giving a second justification for the identical sequence.

The vessel-food-cook-eater image gives each aggregate its own vivid, functional role in a single scene: form is the container holding everything, feeling is the content one tastes, perception is what flavors and identifies that content, the formations are the active agent preparing and processing experience, and consciousness is the one who ultimately consumes or registers it all — a homely, concrete picture for what could otherwise remain a dry list of five abstract categories.

49. A Fourth Reason: Which Realm Manifests Each Aggregate Most Clearly

A final reason ties the aggregates' order to cosmology itself, before the treatise closes this whole digression by returning to why feeling and perception specifically deserved separate treatment.

Chinese Source

或隨界別立蘊次第，謂欲界中有諸妙欲，色相顯了。色界靜慮有勝喜等，受相顯了。三無色中取空等相，想相顯了。第一有中思最為勝，行相顯了。此即識住，識住其中顯似世間田種次第。是故諸蘊次第如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, the aggregates' order is established according to cosmological realm: in the desire realm, there are various exquisite sensory pleasures, where form's character is most manifest. In the form realm's meditative absorptions, there is surpassing joy and the like, where feeling's character is most manifest. In the three lower formless realms, one grasps the character of space and the like, where perception's character is most manifest. In the Peak of Existence, volition is foremost, where the formations' character is most manifest. This is precisely the doctrine of the abodes of consciousness — consciousness abiding within these in a sequence resembling, in the world, a field and its seed. This, then, is why the aggregates' order is as it is.

由此五蘊無增減過，即由如是諸次第因，離行別立受想二蘊，謂受與想於諸行中相麁生染、類食同助，二界中強故別立蘊。

By this, the five aggregates are free from any fault of being too many or too few in number. And it is by just these several causes of order that feeling and perception are established as separate from the formations, as two distinct aggregates: feeling and perception are, among the formations, relatively coarse, give rise to defilement, and resemble food and seasoning working together — being especially prominent in these respects, they are established as separate aggregates.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "abodes of consciousness" (識住, vijñānasthiti) is a standard classical teaching about where consciousness takes root and flourishes across the cosmos, mapped here directly onto the aggregate sequence: the desire realm pairs with form, the form realm's absorptions with feeling, the lower formless attainments with perception, and the Peak of Existence — the highest formless level, "neither perception nor non-perception" — with the formations. The "field and seed" image completes it: just as a seed needs a field to take root and grow, consciousness needs one of these conditions as its field to become established and continue.

This is now the fourth distinct justification offered for the very same order — coarseness, the causal chain, the vessel-and-meal image, and now realm-manifestation — a cumulative style of argument typical of this treatise, stacking independent reasons rather than resting the conclusion on any single line of reasoning. The passage then closes by circling back to the question first raised several sections ago: feeling and perception earn their own aggregates because, among all the formations, they alone are distinctively coarse, defilement-generating, and food-and-seasoning-like — completing the "cause of order" that was promised earlier with a full accounting.

50. Why the Faculties Are Listed Eye, Ear, Nose, Tongue, Body, Mind: Present Objects and Derived Matter

With the aggregates' order settled, the treatise turns to a parallel question for the eighteen elements: why do the six faculties always appear in exactly this sequence?

Chinese Source

處界門中應先辨說六根次第，由斯境識次第可知。頌曰：前五境唯現，四境唯所造，餘用遠速明，或隨處次第。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the topic of the sense fields and elements, the order of the six faculties should be explained first, from which the order of their objects and consciousnesses can then be understood. A verse states: the first five take only present objects; four objects are made only of derived matter; the rest are ordered by reach, speed, and clarity; or, ordering follows bodily location.

論曰：於六根中，眼等前五唯取現境，是故先說。意境不定，三世無為，或唯取一或二三四。

Prose: Among the six faculties, the eye and the other four physical faculties take only present objects, and are therefore listed first. The mind's object is not fixed — it may be of the three times or the unconditioned, and it may take just one of these, or two, three, or all four at once.

所言四境唯所造者，前流至此。五中前四境唯所造，是故先說。身境不定，或取大種、或取造色、或二俱取，餘謂前四。

As for saying that four objects are made only of derived matter — this carries the previous point forward. Among the five physical faculties, the first four's objects are made only of derived matter, and are therefore listed before the fifth. The body faculty's object is not fixed: it may take the primary elements, or derived matter, or both together. "The rest," in the earlier clause, refers to these first four.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The verse is not applying one criterion uniformly to all six faculties — it peels them off in successive rounds, each governed by a different rule. In the first round, mind is set apart from all five physical senses and placed last overall, because its object alone can range across past, present, future, and the unconditioned at once, where the five physical senses are locked to present objects only. In the second round, among the remaining five, the eye, ear, nose, and tongue are grouped ahead of the body faculty because their objects are always derived matter (matter dependent on the four primary elements) rather than potentially being the primary elements themselves — the body faculty is the odd one out, since what it feels can be primary-element-level (hardness, wetness, and the like), derived, or both together. This progressively narrows the group still needing a finer ordering rule, which the next section supplies.

51. Reach, Speed, and Clarity — Then, Failing All Else, Location in the Body

Two more criteria finish sorting the remaining four faculties, offered as complementary rather than competing explanations of the same order.

Chinese Source

如其所應用遠速明，是故先說。謂眼耳根取遠境故，在二先說。二中眼用遠故先說，遠見山河不聞聲故。又眼用速，先遠見人撞擊鍾鼓後聞聲故。鼻舌兩根用俱非遠。先說鼻者，由速明故。如對香美諸飲食時，鼻先嗅香、舌後甞味。

Ru-Yi Translation

Correspondingly, the remaining faculties are ordered by reach, speed, and clarity, and are listed accordingly. That is: the eye and ear faculties take objects at a distance, and so both are listed ahead of the other two. Between these two, the eye is listed first because its reach is greater: one can see mountains and rivers at a distance without hearing any sound from them. Moreover, the eye's function is faster: one sees, at a distance, a person striking a bell or drum before one hears the sound. The nose and tongue faculties both lack reach. The nose is listed first because its function is faster and clearer: when facing fragrant, delicious food and drink, the nose smells the fragrance first, and the tongue tastes the flavor only afterward.

或於身中隨所依處上下差別說根次第，謂眼所依最居其上，次耳鼻舌身多居下，意無方處。有即依止諸根生者，故最後說。

Or, the faculties' order within the body is stated according to the difference in height of what each depends on: the eye's support is situated highest; next come the ear, nose, and tongue; the body faculty, for the most part, occupies the lower regions; the mind has no fixed location at all. Since it is that which arises in dependence on the other faculties, it is listed last of all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the treatise's characteristic move of stacking several independent, converging justifications rather than settling for one. By reach, eye and ear (both work at a distance) outrank nose and tongue (both need proximity). Within the distance pair, the eye beats the ear on both reach and speed — the drum-and-bell example is a classic observation anyone can confirm by watching a distant bell struck: the strike is seen before its sound arrives, because light simply outruns sound. Within the contact pair, the nose beats the tongue via the equally familiar experience of smelling food before tasting it.

The alternative "bodily location" ordering offers a second, complete account of the very same six-item sequence: eye highest, then ear, nose, and tongue, then the body, with mind placed last precisely because it has no fixed location of its own at all — it simply arises leaning on whichever of the other faculties is active. As with the aggregates' order two sections ago, these are not competing final answers but compatible routes to, and confirmations of, the identical conclusion.

52. Why Only One Field Is Called "Form," and Only One "Dharma"

A closing naming puzzle mirrors ones the treatise has already worked through for "support" and for consciousness: all ten physical sense fields are technically "form," yet only one is named "the form field."

Chinese Source

何緣十處皆色蘊攝，唯於一種立色處名？又十二處體皆是法，唯於一種立法處名？頌曰：為差別最勝，攝多增上法，故一處名色，一名為法處。

Ru-Yi Translation

For what reason are all ten physical sense fields subsumed under the aggregate of form, yet only one among them is given the name "form field"? And why is it that, though the essence of all twelve sense fields is "dharma," only one among them is given the name "dharma field"? A verse states: for the sake of distinction, being most excellent, and subsuming many dominant dharmas — for these reasons, one field is named "form," and one is named "the dharma field."

論曰：為差別者，為令了知境有境性種種差別，故於色蘊就差別相建立十處不總為一。若無眼等差別想名，而體是色立名色處，此為眼等名所簡別，雖標總稱而即別名。

Prose: "For the sake of distinction" means: in order to make known the many distinctions among objects and object-possessing natures, the aggregate of form is established as ten distinct sense fields according to their differentiating characteristics, rather than treated as one undifferentiated whole. If there were no distinguishing names such as "eye" and the rest, then whatever has the essential nature of form would simply be named "the form field"; as it stands, this name is what sets it apart from names like "eye" and the others — though it carries the general designation, it thereby functions as a specific name as well.

又諸色中色處最勝，故立通名。由有對故，手等觸時即便變壞。及有見故，可示在此在彼差別。又諸世間唯於此處同說為色，非於眼等。

Moreover, among all forms, the form field is the most excellent, and so it alone is given the general name. Because it is resistant, when the hand and the like come into contact with it, it is immediately altered or damaged. And because it is visible, it can be pointed out as differentiated, here or there. Moreover, in ordinary worldly usage, it is only this field that is uniformly called "form" — not the eye and the other faculties, even though they too are technically form.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The argument for why "form" becomes the proper name of just one of the ten physical sense fields, out of all ten that are technically "form," unfolds in two stages. First, by elimination: nine of the ten already have their own distinguishing names — eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, sound, smell, taste, touch — so the one field left without a more specific name naturally inherits the general category-name itself. Second, several reasons are stacked for why visible-form specifically deserves that honor rather than, say, the eye faculty or sound: resistance is cited almost in passing, since it is shared across all matter, but visibility is the genuinely distinguishing property — visible-form is the one thing among the ten that can literally be pointed out as "here, not there," a directly perceptible spatial differentiation none of the others offer in quite the same way. Ordinary language already confirms this: everyday speech calls visible-form simply "form," and never casually extends that word to the eye or the ear, even though Abhidharma technically classifies both as form as well.

53. The Dharma Field's Special Claim: Nirvana Itself, and a Second Opinion

The mirror-image question for "dharma field" gets a mirror-image answer — not visibility this time, but scope and rank.

Chinese Source

又為差別立一法處，非於一切，如色應知。又於此中攝受想等眾多法故，應立通名。又增上法，所謂涅槃，此中攝故獨立為法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Also for the sake of distinction, one field alone is established as "the dharma field," not applied to every field — this should be understood just as with "form." Moreover, since this field subsumes many dharmas — feeling, perception, and the rest — it deserves the general name. Moreover, the most dominant dharma of all, namely nirvana, is subsumed within it, and so it alone is established as "the dharma field."

有餘師說：色處中有二十種色最麁顯故，肉、天、聖慧三眼境故，獨立色名。法處中有諸法名故、諸法智故，獨立法名。

Other masters say instead: within the form field there are twenty kinds of form, which are the coarsest and most manifest of all, and which serve as the object of three kinds of eye — the fleshly eye, the divine eye, and the eye of noble wisdom — and so it alone is given the name "form." Within the dharma field are found the names of all dharmas, and the wisdom that knows all dharmas, and so it alone is given the name "dharma."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Where "form field" earned its generic name through visibility, "dharma field" — the mind's own object-domain, the twelfth sense field — earns it through scope and rank instead. It is the one field that acts as a genuine catch-all, holding feeling, perception, and the many other mental factors that have no field of their own; and it is the one field that contains nirvana, the single most exalted thing that exists in this system, described here simply as "the dominant dharma." Sheer inclusiveness plus supreme content together justify the generic name.

The "other masters" cited afterward are a recurring device in this treatise for registering a rival Abhidharma opinion without necessarily endorsing it. Their version grounds "form" in the twenty kinds of visible color and shape being maximally coarse and perceptible — visible even to three distinct kinds of eye: the ordinary fleshly eye, the divine eye of higher meditative beings, and the eye of wisdom that directly perceives truth — and grounds "dharma" in its containing both the names of all dharmas and the wisdom that knows them. Where the main account reasons from ontology (nirvana's supremacy, mental factors' abundance), this alternative reasons from perceptibility and cognition instead — a genuinely different angle on the same two naming puzzles.

54. The Buddha's 84,000 Teachings: Speech or Concept?

The naming arc closes, and the long-promised cross-referencing finally begins: other sutras group aggregates, sense fields, and elements under different names, and the treatise sets out to show these all fit inside the scheme already explained.

Chinese Source

且辯攝餘諸蘊名想。頌曰：牟尼說法蘊，數有八十千，彼體語或名，此色行蘊攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Let us first discuss how the other names and concepts for the aggregates are subsumed. A verse states: the Sage taught dharma-aggregates numbering eighty-four thousand; their essence is either speech or name, and these are subsumed under the aggregate of form, or the aggregate of formations.

論曰：諸說佛教語為體者，彼說法蘊皆色蘊攝。諸說佛教名為體者，彼說法蘊皆行蘊攝。此諸法蘊其量云何？

Prose: Those who hold that the essence of the Buddha's teaching is speech-sound say that these dharma-aggregates are all subsumed under the aggregate of form. Those who hold that the essence of the Buddha's teaching is name say that these dharma-aggregates are all subsumed under the aggregate of formations. What, then, is the measure of each of these dharma-aggregates?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a classic Sarvāstivāda debate over what the Buddha's teaching actually consists of: is a teaching fundamentally the physical sound of speech — matter, since sound is one of the ten physical sense fields — or is it fundamentally the name or concept conveyed, classified in this system as "the body of names" (nāmakāya), a formation dissociated from mind: real, but neither physical nor mental in the ordinary sense? Depending on which view one takes, the famous eighty-four thousand dharma-aggregates — the traditional count of the Buddha's discrete teachings — end up subsumed under a different aggregate: form, if teaching is essentially vocal sound, or formations, if it is essentially conceptual. This exercises the very apparatus this fascicle has spent over fifty sections building, now turned outward to cross-check a well-known sutra figure against it.

55. How Big Is Each Teaching-Unit? Two Rival Measures

Having placed the eighty-four thousand teachings within the aggregates, the treatise asks how large each individual "dharma-aggregate" actually is — and records two quite different answers.

Chinese Source

頌曰：有言諸法蘊，量如彼論說；或隨蘊等言，如實行對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: Some say each dharma-aggregate is of the size stated in that treatise; or, corresponding to terms like "aggregate" and the rest, each covers the actual practice of its antidote.

論曰：有諸師言：八萬法蘊一一量等《法蘊足論》，謂彼一一有六千頌，如對法中《法蘊足論》說。

Prose: Some masters say: the eighty-four thousand dharma-aggregates are each equal in size to the Dharmaskandha treatise — that is, each one comprises six thousand verses, just as stated in that Abhidharma text, the Dharmaskandha.

或說：法蘊隨蘊等言一一差別數有八萬，謂蘊、處、界、緣起、諦、食、靜慮、無量、無色、解脫、勝處、遍處、覺品、神通、無諍、願智、無礙解等，一一教門名一法蘊。

Or, it is said: the dharma-aggregates, corresponding to terms like "aggregate" and so on, each differ from one another, numbering eighty-four thousand in total — that is: the aggregates, the sense fields, the elements, dependent origination, the truths, nutriment, the dhyānas, the immeasurables, the formless attainments, the liberations, the bases of mastery, the totality-bases, the branches of enlightenment, the supernormal powers, freedom from contention, the wisdom of aspiration, unobstructed understanding, and so on — each teaching-approach is called one dharma-aggregate.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two measures slice up the same famous figure in genuinely different ways. The first treats "dharma-aggregate" as a fixed length: each of the eighty-four thousand is roughly the size of the Dharmaskandha, one of the seven canonical Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma treatises, traditionally about six thousand verses long — a striking, almost self-referential move, using an actual named text as the literal yardstick for measuring scriptural units. The second treats "dharma-aggregate" as a topical category instead: each major subject the tradition teaches counts as one dharma-aggregate, and the list given here opens, notably, with the very three schemes this fascicle itself has spent over fifty sections unpacking — aggregates, sense fields, elements — before continuing through dependent origination, the four truths, nutriment, the dhyānas, and a long further list of classical topics, all told numbering eighty-four thousand.

A third account of this number — arguably the one most commonly heard — is still to come: that beings have eighty-four thousand distinct patterns of affliction, and the Buddha taught exactly eighty-four thousand dharma-aggregates as their precise antidotes. The treatise is building toward that explanation next.

Fascicle 1: More to Come

The treatise turns to what it calls the "true" account of the eighty-four thousand dharma-aggregates: that beings to be taught have eighty-four thousand distinct behavioral patterns rooted in greed, hatred, and the rest, and the World-Honored One taught exactly that many dharma-aggregates as their antidotes. From there, several more sutra enumerations — the ten totality-bases, the eight bases of mastery, the formless bases, the bases of liberation, and the non-percipient and neither-perception-nor-non-perception states — will be cross-referenced against the aggregates, sense fields, and elements, before fascicle 1 closes with the Bahudhātuka-sūtra's sixty-two elements.
