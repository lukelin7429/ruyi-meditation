The Nine Bonds

Fascicle 21 completes the third part of Chapter 5 (分別隨眠品第五之三, the Anuśaya-nirdeśa), taking up the sūtras' remaining collective names for the anuśayas and their entanglements: "bonds" (saṃyojana), "bondages" (bandhana), "secondary afflictions" (upakleśa), and "entanglements" (paryavasthāna) proper, followed by the five hindrances (nīvaraṇa) and the treatise's full technical account of how afflictions are actually severed — the four causes of severance, the four counteragents, the four kinds of "distance," and the ninefold classification of "full understanding" (parijñā). With this fascicle's close, the treatise's entire discussion of the anuśayas — spanning fascicles 19 through 21 — comes to an end.

1. The Nine Bonds: Why Views and Clinging Each Split into Two

Having discussed the anuśayas and their entanglements under the names outflow, flood, yoke, and clinging, the treatise now asks whether these exhaust the sūtras' vocabulary for these same phenomena, or whether further collective names exist. Indeed there are others: the very same afflictions, considered from the angle of their distinctions as bond, bondage, anuśaya, secondary affliction, and entanglement, are spoken of under five further collective names. Taking up "bond" first: there are nine kinds — the bond of craving, the bond of hatred, the bond of conceit, the bond of ignorance, the bond of views, the bond of clinging, the bond of doubt, the bond of jealousy, and the bond of stinginess. Among these, the bond of craving refers to greed bound to all three realms; the remaining bonds' specific character should be explained analogously, as appropriate. The bond of views refers collectively to the three kinds of view (self-view, extreme views, and wrong view); the bond of clinging refers collectively to the two kinds of clinging (view-attachment and precept-and-vow attachment). On this basis, a genuine question arises: is it possible for something associated with view to be bound by the craving-bond without being bound by the view-bond, even though the view-anuśaya still undergoes latent increase there? Indeed it is possible — specifically, at the stage where the cognition of the origin has already arisen but the cognition of cessation has not yet arisen, the dharmas associated with the two kinds of clinging that are abandoned by seeing cessation and the path are bound, with respect to their object, by the craving-bond rather than the view-bond, since the pervasive view-bond has already been permanently eliminated by that point; yet these same dharmas still undergo latent increase from the view-anuśaya belonging to the two kinds of clinging, since that anuśaya continues to take them as its object. Why are the three views specifically grouped together and established as a single "view-bond," while the two kinds of clinging are separately established as their own distinct "clinging-bond"? Because the three views and the two kinds of clinging are, respectively, equal in number of underlying items — the three views comprise eighteen items, and so do the two kinds of clinging, hence they are called "equal in item-count." Moreover, the three views constitute what is grasped, while the two kinds of clinging constitute the grasping itself, hence they are also called "equal in what is taken and what takes" — and it is precisely because what is grasped and what does the grasping are functionally distinct that they are established as two separate bonds rather than one. Why, among all the various entanglements, are only jealousy and stinginess specifically elevated to the status of bonds, while the rest are not? Because these two alone are both exclusively unwholesome and capable of arising with a certain independent, unrestrained potency of their own — qualities the other entanglements lack. If entanglements numbered only eight, this explanation would suffice on its own; but since this tradition actually recognizes ten entanglements, this explanation alone is not sufficient, since malice and concealment, among the other eight, also share both of these same two qualities. For this reason, it must further be said that jealousy and stinginess are singled out because their fault is especially severe, arising far more frequently than the others; because these two specifically serve as the cause of low social status and poverty; because they pervasively manifest as accompaniments to virtually every other secondary affliction; and because they specifically disturb and trouble both the monastic and lay communities, or both gods and asuras, or both of the two superior destinies of humans and gods, or both other beings and one's own community, depending on which framing one prefers.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section introduces "bond" (saṃyojana, 結) as yet another collective term for the anuśayas, adding to the "outflow/flood/yoke/clinging" scheme already covered in fascicle 20 — we note for readers tracking this project's growing vocabulary that this tradition uses at least five distinct collective terms (bond, bondage, anuśaya, secondary affliction, entanglement) for closely related but not identical groupings of the same underlying afflictions, each carving up the territory slightly differently depending on which specific quality (severity, frequency, sharpness, and so on) is being emphasized.

We should flag that the fine distinction drawn here — something can be "bound" by the craving-bond specifically, rather than the view-bond, even while still undergoing latent increase from a view-anuśaya — depends on tracking the earlier "pervasive vs. non-pervasive" anuśaya framework from fascicle 19 and the "binding via object vs. via association" distinction from fascicle 20; we have tried to make the logic self-contained here, but readers wanting the fullest picture may find it useful to recall those earlier discussions.

2. The Five Lower-Portion Bonds, and the Five Upper-Portion Bonds

The treatise now takes up a further sūtra teaching that speaks of a distinct set of five bonds "conducive to the lower portion": self-view, precept-and-vow attachment, doubt, desire-realm greed, and hatred. Why are precisely these five given this specific name? Because all five specifically favor and reinforce the "lower portion" — that is, only the desire realm is properly called the "lower portion," and each of these five afflictions specifically serves to strengthen and perpetuate a being's continued attachment to it. The latter two of the five (desire-greed and hatred) directly prevent a being from ever transcending the desire realm at all; even should a being manage to transcend it through the other three (self-view, precept-and-vow attachment, and doubt), those same three would then act like a prison guard or a border patrol, pulling the being back down into the desire realm once again. Other teachers offer a related but distinct explanation: "lower" here refers to beings of the lower type (ordinary, unenlightened beings) as well as to the lower realm (the desire realm) — the first three afflictions specifically obstruct such lower beings from transcending their condition, while the latter two specifically prevent them from transcending the lower realm itself, and so all five together earn the name "conducive to the lower portion." A further question arises: since a stream-enterer is actually said to have severed six distinct afflictions in total upon attaining that fruit, why does the sūtra teaching speak only of severing three bonds? Properly speaking, it should indeed be said that six afflictions are severed, but the treatise groups these six under three broader categories for the sake of concision: among what is severed, there are three underlying types, one of which pervasively extends across two categories of abandonment, and another which extends across all four, and it is by naming these three categories that the fuller sixfold severance is being economically summarized. Moreover, within what is severed, three of the anuśayas operate by riding upon three others in a specific derivative relationship — extreme views ride upon self-view, view-attachment rides upon precept-and-vow attachment, and wrong view rides upon doubt — and so, by naming the three underlying anuśayas that these three derivative ones ride upon, all six are, in effect, already accounted for by naming just three. Other teachers explain this differently: anyone setting out toward a different destination faces three general kinds of obstacle — unwillingness to set out at all, being led astray onto a wrong path, and uncertainty about which path is correct. A person seeking liberation faces exactly these same three kinds of obstacle: self-view, out of fear of what liberation might mean for one's sense of self, produces unwillingness to set out toward it at all; precept-and-vow attachment, through clinging to a mistaken path, leads one astray from the true path; and doubt produces deep uncertainty about the true path itself. Since the Buddha wishes to reveal that a stream-enterer has permanently severed these three specific obstacles standing in the way of liberation, the teaching speaks of "three" bonds severed, even though six afflictions are technically eliminated. The sūtras elsewhere similarly speak of five further bonds "conducive to the upper portion," corresponding in a parallel structure to the five just discussed: form-realm greed, formless-realm greed, restlessness, conceit, and ignorance — these five, so long as they remain unabandoned, prevent a being from ever transcending the upper realms (the form and formless realms), and it is because they favor and reinforce continued attachment to those upper realms that they earn this corresponding name.

That is, among what is severed, there are three types — one extends through two categories, [others] through four categories — and speaking of three types encompasses these three categories. The Buddha reveals that a stream-enterer has permanently severed these obstacles to attaining liberation, hence speaks of three as severed. The Buddha, in another sūtra, just as with "conducive to the lower portion," speaks of "conducive to the upper portion" as likewise having five kinds. Verse: conducive to the upper portion also has five — the two cravings of form and formless, restlessness, conceit, and ignorance — since these prevent one from transcending the upper [realms]. Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should highlight the "obstacle to a journey" analogy here as a particularly clear piece of moral psychology: unwillingness to set out (fear of what liberation implies for one's sense of self), being led astray (clinging to an ineffective substitute practice), and doubt (uncertainty about the correct path itself) are presented as the three fundamental ways any spiritual seeker can fail to make progress, with self-view, precept-and-vow attachment, and doubt named as their respective Buddhist-technical causes. This offers a genuinely illuminating rationale for why a stream-enterer's decisive achievement is described in terms of overcoming exactly these three, even though the fuller technical account involves six distinct anuśayas.

We should also flag that this section preserves multiple explanations, presented as alternatives rather than adjudicated to a single winner, for both "why five bonds are called lower-portion" and "why only three are said to be severed despite six being technically eliminated" — consistent with our practice throughout this project, we have kept each explanation distinct rather than blending them into a single composite account.

3. The Three Bondages, and Secondary Affliction

Having finished discussing "bond," the treatise turns to "bondage." Bondage comes in three kinds: the bondage of craving (referring to craving of all three realms), the bondage of hatred (referring to hatred in every form), and the bondage of delusion (referring to delusion in every form). Why are precisely these three, and no others, established under the specific name "bondage"? Because bondage is spoken of specifically in correspondence with the three kinds of feeling — pleasant, painful, and neutral. With respect to pleasant feeling, it is specifically the bondage of craving that undergoes latent increase, both through this feeling serving as its object and through direct association with it; the same pattern holds, correspondingly, for hatred with respect to painful feeling, and for delusion with respect to neutral feeling. Although craving and hatred can, in certain circumstances, also arise with respect to neutral feeling, this correspondence is established with reference to the predominant, most characteristic pattern found within a single being's own mental continuum, not as an absolute, exceptionless rule covering every possible case. Having now discussed "bondage," the treatise turns to "anuśaya" itself, which has already been explained at length earlier — coming in six, seven, ten, or ninety-eight varieties, depending on which classificatory scheme is being used, exactly as previously described. Having discussed "anuśaya," the treatise turns finally to "secondary affliction" (upakleśa). These same root afflictions are also called secondary afflictions, since they all trouble and disturb the mind in a manner analogous to a root affliction's own basic function. But there also exist further mental factors, distinct from these root afflictions, that likewise belong to the aggregate of mental factors and likewise arise specifically in connection with a defiled mind — these, too, are called secondary afflictions, precisely because they arise following upon and in dependence on the root afflictions themselves, even though they are not, strictly speaking, root afflictions in their own right, since they do not constitute the most fundamental, independently arising kind of defilement. Their fuller characteristics will be explained in greater detail in the later chapter on miscellaneous topics; only a brief, preliminary account is given here for now.

4. The Eight (or Ten) Entanglements, Traced to Their Roots

Having discussed secondary affliction generally, the treatise now turns to "entanglement" (paryavasthāna) specifically, since the root afflictions are themselves also called entanglements — the sūtras, for instance, speak of the "entanglement of desire-greed" as a supporting condition. The Prakaraṇapāda recognizes eight entanglements, while the Vaibhāṣika tradition recognizes ten, adding malice and concealment to the original eight. Lack of shame and lack of conscience have already been explained in earlier fascicles. Jealousy is the specific state of mind that cannot bear the flourishing success of others. Stinginess is an attachment to one's wealth or knowledge so possessive that it directly opposes any skillful, generous giving of either. Remorse is identical to the previously discussed factor of "regret" already explained in an earlier fascicle. Drowsiness is characterized by causing the mind to become dull and contracted, lacking the mental energy needed to properly sustain the body's activity; both remorse and drowsiness, as entanglements, refer specifically to their defiled forms. Restlessness and torpor have likewise already been explained previously. Malice is the specific hostile agitation of mind, excluding outright hatred and harmful intent, directed toward either sentient or insentient objects. Concealment is the specific act of hiding one's own faults from others. Among these ten entanglements, lack of shame, stinginess, and restlessness are classified as outflows of greed; lack of conscience, drowsiness, and torpor are classified as outflows of ignorance; jealousy and malice are classified as outflows of hatred; and remorse is classified as an outflow of doubt. As for concealment, some teachers classify it as an outflow of greed, others as an outflow of ignorance, and still others hold that it arises from both roots together, corresponding respectively to cases where one conceals a fault out of a desire for personal gain or advantage, and cases where one conceals a fault simply out of not properly understanding its true gravity.

5. The Six Affliction-Stains, and Which Are Abandoned by Seeing

The treatise now turns to a further set of six "affliction-stains," distinct from the ten entanglements already discussed. Vexation is a stubborn, entrenched clinging to matters one is already at fault in, such that one refuses to accept correction or admonition even when it accords with reason. Harm is the specific capacity to oppress and press upon others, giving rise to acts such as striking or reviling them. Resentment is the repeated, deliberate dwelling upon an object of malice, forming a grudge one refuses to release. Flattery is a crookedness of mind that prevents one from genuinely and accurately revealing oneself as one truly is — either falsely denying what is actually so, or employing indirect means to obscure a matter and keep others from understanding it clearly. Deceit is the deliberate act of misleading others. Arrogance has already been explained in an earlier fascicle. These six arise from the root afflictions and, being coarse and defiling in character, are called "affliction-stains." Among these six, deceit and arrogance are classified as outflows of greed; harm and resentment are classified as outflows of hatred; vexation is classified as an outflow of view-attachment; and flattery is classified as an outflow of views generally. As the saying goes, "what is it that is 'crooked'?" — it refers to wrong views of every kind, and it is for this reason that flattery is definitively classified as an outflow of views. These stains, together with the entanglements already discussed, all arise from the root afflictions, and it is on this basis that all of them are given the collective name "secondary affliction." A further question then arises: by which path — seeing or cultivation — are these stains and entanglements actually abandoned? Among the ten entanglements, lack of shame, lack of conscience, drowsiness, torpor, and restlessness are abandoned by both seeing and cultivation, since these five are capable of arising in association with afflictions belonging to either of these two categories of abandonment — whichever category the affliction they accompany belongs to, that same category applies to them as well. 6. Moral Quality Across Realms, and the God Who Flattered a Monk

The treatise now asks which moral quality these secondary afflictions possess across the three realms. Desire-realm drowsiness, torpor, and restlessness are each capable of being either unwholesome or morally neutral, depending on the specific context in which they arise; all the remaining desire-realm secondary afflictions are exclusively unwholesome. Whatever secondary afflictions exist in the form and formless realms, by contrast, are, in every case, exclusively of a neutral moral quality, since — as already explained in earlier fascicles — no affliction belonging to those higher, more refined realms can bring about a genuinely painful ripened result of the kind that would qualify it as unwholesome. 8. Which Afflictions Pair with Which Feelings

The treatise now works out, in careful detail, exactly which root afflictions are associated with which of the five feeling-faculties. Among the desire-realm afflictions, greed is associated with joy and pleasure, since it operates through a jubilant mode pervading all six consciousnesses. Hatred is associated with distress and pain, since it operates through a distressed mode likewise pervading all six consciousnesses. Ignorance is associated with all four of these (joy, pleasure, distress, and pain), since it can operate through either a jubilant or a distressed mode, again pervading all six consciousnesses. Wrong view is associated with distress or joy, since its jubilant or distressed mode operates only at the level of mental consciousness — the reason wrong view can take on either mode is that it may serve, respectively, as the basis for previously committing unwholesome deeds or wholesome deeds. Doubt is associated with distress, since its distressed mode operates only at the level of mental consciousness, this being because one caught in genuine uncertainty, seeking a definite resolution, naturally experiences a sorrowful, troubled state of mind. The remaining four views (self-view, extreme views, view-attachment, and precept-and-vow attachment) together with conceit are associated with joy, since their jubilant mode operates only at the level of mental consciousness. This account has been given with reference to each affliction's own specific, characteristic association; speaking instead in terms of a more general association applicable to all of them, every one of these afflictions can also be associated with the feeling of equanimity, since, at the specific point where a given anuśaya's continuum is actually being severed, its force has already grown weak and faint, and it necessarily then abides in a state of equanimity. Having explained this pattern for the desire realm, the treatise extends the same analysis to the higher realms: in every case, as appropriate, an affliction is associated with whichever feelings arise together with the specific consciousness of its own realm and stage — that is, in a stage possessing all four types of consciousness, each affliction arising in connection with a given consciousness is associated, pervasively, with whatever feelings arise together with that same consciousness; in a stage possessing only mental consciousness, the afflictions arising there are associated pervasively with whatever feeling accompanies mental consciousness specifically. 10. The Five Hindrances: Why These Five, Why These Pairings

The treatise now turns to a different, sūtra-based classification of afflictions: the five hindrances (nīvaraṇa) that block meditative progress. Verse: the five hindrances exist only in the desire realm, because each pair's food, cure, and function are the same; though each pair consists of two factors, each is established as a single hindrance; and because they obstruct the [five foundational] aggregates [of the path], there are only five. The Buddha spoke, in the sūtras, of five hindrances: the hindrance of desire-and-greed, the hindrance of ill will, the hindrance of torpor-and-drowsiness, the hindrance of restlessness-and-remorse, and the hindrance of doubt. One might ask whether torpor, restlessness, and doubt are confined to the desire realm in the same way as desire-greed, ill will, drowsiness, and remorse, or whether they instead pervade all three realms; it should be understood that all five are confined to the desire realm alone, since the sūtras describe these five as forming a purely and completely unwholesome grouping, and the form and formless realms contain nothing unwholesome. Torpor and drowsiness share the same food (lethargy, displeasure, physical fatigue, dietary imbalance, and a dulled state of mind) and the same non-food (the perception of light), and both alike cause the mind to sink into dullness. Restlessness and remorse share the same food (thoughts of one's relatives, one's homeland, of not dying, and of past amusements) and the same non-food (calm-abiding meditation), and both alike cause the mind to be unable to settle.

We should flag that this passage openly presents three non-identical explanatory accounts of the five hindrances without the treatise declaring a single winner among them, and we have preserved all three exactly as given rather than collapsing them into one. The first account maps torpor-drowsiness onto the wisdom-aggregate and restlessness-remorse onto the concentration-aggregate; this immediately runs into an internal ordering problem (concentration should logically be discussed, and obstructed, before wisdom, yet the sūtra names the torpor-drowsiness hindrance before the restlessness-remorse hindrance). We render all three as the text presents them, without imposing a resolution the treatise itself does not supply.

We should also note the technical term "food" (食) here does not refer to literal nourishment but to whatever mental or environmental condition feeds and sustains a given hindrance's arising, with "non-food" naming its counteragent; this figurative food/cure framework recurs elsewhere in this tradition's analysis of mental cultivation, and we have kept the literal "food/non-food" rendering (rather than paraphrasing as "cause/antidote") since the text's own extended food metaphor — provisions, capacity, nourishment — depends on it.

11. Four Causes by Which an Affliction Is Severed, and Four Kinds of Counteragent

The treatise raises a puzzle: for afflictions universal to other realms, and for afflictions abandoned by seeing cessation and the path that take conditioned objects, at the very stage of severance one does not yet fully know their object, and even once one does fully know their object, they are not thereby severed. By what cause, then, is such an affliction actually severed? It cannot simply be that full knowledge of the object is what severs it. In fact, there are four causes by which an affliction may be severed altogether. The afflictions abandoned by seeing the truths are severed by three of these four: first, by fully knowing their object — this covers the afflictions taking objects within one's own realm that are abandoned by seeing suffering and its origin, and the afflictions taking unconditioned objects abandoned by seeing cessation and the path; second, by severing what takes them as object — this covers the afflictions taking objects in another realm abandoned by seeing suffering and its origin, since once the same-realm afflictions that take these as their own object are severed, these too are severed accordingly; third, by severing their object itself — this covers the afflictions taking conditioned objects abandoned by seeing cessation and the path, since once the unconditioned object underlying them is severed, they too are severed accordingly. The afflictions abandoned by cultivation, by contrast, are severed only by the fourth cause: the mere arising of a counteragent, since once the counteragent path of a given grade arises, every affliction of that grade is severed at once — the afflictions of the highest-of-the-highest grade being countered by the path of the lowest-of-the-lowest grade, and, conversely, the afflictions of the lowest-of-the-lowest grade being countered by the path of the highest-of-the-highest grade, a point to be developed further later.

12. What, Precisely, Is Severed When an Affliction Is Abandoned

The treatise now asks what exactly "severance" consists of. It should be understood that when an affliction attains final severance, it is not that the affliction is somehow detached from the mental factors associated with it; rather, it is made to become permanently separated from its object, such that it no longer arises with respect to that object. This account works straightforwardly for a future affliction, since it is indeed possible to prevent an affliction from ever arising again toward its object. But how, then, can a past affliction be said to be "severed," given that — as this school's own analysis of the three times has already established — a past dharma still exists in its own right, and so is not simply nothing that could be treated as already handled? If one supposes that the verse's phrase "with respect to its object" means that a past affliction is severed simply by now fully knowing its object, this too fails, since that account does not hold good across every case already discussed. The treatise resolves this by locating "severance" not in the object at all, but in the practitioner's own continuum: for afflictions within one's own mental continuum, severance consists in one's own attainment of separation from them; for afflictions within another person's continuum, and for all form dharmas and undefiled dharmas that might otherwise serve as an object of affliction, their "severance" consists simply in this — that every affliction in one's own continuum capable of taking them as an object has itself been finally severed.

13. The Four Kinds of "Distance," and How Past and Future Are "Distant" at All

Returning to the counteragent of distancing introduced two sections earlier, the treatise now asks how many kinds of "distance" there are. There are four. Distance of characteristic: as with the four great elements (earth, water, fire, wind), which, though arising together within a single aggregation, are called distant from one another because their characteristics differ. Distance of counteragent: as with keeping the precepts and violating them, which, though occurring within a single body, are called distant because they counteract one another. Distance of place: as with the eastern sea and the western sea, which, though existing within a single world, are called distant because they are separated by direction and location. Distance of time: as with the past and the future, which, though both established with respect to a single dharma, are called distant because they are separated by their temporal divisions. This raises an immediate difficulty: distance is always measured relative to the present, yet the moment that has just ceased and the moment just about to arise are immediately adjacent to the present — so how can they be called "distant" at all? The answer given is that they earn the name "distant" simply because their temporal nature differs from that of the present, not because a long duration must actually separate them from it.

The treatise then works through a chain of increasingly technical objections probing this answer. If temporal difference alone earns the name "distant," the present too ought to be called distant, since its temporal nature likewise differs from that of the past and future — yet it is not so called. If one instead says the past and future are "distant" because they lack activity, being apart from function, then unconditioned dharmas are equally without function, so why are they called "near"? If one says unconditioned dharmas are "near" because they can be universally attained in the present, the past and future should by the same logic also count as near — yet how, then, can something like space, itself unconditioned, be called "near"? The treatise resolves the puzzle by relocating what "distance" in time actually tracks: according to the correct principle, the past and future are called distant because each is separated from the dharma's own intrinsic characteristic — the future, because it has not yet attained that characteristic; the past, because it has already relinquished it. 14. No Affliction Is Severed Twice, but Its Separation May Be Re-Attained Up to Six Times

The treatise now asks: once an affliction is severed by its counteragent path, and one's practice subsequently advances further, is that same affliction ever severed a second time? And can the state of separation (visaṃyoga) thereby attained itself be attained again, in some more excellent form? Once an affliction obtains the path capable of severing it, that affliction is severed all at once by that path — there is no sense in which it is severed a second time later on. Yet although the separation attained does not itself gradually improve merely by the path's continuing to advance, it is nevertheless possible for that same superior attainment to arise anew as the path advances further. This "re-attainment" can occur at up to six distinct times: the arising of the counteragent path (specifically the path of liberation), the attainment of any of the four fruits (stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, or arhatship), and the refining of one's faculties (converting from duller to sharper faculties). At these six times, as the path advances, the separation already attained from a given affliction gives rise anew to a superior attainment of that same separation.

16. The Nine Parijñās Sorted by Patience, Knowledge, Realm, and Path

The treatise now works through the nine full understandings along six successive axes of classification. First, patience versus knowledge: six are the fruit of the path of patience — the six full understandings belonging to what is abandoned by seeing the truths across the three realms — while three are the fruit of the path of knowledge — the full understandings of the exhaustion of the lower-portion bonds, of craving for form, and of all bonds, since these three are the fruit of the path of cultivation. A patience-fruit can be called "full understanding" because every patience is itself an attendant of knowledge, much as a king's own attendants may provisionally be addressed by the king's own name — or, alternatively, because patience and knowledge here share one and the same fruit. Second, preparatory versus root dhyāna: the preparatory stage of dhyāna has all nine as its fruit, since it alone suffices to sever what is abandoned by both seeing and cultivation across all three realms. The root stage of dhyāna has, according to two competing accounts, either five or eight as its fruit: the Vaibhāṣika masters hold that the root stage can eternally sever only the afflictions of the form and formless realms, leaving the severance of desire-realm afflictions to the preparatory stage alone (hence five); but the Venerable Ghoṣaka holds that the root stage also serves as a counteragent for desire-realm afflictions, since one who has already become free of desire-realm defilement beforehand and then enters the seeing of the truths by relying on the root stage attains an undefiled severance of what is abandoned by seeing within the desire realm by way of a distinct path as well — except for the full understanding of the exhaustion of the lower-portion bonds, which remains the preparatory stage's fruit alone (hence eight).

17. Why Exactly Nine: The Four (or Five) Conditions for "Full Understanding"

Although afflicted dharmas are severed across many distinct stages, only nine of these severances are named "full understanding," because the name requires the joint presence of a specific set of conditions, not the mere fact of severance itself. The six patience-fruits require three conditions together: an undefiled attainment of separation; freedom from attachment to the peak of existence; and the extinguishing of the "double cause" — evidently the two universally pervading causal afflictions, one abandoned by seeing suffering and one by seeing the origin of suffering, within a given realm. All three must be present together; if even one is missing, the severance attained is not called full understanding. An ordinary worldling, for instance, may extinguish the double cause, yet lacks an undefiled attainment of separation and has not yet freed themselves from the peak of existence, so their severance is not full understanding. A noble one who has entered the seeing of the truths already has an undefiled attainment of separation, but from that point up through the analogy-patience regarding suffering, attachment to the peak of existence is not yet absent; by the stage of the analogy-knowledge regarding suffering and the dharma-patience regarding the origin, that attachment is finally absent, but the double cause is still not extinguished, since the universally pervading cause abandoned by seeing the origin remains. Only at the subsequent stages of dharma-knowledge and analogy-knowledge are all three conditions finally complete, and so full understanding is established there, stage by stage.

The three knowledge-fruits require a fourth condition in addition to the same three: transcending a realm entirely, meaning that every afflicted dharma within that realm has been left behind without exception. But since this "freedom from the joint bond" functions no differently, in practice, from extinguishing the double cause and transcending a realm, the treatise does not treat it as a separate condition here, even though its sense is somewhat distinct. And although every case of transcending a realm also involves extinguishing the double cause, the reverse does not hold — extinguishing the double cause does not always amount to transcending an entire realm — which is why transcending a realm must be listed as its own separate condition, alongside extinguishing the double cause, rather than being folded into it.

19. Who Gives Up, and Who Gains, How Many Full Understandings — Fascicle 21 Complete

The treatise closes its discussion of the anuśayas with one final question: at each juncture along the path, who gives up how many full understandings, and who newly gains how many? Giving up one occurs when a practitioner falls back — from the stage of no-more-learning, or from the point where craving for form has been exhausted — all the way into a relapse of desire-realm defilement, losing the single full understanding held at that point. Giving up two occurs for a non-returner who, having exhausted craving for form and so holding two full understandings, either falls back into the bonds of desire or instead advances onward to arhatship, since in both cases those two are lost — in the second case because they are absorbed into the single, more comprehensive full understanding of arhatship itself. Giving up five occurs for one who had already become free of desire beforehand and then enters the seeing of the truths: upon reaching the analogy-knowledge regarding the path, since the exhaustion of the lower-portion bonds is newly attained there, the five seeing-abandonment full understandings held until then are given up, absorbed into this one. Giving up six occurs for a noble one who had not yet become free of desire, at the moment freedom from desire is finally attained, since the six seeing-abandonment full understandings are then superseded. With this account of gain and loss, the treatise's discussion of the anuśayas — undertaken across this fascicle and the two before it, covering the root and secondary afflictions, the bonds and entanglements, the five hindrances, and the entire technical apparatus of severance and full understanding — comes to its conclusion. This closes Fascicle 21 of the Abhidharmakośa.

Giving up five means one who had already become free of desire beforehand, upon entering the seeing of the truths and reaching the analogy-knowledge regarding the path, since one then attains the exhaustion of the lower-portion bonds and thereby gives up the previous five. Giving up six means a noble one who had not yet become free of desire, at the moment freedom from desire is attained. Gaining likewise means that there is gaining of one, gaining of two, and gaining of six, only gaining of five does not occur. Gaining one means attaining what was not attained before, and also falling back, from the stage of no-more-learning, into the bonds of form. Gaining two means falling back, from the stage of no-more-learning, into the bonds of the formless realm. Gaining six means falling back from the state of non-returning. With this, the discussion of the anuśayas, undertaken in the course of explaining severance, comes to its conclusion.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the asymmetry the treatise itself names outright: one can lose one, two, five, or six full understandings at a time, and gain one, two, or six — but never gain five as a distinct event. Five is only ever something given up (when the six seeing-abandonment full understandings collapse into the single lower-portion-bonds understanding), never something freshly attained in its own right, since nothing in this scheme transitions into holding exactly five at once from a lower count. We have preserved this stated asymmetry exactly as the text presents it, rather than smoothing the "gain" and "loss" lists into a symmetrical pair they do not actually form.

We should also note that "giving up" a full understanding here does not always mean falling back or losing ground spiritually — in the case of giving up two or giving up five upon reaching arhatship or the exhaustion of the lower-portion bonds respectively, the practitioner is advancing, and the "loss" is really an absorption of narrower full understandings into a more comprehensive one attained at the same moment. Only some instances of "giving up" in this list (falling back into desire, falling back from non-returning) represent an actual regression. We have translated the term "give up" (捨) uniformly, as the source does, trusting the surrounding explanation in each case to make clear which sense is meant.
