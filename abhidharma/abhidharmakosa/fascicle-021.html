The Nine Bonds

Fascicle 21 continues the third part of Chapter 5 (分別隨眠品第五之三, the Anuśaya-nirdeśa), taking up yet another set of collective names the sūtras use for the anuśayas and their entanglements: "bonds" (saṃyojana), "bondages" (bandhana), "secondary afflictions" (upakleśa), and "entanglements" (paryavasthāna) proper. This fascicle opens with the nine kinds of bond, working out why the three views are grouped into a single "view-bond" while the two kinds of clinging form a separate "clinging-bond," and why jealousy and stinginess alone, among all the entanglements, are elevated to the status of bonds in their own right.

1. The Nine Bonds: Why Views and Clinging Each Split into Two

Having discussed the anuśayas and their entanglements under the names outflow, flood, yoke, and clinging, the treatise now asks whether these exhaust the sūtras' vocabulary for these same phenomena, or whether further collective names exist. Indeed there are others: the very same afflictions, considered from the angle of their distinctions as bond, bondage, anuśaya, secondary affliction, and entanglement, are spoken of under five further collective names. Taking up "bond" first: there are nine kinds — the bond of craving, the bond of hatred, the bond of conceit, the bond of ignorance, the bond of views, the bond of clinging, the bond of doubt, the bond of jealousy, and the bond of stinginess. Among these, the bond of craving refers to greed bound to all three realms; the remaining bonds' specific character should be explained analogously, as appropriate. The bond of views refers collectively to the three kinds of view (self-view, extreme views, and wrong view); the bond of clinging refers collectively to the two kinds of clinging (view-attachment and precept-and-vow attachment). On this basis, a genuine question arises: is it possible for something associated with view to be bound by the craving-bond without being bound by the view-bond, even though the view-anuśaya still undergoes latent increase there? Indeed it is possible — specifically, at the stage where the cognition of the origin has already arisen but the cognition of cessation has not yet arisen, the dharmas associated with the two kinds of clinging that are abandoned by seeing cessation and the path are bound, with respect to their object, by the craving-bond rather than the view-bond, since the pervasive view-bond has already been permanently eliminated by that point; yet these same dharmas still undergo latent increase from the view-anuśaya belonging to the two kinds of clinging, since that anuśaya continues to take them as its object. Why are the three views specifically grouped together and established as a single "view-bond," while the two kinds of clinging are separately established as their own distinct "clinging-bond"? Because the three views and the two kinds of clinging are, respectively, equal in number of underlying items — the three views comprise eighteen items, and so do the two kinds of clinging, hence they are called "equal in item-count." Moreover, the three views constitute what is grasped, while the two kinds of clinging constitute the grasping itself, hence they are also called "equal in what is taken and what takes" — and it is precisely because what is grasped and what does the grasping are functionally distinct that they are established as two separate bonds rather than one. Why, among all the various entanglements, are only jealousy and stinginess specifically elevated to the status of bonds, while the rest are not? Because these two alone are both exclusively unwholesome and capable of arising with a certain independent, unrestrained potency of their own — qualities the other entanglements lack. If entanglements numbered only eight, this explanation would suffice on its own; but since this tradition actually recognizes ten entanglements, this explanation alone is not sufficient, since malice and concealment, among the other eight, also share both of these same two qualities. For this reason, it must further be said that jealousy and stinginess are singled out because their fault is especially severe, arising far more frequently than the others; because these two specifically serve as the cause of low social status and poverty; because they pervasively manifest as accompaniments to virtually every other secondary affliction; and because they specifically disturb and trouble both the monastic and lay communities, or both gods and asuras, or both of the two superior destinies of humans and gods, or both other beings and one's own community, depending on which framing one prefers.

Verse: through the distinctions of bond and the rest, five further kinds are spoken of. Explanation: these same afflictions, through the distinctions of bond, bondage, anuśaya, secondary affliction, and entanglement, are spoken of under five further kinds. What, then, is "bond"? Verse: bond has nine items; equal in what is taken, view and clinging are established as two bonds, since these two alone are unwholesome and arise with independent potency; among the entanglements, only jealousy and stinginess are established as bonds, either because these two occur frequently, because they cause low status and poverty, because they pervasively manifest secondary afflictions, or because they trouble both communities. Explanation: bond has nine kinds — first, the bond of craving; second, the bond of hatred; third, the bond of conceit; fourth, the bond of ignorance; fifth, the bond of views; sixth, the bond of clinging; seventh, the bond of doubt; eighth, the bond of jealousy; ninth, the bond of stinginess. Among these, the bond of craving refers to greed of the three realms; the rest should be explained analogously, as appropriate. The bond of views refers to the three views; the bond of clinging refers to the two kinds of clinging. On this basis, it is said: is there something associated with view that is bound by the craving-bond, not the view-bond, yet does not lack latent increase by the view-anuśaya? Yes. How is this? At the stage where the cognition of the origin has arisen but the cognition of cessation has not yet arisen, dharmas associated with the two kinds of clinging abandoned by seeing cessation and the path are bound, with respect to their object, by the craving-bond, not the view-bond, since the pervasive view-bond has already been permanently abandoned — [these dharmas are] not bound by the non-pervasive view-bond either, since neither the object nor the association [for that] exists. Yet they still have latent increase by the view-anuśaya, since the view-anuśaya of the two clingings undergoes latent increase there. Why are the three views separately established as the view-bond, and the two clingings separately established as the clinging-bond? Because the three views and the two clingings are equal in item-count — that is, the three views have eighteen items, and the two clingings likewise, hence called "equal in items." The three are what is taken, the two are what takes, hence called "equal in taking" — since what is taken and what takes are distinct, they are established as two bonds. Why, among the entanglements, are only jealousy and stinginess established as bonds, and not the others? Because these two alone are unwholesome and arise with independent potency. Only these two fully possess both qualities; the rest do not, hence only these two are established. If entanglements were only eight, this explanation would hold; but since ten entanglements are accepted, this explanation does not suffice, since malice and concealment likewise possess both qualities. Hence, if ten entanglements are accepted, it should further be said that jealousy and stinginess are especially grave in fault, since these two occur frequently. Moreover, these two are the cause of low status and poverty; they pervasively manifest [as accompaniments to] secondary afflictions of both sorrow and joy; they trouble both the monastic and lay communities; or they trouble both gods and asuras; or they trouble both superior destinies of humans and gods; or they trouble both others and one's own community.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section introduces "bond" (saṃyojana, 結) as yet another collective term for the anuśayas, adding to the "outflow/flood/yoke/clinging" scheme already covered in fascicle 20 — we note for readers tracking this project's growing vocabulary that this tradition uses at least five distinct collective terms (bond, bondage, anuśaya, secondary affliction, entanglement) for closely related but not identical groupings of the same underlying afflictions, each carving up the territory slightly differently depending on which specific quality (severity, frequency, sharpness, and so on) is being emphasized.

We should flag that the fine distinction drawn here — something can be "bound" by the craving-bond specifically, rather than the view-bond, even while still undergoing latent increase from a view-anuśaya — depends on tracking the earlier "pervasive vs. non-pervasive" anuśaya framework from fascicle 19 and the "binding via object vs. via association" distinction from fascicle 20; we have tried to make the logic self-contained here, but readers wanting the fullest picture may find it useful to recall those earlier discussions.

2. The Five Lower-Portion Bonds, and the Five Upper-Portion Bonds

The treatise now takes up a further sūtra teaching that speaks of a distinct set of five bonds "conducive to the lower portion": self-view, precept-and-vow attachment, doubt, desire-realm greed, and hatred. Why are precisely these five given this specific name? Because all five specifically favor and reinforce the "lower portion" — that is, only the desire realm is properly called the "lower portion," and each of these five afflictions specifically serves to strengthen and perpetuate a being's continued attachment to it. The latter two of the five (desire-greed and hatred) directly prevent a being from ever transcending the desire realm at all; even should a being manage to transcend it through the other three (self-view, precept-and-vow attachment, and doubt), those same three would then act like a prison guard or a border patrol, pulling the being back down into the desire realm once again. Other teachers offer a related but distinct explanation: "lower" here refers to beings of the lower type (ordinary, unenlightened beings) as well as to the lower realm (the desire realm) — the first three afflictions specifically obstruct such lower beings from transcending their condition, while the latter two specifically prevent them from transcending the lower realm itself, and so all five together earn the name "conducive to the lower portion." A further question arises: since a stream-enterer is actually said to have severed six distinct afflictions in total upon attaining that fruit, why does the sūtra teaching speak only of severing three bonds? Properly speaking, it should indeed be said that six afflictions are severed, but the treatise groups these six under three broader categories for the sake of concision: among what is severed, there are three underlying types, one of which pervasively extends across two categories of abandonment, and another which extends across all four, and it is by naming these three categories that the fuller sixfold severance is being economically summarized. Moreover, within what is severed, three of the anuśayas operate by riding upon three others in a specific derivative relationship — extreme views ride upon self-view, view-attachment rides upon precept-and-vow attachment, and wrong view rides upon doubt — and so, by naming the three underlying anuśayas that these three derivative ones ride upon, all six are, in effect, already accounted for by naming just three. Other teachers explain this differently: anyone setting out toward a different destination faces three general kinds of obstacle — unwillingness to set out at all, being led astray onto a wrong path, and uncertainty about which path is correct. A person seeking liberation faces exactly these same three kinds of obstacle: self-view, out of fear of what liberation might mean for one's sense of self, produces unwillingness to set out toward it at all; precept-and-vow attachment, through clinging to a mistaken path, leads one astray from the true path; and doubt produces deep uncertainty about the true path itself. Since the Buddha wishes to reveal that a stream-enterer has permanently severed these three specific obstacles standing in the way of liberation, the teaching speaks of "three" bonds severed, even though six afflictions are technically eliminated. The sūtras elsewhere similarly speak of five further bonds "conducive to the upper portion," corresponding in a parallel structure to the five just discussed: form-realm greed, formless-realm greed, restlessness, conceit, and ignorance — these five, so long as they remain unabandoned, prevent a being from ever transcending the upper realms (the form and formless realms), and it is because they favor and reinforce continued attachment to those upper realms that they earn this corresponding name.

The Buddha, elsewhere, on the basis of a different classificatory approach, speaks of five kinds using the term "bond" as well. Verse: there are also five conducive to the lower portion — through two, one does not transcend desire; through three, one returns below — [the teaching] encompasses [these] by category and by root; or, [these correspond to] unwillingness to set out, being led astray on the path, and doubt regarding the path, which obstruct the attainment of liberation, hence only three are said to be severed. Explanation: what are the five? Namely, the view of a self, precept-and-vow attachment, doubt, desire-greed, and hatred. Why are these five called conducive to the lower portion? Because these five favor and reinforce the lower portion's realm — that is, only the desire realm receives the name "lower portion," and these five are able to favor and reinforce [attachment to] it. Through the latter two, one is unable to transcend the desire realm; even should one be able to transcend it, through the former three one is drawn back below, like a prison guard or patrol officer. Other teachers say: "lower" refers to lower sentient beings, namely ordinary beings, and to the lower realm, namely the desire realm. The former three obstruct lower sentient beings from transcending [their condition]; the latter two cause one not to transcend the lower realm — hence all five obtain the name conducive to the lower portion. Those who attain stream-entry have severed six afflictions — why, then, does the teaching speak only of severing three bonds? In truth, it should be said that six afflictions are severed, but, encompassing them by category and by root, only three are spoken of. That is, among what is severed, there are three types — one extends through two categories, [others] through four categories — and speaking of three types encompasses these three categories. Moreover, among what is severed, three ride upon three others in turn — that is, extreme views ride upon self-view, view-attachment rides upon precept-and-vow attachment, wrong view rides upon doubt. Speaking of three as severed encompasses these three roots — hence speaking of three severed already expresses six severed. Some explain it this way: one setting out toward a different place faces three obstacles — first, unwillingness to set out; second, being led astray onto a wrong path, relying on a mistaken road; third, doubt regarding the correct path. One seeking liberation likewise has three similar obstacles — that is, through self-view, fearing liberation, one is unwilling to set out; through precept-and-vow attachment, relying on and clinging to a wrong path, one loses the correct route; through doubt regarding the path, one harbors deep uncertainty. The Buddha reveals that a stream-enterer has permanently severed these obstacles to attaining liberation, hence speaks of three as severed. The Buddha, in another sūtra, just as with "conducive to the lower portion," speaks of "conducive to the upper portion" as likewise having five kinds. Verse: conducive to the upper portion also has five — the two cravings of form and formless, restlessness, conceit, and ignorance — since these prevent one from transcending the upper [realms]. Explanation: these five, when not yet abandoned, are able to prevent sentient beings from transcending the upper realms. Since they favor and reinforce [attachment to] the upper realms, they are named bonds conducive to the upper portion.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should highlight the "obstacle to a journey" analogy here as a particularly clear piece of moral psychology: unwillingness to set out (fear of what liberation implies for one's sense of self), being led astray (clinging to an ineffective substitute practice), and doubt (uncertainty about the correct path itself) are presented as the three fundamental ways any spiritual seeker can fail to make progress, with self-view, precept-and-vow attachment, and doubt named as their respective Buddhist-technical causes. This offers a genuinely illuminating rationale for why a stream-enterer's decisive achievement is described in terms of overcoming exactly these three, even though the fuller technical account involves six distinct anuśayas.

We should also flag that this section preserves multiple explanations, presented as alternatives rather than adjudicated to a single winner, for both "why five bonds are called lower-portion" and "why only three are said to be severed despite six being technically eliminated" — consistent with our practice throughout this project, we have kept each explanation distinct rather than blending them into a single composite account.

3. The Three Bondages, and Secondary Affliction

Having finished discussing "bond," the treatise turns to "bondage." Bondage comes in three kinds: the bondage of craving (referring to craving of all three realms), the bondage of hatred (referring to hatred in every form), and the bondage of delusion (referring to delusion in every form). Why are precisely these three, and no others, established under the specific name "bondage"? Because bondage is spoken of specifically in correspondence with the three kinds of feeling — pleasant, painful, and neutral. With respect to pleasant feeling, it is specifically the bondage of craving that undergoes latent increase, both through this feeling serving as its object and through direct association with it; the same pattern holds, correspondingly, for hatred with respect to painful feeling, and for delusion with respect to neutral feeling. Although craving and hatred can, in certain circumstances, also arise with respect to neutral feeling, this correspondence is established with reference to the predominant, most characteristic pattern found within a single being's own mental continuum, not as an absolute, exceptionless rule covering every possible case. Having now discussed "bondage," the treatise turns to "anuśaya" itself, which has already been explained at length earlier — coming in six, seven, ten, or ninety-eight varieties, depending on which classificatory scheme is being used, exactly as previously described. Having discussed "anuśaya," the treatise turns finally to "secondary affliction" (upakleśa). These same root afflictions are also called secondary afflictions, since they all trouble and disturb the mind in a manner analogous to a root affliction's own basic function. But there also exist further mental factors, distinct from these root afflictions, that likewise belong to the aggregate of mental factors and likewise arise specifically in connection with a defiled mind — these, too, are called secondary afflictions, precisely because they arise following upon and in dependence on the root afflictions themselves, even though they are not, strictly speaking, root afflictions in their own right, since they do not constitute the most fundamental, independently arising kind of defilement. Their fuller characteristics will be explained in greater detail in the later chapter on miscellaneous topics; only a brief, preliminary account is given here for now.

Explanation: bondage has three kinds — first, the bondage of craving, meaning craving in general; second, the bondage of hatred, meaning hatred in general; third, the bondage of delusion, meaning delusion in general. Explanation: these afflictions are also called secondary afflictions, since they all trouble and disturb the mind. Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that "secondary affliction" here explicitly defers its fuller treatment to a later chapter of the treatise ("miscellaneous topics," likely corresponding to a dedicated later chapter enumerating the specific secondary afflictions — jealousy, stinginess, malice, concealment, and others already named in passing in fascicle 20's discussion of entanglements) — we have not attempted to anticipate that fuller list here, consistent with our practice of translating fascicle by fascicle and letting the treatise's own forward references stand as genuine deferrals.

The pairing of craving/hatred/delusion with pleasant/painful/neutral feeling respectively is a classic formulation appearing across much Buddhist psychological literature (often associated with the twelve links of dependent origination, where feeling is said to condition craving) — we note this correspondence as a recurring structural motif worth tracking, while preserving the treatise's own careful qualification that this reflects only the predominant pattern, not an exceptionless rule, since craving and hatred can, in specific cases, also arise with respect to neutral feeling.

4. The Eight (or Ten) Entanglements, Traced to Their Roots

Having discussed secondary affliction generally, the treatise now turns to "entanglement" (paryavasthāna) specifically, since the root afflictions are themselves also called entanglements — the sūtras, for instance, speak of the "entanglement of desire-greed" as a supporting condition. The Prakaraṇapāda recognizes eight entanglements, while the Vaibhāṣika tradition recognizes ten, adding malice and concealment to the original eight. Lack of shame and lack of conscience have already been explained in earlier fascicles. Jealousy is the specific state of mind that cannot bear the flourishing success of others. Stinginess is an attachment to one's wealth or knowledge so possessive that it directly opposes any skillful, generous giving of either. Remorse is identical to the previously discussed factor of "regret" already explained in an earlier fascicle. Drowsiness is characterized by causing the mind to become dull and contracted, lacking the mental energy needed to properly sustain the body's activity; both remorse and drowsiness, as entanglements, refer specifically to their defiled forms. Restlessness and torpor have likewise already been explained previously. Malice is the specific hostile agitation of mind, excluding outright hatred and harmful intent, directed toward either sentient or insentient objects. Concealment is the specific act of hiding one's own faults from others. Among these ten entanglements, lack of shame, stinginess, and restlessness are classified as outflows of greed; lack of conscience, drowsiness, and torpor are classified as outflows of ignorance; jealousy and malice are classified as outflows of hatred; and remorse is classified as an outflow of doubt. As for concealment, some teachers classify it as an outflow of greed, others as an outflow of ignorance, and still others hold that it arises from both roots together, corresponding respectively to cases where one conceals a fault out of a desire for personal gain or advantage, and cases where one conceals a fault simply out of not properly understanding its true gravity.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that "remorse is classified as an outflow of doubt" appears at first to be a slightly unusual pairing, since remorse (regret over a past action) does not obviously connect to doubt in the way jealousy connects to hatred or stinginess connects to greed — the underlying logic, as best we can reconstruct from the broader pattern in this text, seems to be that persistent, unresolved regret involves a kind of oscillating uncertainty about whether one's past action was right or wrong, functionally resembling doubt's own back-and-forth character; the source text itself, however, states this pairing without further explaining the connection, so we present it as given rather than supplying our own speculative justification as though it were part of the original.

We should also note the unresolved three-way disagreement over concealment's root (greed alone, ignorance alone, or both together depending on the specific case) as another instance of this treatise's honest preservation of genuine scholastic disagreement rather than a false, single settled answer — we have kept all three positions distinguished rather than picking a winner.

5. The Six Affliction-Stains, and Which Are Abandoned by Seeing

The treatise now turns to a further set of six "affliction-stains," distinct from the ten entanglements already discussed. Vexation is a stubborn, entrenched clinging to matters one is already at fault in, such that one refuses to accept correction or admonition even when it accords with reason. Harm is the specific capacity to oppress and press upon others, giving rise to acts such as striking or reviling them. Resentment is the repeated, deliberate dwelling upon an object of malice, forming a grudge one refuses to release. Flattery is a crookedness of mind that prevents one from genuinely and accurately revealing oneself as one truly is — either falsely denying what is actually so, or employing indirect means to obscure a matter and keep others from understanding it clearly. Deceit is the deliberate act of misleading others. Arrogance has already been explained in an earlier fascicle. These six arise from the root afflictions and, being coarse and defiling in character, are called "affliction-stains." Among these six, deceit and arrogance are classified as outflows of greed; harm and resentment are classified as outflows of hatred; vexation is classified as an outflow of view-attachment; and flattery is classified as an outflow of views generally. As the saying goes, "what is it that is 'crooked'?" — it refers to wrong views of every kind, and it is for this reason that flattery is definitively classified as an outflow of views. These stains, together with the entanglements already discussed, all arise from the root afflictions, and it is on this basis that all of them are given the collective name "secondary affliction." A further question then arises: by which path — seeing or cultivation — are these stains and entanglements actually abandoned? Among the ten entanglements, lack of shame, lack of conscience, drowsiness, torpor, and restlessness are abandoned by both seeing and cultivation, since these five are capable of arising in association with afflictions belonging to either of these two categories of abandonment — whichever category the affliction they accompany belongs to, that same category applies to them as well. The remaining entanglements — jealousy, stinginess, remorse, malice, and concealment — together with all six affliction-stains, arise with a kind of independent potency of their own, and are therefore abandoned only by cultivation, since they are associated exclusively with afflictions that are themselves abandoned by cultivation through the specific power of ignorance operating independently, this being precisely what "arising with independent potency" is meant to indicate here.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should note that "vexation" being traced to view-attachment specifically (rather than to wrong view generally, the way flattery is) reflects a fairly fine-grained distinction: vexation's defining feature is stubborn refusal to accept correction, which the treatise apparently sees as connected specifically to the prideful conviction that one's own (mistaken) position is supreme — the hallmark of view-attachment — rather than to the more general category of holding a wrong view as such. We have preserved this specific pairing exactly as the text presents it.

The "seeing vs. cultivation" classification here usefully closes a loop several fascicles in the making: it applies the same seeing/cultivation-abandonment framework developed at length in fascicle 19 to this fascicle's newly introduced entanglements and affliction-stains, confirming that these secondary afflictions are integrated into, rather than exceptions to, the treatise's overall systematic apparatus for classifying every kind of affliction by how and when it is eliminated on the path.

6. Moral Quality Across Realms, and the God Who Flattered a Monk

The treatise now asks which moral quality these secondary afflictions possess across the three realms. Desire-realm drowsiness, torpor, and restlessness are each capable of being either unwholesome or morally neutral, depending on the specific context in which they arise; all the remaining desire-realm secondary afflictions are exclusively unwholesome. Whatever secondary afflictions exist in the form and formless realms, by contrast, are, in every case, exclusively of a neutral moral quality, since — as already explained in earlier fascicles — no affliction belonging to those higher, more refined realms can bring about a genuinely painful ripened result of the kind that would qualify it as unwholesome. The treatise then asks which of these secondary afflictions are confined to specific realms. Flattery and deceit are confined exclusively to the desire realm and the first meditative absorption of the form realm. How is it known that flattery and deceit can occur even among the exalted beings of the Brahma-heaven? Because the great god Mahābrahmā himself, wishing to conceal his own true state of mind, once presented a deceptive, misleading appearance to the monk Aśvajit — an episode already mentioned in an earlier fascicle, but revisited here in fuller detail since it bears directly on the present point. Torpor, restlessness, and conceit are each capable of occurring throughout all three realms without restriction. All the remaining secondary afflictions, apart from these specific exceptions, are confined exclusively to the desire realm — that is, among the sixteen secondary afflictions discussed, five follow the pattern just explained (flattery and deceit confined to the desire realm and first absorption; torpor, restlessness, and conceit spanning all three realms), while the remaining eleven are confined exclusively to the desire realm.

We should flag the Mahābrahmā/Aśvajit episode as a notable traditional anecdote this treatise treats as settled scriptural precedent (used here as proof that even an exalted Brahma-heaven deity is capable of flattery and deceit) — we present it exactly as the source cites it, as this tradition's own accepted account, without independently verifying the episode's origin or details beyond what this passage itself states.

We should also note the honest asymmetry in how these afflictions distribute across realms: only three secondary afflictions (torpor, restlessness, conceit) are considered subtle or universal enough to arise anywhere in the three realms, while flattery and deceit reach only as far as the first meditative absorption, and the remaining eleven are confined to the comparatively coarser desire realm entirely — a hierarchy that reflects this tradition's general assumption that higher meditative attainment progressively purifies a being of grosser forms of affliction, even while certain more subtle afflictions (like conceit) can persist much further along that path.

7. Which Afflictions Arise Only Through Mental Consciousness

The treatise now asks: among the anuśayas and secondary afflictions, how many arise only in connection with mental consciousness (the sixth, purely mental mode of awareness), and how many can also arise in connection with any of the five sense-consciousnesses? Speaking in broad summary, conceit and drowsiness abandoned specifically by seeing the truths, together with all the secondary afflictions that arise with a kind of independent potency of their own (jealousy, stinginess, remorse, malice, concealment, and the six affliction-stains), arise exclusively in connection with mental consciousness, since none of these are capable of arising in dependence on any of the five sense-consciousness bodies at all. All the remaining anuśayas and secondary afflictions, by contrast, are capable of arising in connection with any of the six consciousnesses — this refers specifically to cultivation-abandoned greed, hatred, and ignorance, together with the secondary afflictions directly associated with them (namely lack of shame, lack of conscience, torpor, and restlessness, together with the remaining factors belonging to the broader category of major-affliction mental factors), all of which are indeed capable of arising in dependence on any of the six consciousness-bodies.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that "the category of major-affliction mental factors" (大煩惱地法, a specific technical grouping within this tradition's broader taxonomy of mental factors, referring to factors that accompany every defiled state of mind) is referenced here as an established category the reader is assumed to already recognize — we have not attempted to enumerate its full membership here, since this passage itself only references the category in passing rather than defining it, and a fuller definition properly belongs to the treatise's earlier chapter on the mental factors themselves (likely Chapter 2, not yet covered in this project's current fascicle-by-fascicle progress through Chapters 4 and 5).

The underlying logic distinguishing "mental-consciousness-only" afflictions from "six-consciousness-capable" ones appears to track a distinction between afflictions requiring sustained, reflective mental engagement (conceit, and the more elaborate secondary afflictions like jealousy or concealment, which seem to require a level of interpretive or self-referential thought only mental consciousness can support) versus more basic afflictions (greed, hatred, ignorance in their cultivation-abandoned forms) that can arise even alongside the comparatively simple, direct sensory awareness the five sense-consciousnesses provide.

8. Which Afflictions Pair with Which Feelings

The treatise now works out, in careful detail, exactly which root afflictions are associated with which of the five feeling-faculties. Among the desire-realm afflictions, greed is associated with joy and pleasure, since it operates through a jubilant mode pervading all six consciousnesses. Hatred is associated with distress and pain, since it operates through a distressed mode likewise pervading all six consciousnesses. Ignorance is associated with all four of these (joy, pleasure, distress, and pain), since it can operate through either a jubilant or a distressed mode, again pervading all six consciousnesses. Wrong view is associated with distress or joy, since its jubilant or distressed mode operates only at the level of mental consciousness — the reason wrong view can take on either mode is that it may serve, respectively, as the basis for previously committing unwholesome deeds or wholesome deeds. Doubt is associated with distress, since its distressed mode operates only at the level of mental consciousness, this being because one caught in genuine uncertainty, seeking a definite resolution, naturally experiences a sorrowful, troubled state of mind. The remaining four views (self-view, extreme views, view-attachment, and precept-and-vow attachment) together with conceit are associated with joy, since their jubilant mode operates only at the level of mental consciousness. This account has been given with reference to each affliction's own specific, characteristic association; speaking instead in terms of a more general association applicable to all of them, every one of these afflictions can also be associated with the feeling of equanimity, since, at the specific point where a given anuśaya's continuum is actually being severed, its force has already grown weak and faint, and it necessarily then abides in a state of equanimity. Having explained this pattern for the desire realm, the treatise extends the same analysis to the higher realms: in every case, as appropriate, an affliction is associated with whichever feelings arise together with the specific consciousness of its own realm and stage — that is, in a stage possessing all four types of consciousness, each affliction arising in connection with a given consciousness is associated, pervasively, with whatever feelings arise together with that same consciousness; in a stage possessing only mental consciousness, the afflictions arising there are associated pervasively with whatever feeling accompanies mental consciousness specifically. Since the number of feelings available at each of these higher stages has already been explained in an earlier fascicle, this point is not repeated separately here.

We should note the interesting logical structure of this section: it presents the "specific" association for each affliction first, then generalizes by pointing out that every affliction can also, in a weaker, terminal sense, be associated with equanimity at the exact moment of its own severance — we have preserved this two-tier structure (specific pairing, then a universal equanimity exception) exactly as the text presents it, rather than collapsing it into a single flat list.

Wrong view's dual association with either distress or joy, depending on whether it underlies past unwholesome or wholesome deeds, is a subtle point worth flagging: it implies that the same categorically mistaken denial of karma (wrong view, as defined in fascicle 19) can manifest with different emotional coloring depending on the specific moral history of the person holding it — someone who has committed wrongdoing and denies karma to avoid guilt experiences this denial with distress, while someone who has done good and denies karma perhaps out of a more detached, philosophical skepticism experiences it with a kind of untroubled satisfaction. We have rendered this distinction as precisely as the compressed source allows.
