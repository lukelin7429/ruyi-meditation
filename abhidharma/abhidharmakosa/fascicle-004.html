What Must Arise Together?

This page carries the opening of fascicle 4, continuing Chapter 2 (根品第二之二, the second part of the Indriya-nirdeśa) with a new topic: which dharmas are so tightly bound that they can never arise apart from one another, from the minimal atomic cluster of matter up through mind's inseparability from its own mental factors.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 17 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 4 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. A New Chapter Question: Which Dharmas Must Arise Together?

Fascicle 4 opens the second part of Chapter 2 with a fresh question, framed against the treatise's basic five-category ontology of all dharmas.

Chinese Source

今應思擇，一切有為如相不同，生亦各異，為有諸法決定俱生、有定俱生。謂一切法略有五品：一色、二心、三心所、四心不相應行、五無為。無為無生，此中不說。今先辯色決定俱生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now one should consider: since all conditioned dharmas differ in their characteristics, their arising likewise differs in each case — are there dharmas that definitely arise together with certain others, in fixed groups? All dharmas, briefly, fall into five categories: form, mind, mental factors, formations dissociated from mind, and the unconditioned. The unconditioned does not arise at all, so it is not discussed here. Let us first discuss form's definite co-arising.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This opens a brand-new major topic within Chapter 2's second installment: which dharmas are bound together so tightly that they never occur apart from one another, always arising as an indivisible package. The five-category ontology named here — form, mind, mental factors, formations dissociated from mind, and the unconditioned — has been implicit throughout this treatise; the unconditioned is set aside immediately since, being entirely outside causation, it never "arises" in the first place, making the question of what it co-arises with simply moot.

2. The Minimal Atomic Cluster: Eight, Nine, or Ten Factors

The treatise establishes the absolute floor for any physical cluster in the desire realm — never a single isolated atom, but always a minimal bundle of at least eight distinct factors.

Chinese Source

頌曰：欲微聚無聲，無根有八事，有身根九事，十事有餘根。論曰：色聚極細立微聚名，為顯更無細於此者。此在欲界無聲無根，八事俱生隨一不減。云何八事？謂四大種及四所造色、香、味、觸。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: In the desire realm, an atomic cluster without sound and without a sense faculty has eight factors; with the body faculty, nine; with the other faculties, ten. Prose: "The finest division of a cluster of form" is given the name "atomic cluster," to indicate that nothing finer than this exists. In the desire realm, a cluster without sound and without a sense faculty has eight factors arising together, with not even one factor reducible. What are these eight? The four primary elements, together with four kinds of derived form: visible form, smell, taste, and the tangible quality.

無聲有根諸極微聚，此俱生事或九或十。有身根聚九事俱生，八事如前，身為第九。有餘根聚十事俱生，九事如前，加眼等一。眼耳鼻舌必不離身，展轉相望處各別故。

For atomic clusters without sound but with a sense faculty, the co-arising factors are nine or ten. A cluster with the body faculty has nine — the eight as before, with the body faculty as the ninth. A cluster with one of the other faculties has ten — the nine as before, plus that faculty. The eye, ear, nose, and tongue can never occur apart from the body faculty, because, though mutually distinct in location, they are all situated upon it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "atomic cluster" (微聚) is the absolute finest-grained unit of matter this system recognizes — never a single atom acting alone, echoing a point made far earlier in this translation that a solitary atom can never by itself produce perception, but always a minimal bundle. Even the barest possible cluster, lacking both sound and any sense faculty, still requires eight distinct factors together: the four primary elements (earth, water, fire, wind) plus four kinds of derived matter (visible form, smell, taste, and the tangible quality as an object). Adding a sense faculty adds factors in a strict, load-bearing order: the body faculty adds one, making nine; and since the eye, ear, nose, and tongue are never found except embedded within a body that also has the tactile faculty right there, adding any of those adds a full two beyond the base eight — the body faculty plus the specific sense itself — making ten.

3. Adding Sound, and a Puzzle About the Four Elements

Sound raises the counts by one across the board, before a genuinely interesting question opens: if the four elements never occur apart, why does ordinary experience only ever notice one at a time?

Chinese Source

於前諸聚若有聲生，如次數增九十十一，以有聲處不離根生，謂有執受大種因起。若四大種不相離生，於諸聚中堅濕煖動，云何隨一可得非餘？

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the aforementioned clusters, if sound also arises, the counts respectively increase to nine, ten, and eleven — since wherever sound arises in this context, it does not occur apart from where a sense faculty arises, because it arises specifically from primary elements that are "grasped," that is, part of a living body. If the four primary elements always arise inseparably together, then within these clusters — solidity, moisture, warmth, and motion — how can only one of these ever be detected, and not the others?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The puzzle is a foundational and genuinely interesting one: the four primary elements — earth (solidity), water (moisture), fire (warmth), wind (motion) — are held to never occur apart from one another. Every particle of matter, however solid-seeming, technically contains all four in some proportion. So why does a rock simply feel hard, without also feeling obviously wet, warm, and in motion? The question sets up the treatise's answer in the next section — one that will turn out to explain a very ordinary, everyday observation everyone has already made without knowing its Abhidharma explanation.

4. Resolving the Puzzle: Dominant Function and Latent Seeds

Two rival explanations are recorded side by side, along with a striking everyday observation offered as evidence, and a rare moment of the treatise admitting the limits of what can be known by inference alone.

Chinese Source

於彼聚中勢用增者明了可得，餘體非無。如覺針鋒與籌合觸，如甞鹽味與麨合味。云何於彼知亦有餘？由有攝熟長持業故。有說：遇緣堅等便有流等相故，如水聚中由極冷故有煖相起，雖不相離而冷用增，如受及聲用有勝劣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within that cluster, whichever element's function is intensified is what becomes clearly detectable — the essential nature of the others is not thereby absent. It is like feeling a needle's point together with a bundled tally-stick in contact with the skin, or tasting salt's flavor mixed together with roasted grain-flour's flavor. How do we know the others are still present too? Because the cluster still performs the functions of cohering, ripening, growing, and sustaining. Some say: under the right conditions, even a solid thing can develop a flowing quality — just as, in a cluster of water, extreme cold gives rise to the appearance of warmth; though never separate, the cold function is ordinarily what is intensified, just as feeling and sound each have their own degrees of dominance.

有餘師說：於此聚中餘有種子未有體相，故契經說：於木聚中有種種界。界謂種子。如何風中知有顯色？此義可信，不可比知。或所合香現可取故，香與顯色不相離故。

Other masters say: within this cluster, the remaining elements exist as seeds without yet having a manifest characteristic — hence a sutra says, "within a cluster of wood there are various elements," meaning by "element" here, a seed. How, then, do we know that visible color is present within wind itself? This should simply be trusted, since it cannot be established by direct inference — or: because the smell mixed together with it can indeed be detected, and smell is never separate from visible color.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The resolution is really about the gap between metaphysical presence and perceptual salience. Nothing is ever missing from the cluster — all four elements are always genuinely there — but only the dominant one registers to ordinary perception, the way a sharp needle-point stands out from a bundle of blunter sticks even though the whole bundle touches the skin, or a strong salt flavor masks a subtler grain flavor in the same mouthful. The "extreme cold feels like warmth" observation — anyone who has touched dry ice or a very cold piece of metal and felt something like a burn knows this directly — is offered as concrete evidence that the non-dominant qualities are still active, and can even flip which one becomes dominant under extreme conditions.

A rival explanation is more deflationary: rather than holding all four always fully manifest with only one salient, this view says the non-dominant three exist merely as latent seeds, not yet actualized — a genuinely different metaphysical picture, which the treatise records without adjudicating between the two. The closing point about wind's invisible color is a rare moment of epistemic humility: some doctrinal claims, the text admits, simply cannot be verified by ordinary inference and must be accepted on the teaching's own authority, or inferred indirectly through an associated quality — here, smell — that can actually be perceived.

5. The Form Realm's Simpler Clusters, and What We're Actually Counting

The same counting exercise closes for the form realm, before a methodological aside asks a deceptively simple question: what exactly is being counted when we say "eight factors"?

Chinese Source

前說色界香味並無，故彼無聲有六七八，有聲有七八九俱生。此可准知，故不別說。此中言事為依體說、為依處說？若爾何過？二俱有過。

Ru-Yi Translation

It was earlier stated that the form realm lacks both smell and taste. Therefore, clusters there without sound have six, seven, or eight factors co-arising; with sound, seven, eight, or nine. This can be inferred by the same reasoning as before, so it is not separately explained. In this context, does the word "factor" refer to distinct substances, or to distinct locations? Either way, is there a problem? Both readings have one.

若依體說，八九十等便為太少，由諸微聚必有形色，有多極微共積集故。重性輕性定隨有一，滑性澁性隨一亦然。或處有冷有飢有渴，是則所言有太少過。若依處說，八九十等便為太多，由四大種觸處攝故應說四等，是則所言有太多失。二俱無過。應知此中所言事者，一分依體說，謂所依大種；一分依處說，謂能依造色。

If explained by substance, then eight, nine, ten, and so on would be too few, since every atomic cluster also necessarily has shape, many atoms being accumulated together; heaviness or lightness definitely accompanies one or the other as well, and so does smoothness or roughness; a given case might also have coldness, hunger, or thirst — so the stated counts would be too few. If explained by location, then eight, nine, ten would be too many, since the four primary elements are all subsumed under the single tangible sense-field, so it should simply be said "four" and so on — so the stated counts would be too many. Neither reading actually has a problem: "factor," as used here, is partly based on substance — the supporting primary elements — and partly based on location — the supported derived matter.

若爾，大種事應成多，造色各別，依一四大種故。應知此中依體類說，諸四大種類無別故。何用分別如是語為？語隨欲生，義應思擇。如是已辯色定俱生。

If that is so, should not the primary-element factors then become numerous, since derived matter is each individually distinct while depending on a single set of four primary elements? One should understand that the primary elements are spoken of by type of substance here, since the four primary elements, as a type, admit no further distinction within that type. What use is there in belaboring such wording? Language follows what one wishes to express; it is the meaning that should be carefully considered. Thus form's definite co-arising has now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The form realm's simpler counts follow directly from a rule established much earlier in this translation — the form realm lacks smell and taste entirely — so its clusters are simply the desire realm's counts minus two. The more interesting payoff is the closing methodological aside: pressed on whether "factor" means substance or location, the treatise refuses to be pinned to either pure reading, since each one alone generates an absurd over- or under-count. Instead it adopts a practical hybrid: the four primary elements are counted by type — there are always exactly four types, however many atoms exist — while derived matter is counted by individual kind, since each type of derived quality is its own distinct thing. The closing remark, that the meaning matters more than the wording, is a small but telling moment of Abhidharma pragmatism, refusing to let a terminological puzzle grow bigger than the substantive point it was meant to serve.

6. Mind and Mental Factors Always Co-Arise, and So Do the Four Marks of Conditioning

Two further, even more universal co-arising rules follow: mind never occurs without its mental factors, and every conditioned dharma whatsoever arises together with the four marks that define conditioned existence itself.

Chinese Source

餘定俱生今次當辯。頌曰：心心所必俱，諸行相或得。論曰：心與心所必定俱生，隨闕一時餘則不起。諸行即是一切有為，謂色、心、心所、心不相應行。前必俱言流至於此，謂色心等諸行生時，必與有為四相俱起。言或得者，謂諸行內唯有情法與得俱生；餘法不然，是故言或。

Ru-Yi Translation

The remaining categories' definite co-arising should be explained next. A verse states: mind and mental factors necessarily co-arise; all formations co-arise with their characteristics; or, also, with possession. Prose: Mind and mental factors necessarily co-arise — whenever even one is lacking, the others do not arise either. "Formations" means all conditioned dharmas: form, mind, mental factors, and formations dissociated from mind. The earlier word "necessarily co-arise" carries forward to apply here too: when form, mind, and the other formations arise, they necessarily arise together with the four characteristics of the conditioned. As for saying "or": this means that, among all formations, only dharmas belonging to sentient beings co-arise together with "possession"; other dharmas do not, hence the word "or."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Mind and its mental factors are as inseparable as any of the form-clusters just discussed — a moment of consciousness never occurs as bare awareness alone, always accompanied by at least some minimal set of mental factors, and vice versa. Even more universal: literally every conditioned dharma whatsoever — form, mind, mental factors, and formations dissociated from mind alike — necessarily arises together with the four defining marks of conditioned existence itself (birth, continuance, decay, and cessation), since to be conditioned at all just is to be marked by this fourfold pattern.

The qualifier "or" narrows one further rule to a specific subset: "possession" (得, a technical formation representing a being's ownership of a dharma) accompanies a dharma's arising only when that dharma belongs to a sentient being's own continuum — inanimate matter, for instance, arises with the four marks like everything else, but has no "owner," and so never co-arises with possession.

7. The Five Categories of Mental Factors

Having established that mind and its mental factors always co-arise, the treatise now asks the natural follow-up: what exactly are these "mental factors"?

Chinese Source

向言心所，何者是耶？頌曰：心所且有五，大地法等異。論曰：諸心所法且有五品。何等為五？一、大地法；二、大善地法；三、大煩惱地法；四、大不善地法；五、小煩惱地法。地謂行處，若此是彼所行處，即說此為彼法地。大法地故名為大地，此中若法大地所有名大地法，謂法恒於一切心有。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the mental factors just mentioned, what are they? A verse states: mental factors, to begin with, are of five categories — the universal-ground dharmas and so on, each different. Prose: Mental factors, to begin with, fall into five categories: universal-ground dharmas, universal-wholesome-ground dharmas, universal-affliction-ground dharmas, universal-unwholesome-ground dharmas, and minor-affliction-ground dharmas. "Ground" means the domain in which something operates — if this dharma is the domain in which that dharma operates, this is called the ground for that dharma. Because it is the ground for universal dharmas, it is called "the universal ground"; within this, whatever dharma belongs to the universal ground is called a "universal-ground dharma" — a dharma present in absolutely every moment of mind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the entry point into one of the most foundational taxonomies in classical Buddhist psychology: every mental factor that can ever accompany a moment of mind is sorted into exactly five categories, by how widely it applies. Some accompany every mental moment whatsoever, wholesome or not; some accompany only wholesome moments; some only afflicted ones; some only specifically unwholesome ones; and some — the "minor" afflictions — only some afflicted moments, not all. The next several sections work through each category's specific membership list, beginning with the ten factors that pervade every mind without exception.

8. The Ten Universal Mental Factors, Defined

The famous list of ten factors present in absolutely every moment of mind, each given a clean, working definition.

Chinese Source

彼法是何？頌曰：受想思觸欲，慧念與作意，勝解三摩地，遍於一切心。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are these dharmas? A verse states: feeling, perception, volition, contact, desire, wisdom, mindfulness, and attention; determination and concentration — these pervade all mind.

論曰：傳說：如是所列十法，諸心剎那和合遍有。此中受謂三種領納：苦、樂、俱非，有差別故。想謂於境取差別相。思謂能令心有造作。觸謂根境識和合生，能有觸對。欲謂希求所作事業。慧謂於法能有簡擇。念謂於緣明記不忘。作意謂能令心警覺。勝解謂能於境印可。三摩地謂心一境性。

Prose: Tradition holds that these ten dharmas just listed are universally present, combined together, in every momentary instance of mind. Among these: "feeling" means threefold experiencing — pain, pleasure, or neither. "Perception" means grasping the distinguishing characteristics of an object. "Volition" means that which causes the mind to act. "Contact" means the coming-together of faculty, object, and consciousness, enabling actual contact. "Desire" means aspiring toward some undertaking to be done. "Wisdom" means the capacity to discern dharmas. "Mindfulness" means clearly remembering an object without forgetting it. "Attention" means that which alerts the mind. "Determination" means the capacity to affirm an object. "Concentration" means the mind's one-pointedness upon its object.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the famous list of ten "universal-ground" mental factors (mahābhūmika-dharmas) — feeling, perception, volition, contact, desire, wisdom, mindfulness, attention, determination, and concentration — held to accompany literally every act of mind that ever occurs, wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral alike. The definitions are deliberately spare and functional rather than descriptive: each names what the factor DOES rather than what it feels like, since these are meant as precise technical categories for analysis, not evocative descriptions. Notice that "wisdom" appears here at the most basic, universal level — even a confused or deluded thought involves some minimal act of discernment, however poor, which is why this baseline capacity is listed as universal rather than reserved only for wholesome states.

9. The Ten Universal Wholesome Factors

The second great list follows immediately: ten factors present in every wholesome moment of mind, and only wholesome ones — but not before a humbling aside on how difficult this whole introspective project really is.

Chinese Source

諸心心所異相微細，一一相續分別尚難，況一剎那俱時而有。有色諸藥色根所取，其味差別尚難了知，況無色法唯覺慧取。如是已說十大地法。

Ru-Yi Translation

The distinguishing characteristics of mind and mental factors are extremely subtle; even distinguishing them one by one across a continuous series is difficult, let alone all arising together within a single instant. Even with physical things — various medicinal herbs perceptible to the physical senses — their differences in flavor are still hard to discern, how much more so for formless dharmas, graspable only by discerning wisdom itself. Thus the ten universal-ground dharmas have now been explained.

大善法地名大善地，此中若法大善地所有名大善地法，謂法恒於諸善心有。彼法是何？頌曰：信及不放逸，輕安捨慚愧，二根及不害，勤唯遍善心。

"The ground for universal wholesome dharmas" is called "the universal wholesome ground"; within this, whatever dharma belongs to it is called a "universal-wholesome-ground dharma" — a dharma always present in every wholesome mind. What are these dharmas? A verse states: faith, and non-heedlessness, tranquility, equanimity, modesty, and conscientiousness, the two roots, and non-harming, diligence — these alone pervade every wholesome mind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing aside on the previous list is worth pausing on: the treatise openly admits that distinguishing ten simultaneously-arising, entirely immaterial mental factors within a single instant is an extraordinarily fine-grained task — harder, it says, than telling apart the subtle flavor differences among medicinal herbs, which are at least physical and perceptible to ordinary taste. This is a genuinely humble moment for a system often associated with confident, exhaustive classification.

The second list — faith, non-heedlessness, tranquility, equanimity, modesty, conscientiousness, the two wholesome roots (non-greed and non-hatred), non-harming, and diligence — names the ten factors present in every wholesome mental moment and only there. Several of these terms will need careful unpacking in what follows, since more than one carries a technical sense quite different from its everyday meaning.

10. Bodily Tranquility, Mental Tranquility, and a Borrowed Name

A genuine puzzle opens: "tranquility" is defined here as purely mental, yet sutras also speak of "bodily tranquility" — how can both be right?

Chinese Source

論曰：如是諸法唯遍善心。此中信者令心澄淨。有說：於諦實業果中現前忍許故名為信。不放逸者，修諸善法離諸不善法。復何名修？謂此於善專注為性。餘部經中有如是釋。能守護心名不放逸。

Ru-Yi Translation

Prose: These dharmas pervade only wholesome mind-moments. Among these: "faith" is that which makes the mind clear and pure. Some say: it is called faith because of directly and readily accepting the truths, karmic action, and karmic results. "Non-heedlessness" means cultivating wholesome dharmas and abandoning unwholesome ones. But what does "cultivating" mean here? It means having, as its essential nature, focused concentration upon what is wholesome — other schools' texts give this explanation. Alternatively: that which guards the mind is called non-heedlessness.

輕安者謂心堪任性。豈無經亦說有身輕安耶？雖非無說，此如身受應知亦爾，如何可立此為覺支？應知此中身輕安者身堪任性。復如何說此為覺支？能順覺支故無有失。以身輕安能引覺支心輕安故。於餘亦見有是說耶？有，如經說：喜及順喜法名喜覺支。瞋及瞋因緣名瞋恚蓋。正見、正思惟、正勤名慧蘊。思惟及勤雖非慧性，隨順慧故亦得慧名，故身輕安順覺支故得名無失。

"Tranquility" means the mind's capacity for its task. But does not a sutra also speak of "bodily tranquility"? Though this is indeed said, it should be understood analogously to "bodily feeling" — how, then, can this be established as a factor of enlightenment? One should understand that "bodily tranquility" here means the body's own capacity for its task. But how, then, can this be spoken of as a factor of enlightenment? Because it accords with and supports the factor of enlightenment, there is no fault in this — bodily tranquility is able to draw forth mental tranquility, which is itself the genuine factor. Is this same pattern seen elsewhere? Yes: as a sutra says, "joy and dharmas conducive to joy are named the joy-factor-of-enlightenment"; "hatred and the conditions for hatred are named the hindrance of ill-will"; "right view, right thought, and right effort are named the aggregate of wisdom." Thought and effort, though not themselves of the nature of wisdom, are given the name "wisdom" because they support wisdom — so likewise, bodily tranquility, according with the enlightenment-factor, is given that name without fault.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The puzzle is real: if tranquility (輕安, praśrabdhi) is by definition a purely mental factor, how can scripture speak of a bodily version too? The resolution is a general linguistic principle the treatise finds useful enough to apply three times over: a supporting or conducive factor can borrow the name of what it supports, even when it isn't literally that thing. Bodily tranquility isn't itself the genuine enlightenment-factor, but it reliably gives rise to mental tranquility, which is — so it earns the name derivatively. The same pattern explains why "joy-conducive dharmas" get folded into "the joy-factor," why the mere conditions for hatred count as part of "the hindrance of ill-will" itself, and why thought and effort, not themselves wisdom, still get called "the aggregate of wisdom" when they support it. This is a small but genuinely useful piece of the treatise's own hermeneutics — a rule for how technical vocabulary is allowed to stretch.

11. Attention and Equanimity: A Genuine Puzzle the Text Doesn't Fully Resolve

A harder problem follows, and the treatise's own answer reads more like an honest acknowledgment of difficulty than a clean solution.

Chinese Source

心平等性、無警覺性說名為捨。如何可說於一心中有警覺性、無警覺性？作意與捨二相應起。豈不前說諸心心所其相微細難可了知。有雖難了，由審推度而復可知。此最難知，謂相違背而不乖反。此有警覺於餘則無，二既懸殊有何乖反？若爾，不應同緣一境，或應一切皆互相應。如是種類所餘諸法，此中應求，如彼理趣，今於此中應知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The mind's evenness, its non-alerted nature" is called "equanimity." How can it be said that, within a single moment of mind, there is both an alerted nature and a non-alerted nature? Attention and equanimity, though seemingly opposite, arise together in association. Was it not said earlier that the characteristics of mind and mental factors are extremely subtle and hard to discern? Though difficult, they can still be known through careful, thorough investigation. But this is the hardest of all to know — that things which seem mutually contradictory are not actually in conflict. This one has alertness, the other lacks it — given they are so utterly different, how could there be no conflict? If that were truly a problem, they should not even be able to take the same object together, or else everything would have to be mutually compatible with everything else without exception. Dharmas of this same kind, the remaining ones, should be worked out following this same line of reasoning.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This exchange reads less like a solved puzzle than an honest admission of one. Attention (作意) is defined as what alerts and rouses the mind; equanimity (捨), in its universal-wholesome sense here, is defined as the mind's even, non-alerted quality — and the objector presses exactly the right question: how can a single moment of mind contain both an alerting factor and a non-alerting one at once, without contradiction? The treatise's answer amounts to: they simply do co-arise, and denying this would force denying other things that plainly do work, such as different mental factors sharing a single object. This is not a triumphant resolution so much as a considered "the model holds even where the intuition strains" — and the text says as much itself, calling this the single hardest case of all to genuinely understand, not merely difficult like the others.

12. Closing the Wholesome Factors: Two Roots, Not Three

A tidy piece of bookkeeping closes the list: the classical "three wholesome roots" appear here as only two, and the reason is a matter of not double-counting.

Chinese Source

慚、愧二種如後當釋。二根者，謂無貪、無瞋，無癡善根慧為性，故前已說在大地法中，不重說為大善地法。言不害者，謂無損惱。勤謂令心勇悍為性。如是已說大善地法。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Modesty" and "conscientiousness" will be explained later, in more detail elsewhere. "The two roots" means non-greed and non-hatred; as for non-delusion, being itself identical in essential nature to wisdom, it was already discussed among the universal-ground dharmas, so it is not repeated here as a universal-wholesome-ground dharma. "Non-harming" means being free of the intent to injure. "Diligence" means having, as its essential nature, that which makes the mind bold and vigorous. Thus the universal-wholesome-ground dharmas have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The classical "three wholesome roots" — non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion — are a set every student of Abhidharma learns early, so it's a small but satisfying detail that only two of them show up explicitly in this list of ten. The third, non-delusion, is simply identical in nature to "wisdom," already counted among the ten universal factors that pervade every mind whatsoever. Rather than list it twice under two different names, the treatise notes the overlap and moves on — a nice example of this system's insistence on tracking essential natures rather than surface vocabulary, even when it means a well-known trio quietly becomes a pair in a particular technical list.

13. Six Factors Present in Every Defiled Mind

The third category arrives: six factors, each the direct counterpart of a wholesome factor already named, present in every defiled mental moment without exception.

Chinese Source

大煩惱法地名大煩惱地，此中若法大煩惱地所有名大煩惱地法，謂法恒於染污心有。彼法是何？頌曰：癡逸怠不信，惛掉恒唯染。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The ground for universal-affliction dharmas" is called "the universal-affliction ground"; within this, whatever dharma belongs to it is called a "universal-affliction-ground dharma" — a dharma always present in every defiled mind. What are these dharmas? A verse states: delusion, heedlessness, laziness, lack of faith, torpor, restlessness — these are constantly and only present in defiled mind.

論曰：此中癡者所謂愚癡，即是無明無智無顯。逸謂放逸，不修諸善，是修諸善所對治法。怠謂懈怠，心不勇悍，是前所說勤所對治。不信者謂心不澄淨，是前所說信所對治。惛謂惛沈。對法中說：云何惛沈？謂身重性、心重性，身無堪任性、心無堪任性，身惛沈性、心惛沈性，是名惛沈。此是心所。如何名身？如身受言，故亦無失。掉謂掉舉，令心不靜。唯有如是六種，名大煩惱地法。

Prose: "Delusion" means ignorance — precisely nescience, lack of knowledge, lack of clarity. "Heedlessness" means not cultivating wholesome dharmas — it is what non-heedlessness counteracts. "Laziness" means indolence, the mind not being bold and vigorous — it is what diligence counteracts. "Lack of faith" means the mind not being clear and pure — it is what faith counteracts. "Torpor" means heaviness. An Abhidharma text asks: what is torpor? It is heaviness of body, heaviness of mind, incapacity of body, incapacity of mind, torpor of body, torpor of mind — this is called torpor. But this is a mental factor — how can it be called "of the body"? Just as we speak of "bodily feeling" without contradiction, so too here there is no fault. "Restlessness" means agitation, that which makes the mind unsettled. There are only these six, called the universal-affliction-ground dharmas.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The pattern from the wholesome list carries over almost perfectly in reverse: heedlessness, laziness, and lack of faith are defined explicitly as what non-heedlessness, diligence, and faith each counteract — three direct opposite pairs. Delusion, torpor, and restlessness round out the six as more intrinsically negative qualities in their own right. The "bodily torpor" aside echoes the earlier "bodily tranquility" puzzle from the wholesome list, resolved the same way: a mental factor's name can extend to describe its bodily manifestation without implying the factor itself is physical.

14. Six or Ten? A Sharp Rebuttal Resolves the Discrepancy

An objector cites the root Abhidharma text's count of ten, not six — and gets a memorably sharp response before the real resolution follows.

Chinese Source

豈不根本阿毘達磨中說有十種大煩惱地法，又於彼論不說惛沈。何者十？謂不信、懈怠、失念、心亂、無明、不正知、非理作意、邪勝解、掉舉、放逸。天愛！汝今但知言，至不閑意旨。意旨者何？

Ru-Yi Translation

But does not the root Abhidharma text state that there are ten kinds of universal-affliction-ground dharmas, and moreover that text does not even list torpor among them? What are these ten? Lack of faith, indolence, forgetfulness, mental distraction, nescience, incorrect awareness, improper attention, wrong determination, restlessness, heedlessness. "Dear one! You now merely know the words, but have not yet grasped the meaning." What is the meaning?

謂失念、心亂、不正知、非理作意、邪勝解，已說彼在大地法中，不應重立為大煩惱地法。如無癡善根慧為體故，非大善地法，彼亦應爾。即染污念名為失念，染污等持名為心亂，諸染污慧名不正知，染污作意勝解名為非理作意及邪勝解故說。

Forgetfulness, mental distraction, incorrect awareness, improper attention, and wrong determination have already been discussed among the universal-ground dharmas, and so should not be listed again as separate universal-affliction-ground dharmas. Just as non-delusion, being identical in nature to wisdom, is not separately listed as a universal-wholesome-ground dharma, the same principle should apply here. That is: defiled mindfulness is called forgetfulness; defiled concentration is called mental distraction; defiled wisdom is called incorrect awareness; defiled attention and determination are called improper attention and wrong determination, respectively.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Dear one, you know the words but not the meaning" is a vivid, pointed rebuke — the kind of rhetorical jab classical Indian philosophical debate was not shy about deploying against an objection that looks devastating on the surface but dissolves once properly understood. The resolution runs exactly parallel to the earlier "two roots, not three" case: five of the root text's ten items are not genuinely new factors at all, but simply the defiled, tainted versions of five factors already counted among the universal-ground ten — mindfulness gone wrong is "forgetfulness," concentration gone wrong is "mental distraction," wisdom gone wrong is "incorrect awareness," and so on. Strip out this double-counting and the ten collapses to five genuinely distinct items, plus torpor (which the root text's list happens to omit), landing exactly on six.

15. A Fourfold Analysis, and Torpor's Curious Absence from Another List

A classic Abhidharma tetralemma checks the precise overlap between the universal and the universal-affliction factors, before a further debate about torpor's puzzling omission elsewhere.

Chinese Source

若是大地法亦大煩惱地法耶？應作四句。第一句，謂受、想、思、觸、欲。第二句，謂不信、懈怠、無明、掉舉、放逸。第三句，謂如前說念等五法。第四句，謂除前相。

Ru-Yi Translation

If something is a universal-ground dharma, is it also necessarily a universal-affliction-ground dharma? A fourfold analysis should be made. First case: feeling, perception, volition, contact, desire. Second case: lack of faith, indolence, nescience, restlessness, heedlessness. Third case: mindfulness and the other four dharmas just mentioned. Fourth case: whatever falls outside what has been stated.

又許惛沈通與一切煩惱相應，不說在大煩惱地法。於誰有過？有作是言：應說在此，而不說者順等持故。彼謂諸有惛沈行者速發等持，非掉舉行。誰惛沈行非掉舉行？誰掉舉行非惛沈行？此二未嘗不俱行故。雖爾，應知隨增說行。雖知說行隨用偏增，而依有體建立地法，故此地法唯六義成，此唯遍染心俱起非餘故。如是已說大煩惱地法。

Moreover, some accept that torpor is compatible with every affliction generally, yet still do not list it among the universal-affliction-ground dharmas. Whose position has the fault here? Some say: it should indeed be listed, and the reason it is not is simply to accord with a certain teaching about concentration — they hold that those inclined toward torpor quickly give rise to concentration, unlike those inclined toward restlessness. But who is inclined toward torpor without also being inclined toward restlessness, or the reverse? These two are never entirely absent from each other. Even so, one should understand that describing someone's "inclination" refers to whichever quality predominates. Though which function predominates can differ from case to case, when establishing the ground-dharmas based on genuine essential substance, this category is fixed at exactly six members, since these six alone universally co-arise with every defiled mind, and nothing else does. Thus the universal-affliction-ground dharmas have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The tetralemma cleanly separates dharmas that are universal-ground but never affliction-ground (feeling, perception, and the like, which occur in wholesome states too and aren't inherently defiled), dharmas that are affliction-ground but never universal-ground (lack of faith and the rest, absent from wholesome moments entirely), dharmas that are genuinely both at once (mindfulness and the other four, when they occur in their tainted form), and whatever is neither.

The torpor debate is a fine piece of technical hair-splitting: torpor and restlessness, it turns out, are held to always co-occur to some degree, never one without a trace of the other — so a text describing someone's "torpid" or "restless" tendency is really just naming whichever of the two currently predominates, not asserting the other's total absence. This lets the treatise explain away an apparent inconsistency in another text's list without needing to declare that other list simply wrong.

16. Two Factors Present in Every Unwholesome Mind

The fourth category is the leanest of all: just two factors, present in every specifically unwholesome — not merely defiled — mental moment.

Chinese Source

大不善法地名大不善地，此中若法大不善地所有名大不善地法，謂法恒於不善心有。彼法是何？頌曰：唯遍不善心，無慚及無愧。論曰：唯二心所但與一切不善心俱，謂無慚、愧，故唯二種名此地法。此二法相如後當辯。如是已說大不善地法。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The ground for universal-unwholesome dharmas" is called "the universal-unwholesome ground"; within this, whatever dharma belongs to it is called a "universal-unwholesome-ground dharma" — a dharma always present in every unwholesome mind. What are these dharmas? A verse states: pervading unwholesome mind alone are shamelessness and lack of conscientiousness. Prose: There are only two mental factors that accompany every unwholesome mind without exception — shamelessness and lack of conscientiousness — hence only these two are named this ground. The characteristics of these two dharmas will be explained further later. Thus the universal-unwholesome-ground dharmas have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Defiled" and "unwholesome" are not quite the same scope in this system — defiled includes states that obstruct progress without being karmically unwholesome in the strict sense, while "unwholesome" is the narrower, morally-charged category. That narrower scope is exactly why this fourth ground has only two members: shamelessness and lack of conscientiousness, the direct opposites of the modesty and conscientiousness named among the wholesome ten, are the only factors specifically tied to every unwholesome act without exception.

17. The Ten Minor Afflictions, and the Five Categories Complete

The fifth and final category closes the whole taxonomy: ten familiar, vivid afflictions that accompany only some defiled states, never all of them together.

Chinese Source

小煩惱法地名小煩惱地，此中若法小煩惱地所有名小煩惱地法，謂法少分染污心俱。彼法是何？頌曰：忿覆慳嫉惱，害恨諂誑憍，如是類名為，小煩惱地法。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The ground for minor-affliction dharmas" is called "the minor-affliction ground"; within this, whatever dharma belongs to it is called a "minor-affliction-ground dharma" — a dharma that accompanies only some portion of defiled minds. What are these dharmas? A verse states: anger, concealment, stinginess, envy, vexation, harmfulness, resentment, flattery, deceit, arrogance — dharmas of this kind are named the minor-affliction-ground dharmas.

論曰：如是類法唯修所斷，意識地起、無明相應、各別現行，故名為小煩惱地法。此法如後隨煩惱中當廣分別。如是已說五品心所。

Prose: Dharmas of this kind are cut off only by cultivation, arise only at the level of mind-consciousness, are associated with nescience, and manifest individually and separately — hence they are named minor-affliction-ground dharmas. These dharmas will be explained in full detail later, in the discussion of the secondary afflictions. Thus the five categories of mental factors have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Unlike the universal factors, which always arise together as a complete package, these ten minor afflictions — anger, concealment, stinginess, envy, vexation, harmfulness, resentment, flattery, deceit, and arrogance — typically manifest one or a few at a time rather than all together, which is exactly what "minor" and "individually manifesting" mean here. They are also confined to mind-consciousness alone (never accompanying the five physical sense-consciousnesses) and are cut off only gradually through cultivation, never all at once by a single moment of insight.

With this, the treatise's complete five-category taxonomy of mental factors closes: ten universal factors present in every mind whatsoever, ten present only in wholesome minds, six present only in defiled minds, two present only in specifically unwholesome minds, and ten minor afflictions present only sometimes, in varying combinations. This framework — thirty-eight named factors across five overlapping categories — will underpin much of the psychological analysis still to come in this treatise.
