What Must Arise Together?

This page carries the opening of fascicle 4, continuing Chapter 2 (根品第二之二, the second part of the Indriya-nirdeśa) with a new topic: which dharmas are so tightly bound that they can never arise apart from one another, from the minimal atomic cluster of matter up through mind's inseparability from its own mental factors.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 6 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 4 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. A New Chapter Question: Which Dharmas Must Arise Together?

Fascicle 4 opens the second part of Chapter 2 with a fresh question, framed against the treatise's basic five-category ontology of all dharmas.

Chinese Source

今應思擇，一切有為如相不同，生亦各異，為有諸法決定俱生、有定俱生。謂一切法略有五品：一色、二心、三心所、四心不相應行、五無為。無為無生，此中不說。今先辯色決定俱生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now one should consider: since all conditioned dharmas differ in their characteristics, their arising likewise differs in each case — are there dharmas that definitely arise together with certain others, in fixed groups? All dharmas, briefly, fall into five categories: form, mind, mental factors, formations dissociated from mind, and the unconditioned. The unconditioned does not arise at all, so it is not discussed here. Let us first discuss form's definite co-arising.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This opens a brand-new major topic within Chapter 2's second installment: which dharmas are bound together so tightly that they never occur apart from one another, always arising as an indivisible package. The five-category ontology named here — form, mind, mental factors, formations dissociated from mind, and the unconditioned — has been implicit throughout this treatise; the unconditioned is set aside immediately since, being entirely outside causation, it never "arises" in the first place, making the question of what it co-arises with simply moot.

2. The Minimal Atomic Cluster: Eight, Nine, or Ten Factors

The treatise establishes the absolute floor for any physical cluster in the desire realm — never a single isolated atom, but always a minimal bundle of at least eight distinct factors.

Chinese Source

頌曰：欲微聚無聲，無根有八事，有身根九事，十事有餘根。論曰：色聚極細立微聚名，為顯更無細於此者。此在欲界無聲無根，八事俱生隨一不減。云何八事？謂四大種及四所造色、香、味、觸。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: In the desire realm, an atomic cluster without sound and without a sense faculty has eight factors; with the body faculty, nine; with the other faculties, ten. Prose: "The finest division of a cluster of form" is given the name "atomic cluster," to indicate that nothing finer than this exists. In the desire realm, a cluster without sound and without a sense faculty has eight factors arising together, with not even one factor reducible. What are these eight? The four primary elements, together with four kinds of derived form: visible form, smell, taste, and the tangible quality.

無聲有根諸極微聚，此俱生事或九或十。有身根聚九事俱生，八事如前，身為第九。有餘根聚十事俱生，九事如前，加眼等一。眼耳鼻舌必不離身，展轉相望處各別故。

For atomic clusters without sound but with a sense faculty, the co-arising factors are nine or ten. A cluster with the body faculty has nine — the eight as before, with the body faculty as the ninth. A cluster with one of the other faculties has ten — the nine as before, plus that faculty. The eye, ear, nose, and tongue can never occur apart from the body faculty, because, though mutually distinct in location, they are all situated upon it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "atomic cluster" (微聚) is the absolute finest-grained unit of matter this system recognizes — never a single atom acting alone, echoing a point made far earlier in this translation that a solitary atom can never by itself produce perception, but always a minimal bundle. Even the barest possible cluster, lacking both sound and any sense faculty, still requires eight distinct factors together: the four primary elements (earth, water, fire, wind) plus four kinds of derived matter (visible form, smell, taste, and the tangible quality as an object). Adding a sense faculty adds factors in a strict, load-bearing order: the body faculty adds one, making nine; and since the eye, ear, nose, and tongue are never found except embedded within a body that also has the tactile faculty right there, adding any of those adds a full two beyond the base eight — the body faculty plus the specific sense itself — making ten.

3. Adding Sound, and a Puzzle About the Four Elements

Sound raises the counts by one across the board, before a genuinely interesting question opens: if the four elements never occur apart, why does ordinary experience only ever notice one at a time?

Chinese Source

於前諸聚若有聲生，如次數增九十十一，以有聲處不離根生，謂有執受大種因起。若四大種不相離生，於諸聚中堅濕煖動，云何隨一可得非餘？

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the aforementioned clusters, if sound also arises, the counts respectively increase to nine, ten, and eleven — since wherever sound arises in this context, it does not occur apart from where a sense faculty arises, because it arises specifically from primary elements that are "grasped," that is, part of a living body. If the four primary elements always arise inseparably together, then within these clusters — solidity, moisture, warmth, and motion — how can only one of these ever be detected, and not the others?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The puzzle is a foundational and genuinely interesting one: the four primary elements — earth (solidity), water (moisture), fire (warmth), wind (motion) — are held to never occur apart from one another. Every particle of matter, however solid-seeming, technically contains all four in some proportion. So why does a rock simply feel hard, without also feeling obviously wet, warm, and in motion? The question sets up the treatise's answer in the next section — one that will turn out to explain a very ordinary, everyday observation everyone has already made without knowing its Abhidharma explanation.

4. Resolving the Puzzle: Dominant Function and Latent Seeds

Two rival explanations are recorded side by side, along with a striking everyday observation offered as evidence, and a rare moment of the treatise admitting the limits of what can be known by inference alone.

Chinese Source

於彼聚中勢用增者明了可得，餘體非無。如覺針鋒與籌合觸，如甞鹽味與麨合味。云何於彼知亦有餘？由有攝熟長持業故。有說：遇緣堅等便有流等相故，如水聚中由極冷故有煖相起，雖不相離而冷用增，如受及聲用有勝劣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within that cluster, whichever element's function is intensified is what becomes clearly detectable — the essential nature of the others is not thereby absent. It is like feeling a needle's point together with a bundled tally-stick in contact with the skin, or tasting salt's flavor mixed together with roasted grain-flour's flavor. How do we know the others are still present too? Because the cluster still performs the functions of cohering, ripening, growing, and sustaining. Some say: under the right conditions, even a solid thing can develop a flowing quality — just as, in a cluster of water, extreme cold gives rise to the appearance of warmth; though never separate, the cold function is ordinarily what is intensified, just as feeling and sound each have their own degrees of dominance.

有餘師說：於此聚中餘有種子未有體相，故契經說：於木聚中有種種界。界謂種子。如何風中知有顯色？此義可信，不可比知。或所合香現可取故，香與顯色不相離故。

Other masters say: within this cluster, the remaining elements exist as seeds without yet having a manifest characteristic — hence a sutra says, "within a cluster of wood there are various elements," meaning by "element" here, a seed. How, then, do we know that visible color is present within wind itself? This should simply be trusted, since it cannot be established by direct inference — or: because the smell mixed together with it can indeed be detected, and smell is never separate from visible color.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The resolution is really about the gap between metaphysical presence and perceptual salience. Nothing is ever missing from the cluster — all four elements are always genuinely there — but only the dominant one registers to ordinary perception, the way a sharp needle-point stands out from a bundle of blunter sticks even though the whole bundle touches the skin, or a strong salt flavor masks a subtler grain flavor in the same mouthful. The "extreme cold feels like warmth" observation — anyone who has touched dry ice or a very cold piece of metal and felt something like a burn knows this directly — is offered as concrete evidence that the non-dominant qualities are still active, and can even flip which one becomes dominant under extreme conditions.

A rival explanation is more deflationary: rather than holding all four always fully manifest with only one salient, this view says the non-dominant three exist merely as latent seeds, not yet actualized — a genuinely different metaphysical picture, which the treatise records without adjudicating between the two. The closing point about wind's invisible color is a rare moment of epistemic humility: some doctrinal claims, the text admits, simply cannot be verified by ordinary inference and must be accepted on the teaching's own authority, or inferred indirectly through an associated quality — here, smell — that can actually be perceived.

5. The Form Realm's Simpler Clusters, and What We're Actually Counting

The same counting exercise closes for the form realm, before a methodological aside asks a deceptively simple question: what exactly is being counted when we say "eight factors"?

Chinese Source

前說色界香味並無，故彼無聲有六七八，有聲有七八九俱生。此可准知，故不別說。此中言事為依體說、為依處說？若爾何過？二俱有過。

Ru-Yi Translation

It was earlier stated that the form realm lacks both smell and taste. Therefore, clusters there without sound have six, seven, or eight factors co-arising; with sound, seven, eight, or nine. This can be inferred by the same reasoning as before, so it is not separately explained. In this context, does the word "factor" refer to distinct substances, or to distinct locations? Either way, is there a problem? Both readings have one.

若依體說，八九十等便為太少，由諸微聚必有形色，有多極微共積集故。重性輕性定隨有一，滑性澁性隨一亦然。或處有冷有飢有渴，是則所言有太少過。若依處說，八九十等便為太多，由四大種觸處攝故應說四等，是則所言有太多失。二俱無過。應知此中所言事者，一分依體說，謂所依大種；一分依處說，謂能依造色。

If explained by substance, then eight, nine, ten, and so on would be too few, since every atomic cluster also necessarily has shape, many atoms being accumulated together; heaviness or lightness definitely accompanies one or the other as well, and so does smoothness or roughness; a given case might also have coldness, hunger, or thirst — so the stated counts would be too few. If explained by location, then eight, nine, ten would be too many, since the four primary elements are all subsumed under the single tangible sense-field, so it should simply be said "four" and so on — so the stated counts would be too many. Neither reading actually has a problem: "factor," as used here, is partly based on substance — the supporting primary elements — and partly based on location — the supported derived matter.

若爾，大種事應成多，造色各別，依一四大種故。應知此中依體類說，諸四大種類無別故。何用分別如是語為？語隨欲生，義應思擇。如是已辯色定俱生。

If that is so, should not the primary-element factors then become numerous, since derived matter is each individually distinct while depending on a single set of four primary elements? One should understand that the primary elements are spoken of by type of substance here, since the four primary elements, as a type, admit no further distinction within that type. What use is there in belaboring such wording? Language follows what one wishes to express; it is the meaning that should be carefully considered. Thus form's definite co-arising has now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The form realm's simpler counts follow directly from a rule established much earlier in this translation — the form realm lacks smell and taste entirely — so its clusters are simply the desire realm's counts minus two. The more interesting payoff is the closing methodological aside: pressed on whether "factor" means substance or location, the treatise refuses to be pinned to either pure reading, since each one alone generates an absurd over- or under-count. Instead it adopts a practical hybrid: the four primary elements are counted by type — there are always exactly four types, however many atoms exist — while derived matter is counted by individual kind, since each type of derived quality is its own distinct thing. The closing remark, that the meaning matters more than the wording, is a small but telling moment of Abhidharma pragmatism, refusing to let a terminological puzzle grow bigger than the substantive point it was meant to serve.

6. Mind and Mental Factors Always Co-Arise, and So Do the Four Marks of Conditioning

Two further, even more universal co-arising rules follow: mind never occurs without its mental factors, and every conditioned dharma whatsoever arises together with the four marks that define conditioned existence itself.

Chinese Source

餘定俱生今次當辯。頌曰：心心所必俱，諸行相或得。論曰：心與心所必定俱生，隨闕一時餘則不起。諸行即是一切有為，謂色、心、心所、心不相應行。前必俱言流至於此，謂色心等諸行生時，必與有為四相俱起。言或得者，謂諸行內唯有情法與得俱生；餘法不然，是故言或。

Ru-Yi Translation

The remaining categories' definite co-arising should be explained next. A verse states: mind and mental factors necessarily co-arise; all formations co-arise with their characteristics; or, also, with possession. Prose: Mind and mental factors necessarily co-arise — whenever even one is lacking, the others do not arise either. "Formations" means all conditioned dharmas: form, mind, mental factors, and formations dissociated from mind. The earlier word "necessarily co-arise" carries forward to apply here too: when form, mind, and the other formations arise, they necessarily arise together with the four characteristics of the conditioned. As for saying "or": this means that, among all formations, only dharmas belonging to sentient beings co-arise together with "possession"; other dharmas do not, hence the word "or."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Mind and its mental factors are as inseparable as any of the form-clusters just discussed — a moment of consciousness never occurs as bare awareness alone, always accompanied by at least some minimal set of mental factors, and vice versa. Even more universal: literally every conditioned dharma whatsoever — form, mind, mental factors, and formations dissociated from mind alike — necessarily arises together with the four defining marks of conditioned existence itself (birth, continuance, decay, and cessation), since to be conditioned at all just is to be marked by this fourfold pattern.

The qualifier "or" narrows one further rule to a specific subset: "possession" (得, a technical formation representing a being's ownership of a dharma) accompanies a dharma's arising only when that dharma belongs to a sentient being's own continuum — inanimate matter, for instance, arises with the four marks like everything else, but has no "owner," and so never co-arises with possession.
