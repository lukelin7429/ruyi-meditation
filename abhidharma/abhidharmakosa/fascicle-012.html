From One Atom to Eighty Kalpas

This page carries the opening of fascicle 12, continuing Chapter 3's fifth installment (分別世品第三之五, the Loka-nirdeśa's fifth part) by supplying the units the previous fascicle's measurements were already built on: the smallest possible unit of form (a single atom), of name (a single syllable), and of time (a single instant), the full sevenfold chain of distance from an atom up to a yojana, the parallel chain of time from an instant up to a year, and the vast cycles of cosmic destruction and formation — measured in kalpas — that a single great kalpa is built from.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 23 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 12 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. The Three Smallest Units: Atom, Syllable, Instant

Before the previous fascicle's measurements in yojanas and years can be fully grounded, the treatise supplies the smallest possible unit each is built from: a single indivisible atom for form, a single syllable for a name, and a single instant for time — defining that instant, in turn, as either the span in which a dharma first attains its own existence, or the time it takes a moving object to cross one atom's width.

Chinese Source

如是已約踰繕那等辯器世間身量差別。約年等辯壽量有殊，二量不同未說應說。此二建立無不依名，前二及名未詳極少，今應先辯三極少量。頌曰：極微字剎那，色名時極少。論曰：分析諸色至一極微，故一極微為色極少。如是分析諸名及時至一字剎那，為名時極少。一字名者，如說瞿名。何等名為一剎那量？眾緣和合法得自體頃，或有動法行度一極微。對法諸師說：如壯士一疾彈指頃六十五剎那，如是名為一剎那量。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus far, bodily size differences within the container world have been discussed in terms of yojanas and the like. Lifespan differences, discussed in terms of years and the like, remain to be discussed, since the two kinds of measurement are not the same. Neither of these two systems of measurement can be established without reference to units of designation — and the smallest possible units of both designation and time have not yet been made clear. It is fitting, therefore, to first discuss the three smallest quantities. Verse: the atom, the syllable, and the instant are the smallest units of form, name, and time, respectively. Prose: analyzing all form down to a single indivisible particle, this single particle is the smallest unit of form. Likewise, analyzing all names and all time down to a single syllable or a single instant, these are the smallest units of name and time. By "a one-syllable name" is meant something like the word "cow." What, then, constitutes the measure of a single instant? It is the span of time in which a dharma produced by the coming-together of its assembled conditions attains its own essential nature, or, alternatively, the time it takes a moving object to traverse a single indivisible particle's width. The Ābhidharmikas say: it is the span of time in which a vigorous, able-bodied man snaps his fingers once, swiftly, divided into sixty-five instants — this is what is called the measure of a single instant.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two definitions of an "instant" offered here are subtly different in kind: the first defines it functionally, by reference to a dharma's own coming-into-being, while the second defines it physically, by reference to motion across a fixed minimal distance — and the Ābhidharmikas' snap-of-the-fingers figure (sixty-five instants) is offered as a third, purely empirical anchor point, translating an abstract minimal unit into something a reader can actually imagine happening in their own hand.

2. From One Atom to One Yojana: A Sevenfold Chain

Every distance measurement in this cosmology, all the way up to the yojana, turns out to rest on a single repeated rule: each unit is exactly seven times the one before it, climbing from an atom through motes of metal-dust, water-dust, rabbit-hair-dust, sheep-hair-dust, ox-hair-dust, and ordinary drifting dust, up through a louse-egg, a louse, a grain of barley, and a finger-joint, before switching to the more familiar units of finger, cubit, bow-length, krośa, and finally yojana.

Chinese Source

已知三極少，前二量云何？今且辯前踰繕那等。頌曰：極微微金水，兔羊牛隙塵，蟣虱麥指節，後後增七倍，二十四指肘，四肘為弓量，五百俱盧舍，此八踰繕那。論曰：極微為初，指節為後，應知後後皆七倍增。謂七極微為一微量，積微至七為一金塵，積七金塵為水塵量，水塵積至七為一兔毛塵，積七兔毛塵為羊毛塵量，積羊毛塵七為一牛毛塵，積七牛毛塵為隙遊塵量，隙塵七為蟣，七蟣為一虱，七虱為穬麥，七麥為指節，三節為一指。世所極成，是故於頌中不別分別。二十四指橫布為肘，竪積四肘為弓謂尋，竪積五百弓為一俱盧舍，一俱盧舍許是從村至阿練若中間道量。說八俱盧舍為一踰繕那。如是已說踰繕那等。

Ru-Yi Translation

The three smallest units are now known — what, then, of the first two larger measurements? Let us now discuss distance measures such as the yojana. Verse: atom, particle, metal-dust, water-dust, rabbit-hair-dust, sheep-hair-dust, ox-hair-dust, gap-dust, louse-egg, louse, barley grain, finger-joint — each subsequent unit increases sevenfold; twenty-four finger-widths make a cubit, four cubits make a bow-length, five hundred bow-lengths make a krośa, and eight of these make a yojana. Prose: beginning with the atom and ending with the finger-joint, each subsequent unit should be understood to increase sevenfold over the last. That is: seven atoms make one particle; accumulating particles to seven makes one metal-dust mote; accumulating seven metal-dust motes makes one water-dust mote; accumulating water-dust to seven makes one rabbit-hair-dust mote; accumulating seven rabbit-hair-dust motes makes one sheep-hair-dust mote; accumulating seven sheep-hair-dust motes makes one ox-hair-dust mote; accumulating seven ox-hair-dust motes makes one gap-dust mote; seven gap-dust motes make one louse-egg; seven louse-eggs make one louse; seven lice make one grain of barley; seven barley grains make one finger-joint; three finger-joints make one finger. The next several units are already commonly established in the world, and so are not separately distinguished in the verse: twenty-four finger-widths, laid side by side, make one cubit; four cubits, stacked vertically, make one bow-length, that is, a fathom; five hundred bow-lengths, stacked vertically, make one krośa — a krośa being, roughly, the distance along the road from a village to a forest hermitage. Eight krośas are said to make one yojana. Distance measures such as the yojana have thus been discussed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chain's naming convention shifts partway through in a telling way: the first eleven steps (atom through finger-joint) are given exotic, constructed names — units nobody actually uses, built purely to bridge the gap between the imperceptibly small and the humanly graspable — while the final several steps (finger, cubit, bow-length, krośa, yojana) are explicitly described as units "already commonly established in the world," ordinary measures any reader would already know. The verse only bothers to specify the sevenfold rule for the invented half of the chain, since the familiar half needs no such justification — a small but clear signal of where technical Abhidharma vocabulary ends and everyday convention begins.

3. From One Instant to One Year, and Why Nights Shorten Twice a Season

Time builds up from the instant the same way distance builds up from the atom: 120 instants make a tatkṣaṇa, 60 of those make a lava, 30 make a muhūrta, 30 make a day-and-night, 30 days make a month, and 12 months make a year — with a precise rule specifying that, within each of the year's three seasons, night measurably shortens during the second half of every one-and-a-half-month stretch.

Chinese Source

今當辯後年等量別。頌曰：百二十剎那，為怛剎那量，臘縛此六十，此三十須臾，此三十晝夜，三十晝夜月，十二月為年，於中半減夜。論曰：剎那百二十為一怛剎那，六十怛剎那為一臘縛，三十臘縛為一牟呼栗多，三十牟呼栗多為一晝夜。此晝夜有時增、有時減、有時等。三十晝夜為一月，總十二月為一年。於一年中分為三際，謂寒、熱、雨各有四月。十二月中六月減夜，以一年內夜總減六。云何如是？故有頌言：寒熱雨際中，一月半已度，於所餘半月，智者知夜減。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now the remaining measures, such as the year, should be discussed. Verse: one hundred twenty instants make one tatkṣaṇa; sixty of these make one lava; thirty of these make one muhūrta; thirty of these make one day-and-night; thirty days-and-nights make a month; twelve months make a year; within this, night decreases by half. Prose: one hundred twenty instants make one tatkṣaṇa; sixty tatkṣaṇas make one lava; thirty lavas make one muhūrta; thirty muhūrtas make one day-and-night. This day-and-night is sometimes lengthening, sometimes shortening, and sometimes equal. Thirty days-and-nights make one month; twelve months in total make one year. Within a single year, it is divided into three seasons — cold, hot, and rainy — each of four months. Among these twelve months, night decreases across six of them, so that, within a single year, night's total decrease amounts to six portions. How is this so? Hence there is a verse that states: "Within the cold, hot, and rainy seasons, once a month and a half has passed, during the remaining half-month, the wise know that night decreases."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This ties directly back to fascicle 11's own account of Jambudvīpa's shifting day-and-night length, now formalized into a precise seasonal rule: night shortens specifically in the second half of a month-and-a-half stretch, within each of the three four-month seasons, rather than gradually and evenly across the whole year. The abstract time-unit chain built in this section — instant to tatkṣaṇa to lava to muhūrta — exists largely to make a rule like this one statable with precision, the same way the distance chain in the previous section existed to make yojana-scale cosmology statable in the first place.

4. Four Kinds of Kalpa, and How Destruction Begins in Hell

The treatise now turns from measuring moments to measuring entire cosmic ages: destruction, formation, the medium kalpa that structures both, and the great kalpa built from eighty of these. A destruction kalpa begins, with almost bureaucratic precision, the moment hell-beings simply stop being reborn there — any being whose karma still demands hellish suffering is instead redirected to a hell in some other, not-yet-destroyed world-system, until this one's hells stand entirely empty.

Chinese Source

如是已辯剎那至年。劫量不同，今次當辯。頌曰：應知有四劫，謂壞成中大，壞從獄不生，至外器都盡，成劫從風起，至地獄初生，中劫從無量，減至壽唯十，次增減十八，後增至八萬，如是成已住，名中二十劫，成壞壞已空，時皆等住劫，八十中大劫，大劫三無數。論曰：言壞劫者，謂從地獄有情不復生至外器都盡。壞有二種：一趣壞、二界壞。復有二種：一有情壞、二外器壞。謂此世間過於二十中劫住已，從此復有等住二十，壞劫便至。若時地獄有情命終無復新生，為壞劫始。乃至地獄無一有情，爾時名為地獄已壞。諸有地獄定受業者，業力引置他方獄中，由此准知傍生鬼趣。然各先壞本處住者，人天雜居者與人天同壞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Time from the instant up to the year has thus been discussed. Kalpa-measures differ from these, and should now be discussed next. Verse: it should be known that there are four kinds of kalpa — namely, destruction, formation, the medium kalpa, and the great kalpa. The destruction runs from when hell-beings cease to be reborn until the container world is entirely exhausted. The formation runs from when the wind first arises until hell-beings are first reborn. A medium kalpa runs from human lifespan being immeasurable, decreasing until lifespan is only ten years; then increasing and decreasing eighteen times in succession; then increasing once more up to eighty thousand years — this, together with abiding-after-formation, is called twenty medium kalpas. Formation, destruction, and the emptiness after destruction each likewise equal the abiding-kalpa in duration. Eighty medium kalpas make one great kalpa; three great kalpas, incalculable in number, are needed for a bodhisattva's practice. Prose: as for the "destruction kalpa," this refers to the period from when hell-beings cease to be reborn until the container world is entirely exhausted. Destruction is of two kinds: destruction of the destinies, and destruction of the realm itself. There are, further, two kinds: destruction of sentient beings, and destruction of the container world. That is: once this world-system has abided for twenty medium kalpas, there then follows a further twenty medium kalpas of equal duration, at the end of which the destruction kalpa arrives. When hell-beings, upon their deaths, are no longer reborn there anew, this marks the beginning of the destruction kalpa. Once there remains not a single being left in hell, this is called "hell already destroyed." Any beings whose karma definitely requires suffering in hell are, by the power of that karma, conveyed to hells in other world-systems instead — from this, the same should be understood, by analogy, for the animal and ghost destinies. However, each destiny is destroyed first in its own original dwelling-place; those destinies that dwell intermixed with humans and gods are destroyed together with the humans and gods in whose territory they dwell.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The redirection of still-karma-bound hell-beings to hells in other, not-yet-destroyed world-systems is a small but load-bearing piece of cosmological bookkeeping: it prevents a world-system's destruction from ever functioning as an accidental amnesty, letting beings escape suffering they still karmically owe simply because the particular hell they were in ran out of world to exist in. The system as a whole is designed so that karma always finds somewhere to ripen, even across the destruction of an entire universe.

5. A Single Word Empties a World: Destruction Spreads Upward

Once the lower destinies are destroyed, the pattern that empties the human and heavenly realms is genuinely remarkable: a single person, entirely without a teacher, spontaneously discovers the first dhyāna, and simply announcing how blissful it is causes everyone who hears to enter the same absorption and, upon death, be reborn in the Brahmā world — a chain reaction that empties an entire continent, then an entire heaven, then the whole desire realm, and finally, by the same mechanism one level up, the Brahmā world itself, until nothing remains but the empty container world.

Chinese Source

若時人趣此洲一人，無師法然得初靜慮，從靜慮起唱如是言：離生喜樂，甚樂甚靜。餘人聞已皆入靜慮，命終並得生梵世中。乃至此洲有情都盡，是名已壞贍部洲人。東西二洲例此應說。北洲命盡生欲界天，由彼無能入定離欲。乃至人趣無一有情，爾時名為人趣已壞。若時天趣四大王天隨一法然得初靜慮，乃至並得生梵世中。爾時彼天有情都盡，是名已壞大王眾天。餘五欲天例同此說。乃至欲界無一有情，名欲界中有情已壞。若時梵世隨一有情，無師法然得二靜慮，從彼定起唱如是言：定生喜樂，甚樂甚靜。餘天聞已皆入彼靜慮，命終並得生極光淨天。乃至梵世中有情都盡，如是名已壞有情世間。唯器世間空曠而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

When, at a certain time, a single person in this continent, without a teacher, spontaneously attains the first dhyāna, and, rising from that absorption, proclaims: "The joy and bliss born of seclusion are truly blissful, truly tranquil!" — the other people, hearing this, all likewise enter that same absorption, and, upon death, are all reborn in the Brahmā world. This continues until the beings of this continent are entirely exhausted; this is called "the people of Jambudvīpa already destroyed." The eastern and western continents should be explained by the same pattern. In the northern continent, upon death, beings are reborn in the desire-realm heavens instead, since those people are incapable of entering absorption or becoming free of desire. This continues until there remains not a single being in the human destiny; at that time, this is called "the human destiny already destroyed." When, at a certain time, among the gods, one of the Four Great Kings' heaven spontaneously attains the first dhyāna, and so on, until they too are all reborn in the Brahmā world — at that time, the beings of that heaven are entirely exhausted; this is called "the Heaven of the Four Great Kings already destroyed." The remaining five desire heavens should be explained by the same pattern. This continues until there remains not a single being in the desire realm; this is called "the sentient beings of the desire realm already destroyed." When, at a certain time, one being in the Brahmā world, without a teacher, spontaneously attains the second dhyāna, and, rising from that absorption, proclaims: "The joy and bliss born of concentration are truly blissful, truly tranquil!" — the other gods, hearing this, all likewise enter that same absorption, and, upon death, are all reborn in the Ābhāsvara Heaven. This continues until the beings of the Brahmā world are entirely exhausted; in this way, this is called "the world of sentient beings already destroyed." Only the container world remains, empty and vast, abiding on its own.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This mechanism is worth sitting with, since it is genuinely different from how the lower destinies were destroyed: hell, animal, and ghost beings simply stop being reborn, a passive cessation, but the human and heavenly realms are emptied through something closer to spontaneous spiritual contagion — one being's unprompted discovery of meditative bliss, described almost as an announcement ("truly blissful, truly tranquil!"), triggers everyone within earshot to follow. Destruction, at this level of the cosmos, is not something that happens to beings so much as something they are drawn toward once shown what lies past ordinary desire — an oddly hopeful mechanism for what is, from a bird's-eye view, the annihilation of an entire world.

The same pattern repeats one level up with a different verse of praise (concentration's bliss rather than seclusion's) and a different destination (Ābhāsvara rather than the Brahmā world), showing that this "contagious absorption" mechanism is not a one-time narrative device but a structural principle applied recursively at each tier of the cosmos that still contains beings capable of meditative attainment.

6. Seven Suns and a Fire That Climbs to Brahmā's Palace

With every sentient being gone, the empty container world must still be destroyed. Seven suns appear in succession, boiling the oceans dry and setting the mountains themselves ablaze, while wind-driven fire climbs relentlessly upward — but only ever consuming its own level before drawing forth the next level's fire in turn, all the way up to the Brahmā world, which burns to nothing but ash. With this, the destruction kalpa is complete.

Chinese Source

餘十方界一切有情，感此三千世界業盡，於此漸有七日輪現，諸海乾竭眾山洞然，洲渚三輪並從焚燎，風吹猛焰燒上天宮，乃至梵宮無遺灰燼。自地火焰燒自地宮，非他地災能壞他地，由相引起故作是言。下火風飄焚燒上地，謂欲界火猛焰上昇為緣引生色界火焰。餘災亦爾，如應當知。如是始從地獄漸減乃至器盡，總名壞劫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once the karma of all sentient beings throughout the ten directions that gave rise to this trichiliocosm has been exhausted, seven suns gradually appear in succession; all the seas dry up completely, the mountains burst into flame, and the continents together with the three wheels are all consumed by fire; the wind fans the fierce flames, burning up to the heavenly palaces above, until, reaching the Brahmā palace, nothing remains but ash. A given ground's own fire burns that same ground's own palaces — a calamity native to one ground cannot, by itself, destroy another ground; it is said to happen only because one ground's fire draws forth and gives rise to the next. The fire from below, driven by wind, spreads upward and burns the ground above — that is, the desire realm's fire, its fierce flames rising upward, serves as a condition that draws forth and gives rise to the form realm's own fire. The remaining calamities should likewise be understood accordingly. In this way, starting from the hells gradually diminishing all the way to the container world's complete exhaustion, this entire process is called, in total, the destruction kalpa.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rule that "a calamity native to one ground cannot, by itself, destroy another ground" is a careful piece of causal discipline applied even to an apocalypse: the fire doesn't simply spread because fire spreads, but because each level's fire specifically serves as a condition drawing forth the next level's own, distinct fire — form-realm fire is not literally the same flame as desire-realm fire grown large enough to reach it, but a new occurrence of fire native to that higher ground, triggered by the lower one. This is the same causal fastidiousness already seen throughout this text's treatment of ordinary causation, applied here at the scale of universal conflagration. The passing mention of "the remaining calamities" — water and wind, which destroy the world by other means in other cycles — signals that fire is only one of three ways a world-system can end, a topic the text flags for later without elaborating here.

7. Formation: The Same Process, Perfectly Reversed

After twenty medium kalpas of pure emptiness, a faint wind stirs and the whole process runs in exact reverse: the wind, water, and gold wheels rebuild themselves, the Brahmā world's palace is the very first thing to form, and the very first being to arrive descends from Ābhāsvara to become its king. From there, beings descend tier by tier — Brahmā's ministers, then retinue, down through the desire heavens and the four continents, finally reaching ghosts, animals, and hell — following the natural law that whatever was destroyed last must be the first thing to form again.

Chinese Source

所言成劫，謂從風起乃至地獄始有情生。謂此世間災所壞已，二十中劫唯有虛空，過此長時次應復有等住二十，成劫便至。一切有情業增上力，空中漸有微細風生，是器世間將成前相。風漸增盛，成立如前所說風輪水金輪等，然初成立大梵王宮乃至夜摩宮。後起風輪等，是謂成立外器世間。初一有情極光淨歿，生大梵處為大梵王。後諸有情亦從彼歿，有生梵輔、有生梵眾、有生他化自在天宮。漸漸下生，乃至人趣俱盧、牛貨、勝身、贍部，後生餓鬼、傍生、地獄。法爾後壞，必最初成。若初一有情生無間獄，二十中成劫應知已滿，此後復有二十中劫名成已住，次第而起，謂從風起造器世間，乃至後時有情漸住。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the "formation kalpa": this refers to the period from when the wind first arises until beings are first born in hell. That is: once this world has been destroyed by calamity, there follows twenty medium kalpas of pure empty space; once this long span has passed, a further twenty medium kalpas of equal duration follow, and the formation kalpa then arrives. Through the dominant power of all sentient beings' collective karma, a subtle, faint wind gradually arises within that empty space — this is the preliminary sign that the container world is about to form. The wind gradually grows and intensifies, forming the wind wheel, water wheel, and gold wheel, and the rest, exactly as described before; and then, first of all, the palace of the Great Brahmā King is established, followed in turn all the way down to the Yāma palace. Afterward, the wind wheel and the rest arise below — this is what is meant by "the container world being established." The very first being to descend is one who passes away from the Ābhāsvara Heaven and is reborn in the Great Brahmā's realm, becoming the Great Brahmā King. Afterward, other beings likewise pass away and are reborn — some in the Heaven of Brahmā's Ministers, some in the Heaven of Brahmā's Retinue, some in the Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations. Beings descend gradually, lower and lower, until they reach the human destinies of Uttarakuru, Aparagodānīya, Pūrvavideha, and Jambudvīpa, and afterward are reborn as hungry ghosts, animals, and hell-beings. It is a natural law that whatever is destroyed last must be what forms first. Once the very first being is reborn in Avīci hell, the twenty medium kalpas of the formation kalpa should be understood as complete. After this, there follows a further twenty medium kalpas called "abiding-after-formation," arising in sequence — that is, from when the wind first arises to construct the container world, up until beings gradually come to abide within it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The principle "whatever is destroyed last must be what forms first" gives the entire cosmic cycle a strict, almost mechanical symmetry: destruction proceeded upward from hell to the Brahmā world, so formation must proceed downward from the Brahmā world back to hell — the two halves of the cycle are exact mirror images of each other, not independent processes that merely happen to bookend one another. This is worth noticing as the same design instinct seen everywhere else in this fascicle's cosmology: a small number of rules, applied consistently and reversibly, generate the entire structure rather than requiring separate, unrelated descriptions for how a world ends and how one begins.

8. Eighteen Cycles of Rising and Falling Lifespan, and the Great Kalpa's Arithmetic

The abiding-after-formation kalpa is itself built from twenty medium kalpas: the first is pure decrease, from immeasurable lifespan down to just ten years; the next eighteen each cycle from ten years up to eighty thousand and back down again; and the twentieth is pure increase, back up to eighty thousand. Formation, destruction, and emptiness each borrow this same twenty-kalpa duration by analogy, and all four together — eighty medium kalpas in total — make one great kalpa, the basic unit in which three "incalculable" kalpas of a bodhisattva's practice are eventually reckoned.

Chinese Source

Decreasing from immeasurable down to a mere ten years constitutes the first of the twenty medium abiding-kalpas. After this, eighteen further kalpas each involve an increase and a decrease: that is, from ten years increasing up to eighty thousand, then from eighty thousand decreasing back down to ten years — this constitutes the second medium kalpa. Each of the following seventeen kalpas follows this same pattern. After these eighteen, lifespan again increases from ten years up to a full eighty thousand years — this is called the twentieth kalpa. In no kalpa does the increase ever exceed eighty thousand years, and in no kalpa does the decrease ever go below ten years. Within these eighteen kalpas, each single increase-and-decrease pair is of equal duration; since the first kalpa involves only a decrease and the last involves only an increase, all twenty kalpas are of equal duration. This entire sequence is called, altogether, the "abiding-after-formation" kalpa. As for the remaining kalpas — formation, destruction, and the emptiness after destruction — none of these involve any twentyfold distinction of increase and decrease; however, since their duration matches that of the abiding kalpa, each of them is likewise reckoned as twenty medium kalpas, by analogy. Within the formation kalpa, the container world arises during its first medium kalpa, while sentient beings gradually come to abide during the remaining nineteen. Within the destruction kalpa, the container world is destroyed during its last medium kalpa, while sentient beings gradually depart during the preceding nineteen. In this way, formation, abiding, destruction, and emptiness, each twenty medium kalpas, accumulate to a total of eighty; all together, these eighty constitute the measure of one great kalpa. Among the four kinds of kalpa discussed above, which kind accumulates to make up these three incalculable kalpas? Accumulating the aforementioned great kalpas by tens, hundreds, thousands, and so on, one accumulates to the point of the three incalculable kalpas. But if they are called "incalculable," why does the text also speak of "three"? The term "incalculable" does not mean utterly beyond number in an absolute sense — it means merely "not countable" in the ordinary way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Defining a kalpa's "essential nature" as simply "the five aggregates" is a quietly important philosophical move: a kalpa is not a container that phenomena happen inside, nor a substance of its own kind, but just a name for a very long stretch of the ordinary five aggregates' own conditioned arising and passing — the same basic building blocks analyzed all the way back in fascicle 1, now applied at the largest possible timescale this cosmology recognizes. Even the eighty-medium-kalpa structure of a single great kalpa is, ultimately, nothing but aggregates continuing.

The resolution of "incalculable, yet three" is a useful piece of technical vocabulary in its own right: "incalculable" (asaṃkhyeya) turns out to be a specific, named number within an extended numeral system — vast beyond ordinary counting, but not literally infinite or indeterminate — which is precisely why the text can meaningfully speak of exactly three of them. The next section will introduce that numeral system directly.

9. Sixty Numbers to "Incalculable" — and Eight Already Lost

The Vimokṣa Sūtra defines "incalculable" (asaṃkhyeya) precisely, as the sixtieth step in a chain where each named number is exactly ten times the last, starting from a bare "one." The treatise candidly admits that eight of these sixty names have already been lost by the time of this citation — and clarifies that "incalculable" itself is not literally beyond counting, only beyond ordinary counting, which is exactly why the sūtras can still meaningfully speak of a bodhisattva needing "three" of them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Honest flag on the named numbers themselves: many of these transliterated terms (mata, tiṭibha, nimbararāja, and several others) do not have a Sanskrit reconstruction we can confidently verify, and some numeral lists preserved in other sources for this same sūtra differ in their exact sequence or count. Rather than assign confident modern equivalents to terms we cannot independently confirm, we have kept the Chinese transliterations exactly as given, preserving the "each is ten times the last" structure the passage itself states clearly and repeatedly — which is the actually load-bearing content here, regardless of any single term's precise historical value.

The text's own admission that "the remaining eight have already been lost from transmission" is a remarkable moment of textual self-awareness: this passage is quoting a sūtra whose complete list Vasubandhu himself did not have full access to, and rather than silently patching the gap or omitting the admission, the treatise states the loss outright. It is worth noting this alongside the fascicle's own numeral-reading correction earlier — this text, across multiple points, treats gaps and errors in transmission as things to disclose rather than paper over.

10. Why Would Anyone Choose Three Incalculable Kalpas of Suffering?

If nirvana can be reached by other means, why would a bodhisattva choose three incalculable kalpas of grueling practice at all? The treatise's answer is unflinchingly direct: because rescuing all sentient beings from suffering requires the full capacity only complete awakening provides, and because a bodhisattva's compassion has been so thoroughly habituated that benefiting others simply is his own benefit — the same way an ordinary being's habituated self-attachment makes bearing suffering for the sake of "self" feel entirely natural.

Chinese Source

Supreme awakening is exceedingly difficult to attain; without extensive vows and practice, there is no way it could be accomplished. A bodhisattva must pass through three incalculable kalpas cultivating the great provisions of merit and wisdom — the six perfections — undergoing many hundreds of thousands of ascetic practices, before finally realizing supreme, perfect awakening; hence it is necessary to make such a long-term vow. If nirvana could also be attained through other means, what use is there in cultivating so many ascetic practices over such a long time merely for the sake of awakening? It is for the sake of benefiting and bringing happiness to all sentient beings that one seeks awakening and makes this long-term vow: "How may I acquire the great capacity needed to rescue all sentient beings from the torrent of suffering?" — it is for this reason that one forsakes the path to mere nirvana and seeks supreme awakening instead. What benefit is there to oneself in rescuing other sentient beings? A bodhisattva, in rescuing other beings, is fulfilling his own compassionate intention — hence rescuing others is itself his own benefit. Who would believe that a bodhisattva actually does such a thing? For someone who harbors only self-interest and lacks great compassion, this would indeed be difficult to believe of such a being. But for one who has no intention of self-benefit and possesses great compassion, this is not at all difficult to believe of such a being. Just as there are those who, through long habituation, lack compassion altogether, and, even though it brings them no benefit, take pleasure in harming others — this is something the whole world already knows and accepts. In just the same way, a bodhisattva, through long habituation in compassion, takes pleasure in benefiting others even though it brings him no benefit — why should this not be believed? Moreover, just as sentient beings, through the power of repeated habituation, fail to understand that conditioned phenomena are without self, and, grasping them as a self, give rise to attachment and craving, and, for this very reason, willingly bear great suffering — this too is something the wise already know and accept. In just the same way, a bodhisattva, through the power of repeated habituation, abandons self-love and increases his attachment and affection toward others, and, for this very reason, willingly bears great suffering — why should this not be believed? Moreover, on account of a difference in spiritual disposition, this kind of aspiration arises: taking others' suffering as one's own suffering, and others' happiness as one's own happiness, rather than taking one's own suffering and happiness as what defines one's own suffering and happiness — seeing no difference between benefiting another and having some separate benefit of one's own. On the basis of this meaning, there is a verse that states: "The lesser person diligently strives, ever seeking his own happiness; the middling person seeks the cessation of suffering, since happiness is not something to rely on where suffering remains; the greater person ever diligently seeks his own suffering and others' peace and happiness, and the permanent cessation of others' suffering — because he takes others as himself."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The argument's central move is a genuinely elegant piece of moral psychology: rather than treating the bodhisattva's selflessness as a mysterious exception to ordinary motivation, the treatise normalizes it by analogy to something already unremarkable — habituated self-attachment. Ordinary beings, through long habituation, treat an impersonal, constructed "self" as worth suffering for; a bodhisattva, through equally long habituation in the opposite direction, treats others as worth suffering for in exactly the same psychologically real way. Compassion this thorough isn't presented as superhuman, but as self-attachment's own structure, redirected.

The closing three-tier verse (lesser/middling/greater person) is a compact summary of this tradition's entire soteriological landscape: the ordinary being seeking pleasure, the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha seeking personal liberation from suffering, and the bodhisattva seeking others' welfare even at the cost of his own suffering — three progressively less self-centered relationships to one's own suffering and happiness, culminating in a stance that treats "other" and "self" as no longer meaningfully distinct categories.

11. Why Buddhas Only Appear While Lifespan Is Falling

Buddhas appear only during one specific window: while human lifespan declines from eighty thousand years down to a hundred, never during a period of increase and never once it falls below a hundred. Rising lifespans make beings too satisfied with saṃsāra to seek liberation, while lifespans below a hundred bring the five defilements — of lifespan, of the kalpa itself, of affliction, of views, and of sentient beings — to such an extreme that beings become impossible to teach at all.

Chinese Source

Through the next two defilements, wholesome qualities become diminished, since beings indulge in sensual pleasure or in self-mortification, thereby damaging the wholesome conduct of both householders and renunciants. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The logic here cuts against a natural intuition: one might expect a buddha to appear precisely when conditions are best, during a period of rising lifespan and flourishing, rather than during decline. Instead, the treatise argues the opposite — rising fortune breeds complacency that makes beings resistant to teaching, while decline (up to a point) makes suffering visible enough to motivate the search for liberation. But decline can go too far: past a certain threshold of degradation, the same five defilements that once made beings receptive make them simply too damaged to teach at all. A buddha's appearance, on this account, requires a surprisingly narrow window — degraded enough to seek a way out, not so degraded as to be beyond reach.

The five defilements are grouped in pairs by what they damage: lifespan and kalpa-defilement erode external conditions (lifespan itself, material livelihood), affliction and view-defilement erode wholesome conduct (through either indulgence or misguided asceticism), and the defilement of sentient beings erodes the person directly — body, strength, and mental faculties all together. This gives the five-part list an internal structure rather than being simply five independent items.

12. Two Kinds of Pratyekabuddha, and Why Neither One Teaches

Unlike buddhas, pratyekabuddhas appear regardless of whether lifespan is rising or falling, and come in two kinds: the group-dwelling type, former disciples who reach the final fruit without a teacher present — illustrated by a startling story of five hundred ascetics converted in an instant by a devoted monkey imitating a pratyekabuddha's demeanor — and the rhinoceros-horn-like type, solitary by necessity, who need a full hundred kalpas of preparation. Both are fully capable of teaching yet deliberately choose not to, for reasons the treatise takes seriously enough to enumerate one by one.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

However, there are two distinct kinds of pratyekabuddha: first, the group-dwelling type; second, the rhinoceros-horn-like type. The group-dwelling pratyekabuddha was formerly a śrāvaka, and, upon attaining the superior fruit, comes to be called "solitarily supreme." There is a further view that such a one was formerly an ordinary being who had previously cultivated the śrāvaka's aids to penetration, and now, awakening to the path entirely on his own, attains the name "solitarily supreme." As is told in the Itivṛttaka: in a certain mountain place there once dwelt, in total, five hundred ascetic non-Buddhist sages. As for the rhinoceros-horn-like type, this refers to one who necessarily dwells entirely alone. Among the two kinds of pratyekabuddha, the rhinoceros-horn-like type requires a full hundred great kalpas of cultivating the provisions for awakening before finally becoming a rhinoceros-horn-like pratyekabuddha. By "pratyekabuddha" is meant one who, in his present existence, awakens to the path entirely on his own, apart from receiving any teacher's instruction — since he is capable of disciplining himself, but not of disciplining others. Why is it said that a pratyekabuddha does not discipline others? It is not that he lacks the capacity to expound the true Dharma, since he too has attained unobstructed understanding, and is moreover able to remember the sacred teachings he has heard proclaimed by buddhas in the past. Nor can it be said that he lacks compassion, since he does manifest supernormal powers in order to gather sentient beings. Nor can it be said that there are no beings receptive to the Dharma at that time, since, even then, there remain sentient beings capable of generating the mundane path counteracting desire. Although this is so, it is because of his own past habituation — having taken little delight in resolute understanding through verbal explanation, he has no wish to teach. Moreover, he recognizes how difficult it is for sentient beings to accept a profound teaching, since, having long flowed downstream with the current, it is difficult to make them turn against that current. Moreover, in order to avoid gathering a following, he does not expound the true Dharma to others, fearing the noise and confusion that a following would bring.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The monkey story is doing careful conceptual work, not just providing color: the text explicitly draws the inference that, since the five hundred sages still needed to undertake ascetic practices after their sudden realization, they cannot have been noble ones all along — meaning the monkey's mimicked deportment alone was enough to trigger a genuine breakthrough in ordinary beings who had never received any actual teaching, only the visual impression of realized conduct. This is a striking claim about how little verbal instruction awakening strictly requires, at least for this specific type of pratyekabuddha.

The list of reasons a pratyekabuddha declines to teach is worth reading as a set of successively harder-to-dismiss objections, each answered in turn: not incapacity, not lack of compassion, not absence of receptive students — but a settled personal disposition (little taste for verbal exposition), a realistic assessment of how hard it is to redirect beings long habituated to saṃsāra, and a preference for solitude over the social complications a following would bring. None of these reasons impugn the pratyekabuddha's attainment; they simply describe a specific, deliberate choice about how to use it.

13. Wheel-Turning Kings: Four Wheels, Four Domains, and Never Two at Once

Wheel-turning kings appear across the same broad window as pratyekabuddhas, but never once lifespan drops below eighty thousand years. Four kinds exist, ranked by their wheel's material and the territory it grants — gold rules all four continents, silver three, copper two, iron just one — with the golden wheel's appearance described in vivid ceremonial detail. Like a buddha, a wheel-turning king never coexists with another of his kind anywhere in the world at the same time.

Chinese Source

輪王出世為在何時？幾種幾俱何威何相？頌曰：輪王八萬上，金銀銅鐵輪，一二三四洲，逆次獨如佛。他迎自往伏，諍陣勝無害，相不正圓明，故與佛非等。論曰：從此洲人壽無量歲乃至八萬歲，有轉輪王生。減八萬時，有情富樂壽量損減、眾惡漸盛，非大人器，故無輪王。此王由輪旋轉應導威伏一切，名轉輪王。《施設足》中說有四種，金、銀、銅、鐵輪應別故。如其次第勝、上、中、下，逆次能王領一二三四洲，謂鐵輪王王一洲界，銅輪王二，銀輪王三，若金輪王王四洲界。契經就勝但說金輪，故契經言：若王生在剎帝利種紹灑頂位，於十五日受齋戒時，沐浴首身受勝齋戒，昇高臺殿臣僚輔翼，東方忽有金輪寶現。其輪千輻具足轂輞，眾相圓淨如巧匠成，舒妙光明來應王所。此王定是轉金輪王。轉餘輪王應知亦爾。輪王如佛，無二俱生，故契經言：無處無位非前非後有二如來、應、正等覺出現於世，有處有位唯一如來。如說如來，輪王亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

When does a wheel-turning king appear in the world? How many kinds are there, how many appear together, what is their majestic power, and what is their appearance? Verse: wheel-kings appear from eighty thousand years of lifespan upward; their wheels are gold, silver, copper, or iron; they rule one, two, three, or four continents respectively, in descending order; they are unique, like a buddha. Some come to them, others they go and subdue themselves; they are victorious over the ranks of battle without causing harm; their bodily marks are not as perfectly round and radiant as a buddha's — hence they are not equal to a buddha. Prose: from the time this continent's people's lifespan is immeasurable down to eighty thousand years, wheel-turning kings arise. Once lifespan declines below eighty thousand, sentient beings' wealth, happiness, and lifespan all diminish, and evils gradually intensify — this is no longer a vessel fit for a great being, hence no wheel-king arises. This king, by means of a wheel that revolves, is able to guide and subdue everyone through his majestic power; hence he is called a "wheel-turning king." The Prajñaptipāda states there are four kinds, since the gold, silver, copper, and iron wheels must be distinguished. In descending order of superiority — superior, upper, middle, lower — each rules, respectively, one, two, three, or four continents: the iron-wheel king rules one continent's territory; the copper-wheel king, two; the silver-wheel king, three; and the gold-wheel king rules the territory of all four continents. The sūtras, speaking in terms of the superior case, mention only the gold wheel; hence a sūtra states: "If a king is born into the kṣatriya caste and succeeds to the anointed crown, then, on the fifteenth day, while observing the fast and precepts, having bathed his head and body and undertaken the excellent fast and precepts, he ascends a high tower-hall attended by his ministers and retinue — suddenly, from the east, a precious golden wheel appears. This king is then certainly a wheel-turning king who turns the golden wheel." The other wheel-turning kings should be understood in the same way. A wheel-turning king, like a buddha, never arises two at once; hence a sūtra states: "There is no place, no position, neither before nor after, where two tathāgatas, worthy ones, perfectly and fully awakened, appear in the world at once — where there is a place, where there is a position, there is only ever a single tathāgata." Just as is said of the tathāgata, so too is it with the wheel-turning king.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The verse's closing comparison to a buddha is doing precise, almost technical work: a wheel-turning king is explicitly described as resembling a buddha in several specific respects — supreme worldly authority, uniqueness (never two at once), even similar auspicious bodily marks — while being just as explicitly denied full equality, since his marks are "not as perfectly round and radiant." This is a calibrated near-miss comparison, common in Buddhist literature's treatment of the wheel-turning king as the closest worldly analogue to a buddha's spiritual authority, without ever collapsing the distinction between temporal and awakened sovereignty.

The "never two at once" rule, applied identically to both buddhas and wheel-turning kings, suggests this cosmology treats supreme authority — whether spiritual or temporal — as inherently singular within a given world-system, rather than something that could exist in plural, competing instances. Two such figures existing simultaneously would apparently violate some deeper structural principle this text does not fully spell out here, but clearly takes as a fixed law, stated by direct citation rather than argued for independently.

14. One Buddha for the Whole Universe, or One Per World-System?

The "no two buddhas at once" rule raises a genuinely hard question: does it mean a single buddha suffices for every world-system everywhere, or only that no single world-system ever has two at the same time? The first position insists on exactly one buddha for the entire universe, backed by a striking exchange between the Buddha and Śāriputra — and reconciles an apparently contradictory sūtra passage about mastery over "this trichiliocosm" by reading it as describing the reach of the Buddha's power only when he exerts special effort, not the limit of his actual domain.

Chinese Source

應審思擇，此唯一言，為據一三千、為約一切界？有說：餘界定無佛生。所以者何？勿薄伽梵功能有礙，唯一世尊普於十方能教化故。若有一處一佛於中無教化能，餘亦應爾。又世尊告舍利子言：設復有人來至汝所，問言：頗有梵志沙門，正於今時與喬答摩氏平等平等得無上覺耶？汝得彼問當云何答？時舍利子白世尊言：我得彼問當如是答：今時無有梵志沙門得無上菩提與我世尊等。所以然者，我從世尊親聞親持，無處無位非前非後有二如來應正等覺出現於世，有處有位唯一如來。若爾，何緣《梵王經》說：我今於此三千大千諸世界中得自在轉？彼有密意。密意者何？謂若世尊不起加行，唯能觀此三千大千；若時世尊發起加行，無邊世界皆佛眼境。天耳通等例此應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should carefully consider and distinguish: does this statement of "only one" refer to a single trichiliocosm, or does it encompass the entirety of all world-systems everywhere? There is a view: that no buddha whatsoever arises in any other world-system. Why is this? Lest the Bhagavān's own capacity be thought limited — there is only a single World-Honored One, who is universally able to teach and transform beings throughout the ten directions. If there were some single place where a single buddha lacked the capacity to teach and transform, beings elsewhere would have to be the same. Moreover, the World-Honored One said to Śāriputra: "Suppose someone were to come to you and ask: 'Is there, at this very time, any brahmin or ascetic equal, entirely equal, to Gautama in having attained supreme awakening?' How would you answer, having been asked this?" At that time, Śāriputra addressed the World-Honored One, saying: "Were I asked this, I would answer as follows: at this time, there is no brahmin or ascetic who has attained supreme awakening equal to my own World-Honored One. Why is this so? Because I have personally heard and personally retained from the World-Honored One himself: there is no place, no position, neither before nor after, where two tathāgatas, worthy ones, perfectly and fully awakened, appear in the world at once; where there is a place, where there is a position, there is only ever a single tathāgata." If this is so, then why does the Brahmajāla Sūtra state: "I now exercise sovereign mastery throughout this entire trichiliocosm of world-systems"? This statement carries a hidden intent. What is this hidden intent? It means: if the World-Honored One does not exert any special effort, he is able to perceive only this one trichiliocosm; but when the World-Honored One does exert such effort, boundless world-systems all fall within the range of his buddha-eye. His divine ear and other such powers should be understood by the same principle.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Śāriputra exchange is a carefully chosen piece of evidence: rather than the Buddha simply asserting his own uniqueness, the text has his foremost disciple in wisdom rehearse the argument in the form of a hypothetical challenge and answer, giving the claim the weight of something the tradition's most rigorous questioner has already tested and accepted. The "hidden intent" reading of the Brahmajāla Sūtra's grander claim is a good example of this treatise's general method: rather than let two authoritative-seeming scriptural statements sit in open contradiction, it finds a reading — here, ordinary perception versus perception under deliberate effort — that lets both stand as true without forcing either to be rejected.

15. The Winning View: Many Buddhas, Many Worlds, Never Two in One

A rival school presses the practical case: with countless bodhisattvas simultaneously completing their path, and a single buddha unable to fully accomplish even one world-system's worth of buddha-activity, boundless world-systems must each have their own buddha appearing at once. This view wins the argument here — but the treatise still owes an explanation for why, even so, a single world-system never gets two buddhas at the same time, and offers four distinct reasons: it would serve no purpose, it would violate a bodhisattva's own founding vow, it would dilute the reverence a sole teacher commands, and it would remove the urgency that drives beings to practice while they still can.

Chinese Source

有餘部師說：餘世界亦別有佛出現世間。所以者何？有多菩薩現俱修習菩提資糧，一界一時可無多佛，多界多佛何理能遮？故無邊界中有無邊佛現。若唯一佛，設住一劫時，尚不遍為一世界佛事，況同人壽能益無邊。然諸有情居無邊界，時處根性差別無邊，佛應遍觀此有情類。如是時處應見世尊，佛便應機現通說法，令其過失未生不生，諸有已生能令斷滅，令其功德未生得生，諸有已生能令圓滿。如何一佛此事頓成？是故同時定有多佛。然彼所引，無處無位非前非後有二如來出於世等。應共思擇，此言為說一界多界？若說多界，則轉輪王餘世界中亦應非有，以說如佛遮俱生故。若許輪王餘界別有，如何不許別界佛耶？佛出世間具吉祥福，多界多佛何過而遮？謂多界中諸佛俱現，便能饒益無量有情，令得增上生及決定勝道。若爾，何故一世界中無二如來俱時出現？以無用故。謂一界中一佛，足能饒益一切。又願力故，謂諸如來為菩薩時先發誓願：願我當在無救無依盲闇界中成等正覺，利益安樂一切有情，為救為依為眼為導。又令敬重故，謂一界中唯有一如來便深敬重。又令速行故，謂令如是知一切智尊甚為難遇，彼所立教應速修行，勿般涅槃或往餘處，便令我等無救無依。故一界中無二佛現。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a further school's view: that other world-systems, too, each separately have their own buddhas appearing in the world. Why is this? There are many bodhisattvas presently cultivating the provisions for awakening together — while it may be that a single world-system, at a single time, cannot have multiple buddhas, by what principle could this forbid many world-systems from each having their own buddha? Hence, throughout boundless world-systems, boundless buddhas appear. If there were only a single buddha, then, even were he to remain for an entire kalpa, he still could not fully accomplish a buddha's work even for a single world-system, let alone benefit boundless world-systems within an ordinary human lifespan. Yet sentient beings dwell throughout boundless world-systems, differing boundlessly in time, place, and capacity — a buddha ought to be able to perceive all these kinds of beings everywhere. Wherever and whenever the World-Honored One ought to be seen, the buddha should respond to that occasion, manifesting supernormal powers and teaching the Dharma, causing faults not yet arisen to not arise, and faults already arisen to be eliminated, causing virtues not yet arisen to arise, and virtues already arisen to become complete. How could a single buddha accomplish all of this at once? Therefore, there must definitely be multiple buddhas existing at the same time. As for the passage cited — "there is no place, no position, neither before nor after, where two tathāgatas appear in the world," and so on — this should be jointly considered: does this statement refer to a single world-system, or to multiple world-systems? If it refers to multiple world-systems, then wheel-turning kings, too, ought not to exist in other world-systems simultaneously, since the statement is said to forbid simultaneous arising in the same way for both buddhas and wheel-kings. But if it is accepted that wheel-kings do exist separately in other world-systems, why should it not likewise be accepted that buddhas exist separately in other world-systems? A buddha's appearance in the world is endowed with auspicious merit — what fault would there be in multiple world-systems each having their own buddha, such that this should be forbidden? That is: when buddhas appear together across multiple world-systems, they are able to benefit immeasurable sentient beings, causing them to attain higher rebirth and the path of definite excellence. If this is so, then why is it that, within a single world-system, two tathāgatas never appear simultaneously? Because it would serve no purpose. That is: within a single world-system, a single buddha alone is fully sufficient to benefit everyone. Moreover, it is due to the power of a prior vow: for all tathāgatas, while still bodhisattvas, first made this vow: "May I attain perfect, complete awakening precisely within a world-system that has no rescuer, no refuge, blind and dark, benefiting and bringing peace and happiness to all sentient beings, becoming their rescuer, their refuge, their eye, their guide." Moreover, it is so that beings will hold him in deep reverence — that is, when there is only a single tathāgata within a single world-system, this alone brings about deep reverence. Moreover, it is so as to spur beings toward swift practice — that is, causing them to understand that encountering a lord of omniscience such as this is exceedingly rare, so that they should swiftly practice the teaching he has established, lest he enter final nirvana or depart to another place, leaving them without rescue or refuge. For these reasons, within a single world-system, two buddhas never appear.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The decisive move in this rival argument is the wheel-turning-king parallel: since the "no two at once" scriptural language is applied to buddhas and wheel-kings in exactly the same phrasing, and everyone already accepts that wheel-kings can exist simultaneously in different world-systems, consistency demands the same reading for buddhas. This is a strong argument specifically because it holds the earlier side to its own citation rather than introducing new evidence — if the language doesn't actually rule out multiple wheel-kings across worlds, it can't be read as ruling out multiple buddhas across worlds either.

The bodhisattva's founding vow deserves particular attention: it specifically commits to awakening "in a world-system that has no rescuer, no refuge" — meaning each buddha's world-system is, by definition and by that buddha's own prior choice, one that currently lacks any other living buddha. This reframes "only one buddha per world" not as an external cosmic law imposed on buddhas, but as something built into what every bodhisattva actually vows to do — a subtle but important difference in how the constraint is grounded.

16. Four Ways to Conquer, Seven Treasures, and Thirty-Two Marks

Each of the four wheel-turning kings subdues foreign lands differently — the gold-wheel king is simply welcomed, the silver-wheel king wins submission by proximity alone, the copper-wheel king through a contest of proclaimed virtue, and the iron-wheel king through an actual show of force, though never through real harm. Every wheel-king's reign brings seven treasures, including living ones karmically synchronized to arise alongside him, and a body marked with the same thirty-two auspicious signs as a buddha's — though never quite as perfectly formed.

Chinese Source

如是所說四種輪王，威定諸方亦有差別。謂金輪者，諸小國王各自來迎作如是請：我等國土寬廣豐饒，安隱富樂多諸人眾。唯願天尊親垂教勅，我等皆是天尊翼從。若銀輪王自往彼土，威嚴近至彼方臣伏。若銅輪王至彼國已，宣威競德彼方推勝。若鐵輪王亦至彼國，現威列陣剋勝便止。一切輪王皆無傷害，令伏得勝已，各安其所居，勸化令修十善業道，故輪王死定得生天。經說輪王出現於世，便有七寶出現世間。其七者何？一者輪寶、二者象寶、三者馬寶、四者珠寶、五者女寶、六者主藏臣寶、七者主兵臣寶。象等五寶有情數攝，如何他業生他有情，非他有情從他業起？然由先造互相屬業，於中若一稟自業生，餘亦俱時乘自業起。如是所說諸轉輪王，非唯有七寶與餘王別，亦有三十二大士相殊。若爾，輪王與佛何異？佛大士相處正明圓，王相不然，故有差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these four kinds of wheel-turning kings just described, there are also distinctions in how their majestic power subdues the various regions. In the case of the gold-wheel king: the various minor kings each come of their own accord to welcome him, making this request: "Our territories are vast and fertile, peaceful and prosperous, with many people. We ask only that Your Divine Majesty grant us instruction and command in person — we are all your loyal followers." In the case of the silver-wheel king: he goes to that land himself, and, by the mere proximity of his majestic authority, that region submits as his vassal. In the case of the copper-wheel king: upon arriving in that country, he proclaims his authority and competes in virtue, and that region concedes his superiority. In the case of the iron-wheel king: he likewise arrives in that country, displays his might by arraying his troops, and, having achieved victory, stops there. In every case, no wheel-turning king ever causes harm; once he has subdued a region and achieved victory, he settles each people in their own dwelling, and encourages them to cultivate the ten wholesome courses of action — hence a wheel-turning king, upon death, is certain to be reborn in heaven. The sūtras state that when a wheel-turning king appears in the world, seven treasures likewise appear in the world along with him. What are these seven? First, the wheel-treasure; second, the elephant-treasure; third, the horse-treasure; fourth, the jewel-treasure; fifth, the woman-treasure; sixth, the treasurer-minister-treasure; seventh, the general-treasure. The elephant and the other four living treasures are included among sentient beings — how, then, could another being's karma give rise to a different sentient being, rather than that other sentient being arising from their own karma? It is rather that, through karma created previously that mutually binds them together, when one of them arises through the ripening of his own karma, the others likewise arise at the same time through the ripening of their own respective karma. As for these wheel-turning kings just described, they are distinguished from other kings not only by possessing the seven treasures, but also by possessing the thirty-two marks of a great being. If this is so, then what is the difference between a wheel-turning king and a buddha? A buddha's marks of a great being are perfectly clear and complete, while a king's marks are not so — hence there is a distinction between them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The explanation for why the elephant, horse, and other living treasures happen to appear alongside a wheel-king is a careful piece of causal bookkeeping applied to a genuine puzzle: since this tradition's karma is strictly individual (my karma cannot directly cause your birth), the simultaneous appearance of king and treasures cannot be one being's karma producing another. The solution — independently self-caused births that happen to be karmically synchronized through prior mutually-binding karma — preserves the individuality of karmic causation while still explaining the coincidence, the same careful move already seen in this fascicle's earlier treatment of causally "linked" but distinct phenomena.

The thirty-two-marks comparison closes with a characteristically precise, almost anticlimactic distinction: a wheel-king and a buddha share the very same set of thirty-two auspicious bodily marks, differing only in degree of perfection, not in kind. This is consistent with the earlier verse's own claim that a wheel-king's marks are "not as perfectly round and radiant" as a buddha's — the two figures are cosmologically adjacent, not categorically different, which is presumably part of why this tradition finds the wheel-turning king such a natural and recurring point of comparison for a buddha's own worldly authority.

17. The Beginning of Humanity: From Radiant Flight to Desire

At the very beginning of the kalpa, people were radiant, sexless, self-luminous beings who flew through the air, indistinguishable from form-realm gods — until a sweet earthly essence appeared, and one person's craving for its taste set off a chain reaction. Each time people grew addicted to one food source, it vanished and a coarser one replaced it, and each replacement made human bodies heavier and grosser, until digestion itself required the body to develop sexual organs — and, from the mere sight of these new differences between bodies, desire itself was born.

Chinese Source

劫初人眾為有王無？頌曰：劫初如色天，後漸增貪味，由墮貯賊起，為防雇守田。論曰：劫初時人皆如色界，故契經說：劫初時人有色意成，支體圓滿諸根無缺，形色端嚴身帶光明，騰空自在飲食喜樂長壽久住。有如是類地味漸生，其味甘美其香欝馥。時有一人稟性耽味，嗅香起愛取甞便食，餘人隨學競取食之。爾時方名初受段食。資段食故身漸堅重，光明隱沒黑闇便生，日月眾星從茲出現。由漸耽味地味便隱，從斯復有地皮餅生。競耽食之，地餅復隱，爾時復有林藤出現。競耽食故，林藤復隱，有非耕種香稻自生，眾共取之以充所食。此食麁故，殘穢在身，為欲蠲除便生二道，因斯遂有男女根生。由二根殊形相亦異，宿習力故便相瞻視，因此遂生非理作意，欲貪鬼魅惑亂身心，失意猖狂行非梵行。人中欲鬼初發此時。

Ru-Yi Translation

At the very beginning of the kalpa, did people have a king or not? Verse: at the kalpa's beginning, people were like the gods of the form realm; afterward, craving for flavor gradually increased; from this, falling, storage, and theft arose; a ruler was hired to guard against this and protect the fields. Prose: at the very beginning of the kalpa, people were all like beings of the form realm; hence a sūtra states: at the kalpa's beginning, people possessed form and were mind-made, their limbs perfectly complete, their faculties without any deficiency, their appearance dignified and upright, their bodies emitting light, soaring through the air at will, joyful in eating and drinking, long-lived and abiding for a long duration. At this time, an earthly essence gradually arose, sweet in flavor and richly fragrant. There was then a certain person whose nature was inclined toward flavor, who, smelling its fragrance, gave rise to craving, took it up, and tasted it, then ate it; the other people, following this example, competed to gather and eat it as well. It was only at this time that people first began to receive edible food. Because they now depended on edible food, their bodies gradually became coarse and heavy, their inner light faded, and darkness arose — it was from this point that the sun, moon, and stars first appeared. As people gradually became addicted to this earthly essence, it in turn disappeared, and, from this, an earth-crust cake arose in its place. Competing to feed on this, the earth-cake likewise disappeared, and, at that time, forest vines appeared. Competing to feed on these as well, the forest vines then disappeared, and non-cultivated fragrant rice grew spontaneously, which everyone gathered together to eat. Because this food was coarse, residual waste remained in the body, and, in order to expel it, the two lower orifices arose — and from this, the male and female sexual organs came to be. Because these two organs differed in form and appearance, through the power of past habituation, people began to gaze at one another, and, from this, improper mental attention arose; desire and craving, like demonic possession, threw body and mind into confusion, and, losing their composure, people engaged in wild, non-celibate conduct. It was at this time that desire-demons first arose among human beings.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The narrative's underlying logic is a strict chain of decline, each link caused by the one before it: craving for taste causes bodies to solidify and darken, which causes the sun and moon to become necessary for light, which correlates with progressively coarser foods each vanishing as soon as beings become attached to it, which finally forces the body to develop organs of elimination, which incidentally produces the physical difference that desire itself first fastens onto. Nothing here is simply "given" as a starting condition — even something as basic as having a sexed body is presented as a late-arriving consequence of craving, not a fact about human nature that needed no explanation.

18. From Hoarding to Kingship: A Social Contract Origin Story

One lazy person's decision to store rice for later triggers the entire architecture of civilization in rapid succession: hoarding creates a sense of "mine," which exhausts the land and forces the division of fields, which creates possessiveness and theft, which forces the community to hire a protector — the first king, paid one-sixth of everyone's harvest — while others who withdraw from household life altogether become the first brahmins. Only later does a greedy king's mismanagement force the invention of punishment, killing, and finally the first lie, told by a criminal desperate to conceal his crime.

Chinese Source

爾時諸人隨食早晚隨取香稻無所貯積，後時有人稟性嬾惰，長取香稻貯擬後食，餘人隨學漸多停貯。由此於稻生我所心，各縱貪情多收無厭，故隨收處無復再生，遂共分田慮防遠盡。於己田分生悋護心，於他分田有懷侵奪，劫盜過起始於此時。為欲遮防共聚詳議，銓量眾內一有德人，各以所收六分之一，雇令防護封為田主，因斯故立剎帝利名。大眾欽承恩流率土，故復名大三末多王，自後諸王此王為首。時人或有情厭居家，樂在空閑精修戒行，因斯故得婆羅門名。後時有王貪悋財物，不能均給國土人民，故貧匱人多行賊事。王為禁止行輕重罰，為殺害業始於此時。時有罪人心怖刑罰，覆藏其過異想發言，虛誑語生此時為首。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, people simply gathered fragrant rice as needed, morning or evening, without storing any of it. Later, there was a person of lazy disposition who gathered a large quantity of fragrant rice and stored it in anticipation of future meals; the other people, following this example, gradually began storing more and more as well. Because of this, a sense of "mine" arose with regard to the rice; each person indulged their own greed without limit, gathering more than they needed, and, as a result, the places they had harvested no longer produced new growth. They therefore divided the fields among themselves and took precautions against future scarcity. With regard to their own allotted field, a sense of stinginess and protectiveness arose; with regard to others' allotted fields, a desire to encroach and seize arose — the faults of robbery and theft first began at this time. In order to prevent this, people gathered together to deliberate in detail, and, having assessed and selected a single virtuous person from among the group, each contributed one-sixth of their own harvest and hired this person to provide protection, appointing him as lord of the fields — and it was from this that the name "kṣatriya" was established. The multitude, in gratitude, submitted to his benevolent rule over the land, and so he was further given the name "Mahāsammata," the "Great Elect"; all later kings take this king as their first ancestor. At that time, there were also certain people who grew weary of household life and took pleasure in solitude, diligently cultivating precepts and conduct — and it was from this that the name "brahmin" was obtained. Later still, there was a king who, out of greed and stinginess for wealth, was unable to distribute it evenly among the people of his land, so that impoverished people increasingly resorted to banditry. The king, in order to prohibit this, carried out punishments both light and severe — and it was at this time that the act of killing first began. At that time, there were guilty persons who, fearing punishment, concealed their offenses and spoke deceptively with false intent — and it was at this time that false speech first began, as the foremost of such offenses.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuine social-contract narrative, strikingly close in structure to political philosophy written millennia later: a protector is not born into authority but hired and paid by common consent, precisely to solve a problem (theft) that private property itself created — kingship here is explicitly a remedy for a prior social breakdown, not a natural or divinely ordained institution. The etymology offered for "kṣatriya" (from a term associated with fields and their protection) and "Mahāsammata" ("the Great Elect," i.e., "consented to by the great [assembly]") both encode this same idea directly into the names themselves.

The moral sequence is worth tracing as a chain rather than a list: theft is caused by possessiveness, possessiveness is caused by scarcity from over-harvesting, scarcity is caused by hoarding, and hoarding is caused by simple laziness — a single person's minor character flaw is presented as the root cause of an entire cascade ending in organized government, capital punishment, and eventually lying itself. Nothing in this narrative treats any social institution, however seemingly fundamental, as having existed from the beginning; each one is a late, contingent patch applied on top of the last problem it caused.

19. The Three Small Calamities: Sword, Plague, and Famine

Tracing the same decline forward from the very first lie, unwholesome conduct compounds generation after generation until lifespan bottoms out at ten years, triggering three small calamities rooted in the same two flaws already identified — addiction to fine food and laziness. Hatred grows so intense that any object in hand becomes a weapon; poison-borne plague spreads unchecked; and the gods and nāgas, provoked by human misconduct, withhold rain until famine forces the starving to desperate measures.

Chinese Source

於劫減位有小三災。其相云何？頌曰：業道增壽減，至十三災現，刀疾飢如次，七日月年止。論曰：從諸有情起虛誑語，諸惡業道後後轉增，故此洲人壽量漸減，乃至極十小三災現。故諸災患二法為本：一耽美食、二性嬾惰。此小三災中劫末起。三災者，一刀兵、二疾疫、三飢饉。謂中劫末十歲時，人為非法貪染污相續，不平等愛映蔽其心，邪法縈纏瞋毒增上，相見便起猛利害心。如今獵師見野禽獸，隨手所執皆成利刀，各逞兇狂互相殘害。又中劫末十歲時人，由具如前諸過失故，非人吐毒疾疫流行，遇輒命終難可救療。又中劫末十歲時人，亦具如前諸過失故，天龍忿責不降甘雨，由是世間久遭飢饉，既無支濟多分命終。是故說言，由飢饉故便有聚集白骨運籌。

Ru-Yi Translation

At the point of a kalpa's decline, there are three small calamities. What are their characteristics? Verse: as unwholesome courses of action increase and lifespan decreases, reaching ten years, the three calamities appear — sword, sickness, and famine, in that order, lasting seven days, seven months, and seven years respectively. Prose: from the point when sentient beings first gave rise to false speech, unwholesome courses of action progressively increased thereafter, causing this continent's people's lifespan to gradually decrease, until it reaches as little as ten years, at which point the three small calamities appear. Hence the root of all these calamities lies in two things: first, addiction to fine food; second, a lazy disposition. These three small calamities arise at the very end of a medium kalpa. The three calamities are: first, the sword; second, plague; third, famine. That is: at the time when, at the end of a medium kalpa, lifespan is ten years, people, continuously defiled by unlawful craving, their minds obscured by inequitable desire, entangled in wrong views, their hatred and malice intensified, the moment they see one another give rise to fierce, murderous intent — just as, nowadays, when a hunter sees wild birds and beasts, whatever he holds in his hand turns into a sharp blade; in just this way, people, each giving free rein to their savage madness, slaughter one another. Moreover, at this same time, because people are possessed of all the aforementioned faults, non-human beings spread poisonous, epidemic disease, and whoever is struck by it dies almost at once, difficult to cure or save. Moreover, at this same time, also because people are possessed of all the aforementioned faults, the gods and nāgas, in their anger and reproach, withhold sweet rain, so that the world suffers prolonged famine, and, having no means of sustenance, most people die. It is for this reason that it is said: because of famine, there comes to be "the gathering of bones" and "the counting with tallies."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The root-cause diagnosis is worth noting for its consistency with everything already established in this fascicle: the same two flaws blamed for humanity's original fall from radiant grace — craving for fine flavor and simple laziness — are named again here as the ultimate source of civilization's worst catastrophes. The narrative treats moral decline as a single continuous thread running from the first taste of the earth's sweetness all the way to a ten-year lifespan and mutual slaughter, not as a series of unrelated historical accidents.

20. Simple Remedies for Unthinkable Suffering

The famine's grim vocabulary gets a precise gloss — "gathering" refers both to the dead piled together and to seeds set aside for a future no one is sure will come, while "counting with tallies" describes food rationed grain by grain within starving households. Against all this, the treatise offers strikingly modest remedies: a single day of not killing, a single piece of fruit or handful of food offered to the monastic community, each said to guarantee complete immunity from one of the three calamities in a future life. Each calamity, once it strikes, runs a fixed course — seven days, seven months, or seven years — before ending on its own.

Chinese Source

由二種因名有聚集：一人聚集，謂彼時人由極飢羸聚集而死。二種聚集，謂彼時人為益後人，輟其所食置於小篋擬為種子。故飢饉時名有聚集。言有白骨亦由二因：一彼時人身形枯燥，命終未久白骨便現。二彼時人飢饉所逼，聚集白骨煎汁飲之。有運籌言亦二因故，一由糧少行籌食之，謂一家中從長至幼，隨籌至日得少麁飡。二謂以籌挑故場蘊，得少穀粒多用水煎，分共飲之以濟餘命。然有至教說治彼方，謂若有能一晝一夜持不殺戒，於未來生決定不逢刀兵災起。若能以一訶梨怛雞，起殷淨心奉施僧眾，於當來世決定不逢疾疫災起。若有能以一摶之食，起殷淨心奉施僧眾，於當來世決定不逢飢饉災起。此三災起各經幾時？刀兵災起極唯七日，疾疫災起七月七日，飢饉七年七月七日，度此便止。人壽漸增，東西二洲有似災起，謂瞋增盛身力羸劣，數加飢渴，北洲總無。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Gathering" is so called for two reasons: first, "gathering of people" — that is, at that time, people, extremely famished and weak, gathered together and died in heaps. Second, "gathering of seeds" — that is, at that time, people, wishing to benefit future generations, set aside a portion of their own food and stored it in small boxes as seed for the future. Hence a time of famine is called a time of "gathering." The term "white bones" likewise has two reasons: first, at that time, people's bodies became so desiccated that, not long after death, white bones would already be exposed. Second, at that time, people, driven by the pressure of famine, would gather up white bones, boil them, and drink the resulting broth. The term "counting with tallies" likewise has two reasons: first, because provisions were so scarce, food was rationed by tally-count — that is, within a single household, from the eldest to the youngest, each would receive, according to their tally, only a small, coarse portion of food per day. Second, tallies were used to scrape together grain-husks from the threshing floor, obtaining only a small quantity of grain, which was then mostly boiled with water, and the resulting gruel shared among all to sustain what life remained. There is, however, a definitive teaching describing a remedy for these calamities: if one is able to observe the precept against killing for a single day and night, one is certain, in a future life, never to encounter the calamity of the sword. If one is able, with a sincere and pure mind, to offer even a single myrobalan fruit to the monastic community, one is certain, in a future life, never to encounter the calamity of plague. If one is able, with a sincere and pure mind, to offer even a single handful of food to the monastic community, one is certain, in a future life, never to encounter the calamity of famine. How long does each of these three calamities last? The calamity of the sword lasts, at most, only seven days; the calamity of plague, seven months and seven days; the calamity of famine, seven years, seven months, and seven days — after which it ceases. As human lifespan gradually increases again, the eastern and western continents experience calamities resembling these — that is, an increase in hatred, physical weakness, and repeated hunger and thirst — but the northern continent experiences none of these at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The disproportion between remedy and calamity is the whole point, not an oversight: a single day's restraint from killing, or a single piece of fruit given in sincerity, is presented as sufficient to guarantee total exemption from a catastrophe that otherwise kills off nearly the entire population of a continent over seven years. This is a recurring feature of karmic reasoning in this literature — the moral weight of an action is measured by the sincerity and category of the act, not by any proportionality to the scale of its eventual karmic consequence.

Uttarakuru's complete exemption from even the milder, "resembling" calamities that strike the eastern and western continents during a period of rising lifespan is consistent with everything already established about that continent throughout fascicles 11 and 12 — fixed thousand-year lifespans, no private property structure described, and now, immunity even from the diminished echo of catastrophes that touch every other inhabited part of the cosmos.

21. Three Great Calamities: Fire, Water, and Wind

Beyond the small calamities that punctuate a single medium kalpa, three great calamities periodically destroy the container world itself down to its very last atom: fire, manifesting through seven suns; water, through endless torrential rain; and wind, through colliding gales. Each corresponds to a specific dhyāna as its uppermost reach, following the same "internal calamity" logic already established for the original fire that ended the previous great kalpa — and the pattern repeats in a fixed sequence: seven fire calamities, then one water, repeated seven times over, and finally a single wind calamity.

Chinese Source

前說火災焚燒世界，餘災亦爾，如應當知。何者為餘？今當具辯。頌曰：三災火水風，上三定為頂，如次內災等，四無不動故，然彼器非常，情俱生滅故，要七火一水，七水火後風。論曰：此大三災逼有情類，令捨下地集上天中。初火災興由七日現，次水災起由雨霖淫，後風災生由風相擊。此三災力壞器世間，乃至極微亦無餘在。

Ru-Yi Translation

It was earlier stated that fire destroys the world by burning it; the remaining calamities should likewise be understood accordingly. What are these remaining calamities? This should now be discussed in full. Verse: the three calamities are fire, water, and wind; the peak of each corresponds to the upper three dhyānas as their limit; in the same order, these correspond to the equivalent internal calamities; the fourth dhyāna alone has none of these, being unmoving. Yet even that container world is not permanent, since it arises and perishes together with sentient beings. The cycle proceeds thus: seven fire calamities, then one water, repeated seven times over, and afterward, a wind calamity follows. Prose: these three great calamities press upon sentient beings, forcing them to abandon the lower grounds and gather in the higher heavens. First, the fire calamity arises, manifesting through seven suns; next, the water calamity arises, through torrential, prolonged rain; last, the wind calamity arises, through winds violently colliding with one another. The power of these three calamities destroys the container world, down to the point where not even a single atom remains.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The claim that destruction reaches "down to the point where not even a single atom remains" is about to become the flashpoint for an extended polemic against non-Buddhist atomist schools, who held that atoms themselves are eternal and therefore must survive even total cosmic destruction. The treatise's own position — total, genuinely complete annihilation with nothing left over, not even at the smallest possible scale — is stated here plainly, before being defended at length against that rival view in what follows.

22. Do Any Atoms Survive to "Seed" the Next World?

Non-Buddhist atomists insist some atoms must survive even total cosmic destruction, on the grounds that later, coarser phenomena could otherwise never arise again without a seed to produce them. The treatise counters that karmically-produced wind already serves this seeding function without requiring anything to have literally endured — and presses the point further by denying that a "seed" is ever the direct cause of a sprout in the first place: sprouts arise from their own finer constituent parts, all the way down to atoms, with the seed doing nothing more than gathering the right atoms into place.

Chinese Source

一類外道執極微常，彼謂爾時餘極微在。何緣彼執猶有餘極微？勿後麁事生無種子故。豈不前說，由諸有情業所生風能為種子。或此即以前災頂風為緣、引生風為種子。又化地部契經中言：風從他方飄種來此。雖爾，不許芽等生時是種等因親所引起。若爾，芽等從何而生？從自分生。如是自分復從自分，展轉乃至最細有分從極微生。於芽等生中，種等有何力？除能引集芽等極微，種等更無生芽等力。

Ru-Yi Translation

A certain group of non-Buddhists holds that atoms are permanent, maintaining that, even at the time of total cosmic destruction, some atoms still remain. Why do they insist that some atoms must remain? Lest later, coarser phenomena arise without any seed to produce them. But has it not already been stated that a wind, produced by the karma of sentient beings, is able to serve as that seed? Or, alternatively, it is precisely the wind at the peak of the preceding calamity that serves as a condition drawing forth and giving rise to the next wind, which then serves as the seed. Moreover, the Mahīśāsaka school's sūtra states: wind carries seeds here from other regions. Even granting this, it is not accepted that, when a sprout and the like arise, this wind or seed is the direct cause that gives rise to them. If this is so, then from what do sprouts and the like actually arise? They arise from their own constituent parts. These constituent parts, in turn, arise from their own further parts, and so on, until, at the very finest level, the smallest possible parts arise from atoms. In the arising of a sprout and the like, then, what power does the "seed" actually have? Apart from being able to draw together and gather the atoms that produce the sprout, the seed has no further power to actually produce the sprout itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a subtle causal move worth spelling out: the treatise doesn't need to deny that seeds matter to plant growth — it only needs to relocate exactly what work a "seed" does. A seed doesn't directly manufacture a sprout the way a potter shapes clay; it merely draws together and arranges the actual producing causes (progressively finer parts, ultimately atoms) into the configuration that becomes a sprout. This lets the treatise accept ordinary talk of seeds causing sprouts while denying the atomist's stronger claim that some literal, numerically identical matter must persist across a total cosmic destruction to make later growth possible at all — freshly arisen atoms, drawn together by a karmically-produced wind, do the same job just as well.

23. Is Cloth Something More Than Its Threads? A Debate Against Substance-Realism

The rival school presses further: substances, unlike mere qualities, can only arise from causes of the very same kind — thread must come from thread-like substance, not atoms. The treatise's response is a sustained nominalist argument: "cloth" and "branch" are not separate substances over and above their threads and vine-material differently arranged, no more than a marching column of ants is some separate entity beyond the ants themselves. Through several rounds of objection — about how a single glance can register a whole piece of cloth, about color and pattern, about a single fire's light affecting near and far differently — the treatise defends aggregated atoms as sufficient to explain everything the opposing view needs a mysterious unified "possessor of parts" to explain, before finally declining to pursue the polemic any further.

Chinese Source

何緣定作如是執耶？從異類生定不應理。不應何理？應無定故。功能定故，無不定失。如聲熟變等，從異類定生。德法有殊，實法不爾。現見實法唯從同類生，如藤生枝及縷生衣等。此非應理，非理者何？引不極成為能立故。今此所引何不極成？非許藤枝縷衣別故。即藤縷合，安布不同得枝衣名，如蟻行等。云何知爾？一縷合中曾不得衣，唯得縷故。有誰為障令不得衣？若一縷中無全衣轉，則應一縷上有衣分無衣。應許全衣唯集諸分，非更別有有分名衣。又如何知衣分異縷？若謂衣要待多所依合，於唯多經合應亦得衣。或應畢竟無得衣理，中及餘邊不對根故。若謂漸次皆可對根，則應眼身唯得諸分，不應說彼得有分衣故，即於諸分漸次了別，總起有分覺，如旋火輪。謂若離縷異色類業，衣色等三不可得故。若錦衣上色等屬衣，則應許實從異類起，一一縷色等無種種異故。或於一分無異色等，邊應不見衣，由彼顯衣故。或即彼分應見異色等，以衣必有異色等相故。彼許有分體唯是一，而有種種色類業殊，審有如斯甚為靈異。又於一火光明界中，遠近不同燒照有異，觸色差別應不得成。各別極微雖越根境，而共聚集可現根證，如彼所宗合能生果，或如眼等合能發識。又如瞖目視散髮時，若多相隣彼則能見，一一遠住便無見能。極微對根理亦應爾。又即於色等，立極微名故。色等壞時極微亦壞，極微實攝，色等德收，異體不應定俱時滅。此二體別，理必不然。以審觀時非離色等有別地等，故非體別。又彼宗中自許地等眼身所取，寧異色觸？又燒毛㲲紅花等時，彼覺則無，故毛等覺但緣色等差別而起，熟變生時形量等故。猶如行伍記識瓶盆，若不觀形不記識故。誰當採錄愚類狂言？故對彼宗廣諍應止。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why do they insist on holding this position so definitely? Because they hold that an effect arising from a cause of a different kind is definitely unreasonable. Unreasonable in what respect? Because there would then be no fixed determination of causal relationships. Since causal function is itself fixed, no fault of indeterminacy follows. Sound, ripening-transformation, and the like are indeed accepted as arising from a different kind of cause — but this holds only for qualities, which are of a distinct category; it does not hold for substances. It is directly observed that substances arise only from a cause of the same kind — as when a vine gives rise to a branch, or thread gives rise to cloth. This is not a reasonable claim. Why is it unreasonable? Because it cites something not yet established as though it were already established, in order to prove its point. What, in what has just been cited, is not yet established? It is not accepted that a vine and its branch, or thread and cloth, are actually distinct entities. It is precisely the union of vine-material or thread, differently arranged, that comes to be called "branch" or "cloth" — just as with a marching column of ants. How is this known to be so? Because within a single thread's union, cloth is never obtained — only thread is obtained. What, then, obstructs it from becoming cloth? If a complete cloth cannot arise within a single thread, then it should be accepted that, upon a single thread, there is merely a part of cloth, not complete cloth. It should be accepted that the complete cloth is simply the collection of all its parts — there is no further, separate "possessor-of-parts" entity, over and above the parts, that is called "cloth." Moreover, how is it known that a part of cloth is different from a thread itself? If it is held that cloth requires the union of many supporting threads, then a union of merely many warp-threads alone ought to also produce cloth. Or else, it would follow that cloth could never be obtained at all, since the middle and the remaining edges of a piece of cloth are not, each on their own, directly confronting a sense-faculty. If it is held that each part can, in succession, come to confront the sense-faculty, then it should follow that the eye and body only ever apprehend the individual parts, and it should not be said that they apprehend a "possessor-of-parts" — rather, it is precisely through successively discerning each part in turn that an overall cognition of a "possessor-of-parts" arises, just as with a whirling firebrand. That is: apart from the thread's own distinct color, category, and action, cloth's color and the other two categories cannot be found as anything separate. If the color and so forth found on a piece of brocade belong to the cloth itself, then it should be accepted that a substance arises from a cause of a different category — since no single individual thread's color, by itself, displays this variegated pattern. Or else, at a single part where there is no distinctive color and so on, one ought not to see the cloth at all there, on the grounds that only certain colored parts reveal the cloth. Or else, that very same part ought nevertheless to display distinctive color, since cloth, being genuinely one substance, must necessarily possess a uniform color and so forth throughout. They accept that the possessor-of-parts' essential nature is a single, unified thing, and yet has all kinds of varying colors, categories, and actions — upon careful examination, a position as strange as this is truly remarkable indeed. Moreover, within the domain of a single fire's light, its burning and illuminating effects differ depending on distance — if the object were genuinely a single undivided substance, this difference in tactile and visual effect ought not to be possible at all. Although each individual atom, by itself, is beyond the range of sense-faculties, when they gather together in aggregation, they become directly evident to sense-perception — just as, on your own school's own principle, a combination of causes is able to produce an effect, or just as the eye and other faculties, in combination, are able to give rise to consciousness. It is just like how someone with a cataract-clouded eye, looking at scattered strands of hair, is able to see them when many are clustered closely together, but has no capacity to see them when each is far apart — the principle of atoms confronting a sense-faculty should be understood in just the same way. Moreover, it is precisely with reference to visible form and the like that the name "atom" is established — hence, when visible form and the like are destroyed, the atom too is destroyed. On the opponent's own scheme, the atom would be classed under substance, while visible form and the like would be classed under quality — and two things of genuinely distinct essential nature ought not to necessarily perish together at the very same moment. This shows that these two supposedly distinct categories cannot, in principle, actually be distinct substances at all. Upon careful examination, there is no separate "earth" and so forth apart from visible form and the rest — hence they are not distinct in essential nature. Moreover, within that very school's own system, it is itself accepted that "earth" and the like are apprehended by the eye and the body faculties — how, then, could it be different from visible form and tangible object? Moreover, when wool, felt, red flowers, and the like are burned, the cognition of them as such simply ceases to occur — hence the cognition of "wool" and the like arises only in dependence on distinctions among visible form and the rest, since, when a substance undergoes ripening-transformation, its shape and dimensions also change accordingly. It is just like recognizing a marching formation, or a jar, or a basin — one fails to recognize them at all if one does not observe their shape. Who, indeed, would bother to record and take seriously such foolish, deluded talk? Hence the extensive polemic against that school's doctrine should stop here.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Honest flag: this passage argues against a specific rival school's substance/quality/action ontology (almost certainly Vaiśeṣika-style categories: dravya, guṇa, karma) without ever naming the school or fully spelling out its terminology, assuming a reader already familiar with the framework being attacked. We have translated "德法" and "實法" as "quality" and "substance" based on standard Sarvāstivāda-versus-Vaiśeṣika polemics elsewhere in this tradition's literature, but the passage itself never defines these terms explicitly, so this identification should be read as our own informed inference rather than something the text states outright.

The core move throughout is a consistent mereological nominalism: wherever the opposing view posits a unified "whole" (cloth, a possessor-of-parts) as something over and above its arranged parts, the treatise insists the whole simply is the parts in a particular arrangement, with no further entity to explain — the same reductive impulse already seen in this tradition's analysis of the self as nothing over and above the five aggregates, now extended to physical composite objects generally. The whirling-firebrand and cataract-clouded-eye similes both make the same point from different angles: a single, apparently unified perceptual experience (a circle of fire, a visible cluster of hairs, a piece of cloth) can arise from successive or aggregated contact with many discrete, individually imperceptible parts, without requiring any additional unifying substance to bind them together.
