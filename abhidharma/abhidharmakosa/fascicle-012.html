From One Atom to Eighty Kalpas

This page carries the opening of fascicle 12, continuing Chapter 3's fifth installment (分別世品第三之五, the Loka-nirdeśa's fifth part) by supplying the units the previous fascicle's measurements were already built on: the smallest possible unit of form (a single atom), of name (a single syllable), and of time (a single instant), the full sevenfold chain of distance from an atom up to a yojana, the parallel chain of time from an instant up to a year, and the vast cycles of cosmic destruction and formation — measured in kalpas — that a single great kalpa is built from.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 12 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 12 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. The Three Smallest Units: Atom, Syllable, Instant

Before the previous fascicle's measurements in yojanas and years can be fully grounded, the treatise supplies the smallest possible unit each is built from: a single indivisible atom for form, a single syllable for a name, and a single instant for time — defining that instant, in turn, as either the span in which a dharma first attains its own existence, or the time it takes a moving object to cross one atom's width.

Chinese Source

如是已約踰繕那等辯器世間身量差別。約年等辯壽量有殊，二量不同未說應說。此二建立無不依名，前二及名未詳極少，今應先辯三極少量。頌曰：極微字剎那，色名時極少。論曰：分析諸色至一極微，故一極微為色極少。如是分析諸名及時至一字剎那，為名時極少。一字名者，如說瞿名。何等名為一剎那量？眾緣和合法得自體頃，或有動法行度一極微。對法諸師說：如壯士一疾彈指頃六十五剎那，如是名為一剎那量。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus far, bodily size differences within the container world have been discussed in terms of yojanas and the like. Lifespan differences, discussed in terms of years and the like, remain to be discussed, since the two kinds of measurement are not the same. Neither of these two systems of measurement can be established without reference to units of designation — and the smallest possible units of both designation and time have not yet been made clear. It is fitting, therefore, to first discuss the three smallest quantities. Verse: the atom, the syllable, and the instant are the smallest units of form, name, and time, respectively. Prose: analyzing all form down to a single indivisible particle, this single particle is the smallest unit of form. Likewise, analyzing all names and all time down to a single syllable or a single instant, these are the smallest units of name and time. By "a one-syllable name" is meant something like the word "cow." What, then, constitutes the measure of a single instant? It is the span of time in which a dharma produced by the coming-together of its assembled conditions attains its own essential nature, or, alternatively, the time it takes a moving object to traverse a single indivisible particle's width. The Ābhidharmikas say: it is the span of time in which a vigorous, able-bodied man snaps his fingers once, swiftly, divided into sixty-five instants — this is what is called the measure of a single instant.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two definitions of an "instant" offered here are subtly different in kind: the first defines it functionally, by reference to a dharma's own coming-into-being, while the second defines it physically, by reference to motion across a fixed minimal distance — and the Ābhidharmikas' snap-of-the-fingers figure (sixty-five instants) is offered as a third, purely empirical anchor point, translating an abstract minimal unit into something a reader can actually imagine happening in their own hand.

2. From One Atom to One Yojana: A Sevenfold Chain

Every distance measurement in this cosmology, all the way up to the yojana, turns out to rest on a single repeated rule: each unit is exactly seven times the one before it, climbing from an atom through motes of metal-dust, water-dust, rabbit-hair-dust, sheep-hair-dust, ox-hair-dust, and ordinary drifting dust, up through a louse-egg, a louse, a grain of barley, and a finger-joint, before switching to the more familiar units of finger, cubit, bow-length, krośa, and finally yojana.

Chinese Source

已知三極少，前二量云何？今且辯前踰繕那等。頌曰：極微微金水，兔羊牛隙塵，蟣虱麥指節，後後增七倍，二十四指肘，四肘為弓量，五百俱盧舍，此八踰繕那。論曰：極微為初，指節為後，應知後後皆七倍增。謂七極微為一微量，積微至七為一金塵，積七金塵為水塵量，水塵積至七為一兔毛塵，積七兔毛塵為羊毛塵量，積羊毛塵七為一牛毛塵，積七牛毛塵為隙遊塵量，隙塵七為蟣，七蟣為一虱，七虱為穬麥，七麥為指節，三節為一指。世所極成，是故於頌中不別分別。二十四指橫布為肘，竪積四肘為弓謂尋，竪積五百弓為一俱盧舍，一俱盧舍許是從村至阿練若中間道量。說八俱盧舍為一踰繕那。如是已說踰繕那等。

Ru-Yi Translation

The three smallest units are now known — what, then, of the first two larger measurements? Let us now discuss distance measures such as the yojana. Verse: atom, particle, metal-dust, water-dust, rabbit-hair-dust, sheep-hair-dust, ox-hair-dust, gap-dust, louse-egg, louse, barley grain, finger-joint — each subsequent unit increases sevenfold; twenty-four finger-widths make a cubit, four cubits make a bow-length, five hundred bow-lengths make a krośa, and eight of these make a yojana. Prose: beginning with the atom and ending with the finger-joint, each subsequent unit should be understood to increase sevenfold over the last. That is: seven atoms make one particle; accumulating particles to seven makes one metal-dust mote; accumulating seven metal-dust motes makes one water-dust mote; accumulating water-dust to seven makes one rabbit-hair-dust mote; accumulating seven rabbit-hair-dust motes makes one sheep-hair-dust mote; accumulating seven sheep-hair-dust motes makes one ox-hair-dust mote; accumulating seven ox-hair-dust motes makes one gap-dust mote; seven gap-dust motes make one louse-egg; seven louse-eggs make one louse; seven lice make one grain of barley; seven barley grains make one finger-joint; three finger-joints make one finger. The next several units are already commonly established in the world, and so are not separately distinguished in the verse: twenty-four finger-widths, laid side by side, make one cubit; four cubits, stacked vertically, make one bow-length, that is, a fathom; five hundred bow-lengths, stacked vertically, make one krośa — a krośa being, roughly, the distance along the road from a village to a forest hermitage. Eight krośas are said to make one yojana. Distance measures such as the yojana have thus been discussed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chain's naming convention shifts partway through in a telling way: the first eleven steps (atom through finger-joint) are given exotic, constructed names — units nobody actually uses, built purely to bridge the gap between the imperceptibly small and the humanly graspable — while the final several steps (finger, cubit, bow-length, krośa, yojana) are explicitly described as units "already commonly established in the world," ordinary measures any reader would already know. The verse only bothers to specify the sevenfold rule for the invented half of the chain, since the familiar half needs no such justification — a small but clear signal of where technical Abhidharma vocabulary ends and everyday convention begins.

3. From One Instant to One Year, and Why Nights Shorten Twice a Season

Time builds up from the instant the same way distance builds up from the atom: 120 instants make a tatkṣaṇa, 60 of those make a lava, 30 make a muhūrta, 30 make a day-and-night, 30 days make a month, and 12 months make a year — with a precise rule specifying that, within each of the year's three seasons, night measurably shortens during the second half of every one-and-a-half-month stretch.

Chinese Source

今當辯後年等量別。頌曰：百二十剎那，為怛剎那量，臘縛此六十，此三十須臾，此三十晝夜，三十晝夜月，十二月為年，於中半減夜。論曰：剎那百二十為一怛剎那，六十怛剎那為一臘縛，三十臘縛為一牟呼栗多，三十牟呼栗多為一晝夜。此晝夜有時增、有時減、有時等。三十晝夜為一月，總十二月為一年。於一年中分為三際，謂寒、熱、雨各有四月。十二月中六月減夜，以一年內夜總減六。云何如是？故有頌言：寒熱雨際中，一月半已度，於所餘半月，智者知夜減。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now the remaining measures, such as the year, should be discussed. Verse: one hundred twenty instants make one tatkṣaṇa; sixty of these make one lava; thirty of these make one muhūrta; thirty of these make one day-and-night; thirty days-and-nights make a month; twelve months make a year; within this, night decreases by half. Prose: one hundred twenty instants make one tatkṣaṇa; sixty tatkṣaṇas make one lava; thirty lavas make one muhūrta; thirty muhūrtas make one day-and-night. This day-and-night is sometimes lengthening, sometimes shortening, and sometimes equal. Thirty days-and-nights make one month; twelve months in total make one year. Within a single year, it is divided into three seasons — cold, hot, and rainy — each of four months. Among these twelve months, night decreases across six of them, so that, within a single year, night's total decrease amounts to six portions. How is this so? Hence there is a verse that states: "Within the cold, hot, and rainy seasons, once a month and a half has passed, during the remaining half-month, the wise know that night decreases."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This ties directly back to fascicle 11's own account of Jambudvīpa's shifting day-and-night length, now formalized into a precise seasonal rule: night shortens specifically in the second half of a month-and-a-half stretch, within each of the three four-month seasons, rather than gradually and evenly across the whole year. The abstract time-unit chain built in this section — instant to tatkṣaṇa to lava to muhūrta — exists largely to make a rule like this one statable with precision, the same way the distance chain in the previous section existed to make yojana-scale cosmology statable in the first place.

4. Four Kinds of Kalpa, and How Destruction Begins in Hell

The treatise now turns from measuring moments to measuring entire cosmic ages: destruction, formation, the medium kalpa that structures both, and the great kalpa built from eighty of these. A destruction kalpa begins, with almost bureaucratic precision, the moment hell-beings simply stop being reborn there — any being whose karma still demands hellish suffering is instead redirected to a hell in some other, not-yet-destroyed world-system, until this one's hells stand entirely empty.

Chinese Source

如是已辯剎那至年。劫量不同，今次當辯。頌曰：應知有四劫，謂壞成中大，壞從獄不生，至外器都盡，成劫從風起，至地獄初生，中劫從無量，減至壽唯十，次增減十八，後增至八萬，如是成已住，名中二十劫，成壞壞已空，時皆等住劫，八十中大劫，大劫三無數。論曰：言壞劫者，謂從地獄有情不復生至外器都盡。壞有二種：一趣壞、二界壞。復有二種：一有情壞、二外器壞。謂此世間過於二十中劫住已，從此復有等住二十，壞劫便至。若時地獄有情命終無復新生，為壞劫始。乃至地獄無一有情，爾時名為地獄已壞。諸有地獄定受業者，業力引置他方獄中，由此准知傍生鬼趣。然各先壞本處住者，人天雜居者與人天同壞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Time from the instant up to the year has thus been discussed. Kalpa-measures differ from these, and should now be discussed next. Verse: it should be known that there are four kinds of kalpa — namely, destruction, formation, the medium kalpa, and the great kalpa. The destruction runs from when hell-beings cease to be reborn until the container world is entirely exhausted. The formation runs from when the wind first arises until hell-beings are first reborn. A medium kalpa runs from human lifespan being immeasurable, decreasing until lifespan is only ten years; then increasing and decreasing eighteen times in succession; then increasing once more up to eighty thousand years — this, together with abiding-after-formation, is called twenty medium kalpas. Formation, destruction, and the emptiness after destruction each likewise equal the abiding-kalpa in duration. Eighty medium kalpas make one great kalpa; three great kalpas, incalculable in number, are needed for a bodhisattva's practice. Prose: as for the "destruction kalpa," this refers to the period from when hell-beings cease to be reborn until the container world is entirely exhausted. Destruction is of two kinds: destruction of the destinies, and destruction of the realm itself. There are, further, two kinds: destruction of sentient beings, and destruction of the container world. That is: once this world-system has abided for twenty medium kalpas, there then follows a further twenty medium kalpas of equal duration, at the end of which the destruction kalpa arrives. When hell-beings, upon their deaths, are no longer reborn there anew, this marks the beginning of the destruction kalpa. Once there remains not a single being left in hell, this is called "hell already destroyed." Any beings whose karma definitely requires suffering in hell are, by the power of that karma, conveyed to hells in other world-systems instead — from this, the same should be understood, by analogy, for the animal and ghost destinies. However, each destiny is destroyed first in its own original dwelling-place; those destinies that dwell intermixed with humans and gods are destroyed together with the humans and gods in whose territory they dwell.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The redirection of still-karma-bound hell-beings to hells in other, not-yet-destroyed world-systems is a small but load-bearing piece of cosmological bookkeeping: it prevents a world-system's destruction from ever functioning as an accidental amnesty, letting beings escape suffering they still karmically owe simply because the particular hell they were in ran out of world to exist in. The system as a whole is designed so that karma always finds somewhere to ripen, even across the destruction of an entire universe.

5. A Single Word Empties a World: Destruction Spreads Upward

Once the lower destinies are destroyed, the pattern that empties the human and heavenly realms is genuinely remarkable: a single person, entirely without a teacher, spontaneously discovers the first dhyāna, and simply announcing how blissful it is causes everyone who hears to enter the same absorption and, upon death, be reborn in the Brahmā world — a chain reaction that empties an entire continent, then an entire heaven, then the whole desire realm, and finally, by the same mechanism one level up, the Brahmā world itself, until nothing remains but the empty container world.

Chinese Source

若時人趣此洲一人，無師法然得初靜慮，從靜慮起唱如是言：離生喜樂，甚樂甚靜。餘人聞已皆入靜慮，命終並得生梵世中。乃至此洲有情都盡，是名已壞贍部洲人。東西二洲例此應說。北洲命盡生欲界天，由彼無能入定離欲。乃至人趣無一有情，爾時名為人趣已壞。若時天趣四大王天隨一法然得初靜慮，乃至並得生梵世中。爾時彼天有情都盡，是名已壞大王眾天。餘五欲天例同此說。乃至欲界無一有情，名欲界中有情已壞。若時梵世隨一有情，無師法然得二靜慮，從彼定起唱如是言：定生喜樂，甚樂甚靜。餘天聞已皆入彼靜慮，命終並得生極光淨天。乃至梵世中有情都盡，如是名已壞有情世間。唯器世間空曠而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

When, at a certain time, a single person in this continent, without a teacher, spontaneously attains the first dhyāna, and, rising from that absorption, proclaims: "The joy and bliss born of seclusion are truly blissful, truly tranquil!" — the other people, hearing this, all likewise enter that same absorption, and, upon death, are all reborn in the Brahmā world. This continues until the beings of this continent are entirely exhausted; this is called "the people of Jambudvīpa already destroyed." The eastern and western continents should be explained by the same pattern. In the northern continent, upon death, beings are reborn in the desire-realm heavens instead, since those people are incapable of entering absorption or becoming free of desire. This continues until there remains not a single being in the human destiny; at that time, this is called "the human destiny already destroyed." When, at a certain time, among the gods, one of the Four Great Kings' heaven spontaneously attains the first dhyāna, and so on, until they too are all reborn in the Brahmā world — at that time, the beings of that heaven are entirely exhausted; this is called "the Heaven of the Four Great Kings already destroyed." The remaining five desire heavens should be explained by the same pattern. This continues until there remains not a single being in the desire realm; this is called "the sentient beings of the desire realm already destroyed." When, at a certain time, one being in the Brahmā world, without a teacher, spontaneously attains the second dhyāna, and, rising from that absorption, proclaims: "The joy and bliss born of concentration are truly blissful, truly tranquil!" — the other gods, hearing this, all likewise enter that same absorption, and, upon death, are all reborn in the Ābhāsvara Heaven. This continues until the beings of the Brahmā world are entirely exhausted; in this way, this is called "the world of sentient beings already destroyed." Only the container world remains, empty and vast, abiding on its own.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This mechanism is worth sitting with, since it is genuinely different from how the lower destinies were destroyed: hell, animal, and ghost beings simply stop being reborn, a passive cessation, but the human and heavenly realms are emptied through something closer to spontaneous spiritual contagion — one being's unprompted discovery of meditative bliss, described almost as an announcement ("truly blissful, truly tranquil!"), triggers everyone within earshot to follow. Destruction, at this level of the cosmos, is not something that happens to beings so much as something they are drawn toward once shown what lies past ordinary desire — an oddly hopeful mechanism for what is, from a bird's-eye view, the annihilation of an entire world.

The same pattern repeats one level up with a different verse of praise (concentration's bliss rather than seclusion's) and a different destination (Ābhāsvara rather than the Brahmā world), showing that this "contagious absorption" mechanism is not a one-time narrative device but a structural principle applied recursively at each tier of the cosmos that still contains beings capable of meditative attainment.

6. Seven Suns and a Fire That Climbs to Brahmā's Palace

With every sentient being gone, the empty container world must still be destroyed. Seven suns appear in succession, boiling the oceans dry and setting the mountains themselves ablaze, while wind-driven fire climbs relentlessly upward — but only ever consuming its own level before drawing forth the next level's fire in turn, all the way up to the Brahmā world, which burns to nothing but ash. With this, the destruction kalpa is complete.

Chinese Source

餘十方界一切有情，感此三千世界業盡，於此漸有七日輪現，諸海乾竭眾山洞然，洲渚三輪並從焚燎，風吹猛焰燒上天宮，乃至梵宮無遺灰燼。自地火焰燒自地宮，非他地災能壞他地，由相引起故作是言。下火風飄焚燒上地，謂欲界火猛焰上昇為緣引生色界火焰。餘災亦爾，如應當知。如是始從地獄漸減乃至器盡，總名壞劫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once the karma of all sentient beings throughout the ten directions that gave rise to this trichiliocosm has been exhausted, seven suns gradually appear in succession; all the seas dry up completely, the mountains burst into flame, and the continents together with the three wheels are all consumed by fire; the wind fans the fierce flames, burning up to the heavenly palaces above, until, reaching the Brahmā palace, nothing remains but ash. A given ground's own fire burns that same ground's own palaces — a calamity native to one ground cannot, by itself, destroy another ground; it is said to happen only because one ground's fire draws forth and gives rise to the next. The fire from below, driven by wind, spreads upward and burns the ground above — that is, the desire realm's fire, its fierce flames rising upward, serves as a condition that draws forth and gives rise to the form realm's own fire. The remaining calamities should likewise be understood accordingly. In this way, starting from the hells gradually diminishing all the way to the container world's complete exhaustion, this entire process is called, in total, the destruction kalpa.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rule that "a calamity native to one ground cannot, by itself, destroy another ground" is a careful piece of causal discipline applied even to an apocalypse: the fire doesn't simply spread because fire spreads, but because each level's fire specifically serves as a condition drawing forth the next level's own, distinct fire — form-realm fire is not literally the same flame as desire-realm fire grown large enough to reach it, but a new occurrence of fire native to that higher ground, triggered by the lower one. This is the same causal fastidiousness already seen throughout this text's treatment of ordinary causation, applied here at the scale of universal conflagration. The passing mention of "the remaining calamities" — water and wind, which destroy the world by other means in other cycles — signals that fire is only one of three ways a world-system can end, a topic the text flags for later without elaborating here.

7. Formation: The Same Process, Perfectly Reversed

After twenty medium kalpas of pure emptiness, a faint wind stirs and the whole process runs in exact reverse: the wind, water, and gold wheels rebuild themselves, the Brahmā world's palace is the very first thing to form, and the very first being to arrive descends from Ābhāsvara to become its king. From there, beings descend tier by tier — Brahmā's ministers, then retinue, down through the desire heavens and the four continents, finally reaching ghosts, animals, and hell — following the natural law that whatever was destroyed last must be the first thing to form again.

Chinese Source

所言成劫，謂從風起乃至地獄始有情生。謂此世間災所壞已，二十中劫唯有虛空，過此長時次應復有等住二十，成劫便至。一切有情業增上力，空中漸有微細風生，是器世間將成前相。風漸增盛，成立如前所說風輪水金輪等，然初成立大梵王宮乃至夜摩宮。後起風輪等，是謂成立外器世間。初一有情極光淨歿，生大梵處為大梵王。後諸有情亦從彼歿，有生梵輔、有生梵眾、有生他化自在天宮。漸漸下生，乃至人趣俱盧、牛貨、勝身、贍部，後生餓鬼、傍生、地獄。法爾後壞，必最初成。若初一有情生無間獄，二十中成劫應知已滿，此後復有二十中劫名成已住，次第而起，謂從風起造器世間，乃至後時有情漸住。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the "formation kalpa": this refers to the period from when the wind first arises until beings are first born in hell. That is: once this world has been destroyed by calamity, there follows twenty medium kalpas of pure empty space; once this long span has passed, a further twenty medium kalpas of equal duration follow, and the formation kalpa then arrives. Through the dominant power of all sentient beings' collective karma, a subtle, faint wind gradually arises within that empty space — this is the preliminary sign that the container world is about to form. The wind gradually grows and intensifies, forming the wind wheel, water wheel, and gold wheel, and the rest, exactly as described before; and then, first of all, the palace of the Great Brahmā King is established, followed in turn all the way down to the Yāma palace. Afterward, the wind wheel and the rest arise below — this is what is meant by "the container world being established." The very first being to descend is one who passes away from the Ābhāsvara Heaven and is reborn in the Great Brahmā's realm, becoming the Great Brahmā King. Afterward, other beings likewise pass away and are reborn — some in the Heaven of Brahmā's Ministers, some in the Heaven of Brahmā's Retinue, some in the Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations. Beings descend gradually, lower and lower, until they reach the human destinies of Uttarakuru, Aparagodānīya, Pūrvavideha, and Jambudvīpa, and afterward are reborn as hungry ghosts, animals, and hell-beings. It is a natural law that whatever is destroyed last must be what forms first. Once the very first being is reborn in Avīci hell, the twenty medium kalpas of the formation kalpa should be understood as complete. After this, there follows a further twenty medium kalpas called "abiding-after-formation," arising in sequence — that is, from when the wind first arises to construct the container world, up until beings gradually come to abide within it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The principle "whatever is destroyed last must be what forms first" gives the entire cosmic cycle a strict, almost mechanical symmetry: destruction proceeded upward from hell to the Brahmā world, so formation must proceed downward from the Brahmā world back to hell — the two halves of the cycle are exact mirror images of each other, not independent processes that merely happen to bookend one another. This is worth noticing as the same design instinct seen everywhere else in this fascicle's cosmology: a small number of rules, applied consistently and reversibly, generate the entire structure rather than requiring separate, unrelated descriptions for how a world ends and how one begins.

8. Eighteen Cycles of Rising and Falling Lifespan, and the Great Kalpa's Arithmetic

The abiding-after-formation kalpa is itself built from twenty medium kalpas: the first is pure decrease, from immeasurable lifespan down to just ten years; the next eighteen each cycle from ten years up to eighty thousand and back down again; and the twentieth is pure increase, back up to eighty thousand. Formation, destruction, and emptiness each borrow this same twenty-kalpa duration by analogy, and all four together — eighty medium kalpas in total — make one great kalpa, the basic unit in which three "incalculable" kalpas of a bodhisattva's practice are eventually reckoned.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

This continent's people's lifespan continues for an immeasurable time; only at the beginning of the abiding kalpa does lifespan begin to gradually decrease. Decreasing from immeasurable down to a mere ten years constitutes the first of the twenty medium abiding-kalpas. After this, eighteen further kalpas each involve an increase and a decrease: that is, from ten years increasing up to eighty thousand, then from eighty thousand decreasing back down to ten years — this constitutes the second medium kalpa. Each of the following seventeen kalpas follows this same pattern. After these eighteen, lifespan again increases from ten years up to a full eighty thousand years — this is called the twentieth kalpa. In no kalpa does the increase ever exceed eighty thousand years, and in no kalpa does the decrease ever go below ten years. Within these eighteen kalpas, each single increase-and-decrease pair is of equal duration; since the first kalpa involves only a decrease and the last involves only an increase, all twenty kalpas are of equal duration. This entire sequence is called, altogether, the "abiding-after-formation" kalpa. As for the remaining kalpas — formation, destruction, and the emptiness after destruction — none of these involve any twentyfold distinction of increase and decrease; however, since their duration matches that of the abiding kalpa, each of them is likewise reckoned as twenty medium kalpas, by analogy. Within the formation kalpa, the container world arises during its first medium kalpa, while sentient beings gradually come to abide during the remaining nineteen. Within the destruction kalpa, the container world is destroyed during its last medium kalpa, while sentient beings gradually depart during the preceding nineteen. In this way, formation, abiding, destruction, and emptiness, each twenty medium kalpas, accumulate to a total of eighty; all together, these eighty constitute the measure of one great kalpa. What is the essential nature of a kalpa? It is simply the five aggregates. The sūtras state that three incalculable kalpas of diligent practice are required to attain buddhahood. Among the four kinds of kalpa discussed above, which kind accumulates to make up these three incalculable kalpas? Accumulating the aforementioned great kalpas by tens, hundreds, thousands, and so on, one accumulates to the point of the three incalculable kalpas. But if they are called "incalculable," why does the text also speak of "three"? The term "incalculable" does not mean utterly beyond number in an absolute sense — it means merely "not countable" in the ordinary way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Defining a kalpa's "essential nature" as simply "the five aggregates" is a quietly important philosophical move: a kalpa is not a container that phenomena happen inside, nor a substance of its own kind, but just a name for a very long stretch of the ordinary five aggregates' own conditioned arising and passing — the same basic building blocks analyzed all the way back in fascicle 1, now applied at the largest possible timescale this cosmology recognizes. Even the eighty-medium-kalpa structure of a single great kalpa is, ultimately, nothing but aggregates continuing.

The resolution of "incalculable, yet three" is a useful piece of technical vocabulary in its own right: "incalculable" (asaṃkhyeya) turns out to be a specific, named number within an extended numeral system — vast beyond ordinary counting, but not literally infinite or indeterminate — which is precisely why the text can meaningfully speak of exactly three of them. The next section will introduce that numeral system directly.

9. Sixty Numbers to "Incalculable" — and Eight Already Lost

The Vimokṣa Sūtra defines "incalculable" (asaṃkhyeya) precisely, as the sixtieth step in a chain where each named number is exactly ten times the last, starting from a bare "one." The treatise candidly admits that eight of these sixty names have already been lost by the time of this citation — and clarifies that "incalculable" itself is not literally beyond counting, only beyond ordinary counting, which is exactly why the sūtras can still meaningfully speak of a bodhisattva needing "three" of them.

If one counts great kalpas up to this number, asaṃkhyeya, this is what is meant by an "incalculable" number of kalpas. Accumulating this incalculable quantity up to three of them is what the sūtras mean by "three incalculable kalpas." It is not that these numbers cannot be reckoned by any calculation whatsoever, or that they cannot be known through counting — hence it is entirely appropriate to still speak of "three incalculable kalpas."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Honest flag on the named numbers themselves: many of these transliterated terms (mata, tiṭibha, nimbararāja, and several others) do not have a Sanskrit reconstruction we can confidently verify, and some numeral lists preserved in other sources for this same sūtra differ in their exact sequence or count. Rather than assign confident modern equivalents to terms we cannot independently confirm, we have kept the Chinese transliterations exactly as given, preserving the "each is ten times the last" structure the passage itself states clearly and repeatedly — which is the actually load-bearing content here, regardless of any single term's precise historical value.

The text's own admission that "the remaining eight have already been lost from transmission" is a remarkable moment of textual self-awareness: this passage is quoting a sūtra whose complete list Vasubandhu himself did not have full access to, and rather than silently patching the gap or omitting the admission, the treatise states the loss outright. It is worth noting this alongside the fascicle's own numeral-reading correction earlier — this text, across multiple points, treats gaps and errors in transmission as things to disclose rather than paper over.

10. Why Would Anyone Choose Three Incalculable Kalpas of Suffering?

If nirvana can be reached by other means, why would a bodhisattva choose three incalculable kalpas of grueling practice at all? The treatise's answer is unflinchingly direct: because rescuing all sentient beings from suffering requires the full capacity only complete awakening provides, and because a bodhisattva's compassion has been so thoroughly habituated that benefiting others simply is his own benefit — the same way an ordinary being's habituated self-attachment makes bearing suffering for the sake of "self" feel entirely natural.

Chinese Source

何緣菩薩發願長時精進修行方期佛果？如何不許願長時修？無上菩提甚難可得，非多願行無容得成。菩薩要經三劫無數修大福德智慧資糧六波羅蜜多，多百千苦行方證無上正等菩提，是故定應發長時願。若餘方便亦得涅槃，何用為菩提久修多苦行？為欲利樂一切有情，故求菩提發長時願：云何令我具大堪能，於苦瀑流濟諸含識，故捨涅槃道求無上菩提。濟他有情於己何益？菩薩濟物遂己悲心，故以濟他即為己益。誰信菩薩有如是事？有懷潤己無大慈悲，於如是有情，此事實難信。無心潤己有大慈悲，於如是有情，此事非難信。如有久習無哀愍者，雖無益己而樂損他，世所同悉。如是菩薩久習慈悲，雖無利己而樂他益，如何不信？又如有情由數習力，於無我行不了有為，執以為我而生愛著，由此為因甘負眾苦，智者同悉。如是菩薩數習力故，捨自我愛增戀他心，由此為因甘負眾苦，如何不信？又由種姓異，有此志願起，以他苦為己苦、用他樂為己樂，不以自苦樂為己苦樂事，不見異益他而別有自益。依如是義故，有頌言：下士勤方便，恒求自身樂，中士求滅苦，非樂苦依故，上士恒勤求，自苦他安樂，及他苦永滅，以他為己故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why does a bodhisattva make the vow to practice diligently for such a long time in order to attain the fruit of buddhahood? How could one not accept the necessity of vowing to practice for so long? Supreme awakening is exceedingly difficult to attain; without extensive vows and practice, there is no way it could be accomplished. A bodhisattva must pass through three incalculable kalpas cultivating the great provisions of merit and wisdom — the six perfections — undergoing many hundreds of thousands of ascetic practices, before finally realizing supreme, perfect awakening; hence it is necessary to make such a long-term vow. If nirvana could also be attained through other means, what use is there in cultivating so many ascetic practices over such a long time merely for the sake of awakening? It is for the sake of benefiting and bringing happiness to all sentient beings that one seeks awakening and makes this long-term vow: "How may I acquire the great capacity needed to rescue all sentient beings from the torrent of suffering?" — it is for this reason that one forsakes the path to mere nirvana and seeks supreme awakening instead. What benefit is there to oneself in rescuing other sentient beings? A bodhisattva, in rescuing other beings, is fulfilling his own compassionate intention — hence rescuing others is itself his own benefit. Who would believe that a bodhisattva actually does such a thing? For someone who harbors only self-interest and lacks great compassion, this would indeed be difficult to believe of such a being. But for one who has no intention of self-benefit and possesses great compassion, this is not at all difficult to believe of such a being. Just as there are those who, through long habituation, lack compassion altogether, and, even though it brings them no benefit, take pleasure in harming others — this is something the whole world already knows and accepts. In just the same way, a bodhisattva, through long habituation in compassion, takes pleasure in benefiting others even though it brings him no benefit — why should this not be believed? Moreover, just as sentient beings, through the power of repeated habituation, fail to understand that conditioned phenomena are without self, and, grasping them as a self, give rise to attachment and craving, and, for this very reason, willingly bear great suffering — this too is something the wise already know and accept. In just the same way, a bodhisattva, through the power of repeated habituation, abandons self-love and increases his attachment and affection toward others, and, for this very reason, willingly bears great suffering — why should this not be believed? Moreover, on account of a difference in spiritual disposition, this kind of aspiration arises: taking others' suffering as one's own suffering, and others' happiness as one's own happiness, rather than taking one's own suffering and happiness as what defines one's own suffering and happiness — seeing no difference between benefiting another and having some separate benefit of one's own. On the basis of this meaning, there is a verse that states: "The lesser person diligently strives, ever seeking his own happiness; the middling person seeks the cessation of suffering, since happiness is not something to rely on where suffering remains; the greater person ever diligently seeks his own suffering and others' peace and happiness, and the permanent cessation of others' suffering — because he takes others as himself."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The argument's central move is a genuinely elegant piece of moral psychology: rather than treating the bodhisattva's selflessness as a mysterious exception to ordinary motivation, the treatise normalizes it by analogy to something already unremarkable — habituated self-attachment. Ordinary beings, through long habituation, treat an impersonal, constructed "self" as worth suffering for; a bodhisattva, through equally long habituation in the opposite direction, treats others as worth suffering for in exactly the same psychologically real way. Compassion this thorough isn't presented as superhuman, but as self-attachment's own structure, redirected.

The closing three-tier verse (lesser/middling/greater person) is a compact summary of this tradition's entire soteriological landscape: the ordinary being seeking pleasure, the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha seeking personal liberation from suffering, and the bodhisattva seeking others' welfare even at the cost of his own suffering — three progressively less self-centered relationships to one's own suffering and happiness, culminating in a stance that treats "other" and "self" as no longer meaningfully distinct categories.

11. Why Buddhas Only Appear While Lifespan Is Falling

Buddhas appear only during one specific window: while human lifespan declines from eighty thousand years down to a hundred, never during a period of increase and never once it falls below a hundred. Rising lifespans make beings too satisfied with saṃsāra to seek liberation, while lifespans below a hundred bring the five defilements — of lifespan, of the kalpa itself, of affliction, of views, and of sentient beings — to such an extreme that beings become impossible to teach at all.

Chinese Source

如是已辯劫量差別。諸佛獨覺出現世間，為劫增時、為劫減位？頌曰：減八萬至百，諸佛現世間，獨覺增減時，麟角喻百劫。論曰：從此洲人壽八萬歲，漸減乃至壽極百年，於此中間諸佛出現。何緣增位無佛出耶？有情樂增難教厭故。何緣減百無佛出耶？五濁極增難可化故。言五濁者：一壽濁、二劫濁、三煩惱濁、四見濁、五有情濁。劫減將末，壽等鄙下如滓穢故，說名為濁。由前二濁，如其次第，壽命資具極被衰損。由次二濁，善品衰損，以耽欲樂自苦行故，或損在家出家善故。由後一濁衰損自身，謂壞自身身量色力念智勤勇及無病故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Kalpa-measures and their distinctions have thus been discussed. Do buddhas and pratyekabuddhas appear in the world during a period of increasing lifespan, or during a period of decreasing lifespan? Verse: buddhas appear in the world during the decline from eighty thousand years down to a hundred; pratyekabuddhas appear during both increase and decrease, though the rhinoceros-horn-like solitary type requires a hundred kalpas of prior preparation. Prose: from the time this continent's people's lifespan is eighty thousand years, gradually decreasing until lifespan reaches as little as a hundred years — it is within this interval that buddhas appear. Why is it that no buddha appears during a period of increase? Because sentient beings, delighting in the increase, are difficult to teach toward disenchantment with saṃsāra. Why is it that no buddha appears once lifespan has decreased below a hundred? Because the five defilements have become so extreme that beings are difficult to transform. What are these five defilements? First, the defilement of lifespan; second, the defilement of the kalpa itself; third, the defilement of affliction; fourth, the defilement of views; fifth, the defilement of sentient beings. As a kalpa's decline nears its end, lifespan and the rest become base and inferior, like dregs and filth — hence they are called "defilements." Through the first two defilements, respectively, lifespan and the material means of livelihood become extremely diminished. Through the next two defilements, wholesome qualities become diminished, since beings indulge in sensual pleasure or in self-mortification, thereby damaging the wholesome conduct of both householders and renunciants. Through the last defilement, beings' own persons become diminished — that is, their own bodily stature, physical strength, mindfulness, wisdom, diligence, courage, and freedom from illness all deteriorate.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The logic here cuts against a natural intuition: one might expect a buddha to appear precisely when conditions are best, during a period of rising lifespan and flourishing, rather than during decline. Instead, the treatise argues the opposite — rising fortune breeds complacency that makes beings resistant to teaching, while decline (up to a point) makes suffering visible enough to motivate the search for liberation. But decline can go too far: past a certain threshold of degradation, the same five defilements that once made beings receptive make them simply too damaged to teach at all. A buddha's appearance, on this account, requires a surprisingly narrow window — degraded enough to seek a way out, not so degraded as to be beyond reach.

The five defilements are grouped in pairs by what they damage: lifespan and kalpa-defilement erode external conditions (lifespan itself, material livelihood), affliction and view-defilement erode wholesome conduct (through either indulgence or misguided asceticism), and the defilement of sentient beings erodes the person directly — body, strength, and mental faculties all together. This gives the five-part list an internal structure rather than being simply five independent items.

12. Two Kinds of Pratyekabuddha, and Why Neither One Teaches

Unlike buddhas, pratyekabuddhas appear regardless of whether lifespan is rising or falling, and come in two kinds: the group-dwelling type, former disciples who reach the final fruit without a teacher present — illustrated by a startling story of five hundred ascetics converted in an instant by a devoted monkey imitating a pratyekabuddha's demeanor — and the rhinoceros-horn-like type, solitary by necessity, who need a full hundred kalpas of preparation. Both are fully capable of teaching yet deliberately choose not to, for reasons the treatise takes seriously enough to enumerate one by one.

Chinese Source

獨覺出現通劫增減。然諸獨覺有二種殊：一者部行、二麟角喻。部行獨覺先是聲聞，得勝果時轉名獨勝。有餘說彼先是異生，曾修聲聞順決擇分，今自證道得獨勝名。由本事中說：一山處總有五百苦行外仙。有一獼猴曾與獨覺相近而住，見彼威儀，展轉遊行至外仙所，現先所見獨覺威儀。諸仙覩之咸生敬慕，須臾皆證獨覺菩提。若先是聖人，不應修苦行，麟角喻者謂必獨居。二獨覺中麟角喻者，要百大劫修菩提資糧，然後方成麟角喻獨覺。言獨覺者，謂現身中離稟至教唯自悟道，以能自調不調他故。何緣獨覺言不調他？非彼無能演說正法，以彼亦得無礙解故，又能憶念過去所聞諸佛所宣聖教理故。又不可說彼無慈悲，為攝有情現神通故。又不可說無受法機，爾時有情亦有能起世間離欲對治道故。雖有此理，由彼宿習少欣樂勝解無說希望故。又知有情難受深法，以順流既久難令逆流故。又避攝眾故，不為他宣說正法，怖諠雜故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pratyekabuddhas appear during both increasing and decreasing kalpas alike. However, there are two distinct kinds of pratyekabuddha: first, the group-dwelling type; second, the rhinoceros-horn-like type. The group-dwelling pratyekabuddha was formerly a śrāvaka, and, upon attaining the superior fruit, comes to be called "solitarily supreme." There is a further view that such a one was formerly an ordinary being who had previously cultivated the śrāvaka's aids to penetration, and now, awakening to the path entirely on his own, attains the name "solitarily supreme." As is told in the Itivṛttaka: in a certain mountain place there once dwelt, in total, five hundred ascetic non-Buddhist sages. There was a monkey who had once lived in close proximity to a pratyekabuddha and had observed his dignified deportment; wandering from place to place, it eventually arrived where these non-Buddhist sages were, and displayed the dignified deportment it had previously seen the pratyekabuddha exhibit. The sages, seeing this, were all filled with reverence and admiration, and, in but a moment, all of them realized pratyekabuddha-awakening. Had they already been noble ones beforehand, they would have had no need to undertake ascetic practices. As for the rhinoceros-horn-like type, this refers to one who necessarily dwells entirely alone. Among the two kinds of pratyekabuddha, the rhinoceros-horn-like type requires a full hundred great kalpas of cultivating the provisions for awakening before finally becoming a rhinoceros-horn-like pratyekabuddha. By "pratyekabuddha" is meant one who, in his present existence, awakens to the path entirely on his own, apart from receiving any teacher's instruction — since he is capable of disciplining himself, but not of disciplining others. Why is it said that a pratyekabuddha does not discipline others? It is not that he lacks the capacity to expound the true Dharma, since he too has attained unobstructed understanding, and is moreover able to remember the sacred teachings he has heard proclaimed by buddhas in the past. Nor can it be said that he lacks compassion, since he does manifest supernormal powers in order to gather sentient beings. Nor can it be said that there are no beings receptive to the Dharma at that time, since, even then, there remain sentient beings capable of generating the mundane path counteracting desire. Although this is so, it is because of his own past habituation — having taken little delight in resolute understanding through verbal explanation, he has no wish to teach. Moreover, he recognizes how difficult it is for sentient beings to accept a profound teaching, since, having long flowed downstream with the current, it is difficult to make them turn against that current. Moreover, in order to avoid gathering a following, he does not expound the true Dharma to others, fearing the noise and confusion that a following would bring.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The monkey story is doing careful conceptual work, not just providing color: the text explicitly draws the inference that, since the five hundred sages still needed to undertake ascetic practices after their sudden realization, they cannot have been noble ones all along — meaning the monkey's mimicked deportment alone was enough to trigger a genuine breakthrough in ordinary beings who had never received any actual teaching, only the visual impression of realized conduct. This is a striking claim about how little verbal instruction awakening strictly requires, at least for this specific type of pratyekabuddha.

The list of reasons a pratyekabuddha declines to teach is worth reading as a set of successively harder-to-dismiss objections, each answered in turn: not incapacity, not lack of compassion, not absence of receptive students — but a settled personal disposition (little taste for verbal exposition), a realistic assessment of how hard it is to redirect beings long habituated to saṃsāra, and a preference for solitude over the social complications a following would bring. None of these reasons impugn the pratyekabuddha's attainment; they simply describe a specific, deliberate choice about how to use it.
