What Makes a Faculty Dominant?

This page carries the opening of fascicle 3, which begins Chapter 2 (根品, the Indriya-nirdeśa, "Exposition of the Faculties"): what "faculty" means as a technical term, and the specific dominant function of each of the twenty-two faculties named at the close of fascicle 2, beginning with the five physical senses and the two sex faculties.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 14 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 3 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. What Does "Faculty" Mean? Opening Chapter Two

Fascicle 3 opens Chapter 2 exactly where fascicle 2 left off — the twenty-two faculties were just listed and mapped onto the eighteen elements — and now asks what "faculty" itself means as a technical term.

Chinese Source

如是因界已列諸根，即於此中根是何義？最勝自在光顯名根，由此總成根增上義。此增上義誰望於誰？頌曰：傳說五於四，四根於二種，五八染淨中，各別為增上。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, by way of the elements, the faculties have already been listed. Now, within this topic, what does "faculty" mean? What is supreme, autonomous, and manifest is named "faculty" — by this, altogether, the meaning of a faculty's dominant function is established. This dominant function — dominant over what, relative to what? A verse states: tradition holds that five are dominant over four matters; four faculties, over two kinds each; five and eight, amid defilement and purity, are each respectively dominant.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Chapter 2 (根品, the Indriya-nirdeśa) picks up directly from where Chapter 1 closed: the twenty-two faculties were just named and mapped onto the eighteen elements at the very end of fascicle 2. Now the treatise asks a deeper question — what does "faculty" (根, indriya, literally "root") actually mean? The answer is a threefold definition: something counts as a faculty when it is supreme in its domain, autonomous — not dependent on something else for its characteristic action — and manifest, its effect being evident and observable. Together these constitute what the tradition calls a faculty's "dominant function" (增上義, adhipatya-artha).

Since "dominant" is inherently relational — dominant over something — the immediate question is what, exactly, each faculty dominates. The verse previews the shape of the answer before filling in any content: the five physical senses are each dominant over four matters; a further group of four faculties are each dominant over two; and the remaining thirteen — split as five and eight — are dominant specifically with respect to defilement and purity. What each of these groupings actually consists of is what the rest of this chapter spells out, faculty by faculty.

2. The Five Senses' Four Dominant Functions

The prose fills in the first group from the verse: the eye and the other four physical faculties are each dominant in exactly the same four respects.

Chinese Source

論曰：眼等五根各於四事能為增上：一莊嚴身、二導養身、三生識等、四不共事。且眼耳根莊嚴身者，謂若盲聾身醜陋故。導養身者，謂因見聞避險難故。生識等者，謂發二識及相應故。不共事者，謂能見色聞聲別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Prose: The eye and the other four faculties are each dominant over four matters: first, adorning the body; second, guiding and nourishing the body; third, generating consciousness and its associates; fourth, their own exclusive function. As for the eye and ear faculties adorning the body: this means that if one were blind or deaf, the body would be disfigured. As for guiding and nourishing the body: this means that, through seeing and hearing, one avoids danger and difficulty. As for generating consciousness and its associates: this means that they give rise to their respective two consciousnesses together with the mental factors that accompany them. As for their exclusive function: this means their distinct capacity to see form and to hear sound.

鼻舌身根莊嚴身者，如眼耳說。導養身者，謂於段食能受用故。生識等者，謂發三識及相應故。不共事者，謂嗅甞覺香味觸故。

As for the nose, tongue, and body faculties adorning the body: this is as explained for the eye and ear. As for guiding and nourishing the body: this means that, through them, one is able to partake of physical food. As for generating consciousness and its associates: this means that they give rise to their respective three consciousnesses together with the mental factors that accompany them. As for their exclusive function: this means their distinct capacity to smell fragrance, taste flavor, and feel touch.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The same four-function template is applied identically to all five physical senses. "Adorning the body" echoes a discussion from far back in fascicle 1, about why the eye and ear come in pairs — there the reasoning was rejected as an explanation for why there are two, but the aesthetic dimension resurfaces here in a different role: a blind or deaf body is conventionally regarded as disfigured by the very absence of these faculties, so their presence is credited with adorning it. "Guiding and nourishing" splits naturally along the reach of each sense: the two distance senses, eye and ear, help one navigate away from danger, while the three contact senses, nose, tongue, and body, directly enable partaking of physical food — smell and taste connect to food obviously enough, and the body faculty's role becomes clear once one recalls that eating and digesting are themselves tactile, bodily processes. "Generating consciousness" is the most expected function of all — each faculty gives rise to its own dedicated consciousness. "Exclusive function" names what only that faculty, and no other, can do: only the eye sees form, only the ear hears sound, and so on through smell, taste, and touch.

3. Female and Male: Marking Out the Sexes

The second group from the verse begins: the female and male faculties, each dominant over two matters of their own.

Chinese Source

女、男、命、意各於二事能為增上。且女、男根二增上者，一有情異、二分別異。有情異者，由此二根令諸有情女男類別。分別異者，由此二根形相言音乳房等別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The female, male, life, and mind faculties are each dominant over two matters. As for the female and male faculties' two dominant functions: first, differentiating beings; second, differentiating characteristics. "Differentiating beings" means that, by means of these two faculties, sentient beings are distinguished into the female and male kinds. "Differentiating characteristics" means that, by means of these two faculties, bodily form, voice, the breasts, and so on are differentiated between the sexes.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sex faculties' two dominant functions operate at two different levels. "Differentiating beings" is almost definitional: having a female or male faculty is precisely what sorts a given being into one sex-category or the other in the first place. "Differentiating characteristics" is the observable, downstream consequence — body shape, voice, and secondary sexual characteristics such as the breasts all follow from, and are credited to, that underlying faculty.

4. An Alternate View: Sex, Defilement, and Purity

A rival opinion reframes the sex faculties' dominant function away from sex-differentiation and toward a claim about the full range of moral and spiritual possibility.

Chinese Source

有說：此於染淨增上故言於二。所以者何？本性損壞。扇搋半擇及二形人無不律儀無間斷善諸雜染法，亦無律儀得果離染諸清淨法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some say instead: these two faculties are dominant with respect to defilement and purity, and that is why it is said they are dominant "over two." Why is this? Because of a fundamentally damaged nature. Ṣaṇḍhas, paṇḍakas, and hermaphrodites have neither the capacity for formal non-restraint nor for the uninterrupted cutting-off of wholesome roots, nor any of the various afflictive dharmas that depend on these; nor do they have the capacity for formal restraint, the attainment of the noble fruits, or any of the various purifying dharmas free from defilement.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

On this alternate view, having an unambiguous, functioning sex faculty is what makes a person capable of the full range from one moral extreme to the other: capable of formally undertaking non-restraint (非律儀, a technical category for publicly vowing to a livelihood built on killing, such as a hunter or butcher — understood in this tradition as a formal negative commitment carrying real karmic weight) and everything that follows from it, all the way to formal monastic restraint and the attainment of the noble fruits.

The claim is that people the tradition classifies as ṣaṇḍha (traditionally, those without male generative capacity), paṇḍaka (a broader classical category for men considered incapable of ordinary sexual function), and hermaphrodites (二形人, beings held to possess both sex faculties) were excluded, in classical Vinaya, from both extremes — barred from full ordination and from the fruits of the path, on the reasoning that their sex faculty was itself considered "damaged." This reflects a specific, historically situated Vinaya doctrine about ordination eligibility that predates and differs sharply from how gender and embodiment are understood today. It is translated here for accuracy to the source, not as an endorsement, and remains a subject of ongoing scholarly and doctrinal reconsideration within contemporary Buddhist communities.

5. Life and Mind: Sustaining a Being, and Carrying It Onward

The verse's second group continues with the life faculty's own two functions, and the mind faculty's — the first of which reaches back to the mechanics of rebirth itself.

Chinese Source

命根二者，謂於眾同分能續及能持。

Ru-Yi Translation

The life faculty's two dominant functions are: sustaining continuity within one's own class of being, and maintaining that continuity.

意根二者，謂能續後有及自在隨行。能續後有者，如契經言：時健達縛於二心內隨一現前，謂或愛俱、或恚俱等。

The mind faculty's two dominant functions are: the capacity to continue into a subsequent existence, and autonomous accompanying activity. As for "the capacity to continue into a subsequent existence" — a sutra says: at that time, the gandharva has one of two states of mind present — that is, either accompanied by desire, or accompanied by aversion, and so on.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Class-commonality" (眾同分, nikāya-sabhāga) is a specific Abhidharma category — a formation dissociated from mind — that accounts for why members of the same species or realm of birth genuinely share a common nature, distinct from other kinds of being. The life faculty's job is to sustain a being's continuity within that shared class across a single lifetime: first establishing it, then upholding it moment to moment until death.

The mind faculty's first function reaches into the mechanics of rebirth itself. "Gandharva" (健達縛) is this tradition's technical term for the intermediate-state being — the being that exists briefly between one life's death and the next life's conception — and the cited sutra describes it as drawn toward its future rebirth by one of two possible orientations: desire or aversion toward the scene of its coming conception. The mind faculty's second function, "autonomous accompanying activity," is still to be explained in what follows.

6. The Mind's Autonomy: A Famous Verse on Mind's Primacy

The mind faculty's second dominant function closes the "four faculties, two matters each" group with a well-known canonical statement of mind's reach.

Chinese Source

自在隨行者，如契經言：心能導世間，心能遍攝受，如是心一法，皆自在隨行。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "autonomous accompanying activity" — a sutra says: mind can guide the world; mind can universally gather and receive it. Thus this single dharma, mind, in all things acts autonomously and accompanies them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a well-known canonical verse celebrating mind's primacy, echoing the same sentiment found across Buddhist literature that mind precedes and leads all things. Where "continuing into a subsequent existence" named mind's specific role in driving rebirth, "autonomous accompanying activity" makes a broader claim: mind leads and gathers in the world at large — every experience, every action, is something mind actively directs and takes hold of, rather than something that merely happens to it. This closes the mind faculty's two dominant functions, completing the group of four faculties — female, male, life, mind — each dominant over two matters, as the chapter's opening verse promised.

7. Feelings and the Path-Faculties: Defilement Versus Purity

The verse's final grouping arrives: thirteen remaining faculties, split five and eight, dominant respectively over defilement and over purity.

Chinese Source

樂等五受、信等八根，於染淨中如次增上。樂等五受染增上者，貪等隨眠所隨增故。信等八根淨增上者，諸清淨法隨生長故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The five feelings — pleasure and the rest — and the eight faculties — faith and the rest — are dominant, respectively in order, with respect to defilement and purity. As for the five feelings being dominant with respect to defilement: this is because the latent afflictions, such as greed, increase in dependence on them. As for the eight faculties being dominant with respect to purity: this is because the various purifying dharmas grow in dependence on them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This completes the chapter's opening verse: thirteen remaining faculties split five and eight between defilement and purity. The five feelings — pleasure, pain, joy, distress, equanimity — are dominant over the defiled side, because the latent afflictions (隨眠, anuśaya — the dormant, deeply rooted tendencies toward greed, hatred, and the rest) are said to increase in dependence on feeling; feeling is what these afflictions cling to and grow from. The eight remaining faculties — the five spiritual faculties (faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom) plus the three "pure" faculties tied to progressively realizing the noble truths — are dominant over the pure side, because the qualities of the path develop in dependence on them. With this, all twenty-two faculties have been assigned their dominant function, exactly matching the shape the opening verse previewed.

8. A Dissenting View: Feelings Can Also Purify

A minority opinion complicates the clean division just drawn, citing canonical formulas that tie specific feelings directly to stages of the path.

Chinese Source

有餘師說：樂等於淨亦為增上。如契經說：「樂故心定」、「苦為信依」，亦「出離依喜及憂、捨」。毘婆沙師傳說如此。

Ru-Yi Translation

Other masters say: pleasure and the rest are also dominant with respect to purity. As a sutra says: "through pleasure, the mind becomes concentrated"; "suffering is the basis for faith"; and also, "renunciation depends on joy, and on distress and equanimity." This is what the Vaibhāṣika masters traditionally say.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This dissenting view complicates the tidy five-defiled, eight-pure split just established, by citing canonical formulas that tie each of the five feelings directly to a specific stage of spiritual progress rather than treating all five as uniformly defilement-generating: pleasure is said to be the condition for concentration to arise, pain the condition for faith to take root, and joy, distress, and equanimity are each said to support renunciation in turn. If feelings can support the path this directly, they cannot be neatly confined to the defiled side alone — a genuine complication the treatise records rather than resolves outright. The closing attribution to the Vaibhāṣika masters describes the preceding mainstream position, the clean five-and-eight division, marking this section's dissenting view as a minority opinion weighed against that consensus rather than replacing it.

9. A Sharper Objection, and the Treatise's Real Answer

A genuinely sharp critique of the earlier fourfold account triggers a single, condensed verse that restates all twenty-two faculties' dominant functions on cleaner grounds.

Chinese Source

有餘師說：能導養身非眼等用，是識增上，識了方能避於險難，受段食故。見色等用亦非異識，故不共事於眼等根，不可立為別增上用，故非由此眼等成根。若爾，云何？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Other masters say: "guiding and nourishing the body" is not actually a function of the eye and the other senses, but rather is the dominant function of consciousness — because it is only once consciousness has cognized something that one can avoid danger and difficulty, or partake of physical food. Moreover, functions like "seeing form" are likewise not distinct from consciousness; therefore, "exclusive function," with respect to the eye and the other faculties, cannot be established as a separate dominant function, and so it is not by virtue of this that the eye and the rest become faculties. If that is so, then how should it be explained? A verse states:

了自境增上，總立於六根，從身立二根，女男性增上，於同住雜染，清淨增上故。應知命五受，信等立為根，未當知已知，具知根亦爾，於得後後道，涅槃等增上。

Cognizing one's own object is the dominant function by which, altogether, the six faculties are established. From the body faculty, the two faculties are established, their dominant function being the establishing of sexual nature. With respect to sustaining continuity, and to defilement, and to purity — accordingly, life, the five feelings, and faith and the rest should be understood as established as faculties. The faculties of "I shall come to know what is not yet known," "full knowledge," and "having complete knowledge" are likewise so, their dominant function lying in attaining each subsequent stage of the path, and in nirvana.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection is a genuine philosophical point, not a technicality: of the earlier fourfold account (adorning the body, guiding and nourishing it, generating consciousness, exclusive function), two of the four — guiding/nourishing and exclusive function — are said to belong not to the bare sense organ at all, but to consciousness, since it is only once a cognitive act has actually occurred that danger gets avoided or food gets eaten. The eye by itself, apart from consciousness, does nothing; it is always consciousness that does the seeing. This is the same theme fascicle 2 explored at length in its debate over whether the eye or the eye-consciousness truly sees.

The verse's answer sidesteps that whole fourfold framework in favor of one clean criterion for the six sense-and-mind faculties: "cognizing one's own object." Each of the six is dominant simply insofar as it is what makes cognition of its own specific domain possible — the eye for form, the ear for sound, mind for whatever only mind can access — a criterion that survives the objection because it never claims the raw organ does the avoiding, eating, or seeing itself, only that it is what enables cognizing its object in the first place. The rest of the verse then restates, in extremely compressed form, everything already explained about the remaining sixteen faculties — sex, life, feelings, and the path-faculties — confirming they all hold up under this same underlying approach.

10. The Three Pure Faculties, Explained Two Ways

The verse's final, newest claim gets its own prose elaboration: two different ways of understanding how the three "pure" faculties build toward liberation.

Chinese Source

論曰：言亦爾者，類顯一一各能為根，謂未知當知根於得已知根道有增上用，已知根於得具知根道有增上用，具知根於得涅槃有增上用，非心未解脫能般涅槃故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Prose: As for saying "likewise so" — this indicates, by analogy, that each one individually can serve as a faculty. That is: the faculty of "I shall come to know what is not yet known" has its dominant function in attaining the path of "full knowledge"; the faculty of "full knowledge" has its dominant function in attaining the path of "having complete knowledge"; the faculty of "having complete knowledge" has its dominant function in attaining nirvana — since a mind not yet liberated cannot achieve final nirvana.

等言為顯復有異門。云何異門？謂見所斷煩惱滅中未知當知根有增上用，於修所斷煩惱滅中已知根有增上用，於現法樂住中具知根有增上用，由此能領受解脫喜樂故。

The word "and the like" is meant to indicate that there is yet another explanatory approach. What is this other approach? Within the cessation of the afflictions to be cut off by seeing the truths, the faculty of "I shall come to know what is not yet known" has its dominant function; within the cessation of the afflictions to be cut off by cultivation, the faculty of "full knowledge" has its dominant function; within dwelling in present-life bliss, the faculty of "having complete knowledge" has its dominant function — because, through it, one is able to experience the joy and bliss of liberation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The treatise records two rival readings of the three pure faculties' progression rather than choosing between them. The first is sequential: each faculty's dominant achievement is unlocking the next one's stage — the first enables reaching the second, the second the third, and the third enables nirvana itself, since, as the text notes, an unliberated mind cannot achieve final nirvana. This maps the three onto successive milestones along a single continuous path.

The second reading instead ties each faculty to a distinct type of affliction being eliminated. The first is dominant specifically during the cutting-off of intellectually-produced afflictions — the path of seeing, when wrong views collapse all at once upon a first genuine insight, the same "cut off by seeing" territory covered much earlier in this translation. The second is dominant during the gradual cutting-off of habitually-produced afflictions, the path of cultivation. The third is dominant specifically during "dwelling in present-life bliss" (現法樂住), the technical term for an arhat's blissful abiding after full liberation but before physical death. This second reading locates each faculty's dominance in a distinct mode of lived experience rather than a stage reached — the same three milestones, seen through a different lens.

11. The Real Test: Six Roles That Complete a Being's Existence

The objection about ignorance and ordinary bodily organs gets its full, considered answer, closing the entire opening inquiry of Chapter Two.

Chinese Source

如是等事不應立根，由所許根有如是相。頌曰：心所依此別，此住此雜染，此資糧此淨，由此量立根。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such things as these should not be established as faculties, because what is accepted as a "faculty" has specifically this character. A verse states: mind's support, this differentiation, this continuance, this defilement, this provision, this purification — by this measure, the faculties are established.

論曰：心所依者，眼等六根。此內六處是有情本，此相差別由女男根，復由命根此一期住。此成雜染由五受根，此淨資糧由信等五，此成清淨由後三根，由此立根事皆究竟。是故不應許無明等及語具等亦立為根，彼無此中增上用故。

Prose: "Mind's support" refers to the eye and the other faculties — these six internal fields are the root of a sentient being. This differentiation is accounted for by the female and male faculties. This continuance for one lifetime, in turn, is accounted for by the life faculty. This becoming defiled is accounted for by the five feeling faculties. This provision for purity is accounted for by faith and the other four. This becoming pure is accounted for by the last three faculties. By this, the matter of establishing the faculties is entirely complete. Therefore, one should not accept that ignorance and the like, or the organs of speech and the like, should also be established as faculties, since they lack a dominant function in this specific sense.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The real criterion for "faculty" was never simply "has some dominant function over some effect" — that loose test was exactly what let ignorance and ordinary bodily organs slip in, since they too obviously dominate their own respective effects. The actual criterion is narrower and more structured: a faculty is dominant with respect to one of exactly six roles that, together, comprehensively map a sentient being's whole existential trajectory. What supports its basic capacity for cognition: the six sense-and-mind faculties. What differentiates it by sex: the two sex faculties. What sustains it through one lifetime: the life faculty. What causes it to become defiled: the five feelings. What provisions the path toward purification: the five spiritual faculties. And what completes its actual purification: the three pure faculties.

Ignorance and the speech, hand, and foot organs, however genuinely "dominant" over their own narrow effects, simply do not correspond to any of these six comprehensive roles — they are dominant over some local process, not over one of the six dimensions that define a being's total arc from arising to liberation. With this, the objection is fully answered, and the entire opening inquiry of Chapter 2 — what makes something a faculty at all — reaches its considered, complete resolution.

12. A Mirror-Image Scheme: Fourteen Faculties for Saṃsāra, Eight for Liberation

A second complete accounting of all twenty-two faculties follows immediately, built on an entirely different organizing principle yet landing on the identical total.

Chinese Source

復有餘師別說根相。頌曰：或流轉所依，及生住受用，建立前十四，還滅後亦然。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, moreover, other masters who explain the character of the faculties differently. A verse states: alternatively, by the support of transmigration, and by arising, sustaining, and experiencing, the first fourteen are established; and likewise, by cessation, for the latter as well.

論曰：或言顯此是餘師意，約流轉還滅立二十二根。流轉所依謂眼等六，生由女男從彼生故。住由命根，仗彼住故。受用由五受因，彼領納故。約此建立前十四根。

Prose: The word "alternatively" indicates that this represents another master's view: organizing the twenty-two faculties according to transmigration and cessation. The support of transmigration refers to the eye and the other five faculties, together six. Arising is accounted for by the female and male faculties, since beings arise from them. Sustaining is accounted for by the life faculty, since a being relies on it to continue. Experiencing is accounted for by the cause of the five feelings, since they register experience. By this, the first fourteen faculties are established.

還滅位中即約此四義類別故立後八根。還滅所依謂信等五，於三無漏由初故生，由次故住，由後受用，根量由此無減無增。

Within the stage of cessation, it is likewise by category of these same four meanings that the latter eight faculties are established. The support of cessation refers to faith and the other four. Among the three uncontaminated faculties, by the first, purification arises; by the next, it continues; by the last, it is experienced. By this, the count of the faculties is neither deficient nor excessive.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This alternate scheme reorganizes the same twenty-two faculties around Buddhism's two grand structural poles: transmigration (流轉, the forward current of saṃsāra) and cessation (還滅, the reverse current toward liberation). The same four-part logic — a support, an arising, a sustaining, and an experiencing — is applied to both sides in turn.

On the saṃsāric side: the six sense-and-mind faculties are what one relies on at all, the support; the two sex faculties are what a being arises from; the life faculty is what sustains a being through its lifetime; the five feelings are what registers lived experience — six plus two plus one plus five makes fourteen. On the liberating side, the same four roles recur in miniature: the five spiritual faculties are the support for the path, and the three pure faculties divide the remaining three roles among themselves — the first is when purification arises, the second when it continues, the third when it is fully experienced — five plus three makes eight. Fourteen plus eight is exactly twenty-two, with nothing left over and nothing missing: a second complete accounting of all twenty-two faculties, built on a wholly different organizing principle than the six-role scheme of the previous section, yet arriving at the identical total.

13. Closing the Reductio: Snakes, Gravity, and Where the Line Has to Be Drawn

The rebuttal turns concrete and almost empirical, working through hands, feet, and the excretory organs one by one before pushing the objection to a deliberate absurdity.

Chinese Source

謂即手足異處異相差別生時名執行故。又離手足亦有執行，如腹行類。是故手足不可於彼建立為根。出大便處於能棄事不應立根，重物於空遍墮落故，又由風力引令出故。出小便處於生樂事不應立根，即女男根起此樂故。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is: it is precisely when the hands and feet, in their different locations, generate their differentiated characteristics, that this is called "grasping and moving." Moreover, apart from the hands and feet, there is also grasping and moving — as with creatures that move upon their belly. Therefore, the hands and feet cannot be established as a faculty with respect to that function. The organ of defecation should not be established as a faculty with respect to the function of eliminating waste, because heavy objects fall downward through empty space on their own, and also because it is the force of wind that draws it out. The organ of urination should not be established as a faculty with respect to producing pleasure, because it is the female or male faculty that gives rise to this pleasure.

又諸喉齒眼瞼支節應立為根，於能吞嚼開閉屈申有力用故。或一切因於自所作有力用故皆應立根，彼雖有用非增上故。不立根者，此語具等亦非增上不應立根。

Moreover, by this same reasoning, the throat, teeth, eyelids, and joints should also be established as faculties, since they have real functional capacity for swallowing, chewing, opening and closing, and bending and stretching. Or, taking it further: every cause has functional capacity with respect to its own effect, so by this logic everything should be established as a faculty. Though these all genuinely have function, they are not dominant in the relevant sense — and this is why they are not established as faculties. In just the same way, the organs of speech and the like are also not dominant in this sense, and so should not be established as faculties.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Hands and feet fail twice over: they have no nature distinct from the body faculty itself — they are just differently located, differently shaped portions of that same faculty — and their supposed function is not even exclusive to having hands and feet at all, since legless, belly-crawling creatures plainly move without them. The excretory organs get their own dismissals: waste falls out simply because heavy things fall, and because the body's internal "wind" pushes it along; any pleasure near the urinary organ is credited to the sex faculty rather than treated as a function of its own.

The argument then escalates deliberately into absurdity: if any body part with a working function counted as a faculty, the list would explode to include the throat, teeth, eyelids, and joints — and pushed to its limit, literally every cause with any effect at all would qualify, which is plainly untenable. That absurdity is the whole point: it proves the criterion was never "has some function," only the six comprehensive roles established two sections ago. With this, the treatise's defense of the twenty-two faculties' exact count is complete.

14. Why Only Eight Faculties Still Need Explaining

Before naming any more faculties, the treatise explains why most of the twenty-two already have a home elsewhere in the treatise — leaving only eight still needing definition here.

Chinese Source

命根體是不相應故，不相應中自當廣辯。信等體是心所法故，心所法中亦當廣辯。樂等五受、三無漏根，更無辯處故今應釋。

Ru-Yi Translation

The life faculty's essential nature is that of a formation dissociated from mind, so this will be explained in full within the later discussion of dharmas dissociated from mind. As for faith and the rest, their essential nature is that of mental factors, so this too will be explained in full within the discussion of mental factors. As for the five feelings — pleasure and the rest — and the three uncontaminated faculties, there is no other place where they will be explained, so they should be explained now.

頌曰：身不悅名苦，即此悅名樂，及三定心悅，餘處此名喜，心不悅名憂，中捨二無別，見修無學道，依九立三根。

Verse: Bodily displeasure is called pain; this same bodily sensation, when pleasant, is called pleasure. Mental pleasantness within the deeper absorption and elsewhere is also called pleasure; within the lower absorptions, this mental pleasantness is instead called joy. Mental displeasure is called distress. The middle state, equanimity, is undifferentiated between body and mind. Based on these nine states across the paths of seeing, cultivation, and no-more-learning, the three pure faculties are established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is useful housekeeping: most of the twenty-two faculties will get their essential-nature explained elsewhere in the treatise, in the chapters dedicated to their actual ontological category — the life faculty among the formations dissociated from mind, faith and the four other spiritual faculties among the mental factors generally. Only the five feelings and the three pure faculties have no other designated home, so this chapter takes them up directly, right now.

The verse's definitions are more precise than they first appear. Pain and bodily pleasure are strictly bodily — feeling tied to the five physical sense-consciousnesses. Mental pleasantness splits by the texture of the absorption it occurs in: in the coarser, more exuberant lower absorptions, it is called "joy," since this quality of pleasantness is still somewhat agitating to the mind; in the calmer, subtler third absorption — and in ordinary mental pleasantness outside absorption altogether, where the joy/pleasure distinction stops being relevant — it is called "pleasure" instead, the same name used for bodily pleasantness. Distress is mental displeasure, and equanimity sits in the middle, neutral with respect to both body and mind at once, without needing separate bodily and mental versions the way pleasure does. The verse's final line looks ahead to the three pure faculties' own definition, built out of nine specific feeling-states distributed across the three stages of the path — the details of which the next section will unpack.

Correction: this section originally had the joy/pleasure mapping backwards — stating that mental pleasantness within absorption was called "pleasure" and everything else "joy." Re-checking the prose commentary's own reasoning (joy is exuberant and mind-agitating, so the calmer state cannot be named joy) showed the correct mapping is the reverse: joy belongs to the coarser, lower absorption states, and pleasure to the calmer third absorption and to ordinary experience outside absorption — matching the well-established doctrine that joy (prīti) is present in the first two dhyānas and drops away from the third onward. Corrected here rather than silently, per this project's practice.
