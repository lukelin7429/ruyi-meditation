Distinguishing the Noble Ones

Fascicle 22 opens Chapter 6 (分別賢聖品第六, the Ārya-pudgala-nirdeśa, "Distinguishing the Noble Ones"), the treatise's account of the path itself and the beings who walk it. It begins by asking what kind of path — the path of seeing or the path of cultivation — actually accomplishes the severances of affliction described across fascicles 19 through 21, then turns to a close analysis of the four noble truths themselves: their names, their order, and the three kinds of suffering that make every contaminated formation, pleasant or not, count as suffering in the end.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 8 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 22 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. What Kind of Path Accomplishes Severance: Seeing Versus Cultivation

Having finished explaining how afflictions attain the nine kinds of "full understanding" through severance, the treatise now opens a new chapter — Distinguishing the Noble Ones — by asking what kind of path actually brings about that severance in the first place. Is this path always undefiled, or can a defiled, worldly path accomplish it as well? The answer distinguishes the two paths already named throughout the preceding fascicles: the path of seeing is exclusively undefiled, while the path of cultivation is of both kinds, undefiled and worldly alike. The reason is that the path of seeing is able to swiftly counter the afflictions of all three realms at once, severing all nine grades of what is abandoned by seeing in a single, rapid stroke — a capacity no merely worldly path possesses, since a worldly path works gradually and only within a single realm at a time. The path of cultivation, being different in character and unfolding gradually rather than all at once, is capable of being either undefiled or worldly.

Chinese Source

如是已說煩惱等斷於九勝位得遍知名。然斷必由道力故得，此所由道其相云何？頌曰：已說煩惱斷，由見諦修故，見道唯無漏，修道通二種。論曰：前已廣說諸煩惱斷，由見諦道及修道故。道唯無漏亦有漏耶？見道應知唯是無漏，修道通二。所以者何？見道速能治三界故。頓斷九品見所斷故，非世間道有此堪能，故見位中道唯無漏。修道有異故通二種，如向所言由見諦故。

Ru-Yi Translation

We have now explained that afflictions attain the name "full understanding" through severance at nine superior stages. But severance must always come about through the power of a path — what is the character of this path? Verse: it has been explained that afflictions are severed through seeing the truths and through cultivation; the path of seeing is exclusively undefiled, while the path of cultivation is of both kinds. Explanation: it has already been explained at length that the severance of afflictions comes about through the path of seeing the truths and the path of cultivation. Is this path exclusively undefiled, or can it also be defiled? It should be understood that the path of seeing is exclusively undefiled, while the path of cultivation is of both kinds. Why is this so? Because the path of seeing is able to swiftly counter the afflictions of all three realms at once, severing all nine grades of what is abandoned by seeing in a single stroke — a capacity no worldly path possesses. For this reason, the path at the stage of seeing is exclusively undefiled. The path of cultivation, being of a different character, is of both kinds, exactly as stated just now with the phrase "through seeing the truths and [through cultivation]."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that this opens an entirely new chapter of the treatise — Chapter 6, Distinguishing the Noble Ones — which shifts the focus from the anuśayas themselves (Chapter 5, just concluded across fascicles 19 through 21) to the path and the persons who walk it. The asymmetry stated here — that seeing the truths is always undefiled, while cultivation can be either undefiled or worldly — sets up the framework for much of what follows in this chapter, since it implies that only the path of cultivation admits of the kind of gradual, this-worldly progress (through ordinary meditative concentration alone, without yet directly realizing the four truths) that a non-Buddhist practitioner could, in principle, also undertake.

2. The Four Truths: Their Order, and Why It Follows Realization, Not Causation

The treatise confirms that the four truths — suffering, origin, cessation, and the path — were already named in the treatise's first chapter, in the section distinguishing contaminated from uncontaminated dharmas, though listed there in a different order (path, cessation, then suffering and origin together) and with the same underlying substance as here. Why, then, are they presented here in this specific order — suffering first, then origin, then cessation, then path — rather than in the order a strict cause-and-effect account would suggest, which would name the cause (origin) before its effect (suffering)? Because this order instead follows the sequence in which a practitioner actually and directly realizes them in meditation. Some topics in this treatise are ordered by their sequence of actual arising (as with the four foundations of mindfulness); others by mere convenience of expression (as with the four right efforts, which have no fixed principled reason to be listed in their given order); the four truths are ordered specifically by the sequence of a yoga practitioner's direct realization, which is itself fixed by how the truths are observed at the preparatory stage that precedes it.

Chinese Source

諦四先已說，謂苦集滅道，彼自體亦然，次第隨現觀。論曰：諦有四種，名先已說。於何處說？謂初品中分別有漏無漏法處。彼如何說？謂彼頌言：無漏謂聖道，此說道諦。擇滅謂離繫，此說滅諦。及苦集世間，此說苦集諦。四諦次第如彼說耶？不爾。云何？如今所列：一苦、二集、三滅、四道。四諦自體亦有異耶？不爾。云何？如先所辯，為顯體同彼，故說亦然聲。四諦何緣如是次第？隨現觀位先後而說，謂現觀中先所觀者便在先說，若異此者應先說因後方說果。然或有法說次隨生，如念住等。或復有法說次隨便，如正勝等。謂此中無決定理趣起如是欲，先斷已生、後遮未生，但隨言便。今說四諦，隨瑜伽師現觀位中先後次第。何緣現觀次第必然？加行位中如是觀故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: the four truths were already named — suffering, origin, cessation, and the path; their own essential nature is likewise as stated then; their order here follows the sequence of direct realization. Explanation: there are four truths, whose names were already given earlier. Where was this stated? In the first chapter, in the section distinguishing contaminated and uncontaminated dharmas. How was it stated there? By that verse's words: "the uncontaminated is the noble path" — referring to the truth of the path; "analytical cessation is separation-from-bondage" — referring to the truth of cessation; "and suffering and its origin are the worldly dharmas" — referring to the truths of suffering and its origin. Does the order of the four truths here match how they were listed there? No. What, then, is the order? As now listed: first suffering, second origin, third cessation, fourth path. Does the essential nature of the four truths differ from what was explained earlier? No. The verse's word "likewise" is used specifically to show that their essential nature is the same, even though the order of presentation differs. Why are the four truths presented in this order? Because they are stated following the sequence in which they are actually directly realized in meditative practice — whichever is observed first in that direct realization is stated first; were it otherwise, one would need to state the cause before the effect. Yet in some cases a topic's stated order follows the sequence of its actual arising, as with the four foundations of mindfulness; in other cases it follows mere convenience of expression, as with the four right efforts, where there is no fixed principled reason for the given order. The four truths spoken of here follow the sequence of a yoga practitioner's direct realization. Why must this sequence be fixed? Because this is how one observes them at the preparatory stage.

Why must the preparatory stage observe the truths in exactly this sequence? Because whatever dharma is both an object of attachment and a source of oppressive distress, and for which one seeks a cause of escape, ought by reason to be observed first of all — hence the practitioner first observes suffering itself, which is precisely the truth of suffering. Next, one asks by what suffering is caused, and observes its cause, which is precisely the truth of the origin. Next, one asks by what suffering is brought to cessation, and observes that cessation, which is precisely the truth of cessation. Finally, one asks by what path that cessation is reached, and observes the path, which is precisely the truth of the path — exactly as one first sees that one is ill, then seeks the cause of the illness, then considers how it might be cured, and only afterward seeks the right medicine. The Sūtra of the Excellent Physician gives the same analogy in scripture: a king among physicians possesses four qualities enabling him to draw out a poisoned arrow — skillfully knowing the illness's symptoms, its cause, its cure, and the right medicine — and the Tathāgata is likewise a great king among physicians, truly understanding suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path. The stage of direct realization follows this same sequence because it is brought about by the force of the preceding preparatory stage, like a horse already familiar with the terrain, now let loose to gallop freely across it. This "direct realization" (abhisamaya) means a direct, complete awakening, and is said to be exclusively undefiled because it is directed toward nirvāṇa, the very object of true and complete awakening.

Chinese Source

何緣加行必如是觀？謂若有法是愛著處能作逼惱，為求脫因，此法理應最初觀察，故修行者加行位中最初觀苦，苦即苦諦。次復觀苦以誰為因？便觀苦因，因即集諦。次復觀苦以誰為滅？便觀苦滅，滅即滅諦。後觀苦滅以誰為道？便觀滅道，道即道諦。如見病已，次尋病因，續思病愈，後求良藥。契經亦說諦次第喻。何契經說？謂《良醫經》。如彼經言：夫醫王者，謂具四德能拔毒箭：一善知病狀、二善知病因、三善知病愈、四善知良藥。如來亦爾，為大醫王，如實了知苦集滅道。故加行位如是次觀，現觀位中次第亦爾，由加行力所引發故，如已觀地縱馬奔馳。此現觀名為目何義？應知此目現等覺義。何緣說此唯是無漏？對向涅槃正覺境故，此覺真淨故得正名。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why must the preparatory stage observe them in this way? Because whatever dharma is both an object of attachment and a source of oppressive distress, and whose escape one seeks a cause for, ought by reason to be observed first of all. Hence the practitioner, at the preparatory stage, first observes suffering — suffering being precisely the truth of suffering. Next, one further observes: by what is suffering caused? One then observes the cause of suffering — that cause being precisely the truth of the origin. Next, one further observes: by what is suffering brought to cessation? One then observes the cessation of suffering — that cessation being precisely the truth of cessation. Finally, one observes: by what path is the cessation of suffering reached? One then observes the path to cessation — that path being precisely the truth of the path. This is like first seeing that one is ill, then seeking the cause of the illness, then considering how the illness might be cured, and only afterward seeking the right medicine. The sūtras, too, give an analogy for this order. Which sūtra states this? The Sūtra of the Excellent Physician. As that sūtra says: a king among physicians is one who possesses four qualities enabling him to draw out a poisoned arrow — skillfully knowing the symptoms of the illness, its cause, its cure, and the right medicine. The Tathāgata is likewise a great king among physicians, truly and fully understanding suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path. Hence the preparatory stage observes them in this sequence, and the stage of direct realization follows the same sequence, since it is brought about by the force of that preparatory stage, like a horse already familiar with the terrain, now let loose to gallop freely across it. What does "direct realization" itself mean? It should be understood to denote a direct, complete awakening. Why is this said to be exclusively undefiled? Because it is directed toward nirvāṇa, the object of true and complete awakening, and, this awakening being truly pure, it rightly earns this name.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the memorable physician analogy here — diagnosing symptoms, identifying cause, confirming curability, and prescribing medicine — as the treatise's own scriptural justification (attributed to the Sūtra of the Excellent Physician) for why suffering is diagnosed before its cause is traced, exactly paralleling how a physician examines a patient before investigating etiology. We have preserved the analogy's own four-part structure and its explicit mapping onto the four truths, since the text uses it as a genuine argument for the truths' presentation order, not merely as decorative illustration.

We should also note the treatise's own honest acknowledgment that not every fourfold or sequential list in this literature follows a single unified logic of ordering — some (like the four foundations of mindfulness) reflect actual temporal arising, others (like the four right efforts) reflect mere expository convenience with "no fixed principled reason," and the four truths reflect yet a third principle, the sequence of meditative direct realization. We have kept this taxonomy of different ordering-principles exactly as the text presents it, rather than assuming a single explanatory scheme must govern every numbered list in this tradition.

3. Why "Noble" Truths, and How Every Formation Counts as Suffering

Why does scripture call these four "noble" truths in the first place? Because they are the truths of the noble ones, and so bear their name. Does this mean they are false for those who are not noble? No — they are true for everyone, since their nature involves no inversion whatsoever; it is simply that only the noble ones truly see them as they are, while others do not, and it is for this reason that the sūtras call them specifically "noble," not because they fail to hold for the non-noble. As a verse puts it: what the noble ones call suffering, the non-noble call pleasurable, and what the noble ones call pleasurable, the non-noble call suffering. Other teachers hold that only two of the four — cessation and the path — are exclusively noble truths, while suffering and its origin are common to noble and non-noble alike. This raises an objection: if suffering is meant to hold universally in this way, then, strictly speaking, only painful feeling itself would seem to count as the true essence of "suffering," with everything else falling outside it — so how can it be claimed, as this tradition does, that all contaminated formations whatsoever constitute the truth of suffering, including pleasant and neutral ones?

Chinese Source

故得聖名。於非聖者此豈成妄？於一切是諦，性無顛倒故。然唯聖者實見非餘，是故經中但名聖諦。非非聖諦，顛倒見故。如有頌言：聖者說是樂，非聖說為苦；聖者說為苦，非聖說是樂。有餘師說：二唯聖諦，餘二通是聖非聖諦。唯受一分是苦自體，所餘並非。如何可言諸有漏行皆是苦諦？頌曰：苦由三苦合，如所應一切，可意非可意，餘有漏行法。

Ru-Yi Translation

They bear the name "noble." Are these truths therefore false for one who is not a noble one? No — they are true for everyone, since their nature involves no inversion whatsoever. Yet only the noble ones truly see them as they are, others do not; and it is for this reason that the sūtras call them specifically noble truths, not because they fail to be truths for the non-noble, but rather because the non-noble see them through an inverted view. As a verse states: what the noble ones call suffering, the non-noble call pleasurable; what the noble ones call pleasurable, the non-noble call suffering. Other teachers say: two are exclusively noble truths, the remaining two are common to both the noble and non-noble. Only a portion of feeling is the essential nature of suffering; the rest is not. How, then, can it be said that all contaminated formations are the truth of suffering? Verse: suffering arises through union with the three kinds of suffering — accordingly, all contaminated formations, whether pleasing, displeasing, or neither.

The answer lies in distinguishing three distinct natures of suffering: the suffering of suffering itself, the suffering of change, and the suffering of formations. Every contaminated formation, as appropriate to its kind, is united with one of these three, and so all of them are, without exception, the truth of suffering. Pleasing formations are called suffering because they are united with the suffering of change — since even a pleasurable feeling, as a sūtra notes, is pleasurable while it arises and persists, but becomes suffering the moment it changes. Displeasing formations are called suffering because they are united with the suffering of suffering itself — a painful feeling, the same sūtra notes, is suffering both when it arises and while it persists, needing no further change to be so. And every remaining formation — neither pleasant nor unpleasant — is called suffering because it is united with the suffering of formations, being, as a further scriptural line has it, simply impermanent, and therefore suffering, since it is fabricated by a multitude of conditions.

Chinese Source

論曰：有三苦性，一苦苦性、二行苦性、三壞苦性。諸有漏行如其所應，與此三種苦性合故，皆是苦諦亦無有失。此中可意有漏行法，與壞苦合故名為苦。諸非可意有漏行法，與苦苦合故名為苦。除此所餘有漏行法，與行苦合故名為苦。何謂為可意非可意餘？謂樂等三受如其次第，由三受力令順樂受等諸有漏行得可意等名。所以者何？若諸樂受由壞成苦性。如契經言。諸樂受生時樂住時樂壞時苦，若諸苦受由體成苦性。如契經言：諸苦受生時苦、住時苦。不苦不樂受由行成苦性，眾緣造故。如契經言：若非常即是苦。如受順受，諸行亦然。

Ru-Yi Translation

Explanation: there are three natures of suffering — the suffering of suffering, the suffering of formations, and the suffering of change. Contaminated formations, each as appropriate, are united with one of these three natures of suffering, and so all of them are indeed, without fault, the truth of suffering. Among these, pleasing contaminated formations are called suffering because they are united with the suffering of change. Displeasing contaminated formations are called suffering because they are united with the suffering of suffering. All remaining contaminated formations are called suffering because they are united with the suffering of formations. What is meant by pleasing, displeasing, and the rest? It refers to the three feelings — pleasure and the others — since it is by the power of these three feelings that the contaminated formations associated with pleasurable feeling and so on come to be called pleasing and so on. Why is this so? Because pleasurable feelings become suffering through their own change — as a sūtra states: pleasurable feelings are pleasurable when they arise, pleasurable while they persist, but suffering when they change. Painful feelings, by contrast, are suffering by their very nature — as a sūtra states: painful feelings are suffering when they arise, and suffering while they persist. Feeling that is neither painful nor pleasurable becomes suffering through its nature as a formation, since it is fabricated by a multitude of conditions — as a sūtra states: whatever is impermanent is suffering. Just as feeling relates to what accompanies it, so too do all other formations.

The treatise adds that speaking of the pleasing and displeasing specifically in terms of the suffering of change and the suffering of suffering reflects only what is distinctive to each; in truth, every formation whatsoever is suffering simply by virtue of being a formation — and this deepest layer of suffering, the suffering of formations pervading even ordinary pleasure and neutral experience alike, is something only the noble ones are actually able to perceive. A verse captures the point vividly: just as a single eyelash placed on the palm of the hand goes entirely unnoticed, yet the same eyelash placed upon the eye causes real harm and discomfort — so the ordinary, unawakened person is like the palm, failing to notice the eyelash-like suffering of formations pervading their whole experience, while the wise noble one is like the eye, met with that very same object and responding with an intense, appropriate sense of revulsion and dread.

Chinese Source

有餘師釋：苦即苦性名苦苦性，如是乃至行即苦性名行苦性。應知此中說可意非可意為壞苦苦苦者，由不共故。理實一切行苦故苦，此唯聖者所能觀見。故有頌言：如以一睫毛，置掌人不覺，若置眼睛上，為損及不安。愚夫如手掌，不覺行苦睫，智者如眼睛，緣極生厭怖。

Ru-Yi Translation

Other teachers explain it this way: suffering that is itself suffering in nature is called the suffering of suffering; and, in just the same way, up through formations that are themselves suffering in nature, called the suffering of formations. It should be understood that, in speaking here of the pleasing and displeasing specifically in terms of the suffering of change and the suffering of suffering, this is done because of what is distinctive to each; in truth, all formations are suffering precisely because they are formations, and it is only the noble ones who are able to directly perceive this. As a verse states: just as a single eyelash, placed on the palm of the hand, goes unnoticed, but if placed upon the eye, causes harm and discomfort — so the ordinary, foolish person is like the palm, failing to notice the eyelash of the suffering of formations, while the wise one is like the eye, generating, upon this same object, an intense sense of revulsion and dread.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "other teachers" view reported here — that only cessation and the path are exclusively noble truths, while suffering and its origin are common to the noble and non-noble alike — as a genuinely different classification than the treatise's own default position (that all four are "noble" in the sense of being truly seen only by the noble, while remaining objectively true for everyone). We have presented this alternate view exactly as the text reports it, without the treatise explicitly adjudicating between the two; the surrounding discussion of the three kinds of suffering that follows applies regardless of which classification one adopts.

The eyelash simile is one of this tradition's most vivid images for a genuinely difficult claim: that ordinary pleasure itself is, at a deep level, a form of suffering (the suffering of formations) — not because pleasure feels bad, but because it is impermanent, conditioned, and inseparable from the underlying instability of all compounded existence, a subtlety only a trained, noble perception can actually detect, the way only an eye (not a callused palm) registers the presence of a single eyelash. We have kept the simile's own structure of contrast (palm versus eye, ordinary person versus noble one) intact, since the analogy's persuasive force depends on that exact pairing.

4. Why the Path Is Not "Suffering," and Why "Suffering" Alone Is Named, Not Pleasure

The treatise draws a striking contrast: ordinary, unawakened beings generate a genuine sense of dread only when undergoing the most extreme suffering imaginable, such as the torment of the Avīci hell — whereas the noble ones experience that same sense of dread even with respect to the exceedingly subtle, seemingly untroubled aggregates found at the very peak of existence, the most refined state within the entire formless realm. This raises an objection: since the truth of the path is itself conditioned, should it not likewise be classified under the suffering of formations? The treatise answers no — what defines the suffering of formations is precisely that it runs contrary to the noble mind, and the noble path is the very opposite of this, since it is precisely what brings about the complete exhaustion of suffering rather than opposing the mind that perceives it. As for the familiar formula that regards all conditioned things as suffering and nirvāṇa alone as peaceful stillness, this too proceeds in two steps — first seeing the relevant dharmas as suffering, then afterward observing their cessation as peaceful — and so the word "conditioned" in that formula should be understood to mean specifically what is contaminated, not the uncontaminated path, which, though technically conditioned, is excluded from this reckoning.

Chinese Source

以諸愚夫於無間獄受劇苦蘊生苦怖心，不如眾聖於有頂蘊。道諦亦應是行苦攝，有為性故。道諦非苦，違逆聖心是行苦相。非聖道起違逆聖心，由此能引眾苦盡故。若觀諸有為涅槃寂靜者，亦由先見彼法是苦、後觀彼滅以為寂靜，故有為言唯顯有漏。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ordinary, unawakened beings generate a fearful sense of suffering only when undergoing the extreme suffering of the aggregates in the Avīci hell of unremitting torment — a fear the noble ones experience even with respect to the exceedingly subtle aggregates found at the very peak of existence. Should not the truth of the path likewise be included within the suffering of formations, since it too has the nature of being conditioned? The truth of the path is not suffering, since running contrary to the noble mind is the defining mark of the suffering of formations, and the arising of the noble path does not run contrary to the noble mind — indeed, it is precisely this path that is capable of bringing about the complete exhaustion of every kind of suffering. As for the formula that regards all conditioned things as suffering and nirvāṇa alone as peaceful stillness, this too proceeds by first seeing those dharmas as suffering, and only afterward observing their cessation as peaceful stillness; hence the word "conditioned," as used in that formula, refers specifically to what is contaminated.

A further question follows: if it is granted that certain dharmas do genuinely involve pleasure, why does the sūtra speak only of suffering, never of pleasure, as a noble truth? One explanation holds that pleasure is simply too rare to warrant its own name here — just as a heap containing mostly black beans with a small admixture of green beans is still called, without qualification, "a heap of black beans," the rare exception being absorbed into the dominant majority's name. Indeed, what wise person, feeling a slight sense of relief from pouring water over a festering boil, would seriously conclude that the boil itself is thereby a source of genuine pleasure? Other teachers offer a further, verse-based account: pleasure is called suffering because it can itself serve as a cause of suffering, because it gathers together many sufferings in its wake, and because there is real suffering simply in longing for it. The treatise's own preferred resolution goes further still: properly speaking, the noble ones observe that the very substance of ordinary existence and of pleasure are, at bottom, one and the same suffering — sharing, from the standpoint of the suffering of formations, a single underlying flavor — and it is precisely for this reason that suffering alone, and not pleasure, is established as the noble truth.

Chinese Source

若諸法中亦許有樂，何緣但說苦為聖諦？有一類釋：由樂少故。如置綠豆烏豆聚中，以少從多名烏豆聚。誰有智者瀝水澆癰有少樂生，計癰為樂？有餘於此以頌釋言：能為苦因故，能集眾苦故，有苦希彼故，說樂亦名苦。理實應言，聖者觀察諸有及樂體皆是苦，以就行苦同一味故，由此立苦為諦非樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

If it is granted that certain dharmas do indeed involve genuine pleasure, why does the sūtra speak only of suffering, and not of pleasure, as the noble truth? One explanation holds that this is because pleasure is comparatively rare — just as a heap containing both green beans and black beans, with far more black beans than green, is simply called a heap of black beans, the minority element being absorbed into the majority's name. Indeed, what wise person, feeling a slight sense of relief from pouring water over a festering boil, would on that basis conclude the boil itself is a source of pleasure? Other teachers offer a further verse in explanation: because it is capable of serving as a cause of suffering; because it gathers together many sufferings; and because there is suffering in the very longing for it — pleasure, too, is for these reasons called suffering. In truth, it should rather be said that the noble ones observe that the substance of both existence and of pleasure alike is suffering, since, considered from the standpoint of the suffering of formations, they share one and the same single flavor — and it is on this basis that suffering alone, not pleasure, is established as the noble truth.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the two vivid images offered here as competing (or complementary) explanations for why pleasure gets no name of its own among the noble truths: the "mostly black beans" heap, suggesting pleasure is simply too statistically minor to deserve separate billing, and the "water on a festering boil," suggesting that what feels like pleasure is really only the temporary and partial relief of an underlying, ongoing suffering rather than a positive good in its own right. The treatise does not clearly rank these explanations against each other or against its own final formulation (that pleasure and suffering share one flavor from the standpoint of the suffering of formations) — we have presented all three as the text offers them, in sequence, without imposing an order of preference the passage itself does not state.

We should also note this section is building toward a substantial and historically significant debate — whether pleasure has any real, independent existence at all, or whether "pleasure" is always, at bottom, nothing more than a lesser degree of suffering — which the treatise takes up explicitly in the section immediately following this one, citing rival scriptural and logical arguments on both sides. We have not anticipated that debate's content here, since the text itself has not yet opened it at this point in the passage.

5. Does Pleasure Really Exist? A Direct Debate

The treatise now confronts the question directly: does pleasure have any real existence of its own, or is every feeling, without exception, actually a form of suffering? Some teachers of other schools argue for the latter, appealing to both scripture and reasoning. Scripturally, they cite the Buddha's own words that "all feelings whatsoever are nothing but suffering," that one "should observe pleasurable feeling as suffering," and that "to regard suffering as pleasure is called an inversion." Logically, they argue that every supposed cause of pleasure is unreliable: things like clothing, food, warmth, or coolness, generally accepted as causes of pleasure, produce suffering instead if enjoyed at the wrong time or in excess — and even a merely inappropriately timed, otherwise moderate indulgence becomes a cause of suffering, revealing that such things are, at bottom, always causes of suffering whose true nature simply becomes visible once the suffering involved grows intense enough. On this view, a sensation of pleasure arises only precisely when some prior suffering is being counteracted or exchanged for relief — one never feels pleasure from a supposed "cause of pleasure" except when already afflicted by hunger, thirst, cold, fatigue, or desire — so what the ordinary person mistakes for pleasure is really nothing more than the exchange of a heavier suffering for a lighter one, exactly like shifting a heavy load momentarily from one aching shoulder to the other.

By both scripture and reasoning. How by scripture? As the Blessed One says: all feelings whatsoever are nothing but suffering. A further sūtra states: you should observe pleasurable feeling as suffering. A further sūtra states: to regard suffering as pleasure is called an inversion. How by reasoning? Because every cause of pleasure is unreliable. That is, things such as clothing, food and drink, warmth, and coolness, which sentient beings generally accept as causes of pleasure, will instead produce suffering if enjoyed at the wrong time or to excess, thereby becoming a cause of suffering rather than remaining a cause of pleasure. Even at a moderate degree of use, if the timing alone is wrong, it likewise becomes a cause of suffering capable of producing suffering. From this it can be known that things like clothing are, at bottom, always causes of suffering, whose true character as such simply becomes apparent once the suffering they produce grows sufficiently intense. The same reasoning should apply to changing one's bodily posture. Moreover, a sensation of pleasure arises only precisely when one is counteracting some suffering, and only when that suffering is thereby exchanged for relief does the sensation of pleasure come about — that is, when one has not yet been afflicted by sufferings such as hunger, thirst, cold, heat, fatigue, or desire, one does not generate a sensation of pleasure with respect to a supposed cause of pleasure. Hence, with respect to whatever counteracts a weighty cause of suffering, the ordinary, unawakened person falsely imagines that this generates pleasure — but in truth, there is no fixed, reliable cause capable of generating pleasure as such. In the mere exchange of one suffering for a lesser one, the ordinary person mistakenly calls this pleasure, just as one shifts a heavy load momentarily from one shoulder to the other. Hence feeling is nothing but suffering; there is definitely no real pleasure.

The masters of Abhidharma reject this conclusion, holding instead that pleasure genuinely exists — and this treatise sides with them. Their argument begins by turning the question back on the opposing view: by what standard do its proponents even define "suffering" in the first place? If suffering consists in being oppressed and afflicted, then, since a state of ease and comfort likewise exists, pleasure should equally be established as real by that same logic; if suffering consists in harm and injury, benefit and enrichment should equally establish pleasure; if suffering consists in what is unwanted, what is genuinely wanted and pleasing should equally establish pleasure. An objection follows: perhaps the "pleasing" object's own nature isn't truly fixed, since when the noble ones become free of defilement, what was formerly pleasing to them becomes unpleasing — showing that "pleasing" was never a real, intrinsic feature to begin with. The treatise rejects this: when the noble ones become free of defilement with respect to something pleasant, this happens because they come to observe it through a different mode — as a basis for heedlessness, or as something that, once effort reveals its impermanence and decay, is no longer to be prized — not because the feeling's own intrinsic character has actually changed. If that feeling's intrinsic nature were unpleasing to begin with, no one could ever have generated desire toward it in the first place, and the noble ones would have had no need to work at seeing through it to feel revulsion. Since desire clearly can and does arise toward it, and since real effort is needed to see past it, pleasant feeling must genuinely possess its own real, intrinsic pleasantness.

When the noble ones become free of defilement, it is because they come to observe it, through a different approach, as unwanted. That is, if a feeling is, by its own intrinsic characteristic, genuinely pleasing, that feeling never actually becomes not-pleasing. Rather, when the noble ones become free of defilement, they come to be revolted by this same feeling through a different mode of observation — observing that it is a basis for heedlessness, and that it must, through great effort, be seen as impermanent and subject to decay, and is therefore not to be prized — but this does not mean its own intrinsic characteristic is itself unpleasing. If that feeling's own nature were itself unpleasing, no one should ever be capable of generating desire toward it. And if no one ever generated desire toward it, the noble ones, at the point of becoming free of defilement, would have no need to observe pleasant feeling through some further mode in order to generate deep revulsion toward it. Hence, by its own intrinsic characteristic, real pleasant feeling does indeed exist.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that this is a genuine, sustained inter-school debate that the treatise resolves with a clear verdict, unlike some of the open scholastic disagreements preserved elsewhere in this project — the treatise explicitly sides with the Abhidharma masters' position that pleasure genuinely exists, against the unnamed "other schools'" position that all feeling is, at bottom, only suffering (a view that — while the text does not say so explicitly here — anticipates positions associated with the Sautrāntika tradition elsewhere in Abhidharma literature). We have translated both sides' full arguments faithfully before presenting the treatise's chosen resolution, rather than only summarizing the losing position.

The "heavy load shifted from shoulder to shoulder" simile is a genuinely effective image for the skeptical position: momentary relief from a burden is not the same as positive comfort, since the shoulder will ache again soon regardless. The rebuttal's counter-argument is correspondingly subtle: it does not directly refute this specific image, but instead argues indirectly, from the very fact that desire arises toward pleasant things and that real spiritual effort is needed to see past that desire — reasoning that an intrinsically unpleasant thing could never have inspired desire in the first place, so pleasure's reality is inferred from the existence and difficulty of overcoming attachment to it, rather than from any positive phenomenological description of pleasure itself.

6. Reconciling "All Feeling Is Suffering" with Pleasure's Real Existence

Having established that pleasure genuinely exists, the treatise must now account for the Buddha's own scriptural statement that "all feelings whatsoever are nothing but suffering." The Buddha himself, in a sūtra addressed to Ānanda, supplies the resolution: he explains that he spoke those words with a hidden intention, based specifically on the fact that all formations are impermanent and all conditioned things undergo change and decay — not as a literal claim that every feeling is, by its own intrinsic nature, the suffering-of-suffering (that is, painful feeling as such). The treatise offers a striking piece of textual evidence for this reading: if "all feeling is suffering" had been meant literally, Ānanda would have had no reason to ask, as he does, what hidden intention lay behind that statement given that the Buddha elsewhere plainly taught three distinct kinds of feeling (pleasure, suffering, and neither) — instead, he would have had to ask what hidden intention lay behind the three-feelings teaching. Since the sūtra's actual question-and-answer runs the opposite direction, the three-feelings teaching must be the literal, direct account, and "all feeling is suffering" the specially-intended one.

As for the Blessed One's statement that all feelings whatsoever are nothing but suffering — the Buddha himself provides the resolution, as a sūtra records. The Buddha told Ānanda: I said this with a hidden intention, based on the fact that all formations are impermanent, and all conditioned things undergo change and decay. From this it can be known that this sūtra's statement is not made with reference to the suffering of suffering as such. If it were meant literally, by intrinsic characteristic, that all feeling is suffering, why would Ānanda have needed to ask: the Buddha, in other sūtras, spoke of three kinds of feeling — pleasure, suffering, and neither-suffering-nor-pleasure. With what hidden intention does this sūtra now state that all feelings whatsoever are nothing but suffering? Ānanda ought instead to have asked with what hidden intention the Buddha spoke of three kinds of feeling, and the Blessed One ought correspondingly to have answered only that it was with such-and-such hidden intention that he spoke of three kinds of feeling. But since the sūtra contains no such question and answer in that direction, it must instead be, by intrinsic characteristic, that three kinds of feeling truly and really exist. Since the Blessed One himself explicitly stated that he spoke with a hidden intention when he said that all feelings whatsoever are nothing but suffering, this itself already shows that this particular sūtra statement was made with a special intention, and is not a statement of the final, definitive truth.

The treatise then addresses the sūtra instruction to "observe pleasurable feeling as suffering," explaining that this reveals pleasurable feeling has two natures at once: a genuinely pleasurable nature, since, considered according to its own intrinsic characteristic, it is indeed pleasing; and a suffering nature, since, considered from a different angle, it is also impermanent and subject to change and decay. Observing it as pleasure invites bondage, since those prone to craving relish its flavor in just that way, whereas observing it as suffering leads toward liberation, since this frees one from craving — and it is precisely because observing it as suffering brings liberation that the Buddha urges this practice, not because pleasure lacks its own genuine, intrinsic pleasantness. A verse confirms this: the buddhas, in their perfect awakening, know that all formations are impermanent and all conditioned things subject to change, and it is on this basis — not by denying feeling's own character — that they say all feeling is suffering. Similarly, the sūtra statement that "to regard suffering as pleasure is an inversion" targets a different error entirely: it refers to how ordinary beings, faced with pleasurable feelings and sensory desires that in truth involve little pleasure and much suffering, one-sidedly regard them as purely and simply pleasurable, ignoring their suffering-nature altogether — that one-sided view is the inversion, not the mere fact of experiencing genuine pleasure. If, by intrinsic characteristic, all feeling really were suffering without exception, there would be no reason for the Buddha to have spoken of three distinct kinds of feeling in the first place; and the suggestion that he merely spoke that way in accordance with worldly convention fails, since he himself distinguished which of his statements were spoken with hidden intention and which were spoken exactly as things truly are.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the treatise's hermeneutic method here as worth making explicit: rather than treating every scriptural statement as equally literal, it distinguishes statements made with a "hidden intention" (密意, an indirect, situationally-motivated teaching) from statements that are "definitive" or "as things truly are" (了義/如實), and argues from the internal structure of a specific sūtra dialogue (the precise direction of Ānanda's question) to determine which category a given passage belongs to. This is a genuine interpretive technique this tradition uses elsewhere in Buddhist scriptural exegesis, not an ad hoc move invented just for this passage — though we note the treatise's argument depends on taking the Ānanda dialogue's exact wording as decisive evidence, a inference some readers might find more suggestive than strictly conclusive.

We should also note the recurring, careful distinction this passage draws between a feeling's own intrinsic character (自相) and how it appears when considered "from a different angle" (異門) — pleasurable feeling is said to be genuinely pleasant in itself, while also being suffering when viewed through the lens of its impermanence; the practical instruction to "observe pleasure as suffering" is framed as a skillful shift of vantage point for the sake of liberation, not a denial that the first, intrinsic description was ever true. We have tried to preserve this two-level structure clearly, since collapsing it into a single flat claim ("pleasure is really suffering") would misrepresent the treatise's own more nuanced position.

7. Finishing the Rebuttal: The Five Feeling-Faculties, and Why Causes of Pleasure Are Not "Unreliable"

The treatise adds a further scriptural point: with respect to observing the five feeling-faculties, the Buddha speaks exactly as things truly are. A sūtra states that whatever is the pleasure-faculty and whatever is the joy-faculty should both be understood as pleasurable feeling, and that observing these five faculties with right wisdom permanently severs the three fetters — language that presupposes their genuine reality, not mere convention. The treatise then presses the opposing view with a series of direct counterarguments. If, as that view holds, the Buddha merely followed worldly convention in distinguishing pleasure, joy, and equanimity to match three respective degrees of underlying suffering (lower, middling, upper), this fails on its own terms, since pleasure itself comes in degrees too — the scheme would then require, absurdly, that only the very highest pleasure ever arise from the very lowest suffering. And when one savors an especially wonderful fragrance, taste, or touch, what "lower suffering" is even present to be relieved? If one insists some lower suffering is present, then once it has already ceased, one should feel the most extreme pleasure imaginable, since no suffering at all remains — yet this plainly does not happen. Nor does the scheme hold together internally: a supposedly "lower" painful feeling is granted to be sharp and unmistakable, while a "middling" feeling is granted to be vague, which is backwards; and since the lower three meditative absorptions are said to involve pleasure and the higher stages equanimity, the scheme would absurdly require that suffering increase precisely as meditative attainment grows more refined.

If it is claimed that, since worldly beings experience three degrees of suffering — lower, upper, and middling — and correspondingly generate three sensations of pleasure, and that the Buddha, following this convention, spoke of pleasure and the others as three, this too does not hold, since pleasure itself is likewise threefold, so it should follow that only the highest degree of pleasurable sensation would arise in connection with the lowest degree of suffering. The same line of questioning applies to the experience of pleasure through sensory desire. A further scriptural point clinches the case: the Buddha told the Śākya chieftain Mahānāma that if form were purely and simply suffering, never pleasurable, and never accompanied by pleasure, and so on — a passage whose very structure presupposes that form is not, in fact, purely suffering, confirming that at least some small portion of genuinely real pleasure must exist. Having shown that neither the opponents' scriptural citations nor their logical arguments succeed, the treatise turns to directly refuting their central claim that "every cause of pleasure is unreliable." This claim misunderstands causation itself: it is only in dependence on the specific condition of the underlying basis receiving it that any external object becomes a cause of pleasure or of suffering, not the object in isolation. When the same object, reaching the same basis in the same condition, reliably produces pleasure every time that exact condition holds, the cause of pleasure is not unreliable at all — it is simply relational. Ordinary fire illustrates the same point: depending on the condition of what is being cooked, fire becomes either a cause of delicious ripeness or a cause of spoiling, and this depends not on the fire alone; when the same fire, applied to the same food in the same condition, reliably produces delicious ripeness, that cause is not unreliable either. The cause of pleasure works in exactly the same way, and its genuine determinacy is thereby established by reason.

If this same external object, upon reaching this same underlying basis in this same specific condition, is capable of serving as a cause of pleasure, it has never failed, upon reaching precisely this condition, to serve as a cause of pleasure — and therefore the cause of pleasure is not, in fact, unreliable. We should flag the fire-and-cooking analogy as a genuinely careful piece of philosophical reasoning about causation: the opposing view had argued that because the same object (food, clothing, warmth) can produce either pleasure or suffering depending on circumstances, no object is a reliable "cause" of pleasure at all. The treatise's response distinguishes an object's causal role from the full causal relation, which always includes the condition of the recipient — a cause paired with the right condition is perfectly reliable, and the earlier argument only showed that the object alone, stripped of its receiving context, is not sufficient by itself, which nobody actually disputed. We have tried to preserve this precise philosophical move rather than flattening it into a simpler claim.

We should also note that the Mahānāma sūtra citation is presented in abbreviated form ("乃至廣說," "and so on, at length"), meaning the source text itself does not quote the full passage — we have preserved this citation exactly as abbreviated, rather than supplying the sūtra's complete wording ourselves, since we have not independently verified the full text of this particular citation beyond what the treatise itself quotes.

8. Why Craving Is Named "the Origin": Seed Versus Water, Karma Versus Craving

Having established that all contaminated formations are correctly called suffering, the treatise turns to the truth of the origin. Scripture names craving alone as the origin of suffering, yet, strictly speaking, other causes — karma and ignorance among them — are also, properly, the truth of the origin; craving is simply singled out because it is the most prominent among them. A verse confirms this broader picture: karma, craving, and ignorance together serve as causes bringing about future formations and the continuity of existence, and it is this continuity that constitutes what is called a "person." A further sūtra speaks of "five kinds of seed" — merely another name for the appropriated consciousness already discussed — and of those seeds being "placed within the earth-element," merely another name for the four abodes of consciousness; such sūtra language is spoken with a hidden intention, whereas the Abhidharma speaks directly in terms of the dharmas' own true characteristics. Properly understood, the sūtra's naming of craving alone as "the origin" refers specifically to the cause of continuation, while the verse naming karma, craving, and ignorance together speaks more fully of both the cause of coming-into-being and the cause of its continuation, together with the causes underlying each.

Chinese Source

經就勝故說愛為集，理實所餘亦是集諦。如是理趣由何證知？餘契經中亦說餘故。如薄伽梵伽他中言：業愛及無明，為因招後行，令諸有相續，名補特伽羅。又契經說五種種子，此即別名說有取識。又彼經說置地界中，此即別名說四識住。故經所說是密意言，阿毘達磨依法相說。然經中說愛為集者，偏說起因。伽他中說業愛無明皆為因者，具說生起及彼因因。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sūtra names craving as the origin because it does so with reference to what is most prominent; in truth, the other causes as well are also, properly speaking, the truth of the origin. How is this principle known? Because other sūtras likewise speak of these other causes. As the Blessed One states in a verse: karma, craving, and ignorance serve as causes bringing about future formations, causing the continuity of existence — this is what is called a person. A further sūtra speaks of five kinds of seed — this is simply another name for the appropriated consciousness. That same sūtra speaks of these being placed within the earth-element — this is simply another name for the four abodes of consciousness. Hence what the sūtra states is spoken with a hidden intention, whereas the Abhidharma speaks directly according to the true characteristics of the dharmas. As for the sūtra's statement that craving alone is the origin, this speaks one-sidedly of only the cause of continuation. The verse's statement that karma, craving, and ignorance are all causes speaks fully of both the cause of coming-into-being and the cause of continuation, together with their underlying causes.

What, precisely, is meant by "coming-into-being" and by "continuation"? The actual manifestation of a distinct individual existence — differentiated by realm, destiny, and mode of birth — is called coming-into-being; the subsequent, undifferentiated continuity of that same existence going forward is called continuation. Karma and craving-for-existence serve, respectively, as the cause of each in turn, much as a seed serves as the cause of a sprout distinguished by its particular kind (rice, wheat, and so on), while water serves as the cause sustaining the continued growth of any sprout whatsoever, without regard to kind. Why is craving specifically the cause of continuation? Because, apart from craving, a future existence does not arise — among those who die possessing craving and those who die free of it, a further future existence is observed to arise only for the former, showing that continuation tracks the presence or absence of craving precisely. It is also observed that wherever craving toward some object is present, the mind's continuum repeatedly and habitually inclines toward that same object — from which it can be inferred that craving is what drives the mental continuum onward toward a future existence in general. And in the grasping of a future body, no other factor clings as tenaciously as craving does — like a paste of vetch-bean powder mixed with water and applied to the skin while bathing, which, once dried, sticks fast and resists removal more stubbornly than anything else; self-love clings to a future body in just this same tenacious way, confirming craving's role as the cause of continuation.

Chinese Source

何法名生？何法名起？界趣生等品類差別自體出現，說名為生。若無差別後有相續，說名為起。業與有愛如其次第為彼二因，譬如種子與穀麥等別種類芽為能生因、水與一切無差別芽為能起因，業及有愛為生起因應知亦爾。愛為起因何理為證？離愛後有必不起故。謂有愛離愛二俱命終，唯見有愛者後有更起。由此理證愛為起因，起有起無定隨愛故。又由愛故相續趣後，現見若於是處有愛，則心相續數趣於彼。由此比知，以有愛故能令相續馳趣後有。又取後身，更無有法封執堅著如貪愛者，如蓽豆屑於澡浴時和水塗身，至乾燥位著身難離，餘無以加。如是無有餘為因法，執取後身如我愛者，由此理證愛為起因。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by coming-into-being, and what is meant by continuation? The actual manifestation of a distinct individual entity, differentiated by realm, destiny, birth, and so forth, is called coming-into-being. The subsequent, undifferentiated continuity of that same existence is called continuation. Karma and craving-for-existence serve, respectively, as the cause of each of these two, just as a seed serves as the cause capable of generating a sprout distinguished by its particular kind, while water serves as the cause capable of sustaining the continued growth of any sprout, without distinction as to kind — so too should karma and craving-for-existence be understood as, respectively, the cause of coming-into-being and the cause of continuation. By what reasoning is craving established as the cause of continuation? Because, apart from craving, a future existence definitely does not arise — among those who die possessing craving and those who die free of craving, only in the case of the one possessing craving is a further future existence observed to arise. By this reasoning it is established that craving is the cause of continuation, since whether continuation occurs or not is determined precisely by the presence or absence of craving. Moreover, it is because of craving that the mental continuum races onward toward a future existence — it is directly observed that wherever craving is present with respect to some object, the mental continuum repeatedly inclines toward that object. By analogy, it can be inferred that it is because of craving that the mental continuum is driven onward toward a future existence. Moreover, in the grasping of a future body, there is no other dharma that clings as tenaciously as craving does — like a paste made of vetch-bean powder mixed with water and applied to the body while bathing, which, once dried, sticks fast to the body and is difficult to remove, surpassing everything else in tenacity. In just the same way, there is no other causal factor that grasps a future body as tenaciously as self-love does — and by this reasoning too, craving is established as the cause of continuation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the seed-and-water analogy as a genuinely precise piece of causal reasoning, distinguishing two different explanatory roles that easily get collapsed together in looser accounts of dependent origination: karma determines the specific kind of future rebirth (which realm, which destiny, which mode of birth — the "seed," determining rice versus wheat), while craving determines only that continuation happens at all, indifferently to which kind (the "water," sustaining any sprout whatsoever). We have preserved this two-factor structure clearly, since the passage's subsequent evidence (the dying-with-craving versus dying-without-craving observation, and the bean-paste simile) is specifically evidence for craving's narrower role as continuation's cause, not for karma's role, which the passage treats as comparatively less in need of separate proof here.

We should also note the vetch-bean paste simile (蓽豆屑, a bean-flour paste used as a period cleansing/exfoliating agent in ancient Indian bathing practice) is a concrete, culturally specific image we have kept in its original form — a paste applied wet that becomes stubbornly adhesive once dry — rather than substituting a more familiar modern analogy, since the text's own point depends on this specific physical property (fluid and easily removed while wet, but nearly impossible to remove once dried) rather than on stickiness in general.
