The Six Kinds of Arhat

Fascicle 25 continues the fourth part of Chapter 6 (分別賢聖品第六之四, Distinguishing the Noble Ones), picking up directly from fascicle 24's account of arhatship to classify the arhat into six distinct kinds by lineage — from "retrogressible," most vulnerable to falling back, through "immovable," permanently secure — including the sūtras' own vivid, sometimes unsettling psychological portrait of how each type relates to the possibility of losing what has been attained.

1. The Six Kinds of Arhat, and Their Origins

Continuing directly from the immovable-lineage arhat's transition from the knowledge of exhaustion to the knowledge of non-arising, the treatise asks whether arhats, like stream-enterers and the others, admit of further distinctions by lineage. They do: six kinds in total — retrogressible-dharma, thought-dharma, protective-dharma, stable-dharma, capable-of-penetrating-dharma, and immovable-dharma. The first five all arise from the "convinced-through-faith" nature already possessed at the stage of noble-one-in-training, and are collectively called "timely, cherished liberation of mind" (or simply "timely liberation," the fuller name abbreviated much as "ghee-pot" might be shortened to "pot"), since their liberation must wait upon favorable conditions converging — adequate provisions, freedom from illness, a suitable place — before concentration can even be entered. The sixth, immovable-dharma, arises instead from the "attained-through-seeing" nature, and is called "immovable liberation of mind," or "non-timely liberation," since its concentration manifests whenever desired, with no need to wait for anything.

Ru-Yi Translation

As already explained, the immovable-lineage arhat gives rise to the knowledge of non-arising immediately following the first knowledge of exhaustion. Do arhats, like stream-enterers and the others, likewise admit of further distinctions? Yes, indeed. What are they? Verse: there are six kinds of arhat, from retrogressible up through immovable; the first five arise from the convinced-through-faith lineage, collectively called timely liberation; the last is non-timely liberation, arising from the attained-through-seeing lineage. Explanation: the sūtras state that arhats, owing to differences in lineage, are of six kinds: retrogressible-dharma, thought-dharma, protective-dharma, stable-dharma, capable-of-penetrating-dharma, and immovable-dharma. Among these six, the first five arise from the convinced-through-faith nature already possessed at the stage of being a noble-one-in-training; these are collectively called timely, cherished liberation of mind, since they must constantly cherish and protect their attainment. They are also called timely liberation, since they must wait for suitable time in order to attain liberation — the fuller term abbreviated, just as one might shorten ghee-pot to pot. It is for this reason that they must wait for the right time to enter concentration, waiting until favorable conditions converge — adequate provisions, freedom from illness, a suitable place — before they are able to enter. The immovable-dharma nature, spoken of last, is called immovable liberation of mind, since it involves no retrogression or disturbance. It is also called non-timely liberation, since it need not wait for any particular time to attain liberation — its concentration manifests whenever desired, without needing to wait for favorable conditions to converge. Alternatively, the names timely and non-timely liberation are established with reference to temporary versus final liberation, since the former admits of times when retrogression is possible, while the latter does not; this last type arises from the attained-through-seeing nature.

Are these six lineages always already possessed beforehand, or can they also be newly attained through practice? Neither exclusively: the retrogressible-dharma lineage is always already possessed beforehand, but the remaining five — including thought-dharma — can also be newly attained afterward, through the refining of faculties from a lower starting lineage. Each of the six is defined by a distinct psychological profile. "Retrogressible-dharma" describes one who falls back from what has been attained upon encountering even the slightest adverse condition. "Thought-dharma" describes one who, fearing the loss of their attainment through retrogression, constantly contemplates ending their own life rather than face that fall. "Protective-dharma" describes one who, delighting in what has been attained, actively guards it. "Stable-dharma" describes one who does not retrogress even without actively guarding their attainment, apart from especially strong adverse conditions, yet likewise does not advance further without especially strong effort. "Capable-of-penetrating-dharma" describes one whose nature is capable of skillfully cultivating the refining of faculties, swiftly reaching the immovable stage. "Immovable-dharma," finally, describes one who will certainly never retrogress at all.

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding these six kinds of arhat-lineage just explained, are they always already possessed beforehand, or can they also be newly attained afterward? Neither exclusively — it is not fixed. How so? Verse: some are already possessed as a prior lineage; others are attained afterward through the refining of faculties. Explanation: the retrogressible-dharma lineage is always already possessed beforehand; the remaining five, including thought-dharma, can also be newly attained afterward. That is, some possess the thought-dharma nature already from the start, while others possess the retrogressible-dharma nature at first and later attain thought-dharma through the refining of faculties — and the same pattern should be understood, as appropriate, all the way up through immovable-dharma. Retrogressible-dharma means one who, upon encountering even a slight adverse condition, immediately retrogresses from what has been attained, unlike the thought-dharma type and the rest. Thought-dharma means one who, fearing the loss of their attainment through retrogression, constantly contemplates ending their own life. Protective-dharma means one who, delighting in what has been attained, actively guards and protects it. Stable-dharma means one who, apart from especially strong conditions for retrogression, does not retrogress even without actively guarding their attainment, yet, apart from especially strong effort, likewise does not advance further. Capable-of-penetrating-dharma means one whose nature is capable of skillfully cultivating the refining of faculties, swiftly reaching the immovable stage. Immovable-dharma means one who will certainly never retrogress.

Among these six lineages, at the earlier stage of being a noble-one-in-training, the first two (retrogressible and thought) both lack constant and reverent effort, distinguished from each other only by a difference in faculty; the third (protective) possesses constant effort alone; the fourth (stable) possesses reverent effort alone; the fifth (capable-of-penetrating), though possessing both kinds of effort, remains of dull faculty; the sixth (immovable), of sharp faculty, possesses both. None of these names describes a certainty rather than a mere possibility: "retrogressible" does not mean one will definitely retrogress, nor does "capable-of-penetrating" mean one will definitely reach the immovable stage — hence all six kinds of arhat can, in principle, be found across all three realms. Some teachers, however, read these names more strictly, as guaranteeing their respective outcomes; on that view, all six types exist fully only within the desire realm, while the form and formless realms contain only the "stable" and "immovable" types, since beings there lack any occasion for retrogression, self-harm, self-protective anxiety, or the cultivation of faculty-refining in the first place.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these six lineages, at the earlier stage of being a noble-one-in-training, the first two lack both constant and reverent effort, distinguished from each other by a difference in faculty. The third possesses constant effort alone. The fourth possesses reverent effort alone. The fifth possesses both, yet remains of dull faculty. The sixth, of sharp faculty, possesses both kinds of effort. The retrogressible-dharma lineage does not necessarily always retrogress, nor does the capable-of-penetrating type necessarily always reach the immovable stage — these names are established merely with reference to the possibility, not the certainty, of such outcomes. Hence, all six kinds of arhat can be found across all three realms. If, however, one holds that retrogressible necessarily means certain retrogression, and capable-of-penetrating necessarily means certain attainment of immovability, then, on this view, all six kinds exist fully only within the desire realm, while the form and formless realms contain only the stable and immovable types, since beings there lack occasion for retrogression, self-harm, self-protection, or the cultivation of faculty-refining, and so only these two types exist there.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "thought-dharma" arhat's defining trait as one of the most psychologically striking and, by modern standards, unsettling details in the entire treatise: such an arhat, out of fear of losing their attainment through retrogression, is described as constantly contemplating ending their own life rather than face falling back. We present this exactly as this classical scheme describes it, as a documented feature of this tradition's own account of arhat psychology, without endorsing the premise or resolving the evident tension between this description and Buddhist ethical teaching on preserving life generally — the text itself offers no further reflection on this tension at this point, and we have not supplied one it does not contain.

We should also note the treatise's honest acknowledgment of two competing readings of what these six names actually guarantee — a looser reading (mere possibility, allowing all six types everywhere) and a stricter reading (guaranteed outcomes, restricting four of the six types to the desire realm alone) — presented as a genuine, unresolved interpretive disagreement rather than the treatise declaring one correct. We have kept both readings visible exactly as offered.

2. Who Retrogresses From What, and Why Seeing-Abandoned Affliction Never Returns

Among the six kinds of arhat, the immovable lineage admits of no retrogression whatsoever. Among the remaining five, retrogressible-dharma alone cannot fall further from its own lineage, since that lineage already occupies the lowest position with nothing further below it; the other four (thought, protective, stable, capable-of-penetrating) can retrogress from their very lineage. All five can retrogress from their currently held fruit — but a lineage or fruit already possessed at an earlier, more fundamental stage (as an ordinary worldling, or as a noble-one-in-training, reinforced by both worldly and transcendent paths) is never lost; only a lineage newly attained later, through faculty-refining, admits of falling back. This yields a specific count of possible subsequent courses for each type after retrogression: retrogressible-dharma has three (advancing again, falling back to noble-one-in-training, or simply remaining and eventually attaining nirvāṇa from that position); thought-dharma has four (the same three, plus falling all the way back to retrogressible-dharma itself); and protective, stable, and capable-of-penetrating have five, six, and seven respectively, each gaining one further possible course. When any of these four types falls back to the stage of noble-one-in-training and later regains arhatship, they return specifically to the retrogressible-dharma lineage, never directly back to their original, higher one — regaining the original lineage directly would actually constitute an advance, not what should properly be called a retrogression.

誰從何退？為性為果？頌曰：四從種性退，五從果非先。論曰：不動種性必無退理。前之五種皆有退義，於中後四有從性退，退法一種無退性理，由此種性最居下故。五種皆有從果退義，雖俱有退然並非先。謂諸無學先學位中所住種性，彼從此性必無退理，學無學道所成堅故。若諸有學先凡位中所住種性，彼從此性亦無退理，世出世道所成堅故。若住此位後修練根所得思等四種種性，彼從此性容有退理。二先位中住思等性，必亦無退此所得果，唯先退法有退果義。又亦無退先所得果，後所得果容有退義，是故定無退預流果。由此應果退法有三：一增進根、二退住學、三住自位而般涅槃。思法有四，三如前說，更加一種退住退性。餘三如次有五六七，應知後後一一增故。思法等四退住學位，時還住退非餘。若異此者，得勝種性故，應是進非退。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these six kinds of arhat, who retrogresses from what — from their lineage, or merely from their fruit? Verse: four retrogress from their very lineage; five retrogress from their fruit, but never from what came before. Explanation: the immovable lineage certainly admits of no retrogression. The preceding five kinds all admit of the possibility of retrogression; among these, the latter four can retrogress from their very lineage itself, while the retrogressible-dharma type alone has no lineage left to retrogress from, since this lineage already occupies the very lowest position. All five admit of the possibility of retrogressing from their fruit, though, even where retrogression occurs, it is never from what came before. The lineage that a now-arhat had already possessed at the earlier stage of being a noble-one-in-training — from that lineage, such a person cannot retrogress, since it has been made firm by both the path of training and the path of no-more-learning. Likewise, the lineage a noble-one-in-training had already possessed at the still-earlier stage of being an ordinary worldling cannot be retrogressed from, since it has been made firm by both worldly and transcendent paths. However, the lineage of thought-dharma and the other three, when newly attained through the refining of faculties, does admit of the possibility of retrogression. For one who already possessed the thought-dharma nature or the like at either of the two earlier stages, such a person certainly cannot retrogress from the fruit thereby attained — retrogression from the fruit applies only to one whose original lineage was retrogressible-dharma. Moreover, one can never retrogress from an earlier-attained fruit; only the later-attained fruit admits of retrogression, and it is for this reason that one can never retrogress from the fruit of stream-entry. On this basis, a retrogressible-dharma arhat has three possible subsequent courses: advancing further, retrogressing to noble-one-in-training, or remaining at their current position and eventually attaining nirvāṇa. The thought-dharma type has four courses, the same three plus retrogressing all the way to retrogressible-dharma. The remaining three types have, respectively, five, six, and seven possible courses, each successive type gaining one more. When the thought-dharma type and the other three retrogress to noble-one-in-training and later regain arhatship, they return specifically to the retrogressible lineage, nothing else — were this not so, it would actually constitute an advance, not a retrogression.

Why is it that one can never retrogress from an earlier-attained, lower fruit specifically? Because what is severed by seeing is grounded in something with no real basis at all: self-view takes a presumed "self" as its foundation, and every seeing-abandoned affliction has this view as its root — yet since "self" does not actually exist, these afflictions are grounded in nothing real, and it is precisely for this reason that there is no possibility of ever retrogressing back into them. This might seem to suggest such afflictions take "nothing" as their object, but this is not so — their object is the truths themselves (the aggregates mistakenly grasped as self), which genuinely exist, even though the affliction grasps them inaccurately. Every affliction, in some sense, fails to grasp its object accurately — yet a real difference remains: cultivation-abandoned afflictions (like greed or hatred toward something pleasing or displeasing) target a characteristic genuinely present, at least in some sense, in the object itself, while seeing-abandoned afflictions (self-view, extreme views) impose a "self" that the object never possessed in the first place, making them uniquely "groundless" in a way cultivation-abandoned afflictions are not.

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is it that one can certainly never retrogress from an earlier-attained fruit? Because what is severed by seeing is grounded in no real basis. Self-view operates by taking a presumed self as its basis; the afflictions abandoned by seeing all have this view as their root. Since the substance of self does not actually exist, these are said to be grounded in no real basis, and, precisely because they lack any real basis, there is certainly no possibility of retrogressing into them. If this is so, one ought to say that these afflictions take nothing as their object — no, that is not so; their object is not literally nothing, since the truths themselves genuinely serve as their object. However, with respect to the object of the truths, this affliction fails to grasp it accurately — but is this not equally true of every affliction? Although this is true of all afflictions alike, there remains a real difference: cultivation-abandoned afflictions each have their own distinct basis, namely, being pleasing or displeasing and so forth, and this characteristic is genuinely not absent from the object they take. Seeing-abandoned afflictions, by contrast, imagine there to be a self, yet the object of the truths does not actually possess any characteristic of self at all, and it is precisely because of this complete lack of any real basis that seeing-abandoned afflictions differ from cultivation-abandoned ones. With respect to an object such as form, self-view falsely superimposes an agent, an experiencer, a sovereign controller operating there, none of which reflects the object's true nature. Extreme views and the rest arise following upon this same self-view, and so are likewise described as grounded in no real basis. Cultivation-abandoned greed, hatred, conceit, and delusion, by contrast, merely give rise to attachment, aversion, arrogance, and confusion with respect to a genuinely present characteristic, and so are described as grounded in a real basis.

A further, complementary explanation appeals to how these two kinds of affliction actually arise. Cultivation-abandoned affliction arises without careful deliberation, being dull and obscure in nature; seeing-abandoned affliction arises only through careful deliberation, being reasoned and inferential in nature. A noble one who fails to deliberate carefully can indeed lapse into a momentary mindfulness-failure with respect to coarse, everyday matters — much as one might mistake a coiled rope for a snake only in a careless, unreflective glance — but this never happens when deliberation is careful. Hence a noble one can experience retrogression with respect to cultivation-abandoned affliction, which can slip in without careful thought, but never with respect to seeing-abandoned affliction, since that requires genuine, deliberate reasoning to arise at all — and a noble one who deliberates carefully will simply see the truth-principle correctly every time. It is for this reason that a noble one's severance by seeing admits of absolutely no possibility of retrogression.

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, seeing-abandoned afflictions grasp, with respect to the principle of the truths, notions such as self, what belongs to self, annihilation, and permanence, yet the truths themselves contain not the slightest trace of any such self — and seeing-abandoned greed and the rest arise by taking these false notions as object, hence they are all called afflictions grounded in no real basis. Cultivation-abandoned afflictions, by contrast, regard objects such as form as beautiful or ugly, yet such objects are not entirely without at least some genuine trace of such distinctions, hence these can rightly be called afflictions grounded in a real basis. Moreover, seeing-abandoned afflictions arise through confusion regarding the principle of the truths, hence grounded in no real basis; cultivation-abandoned afflictions arise through confusion regarding coarser, more concrete matters, hence grounded in a real basis. The principle of the truths is genuinely real and provides a fixed basis to rely upon — once a noble one's wisdom has realized it, there is certainly no possibility of retrogression. Superficial phenomena, by contrast, are unstable and provide no fixed basis, and so, even after severing the confusion concerning them, a lapse of mindfulness leading to retrogression can still occur. Alternatively: cultivation-abandoned afflictions arise without careful deliberation, being dull in nature; seeing-abandoned afflictions arise through careful deliberation, being reasoned in nature. A noble one who fails to deliberate carefully can lapse into a mindfulness-failure regarding coarse matters, but not when deliberation is careful, just as one might mistake a rope for a snake only in a careless glance. Hence noble ones can experience retrogression with respect to cultivation-abandoned affliction, but seeing-abandoned affliction can never arise merely through carelessness. If a noble one deliberates carefully, they simply see the truth-principle correctly, hence a noble one's severance by seeing certainly admits of no retrogression.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "grounded in no real basis versus grounded in a real basis" distinction as a genuinely subtle piece of philosophical reasoning explaining an otherwise puzzling asymmetry: why can a noble one backslide on cultivation-abandoned affliction (still feeling occasional anger or craving) but never on seeing-abandoned affliction (never again believing in a literal self)? The treatise's answer locates the difference not in the intensity or type of affliction but in what each affliction actually targets — a genuinely present, if impermanent, quality (pleasant, unpleasant) versus a complete fiction (a self that never existed at all) — reinforced by a second, complementary explanation appealing to the deliberate, reasoned nature of self-view specifically, which cannot simply "slip back in" the way a careless emotional reaction can.

We should also note the treatise offers two independent explanations for this same phenomenon (the real-basis distinction, and the careful-deliberation distinction) without indicating whether one supersedes the other or whether both are meant to hold together as complementary accounts — we have presented both exactly as given, in the sequence the text offers them, without forcing a single unified explanation the passage itself does not provide.

3. The Sautrāntika Position: Arhatship Itself Never Retrogresses

Against the Vaibhāṣika account just given, the Sautrāntika masters hold that there is no possibility of retrogression from arhatship itself, for any of the six lineages — a position the treatise judges to accord with reason, supported by both scripture and argument. Scripturally, one sūtra states that severance of affliction by noble wisdom is "true severance"; another states that the Buddha counsels vigilance for noble-ones-in-training specifically, not for arhats. Even the sūtra in which the Buddha tells Ānanda that "gain and honor can also obstruct an arhat" never states that the arhat retrogresses from the fruit itself — only that they lose their "joyful abiding in the present life," a temporary meditative attainment distinct from the fruit. A further sūtra states outright that there is no cause or condition by which one retrogresses from "immovable liberation of mind, personally realized within the body." As for the sūtra's mention of "timely, cherished liberation" (seemingly implying instability), the Sautrāntika masters grant this much is true, but insist what actually retrogresses there is the root-level dhyāna-concentration itself — which must wait for favorable conditions to manifest (hence "timely") and is repeatedly hoped for (hence "cherished") — never the arhat-fruit's own essential nature, which, once attained, accompanies the person constantly and needs no further seeking.

4. The "Charcoal Simile" Sūtra, and the Vaibhāṣika's Final Word

A further objection presses the Sautrāntika position with the "charcoal simile" sūtra, which states that "a learned noble disciple, whether walking or standing still, may, at certain places and times, lose mindfulness and thereby give rise to evil, unwholesome thoughts" — seemingly describing a genuine lapse in someone already identified elsewhere in the same sūtra as an arhat, since it goes on to describe that same person's mind as having, "for a long night of practice, been inclined toward seclusion," up to "approaching entry into nirvāṇa," language a further sūtra explicitly calls "the power of a worthy one," and describes that person as having "already permanently expelled all that conduces to contamination" and "already attained coolness" — unmistakably an arhat. The Sautrāntika reply distinguishes the sūtra's timeline: it is true that the person ultimately described is an arhat, but the passage describing the earlier lapse of mindfulness refers to a time before that same person had yet fully and thoroughly penetrated the teaching — while still a noble-one-in-training, walking or standing, a lapse of mindfulness giving rise to affliction remains genuinely possible; but once that same person has become one beyond training, no such arising is possible any longer. The lapse described belongs to the earlier, training stage of that person's own life story, not to their later arhatship — resolving the apparent contradiction without requiring the arhat-fruit itself to admit of retrogression.

5. Three Kinds of Retrogression: Why Even the Buddha Has One

Do only arhats possess six lineages, or do noble-ones-in-training and ordinary worldlings possess six as well? They do, since the six kinds of worthy one derive from these as their prior basis. However, faculty-refining never occurs during the path of seeing itself, since that swift, unbroken stage allows no room for the necessary preparatory effort to arise — only at the stage of an ordinary worldling already convinced through faith, before ever entering the path of seeing, can one cultivate this refinement in advance, just as an arhat can refine faculties after the fact at the stage of no-more-learning. This raises a further puzzle: a sūtra states that any of the four kinds of superior "joyful abiding in the present life" may admit of retrogression, yet also states flatly that there is no cause or condition by which one retrogresses from "immovable liberation of mind, personally realized within the body." How can the immovable-dharma type retrogress from joyful abiding at all, if immovable-dharma admits no retrogression whatsoever?

Retrogression, properly understood, is of three distinct kinds: "retrogression from what has been attained" (losing a superior quality already possessed); "retrogression from what has not yet been attained" (being unable to attain a superior quality one otherwise could have reached); and "retrogression of enjoyment" (a superior quality already attained simply failing to manifest in the present moment, without the underlying attainment itself being lost). The Buddha possesses only this third kind, since, possessing every quality in complete fullness, it is simply not possible for all of them to manifest simultaneously at every instant — leaving room for a given quality to go temporarily unexpressed without this constituting any true loss or deficiency. This resolves the apparent tension entirely: the immovable-dharma arhat can indeed "retrogress" from joyful abiding in the present life in this same harmless, merely-non-manifesting sense, without ever actually losing the underlying, permanently secure attainment the sūtra insists cannot be lost.

6. A Coda on Retrogression, and Why No One Dies Mid-Fall

Before moving on, the treatise closes out the threefold retrogression scheme with two brief points. First, the immovable-dharma arhat (apart from the Buddha) turns out to possess two of the three kinds of retrogression after all — retrogression of enjoyment and retrogression from what has not yet been attained — since even an immovable-dharma arhat has not yet attained qualities superior to their own; only the remaining five, duller lineages can possess all three kinds, including retrogression from what has already been attained. This is why saying the immovable-dharma type can "retrogress" from joyful abiding in the present life creates no real contradiction with the earlier claim that immovable liberation never retrogresses — different senses of "retrogression" are simply in play. Second, the treatise notes in passing that the Sautrāntika "no retrogression" theorists from the earlier debate would simply call every uncontaminated liberation "immovable" in the first place; as for why a separate, sixth "immovable-dharma" lineage is established at all under their view, that has already been addressed in the resolution given earlier and need not be raised again as a fresh objection.

Having established that faculty-refining can produce a superior lineage at both the trainee and non-trainee stages, the treatise now asks a narrower, technical question: exactly how many uninterrupted paths and liberation paths does the actual process of refining involve, what nature do these paths belong to, and what body and meditative ground do they depend upon?

Chinese Source

如上所言，有練根得無學有學，正練根時各幾無間、幾解脫道？何性攝？何所依？頌曰： 練根無學位， 九無間解脫， 久習故學一， 無漏依人三， 無學依九地， 有學但依六， 捨果勝果道， 唯得果道故。 論曰：求勝種性修練根者，無學位中轉一一性各九無間、九解脫道，如得應果。所以者何？彼鈍根性由久串習，非少功力可能令轉，學無學道所成堅故。有學位中轉一一性，各一無間、一解脫道，如得初果，上相違故。彼加行道諸位各一。如是無間及解脫道，一切唯是無漏性攝。聖者必無用有漏道而轉根理，非增上故。依謂身地，此所依身唯人三洲，餘無退故。此所依地，無學通九，謂未至、中間、四定、三無色。有學唯六，謂除後三。所以者何？夫轉根者，容有捨果及勝果道，所得唯果非向道故。無有學果無色地攝，故學練根但依六地。

Ru-Yi Translation

As stated above, faculty-refining yields results at both the non-trainee and trainee stages. When actually refining the faculties, how many uninterrupted paths and how many liberation paths does each conversion involve? What nature do they belong to? What do they depend upon? Verse: faculty-refining at the non-trainee stage takes nine uninterrupted and nine liberation paths, owing to long habituation; at the trainee stage, one each — both uncontaminated, depending on the three continents' humans. Non-trainees depend on nine grounds; trainees depend on only six. Both involve either relinquishing a fruit or the path of a superior fruit, since only the fruit itself, not the path leading toward it, is what is thereby attained. Explanation: one who seeks a superior lineage and cultivates faculty-refining, at the non-trainee stage, converts from each nature to the next through nine uninterrupted paths and nine liberation paths in every case, just as in attaining arhatship. Why is this? Because that dull-faculty nature, having been habituated over a long period, cannot be converted through only a small amount of effort — it has been made firm by both the path of training and the path of no-more-learning. At the trainee stage, converting from each nature takes only one uninterrupted path and one liberation path in every case, just as in attaining the first fruit, the opposite situation from above. The preparatory path, at each of these stages, likewise counts as only one. All these uninterrupted and liberation paths, without exception, belong exclusively to the uncontaminated nature — a noble one certainly never uses a contaminated path to convert their faculties, since a contaminated path lacks the necessary dominant power to do so. "Depend upon" refers to the body and the ground. As for the body depended upon, it is exclusively that of humans of the three continents, since beings elsewhere cannot retrogress and so have no occasion for this. As for the ground depended upon: for non-trainees, it extends through all nine grounds — the stage of not-yet-attained, the intermediate dhyāna, the four dhyānas, and three of the formless spheres. Trainees depend on only six, excluding the latter three. Why is this? Because one who converts faculties may either relinquish a lower fruit or attain a superior fruit's path, but what is thereby attained is only the fruit itself, never merely the path leading toward it — and no trainee-fruit belongs to the formless-realm grounds, hence a trainee's faculty-refining depends only on six grounds.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag this section as one of the treatise's driest and most purely technical passages — a bookkeeping exercise in how many path-moments a given conversion takes, rather than a philosophical argument — and have translated it correspondingly literally rather than smoothing over its enumerative style. The core intuition is nonetheless clear enough: a non-trainee's faculty-refining must overcome faculties habituated across the entire training path, hence nine hard-won steps (mirroring the nine-graded severance that produces arhatship itself), while a trainee's faculty-refining, not yet so deeply set, takes only one.

We should also note the closing restriction — that trainees' faculty-refining depends on only six of the nine grounds, excluding the three lower formless spheres — rests on the stated premise that "no trainee-fruit belongs to the formless-realm grounds." The treatise offers this as a settled fact without further defending it here; we have presented it exactly as given rather than reconstructing the fuller argument (likely resting on material from earlier fascicles' treatment of how the trainee fruits are attained) that this passage assumes but does not repeat.

8. The Nine Kinds of Non-Trainee: Seven Śrāvakas and Two Awakened Ones

Shifting from the mechanics of faculty-refining to a broader census, the treatise asks how many distinct kinds of individual occupy the non-trainee stage altogether, and what accounts for the differences among them.

Chinese Source

諸無學位補特伽羅，總有幾種？由何差別？頌曰： 七聲聞二佛， 差別由九根。 論曰：居無學位聖者有九，謂七聲聞及二覺者。退法等五不動分二，後先別故，名七聲聞。獨覺、大覺名二覺者。由下下等九品根異，令無學聖成九差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among individuals at the non-trainee stage, how many kinds are there in total, and by what basis do they differ? Verse: seven kinds of śrāvaka and two kinds of buddha; the distinction rests on nine grades of faculty. Explanation: noble ones abiding at the non-trainee stage number nine in total — the seven kinds of śrāvaka and the two kinds of awakened one. Retrogressible-dharma and the four kinds following it, together with immovable-dharma divided into two (owing to the distinction between the type attained earlier, directly from the start, and the type attained later, through faculty-refining), make seven kinds of śrāvaka. Pratyekabuddhas and the greatly-awakened one, the Buddha, are called the two kinds of awakened one. It is owing to the nine grades of faculty, from lowest-of-the-low on upward, that the non-trainee noble ones come to differ into these nine kinds.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag how this passage folds the six lineages discussed throughout this fascicle into a wider, nine-part census: the five lower lineages (retrogressible through capable-of-penetrating) plus immovable-dharma split into its two sources of origin (native attainment through the seeing-path versus later attainment through faculty-refining, as established in section 1) together make seven "śrāvaka" grades, to which pratyekabuddhas and the Buddha are then added as two further, higher grades — all nine mapped onto an ascending scale of faculty sharpness. We have kept the passage's own bare enumeration here rather than speculating about the finer content of each grade, since the text itself moves on immediately without elaborating further.

9. The Seven Noble Ones, and Why They Reduce to Six Real Kinds

The treatise now turns from the arhat's own sixfold lineage to a wider classification spanning both trainees and non-trainees together: the seven kinds of noble one that, between them, include every noble person without exception — faith-follower, dharma-follower, convinced-through-faith, attained-through-seeing, body-witness, wisdom-liberated, and both-liberated. It then asks what basis distinguishes these seven, and how many genuinely distinct kinds actually underlie the seven names.

Chinese Source

學無學位有七聖者，一切聖者皆此中攝：一隨信行、二隨法行、三信解、四見至、五身證、六慧解脫、七俱解脫。依何立七？事別有幾？頌曰： 加行根滅定， 解脫故成七， 此事別唯六， 三道各二故。 論曰：依加行異立初二種，謂依先時隨他及法於所求義修加行故，立隨信行、隨法行名。依根不同立次二種，謂依鈍利信慧根增，如次名為信解、見至。依得滅定立身證名，由身證得滅盡定故。依解脫異立後二種，謂依唯慧離煩惱障者立慧解脫，依兼得定離解脫障者立俱解脫。此名雖七事別唯六，謂見道中有二聖者，一隨信行、二隨法行，此至修道別立二名，一信解、二見至，此至無學復立二名，謂時解脫、不時解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among trainees and non-trainees, there are seven kinds of noble one, within which every noble one without exception is included: faith-follower, dharma-follower, convinced-through-faith, attained-through-seeing, body-witness, wisdom-liberated, and both-liberated. On what basis are these seven established, and how many distinct substances do they involve? Verse: by preparatory effort, by faculty, by attaining cessation-concentration, and by kind of liberation, these come to seven; their distinct substances number only six, since three paths each yield two names. Explanation: on the basis of a difference in preparatory effort, the first two kinds are established — on the basis of having, at an earlier time, relied either on another person's guidance or on the teaching itself in cultivating preparatory effort toward the sought-for goal, the names faith-follower and dharma-follower are established. On the basis of a difference in faculty, the next two kinds are established — on the basis of whether the dull faith-root or the sharp wisdom-root predominates, these are named, respectively, convinced-through-faith and attained-through-seeing. On the basis of attaining cessation-concentration, the name body-witness is established, since such a person personally attains cessation-concentration through the body. On the basis of a difference in liberation, the last two kinds are established — on the basis of one liberated from the affliction-obstruction through wisdom alone, the name wisdom-liberated is established; on the basis of one who is also liberated from the liberation-obstruction by additionally attaining concentration, the name both-liberated is established. Though these names number seven, their distinct substances number only six: at the path of seeing there are two noble ones, faith-follower and dharma-follower; these same two, upon reaching the path of cultivation, receive two further names, convinced-through-faith and attained-through-seeing; these same two, upon reaching the non-trainee stage, receive yet two further names, timely-liberated and non-timely-liberated.

The treatise closes this topic with an extended technical demonstration of just how many fine-grained sub-varieties the faith-follower alone divides into, once every relevant axis of variation is taken into account — a deliberately dizzying multiplication meant to convey the sheer combinatorial richness hiding behind a single simple name.

Chinese Source

應知此中一隨信行根故成三，謂下中上；性故成五，謂退法等；道故成十五，謂八忍七智；離染故成七十三，謂具縛離八地染；依身故成九，謂三洲、欲天。若根、性、道、離染、依身相乘，合成一億四萬七千八百二十五種。隨法行等如理應思。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be understood that, within this scheme, the faith-follower alone comes to three by faculty (lower, middling, and upper); comes to five by lineage (retrogressible-dharma and the rest); comes to fifteen by path-moment (the eight forbearances and seven knowledges of the path of seeing); comes to seventy-three by degree of prior freedom from defilement (from being fully bound, through having freed oneself from the afflictions of up to eight grounds); and comes to nine by bodily basis (the three continents and the desire realm's heavens). If faculty, lineage, path-moment, freedom from defilement, and bodily basis are all multiplied together, this yields a combined total of one hundred million, forty-seven thousand, eight hundred and twenty-five kinds. The dharma-follower and the rest should be reasoned through in the very same way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the underlying logic of "seven names, six substances" as resting on a genuinely elegant identification once unpacked: the faith-follower of the path of seeing is the very same person as the convinced-through-faith trainee of the path of cultivation and the timely-liberated arhat of the non-trainee stage — one continuum of practice renamed at each of the three stages it passes through — and likewise for the dharma-follower/attained-through-seeing/non-timely-liberated continuum. That gives six named stages across two underlying life-trajectories. Body-witness, wisdom-liberated, and both-liberated are not additional substances on top of these six, but cross-cutting labels describing the same six types further, according to whether cessation-concentration has been attained (body-witness) and whether concentration has been paired with wisdom in achieving liberation (wisdom-liberated versus both-liberated, corresponding respectively to the timely- and non-timely-liberated arhat already counted).

We should also flag the closing combinatorial total honestly: multiplying the five stated factors (3 × 5 × 15 × 73 × 9) as we understand them yields 147,825, not the "one hundred million, forty-seven thousand, eight hundred twenty-five" the text states — a discrepancy of roughly three orders of magnitude that we have not been able to resolve with confidence, whether through a different intended reading of one of the five factors, a textual transmission issue, or an error already present in the tradition itself. We have translated the figure exactly as the source text states it rather than silently substituting our own recalculated total, since doing so would misrepresent what the text actually says; readers with access to the Sanskrit or other recensions may be able to resolve this discrepancy where we cannot.

10. Both-Liberated and Wisdom-Liberated; What Makes a Trainee "Fulfilled"

Having introduced both-liberated and wisdom-liberated as the last two of the seven noble ones, the treatise now defines the pair precisely, then pivots to a related question: by what combination of qualities is a practitioner, at either the trainee or the non-trainee stage, properly called "fulfilled" within their own stage?

Chinese Source

何等名俱及慧解脫？頌曰： 俱由得滅定， 餘名慧解脫。 論曰：諸阿羅漢得滅定者名俱解脫，由慧定力解脫煩惱解脫障故。所餘未得滅盡定者名慧解脫，但由慧力於煩惱障得解脫故。如世尊說：五煩惱斷不可牽引未名滿學。學無學位各由幾因於等位中獨稱為滿？頌曰： 有學名為滿， 由根果定三； 無學得滿名， 但由根定二。 論曰：學於學位獨得滿名，具由三因，謂根、果、定。有有學者但由根故亦得滿名，謂諸見至未離欲染。有有學者但由果故亦得滿名，謂信解、不還未得滅盡定。有有學者由根果故亦得滿名，謂見至、不還未得滅盡定。有有學者由果定故亦得滿名，謂諸信解得滅盡定。有有學者具由三故獨得滿名，謂諸見至得滅盡定。無有學者但由定故及根定故亦得滿名。諸無學者於無學位由根定二獨得滿名，無學位中無非果滿，故無由果亦立滿名。有但由根亦名為滿，謂不時解脫未得滅盡定。有但由定亦名為滿，謂時解脫得滅盡定。有具由二獨名為滿，謂不時解脫已得滅盡定。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, precisely, is meant by both-liberated and wisdom-liberated? Verse: both-liberated is defined by attainment of cessation-concentration; the rest are named wisdom-liberated. Explanation: those arhats who have attained cessation-concentration are called both-liberated, since they are liberated from both the affliction-obstruction and the liberation-obstruction through the combined power of wisdom and concentration. The remaining arhats, who have not attained cessation-concentration, are called wisdom-liberated, since they are liberated from the affliction-obstruction through the power of wisdom alone. As the Blessed One said: even once the five lower afflictions are severed such that one cannot be dragged back by them, one is not yet called a fulfilled trainee. At the trainee and non-trainee stages, respectively, by how many causes is one uniquely called fulfilled within one's own stage? Verse: a trainee is called fulfilled by three possible causes — faculty, fruit, and concentration; a non-trainee attains the name fulfilled by only two — faculty and concentration. Explanation: a trainee, uniquely attaining the name fulfilled at the trainee stage, does so by three causes in full: faculty, fruit, and concentration. There are trainees who attain the name fulfilled by faculty alone — namely, those of the attained-through-seeing type who have not yet become free from desire-realm defilement. There are trainees who attain it by fruit alone — namely, convinced-through-faith non-returners who have not yet attained cessation-concentration. There are trainees who attain it by faculty and fruit together — namely, attained-through-seeing non-returners who have not yet attained cessation-concentration. There are trainees who attain it by fruit and concentration together — namely, convinced-through-faith non-returners who have attained cessation-concentration. There are trainees who attain it by all three together — namely, attained-through-seeing non-returners who have attained cessation-concentration. No trainee attains the name fulfilled by concentration alone, or by faculty and concentration together without fruit. Non-trainees, at the non-trainee stage, attain the name fulfilled uniquely by the two causes of faculty and concentration; at the non-trainee stage there is no fulfillment apart from the fruit itself, hence no separate fulfilled-name is established by fruit alone. There are some called fulfilled by faculty alone — namely, the non-timely-liberated who has not yet attained cessation-concentration. There are some called fulfilled by concentration alone — namely, the timely-liberated who has attained cessation-concentration. There are some called fulfilled by both together — namely, the non-timely-liberated who has already attained cessation-concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the both-liberated/wisdom-liberated distinction as turning entirely on cessation-concentration (nirodha-samāpatti, the same attainment defining the body-witness noble one introduced in fascicle 24): an arhat who has mastered it is "liberated" by the joint power of wisdom and concentration, freed from both the affliction-obstruction and a further, separate "liberation-obstruction"; an arhat who has not is liberated by wisdom alone, and so remains subject to that second obstruction even after affliction itself is fully exhausted — a reminder that, in this scheme, severing affliction and attaining every possible kind of freedom are not quite the same achievement.

We should also note that the "fulfilled trainee" analysis is a purely combinatorial exercise in why certain combinations of faculty, fruit, and concentration are attested (five of a possible seven combinations for trainees) while others are structurally ruled out — concentration or concentration-plus-faculty alone never qualifies a trainee as fulfilled without also having reached at least the non-returner fruit, since cessation-concentration itself is unattainable below that fruit. We have preserved this structural reasoning as the text presents it, without further elaborating the trainee-stage rationale beyond what the passage itself states.

11. The Four Kinds of Path, and the Four Kinds of Conduct

Having worked through the noble ones themselves in fine detail, the treatise steps back to organize the very notion of "path" that has run through this entire discussion. Path-language proliferates without limit — worldly versus transcendent, seeing versus cultivation, and so on — so the treatise asks how few basic categories suffice to capture every variety.

Chinese Source

廣說諸道差別無量，謂世出世見修道等。略說幾道能遍攝耶？頌曰： 應知一切道， 略說唯有四， 謂加行無間， 解脫勝進道。 論曰：加行道者，謂從此後無間道生。無間道者，謂此能斷所應斷障。解脫道者，謂已解脫所應斷障最初所生。勝進道者，謂三餘道。道義云何？謂涅槃路，乘此能往涅槃城故。或復道者，謂求所依，依此尋求涅槃果故。解脫、勝進如何名道？與道類同，轉上品故，或前前力至後後故、或能趣入無餘依故。道於餘處立通行名，以能通達趣涅槃故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Broadly speaking, the distinctions among paths are countless — worldly and transcendent, seeing and cultivation, and so on. Stated briefly, how many categories of path suffice to encompass them all? Verse: it should be understood that every path, stated briefly, comes to only four — preparatory, uninterrupted, liberation, and supplementary-advancement paths. Explanation: the preparatory path is that from which, immediately following, an uninterrupted path arises. The uninterrupted path is that which is able to sever the obstruction that ought to be severed. The liberation path is that which arises first, immediately upon having become liberated from the obstruction that ought to be severed. The supplementary-advancement path is whatever remains beyond these three. What is the meaning of "path"? It is the road to nirvāṇa, since, mounting it, one is able to travel to the city of nirvāṇa. Alternatively, "path" means seeking a support, since, depending on it, one seeks the fruit of nirvāṇa. How can the liberation and supplementary-advancement paths be called "path," if they themselves do not sever any obstruction? Because they belong to the same class as the uninterrupted path, each advancing to an ever-higher grade; or because the power of each earlier moment reaches through to each later one in unbroken succession; or because they too are capable of leading into the state without remainder. Elsewhere, "path" is given the name "conduct," since it is able to thoroughly penetrate and proceed toward nirvāṇa.

Having renamed "path" as "conduct" in this broader sense, the treatise then asks how many kinds of conduct there are, and on what basis they are distinguished.

Chinese Source

此有幾種？依何建立？頌曰： 通行有四種， 樂依本靜慮， 苦依所餘地， 遲速鈍利根。 論曰：經說通行總有四種：一苦遲通行、二苦速通行、三樂遲通行、四樂速通行。道依根本四靜慮生名樂通行，以攝受支止觀平等任運轉故。道依無色、未至、中間名苦通行，以不攝支止觀不等艱辛轉故。謂無色定觀減止增，未至、中間觀增止減。即此樂苦二通行中，鈍根名遲、利根名速。二行於境通達稽遲故名遲通，翻此名速。或遲鈍者所起通行名遲通行，速此相違。

Ru-Yi Translation

How many kinds of this are there, and on what basis is it established? Verse: conduct is of four kinds — pleasant conduct depends on the root dhyānas, painful conduct depends on the remaining grounds, and each is further slow or quick according to dull or sharp faculty. Explanation: a sūtra states that conduct is, in total, of four kinds: painful-slow conduct, painful-quick conduct, pleasant-slow conduct, and pleasant-quick conduct. A path arising in dependence on the four root dhyānas is called pleasant conduct, since, with its branch-factors fully gathered in, calm and insight operate in balance, spontaneously. A path arising in dependence on the formless attainments, the not-yet-attained stage, or the intermediate dhyāna is called painful conduct, since, its branch-factors not fully gathered in, calm and insight operate unevenly and with difficulty — in the formless concentrations, insight is diminished and calm excessive; in the not-yet-attained stage and the intermediate dhyāna, insight is excessive and calm diminished. Within each of these two kinds of conduct, pleasant and painful, the dull-faculty practitioner's conduct is called slow, and the sharp-faculty practitioner's is called quick — when one is delayed in thoroughly penetrating the object, this is called slow conduct, and the reverse of this is called quick. Alternatively: conduct arising in one who is slow and dull is itself called slow conduct, and quick conduct is the reverse of this.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the fourfold path structure (preparatory / uninterrupted / liberation / supplementary-advancement) as a genuinely load-bearing framework that this entire fascicle — indeed much of the treatise's account of the path of seeing and cultivation — has been using implicitly all along without stopping to define it; this passage is where the treatise finally pauses to make the scaffolding explicit. Worth noting, too, is the honest question the text raises against itself: why call the liberation and supplementary-advancement paths "path" at all, if only the uninterrupted path actually severs anything? The three answers offered (same class, unbroken causal continuity, shared capacity to lead all the way to nirvāṇa) are given as a cluster of complementary justifications rather than a single decisive one.

We should also flag the "pleasant versus painful" conduct distinction as turning on how smoothly calm (śamatha) and insight (vipaśyanā) work together at a given meditative level — evenly balanced in the four root dhyānas, unevenly weighted (toward one or the other) everywhere else — a structural point about meditative mechanics that the text treats as simply given, without further defending why balance specifically should track the root dhyānas rather than any other level.

12. The Thirty-Seven Limbs of Awakening, and Their Ten Real Substances

The treatise now takes up "path" under yet another name familiar from across the broader tradition: the thirty-seven dharmas that are limbs of awakening (bodhipakṣa-dharma) — the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right exertions, the four bases of supernormal power, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of awakening, and the eightfold noble path. "Awakening" here specifically names the two knowledges that complete the path, exhaustion and non-arising, achieved differently by śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and buddhas alike; the thirty-seven are called its "limbs" simply because each accords with and leads toward this awakening.

Chinese Source

道亦名為菩提分法。此有幾種？名義云何？頌曰： 覺分三十七， 謂四念住等， 覺謂盡無生， 順此故名分。 論曰：經說覺分有三十七，謂四念住、四正斷、四神足、五根、五力、七等覺支、八聖道支。盡、無生智說名為覺。隨覺者別立三菩提：一聲聞菩提、二獨覺菩提、三無上菩提。無明睡眠皆永斷故，及如實知已作已事不復作故，此二名覺。三十七法順趣菩提，是故皆名菩提分法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Path is also called "the dharmas that are limbs of awakening." How many kinds are there, and what is the meaning of this name? Verse: the limbs of awakening number thirty-seven — the four foundations of mindfulness and so on; "awakening" refers to the knowledge of exhaustion and the knowledge of non-arising; because these accord with awakening, they are called its limbs. Explanation: a sūtra states that the limbs of awakening number thirty-seven — the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right exertions, the four bases of supernormal power, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of awakening, and the eightfold noble path. The knowledge of exhaustion and the knowledge of non-arising are together called awakening. According to who attains it, three kinds of awakening are separately established: śrāvaka awakening, pratyekabuddha awakening, and unsurpassed awakening. Because the slumber of ignorance is permanently severed, and because one truly knows that what has been done is done and need not be done again, these two knowledges are called awakening. Since the thirty-seven dharmas accord with and lead toward awakening, they are all called the dharmas that are limbs of awakening.

Having named all thirty-seven, the treatise immediately raises the natural question of overlap: with so many mindfulness-faculties, mindfulness-powers, and mindfulness-limbs already named separately, are these really thirty-seven distinct dharmas, or does the list simply repeat a much smaller set of real entities under different names depending on context?

Chinese Source

此三十七體各別耶？不爾。云何？頌曰： 此實事唯十， 謂慧勤定信， 念喜捨輕安， 及戒尋為體。 論曰：此覺分名雖三十七，實事唯十，即慧勤等。謂四念住、慧根、慧力、擇法覺支、正見，以慧為體。四正斷、精進根、精進力、精進覺支、正精進，以勤為體。四神足、定根、定力、定覺支、正定，以定為體。信根、信力，以信為體。念根、念力、念覺支、正念，以念為體，喜覺支以喜為體，捨覺支以行捨為體，輕安覺支以輕安為體，正語、正業、正命以戒為體，正思惟以尋為體。如是覺分實事唯十，即是信等五根力上，更加喜、捨、輕安、戒、尋。毘婆沙師說有十一，身業語業不相雜故戒分為二，餘九同前。

Ru-Yi Translation

Are these thirty-seven each a distinct substance? No. How, then? Verse: their real substances number only ten — wisdom, diligence, concentration, faith, mindfulness, joy, equanimity, pliancy, and precept and inquiry serve as their substance. Explanation: though the limbs of awakening number thirty-seven by name, their real substances number only ten, namely wisdom, diligence, and the rest. The four foundations of mindfulness, the wisdom-faculty, the wisdom-power, the discernment-of-dharmas awakening-factor, and right view have wisdom as their substance. The four right exertions, the diligence-faculty, the diligence-power, the diligence awakening-factor, and right effort have diligence as their substance. The four bases of supernormal power, the concentration-faculty, the concentration-power, the concentration awakening-factor, and right concentration have concentration as their substance. The faith-faculty and the faith-power have faith as their substance. The mindfulness-faculty, the mindfulness-power, the mindfulness awakening-factor, and right mindfulness have mindfulness as their substance. The joy awakening-factor has joy as its substance. The equanimity awakening-factor has formational equanimity as its substance. The pliancy awakening-factor has pliancy as its substance. Right speech, right action, and right livelihood have precept as their substance. Right intention has inquiry as its substance. Thus the limbs of awakening's real substances number only ten — precisely the five faculties and powers of faith and the rest, with joy, equanimity, pliancy, precept, and inquiry further added. The Vaibhāṣika masters say there are eleven instead, since, bodily karma and verbal karma not being mixed together, precept is divided into two; the remaining nine are the same as before.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag this passage as a striking instance of the treatise's recurring reductive method: a famous, long-canonical list of thirty-seven items, recited as a fixed unit across the whole tradition, is shown to compress down to just ten (or, on the Vaibhāṣika's own preferred count, eleven) actual mental factors, each simply renamed according to which of seven different contexts (foundation of mindfulness, faculty, power, exertion, base of power, awakening-factor, path-factor) it appears under. The number thirty-seven itself is thus revealed as an artifact of how many labels a small set of real dharmas accumulates as the path unfolds, not a sign of thirty-seven genuinely separate qualities to be cultivated.

We should also note the one open disagreement the passage itself flags: whether "precept" (śīla) as the substance behind right speech/action/livelihood is a single dharma or splits into two (bodily-karma precept and verbal-karma precept), which is the entire difference between the treatise's stated count of ten and the Vaibhāṣika's count of eleven. We have kept this as the minor, explicit variant reading the text itself presents, rather than silently picking one number as final.

13. Why These Names? Foundations of Mindfulness, Right Exertion, Bases of Power, and the Order of Faith

The previous section's reduction leaves an obvious loose end: the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right exertions, and the four bases of supernormal power were assigned to wisdom, diligence, and concentration respectively — yet each of these three groups, by definition, actually encompasses every preparatory wholesome dharma leading up to the path of seeing, not wisdom, diligence, or concentration alone. The treatise resolves this by appeal to whichever quality simply predominates within each group, then works through a string of etymological questions: why "mindfulness," why "exertion," why "supernormal power," and why faith and its companions are named "faculties" before later being renamed "powers," in that specific order.

Chinese Source

念住等三名無別屬，如何獨說為慧勤定？頌曰： 四念住正斷， 神足隨增上， 說為慧勤定， 實諸加行善。 論曰：四念住等三品善法，體實遍攝諸加行善，然隨同品增上善根，如次說為慧勤及定。何緣於慧立念住名？毘婆沙師作如是說：慧由念力持令住故。理實由慧令念住境，如實見者能明記故，如念住中已廣成立。何故說勤名為正斷？於正修習斷修位中，此勤力能斷懈怠故。或名正勝，於正持策身語意中此最勝故。何緣於定立神足名？諸靈妙德所依止故。有餘師說：神即是定，足謂欲等。彼應覺分事有十三，增欲心故。又違經說，如契經言：吾今為汝說神足等，神謂受用種種神境分一為多，乃至廣說。足謂欲等四三摩地，此中佛說定果名神，欲等所生等持名足。

Ru-Yi Translation

The foundations of mindfulness and the other two groups have no separately dedicated substance of their own — how, then, can they each be said to consist specifically of wisdom, diligence, and concentration? Verse: the four foundations of mindfulness, the right exertions, and the bases of supernormal power, according to whichever quality predominates, are said to be wisdom, diligence, and concentration — though in reality each consists of every preparatory wholesome dharma. Explanation: these three groups of wholesome dharmas — the four foundations of mindfulness and the rest — in substance actually encompass every preparatory wholesome dharma; however, according to whichever wholesome root of the same category predominates within each, they are said, respectively, to be wisdom, diligence, and concentration. Why is the name "foundation of mindfulness" established with reference to wisdom specifically? The Vaibhāṣika masters explain it thus: wisdom, through the power of mindfulness, is held in place and made to abide. In reality, however, it is wisdom that causes mindfulness to abide on its object, since one who sees truly is thereby able to clearly retain it in memory, as already established at length in the earlier discussion of the foundations of mindfulness. Why is diligence called "right exertion"? Because, at the stage of properly cultivating the cultivation of severance, the power of this diligence is able to sever laziness. Alternatively it is named "right superior effort," since, in properly urging on body, speech, and mind, this quality is foremost. Why is the name "base of supernormal power" established with reference to concentration? Because concentration is the support for every wondrous, marvelous quality. Other teachers say instead: "supernormal" is itself concentration, while "base" refers to aspiration and the rest. On their view, the limbs of awakening's real substances should number thirteen, since mind and aspiration would then be added as further substances. This, moreover, contradicts what the sūtra states — as a sūtra says: I shall now explain to you the bases of supernormal power, and so on — supernormal refers to enjoying various supernormal manifestations, dividing oneself as one into many, and so on, at length; base refers to the four kinds of concentration produced by aspiration and the rest. Here the Buddha states that the fruit of concentration is called supernormal, while the concentration produced by aspiration and the rest is called base.

Chinese Source

何緣信等先說為根，後名為力？由此五法依下上品分先後故，又依可屈伏不可屈伏故。信等何緣次第如是？謂於因果先起信心為果修因，次起精進，由精進故念住所緣，由念力持心便得定，心得定故能如實知，是故信等如是次第。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are faith and the rest spoken of first as faculties, and only later named powers? Because these five dharmas, according to their lower and higher grades, divide into an earlier and a later stage, and also according to whether they can be overwhelmed by opposing factors or cannot be overwhelmed. Why do faith and the rest occur in just this sequence? With respect to cause and effect, one first gives rise to faith, cultivating the cause for the sake of the effect; next, one gives rise to diligence; through diligence, mindfulness then abides on its object; through the power of mindfulness holding the mind in place, one then attains concentration; and because the mind is concentrated, one is then able to know truly. It is for this reason that faith and the rest occur in precisely this sequence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the rejected rival etymology of "base of supernormal power" (神足) as a genuinely interesting minority position: rather than treating the whole compound as naming concentration, these unnamed "other teachers" split it — "supernormal" (神) as concentration, "base" (足) as the four separate causes (aspiration, effort, mind, investigation) that produce it — which would add two further real substances (mind and aspiration) to the ten already established, making thirteen rather than ten. The treatise rejects this specifically because it misreads the cited sūtra, which defines "supernormal" as the wondrous fruit itself (dividing one body into many, and the like), not as concentration at all, and "base" as the concentration these four causes produce — the reverse assignment from the rival reading.

We should also note the closing sequence — faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom — as a compact causal chain the treatise presents as self-evidently ordered: each factor is described as the necessary condition that gives rise to the next, from initial trust through to the culminating true knowledge, mirroring the standard progression already used throughout the tradition's account of practice.

14. Which Limb Predominates at Which Stage of Practice

With the thirty-seven limbs reduced to ten real substances organized into seven groups, the treatise asks which group predominates at which stage of the path — offering two accounts side by side, the standard Vaibhāṣika mapping onto the stages of practice already established across earlier fascicles, and a second reading (attributed to unnamed "other teachers") that instead traces a natural causal sequence: why mindfulness must come first, and each subsequent group only once the last has done its work.

Chinese Source

當言何位何覺分增？頌曰： 初業順決擇， 及修見道位， 念住等七品， 應知次第增。 論曰：初業位中能審照了身等四境，慧用勝故說念住增。煖法位中能證異品殊勝功德，勤用勝故說正斷增。頂法位中能持勝善趣無退德，定用勝故說神足增。忍法位中必不退墮，善根堅固得增上義故說根增。第一位中非惑世法所能屈伏，得無屈義故說力增。修道位中近菩提位，助覺勝故說覺支增。見道位中速疾而轉，通行勝故說道支增。然契經中隨數增說先七後八，非修次第。八中正見是道亦道支，餘是道支而非道。七中擇法是覺亦覺支，餘是覺支而非覺。毘婆沙師所說如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

At which stage should it be said that which limb of awakening predominates? Verse: at the initial-practice stage, at the aids-to-penetration stages, and at the stages of cultivation and seeing, it should be understood that the seven groups — foundations of mindfulness and the rest — successively predominate, each at its own stage. Explanation: at the initial-practice stage, one is able to carefully illuminate the four objects — body and the rest; since the function of wisdom predominates there, the foundations of mindfulness are said to predominate. At the stage of heat, one is able to realize superior qualities of a different, higher kind; since the function of diligence predominates, the right exertions are said to predominate. At the stage of the peak, one is able to hold onto the superior, wholesome quality of never retrogressing; since the function of concentration predominates, the bases of supernormal power are said to predominate. At the stage of forbearance, one certainly does not retrogress or fall; since the wholesome roots have become firm, attaining the sense of dominance, the faculties are said to predominate. At the stage of being supreme in the world, one cannot be overwhelmed by afflictive worldly dharmas; since one attains the sense of being unable to be overwhelmed, the powers are said to predominate. At the stage of the path of cultivation, being close to the stage of awakening, the function of assisting awakening predominates, hence the awakening-factors are said to predominate. At the stage of the path of seeing, one turns swiftly and rapidly; the function of conduct predominates, hence the path-factors are said to predominate. However, in the sūtras, these are stated in ascending numerical order, first the group of seven, then the group of eight — this is not the sequence of actual cultivation. Among the eight path-factors, right view is both path and a path-factor; the rest are path-factors but not themselves called path. Among the seven awakening-factors, discernment of dharmas is both awakening and an awakening-factor; the rest are awakening-factors but not themselves awakening. This is what the Vaibhāṣika masters say.

Other teachers, without contradicting the sequence stated in the sūtra, establish the foundations of mindfulness and the rest on a different basis: when a practitioner is about to practice, the mind runs scattered among many objects, so one first cultivates the foundations of mindfulness to subdue and control the mind. Hence a sūtra states: these four foundations of mindfulness are able to bind the mind to its object, and to properly eliminate mindfulness rooted in indulgence — this is why the foundations of mindfulness are said to come first. Through the force of this, diligence then increases and grows; in order to accomplish the four tasks of right exertion, one properly urges on and sustains the mind, hence the right exertions are said to come second. Through diligence, the mind becomes free of worry and regret, and one then becomes capable of cultivating and perfecting superior concentration, hence the bases of supernormal power are said to come third. With superior concentration as a basis, faith and the rest then serve as the dominant condition for transcendent dharmas, hence the five faculties are said to come fourth. Once the sense of faculty is established, one is able to properly subdue and eliminate the currently active afflictions to be counteracted, drawing forth the arising of noble dharmas, hence the five powers are said to come fifth. At the stage of the path of seeing, the awakening-factors are established, since one truly awakens to and knows the four noble truths there. The path-factors, however, are established as pertaining to both the path of seeing and the path of cultivation together, since both alike lead directly to the city of nirvāṇa. As a sūtra states: one who has perfectly cultivated the eight path-factors has, thereby, also perfectly cultivated everything from the four foundations of mindfulness up through the seven awakening-factors. A further sūtra states: Monks, you should know — proclaiming true words is a simile for teaching the four noble truths; causing one to travel the original road swiftly and exit is a simile for causing one to cultivate the eightfold noble path. Hence it is known that the eight path-factors are spoken of as pertaining to both stages together. Since the sequence, spoken of according to which stage each factor predominates in, has now been explained in this way, this is genuinely what should be said.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that the treatise presents two distinct organizing principles for the same seven-part list without declaring one simply wrong: the Vaibhāṣika account maps each group onto a specific named stage of the path (the four aids to penetration, the path of seeing, the path of cultivation) already developed in earlier fascicles, while the second account instead traces an internal, causal logic — why mindfulness must stabilize the mind before diligence can be sustained, why diligence must precede the calm needed for concentration, and so on through to wisdom. The closing line ("this is genuinely what should be said") appears to endorse this second, causal account as the more fundamentally correct explanation, while treating the first as a legitimate but merely stage-indexed description of the same underlying process.

We should also flag the observation that the awakening-factors belong exclusively to the path of seeing while the path-factors span both the path of seeing and cultivation — supported by two separate sūtra citations — as a specific technical point about scope that the text treats as settled by direct scriptural citation rather than by further argument.

15. Contaminated or Not, and How Many Survive at Each Meditative Ground

Two further bookkeeping questions close out the treatise's account of the thirty-seven limbs: which of the seven groups are exclusively uncontaminated versus common to both contaminated and uncontaminated dharmas, and, ground by ground through the entire cosmology, how many of the thirty-seven actually remain present at each level.

Chinese Source

此三十七幾通有漏、幾無漏耶？頌曰： 七覺八道支， 一向是無漏； 三四五根力， 皆通於二種。 論曰：此中七覺、八聖道支唯是無漏，唯於修道見道位中方建立故。世間亦有正見等法，而彼不得聖道支名。所餘皆通有漏、無漏。

Ru-Yi Translation

Of these thirty-seven, how many pertain to both the contaminated and the uncontaminated, and how many are exclusively uncontaminated? Verse: the seven awakening-factors and the eight path-factors are exclusively uncontaminated; the three groups of four together with the two groups of five — the faculties and powers — are all common to both kinds. Explanation: among these, the seven awakening-factors and the eightfold noble path are exclusively uncontaminated, since they are established only at the stages of the path of cultivation and the path of seeing. Though the mundane world also has dharmas such as right view, these do not receive the name noble path-factor. All the rest pertain to both the contaminated and the uncontaminated.

Among these thirty-seven, how many are present at each ground? Verse: at the first dhyāna, all are present; at the not-yet-attained stage, the joy factor is excluded; at the second dhyāna, inquiry is excluded; at the third and fourth dhyānas and the intermediate dhyāna, both are excluded. At the second dhyāna, right intention is excluded, since inquiry is already absent within that dhyāna — on this basis, each of these two grounds has thirty-six. At the third dhyāna, the fourth dhyāna, and the intermediate dhyāna, both joy and inquiry are excluded, leaving thirty-five at each. At the first three formless spheres, the three precept-based path-factors are excluded, together with joy and inquiry, leaving thirty-two at each. Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the pattern running through this ground-by-ground count: the thirty-seven steadily shrink as the meditative grounds grow more refined — losing joy on the border of the first dhyāna, losing inquiry once thought itself falls away in the second, losing the precept-based path-factors once bodily and verbal conduct no longer apply in the formless spheres, and finally losing every awakening- and path-factor at the two extremes of the scale (the coarse desire realm and the exceptionally subtle peak of existence), since uncontaminated dharmas cannot be supported by either. This mirrors the same restriction on the formless peak of existence already encountered in this fascicle's account of faculty-refining, reinforcing that this ground is treated throughout as too subtle a basis for the noble path's direct operation, not only too coarse a basis, as in the desire realm's case.

We should also note the closing clause — that the operation of the limbs of awakening necessarily brings about "confirmed purity" — functions here as a bridge into the treatise's next topic rather than a fully developed claim in its own right; we have translated it as the transition the text itself uses, without importing content from the fuller explanation that follows.
