A Body Between Two Lives

This page carries the opening of fascicle 9, continuing Chapter 3 (分別世品第三之二, the Loka-nirdeśa's second installment) directly from fascicle 8's great debate over whether intermediate existence exists. Having established that it does, the treatise now turns to what it is actually like: a body that already foreshadows the very destiny it is heading toward, moving with unobstructed supernormal speed, sustained on nothing but fragrance, and — in one of this text's most psychologically striking passages — drawn toward its future parents by a species-specific, inverted form of desire at the exact moment of conception.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 21 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 9 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. The Intermediate Being's Shape Already Foreshadows Its Destiny

Continuing directly from fascicle 8's proof that intermediate existence exists, the treatise turns to its concrete characteristics: the very same karma that determines where one will be reborn also shapes the intermediate-existence body itself — raising, and answering, a vivid puzzle about multiple destinies' intermediate beings coexisting within a single womb.

Chinese Source

當往何趣，所起中有形狀如何？頌曰：此一業引故，如當本有形，本有謂死前，居生剎那後。論曰：若業能引當所往趣，彼業即招能往中有。故此中有若往彼趣，即如所趣當本有形。若爾，於一狗等腹中容有五趣中有頓起。既有地獄中有現前，如何不能焚燒母腹？彼居本有亦不恒燒？如暫遊園，況在中有。設許能燒，如不可見亦不可觸，以中有身極微細故，所難非理。諸趣中有雖居一腹，非互觸燒，業所遮故。

Ru-Yi Translation

To which destiny will one go, and what is the shape of the intermediate-existence being that arises? Verse: since it is induced by that same single karma, it resembles the shape of the future actual-existence; "actual existence" means the period before death, following the moment of birth. Prose: whatever karma is capable of inducing the destiny to which one will go, that very same karma also brings about the intermediate-existence being capable of traveling there. Hence, if this intermediate-existence being is heading toward a given destiny, it takes on the very shape of the actual-existence body it will have once it arrives there. If that is so, then within a single dog's womb, for instance, intermediate-existence beings belonging to all five destinies might suddenly arise together. But if a hell-being's intermediate existence is thereby present, how could it fail to burn up the mother's womb? Even while dwelling in its actual hell-existence, does it burn everything constantly? No — it is like someone briefly wandering through a garden, not setting the garden ablaze just by being present; how much more so while merely in the intermediate-existence state. Even granting that it could burn — just as it cannot be seen or touched by ordinary beings, since the intermediate-existence body is extremely subtle, this objection does not hold up as valid reasoning. Although intermediate-existence beings belonging to different destinies may dwell within a single womb, they do not touch or burn one another, since this is prevented by the power of karma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The principle here is elegantly economical: rather than needing a separate explanation for what an intermediate-existence being looks like, the treatise simply says it already looks like whatever it is about to become — the same karmic cause that will produce a specific hell-body, animal-body, or human-body already shapes the in-between body on its way there. This is what generates the genuinely vivid puzzle about a single litter containing embryos destined for entirely different realms of rebirth: since a mother animal's womb can carry several offspring at once, and each offspring's future destiny is independently determined by its own karma, nothing rules out one puppy's intermediate being belonging to the desire realm's animal destiny while another, in the very same womb, belongs to the hells. The reassurance that follows — karma itself prevents these radically different beings from ever actually touching or interfering with one another — is a clean example of how this tradition uses karmic law to do the work that physical law does in the ordinary ranked world: not because the physics doesn't apply, but because a hell-being's own body being present is never a matter of ordinary physical action to begin with, given how "extremely subtle" it is said to be.

2. The Bodhisattva's Radiant Body, and Why His Mother Dreamed of a White Elephant

The desire realm's ordinary intermediate-existence being is child-sized but sharp of faculty; the bodhisattva's, by contrast, appears already in the full bloom of maturity, luminous enough to illuminate a hundred million world-systems — raising a famous puzzle: if this is so, why did his mother dream of a white elephant entering her side, rather than of him as he actually appeared?

Chinese Source

欲中有量雖如小兒年五六歲而根明利；菩薩中有如盛年時形量周圓具諸相好，故住中有將入胎時照百俱胝四大洲等。若爾，何故菩薩母夢中見白象子來入己右脇？此吉瑞相，非關中有。菩薩久捨傍生趣故。如訖栗枳王夢所見十事：謂大象井麨，栴檀妙園林，小象二獼猴，廣堅衣鬪諍。如是所夢，但表當來餘事先兆，非如所見。又諸中有從生門入，非破母腹而得入胎，故雙生者前小後大。法善現說復云何通？白象相端嚴，具六牙四足，正知入母腹，寢如仙隱林。不必須通，非三藏故。諸諷頌言，或過實故。若必須通，如菩薩母所見夢相，造頌無失。

Ru-Yi Translation

The desire-realm intermediate-existence being, although its size is like that of a small child five or six years old, has faculties that are bright and keen. The bodhisattva's intermediate-existence being, however, has the form and stature of one in the prime of life, perfectly formed and complete with all the marks and signs of excellence — hence, while dwelling in this intermediate existence and about to enter the womb, it illuminates a hundred koṭis of the four great continents, and so on. If that is so, then why did the bodhisattva's mother dream that a young white elephant came and entered her right side? This is an auspicious omen-image, unrelated to the actual form of the intermediate-existence being itself — the bodhisattva had, after all, long since abandoned the animal destiny. This is comparable to the ten things King Kṛkin saw in his dream: a great elephant, a well, roasted barley-flour, sandalwood, a wondrous grove, a small elephant, two monkeys, something broad and firm, a garment, and strife. Dreams of this kind merely signify omens portending other future events — they do not depict things exactly as they will literally appear. Moreover, every intermediate-existence being enters through the birth canal; it does not enter the womb by breaking through the mother's flank — hence, among twins, the one born first is smaller, and the one born after is larger. How, then, is Dharmasugupta's statement to be reconciled with this? He wrote: "The white elephant's form was dignified and fine, possessing six tusks and four feet; with correct awareness it entered the mother's womb, resting there as a sage secluded in a forest grove." This need not be reconciled at all, since it does not belong to the canonical scriptures — verses of praise of this kind sometimes exceed the literal truth. If it must be reconciled, then, just as with the dream-image seen by the bodhisattva's mother, composing such a verse involves no fault.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag genuine uncertainty about several items in King Kṛkin's list of ten dream-images — I have translated each term about as literally as the compressed verse allows, but items like "something broad and firm" are genuinely unclear to me without a fuller narrative source explaining what specific object or event each dream-image was meant to foretell, and I have not smoothed over that uncertainty with a confident-sounding gloss I cannot actually support.

The reasoning that settles the elephant-dream puzzle is worth spelling out because it resolves what looks, at first, like a real contradiction: if the bodhisattva's intermediate-existence body already had full human, adult form, why would his mother's dream show an elephant instead? The answer draws a firm line between two very different kinds of representation — the intermediate-existence being's actual physical form, which is simply and literally what it is, and a dream-omen, which functions symbolically, foretelling significance rather than depicting appearance. A white elephant is a traditional symbol of an extraordinary, auspicious birth in this narrative tradition; it was never meant as documentary evidence of what the bodhisattva actually looked like at that moment, any more than King Kṛkin's ten dream-images were meant as literal snapshots of the future events they portended. Dharmasugupta's more elaborate verse, describing an actual six-tusked elephant physically entering the womb, is set aside on the more decisive ground that it simply is not part of the canonical Tripiṭaka at all — though the text notes, almost as an aside, that even if reconciliation were required, the same symbolic reading would still work.

3. Clothing in the Form Realm, and the Four Stages of Existence

The form realm's intermediate-existence being arises already clothed, its heightened sense of modesty demanding no less — illustrated through the striking legend of a nun whose robe, earned through a vow made lifetimes earlier, never once leaves her body. The treatise then formally names the four stages every form-possessing being's existence cycles through.

Chinese Source

色界中有量，圓滿如本有。與衣俱生，慚愧增故，菩薩中有亦與衣俱。鮮白苾芻尼由本願力故，彼於世世有自然衣，恒不離身隨時改變，乃至最後般涅槃時，即以此衣纏屍焚葬。所餘欲界中有無衣，由皆增長無慚愧故。所似本有其體是何？謂死有前、生有後蘊，總說有體是五取蘊。於中位別分析為四：一者中有，義如前說。二者生有，謂於諸趣結生剎那。三者本有，除生剎那死前餘位。四者死有，謂最後念。次中有前，有色有情具足四有，若在無色中闕具三。

Ru-Yi Translation

The form realm's intermediate-existence being's size is complete and full, just like its actual-existence body. It arises together with clothing, since beings of that realm have heightened modesty and shame; the bodhisattva's intermediate-existence being likewise arises together with clothing. The nun Śuklā, through the power of a vow made in a former life, has, life after life, a naturally-arising garment that never leaves her body, changing in accordance with the occasion, even up to her final attainment of complete nirvana, at which time this very garment is used to wrap her corpse for cremation. All remaining desire-realm intermediate-existence beings, however, have no clothing, since they have all cultivated and increased a lack of modesty and shame. What, then, is the essential nature of the actual-existence which the intermediate-existence being resembles? It is the aggregates that occur before the existence of death and after the existence of birth — in general terms, its essential nature is the five aggregates subject to clinging. Within this, distinguishing by stage, there are four: first, intermediate existence, whose meaning has already been explained; second, the existence of birth, namely the moment of conception within any of the destinies; third, actual existence, namely every stage other than the moment of conception and the period before death; fourth, the existence of death, namely the very last moment of that life. Form-possessing sentient beings possess all four kinds of existence in full; those reborn within the formless realm lack one, possessing only three.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nun Śuklā's ever-present robe is a lovely illustration of how this tradition treats a vow's karmic fruit: a single act of aspiration, made once in some distant past life, can ripen across an entire subsequent lifetime — indeed, across the very last moments of that lifetime — as a concrete, physical reality that requires no further maintenance or renewal. The desire realm's ordinary lack of clothing at the intermediate stage is explained, tellingly, not as a mere absence of convention but as a moral deficiency in its own right: a "lack of modesty and shame" that has been positively cultivated and increased, treating nudity at this specific stage as a symptom of an underdeveloped virtue rather than a morally neutral fact about embodiment.

The formless realm's exclusion from intermediate existence — the one of the four stages it lacks — connects directly back to fascicle 8's proof that the formless realm has no spatial location at all: since intermediate existence is defined precisely as the interval spent traveling toward a destination, and formless rebirth involves no travel whatsoever, occurring right where the prior existence ended, there is simply no room for an intermediate stage to occupy in the formless case.

4. A Verse on the Intermediate Being's Nature, and Who Can See It

A dense verse catalogs the intermediate-existence being's remaining properties — visibility, supernormal speed, indestructibility, diet, brief duration, and orientation — beginning with the question of who can actually perceive it: only beings of its own kind, or a cultivator with a sufficiently refined divine eye.

Chinese Source

已說形量，餘義當辯。頌曰：同淨天眼見，業通疾具根，無對不可轉，食香非久住。倒心趣欲境，濕化染香處，天首上三橫，地獄頭歸下。論曰：此中有身同類相見，若有修得極淨天眼亦能得見，諸生得眼皆不能觀，以極細故。有餘師說：天中有眼具足能見。五趣中有，人、鬼、傍生、地獄中有見四三二一，謂自下除上。

Ru-Yi Translation

The shape and size have now been explained; the remaining points should now be discussed. Verse: it is seen by beings of the same kind, and by the pure divine eye; its karma-based supernormal power is swiftest, and its faculties are complete; it is unobstructed and cannot be turned back; it feeds on fragrance, and does not abide for long; with an inverted mind, it approaches the object of its desire; the moisture-born and the transformation-born are drawn to fragrance or to a suitable place; the gods' heads point upward, three lie horizontal, and the hell-beings' heads point downward. Prose: an intermediate-existence body is seen by others of the same kind; if one has cultivated and attained the utterly pure divine eye, one is likewise able to see it — but ordinary, innately-acquired eyes are entirely unable to perceive it, since it is extremely subtle. There is a further view: the gods' intermediate-existence beings possess eyes fully capable of seeing it. Among the intermediate-existence beings of the five destinies, those of humans, ghosts, animals, and hell-beings can see four, three, two, and one kind respectively — that is, each can see its own kind and those below it, but not those above.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The visibility hierarchy is a nice small piece of cosmological bookkeeping: gods can presumably see all five kinds of intermediate-existence being (being the highest destiny), humans can see four (their own and the three below), down to hell-beings, who can see only their own kind and nothing else. This maps status directly onto perceptual range — the "higher" a destiny sits, the further "down" its awareness extends, while the lowest destinies remain perceptually sealed off from everything above them, in a kind of epistemic mirror of their karmic position.

5. Unmatched Speed, and a Worm Found Alive in Molten Iron

The intermediate-existence being's supernormal speed outstrips everything else produced by karma, even the Buddha himself unable to obstruct it — and its body proves genuinely indestructible, illustrated through the striking image of a worm discovered alive at the very center of a block of blazing iron.

Chinese Source

一切通中業通最疾，[夌*欠]虛自在是謂通義，通由業得名為業通，此通勢用速故名疾。中有具得最疾業通，上至世尊無能遮抑，以業勢力最強盛故。一切中有皆具五根，對謂對礙，此金剛等所不能遮，故名無對。曾聞析破炎赤鐵團，見於其中有蟲生故，應往彼趣中有已生，一切種力皆不能轉，謂不可令人中有沒餘中有起，餘類亦然。為往彼趣中有已起，但應往彼，定不往餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among every kind of supernormal power, the power produced by karma is the swiftest. Traversing empty space with unobstructed freedom is what is meant by "supernormal power"; since this power is obtained through karma, it is called "karmic power," and since the force and function of this power are so rapid, it is called "swift." The intermediate-existence being possesses the very swiftest karmic power of all — not even the World-Honored One, at the very summit, is able to obstruct or restrain it, since the force of its karma is at its most overwhelmingly strong. All intermediate-existence beings, moreover, possess all five faculties complete. "Obstruction" means being blocked or impeded; this body cannot be blocked even by something as hard as a diamond, and so it is called "unobstructed." It has been heard that, when a mass of blazing red-hot iron was split open, a worm was found alive within it — this shows that, once the intermediate-existence being destined for a given place has already arisen there, no force of any kind whatsoever is able to turn it aside: that is, one cannot cause a human intermediate-existence being to vanish and some other kind of intermediate-existence being to arise in its place, and the same holds for every other kind as well. Once the intermediate-existence being destined for a given destiny has already arisen, it can only proceed to that destiny, and will definitely never go to any other.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The claim that not even the Buddha can obstruct the intermediate-existence being's motion is a striking admission of karma's absolute authority within this system — karmic momentum, once set in motion with sufficient force, outruns even the highest attainable spiritual power, since obstructing it would require overriding the very causal law the whole tradition is built on, not merely exercising greater personal strength.

The molten-iron worm anecdote is doing double duty as evidence: it demonstrates the intermediate-existence body's genuine indestructibility (surviving conditions that would annihilate any ordinary physical body instantly), while also illustrating the fixity of karmic destination — once a specific intermediate-existence being has committed to its path toward a specific destiny, absolutely nothing, not even conditions as extreme as molten iron, can redirect it toward becoming some other kind of being instead. The two points reinforce each other: a body tough enough to survive literally anything is also, not coincidentally, a body whose trajectory nothing can bend off course.

6. A Diet of Pure Fragrance

The intermediate-existence being does take nourishment, but of the subtlest possible kind — fragrance alone, with the quality of that fragrance tracking the being's own store of merit, giving the being its scriptural name: gandharva, "the fragrance-eater."

Chinese Source

欲中有身資段食不？雖資段食，然細非麁。其細者何？謂唯香氣，由斯故得健達縛名。諸字界中義非一故，而音短者如設建途及羯建途，略故無過。諸少福者唯食惡香，其多福者好香為食。

Ru-Yi Translation

Does the desire-realm intermediate-existence being's body take nourishment from physical food? Although it does take nourishment from physical food, this nourishment is subtle, not coarse. What is meant by "subtle"? It refers to fragrance alone — and it is on this basis that it obtains the name "gandharva." Since the meaning within these syllabic roots is not singular, a shortened pronunciation such as "śakaṇḍa" for "śakuṇḍagandha," or "karkaṇḍa" for a similarly compounded term, involves no fault, being merely an abbreviation. Those of little merit feed only on foul-smelling fragrance, while those of abundant merit feed on pleasant fragrance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag genuine uncertainty about the specific linguistic example offered here (諸字界中義非一故...) — the point being made is evidently that "gandharva" can be pronounced or analyzed in more than one way as a compound of Sanskrit roots, with a shorter pronunciation still being valid as an acceptable abbreviation, but I do not have confident access to the exact Sanskrit forms being illustrated behind the Chinese transliterations given, and I have rendered them as best I can rather than asserting a precise reconstruction I cannot verify.

The link between merit and the quality of fragrance one can access is a quietly elegant piece of karmic bookkeeping: even in this most minimal, in-between state of existence — no body of the ordinary kind, no visible presence to most other beings — one's accumulated moral history still shapes something as basic as what one is capable of finding nourishing. A being of little merit is not merely unlucky in what it happens to encounter; it is drawn to, and sustained only by, what is foul, while a being of abundant merit finds sustenance in what is pleasant — the same logic of karmic fit that governs rebirth destinations governs even this most fleeting interval between them.

7. How Long Does Intermediate Existence Last? Four Views

A genuinely live scholarly disagreement: Vasubandhu's own teacher holds there is no fixed limit at all — the being simply persists until the conditions for rebirth align — while Vasumitra proposes a maximum of seven days with repeated death-and-rebirth cycling within the interval itself, a further view proposes forty-nine days, and the Vaibhāṣika orthodoxy holds the duration is simply brief.

Chinese Source

如是中有為住幾時？大德說言：此無定限。生緣未合中有恒存，由彼命根非別業引，與所趣人等眾同分一故。若異此者，中有命根最後滅時應立死有。尊者世友言：此極多七日，若生緣未合，便數死數生。有餘師言：極七七日。毘婆沙說：此住少時，以中有中樂求生有故非久住，速往結生。

Ru-Yi Translation

How long, then, does intermediate existence last? The Bhadanta states: there is no fixed limit to this. As long as the conditions for rebirth have not yet come together, the intermediate-existence being persists continuously, since its life faculty is not induced by a separate karma distinct from the karma that will produce the class-commonality it shares with the humans or other beings of its destined kind. If it were otherwise, then, at the moment the intermediate-existence being's life faculty finally ceases, this would have to be established as an existence of death in its own right. The Venerable Vasumitra states: this lasts, at most, seven days; if the conditions for rebirth have not yet come together by then, one repeatedly dies and is reborn as a fresh intermediate-existence being. There is a further view: at most forty-nine days. The Vaibhāṣika masters state: this lasts only a brief time, since, while in intermediate existence, one is eager to seek out the existence of birth, and so does not abide for long, swiftly proceeding to conception.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Bhadanta's argument for "no fixed limit" is a tight piece of reasoning worth spelling out: the intermediate-existence being's own life faculty is not produced by some separate karma of its own — it simply borrows its continuity from the very same karma that will eventually produce the class-commonality of its destined birth. Since that karma has not yet finished its work until conception actually occurs, there is no independent moment at which the intermediate being's own "lifespan" could run out on its own terms; if it could simply expire on a fixed schedule regardless of whether rebirth conditions had aligned, its own final moment would then have to count as yet another "existence of death," requiring yet another intermediate-existence being after it, and so on without end. Vasumitra's rival view solves the same problem differently, by allowing repeated cycles of dying and re-arising within intermediate existence itself whenever the week passes without rebirth conditions aligning — a genuinely different mechanism, but one aimed at avoiding exactly the same infinite-regress worry from the opposite direction.

8. A Puzzle: How Do a Thousand Worms Appear at Once?

A vivid thought experiment tests the "no fixed limit, waiting for conditions" view: when a mountain of rotting flesh suddenly swarms with worms in the summer rains, were all those worms' intermediate-existence beings patiently waiting all along, or did they arrive from elsewhere in that very instant?

Chinese Source

設有肉聚等妙高山，至夏雨時變成虫聚。應言諸中有漸待此時？為說從何方頓來至此？雖無經論誠文判釋，然依正理應作是言：有雜類生數無邊際，貪著香味壽量短促，彼諸有情因嗅此氣，貪香味故俱時命終，由愛覺先感虫身業，同時於此受細虫身。或多有情應俱生此，多緣未合住中有中，今遇多緣方頓生此。應俱生者定不異時，如有能招轉輪王業，要至人壽八萬歲時或過此時方頓與果，非於餘位。此亦應然。故世尊言：諸有情類業果差別不可思議。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose there were a mass of flesh as great as Mount Sumeru itself — when the summer rains arrive, it transforms into a swarming mass of worms. Should it be said that all these intermediate-existence beings had been gradually waiting for this very moment? Or should it be said that they suddenly arrived here from some other direction? Although there is no explicit text in the sutras or treatises that settles this question directly, the following should be said, in accordance with correct reasoning: there exist countless different kinds of beings, with short lifespans, who are deeply attached to fragrance and flavor. These beings, upon smelling this scent, and out of craving for its fragrance and flavor, all die together at the very same moment; through the power of the very karma that had, through this craving-driven awareness, already destined them to a worm's body, they simultaneously take on subtle worm-bodies in this very place. Or again: many sentient beings destined to be born together in this very place may, while the many necessary conditions have not yet come together, remain within intermediate existence — and now, upon encountering the full complement of conditions, they are suddenly all born here at once. Those destined to be born together are certainly not born at different times: it is comparable to karma capable of producing a wheel-turning king, which must wait until human lifespans reach eighty thousand years, or exceed that span, before it suddenly bestows its result, never at any other time. This case should be understood in just the same way. Hence the World-Honored One has said: the distinctions among the karmic results of sentient beings are inconceivable.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two distinct mechanisms are offered, and the text is honest that neither is settled by explicit scriptural text — both are presented as reasoned inference rather than citation. The first mechanism keeps things simple: a large population of short-lived, fragrance-craving beings happen to die together, drawn by the very same scent at the very same moment, and their shared karma for worm-rebirth simply activates simultaneously because the triggering condition (the smell reaching them) is genuinely simultaneous for all of them. The second mechanism is more striking, since it treats intermediate existence as capable of extended, patient waiting: beings already destined to be born together may sit in intermediate existence for an unspecified stretch, until every necessary condition finally converges, at which point they all manifest at once — precisely mirroring how an entire cosmological cycle's worth of specific karma (the kind that produces a wheel-turning king) sits dormant until human lifespans happen to reach exactly eighty thousand years, never firing early and never firing late. The closing citation — "the distinctions among the karmic results of sentient beings are inconceivable" — reads almost as a deliberate acknowledgment that this entire explanation, however reasoned, ultimately rests on trusting karma's precision rather than being able to verify it directly.

9. When the Right Conditions Never Come: Transferring to a Similar Birth

A further proposal addresses what happens when an intermediate-existence being's specifically destined conditions never materialize: it may transfer to a similar kind of birth instead, illustrated through domestic and wild animals' differing mating seasons — but the proposal runs headlong into a sharp objection from a principle the treatise has already firmly established, and the tension is left standing rather than resolved.

Chinese Source

其有生緣未即和合，若定此處此類應生，業力即令此緣和合。若非定託此和合緣，便即寄生餘處餘類，有說轉受相似類生。且如家牛及狗熊馬，欲增次屬夏秋冬春，野牛野干羆驢無定。前四中有若不遇時，如次轉生後四同類。豈不中有必無與生眾同分別，一業引故。如何可言轉受相似？

Ru-Yi Translation

If, for one whose conditions for rebirth have not yet immediately come together, it is definitely fixed that one is to be born in this particular place and as this particular kind of being, the force of karma will itself cause these conditions to come together. But if one is not definitely destined to rely on these particular conditions coming together, one then instead takes temporary birth in some other place, as some other kind of being — there is a view that holds one transfers to take birth as a similar, comparable kind of being instead. For instance, domestic cattle, dogs, bears, and horses have their desire for mating increasing in the order belonging to summer, autumn, winter, and spring, respectively, whereas wild oxen, jackals, brown bears, and donkeys have no fixed season. If the first four kinds' intermediate-existence beings fail to encounter their proper time, they transfer, in the same order, to be born instead as the corresponding one of the latter four, similar kinds. But is it not the case that an intermediate-existence being can never differ from the class-commonality of the birth it is to have, since both are induced by one and the same single karma? How, then, can it be said that it transfers to take birth as merely a similar kind instead?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag this honestly as a case where the text raises a real objection and does not supply an answer within this passage. The proposal itself is a natural enough patch for a genuine problem: domestic cattle mate in a fixed season, but wild cattle do not, so if a specific intermediate-existence being were bound only for a domestic cow's womb and that particular seasonal window somehow never arrived, transferring to the closely related wild-cattle line would at least keep the being within a recognizably similar kind of life rather than leaving it stranded. But this directly contradicts the principle established earlier in this very fascicle — that once an intermediate-existence being destined for a specific place has arisen, "no force of any kind whatsoever is able to turn it aside," precisely because a single karma fixes both the intermediate being and its eventual birth together as one indivisible package. If that principle holds without exception, there should be no room for a mere "transfer to something similar" at all; the objection here presses exactly this tension, and the text moves on to a new topic immediately afterward without resolving it. I have preserved this as the genuine, open loose end it appears to be, rather than inventing a reconciliation the passage itself does not provide.

10. Conception: An Inverted Desire, and the Moment of Entry

One of this treatise's most well-known and psychologically striking passages: the intermediate-existence being, approaching its human birth, is drawn to one future parent by desire and repelled from the other by aversion, according to the sex it is to become — a mechanism described here with the same clinical directness as everything else in this text's embryology.

Chinese Source

如是中有為至所生，先起倒心馳趣欲境，彼由業力所起眼根雖住遠方，能見生處父母交會而起倒心，若男緣母起於男欲、若女緣父起於女欲，翻此緣二俱起瞋心。故《施設論》有如是說：時健達縛於二心中隨一現行，謂愛或恚。彼由起此二種倒心，便謂己身與所愛合。所憎不淨泄至胎時，謂是己有便生喜慰，從茲蘊厚中有便沒，生有起已名已結生。若男處胎依母右脇向背蹲坐，若女處胎依母左脇向腹而住。若非男女住母胎時，隨所起貪如應而住。必無中有非女非男，以中有身必具根故。由處中有或女或男，故入母胎隨應而住，後胎增長或作不男。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, in order to reach the place of its birth, the intermediate-existence being first gives rise to an inverted state of mind and rushes toward the object of its desire. Although its karmically-produced eye remains at a distance, it is able to see its future parents uniting at the place of its birth, and, upon seeing this, gives rise to this inverted state of mind: if it is to become male, it conceives desire for the mother; if it is to become female, it conceives desire for the father. Conversely, it gives rise to feelings of aversion toward the parent of the same sex as itself. Hence the Prajñaptiśāstra states as follows: at that time, the gandharva manifests one or the other of two states of mind — either affection or aversion. Through giving rise to these two kinds of inverted mind, it comes to believe that its own body is united with the one it desires. When the impure fluid of the one it resents is discharged into the womb, it takes this to be its own, and thereupon gives rise to a feeling of joy and comfort; from this point, its aggregates thicken, the intermediate-existence being then perishes, and the existence of birth arises — this is called "conception having occurred." If it is to be male, it dwells within the womb leaning against the mother's right side, crouching with its back facing outward; if it is to be female, it dwells leaning against the mother's left side, facing toward the belly. If it is to be neither male nor female while dwelling within the mother's womb, it dwells in accordance with whichever desire has arisen. There is definitely no intermediate-existence being that is neither female nor male, since an intermediate-existence body must necessarily possess a complete set of faculties. Because, while in the intermediate-existence state, one is definitely either female or male, one enters the mother's womb and dwells accordingly; it is only afterward, as the fetus grows and develops, that one might become a eunuch or otherwise sexually ambiguous.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This passage is well known to scholars of comparative religion and psychology for its striking resemblance to what Western psychoanalytic theory would, many centuries later, call the Oedipal and Electra dynamics — desire directed toward the opposite-sex parent, aversion toward the same-sex parent, at the very threshold of conception. I want to be precise about what the text is and is not claiming, since the resemblance is easy to overstate: this is presented as a mechanical, karmically-determined feature of a specific, temporary being (the intermediate-existence being, not the child or adult that will eventually result), operating for the space of a single moment at conception, and explicitly cast as a delusion — "an inverted state of mind" that misperceives the actual situation (the intermediate being genuinely believes, wrongly, that it is uniting bodily with the parent it desires) rather than as an account of ongoing childhood psychology or family dynamics of any kind. The text's tone throughout is clinical and descriptive, of a piece with everything else in this fascicle's embryology, not tinged with any of the developmental or interpersonal framing later psychoanalytic theory would bring to superficially similar material.

The passage also settles, in passing, a point some readers might wonder about: sex is fixed already at the intermediate-existence stage, before conception even occurs, determined by which parent becomes the object of desire and which the object of aversion — it is not something that emerges only later through fetal development. Ambiguous or atypical sexual development, when it does occur, is treated here as a subsequent complication arising during gestation, not as evidence of an unsettled sex at the moment of conception itself.

11. Do the Parents' Fluids Become the Body, or Merely Support It?

A genuine metaphysical question about conception's mechanics: does karma cause the parents' own reproductive fluids to directly become the physical basis for the new being's sense faculties, or does karma instead generate an entirely new, distinct set of great elements that simply comes to rest within that impure mixture? Two views are offered, each claiming equal support from scripture.

Chinese Source

於此義中復應思擇，為由業力精血大種即成根依？為業別生根依大種依精血住？有言：精血即成根依，謂前無根中有俱滅，後有根者無間續生，如種與芽滅生道理。由斯初位名羯剌藍，亦妙順成此經文句：父母不淨生羯剌藍。又告苾芻：汝等長夜執受血滴增羯吒私。有餘師言：別生大種。如依葉糞別有虫生，不淨聚中生羯剌藍，故說父母不淨生羯剌藍。故與彼經無相違失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this topic, a further point should be considered: is it through the power of karma that the parents' reproductive fluids, as great elements, themselves directly become the basis for the sense faculties? Or does karma instead separately generate a distinct basis for the faculties, a new set of great elements, which then simply rests upon the parents' fluids? There is a view that holds: the reproductive fluids themselves directly become the basis for the faculties — that is, the former, faculty-less intermediate-existence being ceases entirely, and what possesses faculties thereafter arises in immediate succession, according to the same principle by which a seed ceases as a sprout arises. It is on this basis that the very first stage is called kalala, and this reading also accords beautifully with the wording of the sutra passage: "From the parents' impure fluids, the kalala is produced." And the Buddha further told the monks: "You have, throughout the long night of saṃsāra, taken hold of and sustained drops of blood, accumulating them into something the size of a small measure." There is a further view held by other masters: a distinct set of great elements is separately generated — comparable to how, in dependence on decaying leaves and dung, a separate worm is born. Within the impure mass, the kalala is generated as something distinct — and it is on this basis that it is said, "From the parents' impure fluids, the kalala is produced." Hence there is no contradiction with that sutra passage either.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both positions are careful to show they can accommodate the very same scriptural line — "from the parents' impure fluids, the kalala is produced" — while disagreeing sharply about what that line actually describes. The first view reads it as directly causal and continuous: the fluids themselves, without remainder, become the new faculty-possessing body, exactly as a seed does not persist alongside its sprout but gives way to it entirely. The second view reads the same line more loosely, as naming the impure environment within which something genuinely new arises, the way rotting leaves and dung provide the setting for a worm's spontaneous appearance without themselves becoming the worm's body. Neither view is declared the winner here; the text simply shows that the disagreement is not actually a disagreement about what scripture says, but about how a shared form of words should be metaphysically unpacked.

12. Attraction to Fragrance, to Place, and — Strikingly — Even to the Hells

Moisture-born and transformation-born beings are drawn to their birthplace by smell or by sight rather than by desire for parents — raising a genuinely striking question: could this same attraction mechanism possibly draw a being toward rebirth in the hells? The answer turns on the same "inverted mind" already seen at work in ordinary conception.

Chinese Source

如是且說胎卵二生，餘隨所應今次當說。若濕生者染香故生，謂遠嗅知生處香氣便生愛染往彼受生，隨業所應香有淨穢。若化生者染處故生，謂遠觀知當所生處，便生愛染往彼受生，隨業所應處有淨穢。豈於地獄亦生愛染？由心倒故起染無失。謂彼中有，或見自身冷雨寒風之所逼切，見熱地獄火焰熾然，情欣煖觸投身於彼。或見自身熱風盛火之所逼害，見寒地獄，心欲清涼投身於彼。先舊諸師作如是說：由見先造，感彼業時己身伴類馳往赴彼。又天中有首正上升如從坐起，人鬼傍生中有行相還如人等。地獄中有頭下足上顛墜其中。故伽他說：顛墜於地獄，足上頭歸下，由毀謗諸仙，樂寂修苦行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus far, womb-birth and egg-birth have been discussed; the remaining kinds should now be explained, each according to what is appropriate. As for moisture-born beings: they are born through attachment to fragrance — that is, they smell, from a distance, the fragrance of their place of birth, and, giving rise to craving and attachment, go there to be born; depending on what is appropriate to their karma, this fragrance may be pure or foul. As for transformation-born beings: they are born through attachment to a place — that is, they observe, from a distance, their future place of birth, and, giving rise to craving and attachment, go there to be born; depending on what is appropriate to their karma, this place may be pure or foul. But could attachment and craving possibly arise even toward the hells? Because the mind is inverted at this moment, the arising of such attachment involves no contradiction. That is: such an intermediate-existence being may see itself being assailed by cold rain and freezing wind, and, seeing the hot hells' flames blazing fiercely, its heart delights at the thought of warmth, and it throws itself into that place. Or it may see itself being harmed by scorching wind and raging fire, and, seeing the cold hells, its mind longs for coolness, and it throws itself into that place. The elder masters of former times explain it this way: because of what it did in the past, at the very moment its karma is about to ripen, it sees companions of its own kind, and rushes to join them there. Moreover, a god's intermediate-existence being rises with its head held upright, as though rising from a seated position; the intermediate-existence beings of humans, ghosts, and animals move about in a manner just like ordinary humans; a hell-being's intermediate-existence being plunges downward head-first, feet upward, falling into that place. Hence a verse states: "Plunging downward into the hells, feet above and head below — this, on account of having slandered the sages who delight in stillness and practice austerities."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The hell-attraction puzzle is genuinely haunting once its mechanism is spelled out: a being about to fall into a hell of extreme cold perceives its own present situation, however comfortable it may seem to an outside observer, as unbearable freezing exposure, so that the hell's own extreme heat registers, in the moment, as longed-for relief — and the same illusion runs in reverse for the hot hells. Nothing about the destination is actually desirable; the being is deceived at the one moment that matters into believing it is escaping suffering rather than walking directly into it, which is a genuinely bleak but conceptually elegant extension of the same "inverted mind" mechanism already seen driving desire at ordinary conception.

The falling orientations form a small but vivid taxonomy of their own: gods rise as though simply standing up from where they were seated, ordinary destinies move about however an ordinary person would, and only hell-bound beings plunge headfirst — a physical detail the closing verse ties to a specific moral cause, slander directed at ascetic sages devoted to stillness and austerity, giving even this small bodily detail its own retributive logic within the larger system.

13. Four Levels of Awareness at Conception — and What the Unaware Actually Perceive

Not every being enters the womb in the same confused, inverted state: a sutra distinguishes four levels of awareness, from complete confusion throughout to full clarity at every stage — with a genuinely vivid account of what the confused majority actually perceive themselves experiencing, shaped entirely by how much merit they carry.

Chinese Source

前說倒心入母胎藏，一切中有皆定爾耶？不爾。經言：入胎有四。其四者何？頌曰：一於入正知，二三兼住出，四於一切位，及卵恒無知。論曰：有諸有情多集福業勤修念慧，故死生時念力所持正知無亂，於中或有正知入胎，或有正知住胎兼入，或正知出兼知入住。兼言為顯後必帶前。有諸有情福智俱少，入住出位皆不正知，入不正知住出必爾，順結頌法故逆說四。諸卵生者入胎等位皆恒無知。如何卵生？從卵而出。言入胎藏，以卵生者先必入胎。或據當來名卵生者，如契經說：造作有為。世間亦言：煮飯磨麨。故說卵生入胎無失。云何三位正不正知？且諸有情若福微薄，入母胎位起倒想解，見大風雨毒熱嚴寒，或大軍眾聲威亂逼，遂見自入密草稠林葉窟茅廬投樹牆下，住時見已住在此中，出位見身從此處出。若福增多入母胎位起倒想解，自見己身入妙園林升花臺殿居勝床等，住出如前。是謂三時不正知者。若於三位皆能正知，於入等時無倒想解，謂入胎位知自入胎，住出胎時自知住出。

Ru-Yi Translation

It was said above that one enters the mother's womb with an inverted mind — but is this definitely the case for every intermediate-existence being without exception? It is not. A sutra states: there are four kinds of entry into the womb. What are the four? Verse: the first has correct awareness only upon entry; the second and third additionally have it while dwelling and while emerging; the fourth has none at any stage at all — and egg-born beings are always without awareness. Prose: there are certain sentient beings who have accumulated abundant merit-producing karma and diligently cultivated mindfulness and wisdom; hence, at the time of death and rebirth, sustained by the power of mindfulness, they possess correct awareness without confusion. Among these, some have correct awareness only upon entering the womb; some have correct awareness while dwelling in the womb, in addition to upon entering; and some have correct awareness upon emerging, in addition to knowing both entry and dwelling — the word "in addition" indicates that each later stage necessarily includes the one before it. There are other sentient beings whose merit and wisdom are both meager; at the stages of entering, dwelling, and emerging, they lack correct awareness entirely — if one lacks correct awareness upon entry, one necessarily lacks it while dwelling and emerging as well, though the verse states the four in reverse order for the sake of fitting the verse form. All egg-born beings, at the stages of entering the womb and so forth, are always entirely without awareness. But how can egg-born beings be said to "enter the womb," if they emerge from an egg? Because an egg-born being must first enter the womb before the egg is laid — "entering the womb" is spoken of on the basis of what is yet to come, comparable to how a sutra speaks of "performing conditioned actions," and how ordinary speech says "cooking rice" or "grinding flour." Hence there is no fault in speaking of an egg-born being's "entry into the womb." What, then, does correct or incorrect awareness at the three stages actually look like? For sentient beings of meager merit, at the stage of entering the mother's womb, an inverted, mistaken perception arises: they see a great storm of wind and rain, or scorching heat and bitter cold, or hear the tumultuous, threatening din of a great army pressing in upon them, and so they perceive themselves as entering into dense grass, a thick forest, a cave of leaves, a thatched hut, or crawling beneath a tree or a wall; while dwelling there, they perceive themselves as continuing to dwell within that same place; at the stage of emerging, they perceive their body as coming out from that very place. For those whose merit is more abundant, at the stage of entering the mother's womb, an inverted, mistaken perception likewise arises, but of a different kind: they perceive themselves as entering a wondrous garden grove, ascending a flower-adorned terrace or palace, or dwelling upon a magnificent couch, and so on; their perception while dwelling and emerging follows the same pattern. This is what is meant by lacking correct awareness at all three stages. If, at all three stages, one is able to maintain correct awareness, then, at the time of entering and so forth, no inverted, mistaken perception arises at all: that is, at the stage of entering the womb, one knows that one is entering the womb, and, at the time of dwelling in and emerging from the womb, one knows for oneself that one is dwelling and emerging.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The confused majority's experience is genuinely striking to sit with: the actual physical event — a fertilized union, a period of gestation, a birth — is not perceived at all as what it literally is, but is instead overwritten entirely by a symbolic narrative keyed precisely to the being's own store of merit. A being of little merit experiences the womb as a threatening storm forcing it to take shelter in a hovel or thicket; a being of greater merit experiences the very same physical event as an inviting garden and a fine terrace. Neither perception bears any resemblance to what is objectively happening — both are equally "inverted" in the text's own terms — yet the specific content of the illusion still tracks moral history exactly, so that even a confused, mistaken experience is never morally arbitrary.

The egg-born beings' "entry into the womb" is a nice small piece of linguistic housekeeping: since an egg-born creature obviously does not develop inside a womb the way a mammal does, the text resolves the apparent contradiction by treating "womb" here as shorthand for the whole process leading toward birth, the same loose, proleptic way ordinary language describes "cooking rice" while the rice is still raw, or "grinding flour" before the grain has actually become flour — describing a process by naming its eventual outcome in advance.

14. Three Awakened Beings, and Why Their Awareness Differs

The first three levels of conception-awareness are identified with three specific kinds of extraordinary person — a wheel-turning king, a pratyekabuddha, and a fully-awakened Buddha — their differing degrees of clarity tracking the differing balance of karma and wisdom each has cultivated across countless past lives.

Chinese Source

前三種入胎，謂輪王二佛，業智俱勝故，如次四餘生。又別顯示四入胎者，且前三種謂轉輪王、獨覺、大覺，如其次第。初入胎者，謂轉輪王，入位正知，非住非出。二入胎者，謂獨勝覺，入住正知，非於出位。三入胎者，謂無上覺，入住出位皆能正知。此初三人以當名顯。何緣如是三品不同？由業智俱如次勝故。第一業勝，謂轉輪王宿世曾修廣大福故。第二智勝，謂獨勝覺久習多聞勝思擇故。第三俱勝，謂無上覺曠劫修行勝福智故。除前三種，餘胎卵生福智俱劣，合成第四。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first three kinds of entry into the womb belong, respectively, to a wheel-turning king and the two kinds of Buddha, since karma and wisdom are progressively more supreme in each; the fourth belongs to all remaining births. To further clarify the four kinds of entry into the womb: the first three, respectively and in order, belong to a wheel-turning king, a pratyekabuddha, and a fully-awakened Buddha. The first kind of entry belongs to the wheel-turning king: correct awareness only at the stage of entry, neither while dwelling nor while emerging. The second kind of entry belongs to the pratyekabuddha: correct awareness while entering and dwelling, but not at the stage of emerging. The third kind of entry belongs to the unsurpassed, fully-awakened Buddha: correct awareness at the stages of entering, dwelling, and emerging, all three. These first three types of persons are identified here by their future title. Why, then, do these three grades differ in this way? Because karma and wisdom are, respectively and in order, progressively more supreme in each. In the first, karma is supreme: the wheel-turning king has, in past lives, cultivated vast and extensive merit. In the second, wisdom is supreme: the pratyekabuddha has, for a long time, cultivated extensive learning and superior discernment. In the third, both are supreme: the unsurpassed, fully-awakened Buddha has, across measureless eons, cultivated supreme merit and wisdom both together. Excepting these first three kinds, all remaining womb-born and egg-born beings, whose merit and wisdom are both inferior, together make up the fourth kind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The progression from wheel-turning king to pratyekabuddha to fully-awakened Buddha maps a clean, cumulative logic onto the three grades of awareness: the king's clarity is purely a matter of karmic magnitude — vast accumulated merit alone, without any special claim to wisdom — and correspondingly reaches only the first threshold, awareness at entry. The pratyekabuddha adds cultivated discernment on top of ordinary merit, and correspondingly reaches one stage further, remaining aware while dwelling in the womb as well. Only the fully-awakened Buddha, whose cultivation across "measureless eons" perfects both karma and wisdom simultaneously and to their fullest possible degree, achieves unbroken awareness across all three stages, including the disorienting passage of birth itself. The lesson embedded in the structure is worth drawing out: raw karmic power alone, however vast, has a ceiling that only wisdom cultivated alongside it can raise.

15. No Self Travels Between Lives — Only a Flame That Never Stops Moving

Heretics who believe in a persisting self seize on rebirth as proof of their doctrine — surely something must travel from one life to the next. The treatise's answer is one of Buddhist philosophy's most celebrated arguments: nothing need persist unchanged at all, since a lamp's flame, though it perishes and is renewed every single instant, nevertheless demonstrably travels from place to place.

Chinese Source

此中外道執我者言：若許有情轉趣餘世，即我所執有我義成。今為遮彼，頌曰：無我唯諸蘊，煩惱業所為，由中有相續，入胎如燈焰，如引次第增，相續由惑業，更趣於餘世，故有輪無初。論曰：汝等所執我為何相？能捨此蘊、能續餘蘊，內用士夫此定非有，如色眼等不可得故。世尊亦言：有業有異熟，作者不可得。謂能捨此蘊及能續餘蘊，唯除法假。法假謂何？依此有彼有，此生故彼生，廣說緣起。若爾，何等我非所遮？唯有諸蘊，謂唯於蘊假立我名，非所遮遣。若爾，應許諸蘊即能從此世間轉至餘世。蘊剎那滅，於輪轉無能，數習煩惱業所為故，令中有蘊相續入胎。譬如燈焰雖剎那滅，而能相續轉至餘方。諸蘊亦然，名轉無失，故雖無我而由惑業諸蘊相續入胎義成。如業所引次第轉增諸蘊相續，復由煩惱業力所為轉趣餘世，謂非一切所引諸蘊增長相續脩促量齊，引壽業因有差別故。隨能引業勢力增徵，齊爾所時次第增長。

Ru-Yi Translation

At this point, heretics who hold to a doctrine of self say: if it is accepted that a sentient being transfers and proceeds to another world, then the meaning of my own doctrine, that there is a self, is thereby established. To refute this, verse: there is no self — there are only the aggregates, brought about by affliction and karma; through the continuity of intermediate existence, one enters the womb, like a lamp's flame; just as, once induced, it increases in sequence, its continuity is due to affliction and karma, proceeding onward to yet another world — hence the wheel of existence has no beginning. Prose: what characteristic does the self you cling to actually have? You hold that it is capable of relinquishing these aggregates and continuing on to other aggregates, functioning as an inner agent — but such a thing definitely does not exist, since it cannot be apprehended, any more than form or the eye could be apprehended as some further entity behind them. The World-Honored One has likewise said: there is karma, there is karmic ripening, but no agent can be found. That is: there is nothing capable of relinquishing these aggregates and continuing on to other aggregates, except for what is merely conventionally designated, on the basis of dharmas. What is meant by "conventionally designated on the basis of dharmas"? It means: depending on this, that exists; because this arises, that arises — as is explained at length under dependent origination. If that is so, then what kind of "self" is not being refuted here? Only the aggregates themselves — that is, it is only on the basis of the aggregates that the name "self" is conventionally established; this alone is not what is being refuted. If that is so, then it should be accepted that the aggregates themselves are capable of transferring from this world to another. But the aggregates perish moment by moment, and are incapable, on their own, of any such turning or transferring — it is specifically because of what is brought about by habitually-cultivated affliction and karma that the intermediate-existence aggregates are made to continue in succession and enter the womb. It is comparable to a lamp's flame: although it perishes moment by moment, it is nevertheless able to continue in succession and travel to another place. The aggregates are just the same — speaking of them as "transferring" involves no error. Hence, although there is no self, the meaning of the aggregates continuing in succession and entering the womb, on account of affliction and karma, is thereby established. Just as, once induced by karma, the aggregates continue in sequence, progressively increasing, they also, through the force of affliction and karma, proceed onward to yet another world. That is: it is not the case that the aggregates induced in every case grow and continue for an equal, uniform span of time, since the karmic causes that induce a given lifespan differ from one another; in accordance with the strength of the karma capable of inducing that lifespan, the aggregates increase in sequence for precisely that corresponding span of time.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The lamp-flame image is doing exceptionally precise philosophical work, and it rewards being read slowly. A flame at one moment and the flame a fraction of a second later are not, strictly speaking, the same flame at all — each is a distinct, momentary event of combustion that ceases as soon as it arises. And yet no one hesitates to say a lamp's flame "traveled" from one side of a room to the other, or that a fire "spread" from one house to the next, even though nothing literally identical persisted across that motion. The heretic's argument assumes that any genuine continuity or transfer requires some single, unchanging thing to do the traveling; the flame example shows this assumption is simply false even for the most mundane physical processes, well before any question of rebirth arises. If a flame can travel without any single, self-identical flame-substance carrying over from moment to moment, a person's aggregates can likewise continue from one life to the next — propelled specifically by affliction and karma rather than by anything resembling a flame's fuel — without requiring an unchanging self to serve as the vehicle.

The citation from the Buddha — "there is karma, there is karmic ripening, but no agent can be found" — is doing double duty as both a scriptural anchor and a genuinely careful piece of conceptual surgery: it affirms the reality of cause and effect (karma genuinely does produce results) while denying only the further claim that some separate, persisting entity must exist behind the causal process to "do" the acting and "receive" the result. What survives the refutation is not nothing at all — the aggregates themselves, and the label "self" applied loosely to them as a conventional shorthand, are both perfectly real in their own way — only the picture of an unchanging inner agent distinct from the aggregates is what gets ruled out.

16. The Five Stages of Embryonic Development

A classical verse names the five stages an embryo passes through in the womb, from the very first coagulation to the point where hair, nails, and the sense faculties themselves begin to take shape.

Chinese Source

云何次第？如聖說言：最初羯剌藍，次生頞部曇，從此生閉尸，閉尸生鍵南，次鉢羅奢佉，後髮毛爪等，及色根形相，漸次而轉增。謂母腹中分位有五：一羯剌藍位、二頞部曇位、三閉尸位、四鍵南位、五鉢羅奢佉位。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, then, is this sequence? As the noble ones have stated: first comes the kalala; next arises the arbuda; from this arises the peśī; the peśī gives rise to the ghana; next comes the praśākhā; and afterward, hair, body-hair, and nails, together with the physical sense faculties and bodily form, gradually increase in succession. That is, within the mother's womb, there are five stages: first, the kalala stage; second, the arbuda stage; third, the peśī stage; fourth, the ghana stage; fifth, the praśākhā stage.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I have kept these five Sanskrit terms untranslated rather than force approximate English equivalents, since they name a classical, precise embryological sequence this tradition's medical and meditative literature returns to repeatedly, and any single English gloss risks implying more anatomical precision than the terms actually carry: kalala (a coagulated, jelly-like mass), arbuda (a soft swelling), peśī (a fleshy lump), ghana (a firmer, more solid mass), and praśākhā (the stage at which limbs and extremities begin to branch out and differentiate). This sequence became foundational well beyond this text alone, recurring throughout later Buddhist embryological and meditative literature whenever the stages of gestation are discussed.

17. The Passage of Birth

The treatise now describes the birth process itself in unflinching, visceral detail — the womb's foulness, the difficulty and danger of labor, the midwife's careful work, and the newborn's first tender days — presented not for shock but as a traditional meditation on the impurity and suffering inherent in embodied existence from its very first moment.

Chinese Source

此胎中箭漸次轉增，乃至色根形相滿位，由業所起異熟風力轉胎中箭令趣產門。如強糞團過量閟澁，從此轉墮劇苦難任。其母或時威儀飲食執作過分，或由其子宿罪業力死於胎內。時有女人或諸醫者，妙通產法善養嬰兒，溫以酥油睒末梨汁用塗其手執小利刀。內如糞坑最極猥賤雜穢充塞，黑闇所居無量千虫之所依止，常流惡汁恒須對治，精血垢膩潰爛臭滑，不淨流溢鄙穢叵觀，穿漏薄皮以覆其上。宿業所引，身瘡孔中分解支節牽出於外。然此胎子乘宿所為，順後受業所趣難了。或復無難安隱得生，體如新瘡細軟難觸。或母愛子或餘女人，以如刀灰麁澁兩手，執取洗拭而安處之。次含清酥飲以母乳，漸令習受細麁飲食，次第轉增至根熟位，復起煩惱積集諸業。由此身壞，復有如前中有相續更趣餘世。如是惑業為因故生，生復為因起於惑業，從此惑業更復有生，故知有輪旋環無始。

Ru-Yi Translation

This arrow-like embryo within the womb gradually increases in stages, until it reaches the point where its physical sense faculties and bodily form are complete; then, through the force of a karmically-produced wind of ripening, this arrow-like embryo within the womb is turned and directed toward the birth canal — like a hardened, oversized lump of excrement, blocked and unable to pass; from this position, it then falls forth amid intense suffering, difficult to bear. At times, the mother may, through excessive bodily movement, eating, or labor, bring on complications; or, through the power of the child's own past unwholesome karma, the child may die within the womb. At such a time, there are women, or physicians, skilled in the art of midwifery and in caring for infants, who warm their hands with ghee and the sap of the śālmali tree, apply this to their hands, and take up a small, sharp knife. Within the womb, it is like a cesspit, utterly filthy and base, filled with mixed impurity; a dark, dwelling place relied upon by countless thousands of worms; constantly flowing with foul fluid that must always be attended to; reproductive fluid and blood, grimy and greasy, decaying and putrid, foul-smelling and slippery; impure discharge overflowing, base and repulsive to look upon — all of this covered over by nothing more than a thin, perforated layer of skin. Drawn forth by past karma, the infant's body, through this wound-like opening, has its limbs disentangled and is drawn out into the open. This fetal being, carried along by what it has done in the past, proceeds toward a karmic result to be experienced later, whose precise nature is difficult to know in advance. Or, alternatively, it may be born without difficulty, safely and peacefully — its body, like a fresh wound, delicate and soft, difficult even to touch. Either the mother, out of love for her child, or some other woman, using hands as rough as a knife's blade or ash, takes hold of the infant, washes and wipes it clean, and settles it into a resting place. Next, the infant is given clear ghee to taste and is nursed with the mother's milk; gradually, it is accustomed to receiving both fine and coarse food, and, growing in stages, it reaches the stage of matured faculties — at which point it once again gives rise to afflictions and accumulates further karma. On account of this, when the body eventually breaks apart, there is once again, just as before, an intermediate-existence being continuing on to yet another world. In this way, affliction and karma serve as the cause for birth, and birth in turn serves as the cause for the arising of further affliction and karma; from this affliction and karma, birth occurs once again — hence it is known that the wheel of existence turns in an endless cycle, without beginning.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I have translated this passage in full and without softening it, since the text itself does not soften it — this belongs to a well-established genre within Buddhist contemplative literature, sometimes called meditation on the impurity of the body or on the sufferings of birth, whose entire purpose is to counteract an unreflective attachment to the body and to ordinary existence by describing, with unsparing precision, exactly what that existence actually consists of at its very origin. The point is not cruelty toward the mother or the child, nor is it meant to provoke disgust for its own sake; it is meant to dissolve a particular kind of unexamined complacency about embodied life by insisting on the details usually left out of how birth is ordinarily described and remembered. Read in that spirit, the passage functions less as clinical description and more as a formal object of contemplation, continuous with this same fascicle's earlier accounts of hell-beings' suffering and the hungry ghost's endless hunger — all three serve the same underlying purpose of making saṃsāra's condition vivid and undeniable rather than abstract.

The closing lines return the whole embryology discussion to its wider frame: a single human life, from this unglamorous beginning through childhood, maturity, renewed affliction, renewed karma, and eventual death, simply feeds directly into another intermediate-existence being and another life exactly like it. Nothing here is presented as an isolated biological curiosity — it is one full turn of the same wheel the entire fascicle has been tracing, offered as the immediate, lived counterpart to everything this text has argued about intermediate existence in more abstract terms.

18. No Beginning, Yet an End: Resolving an Apparent Contradiction

If nothing arises without a cause, and the wheel of birth and death has been circling since beginningless time, does this not require that time itself go back forever without a first cause? The resolution distinguishes a beginningless past from an achievable end — closing this whole line of teaching before the treatise turns to an entirely new topic.

Chinese Source

若執有始，始應無因；始既無因，餘應自起。現見芽等因種等生，由處及時俱決定故。又由火等熟變等生，由此定無無因起法。說常因論，如前已遣。是故生死決定無初，然有後邊由因盡故。生依因故，因滅壞時生果必亡，理定應爾。如種滅壞芽必不生。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one insists that there is a first beginning, then this beginning would have to be without cause; but if the beginning has no cause at all, then everything else, too, ought to arise spontaneously, without cause. Yet it is directly observed that a sprout, for instance, arises from a cause such as a seed, since both the specific place and the specific time of its arising are fixed and determinate; and, again, things such as cooking and ripening produce transformation through causes such as fire. On this basis, it is certain that there is no dharma whatsoever that arises without a cause. As for the doctrine of a permanent, uncaused first cause — this has already been refuted above. For this reason, birth-and-death definitely has no beginning — yet it does have an end, since its cause can be exhausted. Since birth depends upon a cause, when that cause is destroyed and eliminated, the resulting birth must likewise cease to exist — this is certainly how the principle must work, just as, when a seed is destroyed, a sprout definitely cannot arise from it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The asymmetry the treatise insists on here is genuinely important, and easy to miss on a first read: "no beginning" and "no end" are not two halves of the same claim, and this tradition affirms only the first. A beginning would require an uncaused first cause, which the earlier refutation of a single divine creator (in fascicle 7) already ruled out as incoherent — hence the past must stretch back without any first moment. But an end requires nothing so exotic: it only requires that the specific causes sustaining the cycle — affliction and karma — be brought to a stop, exactly as a sprout simply fails to arise once its seed has been destroyed. This is, in miniature, the entire soteriological promise of the Buddhist path stated in its most compressed causal form: saṃsāra's beginninglessness is not a life sentence, since ending the cause ends the result, without needing to somehow unwind or reach back through infinite past time to do so.

With this, the treatise closes its extended teaching on intermediate existence, conception, gestation, and birth — the whole arc that occupied fascicle 8's final debate and the majority of this fascicle so far. It now turns to name, with full technical precision, the very causal chain it has just walked through in narrative form: the twelve links of dependent origination.

19. The Twelve Links, Mapped Across Three Lifetimes

A new and momentous topic: the twelve links of dependent origination, here read specifically as spanning three lifetimes — two links belonging to a past life, eight unfolding within the present one, and two more reaching into a future life — with a careful qualification about who actually experiences the complete twelve-link course.

Chinese Source

如是蘊相續，說三生為位。頌曰：如是諸緣起，十二支三際，前後際各二，中八據圓滿。論曰：十二支者，一、無明；二、行；三、識；四、名色；五、六處；六、觸；七、受；八、愛；九、取；十、有；十一、生；十二、老死。言三際者，一前際、二後際、三中際，即是過未及現三生。云何十二支於三際建立？謂前後際各立二支，中際八支，故成十二。無明行在前際，生老死在後際，所餘八在中際。此中際八，一切有情此一生中皆具有不？非皆具有。若爾，何故說有八支？據圓滿者。此中意說，補特伽羅歷一切位名圓滿者，非諸中夭及色無色，但據欲界補特伽羅。《大緣起經》說具有故。彼說，佛告阿難陀言：識若不入胎，得增廣大不？不也。世尊！乃至廣說。有時但說二分緣起，一前際攝、二後際攝。前七支前際攝，謂無明乃至受；後五支後際攝，謂從愛至老死，前後因果二分攝故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, the continuity of the aggregates is spoken of, taking three lifetimes as its stages. Verse: these conditions of dependent origination number twelve links across three time-periods; the earlier and later periods each have two, while the middle eight apply to one who experiences the full complement. Prose: the twelve links are: first, ignorance; second, formations; third, consciousness; fourth, name-and-form; fifth, the six sense-bases; sixth, contact; seventh, feeling; eighth, craving; ninth, grasping; tenth, existence; eleventh, birth; twelfth, aging-and-death. As for the three time-periods: first, the prior period; second, the later period; third, the middle period — these correspond respectively to the past, future, and present lifetimes. How, then, are the twelve links established across the three periods? The prior and later periods each establish two links, and the middle period establishes eight, thus making twelve in total. Ignorance and formations belong to the prior period; birth and aging-and-death belong to the later period; the remaining eight belong to the middle period. Do all sentient beings, within this single lifetime, necessarily possess all eight of these middle-period links in full? Not necessarily. If that is so, then why does the teaching speak of eight links? This is stated with reference to one who experiences the complete, full course. What is meant here is: a person who passes through every single stage is called "one who experiences the full course" — this does not include those who die prematurely in the middle, nor those in the form and formless realms, but refers only to a desire-realm person, since the Mahānidāna Sutra speaks of possessing them in full. That sutra states: the Buddha said to Ānanda, "If consciousness did not enter the womb, could name-and-form grow and increase in scope?" "No, World-Honored One!" — and so on, at length. At times, dependent origination is spoken of as having only two divisions: one subsumed under the prior period, and one subsumed under the later period. The first seven links are subsumed under the prior period — that is, from ignorance up through feeling; the remaining five links are subsumed under the later period — that is, from craving up through aging-and-death — since these are divided into the two portions of prior cause and later effect.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The qualification about who counts as experiencing "the full complement" is a precise and easily-missed piece of scope-limiting: this specific three-lifetime, twelve-link account is explicitly restricted to a desire-realm person who lives out a complete life-course without premature death, and does not directly describe form-realm or formless-realm beings at all. This matters because it clarifies what kind of explanation is actually on offer here — not a metaphysical claim about every possible causal chain a being could undergo, but a canonical template mapped onto the single most common and complete case, against which every other, partial case can then be measured and understood.

20. What Each Link Actually Consists Of

A dense, technical verse pins down exactly what mental or physical event constitutes each of the twelve links — a task complicated by the fact that terms like "ignorance," "consciousness," and "feeling" already have broader technical meanings elsewhere in this treatise; here, each is narrowed to a specific, precisely-bounded stage within this particular three-lifetime causal chain.

Chinese Source

無明等支何法為體？頌曰：宿惑位無明，宿諸業名行，識正結生蘊，六處前名色，從生眼等根，三和前六處，於三受因異，未了知名觸，在婬愛前受，貪資具婬愛，為得諸境界，遍馳求名取，有謂正能造，牽當有果業，結當有名生，至當受老死。論曰：於宿生中諸煩惱位，至今果熟，總謂無明，彼與無明俱時行故。由無明力，彼現行故。如說王行，非無導從，王俱勝故總謂王行。於宿生中福等業位，至今果熟，總得行名。初句位言流至老死。於母胎等正結生時，一剎那位，五蘊名識。結生識後、六處生前，中間諸位總稱名色。此中應說四處生前，而言六者，據滿立故。眼等已生，至根境識未和合位，得六處名。已至三和，未了三受因差別位，總名為觸。已了三受因差別相，未起婬貪，此位名受。貪妙資具婬愛現行，未廣追求，此位名愛。為得種種上妙境界，周遍馳求，此位名取。因馳求故，積集能牽當有果業，此位名有。由是業力從此捨命，正結當有，此位名生。當有生支，即如今識生剎那後漸增，乃至當來受位，總名老死。如是老死，即如今世名色、六處、觸、受四支。辯十二支體別如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

What dharma constitutes the essential nature of ignorance and the other links? Verse: the stage of past affliction is ignorance; past karma of every kind is called formations; the aggregate precisely at the moment of conception is consciousness; before the six sense-bases arise is name-and-form; from the arising of the eye and other faculties, up until the threefold union, this is the six sense-bases; the stage in which the differing causes of the three feelings are not yet clearly known is called contact; the stage prior to sexual craving is feeling; craving for the necessities that sustain enjoyment, together with sexual craving, in order to obtain various objects, running about everywhere in pursuit, this is called grasping; what is able to actually produce and draw forth the karma for a future resultant existence is called existence; what binds one to that future existence is called birth; and up to the point of undergoing that experience is aging-and-death. Prose: the stage of afflictions occurring within a past life, whose result has now ripened, is collectively called "ignorance," since these afflictions operate together with ignorance at the same time, and manifest specifically through the power of ignorance — comparable to speaking of "the king's procession": although the king is not without an entourage accompanying him, since the king is the most prominent among them all, the whole procession is collectively called "the king's procession." The stage of merit-producing and other karma occurring within a past life, whose result has now ripened, is collectively given the name "formations." The word "stage," introduced in the verse's first line, applies continuously all the way through to "aging-and-death." At the very moment of conception within the mother's womb and the like, that single-instant stage — the five aggregates at that moment — is called "consciousness." After the consciousness of conception, and before the six sense-bases arise, all the intervening stages are collectively called "name-and-form." Strictly speaking, this should be described as the stage before four of the sense-bases arise, but it speaks of six because this is established with reference to the complete, full set. From the point the eye and other faculties have arisen, up until the stage where faculty, object, and consciousness have not yet come together, this is given the name "the six sense-bases." Once the threefold union has occurred, but the distinct causes of the three feelings are not yet clearly discerned, this whole stage is collectively named "contact." Once the distinct characteristics of the three feelings' causes have been clearly discerned, but sexual craving has not yet arisen, this stage is named "feeling." When craving for fine necessities and sexual desire actually manifest, but before one broadly pursues them, this stage is named "craving." When one runs about everywhere in pursuit, in order to obtain all sorts of superior, wonderful objects, this stage is named "grasping." Because of this pursuit, one accumulates karma capable of drawing forth a future resultant existence — this stage is named "existence." Through the force of this karma, one relinquishes this life and precisely binds oneself to that future existence — this stage is named "birth." That future existence's link of "birth" — corresponding, in the present case, to consciousness's moment of arising and its subsequent gradual increase, all the way up to the future stage of feeling — is, in that future life, collectively named "aging-and-death." Thus, that future "aging-and-death" corresponds, in terms of the present life's scheme, to the four links of name-and-form, the six sense-bases, contact, and feeling. This is how the essential nature of each of the twelve links, individually, has been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king's-procession analogy is a genuinely useful key to how this whole verse works: just as "the king's procession" names an entire retinue by its most prominent member without denying that many other people are present, each link-name here designates an entire cluster of mental and physical events occurring during a given stage by whichever single factor is most prominent or definitive at that stage — "ignorance" names an entire bundle of past-life afflictions, not literally just the mental factor of ignorance in isolation, and "feeling" names a whole stage of development, not merely the feeling-aggregate on its own. This is exactly the same "named for its most prominent member" logic used elsewhere in this treatise (recall the twelve minds' 加行/生得 wholesome-mind naming, or the six causes' naming conventions), applied here to compress an entire three-lifetime developmental arc into twelve manageable labels.

The closing structural point deserves to be stated plainly, since it is easy to lose amid the technical detail: the future life's "birth" and "aging-and-death" are not some entirely different kind of event from the present life's "consciousness," "name-and-form," "the six sense-bases," and "contact" — they are the very same kind of developmental sequence, just relabeled once the frame of reference shifts to a life not yet lived. The twelve links are not twelve genuinely different kinds of phenomena; they are the same basic developmental process — consciousness entering a womb, developing sense-bases, encountering objects, generating craving — described three times over, once for each of three lifetimes in sequence, with only the labels shifting to mark which lifetime's version is meant.

21. Four Ways of Reading Dependent Origination

The three-lifetime reading just given is only one of four recognized ways to read the same twelve links — including a genuinely striking "momentary" reading in which all twelve are compressed into the single instant of an act as ordinary as a craving-driven killing.

Chinese Source

又諸緣起差別說四：一者剎那、二者連縛、三者分位、四者遠續。云何剎那？謂剎那頃由貪行殺具十二支，癡謂無明，思即是行，於諸境事了別名識，識俱三蘊總稱名色，住名色根說為六處，六處對餘和合有觸，領觸名受，貪即是愛，與此相應諸纏名取，所起身語二業名有，如是諸法起即名生，熟變名老滅壞名死。復有說者，剎那、連縛如《品類足》，俱遍有為。十二支位所有五蘊皆分位攝。即此懸遠相續無始說名遠續。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, dependent origination is spoken of, with distinctions, as being of four kinds: first, momentary; second, continuously-linked; third, staged; fourth, remotely-continuous. What is meant by "momentary"? It means that, within the very same instant, all twelve links are present together in the act of killing driven by craving: delusion is ignorance; volition is precisely formations; discernment with respect to the object is called consciousness; the three aggregates co-arising with consciousness are collectively called name-and-form; the faculties on which name-and-form depend are called the six sense-bases; the coming-together of the six sense-bases with their respective conditions is contact; the experiencing of that contact is feeling; craving is precisely craving itself; the entanglements associated with this craving are grasping; the bodily and verbal karma thereby produced is existence; the arising of these dharmas in this way is called birth; their ripening and changing is called aging; and their cessation and destruction is called death. There is a further explanation: the momentary and continuously-linked kinds, as explained in the Prakaraṇapāda, both pervade every conditioned dharma universally. At each of the twelve links' stages, all five aggregates present are subsumed under that particular stage. And this same continuity, extending remotely and without beginning, is called "remotely-continuous."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag that the text defines "momentary" explicitly and in vivid detail, but treats "continuously-linked" much more briefly, by cross-reference to the Prakaraṇapāda rather than spelling it out fully here — so my understanding of how the four modes relate to one another is partly reconstructed from context rather than fully explicit in this passage. As I understand the fourfold scheme: the "momentary" reading compresses all twelve links into a single instant of any ordinary act of intention and action, exactly as illustrated by the killing example; the "continuously-linked" reading likely refers to the same twelve-link causal sequence unfolding in unbroken, moment-to-moment succession at any timescale, without requiring the single-instant compression of the first mode; the "staged" reading is the three-lifetime account just given across sections 19 and 20, where each link names an extended period rather than a moment; and "remotely-continuous" names the same causal pattern repeating across the entire beginningless span of saṃsāra, life after life without limit.

The momentary reading is genuinely striking once its implications are drawn out: an act as ordinary and confined as a single moment of craving-driven violence already contains, in miniature, the complete structure of ignorance, volition, discernment, contact, feeling, craving, grasping, and karmic result that the three-lifetime reading spreads out across an entire span of rebirths. This suggests dependent origination is not merely a story about how one life leads to the next, but a structural pattern repeating at every scale simultaneously — the same twelve-part architecture visible equally in a single act of the mind and in the whole arc of a life.
