Black Karma, White Karma, and Neither

Fascicle 16 continues Chapter 4 (分別業品第四之四, the Karma-nirdeśa) directly from fascicle 15's long survey of the sūtras' classifications of karma. It opens with the sūtras' fourfold division into black, white, mixed, and neither-black-nor-white karma, then moves through the three sages, the three purities, a genuine dispute over whether greed, hatred, and wrong view are themselves a form of mental karma, and the opening of the treatise's extended treatment of the ten karma-paths.

1. Black Karma, White Karma, Mixed, and Neither

Continuing directly from fascicle 15's long survey of the sūtras' classification schemes for karma, the treatise takes up one final, especially rich fourfold division: karma that is black with a black ripened result, karma that is white with a white ripened result, karma that is black-and-white with a black-and-white ripened result, and karma that is neither black nor white, having no ripened result at all, and capable of exhausting every other kind of karma. The Buddha teaches this division based on differences in the moral quality and character of karma's own result, and on the differing relationship between what is to be overcome and what overcomes it. All unwholesome karma is called black without exception, since it is defiled in nature and its ripened result is likewise black, being undesirable. Wholesome karma belonging to the form realm is called white without exception, since it is unmixed with anything unwholesome, and its ripened result is likewise white, being desirable. Desire-realm wholesome karma, meanwhile, is called black-and-white, since it is mixed together with unwholesome karma there, and its ripened result is likewise mixed — this label describes an entire continuum's mixed pattern of activity, not any single act or its single result, since no individual karmic act, and no individual ripened result, can itself be both black and white at once, these being mutually exclusive qualities. Finally, uncontaminated karma, capable of permanently exhausting all three of the preceding kinds, is called neither black — since it is undefiled — nor white, in the sense that it does not bring about a white ripened result, though this second "neither" is meant in a specifically qualified sense, since the Buddha elsewhere describes the qualities of one beyond training as themselves "purely wholesome, purely white, entirely without fault."

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, a sūtra states that karma is of four kinds: there is karma that is black with a black ripened result; there is karma that is white with a white ripened result; there is karma that is black-and-white with a black-and-white ripened result; and there is karma that is neither black nor white, without any ripened result, capable of exhausting all other karma. What is the character of these? Verse: with reference to these distinctions of black and the rest, four kinds of karma are spoken of — unwholesome karma, form-realm wholesome karma, desire-realm wholesome karma, and the uncontaminated capable of exhausting these — should, in this order, be understood as named black, white, mixed, and neither. Prose: the Buddha, with reference to differences in the nature and category of karma's result, and to the distinct relationship between what is to be overcome and its antidote, speaks of black and the other three. All unwholesome karma is uniformly called black, since it is defiled in nature, and its ripened result is likewise black, being undesirable. Wholesome karma of the form realm is uniformly called white, since it is unmixed with anything unwholesome, and its ripened result is likewise white, being desirable. [Objection:] Why is formless-realm wholesome karma not mentioned? [Reply:] It is said that this classification applies only where two kinds of ripened result exist — namely, in the intermediate state and at birth — and where all three kinds of karma are complete — namely, bodily, verbal, and mental; hence it is not spoken of with respect to any other case. Yet a sūtra elsewhere also states that desire-realm wholesome karma is called black-and-white, since it is mixed together with unwholesome karma there, and its ripened result is likewise black-and-white, being mixed with an undesirable result. This name "black-and-white" is established with reference to a continuum, not to any single act's own essential nature — why is this so? Because there is no single act of karma, and no single ripened result, that is itself both black and white, these being mutually opposed. [Objection:] Is it not the case that unwholesome karma's result is likewise mixed with wholesome karma's result — should this not, then, equally be called "white-and-black"? [Reply:] Unwholesome karma's result is not necessarily mixed with wholesome karma's result, whereas desire-realm wholesome karma's result is necessarily mixed with unwholesome karma's result, since, within the desire realm, unwholesome karma predominates over wholesome karma. Uncontaminated karma, capable of permanently exhausting the preceding three kinds of karma, is called "not black," since it is undefiled, and also called "not white," in that it is incapable of bringing about a white ripened result. This phrase "not white" is stated with hidden intent, since the Buddha, in the Great Emptiness Sūtra, tells Ānanda: "The dharmas of one beyond training are purely wholesome, purely white, entirely without fault." The root treatise likewise states: "What is a white dharma? It is wholesome dharmas that are not defiled-neutral and have no ripened result" — since these do not fall within any realm, and are opposed in nature to the dharmas of continued cyclic existence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "not white" qualification for uncontaminated karma is a genuinely delicate piece of scriptural reconciliation, and we want to flag exactly what tension it is resolving: on the one hand, this section's own definition calls uncontaminated karma "not white" because it produces no ripened result at all (white specifically meaning "productive of a desirable ripened result" in this classification); on the other hand, a separate, well-known sūtra passage has the Buddha himself describing an arhat's own qualities as "purely white." The treatise's resolution is that these two uses of "white" are simply operating in different technical registers — the fourfold black/white/mixed/neither scheme uses "white" in the narrow, ripening-specific sense just defined, while the Great Emptiness Sūtra passage uses "white" in a broader, more general sense of moral purity — and the root treatise's own citation is marshaled to confirm that "white dharma," properly defined, excludes exactly the kind of result-producing karma this section's fourfold scheme is concerned with.

The point about "black-and-white" describing "a continuum, not... any single act's own essential nature" is worth holding onto as a recurring methodological principle in this treatise: several classification schemes across this and the previous fascicle (recall, for instance, fascicle 13's "mixed karma" bringing about both a pleasant and unpleasant result at once) describe a *pattern across many acts and their results over time*, not some genuinely hybrid quality inhering in any single karmic act — a single act is always cleanly one thing or another, even when a person's overall karmic life story looks decidedly mixed.
