Black Karma, White Karma, and Neither

Fascicle 16 continues Chapter 4 (分別業品第四之四, the Karma-nirdeśa) directly from fascicle 15's long survey of the sūtras' classifications of karma. It opens with the sūtras' fourfold division into black, white, mixed, and neither-black-nor-white karma, then moves through the three sages, the three purities, a genuine dispute over whether greed, hatred, and wrong view are themselves a form of mental karma, and the opening of the treatise's extended treatment of the ten karma-paths.

1. Black Karma, White Karma, Mixed, and Neither

Continuing directly from fascicle 15's long survey of the sūtras' classification schemes for karma, the treatise takes up one final, especially rich fourfold division: karma that is black with a black ripened result, karma that is white with a white ripened result, karma that is black-and-white with a black-and-white ripened result, and karma that is neither black nor white, having no ripened result at all, and capable of exhausting every other kind of karma. The Buddha teaches this division based on differences in the moral quality and character of karma's own result, and on the differing relationship between what is to be overcome and what overcomes it. All unwholesome karma is called black without exception, since it is defiled in nature and its ripened result is likewise black, being undesirable. Wholesome karma belonging to the form realm is called white without exception, since it is unmixed with anything unwholesome, and its ripened result is likewise white, being desirable. Desire-realm wholesome karma, meanwhile, is called black-and-white, since it is mixed together with unwholesome karma there, and its ripened result is likewise mixed — this label describes an entire continuum's mixed pattern of activity, not any single act or its single result, since no individual karmic act, and no individual ripened result, can itself be both black and white at once, these being mutually exclusive qualities. Finally, uncontaminated karma, capable of permanently exhausting all three of the preceding kinds, is called neither black — since it is undefiled — nor white, in the sense that it does not bring about a white ripened result, though this second "neither" is meant in a specifically qualified sense, since the Buddha elsewhere describes the qualities of one beyond training as themselves "purely wholesome, purely white, entirely without fault."

又經中說，業有四種，謂或有業黑黑異熟、或復有業白白異熟、或復有業黑白黑白異熟、或復有業非黑非白無異熟能盡諸業。其相云何？頌曰： 依黑黑等殊， 所說四種業， 惡色欲界善， 能盡彼無漏， 應知如次第， 名黑白俱非。 論曰：佛依業果性類不同，所治能治殊，說黑黑等四。諸不善業一向名黑，染污性故，異熟亦黑，不可意故。色界善業一向名白，不雜惡故，異熟亦白，是可意故。何故不言無色界善？傳說若處有二異熟謂中生有，具三種業謂身語意，則說非餘。然契經中有處亦說：欲界善業名為黑白，惡所雜故，異熟亦黑白，非愛果雜故。此黑白名依相續立，非據自性。所以者何？以無一業及一異熟是黑亦白，互相違故。豈不惡業果善業果雜故，是則亦應名為白黑。不善業果非必應為善業果雜，欲善業果必定應為惡業果雜，以欲界中惡勝善故。諸無漏業能永斷盡前三業者名為非黑，不染污故，亦名非白，以不能招白異熟故。此非白言是密意說，以佛於彼《大空經》中告阿難陀：諸無學法純善純白一向無罪。本論亦言：云何白法？謂諸善法無覆無記無異熟者，不墮界故、與流轉法性相違故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, a sūtra states that karma is of four kinds: there is karma that is black with a black ripened result; there is karma that is white with a white ripened result; there is karma that is black-and-white with a black-and-white ripened result; and there is karma that is neither black nor white, without any ripened result, capable of exhausting all other karma. What is the character of these? Verse: with reference to these distinctions of black and the rest, four kinds of karma are spoken of — unwholesome karma, form-realm wholesome karma, desire-realm wholesome karma, and the uncontaminated capable of exhausting these — should, in this order, be understood as named black, white, mixed, and neither. Prose: the Buddha, with reference to differences in the nature and category of karma's result, and to the distinct relationship between what is to be overcome and its antidote, speaks of black and the other three. All unwholesome karma is uniformly called black, since it is defiled in nature, and its ripened result is likewise black, being undesirable. Wholesome karma of the form realm is uniformly called white, since it is unmixed with anything unwholesome, and its ripened result is likewise white, being desirable. [Objection:] Why is formless-realm wholesome karma not mentioned? [Reply:] It is said that this classification applies only where two kinds of ripened result exist — namely, in the intermediate state and at birth — and where all three kinds of karma are complete — namely, bodily, verbal, and mental; hence it is not spoken of with respect to any other case. Yet a sūtra elsewhere also states that desire-realm wholesome karma is called black-and-white, since it is mixed together with unwholesome karma there, and its ripened result is likewise black-and-white, being mixed with an undesirable result. This name "black-and-white" is established with reference to a continuum, not to any single act's own essential nature — why is this so? Because there is no single act of karma, and no single ripened result, that is itself both black and white, these being mutually opposed. [Objection:] Is it not the case that unwholesome karma's result is likewise mixed with wholesome karma's result — should this not, then, equally be called "white-and-black"? [Reply:] Unwholesome karma's result is not necessarily mixed with wholesome karma's result, whereas desire-realm wholesome karma's result is necessarily mixed with unwholesome karma's result, since, within the desire realm, unwholesome karma predominates over wholesome karma. Uncontaminated karma, capable of permanently exhausting the preceding three kinds of karma, is called "not black," since it is undefiled, and also called "not white," in that it is incapable of bringing about a white ripened result. This phrase "not white" is stated with hidden intent, since the Buddha, in the Great Emptiness Sūtra, tells Ānanda: "The dharmas of one beyond training are purely wholesome, purely white, entirely without fault." The root treatise likewise states: "What is a white dharma? It is wholesome dharmas that are not defiled-neutral and have no ripened result" — since these do not fall within any realm, and are opposed in nature to the dharmas of continued cyclic existence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "not white" qualification for uncontaminated karma is a genuinely delicate piece of scriptural reconciliation, and we want to flag exactly what tension it is resolving: on the one hand, this section's own definition calls uncontaminated karma "not white" because it produces no ripened result at all (white specifically meaning "productive of a desirable ripened result" in this classification); on the other hand, a separate, well-known sūtra passage has the Buddha himself describing an arhat's own qualities as "purely white." The treatise's resolution is that these two uses of "white" are simply operating in different technical registers — the fourfold black/white/mixed/neither scheme uses "white" in the narrow, ripening-specific sense just defined, while the Great Emptiness Sūtra passage uses "white" in a broader, more general sense of moral purity — and the root treatise's own citation is marshaled to confirm that "white dharma," properly defined, excludes exactly the kind of result-producing karma this section's fourfold scheme is concerned with.

The point about "black-and-white" describing "a continuum, not... any single act's own essential nature" is worth holding onto as a recurring methodological principle in this treatise: several classification schemes across this and the previous fascicle (recall, for instance, fascicle 13's "mixed karma" bringing about both a pleasant and unpleasant result at once) describe a *pattern across many acts and their results over time*, not some genuinely hybrid quality inhering in any single karmic act — a single act is always cleanly one thing or another, even when a person's overall karmic life story looks decidedly mixed.

2. Which Path-Moments Exhaust Which Kinds of Karma

The treatise now asks whether uncontaminated karma exhausts all three preceding kinds of karma in the very same way, and finds that it does not — different specific moments of uncontaminated attainment are responsible for exhausting different kinds. The four patience-attainments belonging to the path of seeing (direct insight into suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path itself), together with the first eight uninterrupted paths belonging to the path of cultivation as one progressively eliminates the desire realm's own defilements, comprise twelve total moments of uncontaminated volition, and these exhaust only purely black karma — that is, unwholesome karma. The ninth and final uninterrupted path in this same process of eliminating desire-realm defilement, however, involves a single further moment of uncontaminated volition that exhausts both mixed (black-and-white) karma and the last portion of purely black karma at once, since this is the very moment that finally and completely severs the desire realm's wholesome karma from any further unwholesome admixture, while simultaneously finishing off the ninth and final grade of unwholesome karma itself. Beyond this, the ninth uninterrupted path belonging to each of the four form-realm meditative absorptions, considered one level at a time, exhausts purely white karma — that is, form-realm wholesome karma — specifically at that level. This raises a natural question: why is it always specifically the very last of these paths, and none of the others along the way, that actually exhausts a given level's contaminated wholesome dharmas? The answer is that wholesome dharmas are not overcome by their own intrinsic nature the way afflictions are, and so, even after most of a level's affliction has already been severed, the corresponding wholesome dharmas belonging to that same level can still, in principle, actively manifest again and again — it is specifically the complete exhaustion of the afflictions that take those wholesome dharmas as their object that finally allows those wholesome dharmas to be said to be "severed," since it is only then that they become fully freed from the afflictive bondage that previously kept them tied to that level; so long as even one final grade of such affliction remains outstanding, the wholesome dharmas in question cannot yet be said to be severed, since they have not yet actually been freed from that bondage.

Ru-Yi Translation

Does uncontaminated karma exhaust all three of the preceding kinds of karma equally? It does not. How is this so? Verse: the four patience-attainments, and the first eight uninterrupted paths of eliminating desire — twelve uncontaminated volitions — exhaust only purely black karma; the ninth uninterrupted volition of eliminating desire exhausts both mixed and the remainder of purely black karma at once; and the [ninth uninterrupted volition] of eliminating each of the four meditative absorptions, four in number, causes purely white karma to be exhausted. Prose: within the path of seeing, the four patience-attainments of dharma-knowledge, together with, within the path of cultivation, the first eight uninterrupted noble paths accompanying the elimination of desire-realm defilement, comprise twelve volitions that exhaust only purely black karma. The ninth uninterrupted noble path accompanying the elimination of desire-realm defilement involves one further uncontaminated volition that exhausts both mixed karma and purely black karma at once, since this is the moment that entirely severs desire-realm wholesome karma [from further unwholesome admixture], and also exhausts the ninth grade of unwholesome karma. The ninth uninterrupted path accompanying the elimination of defilement belonging, one at a time, to each of the four meditative absorptions causes purely white karma to be exhausted. For what reason can contaminated wholesome dharmas belonging to any given level be severed only by the very last path there, and not by any other? Because wholesome dharmas are not severed by their own intrinsic nature, since, even once mostly severed, they still admit of manifesting again in the present. Rather, it is only once the affliction that takes them as its object has been exhausted that these wholesome dharmas are said to be severed, since it is at that point that the wholesome dharmas become freed from bondage. Hence, so long as even one further grade of the affliction taking them as its object still remains, the sense of "severing" is not yet established, since the wholesome dharmas have not yet actually become freed from bondage at that point.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction drawn here between how afflictions are severed and how wholesome dharmas are "severed" is conceptually important and easy to blur: an affliction is eliminated by its own antidote acting directly on its own intrinsic nature, moment by moment, until nothing remains — but a wholesome dharma is never itself the target of any antidote (nothing needs to "cure" a wholesome state), so its disappearance from a given level cannot work the same way. Instead, wholesome dharmas become "severed" only in the indirect, relational sense of becoming permanently freed from the affliction that had been binding them to that level — meaning the wholesome dharma's own continuity is only broken as a side effect of the affliction's own complete elimination, which is why it takes until the very last moment of that elimination process (the ninth uninterrupted path) for this severing to actually occur, no matter how much progress has already been made against the affliction beforehand.

This section's careful path-by-path bookkeeping (twelve volitions for purely black karma, one further volition for mixed-and-remaining-black, four more for purely white, one per meditative absorption) is characteristic of this treatise's precision when mapping doctrine onto the technical structure of the path of seeing and path of cultivation — structures already established in earlier fascicles' discussion of the nine grades of affliction-elimination and the uninterrupted-versus-liberation-path distinction (see, for instance, fascicle 13's discussion of the "nine uninterrupted paths" underlying severing restraint).

3. Alternative Views on Black and Mixed Karma, and the Three Sages

The treatise records two further, alternative proposals for how to understand "purely black" and "mixed" karma, distinct from the account already given. Some teachers hold that karma conducive to a hell-rebirth, and desire-realm karma conducive to any other kind of rebirth, should respectively be called purely black and mixed karma — since hell's own ripened result is brought about only by unwholesome karma, karma conducive to that rebirth is called purely black, while every other kind of ripened result within the desire realm is brought about by both wholesome and unwholesome karma alike, so karma conducive to those other rebirths is called black-and-white. Other teachers hold instead that karma abandoned through seeing the truths, and all remaining desire-realm karma, should respectively be called purely black and mixed karma — since karma abandoned through seeing involves no admixture of anything wholesome at all, it is called purely black, while karma abandoned through cultivation within the desire realm includes both wholesome and unwholesome instances, and so is called mixed. The treatise then turns to a related sūtra classification: the three sages, and the three purities, both said to comprise body, speech, and mind. The manifested bodily and verbal karma of one beyond training (an arhat) is called the bodily sage and the verbal sage; the "mental sage," however, refers not to any manifested mental karma at all, but simply to the arhat's own mind beyond training as such — since, in the ultimate sense, a sage's essential nature is mind alone, something inferred indirectly through the evidence of bodily and verbal karma, and since bodily and verbal karma alone possess the character of active renunciation (there being no unmanifested mental karma to speak of), it is specifically through this quality of renunciation that the name "sage" is established, meaning it is really the mind itself, expressed through body and speech, that earns this name. This designation is reserved for one beyond training alone, since only the arhat is a sage in the fullest, truest sense, all afflictions having been permanently stilled without remainder. The three kinds of good conduct — bodily, verbal, and mental — are, correspondingly, called the three purities, since they represent a temporary or permanent freedom from every stain of afflicted misconduct. Both of these designations — sage and purity — exist specifically to correct beings who might otherwise mistake some other, false teacher or false discipline for genuine sagehood or purity.

Verse: some say karma conducive to hell-rebirth and other desire-realm karma are, respectively, purely black and mixed; others say karma abandoned through seeing and the rest of desire-realm karma are, respectively, purely black and mixed. Prose: other teachers say: karma conducive to a hell-rebirth, and desire-realm karma conducive to any other rebirth, are, in this order, called purely black karma and mixed karma. That is, since hell's ripened result is brought about only by unwholesome karma, karma conducive to that rebirth is called purely black karma; excluding hell alone, every other ripened result within the desire realm is brought about by both wholesome and unwholesome karma, so karma conducive to those rebirths is called black-and-white karma. Other teachers say: karma abandoned through seeing, and all remaining desire-realm karma, are, in this order, called purely black karma and mixed karma. That is, karma abandoned through seeing has no admixture of anything wholesome, and so is called purely black karma; karma abandoned through cultivation within the desire realm has both wholesome and unwholesome instances, and so is called mixed karma. Moreover, a sūtra speaks of three sages, and a sūtra speaks of three purities, both comprising body, speech, and mind. What is the character of each? Verse: the bodily and verbal karma of one beyond training are the bodily and verbal sages; the mind is the mental sage; the three purities should be understood as the three kinds of good conduct. Prose: the manifested bodily and verbal karma of one beyond training are called the bodily sage and the verbal sage. The mental sage, however, is the mind of one beyond training, not manifested mental karma. Why is this so? Because, in the ultimate sense, a sage's essential nature is mind alone, which is known indirectly through bodily and verbal karma. Moreover, bodily and verbal karma possess the essential nature of renunciation, whereas mental karma does not, since there is no unmanifested [mental karma]. It is by this meaning of renunciation that the designation "sage" is established — hence it is the mind itself that, through bodily and verbal karma, is capable of renunciation, and is thereby called "sage." Why is the designation "sage" reserved for one beyond training alone? Because the arhat is a true sage, all afflictions having been permanently stilled. The three kinds of good conduct — bodily, verbal, and mental — are called the three kinds of purity of body, speech, and mind, since they represent temporary or permanent freedom from the stain of every bad conduct and affliction. These two designations are spoken of in order to still sentient beings' mistaken notions of a false sage or false purity.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should note plainly that this section records three genuinely distinct, competing proposals for what "purely black" and "mixed" karma mean (this fascicle's own initial account tying them to realm — unwholesome vs. desire-realm wholesome — plus these two further alternatives tying them instead to destiny and to the seeing/cultivation distinction respectively), without the treatise itself declaring any one of the three correct. This is consistent with the pattern already well established across the previous several fascicles: this treatise is often more interested in preserving the full range of received scholastic opinion on a given scriptural term than in adjudicating between the options.

The "mental sage" discussion turns on a genuinely subtle point already familiar from this treatise's broader system: since mental karma, unlike bodily and verbal karma, never generates any unmanifested residue (there is no such thing as "unmanifested mental karma" in this system), mental karma alone lacks the specific quality of active "renunciation" that earns the name "sage" — and so, rather than the arhat's mental karma being called the mental sage, it is instead the arhat's very mind itself, the underlying reality that bodily and verbal karma merely give indirect evidence of, that receives this designation. This lets the treatise preserve its threefold parallel structure (bodily/verbal/mental sage) while still respecting the deeper asymmetry between mental karma and the other two kinds that this whole treatise has maintained since fascicle 13.

4. Are Greed, Hatred, and Wrong View Themselves Mental Karma?

The treatise turns to the sūtras' threefold division of bad conduct and good conduct, each spanning body, speech, and mind. All unwholesome bodily, verbal, and mental karma are named, in turn, bodily, verbal, and mental bad conduct — but mental bad conduct itself further divides into three: greed, hatred, and wrong view, none of which is identical to volition itself, each instead possessing its own distinct essential nature apart from volition. The Dārṣṭāntika masters disagree, holding that greed, hatred, and wrong view simply are mental karma directly, appealing to the Thought Sūtra's own statement that these three constitute mental karma. This provokes an immediate objection: if greed, hatred, and wrong view just are karma, then karma and affliction would collapse into one and the same thing — and if this is accepted, what actual problem would that create? The Vaibhāṣika insist this is untenable, since accepting it would contradict a great deal of established reasoning and scripture, leading to serious difficulties. As for the Thought Sūtra's own statement that these three constitute mental karma, the Vaibhāṣika explain this differently: it is meant to show that volition operates through greed, hatred, and wrong view as its "gateway," that volition is thereby able to bring about an undesirable result, and that this conduct is condemned and rejected by the wise — and it is precisely because this conduct is itself bad that it earns the name "bad conduct," not because greed, hatred, and wrong view are literally identical to the karma itself. The three kinds of good conduct should be understood as the exact reverse of this: all wholesome bodily, verbal, and mental karma, with mental good conduct itself comprising non-greed, non-hatred, and right view. Since right view and wrong view do not, in themselves, constitute volition's aim to benefit or harm another, one might ask how they could count as wholesome or unwholesome at all — but they qualify precisely because they serve as the very root from which the intention to benefit or to harm springs.

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, a sūtra speaks of three kinds of bad conduct, and a sūtra speaks of three kinds of good conduct, both comprising body, speech, and mind. What is the character of each? Verse: unwholesome bodily, verbal, and mental karma are called the three kinds of bad conduct; together with greed, hatred, and wrong view; the three kinds of good conduct are the reverse of this. Prose: all unwholesome bodily, verbal, and mental karma are, in this order, called bodily, verbal, and mental bad conduct. Mental bad conduct, however, is further of three kinds: greed, hatred, and wrong view, which are not themselves volition, since greed and the others possess their own distinct essential nature apart from volition. The Dārṣṭāntika masters say: greed, hatred, and wrong view simply are mental karma, since the Thought Sūtra states that these three constitute mental karma. [Objection:] If this is so, then karma and affliction would merge into a single essential nature. [Response, from the Dārṣṭāntika:] If one accepts that affliction simply is mental karma, what fault would this incur? The Vaibhāṣika say this is not sound reasoning — if one accepts this, it would contradict a great deal of reasoning and scripture, incurring serious faults. As for the sūtra's statement that these are mental karma, this is meant to show that volition operates through these as its gateway, that volition is thereby able to bring about an undesirable result, and that this conduct is condemned and rejected by the wise. Since this conduct is itself bad, it is named bad conduct. The three kinds of good conduct should be understood as the reverse of this — namely, all wholesome bodily, verbal, and mental karma. [Objection:] Non-greed, non-hatred, and right view are not themselves volition — how, then, could they be considered wholesome or unwholesome? [Reply:] Because they serve as the root for the intention to benefit or to harm another.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This debate has real philosophical stakes worth drawing out clearly: the Vaibhāṣika maintain a sharp categorical distinction between karma (volition and what it produces) and affliction (the mental factors, like greed and hatred, that motivate and accompany unwholesome volition) — collapsing the two, as the Dārṣṭāntika propose, would blur a distinction this entire treatise has depended on since fascicle 13's opening definition of karma as "volition, and what volition produces." The Vaibhāṣika's resolution treats the sūtra's "these three are mental karma" as a looser, functional statement (these afflictions are how volition operates, and are judged by the same standard used to judge karma) rather than a strict identity claim.

The final objection-and-reply here quietly resolves an important asymmetry in how this classification works: bodily and verbal good/bad conduct are named directly after actual karmic acts (killing, stealing, and so on), but mental good/bad conduct is named instead after mental factors (greed, hatred, wrong view, and their opposites) that are not themselves acts of volition at all — the reply's answer, that these factors count as wholesome or unwholesome because they are the root from which harmful or beneficial volition springs, is the same "causal root" logic already used elsewhere in this treatise (compare the fourfold wholesome/unwholesome framework from fascicle 13, where the wholesome roots were likewise treated as wholesome by intrinsic nature, prior to and independent of any associated volition).

5. The Ten Karma-Paths, and Which Require Both Manifested and Unmanifested Karma

The treatise now turns to a sūtra teaching of ten karma-paths, each either wholesome or unwholesome. These ten are drawn from the fuller bad-conduct and good-conduct classifications just discussed, but only from whatever is coarse, prominent, and easily recognized within them — unwholesome karma-paths correspond to bad conduct, wholesome karma-paths to good conduct, but neither set of ten actually exhausts its corresponding fuller category. On the unwholesome side, the bodily karma-path picks out only the completed act itself, excluding the preparatory buildup toward it; the verbal karma-paths exclude both the preparatory and follow-up stages, along with any comparatively minor instances; and the mental karma-paths exclude any lesser forms of greed and the like that fall short of full-blown affliction. On the wholesome side, the bodily karma-path excludes preparatory and follow-up action as well as other wholesome bodily acts, such as abstaining from alcohol or making an offering; the verbal karma-path excludes such things as kind, encouraging speech that falls short of the specific listed categories; and the mental karma-path excludes ordinary wholesome intentions that do not rise to the specific listed roots. This raises a further, more technical question: do the first seven of these ten karma-paths (the bodily and verbal ones) all necessarily involve both manifested and unmanifested karma together? The answer is more nuanced than a simple yes: six of the seven unwholesome bodily and verbal karma-paths definitely involve unmanifested karma, but whether they also involve manifested karma depends on whether the offender acts personally or commissions someone else; sexual misconduct alone, among the unwholesome seven, always necessarily involves both, since it can only ever be completed by the offender's own body, never delegated to an agent. All seven wholesome karma-paths, when they arise from formally receiving a precept, likewise necessarily involve both — since precept-based śīla always depends on an actual manifested act of reception — while the specific case of meditation-born or uncontaminated restraint involves only unmanifested karma, arising as it does purely through the power of mind, without needing any manifested act at all.

The seven wholesome karma-paths, if arising from formal reception, necessarily comprise both manifested and unmanifested karma, since śīla arising from formal reception necessarily depends upon a manifested act. Restraint comprised within meditative or uncontaminated attainment is called "arising through meditative attainment," and this has only unmanifested karma, since it arises solely through the power of mind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The principle that "the ten karma-paths pick out only the coarse portion" of the fuller bad-conduct/good-conduct classification is worth stating plainly, since it explains why this treatise needs both a broader threefold scheme (bodily/verbal/mental bad and good conduct) and this narrower tenfold scheme side by side: the ten karma-paths function as a practical, easily-taught summary of the clearest, most recognizable cases of wrongdoing and virtue, while the broader threefold scheme captures the full range including subtler preparatory stages, minor instances, and follow-up actions that the tenfold list deliberately sets aside for the sake of clarity and teachability.

The sexual-misconduct exception is a nice illustration of a point this treatise has made before in a different context (recall fascicle 13's discussion of completing a karma-path by commissioning another): most bodily and verbal offenses can, in principle, be carried out through an intermediary, with the instigator's own karma completing only once the deed is actually done elsewhere — but sexual misconduct is structurally different, since it is, by its very nature, an act that only the offender's own body can perform, making delegation simply impossible, which is why it alone among the unwholesome seven is guaranteed to always involve both manifested and unmanifested karma together.

6. Preparatory Action, Root Act, and Follow-Up: The Sheep-Butcher Example

The treatise now specifies, with precision, when manifested and unmanifested karma occur across a karma-path's three stages. Preparatory action always necessarily involves manifested karma, but whether it also involves unmanifested karma depends on the intensity of the mind behind it: if a fierce affliction, or an unusually sincere and pure intention, is present, unmanifested karma arises alongside it; otherwise, it does not. Follow-up action is the reverse: it necessarily involves unmanifested karma, but its manifested karma is variable — if a later act continues in keeping with the original karma, manifested karma is present; otherwise, it is not. To see exactly how these three stages are established in practice, the treatise offers the paradigm case of killing, illustrated through a sheep-butcher's entire process: from the moment the intention to kill first arises and the butcher rises from bed, going to negotiate a price at the sheep-market, touching and paying for the animal, leading it home to be fed, bringing it into the slaughterhouse, and finally striking or stabbing it with knife or staff, once or repeatedly, up until the moment life has not yet actually ended — every one of these actions is called the preparatory action of killing. The precise instant the victim's life actually ends, coinciding with the manifested act occurring at that very moment, together with its accompanying unmanifested karma, is what constitutes the root karma-path of killing itself — brought about jointly by two conditions: the preparatory action, and the completion of the intended result. From the instant immediately following this root act onward, the unmanifested karma of killing continues on without interruption, and this ongoing continuation is called the follow-up action of killing; later actions, too — skinning, cutting, cleaning, weighing, selling, cooking, eating, or praising the meat's flavor — are, at each such moment their accompanying manifested karma occurs, likewise called follow-up action. The remaining six bodily and verbal karma-paths should each be understood to divide into these same three stages by analogy, with appropriate adjustments. Greed, hatred, and wrong view are different: the very moment they actually manifest is itself already called the root karma-path directly, with no separate preparatory or follow-up stage to speak of.

If a fierce afflictive fixation, or a sincere and pure mind, arises, then unmanifested karma is present; otherwise, it is not. Follow-up action is the reverse of this: it necessarily has unmanifested karma, while at this stage manifested karma may or may not be present — that is, if a later act arises in accordance with the earlier karma, manifested karma is present; otherwise, it is not. In this connection, how are the stages of preparatory action, root act, and follow-up action to be established? Precisely at the moment that manifested act occurs together with the victim's actual death, this instant's manifested and unmanifested karma is called the root karma-path of killing. Reading note — Ru-Yi

This passage's vivid, step-by-step narration of the sheep-butcher's entire process — from rising out of bed through to the final meal and its praise — is one of the more concretely detailed illustrations in this whole treatise, and it is doing careful conceptual work: it shows that "preparatory action" can span an enormous stretch of ordinary, seemingly unremarkable activity (haggling over a price, walking home) that only retroactively counts as part of a killing's preparatory buildup once the root act is actually completed, while "follow-up action" extends symmetrically forward in time to include activities (butchering, cooking, even praising the meat) that occur well after the moment of death itself, so long as they still generate manifested karma continuous with the original act.

The special treatment of greed, hatred, and wrong view — lacking any preparatory or follow-up stage, with only a bare "root act" moment — follows directly from the previous fascicle's and this fascicle's shared premise that these three are mental states rather than physical or verbal acts extended through time: since they have no manifested karma at all (mental karma generates no unmanifested residue in this system, as established since fascicle 13), there is no ongoing physical or verbal continuum for a "preparatory" or "follow-up" phase to attach to — the affliction simply arises, in full, at the single moment it manifests, with nothing before or after it to count as part of the same karma-path.

7. Exactly When Is the Killer's Karma-Path Completed?

A genuinely difficult puzzle now confronts the treatise's own account: precisely when, relative to the victim's death, does the killer's manifested and unmanifested karma actually constitute the completed root karma-path — exactly at the moment the victim enters the state of death, or only afterward? Either answer creates a serious problem. If the killer's karma-path is completed exactly at the moment the victim enters the state of death, then killer and victim would have to be dying at one and the same instant, which the tradition simply does not accept as how karma-paths are completed. But if the killer's karma-path is instead completed only after the victim's death has already occurred, this directly contradicts the very account just given — that the root karma-path consists precisely of the manifested and unmanifested karma occurring at the instant of death itself — and would also conflict with the Vaibhāṣika's own established reading of a passage from the root treatise, which states that there can be a case where a being has already been killed while the killing itself has not yet ceased, explained as referring to a killer whose preparatory action has not yet ended even after the victim's death — with the Vaibhāṣika specifically insisting that "preparatory action" in this passage actually refers to the root act, not literally to preparatory action, since it must be conceded that even after death occurs, the root act has not yet finished. The resolution the treatise offers is this: at the very moment of death, the manifested act occurring then, though by itself insufficient to constitute the completed root karma-path (since it would serve no further purpose on its own), together with the follow-up unmanifested karma actually reaching its full completion only slightly afterward, jointly and only together constitute the root karma-path — meaning the root act is not pinned to any single sharp instant at all, but is properly understood as the combined completion of the preparatory action and the fully-ripened result taken together.

Ru-Yi Translation

This point should now be addressed. At the moment the victim actually enters the state of death, does the killer's manifested and unmanifested karma at that very instant thereby constitute the completed karma-path — or is it completed only after death? If so, either way, what fault would follow? Both possibilities incur a fault. If the killer's karma-path is completed exactly at the moment the victim enters the state of death, then the killer and the victim would be dying at the very same moment, and this should then constitute the completed karma-path — yet our own tenet does not accept that the karma-path is completed in this way. If, instead, the killer's karma-path is completed only after the victim's death has occurred, then it should not have been stated earlier that, at the very instant the victim actually dies, that instant's manifested and unmanifested karma is what constitutes the root karma-path of killing — and this would also conflict with the Vaibhāṣika's own interpretation of the root treatise's statement about preparatory action not yet having ceased. For the root treatise states: "Is there a case where a being has already been killed, yet the killing has not yet ceased?" Answer: "There is — as when, though life has already been cut off, the killer's preparatory action has not yet ceased." The Vaibhāṣika explain this passage as follows: here, what pertains to follow-up action is being spoken of using the word "preparatory action" — one should understand that the word "preparatory action" here is actually being used to refer to the root act, since it must be granted that, even after death has occurred, the root act has not yet ceased. If understood this way, there is no fault, and this is what should be stated here. [Question:] What, in this context, makes this reading free of fault? [Reply:] It is that the word "preparatory action" here refers to the root act. [Objection:] If this is so, how, then, could the manifested karma present at that time constitute the root karma-path? [Reply:] Why should it not? [Objection:] Because it would serve no purpose. [Reply, continuing:] What purpose, then, does the unmanifested karma serve here? Reading note — Ru-Yi

We want to be transparent that this passage's closing exchange is genuinely difficult to parse with full confidence — the rapid volley of short questions and replies ("why should it not?" / "because it would serve no purpose" / "what purpose does the unmanifested karma serve, then?") compresses what is likely a longer dialectical exchange in the tradition's oral commentary into a bare minimum of text, and we have rendered it as literally as possible while marking the turn-structure with bracketed labels, rather than smoothing it into a more confident-sounding resolution than the Chinese itself provides.

The underlying puzzle here is a genuine philosophical one worth stating plainly: this treatise's entire momentariness framework (established at length in fascicle 13) insists that causation happens strictly moment by moment, with no blurring across instants — yet the completion of a karma-path like killing seems to require correlating two separate events (the killer's own manifested act, and the victim's death) that do not obviously occur at exactly the same instant. The proposed resolution — that the root karma-path is constituted by the *joint* completion of preparatory action and result, rather than pinned to one sharp, isolated moment — is a genuinely interesting refinement of the treatise's momentariness commitments, treating "completion" as a relational fact obtaining between two events rather than a property any single instant possesses on its own.
