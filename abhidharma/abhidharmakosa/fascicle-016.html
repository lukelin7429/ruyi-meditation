Black Karma, White Karma, and Neither

Fascicle 16 continues Chapter 4 (分別業品第四之四, the Karma-nirdeśa) directly from fascicle 15's long survey of the sūtras' classifications of karma. It opens with the sūtras' fourfold division into black, white, mixed, and neither-black-nor-white karma, then moves through the three sages, the three purities, a genuine dispute over whether greed, hatred, and wrong view are themselves a form of mental karma, and the opening of the treatise's extended treatment of the ten karma-paths.

1. Black Karma, White Karma, Mixed, and Neither

Continuing directly from fascicle 15's long survey of the sūtras' classification schemes for karma, the treatise takes up one final, especially rich fourfold division: karma that is black with a black ripened result, karma that is white with a white ripened result, karma that is black-and-white with a black-and-white ripened result, and karma that is neither black nor white, having no ripened result at all, and capable of exhausting every other kind of karma. The Buddha teaches this division based on differences in the moral quality and character of karma's own result, and on the differing relationship between what is to be overcome and what overcomes it. All unwholesome karma is called black without exception, since it is defiled in nature and its ripened result is likewise black, being undesirable. Wholesome karma belonging to the form realm is called white without exception, since it is unmixed with anything unwholesome, and its ripened result is likewise white, being desirable. Desire-realm wholesome karma, meanwhile, is called black-and-white, since it is mixed together with unwholesome karma there, and its ripened result is likewise mixed — this label describes an entire continuum's mixed pattern of activity, not any single act or its single result, since no individual karmic act, and no individual ripened result, can itself be both black and white at once, these being mutually exclusive qualities. Finally, uncontaminated karma, capable of permanently exhausting all three of the preceding kinds, is called neither black — since it is undefiled — nor white, in the sense that it does not bring about a white ripened result, though this second "neither" is meant in a specifically qualified sense, since the Buddha elsewhere describes the qualities of one beyond training as themselves "purely wholesome, purely white, entirely without fault."

Moreover, a sūtra states that karma is of four kinds: there is karma that is black with a black ripened result; there is karma that is white with a white ripened result; there is karma that is black-and-white with a black-and-white ripened result; and there is karma that is neither black nor white, without any ripened result, capable of exhausting all other karma. What is the character of these? Verse: with reference to these distinctions of black and the rest, four kinds of karma are spoken of — unwholesome karma, form-realm wholesome karma, desire-realm wholesome karma, and the uncontaminated capable of exhausting these — should, in this order, be understood as named black, white, mixed, and neither. Prose: the Buddha, with reference to differences in the nature and category of karma's result, and to the distinct relationship between what is to be overcome and its antidote, speaks of black and the other three. All unwholesome karma is uniformly called black, since it is defiled in nature, and its ripened result is likewise black, being undesirable. Wholesome karma of the form realm is uniformly called white, since it is unmixed with anything unwholesome, and its ripened result is likewise white, being desirable. [Objection:] Why is formless-realm wholesome karma not mentioned? [Reply:] It is said that this classification applies only where two kinds of ripened result exist — namely, in the intermediate state and at birth — and where all three kinds of karma are complete — namely, bodily, verbal, and mental; hence it is not spoken of with respect to any other case. Yet a sūtra elsewhere also states that desire-realm wholesome karma is called black-and-white, since it is mixed together with unwholesome karma there, and its ripened result is likewise black-and-white, being mixed with an undesirable result. This name "black-and-white" is established with reference to a continuum, not to any single act's own essential nature — why is this so? Because there is no single act of karma, and no single ripened result, that is itself both black and white, these being mutually opposed. [Objection:] Is it not the case that unwholesome karma's result is likewise mixed with wholesome karma's result — should this not, then, equally be called "white-and-black"? [Reply:] Unwholesome karma's result is not necessarily mixed with wholesome karma's result, whereas desire-realm wholesome karma's result is necessarily mixed with unwholesome karma's result, since, within the desire realm, unwholesome karma predominates over wholesome karma. Uncontaminated karma, capable of permanently exhausting the preceding three kinds of karma, is called "not black," since it is undefiled, and also called "not white," in that it is incapable of bringing about a white ripened result. This phrase "not white" is stated with hidden intent, since the Buddha, in the Great Emptiness Sūtra, tells Ānanda: "The dharmas of one beyond training are purely wholesome, purely white, entirely without fault." The root treatise likewise states: "What is a white dharma? It is wholesome dharmas that are not defiled-neutral and have no ripened result" — since these do not fall within any realm, and are opposed in nature to the dharmas of continued cyclic existence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "not white" qualification for uncontaminated karma is a genuinely delicate piece of scriptural reconciliation, and we want to flag exactly what tension it is resolving: on the one hand, this section's own definition calls uncontaminated karma "not white" because it produces no ripened result at all (white specifically meaning "productive of a desirable ripened result" in this classification); on the other hand, a separate, well-known sūtra passage has the Buddha himself describing an arhat's own qualities as "purely white." The treatise's resolution is that these two uses of "white" are simply operating in different technical registers — the fourfold black/white/mixed/neither scheme uses "white" in the narrow, ripening-specific sense just defined, while the Great Emptiness Sūtra passage uses "white" in a broader, more general sense of moral purity — and the root treatise's own citation is marshaled to confirm that "white dharma," properly defined, excludes exactly the kind of result-producing karma this section's fourfold scheme is concerned with.

The point about "black-and-white" describing "a continuum, not... any single act's own essential nature" is worth holding onto as a recurring methodological principle in this treatise: several classification schemes across this and the previous fascicle (recall, for instance, fascicle 13's "mixed karma" bringing about both a pleasant and unpleasant result at once) describe a *pattern across many acts and their results over time*, not some genuinely hybrid quality inhering in any single karmic act — a single act is always cleanly one thing or another, even when a person's overall karmic life story looks decidedly mixed.

2. Which Path-Moments Exhaust Which Kinds of Karma

The treatise now asks whether uncontaminated karma exhausts all three preceding kinds of karma in the very same way, and finds that it does not — different specific moments of uncontaminated attainment are responsible for exhausting different kinds. The four patience-attainments belonging to the path of seeing (direct insight into suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path itself), together with the first eight uninterrupted paths belonging to the path of cultivation as one progressively eliminates the desire realm's own defilements, comprise twelve total moments of uncontaminated volition, and these exhaust only purely black karma — that is, unwholesome karma. The ninth and final uninterrupted path in this same process of eliminating desire-realm defilement, however, involves a single further moment of uncontaminated volition that exhausts both mixed (black-and-white) karma and the last portion of purely black karma at once, since this is the very moment that finally and completely severs the desire realm's wholesome karma from any further unwholesome admixture, while simultaneously finishing off the ninth and final grade of unwholesome karma itself. Beyond this, the ninth uninterrupted path belonging to each of the four form-realm meditative absorptions, considered one level at a time, exhausts purely white karma — that is, form-realm wholesome karma — specifically at that level. This raises a natural question: why is it always specifically the very last of these paths, and none of the others along the way, that actually exhausts a given level's contaminated wholesome dharmas? The answer is that wholesome dharmas are not overcome by their own intrinsic nature the way afflictions are, and so, even after most of a level's affliction has already been severed, the corresponding wholesome dharmas belonging to that same level can still, in principle, actively manifest again and again — it is specifically the complete exhaustion of the afflictions that take those wholesome dharmas as their object that finally allows those wholesome dharmas to be said to be "severed," since it is only then that they become fully freed from the afflictive bondage that previously kept them tied to that level; so long as even one final grade of such affliction remains outstanding, the wholesome dharmas in question cannot yet be said to be severed, since they have not yet actually been freed from that bondage.

Ru-Yi Translation

Does uncontaminated karma exhaust all three of the preceding kinds of karma equally? It does not. How is this so? Verse: the four patience-attainments, and the first eight uninterrupted paths of eliminating desire — twelve uncontaminated volitions — exhaust only purely black karma; the ninth uninterrupted volition of eliminating desire exhausts both mixed and the remainder of purely black karma at once; and the [ninth uninterrupted volition] of eliminating each of the four meditative absorptions, four in number, causes purely white karma to be exhausted. Prose: within the path of seeing, the four patience-attainments of dharma-knowledge, together with, within the path of cultivation, the first eight uninterrupted noble paths accompanying the elimination of desire-realm defilement, comprise twelve volitions that exhaust only purely black karma. The ninth uninterrupted noble path accompanying the elimination of desire-realm defilement involves one further uncontaminated volition that exhausts both mixed karma and purely black karma at once, since this is the moment that entirely severs desire-realm wholesome karma [from further unwholesome admixture], and also exhausts the ninth grade of unwholesome karma. The ninth uninterrupted path accompanying the elimination of defilement belonging, one at a time, to each of the four meditative absorptions causes purely white karma to be exhausted. For what reason can contaminated wholesome dharmas belonging to any given level be severed only by the very last path there, and not by any other? Because wholesome dharmas are not severed by their own intrinsic nature, since, even once mostly severed, they still admit of manifesting again in the present. Rather, it is only once the affliction that takes them as its object has been exhausted that these wholesome dharmas are said to be severed, since it is at that point that the wholesome dharmas become freed from bondage. Hence, so long as even one further grade of the affliction taking them as its object still remains, the sense of "severing" is not yet established, since the wholesome dharmas have not yet actually become freed from bondage at that point.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction drawn here between how afflictions are severed and how wholesome dharmas are "severed" is conceptually important and easy to blur: an affliction is eliminated by its own antidote acting directly on its own intrinsic nature, moment by moment, until nothing remains — but a wholesome dharma is never itself the target of any antidote (nothing needs to "cure" a wholesome state), so its disappearance from a given level cannot work the same way. Instead, wholesome dharmas become "severed" only in the indirect, relational sense of becoming permanently freed from the affliction that had been binding them to that level — meaning the wholesome dharma's own continuity is only broken as a side effect of the affliction's own complete elimination, which is why it takes until the very last moment of that elimination process (the ninth uninterrupted path) for this severing to actually occur, no matter how much progress has already been made against the affliction beforehand.

This section's careful path-by-path bookkeeping (twelve volitions for purely black karma, one further volition for mixed-and-remaining-black, four more for purely white, one per meditative absorption) is characteristic of this treatise's precision when mapping doctrine onto the technical structure of the path of seeing and path of cultivation — structures already established in earlier fascicles' discussion of the nine grades of affliction-elimination and the uninterrupted-versus-liberation-path distinction (see, for instance, fascicle 13's discussion of the "nine uninterrupted paths" underlying severing restraint).

3. Alternative Views on Black and Mixed Karma, and the Three Sages

The treatise records two further, alternative proposals for how to understand "purely black" and "mixed" karma, distinct from the account already given. Some teachers hold that karma conducive to a hell-rebirth, and desire-realm karma conducive to any other kind of rebirth, should respectively be called purely black and mixed karma — since hell's own ripened result is brought about only by unwholesome karma, karma conducive to that rebirth is called purely black, while every other kind of ripened result within the desire realm is brought about by both wholesome and unwholesome karma alike, so karma conducive to those other rebirths is called black-and-white. Other teachers hold instead that karma abandoned through seeing the truths, and all remaining desire-realm karma, should respectively be called purely black and mixed karma — since karma abandoned through seeing involves no admixture of anything wholesome at all, it is called purely black, while karma abandoned through cultivation within the desire realm includes both wholesome and unwholesome instances, and so is called mixed. The treatise then turns to a related sūtra classification: the three sages, and the three purities, both said to comprise body, speech, and mind. The manifested bodily and verbal karma of one beyond training (an arhat) is called the bodily sage and the verbal sage; the "mental sage," however, refers not to any manifested mental karma at all, but simply to the arhat's own mind beyond training as such — since, in the ultimate sense, a sage's essential nature is mind alone, something inferred indirectly through the evidence of bodily and verbal karma, and since bodily and verbal karma alone possess the character of active renunciation (there being no unmanifested mental karma to speak of), it is specifically through this quality of renunciation that the name "sage" is established, meaning it is really the mind itself, expressed through body and speech, that earns this name. This designation is reserved for one beyond training alone, since only the arhat is a sage in the fullest, truest sense, all afflictions having been permanently stilled without remainder. The three kinds of good conduct — bodily, verbal, and mental — are, correspondingly, called the three purities, since they represent a temporary or permanent freedom from every stain of afflicted misconduct. Both of these designations — sage and purity — exist specifically to correct beings who might otherwise mistake some other, false teacher or false discipline for genuine sagehood or purity.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should note plainly that this section records three genuinely distinct, competing proposals for what "purely black" and "mixed" karma mean (this fascicle's own initial account tying them to realm — unwholesome vs. desire-realm wholesome — plus these two further alternatives tying them instead to destiny and to the seeing/cultivation distinction respectively), without the treatise itself declaring any one of the three correct. This is consistent with the pattern already well established across the previous several fascicles: this treatise is often more interested in preserving the full range of received scholastic opinion on a given scriptural term than in adjudicating between the options.

The "mental sage" discussion turns on a genuinely subtle point already familiar from this treatise's broader system: since mental karma, unlike bodily and verbal karma, never generates any unmanifested residue (there is no such thing as "unmanifested mental karma" in this system), mental karma alone lacks the specific quality of active "renunciation" that earns the name "sage" — and so, rather than the arhat's mental karma being called the mental sage, it is instead the arhat's very mind itself, the underlying reality that bodily and verbal karma merely give indirect evidence of, that receives this designation. This lets the treatise preserve its threefold parallel structure (bodily/verbal/mental sage) while still respecting the deeper asymmetry between mental karma and the other two kinds that this whole treatise has maintained since fascicle 13.

4. Are Greed, Hatred, and Wrong View Themselves Mental Karma?

The treatise turns to the sūtras' threefold division of bad conduct and good conduct, each spanning body, speech, and mind. All unwholesome bodily, verbal, and mental karma are named, in turn, bodily, verbal, and mental bad conduct — but mental bad conduct itself further divides into three: greed, hatred, and wrong view, none of which is identical to volition itself, each instead possessing its own distinct essential nature apart from volition. The Dārṣṭāntika masters disagree, holding that greed, hatred, and wrong view simply are mental karma directly, appealing to the Thought Sūtra's own statement that these three constitute mental karma. This provokes an immediate objection: if greed, hatred, and wrong view just are karma, then karma and affliction would collapse into one and the same thing — and if this is accepted, what actual problem would that create? The Vaibhāṣika insist this is untenable, since accepting it would contradict a great deal of established reasoning and scripture, leading to serious difficulties. As for the Thought Sūtra's own statement that these three constitute mental karma, the Vaibhāṣika explain this differently: it is meant to show that volition operates through greed, hatred, and wrong view as its "gateway," that volition is thereby able to bring about an undesirable result, and that this conduct is condemned and rejected by the wise — and it is precisely because this conduct is itself bad that it earns the name "bad conduct," not because greed, hatred, and wrong view are literally identical to the karma itself. The three kinds of good conduct should be understood as the exact reverse of this: all wholesome bodily, verbal, and mental karma, with mental good conduct itself comprising non-greed, non-hatred, and right view. Since right view and wrong view do not, in themselves, constitute volition's aim to benefit or harm another, one might ask how they could count as wholesome or unwholesome at all — but they qualify precisely because they serve as the very root from which the intention to benefit or to harm springs.

[Reply:] Because they serve as the root for the intention to benefit or to harm another.

This debate has real philosophical stakes worth drawing out clearly: the Vaibhāṣika maintain a sharp categorical distinction between karma (volition and what it produces) and affliction (the mental factors, like greed and hatred, that motivate and accompany unwholesome volition) — collapsing the two, as the Dārṣṭāntika propose, would blur a distinction this entire treatise has depended on since fascicle 13's opening definition of karma as "volition, and what volition produces." The Vaibhāṣika's resolution treats the sūtra's "these three are mental karma" as a looser, functional statement (these afflictions are how volition operates, and are judged by the same standard used to judge karma) rather than a strict identity claim.

The final objection-and-reply here quietly resolves an important asymmetry in how this classification works: bodily and verbal good/bad conduct are named directly after actual karmic acts (killing, stealing, and so on), but mental good/bad conduct is named instead after mental factors (greed, hatred, wrong view, and their opposites) that are not themselves acts of volition at all — the reply's answer, that these factors count as wholesome or unwholesome because they are the root from which harmful or beneficial volition springs, is the same "causal root" logic already used elsewhere in this treatise (compare the fourfold wholesome/unwholesome framework from fascicle 13, where the wholesome roots were likewise treated as wholesome by intrinsic nature, prior to and independent of any associated volition).

5. The Ten Karma-Paths, and Which Require Both Manifested and Unmanifested Karma

The treatise now turns to a sūtra teaching of ten karma-paths, each either wholesome or unwholesome. These ten are drawn from the fuller bad-conduct and good-conduct classifications just discussed, but only from whatever is coarse, prominent, and easily recognized within them — unwholesome karma-paths correspond to bad conduct, wholesome karma-paths to good conduct, but neither set of ten actually exhausts its corresponding fuller category. On the unwholesome side, the bodily karma-path picks out only the completed act itself, excluding the preparatory buildup toward it; the verbal karma-paths exclude both the preparatory and follow-up stages, along with any comparatively minor instances; and the mental karma-paths exclude any lesser forms of greed and the like that fall short of full-blown affliction. On the wholesome side, the bodily karma-path excludes preparatory and follow-up action as well as other wholesome bodily acts, such as abstaining from alcohol or making an offering; the verbal karma-path excludes such things as kind, encouraging speech that falls short of the specific listed categories; and the mental karma-path excludes ordinary wholesome intentions that do not rise to the specific listed roots. This raises a further, more technical question: do the first seven of these ten karma-paths (the bodily and verbal ones) all necessarily involve both manifested and unmanifested karma together? The answer is more nuanced than a simple yes: six of the seven unwholesome bodily and verbal karma-paths definitely involve unmanifested karma, but whether they also involve manifested karma depends on whether the offender acts personally or commissions someone else; sexual misconduct alone, among the unwholesome seven, always necessarily involves both, since it can only ever be completed by the offender's own body, never delegated to an agent. All seven wholesome karma-paths, when they arise from formally receiving a precept, likewise necessarily involve both — since precept-based śīla always depends on an actual manifested act of reception — while the specific case of meditation-born or uncontaminated restraint involves only unmanifested karma, arising as it does purely through the power of mind, without needing any manifested act at all.

6. Preparatory Action, Root Act, and Follow-Up: The Sheep-Butcher Example

The treatise now specifies, with precision, when manifested and unmanifested karma occur across a karma-path's three stages. Preparatory action always necessarily involves manifested karma, but whether it also involves unmanifested karma depends on the intensity of the mind behind it: if a fierce affliction, or an unusually sincere and pure intention, is present, unmanifested karma arises alongside it; otherwise, it does not. Follow-up action is the reverse: it necessarily involves unmanifested karma, but its manifested karma is variable — if a later act continues in keeping with the original karma, manifested karma is present; otherwise, it is not. To see exactly how these three stages are established in practice, the treatise offers the paradigm case of killing, illustrated through a sheep-butcher's entire process: from the moment the intention to kill first arises and the butcher rises from bed, going to negotiate a price at the sheep-market, touching and paying for the animal, leading it home to be fed, bringing it into the slaughterhouse, and finally striking or stabbing it with knife or staff, once or repeatedly, up until the moment life has not yet actually ended — every one of these actions is called the preparatory action of killing. The precise instant the victim's life actually ends, coinciding with the manifested act occurring at that very moment, together with its accompanying unmanifested karma, is what constitutes the root karma-path of killing itself — brought about jointly by two conditions: the preparatory action, and the completion of the intended result. From the instant immediately following this root act onward, the unmanifested karma of killing continues on without interruption, and this ongoing continuation is called the follow-up action of killing; later actions, too — skinning, cutting, cleaning, weighing, selling, cooking, eating, or praising the meat's flavor — are, at each such moment their accompanying manifested karma occurs, likewise called follow-up action. The remaining six bodily and verbal karma-paths should each be understood to divide into these same three stages by analogy, with appropriate adjustments. Greed, hatred, and wrong view are different: the very moment they actually manifest is itself already called the root karma-path directly, with no separate preparatory or follow-up stage to speak of.

7. Exactly When Is the Killer's Karma-Path Completed?

A genuinely difficult puzzle now confronts the treatise's own account: precisely when, relative to the victim's death, does the killer's manifested and unmanifested karma actually constitute the completed root karma-path — exactly at the moment the victim enters the state of death, or only afterward? Either answer creates a serious problem. If the killer's karma-path is completed exactly at the moment the victim enters the state of death, then killer and victim would have to be dying at one and the same instant, which the tradition simply does not accept as how karma-paths are completed. But if the killer's karma-path is instead completed only after the victim's death has already occurred, this directly contradicts the very account just given — that the root karma-path consists precisely of the manifested and unmanifested karma occurring at the instant of death itself — and would also conflict with the Vaibhāṣika's own established reading of a passage from the root treatise, which states that there can be a case where a being has already been killed while the killing itself has not yet ceased, explained as referring to a killer whose preparatory action has not yet ended even after the victim's death — with the Vaibhāṣika specifically insisting that "preparatory action" in this passage actually refers to the root act, not literally to preparatory action, since it must be conceded that even after death occurs, the root act has not yet finished. The resolution the treatise offers is this: at the very moment of death, the manifested act occurring then, though by itself insufficient to constitute the completed root karma-path (since it would serve no further purpose on its own), together with the follow-up unmanifested karma actually reaching its full completion only slightly afterward, jointly and only together constitute the root karma-path — meaning the root act is not pinned to any single sharp instant at all, but is properly understood as the combined completion of the preparatory action and the fully-ripened result taken together.

8. When All Ten Karma-Paths Serve a Single Act of Killing

The treatise now observes that the ten karma-paths can serve as each other's preparatory and follow-up actions in striking combinations, illustrated through an elaborate scenario centered on a single act of killing an enemy. Someone wishing to destroy an enemy might scheme and conspire to assemble the conditions for killing (false speech and divisive speech among the karma-paths); might kill other beings and pray to spirits for their assistance (killing); might steal others' property to fund the undertaking (theft); might seduce the enemy's wife specifically in order to bring about the husband's death (sexual misconduct); might spread slanderous rumors to alienate the enemy from friends and family, sowing suspicion through harsh or divisive speech; might, having the power to help but withholding it, let the enemy come to harm; might covet the enemy's wealth outright (greed); might harbor outright hatred toward the enemy (hatred); or might cultivate the killing itself through a wrong view that somehow justifies it (wrong view) — before actually carrying out the killing. In this way, all ten karma-paths together can serve as the preparatory action for a single act of killing. And after the enemy has been killed, one might go on to kill the enemy's own family members, seize their remaining property, violate loved ones left behind, and continue generating greed, hatred, and wrong view in succession — these same ten thereby also serving as the killing's follow-up action. The remaining karma-paths should be understood by analogy to admit similar combinations, though greed, hatred, and wrong view themselves cannot properly serve as anyone else's "preparatory action," strictly speaking, since a karma-path's preparatory action requires an actual undertaking beyond the bare arising of a mental state, and these three are complete the very moment the corresponding mental state itself arises, with no further deed yet performed.

9. The Three Roots Behind Killing and Theft

A sūtra teaches that killing is of three kinds — arising from greed, from hatred, or from delusion — and states that the same threefold division applies all the way through to wrong view as well. This raises a technical question: since not every karma-path is actually completed through all three roots equally, how does the preparatory action leading up to each differ in this respect? The treatise clarifies that it is specifically the preparatory action of each unwholesome karma-path — not necessarily the root act itself — that can arise from any of the three unwholesome roots, this being said with reference to whichever root initiates the process. The preparatory action of killing arises from greed when, for instance, someone kills in order to obtain part of the victim's body, to obtain wealth, for mere sport, or to rescue a friend or oneself. It arises from hatred when someone, wishing to eliminate an enemy, kills out of a mind inflamed with rage. It arises from delusion in cases such as ritual sacrifice performed in the mistaken belief that it constitutes righteous conduct, or when rulers, acting under worldly law, execute enemies and eliminate violent criminals in the belief that this generates great merit. The treatise records, as further examples of delusion-based killing, several non-Buddhist positions it explicitly rejects: the Persian teaching that ending the life of an aged or ill parent, thereby releasing them from suffering, generates superior merit; and various other non-Buddhist teachings holding that killing venomous creatures such as snakes, scorpions, and wasps generates superior merit since they cause people harm, or that killing sheep, deer, water buffalo, and other animals originally intended for food incurs no fault at all. Killing undertaken because of a wrong view of this kind is likewise classified as arising from delusion. 12. Dying Before Your Victim, and Collective Guilt in Group Killing

The treatise now takes up a genuinely difficult case: if someone undertakes the preparatory action of killing, fully intending to kill another, but dies at the very same moment as their intended victim, or even before them, do they still incur the completed root karma-path of killing? The answer is no — such a person definitely does not incur the root karma-path. This confirms a striking possibility: there can indeed be a would-be killer who undertakes the preparatory action of killing and even brings about the intended result, and yet is never actually touched by the fault of killing at all — precisely this case, of dying simultaneously with or before the victim. The reasoning is twofold. From one angle, since the victim's life still continues at the moment the would-be killer dies, there is no way for that would-be killer to be said to have completed the act of killing them. From the other angle, a completed karma-path requires a specific physical basis to depend on, and once the killer's own body — the very basis their preparatory action depended on — has itself perished, even though a new body of a similar general kind may arise for them afterward, that new body is not the basis the original fault could attach to, since no killing-related preparatory action was ever actually undertaken in connection with it. A further, related question then arises: when a group of people — an army setting out to kill an enemy, or a party of hunters — acts together, and only one member of the group actually performs the act of killing, who among them incurs the completed karma-path of killing? The answer is that everyone in the group who shares in the collective undertaking incurs it equally, exactly as if each had personally performed the act themselves, since they have all consented together to the very same shared undertaking — much as, when people mutually instruct and encourage one another toward a single shared purpose, one person's act of killing is sufficient to implicate everyone else in the fault as well. This holds even for someone who was coerced by others into joining the group, provided that, once inside it, their mind comes to align with the group's shared purpose — the sole exception being someone who has made a prior, fixed vow never to kill under any circumstance, even to save their own life: such a person, even if physically coerced into the group's presence, incurs no fault of killing at all, since they never actually share in the group's killing-intention to begin with.

Because, since the victim's life still remains, the would-be killer cannot thereby be said to have completed the fault of killing. Nor can the would-be killer, once their own life has ended, obtain the fault of killing, since the physical basis involved is now a different one — that is, the body that the preparatory action of killing depended upon has now perished; although a body of a similar general class may arise for them afterward, this is not the basis to which the fault could attach, since no preparatory action of killing was ever actually undertaken in connection with it — hence it is not reasonable that the karma-path of killing should be completed in this case. If many people gather together as an army intending to kill an enemy, or as a hunting party, and, among them, any one person performs an act of killing, who then comes to possess the karma-path of killing? Verse: if the army and the like share in the same undertaking, all become as if they were themselves the actor. Prose: within an army or similar group, if any one member performs an act of killing, this is as if each had personally performed it — all of them equally come to possess the karma-path of killing, since they have all consented together to this same shared undertaking; just as, when people mutually instruct one another toward a single shared purpose, one person's act of killing causes all the rest to incur the fault as well. Even if someone was forced by others' power into joining this group, if their mind then comes to align with it, they likewise come to possess the fault of killing. The sole exception is someone who has established a vow to never perform killing even for the sake of saving their own life — even if forced by others' power to be present within the group, since they have no killing-intention, they incur no fault of killing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "different physical basis" resolution to the simultaneous-death puzzle is a genuinely careful piece of reasoning that connects directly back to this treatise's broader momentariness framework (established at length in fascicle 13): a karma-path, on this account, is not simply a matter of intention plus outcome in the abstract, but requires a continuous physical basis — the killer's own body — persisting from the preparatory action all the way through to the completed act; once that specific body has perished, any successor body, however similar in general type, is causally disconnected from the original preparatory action in the relevant sense, and so cannot serve as the basis for a karma-path that was never actually built up in connection with it.

The group-liability doctrine here anticipates, in a striking way, concepts of joint criminal enterprise or accomplice liability familiar from modern legal systems — the underlying principle, that shared intentional participation in a collective undertaking makes each participant fully responsible for the completed act regardless of who literally performs it, is grounded entirely in mental commitment to the shared purpose, which is precisely why the exception for the person who never actually shares that intention (having made a prior, unconditional vow against killing) is treated as decisive: physical presence within the group is irrelevant if the person's mind was never actually a party to its killing-intention in the first place.

13. What Precisely Constitutes Killing, and What Death Itself Actually Is

Having established the general framework, the treatise now defines with precision exactly what constitutes each completed karma-path, beginning with killing. Killing requires three elements together: a premeditated intention to kill, formed in advance; correctly recognizing the target as the sentient being one intends to kill; and actually killing without accident — meaning one kills precisely the intended target and no other, not somehow killing a different being by mistake. Even a case of "doubtful killing" still counts as killing: if someone, uncertain whether their intended target is even alive, or uncertain whether it is really the being they mean to kill, nevertheless resolves, "whether it is or isn't, I will certainly kill it," and does kill the being with this recklessly indifferent attitude, this too completes the karma-path. This raises a genuinely difficult philosophical question: given that all conditioned things perish moment by moment regardless of anyone's intervention, how can "killing" — cutting off something that would otherwise have continued — make coherent sense at all? The treatise answers that life itself consists in the sustained functioning of the in-and-out breath supporting the ongoing operation of body and mind together; killing means causing this continuity to fail to arise again when it otherwise would have — just as extinguishing a lamp's flame or silencing a bell's ringing sound is called "killing" that flame or that sound, without requiring that some single, persisting thing be destroyed. Alternatively, "life" may be understood as the life-faculty itself, and killing as causing this faculty to fail to continue — meaning that, through a malicious intention, one cuts off another's life such that even a single moment of life that would otherwise have arisen fails to arise; it is this, and this alone, that is touched by the fault of killing. This life-faculty that is thereby cut off — to whom does it actually belong? Since whoever lacks it thereby dies, and it has already been identified elsewhere as the "sixth" item in a certain traditional list, what could it belong to, if not to a self? — a question the treatise sets aside for full examination in its later chapter refuting the self. As the Blessed One states in verse: "When lifespan, warmth, and consciousness — these three factors — depart the body, the abandoned body lies fallen, like a piece of wood, without thought or sensation." Hence a body still possessing these faculties is properly called a living being, and a body lacking them is properly, and simply, called dead.

14. Refuting the Jain View That Intention Doesn't Matter, and Defining Theft

The treatise now takes aim at a rival position, held by the Nirgrantha (Jain) ascetics, that even entirely unintentional, accidental harm still incurs the full fault of killing — just as touching fire burns a person regardless of whether they intended to touch it. The treatise dismantles this with a series of pointed counterexamples: if this were so, then someone who mistakenly touches another's spouse, or accidentally brushes against someone's body, should equally incur fault; a compassionate person who pulls out a fellow ascetic's hair to relieve them, or a teacher who kindly urges a student toward austerities, or a host whose offered food happens to disagree with a guest's digestion, would all have to incur the fault of causing suffering to another, which is absurd. Likewise, a mother and her unborn child cause each other considerable physical distress throughout pregnancy, yet no one holds that mother and fetus commit any fault against one another on this basis. Moreover, if a victim's own contact with an inanimate cause of death (as when touching fire) were sufficient for fault regardless of intention, then, by the same logic, the fire itself should be burned by the very death it causes, just as it burns whatever touches it — yet fault is never assigned to the killing implement or agent this way. Furthermore, if intention were truly irrelevant, then commissioning another person to kill on one's behalf ought to leave the instigator entirely without fault, exactly as fire itself bears no responsibility for burning someone who merely follows another's instruction to touch it — an outcome no one actually accepts. And pieces of wood and other inanimate objects would likewise have to be touched by the fault of killing, since a collapsing house or falling timber can just as easily take a life. The point being established here is not merely illustrative — it rests on sound reasoning in its own right, not simply on analogy. Having now settled the characteristics of killing, the treatise turns to define theft with the same precision: theft requires a premeditated intention to steal, correctly recognizing the property as belonging to another, taking possession of it — whether by force or by stealth — without accident, and thereby making it one's own; only to this extent is the fault of theft, in the technical sense, actually completed. Several special cases follow: stealing an offering dedicated to a stupa constitutes theft specifically from the Tathāgata himself, since the Buddha, at the moment of entering final nirvāṇa, out of compassion for the world, is understood to have accepted all future offerings made to him in perpetuity — though other teachers hold this should instead be understood with reference to whoever is currently responsible for guarding such property. Digging up and taking an ownerless buried treasure constitutes theft specifically from the reigning sovereign. And taking property designated for the saṅgha's rotating communal use, whether the formal procedure authorizing its use has already been completed for a particular local community or not yet completed at all, constitutes theft against the Buddha's disciples universally; other similar cases should be reasoned through by analogy.

15. The Precise Definition of Sexual Misconduct, and Its Edge Cases

Having discussed theft, the treatise defines sexual misconduct precisely, identifying four distinct ways an act can be improper, each independently sufficient to constitute this fault. The first is impropriety of partner: engaging in sexual activity with someone else's wife or concubine, or with one's own mother, father, a relative of either parent, or anyone under a ruler's specific protection. The second is impropriety of orifice: engaging in sexual activity with one's own wife through the mouth or through any orifice other than the proper one. The third is impropriety of place: engaging in sexual activity within monastery grounds or at a stupa's isolated precincts. The fourth is impropriety of time: engaging in sexual activity during a spouse's pregnancy, while she is nursing a child, or while she is observing the upavāsa precept — even with one's own lawfully wedded wife or concubine, sexual activity under any of these circumstances still constitutes this same fault, though some hold that this specifically applies only once the husband has agreed to his wife's observing the precept and then violates that agreement, this too being "wrong time." The qualification "without accident," established for the earlier karma-paths, applies here as well. If someone mistakes another's wife for their own, or mistakes their own wife for another's, and errs only as to which orifice or similar detail is proper, then, although the act itself occurs, no karma-path is actually completed, since the mistake removes the required correct recognition. If someone engages in improper conduct with one particular married woman while genuinely believing her to be an entirely different married woman, does this still complete the karma-path? Some hold that it does, since sexual misconduct's preparatory action and its actual consummation have both, in fact, occurred with respect to some married woman; others hold that it does not, reasoning by analogy to killing, where the karma-path is completed only if the very being targeted by the preparatory action is the same being with whom the act is actually completed. Engaging in sexual misconduct with a Buddhist nun raises its own question: from whom, precisely, is the fault of theft-like violation incurred in this specific case? The answer is that it is incurred with respect to the ruling sovereign, since it is the sovereign's own authority that does not tolerate such an act — after all, even engaging with one's own wife or concubine during her observance of the precept is already improper, so how much more so with someone who has gone forth from household life entirely. And with respect to a young, unmarried girl, from whom is the fault incurred? If she has already been promised in marriage to someone, it is incurred with respect to that intended party; if not yet promised to anyone, it is incurred with respect to whoever is currently responsible for her protection; and this case, along with all similar ones, may in every instance also be understood as incurred with respect to the sovereign.

16. Precisely When a Lie Becomes Complete, and the Four Kinds of Testimony

Having discussed sexual misconduct, the treatise turns to false speech: it consists in speaking with a corrupted intention to convey something other than what one actually believes to be true, where the person being deceived actually comes to understand the intended, false meaning of what was said — a defiled mind, speaking without accident, completes the fault. If the person being deceived never actually comes to understand the meaning of what was said, what kind of speech has occurred instead? It counts merely as frivolous speech, not false speech proper. Since a lie is typically composed of many syllables strung together, exactly when does the karma-path become complete? The treatise holds that it is completed together with the very last syllable — that syllable's own manifested vocal sound and its accompanying unmanifested karma together constitute the completed karma-path; alternatively, it may be said to be completed at whatever moment the deceived party actually comes to understand the intended meaning, with that moment's manifested and unmanifested karma constituting the completed karma-path, and every syllable preceding that moment functioning merely as this karma-path's preparatory action. This raises a further technical question: precisely when does "understanding the meaning" itself occur — once the listener, having already heard the utterance, has correctly grasped it, or already at the very moment of properly hearing it, prior to any subsequent processing? If understanding requires already having correctly grasped what was heard, then, since the meaning conveyed by speech is known specifically through mental consciousness, and the auditory consciousness that actually hears the spoken sound has, by that point, already ceased along with the manifested speech-act itself, this would mean the karma-path could only ever be completed through unmanifested karma alone, never through any accompanying manifested karma at that instant. If, instead, understanding is held to occur already at the moment of properly hearing, though this position, too, avoids one difficulty, it raises another: how can mere proper hearing, without yet having actually processed the meaning, already count as "understanding"? For someone skilled with language, however, where no confusion or distraction intervenes, the very arising of auditory consciousness itself may properly be called "understanding" — and this latter position, being free of difficulty, should be adopted as the correct one. A sūtra further teaches that there are, in total, sixteen kinds of statement: stating that one has truly seen, heard, sensed, or known something one has, in fact, not seen, heard, sensed, or known at all, or stating that one has not seen, heard, sensed, or known something one has, in fact, seen, heard, sensed, or known — these eight kinds together constitute "ignoble speech." Conversely, truthfully stating that one has not seen, heard, sensed, or known what one has genuinely not seen, heard, sensed, or known, or truthfully stating that one has seen, heard, sensed, or known what one genuinely has — these eight kinds together constitute "noble speech." What, precisely, distinguishes what is "seen" from what is "heard," "known," and "sensed"? The Vaibhāṣika hold: whatever object is directly apprehended by eye-consciousness is called "seen"; whatever is apprehended by ear-consciousness is called "heard"; whatever is apprehended by mental consciousness is called "known"; and whatever is apprehended by the nose-, tongue-, or body-consciousness is called "sensed" — grouped together under this single term specifically because smell, taste, and touch, being themselves morally neutral in nature like something inert or lifeless, are treated as more passively "sensed" rather than actively cognized the way sight, sound, and mental objects are. This grouping is confirmed both by reasoning and by scripture — as a sūtra states, the Buddha addressing Mahāprajāpatī: "What do you think? With respect to all forms that you have not seen with your own eyes, have never seen, will never see, and do not even wish to see — do you, on account of these, give rise to desire, to craving, to fondness, to attachment, to clinging, to grasping, or to infatuation?" The debate over exactly when "understanding" occurs (after full mental processing, versus at the bare instant of correct auditory perception) is a genuinely fine-grained piece of Abhidharma psychology, and we have preserved its careful back-and-forth structure rather than compressing it, since the stakes are real: the treatise's own broader system requires that manifested vocal karma and the auditory consciousness that hears it occur, and cease, together in the same instant — if "understanding" required a further step of mental processing after that instant, false speech's karma-path could seemingly never involve manifested karma at its moment of completion, only unmanifested karma, which the treatise apparently regards as an unwelcome, overly narrow consequence to accept without argument.

17. Completing the Four Kinds of Testimony, and Lying Through Gesture Alone

The treatise completes its citation of the sūtra to Mahāprajāpatī, in which the Buddha, having already asked about unseen forms, goes on to ask the same question about unheard sounds and unknown mental objects, each time receiving the same answer: no such attachment arises. The Buddha then instructs her that, with respect to what is seen, heard, sensed, and known, one should recognize only the bare fact of seeing, hearing, sensing, or knowing, without embellishing it with any further quality of desirability or undesirability. Since this sūtra explicitly names only form, sound, and mental objects when speaking of "seen," "heard," and "known," this is taken as confirmation that smell, taste, and touch are, correspondingly, grouped together under the single term "sensed." An objection follows: if this grouping were not accepted, what else could these three sense-objects be called? Moreover, since smell, taste, and touch clearly lie outside the scope of "seen" and the other three named terms, no statement could even be formed about them at all unless they were covered by "sensed" — and this, it is argued, settles the matter by sound reasoning. The treatise responds that this argument does not actually succeed, since the sūtra cited does not, in fact, serve as genuine proof for this specific point — its actual purpose was never to settle the technical meanings of "seen," "heard," "sensed," and "known" at all, but rather to encourage the practitioner, with respect to the six sense-objects, to recognize only their bare occurrence without embellishing them with attachment or aversion. If this technical distinction is not settled by this sūtra, what, then, does properly determine each term's precise meaning? Other teachers propose an alternative account: whatever is directly witnessed through the five sense-faculties themselves is called "seen"; whatever is known through another's report is called "heard"; whatever is inferred through one's own reasoning based on accepted principles is called "sensed"; and whatever is directly, mentally cognized is called "known" — under this scheme, each of the five sense-objects independently admits being described by any of these four terms as appropriate, and, for mental objects specifically, three of the four terms apply (excluding only "seen"), meaning the term "sensed" is by no means left without any object of its own, and smell, taste, and touch can indeed each be spoken of in their own right — so the original objection's claim of "no reasoning" is itself shown to be unreasonable. Earlier teachers of the tradition offer yet another account: what is directly perceived by the eye is called "seen"; what is learned through another's account is called "heard"; what one arrives at through one's own reflection and reasoning is called "sensed"; and what one directly experiences or personally realizes within is called "known." Setting this digression aside, we return to the main topic: can the fault of false speech be incurred through a bodily gesture conveying a different meaning than the truth, without any actual speech being uttered at all? Indeed it can — hence the root treatise itself asks: can the fault of killing be incurred without the body moving at all? Yes — through speech alone. Can the fault of false speech be incurred without any speech being uttered at all? Yes — through bodily movement alone. Can both faults be incurred without either the body moving or speech being uttered? Yes — as in the case of a sage's inwardly harbored anger, or the specific circumstance of formally suspending a fellow monastic from participation in the communal recitation. If, in such a case, neither the body moves nor speech is uttered, how can unmanifested karma still arise, given that it is not accompanied by any manifested act at all? This is a point that genuinely calls for careful further consideration.

This appears to be a recognized exception or special case within the tradition specifically tied to a sage's spiritual potency, rather than a general revision of the treatise's usual rule that mental karma alone cannot generate unmanifested karma — though the text does not fully explain the mechanism here, consistent with its own admission that this point requires further effort to resolve.

18. Divisive Speech, Harsh Speech, and Frivolous Speech

Having finished with false speech, the treatise now addresses the remaining three verbal karma-paths, opening with a summary verse: speech arising from a defiled mind that breaks apart another's relationships is called divisive speech; disagreeable, coarse speech is called harsh speech; and speech arising from any of the various defilements is called frivolous speech — while some other teachers hold that frivolous speech is specifically distinguished from the first three defiled categories, naming flattery, song, and heterodox treatises as its characteristic instances. Divisive speech is defined as speech uttered with a defiled mind aiming to break apart another's relationships — the karma-path is completed once the hearer understands the intended meaning without any error, regardless of whether the target relationship actually ends up broken or not, since what matters is the successful communication of the divisive intent, following the same "correct understanding" standard already established for false speech. Harsh speech is defined similarly, but with the aim of disparaging another through disagreeable words spoken from a defiled mind harboring the original intention to scold or revile that person — again completed once the intended meaning is correctly understood by the hearer. Frivolous speech, in its broadest characterization, is simply any speech uttered from a defiled mind that does not otherwise qualify as one of the first three verbal karma-paths — since, the treatise reasons, all speech arising from defilement is inherently corrupting and unproductive in this sense. However, other teachers propose a narrower, more specific characterization: frivolous speech names particular categories of defiled speech distinct from the first three — namely, flattery (obsequious speech from one who sustains himself through an improper livelihood, such as a monastic who speaks ingratiatingly to secure support), song (worldly people teasing one another with defiled playful verses, or performers composing verses from a defiled mind meant to please an audience), heterodox treatises (elaborate discourses that expound and defend wrong views at length), and, more broadly still, defiled lamentation and various forms of idle worldly banter. A pointed question follows: does not a wheel-turning monarch himself sing at times — would this not then also count as frivolous speech? The response is that a wheel-turning monarch's song arises from a mind of renunciation and itself gives rise to further renunciation, rather than proceeding from a prior defiled state, and so does not qualify. Other teachers, however, hold that even at such times there can be defiled speech connected with matters like arranging a marriage, though because the fault involved is comparatively slight, it does not rise to the level of completing a full karma-path. Having now finished with the three verbal karma-paths, the treatise turns to the three mental karma-paths.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag honestly that the two definitions of frivolous speech offered here are genuinely quite different in scope — the first (any defiled speech not already covered by the other three verbal paths) is extremely broad, while the second (specifically flattery, song, heterodox treatises, lamentation, and idle banter) is much narrower and more concrete. The treatise does not clearly state which view it ultimately favors, so we have presented both without artificially resolving the tension between them.

The wheel-turning monarch exception is a small but telling detail: it shows this tradition distinguishing an action's karmic quality not merely by its outward form (singing is singing, whether done by a monarch or a flatterer) but by the state of mind from which it proceeds and the state of mind it tends to produce — a renunciate king's song, arising from and reinforcing renunciation, is treated as categorically different from song arising from and reinforcing attachment, even though the two might look identical to an outside observer.
