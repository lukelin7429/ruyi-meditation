Is Ignorance Something, or Nothing?

This page carries the opening of fascicle 10, continuing Chapter 3 (分別世品第三之三, the Loka-nirdeśa's third installment) with a shift from narrative to precise definitional analysis: what, exactly, do the twelve links' key terms actually refer to? The fascicle opens with a genuinely foundational question — is "ignorance" a real, distinct mental factor in its own right, or merely a name for the absence of wisdom — before working through name-and-form, the many subdivisions of contact, and feeling.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 25 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 10 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. Is Ignorance a Real Thing, or Just the Absence of Wisdom?

A foundational question opens the fascicle: is "ignorance" simply a name for wisdom's absence, the way "non-friend" might seem to just mean "no friend"? The treatise argues no — ignorance has its own genuine essential nature, opposed to wisdom the way an enemy is opposed to a friend, neither identical to its opposite's absence nor something wholly unrelated to it.

Chinese Source

無明何義？謂體非明。若爾，無明應是眼等。既爾，此義應謂明無。若爾，無明體應非有。為顯有體義不濫餘，頌曰：明所治無明，如非親實等。論曰：如諸親友所對怨敵，親友相違名非親友，非異親友、非親友無。諦語名實，此所對治虛誑言論名為非實，非異於實、亦非實無。等言為顯非法非義，非事等性非異非無。如是無明別有實體，是明所治，非異非無。云何知然？說行緣故。復有誠證。頌曰：說為結等故，非惡慧見故，與見相應故，說能染慧故。論曰：經說無明以為結縛、隨眠及漏、軛、瀑、流等，非餘眼等及體全無可得說為結縛等事，故有別法說名無明。如惡妻子名無妻子，如是惡慧應名無明，彼非無明有是見故。諸染污慧名為惡慧，於中有見故非無明。若爾，非見慧應許是無明。不爾，無明見相應故。無明若是慧，應見不相應，無二慧體共相應故。又說無明能染慧故，如契經言：貪欲染心令不解脫，無明染慧令不清淨，非慧還能染於慧體。如貪異類能染於心。無明亦應異慧能染，如何不許？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the meaning of "ignorance"? One might say: its essential nature is simply "not wisdom." If that is so, then ignorance would have to include even things like the eye. Given this problem, the meaning should instead be: ignorance is the absence of wisdom. If that is so, then ignorance would have no essential nature of its own at all. In order to show that it does have its own essential nature, without this being confused with anything else, verse: ignorance is what is remedied by wisdom — comparable to "non-friend," "non-truth," and the like. Prose: just as, with respect to friends, that which stands opposed to friends — an enemy — is called "non-friend" precisely because it is contrary to friendship, this "non-friend" is neither something entirely different from "friend" nor simply the absence of a friend. Truthful speech is called "truth"; that which is remedied by this — deceptive, false speech — is called "non-truth." This, too, is neither something entirely different from truth, nor simply the absence of truth. The word "and so on" is meant to indicate "non-dharma," "non-benefit," "non-thing," and similar terms — these, too, are neither something entirely different from their positive counterparts, nor simply their absence. In just the same way, ignorance has its own separate, real essential nature: it is what is remedied by wisdom, being neither something entirely different from wisdom nor simply the absence of wisdom. How is this known to be so? Because it is said to condition formations. There is, moreover, further decisive evidence. Verse: because it is spoken of as a fetter and the like; because it is not identical to afflicted wisdom accompanied by views; because it is associated with views; and because it is said to be capable of defiling wisdom. Prose: a sutra describes ignorance as a fetter, a bond, a latent tendency, a canker, a yoke, a flood, a stream, and so on. Neither something like the eye, nor a complete absence of anything at all, could possibly be described in these terms as a fetter and the rest — hence there must be a separate, real dharma that is called "ignorance." Just as a bad wife might loosely be called "no wife at all," so too should bad, afflicted wisdom be called "ignorance," rather than positing a separate entity. That afflicted wisdom, however, is not itself ignorance, since it possesses views. Defiled forms of wisdom are called "bad wisdom"; since views are present within this category, it cannot itself be ignorance. If that is so, then at least defiled wisdom that lacks views ought to be accepted as ignorance. It is not so, since ignorance is instead associated with views. If ignorance were itself a form of wisdom, it ought not to be associated with views — but no two distinct essences of wisdom can be mutually associated with each other. Moreover, it is said that ignorance is capable of defiling wisdom, as a sutra states: "Desire defiles the mind, preventing liberation; ignorance defiles wisdom, preventing purity." Wisdom cannot defile its own essential nature. Just as desire, being of a different category from mind, is capable of defiling the mind, ignorance too, being of a different category from wisdom, should likewise be capable of defiling it — why would this not be accepted?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The friend/enemy and truth/falsehood analogies are doing careful conceptual work: they establish a third option beyond the simple binary of "identical to X" versus "mere absence of X." An enemy is not simply "no friend" in the way an empty chair is simply "no person sitting there" — an enemy is a positive, actively opposed presence that happens to occupy the conceptual space defined by its relation to friendship. Ignorance, the treatise argues, works the same way: it is not simply the empty space left where wisdom would otherwise be, but an actively opposed mental factor in its own right, defined by its relation to wisdom without being reducible to wisdom's mere non-occurrence.

The "bad wife" objection is a clever attempt to resist positing a separate entity by suggesting ordinary language already has a model for using a negative-sounding label loosely, without literal precision — but the response shows why this particular analogy doesn't transfer: afflicted wisdom already contains views (a positive mental factor in its own right), and since ignorance is elsewhere established as running alongside views rather than being identical to them, afflicted wisdom cannot simply be relabeled "ignorance" without creating exactly the kind of self-association problem (something being associated with itself) this tradition's psychology does not allow.

2. Refining Ignorance's Definition, and an Honest Non-Resolution

A further round presses whether "defiling" wisdom really requires ignorance to be actively co-present with it, before the treatise rejects the more radical claim that all afflictions simply reduce to ignorance — closing with the Bhadanta Dharmatrāta's alternative proposal and a rare, candid admission that the debate could be pursued much further than the treatise is willing to go here.

Chinese Source

諸染污慧間雜善慧令不清淨說為能染，如貪染心令不解脫，豈必現起與心相應方說能染？然由貪力損縛於心令不解脫，後轉滅彼貪熏習時心便解脫。如是無明染污於慧令不清淨，非慧相應，但由無明損濁於慧。如是分別何理相違，誰復能遮自所分別。然異慧類別有無明，如貪異心，此說為善。有執煩惱皆是無明，此亦應同前理遮遣。若諸煩惱皆是無明，於結等中不應別說，亦不應與見等相應，見等不應自相應故。或亦應說無明染心，若謂此中就差別說，應於染慧不說總名。既許無明別法為體，應說此體其相云何。謂不了知諦實業果。未測何相名不了知？為異了知、為此非有？二俱有過，如無明說。此謂了知所治別法。此復難測，其相是何？此類法爾應如是說。如餘處言：云何為眼？謂清淨色眼識所依。無明亦然，唯可辯用。大德法救說此無明是諸有情恃我類性，異於我慢。類體是何？經言：我今如是知已、如是見已，諸所有愛、諸所有見、諸所有類性、諸我我所執、我慢執、隨眠，斷遍知故無影寂滅，故知類性異於我慢。寧知類性即是無明？不可說為餘煩惱故。豈不可說為餘慢等？若更於此巨細研尋，言論繁雜故應且止。

Ru-Yi Translation

Defiled forms of wisdom, intermixed with wholesome wisdom, causing it to become impure — this is what is meant by "defiling," just as desire "defiling the mind and preventing liberation" — must desire necessarily be currently manifest and associated with the mind at that very moment in order to be said to "defile" it? Rather, it is through the power of desire that the mind is bound and damaged, preventing liberation; only later, once that desire's habitual imprinting is eliminated, does the mind become liberated. In just the same way, ignorance defiles wisdom and renders it impure without being associated with wisdom at all — it is simply that ignorance damages and muddies wisdom. What principle does this distinction actually violate? Who, indeed, could refute a distinction of one's own making? Rather, ignorance exists as something genuinely distinct in kind from wisdom, just as desire is distinct from mind — this account should be accepted as correct. There is a further view that holds all afflictions are simply ignorance — this too should be refuted by the very same reasoning as before. If all afflictions were simply ignorance, then, among the fetters and so forth, there ought to be no separate listing of other afflictions; nor should ignorance be said to be "associated with" views and the rest, since views could not be associated with themselves. Or, it ought also to be said that ignorance "defiles the mind" directly — and if it is claimed that these various statements are simply speaking with reference to specific distinctions, then a general name should not be applied to defiled wisdom specifically. Since it has now been established that ignorance has its own separate essential nature, its specific characteristic should be stated: it is the failure to correctly know the truths, karma, and its results. But what, precisely, does "failure to correctly know" amount to? Is it something distinct from correct knowing, or is it simply the absence of correct knowing? Either answer runs into the same difficulty already discussed regarding ignorance itself. This refers to the separate, real dharma that is remedied by correct knowing. But this, too, is difficult to pin down — what, exactly, is its characteristic? A dharma of this basic kind simply must be described in just this way. As is said elsewhere: "What is the eye? It is the pure form that serves as the basis for eye-consciousness." Ignorance is just the same — it, too, can only be characterized by its function. The Bhadanta Dharmatrāta states: this ignorance is sentient beings' "class-nature" of relying upon a self, distinct from the conceit of self. What is this "class-nature"? A sutra states: "Now that I have thus known, and thus seen, all craving, all views, all class-nature, all attachment to self and what belongs to self, all conceit of self, and all latent tendencies have been cut off through complete understanding, leaving no trace, utterly quiescent" — from this it is known that "class-nature" is distinct from "the conceit of self." But how is it known that this "class-nature" is precisely ignorance? Because it cannot be said to be any of the other afflictions. But could it not equally be said to be some other kind of conceit, and so on? If we were to investigate this matter down to its finest details, the discussion would become excessively elaborate — let us, for now, set it aside.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing admission is genuinely refreshing after such a tightly argued sequence: rather than forcing a decisive verdict on Dharmatrāta's proposal, the text explicitly declines to pursue the question further, noting that finer investigation would simply generate more and more elaborate argument without a clear stopping point. This is a rare and honest acknowledgment that not every doctrinal question this tradition raises actually receives — or needs — a fully adjudicated answer within the text itself.

The final positive characterization of ignorance — "the failure to correctly know the truths, karma, and its results," described only by its function, the way the eye is defined only as "whatever serves as the basis for eye-consciousness" — is a quietly significant methodological point in its own right. Some of this tradition's most basic mental factors, it turns out, cannot be defined by breaking them down into something simpler; they can only be pointed to by what they do, in relation to something else. This is not treated as a failure of analysis, but as the honest terminus every analytical project eventually reaches: some things are basic, and basic things are known by their function, not by further decomposition.

3. Why the Four Formless Aggregates Are Called "Name"

Form has already been explained; now the treatise turns to why feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness are collectively called "name" — because they operate the way names themselves do, turning to designate their objects, or, on a further view, because they turn and carry over into a new life the way a name carries over to something new.

Chinese Source

名色何義？色如先辯，今唯辯名。頌曰：名無色四蘊。論曰：無色四蘊何故稱名？隨所立名根境勢力於義轉變，故說為名。云何隨名勢力轉變？謂隨種種世共立名，於彼彼義轉變詮表，即如牛馬色味等名。此復何緣標以名稱？於彼彼境轉變而緣。又類似名，隨名顯故。有餘師說：四無色蘊，捨此身已轉趣餘生，轉變如名，故標名稱。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the meaning of "name-and-form"? Form has already been discussed earlier; only "name" remains to be discussed here. Verse: "name" refers to the four formless aggregates. Prose: why are the four formless aggregates called "name"? Because, in accordance with the power of whatever name is assigned to them by way of a faculty and its object, their meaning is transformed — hence they are called "name." How, then, are they "transformed by the power of a name"? In accordance with the various names commonly established by convention in the world, they are variously transformed and made to convey their respective meanings — just as with names like "cow," "horse," "color," "flavor," and so on. Why, then, are they specifically labeled with the term "name"? Because they turn and take hold of their respective objects. Moreover, they resemble a name, since they are revealed in accordance with a name. There is a further view held by other masters: the four formless aggregates, having relinquished this body, turn and proceed onward to another life — this turning is comparable to a name — hence they are labeled with the term "name."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The core idea is that mental phenomena, unlike form, cannot be directly pointed to or perceived the way a color or a shape can — they must be picked out and designated the way a name designates its referent, turning attention toward a particular meaning rather than simply presenting themselves to be seen. The second, alternative explanation reaches for a different but related sense of "turning": just as a name can be reapplied to something new (the same word "cow" picking out any number of different cows), the four formless aggregates likewise "turn" from one life to a completely different one at death, carrying the same basic pattern forward into a new body the way a name carries forward into new applications.

4. Contact: A Threefold Union, and a Timing Puzzle

Contact is defined as what arises from the union of faculty, object, and consciousness — straightforward for the five physical senses, where all three arise together, but genuinely puzzling for mental contact, where the mind-faculty belongs to the past, its object may belong to the future, and consciousness alone is present.

Chinese Source

觸何為義？頌曰：觸六三和生。論曰：觸有六種，所謂眼觸乃至意觸。此復是何？三和所生，謂根、境、識三和合故有別觸生。且五觸生可三和合，許根境識俱時起故。意根過去、法或未來、意識現在，如何和合？此即名和合，謂因果義成。或同一果故名和合，謂根境識三同順生觸故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the meaning of "contact"? Verse: there are six kinds of contact, produced by a threefold union. Prose: contact is of six kinds — namely, eye-contact, up through mind-contact. What, then, is this contact? It is produced by a threefold union: that is, because faculty, object, and consciousness come together in union, a distinct contact is produced. As for the first five kinds of contact, their threefold union is unproblematic, since faculty, object, and consciousness are accepted as arising simultaneously in these cases. But the mind-faculty belongs to the past, its object may belong to the future, and mental consciousness belongs to the present — how, then, can these three unite? This very relationship is itself what is meant by "union": that is, the relationship of cause and result is thereby established between them. Or, alternatively, they are called "united" because they share a single common result: that is, faculty, object, and consciousness, all three together, jointly conduce to producing contact.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The resolution redefines "union" away from strict temporal simultaneity and toward a looser, causal-relational sense: three things "unite" not necessarily by occupying the very same instant together, but by standing in the right kind of cause-and-result relationship, or by jointly contributing to one shared outcome. This move is worth noticing because it echoes a pattern already seen elsewhere in this tradition — recall the earlier treatment of the immediately-antecedent condition, which likewise turned out not to require strict adjacency, only the absence of anything intervening. "Union" and "immediacy," in this system, both turn out to be functional, causal notions rather than narrowly temporal ones.

5. Is Contact a Separate Thing, or Just the Union Itself?

Two scriptural citations point in opposite directions: one calls the threefold union itself "contact," the other lists contact separately alongside faculty, object, and consciousness in the "six sixes" scheme. The two sides trade objections and counter-readings until the treatise candidly admits the exchange could run on indefinitely.

Chinese Source

諸師於此覺慧不同。有說：三和即名為觸。彼引經證。如契經言：如是三法聚集和合說名為觸。有說：別法與心相應，三和所生說名為觸。彼引經證。經言：云何六六法門？一、六內處；二、六外處；三、六識身；四、六觸身；五、六受身；六、六愛身。此契經中根境識外別說六觸，故觸別有。說即三和名為觸者，釋後所引六六經言，非由別說便有別體。勿受及愛非法處攝無如是失，離愛受觸別有所餘法處體故。汝宗離觸無別有三，可觸及三差別而說，雖有根境不發於識，而無有識不託根境。故已說三，更別說觸便成無用。有餘救言：非諸眼色皆諸眼識因，非諸眼識皆諸眼色果。非因果者別說為三，因果所收總立為觸。說離三和有別觸者，釋前所引如是三法聚集和合名觸。經言我部所誦經文異此，或於因上假說果名，如說諸佛出現樂等。如是展轉更相難釋，言論煩多故應且止。

Ru-Yi Translation

On this point, the masters' understanding differs. There is a view that holds: the threefold union itself is precisely what is called "contact." This view cites a sutra as evidence, which states: "These three dharmas, gathered together in union, are called contact." There is a further view that holds: contact is a separate dharma, associated with mind, produced by the threefold union. This view, too, cites a sutra as evidence, which states: "What are the six sixes? First, the six internal sense-bases; second, the six external sense-bases; third, the six consciousness-bodies; fourth, the six contact-bodies; fifth, the six feeling-bodies; sixth, the six craving-bodies." In this sutra, six kinds of contact are described separately, beyond faculty, object, and consciousness — hence contact must be a separate, real entity. Those who hold that the threefold union itself is what is called "contact" explain the latter citation by saying: it is not the case that, simply because something is stated separately, it must therefore have a separate essential nature. Then feeling and craving, too, should not be subsumed under the dharma-sense-field — yet no such fault is found in their case, since, apart from craving and feeling, contact still has a further, remaining essential nature belonging to the dharma-sense-field. In your own position, apart from contact there is nothing further beyond the three — so contact could, at most, be spoken of as merely a way of referring to the distinction among these three. But although there can be faculty and object without consciousness being produced, there is never consciousness without it relying on faculty and object; hence, since the three have already been mentioned, to additionally and separately mention "contact" would render it entirely superfluous. There is a further attempt at rescue: not every instance of eye and form serves as a cause for eye-consciousness, and not every instance of eye-consciousness is a result of eye and form. Whatever does not stand in this specific causal relationship is spoken of separately as merely "the three"; whatever is actually subsumed under this cause-and-result relationship is collectively established as "contact." Those who hold that contact is a separate entity apart from the threefold union explain the earlier-cited sutra passage differently: they say the sutra recited in their own school's tradition differs from this citation; or, alternatively, even granting the citation, the name of the result may be figuratively applied to its cause, as when it is said "the arising of Buddhas is bliss." In this way, each side raises further objections and counter-explanations against the other, back and forth — since the discussion would become excessively elaborate, let us, for now, set it aside.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both sides argue from the same basic method — pointing to a sutra that seems to settle the matter, then reinterpreting the other side's sutra to avoid genuine contradiction — and neither citation turns out to be fully decisive once subjected to scrutiny. The identity theorists (contact just is the threefold union) must explain away the "six sixes" sutra's separate listing of contact as merely conventional, not ontological, separateness; the separatists must explain away the "three dharmas gathered together are called contact" sutra as either a textual variant their own tradition doesn't recognize, or as a case of naming a cause after its effect, the way one might loosely say a Buddha's mere arising in the world already "is" the bliss that arising will eventually produce.

The treatise's candid refusal to adjudicate this dispute — the second such admission in this fascicle alone, after ignorance's exact relationship to "class-nature" — is worth noting as a pattern rather than dismissing as evasion. Some questions in this tradition's Abhidharma project remain genuinely contested even at this late, mature stage of systematization, and the text is willing to say so plainly rather than manufacture false confidence. This is itself a useful thing to know about how to read the Abhidharmakośa: not every technical dispute it raises comes with a settled answer, and recognizing which ones don't is part of reading the text accurately.

6. Contact's Many Subdivisions: Resistant vs. Designation, Then Eight, Ten, Sixteen Kinds

Having established that contact is a real, separate dharma, the treatise returns to classify it: first into "resistant contact" (the five physical senses) versus "designation-contact" (the mental sixth, so called because only mental consciousness can attach a name or concept to what it apprehends), then into progressively finer eight-, ten-, and sixteen-fold schemes built from association with clarity or ignorance, from craving and hostility, and from the three kinds of feeling each variety of contact conduces to.

Chinese Source

然對法者說有別觸，即前六觸復合為二。頌曰：五相應有對，第六俱增語。論曰：眼等五觸說名有對，以有對根為所依故。第六意觸說名增語。所以然者，增語謂名，名是意觸所緣長境，故偏就此名增語觸。如說眼識但能了青不了是青，意識了青亦了是青，故名為長。故有對觸名從所依，增語觸名就所緣立。有說：意識語為增上方於境轉，五識不然，是故意識獨名增語，與此相應名增語觸。故有對觸名從所依，增語觸名就相應立。即前六觸隨別相應復成八種。頌曰：明無明非二，無漏染污餘，愛恚二相應，樂等順三受。論曰：明無明等相應成三，一明觸、二無明觸、三非明非無明觸。此三如次，應知即是無漏染污餘相應觸。餘謂無漏及染污餘，即有漏善、無覆無記。無明觸中一分數起，依彼復立愛恚二觸，愛恚隨眠共相應故。總攝一切復成三觸：一順樂受觸、二順苦受觸、三順不苦不樂受觸。此三能引樂等受故，或是樂等受所領故，或能為受行相依故，名為順受。如何觸為受所領行？相依行相極似觸，依觸而生故。如是合成十六種觸。

Ru-Yi Translation

Nevertheless, the Ābhidharmikas maintain that contact is indeed a separate, real entity, and the six kinds of contact already discussed can be further recombined into two. Verse: the first five are associated with resistance; the sixth, together with them, is designation-contact. Prose: the contact of the eye and the other four physical senses is called "resistant contact," because it depends on faculties that possess resistance. The sixth, mind-contact, is called "designation-contact." Why is this so? "Designation" refers to a name, and a name is the extended object that mind-contact takes as its object; hence, specifically with reference to this, it is called "designation-contact." As is said: eye-consciousness is able only to apprehend blue but cannot apprehend that "this is blue," whereas mental consciousness apprehends blue and also apprehends that "this is blue" — hence it is called "extended." Thus "resistant contact" is named from what it depends on, while "designation-contact" is named from what it takes as its object. There is a further view: mental speech serves as the dominant factor by which the mind turns toward its object, which the five sense-consciousnesses do not do; therefore mental consciousness alone is called "designation," and what is associated with it is called "designation-contact." On this view, too, "resistant contact" is named from what it depends on, while "designation-contact" is named from what it is associated with. These same six kinds of contact, further divided according to their distinct associations, in turn become eight kinds. Verse: associated with clarity, with ignorance, or with neither of the two; the remainder is the contaminated-wholesome and the non-obscuring-neutral; associated with craving and with hostility are two more; and conducing to pleasure and the rest are three further kinds. Prose: through association with clarity, ignorance, and so on, contact becomes three kinds — first, clarity-contact; second, ignorance-contact; third, contact that is neither clarity nor ignorance. These three, respectively, should be understood as contact associated with the uncontaminated, with the afflicted, and with the remainder. "The remainder" means what is left over after the uncontaminated and the afflicted — namely, the contaminated-but-wholesome and the non-obscuring-neutral. Within ignorance-contact, a certain portion arises repeatedly; on this basis, craving-contact and hostility-contact are further established, since the latent tendencies of craving and hostility are, respectively, associated with it. Gathering all of these together, contact further becomes three more kinds: first, contact conducive to pleasant feeling; second, contact conducive to painful feeling; third, contact conducive to neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling. These three are called "conducive to feeling" because they give rise to pleasant feeling and the rest, or because they are, in a sense, apprehended by pleasant feeling and the rest, or because they serve as the supporting basis for feeling's own mode of activity. How is it that contact is "apprehended" by feeling's mode of activity? Because feeling's supporting mode of activity closely resembles contact, since it arises in dependence upon contact. In this way, altogether, contact is combined into sixteen kinds.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Designation-contact" builds directly on fascicle 10's own earlier discussion of why the four formless aggregates are called "name" (section 3, above): mental consciousness doesn't just register raw sensory content the way eye-consciousness registers blue, it also turns that content into something nameable — "this is blue" — which is exactly the kind of conceptual, name-attaching operation the earlier section described. The five physical senses only ever touch bare, undesignated objects; the mind alone designates.

Honest flag: the prose states each stage's final count — "eight," "sixteen" — without spelling out, character by character, exactly how the intermediate categories combine to reach those totals. We have translated literally what the text actually enumerates at each step (three by clarity/ignorance/neither, two more for craving/hostility within ignorance-contact, three more for the three feelings), but the text does not make fully explicit whether "eight" also involves crossing these three back against the resistant/designation distinction just established, or some other combination we have not reconstructed with confidence. Rather than supply our own arithmetic to make the numbers come out tidily, we are flagging this compression honestly, as instructed.

7. What Is Feeling? And Does It Arise With Contact, or Only After It?

Contact's classification complete, the treatise defines feeling simply as whatever each of the six kinds of contact directly produces — five bodily feelings, one mental. But this immediately opens onto a genuinely difficult causal question the Vaibhāṣikas spend a long debate defending: can feeling really arise at the very same moment as the contact that produces it, given that nothing which has already arisen should still need anything else to produce it?

Chinese Source

受何為義？頌曰：從此生六受，五屬身餘心。論曰：從前六觸生於六受，謂眼觸所生受至意觸所生受。六中前五說為身受，依色根故。意觸所生說為心受，但依心故。受生與觸，為後、為俱？毘婆沙師說俱時起，觸受展轉俱有因故。云何二法俱時而生？能生所生義可成立。如何不立？無功能故，於已生法餘法無能。此與立宗義意無別，如言二法俱時而生，能生所生義不成立；於已生法餘法無能，義意同前，重說何用？若爾，便有互相生失。許故非失。我宗許二為俱有因，亦互為果。仁雖許爾，而契經中不許此二互為因果。契經但說：眼觸為緣生眼觸所生受。曾無經說，眼受為緣生眼受所生觸。又此義非理，越能生法故。若法極成能生彼法，此法與彼時別極成。如先種後芽、先乳後酪、先擊後聲、先意後識等，先因後果非不極成。亦有極成同時因果，如眼識等，眼色等俱。四大種俱有所造色，此中亦許前根境緣能發後識。前大造聚生後造色，何理能遮？如影與身，豈非俱有。

What is the meaning of "feeling"? Verse: from these six kinds of contact arise six feelings; five belong to the body, the remaining one to the mind. Prose: from the six kinds of contact discussed above, six feelings are produced — namely, the feeling produced by eye-contact, up through the feeling produced by mind-contact. Of these six, the first five are called "bodily feelings," because they depend on the physical faculties. The feeling produced by mind-contact is called "mental feeling," because it depends only on the mind. Does feeling arise after contact, or simultaneously with it? The Vaibhāṣikas hold that the two arise at the same time, because contact and feeling reciprocally serve as each other's co-existent cause. But how can two dharmas that arise simultaneously stand in a relationship of producer and produced? This relationship can indeed be established. How can it be established? Because a thing has no causal efficacy — with respect to a dharma that has already arisen, no other dharma has any further capacity to produce it. This is no different in meaning from the very position being asserted — merely restating that two dharmas which arise simultaneously cannot be established as producer and produced, and that with respect to an already-arisen dharma no other dharma has capacity, says the same thing over again; what use is there in repeating it? If that is so, then there follows the fault of the two mutually producing each other. This is granted, and so it is no fault at all: our own school accepts that the two serve as each other's co-existent cause, and are also mutually each other's result. Though you may grant this, the sūtras do not accept that these two serve as cause and result for each other. The sūtras state only: "eye-contact serves as a condition producing the feeling produced by eye-contact" — never has any sūtra stated that "eye-feeling serves as a condition producing the contact produced by eye-feeling." Moreover, this position is unreasonable, since it transgresses the principle that a producing dharma must be distinct in time from what it produces. Whenever a dharma is definitively established as capable of producing another dharma, this dharma and that dharma are definitively established as occurring at distinct times — as with seed preceding sprout, milk preceding curd, striking preceding sound, mental contact preceding consciousness, and so forth: cause preceding result in this way is not without definitive precedent. But there are also definitively established cause-and-result relationships that are simultaneous — as with eye-consciousness and the rest, which occur together with eye and form. It is likewise accepted here that the four great elements occur together with the derived form they produce, and that the preceding faculty-and-object condition is able to give rise to the subsequent consciousness, just as a preceding aggregation of great elements gives rise to a subsequent derived form. By what principle could this be denied? Is a shadow not simultaneous with the body that casts it?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "co-existent cause" (俱有因) doctrine, already named back in fascicle 6's list of the six causes, is doing real work here: it lets two things be simultaneous and yet still stand in a producer/produced relationship, because "producing" in this system does not require the strict temporal priority ordinary intuition expects of causation. The willingness to say two dharmas are "mutually cause and result of one another" is a genuinely distinctive, non-obvious feature of this causal theory — a modern reader trained on one-directional, asymmetric causation may find it strange, and it is worth flagging as such rather than smoothing it into something more familiar than it actually is.

This also connects back to section 4's note on contact's own "union" (和合) turning out to be a causal-functional notion rather than a strictly temporal one — the same move recurs here: "simultaneous" and "sequential" are both being redefined in terms of causal relationship rather than raw succession in time, a pattern that seems to run throughout this fascicle's treatment of the twelve links' more technical vocabulary.

8. A Rival View, the Ten Mahābhūmika Dharmas, and Four Scriptural Puzzles

A rival position holds that feeling arises only in a third moment after contact, sequentially rather than simultaneously — but this view runs headlong into the doctrine that the ten mahābhūmika dharmas (including feeling) must accompany every single moment of mind without exception, and into four separate scriptural passages that each seem, on a first reading, to describe contact and feeling as strictly simultaneous.

Chinese Source

有說：觸後方有受生，根境為先，次有識起，此三合故即名為觸，第三剎那緣觸生受。若爾，應識非皆有受，諸識亦應非皆是觸。無如是失。因前位觸故，後觸位受生，故諸觸時皆悉有受，所有識體無非是觸。此不應理。何理相違？謂或有時二觸境別，因前受位觸生後觸位受。如何異境受從異境觸生？或應許受此心相應，非與此心同緣一境。既爾，若許有成觸，識是觸無受。於此位前有識有受而體非觸，緣差故然。斯有何過？若爾，便壞十大地法，彼定一切心品恒俱。彼定恒俱依何教立？依本論立。我等但以契經為量，本論非量，壞之何咎？故世尊言：當依經量。或大地法義非要遍諸心。若爾，何名大地法義？謂有三地：一有尋有伺地、二無尋唯伺地、三無尋無伺地。復有三地：一善地、二不善地、三無記地。復有三地：一學地、二無學地、三非學非無學地。若法於前諸地皆有，名大地法。若法唯於諸善地中有，名大善地法。若法唯於諸染地中有，名大煩惱地法。如是等法各隨所應更代而生，非皆並起。餘說如是大不善地法，因誦引來，是今所增益，非本所誦。若於觸後方有受生，經云何釋？如契經說：眼及色為緣生於眼識，三和合觸俱起受想思。但言俱起不說觸俱，此於我宗何違須釋？又於無間亦有俱聲，如契經說：與慈俱行修念覺支。故彼非證。若爾，何故契經中言：是受是想是思是識，如是諸法相雜不離，故無有識離於受等。今應審思，相雜何義？此經復說：諸所受即所思，諸所思即所想，諸所想即所識。未了於此，為約所緣、為約剎那作如是說？於壽與煖俱時起中，亦有如斯相雜言故，例知此說定約剎那。又契經言三和合觸，如何有識而非三和？或是三和而不名觸？故應定許一切識俱悉皆有觸，諸所有觸無不皆與受等俱生。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a further view: feeling arises only after contact. Faculty and object come first; consciousness then arises; and it is the union of these three that is called "contact" — then, in a third moment, feeling arises in dependence on contact. If that is so, then not every instance of consciousness would have feeling, and not every instance of consciousness would be contact. No such fault follows. Because of contact occurring at a prior moment, feeling arises at the moment of the subsequent contact; hence at every moment of contact, feeling is invariably present, and there is no instance of consciousness's essential nature that is not contact. This is not tenable. What principle does it violate? Namely: there are sometimes cases where two moments of contact have different objects, yet feeling is said, on this account, to arise from the prior moment's contact at the position of the subsequent contact. How could feeling with a different object arise from contact with a different object? It should instead be accepted that feeling is associated with this particular mind without needing to share an identical object with this mind. Given this, then, if it is accepted that only three things need combine to constitute contact, consciousness could be contact without feeling. Even prior to this position, there could be consciousness together with feeling whose essential nature is nevertheless not contact, simply because of a deficiency in the necessary conditions — what fault would this entail? If that is so, this would undermine the ten mahābhūmika dharmas, which are definitively held to invariably accompany every class of mind. On what scriptural authority is this "invariable accompaniment" established? It is established on the authority of the foundational treatise. We take only the sūtras as our authoritative standard; the foundational treatise is not an authoritative standard — what fault is there in undermining it? For the World-Honored One has said: one should rely on the authority of the sūtras. Or else, perhaps the doctrine of the mahābhūmika dharmas need not require that they pervade every class of mind. If that is so, then what does "mahābhūmika dharma" even mean? There are three grounds: first, the ground possessing both investigation and analysis; second, the ground lacking investigation but possessing only analysis; third, the ground lacking both investigation and analysis. There are, further, three grounds: first, the wholesome ground; second, the unwholesome ground; third, the neutral ground. There are, further, three grounds: first, the ground of the trainee; second, the ground of the one beyond training; third, the ground that is neither trainee nor beyond training. A dharma present in all of the aforementioned grounds is called a "mahābhūmika dharma." A dharma present only within the wholesome grounds is called a "great wholesome-ground dharma." A dharma present only within the afflicted grounds is called a "great afflicted-ground dharma." Dharmas of these kinds each arise in succession as appropriate, rather than all arising together at once. Other masters say that the so-called "great unwholesome-ground dharmas" were brought in through textual recitation and represent a later addition, not part of the original recitation. Returning to the main debate: if feeling arises only after contact, how is the following sūtra passage to be explained? As the sūtra states: "eye and form serve as conditions producing eye-consciousness; the union of the three is contact, together with which feeling, perception, and volition arise." The passage merely says they "arise together" — it does not say that contact itself is among what arises together with feeling and the rest; how does this conflict with our own position, such that it would need to be explained away? Moreover, the word "together" is also used even for what is merely immediately successive, as the sūtra states: "cultivating the mindfulness awakening-factor, practiced together with loving-kindness" — hence that passage does not prove simultaneity. If that is so, then why does the sūtra state: "this is feeling, this is perception, this is volition, this is consciousness — these dharmas, being intermixed, are inseparable; hence there is no consciousness apart from feeling" and so on? This should now be carefully considered — what does "intermixed" actually mean? This same sūtra further states: "whatever is felt is exactly what is thought; whatever is thought is exactly what is perceived; whatever is perceived is exactly what is cognized." It is not yet clear here whether this is said with reference to a shared object, or with reference to a single moment. Since a similarly "intermixed" way of speaking is also used for the simultaneous arising of lifespan and vital warmth, it can be inferred by analogy that this statement, too, must be made with reference to a single moment rather than strict identity. Moreover, the sūtra speaks of contact as "the union of the three" — how, then, could there be consciousness that is not part of this threefold union? Or, conversely, might there be a threefold union not yet called "contact"? For these reasons, it should be definitively accepted that every instance of consciousness invariably has contact accompanying it, and that no instance of contact whatsoever fails to arise together with feeling and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Honest flag: this passage never actually lists the ten mahābhūmika dharmas (大地法) by name — it pivots instead to defining the abstract criterion for what makes something "mahābhūmika" at all (present across all nine of the three sets of three grounds just enumerated), rather than enumerating the ten dharmas themselves. A reader coming to this passage hoping to find the classic list (traditionally: feeling, perception, volition, contact, wish, wisdom, mindfulness, mental application, resolute understanding, and concentration) will not find it spelled out here; we have translated only what the text itself actually says at this point.

The four scriptural counter-readings at the end are a good example of this treatise's dialectical method: rather than a single knockout argument, the case for simultaneity is built by taking apart four separate passages the opposing side might cite, one at a time — "together" can mean merely adjacent rather than truly simultaneous; "intermixed" is ambiguous between sharing an object and sharing a single moment; and so on. The debate ends with a "definitive" acceptance of simultaneity, but arrives there through cumulative demolition of counter-readings rather than through one decisive proof — worth noticing as a recurring rhetorical shape in how this text argues, not only what it concludes.

9. Eighteen Kinds of Mental Examination

Returning from the digression to its main line, the treatise divides mental feeling into eighteen "mental examinations" (意近行, manaḥ-upavicāra) — joy, grief, and equanimity, each paired with the six sense objects — then works through why the count comes to eighteen rather than three, one, or six, why bodily feeling and the third dhyāna's mental pleasure faculty are excluded, and why the sūtra's language of "having seen form" should not be read too narrowly.

Chinese Source

傍論已終，應辯本義。頌曰：此復成十八，由意近行異。論曰：於前略說一心受中，由意近行異，復分成十八。應知此復聲，顯乘前起後。此意近行十八云何？謂喜、憂、捨各六近行。此復何緣立為十八？若由自性應但有三，喜憂捨三自性異故。若由相應應唯有一，一切皆與意相應故。若由所緣應但有六，色等六境為所緣故。此成十八，具足由三。於中十五色等近行名不雜緣，境各別故。三法近行皆通二種。意近行名為目何義？傳說喜等意為近緣，於諸境中數遊行故。有說喜等能為近緣，令意於境數遊行故。如何身受非意近行？非唯依意故不名近，由無分別故亦非行。第三靜慮意地樂根，意近行中何故不攝？傳說初界意識相應無樂根故，又無所對苦根所攝意近行故。若唯意地，何故經言：眼見色已，於順喜色起喜近行，廣說如經。依五識身所引意地喜等近行，故作是說。如依眼識引不淨觀，此不淨觀唯意地攝。又彼經言眼見色已乃至廣說，故不應難。若雖非見，乃至觸已而起喜憂捨，亦是意近行。若異此者，在欲界中應無緣色界色等意近行。又在色界應無緣欲香味觸境諸意近行。見色等言隨明了說，見色等已於聲等中起喜憂捨亦意近行，隨無雜亂故作是說，於中建立根境定故。為有色等於喜等三，唯能順生一近行不？有就相續非約所緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The digression has now concluded; the main topic should be addressed. Verse: this further becomes eighteen, on account of the differences among the mental examinations. Prose: within the single mental feeling briefly discussed earlier, on account of the differences among the "mental examinations," it further divides into eighteen. It should be understood that the word "further" here indicates that this builds upon, and follows on from, what came before. What are these eighteen mental examinations? Namely, joy, grief, and equanimity, each with six examinations. Why, then, are these established as eighteen? If classified by essential nature, there ought to be only three, since joy, grief, and equanimity differ in essential nature. If classified by association, there ought to be only one, since all of them alike are associated with mind. If classified by object, there ought to be only six, since form and the other five objects serve as their objects. Since this instead becomes eighteen, the classification is complete precisely by way of all three factors together. Among these, fifteen of them — the examinations concerning form and the rest — are called "non-mixed-object" examinations, because their objects are each distinct; the three dharma-examinations, by contrast, are of both kinds. What does the term "mental examination" mean to indicate? Tradition holds: joy and the rest serve as a proximate condition for the mind, because the mind, by their power, repeatedly roams about among its objects. There is a further view: joy and the rest are themselves capable of serving as this proximate condition, causing the mind to repeatedly roam about its object. Why is bodily feeling not a mental examination? It is not called "proximate" to mind, since it does not depend on mind alone; and it is also not "roaming," since it lacks discrimination. Why is the pleasure faculty of the mental ground in the third dhyāna not included among the mental examinations? Tradition holds: because, at that level, there is no pleasure faculty associated with mental consciousness, and also because there is no mental-ground examination classed under a corresponding pain faculty to pair it with. If mental examination pertains only to the mental ground, then why does the sūtra state: "having seen form with the eye, one gives rise to a joy-examination with regard to agreeable form," and so on as fully explained in the sūtra? This refers to mental-ground examinations of joy and the rest that are induced by way of the five sense-consciousness bodies; hence it is spoken of in this way — just as the contemplation of impurity is induced by way of eye-consciousness, even though this contemplation of impurity itself belongs exclusively to the mental ground. Moreover, that sūtra passage says "having seen form with the eye," and so on as fully explained — hence this should not be raised as an objection. Even where there is no seeing at all, joy, grief, and equanimity that arise once mere contact has occurred are likewise mental examinations. Were it otherwise, then within the desire realm there could be no mental examinations taking form belonging to the form realm as their object; and within the form realm there could be no mental examinations at all taking desire-realm smell, taste, or touch objects as their object. The phrase "having seen form," and so on, is spoken merely for the sake of clear illustration; joy, grief, and equanimity arising with regard to sound and the rest, once form and the like have already been seen, are likewise mental examinations — this is said merely to avoid confusion, since it is specifically with regard to this that the fixed correspondence between faculty and object is being established. Is it the case that form and the like can, respectively, give rise to only a single one among joy and the other two examinations? There is a view that this should be understood in terms of continuity across a mental stream, rather than strictly in terms of the object itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The name "mental examination" (意近行, manaḥ-upavicāra) is built from a small pun the translation can only partly preserve: 近 ("proximate/near") and 行 ("roaming/moving"), joined, describe the mind's repeated back-and-forth movement toward an object under the pull of joy, grief, or equanimity — which is also exactly why bodily feeling is excluded: it is "not proximate" because it doesn't depend on mind alone, and "not roaming" because it lacks the discrimination needed to move toward anything in particular.

Worth flagging honestly: the explanation for why the third dhyāna's mental pleasure faculty gets no corresponding examination is introduced twice with 傳說 ("tradition holds" / "it is transmitted that"), a phrase this text tends to use when reporting an explanation without fully committing to it as self-evidently correct — a small signal, alongside this fascicle's more explicit admissions of unresolved debate, that not every classification in this system was considered perfectly self-justifying even by its own exponents.

10. The Eighteen Examinations Across the Three Realms, and Why All Are Contaminated

Those taking the fourth dhyāna as object likewise possess these same four kinds — this is said according to the position that accepts a distinct object for each; if one holds that this ground takes only what is below it as a single undifferentiated object, then there is only a single mixed-object dharma-examination. Taking the formless realm as object, there is only one, namely the dharma-examination accompanying any of the four faculties; the fundamental grounds and the three peripheral spheres have only one, namely the dharma-examination, which takes only its own realm as object, since none of the formless realm's fundamental attainments take what is below as object, and since the three peripheral spheres do not take form as object. The meaning of "not taking what is below as object" will be discussed later. Do these mental examinations extend to the uncontaminated? Verse: the eighteen are exclusively contaminated. Prose: since no mental examination extends to the uncontaminated, it is said that all eighteen are exclusively contaminated.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Honest flag: this is the most compressed numerology the fascicle has offered so far, and at least one clause resists full independent reconstruction from the prose alone. The line "緣欲界境亦有十二，除香味四，餘八自緣" (taking desire-realm objects, likewise twelve; excluding the four for smell and taste, the remaining eight take their own realm) does not cleanly explain how "the remaining eight" and "two of these take the formless realm" relate arithmetically to the twelve just mentioned — whether the "two" are drawn from within the eight or counted separately. We have translated the clauses in the order and wording the source gives them, rather than silently smoothing them into a tidier derivation we are not confident is correct.

The larger pattern is nonetheless clear even without resolving every number: reach narrows as one ascends. Desire-bound examinations can reach upward all the way to form-realm and even formless objects; the higher dhyānas progressively lose entire categories (first grief, then joy as well, leaving only equanimity by the third and fourth dhyānas); and by the formless attainments, only a single dharma-examination remains, confined to its own realm and unable to reach below. This mirrors a structure already familiar from earlier fascicles' treatment of the realms generally — the higher one goes, the narrower and more restricted mental life becomes, even in its capacity for "examination."

11. Who Possesses How Many Mental Examinations

The treatise now asks how many of the eighteen mental examinations a given being actually possesses, depending on their realm of birth and how far they have progressed toward dispassion — a being born in the desire realm without any form-realm attainment possesses a different count than one who has already attained a form-realm wholesome mind but not yet abandoned desire-realm craving, and a being born in the form realm possesses almost none at all.

Chinese Source

誰成就幾意近行耶？謂生欲界，若未獲得色界善心，成欲一切；初二定八，三四定四；無色界一。所成上界皆不下緣，唯染污故。若已獲得色界善心未離欲貪，成欲一切；初靜慮十，餘說如前。初靜慮中唯成四喜，染不緣下香味境故。捨具成六，未至定中善心得緣香味境故。餘隨此理，如應當知。若生色界，唯成欲界一捨法近行，謂通果心俱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Who possesses how many of the mental examinations? Namely, for one born in the desire realm: if one has not yet attained a wholesome mind of the form realm, one possesses all of the desire-realm examinations, eight belonging to the first two dhyānas, four belonging to the third and fourth dhyānas, and one belonging to the formless realm — since whatever is possessed in these higher realms never takes what is below as object, being exclusively afflicted in that capacity. If one has already attained a wholesome mind of the form realm but has not yet become free of desire-realm craving, one possesses all of the desire-realm examinations, and ten belonging to the first dhyāna; the rest are as stated above. Within the first dhyāna, one possesses only the four joy-examinations, since the afflicted type does not take the lower smell and taste objects as object. The equanimity-examinations are possessed in full, as six, since a wholesome mind in the stage of access concentration is able to take smell and taste as objects. The remaining cases should be understood accordingly, by the same principle. If one is born in the form realm, one possesses only a single desire-realm equanimity dharma-examination, namely the one accompanying a mind capable of producing effects in the lower realm.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The specific counts here — eight for the first two dhyānas, four for the third and fourth, one for the formless realm — match exactly the "own-realm" figures already worked out in the previous section's realm-by-realm breakdown, which is a reassuring internal consistency check on an otherwise very compressed run of numerology: a desire-realm being without form-realm attainment can only reach as far into the higher levels as those levels' own "reaching downward" capacity already established would allow.

12. Vasubandhu's Own Dissent: The Six Constant Abodes and the Thirty-Six Master-Verses

Having laid out the Vaibhāṣika system in full, the author steps forward in his own voice — "my own reading of the sūtra's meaning differs from this" — to argue that only afflicted joy, grief, and equanimity, the kind that actively drags the mind toward its object through craving or aversion, truly counts as "mental examination"; this is why the noble ones' six constant abodes of unmoved equanimity are taught, and why joy and the rest are further doubled into thirty-six sayings of the Buddha along the axis of indulgence versus renunciation. With this, the treatise's account of feeling as a link of dependent origination is complete.

Chinese Source

有說如是諸意近行，毘婆沙師隨義而立。然我所見經義有殊。所以者何？非於此地已得離染，可緣此境起意近行，故非有漏喜憂捨三皆近行攝。唯雜染者與意相牽數行所緣是意近行。云何與意相牽數行？或愛或憎、或不擇捨。為對治彼說六恒住，謂見色已不喜不憂，心恒住捨具念正智，廣說乃至知法亦爾。非阿羅漢無有世間緣善法喜，但為遮止雜染近行故作是說。又即喜等為三十六師句，謂為耽嗜出離依別。此句差別，大師說故。耽嗜依者，謂諸染受。出離依者，謂諸善受。如是所說受，有支中應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a view that these mental examinations are established by the Vaibhāṣikas according to their own systematic reasoning. However, my own reading of the sūtra's meaning differs from this. Why is this? It is not the case that, merely because a given ground has already attained freedom from affliction with respect to a certain object, a mental examination arises taking that object — hence it is not the case that all contaminated instances of joy, grief, and equanimity are included among the mental examinations. Only when these feelings are afflicted, drawing the mind along and causing it to repeatedly move toward its object, are they mental examinations. What does it mean for a feeling to draw the mind along and cause it to repeatedly move toward an object? It is through craving, or through aversion, or through an indiscriminate equanimity. In order to counteract this, the six constant abodes are taught: namely, having seen a form, one feels neither joy nor grief, the mind constantly abiding in equanimity, endowed with mindfulness and correct discernment — and so it is fully explained, up through, likewise, cognizing a dharma. It is not that the arhat has no worldly joy at all arising with reference to wholesome dharmas — this is stated only in order to preclude afflicted examination. Moreover, joy and the rest constitute what is called the thirty-six master-verses, namely, distinguished according to whether they serve as a basis for indulgence or for renunciation. This distinction in wording comes from the Great Teacher's own statement. "Basis for indulgence" refers to afflicted feelings; "basis for renunciation" refers to wholesome feelings. The feeling thus discussed should be understood as belonging among the links.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely notable moment: after many pages spent laying out the Vaibhāṣika position in full technical detail, the author explicitly breaks from it in his own first-person voice — "然我所見經義有殊," literally "but as for the meaning of the sūtras as I see it, there is a difference." Rather than simply presenting a rival school's alternative (as in the earlier debates over ignorance's relation to "class-nature," or whether contact is separate from the threefold union), this is Vasubandhu's own critique of the very system he has just spent several sections carefully expounding — a useful reminder that the Abhidharmakośa is not a neutral encyclopedia of Vaibhāṣika doctrine but frequently an argument with it, conducted from within.

The number thirty-six checks out arithmetically as a clean doubling of the eighteen mental examinations (six objects × three feelings = eighteen; ×2 for indulgence-basis versus renunciation-basis = thirty-six), which gives some confidence that "耽嗜依" and "出離依" are being applied as a distinguishing factor across the full set of eighteen rather than something narrower. Beyond this arithmetic check, we do not know the specific sūtra source or its original wording for this "thirty-six master-verses" formula and are translating only what this passage itself states about it.

13. Closing Out the Remaining Links

Having finished the detailed treatment of ignorance, name-and-form, contact, and feeling, the treatise briefly accounts for the twelve links not separately elaborated here: consciousness and the six sense-bases, already covered in earlier fascicles, and formations, craving, and grasping, reserved for the later chapters on karma and the latent afflictions.

Chinese Source

義門無量差別，何緣不說所餘有支？頌曰：餘已說當說。論曰：所餘有支，或有已說、或有當說，故此不論。此中識支如先已說，識謂各了別，此即名意處等。其六處支如先已說，彼識依淨色，名眼等五根等。行有二支，業品當說。愛取二支，隨眠品當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The distinctions in meaning across the twelve links are limitless — why, then, does the treatise not discuss the remaining links in similar detail? Verse: the rest have already been discussed, or will be discussed later. Prose: the remaining links have either already been discussed, or will be discussed later; hence they are not treated here. Among these, the consciousness-link has already been discussed earlier — consciousness means each respective act of discernment, and this is precisely what is called the mind-sense-field and so forth. The six-sense-bases link has likewise already been discussed earlier — the pure form on which consciousness depends is called the eye and the other four faculties, and so on. The formations-link has two aspects, to be discussed in the chapter on karma. The craving- and grasping-links will be discussed in the chapter on the latent afflictions.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This short administrative section is a useful map of the whole treatise's structure: the twelve links are not all explained in one place, but distributed across whichever chapter each link's underlying dharmas most naturally belong to — formations under karma, craving and grasping under the latent afflictions. Fascicle 10's own contribution, in other words, was never meant to be a complete account of dependent origination on its own, only the portion concerned with consciousness's immediate objects and its contact with them.

14. Three Similes: Seed and Dragon, Husked Rice, Cooked Food

The twelve links, condensed, resolve into three: affliction, karma, and the matter of ripened result. The treatise reaches for a set of vivid agricultural and culinary similes to show how each functions differently in perpetuating the cycle — affliction as a self-renewing seed or an inexhaustible dragon guarding its pool, karma as rice still wrapped in its husk of affliction, and ripened result as food already cooked, which can only be consumed, never turned back into anything else.

Chinese Source

此諸緣起略立為三，謂煩惱、業、異熟果事。應寄外喻，顯別功能。頌曰：此中說煩惱，如種復如龍，如草根樹莖，及如糠裹米；業如有糠米，如草藥如花；諸異熟果事，如成熟飲食。論曰：如何此三種等相似，如從種子芽葉等生？從煩惱生煩惱業事，如龍鎮池水恒不竭。煩惱鎮業生續無窮，如草根未拔苗剪剪還生。未拔煩惱根趣滅滅還起，如從樹莖頻生枝花果。從諸煩惱數起惑業事，如糠裹米能生芽等，非獨能生。或得裹業能感餘生，非獨能感。惑如種等應如是知。如米有糠能生芽等，業有煩惱能招異熟。如諸草藥果熟為後邊，業果熟已更不招異熟。如花於果為生近因，業為近因能生異熟。業如米等，應如是知。如熟飲食但應受用，不可復轉成餘飲食。異熟果事既成熟已，不能更招餘生異熟。若諸異熟復感餘生，餘復感餘應無解脫。事如飲食，應如是知。

Ru-Yi Translation

These twelve links of dependent origination, in condensed form, can be established as three: affliction, karma, and the matter of ripened result. External similes should be adduced to reveal their distinct functions. Verse: here, affliction is spoken of as like a seed, and also like a dragon, like a grass-root or a tree-trunk, and like husk wrapping rice; karma is like husked rice, like a medicinal herb, and like a flower; the matter of ripened result is like cooked food. Prose: how do these three resemble one another, the way sprout and leaf and the rest arise from a seed? From affliction, affliction and the matter of karma are produced, just as a dragon dwelling in a pool keeps its water perpetually from drying up. Affliction dwells over karma, causing birth to continue on without end, just as a grass-root, if not uprooted, will sprout again and again no matter how many times its shoots are cut. If affliction's root is not uprooted, then even once it tends toward cessation, it arises again — just as a tree-trunk repeatedly puts forth branches, flowers, and fruit. From afflictions repeatedly arising, the matter of delusion and karma is produced — just as husk wrapping rice is able to produce a sprout and the rest, though the rice inside is not alone able to do so; or, once karma has acquired its wrapping of affliction, it is able to bring about a further rebirth, though karma alone is not able to bring this about. Affliction's likeness to a seed and the rest should be understood in this way. Just as rice, when it has its husk, is able to produce a sprout and the rest, so karma, when it has affliction, is able to invite a ripened result. Just as a medicinal herb reaches maturity as the final stage of a fruit's ripening, so, once karma's result has ripened, it does not invite any further ripened result. Just as a flower serves as the proximate cause for a fruit's production, so karma serves as the proximate cause capable of producing a ripened result. Karma's likeness to rice and the rest should be understood in this way. Just as cooked food is only meant to be consumed, and cannot be turned back into some other kind of food, so, once the matter of ripened result has already ripened, it cannot bring about a further rebirth's ripened result. If ripened results could bring about further rebirths, and those further rebirths could bring about still further ones, there would be no liberation at all. Ripened result's likeness to food should be understood in this way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three similes are carefully chosen to isolate three distinct functional roles rather than just decorate the same point three times over. The dragon and the grass-root both capture affliction's self-perpetuating character — it doesn't just cause one effect and stop, it actively maintains the conditions for its own continuation, the way a dragon keeps a pool from ever running dry, or a grass-root regenerates no matter how often its visible shoots are cut back. The husked-rice image captures something different: karma alone is inert, the way rice alone cannot sprout — it is only karma wrapped in affliction that is capable of producing a further rebirth, just as it is only rice still wrapped in its husk that is capable of germinating. And the cooked-food image captures a strict asymmetry the other two similes don't: ripened result is causally terminal in a way affliction and karma are not — a ripened result cannot itself ripen into a further ripened result, on pain of an infinite regress that would make liberation impossible. This last point is doing real doctrinal work: it is precisely because ripened results don't self-perpetuate the way affliction does that liberation — cutting affliction at its root — is actually possible at all.

15. The Four States of Existence: Which Are Always Afflicted?

This cycle of affliction, karma, and ripened result, across successive lives, never exceeds the four states of existence already introduced in fascicle 8: the intermediate, birth, fundamental, and death states. The treatise now asks which of these are necessarily afflicted and which can be wholesome, afflicted, or neutral depending on circumstance — the birth-state, tellingly, is always afflicted, since it is precisely the afflictions of one's own realm that pull rebirth-linking into being in the first place.

Chinese Source

如是緣起煩惱業事，生生相續不過四有：中、生、本、死，如前已釋。染不染義三界有無，今當略辯。頌曰：於四種有中，生有唯染污，由自地煩惱，餘三無色三。論曰：於四有中生有唯染。由何煩惱？自地諸惑。謂此地生，此地一切煩惱染污此地生有。故對法者咸作是言：諸煩惱中無一煩惱於結生位無潤功能。然諸結生唯煩惱力，非由自力現起纏垢。雖此位中心身昧劣，而由數起或近現行引發力故，煩惱現起。應知中有初續剎那亦必染污，猶如生有。然餘三有一一通三，謂本、死、中三，各善染無記。於無色界除中有三，非彼界中有處隔別，為往餘處可立中有。頌中不說欲色二界，故知於中許具四有。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, dependent origination's matters of affliction and karma, in their birth-after-birth continuation, never exceed the four states of existence: the intermediate, the birth, the fundamental, and the death states, as already explained earlier. The question of whether these are afflicted or unafflicted, and whether they exist in the three realms, should now be briefly discussed. Verse: among the four kinds of existence, the birth-state alone is invariably afflicted, through affliction belonging to its own realm; the other three, in the formless realm, are three. Prose: among the four states of existence, the birth-state alone is afflicted. Through what affliction? Through the afflictions belonging to its own realm — that is, wherever this state is born, all of that realm's afflictions defile this state's birth-existence. Hence the Ābhidharmikas all uniformly say: among all afflictions, there is not a single one that lacks the capacity to nourish the position of rebirth-linking. Yet this rebirth-linking occurs solely through the power of affliction, not through afflictions currently manifesting and arising by their own power — for even though, at this juncture, mind and body are dim and weak, affliction nevertheless becomes manifest through the power induced by its own repeated past arising, or by what is currently proximate. It should be understood that the very first moment of the intermediate-existence's continuity must likewise necessarily be afflicted, just like the birth-existence. However, each of the other three states of existence — the fundamental, the death, and the intermediate — is, individually, of all three moral qualities: wholesome, afflicted, and neutral. In the formless realm, the intermediate existence is excluded from these three, since, in that realm, there is no place set apart from another in the way that would allow for an intermediate existence traveling toward some other place to be posited. The verse does not mention the desire and form realms, and from this it is known that all four states of existence are accepted as present within them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The claim that the birth-state is invariably afflicted is doing important conceptual work: it says that rebirth-linking itself — the very moment a new existence connects to its causes — can never happen through a neutral or wholesome mental state, only through affliction's specific "moistening" power, even though the mind and body at that liminal moment are described as extremely faint and weak. This connects rebirth tightly to affliction at the most fundamental level: one is not reborn by wholesome karma alone, but only where affliction is still present to actually link the karmic seed to a new existence — which is precisely why an arhat, having exhausted all affliction, is never reborn again regardless of any residual karma.

The formless realm's exclusion of intermediate existence is a small but telling piece of this tradition's cosmology: intermediate existence is explained here as fundamentally a matter of travel toward a new place of birth, and since the formless realm has no "place" in any spatial sense at all (as already noted back in fascicle 8), the very concept of an interval spent traveling somewhere simply does not apply there — one is not reborn into the formless realm so much as one's mental continuum itself takes on that attainment's character directly.

16. What Sustains Sentient Beings? The Four Nutriments Introduced

Dependent origination fully discussed, the treatise turns to a new question: what sustains a sentient being once it exists? The answer is four nutriments — edible food, contact, volition, and consciousness — introduced here in a single dense verse previewing the entire topic to come, before the prose settles into edible food's own subdivision into "subtle" (the fragrance-food of intermediate-existence beings and of the gods at the eon's beginning) and "coarse," and its restriction to the desire realm alone.

Chinese Source

有情緣起已廣分別，是諸有情由何而住？頌曰：有情由食住，段欲體唯三，非色不能益，自根解脫故。觸思識三食，有漏通三界，意成及求生，食香中有起。前二益此世，所依及能依，後二於當有，引及起如次。論曰：經說：世尊自悟一法正覺正說，謂諸有情一切無非由食而住。何等為食？食有四種：一段、二觸、三思、四識。段有二種，謂細及麁。細謂中有食，香為食故。及天劫初食，無變穢故，如油沃砂散入支故。或細污虫嬰兒等食說名為細，翻此為麁。如是段食唯在欲界，離段食貪生上界故。唯欲界繫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sentient beings' dependent origination has now been fully discussed; by what do these sentient beings subsist? Verse: sentient beings subsist by nutriment. Edible food, in the desire realm, has only three as its essential nature; not form — it cannot benefit the faculties, and one who is liberated is exempted from it. Contact, volition, and consciousness — these three nutriments, being contaminated, extend throughout the three realms. The one called "mind-made," and "one seeking birth," feeding on fragrance, arises with the arising of the intermediate existence. The first two nutriments benefit this present existence — the support, and what is supported, respectively; the latter two, with respect to a future existence, draw it forth and cause it to arise, in that same order. Prose: a sūtra states: the World-Honored One, having himself awakened to a single dharma, correctly and fully awakened to it and correctly proclaimed it, namely, that there is not a single sentient being who does not subsist by nutriment. What are these nutriments? Nutriment is of four kinds: first, edible food; second, contact; third, volition; fourth, consciousness. Edible food is of two kinds, namely, subtle and coarse. "Subtle" refers to the food of intermediate-existence beings, since fragrance serves as their food, and also to the food of the gods at the eon's beginning, since it undergoes no impure transformation — like oil poured onto sand, which disperses and permeates every part. Or, the food of subtle, impure insects and infants may also be called "subtle"; the opposite of this is called "coarse." Edible food of this kind exists only in the desire realm, since one is reborn into the higher realms precisely by becoming free of craving for edible food; hence it is bound exclusively to the desire realm.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The opening verse previews the whole topic in extremely compressed form — including the claim that form cannot count as edible food and the five names for the intermediate-existence being ("mind-made," "one seeking birth," and so on) — well before the prose has explained either point. We have translated the verse as literally as its compression allows; the fuller prose explanation of exactly why form is excluded from edible food follows in the next section, and should be read together with this verse rather than in isolation.

17. Why Smell, Taste, and Touch Count as Food, but Not Sight

Edible food's substance is fragrance, flavor, and touch — the objects that can be taken in morsel by morsel through mouth and nose — extended even to touch-only cases like anointing or bathing that never involve actual eating. Visible form is pointedly excluded: seeing food may bring pleasure, but that pleasure is produced by the contact involved, not by the form itself, which is why an arhat or non-returner, free of craving, gains no nourishment at all from the mere sight of even the most exquisite meal.

Chinese Source

香味觸三，一切皆為段食自體，可成段別而飲噉故，謂以口鼻分分受之。光影炎涼如何成食？傳說此語從多為論。又雖非飲噉，而能持身，亦細食所攝，如塗洗等。色亦可成段別飲噉，何緣非食？此不能益自所對根，解脫者故。夫名食者，必先資益自根大種，後乃及餘。飲噉色時，於自根大尚不為益，況能及餘，由彼諸根境各別故。有時見色生喜樂者，緣色觸生，是食非色。又不還者及阿羅漢解脫食貪，雖見種種上妙飲食而無益故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fragrance, flavor, and touch — all three of these constitute edible food's own essential substance, since they can be divided into discrete morsels and eaten, being received bit by bit through the mouth and nose. How can light, shadow, warmth, and coolness constitute food? Tradition holds that this way of speaking is stated with reference to the majority of cases. Moreover, even though they are not eaten or drunk, they are nevertheless able to sustain the body, and so are likewise included among subtle food — as with anointing or bathing the body. Visible form, too, could seem to be divisible into morsels and eaten — why, then, is it not counted as food? Because it is unable to benefit the faculty it corresponds to, and because one who is liberated is exempted from it. Now, what is properly called "food" must first nourish and benefit one's own faculties and their great elements, only afterward extending to anything else. When form is eaten or drunk, it does not even benefit one's own faculty and its great elements, much less extend any benefit further, since the various faculties and their respective objects are each entirely distinct from one another. When it sometimes happens that seeing a form gives rise to joy and pleasure, this arises from the contact conditioned by that form — it is this contact that is the nutriment, not the form itself. Moreover, non-returners and arhats, being free of craving for food, gain no benefit even when they see all manner of exquisite food and drink.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This resolves the compressed verse from the previous section: "form cannot benefit, and the liberated are exempted from it" turns out to mean that liberated beings (non-returners and arhats) are the clearest proof that mere sight brings no nutritive benefit at all — since if visual pleasure in food were itself nourishing, an arhat's continued freedom from food-craving would have to somehow block real nourishment from occurring, which is absurd. The actual nutriment in "enjoying the sight of food" is relocated to contact — the same dharma already given an entire multi-section treatment earlier in this fascicle — rather than to the visual experience itself, keeping the boundary between the four nutriments and mere sensory pleasure sharp.

18. Contact, Volition, and Consciousness as Nutriment — Why All Four Are Contaminated

The remaining three nutriments are defined simply: contact is whatever arises from the threefold union already analyzed earlier in this fascicle, volition is mental karma, and consciousness is the consciousness aggregate itself. All three, like edible food, are exclusively contaminated even though they range across all three realms — because "nutriment," by definition, sustains continued existence, while the uncontaminated path exists precisely to extinguish it.

Chinese Source

觸謂三和所生諸觸，思謂意業，識謂識蘊，此三唯有漏，通三界皆有。如何食體不通無漏？毘婆沙師作如是釋：能資諸有是其食義，無漏修生為滅諸有。又契經說：食有四種，能令部多有情安住，及能資益諸求生者。無漏不然，故非食體。言部多者，顯已生義，諸趣生已皆謂已生。復說求生為何所目？此目中有，由佛世尊以五種名說中有故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contact refers to whatever kinds of contact are produced from the threefold union; volition refers to mental karma; consciousness refers to the consciousness aggregate. These three are exclusively contaminated, yet they are present throughout all three realms. How is it that the essential nature of nutriment does not extend to the uncontaminated? The Vaibhāṣikas explain it thus: to be capable of sustaining continued existence is precisely what it means to be nutriment, whereas the arising of uncontaminated cultivation is for the sake of extinguishing all forms of existence. Moreover, a sūtra states: nutriment is of four kinds, capable of causing already-born sentient beings to abide, and capable of sustaining those who seek birth. The uncontaminated is not like this; hence it does not belong to nutriment's essential nature. The term "already-born" indicates the sense of having-already-arisen: once born into any destiny, one is universally said to be "already-born." What, then, does "one seeking birth" refer to? This refers to the intermediate-existence being, since the Buddha, the World-Honored One, described the intermediate existence by five names.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Vaibhāṣika rationale here is elegantly circular in a productive way: nutriment is defined functionally, by what it does (sustain existence), rather than structurally, by what it is made of — which is exactly why the uncontaminated path, whose entire function runs in the opposite direction, is excluded by definition regardless of whether it might otherwise resemble contact, volition, or consciousness in other respects.

19. Five Names for the Intermediate-Existence Being

The Buddha is said to have described the intermediate-existence being — already defended at length back in fascicle 8 — by five different names, each capturing a different facet of this brief, in-between form of life: how it comes into being, what it constantly seeks, what sustains it, where it stands between two destinies, and how briefly it lasts.

Chinese Source

何等為五？一者意成，從意生故，非精血等所有外緣合所成故。二者求生，常喜尋察當生處故。三者食香，身資香食往生處故。四者中有，二趣中間所有蘊故。五者名起，對向當生暫時起故。如契經說：有壞自體起，有壞世間生。起謂中有。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are these five? First, "mind-made," since it is produced from mind, not brought about through the union of external conditions such as sperm and blood and the like. Second, "one seeking birth," since it constantly delights in seeking out its coming place of birth. Third, "one who feeds on fragrance," since its body is sustained by fragrance-food as it travels to its place of birth. Fourth, "intermediate existence," since it consists of the aggregates present between two destinies. Fifth, "arising," since it arises only briefly, oriented toward the birth it is about to undergo. As a sūtra states: "there is the arising of a decaying own-body, and there is the birth of a decaying world" — "arising" here refers to the intermediate existence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Each of the five names isolates a different moment or aspect of the same brief interval: "mind-made" addresses its manner of origination (no physical parents required), "one seeking birth" and "one who feeds on fragrance" address its ongoing activity and sustenance while it lasts, "intermediate existence" addresses its structural position between two lives, and "arising" addresses its sheer brevity. Taken together, the five names read less like five separate technical terms than like five different angles on a single, hard-to-pin-down mode of being — fitting, perhaps, for a form of existence this tradition itself describes as fleeting and transitional by its very nature.

20. Four Alternatives: Who Has Cut Off "Arising" but Not "Birth"?

A further sūtra distinguishes those who have cut off the fetter of "arising" from those who have cut off the fetter of "birth," and the treatise works through all four possible combinations — non-returners streaming upward to a higher rebirth, those who attain nirvana in the interval itself, arhats, and everyone else — before settling the terminology: "already-born" refers specifically to arhats, while anyone with craving still remaining is a "seeker of birth."

Chinese Source

又經說：有補特伽羅已斷起結、未斷生結。於此經中廣說四句：離二界貪諸上流者為第一句，中般涅槃為第二句，諸阿羅漢為第三句，除前諸相為第四句。又部多者謂阿羅漢，餘有愛者說名求生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, a sūtra states: there is a kind of person who has cut off the fetter of "arising" but has not yet cut off the fetter of "birth." In this sūtra, four alternatives are explained in full: those who have become free of craving for both the form and formless realms and stream upward to a higher rebirth constitute the first alternative; those who attain nirvana in the interval constitute the second alternative; arhats constitute the third alternative; and whatever remains, excluding the aforementioned characteristics, constitutes the fourth alternative. Moreover, "already-born" refers to arhats, while those who still have craving remaining are called "seekers of birth."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Honest flag: the sūtra's opening question names one specific combination — cut off "arising" but not "birth" — and the prose then lists all four alternatives of the full fourfold scheme, but does not explicitly state which of the four alternatives corresponds to that opening combination, nor spell out the 起/生 status of each of the other three. We have translated the four alternatives in the order given, without supplying our own assignment of which is which beyond what the text states outright — namely, that arhats occupy the third position, having presumably cut off both fetters, which is also consistent with the closing identification of "already-born" specifically with arhats.

21. All Four Nutriments Sustain Both the Already-Born and the Seeker of Birth

Having distinguished "already-born" beings from "seekers of birth," the treatise asks how many of the four nutriments actually sustain each — and the Vaibhāṣika answer is simply all four, for both. Craving beings are kept going even by edible food, since the Buddha named all four nutriments as conditions perpetuating disease, affliction, aging, and death alike; and volition-food alone, remarkably, is observed to be capable of sustaining a present body all on its own.

Chinese Source

幾食能令部多安住？幾食資益求生有情？毘婆沙師說皆具四。諸有愛者亦由段食為緣資益令招後有，以世尊說四食皆為病癰箭根老死緣故。亦見思食安住現身。

Ru-Yi Translation

How many nutriments cause already-born beings to abide? How many nutriments sustain those who seek birth? The Vaibhāṣikas say both are fully endowed with all four. Those who still have craving are also sustained by edible food as a condition, which brings about a further existence, since the World-Honored One said all four nutriments serve as conditions for disease, boils, the arrow of affliction, aging, and death. It is also observed that volition-food alone is capable of sustaining the present body.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing observation — that volition-food alone can sustain a present body — is a quiet setup for the vivid similes that follow in the next section: it is precisely because sheer mental intention or hope can keep a body alive, independent of any actual edible food, that stories of starving children comforted by a bag of ash, or shipwrecked sailors mistaking foam for land, count as genuine illustrations of a real Abhidharma category rather than mere anecdote.

22. Three Similes for Volition's Power to Sustain Life

Three traditional stories illustrate volition-food's strange power: a father's bag of ash mistaken for flour, shipwrecked sailors mistaking sea-foam for shore, and a sea turtle's eggs said to be kept from spoiling by their mother's continued thought of them. The treatise accepts the first two at face value but firmly corrects the third — it cannot literally be another being's thought that sustains you, only your own.

Chinese Source

世傳有言：昔有一父，時遭飢饉，欲造他方。自既飢羸，二子嬰稚，意欲攜去力所不任，以囊盛灰挂於壁上，慰喻二子云是麨囊。二子希望多時延命，後有人至取囊為開，子見是灰望絕便死。又於大海有諸商人，遭難敗船飲食俱失，遙瞻積沫疑為海岸，意望速至命得延時，至觸知非望絕便死。《集異門足》說：大海中有大眾生，登岸生卵埋於砂內還入海中，母若常思卵便不壞，如其失念卵即敗亡。此不應然，違食義故。豈他思食能持自身？理實應言卵常思母得不爛壞，忘則命終。起念母思在於觸位，諸有漏法皆滋長有。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is a traditionally transmitted account: once, there was a father who, encountering a time of famine, wished to travel to another region. Being himself famished and weak, and having two small infant children whom he wished to bring along but lacked the strength to carry, he filled a bag with ash and hung it on the wall, comforting his two children by telling them it was a bag of roasted flour. The two children, hoping for this food, thereby prolonged their lives for a long while; later, someone came, took down the bag, and opened it — the children, seeing it was only ash, lost all hope and immediately died. Again, there were merchants at sea who, meeting with disaster, lost their ship along with all their food and drink; gazing into the distance at a mass of foam, they mistook it for the shore, and, hoping to reach it swiftly, thereby prolonged their lives for a time — but once they came close enough to touch it and realized it was not what they had hoped, they lost all hope and immediately died. The Saṃgītiparyāya states: in the great ocean there is a great creature that comes ashore to lay its eggs, buries them in the sand, and returns to the sea; if the mother continually thinks of her eggs, they do not spoil, but if she loses this thought, the eggs immediately decay and perish. This should not be taken literally, since it would violate the very meaning of "nutriment" — how could another being's volition sustain one's own body? The correct principle should instead state that it is the eggs' own continual thought of their mother that keeps them from rotting, and that when this thought is forgotten, they perish. The arising of this thought of the mother occurs at the stage of contact, and it is precisely all contaminated dharmas of this kind that nourish and extend existence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The father-and-ash and shipwrecked-sailors stories both work the same way: hope itself — mental volition oriented toward an anticipated future — visibly extends life, and the abrupt collapse of that hope upon contact with reality is what actually kills, not any change in physical circumstance (the children had no food before or after; the sailors were equally shipwrecked before and after touching the foam). This is precisely why they count as genuine illustrations of "volition-food" as a real, distinct nutriment rather than as pure literary flourish.

The correction to the turtle-egg story is a small but telling moment of doctrinal discipline: a scriptural simile that, taken literally, would violate this tradition's basic principle that one being's mental states cannot directly nourish another being's body is not simply accepted on citation-authority — it is reinterpreted so that the causal work is done by the egg's own thought of its mother, occurring at the egg's own moment of contact with that memory, rather than by the mother's thought reaching across space into the egg. The treatise treats internal doctrinal consistency as a real constraint even on how a canonical text's own similes should be read.

23. Why Only Four? The Nursing Mother and the Birth Mother

If every contaminated dharma technically nourishes existence, why did the Buddha single out exactly four as "nutriment"? The treatise answers by distinguishing two functions: edible food and contact sustain what has already come into being, the way a nursing mother feeds a child already born, while volition and consciousness draw forth and give rise to a future existence, the way a birth mother brings a new life into being in the first place.

Chinese Source

如何世尊說食唯四？雖爾就勝說四無失。謂初二食能益此身所依能依，後之二食能引當有能起當有。言所依者，謂有根身，段食於彼能為資益。言能依者，謂心心所，觸食於彼能為資益。如是二食於已生有資益功能最為殊勝。言當有者，謂未來生，於彼當生思食能引。思食引已，從業所熏識種子力後有得起。如是二食於未生有引起功能最為殊勝。故雖有漏皆滋長有，而就勝能唯說四食。前二如養母，養已生故；後二如生母，生未生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If this is so, then how is it that the World-Honored One spoke of only four nutriments? Even so, speaking in terms of what is most excellent, stating four involves no error. That is: the first two nutriments are able to benefit this body's support and what is supported by it, while the latter two are able to draw forth a future existence and cause it to arise. By "support" is meant the physical body endowed with faculties; edible food is able to nourish this. By "what is supported" is meant mind and mental factors; contact-food is able to nourish these. These two nutriments, with respect to an existence already brought into being, have the most excellent nourishing function. By "future existence" is meant a birth yet to come; with respect to that coming birth, volition-food is able to draw it forth. Once volition has drawn it forth, the power of the seed of consciousness, perfumed by karma, is able to bring about the subsequent existence. These two nutriments, with respect to an existence not yet brought into being, have the most excellent function of drawing forth and giving rise. Hence, although all contaminated dharmas nourish existence, in terms of their most excellent function, only four are spoken of as nutriment. The first two are like a nursing mother, since they nourish what has already been born; the latter two are like a birth mother, since they give birth to what has not yet been born.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nursing-mother/birth-mother distinction is a clean way of resolving what could otherwise look like an arbitrary shortlist: the Buddha isn't claiming these four are the only contaminated dharmas that ever nourish existence in any sense (the treatise has just conceded that all of them do, in the turtle-egg correction), only that these four each excel at one of exactly two functions — sustaining an existence already underway, or drawing a new one into being — with edible food and contact assigned to the body and mind of an existing life, and volition and consciousness assigned to seeding the next one via karma's perfuming of consciousness's seed. This is a recognizable structure from the treatise's broader method: naming a canonical shortlist not because everything else is excluded in principle, but because the list captures what is functionally distinctive or "most excellent" among a wider field.

24. Is Everything Edible Actually Nutriment? A Fourfold Analysis

Not everything that can be eaten counts as nutriment in the technical sense — food that harms rather than benefits the faculties falls outside the category entirely. The treatise works through a fourfold analysis to separate genuine nutriment from mere edible substance, extends the same analysis to contact, and settles a subtler question: the Vaibhāṣikas hold that food performs its nutritive function at two distinct moments, both of which count as "being nutriment."

Chinese Source

諸所有段皆是食耶？有段非食，應作四句。第一句者，謂所飲噉為緣損壞諸根大種。第二句者，謂餘三食。第三句者，謂所飲噉為緣資益諸根大種。第四句者，除前諸相。如是觸等隨其所應，一一當知皆有四句。頗有觸等為緣，資益諸根大種而非食耶？有，謂異地無漏觸等。諸有食已捐食者身亦名為食，初資益故。毘婆沙說：食於二時能為食事，俱得名食：一、初食時能除飢渴；二、消化已資根及大。

Ru-Yi Translation

Is everything that is edible-substance necessarily nutriment? There is edible-substance that is not nutriment — a four-cornered analysis should be made. The first alternative: that which, as a condition of being eaten or drunk, damages the faculties and the great elements. The second alternative: the remaining three nutriments. The third alternative: that which, as a condition of being eaten or drunk, nourishes and benefits the faculties and the great elements. The fourth alternative: excluding the aforementioned characteristics. In the same way, contact and the rest, each as appropriate, should likewise be understood to each admit of a fourfold analysis. Could there be a case of contact and the rest serving as a condition that nourishes and benefits the faculties and great elements, and yet is not nutriment? There is: namely, uncontaminated contact and the like belonging to a different ground. Even the body of one who has already eaten and then abstains from food is still called "nutriment," since it continues to provide initial benefit. The Vaibhāṣikas state: food performs the function of nutriment at two distinct times, and is called nutriment in both cases — first, at the moment of eating, when it removes hunger and thirst; second, once it has been digested, when it nourishes the faculties and the great elements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fourfold analysis here does real definitional work: by carving out a category of "edible-substance that harms rather than benefits" (the first alternative) as distinct from genuine nutriment (the third), the treatise is quietly setting up the resolution to the much harder question that follows — how hell-beings' molten iron and boiling copper, which plainly cause harm, could possibly still count as their edible food.

25. All Five Destinies Have All Four Nutriments — Even Hell's Molten Iron

Every destiny and every kind of birth possesses all four nutriments — but this raises an immediate problem: how can hell possibly have "edible food," when what its beings consume is molten iron balls and boiling copper, substances that plainly harm rather than benefit? The treatise resolves the apparent contradiction with the *Prakaraṇapāda*'s own definition, and grants molten iron a strange, limited standing as food after all.

Chinese Source

何趣何生各具幾食？五趣四生皆具四食。如何地獄有段食耶？鐵丸洋銅豈非段食？若能為害亦是食者，則與前說四句相違。又《品類足》言：云何為段食？謂能資益諸根大種，廣說乃至識食亦爾。彼說且依能資益者說名為食，故不相違。然地獄中熱鐵丸等，雖於食已能為損害，而能暫時解除飢渴，得食相故亦名為食。又孤地獄段食如人。故五趣中皆有四食。

Ru-Yi Translation

How many nutriments does each destiny and each kind of birth possess? All five destinies and four kinds of birth possess all four nutriments. But how can hell possess edible food at all? Are not molten iron balls and boiling copper themselves edible food? If whatever is capable of causing harm could also be nutriment, this would contradict the fourfold analysis stated earlier. The Prakaraṇapāda, moreover, states: what is edible food? It is that which is capable of nourishing and benefiting the faculties and the great elements — and so on in full detail, up through consciousness-food as well. That statement speaks only with reference to what is capable of nourishing and benefiting, when it calls something "nutriment"; hence there is no actual contradiction. However, the molten iron balls and the like found in hell, even though they cause harm once eaten, are nevertheless able to temporarily relieve hunger and thirst, and so, having acquired the characteristic mark of food in this respect, they too are called nutriment. Moreover, the edible food of the solitary hells is like that of human beings. Hence all five destinies possess all four nutriments.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The resolution is characteristically precise rather than hand-waving: molten iron doesn't qualify as nutriment by the *Prakaraṇapāda*'s strict definition (which requires actually benefiting the faculties), yet it still earns the label "food" through a second, looser criterion smuggled in from the earlier two-moment analysis — it briefly removes hunger and thirst at the moment of eating, even though it then goes on to harm rather than nourish. This is the same two-moment structure already introduced for ordinary food (removing hunger versus nourishing the faculties), now doing the extra work of covering a genuinely paradoxical edge case: food that satisfies the first moment's criterion while catastrophically failing the second.
