Do Past and Future Really Exist?

Fascicle 20 completes the second part of Chapter 5 (分別隨眠品第五之二, the Anuśaya-nirdeśa). It opens with a technical question about which latent tendencies "bind" a given matter across past, present, and future — a question that immediately opens onto one of the single most consequential debates in the entire treatise, and indeed in the history of this school of thought: do dharmas belonging to the past and future actually, truly exist, or does only the present genuinely exist? This is the very doctrine that gives the Sarvāstivāda ("the teaching that all exists") school its name, and this fascicle presents the school's full scriptural and logical case for it alongside the Sautrāntika's sustained rebuttal, before closing with further technical apparatus — a sixteen-fold classification of dharmas and consciousness, the typical sequence in which the anuśayas arise, and the sūtras' four collective names for affliction: outflow, flood, yoke, and clinging.

1. Which Latent Tendencies "Bind" a Matter Across the Three Times

The treatise opens this second part of Chapter 5 by explaining that whatever sentient beings undergo latent increase with respect to a given matter, that matter is said to be "bound" by the corresponding anuśaya. It then asks precisely which anuśayas — considered as past, present, or future — are capable of binding which matters. The anuśayas divide into two broad types: those with a "self-characteristic" (greed, hatred, and conceit, each possessing its own distinct, individually recognizable character) and those with a "common-characteristic" (views, doubt, and ignorance, which operate more generally). Although any given matter may be bound by many different anuśayas, the treatise here addresses specifically whichever have not yet been abandoned, tracing their operation forward according to this classification. If, with respect to a given matter, greed, hatred, or conceit has already arisen in the past and remains unabandoned, or has already arisen in the present, this is sufficient to bind that matter, since these three self-characteristic afflictions do not necessarily arise uniformly in every sentient being with respect to every possible object. If a future mental-consciousness-associated instance of greed, hatred, or conceit remains unabandoned, it pervasively binds across all three times; a future instance associated specifically with one of the five sense-consciousnesses, if unabandoned and capable of arising, binds only the future; if unabandoned but incapable of arising, it nevertheless binds all three times as well. All the remaining anuśayas — views, doubt, and ignorance — being common-characteristic afflictions that pervasively bind every sentient being without exception, bind all three times pervasively whenever they remain unabandoned, whether past or future. And whenever a present affliction is actively, directly engaging its object, it correspondingly binds that specific matter as appropriate.

We should flag that this opening section's technical apparatus (self-characteristic versus common-characteristic afflictions; which "binds" across which of the three times depending on whether it is capable of arising) is genuinely intricate, and we have aimed for precision over readability in a few of the more compressed clauses — particularly the distinction between a future sense-consciousness-associated affliction that "can arise" (binding only the future) versus one that "cannot arise" (binding all three times), which the source states as a brute technical rule without further explanation of the underlying rationale at this point.

This section is really a springboard: the treatise's own next move is to ask what it even means to speak of a "past" or "future" anuśaya "binding" something at all, if past and future dharmas do not actually, truly exist — a question that opens directly onto the school's own defining metaphysical doctrine, which the next section takes up at length.

2. Do Past and Future Really Exist? The Case For

Having spoken of past and future anuśayas "binding" a given matter, the treatise pauses to ask whether such talk presupposes that past and future dharmas actually, truly exist — or whether, since all conditioned formations are constantly arising and ceasing, one should say they are real only for the fleeting present, with no genuine existence otherwise. If they do not actually exist at all, how could one meaningfully speak of anything as being "bound" or "freed from binding" with respect to them in the first place? The Vaibhāṣika masters answer decisively that all three times — past, present, and future — genuinely, actually exist. Their case rests on two pillars: scriptural authority and logical necessity. On the scriptural side, they cite the Buddha's own words: monks, you should know that if past form did not actually exist, the learned noble disciple could not properly practice weariness and renunciation with respect to past form — yet since past form does genuinely exist, the learned noble disciple can indeed practice weariness and renunciation toward it. Likewise, if future form did not actually exist, the learned noble disciple could not properly practice cutting off eager anticipation with respect to future form — yet since future form does genuinely exist, the learned noble disciple can indeed practice cutting off such anticipation toward it. On the logical side, they argue that consciousness can only ever arise given two necessary supporting conditions, as the sūtras themselves state: "consciousness arises from two conditions" — for instance, the eye and visible form, extending through the mind and mental objects. If past and future dharmas did not actually exist, then any consciousness that takes such a dharma as its object would necessarily be missing one of its two required supporting conditions altogether. Having thus established the reality of past and future first from scriptural authority, the Vaibhāṣika further establish it through direct reasoning: consciousness, whenever it arises, necessarily has a real object — this being an absolutely fixed principle, since consciousness only arises given a genuinely existing object, and never arises in the complete absence of one. If the object-realm of past and future had no actual substantial existence at all, this would mean that consciousness could arise entirely without any object whatsoever — yet since the object simply does not exist in such a case, the consciousness that would supposedly take it as object could not exist either. It is on the strength of this scriptural and logical argument taken together that the Vaibhāṣika masters firmly establish that both the past and future genuinely, actually exist.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should highlight that this section presents this school's own foundational, self-defining argument — the very reasoning behind why this tradition calls itself "Sarvāstivāda" ("the teaching that all [dharmas of all three times] exist"), as the verse itself states explicitly. We are translating this as the Vaibhāṣika's own case, presented with full conviction in the source text; the treatise's author, Vasubandhu, is traditionally understood to have deep Sautrāntika sympathies and will, in the sections that follow, subject this very same doctrine to sustained critical examination — so readers should understand this section as presenting one side of what becomes a genuine, extended philosophical debate within the treatise itself, not the treatise's own final, settled position.

The scriptural citation here (the Buddha's teaching on renunciation toward past form and non-anticipation toward future form) is being read by the Vaibhāṣika as implying the actual existence of what is renounced or not anticipated — a substantive interpretive move, since one could in principle read the same sūtra passage as speaking merely about mental attitudes toward past and future without requiring their literal ontological existence. We flag this as an interpretive choice the Vaibhāṣika are making, since the treatise itself will go on to probe exactly this kind of question in the debate ahead.

3. Four Masters, Four Accounts of How a Dharma Persists

Having established, at least on the Vaibhāṣika's own account, that dharmas of all three times genuinely exist, the treatise must now confront a deeper puzzle: if a dharma's essential substance truly persists identically across past, present, and future, in what sense can it meaningfully be said to change or move between these three times at all, and by what account can distinctions among them even be drawn? Four masters of this tradition offer four distinct answers. The venerable Dharmatrāta holds that dharmas differ across the three times by "class" (or "kind"): as a dharma moves through time, its class changes while its underlying substance does not — comparable to a golden vessel that is melted down and reshaped into some other golden object, where the shape changes but the gold itself remains the same substance throughout, or to milk transforming into curd, where the taste, potency, and other qualities change while the color remains unaltered; in just this way, as a dharma passes from future into present, and from present into past, only its class is relinquished and newly acquired, never its underlying substance. The venerable Ghoṣaka holds instead that dharmas differ by "characteristic": as a dharma moves through time, it is always genuinely joined with all three temporal characteristics simultaneously, yet is only ever named according to whichever characteristic is currently most prominent, without this implying it has ever actually been free of the other two — comparable to a man passionately attached to one particular woman, who is not thereby said to be entirely free of attachment toward his other female companions, merely because his passion for them is not, at that moment, foremost. The venerable Vasumitra holds that dharmas differ by "state" (or "position"): as a dharma moves through the three times, it undergoes different characterizations depending on the specific state or position it currently occupies, its underlying substance again remaining constant throughout — comparable to a single counting-token that, depending on where it is placed on a counting-board, may be called "one" in the ones-place, "a hundred" in the hundreds-place, or "a thousand" in the thousands-place, its own substance as a mere token never actually changing. And the venerable Buddhadeva holds that dharmas differ by mutual "relativity": as a dharma moves through the three times, it receives different designations purely through its relative position with respect to what comes before and after it — comparable to a single woman who may be called "mother" relative to her own child, yet "daughter" relative to her own mother, the change of designation reflecting only a shift in relative standpoint, not any change in the woman herself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that these four analogies (a reshaped gold vessel, a man attached to one of several wives, a counting-token's positional value, a woman who is both mother and daughter) are this tradition's own classical illustrations, cited across many subsequent Abhidharma discussions of this same debate — we have rendered them plainly and literally, since the philosophical force of each master's position depends directly on how well or poorly its chosen analogy actually models the claim being made about time and substance.

Readers should note that all four of these masters are working within the same overarching Sarvāstivāda commitment (that past, present, and future dharmas are all equally, actually real) — the disagreement among them concerns only how to explain the change of temporal designation without abandoning that shared commitment to a persisting, unchanging underlying substance. The treatise's very next move, as our closing placeholder above indicates, is to evaluate these four accounts critically and favor Vasumitra's "state" theory as the strongest of the four — though, as we will see, even that preferred account does not entirely escape further critical scrutiny later in this fascicle.

4. Three Theories Rejected, One Endorsed — Then Immediately Questioned

The treatise now evaluates the four accounts just presented. Dharmatrāta's "class" theory is rejected outright, since it holds that a dharma's own substance genuinely undergoes transformation while merely changing class — a position which, the treatise notes pointedly, effectively aligns him with the rival, non-Buddhist Sāṃkhya school's own doctrine of a single underlying substance transforming into different manifest states, hardly a flattering company for a position within this tradition to keep. Ghoṣaka's "characteristic" theory fares no better: since it holds that a dharma is simultaneously joined with all three temporal characteristics at once, merely differing in which is currently "prominent," the three times become hopelessly entangled with one another, robbing the distinction of any real content — and the analogy Ghoṣaka himself offers actually undermines his own position, since a man passionately attached to one wife, at the very moment of that passion, has no attachment whatsoever actively arising toward his other companions; if his case is supposed to illustrate how a dharma can be joined with all three characteristics "equally" while only one is prominent, the analogy simply fails to establish the claimed equality. Buddhadeva's "relativity" theory fares worst of all: since it defines each time purely by its relation to what precedes and follows, a single dharma-moment would end up having to be simultaneously past, present, and future all at once, depending on which neighboring moment one happened to compare it to — the earlier instant within any given time would have to be called "future" relative to what follows it, the middle instant "present," and so the very same problem of triple-designation recurs endlessly at every level. It is for these reasons that, among the four, Vasumitra's "state" (or activity-based) account is judged the best, since it explains the distinction of the three times specifically in terms of differences in a dharma's activity: a dharma not yet possessing its characteristic activity is called "future"; a dharma currently possessing that activity is called "present"; and a dharma whose activity has already ceased is called "past" — its underlying substance, in every case, remaining unchanged throughout. Yet the treatise does not let even this preferred account rest unexamined for long: if the substance of past and future dharmas genuinely, truly exists just as the present does, why should they not simply be called "present" as well, rather than "past" or "future"? Is this distinction not, after all, supposed to be drawn strictly in terms of activity, as just explained? But this immediately raises a further difficulty: consider a currently existing eye-faculty that happens to belong to the same general class as visual cognition, yet is not, at this very moment, actually engaged in producing any visual cognition at all — what "activity" does such a faculty actually possess right now? Surely it is still capable, in some sense, of receiving a result from its own prior causes and of giving rise to a future result in turn — and if this is granted, then even a genuinely past cause belonging to the same class, which likewise remains capable of eventually giving rise to a future result, would have to be said to possess some kind of "activity" as well, even though it is classified as past. This threatens to leave every dharma with only ever a kind of partial, incomplete "half-activity," blurring the very distinction between the three times that this account was meant to establish clearly.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that this section marks a genuine turning point in the treatise's own argumentative structure: having presented and ranked four internal Sarvāstivādin positions, it immediately begins undermining even its own preferred choice (Vasumitra's account), previewing an extended critical demolition to come. This reflects Vasubandhu's well-documented Sautrāntika sympathies — even while writing within and largely following the Vaibhāṣika/Sarvāstivādin framework throughout most of this treatise, he uses moments like this one to expose what he takes to be the deep incoherence of the "all exists across three times" doctrine specifically.

The "half-activity" problem introduced here — that a currently existing but not-currently-producing eye-faculty already seems to possess some form of ongoing causal capacity, blurring the line between "has activity" (present) and "lacks activity" (future/past) — is the specific wedge the treatise will use to pry open a much larger set of difficulties in the sections that follow. We flag this now so readers can track how this initially narrow technical objection develops into a much broader challenge to the entire coherence of positing eternally existing past and future substances.

5. If Substance Is Eternal, What Ever Stops Its Activity?

The treatise now presses its critique of Vasumitra's "activity" account much further with a dense, pointed verse. If a dharma's own substance genuinely, permanently exists at all times, then it ought, in principle, to be capable of giving rise to its characteristic activity at all times as well — so what possible obstructing force could cause that very same, permanently existing substance to sometimes produce its activity and sometimes not? If one answers that the relevant supporting causes and conditions simply have not yet come together, this response does not actually help, since the position under examination already grants that the dharma's substance is eternally, constantly present regardless of any such external conditions. Moreover, can this "activity" itself coherently be said to belong to the past, present, or future? If it can, does this then require positing some further, separate "activity" to account for that very activity's own arising and ceasing — threatening an infinite regress? If, instead, this activity is said to be entirely outside all three times yet nevertheless still spoken of as "existing," it would then have to be unconditioned, since anything genuinely outside past, present, and future in this way is permanent and cannot properly be said to have "already ceased" at all — yet the entire account depends on being able to say exactly this, that a dharma's activity has ceased, making it "past." If one instead insists that this activity is a genuinely distinct thing from the dharma's own substance, one might try to escape this particular difficulty that way — but the position under examination explicitly denies any such distinction between activity and substance, so this escape route is not actually available to it. If, then, activity is simply identical to the dharma's own substance, and that substance is eternally, constantly existent, its activity would have to be equally eternal and constant as well — yet then how could anything ever meaningfully be said to be "past" or "future" at all? The very account of the three times that this theory was meant to establish thus collapses under its own weight. What, precisely, fails to be established here? Consider that a conditioned dharma not yet arisen is called "future"; if already arisen but not yet ceased, it is called "present"; and if already ceased, it is called "past." The proponent of this view must then explain: if the dharma's substance genuinely, truly exists at every moment exactly as the present does, then in what sense has anything ever "not yet arisen," and in what sense has anything ever "already ceased"? What, precisely, was lacking beforehand, such that it is called "not yet arisen"? And what, precisely, becomes lacking afterward, such that it is called "already ceased"? If one answers honestly — that beforehand the dharma did not yet exist at all, hence "not yet arisen," and that afterward it no longer exists at all, hence "already ceased" — this is exactly the position the Sautrāntika themselves hold, and it directly contradicts the Vaibhāṣika's own claim that a dharma originally did not exist and now exists, or that having existed, it then reverts to non-existence; unless one is willing to accept precisely this account of coming into and passing out of existence, the very meaning of the three times fails to be established in any coherent sense whatsoever. Yet this remains in evident tension with the Vaibhāṣika's own repeated insistence that a dharma is, at every point, still joined with the defining characteristics of conditioned existence (arising, persisting, and ceasing) — leading one to say, in effect, that such formations are "not truly permanent," even while simultaneously maintaining that their substance never actually comes into or goes out of existence at all; this pairing of claims, the treatise observes, has never before been coherently articulated by anyone. On the strength of this reasoning, a further verse drives the point home: to accept that a dharma's substance is eternally existent, while nevertheless claiming its nature to be impermanent, without any actual distinction being drawn between that substance and that nature, is a claim with no rational grounding whatsoever — comparable only to the kind of arbitrary, wondrous pronouncement one might expect from a supposedly all-powerful creator deity acting on sheer, unconstrained will, rather than from careful philosophical reasoning.

6. What the Buddha Actually Meant by "Past and Future Exist"

Returning to the Vaibhāṣika's original scriptural argument — that the Buddha himself declared past and future form to genuinely exist — the treatise now offers the Sautrāntika's own reading of that same passage. The Sautrāntika do not deny that the Buddha spoke of past and future dharmas as, in some sense, "existing"; they too affirm this. But what they mean by this is quite different from the Vaibhāṣika's literal ontological claim: the past is called "existent" in the sense that it once genuinely existed, and the future is called "existent" in the sense that it is certain to come into existence, since its cause is already present — that is, "existence" here is being used specifically with reference to a dharma's causal role, either as a prior cause that has already produced its effect, or as a future effect whose cause already stands ready to produce it, not with reference to that dharma's own substance actually, presently, ontologically subsisting in the way the present genuinely does. No one, the Sautrāntika insist, actually claims that the past or future exists in exactly the same manner as the present. The Buddha's purpose in speaking this way was specifically to counter a nihilistic misunderstanding that would deny cause and effect altogether — and it is with this specific pedagogical aim in view that he speaks loosely of the past and future as "existing," a usage of the word "existence" broad enough to cover both a dharma's presently subsisting reality and its merely having-been or certain-to-be. This is entirely analogous to ordinary, everyday speech: people commonly say "there was a lamp, and now there is no lamp," or "the lamp has already gone out — I am not the one extinguishing it right now" — such statements are perfectly intelligible and true, without anyone supposing that the extinguished lamp somehow still, literally, presently exists. The Buddha's statement that "past and future exist" should be understood in exactly this same spirit; if this reading were rejected, and "existence" were instead taken as requiring genuine, literal ontological identity with the present, then the very distinction between past, present, and future — which this whole discussion was meant to establish — would itself fail to be properly grounded, since anything genuinely, literally "existing" in the full sense would, by that very fact, simply be present, leaving no coherent content left over for "past" or "future" to mean at all.

7. The Topknot Ascetic's Karma, and the Eye That Comes From Nowhere

A further scriptural challenge is raised: if the Sautrāntika reading is correct, why does the Blessed One, addressing a certain staff-and-topknot-bearing ascetic, state that karma, though already past, already ceased, and already transformed, nonetheless still "exists"? Does the ascetic himself not already accept that karma merely "once had" a nature of its own, without needing the Buddha to reaffirm this? The reply is that the Buddha's restatement here should be understood as referring, in a deliberately indirect or "secret" manner, to the specific functional capacity within the present mental continuum that is capable of eventually giving rise to a future result — a capacity carried forward from that karma, drawn out for emphasis in this particular teaching context, not a claim that the past karma's own substance is literally, presently existing in its own right. If this reading were rejected, and the past karma's own substance were instead held to genuinely, presently exist in full, then in what coherent sense could it still be called "past" at all? The very attempt to defend literal past-existence in this passage would undermine the very distinction the term "past" is supposed to preserve. The treatise then offers a further, independent scriptural argument against literal three-times realism, citing the Buddha's own words in the Sūtra on Supreme Emptiness: the Blessed One states that when the eye faculty arises, it does not come from anywhere, and when the eye faculty ceases, it does not go anywhere to be stored or gathered — it is, in the Buddha's own words, "originally non-existent, and now it exists; having existed, it then reverts to non-existence." If the past and future eye-faculty genuinely, substantially existed as the Vaibhāṣika maintain, this sūtra could not coherently speak in these terms at all, since something with an eternally persisting substance could never truly be described as "originally non-existent" or as ultimately "reverting to non-existence." An objection follows: perhaps this sūtra passage is speaking specifically and only about the present eye-faculty, leaving the question of the past and future eye-faculty's own separate, eternal substance untouched. The treatise rejects this defense as unreasonable, since the very nature ascribed to "the present" here is not, in fact, distinct in substance from the eye-faculty's own nature as such — that is, if one grants that the present eye-faculty is genuinely "originally non-existent, now existent, and later reverting to non-existence," one has thereby already conceded that the eye-faculty's very own nature undergoes this same pattern, and there is no principled way to then insist that this same eye-faculty, considered as past or future, somehow escapes this pattern and instead persists as an eternally existing substance all along.

8. The Two-Conditions Dilemma, and How Memory Works Without a Persisting Past

The treatise now returns to the Vaibhāṣika's other main argument — that consciousness requires two conditions to arise, and so a past or future object must genuinely exist to serve as one of them. This claim is probed with a pointed dilemma: when mind and mental objects serve as conditions for mental consciousness to arise, does the mental object function the way the mind itself does, as a full "generating condition" actively producing that consciousness, or does it merely function as an "objective-support condition," providing something for consciousness to be about, without itself needing to generate anything? If a future dharma is meant to function as a genuine generating condition, how could something that will only exist a hundred thousand eons from now already be generating a consciousness in the present moment? And if it is not currently functioning as a generating condition at all, then a present consciousness arising right now clearly does not require any such condition from it. Moreover, since nirvāṇa's very nature is to be entirely free from all conditioned arising, it would be simply incoherent to establish nirvāṇa itself as a "generating condition" for anything — showing that not every object a consciousness takes must function as its generating cause. If, instead, a mental object is meant only to function as an objective-support condition, then the Sautrāntika readily agree: past and future dharmas can indeed serve as the objective-support for a corresponding consciousness — but this in no way requires that they presently, actually exist as substances; if they were required to have that kind of existence merely to serve in this more modest supporting role, how would this differ from claiming they must be fully "generating conditions" as well? What, then, actually constitutes serving as this kind of objective support if there is no genuinely existing substance for consciousness to grasp? The Sautrāntika answer: it is precisely a dharma's having-once-existed, or its being-certain-to-exist, that constitutes this. When one recalls a past form or feeling, one is not directly, vividly perceiving that dharma as though it were clearly, presently observed — rather, one is simply recollecting the mere characteristic of its having-once-existed; and when one anticipates a future dharma in reverse fashion, the same holds true — just as one directly experienced a form's characteristic when it was actually present, one now recollects it as having-been, or anticipates it, in reverse, as going-to-be. If a past or future object were required to exist exactly as the present does in order to be recollected or anticipated this way, it would simply have to be present, not past or future at all; but if its substance genuinely does not presently exist, then one must instead accept that consciousness can indeed take a non-existent object as its support — and this, the Sautrāntika maintain, is exactly the more defensible position to hold.

9. Scattered Atoms Rejected, and the Thirteenth Sense-Base Reductio

A fallback proposal is raised: perhaps past and future form does not exist in the same coherent, organized way the present does, but instead exists merely as dispersed, scattered atoms, in some looser, diffuse sense. The treatise rejects this decisively: when one actually recollects or anticipates such form, one is not grasping any scattered characteristic at all, but rather the very same organized, coherent characteristic the form had when it was present. Moreover, if past and future form really existed identically to the present, differing only in being "scattered" rather than "gathered," then the atoms themselves, considered as bare substances beneath any particular arrangement, would have to be eternally, permanently existing — and this would mean that form, in its most basic sense, amounts to nothing more than atoms merely gathering and scattering, with no portion of it ever genuinely counting as arising or ceasing at all, since the underlying atomic substance would never actually come into or go out of existence. Such a view, the treatise observes pointedly, would effectively align this position with the doctrine of the Ājīvika ascetics (followers of "wrong livelihood," a rival non-Buddhist tradition), abandoning the Well-Gone One's own teaching in the sūtras, which explicitly states that the eye faculty, upon arising, comes from nowhere, and, upon ceasing, is gathered nowhere — a statement that plainly rules out the idea that form is merely a case of pre-existing atoms scattering and reassembling. And it is not the case, in any event, that feeling and the other mental aggregates are themselves composed of scattered atoms to begin with, so how could one even coherently speak of a "scattered" past or future in their case? Yet recollecting and anticipating feeling and the like function in exactly the same way as with form — one recollects or anticipates the very characteristic these had, or will have, when directly experienced as present, not some diffuse or scattered version of it. If these were required to exist just as the present does, their substance would have to be eternal; but if their substance genuinely does not presently exist, then, once again, one must accept that consciousness is indeed capable of taking a non-existent object as its support — a conclusion the reasoning itself makes self-evident. Pressing further: if it were true that consciousness could never, under any circumstances, take a wholly non-existent object at all, then a thirteenth sense-base would have to be posited, alongside the twelve already recognized, specifically to account for cognitions directed at what is simply not there — yet everyone in this tradition already accepts that there is no such thirteenth sense-base. What, then, does this "non-existent-taking" consciousness actually take as its object? If one insists that this consciousness must still be said to take "that" (the merely conceived, non-existent thing) as its object, then one has, in effect, already conceded that this so-called object is properly named "non-existent." And a parallel case makes the same point vividly: if one hears a spoken word that no longer sounds, and then goes searching for that already-vanished sound, what could such a search-consciousness possibly be taking as its object, if not something that is, by that very point, no longer there at all?

10. "I Say What Is Absent Is Absent" — Correctly Understood

An objection appeals to the Bodhisattva's own well-known statement: "whatever the world says is absent, I too say is absent" — does this not establish that consciousness only ever properly takes something genuinely existing as its object, since the Bodhisattva here seems to affirm that his own perception simply tracks whatever does or does not, in fact, exist? The treatise clarifies that this statement is not making the metaphysical claim being attributed to it at all. Rather, the Bodhisattva's meaning is that certain other people, out of overweening pride and arrogance, mistakenly perceive things that are not actually there as though they were genuinely present — and it is specifically this kind of mistaken over-perception that the Bodhisattva is disavowing on his own behalf, saying, in effect, "I do not, like those arrogant people, perceive as existing what does not exist; I only regard as existing what genuinely does exist." If the statement were instead read the way the objection proposes, then every single act of cognition whatsoever would have to take a genuinely, actually existing object — yet this cannot be right, since it would leave no coherent explanation for why people sometimes fall into genuine doubt about whether a given object exists at all, or why different individuals, encountering the very same situation, sometimes reach differing judgments about a matter's existence; if every cognition automatically and infallibly tracked real existence, no such hesitation or disagreement could ever meaningfully arise in the first place. This point is further confirmed by a separate teaching the Blessed One gives elsewhere: "Come, monks! If you are able to be my disciples, free from flattery and deception, possessing faith and diligence, I will teach you in the morning such that you attain a superior realization by evening, or teach you in the evening such that you attain a superior realization by morning" — and it is precisely through such training that a disciple comes to know correctly: what exists as existing, what does not exist as not existing, what is superior as superior, and what is unsurpassed as unsurpassed. This teaching, by explicitly training disciples to distinguish existence from non-existence correctly, itself confirms that these are two genuinely different categories requiring careful discernment — which would make little sense if consciousness were simply incapable, by its very nature, of ever taking a non-existent object as its support to begin with. It follows from all of this that the Vaibhāṣika's claim — that consciousness must have a genuinely existing object, and therefore past and future must exist — does not actually succeed as a valid proof.

11. If Substance Never Changes, Karma Could Never Generate Anything

The treatise now addresses the Vaibhāṣika's final major argument for the reality of the three times: that karma must genuinely have a result, and since a result can only come from a genuinely existing cause, past karma (and its distant future result) must actually exist. This reasoning, the treatise responds, is likewise unsound. The Sautrāntika school does not claim that a past act of karma itself directly reaches across time to generate its distant future result; rather, karma, once performed, initiates a continuous mental continuum that, from that point onward, undergoes a gradual, step-by-step process of transformation and differentiation, until this continuum eventually culminates in the specific fruit that karma is said to produce — a mechanism the treatise indicates will be explained at greater length later, in its chapter dedicated to refuting the notion of a substantial self. If, instead, one insists that past and future genuinely, literally exist as the Vaibhāṣika maintain, then every result, at every point in time, would already have to exist eternally and unchangingly — in which case, what function could karma possibly still perform with respect to that already, eternally existing result? If one answers that karma still somehow "generates" it, this immediately implies that the result was originally non-existent and now newly comes to exist through that generation — which is exactly the very point the Sautrāntika have been arguing for all along, now conceded by the Vaibhāṣika's own reasoning. If, on the other hand, every dharma is held to exist, unchangingly, at every point in time without exception, then the very notion of anything "generating" anything else becomes utterly incoherent, since there would never be any prior absence for a cause to fill. Such a position, the treatise observes, would end up aligning uncomfortably with the doctrine of the rival Vārṣagaṇya school of non-Buddhist ascetics, who explicitly hold that whatever exists must always, eternally exist, and whatever does not exist must always, eternally not exist — meaning that what exists can never cease to exist, and what does not exist can never newly come into existence. If one then proposes instead that karma's actual function is merely to cause an already, eternally-existing future result to become "present" rather than to newly bring it into being at all, one must still explain how, precisely, karma manages to make that result "become present" if the result's own eternal substance never actually changes in any respect. If karma is said to relocate the result to some new position, that result's substance would then have to be capable of a kind of spatial movement, which makes no sense whatsoever for something formless, like a mental state. And if karma is said merely to bring about some qualitative distinction within that already eternally-existing result, without producing it anew, this again directly implies that the result was, in that specific distinguishing respect, originally lacking and only later comes to possess it — precisely the "originally non-existent, now existent" pattern that the entire Vaibhāṣika position has, from the very outset of this whole discussion, been struggling and failing to avoid.

12. The Verdict: A Modest "All Exists," and How Binding Still Makes Sense

The treatise now delivers its considered verdict on this entire extended debate: if this school genuinely wishes to be called "the teaching that all exists" in a manner faithful to the Buddha's own scripture, then asserting the literal, eternal existence of past and future dharmas' substance is simply not the correct way to establish this teaching. A properly scriptural version of the "all exists" doctrine should instead be stated exactly as the Buddha himself states it: "Brahmin, you should know that 'all that exists' refers only to the twelve sense-bases" — or, taking the three times into account, "all that exists" should be understood strictly as whatever presently, actually is, within whichever of the three times it happens to occur, rather than as a claim that every dharma's own substance persists identically and eternally across all three times regardless. This, the treatise maintains, is what genuine fidelity to the Buddha's own words actually requires — a considerably more modest, and more defensible, position than the elaborate metaphysics of eternally persisting substance that this fascicle has spent so long examining and, ultimately, dismantling. A further practical objection remains to be addressed: if past and future genuinely do not exist in this literal, ongoing sense, how can one still meaningfully speak of a matter being "bound" by an affliction, or of it later becoming "freed from binding," with respect to something in the past or future? The treatise answers that this poses no real difficulty: it is precisely because the specific anuśaya that would give rise to renewed affliction with respect to that matter still exists (in the causal, dispositional sense already explained) that one properly speaks of a past or future matter being "bound" by that affliction; and it is precisely because that anuśaya, once abandoned, no longer exists in this way that one speaks of the matter as having become "freed from binding." Nothing about this ordinary, everyday way of speaking about binding and liberation actually requires positing that the past or future matter itself literally, presently subsists as an eternal substance — a person's own present continuum either still harbors the relevant latent tendency or does not, and it is this fact about the present continuum, not any claim about the metaphysical status of a past or future object, that genuinely accounts for what is meant by binding and freedom from binding.

13. The Vaibhāṣika's Last Word, and Returning to the Main Inquiry

Having heard the Sautrāntika's extended case laid out at such length, the Vaibhāṣika masters offer their own final word on the matter, refusing to concede: past and future, they maintain, exist exactly as truly and fully as the present does. As for whatever difficulties in this discussion one finds oneself simply unable to resolve or explain away, those who genuinely cherish this teaching should understand that the nature of dharmas is truly profound, lying beyond the reach of ordinary discursive reasoning — is it really appropriate, they ask pointedly, to dismiss something as non-existent simply because one personally cannot work out how to fully explain it? They then offer several further clarifications of how "arising" and "ceasing" can be spoken of in different senses without requiring the Sautrāntika's account: there is a sense in which a dharma both arises and ceases as "this very thing" (as when form itself is said to arise and that same form is said to cease); there is a sense in which what arises and what ceases are considered "different" (as when what is future is said to "arise" and that same thing, now present, is said to "cease" [into the past]); there is a sense in which the very state of being in a particular time is itself called "arising" (since, at the moment of actually arising, a dharma is included within that time); and there is a sense in which a time period as a whole can be said to have "arising," simply because the future, as a stretch of time, contains many moments yet to occur. With this extended digression now concluded, the treatise returns at last to its interrupted main line of inquiry: are all matters that have already been abandoned thereby necessarily "freed from binding," and are all matters "freed from binding" thereby necessarily already abandoned? If a matter is freed from binding, it must indeed already be abandoned — yet there are matters already abandoned that are not thereby freed from binding. What are these cases of "abandoned but not yet freed from binding"? At the stage of the path of seeing, once the cognition of suffering has already arisen but the cognition of the origin of suffering has not yet arisen, the matters abandoned by seeing suffering are indeed already abandoned — yet if the pervasive anuśayas abandoned by seeing the origin of suffering, which are capable of taking those same matters as their object, have not yet been permanently eliminated, then whatever still takes them as an object remains bound to them nonetheless. Likewise, at the stage of the path of cultivation, whichever specific level of the path has currently arisen: among the nine grades of matters to be cut through, if an earlier grade has already been abandoned, but the anuśayas of the remaining, not-yet-abandoned grades are still capable of taking that same matter as their object, then that matter remains bound as well. This is how "abandoned yet not freed from binding" should be understood.

14. Sixteen Categories of Dharma, Sixteen Kinds of Consciousness

The treatise now opens an extensive new project: determining, for any given matter, exactly how many anuśayas undergo latent increase with respect to it. Rather than working through every individual case one by one, which would require an unmanageable amount of explanation, the treatise offers a streamlined "abbreviated" method for navigating this vast combinatorial space. All dharmas, though numerous, can be sorted into sixteen categories: the five categories of abandonment (by seeing each of the four truths, or by cultivation) within each of the three realms, amounting to fifteen categories, plus the uncontaminated as a sixteenth. 16. Defiled Versus Undefiled Mind, and the Sequence the Anuśayas Arise In

The treatise raises a further refinement: if a particular mind is said to "possess" a given anuśaya, does that anuśaya definitely undergo latent increase with respect to that very mind? This is not fixed. Some minds possess an anuśaya through direct association with it, yet if that associated anuśaya, though taking that mind as its object, has itself already been abandoned, it no longer undergoes latent increase there, even though the mind may still loosely be said to "possess" it in virtue of the constancy of their prior association. Working this out systematically: "mind possessing anuśaya" divides into two overall kinds, according to whether the mind itself is defiled or undefiled. A defiled mind may or may not undergo latent increase: if the anuśaya associated with it, and capable of taking it as object, has not yet been abandoned, latent increase does occur; but if that same anuśaya has already been abandoned, latent increase no longer occurs there, even though the mind continues to be described as "possessing" that anuśaya on account of their constant, ongoing association. An undefiled mind, by contrast, is far more restricted: it can only ever undergo latent increase through an anuśaya that takes it as an object from without, never through any direct internal association, since an undefiled mind, by definition, is never itself directly associated with any affliction — and even this more limited kind of latent increase requires that the specific anuśaya taking it as object has definitely not yet been permanently abandoned; this restricted case is what is meant by describing an undefiled mind as still, in this specific sense, "possessing an anuśaya." Turning next to an entirely different question, the treatise asks: among the ten fundamental anuśayas already described, in what characteristic sequence do they typically arise, one giving rise to the next? Ignorance comes first: through failing to properly understand the truths, a person does not wish to examine suffering, its origin, its cessation, or the path. From this failure to understand, doubt is drawn forth next: having heard two conflicting positions, the person wavers uncertainly over whether suffering is genuinely suffering, and so on similarly for the remaining truths. From this wavering doubt, wrong view is drawn forth: through faulty reasoning and reflection, a settled but mistaken conviction arises, categorically denying the truth of suffering and the rest. From this denial of the truths, self-view is drawn forth: taking the very aggregates that the denial concerns, the person settles into a firm conviction that these constitute an actual "self." From this self-view, extreme views are drawn forth: relying on this notion of a self, the person clings to one of the two extremes of permanence or annihilation regarding it. From these extreme views, precept-and-vow attachment is drawn forth: clinging to the self through one of these two extremes, the person comes to regard this very clinging as itself capable of achieving purification. From precept-and-vow attachment, view-attachment is drawn forth: having settled on this supposed means of purification, the person inevitably comes to regard it as the supreme position of all. From this view-attachment, greed is drawn forth next: the person develops a deep, personal affection and attachment toward their own cherished view. From this greed, conceit is drawn forth: having become so deeply attached to their own view, the person grows arrogant and elevated, looking down upon and disparaging others who hold different views. And from this conceit, hatred is finally drawn forth: having become so deeply attached to and confident in their own view, the person cannot bear it when others hold views contrary to their own, and comes to resent and despise them for it. Other teachers hold instead that this final step of hatred arises specifically at the moment of accepting or rejecting one's own view, when one cannot tolerate having it questioned. When afflictions abandoned by seeing the truths — greed and the rest — arise in this sequence, they take the practitioner's own mental continuum's view as their object. This account describes only the typical sequence of arising; afflictions arising outside this typical sequence follow no fixed order at all.

17. Three Conditions for an Affliction to Arise, and the Fourfold "Outflow"

The treatise now identifies the three specific conditions necessary for any affliction actually to arise in active, manifest form. Take, for instance, the entanglement of desire-greed about to arise: this requires, first, that the anuśaya of desire-greed has not yet been abandoned or fully understood; second, that an object suited to provoking desire-greed is currently, actually present; and third, that improper attention is being directed toward that object. It is through the combined power of these three conditions that desire-greed actually arises. These three conditions correspond, in order, to the power of cause, the power of the surrounding circumstances, and the power of one's own preparatory mental effort; the arising of every other affliction should be understood analogously, each requiring its own version of these same three supporting conditions. This account describes the general case where all three conditions are present together; in some instances, however, an affliction may arise relying merely on the power of surrounding circumstance alone, as with an arhat who has regressed from their attainment, in whom afflictions can still arise even without deliberate improper attention, given sufficiently provocative circumstances. Turning now to a related topic, the treatise notes that the sūtras refer to these same anuśayas, together with their more actively manifest entanglements, under several further collective names: "outflow" (āsrava), "flood" (ogha), "yoke" (yoga), and "clinging" (upādāna). "Outflow" comes in three kinds: the outflow of desire, the outflow of existence, and the outflow of ignorance. "Flood" comes in four kinds: the flood of desire, the flood of existence, the flood of views, and the flood of ignorance. "Yoke" likewise comes in four kinds, corresponding exactly to the four floods just named. And "clinging" comes in four kinds: the clinging of desire, the clinging of views, the clinging of precepts and vows, and the clinging to a doctrine of self.

18. The Precise Numerical Substance of Outflow, Flood, Yoke, and Clinging

The treatise now works out the precise numerical substance underlying each of these four collective categories. The "outflow of desire" comprises forty-one distinct items: all the root afflictions bound to the desire realm, excluding delusion, together with their ten associated entanglements — that is, thirty-one root afflictions plus ten entanglements. The "outflow of existence" comprises fifty-two items: all the root afflictions bound to the form and formless realms, again excluding delusion, twenty-six from each of the two higher realms. An objection is raised here: do these two higher realms not also possess the entanglements of torpor and restlessness? The Vaibhāṣika masters of Kashmir explain that, although this is technically true, these two realms' entanglements are comparatively few and lack the independent, unrestrained potency that would justify including them separately in this particular count. Why are the anuśayas of these two higher realms combined together into a single category of "existence-outflow"? Because they share the same neutral moral quality, operate through an inward-turned mode of activity, and arise specifically in dependence on the meditative absorptions — it is on account of these three shared features that they are combined into one. And why is ignorance, in particular, singled out and established as its own separate, third outflow, rather than being folded together with the other afflictions the way delusion was excluded from the first two categories? Because ignorance specifically serves as the root of all conditioned existence, a distinguishing feature significant enough to warrant its own separate category. As for "flood" and "yoke," their substance is identical to that of "outflow," except that views are additionally singled out and separated into their own distinct category within them, called the "flood of views" and "yoke of views" respectively — since views are especially sharp and penetrating in character, this quality of sharpness is what specifically qualifies something to be called a "flood" in this system's technical sense (as will be explained shortly), whereas views, precisely because of this same sharp, restless quality, do not "abide" or settle in the way the more general "outflow" category requires, and so views could not be independently established as their own category there, but can be here, within "flood" and "yoke," where the required qualifying feature is different. This yields twenty-nine items comprising the "flood of desire" (greed, hatred, and conceit, each in five varieties, plus doubt in four varieties, plus the ten entanglements); twenty-eight items comprising the "flood of existence" (greed and conceit, each in ten varieties across the two higher realms, plus doubt in eight varieties); thirty-six items comprising the "flood of views" (the twelve view-related anuśayas in each of the three realms); and fifteen items comprising the "flood of ignorance" (the five varieties of ignorance in each of the three realms). "Yoke" should be understood to match "flood" exactly in substance, item for item.

19. The Four Clingings, and the Meaning Behind "Anuśaya" Itself — Fascicle 20 Complete

The treatise now works out the precise substance of the four kinds of "clinging" (upādāna), which should be understood to match "yoke" in substance, with one modification: the desire-yoke and the self-doctrine category each additionally absorb ignorance into their count, and views are divided into two separate categories rather than remaining combined, distinguishing this scheme from the "yoke" scheme just given. Specifically, the previously described desire-yoke, now combined together with desire-realm ignorance, comprising thirty-four items in total, is called the "clinging of desire" — that is, greed, hatred, and conceit, together with ignorance, each in five varieties, plus doubt in four varieties, plus the ten entanglements. The previously described existence-yoke, combined together with the ignorance of the two higher realms, comprising thirty-eight items in total, is called the "clinging to a doctrine of self" — that is, greed, conceit, and ignorance, each in ten varieties, plus doubt in eight varieties. Within the views-yoke, excluding specifically precept-and-vow attachment, the remaining thirty items are called the "clinging of views," while the six items excluded (precept-and-vow attachment across the three realms, in its two categories) are called the "clinging of precepts and vows." Why is precept-and-vow attachment specifically singled out and separated from the other views to form its own distinct category of clinging? Because it uniquely functions as an enemy of the noble path in two directions at once, deceiving both householders and renunciants alike: householders are deceived by it into believing that self-starvation and similar ascetic practices constitute the path to heavenly rebirth, while renunciants are deceived by it into believing that abandoning pleasant, desirable objects constitutes, by itself, the genuine path to purification. And why is ignorance not similarly established as its own separate category of clinging, the way it was for outflow? Because the very function that qualifies something to be called "clinging" is its capacity to actively grasp and take hold of further states of existence, and ignorance, understood as a mere failure to correctly understand its object, lacks this active, grasping character and is not, by itself, sufficiently sharp or forceful to independently generate this kind of clinging — it can, however, still be combined together with the other afflictions and included within their respective categories of clinging, as already shown. The sūtras themselves offer their own definition, stating: what is the desire-yoke? It is, among all forms of desire, the greed for desire, the wish for desire, the fondness for desire, the love of desire, the delight in desire, the confusion of desire, the indulgence in desire, the craving for desire, the joy in desire, the hoarding of desire, the following of desire, and the attachment of desire, which entangle and press upon the mind — this is called the desire-yoke. The existence-yoke and views-yoke should be understood analogously. A further sūtra states that desire-greed itself is called "clinging" — from which it is understood that the specific desire-greed arising with respect to each of the four categories (desire, views, precepts and vows, and doctrines of self) is what constitutes the clinging named after each. Having now explained the anuśayas together with their entanglements under the names of outflow, flood, yoke, and clinging as the sūtras themselves use these terms, the treatise closes by asking what these various names — "anuśaya" itself, and each of its synonyms — actually mean at their root. When a root affliction is actively manifest, its precise character and workings are exceedingly difficult to discern, and it is for this reason that it is called "subtle." It is called productive of "twofold increase" because it is capable of intensifying with respect both to the object it takes and to the mental states it is directly associated with, in the manner already explained earlier in this fascicle. It is called "following" and "pursuing" because the possession it repeatedly generates constantly accompanies a sentient being, remaining an enduring source of trouble and affliction, arising again and again even without deliberate effort, and persisting despite whatever effort one expends trying to prevent it — hence it is also called "binding." Through this very meaning, it is what detains sentient beings for a long time within birth-and-death, or causes them to circulate endlessly through birth-and-death, from the very peak of conditioned existence all the way down to the hell of unremitting torment. Because its continuum constantly leaks out fault without end through the six "wounds" (the six sense-doors), it is called "outflow." Because it thoroughly sweeps away wholesome qualities with overwhelming force, it is called "flood." Because it binds sentient beings together into shared bondage, it is called "yoke." And because it is capable of serving as the basis for further clinging and appropriation, it is called "clinging." A more precise gloss adds: because affliction, within the domain of one's sense-objects, continuously leaks out fault without interruption, it is called "outflow" — as a sūtra illustrates, comparing this to a person rowing a boat against the current, who must exert great effort even to make a little headway, yet if that same boat is instead released to drift downstream, no effort at all is needed for it to move easily along; wholesome and defiled states of mind arising should be understood in just the same way. Building on the meaning of this sūtra, affliction that continuously leaks out fault with respect to its objects is called "outflow"; when its force becomes especially overwhelming, it is instead called "flood," since sentient beings who fall into it can only be swept helplessly along, unable to resist its surging, turbulent current. When manifestly active but not yet at this overwhelming degree of force, it is called "yoke," simply because it joins sentient beings together with all the various kinds of suffering; or it is called "yoke" because of how frequently it manifests. And it is called "clinging" because it serves as the basis upon which desire and the other categories are grasped and appropriated. With this, Fascicle 20 of the Abhidharmakośa comes to a close.
