Do Past and Future Really Exist?

Fascicle 20 continues the second part of Chapter 5 (分別隨眠品第五之二, the Anuśaya-nirdeśa), opening with a technical question about which latent tendencies "bind" a given matter across past, present, and future — a question that immediately opens onto one of the single most consequential debates in the entire treatise, and indeed in the history of this school of thought: do dharmas belonging to the past and future actually, truly exist, or does only the present genuinely exist? This is the very doctrine that gives the Sarvāstivāda ("the teaching that all exists") school its name, and this fascicle presents the school's own scriptural and logical case for it, together with several competing internal accounts of exactly how something can remain the same dharma while passing from future to present to past.

1. Which Latent Tendencies "Bind" a Matter Across the Three Times

The treatise opens this second part of Chapter 5 by explaining that whatever sentient beings undergo latent increase with respect to a given matter, that matter is said to be "bound" by the corresponding anuśaya. It then asks precisely which anuśayas — considered as past, present, or future — are capable of binding which matters. The anuśayas divide into two broad types: those with a "self-characteristic" (greed, hatred, and conceit, each possessing its own distinct, individually recognizable character) and those with a "common-characteristic" (views, doubt, and ignorance, which operate more generally). Although any given matter may be bound by many different anuśayas, the treatise here addresses specifically whichever have not yet been abandoned, tracing their operation forward according to this classification. If, with respect to a given matter, greed, hatred, or conceit has already arisen in the past and remains unabandoned, or has already arisen in the present, this is sufficient to bind that matter, since these three self-characteristic afflictions do not necessarily arise uniformly in every sentient being with respect to every possible object. If a future mental-consciousness-associated instance of greed, hatred, or conceit remains unabandoned, it pervasively binds across all three times; a future instance associated specifically with one of the five sense-consciousnesses, if unabandoned and capable of arising, binds only the future; if unabandoned but incapable of arising, it nevertheless binds all three times as well. All the remaining anuśayas — views, doubt, and ignorance — being common-characteristic afflictions that pervasively bind every sentient being without exception, bind all three times pervasively whenever they remain unabandoned, whether past or future. And whenever a present affliction is actively, directly engaging its object, it correspondingly binds that specific matter as appropriate.

Ru-Yi Translation

Chapter 5, Part 2. Sentient beings undergo latent increase with respect to a given matter — this is called "binding" that matter. It should be explained which anuśayas of the past, present, and future bind which matters. Verse: if, with respect to this matter, greed, hatred, or conceit — not yet abandoned — has already arisen in the past or present, [it binds]; a future instance associated with mental consciousness pervasively [binds]; the five [sense-consciousness-associated instances], if capable of arising, [bind] their own time; if incapable of arising, they too pervasively [bind]; the rest, past and future, pervasively [bind]; the present, when directly engaging its object, binds. Explanation: the anuśayas are, in general, of two kinds — first, those with a self-characteristic, namely greed, hatred, and conceit; second, those with a common-characteristic, namely views, doubt, and ignorance. Although a matter may have many [anuśayas bound to it], this discussion concerns what binds it, tracing forward as appropriate whatever remains unabandoned. If, with respect to this matter, greed, hatred, or conceit has already arisen in the past and remains unabandoned, or has already arisen in the present, this is able to bind this matter, since greed, hatred, and conceit are self-characteristic afflictions, not necessarily pervasively arising in every sentient being. If a future instance associated with mental consciousness — greed, hatred, or conceit — remains unabandoned, it pervasively [binds] across the three times. A future instance associated with the five [sense-consciousnesses] — greed or hatred — if unabandoned and capable of arising, binds only the future. If unabandoned but incapable of arising, it too is able to bind the three times. All the remaining views, doubt, and ignorance, past and future, if unabandoned, pervasively bind the three times, since these three are common-characteristic afflictions, pervasively binding every sentient being. When a present [affliction] is directly engaging its object, it binds this matter as appropriate.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that this opening section's technical apparatus (self-characteristic versus common-characteristic afflictions; which "binds" across which of the three times depending on whether it is capable of arising) is genuinely intricate, and we have aimed for precision over readability in a few of the more compressed clauses — particularly the distinction between a future sense-consciousness-associated affliction that "can arise" (binding only the future) versus one that "cannot arise" (binding all three times), which the source states as a brute technical rule without further explanation of the underlying rationale at this point.

This section is really a springboard: the treatise's own next move is to ask what it even means to speak of a "past" or "future" anuśaya "binding" something at all, if past and future dharmas do not actually, truly exist — a question that opens directly onto the school's own defining metaphysical doctrine, which the next section takes up at length.

2. Do Past and Future Really Exist? The Case For

Having spoken of past and future anuśayas "binding" a given matter, the treatise pauses to ask whether such talk presupposes that past and future dharmas actually, truly exist — or whether, since all conditioned formations are constantly arising and ceasing, one should say they are real only for the fleeting present, with no genuine existence otherwise. If they do not actually exist at all, how could one meaningfully speak of anything as being "bound" or "freed from binding" with respect to them in the first place? The Vaibhāṣika masters answer decisively that all three times — past, present, and future — genuinely, actually exist. Their case rests on two pillars: scriptural authority and logical necessity. On the scriptural side, they cite the Buddha's own words: monks, you should know that if past form did not actually exist, the learned noble disciple could not properly practice weariness and renunciation with respect to past form — yet since past form does genuinely exist, the learned noble disciple can indeed practice weariness and renunciation toward it. Likewise, if future form did not actually exist, the learned noble disciple could not properly practice cutting off eager anticipation with respect to future form — yet since future form does genuinely exist, the learned noble disciple can indeed practice cutting off such anticipation toward it. On the logical side, they argue that consciousness can only ever arise given two necessary supporting conditions, as the sūtras themselves state: "consciousness arises from two conditions" — for instance, the eye and visible form, extending through the mind and mental objects. If past and future dharmas did not actually exist, then any consciousness that takes such a dharma as its object would necessarily be missing one of its two required supporting conditions altogether. Having thus established the reality of past and future first from scriptural authority, the Vaibhāṣika further establish it through direct reasoning: consciousness, whenever it arises, necessarily has a real object — this being an absolutely fixed principle, since consciousness only arises given a genuinely existing object, and never arises in the complete absence of one. If the object-realm of past and future had no actual substantial existence at all, this would mean that consciousness could arise entirely without any object whatsoever — yet since the object simply does not exist in such a case, the consciousness that would supposedly take it as object could not exist either. It is on the strength of this scriptural and logical argument taken together that the Vaibhāṣika masters firmly establish that both the past and future genuinely, actually exist.

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be discussed whether these past and future matters are actually existent or not, before one can properly speak of "binding" with respect to them. If they truly exist, then all conditioned formations would exist at all times, and so should be called permanent; if they truly do not exist, how can one speak of there being binding and freedom from binding [with respect to them] at all? The Vaibhāṣika masters firmly establish that they truly exist, yet these formations are not thereby called permanent, since they are still joined with the marks of conditioned existence. To make this established position clear and evident, we should briefly set forth its tenet and reveal its reasoning. Verse: the three times exist, as stated [in scripture]; because of the two, because of an object, because of a result; because the three times are said to exist, one is granted [the name] "the teaching that all exists." Explanation: the three times truly exist. Why is this? Because the Blessed One states so in a sūtra. That is, the Blessed One states: monks, you should know that if past form did not exist, the learned noble disciples should not diligently cultivate weariness and renunciation with respect to past form. Since past form does exist, the learned noble disciples should diligently cultivate weariness and renunciation with respect to past form. If future form did not exist, the learned noble disciples should not diligently cut off eager anticipation with respect to future form. Since future form does exist, the learned noble disciples should diligently cut off eager anticipation with respect to future form. Moreover, consciousness arises only given two conditions, as a sūtra states: consciousness arises from two conditions. What are these two? Namely, the eye and form, and so on at length, through the mind and dharmas. If the past and future did not truly exist, the consciousness taking them as object would lack one of its two conditions. Having proven the existence of the past and future on the basis of the sacred teaching, we should further prove the existence of the past and future on the basis of correct reasoning, since consciousness, when it arises, necessarily has an object. That is, only given an object does consciousness arise; without one, it does not arise — this principle is fixed. If the substance of past and future objects truly did not exist, this would mean there could be a consciousness without any object at all; since the object would not exist, the consciousness too would have to not exist.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should highlight that this section presents this school's own foundational, self-defining argument — the very reasoning behind why this tradition calls itself "Sarvāstivāda" ("the teaching that all [dharmas of all three times] exist"), as the verse itself states explicitly. We are translating this as the Vaibhāṣika's own case, presented with full conviction in the source text; the treatise's author, Vasubandhu, is traditionally understood to have deep Sautrāntika sympathies and will, in the sections that follow, subject this very same doctrine to sustained critical examination — so readers should understand this section as presenting one side of what becomes a genuine, extended philosophical debate within the treatise itself, not the treatise's own final, settled position.

The scriptural citation here (the Buddha's teaching on renunciation toward past form and non-anticipation toward future form) is being read by the Vaibhāṣika as implying the actual existence of what is renounced or not anticipated — a substantive interpretive move, since one could in principle read the same sūtra passage as speaking merely about mental attitudes toward past and future without requiring their literal ontological existence. We flag this as an interpretive choice the Vaibhāṣika are making, since the treatise itself will go on to probe exactly this kind of question in the debate ahead.
