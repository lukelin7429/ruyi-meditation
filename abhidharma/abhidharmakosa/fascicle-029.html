The Four Immeasurables

Fascicle 29 continues Chapter 8 (分別定品第八之二, Distinguishing Concentration), turning from the dhyānas and formless attainments themselves to the meditative qualities cultivated on that foundation — beginning with the four immeasurables: loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity. The treatise explains why there are exactly four, what each one's specific aspiration actually is, defends the practice against the charge of being a kind of pleasant self-deception, and closes this opening stretch with a detailed, practical method for actually cultivating loving-kindness, starting from those closest to us and gradually extending outward to our enemies.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 10 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 29 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. Why Exactly Four Immeasurables, and Are They Not a Kind of Delusion?

Having finished the concentrations themselves in fascicle 28, the treatise turns to the qualities cultivated upon that foundation, beginning with the four immeasurables — and immediately faces a serious objection: since these meditations do not actually make anyone happy or free of suffering, are they not simply a wholesome-seeming delusion?

Chinese Source

如是已說所依止定，當辯依定所起功德。諸功德中先辯無量。頌曰： 無量有四種， 對治瞋等故， 慈悲無瞋性， 喜喜捨無貪。 此行相如次， 與樂及拔苦， 欣慰有情等， 緣欲界有情。 喜初二靜慮， 餘六或五十， 不能斷諸惑， 人起定成三。 論曰：無量有四，一慈、二悲、三喜、四捨。言無量者，無量有情為所緣故、引無量福故、感無量果故。此何緣故唯有四種？對治四種多行障故。何謂四障？謂諸瞋害、不欣慰、欲貪、瞋。治此如次，建立慈等。不淨與捨俱治欲貪，斯有何別？毘婆沙說：欲貪有二，一色、二婬，不淨與捨如次能治。理實不淨能治婬貪，餘親友貪捨能對治。四中初二體是無瞋，理實應言悲是不害，喜即喜受，捨即無貪，若并眷屬五蘊為體。若捨無貪性，如何能治瞋？此所治瞋，貪所引故，理實應用二法為體。此四無量行相別者，云何當令諸有情類得如是樂？如是思惟入慈等至。云何當令諸有情類離如是苦？如是思惟入悲等至。諸有情類得樂離苦豈不快哉！如是思惟入喜等至。諸有情類平等平等無有親怨。如是思惟入捨等至。此四無量不能令他實得樂等，寧非顛倒？願欲令彼得樂等故，或阿世耶無顛倒故，與勝解想相應起故。設是顛倒，復有何失？若應非善，理則不然，此與善根相應起故。若應引惡，理亦不然，由此力能治瞋等故。此緣欲界一切有情，能治緣彼瞋等障故。然契經說：修習慈等，思惟一方一切世界。此經舉器以顯器中。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having explained the concentrations that serve as support, we should now discuss the virtues cultivated upon that foundation; among these virtues, let us first discuss the immeasurables. Verse: the immeasurables number four, since they serve as antidotes to hatred and the rest; loving-kindness and compassion have non-hatred as their nature; joy and equanimity have non-greed as their nature. Their aspects, in sequence, are: bestowing happiness, removing suffering, rejoicing for beings, and regarding them equally; they take desire-realm sentient beings as their object. Joy depends on the first two dhyānas; the remaining three depend on six grounds, or five, or ten; none of them is able to sever any affliction; when a human being gives rise to any one, three are necessarily accomplished together. Explanation: the immeasurables number four — loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity. They are called "immeasurable" because they take immeasurable sentient beings as their object, draw forth immeasurable merit, and yield immeasurable results. Why are there exactly four? Because they serve as antidotes to four kinds of especially prevalent obstruction. What are these four obstructions? Malice and the wish to harm; lack of rejoicing in others' good fortune; sensual craving; and hatred. Loving-kindness and the rest are established, in sequence, as antidotes to these. Impurity-contemplation and equanimity both serve as antidotes to sensual craving — what, then, distinguishes them? The Vibhāṣā states: sensual craving is of two kinds, craving for physical form and craving for intercourse; impurity-contemplation and equanimity serve as antidotes to these, respectively, in that order. In actual principle, impurity-contemplation counters craving for intercourse specifically, while equanimity counters craving directed at friends and family. Among the four, the first two have non-hatred as their substance. In actual principle, it should rather be said that compassion has non-harming as its substance, joy is precisely the feeling of joy, and equanimity is precisely non-greed — or, if their accompanying retinue is included, the five aggregates constitute their substance. If equanimity has non-greed as its nature, how can it serve as an antidote to hatred? Because the hatred it counters here is one drawn forth by craving specifically — hence, in actual principle, both non-greed and non-hatred together should be used as its substance. As for how these four immeasurables' aspects differ from one another: "how might I bring it about that all these sentient beings attain such happiness?" — reflecting in this way is to enter loving-kindness's attainment. "How might I bring it about that all these sentient beings are freed from such suffering?" — reflecting in this way is to enter compassion's attainment. "How wonderful that these sentient beings attain happiness and are freed from suffering!" — reflecting in this way is to enter joy's attainment. "These sentient beings are all equal, with neither intimate nor estranged among them" — reflecting in this way is to enter equanimity's attainment. Since these four immeasurables are not actually capable of causing others to genuinely attain happiness and the rest, are they not then a kind of delusion? No — because one genuinely wishes and aspires for them to attain happiness and the rest, and because one's underlying intention is itself free of any distorting delusion, arising in association with a resolved, wholehearted conceptualization. Even granting this were a kind of delusion, what fault would that actually involve? If it should therefore be non-wholesome, this does not hold in principle, since it arises in association with wholesome roots. If it should therefore lead to something unwholesome, this too does not hold in principle, since this very power is capable of countering hatred and the rest. These take as their object all sentient beings of the desire realm, since they serve as antidotes to hatred and the other obstructions that take those very beings as object. Yet a sūtra states: in cultivating loving-kindness and the rest, one contemplates one direction, then the entirety of every world. This sūtra cites a single vessel-world in order to indicate all the sentient beings contained within it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "is this not a delusion?" objection and its answer as a genuinely important piece of Buddhist moral psychology, worth reading carefully: the treatise concedes, without flinching, that the four immeasurables do not literally make any being happier or less suffering in the moment they are practiced — the aspiration is not magically effective. Its defense rests entirely on the practitioner's own mental state: the wish is sincere, the underlying intention is free of self-deceiving distortion, and the practice arises joined to wholesome roots and is genuinely capable of countering hatred and its allied afflictions. Whether or not the wish changes the world, cultivating it demonstrably changes the mind cultivating it — and the treatise treats that as sufficient justification, without needing to claim more.

We should also flag the debate over each immeasurable's precise substance as left with more nuance than the verse's compressed formula ("loving-kindness and compassion are non-hatred; joy and equanimity are non-greed") initially suggests: the prose commentary immediately complicates this, proposing instead that compassion is more precisely non-harming, joy is precisely the feeling of joy, and equanimity's role as an antidote to hatred specifically (rather than merely greed) requires positing both non-greed and non-hatred jointly as its substance. We have preserved this back-and-forth refinement exactly as the text presents it, since the verse's initial formula and the commentary's more careful correction are both part of how the treatise works through the question.

2. Which Grounds the Four Immeasurables Depend On, and Why They Only Suppress, Never Sever, Affliction

The treatise takes up a technical question — across how many of the dhyāna-grounds can each immeasurable actually be cultivated? — surveying three competing views without settling on one, before explaining a more consequential point: however powerfully cultivated, the four immeasurables can only suppress affliction, never sever it at the root.

Chinese Source

第三但依初二靜慮，喜受攝故，餘定地無。所餘三種通依六地，謂四靜慮、未至、中間。或有欲令唯依五地，謂除未至，是容豫德，已離欲者方能起故。或有欲令此四無量隨其所應通依十地，謂欲四本近分中間，此意欲令定不定地根本加行皆無量攝。前雖說此能治四障，而不能令諸惑得斷，有漏根本靜慮攝故、勝解作意相應起故、遍緣一切有情境故。此加行位制伏瞋等，或此能令已斷更遠故。前說此能治四障，謂欲未至亦有慈等，似所修成根本無量。由此制伏瞋等障已，引斷道生能斷諸惑，諸惑斷已離染位中，方得根本四種無量。於此後位雖遇強緣，而非瞋等之所蔽伏。

Ru-Yi Translation

The third, joy, depends only on the first two dhyānas, since it is included within the feeling of joy, which is absent from the remaining concentration-grounds. The remaining three depend generally on six grounds — the four dhyānas, the not-yet-arrived approach-stage, and the intermediate dhyāna. Some hold they should depend on only five grounds, excluding the not-yet-arrived approach-stage, since this is a virtue that requires unhurried leisure, achievable only by one already free of desire-realm craving. Others hold that these four immeasurables should, as appropriate, depend generally on ten grounds — the desire realm, the four root dhyānas, their approach-stages, and the intermediate dhyāna — intending by this that both the fixed and not-yet-fixed grounds, in both their root and preparatory forms, should all be included among the immeasurables. Although it was stated above that these are capable of countering the four obstructions, they are nevertheless unable to bring about the actual severing of any affliction, since they are included within contaminated root dhyāna, since they arise in association with resolved conceptualization and deliberate mental application, and since they universally take all sentient beings as their object without distinction. At the preparatory stage, they merely suppress hatred and the rest; or, alternatively, they can cause afflictions already severed by other means to remain more distant. As stated above, these can counter the four obstructions — meaning that even the desire realm and the not-yet-arrived approach-stage also have a version of loving-kindness and the rest, resembling in form the cultivated root immeasurables. Once hatred and the other obstructions have been suppressed in this way, one draws forth the severing path, which is capable of actually severing the afflictions; once these afflictions are severed, in the resulting state of freedom from affliction, one then attains the genuine root four immeasurables. From this stage onward, even when one encounters powerful adverse conditions, one is no longer covered over and overwhelmed by hatred and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the three competing views on how many grounds these immeasurables depend on as presented without adjudication, in a pattern this project has seen repeatedly throughout the Abhidharmakośa: six grounds (the straightforward reading), five grounds (excluding the not-yet-arrived approach-stage on the grounds that unhurried cultivation requires already being free of desire-realm craving), or ten grounds (extending all the way down into the desire realm itself, on the theory that a desire-realm approximation of these virtues also counts). We have left all three positions exactly as the text presents them, since the passage itself does not choose among them.

We should also flag the suppress-versus-sever distinction here as connecting directly to a recurring theme across this project's fascicles on the path: the four immeasurables, however sincerely and powerfully cultivated, belong to the same broad category as ordinary contaminated concentration — associated with deliberate conceptualization rather than direct insight into the Four Truths — and so can only hold affliction at bay rather than uproot it. The treatise's proposed sequence is nevertheless constructive rather than dismissive: cultivating the immeasurables first suppresses hatred and its allies, creating the conditions under which a genuinely severing path can then be "drawn forth," after which the fully mature, root immeasurables become available — a virtue that no longer needs active suppression because the affliction it once opposed has actually been cut.

3. How to Actually Cultivate Loving-Kindness: A Step-by-Step Method

The treatise closes this opening stretch with something unusually practical for the Abhidharmakośa: an actual, graduated meditation instruction for cultivating loving-kindness, beginning with those one already loves and only gradually, deliberately extending the same aspiration to strangers and finally to one's enemies.

Chinese Source

初習業位云何修慈？謂先思惟自所受樂，或聞說佛菩薩聲聞及獨覺等所受快樂，便作是念：願諸有情一切等受如是快樂。若彼本來煩惱增盛，不能如是平等運心，應於有情分為三品，所謂親友、處中、怨讐。親復分三，謂上、中、下；中品唯一；怨亦分三，謂下、中、上，總成七品。分品別已，先於上親發起真誠與樂勝解。此願成已，於中下親亦漸次修如是勝解。於親三品得平等已，次於中品、下中上怨亦漸次修如是勝解，由數習力能於上怨起與樂願與上親等。修此勝解既得無退，次於所緣漸修令廣，謂漸運想思惟一邑一國一方一切世界，與樂行相無不遍滿，是為修習慈無量成。若於有情樂求德者能修慈定令速疾成，非於有情樂求失者，以斷善者有德可錄，麟喻獨覺有失可取，先福罪果現可見故。修悲喜法准此應知。謂觀有情沒眾苦海，便願令彼皆得解脫，及想有情得樂離苦，便深欣慰實為樂哉。修捨最初從處中起，漸次乃至能於上親起平等心與處中等。此四無量人起非餘，隨得一時必成三種。生第三定等，唯不成喜故。已辯無量，次辯解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

For one just beginning this practice, how does one cultivate loving-kindness? First, one reflects on the happiness one has personally experienced, or on hearing described the happiness experienced by buddhas, bodhisattvas, śrāvakas, and pratyekabuddhas, and then forms this thought: "may all sentient beings equally experience such happiness." If one's afflictions are originally so strong that one cannot yet extend this thought equally to everyone, one should divide sentient beings into three categories: friends and loved ones, neutral persons, and enemies. Loved ones are further divided into three grades — superior, middling, and lesser; the neutral category has only one grade; enemies are likewise divided into three — lesser, middling, and superior — making seven grades in total. Having divided beings into these grades, one first generates a genuine, wholehearted resolve to bestow happiness upon one's most beloved. Once this aspiration is accomplished, one gradually cultivates the same resolve toward one's middling and lesser loved ones. Having achieved equal regard across all three grades of loved ones, one next gradually cultivates this same resolve toward the neutral person, and then toward one's lesser, middling, and superior enemies in turn — through the power of repeated practice, one becomes able to generate the very same wish to bestow happiness even upon one's worst enemy as one holds toward one's most beloved. Once this resolve is achieved without any regression, one next gradually expands the scope of its object — extending the mental projection, in stages, to one township, one country, one direction, and finally every world-system — until the aspiration to bestow happiness pervades everywhere without exception. This is how the cultivation of the immeasurable of loving-kindness is accomplished. If one directs this practice toward sentient beings admired for their genuine virtues, one can cultivate loving-kindness's concentration and bring it to swift completion; this is not so for beings admired merely for their faults, since one who has cut off their wholesome roots still has some virtue worth recognizing, and even a rhinoceros-like solitary pratyekabuddha has some fault that can be identified, given that the fruits of their prior merit or transgressions are presently visible. Compassion and joy should be cultivated according to this same pattern, understood as follows: contemplating sentient beings as sunk within an ocean of manifold suffering, one wishes for them all to attain liberation from it; and envisioning sentient beings attaining happiness and being freed from suffering, one feels a deep, genuine rejoicing — how truly wonderful this is! Equanimity is cultivated by starting first from the neutral person, then gradually extending, in the same stepwise manner, all the way to generating the very same equanimous mind toward one's most beloved as one holds toward the neutral person. These four immeasurables are given rise to specifically as one begins as a human being, not otherwise; whenever any one is first attained, three are necessarily accomplished together at that same time. For one born into the third dhyāna and above, only joy specifically fails to be accomplished, since joy requires the first two dhyānas alone. The immeasurables have now been explained; liberation is discussed next.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the seven-grade method described here as a genuinely influential piece of contemplative technology that has shaped loving-kindness (mettā) instruction across the Buddhist tradition ever since: rather than attempting to feel equal goodwill toward everyone at once, the practitioner deliberately works outward from the easiest case (one's most beloved) through progressively harder ones (lesser loved ones, then the neutral person, then enemies of increasing difficulty), using success at each stage as the platform for the next. Only once the aspiration has been equalized across all seven human categories does the practice shift to expanding its geographic scope — township, country, direction, and finally every world-system — treating "loving everyone equally" and "loving everyone everywhere" as two genuinely separate dimensions of the same cultivation, tackled in sequence rather than at once.

We should also flag the closing clarification about who can first attain these immeasurables (and in what combination) as resolving the verse's compressed phrase "when a human being gives rise to any one, three are necessarily accomplished together," translated without full context back in the opening section of this fascicle: the commentary here specifies that these four are first cultivated specifically by human beings, and that attaining any one of them brings the other three along together — except that for someone already reborn into the third dhyāna or above, joy specifically is the one exception, since joy alone among the four depends on the feeling of joy present only in the first two dhyānas. We should note that the equanimity-cultivation instruction given here is comparatively terse next to loving-kindness's fully worked-out seven-grade method, and the text does not explicitly confirm whether equanimity is meant to pass through the same intermediate loved-one and enemy categories or move directly from neutral to beloved — we have translated exactly what is stated, without supplying the fuller parallel the text itself does not spell out.

4. The Eight Liberations: What They Are

Having finished the four immeasurables, the treatise turns to the eight liberations (解脫) — a sequence of increasingly refined meditative states beginning with two contemplations of the body's impurity, moving through a contemplation of purity, the four formless attainments, and finally the attainment of cessation itself.

Chinese Source

已辯無量，次辯解脫。頌曰： 解脫有八種， 前三無貪性， 二二一一定， 四無色定善， 滅受想解脫， 微微無間生， 由自地淨心， 及下無漏出， 三境欲可見， 四境類品道， 自上苦集滅， 非擇滅虛空。 論曰：解脫有八：一內有色想觀外色解脫、二內無色想觀外色解脫、三淨解脫身作證具足住、四無色定為次四解脫、滅受想定為第八解脫。八中前三無貪為性，近治貪故。然契經中說想觀者，想觀增故。三中初二不淨相轉，作青瘀等諸行相故。第三解脫清淨相轉，作淨光鮮行相轉故。三并助伴皆五蘊性。初二解脫，一一通依初二靜慮，能治欲界初靜慮中顯色貪故。第三解脫依後靜慮，離八災患心澄淨故。餘地亦有相似解脫而不建立，非增上故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The immeasurables have now been explained; liberation is discussed next. Verse: the liberations number eight; the first three have non-greed as their nature; two, two, one, one depend on their respective concentrations; the next four have the four formless attainments' wholesome states as their nature; the liberation of the cessation of feeling and perception arises immediately after a subtle-subtle mind, by means of one's own ground's pure mind, or by emerging from a lower ground's uncontaminated mind; the first three take as object the desire realm's visible form; the next four take as object the class-inference type of path with respect to one's own and higher grounds' truths of suffering, origination, and cessation, together with their non-analytical cessation and space. Explanation: the liberations number eight: first, the liberation of possessing an internal perception of form while contemplating external forms; second, the liberation of lacking an internal perception of form while contemplating external forms; third, the liberation of pure form, fully abiding having personally realized it in the body; the next four liberations are the four formless attainments themselves; and the liberation of the attainment of cessation of feeling and perception is the eighth. Among these eight, the first three have non-greed as their nature, since they serve as a proximate antidote to craving. Yet a sūtra speaks of them in terms of "perception" and "contemplation," because perception and contemplation are what predominate within them. Among these three, the first two operate through the aspect of impurity, producing mental images such as a corpse's blue-black bruising and the rest; the third liberation operates through the aspect of purity, producing mental images of a clean, bright radiance. All three, together with their accompanying retinue, have the five aggregates as their substance. The first two liberations each depend generally on the first two dhyānas, since they serve as an antidote to craving for visible color specifically within the desire realm, as experienced from the first dhyāna. The third liberation depends on the later dhyānas, since it is only there that the mind, free of the eight calamities, becomes genuinely clear and pure. The remaining grounds also have a similar liberation-like state, but it is not established as one of the eight, since it is not present there at its most heightened, superior degree.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag "internal perception of form" versus "lacking an internal perception of form" as the technical distinction separating the first two liberations, and one this passage does not fully unpack on its own: both take external forms as their object of contemplation (the impurity-images of blue-black bruising and the rest), but the first is practiced by someone who still retains a perception of their own body's form internally, while the second is practiced once that internal perception of form has itself already been abandoned — a progression this project expects to see connected to the earlier fascicles' account of exactly when a practitioner's own bodily form-perception drops away.

We should also flag the exclusion of the third dhyāna from the first two liberations, and of any liberation at all being established beyond the eighth, as following the same "not at its most heightened degree" principle seen elsewhere in this fascicle: a state resembling liberation may technically be present at other grounds, but the tradition names only the specific instances where that state reaches its most superior, exemplary form. The full explanation for why the third dhyāna specifically lacks a liberation of its own is deferred to a later passage in this same discussion, which we will take up as it comes.

5. The Eighth Liberation: Cessation-Attainment and the "Subtle-Subtle Mind"

The treatise now explains the four formless liberations and the eighth and final liberation — the attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception, the most refined meditative state in this entire system — introducing the striking image of a "subtle-subtle mind" as the threshold immediately preceding it.

Chinese Source

次四解脫如其次第，以四無色定善為性。非無記染，非解脫故。亦非散善，性微劣故。彼散善者，如命終心。有說：餘時亦有散善。近分解脫道，亦得解脫名。無間不然，以緣下故。彼要背下地，方名解脫故。然於餘處多分唯說彼根本地名解脫者，以近分中非全分故。第八解脫即滅盡定，彼自性等如先已說。厭背受想而起此故，或總厭背有所緣故，此滅盡定得解脫名。有說：由此解脫定障，微微心後此定現前。前對想心已名微細，此更微細故曰微微。次如是心入滅盡定，從滅定出，或起有頂淨定心，或即能起無所有處無漏心。如是入心唯是有漏，通從有漏無漏心出。

Ru-Yi Translation

The next four liberations, in sequence, have the four formless attainments' wholesome states as their nature — not the neutral or tainted varieties, since these could never count as liberation; nor the scattered, undeliberate wholesome variety either, since its nature is too weak and inferior. Such scattered wholesome states occur, for instance, in the mind at the moment of death. Some say scattered wholesome states also occur at other times. The path belonging to the approach-concentration also receives the name "liberation." The uninterrupted path does not, however, since it still takes a lower ground as its object — a state must specifically have turned away from the lower ground before it can be called a liberation. Yet, in most other contexts, only the root ground itself is called "liberation," since the approach-concentration does not represent the complete, fully-realized portion of that state. The eighth liberation is precisely the attainment of cessation, whose own nature and so forth has already been explained earlier. Because it arises through turning away from and being weary of feeling and perception specifically, or, more generally, through being weary of anything with an object at all, this attainment of cessation receives the name "liberation." Some say: it is by means of this liberation that one's obstruction to concentration is overcome — this attainment becomes actually present immediately following a "subtle-subtle" mind. Compared to a mind still oriented toward perception, the mind immediately preceding cessation is already called subtle; this state is even more subtle still, hence it is called "subtle-subtle." Following such a mind, one enters the attainment of cessation; upon emerging from that cessation-attainment, one either gives rise to the peak of existence's pure concentration-mind, or is directly able to give rise to the sphere of nothing-at-all's uncontaminated mind. The mind by which one enters this attainment is solely contaminated, but one may emerge from it through either a contaminated or an uncontaminated mind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "subtle-subtle mind" (微微心) as a genuinely evocative technical image for the mind's final moment before it ceases altogether in this attainment: the treatise describes a progressive refinement — an ordinary mind oriented toward perception, then a "subtle" mind as one approaches cessation, then a "subtle-subtle" mind immediately at the threshold — as though consciousness itself tapers to its finest possible point before the attainment of cessation extinguishes mental activity entirely. This project encountered cessation-attainment (滅盡定) before, in fascicle 24's treatment of the body-witness (身證), where the striking asymmetry was that the "witnessing" of this profound stillness could only be registered retrospectively, upon emerging, since no mind is present to register anything during the attainment itself — the same basic structure appears to be in play here as well.

We should also flag the striking asymmetry between how one enters and how one may emerge from cessation-attainment as worth sitting with: entry is always through a contaminated mind, yet emergence can be through either a contaminated mind (the peak of existence's pure concentration) or, remarkably, directly into an uncontaminated mind (the sphere of nothing-at-all's uncontaminated attainment) — meaning this deepest of all meditative absorptions can, for some practitioners, function as a direct gateway from an ordinary contaminated state straight into the uncontaminated path, without any contaminated intermediate stage bridging the two.

6. What Each Liberation Takes as Its Object, and Why a Practitioner Cultivates Them

The treatise specifies exactly what each of the eight liberations takes as its object, explains why the third dhyāna alone among the four has none of its own, and closes with a practical rationale for why a practitioner bothers cultivating the pure liberation after the impure ones — along with a note on why only the third and eighth liberations earn the honorific "personally realized in the body."

Chinese Source

八中前三唯以欲界色處為境。有差別者，二境可憎、一境可愛。次四解脫各以自上苦集滅諦，及一切地類智品道，彼非擇滅及與虛空為所緣境。第三靜慮寧無解脫？第三定中無色貪故，自地妙樂所動亂故。行者何緣修淨解脫？為欲令心暫欣悅故。前不淨觀令心沈慼，今修淨觀策發令欣。或為審知自堪能故，謂前所修不淨解脫為成不成？若觀淨相煩惱不起，彼方成故。由二緣故，諸瑜伽師修解脫等，一為諸惑已斷更遠；二為於定得勝自在，故能引起無諍等德及聖神通，由此便能轉變諸事起留捨等種種作用。何故經中第三第八說身作證，非餘六邪？以於八中此二勝故，於二界中各在邊故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these eight, the first three take only the desire realm's visible-form sense-sphere as their object; there is, however, a distinction: two of these objects are repugnant, while one is appealing. The next four liberations each take as their object one's own and higher grounds' truths of suffering, origination, and cessation, together with the class-inference type of path belonging to every ground, as well as that path's own non-analytical cessation and space. Why, then, is there no liberation specifically for the third dhyāna? Because the third dhyāna lacks any craving for form, and because its own ground's exquisite bliss would only agitate and disturb such a practice. Why does a practitioner cultivate the pure liberation at all? In order to bring the mind, for a time, into a state of genuine delight — since the preceding impurity-contemplation causes the mind to sink into gloom and sorrow, cultivating the contemplation of purity afterward spurs and rouses it into delight instead. Or, alternatively, it is done in order to verify one's own capability: has the previously cultivated impurity-liberation actually been accomplished or not? Only if, upon contemplating a pure image, no affliction arises, has that prior liberation truly been accomplished. For two reasons, yogic practitioners cultivate liberation and the rest: first, so that afflictions already severed remain even further away; second, in order to attain superior mastery over concentration itself, thereby becoming able to draw forth qualities such as non-contention and the noble supernormal powers, through which one becomes capable of transforming various matters, giving rise to a variety of functions such as retaining or relinquishing an object at will. Why does the sūtra speak of "personally realizing in the body" specifically for the third and eighth liberations, and not the remaining six? Because, among the eight, these two are the most superior, standing respectively at the outer boundary of the two realms.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the reason given for the third dhyāna's missing liberation as a nice piece of internal consistency with earlier fascicles' account of the dhyānas themselves: the third dhyāna is precisely the level at which bliss reaches its peak (before the fourth dhyāna sheds bliss for neutral feeling entirely), and that same exquisite bliss which makes the third dhyāna distinctive is exactly what would destabilize the kind of impurity- or purity-focused contemplation a liberation requires — the ground's greatest strength is, in this specific respect, an obstacle rather than an asset.

We should also flag the phrase "standing respectively at the outer boundary of the two realms" as referring to the third liberation (the summit of the form-realm-focused liberations, since the formless liberations that follow belong to a different order entirely) and the eighth liberation (cessation-attainment, the outer boundary of meditative attainment altogether, beyond which nothing further is cultivated) — the two liberations singled out for "personally realizing in the body" are thus not arbitrary but mark the two genuine edges of this eightfold scheme, the furthest point reachable within the form-based liberations and the furthest point reachable by the entire sequence.

7. The Eight Mastery-Bases: Mapped onto the Liberations, Yet Doing More

The treatise introduces a second eightfold scheme, the mastery-bases (勝處), explicitly mapped onto the liberations just discussed — and then explains the crucial difference between the two: where a liberation only allows one to turn away from an object, a mastery-base allows one to actively control it.

Chinese Source

已辯解脫，次辯勝處。頌曰： 勝處有八種， 二如初解脫， 次二如第二， 後四如第三。 論曰：勝處有八：一內有色想觀外色少、二內有色想觀外色多、三內無色想觀外色少、四內無色想觀外色多、內無色想觀外青黃赤白為四，足前成八。八中初二如初解脫，次二如第二解脫，後四如第三解脫。若爾，八勝處何殊三解脫？前修解脫唯能棄背，後修勝處能制所緣，隨所樂觀惑終不起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Liberation has now been explained; the mastery-bases are discussed next. Verse: the mastery-bases number eight; two are like the first liberation; the next two are like the second; the last four are like the third. Explanation: the mastery-bases number eight: first, possessing an internal perception of form while contemplating a small quantity of external forms; second, possessing an internal perception of form while contemplating a large quantity of external forms; third, lacking an internal perception of form while contemplating a small quantity of external forms; fourth, lacking an internal perception of form while contemplating a large quantity of external forms; and lacking an internal perception of form while contemplating external forms as blue, yellow, red, and white respectively, making four more — added to the previous four, these make eight in total. Among these eight, the first two are like the first liberation, the next two are like the second liberation, and the last four are like the third liberation. If this is so, how do the eight mastery-bases differ from the three form-based liberations? Cultivating the liberations discussed previously only enables one to turn away from and abandon an object; cultivating the mastery-bases that follow enables one to actually master one's object — one is able to contemplate it however one wishes, and affliction will never arise in response.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "small versus large quantity" distinction structuring the first four mastery-bases as adding a dimension the corresponding liberations did not have: where the first two liberations simply contemplate external forms as impure without further specification, the mastery-bases explicitly divide this same practice by scale, first mastering a small quantity of such objects before mastering a large one — consistent with the "mastery" framing, since genuine control over one's response to an object plausibly needs to be demonstrated at a limited scale before it can be trusted to hold at a greater one.

We should also flag the liberation/mastery-base distinction — turning away from an object versus actively mastering it — as a genuinely important functional upgrade worth holding onto for the totality-bases (遍處) introduced in the next section, which this passage's closing line already signals will represent a further advance beyond even mastery. A liberation is essentially defensive: it lets the mind disengage from what would otherwise disturb it. A mastery-base is offensive in the constructive sense: the practitioner can deliberately summon the very object that liberation once required avoiding, contemplate it exactly as desired, and remain undisturbed regardless — demonstrating that affliction has not merely been evaded but genuinely brought under control.

8. The Ten Totality-Bases, and the Full Sequence: Liberation, Mastery, Totality

The treatise completes this three-part progression with the totality-bases (遍處) — meditative objects contemplated so thoroughly and without any gap that they saturate the whole of one's awareness — and confirms that a practitioner is meant to move through all three schemes in order, each building on what came before.

Chinese Source

已辯勝處，次辯遍處。頌曰： 遍處有十種， 八如淨解脫， 後二淨無色， 緣自地四蘊。 論曰：遍處有十，謂周遍觀地、水、火、風、青、黃、赤、白及空與識二無邊處。於一切處周遍觀察無有間隙，故名遍處。十中前八如淨解脫，謂八自性皆是無貪；若并助伴五蘊為性，依第四靜慮，緣欲可見色。有餘師說：唯風遍處緣所觸中風界為境，後二遍處如次空識二淨無色為其自性，各緣自地四蘊為境。應知此中修觀行者，從諸解脫入諸勝處，從諸勝處入諸遍處，以後後起勝前前故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The mastery-bases have now been explained; the totality-bases are discussed next. Verse: the totality-bases number ten; eight are like the pure liberation; the last two are the pure formless attainments, taking one's own ground's four aggregates as object. Explanation: the totality-bases number ten: contemplating, in total saturation, earth, water, fire, wind, blue, yellow, red, white, and the two spheres of infinite space and infinite consciousness. Because one contemplates every point within the object in total saturation, with no gap left uncovered, this is called a "totality-base." Among the ten, the first eight are like the pure liberation, in that their own nature, in all eight cases, is non-greed; including their accompanying retinue, their nature is the five aggregates; they depend on the fourth dhyāna, and take the desire realm's visible form as their object. Some teachers say that the wind totality-base specifically takes as its object the wind-element within the tangible sense-sphere. The last two totality-bases, in sequence, have the pure varieties of the spheres of infinite space and infinite consciousness as their nature, each taking its own ground's four aggregates as its object. It should be understood that, in this scheme, a practitioner cultivating this contemplative practice proceeds from the liberations into the mastery-bases, and from the mastery-bases into the totality-bases, since each later stage arises as a further advance surpassing the one before it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the etymology given for "totality-base" (遍處, literally "pervading everywhere") as directly continuing the liberation/mastery progression from the previous two sections: where a liberation lets one turn away from an object and a mastery-base lets one actively control it, a totality-base represents a further advance still — the object is now contemplated so completely, without the slightest gap or discontinuity anywhere within it, that it saturates the practitioner's entire field of awareness. The explicit closing statement that "each later stage arises as a further advance surpassing the one before it" confirms this is meant as a genuine three-stage progression, not three independent, unrelated schemes that happen to share overlapping content.

We should also flag the eight form-based totality-bases (earth, water, fire, wind, and the four colors) as extending the three-color scheme already familiar from the third liberation and the color-specific mastery-bases into a more complete set of eight elemental and chromatic objects, while only two of the formless attainments — infinite space and infinite consciousness, the first two of the four — receive a totality-base of their own; the treatise does not explain here why the spheres of nothing-at-all and neither-perception-nor-non-perception are excluded from this final scheme, and we have not supplied a rationale the text itself does not give.

9. How These Are Attained, What Bodies They Depend On, and the Three Special Causes for Higher Rebirth

The treatise addresses how the liberations, mastery-bases, and totality-bases are actually attained and which realms' bodies can support them, then turns to a genuinely striking cosmological question: for a being already born into the form or formless realms, what specific conditions cause them to newly give rise to the dhyānas or formless attainments in the first place?

Chinese Source

此解脫等三門功德，為由何得？依何身起？頌曰： 滅定如先辯， 餘皆通二得， 無色依三界， 餘唯人趣起。 論曰：第八解脫如先已辯，以即是前滅盡定故。餘解脫等通由二得，謂由離染及加行得，以有曾習未曾習故。四無色解脫、二無色遍處，一一通依三界身起，餘唯人起。由教力故，異生及聖皆能現起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through what means are the virtues of these three gateways — liberation, mastery, and totality — actually attained? And on what body do they depend when they arise? Verse: cessation-attainment is as already explained; all the rest are attained through both means; the formless ones among these depend on all three realms; the rest arise only among humans. Explanation: the eighth liberation is as already explained, since it is precisely the previously discussed attainment of cessation. All the remaining liberations, mastery-bases, and totality-bases are attained through both means: through freedom from affliction, and through preparatory practice — since some have previously been cultivated before, and others have not. The four formless liberations and the two formless totality-bases each depend generally on a body from any of the three realms in order to arise; all the rest arise only among human beings. Through the power of the Buddha's teaching, both ordinary beings and noble ones alike are able to give rise to them.

Chinese Source

諸有生在色無色界起靜慮無色，由何等別緣？頌曰： 二界由因業， 能起無色定， 色界起靜慮， 亦由法爾力。 論曰：生上二界總由三緣，能進引生色無色定：一由因力，謂於先時近及數修為起因故。二由業力，謂先曾造感上地生順後受業，彼業異熟將起現前，勢力能令進起彼定。以若未離下地煩惱，必定無容生上地故。三法爾力，謂器世界將欲壞時，下地有情法爾能起上地靜慮。以於此位所有善法，由法爾力皆增盛故。諸有生在上二界中起無色定，由因業力，非法爾力，無雲等天不為三災之所壞故。生在色界起靜慮時，由上二緣及法爾力。若生欲界起上定時，一一應知皆由教力。

Ru-Yi Translation

For those born within the form or formless realms, by what specific conditions do they give rise to the dhyānas or formless attainments? Verse: in the two upper realms, it is through the power of cause and of karma that one is able to give rise to the formless attainments; in the form realm, giving rise to the dhyānas additionally occurs through the power of natural law. Explanation: for one born into either of the two upper realms, there are three conditions in total capable of advancing and drawing forth the dhyānas or formless attainments: first, the power of cause — referring to having, in the past, recently and repeatedly practiced, which itself serves as the cause for the attainment's arising. Second, the power of karma — referring to karma previously created that brings about rebirth in a higher ground, to be experienced in a subsequent life; when the ripening of that karma is about to become actually present, its force is able to advance and draw forth that attainment, since, if one has not yet become free of a lower ground's affliction, one could never possibly be reborn in a higher ground in the first place. Third, the power of natural law — referring to the fact that, when a world-system is about to be destroyed, sentient beings of the lower ground are, by the very nature of things, spontaneously able to give rise to the dhyāna of the ground above, since, at that juncture, whatever wholesome dharmas are present all become intensified through this natural power. For those born within the two upper realms who give rise to the formless attainments, this occurs through the power of cause and the power of karma, not through the power of natural law, since the Cloudless Heaven and the other form-realm heavens at that level are not destroyed by the three calamities. For one born within the form realm who gives rise to the dhyānas, this occurs through the two conditions above together with the power of natural law. If one is born within the desire realm and gives rise to a higher attainment, it should be understood that, in every single case, this occurs through the power of the teaching alone.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "power of karma" mechanism here as a genuinely elegant piece of cosmological bookkeeping, connecting rebirth itself to meditative attainment: a being cannot be reborn into a higher ground without having already become free of the ground below's affliction, which means the relevant higher-ground concentration must, in some sense, already be latent or imminent by the time the karma that causes that rebirth is set to ripen — the ripening of birth-causing karma is described as itself carrying enough force to "advance and draw forth" the corresponding attainment, rather than the attainment and the rebirth being two separate, independently arising facts about the being's new existence.

We should also flag the "power of natural law" (法爾力) as a striking third category distinct from both personal cultivation and karma: at the specific cosmological moment when a world-system approaches its periodic destruction, beings at a lower ground can spontaneously give rise to a higher dhyāna simply because wholesome states in general intensify at that juncture, independent of that particular being's own prior practice or karmic history. This power is explicitly said not to extend to the formless attainments for beings already in the upper two realms, since — as the passage notes — the Cloudless Heaven and the form-realm heavens at that level are not subject to the three calamities that periodically destroy lower world-systems in the first place, so there is no such moment of cosmic upheaval to trigger it.

10. Colophon: How Long the True Dharma Endures, and Vasubandhu's Own Closing Verses

Having finished the eight chapters of systematic Abhidharma analysis that began all the way back at the treatise's opening, Vasubandhu closes with a reflection on what the "true Dharma" actually consists of and how long it can be expected to last, an acknowledgment of his own primary source — the Vaibhāṣika masters of Kashmir — and a set of unusually personal, elegiac closing verses lamenting the Buddha's long absence from the world.

Chinese Source

前來分別種種法門，皆為弘持世尊正法。何謂正法？當住幾時？頌曰： 佛正法有二， 謂教證為體， 有持說行者， 此便住世間。 論曰：世尊正法體有二種，一教、二證。教謂契經、調伏、對法，證謂三乘菩提分法。有能受持及正說者，佛正教法便住世間。有能依教正修行者，佛正證法便住世間。故隨三人住世時量，應知正法住爾所時。聖教總言唯住千載，有釋證法唯住千年，教法住時復過於此。此論依攝阿毘達磨，為依何理釋對法耶？頌曰： 迦濕彌羅議理成， 我多依彼釋對法， 少有貶量為我失， 判法正理在牟尼。 論曰：迦濕彌羅國毘婆沙師議阿毘達磨理善成立，我多依彼釋對法宗，少有貶量為我過失。判法正理唯在世尊，及諸如來大聖弟子。

Ru-Yi Translation

Everything analyzed and distinguished above, across all these various approaches to the teaching, has been undertaken entirely in order to propagate and uphold the Blessed One's true Dharma. What, then, is meant by the "true Dharma"? And for how long will it endure? Verse: the Buddha's true Dharma has two aspects — doctrine and realization constitute its substance; wherever there are those who uphold it, expound it, and practice it, it thereby continues to abide in the world. Explanation: the substance of the Blessed One's true Dharma has two aspects: doctrine and realization. Doctrine refers to the sūtras, the vinaya, and the abhidharma; realization refers to the limbs of awakening belonging to all three vehicles. Wherever there are those capable of upholding and correctly expounding it, the Buddha's true doctrinal Dharma thereby continues to abide in the world. Wherever there are those capable of correctly practicing in accordance with that doctrine, the Buddha's true realized Dharma thereby continues to abide in the world. Hence, corresponding to the duration these three kinds of people remain in the world, it should be understood that the true Dharma abides for precisely that same span of time. The noble teaching generally states that it abides for only a thousand years; some explain that realization specifically abides for only a thousand years, while doctrine's own duration extends still further beyond this. This treatise, in compiling the abhidharma, has relied on what specific reasoning to explain the abhidharma tradition? Verse: the reasoning debated and established by the masters of Kashmir — I have, for the most part, relied on their tradition in explaining the abhidharma; wherever I have somewhat diminished or reduced their positions, this is my own fault; the correct principle for judging the Dharma rests with the Sage alone. Explanation: the Vaibhāṣika masters of the kingdom of Kashmir have debated and soundly established the reasoning of the abhidharma; I have, for the most part, relied on their tradition in explaining the abhidharma school's teachings, and wherever I have somewhat diminished or reduced their positions, this is my own personal fault. The correct principle for judging the Dharma rests solely with the Blessed One, and with the great noble disciples of the Tathāgatas.

Vasubandhu closes the eight chapters of systematic analysis with three unusually personal verses, stepping outside the treatise's normal dialectical voice.

Chinese Source

大師世眼久已閉， 堪為證者多散滅， 不見真理無制人， 由鄙尋思亂聖教。 自覺已歸勝寂靜， 持彼教者多隨滅， 世無依怙喪眾德， 無鉤制惑隨意轉。 既知如來正法壽， 漸次淪亡如至喉， 是諸煩惱力增時， 應求解脫勿放逸。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Great Teacher, the world's eye, has long since closed; those capable of serving as witnesses to realization have mostly scattered and passed away; with no one left who has seen the true principle to restrain them, the noble teaching is thrown into confusion by base, undisciplined speculation. He who awakened by himself has already returned to the supreme, tranquil stillness; those who upheld his teaching have, for the most part, likewise passed away following him; the world, left without any refuge, has lost its manifold virtues, and, with no hook left to restrain them, the afflictions now turn freely wherever they please. Now knowing that the lifespan of the Tathāgata's true Dharma is gradually sinking into decline, as though the water had already risen to the throat — at this very time, when the power of these afflictions is only increasing, one should seek liberation, and never be heedless.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag this colophon as marking a genuine structural boundary in the treatise as a whole, not merely within this fascicle: it closes the eight chapters of systematic Abhidharma analysis that began at the very opening of the entire work, complete with Vasubandhu's own explicit statement of method (relying chiefly on the Kashmiri Vaibhāṣika masters, while taking personal responsibility for any point where he has departed from or reduced their positions) and a frank acknowledgment that final authority over doctrine belongs to the Buddha and his great disciples alone, not to any later commentator including himself. What follows immediately after this colophon, and continues into the remainder of this fascicle and beyond, is Chapter 9 — a distinct, appended essay refuting the pudgalavādin doctrine of a real "person" (補特伽羅), traditionally read as a separate work in its own right rather than a ninth installment of the same eightfold analytical scheme.

We should also flag the closing three verses as a striking shift in register from anything else in this project's translation of the Kośa so far: rather than continuing the treatise's characteristic dispassionate, dialectical voice, Vasubandhu writes in an explicitly personal, elegiac mode — mourning the Buddha's long-past death ("the world's eye has long since closed"), the scattering of those who could once personally verify the teaching through their own realization, and the resulting vulnerability of the true Dharma to "base, undisciplined speculation" absent anyone left to authoritatively correct it. The closing image — the Dharma's lifespan "sinking into decline, as though the water had already risen to the throat" — is a vivid drowning metaphor for a tradition running out of time, and the verses end not with a doctrinal conclusion but with a direct exhortation to the reader: given how urgent the situation is, seek liberation now, without delay.
