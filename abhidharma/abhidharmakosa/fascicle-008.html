Where Does a Formless Being Live?

This page carries the opening of fascicle 8, beginning Chapter 3 (分別世品第三之一, the Loka-nirdeśa, "Discrimination of the World") with the cosmology of the three realms of existence. The desire realm has twenty distinct places and the form realm seventeen (or, on the Kashmiri masters' count, sixteen) — but the formless realm, strikingly, has no spatial location at all, distinguished only by four levels of birth. This raises a genuinely pointed question the fascicle takes up directly: without any body to serve as a basis, what sustains a formless being's mental continuity at all?

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 9 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 8 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. A New Chapter: How Many Places Does Each Realm Contain?

Chapter 3 opens with a compact verse laying out the whole cosmological map at once: the desire realm's twenty places, the form realm's seventeen, and the formless realm's complete lack of any place, distinguished instead by four kinds of birth.

Chinese Source

已依三界分別心等。今次應說，三界是何？各於其中處別有幾？頌曰：地獄傍生鬼，人及六欲天，名欲界二十，由地獄洲異。此上十七處，名色界於中，三靜慮各三，第四靜慮八。無色界無處，由生有四種，依同分及命，令心等相續。

Ru-Yi Translation

Mind and the rest have already been discussed by reference to the three realms. It should next be explained: what, exactly, are the three realms, and how many distinct places does each contain? Verse: the hells, animals, and hungry ghosts, together with humans and the six desire-heavens, are called the desire realm's twenty — owing to the distinctions among the hells and the continents. Above these are seventeen places called the form realm, within which the first three dhyānas each have three, and the fourth dhyāna has eight. The formless realm has no place at all; owing to birth, it is of four kinds — relying on class-commonality and the life faculty to sustain the continuity of mind and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This verse packs the entire cosmological skeleton of Chapter 3 into six lines, and the prose that follows over the next several sections will unpack every piece of it: the desire realm's twenty places (hells, continents, heavens, animals, and hungry ghosts all combined), the form realm's seventeen meditative heavens stacked in four tiers, and the formless realm's genuinely striking claim to have no place whatsoever — a claim significant enough that the treatise immediately raises, and takes seriously, the question of what could possibly hold a formless being's mind together across time without any physical basis at all. That question, flagged already in this opening verse's closing line, becomes one of this fascicle's most philosophically interesting discussions.

2. The Desire Realm's Twenty Places

The desire realm's twenty places are enumerated in full: the six heavens by name, the eight great hells by name, and the four continents by name — twelve locations in all, plus the heavens, animals, and hungry ghosts making twenty.

Chinese Source

論曰：地獄等四及六欲天并器世間，是名欲界。六欲天者，一、四大王眾天；二、三十三天；三、夜摩天；四、覩史多天；五、樂變化天；六、他化自在天。如是欲界處別有幾？地獄洲異，故成二十。八大地獄，名地獄異：一等活地獄、二黑繩地獄、三眾合地獄、四號叫地獄、五大叫地獄、六炎熱地獄、七大熱地獄、八無間地獄。言洲異者，謂四大洲：一南贍部洲、二東勝身洲、三西牛貨洲、四北俱盧洲。如是十二并六欲天、傍生、餓鬼處成二十。若有情界從自在天至無間獄，若器世界乃至風輪，皆欲界攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Prose: the four destinies beginning with the hells, together with the six desire-heavens and the receptacle world that contains them, are called the desire realm. The six desire-heavens are: first, the Heaven of the Four Great Kings; second, the Heaven of the Thirty-Three; third, the Yama Heaven; fourth, the Tuṣita Heaven; fifth, the Heaven of Enjoying Transformations; sixth, the Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations. How many distinct places, then, does the desire realm contain, in detail? Owing to the distinctions among the hells and the continents, there come to be twenty in all. The eight great hells are called the hells' distinction: first, the Reviving Hell; second, the Black-Rope Hell; third, the Crushing-Together Hell; fourth, the Wailing Hell; fifth, the Great Wailing Hell; sixth, the Scorching Hell; seventh, the Great Scorching Hell; eighth, the Hell of Uninterrupted Suffering. As for the continents' distinction: there are four great continents: first, Jambudvīpa; second, Pūrvavideha; third, Aparagodānīya; fourth, Uttarakuru. These twelve, together with the six desire-heavens, the animals, and the hungry ghosts, make twenty. Whether one speaks of the world of sentient beings, from the Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations down to the Hell of Uninterrupted Suffering, or of the receptacle world, down to the wheel of wind, all of it is subsumed under the desire realm.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The count of twenty is reached by treating each hell and each continent as its own distinct "place" while treating the six heavens, the animals, and the hungry ghosts each as a single place apiece: eight hells plus four continents makes twelve locations tied to Jambudvīpa-style geography, and adding the six heavens, one place for all animals everywhere, and one place for all hungry ghosts everywhere brings the total to twenty. The closing line is worth noting for how expansively "desire realm" is being used here: it covers not just the beings inhabiting these twenty places, but the entire physical receptacle-world itself, all the way down to the foundational "wheel of wind" this tradition holds the physical universe ultimately rests upon.

3. The Form Realm's Seventeen Heavens, and a Kashmiri Recount to Sixteen

The form realm's seventeen places are named in full across its four meditative tiers — before the Kashmiri masters offer a striking correction: the Great Brahma Heaven, they argue, is not a separate place at all, but merely a tower within the heaven just below it.

Chinese Source

此欲界上處有十七，謂三靜慮處各有三，第四靜慮處獨有八，器及有情總名色界。第一靜慮處有三者，一梵眾天、二梵輔天、三大梵天。第二靜慮處有三者，一少光天、二無量光天、三極光淨天。第三靜慮處有三者，一少淨天、二無量淨天、三遍淨天。第四靜慮處有八者，一無雲天、二福生天、三廣果天、四無煩天、五無熱天、六善現天、七善見天、八色究竟天。迦濕彌羅國諸大論師皆言：色界處但有十六。彼謂：即於梵輔天處有高臺閣，名大梵天，一主所居非有別地，如尊處座四眾圍繞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Above the desire realm there are seventeen places: the first three dhyāna-levels each have three, and the fourth dhyāna-level alone has eight; the receptacle world and its inhabitants together are called the form realm. The first dhyāna-level's three are: first, the Heaven of Brahma's Retinue; second, the Heaven of Brahma's Ministers; third, the Great Brahma Heaven. The second dhyāna-level's three are: first, the Heaven of Lesser Light; second, the Heaven of Immeasurable Light; third, the Heaven of Light-Sound. The third dhyāna-level's three are: first, the Heaven of Lesser Purity; second, the Heaven of Immeasurable Purity; third, the Heaven of All-Pervasive Purity. The fourth dhyāna-level's eight are: first, the Cloudless Heaven; second, the Heaven of Merit-Born Beings; third, the Heaven of Great Reward; fourth, the Heaven Without Affliction; fifth, the Heaven Without Heat; sixth, the Heaven of Skillful Appearance; seventh, the Heaven of Skillful Vision; eighth, the Heaven of the Ultimate Form. The great treatise-masters of Kashmir all say that the form realm has only sixteen places: they hold that, within the Heaven of Brahma's Ministers, there is a lofty tower-pavilion called the Great Brahma Heaven — a single ruler's dwelling place, not a genuinely separate ground of its own, comparable to a venerable one's throne encircled by the four assemblies of disciples.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four tiers map neatly onto the four dhyānas (meditative absorptions) that produce rebirth there: three refinements each for the first three dhyānas, tracking increasingly subtle degrees of the same basic meditative attainment, and a more elaborate eightfold division for the fourth dhyāna, the most refined level, which this tradition treats as spiritually rich enough to house several distinct sub-categories of being — including, notably, the five "pure abodes" (implicitly among the last five named) reserved specifically for non-returners on their way to final liberation.

The Kashmiri masters' recount to sixteen is a nice small example of this tradition's willingness to treat cosmological claims as genuinely open questions rather than settled dogma: their proposed image — a single great ruler occupying an elevated tower within his ministers' own domain, rather than a wholly separate world-level unto himself — is offered as a matter of comparison to ordinary court life, a king's throne surrounded by attendants rather than a king inhabiting a separate kingdom. I have not found a resolution of this dispute later in the text and present both counts as the treatise itself does, without picking a winner.

4. The Formless Realm Has No Place At All

The treatise's most striking cosmological claim: the formless realm occupies no spatial location whatsoever, its four levels distinguished purely by the kind of birth attained — proven by a genuinely clever observation about where rebirth and the intermediate-existence being actually arise.

Chinese Source

無色界中都無有處，以無色法無有方所，過去未來無表無色不住方所，理決然故。但異熟生，差別有四：一空無邊處、二識無邊處、三無所有處、四非想非非想處，如是四種名無色界。此四非由處有上下，但由生故勝劣有殊。復如何知彼無方處？謂於是處得彼定者，命終即於是處生故。復從彼沒生欲色時，即於是處中有起故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the formless realm, there is no place at all, since formless dharmas have no spatial location — past and future unmanifest form, and formless dharmas generally, do not reside in any location; this is a matter of settled principle. But, based on the distinction in what karmic-ripening is produced, there are four kinds: first, the sphere of infinite space; second, the sphere of infinite consciousness; third, the sphere of nothing-at-all; fourth, the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. These four kinds are called the formless realm. These four are not distinguished by any higher or lower spatial location, but rather by birth itself, through which there is a difference of superior and inferior. How, moreover, is it known that these have no spatial location at all? Because whoever attains that concentration in a given place dies and is reborn in that very same place; and, when passing away from that state to be reborn in the desire or form realm, the intermediate-existence being arises in that very same place as well.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The proof is genuinely elegant: if the formless spheres were real places the way the form-realm heavens are, then dying into one, or being reborn out of one back into the desire or form realm, would require the mind to travel somewhere — to arrive at, or depart from, an actual location. But what is observed instead is that rebirth and the intermediate-existence being both simply occur right where the person already was, physically, at the moment of death — no journey to or from any formless "place" ever takes place, because there is no such place to journey to or from. The four spheres are thus purely qualitative levels of attainment, differentiated only by how refined the birth is, with no spatial hierarchy stacking one above another the way the form realm's four dhyāna-tiers do.

5. Without a Body, What Holds a Formless Mind Together?

A genuinely pointed question follows directly from the formless realm having no body at all: what sustains the continuity of mind across time for a being with no physical basis whatsoever? The Vaibhāṣika answer runs into what looks like real circularity, before the Sautrāntika offers a leaner alternative.

Chinese Source

如有色界一切有情，要依色身心等相續。於無色界受生有情，以何為依心等相續？對法諸師說彼心等依眾同分及與命根而得相續。若爾，有色有情心等，何不但依此二相續？有色界生此二劣故。無色此二因何故強？彼界二從勝定生故，由彼等至能伏色想。若爾，於彼心等相續，但依勝定，何用別依？又今應說，如有色界受生有情同分命根依色而轉，無色此二以何為依？此二更互相依而轉。有色此二何不相依？有色界生此二劣故。無色此二因何故強？彼界此二種，從勝定生故。前說彼定能伏色想，是則還同心相續難。或心心所唯互相依，故經部師說無色界心等相續無別有依。謂若有因未離色愛引起心等，所引心等與色俱生依色而轉。若因於色已得離愛，厭背色故，所引心等非色俱生不依色轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as every sentient being possessing form necessarily relies upon a physical body for the continuity of mind and the rest, for a sentient being reborn in the formless realm, what does the continuity of mind and the rest rely upon as its basis? The Ābhidharmika masters say that mind and the rest, for such beings, achieve continuity by relying upon class-commonality and the life faculty. If that is so, then why do sentient beings possessing form not likewise rely on only these two alone for the continuity of their mind and the rest? Because, for beings born with form, these two are of inferior strength. Why, then, are these same two strong in the formless realm? Because, in that realm, these two arise from a superior concentration, since that attainment is able to subdue the perception of form. If that is so, then, since it is the superior concentration itself that explains their continuity, what need is there for any further, separate basis at all? Moreover, it should now be asked: just as, for a sentient being born with form, class-commonality and the life faculty operate in dependence on form, in the formless realm, what do these same two rely upon as their basis? These two operate by relying on each other, mutually. Why, then, do these two not likewise rely on each other in the case of beings possessing form? Because, for beings born with form, these two are of inferior strength. Why, then, are these same two strong in the formless realm? Because, in that realm, these two kinds arise from a superior concentration. But it was already said that this attainment is able to subdue the perception of form — and this simply returns us to the very same difficulty already raised about the continuity of mind. Alternatively, mind and its mental factors simply rely on each other alone — and so the Sautrāntika masters say that, in the formless realm, the continuity of mind and the rest requires no separate, additional basis at all. That is: if there is a cause whereby one has not yet become free of affection for form, and mind and the rest are thereby brought about, then this induced mind and the rest arise together with form, and operate in dependence on form. But if, through some cause, one has already become free of affection toward form, being wearied of and turned away from form, then this induced mind and the rest do not arise together with form, and do not operate in dependence on form.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag honestly, rather than smooth over, what looks like a genuine circularity in the Ābhidharmika position as the text presents it. Pressed on why class-commonality and the life faculty are strong enough to sustain mind on their own in the formless realm, the answer given is that they arise from a superior concentration; but pressed again on what class-commonality and the life faculty themselves rely on in the formless realm (since they can no longer rely on a body, as they do everywhere else), the answer is that they rely on each other — and when this, too, is challenged by appeal to the very same "superior concentration" explanation, the text itself remarks that this simply returns to the same difficulty already raised. The dialectic does not resolve this loop; it is left standing, and the Sautrāntika position is then offered as a different way of avoiding the problem altogether rather than as a direct refutation of it.

The Sautrāntika alternative is a clean example of this school's characteristic move throughout this translation project: rather than positing additional real entities (class-commonality, life faculty) that then themselves need some further basis, they simply observe that mind and its mental factors already sustain each other everywhere, in every realm, without needing anything extra — and what actually distinguishes a desire- or form-realm mind-stream from a formless one is nothing more than whether affection for form is still present in the causal chain producing it. Where that affection remains, mind arises bound up with form; where it has been abandoned through weariness and turning-away, mind simply continues on its own, needing no body and no additional metaphysical scaffolding to explain how.

6. Why Are They Called "Desire Realm," "Form Realm," "Formless Realm"?

A short etymological digression explains the three realms' names through this tradition's standard elision convention — "X-realm" really means "the realm belonging to X" — and adds a second, complementary reading: a realm is also named for its own power to sustain and hold what belongs to it.

Chinese Source

何故名為欲等三界？能持自相故名為界。或種族義，如前已釋。欲所屬界說名欲界，色所屬界說名色界，略去中言故作是說，如胡椒飲如金剛環。於彼界中色非有故名為無色。所言色者，是變礙義，或示現義。彼體非色立無色名，非彼但用色無為體。無色所屬界，說名無色界，略去中言，喻如前說。又欲之界名為欲界，此界力能任持欲故。色無色界應知亦然。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are these called the desire realm, and so forth, the three realms? Because it is able to hold its own particular characteristic, it is called a "realm" — or, in the sense of "lineage," as already explained before. The realm to which desire belongs is called the desire realm; the realm to which form belongs is called the form realm — the middle word being elided in this manner of speaking, just as "pepper-drink" means a drink flavored with pepper, and "vajra-ring" means a ring set with a vajra-gem. Within that realm, form does not exist at all — hence it is called "formless." What is meant by "form" here is the sense of resistant obstruction, or the sense of manifestation and display. Since that realm's own essential nature is simply not form, the name "formless" is established for it — it is not that this realm's essence is merely "the absence of form" taken as its defining substance. The realm to which formlessness belongs is called the formless realm — the middle word being elided, by the same analogy already given. Moreover, "the realm of desire" is called the desire realm because this realm's own power is able to sustain and hold desire. The form realm and the formless realm should be understood in just the same way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two readings offered here are worth distinguishing clearly, since they trade on two different senses "realm" (界, dhātu) can carry, both already introduced earlier in this translation project. On the first reading, "desire realm" is simply shorthand for "the realm to which desire belongs" — an elided possessive, exactly like "pepper-drink" standing in for "a drink flavored with pepper." On the second, "realm" is read through its more basic sense of "that which holds or sustains its own particular characteristic," so "desire realm" instead means "the realm whose own power sustains and holds desire" — a realm defined by what it actively supports, not merely by what happens to belong to it. Both readings arrive at the same three names, but they locate the defining relationship in different places: one in desire's tie to the realm, the other in the realm's own causal power over desire.

The clarification about "formless" deserves a second look, too: the text is careful to insist that "formless" names something with its own positive nature that simply is not form, rather than a purely negative, parasitic label meaning nothing more than "wherever form happens to be missing." This matters because a merely negative definition would make the formless realm a kind of leftover category with no positive character of its own — exactly the sort of move this tradition otherwise resists when it wants to treat something as a genuine, distinct kind of existence.

7. What Is "Desire," Really? A Verse Debate with a Heretic

A genuinely sharp philosophical exchange, staged as a confrontation between an Ājīvika heretic and the Venerable Śāriputra: is desire the enticing object itself, or the mind's own discriminating craving toward it? Śāriputra's rebuttal turns the heretic's own challenge back on him with precision.

Chinese Source

此中欲言為說何法？略說段食婬所引貪。如經頌言：世諸妙境非真欲，真欲謂人分別貪，妙境如本住世間，智者於中已除欲。邪命外道便詰尊者舍利子言：若世妙境非真欲，說欲是人分別貪，比丘應名受欲人，起惡分別尋思故。時舍利子反質彼言：若世妙境是真欲，說欲非人分別貪，汝師應名受欲人，恒觀可意妙色故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, exactly, does the word "desire" refer to here? Briefly stated: the craving induced by physical nourishment and by sexual union. As a scriptural verse states: "The world's wondrous objects are not true desire; true desire is a person's own discriminating craving. The wondrous objects remain in the world just as they always were; the wise, with respect to them, have already removed desire." An Ājīvika heretic then challenged the Venerable Śāriputra, saying: "If the world's wondrous objects are not true desire, and desire is said to be a person's own discriminating craving, then a monk ought to be called one who indulges in desire, since he too gives rise to unwholesome discriminating thought." Śāriputra then countered him in return, saying: "If the world's wondrous objects were true desire, and desire were not a person's own discriminating craving, then your own teacher ought to be called one who indulges in desire, since he constantly gazes upon agreeable, wondrous forms."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The heretic's challenge is a genuine "gotcha" attempt: if desire is defined as the mind's own discriminating craving rather than the external object, then even a monk, who inevitably still notices and mentally registers pleasant things from time to time, would seem to count as "indulging in desire" merely by having any discriminating thought about them at all — an absurd consequence for someone supposedly free of desire. Śāriputra's reply does not deny the challenge's logical form; it mirrors it back with the opposite premise. If desire were instead simply identified with the wondrous object itself, as the heretic's framing implicitly requires for the challenge to land, then anyone who merely looks at something beautiful — including, pointedly, the heretic's own teacher — would equally have to count as indulging in desire, merely by ordinary seeing. Since that consequence is obviously worse (it would make involuntary sense-perception itself a moral failing), the exchange decisively favors the scripture's original position: desire is the discriminating craving a mind brings to an object, never the object on its own.

I have translated 邪命外道 as "an Ājīvika heretic" — the Ājīvikas were a real, historically attested rival ascetic movement contemporary with early Buddhism, associated with a strict fatalism about karma, and this passage is likely a genuine record, however stylized, of a specific historical or semi-historical polemical encounter rather than a purely hypothetical interlocutor.

8. Being "Bound" to a Realm Is Not the Same as Being Present in It

A precise clarification, illustrated with a memorable circular-definition joke about a horse and its owner: merely manifesting within a realm does not make a dharma bound to it — boundness tracks specifically the latent craving that lies dormant and increases there. A related puzzle closes the topic: how can desire even arise toward a magically-created object?

Chinese Source

若法於彼三界現行，此法即說三界繫不？不爾。云何於中隨增三界貪者是三界繫？此中何法名三界貪？謂三界中各隨增者，今此所言同縛馬答。猶如有問縛馬者誰？答言馬主。即彼復問馬主是誰？答言縛者。如是二答皆不令解。今此所言不同彼答，謂於前說欲界諸處未離貪者，貪名欲貪，此所隨增名欲界繫。於前所說色無色中，隨其所應當知亦爾。或不定地貪名欲貪，此所隨增名欲界繫。諸靜慮地貪名色貪，此所隨增名色界繫。諸無色地貪名無色貪，此所隨增名為無色界繫。於欲化心上如何起欲貪？從他所聞，或自退失生愛味故。或觀化者自在勢力，於彼化心生貪愛故。若心能化香味二法，此能化心是欲界繫，色界心不能化作香味故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a dharma currently manifests within one of the three realms, is that dharma thereby said to be bound to that realm? No, it is not. How, then, should it be put? A dharma with respect to which the craving belonging to one of the three realms lies latent and increases — that is what is bound to that realm. What, then, is meant here by "the craving of the three realms"? If one simply says, "it is whatever lies latent and increases within each of the three realms," this answer is like the answer given about who ties up the horse: it is just as if someone asked, "Who is it that ties up the horse?" and were answered, "The horse's owner." If that person then asked further, "Who, then, is the horse's owner?" and were answered, "The one who ties it up" — both of these answers fail to actually clarify anything. What is said here, however, is not like that circular answer: that is, with respect to the desire realm's various places already discussed, for one who has not yet become free of craving, the craving is called desire-craving, and whatever this craving lies latent in and increases upon is called "bound to the desire realm." With respect to the form and formless realms already discussed, it should be understood, correspondingly, to work in just the same way. Alternatively: craving belonging to the unfixed level is called desire-craving, and whatever this lies latent in and increases upon is called "bound to the desire realm." Craving belonging to any of the dhyāna levels is called form-craving, and whatever this lies latent in and increases upon is called "bound to the form realm." Craving belonging to any of the formless levels is called formless-craving, and whatever this lies latent in and increases upon is called "bound to the formless realm." How, then, can desire-craving arise with respect to a desire-realm transformation-mind? Either through hearing it from another, or through one's own falling back and regressing, a taste for it arises; or, through observing the sovereign power of the one who performs the transformation, craving and affection arise with respect to that transformation-mind. If a mind is able to transform and produce the two dharmas of fragrance and flavor, this transformation-capable mind is bound to the desire realm, since a form-realm mind cannot transform and produce fragrance or flavor.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The horse-and-owner joke is doing real philosophical work, not just providing color: it exposes a specific failure mode in definitions — two terms defined purely in terms of each other, with neither one ever cashed out against anything independent, leave a questioner exactly as much in the dark as before asking. The treatise's own answer avoids this trap by anchoring "boundness" to something independently specified: the presence of latent, still-unabandoned craving of a particular kind (desire-craving, form-craving, or formless-craving), which is itself defined by which level of attainment it belongs to, not by circularly appealing back to "realm" again.

The closing puzzle is a nice small illustration of how desire can attach even to something produced by advanced meditative mastery: a transformation-mind is, in itself, a highly cultivated uncovered-neutral state, yet ordinary desire-realm craving can still fasten onto it — through hearsay, through one's own regression, or through admiration of the transforming being's power — showing that even a spiritually rarefied object offers no automatic immunity from becoming an object of craving for someone whose own mind is not yet free of it. The closing technical detail (a mind able to manufacture fragrance and flavor must itself be desire-realm-bound) follows directly from the fact, established elsewhere in this tradition's cosmology, that form-realm existence simply lacks smell and taste altogether.

9. One Universe, or Infinitely Many?

The three-realms discussion closes on a genuinely cosmic scale: world-systems are without number, arranged side by side rather than stacked, endlessly forming and dissolving in every direction — and becoming free of craving for one's own desire realm turns out to sever craving for every desire realm everywhere at once.

Chinese Source

如是三界唯有一耶？三界無邊如虛空量，故雖無有始起有情，無量無邊佛出於世，一一化度無數有情令證無餘般涅槃界而不窮盡猶若虛空。世界當言云何安住？當言傍住，故契經言：譬如天雨滴如車軸，無間無斷從空下澍。如是東方無間無斷無量世界或壞或成，如於東方，南西北方亦復如是，不說上下。有說亦有上下二方，餘部經中說十方故。色究竟上復有欲界，於欲界下有色究竟。若有離一欲界貪時，諸欲界貪皆得滅離。離色無色應知亦爾。依初靜慮起通慧時，所發神通但能往至自所生界梵世非餘，所餘通慧應知亦爾。已說三界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Are these three realms, then, only a single set — is there only one universe? The three realms are without limit, measureless like space itself; hence, although sentient beings have no beginning, and immeasurable, boundless Buddhas have appeared in the world, each one converting and liberating countless sentient beings and bringing them to realize the sphere of nirvana without remainder, this is still never exhausted, just like space itself. How, then, should it be said that world-systems are arranged? It should be said that they are arranged side by side. Hence a sutra states: "It is like rain from the sky, its drops as thick as cart-axles, falling without interruption or break." In just this way, to the east, without interruption or break, immeasurable world-systems are either being destroyed or being formed; and just as in the east, so too in the south, west, and north — but nothing is said of above or below. There is a further view that there are also the two directions of above and below, since the sutras of other schools speak of the ten directions. Above the Heaven of the Ultimate Form, there is once again a desire realm; and below that desire realm, there is once again a Heaven of the Ultimate Form. When one becomes free of craving for one desire realm, one thereby becomes free of craving for every desire realm at once. Becoming free of craving for the form and formless realms should be understood in just the same way. When one relies on the first dhyāna to give rise to supernormal wisdom, the supernormal power thereby produced can only travel as far as the Brahmā-world of the very universe into which one was born, and no further; the same should be understood to hold for supernormal wisdom relying on the other dhyānas as well. The three realms have now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rain analogy is a genuinely striking image for an inconceivably vast cosmology: just as raindrops fall continuously, one after another without any gap, an unbroken succession of world-systems is likewise always somewhere forming and somewhere dissolving, stretching outward endlessly in every horizontal direction. The treatise's own position keeps this expansion horizontal only — east, south, west, north — while noting, without adopting, a rival view from other schools that would extend it vertically as well, into a full ten directions.

The claim that severing craving for one desire realm severs craving for every desire realm everywhere is a striking universalizing move, and worth sitting with: it suggests that what liberation actually removes is never a craving for this particular instance of desire-realm existence, but craving for the type itself, wherever and however many times it might recur across an infinite cosmos — a liberation that is, in this specific sense, already cosmic in scope the moment it occurs even once. The closing detail about supernormal power's range works as a fitting counterpoint: however vast the universe truly is, a given meditator's own supernormal reach remains sharply local, bounded by the single universe of their own birth and never extending into any of the countless others.

10. A New Topic: The Five Destinies, and Why They Must Be Neutral

A new topic within the same chapter: the five destinies of rebirth — hell, animal, ghost, human, and god — and a precise argument for why their essential nature can only ever be uncovered-neutral, never wholesome or defiled, on pain of the whole five-way scheme collapsing into confusion.

Chinese Source

五趣云何？頌曰：於中地獄等，自名說五趣，唯無覆無記，有情非中有。論曰：於三界中說有五趣，即地獄等如自名說，謂前所說地獄、傍生、鬼及人、天是名五趣。唯於欲界有四趣全，三界各有天趣一分。為有三界非趣所攝，而於界中說有五趣？有，謂善染外器中有，雖是界性而非趣攝。五趣體唯無覆無記，若異此者趣應相雜，於一趣中具有五趣業煩惱故。五趣唯是有情數攝，體非中有。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, then, are the five destinies? Verse: within these realms, the hells and the rest, each under its own name, are said to be the five destinies; their nature is only uncovered-neutral; they belong to sentient beings, not to the intermediate-existence being. Prose: within the three realms, there are said to be five destinies — namely, the hells and the rest, each spoken of under its own name: that is, the previously-discussed hells, animals, and ghosts, together with humans and gods, are called the five destinies. Only in the desire realm are four of the destinies found in their entirety; the god-destiny, however, has one portion in each of the three realms. Is there, then, anything within the three realms that is not subsumed under any destiny, even though the five destinies are said to exist within these realms? There is: namely, wholesome and defiled dharmas, the external receptacle world, and the intermediate-existence being — although these all belong to the nature of the three realms, they are not subsumed under any destiny. The essential nature of the five destinies is only uncovered-neutral. If it were otherwise, the destinies would become mixed together, since a single destiny would then contain within it the karma and afflictions belonging to all five destinies at once. The five destinies are subsumed only under what belongs to the category of sentient beings; their essential nature is not the intermediate-existence being.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The god-destiny's status as "one portion in each of the three realms" is a nice small piece of bookkeeping: unlike the other four destinies, which belong entirely to the desire realm, "god" is really an umbrella term spanning the desire realm's six heavens, the form realm's seventeen, and the formless realm's four spheres all at once — a single destiny stretched across the entire cosmological map this fascicle has just finished surveying.

The core argument for why destiny must be uncovered-neutral is a clean piece of conceptual hygiene: karma and afflictions are not tied to any one destiny in particular — a human can carry hell-bound karma, a god can carry afflictions that would otherwise produce a lower rebirth — so if "destiny" itself were allowed to be wholesome or defiled in nature, every destiny's continuum would end up carrying traces of every other destiny's moral coloring, and the clean five-way distinction the whole scheme exists to draw would simply dissolve. Keeping destiny strictly neutral is what lets it name a stable category (this is what a hell-being's aggregates simply are) that karma and affliction can then act upon, rather than collapsing destiny and moral quality into one another.

11. Scriptural Support: The Seven Kinds of Existence

Two Abhidharma treatises and a sutra are cited in support: the intermediate-existence being and karma-existence are both explicitly distinguished from the five destinies proper, confirming that destiny names something narrower and more specific than "existence" in general.

Chinese Source

《施設足論》作如是說：四生攝五趣，非五攝四生。不攝者何？所謂中有。《法蘊足論》亦作是言：眼界云何？謂四大種所造淨色是眼，眼根、眼處、眼界。地獄、傍生、鬼、人、天趣，修成中有。契經亦簡中有異趣。是何契經？謂《七有經》。彼說七有，謂地獄有、傍生有、餓鬼有、天有、人有、業有、中有。彼經中說五趣及因并趣方便，故趣唯是無覆無記，其理極成。簡業有因，異諸趣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Prajñaptiśāstra states as follows: the four kinds of birth subsume the five destinies, but the five destinies do not subsume the four kinds of birth. What is it, then, that is not subsumed? Namely, the intermediate-existence being. The Dharmaskandha-śāstra likewise states: what is the eye-element? It is the pure form produced by the four great elements that constitutes the eye — the eye-faculty, the eye-sense-field, and the eye-element — found among the destinies of hell-beings, animals, ghosts, humans, and gods, and also cultivated and completed in the intermediate-existence being. A sutra, too, distinguishes the intermediate-existence being from the destinies. Which sutra is this? The Sūtra of the Seven Kinds of Existence. That sutra speaks of seven kinds of existence: hell-existence, animal-existence, hungry-ghost-existence, god-existence, human-existence, karma-existence, and intermediate-existence. That sutra, in speaking of the five destinies together with their cause and the means by which one arrives at a destiny, thereby firmly establishes the principle that destiny is only uncovered-neutral. Karma-existence, functioning as the cause, is distinguished from the destinies precisely because it is different in kind from every one of them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The argument's logic is worth spelling out: if a canonical sutra already lists "karma-existence" and "intermediate-existence" as separate items alongside the five destinies proper, rather than folding them into one of the five, this is treated as good evidence that the tradition itself already recognized destiny as a narrower category than "existence" in general — specifically, one that excludes exactly the two things (karma, which can be wholesome or unwholesome, and the intermediate being, which is not yet settled into any destiny) that would otherwise threaten the "destiny is purely neutral" principle. The two treatise citations reinforce the same point from a different angle, both explicitly naming the intermediate-existence being as something distinct from, and not subsumed by, the five destinies it stands between.
