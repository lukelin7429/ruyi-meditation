Where Does a Formless Being Live?

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 8, beginning Chapter 3 (分別世品第三之一, the Loka-nirdeśa, "Discrimination of the World") with a sweeping cosmological survey: the desire realm's twenty places, the form realm's seventeen heavens, and the formless realm's striking claim to occupy no spatial location at all. From there the fascicle works through the five destinies, the seven and nine abodes of consciousness and sentient beings, the four kinds of birth, and closes with one of this treatise's most extensive debates — a sustained argument, combining logic and scripture, for why the intermediate-existence being between death and rebirth must genuinely exist.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 8 complete, 27 sections Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 8 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. A New Chapter: How Many Places Does Each Realm Contain?

Chapter 3 opens with a compact verse laying out the whole cosmological map at once: the desire realm's twenty places, the form realm's seventeen, and the formless realm's complete lack of any place, distinguished instead by four kinds of birth.

Chinese Source

已依三界分別心等。今次應說，三界是何？各於其中處別有幾？頌曰：地獄傍生鬼，人及六欲天，名欲界二十，由地獄洲異。此上十七處，名色界於中，三靜慮各三，第四靜慮八。無色界無處，由生有四種，依同分及命，令心等相續。

Ru-Yi Translation

Mind and the rest have already been discussed by reference to the three realms. It should next be explained: what, exactly, are the three realms, and how many distinct places does each contain? Verse: the hells, animals, and hungry ghosts, together with humans and the six desire-heavens, are called the desire realm's twenty — owing to the distinctions among the hells and the continents. Above these are seventeen places called the form realm, within which the first three dhyānas each have three, and the fourth dhyāna has eight. The formless realm has no place at all; owing to birth, it is of four kinds — relying on class-commonality and the life faculty to sustain the continuity of mind and the rest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This verse packs the entire cosmological skeleton of Chapter 3 into six lines, and the prose that follows over the next several sections will unpack every piece of it: the desire realm's twenty places (hells, continents, heavens, animals, and hungry ghosts all combined), the form realm's seventeen meditative heavens stacked in four tiers, and the formless realm's genuinely striking claim to have no place whatsoever — a claim significant enough that the treatise immediately raises, and takes seriously, the question of what could possibly hold a formless being's mind together across time without any physical basis at all. That question, flagged already in this opening verse's closing line, becomes one of this fascicle's most philosophically interesting discussions.

2. The Desire Realm's Twenty Places

The desire realm's twenty places are enumerated in full: the six heavens by name, the eight great hells by name, and the four continents by name — twelve locations in all, plus the heavens, animals, and hungry ghosts making twenty.

Chinese Source

論曰：地獄等四及六欲天并器世間，是名欲界。六欲天者，一、四大王眾天；二、三十三天；三、夜摩天；四、覩史多天；五、樂變化天；六、他化自在天。如是欲界處別有幾？地獄洲異，故成二十。八大地獄，名地獄異：一等活地獄、二黑繩地獄、三眾合地獄、四號叫地獄、五大叫地獄、六炎熱地獄、七大熱地獄、八無間地獄。言洲異者，謂四大洲：一南贍部洲、二東勝身洲、三西牛貨洲、四北俱盧洲。如是十二并六欲天、傍生、餓鬼處成二十。若有情界從自在天至無間獄，若器世界乃至風輪，皆欲界攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Prose: the four destinies beginning with the hells, together with the six desire-heavens and the receptacle world that contains them, are called the desire realm. The six desire-heavens are: first, the Heaven of the Four Great Kings; second, the Heaven of the Thirty-Three; third, the Yama Heaven; fourth, the Tuṣita Heaven; fifth, the Heaven of Enjoying Transformations; sixth, the Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations. How many distinct places, then, does the desire realm contain, in detail? Owing to the distinctions among the hells and the continents, there come to be twenty in all. The eight great hells are called the hells' distinction: first, the Reviving Hell; second, the Black-Rope Hell; third, the Crushing-Together Hell; fourth, the Wailing Hell; fifth, the Great Wailing Hell; sixth, the Scorching Hell; seventh, the Great Scorching Hell; eighth, the Hell of Uninterrupted Suffering. As for the continents' distinction: there are four great continents: first, Jambudvīpa; second, Pūrvavideha; third, Aparagodānīya; fourth, Uttarakuru. These twelve, together with the six desire-heavens, the animals, and the hungry ghosts, make twenty. Whether one speaks of the world of sentient beings, from the Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations down to the Hell of Uninterrupted Suffering, or of the receptacle world, down to the wheel of wind, all of it is subsumed under the desire realm.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The count of twenty is reached by treating each hell and each continent as its own distinct "place" while treating the six heavens, the animals, and the hungry ghosts each as a single place apiece: eight hells plus four continents makes twelve locations tied to Jambudvīpa-style geography, and adding the six heavens, one place for all animals everywhere, and one place for all hungry ghosts everywhere brings the total to twenty. The closing line is worth noting for how expansively "desire realm" is being used here: it covers not just the beings inhabiting these twenty places, but the entire physical receptacle-world itself, all the way down to the foundational "wheel of wind" this tradition holds the physical universe ultimately rests upon.

3. The Form Realm's Seventeen Heavens, and a Kashmiri Recount to Sixteen

The form realm's seventeen places are named in full across its four meditative tiers — before the Kashmiri masters offer a striking correction: the Great Brahma Heaven, they argue, is not a separate place at all, but merely a tower within the heaven just below it.

Chinese Source

此欲界上處有十七，謂三靜慮處各有三，第四靜慮處獨有八，器及有情總名色界。第一靜慮處有三者，一梵眾天、二梵輔天、三大梵天。第二靜慮處有三者，一少光天、二無量光天、三極光淨天。第三靜慮處有三者，一少淨天、二無量淨天、三遍淨天。第四靜慮處有八者，一無雲天、二福生天、三廣果天、四無煩天、五無熱天、六善現天、七善見天、八色究竟天。迦濕彌羅國諸大論師皆言：色界處但有十六。彼謂：即於梵輔天處有高臺閣，名大梵天，一主所居非有別地，如尊處座四眾圍繞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Above the desire realm there are seventeen places: the first three dhyāna-levels each have three, and the fourth dhyāna-level alone has eight; the receptacle world and its inhabitants together are called the form realm. The first dhyāna-level's three are: first, the Heaven of Brahma's Retinue; second, the Heaven of Brahma's Ministers; third, the Great Brahma Heaven. The second dhyāna-level's three are: first, the Heaven of Lesser Light; second, the Heaven of Immeasurable Light; third, the Heaven of Light-Sound. The third dhyāna-level's three are: first, the Heaven of Lesser Purity; second, the Heaven of Immeasurable Purity; third, the Heaven of All-Pervasive Purity. The fourth dhyāna-level's eight are: first, the Cloudless Heaven; second, the Heaven of Merit-Born Beings; third, the Heaven of Great Reward; fourth, the Heaven Without Affliction; fifth, the Heaven Without Heat; sixth, the Heaven of Skillful Appearance; seventh, the Heaven of Skillful Vision; eighth, the Heaven of the Ultimate Form. The great treatise-masters of Kashmir all say that the form realm has only sixteen places: they hold that, within the Heaven of Brahma's Ministers, there is a lofty tower-pavilion called the Great Brahma Heaven — a single ruler's dwelling place, not a genuinely separate ground of its own, comparable to a venerable one's throne encircled by the four assemblies of disciples.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four tiers map neatly onto the four dhyānas (meditative absorptions) that produce rebirth there: three refinements each for the first three dhyānas, tracking increasingly subtle degrees of the same basic meditative attainment, and a more elaborate eightfold division for the fourth dhyāna, the most refined level, which this tradition treats as spiritually rich enough to house several distinct sub-categories of being — including, notably, the five "pure abodes" (implicitly among the last five named) reserved specifically for non-returners on their way to final liberation.

The Kashmiri masters' recount to sixteen is a nice small example of this tradition's willingness to treat cosmological claims as genuinely open questions rather than settled dogma: their proposed image — a single great ruler occupying an elevated tower within his ministers' own domain, rather than a wholly separate world-level unto himself — is offered as a matter of comparison to ordinary court life, a king's throne surrounded by attendants rather than a king inhabiting a separate kingdom. I have not found a resolution of this dispute later in the text and present both counts as the treatise itself does, without picking a winner.

4. The Formless Realm Has No Place At All

The treatise's most striking cosmological claim: the formless realm occupies no spatial location whatsoever, its four levels distinguished purely by the kind of birth attained — proven by a genuinely clever observation about where rebirth and the intermediate-existence being actually arise.

Chinese Source

無色界中都無有處，以無色法無有方所，過去未來無表無色不住方所，理決然故。但異熟生，差別有四：一空無邊處、二識無邊處、三無所有處、四非想非非想處，如是四種名無色界。此四非由處有上下，但由生故勝劣有殊。復如何知彼無方處？謂於是處得彼定者，命終即於是處生故。復從彼沒生欲色時，即於是處中有起故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the formless realm, there is no place at all, since formless dharmas have no spatial location — past and future unmanifest form, and formless dharmas generally, do not reside in any location; this is a matter of settled principle. But, based on the distinction in what karmic-ripening is produced, there are four kinds: first, the sphere of infinite space; second, the sphere of infinite consciousness; third, the sphere of nothing-at-all; fourth, the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. These four kinds are called the formless realm. These four are not distinguished by any higher or lower spatial location, but rather by birth itself, through which there is a difference of superior and inferior. How, moreover, is it known that these have no spatial location at all? Because whoever attains that concentration in a given place dies and is reborn in that very same place; and, when passing away from that state to be reborn in the desire or form realm, the intermediate-existence being arises in that very same place as well.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The proof is genuinely elegant: if the formless spheres were real places the way the form-realm heavens are, then dying into one, or being reborn out of one back into the desire or form realm, would require the mind to travel somewhere — to arrive at, or depart from, an actual location. But what is observed instead is that rebirth and the intermediate-existence being both simply occur right where the person already was, physically, at the moment of death — no journey to or from any formless "place" ever takes place, because there is no such place to journey to or from. The four spheres are thus purely qualitative levels of attainment, differentiated only by how refined the birth is, with no spatial hierarchy stacking one above another the way the form realm's four dhyāna-tiers do.

5. Without a Body, What Holds a Formless Mind Together?

A genuinely pointed question follows directly from the formless realm having no body at all: what sustains the continuity of mind across time for a being with no physical basis whatsoever? The Vaibhāṣika answer runs into what looks like real circularity, before the Sautrāntika offers a leaner alternative.

Chinese Source

如有色界一切有情，要依色身心等相續。於無色界受生有情，以何為依心等相續？對法諸師說彼心等依眾同分及與命根而得相續。若爾，有色有情心等，何不但依此二相續？有色界生此二劣故。無色此二因何故強？彼界二從勝定生故，由彼等至能伏色想。若爾，於彼心等相續，但依勝定，何用別依？又今應說，如有色界受生有情同分命根依色而轉，無色此二以何為依？此二更互相依而轉。有色此二何不相依？有色界生此二劣故。無色此二因何故強？彼界此二種，從勝定生故。前說彼定能伏色想，是則還同心相續難。或心心所唯互相依，故經部師說無色界心等相續無別有依。謂若有因未離色愛引起心等，所引心等與色俱生依色而轉。若因於色已得離愛，厭背色故，所引心等非色俱生不依色轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as every sentient being possessing form necessarily relies upon a physical body for the continuity of mind and the rest, for a sentient being reborn in the formless realm, what does the continuity of mind and the rest rely upon as its basis? The Ābhidharmika masters say that mind and the rest, for such beings, achieve continuity by relying upon class-commonality and the life faculty. If that is so, then why do sentient beings possessing form not likewise rely on only these two alone for the continuity of their mind and the rest? Because, for beings born with form, these two are of inferior strength. Why, then, are these same two strong in the formless realm? Because, in that realm, these two arise from a superior concentration, since that attainment is able to subdue the perception of form. If that is so, then, since it is the superior concentration itself that explains their continuity, what need is there for any further, separate basis at all? Moreover, it should now be asked: just as, for a sentient being born with form, class-commonality and the life faculty operate in dependence on form, in the formless realm, what do these same two rely upon as their basis? These two operate by relying on each other, mutually. Why, then, do these two not likewise rely on each other in the case of beings possessing form? Because, for beings born with form, these two are of inferior strength. Why, then, are these same two strong in the formless realm? Because, in that realm, these two kinds arise from a superior concentration. But it was already said that this attainment is able to subdue the perception of form — and this simply returns us to the very same difficulty already raised about the continuity of mind. Alternatively, mind and its mental factors simply rely on each other alone — and so the Sautrāntika masters say that, in the formless realm, the continuity of mind and the rest requires no separate, additional basis at all. That is: if there is a cause whereby one has not yet become free of affection for form, and mind and the rest are thereby brought about, then this induced mind and the rest arise together with form, and operate in dependence on form. But if, through some cause, one has already become free of affection toward form, being wearied of and turned away from form, then this induced mind and the rest do not arise together with form, and do not operate in dependence on form.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag honestly, rather than smooth over, what looks like a genuine circularity in the Ābhidharmika position as the text presents it. Pressed on why class-commonality and the life faculty are strong enough to sustain mind on their own in the formless realm, the answer given is that they arise from a superior concentration; but pressed again on what class-commonality and the life faculty themselves rely on in the formless realm (since they can no longer rely on a body, as they do everywhere else), the answer is that they rely on each other — and when this, too, is challenged by appeal to the very same "superior concentration" explanation, the text itself remarks that this simply returns to the same difficulty already raised. The dialectic does not resolve this loop; it is left standing, and the Sautrāntika position is then offered as a different way of avoiding the problem altogether rather than as a direct refutation of it.

The Sautrāntika alternative is a clean example of this school's characteristic move throughout this translation project: rather than positing additional real entities (class-commonality, life faculty) that then themselves need some further basis, they simply observe that mind and its mental factors already sustain each other everywhere, in every realm, without needing anything extra — and what actually distinguishes a desire- or form-realm mind-stream from a formless one is nothing more than whether affection for form is still present in the causal chain producing it. Where that affection remains, mind arises bound up with form; where it has been abandoned through weariness and turning-away, mind simply continues on its own, needing no body and no additional metaphysical scaffolding to explain how.

6. Why Are They Called "Desire Realm," "Form Realm," "Formless Realm"?

A short etymological digression explains the three realms' names through this tradition's standard elision convention — "X-realm" really means "the realm belonging to X" — and adds a second, complementary reading: a realm is also named for its own power to sustain and hold what belongs to it.

Chinese Source

何故名為欲等三界？能持自相故名為界。或種族義，如前已釋。欲所屬界說名欲界，色所屬界說名色界，略去中言故作是說，如胡椒飲如金剛環。於彼界中色非有故名為無色。所言色者，是變礙義，或示現義。彼體非色立無色名，非彼但用色無為體。無色所屬界，說名無色界，略去中言，喻如前說。又欲之界名為欲界，此界力能任持欲故。色無色界應知亦然。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are these called the desire realm, and so forth, the three realms? Because it is able to hold its own particular characteristic, it is called a "realm" — or, in the sense of "lineage," as already explained before. The realm to which desire belongs is called the desire realm; the realm to which form belongs is called the form realm — the middle word being elided in this manner of speaking, just as "pepper-drink" means a drink flavored with pepper, and "vajra-ring" means a ring set with a vajra-gem. Within that realm, form does not exist at all — hence it is called "formless." What is meant by "form" here is the sense of resistant obstruction, or the sense of manifestation and display. Since that realm's own essential nature is simply not form, the name "formless" is established for it — it is not that this realm's essence is merely "the absence of form" taken as its defining substance. The realm to which formlessness belongs is called the formless realm — the middle word being elided, by the same analogy already given. Moreover, "the realm of desire" is called the desire realm because this realm's own power is able to sustain and hold desire. The form realm and the formless realm should be understood in just the same way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two readings offered here are worth distinguishing clearly, since they trade on two different senses "realm" (界, dhātu) can carry, both already introduced earlier in this translation project. On the first reading, "desire realm" is simply shorthand for "the realm to which desire belongs" — an elided possessive, exactly like "pepper-drink" standing in for "a drink flavored with pepper." On the second, "realm" is read through its more basic sense of "that which holds or sustains its own particular characteristic," so "desire realm" instead means "the realm whose own power sustains and holds desire" — a realm defined by what it actively supports, not merely by what happens to belong to it. Both readings arrive at the same three names, but they locate the defining relationship in different places: one in desire's tie to the realm, the other in the realm's own causal power over desire.

The clarification about "formless" deserves a second look, too: the text is careful to insist that "formless" names something with its own positive nature that simply is not form, rather than a purely negative, parasitic label meaning nothing more than "wherever form happens to be missing." This matters because a merely negative definition would make the formless realm a kind of leftover category with no positive character of its own — exactly the sort of move this tradition otherwise resists when it wants to treat something as a genuine, distinct kind of existence.

7. What Is "Desire," Really? A Verse Debate with a Heretic

A genuinely sharp philosophical exchange, staged as a confrontation between an Ājīvika heretic and the Venerable Śāriputra: is desire the enticing object itself, or the mind's own discriminating craving toward it? Śāriputra's rebuttal turns the heretic's own challenge back on him with precision.

Chinese Source

此中欲言為說何法？略說段食婬所引貪。如經頌言：世諸妙境非真欲，真欲謂人分別貪，妙境如本住世間，智者於中已除欲。邪命外道便詰尊者舍利子言：若世妙境非真欲，說欲是人分別貪，比丘應名受欲人，起惡分別尋思故。時舍利子反質彼言：若世妙境是真欲，說欲非人分別貪，汝師應名受欲人，恒觀可意妙色故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, exactly, does the word "desire" refer to here? Briefly stated: the craving induced by physical nourishment and by sexual union. As a scriptural verse states: "The world's wondrous objects are not true desire; true desire is a person's own discriminating craving. The wondrous objects remain in the world just as they always were; the wise, with respect to them, have already removed desire." An Ājīvika heretic then challenged the Venerable Śāriputra, saying: "If the world's wondrous objects are not true desire, and desire is said to be a person's own discriminating craving, then a monk ought to be called one who indulges in desire, since he too gives rise to unwholesome discriminating thought." Śāriputra then countered him in return, saying: "If the world's wondrous objects were true desire, and desire were not a person's own discriminating craving, then your own teacher ought to be called one who indulges in desire, since he constantly gazes upon agreeable, wondrous forms."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The heretic's challenge is a genuine "gotcha" attempt: if desire is defined as the mind's own discriminating craving rather than the external object, then even a monk, who inevitably still notices and mentally registers pleasant things from time to time, would seem to count as "indulging in desire" merely by having any discriminating thought about them at all — an absurd consequence for someone supposedly free of desire. Śāriputra's reply does not deny the challenge's logical form; it mirrors it back with the opposite premise. If desire were instead simply identified with the wondrous object itself, as the heretic's framing implicitly requires for the challenge to land, then anyone who merely looks at something beautiful — including, pointedly, the heretic's own teacher — would equally have to count as indulging in desire, merely by ordinary seeing. Since that consequence is obviously worse (it would make involuntary sense-perception itself a moral failing), the exchange decisively favors the scripture's original position: desire is the discriminating craving a mind brings to an object, never the object on its own.

I have translated 邪命外道 as "an Ājīvika heretic" — the Ājīvikas were a real, historically attested rival ascetic movement contemporary with early Buddhism, associated with a strict fatalism about karma, and this passage is likely a genuine record, however stylized, of a specific historical or semi-historical polemical encounter rather than a purely hypothetical interlocutor.

8. Being "Bound" to a Realm Is Not the Same as Being Present in It

A precise clarification, illustrated with a memorable circular-definition joke about a horse and its owner: merely manifesting within a realm does not make a dharma bound to it — boundness tracks specifically the latent craving that lies dormant and increases there. A related puzzle closes the topic: how can desire even arise toward a magically-created object?

Chinese Source

若法於彼三界現行，此法即說三界繫不？不爾。云何於中隨增三界貪者是三界繫？此中何法名三界貪？謂三界中各隨增者，今此所言同縛馬答。猶如有問縛馬者誰？答言馬主。即彼復問馬主是誰？答言縛者。如是二答皆不令解。今此所言不同彼答，謂於前說欲界諸處未離貪者，貪名欲貪，此所隨增名欲界繫。於前所說色無色中，隨其所應當知亦爾。或不定地貪名欲貪，此所隨增名欲界繫。諸靜慮地貪名色貪，此所隨增名色界繫。諸無色地貪名無色貪，此所隨增名為無色界繫。於欲化心上如何起欲貪？從他所聞，或自退失生愛味故。或觀化者自在勢力，於彼化心生貪愛故。若心能化香味二法，此能化心是欲界繫，色界心不能化作香味故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a dharma currently manifests within one of the three realms, is that dharma thereby said to be bound to that realm? No, it is not. How, then, should it be put? A dharma with respect to which the craving belonging to one of the three realms lies latent and increases — that is what is bound to that realm. What, then, is meant here by "the craving of the three realms"? If one simply says, "it is whatever lies latent and increases within each of the three realms," this answer is like the answer given about who ties up the horse: it is just as if someone asked, "Who is it that ties up the horse?" and were answered, "The horse's owner." If that person then asked further, "Who, then, is the horse's owner?" and were answered, "The one who ties it up" — both of these answers fail to actually clarify anything. What is said here, however, is not like that circular answer: that is, with respect to the desire realm's various places already discussed, for one who has not yet become free of craving, the craving is called desire-craving, and whatever this craving lies latent in and increases upon is called "bound to the desire realm." With respect to the form and formless realms already discussed, it should be understood, correspondingly, to work in just the same way. Alternatively: craving belonging to the unfixed level is called desire-craving, and whatever this lies latent in and increases upon is called "bound to the desire realm." Craving belonging to any of the dhyāna levels is called form-craving, and whatever this lies latent in and increases upon is called "bound to the form realm." Craving belonging to any of the formless levels is called formless-craving, and whatever this lies latent in and increases upon is called "bound to the formless realm." How, then, can desire-craving arise with respect to a desire-realm transformation-mind? Either through hearing it from another, or through one's own falling back and regressing, a taste for it arises; or, through observing the sovereign power of the one who performs the transformation, craving and affection arise with respect to that transformation-mind. If a mind is able to transform and produce the two dharmas of fragrance and flavor, this transformation-capable mind is bound to the desire realm, since a form-realm mind cannot transform and produce fragrance or flavor.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The horse-and-owner joke is doing real philosophical work, not just providing color: it exposes a specific failure mode in definitions — two terms defined purely in terms of each other, with neither one ever cashed out against anything independent, leave a questioner exactly as much in the dark as before asking. The treatise's own answer avoids this trap by anchoring "boundness" to something independently specified: the presence of latent, still-unabandoned craving of a particular kind (desire-craving, form-craving, or formless-craving), which is itself defined by which level of attainment it belongs to, not by circularly appealing back to "realm" again.

The closing puzzle is a nice small illustration of how desire can attach even to something produced by advanced meditative mastery: a transformation-mind is, in itself, a highly cultivated uncovered-neutral state, yet ordinary desire-realm craving can still fasten onto it — through hearsay, through one's own regression, or through admiration of the transforming being's power — showing that even a spiritually rarefied object offers no automatic immunity from becoming an object of craving for someone whose own mind is not yet free of it. The closing technical detail (a mind able to manufacture fragrance and flavor must itself be desire-realm-bound) follows directly from the fact, established elsewhere in this tradition's cosmology, that form-realm existence simply lacks smell and taste altogether.

9. One Universe, or Infinitely Many?

The three-realms discussion closes on a genuinely cosmic scale: world-systems are without number, arranged side by side rather than stacked, endlessly forming and dissolving in every direction — and becoming free of craving for one's own desire realm turns out to sever craving for every desire realm everywhere at once.

Chinese Source

如是三界唯有一耶？三界無邊如虛空量，故雖無有始起有情，無量無邊佛出於世，一一化度無數有情令證無餘般涅槃界而不窮盡猶若虛空。世界當言云何安住？當言傍住，故契經言：譬如天雨滴如車軸，無間無斷從空下澍。如是東方無間無斷無量世界或壞或成，如於東方，南西北方亦復如是，不說上下。有說亦有上下二方，餘部經中說十方故。色究竟上復有欲界，於欲界下有色究竟。若有離一欲界貪時，諸欲界貪皆得滅離。離色無色應知亦爾。依初靜慮起通慧時，所發神通但能往至自所生界梵世非餘，所餘通慧應知亦爾。已說三界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Are these three realms, then, only a single set — is there only one universe? The three realms are without limit, measureless like space itself; hence, although sentient beings have no beginning, and immeasurable, boundless Buddhas have appeared in the world, each one converting and liberating countless sentient beings and bringing them to realize the sphere of nirvana without remainder, this is still never exhausted, just like space itself. How, then, should it be said that world-systems are arranged? It should be said that they are arranged side by side. Hence a sutra states: "It is like rain from the sky, its drops as thick as cart-axles, falling without interruption or break." In just this way, to the east, without interruption or break, immeasurable world-systems are either being destroyed or being formed; and just as in the east, so too in the south, west, and north — but nothing is said of above or below. There is a further view that there are also the two directions of above and below, since the sutras of other schools speak of the ten directions. Above the Heaven of the Ultimate Form, there is once again a desire realm; and below that desire realm, there is once again a Heaven of the Ultimate Form. When one becomes free of craving for one desire realm, one thereby becomes free of craving for every desire realm at once. Becoming free of craving for the form and formless realms should be understood in just the same way. When one relies on the first dhyāna to give rise to supernormal wisdom, the supernormal power thereby produced can only travel as far as the Brahmā-world of the very universe into which one was born, and no further; the same should be understood to hold for supernormal wisdom relying on the other dhyānas as well. The three realms have now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rain analogy is a genuinely striking image for an inconceivably vast cosmology: just as raindrops fall continuously, one after another without any gap, an unbroken succession of world-systems is likewise always somewhere forming and somewhere dissolving, stretching outward endlessly in every horizontal direction. The treatise's own position keeps this expansion horizontal only — east, south, west, north — while noting, without adopting, a rival view from other schools that would extend it vertically as well, into a full ten directions.

The claim that severing craving for one desire realm severs craving for every desire realm everywhere is a striking universalizing move, and worth sitting with: it suggests that what liberation actually removes is never a craving for this particular instance of desire-realm existence, but craving for the type itself, wherever and however many times it might recur across an infinite cosmos — a liberation that is, in this specific sense, already cosmic in scope the moment it occurs even once. The closing detail about supernormal power's range works as a fitting counterpoint: however vast the universe truly is, a given meditator's own supernormal reach remains sharply local, bounded by the single universe of their own birth and never extending into any of the countless others.

10. A New Topic: The Five Destinies, and Why They Must Be Neutral

A new topic within the same chapter: the five destinies of rebirth — hell, animal, ghost, human, and god — and a precise argument for why their essential nature can only ever be uncovered-neutral, never wholesome or defiled, on pain of the whole five-way scheme collapsing into confusion.

Chinese Source

五趣云何？頌曰：於中地獄等，自名說五趣，唯無覆無記，有情非中有。論曰：於三界中說有五趣，即地獄等如自名說，謂前所說地獄、傍生、鬼及人、天是名五趣。唯於欲界有四趣全，三界各有天趣一分。為有三界非趣所攝，而於界中說有五趣？有，謂善染外器中有，雖是界性而非趣攝。五趣體唯無覆無記，若異此者趣應相雜，於一趣中具有五趣業煩惱故。五趣唯是有情數攝，體非中有。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, then, are the five destinies? Verse: within these realms, the hells and the rest, each under its own name, are said to be the five destinies; their nature is only uncovered-neutral; they belong to sentient beings, not to the intermediate-existence being. Prose: within the three realms, there are said to be five destinies — namely, the hells and the rest, each spoken of under its own name: that is, the previously-discussed hells, animals, and ghosts, together with humans and gods, are called the five destinies. Only in the desire realm are four of the destinies found in their entirety; the god-destiny, however, has one portion in each of the three realms. Is there, then, anything within the three realms that is not subsumed under any destiny, even though the five destinies are said to exist within these realms? There is: namely, wholesome and defiled dharmas, the external receptacle world, and the intermediate-existence being — although these all belong to the nature of the three realms, they are not subsumed under any destiny. The essential nature of the five destinies is only uncovered-neutral. If it were otherwise, the destinies would become mixed together, since a single destiny would then contain within it the karma and afflictions belonging to all five destinies at once. The five destinies are subsumed only under what belongs to the category of sentient beings; their essential nature is not the intermediate-existence being.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The god-destiny's status as "one portion in each of the three realms" is a nice small piece of bookkeeping: unlike the other four destinies, which belong entirely to the desire realm, "god" is really an umbrella term spanning the desire realm's six heavens, the form realm's seventeen, and the formless realm's four spheres all at once — a single destiny stretched across the entire cosmological map this fascicle has just finished surveying.

The core argument for why destiny must be uncovered-neutral is a clean piece of conceptual hygiene: karma and afflictions are not tied to any one destiny in particular — a human can carry hell-bound karma, a god can carry afflictions that would otherwise produce a lower rebirth — so if "destiny" itself were allowed to be wholesome or defiled in nature, every destiny's continuum would end up carrying traces of every other destiny's moral coloring, and the clean five-way distinction the whole scheme exists to draw would simply dissolve. Keeping destiny strictly neutral is what lets it name a stable category (this is what a hell-being's aggregates simply are) that karma and affliction can then act upon, rather than collapsing destiny and moral quality into one another.

11. Scriptural Support: The Seven Kinds of Existence

Two Abhidharma treatises and a sutra are cited in support: the intermediate-existence being and karma-existence are both explicitly distinguished from the five destinies proper, confirming that destiny names something narrower and more specific than "existence" in general.

Chinese Source

《施設足論》作如是說：四生攝五趣，非五攝四生。不攝者何？所謂中有。《法蘊足論》亦作是言：眼界云何？謂四大種所造淨色是眼，眼根、眼處、眼界。地獄、傍生、鬼、人、天趣，修成中有。契經亦簡中有異趣。是何契經？謂《七有經》。彼說七有，謂地獄有、傍生有、餓鬼有、天有、人有、業有、中有。彼經中說五趣及因并趣方便，故趣唯是無覆無記，其理極成。簡業有因，異諸趣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Prajñaptiśāstra states as follows: the four kinds of birth subsume the five destinies, but the five destinies do not subsume the four kinds of birth. What is it, then, that is not subsumed? Namely, the intermediate-existence being. The Dharmaskandha-śāstra likewise states: what is the eye-element? It is the pure form produced by the four great elements that constitutes the eye — the eye-faculty, the eye-sense-field, and the eye-element — found among the destinies of hell-beings, animals, ghosts, humans, and gods, and also cultivated and completed in the intermediate-existence being. A sutra, too, distinguishes the intermediate-existence being from the destinies. Which sutra is this? The Sūtra of the Seven Kinds of Existence. That sutra speaks of seven kinds of existence: hell-existence, animal-existence, hungry-ghost-existence, god-existence, human-existence, karma-existence, and intermediate-existence. That sutra, in speaking of the five destinies together with their cause and the means by which one arrives at a destiny, thereby firmly establishes the principle that destiny is only uncovered-neutral. Karma-existence, functioning as the cause, is distinguished from the destinies precisely because it is different in kind from every one of them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The argument's logic is worth spelling out: if a canonical sutra already lists "karma-existence" and "intermediate-existence" as separate items alongside the five destinies proper, rather than folding them into one of the five, this is treated as good evidence that the tradition itself already recognized destiny as a narrower category than "existence" in general — specifically, one that excludes exactly the two things (karma, which can be wholesome or unwholesome, and the intermediate being, which is not yet settled into any destiny) that would otherwise threaten the "destiny is purely neutral" principle. The two treatise citations reinforce the same point from a different angle, both explicitly naming the intermediate-existence being as something distinct from, and not subsumed by, the five destinies it stands between.

12. What Is a Hell-Being, Really? Śāriputra's Statement and a Rival Reading

A citation from Śāriputra grounds the "destiny is uncovered-neutral" position in a striking claim — apart from the five ripened aggregates, no hell-being can be found at all — before a genuine internal tension surfaces with another Abhidharma treatise, and a rival view argues destiny might, after all, extend to the wholesome and defiled.

Chinese Source

迦濕彌羅國誦如是契經：尊者舍利子作是言：具壽！若有地獄諸漏現前故，造作增長順地獄受業，彼身語意曲穢濁故，於㮏落迦中受五蘊異熟，異熟起已名那落迦，除五蘊法，彼那落迦都不可得。故趣唯是無覆無記。若如是者，《品類足論》當云何通？彼說五趣一切隨眠所隨增故。彼說五趣續生心中容有五部一切煩惱趣及入心。總說為趣，無相違失。譬如村落及村落邊總名村落。有說：趣體亦通善染。然《七有經》簡業有者，非別說故，定非彼攝。如五濁中煩惱與見別說為濁，非別說故，彼見定非煩惱所攝。如是業有雖亦是趣，為顯趣因，是故別說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Kashmiri tradition recites a sutra in which the Venerable Śāriputra states as follows: "Venerable ones! If there is one for whom, through the manifest presence of the contaminants belonging to hell, hell-conducive karma is performed and increased, and whose bodily, verbal, and mental actions are thereby crooked and defiled — such a one, within the hells, undergoes the karmic-ripening of the five aggregates; and it is only once this ripening has arisen that one is called a hell-being. Apart from the dharmas of the five aggregates, no such hell-being whatsoever can be found." Hence destiny is only uncovered-neutral. If that is so, then how is the Prakaraṇapāda-śāstra to be reconciled with this? For it states that all latent tendencies lie latent and increase upon the five destinies. That treatise means that, within the mind of rebirth-continuity belonging to the five destinies, all the afflictions of the five categories may indeed be present, entering into that mind as well as into the destiny. Speaking of this loosely as "the destiny" involves no real contradiction — just as a village and the area surrounding a village are both loosely called "the village." There is a further view: the essential nature of destiny extends to the wholesome and defiled as well. But as for the Sūtra of Seven Existences separating out karma-existence — since it is not thereby separately excluded, it does not definitely follow that affliction is excluded from destiny either. This is comparable to how, among the five defilements, affliction and view are stated separately as defilements, yet this separate statement does not mean that view is definitely not subsumed under affliction. In just the same way, karma-existence, although it too is in some sense a destiny, is stated separately in order to highlight it specifically as the cause of destiny.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to present this exchange as genuinely unresolved rather than force a tidy verdict onto it, since the text itself seems to let the tension stand. The orthodox position's village analogy is elegant — afflictions can genuinely be present "in the vicinity of" a destiny's continuum, entering its rebirth-mind, without that presence making the destiny itself defiled in nature, just as calling the land around a village "the village" doesn't mean the surrounding fields are the same thing as the buildings. But the rival view's counter-analogy lands a real point: if "view" can be a genuine sub-case of "affliction" despite being listed separately for emphasis, why should "karma-existence" not likewise be a genuine sub-case of "destiny" despite being listed separately to highlight its causal role? The text does not resolve this by direct argument here — it lets both readings of the same scriptural evidence stand side by side, and the discussion that follows in the next section pursues a different line of defense instead.

13. Why Intermediate Existence Can Never Be a "Destiny"

A sharper argument for excluding intermediate existence from the five destinies: "destiny" means, quite literally, "that which is traveled to" — and the intermediate-existence being simply arises wherever death occurred, with no travel involved at all. A pointed complication follows, and Śāriputra's original statement is finally clarified.

Chinese Source

若爾，中有亦應是趣。不爾，趣義不相應故。趣謂所往，不可說言中有是所往，即死處生故。若爾，無色亦應非趣，即於死處而受生故。既爾，中有名中有，故不應名趣。二趣中故名為中有，此若趣攝非中間故，是則不應說名中有。然彼尊者舍利子言異熟起已名地獄者，說異熟起方名地獄，非說地獄唯是異熟。然復說言，除五蘊法彼那落迦不可得者，為遮實有能往諸趣補特伽羅故作是說，非遮餘蘊故作是言。毘婆沙師說：趣唯是無覆無記。有說：一向是異熟生。有餘師言：亦通長養。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, then the intermediate-existence being, too, ought to be a destiny. It is not, since the meaning of "destiny" would not correspond to it. "Destiny" means "that to which one goes" — and one cannot say that the intermediate-existence being is "that to which one goes," since it arises right at the very place of death. If that is so, then the formless realm should also not count as a destiny, since beings born there are likewise reborn right at the very place of death. Given this, the intermediate-existence being is named "intermediate-existence" — therefore it should not be called a "destiny." It is because it stands between two destinies that it is named "intermediate-existence"; if it were itself included among the destinies, it would then no longer be something standing between two destinies, and so it would then be inappropriate to call it "intermediate-existence" at all. As for the Venerable Śāriputra's own statement that one is called a hell-being only once karmic-ripening has arisen — this states that the name "hell-being" applies only once ripening has arisen; it does not state that a hell-being is nothing but karmic-ripening in every respect. And as for the further statement that, apart from the dharmas of the five aggregates, no such hell-being can be found at all — this is stated in order to refute the idea that there is a genuinely real "person" capable of traveling to the various destinies; it is not stated in order to deny that other aggregates might also be present. The Vaibhāṣika masters say: destiny is only uncovered-neutral. There is a further view: it is exclusively karmic-ripening. Yet another view holds: it extends to nourished-and-grown aggregates as well.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag honestly that the text raises a genuinely sharp objection here and does not appear to answer it directly: if "arising right at the place of death, with no travel involved" is what disqualifies the intermediate-existence being from counting as a destiny, the very same reasoning was already used, in an earlier section, to prove that the formless realm has no spatial location at all — so why does the formless realm still count as a legitimate portion of the god-destiny, while intermediate existence does not? The passage raises exactly this parallel and then moves on to a different argument (the semantic point that "intermediate" presupposes two destinies flanking it) without closing the loop on the formless-realm case. I have translated the passage as it stands rather than supplying a resolution the text itself does not give.

The closing clarification of Śāriputra's statement is a genuinely careful piece of exegesis: rather than reading his words as claiming a hell-being is nothing but its ripened aggregates in every possible respect, the text narrows the claim to exactly what it needs to establish — that no separate, persisting "person" travels between destinies apart from the aggregates themselves — while explicitly leaving room for other, non-ripened aggregates (ordinary wholesome or unwholesome mental activity, for instance) to coexist within that same being's continuum. This reading is precisely what allows the final three-way summary to make sense: the orthodox Vaibhāṣika position (destiny is uncovered-neutral generally, the broadest of the three), a stricter view (destiny is exclusively the ripened aggregates, and nothing more), and a middle view (destiny includes both the ripened aggregates and the ordinary aggregates sustained and grown afterward) all remain live options precisely because Śāriputra's statement, properly read, never settled the question as narrowly as it might first appear to.

14. The Seven Abodes of Consciousness: Bodily and Perceptual Variety

A new classificatory scheme sorts beings by whether their bodies and perceptions vary or stay uniform — and the second abode, Brahmā's Retinue, comes with a genuinely fascinating psychological puzzle: how does the mistaken belief that Brahmā created his followers actually arise, given that every candidate explanation for it runs into a real problem?

Chinese Source

即於三界及五趣中，如其次第識住有七。其七者何？頌曰：身異及想異，身異同一想，翻此身想一，并無色下三，故識住有七，餘非有損壞。論曰：契經中說，有色有情身異想異，如人一分天，是第一識住。一分天者，謂欲界天及初靜慮，除劫初起。言身異者，謂彼色身種種顯形，狀貌異故。彼由身異，或有異身，故彼有情說名身異。言想異者，謂彼苦樂不苦不樂想差別故。彼由想異，或有異想，故彼有情說名想異。有色有情身異想一，如梵眾天謂劫初起，是第二識住。所以者何？以劫初起，彼諸梵眾起如是想：我等皆是大梵所生。大梵爾時亦起此想：是諸梵眾皆我所生。同想一因故名想一。大梵王身其量高廣，容貌威德言語光明衣冠等事，一一皆與梵眾不同，故名身異。經說：梵眾作是念言：我等曾見如是有情長壽久住，乃至起願：云何當令諸餘有情生我同分？於彼正起此心願時，我等便生彼同分內。梵眾何處曾見梵王？有餘師言：住極光淨，從彼天沒來生此故。云何今時不得第二靜慮，而能憶念彼地宿住事耶？若彼已得第二靜慮，云何緣大梵猶起戒禁取？有餘師說：住中有中，彼住中有中，無長時住義，以於受生無障礙故。如何梵眾可作念言：我等曾見如是有情長壽久住。是故梵眾即住自天，憶念此生前所更事，謂先見彼長壽久住，後重見時起如是念。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the three realms and the five destinies, correspondingly, there are seven abodes of consciousness. What are the seven? Verse: body-variety together with perception-variety; body-variety with a single, unified perception; the reverse of this, a single body with perception-variety; a single body and a single perception; together with the lower three formless spheres — these make the seven abodes of consciousness; the rest are not abodes, since they have something that damages consciousness. Prose: a sutra states that there are form-possessing sentient beings with bodily variety and perceptual variety — such as humans, and a portion of the gods — and this is the first abode of consciousness. "A portion of the gods" means the desire-realm gods and the first dhyāna, excepting those who arose at the very start of the eon. "Bodily variety" means that their physical bodies differ in coloring, shape, and appearance; because of this, such sentient beings are said to have bodily variety. "Perceptual variety" means that their perceptions of pleasure, pain, and neither-pleasure-nor-pain differ; because of this, such sentient beings are said to have perceptual variety. Form-possessing sentient beings with bodily variety but a single, unified perception — such as the Heaven of Brahmā's Retinue at the very start of the eon — constitute the second abode of consciousness. Why is this? Because, at the very start of the eon, all the members of Brahmā's Retinue give rise to this same thought: "We are all brought into being by the Great Brahmā." Great Brahmā, at that time, likewise gives rise to this same thought: "All these members of my retinue are brought into being by me." Since they share a single, common thought as their single cause, this is called "a single perception." The body of the Great Brahmā king is vast in stature; his appearance, majesty, speech, radiance, garments, and crown, each and every one, differs from those of his retinue — hence it is called "bodily variety." A sutra states: the members of Brahmā's Retinue think to themselves, "We have previously seen a being of this kind, long-lived and abiding for a long time," and so on, up to the point of forming this wish: "How might other sentient beings come to be born within my own class-commonality?" At the very moment that being forms this wish in mind, we ourselves then come to be born within that same class-commonality. But where could the members of Brahmā's Retinue have previously seen the Brahmā king? There is a further view: they had previously dwelt in the Heaven of Light-Sound, and, passing away from that heaven, come to be born here. But how could they, not currently having attained the second dhyāna, nevertheless be able to remember past-life events belonging to that level? And if they had indeed already attained the second dhyāna, how could they still give rise to the wrong grasping of rules and vows with respect to the Great Brahmā? There is a further view: they dwelt within the intermediate-existence state. But one dwelling within the intermediate-existence state has no sense of dwelling there for a long time, given that there is no obstruction to being reborn — so how, then, could the members of Brahmā's Retinue think, "We have previously seen a being of this kind, long-lived and abiding for a long time"? For this reason, the members of Brahmā's Retinue, while dwelling in their own heaven, remember events they themselves experienced earlier within this very same life: that is, they first saw that being long-lived and abiding for a long time, and later, upon seeing him again, gave rise to this same thought.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely delightful piece of comparative-explanation reasoning, and worth tracing through in full because the text refuses to settle for the first plausible-sounding answer. If Brahmā's followers falsely believe he created them, where did they get the impression that he predates them at all? Three candidate explanations are tried and two are rejected on their own terms: perhaps they remembered him from a past life in the higher Light-Sound heaven — but then they should retain that heaven's own meditative attainment, which would make their false belief about Brahmā as creator inexplicable in someone with that level of insight; perhaps they observed him during the transitional intermediate-existence state before this birth — but that state is too fleeting to produce any impression of someone "abiding for a long time." The winning explanation is disarmingly simple: they were born into Brahmā's retinue slightly after he himself was, within this very same lifetime, and simply mistook "I have observed this being for as long as I can remember" for "this being existed before me and made me" — an ordinary cognitive illusion of perspective, not a supernatural memory of any kind, elevated by faulty inference into the belief in a creator.

15. The Third Abode of Consciousness, and a Sautrāntika Story About Cosmic Fire

The third abode belongs to the Heaven of Light-Sound, where bodies are uniform but perception varies — and a Sautrāntika alternative offers a genuinely vivid narrative explanation for that variety: newly-arisen gods, panicked by a rising cosmic conflagration, are calmed by veteran gods who remember it has never reached that high before.

Chinese Source

有色有情身一想異，如極光淨天，是第三識住。此中舉後兼以攝初，應知具攝第二靜慮。若不爾者，彼少光天、無量光天何識住攝？彼天顯形狀貌不異，故名身一。樂非苦樂二想交參，故名想異。傳說彼天厭根本地喜根，已起近分地捨根現前；厭近分地捨根，已起根本地喜根現前。如富貴人厭欲樂已便受法樂，厭法樂已復受欲樂。豈不遍淨想亦應然？非遍淨天曾有厭樂，以樂寂靜曾無厭時；喜則不然，擾動心故。經部師說：有餘契經釋彼天中有想異義，謂極光淨有天新生，未善了知世間成壞。彼見下地火焰洞然，見已便生驚怖厭離：勿彼火焰燒盡梵宮令彼皆空上侵我處。彼極光淨有舊生天，已善了知世間成壞，便慰喻彼驚怖天言：淨仙淨仙。勿怖勿怖。昔彼火焰燒盡梵宮，令其皆空即於彼滅。彼於火焰有來不來想及怖不怖想故名想異，非由有樂非苦樂想有交參故得想異名。

Ru-Yi Translation

Form-possessing sentient beings with a single, unified body but perceptual variety — such as the Heaven of Light-Sound — constitute the third abode of consciousness. Here, citing the last heaven of the second dhyāna is meant to include the first two as well — it should be understood that this fully encompasses the whole of the second dhyāna. If it were not so, then which abode of consciousness would the Heaven of Lesser Light and the Heaven of Immeasurable Light be subsumed under? The coloring, shape, and appearance of that heaven's inhabitants do not differ — hence it is called "a single body." The two perceptions of pleasure and of neither-pleasure-nor-pain intermingle there — hence it is called "perceptual variety." Tradition holds that, in that heaven, when one grows weary of the root-level's joy faculty, the neighboring-level's equanimity faculty then manifests in its place; and when one grows weary of the neighboring-level's equanimity faculty, the root-level's joy faculty then manifests in its place once again — like a wealthy person who, growing weary of sensual pleasure, takes up the pleasure of the Dharma, and, growing weary of the pleasure of the Dharma, takes up sensual pleasure once again. But should this not likewise hold for the perception in the Heaven of All-Pervasive Purity? No — the gods of All-Pervasive Purity never grow weary of their pleasure at all, since that pleasure is calm and still, and there is never a time of weariness toward it; joy, however, is not like this, since it agitates and disturbs the mind. The Sautrāntika masters say: there is a further sutra that explains the meaning of "perceptual variety" within that heaven differently. It states that, among the gods of Light-Sound, there are those newly born who have not yet properly come to understand the world's process of formation and dissolution. They see the blazing flames of the lower levels burning fiercely, and, upon seeing this, become frightened and fearful, thinking: "May those flames not burn up the Brahmā-palaces, leaving them entirely empty, and rise up to invade my own abode!" Among the gods of Light-Sound, there are also those long since born there, who have already properly come to understand the world's process of formation and dissolution; they then comfort and reassure those frightened gods, saying: "Pure sages, pure sages! Do not be afraid, do not be afraid! In the past, too, those flames burned up the Brahmā-palaces, leaving them entirely empty — and yet they were extinguished there, going no further." It is because these gods hold differing perceptions regarding the flames — whether they will come or not come, and correspondingly whether to be afraid or not afraid — that this is called "perceptual variety," not on account of pleasure and neither-pleasure-nor-pain perceptions intermingling.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two genuinely different explanations are offered for the same technical label, and I have preserved both rather than picking a winner, since the text itself presents them as live alternatives. The orthodox account is psychologically abstract: "perceptual variety" names an oscillation between two subtle feeling-tones, joy and equanimity, that this heaven's inhabitants cycle between the way a wealthy person alternates between sensual and spiritual pleasure once each grows tiresome in turn. The Sautrāntika alternative is a genuine piece of narrative cosmology: this tradition holds that each world-cycle eventually ends in a conflagration that burns everything up through the first dhyāna level before finally being extinguished, unable to reach the second dhyāna where the Light-Sound heaven sits — and gods newly arrived there, not yet knowing this pattern, panic at the sight of the rising flames, while long-settled gods who have witnessed it before calm them with the reassurance that it always stops short. The "variety" in this reading is not a feeling-tone at all, but a variety of belief and emotional response to the very same event, splitting the community into the frightened and the unshaken.

16. The Seven Abodes Completed, and What "Abode of Consciousness" Actually Means

The fourth and final form-realm abode, and the lower three formless spheres, complete the set of seven — followed by a precise definition of the category itself: a genuine abode of consciousness must lack anything that damages consciousness, and must be a place beings are glad to arrive at and never wish to leave.

Chinese Source

有色有情身一想一，如遍淨天，是第四識住。唯有樂想故名想一。初靜慮中由染污想故言想一，第二靜慮由二善想故言想異，第三靜慮由異熟想故言想一，下三無色名別，如經即三識住是名為七。此中何法名為識住？謂彼所繫五蘊四蘊，如其所應是名識住。所餘何故非識住耶？於餘處皆有損壞識法故。餘處者何？謂諸惡處第四靜慮及與有頂。所以者何？由彼處有損壞識法，故非識住。何等名為損壞識法？謂諸惡處有重苦受能損於識，第四靜慮有無想定及無想事，有頂天中有滅盡定能壞於識，令相續斷故非識住。復說：若處餘處有情心樂來止，若至於此不更求出，說名識住。於諸惡處，二義俱無。第四靜慮心恒求出，謂諸異生求入無想，若諸聖者樂入淨居或無色處，若淨居天樂證寂滅，有頂昧劣故非識住。如是分別七識住已。

Ru-Yi Translation

Form-possessing sentient beings with a single body and a single perception — such as the Heaven of All-Pervasive Purity — constitute the fourth abode of consciousness. Since there is only the perception of pleasure there, it is called "a single perception." Within the first dhyāna, it is on account of a defiled perception that it is called "single"; within the second dhyāna, it is on account of two wholesome perceptions that it is called "varied"; within the third dhyāna, it is on account of a karmic-ripening perception that it is called "single." The lower three formless spheres are named separately, just as a sutra states; these make up the seven abodes of consciousness. What, then, is meant here by "abode of consciousness"? It is the five aggregates, or, in the formless spheres, the four aggregates, bound up with that level, each according to what is appropriate — this is what is called an abode of consciousness. Why, then, are the remaining places not abodes of consciousness? Because, in every one of those remaining places, there is something that damages consciousness. What are these remaining places? They are the evil destinies, the fourth dhyāna, and the Peak of Existence. Why is this? Because those places contain something that damages consciousness, they are not abodes of consciousness. What, exactly, is meant by "something that damages consciousness"? In the evil destinies, there is intense suffering capable of damaging consciousness; in the fourth dhyāna, there is the attainment of non-percipience and the state of non-percipient beings; and at the Peak of Existence, there is the attainment of cessation, capable of damaging consciousness by cutting off its continuity — hence these are not abodes of consciousness. It is further said: a place is called an abode of consciousness if sentient beings from other places are glad to come and settle there, and, once having arrived there, no longer seek to leave. In the evil destinies, neither of these two conditions holds at all. In the fourth dhyāna, the mind constantly seeks to leave: ordinary beings there seek to enter non-percipience, while noble ones there are glad to enter the pure abodes or the formless spheres, and the pure-abode gods there are glad to realize final quiescence. The Peak of Existence, being dim and weak, is likewise not an abode of consciousness. With this, the seven abodes of consciousness have been fully distinguished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two-part definition offered here is doing real conceptual work, and the two criteria turn out to converge on the same exclusions from different directions. The first criterion is almost physiological: does this place contain some specific factor — crushing pain, the attainment of non-percipience, the attainment of cessation — that actually damages or interrupts the continuity of consciousness itself? The second is more psychological: is this a place beings settle into contentedly, or one they are constantly straining to escape? The fourth dhyāna fails both tests for essentially the same underlying reason — its most notable residents (ordinary beings courting non-percipience, noble ones already looking past it toward the pure abodes or the formless attainments) are all, in one way or another, oriented toward leaving that very level behind, which is exactly the restlessness the second criterion is designed to catch.

17. The Nine Abodes of Sentient Beings

A related but distinct scheme: the nine abodes of sentient beings add back two places the seven abodes of consciousness had excluded — the Peak of Existence and the non-percipient beings — since beings can be genuinely content to remain somewhere even where consciousness itself is dim, damaged, or altogether absent.

Chinese Source

因茲復說九有情居。其九者何？頌曰：應知兼有頂，及無想有情，是九有情居，餘非不樂住。論曰：前七識住及第一有、無想有情，是名為九，諸有情類唯於此九欣樂住故立有情居。餘處皆非，不樂住故。言餘處者，謂諸惡處，非有情類自樂居中，惡業羅剎逼之令住故。彼如牢獄不立有情居。第四靜慮除無想天，餘非有情居，如識住中釋。

Ru-Yi Translation

On this basis, it is further said that there are nine abodes of sentient beings. What are the nine? Verse: it should be understood that, together with the Peak of Existence and the non-percipient beings, these make the nine abodes of sentient beings; the rest are not, since beings are not glad to dwell there. Prose: the seven abodes of consciousness already discussed, together with the foremost existence and the non-percipient beings, make nine in all. Sentient beings are glad and delighted to dwell only within these nine, and so these are established as the abodes of sentient beings. All other places are not such abodes, since beings are not glad to dwell there. "Other places" refers to the evil destinies: sentient beings do not willingly dwell there of their own accord — it is their own evil karma, like a rākṣasa demon, that forces and compels them to remain. These are like a prison, and so are not established as abodes of sentient beings. Within the fourth dhyāna, excepting the Heaven of Non-Percipience, the rest are not abodes of sentient beings, exactly as already explained under the abodes of consciousness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nine abodes and the seven abodes of consciousness overlap almost entirely but diverge at exactly the two cases that expose the difference between the two schemes' organizing principles. The Peak of Existence was excluded from the abodes of consciousness for being "dim and weak" — a genuine deficiency in mental clarity — yet beings reborn there are perfectly content to remain, so it earns a place among the abodes of sentient beings on contentment alone. Non-percipient beings are an even sharper case: by definition, they have no consciousness whatsoever during that state, making them the one clear instance where "abode of consciousness" simply cannot apply in any sense — and yet they, too, are entirely willing occupants of their condition, with nothing forcing or tormenting them the way karma compels a hell-being to remain in torment. The hell-being's forced captivity, memorably compared to being held by a rākṣasa demon in a prison, is the clearest illustration of what disqualifies the evil destinies under this second scheme: not a deficiency in the being's own consciousness, but the complete absence of anything resembling willing residence.

18. The Four Abodes of Consciousness, and Why Consciousness Can Never Be Its Own Abode

A different sutra offers a subtly different scheme: the four abodes of consciousness are simply the other four aggregates, since consciousness itself, by its very nature as the occupant rather than the dwelling, can never count as an abode of itself — settled through a genuinely elegant field-and-seed metaphor.

Chinese Source

前所引經說七識住，復有餘經說四識住。其四者何？頌曰：四識住當知，四蘊唯自地，說獨識非住，有漏四句攝。論曰：如契經言：識隨色住，識隨受住，識隨想住，識隨行住，是名四種。如是四種其體云何？謂隨次第有漏四蘊。又此唯在自地非餘，識所依著，名識住故。非於異地色等蘊中識隨愛力依著於彼。如何不說識為識住？由離能住立所住故。非能住識可名所住，如非即王可名王座。或若有法識所乘御，如人船理說名識住。非識即能乘御自體，是故不說識為識住。毘婆沙師所說如是。若爾，何故餘契經言：於識食中有喜有染，有喜染故識住其中、識所乘御。又如何言前七識住五蘊為體？雖有是說，而於生處所攝蘊中不別分析，總生喜染故。識轉時亦名識住，非獨說識。然色等蘊一一能生種種喜染令識依著，獨識不然，故言非住。是故於此四識住中識非識住，於餘可說。又佛意說，此四識住猶如良田，總說一切有取諸識猶如種子，不可種子立為良田。仰測世尊教意如是。又法與識可俱時生，為識良田可立識住。識蘊不爾，故非識住。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sutra cited earlier speaks of seven abodes of consciousness; there is, however, a further sutra that speaks of four abodes of consciousness. What are these four? Verse: the four abodes of consciousness should be understood as the four aggregates, each belonging only to its own level; consciousness alone, it is said, is not itself an abode; how this relates to the sevenfold scheme is subsumed under a fourfold case, since both concern only the contaminated. Prose: as a sutra states: consciousness abides in dependence on form, consciousness abides in dependence on feeling, consciousness abides in dependence on perception, consciousness abides in dependence on formations — these are called the four kinds. What, then, is the essential nature of these four kinds? They are, respectively and in order, the four contaminated aggregates. Moreover, these exist only within their own level, not elsewhere, since it is specifically what consciousness depends upon and clings to that is called an abode of consciousness. It is not the case that, within the form and other aggregates belonging to a different level, consciousness could, through the power of craving, depend upon and cling to those instead. Why, then, is consciousness itself not said to be an abode of consciousness? Because that which is abided in is established precisely by being distinct from that which abides. Consciousness, being that which abides, cannot itself be named that which is abided in — just as the king himself cannot be called the king's throne. Alternatively: whatever dharma consciousness rides upon and is borne by — comparable, in principle, to a person and a boat — is what is called an abode of consciousness. Consciousness is not itself the very thing that rides and is borne, and so consciousness itself is not said to be an abode of consciousness. This is what the Vaibhāṣika masters state. If that is so, then why does another sutra state: "Within the nutriment of consciousness, there is delight and there is attachment; because there is delight and attachment, consciousness abides within it and is borne by it"? And how, moreover, can it be said that the seven abodes of consciousness discussed earlier have all five aggregates as their essential nature? Although this is indeed stated, it is because, within the aggregates subsumed under a given place of birth, no separate analysis is made, since delight and attachment arise toward the whole together; when consciousness itself operates, this too is loosely called "an abode of consciousness," not that consciousness alone, by itself, is being named as a genuine, distinct abode. But form and the other aggregates, each individually, are capable of giving rise to various kinds of delight and attachment that cause consciousness to depend upon and cling to them; consciousness alone, by itself, is not like this — hence it is said not to be an abode. For this reason, within these four abodes of consciousness, consciousness is not itself an abode of consciousness, though it may loosely be spoken of as such elsewhere. Moreover, the Buddha's intent, it is held, was to speak of these four abodes of consciousness as being like a fertile field, while speaking generally of all consciousness bound up with clinging as being like a seed — and a seed cannot itself be established as the fertile field. This, as far as can be inferred, is the intent of the World-Honored One's teaching. Moreover, a dharma capable of arising simultaneously together with consciousness, and capable of serving as consciousness's fertile field, can be established as an abode of consciousness. The aggregate of consciousness itself is not like this, and so it is not an abode of consciousness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king-and-throne and person-and-boat images both make the same basic point from slightly different angles: a category built on a relationship (occupant to dwelling, rider to vehicle) cannot have its "occupant" term double as its own "dwelling" term without collapsing the very distinction the category depends on. But the text is honest enough to flag a real complication rather than let the tidy definition stand unchallenged: a different sutra explicitly describes consciousness itself as an object of "delight and attachment" — language that, in every other context, is exactly what marks something out as an abode. The response threads this carefully: the seven-abode scheme's casual inclusion of consciousness happens only because it treats all five aggregates as one undifferentiated bundle without analyzing out consciousness's distinct role, whereas the four-abode scheme is precisely the more careful, analyzed account that isolates consciousness as structurally different in kind from what it depends on.

The field-and-seed metaphor is the section's real payoff, and it is worth sitting with on its own terms: a fertile field is what receives and supports a seed's growth, while the seed itself is what develops within that support — visibly different roles, even though both are indispensable to anything actually growing. Reading the four abodes as the field and consciousness itself as the seed makes the earlier definitional point vivid rather than merely technical: no matter how much delight and craving cluster around consciousness, it remains the thing being supported, never the ground doing the supporting, and a metaphor built on the fixed asymmetry between soil and seed captures that distinction more memorably than the abstract "abider versus abided-in" language on its own.

19. Reconciling the Seven and the Four

A closing fourfold case works out precisely how the seven-abode and four-abode schemes overlap and diverge — resolving neatly once it is seen that each scheme trades breadth for precision in a different place.

Chinese Source

如是所說七種四種，識住雖殊而皆有漏。為七攝四、四攝七耶？非遍相攝。可為四句。謂審觀察應知二門體互寬陿得成四句，或有七攝非四攝等。第一句者，謂七中識。第二句者，謂諸惡處、第四靜慮及有頂中，除識餘蘊。第三句者，七中四蘊。第四句者，謂除前相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, the seven kinds and the four kinds just discussed, although the abodes of consciousness they describe differ, are both, in every case, contaminated. Does the sevenfold scheme subsume the fourfold, or does the fourfold subsume the sevenfold? Neither subsumes the other completely. This should be treated as a fourfold case: upon careful examination, it should be understood that the essential scope of the two schemes is mutually broader and narrower in different respects, yielding a fourfold case — that is, there may be something subsumed under the seven but not under the four, and so on. The first case: consciousness within the seven. The second case: within the evil destinies, the fourth dhyāna, and the Peak of Existence, the remaining aggregates excepting consciousness. The third case: the four aggregates within the seven. The fourth case: excepting all the aforementioned characteristics.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The resolution is genuinely elegant once its logic is spelled out. The seven-abode scheme trades breadth of aggregate-coverage (it includes consciousness) for narrowness of place-coverage (it only counts the seven good, stable destinations). The four-abode scheme makes the opposite trade: it narrows the aggregate-coverage (it excludes consciousness on principle) but broadens the place-coverage, since form, feeling, perception, and formations exist at every level regardless of whether that level counts as a genuine abode of consciousness at all — including, notably, the evil destinies, the fourth dhyāna, and the Peak of Existence, all three of which the seven-abode scheme excluded outright. This is exactly why consciousness itself falls only under the seven (first case), the four non-consciousness aggregates in the excluded places fall only under the four (second case), the four non-consciousness aggregates within the seven good places satisfy both schemes at once (third case), and everything else — the unconditioned, and anything belonging to no level at all — satisfies neither (fourth case).

20. The Four Kinds of Birth

A new topic within the chapter: egg-birth, womb-birth, moisture-birth, and transformation-birth — with humans and animals uniquely spanning all four, illustrated through a wonderfully specific set of legendary examples for each unusual case.

Chinese Source

於前所說諸界趣中，應知其生略有四種。何等為四？何處有何？頌曰：於中有四生，有情謂卵等，人傍生具四，地獄及諸天，中有唯化生，鬼通胎化二。論曰：謂有情類卵生、胎生、濕生、化生，是名為四。生謂生類。諸有情中雖餘類雜，而生類等。云何卵生？謂有情類生從卵㲉是名卵生，如鵝孔雀鸚鵡鴈等。云何胎生？謂有情類生從胎藏是名胎生，如象馬牛猪羊驢等。云何濕生？謂有情類生從濕氣是名濕生，如虫飛蛾蚊蚰蜒等。云何化生？謂有情類生無所託是名化生，如那落迦、天、中有等，具根無缺支分頓生，無而欻有故名為化。人傍生趣各具四種。人卵生者，謂如世羅鄔波世羅生從鶴卵，鹿母所生三十二子，般遮羅王五百子等。人胎生者，如今世人。人濕生者，如曼馱多遮盧鄔波遮盧，鴿鬘菴羅衛等。人化生者，唯劫初人。傍生三種，共所現見。化生如龍揭路荼等。一切地獄、諸天、中有，皆唯化生。鬼趣唯通胎化二種。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the three realms and destinies already discussed, it should be understood that birth is, in brief, of four kinds. What are these four, and which occurs where? Verse: among these, there are four kinds of birth for sentient beings — namely, egg-birth and the rest. Humans and animals possess all four; the hells and the gods, and the intermediate-existence being, are only transformation-birth; ghosts extend to both womb-birth and transformation-birth. Prose: sentient beings are of the egg-born, womb-born, moisture-born, and transformation-born kinds — these are called the four. "Birth" here means a class or category of birth. Among sentient beings, although other categories may be intermixed, they are nevertheless alike in belonging to one of these classes of birth. What is egg-birth? Sentient beings born from an eggshell are called egg-born — such as geese, peacocks, parrots, and wild geese. What is womb-birth? Sentient beings born from a womb are called womb-born — such as elephants, horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, and donkeys. What is moisture-birth? Sentient beings born from moisture are called moisture-born — such as worms, moths, mosquitoes, and centipedes. What is transformation-birth? Sentient beings born without relying on anything at all are called transformation-born — such as hell-beings, gods, and the intermediate-existence being; their faculties are complete, lacking nothing, and their limbs and parts arise all at once, suddenly coming into being from nothing whatsoever — hence it is called "transformation." The human and animal destinies each possess all four kinds. As for humans born from eggs: for instance, Śaila and Upaśaila, born from a crane's egg; the thirty-two sons born of Mṛgī; and King Pañcāla's five hundred sons, and so on. As for humans born from a womb: such as people of the present age. As for humans born from moisture: such as Māndhātṛ, Cāru and Upacāru, Kapotamālā, and Āmrapālī, and so on. As for humans born by transformation: only the people at the very beginning of the eon. The three kinds of animal birth are commonly witnessed directly; as for transformation-born animals, such as nāgas and garuḍas, and so on. All hell-beings, gods, and intermediate-existence beings are exclusively transformation-born. The ghost-destiny extends only to womb-birth and transformation-birth.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag some genuine uncertainty around the proper names in this passage — Śaila and Upaśaila, Mṛgī's thirty-two sons, King Pañcāla, Māndhātṛ, Cāru and Upacāru, Kapotamālā, and Āmrapālī — since these are drawn from a body of Buddhist legend and jātaka-adjacent narrative I have reconstructed into Sanskrit as best I can from the Chinese transliterations, without being able to verify every reconstruction against an independent source. Āmrapālī is the one figure here with a well-attested legend outside this specific list: the celebrated courtesan of Vaiśālī, traditionally said to have been discovered as an infant within a mango tree, which fits the "moisture-birth" category oddly but is the story the tradition tells.

Transformation-birth's defining feature — arising all at once, fully formed, "from nothing whatsoever" — marks it out as the most direct and least mediated of the four kinds, requiring no egg, no womb, and no moisture to develop within, which is presumably part of why the next section calls it "supreme" among the four. That every hell-being, every god, and every intermediate-existence being is transformation-born is a striking piece of bookkeeping: it means the vast majority of rebirths across the whole cosmology surveyed in this fascicle happen this way, with the egg-born, womb-born, and moisture-born categories crowded into the comparatively narrow band of human, animal, and ghost existence.

21. A Hungry Ghost's Lament, and Why the Bodhisattva Chose the Humbler Birth

A haunting verse from a hungry ghost illustrates womb-birth's darkest possibility, before the treatise turns to a genuinely moving question: if transformation-birth is supreme among the four, why did the bodhisattva, who had already mastered the manner of his own birth, choose the humbler womb-birth instead?

Chinese Source

鬼胎生者，如餓鬼女白目連云：我夜生五子，隨生皆自食，晝生五亦然，雖盡而無飽。一切生中何生最勝？應言最勝唯是化生。若爾，何緣後身菩薩得生自在而受胎生？現受胎生有大利故，謂為引導諸大釋種親屬相因令入正法。又引餘類令知菩薩是輪王種生敬慕心，因得捨邪趣於正法。又令所化生增上心：彼既是人，能成大義；我曹亦爾，何為不能因發正勤專修正法？又若不爾，族姓難知，恐疑幻化為天為鬼。如外道論矯設謗言：過百劫後當有大幻出現於世噉食世間。故受胎生息諸疑謗。有餘師說：為留身界故受胎生，令無量人及諸異類一興供養千返生天及證解脫。若受化生無外種故，身纔殞逝無復遺形，如滅燈光即無所見。若人信佛有持願通能久留身，此不成釋因論生論。若化生身如滅燈光死無遺者，何故契經說化生揭路荼取化生龍為充所食？以不知故為食取龍，不說充飢。斯有何失？或龍未死暫得充飢，死已還飢。暫食何咎！於四生內何者最多？唯化生。何以故？三趣少分及二趣全、一切中有皆化生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for ghosts born from a womb: for instance, a hungry-ghost woman said to Maudgalyāyana: "At night I give birth to five children, and each one, as soon as born, I devour myself; by day I give birth to five more, and it is just the same — though I consume them entirely, I am never satisfied." Among all the kinds of birth, which is supreme? It should be said that transformation-birth alone is supreme. If that is so, then why does the bodhisattva in his final body, who has attained mastery over his own birth, nevertheless undergo womb-birth? Because undergoing womb-birth in that life brings great benefit: namely, in order to guide the great Śākya clan's own kinsmen, bound to him by blood relation, to enter the true Dharma. Moreover, it draws in other kinds of beings as well, letting them know that the bodhisattva is born of the lineage of a wheel-turning king, giving rise to a heart of reverence and admiration, through which they come to abandon wrong paths and turn toward the true Dharma. Moreover, it inspires in those to be converted a heightened resolve: "Since he, too, is a human being, and yet was able to accomplish so great a purpose — we ourselves are likewise human; why, then, should we not likewise be able, through arousing right diligence, to single-mindedly cultivate the true Dharma?" Moreover, if it were not so, his clan and lineage would be difficult to establish, and there would be cause to doubt and suspect that he was merely a magical apparition, a god or a ghost in disguise — comparable to how certain heretical treatises falsely and slanderously assert that, after a hundred eons have passed, a great illusory being will appear in the world and devour it. It is in order to put an end to all such doubts and slanders that he undergoes womb-birth. There is a further view: he undergoes womb-birth in order to leave behind relics of his body in the world, so that immeasurable humans and beings of other kinds, by making even a single offering to them, might be reborn in heaven a thousand times over, and ultimately attain liberation. If he had instead undergone transformation-birth, then, lacking any external, physical seed of origin, his body, the very moment it perished, would leave behind no remaining trace at all — like a lamp's flame going out, after which there is nothing further to be seen. If someone believes that the Buddha, through the sustaining power of his vow and his supernormal mastery, could cause his body to remain for a long time regardless, this does not succeed in actually resolving the question being raised here. If a transformation-born body, upon death, truly leaves nothing behind at all, like a lamp's flame going out, then why does a sutra state that a transformation-born garuḍa seizes a transformation-born nāga to satisfy its hunger? It is out of ignorance that the garuḍa seizes the nāga intending to eat it — the sutra does not actually say that its hunger is thereby satisfied. What fault is there in this? Or, alternatively: while the nāga has not yet died, the garuḍa may indeed obtain temporary satisfaction of its hunger, but once the nāga has died, it becomes hungry once again — what fault could there be in a merely temporary feeding? Among the four kinds of birth, which is the most numerous? Transformation-birth alone, since a portion of three destinies, the entirety of two destinies, and every intermediate-existence being are all transformation-born.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The hungry ghost's verse is one of this text's more viscerally memorable images, and it earns its place here as a genuine illustration of womb-birth's darkest extreme: a mother caught in an endless, self-consuming cycle of birth and devouring, hunger never actually satisfied no matter how much is consumed — a vivid embodiment of craving's essential structure, in this tradition's own account, precisely because craving is bottomless by nature rather than merely large in quantity.

The four reasons given for the bodhisattva's choice of womb-birth build a genuinely coherent case, each addressing a different practical obstacle a transformation-birth would have created: it secures a real family lineage that binds his own relatives to the teaching; it lends him the prestige of royal descent for outsiders; it makes his accomplishment feel attainable rather than merely divine, since an ordinary human birth means an ordinary human could, in principle, follow the same path; and it forecloses the specific slander that he was some kind of temporary illusion rather than a genuine person with real ancestry. The Sautrāntika-flavored second view, about leaving bodily relics behind for the benefit of future generations of worshippers, adds a fifth, more ritual-focused consideration — and the resulting side debate about whether a transformation-born body leaves any trace at all is a nice example of how even a seemingly minor supporting claim (a garuḍa eating a transformation-born nāga) can generate a small dialectical detour of its own before the discussion moves on.

22. What Is Intermediate Existence, and Why Isn't It Called "Birth"?

A precise definition opens one of this treatise's most extensive debates: intermediate existence is the body of five aggregates arising between the existence of death and the existence of birth — and it is denied the name "birth" for a genuinely precise reason: it has arisen, but has not yet arrived.

Chinese Source

此中何法說名中有？何緣中有非即名生？頌曰：死生二有中，五蘊名中有，未至應至處，故中有非生。論曰：於死有後、在生有前，即彼中間有自體起，為至生處故起此身，二趣中間故名中有。此身已起，何不名生？生謂當來所應至處，依所至義建立生名。此中有身其體雖起而未至彼，故不名生。何謂當來所應至處？所引異熟究竟分明，是謂當來所應至處。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, then, is meant here by "intermediate existence"? Why is intermediate existence not simply called "birth"? Verse: between the two states of the existence of death and the existence of birth, the five aggregates are called intermediate existence; since it has not yet reached the place it is to reach, intermediate existence is therefore not birth. Prose: after the existence of death, and before the existence of birth, an essential body arises precisely within that interval — this body arises specifically in order to reach the place of birth, and, since it stands between two destinies, it is called "intermediate existence." Since this body has already arisen, why is it not called "birth"? "Birth" refers to the place one is to reach in the future — the name "birth" is established specifically on the basis of the sense of "having arrived." Although the essential body of this intermediate-existence being has indeed already arisen, it has not yet arrived at that place, and so it is not called "birth." What, then, is meant by "the place one is to reach in the future"? It is the place where the karmic-ripening that has been induced becomes fully and distinctly manifest — this is what is meant by "the place one is to reach in the future."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction drawn here is genuinely precise, and worth not blurring: intermediate existence is not a lesser or incomplete version of the coming birth, nor merely "birth, not yet finished" — it is its own full five-aggregate existence, already arisen and already functioning, that simply has not yet reached the specific destination its karmic-ripening is heading toward. "Birth," on this account, names an arrival, not a process already underway; a body can be entirely real and entirely active while still not counting as "born" in the technical sense, precisely because the ripening that defines where it belongs has not yet become "fully and distinctly manifest." This sets up the stakes for the debate that follows: if intermediate existence really is this distinct, in-between kind of existence, then denying it outright is not a minor technical disagreement but a genuine dispute over whether death and rebirth are directly and immediately contiguous, or separated by a real, if brief, interval.

23. Does Intermediate Existence Really Exist? The Argument from Unbroken Continuity

A rival school denies intermediate existence outright, holding that death and rebirth may simply be separated by a genuine gap. The treatise's response opens with an argument from ordinary continuity — grain giving rise to a sprout without interruption — before considering, and beginning to dismantle, the rival's own counter-example: a reflected image, which seems to arise at a distance from its source without anything traveling between them.

Chinese Source

有餘部說：從死至生處容間絕，故無中有。此不應許。所以者何？依理教故。理教者何？頌曰：如穀等相續，處無間續生，像實有不成，不等故非譬。一處無二並，非相續二生，說有健達縛，及五七經故。論曰：且依正理中有非無。現見世間相續轉法，要處無間剎那續生。且如世間穀等相續，有情相續理亦應然，剎那續生處必無間。豈不現見有法續生，而於其中處亦有間？如依鏡等從質像生。如是有情死有生有，處雖有間何妨續生？

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a further school that holds: from death to the place of rebirth, there may be a genuine interval and interruption — hence there is no intermediate existence. This should not be accepted. Why is this? Because it goes against both reasoning and scriptural teaching. What are this reasoning and teaching? Verse: just as grain and the like continue in succession, rebirth arises in immediate succession at the very place of death, without interruption; the reflected-image analogy does not hold up as genuinely real, and, being dissimilar, fails as an analogy; no two things can occupy a single place at once; what continues is not two separate arisings in succession; and the Gandharva Sutra, and the sutras of the five and seven existences, affirm it. Prose: let us first consider, on the basis of correct reasoning, that intermediate existence is not non-existent. It is directly observed, in the ordinary world, that continuously-transforming dharmas necessarily arise in the very next moment, at the very same place, without interruption. Just as, in the ordinary world, grain and the like continue in succession, the continuity of sentient beings should, by the same principle, work in just the same way: arising in immediate succession, moment by moment, necessarily without interruption at that place. But is it not directly observed that there are dharmas which continue to arise in succession, and yet an interval of place does exist between them? For instance, a reflected image arises from an object by way of a mirror or the like. In just the same way, could not a sentient being's existence-of-death and existence-of-birth continue in succession despite an interval of place, without any contradiction?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The opening argument trades on a simple, empirically-grounded principle: wherever we can actually observe a continuous causal process unfolding, the next stage always arises at the very place the previous stage left off, with no gap in space. A sprout does not appear at some distant field from the seed that produced it; it emerges right where the seed itself was. If sentient-being continuity is genuinely one continuous causal process — as this tradition holds karma and rebirth to be — the same principle should apply: whatever arises immediately after death should arise right there, not at some spatially separate rebirth-destination with nothing in between.

The rival's counter-example is a clever one, and worth appreciating on its own terms before the refutation begins: a mirror's reflected image seems to be a genuine case of one thing (the image) arising continuously from another (the object) despite being spatially separated, with nothing material actually crossing the gap between them. If this counts as legitimate continuity-at-a-distance, the rival argues, then death and rebirth could work the very same way — a specific karmic pattern "reflecting" into a new existence far away, with no need for anything, including an intermediate-existence being, to actually occupy the space in between. The next section takes this analogy apart in careful, sustained detail.

24. Dismantling the Mirror-Image Analogy

A sustained, careful refutation in two stages: first, that reflected images cannot even be established as genuinely real entities at all — argued through several concrete cases of images that seem to violate basic principles about what can occupy the same place at once — and second, that even granting their reality, the analogy would still fail on its own terms.

Chinese Source

實有諸像理不成故，又非等故為喻不成。謂別色生說名為像，其體實有理所不成。設成非等，故不成喻。言像不成故非喻者，以一處所無二並故。謂於一處鏡色及像並見現前，二色不應同處並有，依異大故。又陿水上兩岸色形，同處一時俱現二像。居兩岸者互見分明，曾無一處並見二色，不應謂此二色俱生。又影與光未嘗同處，然曾見鏡懸置影中，光像顯然現於鏡面，不應於此謂二並生。或言一處無二並者，鏡面月像謂之為二，近遠別見如觀井水，若有並生如何別見？故知諸像於理實無。然諸因緣和合勢力令如是見，以諸法性功能差別難可思議。已辯不成所以非喻。言非等故亦非喻者，以質與像非相續故。謂質與像非一相續，唯依鏡等有像現故，像與本質俱時有故。如死生有是一相續前後無間餘處續生，質像相望無此相續，以不相似故不成喻。又所現像由二生故，謂二緣故諸像得生：一者本質、二者鏡等。二中勝者像依彼生，生有無容由二緣起，唯有死有無別勝依，故所引喻非等於法。亦不可說以外非情精血等緣為勝依性，由化生者空中欻生，於中計何為勝依性？已依正理對破彼宗從死至生處容間絕，是故中有決定非無。

Ru-Yi Translation

This analogy fails, since it cannot be established, by reasoning, that reflected images are genuinely real entities at all; and, moreover, even granting that they were, the analogy would still fail due to dissimilarity. That is: the claim that a separate form-dharma arises in the mirror and is called "the image" cannot be established as genuinely real by reasoning — and even granting, for argument's sake, that it were established, it would still be dissimilar, and so still fail as an analogy. As for saying that the analogy fails because the image cannot be established as real: this is because no single place can hold two things existing side by side at once. That is: at one and the same place, both the mirror's own color-form and the image are seen to appear together at once — yet two distinct form-dharmas ought not to exist together, side by side, in the very same place, since each depends on its own distinct set of great elements. Moreover, upon the surface of a narrow stream, the forms and shapes of both banks appear together, as two images, at the very same place, at the very same time — those standing on either bank see each other clearly and distinctly, yet there has never been a case of two distinct colors being seen side by side occupying one and the same place; it should not be said, therefore, that these two reflected colors genuinely co-arise as real, distinct entities. Moreover, shadow and light have never been observed to occupy the same place at once, and yet a mirror hung within a shaded area has indeed been observed to display, clearly and plainly, a bright reflected image upon its surface; it should not be said, in this case either, that the two genuinely arise side by side as real, co-located entities. Or again, regarding the claim that no single place holds two things side by side: the mirror's surface and the moon's reflected image would then have to be regarded as two separate, really-existing things, seen as near and far, just as when gazing into a well of water — but if they genuinely arose side by side as two real entities, how could they be seen as separate at all? Hence it is known that reflected images, by reasoning, have no real existence whatsoever. Rather, it is simply the combined power of various causes and conditions coming together that produces such an appearance, since the distinct functional capacities inherent in the nature of dharmas are difficult to fully fathom. With this, the reason why reflected images cannot be established as real — and so cannot serve as a valid analogy — has been explained. As for saying that the analogy also fails on account of dissimilarity: this is because the object and its image do not stand in a relation of continuity at all. That is: the object and the image do not belong to a single continuous series, since the image appears only in dependence on a mirror or the like, and the image exists at the very same time as the original object itself — whereas the existence-of-death and the existence-of-birth belong to a single continuous series, arising in immediate succession, one after another without interruption, in another place. The object and the image, considered in relation to one another, have no such continuous-series relationship at all; since they are not truly comparable, the analogy fails to hold. Moreover, the appearing image arises from two causes — that is, images come to arise through two conditions: first, the original object; second, the mirror or the like. Of these two, the image arises specifically in dependence on whichever is the dominant one. But the existence-of-birth cannot possibly arise from two such conditions in this way — it has only the existence-of-death, with no separate dominant support distinct from it. Hence the analogy offered does not correspond to the actual case. Nor can it be said that external, non-sentient conditions such as the parents' reproductive fluids serve as this "dominant support," since a transformation-born being arises suddenly in open space — in such a case, what could possibly be reckoned as its dominant support? Having thus refuted, on the basis of correct reasoning, that other school's position that there may be an interval and interruption between death and the place of rebirth, it is hereby established that intermediate existence is definitely not non-existent.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The refutation's first prong is a small masterpiece of close observation pressed into philosophical service. If a mirror-image were a genuinely real, separate form-dharma, it would have to occupy the same place as the mirror's own surface color — yet this tradition holds that no single place can house two distinct form-dharmas at once, each depending on its own set of great elements. The examples marshaled to press this point are genuinely well chosen: two riverbanks' reflections appearing together on one stretch of water without ever being seen as literally co-located and blended into one; a bright image appearing on a mirror suspended in shadow, where light and shadow ought never to coincide; and the moon's reflection being seen as meaningfully "near or far" relative to the mirror's own surface — which would be senseless if image and surface were both equally real, distinct things occupying the very same spot. The conclusion is genuinely deflationary: images are not real entities at all, merely appearances produced by the confluence of causes and conditions, the deep workings of which this tradition candidly admits are "difficult to fully fathom."

The second prong is, if anything, the sharper point, since it grants the rival's image-realism for the sake of argument and still finds the analogy wanting. Object and image, even if both were real, would not belong to one continuous causal series the way a seed belongs to the same series as its sprout — the image exists only simultaneously with its object, never as its temporal successor, whereas death and rebirth are explicitly one unbroken series unfolding across time. And the image additionally depends on a second condition entirely absent from the death-to-rebirth case: a mirror, or some comparable reflecting surface. Rebirth has nothing playing that role — certainly not, the text notes pointedly, in the case of transformation-birth, which arises "suddenly in open space" with no material substrate of any kind to serve as a counterpart to the mirror. Once both disanalogies are laid out side by side, the rival's example turns out to share almost nothing structurally with the phenomenon it was meant to explain.

25. Scriptural Proof: The Gandharva Present at Conception

Having settled the logical case, the treatise turns to scripture — pressing any rival school that denies these citations with a pointed challenge: if you do not recite this sutra, do you also reject that one? The Gandharva Sutra's account of conception, and a further sutra's specific questions about the "gandharva" present at that moment, both make sense only if something like intermediate existence is real.

Chinese Source

次依聖教證有中有，謂契經言：有有七種，即五趣有、業有、中有。若此契經彼部不誦，豈亦不誦《健達縛經》？如契經言：入母胎者要由三事俱現在前，一者母身是時調適、二者父母交愛和合、三健達縛正現在前。除中有身，何健達縛？前蘊已壞，何現在前？若此契經彼亦不誦，復云何釋《掌馬族經》？如彼經言：汝今知不？此健達縛正現前者，為婆羅門、為剎帝利、為是吠舍、為戍達羅？為東方來、為南西北？前蘊已壞不可言來，此所言來固唯中有。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, let intermediate existence be proven on the basis of the noble teaching: a sutra states that existence is of seven kinds — namely, the five destinies' existences, karma-existence, and intermediate-existence. If that school does not recite this sutra, does it, then, also fail to recite the Gandharva Sutra? As that sutra states: entry into a mother's womb requires three things to be simultaneously present — first, that the mother's body is, at that time, in a suitable condition; second, that the father and mother have united in mutual affection; and third, that a gandharva is currently present. Apart from the intermediate-existence body, what could this "gandharva" possibly be? The aggregates of the prior existence having already perished, what could possibly be "currently present" at all? If that school does not recite this sutra either, then how does it explain the Sūtra of the Horse-Clan Chieftain? As that sutra states: "Do you now know whether this gandharva currently present is a brahmin, a kṣatriya, a vaiśya, or a śūdra? Does it come from the east, or from the south, west, or north?" Since the aggregates of the prior existence have already perished, one cannot speak of anything "coming" from them — this reference to "coming," then, can surely only refer to the intermediate-existence being.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The argument's rhetorical structure is worth noticing: rather than simply asserting that scripture supports intermediate existence, the treatise backs the rival school into a corner by chaining citations together — if you reject the first sutra's classification, fine, but can you also reject this second one, whose very wording (a "gandharva" needing to be "currently present" for conception to occur) makes no sense at all unless something continues to exist in the gap between one life's ending and the next one's beginning? The third citation sharpens the point further: a sutra explicitly asks what caste the "gandharva" belongs to and which direction it comes from — questions that presuppose an actual, locatable entity with its own characteristics, not a mere absence or a bare causal transition with nothing occupying the interval at all.

"Gandharva" here functions as this tradition's own scriptural name for the intermediate-existence being specifically in the context of human conception — a borrowing of the term for a class of celestial musician-spirits in broader Indian religious literature, repurposed within this specific doctrinal context to mean, quite precisely, whatever it is that must be present at the moment of conception for a new human life to actually begin.

26. The Five Non-Returners, and Three Sparks From a Dying Flame

A further sutra names a class of non-returner who "attains final nirvana in the interval" — a category that only makes sense if there is an interval to attain it in. Two rival attempts to explain this term without admitting intermediate existence are considered and rejected in turn, the first refuted by a genuinely vivid three-tiered spark analogy.

Chinese Source

若復不誦如是契經，《五不還經》當云何釋？如契經說：有五不還，一者中般、二者生般、三無行般、四有行般、五者上流。中有若無，何名中般？有餘師執：有天名中，住彼般涅槃，是故名中般。是則應許有生等天；既不許然，故執非善。又經說有七善士趣，謂於前五，中般分三，由處及時近中遠故。譬如札火小星迸時，纔起近即滅，初善士亦爾。譬如鐵火小星迸時，起至中乃滅，二善士亦爾。譬如鐵火大星迸時，遠未墮而滅，三善士亦爾。非彼所執別有中天，有此時處三品差別，故彼所執定非應理。有餘復說：或壽量中間、或近天中間，斷餘煩惱成阿羅漢，是名中般。由至界位或想或尋而般涅槃，故有三品：或取色界眾同分已即般涅槃，是名第一。從是次後受天樂已方般涅槃，是名第二。復從此後入天法會乃般涅槃，是名第三。入法會已復經多時方般涅槃，是名生般。或減多壽方般涅槃非創生時，故名生般。如是所說與火星喻皆不相應。所以者何？以彼處行無差別故。又無色界亦應說有中般涅槃，由彼亦有壽量中間般涅槃故。然不說彼有中般者，如嗢拕南伽他中說：總集眾聖賢，四靜慮各十，三無色各七，唯六謂非想。故彼所執皆是虛妄。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that school, moreover, does not recite this sutra, then how does it explain the Sūtra of the Five Non-Returners? As that sutra states: there are five kinds of non-returner: first, one who attains final nirvana in the interval; second, one who attains it upon birth; third, one who attains it without exertion; fourth, one who attains it through exertion; fifth, one who streams upward. If intermediate existence did not exist, how could there be one who attains final nirvana in the interval? There is a further view held by some masters: there is a heaven called "the Intermediate Heaven," and one who dwells there attains final nirvana — hence it is called "attaining nirvana in the interval." But this would then require accepting that there is likewise such a heaven as "Birth," and so on for the others; and since this is not accepted, this position is not sound. Moreover, a sutra states that there are seven destinations of the good practitioner: that is, of the five non-returner types already mentioned, the "attains-in-the-interval" type divides into three, owing to differences in place and time — near, middle, and far. It is comparable to a small spark thrown off from burning kindling: the moment it flies up, it is extinguished close by — the first good practitioner is just the same. It is comparable to a small spark thrown off from burning iron: once it flies up, it travels a middling distance before being extinguished — the second good practitioner is just the same. It is comparable to a large spark thrown off from burning iron: it travels far, and is extinguished before ever falling to the ground — the third good practitioner is just the same. It is not the case that there is some separate "Intermediate Heaven," as that rival view held — rather, there are these three grades of difference in time and place; hence that rival position is definitely not in accordance with correct principle. There is yet a further view: whether at the midpoint of one's lifespan, or near the midpoint before reaching the heavenly realm, one cuts off the remaining afflictions and becomes an arhat — this is called "attaining nirvana in the interval." Depending on whether one attains final nirvana upon reaching the realm's level, or through remaining perception, or through remaining discursive thought, there are three grades: if one attains final nirvana immediately upon taking on the form realm's class-commonality, this is the first; if, following this, one attains final nirvana only after having experienced heavenly pleasure, this is the second; if, following this in turn, one attains final nirvana only after entering the heavenly assembly of the Dharma, this is the third. If, having entered the assembly of the Dharma, a great deal of time passes before one attains final nirvana, this is called "attaining nirvana upon birth" — or, alternatively, if one attains final nirvana only after a great portion of one's lifespan has been spent, rather than at the very moment of birth, this too is called "attaining nirvana upon birth." What is said in this account, however, does not correspond at all to the spark analogy. Why is this? Because, in this account, there is no difference at all in terms of place. Moreover, if this account were correct, the formless realm too ought to be said to have "attaining nirvana in the interval," since there, too, one may attain final nirvana at the midpoint of one's lifespan — yet it is not said that the formless realm has this "interval" type, as stated in the summary verses: "Gathering together all the noble and worthy ones: each of the four dhyānas has ten, each of the three lower formless spheres has seven, while the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception has only six." Hence that position is entirely groundless.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three-spark image is one of this text's most vividly memorable analogies, and it does real conceptual work rather than merely decorating the point: a spark thrown from soft kindling dies almost instantly and close to its source, a spark from iron travels further before fading, and a spark from a truly hot iron mass travels furthest of all, dying out before it ever completes its arc to the ground — three degrees of the very same phenomenon, differing only in how far and how long the momentum carries before extinguishing. Mapped onto the three grades of "attaining nirvana in the interval," this captures something genuinely precise: all three practitioners die and enter intermediate existence, but differ in how much of that interval elapses, and how far along toward the next rebirth they travel, before final liberation intervenes and that journey is cut short for good.

Both rejected alternatives share a common flaw the text is careful to expose: each tries to explain "the interval" away as something other than a genuine gap between death and rebirth — first as an actual heaven bearing that name (absurd once you notice it would require equally literal heavens called "Birth" and the rest), then as merely an early point within one single, continuous form-realm lifespan (which fails to reproduce the spark analogy's essential feature of differing distance traveled, and would wrongly predict the very same category should apply to the formless realm too, which the canonical count of noble ones per level explicitly does not support). Once both attempts to relocate "the interval" somewhere other than the space between two lives are ruled out, only the straightforward reading remains: the interval is intermediate existence itself.

27. Closing the Debate: A Rebuke, and Reconciling the Hardest Sutras

The debate closes with a genuinely emotional rebuke of scripture-denying schismatics, before carefully reconciling the sutras that most seem to threaten the whole argument — passages describing instantaneous falls into hell, and a haunting verse about finding "no place to rest" in the interval — showing that both admit readings fully compatible with intermediate existence.

Ru-Yi Translation

If, moreover, one does not recite sutras such as these — the unsurpassed Dharma-king has long since passed into final nirvana, and the great generals of the Dharma have likewise entered final nirvana; the noble teaching has since fragmented into many different schools, each running headlong after its own divergent interpretations of the text and its meaning, picking and choosing according to personal inclination, and this tendency has, by now, grown ever more pronounced. How lamentable it is that you cling stubbornly to confusion, going against reason and rejecting the teaching — this is a matter for the deepest sorrow. For anyone who relies on the reasoning and teaching already set forth as the standard of judgment, the real existence of intermediate existence is thereby firmly established. If that is so, then how does a sutra explain that one who commits the most extreme evil karma, overpowering Māra, plunges in his very present body straight into the hell of Uninterrupted Suffering? The intent of this sutra is that, before this person's life has even been given up, the fierce flames of hell have already begun to burn his body; it is on this basis that, once his life ends, he takes on that intermediate existence, and, riding upon this, then falls into the hell of Uninterrupted Suffering. Because the power of his evil karma has grown so overwhelmingly strong, the signs of suffering arrive even before his life has ended — he first undergoes an experience received in this very life, and only afterward undergoes the experience received in his next birth. Why, then, does a sutra state that a certain kind of sentient being who has performed and increased any of the five acts of immediate retribution will, without interval, definitely be reborn in the hells? The intent of this sutra is to rule out that such a person could instead go to a different destiny, and to make clear that such karma definitely conduces to a result received in the very next birth. If one clings only to the literal wording, one would then have to hold that all five such acts must be completed together before rebirth in hell occurs, and that lacking even one, or substituting some other karmic cause, would not produce that result — which would then constitute a very serious error. Moreover, as for the statement "without interval, one is reborn in the hells" — if read too literally, this would have to mean that rebirth occurs instantaneously, without even waiting for the body to break apart. Or, alternatively: who does not accept that intermediate existence is itself a kind of "birth"? The name "hell-being" extends to the intermediate-existence being as well, since, in immediate succession after the existence of death, when intermediate existence arises, this too may be called "birth," being the very means by which birth is accomplished. The sutra states that one is "reborn in the hells without interval" — it does not state that this is, at that very moment, already the existence of birth proper. If that is so, then how is a further scriptural verse to be reconciled with this? As a scriptural verse states: "Twice-born one, you have now passed beyond your prime, and, in decline, draw near to King Yama; though you wish to set out upon the road ahead, you have no provisions for the journey, and, seeking to rest in the interval, you find no place to stay." If intermediate existence truly exists, how could the World-Honored One say that, in that interval, there is no place to stay at all? The intent of this verse is to show that, within human existence, one swiftly returns to dissolution, with no momentary pause at all. Or, alternatively: that very intermediate-existence being, in journeying toward its place of rebirth, likewise has no momentary pause, since its movement encounters no obstruction whatsoever. How, then, can it be known that the sutra's intent is as I have just explained, and not otherwise? But equally: how can you, in turn, know that it is otherwise, and not as I have explained? Since these two challenges are equally matched, why should you press the objection only in one direction? Both of these two readings are entirely consistent with the sutra, without any contradiction — how, then, can this verse be used to prove, one-sidedly, that intermediate existence does not exist? Whenever a citation is offered as proof, its reasoning ought to admit of no other possible interpretation; since this citation clearly admits of another interpretation, it fails to serve as valid proof.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The emotional register shifts noticeably here, and I have preserved it rather than flattening it into purely dry argument — this is one of the few places in the text so far where the author's frustration with rival schools becomes genuinely personal, mourning the Buddha's and his chief disciples' long absence as the root cause of the tradition's fragmentation into competing interpretations, each "picking and choosing according to personal inclination." Whether or not one shares the author's confidence in his own reading, the passage is a striking piece of testimony to how live and contentious this specific debate actually was within early Buddhist scholasticism.

The reconciliations that follow share a common method worth naming explicitly: rather than denying that these sutras say what they appear to say, each is shown to admit of a second, equally natural reading that leaves room for intermediate existence — "without interval" read as "without exception" or "without a different destiny intervening" rather than "without any temporal gap," and "no place to rest" read as being about the swiftness of the transition rather than a flat denial that any transitional state exists at all. The closing methodological point is, I think, the sharpest move in the entire debate: a citation can only decisively settle a question if it rules out every other reading. Since the rival's own proof-text admits of at least one reading fully compatible with intermediate existence, it was never capable of serving as decisive proof against it in the first place — which is a genuinely different, and more rigorous, standard than simply insisting one's own preferred reading must be correct.

The fascicle opened by mapping the desire realm's twenty places, the form realm's seventeen (or, on the Kashmiri masters' count, sixteen) heavens, and the formless realm's genuinely striking claim to occupy no spatial location whatsoever — a claim that opened directly onto a real, unresolved tension in the Vaibhāṣika account of what sustains a formless being's mental continuity without any body at all, before the Sautrāntika offered a leaner alternative. The fascicle's second half turned to the five destinies and their required neutral moral nature, working through a genuine, unresolved tension over whether destiny can include affliction, and a sharp argument — honestly left incomplete on one point — for why intermediate existence itself is never counted as a destiny.
