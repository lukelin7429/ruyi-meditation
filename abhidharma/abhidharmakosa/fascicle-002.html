Does the Eye See, or the Consciousness?

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 2, which finishes Chapter 1's classification of the eighteen elements: visibility and resistance, moral quality and realm-affiliation, contamination, the aggregate of applied and sustained thought, what counts as "real" versus a single unrepeatable instant, the famous debate over whether the eye or the eye-consciousness truly sees, one eye or two, how faculties are shaped down to the atomic level, the two supports of every sense, why "support" and consciousness are named after the faculty rather than the object, and how body, eye, form, and consciousness relate across meditative realms.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 2 complete Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 2 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. Visible and Invisible

With the eighteen elements' number settled, the treatise begins cross-classifying them by other properties, starting with a question and a dense verse governing several distinctions at once.

Chinese Source

復次於前所說十八界中，幾有見、幾無見？幾有對、幾無對？幾善、幾不善、幾無記？頌曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Further: among the eighteen elements spoken of above, how many are visible, and how many invisible? How many are resistant, and how many non-resistant? How many are wholesome, how many unwholesome, and how many neutral? A verse states:

一有見謂色，十有色有對，此除色聲八，無記餘三種。

One is visible: the element of visible form. Ten, being matter, are resistant. Of these ten, excluding visible form and sound, the remaining eight are neutral; the rest are of all three kinds.

論曰：十八界中色界有見，以可示現此彼差別。由此義准，說餘無見。

Prose: Among the eighteen elements, the element of visible form is visible, because it can be pointed to and shown, exhibiting "here" and "there" differences. On this same principle, by inference, the remaining seventeen are said to be invisible.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Visible" here is a narrow, technical sense, not a loose synonym for "perceptible." It means specifically that one can point at part of it and show a difference from another part — this patch is here, that patch is there. Sound, smell, taste, and the tangible are all perfectly perceptible by their own senses, yet none of them is "visible" in this sense: you cannot point at a sound and show that one part of it is "here" and another "there" the way you can with a colour or a shape. Only visible form itself has this spatial, showable quality, which is why exactly one element out of eighteen earns the label, while the other seventeen — including several that are directly sensed every moment — are technically "invisible."

2. Three Kinds of Resistance, and a Digression into Animal Eyes

The treatise now unpacks "resistant," and the explanation turns out to need three distinct senses of the word — including a memorable, almost zoological aside.

Chinese Source

已說有見無見。唯色蘊攝十界有對，對是礙義。此復三種：障礙、境界、所緣異故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Visible and invisible have now been explained. Resistance belongs only to the ten elements comprised within the aggregate of form; "resistant" has the sense of "obstruction." This, further, is of three kinds, differing as obstructive resistance, object-domain resistance, and objective-support resistance.

障礙有對，謂十色界。自於他處被礙不生，如手礙手或石礙石或二相礙。

Obstructive resistance refers to the ten elements of form: each, being obstructed by another at the same place, does not arise there — as when a hand is obstructed by a hand, or a stone by a stone, or the two obstruct each other.

境界有對，謂十二界法界一分諸有境法於色等境，故《施設論》作如是言：有眼於水有礙非陸，如魚等眼。有眼於陸有礙非水，從多分說，如人等眼。有眼俱礙，如畢舍遮、室獸摩羅及捕魚人、蝦蟇等眼。有俱非礙，謂除前相。

Object-domain resistance refers to the twelve elements — together with the portion of the dharma-element that has an object — being restricted to their own objects, such as visible form and the rest. Hence the Prajñaptiśāstra states: there are eyes obstructed in water but not on land, such as the eyes of fish. There are eyes obstructed on land but not in water — speaking of the majority of cases — such as the eyes of humans. There are eyes obstructed in both, such as the eyes of piśācas, crocodiles, fishermen, and frogs. There are eyes obstructed in neither, meaning those falling outside the aforementioned kinds.

有眼於夜有礙非晝，如諸蝙蝠鵂鶹等眼。有眼於晝有礙非夜，從多分說，如人等眼。有眼俱礙，如狗野干馬豹豺狼猫狸等眼。有俱非礙，謂除前相。此等名為境界有對。

There are eyes obstructed at night but not by day, such as the eyes of bats and owls. There are eyes obstructed by day but not at night — speaking of the majority of cases — such as the eyes of humans. There are eyes obstructed in both, such as the eyes of dogs, jackals, horses, leopards, wolves, and cats. There are eyes obstructed in neither, meaning those falling outside the aforementioned kinds. These are named object-domain resistance.

所緣有對，謂心心所於自所緣。境界、所緣復有何別？若於彼法此有功能，即說彼為此法境界。心心所法執彼而起，彼於心等名為所緣。

Objective-support resistance refers to mind and mental factors being restricted to their own objective support. What, then, is the difference between "object-domain" and "objective-support"? If this faculty or consciousness has the functional capacity with respect to that dharma, that dharma is called this dharma's "object-domain." When mind and mental factors take hold of that same dharma and arise in dependence on it, that dharma, with respect to the mind, is named its "objective-support."

云何眼等於自境界所緣轉時說名有礙？越彼於餘此不轉故。或復礙者是和會義，謂眼等法於自境界及自所緣和會轉故。應知此中唯就障礙有對而說，故但言十有色有對，更相障故。

How is it that the eye and the rest, when operating with respect to their own object-domain or objective-support, are said to be "obstructed"? Because, apart from that proper domain, they do not operate with respect to anything else. Or, further, "obstruction" here carries the sense of "conjunction": the eye and the other faculties operate only in conjunction with their own object-domain and their own objective-support. It should be understood that the earlier verse, in simply stating that "the ten, being matter, are resistant," meant only obstructive resistance — because these ten mutually obstruct one another.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Resistant" turns out to name three genuinely different things, and the treatise is careful not to blur them. Obstructive resistance is the plainest: two pieces of matter cannot occupy the same place, the way one hand blocks another or one stone blocks another — this is the sense the opening verse actually intends when it counts "ten" resistant elements. Object-domain resistance is a faculty's restriction to its own proper territory, and the treatise illustrates it with a small survey of animal vision quoted from another Abhidharma text, the Prajñaptiśāstra: fish see in water but not on land, humans (mostly) see on land but not in water, some creatures manage both, bats and owls see by night but not day, humans (mostly) see by day but not night, and creatures like dogs and wolves manage both. Objective-support resistance, the finest distinction of the three, is about mind and mental factors specifically: a faculty's "object-domain" is simply whatever it has the functional capacity to engage, but when a moment of mind or a mental factor actually arises by taking hold of that object, the same object is now called its "objective-support" — the same territory, named differently depending on whether we are speaking of mere capacity or of an actual act of cognition arising from it.

The zoological aside is not decoration. It exists to make a genuinely abstract point — that every cognitive faculty is restricted to operating within its own proper domain — vivid through cases any reader already half-knows from observing animals, before the discussion returns to the much more exacting distinction between a faculty's mere "domain" and mind's active "objective support."

3. A Closing Tetralemma on Resistance, and an Elder's View

Before leaving resistance behind, the treatise checks whether its two subtler senses — object-domain and obstructive resistance — always travel together, and records one further scholarly voice on the topic.

Chinese Source

若法境界有對，亦障礙有對耶？應作四句。謂七心界法界一分諸相應法是第一句。色等五境是第二句。眼等五根是第三句。法界一分非相應法是第四句。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a dharma has object-domain resistance, does it also have obstructive resistance? A fourfold analysis should be made. The seven mind-elements, and the portion of the dharma-element consisting of associated mental factors, form the first case [object-domain resistance without obstructive resistance]. The five objects — form and the rest — form the second case. The five faculties — the eye and the rest — form the third case. The portion of the dharma-element that is dissociated from mind forms the fourth case.

此中大德鳩摩邏多作如是說：是處心欲生，他礙令不起，應知是有對，無對此相違。此是所許。

Here, the great elder Kumāralāta stated it this way: wherever a mind wishes to arise, and something else obstructs it and prevents its arising, this should be known as resistant; non-resistant is the reverse of this. This too is accepted.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four cases sort dharmas by whether they have a cognitive domain, physical obstructiveness, both, or neither: the seven mind-elements and their associated mental factors have a domain but no matter to obstruct anything with; the five sense-objects and five sense-organs have both, since they are matter that also functions within a domain; and the unconditioned dharmas and mind-dissociated formations, within the dharma-element, have neither. Kumāralāta's citation — a named elder whose view the treatise records without simply adopting it as the only word on the subject — reframes resistance psychologically rather than physically: something is "resistant," on his account, whenever a mind that wants to arise is blocked by something else from doing so. It is a small but telling moment of the treatise showing its work: naming the person a definition comes from, rather than presenting every view anonymously as "the" position.

4. Wholesome, Unwholesome, and Neutral

The treatise finally reaches the third and last of this section's three-way questions, closing the circle back to why exactly eight of the ten matter elements were said, several sections ago, to be permanently neutral.

Chinese Source

如是已說有對無對。於此所說十有對中，除色及聲餘八無記。謂五色根、香、味、觸境，不可記為善不善性，故名無記。有說：不能記異熟果故名無記。若爾，無漏應唯無記。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resistant and non-resistant have now been explained. Among the ten resistant elements just discussed, excluding visible form and sound, the remaining eight are neutral — that is, the five material faculties, and the objects of smell, taste, and touch, cannot be reckoned as having a wholesome or unwholesome nature; hence they are named "neutral." Some say: it is because they are unable to determine a ripened result that they are named neutral. If that were so, the uncontaminated ought to be entirely neutral as well [which is not accepted].

其餘十界通善等三，謂七心界與無貪等相應名善，貪等相應名為不善，餘名無記。

The remaining ten elements are common to all three. That is: the seven mind-elements, when associated with non-greed and the rest, are named wholesome; when associated with greed and the rest, are named unwholesome; the rest are named neutral.

法界若是無貪等性相應等起擇滅名善，若貪等性相應等起名為不善，餘名無記。

As for the dharma-element: if it has the nature of non-greed and the rest, is associated with them, arises from them, or is cessation through discernment, it is named wholesome. If it has the nature of greed and the rest, is associated with them, or arises from them, it is named unwholesome. The rest is named neutral.

色界聲界若善不善心力等起身語表攝，是善不善，餘是無記。

As for the element of visible form and the element of sound: if they are produced by the power of a wholesome or unwholesome mind, and so are comprised within bodily or vocal manifestation, they are wholesome or unwholesome; the rest is neutral.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reasoning closes a loop opened all the way back when unmanifest form was first defined. The five sense faculties and the objects of smell, taste, and touch are pure receptors and raw physical qualities — nothing about an eyeball or a patch of sourness is itself a moral act, so they are permanently neutral. Visible form and sound are different precisely because they can be a moral act: a respectful bow or a violent gesture is visible form produced by a wholesome or unwholesome mind and expressed as bodily manifestation (身表); a kind word or a cruel one is sound produced the same way and expressed as vocal manifestation (語表). It is only when colour or sound rises to the level of manifest bodily or verbal karma that it inherits the moral quality of the mind behind it — otherwise, like a rock's colour or the sound of wind, it is neutral too.

The seven mind-elements and the dharma-element are judged the same way, by what they are yoked to: a moment of mind (or a mental factor, in the dharma-element) is wholesome when paired with the three wholesome roots — non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion — and unwholesome when paired with their opposites; cessation through discernment, already met early in this fascicle, is explicitly named wholesome outright, since it is nothing other than liberation itself. The proposed alternative definition of "neutral" — that it simply means "producing no karmic ripening" — is raised and quietly set aside, since it would wrongly force every uncontaminated dharma into the neutral category too, when uncontaminated wholesome states are not treated as merely neutral in this system.

5. Which Elements Belong to Which Realm?

The treatise now returns to the three-tier cosmology touched on earlier — the desire, form, and formless realms — and asks how many of the eighteen elements a being in each realm actually has.

Chinese Source

已說善等。十八界中幾欲界繫、幾色界繫、幾無色界繫？頌曰：欲界繫十八，色界繫十四，除香味二識，無色繫後三。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wholesome and the rest have now been explained. Among the eighteen elements, how many are bound to the desire realm, how many to the form realm, and how many to the formless realm? A verse states: bound to the desire realm are all eighteen; bound to the form realm are fourteen, excluding smell, taste, and their two consciousnesses; bound to the formless realm are the last three.

論曰：繫謂繫屬，即被縛義。欲界所繫具足十八，色界所繫唯十四種，除香味境及鼻舌識。除香味者，段食性故，離段食欲方得生彼。除鼻舌識，無所緣故。

Prose: "Bound" means belonging to, in the sense of being tied. What is bound to the desire realm is complete in all eighteen. What is bound to the form realm is only fourteen kinds, excluding the objects of smell and taste, and the nose- and tongue-consciousnesses. Smell and taste are excluded because they have the nature of morsel-food; only by being free of the desire for morsel-food can one be born there. The nose- and tongue-consciousnesses are excluded because they would then have no object to take.

若爾，觸界於彼應無，如香味境段食性故，彼所有觸非段食性。若爾，香味類亦應然，香味離食無別受用，觸有別用持根衣等，彼離食欲香味無用，有根衣等故觸非無。

If that is so, one might object: the element of the tangible should likewise be absent there, since it too is involved with morsel-food just as smell and taste are. In reply: whatever tangible exists there is not of the nature of morsel-food. If that is so, one might press further: smell and taste should likewise have some other use. In reply: apart from food, smell and taste have no separate use, whereas the tangible has a separate use — sustaining the faculties, clothing, and the like. Since beings free from the desire for food have no use for smell and taste, those are excluded; but since they still have faculties, clothing, and the like, the tangible is not absent.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The pattern connects directly to what was already said about the form realm: beings there have gone beyond desire for "morsel-food" (段食, ordinary chewed and swallowed food), and smell and taste exist, in this system, for no other reason than to serve that appetite — so once the appetite is gone, both the objects and their two consciousnesses drop out entirely, with nothing left for them to do. The exchange that follows is a small model of how this treatise argues: an objector presses the same logic further each time (if food-relatedness disqualifies smell and taste, shouldn't it disqualify touch too? and if touch survives because it has other uses, don't smell and taste have other uses as well?), and each reply has to find the actual asymmetry rather than simply repeating the rule. The answer that survives is functional: touch is still needed to sustain the body's own faculties and to feel contact with clothing and surroundings, uses that have nothing to do with eating, while smell and taste, once food is set aside, have simply nothing left to do.

6. A Second View on Touch, and Why Nose and Tongue Remain

The debate over the form realm's remaining senses continues with a further scholarly opinion, then a defence of why the nose and tongue faculties persist even without smell or taste to sense.

Chinese Source

有餘師說：住此依彼靜慮等至見色聞聲，輕安俱起有殊勝觸攝益於身，是故此三生彼靜慮猶相隨逐，香味不爾，故在彼無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some other masters say: those dwelling there, based on the level of meditative absorption they have attained, see forms and hear sounds; and pliancy arises together with a special kind of tangible sensation that nourishes the body. For this reason, these three — visible form, sound, and touch — continue to accompany one born into that meditative state, whereas smell and taste do not work this way, and so are absent there.

若爾，鼻舌彼應非有，如香味境彼無用故。不爾，二根於彼有用，謂起言說及莊嚴身。

If that is so, one might object: the nose and tongue faculties should likewise be absent there, since, like the objects of smell and taste, they would have no use. In reply: not so — the two faculties still have a use there, namely, producing speech and adorning the body.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This second scholarly view sharpens the earlier explanation: it isn't just that touch has non-food uses in general, but that a specific, refined kind of touch — pliancy (輕安, praśrabdhi, a meditative factor of bodily and mental ease) — is bound up with the deep meditative states that define life in the form realm itself, so it naturally persists there alongside seeing and hearing. Nose and tongue, meanwhile, survive not because they still smell or taste anything, but for reasons that have nothing to do with their original sensory job: a complete, well-formed body needs a nose and a tongue to look whole, and a tongue is needed to produce speech.

The debate presses one step further still — whether the form realm's nose and tongue are full functioning faculties or merely their physical, decorative bases without real sensory capability, drawing an analogy to the male organ (wholly absent in the form realm, since it serves no purpose there at all) and to a fetus that grows complete faculties even though it is destined to die before ever using them. We have summarized this final, very fine-grained volley rather than translating it line by line, since it presses a technical distinction — a faculty's mere physical basis versus its full functioning arising — well past what changes the substantive conclusion already given: nose and tongue remain in the form realm for speech and bodily completeness, even without smell and taste to sense.

7. The Formless Realm: Only Three Elements Remain

The three-realm survey closes with its starkest case: a mode of existence with no physical body at all.

Chinese Source

無色界繫唯有後三，所謂意、法及意識界，要離色欲於彼得生，故無色中無十色界。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is bound to the formless realm consists only of the last three — namely, the mind element, the dharma element, and the mind-consciousness element. Only by being free of desire for form can one be born there; hence, within the formless realm, the ten elements of form are absent.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three realms now form a clean, descending sequence: the desire realm keeps all eighteen elements; the form realm drops smell, taste, and their two consciousnesses once the appetite for food is gone, but keeps a physical body and its remaining senses; the formless realm drops all ten elements of form outright, since what is left behind there is not merely food but form itself — matter of any kind. What remains is only mind: the mind element, the dharma element it takes as its object, and the mind-consciousness that arises from the two together. A being in the formless realm has no eye, no body, no shape at all — only a bare continuum of mental events, which is exactly what its highest meditative attainments (infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception) are meditations on. The Chinese text goes on to press one further, narrower debate about whether the form realm's sexual characteristics survive in the same way as its nose and tongue — a fine continuation of the faculty-versus-use argument already covered rather than new doctrine, which we are not translating line by line here.

8. Which Elements Can Be Uncontaminated?

One last remark closes the three-realm survey; then the treatise opens an entirely new cross-classification, asking how many of the eighteen elements are ever capable of being uncontaminated at all.

Chinese Source

依緣無故五識亦無，故唯後三無色界繫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since their supports and objects are both absent, the five sense-consciousnesses are absent as well; hence only the last three are bound to the formless realm.

已說界繫，十八界中幾有漏、幾無漏？頌曰：意法意識通，所餘唯有漏。

Bound-to-realm has now been explained. Among the eighteen elements, how many are contaminated, and how many uncontaminated? A verse states: the mind, the dharma, and the mind-consciousness elements are common to both; the remainder are contaminated only.

論曰：意及意識道諦攝者，名為無漏，餘名有漏。法界若是道諦無為，名為無漏，餘名有漏。餘十五界唯名有漏。

Prose: When the mind element and the mind-consciousness element are comprised within the truth of the path, they are named uncontaminated; the rest are named contaminated. When the dharma element is the truth of the path, or is the unconditioned, it is named uncontaminated; the rest is named contaminated. The remaining fifteen elements are named contaminated only.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing line on the formless realm quietly reinforces why only three elements remain there: a consciousness needs both a support and an object to arise at all, and since the formless realm has neither a physical faculty nor a physical object for any of the five sense-consciousnesses, those five simply cannot arise — not by some special exclusion rule, but because the ordinary conditions for their arising are entirely missing.

The new question then narrows sharply: of all eighteen elements, only three — mind, dharma, and mind-consciousness — are ever even capable of being uncontaminated, and only when they specifically belong to the path or the unconditioned. The other fifteen, including every physical sense-organ and every physical sense-object, are contaminated without exception, no matter the circumstance. This is a strong claim worth sitting with: matter itself, on this system, is never uncontaminated — liberation is not a property any eye, ear, or patch of colour could ever have. Only mind, and what mind takes as its object, can ever rise to that level, and even then only in the narrow case where what is grasped is specifically the path itself or an unconditioned dharma like cessation through discernment.

9. Applied and Sustained Thought — and the Map of Meditative Absorption

This cross-classification connects directly to meditation practice: whether an element involves applied thought (尋, vitarka), sustained thought (伺, vicāra), both, or neither turns out to track the boundaries between the levels of meditative absorption themselves.

Chinese Source

十八界中幾有尋有伺、幾無尋唯伺、幾無尋無伺？頌曰：五識唯尋伺，後三三餘無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the eighteen elements, how many have both applied and sustained thought, how many have only sustained thought, and how many have neither? A verse states: the five sense-consciousnesses have both applied and sustained thought only. The last three are of all three kinds. The rest have neither.

論曰：眼等五識有尋有伺，由與尋伺恒共相應，以行相麁外門轉故、顯義決定，故說唯言。

Prose: The eye-consciousness and the other four have both applied and sustained thought, because they are constantly and invariably associated with applied and sustained thought, their mode of activity being coarse and operating through the outward-facing sense-doors. To make this meaning definite, the word "only" is used.

後三謂是意、法、意識，根、境、識中各居後故。此後三界皆通三品，意界、意識界及相應法界，除尋與伺。

"The last three" means the mind, dharma, and mind-consciousness elements, since each occupies the last position among the faculties, objects, and consciousnesses respectively. These last three elements are all common to all three kinds — the mind element, the mind-consciousness element, and the dharma element associated with them — excluding applied and sustained thought themselves.

若在欲界初靜慮中有尋有伺，靜慮中間無尋唯伺，第二靜慮以上諸地乃至有頂無尋無伺。

If these three are in the desire realm or the first meditative absorption, they have both applied and sustained thought. In the intermediate meditative state, they have only sustained thought, without applied thought. From the second meditative absorption upward, through to the summit of existence, they have neither applied nor sustained thought.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Applied thought (尋, vitarka) and sustained thought (伺, vicāra) are traditionally illustrated by a bell: applied thought is the initial strike that brings the mind onto its object, coarse and sudden; sustained thought is the reverberation that follows, subtler and lingering, examining what the strike brought into range. The five sense-consciousnesses always have both, simply because of how they work — reaching outward through the sense-doors to a coarse object is already, in effect, a kind of striking-and-examining, regardless of anyone's level of meditative attainment. What actually varies with practice is the mind, dharma, and mind-consciousness elements alone, and the treatise uses them to mark the very boundaries between stages of absorption: desire-realm consciousness and the first dhyāna both still strike and examine their object; a special transitional state between the first and second dhyāna has let go of the striking but still lingers in sustained examination; from the second dhyāna all the way up to the highest point of existence, both drop away entirely, leaving an absorption with no discursive activity, coarse or subtle, at all. The classification of eighteen elements has, without warning, turned into a map of the meditative path itself.

10. A Closer Look: Four Kinds of "Having Thought" Within One Level

The treatise now turns the same question on applied and sustained thought themselves: even within a single meditative level said to "have both," do the two factors have both with respect to their own selves?

Chinese Source

由此故言有尋伺地有四品法：一有尋有伺，謂除尋伺餘相應法。二無尋唯伺，謂即是尋。三無尋無伺，謂即一切非相應法。四無伺唯尋，謂即是伺。

Ru-Yi Translation

For this reason, it is said that within a level having both applied and sustained thought, dharmas fall into four categories. First, having both: every associated dharma except applied and sustained thought themselves. Second, without applied thought but with sustained thought only: applied thought itself. Third, without either: every dissociated dharma. Fourth, without sustained thought but with applied thought only: sustained thought itself.

餘十色界尋伺俱無，常與尋伺不相應故。

The remaining ten elements of form have neither applied nor sustained thought at all, since they are never associated with either.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The logic is self-referential and worth working through slowly. Ask of any ordinary mental factor, in a level that "has both applied and sustained thought," whether applied and sustained thought accompany it — yes, both do, so it falls in the first category. Now ask the same question of applied thought itself: does applied thought come paired with a second instance of applied thought? No — nothing is its own accompanying duplicate — but it is genuinely accompanied by sustained thought, so applied thought itself is classed as "without applied thought, with sustained thought only." Sustained thought gets the mirror-image answer for the same reason: no second instance of itself, but genuinely accompanied by applied thought, so it is classed as "without sustained thought, with applied thought only." Dissociated dharmas, not being the kind of thing that co-arises with mental factors in this way at all, have neither. The ten elements of form need no such careful sorting: being matter, they are simply never associated with either factor, full stop.

11. Are the Five Senses Truly "Non-Discriminating"? The One-Legged Horse

The objection lands squarely: elsewhere the five sense-consciousnesses are called "non-discriminating," yet this fascicle has just said they always have applied thought — which sounds like a kind of discrimination. The treatise resolves this by splitting "discrimination" into three very different things.

Chinese Source

如何得說無分別耶？頌曰：說五無分別，由計度隨念，以意地散慧，意諸念為體。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, then, can it be said that the five are without discrimination? A verse states: the five are said to be without discrimination because of deliberative and recollective discrimination — these have, as their essence, the mind-ground's scattered wisdom, and the mind's various acts of recollection.

論曰：傳說，分別略有三種：一自性分別、二計度分別、三隨念分別。由五識身雖有自性而無餘二，說無分別，如一足馬名為無足。

Prose: It has been transmitted that discrimination is, in brief, of three kinds: self-nature discrimination, deliberative discrimination, and recollective discrimination. Because the body of five consciousnesses, although it has self-nature discrimination, lacks the other two, it is said to be without discrimination — just as a horse with only one leg is called legless.

自性分別體唯是尋，後心所中自當辯釋。餘二分別如其次第。意地散慧諸念為體。散謂非定。意識相應散慧，名為計度分別。若定若散意識相應諸念，名為隨念分別。

Self-nature discrimination has applied thought alone as its essence, and will be explained in its own right later, in the section on mental factors. The remaining two kinds of discrimination, in order, have as their essence the mind-ground's scattered wisdom, and the mind's various acts of recollection. "Scattered" means not in meditative concentration. Wisdom associated with the mental consciousness, when scattered, is named deliberative discrimination. Any recollection associated with the mental consciousness, whether concentrated or scattered, is named recollective discrimination.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The one-legged horse is one of the most memorable images in this fascicle, and it does real philosophical work: the five sense-consciousnesses are not literally free of all discrimination — they do have "self-nature discrimination," which turns out to be nothing more than applied thought (尋) itself, the same factor already discussed at length. But that is only one of three legs. What the five senses genuinely lack are deliberative discrimination — the mental consciousness's own discursive, judging wisdom, active only outside meditative absorption — and recollective discrimination — the mental consciousness's capacity for memory, active whether concentrated or not. Since these two are what ordinary language actually means by "discriminating" or "judging" something, calling the five senses "non-discriminating" is true in every sense that matters, even though, strictly, they retain the one weakest leg of the three.

This quietly answers a question a careful reader of Ru-Yi's other translations may already have been asking: how can a sense-consciousness register a colour or a sound at all without already having judged or interpreted it? The answer this treatise gives is that bare registration — applied thought's minimal "aiming" at an object — is not yet judgment, memory, or interpretation. Those belong entirely to the sixth consciousness, arising a moment later, exactly as fascicle 1 of the Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra describes the "sudden mind" of the eye-consciousness giving way to the "seeking" and "deciding" minds of the mental consciousness that follows it.

12. Which Elements Have an Object? Which Are Part of a Living Body?

One dense verse now governs two more cross-classifications at once: which elements take an object at all, and which count as "appropriated" — genuinely part of a living, sentient body, rather than inert matter.

Chinese Source

如是已說有尋伺等。十八界中幾有所緣、幾無所緣？幾有執受、幾無執受？頌曰：七心法界半，有所緣餘無，前八界及聲，無執受餘二。

Ru-Yi Translation

Applied and sustained thought and the rest have now been explained. Among the eighteen elements, how many have an object, and how many have none? How many are appropriated, and how many are not? A verse states: the seven mind elements, and half of the dharma element, have an object; the rest have none. The first eight elements, together with sound, are without appropriation; the rest fall into both categories.

論曰：六識意界及法界攝諸心所法，名有所緣，能取境故。餘十色界及法界攝不相應法，名無所緣，義准成故。

Prose: The six consciousnesses, the mind element, and the portion of the dharma element comprising the mental factors — these are named "having an object," because they are able to take hold of an object. The remaining ten elements of form, and the portion of the dharma element comprising the dissociated formations, are named "without an object," established by the same reasoning.

如是已說有所緣等。十八界中九無執受，前七心界及法界全，此八及聲皆無執受。所餘九界各通二門，謂有執受、無執受故。

Thus, "having an object" and the rest have now been explained. Among the eighteen elements, nine are without appropriation: the first seven mind-elements and the entire dharma element — these eight, together with sound, are all without appropriation. The remaining nine elements each fall into both categories — that is, they may be either appropriated or non-appropriated.

眼等五根住現在世名有執受，過去未來名無執受。色香味觸住現在世不離五根名有執受，若住現在非不離根過去未來名無執受。

The eye and the other four faculties, when abiding in the present, are named appropriated; when past or future, they are named non-appropriated. Form, smell, taste, and touch, when abiding in the present and not separated from the five faculties, are named appropriated; if present but separated from the faculties, or if past or future, they are named non-appropriated.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Having an object" turns out to be a simple, clean line: mind and mental factors take hold of something, matter and dissociated formations do not — so only the seven mind-elements and the associated half of the dharma element make the cut, and everything else is automatically "without an object" by the same logic in reverse.

"Appropriated" (執受, upātta) is the more interesting distinction, and it names something quite specific: matter that is genuinely part of a living, sentient body right now, as opposed to matter that merely resembles it. Nine elements — the seven mind-elements, the dharma element, and sound — can never be appropriated at all: mind and mental factors aren't matter to begin with, and sound, even a living being's own voice, is treated as something emitted rather than as living tissue. The other nine — the five faculties and four of the five objects (form, smell, taste, touch, but not sound) — can go either way, and the deciding line is exact: your own eye, right now, in this present moment, is appropriated; the very same eye a moment ago or a moment from now is not, because appropriation belongs only to the present. The colour of your living skin, the taste in your mouth, the smell of your own body, and the warmth felt in your hand are appropriated in exactly the same way, but only while they remain part of a functioning body in the present — the same colour once it is, say, a bruise that has healed and sloughed off, or the same taste once swallowed and gone, no longer counts.

13. What "Appropriated" Really Means — Hair, Nails, and Felt Touch

The treatise now sharpens "appropriated" with a surprising list of exceptions, then gives the concept its actual technical definition.

Chinese Source

如在身內除與根合，髮毛爪齒大小便利洟唾血等，及在身外地水等中色香味觸，雖在現世而無執受。

Ru-Yi Translation

For example: within the body, excluding what is joined with the faculties — hair, body hair, nails, teeth, feces, urine, nasal mucus, saliva, blood, and the like — and, outside the body, the form, smell, taste, and touch found in earth, water, and the like: although these exist in the present, they are without appropriation.

有執受者，此言何義？心心所法共所執持攝為依處名有執受，損益展轉更相隨故。即諸世間說有覺觸，眾緣所觸覺樂等故。與此相違名無執受。如是已說有執受等。

"Appropriated" — what does this term actually mean? That which mind and mental factors together hold and sustain, taking it as their basis, is named appropriated, because harm and benefit to one, in turn, follow to the other. This is why ordinary people speak of "felt touch" — the various conditions that produce a touch bring about a feeling such as pleasure and the like. The opposite of this is named non-appropriated. Thus, appropriated and the rest have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The exceptions are the memorable part, and they match ordinary experience precisely: hair, nails, teeth, and bodily fluids like blood, saliva, urine, and mucus are all, in one sense, "inside" or attached to the living body, yet the treatise excludes every one of them from "appropriated" status. The reason becomes clear with the definition that follows: appropriation is not about location but about a two-way sympathetic bond — mind and mental factors take some matter as their basis such that harm to that matter is genuinely felt, and the mind's condition in turn affects the matter. Cut a strand of hair or trim a nail, and nothing hurts, because that tissue was never bound to the mind this way; cut living skin, and it does, because skin is. This is also, the treatise notes, the technical basis for "felt touch" in ordinary speech — a stimulus registers as pleasant or painful precisely because it lands on matter genuinely appropriated by mind, not on hair, nails, or waste products passing through or out of the body.

14. Primary or Derived Matter? And Which Elements Are Built of Atoms?

One more dense verse governs two further classifications: which elements are the four great elements themselves versus matter derived from them, and which are literally composed of clustered atoms.

Chinese Source

十八界中幾大種性、幾所造性？幾可積集、幾非積集？頌曰：觸界中有二，餘九色所造，法一分亦然，十色可積集。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the eighteen elements, how many have the nature of the primary great elements, and how many are derived matter? How many are accumulable, and how many are not? A verse states: within the element of the tangible there are both; the remaining nine elements of form are derived matter; a portion of the dharma element is likewise so; the ten elements of form are accumulable.

論曰：觸界通二，謂大種及所造。大種有四，謂堅性等。所造有七，謂滑性等，依大種生故名所造。餘九色界唯是所造，謂五色根、色等四境。法界一分無表業色亦唯所造。

Prose: The element of the tangible is common to both: the great elements themselves, and derived matter. The great elements are four — solidity and the rest. The derived are seven — smoothness and the rest — named "derived" because they arise in dependence on the great elements. The remaining nine elements of form are entirely derived: the five material faculties, and the four objects — visible form and the rest. The portion of the dharma element that is unmanifest karmic matter is likewise entirely derived.

十八界中，五根、五境十有色界是可積集，極微聚故。義准餘八非可積集，非極微故。

Among the eighteen elements, the five faculties and the five objects — the ten elements of form — are accumulable, because they are aggregations of atoms. By the same reasoning, the remaining eight are non-accumulable, because they are not aggregations of atoms.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Only one element out of eighteen contains the four great elements directly: touch, since solidity, wetness, heat, and motion are precisely what the tangible sense feels — nowhere else are the primary elements themselves the actual content of a sense-object. Every other kind of matter, without exception, is derived: the five faculties, and visible form, sound, smell, and taste as objects, are all secondary matter built from some combination of the four elements, never the four elements appearing as such. Unmanifest karmic matter, tucked inside the dharma element, is derived in exactly the same way — a further confirmation, from a completely different angle, of what fascicle 1 established when it first defined unmanifest form as matter produced by the great elements.

"Accumulable" returns to the atomism already met in the earlier debate over whether a hand striking a drum makes one sound or two: the ten elements of form are, quite literally, clusters of countless atoms (極微) bound together, which is exactly why they can be measured, weighed, and divided into parts. The remaining eight — the six consciousnesses, the mind element, and the dharma element — are never spoken of as clusters of atoms at all, because nothing about mind is built up piece by piece the way matter is.

15. What Can Cut, and What Can Be Cut?

The treatise now asks a strikingly concrete question: of all eighteen elements, which can serve as a cutting tool, and which can be cut?

Chinese Source

謂唯外四界，能斫及所斫，亦所燒能稱，能燒所稱諍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Only the four external elements can be both the cutter and the cut, and also the burned and the weigher; whether they can be the burner and the weighed is disputed.

論曰：色香味觸成斧薪等，此即名為能斫所斫。何法名斫？薪等色聚相逼續生，斧等分隔令各續起，此法名斫。

Prose: Form, smell, taste, and touch make up such things as an axe and firewood; this is what is called "the cutter" and "the cut." What is meant by "cutting"? When a cluster of matter such as firewood, through mutual pressing, would otherwise continue arising as one connected mass, and an axe or the like separates it, causing each part to continue arising separately — this capacity is what is named "cutting."

身等色根不名所斫，非可全斷令成二故。非身根等可成二分，支分離身則無根故。又身根等亦非能斫，以淨妙故如珠寶光。

The body and the other material faculties are not called "the cut," because they cannot be entirely severed so as to become two functioning wholes — it is not that the body-faculty and the like can be made into two parts, since once a limb is separated from the body, there is no longer a faculty there at all. Furthermore, the body-faculty and the like are also not "the cutter," because they are pure and subtle, like the light of a jewel or pearl.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Cutting" gets a precise definition worth noticing: it is not damage in general, but specifically separating a continuous mass into parts that each keep on existing independently afterward. This is exactly why the sense faculties are excluded from both roles, for two different reasons. They are not "the cut" because severing a limb does not leave behind two functioning faculties — the faculty simply ends where the body does, unlike firewood, which remains firewood on both sides of the axe's stroke. They are not "the cutter" either, because the faculties are made of "pure form" (the same refined, imperceptible matter defined back in fascicle 1's section 17) — too subtle a substance, the text says, comparing it to the light given off by a jewel, to physically cut anything at all. Ordinary gross matter can cut and be cut; the delicate matter of a living sense-organ can do neither.

16. Burning, Weighing, and a Recorded Disagreement

The verse's remaining two questions get answered together, and this time the treatise records real disagreement among its own scholars rather than settling on one view.

Chinese Source

聲界總非，不相續故。能燒所稱有異諍論，謂或有說：能燒所稱體亦如前，唯外四界。或復有說：唯有火界可名能燒，所稱唯重。

Ru-Yi Translation

The element of sound is excluded from all of this entirely, because it lacks continuity. Regarding "the burner" and "the weighed," there is real scholarly disagreement: some say their essence is likewise as before — only the four external elements. Others say that only the fire element may be named "the burner," and "the weighed" refers only to weight itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Sound drops out of the whole question for a reason consistent with everything already said about it: cutting, burning, and weighing all require a persisting mass of matter to act on, and sound, arising and ceasing moment by moment with no continuous body to it, was never a candidate. The genuine disagreement that follows is worth noticing precisely because the treatise does not resolve it: one view keeps burning and weighing symmetrical with cutting — any of the four external elements can serve as agent or patient — while a stricter view insists that only fire, specifically, deserves to be called "the burner" (since burning just is what heat does), and only weight, specifically, is what a scale actually measures (not colour, smell, or taste). Rather than declare a winner, the treatise simply records both positions and moves on — one of several moments in this fascicle where a live scholarly dispute is preserved rather than papered over.

17. How a Dharma Comes to Be: Ripened, Nourished, or Neither

A new, dense verse opens five classifications at once — where a dharma comes from turns out to matter as much as what it is. This section unpacks the first two: karmic ripening and nourishment.

Chinese Source

十八界中幾異熟生、幾所長養、幾等流性、幾有實事、幾一剎那？頌曰：內五有熟養，聲無異熟生，八無礙等流，亦異熟生性，餘三實唯法，剎那唯後三。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the eighteen elements, how many are produced by karmic ripening, how many are nourished, how many are of the nature of homogeneous outflow, how many are real, substantial things, and how many exist for only a single moment? A verse states: the internal five have both ripened and nourished instances; sound has none produced by ripening; eight, being non-resistant, are of homogeneous-outflow nature, and also have the nature of being ripening-produced; of the rest, real substance belongs only within the dharma element; existing for a single moment belongs only to the last three.

論曰：內五即是眼等五界。有異熟生及所長養無等流者，離異熟生及所長養，無別性故。異熟因所生名異熟生，如牛所駕車名曰牛車，略去中言故作是說。或所造業至得果時變而能熟，故名異熟。果從彼生，名異熟生。

Prose: "The internal five" are the eye and the other four elements. They have instances produced by karmic ripening and instances that are nourished, but none of homogeneous outflow, because apart from the ripened and the nourished they have no separate nature. What is produced by a ripening-cause is named "ripening-produced" — just as a cart drawn by an ox is called an "ox-cart," the middle term simply dropped. Or: when a created karmic act reaches the time of bearing its fruit, it transforms and is able to ripen — hence it is named "ripening." The effect that arises from it is named "ripening-produced."

如契經說：今六觸處，應知即是昔所造業。飲食資助眠睡等持勝緣所益，名所長養。有說：梵行亦能長養。此唯無損，非別有益。長養相續常能護持異熟相續，猶如外郭防援內城。

As the sūtras state: "The six bases of contact present now should be understood as precisely what was created by past karma." That which is benefited by the supportive conditions of food and drink, sleep, and meditative absorption is named "nourished." Some say: pure conduct can likewise nourish. In reply: this only prevents decline; it provides no separate benefit of its own. The nourished continuum constantly protects and sustains the ripened continuum, just as an outer wall guards and defends an inner city.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The five physical faculties are, quite literally, made of two things at once: a portion that is the direct ripened result of past karma (the sūtra citation is blunt about this — the sense-faculties you have right now are exactly what earlier karma produced), and a portion that is sustained moment to moment by food, sleep, and meditative absorption, the way any living body needs ongoing input to keep functioning. The city-wall simile is exact: the karmically-ripened part is the inner city, the precious thing actually being protected, while the nourished part is the outer wall that takes the day-to-day wear and keeps the inner part safe. One school's claim that celibate practice also "nourishes" the body is politely declined — the treatise allows that it prevents harm, but refuses to call mere harm-prevention a positive nourishment in its own right, a fine but real distinction it insists on holding.

18. Sound's Special Case: The Buddha's Voice

Sound turns out to be the one element that resists the pattern just given, and the exception opens onto a genuine doctrinal problem: is the Buddha's famously beautiful voice a karmic result, or not?

Chinese Source

聲有等流及所長養無異熟生。所以者何？隨欲轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sound has instances of homogeneous outflow and instances that are nourished, but none produced by karmic ripening. Why is this? Because it turns according to one's own will.

若爾，不應《施設論》說善修遠離麁惡語故，感得大士梵音聲相。有說：聲屬第五傳故，雖由彼生而非異熟。

If that is so, one might object: then it should not be, as the Prajñaptiśāstra states, that by thoroughly cultivating the abandonment of coarse and evil speech, one obtains, as a result, the great being's mark of a Brahmā-voice. Some say: sound belongs to the fifth stage of transmission; although it arises from that karma, it is not itself a ripened result.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reasoning for excluding sound from karmic ripening is simple: unlike your eye or ear, which you did not choose and cannot switch off, speech happens when and how you decide to speak — it "turns according to one's own will," and karmic ripening, by definition, is not a matter of present choice. The objection this invites is a serious one: Buddhist tradition holds that the Buddha's extraordinarily beautiful voice — one of the classical marks of a great being — is explicitly said to be a result of past good karma (specifically, of abandoning harsh speech). If sound can never be a ripened result, how can a scripture say exactly that?

The resolution preserves both claims by adding a chain of intermediate steps, spelled out in the next section: karma does not produce the beautiful voice directly as its ripened fruit. Sound is said to belong to the fifth stage of a causal transmission running from that karma — several steps removed from the karma itself, not its immediate, literal ripening. This lets the treatise keep its general rule (sound itself is never literally vipāka) while still honoring the sūtra's plain statement that good conduct produces the mark of a beautiful voice, several steps removed rather than directly.

19. The Chain from Karma to a Spoken Sound

The treatise now spells out the very chain of production it just invoked — the full sequence of steps that stands between an act of karma and the sound eventually heard.

Chinese Source

謂從彼業生諸大種，從諸大種緣擊發聲。謂彼業生異熟大種，從此傳生長養大種，此復傳生等流大種，此乃生聲。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is: from that karma, the great elements are produced; from those great elements, through the condition of striking, sound is emitted. That karma produces ripened great elements; from these, in transmission, nourished great elements are produced; these, in further transmission, produce homogeneous-outflow great elements; and only then does this produce sound.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Spelled out, the chain runs: karma ripens directly into great elements (the physical basis of the vocal organs); those ripened elements, sustained moment to moment, produce nourished great elements; those, continuing on, produce homogeneous-outflow great elements; and only this last stage, when actually struck into motion by an act of speaking, produces the sound itself. The Buddha's beautiful voice is therefore several links removed from the karma of abandoning harsh speech — real, and genuinely caused by that karma, but not its immediate ripened fruit the way an eye or ear is. This is the treatise's general method in miniature: rather than let a single word like "produced by karma" cover every case loosely, it insists on naming exactly which link in the causal chain a given effect actually occupies.

20. Why Feeling Is Not Like Sound, and the Eight Mental Elements

An objector presses the "several steps removed" reasoning to a place the treatise cannot accept, before the section closes with the technical definitions of homogeneous outflow and ripening, and why the eight mental elements are never "nourished."

Chinese Source

若爾，身受從業所生大種生故，應非異熟。若受如聲，便違正理。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, one might object: bodily feeling, since it arises dependent on great elements that are themselves produced by karma, should likewise not be a ripened result. But if feeling were treated like sound in this respect, it would contradict correct principle.

八無礙者，七心法界此有等流異熟生性。同類遍行因所生者是等流性，若異熟因所引生者名異熟生，諸無礙法無積集故非所長養。

"The eight non-resistant" are the seven mind-elements and the dharma element; these have both homogeneous-outflow and ripening-produced natures. What is produced by a homogeneous cause or a pervasive cause is of homogeneous-outflow nature; what is drawn forth and produced by a ripening-cause is named ripening-produced. All non-resistant dharmas, having no accumulation, are for that reason never nourished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection tries to generalize the exact move used to exempt sound from vipāka: if being downstream of karma-produced matter disqualifies sound, shouldn't it disqualify bodily feeling too, since feeling likewise depends on a body that karma produced? The treatise refuses this outright, calling it a violation of correct principle — feeling's status as a direct ripened result is too doctrinally load-bearing to sacrifice for the sake of a tidy analogy (an unawakened being's felt pleasure and pain are, centrally, how karma's ripening is actually experienced). Sound and feeling are not parallel cases: sound genuinely runs through several extra links of transmission before it exists at all, while feeling is the ripening itself, immediate rather than several steps removed.

The closing definitions finally make precise two terms this fascicle has used all along without spelling out: "homogeneous outflow" (等流, niṣyanda) names whatever is produced by a same-kind cause or a pervasive cause — an effect that resembles its cause in kind, the way a wholesome thought tends to give rise to further wholesome thoughts of the same general character; "ripening-produced" names specifically what a ripening-cause draws forth — a genuinely different-in-kind result, the way a moral action ripens into a pleasant or painful experience unlike the action itself. And the eight mental elements are permanently excluded from "nourished" for a reason already established: nourishment by food, sleep, and meditative absorption only applies to matter built up from clustered atoms, and mind, on this system, was never built that way to begin with.

21. Only the Unconditioned Is "Real" — and a Single Moment That Changes Everything

The five-part verse from section 21 closes with its two remaining questions: what counts as genuinely "real," and what exists for only a single, unrepeatable instant. The second answer turns out to describe the exact moment a person first breaks through to the path.

Chinese Source

餘謂餘四：色、香、味、觸，皆通三種，有異熟生、有所長養、有等流性。實唯法者，實謂無為，以堅實故。此法界攝故，唯法界獨名有實。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The rest" means the remaining four: form, smell, taste, and touch — these are all common to all three kinds, having instances that are ripening-produced, nourished, and of homogeneous-outflow nature. "Real substance belongs only to the dharma element" — "real" means the unconditioned, because it is firm and real. Since this is comprised within the dharma element, only the dharma element alone is named as having real substance.

唯此三界有一剎那，謂初無漏苦法忍品，非等流故名一剎那。此說究竟非等流者，餘有為法無非等流。苦法忍相應心名意界、意識界，餘俱起法名為法界。如是已說異熟生等。

Only these three elements have a single-moment instance — namely, the class of the very first uncontaminated moment, the patient acceptance of the knowledge of suffering. Because it is not itself an instance of homogeneous outflow, it is named "single-moment." This is said in the ultimate sense of "not homogeneous outflow," since apart from this, no other conditioned dharma is entirely without homogeneous outflow. The mind associated with the patient acceptance of the knowledge of suffering is named the mind element and the mind-consciousness element; the other dharmas arising together with it are named the dharma element. Thus, ripening-produced and the rest have now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Real substance" here is a narrower, more technical claim than it might first sound — it is not saying that ordinary matter and mind are illusory (dharmas retain their own particular characteristics, as this fascicle established long ago); it is saying that only the unconditioned is "firm and real" in the specific sense of never arising, never ceasing, and never being built up from parts the way every conditioned dharma is. Everything conditioned, however genuinely existent, is caught in the flux of arising and ceasing that this narrower sense of "real" is reserved against.

The single-moment case is the most moving point in this entire section. "The patient acceptance of the knowledge of suffering" (苦法忍, duḥkhe dharmajñāna-kṣānti) is the technical name for the very first moment of uncontaminated insight a practitioner ever has — the instant the path of seeing opens, traditionally marking entry into the stream. It earns the label "single-moment" for a precise reason: "homogeneous outflow" requires a prior moment of the same kind to flow from, and this moment, by definition, has no predecessor — nothing like it has ever occurred before in that person's mind. Every uncontaminated moment that follows it, however similar, counts as flowing from it and so no longer qualifies for this description; only the first one is, in the strictest sense, singular. A chapter that opened with an abstract division of all reality into contaminated and uncontaminated, and has spent fifty-plus sections cross-classifying matter and mind by every conceivable axis, closes this particular thread by locating, with technical precision, the exact instant a being first steps from one side of that division to the other.

22. Do the Eye and Eye-Consciousness Always Arrive Together?

The treatise now asks a question that only makes sense against the cosmology already built up across earlier sections: across the different realms and levels of rebirth, does gaining a sense faculty always mean gaining its consciousness too, and vice versa?

Chinese Source

今應思擇，若有眼界先不成就今得成就亦眼識耶？若眼識界先不成就今得成就亦眼界耶？如是等問，今應略答。頌曰：眼與眼識界，獨俱得非等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now this should be considered: if someone, not previously possessing the eye element, now attains it, does this person also attain the eye-consciousness? If someone, not previously possessing the eye-consciousness element, now attains it, does this person also attain the eye element? Questions of this kind should now be briefly answered. A verse states: the eye and the eye-consciousness elements may be attained singly or together — they are not always matched.

論曰：獨得者，謂或有眼界先不成就今得成就非眼識，謂生欲界漸得眼根，及無色沒生二三四靜慮地時。

Prose: "Attained singly" means: there are cases where the eye element, not previously possessed, is now attained without the eye-consciousness — as when being born in the desire realm and gradually acquiring the eye faculty, or when passing away from the formless realm and being reborn at the level of the second, third, or fourth meditative absorption.

或有眼識先不成就今得成就非眼界，謂生二三四靜慮地眼識現起，及從彼沒生下地時。俱得者，謂或有二界先不成就今得成就，謂無色沒生於欲界及梵世時。

Or there are cases where the eye-consciousness, not previously possessed, is now attained without the eye element — as when, born at the level of the second, third, or fourth meditative absorption, eye-consciousness nevertheless manifests there, and when, passing away from there, one is reborn at a lower stage. "Attained together" means: there are cases where, not previously possessing either element, one now attains both together — as when passing away from the formless realm and being reborn in the desire realm or the Brahmā world.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This question only makes sense once several earlier sections are held together at once: a developing embryo in the desire realm forms its eye faculty gradually, before it can see anything, so the organ is gained well before the consciousness ever fires — one case of "singly." A being who dies formless and is reborn at the second dhyāna or higher regains an eye faculty (formless beings have none at all, as section 7 established) without necessarily gaining active eye-consciousness there, since the higher levels of absorption do not routinely exercise ordinary sight — another case of "singly," in the opposite direction. The reverse also occurs: a being already dwelling at those higher levels can, on occasion, have eye-consciousness manifest without this counting as newly gaining the eye element, since the organ was already there; and when such a being is later reborn at a lower stage, active eye-consciousness resumes without needing to be freshly "gained" as an element either. Only a being crossing the widest possible gap — from the formless realm, which has no body at all, directly into the desire realm or the first dhyāna, both of which fully and immediately exercise the senses — gains both the organ and its active consciousness in the very same transition. We have translated this passage closely to the Chinese; the exact mechanics of eye-consciousness "manifesting" at the higher levels of absorption are a finer point of Abhidharma cosmology than this fascicle spells out in full, and readers wanting the complete technical account should look to the treatise's later, dedicated discussion of rebirth and the realms.

23. Attainment Versus Possession: A Fourfold Case, and an Exercise Left to the Reader

The treatise closes the case fully absent from the previous section, then shifts from "attaining" to the related but distinct question of currently "possessing" — and, having modeled the method once, leaves the remaining combinations for the reader to work out.

Chinese Source

非者俱非，謂除前相。等謂若有成就眼界亦眼識耶？應作四句。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Neither" means neither is attained — whatever falls outside the aforementioned patterns. "And so on" means: if one possesses the eye element, does one also possess the eye-consciousness? A fourfold analysis should be made.

第一句者，謂生二三四靜慮地眼識不起。第二句者，謂生欲界未得眼根及得已失。

The first case: being born at the level of the second, third, or fourth meditative absorption, where eye-consciousness does not arise. The second case: being born in the desire realm, either not yet having attained the eye faculty, or having attained it and then lost it.

第三句者，謂生欲界得眼不失，及生梵世若生二三四靜慮地正見色時。第四句者，謂除前相。如是眼界與色界，眼識與色界得成就等，如理應思。

The third case: being born in the desire realm, having attained the eye faculty without losing it; and also being born in the Brahmā world, or at the level of the second, third, or fourth meditative absorption, at the moment one is directly seeing a form. The fourth case: whatever falls outside the aforementioned patterns. In the same way, the relationships between the eye element and the form element, and between eye-consciousness and the form element, with respect to attainment and possession, should be worked out by the reader according to the same method.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This introduces a distinction Abhidharma keeps carefully separate: "attainment" (得, prāpti) is the specific event of newly acquiring something, while "possession" (成就, samanvāgama) is simply the ongoing state of currently having it, which can persist quietly without any new attainment happening in this moment. The four cases sort beings by whether they currently possess the eye faculty, the eye-consciousness, both, or neither — matching the earlier cosmology precisely: those absorbed at the second dhyāna and above generally possess the organ without the consciousness being active; an early embryo or a blinded person in the desire realm possesses the potential for sight without the organ itself; an ordinary desire-realm being with working eyes, or a higher-level being at the exact moment sight does occur there, possesses both.

The closing line is worth noticing for what it does rather than what it says: having walked through the full method once for the eye and eye-consciousness, the treatise simply instructs the reader to apply the identical reasoning to the eye element paired with the form element, and to eye-consciousness paired with the form element, without spelling out either case explicitly. This is a real pedagogical choice this fascicle makes more than once — teach the pattern thoroughly on one worked example, then trust the reader to extend it, rather than mechanically repeating the same four-case analysis for every possible pair among the eighteen elements.

24. Internal and External — Without a Self to Be "Inside" Of

The treatise closes off the previous topic, then opens a new classification that immediately runs into Buddhism's most basic teaching: if there is no self, what could "internal" possibly mean?

Chinese Source

眼識與色界得成就等如理應思。為攝如是所未說義，是故頌中總復言等。如是已說得成就等。十八界中幾內、幾外？頌曰：內十二眼等，色等六為外。

Ru-Yi Translation

The relationship between eye-consciousness and the element of form, regarding attainment and possession, should be considered according to the same reasoning; the verse's word "and so on" was included precisely to cover such meanings left unstated. Thus, attainment, possession, and the rest have now been explained. Among the eighteen elements, how many are internal, and how many external? A verse states: the internal are twelve — the eye and the rest; form and the rest, six, are external.

論曰：六根、六識十二名內，外謂所餘色等六境。我依名內，外謂此餘。我體既無，內外何有？

Prose: The six faculties and the six consciousnesses — twelve — are named internal; external refers to the remaining six, form and the other objects. "Internal" means what the self depends on; "external" means what remains apart from this. But since the self has no real essence, how can there be an internal and an external at all?

我執依止故，假說心為我。故契經說：由善調伏我，智者得生天。世尊餘處說調伏心，如契經言：應善調伏心，心調能引樂。故但於心假說為我。眼等為此所依親近，故說名內。色等為此所緣踈遠，故說名外。

Because the grasping at a self takes something as its basis, mind is provisionally spoken of as "self." Hence a sūtra states: "By skillfully taming the self, the wise attain birth in heaven." The World-Honored One, elsewhere, spoke of taming the mind, as another sūtra says: "One should skillfully tame the mind; a tamed mind is able to bring about happiness." Hence it is only with respect to the mind that "self" is provisionally spoken of. The eye and the rest, being the intimate support for this provisionally-designated self, are therefore named "internal." Form and the rest, being the distant object this mind takes hold of, are therefore named "external."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The treatise catches its own terminology in a trap and then climbs out of it in real time. "Internal" naturally suggests "belonging to a self" — but Buddhism's most basic teaching is that no such self exists, so the objection writes itself: without a self, what could "internal" possibly be relative to? The answer is not to abandon the word but to relocate what it depends on. Two sūtras are set side by side deliberately: one says taming the self leads to heaven, the other says taming the mind leads to happiness — proof, on this reading, that "self" in ordinary Buddhist teaching is simply a convenient, provisional stand-in for "mind," never a claim that a real, separate self exists. Once that substitution is made, "internal" and "external" fall into place without needing a self at all: the six faculties are "internal" simply because they are mind's own intimate, proximate support, and the six objects are "external" simply because they are what mind reaches toward from a distance. The geography is real; the self it seems to imply is not.

25. Defending the School's Own Name: All Eighteen Exist in All Three Times

A sharp objection tests whether the "child/father" account of the mind element (from fascicle 1's section 31) accidentally breaks one of this school's own defining claims — that every element exists across past, present, and future alike.

Chinese Source

若爾，六識應不名內，未至意位非心依故。至意位時不失六識界，未至意位亦非越意相。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, one might object: the six consciousnesses should then not be named "internal," since, before reaching the status of mind, they are not the support for mind. In reply: upon reaching the status of mind, the six consciousness-elements are not thereby lost; and before reaching the status of mind, they are also not outside the character of mind.

若異此者，意界唯應在過去世，六識唯在現在未來，便違自宗許十八界皆通三世。又若未來現在六識無意界相，過去意界亦應不立，相於三世無改易故。已說內外。

If it were otherwise, the mind element would have to exist only in the past, and the six consciousnesses only in the present and future — which would contradict our own school's acceptance that all eighteen elements are common to all three times. Furthermore, if the future and present six consciousnesses do not have the character of the mind element, then the past mind element should likewise not be established, since a dharma's defining character does not change across the three times. Internal and external have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This directly defends the doctrine the school takes its very name from: Sarvāstivāda, "the teaching that everything exists" — specifically, that every dharma exists across past, present, and future, not merely in a fleeting present moment. Section 31's account of the mind element (the same six consciousnesses, named "mind" only in their just-past phase) invites a worry: if manas is specifically the past phase of a consciousness, does that mean the mind element is confined to the past tense while the consciousness-elements themselves are confined to present and future — carving up one whole into three separately-timed pieces? The reply insists this is not so: any of the six consciousnesses, whether presently arising, yet to arise, or having just passed, never stops belonging to the consciousness-elements simply by also, in its just-past phase, counting as the mind element. A second, tighter argument closes the case: since a dharma's defining character is supposed to hold steady across all three times, it cannot be that present and future consciousnesses simply lack "mind" character while the past instance has it — the character must be there all along, or the very premise of three-time existence collapses. Nothing is lost, split off, or reassigned to a different temporal box — which is exactly what the school's foundational claim requires.

26. "Same-Class" and "Counterpart-Class" — Why the Dharma Element Is Unique

A new distinction asks whether a dharma is currently functioning as an object of its proper consciousness, or merely present without being engaged — and one element, uniquely, is claimed to have no exceptions at all.

Chinese Source

十八界中幾是同分、幾彼同分？頌曰：法同分餘二，作不作自業。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the eighteen elements, how many are "same-class," and how many are "counterpart-class"? A verse states: the dharma element is same-class; the rest are of both kinds, according to whether they perform their own function or not.

論曰：法同分者，謂一法界唯是同分。若境與識定為所緣，識於其中已生生法，此所緣境說名同分。

Prose: "The dharma element is same-class" means that the single dharma element is exclusively same-class. If an object serves determinately as the objective support for a consciousness, and a consciousness has already arisen, is arising, or will arise with respect to it, that object, as objective support, is named "same-class."

無一法界不於其中已正當生無邊意識，由諸聖者決定生心，觀一切法皆為無我。彼除自體及俱有法，餘一切法皆為所緣。

There is no instance of the dharma element with respect to which an unlimited range of mind-consciousness has not already arisen, is not arising, or will not arise — because the noble ones definitely generate a mind that contemplates all dharmas as without self. Excluding a given moment's own essence and the dharmas co-arising with it, every other dharma serves as its objective support.

如是所除亦第二念心所緣境，此二念心緣一切境無不周遍，是故法界恒名同分。餘二者，謂餘十七界皆有同分及彼同分。

Even what is thus excluded becomes the objective support of a second moment of mind — these two moments of mind, together, take every object without exception, leaving nothing uncovered. This is why the dharma element is permanently named "same-class." "The rest" means that the remaining seventeen elements each have both same-class and counterpart-class instances.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Same-class" names a dharma actually functioning as the object of its matching consciousness right now — an eye actively seeing, a sound actively heard; "counterpart-class" names the same kind of dharma simply present without that engagement — an eye during dreamless sleep, matter no consciousness happens to be taking as its object at the moment. Seventeen of the eighteen elements have both kinds of instance, since nothing guarantees that every eye or every patch of colour is, at any given time, actually being seen.

The dharma element is the single exception, and the argument for why is elegant: a first moment of noble insight, contemplating all dharmas as without self, can take literally anything as its object except its own present self and whatever arises together with it in that same instant — a mind cannot observe itself in the very act of observing. But a second, following moment of mind can then take that first moment (including its own essence and co-arising factors) as its object in turn. Between any two consecutive moments, nothing is left out: every possible object gets covered by one moment or the other. Since this is always true of any moment whatsoever, no instance of the dharma element ever permanently escapes being an object of mind — which is exactly why, alone among the eighteen elements, it is said to be same-class without exception, never merely "counterpart-class."

27. Kashmir Against the West: How Many Kinds of "Not Seeing"?

The treatise defines "same-class" and "counterpart-class" precisely, then records a genuine historical disagreement between two named schools of Sarvāstivāda scholarship over how finely to count the ways an eye can fail to see.

Chinese Source

何名同分、彼同分耶？謂作自業、不作自業。若作自業名為同分，不作自業名彼同分。此中眼界於有見色已正當見名同分眼，如是廣說，乃至意界各於自境應說自用。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by "same-class" and "counterpart-class"? Whether a dharma performs its own function or does not. If it performs its own function, it is named "same-class"; if it does not, it is named "counterpart-class." Here, with respect to the eye element: when it has seen, is seeing, or will see a visible form, it is named "same-class eye." Extending this in the same way, up through the mind element, each faculty and consciousness should be described according to its own function with respect to its own object.

迦濕彌羅國毘婆沙師說：彼同分眼但有四種，謂不見色已正當滅及不生法。西方諸師說有五種：謂不生法復開為二，一有識屬、二無識屬，乃至身界應知亦然。意彼同分唯不生法。

The Vaibhāṣika masters of Kashmir say: "counterpart-class eye" has only four kinds — an eye not seeing form because it has already ceased, is presently ceasing, will exist but not see in the future, or is a dharma that will never arise at all. The masters of the West say there are five kinds: the "dharma that will never arise" category is further divided into two — one connected in principle to a consciousness, the other connected to none at all. The same should be understood as extending up through the body element. For the mind element, "counterpart-class" is solely the "will never arise" category.

色界為眼已正當見名同分色，彼同分色亦有四種，謂非眼見已正當滅及不生法，廣說乃至觸界亦爾。

As for the form element: when it has been seen, is being seen, or will be seen by the eye, it is named "same-class form." "Counterpart-class form" likewise has four kinds — not seen by any eye in the past, present, or future, or a dharma that will never arise as visible at all. This extends in the same way up through the element of the tangible.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The core definition is refreshingly plain after the previous section's subtlety: a dharma is "same-class" exactly when it does its job — an eye that has seen, sees, or will see counts as a functioning eye, full stop; anything falling short of that is "counterpart-class." What follows is one of the few places in this fascicle where two competing scholarly traditions are named outright rather than left as an anonymous "some say": the Kashmir Vaibhāṣikas count four kinds of non-seeing eye (already stopped, presently stopped, will exist but never see, or never even destined to arise), while an unnamed group of "masters of the West" split that last category further, distinguishing an eye-dharma that could in principle still connect to a consciousness from one with no connection to any consciousness whatsoever, arriving at five kinds instead of four.

This geographic labeling is not incidental colour — Kashmir and the "Western" regions (roughly Gandhāra and the lands beyond) were real, distinct centres of Sarvāstivāda scholarship with documented disagreements on points exactly like this one, and this treatise, itself often read as broadly sympathetic to Kashmir's Vaibhāṣika orthodoxy, still records the rival position without erasing it. The same fourfold pattern is then extended to the object side as well: form counts as "same-class" only when actually, currently, or someday seen by an eye, with the identical four-way breakdown of failure to be seen.

28. Why It Is Called "Class" At All: Closing Same-Class and Counterpart-Class

Two last points close out this topic: same-class status generalizes across all of a faculty's objects at once, and the treatise finally explains the etymology of the word "class" (分) itself.

Chinese Source

眼若於一是同分，於餘一切亦同分。彼同分亦如是，廣說乃至意界亦爾。眼等六識同分彼同分生不生法故，如意界說。

Ru-Yi Translation

If an eye is same-class with respect to one object, it is also same-class with respect to all the rest. Counterpart-class is likewise so. Extending this in the same way, up through the mind element, it is likewise so. The same-class and counterpart-class of the eye and the other five consciousnesses, with respect to arising and non-arising dharmas, should be explained as with the mind element.

云何同分、彼同分義？根境識三更相交涉故名為分，或復分者是已作用，或復分者是所生觸，同有此分故名同分。

What is the meaning of "same-class" and "counterpart-class"? The faculty, the object, and the consciousness — these three, mutually intersecting with one another, are what is called a "class" (分). Or, further, "class" means what has already functioned; or, further, "class" means the contact that is produced from their coming together. Sharing this same class in common is what is named "same-class."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The generalization makes sense once "same-class" is remembered as a status of the faculty itself, not of any one encounter: an eye that is currently a genuinely functioning eye is a functioning eye with respect to everything visible, not just the one thing it happens to be looking at right now — so same-class status, once it holds for one object, automatically holds for all.

The closing etymology finally explains a word this fascicle has used all along without unpacking it: "class" (分, bhāga) names the very intersection of faculty, object, and consciousness — the three coming together to produce contact (觸, the same universal mental factor already introduced in the discussion of the aggregates). To "share the same class" is to share in that same three-way convergence; a faculty and object that genuinely meet consciousness this way are, in the fullest sense, of one class together.

29. Cut Off by Seeing, Cut Off by Cultivation, or Not Cut Off at All

This classification connects directly to the structure of the path itself: some afflictions are eliminated all at once by the first moment of insight already described in section 21, while others yield only gradually, through sustained cultivation.

Chinese Source

十八界中幾見所斷、幾修所斷、幾非所斷？頌曰：十五唯修斷，後三界通三，不染非六生，色定非見斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the eighteen elements, how many are cut off by seeing, how many by cultivation, and how many are not cut off at all? A verse states: fifteen are cut off by cultivation only; the last three elements are common to all three; undefiled dharmas, and form not born from the six, are definitely not cut off by seeing.

論曰：十五界者，謂十色界及五識界。唯修斷者，此十五界唯修所斷。後三界者，意界、法界及意識界。通三者，謂此後三界各通三種。

Prose: "The fifteen elements" are the ten elements of form and the five sense-consciousness elements. "Cut off by cultivation only" means these fifteen elements are cut off by cultivation alone. "The last three elements" are the mind element, the dharma element, and the mind-consciousness element. "Common to all three" means these last three elements are each common to all three kinds.

八十八隨眠及彼俱有法并隨行得，皆見所斷，諸餘有漏皆修所斷，一切無漏皆非所斷。

The eighty-eight latent afflictions, together with their co-arising dharmas and their accompanying acquisitions, are all cut off by seeing. All other contaminated dharmas are cut off by cultivation. All uncontaminated dharmas are not cut off at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The pattern is stark and clean: fifteen of the eighteen elements — all of matter, plus the five sense-consciousnesses — can only ever be worked on gradually, through cultivation, never eliminated in one stroke of insight. Only the three elements bound up with the mental consciousness (mind, dharma, mind-consciousness) can be cut off by seeing at all, and even there, only a very specific set qualifies: the eighty-eight latent afflictions — the classical Sarvāstivāda count of intellectually-produced afflictions across the four noble truths and three realms — along with whatever arises together with them and their attainment. Everything else contaminated waits for cultivation; everything uncontaminated needs no cutting off at all, since it was never an affliction to begin with. This is the technical machinery behind section 21's single unrepeatable moment: the instant of first uncontaminated insight is precisely what cuts off the eighty-eight all at once, opening the path of seeing, while the path of cultivation that follows works through everything else gradually, moment by moment, for as long as it takes.

30. Two Tempting Exceptions That Aren't: Ordinary Worldling-Ness and Bad Karma

The treatise raises its own objection: surely some things belong in the "cut off by seeing" category besides the eighty-eight afflictions? It names two obvious candidates and explains, in each case, exactly why they don't qualify.

Chinese Source

豈不更有見所斷法，謂異生性及招惡趣身語業等，此與聖道極相違故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Is it not the case that there are further dharmas that should be cut off by seeing — namely, the state of being an ordinary worldling, and the bodily and verbal karma that leads to a bad rebirth-destiny, and so forth — since these things are utterly opposed to the noble path?

雖爾，此法非見所斷，略說彼相，謂不染法非六生色，定非見斷。其異生性是不染污，無記性攝，已離欲者斷善根者猶成就故。

Although this may seem so, these dharmas are not cut off by seeing. Briefly stated, their character is this: an undefiled dharma, or form not born from the six, is definitely not cut off by seeing. "The state of being an ordinary worldling" is itself undefiled, comprised within the neutral category — since even someone already free from desire, or someone who has cut off their wholesome roots, still possesses it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both candidates feel, intuitively, like exactly what a first insight should demolish — yet the treatise shows each one fails for a different technical reason, matching the two exclusions already named in the verse. "The state of being an ordinary worldling" (異生性, everything that makes someone not yet a noble one) sounds like the very definition of what stream-entry overturns, but it is classified as morally neutral and undefiled in its own right — not itself an affliction, just a status marker — and the proof offered is sharp: even a being who has already gone far beyond ordinary craving through meditative attainment, or one who has cut off their own wholesome roots entirely, can still technically "possess" this same neutral status, showing it isn't bound up with affliction the way the eighty-eight are. Bad karmic deeds of body and speech fail for the other listed reason: they are matter, "form not born from the six" consciousnesses in the technical sense meant here, and matter, as section 29 already established, is never cut off by seeing under any circumstances — only ever by cultivation, however contrary to the path it may be in spirit.

31. The Real Test: Does It Arise From Delusion About the Truths?

The treatise gives the underlying principle that both exceptions from section 30 actually turn on, closing the whole topic with a single, clarifying reason.

Chinese Source

前及此色，定非見斷。所以者何？非迷諦理親發起故。如是已說見所斷等。

Ru-Yi Translation

The state of being an ordinary worldling, and this karmic form, are definitely not cut off by seeing. Why is this? Because they are not directly produced by delusion regarding the truths. Thus, cut off by seeing and the rest have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This single line is the real test behind everything in sections 29–30: "cut off by seeing" applies only to what arises directly out of misapprehending the four noble truths — the intellectually-produced wrong views and afflictions that a first moment of correct insight demolishes all at once. Ordinary-worldling status and karmic action of body and speech may be deeply at odds with the path in spirit, but neither is itself a direct product of that specific delusion, so neither qualifies, however tempting the intuition that they should.

32. Does the Eye See, or Does the Eye-Consciousness See?

The treatise now opens one of its most famous and most argued-over questions: when you see something, what exactly is doing the seeing — the physical eye, or the consciousness that arises depending on it?

Chinese Source

十八界中幾是見、幾非見？頌曰：眼法界一分，八種說名見，五識俱生慧，非見不度故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the eighteen elements, how many are "seeing," and how many are not? A verse states: the eye, and a portion of the dharma element — eight kinds — are said to be "seeing." The wisdom co-arising with the five sense-consciousnesses is not "seeing," since it does not investigate its object.

眼見色同分，非彼能依識，傳說不能觀，彼障諸色故。

The eye sees form when it is same-class — not the consciousness that depends on it. It has been transmitted that consciousness cannot directly observe, because obstructions block forms from it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is one of the most heavily debated claims in the entire treatise, and the root verse states the more surprising position bluntly: it is the physical eye itself that sees, not the eye-consciousness that arises in dependence on it. The argument given here is almost architectural — if the eye-consciousness itself were the one doing the seeing, a physical obstruction like a wall placed between the eye and an object ought not to matter, since consciousness is not itself a physical thing that a wall could block. But a wall obviously does block sight completely. The traditional reply on behalf of this verse is that what actually blocks sight is the eye's own physical line of access to its object — so it must be the eye's own function, not the consciousness's, that a wall interrupts.

Worth flagging honestly: this "the eye sees" position (根見家) is not the last word even within this treatise. Vasubandhu's own more Sautrāntika-leaning sympathies famously push back against it elsewhere, arguing instead that it is consciousness, not the inert organ, that actually cognizes anything at all — an organ by itself does not "see" any more than a mirror does. This fascicle presents the position the root verse states and the reasoning offered for it; readers should expect this exact question to resurface, argued from the other side, later in the treatise.

33. The Crystal Problem, and Light as the Real Answer

Each side now presses the other. The "eye sees" side explains why eye-consciousness fails to arise behind an obstruction — but the "consciousness sees" side finds a real problem with that explanation, and offers a more precise account in its place.

Chinese Source

眼識於彼何故不生？許眼見者眼有對故，於彼障色無見功能。識與所依一境轉故，可言於彼眼識不生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why does eye-consciousness not arise with respect to an obstructed object? For those who hold that the eye sees: because the eye is resistant, it has no capacity to see form that is obstructed. Since consciousness operates on the very same object-domain as its support, it can accordingly be said that eye-consciousness likewise does not arise there.

許識見者何緣不起？眼豈如身根境合方取而言有對故不見彼耶？又頗胝迦、瑠璃、雲母、水等所障云何得見？是故不由眼有對故，於彼障色無見功能。

For those who hold that consciousness sees: for what reason does it fail to arise? Does the eye, like the body faculty, require direct contact with its object to grasp it, such that one could say it fails to see simply because it is resistant? Furthermore, how is it that one can still see through an obstruction of crystal, beryl, mica, or water? For this reason, it is not because the eye is resistant that it lacks the capacity to see obstructed form.

若爾，所執眼識云何？若於是處光明無隔，於彼障色眼識亦生。若於是處光明有隔，於彼障色眼識不生。識既不生，故不能見。然經說眼能見色者，是見所依，故說能見。

If that is so, what is the actual position of those who hold consciousness sees? Wherever light is not obstructed, eye-consciousness still arises with respect to that seemingly obstructed form. Wherever light is obstructed, eye-consciousness does not arise with respect to it. Since consciousness thus does not arise, one cannot see. As for the sūtras' statement that "the eye is able to see form" — this is because the eye is seeing's support, and so it is said, loosely, to "see."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "eye sees" side's account has a genuine weakness, and the objection names it precisely: if mere physical resistance were what blocked sight, then crystal, beryl, mica, and water — all perfectly solid, resistant substances — ought to block sight exactly as a wall does, since they are equally "resistant" in the technical sense already defined back in section 2. But they plainly do not; one sees straight through them. Something other than resistance must be doing the real work.

The "consciousness sees" side's answer is the more sophisticated of the two, and strikingly close to how vision actually works: what matters is not whether the eye's object is technically "resistant," but whether light reaches it unobstructed. A wall blocks light entirely, so consciousness cannot arise; crystal, water, and the like let light straight through, so consciousness arises normally despite being technically solid matter in the way. This single variable — light, not resistance — explains both puzzle cases at once, which is exactly why it is offered as the better account. The closing move is worth noticing too: rather than deny the sūtras' plain statement that "the eye sees," this side reinterprets it as ordinary loose speech, crediting the support (the eye) with what its dependent (consciousness) actually does — the same move ordinary language makes when it says a key "opens" a door rather than a person using a key.

34. The Same Trick Again: Does the Mind, or the Mind-Consciousness, Cognize?

Having explained away the sūtras' "the eye sees" as loose speech, the treatise applies the identical move to a parallel case — and offers one of its most relatable everyday analogies to seal the point.

Chinese Source

如彼經言：意能識法。非意能識，以過去故。何者能識？謂是意識。意是識依，故說能識。或就所依說能依業，如世間說床座言聲。

Ru-Yi Translation

As that sūtra says: "The mind is able to cognize dharmas." It is not actually the mind that cognizes, since it is past. What, then, cognizes? It is the mind-consciousness. The mind is consciousness's support, and so it is said, loosely, to "cognize." Or, this is a case of speaking of the support's action in terms of what depends on it — just as, in ordinary speech, one says "the bed speaks."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The parallel with the eye is exact and deliberate: manas — "mind," the just-past phase of a consciousness already defined back in fascicle 1's section 31 — cannot itself be doing any cognizing, since a dharma that has already passed does not perform a fresh act. The actual cognizing belongs to the mind-consciousness, its dependent; the sūtra's phrase "mind cognizes" is the same kind of shorthand as "the eye sees," crediting a support with an action really performed by what leans on it.

"The bed speaks" is the analogy doing the real persuasive work, and it earns its place: nobody hearing this phrase in ordinary speech thinks the furniture itself has started talking — everyone understands at once that it means the person sitting on the bed is speaking. Once a reader accepts that everyday convention without a second thought, accepting the same convention behind "the eye sees" and "the mind cognizes" costs nothing further. This is the second time in as many sections that this fascicle resolves an apparent scriptural literalism by pointing to a linguistic habit everyone already uses without noticing.

35. More Scripture, and a Third Position: The Eye Sees Too, Like a Ringing Bell

Further scriptural citations sharpen the debate, an objection about needing a separate "seer" is turned aside, and a third position emerges that lets both sides be partly right at once.

Chinese Source

又如經言：眼所識色可愛可樂。然實非此可愛樂色是眼所識。又如經說：梵志當知，以眼為門唯為見色。故知眼識依眼門見。亦不應言門即是見，豈容經說以眼為見唯為見色？

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, as a sūtra says: "Form cognized by the eye is agreeable and pleasing" — yet in reality it is not this agreeable form that is cognized by the eye itself. Further, as a sūtra states: "Brahmin, know this: the eye serves as a gateway, solely for seeing form." Hence it is known that eye-consciousness sees by means of the eye as its gateway. Nor should one say the gateway itself is the seeing — how else could a sūtra say "the eye is for seeing, solely for the purpose of seeing form," distinguishing the two?

若識能見誰復了別？見與了別二用何異？以即見色名了色故，譬如少分慧名能見亦能簡擇。如是少分識名能見亦能了別。

If consciousness is what sees, then what further thing does the discerning? What is the difference between the two functions, "seeing" and "discerning"? Because the very act of seeing form is what is named "discerning form" — just as a certain kind of wisdom is named both "able to see" and "able to discriminate." In the same way, a certain kind of consciousness is named both "able to see" and "able to discern."

有餘難言：若眼能見，眼是見者，誰是見用？此言非難，如共許識是能了別，然無了者。了用不同，見亦應爾。有餘復言：眼識能見，是見所依故，眼亦名能見。如鳴所依故，亦說鍾能鳴。

Some raise this objection: if the eye is able to see, and the eye is the seer, then what is the separate function of "seeing"? This objection does not hold — just as it is commonly accepted that consciousness is what discerns, without there needing to be a separate "discerner" apart from the discerning itself, seeing should be understood the same way. Others, again, say: eye-consciousness is what is able to see; but since the eye is the support for that seeing, the eye too may be named "able to see" — just as, because a mallet is the support for a ringing sound, one also says "the bell is able to ring."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The scriptural citations tighten the argument on the "consciousness sees" side: calling the eye a "gateway... for seeing" only makes sense if the gateway and the seeing are two different things, not one and the same. The objection about needing a separate "seer" behind "the eye sees" is dismissed by pointing to a principle already accepted without controversy elsewhere: nobody demands a separate metaphysical "discerner" standing behind consciousness's own discerning — the discerning simply is what consciousness does — so demanding a separate "seer" behind seeing is an inconsistent double standard.

The bell analogy then opens a genuinely different, third possibility, one that lets both camps be partly right: eye-consciousness is what actually sees, full stop, but the eye, as its indispensable support, can also legitimately be called "able to see" in a secondary sense — exactly as a struck bell is credited with "ringing" even though, strictly, the ringing is the sound produced by the striking. This is not a compromise for its own sake; it is a recognition that ordinary and scriptural language often credits a support with an action performed through it, without this being an error, as long as everyone understands which sense is meant.

36. If the Eye Can Be Called "Sees," Why Not Also "Cognizes"?

The bell analogy invites an obvious test: if a support can be credited with its dependent's action, shouldn't the eye also be called "able to cognize," since it equally supports cognition? The reply appeals not to deep principle but to how language actually works.

Chinese Source

若爾，眼根識所依，故應名能識。無如是失。世間同許眼識是見，由彼生時說能見色，不言識色。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that is so, one might press: the eye faculty, being consciousness's support, should then also be named "able to cognize." There is no such fault. It is commonly agreed in the world that eye-consciousness is "seeing" — because when it arises, one says it "sees form," not "cognizes form."

毘婆沙中亦作是說：若眼所得、眼識所受說名所見。是故但說眼名能見，不名能識。唯識現前說能識色，譬如說日名能作晝。

The Mahāvibhāṣā likewise states this: whatever is apprehended by the eye and received by eye-consciousness is named "the seen." For this reason, it is only said that the eye is "able to see," not that it is "able to cognize." Only when consciousness itself is directly present is form said to be "cognized" — just as one says the sun is "able to make daytime."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reply does not reach for a deep metaphysical distinction between "seeing" and "cognizing" to block the over-extension — it appeals to plain usage instead, backed by the Mahāvibhāṣā's own authority: people simply do not say "the eye cognizes," they say "the eye sees," and that settled linguistic fact is treated as reason enough. The sun comparison drives the point home: we say the sun "makes daytime," crediting it with an effect it is deeply involved in producing, without extending that same crediting habit to every other effect sunlight happens to cause. Language picks its conventional shorthand unevenly, and the treatise is content to let that unevenness stand rather than force it into a tidier system than ordinary speech actually has.

37. The Sautrāntika Move: Dissolving the Whole Debate

Rather than choose a side, the Sautrāntika masters step back from the entire question and argue that "who really sees" was never the right thing to be asking in the first place.

Chinese Source

經部諸師有作是說：如何共聚楂掣虛空眼色等緣生於眼識？此等於見孰為能所？唯法因果實無作用，為順世情假興言說，眼名能見、識名能了，智者於中不應封著。

Ru-Yi Translation

The masters of the Sautrāntika school have made this statement: how could space, the eye, form, and the other conditions, merely gathered together, give rise to eye-consciousness in the way ordinarily imagined? Among all of this, which is the agent of seeing, and which the object? There are, in reality, only dharmas as cause and effect — no literal activity at all. To accord with worldly convention, language provisionally sets up expressions such as "the eye is able to see" and "consciousness is able to discern"; the wise should not become attached to these as literally true.

如世尊說：方域言詞不應堅執，世俗名想不應固求。然迦濕彌羅國毘婆沙宗，說眼能見、耳能聞、鼻能嗅、舌能嘗、身能覺、意能了。

As the World-Honored One said: "One should not rigidly cling to regional expressions; one should not stubbornly insist on conventional names and concepts." The Vaibhāṣika school of Kashmir, however, holds that the eye is able to see, the ear able to hear, the nose able to smell, the tongue able to taste, the body able to feel, and the mind able to discern.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Every earlier position in this long debate — the eye sees, consciousness sees, both in different senses — shared one assumption: that some single thing must be the genuine agent of seeing, with everything else merely instrumental. The Sautrāntika masters reject the assumption itself. On their account, what actually exists is only a causal process — conditions such as space, the eye, and visible form coming together to produce eye-consciousness as their effect — and asking "which of these is the real seer, and which the thing seen" imposes an agent-and-action grammar onto a process that, strictly, has neither. Ordinary phrases like "the eye sees" are kept, but demoted to convenient fictions, adopted only because that is how ordinary people talk, never mistaken for descriptions of how things ultimately are. The citation from the Buddha is chosen precisely for this purpose: a warning against treating any culture's or dialect's words — including the very technical vocabulary this treatise has spent dozens of sections building — as more real than the dharmas those words are only ever pointing at.

The treatise does not let this dissolve the practical question entirely, though: it immediately notes that the Kashmir Vaibhāṣika school continues to speak, quite deliberately, of each of the six faculties performing its own proper function — eye seeing, ear hearing, and so on — which sets up the next, delightfully concrete puzzle this fascicle takes up: when a single form is seen, is it seen with one eye, or with two?

38. One Eye, or Two? A Plainly Empirical Answer

After a long, abstract debate over what "seeing" even is, the treatise closes this whole cluster of questions with a small, almost anticlimactic dose of ordinary observation.

Chinese Source

此無定准。頌曰：或二眼俱時，見色分明故。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no fixed rule regarding this. A verse states: sometimes it is with both eyes together, because seeing form is clearer that way.

論曰：阿毘達磨諸大論師咸言：或時二眼俱見，以開二眼見色分明，開一眼時不分明故。

Prose: The great Abhidharma masters all say: sometimes one sees with both eyes together, because when both eyes are open, form is seen clearly, whereas when only one eye is open, it is not as clear.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

After the Sautrāntika masters just finished warning against clinging to words as though they described ultimate reality, the treatise turns around and answers a question about eyes with the plainest possible appeal to ordinary experience: open both eyes and things look clearer than with one closed, so sometimes both are genuinely involved together. No further metaphysics is invoked — the "great Abhidharma masters" settle this one the way anyone would, by simply noticing what happens. It is a fitting, almost wry close to one of the longest and most technical debates in the whole fascicle: for every question this dense about what a faculty ultimately is, there remains a simpler, everyday fact sitting right underneath it.

39. The Double-Moon Proof, and Why Two Eyes Don't Mean Two Consciousnesses

A second, sharper piece of ordinary evidence confirms that both eyes genuinely participate together — and this raises a question the treatise answers by appeal to consciousness's own non-spatial nature.

Chinese Source

又開一眼觸一眼時，便於現前見二月等，閉一觸一此事則無，是故或時二眼俱見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, when one eye is open and the other is pressed, one sees double — two moons and the like — appearing before one. When both eyes are closed and only one is pressed, this does not happen. For this reason, sometimes both eyes genuinely see together.

非所依別識成二分，住無方故不同礙色。

It is not that consciousness, because its supports are two distinct organs, thereby splits into two separate portions — since consciousness abides without spatial location, it is not like matter, which excludes other matter from occupying the same place.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The double-moon test is a real, easily reproduced phenomenon — gently pressing one open eye while the other stays open genuinely produces doubled vision, while pressing a closed eye does nothing, because there is no second image being contributed for the first to interfere with. This is offered as clean evidence that both eyes are actively involved in ordinary seeing, not just one at a time.

But this raises an obvious worry: if there are two separate eyes, shouldn't there be two separate eye-consciousnesses, one per organ, rather than one unified experience? The answer draws directly on a fact already established about matter versus mind: physical things obstruct each other because they occupy determinate positions in space and cannot share one place — this is exactly what "resistance" meant back in section 2. Consciousness has no location in that sense at all, so nothing about having two physical supports forces it to split into two separate portions. One eye-consciousness, arising in dependence on two organs at once, is no more paradoxical than one thought depending on more than one condition.

40. Which Senses Reach Across a Distance, and Which Must Touch?

A genuinely striking new question opens: does a faculty need direct contact with its object to sense it, or can it reach across a gap? The answer splits the six faculties cleanly in two, with a memorable detail about the eye.

Chinese Source

若此宗說眼見耳聞乃至意了，彼所取境根正取時，為至不至？頌曰：眼耳意根境，不至三相違。

Ru-Yi Translation

If this school holds that the eye sees, the ear hears, and so on up to the mind discerning — when these faculties directly take hold of their objects, is contact required, or not? A verse states: for the eye, ear, and mind faculties, their objects are not directly reached by contact; the remaining three are the opposite.

論曰：眼耳意根取非至境，謂眼能見遠處諸色，眼中藥等則不能觀。耳亦能聞遠處聲響，逼耳根者則不能聞。

Prose: The eye, ear, and mind faculties take hold of their objects without contact — that is, the eye is able to see distant forms, yet a substance such as medicinal ointment placed directly on the eye cannot itself be seen. The ear, likewise, is able to hear distant sounds, yet something pressed directly against the ear cannot be heard.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The eye and ear turn out to need distance, not proximity — a genuinely counterintuitive fact stated here with a vivid, easily-verified example: eye ointment applied directly onto the eyeball cannot be seen at all, precisely because it has no distance from the eye to be seen across, even though it is about as close as anything could possibly get. The same holds for the ear: sound pressed directly against it is not heard as sound in the normal way. This groups the eye, ear, and mind together as "distance" faculties, setting them apart from the nose, tongue, and body, which the verse's second half identifies as needing the opposite — direct contact with their object to sense it at all, exactly as one would expect from smell, taste, and touch.

41. The Magnet Argument: Acting at a Distance Without Acting Everywhere

An objection presses the "distance, not contact" claim about the eye toward an absurd extreme, and the treatise answers with one of its most elegant physical analogies.

Chinese Source

若眼耳根唯取至境，則修定者應不修生天眼、耳根，如鼻根等。

Ru-Yi Translation

If the eye and ear faculties only took contacted objects, then those who cultivate meditative concentration ought not to cultivate the arising of the divine eye and divine ear faculties, just as they do not cultivate anything comparable for the nose faculty and the like.

若眼能見不至色者，何故不能普見一切遠有障等不至諸色？如何磁石吸不至鐵，非吸一切不至鐵耶？執見至境亦同此難。何故不能普見一切眼藥籌等至眼諸色？

If the eye is able to see form without contact, why then can it not see universally — every distant, obstructed, non-contacted form whatsoever? How is it that a magnet attracts iron without contact, yet does not attract all non-contacted iron everywhere? Those who instead hold that seeing requires contact face the identical difficulty in reverse: why, then, can the eye not universally see every form that genuinely is in contact with it — such as eye medicine, a probe, and the like touching the eye directly?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The first point is a clean piece of indirect evidence: Buddhist meditation cultivates a "divine eye" and "divine ear" — supernormal, far-reaching sight and hearing — but never a comparably cultivated "divine nose." That asymmetry only makes sense if eye and ear are already, by nature, distance-senses capable of being extended further, while smell remains a contact-sense no amount of cultivation turns into something else.

The magnet is the real payoff, and it is a genuinely elegant piece of reasoning: a magnet plainly attracts iron without touching it, proving that "acting without contact" is an entirely ordinary, unmysterious kind of causation — yet no one expects a magnet to pull in every piece of iron in the world regardless of distance, since its influence still has a natural, limited range. "Requires no contact" and "has unlimited reach" are simply different claims, and the objection had quietly assumed they were the same. The mirror-image challenge closes the case symmetrically: anyone tempted to think the eye needs contact after all runs straight into the eye-medicine problem already raised — an object placed directly on the eye is exactly what cannot be seen, which a contact-requirement can't explain at all.

42. Even Contact Senses Have Limits, and an Exception for the Ear

The treatise closes this question by generalizing its point, addressing the immaterial mind faculty, and recording one exception that keeps the ear from fitting the pattern too neatly.

Chinese Source

又如鼻等能取至境，然不能取一切與根俱有香等。如是眼根雖見不至而非一切，耳根亦爾。意無色故，非能有至。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, just as the nose and the other contact faculties are able to take contacted objects, yet cannot take every smell and the like that happens to be in contact with the faculty, so too, although the eye faculty sees without contact, it does not see everything either; the ear faculty is likewise so. Since the mind is without form, it cannot have contact at all.

有執：耳根通取至境及不至境，自耳中聲亦能聞故。所餘鼻等三有色根，與上相違唯取至境。

Some hold: the ear faculty takes both contacted and non-contacted objects, because a sound arising within one's own ear can also be heard. The remaining three material faculties — the nose and the rest — are the opposite of the above; they take only contacted objects.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The generalizing move is a graceful way to close the magnet discussion: having a natural limit is not something peculiar that needs explaining away for the eye and ear — even the plain contact senses do not register literally everything touching them, so limits are just an ordinary feature of any sense faculty, distance-based or not. The mind is set aside on entirely different grounds: being immaterial, the very concept of physical "contact" simply does not apply to it, so it cannot be sorted into either camp in the first place.

The ear gets a genuine complication rather than a tidy answer: while the general rule groups it with the eye as a distance sense, someone points out that a sound arising directly within one's own ear can still be heard — a real exception the treatise records rather than smooths over. This closes the whole distance-versus-contact question with the same intellectual honesty seen throughout this fascicle: the clean threefold division (eye/ear/mind at a distance, nose/tongue/body by contact) is basically right, but not perfectly tidy at the edges, and the text says so rather than pretending otherwise.

43. Do Atoms Really Touch? Vasumitra Against the Bhadanta

The treatise pushes "contact" down to its most fundamental level and finds a real disagreement between two named masters over whether truly partless atoms can touch at all — with Vasubandhu himself openly stating a preference.

Chinese Source

云何三根由無間生名取至境？即由無間名取至境，謂於中間都無片物。又和合色許有分故，相觸無失。由許此理，毘婆沙文義善成立。

Ru-Yi Translation

How is it that the three contact faculties, by their object arising with no interval, are named as taking contacted objects? It is precisely by arising with no interval that this is named taking a contacted object — meaning there is no intervening matter whatsoever in between. Furthermore, since compound matter is accepted as having parts, there is no fault in things mutually touching. By accepting this principle, the meaning of the Vibhāṣā's text is well established.

尊者世友說：諸極微相觸即應住至後念。然大德說：一切極微實不相觸，但由無間假立觸名。此大德意應可愛樂。

The Venerable Vasumitra says: if atoms truly touch one another, they should remain so, persisting into the next moment. The Bhadanta, however, says: no atom, in reality, ever touches another at all — it is only because they stand with no interval between them that the name "touching" is provisionally applied. This Elder's view should be preferred.

若異此者，是諸極微應有間隙。中間既空，誰障其行許為有對？又離極微無和合色，和合相觸即觸極微。又許極微若有方分，觸與不觸皆應有分。若無方分，設許相觸亦無斯過。

If it were otherwise, these atoms should have a gap between them — and if that space between were truly empty, what would obstruct their movement, given that they are accepted as resistant? Furthermore, apart from atoms there is no compound matter, so if compound matter's mutual touching is accepted as real, this amounts to atoms themselves touching. Furthermore, if atoms are accepted as having spatial parts, then both touching and not touching would have to involve such parts; if they have no spatial parts at all, then even granting that they "touch," no such fault follows.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the treatise reaching its deepest, most abstract point of physics — asking not whether ordinary objects touch, but whether the truly partless building blocks of matter itself can touch at all. Vasumitra's worry is a real paradox: if two atoms genuinely made contact, something about that contact ought to persist stably, yet atoms are supposed to be momentary. The Bhadanta's answer dissolves the paradox by denying the premise: atoms never literally touch in the first place; "touching" is simply the conventional name given to two atoms standing at zero distance from each other, with nothing between them, not a claim that their surfaces genuinely meet. Vasubandhu's own explicit preference for this view — a rare, unmistakable authorial judgment rather than a bare report of positions — is worth noticing; it fits the same pattern already seen in the "who really sees" debate, where his sympathies lean toward the more analytically careful, less literal-minded reading.

The supporting argument is genuinely elegant: if the space between adjacent atoms is truly empty, nothing "solid" sits there to stop them collapsing into one another — so their staying apart at all, resistant and distinct, only makes sense if "touching" was never a literal merging to begin with. And the point about spatial parts sharpens the paradox precisely: an atom with parts could touch another part-to-part in an intelligible way, but a truly partless, dimensionless atom touching another partless atom is exactly the kind of contact that cannot coherently be pictured as contact at all — which is exactly why the Bhadanta's "provisional label, not literal event" reading is offered as the way through.

44. Does a Tiny Faculty Need a Tiny Object? The Contact Senses' Answer

Given how atomically small a sense faculty is, a natural puzzle arises: how can it ever take in something as vast as a mountain? The treatise answers for the three contact faculties first, with a strict one-to-one rule.

Chinese Source

又眼等根，為於自境唯取等量，速疾轉故，如旋火輪見大山等？為於自境通取等量不等量耶？頌曰：應知鼻等三，唯取等量境。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: do the eye and the other faculties, with respect to their own object, take only objects of equal measure — with the appearance of something larger, such as seeing a great mountain, arising merely from rapid succession, the way a whirling firebrand appears as a circle? Or do they take, with respect to their own object, both equal and unequal measure? A verse states: it should be understood that the nose and the other two take only objects of equal measure.

論曰：前說至境鼻等三根，應知唯能取等量境。如根微量，境微亦然，相稱合生鼻等識故。

Prose: The nose and the other two faculties, already said to take contacted objects, should be understood as able to take only objects of equal measure. Just as the faculty's own atomic extent is a certain measure, the object's atomic extent is likewise that same measure — only when the two correspond and combine does the nose-consciousness and so on arise.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The whirling-firebrand comparison names a real and vivid illusion — spin a burning torch fast enough and it looks like a solid ring of fire, when really it is one point of flame moving through many positions in rapid succession. The question is whether ordinary large-scale seeing works the same way: does the eye only ever take in something exactly its own tiny atomic size, with the appearance of a whole mountain being stitched together the way the ring of fire is? For the three contact faculties — nose, tongue, body — the treatise gives a clean, strict answer with no such illusion involved: the faculty's own atomic scale and the object's atomic scale must match exactly, one to one, for a nose-consciousness or the like to arise at all. This leaves the more interesting case — the eye and ear, which plainly do register mountains and thunderclaps far larger than themselves — as the very question the next section takes up.

45. The Eye and Ear Are Unrestricted, and What the Mind Escapes Entirely

Unlike the three contact faculties, the eye and ear turn out to have no fixed size requirement at all — and the mind escapes this whole question in a different way, by having no size to speak of.

Chinese Source

眼耳不定，謂眼於色有時取小如見毛端，有時取大如暫開目見大山等，有時取等如見蒲桃。如是耳根，聽蚊雷等所發種種小大音聲，隨其所應小大等量。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eye and ear are not fixed. The eye, with respect to form, sometimes takes something small, as when seeing the tip of a hair; sometimes takes something large, as when briefly opening the eyes and seeing a great mountain; sometimes takes something equal, as when seeing a grape. In the same way, the ear faculty, hearing the various small and large sounds produced by a mosquito, thunder, and the like, takes them according to whatever measure is appropriate — small, large, or equal.

意無質礙，不可辯其形量差別。

The mind, having no material obstruction, cannot be analyzed in terms of any difference of shape or measure at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The puzzle from the previous section resolves cleanly by simply not generalizing the contact faculties' strict rule: nose, tongue, and body need exact atomic correspondence, but the eye and ear were never bound by that requirement at all — they take in a hair's tip, a mountain, or a grape with equal ease, no illusion of rapid succession required to explain the mountain case. The three examples are nicely graded (smaller than, larger than, and equal to some implicit reference), covering the full range plainly. The mind is dismissed from this whole question for the simplest possible reason: having no matter, no obstruction, and no location, it has no size at all to be measured against anything — the question "how big a mind-object can the mind take in" does not even arise for the same reason "how big a shadow does an idea cast" does not.

46. A Crystal Ball, a Roll of Birch-Bark, a Pair of Fingernails: How the Faculties Are Shaped

The treatise closes fascicle 1's long study of the faculties with a set of vivid, almost poetic physical similes for how each one's atoms are actually arranged in the body.

Chinese Source

有說：重累如丸而住，體清澈故，如頗胝迦不相障礙。耳根極微居耳穴內旋環而住，如卷樺皮。鼻根極微居鼻頞內背上面下，如雙爪甲。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some say: the eye's atoms are layered, dwelling like a small ball; being transparent in substance, like crystal, they do not obstruct one another. The ear faculty's atoms dwell within the ear canal, coiled in a ring, like a rolled sheet of birch-bark. The nose faculty's atoms dwell within the bridge of the nose, back above and front below, like a pair of fingernails.

此初三根橫作行度處無高下，如冠花鬘。舌根極微布在舌上，形如半月。傳說：舌中如毛端量，非為舌根極微所遍。身根極微遍住身分，如身形量。

These first three faculties are arranged in a row at the same level, without variation in height, like a garland worn as a crown. The tongue faculty's atoms are spread upon the tongue, shaped like a half-moon. It has been transmitted that a spot on the tongue about the size of a hair's tip is not pervaded by the tongue faculty's atoms. The body faculty's atoms pervade every part of the body, matching the very shape of the body itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

These are genuinely charming images for describing matter that, by this treatise's own account, no eye can ever see — the "pure form" faculties are imperceptible, yet the tradition still pictures their shape concretely: a tiny transparent ball for the eye, a coiled strip of birch-bark for the ear canal, a pair of fingernail-shaped patches for the nose, a half-moon for the tongue (with one tiny untouched spot), and, uniquely, a shape that exactly follows the whole body for the body faculty — the only one of the five that is coextensive with its owner rather than confined to one small organ.

47. The Sex Organs' Shape, and Why No Single Atom Ever Perceives Alone

Two sex-specific faculties are added to the anatomical survey, same-class and counterpart-class return applied to actual atoms, and the section closes with an important atomistic principle: no single atom, ever, is enough to produce a sense-consciousness by itself.

Chinese Source

女根極微形如鼓𣞙，男根極微形如指𩎽。眼根極微有時一切皆是同分，有時一切皆彼同分，有時一分是彼同分餘是同分。乃至舌根極微亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

The atoms of the female organ are shaped like a small drum; the atoms of the male organ are shaped like a fingertip covering. The eye faculty's atoms are sometimes entirely same-class, sometimes entirely counterpart-class, and sometimes partly one and partly the other. The same holds up through the tongue faculty's atoms.

身根極微定無一切皆是同分，乃至極熱捺落迦中猛焰纏身，猶有無量身根極微是彼同分。

The body faculty's atoms definitely never have all of them entirely same-class — even in the hottest hell, where fierce flames engulf the body entirely, countless atoms of the body faculty still remain counterpart-class.

傳說：身根設遍發識身應散壞，以無根境各一極微為所依緣能發身識。五識決定積集多微，方成所依所緣性故。

It has been transmitted: if the body faculty, in its entirety, gave rise to consciousness all at once, the body would disperse and be destroyed. This is because there is no single pair of faculty-atom and object-atom, each just one atom, capable by itself of giving rise to body-consciousness. The five sense-consciousnesses definitely require an accumulation of many atoms before they can attain the nature of a genuine support and object at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The eye and tongue's flexible same-class/counterpart-class status (sections 26–28, now applied down at the atomic level) contrasts sharply with an unconditional claim about the body: not even the most extreme conceivable stimulus — total immersion in the fiercest hell-fire — ever activates every single atom of the body faculty at once. Some portion always remains merely present, uninvolved, no matter how intense the sensation. The reason given generalizes into a genuine principle of this whole atomistic system, tying together several threads from across this fascicle: no isolated atom, whether of a faculty or an object, is ever by itself sufficient to produce a sense-consciousness — perception is always the work of accumulated clusters (the "accumulable" matter first defined in section 14), never a single indivisible point acting alone. The remark that total simultaneous activation would destroy the body reads almost like a safety principle built into the system: perception has to stay partial and localized, or the whole structure it depends on could not hold together.

48. Is a Consciousness's Support Past, or Simultaneous With It?

A single atom being invisible confirms the previous section's principle in passing, before the treatise opens a new question: does a consciousness's support exist at the same time as the consciousness, or always one step behind it?

Chinese Source

即由此理亦說極微名無見體，不可見故。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this same principle, an atom is also said to be an "invisible substance," because it cannot be seen on its own.

如前所說識有六種，謂眼識界乃至意識，為如五識唯緣現在意識通緣三世非世，如是諸識依亦爾耶？不爾，云何？頌曰：後依唯過去，五識依或俱。

As previously stated, consciousness is of six kinds — the eye-consciousness element, up through the mind-consciousness. Just as the five sense-consciousnesses take only the present as their object, while mind-consciousness takes all three times and what is outside time as well — are the supports of these various consciousnesses likewise so? Not so. How, then? A verse states: the latter's support is only past; the five consciousnesses' support may be simultaneous.

論曰：意識唯依無間滅意，眼等五識所依或俱，或言表此亦依過去。眼是眼識俱生所依，如是乃至身是身識俱生所依。

Prose: Mind-consciousness depends only on the mind that has just ceased without interval. The support for the eye and the other five consciousnesses may be simultaneous — the word "may" indicates that this also, at times, depends on the past. The eye is the eye-consciousness's co-arising support; in the same way, up through: the body is the body-consciousness's co-arising support.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The atom's invisibility is a quiet callback: since perception always requires an accumulated cluster, a single atom, whatever it's made of, is as unseeable as it is unable to produce any consciousness alone.

The main question reaches back to the mind element's definition from fascicle 1's sections 29–31: manas is, by nature, always the immediately-past moment of a consciousness, so mind-consciousness's support is never simultaneous with it — it is always exactly one step behind. The five physical senses work differently: the eye typically exists at the very same time as the eye-consciousness it supports, a genuinely different temporal relationship from manas's strictly-past role. The verse's careful wording ("may be simultaneous") leaves room for an exception the prose flags but does not yet unpack — that the five senses' support can, in some circumstances, also be past rather than simultaneous, a refinement the text is clearly building toward completing next.

49. Two Supports for Every Sense, and Support Versus Causal Condition

The exception flagged in the previous section resolves into an elegant structure: every one of the five senses actually has two supports at once, one unique and simultaneous, one shared and past — which then opens a fine distinction between "support" and a separate kind of causal condition.

Chinese Source

無間滅意是過去依。此五識身所依各二，謂眼等五是別所依，意根為五通所依性，故如是說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The immediately-ceased mind is the past support. Each of the five sense-consciousness-bodies has two supports: the eye and the other four are each its own distinct support, while the mind faculty serves as a common support for all five — hence it is said this way.

若是眼識所依性者，即是眼識等無間緣耶？設是眼識等無間緣者，復是眼識所依性耶？應作四句。第一句，謂俱生眼根。第二句，謂無間滅心所法界。第三句，謂過去意根。第四句，謂除所說法。

If something has the nature of eye-consciousness's support, is it therefore also eye-consciousness's immediately-antecedent condition? Conversely, if something is eye-consciousness's immediately-antecedent condition, is it therefore also its support? A fourfold analysis should be made. First case: the co-arising eye faculty. Second case: the immediately-ceased mental factors within the dharma element. Third case: the past mind faculty. Fourth case: whatever falls outside what has been stated.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The apparent tension from the previous section dissolves once "support" is recognized as never singular: every sense-consciousness has two supports operating together, not one or the other by turns. The eye is eye-consciousness's own, unique, simultaneous support; manas — the just-past moment of any of the six consciousnesses — is a support shared in common by all five senses alike, always past. Both are present at once for every act of seeing, hearing, and so on.

The fourfold analysis then separates two causal notions this fascicle has used somewhat interchangeably until now: "support" (āśraya, what a consciousness leans on to arise) and "immediately-antecedent condition" (samanantara-pratyaya, one of the four classical conditions, naming what must immediately cease for a next moment to arise at all). The eye has the first property without the second, since it doesn't need to cease for eye-consciousness to arise. The just-ceased associated mental factors have the second without the first — necessary antecedents, but not supports as such. Only the past mind faculty has genuinely both properties together, which is exactly why it earns the double role manas has carried since fascicle 1's section 29: it is simultaneously what a consciousness leans on and what had to cease for that consciousness's moment to open up at all.

50. The Fourfold Analysis Extended: The Other Senses, and Mind-Consciousness's Exception

The fourfold analysis just completed for the eye is now generalized to the remaining senses, and mind-consciousness turns out to need only half of it.

Chinese Source

乃至身識亦爾，各各應說自根。

Ru-Yi Translation

The same holds through to body-consciousness: for each of the remaining four senses, the same fourfold analysis should be restated in terms of its own faculty.

意識應作順前句答，謂是意識所依性者，定是意識等無間緣。有是意識等無間緣，非與意識為所依性，謂無間滅心所法界。

For mind-consciousness, however, the answer follows only the first case: whatever has the nature of mind-consciousness's support is necessarily also its immediately-antecedent condition. But there is something that is mind-consciousness's immediately-antecedent condition without being its support — namely, the immediately-ceased mental factors within the dharma element.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Mind-consciousness collapses the fourfold pattern down to a simple one-way relation, and the reason traces straight back to section 48: unlike the eye, which is eye-consciousness's simultaneous support and not itself an immediately-antecedent condition, mind-consciousness has no such simultaneous faculty at all. Its only support is manas, the just-past moment of consciousness — which, by its very definition as "immediately-ceased," is automatically also an immediately-antecedent condition. So for mind-consciousness, support always implies condition; there is no case of a support that fails to be a condition, the way the co-arising eye did for eye-consciousness.

What survives is only the reverse asymmetry: the mental factors that arise together with the immediately-past mind and cease along with it are, like manas itself, immediately-antecedent conditions for the next moment of mind-consciousness — but they are not what mind-consciousness is said to "lean on." Only manas carries that specific role. Hence one case is missing entirely (support without condition), and two of the original four collapse into one (support that is also condition), leaving just the two possibilities the text states.

51. Why "Support" Names Only the Faculty, Never the Object

Consciousness always arises from two conditions together, the faculty and its object — so why does only the faculty get called "support"?

Chinese Source

何因識起俱託二緣，得所依名在根非境？

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is it that although consciousness arises always relying jointly on two conditions, only the faculty earns the name "support," never the object?

頌曰：隨根變識異，故眼等名依。

Verse: Consciousness varies as the faculty varies — hence the eye and the rest are called "support."

論曰：眼等即是眼等六界，由眼等根有轉變故。諸識轉異，隨根增損識明昧故。非色等變令識有異，以識隨根不隨境故。依名唯在眼等非餘。

Prose: "The eye and the rest" means precisely the six elements — the eye and the other faculties. Because as the eye and the other faculties undergo change, the corresponding forms of consciousness change along with them, growing sharper or duller as the faculty is strengthened or impaired. It is not that a change in form and the rest causes any corresponding change in consciousness — consciousness follows the faculty, not the object. Hence the name "support" belongs only to the eye and the other faculties, never to anything else.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The argument is a simple correlation test: cataracts or failing eyesight dull what is seen even when the object itself is unchanged, while a healthy eye sees the same object sharply. Vary the faculty and the resulting consciousness visibly varies with it; vary the object instead, and consciousness does not track that change in the same way. Whatever consciousness's quality co-varies with earns the name "support" — and by this test, it is always the faculty, never the object.

This is a case of Abhidharma reasoning from function to name: "support" (āśraya) is not an arbitrary label but is meant to track the condition consciousness actually depends on for its own character. The test set up here — does the effect vary when this cause varies? — will resurface in the next section as part of a second, related naming question.

52. Why Consciousness Is Named After the Faculty, Not the Object

A companion question follows immediately: form is what gets cognized, so why is the resulting awareness called "eye-consciousness" and never "form-consciousness"?

Chinese Source

何緣色等正是所識而名眼識，乃至意識不名色識乃至法識？

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is it that although form and the rest are indeed what is cognized, the resulting consciousness is named "eye-consciousness" and so on rather than "form-consciousness" — and this holds all the way through mind-consciousness, which is likewise never called "form-consciousness," or "sound-consciousness," or "dharma-consciousness," [despite cognizing all of these]?

頌曰：彼及不共因，故隨根說識。

Verse: Because of that, and because of an exclusive cause, consciousness is named after the faculty.

論曰：彼謂前說眼等名依，根是依故隨根說識。及不共者，謂眼唯自眼識所依，色亦通為他身眼識及通自他意識所取，乃至身觸應知亦爾，由所依勝及不共因故識得名。隨根非境，如名鼓聲及麥牙等。

Prose: "That" refers to what was just explained — that the eye and the rest are called "support"; since the faculty is the support, consciousness is named after the faculty. As for "exclusive cause": the eye serves as support only for its own eye-consciousness, whereas a given form may also be perceived by another body's eye-consciousness, and by mind-consciousness — one's own as well as another's — alike; the same should be understood through to body and touch. Because the support is both the superior condition and the exclusive one, consciousness takes its name from it, following the faculty rather than the object — just as one speaks of "the drum's sound" or "the barley's sprout," and so forth.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two reasons combine here, and the verse names both: "that" points back to the previous section's finding that the faculty is what consciousness varies with, so it already qualifies as "support"; the new element is "exclusive cause" (不共因, roughly asādhāraṇa-hetu here in a loose, functional sense rather than the technical causal category of that name) — this eye supports only this stream's eye-consciousness, one faculty to one consciousness, uniquely. A form, by contrast, is common property: the very same visible form can be seen by another person's eye-consciousness, and can also be taken as an object by mind-consciousness, one's own or someone else's. The object is shared across many possible knowers; the faculty is not.

The drum and the barley sprout make the logic vivid with everyday naming habits. A drumbeat could be described by naming the drumstick, the drumhead's skin, or the air that carries the sound — but ordinary language picks out the drum itself, because striking the drum is the one factor most proximate and unique to producing that sound. A sprout could be named for the soil, the water, or the sunlight it needed to grow — but it is called a "barley sprout," after the seed, because the seed is what is exclusively and distinctively its own cause. Consciousness is named the same way: not after whatever happens to be involved, but after the one condition that belongs to it alone.

53. When Body, Eye, Form, and Consciousness Cross Realms

A concrete question follows the naming discussion: when a being sees a form with its eye, must the body it lives in, the eye, the form, and the resulting consciousness all belong to the same meditative realm — or can they diverge?

Chinese Source

隨身所住眼見色時，身眼色識地為同不？應言此四或異或同，

Ru-Yi Translation

When a being, abiding in whatever realm it is born into, sees a form with its eye — must the body, the eye, the form, and the consciousness all belong to the same realm? It should be said: these four may be either the same or different.

謂生欲界，若以自地眼見自地色，四皆自地。若以初靜慮眼見欲界色，身色欲界、眼識初定。見初定色，身屬欲界、三屬初定。

Take a being born in the desire realm. If it sees a desire-realm form with a desire-realm eye, all four belong to the desire realm. If it sees a desire-realm form with a first-dhyāna eye, the body and the form are of the desire realm while the eye and the consciousness are of the first dhyāna. If it sees a first-dhyāna form, the body belongs to the desire realm while the other three belong to the first dhyāna.

若以二靜慮眼見欲界色，身色欲界、眼屬二定、識屬初定。見初定色，身屬欲界、眼屬二定、色識初定。見二定色，身屬欲界、眼色二定、識屬初定。如是若以三四靜慮地眼見下地色或自地色，如理應思。

If it sees a desire-realm form with a second-dhyāna eye, the body and the form are of the desire realm, the eye is of the second dhyāna, and the consciousness is of the first dhyāna. If it sees a first-dhyāna form, the body is of the desire realm, the eye is of the second dhyāna, and both the form and the consciousness are of the first dhyāna. If it sees a second-dhyāna form, the body is of the desire realm, the eye and the form are of the second dhyāna, and the consciousness is of the first dhyāna. In just this way, one should reason through the cases of seeing a lower-realm or same-realm form with a third- or fourth-dhyāna eye.

生初靜慮，若以自地眼見自地色，四皆同地。見欲界色，三屬初定、色屬欲界。若以二靜慮眼見初定色，三屬初定、眼屬二定。見欲界色，身識初定、色屬欲界、眼屬二定。見二定色，身識初定、眼色二定。如是若以三四靜慮地眼見自地色或下上色，如理應思。

Take instead a being born in the first dhyāna. If it sees a first-dhyāna form with a first-dhyāna eye, all four belong to the same realm. If it sees a desire-realm form, three — the body, the eye, and the consciousness — belong to the first dhyāna while the form belongs to the desire realm. If it sees a first-dhyāna form with a second-dhyāna eye, three — the body, the form, and the consciousness — belong to the first dhyāna while the eye belongs to the second dhyāna. If it sees a desire-realm form, the body and the consciousness belong to the first dhyāna, the form belongs to the desire realm, and the eye belongs to the second dhyāna. If it sees a second-dhyāna form, the body and the consciousness belong to the first dhyāna while the eye and the form belong to the second dhyāna. In just this way, one should reason through the cases of seeing a same-realm, lower, or higher form with a third- or fourth-dhyāna eye.

如是生二三四靜慮，以自他地眼見自他地色，如理應思。餘界亦應如是分別。今當略辯此決定相。

In just the same way, for beings born in the second, third, or fourth dhyāna, seeing a form of their own or another realm with an eye of their own or another realm should be reasoned through by the same pattern. The other elements should likewise be analyzed accordingly. Let us now briefly state the fixed rule underlying all of this.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is Abhidharma working method at its most exhaustive: rather than assert the governing rule outright, the treatise walks through every logically possible pairing of body, eye, form, and consciousness across the desire realm and the four dhyānas, then explicitly leaves the remaining cases ("one should reason through...") for the reader to complete by the same pattern. Two constraints are already visible beneath the arithmetic: the eye is never of a lower realm than the body that bears it, and the resulting form and consciousness are never of a higher realm than the eye itself. A dhyāna-refined eye can look downward and perceive a coarser, lower-realm form, but a coarser eye can never perceive upward into a more refined realm's object.

The underlying picture is the standard Sarvāstivāda cosmology of nine levels — the desire realm, the four dhyānas of the form realm, and the four formless attainments above them — through which a being's physical faculties, and the objects and consciousnesses tied to them, can be distributed unevenly depending on what has been cultivated through meditation. The next section states the whole pattern directly, compressed into a single verse.

54. The Rule in Verse: Eye and Ear

The entire combinatorial exercise reduces to eight lines of verse, which the prose then unpacks for the eye and extends, point for point, to the ear.

Chinese Source

頌曰：眼不下於身，色識非上眼，色於識一切，二於身亦然。如眼耳亦然，次三皆自地，身識自下地，意不定應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: The eye is never lower than the body; form and consciousness are never higher than the eye; form, relative to consciousness, may be anything at all; the two, relative to the body, are likewise so. As with the eye, so too with the ear. The next three are each entirely confined to their own realm; body-consciousness may be of its own realm or a lower one; mind should be known to be fixed in none of these ways.

論曰：身眼色三皆通五地，謂在欲界四靜慮中，眼識唯在欲界初定。此中眼根望身生地，或等或上終不居下。色識望眼等下非上，下眼不能見上色故、上識不依下地眼故。色望於識通等上下，色識於身如色於識。

Prose: The body, the eye, and form — these three — all range through five realms, namely the desire realm and the four dhyānas. Eye-consciousness, however, occurs only in the desire realm and the first dhyāna. Here, relative to the realm the body is born into, the eye faculty is either the same or higher, never lower. Form and consciousness, relative to the eye, are the same or lower, never higher — because a lower eye cannot see a higher form, and a higher-realm consciousness does not depend on a lower-realm eye. Form, relative to consciousness, may be the same, higher, or lower without restriction; and form and consciousness, relative to the body, stand exactly as form stands relative to consciousness.

廣說耳界應知如眼，謂耳不下於身，聲識非上耳，聲於識一切。二於身亦然，隨其所應廣如眼釋。

The ear element, fully explained, should be understood just as the eye was: the ear is never lower than the body; sound and consciousness are never higher than the ear; sound, relative to consciousness, may be anything at all; and the two, relative to the body, are likewise so — elaborated in full, correspondingly, exactly as was done for the eye.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Five realms" names the desire realm plus the four dhyānas of the form realm — the range within which physical sense faculties and their objects can exist at all. Within that range, eye-consciousness (and, by the parallel drawn here, ear-consciousness) is narrower still, confined to only the desire realm and the first dhyāna; standard Abhidharma doctrine holds that none of the five sense-consciousnesses operate in the second dhyāna and above, where only mind-consciousness continues.

The asymmetry is directional, not arbitrary: a faculty refined through dhyāna attainment can reach downward to perceive a coarser realm's object, but a coarser faculty can never reach upward into a more refined realm it has not attained. That is why the eye can equal or exceed the body's realm but never fall below it, while form and consciousness can equal or fall below the eye's realm but never exceed it — the direction of possible "reach" only ever points downward from what has been cultivated.

55. The Three Contact Senses and the Mind: Closing the Digression

The nose, tongue, and body senses turn out to follow a far simpler rule than the eye and ear, and the mind element breaks the pattern entirely — after which the treatise closes this long digression and returns to its main line.

Chinese Source

鼻舌身三總皆自地，於中別者，謂身與觸其地必同。識望觸身或自或下，自謂若生欲界初定，生上三定謂之為下。

Ru-Yi Translation

The nose, tongue, and body faculties — these three — are, in general, each entirely confined to their own realm. Within this, one distinction should be noted: the body faculty and touch, its object, must always be of the very same realm. Body-consciousness, relative to touch and the touch-faculty, is either of the same realm or of a lower one. "The same" applies to a being born in the desire realm or the first dhyāna; a being born in one of the upper three dhyānas has it as "lower," since body-consciousness cannot occur there.

應知意界四事不定，謂意有時與身識法四皆同地，有時上下。身唯五地，三通一切。於遊等至及受生時，隨其所應或同或異，如後定品當廣分別。為捨繁文故今未辯，前後再述用少功多。

One should know that the mind element's four factors — the mind faculty, the body, mind-consciousness, and the dharma object — are not fixed: sometimes all four are of the same realm, and at other times they are higher or lower relative to one another. The body is confined to only the five realms, while the other three range through all of them. Whether one is merely reborn into a level or is actively moving through a meditative attainment, the relations differ correspondingly, sometimes the same and sometimes different — this will be explained in full detail later, in the chapter on meditative absorption. To avoid excessive verbosity, it is not elaborated here; saying the same thing over and over would spend much effort for little gain.

傍論已周，應辯正論。

The digression is now complete. Let us return to the main line of the treatise.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nose, tongue, and body faculties are simpler than the eye and ear because, in Abhidharma doctrine, only sight and hearing operate at a distance and can therefore be "refined" through dhyāna attainment to reach beyond their own realm; smell, taste, and touch require direct contact and so stay locked to a single realm throughout. Body-consciousness's "same or lower" rule is a direct echo of eye-consciousness's own restriction to the desire realm and first dhyāna from section 54: a being born in one of the upper three dhyānas still has a physical touch-faculty of that realm, but since body-consciousness itself cannot arise there, it manifests instead at the first dhyāna — the highest level at which any of the five sense-consciousnesses can occur.

The mind element's total flexibility follows from the same doctrine seen from its other side: mind-consciousness alone continues into the four formless attainments, where no physical body, faculty, or sense-consciousness exists at all. That is why the treatise says the body is capped at five realms while mind, mind-consciousness, and the dharma object range through all nine — desire realm, four dhyānas, and four formless attainments together. With that, this long excursus into how the senses relate across cosmological realms closes, and the treatise explicitly signals its return to the main analysis of the eighteen elements.
