The Eighteen Unshared Dharmas

Fascicle 27 continues Chapter 7 (分別智品第七之二, Distinguishing Knowledge) by turning from knowledge in general to the eighteen dharmas possessed by a Buddha alone — the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the three foundations of mindfulness, and great compassion — none of which any other noble one, however advanced, ever attains.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 16 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 27 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. The Ten Powers, and Even Śāriputra's Limits

Having finished distinguishing the ten kinds of knowledge in fascicle 26, the treatise now turns to what that knowledge accomplishes — beginning with the eighteen dharmas unique to a Buddha, cultivated only at the very moment of first becoming a Buddha, at the stage of exhaustion-knowledge, and possessed by no other noble one whatsoever.

Chinese Source

如是已辯諸智差別，智所成德今當顯示，於中先辯佛不共德。且初成佛盡智位修不共佛法有十八種。何謂十八？頌曰： 十八不共法， 謂佛十力等。 論曰：佛十力、四無畏、三念住及大悲，如是合名為十八不共法。唯於諸佛盡智時修，餘聖所無，故名不共。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus distinguished the various knowledges, we should now reveal the virtues that knowledge accomplishes; among these, let us first discuss the Buddha's unshared virtues. At the very moment of first becoming a Buddha, the stage of exhaustion-knowledge, eighteen kinds of unshared Buddha-dharmas are cultivated. What are these eighteen? Verse: the eighteen unshared dharmas are the Buddha's ten powers and the rest. Explanation: the Buddha's ten powers, four fearlessnesses, three foundations of mindfulness, and great compassion — these, combined, are called the eighteen unshared dharmas. They are cultivated only at the time of a Buddha's exhaustion-knowledge; no other noble one possesses them, hence they are called unshared.

The treatise then works through the ten powers one by one, specifying exactly which of the ten knowledges from the previous fascicle constitutes each.

Chinese Source

且佛十力相別云何？頌曰： 力處非處十， 業八除滅道， 定根解界九， 遍趣九或十， 宿住死生俗， 盡六或十智， 宿住死生智， 依靜慮餘通， 贍部男佛身， 於境無礙故。 論曰：佛十力者，一處非處智力，具以如來十智為性；二業異熟智力，八智為性，謂除滅道。三靜慮解脫等持等至智力，四根上下智力，五種種勝解智力，六種種界智力，如是四力皆九智性，謂除滅智。七遍趣行智力，或聲顯此義有二途，若謂但緣能趣為境九智除滅，若謂亦緣所趣為境十智為性。八宿住隨念智力，九死生智力，如是二力皆俗智性。十漏盡智力，或聲亦顯義有二途，若謂但緣漏盡為境六智，除道、苦、集、他心。若謂漏盡身中所得，十智為性。已辯自性。依地別者，第八第九依四靜慮，餘八通依十一地起，欲、四靜慮、未至、中間并四無色，名十一地。已辯依地。依身別者，皆依贍部男子佛身。已辯依身。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, specifically, distinguishes the Buddha's ten powers? Verse: [the powers, and which knowledges constitute each, are detailed in the explanation below]. Explanation: the Buddha's ten powers are as follows. First, the power of knowledge of what is and is not possible, constituted in full by all ten of the Tathāgata's knowledges. Second, the power of knowledge of karma and its ripening, constituted by eight knowledges, excluding cessation and path. Third, the power of knowledge of the dhyānas, liberations, concentrations, and meditative attainments; fourth, the power of knowledge of others' faculties, superior and inferior; fifth, the power of knowledge of various resolute understandings; sixth, the power of knowledge of various dispositions — these four powers are each constituted by nine knowledges, excluding cessation-knowledge. Seventh, the power of knowledge of the path leading everywhere: the text's own "or" indicates two possible readings — if it takes only the leading path itself as object, it is constituted by nine knowledges, excluding cessation; if it also takes the destination as object, it is constituted by all ten. Eighth, the power of the knowledge of recollecting past lives; ninth, the power of the knowledge of death and rebirth — these two are each constituted by worldly knowledge alone. Tenth, the power of the knowledge of the exhaustion of contaminants: again, two readings are possible — if it takes only the exhaustion of contaminants itself as object, it is constituted by six knowledges, excluding path, suffering, origination, and knowledge of others' minds; if it is whatever is possessed within the body in which contaminants have been exhausted, it is constituted by all ten. Their own nature has now been explained. As for their dependence on ground: the eighth and ninth depend on the four root dhyānas; the remaining eight extend through dependence on eleven grounds — the desire realm, the four dhyānas, the not-yet-attained stage, the intermediate dhyāna, together with the four formless spheres, making eleven grounds. Their dependence on ground has now been explained. As for their dependence on body: all depend on a Buddha's body, specifically that of a male in Jambudvīpa. Their dependence on body has now been explained.

Finally, the treatise explains why these are called "powers" at all, illustrating the point with a striking episode from even the wisest of the Buddha's disciples.

Chinese Source

何故名力？以於一切所知境中智無礙轉，故名為力。由此十力唯依佛身，唯佛已除諸惑習氣於一切境隨欲能知，餘此相違故不名力。如舍利子捨求度人、不能觀知鷹所逐鴿前後二際生多少等。如是諸佛遍於所知心力無邊。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are these called powers? Because, with respect to every knowable object, knowledge operates without obstruction — hence they are called powers. For this reason, these ten powers depend exclusively on a Buddha's body: only a Buddha, having eliminated every affliction's habitual residue, is able to know whatever they wish with respect to every object; others, being the reverse of this, are not said to possess this power. For example, Śāriputra once gave up seeking to liberate a certain person, and was unable to perceive how many past and future lifetimes a dove being pursued by a hawk had lived through, and the like. In this way, the buddhas' mental power extends without limit over everything knowable.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the Śāriputra episode as one of the most memorable illustrations in this project so far: Śāriputra, traditionally honored as foremost in wisdom among the Buddha's disciples, is shown here as still finitely limited — unable to perceive the full lifetime-history behind a single hunted dove, and (in the fuller traditional telling this passage alludes to only briefly) unable to determine whether a particular person was even a suitable candidate for liberation, a judgment the Buddha alone could make correctly. The point is not to diminish Śāriputra but to sharpen the contrast: even the wisest arhat's knowledge, vast as it is, remains bounded in a way the ten powers specifically are defined to exceed.

We should also flag the double readings offered for two of the ten powers (the seventh, "path leading everywhere," and the tenth, "exhaustion of contaminants") as the treatise being explicit about genuine interpretive uncertainty in how narrowly or broadly each power's object should be understood — narrower readings exclude cessation-knowledge or several other knowledges, broader readings include all ten — recorded as open alternatives rather than resolved into one official count.

2. The Buddha's Bodily Strength: Nārāyaṇa and the Sevenfold Scale

Before returning to the remaining seventeen unshared dharmas, the treatise pauses on a striking physical claim bound up with the ten powers: the sheer bodily strength of a Buddha, measured against a traditional graded scale of legendary strong beings.

Chinese Source

云何身力？頌曰： 身那羅延力， 或節節皆然， 象等七十增， 此觸處為性。 論曰：佛生身力等那羅延。有餘師言：佛身支節一一皆具那羅延力。大德法救說：諸如來身力無邊，猶如心力。若異此者，則諸佛身應不能持無邊心力。大覺獨覺及轉輪王支節相連，如其次第，似龍蟠結連鎖相鈎，故三相望力有勝劣。那羅延力其量云何？十十倍增象等七力，謂凡象、香象、摩訶諾健那、鉢羅塞建提、伐浪伽、遮怒羅、那羅延，後後力增前前十倍。有說：前六十十倍增，敵那羅延半身之力，此力一倍成那羅延。於所說中唯多應理。如是身力觸處為性，謂所觸中大種差別。有說：是造觸離七外別有。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the Buddha's bodily strength? Verse: his body has the strength of Nārāyaṇa; or, some say, each and every joint possesses this strength in full — reckoned through elephant-strength and the rest, increasing sevenfold; this strength has the tactile sense-field as its substance. Explanation: the Buddha's natural bodily strength is equal to that of Nārāyaṇa. Other teachers say: each and every joint of the Buddha's body individually possesses the full strength of Nārāyaṇa. The Venerable Dharmatrāta says: the buddhas' bodily strength is boundless, just like their mental power — were this not so, the buddhas' bodies would be unable to sustain their own boundless mental power. A fully awakened Buddha's, a pratyekabuddha's, and a wheel-turning king's joints are connected, respectively, in the manner of a coiled dragon, interlocking chain-links, and merely hooked together — hence these three, compared against one another, differ in strength, from superior to inferior. What is the measure of Nārāyaṇa's own strength? Reckoned across seven grades, each increasing tenfold: ordinary elephant, musk elephant, Mahānāgna, Praseṇajit, Baladeva, Cāṇūra, and finally Nārāyaṇa — each later grade's strength exceeds the one before it tenfold. Some say instead: the first six increase tenfold each time up to a level equal to half of Nārāyaṇa's own strength, and doubling this half then yields the full strength of Nārāyaṇa. Of these explanations, the plain tenfold account accords best with reason. This bodily strength has the tactile sense-field as its substance — namely, a distinct configuration of the great elements within what is touched. Some say it is instead a derived tactile quality, existing separately, apart from the seven primary tactile qualities already catalogued.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the sevenfold strength-scale's proper names as transliterated terms for legendary or mythic strong beings drawn from the broader Indian tradition — Nārāyaṇa (a name closely associated with Viṣṇu) at the top, Cāṇūra (a wrestler from the Kṛṣṇa legend, defeated by the young Kṛṣṇa) among the middle grades — though we cannot fully verify the precise Sanskrit reconstruction of every intermediate term (摩訶諾健那, 鉢羅塞建提, 伐浪伽) from this passage alone, and have transliterated them in their most commonly attested scholarly renderings rather than presenting false certainty about each one's exact identity.

We should also flag the three-way joint comparison — dragon-coil, chain-link, mere hook — as a vivid, purely qualitative image for why a Buddha's physical strength categorically exceeds even a pratyekabuddha's or a wheel-turning king's: it is not simply "more" of the same kind of joint-connection, but a structurally different, more deeply interlocking kind of connection altogether. We have preserved the unresolved disagreement over whether the entire body merely equals Nārāyaṇa's strength or whether every single joint individually possesses that same magnitude, and the further disagreement (Dharmatrāta's boundless-strength view against the finite sevenfold-scale view) exactly as the text records them, without picking a winner it does not itself declare.

3. The Four Fearlessnesses, and the Three Foundations of Mindfulness

The treatise now maps the four fearlessnesses onto four of the ten powers already explained, before turning to the three foundations of mindfulness — a Buddha's equanimity toward disciples who honor his teaching, ignore it, or split between the two.

Chinese Source

佛四無畏相別云何？頌曰： 四無畏如次， 初十二七力。 論曰：佛四無畏，如經廣說：一正等覺無畏，十智為性，猶如初力。二漏永盡無畏，六十智性，如第十力。三說障法無畏，八智為性，如第二力。四說出道無畏，九十智性，如第七力。如何於智立無畏名？此無畏名目無怯懼，由有智故不怯懼他，故無畏名目諸智體。理實無畏是智所成，不應說言體即是智。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, specifically, distinguishes the Buddha's four fearlessnesses? Verse: the four fearlessnesses correspond, respectively, to the first, tenth, second, and seventh powers. Explanation: the Buddha's four fearlessnesses, as a sūtra explains at length: first, the fearlessness of perfect awakening, constituted by all ten knowledges, just like the first power. Second, the fearlessness of the permanent exhaustion of contaminants, constituted by six or ten knowledges, like the tenth power. Third, the fearlessness of teaching the obstructing dharmas, constituted by eight knowledges, like the second power. Fourth, the fearlessness of teaching the path of deliverance, constituted by nine or ten knowledges, like the seventh power. How can the name fearlessness be established with reference to knowledge? This name designates the absence of timidity — because one possesses this knowledge, one is not intimidated by others, hence the name fearlessness designates the substance of these knowledges. In strict reality, however, fearlessness is merely accomplished by knowledge as its result; one should not say its substance is simply identical to knowledge itself.

Chinese Source

佛三念住相別云何？頌曰： 三念住念慧， 緣順違俱境。 論曰：佛三念住，如經廣說。諸弟子眾一向恭敬能正受行，如來緣之不生歡喜，捨而安住正念正知，是謂如來第一念住。諸弟子眾唯不恭敬不正受行，如來緣之不生憂慼，捨而安住正念正知，是謂如來第二念住。諸弟子眾一類恭敬能正受行，一類不敬不正受行，如來緣之不生歡慼，捨而安住正念正知，是謂如來第三念住。此三皆用念慧為體。諸大聲聞亦於弟子順違俱境離歡慼俱，此何名為不共佛法？唯佛於此并習斷故。或諸弟子隨屬如來有順違俱應甚歡慼，佛能不起可謂希奇。非屬諸聲聞不起非奇特，故唯在佛得不共名。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, specifically, distinguishes the Buddha's three foundations of mindfulness? Verse: the three foundations of mindfulness have mindfulness and wisdom as their substance, taking as object disciples who are agreeable, disagreeable, or both. Explanation: the Buddha's three foundations of mindfulness, as a sūtra explains at length. When the assembly of disciples is uniformly respectful and correctly receives and practices the teaching, the Tathāgata, taking this as object, does not give rise to joy, but remains equanimous, abiding in right mindfulness and right knowledge — this is the Tathāgata's first foundation of mindfulness. When the assembly of disciples is uniformly disrespectful and does not correctly receive or practice the teaching, the Tathāgata, taking this as object, does not give rise to sorrow, but remains equanimous, abiding in right mindfulness and right knowledge — this is the Tathāgata's second foundation of mindfulness. When, among the assembly of disciples, one portion is respectful and correctly receives and practices the teaching while another portion is disrespectful and does not, the Tathāgata, taking this as object, gives rise to neither joy nor sorrow, but remains equanimous, abiding in right mindfulness and right knowledge — this is the Tathāgata's third foundation of mindfulness. All three have mindfulness and wisdom as their substance. Great śrāvakas, too, remain free of both joy and sorrow with respect to disciples who are agreeable, disagreeable, or both — so why is this called an unshared Buddha-dharma? Because only the Buddha has, with respect to this, severed even the habitual residue of such reactions, not merely the reactions themselves. Alternatively: since the disciples in question belong specifically to the Tathāgata as their own teacher, their agreeable, disagreeable, or mixed conduct ought to provoke intense joy or sorrow, given the personal stake involved — yet the Buddha is able not to give rise to either, which may indeed be called remarkable. Since these same disciples do not belong directly to the other śrāvakas as their own personal students, those śrāvakas' not giving rise to such reactions is unremarkable by comparison — hence only in the Buddha's case does this receive the name unshared.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the treatise's careful hedge on naming "fearlessness" after knowledge: strictly speaking, fearlessness is a psychological effect that knowledge produces, not identical with the knowledge itself, even though ordinary usage freely calls the resulting confidence by the name of its cause. This same care — distinguishing a named quality's loose popular description from its stricter underlying substance — has recurred throughout this fascicle's account of the ten knowledges and will likely recur again through the rest of these eighteen unshared dharmas.

We should also flag the two competing justifications offered for why the three foundations of mindfulness count as unshared, given that experienced śrāvakas can seemingly manage the same emotional equanimity: the first locates the difference in depth (the Buddha has eliminated even the habitual residue of reactive tendencies, not merely their active occurrence, echoing the same distinction already used to explain the ten powers' exclusivity), while the second locates it in degree of temptation (the Buddha's equanimity is remarkable specifically because the disciples in question are his own, where the emotional stakes would naturally run highest — unlike a śrāvaka's composure toward disciples who are not personally his). We have preserved both explanations as the text offers them, without treating either as subordinate to the other.

4. Great Compassion, and the Eighteen Unshared Dharmas Complete

The treatise closes out the eighteen unshared dharmas with the last of the four categories: great compassion — explaining why it earns the name "great," and precisely how it differs from the ordinary compassion that other noble ones, including advanced śrāvakas, are also capable of.

Chinese Source

諸佛大悲云何相別？頌曰： 大悲唯俗智， 資糧行相境， 平等上品故， 異悲由八因。 論曰：如來大悲俗智為性，若異此者則不能緣一切有情，亦不能作三苦行相，如共有悲。此大悲名依何義立？依五義故此立大名：一由資糧故大，謂大福德智慧資糧所成辦故。二由行相故大，謂此力能於三苦境作行相故。三由所緣故大，謂此總以三界有情為所緣故。四由平等故大，謂此等於一切有情作利樂故。五由上品故大，謂最上品更無餘悲能齊此故。此與悲異由八種因：一由自性，無癡無瞋自性異故。二由行相，三苦一苦行相異故。三由所緣，三界一界所緣異故。四由依地，第四靜慮通餘異故。五由依身，唯佛通餘身有異故。六由證得，離有頂欲證得異故。七由救濟，事成希望救濟異故。八由哀愍，平等不等哀愍異故。已辯佛德異餘有情。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, specifically, distinguishes the buddhas' great compassion? Verse: great compassion has worldly knowledge alone as its substance; it is called great by provisions, aspect, object, equality, and superior grade; it differs from ordinary compassion by eight causes. Explanation: the Tathāgata's great compassion has worldly knowledge as its substance — were this not so, it would be unable to take all sentient beings as its object, nor produce its characteristic aspect with respect to the three kinds of suffering, the way ordinary shared compassion does. On what basis is the name great established for this compassion? On the basis of five meanings: first, by provisions, it is great, since it is accomplished through great provisions of merit and wisdom. Second, by aspect, it is great, since its power is able to produce its aspect with respect to the object of all three kinds of suffering. Third, by object, it is great, since it takes, in total, sentient beings of all three realms as its object. Fourth, by equality, it is great, since it equally brings benefit and happiness to all sentient beings without exception. Fifth, by superior grade, it is great, since it is of the most superior grade, and no other compassion can equal it. It differs from ordinary compassion by eight causes: first, by intrinsic nature — non-delusion versus non-hatred, these natures differing. Second, by aspect — the three kinds of suffering versus a single kind, these aspects differing. Third, by object — all three realms versus a single realm, these objects differing. Fourth, by dependence on ground — the fourth dhyāna exclusively versus extending through others, these differing. Fifth, by dependence on body — the Buddha exclusively versus extending through other bodies, these differing. Sixth, by attainment — attained upon becoming free of desire for the peak of existence versus otherwise, these differing. Seventh, by rescue — actually accomplishing rescue versus merely hoping to rescue, these differing. Eighth, by pity — equally extended versus unequally extended, these differing. The Buddha's virtues, as distinct from other sentient beings, have now been explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the eight-fold comparison as the treatise's most systematic attempt yet at pinning down exactly what separates a Buddha's qualities from a superficially similar quality any advanced practitioner might possess: ordinary compassion is rooted in non-hatred and can arise from many grounds and bodies, taking only one realm's beings and one kind of suffering as its object, extending unequal pity, and merely hoping to help — while great compassion is rooted in wisdom itself, depends exclusively on the fourth dhyāna and a Buddha's body alone, spans all three realms and all three kinds of suffering, extends perfectly equal pity to every being, and actually succeeds in rescuing rather than only hoping to. Taken together, this closes out the full eighteen unshared dharmas — the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the three foundations of mindfulness, and great compassion — each one now defined not just positively but by its precise point of departure from whatever comparable quality other noble beings can achieve.

We should also note the recurring pattern across all eighteen: nearly every one of these dharmas has a real, if lesser, counterpart available to ordinary noble ones (the ten powers echo ordinary knowledge; the fearlessnesses echo ordinary confidence; the foundations of mindfulness echo ordinary equanimity; compassion itself is shared in name). What makes each "unshared" is never the mere presence of the quality but always some combination of completeness, universality, and the elimination of habitual residue that only a Buddha achieves — a structural theme worth carrying forward into whatever fascicle 27 takes up next.

5. Are All Buddhas Equal? And the Perfect Virtue of Cause

Having distinguished the Buddha's qualities from every other kind of being, the treatise now asks a different question: comparing buddhas against one another, across the countless world-systems and ages this cosmology allows for, are they all simply identical?

Chinese Source

已辯佛德異餘有情。諸佛相望法皆等不？頌曰： 由資糧法身， 利他佛相似， 壽種姓量等， 諸佛有差別。 論曰：由三事故諸佛皆等，一由資糧等圓滿故、二由法身等成辦故、三由利他等究竟故。由壽種姓身量等殊，諸佛相望容有差別。壽異謂佛壽有短長，種異謂佛生剎帝利、婆羅門種，姓異謂佛姓喬答摩、迦葉波等。量異謂佛身有小大。等言顯諸佛法住久近等。如是有異，由出世時所化有情機宜別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha's virtues, as distinct from other sentient beings, have now been explained. Comparing the buddhas against one another, are their qualities all equal? Verse: by provisions, dharma-body, and benefiting others, the buddhas resemble one another; by lifespan, lineage, clan, and bodily size, and so on, the buddhas have differences. Explanation: by three matters, the buddhas are all equal — first, their provisions are equally perfected; second, their dharma-bodies are equally accomplished; third, their benefiting of others is equally consummate. By lifespan, lineage, clan, and bodily size, and so on, differing, the buddhas, compared against one another, may have differences. Difference in lifespan: buddhas' lifespans may be shorter or longer. Difference in lineage: buddhas may be born into the kṣatriya or brāhmaṇa lineage. Difference in clan: buddhas' clan-names may be Gautama, Kāśyapa, and so on. Difference in size: buddhas' bodies may be smaller or larger. The phrase "and so on" indicates further differences, such as how long each Buddha's teaching endures in the world after their passing. Such differences exist because the sentient beings to be transformed, at the time each Buddha appears in the world, differ in their own capacities and receptiveness.

The treatise then turns to a devotional register, describing three kinds of "perfect virtue" that inspire the wise to deep love and reverence toward the Tathāgata — beginning with the perfect virtue of cause, the four qualities of the bodhisattva path itself that made buddhahood possible.

Chinese Source

諸有智者思惟如來三種圓德深生愛敬。其三者何？一因圓德、二果圓德、三恩圓德。初因圓德復有四種：一無餘修，福德智慧二種資糧修無遺故。二長時修，經三大劫阿僧企耶修無倦故。三無間修，精勤勇猛剎那剎那修無廢故。四尊重修，恭敬所學無所顧惜修無慢故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wise people, contemplating the Tathāgata's three kinds of perfect virtue, give rise to deep love and reverence. What are these three? First, the perfect virtue of cause; second, the perfect virtue of fruit; third, the perfect virtue of beneficence. The first, the perfect virtue of cause, is itself of four kinds. First, cultivation without remainder — cultivating the two provisions of merit and wisdom without leaving anything out. Second, cultivation over a long time — cultivating without weariness across three great incalculable kalpas. Third, uninterrupted cultivation — cultivating with vigorous, courageous diligence, moment by moment, without ever abandoning it. Fourth, cultivation with reverence — respecting what is learned and sparing no effort, cultivating without arrogance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "are all buddhas equal" question as resolving into a clean, memorable structure: perfectly equal in the three things that actually constitute buddhahood (accumulated provisions, the dharma-body, and the completeness of benefiting others), yet variable in every biographical particular (lifespan, birth lineage, clan name, physical stature, how long the teaching endures) — differences the treatise attributes not to any inequality among the buddhas themselves, but to the varying needs of whichever beings each Buddha happens to appear among. Gautama and Kāśyapa, named here as examples of differing clan-names, are two of the seven buddhas of antiquity already familiar from the broader tradition, with Śākyamuni himself belonging to the Gautama clan.

We should also flag the shift in register at "wise people... give rise to deep love and reverence" — a rare moment in this treatise where technical Abhidharma analysis gives way, if only briefly, to explicitly devotional language, introducing the three-part scheme of perfect virtue (cause, fruit, beneficence) that the coming sections will develop in progressively finer subdivisions. The first of these three, the perfect virtue of cause, names four qualities of the bodhisattva path's own cultivation — completeness, duration (the traditional three incalculable kalpas already introduced in fascicle 12), unbroken continuity, and freedom from arrogance despite the immensity of the undertaking.

6. The Perfect Virtue of Fruit: Knowledge, Severance, Majestic Power, and the Physical Body

The treatise turns to the second of the three perfect virtues — the fruit of the path just completed, itself unfolding into four further qualities, each of which further divides into four.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag "knowledge without a teacher" and "effortless knowledge" as pointing to a distinction already implicit throughout this fascicle's account of the ten powers and the eighteen unshared dharmas: unlike even the most advanced śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, who still depend on instruction and sustained effort to develop their knowledge, the Buddha's knowledge is self-arisen and requires no exertion to operate once attained — a claim that gives concrete content to why buddhahood, not arhatship, is treated throughout this treatise as a categorically higher attainment, not merely a more thorough version of the same thing.

We should also flag the physical marks referenced here (the major and minor marks, 相好) as pointing forward to the classical thirty-two major and eighty minor physical marks of a buddha, a traditional scheme this passage names without yet enumerating — together with the vivid image of bones harder than diamond and a radiance outshining a hundred thousand suns, these physical qualities are presented as themselves among the "fruits" of the path, on equal footing with knowledge and severance of affliction, rather than as separate from the Buddha's spiritual attainment.

7. The Perfect Virtue of Beneficence, and the Great Mountain of Treasure

The treatise completes the three perfect virtues with beneficence — what the Buddha's attainment actually does for others — then closes this whole devotional passage with one of the most striking images in the fascicle so far.

Chinese Source

後恩圓德亦有四種，謂令永解脫三惡趣生死，或能安置善趣三乘。總說如來圓德如是，若別分析則有無邊，唯佛世尊能知能說，要留命行經多大劫阿僧企耶說乃可盡。如是則顯佛世尊身具有無邊殊勝奇特因果恩德如大寶山。有諸愚夫自乏眾德，雖聞如是佛功德山及所說法不能信重。諸有智者聞說如斯生信重心徹於骨髓，彼由一念極信重心轉滅無邊不定惡業，攝受殊勝人天涅槃，故說如來出現於世，為諸智者無上福田，依之引生不空可愛殊勝速疾究竟果故。如薄伽梵自說頌言： 若於佛福田， 能殖少分善， 初獲勝善趣， 後必得涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Speaking of it in summary, the Tathāgata's perfect virtues are as described above; if analyzed individually, they are boundless — only the Buddha, the Blessed One, is able to know and expound them fully, and it would take remaining alive through many great incalculable kalpas of explanation before they could be exhausted. This shows that the Buddha, the Blessed One, embodies boundless, superior, marvelous virtues of cause, fruit, and beneficence, like a great mountain of treasure. Wise people, hearing this described, give rise to faith and reverence penetrating to the very marrow of their bones; through a single moment of the most intense faith and reverence, they thereby dissolve boundless indeterminate unwholesome karma, and gain a superior human or heavenly rebirth, culminating in nirvāṇa — hence it is said that the Tathāgata's appearance in the world is, for the wise, an unsurpassed field of merit, since relying on it gives rise to an unfailing, cherished, superior, swift, and ultimate fruit. Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that the source text names the perfect virtue of beneficence as "of four kinds" while explicitly enumerating what reads as only two or three (liberation from the three evil destinies; establishment in a good destiny; establishment in one of the three vehicles) — we have translated this exactly as compressed in the source rather than inventing a fourth category to complete the count, since the passage itself does not spell out a clean fourfold division here the way it did for the perfect virtues of cause and fruit.

We should also flag the "great mountain of treasure" passage as marking a clear tonal shift for this stretch of the fascicle — moving from technical enumeration into devotional reflection on why the Buddha's virtues matter practically, not just descriptively: faith and reverence toward these qualities are said to dissolve vast stores of unwholesome karma and secure an "unfailing" trajectory toward nirvāṇa, illustrated by a citation the treatise attributes directly to the Buddha's own words. We have kept this closing verse exactly as rendered, as the treatise's own chosen capstone to the entire three-part account of perfect virtue.

8. Qualities the Buddha Shares With Others: Non-Contention

Having finished the eighteen unshared dharmas, the treatise turns to a different category entirely: qualities the Buddha possesses that other noble ones — and, for some of them, even ordinary beings — can possess as well. The first three of this new list (non-contention, knowledge of aspiration, and the unimpeded understandings) are shared only with other noble ones; the treatise begins with non-contention, a strikingly other-directed quality.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag "non-contention" as a genuinely distinctive concept in this tradition's ethical psychology: it is not simply an arhat's own freedom from affliction, but an active, outward-facing precaution against becoming an occasion for someone else's affliction — recognizing that being visibly a "superior field of merit" could itself provoke jealousy, resentment, or craving in others, and preemptively defusing that risk before it arises. This inverts the usual direction of concern in this treatise's ethics, which almost always tracks a practitioner's own severance of their own affliction rather than their responsibility for affliction arising in someone else's mind.

We should also flag the explanation for why this power is restricted to the immovable lineage specifically (introduced among the six kinds of arhat in fascicle 25) and to real-basis desire-realm affliction specifically: the treatise reasons that a lesser lineage cannot even guarantee freedom from its own affliction, so it could hardly extend that guarantee to others, and that "groundless" afflictions (such as self-view, discussed at length in fascicle 25 as arising from no real basis at all) cannot be preempted this way since they arise from purely internal confusion rather than being triggered by anything about the arhat's own visible presence.

The treatise continues through the qualities shared only with other noble ones: knowledge of aspiration, which extends non-contention's structure to any object one wishes to know, and the four "unimpeded understandings" — dharma, meaning, language, and eloquence — which, once one is attained, always arrive together as a set.

We should flag the fixed grouping of the four unimpeded understandings as itself a notable claim: unlike most of the qualities catalogued throughout this fascicle, which admit of partial or graduated attainment, the treatise insists that attaining even one of the four automatically brings all four — dharma, meaning, language, and eloquence are treated as a single integrated capacity for expounding the teaching, not four separable skills one could acquire piecemeal. This fits the traditional association of the four unimpeded understandings with a teacher's readiness to expound the Dharma fully, rather than with private realization alone.

We should also flag the ground restrictions as tracking capacities already established elsewhere in this translation: dharma-understanding requires "names" and so stops at the four dhyānas (since the formless realm, as established in earlier fascicles, lacks the conceptual naming activity language-understanding needs), while language-understanding requires inquiry and investigation (vitarka/vicāra) and so stops even earlier, at the first dhyāna, where these factors are last present before the second dhyāna's deeper stillness dispenses with them.

10. "Boundary" Concentration: What Makes the Fourth Dhyāna the Edge

The treatise now explains the shared foundation underlying all six qualities just discussed — non-contention, knowledge of aspiration, and the four unimpeded understandings — all of which depend on a specially perfected form of concentration called "boundary" (邊際) concentration, before closing this stretch of the fascicle and turning to qualities shared even with ordinary beings.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the two distinct senses "boundary" carries here as genuinely different axes of perfection, not two names for the same thing: "universally accommodating" describes a practitioner's ability to move fluidly through every meditative ground in sequence, forward from the desire realm all the way to the peak of existence and back again, cycling repeatedly before settling in the fourth dhyāna; "increasing to the point of ultimacy" instead describes vertical refinement within the fourth dhyāna alone, progressing through lower, middle, and upper grades, each itself subdivided into three, until reaching the single highest sub-grade possible. A dhyāna is called "boundary" only once both kinds of mastery — horizontal range and vertical depth — are complete.

We should also flag the closing point about the Buddha's unique relationship to these six qualities as reinforcing a pattern already seen throughout this fascicle's account of the eighteen unshared dharmas: where every other noble one must deliberately cultivate each of these six through preparatory effort, one at a time, the Buddha receives all six simultaneously and automatically at the very instant of first becoming a Buddha — after which he can manifest any of them at will, with no further effort required at all. This same "instantaneous, complete, effortless" pattern was already used to distinguish unshared Buddha-qualities from their lesser analogues earlier in this fascicle, and reappears here even for qualities explicitly classified as shared with other noble ones.

Having finished the qualities shared only with other noble ones, the treatise turns to qualities shared even with ordinary beings, beginning with the six classical supernormal powers (通) — naming each, specifying which knowledges constitute it, and explaining why five of the six depend on the four dhyānas but never on the formless attainments.

Chinese Source

已辯前三唯共餘聖德，於亦共凡德且應辯通。頌曰： 通六謂神境， 天眼耳他心， 宿住漏盡通， 解脫道慧攝。 四俗他心五， 漏盡通如力， 五依四靜慮， 自下地為境。 論曰：通有六種，一神境智證通、二天眼智證通、三天耳智證通、四他心智證通、五宿住隨念智證通、六漏盡智證通。雖六通中第六唯聖，然其前五異生亦得，依總相說亦共異生。如是六通解脫道攝，慧為自性，如沙門果。解脫道言顯出障義。神境等四唯俗智攝。他心通五智攝，謂法、類、道、世俗、他心。漏盡通如力說，謂或六或十智。由此已顯漏盡智通依一切地、緣一切境。前之五通依四靜慮。何緣此五不依無色？初三別緣色為境故，修他心通色為門故，修宿住通漸次憶念分位差別方得成故，成時能緣處性等故，依無色地無如是能。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first three, shared only with other noble ones, have now been explained; as for the virtues also shared with ordinary beings, let us now discuss the supernormal powers. Verse: the six supernormal powers are supernormal locomotion, the divine eye, the divine ear, knowledge of others' minds, recollection of past lives, and the exhaustion of contaminants, all included within the liberation path, wisdom as their substance. Four are constituted by worldly knowledge; knowledge of others' minds by five; the exhaustion-of-contaminants power as already explained for the tenth power. Five depend on the four dhyānas, taking their own or lower grounds as their object. Explanation: the supernormal powers are of six kinds — the power of supernormal locomotion, the power of the divine eye, the power of the divine ear, the power of knowing others' minds, the power of recollecting past lives, and the power of the exhaustion of contaminants. Although, among these six, the sixth belongs exclusively to noble ones, the first five can also be attained by ordinary beings, hence, speaking in general terms, they are also shared with ordinary beings. These six supernormal powers are included within the liberation path, having wisdom as their intrinsic nature, just like the fruits of asceticism. The term liberation path indicates the sense of emerging from obstruction. Supernormal locomotion and the next three are included within worldly knowledge alone. The power of knowing others' minds is included within five knowledges: dharma, subsequent, path, worldly, and knowledge of others' minds itself. The power of the exhaustion of contaminants is as already explained for the tenth power, constituted by either six or ten knowledges. This already shows that the power of the knowledge of the exhaustion of contaminants depends on every ground and takes every object. The first five powers depend on the four dhyānas. Why do these five not depend on the formless attainments? Because the first three specifically take form as their object; because cultivating the power of knowing others' minds uses form as its gateway; and because cultivating the power of recollecting past lives is accomplished only by gradually recollecting the distinct successive stages of one's past existences, and, once accomplished, is able to take specific places and natures as its object — capacities the formless realm's grounds lack.

The treatise then gives concrete, step-by-step cultivation instructions for two of these five — reading almost like a meditation manual embedded within the technical exposition.

Chinese Source

諸有欲修他心通者，先審觀己身心二相前後變異展轉相隨，後復審觀他身心相，由此加行漸次得成。成已不觀自心諸色，於他心等能如實知。諸有欲修宿住通者，先自審察次前滅心，漸復逆觀此生分位前前差別至結生心，乃至能憶知中有前一念，名自宿住加行已成。為憶念他加行亦爾。此通初起唯次第知，串習成時亦能超憶。諸所憶事要曾領受，憶淨居者昔曾聞故。從無色歿來生此者，依他相續初起此通，所餘亦依自相續起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whoever wishes to cultivate the power of knowing others' minds must first carefully observe the successive changes in their own body and mind, in their mutual correspondence, and afterward carefully observe another's bodily and mental characteristics; through this preparatory effort, the power is gradually accomplished. Once accomplished, one no longer needs to observe the accompanying physical form of one's own mind, and is able to directly and truly know another's mind. Whoever wishes to cultivate the power of recollecting past lives must first carefully examine the mind-moment immediately preceding the present one, then gradually observe backward through the successive, distinct stages of this present life, back to the rebirth-linking mind-moment, and further, until able to recollect the single mind-moment immediately preceding that, in the intermediate-existence state — this is called one's own preparatory effort for recollecting past lives having been accomplished. The preparatory effort for recollecting others' past lives is likewise. When this power first arises, one can only know past lives in strict sequence; once habituated and accomplished, one is also able to recollect them out of sequence. Whatever is recollected must have been personally experienced before — except that one may recollect beings in the Pure Abodes because one has previously heard about them, even without having personally experienced rebirth there. One who has died from the formless realm and been reborn here, when first giving rise to this power, must rely on another's mental continuum to access it; in every other case, it arises in dependence on one's own continuum.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the cultivation instructions for knowing others' minds and recollecting past lives as unusually concrete and practice-oriented compared to most of this fascicle's abstract classification — a rare glimpse of technique rather than taxonomy. Both methods share a structural logic: start from what is closest and most directly knowable (one's own body-mind correspondence; the mind-moment just before the present one), then extend outward or backward step by step (to another's body-mind; through successive past-life stages) — a graduated, self-to-other or present-to-past scaffolding rather than a leap directly to the target.

We should also flag the striking exception for beings in the Pure Abodes (the five highest form-realm heavens reserved for non-returners, introduced in earlier fascicles' cosmology): the general rule that one can only recollect what one has personally experienced is set aside here, since a practitioner can recollect such beings' past-life history on the strength of having heard about them, even without having ever been reborn there — and the equally striking case of someone reborn from the formless realm, who, having spent their prior existence in a state with no memory-continuity of bodily past lives, can only first access this power by relying on someone else's mental continuum rather than their own.

12. How Far the Powers Reach, How They Are Attained, and Why the Divine Eye Is Morally Neutral

The treatise continues its account of the six supernormal powers with three further questions: across how many world-systems can a given practitioner actually exercise them, how are they attained in the first place, and — a genuine puzzle — why are the divine eye and divine ear classed as morally neutral when the other four powers are wholesome?

Chinese Source

修神境等前三通時，思輕光聲以為加行，成已自在隨所應為。故此五通不依無色。又諸無色觀減止增，五通必依止觀均地，未至等地由此已遮，如是五通境唯自下。且如神境隨依何地，於自下地行化自在，於上不然，勢力劣故。餘四亦爾，隨其所應。是故無能取無色界他心、宿住為二通境。即此五通於世界境作用廣陜諸聖不同，謂大聲聞、麟喻、大覺不極作意，如次能於一二三千諸世界境，起行化等自在作用。若極作意，如次能於二千、三千、無數世界。如是五通若有殊勝勢用猛利，從無始來曾未得者由加行得，若曾串習無勝勢用及彼種類由離染得，若起現前皆由加行。佛於一切皆離染得，隨欲現前不由加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

When cultivating supernormal locomotion and the first group of three powers, one takes lightness, light, and sound as the objects of preparatory contemplation; once accomplished, one acts freely and at will as appropriate. This is why these five powers do not depend on the formless attainments. Moreover, in the formless attainments, insight is diminished and calm is excessive, while the five powers necessarily depend on a ground where calm and insight are balanced — this already rules out the not-yet-attained stage and the like as a basis. Hence these five powers' objects are limited to their own ground or lower. For example, supernormal locomotion, depending on whatever ground, grants freedom of movement and transformation with respect to its own ground and lower, but not with respect to higher grounds, since its power is inferior there. The remaining four are likewise, as appropriate — hence there is no capacity to take the formless realm as the object for the powers of knowing others' minds or recollecting past lives. As for these five powers' scope of function across world-systems, different noble ones vary: great śrāvakas, the rhinoceros-horn-like pratyekabuddha, and the greatly awakened Buddha, without exerting their utmost effort, are able, respectively, to exercise free power of movement and transformation across one, two, and three thousand world-systems; exerting their utmost effort, they reach, respectively, two thousand, three thousand, and countless world-systems. As for how these five powers are attained: if they possess superior, vigorous functional power and have never been attained since beginningless time, they are attained through preparatory effort; if previously habituated, that same kind of power is regained through becoming free of defilement instead — but actually bringing any of them into present manifestation always requires preparatory effort. The Buddha, with respect to all of these, attains them entirely through becoming free of defilement, and can bring any of them into present manifestation at will, without needing preparatory effort.

The treatise closes with the powers' classification by foundation of mindfulness and moral nature — resolving an apparent tension over why the divine eye and ear alone are morally neutral.

Chinese Source

六中前三唯身念住，但緣色故，謂神境通緣四外處色香味觸，天眼緣色，天耳緣聲。若爾，何緣說死生智知有情類由現身中成身語意諸惡行等？非天眼通能知此事，有別勝智是通眷屬，依聖身起能如是知。是天眼通力所引故，與通合立死生智名。他心智通三念住攝，謂受、心、法，緣心等故。宿住漏盡四念住攝。通緣五蘊一切境故。此六通中天眼、天耳無記性攝，許此二體是眼耳識相應慧故。若爾，寧說依四靜慮，隨根說故亦無有失。謂所依止眼耳二根，由四靜慮力所引起，即彼地攝，故依四地，通依根故說依四言。或此依通無間道說，通無間道依四地故。餘之四通性皆是善。若爾，何故《品類足》言：通云何？謂善慧。彼據多分，或就勝說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the six, the first three belong only to the body-foundation of mindfulness, since they take only form as their object: supernormal locomotion takes the four external sense-fields of form, smell, taste, and touch; the divine eye takes form; the divine ear takes sound. If this is so, why does it say that the knowledge of death and rebirth knows that sentient beings, in their present bodies, commit various evil bodily, verbal, and mental acts? It is not the divine eye power itself that knows this — rather, there is a separate, superior knowledge accompanying this power, arising in dependence on a noble one's body, which is able to know in this way; because it is drawn forth by the power of the divine eye, it is given the joint name knowledge of death and rebirth together with that power. The power of knowing others' minds is included within three foundations of mindfulness — feeling, mind, and dharma — since it takes mind and mental factors as its object. Recollection of past lives and exhaustion of contaminants are included within all four foundations of mindfulness, since they take the five aggregates, every object, as their scope. Among these six powers, the divine eye and divine ear are included within the morally neutral nature, since these two are granted to have, as their substance, wisdom associated with eye-consciousness and ear-consciousness respectively. If this is so, how can it be said that they depend on the four dhyānas? Since this is stated with reference to their supporting faculty, there is no fault: the eye and ear faculties depended upon are themselves drawn forth by the power of the four dhyānas, and are thus included within that same ground — hence, since the power depends on the faculty, it is said to depend on the four grounds. Alternatively, this is stated with reference to the power's own uninterrupted path, since that uninterrupted path does depend on the four grounds. The remaining four powers are all wholesome in nature. If this is so, why does the Prakaraṇapāda state that a supernormal power is wholesome wisdom? That statement is based on the majority case, or speaks only with reference to the superior instances.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the world-system reach figures as a vivid illustration of the graduated hierarchy running throughout this fascicle: a great śrāvaka's ordinary effort reaches one thousand world-systems, a pratyekabuddha's two thousand, and a Buddha's three thousand — with maximal effort roughly doubling each figure — giving concrete numerical shape to the same superiority already established more abstractly for knowledge of others' minds in fascicle 26 (where śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and the Buddha differed sharply in how much of a stranger's path of seeing they could track).

We should also flag the divine eye/ear puzzle as a genuinely careful piece of reasoning: since these two powers' substance is wisdom associated with eye- and ear-consciousness, and sense-consciousness itself is morally neutral, the powers themselves must be neutral too — yet the text needs to explain how something "neutral" can still be said to "depend on the four dhyānas," since dhyāna states are normally associated with refined mental cultivation, not raw sense-consciousness. The resolution locates the dependency one step removed: the four dhyānas empower the underlying eye and ear faculties themselves, not the neutral consciousness that uses them, so the power counts as "depending on the four dhyānas" through its faculty rather than through its own moral nature. The closing citation-reconciliation (the root Abhidharma text's generalization "wholesome wisdom" applying only to the majority, four of six, rather than literally all six) is a small but telling example of this treatise treating even its own school's canonical texts as loosely stated summaries rather than exhaustive, literal claims.

13. The Three Insights: Counteracting Confusion About Past, Future, and Present

A sūtra speaks of "the three insights of the non-trainee" — a more honorific designation than "supernormal power" — and the treatise identifies exactly which three of the six powers earn this special name, and why.

Chinese Source

如契經說：無學三明。彼於六通以何為性？頌曰： 第五二六明， 治三際愚故， 後真二假說， 學有闇非明。 論曰：言三明者，一宿住智證明、二死生智證明、三漏盡智證明。如其次第，以無學位攝第五二六通為其自性。六中三種獨名明者，如次對治三際愚故。謂宿住智通治前際愚，死生智通治後際愚，漏盡智通治中際愚。此三皆名無學明者，俱在無學身中起故。於中最後容有是真，通無漏故。餘二假說，體唯非學非無學故。有學身中有愚闇故，雖有前二不立為明。雖有暫時伏滅愚闇，後還被蔽故不名明。

Ru-Yi Translation

As a sūtra states: the three insights of the non-trainee. Which of the six powers constitute these, in substance? Verse: the fifth, second, and sixth powers are the three insights, since they counteract confusion regarding the three time-periods; the last is genuinely so, since it can be uncontaminated; the other two are said to be insights only nominally; a trainee has only confusion, not insight. Explanation: the three insights are: the insight of the knowledge of recollecting past lives; the insight of the knowledge of death and rebirth; the insight of the knowledge of the exhaustion of contaminants. These, respectively, have as their substance, at the non-trainee stage, the fifth, second, and sixth of the six powers. Among the six, these three alone are given the special name insight, because, respectively, they counteract confusion regarding the three time-periods: the power of recollecting past lives counteracts confusion regarding the prior period, the power of the knowledge of death and rebirth counteracts confusion regarding the later period, and the power of the exhaustion of contaminants counteracts confusion regarding the middle period. All three are called the non-trainee's insights because they all arise within a non-trainee's person. Among them, the last may genuinely be called insight in the fullest sense, since it can be uncontaminated. The other two are spoken of only nominally, since their substance is neither strictly trainee nor non-trainee. Since a trainee's person still contains confusion and darkness, even when the first two are present in a trainee, they are not established as insight. Although a trainee may temporarily suppress and extinguish confusion and darkness, since it later returns and obscures again, this state is not called insight.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the mapping of "insight" onto the three time-periods as a tidy structural completion: recollecting past lives dispels confusion about the past, knowledge of death and rebirth (itself built on the divine eye, as established in the previous section) dispels confusion about the future, and the exhaustion of contaminants dispels confusion about the present — together covering the full span of time a confused mind could be caught in. Only the third qualifies as genuinely, fully "insight," since only it is necessarily uncontaminated; the first two remain contaminated, worldly-knowledge-based powers even at their best, and so are called insight only loosely, by association with the third.

We should also flag the honorific's exact restriction to non-trainees as pointed rather than incidental: a trainee can certainly possess recollection of past lives or knowledge of death and rebirth, and can even temporarily suppress confusion through them — but because a trainee's own confusion always eventually reasserts itself, the same powers, in a trainee's hands, are never granted the name "insight." The name tracks not the power's raw capability but the durability of the freedom from confusion behind it.

14. The Three Guiding Demonstrations, and Why Instruction Outranks the Other Two

The treatise identifies a further sūtra category — three "guiding demonstrations" used to draw beings toward the teaching — and argues that only one of the three, instruction, is genuinely reliable, since the other two can be counterfeited by ordinary magic.

Chinese Source

契經說有三種示導，彼於六通以何為體？頌曰： 第一四六導， 教誡導為尊， 定由通所成， 引利樂果故。 論曰：三示導者，一神變示導、二記心示導、三教誡示導。如其次第，以六通中第一四六為其自性，唯此三種引所化生令初發心最為勝故，或此能引憎背正法及處中者令發心故。能示能導得示導名。又唯此三令於佛法如次歸伏、信受、修行，得示導名，餘三不爾。於二示導，教誡最尊，唯此定由通所成故，定能引他利樂果故。謂前二導呪術亦能，不但由通故非決定。如有呪術名健馱梨，持此便能騰空自在。復有呪術名伊剎尼，持此便能知他心念。教誡示導除漏盡通，餘不能為，故是決定。又前二導有但令他暫時迴心，非引勝果。教誡示導亦定令他引當利益及安樂果，以能如實方便說故。由是教誡最勝非餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

A sūtra states there are three kinds of guiding demonstration. Which of the six powers constitute these, in substance? Verse: the first, fourth, and sixth powers constitute the guiding demonstrations; the demonstration of instruction is the most eminent, since it alone is necessarily accomplished through its corresponding power, and definitely brings about a beneficial, pleasurable result. Explanation: the three guiding demonstrations are: the demonstration of miraculous transformation, the demonstration of mind-reading, and the demonstration of instruction. Respectively, these have as their substance the first, fourth, and sixth of the six powers. Only these three kinds are most effective at drawing beings to be transformed into first generating aspiration — or, alternatively, these are able to draw even those hostile to or indifferent toward the true Dharma into generating such aspiration. Being able to demonstrate and able to guide, they receive the name guiding demonstration. Moreover, only these three, respectively, cause beings to submit to, place faith in, and practice the Buddha's teaching — hence they alone receive this name; the remaining three powers do not. Among these three demonstrations, instruction is the most eminent, since only this one is necessarily accomplished through its corresponding power, and definitely brings about a beneficial, pleasurable result for others. The first two demonstrations can also be accomplished through magical spells, not solely through genuine supernormal power, hence they are not reliable in the same way. For example, there is a spell called Gandhāri; mastering this, one is able to fly through the air freely. There is a further spell called Īkṣaṇikā; mastering this, one is able to know others' thoughts. The demonstration of instruction, apart from the power of the exhaustion of contaminants, cannot be accomplished by any other means, hence it alone is reliable. Moreover, the first two demonstrations can only cause others to turn their minds temporarily, without bringing about any superior result, whereas the demonstration of instruction definitely brings others future benefit and a result of peace and happiness, since it teaches through skillful means that accord with reality. For this reason, instruction is the most excellent, surpassing the other two.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the treatise's reasoning here as a genuinely pragmatic piece of apologetics: rather than simply asserting that instruction is superior, it makes a falsifiability-style argument — flying through the air or reading minds can both be counterfeited by ordinary spells (naming two specific ones, Gandhāri and Īkṣaṇikā, as if these were known practices of the time), so a display of either power alone cannot reliably prove genuine spiritual attainment. Only teaching that "accords with reality" and produces lasting benefit cannot be faked this way, which is why the treatise ranks it above displays of miraculous power or mind-reading, both of which might impress an audience only to leave them, at best, temporarily persuaded rather than genuinely transformed.

We should also flag the naming logic as consistent with a pattern already seen throughout this fascicle: a quality earns a special honorific name (here, "guiding demonstration," as with "insight" in the previous section) not simply by being powerful, but by reliably producing a specific, valuable downstream effect — in this case, causing beings to progress through submission, faith, and actual practice of the teaching, which the other three ordinary powers (divine eye, divine ear, recollection of past lives) do not accomplish on their own.

15. Inside Supernormal Locomotion: Three Kinds of Movement, Two Kinds of Transformation

The treatise now takes apart the compound name "supernormal locomotion" (神境通) itself, showing that its two halves name genuinely distinct things — a form of concentration, and the domain of feats that concentration makes possible, which in turn splits into movement and transformation.

Chinese Source

神境二言為目何義？頌曰： 神體謂等持， 境二謂行化， 行三意勢佛， 運身勝解通。 化二謂欲色， 四二外處性， 此各有二種， 謂似自他身。 論曰：依毘婆沙所說理趣，神名所目唯勝等持，由此能為神變事故。諸神變事說名為境，此有二種，謂行及化。行復三種：一者運身，謂乘空行猶如飛鳥。二者勝解，謂極遠方作近思惟便能速至。三者意勢，謂極遠方舉心緣時身即能至。此勢如意得意勢名。於此三中意勢唯佛，運身、勝解亦通餘乘。謂我世尊神通迅速隨方遠近舉心即至。由此世尊作如是說：諸佛境界不可思議。故意勢行唯世尊有，勝解兼餘聖，運身并異生。化復二種，謂欲、色界。若欲界化外四處，除聲。若色界化唯二，謂色、觸，以色界中無香、味故。此二界化各有二種，謂屬自身、他身別故。身在欲界化有四種，在色亦然，故總成八。若生在色作欲界化，如何不有成香味失，如衣嚴具作而不成？有說：在色唯化二處，化作、化事。

Ru-Yi Translation

What do the two terms "marvelous" and "realm" in supernormal locomotion each designate? Verse: "marvelous," in substance, refers to concentration; "realm" divides into movement and transformation; movement is of three kinds, mind-power belonging to the Buddha alone, bodily locomotion and resolute-understanding shared more widely; transformation is of two kinds, desire-realm and form-realm, covering four and two external sense-fields respectively; each of these further divides into two, resembling one's own body or another's. Explanation: according to the principle stated by the Vaibhāṣika masters, the name "marvelous" designates superior concentration alone, since, by means of this, one is able to perform marvelous feats. These marvelous feats are called "realm," and are of two kinds: movement and transformation. Movement is, moreover, of three kinds. First, bodily locomotion — riding through the air like a flying bird. Second, resolute understanding — treating an extremely distant place through the thought "it is near," one is thereby able to arrive swiftly. Third, mind-power — at an extremely distant place, the moment the mind takes it as object, the body is instantly able to arrive there; since this power operates just as the mind wills, it receives the name mind-power. Among these three, mind-power belongs to the Buddha alone; bodily locomotion and resolute understanding extend to the other vehicles as well. As it is said: our Blessed One's supernormal power is so swift that, near or far in any direction, the moment his mind takes it as object, he arrives there — on this basis, the Blessed One himself has stated: the domain of the buddhas is inconceivable. Hence mind-power movement belongs to the Blessed One alone; resolute-understanding movement extends to other noble ones as well; bodily locomotion extends even to ordinary beings. Transformation is, moreover, of two kinds: desire-realm and form-realm. Desire-realm transformation covers the four external sense-fields, excluding sound. Form-realm transformation covers only two — form and touch — since the form realm lacks smell and taste. Each of these two realms' transformation is, moreover, of two kinds, distinguished by belonging to one's own body or another's body. For a person whose own body is in the desire realm, transformation comes in four kinds; in the form realm, likewise — hence, in total, eight kinds. If one born in the form realm produces a desire-realm-type transformation, how is there no fault of failing to produce smell and taste, the way transformed clothing and ornaments might fail? Some say: for one in the form realm, transformation covers only two sense-fields — the transformative act and the transformed matter itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the three-tiered hierarchy of movement as another instance of this fascicle's recurring pattern of graduated attainment: bodily locomotion (flying like a bird) is available even to ordinary beings who cultivate it; resolute-understanding movement (treating distance as an act of will) extends to noble ones generally; but mind-power movement — arriving the very instant one's mind takes a destination as object, with no intervening travel at all — belongs to the Buddha alone, illustrated by the treatise's own citation of the Buddha's declaration that "the domain of the buddhas is inconceivable."

We should flag honestly that the precise arithmetic behind "eight kinds" of transformation is compressed in this passage — the text states that a desire-realm body's transformation comes in four kinds and a form-realm body's transformation likewise, totaling eight, without fully spelling out how the self/other distinction and the differing sense-field counts (four for desire-realm content, two for form-realm content) combine to produce this specific total. We have translated the passage exactly as stated rather than reconstructing a combinatorial derivation the text itself does not walk through.

We should also flag the puzzle about a form-realm being producing a desire-realm-style illusion (an object that ought to have smell and taste, which form-realm beings cannot actually produce) as the treatise catching a potential inconsistency in its own system and offering a resolution: such a being's transformation, even when depicting something desire-realm-like, is limited to only the two sense-fields their own realm actually supports — the illusion of smell or taste is never genuinely produced, only whatever the transforming being's own native capacities allow.

16. The Fourteen Transformation-Minds, and How a Transformation Enters and Exits Concentration

The treatise clarifies that transformation itself is not the supernormal power but its fruit — the specific mind capable of generating transformed matter — and works out exactly how many varieties of this transformation-mind exist, and the precise sequence by which a meditator's mind moves into and back out of producing one.

Chinese Source

為即是通不？不爾。云何？是通之果。此有幾種？差別云何？頌曰： 能化心十四， 定果二至五， 如所依定得， 從淨自生二。 化事由自地， 語通由自下， 化身與化主， 語必俱非佛。 論曰：神境通果能變化心，力能化生一切化事。此有十四，謂依根本四靜慮生有差別故。依初靜慮有二化心，一欲界攝、二初靜慮。第二靜慮有三化心，二種如前，加二靜慮。第三有四，第四有五，謂各自下，如理應思。諸果化心依自上地必無依下，下地定心不生上果，勢力劣故。第二定等果下地化心，對初定等果上地化心，由依及行亦得名勝。如得靜慮化心亦然，果與所依俱時得故。諸從靜慮起果化心，此心必無直出觀義，謂從淨定起初化心，此後後心從自類起，此前前念生自類心，最後化心還生淨定。故此從二能生二心，非定果心無記性攝。不還入定有直出義，如從門入還從門出，諸所化事由自地心，無異地化心起餘地化故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Is this transformation itself identical to the supernormal power? No. What then is it? It is the fruit of that power. How many kinds are there, and what distinguishes them? Verse: the transformation-minds number fourteen; the dhyāna-fruits range from two to five, as attained together with their supporting basis; from the pure state, one gives rise to two. Explanation: the fruit of the power of supernormal locomotion is the mind capable of transformation, whose power is able to create all manner of transformed matter. This mind is of fourteen kinds, since, depending on the four root dhyānas, it differs by which ground it arises from. Depending on the first dhyāna, there are two transformation-minds: one included in the desire realm, one in the first dhyāna itself. Depending on the second dhyāna, there are three: the two as before, plus one for the second dhyāna. Depending on the third, there are four; depending on the fourth, there are five — each covering its own ground and every ground below, and this pattern should be reasoned through accordingly. These fruit-transformation-minds depend only on their own ground or a lower one already within reach, never the reverse — a lower ground's concentration-mind cannot produce a higher fruit, since its power is inferior. The second dhyāna's fruit, a lower-ground transformation-mind, compared against the first dhyāna's fruit, a transformation-mind at its own ground — by virtue of both its supporting basis and its own activity, the former can still be called superior. The same holds for attaining a dhyāna's transformation-mind generally: the fruit and its supporting basis are attained simultaneously. Whenever a transformation-fruit-mind arises from a dhyāna, this mind can never emerge directly from meditative absorption without an intervening step: from the actual, pure concentration, the first transformation-mind arises; this is followed by further transformation-minds arising from their own kind in succession; and the very last transformation-mind, in turn, gives rise back to the pure concentration before one finally emerges. Hence from these two — pure concentration and transformation-mind — each gives rise only to these same two kinds, and the transformation-fruit-mind itself belongs to the morally neutral nature. An ordinary emergence from concentration, by contrast, does have the sense of direct emergence, like entering through a door and exiting back through that same door. Every transformed matter is produced by a mind of its own ground; there is no case of a transformation-mind belonging to one ground producing a transformation belonging to a different ground.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the fourteen-fold count as a clean triangular sum once unpacked: each of the four root dhyānas supports a transformation-mind reaching its own ground and every ground below it — two for the first dhyāna (itself plus the desire realm), three for the second, four for the third, five for the fourth — totaling 2+3+4+5=14. The underlying rule is strict: a transformation-mind's own supporting basis must be at least as refined as whatever ground the transformation itself belongs to, so a lower concentration can never manufacture a higher-ground illusion, though a higher concentration can freely produce content at its own level or anything simpler.

We should also flag the "door" simile as capturing a genuinely careful point about mental sequencing: an ordinary meditator exits absorption the same way they entered it, directly, but a meditator who produces a transformation cannot do this — the transformation-mind itself must first arise out of the actual concentration, then (if sustaining or elaborating the illusion) give rise only to further transformation-minds of its own kind, and only the very last such mind is able to return to the base concentration before ordinary exit resumes. This threading of transformation-minds as their own distinct causal chain, inserted between entering and exiting concentration proper, is what the treatise means by transformation never permitting "direct emergence."
