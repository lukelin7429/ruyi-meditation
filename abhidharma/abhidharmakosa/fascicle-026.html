The Ten Kinds of Knowledge

Fascicle 26 opens Chapter 7 (分別智品第七之一, Distinguishing Knowledge), the treatise's systematic account of knowledge (智) itself — how it relates to the forbearance and view already discussed throughout Chapter 6, and the tenfold classification (worldly, dharma, subsequent, the four truth-specific knowledges, knowledge of others' minds, and the two knowledges completing arhatship) that will structure the rest of this chapter.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 15 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 26 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. Forbearance, Knowledge, and View; the Ten Kinds of Knowledge

Chapter 6 spoke often of "forbearance" (忍) and "knowledge" (智), and later of "right view" and "right knowledge," without ever pausing to sort out how these three — forbearance, knowledge, and view — actually relate to one another. Chapter 7 opens by settling this directly: is there forbearance that fails to count as knowledge? Is there knowledge that fails to count as view?

Chinese Source

前品初說諸忍諸智，於後復說正見正智。為有忍非智耶？為有智非見耶？頌曰： 聖慧忍非智， 盡無生非見， 餘二有漏慧， 皆智六見性。 論曰：慧有二種，有漏、無漏，唯無漏慧立以聖名。此聖慧中八忍非智性，自所斷疑未已斷故。可見性攝，推度性故。盡與無生二智非見性，已息求心不推度故。所餘皆通智見二性，已斷自疑、推度性故。諸有漏慧皆智性攝，於中唯六亦是見性，謂五染污見、世正見為六。如是所說聖有漏慧，皆擇法故並慧性攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

The previous chapter, at its outset, spoke of the various forbearances and knowledges, and later on also spoke of right view and right knowledge. Is there forbearance that is not knowledge? Is there knowledge that is not view? Verse: among noble wisdom, forbearance is not of the nature of knowledge, and the knowledges of exhaustion and non-arising are not of the nature of view; the remaining two groups are entirely of the nature of knowledge, and six are of the nature of view. Explanation: wisdom is of two kinds, contaminated and uncontaminated; only uncontaminated wisdom is given the name "noble." Among this noble wisdom, the eight forbearances are not of the nature of knowledge, since the doubt regarding what they themselves are to sever has not yet been fully severed at that point; they are, however, included within the nature of view, since they are of an investigative nature. The two knowledges, exhaustion and non-arising, are not of the nature of view, since the mind's seeking has already ceased, and they no longer investigate. The remaining knowledges are all common to both the nature of knowledge and the nature of view, since they have already severed their own doubt and remain of an investigative nature. All contaminated wisdom is included within the nature of knowledge; among these, only six are also of the nature of view — namely, the five defiled views, together with mundane right view, making six. Thus both the noble wisdom and the contaminated wisdom just discussed, since both consist in the discernment of dharmas, are alike included within the nature of wisdom.

Having placed forbearance, knowledge, and view in relation to one another, the treatise turns to knowledge's own internal classification — the tenfold scheme that will organize everything Chapter 7 has to say.

Chinese Source

智有幾種？相別云何？頌曰： 智十總有二， 有漏無漏別， 有漏稱世俗， 無漏名法類。 世俗遍為境， 法智及類智， 如次欲上界， 苦等諦為境。 論曰：智有十種攝一切智：一世俗智、二法智、三類智、四苦智、五集智、六滅智、七道智、八他心智、九盡智、十無生智。如是十智總唯二種，有漏、無漏性差別故。如是二智相別有三，謂世俗智、法智、類智。前有漏智總名世俗，多取瓶等世俗境故。後無漏智分法、類別。三中世俗遍，以一切有為無為為所緣境。法類二種如其次第，以欲上界四諦為境。

Ru-Yi Translation

How many kinds of knowledge are there, and what distinguishes their individual character? Verse: knowledge, ten in total, reduces to two by nature — contaminated and uncontaminated differ; the contaminated is called worldly; the uncontaminated is named dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge. Worldly knowledge takes everything universally as its object; dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge, respectively, take the desire realm's and the upper realms' four truths as their object. Explanation: knowledge is of ten kinds, encompassing every kind of knowledge: worldly knowledge, dharma knowledge, subsequent knowledge, knowledge of suffering, knowledge of origination, knowledge of cessation, knowledge of the path, knowledge of others' minds, knowledge of exhaustion, and knowledge of non-arising. These ten kinds, taken altogether, reduce to only two, since they differ in nature between contaminated and uncontaminated. These two, further distinguished in character, come to three: worldly knowledge, dharma knowledge, and subsequent knowledge. The former, contaminated knowledge, is entirely named worldly, since it mostly takes worldly objects such as pots and the like. The latter, uncontaminated knowledge, divides into the distinct categories of dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge. Among these three, worldly knowledge is universal, since it takes every conditioned and unconditioned thing as its object. Dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge, respectively, take the four truths of the desire realm and of the upper realms as their object.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the forbearance/knowledge/view analysis as a tidy piece of housekeeping that resolves a genuine ambiguity built up across the previous chapter's constant use of all three terms: forbearance turns out to be a kind of view but not yet knowledge (it still carries unresolved doubt about the very affliction it is severing, in the instant before confirmation), while the two capstone knowledges of arhatship, exhaustion and non-arising, are knowledge but not view (having ceased all further investigation, there is nothing left to "survey"). Everything else in the noble path's eight remaining knowledges counts as both. We have kept this three-way distinction exact, since the entire point of the passage is that these three terms — easy to treat as loosely interchangeable — pick out genuinely different, only partially overlapping categories.

We should also note that "dharma-knowledge" and "subsequent-knowledge" (法智／類智) here reintroduce, in ordinary prose, the same two knowledges already used as the standard building blocks for the sixteen-moment path of seeing described in fascicle 23 — this passage is best read as the treatise stepping back to formally define, chapter by chapter, categories it has already been relying on informally throughout the preceding material.

2. Dharma- and Subsequent-Knowledge Split Four Ways, and the First Arising of Exhaustion and Non-Arising

Having established dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge as the two uncontaminated kinds of knowledge, the treatise now shows how each further divides according to which of the four truths it takes as its object — and how this connects back to the two capstone knowledges, exhaustion and non-arising, that complete arhatship.

Chinese Source

即於如是二種智中，頌曰： 法類由境別， 立苦等四名， 皆通盡無生， 初唯苦集類。 論曰：法智、類智由境差別分為苦、集、滅、道四智。如是六智若無學攝，非見性者名盡、無生。此二初生唯苦、集類，以緣苦、集六種行相觀有頂蘊為境界故。金剛喻定境同此耶？緣苦、集因，緣滅、道異。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within these two kinds of knowledge, verse: dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge, according to differences in their object, are given the four names beginning with suffering; each is common to being named exhaustion or non-arising; but their first arising is exclusively of the suffering- and origination-subsequent-knowledge type. Explanation: dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge, according to differences in their object, divide into the four knowledges of suffering, origination, cessation, and path. Among these six knowledges, whenever they are included within a non-trainee's possession and are not of the nature of view, they are named exhaustion and non-arising instead. When these two — exhaustion-knowledge and non-arising-knowledge — first arise, they are exclusively of the suffering- and origination-subsequent-knowledge type, since they take as their object the peak-of-existence aggregates as contemplated through the six aspects of suffering and origination. Does the diamond-like concentration share this same object? With respect to suffering and origination, the cause is the same; with respect to cessation and path, it differs.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag how this passage quietly connects two things this translation has treated separately until now: the four truth-specific knowledges (suffering, origination, cessation, path) are not a new addition alongside dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge, but simply those same two knowledges sorted by which truth they take as object — and "exhaustion" and "non-arising," the two knowledges that mark the completion of arhatship (introduced in fascicle 24 alongside the diamond-like concentration), turn out to be the very same six knowledges again, renamed once they occur specifically within a non-trainee's mind and no longer carry the investigative character that would qualify them as "view." The fuller explanation of exactly what makes a knowledge "exhaustion" or "non-arising" specifically — the self-referential "I have already known suffering" judgment — is signaled here but developed later in this same chapter.

We should also flag the closing exchange about the diamond-like concentration's object as genuinely compressed, cross-referencing technical material on that concentration's structure developed at greater length in fascicle 24 rather than restating it here; we have translated the question and answer literally rather than reconstructing the fuller argument the passage assumes but does not repeat.

3. Knowledge of Others' Minds: What It Cannot Know, and Who Can Track a Stranger's Path of Seeing

The treatise turns next to knowledge of others' minds (他心智) — which of the nine other kinds of knowledge can actually constitute it, and, more strikingly, exactly what limits govern it: a mind reader in this scheme cannot know just anything, and the ability to track a complete stranger through the sixteen swift moments of the path of seeing turns out to depend sharply on who is doing the watching.

Chinese Source

於前所說九種智中，頌曰： 法類道世俗， 有成他心智， 於勝地根位， 去來世不知， 法類不相知， 聲聞麟喻佛， 如次知見道， 二三念一切。 論曰：有法類道及世俗智成他心智，餘則不然。此智於境有決定相，謂不知勝及去來心。勝心有三，謂地、根、位。地謂下地智不知上地心。根謂信解時解脫根智不知見至不時解脫心。位謂不還聲聞應果、獨覺大覺。前前位智不知後後勝位者心，此智不知去來心者唯以現在他相續中能緣心等為境界故。又法類品不互相知，謂法智攝諸他心智不知類品，類智所攝諸他心智不知法品，由法類智以欲上界全分對治為所緣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the nine other kinds of knowledge already discussed, verse: dharma-knowledge, subsequent-knowledge, path-knowledge, and worldly knowledge can constitute knowledge of others' minds; but minds superior in ground, faculty, or stage, and minds in the past or future, are not known by it. The dharma-type and subsequent-type do not know each other. Śrāvakas, the rhinoceros-horn-like pratyekabuddha, and the Buddha, respectively, know the path of seeing to the extent of two moments, three moments, and all of it. Explanation: dharma-knowledge, subsequent-knowledge, path-knowledge, and worldly knowledge can constitute knowledge of others' minds; the rest cannot. This knowledge has a fixed, determinate character with respect to its object — namely, it does not know minds that are superior to its own, nor minds in the past or future. "Superior mind" is of three kinds: by ground, by faculty, and by stage. By ground: knowledge belonging to a lower ground does not know the mind of a higher ground. By faculty: knowledge belonging to the convinced-through-faith or timely-liberated type does not know the mind of the attained-through-seeing or non-timely-liberated type. By stage: the ranking runs non-returner, arhat, pratyekabuddha, and the greatly-awakened Buddha — the knowledge of each earlier stage does not know the mind of one at a later, superior stage. This knowledge does not know past or future minds, since it takes as its object only the currently-arising, objectively-cognizing mind and mental factors within another's present mental continuum. Moreover, the dharma-type and subsequent-type do not know each other: the knowledge-of-others'-minds included within dharma-knowledge does not know a subsequent-type mind, and that included within subsequent-knowledge does not know a dharma-type mind, since dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge each take the complete counteragent for the desire realm or the upper realms, respectively, as their own restricted object.

The treatise then works out a striking practical case: since the path of seeing itself proceeds too swiftly for knowledge of others' minds to occur during it, how does a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha manage to observe a stranger actually passing through it?

Chinese Source

此他心智見道中無，總觀諦理極速轉故，然皆容作此智所緣。若諸有情將入見道，聲聞獨覺預修加行，為欲知彼見道位心。彼諸有情入見道位，聲聞法分加行若滿，知彼見道初二念心。若為更知類分心故，別修加行至加行滿，彼已度至第十六心，雖知此心非知見道。麟喻法分加行若滿，知彼見道初二念心。若為更知類分心故，別修加行至加行滿，知彼第八集類智心。以此但由下加行故。有說：知初二及第十五心，世尊欲知不由加行，於彼見道一切能知。

Ru-Yi Translation

This knowledge of others' minds does not occur during the path of seeing itself, since the path of seeing contemplates the principle of the truths as a whole, proceeding extremely swiftly; nevertheless, minds at the path of seeing can all serve as the object of this knowledge. When sentient beings are about to enter the path of seeing, śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas who wish to know their minds cultivate preparatory effort in advance. Once those beings enter the stage of the path of seeing: if a śrāvaka's preparatory effort for the dharma-portion is complete, they know the first two mind-moments of that person's path of seeing. If, wishing to further know the subsequent-portion mind as well, the śrāvaka separately cultivates preparatory effort until complete, by the time it is complete that person has already progressed all the way to the sixteenth mind-moment — and although the śrāvaka then knows this mind, this is no longer knowing a mind at the path of seeing, since the sixteenth moment already belongs to the path of cultivation. For a rhinoceros-horn-like pratyekabuddha: if their preparatory effort for the dharma-portion is complete, they likewise know the first two mind-moments. If, wishing to further know the subsequent-portion mind, they separately cultivate preparatory effort until complete, they come to know that person's eighth mind-moment, the subsequent-knowledge-of-origination moment — since even their separately cultivated effort remains only of a lesser grade. Some say instead that a pratyekabuddha knows the first two moments and the fifteenth. The Blessed One, wishing to know, needs no preparatory effort at all — with respect to that person's path of seeing, he is able to know every single moment directly.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the three-part restriction on knowledge of others' minds (never superior in ground, faculty, or stage; never past or future; never crossing between the dharma- and subsequent-knowledge tracks) as establishing this ability as something considerably more constrained than an unlimited telepathic power — it is itself bound by the same hierarchy of attainment that governs everything else in this treatise, so that only a mind at or below one's own level, in one's own temporal present, is ever accessible.

We should also flag the graduated capability among śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, and Buddha as a vivid illustration of just how fast the sixteen-moment path of seeing (introduced in fascicle 23) is held to move: a śrāvaka's best preparatory effort only ever catches the first two moments directly, and by the time a second round of effort is complete for the upper-realm truths, the subject under observation has already finished the entire sixteen-moment sequence and moved on. Only the Buddha, requiring no preparatory effort at all, tracks every single moment as it happens. We have preserved the "some say" variant regarding the pratyekabuddha's exact reach (eighth moment versus first-two-plus-fifteenth) as the open alternative the text itself records, rather than resolving it ourselves.

4. Exhaustion Versus Non-Arising: The Exact Self-Referential Judgment, and How the Ten Knowledges Overlap

The treatise now pins down precisely what distinguishes the knowledge of exhaustion from the knowledge of non-arising — a distinction that turns out to rest on a specific first-person judgment an arhat makes about their own attainment.

Chinese Source

盡、無生智二相何別？頌曰： 智於四聖諦， 知我已知等， 不應更知等， 如次盡無生。 論曰：如本論說：云何盡智？謂無學位若正自知：我已知苦、我已斷集、我已證滅、我已修道，由此所有智、見、明、覺、解、慧、光、觀，是名盡智。云何無生智？謂正自知：我已知苦，不應更知，廣說乃至我已修道，不應更修，由此所有廣說乃至是名無生智。如何無漏智可作如是知？迦濕彌羅諸論師說：從二智出後得智中作如是知，故無有失。由此後得二智別故，表前觀中二智差別。有說：無漏智亦作如是知，然說見言乘言便故，或於諦理現照轉故。由此本論亦作是言。且諸智亦是見。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the difference in character between the knowledge of exhaustion and the knowledge of non-arising? Verse: with respect to the four noble truths, the knowledge that knows "I have already known suffering" and so on is, respectively, exhaustion; the knowledge that further knows "there is no need to know it again" and so on is non-arising. Explanation: as the root treatise states, what is the knowledge of exhaustion? At the non-trainee stage, if one directly knows for oneself: I have already known suffering, I have already severed the origin, I have already realized cessation, I have already cultivated the path — whatever knowledge, seeing, clarity, awakening, understanding, wisdom, illumination, and insight accompanies this is called the knowledge of exhaustion. What is the knowledge of non-arising? It is directly knowing for oneself: I have already known suffering, and need not know it again — stated fully, up through I have already cultivated the path, and need not cultivate it again — whatever accompanies this, stated fully as before, is called the knowledge of non-arising. How can uncontaminated knowledge make such a first-person judgment? The Kashmiri masters say: it is within the subsequently-attained knowledge arising after emerging from these two knowledges that such a judgment is made, hence there is no fault in the description. Because this subsequently-attained pair of knowledges differ from each other, this reveals the distinction between the two knowledges within the preceding direct contemplation itself. Others say: uncontaminated knowledge itself does indeed make such a judgment directly, and the description in terms of subsequently-attained knowledge is merely a convenient manner of speaking, or reflects how the uncontaminated knowledge operates by directly illuminating the principle of the truths in the present moment. It is on this basis that the root treatise makes this statement. These knowledges, moreover, are likewise instances of view.

Having distinguished all ten kinds of knowledge individually, the treatise closes this stretch of the discussion with a compressed accounting of how much they overlap and include one another.

Chinese Source

如是十智相攝云何？謂世俗智攝一全一少分，法類智各攝一全七少分，苦集滅智各攝一全四少分，道智攝一全五少分，他心智攝一全四少分，盡無生智各攝一全六少分。

Ru-Yi Translation

How do these ten knowledges include one another? Worldly knowledge includes one kind entirely and a portion of one other. Dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge each include one entirely and portions of seven others. The knowledges of suffering, origination, and cessation each include one entirely and portions of four others. Path-knowledge includes one entirely and portions of five others. Knowledge of others' minds includes one entirely and portions of four others. The knowledges of exhaustion and non-arising each include one entirely and portions of six others.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the exhaustion/non-arising distinction as turning on a subtle two-step structure: exhaustion is the confirming judgment ("I have already known suffering..."), while non-arising is a further judgment layered on top of it ("...and there is no need to know it again"). The treatise then honestly flags its own philosophical puzzle — how can uncontaminated knowledge, ordinarily understood as directly apprehending the truths themselves, also make a first-person, self-referential judgment about its own activity? — and records two unresolved positions: the Kashmiri masters locate this reflective judgment in a separate "subsequently-attained" knowledge following the direct realization, while others hold the direct, uncontaminated knowledge makes the judgment itself, treating the subsequently-attained framing as loose, convenient language rather than a strict metaphysical claim. We have preserved both readings as the passage presents them, without adjudicating between them.

We should also flag the closing subsumption count honestly: the passage states how many other knowledges each of the ten "includes" (wholly or partially) without spelling out the underlying cross-tabulation that would let a reader verify each figure independently, much as the seven-noble-ones passage in fascicle 25 did with its large combinatorial total. We have translated the stated figures exactly as given rather than reconstructing the full overlap logic ourselves.

5. Seven Grounds for the Tenfold Division, and an Asymmetry Between Dharma- and Subsequent-Knowledge

Having worked through all ten kinds of knowledge individually, the treatise steps back to justify the classification scheme itself: why exactly ten, rather than some other number, given that everything ultimately reduces to just two kinds by nature?

Chinese Source

何緣二智建立為十？頌曰： 由自性對治， 行相行相境， 加行辦因圓， 故建立十智。 論曰：由七緣故立二為十：一自性故立世俗智，非勝義智為自性故。二對治故立法類智，全能對治欲上界故。三行相故立苦集智，此二智境體無別故。四行相境故立滅道智，此二行相境俱有別故。五加行故立他心智，非此不知他心所法，本修加行為知他心，雖成滿時亦知心所，而約加行故立他心智名。六事辦故建立盡智，事辦身中最初生故。七因圓故立無生智，一切聖道為因生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are the two original kinds of knowledge established as ten? Verse: by intrinsic nature, by counteragent, by aspect, by aspect-and-object, by preparatory effort, by task-completion, and by cause-fulfillment — on these grounds, the ten kinds of knowledge are established. Explanation: on seven grounds, the two are established as ten. First, by intrinsic nature, worldly knowledge is established, since its intrinsic nature is not that of ultimate knowledge. Second, by counteragent, dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge are established, since each is able to fully counteract affliction in the desire realm or the upper realms, respectively. Third, by aspect, the knowledges of suffering and origination are established, since these two knowledges' objects are not distinct in substance from each other, only in the aspect under which each contemplates that same object. Fourth, by both aspect and object, the knowledges of cessation and path are established, since, for these two, both aspect and object are distinct from each other. Fifth, by preparatory effort, knowledge of others' minds is established — not that it fails to know others' mental factors as well as their minds, but because the original preparatory effort was undertaken specifically in order to know others' minds; although, once this knowledge is fully accomplished, it also comes to know mental factors, it is named knowledge of others' minds with reference to that original preparatory aim. Sixth, by task-completion, the knowledge of exhaustion is established, since it is the very first knowledge to arise within one whose task has been accomplished. Seventh, by cause-fulfillment, the knowledge of non-arising is established, since it arises only once every noble path serving as its cause has been fully completed.

One asymmetry remains to be addressed: dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge were said to fully counteract the desire realm and the upper realms, respectively, each confined to its own domain. Does this division hold without exception in both directions?

Chinese Source

如上所言法智類智，全能對治欲上界法，為有少分治上欲耶？頌曰： 緣滅道法智， 於修道位中， 兼治上修斷， 類無能治欲。 論曰：修道所攝滅道法智，兼能對治上界修斷，欲之滅道勝上界故，已除自怨能兼他故。由此類智無能治欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

As stated above, dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge each fully counteract the desire realm and the upper realms, respectively — but does either extend even a small portion into the other's domain? Verse: dharma-knowledge taking cessation or the path as its object, at the stage of the path of cultivation, additionally counteracts what is severed by cultivation in the upper realms; subsequent-knowledge, however, has no ability to counteract the desire realm. Explanation: the cessation- and path-type dharma-knowledge included within the path of cultivation is additionally able to counteract what is severed by cultivation in the upper realms, since the desire realm's cessation and path surpass those of the upper realms — having already eliminated the nearer enemy, it is able to additionally handle the farther one as well. For this reason, subsequent-knowledge has no corresponding ability to reach back down and counteract the desire realm.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the seven-ground justification as a genuinely tidy piece of classificatory reasoning: rather than treating "ten kinds of knowledge" as an arbitrary traditional list, the treatise shows each addition to the basic contaminated/uncontaminated split answers a specific organizing question — what is it by nature, what does it counteract, under what aspect, toward what object, through what preparatory aim, marking what completion, fulfilled by what cause. The seven grounds are not all of the same kind (some carve by object, some by function, some by the practitioner's own biographical milestone), and the treatise does not flag this heterogeneity as a problem — the list is offered simply as jointly sufficient to generate all ten names from the underlying two natures.

We should also flag the closing asymmetry as resting on a specific, compressed technical claim — that "the desire realm's cessation and path surpass those of the upper realms" — which the passage treats as already established rather than argued for here; we have translated the stated reasoning (nearer enemy eliminated first, enabling extension to the farther one) exactly as given, without reconstructing the fuller doctrinal basis for why cessation and path specifically do not split as sharply by realm as suffering and origination do.

6. Which Knowledge Has Which Aspects

The treatise now maps each of the ten knowledges onto the "aspects" (行相) under which it contemplates its object — the specific lens, such as "impermanent" or "suffering," through which a given knowledge apprehends what it takes as its object — promising a full explanation of the sixteen classical aspects later in this same chapter.

7. Reading a Stranger's Mind: With or Free of Attachment, Contracted or Scattered, Small or Great

The claim that knowledge of others' minds can only ever grasp one single thing at a time raises an immediate objection: a sūtra has the Buddha speaking of "truly knowing a mind with attachment" and similar phrases, which sound like they combine two things — the mind, and a quality like attachment — within a single act of knowing. The treatise resolves this, then works through the classical sūtra list of paired mental qualities such knowledge can discern.

Chinese Source

若爾，何故薄伽梵說：如實了知有貪心等。非俱時取貪等及心，如不俱時取衣及垢。有貪心者，二義有貪：一貪相應、二貪所繫。貪相應心具由二義，餘有漏心唯貪所繫。有說：經言有貪心者，唯說第一貪相應心。離貪心者，謂治貪心。若貪不相應名離貪心者，餘惑相應者應得離貪名。若爾，有心非貪對治不染污性，應許此心非有貪心離貪心等。是故應許餘師所說，為貪所繫名有貪心，乃至有癡離癡亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

If this is so, why does the Blessed One state: one truly knows a mind with attachment, and so on? It does not grasp attachment and mind simultaneously as two separate things, just as one does not grasp a garment and its stain as two separate things simultaneously. "A mind with attachment" carries two possible senses: associated with attachment, or bound by attachment. A mind associated with attachment satisfies both senses; other contaminated minds satisfy only the sense of being bound by attachment. Some say: the sūtra's phrase refers only to the first sense, a mind associated with attachment at that very moment. "A mind free of attachment" refers to a mind that counteracts attachment. If merely not being associated with attachment at a given moment were enough to be called free of attachment, then a mind associated with some other affliction would also have to be called free of attachment. If, instead, we require it to actively counteract attachment, then a mind that is not itself a counteragent to attachment, yet is undefiled in nature, would have to be granted as neither with attachment nor free of attachment. For this reason, one should accept what other teachers say instead: being bound by attachment, in the broader sense, is what is called a mind with attachment — and the same reasoning applies through to with delusion and free of delusion.

The treatise then works through the remaining paired qualities the classical sūtra list attributes to a mind that can be directly known: contracted or scattered, sunken or braced, small or great.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Vaibhāṣika masters explain it thus: a contracted mind is a wholesome mind, since it does not run scattered with respect to its object; a scattered mind is a defiled mind, since it arises associated with agitation. The Western masters explain it differently: a mind associated with sleep is called contracted; every other defiled mind is called scattered. This is not reasonable, since, on this view, any defiled mind associated with sleep would have to count as both contracted and scattered at once. It would, moreover, contradict what the root treatise states — that truly knowing a contracted mind is accomplished, in full, by four kinds of knowledge: dharma-knowledge, subsequent-knowledge, worldly knowledge, and path-knowledge. A sunken mind is a defiled mind, since it arises associated with laziness; a braced mind is a wholesome mind, since it arises associated with right diligence. A small mind is a defiled mind, since it is what those of little purity delight in cultivating; a great mind is a wholesome mind, since it is what those of abundant purity delight in cultivating. Alternatively, they are named small and great owing to differing amounts of root, worth, retinue, accompanying activity, and functional power. A defiled mind has few roots, since at most it is associated with two of the unwholesome roots at once; a wholesome mind has many roots, since it is constantly associated with all three wholesome roots. A defiled mind has little worth, since it is accomplished without genuine effort; a wholesome mind has great worth, since it is accomplished through great provisions. A defiled mind has a small retinue, since it generates no cultivation for the future; a wholesome mind has a large retinue, since it does generate cultivation for the future. A defiled mind has little accompanying activity, since it involves only three aggregates; a wholesome mind has much accompanying activity, since it extends through all four. A defiled mind has little functional power, since the wholesome roots it severs will certainly reconnect again; a wholesome mind has great functional power, since the noble forbearance permanently severs the latent afflictions once and for all. It is on this basis that defiled and wholesome minds receive the names small and great.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the garment-and-stain simile as doing real philosophical work: it explains how knowledge of others' minds can seem to grasp "a mind with attachment" as a single, richly qualified object without actually violating the earlier rule that it takes only one thing as object at a time — much as ordinary perception registers a "stained garment" as one thing, not a garment plus a stain apprehended as two separate objects. The treatise then resolves an apparent gap in the two-part definition (associated-with versus bound-by) by favoring the broader "bound by" reading, since the narrower "associated with" reading would produce the odd result that a mind currently gripped by hatred counts as "free of attachment" simply because attachment specifically is not present at that instant.

We should also flag the explicit rejection of a named rival school (the "Western masters," presumably referring to Abhidharma teachers outside Kashmir) as a genuine instance of inter-lineage disagreement within the broader Sarvāstivāda tradition itself, not only between the Vaibhāṣika and Sautrāntika as seen elsewhere in this translation — the treatise argues the Western reading of "contracted" (sleep-associated) versus "scattered" (all other defiled states) both produces a logical contradiction (a sleep-associated defiled mind would be both at once) and conflicts with the root treatise's own statement that a contracted mind is knowable by path-knowledge, which could never apply to any defiled state. We have preserved this named disagreement and the treatise's stated grounds for rejecting it exactly as given.

The treatise finishes naming the paired mental qualities — agitated or unagitated, uncalm or calm, unconcentrated or concentrated, uncultivated or cultivated, unliberated or liberated — before turning back, in its own voice, to correct not only the "Western masters'" reading of contracted/scattered already rejected, but the standard Vaibhāṣika reading as well.

An unconcentrated mind is a defiled mind, since it is associated with scattered agitation; a concentrated mind is a wholesome mind, since it counteracts that. An uncultivated mind is a defiled mind, since it is included within neither possession-cultivation nor practice-cultivation; a cultivated mind is a wholesome mind, since it admits of both kinds of cultivation. Having laid out the Vaibhāṣika reading in full, the treatise now turns a critical eye on that very reading — arguing, by direct appeal to the sūtra's own wording, that it fails on its own terms.

What is an externally scattered mind? It is a mind that wanders among the five wondrous objects of desire, following its own scattering and drifting, or is internally associated with insight but lacking calm. Relying on my own explanation, however, one is not unable to distinguish these eight distinct meanings within the sūtra: although scattered and the rest are all alike defiled minds, my reading displays their distinct faults; and although contracted and the rest are all alike wholesome minds, my reading displays their distinct merits — hence, on the basis of eight distinct senses, eight separate names are established. Since the earlier explanation cannot reconcile with what it contradicts in the sūtra, its claimed distinguishing of these terms' meanings is likewise unfounded. We should flag the structure of this debate as genuinely three-cornered, not the usual two-school pattern seen elsewhere in this treatise: the Vaibhāṣika propose a simple wholesome/defiled binary for contracted and scattered; the "Western masters" propose a sleep-associated/all-other-defiled reading, which the Vaibhāṣika reject; and then the treatise's own voice steps in to reject the Vaibhāṣika's binary reading too, on the grounds that it cannot actually distinguish the eight separate terms the sūtra itself names (scattered, sunken, small, agitated as four distinct faults; contracted, braced, great, unagitated as four distinct merits) if it collapses them all into a single "defiled" or "wholesome" bucket. The explicit principle stated here — "better to contradict the secondary treatise's text than to contradict what the sūtra itself says" — is a striking, direct assertion of scriptural authority over Abhidharma commentarial literature, consistent with this treatise's broader Sautrāntika-leaning tendency to test Vaibhāṣika systematization against sūtra wording.

We should also flag the closing resolution of the sunken/agitated objection as turning on a distinction between "cultivation" as an actually manifesting present-moment activity and "cultivation" as merely the mental intention or orientation toward a remedy — allowing the text to say that a sunken mind and an agitated mind call for genuinely different countermeasures (different awakening-factors emphasized) without this requiring that the two states themselves be metaphysically distinct in the more basic wholesome/defiled sense.

9. Reopening "With Attachment": A Reversal, and What Knowledge of Others' Minds Actually Grasps

Before returning to its main line of argument, the treatise revisits the earlier definition of "a mind with attachment" once more, working through several candidate readings before settling — in a genuine reversal of the position favored earlier in this fascicle — on the narrower of the two original options. It then closes this whole stretch of the discussion with the definitive statement of exactly what knowledge of others' minds can and cannot grasp.

Chinese Source

豈不我說亦不違經？雖諸染心皆名沈掉，懈怠增者經說沈心，掉舉增者經說掉心，據恒相應我說體一，隨自意語誰復能遮？然實此經意不如是。前說一切貪所繫心皆名有貪心。貪繫是何義？若貪得隨故，有學無漏心應名有貪，貪得隨故。若貪所緣故，無學有漏心應名有貪，貪所緣故。若不許彼為貪所緣，云何彼心可成有漏？若謂由為共相惑緣應名有癡，癡所緣故。然他心智不緣貪得，亦不可說緣緣心貪。寧知他心是有貪等？故非貪繫名有貪心。若爾，云何？今詳經意，貪相應故名有貪心，貪不相應名離貪等。若爾，何故餘契經言：離貪瞋癡心，不還墮三有。依離得說，故無有過。豈不於前已破此說？餘惑相應者，應得離貪名，彼亦與貪不相應故。若依此意，許亦無違。然不說為離貪心者，彼屬有瞋有癡等故。

Ru-Yi Translation

But does what I say not also avoid contradicting the sūtra? Although all defiled minds alike could be called sunken or agitated, the sūtra speaks of a sunken mind specifically where laziness predominates, and an agitated mind specifically where restlessness predominates; with reference to their constant association, I say their substance is one and the same — who could refute a statement made according to its own intended meaning? But this is genuinely not what the sūtra actually means. It was stated earlier that every mind bound by attachment is called a mind with attachment. What, precisely, is the meaning of bound by attachment? If it means accompanied by the possession of attachment, then a trainee's uncontaminated mind would have to be called with attachment too, since it remains accompanied by that possession. If it means serving as an object attachment can take, then a non-trainee's contaminated mind would have to be called with attachment too, since it can serve as such an object. If one does not grant that it can serve as such an object, how could that mind even count as contaminated in the first place? If one replies that it should instead be called with delusion, since it can serve as the object of a shared-characteristic affliction — but knowledge of others' minds does not take the possession of attachment as its object, nor can it be said to take, as object, a mind together with the attachment that takes that mind as object. How, then, could one ever know another's mind to be with attachment under either reading? Hence, bound by attachment, in either of these senses, is not what is meant by a mind with attachment. If that is so, what then is the correct meaning? Examining the sūtra's intent carefully, a mind is called with attachment because it is associated with attachment; a mind is called free of attachment because it is not associated with attachment, and likewise for hatred and delusion. If this is so, why does a further sūtra state that a mind free of attachment, hatred, and delusion does not fall back into the three realms of existence? This is stated with reference to the possession of separation, hence there is no fault. But was this position not already refuted earlier — that a mind associated with some other affliction would also have to receive the name free of attachment, since it too is not associated with attachment? If we rely on this more careful, moment-by-moment sense, granting this consequence involves no real contradiction after all. It is simply not the ordinary way of speaking to call such a mind free of attachment, since it is more properly classified as with hatred or with delusion instead.

With this settled, the treatise sets the digression aside and returns to its central thesis: precisely what does knowledge of others' minds actually grasp?

We should flag the genuine reversal in this passage: the fascicle's earlier discussion of "a mind with attachment" had favored the broader "bound by attachment" reading over the narrower "associated with attachment" reading, precisely because the narrower reading seemed to produce an absurd result (a mind gripped by hatred would count as "free of attachment"). Here, after testing the "bound by" reading against knowledge of others' minds specifically and finding it unworkable (such knowledge cannot access the possession of attachment or a chain of attachment-taking-mind-as-object), the treatise circles back to the narrower "associated with" reading after all — accepting the seemingly odd consequence (a hatred-filled mind is technically "free of attachment") but noting this simply is not how such a mind would ordinarily be described, since a more specific and applicable label (with hatred) takes precedence in practice. We have preserved this back-and-forth exactly as the text works through it, rather than smoothing the two passages into a single consistent position neither actually states outright.

We should also flag the concluding characterization of knowledge of others' minds as a clarifying constraint worth holding onto for the rest of this chapter: it is a narrow instrument, registering only that another's mind currently has some determinate quality (defiled, contracted, and so on) as its own particular characteristic, without any access to what that mind is thinking about or how it is thinking about it — ruling out, by the treatise's own logic, any risk of a mind somehow cognizing itself through this channel.

10. Why Exhaustion and Non-Arising Drop Two Aspects, and Whether Uncontaminated Aspects Exceed Sixteen

Returning to the sixteen aspects first mentioned when mapping each knowledge onto its objects, the treatise explains why exhaustion-knowledge and non-arising-knowledge specifically drop two of the sixteen — "empty" and "non-self" — before taking up a genuine scholastic dispute: do uncontaminated dharmas ever admit of an aspect beyond these classical sixteen at all?

The treatise then works through the textual argument in full, ultimately siding against the Kashmiri position stated first.

如本論說：頗有不繫心能了別欲界繫法耶？曰：能了別，謂非常故、苦故、空故、非我故、因故、集故、生故、緣故，有是處、有是事，如理所引了別。若謂彼文不為顯示不繫心了別欲界繫法時，除前所明八行相外，別有有是處、有是事行相，但為顯示作八行相斯有是處、斯有是事。此釋不然，餘不說故。謂若彼論依此意說，應於餘處亦說此言，然彼餘文但作是說：頗有見斷心能了別欲界繫法耶？曰：能了別，謂我故、我所故、斷故、常故、無因故、無作故、損減故、尊故、勝故、上故、第一故、能清淨故、能解脫故、能出離故、惑故、疑故、猶豫故、貪故、瞋故、慢故、癡故，不如理所引了別。此等亦應說有是處等言，既無此言，故釋非理。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the root treatise states: is there an unbound mind capable of discerning desire-realm-bound dharmas? Answer: it is capable — as impermanent, as suffering, as empty, as non-self, as cause, as origin, as arising, as condition, and as there being an occasion, and as there being a matter — discernment induced through proper reasoning. If one claims that this passage does not mean to show that, apart from the eight aspects already explained, there exists a further, separate there-being-an-occasion and there-being-a-matter aspect, but rather merely means to show that, in producing any of the eight aspects, there is indeed such an occasion and such a matter — this explanation is not correct, since this same phrase is not stated elsewhere in an analogous passage. That is, if the root treatise intended this merely confirmatory meaning, it should state this same phrase elsewhere too; yet a further passage in that same text states only: is there a seeing-abandoned mind capable of discerning desire-realm-bound dharmas? Answer: it is capable — as self, as what belongs to self, as annihilated, as permanent, as causeless, as agentless, as diminishing, as venerable, as superior, as supreme, as foremost, as capable of purifying, as capable of liberating, as capable of leading to renunciation, as afflicted, as doubtful, as hesitant, as attachment, as hatred, as conceit, as delusion — discernment induced improperly. This passage, too, should include the phrase there being an occasion and so on if that reading were correct — since it does not, the merely confirmatory explanation is unreasonable.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the explanation for why exhaustion- and non-arising-knowledge specifically drop "empty" and "non-self" as a genuinely elegant piece of reasoning: an arhat emerging from meditative absorption declares "my births are exhausted... there is no further becoming" in perfectly ordinary first-person language, and this worldly mode of expression would sit awkwardly beside the simultaneous assertion that there is no self and nothing to call "empty" or "non-self" about — so the treatise has these two knowledges retain the other fourteen aspects while setting these two aside specifically because of how they get expressed at the moment of emergence, not because the underlying realization itself somehow excludes emptiness or non-self.

We should also flag the resolution of the "exceeding sixteen" dispute as a case where the treatise's verse names the Kashmiri position first — often, elsewhere in this project, a sign of the position ultimately favored — yet the actual textual argument that follows sides with the second-named "foreign masters" instead, by a careful comparative reading of two parallel passages in the root treatise: since the confirmatory phrase "there being an occasion, there being a matter" appears in one passage but not in the structurally identical companion passage a few lines later, it cannot be a generic rhetorical tag and must instead name two genuine additional aspects. We have preserved the argument's actual conclusion rather than assuming the verse's word order signals the treatise's final position, since here the prose commentary argues its way to the opposite reading.

11. The Sixteen Aspects Defined: Four Each for Suffering, Origination, Cessation, and Path

Having established that uncontaminated dharmas exceed the classical sixteen aspects, the treatise turns to the sixteen themselves: how many real substances actually underlie them, and precisely what each one means.

Chinese Source

十六行相實事有幾？何謂行相能行所行？頌曰： 行相實十六， 此體唯是慧， 能行有所緣， 所行諸有法。 論曰：有餘師說：十六行相名雖十六實事唯七，謂緣苦諦名實俱四，緣餘三諦名四實一。如是說者實亦十六，謂苦聖諦有四相：一非常、二苦、三空、四非我，待緣故非常，逼迫性故苦，違我所見故空，違我見故非我。集聖諦有四相：一因、二集、三生、四緣，如種理故因，等現理故集，相續理故生，成辦理故緣，譬如泥團輪繩水等眾緣和合成辦瓶等。滅聖諦有四相：一滅、二靜、三妙、四離，諸蘊盡故滅，三火息故靜，無眾患故妙，脫眾災故離。道聖諦有四相：一道、二如、三行、四出，通行義故道，契正理故如，正趣向故行，能永超故出。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the sixteen aspects, how many real substances are there? What, precisely, is meant by aspect, able to function, and what is functioned upon? Verse: the aspects are, in real substance, sixteen; their substance is wisdom alone; able to function applies to whatever has an object; what is functioned upon is every existent dharma. Explanation: other teachers say that, though the sixteen aspects number sixteen by name, their real substance is only seven — those taking the truth of suffering as object number four both in name and substance, while those taking each of the remaining three truths as object number four in name but only one in substance. On the correct account, however, the real substances are likewise sixteen. The noble truth of suffering has four aspects: impermanent, suffering, empty, and non-self — impermanent, since it depends on conditions; suffering, since it has the nature of oppression; empty, since it contradicts the view of what is mine; non-self, since it contradicts the view of self. The noble truth of origination has four aspects: cause, origin, arising, and condition — cause, in the manner of a seed; origin, in the manner of causes manifesting equally in the effect; arising, in the manner of an unbroken continuity; condition, in the manner of bringing about completion, just as a lump of clay, a wheel, a cord, water, and other manifold conditions, coming together, bring about the completion of a pot. The noble truth of cessation has four aspects: cessation, calm, excellence, and release — cessation, since the aggregates are exhausted; calm, since the three fires are extinguished; excellence, since it is free of every affliction; release, since it is liberated from every calamity. The noble truth of the path has four aspects: path, suchness, practice, and deliverance — path, in the sense of a thoroughfare; suchness, since it accords with correct principle; practice, since it rightly proceeds toward its goal; deliverance, since it is capable of permanently transcending saṃsāra.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that these sixteen aspects — introduced by name in earlier fascicles of this translation and invoked repeatedly throughout this fascicle's discussion of the ten knowledges — are only now given their full definitions, confirming the treatise's own promise, made earlier in this fascicle, to explain them "later." Each of the four truths receives four aspects that move from a bare description toward an increasingly technical characterization: suffering's aspects work outward from simple impermanence toward the specifically Buddhist denial of both "mine" and "self"; origination's four aspects are unified by a single agricultural metaphor (seed, equal manifestation, continuity, completion) illustrated by the classic image of a potter's wheel, clay, cord, and water jointly producing a finished pot.

We should also flag the treatise's rejection of the minority view that collapses origination, cessation, and path each into a single real substance (leaving only seven real substances behind the sixteen names) — the passage does not elaborate its reasoning for rejecting this view in detail here, moving directly to lay out all sixteen as equally real substances instead; we have preserved this compressed dismissal exactly as given rather than reconstructing an argument the text does not spell out at this point.

12. A Further Gloss, and the Eighteen Kinds of Craving Behind "Cause, Origin, Arising, Condition"

The tradition, it turns out, already offers more than one way of glossing these same sixteen terms. The treatise records a second traditional phrasing, then — noting that the ancients themselves were never of one mind on this point — offers its own preferred explanation, which for the aspects of origination opens onto a remarkably detailed psychological taxonomy of self-grasping craving.

Chinese Source

又非究竟故非常，如荷重擔故苦，內離士夫故空，不自在故非我。牽引義故因，出現義故集，滋產義故生，為依義故緣。不續相續斷故滅，離三有為相故靜，勝義善故妙，極安隱故離。治邪道故道，治不如故如，趣入涅槃宮故行，棄捨一切有故出。如是古釋既非一門，故隨所樂更為別釋。生滅故非常，違聖心故苦，於此無我故空，自非我故非我。因集生緣如經所釋，謂五取蘊以欲為根、以欲為集、以欲為類、以欲為生，唯此生聲應在後說，與論為異。

Ru-Yi Translation

Alternatively: impermanent, since not ultimate; suffering, like bearing a heavy burden; empty, since internally free of any agent; non-self, since not self-governing. Cause, in the sense of drawing forth; origin, in the sense of manifesting; arising, in the sense of nourishing into being; condition, in the sense of serving as a support. Cessation, since the continuity of continuation is severed; calm, since free of the three conditioned marks; excellence, since good in the ultimate sense; release, since supremely secure. Path, since it counteracts wrong paths; suchness, since it counteracts what is not thus; practice, since it proceeds into the palace of nirvāṇa; deliverance, since it abandons all conditioned existence. Since these traditional explanations are themselves not univocal, let me, according to my own preference, offer a further, separate explanation. Impermanent, since it arises and ceases; suffering, since it goes against the noble mind's wish; empty, since there is no self within it; non-self, since it is not itself a self. Cause, origin, arising, and condition are as a sūtra explains them: the five aggregates subject to grasping have craving as their root, craving as their origin, craving as their class, and craving as their arising — only this last term, "arising," ought properly to be stated last, differing here from how the root treatise orders them.

The treatise then explains what distinguishes these four terms in substance — tracing each back to a different stage of self-grasping craving.

Chinese Source

此四體相差別云何？由隨位別四欲有異：一執現總我起總自體欲、二執當總我起總後有欲、三執當別我起別後有欲、四執續生我起續生時欲。或執造業我起造業時欲，第一於苦是初因，故說名為因，如種子於果。第二於苦等招集，故說名為集，如芽等於果。第三於苦為別緣，故說名為緣，如田等於果，謂由田水糞等力故令果味勢熟德別生。第四於苦能近生，故說名為生，如華蘂於果。或如契經說：有二五二四愛行為四種欲。執現總我有五種異：一執我現決定有、二執我現如是有、三執我現變異有、四執我現有、五執我現無。執當總我亦有五異：一執我當決定有、二執我當如是有、三執我當變異有、四執我當有、五執我當無。執當別我有四種異：一執我當別有、二執我當決定別有、三執我當如是別有、四執我當變異別有。執續生我等亦有四種異：一執我亦當有、二執我亦當決定有、三執我亦當如是有、四執我亦當變異有。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the difference in substance among these four terms? According to differing stages, the craving involved differs in four ways: first, grasping a present, total self gives rise to craving for a total present self-embodiment; second, grasping a future, total self gives rise to craving for a total future existence; third, grasping a future, particular self gives rise to craving for a particular future existence; fourth, grasping a continuously-reborn self gives rise to craving at the moment of continuous rebirth. Alternatively: grasping a karma-creating self gives rise to craving at the moment of creating karma. The first, being the initial cause with respect to suffering, is called cause — like a seed with respect to its fruit. The second brings about the accumulation of suffering, and so is called origin — like a sprout with respect to its fruit. The third serves as a particular condition for suffering, and so is called condition — like a field with respect to its fruit, since it is through the power of the field, water, fertilizer, and the like that the fruit's flavor, potency, ripeness, and other distinct qualities come to arise. The fourth is capable of proximately generating suffering, and so is called arising — like a flower's pistil with respect to its fruit. Alternatively, as a sūtra states, there are eighteen craving-activities constituting these four kinds of craving. Grasping a present, total self has five distinct forms: grasping that I definitely exist now; that I exist now in such-and-such a way; that I exist now, subject to change; that I exist now; and that I do not exist now. Grasping a future, total self likewise has five distinct forms: that I will definitely exist; that I will exist in such-and-such a way; that I will exist, subject to change; that I will exist; and that I will not exist. Grasping a future, particular self has four distinct forms: that I will have a particular existence; that I will definitely have a particular existence; that I will have a particular existence in such-and-such a way; and that I will have a particular existence, subject to change. Grasping a continuously-reborn self likewise has four distinct forms: that I too will exist; that I too will definitely exist; that I too will exist in such-and-such a way; and that I too will exist, subject to change.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that the treatise openly acknowledges multiple traditional glosses of the same sixteen terms already circulating before offering its own — a rare moment of explicit methodological honesty about the state of the received exegetical tradition, rather than presenting a single inherited explanation as simply given. We have kept all layers distinct exactly as the text presents them, without harmonizing the differing phrasings into one composite gloss.

We should also flag the eighteen-fold craving taxonomy as a genuinely remarkable piece of psychological granularity: rather than treating "craving for self" as one undifferentiated impulse, the tradition distinguishes eighteen distinct micro-postures toward a self across four temporal/existential categories (present-total, future-total, future-particular, continuously-reborn), further subdivided by modal qualifiers such as "definitely," "in such-and-such a way," "subject to change," and even the possibility of grasping non-existence itself. This matches a classical scheme found elsewhere in the broader early Buddhist tradition (the eighteen taṇhāvicarita); we have translated the compressed listing exactly as this treatise presents it, in its own numbered order, rather than importing a fuller gloss of each variant from outside sources this passage does not itself provide.

13. Cessation and Path's Aspects, Sixteen Wrong Views Countered, and the Scope of "Aspect"

The treatise completes its preferred explanation with the aspects of cessation and the path, then closes the entire discussion by showing exactly which mistaken view each of the sixteen aspects is designed to counteract — before returning to the technical question of how far "aspect" itself extends as a category.

Chinese Source

流轉斷故滅，眾苦息故靜。如說苾芻諸行皆苦，唯有涅槃最為寂靜，更無上故妙，不退轉故離。如正道故道，如實轉故如，定能趣故行。如說此道能至清淨，餘見必無至清淨理，永離有故出。

Ru-Yi Translation

Cessation, since the flow of transmigration is severed; calm, since every suffering is stilled — as it is said, monks, all formations are suffering, nirvāṇa alone is the most tranquil. Excellence, since nothing surpasses it; release, since it never retrogresses. Path, since it is a right road; suchness, since it operates in accordance with reality; practice, since it certainly leads onward to its goal — as it is said, this path is able to reach purity, no other view could ever possibly reach purity. Deliverance, since it permanently departs from conditioned existence.

With all sixteen now fully glossed, the treatise reveals the deeper logic tying them together: each aspect exists specifically to counteract one identifiable wrong view.

Chinese Source

又為治常樂我所我見故修非常苦空非我行相，為治無因一因變因知先因見，故修因、集、生、緣行相。為治解脫是無見，故修滅行相。為治解脫是苦見，故修靜行相。為治靜慮及等至樂是妙見，故修妙行相。為治解脫是數退墮非永見，故修離行相。為治無道邪道餘道退道見，故修道、如、行、出行相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, in order to counteract the views of permanence, pleasure, mine, and self, one cultivates the aspects of impermanent, suffering, empty, and non-self, respectively. In order to counteract the views of no cause, a single cause, a transforming cause, and a foreknown cause, one cultivates the aspects of cause, origin, arising, and condition. In order to counteract the view that liberation is mere non-existence, one cultivates the aspect of cessation. In order to counteract the view that liberation is itself a kind of suffering, one cultivates the aspect of calm. In order to counteract the view that the pleasure of the dhyānas and meditative attainments is already excellent, one cultivates the aspect of excellence. In order to counteract the view that liberation is something repeatedly subject to retrogression and fall, not permanent, one cultivates the aspect of release. In order to counteract the views of no path, a wrong path, some other inferior path, and a retrogressible path, one cultivates the aspects of path, suchness, practice, and deliverance.

Finally, the treatise returns to a technical loose end: since these aspects have wisdom as their substance, does this mean wisdom itself lacks any aspect of its own?

Chinese Source

如是行相以慧為體。若爾，慧應非有行相，以慧與慧不相應故。由此應言諸心心所取境類別皆名行相，慧及諸餘心心所法有所緣故皆是能行，一切有法皆是所行。由此三門體有寬陿，慧通行相、能行、所行，餘心心所唯能所行，諸餘有法唯是所行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus these aspects have wisdom as their substance. If this is so, should wisdom itself then be without any aspect, since wisdom is not associated with another instance of wisdom? For this reason, one should say instead: whatever distinct category a mind or mental factor uses to apprehend its object is what is called its aspect. Wisdom, together with every other mind and mental factor, since each has an object, is thereby able to function. Every existent dharma is what is functioned upon. On this basis, these three categories differ in scope, broader and narrower: wisdom spans all three — aspect, able to function, and what is functioned upon; other minds and mental factors are only able to function and what is functioned upon; every other existent dharma is only what is functioned upon.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the wrong-view-by-wrong-view mapping as the passage that finally explains why the tradition insists on exactly these sixteen aspects rather than some other set: each one is a targeted counteragent to a specific mistaken belief a practitioner might otherwise hold — not just "impermanence" in the abstract, but a direct answer to the view of permanence; not just "cause" in the abstract, but a direct answer to no-cause, single-cause, transforming-cause, and predetermined-cause views specifically. Read this way, the sixteen aspects are less a neutral descriptive inventory than a precisely engineered set of antidotes, each cut to the shape of one error.

We should also flag the closing technical point as a tidy piece of self-reference resolved without paradox: since wisdom cannot be "associated with" another instance of wisdom, it cannot take an aspect the way ordinary objects are taken under an aspect — but this is resolved by broadening "aspect" to mean simply whatever category-distinction a cognizing mind or mental factor uses to apprehend its object, letting wisdom itself qualify as having aspects (the sixteen just discussed) while explaining why it additionally spans two further categories, "able to function" and "what is functioned upon," that ordinary mental factors and other dharmas only partially occupy.

14. The Ten Knowledges' Moral Nature, and What Ground and Body They Depend On

Having finished the aspects, the treatise closes out its systematic profile of the ten knowledges with three further questions: which moral natures each belongs to, which meditative grounds each depends on, and which realm's body each requires in order to actually manifest.

Chinese Source

已辨十智行相差別，當辨性攝依他依身。頌曰： 性俗三九善， 依地俗一切， 他心智唯四， 法六餘十九， 現起所依身， 他心依欲色， 法智但依欲， 餘八通三界。 論曰：如是十智三性攝者，謂世俗通三性，餘九智唯是善。依地別者，謂世俗智通依欲界乃至有頂，他心智唯依四根本靜慮，法智依此四及未至、中間，餘依此六地及下三無色。依身別者，謂他心智依欲色界俱可現前，法智但依欲界現起，餘八智現起通依三界身。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having distinguished the aspects belonging to the ten knowledges, we should now distinguish their inclusion by moral nature, and their dependence on ground and body. Verse: by nature, worldly knowledge is common to all three, the remaining nine are wholesome. By dependence on ground: worldly knowledge depends on all; knowledge of others' minds only on four; dharma-knowledge on six; the rest on nine. As for the body they arise dependent upon: knowledge of others' minds depends on the desire or form realm; dharma-knowledge depends only on the desire realm; the remaining eight extend through bodies of all three realms. Explanation: as for these ten knowledges' inclusion within the three moral natures — worldly knowledge is common to all three natures; the remaining nine knowledges are exclusively wholesome. As for their difference in dependence on ground: worldly knowledge depends on the entire range from the desire realm up through the peak of existence; knowledge of others' minds depends only on the four root dhyānas; dharma-knowledge depends on these four dhyānas plus the not-yet-attained stage and the intermediate dhyāna; the remaining knowledges depend on these six grounds plus the lower three formless spheres. As for their difference in dependence on body: knowledge of others' minds can manifest depending on a body of either the desire or the form realm; dharma-knowledge manifests only depending on a body of the desire realm; the remaining eight knowledges can manifest depending on a body of any of the three realms.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A transparency note on the verse text: our source scan renders the ground-count for "the remaining" knowledges as "十九" (nineteen), which cannot be correct — no more than eleven grounds exist in this cosmology in total, and the prose commentary immediately following states unambiguously that these knowledges depend on "six grounds plus the lower three formless spheres," i.e. nine. We have translated the verse as "nine," following the prose commentary within this same passage, and flag this rather than silently reproducing what appears to be a transcription artifact in the verse line itself.

We should also flag the underlying logic tying ground-dependency to body-dependency together: worldly knowledge, being contaminated and universal in scope, depends on every ground and every realm's body without restriction; knowledge of others' minds and dharma-knowledge are both restricted to lower grounds (at most the four root dhyānas) and correspondingly restricted to desire- or form-realm bodies, since their objects (another's mind, or the desire realm's own truths) are themselves tied to those lower realms; while the remaining eight, capable of extending into the formless spheres' grounds, are correspondingly unrestricted in which of the three realms' bodies can support them. The restriction pattern is consistent throughout — a knowledge's grounds and its available bodies track each other rather than varying independently.

15. Which Foundation of Mindfulness Each Knowledge Belongs To, and Which Knowledges Can Know Each Other

The treatise now ties the ten knowledges back to the four foundations of mindfulness introduced earlier in the treatise, then asks a reflexive question: since knowledge itself can be an object of knowledge, which of the ten can take which others as their own object?

Chinese Source

已辯性地身，當辯念住攝。頌曰： 諸智念住攝， 滅智唯最後， 他心智後三， 餘八智通四。 論曰：滅智攝在法念住中，他心智後三攝，所餘八皆通四。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having distinguished nature, ground, and body, we should now distinguish which foundation of mindfulness the knowledges belong to. Verse: as for which foundation of mindfulness the knowledges belong to, cessation-knowledge belongs only to the last; knowledge of others' minds belongs to the last three; the remaining eight are common to all four. Explanation: cessation-knowledge is included within the dharma-foundation of mindfulness alone. Knowledge of others' minds is included within the last three foundations. The remaining eight are all common to all four.

Chinese Source

如是十智展轉相望，一一當言幾智為境？頌曰： 諸智互相緣， 法類道各九， 苦集智各二， 四皆十滅非。 論曰：法智能緣九智為境，除類智。類智能緣九智為境，除法智。道智能緣九智為境，除世俗智，非道攝故。苦集二智一一能緣二智為境，謂俗、他心。世俗、他心、盡、無生智皆緣十智為境。滅智不緣諸智為境，唯以擇滅為所緣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these ten knowledges, considered reciprocally against one another, how many of the others can each individually take as its object? Verse: as for how the knowledges take each other as object, dharma-, subsequent-, and path-knowledge each take nine; the knowledges of suffering and origination each take two; four knowledges all take ten; cessation-knowledge takes none. Explanation: dharma-knowledge is able to take nine knowledges as its object, excluding subsequent-knowledge. Subsequent-knowledge is able to take nine knowledges as its object, excluding dharma-knowledge. Path-knowledge is able to take nine knowledges as its object, excluding worldly knowledge, since worldly knowledge is not included within the path. The two knowledges of suffering and origination, each individually, are able to take only two knowledges as their object — worldly knowledge and knowledge of others' minds. Worldly knowledge, knowledge of others' minds, exhaustion-knowledge, and non-arising-knowledge all take all ten knowledges as their object. Cessation-knowledge does not take any other knowledge as its object at all, since it takes only cessation-through-discernment as its object.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag cessation-knowledge's isolation in both passages as consistent and telling: it belongs only to the dharma-foundation of mindfulness (never body, feeling, or mind), and it is the one knowledge among the ten that can never take any other knowledge as its object, since its sole object is the unconditioned cessation-through-discernment itself — an unconditioned dharma, not another cognitive act. Every other knowledge, by contrast, can serve as an object for at least some of the others, reflecting how thoroughly this system treats knowledge as something that can itself be known, examined, and taken as an object of further cognition.

We should also flag why dharma-knowledge and subsequent-knowledge exclude only each other (nine of the other nine, minus one): since these two are defined by taking the desire realm's and the upper realms' truths as their respective, mutually exclusive domains (established earlier in this fascicle), each can know virtually everything else in the system except the other's specialized counterpart — a direct structural echo of the realm-based division that has organized this entire chapter.
