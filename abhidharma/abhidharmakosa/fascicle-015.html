What Restraint Is Obtained From

Fascicle 15 continues Chapter 4 (分別業品第四之三, the Karma-nirdeśa) directly from fascicle 14. Having worked through the vow of individual liberation's full structure, the treatise now asks a more technical question: exactly what does each of the three kinds of restraint arise "from" — which categories of karmic action, which beings, and which of the three times? This precision will matter for the fascicle's later discussion of how restraint and non-restraint are gained and lost.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 11 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 15 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. Does Receiving One Restraint Grant the Other Two?

The treatise opens this fascicle by asking whether receiving any one of the three kinds of restraint — the vow of individual liberation, meditation-born restraint, or path-born restraint — automatically brings the other two along with it. The answer is no: each is obtained separately, from its own distinct scope of karmic action. The vow of individual liberation (bound to the desire realm) is obtained from every root karma-path together with its preparatory and follow-up actions; it is obtained with respect to two categories of object (sentient and non-sentient beings) and two categories of offense (root and proscribed); and it is obtained only with respect to the present aggregates, sense-bases, and elements — never the past or future — since this restraint operates specifically within the domain of currently-existing sentient beings, and the past and future do not themselves constitute such a domain. Meditation-born and uncontaminated restraint, by contrast, are obtained only from the root karma-path itself — not even from its preparatory or follow-up actions, let alone from proscribed offenses — but they are obtained with respect to all three times: past, present, and future aggregates, sense-bases, and elements alike.

Chinese Source

此別解脫、靜慮、無漏三種律儀，從彼得一亦餘二不？不爾。云何？頌曰： 從一切二現， 得欲界律儀， 從根本恒時， 得靜慮無漏。 論曰：欲界律儀謂別解脫，此從一切根本業道及從加行後起而得。從二得者，謂從二類，即情非情、性罪遮罪。從現得者，謂從現世蘊處界得，非從去來。由此律儀有情處轉，去來非是有情處故。若得靜慮無漏律儀，應知但從根本業道。尚不從彼加行後起得此律儀，況從遮罪。從恒時者，謂從過去現在未來蘊處界得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Does obtaining one of these three restraints — the restraint of individual liberation, meditative restraint, and uncontaminated restraint — also bring about obtaining the other two? It does not. How is this so? Verse: desire-realm restraint is obtained from all, from two, and from the present; meditative and uncontaminated restraint are obtained from the root [karma-path alone], across all three times. Prose: desire-realm restraint refers to the restraint of individual liberation; this is obtained from every root karma-path, together with its preparatory and follow-up actions. "Obtained from two" means obtained with respect to two categories: sentient and non-sentient, and root offenses and proscribed offenses. "Obtained from the present" means it is obtained with respect to the present aggregates, sense-bases, and elements, not the past or future, since this restraint operates within the domain of sentient beings, and the past and future are not themselves such a domain. As for obtaining meditative and uncontaminated restraint, one should understand that this is only from the root karma-path — it is not even obtained from its preparatory or follow-up actions, let alone from proscribed offenses. "Across all times" means it is obtained with respect to the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements of the past, present, and future alike.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section's technical vocabulary rewards a little unpacking. A "root karma-path" (根本業道) is the actual completed act of wrongdoing or virtue itself (the moment of killing, say), while its "preparatory action" (加行) is everything leading up to that moment, and its "follow-up action" (後起) is what continues afterward — a threefold division of any karmic act's full temporal extent that this treatise has used elsewhere. The claim that the vow of individual liberation draws on all three of these stages, while meditative and uncontaminated restraint draw only on the root act itself, marks a real structural difference: the prātimokṣa vow's restraining power is calibrated to block wrongdoing across its entire potential unfolding, while the deeper meditative and path-based restraints operate with surgical precision on the completed act alone.

The claim that desire-realm restraint operates only "in the present" while meditative and uncontaminated restraint span all three times connects back to material from the previous two fascicles: since ordinary sentient beings, as objects of the prātimokṣa vow's protection, only exist in the present (the past and future not being active "domains" a currently-living vow-holder can be said to be protecting), this restraint's very definition ties it to present beings only — whereas meditative and uncontaminated restraint, tracking a more abstract class of karma-paths rather than currently-existing beings as such, are not limited this way.

2. A Fourfold Classification, and Why Restraint Only Ever Guards the Future

Building on the previous section's claim that the vow of individual liberation is obtained "from the present" specifically, the treatise now maps out four possible combinations of time (past/future vs. present) and karmic stage (preparatory-and-follow-up vs. root act) to show exactly which yield only the vow of individual liberation, to the exclusion of meditative and uncontaminated restraint. Present-tense preparatory and follow-up actions, together with all proscribed offenses, yield only the vow of individual liberation; past or future root karma-paths yield only meditative and uncontaminated restraint; present-tense root karma-paths yield the vow of individual liberation as well as meditative and uncontaminated restraint together; and past or future preparatory and follow-up actions yield neither. The treatise adds an important clarification: since one cannot literally already possess a completed unwholesome karma-path in the present moment while simultaneously receiving a wholesome restraint against it, all this talk of restraint being "obtained from" root karma-paths and the rest must really be understood as protection extended toward the future — strictly speaking, restraint should never be described as guarding against the past or present at all, only against what is yet to come.

Chinese Source

由此差別應作四句，有蘊處界從彼唯得別解律儀非餘二等。第一句者，謂從現世加行後起及諸遮罪。第二句者，謂從去來根本業道。第三句者，謂從現世根本業道。第四句者，謂從去來加行後起。非於正得善律儀時可有現世惡業道等，是故應言從現處得。理應但說防護未來，定不應言防護過現。

Ru-Yi Translation

By virtue of this distinction, one should set out four cases: there are aggregates, sense-bases, and elements from which one obtains only the restraint of individual liberation and not the other two, and so on. The first case is present-tense preparatory and follow-up actions, together with all proscribed offenses. The second case is past or future root karma-paths. The third case is present-tense root karma-paths. The fourth case is past or future preparatory and follow-up actions. It cannot be that, at the very moment one is properly obtaining a wholesome restraint, a present-tense unwholesome karma-path [that restraint is meant to prevent] already exists — hence, though it is said to be "obtained from the present," in principle one should only speak of it as guarding the future; one should certainly not say that it guards the past or the present.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing clarification here is doing careful, precise work to avoid a real logical trap: if restraint were said to "guard against" a present-tense unwholesome act, this would absurdly imply that the wrongdoing and its prevention could coexist at the very same moment. The resolution is to recognize that "obtained from the present" describes only the temporal location of the *class of karma-path* (root act, as opposed to preparatory/follow-up stages, or past/future instances) that defines the restraint's scope, not a claim that the restraint is simultaneously blocking an act happening right now — the actual protective function always points forward, toward future occasions when a similar act might otherwise occur.

This fourfold classification is a good example of how this treatise regularly turns a single distinction (here, time × karmic stage) into an exhaustive grid of possibilities, systematically checking each cell rather than leaving any combination unaddressed — a method that recurs constantly throughout this fascicle and the two before it.

3. Restraint Is Always Universal in Scope, Though Its Branches and Causes Vary

The treatise now asks whether restraint and non-restraint, when obtained, differ in scope with respect to the class of beings involved, the precept-branches received, and the underlying mental causes at work — and finds that they do differ, but not in the way one might expect. Restraint is always obtained with respect to every single sentient being without exception; there is no such thing as partial restraint covering only some beings. What does vary is the number of branches involved (a bhikṣu's restraint draws on the complete precept code, while other restraints draw only on a subset of four core branches) and the underlying wholesome roots at work (sometimes all three — non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion — arise together as the cause, since these three necessarily co-occur; but restraint can also be classified more narrowly by just one of the three intensity-grades — lower, middling, or superior — since these three grades of mind, unlike the three wholesome roots, never occur simultaneously). Combining these two independent variables (branches and grades) yields four distinct patterns of practitioner: someone receiving the upāsaka or śrāmaṇera vow with a lower, middling, or superior mind (universal in beings, but neither universal in branches nor in grades); someone receiving the full bhikṣu vow with just one grade of mind (universal in beings and branches, but not grades); someone receiving upāsaka, śrāmaṇera, and bhikṣu vows in succession using all three grades of mind (universal in beings, branches, and grades together); and someone receiving upāsaka, upavāsa, and śrāmaṇera vows using all three grades (universal in beings and grades, but not branches). In every single case, however, the restraint obtained pervades every sentient being without exception, since restraint can only be obtained by cultivating a superior, non-harming disposition of mind toward literally all beings at once — falling short of this universal scope would mean one's harmful disposition toward the excluded beings had never actually been fully stilled.

Chinese Source

諸有獲得律不律儀，從一切有情支因有異不？此定有異。相云何？頌曰： 律從諸有情， 支因說不定， 不律從一切， 有情支非因。 論曰：律儀定從一切有情得。無少分理，支因說不定。支不定者，有從一切得，謂苾芻律儀。有從四支得，謂所餘律儀，唯根本業道名律儀支故。因不定者，謂或有義從一切因，或約餘義唯許從一。從一切者，謂從無貪瞋癡，必俱起故。唯從一者，謂從下中上心，不俱起故。此中且就後三因說。或有一類住律儀者，於一切有情得律儀，非一切支非一切因，謂以下心或中或上受近事勤策戒。或有一類住律儀者，於一切有情得律儀，由一切支非一切因，謂以下心或中或上受苾芻戒。或有一類住律儀者，於一切有情得律儀，由一切支及一切因，謂以三心受近事勤策苾芻戒。或有一類住律儀者，於一切有情得律儀，由一切因非一切支，謂以三心受近事近住勤策戒。無有不遍於諸有情得律儀者，以於一切諸有情所住善意樂方得律儀，異則不然，以惡意樂不全息故。

Ru-Yi Translation

For those who obtain restraint or non-restraint, is there a difference with respect to all sentient beings, the branches, and the causes? There is certainly a difference. What is its character? Verse: restraint is obtained from all sentient beings, but its branches and causes are not fixed; non-restraint is obtained from all sentient beings and all branches, but not [necessarily all] causes. Prose: restraint is certainly obtained with respect to all sentient beings; there is no possibility of a partial case. Its branches and causes, however, are not fixed. As for the branches not being fixed: some restraint is obtained with respect to all [precept-branches] — namely, the bhikṣu restraint; some is obtained with respect to only four branches — namely, the remaining restraints — since it is only the root karma-path that is called a "restraint-branch." As for the causes not being fixed: in one sense, restraint may be obtained from all causes at once; in another sense, it may be obtained from only one. "From all" means from non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion, since these necessarily arise together. "From only one" means from a lower, middling, or superior mind, since these do not arise together — this is stated here specifically with reference to these latter three causes. There is one kind of person abiding in restraint who obtains restraint with respect to all sentient beings, but not with respect to all branches nor all causes — namely, one who receives the upāsaka or śrāmaṇera precept with a lower, middling, or superior mind. There is another kind who obtains restraint with respect to all sentient beings, by way of all branches but not all causes — namely, one who receives the bhikṣu precept with a lower, middling, or superior mind. There is another kind who obtains restraint with respect to all sentient beings, by way of all branches and all causes — namely, one who receives the upāsaka, śrāmaṇera, and bhikṣu precepts using all three [grades of] mind. There is another kind who obtains restraint with respect to all sentient beings, by way of all causes but not all branches — namely, one who receives the upāsaka, upavāsa, and śrāmaṇera precepts using all three [grades of] mind. There is no case of obtaining restraint that does not pervade all sentient beings, since restraint is obtained only by abiding in a superior, wholesome disposition toward all sentient beings without exception; otherwise, it is not obtained, since a harmful disposition [toward the excluded beings] would not have been entirely stilled.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between the two kinds of "cause" being discussed here is worth making explicit, since the Chinese moves between them without much signposting: one sense of "cause" refers to the three wholesome roots (non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion), which always arise together as a fixed trio and so are never a source of variation; the other sense refers to the three intensity-grades of mind (lower, middling, superior) introduced back in fascicle 14, which are mutually exclusive at any given moment of vow-reception and so genuinely vary from one vow-taking occasion to the next. The four-case classification that follows is built entirely around this second, grade-based sense of "cause," combined with the branch-count variable, to produce a complete map of how a practitioner's restraint-profile can differ even though its scope across beings never does.

The closing line's insistence that restraint is "obtained only by abiding in a superior, wholesome disposition toward all sentient beings without exception" is the deep principle underlying this section's entire argument, and it reappears, worded almost identically, in the next section's discussion of why one can still obtain restraint even toward beings one has no actual power to harm (such as gods or beings in other realms) — the universality of restraint is grounded not in any practical capacity to affect every being, but purely in the totality of the mental resolve behind it.

4. Five Self-Imposed Limits That Block Genuine Restraint

The treatise now names five specific ways someone might unknowingly fail to obtain real restraint even while going through the motions of taking a vow: fixing a limit on which beings the vow covers ("I shall abstain from killing only with respect to such-and-such class of beings"), on which precept-branches it covers ("I shall only keep such-and-such branch, not the others"), on which places it applies to ("I shall abstain from killing only while residing in such-and-such region"), on which time it covers ("I shall abstain from killing only for the duration of one month," and so on), or on which circumstances it applies to ("I shall abstain from killing except in circumstances such as warfare"). Anyone who receives their vow with any one of these five limitations attached does not actually obtain restraint at all — they obtain only an imitation of virtuous conduct that merely resembles restraint without being the real thing. This raises a natural question: given restraint's requirement of covering literally every sentient being without exception, how could anyone obtain restraint with respect to beings entirely outside their own power to affect at all — such as beings in realms or circumstances the vow-taker could never actually reach or harm? The answer is that restraint arises simply through generating a supreme, universal disposition of not wishing to take any being's life, extended to every sentient being alike, regardless of whether one could ever actually act on that intention toward any particular one of them.

Chinese Source

若人不作五種定限，方可受得別解律儀，謂有情、支、處、時、緣定。有情定者，念我唯於某類有情當離殺等。言支定者，念我唯於某律儀支當持不犯。言處定者，念我唯住某類方域當離殺等。言時定者，念我唯於一月等時能離殺等。言緣定者，念我唯除鬪戰等緣能離殺等。如是受者不得律儀，但得律儀相似妙行。於非所能境，如何得律儀？由普於有情發起增上不損命意樂，故得律儀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Only if a person does not impose any of five kinds of fixed limits can they properly receive the restraint of individual liberation — namely, limits with respect to beings, branches, places, times, and circumstances. A limit with respect to beings means thinking, "I shall abstain from killing and the like only with respect to such-and-such class of beings." A limit with respect to branches means thinking, "I shall only uphold and not transgress such-and-such restraint-branch." A limit with respect to places means thinking, "I shall abstain from killing and the like only while residing in such-and-such region." A limit with respect to times means thinking, "I shall be able to abstain from killing and the like only for the duration of one month," and so on. A limit with respect to circumstances means thinking, "I shall be able to abstain from killing and the like except under circumstances such as warfare." One who receives the precept in any of these ways does not obtain restraint, but only obtains virtuous conduct that merely resembles restraint. [Question:] With respect to an object outside one's own power, how could restraint be obtained? [Reply:] It is obtained through generating a supreme disposition, universally extended to all sentient beings, of not wishing to harm life — this is how restraint is obtained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

These five limitations are worth holding up against the earlier fascicles' discussion of what makes the vow of individual liberation different from an ordinary act of virtue: a limited commitment ("I will not kill, except in wartime") might still be a genuinely good thing to do, but this treatise insists it does not count as the specific, technical achievement called "restraint," precisely because restraint's defining feature is its totality — a single carved-out exception, in any of these five dimensions, reveals that the underlying harmful disposition toward the excepted case was never actually extinguished, only suppressed under certain conditions.

The reply to the "objects outside one's power" question echoes almost verbatim the closing principle of the previous section — restraint depends entirely on the totality of one's inner disposition, not on any actual capacity to act on it — which is precisely why a vow-taker who will, in practice, never encounter (say) a being in a distant hell-realm or an unreachable heaven can still be said to genuinely extend restraint to that being: the disposition of universal non-harm is what constitutes the restraint, independent of whether an opportunity to violate it could ever actually arise.

5. Why Restraint Doesn't Fluctuate as Beings Die and New Ones Are Born

An objection is raised against the claim that restraint is obtained with respect to every sentient being: since beings constantly move between the category of "reachable" and "unreachable" — through death, birth, and other transitions — shouldn't restraint's own scope and strength constantly increase and decrease along with these changes, and shouldn't a vow-holder therefore experience restraint being gained and lost even without any deliberate act of relinquishment on their part? The Vaibhāṣika reject this, offering an analogy: sprouting grass that has not yet grown, or has already withered away, causes no increase or decrease whatsoever in an already-established restraint covering it — and beings moving between reachable and unreachable status should be understood the very same way. When pressed further — why should this analogy actually hold, given that sentient beings' continuity across time seems importantly different from grass's? — the treatise clarifies that the disanalogy is not fatal, since even grass changes its own nature over time (rather than remaining one fixed, unchanging thing), just as sentient beings do; the deeper point is that restraint's scope was never tied to any particular being's ongoing individual existence in the first place, but rather to the totality of the karmic-object domain as such. This becomes especially clear in an extreme case: even once a being has passed into final nirvāṇa and their prior mode of existence is now entirely gone, this causes no decrease in restraint either, since a later buddha's own restraint is understood to be obtained with respect to all sentient beings in general — including, hypothetically, that being, had they still existed — so no actual loss of scope occurs even across such a fundamental change.

Chinese Source

毘婆沙師有作是說：若謂一向於所能境方可受得別解律儀，則此律儀應有增減，以所能境與非所能二類有情有轉易故，如是便有別解律儀離得捨緣有得捨過。彼說不然。如生草等先無後起或起已枯，於彼律儀無增無減。能不能境所得律儀，境轉易時例亦應爾。彼言不爾。所以者何？以諸有情前後性等、草等前後性不同故。若爾，有情般涅槃已，如前性類今時既無，於彼律儀如何無減？故如是釋，於理不然。前所說因於理為善。若爾，前佛及所度生已涅槃者，後佛於彼既不發得別解律儀，如何尸羅無減前過？以一切佛別解律儀，皆從一切有情處得。設彼有情今猶在者，後佛從彼亦得律儀，故後尸羅無減前過。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Vaibhāṣika offer this account: if one held that the restraint of individual liberation could only be received with respect to reachable objects, then this restraint would have to increase and decrease, since beings constantly shift between the two categories of reachable and unreachable — and this would incur the fault of the restraint of individual liberation being gained and lost apart from any actual condition of gaining or relinquishing it. [Response:] This is not so. Just as sprouting grass that did not exist before, or that has arisen and then withered, causes neither increase nor decrease in restraint already obtained with respect to it, restraint obtained with respect to reachable and unreachable objects should likewise be understood, when such objects shift, in just the same way. [Objection:] This is not comparable. Why is this so? Because sentient beings' earlier and later natures, and grass's earlier and later natures, are not alike. [Further objection:] If this is so, then once a being has attained final nirvāṇa, and their prior mode of existence no longer exists at present, how could this cause no decrease in restraint with respect to them? [Reply:] Hence such an explanation is not sound in principle; the reason stated earlier remains the sound one. [Objection:] If this is so, then, with respect to a former buddha and the beings he liberated who have already attained final nirvāṇa, a later buddha does not generate the restraint of individual liberation toward them — so how could there be no decrease in the later buddha's śīla compared to the former? [Reply:] This is because every buddha's restraint of individual liberation is obtained with respect to sentient beings in general; even supposing that being still existed, the later buddha would likewise obtain restraint with respect to them — hence there is no fault of the later śīla being diminished relative to the former.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely subtle metaphysical move, and we want to flag it plainly: the Vaibhāṣika's final resolution effectively de-couples restraint's "universal scope" from any actual, currently-existing population of beings at all — restraint covers the general category "sentient being" (understood almost counterfactually, as "any being that does or could exist, reachable or not, existing now or not") rather than a running tally of particular individuals that would need constant updating as beings are born, die, or attain nirvāṇa. This is what allows the treatise to say, without contradiction, that a later buddha's restraint suffers no "deficit" relative to an earlier buddha's, even though the specific individuals covered by each buddha's restraint are, strictly speaking, not identical sets.

We should note honestly that the dialogue's structure here (with several rounds of objection, partial concession, and further objection) leaves the grass analogy itself somewhat under strain by the end — the text acknowledges the disanalogy between grass and sentient beings without fully resolving it point-by-point, instead pivoting to the deeper "restraint tracks the general category, not the individual" principle as the real answer. We have preserved this dialectical structure rather than smoothing it into a cleaner resolution than the source itself provides.

6. Non-Restraint's Universal Scope, and Who Lives in It

Having finished with how restraint is obtained, the treatise turns to non-restraint, and finds a structural parallel: non-restraint, too, is always obtained with respect to every single sentient being and every single karma-path branch at once — there is no such thing as partial non-restraint covering only some beings or omitting some branches — since the three grades of intensity (lower, middling, superior) never co-occur simultaneously as causes. This last point has a striking practical consequence: someone who originally took up a life of wrongdoing with only a lower-grade intensity of mind, but later, on a specific occasion, kills a being with a superior-grade intensity of mind, still counts, overall, as possessing only the lower-grade non-restraint as their basic condition, even while that one particular act of killing itself counts as a superior-grade manifested act — the same pattern holding for middling-grade cases as well. The treatise then names exactly who counts as "living in non-restraint": those who slaughter sheep, chickens, or pigs for a living; bird-catchers and fishermen; hunters; robbers; executioners; prison wardens; snake-charmers who bind and handle serpents; those who boil dogs [for meat]; trap-setters; and, by extension, kings and officials who administer corporal punishment, along with anyone else whose role involves passing judgment and inflicting punishment on others — provided they carry out these roles with a constant, underlying intention to harm. Someone earns the specific designation "non-restraint" by belonging, as a settled way of life, to one of these categories; to illustrate with the example of a sheep-butcher specifically, this means someone who has committed, for the sake of their livelihood, to a lifelong intention to kill sheep — and every other occupation on the list should be understood analogously.

Chinese Source

已說從彼得諸律儀。得不律儀定從一切有情業道，無少分境及不具支不律儀者。此定無有，由一切因下品等心無俱起故。若有一類由下品心得不律儀，後於異時由上品心斷眾生命，彼但成就下不律儀，亦成殺生上品表等。中品上品例此應知。此中何名不律儀者，謂諸屠羊屠鷄屠猪捕鳥捕魚、獵獸劫盜魁膾典獄、縛龍煮狗及罝弶等。等言類顯王典刑罰及餘聽察斷罪等人，但恒有害心。名不律儀者，由彼一類住不律儀。或有不律儀名不律儀者，言屠羊者，謂為活命要期盡壽恒欲害羊。餘隨所應當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

We have already discussed how these restraints are obtained. Non-restraint is certainly obtained with respect to all sentient beings and all karma-path branches; there is no case of non-restraint covering only a partial object or lacking complete branches. This is certainly so, because a lower-grade mind and the others [middling, superior] never arise together as causes all at once. If there is a person who obtains non-restraint through a lower-grade mind, and, on some later occasion, kills a sentient being through a superior-grade mind, that person still only possesses the lower-grade non-restraint as their basic condition, while also having accomplished a superior-grade manifested killing act on that particular occasion. The same should be understood, by analogy, for middling- and superior-grade cases. What, in this context, is meant by "living in non-restraint"? It refers to those who slaughter sheep, slaughter chickens, slaughter pigs, catch birds, catch fish, hunt beasts, rob and steal, act as executioners, oversee prisons, bind serpents, boil dogs, and set traps, and so on. The word "and so on" is meant to include kings who administer corporal punishment and others engaged in hearing cases and pronouncing judgment, so long as they constantly harbor a mind bent on harm. One earns the name "non-restraint" by belonging, as one of this class, to a settled state of non-restraint. As for what makes a "sheep-butcher" specifically earn the name "non-restraint": it refers to one who, for the sake of their livelihood, has committed with a fixed resolve, for their entire remaining life, to a constant wish to kill sheep. The rest, as appropriate, should be understood in just the same way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section's opening point mirrors, in reverse, the earlier discussion of restraint's own uniform scope from a few sections back — just as taking a partial, limited vow (only some beings, only some branches) fails to generate real restraint at all, only an imitation of good conduct, committing to a life of wrongdoing with any partial exception would likewise fail to generate real non-restraint. The parallel is not accidental: both restraint and non-restraint, in this system, are defined by the totality of an underlying disposition, not by any partial or occasional pattern of behavior.

The lower/middling/superior-grade distinction here produces a genuinely counter-intuitive but carefully argued result: a habitual wrongdoer's baseline non-restraint is fixed by the intensity of mind present when they first committed to their way of life, and does not get upgraded merely because a specific later act happens to be carried out with unusually intense resolve — the specific act's own manifested karma can be graded independently (superior-grade, in this example), while the person's ongoing underlying condition of non-restraint remains whatever grade was originally established. This is the mirror image of the earlier point (in fascicle 14) that an arhat's original vow-grade does not retroactively upgrade even after later spiritual attainment — grade, once fixed at the moment of commitment, persists as a stable background condition largely independent of what happens afterward.

7. Why a Butcher's Non-Restraint Covers Even Their Own Kin

A pointed objection now challenges the claim that non-restraint, like restraint, must cover every sentient being without exception: a sheep-butcher plainly harbors no wish to harm their own close family, and would not wish to kill even to save their own life — so how can their non-restraint really be said to extend to literally everyone, including their own most beloved relatives? The treatise answers that non-restraint's universality rests on the totality of a corrupted disposition (阿世耶, āśaya) willingly adopted for the sake of profit, not on a moment-by-moment inventory of who the person would actually be willing to harm right now — and drives the point further with two even sharper test cases. Might the butcher's close relative, in a future life, actually be reborn as a sheep? If so, doesn't the butcher's non-restraint have to extend even to that future rebirth, and, by symmetry, doesn't a future noble one's present-life self also fall within the butcher's non-restraint's scope now, precisely because that future noble one was once, hypothetically, an ordinary sheep? The treatise resolves this apparent absurdity by insisting non-restraint is not actually indexed to any being's particular future continuum at all — it targets currently-existing sheep as such, in the present, which is precisely why a noble one's present body, never having been and never going to be an actual sheep, falls outside its scope, while an actual present-day sheep falls within it regardless of any relationship the butcher might unknowingly have to it.

Chinese Source

遍於有情界得諸律儀，其理可爾，由普欲利樂勝阿世耶而受得故，非屠羊等不律儀人於己至親有損害意，乃至為救自身命緣亦不欲殺，如何可說普於一切得不律儀？由彼至親若為羊等，於彼亦可有損害心。既知至親現非羊等，如何於彼可有害心？又聖必無作羊等理，如何於彼得不律儀？若觀未來羊等自體，於現相續得不律儀。是則羊等於未來世，亦有至親及聖自體，於彼決定無損害心。是則應觀未來自體，不於現在得不律儀。於羊等現身既有害意，如何不於彼得不律儀？於母等現身既無害意，如何亦於彼得不律儀？於等事中應求異理。

Ru-Yi Translation

That restraint is obtained universally with respect to the realm of sentient beings makes sense, since it is received by virtue of a supreme disposition wishing benefit and happiness for all universally. [Objection:] But a person living in the non-restraint of, say, a sheep-butcher, harbors no intention to harm their own closest kin, and would not wish to kill even to save their own life — how, then, can it be said that they obtain non-restraint universally with respect to all beings? [Reply:] Because, were that close relative to become a sheep or the like, there could indeed be a harmful intention toward them as well. [Objection:] But since one knows one's close relative is not presently a sheep or the like, how could there be a harmful intention toward them [now]? Moreover, a noble one could certainly never become a sheep or the like — so how could non-restraint be obtained with respect to them? [Reply, offering one possible resolution:] If one considers the future embodiment of that sheep and the like, non-restraint is obtained with respect to it within the present continuum. [Objection to this resolution:] But then a sheep and the like, in some future life, would also have close relatives and noble beings among them, toward whom there could certainly be no harmful intention — so, by this logic, one would have to consider a future embodiment [in every direction], and non-restraint would not, in fact, be obtained in the present at all. [Further pressing the point:] Since there is indeed harmful intent toward the present body of an actual sheep, how could non-restraint fail to be obtained with respect to it? And since there is no harmful intent toward the present body of one's mother and the like, how could non-restraint also be obtained with respect to them? A different resolution should be sought for these parallel cases.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should be transparent that this passage, as translated here, ends on an unresolved note — the final line explicitly calls for "a different resolution," acknowledging that the "future embodiment" solution just proposed does not actually work, without yet supplying the resolution that does. This is consistent with this treatise's broader willingness to let a difficult philosophical puzzle sit open for a beat before continuing, and we have preserved that open ending rather than importing a premature resolution the Chinese itself does not yet provide at this point in the text.

The deeper principle at stake, even without a fully worked-out answer yet, is the same one already seen in the earlier discussion of restraint's own universal scope (section 5 of this fascicle): moral commitments in this system are not indexed to a case-by-case inventory of the specific beings one happens to currently recognize or care about, but to a totalizing disposition (whether wholesome or corrupted) extended, as a matter of logical structure, to the entire category of relevant beings — present, actual sheep in this case — regardless of the person's own incomplete or mistaken beliefs about which individuals fall into that category. The puzzle here is precisely about how to state that totalizing structure precisely enough to include current sheep without absurdly including hypothetical future or past embodiments of every being whatsoever.

8. The Mute Butcher, and Whether Non-Restraint Can Ever Be Partial

A further puzzle presses the completeness of non-restraint from another angle: a sheep-butcher who, throughout their entire life, never once takes what is not given, remains content with their own spouse, and — being mute and physically incapable of speech — commits none of the four kinds of verbal wrongdoing at all, would seem to lack any actual basis for the seven bodily-and-verbal branches of bad conduct their non-restraint is supposed to comprise in full. The treatise resolves this by insisting that non-restraint's completeness tracks the totality of a corrupted underlying disposition, not the actual physical performance of every branch of wrongdoing — even a mute person still forms the bodily gestures corresponding to whatever meaning they would have wished to express in speech, and this is sufficient to complete the full branch-count. This immediately raises a mirroring question in the opposite direction: if someone first formally commits to only two or three training-points and only later, informally, takes up killing as well without ever explicitly renouncing goodness with respect to the other branches, how could they be said to have generated the complete sevenfold bad precept? Here the treatise records a genuine, unresolved disagreement: the Vaibhāṣika hold firmly that there is no such thing as a partially-branched or partially-scoped person "living in non-restraint" — it is always complete or not present at all — while the Sautrāntika masters hold instead that non-restraint, like restraint, precisely tracks whatever scope was actually committed to, whether complete or partial, with only the upavāsa vow exempted from this flexibility, since its wholesome and unwholesome character is fixed by mutual exclusion at a specific measure.

Chinese Source

又屠羊等不律儀人，於一生中不與不取，於己妻妾住知足心，瘂不能言無語四過，如何彼亦得具支不律儀？彼遍損善阿世耶故。雖瘂不言，而身表語所欲說義，故得具支。若爾，彼人或時先受二三學處，後但受殺，於餘不損善阿世耶，如何具發七支惡戒？毘婆沙者作如是言：必無缺支及餘一分可得名住不律儀人。經部諸師作如是說：隨所期限支具不具及全分一分皆得不律儀。律儀亦然，唯除八戒，由隨彼量善惡尸羅性相相違互相遮故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, a person living in the non-restraint of a sheep-butcher who, throughout a single lifetime, never takes what is not given, remains content with their own spouse, and, being mute and unable to speak, commits none of the four faults of speech — how could such a person still obtain complete non-restraint across all its branches? [Reply:] Because they have thoroughly ruined their wholesome disposition across the board. Although mute and unable to speak, the bodily gesture corresponding to whatever meaning they would wish to express through speech is still present, and hence the branches are obtained in full. [Objection:] If this is so, then such a person might, at some point, first receive two or three training-points, and only later take up killing alone, without having ruined their wholesome disposition with respect to the remaining branches — how, then, could the complete sevenfold bad precept be generated? The Vaibhāṣika state: there is certainly no such thing as a person "living in non-restraint" who is missing branches or possesses only a partial portion. The Sautrāntika masters state: in accordance with whatever limit was actually committed to, whether the branches are complete or incomplete, and whether the full scope or only a partial scope, non-restraint is obtained accordingly. The same holds for restraint as well, except for the eightfold [upavāsa] precept, since its wholesome or unwholesome character, matched to its own fixed measure, is mutually exclusive and blocks the other from arising.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We want to flag this as a genuinely unresolved, named disagreement between two schools, exactly as this treatise records it — the Vaibhāṣika's all-or-nothing view and the Sautrāntika's scope-matches-commitment view are simply set side by side, with the text moving on to the next topic without adjudicating between them. This mirrors the pattern already seen repeatedly in fascicle 13's debate over unmanifested karma's very existence: rather than treat every doctrinal question as settled Vaibhāṣika orthodoxy, this fascicle continues to preserve visible cracks in the tradition's own internal consensus, and we have kept both positions stated in full rather than silently favoring one.

The mute-butcher resolution — that a bodily gesture "corresponding to whatever meaning [the mute person] would wish to express through speech" substitutes for actual speech in completing the verbal branches — is a fascinating extension of this treatise's much earlier discussion (fascicle 13) of manifested verbal karma as fundamentally about intended meaning rather than the literal physical act of vocalizing; here that same principle is put to work explaining how someone physically incapable of speech can nonetheless be said to have "spoken" wrongly in the technical, karma-relevant sense, purely through the gestural expression of an intended verbal meaning.

9. How Non-Restraint Is Obtained, and Three Causes of Ordinary Merit

The treatise now explains the mechanics by which non-restraint itself actually comes to be obtained, identifying two distinct routes. The first is being born into a family already engaged in a livelihood built on wrongdoing (a family of hunters or butchers, say) and then, for the first time, actually carrying out the preparatory actions leading to killing or the like. The second route does not require such a family background at all: anyone, regardless of birth, who makes a firm, forward-looking commitment — resolving, "I shall take up such-and-such an occupation, in order to acquire wealth and sustain my own life" — thereby generates the bad precept-vow at that very moment of commitment, prior to ever actually performing the act itself. The treatise then turns to a related but distinct topic: the three separate causes by which other kinds of unmanifested karma, such as ordinary merit, can arise outside the context of restraint or non-restraint altogether. The first is a "field" of merit — making a donation, such as a garden or grove, to a worthy recipient, which generates wholesome unmanifested karma the very instant the gift is given, matching material discussed earlier regarding merit tied to a material support. The second is a formal vow — resolving, for instance, "I shall not eat before first paying homage to the Buddha," or setting a recurring commitment to provide food offerings on fast days, half-month intervals, or annual occasions. The third is repeated, wholeheartedly earnest intention — cultivating a habitually solemn and dedicated resolve, whether directed toward wholesome or unwholesome ends. These three causes, distinct from the mechanisms behind restraint and non-restraint proper, are what give rise to this broader category of "other" unmanifested karma.

We have discussed how non-restraint is obtained from this. As for how non-restraint and other unmanifested karma are obtained by what means, this has not yet been discussed and should now be explained. Verse: non-restraint is obtained through performance or through vowed reception; other unmanifested karma is obtained through a field, through a [formal] undertaking, or through solemn conduct. Prose: non-restraint is obtained through two causes. First, being born into a family of non-restraint, through the initial actual performance of preparatory actions leading to killing and the like. Second, even when born into some other family, through initially making a firm commitment to undertake matters such as killing — thinking, "I shall engage in such-and-such an undertaking, in order to seek wealth and sustain my own life" — at which very moment the bad precept-vow is generated. Other unmanifested karma is obtained through three causes. First, through a field — meaning that, with respect to a worthy field of merit to whom a garden, grove, or the like is given, wholesome unmanifested karma arises the very instant the gift is given, as already discussed with respect to meritorious deeds having a support. Second, through a formal undertaking — meaning making a personal vow, such as, "I shall not eat before first paying homage to the Buddha," or establishing a fixed commitment, such as regularly providing food offerings on fast days, half-month intervals, and annual occasions. Third, through solemn conduct — meaning giving rise to earnest, solemn intention in carrying out wholesome or unwholesome conduct. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between non-restraint's two routes of origin — inherited family occupation versus a deliberate, individual vow to take up wrongdoing — mirrors, almost exactly, the earlier fascicle's discussion of how the vow of individual liberation is obtained "through another's instruction": in both cases, the treatise is careful to specify the precise moment a lasting karmic disposition actually crystallizes, rather than leaving it as a vague matter of gradually drifting into a way of life. Someone born into a family of hunters does not automatically possess non-restraint from birth — it is only generated at the moment they themselves first actually perform the relevant preparatory action — while someone from any background at all can generate the very same non-restraint purely through an internal, forward-looking resolution, with no actual act yet performed.

This section's three causes for "other" unmanifested karma (field, vow, solemn conduct) directly cross-reference material discussed at length in fascicle 13's proof of unmanifested karma's existence — specifically the "merit continuing to increase even without conscious thought" proof, which relied on exactly this kind of donation-based merit generation. Readers who recall that earlier discussion will notice this section is filling in the mechanical "how" behind a phenomenon that fascicle 13 had, at that point, only been arguing must exist at all.

10. Four Causes by Which the Vow of Individual Liberation Is Lost

Having finished with how restraint and non-restraint are gained, the treatise turns to how they are lost, beginning with the vow of individual liberation. For every one of its seven kinds except the upavāsa vow, the restraint is lost through any of four causes: deliberately renouncing the training-points, through a manifested verbal act performed in the presence of someone capable of understanding that renunciation; discarding one's present existence altogether, that is, dying; a spontaneous, simultaneous arising of both sexual characteristics in one body; or having one's wholesome roots entirely severed. The upavāsa vow is lost through these same four causes, plus a fifth unique to it: the simple passing of the night, marking the natural end of its fixed one-day duration. Hence, taken together, the vow of individual liberation as a whole is said to be lost through five possible causes. Each cause disrupts the restraint in a structurally distinct way: a manifested act contrary to the original act of reception directly reverses it; death removes the very physical basis the restraint depended on; the sex-change scenario alters that basis without removing it entirely; the severing of wholesome roots eliminates the underlying cause that sustained the restraint; and, for the upavāsa vow alone, the fixed time-limit set at reception simply expires.

Chinese Source

The upavāsa precept is relinquished through these same four causes, plus the passing of the night — hence, taken as a whole, the restraint of individual liberation is said to be relinquished through five causes. Reading note — Ru-Yi

We have kept "taming" (調伏) as the verse's own chosen term for restraint here, since the prose immediately explains why this particular word was selected over the more usual 律儀 — it emphasizes restraint's active, ongoing function of bringing the sense faculties under control, rather than simply naming the vow as a static possession. This is a small but deliberate stylistic choice on the treatise's part, using a more dynamic near-synonym specifically at the point where the vow's eventual loss is being discussed, perhaps to underscore that what is being lost is precisely this active, controlling function.

The five-cause structure is worth holding in mind as a template, since each cause maps onto a different one of the four explanatory factors introduced earlier in this fascicle for why restraint is obtained (support, cause, act of reception) plus the special time-boundary unique to upavāsa — a manifested renunciation is simply the reception act's exact mirror image; death removes the physical support restraint depends on; sex-change alters (without destroying) that same support; and severed wholesome roots remove the causal condition sustaining the restraint altogether. This systematic one-to-one correspondence between causes of gain and causes of loss is characteristic of this treatise's method throughout the fascicle.

11. Grave Offenses, the Disappearance of the Dharma, and the Debt Analogy

Beyond the four (or five) causes already given, other schools propose additional ways the vow of individual liberation might be lost. Some hold that committing even one of the four gravest offenses (the pārājika offenses, which incur expulsion from the monastic community) also causes a bhikṣu or śrāmaṇera to lose their restraint entirely. Others hold that the eventual disappearance of the true Dharma itself brings an end to the vow of individual liberation for everyone still holding it, since, once the Dharma has vanished, all its institutional supports — the bounded monastic territories, formal procedures, and the like — cease to function altogether. The Vaibhāṣika of Kashmir reject the first of these additions outright: committing a grave offense, they argue, does not actually cause the restraint itself to be lost — otherwise, the traditional biweekly recitation of the monastic code would not include the specific provision that a monk who has committed a grave offense should "receive the remaining restraints anew," since this instruction presupposes that some restraint survives the offense to be added to. They offer a vivid analogy: committing a grave offense is like a debt that has gone unpaid — even though the debt has technically lapsed, if the debtor later comes into the means to repay it, the earlier debt does not need to be created anew from scratch. In just the same way, a monk who has committed a grave offense still retains their underlying restraint; if they properly confess before the assembly according to the correct procedure, their earlier transgression is simply cleared, without any need to take the full ordination vow over again from the beginning.

Chinese Source

有餘部說：於四極重感墮罪中若隨犯一，亦捨勤策苾芻律儀。有餘部言：由正法滅亦能令捨別解律儀，以法滅時一切學處結界羯磨等悉皆止息故。迦濕彌羅國毘婆沙師言：犯根本罪不捨律儀，若不爾者半月半月說戒經中不應說言：犯根本罪苾芻應更受得所餘律儀。犯根本罪如負財捨，雖負財捨而彼於後力還財時，前債即無重興債法。如是苾芻雖犯重罪而律儀在，若更如法對僧懺悔，先過即除，不新受戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Other schools say: committing even one of the four extremely grave offenses that incur expulsion also causes the śrāmaṇera or bhikṣu restraint to be relinquished. Other schools say: the disappearance of the true Dharma is also able to cause the restraint of individual liberation to be relinquished, since, once the Dharma has disappeared, all training-points, bounded territories, formal procedures, and the like entirely cease to function. The Vaibhāṣika of Kashmir say: committing a root offense does not cause the restraint to be relinquished — if it did, then the biweekly recitation of the precepts should not state that a bhikṣu who has committed a root offense should receive the remaining restraints anew. Committing a root offense is like a debt being relinquished: although the debt is relinquished, when the debtor later has the means to repay it, there is no need to newly re-establish the earlier debt. In just this way, although a bhikṣu has committed a grave offense, the restraint remains; if they properly confess before the saṅgha in accordance with the proper method, the earlier transgression is thereby removed, without newly receiving the precept.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The debt analogy is doing precise conceptual work worth spelling out: it distinguishes between a debt (or vow) being formally "written off" in some technical sense and the underlying relationship being entirely dissolved — a written-off debt can still be honored later without the parties needing to draft an entirely new loan agreement, and, similarly, a monk's restraint survives a grave offense in some underlying sense even while the offense itself is a serious violation requiring formal confession to resolve. The scriptural evidence marshaled for this view is a nice piece of close textual reasoning: the very existence of a formal procedure for "receiving the remaining restraints anew" after a grave offense presupposes that something of the original restraint must still be there to be added to or restored, since one cannot "add to" or "restore" something that has been completely and simply annihilated.

We should note that this section presents three genuinely distinct positions on what ends the vow of individual liberation — the base four-or-five-cause list from the previous section, an additional "grave offense" cause proposed by unnamed other schools, and a further "disappearance of the Dharma" cause from yet other schools — with the Kashmir Vaibhāṣika explicitly rejecting the middle position while remaining silent, in this passage at least, on the third. Readers should not assume the debt analogy is meant to also refute the Dharma-disappearance view; the text simply does not address that question here.
