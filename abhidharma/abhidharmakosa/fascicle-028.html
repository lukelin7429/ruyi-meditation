The Four Dhyānas

Fascicle 28 opens Chapter 8 (分別定品第八之一, Distinguishing Concentration), the treatise's account of the meditative foundations underlying everything discussed in the two preceding chapters on knowledge — beginning with the four dhyānas themselves, each admitting of two senses, as a cultivated state and as the realm one is born into as its result, and a close technical debate over what "concentration" actually is at the most basic level.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 15 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 28 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. What Concentration Actually Is: A Real Factor, or Just the Mind Itself?

Having finished the virtues accomplished specifically by knowledge across fascicles 26 and 27, the treatise turns to the remaining virtues, organized by nature rather than by the knowledge that produces them — beginning with the concentration that supports them all: the four dhyānas.

Chinese Source

已說諸智所成功德，餘性功德今次當辯，於中先辯所依止定。且諸定內靜慮云何？頌曰： 靜慮四各二， 於中生已說， 定謂善一境， 并伴五蘊性， 初具伺喜樂， 後漸離前支。 論曰：一切功德多依靜慮，故應先辯靜慮差別。此總有四種，謂初二三四，四各有二，謂定及生。生靜慮體世品已說，謂第四八，前三各三。定靜慮體總而言之是善性攝心一境性，以善等持為自性故；若并助伴，五蘊為性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having explained the virtues accomplished by knowledge, we should now discuss the remaining virtues by nature; among these, let us first discuss the concentration that serves as their support. Among the various kinds of concentration, what is meant by dhyāna? Verse: the dhyānas are four, each of two kinds; of these, the sense of birth has already been explained; concentration means a wholesome one-pointedness of mind, together with its accompaniments the nature of the five aggregates; the first possesses inquiry, joy, and bliss, and each subsequent one gradually sheds the preceding factors. Explanation: since most virtues depend on the dhyānas, we should first distinguish the varieties of dhyāna. These number four in total — the first, second, third, and fourth — each having two senses: as concentration, a cultivated meditative state, and as birth, the realm one is born into as its result. The substance of birth-dhyāna was already explained in the chapter on the world: the fourth dhyāna comprises eight heavens, and each of the first three comprises three. The substance of concentration-dhyāna, stated in general, is a one-pointedness of mind included within the wholesome nature, since it has wholesome concentration as its intrinsic nature; including its accompanying factors, its nature is the five aggregates.

The treatise then works through a genuinely close technical debate: is "one-pointedness of mind" a real, distinct mental factor, or merely another name for the mind's own momentary and continuing focus?

Chinese Source

何名一境性？謂專一所緣。若爾，即心專一境位，依之建立三摩地名，不應別有餘心所法。別法令心於一境轉名三摩地，非體即心。豈不諸心剎那滅故皆一境轉，何用等持？若謂令心於第二念不散亂故須有等持，則於相應等持無用。又由此故三摩地成。寧不即由斯心於一境轉？又三摩地是大地法，應一切心皆一境轉。不爾，餘品等持劣故。有餘師說：即心一境相續轉時名三摩地，契經說此為增上心學故，心清淨最勝即四靜慮故。依何義故立靜慮名？由此寂靜能審慮故。審慮即是實了知義，如說心在定，能如實了知，審慮義中置地界故。此宗審慮以慧為體。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by one-pointedness? Exclusive focus on a single object. If this is so, then the name samādhi is established with reference to this very state of mind, and there should be no additional, separate mental factor involved. A distinct dharma that causes the mind to operate upon a single object is what is called samādhi — its substance is not simply identical with the mind itself. But since every mind-moment ceases within a single instant, does not every mind thereby operate on a single object trivially, within its own moment? What use, then, is a separate factor of concentration? If it is said that concentration is needed to prevent the mind, at the next moment, from becoming scattered, then this concentration would be useless with respect to the very moment it is itself associated with. Moreover, it is by virtue of this continuity across moments that samādhi is established — but is this not simply the mind itself continuing to operate on a single object, without needing any separate real dharma? Moreover, samādhi is classified as one of the great-ground mental factors present in every mind, so every mind should equally operate one-pointedly — yet this is not so, since concentration in other, less cultivated categories of mind is weaker. Other teachers say: it is precisely when the mind continues operating on a single object in succession that this is called samādhi, since a sūtra calls this "the training of the heightened mind," and the mind's purest, most excellent state is precisely the four dhyānas. On what basis is the name dhyāna established? Because, through this stillness, one is able to carefully examine and contemplate. Careful examination means truly and accurately knowing, as it is said: "the mind, being in concentration, is able to know truly" — the sense of examination carries this same meaning. According to this position, careful examination has wisdom as its substance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag this opening debate as returning to a foundational question this project has encountered before, in different guises, across earlier fascicles' treatment of the mental factors dissociated from mind and the "great-ground" dharmas established in fascicle 10: is a given quality of experience (here, one-pointed concentration) a genuinely distinct, real dharma that co-arises alongside the mind, or merely a descriptive label for how the mind itself happens to be behaving? The standard Abhidharma position insists samādhi is real and separate, needed to explain continuity across successive mind-moments rather than the trivial "one-pointedness" any single instant already has by definition; the objection presses hard on why, if this is a universally present factor, some minds are so clearly more concentrated than others.

We should also flag the closing etymological move — deriving "dhyāna" from "careful examination" (審慮), itself glossed as "true knowing," with wisdom as its substance — as setting up dhyāna's definition to already anticipate its close relationship with insight and knowledge, a relationship that has run throughout the two preceding fascicles' account of what dhyāna makes possible (the ten powers, the supernormal powers, and more), now finally receiving its own foundational treatment.

2. Why "Dhyāna" Names Only the Superior Kind, and How the Four Are Distinguished

The treatise closes out its definition of dhyāna by explaining why the name applies only to a superior class of concentration, addressing the apparent counterexample of "evil dhyāna," and then showing exactly how the four dhyānas are distinguished from one another — not by any difference in one-pointedness itself, but by which accompanying branch-factors each one still retains.

Chinese Source

若爾，諸等持皆應名靜慮。不爾，唯勝方立此名。如世間言發光名日，非螢燭等亦得日名。靜慮如何獨名為勝？諸等持內唯此攝支，止觀均行最能審慮，得現法樂住及樂通行名，故此等持獨名靜慮。若爾，染污寧得此名？由彼亦能邪審慮故，是則應有太過之失。無太過失，要相似中方立名故，如敗種等。世尊亦說有惡靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

If this is so, every concentration should be called dhyāna. Not so — only the superior kind receives this name. Just as, in ordinary speech, what emits light is called "the sun," yet fireflies and candles, though they too emit light, do not thereby receive the name "sun." Why is dhyāna alone called superior? Among all forms of concentration, only this one possesses branch-factors, with calm and insight operating in balance, most capable of careful examination, receiving the names joyful abiding in the present life and pleasant conduct — hence this concentration alone is named dhyāna. If this is so, how can even a defiled state receive this name? Because that too is capable of a kind of false careful examination — but then would this not incur the fault of overextending the name too broadly? There is no such fault, since the name is established only with reference to what resembles the genuine article, as when a spoiled seed is still called a "seed." The Blessed One himself also spoke of "evil dhyāna," confirming this extended usage.

Chinese Source

若一境性是靜慮體，依何相立初二三四？具伺喜樂建立為初，由此已明亦具尋義，必俱行故，如煙與火。非伺有喜樂，而不與尋俱。漸離前支立二三四，離伺有二，離二有樂，具離三種。如其次第，故一境性分為四種。已辯靜慮。無色云何？

Ru-Yi Translation

If one-pointedness is dhyāna's substance, on what basis are the first, second, third, and fourth established as distinct? The dhyāna possessing inquiry, joy, and bliss is established as the first — and by this it is already clear that it also possesses investigation, since inquiry and investigation necessarily occur together, like smoke and fire; there is no case of inquiry, joy, and bliss occurring without investigation accompanying them. Gradually shedding the preceding branch-factors, the second, third, and fourth are established: having shed investigation and inquiry while still possessing joy and bliss is the second; having shed those and joy as well, while still possessing bliss alone, is the third; having shed all three is the fourth. In this sequence, one-pointedness divides into four kinds. The dhyānas have now been explained. What of the formless attainments?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "sun versus firefly" and "spoiled seed" similes as doing complementary work: the first explains why "dhyāna" names only concentration's most refined, branch-possessing form, not concentration in general, while the second immediately qualifies this by explaining how the tradition can still speak of "evil dhyāna" without contradiction — a spoiled seed is still called a seed by family resemblance to the genuine article, even though it will never sprout, and a defiled concentration capable of its own distorted kind of "careful examination" earns the name the same way, by resemblance rather than by actually possessing the virtue the name usually implies.

We should also flag the progressive shedding of branch-factors (investigation-and-inquiry, then joy, then bliss) as the mechanism the treatise uses to distinguish all four dhyānas using a single underlying substance — one-pointedness of mind never changes across the four, only the company it keeps changes, growing progressively quieter and more stripped-down at each stage. Investigation and inquiry are treated as an inseparable pair throughout (illustrated by the smoke-and-fire simile), which is why the first dhyāna's verse only names "inquiry" explicitly yet is understood to include investigation as well.

3. The Four Formless Attainments: Classification and Naming

Having finished the dhyānas, the treatise turns to the four formless attainments, which mirror the dhyānas' basic structure (four levels, each with a "concentration" and "birth" sense) but differ in one crucial respect: they lack form entirely, and each is defined by having left the ground immediately below it.

Chinese Source

無色云何？頌曰： 無色亦如是， 四蘊離下地， 并上三近分， 總名除色想， 無色謂無色， 後色起從心。 空無邊等三， 名從加行立， 非想非非想， 昧劣故立名。 論曰：此與靜慮數自性同，謂四各二。生如前說，即世品說由生有四。定無色體，總而言之亦善性攝心一境性。依此故說亦如是言。然助伴中此除色蘊，無色無有隨轉色故。雖一境性體相無差，離下地生故分四種。謂若已離第四靜慮生立空無邊處，乃至已離無所有處生立非想非非想處。離名何義？謂由此道解脫下地惑，是離下染義。即此四根本并上三近分，總說名為除去色想。空處近分未得此名，緣下地色起色想故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What of the formless attainments? Verse: the formless attainments are likewise so, four aggregates in substance, each established by leaving behind the ground below; together with the upper three approach-stages, they are collectively called eliminating the perception of form; "formless" means without form, and any later rebirth into form arises purely from the mind; infinite space and the other two are named from their preparatory practice; neither-perception-nor-non-perception is named for being dim and weak. Explanation: these are the same in number and intrinsic nature as the dhyānas — four, each with two senses. The sense of birth is as explained before, in the chapter on the world, which states there are four by birth. The substance of concentration-formless-attainment, stated in general, is likewise a one-pointedness of mind included within the wholesome nature — hence they are said to be "likewise so." Among the accompanying factors, however, this excludes the aggregate of form, since the formless attainments have no accompanying form. Although one-pointedness's own substance shows no difference across the four, they divide into four kinds because each arises upon leaving behind the ground immediately below it: once free of the fourth dhyāna, the resulting attainment is the sphere of infinite space; and so on, up to: once free of the sphere of nothing-at-all, the resulting attainment is the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. What does "leaving behind" mean? Through this path, one is liberated from the affliction of the lower ground — this is the meaning of leaving behind what is below. These four root attainments, together with the upper three approach-stages, are, in total, called "eliminating the perception of form." The approach-stage to the sphere of infinite space does not yet receive this name, since it still takes the lower ground's form as object, still giving rise to the perception of form.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the precise scope of "eliminating the perception of form" as a carefully drawn boundary: it covers the four formless attainments themselves plus the preparatory approach-stages to the second, third, and fourth of them, but explicitly excludes the approach-stage to the very first, the sphere of infinite space — since that particular threshold moment still involves contemplating the lower, form-based ground one is leaving, and so has not yet actually eliminated the perception of form the way everything past it has.

We should also flag the closing verse's claim that "any later rebirth into form arises purely from the mind" as a genuinely striking cosmological detail this passage states without elaborating — a being who has died out of the formless realm and is later reborn with form again does not require any physical, form-based cause to bridge the gap, since the mind alone is sufficient to generate the transition. We expect the treatise to expand on this point, and on the deeper question of whether the formless realm really contains no form whatsoever, in the discussion immediately following.

4. Does the Formless Realm Really Have No Form at All?

The treatise now opens one of its most rigorous debates: does "formless" mean the formless realm's inhabitants have literally no form whatsoever, or merely form so subtle it earns the name only loosely? The Vaibhāṣika masters take the second position — and immediately face a battery of pointed objections testing whether it can be made precise.

Chinese Source

皆無色故立無色名，此因不成，許有色故。若爾，何故立無色名？由彼色微故名無色，如微黃物亦名無黃。許彼界中色有何相？若彼唯有身語律儀。身語既無，律儀寧有？又無大種，何有造色？若謂如有無漏律儀。不爾，無漏依有漏大種故，又彼定中亦遮有故。若許於彼有色根身，如何可言彼色微少？若謂於彼身量小故，水細蟲極微亦應名無色，亦身量小不可見故。若謂彼身極清妙故，中有色界應名無色。若謂彼身清妙中極，應唯有頂得無色名。如定生身有勝劣故，又生靜慮所有色身非下地根所能取故，與彼何異不名無色？若謂欲色隨義立名，無色不然。此有何理？

Ru-Yi Translation

Because they entirely lack form, the name "formless" is established — this reasoning does not hold, since it is granted that there is form present. If this is so, why is the name "formless" established? Because that form is extremely subtle, it is called formless, just as something with only a trace of yellow is still called "without yellow." What characteristic does this granted form in that realm have? Suppose it consists only of bodily and verbal restraint. But bodily and verbal activity are themselves absent there — how, then, could such restraint exist? Moreover, that realm lacks the great elements — how, then, could there be any derived form? Suppose one replies that it is like how uncontaminated restraint exists without an ordinary karmic basis. Not so — uncontaminated restraint still depends on contaminated great elements, and, moreover, the existence of restraint is also specifically excluded within that formless concentration itself. If it is granted that there exists a form-based sense-faculty and body there, how can it be said that this form is merely "minute"? If it is said this is because the body's size is small there, then a water-mite should also be called "formless," since its body's size is likewise small and imperceptible. If it is said this is because that body is extremely pure and subtle, then intermediate-existence beings within the form realm should also be called "formless." If it is said that body is the utmost extreme of purity and subtlety, then only the peak of existence should receive the name "formless." Just as concentration-born bodies differ in superiority across levels, and a body born within the higher dhyānas cannot be perceived by a lower ground's faculties, how does the formless realm's case differ from this pattern? If it is said that "desire" and "form" are named loosely according to their predominant sense, while "formless" is not — what reasoning supports this distinction?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag this passage's method as a sustained reductio ad absurdum campaign against the "merely subtle form" reading: each proposed criterion for why formless-realm form counts as effectively "no form" — small body size, extreme purity, imperceptibility from below — is tested against a parallel case (a water-mite, an intermediate-existence being, the upper dhyānas relative to lower ones) that satisfies the same criterion without anyone calling it "formless." If smallness alone justified the name, tiny creatures would qualify; if purity alone justified it, intermediate-existence beings would qualify; if imperceptibility from below alone justified it, every higher dhyāna would have to be called "formless" relative to a lower one, which the tradition never does. None of these parallels are accepted as grounds for the name elsewhere, so the objector argues none should be accepted here either.

We should also flag the specific objection about restraint (律儀, the "unmanifested karma" discussed extensively in earlier fascicles) as a technically precise challenge: if the formless realm's subtle form is supposed to consist of bodily and verbal restraint, this cannot work, since restraint is by definition a restraint on bodily and verbal action — and the formless realm, lacking any body or speech to restrain in the first place, cannot coherently be said to possess it. The attempted parallel to uncontaminated restraint (which exists without ordinary karmic mechanics) is also rejected, since even uncontaminated restraint still requires contaminated great elements as its physical basis, which the formless realm likewise lacks.

5. Four Scriptural Citations, and a Lesson in Not Over-Generalizing a Sūtra

Having pressed the "merely subtle form" position hard, the treatise now turns to the scriptural case for it — four separate citations that seem to require form to accompany consciousness everywhere, including the formless realm. The response is a masterclass in hermeneutic caution: each citation is tested against exactly how far its own wording actually extends, with absurd consequences exposing where a naive, unqualified reading breaks down.

Chinese Source

若謂經說壽煖合故，又說名色與識相依如二蘆束相依住故，又說名色識為緣故，又遮離色乃至離行識有來有去故，由此無色有色理成。此證不成，應審思故。謂所引教應共審思，且契經言壽煖合者，為約一切界、為約欲界說？名色與識相依住者，為約一切界、為約欲色說？所說名色識為緣者，為說一切識皆為名色緣、為說名色生無不緣於識？遮離色至行識有來去者，為遮隨離一、為遮離一切？若謂契經言無簡別，不應於此更致審思。此說不然，太過失故。謂應外煖亦與壽合。又應外名色依識識為緣。又說四食如四識住，色無色界應有段食。若謂經說有一類天超段食故，又說彼天喜為食故，無斯過者，則無色界不應有色，契經說彼出離色故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If it is said that a sūtra states life and warmth are joined; and further states that name-and-form and consciousness depend on each other, like two bundles of reeds leaning on one another; and states that name-and-form serves as a condition for consciousness; and excludes the possibility of coming and going apart from form, through the formations-aggregate and consciousness — on these grounds, the position that the formless realm has form is established. This proof does not actually hold, and requires careful examination. The cited scriptural passages should be jointly examined: does the statement that life and warmth are joined apply with reference to all realms, or specifically to the desire realm? Does "name-and-form and consciousness depend on each other" apply with reference to all realms, or specifically to the desire and form realms? As for "name-and-form serves as a condition for consciousness" — does this mean every instance of consciousness is conditioned by name-and-form, or that name-and-form's own arising never occurs without depending on consciousness? As for excluding coming-and-going apart from form through the formations-aggregate and consciousness — does this exclude it apart from any single one of these, or only apart from all of them together? If it is said that the sūtra's own wording makes no such distinctions, and therefore no further careful examination should be applied — this is not correct, since it would incur the fault of over-extension. That is, external warmth would then also have to be "joined with life," and external name-and-form would have to depend on consciousness as its condition. Moreover, since a further passage states the four nutriments parallel the four abodes of consciousness, the form and formless realms would then have to possess edible food as well. If it is said that a sūtra states there is a class of gods who transcend edible food, and that those gods take joy as their food instead, thereby avoiding this particular fault — then, by the very same reasoning, the formless realm should not have form either, since a sūtra states that its inhabitants have gone forth from form.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag this passage as a genuinely rigorous piece of scriptural hermeneutics, worth reading as a methodological statement in its own right: citing a sūtra is not, by itself, sufficient to settle a doctrinal question, since the very same sentence can be read as applying universally or only to a restricted scope, and reading it universally without checking often produces consequences the tradition itself rejects. Four separate citations are each subjected to the identical question — how far does this actually reach? — before any of them can be used as evidence for form existing in the formless realm.

We should also flag the reductio strategy as elegant in its symmetry: if "name-and-form and consciousness depend on each other" must be read universally (with no restriction to the desire and form realms), then external, inanimate name-and-form would absurdly have to depend on consciousness too — and if the parallel between the four nutriments and the four abodes of consciousness must likewise be read universally, the form and formless realms would absurdly need edible food, which they demonstrably lack (resolved for the form realm only by a specific counter-citation about gods who feed on joy instead). The treatise then turns this very same resolution against the original claim: if a specific citation can override the general parallel to explain why form-realm gods lack edible food, an equally specific citation — the sūtra's own statement that formless beings "have gone forth from form" — should be allowed to override the general "form and consciousness always depend on each other" principle to explain why the formless realm lacks form. We have preserved this argument's own turn against itself exactly as constructed, since it is the passage's central rhetorical move.

6. The Debate Concludes: No Form at All, and How Form Returns After Death

The treatise now delivers its actual conclusion — the formless realm genuinely has no form whatsoever — clinched by noting exactly what the Buddha does and does not say about it, before turning to a hard practical question the "no form" position must answer: if form is entirely absent there, where does a formless being's form come from when they are later reborn somewhere with form again?

Chinese Source

又契經言：無色解脫最為寂靜，超諸色故。又契經說：無色有情，一切色想皆超越故。若無色界實有色者，定應彼色自相可知，如何可言超色想等？若謂觀下麁色故說，則於段食亦應許然。又諸靜慮超下麁色，亦應可說出離色言，是則亦應名無色界。又亦應說出離受等，彼亦超下麁受等故。經既不說，知無色中遍超色類，非超受等。由此定知，彼界無色。然契經中說有不出有者，於自地有不能出故，非遍出故、非永出故。又薄伽梵於靜慮中說有色類乃至識類，於無色中說有受類乃至識類，不說有色。若無色中實有色者，何不如靜慮說有色類言？故所立因無不成過。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, a sūtra states: formless liberation is the most tranquil, since it transcends all form. A further sūtra states: formless beings have transcended every perception of form entirely. If the formless realm genuinely had form, that form's own particular characteristic would certainly have to be knowable — how, then, could it be said that its inhabitants have transcended the perception of form? If it is said this is stated with reference to contemplating the coarse form of the lower realms specifically, then, by the same reasoning, one would have to grant this equally applies to edible food. Moreover, since each dhyāna transcends the coarser form of the one below it, one should equally be able to say each dhyāna has "gone forth from form," meaning every dhyāna above the first would also have to be called "formless realm." Moreover, one should equally have to say formless beings have "gone forth from feeling," since they also transcend the coarser feeling of the lower realms. Since the sūtra does not say this about feeling, it is known that, within the formless realm, form as a whole category is entirely transcended — not feeling and the rest. On this basis, it is definitely known that realm has no form. As for sūtra passages speaking of "not having gone forth from existence" in other contexts, this refers to not being able to go forth from the existence of one's own ground — not a total, universal, or permanent "going forth." Moreover, the Blessed One, with respect to the dhyānas, speaks of there being a category of form, up through a category of consciousness; with respect to the formless attainments, he speaks only of a category of feeling, up through a category of consciousness, never a category of form. If the formless realm genuinely had form, why would he not speak of a category of form there, the way he does for the dhyānas? Hence, the argument we have established incurs no fault of failing to be proven.

An objector presses a hard practical question: if form is truly absent for the entire duration of a formless existence, how does form ever return?

Chinese Source

在彼多劫色相續斷，後歿生下色從何生？此從心生，非從色起。謂昔所起色異熟因熏習在心，功能今熟，是故今色從彼心生。彼無色身心依何轉？離身何不轉？下曾不見故。色界無段食，身復依何轉？下亦不見身離段食轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, while in that formless realm for many kalpas, the continuity of form is completely severed, then, upon later dying and being reborn in a lower realm with form, from what does that new form arise? This form arises from the mind, not from any prior form. That is: the causal potential for form's ripening, generated long ago, has been perfumed into and remains within the mind; its functional potential now ripens, and it is for this reason that the present form arises from that mind. While in the formless state, lacking a body, what does the mind depend upon in order to operate? Why can it not operate independent of a body, the way it does here? Because such a thing has never been observed in the lower realms. But consider: the form realm has no edible food — so what does the body there depend upon in order to function? Yet this too, a body functioning without edible food, has never been observed in the lower desire realm either.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the closing argument — that the Buddha names a "category of form" for the dhyānas but never for the formless attainments — as the treatise's decisive piece of evidence, treated as settling the matter: if scriptural silence on form applied only to human perceptual limitation rather than actual absence, the Buddha's own systematic listing of aggregates present at each level would have had every reason to include it, the way it does for every other realm discussed in this project's cosmology. The parallel reductios along the way (dhyānas would all count as "formless" relative to what's below them; formless beings would have to have "gone forth from feeling" too, which no sūtra claims) are what rule out the softer, merely-comparative reading of "transcending form."

We should also flag the rebirth mechanism as directly continuous with the Sautrāntika "perfuming" theory of karmic seeds introduced in fascicle 13 as an alternative to unmanifested karma: rather than requiring an unbroken physical continuum to explain how form reappears after a purely formless existence, the treatise locates the causal potential entirely within the mental continuum itself, ripening on its own schedule once conditions are right — the same basic mechanism, applied here to explain the return of an entire aggregate (form) rather than just a subtle karmic residue. The closing analogy to form-realm beings functioning without edible food is a clever move: it does not resolve the "how" of a bodiless mind operating, but it does defang the objection's implicit standard (that nothing without lower-realm precedent can be believed), since the tradition already accepts an analogous gap for the form realm without controversy.

7. Naming the Four Formless Spheres, and the Three Kinds of Meditative Attainment

The treatise closes out the formless attainments by explaining exactly why each of the four bears its specific name — including the curious case of "neither perception nor non-perception," whose name tracks not what a practitioner contemplates on the way there, but the dimness of what remains once arrived — before opening a new topic: the threefold classification of any meditative attainment as tainted, pure, or uncontaminated.

Chinese Source

空無邊等從緣空等得別名耶？不爾。云何？下三無色，如其次第修加行時，思無邊空及無邊識、無所有故建立三名。立第四名由想昧劣，謂無明勝想得非想名，有昧劣想故名非非想。雖加行時亦作是念：諸想如病如箭如癰。若想全無便同癡闇，唯有非想非非想中與上相違寂靜美妙，而不就此加行立名。以若詰言何緣加行作如是念？必應答言以於彼處想昧劣故。由此昧劣故，是立名正因。已辯無色。

Ru-Yi Translation

Do infinite space and the rest receive their individual names by taking space and so on as their object? No. How, then? For the lower three formless spheres, respectively, at the time of cultivating the preparatory practice, one contemplates infinite space, infinite consciousness, and nothing existing — and it is on this basis that these three names are established. The fourth name is established because of the dimness and weakness of perception there: since the remaining perception is not prominent, it is called non-perception; yet since a dim, weak perception is still present, it is called non-non-perception. Although, at the time of preparatory practice, one also contemplates that all perceptions are like a disease, like an arrow, like a boil, and that if perception were entirely absent this would be tantamount to a dull, dark stupor, with only "neither perception nor non-perception" offering something genuinely tranquil and excellent in contrast — the name is nevertheless not established with reference to this particular preparatory contemplation. For if one were to press the question, why does the preparatory practice give rise to this contemplation in the first place, one would have to answer: because, in that place, perception is inherently dim and weak. It is on the basis of this dimness and weakness that the correct reason for the name lies. The formless attainments have now been explained.

The treatise then opens a new topic: every root meditative attainment, dhyāna or formless, admits of three possible varieties.

Chinese Source

云何等至？頌曰： 此本等至八， 前七各有三， 謂味淨無漏， 後味淨二種， 味謂愛相應， 淨謂世間善， 此即所味著， 無漏謂出世。 論曰：此上所辯靜慮無色。根本等至總有八種，於中前七各具有三，有頂等至唯有二種，此地昧劣無無漏故。初味等至，謂愛相應，愛能味著故名為味，彼相應故此得味名。淨等至名目世善定，與無貪等諸白淨法相應起故，此得淨名。即味相應所味著境，此無間滅彼味定生。緣過去淨深生味著，爾時雖名出所味定，於能味定得名為入。無漏定者，謂出世定，愛不緣故非所味著。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the meditative attainments? Verse: the root attainments number eight; the first seven each have three varieties — tainted, pure, and uncontaminated; the last has only two, tainted and pure; tainted means associated with craving; pure means worldly wholesome, and is what is craved and tainted by the tainted variety; uncontaminated means transcendent. Explanation: what has been explained above concerns the dhyānas and formless attainments. The root attainments number eight in total; the first seven each possess three varieties; the peak-of-existence attainment has only two, since this ground, being dim and weak, lacks the uncontaminated variety. The first, tainted attainment, refers to what is associated with craving — since craving taints and clings to its object, and this attainment is associated with craving, it receives the name tainted. Pure attainment designates worldly wholesome concentration, arising associated with non-greed and other pure, wholesome dharmas, hence receiving the name pure. It is precisely this pure attainment that the tainted attainment craves and clings to as its object — immediately upon the pure state ceasing, the tainted concentration taking it as object can arise. Taking the past pure state as object, one gives rise to deep craving and attachment toward it; at that time, although one is said to have "exited" the attainment previously craved, with respect to the now-active craving concentration, one is said to have "entered" it. Uncontaminated concentration refers to transcendent concentration; since craving can never take it as object, it is never something craved or clung to.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the naming logic for "neither perception nor non-perception" as a genuinely subtle point the treatise is careful to get right: one might assume the name comes from the preparatory reflection a practitioner actually undertakes on the way there (perception is like a disease, like an arrow, like a boil), since that reflection is what the practitioner is consciously doing — but the treatise insists the name tracks something more fundamental, the objective dimness and weakness of whatever perception remains at that level itself, which is in turn what makes that particular preparatory reflection arise in the first place. The reflection is downstream of the ground's own character, not the other way around.

We should also flag the tainted/pure relationship as a striking, almost paradoxical mechanic: the "tainted" variety of an attainment does not simply arise on its own, but specifically arises by taking the "pure" variety of that same attainment as its object — one craves and clings to one's own past experience of genuine, wholesome concentration. The treatise's careful language of simultaneously "exiting" the pure state (which has ceased) while "entering" the tainted state (which now craves it) captures a subtle temporal mechanic worth holding onto for later sections, where this three-way distinction (tainted / pure / uncontaminated) will presumably structure further analysis of how practitioners actually move between these states.

8. The Branch-Factors of Each Dhyāna, and the "Four-Branched Army" Simile

The treatise now returns to the dhyānas specifically — noting that, unlike the formless attainments, only the dhyānas possess named "branch-factors" (支) — and lists exactly which factors belong to each of the four.

Chinese Source

如是所說八等至中靜慮攝支，非諸無色。於四靜慮各有幾支？頌曰： 靜慮初五支， 尋伺喜樂定； 第二有四支， 內淨喜樂定； 第三具五支， 捨念慧樂定； 第四有四支， 捨念中受定。 論曰：唯淨無漏四靜慮中初具五支：一尋、二伺、三喜、四樂、五等持。此中等持頌說為定，等持與定名異體同，故契經說心定等定名正等持。此亦名為心一境性，義如前釋。傳說唯定是靜慮亦靜慮支，餘四支是靜慮支非靜慮。如實義者，如四支軍，餘靜慮支應知亦爾。第二靜慮唯有四支：一內等淨、二喜、三樂、四等持。第三靜慮具有五支：一行捨、二正念、三正慧、四受樂、五等持。第四靜慮唯有四支：一行捨清淨、二念清淨、三非苦樂受、四等持。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these eight attainments just discussed, the dhyānas possess branch-factors; the formless attainments do not. How many branches does each of the four dhyānas have? Verse: the first dhyāna has five branches — investigation, inquiry, joy, bliss, and concentration; the second has four — inner purity, joy, bliss, and concentration; the third has five — equanimity, mindfulness, wisdom, bliss, and concentration; the fourth has four — equanimity, mindfulness, neutral feeling, and concentration. Explanation: among only the pure and uncontaminated four dhyānas, the first possesses five branches: investigation, inquiry, joy, bliss, and concentration. Here, "concentration" is what the verse also calls "dhyāna" more generally — the two terms differ in name but share the same substance, hence a sūtra states that the mind's being fixed, being equally fixed, is called right concentration. This is also called one-pointedness of mind, with the meaning as already explained earlier. It is traditionally said that only concentration is both dhyāna itself and a dhyāna-branch, while the remaining four branches are dhyāna-branches but not dhyāna itself. According to the true meaning, this is like a "four-branched army" — infantry, cavalry, chariots, and elephants, none of which alone constitutes "the army," yet each properly called one of its branches — and the remaining dhyāna-branches should be understood in just the same way. The second dhyāna has only four branches: inner equanimous purity, joy, bliss, and concentration. The third dhyāna possesses five branches: formational equanimity, right mindfulness, right wisdom, experiencing bliss, and concentration. The fourth dhyāna has only four branches: purity of formational equanimity, purity of mindfulness, neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling, and concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "four-branched army" simile as a genuinely clarifying image for a subtle point about part-whole relationships: no single one of infantry, cavalry, chariots, or elephants is itself "the army," yet all four are legitimately called the army's branches, since together they constitute it. Applied to dhyāna, only concentration (the fifth branch, where present) is identical with dhyāna's own defining substance established earlier in this fascicle — investigation, inquiry, joy, bliss, mindfulness, wisdom, and equanimity are never themselves "dhyāna," yet each is properly called one of its branches, since dhyāna as an actually experienced state is never just bare one-pointedness alone, but always this specific accompanying configuration of factors.

We should also flag the progressive refinement visible across the four lists: the first dhyāna's five branches shed down to the second's four (losing investigation and inquiry, replaced by "inner purity"), hold steady in count but shift in kind for the third (equanimity and mindfulness and wisdom replacing joy, with bliss and concentration remaining), and settle into their final, most refined form in the fourth (equanimity and mindfulness now specifically "purified," with feeling itself becoming neutral rather than pleasant). This matches the progressive shedding pattern already established earlier in this fascicle, now specified branch by branch rather than only in general terms.

9. Eighteen Names, Eleven Real Substances — and Why "Bliss" Is Actually Pliancy

The treatise now reduces the eighteen branch-factor names across all four dhyānas down to their real underlying substances, using the same overlap-counting method applied to other lists throughout this project — then makes a striking claim: the "bliss" branch of the first two dhyānas is not bodily pleasant feeling at all, but pliancy, a different mental factor entirely.

Chinese Source

靜慮支名既有十八，於中實事總有幾種？頌曰： 此實事十一， 初二樂輕安， 內淨即信根， 喜即是喜受。 論曰：此支實事唯有十一，謂初五支即五實事。第二靜慮三支如前，增內淨支足前為六。第三靜慮等持如前，增餘四支足前為十。第四靜慮三支如前，增非苦樂支足前為十一。由此故說，有是初支非第二支，應作四句。第一句謂尋伺。第二句謂內淨。第三句謂喜、樂、等持。第四句，謂除前餘法。餘支相對，如理應思。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since the dhyāna-branches' names total eighteen, how many real substances are there among these in total? Verse: these real substances number eleven; the first two dhyānas' bliss is actually pliancy; inner purity is precisely the faith-faculty; joy is precisely the feeling of joy. Explanation: these branches' real substances number only eleven. The first dhyāna's five branches constitute five real substances. The second dhyāna's three branches are as before; adding the inner-purity branch brings the total to six. The third dhyāna's concentration is as before; adding its remaining four branches brings the total to ten. The fourth dhyāna's three branches are as before; adding the neither-painful-nor-pleasant branch brings the total to eleven. On this basis, it can be said that something may be a branch of the first dhyāna but not of the second, and a four-cornered analysis should be made: the first corner is investigation and inquiry, belonging to the first but not the second; the second corner is inner purity, belonging to the second but not the first; the third corner is joy, bliss, and concentration, belonging to both; the fourth corner excludes all of the above. The remaining branches, compared against each other across the other dhyānas, should be reasoned through in the same way.

The treatise then explains why the third dhyāna's "bliss" branch counts as a genuinely new addition to the total, while the first two dhyānas' "bliss" does not.

Chinese Source

何故第三說增樂受？由初二樂，輕安攝故。何理為證知是輕安？初二定中無樂根故。非初二定有身受樂，正在定中無五識故。亦無心受樂，以說有喜故。喜即喜受，無一心中二受俱行，故無樂受。不可喜樂更互現前，說具五支及四支故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is it said that the third dhyāna adds a genuinely new bliss-feeling? Because the first two dhyānas' "bliss" is included within pliancy instead. What reasoning proves this is pliancy? Because the first two dhyānas lack the bliss-faculty. It is not the case that the first two dhyānas have bodily-experienced bliss, since, while actually within concentration, the five sense-consciousnesses are absent. Nor is there mentally-experienced bliss in the ordinary sense, since the text already speaks of "joy" as a separate branch — and joy is precisely the feeling of joy itself; since a single mind cannot have two feelings occurring at once, there cannot also be a separate bliss-feeling present. Nor can joy and bliss be manifesting in alternation, since the text states the first dhyāna fully possesses five branches together, and the second, four — meaning both are present simultaneously as co-existing branches, not taking turns.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the core argument here as resting on a strict principle already familiar from this project's earlier fascicles on mental factors: a single mind-moment can support only one feeling (受) at a time. Since "joy" is already established as the first two dhyānas' feeling-branch, and since bodily pleasant feeling is ruled out entirely (the five sense-consciousnesses, which alone could register a bodily sensation, are absent during concentration), there is simply no room left for a second, distinct "bliss" feeling to coexist with joy in the same moment. Whatever the first two dhyānas' "bliss" branch actually names, then, it cannot be a feeling at all — leading the treatise to identify it instead with pliancy (輕安), the mental factor of refined bodily and mental ease already encountered in earlier fascicles' accounts of the wholesome mental factors.

We should also flag this as the opening move in what the source material signals will be an extended, closely argued debate — rival positions on this very question follow immediately in the source text, including direct sūtra citations that appear to describe dhyāna's "bliss" as genuinely bodily. We will take up that fuller debate in the sections that follow, rather than treating this initial argument as the treatise's final, uncontested word.

10. A Sūtra Lists Pliancy and Bliss Separately — So How Can They Be the Same?

The "bliss is pliancy" position just argued for immediately runs into a serious scriptural obstacle: a sūtra listing the five dharmas cultivated on the way to the first dhyāna names delight, joy, pliancy, bliss, and concentration as five distinct items — pliancy and bliss listed separately, not as one and the same thing. The treatise works through this challenge, and the further puzzle of how bodily sensation could occur at all within a state where the five sense-consciousnesses are supposedly absent.

Chinese Source

有說：無有心受樂根，三靜慮中說樂支者，皆是身受所攝樂根。若爾，何故有契經說：云何樂根？謂順樂觸力所引生身心樂受。有餘於此增益心言，諸部經中唯說身故。又第三定所立樂支，契經自說為身所受樂故。若謂於此說意為身，此說身名為有何德？又第四定輕安倍增，而不說彼有樂支故。若謂輕安要順樂受方名為樂，第三靜慮輕安順樂應是樂支。若謂彼輕安為行捨所損。不爾，行捨增輕安故。又彼輕安勝前二故。又契經說：若於爾時諸聖弟子於離生喜身作證具足住，彼於爾時已斷五法，修習五法皆得圓滿，廣說乃至何等名為所修五法？一歡、二喜、三輕安、四樂、五三摩地。此經輕安與樂別說，故初二樂非即輕安。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some say: there is no such thing as a mental bliss-faculty at all; whenever "bliss" is spoken of as a branch within the first three dhyānas, it always refers to the bliss-faculty included within bodily feeling. If this is so, why does a sūtra state: what is the bliss-faculty? It is the bodily and mental pleasant feeling drawn forth by the power of a pleasing contact. Some traditions have added the word "mental" here, since most scriptural collections state only "bodily." Moreover, the bliss-branch established for the third dhyāna is stated by the sūtra itself to be bliss experienced specifically by the body. If it is said that "body" here loosely means "mind," what merit would there be in using the term "body" this way? Moreover, in the fourth dhyāna, pliancy is doubly increased, yet it is not said that this dhyāna has a bliss-branch. If it is said that pliancy is called "bliss" only when it accompanies genuine pleasant feeling, then the third dhyāna's pliancy, which does accompany bliss, should also count as part of the bliss-branch. If it is said that pliancy there is diminished by formational equanimity — not so, since formational equanimity actually enhances pliancy, and, moreover, that fourth dhyāna's pliancy surpasses the first two in strength regardless. Moreover, a sūtra states: at the time when a noble disciple abides having fully realized, in the body, the joy born of seclusion, they have already severed five dharmas and fully perfected the cultivation of five others — stated fully, up through: what are these five cultivated dharmas? Delight, joy, pliancy, bliss, and concentration. Since this sūtra speaks of pliancy and bliss separately, the first two dhyānas' bliss cannot simply be pliancy.

The treatise then works out how bodily sensation is possible at all within concentration, and resolves a further puzzle about a single dhyāna-state containing both contaminated and uncontaminated branches at once.

Chinese Source

若言定中寧有身識？有亦無失，許在定中有輕安風勝定所起，順生樂受遍觸身故。若謂外散故應失壞定者，無如是失，此輕安風從勝定生，引內身樂還能順起三摩地故。若謂起身識應名出定者，此難不然，由前因故。若謂依止欲界身根不應得生色界觸識，緣輕安識，許生無過。若爾，正在無漏定中觸及身識應成無漏。勿所立支少分有漏、少分無漏，成違理失。無違理失。所以者何？許說身輕安是覺支攝故。若謂順彼故說覺支，無漏亦應許如是說。若謂許說，便違契經。如契經言：諸所有眼乃至廣說。此經中說十五界全皆有漏故，無違經過，此約餘觸及餘身識密意說故。

Ru-Yi Translation

But how could there be body-consciousness while actually within concentration? Even granting there is, there is no fault: within concentration, there is a "pliancy-wind" arising from superior concentration, which gives rise to a pleasant feeling pervading and touching the whole body. If it is said this would cause the mind to scatter externally and ruin the concentration — there is no such fault, since this pliancy-wind arises from superior concentration in the first place, and, by drawing forth internal bodily bliss, is actually able to further reinforce concentration itself. If it is said that giving rise to body-consciousness should count as "exiting" concentration — this objection does not hold, for the reason just given. If it is said that, depending on a desire-realm body-faculty, one should not be able to give rise to a form-realm tactile consciousness — granting that a consciousness taking pliancy as its object can arise this way involves no fault. If this is so, then, while actually within uncontaminated concentration, this contact and body-consciousness should themselves become uncontaminated. But one should not have an established branch-factor be partly contaminated and partly uncontaminated within a single attainment — would this not be a fault of contradicting reason? There is no such fault. Why? Because it is already granted that bodily pliancy is included among the awakening-factors, which themselves can be uncontaminated. If it is said that pliancy is called an "awakening-factor" merely because it accompanies uncontaminated dharmas, without itself literally becoming uncontaminated — then, by the same logic, one should equally grant that this present case is spoken of as uncontaminated only in that same accompanying sense. Does granting this contradict a sūtra? As a sūtra states: "whatever eye" — stated fully — this sūtra states that all fifteen contaminated-only elements are entirely contaminated. There is no fault of contradicting this sūtra, since that statement was made with implicit reference to other, ordinary instances of contact and body-consciousness, not this special case.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "pliancy-wind" mechanism as a genuinely inventive piece of meditative physiology: rather than treating bodily sensation during dhyāna as an intrusion that would disrupt concentration, the treatise proposes that superior concentration itself generates a subtle "wind" that produces a pervasive bodily pleasant sensation — and, far from scattering the mind outward, this sensation actually loops back to reinforce the very concentration that produced it. This lets the treatise affirm real bodily bliss within dhyāna without contradicting the earlier claim (from the previous section) that the ordinary five sense-consciousnesses are absent there — the pliancy-wind operates through a special, dhyāna-specific channel rather than ordinary sensory contact.

We should also flag the resolution of the contaminated/uncontaminated puzzle as reusing a precedent already established in this project's account of the thirty-seven limbs of awakening (fascicle 25): pliancy, as one of the seven awakening-factors, is already accepted as capable of being uncontaminated when it accompanies uncontaminated practice, even though pliancy's own basic substance is otherwise ordinarily contaminated — and the treatise argues the same accompanying-without-literally-becoming logic explains how a moment of bodily sensation within uncontaminated dhyāna can be spoken of as "uncontaminated" without contradicting the general rule (cited from elsewhere in scripture) that the sense-consciousnesses and their objects are always contaminated.

11. A Further Puzzle About Mixed Branches, and Why the First Dhyāna Has Exactly Five

The treatise raises one more version of the contaminated/uncontaminated puzzle from the previous section, this time resolved by appeal to timing rather than the awakening-factor analogy, before turning to a different question entirely: why does the first dhyāna have exactly five branches, no more and no fewer?

Chinese Source

如何無漏靜慮現前，少支有漏、少支無漏？起不俱時，斯有何失。若謂喜樂不俱起故，應無五支及四支理。此亦無過，約容有說有喜樂支，如有尋伺。若謂尋伺亦許俱起，於不俱起為喻不成。此非不成，心之麁細互相違故不應俱起。又於不俱起不能說過故。由此可說，依初五支減二三四立第二等，即由此理初說五支，擬漸離前、建立後故。無漸減故，不說想等。或應說何故初唯立五支？若謂此五資初定勝故立為支。此不應理，念慧能資勝尋伺故。雖有一類作如是說，然非古昔諸軌範師共施設故，應審思擇。

Ru-Yi Translation

How can it be that, when an uncontaminated dhyāna manifests, some of its branches are contaminated while others are uncontaminated? Since they do not arise at the same moment, what fault would this involve? If it is said that, because joy and bliss do not arise together, there should then be no basis for establishing five branches or four branches — this too involves no fault: it is said, with reference to what may possibly occur, that there are joy and bliss branches, just as with investigation and inquiry. If it is said that investigation and inquiry are actually granted to arise together, so they cannot serve as an analogy for things that do not arise together — this does not fail as an analogy, since coarse and subtle mental activity are mutually opposed and so should not arise together; moreover, with respect to things that do not arise together, no fault can be stated against listing them jointly as branches. On this basis, it can be said: the second and subsequent dhyānas are established by removing two, three, or four factors from the first dhyāna's five branches — and it is for this very reason that five branches are stated for the first dhyāna in the first place, in order to set up a scheme of gradually departing from what precedes and thereby establishing what follows. Since there is no gradual removal involved in the case of perception and the like, these are not spoken of as branches. Or, one might ask: why are only five branches established for the first dhyāna specifically? If it is said these five specifically support the first dhyāna's superiority, and are therefore established as its branches — this is not reasonable, since mindfulness and wisdom are also capable of supporting a dhyāna even better than investigation and inquiry do. Although there is a certain group who make this claim, it is not something jointly established by the ancient preceptor-masters, and should be carefully examined and judged.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that this passage raises a question strikingly similar in wording to the one just resolved in the previous section ("how can some branches of an uncontaminated dhyāna be contaminated, some not"), yet answers it differently — here by appeal to timing (the branches in question, like joy and bliss, or investigation and inquiry, simply do not arise in the very same instant, so there is no single moment containing a genuine mixture) rather than the awakening-factor analogy used before. We have preserved both arguments as the text presents them rather than harmonizing them, since the Kośa often stacks multiple, not-fully-reconciled replies to a recurring objection, and it is not our place to decide which one the text intends as final.

We should also flag the closing argument about why the first dhyāna has exactly five branches as ending in an explicit refusal to fully settle the question: a proposed rationale (these five specifically support the first dhyāna's superiority) is rejected as unreasonable, since mindfulness and wisdom could make the same claim, yet no alternative rationale is offered in its place — the passage simply notes that a certain group's proposal lacks the authority of the ancient preceptor-masters and should be "carefully examined," without telling us what that examination concludes. We read this as the treatise honestly leaving a structural question about the branch-scheme open rather than manufacturing false certainty.

12. What Is Inner Purity? Identified with the Faith-Faculty

Having settled the first dhyāna's branch-count, the treatise turns to defining the second dhyāna's distinctive branch, inner purity — and, following the same method used for the first two dhyānas' "bliss," argues that it too is not a separate substance in its own right, but is identified with an already-familiar mental factor: the faith-faculty.

Chinese Source

應說何法名內等淨？此定遠離尋伺鼓動，相續清淨轉，名為內等淨。若有尋伺鼓動，相續不清淨轉，如河有浪。若爾，此應無有別體，如何許有十一實事？是故應說此即信根。謂若證得第二靜慮，則於定地亦可離中有深信生，名內等淨。信是淨相，故立淨名。離外均流，故名內等。淨而內等，故立內等淨名。有餘師言：此內等淨等持尋伺皆無別體。若無別體，心所應不成，心分位殊亦得名心所。雖有此理，非我所宗。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, then, should be said to be called "inner equanimous purity"? This dhyāna, being far removed from the agitation of investigation and inquiry, has its mental continuity operate purely — this is called inner purity. Where the agitation of investigation and inquiry is present, the mental continuity operates impurely, like a river disturbed by waves. If this is so, does inner purity then lack any separate substance of its own — and if so, how can it be granted as one of the eleven real substances counted earlier? For this reason, it should be said that this is precisely the faith-faculty: that is, upon realizing the second dhyāna, within that meditative ground, now free of the agitation belonging to the intermediate state before it, a deep faith accordingly arises — this is called inner purity. Faith has purity as its characteristic, hence the name "purity" is established for it. Being free of external disturbance and flowing evenly, it is called "inner" and "equanimous." Being pure and also inner-and-equanimous at once, the name "inner equanimous purity" is thereby established. Other teachers say: this inner purity, as well as concentration, investigation, and inquiry, all lack any separate substance of their own — for if a mere phase or state of the mind, lacking any separate substance, could still properly be called a "mental factor," then mental factors in general would fail to be established as anything more than descriptive labels. Although there is some reasoning to this position, it is not the position of our own school.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the phrase rendered here as "now free of the agitation belonging to the intermediate state before it, a deep faith accordingly arises" (則於定地亦可離中有深信生) as one of the most compressed and difficult clauses we have encountered in this fascicle. The Chinese is terse even by this text's standards, and we are not fully confident our rendering captures the intended sense — it may instead mean something closer to "one is also able to give rise, amid this freedom [from investigation and inquiry], to a deep faith particular to that ground." We flag this honestly rather than presenting either reading with more confidence than it deserves.

We should also flag that this section extends the same interpretive move already made for the first two dhyānas' "bliss" branch (identified with pliancy rather than a feeling) to the second dhyāna's "inner purity" branch: rather than accepting it as some entirely new, otherwise-unattested mental factor, the treatise identifies it with the faith-faculty already established in earlier fascicles' accounts of the wholesome mental factors. The "other teachers" who reject any separate substance for inner purity, concentration, investigation, and inquiry alike are given only a brief, dismissive hearing here — their reasoning is acknowledged as having some merit, but the treatise declines to adopt it, without spelling out exactly where the disagreement lies. We would need to see this rival position developed elsewhere in the literature to assess it more fully than the text allows here.

13. Joy Is Precisely Joy-Feeling: A Cross-School Debate, Settled by Scripture

The treatise returns to a claim made in passing several sections earlier — that the "joy" branch just is the feeling of joy — and now defends it directly against a rival Ābhidharmika position that treats joy as a separate mental factor distinct from feeling altogether, appealing to two scriptural passages that pin down exactly when joy and bliss each cease.

Chinese Source

如上所言，喜即喜受。以何為證知決定然？汝等豈言喜非喜受，如餘部許，我亦許然。餘部云何許非喜受？謂別有喜是心所法，三定中樂皆是喜受，故喜、喜受其體各異。非三定樂可名喜受，二阿笈摩分明證故。如辯顛倒契經中說：漸無餘滅憂等五根，第三定中無餘滅喜，於第四定無餘滅樂。又餘經說：第四靜慮斷樂斷苦先喜憂沒。故第三定必無喜根，由此喜受是喜非樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

As stated above, joy is precisely the feeling of joy. What proof establishes that this is certainly so? Would you yourselves actually claim that joy is not the feeling of joy? As other schools grant this identity, we too grant it in just the same way. How, then, do other schools maintain that it is not the feeling of joy? They hold that there is a separate joy which is its own distinct mental factor, while the bliss present within the first three dhyānas is actually the feeling of joy — hence joy and the feeling of joy have distinct substances, on their account. But the bliss of the first three dhyānas cannot be called the feeling of joy: two āgama passages clearly prove this. As the Sūtra Distinguishing Inversions states: the five faculties, grief and the rest, are extinguished without remainder in stages — within the third dhyāna, joy is extinguished without remainder; within the fourth dhyāna, bliss is extinguished without remainder. A further sūtra states: in the fourth dhyāna, bliss is cut off and suffering is cut off, joy and grief having already vanished earlier. Hence the third dhyāna definitely lacks the joy-faculty — and from this it follows that the feeling called "joy" is precisely joy itself, not bliss.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the opening exchange — "would you yourselves actually claim that joy is not the feeling of joy? as other schools grant this identity, we too grant it in just the same way" — as among the more compressed dialogue in this fascicle, and we are not fully confident we have correctly identified which side of the debate is speaking at each turn. The overall shape of the argument is clear enough (a rival position separates "joy" the mental factor from "joy-feeling," and the treatise rejects this using scriptural timing), but the precise back-and-forth of this opening exchange should be read with appropriate caution.

We should also flag the argument's actual mechanism as elegant once the rival position is in view: since one sūtra places joy's final cessation specifically at the third dhyāna and bliss's final cessation specifically at the fourth, while a second sūtra confirms joy and grief vanish before bliss and suffering are finally cut off, the two feelings must be tracked as genuinely distinct all the way through — which rules out the rival school's claim that the first three dhyānas' "bliss" secretly is the feeling of joy. If that identification were correct, the scriptures' careful staging of when each feeling ends would collapse into redundancy.

14. Which Branches Survive in a Tainted Dhyāna?

Having finished analyzing the pure and uncontaminated dhyānas' branches, the treatise asks whether a tainted, worldly-desire-tinged version of each dhyāna possesses the same full set of branches — and answers that it does not, working through exactly which branch is missing at each level, before noting a rival account of the same question.

Chinese Source

如是所說諸靜慮支，染靜慮中為皆有不？不爾。云何？頌曰： 染如次從初， 無喜樂內淨， 正念慧捨念， 餘說無安捨。 論曰：如上所說諸靜慮支，染靜慮中非皆具有。且有一類隨相說言：初染中無離生喜樂，非離煩惱而得生故。第二染中無內等淨，彼為煩惱所擾濁故。第三染中無正念慧，彼為染樂所迷亂故。第四染中無捨念淨，彼為煩惱所染污故。有餘師說：初二染中但無輕安，後二染中但無行捨，大善攝故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Do all these dhyāna-branches just discussed exist within a tainted dhyāna as well? Not so. How, then? Verse: tainted dhyāna, in sequence from the first, lacks joy, then bliss, then inner purity, then right mindfulness and wisdom, then equanimity and mindfulness; another account says only pliancy and equanimity are absent. Explanation: the dhyāna-branches explained above are not all present within a tainted dhyāna. According to one account, stated with reference to each level's specific character: the first dhyāna's tainted state lacks the joy and bliss born of seclusion, since these do not arise except through freedom from affliction. The second dhyāna's tainted state lacks inner purity, since it is disturbed and muddied by affliction. The third dhyāna's tainted state lacks right mindfulness and wisdom, since it is confused and disordered by tainted bliss. The fourth dhyāna's tainted state lacks purity of equanimity and mindfulness, since it is defiled by affliction. Other teachers say: the tainted states of the first two dhyānas lack only pliancy, while the tainted states of the latter two lack only formational equanimity, since the remaining branches are included among the greater wholesome factors and so can still occur even in a tainted mind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag "tainted dhyāna" (染靜慮) here as referring to concentration that is genuine dhyāna in a technical, structural sense — it still involves the relevant branch-factors and meditative absorption — yet remains associated with worldly craving rather than being cultivated as a wholesome, liberating practice, distinct from the "pure" (淨) and "uncontaminated" (無漏) varieties introduced two sections earlier as one of the three kinds every root attainment admits of. This section specifies exactly how the tainted variety differs structurally from its pure counterpart at each of the four levels.

We should also flag that the treatise presents two genuinely different accounts here without adjudicating between them: the first account removes a different specific branch at each of the four levels (joy-and-bliss, then inner purity, then mindfulness-and-wisdom, then equanimity-and-mindfulness-purity), while the second, attributed to "other teachers," proposes a simpler pattern — only pliancy is absent in the first two dhyānas' tainted states, only formational equanimity in the latter two's — on the grounds that everything else belongs to a broader category of wholesome-adjacent factors capable of persisting even in a tainted mind. The text does not tell us which account it regards as correct, and we have not supplied a resolution it does not offer.

15. Why the Fourth Dhyāna Alone Is Called "Unmoving"

The treatise takes up a scriptural description of the first three dhyānas as "having movement" and the fourth as "unmoving," cataloguing the eight specific calamities the fourth dhyāna alone has left entirely behind.

Chinese Source

契經中說：三定有動，第四不動。依何義說？頌曰： 第四名不動， 離八災患故， 八者謂尋伺， 四受入出息。 論曰：下三靜慮名有動者，有災患故。第四靜慮名不動者，無災患故。災患有八，其八者何？尋、伺、四受、入息、出息。此八災患第四都無，故佛世尊說為不動。然契經說：第四靜慮不為尋伺喜樂所動。有餘師說：第四靜慮如密室燈照而無動。

Ru-Yi Translation

A sūtra states: the first three dhyānas involve movement, while the fourth does not. On what basis is this said? Verse: the fourth is called unmoving because it is free of eight calamities; the eight are investigation, inquiry, the four feelings, and the in-breath and out-breath. Explanation: the lower three dhyānas are called "having movement" because calamities are present within them; the fourth dhyāna is called "unmoving" because it is free of calamities. The calamities number eight — what are these eight? Investigation, inquiry, the four feelings, the in-breath, and the out-breath. The fourth dhyāna entirely lacks all eight of these calamities, and it is for this reason that the Blessed Buddha declared it unmoving. Yet a sūtra also states, more narrowly: the fourth dhyāna is not disturbed by investigation, inquiry, joy, or bliss. Other teachers say: the fourth dhyāna is like a lamp burning steadily within a sealed room, giving light without any flicker of movement.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag "the four feelings" (四受) among the eight calamities as left unspecified in this passage itself, and we have deliberately not supplied a confident gloss. Given the fourth dhyāna's own defining branch is a single neutral feeling free of both pleasure and pain (established in earlier sections of this fascicle), the most natural reading is that these four are the feelings the fourth dhyāna has left behind — pain, pleasure, grief, and joy — but the text does not say this explicitly here, and we would rather flag the gap than assert an identification the passage itself does not make.

We should also flag an apparent tension the passage does not resolve: the verse and its prose commentary list eight calamities in total, yet the scriptural citation offered immediately afterward names only four of them (investigation, inquiry, joy, and bliss), omitting the four feelings and the two breaths entirely. We have translated this exactly as the text presents it rather than smoothing over the mismatch, since the passage itself does not explain why the cited sūtra's list is narrower than the treatise's own eightfold enumeration.
