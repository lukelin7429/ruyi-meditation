The Four Dhyānas

Fascicle 28 opens Chapter 8 (分別定品第八之一, Distinguishing Concentration), the treatise's account of the meditative foundations underlying everything discussed in the two preceding chapters on knowledge — beginning with the four dhyānas themselves, each admitting of two senses, as a cultivated state and as the realm one is born into as its result, and a close technical debate over what "concentration" actually is at the most basic level.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 3 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 28 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. What Concentration Actually Is: A Real Factor, or Just the Mind Itself?

Having finished the virtues accomplished specifically by knowledge across fascicles 26 and 27, the treatise turns to the remaining virtues, organized by nature rather than by the knowledge that produces them — beginning with the concentration that supports them all: the four dhyānas.

Chinese Source

已說諸智所成功德，餘性功德今次當辯，於中先辯所依止定。且諸定內靜慮云何？頌曰： 靜慮四各二， 於中生已說， 定謂善一境， 并伴五蘊性， 初具伺喜樂， 後漸離前支。 論曰：一切功德多依靜慮，故應先辯靜慮差別。此總有四種，謂初二三四，四各有二，謂定及生。生靜慮體世品已說，謂第四八，前三各三。定靜慮體總而言之是善性攝心一境性，以善等持為自性故；若并助伴，五蘊為性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having explained the virtues accomplished by knowledge, we should now discuss the remaining virtues by nature; among these, let us first discuss the concentration that serves as their support. Among the various kinds of concentration, what is meant by dhyāna? Verse: the dhyānas are four, each of two kinds; of these, the sense of birth has already been explained; concentration means a wholesome one-pointedness of mind, together with its accompaniments the nature of the five aggregates; the first possesses inquiry, joy, and bliss, and each subsequent one gradually sheds the preceding factors. Explanation: since most virtues depend on the dhyānas, we should first distinguish the varieties of dhyāna. These number four in total — the first, second, third, and fourth — each having two senses: as concentration, a cultivated meditative state, and as birth, the realm one is born into as its result. The substance of birth-dhyāna was already explained in the chapter on the world: the fourth dhyāna comprises eight heavens, and each of the first three comprises three. The substance of concentration-dhyāna, stated in general, is a one-pointedness of mind included within the wholesome nature, since it has wholesome concentration as its intrinsic nature; including its accompanying factors, its nature is the five aggregates.

The treatise then works through a genuinely close technical debate: is "one-pointedness of mind" a real, distinct mental factor, or merely another name for the mind's own momentary and continuing focus?

Chinese Source

何名一境性？謂專一所緣。若爾，即心專一境位，依之建立三摩地名，不應別有餘心所法。別法令心於一境轉名三摩地，非體即心。豈不諸心剎那滅故皆一境轉，何用等持？若謂令心於第二念不散亂故須有等持，則於相應等持無用。又由此故三摩地成。寧不即由斯心於一境轉？又三摩地是大地法，應一切心皆一境轉。不爾，餘品等持劣故。有餘師說：即心一境相續轉時名三摩地，契經說此為增上心學故，心清淨最勝即四靜慮故。依何義故立靜慮名？由此寂靜能審慮故。審慮即是實了知義，如說心在定，能如實了知，審慮義中置地界故。此宗審慮以慧為體。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by one-pointedness? Exclusive focus on a single object. If this is so, then the name samādhi is established with reference to this very state of mind, and there should be no additional, separate mental factor involved. A distinct dharma that causes the mind to operate upon a single object is what is called samādhi — its substance is not simply identical with the mind itself. But since every mind-moment ceases within a single instant, does not every mind thereby operate on a single object trivially, within its own moment? What use, then, is a separate factor of concentration? If it is said that concentration is needed to prevent the mind, at the next moment, from becoming scattered, then this concentration would be useless with respect to the very moment it is itself associated with. Moreover, it is by virtue of this continuity across moments that samādhi is established — but is this not simply the mind itself continuing to operate on a single object, without needing any separate real dharma? Moreover, samādhi is classified as one of the great-ground mental factors present in every mind, so every mind should equally operate one-pointedly — yet this is not so, since concentration in other, less cultivated categories of mind is weaker. Other teachers say: it is precisely when the mind continues operating on a single object in succession that this is called samādhi, since a sūtra calls this "the training of the heightened mind," and the mind's purest, most excellent state is precisely the four dhyānas. On what basis is the name dhyāna established? Because, through this stillness, one is able to carefully examine and contemplate. Careful examination means truly and accurately knowing, as it is said: "the mind, being in concentration, is able to know truly" — the sense of examination carries this same meaning. According to this position, careful examination has wisdom as its substance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag this opening debate as returning to a foundational question this project has encountered before, in different guises, across earlier fascicles' treatment of the mental factors dissociated from mind and the "great-ground" dharmas established in fascicle 10: is a given quality of experience (here, one-pointed concentration) a genuinely distinct, real dharma that co-arises alongside the mind, or merely a descriptive label for how the mind itself happens to be behaving? The standard Abhidharma position insists samādhi is real and separate, needed to explain continuity across successive mind-moments rather than the trivial "one-pointedness" any single instant already has by definition; the objection presses hard on why, if this is a universally present factor, some minds are so clearly more concentrated than others.

We should also flag the closing etymological move — deriving "dhyāna" from "careful examination" (審慮), itself glossed as "true knowing," with wisdom as its substance — as setting up dhyāna's definition to already anticipate its close relationship with insight and knowledge, a relationship that has run throughout the two preceding fascicles' account of what dhyāna makes possible (the ten powers, the supernormal powers, and more), now finally receiving its own foundational treatment.

2. Why "Dhyāna" Names Only the Superior Kind, and How the Four Are Distinguished

The treatise closes out its definition of dhyāna by explaining why the name applies only to a superior class of concentration, addressing the apparent counterexample of "evil dhyāna," and then showing exactly how the four dhyānas are distinguished from one another — not by any difference in one-pointedness itself, but by which accompanying branch-factors each one still retains.

Chinese Source

若爾，諸等持皆應名靜慮。不爾，唯勝方立此名。如世間言發光名日，非螢燭等亦得日名。靜慮如何獨名為勝？諸等持內唯此攝支，止觀均行最能審慮，得現法樂住及樂通行名，故此等持獨名靜慮。若爾，染污寧得此名？由彼亦能邪審慮故，是則應有太過之失。無太過失，要相似中方立名故，如敗種等。世尊亦說有惡靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

If this is so, every concentration should be called dhyāna. Not so — only the superior kind receives this name. Just as, in ordinary speech, what emits light is called "the sun," yet fireflies and candles, though they too emit light, do not thereby receive the name "sun." Why is dhyāna alone called superior? Among all forms of concentration, only this one possesses branch-factors, with calm and insight operating in balance, most capable of careful examination, receiving the names joyful abiding in the present life and pleasant conduct — hence this concentration alone is named dhyāna. If this is so, how can even a defiled state receive this name? Because that too is capable of a kind of false careful examination — but then would this not incur the fault of overextending the name too broadly? There is no such fault, since the name is established only with reference to what resembles the genuine article, as when a spoiled seed is still called a "seed." The Blessed One himself also spoke of "evil dhyāna," confirming this extended usage.

Chinese Source

若一境性是靜慮體，依何相立初二三四？具伺喜樂建立為初，由此已明亦具尋義，必俱行故，如煙與火。非伺有喜樂，而不與尋俱。漸離前支立二三四，離伺有二，離二有樂，具離三種。如其次第，故一境性分為四種。已辯靜慮。無色云何？

Ru-Yi Translation

If one-pointedness is dhyāna's substance, on what basis are the first, second, third, and fourth established as distinct? The dhyāna possessing inquiry, joy, and bliss is established as the first — and by this it is already clear that it also possesses investigation, since inquiry and investigation necessarily occur together, like smoke and fire; there is no case of inquiry, joy, and bliss occurring without investigation accompanying them. Gradually shedding the preceding branch-factors, the second, third, and fourth are established: having shed investigation and inquiry while still possessing joy and bliss is the second; having shed those and joy as well, while still possessing bliss alone, is the third; having shed all three is the fourth. In this sequence, one-pointedness divides into four kinds. The dhyānas have now been explained. What of the formless attainments?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the "sun versus firefly" and "spoiled seed" similes as doing complementary work: the first explains why "dhyāna" names only concentration's most refined, branch-possessing form, not concentration in general, while the second immediately qualifies this by explaining how the tradition can still speak of "evil dhyāna" without contradiction — a spoiled seed is still called a seed by family resemblance to the genuine article, even though it will never sprout, and a defiled concentration capable of its own distorted kind of "careful examination" earns the name the same way, by resemblance rather than by actually possessing the virtue the name usually implies.

We should also flag the progressive shedding of branch-factors (investigation-and-inquiry, then joy, then bliss) as the mechanism the treatise uses to distinguish all four dhyānas using a single underlying substance — one-pointedness of mind never changes across the four, only the company it keeps changes, growing progressively quieter and more stripped-down at each stage. Investigation and inquiry are treated as an inseparable pair throughout (illustrated by the smoke-and-fire simile), which is why the first dhyāna's verse only names "inquiry" explicitly yet is understood to include investigation as well.

3. The Four Formless Attainments: Classification and Naming

Having finished the dhyānas, the treatise turns to the four formless attainments, which mirror the dhyānas' basic structure (four levels, each with a "concentration" and "birth" sense) but differ in one crucial respect: they lack form entirely, and each is defined by having left the ground immediately below it.

Chinese Source

無色云何？頌曰： 無色亦如是， 四蘊離下地， 并上三近分， 總名除色想， 無色謂無色， 後色起從心。 空無邊等三， 名從加行立， 非想非非想， 昧劣故立名。 論曰：此與靜慮數自性同，謂四各二。生如前說，即世品說由生有四。定無色體，總而言之亦善性攝心一境性。依此故說亦如是言。然助伴中此除色蘊，無色無有隨轉色故。雖一境性體相無差，離下地生故分四種。謂若已離第四靜慮生立空無邊處，乃至已離無所有處生立非想非非想處。離名何義？謂由此道解脫下地惑，是離下染義。即此四根本并上三近分，總說名為除去色想。空處近分未得此名，緣下地色起色想故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What of the formless attainments? Verse: the formless attainments are likewise so, four aggregates in substance, each established by leaving behind the ground below; together with the upper three approach-stages, they are collectively called eliminating the perception of form; "formless" means without form, and any later rebirth into form arises purely from the mind; infinite space and the other two are named from their preparatory practice; neither-perception-nor-non-perception is named for being dim and weak. Explanation: these are the same in number and intrinsic nature as the dhyānas — four, each with two senses. The sense of birth is as explained before, in the chapter on the world, which states there are four by birth. The substance of concentration-formless-attainment, stated in general, is likewise a one-pointedness of mind included within the wholesome nature — hence they are said to be "likewise so." Among the accompanying factors, however, this excludes the aggregate of form, since the formless attainments have no accompanying form. Although one-pointedness's own substance shows no difference across the four, they divide into four kinds because each arises upon leaving behind the ground immediately below it: once free of the fourth dhyāna, the resulting attainment is the sphere of infinite space; and so on, up to: once free of the sphere of nothing-at-all, the resulting attainment is the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. What does "leaving behind" mean? Through this path, one is liberated from the affliction of the lower ground — this is the meaning of leaving behind what is below. These four root attainments, together with the upper three approach-stages, are, in total, called "eliminating the perception of form." The approach-stage to the sphere of infinite space does not yet receive this name, since it still takes the lower ground's form as object, still giving rise to the perception of form.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag the precise scope of "eliminating the perception of form" as a carefully drawn boundary: it covers the four formless attainments themselves plus the preparatory approach-stages to the second, third, and fourth of them, but explicitly excludes the approach-stage to the very first, the sphere of infinite space — since that particular threshold moment still involves contemplating the lower, form-based ground one is leaving, and so has not yet actually eliminated the perception of form the way everything past it has.

We should also flag the closing verse's claim that "any later rebirth into form arises purely from the mind" as a genuinely striking cosmological detail this passage states without elaborating — a being who has died out of the formless realm and is later reborn with form again does not require any physical, form-based cause to bridge the gap, since the mind alone is sufficient to generate the transition. We expect the treatise to expand on this point, and on the deeper question of whether the formless realm really contains no form whatsoever, in the discussion immediately following.
