What Actually Makes One Thing Cause Another?

This page carries the opening of fascicle 6, beginning Chapter 2's fourth and final part (根品第二之四, the Indriya-nirdeśa's closing installment) with a major new topic: the six causes (六因), this tradition's foundational theory of causation. Having spent fascicles 4 and 5 cataloging the formations dissociated from mind, the treatise now asks a more basic question underneath all of it — what does it actually mean for one dharma to cause another at all?

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 4 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 6 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. A New Topic: The Six Causes

Having finished the formations dissociated from mind, the treatise steps back to a question it has been quietly relying on all along — what actually counts as a cause? It answers with a sixfold scheme, opening with the broadest and least demanding cause of all.

Chinese Source

如是已說不相應行。前言生相生所生時，非離所餘因緣和合，此中何法說為因緣？且因六種，何等為六？頌曰：能作及俱有，同類與相應，遍行并異熟，許因唯六種。論曰：因有六種，一能作因、二俱有因、三同類因、四相應因、五遍行因、六異熟因，對法諸師許因唯有如是六種。且初能作因相云何？頌曰：除自餘能作。論曰：一切有為唯除自體，以一切法為能作因，由彼生時無障住故。雖餘因性亦能作因，然能作因更無別稱，如色處等總即別名。

Ru-Yi Translation

The formations dissociated from mind have now been fully explained. It was said earlier that, when the mark of arising generates the dharma it generates, this occurs only together with the coming-together of all other causes and conditions — what, then, are these causes and conditions? Causes are, in brief, of six kinds. What are the six? Verse: efficient and co-existent, homogeneous and associated, universal, together with ripening — causes are accepted to be of exactly six kinds. Prose: there are six kinds of cause: first, the efficient cause; second, the co-existent cause; third, the homogeneous cause; fourth, the associated cause; fifth, the universal cause; sixth, the ripening cause. The Abhidharma masters accept that causes are of exactly these six kinds, and no others. What, then, is the characteristic of the first, the efficient cause? Verse: excepting itself, everything else is an efficient cause. Prose: with the sole exception of its own essence, every conditioned dharma serves as the efficient cause for every other dharma, since, at the moment that other dharma arises, this dharma offers no obstruction to its arising and settling. Although the other five kinds of cause are, in a sense, also efficient causes, "efficient cause" has no separate name of its own beyond this — just as the sense field of form, and so on, is both a general and a specific name at once.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This opens one of the true foundational topics of Abhidharma philosophy — the six causes (六因, ṣaḍ-hetavaḥ), the tradition's complete taxonomy of what it means for anything to cause anything else. Everything the treatise has analyzed so far — arising, possession, the formations dissociated from mind — has quietly presupposed some notion of causation; this section finally makes that notion explicit and systematic.

The efficient cause (能作因, kāraṇa-hetu) is deliberately the broadest and least demanding member of the six: astonishingly, every single conditioned dharma in existence counts as an efficient cause for every other dharma's arising, with the sole exception of itself. The criterion is almost comically minimal — merely not standing in the way. A distant star offers no obstruction to this morning's sunrise; by this definition, it counts as an efficient cause of the sunrise, however causally irrelevant that seems by ordinary standards. This is clearly meant as a kind of causal "background condition," a catch-all category capturing the bare fact that nothing in the universe actively prevents anything else from happening, layered beneath the five much more specific and demanding causes still to come.

2. Can Mere Non-Obstruction Really Count as Causing Something?

An immediate objection tests whether "not getting in the way" is really enough to deserve the name "cause" at all — answered through the examples of nirvana and hell-beings, and a memorable political analogy.

Chinese Source

豈不未知諸漏當起，由已知故諸漏不生，智於漏生能為障礙；日光能障現覩眾星。如何有為唯除自體，以一切法為能作因？應知此生時，彼皆無障住，故彼於此是能作因。若於此生，彼能為障而不為障，可立為因。譬如國人以其國主不為損害，咸作是言：我因國主而得安樂。若於此生彼無障用，設不為障何得為因？且如涅槃及不生法，普於一切有為生中；那落迦等有情相續於無色界諸蘊生中，有如非有無能障用，雖無障用而亦為因，如無力國王，亦得如前說。此即通說諸能作因，就勝為言非無生力。如眼色等於眼識等，生飲食於身種等於牙等。

Ru-Yi Translation

But is it not the case that, before one has understood the truth, the contaminants are bound to arise, whereas, once one has understood it, the contaminants no longer arise — meaning that wisdom is genuinely able to obstruct the arising of contaminants, the way sunlight is able to obstruct the visible appearance of the stars? How, then, can it be said that, with the sole exception of its own essence, every dharma whatsoever serves as an efficient cause for every other? It should be understood that, at the moment a given dharma arises, whatever else exists is simply not obstructing its settling into being — and it is with respect to this that all such other dharmas are called its efficient cause. Something that is genuinely capable of obstructing this arising, yet does not do so, may rightly be established as a cause of it. It is like the people of a country who, because their ruler does no harm to them, all say alike: "It is because of our ruler that we enjoy this peace and ease." But if a given dharma has no capacity to obstruct at all, then, since it was never going to obstruct anyway, how could its mere non-obstruction possibly qualify it as a cause? Consider, for instance, nirvana and other things that never themselves arise: with respect to the arising of any and every conditioned dharma throughout existence, and with respect to the arising of the aggregates of beings such as hell-dwellers within the continuum of the formless realm, whether such a capacity to obstruct is even present or not present, they still count as efficient causes despite lacking any actual capacity to obstruct at all — just as, even a genuinely powerless king may still be described in the very same way as above. This general account covers efficient causes as a whole; but, speaking of the more excellent instances specifically, it is not the case that these lack any actual generative power at all — as when the eye and visible form give rise to eye-consciousness, or food gives rise to the body, or a seed gives rise to a sprout.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection presses a fair point: surely a "cause" ought to involve some actual capacity to obstruct — otherwise, crediting something as a cause merely because it happened not to interfere seems almost empty. The reply draws a careful distinction using a lovely political image: a ruler who genuinely could oppress the people but chooses not to is rightly credited with their peace and ease, in a way that something utterly incapable of harming them in the first place would not be. Yet the treatise then extends the category even further, to cover cases with no capacity to obstruct at all — nirvana (which, being unconditioned, has no causal power of its own whatsoever) and the situation of hell-beings existing "within" formless-realm continua in only the loosest, most technical sense — precisely because "efficient cause" is defined to be this maximally permissive, catch-all category. The closing line is important: this bare, minimal sense of causation is the general rule covering the whole category, but many efficient causes — the eye and visible form producing eye-consciousness, food nourishing the body, a seed producing a sprout — are also genuinely, substantively causally productive in the fuller sense, not merely non-obstructing.

3. Why Doesn't Everything Arise at Once? A Sharp Objection, Resolved

If literally every dharma is an efficient cause of every other, a natural worry follows: shouldn't everything simply arise all at once, all the time? The resolution clarifies exactly what work this cause is, and is not, doing.

Chinese Source

有作是難：若一切法無障住故皆能作因，何緣諸法非皆頓起？一殺生時，何緣一切非如殺者皆成殺業？此難不然。但由無障，許一切法為能作因，非由於生有親作力。有餘師說：諸能作因皆於果生有能作力。且涅槃等於眼識生，云何名為有能作力？意識緣彼為境而生或善或惡，因此意識後時眼識次第得生，展轉因故。彼涅槃等於眼識生有能作力，如是餘法由此方隅展轉應知有能生力。如是已說能作因相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone raises this objection: if all dharmas whatsoever are efficient causes simply because they do not obstruct, then why do not all dharmas arise simultaneously, all at once? When one being kills another, why does not everyone else, in the same way as the killer, likewise commit the very same act of killing? This objection does not actually hold. Dharmas are accepted as efficient causes simply on the basis of not obstructing — not on the basis of possessing any direct, productive causal power over that arising. There is another view, however: that every efficient cause does have some genuine productive power with respect to the arising of its result. Take nirvana, for instance, with respect to the arising of eye-consciousness — in what sense could it be said to have productive power there? Mental consciousness takes it as an object and thereby gives rise to either a wholesome or an unwholesome state; because of this mental consciousness, eye-consciousness subsequently arises in its proper sequence, through a chain of successive causal connections. It is in this sense that nirvana has productive power with respect to the arising of eye-consciousness — and, in the very same way, it should be understood that every other dharma likewise has, from its own particular angle, some form of generative power reaching the result through just such a chain of successive connections. With this, the characteristic of the efficient cause has now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection exposes exactly what would go wrong if "not obstructing" were mistaken for "actively producing": if mere non-obstruction were causally sufficient on its own, literally everything that fails to prevent an event should be credited with equal responsibility for making it happen — absurdly making a bystander as much a killer as the one who actually kills. The resolution is precise: the efficient cause is deliberately not doing that heavier explanatory work at all; it is simply naming the bare background condition of non-interference, while the real, substantive work of explaining why this particular result arose, rather than some other, falls to the remaining five, far more demanding causes still to come.

The rival view offered alongside this is worth noting for its ingenuity: rather than accepting that some efficient causes (like nirvana) are causally inert, it tries to trace even nirvana's "productive power" through an indirect chain — nirvana becomes an object of thought, that thought influences a subsequent mental state, and that mental state eventually conditions a later moment of eye-consciousness. Even the most causally remote thing imaginable, on this reading, can be shown to have some genuine, if highly indirect, productive role in a later event — a more generous reading of "efficient cause" than the passage's own opening admission that this category, at bottom, requires nothing more than simple non-obstruction.

4. The Second Cause: Co-Existent Dharmas That Mutually Produce Each Other

The treatise turns to a far more demanding kind of cause — one requiring two dharmas to arise together and genuinely, mutually produce one another, illustrated through the four great elements and the relationship between a dharma and its own characteristics.

Chinese Source

第二俱有因相云何？頌曰：俱有互為果，如大相所相，心於心隨轉。論曰：若法更互為士用果，彼法更互為俱有因。其相云何？如四大種更互相望為俱有因，如是諸相與所相法心與心隨轉亦更互為因。是則俱有因由互為果遍攝有為法，如其所應。法與隨相非互為果，然法與隨相為俱有因，非隨相於法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, next, is the characteristic of the co-existent cause? Verse: co-existent dharmas are mutually results of one another — as with the great elements and what bears their characteristics, and as with mind and what follows mind's course. Prose: if two dharmas are mutually each other's agent-efficacy result, those two dharmas are mutually each other's co-existent cause. What is this characteristic like? Just as the four great elements, considered in relation to one another, are mutually each other's co-existent cause, so too are a dharma's characteristics and the dharma that bears those characteristics, and mind and the dharmas that follow mind's course, likewise mutually causes of one another. Co-existent cause, then, through this relation of mutual resulting, extends across every conditioned dharma, each in its appropriate way. A dharma and its own secondary characteristics, however, are not mutually results of one another — a dharma and its secondary characteristics do indeed stand as a co-existent cause of one another, but this is not by virtue of the secondary characteristic itself being caused by the dharma in return.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The co-existent cause (俱有因, sahabhū-hetu) is a much stronger relationship than the efficient cause's bare non-obstruction: it requires two dharmas to arise together, at the very same moment, each genuinely producing the other in return — true mutual causation, not one-directional production. The four great elements (earth, water, fire, wind) are the classic textbook case: none of the four can exist without the other three simultaneously present and mutually supporting it, each element's presence causally sustaining every other's. The same reciprocal relationship is then extended to a dharma and its defining characteristics, and to mind and the mental factors and other dharmas that accompany it moment by moment — everywhere co-arising dharmas mutually depend on and produce one another, this is co-existent causation.

The closing clarification is a fine piece of housekeeping, connecting back to the primary-and-secondary-characteristic scheme from fascicle 5: a dharma and its own secondary characteristics (such as arising-of-arising) genuinely do serve as co-existent causes for one another, but not through the same "mutual resulting" relationship that defines the category in general — a technical qualification the treatise flags now and will need to unpack more carefully in what follows.
