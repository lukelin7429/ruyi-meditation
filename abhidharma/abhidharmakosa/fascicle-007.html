What Follows What, and Why?

This page carries the opening of fascicle 7, closing Chapter 2's fifth part (根品第二之五, the Indriya-nirdeśa's final installment) with the four conditions (四緣) — the classical scriptural causal scheme of causal condition, immediately-antecedent condition, objective condition, and dominant condition. The fascicle opens with a genuinely surprising claim: physical form can never serve as an immediately-antecedent condition at all, since its own succession is too irregular — a claim that leads into one of this treatise's most striking epistemological puzzles, how the Buddha can know the fixed order of events yet to come, given that the future itself has no fixed order at all.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 16 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 7 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. A New Topic: The Four Conditions

Having spent fascicle 6 on the six causes, the treatise turns to the classical scriptural scheme of four conditions — mapping most of it directly onto ground already covered, while flagging one genuinely new and demanding case: the immediately-antecedent condition.

Chinese Source

廣說因已。緣復云何？頌曰：說有四種緣，因緣五因性，等無間非後，心心所已生，所緣一切法，增上即能作。論曰：於何處說？謂契經中。如契經中說四緣性，謂因緣性、等無間緣性、所緣緣性、增上緣性。此中性者是緣種類。於六因內除能作因，所餘五因是因緣性。除阿羅漢臨涅槃時最後心心所法，諸餘已生心心所法是等無間緣性。此緣生法等而無間，依是義立等無間名。

Ru-Yi Translation

Causes have now been explained at length. What, then, of conditions? Verse: it is said that there are four kinds of condition — the causal condition is the nature of five of the causes; the immediately-antecedent condition is every already-arisen mind and mental factor, except the very last; the objective condition is all dharmas; the dominant condition is simply the efficient cause. Prose: where is this stated? In a sutra. As a sutra states the nature of the four conditions: the nature of the causal condition, the nature of the immediately-antecedent condition, the nature of the objective condition, and the nature of the dominant condition. Here, "nature" means the kind or category of condition. Among the six causes, excepting the efficient cause, the remaining five causes constitute the nature of the causal condition. Excepting the very last mind and mental factors of an arhat at the moment of final nirvana, all other already-arisen mind and mental factors constitute the nature of the immediately-antecedent condition. This condition gives rise to a dharma equally and without interruption — it is on the basis of this meaning that the name "immediately-antecedent" is established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four conditions (四緣, catuḥ-pratyaya) are the classical scriptural scheme this whole tradition's causal theory ultimately answers to — and the treatise wastes no time showing how neatly three of the four map onto ground already covered: the causal condition is simply five of the six causes bundled together (everything except the efficient cause, which gets its own separate condition below), and the dominant condition turns out to be nothing more than the efficient cause under a new name. The genuinely new material is the immediately-antecedent condition (等無間緣, samanantara-pratyaya) — confined specifically to mind and mental factors, and excluding only the very last moment of an arhat's continuum, since nothing follows that moment at all. Why this condition should be restricted to mind alone, and not to form or the other formations, is the question the next several sections work through in real depth.

2. Why Form Can Never Be an Immediately-Antecedent Condition

A genuinely surprising restriction: physical form, however reliably it seems to cause further form, can never count as an immediately-antecedent condition — because, unlike mind, its own succession is fundamentally irregular, illustrated through burning rice stalks and a single seed's growth into a towering banyan tree.

Chinese Source

由此色等皆不可立等無間緣，不等生故。謂欲界色或無間生欲界色界二無表色，或無間生欲界無漏二無表色。以諸色法雜亂現前，等無間緣生無雜亂，故色不立等無間緣。尊者世友作如是言：於一身中一長養色相續不斷，復有第二長養色生，不相違害，故不可立等無間緣。大德復言：以諸色法無間生起或少或多，謂或有時從多生少，如燒稻稈大聚為灰。或時復有從少生多，如細種生諾瞿陀樹，根莖枝葉漸次增榮，聳幹垂條多所蔭映。

Ru-Yi Translation

For this reason, form and the like can never be established as an immediately-antecedent condition, since their succession is not regular. That is: a desire-realm form dharma may, in the immediately following moment, give rise to either of the two kinds of unmanifest form belonging to the desire realm or the form realm, or may give rise to either of the two kinds of uncontaminated unmanifest form belonging to the desire realm. Because these physical dharmas arise in a jumbled, irregular manner, while the immediately-antecedent condition specifically produces its result in a regular, non-jumbled way, form cannot be established as an immediately-antecedent condition. The Venerable Vasumitra explains it this way: within a single body, one continuous stream of nourished-and-grown form does not cease, and yet a second instance of nourished-and-grown form can arise alongside it without the two conflicting or harming one another — and it is for this reason that form cannot be established as an immediately-antecedent condition. The Bhadanta further explains: physical dharmas arise in the immediately following moment sometimes in smaller quantity, sometimes in greater — that is, sometimes a great mass gives rise to something smaller, as when a large heap of burning rice stalks is reduced to ash; and sometimes, conversely, something small gives rise to something great, as when a tiny seed gives rise to a great banyan tree, its roots, trunk, branches, and leaves gradually flourishing and increasing, its towering trunk and hanging branches casting abundant shade.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely counterintuitive restriction, and worth pausing on: form obviously causes further form in the ordinary sense — a seed becomes a tree, a body persists moment to moment — yet none of this qualifies as "immediately-antecedent condition" in this tradition's precise technical sense, which demands a very specific kind of regularity: a fixed one-to-one, equal succession, never a jump in scale or kind. Two independent explanations are offered for why form fails this test. Vasumitra's focuses on physical overlap: within one body, an existing stream of nourished bodily tissue can persist right alongside a freshly arising second instance, without the two ever being in the kind of clean, exclusive one-after-another relationship the condition requires. The Bhadanta's explanation is more dramatic and more memorable — form's scale is simply unpredictable across a single moment's transition, sometimes collapsing dramatically (a great mass of burning rice stalks reduced to a small heap of ash) and sometimes exploding outward (a single tiny seed becoming an enormous, shade-casting banyan tree) — nothing about the "before" reliably predicts the scale of the "after," which is exactly the irregularity that disqualifies it.

3. Do Mental Factors Show the Same Irregularity? A Careful Clarification

An objection presses the same challenge against mind and its mental factors — don't they, too, vary unpredictably in quantity from moment to moment? The resolution draws a precise distinction between a whole state of mind and any single mental factor considered strictly on its own.

Chinese Source

豈不心所無間生時亦有少多品類非等？謂善不善無記心中，有尋有伺三摩地等，此於異類實有少多，然自類中無非等義，謂無少受無間生多，或復從多無間生少，想等亦爾，無非等過。豈唯自類前能為後等無間緣？不爾。云何？前心品法總為後品等無間緣，非唯自類。且於受等自體類中無少生多，以說等義。唯執同類相續者說，唯自類有等無間緣，心唯生心、受唯生受，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

But is it not the case that, when mental factors arise in immediate succession, there is likewise a difference in quantity and kind, not a regular equality? That is: within a wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral state of mind, there are things like applied thought, sustained thought, and concentration — and, considered across these different categories, there genuinely is a difference in quantity. But within any single one of these categories taken on its own, there is no such irregularity: that is, there is no case of a small amount of feeling giving rise, in immediate succession, to a great amount, or, conversely, a great amount giving rise to a small amount; and the same holds for perception and the rest — no irregularity of this kind occurs. Is it, then, only a dharma of its own same category, occurring earlier, that can serve as an immediately-antecedent condition for a dharma of that same category occurring later? No. What, then, is the case? A whole prior state of mind, taken as a whole, serves as the immediately-antecedent condition for the whole state of mind that follows — not merely a same-category dharma for another same-category dharma. It is only with respect to feeling and the like, considered strictly within its own essential category, that there is no case of a small giving rise to a great, which is what establishes the sense of "regular equality" being discussed here. It is only those who hold that immediately-antecedent condition requires strict same-category continuity who say that only a dharma of the very same category can serve this role — that mind only ever gives rise to mind, feeling only ever gives rise to feeling, and so on, at length.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a careful piece of conceptual disambiguation, distinguishing two very different things that could be meant by "regular succession." Across different categories of mental factor — applied thought versus concentration, say — there is indeed real variation in how many are present from one moment to the next, depending on which meditative state one occupies. But this is not the kind of irregularity that disqualified form: what matters is whether a single mental factor, considered strictly within its own kind (feeling as feeling, perception as perception), ever jumps unpredictably in scale the way form does — and it never does. Feeling always gives rise to feeling in a steady, one-for-one way, never suddenly ballooning or collapsing in quantity the way a seed becomes a tree or ash a heap of rice stalks.

The section also quietly corrects a possible overreading: the immediately-antecedent condition does not require that a dharma's successor belong to the exact same narrow category — a whole state of mind, including its entire cluster of associated mental factors together, serves as the antecedent condition for the next whole state of mind as an integrated unit, not each individual mental factor type matching narrowly to its own kind alone. That stricter, same-category-only reading is flagged as belonging only to a particular rival position, not the treatise's own settled view.

4. A Rejected Account: Can a Long-Past Affliction Condition a Present One?

A rival explanation tries to account for how a defiled mind can follow an undefiled one, borrowing the mechanism already used to explain how mind resumes after the attainment of cessation — but the explanation fails its very first test case.

Chinese Source

若從無染無間染生，此染心中所有煩惱，用先滅煩惱為等無間緣。如出滅定，心還用先滅正入滅定，心為緣故起。彼說非善，初無漏心應闕此緣而得生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a defiled mind arises immediately after an undefiled one, the afflictions present within that defiled mind, on this account, make use of some previously-ceased affliction as their immediately-antecedent condition — comparable to how, when mind emerges from the attainment of cessation, it makes use of the very mind that had previously ceased upon entering that attainment as its condition for arising once again. This explanation is not sound, since the very first uncontaminated mind would then have to arise despite lacking this very condition altogether.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The proposal borrows a genuinely clever mechanism from fascicle 5's discussion of how mind resumes after the mindless attainment of cessation — there, a long-past moment of mind was allowed to serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for a much later moment, skipping over the intervening gap entirely. Applied here, the idea is that a newly arising affliction could likewise reach back to some earlier, already-ceased instance of that same affliction as its condition, explaining how defilement can resurface in a mind that was, in its immediately preceding moment, undefiled.

The rejection is swift and decisive: the very first moment any affliction is ever cut off by the uncontaminated path of seeing has, by definition, no earlier instance of that same affliction to serve as its "previously-ceased" antecedent — there was no prior occasion for it to have already ceased and be waiting in reserve. If this mechanism were genuinely necessary, the first moment of uncontaminated insight could never arise in the first place, which is plainly false.

5. Formations Dissociated from Mind, and the Future, Are Excluded Too

The same irregularity that disqualified form is shown to disqualify the formations dissociated from mind as well — and future dharmas are excluded for a related but distinct reason: they exist in an unsettled, jumbled state with no fixed sequence at all.

Chinese Source

不相應行亦如諸色雜亂現前，故非等無間緣。三界及不繫可俱現前故，何緣不許未來世有等無間緣？以未來法雜亂而住無前後故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The formations dissociated from mind, too, arise in a jumbled, irregular manner just like form, and so likewise cannot serve as an immediately-antecedent condition — since dharmas belonging to the three realms, and dharmas unbound to any realm, can all arise together at once, mixed with one another. Why, then, is it not accepted that there is an immediately-antecedent condition existing in the future? Because future dharmas exist in a jumbled state, with no fixed sequence of before and after among them at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The formations dissociated from mind — possession, class-commonality, the characteristics, and the rest, spanning fascicles 4 and 5 — fail the regularity test for essentially the same reason form does: dharmas belonging to entirely different realms, or to none at all, can arise together in the same moment without any predictable, fixed one-to-one pattern governing their succession. And future dharmas fail for a deeper, more fundamental reason still: the very idea of "immediately following" presupposes a determinate order — this, then that — but the future, on this tradition's account, simply does not yet have any fixed order at all. Everything that might arise sits together in an undifferentiated, "jumbled" state until the moment of its actual arising settles which of the possibilities becomes real, which is exactly what the next section's puzzle turns on.

6. If the Future Has No Fixed Order, How Does the Buddha Know What Comes Next?

A genuinely striking epistemological puzzle: if future dharmas exist in an unsettled, jumbled state with no fixed sequence, how can the Buddha reliably know exactly which dharma will immediately follow which? The answer distinguishes his foreknowledge sharply from ordinary inference.

Chinese Source

如何世尊知未來世此法無間、此法應生？比過現法而現知故。傳說世尊見從過去如此類業此類果生，是法無間生如是法。又從現在如此類業此類果生，是法無間生如是法。如是見已，便於未來諸亂住法，能正了達此法無間此法應生。雖如是知而非比智。由佛比類過去現在因果次第，便於未來亂住諸法能現了達，謂未來世如是有情造如是業招如是果，是願智攝故非比智。若爾，世尊未見前際，於後際法應不能知。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, then, does the World-Honored One know that, in the future, this dharma will immediately follow, and this other dharma is bound to arise? By directly knowing this on the basis of comparison with past and present dharmas. Tradition holds that the World-Honored One observes that, in the past, this kind of karma gave rise to this kind of result, this dharma arising immediately after that one; and, likewise, in the present, this kind of karma gives rise to this kind of result, this dharma arising immediately after that one. Having observed this, he is then able to correctly comprehend, with respect to the many jumbled, indeterminately-existing dharmas of the future, that this dharma will immediately follow, and that dharma is bound to arise. Although he knows in this way, this is not inferential knowledge. Rather, it is by the Buddha's comparing the sequence of cause and effect across the past and the present that he is able to directly comprehend the many jumbled dharmas of the future — knowing that, in the future, a certain being will perform a certain karma and thereby bring about a certain result. This is subsumed under the knowledge of aspiration, and is not inferential knowledge. If that is so, then, since the World-Honored One has not directly witnessed the very beginning of time itself, he ought to be equally unable to know anything about the furthest future either.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The puzzle cuts straight to the heart of what it means for the future to have "no fixed order" at all: if that is literally true, on what basis could even a Buddha reliably predict which specific dharma will immediately follow which? The answer offered is careful on a point that might otherwise seem like a technicality but is philosophically load-bearing: this foreknowledge is explicitly denied the status of ordinary inference (比智, anumāna), even though the mechanism described — extrapolating a pattern from repeatedly observed past and present cause-and-effect sequences — sounds, on its face, exactly like inference. Instead, it is classified as a distinct supernormal faculty, "the knowledge of aspiration" (願智, praṇidhi-jñāna): a direct, immediate comprehension that happens to be triggered or informed by prior observation, rather than a chain of logical steps reasoning from premises to a conclusion.

The closing objection is a sharp one, and genuinely difficult: if the Buddha's foreknowledge of the future depends on having already observed enough past and present pattern to extrapolate from, doesn't this put a hard limit on what he can know — nothing about times too far removed from anything he has actually witnessed, including, presumably, the very beginning of time itself, if such a beginning even exists? The treatise's response to this genuinely difficult challenge follows next.

7. A Rejected Theory of Bodily Omens, and the Sautrāntika's Resolution

One further theory proposes that the Buddha reads the future from a physical precursor-sign within the body itself — rejected because it would reduce the Buddha's omniscience to mere fortune-telling, in favor of a direct, unmediated knowing beyond both inference and omen.

Chinese Source

有餘復言：有情身內有未來世果因先兆，是不相應行蘊差別。佛唯觀此便知未來，非要現遊靜慮通慧。若爾，諸佛便於未來占相故知，非為現證。故如經部諸師所言，世尊舉意遍知諸法非比非占。此說為善。如世尊說：諸佛德用、諸佛境界不可思議。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a further view: within a sentient being's own body, there exists a kind of precursor sign for a future result's cause — a particular kind of formation dissociated from mind. The Buddha, simply by observing this sign, thereby knows the future, without necessarily needing to actually enter into meditative absorption and exercise supernormal wisdom. If that were so, the Buddhas would then come to know the future merely by reading omens and signs, rather than through direct realization. Hence, as the Sautrāntika masters say: the World-Honored One, simply by directing his mind, comprehensively knows all dharmas — neither through inference, nor through reading omens. This explanation is the correct one. As the World-Honored One himself states: "The qualities and functions of the Buddhas, and the objects within the Buddhas' own domain, are inconceivable."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The bodily-omen theory tries to naturalize the Buddha's foreknowledge into something almost mechanical — a physical sign embedded in the body itself, functioning like a readable indicator of what is to come. The rejection is pointed: this would demote the Buddha's omniscience to something closer to divination, reading external signs rather than directly knowing reality. The Sautrāntika's alternative refuses to explain the mechanism away at all, insisting instead on a form of knowing that is genuinely direct and unmediated — neither inference from evidence nor interpretation of a sign, simply comprehensive, immediate cognition. The closing citation is a fitting capstone: rather than press the mechanism further, the treatise lets the Buddha's own words mark the limit of ordinary explanation — some things about a Buddha's own cognitive domain are declared, quite deliberately, beyond what conceptual analysis can fully capture.

8. A Further Puzzle: Fixed Sequences Within Specific Attainments

If the future truly has no fixed order, why does scripture insist that specific advanced meditative states are always followed by one, and only one, specific result? The resolution distinguishes a tight causal dependency from the broader immediately-antecedent condition.

Chinese Source

若於未來無定前後次第安立，何故但言世第一法無間唯生苦法智忍不生餘法？如是廣說，乃至金剛喻定無間，唯生盡智不生餘法。若此法生繫屬彼法，要彼無間此乃得生，如芽等生要藉種等。然此非有等無間緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is truly no fixed sequence of before and after established among future dharmas, why is it said that, immediately following the world-supreme dharma, only the forbearance of the dharma-cognition of suffering arises, and no other dharma? And so on, at length, up through: immediately following the vajra-like concentration, only the knowledge of extinction arises, and no other dharma? Because the arising of this particular dharma is bound up with, and dependent upon, that particular dharma — it is only immediately following that specific dharma that this one can arise at all, just as a sprout's arising depends upon a seed, and so on. This, however, does not amount to there being a genuine immediately-antecedent condition in the fuller sense already established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection presses a real inconsistency: the treatise has just insisted future dharmas exist in an undetermined, jumbled state with no fixed sequence — yet scripture plainly states that certain specific attainments always give rise to one, and only one, specific successor, with no variation at all. The resolution draws a fine but important line: a tight, dedicated causal dependency between two specific dharmas — this one can only ever arise immediately after that one, the way a sprout depends specifically on its own seed — is not the same claim as saying the future in general has a fixed, determinate order running through it. These are narrow, specific bindings between particular pairs of dharmas, carved out as genuine exceptions, rather than evidence that "immediately-antecedent condition," understood in its full technical generality, actually applies to future dharmas as a class.

9. "Manas" Versus "Immediately-Antecedent Condition": A Fine Distinction

Why is an arhat's very last moment of mind still called "manas," yet denied the status of an immediately-antecedent condition? The answer distinguishes a name earned by serving as a structural basis from a name earned only by an actually completed function.

Chinese Source

諸阿羅漢最後心心所，何緣故說非等無間緣？無餘心等續此起故。豈不如是無間滅心亦名為意？後心無間識既不生應不名意，意是依所顯非作用所顯。此最後心有所依義，餘緣闕故後識不生，等無間緣作用所顯。若法此緣取為果已，定無諸法及諸有情能為障礙令彼不起，故最後心雖得名意，而不可說等無間緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is it said that an arhat's very last mind and mental factors are not an immediately-antecedent condition? Because no further mind, and so on, continues to arise following it. But is this immediately-ceasing mind not also called "manas"? If no consciousness arises in the moment immediately following this final mind, should it not then also fail to be called "manas"? "Manas" is a name revealed by virtue of serving as a basis, not a name revealed by virtue of actually performing a function. This final mind does have the sense of being able to serve as a basis — it is merely that, because the remaining necessary conditions are absent, no later consciousness in fact arises. "Immediately-antecedent condition," by contrast, is a name revealed specifically by virtue of a function actually being performed: once a given dharma has taken hold as this specific kind of condition for a result, there is then definitely no dharma, and no sentient being, capable of obstructing that result and preventing it from arising. Hence, although this final mind can indeed still be called "manas," it cannot be said to be an immediately-antecedent condition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely elegant piece of conceptual surgery, distinguishing two labels that might seem, at first glance, to have to travel together. "Manas" (意) names a structural role — being the kind of thing capable of serving as the basis for a following moment of consciousness — and an arhat's final mind genuinely qualifies for this role in principle, regardless of whether anything actually follows it. "Immediately-antecedent condition," by contrast, names something stronger: a guarantee that the result will actually, unstoppably arise, with nothing in the universe capable of obstructing it. The arhat's final mind fails this second, stronger test not because of any deficiency in the mind itself, but simply because the other conditions needed for a further moment of consciousness — most obviously, a continuing living body — are no longer present at the moment of final nirvana. A capacity that goes unexercised, for want of supporting circumstances, still earns the structural name; it does not earn the name that requires the function to have actually, guaranteedly gone through.

10. A Closing Fourfold Case, and the Immediately-Antecedent Condition Concludes

A precise fourfold case tests whether "condition for mind" and "immediately following mind" are truly the same thing — using the two mindless attainments, which create a genuine gap in mental continuity, as the decisive edge case.

Chinese Source

若法與心為等無間，彼法亦是心無間耶？應作四句。第一句者，謂無心定出心心所，及第二等二定剎那。第二句者，謂初所起二定剎那，及有心位諸心心所生住異滅。第三句者，謂初所起二定剎那，及有心位心心所法。第四句者，謂第二等二定剎那，及無心定出心心所生住異滅。若法與心為等無間，與無心定為無間耶？應作四句。謂前第三第四句為今第一第二句，即前第一第二句為今第三第四句。從二定出諸心心所，望入定心中間遠隔，如何為彼等無間耶？中間不隔心心所故。如是已釋等無間緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a dharma serves as an immediately-antecedent condition for mind, is that dharma also necessarily immediately following mind? This should be treated as a fourfold case. The first case: mind and mental factors emerging from a mindless attainment, and the second and subsequent moments of either of the two mindless attainments. The second case: the very first moment of either of the two mindless attainments as they first arise, and, within an ordinary state possessing mind, the arising, abiding, decay, and cessation of the various mind and mental factors. The third case: the very first moment of either of the two mindless attainments as they first arise, and, within an ordinary state possessing mind, mind and mental factors themselves. The fourth case: the second and subsequent moments of either of the two mindless attainments, and the arising, abiding, decay, and cessation of mind and mental factors emerging from a mindless attainment. If a dharma serves as an immediately-antecedent condition for mind, does it also serve as an immediately-antecedent condition for a mindless attainment? This, too, should be treated as a fourfold case: the previous third and fourth cases become the present first and second cases, and the previous first and second cases become the present third and fourth cases. Since the mind and mental factors that emerge from either of the two attainments are separated by a great intervening gap from the mind that entered the attainment in the first place, how can that entering mind possibly serve as an immediately-antecedent condition for them? Because, within that intervening gap, there is no other mind or mental factor interposed at all. With this, the immediately-antecedent condition has now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This closing fourfold case uses the two mindless attainments — non-percipience and cessation — as the perfect test case, since they are exactly the situations where mind is absent for an extended stretch, potentially spanning an entire lifetime in the case of non-percipience. The genuinely interesting payoff comes in the closing exchange: the mind that enters one of these attainments can still serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for the mind that eventually emerges from it, no matter how long the intervening gap, because "immediately-antecedent" never actually required strict temporal adjacency in the first place — only that no other mind or mental factor intervenes in between. A gap filled with nothing at all is, paradoxically, exactly what preserves the "immediate" relationship; it is only another mind wedged in between two others that would actually break it. With this careful resolution, the treatise's extended and genuinely demanding treatment of the immediately-antecedent condition comes to its close.

11. The Objective Condition: Every Dharma Is Always Someone's Possible Object

The third condition is the simplest to state and the broadest in reach — every dharma whatsoever counts as the objective condition for whichever mind or mental factor is capable of taking it as an object, whether or not that cognition is actually happening right now.

Chinese Source

所緣緣性，即一切法望心心所，隨其所應。謂如眼識及相應法，以一切色為所緣緣。如是耳識及相應法，以一切聲。鼻識相應以一切香。舌識相應以一切味。身識相應以一切觸。意識相應以一切法為所緣緣。若法與彼法為所緣，無時此與彼非所緣，於不緣位亦所緣攝，被緣、不緣其相一故。譬如薪等，於不燒時亦名所燒，相無異故。心心所法如於所緣處、事、剎那三皆決定，於所依亦有如是決定耶？應言亦有如是決定，然於現在親附自所依，過去未來與所依相離。有說：在過去亦親附所依。如是已釋所緣緣性。

Ru-Yi Translation

The nature of the objective condition is simply this: every dharma whatsoever, considered in relation to mind and mental factors, each according to its proper correspondence. That is: eye-consciousness and its associated dharmas take all form as their objective condition; likewise, ear-consciousness and its associated dharmas take all sound; the dharmas associated with nose-consciousness take all fragrance; those associated with tongue-consciousness take all flavor; those associated with body-consciousness take all tangible objects; and those associated with mental consciousness take all dharmas whatsoever as their objective condition. If a given dharma serves as the object for another dharma, there is never a time when the first fails to be an object for the second — even during a period when it is not actually being cognized, it is still subsumed under "being an object," since being cognized and not being cognized share exactly the same underlying characteristic. It is comparable to firewood: even at a time when it is not actually burning, it is still called "that which is burned," since its characteristic as fuel does not differ between the two cases. Just as mind and mental factors are fixed with respect to their object's location, their object's specific identity, and the moment of cognition, are they likewise fixed with respect to their basis? It should be said that they are indeed likewise fixed in this way — but, in the present, they are intimately attached to their own basis, whereas in the past or future, they are separated from that basis. There is another view, however, that even in the past, a dharma remains intimately attached to its basis. With this, the nature of the objective condition has now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objective condition (所緣緣, ālambana-pratyaya) is disarmingly simple to state: whatever a mind or mental factor is capable of taking as its object counts as its objective condition, full stop, across every one of the six sense-consciousnesses and their proper domains. The firewood image does real philosophical work here, addressing a subtle worry: does a piece of wood only count as "burnable fuel" while actually burning, or is that status something it holds even when the fire is out? The treatise's answer is that "being an object" works the same way — a dharma's status as something a given mind is capable of taking as its object does not switch on and off depending on whether that cognition happens to be occurring at this exact moment; the underlying relationship holds continuously, whether currently activated or not.

12. The Dominant Condition, and a Dharma's Blind Spot Toward Itself

The fourth and final condition turns out to be nothing more than the efficient cause under a new name — and the treatise pauses to ask whether any dharma could possibly lack all four conditions entirely.

Chinese Source

增上緣性即能作因，以即能作因為增上緣故。此緣體廣名增上緣，一切皆是增上緣故。既一切法亦所緣緣，此增上緣何獨體廣？俱有諸法未嘗為所緣，然為增上故，唯此體廣。或所作廣名增上緣，以一切法各除自性與一切有為為增上緣故。頗有法於法全非四緣不？有，謂自性於自性，於他性亦有。謂有為於無為，無為於無為。

Ru-Yi Translation

The nature of the dominant condition is simply the efficient cause, since the efficient cause itself is precisely what constitutes the dominant condition. This condition's own substance is broad, and so it is called the "dominant" condition, since absolutely everything whatsoever counts as a dominant condition for something else. But since every dharma whatsoever is also an objective condition, why is the dominant condition alone described as having "broad substance"? Because dharmas that co-arise together with a given consciousness are never that very consciousness's own objective condition, and yet they are still its dominant condition — hence the dominant condition alone reaches further, with genuinely broader substance. Alternatively, it may be said that the dominant condition is called "broad" because of the breadth of what it produces, since every dharma whatsoever, with the sole exception of its own essence, serves as a dominant condition for every conditioned dharma there is. Is there any dharma that is entirely lacking all four conditions with respect to some other dharma? There is: namely, a dharma's own essential nature with respect to itself; and this also holds with respect to what is other than itself — that is, a conditioned dharma with respect to the unconditioned, and the unconditioned with respect to the unconditioned.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

I want to flag some genuine uncertainty about the precise sense of "co-arising dharmas that are never an objective condition" here — my best-effort reading takes this to mean a mind's own simultaneously co-arising companions (its associated mental factors, its own characteristics of arising and the rest), which are dominant conditions for that mind but never themselves become that mind's cognized object, since a consciousness does not take its own co-arising companions as the external thing it is aware of. I have translated accordingly, but note this compressed technical point could bear a different precise reading than the one offered here.

The closing puzzle about dharmas entirely lacking all four conditions lands on a genuinely elegant point: nothing is ever, in the full technical sense, a condition for itself — a dharma simply is what it is, with no room for the relational structure any of the four conditions requires. And the unconditioned stands entirely outside the causal web the conditioned world weaves for itself: neither producing the unconditioned, nor being produced by it, in the ordinary causal sense these four categories were built to capture.

13. When Does Each Condition Actually Function? Arising, Ceasing, and Everywhere

A precise timing analysis pins down exactly when each of the conditions actually does its work — some only at the moment of ceasing, some only at arising, and the dominant condition, characteristically, everywhere at once.

Chinese Source

如是諸緣於何位法而興作用？頌曰：二因於正滅，三因於正生，餘二緣相違，而興於作用。論曰：前說五因為因緣性，二因作用於正滅時。正滅時言顯法現在，滅現前故，名正滅時。俱有、相應於法滅位方興作用，由此二因令俱生果有作用故。所言三因於正生者，謂未來法於正生位生現前故，名正生時。同類、遍行、異熟三種，於法生位作用方興。已說因緣二時作用，二緣作用與此相違。等無間緣於法生位而興作用，以彼生時前心心所與其處故。若所緣緣能緣滅位而興作用，以心心所要現在時方取境故。唯增上緣於一切位皆無障住，故彼作用隨無障位一切無遮。

Ru-Yi Translation

At which stage of a dharma, then, does each of these conditions actually exercise its function? Verse: two causes function at the very moment of ceasing; three causes function at the very moment of arising; the remaining two conditions function at the reverse of these. Prose: it was stated earlier that five of the six causes constitute the causal condition — of these, two causes exercise their function precisely at the moment of ceasing. "The very moment of ceasing" means that the dharma is currently present, on the verge of ceasing. The co-existent cause and the associated cause exercise their function only at the stage a dharma ceases, since it is through these two causes that a co-arisen result is given its function. As for the three causes said to function at the very moment of arising: this means that a future dharma, at the stage it is on the verge of arising, is on the point of coming into presence. The homogeneous cause, the universal cause, and the ripening cause — these three — exercise their function only at the stage a dharma arises. Having explained the two timings at which the causal condition functions, the remaining two conditions' functioning is the reverse of this. The immediately-antecedent condition exercises its function at the stage a dharma arises, since, at the very moment of that arising, the preceding mind and mental factors yield their place to it. The objective condition, by contrast, exercises its function precisely at the stage the cognizing dharma ceases, since mind and mental factors can only take hold of their object while genuinely present. Only the dominant condition, at every stage without exception, involves no obstruction whatsoever — and so its function, tracking wherever there is no obstruction, is never restricted at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This timing map divides the causal landscape neatly in two: co-existent and associated cause do their real work exactly as a dharma passes out of existence (since it is precisely at that boundary that their distinctive contribution — enabling genuinely mutual, simultaneous production — becomes fully realized), while homogeneous, universal, and ripening cause do their work at the opposite boundary, the moment of arising, since these are all about bringing a new dharma into being rather than sustaining an already-present one. The two remaining conditions mirror this same divide from a different angle: the immediately-antecedent condition operates at arising (handing off from one moment to the next), while the objective condition operates at ceasing (since a mind can only actually seize hold of its object while genuinely present, right up to the boundary where that presence ends). The dominant condition alone escapes this whole rhythm of arising-and-ceasing timing, since its criterion — simple non-obstruction — applies uniformly at every single stage without exception.

14. How Many Conditions Produce Each Kind of Dharma?

A closing accounting works out exactly how many of the four conditions converge to produce mind, the two mindless attainments, and every other dharma — setting up, in its very last line, one of Buddhist philosophy's most extensive arguments against a single divine cause.

Chinese Source

已說諸緣及興作用。應言何法由幾緣生？頌曰：心心所由四，二定但由三，餘由二緣生，非天次等故。論曰：心心所法由四緣生，此中因緣，謂五因性。等無間緣，謂前無間已生，非後心心所法。所緣緣者，謂隨所應，或色等五或一切法。增上緣者，謂隨所應，各除自性，餘一切法。滅盡、無想二定由三，除所緣緣，非能緣故。由因緣者，謂由二因：一俱有因，謂生等相；二同類因，謂前已生同地善法。等無間緣，謂入定心及相應法。增上緣者，謂如前說。如是二定，心等引生。礙心等起故，與心等但為等無間，非等無間緣。餘不相應及諸色法，由因、增上二緣所生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having explained the conditions and where their function operates, it should now be stated: by how many conditions is each kind of dharma produced? Verse: mind and mental factors are produced by four conditions; the two attainments are produced by only three; the remaining dharmas are produced by two conditions — not by a god, as shown by the sequential order of arising, and so on. Prose: mind and mental factors are produced by four conditions. Among these, the causal condition refers to the nature of five of the causes. The immediately-antecedent condition refers to the immediately preceding, already-arisen mind and mental factors, excepting the very last. The objective condition refers, according to what is appropriate in each case, either to the five categories such as form, or to all dharmas whatsoever. The dominant condition refers, according to what is appropriate, to every other dharma, excepting each dharma's own essence. The two attainments — cessation and non-percipience — are produced by only three conditions, excluding the objective condition, since they are not themselves capable of cognizing anything. As for the causal condition in their case: it consists of two causes — first, the co-existent cause, referring to the characteristics of arising and the rest; second, the homogeneous cause, referring to an earlier-arisen wholesome dharma of the same level. The immediately-antecedent condition refers to the mind that enters the attainment, together with its associated dharmas. The dominant condition is as already explained above. These two attainments, moreover, are drawn forth by mind and the like — but, since they obstruct the arising of mind and the rest, they serve, with respect to the mind that follows them, only as something immediately followed by, not as a genuine immediately-antecedent condition for it. The remaining formations dissociated from mind, and all physical dharmas, are produced by only two conditions: the causal condition and the dominant condition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The accounting descends in a clean hierarchy: mind and its mental factors, the most causally rich category, draw on all four conditions at once; the two mindless attainments, lacking any capacity to cognize an object, draw on three, missing only the objective condition; and everything else — form, and the formations dissociated from mind — gets by on just two, the causal and dominant conditions, missing both the immediately-antecedent condition (reserved for mind alone, as the fascicle's opening sections established at length) and the objective condition (reserved for whatever is capable of cognition).

The verse's closing phrase — "not by a god, as shown by the sequential order of arising" — is easy to read past, but it is doing real work: this entire, painstakingly detailed causal architecture is being offered as the alternative to, and implicit refutation of, the idea that the world could simply be the product of a single divine will. The next section takes this claim and argues it out in full.

15. Against a Single Divine Cause: The Argument from Sequence

The treatise turns to refute the idea that the world could be produced by a single cause — Īśvara, or Primordial Nature — leading with the simple, powerful observation that things in the world plainly arise one after another, never all at once.

Chinese Source

一切世間唯從如上所說諸因諸緣所起，非自在天我勝性等一因所起，此有何因？若一切成許由因者，豈不便捨一切世間由自在等一因生論。又諸世間非自在等一因所起，次第等故。謂諸世間若自在等一因生者，則應一切俱時而生非次第起；現見諸法次第而生，故知定非一因所起。若執自在隨欲故然，謂彼欲令此法今起、此法今滅、此於後時，是則應成非一因起，亦由樂欲差別生故。或差別欲應一時生，所因自在無差別故。若欲差別更待餘因不俱起者，則非一切唯用自在一法為因。或所待因亦應更待餘因差別方次第生，則所待因應無邊際。若更不待餘差別因，此因應無次第生義，則差別欲非次第生。若許諸因展轉差別無有邊際，信無始故，徒執自在為諸法因，不越釋門因緣正理。

Ru-Yi Translation

The entire world arises solely from the causes and conditions already described above — never from a single cause such as Īśvara, or the Self, or Primordial Nature, and the like. What is the reasoning behind this? If it is already granted, on all sides, that everything arises through a cause, does this not already amount to abandoning the theory that the entire world arises from a single cause such as Īśvara? Moreover, the world is not produced by a single cause such as Īśvara, for the following reason: things arise in sequence. That is: if the world were truly produced by a single cause such as Īśvara, then everything ought to arise simultaneously, all at once, rather than arising in sequence — yet it is directly observed that things arise in sequence. Hence it is known for certain that the world is not produced by a single cause. Suppose one holds that this happens simply because Īśvara wills it so — that is, he wills that this dharma arise now, that this other dharma cease now, and that this further dharma arise only at some later time. But this would then mean the world is not, after all, produced by a single cause, since it would also depend on differences among his various acts of will and desire. Alternatively, if these differing acts of will were all supposed to arise simultaneously, then, since the underlying cause — Īśvara himself — is without any internal difference, this leaves nothing to explain why the different acts of will differ at all. If, instead, these differing acts of will depend on yet further causes, and so do not all arise together, then it is no longer the case that everything whatsoever depends solely on Īśvara as its one and only cause. Or, if the causes these acts of will depend upon must themselves further depend on yet other, differentiated causes in order to arise in sequence, then the causes being depended upon would have to extend without any limit at all. And if, instead, one insists that no further differentiated cause is depended upon beyond this point, then this cause itself ought to have no sense of arising in sequence at all — meaning the differing acts of will could not, after all, arise in sequence either. If, however, one is willing to accept that causes differ from one another reciprocally without any limit at all, extending backward without beginning, then insisting on Īśvara as the cause of all dharmas serves no purpose at all, since this does not go beyond the correct principle of cause and condition already taught within our own tradition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This opens one of the most extensive and philosophically serious arguments against theism found anywhere in classical Buddhist literature, and the core move is a clean dilemma. Everything in the observable world arises in a staggered sequence, not all at once — so any single, undifferentiated cause must somehow explain why its output is staggered rather than simultaneous. If Īśvara's will simply differs from moment to moment to produce this staggering, then it is no longer accurate to say the world has one cause; it has as many causes as there are distinct acts of will, and Īśvara's own unity has already been quietly abandoned. If, instead, his will is itself undifferentiated, nothing explains why the world it produces would ever be staggered rather than instantaneous.

The argument's real force lands in its final move: once a theist is pushed to explain differentiation in Īśvara's will by appeal to yet further causes behind that will, they have implicitly conceded the very point Buddhist philosophy has been making all along — that everything, without exception, depends on prior differentiated causes, extending backward without any first beginning. At that point, positing "Īśvara" as a special exception adds nothing beyond what this tradition's own causal theory, painstakingly built up across the last two fascicles, already provides on its own terms.

16. Against a Single Divine Cause: What Benefit, and What Independence?

The refutation closes with two further blows — what could a truly self-sufficient being possibly gain from creating the world at all, and does positing a creator not simply deny the plainly observable causes already all around us? — ending in a famously blunt verse and a call to pity, not anger, toward those who believe otherwise.

Chinese Source

若言自在欲雖頓生，而諸世間不俱起者，由隨自在欲所生故，理亦不然。彼自在欲，前位與後無差別故。又彼自在作大功力，生諸世間得何義利？若為發喜生諸世間，此喜離餘方便不發，是則自在於發喜中既必待餘，應非自在。於喜既爾，餘亦應然，差別因緣不可得故。或若自在生地獄等無量苦具逼害有情，為見如斯發生自喜。咄哉！何用此自在為？依彼頌言，誠為善說。由險利能燒，可畏恒逼害，樂食血肉髓，故名魯達羅。又若信受一切世間唯自在天一因所起，則為誹撥現見世間所餘因緣人功等事。若言自在待餘因緣助發功能方成因者，但是朋敬自在天言，離所餘因緣不見別用故。或彼自在要餘因緣助方能生，應非自在。若執初起自在為因，餘後續生待餘因者，則初所起不待餘因，應無始成，猶如自在。我勝性等隨其所應，如自在天應廣徵遣，故無有法唯一因生。奇哉世間不修勝慧，如愚禽獸，良足可悲。彼彼生中別別造業，自受異熟及士用果，而妄計有自在等因。且止破邪，應辯正義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose one says that, although Īśvara's acts of will arise all at once, instantaneously, the resulting world does not thereby arise all at once, because the world's actual unfolding follows only gradually from what he wills. This reasoning, too, does not hold — since, on this account, Īśvara's own act of will would itself have to be entirely without difference between its earlier and later phases, offering nothing to explain the staggered timing of the world that supposedly results from it. Moreover, what benefit could Īśvara possibly gain by exerting such enormous effort to produce the entire world? If it is said that he produces the world in order to generate joy for himself, then this joy, too, would be unable to arise apart from some further supporting means — meaning that, with respect to generating this joy, Īśvara would necessarily depend on something else, and so could not, after all, be truly self-sufficient and independent. And if this holds true for his joy, it should hold equally for everything else as well, since no genuinely differentiating cause or condition can actually be found to distinguish his case from ordinary dependent causation. Or, if it is Īśvara who creates the hells and the countless other instruments of suffering that torment and oppress sentient beings, taking joy for himself precisely in witnessing such things — then how contemptible! What use could there possibly be for such an "Īśvara"? In this connection, the following verse truly says it well: "Because he is treacherous, sharp, and capable of burning; because he is fearsome and forever oppresses; because he delights in feeding on blood, flesh, and marrow — he is therefore named Rudra." Moreover, if one truly believes that the entire world is produced solely by the single cause of Īśvara, this amounts to a denial of all the other directly observable causes and conditions in the world — human effort, and the like. If it is said that Īśvara depends on other causes and conditions to assist in bringing his creative power into effect, then this is merely a partisan defense spoken out of devotion to Īśvara, since no distinct function of his own can actually be discerned apart from these other causes and conditions. Or, if Īśvara genuinely requires the assistance of other causes and conditions in order to produce anything at all, he cannot, after all, be truly self-sufficient and independent. And if one insists instead that Īśvara alone is the cause of the very first, original arising, with everything that follows afterward depending on other causes, then this original arising itself, depending on no further cause, would have to be beginningless and self-established — exactly like Īśvara himself is claimed to be. The Self, Primordial Nature, and the like should likewise be extensively refuted and set aside, each according to its own case, in exactly the same way as Īśvara. For this reason, there is definitely no dharma whatsoever produced by a single cause alone. How extraordinary it is that the world does not cultivate superior wisdom, remaining instead like foolish birds and beasts — this is truly something to be pitied. In each and every one of their many rebirths, sentient beings themselves individually perform their own karma, and themselves individually experience its ripening result and its agent-efficacy result — and yet they falsely imagine there to be some single cause such as Īśvara behind it all. Let us now set aside this refutation of wrong views, and turn instead to explaining the correct position.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "what benefit?" argument is a genuinely striking move: a being who is supposedly fully self-sufficient and lacking nothing should, by definition, have no need to act at all — action implies a goal not yet achieved, some lack still to be filled. If Īśvara creates the world for his own joy, that joy turns out to depend on something outside himself (the created world, and whatever else is needed to make witnessing it pleasurable), which directly contradicts the very self-sufficiency that was supposed to define him in the first place. And if that joy comes specifically from witnessing suffering — the hells, and the torment of sentient beings — the argument turns from logic to open moral condemnation, culminating in the biting verse identifying such a figure not with a benevolent creator but with Rudra, the fierce and destructive aspect of Śiva in Indian tradition, a being who delights in blood and suffering rather than one worthy of worship as the source of all things.

The closing observation is worth sitting with as much as the logic itself: rather than anger at those who hold this view, the treatise expresses something closer to sorrow — comparing unreflective believers in a single-cause creator to "foolish birds and beasts," not out of contempt, but because they have missed something the treatise considers plainly available to anyone willing to look closely at how karma, cause, and result actually work: that every being's own actions, and their own experienced results, are sufficient explanation on their own, with no need to posit anything beyond the intricate, beginningless web of causes and conditions this entire section has been carefully constructing. With this, the polemic closes, and the treatise turns back to its own constructive project.

17. The Great Elements and Derived Form as Mutual Causes

Having closed the refutation of a single divine cause, the treatise returns to its constructive project with a precise causal accounting: exactly how many and which of the six causes hold between the four great elements and the form derived from them, in each direction.

Chinese Source

前言餘法由二緣生，於中云何大種、所造，自他相望互為因緣？頌曰：大為大二因，為所造五種，造為造三種，為大唯一因。論曰：初言大為大二因者，是諸大種更互相望，但為俱有同類因義。大於所造能為五因。何等為五？謂生、依、立、持、養別故。如是五因，但是能作因之差別。從彼起故說為生因，生已隨逐大種轉故。如依師等說為依因，能任持故。如壁持畫說為立因。不斷因故說為持因。增長因故說為養因。如是則顯大與所造為起、變、持、住、長因性。諸所造色自互相望容有三因，所謂俱有、同類、異熟。其能作因無差別轉，故不恒數。俱有因者，謂隨心轉身語二業，非餘造色。同類因者，一切前生於後同類。異熟因者，謂身語業能招異熟眼根等果。所造於大但為一因，謂異熟因。身語二業能招異熟，大種果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

It was said above that the remaining dharmas are produced by two conditions. Within this, how do the great elements and derived form, considered each in relation to itself and to the other, serve as causes for one another? Verse: the great elements, for one another, are two causes; for derived form, they are five kinds of cause; derived form, for derived form, is three kinds of cause; for the great elements, it is only one cause. Prose: as for the first statement, that the great elements are two causes for one another — this means that, considered in relation to one another, the great elements serve only in the sense of a co-existent cause and a homogeneous cause. The great elements can serve as five causes for derived form. What are the five? The generative cause, the support cause, the establishing cause, the sustaining cause, and the nurturing cause, each distinct. These five causes are simply distinctions drawn within the efficient cause. It is called the generative cause because derived form arises from the great elements. It is called the support cause because, once arisen, derived form continues to follow along with the great elements as they operate — comparable to relying upon a teacher and the like — since the great elements are able to sustain it in this way. It is called the establishing cause, comparable to a wall supporting a painting upon it. It is called the sustaining cause because it is a cause of derived form's uninterrupted continuity. It is called the nurturing cause because it is a cause of derived form's growth and increase. This then shows that the great elements constitute, with respect to derived form, the nature of a cause for arising, for change, for support, for abiding, and for growth. Derived form, considered in relation to other derived form, admits of three causes: namely, the co-existent cause, the homogeneous cause, and the ripening cause. As for the efficient cause, it operates everywhere without distinction, and so is not separately counted here. The co-existent cause refers to the two kinds of karma, bodily and vocal, that operate in accordance with mind — not to other kinds of derived form. The homogeneous cause refers to any earlier-arisen derived form being of the same kind as a later one. The ripening cause refers to bodily and vocal karma being able to bring about a ripened result such as the eye faculty and the like. Derived form, with respect to the great elements, serves as only one cause: namely, the ripening cause — since bodily and vocal karma are able to bring about the great elements themselves as their ripened result.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The five causes the great elements exercise over derived form (生因, 依因, 立因, 持因, 養因) are all, the text is careful to note, simply named subdivisions of the single efficient cause (能作因) — not separate members of the six-cause list itself, but finer distinctions drawn within the broadest and least restrictive of the six. I have translated each somewhat literally — generative, support, establishing, sustaining, and nurturing — since the closing line's own five-term restatement ("起、變、持、住、長", arising, change, support, abiding, growth) does not obviously map onto the five one-for-one in the order given, and I want to flag that uncertainty honestly rather than force a clean correspondence I am not confident of.

The traditional analogies are worth sitting with individually: a wall does not create the painting on it, nor did it generate the pigment, yet without the wall's continued standing the painting could not remain established where it is — this is offered as the model for "establishing cause," distinct from the more straightforward "generative cause" of the elements simply giving rise to derived form in the first place. The reverse direction is deliberately asymmetric and much narrower: derived form gives back to the great elements only a single kind of causal contribution, the ripening cause, since it is specifically bodily and vocal karma — themselves a kind of derived form — that ripen into a new set of great elements as their result. Ordinary derived form such as the sense faculties has no comparable power to produce the elements in return.

Fascicle 7: More to Come

Having settled the causal relations between the great elements and derived form, the treatise turns to a new and genuinely massive topic: the twelve basic types of mind — four in the desire realm, three each in the form and formless realms, and two uncontaminated — and the detailed combinatorial rules governing exactly which of the twelve can arise immediately after which.
