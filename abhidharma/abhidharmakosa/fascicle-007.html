What Follows What, and Why?

This page carries the opening of fascicle 7, closing Chapter 2's fifth part (根品第二之五, the Indriya-nirdeśa's final installment) with the four conditions (四緣) — the classical scriptural causal scheme of causal condition, immediately-antecedent condition, objective condition, and dominant condition. The fascicle opens with a genuinely surprising claim: physical form can never serve as an immediately-antecedent condition at all, since its own succession is too irregular — a claim that leads into one of this treatise's most striking epistemological puzzles, how the Buddha can know the fixed order of events yet to come, given that the future itself has no fixed order at all.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 3 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 7 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. A New Topic: The Four Conditions

Having spent fascicle 6 on the six causes, the treatise turns to the classical scriptural scheme of four conditions — mapping most of it directly onto ground already covered, while flagging one genuinely new and demanding case: the immediately-antecedent condition.

Chinese Source

廣說因已。緣復云何？頌曰：說有四種緣，因緣五因性，等無間非後，心心所已生，所緣一切法，增上即能作。論曰：於何處說？謂契經中。如契經中說四緣性，謂因緣性、等無間緣性、所緣緣性、增上緣性。此中性者是緣種類。於六因內除能作因，所餘五因是因緣性。除阿羅漢臨涅槃時最後心心所法，諸餘已生心心所法是等無間緣性。此緣生法等而無間，依是義立等無間名。

Ru-Yi Translation

Causes have now been explained at length. What, then, of conditions? Verse: it is said that there are four kinds of condition — the causal condition is the nature of five of the causes; the immediately-antecedent condition is every already-arisen mind and mental factor, except the very last; the objective condition is all dharmas; the dominant condition is simply the efficient cause. Prose: where is this stated? In a sutra. As a sutra states the nature of the four conditions: the nature of the causal condition, the nature of the immediately-antecedent condition, the nature of the objective condition, and the nature of the dominant condition. Here, "nature" means the kind or category of condition. Among the six causes, excepting the efficient cause, the remaining five causes constitute the nature of the causal condition. Excepting the very last mind and mental factors of an arhat at the moment of final nirvana, all other already-arisen mind and mental factors constitute the nature of the immediately-antecedent condition. This condition gives rise to a dharma equally and without interruption — it is on the basis of this meaning that the name "immediately-antecedent" is established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four conditions (四緣, catuḥ-pratyaya) are the classical scriptural scheme this whole tradition's causal theory ultimately answers to — and the treatise wastes no time showing how neatly three of the four map onto ground already covered: the causal condition is simply five of the six causes bundled together (everything except the efficient cause, which gets its own separate condition below), and the dominant condition turns out to be nothing more than the efficient cause under a new name. The genuinely new material is the immediately-antecedent condition (等無間緣, samanantara-pratyaya) — confined specifically to mind and mental factors, and excluding only the very last moment of an arhat's continuum, since nothing follows that moment at all. Why this condition should be restricted to mind alone, and not to form or the other formations, is the question the next several sections work through in real depth.

2. Why Form Can Never Be an Immediately-Antecedent Condition

A genuinely surprising restriction: physical form, however reliably it seems to cause further form, can never count as an immediately-antecedent condition — because, unlike mind, its own succession is fundamentally irregular, illustrated through burning rice stalks and a single seed's growth into a towering banyan tree.

Chinese Source

由此色等皆不可立等無間緣，不等生故。謂欲界色或無間生欲界色界二無表色，或無間生欲界無漏二無表色。以諸色法雜亂現前，等無間緣生無雜亂，故色不立等無間緣。尊者世友作如是言：於一身中一長養色相續不斷，復有第二長養色生，不相違害，故不可立等無間緣。大德復言：以諸色法無間生起或少或多，謂或有時從多生少，如燒稻稈大聚為灰。或時復有從少生多，如細種生諾瞿陀樹，根莖枝葉漸次增榮，聳幹垂條多所蔭映。

Ru-Yi Translation

For this reason, form and the like can never be established as an immediately-antecedent condition, since their succession is not regular. That is: a desire-realm form dharma may, in the immediately following moment, give rise to either of the two kinds of unmanifest form belonging to the desire realm or the form realm, or may give rise to either of the two kinds of uncontaminated unmanifest form belonging to the desire realm. Because these physical dharmas arise in a jumbled, irregular manner, while the immediately-antecedent condition specifically produces its result in a regular, non-jumbled way, form cannot be established as an immediately-antecedent condition. The Venerable Vasumitra explains it this way: within a single body, one continuous stream of nourished-and-grown form does not cease, and yet a second instance of nourished-and-grown form can arise alongside it without the two conflicting or harming one another — and it is for this reason that form cannot be established as an immediately-antecedent condition. The Bhadanta further explains: physical dharmas arise in the immediately following moment sometimes in smaller quantity, sometimes in greater — that is, sometimes a great mass gives rise to something smaller, as when a large heap of burning rice stalks is reduced to ash; and sometimes, conversely, something small gives rise to something great, as when a tiny seed gives rise to a great banyan tree, its roots, trunk, branches, and leaves gradually flourishing and increasing, its towering trunk and hanging branches casting abundant shade.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely counterintuitive restriction, and worth pausing on: form obviously causes further form in the ordinary sense — a seed becomes a tree, a body persists moment to moment — yet none of this qualifies as "immediately-antecedent condition" in this tradition's precise technical sense, which demands a very specific kind of regularity: a fixed one-to-one, equal succession, never a jump in scale or kind. Two independent explanations are offered for why form fails this test. Vasumitra's focuses on physical overlap: within one body, an existing stream of nourished bodily tissue can persist right alongside a freshly arising second instance, without the two ever being in the kind of clean, exclusive one-after-another relationship the condition requires. The Bhadanta's explanation is more dramatic and more memorable — form's scale is simply unpredictable across a single moment's transition, sometimes collapsing dramatically (a great mass of burning rice stalks reduced to a small heap of ash) and sometimes exploding outward (a single tiny seed becoming an enormous, shade-casting banyan tree) — nothing about the "before" reliably predicts the scale of the "after," which is exactly the irregularity that disqualifies it.

3. Do Mental Factors Show the Same Irregularity? A Careful Clarification

An objection presses the same challenge against mind and its mental factors — don't they, too, vary unpredictably in quantity from moment to moment? The resolution draws a precise distinction between a whole state of mind and any single mental factor considered strictly on its own.

Chinese Source

豈不心所無間生時亦有少多品類非等？謂善不善無記心中，有尋有伺三摩地等，此於異類實有少多，然自類中無非等義，謂無少受無間生多，或復從多無間生少，想等亦爾，無非等過。豈唯自類前能為後等無間緣？不爾。云何？前心品法總為後品等無間緣，非唯自類。且於受等自體類中無少生多，以說等義。唯執同類相續者說，唯自類有等無間緣，心唯生心、受唯生受，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

But is it not the case that, when mental factors arise in immediate succession, there is likewise a difference in quantity and kind, not a regular equality? That is: within a wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral state of mind, there are things like applied thought, sustained thought, and concentration — and, considered across these different categories, there genuinely is a difference in quantity. But within any single one of these categories taken on its own, there is no such irregularity: that is, there is no case of a small amount of feeling giving rise, in immediate succession, to a great amount, or, conversely, a great amount giving rise to a small amount; and the same holds for perception and the rest — no irregularity of this kind occurs. Is it, then, only a dharma of its own same category, occurring earlier, that can serve as an immediately-antecedent condition for a dharma of that same category occurring later? No. What, then, is the case? A whole prior state of mind, taken as a whole, serves as the immediately-antecedent condition for the whole state of mind that follows — not merely a same-category dharma for another same-category dharma. It is only with respect to feeling and the like, considered strictly within its own essential category, that there is no case of a small giving rise to a great, which is what establishes the sense of "regular equality" being discussed here. It is only those who hold that immediately-antecedent condition requires strict same-category continuity who say that only a dharma of the very same category can serve this role — that mind only ever gives rise to mind, feeling only ever gives rise to feeling, and so on, at length.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a careful piece of conceptual disambiguation, distinguishing two very different things that could be meant by "regular succession." Across different categories of mental factor — applied thought versus concentration, say — there is indeed real variation in how many are present from one moment to the next, depending on which meditative state one occupies. But this is not the kind of irregularity that disqualified form: what matters is whether a single mental factor, considered strictly within its own kind (feeling as feeling, perception as perception), ever jumps unpredictably in scale the way form does — and it never does. Feeling always gives rise to feeling in a steady, one-for-one way, never suddenly ballooning or collapsing in quantity the way a seed becomes a tree or ash a heap of rice stalks.

The section also quietly corrects a possible overreading: the immediately-antecedent condition does not require that a dharma's successor belong to the exact same narrow category — a whole state of mind, including its entire cluster of associated mental factors together, serves as the antecedent condition for the next whole state of mind as an integrated unit, not each individual mental factor type matching narrowly to its own kind alone. That stricter, same-category-only reading is flagged as belonging only to a particular rival position, not the treatise's own settled view.

4. A Rejected Account: Can a Long-Past Affliction Condition a Present One?

A rival explanation tries to account for how a defiled mind can follow an undefiled one, borrowing the mechanism already used to explain how mind resumes after the attainment of cessation — but the explanation fails its very first test case.

Chinese Source

若從無染無間染生，此染心中所有煩惱，用先滅煩惱為等無間緣。如出滅定，心還用先滅正入滅定，心為緣故起。彼說非善，初無漏心應闕此緣而得生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a defiled mind arises immediately after an undefiled one, the afflictions present within that defiled mind, on this account, make use of some previously-ceased affliction as their immediately-antecedent condition — comparable to how, when mind emerges from the attainment of cessation, it makes use of the very mind that had previously ceased upon entering that attainment as its condition for arising once again. This explanation is not sound, since the very first uncontaminated mind would then have to arise despite lacking this very condition altogether.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The proposal borrows a genuinely clever mechanism from fascicle 5's discussion of how mind resumes after the mindless attainment of cessation — there, a long-past moment of mind was allowed to serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for a much later moment, skipping over the intervening gap entirely. Applied here, the idea is that a newly arising affliction could likewise reach back to some earlier, already-ceased instance of that same affliction as its condition, explaining how defilement can resurface in a mind that was, in its immediately preceding moment, undefiled.

The rejection is swift and decisive: the very first moment any affliction is ever cut off by the uncontaminated path of seeing has, by definition, no earlier instance of that same affliction to serve as its "previously-ceased" antecedent — there was no prior occasion for it to have already ceased and be waiting in reserve. If this mechanism were genuinely necessary, the first moment of uncontaminated insight could never arise in the first place, which is plainly false.

5. Formations Dissociated from Mind, and the Future, Are Excluded Too

The same irregularity that disqualified form is shown to disqualify the formations dissociated from mind as well — and future dharmas are excluded for a related but distinct reason: they exist in an unsettled, jumbled state with no fixed sequence at all.

Chinese Source

不相應行亦如諸色雜亂現前，故非等無間緣。三界及不繫可俱現前故，何緣不許未來世有等無間緣？以未來法雜亂而住無前後故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The formations dissociated from mind, too, arise in a jumbled, irregular manner just like form, and so likewise cannot serve as an immediately-antecedent condition — since dharmas belonging to the three realms, and dharmas unbound to any realm, can all arise together at once, mixed with one another. Why, then, is it not accepted that there is an immediately-antecedent condition existing in the future? Because future dharmas exist in a jumbled state, with no fixed sequence of before and after among them at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The formations dissociated from mind — possession, class-commonality, the characteristics, and the rest, spanning fascicles 4 and 5 — fail the regularity test for essentially the same reason form does: dharmas belonging to entirely different realms, or to none at all, can arise together in the same moment without any predictable, fixed one-to-one pattern governing their succession. And future dharmas fail for a deeper, more fundamental reason still: the very idea of "immediately following" presupposes a determinate order — this, then that — but the future, on this tradition's account, simply does not yet have any fixed order at all. Everything that might arise sits together in an undifferentiated, "jumbled" state until the moment of its actual arising settles which of the possibilities becomes real, which is exactly what the next section's puzzle turns on.

6. If the Future Has No Fixed Order, How Does the Buddha Know What Comes Next?

A genuinely striking epistemological puzzle: if future dharmas exist in an unsettled, jumbled state with no fixed sequence, how can the Buddha reliably know exactly which dharma will immediately follow which? The answer distinguishes his foreknowledge sharply from ordinary inference.

Chinese Source

如何世尊知未來世此法無間、此法應生？比過現法而現知故。傳說世尊見從過去如此類業此類果生，是法無間生如是法。又從現在如此類業此類果生，是法無間生如是法。如是見已，便於未來諸亂住法，能正了達此法無間此法應生。雖如是知而非比智。由佛比類過去現在因果次第，便於未來亂住諸法能現了達，謂未來世如是有情造如是業招如是果，是願智攝故非比智。若爾，世尊未見前際，於後際法應不能知。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, then, does the World-Honored One know that, in the future, this dharma will immediately follow, and this other dharma is bound to arise? By directly knowing this on the basis of comparison with past and present dharmas. Tradition holds that the World-Honored One observes that, in the past, this kind of karma gave rise to this kind of result, this dharma arising immediately after that one; and, likewise, in the present, this kind of karma gives rise to this kind of result, this dharma arising immediately after that one. Having observed this, he is then able to correctly comprehend, with respect to the many jumbled, indeterminately-existing dharmas of the future, that this dharma will immediately follow, and that dharma is bound to arise. Although he knows in this way, this is not inferential knowledge. Rather, it is by the Buddha's comparing the sequence of cause and effect across the past and the present that he is able to directly comprehend the many jumbled dharmas of the future — knowing that, in the future, a certain being will perform a certain karma and thereby bring about a certain result. This is subsumed under the knowledge of aspiration, and is not inferential knowledge. If that is so, then, since the World-Honored One has not directly witnessed the very beginning of time itself, he ought to be equally unable to know anything about the furthest future either.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The puzzle cuts straight to the heart of what it means for the future to have "no fixed order" at all: if that is literally true, on what basis could even a Buddha reliably predict which specific dharma will immediately follow which? The answer offered is careful on a point that might otherwise seem like a technicality but is philosophically load-bearing: this foreknowledge is explicitly denied the status of ordinary inference (比智, anumāna), even though the mechanism described — extrapolating a pattern from repeatedly observed past and present cause-and-effect sequences — sounds, on its face, exactly like inference. Instead, it is classified as a distinct supernormal faculty, "the knowledge of aspiration" (願智, praṇidhi-jñāna): a direct, immediate comprehension that happens to be triggered or informed by prior observation, rather than a chain of logical steps reasoning from premises to a conclusion.

The closing objection is a sharp one, and genuinely difficult: if the Buddha's foreknowledge of the future depends on having already observed enough past and present pattern to extrapolate from, doesn't this put a hard limit on what he can know — nothing about times too far removed from anything he has actually witnessed, including, presumably, the very beginning of time itself, if such a beginning even exists? The treatise's response to this genuinely difficult challenge follows next.

7. A Rejected Theory of Bodily Omens, and the Sautrāntika's Resolution

One further theory proposes that the Buddha reads the future from a physical precursor-sign within the body itself — rejected because it would reduce the Buddha's omniscience to mere fortune-telling, in favor of a direct, unmediated knowing beyond both inference and omen.

Chinese Source

有餘復言：有情身內有未來世果因先兆，是不相應行蘊差別。佛唯觀此便知未來，非要現遊靜慮通慧。若爾，諸佛便於未來占相故知，非為現證。故如經部諸師所言，世尊舉意遍知諸法非比非占。此說為善。如世尊說：諸佛德用、諸佛境界不可思議。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a further view: within a sentient being's own body, there exists a kind of precursor sign for a future result's cause — a particular kind of formation dissociated from mind. The Buddha, simply by observing this sign, thereby knows the future, without necessarily needing to actually enter into meditative absorption and exercise supernormal wisdom. If that were so, the Buddhas would then come to know the future merely by reading omens and signs, rather than through direct realization. Hence, as the Sautrāntika masters say: the World-Honored One, simply by directing his mind, comprehensively knows all dharmas — neither through inference, nor through reading omens. This explanation is the correct one. As the World-Honored One himself states: "The qualities and functions of the Buddhas, and the objects within the Buddhas' own domain, are inconceivable."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The bodily-omen theory tries to naturalize the Buddha's foreknowledge into something almost mechanical — a physical sign embedded in the body itself, functioning like a readable indicator of what is to come. The rejection is pointed: this would demote the Buddha's omniscience to something closer to divination, reading external signs rather than directly knowing reality. The Sautrāntika's alternative refuses to explain the mechanism away at all, insisting instead on a form of knowing that is genuinely direct and unmediated — neither inference from evidence nor interpretation of a sign, simply comprehensive, immediate cognition. The closing citation is a fitting capstone: rather than press the mechanism further, the treatise lets the Buddha's own words mark the limit of ordinary explanation — some things about a Buddha's own cognitive domain are declared, quite deliberately, beyond what conceptual analysis can fully capture.

8. A Further Puzzle: Fixed Sequences Within Specific Attainments

If the future truly has no fixed order, why does scripture insist that specific advanced meditative states are always followed by one, and only one, specific result? The resolution distinguishes a tight causal dependency from the broader immediately-antecedent condition.

Chinese Source

若於未來無定前後次第安立，何故但言世第一法無間唯生苦法智忍不生餘法？如是廣說，乃至金剛喻定無間，唯生盡智不生餘法。若此法生繫屬彼法，要彼無間此乃得生，如芽等生要藉種等。然此非有等無間緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is truly no fixed sequence of before and after established among future dharmas, why is it said that, immediately following the world-supreme dharma, only the forbearance of the dharma-cognition of suffering arises, and no other dharma? And so on, at length, up through: immediately following the vajra-like concentration, only the knowledge of extinction arises, and no other dharma? Because the arising of this particular dharma is bound up with, and dependent upon, that particular dharma — it is only immediately following that specific dharma that this one can arise at all, just as a sprout's arising depends upon a seed, and so on. This, however, does not amount to there being a genuine immediately-antecedent condition in the fuller sense already established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The objection presses a real inconsistency: the treatise has just insisted future dharmas exist in an undetermined, jumbled state with no fixed sequence — yet scripture plainly states that certain specific attainments always give rise to one, and only one, specific successor, with no variation at all. The resolution draws a fine but important line: a tight, dedicated causal dependency between two specific dharmas — this one can only ever arise immediately after that one, the way a sprout depends specifically on its own seed — is not the same claim as saying the future in general has a fixed, determinate order running through it. These are narrow, specific bindings between particular pairs of dharmas, carved out as genuine exceptions, rather than evidence that "immediately-antecedent condition," understood in its full technical generality, actually applies to future dharmas as a class.

9. "Manas" Versus "Immediately-Antecedent Condition": A Fine Distinction

Why is an arhat's very last moment of mind still called "manas," yet denied the status of an immediately-antecedent condition? The answer distinguishes a name earned by serving as a structural basis from a name earned only by an actually completed function.

Chinese Source

諸阿羅漢最後心心所，何緣故說非等無間緣？無餘心等續此起故。豈不如是無間滅心亦名為意？後心無間識既不生應不名意，意是依所顯非作用所顯。此最後心有所依義，餘緣闕故後識不生，等無間緣作用所顯。若法此緣取為果已，定無諸法及諸有情能為障礙令彼不起，故最後心雖得名意，而不可說等無間緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is it said that an arhat's very last mind and mental factors are not an immediately-antecedent condition? Because no further mind, and so on, continues to arise following it. But is this immediately-ceasing mind not also called "manas"? If no consciousness arises in the moment immediately following this final mind, should it not then also fail to be called "manas"? "Manas" is a name revealed by virtue of serving as a basis, not a name revealed by virtue of actually performing a function. This final mind does have the sense of being able to serve as a basis — it is merely that, because the remaining necessary conditions are absent, no later consciousness in fact arises. "Immediately-antecedent condition," by contrast, is a name revealed specifically by virtue of a function actually being performed: once a given dharma has taken hold as this specific kind of condition for a result, there is then definitely no dharma, and no sentient being, capable of obstructing that result and preventing it from arising. Hence, although this final mind can indeed still be called "manas," it cannot be said to be an immediately-antecedent condition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely elegant piece of conceptual surgery, distinguishing two labels that might seem, at first glance, to have to travel together. "Manas" (意) names a structural role — being the kind of thing capable of serving as the basis for a following moment of consciousness — and an arhat's final mind genuinely qualifies for this role in principle, regardless of whether anything actually follows it. "Immediately-antecedent condition," by contrast, names something stronger: a guarantee that the result will actually, unstoppably arise, with nothing in the universe capable of obstructing it. The arhat's final mind fails this second, stronger test not because of any deficiency in the mind itself, but simply because the other conditions needed for a further moment of consciousness — most obviously, a continuing living body — are no longer present at the moment of final nirvana. A capacity that goes unexercised, for want of supporting circumstances, still earns the structural name; it does not earn the name that requires the function to have actually, guaranteedly gone through.

10. A Closing Fourfold Case, and the Immediately-Antecedent Condition Concludes

A precise fourfold case tests whether "condition for mind" and "immediately following mind" are truly the same thing — using the two mindless attainments, which create a genuine gap in mental continuity, as the decisive edge case.

Chinese Source

若法與心為等無間，彼法亦是心無間耶？應作四句。第一句者，謂無心定出心心所，及第二等二定剎那。第二句者，謂初所起二定剎那，及有心位諸心心所生住異滅。第三句者，謂初所起二定剎那，及有心位心心所法。第四句者，謂第二等二定剎那，及無心定出心心所生住異滅。若法與心為等無間，與無心定為無間耶？應作四句。謂前第三第四句為今第一第二句，即前第一第二句為今第三第四句。從二定出諸心心所，望入定心中間遠隔，如何為彼等無間耶？中間不隔心心所故。如是已釋等無間緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a dharma serves as an immediately-antecedent condition for mind, is that dharma also necessarily immediately following mind? This should be treated as a fourfold case. The first case: mind and mental factors emerging from a mindless attainment, and the second and subsequent moments of either of the two mindless attainments. The second case: the very first moment of either of the two mindless attainments as they first arise, and, within an ordinary state possessing mind, the arising, abiding, decay, and cessation of the various mind and mental factors. The third case: the very first moment of either of the two mindless attainments as they first arise, and, within an ordinary state possessing mind, mind and mental factors themselves. The fourth case: the second and subsequent moments of either of the two mindless attainments, and the arising, abiding, decay, and cessation of mind and mental factors emerging from a mindless attainment. If a dharma serves as an immediately-antecedent condition for mind, does it also serve as an immediately-antecedent condition for a mindless attainment? This, too, should be treated as a fourfold case: the previous third and fourth cases become the present first and second cases, and the previous first and second cases become the present third and fourth cases. Since the mind and mental factors that emerge from either of the two attainments are separated by a great intervening gap from the mind that entered the attainment in the first place, how can that entering mind possibly serve as an immediately-antecedent condition for them? Because, within that intervening gap, there is no other mind or mental factor interposed at all. With this, the immediately-antecedent condition has now been fully explained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This closing fourfold case uses the two mindless attainments — non-percipience and cessation — as the perfect test case, since they are exactly the situations where mind is absent for an extended stretch, potentially spanning an entire lifetime in the case of non-percipience. The genuinely interesting payoff comes in the closing exchange: the mind that enters one of these attainments can still serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for the mind that eventually emerges from it, no matter how long the intervening gap, because "immediately-antecedent" never actually required strict temporal adjacency in the first place — only that no other mind or mental factor intervenes in between. A gap filled with nothing at all is, paradoxically, exactly what preserves the "immediate" relationship; it is only another mind wedged in between two others that would actually break it. With this careful resolution, the treatise's extended and genuinely demanding treatment of the immediately-antecedent condition comes to its close.

Fascicle 7: More to Come

With the immediately-antecedent condition now fully explained, the treatise turns to the objective condition (所緣緣) — every dharma whatsoever, considered in relation to the mind and mental factors that can take it as their object. Ru-Yi's translation will continue section by section from here.
