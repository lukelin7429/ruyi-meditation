The Six Roots of Existence

Fascicle 19 opens an entirely new chapter of the treatise — Chapter 5, 分別隨眠品第五 (the Anuśaya-nirdeśa, "Discernment of the Latent Tendencies") — turning from the theory of karma to the theory of the afflictions that drive it. Having spent four full chapters explaining that karma is what generates the differences among beings and their worlds, the treatise now asks what generates karma itself: the answer is the anuśayas, the latent, dormant tendencies (traditionally rendered "proclivities" or "latent afflictions") without which karma has no power to bring about further existence at all. This fascicle opens with the six root anuśayas, then turns to a genuine three-way scholastic debate over what an anuśaya actually is at the deepest level — an affliction itself, something merely associated with mind, or something dissociated from mind entirely.

1. The Six Root Anuśayas, and What They Really Are

The treatise opens Chapter 5 by recalling a claim already established across the preceding four chapters: that the differences among the various worlds and forms of existence all arise from karma. It now poses the deeper question this claim leaves open — karma itself only has the power to generate further existence in dependence on the anuśayas (latent tendencies, sometimes rendered "proclivities" or "latent afflictions"); apart from these, karma alone is powerless to bring about rebirth. How many anuśayas are there? In brief, there are six: greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, [wrong] views, and doubt. These are called the "roots of all existence" because, in their fully manifest form as active afflictions, they perform ten distinct functions: firmly establishing the root of affliction, establishing its continuity, cultivating their own proper field, drawing forth their outflow, generating the existence brought about by karma, gathering their own accompanying factors, obscuring their object, guiding the stream of consciousness, transgressing wholesome qualities, and broadly binding a being so that it cannot pass beyond its own realm and stage. It is by virtue of performing these ten functions that the anuśayas serve as the root of existence, and it is only in dependence on them that karma retains the power to generate further existence. The verse's use of the word "also" is meant to indicate that conceit and the others likewise increase with respect to their objects through the power of greed, in a manner to be explained later; the word "and" indicates that these six each have a distinct essential nature from one another. If there are truly only six anuśayas at their most basic level, why do the sūtras elsewhere speak of seven? The treatise explains that this sevenfold count simply divides one of the six — greed — into two: desire-realm greed and higher-realm greed (that is, greed directed at the form and formless realms together, called "greed for existence" because such craving operates predominantly through inward absorption rather than through the outward senses, and also because this label counters a mistaken notion, common among certain other practitioners, that absorption in the higher meditative states is itself already a kind of liberation — the "existence" named here in fact refers only to a subtle clinging to that very state of absorption and its underlying basis, not to the objects of the senses, since craving of that specific character has already been left behind by anyone who has attained such absorption). With greed thus counted separately for the desire realm and the higher two realms, the six anuśayas become seven: desire-greed, hatred, existence-greed, conceit, ignorance, [wrong] views, and doubt.

It was stated earlier that the differences among worlds all arise from karma, and that karma, in turn, only grows and produces its effect in dependence on the anuśayas — apart from the anuśayas, karma has no power to bring about existence. Because the anuśayas are able to perform these functions, they serve as the root of existence, and karma, on their account, has the power to bring about existence. This should, in brief, be understood to have six distinctions — namely, greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, views, and doubt. The verse's word "also" is meant to show that conceit and the rest likewise increase with respect to their objects through the power of greed, the meaning of which increase will be explained later. Verse: the six become seven through the division of greed, [namely] greed for existence, in the upper two realms, since it operates through the inward gate, and in order to counter the notion of liberation. Explanation: among the six anuśayas stated previously, greed is divided into two, and so the sūtras speak of seven. What are the seven? First, desire-greed anuśaya; second, hatred anuśaya; third, existence-greed anuśaya; fourth, conceit anuśaya; fifth, ignorance anuśaya; sixth, views anuśaya; seventh, doubt anuśaya.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This fascicle opens an entirely new chapter of the treatise, and with it a substantial shift in vocabulary: "anuśaya" (隨眠, literally "that which lies dormant" or "sleeps alongside") is a foundational technical term in Abhidharma psychology, often rendered "latent tendency," "proclivity," or "latent affliction" in English scholarship. We will use "anuśaya" and "latent tendency" interchangeably going forward, following the pattern already established for other untranslated technical terms (dhyāna, saṃvara, and the like) throughout this project.

We should flag that the ten functions of the anuśayas (firmly establishing the root, establishing continuity, and so on) are listed here in compressed form without full individual explanation — the treatise appears to assume these ten are either self-explanatory or will be elaborated elsewhere; we have translated them as literally as the compressed source allows, and will note if the treatise returns to unpack any of them more fully in later sections.

2. What Is a Latent Tendency, Really?

The treatise now takes up a genuinely difficult question: with respect to "desire-greed anuśaya," how should this compound term itself be understood? Does the essential nature of desire-greed simply consist in being an anuśaya, or does desire-greed instead merely possess an anuśaya as something distinct from itself? The same question applies equally to each of the other six terms in the list. Either interpretation, the treatise notes, runs into a genuine difficulty. If the essential nature of desire-greed simply is the anuśaya itself, this appears to contradict a sūtra passage stating that a person may go for a long stretch of time without their mind being actively bound by a fetter of desire-greed, even though a momentary fetter of desire-greed might arise briefly and then, once its means of release is correctly understood, be thoroughly eliminated together with its anuśaya — a wording that seems to distinguish the momentary "fetter" from the anuśaya that is separately said to be eliminated alongside it, implying the anuśaya is not simply identical to the active affliction. But if, instead, desire-greed is something distinct that merely possesses an anuśaya, then the anuśaya itself would have to be a formation dissociated from mind altogether, which would contradict the Abhidharma treatises' own statement that the desire-greed anuśaya is associated with three specific feeling-faculties. The Vaibhāṣika masters resolve this by holding that the afflictions themselves, in their essential nature, simply are the anuśayas — so does this not then contradict the sūtra just cited? They reply that it does not, since the sūtra's phrase "eliminated together with its anuśaya" refers to eliminating the affliction together with its binding, entangling power, not to two genuinely separate things; or else, they suggest, the sūtra may be speaking loosely of a mere potential or capacity using the same word "anuśaya," much as one might loosely speak of "suffering" when what is actually meant is merely the perception or notion of suffering associated with fire and similar objects. The Abhidharma treatises, they maintain, speak according to how things truly and essentially are, and it is precisely the afflictions themselves, in this true sense, that are properly called anuśayas — and it follows from this that the anuśayas are indeed associated with mind, not dissociated from it. What reasoning, then, confirms that they must definitely be mind-associated? Because the anuśayas defile and afflict the mind, obscure and obstruct it, and are capable of opposing wholesome states — that is, it is specifically through the power of the anuśayas that wholesome states not yet arisen fail to arise, and wholesome states already arisen degenerate and are lost; hence the anuśayas cannot possibly be dissociated from mind. If something dissociated from mind were capable of producing these same effects, then wholesome dharmas would never have any opportunity to arise at all, since a dissociated formation, by its very nature, is constantly present without interruption; yet wholesome dharmas plainly do have opportunities to arise, and so it must be known that the anuśayas are indeed mind-associated dharmas. The treatise, however, immediately declares that none of these three considerations actually proves the point conclusively. Why not? Because, even granting that the anuśayas are not mind-associated, one need not therefore accept that these three specific effects (obstructing wholesome arising, and so on) are necessarily what the anuśayas themselves directly accomplish — some other mechanism might account for them instead. In fact, the treatise declares, it is the Sautrāntika school's own explanation of this matter that is the most excellent of all the positions on offer.

3. The Sautrāntika's Seed Theory: Dormant Versus Waking Affliction

Having declared the Sautrāntika's explanation the most excellent among the positions on offer, the treatise now sets out that explanation in full. According to the Sautrāntika, "desire-greed anuśaya" refers to a specific meaning of "anuśaya" belonging to desire-greed — yet, crucially, the essential nature of this anuśaya is neither associated with mind nor dissociated from it, since it is not, in fact, a separate, distinct entity of its own kind at all. Rather, the very same affliction, in its dormant, non-manifest state, is called an "anuśaya" (a latent tendency lying dormant), while that identical affliction, in its actively arising state, is called a "paryavasthāna" (an "entanglement," the active, manifest affliction gripping the mind) — the two terms name one and the same underlying continuum of affliction at two different phases of its existence, rather than two separate things. What, then, is meant by "dormant"? It means the affliction persists as a seed that is not currently manifesting in active form. And what is meant by "waking" or "arising"? It means the affliction has become manifest, actively gripping and entangling the mind. What, in turn, constitutes this "seed" of affliction? It is a distinctive functional capacity residing within one's own mental continuum, itself arising from a prior affliction and capable of generating a future affliction in turn — comparable to the "seed" of memory, which arises from an initial direct experience or realization and is itself the distinctive capacity capable of generating a later act of recollection, or comparable to how a sprout carries forward the distinctive capacity, inherited from an earlier fruit, to eventually generate a later fruit in turn. If, instead, one insisted that the affliction's dormant "seed" must be some entirely separate entity dissociated from mind — a view already considered and set aside above — then, by the very same logic, one would have to accept that the "seed" of memory is likewise not simply a functional capacity but some entirely separate, dissociated entity capable of drawing forth a later moment of recollection; yet this is plainly not how memory is actually understood to work. Since the parallel case of memory is not explained this way, why should the case of affliction be explained this way instead? No distinguishing cause can actually be found that would justify treating the two cases differently. An objection follows, invoking the sūtras' well-known "six sixes" teaching, which appears to state that a certain feeling of pleasure is itself accompanied by a greed-anuśaya even while that feeling is merely present, seemingly implying the anuśaya persists continuously as some kind of standing entity throughout, rather than existing only intermittently as dormant seed and occasional manifest affliction. The treatise responds that the sūtra merely states that the anuśaya "exists" in connection with that pleasant feeling, without specifying that the anuśaya is manifestly, actively present at that very moment — so no genuine contradiction arises. At what time, then, does it properly "exist"? Precisely during the dormant, seed-like phase — or else, the treatise adds, the sūtra may simply be speaking loosely of the underlying cause (the seed) using the very name "anuśaya" that strictly applies only to the fully activated case, a common enough looseness of expression. Having addressed this side matter, the treatise now returns to the primary line of inquiry it had been pursuing before this digression began.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Sautrāntika masters' explanation of this matter is the most excellent. What do the Sautrāntika masters say on this point? They say that "desire-greed anuśaya" refers to the meaning of "anuśaya" belonging to desire-greed, yet the essential nature of the anuśaya is neither associated with mind nor dissociated from it, there being no separate entity — the affliction, in its dormant state, is called "anuśaya"; in its waking, [manifest] state, it is called "paryavasthāna" [entanglement]. What is meant by "dormant"? It means [the affliction] does not manifest in activity, but persists as a seed. What is meant by "waking"? It means the afflictions arise manifestly, entangling the mind. What is the "seed" of affliction? It is a distinctive functional capacity, on one's own continuum, arising from affliction, able to generate [further] affliction — like the seed of memory, arising from direct realization, being the distinctive functional capacity able to generate a future act of recollection; or like a sprout, having arisen from a prior fruit, being the distinctive functional capacity able to generate a later fruit. If one insists that affliction has, apart from this, a separate anuśaya dissociated from mind, called the "seed of affliction," one should then also accept that the "seed" of memory is not merely a distinctive functional capacity but a separate, dissociated entity able to draw forth a later act of recollection. Since this is not so [for memory], why should it be so [for affliction]? No distinguishing cause can be found. [Objection:] If so, this contradicts the sūtra of the "six sixes," which states that, with respect to a pleasant feeling, there is a greed-anuśaya. [Reply:] The sūtra only states that it "exists"; it does not say that, at that very time, the anuśaya is [manifestly] present. What contradiction is there? [Objection:] At what time, then, does it exist? [Reply:] At the time of its dormancy. Or, the sūtra may speak loosely of the cause, using the name "anuśaya." Setting this side discussion aside, we should return to the proper topic.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should highlight that this Sautrāntika "seed" account — an affliction as a functional capacity within the mental continuum, neither a separate substance dissociated from mind nor identical to an actively arising mental state — closely parallels the "perfuming" (vāsanā) theory this same school used in fascicle 13 to explain unmanifested karma without positing it as real, distinct matter. Readers who recall that earlier discussion will notice the same underlying strategy at work here: explaining apparent persistence or dormancy through a causal capacity built into the mental continuum's own continuity, rather than through some additional, separately existing entity. This continuity across topics is worth flagging as a hallmark of the Sautrāntika's broader philosophical approach throughout this treatise.

The analogy to a "seed of memory" is a vivid and philosophically significant move: it grounds a fairly abstract metaphysical claim (how a dormant affliction can later reactivate) in something ordinary and readily accepted (how a past experience can later be recollected) — we have preserved this argumentative structure exactly as presented, since the force of the Sautrāntika's case depends on the reader accepting the memory case as unproblematic and then extending the same reasoning to affliction.

4. From Six to Ten: The Five Kinds of Wrong View

The treatise now completes its earlier explanation of why "existence-greed" is named as it is — noting that beings absorbed in the form and formless realms characteristically develop a deep attachment specifically to the meditative absorption itself and to its underlying basis, rather than to any external sense-object, since sensory craving of the ordinary kind has already been left behind by anyone capable of attaining such absorption in the first place; this is why the term "existence-greed" is applied only to the higher two realms, while the plain term "greed," when used for the desire realm specifically, is called "desire-greed" by straightforward extension, without needing separate explanation in the summary verse. Returning now to its central line of inquiry, the treatise explains that the same six anuśayas already introduced are, in the Abhidharma treatises, further divided into ten. How does this expansion to ten occur? Among the six, the single category of "views" is subdivided into five distinct kinds, while the remaining five anuśayas (greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt) are not "views" at all and so remain undivided; adding these five non-view anuśayas to the five varieties of view yields a total of ten. The five varieties of view are: the view of a self (satkāyadṛṣṭi, the belief in an substantially existing "I" or "mine" with respect to the five aggregates); extreme views (belief in either eternal permanence or utter annihilation with respect to that presumed self); wrong view (the specific view, already discussed at length in fascicle 17, that denies the causal efficacy of karma and its fruit, and denies the noble ones altogether); view-attachment (clinging to any of these three preceding views, or to the aggregates underlying them, as being the supreme, most excellent position, superior to all others); and precept-and-vow attachment (clinging to certain moral precepts, or to certain ascetic vows and observances taken up for the sake of purification, as themselves being sufficient, by their own power alone, to bring about purification, liberation, or release from suffering). The remaining five categories — greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt — are not "views" in this technical sense, since none of them involves the specific act of investigating and settling upon a considered judgment about how things are, which is the defining mark of a "view" in this precise Abhidharma sense. Combining the five non-view anuśayas with the five varieties of view thus yields exactly ten fundamental anuśayas in total.

5. The Full Ninety-Eight Anuśayas

The treatise now expands the ten fundamental anuśayas into the full technical count of ninety-eight, by distinguishing each according to its mode of activity, its specific category of abandonment, and the realm to which it belongs. The ten anuśayas already differ in mode of activity (view versus non-view), as just explained; they further differ according to which of five categories of abandonment they belong to — abandoned upon seeing each of the four noble truths (suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path), or abandoned only through sustained cultivation thereafter — and according to which of the three realms (desire, form, formless) they belong to. Combining these distinctions produces the full ninety-eight. Within the desire realm alone, the ten anuśayas, distributed across the five categories of abandonment, yield thirty-six: seventeen abandoned upon seeing suffering, seven upon seeing its origin, seven upon seeing its cessation, eight upon seeing the path, and four abandoned only through cultivation. This distribution follows a specific logic: what is abandoned upon seeing suffering possesses all ten anuśayas in full; what is abandoned upon seeing the origin or cessation of suffering possesses only seven each, excluding the view of a self, extreme views, and precept-and-vow attachment (since these three views specifically presuppose taking one's own aggregates as an object, or presuppose the possibility of liberation through mere observance, neither of which pertains to the truths of origin or cessation); what is abandoned upon seeing the path possesses eight, excluding only the view of a self and extreme views; and what is abandoned only through cultivation possesses just four, excluding all five varieties of view along with doubt, leaving only greed, hatred, conceit, and ignorance. The first thirty-two of these are called "abandoned by seeing," since they are eliminated the very moment the truths are directly seen; the remaining four are called "abandoned by cultivation," since they are eliminated only gradually, through the sustained, repeated practice of the path after the truths have already been seen. This distribution also reveals an important structural fact: among the ten anuśayas, the view of a self and extreme views each belong to only a single category — abandoned upon seeing suffering alone; precept-and-vow attachment belongs to two categories — abandoned upon seeing suffering or upon seeing the path; wrong view, view-attachment, and doubt each belong to all four truths' seeing-categories; and the remaining four (greed, hatred, conceit, and ignorance) each belong to all five categories, including cultivation. Thus, within the ten, views divide into twelve instances, doubt divides into four, and each of the remaining four non-view anuśayas divides into five — yielding the desire realm's total of thirty-six. In the form and formless realms, the same fivefold distribution applies to each realm, except that hatred is entirely absent in both (since hatred, being rooted in aversion toward an object, cannot arise in realms whose beings have already transcended such aversion) — leaving thirty-one anuśayas in each of these two higher realms. Combining the desire realm's thirty-six with the form realm's thirty-one and the formless realm's thirty-one yields the full total of ninety-eight anuśayas, distinguished by mode of activity, category of abandonment, and realm. Among these ninety-eight, eighty-eight are "abandoned by seeing," since they are eliminated through the specific power of forbearance (the direct, penetrating cognition that immediately precedes and accompanies the seeing of each truth); the remaining ten are "abandoned by cultivation," since they are eliminated through the power of full wisdom (knowledge) attained afterward, through sustained practice.

6. A Complication: The Peak of Existence, and Ascetics Who Keep Their Views

The treatise immediately qualifies the clean division just drawn between anuśayas "abandoned by seeing" and "abandoned by cultivation." Is it definitely the case that anuśayas harmed by forbearance are always abandoned-by-seeing, and anuśayas harmed by wisdom are always abandoned-by-cultivation, with no exceptions in either direction? It is not so definite. Among the anuśayas harmed by forbearance, those belonging specifically to the peak of existence (the highest stage of the formless realm) are exclusively abandoned by seeing, never by cultivation, since only the uncontaminated forbearance of the "subsequent-type" cognition (as opposed to the initial "dharma-type" cognition) is capable of eliminating them at all — the remaining eight stages below the peak of existence, by contrast, admit both possibilities: for noble ones, these anuśayas are abandoned only by seeing, never by cultivation, since the corresponding forbearance (whether dharma-type or subsequent-type) eliminates them as appropriate; but for ordinary, unenlightened beings, these same anuśayas are abandoned only by cultivation, never by seeing, since ordinary beings can only suppress them gradually, through the repeated cultivation of mundane, contaminated wisdom, lacking access to the uncontaminated forbearance available to noble ones. Anuśayas harmed by wisdom, meanwhile, belong to every stage without exception and are always abandoned only by cultivation, since both noble ones and ordinary beings alike eliminate them only through the sustained, repeated cultivation of wisdom — whether uncontaminated wisdom for noble ones or contaminated, mundane wisdom for ordinary beings, as appropriate in each case. Other teachers raise here a striking objection concerning certain non-Buddhist ascetics and sages who, despite having already suppressed ordinary sensory desire, are still observed to actively hold wrong views specifically pertaining to the desire realm — a puzzle, since anuśayas "abandoned by seeing" ought, on the framework just given, to already be entirely absent once their corresponding truth has been directly seen, yet these ascetics have plainly not seen any noble truth at all, and their desire-realm views should, by ordinary logic, already have been suppressed along with their desire, given how thoroughly they have cultivated detachment. The Great Discernment of Karma Sūtra confirms this puzzle directly, stating that certain non-Buddhist ascetics who have become free from desire-realm greed nonetheless still actively hold wrong views pertaining to the desire realm; the Brahmajāla Sūtra likewise states that such ascetics still actively hold various desire-realm views — including, among the theorists concerned with the ultimate origin of things, some who assert the world is entirely eternal, some who assert it is eternal only in part, and some who assert that all things arise entirely without cause. Since it is not the case that form-realm afflictions can take the desire realm as their object, and since these ascetics have already become free from desire-realm greed with respect to desire-realm objects themselves, it must be concluded that their desire-realm views, at least, remain definitely unabandoned even after such detachment is otherwise achieved. The Vaibhāṣika masters explain this apparent tension in the sūtra's meaning by holding that these views, though ordinarily suppressed by such ascetics' meditative attainment, can still temporarily reassert themselves and arise again under certain conditions — citing the example of Devadatta, whose own wrong views, though ostensibly suppressed for a time through his own meditative accomplishment, are said to have resurfaced when the underlying conditions for their reassertion arose once more.

7. The Precise Essential Nature of the Five Views

Having already listed the five varieties of view by name, the treatise now defines each one precisely. The view of a self (satkāyadṛṣṭi) is the view that clings to a self and to what belongs to a self, with respect to the five aggregates that are subject to appropriation. Why is this particular view named "satkāyadṛṣṭi"? "Sat" means "destructible" or "impermanent"; "kāya" means "an aggregation," referring to the composite, ever-changing gathering of the aggregates; joined together, "satkāya" thus already signifies "an impermanent aggregation" — this specific name is established precisely to counter the mistaken notion that what one takes to be a permanent, unified self is anything other than this destructible, composite aggregation. Since this specific mistaken notion of permanence and unity must already be operative as a precondition before the self-view can even arise in the first place, the term "satkāya" is used to directly target and undercut that precondition. The Vaibhāṣika masters offer a related, though distinct, explanation: "sat" here simply means "existing," and the term is meant to establish that this view does indeed take something existent — namely, the aggregates themselves — as its object, lest one suppose the view arises with no object at all; hence this view is said to take "satkāya" (an existing aggregation) as its object, and it is from this that the view itself derives its name. If, however, every view whatsoever that merely takes some existing, contaminated dharma as its object could be called by this same name, then this label ought to apply just as well to any of the other views — yet the Buddha specifically reserves this name only for the clinging to a self and to what belongs to a self, precisely so that it will be understood that this view takes "satkāya" as its object, without itself being reducible to any literal, substantially existing self or possession of a self, since, ultimately, no such self or possession of a self exists at all. As a sūtra states: monks, you should know that in this world, ascetics and brahmins of various kinds who cling to the notion of a self, in whatever manner they conceive of it, are in every case only conceiving something with respect to these five aggregates subject to appropriation. Building directly on this same clinging to a self and to what belongs to a self, one who then further clings, with respect to that presumed self, to the extreme notion that it either persists eternally or is utterly annihilated, holds an "extreme view," so called because it wrongly seizes upon one or the other of these two extremes. With respect to the truths of suffering and the others, which genuinely exist, giving rise to a view that categorically denies their existence is called "wrong view" — and although every mistaken view is, in a sense, equally a distorted inversion of reality and might in that broad sense equally deserve the name "wrong," it is specifically this categorically denying view that receives the name "wrong view" in the strict, technical sense, because its error is especially severe: unlike other views, which merely add something extra that is not actually there (an exaggeration, so to speak), this view specifically subtracts and denies something that genuinely is there, and subtraction of this kind is judged the more serious distortion. Clinging to any of these inferior views (self-view, extreme views, or wrong view) — or clinging to the aggregates that underlie them — as being the supreme, most excellent position, is called "view-attachment"; since anything contaminated is, by definition, inferior (having already been abandoned or destined to be abandoned by the noble ones), clinging to something inferior as though it were supreme is precisely what this term names. Finally, with respect to something that is not actually a cause or a path at all, mistakenly holding it to be a cause or a path, is, taken altogether, called "precept-and-vow attachment" — as when certain ascetics wrongly take a supposed creator deity or some other worldly cause as the actual cause of liberation, or wrongly take various painful ascetic practices (throwing oneself into water or fire, and the like) as themselves being a genuine cause of heavenly rebirth, or wrongly take the mere holding of certain precepts and vows, together with associated concentrated states of mind, as themselves already constituting the path to liberation, without these practices actually being any such cause or path at all.

We should flag that the etymological analysis of "satkāya" offered here (two competing explanations of "sat" as "destructible" versus "existing") reflects a genuine internal debate within this Abhidharma tradition about how to parse this compound term, which different sub-traditions of Sanskrit Buddhist scholasticism resolved differently; we have presented both explanations as the text itself does, without picking a winner, since the text does not clearly state that one supersedes the other.

We should also note the philosophically significant asymmetry the treatise draws between "wrong view" (categorical denial of something real, judged the more severe error) and the other distorted views (which add something unreal rather than subtract something real) — this "subtraction is worse than addition" principle is a distinctive piece of this tradition's moral-epistemic reasoning that recurs in how Buddhist thought more broadly treats nihilism as especially dangerous compared to other philosophical errors, and we have preserved the reasoning exactly as the text presents it.

8. Why Belief in a Creator Is Abandoned by Seeing Suffering, and the Four Inverted Views

The treatise now explains why holding that a creator deity such as Maheśvara, or some other worldly cause, is the ultimate cause of the world counts specifically as an offense abandoned by seeing suffering, even though this belief concerns a supposed cause and might therefore seem more naturally to belong under the truth of origin instead. The explanation is that anyone who conceives of the world as generated by such a creator must first, necessarily, have already conceived of that creator's own underlying nature as a permanent, unified self acting as an agent — it is only on the basis of this prior, more fundamental self-view that the mistaken causal belief about a creator can arise at all. The moment suffering is directly seen, the underlying clinging to permanence and to a unified self with respect to a deity like Maheśvara is permanently and completely severed without remainder, and so the causal belief that depended on it is severed along with it. A parallel question then arises regarding those who wrongly believe that throwing themselves into water or fire, or that merely holding certain precepts and vows, is itself sufficient for liberation — should this belief not, then, likewise be excluded from what is abandoned by seeing suffering, given how thoroughly it seems bound up with a mistaken notion of the path rather than of suffering? The treatise firmly rejects any suggestion that these should instead be classified as abandoned by seeing the path, since being confused specifically about the truth of suffering is already an overwhelming, all-encompassing error — every affliction that takes any contaminated object whatsoever is already, in that very act, confused about suffering, this confusion being pervasive and foundational to virtually every other error. It then addresses a further, more difficult question: distorted views specifically concerning the path (mistaken beliefs about what does or does not lead to liberation) would seem, by the same reasoning that groups causal-belief errors under suffering, equally to have to be grouped there — yet how can we speak of "confusion about suffering" for something that specifically concerns the path? The treatise resolves this by carefully distinguishing precept-and-vow attachment (which mistakes an ineffective practice for the path) from wrong view and doubt directed at the path itself (which categorically deny that any path to liberation exists, or which merely doubt its existence) — the latter two, precisely because they deny or doubt any path's existence altogether, cannot coherently also affirm some substitute practice as itself a genuine path to purification, and so their case is different and does not generalize back to precept-and-vow attachment. Having settled this, the treatise turns to what it calls the four fundamental "inversions" (viparyāsa): mistaking what is impermanent for permanent, mistaking what is inherently suffering for pleasurable, mistaking what is impure for pure, and mistaking what is without self for having a self. These four inversions are constituted, respectively, from three of the five views already discussed: the inversion regarding permanence is drawn specifically from the "eternalist" branch of extreme views; the inversions regarding pleasure and purity are drawn from view-attachment (specifically, clinging to what is in fact suffering or impure as though it were pleasurable or pure); and the inversion regarding self is drawn from the view of a self. Strictly speaking, it is the view itself, in each case, that constitutes the actual inversion, though the accompanying perception and the accompanying state of mind are also loosely said to be "inverted" as well, since they arise together with, and share the same operative character as, the view that truly defines each inversion.

9. Perception's Borrowed Inversion, and the Seven Kinds of Conceit

The treatise raises a follow-up puzzle: if it is strictly the view itself, in each case, that constitutes the true inversion, why do the sūtras elsewhere speak of perception and mind themselves as being "inverted" with respect to what is impermanent, suffering, impure, and without self? Strictly speaking, it should be understood that it is only the view that is genuinely inverted; perception and mind are said to be "inverted" only in a secondary, borrowed sense, since they arise together with, and take on the same operative character as, the view that actually defines the inversion. If this is so, why does the sūtra not similarly speak of feeling as being inverted, given that feeling too generally accompanies view? The reply is that this is because the notion of "inverted feeling" is not something the world generally recognizes or accepts as a meaningful category, whereas "inverted perception" and "inverted mind" are both widely and readily accepted; hence the sūtra speaks only of the latter two, leaving feeling unmentioned. On this same topic, the treatise notes that all four inversions are already permanently abandoned once a person becomes a stream-enterer, since they are inseparably bound up with the very views, and the states associated with those views, that are eliminated by seeing the truths. Other schools within the broader tradition, however, hold a different position: that inversion is properly counted as twelve kinds rather than four, since within the single inversion of clinging to permanence with respect to what is impermanent, for instance, there are already three distinct sub-varieties — an inverted perception, an inverted mind, and an inverted view — and the same threefold division applies to each of the remaining three inversions as well. Of these twelve, eight are said to be abandoned only by seeing, while four — specifically the perception and mind components (though not the view components) of the pleasure-inversion and the purity-inversion — are said to be abandoned by both seeing and cultivation. This position is defended by an appeal to ordinary experience: if it were not the case that even a noble being who has not yet fully overcome sensory craving could still, at least momentarily, experience something resembling a perception or state of mind oriented toward pleasure or purity, how could desire-realm craving continue to arise in such a person at all after they have already directly seen the truths? The Vaibhāṣika masters explicitly reject this reasoning: to grant that a noble one could have any residual pleasure-or-purity "inversion" of perception or mind still active would, by exactly parallel logic, force one to admit a residual self-inversion as well, since noble ones plainly do continue to experience ordinary, sentient-being-directed perceptions and mental states even after seeing the truths — yet craving toward women or toward one's own body does not, in fact, arise merely from having such an ordinary sentient-being-perception present. The sūtras confirm the stricter position directly, stating that once a well-learned noble disciple has truly and directly seen and understood the noble truth of suffering, that disciple's inverted perception, mind, and view with respect to clinging to permanence regarding the impermanent are, at that very point, already permanently and completely eliminated — and this same principle applies, at length, to all four inversions equally, confirming that it is specifically the power associated with the view-component of each inversion that perception and mind borrow their own "inverted" character from, not some independent inversion of their own; a noble one's occasional lapse into desire is instead better explained as a brief, momentary confusion, arising unexpectedly toward an object, much as a person can momentarily mistake a painted or illusory image of a fearsome spirit glimpsed on a spinning wheel of fire for something real, without genuinely believing, on reflection, that any such spirit truly exists there.

10. The Seven and Nine Kinds of Conceit

Having asked whether the anuśaya of doubt or view alone displays this kind of further internal variety, or whether other anuśayas do as well, the treatise answers that conceit, too, has multiple distinct varieties — seven in total. Causing the mind to become elevated and puffed up is, in general, called "conceit," but this single overarching category divides into seven kinds depending on the specific manner in which it operates. Toward one who is genuinely inferior to oneself, or genuinely equal to oneself, thinking "I am superior" or "I am equal," respectively, and thereby causing the mind to become elevated, is called simply "conceit." Toward one who is genuinely equal to oneself, or genuinely superior to oneself, thinking "I am superior" or "I am equal," respectively, is called "excessive conceit." Toward one who is genuinely, definitively superior to oneself, still thinking "I am superior," is called "the conceit of excessive conceit." Clinging to the five aggregates subject to appropriation as constituting "I" and "mine," and thereby causing the mind to become elevated, is called "self-conceit." Not yet having attained some distinguished spiritual quality, yet thinking one has already attained it, is called "conceit of overestimation." With respect to those who are, in fact, vastly superior to oneself, thinking that one is only slightly inferior to them, is called "deficiency-conceit." And, finally, lacking any genuine virtue at all, yet thinking that one does possess virtue, is called "wrong conceit." The root treatise, however, records an alternative classification, dividing conceit instead into nine distinct types: thinking "I am superior to my superior," "I am equal to my superior," "I am inferior to my superior"; thinking "I am superior to my equal," "I am equal to my equal," "I am inferior to my equal"; and thinking "I am superior to my inferior," "I am equal to my inferior," "I am inferior to my inferior." These nine varieties are derived by taking three of the original seven kinds of conceit already listed — ordinary conceit, excessive conceit, and deficiency-conceit — and analyzing each of these three according to whether it operates with respect to an object that is genuinely inferior, equal, or superior to oneself, which yields three groups of three, or nine total, depending on which specific pairing of self-assessment and actual relative standing is involved. Among these nine, "deficiency-conceit toward one who is vastly superior" (that is, thinking oneself only slightly inferior to someone genuinely far superior) can indeed arise as a form of conceit, since there remains some "elevated" ground for it — namely, comparing oneself favorably against an even larger gap that might otherwise have been claimed. What, though, of "deficiency-conceit toward one who is genuinely inferior" — how can there be any elevated ground for conceit there at all, given that one is already comparing oneself, quite modestly, against someone one openly regards as inferior? Even in this case, an elevated ground for conceit remains, since the person still takes pride in the specific respect in which they consider themselves superior to that inferior party — even a very modest sense of superiority, so long as it is present at all, retains the essential elevating character that defines conceit as such, and this holds true according to one specific line of interpretation offered on the Jñānaprasthāna's own account of this ninefold scheme, one grounded specifically in observing how the object of the conceit — inferior, equal, or superior — differs from case to case.

11. Afflictions That Never Manifest in a Noble One at All

Having laid out the seven (or nine) kinds of conceit, the treatise notes simply that all of them are abandoned by both seeing and by cultivation, without further complication. It then turns to a genuinely striking related point: even among afflictions properly classified as "abandoned by cultivation" — meaning that a noble one has not yet formally severed them at the moment of first seeing the truths, and only eliminates them gradually afterward through sustained practice — some of these afflictions are such that a noble one, even prior to their formal abandonment, will simply never actually allow them to manifest in active form at all. The clearest example is the specific entanglement of affliction that gives rise to the intention to kill: this entanglement is technically classified as abandoned only by cultivation, and yet noble ones, once they have become noble ones at all, are said to never actually kill a living being under any circumstances whatsoever — the entanglement that could give rise to such an act, though not yet formally uprooted, simply never manifests in their actual conduct. The verse's use of the phrase "and the like" indicates that the same holds true, correspondingly, for the entanglements underlying theft, sexual misconduct, and false speech, as well as for the entirety of "non-existent-craving" and a portion of "existence-craving." "Non-existent-craving" refers to the specific craving, common among ordinary beings, for the complete cessation and non-continuation of the three realms altogether — a nihilistic longing for utter extinction, distinct from any properly understood aspiration toward nirvāṇa. "Existence-craving," by contrast, refers to a partial, still-lingering craving directed at a specific, elevated future rebirth — as when one aspires to become a being such as the great nāga king Airāvata. All of these entanglements and cravings take as their object only what is abandoned by cultivation, and are for this reason abandoned only by cultivation. Having explained that certain kinds of conceit and the like are of this cultivation-abandoned type, the treatise then asks: by what reason is it that noble ones, though not yet having formally abandoned these, nonetheless never give rise to them in practice? The answer is that specific afflictions of this kind are each directly "augmented" by one of the two anuśayas already eliminated at the very moment of seeing the truths — view or doubt — and once that augmenting support has already been broken and turned back, the affliction that depended on it can no longer actually arise, even though it has not yet, strictly speaking, been formally abandoned through cultivation. Specifically: the type of conceit called self-conceit is augmented by the view of a self; the entanglements of killing and the rest are augmented by wrong view; the entirety of non-existent-craving is augmented by the annihilationist branch of extreme views; the portion of existence-craving already described is augmented by the eternalist branch of extreme views; and unwholesome remorse is augmented by doubt — and it is for this reason that none of these can ever actually arise within a noble one's own being, once view and doubt have already been eliminated by seeing the truths.

Ru-Yi Translation

Which of these seven kinds of conceit are abandoned? All are abandoned by both seeing and cultivation. Among those abandoned by cultivation, before a noble one has abandoned them, can they still manifest? This is not fixed — that is, there are some abandoned by cultivation that noble ones definitely do not manifest, such as the entanglement of killing, which is abandoned by cultivation, yet noble ones certainly do not manifest it. "The entanglement of killing" refers to the affliction that gives rise to the deliberate intention to end a sentient being's life. The word "and the like" indicates the entanglements of theft, sexual misconduct, and false speech, [as well as] the entirety of non-existent-craving and a portion of existence-craving. What is meant by "non-existent"? It refers to craving for the impermanence — that is, the cessation — of the three realms; craving of this kind is called "non-existent-craving." "A portion of existence-craving" refers to aspiring to become, for instance, the great nāga king Airāvata and the like. All these entanglements and cravings take as their object only what is abandoned by cultivation, and are therefore abandoned only by cultivation. We have discussed the kinds of conceit and the rest — some are abandoned by cultivation; why is it that noble ones, though not yet having abandoned them, do not give rise to them? Verse: certain kinds of conceit, self-conceit, and unwholesome remorse — noble ones possess but do not give rise to these, since they are augmented by view and doubt. Explanation: the word "and the like" indicates the entanglements of killing and the rest, [and] the entirety of non-existent-craving and a portion of existence-craving. These kinds of conceit, self-conceit, and remorse are directly augmented by view and doubt; although abandoned by cultivation, because view and doubt have already been broken and turned back [by seeing the truths], noble ones are unable to give rise to them. That is, the type of conceit called self-conceit is augmented by the view of a self; the entanglement of killing and the rest are augmented by wrong view; non-existent-craving is augmented by the extinction [i.e. annihilationist] view; the portion of existence-craving is augmented by the permanence [i.e. eternalist] view; unwholesome remorse is augmented by doubt — hence, within a noble one's own being, none of these definitely ever arise.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section introduces an important and somewhat counterintuitive structural point: certain afflictions are formally classified as "abandoned by cultivation" (meaning a noble one has not technically eliminated them yet upon first seeing the truths) yet are said never to actually manifest in a noble one's conduct even before that formal elimination is complete. The explanation is that these specific afflictions are each "augmented" by a particular view or by doubt, which are themselves already fully eliminated at the moment of seeing the truths — once that augmenting support is removed, the affliction has no occasion to become active, even while technically still present in a residual, unmanifest form until cultivation formally removes it as well.

We should flag that "Airāvata" (藹羅筏拏, more commonly known in Indian literature as Airāvata, the great elephant or nāga associated with Indra) is treated here as a familiar reference point for an exalted class of being — we render the name in its more standard transliteration for readability, consistent with our approach to other proper names throughout this project.

12. The Eleven "Pervasive" Anuśayas

Among the ninety-eight anuśayas, the treatise now asks: how many are "pervasive" (capable of taking as their object every dharma abandoned within their own realm and stage, rather than being confined to whatever is abandoned specifically upon seeing one single truth), and how many are not? The anuśayas abandoned upon seeing suffering and upon seeing the origin of suffering — specifically the various views and doubt belonging to these two categories, together with the specific "independent" ignorance (ignorance not arising in direct association with any other affliction) that accompanies them — are, by virtue of their power, capable of pervasively operating across all five categories of what is abandoned within their own realm and stage. This yields exactly eleven pervasive anuśayas: seven kinds of view (the view of a self, extreme views, wrong view, view-attachment, and precept-and-vow attachment, each counted once for what is abandoned by seeing suffering and once for what is abandoned by seeing the origin, though the view of a self and extreme views apply only to suffering, yielding seven total instances), two kinds of doubt (belonging to seeing suffering and seeing the origin respectively), and two kinds of independent ignorance (again belonging to seeing suffering and seeing the origin respectively). These eleven, through this pervasive reach across all five categories within their own realm and stage, are able to give rise to defiled dharmas throughout the entirety of that realm and stage, and it is by virtue of these three considerations — a broad reach across every abandonment-category, functioning as a cause for the defiled dharmas that pervasively arise as a result, and a scope that spans the entirety of their own realm and stage — that the name "pervasive" is properly established for them. A subtle question then arises: does this pervasive reach mean these eleven anuśayas take every dharma within their own realm and stage as their object all at once, in a single act of cognition, or do they instead take each such dharma as their object only gradually, one at a time in succession? If it were only gradual, one-at-a-time cognition, then every other anuśaya, in principle, ought equally to be capable of the same kind of gradual, cumulative reach across every category over time, undermining any special distinction for these eleven; yet if it is instead a single, simultaneous act of cognition spanning every dharma within the realm and stage at once, this raises a further difficulty: who, then, could possibly be said to grasp all the various things within the desire realm all at once, in a single moment, as being the same "supreme" cause of purification, or the same singular object of liberation? The treatise clarifies that "pervasive" is not meant to imply that a single anuśaya, in a single instant, simultaneously and directly grasps every dharma abandoned within its own stage all at once; rather, it means that these eleven anuśayas possess the requisite power to pervasively and rapidly extend across all five categories of abandonment within their own realm and stage, one after another in rapid succession, whereas other anuśayas lack this capacity to extend so broadly and are instead confined only to whatever specific category directly corresponds to their own particular nature.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the ninety-eight anuśayas, how many are pervasive, and how many are not? Verse: the views and doubt associated with what is abandoned by seeing suffering and the origin, together with independent ignorance, pervasively operate within their own realm and stage; among these, excluding two views, the remaining nine are also able to take higher [realms] as their object; excluding [what is obtained through] attainment, the rest, together with their accompanying factors, are also included as pervasive. Explanation: only the views and doubt abandoned by seeing suffering and the origin, together with the independent ignorance associated with them, have the power to pervasively operate across the five categories within their own realm and stage; these eleven all obtain the name "pervasive" — namely, seven views, two [instances of] doubt, and two [instances of] ignorance, [totaling] eleven. These eleven, taking as their object, pervasively, all dharmas across the five categories within their own realm and stage, serve, as anuśayas, as the cause that pervasively gives rise to defiled dharmas across the five categories. On the basis of these three meanings, the name "pervasive" is established. [Objection:] Does this pervasive taking-as-object of the five categories refer to gradual, successive objectification, or to simultaneous objectification? If gradual and successive, others should equally be pervasive [by the same reasoning]. If simultaneous, who could possibly, all at once, universally regard everything within the desire realm as being the supreme [cause] able to bring about purification, or as the cause of the world? [Reply:] It is not said that [these anuśayas] simultaneously take as their object everything within their own realm and stage all at once; rather, it is said that they possess the power to pervasively and rapidly extend across the five categories within their own realm and stage.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We should flag that "independent ignorance" (不共無明, literally "ignorance not shared/combined [with other afflictions]") is a technical term this passage introduces without a full standalone definition — based on context, it appears to refer to ignorance arising on its own, unaccompanied by another affliction like greed or view, as distinct from ignorance that merely co-arises alongside a different primary affliction. We render it literally and will refine this working definition if the treatise elaborates on it more explicitly in a later section.

The clarification that "pervasive" means a rapid, successive capacity to extend across categories rather than one single, simultaneous cognitive act grasping everything at once is an important precision — without it, "pervasive" might be misread as claiming these eleven anuśayas somehow cognize the entirety of a realm's contaminated dharmas in one indivisible mental event, which the treatise explicitly denies. We have made sure this distinction comes through clearly in our translation rather than leaving it ambiguous.
