The Six Roots of Existence

Fascicle 19 opens an entirely new chapter of the treatise — Chapter 5, 分別隨眠品第五 (the Anuśaya-nirdeśa, "Discernment of the Latent Tendencies") — turning from the theory of karma to the theory of the afflictions that drive it. Having spent four full chapters explaining that karma is what generates the differences among beings and their worlds, the treatise now asks what generates karma itself: the answer is the anuśayas, the latent, dormant tendencies (traditionally rendered "proclivities" or "latent afflictions") without which karma has no power to bring about further existence at all. This fascicle opens with the six root anuśayas, then turns to a genuine three-way scholastic debate over what an anuśaya actually is at the deepest level — an affliction itself, something merely associated with mind, or something dissociated from mind entirely.

1. The Six Root Anuśayas, and What They Really Are

The treatise opens Chapter 5 by recalling a claim already established across the preceding four chapters: that the differences among the various worlds and forms of existence all arise from karma. It now poses the deeper question this claim leaves open — karma itself only has the power to generate further existence in dependence on the anuśayas (latent tendencies, sometimes rendered "proclivities" or "latent afflictions"); apart from these, karma alone is powerless to bring about rebirth. How many anuśayas are there? In brief, there are six: greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, [wrong] views, and doubt. These are called the "roots of all existence" because, in their fully manifest form as active afflictions, they perform ten distinct functions: firmly establishing the root of affliction, establishing its continuity, cultivating their own proper field, drawing forth their outflow, generating the existence brought about by karma, gathering their own accompanying factors, obscuring their object, guiding the stream of consciousness, transgressing wholesome qualities, and broadly binding a being so that it cannot pass beyond its own realm and stage. It is by virtue of performing these ten functions that the anuśayas serve as the root of existence, and it is only in dependence on them that karma retains the power to generate further existence. The verse's use of the word "also" is meant to indicate that conceit and the others likewise increase with respect to their objects through the power of greed, in a manner to be explained later; the word "and" indicates that these six each have a distinct essential nature from one another. If there are truly only six anuśayas at their most basic level, why do the sūtras elsewhere speak of seven? The treatise explains that this sevenfold count simply divides one of the six — greed — into two: desire-realm greed and higher-realm greed (that is, greed directed at the form and formless realms together, called "greed for existence" because such craving operates predominantly through inward absorption rather than through the outward senses, and also because this label counters a mistaken notion, common among certain other practitioners, that absorption in the higher meditative states is itself already a kind of liberation — the "existence" named here in fact refers only to a subtle clinging to that very state of absorption and its underlying basis, not to the objects of the senses, since craving of that specific character has already been left behind by anyone who has attained such absorption). With greed thus counted separately for the desire realm and the higher two realms, the six anuśayas become seven: desire-greed, hatred, existence-greed, conceit, ignorance, [wrong] views, and doubt.

Chinese Source

前言世別皆由業生，業由隨眠方得生長，離隨眠業無感有能。所以者何？隨眠有幾？頌曰：隨眠諸有本，此差別有六，謂貪瞋亦慢，無明見及疑。論曰：由此隨眠是諸有本，故業離此無感有能。何故隨眠能為有本？以諸煩惱現起能為十種事故，一堅根本、二立相續、三治自田、四引等流、五發業有、六攝自具、七迷所緣、八導識流、九越善品、十廣縛義，令不能越自界地故。由此隨眠能為有本，故業因此有感有能。此略應知差別有六，謂貪、瞋、慢、無明、見、疑。頌說亦言，意顯慢等亦由貪力於境隨增，由貪隨增義如後辯。及聲顯六體各不同。若諸隨眠體唯有六，何緣經說有七隨眠？頌曰：六由貪異七，有貪上二界，於內門轉故，為遮解脫想。論曰：即前所說六隨眠中分貪為二，故經說七。何等為七？一欲貪隨眠、二瞋隨眠、三有貪隨眠、四慢隨眠、五無明隨眠、六見隨眠、七疑隨眠。

Ru-Yi Translation

It was stated earlier that the differences among worlds all arise from karma, and that karma, in turn, only grows and produces its effect in dependence on the anuśayas — apart from the anuśayas, karma has no power to bring about existence. Why is this? How many anuśayas are there? Verse: the anuśayas are the root of all existence; their distinctions are six — namely, greed, hatred, and also conceit, ignorance, views, and doubt. Explanation: because these anuśayas are the root of all existence, karma, apart from them, has no power to bring about existence. Why are the anuśayas able to serve as the root of existence? Because afflictions, in their manifest activity, are able to perform ten kinds of function: first, firmly establishing the root; second, establishing continuity; third, cultivating their own field; fourth, drawing forth outflow; fifth, generating karmic existence; sixth, gathering their own equipment; seventh, obscuring the object; eighth, guiding the stream of consciousness; ninth, transgressing wholesome categories; tenth, broadly binding — causing one to be unable to pass beyond one's own realm and stage. Because the anuśayas are able to perform these functions, they serve as the root of existence, and karma, on their account, has the power to bring about existence. This should, in brief, be understood to have six distinctions — namely, greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, views, and doubt. The verse's word "also" is meant to show that conceit and the rest likewise increase with respect to their objects through the power of greed, the meaning of which increase will be explained later. The word "and" shows that these six each have a distinct essential nature. If the anuśayas' essential nature is only six, why do the sūtras speak of seven anuśayas? Verse: the six become seven through the division of greed, [namely] greed for existence, in the upper two realms, since it operates through the inward gate, and in order to counter the notion of liberation. Explanation: among the six anuśayas stated previously, greed is divided into two, and so the sūtras speak of seven. What are the seven? First, desire-greed anuśaya; second, hatred anuśaya; third, existence-greed anuśaya; fourth, conceit anuśaya; fifth, ignorance anuśaya; sixth, views anuśaya; seventh, doubt anuśaya.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This fascicle opens an entirely new chapter of the treatise, and with it a substantial shift in vocabulary: "anuśaya" (隨眠, literally "that which lies dormant" or "sleeps alongside") is a foundational technical term in Abhidharma psychology, often rendered "latent tendency," "proclivity," or "latent affliction" in English scholarship. We will use "anuśaya" and "latent tendency" interchangeably going forward, following the pattern already established for other untranslated technical terms (dhyāna, saṃvara, and the like) throughout this project.

We should flag that the ten functions of the anuśayas (firmly establishing the root, establishing continuity, and so on) are listed here in compressed form without full individual explanation — the treatise appears to assume these ten are either self-explanatory or will be elaborated elsewhere; we have translated them as literally as the compressed source allows, and will note if the treatise returns to unpack any of them more fully in later sections.
