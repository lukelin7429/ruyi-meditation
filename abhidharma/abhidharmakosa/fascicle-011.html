What Holds the World Up

This page carries the opening of fascicle 11, beginning Chapter 3's fourth installment (分別世品第三之四, the Loka-nirdeśa's fourth part) with a decisive shift in subject: having finished the world of sentient beings, the treatise now turns to the container world itself — the physical cosmos's own literal architecture, starting from the very bottom with a wind wheel resting on empty space, a water wheel formed by karmically-caused rain falling upon it, and a gold wheel congealing from that water's upper layer, exactly the way a skin forms on top of boiled milk.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 10 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 11 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. The Wind Wheel, the Water Wheel, and the Gold Wheel

Having finished the world of sentient beings, the treatise turns to the container world's literal architecture: a wind wheel at the very bottom, immeasurably vast and harder than diamond; a water wheel formed from karmically-caused rain falling upon it, so enormous it takes some explaining why it doesn't simply disperse; and a gold wheel congealing from the water's upper layer under the beating of a further karmic wind, exactly the way a skin forms on the surface of boiled milk left to stand.

Chinese Source

如是已說有情世間。器世間今當說。頌曰：安立器世間，風輪最居下，其量廣無數，厚十六洛叉，次上水輪深，十一億二萬，下八洛叉水，餘凝結成金，此水金輪廣，徑十二洛叉，三千四百半，周圍此三倍。論曰：許此三千大千世界如是安立形量不同。謂諸有情業增上力，先於最下依止虛空有風輪生，廣無數、厚十六億踰繕那。如是風輪其體堅密，假設有一大諾健那，以金剛輪奮威懸擊，金剛有碎風輪無損。又諸有情業增上力，起大雲雨澍風輪上，滴如車軸積水成輪。如是水輪於未凝結位，深十一億二萬踰繕那。如何水輪不傍流散？有餘師說：一切有情業力所持令不流散。如所飲食未熟變時，終不流移墮於熟藏。有餘部說：由風所持令不流散，如篅持穀。有情業力感別風起，搏擊此水上結成金，如熟乳停上凝成膜，故水輪減唯厚八洛叉，餘轉成金，厚三億二萬。二輪廣量其數是同，謂徑十二億三千四百半。周圍其邊數成三倍，謂周圍量成三十六億一萬三百五十踰繕那。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sentient beings' world has thus been fully discussed. The container world should now be discussed. Verse: in the establishment of the container world, the wind wheel dwells at the very lowest position; its extent is immeasurably vast, and its thickness is 1,600,000 yojanas. Next, above it, the water wheel: its depth is 1,120,000 yojanas. Of this, the lower 800,000 remains water, while the rest congeals into gold. This water-and-gold wheel's breadth, in diameter, is 1,203,450 yojanas; its circumference is three times this. Prose: it is accepted that this great trichiliocosm is established with distinct shapes and measurements in the following way. That is: through the dominant power of sentient beings' karma, first, at the very lowest position, resting upon empty space, a wind wheel arises — immeasurably vast in extent, 1,600,000 yojanas thick. This wind wheel's substance is so solid and dense that, even supposing a great nārāyaṇa deity were to swing a vajra wheel with all his might and strike it, the vajra might shatter while the wind wheel remains undamaged. Furthermore, through the dominant power of sentient beings' karma, great clouds arise and pour rain down upon the wind wheel, the drops as large as cart-axles, accumulating water that forms a wheel. Before it congeals, this water wheel is 1,120,000 yojanas deep. How does the water wheel not disperse and flow away at its sides? Some masters say: it is held together by the karmic power of all sentient beings collectively, preventing it from dispersing — just as food not yet transformed by digestion never flows out and falls into the organ that processes what is already digested. Other schools say: it is held together by wind, preventing it from dispersing — just as a grain-basket holds grain. Sentient beings' karmic power gives rise to a specific wind, which beats upon this water from above, causing it to congeal into gold — just as milk, once boiled and left standing, congeals a skin on its surface. Hence the water wheel is reduced to only 800,000 yojanas thick, and the remainder turns into gold, 320,000 yojanas thick. The breadth of these two wheels is the same in measure — namely, a diameter of 1,203,450 yojanas. Their circumferential measure comes to three times this — namely, a circumference of 3,610,350 yojanas.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A note on units, established here at the outset and applying to all of fascicle 11's cosmological measurements: the text uses 洛叉 (lakṣa, 100,000) and 億 interchangeably for the very same figure — the verse gives the wind wheel's thickness as "十六洛叉" (16 lakṣa), and the prose restates this identical figure as "十六億." We take this as direct textual evidence that, in this passage, 億 functions as a synonym for lakṣa (100,000) rather than the modern Chinese value of 100,000,000, and have translated all such figures throughout on this basis, converting the classical numeral compounds into plain decimal yojana counts for readability.

The whole passage insists on a strikingly consistent principle: every stage of the physical universe's formation — the wind wheel's very existence, the rain that becomes the water wheel, the specific secondary wind that congeals part of that water into gold — is explicitly attributed to "the dominant power of sentient beings' karma" (有情業增上力), not to impersonal physical law. The cosmos, on this account, is not a neutral stage on which karma later plays out; it is itself already a collective karmic product from its very first, most basic structural layer.

Correction (added after initial publication): this note originally read the verse's "三千四百半" as 3,400.5 and flagged an apparent ~148.5-yojana gap between the stated diameter and circumference. That reading was our error. Cross-checking against the parallel figure for Aparagodānīya's circumference later in this fascicle ("周圍七千半" = 7,000 plus half of the just-named thousands-place = 7,500, exactly three times its 2,500 diameter) shows that "半" here means half of the nearest larger place-value already named in the same numeral, not a bare "point five." Applied to "三千四百半," that gives 3,400 plus half of the hundreds-place just named (100 ÷ 2 = 50) = 3,450, not 3,400.5 — and 1,203,450 × 3 = 3,610,350 exactly, matching the prose's circumference figure with no gap at all. We have corrected the diameter above from 1,203,400.5 to 1,203,450 accordingly. We leave this note in place, rather than silently deleting it, as a record of the correction.

2. Nine Mountains on the Gold Wheel: Sumeru and Its Seven Rings

Upon the gold wheel rise nine great mountains: Sumeru at the very center, surrounded by seven concentric rings of gold mountains, with the Cakravāḍa — the iron mountain range — encircling the whole system like a rim. Sumeru alone is built of four precious substances, one to each face, its southern lapis-lazuli side explaining why the sky above Jambudvīpa itself looks blue.

Chinese Source

頌曰：蘇迷盧處中，次踰健達羅，伊沙馱羅山，朅地洛迦山，蘇達梨舍那，頞濕縛羯拏，毘那怛迦山，尼民達羅山，於大洲等外，有鐵輪圍山，前七金所成，蘇迷盧四寶，入水皆八萬，妙高出亦然，餘八半半下，廣皆等高量。論曰：於金輪上有九大山，妙高山王處中而住，餘八周匝繞妙高山。於八山中前七名內，第七山外有大洲等。此外復有鐵輪圍山，周匝如輪圍一世界。持雙等七唯金所成。妙高山王四寶為體，謂如次四面北、東、南、西，金、銀、吠琉璃、頗胝迦寶。隨寶威德色顯於空，故贍部洲空似吠琉璃色。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: Mount Sumeru dwells at the center; next comes Yugandhara, Mount Īṣadhara, Mount Khadiraka, Sudarśana, Aśvakarṇa, Mount Vinataka, Mount Nimindhara; and beyond the great continents and the rest, there is the Cakravāḍa, the iron encircling mountain range. The first seven are made entirely of gold; Sumeru is composed of four precious substances. All sink 80,000 yojanas into the water; Sumeru's height above the water is likewise 80,000. The remaining eight decrease by half each in succession; each one's breadth equals its own height above the water. Prose: upon the gold wheel there are nine great mountains. King Sumeru dwells at the center, while the remaining eight surround it on all sides. Among these eight, the first seven are called "inner"; outside the seventh mountain lie the great continents and the rest. Beyond these, there is furthermore the Cakravāḍa, encircling the whole like a wheel-rim around a single world-system. Yugandhara and the six mountains after it are made entirely of gold. King Sumeru's substance consists of four precious materials — namely, on its four faces, in order, north, east, south, and west: gold, silver, lapis lazuli, and crystal. In accordance with each jewel's radiant power, its color is reflected in the sky above; hence the sky above Jambudvīpa resembles the color of lapis lazuli.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seven inner mountain names — Yugandhara, Īṣadhara, Khadiraka, Sudarśana, Aśvakarṇa, Vinataka, and Nimindhara — are given here in their standard Sanskrit forms rather than as literal translations of Xuánzàng's phonetic transliterations, since these are well-established proper names in Buddhist cosmological literature rather than descriptive terms coined by this text. Jambudvīpa's lapis-colored sky is a small but memorable piece of applied cosmology: it isn't simply blue by nature, but blue specifically because it faces the lapis-lazuli southern side of a mountain most of its inhabitants will never see, hundreds of thousands of yojanas away.

3. Where the Jewels Come From, and a Refutation of Sāṃkhya "Transformation"

Sumeru's precious substances arise the same way everything else in the container world has so far: karmically-driven clouds, rain, and wind acting on water. But the treatise pauses here to make a sharper philosophical point about exactly what kind of change this is — water turning into jewels is cause ceasing as effect arises, never a single persisting substance merely taking on new forms, and this becomes the occasion for a pointed refutation of the Sāṃkhya school's rival theory of "transformation."

Chinese Source

如是寶等從何而生？亦諸有情業增上力。復大雲起雨金輪上滴如車軸，積水奔濤，其水即為眾寶種藏。由具種種威德猛風鑽擊變生眾寶類等。如是變水生寶等時，因滅果生，體不俱有，非如數論轉變所成。數論云何執轉變義？謂執有法自性常存，有餘法生、有餘法滅。如是轉變何理相違？謂必無容有法常住，可執別有法滅法生。誰言法外別有有法？唯即此法於轉變時異相所依名為有法。此亦非理。非理者何？即是此物而不如此，如是言義曾所未聞。

Ru-Yi Translation

From where, then, do these jewels and the like arise? This, too, is through the dominant power of sentient beings' karma. Once again, great clouds arise and rain down upon the gold wheel, the drops as large as cart-axles; the accumulated water, surging and churning, becomes itself the very womb and repository of the various jewels' seeds. Through the action of fierce winds possessed of various kinds of majestic power, boring and striking, this water is transformed, giving rise to the various kinds of jewels. In this transformation of water into the production of jewels and the like, the cause ceases as the effect arises — the two essential natures are never simultaneously present — unlike what the Sāṃkhya school's theory of "transformation" holds. How does the Sāṃkhya school maintain its notion of "transformation"? It holds that there is an underlying substrate-dharma whose own essential nature persists permanently, while other dharmas arise and cease upon it. What principle does such a "transformation" actually violate? Namely: there can be no case in which a persisting substrate-dharma is simultaneously held to have some other, distinct dharma cease and arise upon it. Who says there exists, apart from the dharma itself, some further, separate "substrate-dharma"? It is precisely this very same dharma that, at the moment of "transformation," is merely the basis of a different characteristic — and this alone is what gets called the "substrate-dharma." This, too, is unreasonable. Why is it unreasonable? Because saying that this very same thing is not, in fact, this same thing — a statement of this kind has never been heard of before.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This brief digression is a compressed but genuine philosophical argument, worth pausing on: Sāṃkhya's pariṇāma ("transformation") theory of causation holds that an effect is already latently present within its cause, which then merely modifies its outward appearance while its underlying essential nature (prakṛti) persists unchanged — much like clay remaining clay while taking the shape of a pot. The Abhidharma position argued here is the opposite: causation is strictly sequential, the cause's own existence ceasing at the very moment the effect's existence begins, with no single persisting substance carried across the transition at all. The closing jab — "saying that this very same thing is not, in fact, this same thing has never been heard of" — is aimed at Sāṃkhya's attempt to have it both ways: claiming a persisting substrate while also admitting that substrate takes on genuinely different characteristics, which this text considers simply incoherent once stated plainly. This same momentariness (kṣanika) principle underlies much of this tradition's Abhidharma project as a whole, and it is notable that even a physical-cosmology passage about jewels forming from water pauses to make the point explicitly.

4. Sorting the World: Mountains, Continents, Fresh and Salt Seas

A further karmic wind now sorts the transformed jewels and water into their final places — gathering matter into mountains and continents, and dividing water into fresh and salt to form the inner and outer seas. The section closes with the precise depths at which all nine mountains sink into the water, and the halving pattern by which the eight outer mountains' heights above the water — and thus their widths — decrease all the way down to the outermost Cakravāḍa.

Chinese Source

如是變生金寶等已，復由業力引起別風，簡別寶等攝令聚集成山成洲，分水甘醎令別成立內海外海。如是九山住金輪上，入水量皆等八萬踰繕那，蘇迷盧山出水亦爾。餘八出水半半漸卑，謂初持雙出水四萬，乃至最後鐵輪圍山出水三百一十二半。如是九山一一廣量，各各與自出水量同。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once gold, jewels, and the like have thus been produced through transformation, karmic power further gives rise to a distinct wind, which sorts and separates the jewels and the rest, gathering them together to form mountains and continents, and dividing the water into fresh and salt, thereby separately establishing the inner and outer seas. In this way, the nine mountains, resting upon the gold wheel, each sink into the water to a depth equally of 80,000 yojanas; Mount Sumeru's height above the water is likewise 80,000. The remaining eight mountains rise above the water by half as much again in succession, decreasing in height — that is, the first, Yugandhara, rises 40,000 above the water, and so on, down to the last, the Cakravāḍa, which rises 312.5 yojanas above the water. In this way, each of the nine mountains' breadth is, in each and every case, equal to its own height above the water.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The halving sequence checks out exactly: starting from Yugandhara's 40,000 yojanas and halving seven more times (once for each of the remaining seven outer mountains) gives 20,000, 10,000, 5,000, 2,500, 1,250, 625, and finally 312.5 for the Cakravāḍa — precisely the figure the text states. This kind of internal arithmetic consistency, verifiable by simply running the stated halving rule forward, gives real confidence in the numbers as transmitted, in contrast to some of the more compressed figures earlier in this fascicle whose derivation could not be independently checked.

The elegant rule that each mountain's width always equals its own height above the water means the whole nine-mountain system is built from a single repeated proportion: every ring, going outward from Sumeru, is a smaller version of the same basic shape, halved at each step — a tidy, self-similar architecture governing the entire visible structure of a world-system.

5. Eight Seas: The Seven Inner Waters and the Vast Outer Ocean

Between the nine mountains lie eight seas. The seven inner ones, nestled between Sumeru and the surrounding rings, share water with eight distinct excellent qualities and narrow by half at each successive ring; the eighth and outermost, beyond the seventh mountain, is instead filled with ordinary salt water and dwarfs all seven inner seas combined.

Chinese Source

頌曰：山間有八海，前七名為內，最初廣八萬，四邊各三倍，餘六半半陿，第八名為外，三洛叉二萬，二千踰繕那。論曰：妙高為初、輪圍最後，中間八海，前七名內。七中皆具八功德水：一甘、二冷、三軟、四輕、五清淨、六不臭、七飲時不損喉、八飲已不傷腹。如是七海初廣八萬，約持雙山內邊周量，於其四面數各三倍，謂各成二億四萬踰繕那。其餘六海量半半陿，謂第二海量廣四萬，乃至第七量廣一千二百五十。此等不說周圍量者，以煩多故。第八名外，醎水盈滿，量廣三億二萬二千。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: among the mountains there are eight seas; the first seven are called "inner." The very first is 80,000 wide, with each of its four sides three times this in circumference. The remaining six narrow by half each in succession. The eighth is called "outer," measuring 322,000 yojanas wide. Prose: with Sumeru first and the Cakravāḍa last, there are eight seas in between; the first seven are called "inner." All seven possess water endowed with eight excellent qualities: first, sweet; second, cool; third, soft; fourth, light; fifth, clear and pure; sixth, odorless; seventh, does not harm the throat when drunk; eighth, does not injure the stomach once drunk. These seven seas: the first is 80,000 wide, reckoned along Yugandhara's inner edge, and, on each of its four sides, three times this figure — that is, each side comes to 240,000 yojanas. The remaining six seas narrow by half each in succession — that is, the second sea measures 40,000 in width, and so on down to the seventh, which measures 1,250 in width. For these, the circumferential measure is not given, since it would be overly elaborate. The eighth is called "outer"; filled with salt water, it measures 322,000 yojanas in width.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The halving sequence again checks out precisely: 80,000 halved six times (once per remaining inner sea) gives 40,000, 20,000, 10,000, 5,000, 2,500, and 1,250 for the seventh — exactly matching the stated figure, and consistent with the same halving logic already verified for the mountains themselves in the previous section. The whole nine-mountain, eight-sea system is evidently built from a single, elegant, repeatedly-halved proportion radiating outward from Sumeru, broken only by the outermost outer sea and the Cakravāḍa, which stand apart from this pattern as the boundary of the entire world-system.

The eight qualities of the inner seas' water read like a connoisseur's tasting notes for a substance meant to be perfect in every register — taste (sweet), temperature (cool), texture (soft, light), purity (clear, odorless), and even its effect on the body when swallowed (gentle on the throat, gentle on the stomach). This is a notably embodied, almost sensory list for a cosmological text otherwise dominated by pure measurement, and it stands in deliberate contrast with the eighth sea's bare, unqualified salt water beyond the last inner ring.

6. The Four Great Continents: Cart, Half-Moon, Full Moon, and Square

Beyond the seventh mountain, in the outer sea, four great continents face Mount Sumeru on its four sides, each a different shape facing a different direction: Jambudvīpa in the south, cart-shaped, home to the Vajrāsana where every bodhisattva sits to attain awakening; Pūrvavideha in the east, half-moon-shaped; Aparagodānīya in the west, perfectly round; and Uttarakuru in the north, square. Even the inhabitants' faces are said to mirror their own continent's shape.

Chinese Source

頌曰：於中大洲相，南贍部如車，三邊各二千，南邊有三半；東毘提訶洲，其相如半月，三邊如贍部，東邊三百半；西瞿陀尼洲，其相圓無缺，徑二千五百，周圍此三倍；北俱盧畟方，面各二千等，中洲復有八，四洲邊各二。論曰：於外海中大洲有四，謂於四面對妙高山。南贍部洲北廣南陿，三邊量等，其相如車。南邊唯廣三踰繕那半，三邊各有二千踰繕那。唯此洲中有金剛座，上窮地際下據金輪，一切菩薩將登正覺皆坐此座上起金剛喻定，以無餘依及餘處所有堅固力能持此故。東勝身洲東陿西廣，三邊量等，形如半月。東三百五十，三邊各二千。西牛貨洲圓如滿月，徑二千五百，周圍七千半。北俱盧洲形如方座，四邊量等，面各二千。等言為明無少增減。隨其洲相人面亦然。復有八中洲，是大洲眷屬，謂四大洲側各有二中洲。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: among these, the great continents' shapes: the southern, Jambudvīpa, is shaped like a cart, its three sides each 2,000 yojanas, its southern side 3.5. The eastern, Videha, is shaped like a half-moon; its three sides are as Jambudvīpa's, its eastern side 350. The western, Godānīya, is perfectly round without any gap, 2,500 in diameter, its circumference three times this. The northern, Kuru, is square, each face equally 2,000. There are, besides, eight intermediate continents, two beside each of the four great continents. Prose: within the outer sea there are four great continents, situated facing Mount Sumeru on its four sides. Jambudvīpa in the south is broad in the north and narrow in the south; its three equal sides are of equal measure, and its shape resembles a cart. Its southern side alone is only 3.5 yojanas wide; its other three sides are each 2,000 yojanas. Only within this continent is there the Vajrāsana, the Diamond Seat — reaching upward to the very limit of the earth's surface and resting below upon the gold wheel — upon which every bodhisattva, when about to attain complete awakening, sits to enter the vajra-like concentration, since nowhere else has ground with sufficient firm supporting power to hold it. Pūrvavideha in the east is narrow in the east and broad in the west; its three sides are of equal measure, its shape like a half-moon. Its eastern side is 350; its other three sides are each 2,000. Aparagodānīya in the west is round like a full moon, 2,500 in diameter, with a circumference of 7,500. Uttarakuru in the north is shaped like a square seat, its four sides of equal measure, each face 2,000. The word "equally" is meant to clarify that there is not the slightest increase or decrease among the four faces. The inhabitants' faces, in each continent, correspondingly resemble the shape of their own continent. There are, moreover, eight intermediate continents, satellite to the great continents — that is, beside each of the four great continents there are two intermediate continents.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Jambudvīpa's Vajrāsana is a striking detail: this text locates the literal, physical seat of every buddha's awakening at a single fixed point in its cosmology, grounded — quite literally — all the way down to the gold wheel itself, on the grounds that no other location in the entire universe has ground firm enough to bear a bodhisattva's vajra-like concentration. This is a good example of how this cosmology isn't purely decorative background: it is load-bearing for the tradition's central soteriological narrative, explaining in physical terms why awakening happens where it does.

The claim that each continent's inhabitants' faces mirror their continent's shape is a small, charming piece of correlative cosmology — cart-shaped faces in Jambudvīpa, half-moon faces in Pūrvavideha, round faces in Aparagodānīya, square faces in Uttarakuru — treating geography and physiognomy as expressions of the same underlying pattern rather than unrelated facts.

7. The Eight Intermediate Continents, and a Question of Who Lives There

Each of the four great continents has two smaller satellite continents beside it, eight in total, individually named. Most are said to be inhabited by ordinary human beings — but one further view holds that a single one of these eight is instead the exclusive domain of rākṣasas, flesh-eating demons.

Chinese Source

贍部洲邊二中洲者，一遮末羅洲、二筏羅遮末羅洲。勝身洲邊二中洲者，一提訶洲、二毘提訶洲。牛貨洲邊二中洲者，一舍搋洲、二嗢怛羅漫怛里拏洲。俱盧洲邊二中洲者，一矩拉婆洲、二憍拉婆洲。此一切洲皆人所住。有說：唯一邏剎娑居。

Ru-Yi Translation

The two intermediate continents beside Jambudvīpa are: first, Cāmara; second, Aparacāmara. The two intermediate continents beside Videha are: first, Deha; second, Videha. The two intermediate continents beside Godānīya are: first, Śāṭha; second, Uttaramantriṇa. The two intermediate continents beside Kuru are: first, Kurava; second, Kaurava. All of these continents are inhabited by human beings. There is a further view: that only one of these is inhabited, and solely by rākṣasas.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The eight names are given here in their standard Sanskrit reconstructions rather than as literal translations of Xuánzàng's phonetic transliterations, matching the treatment already given to the seven ring-mountains' names. The dissenting view that a single intermediate continent belongs exclusively to rākṣasas is stated without further elaboration or argument here — the text simply records the alternative and moves on, leaving open which of the eight this would be.

8. Lake Anavatapta, the Four Great Rivers, and How Jambudvīpa Got Its Name

North of Jambudvīpa's center lie three tiers of black mountains, then the Great Snow Mountain, then the Fragrant-Intoxicating Mountain — and nestled between the snow and the fragrance, Lake Anavatapta, the Lake Without Heat, an inaccessible fifty-yojana square of perfect water from which the four great rivers of the known world flow. Beside this lake grows the Jambu forest, whose sweet fruit gives the entire continent its name.

Chinese Source

頌曰：此北九黑山，雪香醉山內，無熱池縱廣，五十踰繕那。論曰：此贍部洲從中向北，三處各有三重黑山。有大雪山在黑山北，大雪山北有香醉山。雪北香南有大池水，名無熱惱，出四大河：一殑伽河、二信度河、三徙多河、四縛芻河。無熱惱池縱廣正等，面各五十踰繕那量，八功德水盈滿其中，非得通人無由能至。於此池側有贍部林，樹形高大其果甘美，依此林故名贍部洲，或依此果以立洲號。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: to the north of this are nine black mountains; within the snow and fragrant-intoxicating mountains lies the Lake Without Heat, its length and breadth each fifty yojanas. Prose: from the center of Jambudvīpa going north, there are three places, each with three layers of black mountains. Beyond these black mountains to the north lies the Great Snow Mountain; north of the Great Snow Mountain lies the Fragrant-Intoxicating Mountain. North of the snow and south of the fragrance lies a great lake, called Anavatapta, the Lake Without Heat, from which flow four great rivers: first, the Gaṅgā; second, the Sindhu; third, the Sītā; fourth, the Vakṣu. Lake Anavatapta's length and breadth are perfectly equal, each face measuring fifty yojanas, filled with water endowed with the eight excellent qualities — one who has not attained supernormal penetration has no way to reach it. Beside this lake there is a Jambu forest, its trees tall and great, their fruit sweet and delicious; it is on account of this forest that the continent is called Jambudvīpa, or, alternatively, it is on account of this fruit that the continent's name was established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four rivers named here — the Gaṅgā, Sindhu, Sītā, and Vakṣu — correspond to real, historically identifiable rivers of the Indian subcontinent and Central Asia (the Ganges and Indus among them), even as their common source at a lake "which one has not attained supernormal penetration has no way to reach" places that source definitively outside ordinary geography. This is a recurring pattern worth noticing in Buddhist cosmological literature: real, known rivers are given a shared mythic point of origin that is explicitly, deliberately inaccessible to ordinary travel, blending an inherited geographic reality with a cosmology that exceeds it.

The text offers two possible etymologies for "Jambudvīpa" without choosing between them — named for the forest itself, or for its fruit — and does not treat this as a problem requiring resolution, simply presenting both as live possibilities. This is a lighter, more casual kind of open-endedness than the fascicle 10's explicit debates over doctrine, but it fits the same general pattern of this text declining to manufacture false precision where the tradition itself preserves more than one account.

9. Avīci and the Seven Hells Stacked Above It

The treatise now turns to the hells beneath Jambudvīpa, beginning with Avīci at the very bottom — named "Uninterrupted" because its suffering never lets up, unlike the Reviving Hell just below the surface, where beings are literally revived by a cooling wind after every torture, only for it to begin again. The other six hells named already in fascicle 8's cosmology are stacked in between, worst to mildest, from Avīci up to Reviving.

Chinese Source

復於何處置㮈落迦、大㮈落迦？何量有幾？頌曰：此下過二萬，無間深廣同，上七㮈落迦，八增皆十六。謂煻煨屍糞，鋒刃烈河增，各住彼四方，餘八寒地獄。論曰：此贍部洲下過二萬，有阿鼻旨大㮈落迦。深廣同前，謂各二萬故。彼底去此四萬踰繕那。以於其中受苦無間，非如餘七大㮈落迦受苦非恒，故名無間。且如等活㮈落迦中，諸有情身雖被種種斫刺磨擣，而彼暫遇涼風所吹還活如本，由斯理故立等活名。阿鼻旨中無如是事。有餘師說：阿鼻旨中無樂間苦故名無間，餘地獄中有樂間起，雖無異熟而有等流。七㮈落迦在無間上重累而住。其七者何？一者極熱、二者炎熱、三者大叫、四者號叫、五者眾合、六者黑繩、七者等活。有說：此七在無間傍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Where, then, are the hells and the great hells located? What are their measurements, and how many are there? Verse: below this, past 20,000 yojanas, lies Avīci, equal in depth and breadth; above it are seven more hells. The eight hells each have sixteen surrounding torments — namely, the ember-pit, the corpse-mire, the blade, and the scalding-river torments, each situated on its own of the four sides. There are, besides, eight further cold hells. Prose: below this Jambudvīpa, past 20,000 yojanas, lies the great hell Avīci. Its depth and breadth are the same as stated before — that is, each 20,000. Its bottom lies 40,000 yojanas from here. Because suffering within it is uninterrupted — unlike the other seven great hells, where suffering is not constant — it is called "Uninterrupted." Consider, for instance, the Reviving Hell: although the sentient beings there have their bodies chopped, stabbed, ground, and pounded in various ways, they briefly encounter a cooling wind and are revived exactly as before — hence, by this logic, it is named "Reviving." In Avīci, no such thing occurs. There is a further view: in Avīci, there is no pleasure interspersed with suffering, hence it is called "Uninterrupted"; in the other hells, pleasure does arise interspersed with suffering — though not as a karmic ripening, it does occur as a karmic outflow. The seven other hells are stacked, layer upon layer, above Avīci. What are these seven? First, Great Scorching; second, Scorching; third, Great Wailing; fourth, Wailing; fifth, Crushing-Together; sixth, Black-Rope; seventh, Reviving. There is a further view: that these seven lie beside Avīci, not stacked directly above it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seven hells above Avīci are named here in the same English forms already established in fascicle 8's opening cosmology (Reviving / Black-Rope / Crushing-Together / Wailing / Great Wailing / Scorching / Great Scorching), kept consistent with that earlier list rather than retranslated fresh. Ordered from the passage's "first" to "seventh," they run from most severe (Great Scorching, nearest Avīci) to least severe (Reviving, nearest the surface) — the reverse of how a reader encountering them for the first time might expect a "first, second, third..." list to run.

The explanation of why Avīci alone is "uninterrupted" is doing careful conceptual work: it isn't simply the hottest or most painful hell in some vague intensifying sense, but specifically the one hell where suffering has no gaps at all — not even the brief, cyclical relief-and-renewal that defines the Reviving Hell just one level up. The second view offered — that even the other hells have some interspersed pleasure, "not as karmic ripening but as karmic outflow" — draws a fine technical distinction between pleasure that is itself a direct fruit of past wholesome karma, versus pleasure that merely continues an existing habitual pattern (等流, niṣyanda) without constituting a fresh karmic reward, worth flagging since the distinction is stated but not elaborated further here.

10. The Buddha's Verse on the Eight Hells, and the First Two Surrounding Torments

The Buddha himself is cited describing the eight hot hells' iron ground, iron walls, and iron-sealed gates, each hell surrounded by sixteen additional torments beyond its main suffering. The treatise begins working through these sixteen one by one, starting with the knee-deep ember pit, whose burns heal instantly only to be inflicted again with every step, and the corpse-mire, where needle-sharp worms bore through skin and bone to devour the marrow within.

Chinese Source

有說：此七在無間傍。八㮈落迦增各十六，故薄伽梵說此頌言：此八㮈落迦，我說甚難越，以熱鐵為地，周匝有鐵牆，四面有四門，關閉以鐵扇，巧安布分量，各有十六增，多百踰繕那，滿中造惡者，周遍焰交徹，猛火恒洞然。十六增者，八㮈落迦四面門外各有四所：一煻煨增，謂此增內煻煨沒膝，有情遊彼纔下足時，皮肉與血俱燋爛墜，舉足還生平復如本。二屍糞增，謂此增內屍糞泥滿，於中多有娘矩吒蟲，利如針身白頭黑，有情遊彼皆為此蟲鑽皮破骨咂食其髓。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a further view: that these seven lie beside Avīci. The eight hells each have sixteen surrounding torments; hence the Bhagavān spoke this verse: "These eight hells, I declare, are exceedingly difficult to escape — their ground is made of heated iron, surrounded on all sides by iron walls; on the four sides are four gates, sealed shut with iron doors; skillfully arranged and measured out, each has sixteen surrounding torments, extending over many hundreds of yojanas, filled with evildoers, pervaded through and through by intermingling flames, where a fierce fire perpetually blazes." As for the sixteen surrounding torments: outside the four gates on the four sides of each of the eight hells, there are four such places. First, the ember-pit torment: within this torment, embers bury one to the knees; the moment a being walks there and sets foot down, the skin, flesh, and blood all scorch, decay, and fall away — yet the moment the foot is lifted, it is restored exactly as before. Second, the corpse-mire torment: within this torment, corpses and filth fill the mire, in which there are many nyaṅku worms, sharp as needles, white-bodied with black heads; beings who walk there are all bored through the skin, their bones broken, by these worms, which suck and devour their marrow.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The instant-healing detail attached to the ember pit — flesh burns away with each step, then is "restored exactly as before" the moment the foot lifts — is a recurring structural feature of these hell-torments worth naming explicitly: injury without any cumulative damage, so that the same maximal suffering can be inflicted again and again without end, rather than mounting toward death or numbness. This is the same underlying logic already seen in Avīci's definition as "uninterrupted": these torments are engineered, in the text's own cosmology, specifically to prevent suffering from ever tapering off through injury, exhaustion, or death.
