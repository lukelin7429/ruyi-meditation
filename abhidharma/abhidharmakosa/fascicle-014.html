Restraint, Non-Restraint, and the Vows Between

Fascicle 14 continues Chapter 4 (分別業品第四之二, the Karma-nirdeśa) directly from where fascicle 13 left off. Having spent the previous fascicle establishing what unmanifested karma is and whether it is real at all, the treatise now turns to classifying it: the threefold division into restraint, non-restraint, and neither; the three kinds of restraint (monastic vow, meditation-born, and path-born); and, at length, the structure of the prātimokṣa vows themselves — bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī, śrāmaṇera, upāsaka, and their companions.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress, 11 sections so far Source: Taishō T1558, vol. 29, fascicle 14 Author: Vasubandhu · Translator into Chinese: Xuánzàng

1. Three Kinds of Unmanifested Karma, and Three Kinds of Restraint

Picking up directly from fascicle 13's long inquiry into manifested and unmanifested karma, the treatise now classifies unmanifested karma into three basic types: restraint (律儀, saṃvara — karma that holds back wrongdoing), non-restraint (不律儀 — karma that commits one to wrongdoing), and a third category that is neither. Restraint itself earns its name because it is able to block and extinguish the continuity of bad conduct. The treatise then distinguishes three kinds of restraint by their source: the vow of individual liberation (別解脫律儀, prātimokṣa-saṃvara), tied to the desire realm's precepts; meditation-born restraint (靜慮生律儀), tied to the form realm's precepts, arising automatically alongside meditative absorption; and path-born restraint (道生律儀), the uncontaminated restraint that arises together with the noble path itself.

Chinese Source

傍論已了，復應辯前表無表相。頌曰： 無表三律儀， 不律儀非二。 論曰：此中無表，略說有三：一者律儀、二不律儀、三者非二謂非律儀非不律儀。能遮能滅惡戒相續故名律儀。如是律儀差別有幾？頌曰： 律儀別解脫， 靜慮及道生。 論曰：律儀差別，略有三種：一別解脫律儀，謂欲纏戒；二靜慮生律儀，謂色纏戒；三道生律儀，謂無漏戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

The digression having now concluded, we should further explain the characteristics of manifested and unmanifested karma discussed earlier. Verse: unmanifested karma is restraint, non-restraint, or neither. Prose: unmanifested karma, briefly stated, is of three kinds: first, restraint; second, non-restraint; third, neither of the two, meaning neither restraint nor non-restraint. It is called restraint because it is able to block and extinguish the continuity of bad conduct. How many distinctions does this restraint itself have? Verse: restraint is the vow of individual liberation, and that born of meditative absorption and of the [noble] path. Prose: restraint, briefly stated, is of three kinds: first, the restraint of individual liberation, meaning precepts bound to the desire realm; second, restraint born of meditative absorption, meaning precepts bound to the form realm; third, restraint born of the [noble] path, meaning uncontaminated precepts.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section's opening line — "the digression having now concluded" — is the treatise's own acknowledgment that the entire previous fascicle's debate over whether unmanifested karma is real at all was itself a lengthy aside, prompted by the original question of what bodily manifestation is; the systematic classification promised back near the start of that debate resumes only now. Readers coming from fascicle 13 should understand this fascicle as continuing that same unresolved metaphysical backdrop: everything said here about unmanifested karma's threefold division and the three kinds of restraint is stated primarily from the Vaibhāṣika's own framework, without re-litigating the Sautrāntika's skepticism about whether any of this "unmanifested matter" exists as a separate substance in the first place.

The three kinds of restraint map cleanly onto this treatise's three-realm cosmology: the vow of individual liberation belongs to ordinary monastic and lay precepts taken by beings in the desire realm; meditation-born restraint arises automatically, without a separate ceremony, the moment one actually enters meditative absorption associated with the form realm; and path-born restraint arises automatically together with uncontaminated, liberating insight itself. The bulk of this fascicle, as its opening promises, will focus on the first of these three — the vow of individual liberation — in far greater structural detail than the other two.

2. Eight Named Vows, Four Real Substances

The vow of individual liberation carries eight traditional names — bhikṣu (fully ordained monk), bhikṣuṇī (fully ordained nun), śikṣamāṇā (probationary nun), śrāmaṇera (novice monk), śrāmaṇerī (novice nun), upāsaka (layman), upāsikā (laywoman), and upavāsa (one-day precept-holder) — but only four genuinely distinct underlying substances, since a bhikṣu and bhikṣuṇī vow, a śrāmaṇera/śikṣamāṇā and śrāmaṇerī vow, and an upāsaka and upāsikā vow are each really the same vow, differing only by the practitioner's sex. The proof offered is a striking one: should a person's sex organs change — a recognized possibility in this cosmology — their vow's underlying substance is never actually relinquished and reacquired, only renamed, showing that the difference between the paired vows was never a difference in substance at all, only in the label attached according to the practitioner's bodily form at the time.

Chinese Source

初律儀相差別云何？頌曰： 初律儀八種， 實體唯有四， 形轉名異故， 各別不相違。 論曰：別解脫律儀，相差別有八：一苾芻律儀、二苾芻尼律儀、三正學律儀、四勤策律儀、五勤策女律儀、六近事律儀、七近事女律儀、八近住律儀。如是八種律儀相差別，總名第一別解脫律儀。雖有八名，實體唯四：一苾芻律儀、二勤策律儀、三近事律儀、四近住律儀。唯此四種別解律儀皆有體實，相各別故。所以者何？離苾芻律儀，無別苾芻尼律儀；離勤策律儀，無別正學、勤策女律儀；離近事律儀，無別近事女律儀。云何知然？由形改轉，體雖無捨得，而名有異故。形謂形相，即男女根。由此二根男女形別，但由形轉，令諸律儀名為苾芻、苾芻尼等，謂轉根位，令本苾芻律儀名苾芻尼律儀，或苾芻尼律儀名苾芻律儀；令本勤策律儀名勤策女律儀，或勤策女律儀及正學律儀名勤策律儀；令本近事律儀名近事女律儀，或近事女律儀名近事律儀。非轉根位有捨先得得先未得律儀因緣，故四律儀非異三體。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the distinctions among the characteristics of the first kind of restraint [the vow of individual liberation]? Verse: the first restraint has eight characteristics, but its real substance is only four; because the bodily form changes, the names differ, and there is no contradiction in each being distinct. Prose: the vow of individual liberation, in terms of its distinguishing characteristics, is of eight kinds: first, the bhikṣu vow; second, the bhikṣuṇī vow; third, the śikṣamāṇā vow; fourth, the śrāmaṇera vow; fifth, the śrāmaṇerī vow; sixth, the upāsaka vow; seventh, the upāsikā vow; eighth, the upavāsa vow. These eight kinds of restraint, distinguished by their characteristics, are collectively named the first kind, the vow of individual liberation. Although there are eight names, the real substance is only four: first, the bhikṣu vow; second, the śrāmaṇera vow; third, the upāsaka vow; fourth, the upavāsa vow. Only these four vows of individual liberation each have a real substance, since their characteristics are each genuinely distinct. Why is this so? Apart from the bhikṣu vow, there is no separate bhikṣuṇī vow; apart from the śrāmaṇera vow, there is no separate śikṣamāṇā or śrāmaṇerī vow; apart from the upāsaka vow, there is no separate upāsikā vow. How is this known? Because, when the bodily form changes, although the substance [of the vow] is neither relinquished nor newly acquired, its name nevertheless differs. "Form" here means bodily form, namely the male or female organ. Because of these two organs, male and female bodily forms differ, and it is solely by virtue of a change in bodily form that these restraints come to be named bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī, and so on — that is, at the moment the faculty changes, what was originally the bhikṣu vow comes to be named the bhikṣuṇī vow, or the bhikṣuṇī vow comes to be named the bhikṣu vow; what was originally the śrāmaṇera vow comes to be named the śrāmaṇerī vow, or the śrāmaṇerī vow and the śikṣamāṇā vow come to be named the śrāmaṇera vow; what was originally the upāsaka vow comes to be named the upāsikā vow, or the upāsikā vow comes to be named the upāsaka vow. Apart from the occasion of the faculty changing, there is no cause or condition for relinquishing what was previously obtained or obtaining what was not previously obtained — hence the four restraints are not different from these three pairs' substance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sex-change argument here is a genuinely distinctive piece of Abhidharma reasoning, and worth being explicit about: this treatise's broader cosmology treats a person's sex as a physical faculty (根) that can, in certain circumstances, change during a single lifetime, and the argument exploits this directly — since the underlying vow-substance persists continuously through such a change, with only its label switching to match the new bodily form, this is offered as proof that the vow's substance was never sex-specific to begin with, only conventionally renamed according to whichever sex the vow-holder currently has. We have translated 苾芻/苾芻尼 as "bhikṣu"/"bhikṣuṇī" and 正學 as "śikṣamāṇā" (the intermediate probationary ordination a novice nun undertakes before full ordination) rather than using looser English equivalents, since these are established technical terms in Buddhist monastic law with no single-word English equivalent that fully captures their specific status.

We should flag that this passage's logic works specifically for the paired vows it names (bhikṣu/bhikṣuṇī, śrāmaṇera-śikṣamāṇā/śrāmaṇerī, upāsaka/upāsikā) and does not extend to the fourth vow, upavāsa (the one-day lay precept), which has no male/female pairing at all in the traditional eightfold list — it stands as its own, singular vow-type from the start, which is presumably why the treatise's arithmetic ends at four real substances rather than needing to explain away a fifth.

3. Three Vows Held at Once, Never in Conflict

A practitioner typically progresses through the vow of individual liberation in stages — first taking the upāsaka vow, then the śrāmaṇera vow, then the full bhikṣu vow — and the treatise asks whether these three should really count as three separate substances, or as one substance simply expanded by degrees. It settles on three genuinely separate substances, illustrated by an analogy to coinage: much as single and double gold coins, and pieces of fifty or twenty [smaller denomination], are each their own distinct substance even though a larger one is obtained after a smaller one, so too do the three restraints each arise in full completeness, without mixing into one another. Each level shares the same basic precepts (all three include abstaining from killing, up through abstaining from alcohol), yet they differ by degree of scope — the same underlying precept becomes progressively more comprehensive as one advances to more numerous and more demanding trainings, cutting off "occasions of heedlessness" (the circumstances that give rise to wrongdoing) ever more broadly. Because the three vows are, in this sense, genuinely distinct, relinquishing the highest one (the bhikṣu vow) does not cause the lower two to be relinquished as well, even though the higher vow is typically taken while the lower ones are still in force — all three coexist simultaneously within a single person, without a later vow ever displacing an earlier one.

Chinese Source

若從近事律儀受勤策律儀，復從勤策律儀受苾芻律儀，此三律儀為由增足遠離方便立別別名，如隻雙金錢及五十二十，為體各別具足頓生。三種律儀體不相雜，其相各別具足頓生。三律儀中具三離殺，乃至具足三離飲酒，餘數多少隨其所應。既爾相望同類何別？由因緣別，相望有異。其事云何？如如求受多種學處，如是如是能離多種憍逸處時，即離眾多殺等緣起。以諸遠離依因緣發，故因緣別遠離有異。若無此事，捨苾芻律儀，爾時則應三律儀皆捨，前二攝在後一中故。既不許然，故三各別。然此三種互不相違，於一身中俱時而轉，非由受後捨前律儀，勿捨苾芻戒便非近事等。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one takes the śrāmaṇera vow from the upāsaka vow, and then takes the bhikṣu vow from the śrāmaṇera vow, are these three restraints given separate names merely by way of an expanded means of renunciation [i.e., the same substance simply enlarged], or [are they genuinely distinct substances, comparable] to a single gold coin and a double gold coin, and to pieces of fifty and of twenty, each having its own distinct substance and each arising in full completeness [rather than one being merely an expansion of the other]? [Reply:] The substances of the three kinds of restraint are not mixed together; each has its own distinct characteristic and arises in full completeness. Among the three restraints, each is complete with the abstention from killing, and so on up through complete abstention from drinking alcohol; the remaining number [of precepts each includes] varies as is appropriate to each. [Objection:] Given this, how, then, do these restraints differ from one another when compared as belonging to the same general category? [Reply:] They differ from one another because of a difference in their causes and conditions. [Objection:] How is this so? [Reply:] Just as, the more numerous kinds of training one seeks to undertake, the more numerous the occasions of heedlessness one is thereby able to renounce, one thereby cuts off correspondingly more numerous occasions giving rise to killing and the like. Since each renunciation arises depending on its own causes and conditions, and these causes and conditions differ, the renunciations themselves differ. If this were not the case, then, at the moment one relinquished the bhikṣu vow, all three restraints would have to be relinquished together, since the first two would be subsumed within the last. But since this is not accepted to be so, the three are each distinct. Yet these three kinds are mutually non-conflicting, operating together simultaneously within a single person — it is not the case that taking a later vow causes an earlier restraint to be relinquished, or else relinquishing the bhikṣu precepts would mean one was no longer even an upāsaka and so on.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The coin analogy is doing careful work distinguishing two models that might otherwise look identical from the outside: on one model, taking the śrāmaṇera vow after the upāsaka vow would just be "the same restraint, now bigger" — like adding more water to the same cup; on the treatise's actual model, it is "a wholly new, complete restraint, arising alongside the still-intact earlier one" — like acquiring a second, larger coin without losing the first. The practical payoff, spelled out in the closing lines, is significant for monastic law: if a monk's higher ordination is later invalidated or relinquished, this does not strip away the more basic upāsaka and śrāmaṇera-level restraints underneath it, since those were never actually absorbed into or replaced by the bhikṣu vow in the first place — they were always separate, coexisting substances.

We should note that the "same precepts, different scope" account given here (each level shares the same core prohibitions — killing, stealing, and so on — but differs in how many total training-rules and heedlessness-occasions it covers) is somewhat compressed in the source, and later in this fascicle the treatise will spell out the exact precept-counts for each of the four levels (five, eight, ten, and the full monastic code) in much more explicit detail — this section's claim that "the remaining number varies as is appropriate to each" is essentially a forward pointer to that fuller breakdown.

4. The Precise Precepts Behind Each of the Four Levels

The treatise now names the exact precepts underlying each of the four levels of restraint. The upāsaka (lay follower) vow consists of abstaining from five things: killing, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, false speech, and drinking alcohol. The upavāsa (one-day precept-holder) vow expands this to eight: killing, taking what is not given, all sexual activity (a stricter standard than the upāsaka's "misconduct" alone), false speech, alcohol, adorning oneself with scent and garlands and attending song and dance, sleeping or sitting on high and luxurious beds, and eating at improper times. The śrāmaṇera (novice) vow expands to ten by splitting the sixth upavāsa precept into two separate rules and adding a tenth: handling gold, silver, and other valuables. The bhikṣu (fully ordained monk) vow, finally, consists of abstaining from every bodily and verbal act that ought to be abstained from — the complete monastic code.

Chinese Source

近事、近住、勤策、苾芻四種律儀云何安立？頌曰： 受離五八十， 一切所應離， 立近事近住， 勤策及苾芻。 論曰：應知此中如數次第，依四遠離立四律儀。謂受離五所應遠離，安立第一近事律儀。何等名為五所應離？一者殺生、二不與取、三欲邪行、四虛誑語、五飲諸酒。若受離八所應遠離，安立第二近住律儀。何等名為八所應離？一者殺生、二不與取、三非梵行、四虛誑語、五飲諸酒、六塗飾香鬘舞歌觀聽、七眠坐高廣嚴麗床座、八食非時食。若受離十所應遠離，安立第三勤策律儀。何等名為十所應離？謂於前八塗飾香鬘舞歌觀聽開為二種，復加受畜金銀等寶以為第十。若受離一切應離身語業，安立第四苾芻律儀。

Ru-Yi Translation

How are the four kinds of restraint — upāsaka, upavāsa, śrāmaṇera, and bhikṣu — established? Verse: undertaking the abstention from five, eight, ten, or all that should be abstained from establishes the upāsaka, upavāsa, śrāmaṇera, and bhikṣu [restraints, respectively]. Prose: one should understand that, in this matter, the four restraints are established, in numerical order, based on four kinds of renunciation. That is, undertaking the abstention from the five things that should be abstained from establishes the first, the upāsaka restraint. What are the five things that should be abstained from? First, killing living beings; second, taking what is not given; third, sexual misconduct; fourth, false speech; fifth, drinking alcohol. If one undertakes the abstention from the eight things that should be abstained from, this establishes the second, the upavāsa restraint. What are the eight things that should be abstained from? First, killing living beings; second, taking what is not given; third, unchaste conduct; fourth, false speech; fifth, drinking alcohol; sixth, adorning oneself with scent and garlands, and dancing, singing, and watching or listening [to such performances]; seventh, sleeping or sitting upon a high, broad, or ornately adorned bed or seat; eighth, eating food at an improper time. If one undertakes the abstention from the ten things that should be abstained from, this establishes the third, the śrāmaṇera restraint. What are the ten things that should be abstained from? It is [the same eight as above, except that] the sixth item — adorning oneself with scent and garlands, and dancing, singing, and watching or listening — is divided into two separate items, and, further, accepting and keeping gold, silver, and other such valuables is added as the tenth. If one undertakes the abstention from every bodily and verbal act that ought to be abstained from, this establishes the fourth, the bhikṣu restraint.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We want to flag one precise but easy-to-miss distinction the Chinese draws between the third precept at the upāsaka level and the corresponding precept at the upavāsa and higher levels: the upāsaka vow's 欲邪行 specifically targets sexual *misconduct* (i.e., certain kinds of sexual activity, appropriate to a layperson's ordinary household life), whereas the upavāsa and higher vows' 非梵行 targets "unchaste conduct" more broadly — effectively all sexual activity without exception — reflecting the more renunciate, temporary-monastic character of the upavāsa vow and above. We have deliberately used different English phrasing ("sexual misconduct" vs. "unchaste conduct") to preserve this distinction, since collapsing both into a single English phrase would erase a real difference in scope between the lay and monastic-adjacent precepts.

The śrāmaṇera vow's arithmetic is worth spelling out plainly, since the Chinese states it tersely: it does not add two entirely new precepts to the upavāsa's eight to reach ten, but rather splits one existing precept (the sixth, covering both self-adornment and entertainment-watching) into two separate precepts, and then adds only one genuinely new rule (avoiding money and valuables) — meaning the śrāmaṇera's ten precepts share nine-tenths of their actual content with the upavāsa's eight, differing mainly in the added prohibition against handling gold and silver, which marks the transition from a temporary lay observance to an ongoing monastic identity with no personal property.

5. Why Only the First Instant of a Vow Is Called "Prātimokṣa"

The treatise now works through several names applied to the vow of individual liberation, unpacking each one's etymology. It is called śīla ("moral discipline") because it levels out the dangerous terrain of bad karma — glossed, in a verse, as being "cool," since one who upholds this precept-happiness experiences no bodily distress. It is called sadācāra ("wholesome conduct") because the wise praise it. It is called karma because it is a self-standing, completed act — and, addressing an apparent puzzle, the treatise clarifies that even unmanifested karma, though sometimes called "non-doing," still counts as a completed act, since a person with a sense of shame relies on unmanifested karma's power specifically in order to refrain from committing evil, which is itself a kind of doing. It is called restraint because it guards against wrongdoing in body and speech. Most strikingly, the treatise holds that the names "prātimokṣa" (individual liberation) and "karma-path" apply, strictly speaking, only to the very first instant of taking a vow — the moment various evils are each individually renounced for the first time and the karmic act reaches its completion — while every moment after that first one, though the vow continues unbroken, is technically named only "restraint of individual liberation," not "prātimokṣa" itself, and "what arises afterward," not "karma-path."

Chinese Source

別解律儀名差別者，頌曰： 俱得名尸羅， 妙行業律儀， 唯初表無表， 名別解業道。 論曰：能平險業故名尸羅。訓釋詞者，謂清涼故。如伽他言：受持戒樂，身無熱惱，故名尸羅。智者稱揚，故名妙行。所作自體，故名為業。豈不無表亦名不作，如何今說所作自體？有慚恥者受無表力不造眾惡，故名不作。表思所造，得所作名。有餘釋言：是作因故、是作果故，名作無失。能防身語，故名律儀。如是應知，別解脫戒通初後位無差別名，唯初剎那表及無表，得別解脫及業道名。謂受戒時初表無表，別別棄捨種種惡故。依初別捨義，立別解脫名。即於爾時所作究竟，依業暢義立業道名，故初剎那名別解脫，亦得名曰別解律儀，亦得名為根本業道。從第二念乃至未捨，不名別解脫，名別解律儀；不名業道，名為後起。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the distinctions among the names for the restraint of individual liberation, verse: all of these are called śīla, wholesome conduct, karma, and restraint; only the first manifested and unmanifested karma is called individual liberation and karma-path. Prose: it is called śīla because it is able to level out dangerous karma. As for its etymological gloss, it means "cool" — as a verse states: "One who upholds precept-happiness has a body free of feverish distress; hence it is called śīla." It is called wholesome conduct because the wise praise it. It is called karma because it is a self-standing, completed act. [Objection:] But is unmanifested karma not also called "non-doing"? How, then, can it now be said to be a self-standing, completed act? [Reply:] One possessing a sense of shame relies on the power of unmanifested karma to refrain from committing many evils; hence it is called "non-doing" [in that specific sense]. What manifested [karma], produced by volition, [directly does] is what earns the name "doing." Other explanations say: it is because it serves as the cause of an act, and as the result of an act, that the name "act" involves no error [when applied to it]. It is called restraint because it is able to guard against bodily and verbal [wrongdoing]. One should understand it in this way: the precept of individual liberation, spanning both its initial and later stages, carries no distinguishing name [between these stages] in most respects — it is only the very first instant's manifested and unmanifested karma that earns the names "individual liberation" and "karma-path." That is, at the time of receiving the precept, the initial manifested and unmanifested karma individually and distinctly renounces various evils; based on this meaning of an initial, distinct renunciation, the name "individual liberation" (prātimokṣa) is established. And it is at that very moment that the karmic act reaches its completion; based on this meaning of the act's fulfillment, the name "karma-path" is established. Hence the first instant is named "individual liberation," and may also be named "restraint of individual liberation," and may also be named "root karma-path." From the second moment onward, until the vow is relinquished, it is not called "individual liberation," but is called "restraint of individual liberation"; it is not called "karma-path," but is called "what arises afterward."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a fine but genuinely significant technical distinction, and it is easy to read past it: "prātimokṣa" (個別解脫/別解脫, "individual liberation," so named because each precept individually liberates the holder from a specific kind of wrongdoing) and "karma-path" (業道) name only the singular founding instant of taking a vow — the dramatic moment various evils are renounced all at once and the karmic act is completed — while the entire subsequent stretch of a monastic's or layperson's life spent actually upholding that vow is given the humbler names "restraint of individual liberation" and "what arises afterward." The distinction is not merely terminological trivia: it reflects a substantive Abhidharma view that the vow's founding act and its ongoing maintenance are, in some sense, different in kind, even though they share one uninterrupted causal continuity — the founding moment does something (an active renunciation) that its later maintenance, strictly speaking, no longer does in the same way.

The "cool" etymology for śīla is a nice, concrete illustration of how Sanskrit Buddhist vocabulary was explained by its Chinese translators and commentators using folk-etymological glosses rather than strict historical linguistics — the actual Sanskrit śīla is not linguistically related to any word for "coolness," but the gloss captures an experiential point the tradition wanted to make: that moral discipline brings a felt ease and freedom from remorse's "heat," which is presented here as the reason for calling it what it is called, regardless of the term's actual etymological history.

6. Who Possesses Which Kind of Restraint

The treatise now asks who is actually capable of possessing each of the three kinds of restraint. The vow of individual liberation belongs to the eight assemblies — from the bhikṣu down to the upavāsa precept-holder — and to no one outside this circle: non-Buddhist ascetics do take their own vows, but these are never called "restraint of individual liberation," since their vows lack the capacity to permanently free the holder from evils they remain attached to. Meditation-born restraint belongs to anyone who has actually attained meditative absorption, including its neighboring, preliminary stages (extending the name "meditative absorption" to its borders the way a village's name is extended to its surrounding rice fields). Path-born restraint belongs only to noble ones (āryas) — those in either the stage of training or the stage beyond training. Of these three, only meditation-born and path-born restraint are said to "follow the mind" moment by moment, continuing even through different or absent states of mind, unlike the vow of individual liberation.

Chinese Source

誰成就何律儀？頌曰： 八成別解脫， 得靜慮聖者， 成靜慮道生， 後二隨心轉。 論曰：八眾皆成就別解脫律儀，謂從苾芻乃至近住。外道無有所受戒耶？雖有，不名別解脫戒。由彼所受無有功能永脫諸惡依著有故。靜慮生者，謂此律儀從靜慮生、或依靜慮。若得靜慮者，定成此律儀。諸靜慮邊亦名靜慮，如近村邑得村邑名故。有說言於此村邑有稻田等，此亦應然。道生律儀聖者成就，此復有二：謂學、無學。於前分別俱有因中說二律儀是隨心轉，於此三內其二者何？謂靜慮生及道生二，非別解脫。所以者何？異心無心亦恒轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Who possesses which restraint? Verse: the eight [assemblies] possess the restraint of individual liberation; those who have attained meditative absorption and noble ones possess meditation-born and path-born restraint respectively; the latter two follow the mind. Prose: all eight assemblies possess the restraint of individual liberation — that is, from the bhikṣu down through the upavāsa precept-holder. [Objection:] Do non-Buddhist ascetics not also receive precepts? [Reply:] Although they do, these are not called the precept of individual liberation, since what they receive lacks the capacity to permanently free them from evils to which they remain attached. "Meditation-born" means that this restraint is either born from meditative absorption, or depends on meditative absorption. If one has attained meditative absorption, one certainly possesses this restraint. The neighboring, preliminary stages of the meditative absorptions are also called "meditative absorption," just as a place near a village comes to be called by the village's own name. Some say that this is so in the same way that one speaks of "this village's rice paddies" [extending the village's name to what surrounds it]; this should be understood the same way here. Path-born restraint is possessed by noble ones — this, again, divides into two: those still in training, and those beyond training. In the earlier discussion of co-existent causes, it was stated that two kinds of restraint "follow the mind" — which two, among these three, are meant? Meditation-born and path-born restraint, not the restraint of individual liberation. Why is this so? Because these two continue on even through a different state of mind, or through the absence of mind altogether, whereas [the restraint of individual liberation does not continue in quite the same automatic way].

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The village-and-rice-paddies analogy is explaining a genuinely useful piece of Abhidharma terminology-extension: rather than requiring a separate technical term for the preliminary, "not-yet-arrived" (未至, anāgamya) stage that precedes full meditative absorption proper, the tradition simply extends the word "meditative absorption" itself to cover this neighboring stage too, the way a village's name naturally extends to the fields surrounding it without needing a new word for "near-the-village." This lets the treatise say simply that "meditation-born restraint" is possessed by "those who have attained meditative absorption" without having to separately specify that the preliminary access-concentration stage counts as well.

The distinction between restraints that "follow the mind" and the vow of individual liberation, which does not, is worth flagging since it will matter for the material still ahead: meditation-born and path-born restraint are tied moment-by-moment to an underlying meditative or noble state of mind and persist automatically as long as that state persists (even through a differently-occupied or temporarily absent mind, in certain technical senses), whereas the vow of individual liberation, once taken, continues as its own independent unmanifested karma stream regardless of what the mind is currently doing — a difference in how each kind of restraint is causally sustained, not merely a difference in name.

7. "Severing Restraint": A Fourfold Classification

Meditation-born and uncontaminated restraint are also given a further name: "severing restraint" (斷律儀), so called because they are capable of permanently cutting off the desire realm's bad conduct and the afflictions that give rise to it. The treatise specifies exactly when this name applies: at the nine uninterrupted paths (無間道) occurring within the preliminary access-concentration stage (未至定), the meditative and uncontaminated restraint that arises together with them earns the name "severing restraint," since it is precisely at these moments that afflictions are being actively cut off. This means some instances of meditative restraint are "severing restraint" and some are not, and the treatise works this out as a fourfold classification: ordinary meditative restraint outside these nine specific moments (severing but not at those moments); uncontaminated restraint specifically at these nine moments (severing restraint proper); contaminated restraint specifically at these nine moments (this case, though occurring at the right moment, is not itself doing the severing); and all remaining uncontaminated restraint outside these nine moments (uncontaminated, but not severing). A parallel fourfold classification is offered for uncontaminated restraint that is not "severing restraint" as well.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Meditative and uncontaminated restraint are also called "severing restraint." At what stage is this name established? Verse: at the nine uninterrupted paths of the preliminary access-concentration stage, the two [kinds of restraint] arising together [with them] are called "severing." Prose: at the nine uninterrupted paths within the preliminary access-concentration stage, the meditative and uncontaminated restraint arising together with them is called "severing restraint," since it is able to permanently sever the desire realm's bad conduct and the afflictions capable of giving rise to it. Because of this, there may be instances of meditative restraint that are not severing restraint, which should be set out in four cases. The first case is contaminated meditative restraint apart from that of the nine uninterrupted paths within the preliminary access-concentration stage. The second case is uncontaminated restraint occurring at the nine uninterrupted paths within the preliminary access-concentration stage. The third case is contaminated restraint occurring at the nine uninterrupted paths within the preliminary access-concentration stage. The fourth case is all remaining uncontaminated restraint, apart from that of the nine uninterrupted paths within the preliminary access-concentration stage. In the same way, there may be instances of uncontaminated restraint that are not severing restraint, which should likewise be set out in four cases, to be understood by analogy with the four cases just given.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "nine uninterrupted paths" (九無間道) refer to a standard Abhidharma framework in which the process of cutting off afflictions tied to any given meditative level is divided into nine successive degrees of intensity, each paired with an "uninterrupted path" (the moment of actively severing that degree of affliction) and a "liberation path" (the moment immediately after, when the severing is confirmed complete) — this ninefold division recurs throughout Abhidharma soteriology wherever affliction-elimination is being tracked with precision, and this passage is applying it specifically to the preliminary access-concentration stage that precedes full absorption in the first dhyāna.

The four-case classification here is a standard Abhidharma logical device (catuṣkoṭi-style fourfold analysis) for precisely mapping which members of one category (meditative restraint) fall inside versus outside another category (severing restraint) — worth noting because this same four-case structure will recur through the rest of this fascicle's technical material. We have kept the translation of each case as literal and parallel in structure as the Chinese itself, since the whole point of this device is the systematic exhaustiveness of the four combinations, and any paraphrase risks obscuring which logical slot each case is meant to fill.

8. The Buddha's Fourfold Restraint Verse, and What Guards the Sense Faculties

The treatise now addresses a verse of the Buddha's own summarizing restraint into four kinds: bodily restraint, verbal restraint, mental restraint, and "pervasive" restraint (a comprehensive restraint covering all three together). This raises a question, since a separate sūtra passage speaks of "restraint of the eye faculty" and the other sense faculties, urging that they be well guarded and well established — what, then, is the essential nature of this "mental root-faculty restraint"? The treatise rules out identifying it with unmanifested matter, since restraining the eye and the other sense faculties is not itself a bodily or verbal act with a physical basis. Instead, it identifies this restraint with the combined operation of two mental factors acting together: clear awareness (正知, saṃprajanya) and mindfulness (正念, smṛti) — the coordinated capacity to know clearly what one is perceiving and to keep that awareness continuously present, which together constitute what actually guards the senses against being drawn into unwholesome reactions to what they perceive.

Chinese Source

若爾，世尊所說略戒： 身律儀善哉， 善哉語律儀， 意律儀善哉， 善哉遍律儀。 又契經說：應善守護、應善安住眼根律儀。此意根律儀，以何為自性？此二自性非無表色。若爾，是何？頌曰： 正知正念合， 名意根律儀。 論曰：為顯如是二種律儀，俱以正知正念為體，故列名已復說合言，謂意律儀慧念為體，即合二種為根律儀。故離合言顯勿如次。

Ru-Yi Translation

If this is so, then, as the Blessed One has stated in a summary of the precepts: "Excellent is bodily restraint; excellent, too, is verbal restraint; excellent is mental restraint; excellent, too, is pervasive restraint." Moreover, a sūtra states: one should well guard, and should well establish, restraint of the eye faculty [and the other sense faculties]. What is the essential nature of this restraint of the mental faculty [as it applies to the senses]? The essential nature of these two [i.e., what is called "mental restraint" in the summary verse, and this "sense-faculty restraint"] is not unmanifested matter. If this is so, what is it? Verse: clear awareness and mindfulness, combined, are called restraint of the sense faculties. Prose: in order to show that these two kinds of restraint [mental restraint and sense-faculty restraint] both have clear awareness and mindfulness as their essential nature, the verse, having listed the names, further adds the word "combined" — meaning that mental restraint has wisdom [clear awareness] and mindfulness as its essential nature, and it is by combining these two that "restraint of the [sense] faculties" is formed. The word "combined" is stated separately precisely to make clear that one should not assume these correspond one-to-one, in strict order [to the two named factors].

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing clause here is subtle and worth spelling out plainly, since the Chinese states it almost too tersely to parse on a first pass: one might assume, from the verse's phrasing ("clear awareness and mindfulness, combined"), that clear awareness alone constitutes mental restraint while mindfulness alone constitutes sense-faculty restraint (i.e., a simple one-to-one pairing) — but the treatise explicitly blocks this reading, clarifying that both restraints are constituted by the *combination* of both factors together, not by splitting the two factors between the two restraints. We have added the bracketed clarification "[i.e., what is called 'mental restraint'... and this 'sense-faculty restraint']" to make explicit which "two" the passage means, since the referent is only implicit in the Chinese.

This section marks an interesting expansion of what "restraint" (律儀) can mean in this treatise's system: up to this point, restraint has always been a form of karma, whether manifested or unmanifested — but sense-faculty restraint, identified here as simply the joint operation of two ordinary mental factors (wisdom/clear awareness and mindfulness), is not itself a form of karma at all, but rather a state of attentive vigilance that happens to share the functional role of "guarding against wrongdoing" that motivates the term "restraint" in the first place. This is presumably why the treatise felt the need to explicitly rule out unmanifested matter as its essential nature before offering the alternative account.

9. Possessing Restraint Across Past, Present, and Future

The treatise now asks a temporal question: across past, present, and future, exactly when does a person "possess" a given kind of unmanifested karma? A person upholding the vow of individual liberation possesses it continuously in the present for as long as it is unrelinquished; from the second moment onward, that same unmanifested karma also comes to be possessed as something now past (since the earlier instant, though gone, is not simply erased); and, because this kind of restraint is not "meditation-linked" and its causal force is comparatively weak, it is never possessed as something future. Non-restraint (committed wrongdoing) works exactly the same way. Meditation-born restraint, once attained, is possessed continuously as both past and future for as long as it is unrelinquished, since even after losing it in one lifetime, the very first instant of regaining meditative absorption in a later life necessarily reconnects to that same prior attainment. Uncontaminated (path-born) restraint is possessed as both past and future by every noble one without exception, with one difference: at its very first instant of arising, it is necessarily possessed only as future, never as past, since that particular noble path has, by definition, never arisen before that moment. A noble one currently and actually absorbed in meditation or in the path itself possesses the corresponding restraint as present as well — but this "present" possession does not continue once one has emerged from that absorption.

Chinese Source

今應思擇，表及無表誰成就何？齊何時分？且辯成無表律儀不律儀。頌曰： 住別解無表， 未捨恒成現， 剎那後成過， 不律儀亦然。 得靜慮律儀， 恒成就過未， 聖初除過去， 住定道成中。 論曰：住別解脫補特伽羅，未捨以來恒成現世，此別解脫律儀無表。初剎那後亦成過去，前未捨言遍流至後，無散無表有成未來，不隨心色勢微劣故。如說安住別解律儀，住不律儀應知亦爾，謂至未捨惡戒以來恒成現世，惡戒無表初剎那後亦成過去。諸有獲得靜慮律儀，乃至未捨恒成過未，餘生所失過去定律儀，今初剎那必還得彼故。一切聖者無漏律儀，過去未來亦恒成就，有差別者，謂初剎那必成未來非成過去，此類聖道先未起故。若有現住靜慮彼道，如次成現在靜慮道律儀，非出觀時有成現在。

Ru-Yi Translation

We should now carefully consider: who possesses which manifested and unmanifested karma, and up to what point in time? Let us first discuss the possession of unmanifested restraint and non-restraint. Verse: one abiding in the restraint of individual liberation constantly possesses it as present, for as long as it is unrelinquished; from the [second] instant onward, it is also possessed as past; non-restraint is the same. One who has attained meditative restraint constantly possesses it as past and future; a noble one, at the first instant, possesses it as future only, excluding the past; abiding in absorption or in the path, one possesses it as present as well. Prose: a person abiding in the vow of individual liberation constantly possesses this unmanifested restraint of individual liberation as present, for as long as it remains unrelinquished. From the instant after its first arising, it is also possessed as past — the phrase "for as long as it remains unrelinquished" carries through to apply here as well. It is never possessed as future, since it is not a "non-scattered" [meditation-linked] kind of unmanifested karma, and its force is comparatively weak. Just as has been said for one abiding in the restraint of individual liberation, one abiding in non-restraint should be understood the same way — that is, for as long as the bad precept-vow remains unrelinquished, it is constantly possessed as present, and from the instant after its first arising, this unmanifested bad-precept karma is also possessed as past. All those who have attained meditative restraint constantly possess it as past and future, for as long as it is unrelinquished, since, even if it was lost in another lifetime, the present first instant of [regaining meditative absorption] necessarily reconnects to that same prior attainment. All noble ones constantly possess uncontaminated restraint as both past and future; the difference is that, at the very first instant, it is necessarily possessed only as future, not as past, since this particular kind of noble path has not previously arisen. If one is presently and actually abiding in that meditative absorption or that path, one possesses, in due order, the corresponding meditative or path restraint as present as well — but this is not the case once one has emerged from that absorption.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section is establishing a technical Abhidharma doctrine of "possession" (成就, prāpti) across the three times that will recur throughout the rest of this fascicle's classifications, so it is worth being explicit about the underlying picture: a dharma can be "possessed" as present (currently manifesting), as past (having previously arisen and not yet fully relinquished from one's continuum, even though it is no longer actively occurring), or as future (guaranteed or disposed to arise again, given the right conditions) — and different kinds of restraint have different "possession profiles" across these three times depending on their underlying causal structure. The vow of individual liberation, once its founding instant passes into the past, is never possessed as future because — as an earlier section already explained — it belongs to the causally weaker, non-meditative category, unlike meditation-born and path-born restraint, which are tied to a stronger, self-reinforcing causal continuity that guarantees their future recurrence once first attained.

The claim that meditation-born restraint is "constantly possessed as past and future... even if it was lost in another lifetime" is a striking one, and worth drawing out: it means that someone who attained deep meditative absorption in a past life, then was reborn in different circumstances and lost that attainment, does not start from zero if they take up meditation again — the very first instant of regaining absorption is treated as reconnecting to, rather than newly creating, that same underlying capacity, which is why the "future" possession of that restraint is said to hold continuously even through an intervening lifetime in which it is not actively possessed as present at all.

10. The "Neither" Category, and Unmanifested Karma That Crosses Moral Lines

Having covered restraint and non-restraint, the treatise turns to the intermediate "neither" category — actions that are neither restraint nor non-restraint. Such actions do not necessarily generate any unmanifested karma at all, but when they do, that unmanifested karma is possessed only as present at its very first instant, and, from then on, as both past and present (since "neither" karma, unlike the vow of individual liberation, never carries the special weight needed to be possessed as future either). A related and practically important case follows: what happens when someone already holding restraint (or non-restraint) performs an act of the opposite moral quality — a precept-holder who, overcome by a strong affliction, commits an unwholesome deed such as killing or binding another person, or a non-restrained person who, moved by sincere faith, performs a wholesome act such as venerating the Buddha? Either case generates its own additional unmanifested karma, of the opposite moral color to the person's underlying restraint or non-restraint, which continues for as long as the triggering mental state persists — possessed as present only at its first instant, and thereafter as both past and present, exactly like the ordinary "neither" case.

Chinese Source

已辯安住善惡律儀，住中云何？頌曰： 住中有無表， 初成中後二。 論曰：言住中者，謂非律儀非不律儀，彼所起業未必一切皆有無表。若有無表，即是善戒或是惡戒種類所攝，彼初剎那但成現在。然現在世處過未中，故以成中說成現在。初剎那後未捨以來，恒成過現二世無表。若有安住律不律儀，亦有成惡善無表不。設有成就，為經幾時？頌曰： 住律不律儀， 起染淨無表， 初成中後二， 至染淨勢終。 論曰：若住律儀，由勝煩惱作殺縛等諸不善業，由此便發不善無表住不律儀。由淳淨信作禮佛等諸勝善業，由此亦發諸善無表。乃至此二心未斷來，所發無表恒時相續，然其初念唯成現在，自茲以後通成過現。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having discussed abiding in wholesome or unwholesome restraint, what of abiding in "neither"? Verse: for one abiding in "neither," if there is unmanifested karma, its first instant is possessed as present, and thereafter as both. Prose: "abiding in neither" refers to being neither restraint nor non-restraint; the karma that arises from this state does not necessarily always have unmanifested karma. If there is unmanifested karma, it is subsumed under the wholesome or unwholesome precept-type; its first instant is possessed only as present — and, since the present occupies the middle position between the past and future, this is expressed by saying its "middle" instant is possessed as present. From the instant after its first arising, for as long as it remains unrelinquished, it is constantly possessed as both past and present. [Question:] If one abiding in restraint or non-restraint also comes to possess unwholesome or wholesome unmanifested karma [of the opposite quality], for how long is this possessed, granting that it is possessed at all? Verse: for one abiding in restraint or non-restraint, unmanifested karma arising from a defiled or pure mind has its first instant possessed as present, and thereafter as both, until the force of that defiled or pure state comes to an end. Prose: if one abiding in restraint, overcome by a powerful affliction, commits unwholesome deeds such as killing or binding another, this gives rise to unwholesome unmanifested karma [despite one's restraint]. If one abiding in non-restraint, moved by pure and sincere faith, performs superior wholesome deeds such as venerating the Buddha, this likewise gives rise to wholesome unmanifested karma. For as long as these two [respectively defiled or pure] states of mind remain uninterrupted, the unmanifested karma thus generated continues without interruption; yet its very first moment is possessed only as present, and from that point onward, it is possessed as both past and present.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section captures something ethically significant about how this system understands moral character: holding a precept vow or lacking one does not lock a person into a single moral trajectory — a precept-holder can, under the force of strong affliction, generate genuinely unwholesome karma layered on top of their underlying restraint, and an otherwise unrestrained person can, moved by real faith, generate genuinely wholesome karma layered on top of their underlying non-restraint. The two streams (the person's baseline restraint/non-restraint, and this episode-specific opposite-quality karma) run concurrently rather than one overriding or erasing the other — a person's ongoing vow is not "suspended" by a single lapse, nor is an unrestrained person's occasional virtue folded into some formal restraint they never took.

We should note that "possessed as present only at its first instant, and thereafter as both past and present" is a technical possession-pattern this section shares exactly with the intermediate "neither" case described just before it — both are grouped together because both lack the special reinforcing structure (meditative or path-linked continuity) that would let them additionally be possessed as future, unlike meditation-born and path-born restraint discussed in the previous section.

11. Manifested Karma's Own Possession Pattern

Having finished with unmanifested karma, the treatise turns to manifested karma's own possession across the three times. For anyone — whether abiding in restraint, non-restraint, or neither — manifested karma is possessed as present for exactly as long as the bodily or verbal act is actually being carried out; from the instant after that act, for as long as it remains unrelinquished, it is possessed as past; but manifested karma is never possessed as future, following the same reasoning already given for unmanifested karma. Defiled-neutral and undefiled-neutral manifested karma, however, are singled out as a special case: these two are never possessed as past or future at all, only ever as present, since a morally neutral dharma's causal force is inherently weak, and the capacity to obtain something from the past requires a correspondingly strong force. This weakness, the treatise clarifies, belongs to the manifested karma itself rather than to the underlying neutral mind that produces it — an important distinction, since a neutral mind itself can indeed be possessed as past or future, while the manifested karma that mind produces cannot, precisely because manifested karma is inherently duller and more derivative than the mind that gives rise to it, making a neutral act's karma weaker still than the already-weak neutral mind behind it.

Chinese Source

已辯無表，成表云何？頌曰： 表正作成中， 後成過非未， 有覆及無覆， 唯成就現在。 論曰：諸有安住律不律儀及住中者，乃至正作諸表業來恒成現表。初剎那後至未捨來恒成過去，必無成就未來表者，如無表釋。有覆無覆二無記表，定無有能成就過未，法力既劣得力亦微，是故無能逆追成者。此法力劣誰之所為？是心所為。若爾，有覆無記心等勿成過未。此責非理，表昧鈍故、依他起故；心等不然，無記表業從劣心起，其力倍劣彼能起心，故表與心成有差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verse: manifested karma, while actually being carried out, is possessed as present; afterward, it is possessed as past, not as future; defiled-neutral and undefiled-neutral [manifested karma] are possessed only as present. Prose: for all those abiding in restraint, non-restraint, or neither, manifested karma is constantly possessed as present for as long as the manifested act is actually being carried out. From the instant after this, for as long as it remains unrelinquished, it is constantly possessed as past; it is never possessed as future, for the same reason already explained for unmanifested karma. The two kinds of neutral manifested karma — defiled-neutral and undefiled-neutral — definitely cannot be possessed as either past or future, since the dharma's force is weak, and the capacity to obtain [something] retroactively is likewise feeble; hence there is nothing capable of reaching backward or forward to be possessed. [Objection:] To whom does this weakness of the dharma's force belong? [Reply:] It belongs to the mind that produces it. [Objection:] If this is so, then a defiled-neutral mind and the like should likewise fail to be possessed as past or future. [Reply:] This objection is not sound, since manifested karma is dull and inert, and arises dependent on something else, whereas mind and the like are not so; unwholesome-neutral manifested karma arises from an [already] inferior mind, and its force is doubly inferior to that of the mind capable of giving rise to it — hence manifested karma and mind differ in this respect regarding what can be possessed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The key move in this passage's closing exchange is a "doubly inferior" (倍劣) causal chain: an already-weak neutral mind produces an even weaker manifested karma, since matter (which is what manifested karma is) is inherently "dull and inert" (昧鈍) and entirely dependent on the mind that shapes it, rather than being an independently active cause in its own right. This lets the treatise hold two claims that might otherwise seem to be in tension: a neutral mind itself is strong enough to be possessed as past and future (minds, in this system, are generally capable of this), but the bodily or verbal act that neutral mind produces is not, because that act inherits its cause's weakness and then compounds it further through its own dependent, matter-based nature.

We should note this passage completes a pattern that has now appeared twice in this fascicle: both unmanifested and manifested defiled/undefiled-neutral karma are singled out as uniquely unable to be possessed as past or future, tying back to this fascicle's and the previous fascicle's repeated emphasis on neutral mental states' comparative causal weakness (see, for example, fascicle 13's explanation of why unmanifested karma is never morally neutral at all, for the same underlying reason).
