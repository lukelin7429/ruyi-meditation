Restraint, Non-Restraint, and the Vows Between

Fascicle 14 continues Chapter 4 (分別業品第四之二, the Karma-nirdeśa) directly from where fascicle 13 left off. Having spent the previous fascicle establishing what unmanifested karma is and whether it is real at all, the treatise now turns to classifying it: the threefold division into restraint, non-restraint, and neither; the three kinds of restraint (monastic vow, meditation-born, and path-born); and, at length, the structure of the prātimokṣa vows themselves — bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī, śrāmaṇera, upāsaka, and their companions.

1. Three Kinds of Unmanifested Karma, and Three Kinds of Restraint

Picking up directly from fascicle 13's long inquiry into manifested and unmanifested karma, the treatise now classifies unmanifested karma into three basic types: restraint (律儀, saṃvara — karma that holds back wrongdoing), non-restraint (不律儀 — karma that commits one to wrongdoing), and a third category that is neither. Restraint itself earns its name because it is able to block and extinguish the continuity of bad conduct. The treatise then distinguishes three kinds of restraint by their source: the vow of individual liberation (別解脫律儀, prātimokṣa-saṃvara), tied to the desire realm's precepts; meditation-born restraint (靜慮生律儀), tied to the form realm's precepts, arising automatically alongside meditative absorption; and path-born restraint (道生律儀), the uncontaminated restraint that arises together with the noble path itself.

Chinese Source

傍論已了，復應辯前表無表相。頌曰： 無表三律儀， 不律儀非二。 論曰：此中無表，略說有三：一者律儀、二不律儀、三者非二謂非律儀非不律儀。能遮能滅惡戒相續故名律儀。如是律儀差別有幾？頌曰： 律儀別解脫， 靜慮及道生。 論曰：律儀差別，略有三種：一別解脫律儀，謂欲纏戒；二靜慮生律儀，謂色纏戒；三道生律儀，謂無漏戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

The digression having now concluded, we should further explain the characteristics of manifested and unmanifested karma discussed earlier. Verse: unmanifested karma is restraint, non-restraint, or neither. Prose: unmanifested karma, briefly stated, is of three kinds: first, restraint; second, non-restraint; third, neither of the two, meaning neither restraint nor non-restraint. It is called restraint because it is able to block and extinguish the continuity of bad conduct. How many distinctions does this restraint itself have? Verse: restraint is the vow of individual liberation, and that born of meditative absorption and of the [noble] path. Prose: restraint, briefly stated, is of three kinds: first, the restraint of individual liberation, meaning precepts bound to the desire realm; second, restraint born of meditative absorption, meaning precepts bound to the form realm; third, restraint born of the [noble] path, meaning uncontaminated precepts.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section's opening line — "the digression having now concluded" — is the treatise's own acknowledgment that the entire previous fascicle's debate over whether unmanifested karma is real at all was itself a lengthy aside, prompted by the original question of what bodily manifestation is; the systematic classification promised back near the start of that debate resumes only now. Readers coming from fascicle 13 should understand this fascicle as continuing that same unresolved metaphysical backdrop: everything said here about unmanifested karma's threefold division and the three kinds of restraint is stated primarily from the Vaibhāṣika's own framework, without re-litigating the Sautrāntika's skepticism about whether any of this "unmanifested matter" exists as a separate substance in the first place.

The three kinds of restraint map cleanly onto this treatise's three-realm cosmology: the vow of individual liberation belongs to ordinary monastic and lay precepts taken by beings in the desire realm; meditation-born restraint arises automatically, without a separate ceremony, the moment one actually enters meditative absorption associated with the form realm; and path-born restraint arises automatically together with uncontaminated, liberating insight itself. The bulk of this fascicle, as its opening promises, will focus on the first of these three — the vow of individual liberation — in far greater structural detail than the other two.

2. Eight Named Vows, Four Real Substances

The vow of individual liberation carries eight traditional names — bhikṣu (fully ordained monk), bhikṣuṇī (fully ordained nun), śikṣamāṇā (probationary nun), śrāmaṇera (novice monk), śrāmaṇerī (novice nun), upāsaka (layman), upāsikā (laywoman), and upavāsa (one-day precept-holder) — but only four genuinely distinct underlying substances, since a bhikṣu and bhikṣuṇī vow, a śrāmaṇera/śikṣamāṇā and śrāmaṇerī vow, and an upāsaka and upāsikā vow are each really the same vow, differing only by the practitioner's sex. The proof offered is a striking one: should a person's sex organs change — a recognized possibility in this cosmology — their vow's underlying substance is never actually relinquished and reacquired, only renamed, showing that the difference between the paired vows was never a difference in substance at all, only in the label attached according to the practitioner's bodily form at the time.

Chinese Source

初律儀相差別云何？頌曰： 初律儀八種， 實體唯有四， 形轉名異故， 各別不相違。 論曰：別解脫律儀，相差別有八：一苾芻律儀、二苾芻尼律儀、三正學律儀、四勤策律儀、五勤策女律儀、六近事律儀、七近事女律儀、八近住律儀。如是八種律儀相差別，總名第一別解脫律儀。雖有八名，實體唯四：一苾芻律儀、二勤策律儀、三近事律儀、四近住律儀。唯此四種別解律儀皆有體實，相各別故。所以者何？離苾芻律儀，無別苾芻尼律儀；離勤策律儀，無別正學、勤策女律儀；離近事律儀，無別近事女律儀。云何知然？由形改轉，體雖無捨得，而名有異故。形謂形相，即男女根。由此二根男女形別，但由形轉，令諸律儀名為苾芻、苾芻尼等，謂轉根位，令本苾芻律儀名苾芻尼律儀，或苾芻尼律儀名苾芻律儀；令本勤策律儀名勤策女律儀，或勤策女律儀及正學律儀名勤策律儀；令本近事律儀名近事女律儀，或近事女律儀名近事律儀。非轉根位有捨先得得先未得律儀因緣，故四律儀非異三體。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the distinctions among the characteristics of the first kind of restraint [the vow of individual liberation]? Verse: the first restraint has eight characteristics, but its real substance is only four; because the bodily form changes, the names differ, and there is no contradiction in each being distinct. Prose: the vow of individual liberation, in terms of its distinguishing characteristics, is of eight kinds: first, the bhikṣu vow; second, the bhikṣuṇī vow; third, the śikṣamāṇā vow; fourth, the śrāmaṇera vow; fifth, the śrāmaṇerī vow; sixth, the upāsaka vow; seventh, the upāsikā vow; eighth, the upavāsa vow. These eight kinds of restraint, distinguished by their characteristics, are collectively named the first kind, the vow of individual liberation. Although there are eight names, the real substance is only four: first, the bhikṣu vow; second, the śrāmaṇera vow; third, the upāsaka vow; fourth, the upavāsa vow. Only these four vows of individual liberation each have a real substance, since their characteristics are each genuinely distinct. Why is this so? Apart from the bhikṣu vow, there is no separate bhikṣuṇī vow; apart from the śrāmaṇera vow, there is no separate śikṣamāṇā or śrāmaṇerī vow; apart from the upāsaka vow, there is no separate upāsikā vow. How is this known? Because, when the bodily form changes, although the substance [of the vow] is neither relinquished nor newly acquired, its name nevertheless differs. "Form" here means bodily form, namely the male or female organ. Because of these two organs, male and female bodily forms differ, and it is solely by virtue of a change in bodily form that these restraints come to be named bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī, and so on — that is, at the moment the faculty changes, what was originally the bhikṣu vow comes to be named the bhikṣuṇī vow, or the bhikṣuṇī vow comes to be named the bhikṣu vow; what was originally the śrāmaṇera vow comes to be named the śrāmaṇerī vow, or the śrāmaṇerī vow and the śikṣamāṇā vow come to be named the śrāmaṇera vow; what was originally the upāsaka vow comes to be named the upāsikā vow, or the upāsikā vow comes to be named the upāsaka vow. Apart from the occasion of the faculty changing, there is no cause or condition for relinquishing what was previously obtained or obtaining what was not previously obtained — hence the four restraints are not different from these three pairs' substance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sex-change argument here is a genuinely distinctive piece of Abhidharma reasoning, and worth being explicit about: this treatise's broader cosmology treats a person's sex as a physical faculty (根) that can, in certain circumstances, change during a single lifetime, and the argument exploits this directly — since the underlying vow-substance persists continuously through such a change, with only its label switching to match the new bodily form, this is offered as proof that the vow's substance was never sex-specific to begin with, only conventionally renamed according to whichever sex the vow-holder currently has. We have translated 苾芻/苾芻尼 as "bhikṣu"/"bhikṣuṇī" and 正學 as "śikṣamāṇā" (the intermediate probationary ordination a novice nun undertakes before full ordination) rather than using looser English equivalents, since these are established technical terms in Buddhist monastic law with no single-word English equivalent that fully captures their specific status.

We should flag that this passage's logic works specifically for the paired vows it names (bhikṣu/bhikṣuṇī, śrāmaṇera-śikṣamāṇā/śrāmaṇerī, upāsaka/upāsikā) and does not extend to the fourth vow, upavāsa (the one-day lay precept), which has no male/female pairing at all in the traditional eightfold list — it stands as its own, singular vow-type from the start, which is presumably why the treatise's arithmetic ends at four real substances rather than needing to explain away a fifth.

3. Three Vows Held at Once, Never in Conflict

A practitioner typically progresses through the vow of individual liberation in stages — first taking the upāsaka vow, then the śrāmaṇera vow, then the full bhikṣu vow — and the treatise asks whether these three should really count as three separate substances, or as one substance simply expanded by degrees. It settles on three genuinely separate substances, illustrated by an analogy to coinage: much as single and double gold coins, and pieces of fifty or twenty [smaller denomination], are each their own distinct substance even though a larger one is obtained after a smaller one, so too do the three restraints each arise in full completeness, without mixing into one another. Each level shares the same basic precepts (all three include abstaining from killing, up through abstaining from alcohol), yet they differ by degree of scope — the same underlying precept becomes progressively more comprehensive as one advances to more numerous and more demanding trainings, cutting off "occasions of heedlessness" (the circumstances that give rise to wrongdoing) ever more broadly. Because the three vows are, in this sense, genuinely distinct, relinquishing the highest one (the bhikṣu vow) does not cause the lower two to be relinquished as well, even though the higher vow is typically taken while the lower ones are still in force — all three coexist simultaneously within a single person, without a later vow ever displacing an earlier one.

Chinese Source

若從近事律儀受勤策律儀，復從勤策律儀受苾芻律儀，此三律儀為由增足遠離方便立別別名，如隻雙金錢及五十二十，為體各別具足頓生。三種律儀體不相雜，其相各別具足頓生。三律儀中具三離殺，乃至具足三離飲酒，餘數多少隨其所應。既爾相望同類何別？由因緣別，相望有異。其事云何？如如求受多種學處，如是如是能離多種憍逸處時，即離眾多殺等緣起。以諸遠離依因緣發，故因緣別遠離有異。若無此事，捨苾芻律儀，爾時則應三律儀皆捨，前二攝在後一中故。既不許然，故三各別。然此三種互不相違，於一身中俱時而轉，非由受後捨前律儀，勿捨苾芻戒便非近事等。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one takes the śrāmaṇera vow from the upāsaka vow, and then takes the bhikṣu vow from the śrāmaṇera vow, are these three restraints given separate names merely by way of an expanded means of renunciation [i.e., the same substance simply enlarged], or [are they genuinely distinct substances, comparable] to a single gold coin and a double gold coin, and to pieces of fifty and of twenty, each having its own distinct substance and each arising in full completeness [rather than one being merely an expansion of the other]? [Reply:] The substances of the three kinds of restraint are not mixed together; each has its own distinct characteristic and arises in full completeness. Among the three restraints, each is complete with the abstention from killing, and so on up through complete abstention from drinking alcohol; the remaining number [of precepts each includes] varies as is appropriate to each. [Objection:] Given this, how, then, do these restraints differ from one another when compared as belonging to the same general category? [Reply:] They differ from one another because of a difference in their causes and conditions. [Objection:] How is this so? [Reply:] Just as, the more numerous kinds of training one seeks to undertake, the more numerous the occasions of heedlessness one is thereby able to renounce, one thereby cuts off correspondingly more numerous occasions giving rise to killing and the like. Since each renunciation arises depending on its own causes and conditions, and these causes and conditions differ, the renunciations themselves differ. If this were not the case, then, at the moment one relinquished the bhikṣu vow, all three restraints would have to be relinquished together, since the first two would be subsumed within the last. But since this is not accepted to be so, the three are each distinct. Yet these three kinds are mutually non-conflicting, operating together simultaneously within a single person — it is not the case that taking a later vow causes an earlier restraint to be relinquished, or else relinquishing the bhikṣu precepts would mean one was no longer even an upāsaka and so on.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The coin analogy is doing careful work distinguishing two models that might otherwise look identical from the outside: on one model, taking the śrāmaṇera vow after the upāsaka vow would just be "the same restraint, now bigger" — like adding more water to the same cup; on the treatise's actual model, it is "a wholly new, complete restraint, arising alongside the still-intact earlier one" — like acquiring a second, larger coin without losing the first. The practical payoff, spelled out in the closing lines, is significant for monastic law: if a monk's higher ordination is later invalidated or relinquished, this does not strip away the more basic upāsaka and śrāmaṇera-level restraints underneath it, since those were never actually absorbed into or replaced by the bhikṣu vow in the first place — they were always separate, coexisting substances.

We should note that the "same precepts, different scope" account given here (each level shares the same core prohibitions — killing, stealing, and so on — but differs in how many total training-rules and heedlessness-occasions it covers) is somewhat compressed in the source, and later in this fascicle the treatise will spell out the exact precept-counts for each of the four levels (five, eight, ten, and the full monastic code) in much more explicit detail — this section's claim that "the remaining number varies as is appropriate to each" is essentially a forward pointer to that fuller breakdown.

4. The Precise Precepts Behind Each of the Four Levels

The treatise now names the exact precepts underlying each of the four levels of restraint. The upāsaka (lay follower) vow consists of abstaining from five things: killing, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, false speech, and drinking alcohol. The upavāsa (one-day precept-holder) vow expands this to eight: killing, taking what is not given, all sexual activity (a stricter standard than the upāsaka's "misconduct" alone), false speech, alcohol, adorning oneself with scent and garlands and attending song and dance, sleeping or sitting on high and luxurious beds, and eating at improper times. The śrāmaṇera (novice) vow expands to ten by splitting the sixth upavāsa precept into two separate rules and adding a tenth: handling gold, silver, and other valuables. The bhikṣu (fully ordained monk) vow, finally, consists of abstaining from every bodily and verbal act that ought to be abstained from — the complete monastic code.

Chinese Source

近事、近住、勤策、苾芻四種律儀云何安立？頌曰： 受離五八十， 一切所應離， 立近事近住， 勤策及苾芻。 論曰：應知此中如數次第，依四遠離立四律儀。謂受離五所應遠離，安立第一近事律儀。何等名為五所應離？一者殺生、二不與取、三欲邪行、四虛誑語、五飲諸酒。若受離八所應遠離，安立第二近住律儀。何等名為八所應離？一者殺生、二不與取、三非梵行、四虛誑語、五飲諸酒、六塗飾香鬘舞歌觀聽、七眠坐高廣嚴麗床座、八食非時食。若受離十所應遠離，安立第三勤策律儀。何等名為十所應離？謂於前八塗飾香鬘舞歌觀聽開為二種，復加受畜金銀等寶以為第十。若受離一切應離身語業，安立第四苾芻律儀。

Ru-Yi Translation

How are the four kinds of restraint — upāsaka, upavāsa, śrāmaṇera, and bhikṣu — established? Verse: undertaking the abstention from five, eight, ten, or all that should be abstained from establishes the upāsaka, upavāsa, śrāmaṇera, and bhikṣu [restraints, respectively]. Prose: one should understand that, in this matter, the four restraints are established, in numerical order, based on four kinds of renunciation. That is, undertaking the abstention from the five things that should be abstained from establishes the first, the upāsaka restraint. What are the five things that should be abstained from? First, killing living beings; second, taking what is not given; third, sexual misconduct; fourth, false speech; fifth, drinking alcohol. If one undertakes the abstention from the eight things that should be abstained from, this establishes the second, the upavāsa restraint. What are the eight things that should be abstained from? First, killing living beings; second, taking what is not given; third, unchaste conduct; fourth, false speech; fifth, drinking alcohol; sixth, adorning oneself with scent and garlands, and dancing, singing, and watching or listening [to such performances]; seventh, sleeping or sitting upon a high, broad, or ornately adorned bed or seat; eighth, eating food at an improper time. If one undertakes the abstention from the ten things that should be abstained from, this establishes the third, the śrāmaṇera restraint. What are the ten things that should be abstained from? It is [the same eight as above, except that] the sixth item — adorning oneself with scent and garlands, and dancing, singing, and watching or listening — is divided into two separate items, and, further, accepting and keeping gold, silver, and other such valuables is added as the tenth. If one undertakes the abstention from every bodily and verbal act that ought to be abstained from, this establishes the fourth, the bhikṣu restraint.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

We want to flag one precise but easy-to-miss distinction the Chinese draws between the third precept at the upāsaka level and the corresponding precept at the upavāsa and higher levels: the upāsaka vow's 欲邪行 specifically targets sexual *misconduct* (i.e., certain kinds of sexual activity, appropriate to a layperson's ordinary household life), whereas the upavāsa and higher vows' 非梵行 targets "unchaste conduct" more broadly — effectively all sexual activity without exception — reflecting the more renunciate, temporary-monastic character of the upavāsa vow and above. We have deliberately used different English phrasing ("sexual misconduct" vs. "unchaste conduct") to preserve this distinction, since collapsing both into a single English phrase would erase a real difference in scope between the lay and monastic-adjacent precepts.

The śrāmaṇera vow's arithmetic is worth spelling out plainly, since the Chinese states it tersely: it does not add two entirely new precepts to the upavāsa's eight to reach ten, but rather splits one existing precept (the sixth, covering both self-adornment and entertainment-watching) into two separate precepts, and then adds only one genuinely new rule (avoiding money and valuables) — meaning the śrāmaṇera's ten precepts share nine-tenths of their actual content with the upavāsa's eight, differing mainly in the added prohibition against handling gold and silver, which marks the transition from a temporary lay observance to an ongoing monastic identity with no personal property.

5. Why Only the First Instant of a Vow Is Called "Prātimokṣa"

The treatise now works through several names applied to the vow of individual liberation, unpacking each one's etymology. It is called śīla ("moral discipline") because it levels out the dangerous terrain of bad karma — glossed, in a verse, as being "cool," since one who upholds this precept-happiness experiences no bodily distress. It is called sadācāra ("wholesome conduct") because the wise praise it. It is called karma because it is a self-standing, completed act — and, addressing an apparent puzzle, the treatise clarifies that even unmanifested karma, though sometimes called "non-doing," still counts as a completed act, since a person with a sense of shame relies on unmanifested karma's power specifically in order to refrain from committing evil, which is itself a kind of doing. It is called restraint because it guards against wrongdoing in body and speech. Most strikingly, the treatise holds that the names "prātimokṣa" (individual liberation) and "karma-path" apply, strictly speaking, only to the very first instant of taking a vow — the moment various evils are each individually renounced for the first time and the karmic act reaches its completion — while every moment after that first one, though the vow continues unbroken, is technically named only "restraint of individual liberation," not "prātimokṣa" itself, and "what arises afterward," not "karma-path."

Chinese Source

別解律儀名差別者，頌曰： 俱得名尸羅， 妙行業律儀， 唯初表無表， 名別解業道。 論曰：能平險業故名尸羅。訓釋詞者，謂清涼故。如伽他言：受持戒樂，身無熱惱，故名尸羅。智者稱揚，故名妙行。所作自體，故名為業。豈不無表亦名不作，如何今說所作自體？有慚恥者受無表力不造眾惡，故名不作。表思所造，得所作名。有餘釋言：是作因故、是作果故，名作無失。能防身語，故名律儀。如是應知，別解脫戒通初後位無差別名，唯初剎那表及無表，得別解脫及業道名。謂受戒時初表無表，別別棄捨種種惡故。依初別捨義，立別解脫名。即於爾時所作究竟，依業暢義立業道名，故初剎那名別解脫，亦得名曰別解律儀，亦得名為根本業道。從第二念乃至未捨，不名別解脫，名別解律儀；不名業道，名為後起。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the distinctions among the names for the restraint of individual liberation, verse: all of these are called śīla, wholesome conduct, karma, and restraint; only the first manifested and unmanifested karma is called individual liberation and karma-path. Prose: it is called śīla because it is able to level out dangerous karma. As for its etymological gloss, it means "cool" — as a verse states: "One who upholds precept-happiness has a body free of feverish distress; hence it is called śīla." It is called wholesome conduct because the wise praise it. It is called karma because it is a self-standing, completed act. [Objection:] But is unmanifested karma not also called "non-doing"? How, then, can it now be said to be a self-standing, completed act? [Reply:] One possessing a sense of shame relies on the power of unmanifested karma to refrain from committing many evils; hence it is called "non-doing" [in that specific sense]. What manifested [karma], produced by volition, [directly does] is what earns the name "doing." Other explanations say: it is because it serves as the cause of an act, and as the result of an act, that the name "act" involves no error [when applied to it]. It is called restraint because it is able to guard against bodily and verbal [wrongdoing]. One should understand it in this way: the precept of individual liberation, spanning both its initial and later stages, carries no distinguishing name [between these stages] in most respects — it is only the very first instant's manifested and unmanifested karma that earns the names "individual liberation" and "karma-path." That is, at the time of receiving the precept, the initial manifested and unmanifested karma individually and distinctly renounces various evils; based on this meaning of an initial, distinct renunciation, the name "individual liberation" (prātimokṣa) is established. And it is at that very moment that the karmic act reaches its completion; based on this meaning of the act's fulfillment, the name "karma-path" is established. Hence the first instant is named "individual liberation," and may also be named "restraint of individual liberation," and may also be named "root karma-path." From the second moment onward, until the vow is relinquished, it is not called "individual liberation," but is called "restraint of individual liberation"; it is not called "karma-path," but is called "what arises afterward."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a fine but genuinely significant technical distinction, and it is easy to read past it: "prātimokṣa" (個別解脫/別解脫, "individual liberation," so named because each precept individually liberates the holder from a specific kind of wrongdoing) and "karma-path" (業道) name only the singular founding instant of taking a vow — the dramatic moment various evils are renounced all at once and the karmic act is completed — while the entire subsequent stretch of a monastic's or layperson's life spent actually upholding that vow is given the humbler names "restraint of individual liberation" and "what arises afterward." The distinction is not merely terminological trivia: it reflects a substantive Abhidharma view that the vow's founding act and its ongoing maintenance are, in some sense, different in kind, even though they share one uninterrupted causal continuity — the founding moment does something (an active renunciation) that its later maintenance, strictly speaking, no longer does in the same way.

The "cool" etymology for śīla is a nice, concrete illustration of how Sanskrit Buddhist vocabulary was explained by its Chinese translators and commentators using folk-etymological glosses rather than strict historical linguistics — the actual Sanskrit śīla is not linguistically related to any word for "coolness," but the gloss captures an experiential point the tradition wanted to make: that moral discipline brings a felt ease and freedom from remorse's "heat," which is presented here as the reason for calling it what it is called, regardless of the term's actual etymological history.

6. Who Possesses Which Kind of Restraint

The treatise now asks who is actually capable of possessing each of the three kinds of restraint. The vow of individual liberation belongs to the eight assemblies — from the bhikṣu down to the upavāsa precept-holder — and to no one outside this circle: non-Buddhist ascetics do take their own vows, but these are never called "restraint of individual liberation," since their vows lack the capacity to permanently free the holder from evils they remain attached to. Meditation-born restraint belongs to anyone who has actually attained meditative absorption, including its neighboring, preliminary stages (extending the name "meditative absorption" to its borders the way a village's name is extended to its surrounding rice fields). Path-born restraint belongs only to noble ones (āryas) — those in either the stage of training or the stage beyond training. Of these three, only meditation-born and path-born restraint are said to "follow the mind" moment by moment, continuing even through different or absent states of mind, unlike the vow of individual liberation.

Chinese Source

The village-and-rice-paddies analogy is explaining a genuinely useful piece of Abhidharma terminology-extension: rather than requiring a separate technical term for the preliminary, "not-yet-arrived" (未至, anāgamya) stage that precedes full meditative absorption proper, the tradition simply extends the word "meditative absorption" itself to cover this neighboring stage too, the way a village's name naturally extends to the fields surrounding it without needing a new word for "near-the-village." This lets the treatise say simply that "meditation-born restraint" is possessed by "those who have attained meditative absorption" without having to separately specify that the preliminary access-concentration stage counts as well.

The distinction between restraints that "follow the mind" and the vow of individual liberation, which does not, is worth flagging since it will matter for the material still ahead: meditation-born and path-born restraint are tied moment-by-moment to an underlying meditative or noble state of mind and persist automatically as long as that state persists (even through a differently-occupied or temporarily absent mind, in certain technical senses), whereas the vow of individual liberation, once taken, continues as its own independent unmanifested karma stream regardless of what the mind is currently doing — a difference in how each kind of restraint is causally sustained, not merely a difference in name.

7. "Severing Restraint": A Fourfold Classification

Meditation-born and uncontaminated restraint are also given a further name: "severing restraint" (斷律儀), so called because they are capable of permanently cutting off the desire realm's bad conduct and the afflictions that give rise to it. The treatise specifies exactly when this name applies: at the nine uninterrupted paths (無間道) occurring within the preliminary access-concentration stage (未至定), the meditative and uncontaminated restraint that arises together with them earns the name "severing restraint," since it is precisely at these moments that afflictions are being actively cut off. This means some instances of meditative restraint are "severing restraint" and some are not, and the treatise works this out as a fourfold classification: ordinary meditative restraint outside these nine specific moments (severing but not at those moments); uncontaminated restraint specifically at these nine moments (severing restraint proper); contaminated restraint specifically at these nine moments (this case, though occurring at the right moment, is not itself doing the severing); and all remaining uncontaminated restraint outside these nine moments (uncontaminated, but not severing). A parallel fourfold classification is offered for uncontaminated restraint that is not "severing restraint" as well.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "nine uninterrupted paths" (九無間道) refer to a standard Abhidharma framework in which the process of cutting off afflictions tied to any given meditative level is divided into nine successive degrees of intensity, each paired with an "uninterrupted path" (the moment of actively severing that degree of affliction) and a "liberation path" (the moment immediately after, when the severing is confirmed complete) — this ninefold division recurs throughout Abhidharma soteriology wherever affliction-elimination is being tracked with precision, and this passage is applying it specifically to the preliminary access-concentration stage that precedes full absorption in the first dhyāna.

The four-case classification here is a standard Abhidharma logical device (catuṣkoṭi-style fourfold analysis) for precisely mapping which members of one category (meditative restraint) fall inside versus outside another category (severing restraint) — worth noting because this same four-case structure will recur through the rest of this fascicle's technical material. We have kept the translation of each case as literal and parallel in structure as the Chinese itself, since the whole point of this device is the systematic exhaustiveness of the four combinations, and any paraphrase risks obscuring which logical slot each case is meant to fill.

8. The Buddha's Fourfold Restraint Verse, and What Guards the Sense Faculties

The treatise now addresses a verse of the Buddha's own summarizing restraint into four kinds: bodily restraint, verbal restraint, mental restraint, and "pervasive" restraint (a comprehensive restraint covering all three together). This raises a question, since a separate sūtra passage speaks of "restraint of the eye faculty" and the other sense faculties, urging that they be well guarded and well established — what, then, is the essential nature of this "mental root-faculty restraint"? The treatise rules out identifying it with unmanifested matter, since restraining the eye and the other sense faculties is not itself a bodily or verbal act with a physical basis. Instead, it identifies this restraint with the combined operation of two mental factors acting together: clear awareness (正知, saṃprajanya) and mindfulness (正念, smṛti) — the coordinated capacity to know clearly what one is perceiving and to keep that awareness continuously present, which together constitute what actually guards the senses against being drawn into unwholesome reactions to what they perceive.

Prose: in order to show that these two kinds of restraint [mental restraint and sense-faculty restraint] both have clear awareness and mindfulness as their essential nature, the verse, having listed the names, further adds the word "combined" — meaning that mental restraint has wisdom [clear awareness] and mindfulness as its essential nature, and it is by combining these two that "restraint of the [sense] faculties" is formed. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing clause here is subtle and worth spelling out plainly, since the Chinese states it almost too tersely to parse on a first pass: one might assume, from the verse's phrasing ("clear awareness and mindfulness, combined"), that clear awareness alone constitutes mental restraint while mindfulness alone constitutes sense-faculty restraint (i.e., a simple one-to-one pairing) — but the treatise explicitly blocks this reading, clarifying that both restraints are constituted by the *combination* of both factors together, not by splitting the two factors between the two restraints. We have added the bracketed clarification "[i.e., what is called 'mental restraint'... and this 'sense-faculty restraint']" to make explicit which "two" the passage means, since the referent is only implicit in the Chinese.

This section marks an interesting expansion of what "restraint" (律儀) can mean in this treatise's system: up to this point, restraint has always been a form of karma, whether manifested or unmanifested — but sense-faculty restraint, identified here as simply the joint operation of two ordinary mental factors (wisdom/clear awareness and mindfulness), is not itself a form of karma at all, but rather a state of attentive vigilance that happens to share the functional role of "guarding against wrongdoing" that motivates the term "restraint" in the first place. This is presumably why the treatise felt the need to explicitly rule out unmanifested matter as its essential nature before offering the alternative account.

9. Possessing Restraint Across Past, Present, and Future

The treatise now asks a temporal question: across past, present, and future, exactly when does a person "possess" a given kind of unmanifested karma? A person upholding the vow of individual liberation possesses it continuously in the present for as long as it is unrelinquished; from the second moment onward, that same unmanifested karma also comes to be possessed as something now past (since the earlier instant, though gone, is not simply erased); and, because this kind of restraint is not "meditation-linked" and its causal force is comparatively weak, it is never possessed as something future. Non-restraint (committed wrongdoing) works exactly the same way. Meditation-born restraint, once attained, is possessed continuously as both past and future for as long as it is unrelinquished, since even after losing it in one lifetime, the very first instant of regaining meditative absorption in a later life necessarily reconnects to that same prior attainment. Uncontaminated (path-born) restraint is possessed as both past and future by every noble one without exception, with one difference: at its very first instant of arising, it is necessarily possessed only as future, never as past, since that particular noble path has, by definition, never arisen before that moment. A noble one currently and actually absorbed in meditation or in the path itself possesses the corresponding restraint as present as well — but this "present" possession does not continue once one has emerged from that absorption.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section is establishing a technical Abhidharma doctrine of "possession" (成就, prāpti) across the three times that will recur throughout the rest of this fascicle's classifications, so it is worth being explicit about the underlying picture: a dharma can be "possessed" as present (currently manifesting), as past (having previously arisen and not yet fully relinquished from one's continuum, even though it is no longer actively occurring), or as future (guaranteed or disposed to arise again, given the right conditions) — and different kinds of restraint have different "possession profiles" across these three times depending on their underlying causal structure. The vow of individual liberation, once its founding instant passes into the past, is never possessed as future because — as an earlier section already explained — it belongs to the causally weaker, non-meditative category, unlike meditation-born and path-born restraint, which are tied to a stronger, self-reinforcing causal continuity that guarantees their future recurrence once first attained.

The claim that meditation-born restraint is "constantly possessed as past and future... even if it was lost in another lifetime" is a striking one, and worth drawing out: it means that someone who attained deep meditative absorption in a past life, then was reborn in different circumstances and lost that attainment, does not start from zero if they take up meditation again — the very first instant of regaining absorption is treated as reconnecting to, rather than newly creating, that same underlying capacity, which is why the "future" possession of that restraint is said to hold continuously even through an intervening lifetime in which it is not actively possessed as present at all.

10. The "Neither" Category, and Unmanifested Karma That Crosses Moral Lines

Having covered restraint and non-restraint, the treatise turns to the intermediate "neither" category — actions that are neither restraint nor non-restraint. Such actions do not necessarily generate any unmanifested karma at all, but when they do, that unmanifested karma is possessed only as present at its very first instant, and, from then on, as both past and present (since "neither" karma, unlike the vow of individual liberation, never carries the special weight needed to be possessed as future either). A related and practically important case follows: what happens when someone already holding restraint (or non-restraint) performs an act of the opposite moral quality — a precept-holder who, overcome by a strong affliction, commits an unwholesome deed such as killing or binding another person, or a non-restrained person who, moved by sincere faith, performs a wholesome act such as venerating the Buddha? Either case generates its own additional unmanifested karma, of the opposite moral color to the person's underlying restraint or non-restraint, which continues for as long as the triggering mental state persists — possessed as present only at its first instant, and thereafter as both past and present, exactly like the ordinary "neither" case.

This section captures something ethically significant about how this system understands moral character: holding a precept vow or lacking one does not lock a person into a single moral trajectory — a precept-holder can, under the force of strong affliction, generate genuinely unwholesome karma layered on top of their underlying restraint, and an otherwise unrestrained person can, moved by real faith, generate genuinely wholesome karma layered on top of their underlying non-restraint. The two streams (the person's baseline restraint/non-restraint, and this episode-specific opposite-quality karma) run concurrently rather than one overriding or erasing the other — a person's ongoing vow is not "suspended" by a single lapse, nor is an unrestrained person's occasional virtue folded into some formal restraint they never took.

We should note that "possessed as present only at its first instant, and thereafter as both past and present" is a technical possession-pattern this section shares exactly with the intermediate "neither" case described just before it — both are grouped together because both lack the special reinforcing structure (meditative or path-linked continuity) that would let them additionally be possessed as future, unlike meditation-born and path-born restraint discussed in the previous section.

11. Manifested Karma's Own Possession Pattern

Having finished with unmanifested karma, the treatise turns to manifested karma's own possession across the three times. For anyone — whether abiding in restraint, non-restraint, or neither — manifested karma is possessed as present for exactly as long as the bodily or verbal act is actually being carried out; from the instant after that act, for as long as it remains unrelinquished, it is possessed as past; but manifested karma is never possessed as future, following the same reasoning already given for unmanifested karma. Defiled-neutral and undefiled-neutral manifested karma, however, are singled out as a special case: these two are never possessed as past or future at all, only ever as present, since a morally neutral dharma's causal force is inherently weak, and the capacity to obtain something from the past requires a correspondingly strong force. This weakness, the treatise clarifies, belongs to the manifested karma itself rather than to the underlying neutral mind that produces it — an important distinction, since a neutral mind itself can indeed be possessed as past or future, while the manifested karma that mind produces cannot, precisely because manifested karma is inherently duller and more derivative than the mind that gives rise to it, making a neutral act's karma weaker still than the already-weak neutral mind behind it.

[Objection:] If this is so, then a defiled-neutral mind and the like should likewise fail to be possessed as past or future. [Reply:] This objection is not sound, since manifested karma is dull and inert, and arises dependent on something else, whereas mind and the like are not so; unwholesome-neutral manifested karma arises from an [already] inferior mind, and its force is doubly inferior to that of the mind capable of giving rise to it — hence manifested karma and mind differ in this respect regarding what can be possessed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The key move in this passage's closing exchange is a "doubly inferior" (倍劣) causal chain: an already-weak neutral mind produces an even weaker manifested karma, since matter (which is what manifested karma is) is inherently "dull and inert" (昧鈍) and entirely dependent on the mind that shapes it, rather than being an independently active cause in its own right. This lets the treatise hold two claims that might otherwise seem to be in tension: a neutral mind itself is strong enough to be possessed as past and future (minds, in this system, are generally capable of this), but the bodily or verbal act that neutral mind produces is not, because that act inherits its cause's weakness and then compounds it further through its own dependent, matter-based nature.

We should note this passage completes a pattern that has now appeared twice in this fascicle: both unmanifested and manifested defiled/undefiled-neutral karma are singled out as uniquely unable to be possessed as past or future, tying back to this fascicle's and the previous fascicle's repeated emphasis on neutral mental states' comparative causal weakness (see, for example, fascicle 13's explanation of why unmanifested karma is never morally neutral at all, for the same underlying reason).

12. The Five Names for Non-Restraint

Mirroring the naming scheme already given for the vow of individual liberation, the treatise now gives non-restraint its own five names, each with its own etymology. It is called "bad conduct" because the wise despise and reject it, and because its result is undesirable. It is called "bad precept-vow" because it obstructs pure śīla. It is called "karma" because it is produced through body and speech. It is called "karma-path" because it is subsumed under the root [of the karmic act]. And it is called "non-restraint" because it fails to guard body and speech from wrongdoing. As with the vow of individual liberation, the name "karma-path" applies strictly only to the very first instant of committing to a course of bad conduct, while the other four names extend across both the initial and every later moment for as long as the bad vow continues.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This naming scheme is a precise mirror image of the vow of individual liberation's five names from earlier in this fascicle (śīla, wholesome conduct, karma, restraint, and the special "prātimokṣa"/"karma-path" reserved for the first instant) — down to the same underlying structural distinction, where one name ("karma-path" on both sides) is reserved specifically for the founding instant of commitment, while the other names extend across the entire ongoing duration of the vow or anti-vow. This structural symmetry is itself instructive: the treatise treats wholesome and unwholesome commitment as governed by exactly parallel logic, differing only in moral content, not in their underlying temporal or causal architecture.

13. Who Has Manifested Karma Without Unmanifested, or Unmanifested Without Manifested

The treatise now works through a fourfold classification of exactly how manifested and unmanifested karma can occur separately. Someone abiding in neither restraint nor non-restraint who performs a weak, feeble act of wholesome or unwholesome volition possesses manifested karma but no corresponding unmanifested karma at all, since such a weak intention only barely manages to produce the manifested act itself, let alone anything further — and this applies all the more to manifested karma produced by a morally neutral intention, except in the special cases of merit tied to a material support (discussed back in fascicle 13's proofs for unmanifested karma) or an act that completes a full karma-path. Conversely, a noble one predisposed toward easy rebirth [into a higher state] who has not yet generated manifested karma — or who has generated it but has since relinquished it — possesses unmanifested karma without any accompanying manifested karma. The remaining two logical possibilities (both, or neither) should be filled in by analogy with what has already been established.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The phrase "易生聖補特伽羅" (a noble one of the type disposed toward easy rebirth) is technical enough to warrant a note: this refers to a particular category of noble person, within this tradition's detailed taxonomy of the stages of realization, whose remaining karma predisposes them toward a smooth, favorable further rebirth without requiring extensive additional bodily or verbal karma to secure it — hence such a person can possess significant unmanifested karma (the residue of their spiritual attainment) without necessarily having any currently active manifested karma standing behind it. We have kept this rendering close to the technical term rather than expanding it into a full doctrinal explanation, since the text itself does not elaborate on this category further here.

This four-case classification quietly resolves something that might otherwise seem puzzling from earlier in this fascicle: earlier sections established general "possession profiles" for restraint, non-restraint, and the "neither" category as if manifested and unmanifested karma always traveled together, but this section makes explicit that the two can, in fact, come apart in either direction — a point the earlier discussion of the "neither" category's own unmanifested karma ("not necessarily always") had already flagged in passing without fully spelling out the mechanism, which this section now supplies.

14. How Each Kind of Restraint Is Actually Obtained

The treatise now asks how each of the three kinds of restraint comes to be obtained in the first place. Meditative restraint is obtained automatically, the instant one attains the mind of either the root meditative absorption or its neighboring preliminary stage — since this restraint necessarily arises together with that very mind, with no separate act of receiving it required. Uncontaminated restraint works the same way, arising together with an uncontaminated mind at either the root or preliminary stage of any of the six levels capable of uncontaminated mental states (the preliminary access-concentration stage, the "intermediate" stage between the first and second absorptions, and the four root absorptions themselves — though not the three higher preliminary stages, for reasons to be explained later). The vow of individual liberation, by contrast, is obtained only "through another's instruction" — meaning through the guiding power of someone else who administers the vow — and divides into two further kinds depending on who administers it: the bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī, and śikṣamāṇā vows must be received from the saṅgha (the monastic community as a body), while the remaining five vows may be received from a single individual.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This section draws a clean line between two fundamentally different modes of obtaining restraint that this fascicle has been implicitly building toward: meditative and path-born restraint are obtained automatically and impersonally, arising the instant the corresponding mental state itself arises, with no ceremony, teacher, or social component required at all — while the vow of individual liberation is, by definition, a social and relational act, requiring another person's instruction and administration. This is why the vow of individual liberation alone, among the three restraints, needs the elaborate apparatus of ordination procedure that the fascicle is about to describe in detail (the ten traditional modes of full ordination), whereas meditative and path-born restraint need no such procedure at all.

The saṅgha-versus-individual distinction here maps directly onto monastic hierarchy: the higher, more binding vows (bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī, and the intermediate śikṣamāṇā stage a woman passes through on her way to full ordination) require the formal, collective authority of the monastic community itself to confer, reflecting their greater weight and the community's collective responsibility for admitting new full members — while the lower five vows (śrāmaṇera, śrāmaṇerī, upāsaka, upāsikā, upavāsa) may be administered by any single qualified individual, since they carry a correspondingly lighter institutional weight.

15. The Ten Traditional Ways Full Ordination Has Been Granted

The Vinaya masters record ten distinct historical modes by which full monastic ordination has, at different times, been granted — a list the verse's closing "and so on" is meant to gesture toward. First, by nature itself: the Buddha and the pratyekabuddhas, who require no external ordination at all. Second, by entering the state of correct assurance (i.e., attaining the fruit of stream-entry directly): the first five disciples. Third, by the Buddha's own summons, "Come, monk": Yaśas and others. Fourth, through faithfully accepting the Buddha as one's teacher: Mahākāśyapa. Fifth, through skillfully answering questions put to oneself: Sudāyin. Sixth, through respectfully accepting the eight special rules: Mahāprajāpatī. Seventh, through a sent messenger: the nun Dharmadatta (法授尼, literally "Dharma-Bestowing Nun"). Eighth, through a quorum of five including a Vinaya-holder: for those in a border region. Ninth, through a quorum of ten: for those in the Middle Country. Tenth, through the threefold recitation of taking refuge in Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha: the sixty worthy companions who received full ordination together in one assembly.

This list is a compact index of well-known episodes from early Buddhist narrative literature, and we want to flag that the treatise itself does not retell any of these stories — it simply names the mode of ordination and the person or persons associated with it, assuming the reader already knows the fuller account. A few identifications worth being explicit about, since the Chinese transliterations and epithets could otherwise be opaque: "the five monks" (五苾芻) are the first five disciples (including Aśvajit/Koṇḍañña) who heard the Buddha's first sermon at Sarnath; "Yaśas" (耶舍) was an early lay convert who requested ordination directly; "Mahākāśyapa" (大迦葉) became one of the Buddha's most senior disciples and later presided over the first monastic council; "Sudāyin" (蘇陀夷, Sk. The ninth and tenth modes are worth noting because they describe what became the standard, ongoing procedure rather than a one-time historical exception: a quorum of ten monks (or five, in remote "border regions" where ten qualified monks might not be available) administering ordination is the mechanism by which the monastic community has continued to induct new members ever since the earliest period described by modes one through eight — meaning items one through eight are historically closed categories (they can no longer occur, since the specific circumstances, like the Buddha's own presence, no longer exist), while items nine and ten remain the live, ongoing basis for ordination. We should also flag that "the sixty worthy ones" in the tenth case appears to be presented as its own distinct precedent alongside the ninth mode, though the text does not fully explain why this particular episode is not simply subsumed under the ordinary ten-monk quorum procedure — we leave this open rather than supplying a resolution the text itself does not give.

16. Lifetime Vows Versus the One-Day Vow, and Whether Upavāsa Could Be Extended

Continuing directly from the ten modes of ordination — noting in passing that the resulting vow of individual liberation does not necessarily depend on an actual manifested karmic act to arise — the treatise asks over what span of time this vow must be undertaken. The seven assemblies other than the one-day precept-holder must commit to their vow for their entire remaining lifetime; only the upavāsa vow is undertaken for a single day and night. This is fixed for a structural reason: a precept's temporal boundary can only be set at one of two points — the boundary of one's lifespan, or the boundary of a day and night — and taking a vow for even an intermediate span, such as half a month, is not an option, since a vow taken to expire at the moment of death cannot actually arise after death has already occurred, there being no further physical basis, deliberate effort, or capacity for continued intention in that different existence. This raises a further question: since a person could, in principle, take the upavāsa vow again after the first day-night ends, and again after that, is there some real obstacle blocking multiple consecutive upavāsa vows from ever combining into something longer? The Vaibhāṣika conclude firmly that there is no scriptural basis for any such extended reception — the Buddha's own teaching specifies only the single day-night span, and no other span is ever described.

The passage's central open question — did the Buddha limit upavāsa to one day-night because it is metaphysically impossible to extend it, or merely because that is what ordinary practitioners can manage? — is a genuinely interesting piece of hermeneutics that the treatise raises explicitly and then declines to resolve through reasoning alone, instead simply appealing to the absence of any contrary scriptural precedent. This is characteristic of how this treatise generally settles disputes about religious practice specifically (as opposed to disputes about metaphysics): scriptural silence on an alternative practice is treated as decisive against inventing that practice, even where pure logic might not obviously rule it out.

17. Why Non-Restraint Has No One-Day Version

The treatise now asks over what temporal boundary non-restraint (bad conduct) is obtained, and finds an asymmetry with the vow of individual liberation: committing to a lifetime of unwholesome deeds produces non-restraint, but there is no "one day and night" version of non-restraint corresponding to the upavāsa vow, because non-restraint is not received the way a wholesome precept is received. No one formally commits before a teacher, setting a fixed limit, to "definitely undertake non-restraint for one day and night" — such a thing would itself be an object of contempt to any wise person, whereas a formal, teacher-witnessed commitment to a lifetime of bad conduct is likewise never actually made; rather, non-restraint arises simply through generating an intention that thoroughly and irreversibly ruins one's own wholesome disposition, sustained without a teacher's involvement, unlike the upavāsa vow's brief, teacher-administered commitment which produces restraint even without any such thoroughgoing ruin of intention. Since only a thoroughly ruinous intention — not a merely temporary one — can generate non-restraint at all, and such an intention has no natural "one day and night" boundary the way a formally administered vow does, non-restraint simply never comes packaged in a one-day form. The treatise closes this discussion by briefly noting the Sautrāntika's alternative account: just as they deny any real unmanifested matter behind wholesome restraint, they likewise deny it behind non-restraint, holding instead that non-restraint just is the sustained, unrelinquished continuation of an intention to commit evil — meaning even someone who later experiences a wholesome thought can still be said to "possess" non-restraint throughout, simply because that underlying intention has never actually been abandoned.

[Objection:] If this is so, then there should also be no case of formally committing before a teacher, with a fixed limit, "I shall definitely undertake bad conduct until the end of my life" — so how could one obtain non-restraint for one's whole life either? [Reply:] Although there is no formal commitment before a teacher to commit unwholesome deeds for one's whole life, one obtains non-restraint by generating an intention that thoroughly and irreversibly ruins what is wholesome — it is not the case that generating a merely temporary intention that ruins what is wholesome, without a teacher, causes one to obtain non-restraint; hence non-restraint has no one-day-and-night version. The upavāsa precept, however, by the power of a formal commitment made in the actual presence of a teacher, produces restraint even without any thoroughly ruinous intention toward evil; and if someone were to formally commit, in the presence of a teacher, to a brief reception of non-restraint, this, too, would necessarily have to be obtainable — yet this has never actually been observed, and so it is not held to exist. The Sautrāntika school says: just as there is no separate real entity called unmanifested matter behind wholesome restraint, this non-restraint, too, should likewise not be real. Rather, the continuous, unabandoned intention to commit evil is what is called non-restraint; hence, even if a wholesome mind later arises, one is still said to possess non-restraint, since this underlying disposition has not been abandoned.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The logic distinguishing why a lifetime commitment to non-restraint works while a one-day commitment does not is genuinely subtle, and worth restating plainly: the treatise's claim is that non-restraint is generated purely by the strength and totality of the underlying corrupting intention (a decision to irreversibly abandon wholesome conduct), with no formal, teacher-witnessed ceremony playing any causal role at all — whereas the upavāsa vow's restraint is generated specifically by the formal ceremony itself, even absent any such totalizing intention. Since it is the ceremony (which can be brief) that makes a one-day wholesome vow possible, and non-restraint by definition has no such ceremony doing any of the causal work, there is simply no mechanism available to produce a "one-day" version of non-restraint — the only thing capable of producing non-restraint (a thorough, life-ruining intention) is, by its very nature, not the kind of thing that comes in a one-day size.

The Sautrāntika's closing remark here briefly echoes the exact same nominalist strategy already seen at length in fascicle 13's extended debate over unmanifested karma generally — reducing restraint and non-restraint alike to a persisting intention or disposition rather than a separate species of matter. We flag that this passage does not restage that full debate, but simply notes, in passing, that the same disagreement applies symmetrically to non-restraint as it did to restraint; readers wanting the fuller back-and-forth should look back to fascicle 13's extended treatment.

18. The Upavāsa Ceremony, and Why It Is Called "Near-Dwelling"

The treatise now specifies exactly how the upavāsa vow must be received. It must be taken at dawn, at sunrise, since the vow's duration is fixed at one full day and night from that point — though someone who has already resolved in advance to observe this vow on a fixed recurring date (such as the eighth day of each month) may still receive it later that same day if some obstacle prevented the morning reception. The precept-receiver must sit in a lower seat than the teacher — crouching, kneeling, bowing, palms joined — except when illness prevents this, since a lack of outward reverence would fail to actually generate the restraint at all. The vow must always be received from a teacher, never taken on one's own initiative, partly because the resulting sense of shame before that same teacher later helps the precept-holder resist any temptation to break the vow; and the receiver must recite the words after the teacher, never before or simultaneously, since only this order actually constitutes "receiving from a teacher's instruction." All eight precept-branches must be received together as a complete set — omitting even one means the upavāsa restraint fails to arise at all — and all customary bodily ornamentation must be set aside, since it is itself an "occasion of heedlessness" (though ordinary permanent adornments need not be removed, since they lack the power to inspire indulgence the way something novel and unfamiliar would). The vow's fixed duration of a full day and night, ending precisely at the next sunrise, together with its comprehensive prohibition against those living by hunting, killing, adultery, or theft, is what makes the upavāsa restraint genuinely effective in practice. As for its name: "upavāsa" (近住, "near-dwelling") means that this restraint dwells near the conduct of an arhat, since the precept-holder is training in imitation of that model — though some instead explain it as "dwelling near" the fuller lifetime precepts held by those in the other seven assemblies. This restraint is also called "long-nourishing" (長養), since it nourishes and gradually increases the meager wholesome roots of beings who have very little to begin with, as a verse states: because this is able to nourish one's own and others' wholesome, pure mind, the Blessed One therefore calls this restraint "long-nourishing."

The treatise offers two different explanations of what the "near" refers to (dwelling near an arhat's conduct, or dwelling near the fuller lifetime precepts of ordained and lay life) without choosing between them, which is consistent with this fascicle's general willingness to record multiple explanatory traditions side by side rather than adjudicating between them.

The ceremonial details here (reciting only after the teacher, never before or simultaneously; sitting in a lower seat; requiring all eight branches together as an indivisible whole) collectively make a point about the vow's essentially relational, ceremony-dependent nature that connects back to an earlier section's distinction between how the vow of individual liberation and meditation-born/path-born restraint are each obtained — this passage is filling in exactly why the ceremonial form matters so precisely for a restraint that (unlike meditative restraint) does not arise automatically from any internal mental attainment alone.

19. Why Exactly Eight Branches, Grouped in Threes

The treatise now explains why the upavāsa vow requires precisely eight precept-branches, organized into three functional groups. The first four branches — abstaining from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech — are called the "śīla branches," since these four alone directly counter the root offenses (性罪, acts that are inherently wrong regardless of any formal precept). The fifth branch — abstaining from alcohol — is the single "heedfulness branch," since drinking alcohol scatters the mind and directly undermines one's ability to keep the four śīla branches at all; without it, someone could technically hold the other four precepts while alcohol itself erodes their capacity to actually keep them. The final three branches — abstaining from self-adornment and entertainment, from high and luxurious beds, and from eating at improper times — are called the "restraining branches," since each addresses a different way that indulgence and heedless mental scattering can arise and gradually undermine one's resolve, even without technically breaking any of the first five precepts outright.

Prose: among the eight, the first four are the śīla branches — namely, from abstaining from killing living beings through abstaining from false speech — since these four kinds counter root offenses. The next — abstaining from alcohol — guards against lapses of mindfulness, since drinking alcohol causes one to forget what ought and ought not to be done. The final three guard against heedless indulgence, since indulging in various fragrances, garlands, high and broad beds and seats, and becoming accustomed to song and dance causes the mind to become conceited and heedless, quickly leading to breaking the precept; by keeping distant from these, the mind is thereby kept free of such conceit. Reading note — Ru-Yi

This threefold structure (śīla / heedfulness / restraint) gives a genuinely coherent rationale for what could otherwise look like an arbitrary list of eight rules: the first four target root wrongdoing directly; the fifth targets the specific psychological vulnerability (intoxication) that would otherwise erode one's ability to keep the first four; and the final three target subtler forms of indulgence that, while not root offenses in themselves, gradually cultivate the very heedlessness and self-satisfaction that makes root offenses and lapses of mindfulness more likely to occur in the first place. In this sense, the eight branches are not eight independent prohibitions but a layered defense system, with each layer addressing a different point at which discipline could break down.

We should flag that this section, in the source text, continues directly into a separate scholastic dispute over whether "abstaining from eating at improper times" specifically constitutes the upavāsa vow's core "essence" (體) with the other seven merely being its supporting "branches" (支) — a debate the Vaibhāṣika explicitly reject, along with several other schools' competing proposals about which branch, if any, deserves this special status. We have not included that further debate in this section, since it shifts to a narrower technical dispute about how to parse "essence" versus "branch" terminology (illustrated by analogies to a chariot's parts, a four-division army, and the sevenfold factors of awakening) that would need its own dedicated treatment; it remains for a future section.

20. Who Can Receive Upavāsa, and Whether Refuge Alone Makes One an Upāsaka

The treatise asks whether only someone already established as an upāsaka can receive the upavāsa restraint, or whether others can as well — and answers that anyone who has not yet received the upāsaka precept can still receive the upavāsa restraint, provided they take the threefold refuge (in the Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha) within that same day-and-night period; apart from this, there is no way to obtain it, except through simple ignorance of the requirement. This raises a related question, prompted by a sūtra passage in which the Buddha describes a lay follower simply as someone with functioning male faculties who takes refuge in the Three Jewels and, with sincere and pure intention, truthfully declares themselves an upāsaka: does this declaration of refuge alone suffice to make one a genuine upāsaka? Non-Indian teachers hold that it does; but the masters of Kashmir insist that without also taking on the upāsaka restraint itself, one is not truly an upāsaka at all — which might seem to conflict with the sūtra's own wording, except that, as the treatise clarifies, the declaration itself already generates the restraint, so there is no actual conflict.

The next section will explain the precise mechanics of exactly when, within that declaration, the restraint is generated.

21. The Exact Moment the Upāsaka Vow Takes Effect

The treatise now pinpoints exactly when the upāsaka's restraint is generated: at the very instant one, with sincere and pure intention, truthfully declares "I am an upāsaka; may the Honored One bear this in mind and, with compassion, watch over and protect me" — this declaration itself, and nothing beyond it, is what generates the restraint, just as a bhikṣu's or śrāmaṇera's restraint is generated at a specific instant during their own ordination formulas. This raises a question, since the same sūtra passage continues with the words "from this time until the end of my life, I renounce the taking of life" — does this mean the actual five precepts are only granted afterward, separately from the initial declaration? The treatise clarifies that this later clause is simply the sūtra's own abbreviated way of referring to the renunciation of killing and the other four root offenses (using "renounce life" as shorthand for the fuller list), meaning the five precepts were, in fact, already obtained at the earlier moment of self-declaration. The explicit recitation of the five precepts that follows is not what generates the restraint a second time, but instead serves a purely pedagogical purpose: to make sure the newly-minted upāsaka clearly understands and firmly retains exactly which training rules they are now committed to — precisely as happens when a newly ordained bhikṣu, having already obtained the full monastic precept through the ordination ceremony itself, is then separately taught the detailed training rules so as to understand and retain them, and precisely as happens for the śrāmaṇera as well. Hence the upāsaka's restraint, like these others, is necessarily accompanied by real restraint from the moment of declaration onward — it is never a case of the declaration alone, without any restraint following from it.

This section resolves what would otherwise be a real scriptural puzzle by drawing a clean line between the act that generates a vow and the subsequent teaching that clarifies its content — the same pattern the treatise now points out recurs across all three levels of ordination discussed in this fascicle (upāsaka, śrāmaṇera, and bhikṣu): the founding, restraint-generating moment is always a specific, singular declaration or ceremonial act, while any subsequent recitation of detailed rules is purely instructional, ensuring the newly-vowed person actually understands what they have already committed to, rather than being the moment that commitment itself takes effect.

This closing line — "hence the upāsaka necessarily possesses restraint" — directly resolves the disagreement from the previous section between the "outside teachers" (who held that mere refuge-taking suffices to make one an upāsaka) and the Kashmir masters (who insisted restraint was additionally required): by locating restraint's generation at the very moment of the refuge-and-declaration formula itself, rather than treating it as a separate, later-added requirement, the treatise shows both camps were describing the same single event, just emphasizing different aspects of it.

22. The Four Grades of Upāsaka: A Difference in Practice, Not in Vow

An objection now presses hard on the previous section's conclusion: if every upāsaka necessarily possesses the complete restraint of all five precepts, why does the Buddha elsewhere speak of four distinct kinds of upāsaka — one able to train in only a portion, one able to train in a small portion, one able to train in most, and one able to train in the complete set? The treatise resolves this by insisting these four grades describe differing capacities to actually keep precepts already fully received, not differing amounts of precept received in the first place — "able to train" in the Buddha's statement means "able to uphold what was previously undertaken." An alternative reading, that the Buddha's statement instead describes people receiving a genuinely partial restraint (some receiving only one precept, some several, and so on), is rejected as contradicting scripture: no sūtra anywhere describes the founding declaration itself ("I am an upāsaka...") as generating only a fraction of the five precepts, and the passage sometimes cited in support of a partial reading is, on the treatise's analysis, actually about something else — the sincere and total commitment of a stream-enterer who has directly seen the truths, not a technical description of how the upāsaka precept itself is received.

23. Why Precept-Scope Is Fixed by the Buddha's Authority, and the Three Grades of Restraint

The treatise now asks why the scope of restraint at each level — five branches for the upāsaka, eight for the upavāsa, ten for the śrāmaṇera, the complete code for the bhikṣu — is fixed at exactly these specific amounts rather than any other. The answer offered is simply that this is how the Buddha's own teaching has established it: there is no further, independent reason beyond the Buddha's authority itself for why these particular boundaries were drawn where they were. This immediately raises a pointed follow-up: if the Buddha's authority alone is sufficient to fix these scopes, why not simply let that same authority declare that someone lacking restraint entirely could still be called an "upāsaka," the way this fascicle has been arguing that a full upāsaka's vow cannot be partial? The Kashmir masters hold firm: lacking restraint entirely simply disqualifies someone from being an upāsaka in the first place, no matter what discretion the Buddha's authority might otherwise have. The treatise then turns to a related question: how does the very same vow come to differ in strength — lower, middling, or superior grade — from one person to another? The answer is that this variation tracks nothing but the strength of the mind present at the moment of taking the vow, which is why even a fully liberated arhat might, in principle, hold only a lower-grade restraint, while an ordinary, unenlightened person could hold a superior-grade one, depending entirely on the intensity of intention each brought to their own reception of the vow.

24. What "Taking Refuge" in the Three Jewels Actually Means

The treatise now asks, with technical precision, exactly what one takes refuge in when taking refuge in the Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha. Refuge in the Buddha means refuge specifically in the qualities of one beyond training that make a buddha a buddha — chiefly the exhaustive knowledge that ends all outflows, together with its accompanying qualities — not refuge in his particular physical body, since it is by possessing these superior qualities that a body earns the name "buddha" in the first place, or because it is by attaining these very qualities that a buddha awakens to all things. And since all buddhas share an identical realization, one properly takes refuge in every buddha at once, not merely the historical Buddha alone. Refuge in the Saṅgha means refuge in the training and beyond-training qualities that make the eight kinds of noble persons a genuine, indestructible community, and likewise extends to every such community across time, not merely those alive in the present. Refuge in the Dharma means refuge in nirvāṇa itself — specifically the cessation attained through discernment, which extinguishes the afflictions and suffering of both oneself and others in the very same undifferentiated way, which is precisely why refuge in this one Dharma can be taken universally by all beings alike.

25. The Shedding-Blood Puzzle, and the Famous Verse on True and False Refuge

The previous section's claim — that refuge in the Buddha is refuge in his beyond-training qualities, not his physical body — immediately invites a sharp objection: if this is so, why does drawing the Buddha's blood with malicious intent count as one of the five gravest possible offenses (acts of immediate retribution), when this act damages only his physical body, not the beyond-training qualities themselves? The Vaibhāṣika resolve this by arguing that the qualities' physical support is thereby damaged, and the qualities are damaged along with it — though the treatise notes, on closer inspection of the root text, that it never actually claimed the beyond-training qualities alone constitute "the Buddha" in some exclusive sense; it only said these qualities are what makes a buddha a buddha, without denying that the physical body, as the qualities' indispensable support, is also part of what the term encompasses. The treatise adds a helpful analogy: just as someone wishing to make an offering to "a bhikṣu" is understood to be making an offering to the bhikṣu-precept that constitutes his bhikṣu-status specifically, without this meaning the bhikṣu's body is somehow irrelevant to the offering, so too does refuge in "the Buddha" work the same way. Some other teachers hold instead that refuge in the Buddha means refuge in the eighteen unique qualities exclusive to a Tathāgata. As for what taking refuge itself consists of and what purpose it serves: its essential nature is manifested verbal karma (the actual spoken words of commitment), and its purpose is rescue — since relying on the Three Jewels is what permanently frees a being from all suffering, unlike the false refuges people turn to out of fear, memorably contrasted in a well-known verse: many, driven by terror, take refuge in mountains, groves, parks, and solitary shrine-trees — but such refuge is neither excellent nor noble, and grants no true freedom from suffering; only one who takes refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, and constantly examines the Four Noble Truths with wisdom — seeing suffering, its origin, its final transcendence, and the Noble Eightfold Path leading to the peace of nirvāṇa — has found the refuge that is truly excellent and noble, the one refuge that actually and necessarily liberates from all suffering. Refuge in the Three Jewels is, accordingly, the universal gateway through which every kind of restraint, at every level, must first be entered.

26. Why the Upāsaka Precept Targets Only Sexual Misconduct, Not Full Chastity

Having established refuge as the universal gateway into every level of restraint, the treatise returns to a practical question about the upāsaka precepts specifically: why does the Buddha require complete chastity (abstention from all sexual activity) in every other level of restraint, yet require only abstention from sexual misconduct — not full chastity — from the upāsaka alone? Sexual misconduct earns the world's harshest censure, since it violates another's spouse and leads to unfortunate rebirth, and, unlike full chastity, it is realistically achievable — householders, deeply attached to sensory desire, would find full chastity impossible to sustain over a lifetime, and the Buddha, recognizing they could not maintain such a demanding standard long-term, declined to impose it. There is also a deeper reason: every noble one, without exception, has already permanently attained the restraint of never committing sexual misconduct, even across successive lifetimes as a noble being — but this is not true of full chastity, which even noble householders may not maintain. Hence the upāsaka's restraint is set at the level guaranteed never to be violated by any noble one across rebirths, so that a noble one reborn as a lay follower could never be said to have broken their upāsaka vow. This raises a further practical question: does someone who marries after already having received the upāsaka vow actually possess restraint with respect to that later spouse from the very moment of the original vow? The treatise answers yes — but resolves an apparent tension (since one clearly can still marry without this counting as a violation) by clarifying that the vow's scope was never a blanket commitment covering every being in the universe, but precisely mirrors the original wording of the vow itself: abstention from sexual misconduct specifically, universally applied to all beings — meaning a later marriage is simply an instance of licit conduct falling outside that same narrower scope, not a breach of an earlier, broader promise.

Hence, in the restraint received by the upāsaka, only abstention from sexual misconduct is established — lest a noble one reborn again [as a layperson] should be said to violate the upāsaka restraint. "Restraint of non-performance" means definite non-performance. [Question:] For one who first receives the upāsaka restraint and afterward marries a wife or takes a concubine, does one, with respect to that spouse, already possess this restraint from the time of the original reception? In reality, one should indeed possess it — lest the restraint of individual liberation be obtained only with respect to a portion [of beings, rather than universally]. [Objection:] If this is so, how does the later act not then constitute breaking the precept? Verse: restraint is obtained exactly as vowed, not as a blanket commitment covering the [entire] continuum [of all beings without distinction of relation]. Prose: restraint is obtained exactly according to the original vow taken. What was the original vow? Abstention from sexual misconduct. It was not a statement that, with respect to the continuum of every sentient being, "I shall abstain from all sexual activity with each of them" — rather, by this vow, one universally obtains, with respect to the continuum of every sentient being, only the precept of abstaining from sexual misconduct. Since this is not the restraint of abstaining from all sexual activity, later taking a wife or concubine does not violate the earlier precept.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The claim that "every noble one has attained the restraint of never performing sexual misconduct, even across successive lifetimes" is a striking piece of moral psychology worth drawing out: it treats certain ethical achievements (freedom from ever violating another's marriage through desire, specifically) as so deeply and permanently secured by the attainment of any level of noble realization that they persist automatically across rebirths without needing to be re-vowed each lifetime — while other, more demanding practices (complete lifelong chastity) are not guaranteed this same automatic carry-over, even for noble householders. This is offered as the deeper reason (beyond mere practical accommodation) for why the upāsaka precept is calibrated exactly to the level that tracks this permanent noble attainment, rather than set higher or lower.

The resolution of the marriage puzzle is a fine piece of precise thinking about what a vow's "scope" actually covers: rather than being a promise made toward specific individuals (which would then need updating or exception-carving as one's life circumstances change, such as through marriage), the vow is universally scoped by *content* (abstaining from misconduct, applied to literally everyone) rather than by *person* (a blanket promise of total abstinence toward everyone), meaning later licit marital relations were never within the vow's prohibited scope to begin with — nothing about the vow needs to be revised or partially relinquished when one later marries.
