Fascicle 10 · 阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論卷第十 · Complete

The Narrow Path Every Mind Must Take

Fascicle 10 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa's second part (雜蘊第一中智納息第二之二), first completing the non-self aspect's full technical profile left open at the end of Fascicle 9, then opening an entirely new investigation into causation itself. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation — the miser's single gold coin atop a heap of copper, the outsiders' spring-water analogy for why a later moment cannot cause an earlier one, the narrow path and the sheep-pen gate illustrating why no two minds ever arise together, and the rigorous seven-master survey of what actually makes the immediately-antecedent condition the thing it is.

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 10 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa opened mid-fascicle in Fascicle 9, remaining throughout in its second part (之二). Terms established in earlier fascicles, such as the five aggregates of clinging and the aspect that takes all dharmas as non-self, recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The Non-Self Aspect's Own Substance and Name

Fascicle 10 opens by finishing what Fascicle 9 left dangling: the aspect that takes all dharmas as non-self is now given its own technical profile, beginning with its substance (wisdom itself) and the reason for its name.

Chinese Source

問緣一切法非我行相。自體是何。答慧為自體。如自體我物相分。自性亦爾。已說自體。所以今當說。問何故名為緣一切法非我行相。答此慧行一切法起非我相故。名緣一切法非我行相。界者此行相。唯欲色界無色界中。亦有此行相而不能緣一切法。後當廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: as for the aspect that takes all dharmas as non-self — what is its own substance? The answer: wisdom is its own substance. As with own-substance, so too with "thing" and "aspect," "own-nature" as well — these terms are synonymous. Its own substance has now been stated; what should be stated next follows. One asks: why is it named "the aspect that takes all dharmas as non-self"? The answer: because this wisdom, operating with respect to all dharmas, gives rise to the aspect of non-self — hence it is named "the aspect that takes all dharmas as non-self." As for its realm: this aspect occurs only in the desire realm and the form realm; the formless realm, too, possesses this aspect, but there it is unable to take all dharmas as its object, as will be explained at length below.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This kind of formal profile — own-substance, then name, then realm, then stage — is the standard Abhidharma template applied to nearly every technical category this treatise discusses, and its reappearance here confirms that fascicle 10 is not starting a new investigation so much as completing the itemized checklist fascicle 9 had only begun for this particular aspect.

2. Its Realm and Stage: Seven Stages, Then the Formless Realm's Limits

The non-self aspect occurs across the desire realm, proximate concentration, the intermediate dhyāna, and the four fundamental dhyānas — seven stages in general, refined by which kind of wisdom (learning, reflection, cultivation) occupies which subset. The four formless attainments possess a corresponding aspect too, but each one's scope is sharply curtailed to its own and higher formless spheres alone.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The formless realm's radically narrower scope here — boundless space's non-self aspect reaching only the four formless attainments themselves, never the desire or form realms below it — reflects a consistent Abhidharma principle that a meditative attainment can only take as its object what is equal to or subtler than its own level, never anything coarser. This is the same asymmetry that will resurface throughout this fascicle in different guises: higher and subtler states are cognitively cut off from the coarser states beneath them in ways that lower states are not cut off from what lies above.

3. An Alternate Count, Five Reasons the Formless Realm Falls Short, and a Full Technical Profile

A rival position trims the formless aspects' scope even further, before the treatise settles the matter and turns to why the formless realm altogether lacks any non-self aspect capable of taking all dharmas as object — five different explanations are offered, none contradicting the others — followed by the aspect's complete technical profile: its support, its foundation of mindfulness, its associated faculties, its moral quality, and more than a dozen further classificatory questions.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The five explanations for the formless realm's limitation (lacking the nirvedhabhāgīya factors, lacking the truth-contemplating wholesome root, lacking the direct-realization-adjacent worldly knowledge, lacking the path of seeing, or simply favoring stillness over contemplation) are not really five competing theories so much as five facets of one underlying fact: the formless attainments are, by design, meditative endpoints rather than platforms for further analytical work, and every explanation offered here approaches that same basic asymmetry from a different angle. The miser-and-a-single-gold-coin image that will close this whole opening section a little later is foreshadowed here in miniature by the discussion of why this aspect is invariably joyful even when some of its objects are disagreeable — a psychological subtlety this treatise clearly considered worth pinning down precisely.

4. Attained Through Effort or by Birth?

A finer distinction follows: this aspect, depending on realm and the kind of wisdom involved, may be attained purely through one's own preparatory effort, purely by virtue of being reborn into a stage where it is already available, or through some combination — with the desire realm's own version restricted to effort alone, while the form realm's admits of both.

Chinese Source

色界聞所成非我行相。可言加行得可言生得。云何可言加行得。謂若此間於自共相善。修習者生彼便得。若不爾者生彼不得。云何可言生得。謂雖此間善修習已。若未生彼終不能得。生彼方得。彼聞所成非我行相。必依此間所修加行。生彼得故。色界修所成非我行相。是加行得及離染得。亦可言是生得。問若欲界沒。生第二靜慮。第二靜慮沒生初靜慮。彼得初靜慮非我行相不。答若先善修習者得。不爾者不得。乃至生第四靜慮亦爾。問若欲界沒。生無色界。無色界沒。生初靜慮。彼得初靜慮非我行相不。有說。不得。

6. Ordinary Beings Within and Outside the Teaching, and How the Aspect Actually Arises

Even ordinary beings, not just the noble ones, can attain this aspect — but a further split follows between those within the Buddhist teaching and outsiders, the latter's self-attachment blocking a fully secure attainment even when it does arise. The treatise then works out, stage by stage, exactly how much the aspect can take as its object depending on where in the cosmos the practitioner happens to be born.

Chinese Source

The asymmetry between insiders and outsiders here is carefully calibrated rather than absolute: outsiders can attain the aspect (contradicting any simple claim that non-Buddhist practice accomplishes nothing at all), but only by birth, never through their own deliberate effort, and never in a way that stays present and possessed in the body — a nuanced middle position between dismissing outside contemplative achievement outright and granting it full parity with the Buddhist path.

7. Desire Realm Versus Form Realm, and the Miser's Single Gold Coin

A close comparison shows the desire realm's own non-self aspect actually outranges the form realm's whenever the latter is “fixed” to its own stage, since only the desire realm's version can take its own “following form” as object. The section closes on a psychological note: this aspect always accompanies a joyful attention, even when some of what it surveys is disagreeable, since a single agreeable thing anywhere in the field is enough to color the practitioner's whole outlook — like one gold coin resting on a heap of copper.

Chinese Source

問欲色二界非我行相。何者緣法多耶。答色界非我行相。若不定者。所緣法與欲界等。若定者則所緣法少於欲界。謂不能緣自隨轉色。欲界非我行相。無隨轉色故。能緣一切色故。有說。言有身念住。緣一切色無有受心。法念住緣一切受心法。問此緣一切法非我行相。為有漏為無漏耶。答是有漏非無漏。所以者何。有世俗非我行相。尚不能緣一切法。如順決擇分。何況無漏非我行相。能緣一切法耶。以此行相別諦緣故。如諸邪見尚無一時緣二諦者。況緣多耶。如所對治能對治亦爾。問有漏非我行相。能斷煩惱不。答不能斷。問若爾聖者何故起耶。答令根轉利入聖道故。復次由四緣故聖者起之。一為現法樂住故。二為觀本所作故。三為遊戲功德故。四為受用聖財故。問此非我行相。為欣作意俱。為厭作意俱耶。設爾何失。若欣作意俱者。云何緣可厭法。若厭作意俱者。云何緣可欣法。答應作是說。欣作意俱。問若爾云何緣可厭法。答彼瑜伽師。

8. A New Topic: Can Two Minds Cause Each Other?

With the non-self aspect's profile complete, the treatise turns to a wholly different question: can two minds ever serve as cause for one another? Three rival positions are set up for refutation — that causation has no real substance at all, that the Mahāsāṃghikas' single person can host two simultaneous minds, and an outsiders' argument, drawn from watching a spring's later water push the earlier water forward, that a later moment can cause an earlier one.

That is, some maintain that the causal condition has no real substance at all; in order to refute this position, the treatise wishes to show that the causal condition does indeed really possess its own substantial nature. There are also outsiders who, drawing on a worldly, visible analogy, maintain that a later moment serves as cause for an earlier one. They say as follows: it is plainly observed that when a spring gushes forth, the later water presses upon the earlier water, causing it to gush and flow — here, the later water serves as cause for the earlier water. In just the same way, they say, as dharmas pass through the three time-periods, the future presses what is in it into the present, and the present presses what is in it into the past — hence the future serves as cause for the present, and the present in turn serves as cause for the past. In order to refute this position, the treatise wishes to show that a later dharma is never the cause of an earlier one. Were it truly so, this would contradict both the internal and external processes of dependent origination. Contradicting the internal process of dependent origination would mean: formations would then be conditioned by ignorance, and so on, up through birth conditioning old age and death — parents would be caused by their children, the eye and form would be caused by eye-consciousness, and so on, up through the mental faculty and dharmas being caused by mental consciousness; further, the kalala embryonic stage would be caused by the arbuda stage, and so on, up through maturity being caused by old age. Contradicting the external process of dependent origination in the same way would mean: the seed would then be caused by the sprout, and so on, up through the flower being caused by the fruit. Such a position, moreover, incurs a further, grave fault: one would have to receive the effect of karma before having ever created it, and only create the karma after already having received its effect. What would this actually mean? One would receive the ripened effect of pleasure and pain before creating the wholesome or unwholesome karma that produces it; one would obtain the effect of ethical or unethical conduct before undertaking the very precepts, ethical or unethical, that produce it; one would fall into hell before ever committing an act of immediate retribution; one would become a wheel-turning king before ever performing the karma that produces it; one would attain unsurpassed perfect enlightenment before ever cultivating the six perfections that lead to it. And if one could receive an effect before creating its karma, then having created the karma afterward would amount to nothing, its effect already spent — and this would mean there could be no liberation or emancipation at all. The spring-water analogy for “later causes earlier” is a vivid piece of naturalistic reasoning gone wrong in a very instructive way: it mistakes spatial displacement (water physically pushing water ahead of it) for temporal causation (a later moment producing an earlier one), and the treatise's refutation — that this would make children the cause of their parents and fruit the cause of flowers — is a masterclass in reductio ad absurdum applied to a category confusion between two entirely different senses of “before” and “after.”

9. The Answer Is No — What Exactly Is Being Refuted

The treatise answers its own question flatly: no, there is no case of two minds mutually causing one another, since no single person ever has two minds arising side by side, and a later mind is never the cause of an earlier one — a terse answer that turns out to be doing three separate pieces of refutation at once, parsed word by word.

A metatextual question interrupts the flow: shouldn't the treatise have explained why two minds never coincide before simply asserting that they don't? Two answers are offered — that Abhidharma composition doesn't owe its reader any fixed sequence, or that root topics (this one) are properly stated before their own ancillary explanations (the next one) regardless of which logically depends on which.

20. Does Mind Only Condition Mind? The Heap of Beans

Two final restrictive proposals are considered and rejected: that mind can only serve as an immediately-antecedent condition for mind (never for the accompanying mental factors, and vice versa), and that this conditioning relation only reaches “nearby” dharmas, not more distant ones.
