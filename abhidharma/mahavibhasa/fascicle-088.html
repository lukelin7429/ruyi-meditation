The Faculties Reflected in Consciousness

Fascicle 88 resumes the sixteen-consciousness classification exactly where fascicle 87 broke off, working through the mind, equanimity, pleasure, joy, sorrow, and faith faculties, the three undefiled faculties, the sense elements and sense-fields, the aggregates, and the undefiled and unconditioned dharma. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 88 continues the Ten Gates Nyāsa (結蘊第二中十門納息第四之十八), resuming the sixteen-fold classification of consciousness (十六識) opened in fascicle 87 — which consciousness takes which dharma as its object (緣識), both directly and as a further object of that object (緣緣識) — now worked through the remaining faculties, the sense elements and sense-fields, the aggregates, and the undefiled dharma. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right.

1. The Mind Faculty and the Equanimity-Faculty

Resuming exactly where fascicle 87 broke off mid-sentence at the life faculty, the sweep turns to the mind faculty — pervading both the contaminated and the undefiled across the three realms' nine grounds and five categories, and, remarkably, taken as object by all sixteen of the sixteen possible consciousnesses at once, both directly and as an object-of-that-object, since a mind capable of any contaminated or undefiled state whatsoever can in principle reflect upon any other such state. The equanimity-faculty follows the identical pattern.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the mind faculty being taken as both a direct object and an object-of-that-object solely by consciousness that takes a conditioned dharma as its object: the mind faculty, pervading the three realms' nine grounds and five categories, both contaminated and undefiled, belonging to dharma- and class-knowledge, is taken as object by sixteen consciousnesses — first, the consciousness of the desire realm associated with every latent tendency abandoned by seeing suffering, this consciousness being increased by every latent tendency of the desire realm abandoned by seeing suffering, and by the universally pervasive latent tendency abandoned by seeing accumulation; second, the consciousness of the desire realm associated with every latent tendency abandoned by seeing accumulation, this consciousness being increased by every latent tendency of the desire realm abandoned by seeing accumulation, and by the universally pervasive latent tendency abandoned by seeing suffering; third, the consciousness of the desire realm associated with the latent tendency abandoned by seeing cessation that takes a conditioned dharma as its object, this consciousness being increased by the desire realm's own latent tendency abandoned by seeing cessation that takes a conditioned dharma as its object, and by the universally pervasive latent tendencies; fourth, the consciousness of the desire realm associated with every latent tendency abandoned by seeing the path, this consciousness being increased by every latent tendency of the desire realm abandoned by seeing the path, and by the universally pervasive latent tendencies; fifth, the wholesome, together with the defiled and neutral-and-unobscured, consciousness of the desire realm abandoned by cultivation, this consciousness being increased by every latent tendency of the desire realm abandoned by cultivation, and by the universally pervasive latent tendencies — just as with the desire realm's own five categories of consciousness that take a conditioned dharma as their object, so too, correspondingly, should the form and formless realms' own five categories of such consciousness each be understood, making fifteen consciousnesses in all; sixteenth, the undefiled consciousness — namely, the forbearances and knowledges of the dharma- and class- kind belonging to suffering, accumulation, and the path — this consciousness not being increased by any latent tendency whatsoever. It is for this reason that it is said that the mind faculty is taken as a direct object solely by consciousness that takes a conditioned dharma as its object. The mind faculty is, further, taken as an object-of-that-object by these same sixteen consciousnesses, and the latent tendencies increased with respect to the mind faculty's own object-of-that-object are likewise just as stated for the mind faculty's own direct object. It is for this reason that it is said that the mind faculty is taken as an object-of-that-object solely by consciousness that takes a conditioned dharma as its object. As for the equanimity-faculty being just the same: the equanimity-faculty, too, pervades the three realms' nine grounds and five categories, both contaminated and undefiled, belonging to dharma- and class-knowledge, just like the mind faculty.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the mind faculty alone, among every dharma treated across these three fascicles, is taken as object by the complete set of sixteen consciousnesses without exception is a fitting culmination of everything this whole classification has been building toward: precisely because mental consciousness's own reach was the very point at issue in the polemic against the Dārṣṭāntikas, showing it capable of taking in absolutely everything — every realm, every category, contaminated and undefiled alike — is the strongest possible demonstration that its object is not confined to any separate, narrower domain.

2. The Pleasure-Faculty

The pleasure-faculty, confined to the desire realm's five sense-consciousnesses, the first dhyāna's three kinds of consciousness, and the third dhyāna's mental consciousness alone, is worked through in the now-familiar two-part structure — twelve consciousnesses reach it directly, fourteen reach it as an object-of-that-object — with each and every one of the twelve direct consciousnesses individually named and matched to its own bond of increase.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The pleasure-faculty's own oddly scattered distribution — present in the desire realm's five senses, the first dhyāna's three senses, and then only in the third dhyāna's mental consciousness, skipping the second dhyāna's own experience of joy entirely — reflects a genuine feature of the dhyāna scheme rather than an arbitrary restriction: bodily pleasure belongs to the coarser sense-based stages, while the joy of the second dhyāna is mental rather than bodily, and it is only once directed thought and sustained reflection have both been left behind, in the third dhyāna, that the subtler mental pleasure proper to that stage reappears as pleasure once more rather than as joy.

3. The Joy-Faculty

The joy-faculty, confined to the desire realm and the first two dhyānas, follows the same thirteen-consciousness direct count and fifteen-consciousness indirect count, worked through name by name — each of its own five categories' consciousnesses in the desire realm, extended to the form realm, and reached indirectly from the formless realm above.

5. The Three Undefiled Faculties

The three undefiled faculties — belonging solely to the knowledges of suffering, accumulation, cessation, and the path — are reached directly by seven consciousnesses and indirectly by thirteen, the underlying logic being that, since these faculties are themselves entirely undefiled, no ordinary contaminated consciousness can take them as a direct object except by way of the path-knowledge consciousness that is itself undefiled, while a great many more consciousnesses can still reach them indirectly, by first taking that undefiled consciousness as their own object.
