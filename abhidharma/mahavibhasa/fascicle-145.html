Four Truths, One View, and Why the Higher Realms Feel No Pain

Fascicle 145 refines the path of seeing into its own four separate truths with a precise census of underlying tendencies, explains why only the eye-faculty and a sliver of wisdom ever count as “view,” defends discursive thought's true extent against the Dṛṣṭāntikas, and closes with why the form realm lacks the male and female faculties, including the story of Mahāprajāpatī. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Four Kinds of Seeing, One Kind of Cultivation: The Mind-Faculty's Own Precise Census

Fascicle 145 opens by refining the previous fascicle's binary distinction between seeing and cultivation into a fourfold one, splitting the path of seeing itself into its own four separate moments — seeing suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path — each with its own precise, non-overlapping set of underlying tendencies it alone cuts off. The mind-faculty's own census is worked out in exhaustive detail: twenty-eight tendencies cut off by seeing suffering, nineteen each by seeing origin and cessation, twenty-two by seeing the path, and a final ten surviving into the more gradual work of cultivation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the four truths, so often treated as a single unified insight arrived at all at once, are shown here to each cut off a genuinely distinct and non-overlapping set of underlying tendencies is a reminder of how much technical precision this tradition packs beneath what can sound, in summary, like a single moment of awakening: seeing the four truths is not one event but four separate surgical strikes, each targeted at its own specific portion of what binds a mind.

2. Joy, Displeasure, and the Exact Count of What Binds Them

The same exhaustive four-truths breakdown continues through the joy- and displeasure-faculties, each carrying its own distinctive numerical signature — joy bound by seventeen tendencies at seeing suffering (every desire-realm tendency except hatred and doubt, plus the entire form realm), displeasure by a much narrower four (wrong view, hatred, doubt, and ignorance, each specific to the desire realm alone) — with displeasure's own final, cultivation-stage remainder narrowing to just two: hatred and ignorance, the last survivors of an otherwise thoroughly dismantled affliction.

Displeasure's own final surviving pair — hatred and ignorance, and nothing else — after sixteen tendencies have already been swept away at the path of seeing, offers a small but vivid statistical confirmation of something this tradition believes about affliction generally: whatever remains after the most decisive breakthroughs tend to be the deepest, most stubbornly rooted tendencies, the ones too fundamental to be dislodged by insight alone and requiring the slower, repeated labor of cultivation instead.

3. What Actually Distinguishes Seeing from Cultivation?

Having completed the numerical census, the treatise turns to defining the distinction itself: a dharma is cut off by seeing suffering, its origin, cessation, or the path specifically when it has a fixed, determinate antidote and a fixed, determinate object corresponding to exactly one of the four truths; whatever lacks this fixed correspondence — cut off by no single determinate antidote or object — is instead cut off by cultivation.

Chinese Source

Others say: whatever dharma is cut off by the patient acceptance with respect to suffering is called cut off by seeing suffering, up through whatever dharma is cut off by the patient acceptance with respect to the path being called cut off by seeing the path; whatever dharma is cut off by any of the further kinds of knowledge is called cut off by cultivation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Defining cultivation not by any positive feature of its own but by the simple absence of the fixed, one-to-one correspondence that characterizes seeing is a clean piece of logical economy: rather than needing its own separate defining mark, cultivation is elegantly captured as everything that seeing's own precise, targeted structure does not reach.

4. Only One Faculty, and a Sliver of Wisdom, Ever Get to Be Called 'View'

A sharp definitional dispute opens: does every dharma, since each exercises some sharp function within its own domain, thereby deserve the technical name "view"? The treatise firmly rejects this expansive claim, confining the term to just the eye-faculty and a carefully carved-out portion of wisdom itself — specifically excluding whatever wisdom belongs to the knowledge of exhaustion and non-arising, since these final two kinds of knowledge, though genuinely wisdom, no longer perform the specific observing function that earns the name "view."

Answer: in order to refute the claim that every dharma whatsoever is, by its very nature, "view" — for some hold that whatever exercises a sharp, penetrating function is thereby called "view," since every dharma, with respect to its own particular undertaking, exercises some sharp, penetrating function, and hence all alike deserve the name "view." Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even wisdom itself, the very faculty most associated with genuine insight throughout this treatise, only partially qualifies as "view" — with its own most advanced, terminal forms specifically excluded — reveals "view" to be a far narrower and more technical term than its everyday English translation might suggest: it names a particular observing function, not simply the presence of correct understanding wherever it happens to occur.

5. Four Reasons Even a Wrong View Still Counts as Seeing

That a single technical term, "bound," receives seven entirely distinct explanatory images — a tethered ox, feet for traveling, a storehouse, a moistening, a joining, a staining — rather than one settled definition, is a fitting way to close this fascicle's own extended meditation on precision and multiplicity: even the most basic vocabulary of bondage itself turns out to admit of as many illuminating angles as this tradition can find the patience to offer.
