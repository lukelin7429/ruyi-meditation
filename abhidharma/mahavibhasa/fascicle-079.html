1. Why Only Suffering "Should Be Fully Known"? Six Reconciling Distinctions

A scripture states that only the noble truth of suffering should be fully known by wisdom, yet the Abhidharma states that wisdom fully knows every dharma whatsoever. Six converging distinctions reconcile the two: the scripture speaks solely of transmundane wisdom, the Abhidharma of both mundane and transmundane; the scripture of wisdom's near full knowledge, the Abhidharma of both near and distant; the scripture of the wisdom that observes only the shared characteristic, the Abhidharma of wisdom observing both particular and shared; the scripture of wisdom not shared with outsiders, the Abhidharma of wisdom both shared and unshared; the scripture of the very moment of direct realization, the Abhidharma of the ongoing practice of the truths; and, most tellingly, the scripture speaks through provisional cognition, resting on what is coarse and evident, while the Abhidharma speaks through ultimate cognition, exhausting the principle in full.

Ru-Yi Translation

As a scripture states, the noble truth of suffering should be fully known by wisdom. The Abhidharma states: what is fully known by knowledge refers to every dharma whatsoever. One asks: if every dharma whatsoever is what is to be fully known, as the Abhidharma states, why does the scripture speak only of suffering as what should be fully known by wisdom? The answer: the scripture relies solely upon transmundane wisdom in stating that the noble truth of suffering is what should be fully known; the Abhidharma relies, in general, upon both mundane and transmundane wisdom, stating that every dharma whatsoever is what is to be fully known — and exactly as with mundane and transmundane, contaminated and undefiled, bound and freed, joined and unjoined, so too should this be understood in the corresponding manner. Further, the scripture relies solely upon the wisdom of near full knowledge in stating that the noble truth of suffering is what should be fully known; the Abhidharma relies upon the wisdom of both near and distant full knowledge in stating that every dharma whatsoever is what is to be fully known — and exactly as with near and distant, so too should proximate pressure and non-proximate pressure, joined and unjoined, be understood in the corresponding manner. Further, the scripture relies solely upon the wisdom that observes the shared characteristic in stating that the noble truth of suffering is what should be fully known; the Abhidharma relies, in general, upon the wisdom that observes both the particular and the shared characteristic, stating that every dharma whatsoever is what is to be fully known — and exactly as with the wisdom of the particular and the shared characteristic, so too should the cognition of the particular and the shared characteristic, and the mental application toward the particular and the shared characteristic, be understood in the corresponding manner. Further, the scripture relies solely upon wisdom not shared with outsiders in stating that the noble truth of suffering is what should be fully known; the Abhidharma relies, in general, upon wisdom both shared and not shared with outsiders, stating that every dharma whatsoever is what is to be fully known. Further, the scripture speaks with reference to the time of direct realization in stating that the noble truth of suffering is what should be fully known; the Abhidharma speaks with reference to the time of practicing the truths in stating that every dharma whatsoever is what is to be fully known. Further, the scripture speaks with reference to provisional cognition in stating that the noble truth of suffering is what should be fully known; the Abhidharma speaks with reference to ultimate cognition in stating that every dharma whatsoever is what is to be fully known. One asks: what is the meaning of provisional cognition? The answer: relying on the fruit's own coarse and evident, easily seen nature, it is provisionally said that suffering should be fully known; relying on the fact that the cause of birth-and-death does not continue, it is provisionally said that accumulation should be forever cut off; relying on the fact that it possesses both qualities and is not present within the body, it is provisionally said that cessation should be verified; relying on the fact that it is the path capable of forever cutting off every affliction, it is provisionally said that the path should be cultivated — such, then, is named the meaning of provisional cognition, provisional names being established, not because they exhaust the principle. The Venerable Pārśva says: the World-Honored One spoke only of suffering as what should be fully known, since one might otherwise suppose that suffering alone is what should be fully known, and it is for this reason that, within the Abhidharma, it is stated that every dharma whatsoever is what is to be fully known.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The final distinction — between provisional and ultimate cognition — is this passage's own frank admission that scripture sometimes teaches through deliberately simplified, pedagogically convenient formulations rather than the complete truth, and that the Abhidharma's own job is precisely to supply the fuller account scripture leaves provisional, without thereby accusing scripture of being wrong.

2. Fourteen More Reasons Full-Knowing Suffering Matters

An extraordinary cascade of further reasons piles up for why the Buddha singles out suffering alone. Full-knowing suffering severs the very root of the whole causal chain — self-view begets the sixty-two views, which beget every affliction, which begets karma, which begets ripening, which begets the entire wheel of birth and death — and cuts off the five views of self and fifteen views of what belongs to self at a stroke. Sentient beings cling to the aggregates exactly as an infant clings to a wet-nurse who strikes and scolds him; only full-knowing suffering breaks this clinging, straightens mind and mental factors bent crooked toward their object since beginningless time, and lets a practitioner answer without hesitation that the aggregates are solely suffering, impermanent, impure, and without self, exactly like a burning-hot ball of iron, a heap of filth, an empty aggregation of formations. The aggregates themselves are likened to a sickness, a boil, an arrow, a blade, poison, fire, an enemy, and a border town forever assailed by karmic and afflictive bandits — and one who fully knows suffering is named one who has, for the first time, truly encountered the Buddha's own appearance in the world.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The infant-and-wet-nurse image is this passage's sharpest psychological observation: it captures something genuinely uncomfortable about ordinary attachment — that clinging to the very source of one's own suffering, rather than to something genuinely benign, is not an aberration but the normal condition of an unawakened being, exactly as an infant's attachment to a sometimes-harsh caregiver is neither irrational nor unusual, simply unexamined.

3. Why Accumulation Alone "Should Be Forever Cut Off"

Paralleling the truth of suffering, the Abhidharma states that every contaminated dharma whatsoever, not craving alone, should be forever cut off — yet the Buddha speaks only of accumulation, since it is by cutting off the cause that the fruit of suffering, too, is genuinely relinquished, exactly as damming a stream's own source is the only way to truly halt its flow, and exactly as cutting the cord of a heavy burden, rather than merely wishing it away, is the only way to actually set it down. The outsiders are said to resemble a foolish dog that chases the clod of earth thrown at it rather than the one who threw it, wearied by suffering's own fruit yet never touching its cause — while cutting off accumulation forever ends the lower, middling, and upper fruit of the three realms, the three and four and five kinds of suffering that accumulation alone generates and grows.

6. The Sixteen Aspects: How Many Real Substances?

The sixteen classic aspects of contemplation — four taken toward each truth, suffering through no-self, cause through condition, cessation through freedom, path through deliverance — raise the question of how many genuinely distinct substances underlie these sixteen names. One view holds there are only seven real substances, since the four aspects of suffering alone map onto four distinct substances, being the near antidote to the four classical distortions, while each remaining truth's own four aspects share but a single underlying substance. The compilers reject this reduction outright: name and substance, provisional name and provisional substance, run together in exact correspondence throughout — sixteen names, sixteen substances, no fewer. The aspect's own-nature, moreover, is wisdom itself, which is both what actively engages its object and what is itself engaged, associated mental factors being engaged without themselves being the aspect, and dissociated dharmas being merely what is engaged, neither the aspect nor what actively engages.

7. Etymologies of the Sixteen Aspects, Twice Over

Each of the sixteen aspects receives not one but two layered etymologies. Suffering wounds and oppresses like a heavy burden; impermanence follows from a conditioned dharma's single moment of activity, or from its utter dependence on a convergence of conditions; emptiness and no-self both counter the view of self, the second time glossed as the absence of any free agent, doer, or experiencer whatsoever. Cause is like a seed, accumulation causes an equal manifestation, arising causes continuity, condition accomplishes something exactly as clay, wheel, cord, and water together accomplish a jar. Cessation exhausts the grasping-aggregates without remainder; calm stills the conditioned characteristic and the three fires alike; sublimity is being wholesome and permanent, or freed from every calamity; freedom is being utterly tranquil, itself a departure rather than something merely departing, or emerging from every fault. The path harms the deviant path and is the very road of emergence; suchness accords with correct principle; conduct proceeds toward nirvāṇa's own palace or simply proceeds correctly forward; and deliverance forever transcends birth and death.

9. The Four Heavenly Kings and the Buddha's Many Languages

A story from the Vinaya raises a puzzle about the Buddha's own linguistic power: teaching the four heavenly kings the truths first in the noble language, only two understood; switching to a border-country vernacular, a third understood; only a Mleccha tongue reached the fourth. Yet a celebrated verse claims the Buddha speaks with a single voice that every listener, of every nation and disposition, hears as uniquely his own language and his own needed teaching. Several resolutions are offered: the four kings' own differing intentions required differing speech to fulfill each one; the Buddha wished to display his mastery of every tongue, having some converted through his unaltered speech and others only through his altered speech; or, more radically, even the Buddha's own supernormal power cannot override the objective domain itself, exactly as he cannot make the ear see color — the verse being mere devotional excess, not literal Tripiṭaka doctrine, or else explained by the Buddha's speech being so swift in switching between languages that its many tongues are perceived, like a whirling firebrand, as a single continuous voice.

10. "I Had Never Heard This Before": The Three Faculties

A foundational scripture has the Buddha declare, of each truth in turn, that he had never heard it before, that it should be known by wisdom, and that it has now already been known — three formulas the treatise maps onto the three progressive faculties of the path: "I shall come to know what is not yet known," "having already come to know," and "having come to know fully."

Chinese Source

11. Dharmatrāta's Shocked Reaction, and Reversing the Scripture

The Great Virtuous Dharmatrāta confesses that this same scripture makes his whole body's hair stand on end: it speaks of the faculty of "having come to know fully" before the faculty of "coming to know what is not yet known" — a sequence that would require the very first undefiled faculty to arise all over again after full knowledge had already been attained, which no Buddha, solitary-realizer, or disciple could possibly undergo, and yet the scripture cannot simply be discarded, since it converted the five monks and eighty thousand gods at the very founding of the teaching. His own resolution is not to abandon the scripture but to silently reorder its wording, restoring what he takes to be its true, intended sequence. The Abhidharma masters push back firmly: countless treatise-masters of greater learning than Dharmatrāta himself never dared reverse this scripture's own wording, and one should instead recognize two entirely legitimate kinds of sequence — the sequence proper to expounding the dharma, which the scripture itself follows, and the sequence proper to direct realization, which Dharmatrāta's own reordering captures. Pārśva offers a still different resolution: the scripture does not describe the three undefiled faculties at all, but the Bodhisattva's own hearing-and-reflection wisdom beneath the bodhi tree, sufficient to subjugate every delusion regarding the truths even before full enlightenment, exactly as a face once covered by a damp, obstructive hide may later be covered by only the thinnest silk veil.

12. Three Turnings, Twelve Aspects: Eye, Knowledge, Brightness, Awareness

A further formula has the Buddha declare his own eye, knowledge, brightness, and awareness arising through "three turnings and twelve aspects" with respect to the four truths — a number that seems to demand twelve turnings and forty-eight aspects across all four truths together, yet never exceeds three turnings and twelve aspects in total, exactly as a stream-enterer is bound by at most seven further rebirths regardless of how that number is reached. The eye is the forbearance of dharma-knowledge; the knowledge is the various dharma-knowledges themselves; the brightness is the forbearance of subsequent-application knowledge; the awareness is the various knowledges of subsequent application — eye as the meaning of observing, knowledge as resolving, brightness as illuminating, awareness as alert inspection.

13. A Closing Tetralemma: Cutting Off by Own-Nature or by Object

The fascicle closes with a systematic tetralemma on how the four truths are cut off: some dharmas are cut off by own-nature but not by way of their object — taking the undefiled truths of suffering and accumulation as object, or any contaminated dharma lacking an object of its own; some are cut off by way of their object but not by own-nature — taking the contaminated noble path as object; some are cut off both ways at once — taking the contaminated truths of suffering and accumulation as object; and some are cut off neither way — taking the undefiled noble path as object, or the truth of cessation, which possesses no object of its own at all.
