Beneath the Bodhi Tree, Forward and Backward

Fascicle 23 closes the previous nyāsa's final combinatorial threads before opening the Person Nyāsa, the third major investigation of the Miscellaneous Chapter — asking how many of the twelve links of dependent origination, traversed by a single person within a single life, belong to the past, the future, and the present, and refuting the view that dependent origination itself is unconditioned. The fascicle works through Śrīlāta's single-moment killer, the Prajñaptiśāstra's precise embryonic stages, and the Buddha's own bodhi-tree contemplation traced both forward and backward. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 23 opens mid-argument, closing fascicle 22's own investigation into minds possessing latent tendency, before opening an entirely new nyāsa — the Person Nyāsa — within the Miscellaneous Chapter. Terms established in earlier fascicles, such as the six causes, the four conditions, and the two-factor grid of increase and association, recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Bodily Realization Versus Wisdom's Seeing: The Final Tetralemma

Fascicle 23 opens by closing out fascicle 22's own investigation with one final tetralemma: does bodily realizing a latent tendency's cessation always coincide with wisdom directly seeing that cessation? The two can come apart in every possible combination across the stages of freeing oneself from each realm's defilement and each noble truth's own direct realization, before the treatise re-derives the identical four alternatives at the finest possible grain, a single moment such as attaining the fruit of once-returning through the very knowledge of cessation.

Chinese Source

頗有於隨眠滅身作證而慧不見彼滅耶。答應作四句。有於隨眠滅身作證而慧不見彼滅。謂諸異生離欲界乃至無所有處染時。所有自地自品諸隨眠滅。若諸聖者苦現觀時。見苦所斷諸隨眠滅。集現觀時見集所斷諸隨眠滅。道現觀時見道所斷諸隨眠滅。修道位中以苦集道及世俗智隨其所應。離欲界乃至非想非非想處染時。所有自地自品修所斷諸隨眠滅。若以苦集道及世俗智隨其所應得果及練根成時所證已斷諸隨眠滅。有慧見彼滅而身不作證。謂滅現觀時見苦集道及修所斷諸隨眠滅。修道位中若以滅智離欲界乃至非想非非想處染時。除得果位。隨現所觀已斷未斷諸隨眠滅。及餘一切異生聖者不證擇滅而見滅時。即彼所見諸隨眠滅。有於隨眠滅身作證慧亦見彼滅。謂滅現觀時見滅所斷諸隨眠滅。修道位中若以滅智離欲界乃至非想非非想處染時。所證所觀諸隨眠滅若以滅智練根成時。所證所觀諸隨眠滅。有於隨眠滅身不作證慧亦不見彼滅。謂除前相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Is there a case of a latent tendency's cessation being bodily realized while wisdom does not see that cessation? The answer: four alternatives should be formulated. There is a case of a latent tendency's cessation being bodily realized while wisdom does not see that cessation — namely, when an ordinary being becomes free of the desire realm's defilement, up through the sphere of nothingness's defilement, the cessation of the own-stage's own grade of latent tendency; or, when noble ones directly realize the truth of suffering, the cessation of the latent tendencies abandoned by seeing suffering; upon directly realizing the truth of the origin, the cessation of the latent tendencies abandoned by seeing the origin; upon directly realizing the truth of the path, the cessation of the latent tendencies abandoned by seeing the path; within the path of cultivation, through the dharma-knowledge of suffering, the origin, and the path, and through worldly conventional knowledge, when becoming free, as appropriate, of the desire realm's defilement, up through the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception's defilement, the cessation of the own-stage's own grade of latent tendency abandoned by cultivation; or, through the dharma-knowledge of suffering, the origin, and the path, and through worldly conventional knowledge, when attaining a fruit or sharpening the faculties, as appropriate, the cessation of the already-cut-off latent tendency thereby realized. There is a case where wisdom sees that cessation while it is not bodily realized — namely, at the direct realization of cessation, the cessation of the latent tendencies abandoned by seeing suffering, the origin, the path, and cultivation; within the path of cultivation, through the knowledge of cessation, when becoming free of the desire realm's defilement, up through the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception's defilement, excluding the stage of attaining a fruit, the cessation of whatever latent tendency, cut off or not yet cut off, is presently being contemplated; and every other ordinary being or noble one who, without realizing a non-selective cessation, sees a cessation, the cessation of whatever latent tendency is thereby seen. There is a case of a latent tendency's cessation being bodily realized while wisdom also sees that cessation — namely, at the direct realization of cessation, the cessation of the latent tendencies abandoned by seeing cessation; within the path of cultivation, through the knowledge of cessation, when becoming free of the desire realm's defilement, up through the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception's defilement, the cessation of the latent tendency both realized and contemplated; or, through the knowledge of cessation, when sharpening the faculties, the cessation of the latent tendency both realized and contemplated. There is a case of a latent tendency's cessation being neither bodily realized nor seen by wisdom — namely, every case excluded from those just described.

頗有一剎那頃於隨眠滅身作證而慧不見彼滅耶。答應作四句。謂以滅法智得一來果時。有於隨眠滅身作證而慧不見彼滅。謂爾時色無色界見所斷諸隨眠滅。有慧見彼滅而身不作證。謂欲界修所斷後三品諸隨眠滅。有於隨眠滅身作證慧亦見彼滅。謂欲界一切見所斷及修所斷前六品諸隨眠滅。有於隨眠滅身不作證慧亦不見彼滅。謂色無色界修所斷諸隨眠滅。如以滅法智得一來果時。一剎那頃有四句。如是以滅智預流一來或不還者轉根時。一剎那頃皆得作四句。

One asks: is there a case, within the span of a single moment, of a latent tendency's cessation being bodily realized while wisdom does not see that cessation? The answer: four alternatives should be formulated. That is, at the moment of attaining the fruit of once-returning through the dharma-knowledge of cessation, there is a case of a latent tendency's cessation being bodily realized while wisdom does not see that cessation — namely, at that time, the cessation of the latent tendencies of the form and formless realms abandoned by seeing. There is a case where wisdom sees that cessation while it is not bodily realized — namely, the cessation of the desire realm's own latent tendencies abandoned by cultivation, of the latter three grades. There is a case of a latent tendency's cessation being bodily realized while wisdom also sees that cessation — namely, the cessation of every one of the desire realm's own latent tendencies abandoned by seeing, together with the latent tendencies abandoned by cultivation of the first six grades. There is a case of a latent tendency's cessation being neither bodily realized nor seen by wisdom — namely, the cessation of the form and formless realms' own latent tendencies abandoned by cultivation. Just as, at the moment of attaining the fruit of once-returning through the dharma-knowledge of cessation, all four alternatives occur within a single moment, so too, at the moment of sharpening one's faculties as a stream-enterer, a once-returner, or a non-returner through the knowledge of cessation, all four alternatives may likewise occur within a single moment.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Closing an investigation with the same two-scale verification method already seen elsewhere in this treatise — first the whole spiritual path, then a single moment — signals that this fascicle inherits fascicle 22's own rigor even while it is about to pivot to an entirely different, much larger topic: the treatise does not rush past unfinished business simply because a new subject is imminent.

2. A Consciousness Whose Cause and Object Are Both Severed

A precise technical term is defined: a “cause-and-object-severed consciousness” is a moment of mind whose associated latent tendency has been entirely cut off, and whose object-taken latent tendency has likewise been entirely cut off, while the mind's own substance persists uncut — illustrated by the exact moment the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering has arisen but the dharma-knowledge of the origin has not, when a mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing both suffering and the origin. Two rival schools then dispute exactly how many of this mind's own latent tendencies actually count as cut off at that moment, depending on whether a universally-pervasive latent tendency is held to serve as a non-pervasive cause within its own section, or as a pervasive cause there as well.

Chinese Source

云何因境斷識。答苦智已生集智未生。若心見集所斷見苦所斷緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

How is a cause-and-object-severed consciousness defined? The answer: this refers to a case where the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering has already arisen, but the dharma-knowledge of the origin has not yet arisen, and the mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing the origin and by seeing suffering.

是謂因境斷識。此中若識因斷境斷。自體未斷名因境斷識。諸有欲令遍行隨眠及彼相應俱有諸法於自部非遍行因者。彼作是說。苦智已生集智未生。若心見集所斷見苦所斷緣。此心因全斷境全斷。自體未斷故名因境斷識。爾時若心見集所斷見集滅道修所斷緣。此心因雖全斷而境未斷故。非因境斷識。爾時若心見集所斷見苦集滅道修所斷緣。此心因雖全斷而境有斷有未斷故。非因境斷識。諸有欲令遍行隨眠及彼相應俱有諸法亦作自部遍行因者。彼作是說。苦智已生集智未生。若心見集所斷見苦所斷緣。此心自部因雖未斷而他部因全斷境全斷。自體未斷故名因境斷識。爾時若心見集所斷見集滅道修所斷緣。此心因有斷有未斷境未斷故非因境斷識。爾時若心見集所斷見苦集滅道修所斷緣。此心因境俱有斷有未斷故非因境斷識。已顯因境斷識自性。次應顯彼隨眠隨增。問於此識有幾隨眠隨增耶。答十九。問一心耶。答不爾。其事云何。謂未離欲染。苦法智已生集法智未生。若心欲界見集所斷見苦所斷緣。此因境斷識。

This, then, is what is meant by a cause-and-object-severed consciousness. Here, if a consciousness's own cause is severed, and its object is severed, while its own substance is not yet severed, it is called a cause-and-object-severed consciousness. As for those masters who hold that a universally-pervasive latent tendency and its associated and co-existent dharmas do not serve as a non-pervasive cause within their own section, they explain it in this way: when the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering has already arisen but the dharma-knowledge of the origin has not yet arisen, if a mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing the origin and by seeing suffering, this mind's own cause is entirely severed and its object is entirely severed, while its own substance is not yet severed, and it is therefore called a cause-and-object-severed consciousness. At that time, if a mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing the origin, cessation, the path, and cultivation, this mind's cause, though entirely severed, has an object not yet severed, and so it is not a cause-and-object-severed consciousness. At that time, if a mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing suffering, the origin, cessation, the path, and cultivation, this mind's cause, though entirely severed, has an object partly severed and partly not, and so it is not a cause-and-object-severed consciousness. As for those masters who hold that a universally-pervasive latent tendency and its associated and co-existent dharmas do also serve as their own section's own pervasive cause, they explain it in this way: when the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering has already arisen but the dharma-knowledge of the origin has not yet arisen, if a mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing the origin and by seeing suffering, this mind's own-section cause is not yet severed, but its other-section cause is entirely severed, and its object is entirely severed, while its own substance is not yet severed, and it is therefore called a cause-and-object-severed consciousness. At that time, if a mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing the origin, cessation, the path, and cultivation, this mind's cause is partly severed and partly not, with an object not yet severed, and so it is not a cause-and-object-severed consciousness. At that time, if a mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing suffering, the origin, cessation, the path, and cultivation, this mind's cause and object are both partly severed and partly not, and so it is not a cause-and-object-severed consciousness. Its own substance has now been displayed; next, that latent tendency's own increasing should be displayed. One asks: within this consciousness, how many latent tendencies increase? The answer: nineteen. One asks: is this a single mind? The answer: it is not. What, then, is the matter? That is, in one not yet free of desire's own defilement, once the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering has arisen but the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of the origin has not yet arisen, if a mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing the origin, within the desire realm, and by seeing suffering, this is a cause-and-object-severed consciousness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The very existence of a technical term for “cause and object both severed, substance not yet severed” reveals how finely this treatise slices apart what might look, from a distance, like a single unified fact about a moment of mind — a consciousness can be simultaneously alive (its own substance persisting) and doubly bereaved (both its associational and its representational ties to affliction already cut), and Sarvāstivāda insists on a dedicated name for exactly this combination rather than folding it into some coarser, more familiar category.

3. Nineteen Latent Tendencies, Mapped Across Three Realms

Working out precisely which latent tendencies increase within this cause-and-object-severed consciousness, the treatise counts nineteen in total — the desire realm's own seven latent tendencies abandoned by seeing the origin (four through both association and object-taking, three through object-taking alone), and the form and formless realms' own six apiece, following the identical pattern once a being has become free of the respective lower realm's defilement.

Chinese Source

欲界見集所斷七隨眠隨增。四於此識相應所緣隨眠隨增。謂邪見見取疑無明。三於此識唯有所緣隨眠隨增。謂愛恚慢。已離欲染未離色染。苦類智已生集類智未生。若心色界見集所斷見苦所斷緣。此因境斷識。色界見集所斷六隨眠隨增。四於此識相應所緣隨眠隨增。如欲界說。二於此識唯有所緣隨眠隨增。謂愛慢。問未離欲染者亦可爾。何故說已離欲染耶。答此中說。容現行者要離下地染。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this, the desire realm's own seven latent tendencies abandoned by seeing the origin increase — four of them increasing within this consciousness through both association and object-taking, namely wrong view, view-grasping, doubt, and ignorance; three of them increasing within this consciousness only through object-taking, namely craving, hatred, and conceit. For one already free of desire's own defilement but not yet free of the form realm's own defilement, once the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering in the class-of-similar-kind has arisen but the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of the origin has not yet arisen, if a mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing the origin, within the form realm, and by seeing suffering, this is a cause-and-object-severed consciousness. The form realm's own six latent tendencies abandoned by seeing the origin increase — four of them increasing within this consciousness through both association and object-taking, exactly as stated for the desire realm; two of them increasing within this consciousness only through object-taking, namely craving and conceit. One asks: this ought to hold equally for one not yet free of desire's own defilement — why, then, does the treatise speak of one already free of desire's own defilement? The answer: this passage speaks of what can actually manifest, and only after becoming free of a lower stage's own defilement

上地煩惱方得現行故作是說。已離色染。苦類智已生集類智未生。

can a higher stage's own affliction actually manifest — it is for this reason that the treatise speaks in this way. For one already free of the form realm's own defilement, once the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering in the class-of-similar-kind has arisen but the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of the origin has not yet arisen,

若心無色界見集所斷見苦所斷緣。此因境斷識。無色界見集所斷六隨眠隨增。四於此識相應所緣隨眠隨增。二於此識唯有所緣隨眠隨增。如色界說。此中有誦已離色染未離無色染。彼不應誦說。集類智未生已顯未離無色染故。問修所斷中亦可建立因境斷識。謂已斷上上品乃至下中品者。所未斷八品乃至一品心。此心緣前已斷一品乃至八品。前一品乃至八品因已斷故。亦應名為因境斷識。何故不說。答亦應說而不說者。當知此義有餘。復次若異部識異部為因異部為境而因境斷體未斷者。此中說之。修所斷心自部為因自部為境。雖因境斷而不說為因境斷識。復次此中因者說遍行因。唯染污故餘因不定。此中識者唯說隨眠相應諸心唯染污故。由此所說因境斷識。唯在三界見集所斷。

if a mind takes as its object what is abandoned by seeing the origin, within the formless realm, and by seeing suffering, this is a cause-and-object-severed consciousness. The formless realm's own six latent tendencies abandoned by seeing the origin increase — four of them increasing within this consciousness through both association and object-taking, and two of them increasing within this consciousness only through object-taking, exactly as stated for the form realm. There is a recited variant here that reads "already free of the form realm's own defilement but not yet free of the formless realm's own defilement" — this recitation ought not to be maintained, since the phrase "the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of the origin in the class-of-similar-kind has not yet arisen" has already made clear that one is not yet free of the formless realm's own defilement. One asks: a cause-and-object-severed consciousness ought also to be establishable within the section abandoned by cultivation — that is, for one who has already cut off the ninth grade of affliction, up through the first grade, whose mind takes as its object the preceding one grade, up through eight grades, already cut off, this mind, too, ought to be called a cause-and-object-severed consciousness, since the preceding one grade, up through eight grades, has already had its own cause cut off — why, then, does the treatise not speak of this? The answer: it ought to be spoken of but is not, and this should be understood as a matter this discussion leaves incomplete. Further, this present passage speaks of a case where a mind of one section serves as cause and a mind of a different section serves as object, and where the cause and object are severed while the substance is not — this is the case spoken of here; a mind abandoned by cultivation, though its own section serves as cause and its own section serves as object, and though its cause and object are severed while its substance is not, is not spoken of as a cause-and-object-severed consciousness here. Further, "cause" here refers specifically to the universally-pervasive-cause, since this cause is defiled alone, while every other cause is not fixed to defilement in this way; "consciousness" here refers specifically to minds associated with a latent tendency, since these are defiled alone — and it is on this account that a cause-and-object-severed consciousness, as spoken of here, occurs only within the three realms' own sections abandoned by seeing the origin.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The treatise's own careful note that this analysis only holds for one already free of a lower realm's defilement — since a higher stage's affliction can never manifest until the lower stage beneath it has first been overcome — quietly reaffirms a cosmological rule this nyāsa has relied on since much earlier fascicles: spiritual progress is strictly sequential, bottom to top, and no combinatorial catalogue of latent tendencies can ever license skipping a stage.

4. Why Cause Comes Before Latent Tendency: Fourteen Things Every Master Must Know

A brief methodological aside explains why the treatise's own topic sentence names “cause” before naming “latent tendency”: understanding the Abhidharma's own treasury correctly requires mastery of fourteen distinct matters — the six causes, the four conditions, association, and possession and non-possession — or, on an alternative reckoning, seven matters of skill spanning cause, condition, own- and shared-characteristic, inclusion, association, and possession. Whoever masters the Abhidharma through these matters, not merely by reciting its text, earns the name of a true Abhidharma master.

Chinese Source

問何故因後說隨眠耶。答以阿毘達磨藏義應以十四事覺知。謂六因四緣攝相應成就不成就若以如是十四事。覺知阿毘達磨無錯謬者。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: why does the treatise speak of the cause before speaking of the latent tendency? The answer: because the meaning of the Abhidharma treasury ought to be understood through fourteen matters — namely, the six causes, the four conditions, association, possession, and non-possession — and one who understands the Abhidharma without error through these fourteen matters

名阿毘達磨師。非但誦持文者。有餘師說。應以七事覺知阿毘達磨藏義。謂因善巧緣善巧。自相善巧。共相善巧。攝不攝善巧。相應不相應善巧成就不成就善巧。若以如是七事覺知阿毘達磨無錯謬者。名阿毘達磨師非但誦持文者。是故因後說諸隨眠於義無失。

is called a master of the Abhidharma, not merely one who recites its text. There are yet other masters who say: the meaning of the Abhidharma treasury ought to be understood through seven matters — namely, skill in cause, skill in condition, skill in own-characteristic, skill in shared characteristic, skill in inclusion and non-inclusion, skill in association and non-association, and skill in possession and non-possession — and one who understands the Abhidharma without error through these seven matters is called a master of the Abhidharma, not merely one who recites its text. It is for this reason that the treatise speaks of the cause before speaking of the various latent tendencies, this being sound in principle.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This aside functions as something like a professional credential requirement stated in passing: reciting the Abhidharma's own words, however faithfully, does not make one a master of it, and the deliberate ordering of “cause” before “latent tendency” in this treatise's own topic sentences is offered as a small, concrete demonstration of exactly the kind of structural fluency — knowing which of the fourteen or seven matters governs a given passage — that separates genuine mastery from mere memorization.

5. A New Nyāsa Opens: Is Dependent Origination Itself Unconditioned?

Fascicle 23 now opens an entirely new investigation, the third nyāsa of the Miscellaneous Chapter, called the Person Nyāsa — beginning with the question of how many of the twelve links of dependent origination, traversed by a single person within a single life, belong to the past, the future, and the present. The treatise composes this nyāsa explicitly to refute two rival positions: that the past and future have no real substance while the present, though real, is somehow unconditioned; and, more strikingly, the Vibhajyavādin claim that dependent origination itself is unconditioned, grounded in scripture's own statement that “whether the Tathāgata arises in the world or not, the dharma abides, the dharma-nature persists.”

Chinese Source

大毘婆沙論雜蘊第一中補特伽羅納息第三之一一補特伽羅。於此生十二支緣起。幾過去幾未來幾現在。如是等章及解章義既領會已。次應廣釋。問何故作此論。答為止他宗顯正義故謂或有執過去未來體非實有。現在雖有而是無為。為止彼宗顯過去未來體是實有。現在是有為世所攝故。或復有執。緣起是無為如分別論者。問彼因何故作如是執。答彼因經故。謂契經說。如來出世若不出世法住法性。佛自等覺為他開示乃至廣說。故知緣起是無為法。為止彼宗顯示緣起是有為法墮三世故。無無為法墮在三世。

Ru-Yi Translation

Mahāvibhāṣā Śāstra, Miscellaneous Chapter, the Person Nyāsa, third nyāsa, part one. A single person, within this one life, undergoes the twelve-linked dependent origination — how many links are past, how many future, how many present? Having thus grasped this chapter heading and its meaning, we should now explain it at length. One asks: why does the treatise take up this topic? The answer: in order to refute a rival school and manifest the correct principle. That is, some maintain that the past and future possess no real substance at all, and that, although the present does exist, it is unconditioned — in order to refute this, the treatise shows that the past and future genuinely possess real substance, and that the present is itself conditioned, included within the three time-periods, since no unconditioned dharma ever falls within any of the three time-periods. Others maintain that dependent origination is itself unconditioned, just as the Vibhajyavādins hold. One asks: on what basis do they maintain this position? 6. The Five Aggregates Are Always Impermanent Too: Reconciling the Unconditioned-Sounding Verse

The treatise's own resolution reads the “dharma abides, the dharma-nature persists” formula as describing only the fixed, determinate relationship between cause and effect — ignorance is always, without exception, the formations' own cause, whether a Buddha ever appears to teach it or not — never the unconditioned status of the causal chain itself. A decisive parallel passage clinches the point: scripture uses this very same “whether the Tathāgata arises or not” formula to describe form's own permanent characteristic of impermanence, and earth's permanent characteristic of hardness — no one would conclude from this that the five aggregates or the four great elements are themselves unconditioned, and dependent origination's own case is exactly the same.

7. One Person, One Life, One Present: Five Preliminary Clarifications

Before the treatise's own numerical answer can even be stated, five preliminary matters require clarification: why the topic sentence speaks of only a single person (to avoid needless repetition, since one example implies the rest); what kind of single person is meant (specifically one who traverses every one of the twelve links in strict succession, like climbing a staircase, illustrated by the parallel case of a learner who progresses through every meditative attainment in sequence, and by a woman's corpse observed decaying stage by stage in a charnel-ground meditation); why it speaks of only this present life (since stating the present implies the same for past and future); what “this life” means (one single instance of the class of homogeneous being); and what “present” means (the class of being's own current existence, neither a single citta-moment nor a mere positional stage).

8. Two Past, Two Future, Eight Present: Why This Asymmetric Count?

The treatise's own long-awaited numerical answer finally arrives: ignorance and the formations are past; birth and aging-and-death are future; the remaining eight links — consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense fields, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, and becoming — are present. Since, in principle, every link could occur in any time-period, the treatise explains this asymmetric count as accommodating one lacking the wisdom-eye, who can only infer the future fruit from present craving, clinging, and becoming, and the past cause from present consciousness through feeling — with five further explanatory angles offered besides, refuting a beginningless-now-existing view of saṃsāra, refuting its eventual annihilation, and establishing the continuous chain of cause and effect that lets ordinary beings actually accomplish their own task of liberation.

9. Provisional Versus Definitive: Reconciling the Prakaraṇapāda's Simpler Answer

A now-familiar comparison distinguishes this treatise's own fuller account from the Prakaraṇapāda's terser definition of dependent origination as simply “every conditioned dharma” — provisional versus definitive meaning, worldly convention versus ultimate truth, restricted to dharmas belonging to a sentient being versus dharmas of every kind, restricted to what possesses faculties, mind, and appropriation versus what may lack any of these. A fourfold taxonomy of dependent origination itself follows — momentary, connected in succession, positional, and far-extending — with this treatise speaking only of the positional and far-extending kinds, leaving the momentary and connected-succession kinds to the Prakaraṇapāda's own account.

10. Why the Buddha Spoke Only of a Sentient Being's Own Dependent Origination

A further question presses on why this treatise, following scripture itself, restricts dependent origination specifically to dharmas belonging to a sentient being, excluding inanimate matter and non-sentient dharmas from this particular teaching. The Buddha's own reason, the treatise explains, was calibrated to his actual audience's own readiness to hear precisely this teaching, and to the deepest sense of the very word “existence” (bhava) itself — sentient beings alone, revolving ceaselessly through the three states of existence since beginningless time, fully embody what “existence” most essentially means, and it is specifically the deluded clinging to sentient-being dharmas as a self that drives the entire cycle of suffering the Buddha's teaching exists to dissolve.

11. The Five Aggregates Themselves Are Dependent Origination's Own Substance

A concise answer settles what dependent origination actually, substantially is: the five aggregates in the desire and form realms, or the four aggregates in the formless realm, constitute its very substance — self-nature, own substance, characteristic component, and fundamental original nature all naming this same underlying reality.

12. Why 'Equal'? Four Senses of a Single Word

The treatise's own etymology of “dependent origination” cycles through several proposed meanings — arising in dependence on a condition, possessing the very nature required to so arise, arising distinctly through one's own particular combination of conditions — before settling on “equal” as the most textured sense: equal not because every dharma needs the identical number of conditions (mind needs four, the concentration of cessation needs three, bare form needs only two), but because whatever truly needs a given number always receives exactly that number, never more or less; equal also because every dharma but one's own self-nature serves as its dominant-condition, and because every sentient being carries the identical set of twelve links from beginningless time until final liberation, regardless of when that liberation actually arrives.

14. One Moment or Twelve Stages? Śrīlāta's Killer and the Prajñaptiśāstra's Precise Boundaries

18. Why Even Spontaneously-Born Beings Still Pass Through 'Name-and-Form'

A final cluster of puzzles closes the fascicle: since name-and-form conditioning the six sense fields seems to describe only gradual embryonic development, how can it apply to beings born through instantaneous spontaneous transformation, whose faculties arise all at once? The treatise resolves this by relocating the name-and-form stage from physical incompleteness to functional immaturity — even a spontaneously-born being's faculties, though complete from the very first moment, remain merely present rather than sharp and keen until a brief further interval passes, and it is this functional threshold, not physical completeness, that marks the boundary between the name-and-form and six-sense-fields stages.
