Fascicle 44 · 阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論卷第四十四 · Complete

The Wound Covered by Mindfulness

Fascicle 44 continues the Volition Nyāsa with the future Buddha's own candid account of the sensual, hostile, and harmful thoughts that arose even on the eve of his enlightenment, then turns to a direct confrontation with the Mahāsāṃghikas over whether the Buddha's own body is contaminated, an extended meditation on the many hidden meanings within the word for discipline, and a scriptural deity's own memorable question — with what could one possibly cover a festering wound? — answered simply: with correct mindfulness and correct awareness. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Sensual Thought: Self-Harm and Harming Another

Fascicle 44 opens the Volition Nyāsa's third part with the Buddha's own frank admission that, before his enlightenment, he too gave rise to sensual, hostile, and harmful thoughts — though heedfully, recognizing them as capable of self-harm, harming another, or harming both. The treatise begins working through sensual thought specifically: its self-harming form burns and wearies body and mind, ripening into an unwanted destiny; its other-harming form is illustrated through the case of a man gazing at another's wife, whose husband's own resulting anger and grief is the injury inflicted, even though the gazer himself does wrong.

Chinese Source

如契經說。若起欲尋恚尋害尋。或自害。或害他。或俱害。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為廣分別契經義故。謂契經說佛告苾芻。我未證得三菩提時。或起欲尋恚尋害尋。或起出離尋。無恚尋無害尋。雖起欲尋恚尋害尋而不放逸。便作是念。若起欲尋恚尋害尋。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Grounding a philosophical distinction (self-harm versus harming another) in such a plainly recognizable social scenario — a man's covetous glance provoking another man's jealous rage — is a deliberate move toward moral realism: the treatise isn't interested in abstract harm alone, but in tracing exactly how one person's inner unwholesome state becomes another person's genuine suffering, even without any direct physical act connecting them.

2. Sensual Thought Harming Both, and Hostile Thought's Own Self-Harm

The treatise completes sensual thought's threefold analysis with the case of outright adultery and its violent aftermath, explaining that even though a wronged husband may retaliate and himself suffer for it, this is still called "mutual harm" rather than a threefold harm, since he too counts simply as "another" within the framework. Hostile thought's own self-harming form then opens, following the identical burning, wearying pattern already established for sensual thought.

3. Hostile and Harmful Thought Complete the Set, and Their Underlying Nature

The treatise completes hostile thought's own threefold analysis and works through harmful thought in the identical pattern, before asking what these three unwholesome thoughts are actually made of at bottom: sensual thought is investigative thought conjoined with craving, hostile thought investigative thought conjoined with hatred — and harmful thought receives multiple competing explanations, one tying it to a portion of hatred (the wish to strike rather than to kill), another to a portion of ignorance.

Splitting hatred itself into two distinct sub-varieties — the wish to end a life versus the merely lesser wish to strike or bind — in order to explain the difference between hostile thought and harmful thought shows this taxonomy's own characteristic instinct: rather than accepting a rough overlap between two named categories, it keeps drilling downward until it finds the precise structural seam that actually separates them.

4. The Verdict on Harmful Thought, and the Three Wholesome Thoughts

The treatise settles that harm is neither hatred nor ignorance but a genuinely distinct mental factor of its own, drawn forth by hatred yet not identical with it. It then introduces the three antidotal wholesome thoughts — renunciation, freedom from hostility, and freedom from harm — noting a structural asymmetry worth pausing over: unlike the three unwholesome thoughts, each with its own separate inherent nature, all three wholesome thoughts share one and the same substance, investigative thought conjoined with any wholesome mind whatsoever.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That affliction requires three separately-natured mental factors while its antidote gets by on a single shared one is a quietly optimistic structural fact buried inside this taxonomy: unwholesome states, on this account, are inherently more fragmented and specialized than wholesome ones, each vice needing its own dedicated machinery, while virtue operates through one unified capacity simply turned toward three different targets.

5. Same Substance, Different Function — and How a Future Buddha Could Still Give Rise to Bad Thoughts

The treatise explains how three seemingly separate wholesome thoughts can share one identical inherent nature: their distinction lies not in what they are but in what they oppose, functioning as the specific antidote to each of the three unwholesome thoughts in turn. This sets up a genuinely pointed question: scripture has the Buddha admit that, before his own awakening, he still gave rise to sensual, hostile, and harmful thought even while remaining heedful — how can heedfulness coexist with unwholesome thought at all? Vasumitra offers the first of several resolving explanations: heedfulness here means swiftly recognizing the thought as unwholesome and cultivating its antidote, not never giving rise to it in the first place.

Vasumitra's redefinition of heedfulness — not the absence of unwholesome thought but the speed and skill with which one recognizes and counters it — quietly reframes what spiritual maturity actually looks like at this stage of the path: the goal was never a mind so pure that affliction simply never arises, but a mind so well-trained that affliction, however it arises, is caught and corrected almost as soon as it appears.

6. Further Explanations: A Drop on Hot Iron, a Fire on One's Own Head

Two further masters add their own vivid images to the same puzzle: the Great Virtuous One compares the Bodhisattva's swift subduing of unwholesome thought to a single drop of water instantly vaporizing on red-hot iron, while the Venerable Pārśva compares his equally swift relinquishing of it to someone rushing to extinguish a fire that has caught on his own head — both images converging on the same point, that heedfulness is measured by the speed of response rather than by the complete absence of any provocation.

Both similes chosen here — vaporizing water, a burning head — share an urgency that a gentler image (a candle being calmly snuffed, say) would not convey: heedfulness, on this account, isn't a placid, unbothered equanimity but something closer to a practiced reflex, engaging with real speed and real stakes each time affliction actually arises.

7. Where Did the Bodhisattva Actually Give Rise to These Thoughts? The Crowd at Rājagṛha

The treatise turns to a genuinely narrative question: in what concrete circumstances did the future Buddha actually experience these three unwholesome thoughts? Pārśva offers a general answer — through the sheer force of habitual cause, wherever and whenever, with no fixed occasion required — before the treatise moves to specific scriptural anecdotes. The first: begging for alms at Rājagṛha, mobbed by an adoring crowd, the Bodhisattva first delights in their attention (sensual thought), then grows frustrated as they block his access to food (hostile thought), and briefly wishes them harm before catching himself in shame (harmful thought).

Chinese Source

13. Why These Two Thoughts Predominate, and a New Question: Knowledge or Object, Which Is More?

The treatise offers its own reasons why peace and seclusion specifically predominate at the moment of awakening — one counteracting the Bodhisattva's former life of householder pleasure, the other counteracting his fruitless years of austerity, and both together marking his own rejoicing in newly-won virtue alongside his wish to liberate others. An entirely new topic then opens: are there more instances of knowledge, or more instances of what knowledge takes as its object? The treatise refutes the Dārṣṭāntika claim that some knowledge takes no genuine object at all — knowledge of illusions, mirages, and phantom cities — insisting instead that every kind of knowledge takes a genuinely existent object.

16. Is the Buddha's Own Body Contaminated? A Direct Confrontation with the Mahāsāṃghikas

19. Nine Meanings of Śīla: Coolness, Sleep, Habit, Concentration, and the Crossing-Place

The treatise embarks on an extended etymological meditation on the word śīla (discipline) itself, drawing out no fewer than nine distinct senses buried within the single term — coolness, since discipline soothes what evil burns; peaceful sleep, since the disciplined dream well; habitual practice, since discipline is constant repetition of the wholesome; the attainment of concentration, since a disciplined mind settles easily; and a ford or crossing-place, illustrated through a verse describing discipline as the very landing where the noble bathe in the cool pool of the Dharma without ever getting wet.

The treatise turns to the companion term, defining "protection fulfilled" as the non-learner's own restraint of the senses — mindfulness and wisdom guarding the eye and every other faculty from giving rise to fault with respect to its object, exactly as a hook restrains a wild elephant from running loose. 22. A Deity's Warning: "Do Not Open Your Own Wound"

The treatise proves that mindfulness and awareness constitute the very substance of sense-restraint through a vivid scriptural exchange: a deity warns a monk not to expose his own festering wound, the monk replies that he will cover it, and, when the deity presses him on what could possibly cover so serious a wound, the monk answers simply — with correct mindfulness and correct awareness. The deity's approving reply ("this is indeed a true covering") settles the matter, and the treatise then resolves an apparent circularity in scripture's own claim that mindfulness and wisdom's own fulfillment causes the restraint of the faculties' fulfillment, by distinguishing mindfulness and wisdom as cause from that same restraint as their resulting fruit.

24. Rival Theories, and Two Brothers Who Settled the Debate

Several further, competing accounts of sense-restraint's own inherent nature are catalogued — heedfulness itself, the six constant abidings, the presence or absence of already-cut-off afflictions on the six faculties, and simply every wholesome or defiled dharma taken in bulk — before the treatise turns to two celebrated Kaśmīri arhat brothers, sons of a brahmin father, both masters of the three knowledges and the eight liberations, who proposed that restraint and non-restraint are simply dissociated formations in their own right, distinguished only by whether they accord with purity or with defilement.

25. The Verdict, and Mindfulness-Wisdom Under Yet Another Name: "Cutting-Off Restraint"
