Egg-Born Arhats and the Bodhisattva's Choice

Fascicle 120 surveys the four kinds of birth with a vivid catalog of egg-born arhats and a hungry ghost's devoured children, asks why the bodhisattva refused spontaneous birth for his own final existence, resolves a garuḍa's puzzling meal, and closes with an exhaustive matrix of body and karma. 1. Prātimokṣa vs. Dhyāna Restraint: Three Times, and Why All Buddhas' Restraint Is "Equal"

Fascicle 120 continues the Killing-of-Life Nyāsa with a fine distinction: prātimokṣa restraint is obtained only with respect to present beings, while dhyāna and undefiled restraint extend across the objects of all three times — reconciling an apparent contradiction in Dharmatrāta's treatise through the theory of implicit intent. This raises a genuine puzzle: since each buddha's own restraint covers a different, non-overlapping population of beings (many already having entered nirvāṇa by the time a later buddha arises), how can all buddhas' restraint be called "equal"? The answer relocates equality away from identical scope and onto three deeper respects — equal practice, equal benefit to beings, and equal Dharma-body.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: is prātimokṣa restraint obtained solely with respect to beings capable of being harmed, or is it also obtained with respect to what is incapable of being harmed? If it is obtained solely with respect to what is capable of being harmed, then this restraint ought to increase and decrease — that is, as beings pass from what is incapable of being harmed into what is capable of it, the restraint ought to increase, and as they pass from what is capable of being harmed into what is incapable of it, the restraint ought to decrease. Moreover, this restraint would then be received only with respect to a partial portion, yet the World-Honored One has said that this restraint is never received only in part. Moreover, this would validate the position of the Nirgranthas — for those outside-path teachers, in order to entice others, say things such as: "Welcome, good man — there are beings more than a hundred yojanas from here; if you give rise to a wholesome mind, cast aside your weapons, and vow never to harm them, you will thereby obtain the restraint of not killing with respect to them." But if it is said that restraint is also obtained with respect to what is incapable of being harmed, then how is the statement of the Dharmatrāta-treatise to be reconciled, where it says: moral discipline is obtained with respect to what is capable of being harmed, while loving-kindness is obtained with respect to both what is capable and what is incapable of being harmed? Answer: one should rather say that restraint is obtained with respect to both what is capable and what is incapable of being harmed. Question: if the first three objections are thereby well reconciled, how is the statement of the Dharmatrāta-treatise itself to be reconciled? Answer: one should know that that venerable one spoke with an implicit intent — just as the Buddha himself sometimes spoke the sūtras with an implicit intent — how much more so should that venerable one's own words be understood in this way. What is this implicit intent? That venerable one, by "what is capable of being harmed," means the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields of the present time, and by "what is incapable of being harmed," means those of the past and future; by "moral discipline" he means prātimokṣa restraint, and by "loving-kindness" he means the two restraints of dhyāna and the undefiled. He holds that prātimokṣa restraint is obtained only with respect to the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields belonging to present beings, not with respect to the past or future, since the past and future fall within the category of what is merely nominal dharma.

靜慮無漏二種律儀。通於三世蘊界處得。故彼所說理亦無違。是故別解脫律儀。與靜慮無漏二種律儀有差別相。謂別解脫律儀唯於現在有情處得。靜慮無漏二種律儀通於三世境界處得。別解脫律儀。

— whereas the two restraints of dhyāna and the undefiled are obtained with respect to the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields of all three times — hence his statement, too, is not contrary to reason. It is for this reason that prātimokṣa restraint has a mark distinguishing it from the two restraints of dhyāna and the undefiled: prātimokṣa restraint is obtained only with respect to present beings, while the two restraints of dhyāna and the undefiled are obtained with respect to the objects of all three times. Prātimokṣa restraint

通於業道根本加行後起處得。靜慮無漏二種律儀。唯於業道根本處得。由此差別應作四句。有蘊界處於彼得別解脫律儀。非靜慮無漏。謂現在業道加行後起。有蘊界處於彼得靜慮無漏律儀。非別解脫。謂過去未來根本業道。有蘊界處於彼得別解脫律儀。亦靜慮無漏。謂現在世根本業道。有蘊界處不從彼得別解脫律儀。亦非靜慮無漏。謂過去未來業道加行後起。問若別解脫律儀。唯於現在有情處得。非於去來蘊界處者。則諸如來應正等覺律儀不等。所以者何。過去諸佛出現世時。無量有情為律儀境。彼有情類已入涅槃。

— is further obtained with respect to the preparatory action and the after-arising of the root path of karma as well, whereas the two restraints of dhyāna and the undefiled are obtained only with respect to the root path of karma itself. On account of this distinction, a tetralemma should be constructed. There are aggregates, elements, and sense-fields with respect to which prātimokṣa restraint is obtained but not the restraints of dhyāna or the undefiled — namely, the preparatory action and after-arising of a present path of karma. There are aggregates, elements, and sense-fields with respect to which the restraints of dhyāna or the undefiled are obtained but not prātimokṣa restraint — namely, the root path of karma belonging to the past or future. There are aggregates, elements, and sense-fields with respect to which both prātimokṣa restraint and the restraints of dhyāna or the undefiled are obtained — namely, the root path of karma belonging to the present. And there are aggregates, elements, and sense-fields with respect to which neither prātimokṣa restraint nor the restraints of dhyāna or the undefiled are obtained — namely, the preparatory action and after-arising of a path of karma belonging to the past or future. Question: if prātimokṣa restraint is obtained only with respect to present beings, and not with respect to the aggregates and elements of the past or future, then the restraint of the various tathāgata-arhat-fully-awakened-ones would be unequal, for why is this? When buddhas of the past appeared in the world, countless beings served as the objects of their restraint, and those beings have since entered nirvāṇa —

釋迦牟尼於彼境上不得律儀。今釋迦佛出現世時。無量有情為律儀境。彼有情類已入涅槃。慈氏如來於彼境上不得律儀。境有寬狹律儀亦爾。豈非諸佛律儀不等。答應作是說。律儀境界雖有多少。而律儀體前後無異。俱從一切有情境處總發得故。有作是說。三世如來律儀不等亦無有失。問若爾施設論說當云何通。如彼說一切如來應正等覺皆悉平等。答由三事等故名平等。一修行等。謂諸如來皆於過去三無數劫。勤修四種波羅蜜多。究竟圓滿得菩提故。二利益等。謂諸如來等於無量應化有情。作利樂事皆究竟故。三法身等。

— and so Śākyamuni obtains no restraint with respect to those particular objects; and now, when the buddha Śākyamuni has appeared in the world, countless beings serve as the objects of his restraint, and once those beings have entered nirvāṇa, the Tathāgata Maitreya will obtain no restraint with respect to those particular objects either — the scope of the object varying, so too does the restraint vary in scope, and is this not to say that the restraint of the various buddhas is unequal? Answer: one should say that, although the scope of the objects of restraint may be greater or lesser, the own-substance of the restraint itself is no different from one buddha to the next, since it is universally brought forth with respect to the totality of all beings as its object. Some say: it involves no fault at all to say that the restraint of the buddhas of the three times is unequal. Question: if so, then how is the statement of the Prajñaptiśāstra to be reconciled, which says that all the tathāgata-arhat-fully-awakened-ones are entirely equal? Answer: they are called equal on account of three respects being equal. First, equal practice — since every tathāgata, throughout the past three incalculable eons, diligently cultivated the four perfections to their final completion and thereby attained awakening. Second, equal benefit — since every tathāgata, with respect to the countless beings he converts, brings the work of benefit and joy to its full completion equally. Third, equal Dharma-body —

謂諸如來皆得十力四無所畏。三念住大悲十八不共法等勝功德故。

— since every tathāgata equally attains the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, the three foundations of mindfulness, great compassion, the eighteen exclusive qualities, and other such supreme virtues.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The theory of "implicit intent" used to rescue Dharmatrāta's own statement from apparent contradiction is a quietly important interpretive tool that recurs throughout this tradition: rather than dismissing an earlier master's words as simply wrong, the commentators first ask whether he might have meant something more precise than his surface wording suggests — a hermeneutic of charity that treats venerable authorities as worth the effort of careful reconstruction before any correction is offered.

2. How Many Precepts? Breaking One Branch, and a Startling Ranking

An extended taxonomy shows precepts can be counted as one, two, three, four, six, seven, nine, twelve, fourteen, eighteen, twenty-one, or forty-two depending on the classificatory scheme chosen, before settling on the standard sevenfold division to address a genuine dispute: when one breaks a single branch of the precepts toward one being, is that branch severed toward every being at once, or only toward that one being while remaining intact everywhere else? The section closes with one of the tradition's more startling rankings — better to go forth and violate the precepts than to remain a five-precept lay follower, since a monk who breaks one vow still retains many others, while a lay follower who breaks one of only five is left holding nothing at all.

Chinese Source

It is on account of these three respects that they are called equal, not because the own-substance of their restraint has no difference in scope. It is also on account of an equality of faculties that they are called equal, since every tathāgata abides in the supreme grade of faculties; and on account of an equality of moral discipline, since every tathāgata attains the supreme grade of moral discipline. Other masters say: it is because the restraint of every tathāgata-arhat-fully-awakened-one is obtained with respect to every being whatsoever that they are called equal — not that their own-substance has no difference. That is, as for the beings who were the objects of a past buddha's own restraint, were Śākyamuni Buddha to exist now, he would not thereby also obtain restraint with respect to those same objects — there is no such principle; and as for the beings who are the objects of the buddha Śākyamuni's own restraint, were the Tathāgata Maitreya to exist at the same time, he would not thereby also obtain restraint with respect to those same objects — there is likewise no such principle; hence to speak of them as equal involves no fault either way. Now, restraint should properly be spoken of as a single thing, as when one speaks of the aggregate of moral discipline, the cultivation of moral discipline, and the training in moral discipline; or it should be spoken of as two, namely manifest and unmanifest; or as three, namely inferior, middling, and superior, or the distinctions arising from non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion; or as four, namely bodily and verbal karma, each having a manifest and unmanifest aspect; or as six, namely manifest and unmanifest each having an inferior, middling, and superior grade, or arising from the three roots; or as seven, namely abstaining from taking life, and so on up to abstaining from idle chatter; or as nine, namely from the very lowest of the low up to the very highest of the high; or as twelve, namely manifest and unmanifest bodily and verbal karma each having three grades, or arising from the three roots; or as fourteen, namely abstaining from taking life, and so on up to abstaining from idle chatter, each having a manifest and unmanifest aspect; or as eighteen, namely manifest and unmanifest each having nine grades; or as twenty-one, namely abstaining from taking life, and so on up to abstaining from idle chatter, each having three grades, or arising from the three roots; or as forty-two, namely abstaining from taking life, and so on up to abstaining from idle chatter, each having a manifest and unmanifest aspect, each in turn having three grades or arising from the three roots. 所以者何。彼若毀犯五種學處身中便空。諸出家者設犯五處而更有餘眾多猶轉。迦濕彌羅國諸論師言。雖犯律儀而律儀不斷。如法悔除還名持戒。無有頓受別捨得故。未悔除位具得二名。若已悔除但名持戒。

— for why is this? If a lay follower violates his five precepts, he is thereby left with none at all in his own body, whereas one gone forth, even should he violate five of his own precepts, still has many others that continue to function. The treatise-masters of Kashmir say: although one may violate a precept, restraint is not thereby cut off at all — once one properly repents and removes the fault, one is called once more a keeper of the precepts, since there is no such thing as receiving all at once yet giving up and regaining separately, one branch at a time. Before one has repented and removed the fault, one holds both names at once; once one has repented and removed it, one is called simply a keeper of the precepts.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Kashmiri masters' closing position — that a violated precept is never actually severed at all, merely awaiting proper confession to be called "keeping precepts" once more — offers a genuinely more forgiving picture of moral repair than either of the two severing theories debated before it: rather than treating restraint as something that fractures and must be pieced back together branch by branch, it treats the underlying commitment as continuous throughout, with confession simply restoring the name that a lapse had temporarily obscured.

3. Restraint Over the Inanimate World

A delightful philosophical puzzle asks whether restraint can even be obtained with respect to external, lifeless objects like grass or wine — for if it can, it should fluctuate as grass withers and wine spoils, yet the Buddha explicitly taught that this restraint is never received only in part. The resolution is elegant: restraint is indeed obtained with respect to such things, but as a single, unified totality across the entire class at once (all grass everywhere, all wine everywhere), rather than incrementally with each individual blade or bottle — and since the class itself never goes extinct, the restraint never fluctuates at all.

Chinese Source

問於外物中得律儀不。若有得者。所得律儀應有增減。謂生草枯時酒味壞時應減。即彼生時熟時應增。如是等事其類寔繁。是故律儀應有增減。若無得者即此律儀境應少分處受。而世尊說。如是律儀無少分受。又斷生草等悔除應無用。有作是說。雖有得者而不名律儀。但是順律儀法。問此順律儀法為是律儀攝。為非律儀攝。若是律儀攝說為律儀。或說順律儀竟有何異。若非律儀攝。此有何相而言順律儀非律儀攝。如是說者。於外法中亦得律儀。問若爾律儀應有增減。答無增減。以總得故。謂此律儀總於一切生草等上得一無表。而世間無有無生草等時。總於一切蒲桃等酒。則不壞時得一無表。世間無有無諸酒時。是故律儀無有增減。餘亦如是。問別解脫律儀由何等心得。答由普於一切有情。起善意樂。無損害心得。若起此心於某處受某處。不受不得律儀。由此律儀遍於一切所應受處。得防護故。是故說。若此律儀有方域者大地所不受。由此律儀於有情處得。有情界多地界少故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: is restraint obtained with respect to external, inanimate things? If it is, then the restraint so obtained ought to increase and decrease — since growing grass withers, and the taste of wine spoils, restraint ought to decrease at such times, and increase again when grass sprouts anew or wine ripens, and such instances are truly countless — hence restraint ought to increase and decrease. But if it is not obtained at all, then the object of this restraint would be received only in part, yet the World-Honored One has said that this restraint is never received only in part; moreover, cutting living grass and the like would then make no use of repentance and removal of fault at all. Some say: although something is indeed obtained, it is not called restraint, but merely a dharma that accords with restraint. According to this last account, restraint is indeed obtained with respect to external dharmas as well. Question: if so, then restraint ought to increase and decrease. Answer: it does not increase or decrease, since it is obtained as a totality — that is, this restraint is obtained as a single unmanifest totality with respect to all growing grass everywhere at once, and the world is never without growing grass of some kind anywhere; and likewise it is obtained as a single unmanifest totality with respect to all grape wine and the like everywhere at once, so long as it has not yet spoiled, and the world is never entirely without wine of some kind anywhere — hence restraint neither increases nor decreases, and the same holds for other such cases. Question: by what kind of mind is prātimokṣa restraint obtained? Answer: it is obtained by giving rise, universally toward every being, to a wholesome intention free of any wish to harm. If one gives rise to this mind while accepting the precept only with respect to some particular place, one does not obtain restraint with respect to what one has not accepted — for it is precisely because this restraint extends its protection over every place that ought to be accepted that it is called protection. Hence it is said that if this restraint had a fixed territorial boundary, the great earth itself could not contain it, since this restraint is obtained with respect to beings, and beings vastly outnumber the mere extent of the ground they occupy.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

5. Why Losing the Aids to Penetration Doesn't Cause a Fall: A Dozen Explanations

A genuinely difficult puzzle receives an extraordinary battery of answers: an ordinary being who attains "forbearance" (a threshold stage just short of the path of seeing) loses this attainment at death, yet — unlike losing any other wholesome root — this loss does not expose him to falling into an unhappy destiny. A dozen independent explanations are offered in succession: the wholesome root's own power is so great its influence lingers after it is gone, like a purgative that clears illness even once the medicine itself has passed through the body, or a lion's abandoned den still too scent-marked for smaller animals to enter; proximity to the noble path lends a kind of borrowed royal protection, like a frightened man sheltering near a king whom bandits dare not even look at directly; and the body itself becomes a reserved "royal residence" that lesser afflictions simply cannot occupy.

6. The Four Kinds of Birth Defined

A rich, encyclopedic catalog names the four kinds of birth — egg-born (geese, parrots, certain nāgas, and even some humans), womb-born (elephants, horses, and likewise a range of nāgas, ghosts, and humans), moisture-born (mosquitoes, centipedes, bees, and their own scattered nāga and human counterparts), and spontaneously born (every hell-being, every god, every being in intermediate existence, and yet more nāgas, ghosts, and humans) — before a charming puzzle asks whether the gods' own beautifully colored birds are egg-born (which would mean an unclean corpse the ever-pleasure-surrounded gods somehow never see) or spontaneously born, resolved by explaining that a swift wind simply sweeps every such corpse far away before any god can behold it.

8. Which Birth Is Most Extensive and Most Supreme

A lively debate over which of the four births produces the greatest sheer number of beings runs through vivid folk-natural-history claims — countless eggs filling foreign mountain valleys unnoticed even under elephants' feet; a single toad birthing seven broods; a single fish filling seven ponds with offspring; any rotting matter anywhere from the earth's foundation to the Brahmā heaven spontaneously breeding worms — before settling on spontaneous birth as most extensive, since it alone comprises entire destinies, entire realms, and every being in intermediate existence at once. Spontaneous birth is then also named most supreme, raising an immediate puzzle: why, then, did the bodhisattva not take spontaneous birth for his own final existence? The first answers offered turn on timing (buddhas and spontaneously-born humans never coincide) and on spontaneous birth's own lightness and feebleness, unfit to bear the weight of supreme awakening.

9. Why the Bodhisattva Refused Spontaneous Birth: Relatives, Lineage, and Protection

More reasons accumulate for the bodhisattva's refusal of spontaneous birth: it would waste the karma he and his parents had each cultivated across countless lifetimes specifically to bring about their family bond; it would fail to display his supremacy in wisdom, lineage, and rank, since supremacy needs an ordinary baseline to be measured against; being born into a powerful household and then renouncing it utterly, "as one spits out phlegm," inspires a uniquely rare reverence that a lineage-less renunciation never could; and, most strikingly, a buddha needs relatives to serve as an outer protector of the Dharma alongside the inner protection of his own awakened qualities — illustrated by the Śākya clan's own role in shielding the Buddha from outside-path aggressors, and even the wayward "group of six" monks once boasting they would forcibly defend the Buddha themselves if given the chance.

10. Why Spontaneous Birth Leaves No Remains, and the Garuḍa's Puzzle

A final reason for refusing spontaneous birth turns out to be the most consequential: taking womb-birth ensures relics remain after the bodhisattva's parinirvāṇa, and even a thousand years later, countless beings who offer as little as a mustard seed's worth of sincere reverence toward those relics obtain boundless merit — none of which would be possible for a spontaneously-born being, who vanishes at death like an extinguished lamp, utterly without remainder, since all such a being's faculties arise together at birth and hence cease together at death. This raises an immediate puzzle: if spontaneously-born beings truly leave nothing behind, how can scripture describe a spontaneously-born garuḍa eating spontaneously-born nāgas as food? Four competing explanations follow, each preserving the no-remains rule while still making sense of the garuḍa's apparent meal.

The garuḍa-eating-nāga puzzle is solved not by abandoning the no-remains principle but by relocating exactly when and how nourishment actually happens — whether through the nāga's still-living flesh, its instantly-digestible substance, or the garuḍa's own dissolving saliva — a pattern of reasoning that recurs throughout this fascicle: rather than let an inconvenient scriptural detail simply overturn an established rule, the commentators patiently search for the precise mechanism that lets both truths stand together.

12. Completing the Matrix and Its Summary Rules

The matrix concludes with the remaining cross-tabulations of bodily, verbal, and mental karma against one another, before distilling everything into a tidy set of closing rules: a body is never possessed in the formless realm, but definitely possessed in the desire and form realms, where bodily and verbal karma are then definitely possessed as well; those in the egg-stage, the embryo, and ordinary beings of the formless realm definitely lack it; every noble one and every ordinary being properly abiding in precepts, good or bad, definitely possesses it; and mental karma alone, unlike its bodily and verbal counterparts, is possessed by every being whatsoever, without a single exception.
