Sāgata and the Dragon

Fascicle 123 works through the four kinds of cultivation and restraint-class, explains why the seven Buddhist communities are defined by prātimokṣa restraint alone, and asks why the upāsaka precepts single out adultery, false speech, and alcohol — closing with the stories of a layman who broke all five precepts in one afternoon and the monk Sāgata who once tamed a dragon. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Karma Bound by Defilement and Its Fruit: Two Schools, One Verdict

Fascicle 123 continues the Manifest-and-Unmanifest Nyāsa with a technical dispute between the Kashmiri masters (who read the question "does contaminated karma have contaminated fruit?" as one cause yielding multiple fruits) and the Western masters (who read it as multiple causes yielding one fruit) — the two readings producing subtly different tetralemma answers throughout. The verdict sides with Kashmir, since the base treatise's own wording consistently proceeds question by question with reference to a single, unified karma rather than a single, unified fruit.

Ru-Yi Translation

Is there karma that is contaminated whose fruit is likewise contaminated? And so on in full. One should know that this discussion proceeds with reference to three of the fruits, excluding the fruit of exertion and the fruit of predominance — the fruit of exertion being excluded because it is too often mixed and confused, since many dharmas serving as cause may yield a single fruit, and the fruit of predominance being excluded because it is indeterminate and exceedingly broad in scope, as already explained before. The treatise-masters of Kashmir say that this discussion proceeds with reference to a single cause yielding multiple fruits. According to this account: is there karma that is contaminated whose fruit is likewise contaminated? Answer: there is — namely, the outflow-fruit and the ripening-fruit. Is there karma that is contaminated whose fruit is undefiled? Answer: there is — namely, the fruit of freedom from bondage. Is there karma that is contaminated whose fruit is both contaminated and undefiled? Answer: there is — namely, the outflow-fruit, the ripening-fruit, and the fruit of freedom from bondage together. Is there karma that is undefiled whose fruit is likewise undefiled? Answer: there is — namely, the outflow-fruit and the fruit of freedom from bondage. Is there karma that is undefiled whose fruit is contaminated? Answer: there is not. Is there karma that is undefiled whose fruit is both contaminated and undefiled? Answer: there is not. The verdict: one should know that the first account given is the better one, since the base treatise's own wording more often agrees with it, proceeding as a question and answer with reference to a single, unified karma.

無一業體通染淨故。

— since there is no single karma whose own-substance spans both the contaminated and the pure.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That two equally rigorous schools can extract genuinely different tetralemma answers from the identical base-text question, purely by deciding whether "cause" or "fruit" is the fixed, singular term being interrogated, is a quiet demonstration of how much interpretive weight a single grammatical choice can carry — the treatise itself never states which reading is intended, leaving centuries of commentators to infer it from patterns of phrasing elsewhere in the text.

2. The Four Kinds of Cultivation, and What It Means Not to Cultivate the Body

A precise account of "not cultivating the body, precepts, mind, or wisdom" distinguishes two of the four kinds of cultivation at work — antidote-cultivation (the relevant antidote path not yet having arisen) and elimination-cultivation (the afflictions taking that object not yet cut off) — before working out, term by term, exactly what it means to be "not free from craving, moistness, joy, and thirst" with respect to the body, and for the uninterrupted path capable of exhausting form-realm craving to remain "not yet cultivated or pacified."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four-term breakdown of craving into desire, moistness, joy, and thirst is not mere synonym-piling but a genuine attempt to name craving's own internal structure — its wanting, its capacity to soften and prepare the ground for further attachment, its accompanying delight, and its restless, unsatisfied reaching — suggesting that even a single affliction as familiar as craving contains distinguishable moving parts once examined closely enough.

3. Cultivating Body, Precepts, Mind, and Wisdom — and Which Pairs Fall Together

The positive counterpart follows in exact mirror image — cultivating body, precepts, mind, and wisdom defined as becoming free of the same craving and completing the same antidote path — before a careful tetralemma maps which of the four always arrive together: body and precepts are cut off only simultaneously, at freedom from the fourth dhyāna's defilement, while mind and wisdom are likewise cut off only simultaneously, at freedom from the peak of existence's defilement, with body/precepts and mind/wisdom forming two separate matched pairs rather than one single fourfold bundle.

7. Why the Seven Communities Are Defined by Prātimokṣa Alone

A rich institutional argument explains why the sevenfold Buddhist community — bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī, probationary nun, novice, female novice, upāsaka, upāsikā — is defined exclusively through prātimokṣa restraint rather than dhyāna or undefiled restraint: prātimokṣa alone is obtained gradually (matching the community's own graded structure), remains continuously possessed through sleep, intoxication, and every mental state without exception (unlike dhyāna restraint, present only while actually in concentration), and applies uniquely to the desire realm, the human destiny, a buddha's own dispensation, and both ordinary beings and nobles alike.

8. Seven Names for Upāsaka, Seven Names for Training Rule

A delightful etymological cascade offers seven different explanations for why a lay follower is called an upāsaka — one who draws near to wholesome dharmas, to good and worthy persons, to diligent practice, or to the Buddha's own Dharma and Vinaya — before offering seven more names for the five precepts themselves: traces of training (quoting Aniruddha's famous image of precepts as a ladder to the palace of wisdom), the training that destroys evil conduct, the shortcut path through which all restraint flows, the vow that marks the noble community as outside paths' vows mark theirs, the root of all further learning, and the very foundation of the city of nirvāṇa.

9. Why Only Adultery? Seven Theories, and the Buddha's Compassionate Restraint

A genuinely fascinating puzzle asks why the lay precept against sexual misconduct singles out only violating another's wife, never abstention from one's own — answered through seven competing theories, from the old masters' claim that only adultery counts as the foundational lay restraint, through Pārśva's gatekeeper simile (the Dharma-king watches precisely the one gate most likely to be breached), to the frankly pragmatic observation that demanding full chastity even in marriage would have driven away every king, minister, and elder who needed an heir. Most beautifully, one theory holds this restriction is itself a compassionate skillful means: having eliminated evil as vast as the four seas, the layperson will, in time, come to see for himself that even the dewdrop-sized fault remaining in his own marriage deserves to be given up as well.

Some further say: this is one of the buddhas' own skillful, expedient devices — for had the Buddha prohibited relations with one's own wife, then kings, ministers, and elders, unable to give up their own household wives, would have said to the Buddha, "We cannot undertake the Tathāgata's own prohibitions," and would have further requested to be excused from abstaining from their own wives, since it is by this means alone that they can have heirs — and hence the Buddha established the training rule solely with reference to abstaining from violating another's wife. Other masters say: it is because noble ones, throughout their subsequent births, do not violate this precept, whereas the same is not true of one's own wife, for why is this? Violating another's wife is of three kinds — arising from greed, from hatred, or from delusion — and noble ones, throughout their subsequent births, though they do not commit the kind arisen from delusion (since it is included within the category of view, already cut off by the noble one), may still commit the kind arisen from greed or hatred — and hence this is not established as a prohibition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The dewdrop-and-four-seas image for the Buddha's own compassionate restraint is a genuinely moving piece of pedagogical wisdom: rather than demanding the whole of moral perfection at once and risking total refusal, the precept deliberately leaves a visible remainder of imperfection, trusting that a person who has already crossed an ocean of moral progress will, unprompted, notice and want to close the small remaining gap on their own — treating voluntary further growth as more durable than compliance extracted by an impossible initial demand.

如苾芻等若更受持十二杜多功德。更不別得異先所受苾芻等戒。然名最勝大苾芻等。以別受持遠離行故。彼亦如是。問何故語四善業道中。離虛誑語。獨立學處而非餘耶。答舊對法諸師。及迦濕彌羅國諸論師說。離虛誑語是近事者所受律儀家族本地。離離間等則不如是。故此唯立離虛誑語。脇尊者曰。法王法主知此律儀。有法能為障礙遮止。有法不為障礙遮止。謂虛誑語於此律儀最極能為障礙遮止。如守門者禁門不開。離間語等則不如是。有作是說。虛誑語性罪所攝譏嫌最重。離間語等雖性罪攝譏嫌少輕。故不立為近事學處。有作是說。離虛誑語易可防護非離餘三。謂處居家御僮僕等。

Just as bhikṣus who further undertake and uphold the twelve ascetic virtues do not thereby obtain any separate precept distinct from what they previously received as bhikṣus, but are called most excellent great bhikṣus, since they separately undertake and uphold this further conduct of abstention — it is just the same here. Question: why, among the four wholesome verbal paths of karma, is abstaining from false speech alone established as a training rule, and not the others? Answer: the old Ābhidharmikas and the treatise-masters of Kashmir say that abstaining from false speech is the restraint proper to what a lay follower undertakes at the foundational level of household life, whereas abstaining from divisive speech and the rest is not so — hence the training rule is established solely with reference to abstaining from false speech. The Venerable Pārśva says: the Dharma-king, the master of the Dharma, knows precisely which dharmas can obstruct and hinder this restraint and which cannot — false speech is the one thing most capable of obstructing and hindering this restraint, like a gatekeeper who watches precisely the one gate that might be breached — while divisive speech and the rest are not so. Some say: false speech is a natural offense that carries the very heaviest censure, while divisive speech and the rest, though likewise natural offenses, carry a somewhat lighter censure, and hence are not established as training rules for the upāsaka. Some say: it is easier to guard against false speech than against the other three — for one dwelling at home, managing servants and the like,

難可遠離離間等三。及身業中捶撻等事。復有說者。作虛誑語業道最重。餘三少輕故不立為近事學處。有餘師說。唯虛誑語能破壞僧。故立學處餘三不爾。有餘復說。若諸聖者經生不犯立近事戒。

— finds it difficult to entirely avoid divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle chatter, along with such bodily deeds as striking and beating. Other masters say: the path of karma of false speech is the very heaviest, while the other three are somewhat lighter, and hence only false speech is established as a training rule for the upāsaka. Other masters say: only false speech is able to cause a schism in the saṅgha, and hence it alone is established as a training rule, unlike the other three. Others further say: it is because noble ones, throughout their subsequent births, do not violate the training rule established for the upāsaka in this way,

聖者經生必定遠離虛誑語業。非餘語業。所以者何。餘語有三。謂從貪瞋癡生。經生聖者。雖不犯從癡所生癡見品攝故。聖者已斷而犯貪瞋所生是故不立。問世尊何故於遮罪中。唯離飲酒立為學處。

— that is, noble ones, throughout their subsequent births, definitely abstain from false speech, but not necessarily from the other kinds of speech, for why is this? The other kinds of speech are of three kinds, arising from greed, from hatred, or from delusion, and noble ones, throughout their subsequent births, though they do not commit the kind arisen from delusion (since it is included within the category of view, already cut off by the noble one), may still commit the kind arisen from greed or hatred — and hence these are not established as training rules. Question: why, among the prohibited offenses, does the World-Honored One establish abstaining from drinking wine alone as a training rule?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The claim that noble ones can still speak harshly or divisively but never falsely draws a precise line through the four verbal faults that a casual reading might have expected to fall together: it turns out that lying alone requires a specific cognitive distortion about reality itself, while the other three verbal faults require only ordinary emotional excess — meaning the path's own progress cuts unevenly across superficially similar categories of wrongdoing, eliminating the one rooted in confusion long before it eliminates the ones rooted merely in feeling.

11. Why Only Alcohol? And the Layman Who Broke All Five Precepts in One Afternoon

The same sevenfold structure explains why abstaining from alcohol alone, among all merely conventional prohibitions, earns a place among the five precepts — capped by an unforgettable cautionary tale: a virtuous, precept-keeping layman, made thirsty by salty food at a family gathering, mistakes a jar of wine for water and drinks it; now drunk, he steals and cooks a neighbor's wandering chicken, rapes the neighbor's daughter who comes looking for it, and, hauled before the magistrate, denies everything — a single lapse into drink cascading, within a single afternoon, into breaking all five precepts at once.

Chinese Source

答舊對法諸師。及迦濕彌羅國諸論師說。唯離飲酒是近事者。所受律儀家族本地。離餘遮罪則不如是。故此唯立離飲諸酒。脇尊者曰。法王法主知此律儀有法能為障礙遮止。有法不為障礙遮止。謂飲諸酒於此律儀。最極能為障礙遮止。如守門者禁門不開。離餘遮罪則不如是。故此唯立離飲諸酒。有作是說。離飲諸酒易可防護非餘遮罪。謂酪清漿沙糖水等足能止渴何用酒為。有餘師言。離飲酒戒能總防護諸餘律儀。如塹垣城能總防護。復有說者。若不防護離飲酒戒。則總毀犯諸餘律儀餘則不爾。曾聞有一鄔波索迦。稟性仁賢受持五戒專精不犯。後於一時家屬大小當為賓客。彼獨不往留食供之時至取食。醎味多故須臾增渴。見一器中有酒如水。為渴所逼遂取飲之。爾時便犯離飲酒戒。時有隣雞來入其舍。盜心捕殺烹煮而噉。於此復犯離殺盜戒。隣女尋雞來入其室。復以威力強逼交通。緣此更犯離邪行戒。隣家憤怒將至官司。時斷事者訊問所以。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: the old Ābhidharmikas and the treatise-masters of Kashmir say that abstaining from drinking wine alone is the restraint proper to what a lay follower undertakes at the foundational level of household life, whereas abstaining from the other prohibited offenses is not so — hence the training rule is established solely with reference to abstaining from drinking any wine. The Venerable Pārśva says: the Dharma-king, the master of the Dharma, knows precisely which dharmas can obstruct and hinder this restraint and which cannot — drinking wine is the one thing most capable of obstructing and hindering this restraint, like a gatekeeper who watches precisely the one gate that might be breached — while the other prohibited offenses are not so, hence the training rule is established solely with reference to abstaining from drinking any wine. Some say: it is easier to guard against drinking wine than against the other prohibited offenses — for clarified whey, congee, sugared water, and the like are entirely sufficient to quench thirst, so what need is there for wine at all? Other masters say: the precept of abstaining from wine, as a whole, guards and protects every other restraint, just as a moat and rampart together guard and protect an entire city. Others further say: if one fails to guard the precept of abstaining from wine, one thereby violates every other restraint entirely, unlike the failure of any other single precept. It has been heard that there was once an upāsaka of a naturally kind and virtuous disposition, who upheld the five precepts single-mindedly without ever violating them. Later, on one occasion, when his whole household, young and old, were to receive guests, he alone did not go out but stayed behind to attend to the food; when the time came to eat, the food being heavily salted, he grew thirsty within a short while, and, seeing what looked like water in a vessel — which was in fact wine — driven by his thirst, took and drank it. At that moment he violated the precept of abstaining from wine. A neighbor's chicken then wandered into his house, and, with a thieving intent, he caught, killed, cooked, and ate it — thereby further violating the precepts against killing and stealing. The neighbor's daughter, coming in search of the chicken, entered his house, and he, by force, violated her — thereby further violating the precept against sexual misconduct. The neighbors, enraged, hauled him before the authorities, and when the magistrate questioned him as to what had happened,

彼皆拒諱。因斯又犯離虛誑語。如是五戒皆因酒犯。故遮罪中獨制飲酒。有餘師說。酒令失念增無慚愧。其過深重故偏制立。如律中說。制地國中有一毒龍。性極暴惡為稼穡害。其所居池水陸空飛無敢近者。時有尊者名曰善來。以巧方便令其調伏。因此名稱流布八方。於是信心競興供養。漸次遊化至室羅筏。值彼城中請僧設會。

— he denied everything, and thereby further violated the precept against false speech as well. Thus all five precepts were violated on account of wine, and this is why abstaining from wine alone is prohibited among the prohibited offenses. Other masters say: wine causes one to lose mindfulness and increases shamelessness and lack of dread of blame, its fault being especially grave, and hence it alone is singled out and prohibited. As the Vinaya says: in the kingdom of Kaśmīra there was once a poisonous dragon of an extremely violent and evil nature, who harmed the crops of the fields, and whose ponds, land, and skies no one dared approach. At that time there was a venerable one named Sāgata, who, through skillful means, subdued him — and, on account of this, his fame spread in every direction, so that the faithful competed with one another to make offerings to him. Traveling gradually onward to teach, he came in time to Śrāvastī, where, on that occasion, the saṅgha was invited to a gathering held within that city.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The drunk layman's story performs, in miniature narrative form, exactly the argument the surrounding philosophical reasons make abstractly: that alcohol is prohibited not because drinking is itself the gravest sin but because it dissolves the very faculty of mindfulness that keeps every other precept intact, making it less a sixth vice among equals than the single structural weak point through which all the others can collapse at once.

12. Sāgata and the Dragon: Can He Subdue Even a Toad?

The fascicle closes with one of the tradition's most beloved cautionary narratives: the monk Sāgata, famed for skillfully subduing a ferocious poisonous dragon that had terrorized a kingdom's crops and waters, is later, on alms-round, given wine mistaken for water by a well-meaning but naive laywoman, and collapses drunk and exposed in the road, his robe and bowl scattered. The Buddha, finding him there, gathers the assembly and asks with devastating understatement whether the very monk who once subdued a mighty dragon could now subdue so much as a toad — met only by the monks' ashamed silence — before declaring that none of his followers should ever again drink so much as a dewdrop of wine clinging to a blade of grass.

Chinese Source

有近事女家不豐饒。獨請善來奉上飲食。食多鹽味須臾增渴。為渴所逼現相求飲。時近事女作是思惟。尊者所食極為肥膩。若飲冷水或當致疾。遂設方便授以清酒。彼不審察便取飲之。讚慰收衣趣勝林寺。將至醉悶䤄眩便倒。衣鉢錫杖狼藉在地。露體而臥無所覺知。佛將阿難經行遇見。知而故問此臥者誰。何為此間醉酒而臥。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was an upāsikā of an impoverished household, who alone invited Sāgata and offered him food and drink. The food being heavily salted, he grew thirsty within a short while, and, driven by thirst, made a sign asking for something to drink. The upāsikā thought to herself, "The venerable one's food was extremely rich and oily — should he drink cold water now, he might well fall ill," and so, by some skillful device, gave him clear wine instead. He, without examining it closely, took and drank it, and, after offering his thanks and blessing, gathered up his robe and set off toward the Jetavana monastery; but before he arrived, he grew drunk, dizzy, and disoriented, and collapsed — his robe, bowl, and staff scattered in disarray upon the ground, his body exposed as he lay there, aware of nothing at all. The Buddha, walking with Ānanda on his alms-round, came upon him, and, though he already knew, deliberately asked, "Who is this lying here? Why does he lie here drunk?"

阿難白佛此是善來。佛告阿難可集僧眾。僧眾集已佛在眾中。敷如常座結加趺坐。爾時世尊告苾芻眾。汝等聞見苾芻善來曾以巧便伏毒龍不。諸苾芻眾隨已見聞。各白佛言。我曾聞見。佛言。汝等於意云何。善來今能伏蝦蟆不。苾芻皆曰。不也世尊。爾時如來種種方便呵毀酒過告諸苾芻。汝等若稱佛為師者。自今已往下至茅端所沾酒渧亦不得飲。故遮罪中獨制飲酒。有作是說。飲酒能令智慧衰退。如說長者智慧衰退。是第六失故遮罪中獨制飲酒。有餘師說。

Ānanda replied to the Buddha, "This is Sāgata." The Buddha told Ānanda to gather the assembly of monks. Once the assembly had gathered, the Buddha, seated among them upon his usual seat, cross-legged, then addressed the assembly of bhikṣus: "Have you all heard or seen how the bhikṣu Sāgata once subdued a poisonous dragon through skillful means?" The bhikṣus, according to what each had heard or seen, all replied to the Buddha, "We have indeed heard and seen this." The Buddha said, "What do you think — could Sāgata now subdue so much as a toad?" The bhikṣus all replied, "No, World-Honored One." The Tathāgata then, through various skillful means, censured the fault of drinking wine, and told the bhikṣus, "If you call the Buddha your teacher, then from this day forward, you must not drink so much as a dewdrop of wine clinging to the tip of a blade of grass" — and this is why abstaining from wine alone is prohibited among the prohibited offenses. Some say: drinking wine causes wisdom to decline — as it is said that the decline of wisdom is the sixth of the elder's own faults — and hence abstaining from wine alone is prohibited among the prohibited offenses. Other masters say:

聖者經生必不飲酒。雖嬰孩位養母以指。強渧口中不自在故而無有失。纔有識別設遇強緣。為護身命亦終不飲。故遮罪。中獨立酒戒。

— noble ones, throughout their subsequent births, definitely do not drink wine; even at the stage of infancy, though a nurse might forcibly place a drop upon their lips against their own will, there is no fault in this, since as soon as they gain discernment, even should they encounter some overwhelming circumstance, they would still never drink even to save their own body and life — and this is why abstaining from wine alone is established as a prohibited-offense precept.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's toad question is a masterclass in rhetorical restraint: rather than lecture Sāgata on the dangers of alcohol directly, he simply holds up the monk's own greatest triumph against his present degradation and lets the assembly's own stunned silence supply the entire moral — a single, deceptively simple public question accomplishing what a lengthy sermon on the perils of drink never could.
