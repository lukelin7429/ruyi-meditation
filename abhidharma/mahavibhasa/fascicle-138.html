What Grasps, What Is Grasped, and What Grounds a View

Fascicle 138 continues the exhaustive causal ledger for contaminated and uncontaminated dharmas and the conditioned and unconditioned, works through the nourishment of faculties across every absorption, and undertakes a rich definitional study of “grasped,” “conducive to grasping,” and a “ground of views,” including the famous six grounds of views from the Snake-Simile Sūtra. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Concordance Ledger Continues: Cause-Bound and Cause-Free Dharmas

Fascicle 138 opens by continuing the exhaustive causal ledger begun in fascicle 137, now applied to dharmas concordant with the associated cause against those that are not — cause-concordant dharmas (essentially, mind and its mental factors) turning out to serve as all four conditions toward one another, while cause-non-concordant dharmas (form, the unconditioned, and dissociated formations) lack the object-condition entirely toward each other, since they possess no object of their own to take.

2. Six More Pairs: Object, Form, Visibility, Resistance

3. Contaminated and Uncontaminated: Where the Causal Condition Breaks Down

A pivotal asymmetry emerges when contaminated and uncontaminated dharmas are cross-examined: contaminated dharmas can serve as an immediately-preceding condition and object-condition for uncontaminated ones (since a contaminated mind can indeed precede, and even take as its object, an uncontaminated one, as when patient acceptance and knowledge of suffering arise), yet can never serve as their causal condition, and the reverse holds equally — since a cause functions like a seed, and a contaminated dharma can never be the seed from which something genuinely uncontaminated grows, nor can the uncontaminated ever seed the contaminated.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The single missing causal condition between the contaminated and the uncontaminated is, in miniature, this entire tradition's account of what liberation actually requires: proximity, sequence, and even shared attention are not enough to produce freedom from defilement — only a genuinely different kind of seed, belonging to an entirely separate causal lineage, can ever bear that particular fruit.

4. Why the Unconditioned Needs No Cause: Four Images of Strength and Weakness

A cascade of vivid analogies explains why conditioned dharmas depend on causes and conditions while the unconditioned depends on none at all: the weak lean on another for support while the strong stand alone; a reaper needs a sickle and a digger needs a spade, but one who does nothing needs no tool at all; a traveler needs provisions but one who stays home needs none; and, most vividly, the conditioned resembles both a king and a king's own retinue, bound together in mutual need, while the unconditioned resembles only the king himself, never needing a retinue to be what it already is.

5. The Five Kinds of Continuity, and Why Nothing Ever Obstructs the Unconditioned

A subtle clarification insists that, whenever a conditioned dharma fails to arise, the obstacle is always some other conditioned dharma, never the unconditioned itself, which serves only as an unobstructing predominant condition throughout. This opens into a precise fivefold taxonomy of "continuity" itself — intermediate-state, rebirth-state, phase, dharma, and moment continuity — each describing a different scale at which one thing gives way to the next, from an entire lifetime's transition between death and rebirth down to the succession of a single moment's own aggregates into the next.

Answer: it is conditioned dharmas that create this obstacle, never the unconditioned, since the unconditioned always serves conditioned dharmas as an efficient cause and as a predominant condition, presenting no obstacle whatsoever to either their arising or their non-arising — exactly as, at the side of a spring or pool, a lion's-mouth water-outlet or the like, when water is not presently flowing, this is due to some other condition, not because the outlet itself is obstructing it. Question: when unconditioned dharmas serve conditioned dharmas as a predominant condition and as an object-condition, is there any difference in superiority between serving as a predominant condition for something capable of taking it as object and for something incapable of doing so? Answer: with respect to the predominant condition, the meaning is equal, without any difference, since, whether taken as object or not, there is no obstruction either way. With respect to the object-condition, however, there is indeed a difference — for something capable of taking it as object, it serves as an object-condition; for something incapable of doing so, it does not. "Bound by every kind of entanglement, continuing on into hellish existence" — and so on, in full. The word "existence," as used here, carries several distinct meanings, as fully explained in the Aggregation Nyāsa. Here, it speaks of the continuation of a class-nature — the five aggregates belonging to a sentient being are called "existence." Yet "continuity" itself is of five kinds: first, intermediate-state continuity; second, rebirth-state continuity; third, phase continuity; fourth, dharma continuity; fifth, moment continuity. "Intermediate-state continuity" means that, once the death-state's own aggregates have perished, the intermediate-state's own aggregates arise, the intermediate state continuing on from the death state — this is called intermediate-state continuity. "Rebirth-state continuity" means that, once the intermediate state's own aggregates have perished, the rebirth-state's own aggregates arise, the rebirth state continuing on from the intermediate state — this is called rebirth-state continuity. "Phase continuity" means that, once the aggregates of the kalala stage have perished, the aggregates of the arbuda stage arise, and so on, up through, once the aggregates of middle age have perished, the aggregates of old age arising — every case in which a later phase continues on from an earlier phase is called phase continuity. "Dharma continuity" means that, immediately following a wholesome dharma, a defiled or neutral dharma manifests in the present; immediately following a defiled dharma, a wholesome or neutral dharma manifests in the present; immediately following a neutral dharma, a wholesome or defiled dharma manifests in the present — every case in which a later dharma continues on from an earlier dharma is called dharma continuity. "Moment continuity" means that, immediately following the aggregates of the first moment, the aggregates of the second moment manifest in the present — a later moment continuing on from an earlier moment is called moment continuity. All five of these are, in fact, subsumed under two kinds of continuity — namely, dharma continuity and moment continuity — since none of them ever departs from dharmas and moments themselves. As for their distribution by realm: the desire realm possesses all five; the form realm possesses four, excepting phase continuity; the formless realm possesses three, further excepting intermediate-state continuity as well. As for their distribution by destiny: hell possesses four, excepting phase continuity; the remaining destinies possess all five. As for their distribution by birth: every kind of birth possesses all five. Among these five kinds of continuity, this treatise relies upon two specifically in composing itself — namely, intermediate-state continuity and rebirth-state continuity.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Distinguishing five entirely different scales of "continuity," from an entire rebirth down to a single instant, and then showing that all five ultimately reduce to just two — dharma continuity and moment continuity — is a small demonstration of this tradition's own preferred method: multiply distinctions first, in order to see clearly, and only then discover which of them were, all along, simply the same underlying pattern appearing at different magnifications.

6. Rebirth's Own Puzzle: Why Only One Condition Is Named, Not Two

A genuinely sharp logical puzzle asks why the treatise describes newly-obtained faculties and great elements at the moment of rebirth as serving only a single predominant condition for the accompanying mind, when, if that very mind takes those same faculties and elements as its own object, it would seem to require a second, object-condition as well. Pausing to distinguish three genuinely independent senses of "internal" before proceeding to use just one of them is a small but important act of philosophical hygiene: much confusion in ordinary speech about what counts as "inside" versus "outside" a person comes precisely from silently sliding between these three different criteria without ever noticing the switch.

20. The Full Tetralemma: When Inner Doesn't Match 'Internal,' and Outer Doesn't Match 'External'

A precise closing tetralemma, drawn directly from the classic foundations-of-mindfulness formula, shows that being "internal" by continuum and being "subsumed under an internal sense-field" are not actually the same thing: one's own external objects, like one's own visible form, are internal by continuum yet not an internal sense-field, since mind does not depend on them; while another person's own sense-faculties are an internal sense-field for that person yet external by continuum from one's own perspective — with the identical, mirrored tetralemma then worked out for the external case as well.

Chinese Source

若法是內。彼法內處攝耶。答應作四句。有法是內。非內處攝。如說於內受內法住。修法觀。彼法是內者在自身故。非內處攝者。非心心所所依故。應知此中亦攝自身色等五境。有法內處攝非是內。

Is an internal dharma necessarily subsumed under an internal sense-field? Answer: this should be worked out as a tetralemma. There is a dharma that is internal, yet not subsumed under an internal sense-field — as when it is said, "dwelling in the contemplation of dharmas with respect to inner feelings and inner dharmas, cultivating the contemplation of dharmas." That dharma is "internal" since it exists within one's own body; it is "not subsumed under an internal sense-field" since it is not what mind and mental factors depend upon — one should understand that this also includes the five external objects, such as one's own form and the rest.
