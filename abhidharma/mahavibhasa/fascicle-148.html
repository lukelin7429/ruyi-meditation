Dharma-Knowledge, Class-Knowledge, and Liberation of Mind

Fascicle 148 gives dharma-knowledge and class-knowledge a full four-part anatomy, investigates time-liberation and immovable liberation of mind and which faculties obtain them, catalogues what powers the uninterrupted path at each of the four fruits, and closes with an exhaustive census of what is cut off, laid to rest, and newly arisen at each fruit. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 148 complete, 13 sections Source: Taishō T1545, vol. 27, fascicle 148 Topic: 根蘊第六中有納息第二之二 concludes (the Faculties Aggregate; the Existence Nyāsa concludes)

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

1. Does an Uncontaminated Faculty Bound to the Desire Realm Match Dharma-Knowledge?

Fascicle 148 opens with an intricate tetralemma cross-tabulating two properties of an uncontaminated faculty: whether it takes the desire realm as its object, and whether it is associated with dharma-knowledge. The two properties turn out not to coincide — patient-acceptance itself, and knowledge of the form and formless realms' own suffering and origin, both take the desire realm as object without being associated with dharma-knowledge, while cessation- and path-dharma-knowledge are associated with dharma-knowledge yet never touch the desire realm at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The recurring explanatory refrain — patient-acceptance and knowledge are never mutually associated, since a substance is never associated with itself — quietly does an enormous amount of structural work across this entire tetralemma: it is the single principle responsible for nearly every one of the many exceptions the census has to carve out.

2. And Does an Uncontaminated Faculty Bound to the Higher Realms Match Class-Knowledge?

The mirror-image tetralemma follows for the form and formless realms and class-knowledge, with exactly the same underlying logic producing exactly the same pattern of exceptions — patient-acceptance again pulling apart from knowledge, and cessation- and path-class-knowledge again floating free of any realm-object at all, since the unconditioned itself belongs to no realm.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Running the identical tetralemma twice, once for the lower realm and once for the higher ones, and watching it produce a perfectly mirrored set of exceptions each time, is itself a small proof of the treatise's own internal consistency: the same logical machinery, applied to structurally parallel material, yields structurally parallel results without needing any special-casing at all.

3. Why Frame This Discussion Around Just These Two Kinds of Knowledge?

Before analyzing dharma-knowledge and class-knowledge in detail, the treatise pauses to ask a genuinely curatorial question: why does this particular discussion get framed specifically around these two kinds of knowledge, when earlier discussions in this very treatise were framed around entirely different pairs or larger sets — the knowledge of others' minds and of past lives, the knowledge of exhaustion and non-arising, the four truths' own four knowledges, or the full set of eight or ten? The answer, refreshingly, is simply that each author composed according to his own intention, so long as the true nature of dharmas was never contradicted.

Chinese Source

法智當言法智耶。乃至廣說。問何故此中但依法類二智作論。答彼作論者意欲爾故。隨彼意欲而造論。但不違法性便不應責。彼意欲依二智作論即便作之。謂法智類智如前智蘊。隨論者意依二智作論。謂他心智宿住隨念智如後定蘊。隨論者意亦依二智作論。謂盡智無生智。如此蘊前隨論者意依四智作論。謂苦智集智滅智道智。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Should dharma-knowledge itself be called dharma-knowledge, and so on, as stated at length?" Why raise this question at all, framing it specifically around dharma-knowledge and class-knowledge alone? Because the author of this treatise simply intended it so, and a treatise is composed according to its own author's intention — so long as it does not contradict the true nature of dharmas, no objection should be raised against it. Since this author's own intention was to frame the discussion around these two kinds of knowledge, that is precisely what was done — exactly as dharma-knowledge and class-knowledge were framed earlier in the Knowledge Aggregate; exactly as, following the intention of a different author, the knowledge of others' minds and the knowledge of recollecting past abodes were framed later in the Concentration Aggregate; exactly as, again following a different author's intention, the knowledge of exhaustion and the knowledge of non-arising were framed earlier in this very aggregate; and exactly as, following yet another author's intention, the four kinds of knowledge — of suffering, of origin, of cessation, and of the path — were framed earlier still.

如前結蘊隨論者意依八智作論。謂法智類智他心智世俗智苦集滅道智。如所知納息。隨論者意依十智作論。謂前八智。及盡無生智。

Just as a skilled potter, having placed a lump of moist clay upon the wheel, can freely shape it into whatever vessel he wishes without ever violating the true nature of the craft, so too a skilled composer of treatises, having illuminated and dispelled confusion about the objects known through the wisdom born of listening and reflection, may afterward freely compose whatever treatise he wishes without ever violating the true nature of dharmas. Another position holds that dharma-knowledge and class-knowledge alone are mutually exclusive of one another, yet both equally take as their pervasive object whatever is not included in the other — namely, the contaminated and the uncontaminated, the conditioned and the unconditioned, and the dharmas of the four noble truths. Another position holds that dharma-knowledge and class-knowledge together encompass the entirety of uncontaminated knowledge, since every other kind of knowledge is fundamentally rooted in these two. Another position holds that dharma-knowledge and class-knowledge together mark out the outer boundary of every object — dharma-knowledge taking the lower realm as its object, class-knowledge taking the upper realms as its object. It is for reasons such as these that the discussion is framed specifically around these two kinds of knowledge alone.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

6. A New Investigation: What Kind of Faculty Does Liberation of Mind Actually Obtain?

The fascicle turns to an entirely new investigation, opened by recalling the Buddha's own teaching to Ānanda: a monk who delights in noisy, crowded dwellings can never attain either time-liberation of mind or immovable liberation of mind, while one who delights in solitude and stillness can attain both. 7. Time-Liberation, Immovable Liberation, and Every Fetter Exhausted: Trainee or Beyond-Training?

Having established what liberation of mind actually is, the treatise now asks which faculty obtains it — and the answer, in five different but equivalent formulations, is consistently "both a trainee's faculty and a faculty of the one beyond training," since the uninterrupted path that immediately precedes the moment of liberation still belongs to the trainee, while the path of liberation that completes it already belongs to the one beyond training. The same analysis extends to the exhaustion of every fetter, closing with a careful explanation of why this final, unconditioned liberation is described more simply than the two conditioned liberations of mind that precede it: conditioned liberation naturally admits of lower, middle, and higher grades, since what is obtained first genuinely differs from what is obtained later, while unconditioned liberation, always exactly the same however many times it is reached, needs no such graded description at all.

Conditioned liberation admits of lower, middle, and higher grades, since what is first obtained differs from what is obtained later, and this difference is accordingly spelled out in full. Unconditioned liberation admits of no such lower, middle, or higher grades, since what is obtained later is exactly the same as what was obtained at first, and so no further distinction is drawn.

One might ask: at the ninth and final path of liberation in becoming free from desire-realm contamination, who enters some dhyāna proper at that point, and who does not? Answer: one whose underlying basis has strong power enters; one whose underlying basis has weak power does not enter. Another position holds the reverse — one whose underlying basis has weak power enters, in order to nourish and strengthen that very basis, while one whose underlying basis already has strong power does not enter, having no such need to nourish it further. Another position holds that one with abundant joyful aspiration enters, while one with abundant weariness does not enter. Another position holds that one with abundant joy and pleasure enters, while one with abundant sorrow and distress does not enter. Another position holds that one who becomes free of desire-realm contamination by means of the cessation- and path-knowledges enters, while one who becomes free by means of the suffering- and origin-knowledges does not enter. Another position holds that one who becomes free of desire-realm contamination by means of the signless and wishless paths enters, while one who becomes free by means of the empty, suffering, origin, and wishless paths does not enter. Another position holds that one weary of the dharmas of the desire realm alone, in becoming free of contamination, enters, while one weary of the dharmas of all three realms, in becoming free of contamination, does not enter. Another position holds that one who becomes free of contamination seeking dhyāna itself enters, while one who becomes free of contamination seeking liberation does not enter. Another position holds that one of sharp faculties enters, while one of dull faculties does not enter — and exactly as with sharp and dull faculties, so too, mutatis mutandis, with the power of one's own causal basis and the power of external conditions, the power of one's own inner disposition and the power of an outer disposition, and the power of correct reflection within versus the power of hearing the correct teaching from without.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nine competing explanations offered for who enters a dhyāna at this final threshold and who does not — strong basis versus weak, joy versus weariness, sharp faculties versus dull, and more — read almost like a catalogue of every dimension along which two practitioners walking the very same path might nonetheless diverge, refusing to reduce so consequential a choice to any single explanatory factor.

13. The Final Census: What Is Cut Off, Laid to Rest, and Arisen at Arhatship

The fascicle's closing investigation completes the census at arhatship itself, where for the first time the life-faculty joins the ranks of what is permanently cut off, and where the precise count of what is laid to rest and arisen shifts depending on whether arhatship is reached from proximate access or from any of the actual dhyānas — with the third dhyāna alone requiring pleasure, rather than joy, as the faculty laid to rest and arisen.

Ru-Yi Translation

How many faculties are permanently cut off, laid to rest, and arisen upon attaining the fruit of arhatship? Answer: if one attains the fruit of arhatship relying on proximate access, one faculty is permanently cut off; seven faculties are permanently cut off yet also laid to rest and arisen; one is laid to rest without arising, and one arises without being laid to rest. The one faculty permanently cut off is the life-faculty. The seven faculties permanently cut off yet also laid to rest and arisen are the mind-faculty, equanimity, and faith with its four companions. "Permanently cut off" means belonging to the contaminated; "laid to rest" means belonging to the uninterrupted path; "arisen" means belonging to the path of liberation. Again, "permanently cut off" means belonging to the mundane; "laid to rest" means belonging to the path of approach; "arisen" means belonging to the path of the fruit. Again, "permanently cut off" means belonging to what is neither trainee nor beyond training; "laid to rest" means belonging to the trainee; "arisen" means belonging to the one beyond training. Again, "permanently cut off" means belonging to the peak of existence; "laid to rest" means belonging to the class of the vajra-like concentration; "arisen" means belonging to the class of the knowledge of exhaustion. Again, "permanently cut off" means belonging to what is cut off by cultivation; "laid to rest" means belonging to the already-known faculty; "arisen" means belonging to the faculty of complete knowledge. Again, "permanently cut off" means belonging to what is bound to the formless realm; "laid to rest" means belonging to the ground of cultivation; "arisen" means belonging to the ground of the one beyond training. The one laid to rest without arising is the already-known faculty; the one that arises without being laid to rest is the faculty of complete knowledge.

Exactly as when relying on proximate access, so too, mutatis mutandis, when relying on the meditative interval, the fourth dhyāna, or the three lower formless attainments. If one attains the fruit of arhatship relying on the first dhyāna, two faculties are permanently cut off; six faculties are permanently cut off yet also laid to rest and arisen; one faculty is laid to rest and arisen; one is laid to rest without arising, and one arises without being laid to rest. The two faculties permanently cut off are the life- and equanimity-faculties. The six faculties permanently cut off yet also laid to rest and arisen are the mind-faculty and faith with its four companions, the sense of "permanently cut off," "laid to rest," and "arisen" exactly as already explained. The one faculty laid to rest and arisen is the joy-faculty, the sense of "laid to rest" and "arisen" exactly as already explained. The one laid to rest without arising is the already-known faculty; the one that arises without being laid to rest is the faculty of complete knowledge. Exactly as when relying on the first dhyāna, so too, mutatis mutandis, when relying on the second or third dhyāna — except that, when relying on the third dhyāna, the pleasure-faculty rather than joy should be spoken of as being laid to rest and arisen.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the life-faculty itself is finally, permanently cut off only at arhatship — after having survived every earlier fruit's own census entirely intact — is a fitting note on which this long investigation closes: even the most basic thread of continuity a being can possess turns out to be something the path eventually severs, not something any lesser attainment ever touches.
