Unmoved Like Sumeru

Fascicle 173 marks a major milestone: it is the final installment (攝納息第三之八) of the Subsumption Nyāsa, which has run continuously since fascicle 166. The fascicle closes this long arc with Śāriputra's debate on the true nature of desire, and then its own centerpiece — a landmark polemic against the Vibhajyavādin and Mahāsāṅghika view that the Buddha's physical body is uncontaminated, arguing instead that his body is genuinely contaminated yet, like Mount Sumeru amid the eight directional winds, entirely unmoved by the eight worldly winds of gain, loss, fame, disrepute, praise, blame, pleasure, and pain. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. What Desire Really Is: Śāriputra's Debate with a Heretic

Fascicle 173 opens with the five desirable sense-objects (五妙欲) — completing the deferred list first promised back in fascicle 171 — before turning to a genuinely sharp philosophical question, illustrated by a verse-debate: is "desire" the external object itself, or the mind's own discriminating craving toward it? The Venerable Śāriputra, challenged twice by a heretic ascetic, wins the exchange with a single devastating counter-verse, silencing his opponent by pointing out that the heretic's own teacher gazes constantly upon beautiful things without, by the heretic's own logic, ever being described as desiring them.

The second: there are sentient beings who delight in experiencing desirable sense-objects that they themselves transform into being, and who move about freely among such self-transformed desirable objects — this refers to the fifth heaven, the Heaven of Delight in Transformation. The third: there are sentient beings who delight in experiencing desirable sense-objects that others transform into being for them, and who move about freely among such others-transformed desirable objects — this refers to the sixth heaven, the Heaven of Controlling Others' Emanations. Reading note — Ru-Yi

Śāriputra's winning move — turning the heretic's own argument back on his teacher's own visible behavior — is a small masterclass in dialectical technique: rather than asserting a counter-doctrine, he simply shows that the heretic's own position, followed consistently, produces an absurd conclusion the heretic himself would never accept about his own guru.

2. The Five Precepts, and the Six-Fold Bodies of Experience

The treatise briefly dispatches the five lay precepts of training before turning to the six-fold classification of experience — the six internal and external sense-spheres, and the six "bodies" of consciousness, contact, feeling, perception, volition, and craving arising from each sense — offering two rival explanations for why the Buddha's sūtras organize experience this way: either to display the five aggregates and their cause, or to display the threefold accumulation of karma, affliction, and suffering.

4. The Eight Worldly Winds and the Buddha's Own Body

The fascicle's centerpiece is a sustained doctrinal polemic: the Vibhajyavādin and Mahāsāṃghika schools held that the Buddha's own physical body was itself uncontaminated, citing a sūtra's claim that the Tathāgata is "not defiled by worldly dharmas." This treatise rejects that reading outright, marshaling a remarkable roll call of concrete proof-cases — a woman's lust, Aṅgulimāla's hatred, Uruvilvā-Kāśyapa's delusion, a brahmin's conceit, all directed at the Buddha's own body — to argue that a body capable of provoking such afflictions in others cannot itself be uncontaminated, and that the sūtra's phrase refers instead to the Buddha's dharma-body alone.

By which level of concentration are the knowledge of others' minds and mundane wisdom eliminated? Answer: the knowledge of others' minds is eliminated by the four dhyānas or the stage of proximate access; mundane wisdom is eliminated by the seven fundamental grounds or the stage of proximate access. The knowledge of others' minds and mundane wisdom are both explained at length elsewhere. The knowledge of others' minds is fettered to four grounds, or is unfettered. That fettered to the first dhyāna is eliminated by the first dhyāna's own stage of proximate access, the meditative interval, or the second dhyāna's own proximate-access ground; and so on clear through that fettered to the fourth dhyāna, which is eliminated by the four dhyānas' own stage of proximate access, the meditative interval, or the proximate-access ground of the sphere of infinite space.
