The Faculties Aggregate Concludes

Fascicle 156 completes the One-Mind Nyāsa's cross-association census, opens two further new chapters in swift succession — the Fish Nyāsa's exhaustive possession census, named for how difficult it is to pin down, and the Cause-Condition Nyāsa — and with them brings the entire Sixth Aggregate, the Faculties Aggregate, to its close after sixty-four fascicles. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 156 concludes the One-Mind Nyāsa (一心納息第五之二), then opens the Fish Nyāsa (魚納息第六) and the Cause-Condition Nyāsa (因緣納息第七) in succession. Terms established across fascicles 93–155 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Right View and Right Thought: A Full Tetralemma of Mutual Association

Fascicle 156 opens by completing the One-Mind Nyāsa's investigation into the unlearner's own noble path-factors, asking whether the arhat's right view and right thought are mutually associated: since right view is available across every meditative ground but not every uncontaminated mind, while right thought is the reverse, a full four-way tetralemma results, carefully distinguishing the mental factors found in each cluster from those found in neither.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three technical reasons given for why a substance is never associated with itself — no single nature co-arises with itself twice, earlier and later instants never coincide, and no dharma treats its own substance as its own condition — are a small masterclass in metaphysical bookkeeping: association, on this account, always requires two genuinely distinct things standing in a real relation, never a thing quietly relating to itself under two different names.

2. Full, Middling, and Narrow Tetralemmas Across Every Remaining Path-Factor

The same cross-association census runs through right view against right effort, right thought against right effort and wisdom, and right effort against right mindfulness — each pairing classified as a "full," "middling," or "narrow" tetralemma depending on how widely each factor is itself obtainable, with right view and genuine wisdom alone declared strictly incompatible, since the two can never co-arise in the same moment at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That this census bothers to name three distinct grades of tetralemma — full, middling, narrow — purely based on how broadly each paired factor is independently obtainable, is a tidy piece of logical taxonomy: the same four-cornered question format is shown to behave in structurally different ways depending on nothing more than the relative scope of what is being compared.

3. A New Chapter Opens: The Fish Nyāsa, Named for What Is Impossible to Grasp

The fascicle abruptly opens an entirely new nyāsa — asking, for each of the twenty-two faculties in turn, which others its possessor invariably also possesses — and pauses first to explain its curious name: "Fish," because faculty-possession shifts across so many different positions (complete or incomplete organs, sexual form, mindful or mindless states, fixed or unfixed destiny, realm of birth, severed or intact wholesome roots, and more) that pinning it down feels exactly like trying to grasp a slippery fish.

4. The Eye-Faculty's Own Guaranteed Companions, and the Faculties That Follow It

The census proper begins: whoever possesses the eye-faculty invariably possesses five further faculties (eye, body, life, mind, equanimity), while every other faculty's presence remains genuinely unfixed, tracking gradual death, ordinary versus noble status, and freedom from contamination — the same pattern then extended through the body-, female-, and male-faculties, each anchored by its own small, invariant core.

The fascicle closes by opening yet a third new nyāsa in a single fascicle — the Cause-Condition Nyāsa — framed specifically around two of the four classical conditions, the cause-condition and the object-condition, since the immediately-preceding condition applies only for a single instant and the dominant condition applies so universally that framing the discussion around either would make the text unmanageably sprawling; a dense summary then maps, faculty by faculty, exactly which time-periods and which of the five categories of cutting-off each faculty can take as its object.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Faculties Aggregate, within the Cause-Condition Nyāsa, Chapter Seven: "whatever faculty is bound to the past — does that faculty take the past as its object?" Such is the chapter heading, and such is the explanation of its meaning. Having grasped this, a full analysis should now be given. One might ask: why raise this discussion at all? Answer: to block the view of those who deny the reality of the past and future time-periods, and of those who deny the reality of the cause-condition and object-condition altogether, and so on, as stated at length — it is for this reason that the present discussion is raised. This discussion is framed specifically around two conditions — the cause-condition and the object-condition. One might ask: why is it not framed around the remaining two conditions as well? Answer: the immediately-preceding condition applies only for a single instant, while the dominant condition applies universally to every dharma whatsoever — were the discussion framed around these instead, its own wording would become far too unwieldy and sprawling; it is for this reason that the discussion is framed specifically around these two conditions alone. A brief summary of the Vibhāṣā's own treatment should be given here: among the twenty-two faculties, fourteen take an object, and eight take no object at all. Among those that take an object, the class associated with the five sense-consciousness-bodies takes the past as its object when itself past, and the present as its object when itself present; when future, if it is a dharma capable of arising, it takes the future as its object, while if it is a dharma incapable of arising, it takes all three time-periods as its object. The class associated with the mental-consciousness-body, whichever time-period it itself belongs to, whether it is a dharma capable of arising or incapable of arising, always takes all three time-periods and the unconditioned as its object. Moreover, whatever faculty is cut off by seeing suffering or by seeing origin pervasively takes all five categories as its object; whatever faculty is cut off by seeing cessation or by seeing the path takes only its own category and what is uncuttable as its object; whatever faculty is uncuttable pervasively takes all five categories together with the uncuttable as its object. Moreover, whatever faculty is bound to the desire or form realm, or unbound, pervasively takes as its object whatever is bound to any of the three realms, or unbound; whatever faculty is bound to the formless realm takes as its object only whatever is bound to the form or formless realm, or unbound. The differences of cause and condition should likewise be inferred accordingly. This, then, is the brief exposition of the meaning taught here; following the text, its fuller explanation should be understood exactly as reason dictates. Within this passage, every first turn of phrase states the cause briefly while stating the object at length, and every later turn of phrase states the object briefly while stating the cause at length. Throughout this passage, whenever the various patient-acceptances are spoken of, they are named after "knowledge," since they belong to knowledge's own retinue.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The frank admission that two of the four classical conditions are simply too extreme in scope — one too narrow, one too broad — to organize a coherent discussion around is a rare moment of explicit compositional reasoning: rather than mechanically applying every available analytical category to every topic, this tradition is shown here actively selecting its own tools based on which ones will actually produce a manageable, well-proportioned text.
