The King Who Remembered His Own Palace

Fascicle 18 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa's tenth part (智納息第二之十), opening in the middle of a dispute over whether matter's own homogeneous-cause can cross between different bodies, then working out an intricate lattice of causal rules across wholesome, unwholesome, and undefined dharmas alike — illustrated along the way by the Venerable Mṛgaśiras discovering, tree by tree, that a single forest was once the site of his own past-life palace, execution ground, and labor camp. The fascicle closes by opening the universally-pervasive-cause, mapped realm by realm and stage by stage all the way to the very peak of existence. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 18 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa opened in Fascicle 9, now in its tenth part (之十), and opens mid-argument, directly continuing fascicle 17's own closing debate over whether material form possesses a homogeneous-cause. Terms established in earlier fascicles, such as the homogeneous-cause's own basic definition and the nine grades of affliction, recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Does Matter's Homogeneous-Cause Cross Between Bodies? Five Rival Positions

Fascicle 18 opens mid-debate, continuing directly from fascicle 17's grass-without-seed dispute: granting that material form does possess a homogeneous-cause, does that causal relationship ever cross from one body to another, and does it ever skip between dissimilar embryological stages (the classical sequence of kalala, arbuda, and so on) rather than only linking a stage to its own identical counterpart? Four increasingly permissive positions are tested in turn — same body and same stage only; same body across dissimilar stages; both bodies but same stage only; both bodies and dissimilar stages, but never reaching back to an earlier stage.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Every one of the first four positions is undone by the very same objection, restated with mechanical precision each time: whichever boundary a theory draws, it strands either the very first moment of matter in that boundary without any cause, or the very last moment without any effect — a violation this school treats as intolerable. The final verdict resolves the deadlock by simply removing the restriction on temporal direction as well as bodily and stage boundaries: a later dharma that has already arisen can serve as homogeneous-cause for an earlier dharma not yet arisen, since “earlier” and “later” track only the given pair's own arising order, not some fixed global sequence — a genuinely elegant solution once stated, though it takes four failed attempts to arrive at it.

2. The Five Aggregates, and the Four-Way Split of the Undefined

The discussion moves from a single individual's own body to the causal texture of moral quality itself: wholesome, defiled, and undefined aggregates each mutually cause their own kind, and the treatise then subdivides the undefined into four types — ripening-produced, tied to deportment, tied to craft, and belonging to transformation-fruit consciousness — before working out, position by position, exactly which of these four can cause which others, and settling a further dispute over whether undefined form and the undefined mental aggregates can cause one another at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The recurring justification that “a superior dharma cannot be caused by an inferior one” does real structural work throughout this whole fascicle: it is invoked here to block undefined form from causing the undefined mental aggregates, invoked again below to block innate wholesomeness from receiving cause from effort-based wholesomeness, and invoked once more to rank freed-from-defilement wholesomeness above the merely innate — a single asymmetry principle, superior can cause inferior but never the reverse, quietly generating an entire lattice of permitted and forbidden causal links across totally different subject matter.

3. Nine Grades of Affliction, Three Kinds of Wholesome, Four Grades of the Path

The moral-quality taxonomy sharpens further: defiled dharmas sort into nine grades (lower-lower through upper-upper) that freely cause one another, justified by the matching nine grades of antidote and of actual manifestation; wholesome dharmas split into innate versus effort-based, or, on a finer three-way cut, into effort-based, freed-from-defilement, and innate, each ranked so that only the equal or superior can serve as homogeneous-cause for the inferior, never the reverse; and effort-based wholesomeness itself further divides into hearing, reflection, and cultivation, with cultivation's own four grades — heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme mundane dharma — mapped onto the desire realm's four transformation-fruit minds tied to the four dhyānas.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rejected symmetric position — that these various grades or categories simply cause one another freely, “since they share the same binding and the same nature” — is refuted the same way twice over in this single passage, once for the nine grades' own moral pairing and once for the two kinds of wholesome, and each time the refutation appeals to a feared practical consequence rather than pure definition: symmetric causation would let a practitioner's careful cultivation of a superior discipline retrogress into causing an inferior one, or would make preparatory-effort virtues promiscuously cause each other's inferior counterparts — the treatise consistently prefers whichever causal rule protects the directionality of spiritual progress from being undone by its own logic.

4. Consciousness and Its Own Transformation Mind: An Asymmetric Pair

A final case study closes out the moral-quality survey: the first dhyāna's ordinary body of consciousnesses can serve as homogeneous-cause for that same stage's transformation mind (the mind that produces supernormal manifestations), but never the reverse, since the transformation mind is judged too weak to cause anything back. The treatise then compiles a tidy summary of every boundary condition uncovered so far — different realm, different stage, contaminated versus uncontaminated, different category or nature, and superior over inferior — each illustrated as a case where an earlier-arising dharma fails to serve as homogeneous-cause for a later one.

10. Defining the Universally-Pervasive-Cause: Only Forward, Never Backward

The treatise's own definition finally arrives: an earlier-arising universally-pervasive latent tendency abandoned by seeing suffering causes only later-arising, same-realm latent tendencies abandoned by seeing the origin, cessation, and cultivation — never a dharma abandoned by seeing suffering in return. The same close reading applied to the homogeneous-cause's defining passage in fascicle 17 (earlier-later phrasing rather than a bare reference to the past, to avoid implying backward causation or same-moment mutual causation) is applied again here, together with a fresh question about whether the treatise's mention of “own realm” ought equally to have specified “own stage.”

The universally-pervasive-cause turns out to be, in effect, a specialized, direction-locked subspecies of the homogeneous-cause: it shares the same earlier-later logic and the same same-realm restriction, but adds a strict one-way traffic rule — from what is abandoned by seeing suffering toward everything abandoned afterward, never the reverse and never sideways within the very same section — a refinement that lets the treatise capture something the plain homogeneous-cause could not: why seeing the first noble truth carries a uniquely foundational, forward-propagating causal weight relative to what is abandoned by seeing the truths that follow it.

11. The Eleven Universally-Pervasive Latent Tendencies, Realm by Realm

12. Twenty-Seven or Thirty-Three? A Reciting Tradition's Own Dispute

A genuine cross-lineage textual variant surfaces: the Western masters' own recited version of the Prakaraṇapāda gives twenty-seven universally-pervasive latent tendencies rather than thirty-three, with six requiring further case-by-case distinction — a formulation the treatise judges more precise, since it explicitly carves out the not-held-in-common ignorance as a separate, independently-pervasive category, rather than folding it silently into the associated ignorance's own count. Yet the Kashmiri masters deliberately decline to adopt this more precise recitation, preferring their own thirty-three-count tradition because, on their reckoning, the greater portion of the relevant instances already are pervasive.

13. What Does 'Universally Pervasive' Actually Mean? Three Kinds of 'All'

The treatise supplies its own rich etymology for “pervasive”: taking everything as its object, and sustaining its own reach through the power of that object. A threefold gloss of “all” follows — the “original all,” meaning it has manifested across every one of the nine grades since beginningless time; the “middle all,” meaning no sentient being has ever failed to give rise to it; and the “final all,” meaning it universally takes every contaminated phenomenon as its object — grounded in the Prajñaptiśāstra's own sweeping claim that no ordinary being has ever gone through beginningless time without clinging to self, grasping eternalism or annihilationism, or falling into doubt and blind unknowing.

14. Do a Pervasive Latent Tendency's Own Companions Also Count as Pervasive?

A structurally familiar question, already answered once for the homogeneous-cause in fascicle 17, is now re-asked for the universally-pervasive-cause: do the dharmas associated with, and co-existent with, a universally-pervasive latent tendency also themselves count as a universally-pervasive-cause? The treatise's own verdict says yes to both, since the very meaning of “cause” extends to whatever shares the same fruit — but then has to explain why the Prakaraṇapāda's count of thirty-three latent tendencies never separately mentions these associated and co-existent dharmas at all.

The three-feature analysis offered here — pervasive latency, pervasive object-taking, and pervasive causal function, of which the latent tendency itself possesses all three, its associated dharmas only two, and its co-existent dharmas only one — is a near-exact structural echo of the earlier correspondence-cause versus co-existent-cause distinction from fascicle 16, where a shared grip on support, object, and aspect was similarly split apart, feature by feature, from what that shared grip actually goes on to accomplish; the same analytic instinct for decomposing an apparently single relation into a graded bundle of separable features recurs across entirely different causal categories throughout this treatise.

15. Can an Acquisition Itself Be Universally Pervasive? A Refuted Argument

A parallel dispute to one already settled for the homogeneous-cause's arising-and-ceasing marks is now run for the universally-pervasive-cause: is the mere “acquisition” (prāpti) of a universally-pervasive latent tendency itself to be counted as pervasive? The Venerable Saṃghavasu argues yes, by a symmetry consideration — if the pervasive's own acquisition were not pervasive, the non-pervasive's own acquisition would have to be pervasive instead, which is absurd — but the treatise rejects this reasoning outright, comparing it to the equally absurd suggestion that the mere acquisition of matter should itself count as a form of matter.

Saṃghavasu's argument and its refutation together stage a small lesson in category mistakes: acquisition is a formation dissociated from mind, tracking whether a practitioner currently possesses a given dharma, and it is a mistake to expect it to inherit that dharma's own substantive qualities merely because the two are intimately linked — just as owning a house does not make the deed itself into lumber and nails, possessing a pervasive latent tendency does not make the mere fact of possession into something pervasive in its own right.

16. Why Only Suffering and Origin? Seven Competing Explanations

The fascicle's most sustained single debate closes it out: why is a universally-pervasive latent tendency established only among the dharmas abandoned by seeing suffering and the origin, and never among those abandoned by seeing cessation and the path? Seven distinct explanations are canvassed in turn — kinship and lineage; extreme firmness born of shared intent, illustrated by a city unified against a besieging enemy; sustenance by self-view; the capacity to take every contaminated cause and effect as object; a fixed pattern of ever-increasing growth (moon-gazing benefits the eyes, sun-gazing damages them); operating through two distinct gateways of latent function rather than one; and the bare fact that every latent tendency falls under either the truth of suffering or the truth of origin to begin with.

Some say: it is because, with respect to their own object, these latent tendencies are fixed in a pattern of ever-increasing growth — that is, the latent tendencies abandoned by seeing suffering and the origin all take a contaminated object, and, in following after this contaminated object, they grow ever more increased, just as a person gazing at the moon thereby benefits his own eyesight; whereas the latent tendencies abandoned by seeing cessation and the path may take either a contaminated or an uncontaminated object — and those that take an uncontaminated object thereby grow ever more diminished, in whatever degree they take that object, just as a person gazing at the sun thereby damages his own eyesight — and it is because this pattern is not fixed that no universally-pervasive latent tendency is established among them. Some say: it is because these latent tendencies here operate through two distinct kinds of latent function — that is, the latent tendencies abandoned by seeing suffering and the origin both perform their latent function through two distinct gateways: first, through taking an object; second, through association. The latent tendencies abandoned by seeing cessation and the path, by contrast, may perform their latent function through either of these two gateways, or through association alone — being, in this respect, not fixed — and it is for this reason that no universally-pervasive latent tendency can be established among them. Some say: this matter is fixed in that it rests upon exactly two feet, the meaning being as already stated above. 17. Why Craving, Hatred, and Conceit Never Qualify

Even within the section of dharmas abandoned by seeing suffering and the origin, three specific afflictions — craving, hatred, and conceit — are excluded from pervasive status, for reasons distinct from the seven just canvassed: they are each tied to one's own particular body or a particular part of it, arising with respect to this person and not that one, unlike self-view's own capacity to seize an entire realm, destiny, and birth all at once in a single moment of thought; and they can only be brought to a blazing pitch through dependence on a spouse, wealth, or a rival, unlike a pervasive latent tendency's effortless, self-sustaining flow, like a river.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: why is it that craving, hatred, and conceit, though abandoned by seeing suffering and the origin, are not universally pervasive? The answer: because none of these three bear the characteristic mark of universal pervasiveness — that which is universally pervasive must be able to take everything as its object, whereas these three are unable to do so. Some say: these three are subsumed under afflictions of one's own particular characteristic, whereas only those subsumed under afflictions of a shared, common characteristic can be established as universally pervasive. How, then, are these three each subsumed under one's own particular characteristic? Whoever gives rise to craving does so either with respect to his own body and not another's, or with respect to another's body and not his own, or with respect to this or that particular part of the body, distinctly, in each separate case — and hatred and conceit are exactly the same — and so these are each subsumed under one's own particular characteristic. Self-view and the rest, by contrast, within a single moment of thought, universally take an entire realm, an entire destiny, and the very fact of birth itself as their object, whether clinging to it as a self or as belonging to a self, or persisting on through every degree of blind, unknowing darkness — and so these are subsumed under a shared, common characteristic. Some say: it is because craving, hatred, and conceit are difficult to bring to a blazing pitch — that is, craving, hatred, and conceit can only be brought to a blazing pitch in dependence upon a spouse, wealth and possessions, or an enemy and a rival, whereas a universally-pervasive latent tendency can easily be brought to a blazing pitch, continuing on of its own accord, effortlessly, like the ceaseless flowing of a river. Some say: it is because wrong view, doubt, and ignorance are able to take all four noble truths as their object, and it is among these that a universally-pervasive latent tendency can be established, whereas craving, hatred, and conceit possess no such capacity, and so no universally-pervasive latent tendency is established among them. One asks: why is it that craving, hatred, and conceit, together with view-grasping and precept-and-vow-grasping, when abandoned by seeing cessation and the path, do not take an uncontaminated object? The answer: because it would be improper to reproach the uncontaminated with these; the uncontaminated bears no characteristic of malice; its nature is gentle and accommodating; it is the very finest and most supreme; and it is utterly pure — and it is for these reasons that craving and the rest do not take an uncontaminated object.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction drawn here between “own particular characteristic” and “shared common characteristic” afflictions gives this whole discussion a genuinely different axis of analysis than the seven explanations preceding it: those explanations addressed why the truth of suffering and origin, as sections, uniquely license pervasiveness, while this passage addresses why, even within that licensed territory, some individual afflictions still fail to qualify — a needed second cut, since without it nothing would explain why view and doubt succeed where craving, hatred, and conceit, despite belonging to the very same two truths, do not.

18. Which Realm Can an Affliction Reach? The Unfaithful Husband Simile

The fascicle's closing survey maps exactly which realms and stages a universally-pervasive latent tendency can take as its object: nine of the desire realm's own eleven can reach across into the higher realms, while self-view and extreme-view remain confined to their own realm, since they operate only on directly-perceived coarse aggregates; and a general principle emerges that a higher realm's afflictions can never reach down into a lower one, since one must already be free of a lower realm's defilement before a higher realm's affliction can even manifest, whereas a lower realm's own indeterminate, unrestrained nature lets its afflictions freely reach upward.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

19. Form, Formless, and the Nine Stages: Closing the Ceiling

The same object-reach survey is completed for every remaining stage: the form realm mirrors the desire realm's own nine-reach-across, two-confined pattern; the formless realm, having no realm above it, confines every one of its eleven latent tendencies strictly to itself, with a rejected minority view proposing an unmanifested capacity to reach upward regardless; and the same nine-and-two pattern recurs across the four dhyānas and the lower formless spheres, before the very peak of existence, the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, closes the entire hierarchy by confining all eleven of its own latent tendencies strictly to itself, for lack of anywhere higher left to reach.

Fascicle 19 continues the same Knowledge Nyāsa, now in its eleventh part (智納息第二之十一).
