A Meditation on Being Insulted

Fascicle 14 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa's sixth part (雜蘊第一中智納息第二之六), first completing the fourfold-alternative analysis of past, exhausted, and ceased fetters across every stage of the path, then opening a new question about doubt itself — illustrated by ten legendary Vedic sages who never managed to cross it. The fascicle closes with an extended treatise on language as real, substantial dharma, including one of this treatise's most immediately practical passages: a step-by-step meditation for defusing anger at being insulted, grounded in the story of Prince Virūḍhaka's catastrophic massacre. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Is Everything Past Also Exhausted? The Stream-Enterer's Freedom From Three Evil Destinies

Fascicle 14 opens by continuing the fourfold-alternative method from Fascicle 13, now applied to a new pairing: is everything past also exhausted? The Buddha's own declaration that a noble disciple has already exhausted hell, animal rebirth, hungry ghosts, and every dangerous pit of evil destiny draws nine separate explanations for what that final, seemingly redundant fourth phrase actually adds.

Chinese Source

諸過去彼一切盡耶。答應作四句。過去有二種。如前說。盡亦有二種。如過去說。此中俱依二種作論。過去與盡互有廣狹。故作四句。有過去非盡。謂如具壽鄔陀夷言。一切結過去。乃至廣說。過去者是第二過去。非盡者非初盡。佛身現在已斷盡故。有盡非過去。謂如佛言。此聖弟子已盡地獄。已盡傍生。已盡餓鬼。已盡所有險惡趣坑。問此中已盡地獄者。顯聖弟子已盡地獄。已盡傍生者。顯此已盡傍生。已盡餓鬼者。顯此已盡餓鬼。已盡所有險惡趣坑者。顯此更何所盡耶。答即顯上三然前廣後略。前別後總前漸後頓。前分別後不分別。有說。已盡地獄傍生餓鬼者。顯此已盡三惡趣自性。已盡所有險惡趣坑者。顯此已盡彼中有。有說。已盡地獄等者。顯已盡地獄等。已盡所有險惡趣坑者。顯已盡人中扇[打-丁+虎]半擇迦無形二形。彼是人中險惡趣坑故。有說。前顯已盡地獄傍生餓鬼。後顯已盡住不律儀。彼當墮險惡趣坑故有說。前顯已盡地獄傍生餓鬼。後顯已盡造五無間業。彼無間生墮地獄故。有說前顯已盡地獄傍生餓鬼。後顯已盡斷善根者。以斷善者如險惡趣坑故。若不續善根死必墮地獄故。有說。前顯已盡地獄等果。後顯已盡地獄等因。如契經說。汝等苾芻。若見行身語意惡行者。應知已見地獄或餘惡趣。有說。前顯已盡地獄傍生餓鬼。後復言已盡所有險者。重顯已盡地獄。以地獄中無善果故。惡趣者重顯已盡餓鬼。彼常貧窮乏資緣故。坑者重顯已盡傍生。身心墮彼難可出故。有成劫時生。彼壞劫時方命終故。有說。前顯已盡地獄傍生餓鬼。後復言已盡所有險惡趣坑者。皆重顯已盡三惡趣果。謂三惡趣皆是極險。眾惡所趣所墮坑故。問今地獄中猶有種種鑊湯爐炭。及獄卒等無量苦具。如何言盡耶。答不往不生故說為盡。謂聖弟子不復往彼處。不復生彼蘊界處故。此已得彼非擇滅故。問亦有異生得地獄等非擇滅者。何故但說聖弟子耶。答諸聖弟子皆已盡故。異生品中有未盡者。是故不說。問諸聖弟子亦盡人天。何故但言盡地獄等。答此中但說一切盡者。諸聖弟子於人天趣。有未盡者故不說之是謂盡。非過去盡者是第二盡。非過去者非初過去。有過去亦盡。謂所有行已起等起。乃至廣說。此皆顯示過去諸行。過去者是世過去盡亦爾。有非過去亦非盡謂除前相。

Ru-Yi Translation

[The topic reads:] "Is everything past also exhausted?" The answer: this should be put in the form of four alternatives. "Past" is of two kinds, as explained above; "exhausted" is likewise of two kinds, in the same way as "past." Here, the topic is composed with reference to both kinds of each together, and since "past" and "exhausted" differ in scope, broader and narrower, four alternatives are accordingly formulated. There is a case of what is past but not exhausted — as when the Venerable Udāyin said: "all fetters have passed away" — and so on, as stated at length; "past" here is the second kind of "past" (yogic past), and "not exhausted" means not the first kind of "exhausted," since the Buddha's own body, present at that time, had already cut off and exhausted the afflictions. There is a case of what is exhausted but not past — as when the Buddha said: "this noble disciple has already exhausted hell, already exhausted the animal realm, already exhausted the hungry-ghost realm, already exhausted every dangerous pit of evil destiny." One asks: here, "already exhausted hell" displays that the noble disciple has already exhausted hell; "already exhausted the animal realm" displays that he has already exhausted the animal realm; "already exhausted the hungry-ghost realm" displays that he has already exhausted the hungry-ghost realm; but "already exhausted every dangerous pit of evil destiny" — what further thing does this display as exhausted? The answer: it simply displays the same three just named, but the earlier statement is detailed while the latter is condensed; the earlier is particular while the latter is general; the earlier is gradual while the latter is all at once; the earlier is analytical while the latter is not. Some say: "already exhausted hell, the animal realm, and the hungry-ghost realm" displays that he has already exhausted the very own-nature of the three evil destinies, while "already exhausted every dangerous pit of evil destiny" displays that he has already exhausted the intermediate-state existence leading into them. Some say: "already exhausted hell and the rest" displays that he has already exhausted hell and the rest, while "already exhausted every dangerous pit of evil destiny" displays that he has already exhausted rebirth as a eunuch, a paṇḍaka, one without genitalia, or one with both sets of genitalia among human beings, since these are themselves a dangerous pit of evil destiny within the human realm. Some say: the earlier statement displays that he has already exhausted hell, the animal realm, and the hungry-ghost realm, while the latter displays that he has already exhausted abiding in unrestrained conduct, since such conduct is bound to lead to a dangerous pit of evil destiny. Some say: the earlier statement displays the same three, while the latter displays that he has already exhausted the committing of the five acts of immediate retribution, since these lead, without any interval, straight into hell. Some say: the earlier statement displays the same three, while the latter displays that he has already exhausted the cutting-off of wholesome roots, since one who has cut off wholesome roots is himself like a dangerous pit of evil destiny — for if one dies without having restored one's wholesome roots, one is bound to fall into hell. Some say: the earlier statement displays that he has already exhausted the very effects of hell and the rest, while the latter displays that he has already exhausted their causes, as the discourses state: "you monks, if you see one who practices evil conduct of body, speech, and mind, you should know that you have thereby already seen hell or some other evil destiny." Some say: the earlier statement displays that he has already exhausted hell, the animal realm, and the hungry-ghost realm, while the further statement "already exhausted every dangerous pit" repeats and re-emphasizes that he has already exhausted hell, since hell contains no wholesome fruit at all; "evil destiny" re-emphasizes that he has already exhausted the hungry-ghost realm, since hungry ghosts are always destitute and lack supporting conditions; "pit" re-emphasizes that he has already exhausted the animal realm, since a body and mind falling into it are difficult to escape — indeed, some animals born during the aeon of formation only die at the very aeon of destruction, so long is their span there. Some say: the earlier statement displays that he has already exhausted hell, the animal realm, and the hungry-ghost realm, while the further statement "already exhausted every dangerous pit of evil destiny" re-emphasizes, all together, that he has already exhausted the very fruit of all three evil destinies, since all three evil destinies are themselves an extreme danger — the very pit into which every evil deed leads and falls. One asks: hell still contains, even now, its various cauldrons, furnaces, and hot coals, and its hell-guardians and countless other instruments of torment — how, then, can it be said to be "exhausted"? The answer: it is called "exhausted" in the sense of no longer going there and no longer being born there — that is, a noble disciple no longer goes to that place, and no longer takes rebirth within its aggregates, elements, and sense fields, since he has already attained a non-analytical cessation with respect to it. One asks: there are also ordinary beings who attain a non-analytical cessation with respect to hell and the rest — why, then, does the treatise speak only of the noble disciple? The answer: because every noble disciple has already exhausted it completely, whereas, among the class of ordinary beings, there are some who have not yet exhausted it — and so the treatise does not speak of them. One asks: noble disciples have also exhausted the human and divine destinies as objects of ultimate concern — why, then, does the treatise speak only of exhausting hell and the rest? The answer: because this passage speaks only of what is exhausted entirely without exception, and, with respect to the human and divine destinies, there are noble disciples who have not yet exhausted rebirth there — and so the treatise does not speak of them. This, then, is what is meant by "exhausted." "Not past, exhausted" is the second kind of "exhausted" (not the deliberately achieved kind), and "not past" means not the first kind of "past." There is a case of what is both past and exhausted — namely, whatever formations have already arisen, already come to be — and so on, as stated at length; all such phrases together display past formations. "Past" here is past by time-period, and "exhausted" here is likewise so. There is a case of what is neither past nor exhausted — namely, whatever remains apart from the characteristics just described.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nine competing readings of one short phrase — is it summarizing, or singling out eunuchs and those without genitalia, or unrestrained conduct, or the five acts of immediate retribution, or cutting off wholesome roots, or simply re-emphasizing what came before — is a striking demonstration of how much interpretive energy this tradition was willing to invest in a single canonical sentence, treating no phrase as filler even when a plausible reading (mere repetition for emphasis) was sitting right there the whole time.

2. The Fourth Alternative, and Reframing "Exhausted" as Severing the Fetters

The residual fourth alternative closes out the time-based reading of "exhausted," before the treatise pivots to a second, stricter sense of the same word: not being exhausted by time or by no-longer-being-reborn, but by the actual, technical cutting off of a specific fetter through the path.

Here, the meaning of the word "characteristic" is to be understood exactly as explained at length above — and so on, up through: what, specifically, is this fourth alternative? It excludes every dharma of the past time-period, the Buddha's own present body, and the future aggregates, elements, and sense fields of hell and the rest belonging to a noble disciple, taking instead every remaining present dharma, every future dharma, and the unconditioned dharmas, thus forming the fourth alternative.

3. Past-Unexhausted Fetters Traced Across Every Stage of the Path

A genuinely exhaustive survey follows, tracking exactly which fetters count as past-but-unexhausted, exhausted-but-not-past, or both, for every single stage of realization — ordinary beings at every level of detachment, someone in the very moment of entering the path, and each of the four fruits from stream-entry through arhatship.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Read end to end, this survey functions as a complete map of the entire path of liberation viewed through a single lens — which fetters have and haven't yet been permanently severed — and the fact that every single stage, from a completely bound ordinary being to a fully liberated arhat, can be located precisely within this scheme is itself a quiet demonstration of the system's own claimed completeness: there is no possible spiritual condition this fourfold grid cannot classify exactly.

4. The Analysis Concludes, and a New Question: Is Everything Past Also Ceased?

11. The Five Mental Splinters: Four Doubts and One Anger

A parallel category — five "mental splinters" obstructing spiritual progress — turns out to be built from two entirely different afflictions: doubt regarding the Buddha, Dharma, precepts, and teaching, but anger, not doubt, regarding the Sangha — prompting a careful explanation of why the Sangha alone gets a different diagnosis, rooted in the plain fact that monastics, unlike the Buddha, do sometimes have real faults to notice.

12. A New Topic: Why Language Itself Deserves Its Own Treatise

The fascicle turns to an entirely different domain: language itself, treated as a real, technical subject matter in its own right — name, phrase, and letter, each held to be genuine dharmas dissociated from mind, not mere sound (against the Sound school) and not mere fiction (against the Dārṣṭāntikas) — with the treatise offering its own account of why a topic this seemingly mundane earns such extended treatment.

14. The Practical Payoff: Prince Virūḍhaka's Massacre, and a Meditation on Being Insulted

The treatise turns from abstract justification to devastating example: Prince Virūḍhaka, unable to see past a handful of insulting syllables — "you slave-girl's son, why do you ascend my Śākya hall?" — was driven to a massacre of countless beings, a catastrophe the treatise uses to introduce an extraordinarily practical meditation technique for defusing anger at being insulted, breaking an abusive phrase down syllable by syllable until its power to wound simply dissolves.

15. Four More Ways to Defuse Anger: Elements, Moments, and No-Self

The meditation on insult continues through four further angles: noticing that the abuser's own body contains vastly more non-abusing dharmas than abusing ones, that no single syllable spoken alone can ever constitute an insult, that both the abuser and the abused are perishing moment to moment so that neither party who could feel resentment actually persists long enough to do so, and finally that no self or agent can be found anywhere in the transaction at all.

17. What Is a Name? Following, Summoning, and Uniting in Correspondence

The technical grammar begins in earnest: a "name" is defined by three distinct functions — following whatever a thing does, summoning that thing's meaning whenever sought, and uniting with correspondence into whatever composition it appears in — a definition precise enough to exclude both bare mental events (which follow and summon, but never fully unite) and ordinary form (which follows and unites, but never summons).

19. The Multiple-Phrase-Body: "Do No Evil, Cultivate Good, Purify the Mind"

The famous four-line verse summarizing the teaching of every Buddha — "commit no evil, cultivate every wholesome deed, purify one's own mind, this is the teaching of all the Buddhas" — becomes the treatise's working example for phrase-structure, with each phrase analyzed into its own topic-and-explanation pair, and the verse as a whole tracked through four distinct levels of completion as it unfolds line by line.

Chinese Source

云何多句身。答諸句能滿未滿足義。於中連合是謂多句身。為成此義引經為證。如世尊說。

Ru-Yi Translation

"What is a multiple-phrase-body?" The answer: it is those phrases capable of fulfilling a meaning not yet fulfilled, joined together in sequence — this is what is meant by a multiple-phrase-body. In order to establish this meaning, the discourses are cited as proof, as the World-Honored One states:

諸惡莫作　諸善奉行　自淨其心是諸佛教如是四句各能滿足。未滿足義於中連合。是謂多句身。如是四句一一能滿。各自句中。未滿足義於中連合者。於四句中一一各別。連合諸字顯未滿義。或復連合諸句。顯成頌義為多句身。一一句中有標有釋。謂諸惡者是標。莫作者是釋。乃至是諸佛者是標。教者是釋。故此頌中有四事滿。一標二釋三句四頌。若說諸惡於標名滿。

"Commit no evil whatsoever; / cultivate every wholesome deed; / purify one's own mind entirely — / this is the teaching of all the Buddhas." Each of these four phrases is able to fulfill a meaning not yet fulfilled, joined together in sequence — this is what is meant by a multiple-phrase-body. As for each of these four phrases individually fulfilling, within its own phrase, a meaning not yet fulfilled, joined together in sequence: within each of the four phrases separately, the syllables are joined together to display the meaning not yet fulfilled; and, further, the phrases themselves are joined together to display the completed meaning of the verse as a whole, constituting the multiple-phrase-body. Within each individual phrase, there is a topic and an explanation — "every evil" is the topic, and "commit... whatsoever" is the explanation; and so on, up through "all the Buddhas" being the topic, and "teaching" being the explanation. Hence, within this verse, four things are fulfilled: first, the topic; second, the explanation; third, the phrase; fourth, the verse as a whole. If one merely says "every evil," this fulfills the topic —

於釋於句於頌未滿。復說莫作於標釋句三種名滿。於頌未滿。復說諸善若於總頌摽釋句滿。若於別句摽滿非餘。乃至復說是諸佛者若於總頌摽釋句滿。若於別句摽滿非餘。復說教者一切皆滿。此頌處中不長不短八字為句。三十二字為頌。諸經論頌多依此法。計書寫數亦依此法。又從六字乃至二十六字皆得為句。然六字者名為初句。二十六字者名為後句。減六字者名短句。過二十六字者名長句。

but not yet the explanation, the phrase, or the verse; when one further says "commit... whatsoever," this fulfills the topic, the explanation, and the phrase together, but not yet the verse; when one further says "every wholesome deed," this fulfills the topic, the explanation, and the phrase with respect to the verse as a whole, but with respect to that particular phrase alone, only the topic is fulfilled, nothing further; and so on, up through, when one further says "all the Buddhas," this fulfills the topic, the explanation, and the phrase with respect to the verse as a whole, but with respect to that particular phrase alone, only the topic is fulfilled, nothing further; when one further says "teaching," everything is entirely fulfilled. This verse's own meter — neither too long nor too short — takes eight syllables to a phrase, and thirty-two syllables to a verse; the verses found throughout the discourses and treatises mostly follow this same convention, and the reckoning of a text's own written length likewise follows this same convention. Further, anything from six syllables up to twenty-six syllables may constitute a phrase; a phrase of six syllables is called the shortest standard phrase, and one of twenty-six syllables the longest standard phrase; anything fewer than six syllables is called a "short phrase," and anything exceeding twenty-six syllables is called a "long phrase."

20. The Multiple-Letter-Body, and a Final Table of Syllable Counts

The grammatical survey closes with "letter" itself — the raw syllable, prior to any name or phrase — illustrated by a verse locating the true root of any composed verse in the composer's own desire to compose it, before a final systematic table walks through exactly how many syllables are needed to produce a name, phrase, or letter at each of their respective "body" and "multiple-body" levels, from a single syllable up through thirty-two.

Chinese Source

云何多文身。答諸字眾是謂多文身。為成此義引經為證。如世尊說。

Ru-Yi Translation

"What is a multiple-letter-body?" The answer: a mass of syllables — this is what is meant by a multiple-letter-body. In order to establish this meaning, the discourses are cited as proof, as the World-Honored One states:

"Desire is the root of the verse; / a letter is simply a syllable; / a verse depends on a name, / and on the one who composes the verse." Here, "desire" refers to the desire, the delight, the joy, and the fondness for composing a verse; "is the root of the verse" means that this very desire is the cause, the accumulating condition, and the arising condition of the verse. "A letter is simply a syllable" means that what is skillfully and clearly manifest is called a "letter" — it is precisely this same syllable, never transforming into anything else — this simply displays that the letter which manifests the verse's own text has the syllable as its very substance. "A verse depends on a name" means that a verse is a merely provisional existent, established in dependence on a name; it likewise depends on letters and phrases, though the verse speaks here only of depending on a name. Here, the word "depends" is used just as a forest is said to depend on its trees. "And on the one who composes the verse" means one who reflects, observes, and composes such verses is called "the one who composes the verse";
