The Sixty-Two Views

Fascicle 199 continues the Nyāsa of Views with a creator-god view logically refuted, the biographical episode of the naked ascetic Kāśyapa cycling through four causal theories across four failed sea voyages, four ways the eye might be identified with the self, and two views mistaking sensory pleasure or the four dhyānas themselves for nirvāṇa. A digression explains why this chapter on views also analyzes nine kinds of conceit, before the fascicle closes with the Brahmajāla Sūtra's celebrated taxonomy of sixty-two view-tenets — eternalism, causeless arising, finitude and infinitude, and the four calculated equivocators who refuse ever to give a definite answer. This page carries Ru‑Yi's complete translation.

1. "A Sovereign Lord's Transformation Is the Cause": A Creator-God View Refuted

The catalogue of wrong views continues with the claim that everything a person experiences is caused by the transformation of a sovereign lord — met not with a bare counter-assertion but with a chain of logical objections: if the cause is always present, why does everything not arise all at once, and if the products of a single, undifferentiated cause ought themselves to be undifferentiated and permanent, how do they come to differ and to perish?

Chinese Source

諸有此見一切士夫補特伽羅。諸有所受皆以自在變化為因。此非因計因戒禁取見苦所斷。此非因計因戒禁取者顯彼自性。見苦所斷者顯彼對治廣說如前。彼等起云何。謂有外道或因不正尋思。或因得定或因惡友而起此見如前應知。然諸法生非因自在漸次生故。謂諸世間若因自在變化生者則應一切俱時而生。彼因皆有無能障礙令不生故。若謂自在更待餘因方能生者便非自在如餘因故。若謂諸法皆從自在欲樂而生故不頓起。自在欲樂何不頓生彼生欲樂自在恒有無能障故。若謂自在更待餘因方生欲樂便非自在。又應無窮彼因復待餘因生故。又若自在生諸法者因無別故法應無別。若謂自在生初一法後從彼法轉復生多。彼法云何能生多法亦如自在體是一故。又所生法亦應是常果似因故。又自在體應不能生彼體是常如虛空故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whoever holds this view — that whatever every person or individual experiences invariably has the transformation of a sovereign lord as its cause — this is a wrong grasping at what is not a cause as if it were a cause, a grasping at precepts and vows, to be abandoned by seeing suffering. That this is a wrong grasping at what is not a cause as if it were a cause, a grasping at precepts and vows, reveals its intrinsic nature; that it is to be abandoned by seeing suffering reveals its antidote, explained at length as before. What gives rise to it? It means that there are ascetics who give rise to this view either through incorrect reasoning, or through attaining concentration, or through evil companions, as should be understood as explained before. Yet dharmas do not arise gradually caused by a sovereign lord — that is, if the various worlds arose caused by the transformation of a sovereign lord, then all of them ought to arise simultaneously, since that cause is always present and there is nothing capable of obstructing it from producing them. If it is said that the sovereign lord must still depend on some further cause before being able to produce them, then it is no longer sovereign, being just like any other cause. If it is said that all dharmas arise from the desire and pleasure of the sovereign lord, and so do not arise all at once — then why do the desire and pleasure of the sovereign lord themselves not arise all at once, since the desire and pleasure that give rise to them are, on their account, always present in the sovereign lord and nothing can obstruct them? If it is said that the sovereign lord must still depend on some further cause before even producing desire and pleasure, then it is no longer sovereign — and, further, this would lead to an infinite regress, since that further cause would itself depend on yet another cause to arise. Further, if the sovereign lord gives rise to all dharmas, then, since the cause is undifferentiated, the dharmas produced ought likewise to be undifferentiated. If it is said that the sovereign lord first produces a single dharma, and thereafter, turning from that dharma, produces many more — then how could that single dharma be capable of producing many dharmas, since it too, like the sovereign lord itself, has but a single substance? Further, the dharmas produced ought likewise to be permanent, since a fruit resembles its cause. Further, the substance of the sovereign lord itself ought to be incapable of producing anything at all, since its substance is permanent, just like empty space.

2. Experience Without Any Cause at All

The opposite extreme: that what one experiences has no cause or condition whatsoever, traced to ascetics who notice that effort does not always yield its expected fruit and conclude, too hastily, that there is no causal order at all.

Chinese Source

諸有此見一切士夫補特伽羅所受皆是無因無緣。此謗因邪見見集所斷。此謗因邪見者顯彼自性。見集所斷者顯彼對治廣說如前。彼等起云何。謂有外道見諸世間因果形相非定相似。諸有營求或不果遂便撥所受無因無緣。然諸所受非無因緣。現見諸法因緣生故非一切法一時生故。若無因緣應皆頓起應一切法無差別故。若無因緣。由何差別故諸所受非無因緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whoever holds this view — that whatever every person or individual experiences is entirely without cause and without condition — this is a wrong view that denies causation, to be abandoned by seeing origin. That it is a wrong view that denies causation reveals its intrinsic nature; that it is to be abandoned by seeing origin reveals its antidote, explained at length as before. What gives rise to it? It means that there are ascetics who observe that, in the world, the form of cause and effect is not always alike, and that, when people strive and seek something, it sometimes does not come to fruition, and so they deny that what is experienced has any cause or condition at all. Yet what is experienced is not without cause or condition — for it is directly observed that dharmas arise from causes and conditions, since not all dharmas arise all at once. If there were no cause or condition, everything ought to arise all at once, and every dharma ought to be undifferentiated. Since there is such differentiation, it is on this account that what is experienced is not without cause or condition.

3. Self-Caused or Other-Caused Suffering: The Naked Ascetic Kāśyapa

A rare biographical episode grounds the doctrine: the naked ascetic Kāśyapa, once a merchant sea-captain, cycles through four theories of why his voyages met disaster — his own fault, another's fault, both, and finally no cause at all — before abandoning household life. The treatise uses this case to draw a careful structural distinction within grasping at precepts and vows: grasping at what is not a cause as a cause, and grasping at what is not the path as the path, each cut off at a different stage of the path.

Chinese Source

諸有此見自作苦樂他作苦樂自他作苦樂。此非因計因戒禁取見苦所斷。諸有此見所受苦樂非自作非他作無因而生。此謗因邪見見集所斷。此非因計因戒禁取。及謗因邪見者顯彼自性。見苦及見集所斷者顯彼對治廣說如前。彼等起云何。謂無衣迦葉波因緣是此見等起。彼無衣迦葉波昔在家時曾為商主。數入海採寶最初入時逢諸海難辛苦得出便作是念。此難苦者是我自作坐入海時不洗浴故。彼於第二入時便自洗浴。既入海已遇難如前辛苦得還復作是念。此難苦者是他所作坐入海時不祠天故。彼於第三入時便自洗浴。及亦祠天既至海中如前遇難。困而得免。便作是念如是艱苦自作他作。坐入海時洗浴祠天不殷重故。彼於最後便極殷重洗浴祠天然後入海。入已遇難亦復如前僅得迴還便作是念。此所遭苦不由自他。但無因得。彼由此故便見居家攝受過失。即往無衣外道法中出家。後於王舍城見佛便問苦由誰作。爾時世尊以四記論法而調伏之。廣說如無衣迦葉波經。故彼因緣是此見等起。前來所說諸戒禁取皆見苦所斷者。依我常倒起於果處轉故。雖非因計因而見苦所斷。謂戒禁取總有二類。一非因計因。二非道計道。非因計因復有二類。一迷所執我常法起。二迷宿作苦行等起。前依我常倒亦於果處轉故。隨二倒見苦所斷後唯於果處轉。果相麁顯易可見故。計因為因非全邪故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whoever holds this view — that one causes one's own suffering and pleasure, or that another causes it, or that both oneself and another together cause it — this is a wrong grasping at what is not a cause as if it were a cause, a grasping at precepts and vows, to be abandoned by seeing suffering. Whoever holds this view — that the suffering and pleasure one experiences is caused neither by oneself nor by another, but arises without any cause at all — this is a wrong view that denies causation, to be abandoned by seeing origin. That this is a wrong grasping at what is not a cause as if it were a cause, a grasping at precepts and vows, and that this is a wrong view that denies causation, reveals their intrinsic nature; that they are to be abandoned by seeing suffering and by seeing origin reveals their antidote, explained at length as before. What gives rise to it? It means that the incident of the naked ascetic Kāśyapa is what gives rise to this view. This naked Kāśyapa, when he was formerly a householder, had once been the leader of a merchant caravan, and repeatedly went out to sea to gather treasure. The first time he went out, he met with disaster at sea and, after great hardship, managed to escape; he then reflected: this disaster and hardship is of my own making, because, when I set out to sea, I did not bathe. On his second voyage, he therefore bathed himself before setting out; yet, once at sea, he again met with disaster and, after hardship as before, managed to return, and reflected: this disaster and hardship is caused by another, because, when I set out to sea, I did not offer sacrifice to the gods. On his third voyage, he therefore bathed himself and also offered sacrifice to the gods; yet, upon reaching the sea, he again met with disaster as before, and, though in distress, managed to escape, and reflected: such hardship is caused both by myself and by another — because, when I set out to sea, my bathing and sacrifice were not performed with sufficient earnestness. On his final voyage, he therefore bathed and offered sacrifice with the utmost earnestness before setting out to sea; yet, having set out, he again met with disaster, and, as before, barely managed to return, and reflected: this suffering I have met with is caused neither by myself nor by another, but is simply obtained without any cause at all. On account of this, he came to see the fault of remaining attached to household life, and went forth as a renunciant among the naked ascetics. Later, in Rājagṛha, he met the Buddha and asked him by whom suffering is caused; at that time the World-Honored One tamed him by means of the four modes of explanation. This is explained at length in the Sūtra of the Naked Ascetic Kāśyapa. Thus this incident is what gives rise to this view. As for why every one of the grasping-at-precepts-and-vows views spoken of above are said to be abandoned by seeing suffering: because they arise from the distortion of holding the self to be permanent, and operate within the domain of the fruit; although they are a case of grasping at what is not a cause as if it were a cause, they are nonetheless to be abandoned by seeing suffering. That is, grasping at precepts and vows is altogether of two kinds: first, grasping at what is not a cause as if it were a cause; and second, grasping at what is not the path as if it were the path. Grasping at what is not a cause as if it were a cause is, in turn, of two kinds: first, that which arises from delusion about the self one grasps at as permanent; and second, that which arises from delusion about the ascetic practices one performed in a former life. The former, arising from the distortion of holding the self to be permanent, also operates within the domain of the fruit; following upon these two distortions, it is to be abandoned by seeing suffering, since it operates only within the domain of the fruit thereafter, the characteristic of the fruit being coarse and manifest and easily perceived, and grasping at a cause as if it were a cause not being altogether erroneous —

既迷果相故亦見苦所斷。非道計道亦有二類。一執有漏戒等為道。

and because it is deluded about the characteristic of the fruit, it too is to be abandoned by seeing suffering. Grasping at what is not the path as if it were the path is likewise of two kinds: first, grasping at contaminated precepts and the like as the path;

此迷麁顯果相起故見苦諦時便能永斷。二執謗道諦邪見等為道。此親違道於因果相不別迷執故見道時方能永斷。謗集滅時既撥所斷所證法相。若執為道便為無用。定依所斷及所證法而立道故。又彼所撥與道相異必無彼無間執彼為道者。若於後時執彼為道定於果處而起道執。見苦諦時此見便斷故。無戒禁取見集滅所斷。見取所執無所待對但執為勝諸邪見後皆得現前故通四部。此中所說諸戒禁取唯非因計因故見苦所斷。

since this arises from delusion about a coarse and manifest characteristic of the fruit, it is thereupon permanently cut off at the moment of seeing the truth of suffering. Second, grasping at the wrong view that denies the truth of the path, and the like, as the path itself. Since this directly opposes the path, and is deluded and attached without distinguishing the characteristics of cause and effect, it is only cut off at the moment of seeing the path. As for the moment of denying the truths of origin and cessation: since one has already dismissed the very dharma-characteristics that are to be abandoned and to be realized, grasping at this denial itself as the path would be of no use, since the path is necessarily established in dependence on the dharmas to be abandoned and to be realized. Further, what one has thereby dismissed is distinct from the path, and there can be no case of grasping it as the path immediately thereafter; if, at some later time, one does grasp it as the path, this grasping of the path necessarily arises only within the domain of the fruit, and is thereupon cut off at the moment of seeing the truth of suffering. It is for this reason that grasping at precepts and vows is not to be abandoned by seeing origin or cessation. As for grasping at mere views: since what it grasps at has no counterpart to depend upon, but simply grasps at something as supreme, and every wrong view can subsequently come to be present in this way, it extends through all four categories. What is spoken of here as grasping at precepts and vows, however, is solely a case of grasping at what is not a cause as if it were a cause, and is therefore to be abandoned by seeing suffering alone.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story of the naked ascetic Kāśyapa is one of the few genuinely narrative, psychologically observed episodes in this catalogue of wrong views — a man empirically iterating through four causal hypotheses across four failed voyages before concluding there is no cause at all, only then abandoning household life. It reads less like a doctrinal abstraction than a case study in how a reasonable person, working only from repeated personal failure, can be driven step by step toward nihilism.

4. The Self and World Are Permanent; Four Ways the Eye Observes the Self

A cluster of eternalist and self-view doctrines: that the self and world are firm and unchanging; a subtler pair of formulas — "the self exists as self" against "the self is without self" — distinguished from the Buddhist teaching of non-self by whether a self is falsely presupposed in the first place; and four permutations of how the eye and visible form might each be identified as the self or as its mere accoutrement.

Chinese Source

諸有此見我及世間常恒堅住無變易法正爾安住。此邊執見常見攝見苦所斷。此邊執見常見攝者顯彼自性。見苦所斷者顯彼對治。廣說如前。彼等起云何。謂有外道或因不正尋思。或因得定或因惡友而起此見。如前應知。然彼所執我及世間皆非常住。實我我所不可得故。現見一切有情世間器世間物有轉變故。因緣生故。諸有生者。

Whoever holds this view — that the self and the world are permanent, everlasting, firm, abiding, unchanging dharmas, presently and permanently abiding — this is a view of extremism subsumed under eternalism, to be abandoned by seeing suffering. That this view of extremism is subsumed under eternalism reveals its intrinsic nature; that it is to be abandoned by seeing suffering reveals its antidote, explained at length as before. What gives rise to it? It means that there are ascetics who give rise to this view either through incorrect reasoning, or through attaining concentration, or through evil companions, as should be understood as explained before. Yet what they grasp at as the self and the world are neither of them permanent, since a real self and what belongs to a real self are nowhere to be found; it is directly observed that all sentient beings, the world of beings and the world of vessels, and all things undergo change, since they arise from causes and conditions, and whatever has arisen —

Whoever holds this view — that 'because it is true, and because it abides, the self exists as self' — this view of extremism is subsumed under eternalism, to be abandoned by seeing suffering. 'Because it is true' means because it is a real meaning; 'because it abides' means because it is simply so by nature; 'the self exists as self' means that the self exists eternally. That this view of extremism is subsumed under eternalism reveals its intrinsic nature; that it is to be abandoned by seeing suffering reveals its antidote, explained at length as before. What gives rise to it? It means that there are ascetics who give rise to this view either through incorrect reasoning, and so on, as stated at length. Whoever holds this view — that 'because it is true, and because it abides, the self is without self' — this view of extremism is subsumed under annihilationism, to be abandoned by seeing suffering; 'because it is true, and because it abides' is as explained before; 'the self is without self' means that the self will one day cease to exist. Question: the true Dharma likewise teaches non-self, yet this is not called an evil view — these ascetics also teach non-self; why, then, is theirs called an evil view? Answer: the true Dharma, within the cluster of formations that are empty of self, sees them as empty and without self and so speaks of non-self, and this is therefore not an evil view; these ascetics, within that very cluster of formations empty of self, falsely suppose there to be a self, and merely claim that this self will, in the future, cease to exist — it is for this reason that theirs is an evil view. That this view of extremism is subsumed under annihilationism reveals its intrinsic nature; that it is to be abandoned by seeing suffering reveals its antidote, explained at length as before. What gives rise to it? It means that there are ascetics who give rise to this view either through incorrect reasoning, and so on, as stated at length. Whoever holds this view — that 'the self observes the self; the eye and visible form are precisely the self' — this is the view of a real self, to be abandoned by seeing suffering. 'The self observes the self' means that these ascetics grasp at the self as pervading both internal and external dharmas, and so, when the eye sees visible form, they hold that the self observes the self, since both the eye-faculty and visible form are precisely the self. Whoever holds this view — that 'the self observes non-self; the eye is precisely the self, and visible form is its accoutrement' — this too is the view of a real self, to be abandoned by seeing suffering. 'The self observes non-self' means that these ascetics, since the eye is unshared with others and is an internal dharma, grasp at it as the self, while visible form, being the opposite of this, is merely the self's accoutrement; and so, when the eye sees visible form, they hold that the self observes what is non-self. Whoever holds this view — that 'non-self observes the self; visible form is precisely the self, and the eye is its accoutrement' — this too is the view of a real self, to be abandoned by seeing suffering. 'Non-self observes the self' means that these ascetics, observing directly in the world that the great earth and the various mountains remain unchanged over long ages, hold that this accords with the principle of a self and so grasp at it as the self, while the eye, being the opposite of this, is merely an accoutrement; and so, when the eye sees visible form, they hold that non-self observes the self. Question: why is 'non-self observes non-self' not spoken of? Answer: because no dharma whatsoever truly has a self; if one were to say 'non-self observes non-self,' this would simply be right view, and it is for this reason that it is not spoken of here. Question: if there are ascetics who grasp at the ear, sound, and the like as the self, and nothing else — then, when the eye sees visible form, would their saying 'non-self observes non-self' not itself be right view? Question: what is the difference between 'attached' and 'bound'? Answer: the very names differ; furthermore, their meanings also differ — 'attached' means firmly clinging, in the sense of being difficult to wash away; 'bound' means being entangled, in the sense of being difficult to be freed from. Furthermore, 'attached' refers to the binding of conjunction, while 'bound' refers to the binding of the object — that is, the seven kinds of conceit possess both kinds of binding, and so are able to attach and bind those beings. Furthermore, 'attached' means attaching the mind, while 'bound' means binding the body — this is the distinction in meaning between binding and attachment. As for 'mutually opposed in their views of annihilationism and eternalism' — just as householders, bound and attached by craving, are mutually opposed over what they possess, so too renunciants, bound and attached by conceit, are mutually opposed in their views of annihilationism and eternalism. As for 'the boundless expanse of birth-and-death' — the ceaseless wandering through the various destinies and births is the meaning of birth-and-death; such birth-and-death has no discernible prior limit, and so cannot be known, yet, since it has a prior cause, it does have a later limit, since final nirvāṇa marks its final boundary. Furthermore, since the extent of birth-and-death is so vast and far-reaching that one cannot know when liberation will be attained, it is called boundless. 'What is attained and what is yet to be attained are both covered in dust' — and so on, as stated at length. This reveals that these ascetics, with respect to the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields already attained and yet to be attained, are covered by the dust of craving, aversion, and delusion, and so do not truly see the fault in the two extremes of conduct — painful austerity and pleasure-seeking. Because they do not see this, they either sink too deeply or run too swiftly: sinking means being too lax to make any progress, while running means being too hasty to ever reach the goal. The remaining meaning of the verse is explained fully in the treatise itself.

9. Five Disputes Reduced to Three; the Brahmajāla's Sixty-Two Views

Every philosophical dispute recorded in the sūtras, the treatise notes, falls into just five categories — perception after death, its absence, neither, annihilation, or nirvāṇa in this life — which reduce to the three doctrines already classified as eternalism, annihilationism, and grasping at views. 10. Eternalism, Partial Eternalism, and Causeless Arising

The eighteen past-oriented views begin: four doctrines of complete eternalism, each grounded in some genuine meditative or mnemonic feat — recalling eons of cosmic dissolution and formation, recalling many past lives, or observing the moment-by-moment continuity of the aggregates with the divine eye — misread through theories of mere transformation or of an unchanging nature that only hides and reappears; four doctrines of partial eternalism pinned on the impermanence of ordinary gods against the permanence of Brahmā or of mind itself; and two doctrines of causeless arising, reasoned from the limits of memory and from the apparent absence of any maker behind natural forms.

The third arises through both the divine eye and the superknowledge of supernormal locomotion: by the divine eye one sees, below, only as far as the Avīci hell, and, above, only as far as the heaven of the first dhyāna; by the superknowledge of supernormal locomotion one moves one's body laterally without reaching any limit; one thereupon gives rise to a notion of finitude with respect to above and below, and a notion of infinitude with respect to the world laterally, grasping that the self entirely and completely pervades this range; on this basis, one grasps that the self and the world are both finite and infinite — this is the sense in which both are held to be, at once, limited and unlimited. The fourth, neither finite nor infinite, is precisely a rejection of the third position, in favor of this fourth one: one reflects that neither the self nor the world can be definitively said to be finite, nor definitively said to be infinite, and yet both truly exist. Some further say: observing that the world is laterally without limit, one grasps that the self and the world are both not finite; observing that the world is vertically limited, one grasps that the self and the world are both not infinite — though without any fixed certainty, one nonetheless holds that a self truly exists. Others further say: one grasps that the substance of the self can either expand or contract, and so cannot be fixedly described — since, when expanded, it is without limit, one says it is not finite; and since, when contracted, it is limited, one says it is not infinite. Question: since these four doctrines all take the present as their object, how can they be said to be constructed with reference to the prior limit? Answer: because they also anticipate the future, they too bear the name of the prior limit. Others further say: because these four are established through recollecting the eons of formation and dissolution, all of them can be said to be constructed with reference to the prior limit — that is, the first doctrine arises from recollecting a past eon of formation, when the self and the world were vertically limited, and so gives rise to a notion of finitude; the second doctrine arises from recollecting a past eon of formation, when the self and the world were laterally without limit, and so gives rise to a notion of infinitude; the third doctrine arises from recollecting a past eon of formation, when the self and the world were vertically limited and laterally without limit, giving rise to a notion of being both finite and infinite; and the fourth doctrine arises from recollecting a past eon of dissolution, when the self and the world, although their extent — narrow or broad — could not be determined, nonetheless truly existed, giving rise to a notion of being neither finite nor infinite. Some say: grasping at finitude is precisely annihilationism; grasping at infinitude is precisely eternalism; grasping at being both finite and infinite is precisely partial annihilationism and partial eternalism; and grasping at being neither finite nor infinite is precisely the mere arising of the view of a real self. These four kinds of views constructed with reference to the prior limit, doctrines concerning finitude and the like, arise, in accordance with what was explained before, chiefly on account of four matters.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

There is a quiet irony in how this fascicle ends: a position built entirely around the refusal ever to be pinned down — answer every question by asking what the questioner wants to hear — is itself pinned down without hesitation, classified precisely as a form of eternalism and grasping at precepts and vows. The most evasive stance in the whole catalogue receives no credit whatsoever for its evasiveness.

Fascicle 199 Complete

This closes fascicle 199 of Ru‑Yi's translation. The fascicle opens with a creator-god view logically refuted point by point, a view denying any cause whatsoever, and the biographical episode of the naked ascetic Kāśyapa, whose four failed sea voyages produced four successive causal theories before he abandoned household life — used to draw a careful distinction between grasping at what is not a cause as a cause and grasping at what is not the path as the path. A cluster of eternalist and self-view doctrines follows, including four permutations of how the eye and visible form might be identified with the self, and two views mistaking sensory pleasure or the four dhyānas for nirvāṇa in this very life. Four masters then debate why a chapter on views also analyzes conceit, and the treatise works through nine kinds of conceit in detail, before a further eternalist view holds that a hidden, permanent self secretly moves the wind and rivers, and an extended exegesis unpacks a canonical verse comparing evil views to a poisoned arrow. The fascicle closes by reducing every philosophical dispute in the sūtras to three doctrines, then expanding into the Brahmajāla Sūtra's celebrated taxonomy of sixty-two view-tenets, all rooted in the view of a real self: four doctrines of complete eternalism grounded in genuine meditative feats misread, four of partial eternalism keyed to the impermanence of ordinary gods, two of causeless arising, four concerning the finitude or infinitude of the self and world, and four calculated equivocators who, fearing false speech, wrong view, ignorance, or foolishness, resort to never giving a definite answer at all — a stance the treatise nonetheless classifies decisively as its own form of eternalism.
