The Diamond That Cuts Every Thread

Fascicle 28 continues the Person Nyāsa's sixth part (補特伽羅納息第三之六), opening by naming the vajropama-samādhi — the “diamond-like concentration” that severs every affliction of the three realms in a single stroke — and surveying four masters' sharply divergent counts of exactly how many such concentrations exist across the nine possible stages of attainment. A grammatical puzzle over whether an already-liberated mind can be said to “become” liberated leads into the fascicle's second major topic: a full scholastic unpacking of the Buddha's own compressed formula, renunciation depends on dispassion, dispassion on liberation, liberation on nirvāṇa, closing with an extraordinary cascade of etymologies for extinction itself. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Vajropama-Samādhi and Why It Is Called “Diamond-Like”

Fascicle 28 opens mid-discussion, continuing directly from fascicle 27's identification of the future arhat's mind at the moment of arising as what attains total liberation. The treatise now names the mechanism precisely: the uninterrupted path is the vajropama-samādhi, the “diamond-like concentration,” and the path of liberation immediately following it is the knowledge of exhaustion. The etymology is exact — nothing withstands a diamond's cutting edge, and this concentration is so named because it can, in a single application, sever every affliction of the three realms at once, in the same way the path of seeing or the four modes of practice are named for whichever mental factor predominates within their otherwise mixed aggregate composition.

Chinese Source

其事如何。答如無間道金剛喻定將滅解脫道盡智將生。若無間道金剛喻定正滅。解脫道盡智正生。爾時名未來無學心生時解脫一切障。此中金剛喻定將滅者。謂生相用時。盡智將生者。謂臨至生相。爾時猶名未得解脫。未定行世在相續故。金剛喻定正滅者。謂滅相用時。盡智正生者。謂生相正用。爾時乃名今得解脫。定能行世在相續故。若金剛喻定已滅盡智已生。爾時名為已得解脫。此中且舉將解脫位顯正解脫。問何故名為金剛喻定。答無有煩惱不斷不破不穿不碎。譬如金剛無有若鐵若牙若貝若珠石等不斷不破不穿不碎。是故此定名金剛喻。假使具縛有情身中能起此定。爾時即能頓斷三界一切煩惱。云何知然。金剛喻定現在前時。頓證三界見修所斷煩惱斷故。問此無間道四蘊五蘊為自性何故但說定耶。答定偏增故。如見道五蘊為自性。見偏增故但名見道。如現觀邊世俗智四蘊五蘊為自性。智偏增故但說名智。如四通行四蘊五蘊為自性。通慧偏增故但名通行。如是此道雖四蘊五蘊為自性而定偏增。是故但名金剛喻定。問何故此道定偏增耶。答非想非非想處下下煩惱難斷難破極難越度。須堅固定為所依止。發大精進乃能除遣。譬如有人欲殺香象。先安其足後發武勇成其殺事。是故此道定用偏增。復次非想非非想處下下煩惱最極微細不明不顯難可覺知。須依勝定令心澄細方能除斷。如善射者欲射毛端。依巧便法令心澄細發箭方中。是故此道定用偏增。

The question: how does this come about? The answer: it is when the uninterrupted path, the vajropama-samādhi, is about to cease, and the path of liberation, the knowledge of exhaustion, is about to arise. When the uninterrupted path, the vajropama-samādhi, is actually ceasing, and the path of liberation, the knowledge of exhaustion, is actually arising — at that very moment, this is called the future mind of one beyond training, at the moment of its own arising, becoming liberated from every obstruction. Here, "the vajropama-samādhi about to cease" refers to the time when its characteristic of abiding is still in operation; "the knowledge of exhaustion about to arise" refers to the time immediately approaching the characteristic of arising. At that time, one is still called not-yet-liberated, since the determined operation across time within the continuum has not yet occurred. "The vajropama-samādhi actually ceasing" refers to the time when its characteristic of ceasing is in operation; "the knowledge of exhaustion actually arising" refers to the time when its characteristic of arising is actually in operation. At that time, one is called now becoming liberated, since the determined operation across time within the continuum does actually occur then. If the vajropama-samādhi has already ceased and the knowledge of exhaustion has already arisen, then, at that time, one is called already liberated. This passage, in speaking only of the stage of about-to-be-liberated, thereby reveals the moment of actual liberation. One asks: why is this called the vajropama-samādhi, the "diamond-like concentration"? The answer: there exists no affliction that it does not cut off, does not break, does not pierce, does not shatter — just as a diamond is such that there exists no iron, no ivory, no shell, no pearl, or stone, that it cannot cut off, break, pierce, or shatter. It is for this reason that this concentration is called "diamond-like." Suppose a sentient being still bound by every fetter were able to give rise to this concentration within his own body — at that moment, he would be able all at once to cut off every affliction of the three realms entirely. How is this known to be so? Because, when the vajropama-samādhi manifests before one, one thereby all at once realizes the cutting-off of every affliction abandoned by seeing and by cultivation across the three realms. One asks: this uninterrupted path takes the four aggregates, or the five aggregates, as its own nature — why, then, does the treatise speak of it only as a "concentration"? The answer: it is because concentration is the abundantly predominant factor here. Just as the path of seeing takes the five aggregates as its own nature, yet, because seeing is the abundantly predominant factor, it is called only "the path of seeing"; just as the conventional knowledge on the border of direct realization takes the four aggregates, or the five aggregates, as its own nature, yet, because knowledge is the abundantly predominant factor, it is called only "knowledge"; and just as the four modes of practice take the four aggregates, or the five aggregates, as their own nature, yet, because penetrating insight is the abundantly predominant factor, they are called only "modes of practice" — so too, although this path takes the four aggregates, or the five aggregates, as its own nature, concentration is here the abundantly predominant factor, and it is for this reason that it is called only the vajropama-samādhi. The three-stage analysis of “about to cease,” “actually ceasing,” and “already ceased” maps liberation onto the same fine-grained momentary structure the Sarvāstivāda applies everywhere else in its psychology — birth, abiding, and cessation are not loose metaphors here but exact technical markers, and the treatise's insistence on distinguishing “determined to operate across time” from mere absence of affliction is what lets it later resolve the seeming paradox of calling an already-liberated mind “becoming liberated,” a puzzle this fascicle returns to at length below.

2. The Six Kinds of Knowledge Within the Diamond-Like Concentration

Having explained why concentration is called “predominant” within a path that in fact comprises the full aggregate of mind and mental factors, the treatise turns to enumerate exactly which of the sixteen moments of knowledge belonging to the path of seeing and cultivation can serve as the vajropama-samādhi's own vehicle — four kinds of subsequent knowledge together with the dharma-knowledge of cessation and of the path — closing this opening unit with a one-line marker noting that this constitutes only the Vibhāṣā's brief treatment of the topic before the far more exhaustive counting that follows.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That six kinds of knowledge, not just one, can each independently serve as the final uninterrupted path shows the abhidharma's characteristic refusal to let even the single most decisive moment in the entire path be described by only one formula — the same event, final liberation, can be reached by cutting off the last affliction of any of the nine stages through any of six different cognitive routes, depending on which object and which aspect one's own final act of insight happens to take.

3. Counting the Diamond-Like Concentrations: Fifty-Two, Depending on Stage

The treatise now asks a startlingly technical question — not simply how many kinds of vajropama-samādhi exist in the abstract, but how many exist relative to each of the nine possible stages of concentration one might depend upon to attain arhatship, since the final uninterrupted path can, in principle, be practiced from the not-yet-arrived stage, any of the four dhyānas, or any of the four formless spheres. The first position on record works this out arithmetically: fifty-two at the not-yet-arrived stage and each dhyāna, tapering to twenty-eight, twenty-four, and twenty at the three lower formless spheres, since a practitioner at a higher formless stage has correspondingly fewer lower stages of cessation left to contemplate.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The tapering count — fifty-two, then twenty-eight, twenty-four, twenty — is not an isolated curiosity but a direct consequence of the earlier fascicles' account of formless absorptions taking only their own stage and stages above as objects: a meditator resting in the sphere of nothing-whatsoever has already left behind the possibility of contemplating the cessation of space or consciousness as a fresh object, which mechanically shrinks his own tally of possible final paths.

4. A Rival Count: Eighty at Each Base Level

A second position, introduced simply as “other masters,” arrives at a substantially larger figure by adding subsequent knowledge of the path itself as a distinct route to arhatship at every dhyāna level — not merely subsequent knowledge of cessation — doubling the sixteen configurations already established for cessation into thirty-two more for the path, and thereby raising the not-yet-arrived stage's own total from fifty-two to eighty, with proportional increases at the formless spheres following the identical logic.

The disagreement between these first two masters turns entirely on whether contemplating the path itself, not merely the cessation reached by that path, can serve as the immediately final act of insight before arhatship — a genuinely live doctrinal question, since the treatise elsewhere insists that cessation and path are companion truths, always investigated together, and different masters here draw the boundary of “final act” differently depending on how strictly they read that companionship.

5. The Venerable Ghoṣa's Minimalist Count: Thirteen

Ghoṣa's approach is conceptually the cleanest of the four: rather than multiplying every possible object and aspect combinatorially, he simply asks which concentrations actually occupy the final uninterrupted-path slot in an ordinary practitioner's own progression through the nine grades of the path of cultivation, yielding a count that tracks lived practice rather than exhaustive logical possibility — the same methodological tension between combinatorial completeness and practical realism that recurs throughout the Vibhāṣā's treatment of enumerated lists.

6. The Largest Count: One Hundred Sixty-Four, by Exhaustive Combination

The fourth and final position, introduced simply as “those who hold this,” dwarfs every prior count by systematically combining every possible span of contiguous dhyāna and formless stages whose cessation might jointly serve as the final object of contemplation — not only each single stage individually, but every pair, every triple, all the way up to the full span from the first dhyāna through the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — arriving at a running tally that climbs from forty-eight through seventy-six, one hundred, one hundred twenty, one hundred thirty-six, one hundred forty-eight, one hundred fifty-six, and finally to one hundred sixty before the single additional path-contemplation brings the not-yet-arrived stage's own total to one hundred sixty-four, with the formless spheres following the identical combinatorial logic scaled down to fifty-two, thirty-six, and twenty-four respectively. The passage then closes this entire question of how many diamond-like concentrations exist with a note on why formless-realm birth never permits dharma-knowledge or contemplation of a lower stage's suffering and origin, before pivoting, in the very same breath, into an entirely new investigation: whether an unliberated mind and an already-liberated mind can both properly be described as “becoming liberated.”

8. The Four Refuges, and a Transition to Renunciation

The treatise draws its conclusion from the two verses just examined — that scripture must be read for its intended meaning, not merely its literal wording, on pain of making the noble teaching contradict itself — and illustrates the point with a further verse on four kinds of refuge: the beast to forest and marsh, the bird to open sky, the noble one to nirvāṇa, and the dharma to discrimination. This closes the tense-puzzle investigation and pivots directly into the fascicle's second major topic, quoting the Buddha's own compressed formula: renunciation depends on dispassion, dispassion on liberation, liberation on nirvāṇa.

12. Liberation as Resolved Conviction, and Its Branching Taxonomy

The treatise turns to “dispassion depends on liberation,” defining liberation here as resolved conviction — one of the mental factors common to every mind — after disposing of two further positions on why non-greed and non-hatred, not simply non-delusion, still belong in dispassion's own formal definition. It then works through liberation's branching taxonomy in full: unconditioned versus conditioned, defiled versus undefiled, contaminated versus uncontaminated, belonging to a learner versus to one beyond training, timed versus unshakable, and finally the arhat's own twofold liberation of mind through departing from craving and liberation of wisdom through departing from ignorance, reconciling this account with the Prajñapti-śāstra's own seemingly conflicting definition along the way.
