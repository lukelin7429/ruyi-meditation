Subsumption and Correlation

Fascicle 167 continues the Subsumption Nyāsa (攝納息第三之二) with a systematic two-part analysis: first, how much each of the ten perceptions, dhyānas, immeasurables, formless spheres, liberations, and kinds of knowledge subsumes (攝) by virtue of sharing the same underlying aggregates; then, in a parallel second half, what each of these same categories correlates with (相應) as genuinely conascent mental factors. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Why the Ten Perceptions Do Not Subsume Themselves

Fascicle 167 opens the treatise's systematic answer to a genuinely new question: not what each of the ten perceptions is, but how much each one subsumes (攝) — that is, which dhyānas, formless spheres, and liberations each perception's own aggregate-nature encompasses. The treatise first defends why a perception is said to subsume itself only implicitly, offering roughly seven independent literary and doctrinal justifications, including a striking image of paired "gateways, abridgments, torches, and textual reflections" designed so that two parallel passages illuminate each other by what each chooses to state explicitly and what each leaves for the reader to infer.

Others hold: this passage distinguishes the meaning of subsumption from the meaning of correlation — with respect to a perception paired against itself, there is only the meaning of subsumption, and no meaning of correlation, and so it is not stated here. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The passage's own account of why it sometimes states the obvious and sometimes doesn't — not carelessness, but a deliberate literary strategy of two mutually illuminating passages, each completing what the other omits — is a rare moment of the text reflecting on its own compositional method, and it is worth remembering through the rest of this fascicle's often relentless enumeration: the compilers were not simply listing facts, but constructing a specific kind of cross-referenced reading experience.

2. The Perception of Impurity and Its Puzzling Restriction to Two Dhyānas

The treatise turns to a genuine doctrinal puzzle: the perception of impurity occurs, in principle, within all four dhyānas, yet the text states that it subsumes only the third and fourth. The debate that follows is unusually vivid for a classificatory passage, invoking the incompatibility of joyful and weary mental aspects within a single moment of mind, a king sharing victory-joy with his generals after crushing an enemy, and the striking image of the first dhyāna "stirred by the wind of investigation and reflection" and the second "swept along by the water of intense joy" — both too turbulent for the perception of impurity to appear there with any clarity.

How many concentrations, and the like, does dharma-knowledge, and the rest, correlate with? Answer: dharma-knowledge is correlated with a portion of the three concentrations — and so on at length. As for dharma-knowledge being correlated with a portion of the three concentrations: this knowledge is not correlated with the portion of the three concentrations conjoined with class-knowledge or with any patient acceptance, which is why it is called "a portion." As for the statement that "the same holds for class-knowledge": this knowledge, likewise, is not correlated with the portion of the three concentrations conjoined with dharma-knowledge or with any patient acceptance, which is why it says "the same holds." As for the knowledge of others' minds being correlated with a portion of one concentration: this knowledge is not correlated with the concentrations conjoined with the knowledge of suffering, of origin, or of cessation, nor with the portion of the knowledge of the path not subsumed under the knowledge of others' minds, nor with any concentration conjoined with patient acceptance, which is why it is called "a portion" — that is, it means being correlated with only a portion of the wishlessness-concentration that takes the path as its object. As for the statement that "the same holds, according to the knowledge of others' minds, for the knowledge of origin, of cessation, and of the path": this is said on the basis that the number is the same, which is why it says "the same holds" — yet, in fact, the knowledge of origin is correlated only with a portion of the wishlessness-concentration that takes origin as its object; the knowledge of cessation is correlated only with a portion of the signlessness-concentration; and the knowledge of the path is correlated only with a portion of the wishlessness-concentration that takes the path as its object — in every case excluding the concentration conjoined with patient acceptance. As for the knowledge of suffering being correlated with a portion of two concentrations: this knowledge is correlated only with the emptiness-concentration conjoined with knowledge, and with the wishlessness-concentration conjoined with the knowledge of suffering, which is why it is called "a portion." As for why the passage on the three concentrations themselves does not separately state their own correlation: this is because it has already been fully explained with respect to all the preceding categories above, and because, within its own category, a dharma has no correlation with itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fascicle's closing observation — that the passage on the three concentrations does not separately restate their own correlation, both because it has already been covered from every other angle and because nothing is ever correlated with itself — brings the entire subsumption/correlation pairing to a tidy conceptual close: everything that needed saying about how these categories relate to one another has now, by design, been said exactly once.
