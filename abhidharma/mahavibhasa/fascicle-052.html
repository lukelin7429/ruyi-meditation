Flowers and Fruit That Have Fallen

Fascicle 52 pushes fascicle 51's seeing/cultivation split down to each of the four truths individually, then runs every taxonomy given so far through further classifications — whether it counts as a view, whether it involves investigation and examination (opening with the Dārṣṭāntikas' own image of a merchant caravan filing one by one down a narrow road), its association with the five feeling faculties, and which of the three realms it binds to. The fascicle closes with the canonical story of a Brahmā deity who mistook his own realm for permanent and eternal, refuted through the image of every past Brahmā who fell into the desire realm being exactly like flowers and fruit that have already fallen from the tree, and the evil Māra's exposed, humiliated retreat. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 52 continues the Unwholesome Nyāsa's seventh part (結蘊第二中不善納息第一之七), refining the seeing/cultivation classification opened in fascicle 51 down to the level of each of the four truths, then running the same taxonomies through further classifications — view versus non-view, investigation and examination, association with the feeling faculties, and binding to the desire, form, or formless realm. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The View of Self, Cut Off by Seeing Suffering Alone

Fascicle 52 refines fascicle 51's seeing/cultivation split down to which of the four truths specifically cuts off a given affliction. The view of the perishable aggregation of self is abandoned solely by seeing suffering, and the treatise offers a cascade of reasons why — the cloth-stain and greasy-vessel similes distinguishing a coarse affliction that a single washing removes from a subtle one requiring vigorous scrubbing, and the shallow-versus-deep tree-root simile distinguishing what a slight wind topples from what only a mighty wind can fell — before three named masters each locate the view's own cutting-point differently: in seeing the five aggregates as they truly are, in cutting off the four notions of permanence, pleasure, purity, and self, or in seeing the existing body as empty of self.

It is now in order to refute and to display the sense of sudden versus gradual direct realization that this discourse is composed. There are those who say: the earlier gateway, though it has already refuted the intent of those who hold direct realization occurs all at once, and has already made manifest the sense in which direct realization occurs gradually, did not display this coarsely, clearly, and directly enough — and it is in order to make this coarsely, clearly, and directly visible that this discourse is now composed. One asks: for what reason is the view of the perishable aggregation of self abandoned solely by seeing suffering? The answer: this view operates solely with respect to the topic of suffering, and is therefore cut off at the very moment suffering is contemplated. Further, this view operates solely with respect to the topic of a fruit, and is therefore cut off at the very moment a fruit is contemplated. Further, the fetter of the view of the perishable aggregation of self is, by its own nature, an inversion, and every inversion is cut off through seeing suffering — once the inversion is cut off, this view is likewise cut off, since they share the very same antidote. Further, this affliction, being coarse, is forever cut off the very moment the first uninterrupted path — the dharma-forbearance and subsequent-class forbearance regarding suffering — manifests before the mind; were this affliction subtle, it would require the later uninterrupted path — the diamond-like concentration — to manifest before the mind before it could finally be cut off entirely, exactly as, should a garment bear a stain not firmly set into it, a single washing suffices to cleanse it, while, should the stain be firmly set, only vigorous scrubbing with pure ash and the like will finally cleanse it; and exactly as, should a clay vessel's own greasy film not have penetrated deeply, a single rinsing with water suffices to cleanse it, while, should the greasy film have penetrated deeply, only boiling with hot water, or scorching with fire, will finally cleanse it. The Venerable Vasumitra states as follows: this view of the perishable aggregation of self arises taking the five aggregates as its object, and, the moment one genuinely and directly perceives the five appropriated aggregates as they truly are, this view is thereby cut off. Further, this view of the perishable aggregation of self arises from the four notions of permanence, pleasure, purity, and self, and, the moment these four notions are cut off, this view is thereby cut off. The Great Virtuous One states as follows: this view of the perishable aggregation of self arises taking the existing body as its object, and is therefore named the view of the perishable aggregation of self; the moment one contemplates that existing body as devoid of a self and of what belongs to a self, this view is thereby cut off. It is for this reason that the fetter of the view of the perishable aggregation of self is abandoned solely by seeing suffering. Exactly as with the fetter of the view of the perishable aggregation of self, so too with the fetter of the view of the perishable aggregation of self among the five fetters pertaining to the lower portion, and with the view of the perishable aggregation of self and the extreme view among the five views — their own nature being identical, all alike being confused solely about suffering.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The shallow-root-versus-deep-root image quietly makes a claim about effort and depth that the cloth-and-vessel similes only imply: it isn't that some afflictions require more force to remove, but that their own mode of attachment to the object is structurally different — shallow rootedness and deep rootedness are not two points on the same scale of strength, but two different relationships to the ground itself, which is why one falls to an ordinary wind and the other requires something as singular as the diamond-like concentration.

2. Fire That Sinks Deep vs. Fire That Doesn’t: Clinging to Rules and Doubt

Clinging to rules and vows is abandoned only by seeing suffering or by seeing the path — never by seeing arising or cessation — because outsiders, the treatise explains, conceive of arising as a filthy place they feel no urge to escape, and cessation as a bathing place they mistakenly long for, taking up every manner of pointless austerity as a result; the more they bathe, the treatise notes, the dirtier they become, moving further from nirvāṇa with every effort spent. The fetter of doubt, by contrast, spans all four truths but never cultivation, since doubt is definitionally resolved the instant its object is actually seen.

The fetter of clinging to rules and vows is of two kinds — either abandoned by seeing suffering, or abandoned by seeing the path. One asks: for what reason is clinging to rules and vows not abandoned by seeing its arising or its cessation? The answer: this is because the outsiders give rise to this clinging to rules and vows solely with respect to suffering and the path — that is, the outsiders are indeed capable of conceiving of the truth of arising as a place of filth, and are likewise capable of conceiving of the truth of cessation as a place of bathing; since they conceive of arising as a place of filth, they give rise to no aspiration toward it, while, since they conceive of cessation as a place of bathing, they mistakenly give rise to an aspiration toward it, and, through this mistaken aspiration, they take up every manner of pointless austerity.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The bathing-in-filthy-water image is a genuinely sharp diagnosis of a specific failure mode: not merely being wrong about the path, but being wrong about the path in a way that converts sincere effort into its own opposite — the harder the outsider practices, the more defiled he becomes, since the very energy of practice is being spent reinforcing rather than correcting the underlying error. Effort alone, on this account, guarantees nothing about direction.

3. Which Afflictions Are Views?

A new inquiry: of every taxonomy given so far, how many members are, by their own nature, views? Two extreme positions are dispatched — that every affliction is a view, since each grips its own object with a keen and forceful aspect (greed's own attachment, hatred's own aversion, conceit's own elevation, each cataloged in turn), and that no affliction is a view, since "view" properly means fully comprehending the dharmas, which no affliction ever does. Among the three fetters, the answer lands precisely in between: the view of the perishable aggregation of self and clinging to rules and vows are views; doubt is not.

4. The Caravan on the Narrow Road: Investigation and Examination

The treatise takes up whether investigation and examination — coarse and subtle mental activity — extend throughout all three realms or only the lowest two grounds, opening with the Dārṣṭāntikas' own image of merchants on a narrow road, passing one at a time, never two abreast, offered as proof that no two conditioned dharmas can ever arise jointly. The Ābhidharmikas answer by distinguishing two entirely different senses of "combined arising" — separately, by each dharma's own generating characteristic, and jointly, by the single moment inseparable dharmas share — before demonstrating that "coarse" and "subtle" name at least four different distinctions across scripture, none of which fixes investigation and examination as existing everywhere.

6. Doubt, Joy, and Seven Reasons for a Mismatch

Why doesn't doubt, within the desire realm, ever associate with the joy faculty, when it does within the first two dhyānas? 11. The Brahmā Who Thought His Realm Eternal

The treatise resolves its tetralemma's first case — a fetter falling within the desire realm yet not currently existing there — through the canonical story of a Brahmā deity who, dwelling in what he took to be a permanent, unchanging, and purely liberated realm, invited the Buddha to abandon the calamity-ridden desire realm and settle there for good. The Buddha's reply reaches for a single devastating image: examine how every Brahmā deity of the past who has fallen into the desire realm is exactly like flowers and fruit that have already fallen from the tree — how, then, can this place be called permanent at all?

12. Māra’s Threats, and the Buddha’s Reply

Unable to out-argue the Buddha directly, and disliking contention himself, the Brahmā deity summons his own eternal adversary, Māra, disguising the Brahmā world as the desire realm to draw him there. Māra threatens the Buddha with three escalating similes of inevitable disaster — driving out a visiting goddess of fortune, releasing one's grip while falling from a height, releasing one's grip while falling from a treetop — urging total submission to Brahmā's own word. The Buddha's reply is unadorned and precise: you are neither Brahmā nor his assembly, but the evil Māra himself, shameless, come here to disturb me for no reason at all — at which Māra, exposed, can only retreat in humiliation.
