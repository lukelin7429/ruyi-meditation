Can an Arhat Regress?

Fascicle 60 moves through four distinct inquiries within the Single-Class-of-Conduct Nyāsa. It asks which afflictive categories mutually contain one another, which fetters cause rebirth in the desire, form, and formless realms — cataloguing eight scriptural senses of the word "existence" along the way — and upon which ground of meditative absorption each fetter finally is cut off, framed by the wheel-turning king's own road, submerged beneath the ocean until a true king arises to travel it. At its greatest length, the fascicle takes up the celebrated Sarvāstivāda defense of an arhat's genuine capacity to regress from his own attainment — citing the arhat Gotika's own sixfold fall and eventual suicide, refuting two Vibhājyavādin similes, and closing on the verdict that regression's own nature is nothing other than non-possession itself. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Which Contains Which?

The fascicle opens by working systematically through whether the three fetters and a long series of traditional afflictive categories — the unwholesome roots, the contaminants, the floods, the yokes, the graspings, the bodily ties, the hindrances, the fetters, the lower- and upper-fetters, the views, the craving-bodies, the latent tendencies — mutually contain one another, category by category, before extending the same method from three fetters up through all nine, and from three latent tendencies up through all ninety-eight.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That mutual containing turns out to hold only for a narrow overlap — usually just view-of-self, clinging to rules and vows, and doubt, wherever a category happens to include exactly these — rather than for the categories as wholes, confirms the principle fascicle 59 spent itself establishing: containing tracks precise, shared own-nature, not family resemblance or rough thematic overlap between two different traditional lists.

2. Does an Undefiled Mind Cause Rebirth?

The treatise turns to a new question: which fetters cause the continuity of existence in the desire, form, and formless realms? The Vibhājyavādins are cited holding that even an undefiled mind can cause rebirth, appealing to the scriptural account of the Bodhisattva entering, dwelling in, and emerging from his mother's womb "with correct knowledge" — refuted by reinterpreting "correct knowledge" as freedom from the ordinary distortion of craving one parent and resenting the other, present even in the Bodhisattva's own affection-laden, and therefore still defiled, mind.

The answer: every case must be distinguished individually. One asks: for what reason is this treatise composed? The answer: in order to put a stop to other schools and to display one's own correct position. That is, there are those who hold that even an undefiled mind can cause the continuity of existence, as the Vibhājyavādins do. One asks: for what reason do they hold this? The answer: they hold this on the basis of scripture, that is, the scripture states that the Bodhisattva, with correct knowledge, enters the mother's womb, with correct knowledge dwells in the mother's womb, and with correct knowledge emerges from the mother's womb — and, since he possesses correct knowledge upon entering the womb, this correct knowledge must reside within an undefiled mind, and so an undefiled mind, too, can cause the continuity of existence. In order to put a stop to this position, and to display that only a defiled mind is capable of causing the continuity of existence, this treatise is composed. One asks: how, then, is the scripture they cite to be reconciled? The answer: the term "correct knowledge" is spoken with reference to the notion being free of distortion. That is, most sentient beings give rise to a distorted notion upon entering the womb: a male entering the womb gives rise to craving toward the mother and hatred toward the father; a female entering the womb gives rise to craving toward the father and hatred toward the mother — since each perceives the one parent as favorable and the other as unfavorable to itself. But when the Bodhisattva subsequently enters the womb, his mind is free of such distortion: he holds the notion "father" toward his father and the notion "mother" toward his mother, and, though he feels affectionate attachment toward both, his mind does not differ between them. Since there is affectionate attachment, his mind is defiled; since the notion is free of distortion, it is called correct knowledge. The scripture cited therefore does not contradict our own position. The refutation turns on a genuinely subtle distinction: the Bodhisattva's own mind is shown to be undistorted without being undefiled — he feels real affection for both parents rather than craving one and resenting the other, yet affection itself is still classified as a defilement, since the treatise's category of "undefiled" is reserved for something narrower than merely being free of ordinary confusion.

3. The Gandharva's Two Minds, and Every Affliction's Reach

Two further rival positions are dispatched in turn: the Sautrāntikas, who hold that craving and hatred alone cause rebirth-linking, citing the intermediate-state being's alternating desire for the mother and hostility toward the father at the moment of conception; and those who hold that hatred alone causes rebirth into evil destinies while craving alone causes rebirth into good ones. Both are refuted by insisting that any one of the desire, form, or formless realm's own full roster of latent tendencies — thirty-six, thirty-one, and thirty-one respectively — is capable of causing rebirth-linking's own continuity, not craving and hatred exclusively.

4. Eight Senses of "Existence"

Having settled which fetters cause the continuity of existence, the treatise pauses to catalogue the word "existence" (有, bhava) itself, cited across eight or more distinct scriptural senses — the class of being together with its aggregates, all contaminated dharmas as such, the mind that links to rebirth, the volition that draws forth a future existence, the temporal-span aggregates, the karmic actions that draw forth future existence, and the five destinies together with their cause and their means — with the Venerable Ghoṣa's own reading given particular attention, before a technical discrepancy with the Dvāra-śāstra's own account of which latent tendencies increase their disposition in the form and formless realms is carefully reconciled.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

5. Why "Existence" Is Named So

The treatise asks why existence bears its own name at all, working through four increasingly precise criteria — possessing increase and decrease, sustaining rather than undermining the continuity of birth and death, tending toward the arising rather than the cessation of suffering, serving as the very basis for self-view and craving — each one carefully distinguishing existence from the noble path, which also increases and decreases yet fails every other criterion; a final criterion, that existence is a "vessel for suffering," is defended against the objection that existence also contains real pleasure, using the image of a single drop of honey in a jar of poison, still rightly called a poison jar since the poison predominates.

7. The Three Fetters and the Three Existences

Returning to its opening question, the treatise explains that the three fetters cause continuity in whichever realm they themselves belong to, occurring at rebirth-linking's very first moment — and then addresses a genuine puzzle: since rebirth-linking occurs through mental consciousness alone, why does the base treatise attribute continuity to certain hindrances and craving-bodies tied to the five sense-consciousnesses at all? The answer distinguishes three separate conditions under which an affliction is said to cause continuity — remaining uncut, being capable of yielding a ripened result, and actually moistening existence at the moment of rebirth-linking — with some afflictions satisfying only the first two, capable of continuing existence in a weaker sense without actually moistening it.

9. Two Masters on Worldly and Noble Paths

The treatise next refutes the Vibhājyavādin claim that a noble one reborn in the highest formless realm, lacking any capacity for the noble path to manifest there, simply exhausts his afflictions along with his lifespan and becomes an arhat by default — a doctrine called "reaching the very summit." A genuine historical debate is then preserved without being resolved: the Venerable Sthavira holds that final abandonment of the fetters belongs to noble ones alone, using the noble path alone, since the treatise's own source scripture speaks only of the seven fundamental grounds; the Venerable Ghoṣavarman holds that ordinary persons, too, can achieve final abandonment using the worldly path from the border regions, since the Abhidharma's entire purpose is to state what the sūtras leave unstated.

One asks: is it not the case that this treatise is based on the Seven-Grounds Sūtra — how, then, can an ordinary person, relying on the fundamental stages, give rise to the worldly path and achieve final abandonment? Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ghoṣavarman's argument that the Abhidharma exists precisely to say what scripture does not is a remarkably direct statement of this whole genre's own self-understanding: rather than treating the base treatise's silence on ordinary persons and border regions as evidence against their inclusion, he treats that very silence as exactly the kind of gap Abhidharma commentary is licensed, even obligated, to fill — and the Mahāvibhāṣā itself declines to choose between the two masters, finding the base text genuinely open to both readings.

15. Refuting the Similes, and the Seeds That Cannot Regrow

The treatise dismantles both similes on their own terms: a broken jar necessarily still contains its potsherds, and burnt wood necessarily still leaves ash, yet neither residue corresponds to anything real remaining in a liberated arhat, so the analogy fails on its own logic; wood, moreover, is never truly "burned" at all in a literal sense — its subtlest particles simply cease as cause for fire's own subtlest particles, which cease in turn as cause for ash. Cutting off an affliction, the treatise insists, does not mean it ceases to exist altogether across all three times, only that it no longer operates within a given person, exactly as saying "Devadatta is not in this house" does not mean Devadatta does not exist at all — and it is precisely because past and future afflictions genuinely, truly exist that a chance encounter with a condition favoring regression can draw one forth again. The Vibhājyavādins' own attempted reply, and their further claim that the wrapping-defilements alone (not the dormant latent tendencies beneath them) cause regression, are both shown to collapse into absurdity or into disciples existing outside the fourfold community of ordinary persons and the two stages of training altogether.

16. What Is Regression's Own Nature?

A final, deeply technical question closes the fascicle: what, precisely, is the own-nature of regression itself? Three positions are considered and rejected in turn — that it is simply the unwholesome affliction that arises, that it is every dharma that happens to accompany a regressive moment — before the Sautrāntikas deny it any own-nature whatsoever, offering three vivid images of pure loss without positive substance: a robbed man asked what his "lost wealth" consists of, a stripped man asked what his "nakedness" consists of, a man whose garment has torn asked what his garment's "tornness" consists of. The verdict: regression's own nature is nothing other than non-possession itself — a neutral, non-obscuring dharma dissociated from mind, exactly analogous to how a schism in the monastic community is itself a real, dissociated dharma distinct from the offense of having caused it.
