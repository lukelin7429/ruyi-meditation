Fascicle 108 settles how a single unimpeded path both cuts off a fetter and witnesses its cessation at once, works through the notion of impermanence's own full technical profile, and closes with an extensive treatment of the seven stations of skill and the threefold contemplation. 1. One Path, Two Functions: Cutting Off and Witnessing at Once

Fascicle 108 opens by settling a dispute with foreign treatise-masters: rather than splitting the work between an unimpeded path that merely cuts off a fetter's own attainment and a separate path of liberation that only afterward witnesses its cessation, the treatise insists a single unimpeded path does both at once — traced in exhaustive detail through dharma-cognition, subsequent cognition, and the knowledge of suffering (and, by extension, accumulation, cessation, and the path) as each, in turn, serves as the unimpeded path across the stream-enterer's, once-returner's, non-returner's, and arhat's own successive transitions.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The proof by contradiction the treatise offers is elegantly economical: if the path of liberation alone could witness a cessation, then whenever dharma-cognition itself serves as the unimpeded path with subsequent cognition as the path of liberation, it could never be dharma-cognition's own cessation that gets witnessed — directly contradicting the plain scriptural statement that the fetters dharma-cognition cuts off are witnessed as cessation by dharma-cognition itself, a single sentence the treatise treats as sufficient, by itself, to settle the entire dispute.

2. Six Similes for One Distinction

Having established that a single unimpeded path both cuts off and witnesses, the treatise turns to a genuinely subtle question: what, then, distinguishes speaking of "cutting off a fetter's attainment" from speaking of "witnessing its cessation's attainment," if both name the very same event? Six paired similes answer in turn — relinquishing fault versus cultivating virtue, relinquishing what is without meaning versus attaining what has meaning, relinquishing the inferior versus attaining the supreme, relinquishing suffering versus witnessing tranquil bliss, cutting off the conditioned versus witnessing the unconditioned, and a sudden cutting-off versus a repeated one.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That six entirely different similes are offered for what is, in the end, one single distinction (a negative and a positive description of the identical event) is itself instructive: the treatise clearly regards no single image as sufficient to convey how something can be simultaneously a relinquishing and an attaining, and prefers to let six partial illustrations accumulate rather than settle for one that might mislead by seeming too complete on its own.

3. No Knowledge Without an Object, No Object Without Knowledge

A new topic opens by refuting two rival errors at once: that cognition can be entirely objectless, exactly as one might mistake an illusion, a gandharva's phantom city, a mirror's reflection, the moon in water, a shadow, a mirage, or a whirling firebrand for something real; and that there could exist either a knowledge failing to know its own proper object, or an object belonging to no knowledge whatsoever. Every dharma is then sorted into five categories according to the four truths (each truth-category further split into associated and non-associated portions), with a precise count of exactly how many of the ten kinds of knowledge know each category.

13. Four Grounds, and Why the Path of Seeing States More

A further puzzle asks why the first three truths are each stated twice among the seven stations while the truth of the path is stated only once — resolved by noting that the path is already implicitly present throughout every other station (since it alone is capable of knowing, cutting off, and witnessing already-arisen and not-yet-arisen suffering, its cause, and its cessation alike, whether proximate or distant), and by a final, striking asymmetry: the first three truths each possess a boundary that can be exhaustively known, cut off, or witnessed, while the path, never exhaustible through cultivation, is boundless.
