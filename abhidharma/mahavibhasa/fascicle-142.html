Twenty-Two Faculties, and Why Each One Rules Its Own Domain

Fascicle 142 opens the Faculties Aggregate with the story of Ānanda's own objection to the heretic Pāraśarya, a survey of rival theories of the faculties, a three-way dispute over how many genuinely distinct substances the twenty-two faculties comprise, and a precise account of exactly how and why each one is predominant in its own domain, closing with the individual definition of every faculty in turn. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Twenty-Two Faculties: An Ocean the Buddha Alone Can Fully Sound

Fascicle 142 opens an entirely new aggregate of the treatise — the Faculties Aggregate — with its own founding list of twenty-two faculties, from the five physical senses through life, mind, the five feeling-faculties, the five faculties of spiritual cultivation, and the three uncontaminated faculties marking the path's own successive stages. Before analyzing a single one, the treatise pauses to defend the number itself: the Buddha's own teaching, like the ocean, is at once immeasurable in depth and boundless in breadth, so vast that even Śāriputra and the greatest treatise-masters, composing hundreds of thousands of commentaries on a single sentence of it, could never exhaust its furthest meaning.

根蘊第六中根納息第一之一二十二根。眼根耳根鼻根舌根身根女根男根命根意根樂根苦根喜根憂根捨根信根精進根念根定根慧根未知當知根已知根具知根。此二十二根。幾學。幾無學。幾非學非無學。如是等章。及解章義。既領會已應廣分別。問何故尊者以二十二根而作論。答是彼尊者所樂欲故。隨彼所欲不違法相而作斯論。不應責其所以。有說。不應問。彼尊者以契經說二十二根。彼根契經是此論根本。依彼經造論不可責其因緣。以彼尊者於佛所說二十二根不能少減說二十一。不能少增說二十三。以佛所說無增減故不可增減。如不可增減。不可多少。不可損益。無量無邊應知亦爾。以佛所說無量無邊。不可於中作量邊故。無量者義難測故。無邊者文難知故。譬如大海無量無邊。無量者深。無邊者廣。世尊所說應知亦然。雖百千俱胝那庾多數。如舍利子等諸大論師。於佛所說二句文義。造百千論分別解釋。盡其覺性不得邊際。是故不應責彼尊者。問置彼尊者。世尊何故說此契經。答觀所化者應聞此法得饒益故。復次此經有別緣起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth Aggregate, the Faculties: the Faculty Nyāsa, Chapter One, Part One. The twenty-two faculties: the eye-faculty, ear-faculty, nose-faculty, tongue-faculty, body-faculty, female faculty, male faculty, life-faculty, mind-faculty, pleasure-faculty, pain-faculty, joy-faculty, displeasure-faculty, equanimity-faculty, faith-faculty, diligence-faculty, mindfulness-faculty, concentration-faculty, wisdom-faculty, the faculty of what is yet unknown but about to be known, the faculty of what is already known, and the faculty of complete knowledge. Of these twenty-two faculties, how many belong to the trainee, how many to the one beyond training, and how many to neither? And so on, in such chapter headings. Having now grasped this chapter heading and its explanatory meaning in full, we should next analyze it in full. Question: why does the Venerable author compose this treatise using precisely twenty-two faculties? Answer: it is simply the intention of the treatise's own author, that this order be followed — the treatise was composed following his own preference, and, so long as this does not contradict the proper form of the Dharma, this ought not to be demanded as an objection. Others say: this ought not to be asked at all — the Venerable author relies on the sūtra's own statement of the twenty-two faculties, and that sūtra on the faculties is itself the very foundation of this treatise; composing a treatise in reliance on that sūtra, one ought not to demand its underlying reason, since the Venerable author, with respect to the Buddha's own stated twenty-two faculties, could not diminish them by even a single one to make twenty-one, nor increase them by even a single one to make twenty-three — for what the Buddha himself has taught admits of neither increase nor decrease, and hence cannot be increased or decreased, nor made more or fewer, nor added to or subtracted from; it should be understood that this holds equally for what is boundless and immeasurable, since what the Buddha teaches is boundless and immeasurable, admitting of no measure or boundary within it — "immeasurable" meaning its own significance is difficult to fathom, "boundless" meaning its own text is difficult to fully grasp — exactly as the great ocean itself is boundless and immeasurable, "immeasurable" referring to its depth, "boundless" to its breadth; the World-Honored One's own teaching should be understood in just the same way. Though hundreds of thousands of koṭis of niyutas of great treatise-masters, such as Śāriputra and the rest, have composed hundreds of thousands of treatises analyzing and explaining the meaning of even a single one of the Buddha's own sentences, they exhaust the very limits of their own faculty of understanding and still cannot reach its furthest boundary — and it is for this reason that the Venerable author's own choice ought not to be demanded an explanation for. Question: setting aside the Venerable author's own reason — why did the World-Honored One himself teach this sūtra? Answer: because he observed that those to be taught would attain benefit upon hearing this teaching. Further, this sūtra has its own particular occasion for arising.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Comparing the Buddha's teaching to an ocean that even its most devoted scholars can never fully sound is more than decorative praise — it is a genuine methodological warning placed at the very threshold of an entire new aggregate: whatever exhaustive analysis is about to follow, the treatise wants its reader to hold firmly in mind that completeness of method is never the same thing as completeness of the subject itself.

2. A Brahmin's Question, and Ānanda's Own Second Objection

A vivid narrative frame arrives: the brahmin Jyotiṣka asks the Buddha how many faculties comprehensively subsume every faculty, prompting the Buddha's own firm answer of twenty-two, no more and no less. The passage traces this question back to its own occasion — a survey of rival ninety-six heterodox paths on the nature of "faculty," including the Jain single life-faculty said to pervade even inanimate matter, and the heterodox teacher Pāraśarya's own claim that true "noble cultivation of the faculties" means the eye simply not seeing form and the ear not hearing sound — a claim the Buddha demolishes with a single question (would the blind, then, count as noble?), immediately echoed by Ānanda's own parallel objection about the deaf.

Chinese Source

謂有梵志名曰生聞。來詣佛所歡喜。問訊。在一面坐而白佛言。施設幾根攝諸根盡。佛言。我說二十二根攝諸根盡。若有遮此更說餘根。當知彼說有言無義。若還問彼反生愚惑。所以者何。非其境故由梵志問。說此契經。是故不應徵佛說意。問置佛說意。梵志何故但問諸根。不問蘊界處等。答隨彼所疑而問。不應為責。或彼梵志性善尋思。隨有所聞憙便歷問。為知根義周遍遊歷九十六道問諸根量。如離繫者施設一根所謂命根遍內外物。故彼立制不飲冷水。不斷生草以有命故。問諸外道於外物中。執有何根名有根法。答有於外物執有意根。有於外物執有命根。有於外物執有二根。由意根故名有根法。由命根故名有命法。有於外物執有二根。謂業與意隨應當說。又如外道波羅設利。作如是說。眼不見色。耳不聞聲。名聖修根。問何故彼名波羅設利。答是所立名不應問立名所以。名隨假立不必皆有實義故。有說。此是彼姓。謂婆羅門有姓憍蹉。有姓筏蹉。有姓扇祑略。有姓憍陳那。有姓婆羅墮闍。有姓波羅設利。有說。此是雜種。謂從剎帝利婆羅門生。名波羅設利。如從驢馬所生名騾。如是說者。此是彼姓。彼有弟子名嗢怛羅。曾於一時來詣佛所。歡喜問訊在一面坐。佛時告曰。汝師波羅設利。為汝等說修根法耶。嗢怛羅言。我師曾說。佛問云何。彼云。我師作如是說。眼不見色。耳不聞聲。名聖修根無所取故。佛即難曰。若爾盲者應名聖修根不見色故。時阿難陀佛邊侍立。為佛搖扇。尋亦難言。聾者亦應名聖修根不聞聲故。問設有外道。百千俱胝那庾多數。智慧辯才如舍利子。佛皆能伏。何故世尊作初難已。尊者慶喜作第二難。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a certain brahmin named Jyotiṣka, who came before the Buddha, and, rejoicing, exchanged greetings, then withdrew to sit to one side, saying to the Buddha, "How many faculties, when established, comprehensively subsume every faculty?" The Buddha said, "I teach twenty-two faculties, which comprehensively subsume every faculty. Should anyone deny this and speak instead of some further faculty, know that such speech is mere words without genuine meaning; and, were one to question them further on this, it would only give rise to greater confusion — for why is this? Because it lies outside their own proper domain." It was on account of this brahmin's own question that this sūtra was spoken, and hence one ought not to interrogate the Buddha's own underlying intention in speaking it. Question: setting aside the Buddha's own intention — why did the brahmin ask only about the faculties, and not about the aggregates, elements, sense-fields, and the rest? Answer: he asked according to whatever doubt he himself held, and this ought not to be demanded an explanation for; or, perhaps this brahmin was, by nature, given to careful reflection, and, upon hearing of anything, delighted in inquiring into it thoroughly — wishing to understand the meaning of "faculty," he had traveled comprehensively through all ninety-six heterodox paths, inquiring everywhere into the measure of the faculties — for instance, the Jain ascetics posit a single faculty, namely the life-faculty, said to pervade both internal and external objects alike, and it is on this very basis that they establish the prohibition against drinking cold water or cutting living grass, since they hold that these, too, possess life. Question: among the various outside paths, what do they hold to be the "faculty-endowed" dharma with respect to external objects? Answer: some hold that external objects possess a mind-faculty; some hold that external objects possess a life-faculty; some hold that external objects possess two faculties at once — on account of the mind-faculty, being called a "faculty-endowed dharma"; on account of the life-faculty, being called a "life-endowed dharma"; and some hold that external objects possess two faculties together, namely karma and mind, each spoken of accordingly. Further, as the heterodox teacher Pāraśarya himself taught: "The eye does not see form; the ear does not hear sound — this is called the noble cultivation of the faculties." Question: why is he named Pāraśarya? Answer: this is simply the name given him, and one ought not to ask the reason behind a given name, since names are assigned by convention and need not always carry genuine meaning. Others say: this is his own clan-name — for, among brahmins, there are the Kauṇḍinya clan, the Vātsa clan, the Śāṇḍilya clan, the Kauṇḍinya-proper clan, the Bhāradvāja clan, and the Pāraśarya clan. Others say: this refers to one of mixed birth — that is, born from a kṣatriya father and a brahmin mother, one is called Pāraśarya, exactly as the offspring of a donkey and a horse is called a mule — and, according to this final account, it is indeed his own clan-name. This teacher had a disciple named Uttara, who once came before the Buddha, and, rejoicing, exchanged greetings, then withdrew to sit to one side. The Buddha then asked him, "Does your own teacher Pāraśarya teach you the cultivation of the faculties in this way?" Uttara said, "My teacher has indeed taught this." The Buddha asked, "How does he teach it?" Uttara replied, "My teacher teaches it thus: 'the eye does not see form, the ear does not hear sound — this is called the noble cultivation of the faculties, since nothing at all is grasped.'" The Buddha at once raised an objection, saying, "If this were so, then the blind, too, ought to be called noble in the cultivation of the faculties, since they, too, fail to see form." At that time, Ānanda was standing at the Buddha's own side, fanning him, and he, too, at once raised a further objection, saying, "The deaf, too, ought then to be called noble in the cultivation of the faculties, since they, too, fail to hear sound." Question: even supposing there were outside-path teachers numbering in the hundreds of thousands of koṭis of niyutas, matching Śāriputra himself in wisdom and eloquence, the Buddha would still be capable of subduing every one of them alone — why, then, did the World-Honored One raise only the first objection, leaving the Venerable Ānanda to raise the second?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That Ānanda, standing quietly at the Buddha's side fanning him, spontaneously raises his own matching objection the instant the Buddha finishes his is a small narrative detail doing real work: it shows a disciple's understanding operating in such close harmony with his teacher's own reasoning that the same devastating logical move occurs to both of them independently, in the very same breath.

3. Nine Reasons the Buddha Let His Disciple Speak

A cascade of explanations addresses a pointed question: why did the Buddha, who could easily have refuted the heretic entirely alone, allow Ānanda to add a second objection rather than doing so himself? The reasons converge on a consistent theme — the Buddha noticed a sign in Ānanda's own throat signaling his wish to speak, has never once suppressed another's eloquence even across three incalculable eons as a bodhisattva, and wished to demonstrate that both teacher and disciple alike are capable of subduing an opponent, since only when both can do so is an opponent truly, thoroughly defeated.

世尊何故而不遮止。答佛觀慶喜咽喉有相。知欲設難故便自止。以佛昔在三無數劫。精懃修習菩薩行時。尚不斷他所有才辯。乃至弟子亦不遮遏。況今成佛斷他辯才。又佛了知若自所說。若慶喜說平等無異。無有增減故不遮止。又顯師與弟子俱能摧伏他故。若師弟子俱能伏者。乃名善伏故不遮制。又欲令彼無餘言故。謂若世尊作第一難。慶喜不作第二難者。則彼外道還自眾中。以餘慢心作如是言。雖為彼師所伏而非弟子。彼雖能伏我等而非我師。若佛世尊作第一難。慶喜復作第二難者。則彼永捨憍慢之心。弟子尚能摧伏我等何況彼師。設我師來亦不能對何況我等。又欲滿彼梵志意故彼作是念。喬答摩尊諸力士中。為無有上能伏能破一切論者而無屈撓。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why did the World-Honored One not himself prevent this? Answer: the Buddha, observing a certain sign appearing in Ānanda's own throat, knew that he wished to raise an objection of his own, and hence refrained from stopping him — for the Buddha, in ages past, across three incalculable eons, while diligently cultivating the bodhisattva path, never once suppressed even another's own gift for eloquent speech, let alone that of his own disciple — how much less, then, having now attained Buddhahood, would he suppress another's eloquence? Further, the Buddha, knowing full well that whatever he himself might say and whatever Ānanda might say would be entirely equal, without the slightest difference, neither more nor less, saw no reason to prevent it. Further, it reveals that both teacher and disciple alike are capable of subduing others — for only when both teacher and disciple alike are capable of subduing an opponent is one properly said to have subdued them thoroughly, and it was for this reason that the Buddha did not prevent it.

諸論師中最為尊勝。往昔所有諸大論師。尚無能對何況我等。若彼弟子與我談論。我當酬對豈不宜哉。佛一切時滿所化意。如其所念而汲引之。故不遮止慶喜所難。復次佛以慶喜為證義人。故彼外道眾信重慶喜。以彼尊者形貌端嚴。善知因陀羅聲明論故。佛意令彼問所信人。自驗師宗應正理不。慶喜時說故佛不止。復次欲顯世尊無勝己慮故。謂諸外道慮弟子中有勝己故。遮其言辯佛無是事。設有弟子百千俱胝那庾多數。辯才智慧如舍利子。亦無問難與佛等者況能勝佛。復次欲顯己斷法慳垢故。謂諸外道不許弟子與他論難。

Further, the Buddha wished to leave that heretic with no further words at all — that is, had the World-Honored One raised only the first objection, with Ānanda raising no second one, that heretic might then have returned to his own assembly and said, with lingering arrogance, "Though I was defeated by that teacher, I was not defeated by his disciple; though he was able to subdue me, his own teacher was not the one who did so." But, since the World-Honored One raised the first objection, and Ānanda then raised the second, that heretic was left to abandon his arrogance entirely, thinking, "If even the disciple was capable of subduing us, how much more so the teacher himself — even were my own teacher to come forward, he could not withstand this, let alone the rest of us." Further, the Buddha wished to fulfill this brahmin's own secret wish — for the brahmin had thought to himself, "Among all the mighty champions of debate, is there truly none who can surpass the ascetic Gautama, subduing and refuting every disputant without ever faltering — the most honored among all the great debaters of every age, whom even the greatest masters of the past could never withstand, let alone we ourselves? Should this teacher's own disciple engage me in debate, would it not be fitting for me to offer my own reply?" The Buddha, always fulfilling the deepest wish of those he taught, drew him forward exactly according to his own secret thought, and hence did not prevent Ānanda's own objection.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the standard for a truly complete refutation requires not just the teacher but the disciple as well to be independently capable of defeating the same opponent is a striking definition of pedagogical success: teaching is not judged finished merely because the teacher has won an argument, but only once the student, too, has genuinely absorbed the same reasoning well enough to wield it unprompted.

4. Rival Theories of the Faculties, and Why the Buddha's Count Cannot Be Moved

Further reasons for allowing Ānanda's objection pile up — leaving the heretic no residual excuse to save face, fulfilling the heretic's own secret wish to test whether even a disciple could out-argue him, using Ānanda as a witness the heretic assembly itself already trusted — before the treatise surveys the full landscape of rival faculty-theories the brahmin Jyotiṣka had encountered: the Vaiśeṣika school's five, the Sāṃkhya school's eleven, and an extravagant theory of one hundred and twenty faculties multiplied across six destinies. Confused and unconvinced by all of them, Jyotiṣka comes to the newly-awakened Buddha and asks his question directly, without repeating any rival count, precisely so the Buddha cannot simply be led to endorse whichever existing theory sounds best — receiving instead the Buddha's own firm declaration that not even omniscience itself could add or subtract a single faculty from the twenty-two he teaches.

勿彼由斯多獲名譽恭敬利養。佛則不爾。假使世間。一切皆得無邊名利。佛無一毫終無嫉妬。是故不止慶喜所說。復次欲顯弟子亦勝他故。謂諸外道恐己門徒與他論難墮負受辱。世尊弟子無不勝他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the Buddha made Ānanda his own witness in this matter of doctrine, since that heretic assembly held Ānanda in deep trust and respect, on account of his own dignified appearance and his own mastery of the treatises on Sanskrit grammar and phonetics — the Buddha's own intention being to let them question one whom they themselves trusted, to test for themselves whether their own teacher's doctrine was truly correct — and, since Ānanda spoke up at that very moment, the Buddha did not prevent it. Further, the Buddha wished to demonstrate that he himself harbors no anxiety over any rival — for the heretics feared that some disciple might surpass their own teacher, and hence suppressed their disciples' own eloquence, but the Buddha has no such concern; even were there disciples numbering in the hundreds of thousands of koṭis of niyutas, matching Śāriputra himself in eloquence and wisdom, none could ever raise an objection equal to the Buddha's own, let alone surpass him.

5. Twenty-Two Names, How Many Real Things? Three Rival Answers

A genuinely foundational metaphysical dispute follows: though twenty-two faculties are named, how many genuinely distinct substances do they actually comprise? 15. Why This One Small Region of the Body Gets Its Own Name

A genuinely pointed question asks why, given that the body-faculty's own subtle particles pervade the entire body equally, only one small region earns the separate name of the male or female faculty — answered by its unique capacity to manifest and display a being's own sex, a capacity that notably fails for a hermaphrodite, whose own indeterminate nature is precisely why such a being counts as neither male nor female at all. Other masters locate the same region's significance instead in its role as the site where beings either "turn" into further rebirth or finally "return" out of it altogether.

16. Defining Every Faculty, One by One

Having settled every general question of why the faculties as a whole earn their name, the treatise turns at last to defining each one individually, in the treatise's own precise, formulaic style: the eye-faculty as whatever has seen, now sees, or will see form; the female and male faculties as a small portion of the body-faculty; the life-faculty as the very lifespan belonging to the three realms; and each of the five feeling-faculties defined by its own precise combination of bodily or mental location, agreeable or disagreeable contact, and balanced or unbalanced classification.

17. Why Wisdom Alone Gets Mentioned Twice

A closing question notices something curious in the definition of the faculty of what is yet unknown but about to be known: among its nine constituent faculties, wisdom alone is named explicitly twice, while the remaining eight are simply gathered under one collective mention — answered by an extraordinary, almost lyrical cascade of images for wisdom's own unique supremacy, comparing it to a general, a guide, a lamp no thief can rob, a sharp blade cutting every fetter, a lofty tower, and the single quality capable of manifesting that the Buddha himself has no equal in all the world.
