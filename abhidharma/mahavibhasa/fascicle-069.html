The Great Debate on the In-Between

Fascicle 69 completes the latent-tendency ledger for the six kinds of being, then stages the historic debate between the Vibhajyavādins and the Proponents of Correct Reasoning over whether the intermediate state between death and rebirth truly exists, whether it counts as a destiny in its own right, and which realms possess one. It closes with three vivid stories testing whether intermediate-state karma can ever be diverted — the unlearned monk, the virtuous and wicked men of Śrāvastī, and King Bimbisāra's own poignant final hours. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Completing the Latent-Tendency Ledger for the Six Kinds of Being

Opening part seven of the Sentient-Being Nyāsa, the treatise tallies exactly how many latent tendencies increase, and how many fetters bind, an ordinary person and a noble one in each of the three realms — ninety-eight and nine for a desire-realm ordinary person, dwindling down to three and three for a formless-realm noble one. A cascade of five independent justifications then explains why a being is still said to have an upper realm's own latent tendencies increasing upon him even when those latent tendencies plainly do not belong to his own present realm at all — appeal to an acquisition not yet released, to that acquisition's own current operation, to its standing across the three times, to its capacity to presently arise, and to its having, at some beginningless point, already manifested.

The five justifications offered here are not really five competing theories so much as five different lenses trained on the very same underlying fact: a being's relationship to a latent tendency he does not currently manifest is genuinely multi-layered, and this treatise refuses to settle for any single account of what 'increase' means when a more complete picture is available by simply stacking every true description on top of the others.

2. Two Scriptural Parallels, and a New Question Opens

The treatise notes that its own classification of a noble one's latent tendencies rests entirely on what currently manifests, not on bare technical possession — precisely mirroring two scriptural precedents where the Buddha's own status is likewise fixed by present operation rather than latent potential: his declaration to a brahmin that he is neither a god, a nāga, nor a human, since his contaminants no longer manifest at all, and a verse naming him the one true person, sustaining a ripened result's own continuity by having realized the truly real dharma. From this scriptural parallel the fascicle pivots to an entirely new and far larger question: why does this nyāsa so often compose its treatises with reference to the intermediate state between death and rebirth, opposing the Vibhajyavādins' outright denial of it and affirming the Proponents of Correct Reasoning's own position that the desire and form realms definitely possess one.

3. The Vibhajyavādins' Case Against the Intermediate State

The Vibhajyavādins ground their denial of the intermediate state in scriptural authority alone: a sutra stating that whoever commits one of the five acts of immediate retribution is reborn in hell without any interval, and a verse lamenting that the dying, seeking somewhere to stand along the road ahead, find nowhere at all to rest in between — both read as proving that death passes into rebirth with no intervening gap whatsoever. A further argument by analogy compares this transition to the vanishing of shadow into light, with no interval separating the two.

Both scriptural citations share a common vulnerability that will occupy the rest of this debate: each speaks of there being 'no interval,' but neither actually specifies what kind of interval is being denied — a gap in time, or merely a gap in place — and the entire subsequent argument turns on which of these two very different claims the words were ever meant to make.

4. The Proponents of Correct Reasoning's Case For the Intermediate State

The Proponents answer with their own scriptural citations: a sutra stating that conception requires three things to coincide, the third being a gandharva presently manifest — a term that, apart from an intermediate-state being, denotes nothing at all, since the prior aggregates have already perished; a further sutra naming those who attain final nirvāṇa midway, a term with no possible referent unless an intermediate state exists to attain it in; and a scriptural declaration that, this body already destroyed and the next not yet arisen, a mind-made being clings to grasping through craving — language that, absent an intermediate state, denotes nothing whatsoever. A further argument by consequence observes that denying the intermediate state entirely would force one to say that a being who dies on one continent and is reborn on another suffers an actual ontological gap, with a body coming to exist that never existed before and one that did exist ceasing to exist altogether — a violation so serious that the intermediate state must exist simply to prevent it.

5. The Proponents Reinterpret the Vibhajyavādins' Scriptures as Being of Provisional Meaning

The Proponents of Correct Reasoning turn to explain away the Vibhajyavādins' own two citations, arguing that both merely rule out other destinies and other kinds of karma, not the intermediate state itself — the five-act sutra specifying only that this particular karma summons hell and no other destiny, ripening in the very next life rather than this one or some later one, with the number five and the phrase "without interval" both collapsing into absurdity if read with strict literalism. Turning to the argument by analogy, they observe that shadow and light are not even sentient beings at all, and that the very absence of a gap between them, read correctly, proves the intermediate state rather than disproving it — since death, intermediate state, and rebirth likewise pass into one another with no gap whatsoever, exactly as shadow passes into light.

6. The Vibhajyavādins Reinterpret the Proponents' Three Scriptures in Turn

7. The Final Refutation and Verdict

The Vibhajyavādins offer one last defense against the argument-by-consequence: death and rebirth touch without any true gap, exactly as an inchworm plants its front foot before lifting its hind foot from the ground. The Proponents demolish this with a single devastating consequence — were this true, a person dying among humans and being reborn in hell would, for one horrifying instant, possess a body that is simultaneously human and hellish, a destiny simultaneously ruined and not yet ruined, and two minds arising together within what is supposed to be a single continuous being. The compilers then render their final verdict: the Proponents of Correct Reasoning prevail decisively, the Vibhajyavādins' denial being nothing but the fruit of ignorance and undiligence, and this treatise is composed not merely to defeat a rival school but to display the correct principle of every dharma for the genuine benefit of students.

8. Is the Intermediate State Included Within a Destiny?

A new tetralemma opens: is the intermediate state included within one of the five destinies, or not? Both answers strain against scripture. If included, the Prajñaptiśāstra's own careful statement that the four modes of birth include the five destinies — never the reverse — seems to leave the intermediate state stranded as precisely what is not included, while the Dharmaskandha and Prakaraṇapāda both explicitly list an intermediate-state being's own eye alongside the eyes of hell-beings, animals, and the rest, suggesting it is not simply absorbed into any one destiny. Each treatise is reconciled in turn: the Prajñaptiśāstra's own text is read as speaking of mutual inclusion rather than one-way containment, the two Abhidharma treatises' extra mention of an intermediate-state eye is read as singling out something already included for special emphasis on account of its subtlety, and Dharmadatta's rice-sprout is read as speaking only to outward resemblance, not real inclusion.

12. Is Intermediate-State Karma Changeable? The Dārṣṭāntika View and the Abhidharma Masters' Refusal

The Dārṣṭāntikas argue that intermediate-state karma, like all karma, can be diverted from its course, reasoning that even the five acts of immediate retribution must be alterable, since otherwise no one could ever surpass the very peak of existence — its own wholesome karma being the most excellent possible — and yet some clearly do surpass it. The Abhidharma masters flatly refuse this conclusion: the intermediate state cannot be diverted in realm, destiny, or place, since the very karma that summons it is of such extreme, concentrated force that nothing could turn it aside once it has begun to operate.

13. The Unlearned Monk Who Denied Liberation

A vivid story tests the claim that intermediate-state karma cannot be diverted in realm: a sincere but unlearned monk, mistaking each worldly dhyāna he attains for successive fruits of the path up through arhatship, sees the fourth dhyāna's own rebirth-sign at the moment of death — and, unable to explain why any further rebirth-sign should appear at all to one he believes has already ended rebirth forever, denies liberation itself out of wounded pride, and the sign shifts at once to the hell of unrelenting torment, where he is in fact reborn. 14. The Virtuous Man and the Wicked Man of Śrāvastī, Whose Destiny-Signs Flip at the Very Last Moment

A parallel story tests whether the intermediate state can be diverted in destiny: a lifelong virtuous man, at the point of death, is startled to see a hell-bound sign rather than the heavenly one his own conduct would seem to warrant, until he recalls that a single residual evil karma must simply be ripening now — and the very act of then recollecting his life's own abundant virtue, with genuine joy, causes the hell-sign to vanish and a heavenly one to appear instead, sending him to rebirth among the gods. A lifelong wicked man undergoes the mirror-image reversal: seeing an undeserved heavenly sign, he denies the very existence of karmic cause and effect out of confusion, and this denial itself causes the heavenly sign to vanish, replaced by the hell-sign he is then reborn into. Both reversals, again, are located within the dying moment itself, prior to any actual intermediate state.

15. King Bimbisāra's Final Hours, and His Rebirth as Vaiśravaṇa's Son

The most affecting of the three stories tests whether the intermediate state can be diverted in place. King Bimbisāra, imprisoned and starved by his own son Ajātaśatru, is comforted by a ray of the Buddha's own compassionate light through his prison window, and, at the Buddha's own bidding, the Venerable Mahāmaudgalyāyana visits him to explain that even the Buddha cannot escape every last trace of karmic suffering. Too weak from hunger to follow the deeper teaching, the dying king asks instead which heaven's own food is most delicious — and, hearing Maudgalyāyana describe the feast prepared for Vaiśravaṇa, the Multi-Hearing Heavenly King, he dies craving that meal above his own long-cultivated path to Tuṣita, and is reborn instead as Vaiśravaṇa's own son, taking the name Most Victorious Honored One, before joyfully returning to visit the Buddha in person. As with the two stories before it, the entire diversion is located within the king's own dying moment, the Tuṣita-sign quietly giving way to the Vaiśravaṇa-sign before any intermediate state had yet arisen at all.

That a king who spent a lifetime cultivating the most excellent karma for Tuṣita Heaven can, in his very last moments, be redirected by nothing more than a craving for a well-described meal is the fascicle's most human illustration of everything the two earlier stories established more abstractly: the dying moment is not a passive antechamber to whatever destiny one's karma has already fixed, but the single most consequential moment of mental activity in an entire lifetime, where even a lifetime's accumulated merit can still be quietly redirected by one final, unguarded desire.
