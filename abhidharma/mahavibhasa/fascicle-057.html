The Donor Who Gave Unequal Gifts

Fascicle 57 continues the Single-Class-of-Conduct Nyāsa, completing the hatred-and-view tetralemma and resolving a puzzle about disjunction with a simile drawn from the Vinaya's own two kinds of purity. The treatise then works systematically through hatred, ignorance, view, grasping, doubt, envy, and stinginess, tetralemma by tetralemma, naming its own recurring method — "a single class of conduct" — before closing on two small, vivid scenes: a monk who envies his own imagined better fortune, and a stranger who resents a gift that was never his property to begin with. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Hatred and View: The Final Case

Fascicle 57 opens mid-sentence, completing the hatred-and-view tetralemma's own final case from the previous fascicle: the precise conditions under which a fully bound person, or one who has progressed through the four truths' cognitions in sequence, or one who has left the desire, form, or formless realm's own defilement behind entirely, comes to be bound by neither fetter at all — because, in every dharma under discussion, both what binds and what is bound have already been completely cut off.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The phrase "binding by neither" recurs constantly through this fascicle's tetralemmas, and it is worth noticing that it names two structurally different situations at once — a dharma that was once bound and has since been liberated, and a dharma that, by the very realm it belongs to, was never a candidate for that particular fetter to begin with. The next block makes this ambiguity explicit and resolves it directly.

2. The Vinaya's Two Kinds of Purity

Asked how the form and formless realms can be called "disjoined" from a hatred-fetter they never possessed in the first place, the treatise draws a precise distinction between two senses of the word: disjunction attained from a former binding, and disjunction that was simply the dharma's own original nature all along — illustrated by the Vinaya's own two kinds of pure monastic, one who never transgressed the precepts to begin with and one who transgressed and then repented in accordance with the Dharma. Doubt is then noted to behave exactly like view in every technical respect, and hatred's own tetralemma against grasping opens with its first case: binding by hatred without binding by grasping.

Reaching for a Vinaya simile to solve an Abhidharma classification problem is a small but telling cross-reference: the treatise trusts its audience to already carry the monastic disciplinary literature in mind, and finds in it exactly the conceptual distinction — purity by original innocence versus purity by recovered innocence — that the metaphysics of binding and disjunction needs at this exact moment.

3. Hatred Against Grasping, Then Against Envy

The hatred-grasping tetralemma runs its full course — hatred without grasping, grasping without hatred, both together, and neither together — tracked section by section through the desire realm's own detailed stage-by-stage ledger, before hatred's relationship to envy resolves far more simply: envy, confined solely to the desire realm's own section abandoned by cultivation, is shown to be strictly narrower than hatred, so that whatever is bound by envy is certainly bound by hatred, though the reverse never holds.

4. Ignorance Against View

The treatise turns to ignorance, the broadest-reaching affliction in this entire taxonomy, spanning all three realms, all five sections, and both contaminated and uncontaminated objects alike. Set against view, which reaches only four sections, the relationship resolves in one direction only: whatever is bound by view is certainly bound by ignorance, and the treatise works out, in exhaustive detail, every case where ignorance still binds a dharma that view no longer touches — most strikingly, the dharmas dissociated from the view-fetter itself, where ignorance persists precisely because it does not depend on the narrower fetter it accompanies.

7. View and Doubt Bound Together, and a Single Class of Conduct

View and doubt's own tetralemma reaches its remaining two cases in full — every stage of the path counted out section by section for a fully bound person, down to the exact moment where both fetters together vanish from a dharma at once — before the treatise turns to view against envy, running the identical four-case machinery a second time, and finally stops to name its own method out loud: view's comparison against everything that follows it constitutes "a single class of conduct," and doubt's own comparison constitutes another, the very phrase this entire nyāsa takes its name from.

That the treatise waits until its eleventh block to explicitly name the analytical pattern it has been running since fascicle 56's opening page is a deliberate structural choice, not an oversight: the method needed to be demonstrated exhaustively, pair by pair, tetralemma by tetralemma, before naming it could mean anything more than a label — by the time the name finally arrives, the reader has already seen enough repetitions to recognize exactly what single, repeatable procedure is being pointed at.

8. Grasping Against Doubt and Envy

Grasping and doubt prove strictly asymmetric, grasping's broader reach absorbing doubt entirely wherever doubt still binds, while grasping alone continues to bind the dharmas dissociated from doubt that remain after doubt's own universally-pervading portion has already been cut off. Grasping against envy then runs a full tetralemma of its own, structurally identical to hatred's earlier comparison against the very same narrow, cultivation-only fetter.

9. The Donor Who Gave Unequal Gifts

The fascicle closes with envy and stinginess, the two narrowest fetters in the entire taxonomy, proving an exact match in every respect — whatever is bound by one is certainly bound by the other. Asked why this symmetric answer holds despite envy nominally arising toward others and stinginess toward oneself, the treatise offers two narrative proofs that each can cross the boundary: a monk who receives poorly made requisites can envy even his own imagined better fortune, comparing himself to himself, and an onlooker who owns nothing at all in a gift can still resent a stranger's decision to give it away.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both closing examples work by showing that the ordinary grammar of "toward myself" versus "toward another" is not, in fact, what envy and stinginess actually track: the monk's envy arises entirely within his own mind, yet still counts as taking himself as its object in comparison with an imagined alternative self, while the onlooker's stinginess arises over property he has no claim to whatsoever — the treatise using two small, vivid, entirely ordinary scenes to demonstrate that these afflictions follow the structure of comparison and possessiveness itself, not the literal boundary of whose property is actually at stake.
