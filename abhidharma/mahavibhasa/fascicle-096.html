Thirty-Seven Names, Ten Substances

Fascicle 96 closes the debate over simultaneous arising, then gives a full accounting of the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment — their genuine substance, sequence, and distribution across every ground — closing with how mere listening can be credited with cutting off the five hindrances. Fascicle 96 continues the Branches of the Learner Nyāsa (學支納息第一之四), closing the debate over simultaneous versus sequential arising of the seven awakening-factors (七覺支) opened in fascicle 95, then turning to a full technical accounting of the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment (三十七菩提分法) — the four applications of mindfulness, four right exertions, four bases of supernormal power, five faculties, five powers, seven awakening-factors, and eight path-branches. Terms established across fascicles 93–95 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Nine Ways to Reconcile Śāriputra's Scripture with Simultaneous Arising

Fascicle 96 opens exactly where fascicle 95 broke off, completing the defense of simultaneous arising against the Dārṣṭāntikas and the Great Virtuous One. The scripture in which Śāriputra claims to dwell in the awakening-factors' concentration at any hour of the day is shown not to require sequential arising at all — it merely describes abiding within one predominant aspect at will. No fewer than five further scriptural regroupings then reinforce the same point: the same "morning, midday, evening" language works equally well applied to the three grounds, the three faculties, the three concentrations, the three knowledges, and the nine grounds, in every case describing a shift of predominance rather than a sequence of arising — and the section closes by naming and refuting three further rival positions about which grounds do or do not possess joy, right intention, and precepts.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the very same three-part temporal structure — morning, midday, evening — can be remapped five separate times onto five entirely different sets of three categories, and in every case yield a coherent, non-contradictory reading, is itself a quiet argument for the Vaibhāṣika position: a scriptural formula this flexible, bending equally well to grounds, faculties, concentrations, knowledges, and nine-fold classifications alike, is far more plausibly describing a general principle of shifting predominance than any single literal claim about which mental factor happens to arise at which hour.

2. Counting Awakening-Factors and Path-Branches, Branch by Branch and Ground by Ground

An exhaustive technical apparatus now tracks, for each individual awakening-factor and path-branch in turn — mindfulness, joy, right intention, right speech, right action, and right livelihood — exactly how many awakening-factors and path-branches manifest together with it at every concentration-ground, for both the learner and the non-learner. Access grounds are shown to lack the joy awakening-factor because a mind not yet decisively free of the defilement below finds nothing sufficiently remarkable to rejoice over; grounds above the first dhyāna lack right intention because it is itself a coarse, seeking, verbal-and-bodily-karma-dependent factor with no place in increasingly subtle attainments; and the formless attainments lack all three verbal and bodily precepts because precepts are themselves a portion of form, accomplished by the very great elements a formless attainment has, by definition, left behind.

3. The Thirty-Seven Factors of Enlightenment, and Their Genuine Substance

5. Why This Order? The Seven Categories, and the Order Within Each

The fascicle explains why the seven categories of the factors of enlightenment, and the individual members within the awakening-factors and path-branches, are taught in exactly the sequence they are — first through simple appeals to elegant phrasing and pedagogical convenience, then through a genuinely developmental account: mindfulness first breaks down the body and its objects, enabling right exertion, which purifies the mental continuum enough to support the concentration-bases, which in turn empower the faculties, which mature into unshakeable powers, which finally allow full awakening to the four truths through the seven awakening-factors, culminating in the eight path-branches' own weariness with birth-and-death. 6. How Many Factors of Enlightenment Exist, and Manifest Together, at Each Ground

A final combinatorial count closes the ground-by-ground apparatus: the not-yet-arrived concentration possesses thirty-six of the thirty-seven factors, lacking only joy; the first dhyāna alone possesses the full thirty-seven; the intermediate, third, and fourth dhyānas possess thirty-five, lacking joy and right intention; the second dhyāna possesses thirty-six, lacking only right intention; the three lower formless attainments possess thirty-two; and the desire realm and the peak of existence possess only twenty-two, lacking every awakening-factor entirely, since these are solely undefiled. A further count then asks how many can manifest together in a single moment at each of these same grounds, invariably three fewer than the ground's own total, since the four applications of mindfulness — each keyed to a distinct object — can never manifest more than one at a time.

7. Joy, Tranquility, Equanimity, and Why Mind Itself Is Excluded

A rich tetralemma maps which of the thirty-seven are awakening-factors without being path-branches (joy, tranquility, equanimity), path-branches without being awakening-factors (right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood), both (nearly everything else), or neither (faith alone) — with joy earning its place through the same mutually reinforcing cycle of digging and delight already seen with treasure-digging similes elsewhere in this fascicle, and right intention earning its own exclusion from the awakening-factors through its restless, ox-goading character, wholly incompatible with the tranquility that genuine awakening requires. A further puzzle asks why mind itself is never counted among the factors of enlightenment at all, resolved through no fewer than six converging reasons, before the universal mental factors are individually sorted: mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom qualify outright, feeling qualifies despite its double allegiance to both the defiled and pure categories, and perception, volition, contact, and desire are excluded as too strongly inclined toward defilement.

9. The Three Faculties, and Why the Awakening-Factor Formula Says More

A final major topic opens: the Vibhajyavādins' claim that there exist forty-one factors of enlightenment (the standard thirty-seven plus the four noble lineages) is rejected outright as contrary to principle, before the *Prakaraṇapāda*'s own precise definitions of the mindfulness awakening-factor and right view are laid out through the three faculties — "I shall come to know the unknown," belonging to the noble disciple newly reflecting on the four truths; "already knowing," belonging to the learner who has seen the calamity of birth-and-death; and "fully knowing," belonging to the arhat contemplating his own mind's liberation. A puzzle then asks why the awakening-factor formula explicitly says "capable of assisting enlightenment" while the parallel path-branch formula omits this phrase entirely, resolved through four converging reasons culminating in the observation that the path of cultivation lies close to the knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising — itself named "enlightenment" — while the path of seeing remains comparatively remote from it. The section closes with the Venerable Vasumitra's resolution of how the contaminated contemplation of impurity can be said to "co-arise" with the wholly undefiled mindfulness awakening-factor: not literally at once, but as two states passing the baton back and forth in immediate, uninterrupted succession.

10. How Mere Listening Can Cut Off the Five Hindrances

The fascicle closes with a final puzzle: how can a scripture credit "listening to the dharma with a single, one-pointed mind through the ear" with cutting off the five hindrances, when it is only mental consciousness's own wisdom accomplished through cultivation — never the ear-consciousness itself, nor the innate wisdom of mere hearing or thinking — that is genuinely capable of cutting off affliction? The answer traces an unbroken chain of immediately successive causes: wholesome ear-consciousness gives rise to wholesome mental consciousness, which gives rise to the wisdom accomplished through hearing, then through thinking, then through cultivation, which alone, once fully matured, cuts off the five hindrances — hearing thus deserves the credit as the chain's own first link, not as the cutting-off's own direct cause.
