What Makes an Ordinary Being Ordinary

Fascicle 45 continues the Volition Nyāsa with a foundational question the tradition had never fully settled: what precisely is the "nature of an ordinary being" that separates every unenlightened worldling from a noble one? The treatise works through a genuine dilemma about non-attainment, a nature relinquished all at once yet cut off across nine separate meditative stages, and closes with an exhaustive combinatorial map of how the eight limbs of the wrong eightfold path — wrong view, intention, effort, mindfulness, concentration, speech, action, and livelihood — relate to one another across every realm and every defiled state of mind. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. What Is "the Nature of an Ordinary Being"?

Fascicle 45 opens the Volition Nyāsa's fourth part with a foundational question: what actually makes someone an "ordinary being" (an unenlightened worldling) rather than a noble one? The treatise refutes the Vātsīputrīyas, who confine this nature to ten specific latent tendencies bound to the desire realm alone, and the Dārṣṭāntikas, who deny it any real substance at all — insisting instead that it spans all three realms, is abandoned only through cultivation, and genuinely exists as its own dissociated mental factor. A curious naming puzzle opens alongside this: this treatise speaks of "the nature of an ordinary being," while the companion Prakaraṇapāda speaks instead of "the dharmas of an ordinary being."

Chinese Source

云何異生性。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為止他宗顯正義故。謂或有執欲界見苦所斷十隨眠是異生性。如犢子部。彼說異生性是欲界繫。是染污性是見所斷。是相應行蘊攝。為遮彼執顯異生性是三界繫。是修所斷。是不染污。是不相應行蘊所攝。或復有執。異生性無實體。如譬喻者為遮彼執顯異生性自體實有。為遮此等諸部異執。顯示正理故作斯論。此本論中說異生性。品類足論說異生法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the nature of an ordinary being? And so on, at length. One asks: why is this treatise composed? The answer: it is composed in order to refute other schools and to reveal the correct meaning — that is, there are those who hold that the ten latent tendencies of the desire realm abandoned by seeing suffering are themselves the nature of an ordinary being, as the Vātsīputrīyas maintain, who say that the nature of an ordinary being is bound to the desire realm, is of a defiled nature, is abandoned by seeing, and is subsumed within the associated aggregate of formations; in order to block this position, and to reveal that the nature of an ordinary being is bound to all three realms, is abandoned by cultivation, is undefiled, and is subsumed within the dissociated aggregate of formations, or, further, there are those who hold that the nature of an ordinary being possesses no genuine substance at all, as the Dārṣṭāntikas maintain; in order to block this position, and to reveal that the nature of an ordinary being genuinely possesses its own substance, and in order to block these various mistaken positions held by different schools, and to reveal correct principle, this treatise is composed. This foundational treatise speaks of "the nature of an ordinary being"; the Prakaraṇapāda speaks instead of "the dharmas of an ordinary being."

如說。云何異生法。謂地獄傍生鬼界比俱盧洲無想天。彼業彼生是謂異生法。問何故此本論中。說異生性非異生法。品類足論。說異生法非異生性耶。答是作論者意欲爾故。復次此彼皆是有餘說故。

As it states: what are the dharmas of an ordinary being? They refer to the hell-destiny, the destiny of beasts, the realm of hungry ghosts, the continent of Uttarakuru, and the heaven of no-perception — the karma and the birth belonging to these are called the dharmas of an ordinary being. One asks: why does this foundational treatise speak of the nature of an ordinary being rather than the dharmas of an ordinary being, while the Prakaraṇapāda speaks of the dharmas of an ordinary being rather than the nature of an ordinary being? The answer: this is simply because the author of each treatise intended it so.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That two companion treatises from the same broad tradition can use different terms for what turns out to be closely related territory, without either being simply wrong, is a reminder that even a system this committed to precise definition still developed across multiple texts with their own emphases and vocabularies — consistency here is achieved by careful cross-reference and explanation, not by forcing every text to use identical language.

2. Why Two Different Names, and What "Ordinary" Actually Means

The treatise offers several explanations for why it and the Prakaraṇapāda chose different terms — perhaps sheer authorial preference, perhaps each treatise illuminating the other by implication, perhaps a genuine clue to which text was composed first. It then turns to etymology: Vasumitra explains that this nature is named for its capacity to cause beings to give rise to divergent views, afflictions, karma, and destinies — the very word for "ordinary being" in the source language carrying the sense of difference and divergence at its core.

Chinese Source

復次此彼二論各說一種互相顯故。復次異生性勝。非異生法。此本論中且就勝說。此論已說異生性。故品類足論不重說之。此論未說異生法故。品類足論說異生法。此顯彼論在此後造。有作是說。彼論已說異生法故此不重說。彼論未說異生性。故此論說之。此顯彼論在此先造。問何故名異生性。尊者世友作如是說。能令有情起異類見異類煩惱造異類業。受異類果異類生故。名異生性。復次能令有情墮異界故。往異趣故。受異生故。名異生性。復次能令有情信異師故。作異相故。受異法故。行異行故。求異果故。名異生性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, both are, in each case, a matter of surplus meaning left implicit. Further, these two treatises each speak of one and thereby illuminate the other by implication. Further, the nature of an ordinary being is the more preeminent of the two, not the dharmas of an ordinary being, and this foundational treatise, speaking with reference to what is preeminent, has already spoken of the nature of an ordinary being, and it is for this reason that the Prakaraṇapāda does not speak of it again; this treatise has not yet spoken of the dharmas of an ordinary being, and it is for this reason that the Prakaraṇapāda speaks of them — this shows that that treatise was composed after this one. There are those who say: that treatise had already spoken of the dharmas of an ordinary being, and it is for this reason that this treatise does not speak of them again; that treatise had not yet spoken of the nature of an ordinary being, and it is for this reason that this treatise speaks of it — this shows that that treatise was composed before this one. One asks: why is it named the nature of an ordinary being? The Venerable Vasumitra offers this explanation: it is able to cause sentient beings to give rise to divergent views, divergent afflictions, and to create divergent karma, undergoing divergent fruits and divergent births, and it is for this reason named the nature of an ordinary being. Further, it is able to cause sentient beings to fall into divergent realms, to proceed toward divergent destinies, and to undergo divergent births, and it is for this reason named the nature of an ordinary being. Further, it is able to cause sentient beings to place their faith in divergent teachers, to adopt divergent marks, to undergo divergent dharmas, to practice divergent conduct, and to seek divergent fruits, and it is for this reason named the nature of an ordinary being.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Rooting the very word for "ordinary being" in a sense of divergence and difference — rather than, say, ignorance or attachment — frames unenlightened existence in a distinctive way: what defines an ordinary being isn't primarily a deficit of knowledge but a kind of scattered multiplicity, wandering into whatever divergent view, affliction, or destiny circumstance happens to draw one toward, in sharp contrast to the noble ones' own shared convergence on a single, unified truth.

3. More Etymology, and the Precise Difference Between "Nature" and "Dharmas" of an Ordinary Being

Four further masters add their own gloss on why this nature is so named, before the treatise turns to explaining the companion term, "the dharmas of an ordinary being" — the specific hell, animal, ghost, and similarly unfortunate rebirths and karma that mark a being as unmistakably ordinary, a term reserved for beings alone even though noble ones could, in principle, technically satisfy the same description. The treatise then draws out an extensive, itemized list of technical distinctions between the two closely related terms — one non-material and dissociated, the other spanning both material and associated categories.

Chinese Source

大德說曰。能令有情依止異類界趣生有。發起種種顛倒煩惱。造作增長感後有業。輪轉生死無分限故。名異生性。阿毘達磨諸論師言。異生分故異生體故名異生性。尊者妙音作如是說。異生類故名異生性。脇尊者言。異生依故障聖性故。名異生性。問何故名異生法。答諸異生者有此法故。名異生法。譬如世間王法臣法。問諸異生法。聖者亦有。何故但立異生法名。答諸異生法聖者多無。設有少者不名聖法。以聖者於彼得而不在身成就不現前故。唯異生於彼得而亦在身成就亦現前故名異生法。復次異生成就彼法。能令彼法取果與果故。名異生法。聖者雖成就彼法。而不爾故不名聖法。復次異生成就彼法。能令彼法往異趣異界異處異生受異果故。名異生法。聖者雖成就彼法。而不爾故不名聖法。復次異生性是有漏。彼法亦有漏故名異生法。聖性是無漏彼法非無漏故不名聖法。復次異生為彼所覆蔽故。所纏縛故。所誑惑故。名異生法。聖不爾故不名聖法。復次諸異生類隨順彼法生長彼法故。名異生法。聖不爾故不名聖法。問異生性異生法。何差別。答異生性唯非色。異生法通色非色。異生性唯無見。異生法通有見無見。異生性唯無對。異生法通有對無對。異生性唯不相應。異生法通相應不相應。異生性唯無所依無所緣無行相異生法皆通二種。異生性唯不染污。無罪無異熟。異生法皆通二種。復次異生性唯無記。異生法通善不善無記。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Great Virtuous One states: it is able to cause sentient beings to rely upon divergent realms, destinies, and states of existence, giving rise to every kind of distorted affliction, creating and increasing the karma that draws forth future existence, revolving through birth and death without any bound or limit, and it is for this reason named the nature of an ordinary being. The masters of the Abhidharma state: it is on account of the ordinary being's own portion, and the ordinary being's own substance, that it is named the nature of an ordinary being. The Venerable Ghoṣa states as follows: it is on account of the ordinary being's own class that it is named the nature of an ordinary being. The Venerable Pārśva states: it is the basis of an ordinary being, and it obstructs the nature of nobility, and it is for this reason named the nature of an ordinary being. One asks: why is it named the dharmas of an ordinary being? The answer: it is because ordinary beings possess these dharmas that they are named the dharmas of an ordinary being, exactly as, in the world, one speaks of the king's own law, or the minister's own law. One asks: noble ones, too, possess these dharmas — why, then, is the name "dharmas of an ordinary being" established for them alone? The answer: noble ones, for the most part, do not possess these dharmas, and even should they possess a small portion, this would not be called a noble dharma, since a noble one, with respect to such a dharma, may have attained it without it being present within his own continuum, possessed without it currently manifesting before him; it is the ordinary being alone who, with respect to it, has both attained it and possesses it within his own continuum, both possessed and currently manifesting before him, and it is for this reason called the dharmas of an ordinary being. Further, an ordinary being's own possession of these dharmas causes these dharmas to take hold of their own fruit and to confer their own fruit, and it is for this reason called the dharmas of an ordinary being; a noble one, though he too may possess these dharmas, is not so, and they are therefore not called noble dharmas. Further, an ordinary being's own possession of these dharmas causes these dharmas to proceed toward a divergent destiny, a divergent realm, a divergent place, and a divergent birth, undergoing a divergent fruit, and it is for this reason called the dharmas of an ordinary being; a noble one, though he too may possess these dharmas, is not so, and they are therefore not called noble dharmas. Further, the nature of an ordinary being is itself contaminated, and these dharmas, too, are contaminated, and it is for this reason called the dharmas of an ordinary being; the nature of nobility is itself uncontaminated, and these dharmas are not uncontaminated, and it is for this reason not called noble dharmas. Further, it is because the ordinary being is covered over by these dharmas, bound by them, and deceived and confused by them, that they are called the dharmas of an ordinary being; the noble one is not so, and they are therefore not called noble dharmas. Further, every class of ordinary being accords with these dharmas and cultivates their growth, and it is for this reason called the dharmas of an ordinary being; the noble one is not so, and they are therefore not called noble dharmas. One asks: what distinction is there between the nature of an ordinary being and the dharmas of an ordinary being? The answer: the nature of an ordinary being is solely non-material; the dharmas of an ordinary being span both material and non-material. The nature of an ordinary being is solely invisible; the dharmas of an ordinary being span both visible and invisible. The nature of an ordinary being is solely non-resistant; the dharmas of an ordinary being span both resistant and non-resistant. The nature of an ordinary being is solely dissociated; the dharmas of an ordinary being span both associated and dissociated. The nature of an ordinary being possesses solely no basis, no object, and no aspect; the dharmas of an ordinary being span both possessing and lacking these. The nature of an ordinary being is solely undefiled, blameless, and lacking any ripening; the dharmas of an ordinary being span both possessing and lacking these qualities. Further, the nature of an ordinary being is solely undefined; the dharmas of an ordinary being span the wholesome, the unwholesome, and the undefined.

異生性通三界繫。異生法唯欲色界繫。異生性唯修所斷。異生法通見修所斷。復次異生性是因。異生法是果。如因果能作所作亦爾。

The nature of an ordinary being is bound to all three realms; the dharmas of an ordinary being are bound solely to the desire and form realms. The nature of an ordinary being is solely abandoned by cultivation; the dharmas of an ordinary being span both what is abandoned by seeing and by cultivation. Further, the nature of an ordinary being is the cause; the dharmas of an ordinary being are the fruit, exactly as cause and effect, and agent and object, likewise relate to one another.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Explaining why noble ones never earn the label "dharmas of an ordinary being," even on the rare occasion they might technically possess such a dharma, by noting they possess it without it currently manifesting or bearing fruit, draws a genuinely careful distinction between formally satisfying a description and actually living inside it: a noble one's own relationship to an ordinary rebirth-condition is fundamentally inert in a way an actual ordinary being's is not, and the taxonomy tracks this functional difference rather than a bare technical checklist.

4. The Ground of the Ordinary Being, and a Genuine Puzzle About Attainment

A canonical scriptural phrase — "transcending the ground of the ordinary being" — receives its own etymological treatment before the treatise poses the puzzle that will occupy the bulk of this fascicle: is the nature of an ordinary being simply non-attainment of one specific noble dharma (the forbearance marking entry to the path of seeing), or non-attainment of every noble dharma whatsoever? Either answer creates an immediate difficulty — the first would perversely reclassify advanced practitioners on higher paths as ordinary beings once they move past that specific forbearance, while the second would make even the Buddha himself an ordinary being, since no single being possesses literally every noble dharma across every vehicle at once.

Chinese Source

復次異生性法界法處行蘊所攝。異生法十八界十二處五蘊所攝。復次異生性苦法智忍時捨。異生法餘時捨。如是等門是謂差別。如世尊說。隨信隨法行超異生地。未得預流果定不命終。問何故名異生地。答一切聖者皆名同生。此異於彼故名異生。容受異生名異生地。問若爾。聖者異異生故應名異生。答一切聖者同會真理。同見同欲故名同生。異生不爾。可厭賤故立異生名。不應為難。尊者世友作如是說。容起異見異類煩惱。容造異業。容墮異界。往異趣等。而受生故。名異生地。復次容信異師。廣說乃至求異果故。名異生地。大德說曰。異於正法及毘奈耶。而受生故。名為異生。是諸異生生長依處名異生地云何異生性。答若於聖法聖暖聖見聖忍聖欲聖慧。諸非得已非得當非得。是謂異生性。問為不得苦法智忍是異生性。為不得一切聖法是異生性耶。設爾何失。若不得苦法智忍是異生性者。道類智已生捨苦法智忍。爾時苦法智忍非得。應是異生性。是則住修道無學道者。亦應名異生。若不得一切聖法是異生性者。則應一切有情皆名異生。無聖者成就一切聖法故。謂乃至佛亦不成就二乘聖法。及自乘學法。亦應名異生。有作是說。不得苦法智忍是異生性。問若爾道類智已生捨苦法智忍。爾時苦法智忍非得。應是異生性。是則住修道無學道者亦應名異生。答苦法智忍生時。害彼非得。令於自相續永不復生故。住修道無學道者。於苦法智忍雖不成就。而不名不得。亦不名得。如眼根生時害彼非得。令於自相續永不復生。眼根滅已雖不成就。而不名不得。亦不名得此亦如是。故無前過。復次道類智已生。苦法智忍雖不成就。而成就彼等流果故。不名異生。復有說者。不得一切聖法是異生性。問若爾。則應一切有情皆名異生。無聖者成就一切聖法故。答雖無聖者具足成就一切聖法。而非異生。以彼非得雜聖得故。謂若身中聖法非得不雜得者。是異生性。聖者身中聖法非得雜聖得故非異生性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the nature of an ordinary being is subsumed within the dharma-realm, the dharma-sense-field, and the aggregate of formations; the dharmas of an ordinary being are subsumed within the eighteen realms, twelve sense-fields, and five aggregates. Further, the nature of an ordinary being is relinquished at the moment of the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering; the dharmas of an ordinary being are relinquished at other times — such gateways as these constitute their distinction. As the Blessed One has stated: whoever practices dharma-following through faith or through the Dharma transcends the ground of the ordinary being; before attaining the fruit of stream-entry, he is certain never to undergo death in the interim. One asks: why is it named the ground of the ordinary being? The answer: every noble one alike is named "born together"; this is different from that, and it is therefore named "born differently" — the ground that accommodates the name "ordinary being" is named the ground of the ordinary being. One asks: if that is so, then, since noble ones, too, are different from ordinary beings, ought they not also to be named ordinary beings? The answer: every noble one alike gathers together in true principle, alike sees and alike aspires, and it is for this reason named "born together"; ordinary beings are not so, and, being worthy of disdain, the name "ordinary being" is established for them — this ought not to be pressed as an objection. The Venerable Vasumitra offers this explanation: it accommodates the arising of divergent views, divergent afflictions, and the creation of divergent karma, falling into divergent realms, proceeding toward divergent destinies, and so on, undergoing birth thereby, and it is for this reason named the ground of the ordinary being. Further, it accommodates faith in divergent teachers, broadly, up through the seeking of divergent fruits, and it is for this reason named the ground of the ordinary being. The Great Virtuous One states: it is on account of being different from the true Dharma and the Vinaya, and yet undergoing birth thereby, that one is named an ordinary being — this being the very basis upon which ordinary beings arise and grow is named the ground of the ordinary being. What is the nature of an ordinary being? The answer: with respect to the noble dharmas, the noble heat, the noble view, the noble forbearance, the noble aspiration, and the noble wisdom, whatever has not yet been attained, is not presently attained, and will not come to be attained — this is called the nature of an ordinary being. One asks: is it non-attainment of the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering that constitutes the nature of an ordinary being, or is it non-attainment of every noble dharma whatsoever that constitutes the nature of an ordinary being? Suppose either — what fault follows? If it is non-attainment of the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering that constitutes the nature of an ordinary being, then, once the subsequent-knowledge of the path has already arisen, the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering is relinquished, and, at that time, this forbearance is not attained — this ought then to be the nature of an ordinary being, and one abiding in the path of cultivation or the path beyond further training ought then to be named an ordinary being as well. If it is non-attainment of every noble dharma whatsoever that constitutes the nature of an ordinary being, then every sentient being whatsoever ought to be named an ordinary being, since no noble one possesses every noble dharma — even the Buddha does not possess the noble dharmas of the two lesser vehicles, nor the learner's dharmas belonging to a vehicle other than his own, and he, too, ought then to be named an ordinary being. There are those who say: it is non-attainment of the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering that constitutes the nature of an ordinary being.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even framing this question properly requires threading between two absurd extremes — either trivializing advancement into a nonsense category-flip, or collapsing the entire distinction between ordinary and noble beings altogether — shows how carefully this treatise's own definitions have to be built: a concept this foundational to the whole tradition cannot survive a careless formulation, and the treatise is visibly working to find language precise enough not to break under its own weight.

5. Resolving the Puzzle: A Non-Attainment That Cannot Recur

The treatise resolves the first horn of its own dilemma with a striking image: the very moment the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering first arises, it permanently destroys the earlier non-attainment itself, exactly as the eye faculty's own arising permanently destroys the prior state of not-yet-possessing an eye — so that one who later loses this specific forbearance through further progress is never restored to the original non-attainment that once defined him as an ordinary being, any more than losing one's eyesight later in life restores the specific state of never having had eyes at all.

Chinese Source

彼得非得恒俱生故。復次彼非得有二種。一共。二不共。不共者是異生性。共者非異生性。聖者身中聖法非得。一向是共故無前失。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: if that is so, then, once the subsequent-knowledge of the path has already arisen, the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering is relinquished, and, at that time, this forbearance is not attained — ought this not then to be the nature of an ordinary being, and one abiding in the path of cultivation or the path beyond further training ought then to be named an ordinary being as well? The answer: at the very moment the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering arises, it destroys that very non-attainment, causing it never again to arise within one's own continuum — one abiding in the path of cultivation or the path beyond further training, though he no longer possesses the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering, is nonetheless not thereby said to lack its attainment, nor is he said to possess it, exactly as, when the eye faculty first arises, it destroys the prior non-attainment, causing it never again to arise within one's own continuum — once the eye faculty has ceased, though one no longer possesses it, one is nonetheless not thereby said to lack its attainment, nor is one said to possess it; this, too, is exactly the same, and it is for this reason that no such fault as stated above follows. Further, once the subsequent-knowledge of the path has already arisen, though the forbearance for the dharma-knowledge of suffering is no longer possessed, one nonetheless possesses its equal-outflow fruit, and one is therefore not thereby named an ordinary being. There are yet others who say: it is non-attainment of every noble dharma whatsoever that constitutes the nature of an ordinary being.
