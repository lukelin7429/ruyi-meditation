The Buddha's Final Nirvāṇa

Fascicle 191 continues the Mindfulness-Foundations Nyāsa (念住納息第一之五) with an extended narrative treatment of the Buddha's own final days at Kuśinagara: why Śāriputra and Mahāmaudgalyāyana, and later Subhadra, entered final nirvāṇa before him; the unshakeable, tranquil concentration and the reverse-superseding ascent through every dhyāna, liberation, and attainment; why he chose Kuśinagara, lay with his head to the north on his right side like a lion king, and died at midnight; the mallas' unusual ordination; the exception granted to his own Śākya kinsmen; his instruction to "behold and examine" him; and his very last teaching. This page carries Ru‑Yi's complete translation.

A narrative highlight: unlike most fascicles of this treatise, which proceed by doctrinal question and answer, fascicle 191 unfolds as sustained narrative — among the most vivid and emotionally direct passages in the entire two-hundred-fascicle work, anchored throughout in the historical events of the Buddha's own final hours.

Fascicle 191 continues the Mindfulness-Foundations Nyāsa (念住納息第一之五) inside the Views Aggregate (見蘊第八), directly following fascicle 190's classification of mind-states and the terminal feeling at death. Here the treatise pauses its usual combinatorial analysis to work through, at length, the events surrounding the Buddha's own passing, treating each narrative detail — which direction he faced, which side he lay on, which watch of the night he chose — as worthy of the same careful, multi-angled explanation given to any point of doctrine. Terms established across fascicles 93–190 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Why an Arhat's Final Mind Is Neutral

Opening a stretch of the treatise devoted to the Buddha's own final days, the text first settles why an arhat's mind at the moment of final nirvāṇa is neutral rather than wholesome — through seven explanations culminating in the image of successive friends escorting a traveler only so far, the neutral mind alone walking all the way to the border — before asking what an arhat's very last moment of mind actually takes as its object.

Chinese Source

阿羅漢般涅槃心當言善耶無記耶。答當言無記。問何故作此論。答欲令疑者得決定故。謂阿羅漢已斷不善法成就善法。或有便疑。若爾阿羅漢應住善心而般涅槃。欲令此疑得決定故。明阿羅漢雖斷不善法成就善法。然住無記心而般涅槃非善。若先不作此論者則乃至于今若作是問。阿羅漢般涅槃心當言善耶無記耶。彼不解故。或作是答。是善非無記。以阿羅漢已斷一切不善法成就善法故。由先作此論故。乃至于今皆得正解。由是因緣故造斯論。問何故阿羅漢唯住無記心而般涅槃。答唯無記心順心斷故。謂善心強盛堅住難壞能令餘心長時續起。於心斷不順。無記心羸劣如朽敗種不堅住易壞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should the mind at an arhat's final nirvāṇa be said to be wholesome, or neutral? Answer: it should be said to be neutral. Question: why is this treatise composed? Answer: it is in order to settle a lingering doubt definitively — that is, an arhat has already severed the unwholesome dharmas and possesses the wholesome dharmas, and a doubt might therefore arise as follows: if so, then an arhat ought to abide in a wholesome mind at his final nirvāṇa. It is in order to settle this doubt definitively, clarifying that, although an arhat has severed the unwholesome dharmas and possesses the wholesome dharmas, he nevertheless abides in a neutral mind, not a wholesome one, at his final nirvāṇa, that this treatise is composed. Had this treatise not been composed earlier, then, even up to the present day, were the question asked, "should the mind at an arhat's final nirvāṇa be said to be wholesome, or neutral?" people, failing to understand, might answer, "it is wholesome, not neutral, since an arhat has already severed every unwholesome dharma and possesses the wholesome dharmas." It is because this treatise was composed earlier that, even up to the present day, this is everywhere correctly understood — and it is for this reason that this treatise is composed. Question: why does an arhat abide only in a neutral mind at his final nirvāṇa? Answer: only a neutral mind accords with the severing of mind — that is, a wholesome mind, being powerful, firm, stable, and difficult to destroy, is able to cause the subsequent continuation of mind for a long span of time, and hence does not accord with the severing of mind; a neutral mind, being feeble, like a rotten seed, unstable and easily destroyed,

不能令餘心長時續起故。於心斷最為隨順。有說。以無記心起過患少故。謂善不善心由二門於生死中起多過患。一由異熟果門。二由等流果門。無記心但由等流果門起過患非異熟果。有說。以阿羅漢背一切生故。謂餘有情將命終時為當生故極作意力。令善心起。勿我當墮諸非愛趣。阿羅漢背一切生故不復作意。但住無記心而般涅槃。有說。以阿羅漢不求趣異熟器故。謂餘有情求趣當來異熟器故將命終時以極加行令善心起。阿羅漢不求趣如是異熟器故但住無記心而般涅槃。有說。阿羅漢住自性心入於涅槃。自性心者則無記心。以生生中未嘗無故。或有一眾同分中無善心起。謂已斷善根未相續者。或有一眾同分中無不善心起。謂已離欲界染者。無有一眾同分中不起無記心者。是以說無記心名自性心。唯住此心入於涅槃。有說。阿羅漢要住如上親友心而涅槃故。如人欲適他土親友追送其下親友至門而返。中至村界上至國境。如是阿羅漢趣涅槃時不善染污心如下親友。於離欲界及非想非非想處染時即便捨離。善心如中親友。於起無記心時而便捨離。無記心如上親友。於般涅槃時乃便捨離。有說。此是阿羅漢漸捨生死法故。謂離欲界染時捨一切不善心。離非想非非想處染時捨一切染污心。起無記心時捨一切善心。入無餘依涅槃時捨一切無記心。尊者妙音說曰。一切善心皆是作功用起將命終時不能復作功用。是故唯住無記心而般涅槃。

is unable to cause the subsequent continuation of mind for a long span of time, and hence accords most fully with the severing of mind. Some say: it is because a neutral mind gives rise to fewer faults — that is, a wholesome or unwholesome mind gives rise to many faults within birth-and-death through two gateways: first, through the gateway of the retributive fruit; and second, through the gateway of the outflow fruit; a neutral mind gives rise to faults only through the gateway of the outflow fruit, not through the retributive fruit. Some say: it is because an arhat has turned his back on every future birth — that is, every other sentient being, when about to die, exerts the utmost force of mental application for the sake of a future birth, causing a wholesome mind to arise, lest he fall into some undesirable destiny; an arhat, having turned his back on every future birth, no longer engages in such mental application, and merely abides in a neutral mind at his final nirvāṇa. Some say: it is because an arhat does not seek to proceed toward any vessel of future retribution — that is, every other sentient being, seeking to proceed toward a vessel of future retribution, exerts the utmost effortful practice when about to die, causing a wholesome mind to arise; an arhat, not seeking to proceed toward any such vessel of retribution, merely abides in a neutral mind at his final nirvāṇa. Some say: an arhat abides in the mind of his own intrinsic nature upon entering nirvāṇa — this mind of intrinsic nature being precisely the neutral mind, since it is never absent from any single instance of birth. There may be a given class of common existence in which no wholesome mind ever arises — namely, one whose wholesome roots have already been severed and not yet reconnected; and there may be a given class of common existence in which no unwholesome mind ever arises — namely, one already free of the defilement of the desire realm; but there is no class of common existence at all in which a neutral mind never arises — and it is for this reason that the neutral mind is named the mind of intrinsic nature, and it is in this mind alone that one enters nirvāṇa. Some say: this is because an arhat must abide in each of these minds, in turn, as if in the company of a friend of successively higher rank, before entering nirvāṇa — just as a man, wishing to travel to another land, is escorted by his friends: the lesser friends accompany him to the gate and turn back; the middling friends accompany him as far as the village boundary; and the higher friends accompany him as far as the border of the country. It is the same with an arhat proceeding toward nirvāṇa: the unwholesome, defiled mind is like the lesser friend, taking his leave upon becoming free of the defilement of the desire realm and of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception; the wholesome mind is like the middling friend, taking its leave when the neutral mind arises; and the neutral mind is like the higher friend, taking its leave only at the moment of final nirvāṇa itself. Some say: this is because the arhat gradually relinquishes the dharmas of birth-and-death — that is, upon becoming free of the defilement of the desire realm, he relinquishes every unwholesome mind; upon becoming free of the defilement of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, he relinquishes every defiled mind; upon the neutral mind arising, he relinquishes every wholesome mind; and upon entering nirvāṇa without remainder, he relinquishes every neutral mind. The Venerable Ghoṣa said: every wholesome mind arises only through deliberate effort, and, when about to die, one is no longer able to exert such deliberate effort — and it is for this reason that one abides only in a neutral mind at one's final nirvāṇa.

問阿羅漢最後心為何所緣耶。有說。緣自身中諸根大種。有說。緣內六處。有說。緣外六處。有說。緣十二處。尊者說曰。阿羅漢最後心為何所緣。答緣自身彼於自身作無命離命者想。空解脫門現在前而般涅槃。有說。彼心緣一切行以於諸行深見過失。無願解脫門現在前而般涅槃。有說。彼心緣涅槃以於涅槃觀寂靜功德。無相解脫門現在前而般涅槃。應知此中依阿羅漢相續命終心說。非剎那最後心以彼心唯無記故。大德說曰。阿羅漢最後心緣所見聞覺知境。

Question: what does an arhat's very last mind take as its object? Some say: it takes as its object the faculties and great elements within his own body. Some say: it takes as its object the six internal spheres. Some say: it takes as its object the six external spheres. Some say: it takes as its object all twelve spheres. The Venerable one said: what does an arhat's very last mind take as its object? Answer: it takes his own body as its object, forming, with respect to his own body, the notion that it is without a life-principle and devoid of any possessor of life, and it is with the liberation-gate of emptiness manifesting before the mind that he enters final nirvāṇa. Some say: that mind takes every formation as its object, since, with respect to formations, he perceives deeply their every fault, and it is with the liberation-gate of the wishless manifesting before the mind that he enters final nirvāṇa. Some say: that mind takes nirvāṇa as its object, since, with respect to nirvāṇa, he contemplates the merit of its tranquility, and it is with the liberation-gate of the signless manifesting before the mind that he enters final nirvāṇa. It should be understood that what is spoken of here is with reference to the continuum of the arhat's own dying mind, not the single very last moment of mind, since that final moment of mind is exclusively neutral. The Bhadanta said: an arhat's very last mind takes as its object whatever realm is seen, heard, sensed, or cognized,

以彼心是異熟生自體所攝。由先業行盡故自然斷滅。如陶家輪勢極則止。

since that mind is subsumed under the retribution-born intrinsic being, and, once the force of his prior karmic action is exhausted, it naturally ceases and is extinguished — just as a potter's wheel, once its momentum is spent, comes to rest of itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The friendship simile — the unwholesome mind as the lesser friend turning back at the gate, the wholesome mind as the middling friend accompanying to the village boundary, and the neutral mind alone as the higher friend who walks all the way to the country's edge — quietly overturns the ordinary hierarchy of virtue: at the very threshold of nirvāṇa, it is the neutral, not the wholesome, that proves the more faithful companion, because only what asks nothing of the future can walk that far.

2. Why the Twin Sage-Disciples Died Before the Buddha

Śāriputra and Mahāmaudgalyāyana, the Buddha's two chief disciples, entered final nirvāṇa before their teacher — the treatise traces this to karma cultivated over countless past lives, then to natural law itself, and finally to a cascade of reasons touching on the psychology of grief, culminating in the celebrated simile of lightning flashing before thunder crashes, so that sentient beings, forewarned, do not swoon at the Buddha's own eventual passing.

Chinese Source

何故雙賢弟子先般涅槃然後佛耶。答彼二尊者先長夜中造作增長感無斷業勿空無果異熟故。由二因緣彼二尊者求如是處發起此業。一以見為先故。二以聞為先故。見為先者彼二尊者過去曾見先三藐三佛陀雙賢弟子先般涅槃然後彼佛。聞為先者彼二尊者過去曾聞先三藐三佛陀雙賢弟子先般涅槃然後彼佛。既見聞已而便引起隨順彼因。諸我所行若戒若禁苦行梵行一切迴向。願我未來得作如斯善士行類。恒與大師現受法樂而無間斷。若佛先般涅槃然後雙賢弟子者。則彼所造作增長感無斷業應空無果異熟。問一切造作增長感無斷業無如佛者。若雙賢弟子先般涅槃則佛感無斷業便空無果異熟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why do the twin sage-disciples enter final nirvāṇa before the Buddha, and the Buddha only afterward? Answer: those two venerable ones, throughout the long night of time past, have accumulated and increased karma bringing an uninterrupted retribution, lest it become empty and without fruit or ripening. By two causal conditions did these two venerable ones seek out and give rise to just such karma directed toward this very outcome: first, on account of what they had previously seen; and second, on account of what they had previously heard. As for "on account of what they had previously seen": these two venerable ones had, in a past life, witnessed the twin sage-disciples of an earlier fully and perfectly awakened Buddha enter final nirvāṇa before that Buddha, and that Buddha only afterward. As for "on account of what they had previously heard": these two venerable ones had, in a past life, heard that the twin sage-disciples of an earlier fully and perfectly awakened Buddha entered final nirvāṇa before that Buddha, and that Buddha only afterward. Having seen and heard this, they thereupon drew forth a cause conforming to it, dedicating everything of their own conduct — whether moral discipline, or ascetic restraint, or austerities, or the holy life — with the aspiration: may I, in the future, come to be just such a class of virtuous being, forever receiving the bliss of the Dharma directly from the great Teacher, without interruption. Were the Buddha to enter final nirvāṇa first, and the twin sage-disciples only afterward, then the karma they had accumulated and increased, bringing an uninterrupted retribution, would become empty and without fruit or ripening. Question: no one's accumulated and increased karma, bringing an uninterrupted retribution, surpasses the Buddha's own — if the twin sage-disciples enter final nirvāṇa first, would this not mean that the Buddha's own karma bringing an uninterrupted retribution becomes empty and without fruit or ripening instead?

豈雙賢弟子感無斷業於佛為勝耶。答弟子於師有二種受用勝。一財受用。二法受用。師於弟子有一種受用勝。謂財非法。然造作增長感無斷業但為法故非為財故是以無前過失。問雙賢弟子中般若勝者復先涅槃非神通勝者。神通勝者於彼既失法受用義。云何非感無斷業空無果異熟耶。答般若勝者無如世尊。彼雖涅槃以世尊在故於法受用非空無果。復次由法爾故雙賢弟子先佛般涅槃。何謂法爾。謂法應如是不可改易不可徵詰是法爾義。此顯一切諸佛雙賢弟子法應先佛而般涅槃此理無異。有說。與轉輪王相似法故。如轉輪王欲往彼彼未至方域必令前軍勇將先導而往。如是十力法轉輪王欲往未至無餘依涅槃界。亦令如前軍勇將雙賢弟子先導而往。有說。欲令所化有情入佛法故。謂有所化有情雖近佛而住盡眾同分。不欲來詣佛所受行佛法。若見雙賢弟子般涅槃時便於生死厭怖。來詣佛所受行佛法。有說。為解所化有情愁憂心故。謂若佛先般涅槃則無有能解所化有情愁憂心者。若雙賢弟子先般涅槃則有如來能於雨四月中。

Would this, then, mean that the twin sage-disciples' own karma bringing an uninterrupted retribution surpasses the Buddha's own? Answer: a disciple's enjoyment of his teacher is of two kinds — enjoyment through material provision, and enjoyment through the Dharma. A teacher's enjoyment of his disciples is of one kind alone — namely, through material provision, not through the Dharma. Yet the karma accumulated and increased, bringing an uninterrupted retribution, is directed solely toward the Dharma, not toward material provision, and it is for this reason that no such fault as the one raised above follows. Question: among the twin sage-disciples, the one superior in wisdom entered nirvāṇa first, not the one superior in superknowledge — but the one superior in superknowledge has thereby lost the enjoyment of the Dharma from him — how, then, is this not a case of his own karma bringing an uninterrupted retribution becoming empty and without fruit or ripening? Answer: no one is superior in wisdom to the World-Honored One himself — although that disciple has entered nirvāṇa, since the World-Honored One remains, his own enjoyment of the Dharma is not thereby made empty and without fruit. Furthermore, it is on account of natural law that the twin sage-disciples enter final nirvāṇa before the Buddha. What is meant by "natural law"? It means that the Dharma must simply be so, admitting neither of alteration nor of dispute — this is the meaning of natural law. This displays that it is, without exception, the natural law of every Buddha that his twin sage-disciples must enter final nirvāṇa before him. Some say: this is on account of a law resembling that of a wheel-turning king — just as a wheel-turning king, wishing to proceed to some region not yet reached, invariably has his vanguard of valiant generals lead the way before him, so, too, does the Dharma-king of the ten powers, wishing to proceed to the realm of nirvāṇa without remainder not yet reached, likewise have his own vanguard of valiant generals — the twin sage-disciples — lead the way before him. Some say: it is in order to bring the sentient beings he converts into the Buddha's own Dharma — that is, there are sentient beings he converts who, although dwelling near the Buddha, exhaust their entire class of common existence without ever wishing to come before the Buddha to receive and practice the Buddha's own Dharma; yet, seeing the twin sage-disciples enter final nirvāṇa, they thereupon become fearful and averse toward birth-and-death, and come before the Buddha to receive and practice the Buddha's own Dharma. Some say: it is in order to resolve the grief and sorrow in the minds of the sentient beings he converts — that is, were the Buddha to enter final nirvāṇa first, there would be no one at all able to resolve the grief and sorrow in the minds of the sentient beings he converts; but, were the twin sage-disciples to enter final nirvāṇa first, then the Tathāgata would still remain, able, within the four months of the rainy season,

依彼及自說無常教解彼愁憂令修勝行。有說。欲令所化有情於佛當般涅槃預繫念住。故謂由雙賢弟子先般涅槃。所化有情便作是念。

to teach impermanence, both with reference to those disciples and in his own words, resolving their grief and sorrow and causing them to cultivate a superior practice. Some say: it is in order to cause the sentient beings he converts to fix their mindfulness in advance upon the Buddha's own eventual final nirvāṇa — that is, on account of the twin sage-disciples entering final nirvāṇa first, the sentient beings he converts thereupon reflect as follows:

佛亦不久當般涅槃。以雙賢弟子已涅槃故。如天欲雷必先掣電。若不以電為先而震雷者則令怯弱。有情聞之驚慴或復致死。是故天欲雷時愍有情故先流電耀。彼既知已虛心待之。雖聞吒雷則無驚駭。

"the Buddha, too, will soon enter final nirvāṇa, since the twin sage-disciples have already entered nirvāṇa" — just as, when the heavens are about to thunder, lightning necessarily flashes first; were the thunder to crash without the lightning coming first, timid and feeble sentient beings, hearing it, would be startled and terrified, and some might even die of it — and it is for this reason that, when the heavens are about to thunder, out of compassion for sentient beings, the lightning is made to flash and flare first, so that they, already forewarned, may await it with settled minds, and, even upon hearing the thunder crash, feel no alarm at all.

如是若佛先般涅槃者則令一類於佛慕戀渴仰有情驚怛悶絕。若雙賢弟子先涅槃者。則令彼類預起如來般涅槃想。至佛涅槃則無悶絕故。有頌言。

It is the same here: were the Buddha to enter final nirvāṇa first, this would cause a certain class of sentient being, who cherish and yearn for him, to be startled into terror and swoon away; but, were the twin sage-disciples to enter nirvāṇa first, this would cause that same class of being to form, in advance, the notion of the Tathāgata's own eventual final nirvāṇa, so that, when the Buddha does enter nirvāṇa, they no longer swoon away at all. There is a verse that says:

恒作無常想　變壞則無憂如覩電為先　聞雷不驚怖有說。為息謗故。謂有外道恒謗佛言。沙門喬答摩攝受鄔波底沙及俱履多故。夜從諮受晝為他說。若彼二人般涅槃已世尊說法不異先時則諸外道誹謗皆息。有說。為顯世尊不久住世必當般涅槃故。如世界將欲壞時。蘇迷盧山數為難陀鄔波難陀二大龍王纏遶捨去。諸天見已即知世界不久當壞。如是尊者舍利子大目揵連先般涅槃。世便知佛不久滅度由如是等種種因緣故。雙賢弟子先般涅槃然後佛滅。

"Constantly forming the notion of impermanence, one grieves not when change and decay come; just as one who has seen the lightning first feels no alarm upon hearing the thunder." Seven explanations account for why the Buddha had his deathbed prepared with its head toward the north, and further reasons explain why he lay on his right side like a lion king rather than sitting upright to die — reasons ranging from local custom and refuting superstition to forestalling future slander against feeble, dying arhats and cutting off the pride of those who rely on physical strength.

7. Why Midnight, and the Mallas' Unusual Ordination

The choice of the middle watch of the night for the Buddha's passing is traced to his lifelong preference for the middle course — born in the middle realm, the middle land, ordained at midnight, teaching a middle Dharma — before the treatise turns to the curious case of the mallas of Kuśinagara, whose monastic precepts, contrary to the ordinary rule, were received through another's declaration rather than their own.

Should any member of the Śākya clan, wearing the garb of a non-Buddhist ascetic, come seeking to leave the household life, you should ordain him at once, granting him monastic ordination and full precepts. Question: the World-Honored One, who has accomplished a great compassion extending to all, why does he grant this exception only to his own kinsmen? Answer: there were certain members of the Śākya clan who had earlier taken refuge in non-Buddhist teachers, not yet having taken refuge in the Buddha; it was in order to draw them in through skillful means that he made this statement — that is, on account of the wicked king Virūḍhaka's slaughter of the Śākya clan at the city of Kapilavastu, certain remaining Śākyans, out of fear, had taken refuge as non-Buddhist ascetics in order secretly to preserve their own lives. It was for their sake that the Buddha spoke with this meaning: "you, out of fear, took refuge as non-Buddhist ascetics and donned their robes; now that there is no longer any cause for fear, you ought surely to return and take refuge in the Buddha's own Dharma" — and it was for this reason that I have instructed my disciples to hold to this rule, ordaining them; and it was on this account that countless Śākyan non-Buddhist ascetics came to take refuge in the Buddha's own Dharma. Some say: it was in order to draw in and induce those Śākyans, overcome by excessive pride, who had not yet entered the Buddha's own Dharma, causing them to enter it — that is, there were certain Śākyans who, their minds entangled by excessive pride, exhausted their entire class of common existence without ever coming to see the Buddha; once the Tathāgata had spoken these words, he thereupon entered final nirvāṇa, and, when they later heard of it, they were bound to reflect as follows: "did not the Buddha, even at the very moment of his final nirvāṇa, still extend his compassion on my account, on the grounds that I am his own kinsman?" — and it was on this account that there was not one among them who failed to give rise to a pure and sincere mind, coming to take refuge in the Buddha's own Dharma, leaving the household life, and receiving the precepts. The Venerable Vasumitra said: it was in order to cause the Śākya clan, his own kinsmen, to accumulate and increase their own excellent wholesome roots that the Tathāgata, at the moment of his nirvāṇa, entrusted this charge to them. As a sūtra states, at that time the World-Honored One bared the upper part of his body and told the assembly of monks: you should behold me, you should examine me. Why is this? Because a Tathāgata, an Arhat, a Fully and Perfectly Awakened One, is difficult indeed to bring into the world, and difficult indeed to encounter, rarer even than the udumbara flower. Question: why did the World-Honored One bare the upper part of his body and tell the assembly of monks, "you should behold me," and so on at length? Answer: suppose there were a person who practiced calm-abiding for a full twelve years — the wholesome quality thereby generated would not equal the merit obtained by contemplating, for even a brief moment, the Buddha's own body with its major and minor marks. Here, the Buddha's own words carry this meaning: "the accumulated stock of merit I have gathered over three incalculable eons has not yet, up to this very moment, been reduced to a mere heap of ashes — you ought therefore to gaze up and contemplate it closely, seeking the firm and stable Dharma." Question: what is the difference between "you should behold me" and "you should examine me"? Answer: "you should behold me" refers to beholding by way of eye-consciousness; "you should examine me" refers to examining by way of mind-consciousness. Furthermore, "beholding me" refers to a mind without discrimination; "examining me" refers to a mind with discrimination. Furthermore, "beholding me" refers to the present; "examining me" refers to the future. Furthermore, "beholding me" refers to the born, physical body; "examining me" refers to the Dharma-body. Furthermore, "beholding" refers to beholding what has been accumulated; "examining" refers to examining what has been realized. Furthermore, "beholding" refers to beholding what is to be shunned; "examining" refers to examining what is to be delighted in. Furthermore, "beholding" refers to beholding the major and minor marks;

察我者。謂察功德。是謂觀察差別。

"examining" refers to examining the merits and virtues. This, then, is what distinguishes beholding from examining.

9. The Buddha's Final Teaching

The fascicle closes with the Buddha's very last words — "monks, be silent now; you should contemplate that all formations are dharmas of exhaustion and cessation" — and the Venerable Ghoṣa's own striking observation that, in returning at the very end to the same teaching he had given not long after his awakening, the Buddha demonstrates, in his final breath, his own accomplishment of the dharma of never forgetting.

Ru-Yi Translation

As a sūtra states: monks, be silent now — you should contemplate that all formations are dharmas of exhaustion and cessation. This is the World-Honored One's very last teaching. Question: why did the World-Honored One speak these words? Answer: the assembled monks, on account of the Buddha's approaching nirvāṇa, were consumed with grief and sorrow, weeping and wailing one after another; the Buddha, wishing to bring their grief to rest and cause them to give rise to contemplative practice, spoke these words. Here, "monks, be silent now" causes them to abide in correct mindfulness; "you should contemplate that all formations are dharmas of exhaustion and cessation" causes them to give rise to correct awareness. Furthermore, "be silent" causes them to cultivate calm-abiding; "contemplate formations" causes them to cultivate insight. Furthermore, "be silent" causes their grief and sorrow to cease; "contemplate formations" causes contemplative practice to arise. The Venerable Ghoṣa said: "monks, be silent now" is in order to bring their grief to an end; "you should contemplate that all formations are dharmas of exhaustion and cessation" displays that he himself has accomplished the dharma of never forgetting. Here, the Buddha's own words carry this meaning: "not long after I attained buddhahood, I already spoke these very words —

諸行無常　有生滅法以起盡故　彼寂為樂今復依彼說言諸行是盡滅法。豈非我成就無忘失法耶。

'all formations are impermanent, possessing the dharma of arising and ceasing; through their arising and ceasing, that very stillness is bliss' — and now, once again, in reliance upon that very same teaching, I say that all formations are dharmas of exhaustion and cessation. Is this not itself proof that I have accomplished the dharma of never forgetting?'"
