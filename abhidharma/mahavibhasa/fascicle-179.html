Subhūti Cannot Say His Own Name

Fascicle 179 continues the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之六): wish-knowledge's own preparatory practice and how it differs from recollecting past lives and reading others' minds, how it reaches the future and the formless realm, the Venerable Subhūti's non-contention practice and the five concrete behaviors by which an arhat prevents affliction in others, why an already-liberated arhat still binds himself to this discipline, Subhūti's own inability to say "I am Subhūti," why the Buddha declares his disciples foremost in pairs, and the story of how the Buddha converted Ina, the young scholar, on the very morning of his own wedding. 1. Wish-Knowledge's Preparatory Practice, and How It Differs from Recollecting Past Lives and Reading Others' Minds

Fascicle 179 continues the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之六) by returning to wish-knowledge, first asking whether it is attained through effortful practice or the abandonment of afflictions, then giving a remarkably concrete description of that effortful practice: the practitioner cycles through every dhyāna and formless attainment in ascending and descending order, again and again, until the fourth dhyāna itself has been rendered so pliant that it can be worked through its own lower, middle, and upper grades — this uppermost grade alone being the "terminal fourth dhyāna" from which wish-knowledge is finally drawn. The treatise then carefully distinguishes wish-knowledge from its two closest neighbors — the cognition of recollecting former lives and the cognition of others' minds — a comparison that keeps landing on the same six-fold measure of superiority: wish-knowledge is more resplendent, more dominant, subtler, purer, clearer, and swifter than either, even when operating on the very same object those other cognitions already know.

Answer: it takes as its effortful practice every ground together with the terminal concentration — that is, when the practitioner is about to give rise to this wish-knowledge, he first gives rise to a wholesome mind of the desire realm; next, immediately thereafter, he enters the first dhyāna; next he enters the second dhyāna; and so on in succession up to entering the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. From this, in succession, wish-knowledge is drawn forth. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image of a meditator ascending and descending through all nine levels of absorption over and over, purely as calisthenics to prepare a single concentration for a single further purpose, reveals how this tradition understood meditative mastery: not as reaching some peak and stopping, but as first rendering the entire ladder so frictionlessly traversable that a single rung near the top can then be worked with a precision no ordinary attainment of that rung could support.

2. How Wish-Knowledge Reaches the Future, the Formless Realm, and Every Mind

A genuinely pointed epistemological puzzle follows: since the future has not yet occurred and the formless realm has no perceptible form, how can wish-knowledge know either directly, rather than merely inferring them the way a farmer infers a future harvest from a planted seed? The treatise insists, against this inferential model, that wish-knowledge is direct perception, not inference — it simply knows its effect without needing to trace back to its cause, and knows its cause without needing to trace forward to its effect. The section closes by asking how the Buddha's own wish-knowledge can grasp the distinguishing mental characteristics of literally every sentient being at once, canvassing several proposed answers before settling on the plainest: he already attained this entire class of cognition, in full, at the very moment of his awakening, and now simply knows whatever he wishes to know the instant he turns his mind to it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Insisting that wish-knowledge is direct perception rather than inference, even when its object is literally the future or the formless, is a bold epistemological claim: the treatise is willing to expand what counts as "directly perceived" well beyond ordinary sense-experience, so long as the cognition in question does not have to reconstruct its object piece by inferential piece — a definition of directness keyed to the psychological structure of the knowing, not to the ordinary means by which knowledge usually reaches us.

3. Is Wish-Knowledge Wholesome or Neutral?

A cascade of rival head-counts for wish-knowledge's own moral quality — one kind, two, three, five, six, seven, or ten — is resolved by treating the six-kind account as authoritative, then explaining away the apparent inconsistency of calling something "neutral" that exists, strictly speaking, only within the wholly wholesome fourth dhyāna: what is actually neutral is not wish-knowledge itself but the subsequent cognition, in the desire realm, that goes on to articulate in words what wish-knowledge has already silently known, and this expository afterthought borrows the name "wish-knowledge" by association.

4. What Is Non-Contention Practice?

The treatise turns to Subhūti's famous epithet — foremost in non-contention practice — and works through what the term could mean: every arhat has already mastered the "inner time" of his own afflictions, but non-contention specifically names mastery of the "outer time," the afflictions of others, whether measured across three daily periods or six. The Venerable Ghoṣaka reframes the whole question sharply: non-contention was never about the practitioner's own inner peace at all, since "contention" is a word that only ever applies to how one stands toward others. Multiple candidate glosses for the term itself follow — remaining unstained by others' afflictions, restraining others' afflictions without remainder, and restraining them without favoritism toward friend, enemy, or stranger alike.

5. The Arhat's Five Practices to Prevent Others' Afflictions

The fascicle catalogues, in vivid procedural detail, the five concrete behaviors by which an arhat actually accomplishes non-contention: constantly monitoring and adjusting his own bodily deportment, choosing to speak or fall silent based on which would provoke affliction, judging moment to moment whether to stay in a place or leave it, discerning whether to accept or decline an offered gift, and, before entering any household to beg for alms, silently checking whether his presence might trouble anyone within. Each rule is stated with its own built-in exception-handling — if speaking would provoke one person's affliction but silence would provoke another's, he falls silent toward both; if neither speaking nor silence would help, he simply avoids the encounter altogether — closing with the arhat's own extreme resolve: rather than let any being's affliction arise on his account, he would rather withdraw to an empty place and die there of self-imposed starvation.

6. Why an Already-Liberated Arhat Still Binds Himself

A natural objection follows: why would an arhat, already free, take on this elaborate self-imposed restraint at all? The answer reaches back to his bodhisattva-like disposition — a felt debt to all the beings he was once bound together with through countless lives of shared suffering, sharpened by the memory of his own past lives as an actor or a prostitute, when hundreds of thousands of beings' affliction toward him cost them dearly through the long night of saṃsāra; now that he causes no such cost even when affliction arises toward him, he still refuses to be its occasion at all. The section then draws a precise technical limit on the practice itself: only particular-characteristic afflictions in another's mind can actually be restrained by this discipline, never the general-characteristic afflictions — such as the innate ignorance running through every sentient being without exception — which arise spontaneously and admit of no outside intervention whatsoever.

9. Does the Buddha Himself Abide in Non-Contention?

A pointed objection asks whether the Buddha and the śrāvaka who has reached the ultimate abide in non-contention at all — for if they do, why do hundreds of thousands of beings still give rise to affliction toward them, when non-contention is supposed to prevent exactly that? The resolution turns on wish-knowledge itself: the Buddha first observes whether a given being can be converted without any affliction arising at all, and only when this is impossible, yet the being can still be led to plant a wholesome root by means of that very affliction, does the Buddha deliberately allow it to arise — reasoning that even a hair's-breadth of resulting wholesomeness can crush a mountain of affliction in the long run. The alternative view holds that the Buddha does abide in non-contention but simply does not dwell there continuously, since converting beings of unequal disposition sometimes calls for consolation, sometimes for reproach, and reproach can provoke exactly the reaction non-contention would otherwise prevent.

10. Non-Contention's Own Effortful Practice, and Subhūti's Ancient Vow

Non-contention, like wish-knowledge before it, is shown to be attained either through effortful practice or through the abandonment of afflictions, following the identical Buddha/pratyekabuddha/śrāvaka gradient and the identical terminal-concentration preparatory practice. The fascicle then answers a lingering doubt: since non-contention cannot itself sever affliction, why does the sūtra credit Subhūti's non-contention practice with his own realization? Furthermore, the World-Honored One wishes to put an end to the disdain of those who, seeing certain monks of haggard appearance, come to look down upon them, by displaying that these very monks all possess surpassing virtues. The masters of the Abhidharma say: it is in accordance with the Dharma of all Buddhas, since, throughout as many world-systems as there are grains of sand in the River Gaṅgā, every Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Completely Awakened One who has appeared in the world has declared the virtues of the many foremost pairs among his own assembly of disciples, and the present Buddha does likewise. Furthermore, he wishes to cause those in the world who each delight differently in different virtues to rejoice upon hearing of them, giving rise to a reverent mind toward the Buddha's true Dharma, planting wholesome roots, and obtaining great benefit thereby. Furthermore, it is in order to adorn the assemblies of future Buddhas — that is, once the Buddha has declared his disciples, countless sentient beings, whether seeing or hearing of this, all give rise to rejoicing and set forth a proper aspiration, dedicating whatever they cultivate — giving, moral discipline, extensive learning, right diligence, pure conduct — toward foremost virtue, vowing: "may I, in a future life, within a Buddha's Dharma, be numbered among such noble ones as these" — and this thereby adorns that future Buddha's own assembly of disciples. Furthermore, it is in order to let the disciples thus declared rejoice that their own aspiration has been fulfilled — that is, Ina and the other five hundred monks had, in the distant past, before five hundred Buddhas, whether seeing or hearing those Buddhas declare the virtues of their disciples, rejoiced and set forth the vow: "may whatever giving, moral discipline, extensive learning, right diligence, and pure conduct I possess cause me, in a future life, within a Buddha's true Dharma, to be numbered among such great disciples as these" — and now, this vow having been fulfilled, upon hearing the Buddha's own declaration, they rejoiced and leapt for joy, deeply congratulating themselves; and it is for this reason that it is said,

一一如來大弟子眾皆於過去五百佛所大誓莊嚴方得成就。由如是等種種因緣佛記弟子雙雙第一。

each and every great assembly of a Tathāgata's disciples attains its own accomplishment only by means of a great vow adorned before five hundred Buddhas in the past. It is on account of such various causal conditions as these that the Buddha declares his disciples foremost in pairs.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Piling up more than a dozen distinct rationales for a single practice — publicly ranking disciples — rather than settling on the one "real" reason is itself characteristic of this treatise's method: it treats a scriptural custom as overdetermined, worth explaining from every angle at once, since no single motive need exclude the others from also being true.

12. Ina, the Young Scholar, and the Wedding-Day Conversion

The fascicle closes with its most vivid narrative: distinguishing Ina, foremost in intelligence, from Bāhika, foremost in quickness, the treatise tells how the Buddha converted Ina on the very morning of his own wedding, arriving at his gate to beg alms only to be jeered away by the tīrthikas gathered for the sacrificial rite — until the Buddha, disguised as a brahmin descending from the sun itself, wins Ina's reverent homage, then, in his own borrowed form, presses him with an escalating series of questions: what did the wedding cost, and was some of that price spent simply to "buy" a bride? Ina's own straightforward honesty carries him, item by item, through appraising his bride-to-be's hair, her nails, and finally all thirty-six impure constituents of her body as worth nothing at all — at which point his own long-dormant wholesome roots, cultivated ten thousand years earlier under the Buddha Kāśyapa, awaken, his desire subsides, and, upon hearing the four noble truths from the Buddha's own restored form, he attains the fruit of non-return on the very morning he meant to marry.

Question: as it is said, "among my disciples, Ina, the young scholar, is foremost in intelligence," while "Bāhika and others are foremost in quickness" — what difference is there between these two? Answer: the Venerable Ina excels in a straightforward mind, a mind without crookedness, and a mind of simple sincerity, whereas the Venerable Bāhika and others excel in a soft mind, a pliant mind, and a harmonious mind — and it is on account of these that both alike are named foremost. Here, intelligence is itself quickness, and quickness is itself intelligence; yet, since the Buddha named each of them foremost separately, their distinguishing characteristics must be asked after. As for the Venerable Ina excelling in a straightforward mind, a mind without crookedness, and a mind of simple sincerity —

直無曲淳質名異義一如其次第以後釋前。或心直者是總句。心無曲者別。顯心無諂心淳質者別顯心無誑。如黠慧馬善識人意。雖或驚觸無損於人。尊者因儒童亦復如是由黠慧故善知佛意。於詰問時隨順正答。其事云何如契經說。彼在家時豐饒財寶親屬豪貴。常事日天彼以盛年時當娉婦。廣請外道婆羅門眾於自宅中大設祠祀。佛於化事終不失時。是日晨朝為化彼故著衣持鉢命阿難陀。汝可隨我入城乞食。命已便入室羅筏城至因儒童宅門而住。外道梵志遙見叱言。今日此家設吉祥會此不祥物何用來為。佛聞便告阿難陀曰。汝往語彼諸外道等。三界大師吉祥中最。汝不欲見吉事。豈成此因儒童定於今日棄捨汝等投我出家。盡汝技術能留難不。時阿難陀受佛教勅如師子王無所畏難往如群鹿外道眾中告言。如來善達因果所言誠諦語汝等曰。三界大師吉祥中最。汝不欲見吉事。豈成此因儒童定於今日棄捨汝等投我出家。盡汝伎術能留難不。外道聞已相視笑言。沙門喬答摩隨情詭說。何有將臨祀會延屈我等內外慶集而歸汝出家耶。奇哉沙門如是妄語。時有婆羅門名為五頂。曾見世尊記事不謬告外道眾曰。此因儒童定當出家莫生異念。外道咸曰。設有斯事我等必能為作留難。即共相率彈指拊掌。遶因儒童室七重而住。

"straightforward," "without crookedness," and "simple sincerity" differ in name but are one in meaning, each in turn explaining the one before it — or, "straightforward mind" may be taken as the general term, "without crookedness" as a particular term displaying that the mind is without flattery, and "simple sincerity" as a particular term displaying that the mind is without deceit. It is like a keen, intelligent horse that reads its rider's intention well — even should it startle or shy, it causes its rider no harm; the Venerable Ina was just the same, for, by his own intelligence, he read the Buddha's intention well, and, when questioned, answered accordingly and correctly. How did this come about? As the sūtra relates: while still a householder, he was rich in wealth and treasure, of noble and prominent family, and constantly worshipped the sun-deity. When he reached the prime of his youth, he was to take a bride, and broadly invited an assembly of tīrthikas and brahmins to hold a great sacrificial rite at his own house. Now, the Buddha never fails to seize the right moment for converting beings; and so, on the very morning of that day, in order to convert him, he donned his robe, took up his bowl, and bid Ānanda: "you may accompany me into the city to beg for alms." Having so bid him, he entered the city of Śrāvastī and came to stand at the gate of Ina's house. The tīrthika ascetics, seeing him from afar, cried out in rebuke: "today this house holds an auspicious rite — what use is there for this inauspicious creature to come here?" The Buddha, hearing this, told Ānanda: "go and tell those outsiders: the great teacher of the three realms is himself the most auspicious of all — do you not wish to witness an auspicious event? Will not this very Ina, this very day, abandon you all and go forth under me? Use every one of your own arts and see whether you can hinder it." Ānanda, having received the Buddha's instruction, went forth as fearlessly as a lion-king among a herd of deer, into the midst of that assembly of tīrthikas, and proclaimed: "the Tathāgata, who thoroughly comprehends cause and effect, speaks to you in perfect truth, saying: the great teacher of the three realms is himself the most auspicious of all — do you not wish to witness an auspicious event? Will not this very Ina, this very day, abandon you all and go forth under me? Use every one of your own arts and see whether you can hinder it." The tīrthikas, upon hearing this, looked at one another and laughed, saying: "the ascetic Gautama speaks however suits his own fancy — how could it be that, on the very eve of a sacrificial rite, with all our guests, near and far, gathered here in celebration, he should be handed over to that ascetic to go forth? At that time there was a brahmin named Pañcaśikha, who had once seen that the World-Honored One's predictions never erred, and told the assembly of tīrthikas: "this Ina will surely go forth — do not think otherwise." Ina replied: "this is not worth much — were an entertainer or a prostitute to buy it, it would be worth but a single kārṣāpaṇa, or perhaps only half of one." The Buddha further asked how much a single fingernail of hers was worth. Ina replied: "this is worth nothing at all." The Buddha then, one by one, in succession, raised each of the thirty-six impure constituents of her body and asked its worth, and Ina, one by one, answered each in turn: "this, too, is worth nothing at all." Now, Ina had, in a past life, within the Dharma of the Buddha Kāśyapa, spent ten thousand years cultivating the preparatory contemplation on the elements; and, because the Buddha had specifically questioned him about these impure constituents, the wholesome roots he had cultivated in that past life now manifested before him, and he subdued his own craving desire, becoming free of the defilement of the desire realm. The World-Honored One then resumed his original form, and taught Ina the four noble truths; upon hearing them, he at once realized the fruit of non-return. The Buddha then took Ina's hand and rose with him into the sky, speaking a verse, saying:

"Though adorned in the utmost splendor, if one practices the Dharma, calming and taming the breath, devoted to cultivating pure conduct, and relinquishing every worldly entanglement — this indeed is a true renunciant, a pure-willed ascetic monk."

It is by this very exchange that his mind is known to have been simply sincere; for, had it not been so, when the conjured man questioned him, he would instead have answered: "you are a brahmin — what business have you knowing of my taking a bride? It is by the custom of marriage, so how can you call it 'buying'?" Yet, owing to his simple sincerity, he answered truthfully whatever he was asked, and thereby his wholesome roots ripened to maturity and he attained the fruit of non-return. It is for this reason that the World-Honored One praised Ina, the young scholar, as foremost in intelligence.
