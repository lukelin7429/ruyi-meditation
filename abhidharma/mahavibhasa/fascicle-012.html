Two Seal-Carvers Who Never Spoke

Fascicle 12 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa's fourth part (雜蘊第一中智納息第二之四), first completing the treatise's own theory of memory without a persisting self, then opening an entirely new question rooted in one of the most famous discourses on death and offerings: why does food offered for the deceased reach hungry ghosts specifically, and no other destiny? This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation — the seal-carvers and telepaths who recognize each other's work without ever comparing notes, the brahmin who tests whether his dog is his reincarnated father, the wolf who explains a five-hundred-life grudge with chilling precision, and two rival accounts of exactly how a grieving family's offering finally reaches a hungry ghost.

1. The Treatise's Own Theory of Memory: Two Seal-Makers Who Never Spoke

Fascicle 12 opens with the treatise's own positive answer to the memory puzzle Fascicle 11 left open: sentient beings, through sheer habituation, attain a “homogeneous cognition” that lets them know what they previously experienced — no persisting self required. Two vivid analogies carry the weight: seal-carvers who recognize each other's handwriting despite never having compared notes, and telepaths who read each other's minds despite never having discussed how.

Ru-Yi Translation

[The topic continues:] "Given that the person cannot be found, and there is no principle whereby an earlier mind passes on to a later mind, how, then, can one remember what was previously done?" The answer: sentient beings, through the force of habituation with respect to dharmas, attain a homogeneous cognition of this kind, and are thereby able to know, in this way, whatever they have previously experienced. One asks: the foregoing passage stated that there is no person at all — why, then, does this answer now speak of "sentient beings" with respect to dharmas? The answer: the foregoing statement is expressed in terms of the noble ones' own conception, while this present statement is expressed in terms of worldly conception; the foregoing statement is expressed in terms of the noble ones' own manner of speech, while this present statement is expressed in terms of worldly speech; the foregoing statement is made with reference to ultimate truth, while this present statement is made with reference to worldly convention. Some say: it is for the sake of grammatical fluency — if one spoke only of "dharmas with respect to dharmas," this would accord with the meaning but not with the grammar, whereas speaking of "sentient beings with respect to dharmas" accords with both grammar and meaning together, since, by worldly principle, sentient beings do exist. Some say: it is for the sake of producing understanding — if one spoke only of "dharmas with respect to dharmas," disciples would fail to understand who remembers what, whereas speaking of "sentient beings with respect to dharmas" lets disciples understand that it is the sentient being who remembers the dharma. As for "sentient beings, through the force of habituation with respect to dharmas, attain a homogeneous cognition of this kind": this means that a sentient being's own cognition, with respect to the dharmas it comes to know, becomes fixed through habituation, operating freely and resembling itself from earlier to later — and it is for this reason that it is called "homogeneous." As for "are thereby able to know, in this way, whatever they have previously experienced": this means that, in accordance with what was originally seen and originally experienced, one is thereby able to remember in just this way. Some say: this passage should instead be read as "are thereby able to know, in this way, whatever things there are" — meaning that, in accordance with the substance, characteristic, self-nature, entity, own-nature, and portion originally possessed by a thing, one is thereby able to remember in just this way. Some say: this passage should instead be read as "are thereby able to know, in this way, whatever abides" — meaning that, in accordance with the visible color and the shape originally abided in, one is thereby able to remember in just this way. In order to make this meaning fully clear, a worldly, visible analogy is introduced: it is just as when there are two seal-makers, each able to recognize the characters carved by the other, even though these two people have never gone to ask one another, "how did you carve this character?" nor has either ever answered, "this is how I carved this character" — yet these two people, through the force of habituation, attain a homogeneous cognition of this kind, by which each is able to recognize the characters carved by the other. Sentient beings are exactly the same, through the force of habituation — and so on, as stated at length. Here, whoever is capable of writing is called a "seal-maker." Just as these two people, although they never question or answer one another, are nonetheless able, through the force of habituation, to attain a homogeneous cognition of this kind, by which each recognizes the characters carved by the other — indeed, even a letter arriving from across the sea can still be read and understood — sentient beings are exactly the same: even though there is no real person at all, and no principle whereby an earlier mind passes on to a later mind, they are nonetheless able, through the force of habituation, to attain a homogeneous cognition of this kind, and are thereby able to know, in this way, whatever they have previously experienced. In order to make this same point doubly clear, a second analogy is introduced: it is just as when there are two people, each possessing the ability to know another's mind, who mutually know each other's minds, even though these two people have never gone to ask one another, "how do you know my mind?" nor has either ever answered, "this is how I know your mind" — yet these two people, through the force of habituation, attain a homogeneous cognition of this kind, by which they mutually know each other's minds. Sentient beings are exactly the same, through the force of habituation — and so on, as stated at length. Here, "one who knows another's mind" refers to one who has attained the supernormal power of knowing others' minds. Just as these two people, although they never question or answer one another, are nonetheless able, through the force of habituation, to attain a homogeneous cognition of this kind, by which they mutually know each other's minds — indeed, even at a distance of a hundred yojanas, they can still know each other's minds — sentient beings are exactly the same: even though there is no real person at all — and so on, as stated at length. One asks: if it is the case that a differing mind can still remember what a differing mind previously experienced, then why can it not be that what Devadatta experienced is remembered by Yajñadatta, or that what Yajñadatta experienced is remembered by Devadatta? The answer: because their respective continua differ — an earlier mind and a later mind within one and the same continuum involve no such difference, and so this objection ought not to be raised. Some say: because there is, between their two minds, no sense of a causal relation at all, whereas an earlier mind is indeed able to serve as a cause for a later mind within the same continuum — this objection ought not to be raised. Some say: because their two bodies and minds are not mutually connected, whereas an earlier and a later body and mind, being mutually connected in succession, allow what was earlier experienced to be later remembered. One asks: if it is mere connectedness of mind that allows for remembering, then why does seeing a different ox not call to mind that it is the very same ox as before? The answer: if, with respect to something previously experienced, what is now seen resembles it, then one is indeed able to remember; but if, although something was previously experienced, what is now seen does not resemble it, then one is unable to remember. What was experienced during the first portion of the day can be remembered during the latter portion of the day, and what was experienced during the latter portion of the day can be remembered during the first portion of the day, since the bodily appearance seen earlier and later resembles itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seal-carver analogy is doing something philosophically precise: recognizing a familiar hand in a carved character requires no communication between the two carvers and no shared substance underlying their skill — only a reliably similar pattern produced by reliably similar training, which is exactly the model the treatise proposes for memory itself. The follow-up question about why Devadatta's memories can't simply transfer to Yajñadatta is answered by continuity of a single mind-stream, not by any special metaphysical glue — a modest, structural answer that asks for nothing more than habituation within one ongoing causal sequence.

2. Why Mind Always Finds Its Object: The Man With a Hundred Sons

The treatise's second load-bearing claim follows: mind and mental factors are permanently fixed to their object, never drifting elsewhere, which is precisely what makes remembering a stable, non-arbitrary affair rather than a lottery. The relationship between one object and its countless simultaneous cognizers is likened to a single father's identical relationship to each of a hundred sons.

The single-father-hundred-sons image elegantly solves a problem that could otherwise seem baffling: how can countless different collections of mind and mental factors, arising at different times, all take the very same object in exactly the same way, without any of them communicating with each other? The answer is that the relation itself (father-to-son, mind-to-object) is simply repeatable without limit — the same structural relationship instantiated independently, over and over, requiring no coordination between instances at all.

3. Fixed to the Sense-Field, the Color, or the Moment? Three Rival Answers

A finer question follows: exactly how is mind fixed to its object — only by sense-field (eye-consciousness always takes form, never sound), also by particular color, or also by particular moment? Each successively stricter proposal is tested and found to generate the same fatal flaw: it would let a single act of cognition contain multiple, distinct capacities of discernment, violating the basic principle that one dharma has one substance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The refutation of the color-fixed position is a nice piece of conceptual pressure-testing: since “blue” itself covers roots, stems, branches, leaves, flowers, and fruit, fixing cognition to “blue” as a single category would collapse the cognition of a blue root into the cognition of a blue stem — precisely the kind of multiple-capacities-in-one-cognition problem the whole exercise was trying to avoid. The treatise's preferred solution — fixed only to sense-field, with the paradox of countless minds "crowding" a dharma that never arises resolved by noting these dharmas already possess their own place from the very outset — shows a recurring pattern in this fascicle: apparent metaphysical crowding problems dissolve once one stops imagining conditioned dharmas as needing to compete for scarce space the way physical objects do.

The reconciliation offered here — that the Prakaraṇapāda's own wording displays the noble path's own useful function rather than making a claim about what it lacks — is a good specimen of this treatise's preferred harmonizing move: rather than treating a citation's silence about something as a denial of it, silence is read as simply beside that citation's particular point, which the treatise's own more complete statement is free to supplement.

5. When Does Mind Actually Seize Its Object — Arising or Ceasing?

A genuinely counterintuitive answer emerges to a natural question: does a mental event grip its object at the moment it arises, or at the moment it ceases? Arising happens in the future, where nothing yet has any function; ceasing looks like decline and dissolution — yet the treatise insists it is precisely at the moment of ceasing, when a dharma is already dispersing, that it actually performs the work of taking hold of its object.
