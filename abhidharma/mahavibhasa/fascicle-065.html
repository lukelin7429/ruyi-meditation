Why Exactly Four Fruits

Fascicle 65 completes the fruit-containment ledger across sixteen chapters of affliction and the ninety-eight latent tendencies, then turns to a genuinely foundational question: why does the tradition speak of exactly four fruits of a śramaṇa? The Vibhājyavādin claim that these fruits are purely unconditioned is refuted, each fruit's own conditioned and unconditioned nature detailed in full, and reason after independent reason offered for why the count is fixed at four — a farmer's own harvest-joy, a village theft with witnesses, the ninety-eight latent tendencies themselves — before closing on why the desire realm alone, among the three realms, requires two full fruits to finally escape. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Completing the Sixteen Chapters, Part One

The treatise extends its fruit-containment ledger across sixteen further traditional categories of affliction, showing that the view-of-self fetter, clinging to rules and vows, and doubt each follow exactly the pattern already established for the three fetters — since these same afflictions recur, under different names, across the five lower-fetters, the five views, the four floods and yokes, the four graspings, the four bodily ties, the seven latent tendencies, and the nine fetters, each sharing the same own-nature and the same counteraction, and therefore the identical containment behavior.

The recurring formula "since these share the same own-nature and the same counteraction" is doing enormous compressive work throughout this section: rather than re-deriving the containment pattern from scratch for each of sixteen different traditional lists, the treatise simply identifies which items across these lists are, at bottom, the very same affliction wearing a different name, letting one worked example stand in for an entire family of categories at once.

2. Completing the Sixteen Chapters, Part Two

The pattern continues through the three unwholesome roots, the contaminants, the doubt-hindrance, and the upper-fetter of craving for form, each mapped onto its own counterparts across the remaining traditional categories — hatred and craving recurring under the floods, graspings, bodily ties, hindrances, fetters, craving-bodies, and latent tendencies — with one genuine exception carefully flagged: the craving-bodies born of eye-, ear-, and body-contact require their own distinct "belonging to no fruit" clause, referencing the Brahmā-world specifically rather than the form realm as a whole.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The single flagged exception here is worth noticing precisely because it is so rare in this otherwise uniform section: the treatise's default move is always "understand this likewise," and it is only when a genuine structural difference exists — here, a narrower realm-reference than the pattern would otherwise predict — that it pauses to spell out the difference explicitly, rather than letting a false analogy pass silently.

3. The Ninety-Eight Latent Tendencies

The treatise reaches the most granular level of its taxonomy, the ninety-eight latent tendencies themselves, confirming that their own fruit-containment across all three realms and both sections — seeing and cultivation — simply mirrors, section by section and realm by realm, everything already established for the fifteen sections of fetters, with no new pattern required at this final level of resolution.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the ninety-eight latent tendencies introduce no new containment behavior whatsoever — merely restating, at maximum granularity, exactly what the fifteen-section ledger already established — is itself a kind of proof: having verified the containment principle survives being sliced by two, five, nine, fifteen, and now ninety-eight, the treatise has effectively demonstrated that fruit-containment is a genuine structural fact about the fetters themselves, invariant under every possible way of dividing them.

4. Approach and Fruit: What Each Contains

The treatise turns from individual fetter-categories to the eight persons themselves, tracking exactly what each approach and fruit comprehensively contains — the approach to stream-entry containing nothing at all, since no fruit yet exists to contain it; each successive fruit containing everything the fetters abandoned by seeing yield, together with an ever-larger portion of what is abandoned by cultivation — before confronting a genuine dilemma: does this ledger describe fetters already possessed within a given stage, or newly realized there? Both readings generate objections, resolved by distinguishing the approach-stage, which speaks generally of what is possessed, from the fruit-stage, which speaks specifically of what is newly realized.

6. Why Exactly Four Fruits? Refuting "Purely Unconditioned"

The treatise turns to an entirely new, foundational question: why does this treatise speak of exactly four fruits of a śramaṇa? 8. Fifteen Reasons for Four Fruits

The treatise offers reason after independent reason why exactly four fruits are named, rather than the innumerable finer gradations that could, in principle, be distinguished thought-moment by thought-moment: these four alone are easily seen and designated; these four alone generate a farmer's own harvest-joy; these four alone satisfy three, then five, specific conditions in full; these four alone are the practitioner's supreme place of rest; these four alone allow correct discernment of the four noble truths' own characteristics; and, at these four alone, a witness exists should the fruit ever be lost, exactly as theft within a village, unlike theft between two villages, is always eventually discovered.

10. Why the Desire Realm Alone Yields Two Fruits

The fascicle closes with a genuinely rich question: why does becoming free of the desire realm's own defilement establish two fruits, once-returning and non-returning, while becoming free of the form and formless realms' own defilement establishes only arhatship, a single fruit? A cascade of vivid reasons follows: the desire realm alone is unfixed, difficult to cross like a flood, perilous like a mountain path under a heavy load, filthy as a palace built on a dung-heap, home to both sexes and both faculties, riddled with the pain-faculty, shamelessness, envy, and stinginess — the Venerable Saṃghavasu closing with a stark catalogue of ordinary human loss: parents, siblings, spouse, wealth, limbs, and four hundred and four diseases, concluding that even granting all four fruits at once, for such a realm, would still be too few.

One asks: for what reason does becoming free of the desire realm's own defilement establish two fruits of a śramaṇa — namely, once-returning and non-returning — while becoming free of the form and formless realms' own defilement establishes only one — namely, arhatship? The Venerable Pārśva says: only the Buddha, the World-Honored One himself, is capable of fully comprehending every dharma's own nature, characteristic, function, and precise boundary — no one else is capable of knowing this; wherever becoming free of a given defilement is fit to establish two fruits, he thereupon establishes two, and wherever it is fit to establish only one, he thereupon establishes one — and so this ought not to be questioned. Further, the four fruits of a śramaṇa are, without exception, all established precisely through becoming free of the three realms' own defilement — that is, becoming free of the three realms' own defilement abandoned by seeing establishes the fruit of stream-entry; becoming free of the three realms' own defilement abandoned by seeing, together with the desire realm's own first six grades abandoned by cultivation, establishes the fruit of once-returning; becoming free of the three realms' own defilement abandoned by seeing, together with the desire realm's own defilement abandoned by cultivation entirely, establishes the fruit of non-returning; becoming free of the three realms' own defilement abandoned by both seeing and cultivation entirely establishes the fruit of arhatship. Further, since the desire realm is an unfixed realm, neither a ground of cultivation nor a ground of freedom from defilement, becoming free of its own defilement establishes two fruits of a śramaṇa; the form and formless realms are fixed realms, grounds of cultivation and of freedom from defilement, and so becoming free of their own defilement establishes only one fruit of a śramaṇa. Further, since the desire realm is difficult to cut off, difficult to break, and difficult to surpass, becoming free of its own defilement establishes two fruits of a śramaṇa; the form and formless realms are easy to cut off, easy to break, and easy to surpass, and so becoming free of their own defilement establishes only one fruit of a śramaṇa. Further, since the desire realm's own faults are abundant, firmly rooted, and numerous, becoming free of its own defilement establishes two fruits of a śramaṇa; the form and formless realms are the opposite of this, and so becoming free of their own defilement establishes only one fruit of a śramaṇa.
