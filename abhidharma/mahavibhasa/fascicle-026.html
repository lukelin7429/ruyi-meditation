Fascicle 26 opens an entirely new topic within the Person Nyāsa's fourth part (補特伽羅納息第三之四): mindfulness of breathing, worked out from first principles — does breath depend on body or on mind, why does it stop entirely in the fourth dhyāna, and how does the Buddha's own six-stage practice of counting, following, fixing, contemplating, turning, and purifying carry a practitioner from a single breath all the way to the noble path itself. The fascicle closes with the Buddha's own two-month meditative seclusion and three deities disputing whether his motionless body is alive. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. A New Topic: Does Breath Depend on Body, or on Mind?

Fascicle 26 opens an entirely new investigation within the Person Nyāsa: does in-and-out breath depend on the body, or on the mind? Scripture itself seems to pull in two directions — one sūtra calls breath “a bodily dharma, rooted in body,” while the Prajñaptiśāstra explains that a corpse stops breathing specifically because breath operates through the power of mind, and a corpse has no mind left at all. The treatise resolves the apparent tension by insisting breath depends on both, always “as appropriate,” cashed out as four necessary factors together: a body capable of supporting breath, open wind-channels, open pores, and a coarse mind of the breath-stage currently manifest — proven by the two limiting cases where any single factor is missing and breath accordingly fails to operate.

Chinese Source

入息出息當言依身轉耶。依心轉耶。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為令疑者得決定故。謂契經說。佛告長者。此入出息是身法身為本繫屬身依身而轉。施設論說。何緣死者入出息不轉耶。謂入出息由心力轉。死者無心但有身故。此入出息一說依身一說依心。或有生疑。如是二說俱不了義。或俱了義。欲顯此二真實義趣故作斯論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should in-breath and out-breath be said to depend on body, or to depend on mind? And so on, as elaborated at length. One asks: why does the treatise take up this topic? The answer: it is in order to resolve a genuine doubt — that is, scripture states: the Buddha told the elder: this in-and-out breath is a bodily dharma, rooted in body, tied to body, operating in dependence on body. Yet the Prajñaptiśāstra states: why is it that a dead person's in-and-out breath no longer operates? Because in-and-out breath operates through the power of mind, and a dead person, having no mind, possesses only a body. This in-and-out breath is thus said, in one place, to depend on body, and, in another, to depend on mind — and one might come to doubt that these two statements are both incomplete in meaning, or, alternatively, that they are both complete in meaning — and it is in order to display the true, genuine intent of both statements that this treatise is composed.

入息出息當言依身轉耶。依心轉耶。答應言亦依身轉亦依心轉如其所應。云何名為如其所應。有作是說。於下中上如其所應。謂入出息小時下品壯時中品老時上品。如是說者。由四事故名如所應。謂入出息由四事轉。一有息所依身。二風道通。三毛孔開。四入出息地麁心現前。必具此四入出息轉。由此故說如所應言。為顯此義復作是說若入出息但依身轉不依心轉。則在無想定滅盡定位入出息亦應轉。彼有入出息所依身。風道亦通毛孔亦開。唯無入出息地麁心現前以無心故雖有三事而闕一事故息不轉。若入出息但依心轉不依身轉。則無色界有情入出息亦應轉。彼界四事一切皆無故息不轉。

Should in-breath and out-breath be said to depend on body, or to depend on mind? The answer: it should be said that they depend both on body and on mind, each as appropriate. What, exactly, is meant by "as appropriate"? Some say: this refers to the lower, middling, and upper grades, each as appropriate — that is, in-and-out breath in infancy is of the lower grade, in the prime of life is of the middling grade, and in old age is of the upper grade. There are yet others who say: it is through four factors that in-and-out breath is said to be "as appropriate," since in-and-out breath operates only through the presence of all four: first, possessing a body that breath depends upon; second, the wind-channels being open; third, the pores being open; fourth, a coarse mind belonging to the breath-stage manifesting before one. In-and-out breath necessarily requires the presence of all four of these factors together in order to operate, and it is on this account that it is said to be "as appropriate." In order to display this very meaning, it is further stated: if in-and-out breath depended only on body, and not at all on mind, then, in the state of the concentration of non-perception or the concentration of cessation, breath ought likewise to operate, since one there does possess a body that breath depends upon, and the wind-channels are indeed open, and the pores are indeed open — it is only that no coarse mind belonging to the breath-stage manifests before one, since one there is without mind altogether; though three of the four factors are present, one is lacking, and it is for this reason that breath does not operate. If in-and-out breath depended only on mind, and not at all on body, then sentient beings of the formless realm ought likewise to have in-and-out breath operating — yet that realm entirely lacks all four factors, and it is for this reason that breath does not operate there.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four-factor analysis here is a small masterclass in resolving an apparent scriptural contradiction without declaring either side wrong: rather than picking body or mind as the “real” cause, the treatise identifies breath as the joint product of several independently necessary conditions, so that a scripture emphasizing body and a scripture emphasizing mind are each simply highlighting one indispensable ingredient among several, neither statement false, neither complete on its own.

2. Why the Fetus Doesn't Breathe, and Why the Fourth Dhyāna Doesn't Either

The four-factor test is now run against two genuinely difficult edge cases: why doesn't a fetus breathe at the earliest embryonic stages, when it does possess a body? And why does breath stop entirely upon entering the fourth dhyāna, the very peak of form-realm meditation? The kalala stage fails because it is too insubstantial for breath to safely pass through it; the later embryonic stages fail because the wind-channels and pores are not yet open; and the fourth dhyāna, six named masters each explain in their own way, fails because its own mind has become too subtle, too inward-turned, too quiescent, or its own body's pores too completely sealed by that stage's own refined great elements ever to support the coarse, restless activity breathing requires.

Ru-Yi Translation

If in-and-out breath depended on both body and mind together but were not "as appropriate," then, within the eggshell or the mother's womb, at the kalala, arbuda, peśī, and ghana stages, the faculties not yet complete or mature, and also within the fourth dhyāna, breath ought likewise to operate. One asks: why does breath not operate at the kalala stage? The answer: because it is too sparse and thin at that stage — were breath to operate there, it would flow away and be lost. One asks: why does breath not operate at the arbuda, peśī, and ghana stages, the faculties not yet complete or mature? The answer: because, at that stage, the body's own wind-channels are not yet open, and the pores are not yet open — were breath to operate there, the body would disperse and be destroyed. Yet, within the eggshell or the mother's womb, from the kalala stage up through the stage of the faculties not yet complete or mature, at that time there is not yet any body that breath depends upon, the wind-channels are not yet open, and the pores are not yet open — only the coarse mind belonging to the breath-stage is present; though one of the four factors is present, three are lacking, and it is for this reason that breath does not operate. One asks: why does breath not operate within the fourth dhyāna? The answer: because mind, at that stage, is subtle — that is, in-and-out breath depends upon a coarse mind in order to operate, and, from the fourth dhyāna upward, the mind belonging to every stage is exceedingly subtle, and it is for this reason that breath does not operate. Further, because mind there operates through an inward gateway — that is, breath necessarily depends upon a mind operating through an outward gateway, and, from the fourth dhyāna upward, mind operates through an inward gateway, and it is for this reason that breath does not operate. Further, because mind there is engaged with inward matters — that is, breath necessarily depends upon a mind engaged with outward matters, and, from the fourth dhyāna upward, mind is engaged with inward matters, and it is for this reason that breath does not operate. Further, because of its own extreme quiescence — that is, breath necessarily depends upon an agitated, restless mind, just as a person walking along a road, when restless, stirs up the dust — if mind is restless, in-and-out breath thereby arises; and, from the fourth dhyāna upward, mind is exceedingly quiescent, and it is for this reason that breath does not operate. The Venerable Vasumitra explained it in this way: upon entering the fourth dhyāna, one's very support is thereby transformed — that is, the fourth dhyāna's own subtle great elements cause every pore to seal completely shut, without the slightest gap remaining, no longer capable of supporting breath — and it is for this reason that breath no longer operates there at that time. The Great Virtuous One stated: upon entering the fourth dhyāna, mind becomes unmoving; since mind is unmoving, the body, too, becomes unmoving; and, since the body is unmoving, breath no longer operates — for, upon entering that concentration, every moving dharma is stilled and extinguished. The Venerable Ghoṣa explained it in this way: upon entering the fourth dhyāna, every coarse heaviness is stilled and extinguished, and it is for this reason that breath no longer operates — that is, the desire realm possesses coarse sensual craving; the first dhyāna possesses applied and sustained thought; the second dhyāna possesses joy; the third dhyāna possesses bliss — and it is from these that bodily and mental coarse heaviness arises, and it is from this very coarse heaviness that in-and-out breath operates; the fourth dhyāna possesses none of these, and it is for this reason that breath does not operate there. In this way, if one, while still in a lower stage, enters the fourth dhyāna, one still possesses the body that breath depends upon, and the wind-channels remain open, yet the pores are not open, and no coarse mind belonging to the breath-stage manifests before one — though two of the four factors are present, two are lacking, and it is for this reason that breath does not operate. One asks: why does the treatise state only that entering the fourth dhyāna causes breath to no longer operate, without mentioning that being born in the fourth dhyāna does so as well? The answer: this is simply what the treatise's author intended, and so on, as stated at length. Further, it ought also to be stated that being born in the fourth dhyāna causes breath to no longer operate, and that this is not so stated should be understood as a matter left incomplete. Further, having already stated that entering that concentration has this effect, one should understand that being born there has this same effect as well, as scripture states: one first enters that dhyāna in this present life, and only afterward is reborn there. One asks: the fourth dhyāna, too, possesses the wind-element, since the four great elements are never mutually separated from one another — why, then, is it that one born there has no in-and-out breath operating? The answer: the fourth dhyāna, though it does possess the wind-element, this is not properly called "in-breath and out-breath," since, with respect to that body, it never actually goes in or out. Some say: one born there, though possessing the wind-element, lacks the four factors already stated above, and it is for this reason that this is not called "breath." Since in-and-out breath depends both on body and on mind, and operates "as appropriate," it is therefore the case that, from below the uninterrupted hell up through the heaven of universal purity, sentient beings whose faculties are complete and mature have in-and-out breath operating in dependence on body and mind. Here, "faculties complete and mature" means that the four factors already mentioned are complete — it does not mean that the eye and the other sense faculties are complete and mature. Since these four factors are what give rise to breath, they are called "faculties"; their completeness is called "full"; their ripeness, meaning their actual function, is precisely these four factors being complete and actually functioning — and it is on this account that it is said that the faculties are full and mature. Some say that the phrase "faculties full and mature" specifically distinguishes this from the stage within the womb or egg where the faculties are not yet full or mature, revealing the sense of the wind-channels being open and the pores being open; the two factors of body and mind are then separately explained immediately afterward. It should be understood, in this connection, that there is an in-breath, and there is an out-breath, and there is a stage possessing in-and-out breath, and a stage lacking in-and-out breath. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The six independent explanations offered for why the fourth dhyāna alone, among all four dhyānas, stops breath entirely converge on a single underlying intuition worth naming explicitly: breathing is, at bottom, a coarse, restless activity, and the fourth dhyāna is defined precisely by the complete stilling of every form of coarseness and restlessness a meditator's mind and body can possess — Vasumitra's sealed pores, the Great Virtuous One's unmoving mind, and Ghoṣa's extinguished coarse-heaviness are simply three different vocabularies for describing the very same underlying cessation of agitation.

3. Does Breath Follow the Body's Stage, or the Mind's Stage?

A genuinely intricate question opens: is breath bound to whichever stage the body itself belongs to, or to whichever stage the currently-manifesting mind belongs to? Those who hold breath follows the body work through every combination in exhaustive detail — a desire-realm body's own desire-realm breath can follow the course of a desire-realm mind, a first-dhyāna mind, or even a second- or third-dhyāna mind, always taken as object by whichever mind it follows — the same pattern repeating for bodies born in each successively higher dhyāna, each time noting precisely which higher or lower minds can and cannot properly take that body's own breath as their object.

13. The Sign That Called the Monks Back, and Why Breathing Alone Was Named

The treatise explains this deliberate naming, despite outside teachers not even knowing the term, as a device for drawing curious seekers toward the Buddha's own teaching and for steadying newly-ordained monks tempted to defect — and, further, because mindfulness of breathing, though only one among every dhyāna and attainment the Buddha actually practiced during those two months, stands as the very foremost among them, every other attainment merely its accompanying retinue.

14. Short Breath First, Long Breath After: The Buddha's Own First-Person Account

The Buddha's own celebrated first-person description of breath-meditation unfolds in careful stages — mindful of breath in general, then specifically of short breath, then long breath, then breath pervading the entire body, then finally stilling the body's own activity altogether. The treatise settles that short breath always precedes long breath, illustrated by the Bodhisattva's own experience: breathing swift and rapid like a person hauling a heavy load over treacherous terrain, only settling into calm, steady breath once level ground, and deep concentration, has been reached — and maps each stage of this sequence onto a specific dhyāna, from the desire realm's own general mindfulness through the fourth dhyāna's own complete stilling of bodily activity.

How is this known to be so? As the Prajñaptiśāstra states: when the Bodhisattva first entered concentration, his own breath was swift and rapid; only once he had dwelt long in that concentration did his breath become calm and settled — just as a person carrying a heavy load along a treacherous path breathes swiftly and rapidly, but, upon later reaching level ground, breathes calmly and steadily — and it is for this reason that in-and-out breath is short at first, and long afterward. One asks: this contemplation observes the breath-wind entering through the nose and exiting again through the nose — why, then, does the sūtra speak of being aware of the breath pervading the whole body? The answer: before mindfulness of breathing has been perfected, one contemplates the breath entering and exiting through the nose alone; once mindfulness of breathing has been perfected, one contemplates the breath permeating the body's own pores, exactly like the fibers running through a lotus root, the breath-wind pervading and moving through them everywhere. One asks: if that is so, why does this not then count as having exited concentration? 15. Joy, Bliss, and the Cutting-Off of Every Fetter

The Buddha's own account continues through increasingly refined stages of awareness — joy, then bliss, then mental activity itself, and the stilling of that mental activity — mapped onto the ascending dhyānas, before turning toward insight proper: awareness of mind's own very substance, causing mind to rejoice, to be gathered, and finally to be liberated, followed by contemplation of impermanence, cutting-off, dispassion, and cessation. Vasumitra offers his own precise gloss on these final four contemplations, tying cutting-off to the eight fetters, dispassion specifically to the craving-fetter, and cessation to every fettering dharma whatsoever.

16. Four Masters, Four Different Readings of the Same Four Words

Three further masters each offer their own competing gloss on impermanence, cutting-off, dispassion, and cessation — one tying them to the four great elements and the ignorance-fetter, another to the physical body and past-present-future fetters, a third to feeling itself across its pleasant, painful, and neutral varieties — before the Great Virtuous One settles on the broadest reading: contemplating the five aggregates of clinging themselves as impermanent, empty and selfless, suffering, and finally utterly quiescent and no longer operating at all. The Buddha's own account then turns reflective, judging even this deep concentration “still coarse and shallow,” prompting speculation over whether the still deeper concentration he sought was the fourth dhyāna, a formless attainment, or the concentration of cessation itself.

17. Three Deities Debate Whether the Buddha Is Dead

A genuinely vivid closing scene has three deities, radiant beyond even the deepest darkness of night, disagree over whether the motionless, breathless Buddha before them is dead, dying, or simply absorbed in supreme concentration — their disagreement traced directly to the sharpness or dullness of their own faculties, the dullest concluding death from stillness alone, the middling noting retained warmth, and the sharpest recognizing, from having witnessed this very state in other Buddhas and noble disciples before, that he will simply emerge again. The fascicle closes with the Buddha's own answer to what constitutes “dwelling as a noble one, a god, Brahmā, a Buddha, a learner, or one beyond training”: in every case, it is mindfulness of breathing itself, its own purity, brightness, and quiescence earning it every one of these six exalted names at once.
