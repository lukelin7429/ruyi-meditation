The Ancient Path, Freshly Walked

Fascicle 93 opens the Knowledge Aggregate — the treatise's third of eight great aggregates — re-establishing the reality of possession, resolving the Vibhajyavādins' claim that the noble path is unconditioned through the nun Dharmadinnā's own answer, and working through the learner's eight branches and the four kinds of practice-path. 1. A New Aggregate Opens: Why This Treatise Is Composed

Fascicle 93 opens the Knowledge Aggregate (智蘊), the third of the treatise's eight great aggregates, with a scriptural question: the learner's own practice-path possesses the learner's own eight branches — of these, how many are past, how many future, how many present? The reason for composing this new treatise is once again polemical and foundational at once: against those who deny any genuinely real own-nature to the past and future, against the Dārṣṭāntikas' claim that possession is a mere conventional label (illustrated vividly through a wheel-turning king's seven treasures, whose reality would supposedly break down if possession were genuinely real), and against several converging scriptural contradictions that would follow if the property of possession were not itself real.

Chinese Source

如世尊說。學行迹成就學八支。彼成就。過去幾。未來幾現在幾。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the Bhagavān has said: the learner's own practice-path possesses the learner's own eight branches — of these that he possesses, how many are past, how many future, and how many present?

如是等章及解章義既領會已。次應廣釋。問何故作此論。答為欲解釋契經義故。如契經說。學行迹成就學八支。漏盡阿羅漢成就十無學支。契經雖作是說。而不說彼成就。過去幾。未來幾。現在幾。

Such chapters as these, and the meaning of their explanation, having already been grasped, ought now to be explained at length. One asks: for what reason is this treatise composed? The answer: it is in order to explain the meaning of a certain scripture — that is, the scripture states: the learner's own practice-path possesses the learner's own eight branches, while the arhat who has exhausted the outflows possesses the non-learner's own ten branches. Although the scripture speaks in this way, it does not state how many of these he possesses are past, how many future, and how many present.

契經是此論所依根本。彼未說者今應說之。復次為止他宗顯正理故。謂或有執。過去未來無實自性。現在雖有而是無為。為遮彼意顯示實有過去未來可成就故。若彼非有應不成就。如第二頭。第六蘊等既可成就故知實有。又為顯示現在世法定是有為有生滅故。或復有執。無實成就不成就性。如譬喻者。彼說有情不離諸法說名成就。離諸法時名不成就。俱假施設。如五指合假說為拳。離即非拳此亦如是。問彼何故作是執。答彼依契經故作是執。謂契經說。有轉輪王成就七寶。若成就性是實有者。成就輪寶神珠寶故。應法性壞。所以者何。亦是有情亦非情故。成就象寶及馬寶故。復應趣壞。所以者何。亦是傍生亦是人故。成就女寶故復應身壞。所以者何。亦是男身亦女身故。成就主兵主藏臣故復應業壞。所以者何。

The scripture is the very root this treatise relies upon, and what it does not state ought now to be stated. Further, it is in order to put a stop to what other schools maintain, and to display the correct principle — that is, there are those who hold that the past and future possess no genuinely real own-nature, and that the present, although it does exist, is itself unconditioned. In order to put a stop to this intention, it is displayed that the past and future are indeed genuinely real and capable of being possessed — for, were they not existent, they ought not to be capable of being possessed at all, exactly as a second head, or the sixth aggregate, and the like, being incapable of being possessed, are thereby known not to be real; whereas the past and future, being indeed capable of being possessed, are thereby known to be genuinely real. Further, it is in order to display that the dharma of the present world are certainly conditioned, possessing arising and ceasing. There are, further, those who hold that there exists no genuinely real property of possession or non-possession, as the Dārṣṭāntikas hold, who maintain that a sentient being not separated from various dharma is named a possessor, while, at the time of being separated from those dharma, he is named a non-possessor, both being merely conventional designations — exactly as the five fingers, joined together, are conventionally called a fist, while, separated, they no longer constitute a fist; this, too, is just the same. One asks: for what reason do they hold this position? The answer: they hold this position in reliance upon a certain scripture — that is, the scripture states that there was a wheel-turning king who possessed the seven treasures. If the property of possession were genuinely real, then, by possessing the wheel-treasure and the wish-fulfilling-jewel treasure, the very own-nature of dharma itself ought to break down — for what reason is this so? Because these are at once sentient and non-sentient. By possessing the elephant-treasure and the horse-treasure, the very category of destiny ought, further, to break down — for what reason is this so? Because these are at once an animal and a human being. By possessing the woman-treasure, the very category of body ought, further, to break down — for what reason is this so? Because this is at once a male body and a female body. By possessing the general-treasure and the treasurer-treasure ministers, the very category of karma ought, further, to break down — for what reason is this so?

君臣雜故。勿有此失故成就性定非實有。為遮彼意顯成就性定是實有。若不爾者便違契經。如說。學行迹成就學八支。漏盡阿羅漢成就十無學支。聖者現起有漏心時。應不成就過去未來諸無漏法。云何成就八支十支。復違餘經。如說。如是補特伽羅。成就善法及不善法。若彼善法現在前時。應不成就不善法。若不善法現在前時。

Because the ruler's own karma and the minister's own karma would become confused with one another. Lest there be this very fault, the property of possession is thereby certainly not genuinely real. In order to put a stop to this intention, it is displayed that the property of possession is certainly genuinely real — for, were it not so, this would contradict the scripture. As it is stated: the learner's own practice-path possesses the learner's own eight branches, while the arhat who has exhausted the outflows possesses the non-learner's own ten branches — and, at the very moment a noble one gives rise to a contaminated mind, he ought then not to possess any of the past or future undefiled dharma whatsoever, so how could he be said to possess the eight branches or the ten branches? This would, further, contradict yet another scripture, as it states: such-and-such an individual possesses wholesome dharma and unwholesome dharma — and, at the very moment that wholesome dharma manifests, he ought then not to possess the unwholesome dharma, while, at the very moment the unwholesome dharma manifests,

應不成就善法。若無記法現在前時。應不成就善不善法。云何說成就善不善法耶。復違餘經。如說。成就七善法者彼現法中多住喜樂如理勤修必能盡漏七善法者知法知義乃至廣說。七中若一現在前時應不成就餘六善法。若起餘法現在前時。應不成就七。云何成就七善法耶。復違餘經。如說。如來成就十力。若起一力現在前時。應不成就九。若起餘法現在前時。應不成就十。云何如來成就十力。

he ought then not to possess the wholesome dharma; and, at the very moment a neutral-and-unobscured dharma manifests, he ought then not to possess either the wholesome or the unwholesome dharma — so how could it be said that he possesses both wholesome and unwholesome dharma? This would, further, contradict yet another scripture, as it states: whoever possesses the seven wholesome dharma dwells, within that very visible life, largely in joy and happiness, and, cultivating diligently in accordance with principle, is certain to be capable of exhausting the outflows — the seven wholesome dharma being: knowing the dharma, knowing its meaning, and so on, stated at length — and, were any single one of these seven to manifest, he ought then not to possess the remaining six wholesome dharma, while, were some other dharma to manifest, he ought then not to possess any of the seven at all — so how could it be said that he possesses the seven wholesome dharma? This would, further, contradict yet another scripture, as it states: the Tathāgata possesses the ten powers — and, were any single power to manifest, he ought then not to possess the remaining nine, while, were some other dharma to manifest, he ought then not to possess any of the ten at all — so how, then, could the Tathāgata be said to possess the ten powers?

復有餘失。謂諸異生不染污心現在前住。應成無學。不成就三界一切煩惱故。諸無學者起有漏心現在前位。應成異生。不成就一切學無學法故。勿有此失。故成就性決定實有。問若成就性是實有者。

There is, further, yet another fault: whenever an ordinary being's own undefiled mind manifests and abides in the present, he ought then to become a non-learner, since he would not, at that moment, possess any of the three realms' own afflictions whatsoever; and whenever a non-learner gives rise to a contaminated mind that manifests in the present, he ought then to become an ordinary being, since he would not, at that moment, possess any of the learner's or the non-learner's own dharma whatsoever. Lest there be this very fault, the property of possession is thereby certainly and decidedly genuinely real. One asks: if the property of possession is indeed genuinely real, then

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That an entirely new aggregate opens by re-litigating, in far greater depth, the very question of whether possession is genuinely real — a question the Fetters Aggregate had already settled back in fascicle 90 — is a meaningful structural choice: the compilers are not simply assuming their earlier conclusion carries over, but re-establishing the foundation from first principles for this new aggregate's own purposes, since the entire analysis to follow, of how many branches of the path are possessed as past, future, and present, depends on possession being something genuinely real for a moment to attach to at all.

2. Possession Defended Further, and the Puzzle of the Ancient Path

The defense of possession's reality continues, incorporating the mutual-dependence argument (light needs darkness, day needs night, and so a genuinely real non-possession is needed for possession itself to be genuinely established) before pivoting to an entirely different but related controversy: the Vibhajyavādins' claim that the noble path itself is unconditioned and eternal, one single awakening ridden by different Buddhas the way many riders mount the same unchanging elephant — a claim resolved by citing the nun Dharmadinnā's own reported answer that the path is indeed conditioned, and by explaining the Buddha's own phrase "the ancient path" through a fivefold resemblance every Buddha's own awakening shares, rather than through any literal eternity of the path itself.

Chinese Source

前譬喻者所引契經當云何通。答輪王於彼有自在力隨意受用如成就故。立成就名。若全撥無實成就性。如何於彼立成就名。復有餘師許有成就撥不成就。彼作是說。諸成就性有作用故可是實有。不成就性既無作用。云何實有。為遮彼執顯不成就亦是實有。若不成就非實有者。亦應非有實成就性相待立。故如影與光明暗晝夜寒熱等事相待而立闕一不成。此亦如是。復次不成就性與成就性近互相違故。俱實有如貪無貪瞋無瞋癡無癡等近互相違故俱實有。復次若不成就非實有性應不施設煩惱斷法。所以者何。非聖道生斷諸煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

how is one to reconcile this with the scripture the Dārṣṭāntikas cited earlier? The answer: the wheel-turning king possesses, with respect to these treasures, a genuinely sovereign power, using and enjoying them freely, exactly as though he genuinely possessed them, and it is on this basis that the name "possession" is applied — but, if one entirely denies the existence of any genuinely real property of possession whatsoever, how could the name "possession" ever be applied to him at all? There are, further, yet other teachers who grant the existence of possession while denying non-possession, who maintain as follows: the property of possession, since it possesses a genuine function, may indeed be genuinely real, but the property of non-possession, possessing no function whatsoever, how could it be genuinely real? In order to put a stop to this holding, it is displayed that non-possession, too, is genuinely real. If non-possession were not genuinely real, then possession itself, being established only in mutual dependence upon its counterpart, ought likewise not to be genuinely real — exactly as shadow and light, brightness and darkness, day and night, cold and heat, and the like, are established only in mutual dependence upon one another, neither being possible without the other; this, too, is just the same. Further, the property of non-possession and the property of possession, being closely mutually opposed to one another, are both genuinely real, exactly as craving and non-craving, aversion and non-aversion, delusion and non-delusion, and the like, being closely mutually opposed to one another, are both genuinely real. Further, if non-possession were not a genuinely real property, then the cutting-off of affliction ought not to be established as a genuine dharma at all — for what reason is this so? It is not that the noble path itself arises and thereby cuts off the various afflictions,

如刀斷物。如石磨香。然諸聖道現在前時。令諸煩惱成就得滅。亦令彼煩惱不成就得生。爾時說為斷諸煩惱。有餘復執道是無為。如分別論者。彼作是說。唯一無上正等菩提常住不滅隨彼彼佛出現世間。能證者雖異而所證無別。如一龍象妙飾莊嚴。雖有多人次第乘御。而彼龍象前後是一。問彼何故作此執。答彼依契經。如契經說。佛告苾芻。我證舊道故知聖道定是無為。為遮彼執欲顯聖道墮三世故。定是有為非無為法。墮三世故。又執聖道體是無為。便違契經。如契經說。有一近事名毘舍佉。來詣法授苾芻尼所。問言。

exactly as a knife cuts an object, or a stone grinds fragrant wood. Rather, at the very moment the various noble paths manifest, they cause the possession of the various afflictions to cease, and likewise cause the non-possession of those same afflictions to arise — and it is at that very moment that one speaks of "cutting off the various afflictions." There are, further, yet others who hold that the path is itself unconditioned, as the Vibhajyavādins hold, who maintain as follows: there exists only a single, unsurpassed, perfect awakening, eternally abiding and never perishing, and, according to whichever Buddha appears within the world, although the ones who realize it differ, that which is realized is never distinct — exactly as a single, mighty elephant, wondrously adorned and caparisoned, though mounted and ridden in succession by many different people, remains, before and after, one and the same elephant. One asks: for what reason do they hold this position? The answer: they hold this position in reliance upon a certain scripture — that is, the scripture states: the Buddha told the monks, I have realized the ancient path — and it is by virtue of this that they know the noble path is certainly unconditioned. In order to put a stop to this holding, it is intended to display that the noble path falls within the three times, and is thereby certainly conditioned, not an unconditioned dharma, since it falls within the three times. Further, to hold that the noble path's own very substance is unconditioned directly contradicts the scripture — as the scripture states: there was a certain lay devotee named Viśākha, who came before the nun Dharmadinnā and asked her:

聖道為是有為。為是無為。苾芻尼言。聖道有為墮三世故。問若聖道是有為分別論者所引契經當云何通。答由五事相似故說名舊道。

is the noble path conditioned, or is it unconditioned? The nun replied: the noble path is conditioned, since it falls within the three times. One asks: if the noble path is indeed conditioned, then how is one to reconcile this with the scripture the Vibhajyavādins cited earlier? The answer: it is by virtue of a resemblance in five respects that it is named "the ancient path."

一地相似。諸佛皆依第四靜慮得菩提故。二加行相似。諸佛皆經三無數劫修習六種波羅蜜多得圓滿故。三所緣相似。諸佛皆緣四聖諦理證菩提故。四行相相似。諸佛皆以苦非常等十六行相修聖道故。

First, a resemblance in ground, since every Buddha attains awakening in dependence upon the fourth dhyāna. Second, a resemblance in preparatory effort, since every Buddha passes through three incalculable eons cultivating the six perfections to their full completion. Third, a resemblance in object, since every Buddha realizes awakening by taking the principle of the four noble truths as his object. Fourth, a resemblance in aspect, since every Buddha cultivates the noble path by means of the sixteen aspects, such as suffering and impermanence and the rest.

五所作相似。諸佛皆以無漏道力斷自身中一切煩惱。亦令無量無邊有情得涅槃樂故。若不依此釋舊道名而執無為。是舊義者。即彼經說舊城舊都豈舊城都是無為法。又若經說。證舊聖道即執聖道是無為法。契經亦說。蛇退舊皮豈蛇舊皮是無為法。如伽他說。

Fifth, a resemblance in accomplished deed, since every Buddha, by the power of the undefiled path, cuts off every affliction within his own person, and likewise causes measureless, boundless sentient beings to attain the bliss of nirvāṇa. If one does not rely upon this explanation of the term "ancient path," but instead holds that "ancient" itself means unconditioned, then, by that same reasoning, when that very scripture speaks of an "ancient city" or an "ancient capital," ought that ancient city or capital, too, be an unconditioned dharma? Further, if, because the scripture states "I have realized the ancient noble path," one thereby holds that the noble path is an unconditioned dharma, the scripture likewise states that a snake sheds its own old skin — ought a snake's own old skin, too, be an unconditioned dharma? As the verse states:

若斷愛無餘　如蓮花處水苾芻捨此彼　如蛇脫舊皮如舊城等皆是有為舊聖道言理亦應爾。為止此等他宗所說及為顯示諸法正理故作斯論。

If one has cut off craving entirely without remainder, exactly as a lotus flower stands within water, the monk relinquishes both this shore and that, exactly as a snake sheds its own old skin. Exactly as the ancient city and the rest are all conditioned, so too, by the same principle, ought the term "the ancient noble path" to be understood. It is in order to put a stop to what these other schools maintain, and to display the correct principle of the various dharma, that this treatise is composed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image of a snake shedding its own old skin, deployed here to puncture the Vibhajyavādins' reading of "ancient path" as meaning unconditioned, is a small masterstroke of argument: if calling something "ancient" implied it must be eternal and unconditioned, then a snake's shed skin — itself the most ordinary, perishable, conditioned thing imaginable — would have to be unconditioned too, and the absurdity of that conclusion does more to settle the question than any amount of further doctrinal argument could.

3. Who Possesses? Neither the Dharma nor the Person

Having established that possession is genuinely real, the treatise now asks who or what actually does the possessing — neither the dharma themselves (which are entirely without function) nor the individual (which cannot ultimately be found at all), but rather a genuinely real property of possession and non-possession that attaches to the aggregates' own arising, without there being any genuinely real possessor underlying it. Four successive attempts to define the very meaning of "possession" are offered and refined in turn — not yet cut off, already attained, not yet relinquished — before settling on the fourth and final formulation: already attained and not yet relinquished.

Chinese Source

如世尊說。學行迹成就學八支。此中云何學八支。謂學正見乃至正定成就者。問誰成就為法成就。為補特伽羅成就耶。設爾何失。二俱有過。所以者何。若法成就者。一切法既無作用。於無作用一切法中何法能成就何法所成就。若補特伽羅成就者。諦義勝義。補特伽羅都不可得。既無真實補特伽羅。云何說彼能成就法。答應作是說。非法成就亦非補特伽羅成就。然有真實成就性及不成就性。而無真實成就者及不成就者。如有真實雜染清淨繫縛解脫流轉還滅因果死生諸業異熟道及道果。而無真實雜染清淨者乃至修道證道果者。有作是說。法成就。問若爾法無作用云何成就。答諸法雖無作用而有功能。問若爾眼處應成就十一處。十一處亦應成就眼處。答依此理說亦無有過。皆有功能互相引故。評曰。應作是說。能成就者非法。亦非補特伽羅。無真實作用故。補特伽羅非實有故。然有四蘊五蘊生時。與如是類諸得俱轉說名成就。與如是類非得俱轉名不成就。問若爾經說當云何通。如說。如是補特伽羅成就善法。及不善法答此是世尊於諸蘊中依世俗說。不言實有補特伽羅成就諸法。問此中何者是成就義。尊者世友作如是說。不斷義是成就義。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the Bhagavān has said: the learner's own practice-path possesses the learner's own eight branches — here, what are the learner's own eight branches? This refers to the learner's own right view, up through right concentration, being possessed. One asks: who possesses these — is it the dharma themselves that possess, or is it the individual who possesses? Whichever position one grants, there is a fault. Why is this so? If it is the dharma themselves that possess, then, since every dharma is entirely without any function whatsoever, among these functionless dharma, which dharma is capable of possessing, and which dharma is the very thing possessed? If it is the individual who possesses, then, in ultimate truth and ultimate meaning, no individual whatsoever can genuinely be found at all — and, there being no genuinely real individual, how could one say that he is capable of possessing the dharma? The answer: one ought to say as follows — it is neither the dharma that possess, nor the individual who possesses; rather, there exists a genuinely real property of possession and of non-possession, yet there exists no genuinely real possessor or non-possessor — exactly as there exists a genuinely real defilement and purification, bondage and liberation, transmigration and cessation, cause and fruit, dying and being born, karma and its ripened fruit, the path and the fruit of the path, yet there exists no genuinely real defiled or purified being, nor one who cultivates the path or realizes the fruit of the path. There are those who say: it is the dharma that possess. One asks: if this is so, then, the dharma being without function, how could they possess? The answer: although the various dharma are indeed without function, they do possess a genuine capacity. One asks: if this is so, then the eye sense-field ought to possess the remaining eleven sense-fields, and the remaining eleven sense-fields ought likewise to possess the eye sense-field. The answer: reasoning in this way, too, is without fault, since all of them possess a genuine capacity to draw one another forth in mutual dependence. The verdict: one ought to say as follows — that which possesses is neither the dharma, nor the individual, since there exists no genuinely real function, and since the individual is not genuinely real; rather, at the very moment the four or five aggregates arise, their arising together with an attainment of this same kind is what is named "possession," while their arising together with a non-attainment of this same kind is named "non-possession." One asks: if this is so, then how is one to reconcile this with what the scripture states? As it states: such-and-such an individual possesses wholesome dharma and unwholesome dharma. 4. Why Is the Learner Called "Learner"?

A further definitional puzzle follows directly: is "the learner" named for actively studying what is to be learned, or for having already attained the learner's own dharma? 7. The Idealized Learner, and the Four Kinds of Practice

A genuine puzzle closes this stretch: the treatise's own count of past and future branches, when a learner moves from a formless concentration back into one possessing directed thought and sustained reflection, seems to disregard branches that ought logically still be counted as past — resolved by clarifying that the entire analysis describes only an idealized learner who ascends through every concentration in strict, unbroken sequence, exactly as one climbs a staircase step by step, comparable to the scripture's own idealized woman traced from youthful beauty through old age, illness, death, and decay. This resolved, the treatise opens an entirely new topic: the four kinds of practice-path — painful-and-slow, painful-and-fast, pleasant-and-slow, and pleasant-and-fast — distinguished by ground (the difficult preliminary and formless stages versus the easy fundamental dhyānas) and by faculty or by the specific kind of practitioner (one who follows through faith versus one who follows through the dharma, and their own further refinements).

8. Why "Painful," "Slow," and "Penetrating"

Each element of the four practice-paths' own name is explained in turn: "painful" describes not the feeling accompanying the path but the sheer difficulty of accomplishing it within the preliminary and formless stages, against the ease of the fundamental dhyānas; "slow" and "fast" describe how quickly the practitioner's own faculty reaches final nirvāṇa — provoking a genuine objection, since one who follows through faith transformed into resolve can, through sheer diligence, reach nirvāṇa before an indolent "one who has arrived at insight," resolved by clarifying that the comparison assumes equal diligence on both sides; and "penetrating" names the whole complex of aggregates solely by its predominant element of wisdom, exactly as the path of seeing, though itself a complex of all five aggregates, is named for its wisdom alone.

9. The Middling Faculty, Reconciled

A further puzzle asks whether a middling faculty exists at all, given that the path of seeing, cultivation, and non-learning each admit of only two kinds (faith-follower and dharma-follower, and their own further refinements), leaving no room for a genuine third path — yet scripture plainly speaks of beings with keen, middling, and dull faculties alike. The resolution locates the middling faculty not as an independent third category but as a relative position within the very same twofold division, illustrated by the striking observation that the Great Awakened One himself, the solitary-realizer, and Śāriputra, though all sharing the single lineage of one who follows through the dharma, plainly differ enough among themselves that this same lineage must itself admit of at least three internal grades.

The answer: it ought indeed to speak of it, and that it does not is a case whose meaning has been left over for elsewhere. Further, the middling faculty is itself gathered within the keen and the dull — for what reason is this so? When speaking of the dull faculty, the middling faculty is named "keen," since it surpasses the dull faculty; when speaking of the keen faculty, the middling faculty is named "dull," since it falls short of the keen faculty. It is by virtue of this that the Venerable Vasumitra speaks as follows: the middling faculty ought to be said to be gathered within the keen faculty, since it surpasses the dull faculty; it may, further, be said to be gathered within the dull faculty, since it falls short of the keen faculty. The Great Virtuous One speaks as follows: the middling faculty may be said to be gathered within the keen or the dull faculty, yet it cannot be said to be gathered within a superior or inferior faculty — for what reason is this so? Because the keen and the dull faculties each possess three grades. How is this known to be so? The Great Awakened One, the solitary-realizer, Śāriputra, and the like, are all gathered within the very lineage of one who follows through the dharma — how, then, could these three kinds of persons possibly all be alike within the very nature of the keen faculty? Since there do indeed exist three grades, it is thereby known that the dull faculty's own nature, too, must admit of three grades. The keen and dull paths each possessing three grades, it is for this reason that the scripture speaks of there being three grades of faculty; and, since a third path cannot be found, the Abhidharma states that there exists no middling faculty. Thus are the scripture's and the treatise's two statements soundly reconciled.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the very same lineage — those who follow through the dharma — must itself contain grades sharp enough to distinguish the Buddha himself from Śāriputra is a genuinely elegant solution to what looked like a real contradiction: rather than inventing a third path the Abhidharma's own architecture has no room for, the compilers simply observe that "keen" and "dull" were never absolute categories to begin with, only relative ones, and a middling position within either category satisfies the scripture's own threefold language without requiring any structural change to the twofold path at all.

10. Who Possesses, and Who Makes Use of, How Many Practices

The fascicle closes with two related questions: how many of the four practice-paths does a given practitioner possess, and how many does he actually make use of in accomplishing his own tasks? At most two are ever possessed simultaneously — never three or four, since no single individual can be both keen and dull, or both a faith-follower and a dharma-follower, at once, a position the compilers defend against the Venerable Saṃghavasu's own contrary view. Yet a practitioner may make use of one, two, three, or, across an entire lifetime of practice, even all four in succession, worked through in exhaustive combinatorial detail — every dull-faculty and keen-faculty pairing across every stage of the path traced to its own final nirvāṇa.
