Fascicle 171 continues the Subsumption Nyāsa (攝納息第三之六), opening with sudden-versus-gradual attainment across the equipoises, then pivoting to a running "eliminated by which concentration" survey covering bodily and verbal karma, the three kinds of conduct, the four kinds of noble and ignoble speech, the four modes of birth, and — in this fascicle's richest material — the four types of entering the womb, closing with the Bodhisattva's own final descent from Tuṣita Heaven. 1. Sudden Versus Gradual: A New Lens on Attainment and Loss

Fascicle 171 opens by applying an entirely new lens to the equipoise-analysis that has run since fascicle 168: not what a practitioner possesses, but whether attainment and relinquishment happen suddenly, all at once, or gradually, one stage at a time. The treatise works through the flavor-associated, pure, and uncontaminated dhyānas and formless spheres in turn, showing a striking asymmetry — the flavor-associated dhyānas can be suddenly attained (through regression or rebirth) but never suddenly relinquished, while the pure dhyānas can never be suddenly attained at all, since a first attainment is inherently gradual.

Ru-Yi Translation

Is there a case of suddenly attaining the flavor-associated four dhyānas all at once? Answer: there is — this refers to someone whose craving for the form realm has been exhausted who, upon the arising of an affliction, regresses into the desire realm or the Brahmā world, or who, dying in the formless realm, is reborn in the desire realm or the Brahmā world. Question: why is this treatise composed? Answer: although the preceding passage has already explained attainment and relinquishment, it has not yet distinguished gradual from sudden — this treatise now wishes to display the distinction between the gradual and the sudden. Furthermore, the preceding passage explained matters by cross-referencing the three categories (flavor-associated, pure, uncontaminated) against one another, whereas this treatise now wishes to explain each category purely on its own terms, and it is for this reason that it is composed. "Sudden" means simultaneous; "gradual" means occurring one after another in succession. This passage shows that the flavor-associated four dhyānas are suddenly attained all at once on two occasions — namely, the moment of regression and the moment of rebirth. "Regression" refers to someone whose craving for the form realm has been exhausted; "rebirth" refers to someone dying in the formless realm — such a person, upon regressing into the desire realm or the Brahmā world through the arising of an affliction, or upon being reborn in the desire realm or the Brahmā world, attains all four flavor-associated dhyānas simultaneously at once. Is there a case of suddenly relinquishing the flavor-associated four dhyānas all at once? Answer: no, since there is no case of simultaneously becoming free from the defilement of all four grounds at once. Is there a case of gradually attaining the flavor-associated four dhyānas? Answer: there is — this, too, occurs gradually on two occasions, namely regression and rebirth. "Regression" refers to someone whose craving for the form realm has been exhausted who, upon the arising of an affliction, regresses successively through each dhyāna one after another from the bottom up; "rebirth" refers to someone dying in the formless realm who is reborn successively in each dhyāna-ground one after another from the bottom up — such a person attains the flavor-associated four dhyānas gradually, one after another. Is there a case of gradually relinquishing the flavor-associated four dhyānas? Answer: there is, since one necessarily first becomes free from the first dhyāna's own defilement, and only afterward, in succession, becomes free from the fourth dhyāna's own defilement. Is there a case of suddenly attaining the pure four dhyānas all at once? Reading note — Ru-Yi

The recurring principle that a fresh attainment is inherently incapable of being sudden, while regression and rebirth can trigger a genuinely simultaneous gain, reveals something important about how this tradition understood spiritual progress and its opposite: building something new is patient and cumulative, but the machinery of loss and re-acquisition through worldly cycling operates by an entirely different, more abrupt logic.

2. Why the Upper Dhyānas Have No Bodily or Verbal Action at All

The fascicle pivots, without any formal chapter break, to an entirely new investigation: by which level of concentration is each of a long list of dharma-categories eliminated? It opens with bodily and verbal manifest and unmanifest karmic action, explaining the strikingly concrete reason the upper three dhyānas have no bodily or verbal manifestation whatsoever — investigation and reflection (vitarka and vicāra) have already ceased there, and manifest action cannot arise without them, since a sūtra states plainly that one speaks only after having investigated.

Ru-Yi Translation

By which level of concentration are bodily and verbal manifest and unmanifest action eliminated? Answer: bodily and verbal manifest action is eliminated by the first dhyāna or the stage of proximate access; bodily and verbal unmanifest action is eliminated by the four dhyānas or the stage of proximate access. Question: why is this treatise composed? Answer: in order to refute those who hold that manifest and unmanifest action is not genuinely real, and to make plain that manifest and unmanifest action is indeed genuinely real; and also in order to refute the view that bodily and verbal manifest action extends clear through the fourth dhyāna, and unmanifest action clear through the peak of existence — this treatise now wishes to display that bodily and verbal manifest action extends only clear through the Brahmā world, and unmanifest action only clear through the fourth dhyāna, and it is for this reason that it is composed. Question: why do the upper three dhyānas have no bodily or verbal manifest action? Answer: bodily and verbal manifest action arises together with investigation and reflection — wherever investigation and reflection are found in a given ground, bodily and verbal manifest action is also found there. A sūtra states: "having investigated, one then speaks — one does not speak without having investigated"; and bodily manifestation, too, should likewise be understood as occurring only after investigation, never without it. Since investigation and reflection have ceased in the upper three dhyānas, there is no bodily or verbal manifest action there. Question: why do the upper grounds lack investigation and reflection? Answer: investigation and reflection are coarse, agitated, and not tranquil, whereas the upper grounds are subtle and tranquil. Question: why does bodily and verbal unmanifest action extend only up through the fourth dhyāna, and not into the upper grounds? Answer: wholesome unmanifest action serves as the antidote for unwholesome unmanifest action, and unwholesome unmanifest action exists only in the desire realm — the formless realm is too far removed from the desire realm in all four respects for it to exist there. Others hold: the form realm, clear through the fourth dhyāna, is capable of feeling revulsion toward the desire realm's own evil deeds, whereas the upper grounds are not. Others hold: unmanifest action depends upon the physical body's own great elements, and, since the formless realm has no physical body or great elements, it likewise has no unmanifest action. Here, bodily and verbal manifest action may be wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral. The unwholesome is fettered to the desire realm, and is eliminated by the stage of proximate access; the wholesome and the neutral are fettered to the desire realm or the first dhyāna. That fettered to the desire realm is eliminated by the stage of proximate access; that fettered to the first dhyāna is eliminated by the first dhyāna's own stage of proximate access, the meditative interval, or the second dhyāna's own proximate-access ground. The term "stage of proximate access" here extends to all eight proximate-access grounds together with the meditative interval, since none of these has actually reached the fundamental level of concentration itself. As for the concentration spoken of in this passage, some hold that it refers to the uncontaminated concentration alone; others hold that it refers to both the contaminated and uncontaminated concentration together. If it refers to the uncontaminated concentration alone, then the term "stage of proximate access" here denotes the stage of proximate access and the meditative interval; if it refers to both the contaminated and uncontaminated together, then the term "stage of proximate access" here denotes the stage of proximate access, the meditative interval, and the second dhyāna's own proximate-access ground. Furthermore, as for the "elimination" spoken of in this passage, some hold that it refers generally to generic-type elimination; others hold that it refers exclusively to final elimination. If it refers generally to generic-type elimination, then the term "stage of proximate access" here subsumes all three grounds, and, within the stage of proximate access, subsumes both the contaminated and the uncontaminated, since this is capable of eliminating bodily and verbal manifest action fettered to the desire realm and the first dhyāna.

The third type belongs to the Bodhisattva bound to just one more birth — that is, at the moment of dying in Jambudvīpa and being reborn in Tuṣita Heaven. The second type belongs to the Bodhisattva in the birth immediately preceding that one — that is, at the moment of dying in that prior existence and being reborn in Jambudvīpa to practice the holy life under Kāśyapa Buddha's own teaching. The first type belongs to the Bodhisattva prior even to that. The verdict: this account should not be given, since the Bodhisattva, ever since ninety-one eons ago, has continuously remembered his past lives, and, throughout the existence at death, the intermediate existence, the existence at rebirth, and the existence of an ordinary lifetime, has always been free of any distorted perception and has never lost mindfulness — and it is for this reason that the earlier account given above should be understood as the sound one. Question: at every stage of taking on the bond of rebirth, a defiled mind necessarily arises, and every defiled mind is necessarily associated with the absence of clear awareness — how, then, can it be said that one enters the mother's womb with clear awareness? Answer: the "clear awareness" spoken of here means simply the absence of any distorted perception or distorted resolve — it does not require the absence of association with ignorance altogether, and it is in this sense that it is called clear awareness. The Bodhisattva, however, at the moment of taking on the bond of rebirth, does indeed give rise to a craving attachment to his own embodied self and a craving attachment to his parents, and so he, too, has a defiled mind at that time. Question: if so, how does he differ from every other sentient being? Answer: as for every other sentient being, at the moment of about to enter the womb, he forms the perception of the father as not-father and the mother as not-mother — and it is because of this that a male being gives rise to hatred toward the father and craving toward the mother, forming the distorted perception that he is uniting with the mother,

while a female being gives rise to hatred toward the mother and craving toward the father, forming the distorted perception that she is uniting with the father — and it is through this very distorted perception that the bond of rebirth is taken on.

菩薩不爾。將入胎時。於父父想於母母想。我依彼故增長後蘊。當 於贍部洲中受尊勝報依此證得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。與諸有情作饒 益事。作是念已。便於父母等生親愛。由此結生。是故正知。謂無 倒想非要無染。此四種入胎唯欲界繫。是故但依未至定滅。

The Bodhisattva is not like this: at the moment of about to enter the womb, he forms the perception of the father as father and the mother as mother, thinking, "it is by depending on them that I shall increase my future aggregates, and, receiving an honored and excellent recompense within Jambudvīpa, shall thereby attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, and so bring benefit to every sentient being" — and, having thought this, he then gives rise to an affectionate love for his parents, and it is through this that he takes on the bond of rebirth. It is for this reason that he is said to have clear awareness, meaning simply the absence of any distorted perception, and not necessarily the absence of all defilement. These four types of entering the womb are fettered only to the desire realm, and it is for this reason that they are eliminated only by the stage of proximate access.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The frank psychological account of an ordinary being's confused desire at the moment of conception — perceiving the parent of the opposite sex as a spouse rather than a parent — is a striking piece of pre-modern depth psychology, offered here not as scandal but as sober doctrinal explanation for why most rebirths happen in confusion while the Bodhisattva's does not: the very same craving that drives ordinary conception is, in his case alone, purified into a lucid wish to benefit all beings.

9. The Four Abodes of Consciousness, Closing the Fascicle

The fascicle closes by completing its running template one final time for the four abodes of consciousness — the last three of which are fettered to all nine grounds and eliminated only by the seven fundamental grounds or the stage of proximate access, while the form-realm abode of consciousness, fettered to only five grounds, is eliminated by the four dhyānas or the stage of proximate access — with the now-familiar doctrinal fork over whether "elimination" and "proximate access" should be read narrowly or broadly recurring one last time before the fascicle ends.

That this fascicle can move, within a single continuous discussion under one unchanged chapter heading, from the driest possible technical fork about what "proximate access" denotes to the vivid, almost novelistic account of the Bodhisattva's conception — and back again to dry technicality by its close — is itself a fair sample of the Mahāvibhāṣā's own range: the same rigorous classificatory machinery is applied without discrimination to both the most abstract metaphysics and the most human of moments.
