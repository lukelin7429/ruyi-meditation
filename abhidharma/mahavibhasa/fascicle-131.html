Are Conditions Even Real?

Fascicle 131 closes the merit-in-giving narrative with the householder Ugra's equanimous giving and the equal merit of Sujātā's milk-porridge and Cunda's final meal, then opens the Condition Nyāsa, refuting the Dṛṣṭāntikas' denial that causal conditions are real and building an exhaustive catalog of causal relations across the great elements, derived form, mind, and the twenty-two faculties. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Who Was the Deva in the Sky? And Two Kinds of 'Approaching Stream-Entry'

Fascicle 131 opens by completing the merit-in-giving arc from the previous fascicle: the householder Ugra tells the Buddha that a deity once whispered to him, while he served food, exactly which recipient held which fruit of the path — yet he gave with an equal heart throughout regardless. Competing theories identify the deity as a Māra obstructing his virtue, a deceptive ghost, a deity he regularly worshipped, or a deceased relative reborn as a god. A careful distinction then separates two senses of "approaching stream-entry" — the merely conventional stage even a deity could perceive, and the ultimate stage occurring within the swift, imperceptible path of seeing itself, illustrated by the Buddha's own rebuke of the monk Bāhiya's hesitation.

Chinese Source

As the sūtra says: the householder Ugra told the Buddha, "World-Honored One, once, while I myself, ladle in hand, was giving food and drink to the community, a deity in the sky spoke to me, saying, 'Householder, you should know — this one has the fruit of arhatship; this one approaches arhatship; this one has the fruit of non-returning; this one approaches non-returning; this one has the fruit of once-returning; this one approaches once-returning; this one has the fruit of stream-entry; this one approaches stream-entry; this one keeps the precepts purely; this one violates them.' Though I heard these words at that time, I examined myself and found no partiality in my own mind, and gave to the assembly of monks with an equal heart throughout." Question: who was this deity, and for what reason did it come to speak to the householder? Some say: it was a deity of Māra's own host, wishing to create an obstacle to the householder's own wholesome practice. Others say: it was a ghost, seeking to confuse the householder with false words. Other masters say: it was a deity the householder regularly made offerings to, who came into the sky to show him the distinctions among the various fields of merit. Others, further, say:

彼是長者過去親屬生在天中。以誠實言汲引長者。問若是長者過去親屬於預流向云何能知。見道迅速非其境故。答預流向有二種。一者世俗。二者勝義。得順決擇分名為世俗。已入見道名為勝義。住世俗向是彼天境。若勝義向舍利子等。尚不盡知況彼天等。彼天所示但是世俗。能受長者所施食故。亦有餘經說勝義向。如世尊告婆拕梨言。若有苾芻是俱解脫。我設告彼汝來以身於此濘渠為我作彴。婆拕梨聽於意云何。彼聞我命為拒逆不。將登躡時為退避不正踐蹈時為轉側不。婆拕梨曰不也。世尊復告婆拕梨。置俱解脫。若有苾芻是慧解脫。我設告彼乃至廣說。復置慧解脫說於身證。復置身證說於見至。復置見至說信勝解置信勝解說隨法行。置隨法行說隨信行。佛問彼答一一如前。有說。此經亦說世俗預流果向。以見道中不能聽受佛語義故。評曰此中說勝義向於理為善。以說隨信隨法行故。問住見道時無能聽受。佛說義者無異心故。如何世尊以言告彼。答世尊依彼志樂而說。無如是事為分別故。假在見道有異分心。能受如來此言義者。必捨見道作所勅事。佛意呵責婆拕梨言。

— this was a deity who had been the householder's own relative in a past life, reborn among the gods, come to guide the householder with truthful words. Question: if this was the householder's own past relative, how could it know one approaching stream-entry, given that the path of seeing occurs too swiftly to be its object? Answer: "approaching stream-entry" is of two kinds — the conventional, and the ultimate. Attaining the wholesome roots conducive to liberation is called the conventional; already having entered the path of seeing is called the ultimate. It is the conventional approacher who falls within that deity's own range; even the ultimate approacher is not fully known even by Śāriputra and others like him, let alone by that deity — what the deity indicated was only the conventional kind, since such a one is still capable of receiving the householder's own offered food. There is, however, another sūtra passage that does speak of the ultimate approacher — as when the World-Honored One told Bāhiya, "Were there a bhikṣu doubly liberated, and were I to tell him, 'Come, use your own body to serve as a footbridge for me across this muddy channel' — Bāhiya, listen, what do you think? Upon hearing my command, would he refuse? When about to step upon him, would he draw back? When actually being trodden upon, would he flinch?" Bāhiya answered, "He would not." The World-Honored One then told Bāhiya, "Set aside the doubly liberated — were there a bhikṣu liberated through wisdom alone, were I to tell him," and so on in full; "set aside the wisdom-liberated, and speak of the body-witness; set aside the body-witness, and speak of the view-attainer; set aside the view-attainer, and speak of one resolved through faith; set aside one resolved through faith, and speak of one following the Dharma; set aside one following the Dharma, and speak of one following faith" — the Buddha asking, and Bāhiya answering, each in turn just as before. Some say: this sūtra, too, speaks only of the conventional approacher to the fruit of stream-entry, since one in the very midst of the path of seeing is incapable of hearing and receiving the Buddha's own spoken meaning. The verdict: it is better to say this passage speaks of the ultimate approacher, since it speaks of one following faith and one following the Dharma specifically. Question: while abiding in the path of seeing, one is incapable of receiving anything, the Buddha's own spoken meaning included, since one's mind admits of no other object at that time — how, then, could the World-Honored One address him with such words? Answer: the World-Honored One spoke according to that person's own aspiration, there being no actual instance of this occurring, spoken only for the sake of analysis — for, were one within the path of seeing to have a differently-directed mind capable of receiving the Tathāgata's own spoken meaning, he would necessarily have already left the path of seeing to carry out what was commanded. The Buddha's own intent was to rebuke Bāhiya, saying,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rebuke of Bāhiya works by a striking rhetorical device: rather than describing what a true path-of-seeing practitioner's obedience would look like, the Buddha instead asks Bāhiya to imagine himself commanded to serve as a literal human footbridge, and to judge for himself whether such a person would flinch — turning an abstract question about spiritual attainment into a vivid, almost uncomfortable thought experiment about total, unhesitating submission.

2. Why Ugra Gave with an Equal Heart, Regardless What the Deva Revealed

A rich cascade of reasons is offered for why the householder maintained equanimity in giving despite the deity's revelations: the whole assembly was gathered by his own single summons and so deserved equal treatment; all recipients equally have their hunger relieved; his intent was only to benefit others, never himself; partiality risks breeding attachment or aversion that would undermine his own purity as a donor; he followed the Buddha's own teaching on the five faults of favoring a single recipient; he expected no personal reward since any fruit would ripen only in a realm he hoped to leave; he revered renunciants for relinquishing what he himself, though advanced in practice, still could not; he valued the shared outward form all monastics present to the world regardless of inner virtue; he felt gratitude toward the Dharma as a whole; and he wished to demonstrate that his own settled wisdom could not be swayed by a deity's revelation.

住見道中尚從我命。況汝遠離一切功德。而於我所生違逆心。是故此中說勝義向問長者何故雖聞天語。猶於僧中平等心施。有說。僧眾皆是長者一揵椎聲所召集故。彼作是念。此皆是我一揵椎聲之所召集。無宜於此不等心施。有說。僧眾受此飲食皆除飢渴無差別故。彼作是念。我施飲食為除飢渴。如阿羅漢受我施已飢渴得除。

Question: why did the householder, though he heard the deity's words, still give with an equal heart throughout the assembly? — so too does a fully-fettered ordinary being, receiving my offering, have his removed in just the same way — it would not be fitting to give unequally among them." Others say: the householder's original intent in giving food and drink to the community was solely to benefit others, and he reflected, "I give food and drink in order to benefit others, not to benefit myself —

— the householder gave with an equal heart in order to avoid attachment and aversion, reflecting, "Should I give unequally, some among the community might give rise to attachment or aversion toward me on that account, bringing about an undesired result, and I would thereby become their own enemy — how, then, could I be called a truly pure donor?" Others say: the householder followed the Buddha's own teaching, reflecting, "The Tathāgata has always taught that showing partial devotion and reverence toward a single person carries five faults — and if even one fault ought not to be risked, how much less all five?" Others say: the householder gave without hoping for reward, reflecting, "The ripening-fruit of giving is experienced only within the desire realm — and, should I die and be reborn in the form realm, the fruit of my giving would then bring me no benefit at all; and, even were there benefit, I should not seek it for its own sake, let alone when there is none — and hence I should simply give with an equal heart." Others say: the householder revered renunciants, reflecting, "Though I have become free of the desire realm's defilement and attained the fruit of non-returning, I am still unable to give up my own household relations and possessions — yet renunciants, though they may still be fully fettered, are capable of giving up, relinquishing, and refraining from accumulating household relations and property, undertaking the Buddha's own precepts and practicing them for their entire lives, in pure, complete, undefiled conduct; and, should any of them, through a lapse of mindfulness, violate a precept, they still feel deep shame over it, and constantly aspire to purity —

若在居家不能如是。故我於此應等心施。有說。長者重儀相。故彼作是念。諸出家人剃髮染衣。儀相同佛持戒破戒。俱令世間瞻覩生福。為作福田故我於中應等心施。有說。長者荷佛恩故彼作是念。

— whereas, remaining a householder, I myself am incapable of this — and hence I should give with an equal heart toward them all." Others say: the householder valued the outward form of renunciation, reflecting, "Renunciants, with shaved heads and dyed robes, share an outward form resembling the Buddha's own — and, whether precept-keeping or precept-violating, this same outward form alike causes the world, upon seeing it, to generate merit, serving as a field of merit for all — and hence I should give with an equal heart among them." Others say: the householder felt gratitude for the Buddha's own kindness, reflecting,

我依佛法獲得忍智。金剛杵劍摧破二十身見山峯。斷截無邊惡趣根本。作有邊際定趣涅槃。於四諦中慧眼清淨。盡下分結出欲淤泥。

— "Relying on the Buddha's own Dharma, I have obtained forbearance and wisdom, and, with the vajra club and sword, have shattered the twenty peaks of the mountain of self-view, severed the boundless root of the evil destinies, set a definite limit on my own future existence and set out toward nirvāṇa, purified my wisdom-eye with respect to the four truths, exhausted the lower-portion fetters, and emerged from the mire of desire —

故我不應於佛弟子。心不平等而行惠施。有說。長者顯自所得覺慧堅牢不可轉故。彼作是念。我慧堅牢豈隨天言輕有轉變。故於僧眾施心平等。顯己如是故來白佛。

— and hence I ought not to give with a partial mind toward the Buddha's own disciples." Others say: the householder wished to show that his own attained wisdom was so firm and unshakable that it could not be swayed, reflecting, "My wisdom is firm and steadfast — how could it be lightly overturned by a mere deity's words?" — and it was in order to display this steadfastness in his own giving toward the community that he came to tell the Buddha of this matter.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sheer number of independently sufficient reasons offered — ten or more, none contradicting the others — for a single, simple act of equanimous giving suggests the treatise finds something genuinely overdetermined and admirable in Ugra's response: rather than settling on the one true motive, it treats his equanimity as robust enough to survive being explained a dozen different ways, each account illuminating a different facet of what made his gift exemplary.

3. Two Gifts, One Fruit: Sujātā's Milk-Porridge and Cunda's Final Meal

A related sūtra passage teaches that two specific acts of giving food yield exactly equal, undifferentiated merit: the milk-porridge Sujātā and her sister offered the bodhisattva just before his awakening, and Cunda's final meal offered to the Buddha just before his parinirvāṇa — despite the first recipient still harboring greed, hatred, and delusion, and the second having already exhausted them entirely. The resolution locates the equalizing factor differently in each case: the sisters' own exalted intention compensated for an as-yet-unawakened field, while the supreme excellence of the field itself — the Buddha on the verge of final nirvāṇa — compensated for Cunda's own grief-stricken, less exalted state of mind.

Chinese Source

如契經中佛告慶喜。施食有二果無差別。一者菩薩受彼食已。證得無上正等菩提。二者如來受彼食已。入於無餘大涅槃界。問初受食者有貪瞋癡。後受食者貪瞋癡盡。何緣施果無差別耶。答由思及田有偏勝故。佛依偏勝說果無差。謂初難陀難陀跋羅姊妹二人。聞說菩薩受十六轉甘味乳糜。必當得成無上等覺。歡喜踊躍發殊勝思。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the sūtra says: the Buddha told Ānanda that two acts of giving food yield equal, undifferentiated fruit — first, when the bodhisattva, having received that food, went on to realize unsurpassed, perfect awakening; and second, when the Tathāgata, having received that food, entered the great realm of nirvāṇa without remainder. Question: the first recipient still had greed, hatred, and delusion, while the second recipient had already exhausted them — why, then, is the resulting fruit of giving undifferentiated? Answer: it is on account of the excellence of either the donor's intention or the field, whichever predominates in each case — the Buddha speaks of the undifferentiated fruit with reference to whichever of these two predominates. As to the first: the two sisters, Nandā and Nandabalā, having heard that the bodhisattva, upon receiving sixteen portions of sweet milk-porridge, would certainly attain unsurpassed, perfect awakening, were filled with joy and gave rise to an especially exalted intention,

持上乳糜奉施菩薩。菩薩食已即於是夜。降伏魔軍成等正覺。女聞倍喜更起勝思。彼所施田雖非殊勝。由思勝故能招勝果。准陀於佛將涅槃時。見佛身形少如衰變。又聞不久必入涅槃。戀慕不堪其心擾亂。殊勝思願不能現前。然由勝田能招勝果。佛依此故說無差別。有作是說。欲遮准陀變悔心故。如彼經說。佛告阿難。若彼准陀工巧之子。或他所引或自尋思。於施食中而生變悔。於難得事便為不得。難得事者所謂諸佛將涅槃時最後供養。彼若生變悔者。汝便應以六處而勸喻之。謂施食因緣能招長壽。色力樂譽富貴臣僚。

— and offered the milk-porridge to the bodhisattva. The bodhisattva, having eaten it, that very night subdued Māra's army and achieved perfect awakening — and, upon hearing this, the sisters rejoiced all the more, giving rise to an even more exalted intention. Though the field they gave to was not, at that moment, itself supreme, it was on account of their exalted intention that they obtained a supreme fruit. As to the second: Cunda, at the time the Buddha was about to enter nirvāṇa, saw the Buddha's own body showing slight signs of decline, and, hearing that he would soon enter nirvāṇa, was so overcome with longing and grief that his mind became disturbed, unable to summon an equally exalted intention — yet it was on account of the supreme field itself that he obtained a supreme fruit. It is with reference to this that the Buddha speaks of the undifferentiated fruit. Some say: this was spoken in order to forestall any regret arising in Cunda's own mind — as that sūtra says, the Buddha told Ānanda, "Should this Cunda, the craftsman's son, whether prompted by another or reflecting on his own, come to feel regret over his own act of giving food, thinking that he had failed to make the most of a rare and precious opportunity" — such a rare opportunity being the final offering made just before a buddha's own entry into nirvāṇa — "then, should he feel such regret, you should console him with six considerations: that the causes and conditions of giving food are capable of bringing about long life, beauty, strength, happiness, renown, wealth, and eminent office —

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Locating the source of equal merit in different places for each gift — donor intention in one case, recipient excellence in the other — quietly resolves what looks like a troubling asymmetry: rather than requiring both factors to be equally present in every meritorious gift, the treatise treats intention and field as two independent paths to the same result, either one alone sufficient to reach the ultimate ceiling of merit when the other happens to fall short.

4. A Cascade of Equivalences: Crossing the Same River Twice, By Two Different Roads

An extraordinary list of paired metaphors explains, from every possible angle, why the two gifts are equal: at the first reception he attained the Dharma, at the second he made use of it; at the first he crossed the river of affliction, at the second the river of birth-and-death; at the first he dried up affliction's ocean, at the second birth-and-death's ocean — and so on through uprooting each one's tree, breaking each one's mountain, transcending each one's floods, yokes, clingings, bonds, and hindrances, and finally defeating, at the first reception, the Māras of affliction and of the sovereign gods, and, at the second, the Māras of the aggregates and of death itself.

Chinese Source

我從世尊親聞是事。施食有二果無差別。一者菩薩受彼食已。證得無上正等菩提。二者如來受彼食已。入於無餘大涅槃界。復應告彼准陀。當知於施食中。若生變悔汝於如是難得事中便為不得。如失菩薩將成佛。時奉施乳糜。所生勝福慎莫變悔。由此故言二果無別。有說。二時俱能資益離染身故。謂食於消化時能作食事。佛於後夜成正覺時。彼食消化如成正覺。涅槃亦爾。故說二施果無差別。有說。初受食已證得佛法。後受食已受用佛法。得修習修說亦如是。有說。初受食已便入一切靜慮解脫等持等至。後受食已亦入一切靜慮解脫等持等至。有說。初受食已渡煩惱河。後受食已渡生死河。有說。初受食已涸煩惱海。後受食已涸生死海。如是拔煩惱樹。拔生死樹。破煩惱山。破生死山。越煩惱依。越生死依。說亦如是。有說。初受食已棄捨集諦。後受食已棄捨苦諦。有說。初受食已證入道諦。後受食已證入滅諦。有說。初受食已捨集入道。後受食已捨苦入滅。有說。初受食已超越諸漏。後受食已超順漏法。

Ru-Yi Translation

— and that I myself have personally heard this from the World-Honored One: two acts of giving food yield equal, undifferentiated fruit — first, when the bodhisattva, having received that food, went on to realize unsurpassed, perfect awakening; and second, when the Tathāgata, having received that food, entered the great realm of nirvāṇa without remainder. You should further tell Cunda that, should he feel regret over his own act of giving food, he would thereby fail to make the most of just such a rare and precious opportunity — like the milk-porridge offered to the bodhisattva just before he became a buddha, this excellent merit he has generated should never be regretted, for this very reason it is said that the two fruits are undifferentiated." Others say: both moments alike sustain a body about to become free of defilement, since eating accomplishes the function of nutriment precisely at the moment of digestion — and, just as the Buddha achieved perfect awakening in the latter part of that night, that food's own digestion coincided with his awakening; and it was likewise so with his nirvāṇa — and hence the two acts of giving food are said to yield undifferentiated fruit. 5. A New Chapter Opens: Are Conditions Even Real? Refuting the Analogists

An entirely new topic begins: the Condition Nyāsa, composed specifically to refute the Dṛṣṭāntika masters, who held that "condition" itself has no real, independent existence — citing the sūtra's own claim that a single, uniform ignorance produces volitional formations of endlessly varied character as proof that causal relations are merely conventional labels. The treatise's refutation is sweeping: if conditions weren't real, nothing conditioned would be real either; dependent origination's own profundity, surpassing the four great oceans, would collapse into shallow triviality; the three distinct paths to buddhahood, pratyekabuddhahood, and śrāvakahood would make no sense; and no teacher could ever genuinely improve a disciple's wisdom from lower to higher, since nothing real would exist to drive that growth.

Answer: because the great elements' own functional strength is coarse and evident, its increase or diminishment easy to discern, and hence it is spoken of in this way — mind and mental factors are not like this, and it is on this account that the great elements are not called "conjoined."

又心心所法皆有所緣。四大種無所緣。非無所緣法可說相應。所以者何。心心所法。有所依。有行相。有發悟故。必有所緣。有所緣故恒相應不相離。四大種與此相違。故無所緣無所緣故。雖不相離而不相應。不相離有二種。一大種不相離共造一色故。二心心所法不相離共緣一境故。五蘊雖復同在一身。無此二事非不相離。是故大種不說相應。

Moreover, mind and mental factors always have an object, whereas the four great elements have no object at all — and a dharma lacking an object can never properly be called "conjoined," for why is this? Mind and mental factors have a basis, a mode of apprehension, and a capacity for awakening awareness, and hence necessarily have an object — and, having an object, they are constantly conjoined and never separate. The great elements are the opposite of this, having no object at all — and, lacking an object, though inseparable, they are not called conjoined. "Inseparable" is of two kinds — first, the great elements are inseparable in that they jointly produce a single instance of form; second, mind and mental factors are inseparable in that they jointly take a single object. Though the five aggregates alike occupy a single body, without these two specific relations, this alone does not make them inseparable in the relevant sense — and it is for this reason that the great elements are not called "conjoined."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Locating the crucial difference in whether relative quantity can vary — the elements can, mind and its factors cannot — turns what might seem like an arbitrary terminological distinction into a genuine structural fact about two different kinds of togetherness: material elements combine like ingredients in variable proportion, while a single moment of mental experience combines like a fixed, non-negotiable ensemble, each part occurring exactly once.

13. Proving the Four Are Never Separated: A Function-by-Function Demonstration

A rigorous demonstration proves the four elements' permanent inseparability by showing that even within a mass dominated by one element, the other three remain functionally indispensable: within a solid mass, water is still needed to melt metal and hold the mass together, fire to spark and ripen it, wind to move and grow it; the same fourfold demonstration is then run in full for liquid, warm, and moving masses, each time showing that removing any one of the supposedly "absent" three elements would make some observed function impossible.

Chinese Source

問云何得知此四大種恒不相離。答自相作業一切聚中皆可得故。謂堅聚中地界自相現可得故有義極成。於此聚中若無水界。金銀錫等應不可銷。又水若無彼應分散。若無火界石等相擊火不應生。又火若無無能成熟彼應腐敗。若無風界應無動搖。又若無風應無增長。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: how is it known that the four great elements are always inseparable from one another? Answer: because each element's own characteristic and function can be found within every kind of aggregate mass. That is, within a solid mass, earth's own characteristic is directly evident, and this point is beyond dispute; yet, within this same mass, were there no water-element, gold, silver, tin, and the like could never be melted down at all, and, without water, the mass would simply scatter apart; were there no fire-element, striking stones together would produce no fire at all, and, without fire, nothing could ripen to maturity but would simply rot and decay; were there no wind-element, there would be no motion at all, and, without wind, there would be no growth at all.

於濕聚中水界自相現可得故有義極成。於此聚中若無地界至嚴寒位應不成氷。又地若無船等應沒。若無火界應無煖時。又火若無彼應腐敗。若無風界應不動搖。又風若無應無增長。於煖聚中火界自相現可得故有義極成。於此聚中若無地界燈燭等焰應不可迴。又地若無不應持物。若無水界應不生流。又水若無焰不應聚。若無風界應不動搖。又若無風應無增長。於動聚中風界自相現可得故有義極成。於此聚中若無地界觸牆等障應不折迴。又地若無應不持物。若無水界應無冷風。又水若無彼應分散。若無火界應無煖風。又火若無彼應腐敗。問此四大種其相各異展轉相違。云何一時不相離起。

Within a liquid mass, water's own characteristic is directly evident, and this point is beyond dispute; yet, within this same mass, were there no earth-element, ice could never form even in the depths of severe cold, and, without earth, boats and the like ought to sink; were there no fire-element, there would be no warm season at all, and, without fire, the mass would simply rot and decay; were there no wind-element, there would be no motion at all, and, without wind, there would be no growth at all. Within a warm mass, fire's own characteristic is directly evident, and this point is beyond dispute; yet, within this same mass, were there no earth-element, a lamp's or torch's own flame could never be guided or redirected at all, and, without earth, nothing could be held or supported; were there no water-element, no flowing fuel could ever gather at all, and, without water, the flame could never properly coalesce; were there no wind-element, there would be no motion at all, and, without wind, there would be no growth at all. Within a moving mass, wind's own characteristic is directly evident, and this point is beyond dispute; yet, within this same mass, were there no earth-element, an obstruction such as a wall could never turn the wind's own motion back at all, and, without earth, nothing could be held or supported; were there no water-element, there would be no cool breeze at all, and, without water, the mass would simply scatter apart; were there no fire-element, there would be no warm breeze at all, and, without fire, it would simply rot and decay. Question: these four great elements each bear mutually different, seemingly contrary characteristics — how, then, can they arise together, at one and the same time, remaining forever inseparable?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Running the identical fourfold proof four separate times — once for each dominant element — rather than stating the general principle once and trusting the reader to generalize, reflects a deep methodological commitment: a claim this important, that the four elements can never be separated under any circumstance, is not allowed to rest on a single demonstrated case extended by analogy, but must be independently verified in every one of the four possible dominant configurations.

14. Different, But Not Opposed: Vasumitra's Final Distinction

The fascicle closes on an elegant philosophical resolution from the Venerable Vasumitra: having different characteristics and being genuinely mutually contrary are two entirely separate matters, not one implying the other. Dharmas that differ in characteristic yet are not contrary — like the four elements themselves, or the various colors and derived sense-qualities — can arise together in permanent, inseparable unity; but dharmas that are also genuinely contrary — firewood and fire, hail and a ripening crop, the eclipse-demon Rāhu and the full moon, medicine and disease, light and darkness — can never coexist at the same time in the same place.

Chinese Source

尊者世友作如是說言。異相相違因緣各別。非諸相異皆必相違。諸不相違而相異者。容俱時起互不相離。如四大種及香味觸青黃色等。諸有異相而互相違。必無一時不相離起。如薪與火。雹與稼穡。邏呼月輪。藥病明闇。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Venerable Vasumitra explains it this way: having different characteristics and being mutually contrary are two entirely separate causal matters, and not every pair of dharmas differing in characteristic is thereby necessarily mutually contrary as well. There are dharmas which, though differing in characteristic, are not mutually contrary, and such dharmas may indeed arise together at one and the same time, remaining forever inseparable — as with the four great elements themselves, together with smell, taste, touch, and the colors blue, yellow, and the rest. But there are also dharmas which, differing in characteristic, are also genuinely mutually contrary — and such dharmas can never, at one and the same time, arise together remaining inseparable — as with firewood and fire, hail and a ripening crop, Rāhu and the full disk of the moon, medicine and disease, and light and darkness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Vasumitra's distinction does real philosophical work beyond this immediate puzzle: by denying that mere difference automatically implies exclusion, it opens conceptual space for exactly the kind of stable, permanent diversity-in-unity the four great elements themselves exhibit — showing that apparent tension between distinctness and coexistence was never a logical necessity to begin with, only a habit of thought the treatise here explicitly corrects.
