The Leprous Insect's Own Blindness

Fascicle 47 continues the Unwholesome Nyāsa with the three unwholesome roots — greed, hatred, and delusion — defined through five precise conditions no rival affliction fully satisfies, before turning to the three contaminants and their own six hidden etymological meanings. The fascicle traces an unbroken causal chain from ignorance's own initial failure to delight in the four truths, through doubt and wrong view, all the way to pride, and closes with a careful reconciliation of scripture's varying counts of three, seven, and even two contaminants. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Three Unwholesome Roots Open

Fascicle 47 continues the Unwholesome Nyāsa with its own foundational triad: the unwholesome roots of greed, hatred, and delusion, defined technically as fifteen distinct factors spanning the desire realm's five sections. A precise puzzle opens immediately: since ignorance associated with self-view and the extreme views is itself the cause of every unwholesome dharma, why isn't it counted among these roots too? The answer draws a sharp line between being a cause of the unwholesome and being unwholesome oneself — only a dharma unwholesome in its own substance, capable of causing every unwholesome dharma, earns the title "root."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The exclusion of self-view-associated ignorance from the unwholesome roots despite its own causal reach is a precise piece of definitional discipline: causing unwholesome effects and being unwholesome oneself turn out to be logically independent properties, and this treatise refuses to let the first stand in for the second, however tempting the shortcut might otherwise be.

2. Eight Reasons These Three Stand Supreme

A cascade of reasons accumulates for why greed, hatred, and delusion specifically earn root-status among every unwholesome dharma — they are weightiest and most proximate in causal power, most difficult to cut off, carriers of the heaviest faults, the direct enemies of the three wholesome roots, the fiercest obstacles at the threshold of freedom from desire, and, like a valiant general at the front of an army, the very force by which every other unwholesome dharma is able to grow and flourish.

3. Five Conditions No Other Dharma Fully Satisfies

The treatise articulates nine synonym-pairs for what "root" itself means — cause, seed, guide, gathering-point, foundation — before settling into the real technical payload: five precise conditions that only greed, hatred, and delusion satisfy together. Each must span all five sections of the path, pervade all six consciousnesses, be of the very nature of a latent tendency, be capable of drawing forth coarse bodily and verbal karma, and serve as the driving, applied effort behind the catastrophic act of cutting off one's own wholesome roots entirely.

4. The Scriptural Proof Texts for the Final Two Conditions

5. Why Wrong View Is Excluded Despite Also Cutting Off the Wholesome Roots

A genuine puzzle follows: overreaching wrong view is likewise capable of severing one's own wholesome roots entirely — why, then, is it not itself an unwholesome root? The treatise's answer distinguishes the difficult work of applied effort from the comparatively easy moment of actual culmination, illustrated through an unexpectedly moving comparison: exactly as a bodhisattva's very first arising of the aspiration for enlightenment is the hardest moment of the entire path, with everything that follows across three incalculable eons proceeding with comparative ease, so too is the applied effort leading up to cutting off the wholesome roots the genuinely difficult phase, with wrong view supreme only at the easy, culminating instant.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Reaching for the bodhisattva's own first aspiration toward enlightenment as a comparison for the psychology of cutting off one's own wholesome roots is a striking rhetorical choice — using the single most exalted moment in this tradition's entire narrative to illuminate one of its darkest — and it works precisely because both share the same underlying structural insight: in any transformation, momentous or catastrophic, the threshold moment of committed effort is where the real difficulty lies, not the eventual completion that follows from it.

6. A Systematic Elimination of Every Rival Candidate

The treatise completes its reasoning about wrong view's exclusion, then turns to a comprehensive sweep, checking every other unwholesome dharma against the same five conditions in turn — material form, feeling, perception, consciousness, dissociated formations, the five views, doubt, conceit, and each of the ten entanglements — showing, one by one, exactly which condition each candidate fails to satisfy, so that only greed, hatred, and delusion ever pass the complete test.

16. Why Karma Isn't a Contaminant, and Why Desire, Existence, and Ignorance Get Their Own Categories

A genuine puzzle opens: karma, too, is capable of detaining beings within birth and death for long spans of time — some who cut off every affliction at only eight or ten years old nonetheless live on to ninety or a hundred through karma's own residual force alone — so why is only affliction, never karma, called a contaminant? The treatise answers that karma's own detaining power is never fixed the way affliction's is, and that karma itself is rooted in affliction rather than the reverse, illustrated through a dried clay lump thrown against a wall, still clinging by nothing more than the residual moisture of its own earlier dampness. It then explains why afflictions sort into exactly three named contaminants: desire-realm beings dwell there because their minds are set on desire; form- and formless-realm beings dwell there because their minds are set on existence itself; and it is, in every case, underlying ignorance's own power that causes any being to set its mind on either desire or existence in the first place — which is why ignorance alone, spanning all three realms, earns its own separate, third category.

25. The Great Virtuous One's Reading, and Reconciling Three Contaminants With Seven

The Great Virtuous One offers a final variant: within any single existence, every affliction matching a given being's own stage is already fully present in equal measure from the very start, with "redoubling" describing not any change in the underlying inventory but simply how frequently those same afflictions manifest before the mind — improper attention letting them surface again and again, proper attention with a genuine antidote keeping them dormant. The treatise then reconciles the three contaminants with scripture's own separate list of seven, through Pārśva's audience-calibration explanation and Pūrṇāśa's more technical account distinguishing the contaminant abandoned by seeing from six further gradations of the contaminant abandoned by cultivation.

26. Eight Ways to Explain "Liberated From Three Contaminants" at a Single Moment

The fascicle closes with a final puzzle: scripture describes an arhat's mind as liberated from all three contaminants together at the single moment of attaining arhatship, even though freedom from the desire-contaminant was, in fact, already achieved much earlier, at the point of becoming free from the desire realm's own defilements. Eight distinct explanations follow — perhaps this is simply how scripture speaks of something long-since-accomplished using the language of the present moment, perhaps it marks the instant every remaining contaminant's own causal continuity is finally, decisively severed, or perhaps it names a single, comprehensive weariness toward all three contaminants together that only fully crystallizes at this final, climactic threshold.

One asks: it is at the moment of becoming free from the desire realm's own defilements that the mind is liberated from the desire-contaminant, and it is at the moment of becoming free from the defilements of the very peak of existence that the mind is liberated from the existence-contaminant and the ignorance-contaminant — why, then, does the Buddha state that it is at the moment of attaining the fruit of arhatship, through correct knowledge and correct vision, that the mind is liberated from these three contaminants together? The answer: within this passage, the language of "liberation now" is used even with respect to what has, in fact, already been liberated earlier — this is speaking of what is, in truth, distant, using the language proper to what is near, exactly as one might ask, "where have you come from just now?" of someone who set out long before, or exactly as, in other passages, "already cut off" is spoken of as "cutting off," "already entered" is spoken of as "entering," and "already received" is spoken of as "receiving" — this, too, is exactly the same. Further, this is spoken with reference to the fact that, at that moment, one attains the non-learner's own freedom from their binding nature.
