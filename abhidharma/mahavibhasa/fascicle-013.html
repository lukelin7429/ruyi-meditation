Gold Emerging From a Mountain Peak

Fascicle 13 opens the Knowledge Nyāsa's fifth part (雜蘊第一中智納息第二之五) with a full technical survey of perception itself — does one see with one eye or two, how do the six sense-fields carve up experience, and just how acute were the bodhisattva's own senses — before pivoting to a new question about the metaphysics of time: is everything past also "not manifest"? This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation — the hundred-faceted mirror that explains how widely separated eyes still see as one, the bodhisattva who could count five hundred lamps by their light alone, Udāyin's verse comparing the bathing Buddha to gold emerging from a mountain peak, and the Great Brahmā king's increasingly desperate game of hide-and-seek.

Fascicle 13 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa opened in Fascicle 9, now in its fifth part (之五). Terms established in earlier fascicles, such as the five aggregates of clinging and the immediately-antecedent condition, recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Do We See With One Eye or Two? The Rival Positions

Fascicle 13 opens the Knowledge Nyāsa's fifth part with a deceptively simple question: does one see form with a single eye, or with two? Four rival schools are named and refuted in turn — that eye-consciousness itself sees, that the wisdom associated with it sees, that mere coincidence of conditions sees, or that only one eye ever really does the work — before the treatise raises a genuinely strange case: beings whose two eyes sit a hundred yojanas apart.

Chinese Source

當言一眼見色。二眼見色耶。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為止他宗顯己義故。謂或有執眼識見色。如尊者法救。或復有執眼識相應慧見色。如尊者妙音。或復有執和合見色。如譬喻者。或復有執一眼見色。如犢子部。為止如是他宗異執。顯示己宗二眼見色。故作斯論。所以者何。若眼識見色者。識應有見相。然識無見相故不應理。若眼識相應慧見色者。耳識相應慧亦應聞聲。然慧無聞相故不應理。若和合見色者。應一切時見色。以無時不和合故亦不應理若一眼見色非二眼者。身諸分亦應不俱時。覺觸如身根兩臂相去雖遠而得俱時覺觸生一身識。兩眼亦爾。相去雖遠。何妨俱時見色生一眼識。問若眼見色者。餘識俱時何故不見。又無識時亦應見色。答眼有二種。一者識合。二者識空。識合者能見識空者不能見。故無有失。復次所以作此論者。欲令疑者得決定故。謂諸有情兩眼相去。或半麻一麻半。麥一麥半。指一指半。搩手一搩手半。弓一弓半。俱盧舍一俱盧舍半。踰繕那一踰繕那。或復乃至百踰繕那。如大海中有大身眾生。或長百踰繕那。乃至或長二千一百踰繕那。如曷邏呼。阿素洛王身量極大。又如天趣色究竟身。一萬六千踰繕那量。此等二眼相去甚遠。或有生疑。云何眼識依之而轉。為二眼識。俱時各依一眼生耶。為一眼識。依一眼生已。復依第二眼轉耶。為一眼識分為二分。於二眼處各半生耶。為一眼識。如橫一物通二眼耶。若二眼識俱時各依一眼生者。應一有情二心俱轉此不應理。若一眼識依一眼生已。復依第二眼轉者。則應一法二剎那住然無是事。若一眼識分為二分。於二眼處各半生者。則應一法體有二分。然一切法體無細分。若一眼識如橫一物通二眼者。則應一識亦是眼識亦是身識。二眼中間依身根故。然五識身所依各異所緣各異。不應一識二依二緣。欲令此疑得決定故。顯雖無有二眼識俱。乃至無一識橫通二眼。然其非不依於二眼一眼識生。二眼雖隔百踰繕那亦無有過。如是理趣微細甚深難可覺了。今欲顯斯甚深理趣故作斯論。

Ru-Yi Translation

[The topic reads:] "Should one say that form is seen with one eye, or with two eyes?" — and so on, as stated at length. One asks: why does the treatise take up this topic? The answer: in order to refute a rival school and manifest its own correct meaning. That is, some maintain that eye-consciousness itself sees form — as the Venerable Dharmatrāta holds; others maintain that the wisdom associated with eye-consciousness sees form — as the Venerable Ghoṣa holds; others maintain that it is the coming-together of conditions that sees form — as the Dārṣṭāntikas hold; others maintain that form is seen with a single eye — as the Vātsīputrīyas hold. In order to refute these various rival schools' distinct positions and manifest its own school's own position — that form is seen with two eyes — the treatise is composed. Why is this? Because if eye-consciousness itself saw form, consciousness would then have to possess the very characteristic of seeing, yet consciousness has no such characteristic, and so this is unreasonable. If the wisdom associated with eye-consciousness saw form, then the wisdom associated with ear-consciousness ought equally to hear sound, yet wisdom has no characteristic of hearing, and so this too is unreasonable. If the mere coming-together of conditions saw form, one ought then to see form at all times, since there is never a moment without such a coming-together, and so this too is unreasonable. And if form were seen with a single eye rather than with two, then the various parts of the body ought likewise to be unable to register touch-sensation simultaneously — yet, just as the body faculty's two arms, though far apart from one another, are still able to register touch-sensation simultaneously, giving rise to a single body-consciousness, so too the two eyes, though far apart from one another, are equally able, without contradiction, to see form simultaneously, giving rise to a single eye-consciousness. One asks: if the eye sees form, why is it that the other consciousnesses, arising simultaneously, do not see it as well? And why, at a time when consciousness is altogether absent, should form not still be seen? The answer: the eye is of two kinds — first, joined with consciousness; second, empty of consciousness. What is joined with consciousness is able to see; what is empty of consciousness is not — and so no fault follows. Further, the reason the treatise takes up this topic is in order to bring certainty to those in doubt. That is, sentient beings' two eyes may be separated by as little as half a sesame seed's width, or a whole sesame seed's width and a half, or a barley grain's width and a half, a finger's width and a half, a span's width and a half, a bow's-length and a half, a krośa's-length and a half, or a full yojana, or even, in certain cases, up to a hundred yojanas — just as, within the great ocean, there exist enormous beings whose bodies may measure a hundred yojanas in length, or even, in the case of the asura-king Rāhu, as much as two thousand one hundred yojanas; and just as, in the divine destiny, a body in the Akaniṣṭha heaven may measure sixteen thousand yojanas. For beings such as these, whose two eyes are separated by so vast a distance, a doubt may arise: how, exactly, does eye-consciousness operate in dependence on them? Do two instances of eye-consciousness arise simultaneously, each depending on one eye separately? Or does a single instance of eye-consciousness, having arisen in dependence on one eye, then go on to operate in dependence on the second eye as well? Or does a single instance of eye-consciousness split into two halves, each arising separately at the location of each eye? Or does a single instance of eye-consciousness, like some single object laid crosswise, extend across both eyes at once? If two instances of eye-consciousness arose simultaneously, each depending on one eye separately, then a single sentient being would have two minds operating together at once, which is unreasonable. If a single instance of eye-consciousness, having arisen in dependence on one eye, then went on to operate in dependence on the second eye as well, then a single dharma would have to abide across two moments — yet this never happens. If a single instance of eye-consciousness split into two halves, each arising separately at the location of each eye, then a single dharma's own substance would have to possess two parts — yet the substance of every dharma is without any finer division at all. And if a single instance of eye-consciousness, like some single object laid crosswise, extended across both eyes at once, then a single consciousness would have to be both eye-consciousness and body-consciousness at once, since the space between the two eyes still depends on the body faculty — yet each of the five sense-consciousness bodies has its own distinct support and its own distinct object, and no single consciousness ought to have two supports and two objects at once. In order to bring certainty to this very doubt, the treatise shows that, although there is indeed no case of two instances of eye-consciousness arising together, and so on, up through there being no case of a single consciousness extending crosswise across both eyes, it is nonetheless not the case that a single instance of eye-consciousness fails to arise in dependence on both eyes together — and even should the two eyes be separated by a hundred yojanas, no fault follows. Such a principle is subtle, profound, and exceedingly difficult to fathom, and it is in order to display this very profound principle that the treatise is composed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The hundred-yojana-eyes thought experiment is a wonderful piece of speculative cosmology in service of a hard analytic question: if a single consciousness can arise from two widely separated eyes without splitting into two minds, without existing across two moments, and without literally stretching across the gap like a physical object, what kind of unity does it actually have? The treatise's refusal to accept any of the tidy mechanical pictures (two minds, one mind moving, one mind splitting, one mind stretching) forces it toward a subtler notion of a single consciousness simply arising in joint dependence on multiple supports at once — a notion the mirror-with-a-hundred-facets image in the next section will make vivid.

2. The Verdict: Two Eyes, One Consciousness — The Hundred-Faceted Mirror

The treatise settles the matter: two eyes see, jointly giving rise to a single consciousness — illustrated by a mirror-device with a hundred small facets, each reflecting the same face at once, and confirmed empirically by the plain difference in clarity between one-eyed and two-eyed vision.

Chinese Source

當言一眼見色二眼見色耶。答應言二眼見色。問云何二眼相去甚遠。一識依之令俱見色。答俱是眼識所依根故。設有百眼一一相去百踰繕那。亦可依之生於一識。令俱時見。如迦遮器有百小輪。一面對之百面像現。如是一識依多眼生。令俱時見其義亦爾。應知此中眼見色者。遮法救等三種異執。二眼見者。遮犢子部。一眼見色。所以者何。若合一眼起不淨識。開二眼時起淨識故。設合一眼起如是識。開二眼時亦起此識。則不應言二眼見色。然合一眼起不淨識。開二眼時便起淨識。是故應言二眼見色。非但立宗義即成立。故復問答。顯示此因起淨識者。謂於多境明白顯了。與此相違名不淨識。如合覆損破壞亦爾。覆謂以手以衣以葉以餘物覆。損謂垢烟塵等所損。破謂諸膜諸瞖所破。壞謂枯爛挑出自脫虫食等壞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should one say that form is seen with one eye, or with two? The answer: it should be said that form is seen with two eyes. One asks: how, then, can the two eyes, separated by so great a distance, still give rise to a single consciousness that sees form together with both? The answer: because both are alike a support for eye-consciousness. Even if there were a hundred eyes, each separated from the next by a hundred yojanas, they too could still, in dependence on all of them, give rise to a single consciousness, allowing simultaneous seeing — just as a mirror-device might have a hundred small facets, and, when a single face is presented before it, a hundred reflected images appear at once. In just the same way, a single consciousness, arising in dependence on many eyes, allows simultaneous seeing — the meaning is exactly the same. It should be understood that here, "the eye sees form" refutes the three distinct positions of Dharmatrāta and the others, while "two eyes see" refutes the Vātsīputrīyas' position that form is seen with a single eye. Why is this? Because closing one eye gives rise to an impure cognition, while opening both eyes gives rise to a pure cognition — and were closing one eye to give rise to this very same cognition, and opening both eyes to give rise to this very same cognition as well, then one ought not to say that form is seen with two eyes at all. But since closing one eye gives rise to an impure cognition, while opening both eyes gives rise to a pure cognition, it should therefore be said that form is seen with two eyes — this is not a claim established merely by asserting the thesis, and so the question-and-answer format is used to establish it further. As for what displays this reason for a pure cognition arising: it is that, with respect to many objects, seeing is clear and fully manifest; the reverse of this is called an "impure" cognition — and the same holds for what is closed off, covered, impaired, damaged, or destroyed. "Covered" means covered by a hand, by clothing, by a leaf, or by some other object; "impaired" means impaired by dirt, smoke, dust, and the like; "damaged" means damaged by membranes or cataracts; "destroyed" means destroyed by withering and decay, by being gouged out, by falling out on its own, by being eaten by insects, and the like.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The mirror-with-a-hundred-facets analogy elegantly generalizes past the original two-eye puzzle to the thought experiment's own hundred-eyed extreme, showing the same principle scales without strain: a single consciousness is not stitched together from fragments contributed by each eye, but arises whole, in dependence on however many qualifying supports happen to be present — the closing/opening-eye experiment is offered as direct, testable evidence, a nice example of this treatise reaching for an empirical check rather than resting on pure argument alone.

3. Pure and Impure Seeing: Four Combinations of Eye and Cognition

A finer distinction follows, worked out in a careful four-alternative grid: the health of the eye itself and the moral quality of the resulting cognition are shown to vary independently — a damaged eye can still give rise to a wholesome cognition, and a healthy eye can still give rise to a defiled one.

Chinese Source

覆等如合起不淨識。與此相違便起淨識。此隨所依說淨不淨依世俗理。若依勝義。善識名淨染名不淨。由此對眼應作四句。有眼淨識不淨。謂依具眼起染眼識。有識淨眼不淨。謂依不具眼起善眼識。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being covered and the like, just like closing one eye, give rise to an impure cognition, while the reverse gives rise to a pure cognition — this is spoken of "purity" and "impurity" with reference to the support, by worldly principle. If spoken with reference to ultimate truth, a wholesome cognition is called "pure," and a defiled one "impure." On this basis, four alternatives should be formulated with respect to the eye: there is a case of a pure eye but an impure cognition — namely, a defiled eye-consciousness arising in dependence on a complete, healthy eye. There is a case of a pure cognition but an impure eye — namely, a wholesome eye-consciousness arising in dependence on an incomplete, damaged eye.

有眼識俱淨。謂依具眼起善眼識。有眼識俱不淨。謂依不具眼。起染眼識。如眼見色耳聞聲鼻嗅香亦爾。俱有二處與眼同故。問何故眼耳鼻各有二處。而舌身唯有一耶。答諸色根處為莊嚴身。若有二舌是鄙陋事。世便嗤笑。云何此人。而有二舌如似毒蛇。若有二身亦是鄙陋。世所嗤笑。云何一人。而有二身如兩指並。問眼耳鼻處何故唯二不增不減。脇尊者言。一切生疑故不應責。謂若增減亦復生疑。云何此三各唯爾所。然各二處不違法性。有說。根處為莊嚴身。若減若增身便醜陋。有說。色根為生淨識。若當三識依二處生則明不亂。增便識亂減則不明。有說。色根為取自境各唯有二。取境事足減則不明增便無用。問何故二眼二耳二鼻。合立一界一處一根。答作用一故。謂雖有二處而共發一識共取一境。如身眾分處所雖多作用同故。但立一界一處一根此亦如是。

There is a case of both the eye and the cognition being pure — namely, a wholesome eye-consciousness arising in dependence on a complete, healthy eye. There is a case of both the eye and the cognition being impure — namely, a defiled eye-consciousness arising in dependence on an incomplete, damaged eye. And just as the eye sees form, the ear hears sound and the nose smells fragrance in exactly the same way, both alike having two locations just as the eye does. One asks: why is it that the eye, ear, and nose each have two locations, while the tongue and body faculty have only one? The answer: the various material faculties are located where they are in order to adorn the body — if a person had two tongues, this would be a repulsive thing, and the world would surely mock him, saying "how is it that this person has two tongues, like a venomous serpent?" And if a person had two bodies, this too would be repulsive, and the world would mock him, saying "how is it that a single person has two bodies, like two fingers placed side by side?" One asks: why do the locations of the eye, ear, and nose number exactly two — neither more nor fewer? The Venerable Pārśva said: this would give rise to doubt in every case, and so ought not to be pressed — that is, were the number increased or decreased, doubt would equally arise as to why these three should each be limited to that particular number instead; yet each having exactly two locations does not contradict the nature of dharmas. Some say: the faculties are located where they are in order to adorn the body, and either fewer or more would make the body appear ugly. Some say: the material faculties exist in order to give rise to a clear cognition — were three consciousnesses to arise in dependence on two locations each, clarity would remain unconfused; more locations would confuse the cognition, and fewer would leave it unclear. Some say: the material faculties exist in order to apprehend their own respective object, and each requires exactly two locations to do so adequately — fewer would leave apprehension unclear, and more would serve no further purpose. One asks: why are the two eyes, two ears, and two nostrils each established as a single element, a single sense-field, and a single faculty? The answer: because their function is one and the same — that is, although there are two locations, they together give rise to a single consciousness and together apprehend a single object, just as, although the body has many parts and locations, since its function is the same throughout, it too is established as a single element, a single sense-field, and a single faculty — the case here is exactly the same.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This four-alternative grid quietly does important philosophical work: it separates a purely physiological question (is the organ intact?) from a purely ethical one (is the resulting mental state wholesome or defiled?), refusing to let either one collapse into the other — a person with perfect eyesight can still look with a covetous mind, and a person with damaged eyesight can still look with a generous one, and this treatise wants both facts on record as genuinely independent variables.

4. Nourished Eyes and Ripening Eyes: Four Grades Across a Lifetime

The eye is shown to be composite in yet another sense: a “nourished” component built up through ongoing bodily sustenance, and a “ripening-produced” component inherited directly from past karma — their relative strength shifting predictably across a single lifetime (youth favors the nourished, old age the ripening-produced) and across different lives (wealth favors nourishment, poverty favors karmic inheritance).

Chinese Source

眼有二種謂長養異熟生。無別等流不可得故。問頗唯有長養眼無異熟生。或唯有異熟生眼無長養耶。答無異熟生眼離長養眼。如人重人如牆重牆長養。防護異熟亦爾。然有長養眼離異熟生眼如從無眼得天眼者。問為長養眼見色多為異熟生耶。答長養眼見色多非異熟生。以天眼根是長養故。然約時分二眼勝劣應作四句。有長養眼勝非異熟生。如幼年時爾時異熟相續小故。有異熟生眼勝非長養眼。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eye is of two kinds: nourished and ripening-produced; there is no separate homogeneous-outflow variety to be found. One asks: is there ever a case of a purely nourished eye lacking any ripening-produced component, or a purely ripening-produced eye lacking any nourished component? The answer: there is no case of a ripening-produced eye apart from a nourished eye — just as a person relies upon another person, or a wall relies upon another wall, for support, nourishment likewise safeguards ripening in this way. There is, however, a case of a nourished eye apart from any ripening-produced eye — as with one who attains the divine eye starting from having no eye at all. One asks: does the nourished eye see more form, or does the ripening-produced eye? The answer: the nourished eye sees more form than the ripening-produced eye, since the divine eye faculty itself is nourished. Yet, with respect to different periods of time, the relative superiority of the two eyes should be formulated in four alternatives: there is a case of the nourished eye being superior, not the ripening-produced — as in one's youth, when the ripening-produced continuum is still slight; there is a case of the ripening-produced eye being superior, not the nourished —

如老病時爾時長養相續小故。有二眼俱勝如盛年時。有二眼俱不勝謂除前位。約有情相續二眼勝劣亦應作四句。有長養眼勝非異熟生。如有富貴者。異熟生眼劣資緣多故長養眼勝。有異熟生眼勝非長養眼。如有貧賤者。異熟生眼勝乏資緣故長養眼劣。有二眼俱勝。如有富貴者異熟生眼勝。資緣多故長養亦勝。有二眼俱不勝。

as in old age or sickness, when the nourished continuum is slight; there is a case of both eyes being superior — as in the prime of life; and there is a case of neither being superior — namely, apart from the stages just described. With respect to a given sentient being's own continuum, the relative superiority of the two eyes should likewise be formulated in four alternatives: there is a case of the nourished eye being superior, not the ripening-produced — as with one who is wealthy and noble, whose ripening-produced eye is inferior yet whose nourished eye is superior, on account of abundant supporting conditions. There is a case of the ripening-produced eye being superior, not the nourished — as with one who is poor and lowly, whose ripening-produced eye is superior yet whose nourished eye is inferior, on account of a lack of supporting conditions. There is a case of both eyes being superior — as with one who is wealthy and noble and whose ripening-produced eye is also superior, so that, with abundant supporting conditions, the nourished eye is superior as well. There is a case of neither eye being superior —

如有貧賤者。異熟生眼劣。乏資緣故長養亦劣。又長養眼有二種。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The wealth/poverty asymmetry here is a genuinely pointed piece of social observation dressed in technical vocabulary: a person of means can, through good nutrition and care, build up a nourished eye that outperforms whatever their karmic inheritance alone would have given them, while a person without such resources is left relying on karmic ripening unsupplemented by nourishment — the treatise is quietly noting that material circumstance measurably shapes even something as seemingly fixed as sensory acuity, layered on top of, but distinct from, karmic desert.

5. Wholesome and Unwholesome Roots of Sight, and Six Kinds of Mind and Form

The nourished/ripening-produced distinction is refined once more by moral cause: an eye nourished by wholesome states sees better than one nourished by unwholesome ones, and — in a pointed exception — an eye ripening from unwholesome karma (a nāga-king's) can still outperform an eye ripening from wholesome karma (an ordinary human's), before the fascicle catalogues the full range of varieties across mind, form, sound, and dharmas.
