The Seven Sage-Destinies

Fascicle 175 continues the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之二) with the seven sage-destinies (七善士趣), explaining why they are organized by superior advancement rather than realm, ground, or faculty, why the sūtra illustrates only some of them with similes, and why the stream-enterer and once-returner never qualify despite already being called "sages." The fascicle's second half opens the mixed cultivation of dhyāna (雜修靜慮), the practice underlying rebirth into the five Pure Abodes, detailing its fifteen-mind-moment mechanics and closing with a debate over why the Pure Abodes are exactly five. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Seven Sages: Not by Realm, Ground, or Faculty, but by Advancement

Fascicle 175 continues the Non-Returner Nyāsa with the seven sage-destinies (七善士趣) — the five types of non-returner from fascicle 174, with the arising and non-arising categories each further split into three grades, and the upstream type standing alone as a seventh. The treatise rejects three plausible organizing principles in turn (realm and fruit, ground and station, faculty and affliction) before settling on the true basis: the arising and non-arising types are subdivided identically by no fewer than eight independent criteria — speed of path, faculty, path-grade, karma-grade, effort paired with wisdom, constancy of effort, degree of perceived saṃsāric fault, and grade of calm and insight — with the upstream type left as a single category precisely because its own three sub-kinds already distinguish themselves clearly enough on their own.

As a sūtra states: the Buddha told the monks, "there are seven sage-destinies, capable of progressing to sever the remaining fetters and attain parinirvāṇa." Question: on what basis are the seven sage-destinies established — is it by realm and ascetic fruit, by ground and station, or by faculty and affliction-grade? And, granting any of these, what fault would follow? If established by realm and ascetic fruit, then only a single one ought to be spoken of — namely, the non-returner who proceeds through the form realm. If established by ground and station, then only four, or else sixteen, ought to be spoken of. If established by faculty and affliction-grade, then nine ought to be spoken of, since each of these has nine grades of its own. Answer: none of these three grounds is what establishes them — rather, the seven kinds are established solely on the basis of superior advancement within the categories of arising and non-arising. Whatever basis establishes the first grade of the arising type establishes the first grade of the non-arising type as well; whatever basis establishes the second grade of the arising type establishes the second grade of the non-arising type as well; and whatever basis establishes the third grade of the arising type establishes the third grade of the non-arising type as well — and then, furthermore, since the meaning of superior advancement is itself most excellent in the upstream type, it is established as a single seventh category on its own, and it is on this basis that the seven sage-destinies are established. That is, among the arising type, there is one who possesses both the diligently-cultivated path and the swiftly-advancing path; one who possesses the diligently-cultivated path but not the swiftly-advancing path; and one who possesses neither the diligently-cultivated path nor the swiftly-advancing path — the first being the first grade, the second the second grade, and the third the third grade; and the very same three grades hold for the non-arising type as well. Furthermore, since the meaning of superior advancement is itself most excellent in the upstream type, it is established as a single category on its own, and it is on this basis that the seven sage-destinies are established. Furthermore, among the arising type, there is one of superior faculty, one of middling faculty, and one of inferior faculty, corresponding in turn to the three grades — and the very same three grades hold for the non-arising type as well, the rest being exactly as explained above.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sheer number of independent, mutually consistent ways offered to carve the very same three grades — by speed, by faculty, by path, by karma, by effort, by constancy, by perceived fault, by calm-and-insight balance — is itself a striking demonstration of how thoroughly interconnected this tradition took spiritual progress to be: eight different lenses converge on an identical threefold division because, on this view, sharper faculties, better karma, sustained effort, and clearer perception of saṃsāra's faults are never really separate achievements, only different angles on one and the same underlying advancement.

2. Why the Sūtra Uses Similes Only for Three of the Seven

The treatise asks why the sūtra illustrates only the non-arising type's three sub-kinds with similes — likening them to a traveler who reaches parinirvāṇa near the start, midway, or almost at the very end of a road — while leaving the arising type's three sub-kinds and the upstream type entirely unillustrated. Among several explanations offered, one stands out: the Buddha specifically anticipated that future critics, such as the Vibhajyavādin masters, would deny the very existence of the intermediate state, and so illustrated its three sub-kinds with particular care precisely because this doctrine, uniquely among the seven, would one day need defending.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: just as the arising and non-arising types each have three sub-kinds, the upstream type, too, has three sub-kinds — namely, fully-transcending, half-transcending, and dying-in-every-station — why, then, is it spoken of as only a single category? Answer: the arising and non-arising types are each a single, continuous existence, and, since the distinctions in stage within them are difficult to discern, the treatise wishes to make them known, and so speaks of three sub-kinds for each; whereas the upstream type's own three sub-kinds distinguish themselves readily and are easily known on their own, and it is for this reason that, following the principle that its own superior advancement is most excellent, it is spoken of together as a single category. Furthermore, the arising and non-arising types both involve only a single, brief span of existence, and the distinctions between them are minor and their boundaries are easily discerned; whereas the upstream type's own duration is long, its distinctions are numerous, and its boundaries are difficult to discern, and it is for this reason that it is established together as a single category. Furthermore, the arising and non-arising types share a certain equality of meaning, while the upstream type has a meaning all its own, and the treatise, wishing to display these two textual treatments in mutual contrast, speaks of them in just this way. Furthermore, the arising and non-arising types' own sage-destiny characteristics manifest readily and are easily understood, since they proceed swiftly toward parinirvāṇa, and it is for this reason that each is divided into three; whereas the upstream type's own sage-destiny characteristics are subtle, hidden, and difficult to discern, since it still passes through many rounds of birth-and-death, and it is for this reason that it is spoken of together as only a single category.

問何故經中不生三種以三喻顯。於生三種則不爾耶。答如以三喻顯 不生三。當知亦已顯生三種是故前說。若由此故立初生品則由此故 立初不生乃至廣說。復次以不生者趣生處時如涉路者。於中有去未 遠便般涅槃。有去少遠而般涅槃。有去已遠垂至生處而般涅槃。是 故世尊以三喻顯生中三種皆至生處。更無所趣遠近差別故無別喻。

Question: why does the sūtra illustrate the three sub-kinds of the non-arising type with three similes, but not do the same for the three sub-kinds of the arising type? Answer: in illustrating the three sub-kinds of the non-arising type with three similes, one should understand that the three sub-kinds of the arising type are already thereby implicitly displayed as well, and it is for this reason that the earlier passage states, "whatever basis establishes the first grade of the arising type establishes the first grade of the non-arising type as well," and so on at length. Furthermore, as for the non-arising type approaching his own destination, he is like a traveler upon a road: some, having traveled not yet far, already attain parinirvāṇa; some, having traveled a somewhat greater distance, attain parinirvāṇa; and some, having traveled very far indeed, attain parinirvāṇa just as they are on the very verge of reaching their destination. The World-Honored One therefore illustrates these three sub-kinds with three similes. As for the three sub-kinds of the arising type, however, each and every one of them has already actually reached its own destination, with no further distinction of distance yet to be traveled, and it is for this reason that no separate simile is given for them.

Furthermore, the three sub-kinds of the non-arising type are subtle, inconspicuous, and difficult to perceive and understand, and it is for this reason that they are illustrated with similes; the three sub-kinds of the arising type, by contrast, are coarse, conspicuous, and easily known, and are therefore not illustrated with similes. Furthermore, the three sub-kinds of the non-arising type belong properly to a realm-classification rather than a destiny-classification, whereas the arising type's own classification, though it belongs to a destiny, has a mark that is not itself fully complete, and it is for this reason that similes are used; the three sub-kinds of the arising type are not like this, and so no similes are used for them. Furthermore, the Buddha foresaw that, in future times, there would be those holding perverse views who would slander the very notion of the intermediate state — such as the Vibhajyavādin masters and the like — and so, wishing to settle the meaning of the intermediate state definitively, the Buddha illustrated the distinctions in cessation with three similes; since there is no such slander directed at the arising-type existence and the like, no similes are given for its own three sub-kinds. Furthermore, parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state is superior to parinirvāṇa upon rebirth and the rest in three respects: first, it undergoes less suffering; second, it more swiftly extinguishes the fire of affliction; and third, it more quickly casts off the burden of the aggregates — and it is for this reason that it is illustrated with three similes, whereas the arising type and the rest are not like this, and so no similes are given for them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The claim that the Buddha crafted a particular teaching's imagery in anticipation of a specific future doctrinal controversy is a remarkable piece of retrospective apologetics: it treats the sūtra's own literary choices — which teachings get a vivid simile and which do not — as evidence the Buddha foresaw exactly which points his own tradition would later need to defend most carefully.

3. Why Stream-Enterers and Once-Returners Never Make the List

The treatise turns to a genuinely vivid question: why are the stream-enterer and the once-returner — themselves already called "sages" in the canonical sense — excluded from the seven sage-destinies? After considering several technical distinctions (whether they have transcended their own realm, severed the five lower fetters, or attained the mixed cultivation of dhyāna), the treatise settles on an unusually concrete answer: they still retain the ordinary lay attachments of household life — cherished spouses and children, fine bedding and clothing, jewelry and hoarded wealth, unclean sexual desire mistaken for purity, and fits of anger — the very "dharmas shared in common with those who are not sages" that a genuine sage-destiny must have already eternally severed.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: by relying on the noble path of which ground does one who attains parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state proceed to sever the remaining fetters and attain the fruit of arhatship? Answer: he relies on the noble path of his own ground alone — that is, if he attains parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state while abiding at the level of the first dhyāna, he relies on the noble path of the first dhyāna; and so on, clear through, if he attains parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state while abiding at the level of the fourth dhyāna, he relies on the noble path of the fourth dhyāna. Question: once he has attained the fruit, is there also a case of his relying on the noble path of the stage of proximate access or the meditative interval? Answer: some hold that there is indeed such a case, namely, if he had previously attained the fruit of non-return by relying on that same level, he is now likewise capable of relying on it again. According to this latter account, however, it should instead be said that he does not rely on it at all — why so? Because this is simply the natural principle of the matter. Furthermore, because the span of time is so brief, he attains parinirvāṇa so swiftly once he has attained the fruit that there is no room for him to give rise to the uncontaminated of any other ground. Furthermore, the intermediate state is capable of according with the noble path only at the level of a fundamental ground, never at the level of a proximate-access ground, since the noble path belonging to a proximate-access ground is itself subsumed under the painful path, and is therefore difficult to manifest there. Furthermore, only a ground that possesses both concentration and rebirth allows one to give rise to its own noble path while abiding in the intermediate state — the stage of proximate access possesses concentration but not rebirth; the meditative interval, although it does have rebirth, is a rebirth belonging only to ordinary worldlings, not to noble ones; whereas the four fundamental dhyānas possess both concentration and rebirth, and it is for this reason that one abiding in the intermediate state gives rise to their own noble path. Question: why does he not give rise to the noble path of the formless spheres? Answer: because, at the earlier stage, he had not yet attained it, and, at the later stage, it would serve no purpose. Furthermore, because it is subsumed under a different ground, and is therefore difficult to manifest. Furthermore, because it is subsumed under the painful path, and he has no wish to give rise to it.

問何故不說預流一來為善士趣耶。答應說而不說者當知此義有餘。

"The arhat's own supreme bliss, / attained by a mind free of craving, / and by pride already severed, / and by the net of delusion eternally cut off" — and, on other occasions and in other contexts, he praises the youngest son instead, as explained at length in the Sūtra of the Pond Simile. Here, in this present passage, he is likewise praising the middle son, and it is for this reason that the stream-enterer and the once-returner are not mentioned. Others hold: the World-Honored One speaks of these seven sage-destinies precisely in order to distinguish them from the stream-enterer and the once-returner — that is, although the latter, too, are indeed given the name "sage," as a sūtra states, "what is meant by a sage? It refers to one who possesses the trainee's own right view, and so on clear through right concentration; what is meant by a superior sage? It refers to one who possesses the non-trainee's own right view, and so on clear through right concentration" — yet they cannot be named a "sage-destiny," since they still remain far off, and are not yet capable of drawing near to the superior sage. Only the non-returner is named both a "sage" and a "sage-destiny," since he is indeed a sage, and is also capable of drawing near to the superior sage — and it is for this reason that the stream-enterer and the once-returner are not sage-destinies. 4. What Is "Mixed" About the Mixed Cultivation of Dhyāna

5. Fifteen Mind-Moments: The Mechanics of Mixed Cultivation

The treatise details the actual meditative technique with real precision: a practitioner in the fourth dhyāna alternates uncontaminated and contaminated mind-moments in blocks that shrink with each cycle, until the pattern narrows to a final, stable rhythm of a single contaminated instant sandwiched between single uncontaminated instants on either side — a process totaling fifteen mind-moments across five progressively refined grades, deliberately compared to the fifteen moments of the path of seeing itself. Three motivations drive a noble one to undertake this demanding practice: delight in the meditative state itself, fear that regression might otherwise let affliction resurface, and the specific wish to be reborn into the Pure Abodes unavailable to ordinary worldlings.

Answer: for three reasons: first, delight in the equipoise itself; second, fear of affliction; and third, delight in a favorable rebirth. "Fear of affliction" means fearing that, should he regress and afflictions manifest before him, he would lose his own excellent qualities, or fearing that, should afflictions manifest before him upon regression, they would scorch his own body and mind. Furthermore, the mixed cultivation of dhyāna is itself an excellent quality, not subsumed under the inferior or middling grades at all — there being only the superior, the superior-inferior, the superior-middling, the superior-superior, the superior-excellent, and the superior-utmost, and it is for this reason that there are exactly five. Certain other masters hold: the mixed cultivation of dhyāna is precisely the tempering and training of the five faculties beginning with faith, and, according to their own proper sequence, each one in turn becomes dominant, thereby forming five grades — and it is for this reason that there are exactly five, and it is because the mixed cultivation has exactly five grades that the Pure Abodes, too, are exactly five in number. Others hold: the fourth dhyāna has nine grades of wholesome root, which, in succession, produce rebirth in nine stations — namely, the lowest of the low wholesome root produces rebirth in Anabhraka; the lowest of the middle produces rebirth in Puṇyaprasava; and so on, clear through the highest of the high producing rebirth in Akaniṣṭha — and, among these various stations, no increase or decrease is possible, and it is for this reason that the Pure Abodes should be exactly five in number. Others hold: the fourth dhyāna has nine grades of affliction — if none of the nine grades has yet been suppressed at all, one is reborn in Anabhraka; if the highest of the high grade has been suppressed, one is reborn in Puṇyaprasava; and so on, clear through, if the lowest of the middle grade has been suppressed, one is reborn in Akaniṣṭha — there being no case of one who has suppressed only the lowest of the low grade and is thereby reborn, and it is for this reason that the Pure Abodes are exactly five kinds. Question: is there a case of one who engages in the mixed cultivation of dhyāna yet is not reborn into the Pure Abodes? Answer: there is — that is, having engaged in the mixed cultivation of dhyāna, he may instead attain parinirvāṇa in this very life, or regress and be reborn into a lower ground, or advance further and be reborn into the formless realm — this refers to one who engages in the mixed cultivation of dhyāna without actually delighting in the Pure Abodes themselves. Question: is there a case of one who fully gives rise to all five grades of the mixed cultivation of dhyāna, yet is reborn into Anabhraka and the like anyway? Answer: there is — that is, there is a case of one first reborn in Anabhraka, then reborn in Puṇyaprasava, then reborn in Bṛhatphala, and only afterward, in succession, reborn through all five Pure Abodes in turn.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Pārśva's blunt refusal to answer why the Pure Abodes number exactly five — declaring the question itself out of bounds, since any answer risks inviting doubt rather than resolving it — stands out against the surrounding avalanche of eager explanations from every other master cited: it is a rare moment in this endlessly explanatory text where a revered authority chooses to let a settled fact simply stand on its own, unjustified, rather than risk making it seem more contingent than it is.
