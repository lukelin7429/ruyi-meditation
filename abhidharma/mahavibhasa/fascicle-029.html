Love and Respect Proceeding Together

Fascicle 29 opens with Ānanda's visit to the elder Nāmasthavira on calming, insight, and the three realms of cutting-off, dispassion, and cessation — a story staged to silence a running slander against the aged elder and his five hundred monks — before the Person Nyāsa that has run since fascicle 23 concludes, and the treatise opens its fourth nyāsa on love and respect, and on offering and reverence toward the Three Jewels, closing with a gallery of Dharma offering reaching every one of the five destinies, from the hells to the heavens. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Ānanda Visits the Elder Nāmasthavira: A Discourse of the Same Kind

Fascicle 29 opens a new investigation into the sūtra's three realms — cutting-off, dispassion, and cessation — by relating why Ānanda, ever watchful for monks growing idle in forest solitude, sought out the aged elder Nāmasthavira, and why he took care to question him only in “same-kind discourse”: asking a forest-dweller about forest-dwelling, a vinaya-holder about the vinaya, rather than risking an unanswerable question that might embarrass his host into needless contention.

Chinese Source

如世尊說。有三界。謂斷界離界。滅界乃至廣說。問何故作此論答為廣分別契經義故。謂契經說。具壽阿難往詣尊者名上座所。問何故阿難往詣彼所。答尊者阿難。是樂法者。是正法將攝受聖教。御聖教船。恒巡四眾。教授教誡。數數觀察諸苾芻等。勿有懈怠耽著戲論。或於境界顛倒思惟。令彼一生空過顛墜。故往彼所。復次阿難作如是念。彼名上座恒樂寂靜居阿練若。勇猛精勤證何妙德。我應往問。若能為我說所證德。我當合掌隨喜讚歎。若不爾者。方便慇懃示其加行。令速證得。勿彼多時居阿練若空無所獲。故往彼所。如彼經說。具壽阿難到已施設同分言論。非不同分。問何等名為同分言論。答若居阿練若者。問以阿練若法。若持毘奈耶者。問毘奈耶。若誦素怛纜者。問素怛纜若學阿毘達磨者。問阿毘達磨。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the Blessed One has stated: "There are three realms — the realm of cutting-off, the realm of dispassion, and the realm of cessation," and so on, at length. One asks: why is this treatise composed? The answer: it is composed in order to fully discriminate the meaning of this sūtra passage. That is, the sūtra relates that the venerable Ānanda went to visit the elder named Nāmasthavira. One asks: why did Ānanda go to visit him there? The answer: the venerable Ānanda was one who delighted in the Dharma, one who upheld the true Dharma and gathered in the noble teaching, steering the ship of the noble teaching, forever making his rounds among the fourfold assembly, instructing and admonishing them, again and again observing the monks and the rest, lest they grow slothful, indulge in idle talk, or think in a distorted way about the objects of the senses, causing their one life to pass by in vain and to fall to ruin — it was for this reason that he went to visit him. Further, Ānanda thought as follows: this elder named Nāmasthavira forever delights in stillness and dwells in the forest wilderness — through what vigorous and diligent effort has he realized what excellent virtue? I ought to go and ask him. If he is able to tell me the virtue he has realized, I shall join my palms, rejoice, and praise him accordingly. If he is not able, then I shall, through skillful and earnest means, point out to him the practice he should undertake, so that he may swiftly attain realization, lest he dwell in the forest wilderness for a long time and yet gain nothing — it was for this reason that he went to visit him.

是名同分言論。與此相違名不同分言論。謂居阿練若者。問以三藏。持毘奈耶者。問阿練若及餘二藏。誦素怛纜者。問阿練若及餘二藏。學阿毘達磨者。問阿練若及餘二藏。或更問餘事。皆名不同分言論。尊者阿難所以唯作同分言論。若作不同分言論者。彼不解故。便不能答。既不能答。心便羞恥。以羞恥故鬪諍違拒。不欲令彼起如是過。是故唯作同分言論。謂但問彼阿練若法。如彼經說。

As that sūtra relates, the venerable Ānanda, upon arriving, set forth a discourse of the same kind, not a discourse of a different kind. One asks: what is meant by a "discourse of the same kind"? The answer: if one who dwells in the forest wilderness is asked about the practice proper to forest-dwelling; if one who upholds the vinaya is asked about the vinaya; if one who recites the sūtras is asked about the sūtras; if one who studies the abhidharma is asked about the abhidharma —

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The etiquette of “same-kind discourse” is a small but telling piece of monastic sociology: the treatise treats the risk of causing another monk shame through an unanswerable question as a real pastoral concern worth an entire doctrinal digression, revealing how much weight this literature places on preserving harmony within the saṅgha even in the middle of a technical philosophical investigation.

2. Calming, Insight, and the Three Realms as Objects of Resolute Conviction

The elder's answer names two practices — calming (śamatha) and insight (vipaśyanā) — and the treatise uses the exchange to work out, in careful detail, what distinguishes a “practitioner of calming” from a “practitioner of insight”: differing preparatory inclinations, differing faculties, differing balance between one-pointed absorption and discriminating analysis. A sharp objection then follows — how can the three unconditioned realms serve as what liberation depends on? — resolved by identifying the resolute conviction that takes nirvāṇa as its very object as what the sūtra means by “realm.”

Chinese Source

爾時阿難問名上座。若有苾芻。居阿練若。或居樹下。或居靜室。

Ru-Yi Translation

this is called a discourse of the same kind. What is contrary to this is called a discourse of a different kind — that is, asking one who dwells in the forest wilderness about all three collections of scripture; asking one who upholds the vinaya about forest-dwelling and the other two collections; asking one who recites the sūtras about forest-dwelling and the other two collections; asking one who studies the abhidharma about forest-dwelling and the other two collections; or asking about still other matters besides — all of these are called discourses of a different kind. The reason the venerable Ānanda set forth only a discourse of the same kind is that, were he to set forth a discourse of a different kind, the other would not understand it, and, not understanding it, would be unable to answer; and, being unable to answer, would feel ashamed in his heart, and, out of shame, would fall into contention and opposition. Not wishing to cause him to fall into such a fault, Ānanda therefore set forth only a discourse of the same kind, asking him only about the practice proper to forest-dwelling. As that sūtra relates,

或在塚間。應數思惟何等行法。時名上座白阿難言。若有苾芻。居阿練若。或居樹下。或居靜室。或在塚間。應數思惟二種行法。謂奢摩他毘鉢舍那所以者何。若奢摩他熏修心者。依毘鉢舍那而得解脫。若毘鉢舍那熏修心者。依奢摩他而得解脫。若奢摩他毘鉢舍那熏修心者依三種界而得解脫。云何三界。所謂斷界離界滅界。問依對法義於一心中有奢摩他毘鉢舍那云何建立如是二種行者差別。答由加行故二種差別。謂加行時或多修習奢摩他資糧。或多修習毘鉢舍那資糧多修習奢摩他資糧者。謂加行時恒樂獨處閑居寂靜怖畏憒鬧見諠雜過恒居靜室入聖道時。名奢摩他行者。多修習毘鉢舍那資糧者謂加行時恒樂讀誦思惟三藏。於一切法自相共相數數觀察入聖道時。名毘鉢舍那行者。復次或有繫心一緣不分別法相。或有分別法相不繫心一緣。若繫心一緣不分別法相者入聖道時。名奢摩他行者。若分別法相不繫心一緣者入聖道時。名毘鉢舍那行者。復次若利根者。名毘鉢舍那行者若鈍根者。名奢摩他行者。如利根鈍根如是。因力緣力。內分力外分力。內正思惟力外聞他音力。應知亦爾。問斷離滅界體是無為無因無果。云何乃說若奢摩他毘鉢舍那熏修心者依三種界而得解脫。答彼契經於緣涅槃勝解以界聲說。謂修行者雖加行時精進勇猛修習止觀二種資糧。若於涅槃不起勝解決定趣證。畢竟不能斷諸煩惱心得解脫。故緣涅槃勝解名界。依此界故心得解脫。如彼經說。爾時阿難問名上座。何等斷故名為斷界。何等離故名為離界。何等滅故名為滅界。名上座言。一切行斷故名斷界。一切行離故名離界。一切行滅故名滅界。尊者阿難聞已合掌隨喜讚歎辭退。復詣竹林道場以此事問五百苾芻。彼復皆如名上座答。問彼諸苾芻云何而答。有作是說。從少至老次第而答。如法集時少者先問。有餘師說。從老至少次第而答。如行施物自老至少。

the venerable Ānanda then asked the elder named Nāmasthavira: if a monk dwells in the forest wilderness, or dwells beneath a tree, or dwells in a quiet cell, or dwells among the charnel grounds, upon what practice ought he repeatedly to reflect? At that time, the elder named Nāmasthavira said to Ānanda: if a monk dwells in the forest wilderness, or dwells beneath a tree, or dwells in a quiet cell, or dwells among the charnel grounds, he ought repeatedly to reflect upon two kinds of practice — namely, calming and insight. Why is this so? If one whose mind is cultivated by calming, it is in dependence on insight that one attains liberation; if one whose mind is cultivated by insight, it is in dependence on calming that one attains liberation; and if one whose mind is cultivated by both calming and insight together, it is in dependence on the three realms that one attains liberation. What are the three realms? They are, namely, the realm of cutting-off, the realm of dispassion, and the realm of cessation. One asks: according to the meaning of the Abhidharma, within a single mind there exist both calming and insight together — how, then, is this distinction between the two kinds of practitioner to be established? The answer: the distinction arises through the exertion applied during one's preparatory effort — that is, during this preparatory exertion, one either cultivates predominantly the provisions of calming, or cultivates predominantly the provisions of insight. One who cultivates predominantly the provisions of calming is one who, during this preparatory exertion, forever delights in dwelling alone in leisure and stillness, fearing the clamor of crowds and regarding disorder and mixture as a fault, forever dwelling in a quiet cell — and, upon entering the noble path, is called a practitioner of calming. One who cultivates predominantly the provisions of insight is one who, during this preparatory exertion, forever delights in reciting and reflecting upon the three collections of scripture, again and again observing the particular and general characteristics of all dharmas — and, upon entering the noble path, is called a practitioner of insight. Further, there is one who binds his mind to a single object without discriminating the characteristics of dharmas, and there is one who discriminates the characteristics of dharmas without binding his mind to a single object; if one binds his mind to a single object without discriminating the characteristics of dharmas, then, upon entering the noble path, he is called a practitioner of calming; if one discriminates the characteristics of dharmas without binding his mind to a single object, then, upon entering the noble path, he is called a practitioner of insight. Further, one of sharp faculties is called a practitioner of insight, while one of dull faculties is called a practitioner of calming; and exactly as with sharp and dull faculties, so too should this be understood with respect to the power of causes and the power of conditions, the power of one's own internal disposition and the power of external factors, the power of correct internal reflection and the power of hearing another's voice externally. One asks: the substance of the realms of cutting-off, dispassion, and cessation is unconditioned, without cause and without effect — how, then, can it be said that one whose mind is cultivated by both calming and insight together attains liberation in dependence on the three realms? The answer: that sūtra, with respect to the resolute conviction that takes nirvāṇa as its object, speaks of it by the word "realm" — that is, although a practitioner, during his preparatory exertion, diligently and vigorously cultivates the two provisions of calming and insight, if he does not give rise to resolute conviction with respect to nirvāṇa, determined to advance toward its realization, then he will, in the end, be unable to cut off the afflictions or attain liberation of mind. It is for this reason that the resolute conviction taking nirvāṇa as its object is called "realm," and it is in dependence on this realm that mind attains liberation. As that sūtra relates, the venerable Ānanda then asked the elder named Nāmasthavira: through the cutting-off of what is it called the realm of cutting-off? Through the dispassion regarding what is it called the realm of dispassion? Through the cessation of what is it called the realm of cessation? The elder named Nāmasthavira said: it is through the cutting-off of all formations that it is called the realm of cutting-off; it is through the dispassion regarding all formations that it is called the realm of dispassion; it is through the cessation of all formations that it is called the realm of cessation. Having heard this, the venerable Ānanda joined his palms, rejoiced, praised him, and took his leave, then proceeded to the Bamboo Grove monastery and, with this same matter, questioned five hundred monks, who all likewise answered exactly as the elder named Nāmasthavira had. One asks: how did those monks answer? There are those who say: they answered in sequence from the youngest to the oldest, just as, at the time of the communal recitation, the youngest asks first. There are other masters who say: they answered in sequence from the oldest to the youngest, just as, in the distribution of alms, one proceeds from the eldest to the youngest.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The resolution of the unconditioned-realm objection is a small masterstroke of abhidharma precision: rather than treating “depends on the three realms” as loose devotional language, the treatise insists on locating an actual conditioned mental factor — resolute conviction — that can genuinely serve as a cause, refusing to let an unconditioned dharma do causal work it is, by definition, incapable of doing.

3. Five Hundred Arhats Confirm the Elder's Answer

Satisfied, Ānanda carries the same question to five hundred monks at the Bamboo Grove monastery, who answer identically — the treatise pausing to canvass three different accounts of how, procedurally, five hundred monks might jointly deliver one answer, from youngest-first to a formal proposal-and-counting-stick vote the Venerable Pārśva describes. Ānanda then takes the same question to the Buddha himself, raising the question of why he would ask what he already believed.

Chinese Source

復有說者。一苾芻答餘皆隨喜。脇尊者言。先作白已後次行籌。受籌名答。如彼經說。爾時阿難聞已合掌隨喜讚歎。辭詣佛所到已頂禮世尊雙足。却住一面。以此句義問佛世尊。佛還如彼上座等答。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are yet others who say: one monk answered, and the rest all rejoiced in his answer. The Venerable Pārśva says: first the formal proposal was made, and then the counting-sticks were passed around; taking up a counting-stick constituted one's answer. As that sūtra relates, the venerable Ānanda, having heard this, joined his palms, rejoiced, and praised them, then took his leave and proceeded to where the Buddha was, and, upon arriving, bowed his head at the feet of the Blessed One, withdrew to one side, and questioned the Blessed One with this same matter, and the Buddha answered exactly as the elder and the rest had.

問尊者阿難忍可上座五百苾芻所說義不。設爾何失。若忍可者。何故復以問佛世尊。若不忍可何故合掌隨喜讚歎。答阿難忍可彼所說義。問何故復以問佛世尊。答如佛世尊知而故問。尊者阿難亦復如是。所以者何。阿難欲顯善說法中同見同欲文義決定。如大師說。

One asks: did the venerable Ānanda accept the meaning of what the elder and the five hundred monks had said, or not? Suppose either is granted — what fault would follow? If he accepted it, then why did he further put the question to the Blessed One? If he did not accept it, then why did he join his palms, rejoice, and praise it? The answer: Ānanda did accept the meaning of what they had said. One asks: then why did he further put the question to the Blessed One? The answer: just as the Blessed One, though already knowing, deliberately asked, so too did the venerable Ānanda do likewise. Why is this so? It is because Ānanda wished to make clear that, within the well-spoken Dharma, there is agreement in view and agreement in intent, and that its meaning is fixed and determined —

徒眾亦然。如親教說。弟子亦然。如軌範說。受學亦然。如是文義微妙決定。依之修學乃至能證阿羅漢果。非如外道所說文義師徒眾等展轉相違。依之修學空無所證。復次阿難欲以佛妙言印印所說義。故重問佛。若不以佛妙言印之則所說義猶可傾動。當來四眾不敬信故。如世文符。若無王印則所行處人不敬受。此亦如是。故重問佛。如彼經說佛問阿難汝知上座五百苾芻有何功德。阿難白佛。

exactly as the Great Teacher himself has said: "so too is it with the assembly of followers; so too, as the preceptor has said, is it with the disciples; so too, as the instructor has said, is it with those receiving instruction" — that the meaning of such teaching is subtle and determined, and that, in dependence on it, one may cultivate practice and thereby, in the end, realize the fruit of arhatship, unlike the teachings of the heretical outsiders, where teacher and disciples, and the assembly and the rest, contradict one another at every turn, so that, in dependence on them, one cultivates practice in vain and realizes nothing at all. Further, Ānanda wished to seal the meaning that had been spoken with the Buddha's own excellent word, and it was for this reason that he asked the Buddha once more — for, were it not sealed with the Buddha's own excellent word, then the meaning spoken might still be shaken and overturned, and the fourfold assembly of the future would not revere and trust it, just as, in the writs of the ordinary world, if there is no royal seal, then people will not reverently accept what is proclaimed therein. The same holds true here as well, and it was for this reason that he asked the Buddha once more. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own precedent of “asking though he already knows” — invoked to explain why Ānanda needed the Buddha's confirmation despite already accepting the elder's answer — is a recurring device in this literature: authoritative confirmation is not about resolving genuine uncertainty but about sealing a teaching with the weight needed to prevent future generations from doubting it, exactly as a royal seal validates a document whose content was never really in question.

4. Why the Buddha Asked About Their Virtue: The Hidden Treasure and the Slander

The Buddha asks Ānanda directly what virtues the elder and his five hundred monks possess, and Ānanda confirms they are all fully liberated arhats. The treatise then supplies an unusually rich set of motives for why the Buddha would ask a question to which he already knew the answer: to bring hidden virtue to light like unearthing a buried treasure, to reward unknowing donors with the joy of discovering their own good fortune, and — most pointedly — to silence a running slander against the elder, mocked for his aged, unimpressive appearance and accused of exploiting five hundred followers he had supposedly lured from a rival teacher.

Chinese Source

彼名上座五百苾芻皆阿羅漢諸漏已盡已捨重擔盡諸有結。逮得己利善辦聖旨心善解脫。佛告阿難。如汝所說。問何故世尊問彼功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

this elder and the five hundred monks are all arhats, whose contaminants are already exhausted, who have already cast off the heavy burden, who have exhausted every fetter of existence, who have attained their own true benefit, who have thoroughly accomplished the noble aim, and whose minds are well liberated. The Buddha said to Ānanda: it is exactly as you have said. One asks: why did the Blessed One ask about their virtues?

答為欲開發少欲喜足所覆真實功德寶藏。令諸世間知已敬養得勝果故。如世伏藏雖多珍寶沙土覆之不得顯現。若有開發令無量人採取受用得世富樂。此亦如是。故佛問之。復次開覺施主勝思願故。謂有施主。恒以衣服等四種供具。施彼上座及五百苾芻。而不知彼有勝功德。欲令知已歡喜踊躍起勝思願。我等得遇如是福田已種善種。定於來世受大快樂。是故世尊問彼功德。復次為止世間誹謗事故。謂彼上座在母胎中經六十年。既出胎已形容衰老無有威德。故初生已立上座名。後雖出家而被嗤笑。少年強盛晝夜精勤尚難得果況此衰老氣力羸劣能得果耶。又彼上座所度五百新學苾芻先隨天授。眾人毀曰。如是老叟貪著名利度五百人。為充自身驅役供侍。

The answer: it was because he wished to bring to light the treasury of their true and genuine virtue, concealed as it was beneath their own contentment with few desires, so that the world might come to know it, revere and support them, and thereby obtain a superior fruit — just as, in the ordinary world, a buried treasure, though rich in precious things, remains hidden beneath sand and soil and cannot come to light, yet, if it is unearthed, it enables countless people to gather and make use of it, thereby obtaining worldly wealth and happiness. The same holds true here as well, and it was for this reason that the Buddha asked about it. Further, it was to awaken in donors a superior aspiration of their own — that is, there were donors who, with the four kinds of provisions such as robes and the rest, constantly made offerings to the elder and the five hundred monks, yet did not know that they possessed such superior virtue; the Buddha wished to cause them, once they knew of it, to rejoice and leap with joy, and to give rise to a superior aspiration of their own — thinking, "we have had the good fortune to encounter such a field of merit, and have already planted a wholesome seed there, and are certain, in a future life, to receive great happiness" — and it was for this reason that the Blessed One asked about their virtue. Further, it was in order to put a stop to a slanderous matter current in the world — that is, this elder had dwelt within his mother's womb for sixty years, and, once born, his form and countenance were already aged and worn, without any dignity or majesty, and it was for this reason that, from the moment of his birth, he was already given the name "Elder." 6. The Punchline: All Three Realms Are, in Substance, Identical

8. The Verdict: No Wisdom Beyond the Sixteen Aspects, Yet the Three Notions Diverge

Having canvassed the countervailing position — that a further, distinct uncontaminated wisdom does exist beyond the sixteen aspects, active only after the fruit of arhatship in ways the systematic treatises simply had no occasion to describe — the treatise renders its considered verdict for the more austere reading: no such extra wisdom exists, and the three notions of cutting-off, dispassion, and cessation are established by their object, not by any distinct aspect, making them mutually inclusive exactly like the three realms above. Yet the treatise closes by refusing to make this fully symmetrical: uncontaminated notions are indeed interchangeable, but contaminated ones, it allows, may still form three genuinely distinct aspects without full interchangeability.

9. A New Nyāsa Opens: Love and Respect

Fascicle 29 now marks a major structural transition: the Person Nyāsa that has run continuously since fascicle 23 concludes, and the treatise opens its fourth nyāsa, on love (愛) and respect (敬). The opening move is characteristic — distinguishing the “not-good person,” for whom love and respect obstruct one another, as when doting parents receive love without respect, or severe ones receive respect without love, from the “good person,” in whom the two proceed together, a virtue the treatise identifies as the signature trait of a bodhisattva.

The eggshell-of-ignorance image, in which the Buddha's own emergence into awakening becomes the very means by which countless other beings likewise break free of their shells, captures something the whole passage is building toward: reverence for the Buddha is repeatedly justified not by his personal attainment in isolation but by its multiplying, world-transforming effect on everyone else's own liberation — gratitude here is inseparable from recognizing a cause with genuinely unlimited downstream consequence.

12. The Fourfold Object Analysis, and Where Love-and-Respect Truly Belongs

The treatise closes its examination of love-and-respect with a tetralemma distinguishing objects that draw love without respect, respect without love, both together, or neither, before locating this superior combined love-and-respect with striking specificity: though it could in principle arise anywhere across the three realms and five destinies, the treatise insists it exists, in its superior form, only within the human destiny of the desire realm, and only within the Buddha's own teaching.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

It is with respect to these superior objects already spoken of above — the Three Jewels, and one's teachers and friends — that love and respect both arise together; in every other case, this is not fixed, and four alternatives should here be formed: there is an object with respect to which love, but not respect, arises — as with parents toward their child, or a teacher toward his disciple; there is an object with respect to which respect, but not love, arises — as with one of virtue who is not one's own teacher or elder; there is an object with respect to which both love and respect arise — as with a certain kind of child toward his parents, or a disciple toward his teacher; there is an object with respect to which neither love nor respect arises — namely, whatever remains apart from the foregoing. One asks: in what place does such love-and-respect exist? The answer: although it may, in principle, be found across all three realms and the five destinies, this passage speaks of a superior love-and-respect that exists only within the human destiny of the desire realm, and nowhere else; this love-and-respect exists only within the Buddha's own teaching.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Restricting superior love-and-respect to the human realm specifically is a quiet but important claim about human rebirth's own soteriological value elsewhere echoed across Buddhist literature: gods enjoy greater pleasure and longer life, but it is the human condition's peculiar mixture of suffering and rational reflection that makes this particular, fully integrated devotion — neither the doting indulgence nor the fearful deference the treatise has spent this whole section distinguishing from it — possible at all.

13. Offering and Reverence: A Verse, and the Same-Structure Warning

A new but structurally identical investigation opens on offering (供養) and reverence, anchored to a canonical verse praising those who offer and revere the Three Jewels, one's precepts, diligence, and superior concentration as never falling back from the path toward nirvāṇa. The treatise recycles its now-familiar diagnostic: the “not-good person” for whom offering obstructs reverence, as when a wealthy child's material support breeds contempt rather than esteem, or reverence obstructs offering, as when fear of another's power produces deference without any actual gift.

14. Material Offering: Self-Nature, Meaning, and Who Gives to Whom

The treatise works out material offering's own nature through the treatise's characteristic four-position debate — the goods given, the giver's bodily and verbal action, the mental factors behind that action, or the recipient's own resulting growth — before its verdict combines all four into a single five-aggregate whole. A careful distinction then separates mere “giving” from true “offering” depending on whether the recipient's body and mind actually benefit, before the treatise maps exactly which of the four desire-realm destinies may offer to which others, closing with the curious case of gods who conjure their own food yet still receive offerings of incense and flowers.

15. Dharma Offering: From the Hell Realms to the Formless Heavens

Dharma offering receives the same careful nature-analysis before the treatise supplies a remarkable set of concrete examples spanning every one of the five destinies — a being reborn in hell who recites a verse of impermanence and thereby liberates countless fellow sufferers, the kalaviṅka bird who teaches even as an animal, the hungry-ghost mother Priyaṅkarā comforting her own ghost-children in verse, and gods teaching gods all the way up through the heaven of no affliction — before closing with a final accounting of which destinies may offer the Dharma to which others, and a last brief return to respect's own definition, closing the fascicle exactly where its Love-and-Respect Nyāsa began.
