Four Masters, Four Truths

Fascicle 77 opens with the Sarvāstivāda school's own four great masters debating what differs across the three times, closes out the learner and non-learner and related dharma-triads, and then opens the treatise's celebrated treatment of the Four Noble Truths — their rival definitions, whether an arhat possesses all four, why the Buddha speaks of "one truth," and the conventional and ultimate truth. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Four Masters' Theories of the Three Times

A famous passage opens this fascicle: the Sarvāstivāda school's own four great treatise-masters, each proposing a distinct account of what actually differs across the three times. Dharmatrāta holds it is class that differs, not substance, exactly as a broken golden vessel keeps its color while losing its shape. Ghoṣa holds it is aspect that differs, each dharma conjoined with all three temporal aspects at once, though only one aspect predominates, exactly as a man enamored of one woman is not thereby free of desire for others. Vasumitra holds it is state that differs, exactly as a single counting-rod named one, ten, or a hundred depending on its position, its own substance never changing — and it is Vasumitra's own theory the compilers endorse, since it alone avoids the confusion the other three incur: Buddhadeva's relativity-theory makes each single time secretly contain all three times within it, Ghoṣa's aspect-theory already concedes every dharma is conjoined with all three aspects, and Dharmatrāta's class-theory absurdly makes the past arise and the future cease.

Chinese Source

說一切有部有四大論師。各別建立二世有異。謂尊者法救說類有異。尊者妙音說相有異。尊者世友說位有異。尊者覺天說待有異。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Sarvāstivādins possess four great treatise-masters, each establishing, in his own distinct way, a difference regarding the three times — namely, the Venerable Dharmatrāta speaks of a difference of class,

說類異者。彼謂諸法於世轉時。由類有異非體有異。如破金器等作餘物時形雖有異而顯色無異。又如乳等變成酪等時捨味勢等非捨顯色。如是諸法從未來世至現在世時。雖捨未來類得現在類。而彼法體無得無捨。復從現在世至過去世時。雖捨現在類得過去類。而彼法體亦無得無捨。說相異者。彼謂諸法於世轉時。由相有異非體有異。一一世法有三世相。一相正合二相非離。如人正染一女色時。

the Venerable Ghoṣa speaks of a difference of aspect, the Venerable Vasumitra speaks of a difference of state, and the Venerable Buddhadeva speaks of a difference of relativity. As for speaking of a difference of class: he holds that, as dharmas turn through the three times, it is by a difference of class, not a difference of substance — exactly as, when a golden vessel is broken and made into some other object, its shape differs while its visible color does not differ, and exactly as, when milk and the like are transformed into curd and the like, their taste and force are relinquished, but not their visible color — so too, when these dharmas pass from the future state into the present state, although they relinquish the future class and obtain the present class, that dharma's own substance is neither obtained nor relinquished; and, further, when they pass from the present state into the past state, although they relinquish the present class and obtain the past class, that dharma's own substance, too, is neither obtained nor relinquished. As for speaking of a difference of aspect: he holds that, as dharmas turn through the three times, it is by a difference of aspect, not a difference of substance — each dharma of a given time possessing all three temporal aspects, one aspect genuinely conjoined, the other two not thereby separated, exactly as a man, at the very moment he is genuinely enamored of one woman's form,

is not, with respect to every other woman's form, thereby said to be free of desire. So too, these dharmas, while abiding in the past state, are genuinely conjoined with the past aspect, and are not, with respect to the remaining two temporal aspects, thereby said to be separated from them; while abiding in the future state, they are genuinely conjoined with the future aspect, and are not, with respect to the remaining two temporal aspects, thereby said to be separated from them; and, while abiding in the present state, they are genuinely conjoined with the present aspect, and are not, with respect to the remaining two temporal aspects, thereby said to be separated from them. As for speaking of a difference of state: he holds that, as dharmas turn through the three times, it is by a difference of state, not a difference of substance — exactly as a single counting-rod, placed in the ones position, is named one, placed in the tens position, is named ten, and placed in the hundreds position, is named a hundred, the rod's own substance remaining undifferentiated through every position it passes through — so too, these dharmas, passing through the states of the three times, although they obtain three distinct names, remain, in their own substance, undifferentiated. This master's own establishment of the three times incurs no confusion whatsoever, since it is by operative function that the distinction of the three times is established — that is, a conditioned dharma not yet possessing operative function is named the future time; precisely possessing operative function is named the present time; its operative function already ceased is named the past time. As for speaking of a difference of relativity: he holds that, as dharmas turn through the three times, it is by their prior and posterior mutual relativity that their names differ — exactly as one and the same woman, relative to her mother, is named a daughter, and, relative to her own daughter, is named a mother, her own substance remaining undifferentiated, yet, owing to this difference of relativity, obtaining the distinct names of daughter and mother — so too, these dharmas, relative to what comes after, are named past; relative to what comes before, are named future; and, relative to both at once, are named present. This master's own establishment of the three times does incur confusion, for what reason is this so? Because, through this prior-and-posterior mutual relativity, each single time itself already contains all three times within it — that is, within the past time itself, an earlier and a later moment are respectively named its own past and future,

中間名現在未來。三世類亦應然。現在世法雖一剎那待後待前及俱待故應成三世。豈應正理。說相異者。所立三世亦有雜亂。一一世法彼皆許有三世相故。說類異者。離法自性說何為類故亦非理。諸有為法從未來世至現在時前類應滅。從現在世至過去時後類應生。

while its middle moment is named its own present; and the future time, too, ought to be just the same in kind. Further, a dharma of the present time, though occupying but a single moment, being relative to what comes after, relative to what comes before, and relative to both at once, would thereby have to become three times all at once — how could this possibly accord with correct principle? As for the theory of a difference of aspect: the three times it establishes, too, incur confusion, since each single dharma of a given time is, on that theory's own admission, conjoined with all three temporal aspects. As for the theory of a difference of class: to speak of some "class" apart from a dharma's own nature is likewise contrary to principle — every conditioned dharma, passing from the future time into the present time, would have to have its prior class cease, and, passing from the present time into the past time, would have to have its posterior class arise

過去有生未來有滅豈應正理。故唯第三立世為善。諸行容有作用時故。

— and how could it accord with correct principle that the past should be said to arise, and the future to cease? It is for this reason that only the third theory establishes the three times correctly, since it is precisely at the time a formation may possess operative function that this theory locates its own distinction.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The counting-rod analogy Vasumitra reaches for is a remarkably compact solution to how something can be genuinely, radically differentiated by time without being a different thing each time: a single token's own value is entirely a function of the position it occupies, never touching its own material substance at all, exactly as this treatise wants a dharma's own substance to remain constant while its temporal designation as past, present, or future depends purely on where it sits with respect to operative function.

2. Wholesome, Unwholesome, Neutral Dharma; the Three Realms

Two further triads receive brief, formulaic treatment, referred back to the earlier Unwholesome Nyāsa for their fuller elaboration: wholesome, unwholesome, and karmically neutral dharma — the wholesome five aggregates together with analytical cessation, the unwholesome five aggregates alone, the neutral five aggregates together with space and non-analytical cessation — and the dharmas bound to the desire, form, and formless realms respectively.

The answer: the wholesome five aggregates, together with analytical cessation. One asks: what is an unwholesome dharma? The answer: the unwholesome five aggregates. One asks: what is a karmically neutral dharma? The answer: the karmically neutral five aggregates, together with space and non-analytical cessation. Every remaining point of meaning is stated at full length exactly as in the earlier Unwholesome Nyāsa.

復有三法。謂欲界色界無色界繫法。問欲界繫法云何。答欲界繫五蘊。問色界繫法云何。答色界繫五蘊。問無色界繫法云何。答無色界繫四蘊。餘義廣說亦如前不善納息。復有三法。謂學無學非學非無學法。問學法云何。答學五蘊。問無學法云何。答無學五蘊。問非學非無學法云何。答有漏五蘊及三無為。問學等三法其義云何。

There exist, further, three kinds of dharma: dharmas bound to the desire realm, dharmas bound to the form realm, and dharmas bound to the formless realm. One asks: what is a dharma bound to the desire realm? The answer: the five aggregates bound to the desire realm. One asks: what is a dharma bound to the form realm? The answer: the five aggregates bound to the form realm. One asks: what is a dharma bound to the formless realm? The answer: the four aggregates bound to the formless realm. Every remaining point of meaning is likewise stated at full length exactly as in the earlier Unwholesome Nyāsa. There exist, further, three kinds of dharma: the dharma of the learner, the dharma of the non-learner, and the dharma that is neither the learner's nor the non-learner's. One asks: what is the dharma of the learner? The answer: the five aggregates of the learner. One asks: what is the dharma of the non-learner? The answer: the five aggregates of the non-learner. One asks: what is the dharma that is neither the learner's nor the non-learner's? The answer: the contaminated five aggregates, together with the three unconditioned dharmas. One asks: what is the meaning of these three — the learner and the rest?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That analytical cessation alone joins the wholesome aggregates, while space and non-analytical cessation join only the neutral ones, is a small but telling piece of bookkeeping: it locates the two unconditioned near-twins, analytical and non-analytical cessation, on opposite sides of the wholesome/neutral divide, confirming that despite their parallel names they are not moral equivalents at all.

3. What Distinguishes the Learner from the Non-Learner? Ten Definitions

A new triad opens — the learner, the non-learner, and what is neither — and receives an extraordinarily thorough tenfold definition: by whether one is still learning to cut off craving, hatred, and delusion, or has already learned this; by whether one still engages what is not craving's own matter; by whether one is still learning the direct realization of the truths; by whether one still seeks desire and existence or has fulfilled the seeking for the holy life; by whether affliction is still being cut off within a continuum that already possesses the undefiled path; by whether craving specifically has yet been become free of; by inclusion within the paths and grounds of seeing and cultivation versus the non-learner's own path and ground; by inclusion within the faculties of coming-to-know versus having-fully-known; by which of the five or seven kinds of noble one's own continuum the undefiled path belongs to; and, finally, by inclusion within the eighteen learner-types versus the nine non-learner types.

答以無貪瞋癡道學斷貪瞋癡是學義。以無貪瞋癡道不學斷貪瞋癡。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That ten entirely independent criteria — cutting off craving, direct realization, the two seekings, possession of affliction, the paths and grounds, the faculties, and the noble one's own rank — all converge on drawing the identical line between learner and non-learner is this treatise's characteristic proof by overdetermination: no single criterion is trusted alone, but their sheer convergence from every conceivable angle is treated as confirmation that the distinction is real.

4. Dharma Abandoned by Seeing, by Cultivation, and Subject to No Further Cutting-Off

A further triad — dharma abandoned by seeing, abandoned by cultivation, and subject to no further cutting-off — gathers, respectively, the eighty-eight latent tendencies cut off by the forbearance at the margin of direct realization, the ten latent tendencies cut off by the learner's own path of cultivation together with every non-defiling contaminated dharma, and the undefiled five aggregates together with the three unconditioned dharmas. This closes the run of paired and tripled dharma-categories opened earlier in this nyāsa, and the treatise turns at last to its next great topic: the four truths themselves — suffering, its accumulation, its cessation, and the path — composed to analyze at length a scripture that names them without elaborating.

Chinese Source

復有三法。謂見所斷修所斷無斷法。問見所斷法云何。答隨信隨法行現觀邊忍所斷。此復云何。謂見所斷八十八隨眠及彼相應心心所法。彼所等起不相應行。是名見所斷法。問修所斷法云何。答學見迹修所斷。此復云何。謂修所斷十隨眠。及彼相應彼所等起身語二業。彼所等起不相應行。并不染污諸有漏法。是名修所斷法。問無斷法云何。答無漏五蘊。及三無為。餘義廣說如前不善納息。四諦者。謂苦諦集諦滅諦道諦。問何故作此論。答為廣分別契經義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

There exist, further, three kinds of dharma: dharma abandoned by seeing, dharma abandoned by cultivation, and dharma subject to no further cutting-off. One asks: what is dharma abandoned by seeing? The answer: whatever is cut off by the forbearance at the very margin of direct realization, belonging to the faith-follower and the dharma-follower. What, further, does this refer to? It refers to the eighty-eight latent tendencies abandoned by seeing, together with the mind and mental factors associated with them, and the dissociated formations they co-arise with — this is named dharma abandoned by seeing. One asks: what is dharma abandoned by cultivation? The answer: whatever is abandoned by cultivation in the learner's own path of vision. What, further, does this refer to? It refers to the ten latent tendencies abandoned by cultivation, together with the bodily and verbal karmic action associated with and co-arisen with them, the dissociated formations they co-arise with, and every undefiled contaminated dharma — this is named dharma abandoned by cultivation. One asks: what is dharma subject to no further cutting-off? The answer: the undefiled five aggregates, together with the three unconditioned dharmas. Every remaining point of meaning is stated at full length exactly as in the earlier Unwholesome Nyāsa. As for the four truths: this refers to the truth of suffering, the truth of its accumulation, the truth of its cessation, and the truth of the path. One asks: for what reason is this treatise composed? The answer: it is in order to analyze at length the meaning of a certain scripture.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The quiet placement of this triad immediately before the four truths is not incidental: seeing and cultivation are precisely the two paths by which the four truths are directly realized and then thoroughly cultivated, so that this triad's own threefold division of what remains to be cut off supplies the very machinery the coming discussion of the truths will need to describe how a practitioner actually progresses through them.

5. The Four Truths: Three Rival Definitions

Three schools define the four truths' own substance differently. The Abhidharma masters say the five grasping-aggregates are the truth of suffering, the contaminated cause is accumulation, analytical cessation is cessation, and the dharma of the learner and non-learner is the path. 9. Reconciling Truth with Distortion and False Speech

Two objections test the treatise's own definition of truth. If truth means the undistorted, how can the four distortions — mistaking the impermanent for permanent, suffering for pleasant, impure for pure, selfless for self — still count as truths at all, given they proceed by way of distortion? The answer distinguishes conditions: three conditions (decisive judgment, augmentation, one-sided inversion) establish something as a distortion, while the further fact that it is existent, real, and accords with real characteristics still gathers it within truth. If truth means what is free of falsehood, how can false speech — claiming to have seen what one has not, or not to have seen what one has — still be gathered within truth? The same double-conditioning resolves it: contradicting one's own thought to deceive another establishes it as false speech, while its still possessing the nature of cause and fruit gathers it within truth regardless.

10. Are There Three, Five, or Eight Truths? The Basis of the Four Truths

A structural puzzle asks whether the four truths are established with real entities, with cause and fruit, or with direct realization as their basis — each option apparently yielding the wrong number. Real entities would yield only three, since suffering and accumulation share one substance; cause and fruit would yield five, since the undefiled path's own cause and fruit are as distinct as a contaminated dharma's; direct realization would yield eight, since a practitioner separately realizes each truth first in the desire realm, then again, jointly, in the form and formless realms. The resolution: the truths rest on cause and fruit, but the noble path's own cause and fruit are combined into one truth precisely because their aspect — path, suchness, conduct, deliverance — is entirely shared, unlike a contaminated dharma's own four aspects as fruit (suffering, impermanence, emptiness, no-self) and four as cause (cause, accumulation, arising, condition), which remain genuinely distinct and so are not combined.

11. The Characteristics of the Four Truths: Pārśva, Vasumitra, and the Iron Ball

Pārśva assigns oppression, growth, tranquility, and deliverance as the four truths' own characteristics; Vasumitra offers two parallel formulas built on circulation and its reverting, and on birth's own basis circulating and coming to rest. The Great Virtuous One reaches for a single unforgettable image: the five grasping-aggregates are like an extremely heated iron ball drawn forth from a furnace, still pursued by fire's own force even once removed from the flame — sunk in the ocean of suffering, pervaded by the three kinds of suffering, they are as thoroughly fused with suffering as that iron ball with fire, and it is this very fusion that constitutes the characteristic of the truth of suffering; the same aggregate, arising from affliction and transformed by karma, circulates through the destinies without beginning, giving the characteristic of accumulation; its final freedom from affliction and karma ends this circulation, giving cessation; and cultivating pure moral discipline and concentration to directly realize this exhaustion gives the characteristic of the path.

12. Why Does the Buddha Speak of "One Truth"? Refuting Rival Liberations and Rival Paths

If there are four truths, why does a verse speak of only one — "there is one truth, with no second" — the outsiders' own doubt and their many rival truths met with the Buddha's declaration that among them there exists no renunciant fruit of the path at all? Pārśva offers three readings: each of the four truths is itself singular, with no second suffering or second path, so "one" need not contradict "four"; or "one truth" means the single truth of cessation, spoken to refute the outsiders' four rival liberations — the spheres of infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception — none of which is genuine liberation at all, being still within formless existence; or "one truth" means the single truth of the path, spoken to refute the outsiders' many rival paths — self-starvation, lying in ashes, following the sun's course, eating only fruit or vegetables, lying on thorns, never lying down, wearing cast-off rags — every one of them a deviant, false, and fraudulent path that only evil persons practice, the truly pure path being nothing other than the noble eightfold path itself.

13. The Conventional and the Ultimate Truth: Four Views

A further scriptural pair, the conventional truth and the ultimate truth, receives four competing accounts of how they map onto the four noble truths. One view assigns suffering and accumulation to the conventional (male and female, vessels and clothing, every worldly matter falling there) and cessation and the path to the ultimate. A second assigns the first three truths to the conventional, since the Buddha speaks even of cessation using conventional images like a city or a palace, leaving only the path, spoken of with no conventional designation at all, as purely ultimate. A third assigns all four truths to the conventional, since even the path is spoken of using the conventional names "renunciant" and "brahmin," leaving only the bare principle that all dharmas are empty and selfless as the ultimate. The compilers settle a fourth way: every one of the four truths possesses both a conventional and an ultimate aspect at once — suffering's own ultimate truth being that it is impermanent, empty, and selfless; accumulation's, the principle of cause, arising, and condition.

14. Is the Conventional Existent Because of the Ultimate?

A sharp dilemma follows: is the conventional nature within what is conventional existent because of the ultimate, or non-existent because of it? Either answer seems to collapse the two truths into one, the ultimate truth alone. The resolution affirms existence — since the Buddha's own statement of two truths is genuine, the conventional must be existent because of the ultimate — yet insists that, in reality, there is indeed only a single truth, the ultimate; two truths are established only in dependence upon a distinguishing condition, never in dependence upon a real entity, exactly as a single feeling possesses four conditions or six causes as its nature, one condition establishing it as causal, a different condition establishing it as predominant, without the feeling itself ever splitting into multiple substances.

15. The Three Brahmin-Truths: Two Interpretations

A scripture names three brahmin-truths: no sentient being whatsoever ought to be harmed; I do not belong to that one, that one does not belong to me; and whatever possesses the nature of accumulation invariably possesses the nature of cessation. One reading takes "brahmin" literally, as any renunciant of another path, with these three statements alone true among everything such renunciants say. A second reading takes "brahmin" to mean one who abides within the Buddha's own dharma, the whole passage aimed at three specific outsider claims: those who kill animals in sacrifice while calling themselves true brahmins are answered with non-harming; those who cultivate the holy life merely for rebirth in heaven's own pleasures are answered with non-attachment; and those who cling to annihilationism or eternalism are answered with the principle that accumulation is not annihilation and cessation is not eternity, the middle way running exactly between the two.

16. Observing the Four Directions, and the Faculty of Wisdom

Two closing scriptural puzzles round out the fascicle. Why does the Buddha speak of the four truths using the language of the four directions? Simply because those to be converted, hearing the truths spoken this way, more readily awaken — the Buddha assigning east to suffering and west to accumulation since direct realization observes suffering first, south to the path since path and south are both worthy of offerings, and north to cessation since both are supreme. And why does scripture say one ought to know the faculty of wisdom specifically with respect to the four truths, when wisdom and the truths do not strictly include one another, and wisdom can in any case take every dharma as its object? The answer is neither inclusion nor object: it is simply that wisdom's own function is most supreme precisely at the moment the four truths are established, exactly as faith is most supreme in establishing the four verifications of pure faith, diligence in the four right efforts, mindfulness in the four foundations of mindfulness, and concentration in the four bases of supernormal power.

Fascicle 78 continues the same nyāsa, opening with Śāriputra's own statement that every wholesome dharma that arises is gathered within the four noble truths.
