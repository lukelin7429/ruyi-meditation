The Wish-Fulfilling Jewel

Fascicle 102 works through the maṇi-jewel simile for unshakeable liberation, the classic fourfold arhat proclamation, and a new topic distinguishing genuine clarity from false worldly arts — closing with why only the Buddha's own knowledge of exhaustion is honored as a power. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 102 continues the Mind-Reading-Knowledge Nyāsa (他心智納息第三之四), completing the analysis of time-bound and unshakeable mental liberation (時解脫 / 不動心解脫) before opening a further topic on the learner's and non-learner's own clarity (學明 / 無學明) — the three genuine clarities distinguished from false worldly arts, and the four kinds of supreme virtue distributed across the Buddha, the solitary-realizer, and the disciple. Terms established across fascicles 93–101 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Why "Cherished," Why "Unshakeable"

Fascicle 102 opens exactly where fascicle 101 broke off, working through seven converging reasons why time-bound liberation is called "cherished" — a dull-faculty practitioner's own greater attachment to his own virtue, the constant effort still required to manifest it, the fear of retrogression, faith rather than wisdom as the dominant faculty, a gentler temperament, and a lingering weariness toward the very emptiness, wishlessness, and signless concentrations that ought to carry him further. Three further reasons explain why the unshakeable-dharma arhat's own liberation is called "unshakeable": its own supreme excellence beyond comparison, its complete freedom from being agitated or destabilized by affliction, and its own guaranteed permanence, exactly like an arrow that a master archer never fails to land.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seven reasons for "cherished" quietly assemble a composite psychological portrait of the lesser arhat that is more sympathetic than clinical: dull faculty, gentleness, faith over wisdom, and a residual fondness for one's own hard-won attainment are none of them failures exactly — they simply describe an arhat who arrived at the very same final freedom by a route that never stopped requiring conscious tending, in contrast to the unshakeable arhat's own attainment, secure enough that he no longer needs to hold onto it at all.

2. The Wish-Fulfilling Jewel

A rich, cascading simile compares unshakeable mental liberation to the maṇi-jewel — firm, supreme, faultless, luminously clear, stainless, pure, and rare — capable of shattering the darkness of ignorance exactly as a luminous gem dispels a dark room's own shadows, of purifying affliction's own turbid water exactly as a water-clarifying gem, of remaining permanently well-founded exactly as a square-based gem set down anywhere, of removing the poverty of noble wealth exactly as a priceless gem drawn into a household, and, most movingly, of benefiting every sentient being exactly as a wish-fulfilling gem atop a lofty banner rains down treasure according to each supplicant's own need — the Buddha himself, atop the banner of heedfulness, raining down the treasure of the true dharma according to each being's own distinct delight.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The final image in this cascade — the Buddha's own unshakeable liberation functioning not merely as his own private attainment but as a wish-fulfilling gem raining down the dharma according to what each listener specifically needs — quietly reframes everything the preceding six similes established about firmness, purity, and permanence: liberation's own ultimate value, on this reading, is not that it makes one personally invulnerable, but that it becomes, once achieved, an inexhaustible resource other beings can draw from freely, each according to his own particular thirst.

3. Two Kinds of Undefiled Wisdom, Seven Ways to Tell Them Apart

A tetralemma resolves whether time-bound mental liberation is always associated with the knowledge of exhaustion (yes, necessarily) before turning to what actually distinguishes the arhat who cultivates only two undefiled wisdoms (the knowledge of exhaustion and the non-learner's own right view) from the arhat who fully cultivates all three (adding the knowledge of non-arising). No fewer than a dozen converging pairs of distinctions are offered — mind-liberated-only against liberated-in-both-mind-and-wisdom, narrow against vast force of cause and effort, calm-abiding conduct against insight conduct, tranquility-first against insight-first entry into the path, dull against sharp faculty, and the wholesome root of non-attachment against the wholesome root of non-delusion predominating — each one another lens onto the very same underlying division.

Is every time-bound mental liberation associated with the knowledge of exhaustion? And so on, stated at length. One asks: for what reason is this treatise composed? The answer: it is in order to bring resolution to those who harbor doubt — that is, there are those who, having heard it said that the arhat liberated in time cultivates solely the knowledge of exhaustion and the non-learner's own right view, thereupon doubt that the unshakeable-dharma arhat, too, cultivates solely these same two; there are, further, those who, having heard it said that the unshakeable-dharma arhat cultivates the knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising, together with the non-learner's own right view, thereupon doubt that the arhat liberated in time, too, cultivates these same three kinds. A precise sevenfold comparison distinguishes these two undefiled knowledges — cause against fruit, already-acted against nurtured-by-that-action, newly-or-previously attained against solely newly attained, the path of liberation or advancement against solely advancement, five kinds of arhat against a single kind, dull-or-sharp faculty against sharp-faculty-only — before a debate over which specific subsequent cognition constitutes the very first knowledge of exhaustion (suffering's, or accumulation's) resolves in favor of both, arising as genuine alternatives rather than in fixed sequence.

5. My Birth Is Already Exhausted

The classic fourfold arhat proclamation — "my birth is already exhausted, the pure conduct is established, what needed to be done is done, I do not receive further existence" — receives extraordinarily careful analysis. Five distinct senses of "birth" are distinguished before the text settles on the most technical: the four aggregates of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, exhausted across all three times through a specifically formless-realm lens; a genuinely moving simile then likens the practitioner's own relationship to future birth to a person confronting three kinds of peril — past danger already endured needs no guarding against, present danger cannot be guarded against, and only future danger is worth defending against through wealth, friendship, or skillful means. No fewer than eight competing schemes then map each of the four clauses onto specific technical categories — the knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising, or the four truths' own separate knowledges, or their full comprehension, observation, witnessing, or cutting-off — with the Venerable Pārśva dissenting that the whole fourfold clause describes no separate technical category at all, but simply praises the arhat's completion as an undivided whole.

6. Why "Knowledge of Exhaustion"? And a Puzzle About the Sixteen Aspects

A careful etymological puzzle asks whether the knowledge of exhaustion is named for taking exhaustion (cessation) as its object, or for arising within an already-exhausted body — resolved in favor of the latter, with the crucial refinement that it must be both the very first knowledge to arise there and pervasively present, distinguishing it precisely from the non-learner's own right view (pervasive but not first) and the knowledge of non-arising (neither pervasive, since one liberated in time lacks it, nor first, since it always follows the knowledge of exhaustion). A further, genuinely delicate puzzle then asks whether any undefiled wisdom exists apart from the classic sixteen aspects of the four truths — reconciling several treatises' own seemingly freer phrasing ("I have already known suffering and will no longer come to know it") by showing each is simply the same sixteen aspects addressed through five different lenses: preparatory effort, counteraction, function, continuity, and the describable person.

7. False Clarity and True: A New Topic Opens

An entirely new topic opens: the learner's and the non-learner's own "clarity" (vidyā), defined as their own undefiled wisdom and eight kinds of knowledge respectively — introduced by refuting an extensive catalogue of worldly arts the world falsely mistakes for genuine clarity: limb-lore, beast-lore, bird-lore, star-lore, crow- and peacock-divination, snake-charming, and more. True clarity, by contrast, requires not merely thorough understanding but the capacity to discern and directly realize the four noble truths themselves, remaining permanently free of confusion by ignorance, doubt, or wrong view — disqualifying even the sharpest worldly, contaminated wisdom, since it remains entangled with both wisdom and ignorance at once, like a person involved with both his own kinsman and his own enemy. A rich puzzle then reconciles this strict definition with scripture's own naming of "three clarities" (recollection of former abodes, knowledge of death and rebirth, exhaustion of outflows): only the third is clarity in the fullest, supreme-meaning sense; the first two earn the name only figuratively, for opposing affliction and drawing forth the genuine article, without yet being it themselves.

8. Why Only Three of Six Superknowledges Count as Clarity

A cascade of five converging explanations addresses why, among the six superknowledges, only the latter three — recollection of former abodes, death and rebirth, and exhaustion of outflows — are honored with the name "clarity," while miraculous transformation, the divine ear, and the knowledge of others' minds are not: each of the three genuine clarities gives rise to a profound weariness toward either the past or the future limit of birth-and-death, culminating in delight in nirvāṇa, while the other three superknowledges function merely like a craft, a sense faculty, or a narrow particular-characteristic cognition, lacking any such transformative weight. A further puzzle reconciles the *Saṅgītiparyāya*'s own naming of all three as "the non-learner's own clarity," even though the first two are not, strictly, learner-or-non-learner dharmas at all — resolved through the same principle that names a wholesome, contaminated concentration "noble" simply because it arises within a noble one's own body.

9. Four Kinds of Supreme Virtue, and Why Only the Buddha Has a Power

A precise ranking distributes four kinds of supreme virtue — superknowledge, clarity, power, and instructive guidance — across the Tathāgata's, the solitary-realizer's, the disciple's, and the ordinary learner's own body: only the Buddha's own knowledge of the exhaustion of outflows carries all four; the two vehicles' own equivalent carries three, lacking power alone. Two beautifully matched stories dramatize the gap: Śāriputra, using every grade of the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge across all four dhyānas, cannot see how long ago a stranger was once his own friend, nor, using the full range of the divine eye, where a person who just died was reborn — in both cases, the Buddha reveals an answer lying entirely beyond the disciples' and solitary-realizers' own domain. The fascicle closes on its own real climax: the Buddha's own knowledge of exhaustion is named a "power" not merely because it exhausts his own outflows faster and more surely than any other being's (illustrated through two woodcutters, one wielding a sharp axe, one a dull one), but because he alone wields it to diagnose precisely which practice-path — painful and slow, or pleasant and swift — will lead each and every other sentient being to exhaust their own outflows as well.
