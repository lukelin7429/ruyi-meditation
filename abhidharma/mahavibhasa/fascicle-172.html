Mapping the Five Destinies

Fascicle 172 continues the Subsumption Nyāsa (攝納息第三之七) with the treatise's fullest cosmology yet: the architecture and etymology of the eight great hells, the animal kingdom's foot-counts across every realm, the ghost-world toured by King Nimi, the four great continents and eight sub-continents of the human destiny, the heavens from the Four Great Kings to Akaniṣṭha, and an extended investigation into whether the asura belongs among the gods or the ghosts. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Closing the Aggregates, Opening the Five Destinies

Fascicle 172 briefly closes out the running "eliminated by which concentration" survey for the five aggregates and five aggregates of attachment, before turning to a subject that will occupy nearly the entire fascicle: the five destinies (五趣) into which every sentient being is reborn. The treatise opens with a genuinely difficult doctrinal puzzle — is a destiny's own nature neutral, or does it span wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral alike — resolving an apparent contradiction with the Prakaraṇapāda by proposing, in one striking move, that the earlier treatise simply contains a scribal error.

2. Why Hell Is Called Naraka: Seven Rival Etymologies

The treatise turns to its first destiny, naraka, offering a cascade of no fewer than seven competing etymological explanations for the name itself — from the straightforward claim that it is simply the place devoid of joy, to elaborate wordplay breaking the term into syllables meaning "person" and "evil," or "joy" and "destroyed," each theory illustrated with its own reasoning or verse.

The sheer number of competing etymologies offered without any attempt to declare a winner is itself revealing: this tradition treated a name's meaning as worth exploring from every angle available, comfortable leaving multiple explanations standing side by side rather than forcing a single verdict where the canon itself gave none.

3. The Architecture of Hell: Depth, Dimensions, and the Sixteen Adjuncts

The treatise turns to hard cosmological measurement: two competing traditions for exactly how many yojanas beneath Jambudvīpa each of the eight great hells lies, resolved against an apparent contradiction with the Prajñaptiśāstra's much smaller measurement of Jambudvīpa's own circumference by comparing the continent's shape to a mound of grain, narrow at the top and broad at the bottom. Answer: their own subordinate villages dwell within the salt sea, though the asura king himself dwells within that very mountain. Others hold: within the great salt sea, upon a golden wheel, there stands a great golden platform, five hundred yojanas high and five hundred yojanas broad, and, upon this platform, there lies a city, which is their own dwelling-place. Question: if so, then why does the Prajñaptiśāstra state that the nāgas of the inner sea, upon seeing the asura army clad in armor of gold, silver, beryl, and crystal, wielding every manner of weapon of gold, silver, and the like, emerging from the asura city, then report this to the gods? Answer: the gods station the nāgas upon the golden mountains as sentinels, and it is from there that they see, from afar, the asura army emerging from its city within the great salt sea, and report this to the assembled gods. Just as the Trāyastriṃśa heaven has four excellent gardens — namely, the Garden of Many Chariots, the Coarse and Evil Garden, the Mixed Forest, and the Garden of Joy — the asura king likewise has four gardens: the first named Delight, the second Joy, the third Extreme Joy, and the fourth Loveliness. Just as the Trāyastriṃśa heaven has its Pārijāta tree, the asura king likewise has his Citrapāṭalī tree. Just as Śakra presides as lord over the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, Vemacitra presides as lord over the asuras. Question: what is the physical form of the asuras? Answer: their bodies stand upright. Question: what of their speech? Answer: they all speak in the noble language. Question: to which destiny do the asuras belong? Answer: some hold: they are subsumed under the destiny of the god. Question: if so, then why are they incapable of entering correctly determined liberation from birth? Answer: because they are covered over by flattery and deceit. What is the nature of this? Answer: they constantly suspect the Buddha of favoring the gods as his own faction — so that, were the Buddha to teach them the four foundations of mindfulness, they would think to themselves, "the Buddha has taught us four foundations of mindfulness, but he must surely have taught the gods five"; and, were the Buddha to teach them the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, they would think to themselves, "the Buddha has taught us thirty-seven, but he must surely have taught the gods thirty-eight" — and it is because their minds are covered over by just such flattery and deceit that they are incapable of entering correctly determined liberation from birth. Furthermore, one ought not conclude, merely from their incapacity to enter correctly determined liberation from birth, that they must therefore be subsumed under an evil destiny — for the Dasyus and the Mlecchas, too, are likewise incapable of entering correctly determined liberation from birth, yet are not thereby subsumed under any evil destiny, and the very same should hold for the asuras as well. According to this latter account, the asuras are instead subsumed under the ghost-destiny. Question: if so, then why does a sūtra report Śakra as saying to the asura king Vemacitra, "you were originally a god of this place"? Answer: Śakra ought properly to have said, "you were originally a ghost of this place" — but he spoke of him as a god instead out of respect for his own father-in-law, offering this affectionate speech, and also in order to please Śacī, who was listening, and bring her joy. Question: if they are indeed ghosts, then how could they possibly intermarry so closely with the gods? Answer: the gods, craving physical beauty, pay no regard to lineage or clan — just as Śacī, the asura maiden, was of matchless, incomparable beauty, and it was for this reason that Śakra took her as his own wife; and just as the daughter of the great Kiṃnara king Mahādruma, named "Mind-Ravisher," was likewise of unrivaled, matchless beauty, and, although she was subsumed under the animal-destiny, Prince Sudāna, the son of the Sumeru king — the Bodhisattva himself — sought her hand in marriage, exactly the same holds true here as well. Question: how, then, could they also wage war against the gods? Answer: it does sometimes happen that an inferior party contends against a superior one, just as a slave may quarrel with his master, or a dog may fight with a man.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The asuras' displacement — driven out not by force but by the Trāyastriṃśa gods simply refusing to recognize them as "our own kind" while quietly moving in around them — is a striking cosmological parable about how exclusion actually operates: no single violent act evicts the asuras, only the accumulating weight of never being acknowledged as belonging.

9. Śakra's Asura Bride, and the Household Spirits People Mistake for Gods

The investigation into the asuras' own destiny-classification runs remarkably long: are they gods, incapable of the noble path only because of their paranoid suspicion that the Buddha secretly favors the gods over them, or are they in fact ghosts, wrongly called "gods" by Śakra himself only out of in-law courtesy toward his asura bride Śacī? The fascicle closes by extending the very same classificatory question to a list of household and guardian spirits worshipped as gods by ordinary people — Hārītī and Skanda among them — each defended, when their own boastful claims to divinity are shown to be mistaken, on the grounds that pride and self-aggrandizement, not deliberate deception, produced their false statements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The recurring principle that a spirit's own sincere but mistaken claim to be a god — born of pride, ignorance of its true destiny, or simple flattery toward those it serves — never constitutes actual false speech is a direct echo of fascicle 171's careful distinction between an honest mistake and a deliberate lie: this fascicle's cosmology and that fascicle's ethics of speech turn out to be governed by exactly the same underlying principle, applied now to beings confused about their own metaphysical status rather than about ordinary facts.
