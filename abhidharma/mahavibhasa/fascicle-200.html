The Middle Way

The final fascicle of the Mahāvibhāṣā Śāstra closes the sixty-two views with the last doctrines of possessing, lacking, and neither possessing nor lacking perception after death, seven doctrines of utter extinction, and five of nirvāṇa mistaken for the four dhyānas — then undertakes a sweeping act of classification, sorting every view catalogued across three fascicles, teacher by named teacher, into eternalism or annihilationism. A four-way debate over whether the life-force is identical with or distinct from the body leads into the treatise's own positive case for the Buddhist middle way: matter and mind are neither utterly extinguished nor permanently abiding. The fascicle, and the entire two-hundred-fascicle treatise, closes with Xuánzàng's own two verses marking the completion of his translation. This page carries Ru‑Yi's complete translation.

The Mahāvibhāṣā Śāstra is complete. This fascicle closes the Nyāsa of Views (見納息), the Views Aggregate (見蘊第八), and all two hundred fascicles of the treatise. Ru‑Yi's translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), begun at fascicle 93, concludes here.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

This fascicle closes the treatise's long catalogue of heterodox views by naming, where the Chinese source itself names, the historical teachers behind them — reconstructed here as Pūraṇa (Kāśyapa), Maskarin (Gośāla), Sañjayin, Kakuda Kātyāyana, and the Nirgrantha (Jain) disciples. Terms established across fascicles 93–199 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Sixteen Doctrines: Self Possessing Perception After Death

The final fascicle opens mid-stream in the Brahmajāla's sixty-two views, working through the first four of sixteen doctrines that a self possesses perception after death — form, formless, both, and neither — each keyed to whether matter or the formless aggregates are grasped as the self's own substance, and to a striking simile: no single, unified flavor can be found by separately tasting sweet, sour, salty, pungent, and bland, yet these ascetics still fuse the five aggregates into a single self.

2. Finite, Infinite, and the Textures of Perception and Feeling

Four more doctrines specify whether this perceiving self is finite, infinite, both, or neither, grounded in vivid analogies — a self blazing like a finger-joint of light, or vast as the sun and unseeable by those in darkness. A further set of distinctions covers whether the self's perception is single or manifold, slight or immeasurable, and whether its lot is pure pleasure, pure pain, a mixture, or neither, closing the sixteen doctrines before opening the eight doctrines of a self that altogether lacks perception after death.

9. Why Death Is Not Annihilation, and Why Nothing Is Permanent

The Views Aggregate closes with its own positive argument for the middle way: a chain of momentary causal succession — mind drawing forth mind, body drawing forth body, each prior moment powerful enough to produce the next until only utter aversion cuts the chain — explains why death is not annihilation, while the observed gradual, gapless unfolding of infancy into childhood into old age, rather than a hidden constant self merely appearing and vanishing, explains why nothing is permanent either.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two positive arguments closing the Views Aggregate are asymmetrical in a way worth noticing: annihilation is refuted by tracing an unbroken chain of causal force from mind to mind and body to body, while permanence is refuted by the much more mundane observation that infancy becomes childhood becomes old age gradually and without gaps — one argument reaches for metaphysics, the other simply asks the reader to remember what growing up actually looked like.

Fascicle 200 Complete — The Treatise Is Finished

This closes fascicle 200 — the final fascicle — of Ru‑Yi's translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra. The fascicle finishes the sixty-two views with the last doctrines of a self possessing, lacking, and neither possessing nor lacking perception after death; seven doctrines of utter extinction, one for each cosmological level from the desire realm to the summit of existence; and five doctrines mistaking sensory pleasure or the four dhyānas for nirvāṇa in this very life. It then undertakes the treatise's most sweeping act of classification, sorting every view catalogued across fascicles 198 through 200 — named, where the source itself names them, after historical teachers such as Pūraṇa, Maskarin, Sañjayin, Kakuda Kātyāyana, and the Nirgrantha disciples — into just two categories: the view of existence (eternalism) and the view of non-existence (annihilationism). A four-way debate, credited to the named masters Vasumitra and Bhadanta, weighs whether the life-force is identical with the body, distinct from it, not identical, or not distinct, each position grounded in a cascade of everyday observations. The Views Aggregate then closes with the treatise's own positive case for the Buddhist middle way — a chain of momentary causal succession explaining why death is not annihilation, and the observed gradual, gapless unfolding of a single life explaining why nothing is permanent either.
