1. Defining Vision: the Eye, the Five Views, and Four Similes of Night and Day

Fascicle 95 opens the third part of the Branches of the Learner Nyāsa with a systematic return to the Knowledge Aggregate's own founding question — what is vision, knowledge, and wisdom? — first dismissing three rival positions (that every conditioned dharma is itself vision, that the forbearance at the border of direct realization is knowledge, and that the knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising is itself vision) before defining vision (見) as exactly four things: the eye faculty, the five wrong views, worldly right view, and the learner's and non-learner's own vision. The eye faculty earns the name through four converging proofs — the noble ones' own usage, worldly convention, scripture, and direct observation — while the five views earn it through the memorable simile of the śiśumāra, a sea creature that clenches its prey so firmly that only the sword of the noble path can pry it loose. Four graded similes then rank the four kinds of vision by clarity: the five views see as though on a misty night, worldly right view as though on a clear night, the learner's own vision as though on a misty day, and the non-learner's own vision as though on a clear day.

The answer: by virtue of four matters — first, because the noble ones speak of it thus; second, because worldly convention speaks of it thus; third, because the scripture speaks of it thus; fourth, because it is directly observed to be so in the world. As for the noble ones and worldly convention speaking of it thus: this refers to the fact that the various noble ones and the various worldly people alike say: my eye sees that person's own coming and going, walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, and the like — and, further, upon seeing a person stumble and fall into confusion, they alike say: since you saw it with your own eye, why did you act this way? As for the scripture speaking of it thus: this refers to the fact that the scripture states: once the eye has seen a form, one ought not to grasp at its overall sign, nor at its particular features; it, further, states: once the eye has seen a form, one ought to contemplate its impurity, reflecting properly upon it; it, further, states: once the eye has seen a form, whether pleasing or displeasing, one ought neither to crave it nor to feel aversion toward it; it, further, states: once the eye has seen a form, the three mental approaches of joy, sorrow, and equanimity arise; it, further, states: once the eye has seen a form, one ought not to feel elation or grief, but ought solely to abide in equanimity, right mindfulness, and right awareness. As for it being directly observed to be so in the world: this refers to the fact that it is directly observed in the world that whoever possesses a bright, clear eye sees without error, while whoever does not possess a bright, clear eye sees with error; and, further, it is directly observed in the world that whoever possesses the eye faculty is capable of seeing the various forms,

worldly right view, with respect to its object, is like seeing form on a clear night; the learner's own vision, with respect to its object, is like seeing form on a misty day; the non-learner's own vision, with respect to its object, is like seeing form on a clear day.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The śiśumāra simile is doing real philosophical work, not just decoration: it explains how something as thoroughly mistaken as a wrong view can still deserve the name "vision" at all — not because it sees correctly, but because it clenches its object with the same sharp, unrelenting force that genuine vision does, differing from right vision only in what it has seized, not in how tightly. The four-similes ranking is equally precise in what it implies: night versus day tracks purity (defiled views versus the wholesome), while misty versus clear tracks completeness (still-learning versus finished) — two independent axes of clarity, quietly nested inside what looks at first like a single graded list.

2. Defining Knowledge and Wisdom

Knowledge (智) is defined as the wisdom associated with the five sense-consciousnesses together with, excepting the undefiled forbearance, the remaining wisdom associated with mental consciousness — each sorted into wholesome, defiled, and neutral-and-unobscured varieties, with the wholesome further divided into what is attained through preparatory effort (the wisdom accomplished through hearing, thinking, and cultivation), through becoming free of defilement, or innately at birth into a given ground, and the undefiled divided into the learner's own eight kinds of knowledge and the non-learner's own knowledge of exhaustion, of non-arising, and right view. Wisdom (慧) is then defined more simply as the wisdom associated with all six consciousnesses, differing from knowledge only in explicitly including the eight undefiled forbearances, since investigation-of-dharma pervades every mind whatsoever.

The wholesome is of two kinds — first, contaminated; second, undefiled. The contaminated wholesome is of three kinds — first, attained through preparatory effort; second, attained through becoming free of defilement; third, innate. "Attained through preparatory effort" refers to the wisdom accomplished through hearing, the wisdom accomplished through thinking, and the wisdom accomplished through cultivation. What is wisdom? The answer: the wisdom associated with the six consciousnesses. This is of three kinds — namely, wholesome, defiled, and neutral-and-unobscured, exactly as fully stated before, with the following distinction: the eight undefiled forbearances, too, are gathered within wisdom, since investigation-of-dharma pervades all of them, and every mind whatsoever, together with what accompanies it, does indeed possess wisdom. Reading note — Ru-Yi

That wisdom is defined as the broadest of the three terms — gathering in even the undefiled forbearances that knowledge itself excludes — sets up the entire tetralemma apparatus that follows: vision, knowledge, and wisdom are not three parallel categories of equal size but three overlapping circles of different radii, and the treatise's real work in the sections ahead is mapping exactly where each pair's boundaries diverge.

3. Is Every Vision Knowledge? The Eye, the Forbearance, and the Question of Exhaustion

A tetralemma opens: the eye faculty and the undefiled forbearance are vision but not knowledge, the former because it possesses form and lacks any of knowledge's own mental characteristics, the latter because — as the Great Virtuous One, the Venerable Vasumitra, and the Venerable Wu each explain in their own terms — forbearance has not yet reached a final, scrutinized decision about its object. A far longer debate then asks whether the knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising, the arhat's own culminating attainments, are vision or knowledge, with the Venerable Ghoṣa and others arguing they are knowledge alone, since their own task is already accomplished and they no longer seek — while the Great Virtuous One insists they remain vision, since they are still of the very own-nature of decisive discernment. The Vaibhāṣika verdict sides against the Great Virtuous One: because the arhat is said to possess exactly ten non-learner's branches, not nine, the exhaustion and non-arising knowledges must be classified as knowledge, not vision, their task having reached its ultimate completion.

Ru-Yi Translation

The answer: a tetralemma ought to be cast, since vision and knowledge, in their own-nature, differ in breadth and narrowness. There is vision that is not knowledge — namely, the eye faculty and the undefiled forbearance. One asks: for what reason is the eye faculty not named knowledge? The answer: the eye faculty possesses form, whereas knowledge does not possess form. Further, the eye faculty is not associated with anything, possesses no support, no object, no aspect, and no alertness — knowledge is not so. One asks: for what reason is the undefiled forbearance not knowledge? The answer: because, with respect to the truth being observed, the undefiled forbearance, although it forbears, has not yet reached a final decision; although it observes, has not yet fully scrutinized; although it seeks, has not yet reached its ultimate completion; although it examines, has not yet fully comprehended; although it directly realizes, has not yet re-examined — solely engaging in effort and preparatory activity without cease — and it is for this reason that it is not named knowledge. Further, the very meaning of "decisiveness" is the very meaning of "knowledge"; forbearance co-arises together with the very doubt yet to be cut off, not having reached the utmost decisiveness with respect to the object seen, and it is for this reason that it is not named knowledge. The Venerable Vasumitra speaks as follows: although forbearance genuinely endures with respect to the noble truths, it has not yet fully scrutinized and known them, and it is for this reason that it is not named knowledge. 4. Craft-Wisdom, Wrong Livelihood, and the Vision-Knowledge Overlap

The analysis turns to why the remaining contaminated mental-consciousness wisdom — apart from the five views and worldly right view — fails to count as vision at all: defiled wisdom is covered over by its own accompanying affliction, while neutral-and-unobscured wisdom (born of ripening, deportment, craft, or the all-pervading fruit) simply lacks sufficient force. The craft-wisdom case draws out a genuinely fine distinction: even the god of craft Viśvakarman's own most skillful work is covered over by wrong livelihood, since every act of craft ultimately arises from the wish to sustain one's own life — and this holds, the text insists, even at the very moment the craftsman's mind is not itself defiled, because the drawing force behind the act remains wrong livelihood regardless. The section closes by naming what vision and knowledge do share: the eight forbearances, the learner's own eight kinds of knowledge, and the non-learner's own right view, each possessing both characteristics at once.

7. Cutting Off and Fully Comprehending Vision, Knowledge, and Wisdom

The five-gateway apparatus familiar from earlier fascicles — concentration, gathering-in, possession, and now cutting-off — closes the possession analysis: whatever vision has already been cut off and fully comprehended, that same knowledge and wisdom have likewise already been cut off and fully comprehended, since the three share identical stages of abandonment. 8. Is Right View the Investigation-of-Dharma Awakening-Factor?

The fascicle turns to a new question inherited from the preceding treatise: how do right view and right knowledge relate to the investigation-of-dharma awakening-factor (擇法覺支), one of the seven factors of awakening? Since the awakening-factors are said to be solely undefiled, worldly right view fails to qualify, while the knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising fails to qualify as right view at all — leaving only the undefiled forbearances, the learner's own eight kinds of knowledge, and the non-learner's own right view as genuinely both. 9. Do the Awakening-Factors Arise All at Once, or One by One?

The fascicle closes with a vigorous debate against the Dārṣṭāntikas and the Great Virtuous One, who hold that mental factors arise strictly one by one in succession — like a long line of merchants filing single-file down a narrow road — citing two scriptures as proof: one distinguishing which three awakening-factors it is "time" versus "not time" to cultivate depending on whether the mind is sinking or agitated, the other reporting Śāriputra's own claim to dwell in any of the seven awakening-factors' concentration at will, at any hour of the day. Against this, the Vaibhāṣikas insist the awakening-factors do indeed arise together, all at one and the same time — the "time and not-time" language describing not sequential arising but shifting functional predominance between the calm-abiding aspect (tranquility, concentration, equanimity) and the insight aspect (investigation-of-dharma, effort, joy), depending on whether the mind needs restraining or lifting, and further depending on which aspect the practitioner originally relied upon to enter the noble path.
