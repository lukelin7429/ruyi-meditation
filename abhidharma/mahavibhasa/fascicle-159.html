What Mind Does an Attainment Keep?

Fascicle 159 continues the Attainment Nyāsa with an exhaustive cross-tabulation of non-material dharmas — attainment and the four conditioned characteristics — against the mind they co-arise with, first across the three moral qualities and then across the desire, form, and formless realms and the unfettered. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Why Only Non-Material Dharmas? Setting Up a New Cross-Tabulation

Fascicle 159 opens the Attainment Nyāsa's third part by asking why the treatise only investigates whether a non-material dharma — a mind-dissociated formation like attainment itself, or the four conditioned characteristics — shares its moral quality with the mind it arises alongside, never asking the same of matter: matter, unlike these formations, is mostly not conascent with mind, does not arise through mind's power, and does not follow mind's course, so the question would not even apply to it.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Whenever a dharma arises as a wholesome non-material dharma, is that dharma co-arisen with a wholesome mind?" — and so on in full. Question: why is this treatise composed? Answer: in order to check the view of those who hold that dissociated dharmas have no arising, aging, abiding, ceasing, or regressing, and in order to make clear our own school's correct principle that they are indeed associated dharmas tracking a mind, this treatise is composed. Question: why does this passage ask only about a non-material dharma's arising, and not about a material dharma's? Answer: the author of this treatise simply intended it so, and composed the treatise according to that very intention — so long as it does not contradict the characteristics of the dharmas involved, no objection should be raised. Some hold: whatever dharma can be found across every realm, every ground, every category, and every kind of mind is asked about here; matter cannot be found across every realm, and so on down to every kind of mind, and so it is not asked about. Others hold: whatever dharma is, for the most part, conascent with mind, arising through the power of mind and following mind's own course, is asked about here; matter, for the most part, is not conascent with mind, does not arise through the power of mind, and does not follow mind's own course. For instance, at the moment of the patient acceptance of the knowledge of suffering-in-the-Dharma, ninety-eight latent defilements' worth of attainment arise, yet none of those attainments are themselves conascent with that patient acceptance, nor do they arise through its power, nor do they follow its course — and even though these are non-material dharmas conascent in time with that patient acceptance, this passage still does not speak of them as "co-arisen with" that patient-acceptance mind, let alone speak so of any material dharma — and it is for this reason that matter is not asked about here. What this passage means by a non-material dharma's arising "co-arisen with" a wholesome mind and so on is specifically: those non-material dharmas that are drawn forth by the very power of that mind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The example given to prove the point is a small gem of technical precision: at the very moment the patient acceptance of suffering-in-the-Dharma arises, ninety-eight latent defilements' worth of attainment come into being at once, yet none of them is said to be "co-arisen with" that patient acceptance, even though every one of them is a non-material dharma occurring at the very same instant — mere simultaneity, the passage insists, is not enough; what counts is being drawn forth by the mind's own power, and simultaneous attainments of that kind are drawn forth by something else entirely.

2. A Wholesome Non-Material Dharma's Three Possible Companions

The census proper begins: a wholesome non-material dharma may be co-arisen with a wholesome, an unwholesome, or a neutral mind — the wholesome case being simple direct association, while the unwholesome and neutral cases arise through the surprising mechanism of regression and rebirth, where losing a fruit or dying into a new realm can cause a wholesome attainment to spring up alongside a mind of an entirely different moral color.

That losing the once-returner's fruit through regression is itself the very occasion on which a wholesome attainment (of whatever lesser fruit remains available) comes into being alongside an unwholesome mind is a genuinely counterintuitive result: moral regression and the birth of a wholesome possession turn out to be, quite literally, the same event viewed from two different angles.

3. Reversing the Question, and the Special Case of Growth

The treatise reverses direction: given a mind of a certain moral quality, what moral quality can its co-arisen non-material dharma have? The answer introduces a second mechanism beyond direct association and regression/rebirth — sheer continuous growth, whereby faculties and the great elements are gradually nourished while a mind of a given quality flows on for a long duration, and this growth itself has the four characteristics, whose own attainment must share the mind's own quality.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sprawling catalogue of specific practices whose growth counts here — the five superknowledges, the four immeasurables, the eight liberations and bases of totality, unhindered understanding, araṇā-samādhi, wish-knowledge, boundary concentration, the paired emptiness/wishlessness/signlessness contemplations — reads almost like a checklist of the entire advanced meditative curriculum, folded into a single technical point about which mind a growing faculty's own attainment must be co-arisen with.

4. The Same Census for Unwholesome and Neutral Non-Material Dharmas

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the fascicle closes on the very same kind of unresolved textual puzzle it raised only a few sections earlier — an unfettered dharma with a decidedly non-unfettered attainment — gives this otherwise highly mechanical census an oddly reflective final note: even after nine grounds' worth of exhaustive combinatorial coverage, the relationship between an unconditioned dharma's own nature and the nature of what merely possesses it remains subtle enough to generate genuine, lasting disagreement.
