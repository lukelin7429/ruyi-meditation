Cutting Off, and Growing Back

Fascicle 35 defines shame and moral dread as the two bright dharmas that protect the world and distinguish humans from animals, before turning to a remarkable, exhaustive investigation into how wrong view severs the wholesome roots entirely — who is capable of such a fall, which realms it reaches, whether it happens all at once or grade by grade — and, most strikingly, how even this gravest of falls can be reconnected, sometimes within this very life, so that the noble path remains walkable even for one who has cut himself off from every wholesome root he once possessed. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 35 continues the second part of the Shamelessness Nyāsa (無慚愧納息第五之二), opened in fascicle 34. Terms established in earlier fascicles, such as shamelessness, lack of moral dread, and the nine grades of affliction, recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Shame and Moral Dread: The Two Bright Dharmas That Protect the World

Fascicle 35 opens by explaining why shame and moral dread — the proximate antidotes to the shamelessness and lack of moral dread from the fascicle before — deserve a treatise of their own: the Buddha calls them the two bright dharmas capable of protecting and guarding the world, without which there would exist neither a wholesome destiny nor liberation, and they are what makes possible every distinction of parent and child, elder and younger, noble and base — without them, the treatise says bluntly, sentient beings would be exactly like oxen and sheep.

Chinese Source

云何慚云何愧乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為廣分別契經義故。謂契經說有慚有愧。雖作是說而不廣辨。云何為慚云何為愧。經是此論所依根本彼所不分別者今應廣分別之。復次欲令疑者得決定故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is shame? What is moral dread? And so on, at length. One asks: why is this treatise composed? The answer: it is composed in order to fully discriminate the meaning of this sūtra passage. That is, the sūtra states that there exists shame and moral dread, yet, having said this much, does not discriminate it fully — what is meant by shame, and what is meant by moral dread? That sūtra is the fundamental basis upon which this treatise depends, and whatever it has left undiscriminated ought now to be discriminated fully. Further, this treatise is composed in order to bring those in doubt to a settled resolution —

謂此二法展轉相似。世間有情見有慚者言是有愧。見有愧者言是有慚。勿謂此二其體是一。今欲顯示性相差別令彼疑者得決定解。復次前雖已說無慚無愧而未說彼近對治法。今欲說彼近對治法所謂慚愧。復次如是二法唯是善性亦是施設善法勝因。如說何法相應心品一向是善。謂慚與愧欲顯其相令勤修習。復次如是二法守護世間。

that is, these two dharmas closely resemble one another in their mutual operation, and worldly sentient beings, upon seeing one who possesses shame, call him one who possesses moral dread, and, upon seeing one who possesses moral dread, call him one who possesses shame, as though these two possessed but a single substance; this treatise now wishes to reveal the distinction in their own inherent nature and characteristic, so that those in doubt may attain a settled understanding. Further, the foregoing has already spoken of shamelessness and lack of moral dread, without yet speaking of their own proximate antidotes; this treatise now wishes to speak of these proximate antidotes — namely, shame and moral dread. Further, these two dharmas are solely wholesome in nature, and are further posited as the predominant cause of wholesomeness generally, exactly as it is said: which mental cluster is wholesome in every case? It is that associated with shame and moral dread — this treatise now wishes to reveal their characteristic, so that they may be diligently cultivated. Further, these two dharmas protect and guard the world,

如世尊說有二白法能護世間。謂慚與愧。若無此二是則應無善趣解脫。欲顯其相令勤修習。復次如是二法能令有情種種差別。所謂父母兄弟姊妹男女眷屬尊卑長幼。若無此二如牛羊等。便無尊卑長幼差別。欲顯其相令勤修習故作斯論。

exactly as the Blessed One has stated: there are two bright dharmas capable of protecting and guarding the world — namely, shame and moral dread; were these two absent, there would then exist no wholesome destiny and no liberation — this treatise now wishes to reveal their characteristic, so that they may be diligently cultivated. Further, these two dharmas are able to bring about every manner of distinction among sentient beings — namely, parent and child, elder and younger brother, elder and younger sister, male and female, kinsfolk, noble and base, elder and junior; were these two absent, sentient beings would be exactly like oxen and sheep, possessing no such distinction of noble and base, elder and junior whatsoever — this treatise now wishes to reveal their characteristic, so that they may be diligently cultivated, and it is for this reason that this treatise is composed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The claim that shame and moral dread alone preserve the entire structure of human social distinction — parent from child, elder from younger, noble from base — mirrors exactly what fascicle 34 said about their absence erasing the difference between humans and pigs and dogs, and together these two passages build a consistent picture: civilization itself, in this treatise's own view, rests not on intelligence or law but on these two ordinary capacities for feeling exposed before oneself and before others.

2. Defining Shame and Moral Dread, and Their Distinction

The treatise works through the identical nature-aspect-object analysis it applied to shamelessness and lack of moral dread, defining shame as awe before what is worthy of mastery and moral dread as a profound perception of dread within wrongdoing itself, before addressing a genuine puzzle: if these two are solely contaminated, as one canonical text implies, how could a noble one's own mind ever be said to possess them at their most predominant? The resolution distinguishes preparatory effort, where shame and moral dread predominate, from the fundamental stage, where faith predominates — with both remaining fully compatible with the uncontaminated path.

What is shame? The answer: whatever shame there is, whatever possession of shame there is, whatever divergent shame there is, whatever modesty there is, whatever possession of modesty there is, whatever divergent modesty there is, whatever respect there is, whatever possession of the characteristic of respect there is, whatever mastery there is, whatever possession of the characteristic of mastery there is — with respect to one who possesses such mastery, there arises a kind of awe — this is called shame. This treatise's own author, being skilled in the various wordings of the meaning, expresses this through a variety of wordings, though its substance is not thereby distinct. One asks: do the various distinct terms spoken of in this passage reveal its own inherent nature, or do they reveal its aspect, or do they reveal its object? There are those who say: this reveals shame's own inherent nature. One asks: if so, then what is shame's own aspect? The answer: it is like the aspect of any wholesome mind and mental factor generally — this aspect, too, is of this same kind, since it stands in association with them. One asks: what is shame's own object? The answer: all dharmas. There are other masters who say: this passage reveals shame's own aspect. This aspect, in relation to other things, ought to be set out in four alternatives: there is a case of shame that does not operate through shame's own aspect — namely, shame operating through some other aspect; there is a case of operating through shame's own aspect that is not shame — namely, a dharma associated with shame operating through shame's own aspect; there is a case that is both shame and operates through shame's own aspect — namely, shame operating through shame's own aspect; there is a case that is neither shame nor operates through shame's own aspect — if this class is taken broadly, one ought to say that a dharma associated with shame operates through some other aspect; if not, one ought to say that this refers to whatever remains apart from the foregoing. Just as the case of "operating" admits of these four alternatives, so too, "having already operated" and "about to operate" should be understood in just the same way. Just as shame's own aspect admits of these three sets of four alternatives, so too should every other aspect be understood. One asks: if so, then what is shame's own inherent nature? The answer: its own substance, its own particular characteristic, is precisely its own inherent nature — exactly as it is said, the inherent nature of dharmas is precisely their own particular characteristic, and their belonging to the same class is their shared characteristic. One asks: what is shame's own object? The answer: all dharmas. There are yet others who say: this passage reveals shame's own object — that is, "whatever shame there is, whatever possession of shame there is, whatever divergent shame there is, whatever modesty there is, whatever possession of modesty there is, whatever divergent modesty there is" — these are said to take the truths of suffering and origin as their object; "whatever respect there is, whatever possession of the characteristic of respect there is, whatever mastery there is, whatever possession of the characteristic of mastery there is — with respect to one who possesses such mastery, there arises a kind of awe" — these are said to take the truths of cessation and the path as their object; their own inherent nature and aspect are as explained above.

What distinction is there between shame and moral dread? The answer: with respect to one who possesses mastery, there arising a kind of awe, is shame; with respect to every wrongdoing, profoundly perceiving a cause for dread and fear, is moral dread — such is their distinction. One asks: why is this treatise further composed? The answer: the Abhidharma states that these two dharmas mutually stand in association with one another, their own characteristics closely resembling one another; this treatise now wishes to discriminate the distinction in the own-nature and characteristic of shame and moral dread, and it is for this reason that this treatise is composed. That is, with respect to one who possesses mastery, there arising a kind of awe, is shame; with respect to every wrongdoing, profoundly perceiving a cause for dread and fear, is moral dread. Further, possessing a degree of reverence is shame; possessing a degree of dread is moral dread — this same sequence, and every distinction that follows from it, stands in direct contrast to the distinctions already given above between shamelessness and lack of moral dread, and should be broadly stated accordingly. These two dharmas alike are bound to the three realms, and are also unbound; they are solely wholesome, and pervasively stand in association with every wholesome mind. One asks: if so, then how is what the Prajñaptiśāstra states to be reconciled? As it is stated: of the seven powers, how many are contaminated, and how many uncontaminated? The answer: two are contaminated alone — namely, shame and moral dread; the remaining five extend to both the contaminated and the uncontaminated — namely, faith and the rest. The answer: that treatise ought properly to state that all seven powers extend to both the contaminated and the uncontaminated; that it does not so state reflects a distinct underlying intent — that is, that treatise speaks of power in terms of preparatory effort and its fundamental basis: at the stage of preparatory effort, shame and moral dread predominate, and it is for this reason that they are said to be contaminated alone; at the fundamental stage, faith and the rest predominate, and it is for this reason that they are said to extend to both. Were it not so, then the noble path ought not to stand in association with shame and moral dread, and it would then follow that a noble one's own shame and moral dread ought never to predominate — and it is for this reason that shame and moral dread most certainly do extend to the uncontaminated as well. There are cases of shamelessness that resemble shamelessness in their operation — that is, when committing evil, there is no modesty, as with butchering and hunting; and there are cases of shame that resemble shame in their operation — that is, when doing what is wholesome, there is modesty, as with repenting one's own faults. There are cases of lack of moral dread that resemble moral dread in their operation — that is, when committing evil, there is nonetheless a kind of remorse, as with the arrangements of marriage; and there are cases of moral dread that resemble lack of moral dread in their operation — that is, when doing what is wholesome, there is no remorse, as with the act of giving. There are cases of lack of moral dread that resemble lack of moral dread in their operation — that is, when committing evil, there is no remorse, as with butchering and hunting; and there are cases of moral dread that resemble moral dread in their operation — that is, when doing what is wholesome, there is remorse, as with repenting one's own faults.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The catalog of paradoxical cases closing this section — shamelessness that looks like shame in arranging a marriage, shame that looks like shamelessness in the unselfconscious act of giving — is a genuinely subtle piece of moral psychology: it warns against reading outward modesty or its absence as a reliable guide to a person's actual inner state, since social convention and genuine virtue can point in opposite directions depending entirely on what specifically is being done.

3. A New Topic: Predominant and Subtly Co-Arising Unwholesome Roots

The fascicle turns to a new sūtra distinction — predominant unwholesome roots, powerful enough to sever wholesome roots entirely, against subtly co-arising unwholesome roots, the very last and most stubborn traces relinquished only at the final moment of departing from the desire realm's own defilement — addressing first why the sūtra passes over the middling grade entirely, since it is neither as dramatically visible as the extremes nor as rare, exemplified instead by ordinary people rather than famous figures like Aṅgulimāla.

Chinese Source

云何增上不善根。云何微俱行不善根乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為廣分別契經義故。如契經說彼猶有微俱行不善根未斷。從此有餘不善法生由是當退。契經雖作是說而不廣辨其義。亦不說云何增上不善根。云何微俱行不善根。經是此論所依根本彼所不分別者今應廣分別之故作斯論。問何故此中不說中品不善根耶。答是作論者意欲爾故乃至廣說。復次應說而不說者當知此義有餘。復次已說初後即已顯中。如初後上下趣入已出加行究竟應知亦爾。復次若麁現易了易可施設易顯易說者此中說之。中品不爾是故不說。謂利根者如指鬘等。鈍根者如蛇奴等。麁現易了易可施設易顯易說中根不爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a predominant unwholesome root? What is a subtly co-arising unwholesome root? And so on, at length. One asks: why is this treatise composed? The answer: it is composed in order to fully discriminate the meaning of this sūtra passage. As the sūtra states: he still possesses a subtly co-arising unwholesome root, not yet cut off, from which there will further arise unwholesome dharmas, and it is on this account that he will retrogress — yet, having said this much, the sūtra does not discriminate its meaning fully, nor does it state what is meant by a predominant unwholesome root, or what is meant by a subtly co-arising unwholesome root. That sūtra is the fundamental basis upon which this treatise depends, and whatever it has left undiscriminated ought now to be discriminated fully, and it is for this reason that this treatise is composed. One asks: why does this passage not speak of the middling grade of unwholesome root? The answer: this is simply because the author of the treatise intended it so, and so on, at length. Further, that it ought to be stated but is not stated here should be understood as a matter of surplus meaning left implicit. Further, having already spoken of the first and the last, the middle is thereby already indicated as well — exactly as, in speaking of first and last, or superior and inferior, or entering and emerging, or the beginning of preparatory effort and its ultimate completion, this same principle should be understood to apply. Further, this passage speaks only of whatever is coarse, manifest, easily understood, easily designated, easily displayed, and easily expressed; the middling grade is not so, and it is for this reason that it is not spoken of here — that is, one of sharp faculties is exemplified by such a figure as Aṅgulimāla, and one of dull faculties is exemplified by such a figure as the serpent-servant; these are coarse, manifest, easily understood, easily designated, easily displayed, and easily expressed, whereas one of middling faculties is not so.

8. Which Wholesome Roots Are Severed: The Desire Realm and Beyond

A subtle question follows: since wholesome roots belonging to the form and formless realms are never even possessed by most who cut off their wholesome roots in the desire realm, how can canonical texts speak of “cutting off the wholesome roots of all three realms”? The treatise answers through several complementary images — becoming further removed from what was never possessed counts as a kind of cutting-off in its own right, and, since the higher realms' wholesome roots depend on the desire realm's own for their growth and nourishment, severing the root in the desire realm withers what depended on it even in realms never actually reached.
