Consciousness Taking Consciousness as Object

Fascicle 87 closes out the latent tendencies' own mutual bonds and the threefold division of directed thought and sustained reflection, then opens an argument against the Dārṣṭāntikas: a detailed classification of which consciousness takes which dharma as its object, both directly and as a further object-of-that-object. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 87 continues the Ten Gates Nyāsa (結蘊第二中十門納息第四之十七), first closing out the analysis of how latent tendencies bind one another and how directed thought (尋) and sustained reflection (伺) relate to bondage, then opening a new topic argued against the Dārṣṭāntikas (譬喻者): which consciousness (識) takes which dharma as its object, both directly (緣識) and as a further object of that object (緣緣識). Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. How the Latent Tendencies Bind One Another

The fascicle opens by turning the whole apparatus of bondage back upon itself: do the latent tendencies themselves bind one another, and if so, how? A tetralemma sorts every latent tendency's own relationship to the class of latent tendencies as a whole, hinging entirely on the ignorance not shared with others — since ordinary latent tendencies simply take other latent tendencies as their object without being associated with them, while this same ignorance, when it takes a contaminated latent tendency as its object, achieves only the bond of an object, and when it takes an undefiled dharma as its object, achieves neither bond at all. The Venerable Wu, on this same point, offers an independent tetralemma organized instead around ground, realm, and whether the latent tendency in question has already been cut off.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the very first question this fascicle asks is whether latent tendencies bind other latent tendencies is a natural next step after the whole of fascicle 86: having spent an entire fascicle cataloguing how the ninety-eight latent tendencies increase with respect to every ordinary dharma, the treatise now turns the same lens on the latent tendencies as objects in their own right, treating them exactly as it would any other contaminated dharma capable of being taken as an object and, in specific circumstances, being increased by that very taking.

2. Directed Thought, Sustained Reflection, and Neither

A parallel investigation opens onto the classic threefold division of dharma by directed thought (vitarka) and sustained reflection (vicāra) — possessing both, possessing only sustained reflection, and possessing neither, corresponding respectively to the desire realm and first dhyāna, the intermediate dhyāna, and the second dhyāna and above. Three successive tetralemmas work out, category by category, exactly which latent tendencies constitute a bond of an object with respect to dharma of each of these three kinds, the recurring principle being that a latent tendency of the same ground, not yet cut off, and not directed toward another realm, ground, or an undefiled object, binds a dharma sharing its own same characteristic — while a latent tendency belonging to a coarser category can still bind a subtler dharma, since directed thought and sustained reflection both remain present at the coarser stage.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The asymmetry running through all three tetralemmas — a coarser latent tendency (possessing both directed thought and sustained reflection) can bind a subtler dharma (possessing only sustained reflection, or possessing neither), but a subtler latent tendency can never bind a coarser dharma — mirrors the very structure of the dhyānas themselves: just as a being who has attained the second dhyāna has left directed thought behind for good and cannot regress into experiencing it again while remaining at that level, a latent tendency belonging to the subtler stage simply lacks the coarser characteristic needed to reach back and bind what belongs to the stage below it.

3. Association-Bonds Across the Same Threefold Division, and a Puzzling Cluster

The same threefold division is now tested for the bond of association rather than the bond of an object, yielding a cleaner rule throughout: a latent tendency can only be associated with a dharma of its own identical kind, since association requires arising together, and a dharma possessing both directed thought and sustained reflection can never arise bundled together with one lacking either. Four illustrative cases close this run — desire-realm directed thought that is not increased by its own matching latent tendency, desire-realm and first-dhyāna directed thought that is instead increased by the coarser latent tendency possessing both directed thought and sustained reflection, intermediate-dhyāna sustained reflection that escapes its own matching latent tendency's increase — and, most strikingly, the cluster of the ignorance not shared with others, which is associated with its own accompanying dharma yet, quite unable to be associated with its own identical self-nature, is simultaneously bound and unbound within one and the same arising, abiding, and ceasing.

4. Why This New Treatise Is Composed: Against the Dārṣṭāntikas

An entirely new investigation opens, again spanning the eye faculty through the formless realm's own latent tendency of ignorance, but now asking after both the directly cognizing consciousness and a further consciousness that takes that first consciousness itself as its own object. The reason given for composing this fresh treatise is polemical: the Dārṣṭāntikas maintain that the six kinds of consciousness each take strictly separate objects, that mental consciousness possesses an object entirely its own, unconnected to whatever the five senses perceive, and that no consciousness whatsoever ever takes an internal faculty or another consciousness as its own object, restricting all cognition to external objects alone. Against this, the treatise displays that while the five sense-consciousnesses do indeed take only their own separate external objects, mental consciousness's own object, though partly overlapping with theirs, is genuinely broader, since it takes the internal faculties and the various consciousnesses themselves as object as well.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the female faculty needs only five consciousnesses to reach it directly, against the eye faculty's eight, while still requiring nine consciousnesses to reach it indirectly — nearly as many as the eye faculty's own thirteen — shows that a narrower direct reach does not simply propagate into an equally narrower indirect one: precisely because the female faculty's own reaching consciousnesses are themselves taken as objects by consciousnesses spanning every realm above it, the chain of indirect access can still grow surprisingly long even for a dharma confined to a single realm.

11. The Life Faculty, Closing the Fascicle

The life faculty, pervading all three realms' nine grounds and solely abandoned by cultivation, is taken as a direct object by ten consciousnesses — three within each realm (its own universally pervasive latent tendencies abandoned by seeing suffering and accumulation, together with its own wholesome and neutral-and-unobscured consciousness abandoned by cultivation), plus the undefiled consciousness of both dharma- and class-knowledge together — and as an object-of-that-object by thirteen consciousnesses, the fascicle closing precisely at this point, mid-analysis, with the promise of further such faculties to come.

Fascicle 87 Complete

This closes fascicle 87 of Ru-Yi's translation, drawn from the treatise's true CBETA boundary. Fascicle 87 continued the Ten Gates Nyāsa (結蘊第二中十門納息第四之十七), opening with how the latent tendencies bind one another, working through three tetralemmas on directed thought and sustained reflection, and then turning to an entirely new topic argued explicitly against the Dārṣṭāntikas — a sixteen-fold (then thirty-two-fold, then one-hundred-and-twenty-fold) classification of dharma and the consciousnesses that take them as object, both directly and as a further object-of-that-object, worked through in exhaustive numerical detail for the eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, female, male, suffering, and life faculties, breaking off mid-analysis at the life faculty's own count. Fascicle 88 continues within the same nyāsa, resuming the identical sweep through the remaining faculties.
