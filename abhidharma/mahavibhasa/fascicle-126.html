Retaining and Releasing Life

Fascicle 126 explains how an arhat may deliberately retain or release his own remaining lifespan, why the Buddha himself lived only eighty years, the causes and scope of mental derangement, and offers a rigorous definition of "the Buddha's teaching" alongside a full survey of the twelve traditional genres of scripture — closing with analyses of writing, number, calculation, and poetry. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. A New Question Opens: What Does It Mean to 'Retain Much Life'?

Fascicle 126 opens a striking new topic within the Own-Karma Nyāsa: the sūtra's account of the Buddha and, separately, Mahāprajāpatī's five hundred bhikṣuṇīs "retaining much life-formation" and "releasing much life-span-formation" before death — language the base scripture never explains in full, prompting this treatise's own detailed analysis of exactly what such retaining and releasing involves.

Chinese Source

業蘊第四中自業納息第五之三云何苾芻留多壽行。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為欲分別契經義故。如契經說。時薄伽梵留多命行。捨多壽行。又欲分別毘奈耶義故。如毘奈耶說。大生主為首。五百苾芻尼留多命行捨多壽行。經毘奈耶雖有是說。而未分別留捨因緣。彼經及毘奈耶是此論根本。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth Aggregate, Karma: the Own-Karma Nyāsa, Chapter Five, Part Three. What is meant by a bhikṣu "retaining much life-formation"? And so on in full. Question: why compose this treatise? Answer: in order to analyze the meaning of the sūtra. For the sūtra says: at that time the Blessed One retained much life-formation and released much life-span-formation. It is also in order to analyze the meaning of the Vinaya. For the Vinaya says: Mahāprajāpatī, foremost among them, together with five hundred bhikṣuṇīs, retained much life-formation and released much life-span-formation. Though the sūtra and the Vinaya say this much, they have not analyzed the causes and conditions of retaining and releasing — and this sūtra and this Vinaya passage are the very root this treatise depends upon,

彼未說者今應說之。故作斯論。又諸造論皆為分別諸所有法自相共相。今此亦然。不應為問。

— and what they have not explained should now be explained, and so this treatise is composed. Moreover, every treatise composed exists in order to analyze the particular and general characteristics of all existing dharmas, and this is no different here — this need not be raised as an objection.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the treatise feels compelled to explain a phrase the Buddha himself used about his own final months signals how seriously Abhidharma scholarship treats even the most intimate biographical details of the tradition's founder: nothing, not even the mechanics of the Buddha's own choice about how long to live, is left as mere narrative color rather than a technical claim demanding precise philosophical analysis.

2. Converting Wealth into Years: The Technique of Retaining Life

The mechanism itself is remarkably concrete: an arhat with mastery over his own mind gives a gift of robes, a bowl, or requisites, makes a vow, and enters the boundary state of the fourth dhyāna — emerging to redirect karma originally set to ripen as wealth so that it instead ripens as longevity. Two motives justify this: benefiting others (extending life long enough to see disciples reach the excellent Dharma) and sustaining the Buddhadharma itself (seeing through unfinished monastic projects, or standing against a ruler bent on destroying the teaching).

Chinese Source

云何苾芻留多壽行。答謂阿羅漢成就神通得心自在。若於僧眾若別人所。以衣以鉢。或以隨一沙門命緣眾具布施施已發願即入邊際第四靜慮。從定起已心念口言。諸我能感富異熟業。願此轉招壽異熟果。時彼能招富異熟業。則轉能招壽異熟果。問彼有何緣留多壽行。答留多壽行。略有二緣。謂為饒益他及住持佛法。為饒益他者。謂教弟子修諸觀行。彼審觀察齊我壽住。此諸門人逮勝法不。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by a bhikṣu "retaining much life-formation"? Answer: it means that an arhat, having attained supernormal powers and mastery over his own mind, gives a gift of robes, of a bowl, or of any of the various requisites proper to a renunciant's livelihood, to the community of monks or to a particular individual, and, having given this gift, makes a vow and enters at once the boundary state of the fourth dhyāna. Rising from this absorption, he reflects in thought and declares aloud, "Whatever karma of mine is capable of bringing about a ripening-fruit of wealth, may it now instead bring about a ripening-fruit of longevity" — and at that moment, the karma that was capable of bringing about a ripening-fruit of wealth instead comes to bring about a ripening-fruit of longevity. Question: for what reason does he retain much life-formation? Answer: retaining much life-formation rests, in brief, on two grounds — benefiting others, and sustaining the Buddhadharma. As to benefiting others: this means that, in teaching his disciples to cultivate their contemplative practice, he carefully observes whether, for as long as he himself remains alive, these disciples will attain the excellent Dharma or not,

設我壽盡為更有餘。能善開示道非道不若見無能便留壽行。為住持佛法者。謂營佛像僧房等事。彼審觀察齊我壽住。此所營事得成辦不。設我壽盡為更有餘善巧方便能成辦不。若見無能便留壽行。又彼觀見當有國王大臣長者等欲毀滅佛法。便審觀察齊我壽住當有方便令不毀滅不。設我壽盡為更有餘善巧方便能住持不。若見無能便留壽行為留壽行。以衣鉢等施僧別人。依契經說。謂世尊說。若有施主能施他物名施五事。由此還當得五事果。一壽二色三力四樂五辯。彼審觀察為施僧眾。當獲大果為施別人。若見施僧當獲大果便施與僧。若施別人當獲大果便施別人。是故於僧或別人所以衣以鉢。或以隨一沙門命緣眾具布施。施已發願即入邊際第四靜慮。從定起已心念口言。諸我能感富異熟業。願此轉招壽異熟果。時彼能招富異熟業則轉能招壽異熟果。

— and whether, should his own life come to an end, there would be anyone else capable of properly showing them the true path from the false. If he sees that there would be no one capable of this, he then retains his life-formation. As to sustaining the Buddhadharma: this means that, in overseeing matters such as the making of Buddha images and the building of monastic dwellings, he carefully observes whether, for as long as he himself remains alive, these undertakings will be accomplished or not, and whether, should his own life come to an end, there would be anyone else skilled enough to accomplish them. If he sees that there would be no one capable of this, he then retains his life-formation. He may also foresee that some king, minister, elder, or the like is about to destroy the Buddhadharma, and carefully observe whether, for as long as he himself remains alive, there would be some means of preventing this destruction, and whether, should his own life come to an end, there would be anyone else skilled enough to sustain the Dharma. If he sees that there would be no one capable of this, he then retains his life-formation. As to retaining life-formation by giving robes, a bowl, and the like to the community or to an individual — this is grounded in the sūtra's own teaching, where the World-Honored One said that a donor capable of giving another these five things — what is called giving in five respects — will thereby, in return, obtain five corresponding fruits: longevity, beauty, strength, happiness, and eloquence. He carefully observes whether giving to the community as a whole, or giving to a particular individual, will obtain the greater fruit, and, seeing which will obtain the greater fruit, gives accordingly — and so, giving robes, a bowl, or any of the various requisites proper to a renunciant's livelihood to the community or to an individual, and, having given this gift, makes a vow and enters at once the boundary state of the fourth dhyāna. Rising from this absorption, he reflects in thought and declares aloud, "Whatever karma of mine is capable of bringing about a ripening-fruit of wealth, may it now instead bring about a ripening-fruit of longevity" — and at that moment, the karma that was capable of bringing about a ripening-fruit of wealth instead comes to bring about a ripening-fruit of longevity.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That an arhat's decision to extend his own life is grounded not in self-preservation but in a careful, almost administrative assessment of whether anyone else is capable of finishing his work is a quietly striking picture of enlightened responsibility: longevity itself becomes a resource to be allocated only where genuinely needed, never simply clung to.

3. Does a Ripening-Fruit Really Change Kind? Four Competing Theories

A sharp philosophical puzzle follows: in principle a fruit of wealth cannot literally become a fruit of longevity — so what is actually happening when the text says wealth-karma is redirected toward longevity? Four rival explanations compete: it is the force of the karma, not the substance of the fruit, that is redirected; or a fruit of longevity was already set but obstructed by calamity, now cleared; or a fruit of longevity was already set but indeterminate, now made certain; or the technique simply draws forward some unspent remainder of longevity-ripening left over from a past existence.

Chinese Source

問理無富異熟果可成壽異熟果。何故乃說富異熟業。則轉能招壽異熟果。答無轉果體。有轉業力。謂由布施邊際定力。轉富異熟業招壽異熟果。雖俱可轉而彼今時不顧富果。祈壽果故。有餘師說。有業先感壽異熟果。然有災障由今布施邊際定力。彼災障滅壽異熟起。雖俱可轉而彼今時不顧富果。祈壽果故。有作是說。有業先招壽異熟果。然不決定由今布施邊際定力。令招壽業決定與果。復有欲令由施定故引取宿世殘壽異熟。謂阿羅漢有餘生中殘壽異熟。由今布施邊際定力引令現前定力不思議令久斷還續。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: in principle, a ripening-fruit of wealth cannot actually become a ripening-fruit of longevity — why then does the text say that karma capable of bringing about a ripening-fruit of wealth instead comes to bring about a ripening-fruit of longevity? Answer: it is not the substance of the fruit that is converted, but the force of the karma — that is, through the power of the boundary absorption following the gift, karma capable of bringing about a ripening-fruit of wealth is redirected to bring about a ripening-fruit of longevity instead; though both remain, in principle, convertible, at this particular moment he simply disregards the fruit of wealth and seeks only the fruit of longevity. Other masters say: there is karma that had already been set to bring about a ripening-fruit of longevity, yet obstructed by some calamity — and, through the power of the boundary absorption following this gift, that calamitous obstruction is dissolved, and the ripening of longevity arises; though both remain convertible, at this moment he simply disregards the fruit of wealth and seeks only the fruit of longevity. Others say: there is karma that had already been set to bring about a ripening-fruit of longevity, yet not with certainty — and it is through the power of the boundary absorption following this gift that the karma set to bring about longevity is made to deliver its fruit with certainty. Still others hold that it is through the power of this giving and absorption that some remainder of longevity-ripening from a past life is drawn forth — that is, an arhat may have some remaining longevity-ripening left over from some other existence, and it is through the power of the boundary absorption following this gift that this remainder is drawn forth and made to manifest — the inconceivable power of absorption causing what was long since interrupted to resume once more.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Each of the four theories carries a different, quietly consequential metaphysics of what a "fruit" even is: whether karmic potential is a single malleable force that can be steered toward different outcomes, a fixed destiny merely obstructed or made certain, or a kind of unclaimed balance still owed from a previous life — the debate never resolves which picture is correct, leaving the reader to notice how much rests on a metaphor no one fully cashes out.

4. By Giving or by Absorption? And Why an Arhat Releases Life

A further question asks whether the retained life-formation is drawn forth by the power of the gift or the power of absorption — the treatise's own answer insisting on both together, since giving without absorption, or absorption without giving, can never alone draw forth the fruit of longevity. The mirror-image act, "releasing much life-span-formation," follows the identical technique in reverse, undertaken once an arhat's own benefit and his benefit to others are both already complete.

Chinese Source

問所留壽行正由誰引。為由施力為定力耶。若由施力不應入定。若由定力不應行施。有說由施。有說由定。如是說者。俱由二種。雖多行施若不入定彼終不能引壽果故。雖數入定若不行施彼終不能引壽果故。然施力能引定力令決定。由此故言俱由二種。云何苾芻捨多壽行。答謂阿羅漢成就神通得心自在。如前布施。施已發願即入邊際第四靜慮。從定起已心念口言。諸我能感壽異熟業。願此轉招富異熟果。時彼能招壽異熟業。則轉能招富異熟果。問彼有何緣捨多壽行。答自利利他俱究竟故。已得盡智故名自利究竟。於利他事。若有堪能此事成已便歸圓寂。若無堪能亦名究竟。有作是說。

Question: is the life-formation thus retained drawn forth precisely by the power of giving, or by the power of absorption? If by the power of giving, there should be no need to enter absorption; if by the power of absorption, there should be no need to give. Some say it is by the power of giving; others say it is by the power of absorption. According to this last account: it is by both together — for though one may give abundantly, without entering absorption he could never draw forth the fruit of longevity, and though one may enter absorption repeatedly, without giving he could equally never draw forth the fruit of longevity; rather, the power of giving is what enables the power of absorption to become decisive, and hence it is said that both are required together. Answer: it means that an arhat, having attained supernormal powers and mastery over his own mind, gives a gift just as before, and, having given this gift, makes a vow and enters at once the boundary state of the fourth dhyāna. Rising from this absorption, he reflects in thought and declares aloud, "Whatever karma of mine is capable of bringing about a ripening-fruit of longevity, may it now instead bring about a ripening-fruit of wealth" — and at that moment, the karma that was capable of bringing about a ripening-fruit of longevity instead comes to bring about a ripening-fruit of wealth. 5. Casting Off the Body Like a Poison Vessel: The Verse, and Converting Years Back into Wealth

An arhat releases his remaining years, the text explains, because he regards his own body with the same weariness one feels toward a container that once held poison — a sentiment distilled into four memorable lines of verse. The technique for releasing life then mirrors, in precise reverse, the technique for retaining it: the same gift, the same vow, the same entry into the boundary fourth dhyāna, this time redirecting longevity-karma toward a fruit of wealth instead, raising the identical puzzle of what such a conversion actually consists in.

The poison-vessel image reframes the arhat's release of life not as resignation or defeat but as a natural culmination: having drunk everything of value the vessel had to offer — the completed cultivation of the noble path — there is nothing left to regret in setting the container itself aside, an image of death stripped of any trace of loss.

6. Coarse Fruit into Fine, and What Actually Drives the Conversion

The parallel set of theories for converting longevity-karma into wealth-karma adds a further nuance: karma originally set to bring about only a coarse, delayed fruit may instead, through this same technique, be redirected toward a fine fruit arriving sooner — alongside the same possibility of drawing forth some leftover wealth-ripening from a past existence. A closing question asks whether it is truly the power of giving or the power of absorption that drives the whole conversion, again answered: both together, since neither alone can ever succeed.

Chinese Source

令感麁業轉招妙果。謂彼先引長時麁果。今由施定祈願力故。令彼轉招今時妙果。復有欲令由施定故引取宿世殘富異熟。謂阿羅漢有餘生中殘富異熟。由今布施邊際定力引令現前。定力不思議令久斷還續。

Question: is this wealth-ripening drawn forth precisely by the power of giving, or by the power of absorption? If by the power of giving, there should be no need to enter absorption; if by the power of absorption, there should be no need to give. Some say it is by the power of giving; others say it is by the power of absorption. According to this last account: it is by both together — for though one may give abundantly, without entering absorption he could never draw forth the fruit of wealth, and though one may enter absorption repeatedly, without giving he could equally never draw forth the fruit of wealth; rather, the power of giving is what enables the power of absorption to become decisive, and hence it is said that both are required together. Question: if beings' fruits of longevity and wealth are not fixed, then retaining or releasing them may well be possible — but if both are already fixed, how could they be retained or released at all? Answer: it is merely that a set limit is established, with nothing capable of exceeding it — just as a skilled physician's prognosis of a set limit admits of nothing beyond it, so too here. Reading note — Ru-Yi

Placing the illusion of "seeing two moons" in mental consciousness alone, never in the eye-consciousness itself, is a precise piece of Abhidharma psychology: the senses are exonerated of ever lying, and derangement is relocated entirely to the act of interpretation layered on top of perfectly accurate raw perception — a distinction between seeing and misjudging what one sees that the tradition insists on with some rigor.

A short technical digression asks which dharma, when it accompanies an affliction, guarantees that affliction is unwholesome — the answer being shamelessness and non-remorse, the only two of the ten classic entanglements that both remain exclusively unwholesome in every instance and accompany every unwholesome mind without exception, unlike anger or stinginess (unwholesome but not universal) or torpor and restlessness (universal but not exclusively unwholesome).

13. What Is the Buddha's Teaching? Why It Is Speech, Not Silent Karma

A major new topic opens: what precisely counts as "the Buddha's teaching," given that people today casually claim to teach or hear it despite having no direct connection to the historical Buddha? Four justifications for this continuity are offered — shared root and origin, resemblance in sequential form, conformity in pattern, and shared soteriological power to liberate a listener just as the Buddha's own living voice once did. The treatise then defines the Buddha's teaching with precision as his own manifest verbal karma specifically — never unmanifest karma — since only manifest speech is audible to the ear and capable of producing correct understanding in another.

Restricting "the Buddha's teaching" to manifest speech alone carries a quietly democratic implication: whatever cannot in principle be heard, verified, and transmitted by another person simply does not count as teaching at all, however exalted its inner source — a criterion that ties the very definition of scripture to its communicability rather than to any private, unspeakable attainment behind it.

14. A Contradiction in the Text: Is the Buddha's Teaching Speech, or Name and Phrase?

A sharp internal puzzle emerges: if the Buddha's teaching is essentially verbal karma, how is this reconciled with a later passage of this very treatise defining it instead as name-body, phrase-body, and syllable-body — the classic Sarvāstivāda doctrine of language as its own distinct, mind-disjoined reality? Multiple reconciliations are offered — the later passage describes function rather than essential nature, or addresses only the sense in which teaching operates through names rather than meaning, or reasons through a chain of successive causation where speech gives rise to name, which in turn reveals meaning — with the treatise ultimately affirming verbal karma as the essential nature, since it is the Buddha's own intended meaning, directly heard by another.

15. Is the Buddha's Teaching Wholesome or Neutral? And Why a Buddha's Karma Never Declines

The Buddha's teaching may be either wholesome or neutral depending on the mind from which it issues — with several competing criteria for sorting instances: most Abhidharma and Sūtra content is wholesome, while most procedural Vinaya rulings (where to place a bowl, when to close a door) are neutral; or wholesome speech addresses the object of conversion while neutral speech addresses incidental matters; or wholesome speech is spoken with deliberate effort while neutral speech arises spontaneously. A subtle final point distinguishes ordinary disciples from the Buddha himself: an ordinary disciple's wholesome-minded speech may complete on either a wholesome or neutral mind, but a buddha's wholesome-minded speech always completes on a wholesome mind, since a buddha's own teaching and karma are said never to decline.

16. The Flower-Garland and the Carpenter's Line: Sūtra, Verse, and the Rhinoceros's Horn

Turning to the Buddha's teaching's own function, the treatise works through the traditional twelve genres of scripture one by one. Sūtra carries two etymological senses — gathering meaning together like a thread through a flower garland, and adjudicating meaning like a carpenter's ink-line separating crooked from straight — illustrated by the Buddha's own verse on knowledge, vision, and the ending of afflictions. Geya restates prose already given in verse form; vyākaraṇa covers question-and-answer explanation; and gāthā stands as an independent, self-contained verse, illustrated by the haunting image of the solitary sage walking "alone, as solitary as a rhinoceros's horn," avoiding both the love and the hatred that closeness to others equally breeds.

It is when, within the scriptures, the World-Honored One speaks spontaneously on the occasion of some joyful or sorrowful matter. "The elephant-king dwells in the open wilderness, At ease, his mind free of sorrow — The wise one dwells in the quiet forest, Wandering free, his will at peace." 少不修梵行　喪失聖財寶今如二老鶴　共守一枯池因緣云何。謂諸經中遇諸因緣而有所說。如義品等種種因緣。如毘奈耶作如是說。由善財子等最初犯罪。是故世尊集苾芻僧制立學處。

"In youth they did not cultivate the pure conduct, And lost the noble treasure — Now they are like two old cranes, Together guarding a single dried-up pond." What is nidāna? It is when, within the scriptures, something is spoken in response to some particular occasion or circumstance — the various occasions behind the Arthavarga and the like. As the Vinaya explains: it was on account of Sudhanakumāra and others first committing an offense that the World-Honored One gathered the assembly of bhikṣus and established a training rule.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The flower-garland and carpenter's-line images, offered as two complementary etymologies for the single word "sūtra," together capture something genuinely dual about scripture's own function: it must hold meaning together across time without letting it scatter, and it must also cut decisively between right and wrong — binding and discerning treated as two faces of the very same act of teaching.

17. Parables, Past Lives, and the Vast Teaching: Completing the Twelve Genres

The remaining genres round out the classification: avadāna gathers the many parables and similes scattered through the scriptures; itivṛttaka recounts matters witnessed or heard from a bygone age, including the teachings of buddhas long before Śākyamuni; jātaka tells of the Buddha's own countless past-life births, including the five hundred birth-stories told in connection with Devadatta; vaipulya covers the broad, profound expositions of Dharma — which the Venerable Pārśva specifically identifies with the Prajñāpāramitā literature on account of its vast application; adbhutadharma records the Three Jewels' own rare and marvelous qualities, or disciples' praise of them; and upadeśa covers the analytical discourse through which the great disciples, during the Buddha's own periods of silent retreat, independently expounded and interpreted his teaching in various ways.

It is the account, within the scriptures, of the exceedingly rare and marvelous matters concerning the Three Jewels and the like. What is upadeśa? It is the teaching, within the scriptures, that adjudicates and settles what is spoken in silence, what is spoken at length, and the like. It is also as when the Buddha, having once taught a scripture in brief, entered a quiet chamber and remained in silent retreat for a long while, and the great śrāvakas, gathering together in one place, each explained the Buddha's own teaching through various different wordings and interpretations of its meaning.

18. What Is Writing? What Is Number? Ānanda and the Leaves of the Great Tree

A cluster of everyday skills receives the same rigorous analysis: writing is defined not as the characters produced but as the underlying bodily skill capable of producing them, paired with the wisdom that guides it; number, likewise, is not the quantities counted but the capacity to count them at all — illustrated by a wonderful anecdote of the Venerable Ānanda's supreme skill, tested by a skeptical outside-path ascetic who has him count the leaves of an enormous tree, secretly removes a handful, and finds Ānanda able to instantly report the exact, corrected new total on his return.

19. Calculation, the Seal, and Whether the Buddha's Own Verses Count as Poetry

The same formula extends to calculation (the capacity to compute, not the computed sums themselves) and the seal (the capacity to produce an impression, not the impression itself), before turning to a lighter, almost literary question: does the Buddha's own verse count as poetry in the ordinary sense? The treatise's verdict is no — true poetry is defined by the mere formal correspondence of wording and meaning without necessarily bringing any real benefit, while the Buddha's verses, however elegant, are always aimed at genuine spiritual benefit, and hence belong to a different category altogether.

21. Closing the Ledger: Trainee, Adept, and Ordinary Precepts Across the Three Realms

The fascicle closes by returning to the ledger of precepts across realms of rebirth: tetralemmas map exactly which combinations of trainee precepts, adept precepts, and "neither trainee nor adept" (ordinary worldly) precepts a being possesses depending on whether he is a trainee, an arhat, or an ordinary being, and whether he is reborn in the desire, form, or formless realm — with conventional precepts always given up upon crossing into the formless realm. A final cluster of questions closes the entire nyāsa's opening arc by confirming that every precept is itself a form of karma, though not all karma is a precept, with the formless realm supplying the clearest case of karma existing without any precept at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That rebirth in the formless realm strips away every precept a being once held — trainee, adept, or ordinary alike — while karma itself persists there in other forms, is a precise reminder of just how tightly this tradition ties moral discipline to embodiment: precepts, unlike karma in general, turn out to be inseparable from having a body capable of manifest action in the first place.

Fascicle 126 continues the Own-Karma Nyāsa (自業納息第五之三) across an unusually wide range of topics: how an arhat may deliberately retain or release his own remaining lifespan, why the Buddha himself lived only eighty years, the causes and scope of mental derangement grounded in the story of a grieving mother, a rigorous definition of "the Buddha's teaching" as manifest speech and a full survey of the twelve traditional genres of scripture — the flower-garland and the rhinoceros's horn among them — and closing analyses of writing, number (with Ānanda's celebrated feat of counting a great tree's leaves), calculation, poetry, bodily comportment, and the precepts held across the three realms — and, with it, the entire Karma Aggregate (業蘊第四) reaches its own close.
