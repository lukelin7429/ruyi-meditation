The Bodhisattva as a Rescuing Bear

Fascicle 114 classifies karma into black, white, mixed, and the karma beyond all three that exhausts every other, then turns to karma sorted by when it ripens, illustrated by six unforgettable narratives including the Bodhisattva's own life as a bear who rescues a lost woodcutter. 1. Four Kinds of Karma: Black, White, Mixed, and Beyond Both

Fascicle 114 continues the Bad-Conduct Nyāsa with a new fourfold classification: black karma with a black ripening (unwholesome karma bringing forth a perilous destiny), white karma with a white ripening (form-realm wholesome karma), karma both black and white with a mixed ripening (desire-realm wholesome karma, admixed with the unwholesome it cannot fully subdue), and karma neither black nor white, without ripening at all, capable of exhausting every other karma — the undefiled volition of the noble path itself. A careful etymological note explains why the ripening itself, though technically neutral, still earns the name "black": exactly as a thief's own son is reviled by association with his father's crime, ripening is called black solely by association with the disagreeable karma that produced it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The thief's-son simile for why a neutral ripened fruit still earns the name "black" is a precise piece of semantic reasoning: it distinguishes calling something black because it is itself defiled from calling it black merely because it keeps company with what is defiled, and insists that ripening's own black name belongs to the second, weaker sense — disagreeable by association, never itself a stain on the fruit's own genuinely neutral nature.

2. Pūrṇa and Seniya: The Cow-Ascetic and the Dog-Ascetic

A justly celebrated scriptural narrative illustrates why unwholesome karma bringing forth hell specifically earns the name "black karma with a black ripening": two outside-path ascetics, Pūrṇa Kauḍika (who upheld the vow of the cow) and Acela Seniya (who upheld the vow of the dog), each having fully completed his own difficult ascetic practice, come to the Buddha seeking a prediction of what their vows will bring forth — the Buddha, thrice refusing before relenting, tells them plainly that whoever keeps such a vow perfectly is reborn among the very animal he imitated, and whoever keeps it imperfectly falls into hell, a teaching meant, the treatise explains, to instill dread of grave and evil karma specifically.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own threefold refusal before finally answering is not mere narrative decoration — it performs real ethical work within the story itself: by warning Pūrṇa and Seniya three times that the answer will bring them only grief, the Buddha ensures that whatever sorrow follows is squarely the product of their own insistence, never something imposed on them unbidden, a small but exacting lesson in taking full responsibility for the truths one demands to hear.

3. White Karma and Mixed Karma

The technical apparatus turns to why form-realm wholesome karma alone earns the name "white karma with a white ripening," not formless-realm karma, through nine converging explanations (its capacity to bring forth both intermediate and basic existence, both form and non-form, all three kinds of bodily action, all five aggregates, all ten wholesome paths of karma, and two kinds of freshness where the formless realm possesses only one) — before turning to the desire realm's own wholesome karma, named "black and white" not because any single karma is literally both, but because a single continuum undergoes the ripening of both black and white karma admixed together, desire-realm wholesome being too feeble to ever fully subdue its own realm's unwholesome the way the reverse can happen.

11. Ripening Next Life, Ripening Later, and Whether Karma Can Be Altered

The remaining two temporal categories are defined in turn — karma ripening in the very next life, and karma ripening only in the third life or beyond — before a genuine disagreement surfaces: the Dārṣṭāntikas hold that every karma whatsoever, even an act of immediate retribution, remains capable of being altered, the very names "present-life," "next-life," and "later-life" karma describing only where the fruit falls if it ripens at all, not any fixed guarantee that it must; the Ābhidharmika treatise-masters, by contrast, hold these three kinds of karma to be genuinely and decisively fixed in when they ripen.

12. Four, Five, or Eight Kinds of Karma, and a Governing Tetralemma

Further schools expand the threefold scheme: a fourth category, karma whose ripening is genuinely indeterminate, alterable through precisely the discipline and holy conduct undertaken with that hope; a finer five-fold division splitting indeterminate karma itself into what is fixed and unfixed in its own ripening; and a governing eightfold scheme splitting every one of the original three temporal categories, plus the indeterminate fourth, into a ripening-fixed and a ripening-unfixed half — from which a final, elegant tetralemma emerges, crossing fixed-in-time against fixed-in-ripening in every possible combination.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ruling that intermediate and basic existence count as a single shared class of existence for the purposes of classifying karma as "present-life" is a precise piece of definitional housekeeping with real stakes: without it, karma formed in the shadowy interval between death and rebirth, ripening only once the new life properly begins, would risk being classified as ripening in some entirely different life altogether — the treatise's own ruling ensures that the transition from intermediate to basic existence is treated as a continuous unfolding of one and the same existence, not a boundary between two.

16. A Final Tetralemma: The Two Threefold Schemes Compared

The fascicle closes exactly as its predecessor did: a tetralemma mapping the bodily-verbal-mental threefold scheme of karma against the present-life-next-life-later-life threefold scheme, with the former fully gathering the latter and a substantial remainder — indeterminate karma, neutral karma, and undefiled karma — left over, since the temporal scheme, unlike the bodily-verbal-mental one, applies solely to fixed, contaminated, wholesome-or-unwholesome karma.
