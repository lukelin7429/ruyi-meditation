Why the Willow Cannot Be Swept Away

Fascicle 125 completes the karmic ledger with a precise account of "final position" and certainty of ripening, then asks why stream-enterers never fall into evil destinies despite still holding unripened unwholesome karma — closing with the vivid narratives of King Virūḍhaka's massacre of the Śākyas, an arhat falsely imprisoned as a cattle thief, and the failed plots against the persecutor Puṣyamitra at the Bodhi Tree. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Ladder of Final Positions: From the Last Thousand Kalpas to the Last Instant

Fascicle 125 opens with a precise refinement of "one's own karma": is such karma certain to still receive further ripening, or has it already reached its "final position" — the last stretch of a fixed span, whether measured in a thousand kalpas, a single kalpa, a thousand years, a year, a month, or a single instant? A cascade of paired examples matches each unit of time to the specific realm or lifespan it belongs to, from the eighty-thousand-kalpa span of the highest formless heaven down to a life of a mere hundred instants.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the treatise finds it necessary to define "final position" across nine entirely different scales of time — from thousand-kalpa cosmic epochs down to a single fleeting instant — suggests karma's ripening is being modeled as something closer to a countdown than a single event: whatever the length of a given span, ripening is understood to consume it steadily, with only the very last portion of it properly called "final."

2. A Countdown of Ripening Still Owed

The most striking image in this section is a running ledger: a single karma capable of producing an eighty-thousand-kalpa lifespan is, during its very first thousand kalpas, already "one's own karma" certain to receive further ripening — specifically, certain to receive exactly seventy-nine thousand more kalpas of it — with the same exact figure recalculated at every subsequent thousand-kalpa mark, all the way down to the final thousand kalpas where the countdown reaches zero.

3. The Full Matrix: Possession Versus Certainty of Ripening

A further tetralemma cross-references two different questions — whether karma is still possessed, and whether it is certain to ripen — working through every combination: karma possessed yet certain never to ripen (its debt already paid, or its neutral nature simply lacking the moistening pull of craving that would let it bear fruit at all), karma certain to ripen yet no longer possessed (already lost, yet its consequence still pending), and karma both possessed and certain to ripen, whether past, future, or presently unfolding.

9. What Is a Learner's 'Plotting of Harm'? Self-Directed Anger, Never Aimed Outward

A precise technical account defines a learner's "plotting of harm" as the vengeful thought — "may he perish, may his mother lose her beloved child" — that a stream-enterer or once-returner, not yet free of desire, may give rise to when wrongfully harmed. The treatise insists this wish is aimed at the sufferer's own self, not at the offender, and hence carries no fault, before extending the puzzle to whether such a thought can arise even after falling back from meditative attainment — where it can, empowered by the residual force of the path once attained — and closing with the case of the Buddha's own rebuke of Māra's messenger, whose fall into hell was his own spent karma at work, not the Buddha's wish to harm him.

10. The Arhat Falsely Imprisoned as a Cattle Thief

One of the collection's most vivid narratives: an arhat in Kashmir, dyeing his own robes beside his monastery, is mistaken by a grieving man for a cattle-thief and butcher — his own past karma distorting the man's perception so that dyed cloth appears as bloodied hide — and is beaten, bound, and imprisoned for years, forgotten even by his own disciples until his karma is finally spent. 12. Puṣyamitra and the Bodhi Tree's Guardian: When Even Vengeful Plotting Fails

The fascicle closes by clarifying that a learner's plotting of harm is never guaranteed to succeed — if beings have created karma of sufficiently overwhelming power, its own ripening simply overrides any plot against them. The illustration is the historical persecution of King Puṣyamitra, who burned scriptures, destroyed hundreds of monasteries, and slaughtered monks with the secret aid of Māra's own demonic allies, advancing even to the sacred Bodhi Tree itself — until its guardian deity, Truth-Speaker, transformed herself into a woman of surpassing beauty, and the Dharma-protecting spirits, seizing the king's resulting distraction, destroyed him and his entire army. Yet within the Buddhadharma, many learners who separately plotted against him all failed — as, in a parallel episode, did the noble ones who rose up against King Tarāḍa's own earlier persecution — since both kings' overwhelming store of merit shielded them until their own karma, not anyone else's plotting, finally brought them down.
