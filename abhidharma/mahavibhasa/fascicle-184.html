The Small Seven and the Great Seven

Fascicle 184 continues the Single Practice Nyāsa (一行納息第五之二): the exhaustive sixfold analysis of possessing the empty and wishless concentrations in every combination of past, present, and future, the parallel sevenfold analysis for the empty and signless concentrations (whose asynchronous attainment produces its own distinctive cases), and a closing methodological reflection on the "great seven" — combining two concentrations against a third — together with a careful taxonomy of the four interrogative methods this literature uses throughout: single line, running through six, small seven, and great seven. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 184 continues the Single Practice Nyāsa (一行納息第五之二) inside the Concentration Aggregate (定蘊第七), directly following fascicle 183's opening of the three concentrations. This fascicle is unusually technical even by this treatise's own standards, consisting almost entirely of an exhaustive combinatorial analysis; the translation follows the source precisely, including its characteristic repetition, since the repetition itself is the argument. Terms established across fascicles 93–183 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Empty and Wishless: The Full Sixfold Analysis

Fascicle 184 continues the Single Practice Nyāsa (一行納息第五之二) by extending the previous fascicle's sixfold temporal analysis — does possessing a concentration in the past guarantee possessing it in the future, the present, both, or neither? — from a single concentration to a genuine pair: the empty and the wishless. The treatise works through every combination of possessing one in one time-frame and the other in another, each answer anchored to a precise meditative stage (which mind-moment of which direct realization of which of the four truths, or which noble fruit, or which type of refined faculty a given practitioner must occupy), producing an exhaustive cross-referenced map of exactly when the two concentrations travel together and when they can, briefly, come apart.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That two concentrations shown, only a fascicle earlier, to be attained simultaneously and to counteract the very same afflictions can nonetheless diverge in their temporal possession — one already ceased and lost while the other still manifests before the mind — reveals a subtlety this tradition insists on preserving: simultaneous attainment at the moment of arising does not collapse two distinct dharmas into a single fate thereafter, and each retains its own separate career across past, present, and future even after being born in the very same instant.

2. Empty and Signless: The Full Sevenfold Analysis

The same exhaustive method is now applied to the empty and the signless concentrations — a pairing with its own distinctive rhythm, since the signless is attained only later, at the specific moment the patient acceptance of the cognition of the Dharma of cessation arises, rather than simultaneously with the empty from the very outset. This asynchrony ripples through the entire combinatorial analysis, producing cases unique to this pairing — such as possessing the empty in the past while the signless has "not yet been attained" at all, a genuinely different condition from the signless having already arisen and then been lost — and the section closes by noting that the same sevenfold method, run in full for the empty against the signless, applies equally to the wishless against the signless.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Distinguishing "not yet attained" from "already attained and then lost" as two genuinely different non-possessions — rather than treating all absence as one undifferentiated category — is exactly the kind of fine-grained discrimination this tradition builds its entire technical vocabulary around: a person who has never yet reached a given attainment and a person who reached it and then regressed are, on this analysis, in meaningfully different states, even though both currently lack the same thing.

3. The Great Seven: Combining Two Concentrations Against One

The fascicle closes with a remarkable methodological reflection: having run the "small seven" (one concentration set against one, across seven temporal combinations), the treatise now considers the "great seven" — two concentrations taken together and set as a single unit against a third, again across seven temporal combinations, which could in principle be run recursively into an unwieldy "sevenfold-sevenfold" structure. After walking through a wasteful, technically correct but practically useless way of generating this structure, the treatise settles on the version that is "of genuine benefit to the text and to the meaning," before closing with a careful terminological taxonomy distinguishing four distinct interrogative methods used throughout this literature: "single line," "running through six," "small seven," and "great seven" — each defined by whether it fixes its terms by time, and whether it sets one term against one or two terms against one.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Explicitly rejecting a technically valid but practically wasteful way of generating the sevenfold-sevenfold structure, in favor of the version that is "of genuine benefit to the text and to the meaning," is a rare and valuable moment of methodological self-awareness: the treatise distinguishes formal correctness from actual usefulness, insisting that a combinatorial exercise earn its place in the text by illuminating something, not merely by being logically exhaustive.
