Why the Buddha Renounced Renunciation

Fascicle 94 tracks who attains and relinquishes the four practice-paths across every stage, resolves the Buddha's own six years of austerity through Mālā's Mother's own challenge, distinguishes Śāriputra's and Mahāmaudgalyāyana's diverging paths, and establishes the non-learner's own right liberation and right knowledge. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 94 continues the Branches of the Learner Nyāsa (學支納息第一之二), closing out the four practice-paths' own attainment and relinquishment across every stage, then establishing the non-learner's own ten branches (無學十支) — the learner's own eight, together with right liberation (正解脫) and right knowledge (正智) — through an extended defense of why these final two are formally established only for the non-learner. Terms established in fascicle 93 and across the preceding Fetters Aggregate recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Who Attains, and Who Relinquishes, How Many Practice-Paths

Fascicle 94 opens exactly where fascicle 93 broke off, tracking in exhaustive detail how many of the four practice-paths a practitioner attains or relinquishes at every stage of the path — advancing through the sixteen mind-moments of seeing, progressing from stream-entry through once-returning, non-returning, and arhatship, sharpening one's own faculty, cultivating further virtues, and, in the stage of retrogression, falling back from a superior faculty or ground to an inferior one. A closing puzzle addresses the Prajñaptiśāstra's own four kinds of individual — slow-then-swift, swift-then-slow, and so on across two lifetimes — resolved by clarifying that this speaks of diligence and indolence alone, not of any genuine transformation of faculty or retrogression, which a noble one who has already advanced can never undergo.

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: with respect to these four kinds of practice-path, who attains how many, and relinquishes how many? The answer: throughout the stage of an ordinary being, there is neither attainment nor relinquishment, since these four practice-paths are solely undefiled. At the very moment the supreme worldly dharma manifests, one attains one or two, without yet having relinquished any. One asks: here, this treatise does not proceed with reference to an ordinary being, but solely with reference to a noble one. The answer: there is likewise a noble one who, with respect to the four practice-paths, possesses neither attainment nor relinquishment — namely, one abiding in his own self-nature; it is within the stages of advancement and retrogression that the meaning of attainment and relinquishment applies. As for the stage of advancement: if, not yet free of the desire realm's own defilement, one enters the fixed state of freedom from birth, then, from the moment one abides in the forbearance of dharma-knowledge of suffering up through the dharma-knowledge of the path, there is, in every case, no relinquishment, and one attains one; at the moment of the forbearance of class-knowledge of the path, one relinquishes one and attains one. If, already free of the desire realm's own defilement, one enters the fixed state of freedom from birth in dependence upon the preliminary stage bordering upon the first dhyāna, then, from the moment one abides in the forbearance of dharma-knowledge of suffering up through the dharma-knowledge of the path, there is likewise, in every case, no relinquishment, and one attains one; at the moment of the forbearance of class-knowledge of the path, one relinquishes one and attains two. If one enters the fixed state of freedom from birth in dependence upon a higher ground, then, from the moment one abides in the forbearance of dharma-knowledge of suffering up through the dharma-knowledge of the path, there is, in every case, no relinquishment, and one attains two; at the moment of the forbearance of class-knowledge of the path, one relinquishes two and attains two. If a stream-enterer proceeds toward the fruit of once-returning, then, at the moment of every preparatory path, five uninterrupted paths, and five paths of liberation, there is, in every case, no relinquishment, and one attains one; at the moment of the sixth uninterrupted path, one relinquishes one and attains one. If a once-returner proceeds toward the fruit of non-returning, then, at the moment of every preparatory path, two uninterrupted paths, and two paths of liberation, there is no relinquishment, and one attains one; at the moment of the ninth uninterrupted path, one relinquishes one and attains two. If a non-returner proceeds toward the fruit of arhatship, becoming free of the first dhyāna's own defilement up through the sphere of nothingness' own defilement, then, at the moment of every preparatory, uninterrupted, and liberation path, there is, in every case, no relinquishment, and one attains two; becoming free of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception's own defilement, then, at the moment of every preparatory path, eight uninterrupted paths, and eight paths of liberation, there is likewise, in every case, no relinquishment, and one attains two; at the moment of the ninth uninterrupted path, one relinquishes two and attains two. This is spoken of as the stage of becoming free of defilement. If, not yet free of the desire realm's own defilement, one who follows through faith transformed into resolve sharpens his own faculty and becomes one who has arrived at insight, then, at the moment of every preparatory path, there is no relinquishment, and one attains one; at the moment of the uninterrupted path, one relinquishes one and attains one. If, already free of the desire realm's own defilement, one who follows through faith transformed into resolve sharpens his own faculty and becomes one who has arrived at insight, then, at the moment of every preparatory path, there is no relinquishment, and one attains two; at the moment of the uninterrupted path, one relinquishes two and attains two. If an arhat liberated depending on occasion sharpens his own faculty and becomes immovable, then, at the moment of every preparatory path, eight uninterrupted paths, and eight paths of liberation, there is, in every case, no relinquishment, and one attains two; at the moment of the ninth uninterrupted path, one relinquishes two and attains two. This is spoken of as the stage of transforming one's own faculty. If a noble one not yet free of the desire realm's own defilement gives rise to the various resembling immeasurables, liberations, masteries, and totalities, together with the contemplation of impurity, the mindfulness of breathing, the applications of mindfulness, and the like, then, at the moment of every such virtue, there is, in every case, no relinquishment, and one attains one. If a noble one already free of the desire realm's own defilement gives rise to the immeasurables, liberations, masteries, and totalities, the contemplation of impurity, the mindfulness of breathing, the applications of mindfulness, the unimpeded understandings, the concentration free of contention, the knowledge of aspiration, the concentration at the very limit, the three concentrations, the three further concentrations, the mixed cultivation of the dhyānas, and the drawing forth of the five superknowledges, then, at the moment of every preparatory path, five uninterrupted paths, and three paths of liberation, and the like, there is, in every case, no relinquishment, and one attains two. If, entering the attainment of cessation, one's own mind of perception grows ever subtler, there is likewise no relinquishment, and one attains two; at the moment of the most subtle mind of all, there is neither relinquishment nor attainment. All such cases as these are spoken of as the stage of cultivating virtues. As for the stage of retrogression: if an arhat, or a learner already free of the desire realm's own defilement, retrogresses from a superior faculty and abides in an inferior one, he relinquishes two and attains two in every case; a learner not yet free of the desire realm's own defilement, retrogressing from a superior faculty and abiding in an inferior one, relinquishes one and attains one in every case. This is spoken of as the stage of retrogressing in faculty. If an arhat, giving rise to an entanglement of the form or formless realm, retrogresses, he relinquishes two and attains two; giving rise to an entanglement of the desire realm and retrogressing, he relinquishes two and attains one. If a non-returner, already free of the form realm's own defilement and the like, gives rise to an entanglement of the form realm and the like and retrogresses, he relinquishes two and attains nothing; this same non-returner, giving rise to an entanglement of the desire realm and retrogressing, relinquishes two and attains one. If a once-returner retrogresses from the path of the superior fruit, he relinquishes one and attains nothing; retrogressing from the fruit of once-returning itself, he relinquishes one and attains one. If a stream-enterer retrogresses from the path of the superior fruit, he relinquishes one and attains nothing. This is spoken of as the stage of retrogressing from freedom from defilement. The meaning of retrogressing from any of the remaining virtues should be understood according to this same pattern. As the Prajñaptiśāstra states: there are four kinds of individual — there is an individual who, within this visible life, is slow, yet, after the body has broken up, is swift; there is an individual who, within this visible life, is swift, yet, after the body has broken up, is slow; there is an individual who, within this visible life, is slow, and, after the body has broken up, is likewise slow; there is an individual who, within this visible life, is swift, and, after the body has broken up, is likewise swift. One asks: the latter two may well be so, but how can this be true of the former two? A noble one, transmigrating into a further birth, certainly neither retrogresses nor transforms his own faculty; and, taking rebirth within the desire realm, he certainly does not enter the form or formless realms — how, then, can it be said that he is presently slow yet later swift, or presently swift yet later slow? How could there possibly be one who has arrived at insight who, taking a further birth, retrogresses into becoming one who follows through faith transformed into resolve? The answer: that treatise does not speak of transforming one's own faculty or of retrogression, but speaks solely of diligence and indolence — if one is indolent in this present body yet diligent in a later body, this is named "slow within this visible life, yet swift after the body has broken up"; if one is diligent in this present body yet indolent in a later body, this is named "swift within this visible life, yet slow after the body has broken up"; if one is indolent in this present body and likewise indolent in a later body, this is named "slow within this visible life, and likewise slow after the body has broken up"; if one is diligent in this present body and likewise diligent in a later body, this is named "swift within this visible life, and likewise swift after the body has broken up." Such is the very intention of what that treatise states.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The resolution of the Prajñaptiśāstra's puzzle is a small masterclass in reading two different technical vocabularies correctly: a genuine transformation of faculty (from dull to keen) and a genuine retrogression (from a higher to a lower attainment) are both governed by strict rules elsewhere in this treatise that make a noble one's own later-life reversal impossible, so when the Prajñaptiśāstra speaks of someone "slow in this life, swift in the next," it cannot be describing either of those — it must instead be describing something far more mundane and universally applicable: simple diligence or indolence, which any practitioner, noble or not, can display or fail to display from one life to the next.

2. Four Kinds of Conduct, and the Question of Inferiority

A parallel classification opens: the four kinds of conduct — unable to endure, able to endure, subduing, and tranquil — mapping onto but not identical with the four practice-paths, the first three of conduct falling entirely outside what the practice-paths themselves gather in. A related puzzle then asks why scripture calls three of the four "kinds of cutting-off" inferior at all, given that the noble path is itself sublime by definition — resolved by distinguishing defiled inferiority (which the noble path can never possess) from merely diminished inferiority (which even a genuinely sublime path can still possess relative to a still more excellent one).

Ru-Yi Translation

As the scripture states: there are four kinds of conduct — first, conduct unable to endure; second, conduct able to endure; third, conduct of subduing; fourth, conduct of tranquility. What is conduct unable to endure? It refers to being unable to endure cold, heat, hunger, thirst, mosquitoes and gnats, wind and sun-exposure, snakes and scorpions and injurious contact, the encroachment and harassment of wicked persons and their unreasonable speech, and the various kinds of pain arising within the body — being incapable of enduring such matters as these is named conduct unable to endure. What is conduct able to endure? It refers to being capable of enduring cold, heat, and the like as stated before — this is named conduct able to endure. What is conduct of subduing? It refers to restraint of the faculties — this is named conduct of subduing. What is conduct of tranquility? It refers to the undefiled path — this is named conduct of tranquility. One asks: do the four practice-paths gather in these four kinds of conduct, or do these four kinds of conduct gather in the four practice-paths? The answer: these four kinds of conduct gather in the four practice-paths; it is not the four practice-paths that gather in these four kinds of conduct. What, then, do they not gather in? This refers to the first three of these.

As the scripture states: there are four kinds of cutting-off — first, painful-and-slow cutting-off; second, painful-and-fast cutting-off; third, pleasant-and-slow cutting-off; fourth, pleasant-and-fast cutting-off. Here, painful-and-slow cutting-off, being both painful and slow, is thereby named inferior; painful-and-fast cutting-off is named inferior solely on account of being painful; pleasant-and-slow cutting-off is named inferior solely on account of being slow; and pleasant-and-fast cutting-off, in the case where it is not genuinely capable of displaying vast and abundant benefit to the multitude of gods and men, being thereby not vast, is likewise named inferior; the Bhagavān's own cutting-off through practice, being genuinely capable of displaying vast and abundant benefit to the multitude of gods and men, is alone named sublime. One asks: do the four practice-paths gather in the four kinds of cutting-off, or do the four kinds of cutting-off gather in the four practice-paths? The answer: they gather into one another reciprocally, each according to its own particular matter — that is, painful-and-slow cutting-off is precisely painful-and-slow practice, and so on up through pleasant-and-fast cutting-off being precisely pleasant-and-fast practice, and it is for this reason that, according to each particular matter, they gather into one another reciprocally. There are, further, those who say: the four kinds of cutting-off belong solely to the non-learner, while the four practice-paths pervade both the learner and the non-learner. If one holds this position, then the four practice-paths gather in the four kinds of cutting-off, but the four kinds of cutting-off do not gather in the four practice-paths. What, then, do they not gather in?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between defiled inferiority and merely diminished inferiority is a genuinely useful piece of conceptual hygiene: it lets the treatise call three-quarters of the noble path "inferior" without any contradiction, since inferiority-by-comparison (falling short of something still more excellent) and inferiority-by-corruption (being tainted by affliction) are simply different properties, and the noble path, incapable of the second, remains fully capable of the first whenever a genuinely more excellent alternative exists to be measured against.

3. The Three Vehicles Mapped onto the Four Cuttings-Off

An extended, many-voiced debate maps the Buddha's, the solitary-realizer's, and the disciple's own noble paths onto the four kinds of cutting-off — with painful-and-slow and painful-and-fast gathered within the disciple's own two liberations, pleasant-and-slow likewise disciple-bound, and pleasant-and-fast split in two: whatever falls short of vast, universal benefit belonging to the disciple or solitary-realizer, whatever achieves it belonging to the Buddha alone. No fewer than five named positions dispute exactly where the solitary-realizer himself belongs in this scheme, before the compilers settle the whole debate by insisting that the genuine criterion cuts straight across the fourth category alone, regardless of how many named teachers place the boundary elsewhere.

A rich etymological excursus closes out the doctrinal portion of the fascicle: "arhat" is explained through no fewer than three converging derivations — worthy of the world's own supreme offerings; ari (affliction) plus han (able to slay), the wisdom-sword having slain the bandit of affliction entirely; and a (negation) plus rahat (birth), since the arhat is never born again — with a fourth reading through han as every evil, unwholesome dharma and rahita as remoteness, since the arhat stands utterly remote from all of it.

The lamp simile deployed here does real philosophical work beyond mere illustration: it resolves what could otherwise look like a contradiction — the noble path is said to oppose affliction directly, yet also to cause the outflows' own exhaustion, a seemingly separate effect — by distinguishing a cause's own primary target from its incidental byproducts, exactly as a flame's primary opposition to darkness does not prevent it from also, quite independently, consuming its own fuel.

10. The Non-Learner's Own Ten Branches, Ground by Ground

The answer to this closing puzzle — that the non-learner's own task has already been fully accomplished, with no further seeking whatsoever, and it is precisely this cessation of seeking that disqualifies the term "vision" from describing his own final moment — reveals something quietly profound about how this tradition understands the very word "vision" itself: vision names not mere awareness but active, ongoing discernment still reaching toward something not yet settled, and it is for this reason that the non-learner's own path of liberation, marking the definitive end of all further seeking, can only ever be named "knowledge," the word "vision" having, by its very nature, nothing left to attach to there.
