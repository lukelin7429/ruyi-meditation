Eight Liberations, One Tamer

Fascicle 84 completes the four formless attainments — infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception — before treating the eight liberations at length, anchored in the story of King Prasenajit's elephant and its tamer's confession: I can tame the body, but not the mind. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Sphere of Infinite Space: Transcending Form

The Prakaraṇapāda distinguishes the sphere of infinite space into attainment and rebirth, before the treatise unpacks the canonical formula's own three moments — transcending every notion of form, extinguishing every notion of resistance, and no longer giving mental application to any manifold notion — each raising the identical puzzle: since this transcendence was already accomplished far earlier, at the very moment one became free of the first dhyāna's own defilement, why does the formula speak of it again only now, at the threshold of the formless? The resolution distinguishes exceeding a dharma's own-nature from exceeding its own basis, and exceeding through cutting-off from exceeding through non-manifest-activity — a distinction that recurs, with minor variation, to explain why the extinguishing of resistance-notions and the ceasing of manifold notions are likewise spoken of only at this final threshold, though their own more basic forms were relinquished stage by stage long before.

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the sphere of infinite space? The Prakaraṇapāda states: the sphere of infinite space is, altogether, of two kinds: attainment, and rebirth. If one is reborn there, the neutral-and-unobscured feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness — such, in general, is named the sphere of infinite space, and so on, up through, the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, stated in just the same way. Here, as for attainment: this refers to a formless concentration. As for rebirth: this speaks of birth within the formless realm — if one is reborn there, the neutral-and-unobscured feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness — this speaks of the ripening of those four aggregates. As a scripture states, transcending every notion of form, extinguishing every notion of resistance, and, no longer giving mental application to any manifold notion whatsoever, entering boundless space, one dwells having fully attained the sphere of infinite space. Here, as for transcending every notion of form: this refers to transcending the notion associated with eye-consciousness. One asks: at the very time one becomes free of the first dhyāna's own defilement, one has already transcended this notion — why, then, does it speak now of transcending every notion of form? The answer: this speaks with reference to exceeding its own basis. Exceeding is of two kinds: first, exceeding its own-nature; second, exceeding its own basis. At the time one becomes free of the first dhyāna's own defilement, exceeding its own-nature is named transcending; at the time one becomes free of the fourth dhyāna's own defilement, exceeding its own basis is named transcending it. Further, this speaks with reference to exceeding its own manifest activity — exceeding is of two kinds: first, the exceeding of cutting-off; second, the exceeding of non-manifest-activity. At the time one becomes free of the first dhyāna's own defilement, cutting off every notion of form is named transcending; at the time one becomes free of the fourth dhyāna's own defilement, its no longer being manifestly active is named transcending it.

復次過住處故。過有二種。一過欲貪。二過住處。離初靜慮染時。

Further, if one is reborn within the fourth dhyāna, eye-consciousness still draws forth a manifest craving for form, and it is for this reason that, at the time one becomes free of the fourth dhyāna's own defilement, it, too, is spoken of as transcending every notion of form, since it no longer draws forth any craving that takes form as its object. As for extinguishing every notion of resistance: this refers to extinguishing the notion associated with ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-consciousness. One asks: at the very time one becomes free of the desire realm's own defilement, one has already extinguished the notions associated with nose- and tongue-consciousness; at the time one becomes free of the first dhyāna's own defilement, one has already become free of the notions associated with ear- and body-consciousness — why, then, does it speak now of extinguishing every notion of resistance? The answer: every one of the foregoing answers, each as is fitting, applies equally to this question as well. There are further masters who say: the notion associated with aversion is named the notion of resistance. One asks: at the very time one becomes free of the desire realm's own defilement, one has already extinguished every notion associated with aversion — why, then, does it speak now of extinguishing the notion of resistance? The answer: this speaks with reference to exceeding its own basis — that is, whatever basis is capable of giving rise to the notion of aversion, at the very time one now becomes free of the fourth dhyāna's own defilement, one entirely exceeds and transcends it, and it is for this reason that this is named extinguishing. One asks: for what reason does this not instead speak of extinguishing every notion of form and transcending the notion of resistance? The answer: this, too, could equally be spoken either way — the desire to present a differing wording is what gives rise to delight. Further, there are two gateways of desire and manifestation, and so on, stated at full length. As for no longer giving mental application to any manifold notion whatsoever: this refers to no longer causing the fourth dhyāna's own mind-consciousness-associated, manifold, disordered notions to be manifestly active. One asks: what is the meaning of a manifold notion?

答此想緣種種處差別相故。謂染污者緣十處差別相。不染污者緣十二處差別相。是故此想名種種想。問何故說不思惟種種想耶。答以種種想離第四靜慮染時。極作留難繫縛障礙如暴獄卒。故世尊說。

The answer: this notion takes as its object the distinct characteristics of manifold locations — that is, when defiled, it takes the distinct characteristics of ten sense-fields as its object; when undefiled, it takes the distinct characteristics of twelve sense-fields as its object, and it is for this reason that this notion is named a manifold notion. One asks: for what reason does it speak of no longer giving mental application to a manifold notion? The answer: because a manifold notion, at the very time one becomes free of the fourth dhyāna's own defilement, serves as an especially great obstacle, binding one exactly as a violent jailer, the World-Honored One therefore states:

離第四靜慮染時。不應思惟起種種想。如是便能速離此染入無邊空。空無邊處者。問此何故名空無邊處。為以自性。為以所緣。設爾何失。二俱有過。所以者何。若以自性。空無邊處以四蘊為自性不應名空。若以所緣空無邊處緣四聖諦及虛空非擇滅。云何但名空無邊處。答應作是說。此不以自性亦不以所緣。但以加行故名空無邊處。如施設論說以何加行修空無邊處定。由何加行入空無邊處定。謂初業者先應思惟牆上樹上崖上舍上等諸虛空相。取此相已假想勝解。觀察照了無邊空相。以先思惟無邊空相。而修加行。展轉引起初無色定故。說此名空無邊處。復次法爾初遠離色地名空無邊處。復次法爾初解脫色地名空無邊處。謂瑜伽師先攀上色地離下色地染。若離第四靜慮染時。攀空無邊處四蘊而離第四靜慮染。先緣上地作虛空想。後方引起離下染道。如人上樹先攀上枝而捨下枝。

As for the sphere of infinite space: one asks, for what reason is this named the sphere of infinite space? Is it named so by its own-nature, or by its object? Suppose either is granted, what fault follows? Both alternatives carry a fault. For what reason is this so? If by its own-nature, the sphere of infinite space takes the four aggregates as its own-nature, and ought not to be named "space" at all; if by its object, the sphere of infinite space takes the four noble truths, and space and non-analytical cessation, as its object, so how could it be named solely the sphere of infinite space? The answer: one should say as follows — this is named neither by its own-nature nor by its object, but solely by its preliminary practice is it named the sphere of infinite space, exactly as the Prajñaptiśāstra states: by what preliminary practice does one cultivate the concentration of the sphere of infinite space? By what preliminary practice does one enter the concentration of the sphere of infinite space? A beginner in this practice ought first to reflect upon the characteristic of space found above a wall, above a tree, above a cliff, or above a house, and the like, and, having grasped this characteristic, through imagined, provisional conviction, observe and illuminate the characteristic of boundless space — it is by first reflecting upon the characteristic of boundless space in this way, and thereby cultivating this preliminary practice, gradually drawing forth the first formless concentration, that this is named the sphere of infinite space. Further, it is simply the very nature of things that the ground first removed from form is named the sphere of infinite space. Further, it is simply the very nature of things that the ground of the first liberation from form is named the sphere of infinite space — that is, a practitioner of yoga, first ascending toward a higher ground of form and becoming free of a lower ground of form's own defilement — at the time he becomes free of the fourth dhyāna's own defilement, he ascends toward the four aggregates of the sphere of infinite space and thereby becomes free of the fourth dhyāna's own defilement, first taking a higher ground as his object in the form of a notion of space, and only afterward drawing forth the path that becomes free of the lower ground's own defilement, exactly as a man climbing a tree first grasps a higher branch before releasing a lower one —

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The repeated appeal to "exceeding its own basis" rather than merely "exceeding its own-nature" is a genuinely useful distinction for tracking spiritual progress across many stages: a coarse form of a given obstacle can be cut off quite early, yet its own underlying basis — the deeper structural possibility of that obstacle recurring — may persist for many further stages, so that a scripture's repeated claim to "transcend" the very same thing at different points is not redundant restatement but tracking progressively deeper layers of the identical obstacle.

2. The Tree-Climbing Simile, and the Monk Who Searched for His Own Body

Since the sphere of infinite space takes the four aggregates as its own-nature, and takes the four noble truths and the unconditioned as its object, in what sense is it named specifically for "space" at all? The resolution locates its name not in own-nature or object but in its own preliminary practice — a beginner first reflects on the space glimpsed above a wall or a tree, gradually cultivating this into the boundless space of the attainment itself, exactly as a man climbing a tree grasps each higher branch before releasing the one below, until, reaching the very top with no further branch to grasp, he holds to nothing but space alone. A charming anecdote confirms this concentration's own lingering outflow: a monk, emerging from this very attainment, was seen groping through the empty air with both hands — asked what he sought, he answered, quite seriously, that he was searching for his own body, prompting the gentle correction that his body lay right there upon his own bed.

As for one dwells having fully attained: this refers to having obtained, acquired, and attained the possession of the sphere of infinite space's own wholesome four aggregates, this obtaining, acquiring, and attaining of possession being spoken of using the words "dwelling having fully attained," and it is for this reason that this is named the sphere of infinite space.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The monk's own earnest search for a body he has momentarily forgotten is more than a piece of comic relief: it is offered as genuine evidence of how thoroughly the concentration of infinite space reorients a practitioner's own habitual sense of embodiment, the disorientation upon emerging serving as exactly the kind of outflow-effect this treatise elsewhere uses to confirm what a preceding concentration must actually have been like from the inside.

3. The Sphere of Infinite Consciousness

The sphere of infinite consciousness receives the identical treatment: neither its own-nature nor its object could alone justify its specific name, and it is solely its own preliminary practice — reflecting first on the purity of the six kinds of consciousness before gradually cultivating this into boundless consciousness itself — that earns it the name. A practitioner emerging from this attainment likewise carries its own distinctive outflow, abiding afterward with a lingering delight in the very characteristic of consciousness itself.

云何識無邊處。如契經說。超一切空無邊處入無邊識。識無邊處。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That both the sphere of infinite space and the sphere of infinite consciousness are named, in the end, neither for what they fundamentally are nor for what they take as their object, but purely for the specific technique by which a practitioner enters them, is a quietly significant methodological choice: it treats these two attainments' own names as records of a path, not descriptions of a destination, preserving in the very vocabulary the memory of how one gets there rather than what, in its final own-nature, is actually reached.

4. The Sphere of Nothingness: Why So Named?

The sphere of nothingness earns its name through a cluster of converging reasons: no other ground so thoroughly weakens the grasping of self and of what belongs to self; no other ground so effectively subdues the view of permanence, possessing no genuinely real, unchanging dharma whatsoever; and no other ground so thoroughly vanquishes arrogance and negligence, possessing no destination, refuge, or shelter to rely upon. It is, further, the very first ground where the boundless aspect itself is relinquished — the Venerable Vasumitra adding that within this concentration there turns no aspect of grasping or of being grasped at all, exactly as it is said that there exists neither a self possessing any location, time, or object, nor any location, time, or object belonging to a self. This ground alone, moreover, earns the specific name "relinquishment," since it is the very last ground among those that still possess the noble path, the Great Virtuous One adding that it relinquishes the very operative function of mental application itself, abiding without any further activity whatsoever.

Chinese Source

云何無所有處。如契經說。超一切識無邊處。入無所有無所有處具足住。是名無所有處。問此何故名無所有處。答此中無我無我所故。問一切地中無我我所。何獨此名無所有處。答無有餘地能令我執及我所執。羸劣穿薄勢力減少如此地者。故此獨名無所有處。復次此地無有真實常恒不變易法。損伏常見勝諸餘地。故此獨名無所有處。復次此地無有所趣所歸屋舍室宅能為救護。摧伏憍慢懈怠放逸勝諸餘地。故此獨名無所有處。復次此地中無無邊行相初捨彼相。故此獨名無所有處。尊者世友作如是說。於此定中無能所攝行相轉故。如說非我有處有時有所屬物。亦無處時物屬我者。故此獨名無所有處。具足住者。謂得獲成就無所有處善四蘊。於得獲成就說具足住聲。是故說名無所有處。問佛何故說無所有處獨名捨耶。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the sphere of nothingness? As a scripture states, transcending every sphere of infinite consciousness entirely, entering nothingness, one dwells having fully attained the sphere of nothingness — this is named the sphere of nothingness. One asks: for what reason is this named the sphere of nothingness? The answer: it is because, here, there exists neither a self nor what belongs to a self. One asks: within every ground there exists neither self nor what belongs to a self — why, then, is this ground alone named the sphere of nothingness? The answer: no remaining ground is capable of causing the grasping of self and the grasping of what belongs to self to become as weakened, as thinned, and as diminished in force as within this ground, and it is for this reason that this ground alone is named the sphere of nothingness. Further, this ground possesses no genuinely real, permanent, constant, unchanging dharma, subduing the view of permanence more thoroughly than every remaining ground, and it is for this reason that this ground alone is named the sphere of nothingness. Further, this ground possesses no destination, no refuge, no dwelling or house capable of serving as a shelter, vanquishing arrogance, indolence, and negligence more thoroughly than every remaining ground, and it is for this reason that this ground alone is named the sphere of nothingness. Further, within this ground there exists no boundless aspect, this being the very first relinquishing of that aspect, and it is for this reason that this ground alone is named the sphere of nothingness. The Venerable Vasumitra speaks as follows: within this concentration there turns no aspect of what is grasped or of what grasps, exactly as it is said, there is neither a self that possesses a location, a time, or an object belonging to it, nor is there any location, time, or object that belongs to a self, and it is for this reason that this ground alone is named the sphere of nothingness. As for one dwells having fully attained: this refers to having obtained, acquired, and attained the possession of the sphere of nothingness's own wholesome four aggregates, this obtaining, acquiring, and attaining of possession being spoken of using the words "dwelling having fully attained," and it is for this reason that this is named the sphere of nothingness. One asks: for what reason does the Buddha speak of the sphere of nothingness alone as named "relinquishment"?

答捨謂聖道能盡捨故。有聖道地此最為後故於此地獨立捨名。尊者世友作如是說。此地近捨假想勝解無邊行相。麁觀解故獨立捨名。

The answer: relinquishment refers to the noble path, since it is capable of ultimately relinquishing — among the grounds that possess the noble path, this one comes last, and it is for this reason that this ground alone is established with the name of relinquishment. The Venerable Vasumitra speaks as follows: this ground draws near to the relinquishing of imagined, provisional conviction's own boundless aspect, being coarser in its own observation, and it is for this reason that it alone is established with the name of relinquishment.

大德說曰。此地棄捨作意功用無邊行相心心所法。無功用住故獨名捨。

The Great Virtuous One says: this ground relinquishes the very operative function of mental application, the mind and mental factors possessed of a boundless aspect, abiding without any further operative function whatsoever, and it is for this reason that it alone is named relinquishment.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The observation that within the sphere of nothingness "there turns no aspect of grasping or of being grasped" is a strikingly advanced epistemological claim for what might otherwise seem a mere cosmological way-station: it suggests this attainment briefly dissolves the very subject-object structure that ordinarily organizes experience, which is precisely why the treatise elsewhere treats it as uniquely effective against the deepest grasping of self.

7. Why the Formless Alone Is Described as "Transcending"

Why does scripture speak of the formless attainments using the word "transcending," while the dhyānas are almost never so described? 8. The Lifespans of the Four Formless Attainments

The four formless attainments carry lifespans of twenty, forty, sixty, and eighty thousand aeons respectively — a progression that seems irregular at first (doubling, then increasing by only a smaller fraction) until several converging explanations resolve it. Both the boundless aspect and every remaining aspect of the first two spheres draw forth an identical measure of lifespan, but no boundless aspect exists above the second sphere, so the doubling stops there; the sphere of nothingness instead possesses a uniquely superior contemplative practice, subduing self-grasping more thoroughly than any other ground, and it is this distinctive quality that resets the progression, causing every subsequent aspect's own contribution to double again. Most simply, each of the upper three spheres, having become free of one, two, or three formless grounds below it respectively, adds that same freed ground's own twenty-thousand-aeon increment on top of the original twenty thousand, yielding the neat arithmetic progression the scripture actually states.

10. The First, Second, and Third Liberations: Impurity and Purity

The first liberation observes external forms — festering, bloated, reduced to bone — while still possessing an internal notion of form oneself, precisely in order to relinquish that very notion; the second observes the identical external forms only once that internal notion has already been relinquished, no longer needing to relinquish anything further. A genuinely difficult puzzle follows: can one simultaneously possess and lack an internal notion of form while observing external form, since a single mind cannot hold two contradictory understandings at once? The resolution insists one genuinely lacks the internal notion throughout, the scripture's own wording describing instead the practitioner's own prior anticipatory resolve or preliminary discriminating practice. The third liberation, the pure, differs from the second in ground, continuum, aspect, and object alike — the second may depend on either an inner or outer path and bears the aspect of impurity, counteracting craving for form, while the pure liberation depends solely on the inner path and bears the aspect of purity, counteracting the contemplation of impurity itself, its own own-nature and object both alike bright, pure, and sublime where the second liberation's own object remains unrefined even while its own-nature shines.

11. Why Cultivate the Pure Liberation? The Monk Who Attained Arhatship Overnight

Why does a practitioner move on from the contemplation of impurity to cultivate the pure liberation at all? Several converging motives answer this: to test whether one's own wholesome root has genuinely reached completion, since freedom from affliction while contemplating something pure is a far more demanding proof than freedom from affliction while contemplating something already repulsive; to lift a mind grown gloomy and stagnant from prolonged contemplation of decay, exactly as one wearied by repeatedly visiting a graveyard refreshes himself by wandering a beautiful garden instead; and to demonstrate the sheer firmness of one's own wholesome root, since remaining unmoved even by a genuinely pure object proves far more than remaining unmoved by an object already designed to repel. A vivid story illustrates this liberation's own restricted availability: a monk who insisted on an elaborately adorned, perfumed dwelling before he would even accept it — behavior that might seem like simple vanity — turned out to have died from the heaven of pure deities and been reborn among humans, so that only this exquisite dwelling could support his cultivation of the pure liberation, through which, in a single night, he attained the entire sequence of liberations, became an arhat, drew forth supernormal power, and departed by dawn, leaving Ānanda to report to the Buddha only an empty bed.

The answer: it is in order to test whether his own wholesome root is complete or incomplete — that is, a practitioner of contemplation reflects as follows: although I contemplate the impure aspect and give rise to no affliction,

而未知善根為成滿不。若觀淨相煩惱不生。乃知善根已得成滿故觀淨相修淨解脫。復次修觀行者觀不淨相心沈慼故善品不增。為令善品更增進故。復觀淨相修淨解脫。如遊塚間數觀屍穢。心沈慼故善品不增。為令善品更增進故。觀妙園林流泉池沼。或遊城邑觀諸妙事令心欣悅能修勝善。此亦應然故觀淨相。復次修觀行者久觀不淨。心便樂著善品不增。為令善品得增進故。捨不淨觀修淨解脫。

I do not yet know whether my own wholesome root has already come to be complete. If, contemplating the pure aspect, no affliction arises, then I shall know that my own wholesome root has already come to be complete — and it is for this reason that he contemplates the pure aspect and cultivates the pure liberation. Further, a practitioner of contemplation, contemplating the impure aspect, finds his own mind sunk in gloom, and his own wholesome quality fails to increase; in order to cause his own wholesome quality to further advance, he further contemplates the pure aspect and cultivates the pure liberation — exactly as one who wanders among graves, repeatedly contemplating a corpse's own filth, finds his own mind sunk in gloom, and his own wholesome quality fails to increase; in order to cause his own wholesome quality to further advance, he observes a wondrous garden and grove, flowing springs and pools, or wanders through a city or town, observing every wondrous matter there, causing his own mind to delight and rejoice, and thereby able to cultivate a superior, wholesome quality — this, too, is just the same, and it is for this reason that he contemplates the pure aspect. Further, a practitioner of contemplation, having long contemplated impurity, finds his own mind coming to delight and cling to it, and his own wholesome quality fails to increase; in order to cause his own wholesome quality to advance, he relinquishes the contemplation of impurity and cultivates the pure liberation.

復次修觀行者欲顯自心堅牢不退。謂緣淨境煩惱不生況緣餘境。故觀淨相修淨解脫。復次修觀行者顯自善根有大勢力。謂緣淨境煩惱不生況緣餘境。故觀淨相修淨解脫。復次顯淨解脫非諸有情皆能修起。唯妙勝解樂淨天歿。來生人中乃能修起。故修行者修淨解脫。

Further, a practitioner of contemplation wishes to display that his own mind is firm and sturdy, never regressing — that is, taking a pure object as his object, no affliction arises, let alone taking any other object as his object — and it is for this reason that he contemplates the pure aspect and cultivates the pure liberation. Further, a practitioner of contemplation displays that his own wholesome root possesses great force and power — that is, taking a pure object as his object, no affliction arises, let alone taking any other object as his object — and it is for this reason that he contemplates the pure aspect and cultivates the pure liberation. Further, this displays that the pure liberation is not something every kind of sentient being is capable of cultivating and giving rise to — only one whose exquisite conviction delighted in purity has died from the heaven of pure deities and come to be reborn among human beings is capable of cultivating and giving rise to it, and it is for this reason that a practitioner of contemplation cultivates the pure liberation.

曾聞苾芻於日後分來詣佛所求好房舍佛勅阿難與好房舍。阿難受勅而授與之。彼苾芻言宜淨掃灑。懸繒幡蓋燒香散花。敷軟床褥安置好枕。我乃受之不爾不用。阿難於是具以白佛。佛言隨索皆應與之。爾時阿難具辦授與。苾芻受已於夜初分起淨解脫。因是次第起餘解脫。諸漏永盡成阿羅漢復修加行引起神通。於晨朝時乘通而去。阿難於後往詣彼房不見苾芻。但見床座尋往白佛。佛告阿難汝勿輕彼。彼於昨夜起淨解脫及餘解脫成阿羅漢引起神通晨朝已去。

It has been heard that there was a monk who, in the latter part of the day, came to where the Buddha was, seeking a fine dwelling. The Buddha instructed Ānanda to give him a fine dwelling. Ānanda, receiving this instruction, gave it to him. The monk said: it should be well swept and cleaned, hung with silk banners and canopies, incense burned and flowers scattered, a soft couch and bedding spread, and fine pillows arranged — only then shall I accept it; otherwise, I shall not use it. Ānanda accordingly reported all this in full to the Buddha, and the Buddha said: whatever he asks for, all of it should be given to him. Ānanda thereupon prepared and furnished it in full. The monk, having received it, in the first part of the night gave rise to the pure liberation, and, by this, in due sequence, gave rise to every remaining liberation as well, forever exhausting his own contaminants and becoming an arhat, and, further cultivating the preliminary practice, drew forth supernormal power, and, in the early morning, rode upon this supernormal power and departed. Ānanda, afterward, went to that very dwelling, and, not seeing the monk there, saw only the bed and seat, and went at once to report this to the Buddha. The Buddha told Ānanda: do not think lightly of him — last night, he gave rise to the pure liberation and every remaining liberation, becoming an arhat and drawing forth supernormal power, and has already departed this very morning.

然彼苾芻從妙勝解。樂淨天歿來生人中。彼若不得淨妙房舍便不能修第三解脫。乃至不得極果神通。由是故知此淨解脫非諸有情皆能修起。唯樂淨者乃能起之。第三與解脫亦如前說。四無色解脫如四無色說想受滅解脫如後根蘊當廣分別。數及解脫准前應知問何故靜慮少分善根立為解脫。無色地一切皆立解脫耶。答靜慮麁顯明了見見。故少善根立為解脫。無色細隱不明了不現見故根本地皆立解脫。復次靜慮中有種種異相不相似法。故少善根立為解脫。無色不爾是故總立。復次靜慮中有異相根受心心所法。故少善根立為解脫。無色不爾是故總立。復次靜慮有多功德勝利。故少善根立為解脫。無色不爾是故總立。復次靜慮遍緣自上下地。故少善根立為解脫。無色唯緣自上非下是故總立。復次靜慮解脫唯是有漏故別建立。無色解脫亦通無漏是故總立。問因論生。論何故靜慮解脫唯有漏。無色解脫通有漏無漏耶。答前說五門亦通。答此此中復有一不共。答謂靜慮中所有解脫唯與勝解作意相應諸無色中所有解脫多與真實作意相應故不相似。

Yet this monk, from an exquisite conviction that delighted in purity, had died from the heaven of pure deities and come to be reborn among human beings — had he not obtained a pure, exquisite dwelling, he would have been unable to cultivate the third liberation at all, let alone attain the ultimate fruit and supernormal power. It is by this that it is known that this pure liberation is not something every kind of sentient being is capable of cultivating and giving rise to — only one who delights in purity is capable of giving rise to it. The third, and "liberation," too, are stated as before. The four formless liberations are stated exactly as the four formless attainments themselves are stated. The liberation of the cessation of perception and feeling shall be extensively distinguished later, within the Faculties Aggregate. Their number and their status as liberation should be understood in accordance with what has already been stated before. One asks: for what reason is only a small portion of the dhyāna's own wholesome root established as liberation, while every wholesome root of the formless ground is established as liberation? The answer: the dhyānas are coarse, evident, clear, and directly seen, and it is for this reason that only a small portion of their wholesome root is established as liberation; the formless is subtle, hidden, unclear, and not directly seen, and it is for this reason that the root ground, as a whole, is established as liberation. Further, within the dhyānas there exist manifold, distinct, and dissimilar dharmas, and it is for this reason that only a small portion of their wholesome root is established as liberation; the formless is not so, and it is for this reason that it is established as a whole. Further, within the dhyānas there exist distinctly characterized faculties, feelings, and mind and mental factors, and it is for this reason that only a small portion of their wholesome root is established as liberation; the formless is not so, and it is for this reason that it is established as a whole. Further, the dhyānas possess many kinds of merit and superior advantage, and it is for this reason that only a small portion of their wholesome root is established as liberation; the formless is not so, and it is for this reason that it is established as a whole. Further, the dhyānas pervasively take their own ground, and the grounds above and below it, as their object, and it is for this reason that only a small portion of their wholesome root is established as liberation; the formless can take only its own ground, and the grounds above it, and not below, as its object, and it is for this reason that it is established as a whole. Further, the liberation of the dhyānas is solely contaminated, and it is for this reason that it is separately established; the liberation of the formless spans both the contaminated and the undefiled, and it is for this reason that it is established as a whole. One asks, on account of this discussion raising a further discussion: for what reason is the liberation of the dhyānas solely contaminated, while the liberation of the formless spans both the contaminated and the undefiled? The answer: the five gateways already stated above equally apply here as well, and this raises a further, unshared answer — that is, whatever liberation exists within the dhyānas is associated solely with mental application through resolved conviction, while whatever liberation exists within any of the formless attainments is, for the most part, associated with genuine mental application, and it is for this reason that they are not alike.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The monk's own seemingly fussy insistence on a perfumed, elaborately furnished room, which a careless reader might mistake for ordinary vanity, is in fact this entire episode's real point: his own prior life among the pure deities left him constitutionally unable to cultivate the pure liberation in anything less than a genuinely pure environment, so that what looks like fastidiousness is instead a precise, karmically determined necessity — and the story's real payoff is the Buddha's own gentle rebuke to Ānanda not to judge by appearances what he could not have known lay behind them.

12. Direction and Liberation: Ghoṣa's Three Resemblances, Three Differences, and King Prasenajit's Elephant

Why does the Buddha speak of the eight liberations using the language of direction, exactly as the four truths were earlier spoken of using the four directions? Liberation and direction share the same number, eight, and the Venerable Ghoṣa develops the elephant-taming analogy already used for the truths into a precise structure of three resemblances and three differences: like an elephant-tamer, the Buddha must proceed toward liberation before he can tame anyone, tames only one person through one liberation at a time, and, in causing one liberation to manifest, draws every other liberation temporarily far off — yet, unlike the tamer, the Buddha needs no journey at all, sitting in a single place; can tame many people through many liberations simultaneously; and, once a liberation has been accomplished, every other liberation remains near rather than distant. This whole extended comparison is then anchored in a story: King Prasenajit's own prized elephant, thoroughly tamed, nonetheless breaks into a wild, uncontrollable chase at the mere sight of a herd of females, injuring both the king and his elephant-keeper before its own desire finally subsides and it returns docilely to the palace.

Chinese Source

問何故世尊於八解脫以方聲說。答觀所化故如於四諦說四方聲。問解脫與方有何相似。答解脫與方俱有八故。問方乃有十如何相似。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: for what reason does the World-Honored One speak of the eight liberations using the language of direction? The answer: this observes the fitness of those to be converted, exactly as, with respect to the four truths, the language of the four directions is used. One asks: what resemblance is there between liberation and direction? The answer: liberation and direction alike number eight. One asks: yet there are, in fact, ten directions — how, then, can this resemblance hold?

答如調象法唯依八方。非上下方是故相似。復次如人平面唯視八方。如是正心修八解脫。復次如依八方能調龍象。解脫亦爾。依八加行而現在前除解脫障。尊者妙音作如是說。方與解脫三同三異。

The answer: exactly as the method of taming an elephant relies solely upon eight directions, not upon the directions of above and below, so too is this resemblance found here. Further, exactly as a man standing upon level ground sees only the eight directions, so too does a correctly settled mind cultivate the eight liberations. Further, exactly as one relies upon the eight directions to be able to tame a dragon-elephant, so too is liberation just the same — one relies upon eight preliminary practices for it to manifest before one, removing the very obstacle to liberation. The Venerable Ghoṣa speaks as follows: direction and liberation share three points of resemblance and differ in three points.

言三同者。一如調象者要趣於方乃能調象。世尊亦爾。要趣解脫能調所化。二如調象者。於一時間唯趣一方而調一象。世尊亦爾。一時唯依一種解脫調一所化。三如調象者。令所調象趣一方時去餘方遠。世尊亦爾。令所化生起一解脫現在前時餘解脫遠不現行故。言三異者。一如調象者。要趣於方乃能調象。世尊不爾。端坐一處亦令所化能起解脫。二如調象者。於一時間唯趣一方而調一象。世尊不爾。於一時間令多所化起多解脫。三如調象者。令所調象趣一方時去餘方遠。世尊不爾。令所化生起一解脫現在前時餘解脫近由成就故。復次佛欲自顯是勝調御。故於解脫說以方聲。曾聞憍薩羅主勝軍大王。勅捕象人捕大野象。令調象者依調象法善調御之。象既調已王與象師共乘遊獵。時所乘象見雌象群。欲心熾盛即便奔逐。

As for the three points of resemblance: first, exactly as one who tames an elephant must proceed toward a direction before he can tame the elephant, so too must the World-Honored One proceed toward liberation before he can tame those to be converted. Second, exactly as one who tames an elephant, within a single span of time, proceeds toward only a single direction and tames a single elephant, so too does the World-Honored One, within a single span of time, rely upon only a single kind of liberation to tame a single one to be converted. Third, exactly as one who tames an elephant, causing the elephant being tamed to proceed toward a single direction, thereby draws it far from every other direction, so too does the World-Honored One, causing one to be converted to give rise to a single liberation manifest before him, thereby cause every remaining liberation to remain far off, without manifest activity. As for the three points of difference: first, exactly as one who tames an elephant must proceed toward a direction before he can tame the elephant, the World-Honored One is not so — sitting upright in a single place, he is still able to cause those to be converted to give rise to liberation. Second, exactly as one who tames an elephant, within a single span of time, proceeds toward only a single direction and tames a single elephant, the World-Honored One is not so — within a single span of time, he causes many of those to be converted to give rise to many liberations. Third, exactly as one who tames an elephant, causing the elephant being tamed to proceed toward a single direction, thereby draws it far from every other direction, the World-Honored One is not so — causing one to be converted to give rise to a single liberation manifest before him, every remaining liberation, by virtue of having already been accomplished, still remains near. Further, the Buddha, wishing to display that he himself is the most excellent tamer, therefore speaks of liberation using the language of direction. It has been heard that King Prasenajit, the ruler of Kośala, ordered elephant-catchers to capture a great, wild elephant, and had an elephant-tamer, in accordance with the method of taming elephants, skillfully tame and subdue it. Once the elephant had been tamed, the king and the elephant-keeper together rode it out on a hunting excursion. At that time, the elephant being ridden caught sight of a herd of female elephants, and, his own desire blazing fiercely, he at once broke into a wild chase.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ghoṣa's own careful separation of exactly three resemblances from exactly three differences, rather than a vaguer general comparison, is characteristic of this treatise's preferred method of building an analogy: it refuses to let the elephant-taming image simply illustrate the Buddha's own teaching in some impressionistic way, instead specifying with precision where the comparison holds and where the Buddha's own capacities decisively exceed it, so that the analogy teaches exactly as much as it is meant to and no more.

13. The Hot Iron Ball, and "I Can Tame the Body, Not the Mind"

Facing execution for his failure, the elephant-keeper insists the elephant truly is tamed and asks leave to prove it — and, once the elephant's own desire has subsided and it has returned of its own accord, he tests it with a burning-hot ball of iron placed upon its very head, gently warning that this is the last stage of its taming and that refusal will mean repeating every harsh trial that came before. The elephant endures without flinching, its scalp scorching exactly like burning birch bark, moving the king to marvel and order the iron removed at once — yet, asked why so thoroughly tamed a creature had broken loose before, the elephant-keeper offers a startlingly honest confession: I am capable of taming the body, but I am not capable of taming the mind.

Chinese Source

象師盡術制不能迴。王與象師俱被傷損。遇因攀樹命濟還宮。王責象師付法刑罰。時調象者白大王言。彼象實調願王許驗。時象貪息便速還宮。象師見之將詣王所。遂於象頂置熱鐵丸徐語之言。此是最後調伏汝法。應忍受之。若不忍受必以先來調汝苦事次第調汝。

Ru-Yi Translation

The elephant-keeper exhausted every skill in an attempt to turn him back, yet could not succeed, and both the king and the elephant-keeper suffered injury from this. Coming, by chance, upon a tree to cling to, they were able to save their own lives and return to the palace. The king reproached the elephant-keeper and handed him over for legal punishment. At that time, the elephant-tamer said to the great king: that elephant is, in genuine truth, tamed — I only ask that the king allow me to demonstrate this. At that time, the elephant's own desire had already subsided, and it swiftly returned to the palace of its own accord. The elephant-keeper, seeing this, led it before the king, and then placed a burning-hot ball of iron upon the crown of its head, gently saying to it: this is the very last stage of your own taming — you should endure and accept it. If you do not endure and accept it, I shall certainly, in due sequence, tame you once more through every one of your own former, painful trials.

象聞便忍不動如山。時熱鐵丸燒然象頂。如燒樺皮王見嗟怪令去鐵丸。告象師曰此象既調先何故爾。象師跪白。我能調身不能調心。

The elephant, hearing this, endured it without moving, exactly like a mountain, even as the burning-hot iron ball scorched the crown of its head, exactly as one might scorch a strip of birch bark. The king, seeing this, marveled and exclaimed in wonder, and ordered the iron ball to be removed, and said to the elephant-keeper: this elephant, being already tamed, why did it act as it did before? The elephant-keeper knelt and answered: I am capable of taming the body, but I am not capable of taming the mind.

王言頗有能調心者。象師曰有謂佛世尊。能調眾生身心諸病。王聞歡喜尋與象師。乘所調象往詣佛所。見佛世尊多百千眾圍遶說法。

The king asked: is there, then, anyone at all capable of taming the mind? The elephant-keeper answered: there is — namely, the World-Honored One, who is capable of taming every sentient being's own sickness of body and mind alike. The king, hearing this with delight, at once took the elephant-keeper along with him, riding the very elephant that had now been tamed, and went to where the Buddha was. He saw the World-Honored One, surrounded by many hundreds of thousands of people, expounding the dharma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The elephant-keeper's own admission — that he can command the body completely while remaining utterly powerless over the mind — is the whole episode's real philosophical center, reframing what looked like a story about animal training into a precise diagnosis of a much more universal problem: external behavior can be perfectly disciplined through method and consequence alone, yet the very desire that produces that behavior in the first place lies entirely beyond such external control, requiring something categorically different to address at all.

14. Meeting the Buddha: The Simile Made Explicit

Learning from the elephant-keeper that only the Buddha himself is capable of taming sickness of both body and mind together, the delighted king rides his own newly tested elephant directly to where the Buddha sits teaching, and the Buddha, after expounding a dharma no disciple or solitary-realizer could ever fathom, makes the entire preceding analogy explicit to the assembled monks: exactly as an elephant-tamer proceeds toward only one of the eight directions at a time in taming a single elephant, the unsurpassed tamer relies upon all eight directions at once in taming those to be converted — these eight directions serving precisely as the simile for the eight liberations, displaying the World-Honored One as the most excellent tamer of all.

Chinese Source

前禮佛足退坐一面。佛即為王說甚深法。非諸獨覺聲聞所知。因告苾芻如調象者。正調象時於八方內但趣一方而調於象。調馬牛等亦復如是。無上調御調所化時頓依八方而調所化言八方者喻八解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

He came forward, bowed at the Buddha's own feet, and withdrew to sit to one side, and the Buddha thereupon expounded a most profound dharma for the king, one that no solitary-realizer or disciple could ever know, and then told the monks: exactly as one who tames an elephant, at the very time he genuinely tames the elephant, proceeds toward only a single one of the eight directions in taming it — and the taming of a horse, an ox, and the like is just the same — the unsurpassed tamer, at the time he tames those to be converted, relies all at once upon all eight directions in taming them, these eight directions here serving as a simile for the eight liberations.

故顯世尊是勝調御。於八解脫說以方聲。

It is for this reason that, in order to display that the World-Honored One is the most excellent tamer, the eight liberations are spoken of using the language of direction.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own closing move — simultaneously taming through all eight liberations at once, where the elephant-tamer can address only one direction at a time — completes the comparison's earlier three-differences list with its most decisive point: the very asymmetry that makes the Buddha superior to even the finest human tamer is not a difference of degree within the same kind of skill, but a difference in kind, since human taming is inherently sequential while the Buddha's own capacity to address every liberation simultaneously has no genuine analogue in ordinary training at all.
