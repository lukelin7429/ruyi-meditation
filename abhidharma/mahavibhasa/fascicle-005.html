Five Rivers Into the Sea

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 5 — the closing installment of the treatise's direct analysis of supreme mundane dharma within the Miscellaneous Chapter's ongoing first section (雜蘊第一中世第一法納息第一之四), which then turns the treatise's attention to forbearance (忍), the nirvedhabhāgīya root just below it. The fascicle establishes that supreme mundane dharma is a single moment of mind, never many; proves, through the unforgettable image of five great rivers that cannot be turned back once flowing toward the sea, that it can never retrogress; works out several four-alternative logical grids for what it takes as its object and what it means to possess it; explains why forbearance alone, among the four roots, earns the name "according with the truths"; and closes by tracing forbearance's own remarkable structure — a wide field of contemplation that narrows, round after round, down to a single moment's single aspect, the doorway supreme mundane dharma itself opens onto. Fascicle 6 continues the same nyāsa section, turning to summit's own detailed treatment.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

This fascicle continues directly from Fascicle 4 and closes out the long nyāsa (納息) section on supreme mundane dharma (世第一法) begun in Fascicle 2 — terms established across the previous three fascicles, such as the certainty of rightness (正性離生) and the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering (苦法智忍), recur constantly here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. One Mind, Not Many

The fascicle opens with a question left implicit since Fascicle 2's own debate over future cultivation: is supreme mundane dharma one single moment of mind, or does it consist of many? The treatise settles the question decisively, then explains a further Vibhajyavādin position it means to refute along the way.

Chinese Source

世第一法。當言一心多心耶。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答雖已說彼相應差別。而未顯示現前多少。今欲顯示唯一剎那。故作斯論復次為欲遮遣他宗義故。如分別論者執。世第一法。相續現前。彼說相續總有三種。一時相續。二生相續。三相似相續。世第一法。雖無前二。而有後一。今欲遮遣彼所執義顯世第一法唯一念現前。復次為令疑者得決定故。謂先已說。若心心所法。為等無間入正性離生。是謂世第一法。勿有生疑。如心所法既有多種心亦應然。為令彼疑得決定故。顯心所法雖有多種而心唯一。由此因緣。故作斯論。世第一法當言一心多心耶。答應言一心。問如前已說。未來修者。亦得名為世第一法。是則此法應有多心。而言一心斯有何意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should supreme mundane dharma be said to be a single moment of mind, or many, and so on, as explained at length? One asks: why was this further passage composed? The answer: although the distinctions in what it is associated with have already been explained, how many moments it occurs in has not yet been shown, and this passage now shows that it occurs for exactly a single moment. Further: it is in order to refute another school's position — for the Vibhajyavādins hold that supreme mundane dharma occurs present in continuous succession. They hold that "continuous succession" is, in total, of three kinds: succession of a single occasion, succession of rebirth, and succession of mere similarity. Supreme mundane dharma, though it lacks the first two, does possess the third — and this passage now means to refute their position and show that supreme mundane dharma occurs present for exactly a single thought-moment. Further: it is in order to bring certainty to those in doubt — for it was already said that if mind and mental factors serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the certainty of rightness, this is called supreme mundane dharma; lest one doubt that, just as the mental factors are of many kinds, the mind, too, ought to be many, this passage now shows, in order to settle that doubt, that although the mental factors are of many kinds, the mind itself is only ever one. It is for this reason that this passage was composed. Should supreme mundane dharma be said to be a single moment of mind, or many? The answer: it should be said to be a single moment. One asks: as already explained, the future instances it cultivates are also named supreme mundane dharma — should this dharma not then consist of many moments of mind? What, then, is the point of saying "a single moment"?

The answer: here, only what is presently occurring is spoken of, and this is why it is said to be "a single moment." One asks: why, then, does this passage not mention the future instances it cultivates? The answer: wherever a passage ought to state something yet does not, one should understand that its meaning is left for elsewhere — and there are, further, many additional reasons for this, already given earlier, which need not be repeated here. Although it has been said to be a single moment, the reasoning has not yet been explained — why is this dharma not many moments of mind? A bare assertion is not enough to make the meaning clear. The answer: immediately following this mind and its mental factors, no other mundane mind arises — only a supramundane mind arises. A "mundane mind" means a contaminated mind still fallen within existence — namely, exactly the second moment and beyond of supreme mundane dharma, which this passage rules out. A "supramundane mind" means an uncontaminated mind that cuts off existence — namely, the mind associated with the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering, which this dharma draws forth. If some other mundane mind were to arise instead, would it be weaker, equal, or stronger than the one before it? None of these is possible. This threefold question — weaker, equal, or stronger — is posed here simply in order to work through the analysis, since only these three relations to the preceding moment exist. If it were weaker, it could not enter the certainty of rightness — why is this? Because it is never a path of retrogression that enters the certainty of rightness, but only a path of advancement, ever more vigorous and firm, that can do so — a path of decline, withering, falling, and ruin can never enter the certainty of rightness. If it were equal, it likewise could not enter the certainty of rightness — why is this? Because a path of exactly this same kind has already failed to enter the certainty of rightness once before — that is, since this later moment is of the very same kind as the very first moment, which was itself obstructed, beset with difficulty, and lacking in power, and so could not enter the certainty of rightness, the moments that follow, being of the very same kind, should be exactly the same: just as the first moment was unable to give rise, without interval, to the noble path, so too should every later moment of the same kind —

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Vibhajyavādins' threefold taxonomy of "continuous succession" — a single occasion, rebirth, and mere similarity — is a genuinely useful piece of technical vocabulary: mere similarity is the weakest sense, meaning only that later moments resemble earlier ones in kind, without any of the stronger continuity a single sustained occasion or a causal chain across rebirths would imply. The treatise's rebuttal does not deny even this weak sense outright — it grants that later moments would resemble the first, and then uses exactly that resemblance to argue they must fail in exactly the same way the first moment did, since nothing has changed to make a repetition of the same weak, obstructed state suddenly succeed.

2. Weak, Equal, Strong — None Could Enter the Path

The exhaustive elimination continues: even the "stronger" alternative fails, since it would contradict the very definition of supreme mundane dharma as a single, unique moment — closing with a question about why the path of seeing, unlike the path of cultivation, demands such heightened effort.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reasoning that a "stronger" second moment would simply relocate the name "supreme mundane dharma" onto itself, demoting the first moment to mere "superior forbearance," is a nice illustration of how tightly this treatise ties a technical name to a structural position rather than to any fixed content — whatever moment turns out to be the single, unrepeatable peak earns the name, and nothing else can share it, no matter how similar.

3. Causes and Conditions, Moment to Moment

The treatise works out, in full technical detail, exactly which of the four classical conditions link supreme mundane dharma's own past, present, and future moments to one another.

Chinese Source

謂相應俱有。與未來為一因。謂同類未來與未來為二因。謂相應俱有。現在與現在為二因。謂相應俱有。與未來為一因。謂同類增上緣者。謂不礙生及唯不障。

Ru-Yi Translation

the associated cause and the co-existent cause; and serves as one cause for a future moment — the cause of the same kind. A future moment serves as two causes for a future moment — the associated cause and the co-existent cause. A present moment serves as two causes for a present moment — the associated cause and the co-existent cause — and as one cause for a future moment — the cause of the same kind. As a dominant condition: it means simply not obstructing arising, and nothing more than not obstructing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This dense causal grid is the treatise's standard machinery — introduced already in Fascicle 3's discussion of conditions — applied here to a single dharma's relationship with its own earlier and later moments across the three times. The dominant condition's minimal definition, "not obstructing," is worth noting: it is the weakest of the four conditions precisely because almost everything qualifies for it by simply staying out of the way.

4. Why This Dharma Can Never Retrogress

The treatise turns to its next major claim: supreme mundane dharma, once arisen, can never be lost or reversed. The argument begins with the image of a dharma "according with, advancing toward, and about to enter" the truth, with no room in between for anything to intervene.

Chinese Source

世第一法。當言退不退耶。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答雖已說彼一心非多。而未分別為退不退。今欲分別故作此論。復次為止他宗顯正義故。謂或有執。世第一法亦有退者。為止彼意。顯示此法決定不退故作斯論。世第一法當言退不退耶。答應言不退。雖有此說應更顯示不退因緣。非但有言義便顯了。何故此法定不退耶。答世第一法。隨順諦趣向諦臨入諦。此彼中間。無容得起不相似心。令不得入聖諦現觀。問云何名為隨順諦。趣向諦。臨入諦耶。有說此中現觀說名為諦。謂世第一法。隨順現觀。趣向現觀。臨入現觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should supreme mundane dharma be said to retrogress, or not, and so on, as explained at length? One asks: why was this further passage composed? The answer: although it has already been shown that this dharma is a single moment of mind, not many, whether it can retrogress has not yet been analyzed, and this is now taken up. Further: it is in order to stop another school's position and make this treatise's own correct meaning clear — for some hold that supreme mundane dharma, too, can retrogress; in order to stop this position, the passage shows that this dharma certainly cannot retrogress, and this is why it was composed. Should supreme mundane dharma be said to retrogress, or not? The answer: it should be said not to retrogress. Though this much is stated, the reason it cannot retrogress should be further shown — a bare assertion does not make the meaning clear. Why is this dharma certainly unable to retrogress? The answer: supreme mundane dharma accords with the truth, advances toward the truth, and is about to enter the truth — and in the interval between this dharma and that truth, there is no room whatsoever for a dissimilar mind to arise that could prevent the direct realization of the noble truths from occurring. One asks: what is meant by "according with the truth," "advancing toward the truth," and "about to enter the truth"? Some say: "the truth," here, names direct realization itself — that is, supreme mundane dharma accords with, advances toward, and is about to enter direct realization.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The claim here is stronger than simple momentum: it is not merely that supreme mundane dharma tends toward the truth, but that the gap between it and the forbearance that follows is, structurally, too narrow for anything else to fit into — no dissimilar mind has anywhere to occur. Retrogression is ruled out not by willpower or merit but by the sheer absence of an interval in which it could happen at all.

5. Four Readings of "the Truth" Itself

Three further readings of what "the truth" means in this formula follow — the truth of the path, the path of seeing, and the truth of suffering — before the treatise settles on a fourth: the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering itself.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Some say: "the truth," here, names the truth of the path — that is, supreme mundane dharma accords with, advances toward, and is about to enter the truth of the path. Some say: "the truth," here, names the path of seeing itself — that is, supreme mundane dharma accords with, advances toward, and is about to enter the path of seeing. Some say: "the truth," here, names the truth of suffering — that is, supreme mundane dharma accords with, advances toward, and is about to enter the truth of suffering. Some say: "the truth," here, names the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering itself — that is, supreme mundane dharma accords with, advances toward, and is about to enter the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering. Now, "according with" is here of two kinds: according-with-as-advancing-toward, and according-with-as-about-to-enter. Supreme mundane dharma possesses both kinds of according-with, with respect to the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering, since it serves as the immediately-antecedent condition that draws it forth. As for "in the interval between this dharma and that truth, there is no room for a dissimilar mind to arise": this means that, in the interval between supreme mundane dharma and the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering, there is no room whatsoever for a contaminated mind still fallen within existence — a "dissimilar" mind — to arise and prevent the direct realization of the noble truths, that is, to prevent the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering from becoming present. One asks: supreme mundane dharma is itself contaminated, and so ought to be "dissimilar" to an uncontaminated mind — why, then, does the passage call a contaminated mind still fallen within existence "dissimilar," yet call an uncontaminated mind that cuts off existence "similar"? The answer: supreme mundane dharma is itself disgusted with the contaminated and inclines toward the uncontaminated — this is why a contaminated mind is called "dissimilar" and an uncontaminated mind "similar": because it turns away from the one and toward the other, just as someone troubled by their own relatives might rely on a stranger for protection, coming to regard their own relatives as strangers and the stranger as a relative. The case here is exactly the same. Others say: supreme mundane dharma accomplishes the very same single task as the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering — namely, relinquishing the nature of an ordinary being and the rest — and this is why a contaminated mind is called "dissimilar" and an uncontaminated mind "similar": because they accomplish the very same single task together. Reading note — Ru-Yi

Four masters, four different referents for the single word "truth" — the path's truth, the path of seeing itself, the truth of suffering, and the forbearance that is its own direct object — a reminder that even a short technical formula repeated constantly across this fascicle can still generate genuine interpretive disagreement about what its key term actually points to. The relative-and-stranger simile does real work: it explains why "contaminated" gets called the dissimilar, alien state and "uncontaminated" the similar, welcome one, even though ordinarily one would expect a person's own contaminated mind to feel far more like home.

6. The Warrior Crossing the River

The treatise reaches for its first extended simile to make the "no room for a dissimilar mind" claim vivid: a warrior committed to crossing a river, valley, mountain, or cliff cannot simply turn back midway, his forward momentum already too great.

Chinese Source

亦能解了。故說現喻。譬如壯士渡河渡谷渡山渡崖。中間無能迴轉。彼身還至本處。或往餘處。先所發起。增上身行未至所趣。必不止息。世第一法亦復如是。隨順諦。趣向諦。臨入諦。此彼中間。無容得起不相似心。令不得入聖諦現觀。此中渡河者。謂從此岸往趣彼岸。渡谷者。謂從此邊往趣彼邊渡山者。謂從此峯往趣彼峯渡崖者。謂從高趣下。或如有人。從屋[木*脊]墮。未至地頃。

Four distinct crossings packed into one simile — river, valley, mountain, cliff — each a slightly different physical situation, yet all sharing the one property the argument needs: a body already in motion, past the point where reversal is even physically coherent, not merely inadvisable.

7. The Man Falling From a Roof

The falling-man image continues into its punchline: no ordinary wish, however sincere, can undo momentum already set in motion — only supernatural power could, and no such power exists to interrupt supreme mundane dharma's own momentum toward the forbearance that follows it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The falling man's wish is the whole point: sincerity and effort of will are explicitly ruled out as capable of reversing what is already in motion — only an external power of a wholly different order (magic, potent substances) could do it, and none exists here. This sets up the second, grander simile that follows: if even a falling body strains ordinary intuition about reversal, a river is a far more natural image for something that, once flowing a certain way, simply does not flow the other way at all.

8. Five Great Rivers Flowing to the Sea

11. Nothing Faster Than Mind

The treatise offers a final, non-simile argument for why nothing can intervene between supreme mundane dharma and the forbearance it draws forth: nothing whatsoever moves faster than mind itself, and so nothing could ever catch up in time to obstruct it.

This argument is worth setting beside the five-rivers simile: where the river illustrated an absence of any path back or sideways, this passage adds a temporal dimension — even granting that some obstruction might in principle exist, nothing could ever arrive in time to apply it, since mind itself outraces everything else that exists. The list of hypothetical obstructors is notably comprehensive, running all the way up to buddhas and pratyekabuddhas themselves — the point being not that such beings would ever want to interfere, but that not even they could, structurally, act fast enough.

16. Three Kinds of Nirvedhabhāgīya, and Why Supreme Mundane Dharma Needs No Special Name

Three further, brief reasons close the retrogression discussion, followed by a technical classification of the nirvedhabhāgīya roots into three divisions — leaning toward retrogression, toward stability, or toward advancement — and an explanation of why supreme mundane dharma alone needs no separate name for its own division.

Chinese Source

The progression across the four nirvedhabhāgīya roots is worth tracking as a single narrative arc: heat admits all three divisions (a beginner can still slide back, hold steady, or advance); summit, sitting at the acknowledged "borderline" stage, drops the stable middle option entirely — you either progress or regress, no standing still; forbearance drops the possibility of regression altogether; and supreme mundane dharma, finally, admits only advancement. Read together with Fascicle 2's earlier list of reasons this dharma comes first, this passage gives the technical backbone beneath all that imagery: irreversibility is not a poetic flourish but a formally defined classification this dharma alone, among the four roots, wholly satisfies.

17. Four Alternatives: What Does It Take as Object?
