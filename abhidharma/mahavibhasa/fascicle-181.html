The Four Noble Lineages of Contentment

Fascicle 181 continues the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之八): Mahākāśyapa and Vakkula's two opposite ways of not discriminating among food, the four noble lineages of contentment with food, robes, and bedding, together with delighting in cutting off affliction and cultivating the path, why "wanting little" is passed over for "contentment," why every past Buddha praised the rag-robe yet forbade its wearing while Śākyamuni permits it, the four noble lineages as supreme knowledge, Mahānāman and the gradual severance of the five hindrances, the causal loop between a breach in the Dharma and a breach in the Vinaya, and what it means to practice the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma. 1. Mahākāśyapa and Vakkula: Two Ways of Not Discriminating Food

Fascicle 181 opens the Non-Returner Nyāsa's next stretch (不還納息第四之八) by comparing two disciples declared foremost in different ascetic qualities: Mahākāśyapa, who eats whatever food comes to him in the order received, refusing nothing fine and demanding nothing coarse, versus Vakkula, who deliberately sets aside excellent food to eat only the coarse. The treatise works out exactly why each behaves as he does — Mahākāśyapa's own delight in the excellent means he need not discriminate against it, while Vakkula's delight in the coarse leads him to set the fine aside — before cataloguing five separate reasons Vakkula might refuse delicious food (the labor it costs others, the craving it nourishes, the wealth it presupposes, the slaughter it requires, the faults it breeds), and resolving a genuine puzzle: does Vakkula's asceticism mean simply never accepting fine food, or accepting and discarding it? The treatise insists on the former, since discarding food already accepted would damage a donor's own gift.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Refusing to let either Mahākāśyapa's easy omnivorousness or Vakkula's fastidious restriction serve as the single model of ascetic virtue, and instead explaining each as the natural expression of a different underlying disposition, is characteristic of how this tradition treats saintly conduct: not as a single behavioral standard everyone should imitate, but as the visible signature of an inner cultivation that can validly take more than one outward shape.

2. The Four Noble Lineages Introduced

The treatise formally introduces the four noble lineages (聖種) — contentment with whatever food, robes, or bedding one receives, and delighting in cutting off affliction while delighting in cultivating the path — establishing their shared intrinsic nature as the wholesome root of non-greed (with the fourth alternatively analyzed as diligence), each counteracting its own specific craving. A cascade of etymologies for "noble lineage" follows — because it is itself noble and itself a lineage, because it is the lineage belonging to the noble ones, because it sustains an unbroken continuum in the noble one's own mind, because it gives rise to a lovable ripened fruit, because Buddhas and disciples are literally born from it, because it sustains the true Dharma in the world the way beams sustain a house — before the treatise firmly excludes two look-alikes from counting as noble lineages at all: an innate wholesome quality (lest even an insect's egg qualify), and any virtue, however disciplined, cultivated by outsiders who still seek only rebirth and pleasure rather than genuine liberation.

評曰。不應作是說。所以者何。五識中善唯生得故。問若是加行善者外道所得。靜慮無量無色解脫勝處遍處等亦是聖種耶。答彼非聖種。所以者何。若欣樂解脫厭背生死彼善根是聖種。外道所樂皆是生死故彼善根不名聖種。復次於有有具生喜足名聖種。外道善根與此相違故非聖種。以五頂外道等亦求生天受欲樂故。復次聖種皆是出家品善根。外道善根皆是在家品攝故非聖種。

Comment: this ought not to be said. Why is this? Because the wholesome quality found among the five sense-consciousnesses is only ever innate. Question: if they are wholesome qualities attained through effortful practice, then would the dhyānas, the immeasurables, the formless attainments, the liberations, the bases of vanquishing, and the totality-bases attained by outsiders also count as noble lineages? Answer: those are not noble lineages. Why is this? Because a wholesome root counts as a noble lineage only if it delights in liberation and turns away in aversion from saṃsāra; whatever outsiders delight in is entirely bound up with saṃsāra, and hence their wholesome roots are not named noble lineages. Furthermore, a noble lineage is named for giving rise to contentment with respect to existence and the requisites of existence; the wholesome roots of outsiders run contrary to this, and hence are not noble lineages, since even outsiders such as the followers of the Pañcaśikha school still seek rebirth in heaven to enjoy sensual pleasures. Furthermore, the noble lineages are, in every case, wholesome roots belonging to the category of one who has gone forth, whereas the wholesome roots of outsiders are, in every case, subsumed under the category of the householder, and hence are not noble lineages.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ruling out an innate wholesome disposition from counting as a noble lineage by pointing out that even an ant's egg or a gnat would then qualify is a small but pointed piece of philosophical hygiene: the treatise is not content to let a definition stand merely because it captures the intended cases — it actively hunts for absurd edge cases that a careless definition would also have to admit, and rejects the definition rather than accept the absurdity.

3. Wanting Little versus Contentment

Why is contentment, and not simply "wanting little," established as a noble lineage, when both alike counteract greed? The treatise finds a precise conceptual flaw in the rival term: "wanting little" understates the ideal (it falls short of wanting nothing at all) while simultaneously overstating some people's actual attainment (calling truly desireless people merely "of few wants"), a double imprecision contentment avoids entirely. A further, more polemical reason follows: naming "wanting little" a noble lineage would hand ascetic naked wanderers exactly the rhetorical opening they wanted, letting them claim genuine superiority over robed, alms-begging, tree-sheltered monks simply by wanting even less materially, however hollow their own inner contentment. The distinction is finally reduced to pure tense: wanting little concerns what has not yet been obtained, in the future, while contentment concerns what has already been obtained, in the present.

Chinese Source

問少欲喜足俱對治貪無貪為性。何故喜足立聖種非少欲耶。答少欲之名有過失有增益。喜足不爾。有過失者但言少欲不言無欲故。有增益者於實無欲而名少欲故。於喜足中無如是事故立聖種。有說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: both wanting little and contentment alike counteract greed, and are of the nature of non-greed — why, then, is contentment established as a noble lineage, and not wanting little? Answer: the term "wanting little" carries both a fault and an excess, whereas "contentment" does not. The fault is that it merely says "wanting little" without saying "wanting nothing at all"; the excess is that it applies the term "wanting little" even to one who in fact wants nothing at all. Since contentment involves no such difficulty, it alone is established as a noble lineage. Some say:

少欲於未來處未得事轉。喜足於現在處已得事轉。不取現在一迦履沙鉢拏為難非於未來。轉輪王位以喜足難故立為聖種。有說。為異外道故。不說少欲為聖種。若說少欲為聖種者。諸外道輩當作是言我等真是住聖種者。所以者何。汝等猶著糞掃衣而我等露形無衣。

wanting little operates with respect to what is not yet obtained, in the future; contentment operates with respect to what has already been obtained, in the present. It is difficult to refrain from taking even a single kārṣāpaṇa presently in hand, but not difficult with respect to some future gain, even the position of a wheel-turning king itself — and it is because contentment is difficult in just this way that it is established as a noble lineage. Some say: it is in order to differ from outsiders that wanting little is not spoken of as a noble lineage. Were wanting little declared a noble lineage, the outsiders would then say: "we, in truth, are the ones who abide in the noble lineage — for you monks still wear rag-robes, while we go about entirely naked, without robes at all;

汝等猶乞食自活。而我等多自餓不食。汝等猶坐樹下而我等或常舉手蹻足而住。是故我等真名住聖種者。為遮彼故但說喜足為聖種。

you monks still sustain yourselves by begging for alms, while many of us starve ourselves and eat nothing at all; you monks still sit beneath trees, while we constantly stand with a hand raised and one leg lifted — hence we, in truth, are the ones named as abiding in the noble lineage." It is in order to forestall such a claim that only contentment, and not wanting little, is spoken of as a noble lineage,

外道於有有具不喜足故。

since outsiders are not content with respect to existence and the requisites of existence.

問少欲喜足何差別。有說。少欲唯在意地。喜足通六識身。有說。

Question: what difference is there between wanting little and contentment? Some say: wanting little exists only in the mental-consciousness ground, whereas contentment extends to all six consciousness-bodies. Some say:

少欲唯欲界。喜足通欲色界。有說。少欲唯欲色界。喜足通三界。

wanting little exists only in the desire realm, whereas contentment extends to the desire and form realms. Some say: wanting little exists only in the desire and form realms, whereas contentment extends to all three realms.

有說。少欲墮三界。喜足三界繫及不繫。如是說者少欲喜足俱通三界繫及不繫。問若爾何差別。答少欲於未來處未得事轉。喜足於現在處已得事轉。是謂差別。

Some say: wanting little falls within all three realms, whereas contentment is bound to all three realms as well as unbound. According to this latter view, both wanting little and contentment alike extend to being bound to all three realms as well as unbound. Question: if so, what difference remains between them? Answer: wanting little operates with respect to what is not yet obtained, in the future, whereas contentment operates with respect to what has already been obtained, in the present — this is the difference between them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Anticipating precisely the rhetorical move naked ascetics would make — a simple one-upmanship of external deprivation — and closing off that avenue in advance by refusing the more externally showy virtue the status of a noble lineage, reveals this tradition treating terminology itself as contested ground: a word's exact definition was never merely academic, since the wrong definition would have handed a real polemical victory to a rival school.

4. Why Medicine Has No Noble Lineage of Its Own

If robes, food, and bedding each earn their own noble lineage, why not medicine, the fourth requisite? The treatise offers several converging reasons: contentment with medicine risks the opposite pastoral effect, letting an unmotivated sick monk hide behind his illness rather than press forward in practice; medicine's enjoyment, unlike the other three, only ever removes an ailment rather than risking heedlessness; and, unlike food, robes, or bedding, medicine is never something everyone, everywhere, at every time actually makes use of — illustrated by Vakkula's own extraordinary boast that in over eighty years as a monk he never once recalled so much as a headache, let alone needing medicine for it.

Chinese Source

問病緣醫藥所生喜足何故不說聖種耶。答亦應說聖種而不說者當知有餘。有說。已攝在前所說中。謂病緣醫藥有二種。一可食二不可食。可食者攝食中。不可食者攝衣服臥具中。有說。為欲饒益病苾芻故不說於藥喜足為聖種。謂有苾芻身雖有病以少務故不求醫藥。

Question: why is the contentment arising from medicine, as a condition for illness, not spoken of as a noble lineage? Some say: it is already subsumed under what was spoken of before — that is, medicine, as a condition for illness, is of two kinds, edible and inedible; the edible kind is subsumed under food, and the inedible kind is subsumed under robes and bedding. Some say: it is in order to benefit sick monks that contentment with medicine is not spoken of as a noble lineage — that is, there may be a monk who, though his body is ill, does not seek out medicine on account of having few concerns,

若佛立此為聖種者。彼便守病不能勤修聖道加行。為饒益彼令勤修道是故不說彼為聖種。有說。若受用時能生放逸於彼喜足立為聖種。病緣醫藥於受用時但能除病不增放逸是故不說。有說。若一切處一切人一切時受用者於彼喜足立為聖種。病緣醫藥非有如是一切受用故彼喜足不立聖種。是以尊者薄矩羅言。我於佛法中出家年過八十。曾不憶身有疾疹乃至頭痛。亦不憶受用病緣醫藥乃至訶梨怛雞。生欲界者尚然何況生色無色界。問何故別解脫律儀唯無表立聖種非表耶。答前說相續不斷名為聖種。表非相續不斷是故不說。有說。無表可與聖道俱故立聖種。表不與聖道俱是故不說。出家者有四聖種。在家者亦有四聖種。然出家者二因緣故名有聖種。一意樂故。二受用故。諸在家者由一因緣名有聖種。謂意樂故非受用故。

and, were the Buddha to establish this as a noble lineage, such a monk would then simply hold onto his illness and fail to diligently cultivate the preparatory practice of the noble path; it is in order to benefit him, causing him to cultivate the path diligently, that this is not spoken of as a noble lineage. Some say: contentment is established as a noble lineage only where its enjoyment is capable of giving rise to heedlessness; medicine, as a condition for illness, when enjoyed, is only able to remove illness and does not increase heedlessness, and hence it is not spoken of as such. Some say: contentment is established as a noble lineage only where it is enjoyed in every place, by every person, and at every time; medicine, as a condition for illness, does not admit of this kind of universal enjoyment, and hence contentment with it is not established as a noble lineage. It is for this reason that the Venerable Vakkula said: "I have, within the Buddha's Dharma, gone forth for more than eighty years, and I do not recall my body ever having had so much as a headache, nor do I recall ever having made use of medicine as a condition for illness, down to even a single harītakī fruit." If this is so even for one born in the desire realm, how much more so for one born in the form or formless realm! Question: why is the noble lineage established only with respect to the unmanifest form of the prātimokṣa restraint, and not its manifest form? Answer: as explained before, being unbroken in continuity is what is named a noble lineage; the manifest form is not unbroken in continuity, and hence it is not spoken of. Some say: the unmanifest form is able to occur together with the noble path, and hence is established as a noble lineage; the manifest form does not occur together with the noble path, and hence is not spoken of. Both those who have gone forth and those who remain householders possess the four noble lineages; yet those who have gone forth are said to possess the noble lineages on two counts — through disposition, and through actual enjoyment — whereas householders are said to possess them on only one count, through disposition alone, not through actual enjoyment,

如天帝釋處妙花座有十二那庾多侍女圍遶六萬音樂而自娛樂。於四聖種恒有意樂而無受用。頻毘娑羅等諸國王蘇達多等諸長者亦爾。

just as Śakra, lord of the devas, seated upon his wondrous flower-throne, surrounded by twelve nayuta attendant maidens and entertained by sixty thousand musical instruments, constantly possesses the disposition of the four noble lineages, yet without their actual enjoyment; and kings such as Bimbisāra and elders such as Sudatta are likewise so.

問為有一一聖種即名住聖種者耶。答不爾。要具四種方名住聖種者。然得一聖種時必具得四。如無礙解得一必具四。

Question: does possessing any single one of the noble lineages already qualify one to be named one who abides in the noble lineage? Answer: it does not. Only one who possesses all four together is named one who abides in the noble lineage; yet, at the very moment one attains a single noble lineage, one necessarily attains all four together, just as, with the unimpeded understandings, attaining one necessarily means attaining all four.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Citing Vakkula's own eighty-year freedom from even a headache as evidence for why medicine cannot anchor a noble lineage the way food, robes, and bedding do is a wry piece of argument: the very existence of someone for whom the fourth requisite was simply never needed becomes proof that it lacks the universal, everyone-everywhere-always character the other three requisites' own lineages depend upon.

5. The Rag-Robe: Praised by Every Buddha, Permitted Only by Śākyamuni

A genuinely striking historical claim follows: every past Buddha praised the rag-robe pieced together from discarded cloth, yet none of them actually permitted monks to wear it, whereas Śākyamuni both praises and permits it. The treatise offers several explanations rooted in changing human nature across ages — people in the past had such slight greed that even luxurious robes barely tempted them, or such delicate bodies that only fine cloth could sustain them, or such generous dispositions that seeing monks well-provisioned inspired their faith, all the reverse of the present degraded age, where only visible austerity now inspires devotion. The section closes by pairing each of the four noble lineages with two illustrative examples from the early sangha — one disciple reproached for failing to embody it, another praised for embodying it perfectly.

Chinese Source

過去諸佛皆稱讚糞掃衣而不許著。今釋迦佛亦稱讚糞掃衣而便許著。問何故爾耶。答過去時人貪心微薄雖得價直百千衣服。染著之心不如今人染著凡衣故。有說。彼時人眾豐饒財寶。諸苾芻等欲求價直百千衣服。易於今時求糞掃衣故。尊者世友說曰。過去時人意樂廣大。見諸苾芻畜上妙資具。便生歡喜信敬之心。今時人意樂狹劣。若見苾芻畜麁弊資具。乃生歡喜信敬之心故。尊者覺天說曰。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every past Buddha alike praised the rag-robe, yet did not permit its wearing; the present Buddha Śākyamuni likewise praises the rag-robe, yet does in fact permit its wearing. Question: why is this so? Answer: people in the past had such slight greed of mind that, even upon obtaining robes worth a hundred thousand pieces of gold, their attachment to them was not as strong as present-day people's attachment even to a mere ordinary robe. Some say: people in that former age were abundant in wealth and treasure, so that monks seeking robes worth a hundred thousand pieces of gold found this easier than present-day monks find it to seek out a mere rag-robe. The Venerable Vasumitra states: people in the past had such vast and generous dispositions that, upon seeing monks possessed of fine, excellent requisites, they gave rise to joy and reverent faith; whereas present-day people have such narrow and inferior dispositions that it is only upon seeing monks possessed of coarse, worn-out requisites that they give rise to joy and reverent faith. 往昔時人身體細濡若受用麁弊物者則不能自存。今世時人身體麁䩕雖受用麁弊則能自存故。大德說曰。過去諸佛稱讚糞掃衣當知即是亦許受用。若不爾者何故稱讚。諸佛不以無事而有所說故。

people in ancient times had bodies so delicate and soft that, were they to make use of coarse, worn-out things, they could not sustain themselves; whereas present-day people have bodies so coarse and hardy that, even making use of coarse, worn-out things, they are able to sustain themselves. The Venerable One states: it should be understood that, when past Buddhas praised the rag-robe, this was itself already a permission to make use of it — for, were it not so, why would they have praised it at all? Buddhas never speak without good reason.

如世尊說糞掃衣少易得無罪。云何少云何易得云何無罪。尊者世友說曰。少用功而辦故。非多人受用故名少。隨彼彼時處皆可得故名易得。佛所聽許故智者受用故名無罪。大德說曰。少價量故名少。

As the World-Honored One states, the rag-robe is little, easily obtained, and blameless — what is meant by "little," by "easily obtained," and by "blameless"? The Venerable Vasumitra states: it requires little effort to prepare, and is not made use of by many people, and it is hence named "little"; it can be obtained in whatever place or time one happens to be, and it is hence named "easily obtained"; the Buddha himself has sanctioned and permitted it, and the wise make use of it, and it is hence named "blameless." The Venerable One states: it is of little monetary value, and is hence named "little";

不從他求故名易得。無攝受故名無罪。由二因緣佛說於衣喜足為聖種。一為訶責於衣不喜足如難陀等。二為讚美於衣喜足如大迦葉波等。由二因緣佛說於食喜足為聖種。一為訶責於食不喜足如婆拕梨等。二為讚美於食喜足如薄矩羅等。由二因緣佛說於臥具喜足為聖種。一為訶責於臥具不喜足。如愚王苾芻等。如契經說愚王苾芻白佛言。唯願世尊觀我床座麁弊如是。二為讚美於臥具喜足如頡戾茷多等。由二因緣佛說樂斷樂修為聖種。一為訶責懈怠如闡陀等。二為讚美精進如室路拏等。由四因緣則知彼是安住聖種補特伽羅。一不樂聞他說得利養。二不樂親近貪美食人。三所畜資具少而清淨不生染著。四於諸利養得與不得不生愛憎。

it is not sought from others, and is hence named "easily obtained"; and it involves no attachment, and is hence named "blameless." On account of two causal conditions the Buddha spoke of contentment with bedding as a noble lineage: first, in order to reproach those discontent with bedding, such as the monk called "Foolish King" and the like — as the sūtra relates, the monk Foolish King said to the Buddha, "World-Honored One, please observe how coarse and worn my own bedding and seat are" — and second, in order to praise those content with bedding, such as Hastaka and the like. On account of two causal conditions the Buddha spoke of delighting in cutting off and cultivating as a noble lineage: first, in order to reproach idleness, such as Chanda's; and second, in order to praise diligence, such as Śroṇa's. On account of four causal conditions one may know that a given person truly abides in the noble lineages: first, he takes no delight in hearing of others' gains; second, he takes no delight in associating with those who crave delicious food; third, the requisites he keeps are few, yet pure, and he gives rise to no attachment toward them; and fourth, whether he obtains gain or not, he gives rise to neither craving nor aversion.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Explaining a change in monastic policy across the procession of Buddhas by appeal to a steadily degrading human constitution — bodies once so delicate that coarse robes would have killed their wearers, now so coarse that only coarse robes suit them — quietly naturalizes moral and physical decline together: virtue and physiology are treated as running in lockstep across cosmic time, so that even a robe's own fabric has to answer to which age of the world it is worn in.

6. The Four Noble Lineages as Supreme Knowledge

The treatise unpacks the sūtra's own fourfold praise of the noble lineages — as supreme knowledge, the knowledge of one's own lineage, delightful knowledge, and untainted knowledge, beyond the reproach of any being anywhere in the cosmos. Each epithet receives several candidate explanations: "supreme" because these four satisfy every aspiration where most beings die with theirs unmet, or because, unlike a wheel-turning king's own wealth, they can be enjoyed endlessly without ever requiring accumulation, a locked door, or fear of theft; "knowledge of one's own lineage" because every Buddha, without exception, throughout every world-system, is literally born from them; "delightful" because practitioners can abide contentedly absorbed in them from one afternoon clear through to the next morning without ever tiring.

Chinese Source

如世尊言此四聖種是最勝知。是種性知是可樂知是無雜染。不可訶責一切世間。若沙門若婆羅門若天若魔若梵若餘世間。無能如法說其過者。云何名最勝知。有說。此四聖種能引最勝故得最勝果故趣向最勝故隨順最勝故名為最勝。佛所施設佛所知故說名為知。有說。佛說五百聲聞各有最勝之法。此四墮在彼最勝法中故名最勝。

As the World-Honored One states, these four noble lineages are the supreme knowledge, the knowledge of one's own lineage, the delightful knowledge, and the untainted — beyond reproach by any being in the whole world, whether ascetic, brahmin, god, māra, or Brahmā, or any other being whatsoever, none of whom can rightly find any fault in them. Some say: the Buddha states that each of his five hundred śrāvakas possesses his own supreme quality, and, since these four fall within the scope of that supreme quality, they are named "supreme";

佛知此四是真喜足故名為知。有說。佛及弟子名最勝者彼所讚述名最勝知。有說。佛知此四是勝寶藏能令住者意望滿足故名最勝知。

and, since the Buddha knows these four to be true contentment, they are named "knowledge." since most sentient beings die with their aspirations left unsatisfied, whereas only those who abide in the noble lineages die with their aspirations fully satisfied. Some say: "lineage" here carries the sense of sustaining, that is, since these are able to sustain and maintain the noble teaching so that it does not perish for a long time, they are named "lineage",

是佛所了故名為知。云何名可樂知。答此四是智者所樂智者所知故名可樂知。有說。佛知此四皆能資助樂斷樂修故名可樂知。有說。

and, since they are comprehended by the Buddha, they are named "knowledge." What is meant by "the delightful knowledge"? Reading note — Ru-Yi

Contrasting the noble lineages' inexhaustible, theft-proof enjoyment against even a wheel-turning king's own wealth, which "can be speedily exhausted," makes a pointed economic argument out of spiritual contentment: unlike every worldly possession, which requires ongoing accumulation, guarding, and eventual depletion, these four are presented as the one genuinely limitless resource available to any practitioner, regardless of actual material circumstance.

7. A Verse on Subduing Craving and Aversion

A scriptural verse — praising one who has subdued craving and aversion, dwells in a solitary seat, abides in heedfulness, and uproots the latent tendency of craving for existence — is read as a compressed summary of all four noble lineages at once: its opening clause covers contentment with robes and food (defined precisely as not savoring what is obtained and not sorrowing over what is not), its second clause covers contentment with bedding, and its closing two clauses cover delighting in cutting off and cultivating.

"Whoever has subdued craving and aversion, ever dwelling in a solitary seat, constantly abiding in heedfulness, uproots the latent tendency of craving for existence."

Question: what meaning does this verse set forth? Answer: "craving" here refers to craving desire; "aversion" refers to hatred and resentment. Should the Buddha's noble disciple subdue these two,

whenever one's mind turns toward whatever one has obtained with a savoring attachment, this is named "craving"; and whenever one's mind turns toward whatever one has not obtained with sorrowful longing, this is named "aversion." The Buddha's noble disciple does not savor what he has already obtained, nor sorrow over what he has not yet obtained, and is thus able to subdue both alike; and, having subdued both, dwelling in a solitary seat and abiding in heedfulness, he is thereby able to eternally uproot the latent tendency of craving for existence. Furthermore, craving after fine, excellent robes and food is named "craving," while feeling aversion toward coarse, worn-out robes and food is named "aversion"; the Buddha's noble disciple is able to subdue both alike. This first clause displays the noble lineage of contentment with robes and food; the second clause displays the noble lineage of contentment with bedding; and the last two clauses display the noble lineage of delighting in cutting off and delighting in cultivating.

Reading a single four-line verse as a complete, compressed statement of all four noble lineages at once, with each clause mapped onto a specific one, shows this tradition's confidence that its own technical categories were not imposed from outside onto scripture but were already latent within even its most compact devotional poetry, waiting only to be unpacked.

8. Delighting in Cutting Off versus Delighting in Cultivating

The fourth noble lineage is itself a pair, and the treatise distinguishes its two halves through several parallel technical lenses at once: delighting in cutting off targets affliction while delighting in cultivating targets the noble path itself; the uninterrupted path belongs to the former while the path of liberation belongs to the latter; the path of seeing belongs to the former while the path of cultivation belongs to the latter; and, in the sharpest formulation, delighting in cutting off displays the patient acceptances while delighting in cultivating displays the cognitions.

Answer: delighting in cutting off refers to affliction; delighting in cultivating refers to the noble path. Furthermore, the uninterrupted path is named delighting in cutting off, and the path of liberation is named delighting in cultivating. Furthermore, the path of seeing is named delighting in cutting off, and the path of cultivation is named delighting in cultivating; and, just as with the path of seeing and the path of cultivation,

so too should it be understood with the stage of seeing and the stage of cultivation, and with the faculty of "I shall come to know the unknown" and the faculty of "having come to know." 9. Mahānāman and the Gradual Severance of the Five Hindrances

Turning to the Buddha's own teaching to Mahānāman that "a trainee for the most part dwells in the gradual severance of the five hindrances," the treatise narrows "trainee" here to the stream-enterer and once-returner specifically, since those following through mere conviction or through the Dharma are not fixed in this respect, some having already fully severed the hindrances and others still severing them gradually. A close technical puzzle follows: the stream-enterer has, in fact, already severed the hindrance of doubt outright, and the hindrance of remorse no longer manifests even though not yet fully severed — so why does the sūtra still speak of five hindrances being gradually severed rather than three? The answer distinguishes what is spoken of in general ("the hindrances," without fixing a number) from why five is specified at all (because five distinct afflictions are genuinely at issue), then works through four closely related but genuinely distinct verbs — severing, departing from, suppressing, and turning away from — each mapped onto a different stage of the path.

Distinguishing four verbs for essentially the same act of overcoming affliction — sever, depart from, suppress, turn away from — and insisting each maps onto a genuinely distinct moment or mechanism in the path's own structure, rather than treating them as interchangeable synonyms, is this tradition's characteristic refusal to let ordinary language's own looseness stand in for doctrinal precision.

10. Dharma, Vinaya, and the Teacher's Own Instruction

A striking causal loop from scripture — "through a breach in the Dharma comes a breach in the Vinaya, and through a breach in the Vinaya comes a breach in the Dharma" — receives two competing readings, depending on whether "breach" (珊度沙) means mere contentment or outright ruin. Read as contentment, complacency with the path of seeing prevents one from cultivating the path of cultivation, which in turn prevents severing the afflictions that only cultivation can remove — and the same loop runs in reverse. Read as ruin, since the noble path and cessation themselves, being faultless, cannot literally be destroyed, what is actually ruined is only the practitioner's own mental continuum, rendering the path ever more remote. The section closes by distinguishing three terms scripture treats as near-synonyms — the Dharma (the eightfold path), the Vinaya (the extinction of greed, hatred, and delusion), and the Teacher's own instruction (the Buddha's actual words) — mapped, on an alternative reading, onto the three piṭakas themselves.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Insisting that the noble path and nirvāṇa themselves cannot literally be "ruined," and relocating all the damage onto the practitioner's own mental continuum instead, protects a foundational commitment — that liberation and cessation are metaphysically secure, never contingent on any individual's failure — while still making full sense of scripture's own alarming language about a collapsing Dharma.

11. Practicing the Dharma in Accordance with the Dharma

The fascicle closes by unpacking "practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma" (法隨法行) through several layered pairings — nirvāṇa as "the Dharma" and the eightfold path as "that which accords with it," since nirvāṇa alone stands beyond birth, aging, sickness, and death, though a different sūtra praising Śāriputra reverses the pairing, naming the path itself "the Dharma" and nirvāṇa "that which accords with it," since the path is what is actually attained first. The same structure recurs with the prātimokṣa and its own restraint, and with bodily/verbal restraint and purity of livelihood, prompting a final, genuinely delicate puzzle: since the recitation of the precepts (the prātimokṣa) is merely spoken words, sometimes uttered with a wholesome mind and sometimes not, how can it serve as "the very head and forerunner of every wholesome dharma"? The answer locates its power not in the reciter's own state of mind at all, but in its function as an unobstructing cause for whichever listener actually puts it into practice.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Locating the prātimokṣa's own power in its effect on the listener who acts on it, entirely independent of whatever mental state the reciter happened to be in while speaking the words, is a clarifying piece of Abhidharma pragmatism: it treats a ritual recitation's spiritual efficacy as a fact about its downstream causal role in the world, never as something that could be compromised by an imperfect performer's own private state of mind.
