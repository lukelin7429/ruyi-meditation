Fourteen Names for the Unconditioned

Fascicle 32 completes the fourteen names of analytical cessation with a cascade of etymologies — nirvāṇa, non-aggregate, non-manifest, supreme, near, emancipation — before mapping non-analytical cessation in exhaustive detail across every grade of affliction, every path of practice, and every possible transition of rebirth through the nine cosmic stages. The fascicle closes by opening the two nirvāṇa-realms, with residue and without, working through a vivid physiological account of an arhat's final hours and the edge cases the definitions leave stranded. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Fourteen Names for Nirvāṇa: The First Three Etymologies

Fascicle 32 opens by listing fourteen further names for analytical cessation, then working through them one by one. The first three receive a cluster of etymologies apiece: nirvāṇa itself is unpacked through four separate word-plays on “vāna” and “nir” — emergence from destiny, freedom from foulness, departure from the dense thicket of the aggregates, and the absence of any further weaving of karma's own thread — before the treatise turns to why cessation is called “not of the same kind” and, opening the third name, “non-aggregate.”

Chinese Source

如是擇滅亦名涅槃。亦名不同類。亦名非聚。亦名非顯。亦名最勝。亦名通達。亦名阿羅漢。亦名不親近。亦名不修習。亦名可愛樂。亦名近。亦名妙。亦名出離。問何故擇滅亦名涅槃。答槃名為趣涅名為出。永出諸趣故名涅槃。復次槃名為臭涅名為無。永無臭穢諸煩惱業故名涅槃。復次槃名稠林涅名永離。永離一切三火三相諸蘊稠林故名涅槃。復次槃名為織涅名為不。此中永無煩惱業縷。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus analytical cessation is also called nirvāṇa; it is also called not of the same kind; it is also called non-aggregate; it is also called non-manifest; it is also called supreme; it is also called penetration; it is also called arhat; it is also called not to be approached; it is also called not to be cultivated; it is also called what is beloved; it is also called near; it is also called wondrous; it is also called emancipation. One asks: why is analytical cessation also called nirvāṇa? The answer: "vāna" means destiny, and "nir" means emergence — because it permanently emerges from every destiny, it is called nirvāṇa. Further, "vāna" means foulness, and "nir" means absence — because it is permanently free of the foulness of every affliction and karma, it is called nirvāṇa. Further, "vāna" means a dense thicket, and "nir" means permanent departure — because it permanently departs from the three fires, the three characteristics, and the dense thicket of the aggregates, it is called nirvāṇa. Further, "vāna" means weaving, and "nir" means not — because it is permanently without the thread of affliction and karma,

不織生死異熟果絹故名涅槃。餘如前說。問何故擇滅亦名不同類。

weaving no silken cloth of the ripened fruit of birth and death, it is called nirvāṇa. The rest is as stated above. One asks: why is analytical cessation also called "not of the same kind"?

答無同類因故。亦非同類因故。餘如前說。問何故擇滅亦名非聚。

The answer: because it possesses no homogeneous cause, and because it is not itself a homogeneous cause. The rest is as stated above. One asks: why is analytical cessation also called "non-aggregate"?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four-fold etymology of “nirvāṇa” itself is a small masterclass in how this literature treats a single canonical term as a container for multiple, non-competing insights: emergence, absence of foulness, departure from a thicket, and unwoven cloth are four entirely different images, yet the treatise never asks which one is “really” correct — each illuminates a different facet of the same reality, and the accumulation itself is the point.

2. Non-Aggregate Through Non-Cultivation: Ten Etymologies in One Sweep

A single enormous passage works through eight further names in succession: “non-aggregate” because cessation stands apart from every category conditioned dharmas necessarily aggregate into; “non-manifest” through six distinct senses of what “manifest” could mean, from needing no praise to admitting no comparison; “supreme,” “penetration,” and “arhat” each unpacked through wordplay on their own Sanskrit roots; and finally “not to be approached” and “not to be cultivated,” each turning on nirvāṇa's total freedom from the ordinary logic of fruit-bearing greed or continuum-based growth.

Chinese Source

答離諸聚故。謂說有為無住相者。彼說有為法必四類共聚。謂自體及三相。若說有為有住相者。彼說有為法必五類共聚。謂自體及四相。復次諸界諸趣諸生諸蘊諸世諸苦皆名為聚。擇滅異彼故名非聚。問何故擇滅亦名非顯。答顯謂稱讚涅槃功德智者極成。不待稱讚故名非顯。復次涅槃功德無邊際故不可稱讚。如說。此人技術無邊不可稱讚故名非顯。復次顯名毀呰涅槃功德究竟圓滿。如末尼珠周圓光淨不可毀呰故名非顯。復次涅槃功德究竟安住。如末尼珠體無增減。隨所置處即便安住不可毀呰故名非顯。復次顯謂顯說涅槃是聖現量所證不可顯說故名非顯。復次諸有為法有因有果。可得以因顯說其果。亦得以果顯說其因。涅槃無為無因無果不可顯說故名非顯復次顯謂顯示。涅槃寂靜無婆羅門剎帝利等種姓差別可以顯示。亦無青黃赤白等相可以顯示。故名非顯。復次顯謂顯現。諸有為法或體是色其相顯現或雖非色而依色轉亦可顯現。涅槃不然故名非顯。問何故擇滅亦名最勝。答以上妙故。如世上妙衣服飲食莊嚴具等名為最勝。涅槃亦爾。尊者妙音作如是說。擇滅涅槃於諸法中是最勝法。於諸義中是最勝義。於諸事中是最勝事。於諸理中是最勝理。於諸果中是最勝果。故名最勝。問何故擇滅亦名通達。答通達謂慧涅槃是慧果故亦名通達。如九遍知是智果故亦名遍知。亦如六處是業果故說名故業。復次擇滅涅槃是通達果故名通達。如天眼耳是通果故亦名為通。問何故擇滅亦名阿羅漢。答應受供養故。謂阿羅漢總目應義無有世間上妙供具擇滅涅槃不應受者。復次羅漢名應。阿之言不。擇滅涅槃於諸界趣不應流轉故名不應。復次羅漢名賊。亦名為怨。阿之言無。涅槃中無煩惱怨賊。是故擇滅名阿羅漢。問何故擇滅亦名不親近。答離親近本故。謂有親近有為法者以貪其果。如貪蔭涼花葉果等親近於樹。涅槃無果可令彼貪名不親近。問何故說涅槃智者應親近。答智者。謂佛及佛弟子。彼應解了及起得證故名親近。非貪其果故亦名不親近。問何故擇滅亦名不修習。答不在相續故。若在相續數數現前漸漸增進。名可修習。涅槃不爾故亦名不修習。問若爾頌說當云何通。

Ru-Yi Translation

The answer: because it is free of every aggregation — that is, those who hold that conditioned dharmas possess no characteristic of abiding maintain that a conditioned dharma is necessarily an aggregation of four categories together, namely its own substance together with the three characteristics; those who hold that conditioned dharmas do possess a characteristic of abiding maintain that a conditioned dharma is necessarily an aggregation of five categories together, namely its own substance together with the four characteristics. Further, every realm, every destiny, every birth, every aggregate, every era, and every suffering is called an aggregation; analytical cessation differs from all of these, and it is for this reason that it is called "non-aggregate." One asks: why is analytical cessation also called "non-manifest"? The answer: "manifest" means that the virtues of nirvāṇa are praised and acclaimed, fully established among the wise; since it does not depend on being so praised and acclaimed, it is called "non-manifest." Further, the virtues of nirvāṇa are boundless and cannot be exhaustively praised — just as one might say, "this person's skill is boundless and cannot be exhaustively praised" — and it is for this reason that it is called "non-manifest." Further, "manifest" means to disparage — the virtues of nirvāṇa are ultimately and perfectly complete, just as a maṇi jewel, perfectly round, radiant, and pure, cannot be disparaged, and it is for this reason that it is called "non-manifest." Further, the virtues of nirvāṇa abide ultimately and unchangingly — just as a maṇi jewel's own substance neither increases nor decreases, and, wherever it is placed, at once abides securely there, unable to be disparaged, and it is for this reason that it is called "non-manifest." Further, "manifest" means to demonstrate through exposition — nirvāṇa is realized only through the noble ones' own direct perception, and cannot be demonstrated through exposition, and it is for this reason that it is called "non-manifest." Further, every conditioned dharma possesses a cause and a fruit, and one may demonstrate its fruit through its cause, or demonstrate its cause through its fruit; nirvāṇa, being unconditioned, possesses neither cause nor fruit, and cannot be demonstrated in this way, and it is for this reason that it is called "non-manifest." Further, "manifest" means to display — nirvāṇa's own quiescence admits of no distinction of brahmin, kṣatriya, or other lineage that could be displayed, nor any characteristic of blue, yellow, red, or white that could be displayed, and it is for this reason that it is called "non-manifest." Further, "manifest" means to appear visibly — every conditioned dharma either has visible form as its own substance, its characteristic thereby appearing visibly, or, though not itself visible form, operates in dependence on visible form and may thereby likewise appear; nirvāṇa is not so, and it is for this reason that it is called "non-manifest." One asks: why is analytical cessation also called "supreme"? The answer: because it is superior and wondrous — just as the world's own superior and wondrous clothing, food and drink, and ornaments are called "supreme," so too is nirvāṇa. The Venerable Ghoṣa states as follows: analytical cessation, nirvāṇa, is, among all dharmas, the supreme dharma; among all meanings, the supreme meaning; among all matters, the supreme matter; among all principles, the supreme principle; among all fruits, the supreme fruit — and it is for this reason that it is called "supreme." One asks: why is analytical cessation also called "penetration"? The answer: "penetration" refers to wisdom; nirvāṇa is the fruit of wisdom, and it is for this reason that it, too, is called "penetration" — just as the nine kinds of full understanding, being the fruit of knowledge, are likewise called "full understanding," and just as the six sense-fields, being the fruit of karma, are spoken of as "former karma." Further, analytical cessation, nirvāṇa, is the fruit of penetration, and it is for this reason that it is called "penetration" — just as the divine eye and divine ear, being the fruit of supernormal penetration, are likewise called "penetration." One asks: why is analytical cessation also called "arhat"? The answer: because it is worthy of receiving offerings — that is, "arhat" broadly signifies "worthy," and there exists no supreme worldly offering that analytical cessation, nirvāṇa, is not worthy to receive. Further, "arhat" means "worthy," and the "a" signifies negation — analytical cessation, nirvāṇa, does not flow on through any realm or destiny, and it is for this reason that it is called "not-worthy-of-flowing-on." Further, "arhat" also means "thief," and also "enemy"; the "a" signifies "without" — within nirvāṇa there exists no thieving enemy of affliction, and it is for this reason that analytical cessation is called "arhat." One asks: why is analytical cessation also called "not to be approached"? The answer: because it is free of the very root of approach — that is, one who approaches a conditioned dharma does so out of greed for its fruit, just as one approaches a tree out of greed for its shade, its flowers, its leaves, and its fruit; nirvāṇa possesses no fruit that could occasion such greed, and it is for this reason that it is called "not to be approached." One asks: why, then, does it say that the wise ought to approach nirvāṇa? The answer: "the wise" refers to the Buddha and the Buddha's disciples; they ought to understand it fully and give rise to its realization, and it is for this reason that this is called "approach" — it is not out of greed for any fruit, and it is for this reason that it is also called "not to be approached." One asks: why is analytical cessation also called "not to be cultivated"? The answer: because it does not exist within a continuum — if something exists within a continuum, manifesting again and again, gradually growing and advancing, it is called "able to be cultivated"; nirvāṇa is not so, and it is for this reason that it, too, is called "not to be cultivated." One asks: if so, then how is the verse to be reconciled?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The maṇi-jewel simile recurs twice in quick succession here — once for why nirvāṇa cannot be praised (its own perfection makes praise redundant) and once for why it cannot be disparaged (it neither increases nor decreases wherever placed) — and the doubled use is telling: the treatise reaches for the same image from two different angles because ordinary language, built for things that can improve or worsen, simply has no native vocabulary for a reality already and permanently complete.

3. A Verse Reconciled, and Why Nirvāṇa Is “Beloved”

A canonical verse describing nirvāṇa as “dwelling within the mind” threatens to contradict the claim that cessation cannot be cultivated, since dwelling within something usually implies exactly the kind of continuum-based residence the treatise has just denied it — resolved by distinguishing nirvāṇa's attainment, which does depend on mind, from nirvāṇa's own nature, which does not. The treatise then explains why cessation is called “beloved,” tracing the noble ones' own love for it through their dread of suffering, their weariness with birth and death, and their weariness with any breaking of the precepts.

Chinese Source

喬答摩樹下　靜慮不放逸不久履道迹　涅槃在心中答以涅槃得依心起故名在心中。非謂涅槃有修習義。問何故擇滅亦名可愛樂。答聖所愛樂故。謂諸聖者怖畏眾苦。涅槃離苦聖者愛樂。復次聖厭生死。彼無生死故聖愛樂。謂涅槃中永離一切流轉事故。復次聖厭破戒。彼無破戒故聖愛樂。謂涅槃中永離一切破戒事故。由涅槃故佛說無漏戒。名聖所愛戒。此戒能證得離破戒滅故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Beneath the tree of Gautama, in meditation, undistracted, not long treading the path, nirvāṇa dwells within the mind" — the answer: it is because nirvāṇa's own attainment arises in dependence on mind that it is said to "dwell within the mind" — it does not mean that nirvāṇa possesses the character of being cultivated. One asks: why is analytical cessation also called "beloved"? The answer: because the noble ones hold it beloved — that is, the noble ones dread every kind of suffering, and, since nirvāṇa is free of suffering, the noble ones hold it beloved. Further, the noble ones are weary of birth and death, and, since nirvāṇa is free of birth and death, the noble ones hold it beloved — that is, within nirvāṇa, every affair of transmigration is permanently absent. Further, the noble ones are weary of breaking the precepts, and, since nirvāṇa is free of any breaking of the precepts, the noble ones hold it beloved — that is, within nirvāṇa, every affair of precept-breaking is permanently absent. It is on account of nirvāṇa that the Buddha speaks of the uncontaminated precepts, calling them "the precepts the noble ones hold beloved," since these precepts are able to bring about the realization of the cessation of precept-breaking.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Resolving the “dwells within the mind” verse by distinguishing what depends on mind (the attainment) from what does not (nirvāṇa's own nature) is the same razor-thin distinction between substance and acquisition that runs through this entire fascicle's treatment of cessation — a single conceptual tool, reached for again and again, each time a canonical verse's ordinary language threatens to smuggle in more structure than the unconditioned can actually bear.

4. Five Reasons Nirvāṇa Is Called “Near”

The treatise offers a genuinely striking cluster of etymologies for why cessation is called “near”: because the noble ones realize it directly before them, because it plays no favorites among who may realize it — kṣatriya or brahmin alike — nor where, in a city or in forest solitude alike, because resolute conviction makes it as vivid as something standing before one's own eyes, and because, in the Prakaraṇapāda's own technical sense, the present moment itself is what “near” properly means.

Chinese Source

由此亦名時愛解脫待時愛樂得涅槃故問何故擇滅亦名為近答得聖道者現證得故如契經說。精進成就十五法者。名履學迹及近涅槃。復次不選相續而證得故。擇滅名近。謂剎帝利婆羅門等能修道者皆證涅槃。復次不選處所而證得故擇滅名近。謂在城邑或阿練若修習聖道皆得涅槃。復次由勝解故擇滅名近。謂諸聖者滅忍滅智現在前時。由勝解力如對目前明了觀故。復次如近事故擇滅名近。謂隨在處皆可證得。如品類足說。云何遠法。謂過去未來法。云何近法。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is for this reason that they are also called "the timed liberation that awaits its time, beloved and longed for, through which nirvāṇa is attained." One asks: why is analytical cessation also called "near"? The answer: because one who attains the noble path directly realizes it before him — as the sūtra states, one who accomplishes the fifteen dharmas of diligence is called one who treads the path of the learner and draws near to nirvāṇa. Further, it is because it is realized without regard to any particular continuum that analytical cessation is called "near" — that is, kṣatriyas, brahmins, and the rest, whoever is able to cultivate the path, all alike realize nirvāṇa. Further, it is because it is realized without regard to any particular place that analytical cessation is called "near" — that is, whether in a city or town, or in the forest wilderness, cultivating the noble path, one alike attains nirvāṇa. Further, it is on account of resolute conviction that analytical cessation is called "near" — that is, when the noble ones' own forbearance and knowledge with respect to cessation manifest before them, through the power of resolute conviction, they observe it clearly, as though it stood directly before their eyes. Further, it is because it is like a matter close at hand that analytical cessation is called "near" — that is, it may be realized wherever one happens to be, exactly as the Prakaraṇapāda states: what is meant by "distant dharmas"? It refers to past and future dharmas. What is meant by "near dharmas"?

謂現在法及諸無為。復次依近得故擇滅名近。謂現在世說名為近。

It refers to present dharmas and every unconditioned dharma. Further, it is on account of being obtained through a nearby attainment that analytical cessation is called "near" — that is, the present time is spoken of as "near," and it is in dependence on this present time that the acquisition of freedom from bondage arises, through which analytical cessation is realized, and it is for this reason that it is called "near."

依現在世起離繫得證得擇滅故名為近。復次捨近入故擇滅名近。謂現在世說名近法。捨此近法而入涅槃。是故涅槃亦名為近。脇尊者曰。勤修道者直趣涅槃。故名為近。復次聖道所依各有差別。涅槃無定故名為近。能修道者皆證得故。問聖道亦妙。如品類足說。云何妙法。謂學無學法及擇滅無為。何故涅槃獨名為妙。答以涅槃是妙中妙故復次聖道雖妙而雜無常。涅槃不爾故獨名妙。復次聖道雖妙有能對治。厭患善法謂空空等。涅槃不爾故獨名妙。問聖道亦是出離之法。如品類足說。

Further, it is on account of relinquishing what is near in order to enter it that analytical cessation is called "near" — that is, the present time is spoken of as "the near dharma"; one relinquishes this near dharma and enters nirvāṇa, and it is for this reason that nirvāṇa, too, is called "near." The Venerable Pārśva says: one who diligently cultivates the path proceeds directly toward nirvāṇa, and it is for this reason that it is called "near." Further, the supports of the noble path each differ from one another, while nirvāṇa possesses no such fixed determination, and it is for this reason that it is called "near," since whoever is able to cultivate the path may alike realize it. One asks: the noble path, too, is wondrous, as the Prakaraṇapāda states: what is meant by "wondrous dharmas"? It refers to the dharmas of a learner and of one beyond training, together with the unconditioned analytical cessation — why, then, is nirvāṇa alone called "wondrous"? The answer: it is because nirvāṇa is the wondrous among the wondrous. Further, although the noble path is wondrous, it is nonetheless mixed with impermanence; nirvāṇa is not so, and it is for this reason that it alone is called "wondrous." Further, although the noble path is wondrous, it possesses an antidote capable of opposing it — namely, the wholesome dharma that grows weary of it, such as the concentration on emptiness applied even to emptiness itself, and the like; nirvāṇa is not so, and it is for this reason that it alone is called "wondrous." Reading note — Ru-Yi

That nirvāṇa is called “near” precisely because it discriminates against no one — no particular caste, no particular location, no particular kind of practitioner — reframes the entire preceding cluster of remote-sounding, negatively defined names (non-manifest, not to be approached, not to be cultivated) with a startlingly intimate counterpoint: for all its unconditioned remoteness from ordinary categories, nirvāṇa turns out to be the single most accessible reality there is, available to anyone, anywhere, who cultivates the path.

5. Why Nirvāṇa Alone Is Called “Emancipation”

The list of fourteen names closes with “emancipation,” distinguished sharply from the noble path itself: the path, however wondrous, is still something one must eventually be emancipated from at the moment of final nirvāṇa, while nirvāṇa itself requires no further emancipation, being the one dharma that admits of neither of the two ordinary kinds of relinquishment — cutting-off and casting-aside — that govern every other dharma, contaminated or uncontaminated alike.

Chinese Source

云何出離法。謂欲界善戒。及色無色界離生善定。并學無學擇滅無為。何故涅槃獨名出離。答涅槃是出離非有出離故。聖道是出離亦是有出離。般涅槃時出離彼故。復次涅槃是真出離法故。謂有漏法有二種捨。一斷捨。二棄捨。無漏有為雖無斷捨而有棄捨。唯有擇滅二捨俱無。名真出離。復次涅槃是究竟出離功德故。如契經說。

Ru-Yi Translation

what is meant by "dharmas of emancipation"? It refers to the wholesome precepts of the desire realm, together with the wholesome concentrations of the form and formless realms that emerge from birth, together with the unconditioned analytical cessation belonging to a learner and to one beyond training. Why, then, is nirvāṇa alone called "emancipation"? The answer: nirvāṇa is itself emancipation, yet possesses no further emancipation of its own; the noble path is itself emancipation, yet also possesses a further emancipation — since, at the time of complete nirvāṇa, one becomes emancipated from the path itself as well. Further, it is because nirvāṇa is the true dharma of emancipation that it is so called — that is, a contaminated dharma admits of two kinds of relinquishment: first, relinquishment through cutting-off; second, relinquishment through casting-aside. An uncontaminated conditioned dharma, though it admits of no relinquishment through cutting-off, does admit of relinquishment through casting-aside; analytical cessation alone admits of neither of these two kinds of relinquishment, and it is for this reason that it alone is called "true emancipation." Further, it is because nirvāṇa is the ultimate virtue of emancipation that it is so called, exactly as the sūtra states:

以色出離欲以無色出離色。以聖道出離無色。以涅槃出離一切有為法。

it is through visible form that one is emancipated from desire; it is through the formless that one is emancipated from visible form; it is through the noble path that one is emancipated from the formless; and it is through nirvāṇa that one is emancipated from every conditioned dharma whatsoever.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The observation that even the noble path itself must eventually be relinquished at the moment of final nirvāṇa is a quietly radical piece of soteriology: it means the path is not simply left behind as obsolete once its work is done but is positively “emancipated from,” placing the path itself, for all its wondrous uncontaminated status, still one rung below the single reality that requires no further departure from anything at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The step-by-step physiological cascade — wind disturbing fire, fire failing digestion, failed digestion killing appetite, diminished eating shrinking the great elements, shrinking elements weakening the faculties, weakened faculties finally starving mind of its support — reads almost like a piece of ancient clinical observation embedded within abstract Abhidharma argument, grounding the most metaphysically loaded event in the entire tradition, an arhat's final passing, in the plainest possible account of a body simply ceasing to sustain itself.

19. Edge Cases, and Where Ordinary Beings, Learners, and Arhats Each Stand

The fascicle closes by working through genuine edge cases the definitions seem to leave stranded — a formless-realm arhat, having no material body at all, technically dwells in neither nirvāṇa-realm, as does one in the attainment of cessation, or one born with incomplete faculties — before the treatise proposes and then declines a simpler alternative definition, on the grounds that the more complex one, though harder to state, better serves the disciples it was written to instruct. A final tetralemma locates exactly which of cutting-off, full understanding, the fruit of a renunciant, and the two nirvāṇa-realms apply to the freedom attained by an ordinary being, a learner, and one beyond training respectively.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The treatise's explicit acknowledgment that a simpler, more elegant definition was available and consciously rejected — not because it was technically wrong, but because the author judged the more demanding one more genuinely useful for the disciples who would have to work with it — is a rare moment of pedagogical self-awareness breaking through the usual register of pure doctrinal argument, and closes the fascicle's long meditation on cessation with a reminder that even this most rarefied of topics was, in the end, composed for someone's actual benefit.
