1. What Is the Dharma-Wheel?

Fascicle 182 opens the Non-Returner Nyāsa's next stretch (不還納息第四之九) by asking what the sūtra means in declaring that the Buddha turns the Dharma-wheel, and that no other being in the cosmos can turn it likewise. The treatise identifies its intrinsic nature as the eightfold noble path itself, then works through several converging etymologies for "wheel": it is accomplished by the Dharma the way a golden wheel is accomplished by gold; it discerns and awakens to the nature of every dharma; it purifies the noble wisdom-eye; and it counteracts its opposite number, the "non-Dharma-wheel" of eight wrong path-factors taught by rival schools.

Chinese Source

云何法輪乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為欲分別契經義故。如契經說世尊轉法輪諸餘世間沙門婆羅門天魔梵等皆無有能如法轉者。契經雖作是說而不分別。云何法輪齊何當言轉法輪。契經是此論所依根本。彼所不說者今應說之故作斯論。云何法輪。答八支聖道。若兼相應隨轉則五蘊性。此是法輪自性。是我是物是相是性是本性。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the Dharma-wheel? — and so on at length. Question: why is this treatise composed? Answer: in order to elucidate the meaning of a sūtra passage — for a sūtra states that the World-Honored One turns the Dharma-wheel, and that no other being in the world, whether ascetic, brahmin, god, māra, or Brahmā, is able to turn it in accordance with the Dharma. Although the sūtra speaks in this way, it does not explain the matter — what is the Dharma-wheel, and at what point should one say that the Dharma-wheel has been turned? Since that sūtra serves as the very foundation this treatise relies upon, what it leaves unexplained ought now to be explained, and it is for this reason that this treatise is composed. What is the Dharma-wheel? Answer: the eightfold noble path. When taken together with its conjoined and concomitant factors, it is of the nature of the five aggregates. This is the intrinsic nature of the Dharma-wheel — that is, its own substance, its own characteristic, its own nature, and its own inherent nature.

Its intrinsic nature has now been explained; what is to be explained next follows. Question: why is it named the Dharma-wheel? Answer: this wheel is accomplished by the Dharma, and the Dharma constitutes its intrinsic nature, and it is for this reason that it is named the Dharma-wheel — just as, in the world, a wheel made of gold or the like, gold or the like constituting its nature, is named a golden wheel or the like; it is the same in this case. this wheel is able to discern, and discern thoroughly, the nature of every dharma, and is able to awaken, and awaken thoroughly, to it, realizing and directly witnessing it, and it is for this reason that it is named the Dharma-wheel. Some say: this wheel is able to purify the noble wisdom-eye of the Dharma, and it is for this reason that it is named the Dharma-wheel. Some say: this wheel is able to counteract the non-Dharma-wheel, and it is for this reason that it is named the Dharma-wheel — the non-Dharma-wheel referring to the wheel of the eight wrong path-factors turned by the six teachers, such as Pūraṇa and the rest. Question: why is it named a "wheel"? What is the meaning of "wheel"? Answer: the meaning of turning without remaining still is the meaning of "wheel"; the meaning of leaving one place to proceed toward another is the meaning of "wheel"; the meaning of being able to subdue one's enemies is the meaning of "wheel" — it is on account of such meanings as these that it is named "wheel." As the Sūtra of the Forty Great Dharma-Gateways states: there are twenty wholesome categories and twenty unwholesome categories, and this is named the Brahmā-wheel, and so on at length. Question: the twenty wholesome categories may well be so, but how can the twenty unwholesome categories be named the Brahmā-wheel? Answer: it is not the Buddha's own intention to name those the Brahmā-wheel; rather, he speaks only of the turning of patient acceptance and cognition with respect to both the wholesome and unwholesome categories of dharmas as being named the Brahmā-wheel.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Naming a rival teaching's own eightfold scheme the "non-Dharma-wheel," precisely mirroring the Buddha's own eightfold path point for point, reveals this tradition thinking in deliberately symmetrical opposition to its competitors: the wrongness of a rival path is defined not as some unrelated error but as the exact photographic negative of the true path, sharing its very structure while inverting its content.

2. The Brahmā-Wheel

A parallel term from another sūtra — the "Brahmā-wheel" — receives its own etymology: "Brahmā" itself names utter tranquility, freedom from calamity, and harmlessness, while the compound "Brahmā-wheel" is explained through a cascade of reasons converging on the Brahmā world's own special status — it is both the very first ground attainable and complete with the noble path, unlike the second and third dhyānas (not the first attainable) or even the fourth dhyāna, the Buddha's own first attainment (not complete with the path). Further explanations run through what exactly the Brahmā-wheel counteracts (a long catalogue of "non-Brahmā" afflictions across all three realms) and what unique fruits only the Brahmā world possesses in full — the four ascetic fruits, the nine kinds of full understanding, and all eighty-nine varieties of the ascetic fruit.

Insisting that the fourth dhyāna — literally the very first ground the Buddha's own body attained enlightenment within — still fails to qualify as the "Brahmā-wheel" because it lacks the noble path's own completeness shows this tradition unwilling to let biographical proximity to the Buddha substitute for the actual technical criterion; even the ground of the Buddha's own awakening is held to the same impersonal standard as everything else.

3. Why the Path of Seeing Alone Is the Dharma-Wheel

Since "wheel" carries the meaning of turning without remaining still, the treatise asks why the path of seeing specifically — and not the slower path of cultivation — alone earns the name Dharma-wheel, working through no fewer than seven images at once: leaving one truth's realization to proceed toward the next; the fourfold structure of leaving, proceeding, subduing, and guarding; the cyclical alternation of patient acceptance and cognition, like a turning cart-wheel; the alternating ascent and descent between the desire realm and the peak of existence; and, most elaborately, a full mapping of spoke, hub, and rim onto the sixteen patient acceptances and cognitions of the four truths, or onto the eightfold path's own eight factors, with several rival correspondences proposed. The section closes by comparing the path of seeing's subduing of every affliction bound to the four truths to a wheel-turning king's own wheel-treasure subduing every enemy across the four continents.

as explained before, the meaning of leaving one place to proceed toward another is the meaning of "wheel"; within the path of seeing, one leaves the direct realization of suffering to proceed toward the direct realization of origin, and so on up to leaving the direct realization of cessation to proceed toward the direct realization of the path, and it is for this reason that the path of seeing alone is named the Dharma-wheel. Some say: it is on account of four matters that it is named "wheel": first, leaving this; second, proceeding toward that; third, subduing what has not yet been subdued; and fourth, guarding what has already been subdued. Within the path of seeing this is likewise so: "leaving this" refers to leaving the direct realization of suffering; "proceeding toward that" refers to proceeding toward the direct realization of origin; "subduing what has not yet been subdued" refers precisely to the direct realization of origin; and "guarding what has already been subdued" refers precisely to the direct realization of suffering — and so on likewise through cessation and the path — and it is for this reason that it is named the Dharma-wheel. Some say: the meaning of revolving is the meaning of "wheel," just as a cart-wheel turns round and round; in just this way, the path of seeing's own patient acceptances and cognitions cycle round — that is, after a patient acceptance a cognition manifests before the mind, and after that cognition a patient acceptance again manifests before it — and the cycling of the dharma-class and the subsequent-class categories is likewise so, and it is for this reason that it is named the Dharma-wheel. Some say: the meaning of ascending and descending is the meaning of "wheel," just as a cart-wheel turns now upward, now downward; in just this way, the path of seeing takes its object now ascending, now descending — that is, having taken the desire realm as its object, it then takes the peak of existence as its object, and, having taken the peak of existence as its object, it again takes the desire realm as its object — and the same is said of its counteraction, ascending and descending, and it is for this reason that it is named the Dharma-wheel. Reading note — Ru-Yi

Proposing five or six entirely different ways to map spoke, hub, and rim onto the path's own components, without ever settling which mapping is correct, treats the wheel-image itself as a generative template rather than a fixed diagram: the treatise seems less interested in choosing the one true correspondence than in demonstrating how richly the same physical object can be made to illuminate the path's own inner architecture from multiple angles at once.

4. Two Kinds of Turning: Within Oneself, Within Another

Against the Mahāsāṃghika school's claim that the Dharma-wheel is constituted by the Buddha's own words, the treatise insists it is the noble path itself — for otherwise the wheel would already have been turned beneath the Bodhi tree, or even when the Buddha first spoke to passing merchants, rather than only later at Vārāṇasī. The resolution distinguishes turning within one's own mental continuum (which happened beneath the Bodhi tree) from turning within another's (which happened only at Vārāṇasī, when the noble path first arose in Kauṇḍinya), with a further cascade of reasons why this second turning alone counts as the "first": because benefiting others is the Buddha's true task, because the two vehicles do not share the Buddha's own ability to turn it within others, because someone must be present to serve as living witness to the transmission, and, poignantly, because this second turning represents the actual fruit of three incalculable eons of the Buddha's own accumulated austerities.

為止摩訶僧祇部說法輪語為自性。彼作是說一切佛語皆是法輪。若謂聖道是法輪者則菩提樹下已轉法輪。何故至婆羅痆斯方名轉法輪耶。為止彼意顯法輪體但是聖道非佛語性。若是佛語者則應菩提樹邊為商人說法已名轉法輪。何故後至婆羅痆斯國乃言轉法輪耶。故知爾時令他身中有聖道起方名轉法輪。法輪聖道為體故。說齊阿若多憍陳那見法名佛轉法輪。問若爾佛於菩提樹下已名轉法輪。何故於婆羅痆斯國乃言初轉法輪耶。答轉法輪有二種。一自相續中轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

it is in order to refute the Mahāsāṃghika school's claim that the very words expressing the Dharma constitute the intrinsic nature of the Dharma-wheel, which holds that every word of the Buddha is itself the Dharma-wheel — for, were the noble path itself said to be the Dharma-wheel, then the Dharma-wheel would already have been turned beneath the Bodhi tree; why, then, is it only upon reaching Vārāṇasī that it is named the turning of the Dharma-wheel? It is in order to refute this position that the treatise displays that the substance of the Dharma-wheel is precisely the noble path, and not the nature of the Buddha's own words — for, were it the Buddha's own words, then the Dharma-wheel ought already to have been turned when he taught the Dharma to the merchants beside the Bodhi tree; why, then, is it only afterward, upon reaching the country of Vārāṇasī, that the turning of the Dharma-wheel is spoken of? It should therefore be understood that it is only at the very moment the noble path arises within another's own body that the turning of the Dharma-wheel is named, since the noble path constitutes the very substance of the Dharma-wheel — and it is for this reason that the turning of the Dharma-wheel by the Buddha is said to occur precisely at the moment Ājñāta-Kauṇḍinya sees the Dharma. Question: if so, then the Buddha ought already to have been named as having turned the Dharma-wheel beneath the Bodhi tree — why, then, is it in the country of Vārāṇasī that the first turning of the Dharma-wheel is spoken of? Answer: the turning of the Dharma-wheel is of two kinds: first, turning within one's own mental continuum,

5. The Causes and Conditions of Right View, and When the Turning Is Complete

The treatise names the two causes and conditions from which right view is born — hearing another's teaching of the Dharma, and proper mental application — mapping the former onto the Buddha's own first turning and the latter onto a disciple's own subsequent turning, then expanding this into a fuller fourfold formula: drawing near to a good friend, hearing the true Dharma, applying the mind properly, and practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma. A precise technical question follows: since Kauṇḍinya already entered the path of seeing at the very first patient acceptance regarding suffering, why is the Buddha's turning of the wheel only named complete at the much later moment of the subsequent cognition of the path? The answer specifies three, then five, converging causal conditions that only become complete at that final moment — relinquishing the path of seeing, attaining the path of cultivation, and the uniform realization that severs the fetters all at once.

6. Kauṇḍinya Sees the Dharma, and the Cognition Behind the Three Questions

Turning to the sūtra's own account of the first sermon, the treatise explains why Kauṇḍinya alone, among the five ascetics, is named as seeing the Dharma at that moment — he alone had already entered the path of seeing, earning the name Ājñāta, "he who has understood." It then offers a genuinely moving explanation for why the Buddha questioned him three times: having examined, through the cognition of the prior and posterior limits, the vast span of suffering Kauṇḍinya would otherwise have undergone in hell across countless mutual entanglements with every sentient being throughout beginningless time, the Buddha reflects that liberating this one person alone already fully repays three incalculable eons of his own accumulated austerities — and it is out of this overwhelming, joyful satisfaction that he asks, three times over, whether Kauṇḍinya has truly understood.

7. The Bodhisattva's Awakening and the Death of His Two Former Teachers

The narrative turns to the bodhisattva's own decisive night: taking sixteen portions of milk-rice, seating himself beneath the Bodhi tree with the vow never to rise until awakening, crushing Māra's own thirty-six koṭi of demonic armies, and attaining unsurpassed awakening in thirty-four mind-moments. Surveying the world for the first fit recipient of his teaching, he learns in succession that both Rudraka Rāmaputra's son and Ārāḍa Kālāma — his own two former meditation teachers — have already died, missing the Dharma by mere days. The treatise offers several careful explanations for why the newly awakened Buddha did not simply rush to teach them immediately, converging on the plainest: their own wholesome roots, though close, were genuinely not yet ripe, and the Buddha, while able to prolong his own life to await a ready disciple, has no power to prolong another's on their behalf.

"just as you, today, though I am truly innocent, have severed my own body into seven pieces, making seven wounds — so, in a future life, when I attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, I shall, with a mind of great compassion, and without waiting even for your own request, be the very first to cause you to cultivate seven kinds of path and sever seven latent tendencies." It should be understood that the sage Kṣāntivādin at that time is none other than the present World-Honored One, Śākyamuni, and that King Kalirāja is none other than the present Venerable Kauṇḍinya. It was for this reason that, once Kauṇḍinya had seen the noble truths, the Buddha, by his own supernormal power, removed the dark obstruction from his mind, causing him to recollect the events of that past life; and he then saw for himself that he had been King Kalirāja, and the Buddha the sage, and that he himself, with a sharp sword, had severed the Buddha's own body into seven parts, making seven wounds — yet the Buddha, far from harboring any resentment, had instead made a vow to benefit him. It was for this reason that the Buddha, the World-Honored One, questioned him three times whether he understood — meaning by this: "have I in any way gone back on my former vow? Has my original vow not now been entirely fulfilled?" Kauṇḍinya, overcome with the deepest shame, joined his palms in reverence and likewise replied three times, "I have understood" — meaning by this: "I now truly know that the World-Honored One has not gone back on his former vow, and that his original vow has now been entirely fulfilled; I, in my former folly, committed so extreme an evil — I beg only for your compassionate forgiveness of my grave offense." It is therefore in order to fulfill his own former vow that the Buddha questioned him three times. 10. Why the Dharma-Wheel Is Turned Only Among Humans

Since eighty thousand devas saw the Dharma alongside Kauṇḍinya at the very first sermon, why is the turning of the Dharma-wheel credited only to the five human ascetics? The treatise runs through a full cascade of proposed reasons — humans saw the Dharma first; humans are the Buddha's own directly, visibly present audience where gods are not; the Dharma can be said to have "perished" wherever it is absent among humans even if devas still realize the deathless; only among humans does the Buddha's own fourfold assembly of disciples exist; and, in the sharpest formulation, only among humans are there beings capable of turning the wheel themselves, and not merely being turned by it, whereas the gods can only ever be its recipients.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Distinguishing sharply between being merely turned by the Dharma-wheel and being capable of turning it oneself, and locating only the latter capacity among humans, quietly makes an argument about human dignity within this cosmology: however much loftier the heavens may be in pleasure or power, this tradition reserves the capacity for genuine spiritual agency — becoming, oneself, a transmitter of liberation rather than merely its beneficiary — for the human condition alone.
