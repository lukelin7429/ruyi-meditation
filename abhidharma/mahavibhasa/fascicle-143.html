Why Only the Buddha Is Called Buddha

Fascicle 143 completes the three uncontaminated faculties and the story of Śāriputra's question about the five hundred bhikṣus, gives an extended account of why only the Buddha uniquely bears that title, and systematically surveys why perception, the afflictions, volition, and even nirvāṇa itself never qualify as faculties. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Faculty of What Is Already Known — and Why Only the Trainee Repeats the Realization

Fascicle 143 continues defining the three uncontaminated faculties, turning first to the faculty of what is already known — the wisdom of any of the three intermediate noble types, resolved-through-faith, attained-through-vision, or a bodily witness, directly realizing the four truths a second time. A pointed question asks why only the trainee is described this way, when the one beyond training likewise realizes the truths anew at every successive refinement of their own faculties — answered by five converging explanations, each holding that citing the trainee's own case already implicitly covers the one beyond training as well, exactly as citing a beginning already implies its own eventual completion.

Chinese Source

根蘊第六中根納息第一之二問已知根云何。答已見諦者已現觀者。諸學慧慧根。及所有根。信解見至身證。於四聖諦已現觀重現觀。是謂已知根。此中於四聖諦已見故。名已見諦者。已現觀故名已現觀者。諸學慧慧根者。此說慧根及所有根。信解見至身證。於四聖諦已現觀。重現觀者。說餘八根。總此九根名已知根。問諸無學者於四聖諦亦重現觀。如從退法轉至思法。乃至從堪達法轉至不動。何故但說學重現觀非無學耶。答亦應說無學。而不說者。當知有餘。有說。此中舉初顯後。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth Aggregate, the Faculties: the Faculty Nyāsa, Chapter One, Part Two. Question: what is the faculty of what is already known? Answer: one who has already seen the truths, has already directly realized them — this refers to the wisdom and wisdom-faculty belonging to any trainee, together with whatever other faculties accompany it; one who is resolved-through-faith, attained-through-vision, or a bodily witness, with respect to the four noble truths already directly realized, directly realizing them once more — this is called the faculty of what is already known. Here, "having already seen the four noble truths" is why one is called "having already seen the truths"; "having already directly realized them" is why one is called "having already directly realized"; "the wisdom and wisdom-faculty belonging to any trainee" refers to the wisdom-faculty itself, together with whatever other faculties accompany it; "with respect to the four noble truths already directly realized, directly realizing them once more" refers to the remaining eight faculties — altogether, these nine faculties together are called the faculty of what is already known. Question: those beyond training, too, directly realize the four noble truths once more, as when one turns from the retrogressible type to the reflective type, and so on, up through turning from the aspiring type to the unshakable type — why, then, does the passage speak only of the trainee's own repeated direct realization, and not that of the one beyond training? Answer: it ought properly to have mentioned the one beyond training as well, and its failure to do so should be understood as a matter left with some further, supplementary account. Others say: this passage cites the first case in order to reveal the latter —

若說學重現觀。當知已說無學亦重現觀。如舉初顯後。舉始入顯已度。舉加行顯究竟。當知亦爾。有說。若斷未曾斷煩惱。得未曾得沙門果者。說重現觀。無學爾時非斷未曾斷煩惱。亦非得未曾得沙門果。是故不說。有說。若斷未曾斷繫得。得未曾得離繫得者。說重現觀。無學爾時雖得未曾得離繫得而不斷未曾斷繫得。是故不說。如斷繫得。得離繫得。除過患修功德。捨下劣證上妙。去無義取有義。盡渴愛苦受無熱樂。應知亦爾。有說。捨未曾捨無智。得未曾得智者。說重現觀。無學爾時雖得未曾得智。而非捨未曾捨無智。是故不說。應知此依染無智說。由如是等種種因緣。學說重現觀非無學。

that is, in speaking of the trainee's own repeated direct realization, it should be understood that the one beyond training's own repeated direct realization has already been implicitly stated as well — exactly as citing the beginning reveals the completion, or citing the preparatory stage reveals the final result; it should be understood accordingly here too. Others say: this speaks of repeated direct realization specifically where one cuts off an affliction never before cut off, or attains a fruit of the ascetic life never before attained; the one beyond training, at that point, is neither cutting off an affliction never before cut off, nor attaining a fruit never before attained, and hence is not mentioned. Others say: this speaks of repeated direct realization specifically where one attains a freedom-from-bondage never before attained, having cut off a bondage never before cut off; the one beyond training, at that point, though attaining a freedom-from-bondage never before attained, is not cutting off any bondage never before cut off, and hence is not mentioned — and, just as with cutting off bondage and attaining freedom from it, so too should this be understood for removing faults and cultivating virtues, abandoning the inferior for the superior and excellent, departing from the meaningless for the meaningful, and exhausting the suffering of thirsting craving for the coolness of ease free from heat. Others say: this speaks of repeated direct realization specifically where one abandons an ignorance never before abandoned, having attained a knowledge never before attained; the one beyond training, at that point, though attaining a knowledge never before attained, is not abandoning any ignorance never before abandoned, and hence is not mentioned — it should be understood that this speaks specifically of defiled ignorance. It is through causes and conditions such as these, of many kinds, that the trainee is spoken of as repeatedly directly realizing, and not the one beyond training.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The recurring principle that citing one case already implies its own counterpart — the beginning implying the end, the preparatory stage implying the final result — is a small but genuinely useful piece of interpretive charity: rather than treating every silence in a text as an oversight demanding correction, this tradition first asks whether the missing case was simply left to be inferred by the reader already trusted to follow the pattern.

2. The Faculty of Complete Knowledge — Reserved for the One Beyond Training

The faculty of complete knowledge receives the same treatment, defined through the wisdom of an outflow-exhausted arhat capable of present, visible ease — with the identical question now inverted: since trainees, too, experience a kind of ease, why is only the one beyond training named? The answer turns on a qualitative rather than merely definitional distinction: the trainee's own ease remains troubled by the lingering heat of affliction, un-sovereign and unexpansive, like a king who still has enemies left to conquer, while only the one beyond training experiences an ease genuinely stilled, self-sovereign, and confined to the present alone, with no further future-life ease still owed.

Chinese Source

問具知根云何。答漏盡阿羅漢。諸無學慧慧根。及所有根。慧解脫俱解脫能得現法樂住。此中漏盡阿羅漢諸無學慧慧根者。此說慧根及所有根。慧解脫俱解脫能得現法樂住者。說餘八根。總此九根名具知根。問學者亦有現法樂住。何故但說無學耶。答亦應說學。而不說者。當知有餘。有說。此中舉終顯始。若說無學得現法樂住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: what is the faculty of complete knowledge? Answer: an arhat whose outflows are exhausted, the wisdom and wisdom-faculty belonging to any one beyond training, together with whatever other faculties accompany it; one liberated through wisdom or liberated in both respects, capable of attaining present, visible ease — this is what is meant. Here, "an arhat whose outflows are exhausted, the wisdom and wisdom-faculty belonging to any one beyond training" refers to the wisdom-faculty itself, together with whatever other faculties accompany it; "one liberated through wisdom or liberated in both respects, capable of attaining present, visible ease" refers to the remaining eight faculties — altogether, these nine faculties together are called the faculty of complete knowledge. Question: the trainee, too, attains present, visible ease — why, then, does the passage speak only of the one beyond training? Answer: it ought properly to have mentioned the trainee as well, and its failure to do so should be understood as a matter left with some further, supplementary account. Others say: this passage cites the final case in order to reveal the initial one — that is, in speaking of the one beyond training's own attainment of present, visible ease, it should be understood that the trainee's own attainment of present, visible ease has already been implicitly stated as well —

exactly as citing the completion reveals the beginning, or citing the final result reveals the preparatory stage; it should be understood accordingly here too. Further, this passage speaks of what is superior in name and meaning — for, speaking in terms of dharma, the dharma belonging to the one beyond training is superior; speaking in terms of the individual, the individual beyond training is superior. Others say: this passage speaks of one who has attained a tranquil ease untroubled by the heat of affliction — the trainee, though attaining a tranquil ease, is nonetheless still troubled by the heat of affliction, and hence is not mentioned. Further, this passage speaks of one who experiences a tranquil ease that is self-sovereign and vast — the trainee, having still further tasks yet to accomplish, though experiencing a tranquil ease, is neither self-sovereign in it nor experiences it as vast, exactly as a king who has not yet eliminated every hostile enemy, though experiencing royal pleasure, is neither self-sovereign in it nor experiences it as vast — it is the very same here, and hence the trainee is not mentioned. Others say: this passage speaks of one whose own afflictive intention has already been stilled, one who has already fully realized the mark of a sage — the trainee is not so, and hence is not mentioned. Others say: this passage speaks of one who possesses a present-life abiding-in-ease, without any further abiding-in-ease belonging to a future life — the trainee possesses an abiding-in-ease belonging to both this life and the next, and hence is not mentioned. It is through causes and conditions such as these, of many kinds, that abiding-in-ease is spoken of for the one beyond training, and not for the trainee.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image of a king who has not yet defeated every enemy, and hence cannot fully enjoy his own royal pleasure however genuine it is, elegantly distinguishes two senses of "having" a good thing that ordinary language often conflates: possessing ease and possessing it with the kind of untroubled sovereignty that lets it actually be enjoyed in full turn out to be two entirely separate achievements.

3. Śāriputra's Question: Did He Already Know the Answer?

A beloved narrative interlude opens: Śāriputra publicly asks the Buddha how many of five hundred newly-liberated bhikṣus possess the three knowledges, how many are liberated in both respects, and how many through wisdom alone — a question the treatise immediately complicates, since Śāriputra, foremost in wisdom among the Buddha's disciples, surely already knew the answer. A cascade of at least a dozen explanations follows, ranging from benefiting others too timid to ask themselves, to revealing hidden distinctions of practice beneath an apparent uniformity of attainment, to reassuring anxious donors that their offerings had truly borne fruit, to silencing heretical slander that the Buddha secretly coaches his own disciples by night.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: is there such a thing as an arhat possessing the three knowledges? If there is, why does this present passage not mention it? And if there is not, how is the sūtra's own freely-cited statement to be reconciled — as it is said: the Venerable Śāriputra, reverently pressing his palms together, said to the Buddha, "World-Honored One, of these five hundred bhikṣus, how many possess the three knowledges, how many are liberated in both respects, and how many are liberated through wisdom alone?" On this point, one further question arises within the discussion itself: did the Venerable Śāriputra himself already know the answer to this? If he knew, why did he ask? And if he did not know, how could he be called a disciple who has reached the far shore of perfection? It should be said that the Venerable One did indeed know — but, if so, why did he ask? There are indeed cases of asking despite already knowing, as the Vinaya itself says: at that time, the World-Honored One, though already knowing, still deliberately asked — and hence one ought not to refuse his question on the grounds that he already knew the answer. Others say: the Venerable One wished to benefit others — that is, though Śāriputra himself already understood this matter clearly, he knew that others within the assembly did not, yet, lacking the fearless confidence to do so themselves, could not ask the Buddha directly; Śāriputra, having no such hesitation, asked on their behalf, in order to benefit them. Others say: the Venerable One wished to reveal a distinction hidden within an apparent uniformity — that is, the Buddha had taught the Dharma to these five hundred bhikṣus exactly as fitting, and, upon hearing it, every one of them had attained the fruit of arhatship, permanently cutting off any further existence, each one genuinely fulfilling the Buddha's own heart's intention —

The Venerable Śāriputra reflected: though these bhikṣus have all alike attained the fruit of arhatship, permanently cutting off further existence, it is not yet known which of them had, from the very outset, diligently undertaken the preparatory practice, and which had not — and, wishing to reveal this hidden distinction within their apparent uniformity, he raised this question. Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a single question from a fully awakened disciple can require a dozen separate explanations, none of them mutually exclusive, is this treatise's own quiet demonstration of how much can be accomplished by a single well-timed act of public speech: Śāriputra's question turns out to be simultaneously an act of pedagogy, pastoral care, institutional etiquette, and public relations, all performed at once without any of these purposes crowding out the others.

4. A Pointless Debate Resolved: Wisdom or Cessation, Which Comes First?

The technical machinery behind Śāriputra's question resolves an apparently unresolved category — the "arhat possessing the three knowledges" — showing it was already implicit in the earlier definitions all along, before turning to record, and then decisively dismiss, an old scholarly dispute between two named masters: Timira, who held wisdom superior to the meditative attainment of cessation since only wisdom takes a genuine object, and Ghoṣavarman, who held cessation superior since it belongs to noble ones alone. The treatise declares both accounts entirely wasted effort, since a person possessing the three knowledges may or may not also have attained cessation, yielding simply two different, equally valid combinations rather than any real hierarchy between the two.

9. Two Faculties for Pleasure and Pain, but Only One for Neutral — and Why Perception Never Qualifies

13. Why Life Alone, Among the Formations Dissociated from Mind

Among the various formations dissociated from mind — the four conditioned characteristics, class-nature, the mindless attainments, linguistic constructs, and acquisition itself — only the life-faculty earns faculty status, meeting all three required criteria at once: belonging exclusively to sentient beings, being exclusively a ripening-result, and being capable of comprehensively sustaining a being's own continued existence — a precise combination every other candidate in this entire category fails to satisfy in full.

14. Why Nirvāṇa Itself Never Becomes a Faculty

The fascicle closes on its most striking philosophical puzzle: if "supreme" is genuinely part of what "faculty" means, and nirvāṇa is supreme among all dharmas whatsoever, why is nirvāṇa itself never established as a faculty? The treatise's own resolution is quietly profound — nirvāṇa is precisely the place where every faculty is itself extinguished, and a place of extinction can never bear the name of what has been extinguished there, exactly as the shattered remains of a vase are never themselves called a "vase"; more technically, nirvāṇa possesses no arising, no cause, no function, and no object of cognition at all, so that its own supremacy, unlike every conditioned faculty's own, comes bundled with no accompanying activity whatsoever.

Question: if the sense of the supreme is the meaning of "faculty," then, since nirvāṇa is supreme among all dharmas whatsoever, why is it not established as a faculty? Answer: nirvāṇa is itself the very place where every faculty is extinguished and dissolved — and a place where something is extinguished and dissolved is never itself given the name of what has been extinguished there, exactly as the broken remains of a vase and the like are no longer called by the name "vase" and the like. Others say: whatever dharma traverses the world, takes up its own result, gives rise to a result in turn, possesses genuine function, and cognizes an object, can be established as a faculty — nirvāṇa is not so. Others say: whatever dharma arises and perishes, possesses a cause and a result, and bears a conditioned characteristic, can be established as a faculty — nirvāṇa is not so. Others say: a faculty belongs to what arises through the combination of cause and condition together — nirvāṇa is not so. Others say: whatever dharma is born by birth, aged by aging, and destroyed by destruction, can be established as a faculty — nirvāṇa is not so. Others say: a faculty falls within the aggregates, falls within time, and is followed everywhere by every manner of suffering — nirvāṇa is not so. Others say: a faculty possesses an anterior-and-posterior aspect, and an upper-middling-and-lower aspect — nirvāṇa is not so. Others say: the sense of the supreme, as the meaning of "faculty," refers specifically to what is supreme among conditioned dharmas while yet possessing genuine function; nirvāṇa, though supreme among all dharmas whatsoever, possesses no function at all, and hence is not established as a faculty. Space and analytical non-cessation likewise lack the sense of a faculty, and should be understood accordingly, by the very same reasoning.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The final distinction — that "supreme" as applied to a faculty always means supreme while still possessing genuine function, whereas nirvāṇa's own supremacy comes with no function at all — is this entire fascicle's most elegant closing move: it lets the treatise affirm nirvāṇa's own unmatched status without contradiction, simply by showing that "faculty" was never a claim about mere rank, but always, at bottom, a claim about a very specific kind of active, ongoing work that nirvāṇa, by its very nature, has already finished doing.
