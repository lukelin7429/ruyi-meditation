Four Attendants for a Sick Man

Fascicle 55 opens the theory of the four conditions — cause, immediately-antecedent, object, and dominant — defended against those who claim conditions have no real nature through the image of a sick, weakened person who needs four attendants, or three, or two, before he can finally rise and stand, never one alone. The full apparatus then runs against the view of the perishable aggregation of self and every other affliction, worked through every combination across realm, time, and taxonomic section, extending outward toward a complete fifteen- and twenty-seven-part combinatorial grid the reader is left to finish using the templates already given in full. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 55 continues the Unwholesome Nyāsa's tenth part (結蘊第二中不善納息第一之十), opening the theory of the four conditions — cause, immediately-antecedent, object, and dominant — and applying it systematically to pairs of afflictions across realm, time, and taxonomic section. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Refuting “Conditions Have No Real Nature”

Fascicle 55 opens by defending the very reality of conditions themselves against the Dārṣṭāntikas, who argue from a scriptural puzzle — ignorance, which has no distinguishing characteristic of its own, somehow conditions formations, which do — that "condition" is merely a designation with no genuine existence. The treatise answers first by consequence: if conditions weren't real, no dharma anywhere would be real, since the four conditions together subsume everything; the three kinds of awakening, distinguished only by the depth of wisdom that contemplates conditions, could never be established; and wisdom itself could never progress from inferior to superior, since nothing would exist to make that progress happen.

One asks: for what reason is this discourse composed? The answer: it is in order to check other schools' own tenets and to make manifest the correct meaning — that is, there are those who hold that a condition possesses no real nature, as the Dārṣṭāntikas maintain. One asks: for what reason do these masters hold this view? The answer: it is on the strength of scripture that they hold this view, that is, scripture states, ignorance conditions formations — they state as follows: ignorance possesses no distinguishing characteristic, while formations do possess a distinguishing characteristic — how, then, can a dharma possessing no distinguishing characteristic serve as a condition for a dharma that does possess one, and yet possess a real nature? The Great Virtuous One states: the various masters, following their own conception, merely designate the name ‘condition,’ which possesses no real, existent nature. It is in order to check this very tenet and to make manifest that conditions do genuinely, really exist, that this discourse is composed. Were one to hold that conditions possess no real nature, then every dharma whatsoever would possess no real nature, since the four conditions together subsume every dharma whatsoever — that is, the cause-condition subsumes every conditioned dharma; the immediately-antecedent-condition subsumes every past and present mind and mental factor, excepting the very last mind and mental factors of a past or present arhat;

義。謂若顯示一切法時。若不攝在諸緣觀察則為麁淺易可了知。若

the object-condition and the dominant-condition together subsume every dharma whatsoever. Further, were the nature of conditions not truly existent, then no dharma whatsoever would possess any profound meaning — that is, whenever the whole of the dharmas is displayed, should they not be subsumed within, and examined through, their own conditions, they would appear coarse and superficial, readily comprehended; should they be subsumed within, and examined through, their own conditions, they would prove profound beyond the four great oceans, fathomable to their very depths solely by the Buddha's own seed-wisdom.

Further, were the nature of conditions not truly existent, the three kinds of awakening could not be established at all — that is, contemplating the nature of conditions through superior wisdom is named buddhahood's own awakening; contemplating the nature of conditions through middling wisdom is named the awakening of the solitary buddha; contemplating the nature of conditions through inferior wisdom is named the awakening of the śrāvaka.

Further, were the nature of conditions not truly existent, wisdom itself ought to possess no sense of progressing through three grades — that is, whatever wisdom is inferior ought to remain forever inferior,

實有體。有功能故。由此尊者妙音說曰。若諸緣性非實有者。師應
不能令弟子慧初劣後勝。弟子亦應常為弟子不轉成師。然由諸緣性
實有故。師令弟子慧得漸增。弟子有時得成師義。故諸緣性決定實

whatever is middling ought to remain forever middling, and whatever is superior ought to remain forever superior. Yet wisdom that is inferior may become middling, and wisdom that is middling may become superior — and it is for this reason that the nature of conditions is certainly, really existent, possessing genuine causal efficacy. It is on this very account that the Venerable Ghoṣa states: were the nature of conditions not truly existent, a teacher would be incapable of causing his own student's wisdom to begin inferior and later become superior, and the student, too, would remain forever a student, never becoming a teacher himself. Yet, because the nature of conditions is genuinely, really existent, a teacher is capable of causing his own student's wisdom to increase by degrees, and the student, too, sometimes comes to attain the very status of teacher — and it is for this reason that the nature of conditions is certainly, really existent. One asks: if the nature of conditions is truly existent, how, then, is the scripture cited above to be reconciled?

答無明自體雖無異相而所作業得有異相。謂無量門無量梯隥。功能

欲故作斯論。自性羸劣者。謂諸有為法從緣生性立自性名。有說。

The answer: though ignorance's own substance possesses no distinguishing characteristic, the karmic function it accomplishes does indeed possess a distinguishing characteristic — that is, through immeasurable gateways and immeasurable degrees, its own capacity differs, and it thereby serves as a condition for formations, exactly as a single person possessing five separate skills has but one substance, yet a fivefold function.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Venerable Ghoṣa's own argument — that a teacher's real capacity to raise a student's wisdom, and a student's real capacity to eventually become a teacher, would be flatly impossible if conditions were mere designations — is a genuinely elegant move: it grounds an abstract metaphysical claim about causal efficacy in the most concrete, verifiable fact available to anyone in this tradition, the lived experience of actually being taught.

2. Why Conditioned Dharmas Are Feeble

The treatise turns from defending conditions' reality to explaining why conditioned existence needs them at all: every conditioned dharma is feeble in its own nature, lacking self-mastery, dependent on another, incapable of generating its own function, and never following its own wish. A sick, weakened person may need four attendants, or three, or two, before he can finally rise and stand — never one alone, still less none at all — and conditioned dharmas are exactly the same, arising only ever through four conditions, or three, or two, never through a single one working alone.

可得生。依怙於他者。謂諸有為法必依怙他方能起用無自作用者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, it is in order to display that every conditioned dharma is feeble in its own nature, lacking self-mastery, dependent upon another, possessing no self-generated function, and not following its own desire, that this discourse is composed. ‘Feeble in its own nature’ refers to the fact that every conditioned dharma, its own nature arising from conditions, is thereby given the name ‘own-nature.’ There are those who say:

謂諸有為法不能自起分別作用。誰造於我我為造誰。不隨己欲者。

conditioned existence, possessing arising and cessation, is thereby feeble in its own nature. There are those who say: conditioned existence, arising from conditions, is thereby feeble in its own nature, exactly as scripture states: monks, you should know, form is impermanent, and the very causes and conditions capable of generating form are likewise impermanent — since it is impermanent causes and conditions that give rise to form, how could form itself be permanent? Feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness are likewise so. On account of this very feebleness, every conditioned dharma arises through either four conditions, or three, or two — there being not even a single condition capable of generating it alone, still less no condition at all — and it is for this reason that conditioned dharmas are feeble in their own nature, exactly as a sick, weakened person may be supported by four attendants, or three, or two, before he is finally capable of rising and standing — there being not even a single attendant alone capable of causing him to rise and stand, still less no attendant at all. ‘Lacking self-mastery’ refers to the fact that every conditioned dharma possesses no power or function of its own by which it might arise. ‘Dependent upon another’ refers to the fact that every conditioned dharma must depend upon another before it is capable of generating its own function. ‘Possessing no self-generated function’ refers to

緣起者。緣起正理故作斯論。謂或有執。唯有無明緣行乃至生緣老
死是緣起法。為令彼迷得開解故。顯有為法皆是緣起。眾世所言。

the fact that every conditioned dharma is incapable of generating, of itself, the function of discrimination — who, then, creates me, and whom, then, do I create? ‘Not following its own desire’ refers to the fact that no conditioned dharma possesses any self-generated wish or delight — may I not arise, may I not cease — that is ever thereby fulfilled. Further, it is in order to disclose the correct principle of dependent origination to those confused about dependent origination that this discourse is composed — that is, there are those who hold that only the twelve links, from ignorance conditioning formations up through birth conditioning old age and death, are dharmas of dependent origination; it is in order to bring those so confused to understanding that this passage displays that every conditioned dharma whatsoever is, one and all, dependent origination — exactly as ordinary worldly speech says,

this ought to be stated here. Further, it is not in order to check others and display one's own tenet, but solely in order to disclose the correct principle of dependent origination and thereby bring others to understanding, that this discourse is composed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sick-man-and-four-attendants image reframes the whole doctrine of conditions as being fundamentally about weakness rather than about causal machinery: a conditioned dharma doesn't simply happen to have multiple causes the way a machine has multiple parts, it requires them precisely because it possesses no independent standing of its own — dependency, on this account, isn't incidental to conditioned existence, it is what conditioned existence most essentially is.

3. The View of Self as Its Own Condition: Four Ways

Having established that conditions are real and necessary, the treatise turns to its actual technical apparatus, working through the paradigm case in full: the view of the perishable aggregation of self, arising immediately after an earlier instance of itself and taking that earlier instance as its own object, thereby satisfies all four conditions at once — cause, immediately-antecedent, object, and dominant — each illustrated through its own homely image: the cause-condition like a seed, the immediately-antecedent like clearing the way, the object like a leaning-staff, and the dominant condition like the simple absence of any obstruction.

One asks: why does this passage ask, of how many conditions is the view of the perishable aggregation of self a condition for the view of the perishable aggregation of self, and answer, either four, three, two, or one, while, later, within the Wisdom Aggregate, the question, of how many conditions is dharma-cognition a condition for dharma-cognition, is answered, the cause, immediately-antecedent, object, and dominant conditions? Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four similes — seed, clearing-the-way, leaning-staff, non-obstruction — sort the four conditions along a genuinely illuminating axis of directness: a seed actively generates its fruit, clearing the way merely permits succession, a leaning-staff offers passive support to something that leans on it, and non-obstruction is barely a positive contribution at all, merely the absence of a blocking force — four entirely different grades of causal involvement, all classified together under the single word "condition."

4. Three Conditions, Two Conditions, One

Not every later instance of the view of self relates to an earlier one the same way. Should the later instance arise directly from the earlier without taking it as an object, only three conditions apply; should some other mental state intervene between the two instances, the immediately-antecedent-condition drops away, though the object-condition may return if the later instance still recalls the earlier as its object; and should neither the immediate sequence nor the object-relation hold, only the bare dominant-condition — mere non-obstruction — remains.

5. Crossing Realms, and the View of Self as Condition for Clinging to Rules and Vows

The analysis extends across realm boundaries: desire-realm self-view conditioning form- or formless-realm self-view can only ever be a bare dominant-condition, since realms differ and neither an immediately-antecedent sequence nor a shared object ever crosses that boundary — while the reverse direction, a higher-realm self-view conditioning a lower-realm one at the very moment of death and rebirth, may add the immediately-antecedent-condition as well. The treatise then opens a ten-class taxonomy of the latent tendencies (five views and five non-views, further split by whether each universally pervades its own ground) before running the entire four-condition apparatus a second time, now for self-view conditioning clinging to rules and vows.
