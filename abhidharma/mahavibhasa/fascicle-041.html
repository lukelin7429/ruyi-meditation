Eighty-Four Thousand Ministers and the Question of Desire

Fascicle 41 continues the Meaninglessness Nyāsa, working out precisely how earth-spirits and gods come to know of profound spiritual attainments that fall entirely outside their own direct perception — the Buddha's first turning of the wheel of the Dharma, and an arhat's own exhausted contaminants. It then turns to the eighty-four thousand ministers of Magadha, slain in a single night, whose stream-entry the Buddha withdraws to contemplate before revealing, and closes with a precise, carefully calibrated account of great desire, dissatisfaction, few desires, and contentment. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Why the Earth-Spirits Announced the Buddha's First Turning of the Wheel

Fascicle 41 opens with a scriptural puzzle: when the Buddha first turned the wheel of the Dharma at the Deer Park, earth-spirits and yakṣas raised their voices and proclaimed it everywhere — but did they actually witness this directly, or know of it some other way? The treatise insists this could only be inferential knowledge, since so profound a matter falls outside their own domain of direct perception, and works out the first of five distinct channels by which such knowledge could have reached them: the Buddha deliberately giving rise to a specific, previously-experienced conventional thought calibrated to exactly the audience he wished to reach — not too subtle for the dull-facultied, not so accessible that even animals would grasp it.

Chinese Source

如契經說。佛轉法輪憍陳那等苾芻見法。地神藥叉舉聲遍告。世尊今在婆羅痆斯仙人鹿苑。三轉法輪具十二相。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為欲分別契經義故。謂契經說。佛轉法輪。地神藥叉舉聲遍告。契經雖作是說。而不分別其義。不說地神為自有智見。為因他故知舉聲遍告。經是此論所依根本。彼不說者今應說之。復次欲令疑者得決定故。經說。地神舉聲遍告。佛在鹿苑三轉法輪。或有生疑。地神自有現量智見知如是事。欲令此疑得決定故。顯彼但有比量智見生處得智。於轉法輪非現境故。由是因緣故作斯論。為彼地神有正智見知佛轉法輪苾芻見法不。答無。此事甚深非彼境故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As scripture states: when the Buddha turned the wheel of the Dharma, Kauṇḍinya and the other monks saw the Dharma, and the earth-spirits and yakṣas raised their voices, proclaiming everywhere that the Blessed One was now at the Ṛṣipatana Deer Park at Vārāṇasī, having turned the wheel of the Dharma three times, complete with its twelve aspects, and so on, at length. One asks: why is this treatise composed? The answer: it is composed in order to analyze the meaning of scripture — that is, scripture states that the Buddha turned the wheel of the Dharma, and the earth-spirits and yakṣas raised their voices, proclaiming this everywhere. Although scripture states this much, it does not analyze its meaning, nor does it state whether the earth-spirits themselves possessed this knowledge and insight, or came to know it through some other cause and thereby proclaimed it everywhere; scripture is the very root this treatise depends upon, and what it leaves unstated, this treatise should now state. Further, it is composed in order to bring those in doubt to a settled resolution — that is, scripture states that the earth-spirits raised their voices, proclaiming everywhere that the Buddha, at the Deer Park, had turned the wheel of the Dharma three times, and there are those who thereby give rise to the doubt that the earth-spirits themselves possessed direct, immediate knowledge and insight of this matter — in order to bring this doubt to a settled resolution, this treatise reveals that they possessed only inferential knowledge and insight, born of their own particular station, since the turning of the wheel of the Dharma does not fall within their own domain of direct perception — and it is on account of these causes and conditions that this treatise is composed. Did those earth-spirits possess genuine knowledge and insight, knowing that the Buddha had turned the wheel of the Dharma and that the monks had seen the Dharma? The answer: they did not, since this matter is exceedingly profound, and does not fall within their own domain.

彼云何知。答由五緣故。一者信世尊故。謂佛起世俗心我轉法輪苾芻見法。由是彼知。謂佛若起無漏心或未曾得世俗心。一切有情無能知者。若起曾得世俗心時。諸有情類有能知者。謂佛起此世俗心時。若欲令鈍根者亦知。則蛇奴等亦能了知。若欲令利根者亦不知。則舍利子等入邊際第四靜慮。起妙願智亦不能知若欲令惡趣亦知。則猨猴等亦能了知。若欲令善趣亦不知。則諸人天無能知者。

How, then, did they come to know it? The answer: through five conditions. First, through faith in the Blessed One — that is, when the Buddha gives rise to the conventional thought, "I have turned the wheel of the Dharma, and the monks have seen the Dharma," it is on this account that they come to know it. That is, were the Buddha to give rise only to an uncontaminated thought, or to a conventional thought never previously experienced, no sentient being whatsoever would be capable of knowing it; it is only when he gives rise to a conventional thought of a kind previously experienced that certain classes of sentient beings become capable of knowing it. When the Buddha gives rise to this conventional thought, were he to wish even the dull-facultied to know it, then even snakes and slaves would be able to understand it; were he to wish even the sharp-facultied not to know it, then even Śāriputra, entering the fourth dhyāna at its very outer limit and giving rise to the wondrous knowledge of aspiration, would still be unable to know it; were he to wish even those of the evil destinies to know it, then even monkeys would be able to understand it; were he to wish even those of the good destinies not to know it, then no human or god whatsoever would be capable of knowing it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The calibration problem worked out here is genuinely delicate: a thought pitched to reach even the dull-facultied would trivialize the matter down to something snakes and slaves could grasp, while a thought exclusive enough for the sharp-facultied would leave even Śāriputra in the dark — the treatise is describing something like a precisely tuned broadcast frequency, deliberately set to reach earth-spirits specifically and no one above or below their own level of capacity.

2. Why the Buddha Chose to Announce His Own Triumph

The treatise explains why the Buddha would deliberately give rise to this thought at all — three incalculable eons of arduous practice, a vast vow made long ago for the benefit of others, now finally bearing fruit, the resulting joy itself the impulse behind the announcement. A second channel of knowledge follows: the Buddha directly telling another, reserved for those who need spoken words rather than mind-reading alone, explained through nine distinct reasons ranging from displaying the truthfulness of his own teaching to simply wishing to instill reverence and joy in gods and humans.

Chinese Source

今佛欲令地神知故。起曾得世俗心我轉法輪苾芻見法。地神知已舉聲遍告。問佛何故起此世俗心。答三無數劫修集種種難行苦行。為益有情。今轉法輪苾芻見法。即昔加行今初得果。深生歡喜故起此心。復次昔發弘誓。為饒益他今始得果。故起此心。復次昔發大願。為拔濟他今始得果。故起此心。復次所期勝義。利樂有情今創果遂。故起此心。二者或佛告他我轉法輪苾芻見法。故彼得聞。謂若於心得善巧者佛起心已即能了知。若但於言得善巧者。佛告他已方能了知。問世尊何故告他令知。答世尊欲顯善說法中所言誠諦一見一樂。眾皆同許故告他知。復次三無數劫修多苦行。為益有情今初得果。深生歡喜故告他知。復次世尊自顯於九十六諸道法中最尊最勝無能及者。故告他知。復次欲顯佛法實能出離有大神變。故告他知。復次佛欲顯示憍陳那等真實功德。亦示世間良福田。故告他令知。復次欲令天人於佛聖教深敬信故。告他令知。復次世尊自顯遠離法慳。於希有法無師拳故告他令知復次佛欲顯己有大士法非餘道故告他令知。復次世尊自顯有聰明相非餘道故告他令知。如契經說。諸聰明者有三種相。一善思所思。二善作所作。三善說所說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, the Buddha, wishing the earth-spirits in particular to know it, gives rise to the conventional thought, previously experienced, "I have turned the wheel of the Dharma, and the monks have seen the Dharma" — the earth-spirits, having come to know this, raise their voices and proclaim it everywhere. One asks: why does the Buddha give rise to this conventional thought? The answer: across three incalculable eons, he cultivated manifold difficult and arduous practices for the benefit of sentient beings; now that the wheel of the Dharma has been turned and the monks have seen the Dharma, this is precisely the fruit of that earlier undertaking newly realized, and it is on account of the profound joy this gives rise to that he gives rise to this thought. Further, he had, in the past, made a vast vow for the benefit of others, and now, this fruit having first been realized, he gives rise to this thought on that account. Further, he had, in the past, made a great aspiration to rescue and deliver others, and now, this fruit having first been realized, he gives rise to this thought on that account. Further, the supreme aim he had long anticipated, benefiting and bringing joy to sentient beings, has now, for the first time, come to fruition, and it is on this account that he gives rise to this thought. Second, or else the Buddha tells another, "I have turned the wheel of the Dharma, and the monks have seen the Dharma," and it is thereby that they come to hear of it. That is, one who has attained skill with respect to mind alone is able to understand as soon as the Buddha has merely given rise to the thought; one who has attained skill only with respect to speech is able to understand only once the Buddha has told him directly. One asks: why does the Blessed One tell another, causing him to know? The answer: the Blessed One wishes to reveal that, within his own well-taught Dharma, what is spoken is true and sincere, seen and delighted in alike by all, and it is for this reason that he tells another, causing him to know. Further, across three incalculable eons, he cultivated manifold difficult austerities for the benefit of sentient beings, and now that this fruit has first been realized, it is on account of the profound joy this gives rise to that he tells another, causing him to know. Further, the Blessed One thereby reveals himself as the most honored and supreme among all ninety-six paths, one whom none can equal, and it is for this reason that he tells another, causing him to know. Further, it is in order to reveal that the Buddhadharma is genuinely capable of bringing about liberation, possessing great supernormal transformation, that he tells another, causing him to know. Further, the Buddha wishes to display Kauṇḍinya's own true and genuine virtue, and thereby to show the world a genuine field of merit, and it is for this reason that he tells another, causing him to know. Further, it is in order to cause gods and humans to give rise to profound reverence and faith in the Buddha's own noble teaching that he tells another, causing him to know. Further, the Blessed One thereby reveals his own freedom from any stinginess with respect to the Dharma, holding nothing back within his own closed fist with respect to what is rare and difficult to obtain, and it is for this reason that he tells another, causing him to know. Further, the Buddha wishes to display his own possession of the qualities of a great being, unlike those of any other path, and it is for this reason that he tells another, causing him to know. Further, the Blessed One thereby reveals his own possession of the marks of a truly wise one, unlike those of any other path, and it is for this reason that he tells another, causing him to know — exactly as scripture states, those who are truly wise possess three marks: first, they think well upon what is to be thought; second, they act well upon what is to be done; third, they speak well of what is to be spoken.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the Buddha needs nine separate justifications for simply telling someone good news is a small but telling window into how this treatise treats even the most ordinary-seeming communicative act: nothing the Buddha does is permitted to be merely incidental, and every gesture, however natural it might look on its surface, is presumed to carry deliberate, multiply-overdetermined pedagogical purpose.

3. A Third Channel: The Sage-Devas, and Kauṇḍinya's Own Parallel Joy

A third channel opens the question of just who these "great, virtuous sage-devas" might be, with masters proposing gods of the pure abodes, gods of the desire realm who have already seen the truths, or a long-lived deva who once witnessed an earlier buddha's own first turning and now rejoices to see the pattern repeat. The treatise then pivots to Kauṇḍinya's own parallel experience: he, too, gives rise to a spontaneous thought of profound joy upon attaining stream-entry, having slain the twenty forms of self-view and bounded what was once boundless suffering, and, exactly like the Buddha, may either be known through this thought directly or choose to tell others himself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Mapping Kauṇḍinya's own joy onto precisely the same five-channel structure just used for the Buddha's is a quiet but significant equalizing move: the newly-liberated disciple's inner experience of breakthrough is treated as epistemologically identical in kind to the Buddha's own, differing only in scale and audience, not in the basic architecture of how private realization becomes knowable to others.

4. A Parallel Puzzle: How Do the Gods Know an Arhat's Contaminants Are Exhausted?

A structurally identical question now opens for an entirely separate scriptural passage: the gods of the Thirty-Three Heavens are said to gather in the Hall of Good Dharma and proclaim when a monk has attained arhatship — but, exactly as with the earth-spirits, this cannot be direct perception, since the exhaustion of the contaminants is far too profound and subtle a matter to fall within even a god's own domain. The same five-channel framework applies here as well, beginning again with the Buddha's own deliberate, previously-experienced thought.

5. Two of the Five Channels: The Buddha's Resolve, and His Telling of Others

The first channel is explained: this thought accords fully with the Buddha's own deepest intention, since nothing more perfectly satisfies him than a monk who has permanently cut off any future existence. The second channel follows the same nine-reason structure already established for the earlier announcement, since the underlying motivations — displaying the Dharma's truthfulness, instilling reverence, encouraging others toward diligence — apply equally here.

9. Why Does an Omniscient Buddha Need to Withdraw and Contemplate?

11. "Converted Through Transformation" Versus "Dharma-Following Practice": Ten Parallel Distinctions

The treatise now works through, in exhaustive, almost liturgical parallel structure, exactly what distinguishes one who "sees the Dharma while among the gods" from one who "sees the Dharma while among humans": ten separate pairings, each tracking the same underlying distinction through a different technical lens — precept-keeping versus not, wholesome roots planted in a human life and ripening only after celestial rebirth versus ripening within that same human life, the higher trainings in discipline, concentration, and wisdom, and even the specific factors that constitute stream-entry itself — each time, attainment completed within a single human lifetime earns the name "dharma-following practice," while attainment spanning a human life and a subsequent celestial rebirth earns the name "converted through transformation." The treatise closes by explaining why: the human path demands vastly more applied effort — years of reverent study under teachers, memorizing scripture, mastering the Vinaya, and only then retreating to mountain solitude for grueling, disciplined meditation — while celestial attainment simply allows what was cultivated in an earlier human life to manifest spontaneously, its own hard work already banked and merely coming due.

And naming the harder, more effortful human path with the more honorific title, rather than the easier celestial one, is a value judgment worth noticing: heaven, on this reading, is where earned fruit ripens, not where the real work happens.

12. What Is "Great Desire"? What Is "Dissatisfaction"?

An entirely new topic opens with a pair of precise technical definitions: "great desire" names whatever one yearns, seeks, longs, and schemes after with respect to what one has not yet obtained — pleasing sensory objects for a householder, robes and requisites for one gone forth — while "dissatisfaction" names the parallel failure to find contentment in what one has already obtained, always yearning for more. The treatise notes that these two dharmas so closely resemble one another in ordinary speech that people commonly conflate them, calling a greatly desirous person dissatisfied and a dissatisfied person greatly desirous, and sets out to untangle exactly where the genuine distinction lies.

13. Their Common Root, and the Antidotes: "Few Desires" and "Contentment"

The treatise settles that both great desire and dissatisfaction share the very same inherent nature — the unwholesome root of greed — distinguished only by whether their object has or has not yet been obtained, spanning all six consciousnesses within the desire realm. It then turns to these vices' own precise antidotes: "few desires," the simple absence of yearning after what remains unobtained, and "contentment," the absence of further craving for what has already been secured — noting, in a careful aside, that this absence of desire is not indiscriminate, since desire that accords with the Dharma, aims at ending suffering, or benefits others remains entirely wholesome and is never what "few desires" asks one to relinquish.

14. The Verdict, and a Closing Paradox: "Few Desires" Can Still Be "Great Desire"

The treatise reaches its own verdict — few desires and contentment, like their opposite vices, share a single inherent nature (the wholesome root of non-greed), spanning all three realms and the unbound alike, distinguished only by whether their object has or has not yet been obtained. A final, genuinely striking qualification closes the discussion: whether a given desire counts as "great" or "few" is never an absolute, context-free measure, but is always relative to what a person's own circumstances actually require — one who needs only a single dose of medicine yet wants two is greatly desirous even by a small absolute margin, while one who genuinely needs a hundred implements and wants exactly that many, no more, counts as having few desires despite the seemingly large number.
