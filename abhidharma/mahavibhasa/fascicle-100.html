Five Hundred Lifetimes in a Single Thought

Fascicle 100 maps the Great Snow Mountains simile onto the path of seeing, defines the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge in full technical detail, and closes with the Venerable Śubha's own vindication and the innate knowledge even a being newly reborn in hell still briefly possesses. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 100 continues the Mind-Reading-Knowledge Nyāsa (他心智納息第三之二), closing the full technical apparatus for the knowledge of others' minds (他心智) before opening the parallel apparatus for the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge (宿住隨念智) — own-nature, classification, realm and ground, cultivation, and the precise limits of what each knowledge can and cannot reach. Terms established across fascicles 93–99 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The Great Snow Mountains: What Only a Buddha Can Know

Fascicle 100 opens exactly where fascicle 99 broke off, distinguishing capacity from manifest exercise: a noble one reborn in the formless realm can still cultivate a lower knowledge of others' minds even though it can never actually arise there, exactly as a dharma-cognition can be cultivated from above. A beloved scriptural simile then maps the path of seeing onto the Great Snow Mountains — places where neither monkey nor human can travel, places only a monkey (the solitary-realizer) can travel, and places both monkey and human (the disciple) can travel — cashing out into an exact count: the disciple's own knowledge of others' minds reaches only two of the path of seeing's fifteen moments, the solitary-realizer's reaches three, and the Buddha's alone reaches all fifteen in sequence, since only the Buddha's own knowledge needs no preparatory effort at all to manifest. A further puzzle asks why the disciple, capable of directly realizing the Buddha's and solitary-realizer's own path in a general sense, still cannot know their minds through the knowledge of others' minds specifically — resolved by distinguishing general from particular characteristic.

Ru-Yi Translation

Among sentient beings, there are those who circulate onward, and there are those who return and cease — "circulating onward" refers to receiving further birth; "returning and ceasing" refers to proceeding toward nirvāṇa. If a sentient being is born within the desire or form realm, or if an ordinary being is born within the formless realm, then, for one who cultivates the knowledge of others' minds, with respect to that same being's own mind and mental concomitants, the knowledge of others' minds is attained by virtue of two matters — first, the capacity to take as object; second, manifest arising. If a sentient being has already entered complete nirvāṇa, or if a noble one is born within the formless realm, then, for one who cultivates the knowledge of others' minds, with respect to that same being's own mind and mental concomitants, the knowledge of others' minds is attained by virtue of a single matter alone — that is, solely the capacity to take as object, without manifest arising. One asks: can a noble one born within the formless realm still cultivate the knowledge of others' minds belonging to a lower ground? There are those who say: he cannot, since it is utterly impossible for it ever to arise there. The verdict: it ought to be said that, although it certainly does not arise, he can nonetheless still cultivate it, since one born above can still cultivate the undefiled dharmas of below, exactly as with the dharma-cognition category, this being consistent with principle; an ordinary being born there possesses no such cultivation at all.

或有心心所法是佛他心智境非獨覺聲聞。或有心心所法是佛獨覺他心智境非諸聲聞。或有心心所法是佛獨覺聲聞他心智境。如契經說。苾芻當知。大雪山中有如是處獼猴與人俱不能行。有如是處。

There exists mind and mental concomitants that are the object of a buddha's own knowledge of others' minds, but not of a solitary-realizer's or disciple's; there exists mind and mental concomitants that are the object of a buddha's and a solitary-realizer's own knowledge of others' minds, but not of the various disciples'; there exists mind and mental concomitants that are the object of a buddha's, a solitary-realizer's, and a disciple's own knowledge of others' minds all alike. Exactly as the scripture states: monks, you should know that, within the Great Snow Mountains, there exist such places as these — where a monkey and a human being alike are unable to travel; there exist such places as these — where a monkey is capable of traveling, yet a human being is not; and there exist such places as these — where a monkey and a human being are both alike capable of traveling.

Within this same scripture, "the Great Snow Mountains" displays the dharma to be known; "such places as these" displays the fifteen moments belonging to the path of seeing; "monkey" displays the solitary-realizer; "human being" displays the disciple; "capable of traveling" and "not capable of traveling" display the various knowledges of others' minds. Yet the knowledge of others' minds is capable of knowing solely mind and mental concomitants of its own same kind, not of a different kind — that is, what is contaminated knows the contaminated; what is undefiled knows the undefiled; what is previously attained knows what is previously attained; what is not previously attained knows what is not previously attained; what belongs to the category of dharma-cognition knows what belongs to the category of dharma-cognition; what belongs to the category of subsequent cognition knows what belongs to the category of subsequent cognition. The disciple's own knowledge of others' minds is, within the path of seeing, capable of knowing solely two moments of mind; the solitary-realizer's own knowledge of others' minds is, within the path of seeing, capable of knowing solely three moments of mind; the buddha's own knowledge of others' minds is, within the path of seeing, capable of knowing, in sequence, all fifteen moments of mind — for what reason is this so? The buddha's own knowledge of others' minds does not depend upon preparatory effort in order to manifest in the present; the solitary-realizer's own knowledge of others' minds attains manifestation through an inferior grade of preparatory effort; the disciple's own knowledge of others' minds attains manifestation only through a middling or superior grade of preparatory effort — that is, when the one practicing insight is about to enter the path of seeing, a disciple wishing to know that same person's own path-of-seeing mind first cultivates the preparatory effort of the knowledge of others' minds belonging to the dharma-cognition category; once this same practitioner of insight has already entered the path of seeing, this same preparatory effort for the knowledge of others' minds is thereby already complete, and he becomes capable of knowing those same two moments of mind — namely, the forbearance regarding suffering, a dharma-cognition, together with the mind co-arising with the knowledge of suffering, a dharma-cognition. Once this same practitioner of insight enters the subsequent-cognition category, the disciple further cultivates the preparatory effort of the knowledge of others' minds belonging to the subsequent-cognition category, and, after thirteen moments of preparatory effort have passed, this same preparatory effort is only then complete, and he becomes capable of knowing this same person's own sixteenth moment of mind — that is, although he originally wished to know this same person's own third moment of mind, he comes, instead, only to know this same person's own sixteenth moment of mind, and it is for this reason that a disciple's own knowledge of others' minds is capable of knowing solely the path of seeing's own first two moments of mind. When the one practicing insight is about to enter the path of seeing, a solitary-realizer wishing to know that same person's own path-of-seeing mind likewise first cultivates the preparatory effort of the knowledge of others' minds belonging to the dharma-cognition category; once this same practitioner of insight has already entered the path of seeing, this same preparatory effort for the knowledge of others' minds is thereby already complete, and he becomes capable of knowing those same two moments of mind — namely, the forbearance regarding suffering, a dharma-cognition, together with the mind co-arising with the knowledge of suffering, a dharma-cognition. Once this same practitioner of insight enters the subsequent-cognition category, the solitary-realizer further cultivates the preparatory effort of the knowledge of others' minds belonging to the subsequent-cognition category, and, after five moments of preparatory effort have passed, this same preparatory effort is only then complete, and he comes to know this same person's own eighth moment of mind — that is, although he originally wished to know this same person's own third moment of mind, he comes, instead, only to know this same person's own mind co-arising with the subsequent knowledge of accumulation. There are those who say: he comes to know this same person's own fifteenth moment of mind, since twelve moments of preparatory effort are required for it to be complete. There are those who say: the solitary-realizer is capable of knowing four moments of mind — namely, knowing the first two moments, together with the eighth and the fourteenth moments. There are further teachers who say: he is capable of knowing the first two moments, together with the eleventh and the twelfth moments — this being precisely the forbearance regarding cessation, a subsequent cognition, together with the mind co-arising with the knowledge of cessation, a subsequent cognition. The buddha's own knowledge of others' minds, not depending upon any preparatory effort at all, fully knows every one of these fifteen moments of the path of seeing.

佛智於三道皆能知自相共相。獨覺智於獨覺聲聞道。能知自相共相。於佛道能知共相非自相。聲聞智於聲聞道能知自相共相。於佛獨覺道能知共相非自相。問聲聞入現觀時。於佛獨覺道能現觀不設爾何失。若能現觀。云何聲聞他心智不能知佛及獨覺心。若不能現觀。云何亦得緣彼證淨。又應現觀時不遍觀道諦。答應作是說。聲聞入現觀時。於佛獨覺道亦能現觀。問若爾云何聲聞他心智不能知佛及獨覺心。答入現觀時知彼共相非自相故。能現觀他心智唯知自相故。彼他心智。不知佛及獨覺心。

The buddha's own wisdom, with respect to all three paths, is capable of knowing both the particular and the general characteristic; the solitary-realizer's own wisdom, with respect to the solitary-realizer's own and the disciple's own path, is capable of knowing both the particular and the general characteristic, while, with respect to the buddha's own path, is capable of knowing solely the general characteristic, not the particular; the disciple's own wisdom, with respect to the disciple's own path, is capable of knowing both the particular and the general characteristic, while, with respect to the buddha's and the solitary-realizer's own path, is capable of knowing solely the general characteristic, not the particular. One asks: at the very moment the disciple enters direct realization, is he capable of directly realizing the buddha's and the solitary-realizer's own path? Suppose this were so — what fault would there be? If he is capable of directly realizing it, then how is it that the disciple's own knowledge of others' minds is incapable of knowing the buddha's or the solitary-realizer's own mind? If he is incapable of directly realizing it, then how is it that he can still take it as an object of undefiled confirmed purity through it, and, further, ought not direct realization, when it occurs, to fully observe the truth of the path in its entirety? The answer: it ought to be said that, at the very moment the disciple enters direct realization, he is indeed capable of directly realizing the buddha's and the solitary-realizer's own path as well. One asks: if this is so, then how is it that the disciple's own knowledge of others' minds is incapable of knowing the buddha's or the solitary-realizer's own mind? The answer: at the very moment of entering direct realization, he knows solely their general characteristic, not their particular characteristic, whereas the knowledge of others' minds capable of direct realization knows solely the particular characteristic, and it is for this reason that this same knowledge of others' minds does not know the buddha's or the solitary-realizer's own mind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The disciple's own convoluted path to knowing even a single further moment of another's mind — cultivating an entirely new preparatory effort, sustaining it across thirteen further moments, only to land, by the text's own admission, on the sixteenth moment rather than the third he originally aimed for — is a small, almost comic illustration of a much larger point this fascicle keeps returning to: procedural knowledge bought through preparatory effort is a blunt, imprecise instrument compared to the Buddha's own effortless precision, arriving exactly where aimed without any of the same drift.

2. Does It Know a Whole Category, or Each Single Moment?

A subtle question probes how a single sentient being's own knowledge of others' minds can possibly track every other being's own boundless stream of individual mind-moments without becoming itself infinitely burdened: does it know an entire category at a glance, or each single moment one at a time? The resolution insists on the latter, defusing the worry about an overburdened support by observing that every sentient being's own cluster of the knowledge of others' minds is itself equally boundless, so that boundless knowers matching boundless objects one-to-one poses no disproportion at all. A further puzzle then asks whether this same knowledge takes all three times as its object, despite an apparently contradictory formula elsewhere in the treatise — resolved through a careful reading of what that formula's own underlying intent actually was.

Chinese Source

問一有情相續中所有他心智。能知一切有情隨其所應心心所法。此智於彼為知總物類。為知別剎那。設爾何失。若知總物類非別剎那者。於別剎那云何能知。若知別剎那者。云何此智所依非多。謂我他心智聚。有二十一法緣一有情一剎那受。如一剎那受。一切剎那受亦爾。如受剎那無量無邊。諸餘一切心心所法剎那亦爾。如一有情心心所法剎那無量無邊。諸餘一切有情心心所法剎那亦爾。如是我心心所法則為多。餘一切有情由智多故所依亦多。有作是說。知總物類非別剎那。問若爾於別剎那云何能知。答無有是處。然為分別。假使能知物類智盡。於餘剎那不復欲知。設復欲知亦不能知。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: the knowledge of others' minds present within a single sentient being's own continuum is capable of knowing every sentient being's own mind and mental concomitants, each according to what is fitting — does this same knowledge, with respect to those same beings, know them as a single, gathered category, or does it know each single moment individually? Suppose either were so — what fault would there be? If it knows them as a single, gathered category rather than each single moment, then how can it be capable of knowing each single moment individually? If it knows each single moment individually, then how is it that this same knowledge's own support is not thereby manifold — that is, my own cluster of the knowledge of others' minds, consisting of twenty-one dharmas, takes as its object a single sentient being's own single moment of feeling; exactly as with a single moment of feeling, so too with every single moment of feeling; exactly as the moments of feeling are measureless and boundless, so too are every remaining moment of every remaining mind and mental concomitant likewise measureless and boundless; exactly as a single sentient being's own moments of mind and mental concomitants are measureless and boundless, so too are every remaining sentient being's own moments of mind and mental concomitants likewise measureless and boundless — in this way, my own mind and mental concomitants would then be manifold, and, since every remaining sentient being is likewise known by manifold knowledge, its own support would thereby be manifold as well. There are those who say: it knows them as a single, gathered category, not each single moment individually. One asks: if this is so, then how can it be capable of knowing each single moment individually? The answer: this is not the case at all — yet, for the sake of distinguishing it, suppose that the knowledge capable of knowing a given category were already exhausted with respect to it — with respect to any remaining moment, it would then no longer wish to know it; and, were it to still wish to know it, it would nonetheless be incapable of knowing it.

智若未盡欲知即知。又物類智於諸剎那不欲別知。設欲別知亦不能知。諸欲知者唯於物類。復有說者。知別剎那。問若爾云何此智所依非多。答無如是失。此彼等故。如我他心智聚二十一法。有無量無邊剎那。於餘一切有情一切心心所法。一一剎那別別能緣。如是諸餘一切有情他心智聚。亦各有無量無邊剎那。於餘一切有情一切心心所法。一一剎那別別能緣。故智所依無偏多失。評曰。應知此中後說為善。心心所法所緣定故。

If the knowledge has not yet been exhausted, then, wishing to know it, it thereupon knows it. Further, this same knowledge of a given category has, with respect to the various moments, no wish to know each one individually; and, were there a wish to know each one individually, it would nonetheless be incapable of doing so. Whatever wishes to know anything at all does so solely with respect to the category as a whole. There are, further, those who say: it knows each single moment individually. One asks: if this is so, then how is it that this same knowledge's own support is not thereby manifold? The answer: there is no such fault at all, since this and that are equal — that is, exactly as my own cluster of the knowledge of others' minds, consisting of twenty-one dharmas, possesses measureless and boundless moments, and, with respect to every remaining sentient being's own every mind and mental concomitant, is capable of taking each single moment individually as its object, so too does every remaining sentient being's own cluster of the knowledge of others' minds likewise each possess measureless and boundless moments, and is likewise capable of taking each single moment individually as its object with respect to every remaining sentient being's own every mind and mental concomitant — and it is for this reason that there is no fault of the knowledge's own support becoming disproportionately manifold. The verdict: it should be understood that the latter position, stated here, is the better one, since the object taken by mind and mental concomitants is fixed and determinate.

問諸他心智。為能通緣三世。為但緣現在耶。答諸他心智但緣現在。問若爾論說當云何通。如說過去未來法九智知。除滅智。答應作是說。過去未來法八智知。除滅智。他心智現在法九智知。除滅智。而不作是說者。應知有別意趣。謂彼種類九智所知。若在過去即是過去他心智所知。亦是未來不生法。他心智所知。若在未來唯是未來他心智所知。若在現在即是現在他心智所知。亦是未來不生法他心智所知。依此意說過去未來法是九智所知。不說過去未來法是現在他心智所知。復有欲令他心智現在前時能知他相續三剎那心。謂現在前者。次前滅者次後生者。論依此說亦不相違。評曰。

One asks: does the knowledge of others' minds pervade all three times as its object, or does it take solely the present as its object? The answer: every knowledge of others' minds takes solely the present as its object. One asks: if this is so, then how is one to reconcile this with what another treatise states — namely, that past and future dharmas are known by nine kinds of knowledge, excepting the knowledge of cessation? The answer: it ought to be said that past and future dharmas are known by eight kinds of knowledge, excepting the knowledge of cessation; present dharmas of the knowledge of others' minds are known by nine kinds of knowledge, excepting the knowledge of cessation. As for why it is not stated in this way, one should understand there to be a separate underlying intent — that is, this same kind, known by the nine kinds of knowledge, if it belongs to the past, is then known by the past-belonging knowledge of others' minds, and is also known by the knowledge of others' minds that knows future non-arisen dharmas; if it belongs to the future, it is known solely by the future-belonging knowledge of others' minds; if it belongs to the present, it is then known by the present-belonging knowledge of others' minds, and is also known by the knowledge of others' minds that knows future non-arisen dharmas. It is in reliance upon this same underlying intent that it is said that past and future dharmas are known by the nine kinds of knowledge — it is not said that past and future dharmas are known by the present knowledge of others' minds. There are, further, those who wish to hold that, at the very moment the knowledge of others' minds manifests in the present, it is capable of knowing another's own continuum's three moments of mind — namely, the one presently manifesting, the one that has just ceased before it, and the one that will arise just after it — the treatise, in reliance upon this same account, likewise does not contradict itself. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The resolution to the overburdened-support worry is a quietly elegant piece of infinite arithmetic: rather than trying to show that the knowledge of others' minds somehow economizes on what it must track, the Vaibhāṣikas simply match one infinity against another and declare the books balanced — my own boundless moments of knowing are answered by your own boundless moments of being known, and since neither side is finite to begin with, there is no meaningful sense in which either one could ever be said to overflow the other.

3. One Object at a Time, and What the Buddha Really Meant

A vivid dirty-garment simile resolves whether the knowledge of others' minds takes a single object or a whole cluster at once: exactly as observing a stain and observing the garment itself cannot happen in the same instant, so too does knowing the craving and knowing the mind it accompanies alternate rather than co-occur, each moment taking only one of them as its object. This raises an apparent contradiction with the Buddha's own claim to know, with a single mental application, the thoughts of the entire assembly of monks at once — resolved through several competing accounts (this being inferential knowledge built on a single instance of genuine mind-reading, or wish-knowledge, or an entirely unprecedented neutral mental state unique to the Buddha) before the section closes with the fixed technical profile of the knowledge of others' minds in miniature: one dharma, one real thing, one particular characteristic, the present alone, another's continuum alone.

4. Defining the Recollection of Former Abodes

A parallel apparatus now opens for this nyāsa's second knowledge: the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge, defined as cultivated, fruit-of-cultivation wisdom capable of manifestly recollecting one's own or another's past lives, together with their "various characteristic-forms" (the describable shapes of rebirth, the fundamental state, and death) and "utterances" (the merely-speakable, indescribably subtle intermediate state). A rich scriptural dialogue between the Buddha and Ānanda over the six internal versus external sense-fields, together with a closing verse praising undefiled wisdom as flawless as Jambudvīpa's own pure gold, rounds out the picture of exactly what this knowledge takes as its object: the contaminated five aggregates of one's own or another's past lives within the desire or form realm.

5. Seventy-Two Classifications, and Why "Recollection" in the Name

The now-familiar combinatorial exercise returns: this single knowledge admits of one, two, three, four, six, eight, nine, twelve, eighteen, twenty-four, thirty-six, or seventy-two distinct classifications, depending on which dimension (previously attained, grade, dhyāna-fruit) one slices it along. A rich etymological note then explains why, though its own substance is wisdom like every other knowledge in this treatise, it is named for "recollection" specifically — because mindfulness's own force predominates within its cluster of co-arising factors, exactly as the four applications of mindfulness, the mindfulness of breathing, the inherent recollection-born knowledge, and even subduing the perception of form are all, despite being wisdom at bottom, named for whichever single factor happens to predominate within them.

7. Why the Buddha Cannot Recollect the Formless Realm — and Why That Doesn't Diminish Him

A genuinely delicate puzzle asks how to reconcile the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge's own inability to reach the formless realm with scripture's own claim that the Buddha recollects every matter of his own former abodes "whether possessing form or not." The resolution insists the Buddha never uses this same knowledge for the formless realm at all — he uses inferential knowledge instead — and a rich three-way comparison then ranks exactly how reliable that inferential knowledge is across three kinds of knower: the outside paths, observing no further lifetimes past a certain point, wrongly conclude total annihilation; the disciples, observing the same silence, guess at which specific formless sphere a being was reborn into, but guess wrong as often as right, since a being's own actual lifespan may have run shorter or longer than assumed; the Buddha alone, observing directly the very mind of death and of conception-linking, knows with total accuracy in every single case. 12. Recollecting the Never-Seen: How the Pure Abodes Can Still Be Known

A genuinely elegant puzzle closes this stretch of the fascicle: since this same knowledge recollects solely what has been experienced before, how can it ever recollect the five Pure Abodes heavens, given that one has, from beginningless time, never once been reborn there? 13. How Many Lifetimes and Destinies Fit in a Single Moment

A cascade of precise, quantified questions closes out the main body of this apparatus: how many lifetimes can be known upon entering a single concentration (one at first, a hundred or a thousand once mature, always a hundred or a thousand for the Buddha); whether near lifetimes can be set aside in favor of more distant ones (again, no at first, yes once mature); how one withdraws from this recollection (gradually or all at once, according to inclination); and whether recollecting the past can immediately give rise to knowledge of future death and rebirth (only the Buddha, since only his own virtues manifest without preparatory effort). A justly famous Vinaya episode then settles exactly how many lifetimes fit within a single moment of this knowledge: the Venerable Śubha, rebuked and nearly expelled for claiming to recollect five hundred lifetimes in a single arising of mind, is defended by the Buddha himself — Śubha had, in fact, recollected a single five-hundred-kalpa lifespan among the percept-less beings, describing it, in accordance with genuine perception, as "five hundred lifetimes" without having committed any offense at all. A parallel question about how many destinies fit within a single moment is settled through the vivid image of a wheel-turning king: recollecting his own ministers, the ghosts who serve him, and his elephants and horses lets a single moment take in the human, hungry-ghost, and animal destinies all at once — and, extended to King Māndhātṛ's own legendary meeting with Śakra, even a fourth, the heavenly destiny, besides. A final passage grades three tiers of eternalist theorists by how many tens of thousands of kalpas their own faculty, or vehicle-disposition, allows them to recollect.

There are those who say: both of the two examples cited above are gathered within the knowledge that comes from hearing speech, or, alternatively, both are gathered within the knowledge that comes from beholding a sign. This being the base treatise's own text, it ought rather to be said: through beholding a sign, or through divination and prognostication, one is capable of presently knowing another's mind and mental concomitants. As for "through beholding a sign": this is exactly as stated before, together with every other case of knowing another's mind through seeing or hearing a sign of bodily or verbal karma. As for "through divination and prognostication": this refers to such cases as the various outside paths' own various methods of divination and prognostication for knowing another's mind. As for presently knowing another's mind and mental concomitants through the knowledge attained upon a certain birth: this refers to such cases as the hell-realms, and the like — what, precisely, is this matter? Even within the hells, too, there exists the knowledge attained upon that same birth, capable of knowing another's mind and the like, yet there exists no separately manifest matter that can be cited to illustrate it. One asks: at what time do they know another's mind and the like? The answer: at the very moment of first being born within the hells, before yet undergoing suffering — once they have already begun to undergo suffering, they are then incapable even of knowing their own mind's own thoughts, how much less capable of knowing another's mind and mental concomitants.

One asks: abiding in what kind of mind do they know another's mind and the like — the wholesome, the defiled, or the neutral-and-unobscured? The answer: all three kinds are capable of knowing it. One asks: is it while abiding in mental consciousness, or while abiding in the five sense-consciousnesses, that they know another's mind and the like? The answer: solely while abiding in mental consciousness. One asks: is it while abiding in a deportment-related mind, a craft-related mind, or a ripening-born mind, that they know another's mind and the like? The answer: solely while abiding in a deportment-related mind — for what reason is this so? They possess no manifest craft-related mind at all, and their own ripening-born mind exists solely within the five sense-consciousnesses.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even a being freshly arrived in hell retains, for a brief window, a capacity to read another mind — a capacity that vanishes the instant genuine suffering begins — is one of the most quietly poignant details in this entire fascicle: it locates a small, residual trace of ordinary cognitive sophistication surviving even at the very threshold of hell's own suffering, only to have that trace extinguished not by any moral or karmic mechanism specific to hell, but by the same simple, universal fact that overwhelming pain crowds out subtler mental functions for any being whatsoever, in any realm.
