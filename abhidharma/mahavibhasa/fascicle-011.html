How Can Anyone Remember, Without a Self?

Fascicle 11 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa's third part (雜蘊第一中智納息第二之三), first settling whether the future possesses an immediately-antecedent condition at all, then working through an exhaustive combinatorial survey of which kinds of mind can precede or follow which other — twelve kinds, then twenty, cross-referenced in full. The fascicle closes with an entirely new and genuinely difficult question: without a persisting self, how is memory of one's own past deeds possible at all? This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation — the seed that could become either a sprout or ash, two travelers separated by miles yet still called “right behind” one another, and eight rival theories of memory surveyed before the Buddha's own deliberate silence on mechanism.

1. Does the Future Possess an Immediately-Antecedent Condition?

Fascicle 11 opens directly where Fascicle 10 left off: if the future lacks this condition, preparatory effort and the suppression of affliction remain meaningful, but two canonical citations seem to demand the opposite — the Views Chapter's own wording, and the puzzle of why supreme mundane dharma is always followed by exactly the same next moment.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: does the immediately-antecedent condition exist within the future time-period? Suppose it does — what fault follows? If it does, then future dharmas ought to abide in a fixed sequence, and correct preparatory effort would then become useless. Why is this? Because, if a given dharma already abides, without interval, in a fixed position relative to another, then that other dharma must necessarily arise from it without interval — what further use, then, would correct preparatory effort serve? Further, there would then be no sense in which the afflictions could be suppressed and their antidote could arise, and this would mean there could be no ultimate liberation at all. Suppose, on the other hand, that it does not exist — how, then, is the statement of the Views Chapter to be reconciled with this? As it states: "if a dharma serves as the immediately-antecedent condition for another dharma, is there ever a time when it does not so serve?" The answer given there: yes — namely, whenever this latter dharma has not yet arrived at having already arisen. 2. The Eightfold Discourse's Challenge, and a Name Fixed Without the Event Being Fixed

A second citation sharpens the puzzle further: how can the discourses speak so precisely about karma ripening across exactly thirteen, fourteen, or twenty aeons, or sort every karmic result into exactly three timed categories, unless the future already has real causal structure? The treatise's resolution distinguishes what is fixed in name and number from what is fixed in actual, particular event.

The answer: the future does not possess an immediately-antecedent condition. One asks: if that is so, why is it that, immediately after supreme mundane dharma, only the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering arises, and nothing else, up through the knowledge of non-arising, ever arises? The answer: this is fixed as to name and number, not fixed as to the actual event and its particular character. Why is this? Because the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering, occurring within six possible stages, when it is not yet known which stage it will occur in, is bound to arise associated with one of three faculties; when it is not yet known which faculty it will be associated with, it is bound to arise with one of four aspects; when it is not yet known which aspect it will have, it is bound to arise at one of countless possible moments; and when it is not yet known at which moment it will arise — well, up through the stage of heightened forbearance, the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering is fixed only with respect to three matters, namely its stage, its faculty, and its aspect, while it remains unfixed with respect to two further matters, namely its particular moment and its immediately-antecedent condition. Only once one abides at supreme mundane dharma itself are all five matters fixed. Further, it is not strictly necessary that an immediately-antecedent condition exist in order for dharmas to arise in continuous succession one after another — why is this? Because, if a given dharma depends and relies upon another dharma, it is bound to arise, without interval, immediately following that other dharma, whereas this is not so for any unrelated dharma — just as, with external things, even without any immediately-antecedent condition, they still arise dependent and reliant upon one another in a fixed sequence, one after another: as with a seed, sprout, stem, branch, twig, flower, and fruit, whatever depends and relies upon a given prior thing arises, without interval, immediately after it, while what does not so depend does not. In just the same way, internal dharmas within the future time-period, even lacking any immediately-antecedent condition, still arise, without interval, immediately after whatever they depend and rely upon, while what does not so depend does not. The forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering depends and relies upon supreme mundane dharma, whereas the dharma-knowledge of suffering and the rest do not depend upon it in the same way — and it is for this reason that, immediately after supreme mundane dharma, only the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering arises, and nothing else, up through the knowledge of non-arising, ever arises. Further, the arising of a future dharma depends upon a present dharma: if the present dharma's own conditions come together, that future dharma is then able to arise; if they do not come together, it does not arise. Although there is, strictly speaking, no fixed "place" involved here, still, for the sake of analysis: suppose the conditions for the arising of the path of cultivation came together first — it, too, ought then to arise first; yet this simply never happens. It is only the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering that arises through the coming-together of conditions dependent upon supreme mundane dharma; the dharma-knowledge of suffering and the rest depend upon a different coming-together of conditions altogether — and it is for this reason that, immediately after supreme mundane dharma, only the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering arises, and nothing else, up through the knowledge of non-arising, ever arises. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The name/event distinction offered here is a genuinely elegant piece of conceptual surgery: the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering is guaranteed to occur next after supreme mundane dharma as a matter of category (there is no alternative slot for it to fall into), while the concrete details — which of six stages, which of three faculties, which of countless possible moments — remain entirely open until the very last instant, when the specifics finally lock into place all at once. It is the difference between knowing a train will arrive at platform one and knowing which specific train, with which specific passengers, will actually pull in.

3. How the Buddha Knows the Future: Direct Perception, Not Inference

A tempting shortcut is offered and rejected: perhaps the Buddha's precise predictions about future karmic ripening are simply careful inferences from patterns observed in the past and present, or read off some hidden bodily mark. The treatise insists on something bolder — the Buddha genuinely sees the chaotic, unordered future directly, with the same clarity as the present, even though it possesses no fixed sequence at all for an ordinary mind to trace.

Chinese Source

The rejected inference-theory is dismissed on a point of principle that matters enormously to how this school understood Buddhahood: reducing the Buddha's foreknowledge to sophisticated pattern-matching from past cases would make his omniscience merely a matter of superior reasoning, no different in kind from an unusually perceptive ordinary mind — whereas the treatise insists on a difference in kind, not degree, positing a mode of perception that reaches directly into an unordered future the way ordinary sight reaches into an ordered present. This is one of the treatise's clearest statements of what it takes actually to distinguish a Buddha's knowledge from every lesser form of prediction.

4. The Verdict: A Fixed Nature, But No Fixed Sequence — and Why Preparatory Effort Still Matters

The treatise's own considered position threads the needle: future dharmas do possess the immediately-antecedent condition's fixed nature (rather like a crowd of monks whose seniority is already settled even before they line up), but not yet any fixed sequence — and a single present mind can still branch toward either of two future outcomes, exactly the way a single seed can become either a sprout or ash depending on which conditions actually come together.

Chinese Source

Correct preparatory effort does not become useless, since a single present mind can have, immediately following it, either of two possible future minds — that is, immediately following a single present mind, there abide, in the future, two possible minds: one wholesome, one defiled. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seed-that-could-become-sprout-or-ash image captures something genuinely subtle about how this school reconciled real causal structure with real freedom: the future is not an empty void waiting to be filled by anything whatsoever, but neither is it a rigid script already written — it holds a determinate menu of possibilities whose final resolution depends on what actually happens in the present, which is exactly the room genuine preparatory effort needs to operate in. Devadatta's fall from a would-be reformer into an infamous schismer, and Aṅgulimāla's reversal from serial murderer to arhat, are offered as living proof that no future moment is ever locked in by the immediately preceding one alone.

5. Why Form and the Dissociated Formations Never Qualify

Two categories of dharma are permanently excluded from ever serving as an immediately-antecedent condition: material form, since it lacks the requisite mental support and can arise in wildly unequal quantities from one moment to the next, and the formations dissociated from mind, disqualified for essentially the same reasons.

Chinese Source
