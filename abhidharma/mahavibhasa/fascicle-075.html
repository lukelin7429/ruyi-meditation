Grasping-Aggregates, Elements, and the Reality of Reflections

Fascicle 75 treats the five grasping-aggregates and the puzzle of fear's own nature, then the six elements — earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness, with a sustained inquiry into whether space itself can be known to exist — before closing with form and formless dharma, visible and invisible dharma, and a final debate over whether mirror-reflections and echoes are genuinely real. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Five Grasping-Aggregates, and the Form-Grasping-Aggregate's Own Definition

Opening a new topic within the same Ten Gates Nyāsa, the treatise turns to the five grasping-aggregates — form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness — composed, as always, to analyze at length a scripture that names them without elaborating. The form-grasping-aggregate is defined as any contaminated, graspable form across the three times that gives rise to desire, craving, hatred, delusion, or fear, or to any secondary affliction riding along with a given mental factor, with desire and craving tied to the fetter of craving, hatred to the fetter of aversion, and delusion to the fetter of ignorance.

Chinese Source

結蘊第二中十門納息第四之五五取蘊者。謂色取蘊受取蘊想取蘊行取蘊識取蘊。問何故作此論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Continuing the Fetters Aggregate within the Ten Gates Nyāsa, part five. As for the five grasping-aggregates: the form-grasping-aggregate, the feeling-grasping-aggregate, the perception-grasping-aggregate, the formations-grasping-aggregate, and the consciousness-grasping-aggregate. One asks: for what reason is this treatise composed?

答為欲分別契經義故。謂契經說。有五取蘊。謂色取蘊乃至識取蘊。契經雖作是說而不廣辯其義。經是此論所依根本。彼不說者今應分別故作斯論。問色取蘊云何。答若色有漏有取。彼色在過去未來現在。或起欲或起貪或起瞋或起癡或起怖。或復隨起一心所隨煩惱是名色取蘊。此中起欲起貪者謂起愛結。起瞋者謂起恚結。起癡者謂起無明結。起怖者有作是說。此中不應說或起怖。所以者何。

The answer: in order to analyze the meaning of a certain scripture. That is, a scripture states that there exist five grasping-aggregates, the form-grasping-aggregate, and so on, up through, the consciousness-grasping-aggregate, and, although it states this much, it does not analyze this at any length — this scripture being the very root upon which this treatise depends, and, since it does not itself state what remains to be stated, this treatise now wishes to analyze it, and it is for this reason that this treatise is composed. One asks: what is the form-grasping-aggregate? The answer: whatever form is contaminated and involves grasping — that form, whether past, future, or present, whether giving rise to desire, to craving, to hatred, to delusion, or to fear, or whether giving rise to any one of the secondary afflictions that follow along with a given mental factor — is named the form-grasping-aggregate. Here, giving rise to desire and to craving refers to giving rise to the fetter of craving; giving rise to hatred refers to giving rise to the fetter of aversion; giving rise to delusion refers to giving rise to the fetter of ignorance. As for giving rise to fear: there are those who say that this ought not to be mentioned as a separate case at all, for what reason?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Tying each of the four ordinary afflictions in this list to one of the classical fetters, while leaving fear to be argued over separately in what follows, already signals that fear occupies a different logical status here than desire, hatred, or delusion — it is not simply a fifth member of the same list, but a phenomenon whose own basic nature the treatise still needs to establish before it can be assigned a place at all.

2. What Is Fear? Three Rival Theories of Its Own Nature

Is fear simply a redundant name for affliction in general, or does it possess some more specific nature of its own? Three masters offer three different candidates — the view of self, craving, and ignorance respectively — each defended by the observation that whoever holds this particular affliction is, for the most part, fearful. The compilers reject all three reductions, insisting that fear is its own distinct mental factor, associated with mind, existing only within the desire realm and nowhere in the two upper realms.

Chinese Source

怖即煩惱。若說煩惱即已說怖。問若爾此怖以何煩惱為自性。有作是說。以有身見為自性。所以者何。執有我者多怖畏故。若說有身見即已說怖。有餘師說。以愛為自性。所以者何。若有愛者多怖畏故。復有說者。以無智為自性。所以者何。諸無智者多怖畏故。若說無明即已說怖。評曰應作是說。此所起中應別說怖。所以者何。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fear is itself simply affliction — to speak of affliction is already to have spoken of fear. One asks: granting this, what affliction serves as this fear's own nature? There are those who say: the view of self serves as its own nature, for what reason? Because whoever grasps at the existence of a self is, for the most part, fearful — to speak of the view of self is already to have spoken of fear. Other masters say: craving serves as its own nature, for what reason? Because whoever possesses craving is, for the most part, fearful. Others, further, say: ignorance serves as its own nature, for what reason? Because whoever is ignorant is, for the most part, fearful — to speak of ignorance is already to have spoken of fear. The compilers say: one should say as follows — among these instances that give rise to affliction, fear ought to be mentioned as a separate case in its own right, for what reason?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That three respected masters each try to dissolve fear into some other, already-recognized affliction, rather than granting it independent status, reveals an economizing instinct running through this whole literature: multiplying mental factors is treated as a cost to be justified, not a default move, so fear has to actively earn its own place in the list rather than simply being handed one.

3. The Radiant Gods' Terror at the Kalpa-Fire, and a Verse on the Long-Lived

If fear belongs to the desire realm alone, how is a scripture's own account to be reconciled — the gods of pure radiant light, witnessing the kalpa-fire consume the empty palaces of Brahmā, giving rise to fear in their own hearts, and being comforted by their elders' assurance that this same fire has always, in the end, been extinguished there? A companion verse, too, speaks of long-lived gods harboring dread at the sight of a lion-like power. Both passages, the treatise answers, use the word "fear" loosely, meaning aversion — a state that, unlike fear proper, does pervade all three realms.

Chinese Source

有別心所與心相應。是怖自性此即攝在。復有所餘如是類法與心相應心所法內非諸煩惱。問此怖自性於何處有。答在欲界有非上二界。問若怖自性色界中無。云何釋通契經所說。如契經說。苾芻當知。有極光淨先生。諸天見後生者覩劫火焰心生恐怖。而慰喻言。

Ru-Yi Translation

There exists a distinct mental factor, associated with mind, that is precisely fear's own nature, gathered here within this very list; there exist, further, other dharmas of this same kind, associated with mind, mental factors that are not themselves any of the afflictions at all. One asks: where does this own-nature of fear exist? The answer: it exists in the desire realm, not in either of the upper two realms. One asks: if fear's own nature does not exist within the form realm, how is a certain scripture's own statement to be reconciled? As the scripture states: monks, you should know, there are gods of pure radiant light, born earlier, who, beholding those born later witness the very flames of the kalpa-fire and give rise to fear in their own hearts, and are comforted with these words:

大仙勿怖大仙勿怖。我數曾見此劫火焰。燒空梵宮即於彼滅。伽他所說。復云何通。如說。

'Great sages, do not be afraid; great sages, do not be afraid — we ourselves have repeatedly witnessed this very kalpa-fire, burning the empty palaces of Brahmā, only to be extinguished right there.' How, then, is a verse's own statement to be reconciled, as it states:

聞說長壽天　有妙色名譽深心懷厭怖　如鹿對師子答經頌於厭以怖聲說。問若爾厭怖有何差別。答名即差別。謂彼名厭此名為怖。尊者世友作如是說。怖唯欲界厭通三界。復作是說。

'Hearing that the long-lived gods, possessing exquisite form and renown, still harbor deep aversion and dread within their own hearts, exactly as a deer before a lion.' The answer: the scripture and the verse both speak of aversion using the term fear. One asks: granting this, what distinction is there between aversion and fear? The answer: they differ in name alone — that one is named aversion, this one is named fear. The Venerable Vasumitra says as follows: fear belongs to the desire realm alone; aversion pervades all three realms. Others, further, say:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Resolving an apparent scriptural contradiction by drawing a technical distinction the ordinary language of the sutra itself does not observe — treating "fear" in these two passages as really meaning "aversion" — is a move this treatise relies on constantly: canonical wording is trusted as authoritative, but never trusted to be terminologically precise in the treatise's own narrower, more exacting sense.

4. Fear Versus Aversion, and the Five Fears No Noble One Feels

Vasumitra locates the difference between fear and aversion in scope — fear confined to the desire realm, aversion spanning all three — while others locate it in moral quality, or say fear may be neutral where aversion is always wholesome, or, as the Great Virtuous One puts it most memorably, that apprehension over some anticipated loss, together with the wish to escape it, is fear, while revulsion toward a loss already suffered is aversion. A further debate over who feels fear at all is settled by distinguishing the ordinary person's fear from the noble one's: the noble one has become free of the five great fears — of not surviving, of ill repute, of timidity before an assembly, of death, and of an evil destiny — yet may still feel lesser, temporary fears, available even to the learner and the non-learner alike, the Buddha alone wholly exempt.

Chinese Source

怖在煩惱品厭在善根品。復作是說。怖通染污無覆無記厭唯是善。

Ru-Yi Translation

fear belongs to the category of affliction; aversion belongs to the category of wholesome roots. Others, further, say: fear may be either defiled or neutral-and-unobscured; aversion is solely wholesome.

大德說曰。於衰損事深心疑慮欲得遠離說名為怖。已得遠離深心憎惡說名為厭。如是名為厭怖差別。問異生聖者誰有怖耶。有作是說。異生有怖聖者無怖。所以者何。聖者已離五怖畏故。五怖畏者。一不活畏。二惡名畏。三怯眾畏。四命終畏。五惡趣畏。評曰應作是說。異生聖者二皆有怖。問聖者已離五種怖畏如何有怖。答聖者雖無五種大怖而有所餘暫時小怖。問何等聖者有餘小怖。為有學位無學位耶。有作是說。唯有學位有餘小怖。以怖唯是煩惱品故。評曰應作是說。學無學位皆容有怖。學謂預流一來不還者。無學謂阿羅漢獨覺除佛世尊。佛無恐怖毛竪等事。於一切法如實通達得無畏故。或復隨起一心所隨煩惱者。謂緣色生諸餘遍行及修所斷餘煩惱等問受取蘊云何。答若受有漏有取。彼受在過去未來現在。

The Great Virtuous One says: with respect to some matter of decline or loss, deep doubt and apprehension, together with the wish to be free of it, is named fear; having already become free of it, deep aversion and revulsion toward it is named aversion — such, then, is the distinction between aversion and fear. One asks: which experiences fear — the ordinary person, or the noble one? There are those who say: the ordinary person experiences fear; the noble one does not, for what reason? Because the noble one has already become free of the five fears. As for the five fears: first, the fear of not surviving; second, the fear of ill repute; third, the fear of timidity before an assembly; fourth, the fear of death; fifth, the fear of an evil destiny. The compilers say: one should say as follows — both the ordinary person and the noble one experience fear. One asks: if the noble one has already become free of the five fears, how could he still experience fear at all? The answer: although the noble one is free of these five great fears, he still experiences certain other, minor, temporary fears. One asks: which noble ones experience these other, minor fears — those at the stage of a learner, or those at the stage of a non-learner? There are those who say: only those at the stage of a learner experience these other, minor fears, since fear belongs solely to the category of affliction. The compilers say: one should say as follows — both the learner's and the non-learner's stage may admit of fear. As for the learner: this refers to the stream-enterer, the once-returner, and the non-returner. As for the non-learner: this refers to the arhat and the solitary-realizer, the Buddha alone excepted, since the Buddha experiences no fear, no trembling, and the like at all, having attained, with respect to every dharma whatsoever, genuine, direct penetration, and thereby fearlessness. As for giving rise to any one of the secondary afflictions that follow along with a given mental factor: this refers to whatever universally pervasive factor and whatever other affliction abandoned by cultivation arises with reference to form. One asks: what is the feeling-grasping-aggregate? The answer: whatever feeling is contaminated and involves grasping — that feeling, whether past, future, or present,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Carving out an exemption for the Buddha alone, even after already granting that arhats and other non-learners can still feel minor fears, draws the sharpest possible line between liberation from affliction in general and the Buddha's own total, direct penetration of every dharma — an arhat's freedom is real but leaves room for small residual apprehensions, while the Buddha's fearlessness is total precisely because nothing whatsoever remains for him to apprehend.

5. The Remaining Four Grasping-Aggregates

The feeling-grasping-aggregate receives the identical definition already given for form, differing only in that its secondary afflictions are those tied to feeling rather than color; the perception-, formations-, and consciousness-grasping-aggregates follow the same pattern by direct analogy, each substituting its own aggregate for the term "form" throughout.

Chinese Source

或起欲或起貪或起瞋或起癡或起怖。或復隨起一心所隨煩惱。是名受取蘊。此中廣釋如前。應知有差別者。謂隨起一隨煩惱中。緣此受生諸餘遍行及見所斷餘非遍行。

Ru-Yi Translation

whether giving rise to desire, to craving, to hatred, to delusion, or to fear, or whether giving rise to any one of the secondary afflictions that follow along with a given mental factor, is named the feeling-grasping-aggregate. Here, this should be explained at length exactly as before, with the one difference that, as for giving rise to any one of the secondary afflictions that follow along with a given mental factor, this refers to whatever universally pervasive factor and whatever other affliction abandoned by seeing, not itself a universally pervasive factor, arises with reference to this feeling.

如受取蘊如是想行及識取蘊廣說。應知是名取蘊自性我物自體相分本性。

Exactly as with the feeling-grasping-aggregate, so too should the perception-, formations-, and consciousness-grasping-aggregates be stated at full length, in the corresponding manner. Such, then, is named the own-nature of the various grasping-aggregates — their own substance, own character, and inherent nature.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Stating the feeling-grasping-aggregate's definition in full while merely gesturing at the remaining three by analogy is itself a small piece of evidence about which distinctions this treatise judges load-bearing: once the underlying logical form of the definition has been established once, in detail, repeating it four more times in full would add length without adding content.

6. Eleven Reasons for the Name "Grasping-Aggregate"

Why "grasping-aggregate" at all? The treatise offers a cascade of near-synonymous glosses — arising from grasping and capable of giving rise to it; turned by grasping and capable of turning it; drawn forth, nourished, grown, increased, and caused to flow by grasping and capable of the same in return; belonging to grasping exactly as a minister belongs to a king, so that any contaminated formation, asked to whom it belongs, must answer "to grasping"; serving as the very place where grasping arises, abides, and takes hold, grows, and is nourished; clinging here as dust clings to a stained garment, or as fish and turtles delight in a river's pool; and, finally, serving as the very nest and dwelling from which craving, hatred, delusion, conceit, view, and doubt are all able to grow. This naming is understood strictly by same-class grasping across realm and ground, though a single continuum may still admit some confusion, since even an external object may be named a grasping-aggregate with reference to another sentient being's grasping of it, the two mutually nourishing one another.

Chinese Source

已說自性。所以今當說。問何故名取蘊。取蘊是何義。答此從取生復能生取故名取蘊。復次此從取轉復能轉取故名取蘊。復次此由取引復能引取故名取蘊。復次此由取長養復能長養取故名取蘊。復次此由取增廣復能增廣取故名取蘊。復次此由取流派復能流派取故名取蘊。復次此蘊屬取故名取蘊。如臣屬王故名王臣。諸有漏行都無有我。設有問言。汝屬於誰。應正答言。我屬於取。復次諸取於此應生時生。應住時住。應執時執。故名取蘊。復次諸取於此增長廣大故名取蘊。復次諸取於此長養攝受故名取蘊。復次諸取於此染著難捨。猶如塵垢染著衣服故名取蘊。復次諸取於此深生樂著。如魚鼈等樂著河池故名取蘊。復次此是諸取巢穴舍宅故名取蘊。謂依此故貪瞋癡慢見疑纏垢。皆得生長。應知此中依同分取立取蘊名。謂依欲界取名欲界取蘊。依色界取名色界取蘊。依無色界取名無色界取蘊。如依三界同分取立取蘊名。依九地取應知亦爾。此於界地無雜亂故。若於相續容有雜亂。謂依自取他蘊名取蘊亦依他取自蘊名取蘊。若於相續無雜亂者。一切外物應非取蘊。以外物中無諸取故。然諸外物依有情取立取蘊名互生長故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having already spoken of the own-nature of the grasping-aggregates, the very reason for this shall now be stated. One asks: for what reason is it named a grasping-aggregate? What is the meaning of a grasping-aggregate? The answer: this arises from grasping, and is, further, capable of giving rise to grasping, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, this turns from grasping, and is, further, capable of turning grasping, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, this is drawn forth by grasping, and is, further, capable of drawing forth grasping, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, this is nourished and grown by grasping, and is, further, capable of nourishing and growing grasping, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, this is increased and enlarged by grasping, and is, further, capable of increasing and enlarging grasping, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, this flows forth from grasping, and is, further, capable of causing grasping to flow forth, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, this aggregate belongs to grasping, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate — exactly as a minister belongs to a king and is therefore named the king's own minister — every contaminated formation possessing no self whatsoever, so that, were one to ask, to whom do you belong, one ought properly to answer: I belong to grasping. Further, whatever grasping arises here, arises precisely when it ought to arise, abides precisely when it ought to abide, and takes hold precisely when it ought to take hold, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, whatever grasping grows and enlarges itself here does so, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, whatever grasping is nourished and embraced here is so, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, whatever grasping clings here, defiled and difficult to relinquish, exactly as dust clings, staining a garment, is so, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, whatever grasping deeply delights and clings here, exactly as fish and turtles delight in clinging to a river or a pool, is so, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, this is the very nest and dwelling of every grasping, and it is for this reason that it is named a grasping-aggregate — that is, it is by relying upon this that craving, hatred, delusion, conceit, view, and the entanglements and stains of doubt are all able to grow. It should be understood that, here, the name grasping-aggregate is established with reference to a same-class grasping — that is, with reference to the grasping of the desire realm, it is named the desire realm's own grasping-aggregate; with reference to the grasping of the form realm, it is named the form realm's own grasping-aggregate; with reference to the grasping of the formless realm, it is named the formless realm's own grasping-aggregate. Exactly as the name grasping-aggregate is established with reference to a same-class grasping across the three realms, so too should it be understood, likewise, with reference to the nine grounds. This holds without any confusion at all with respect to realm and ground; yet, with respect to a single continuum, some confusion may indeed be admitted — that is, with reference to one's own grasping, another's own aggregate may be named a grasping-aggregate, and, with reference to another's own grasping, one's own aggregate may likewise be named a grasping-aggregate. Were no confusion at all admitted with respect to a single continuum, then every external object whatsoever ought not to count as a grasping-aggregate, since no genuine grasping exists among external objects — and yet these external objects, too, receive the name grasping-aggregate with reference to a sentient being's own grasping, since the two mutually nourish and grow one another.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king's-minister analogy carries a striking metaphysical claim past almost unnoticed: every contaminated formation, asked to whom it belongs, has only one honest answer — to grasping, since no genuine self exists to claim ownership instead. The whole elaborate cascade of near-synonyms is really one sustained argument that a formation's very identity as "grasping-aggregate" already forecloses the person-view this treatise is everywhere concerned to refute.

7. Aggregate Versus Grasping-Aggregate, and the Six Elements Newly Opened

An aggregate and a grasping-aggregate differ in five ways: the former spans both contaminated and undefiled dharma, the latter only the contaminated; the former is gathered within three truths, the latter within two; the former circulates and may return to cessation, the latter only ever circulates. A wholly new topic then opens — the six elements, earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness — composed, the treatise first insists, simply because the treatise's own author wished it so, since a master expounding the true character of dharmas ought never to be interrogated on his own motives.

Chinese Source

問蘊與取蘊有何差別。答名即差別彼名為蘊此名取蘊。復次蘊通有漏無漏。取蘊唯有漏。復次蘊攝三諦取蘊攝二諦。復次蘊攝十七界一界少分。取蘊攝十五界三界少分。復次蘊攝十一處一處少分。取蘊攝十處二處少分。復次蘊攝五蘊取蘊攝五蘊各少分。復次於蘊中有流轉者受訶責。有還滅者受讚歎。於取蘊中有流轉者受訶責。無還滅者受讚歎。蘊與取蘊是謂差別六界者。謂地界水界火界風界空界識界。問何故作此論。答是作論者意欲爾故。謂本論師隨自意欲而作此論不違法相故不應責。復次不應詰問此本論師。所以者何。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: what distinction is there between an aggregate and a grasping-aggregate? The answer: they differ in name — that one is named an aggregate, this one is named a grasping-aggregate. Further, an aggregate spans both the contaminated and the undefiled; a grasping-aggregate is solely contaminated. Further, an aggregate is gathered within three of the truths; a grasping-aggregate is gathered within two of the truths. Further, an aggregate is gathered within seventeen elements, together with a portion of one further element; a grasping-aggregate is gathered within fifteen elements, together with a portion of three further elements. Further, an aggregate is gathered within eleven sense-fields, together with a portion of one further sense-field; a grasping-aggregate is gathered within ten sense-fields, together with a portion of two further sense-fields. Further, an aggregate is gathered within all five aggregates; a grasping-aggregate is gathered within a portion of each of the five aggregates. 11. The Space-Element: Color Adjacent to an Obstruction

Scripture locates the space-element in the eye-socket, the ear-canal, the throat, and every other bodily cavity and pore; the Abhidharma instead defines it more technically as color adjacent to an obstruction — the space between walls, between the leaves of a thicket, of a window or a passageway — since form itself divides into what is easily removed (the sentient) and what is difficult to remove (the insentient, like a wall or a tree), and the space-element's own color lies characteristically near the latter. Both this country's masters and the earlier Abhidharma-masters agree this color exists everywhere, day and night alike, though only some hold that eye-consciousness can actually take it as an object — its clarity obscured, when it is, only by the alternating cover of daylight and darkness rather than by any deficiency in the eye itself.

14. How Is Space Itself Known to Exist? A Question Asked but Answered by Its Own Element

The compilers reject this dissent outright, insisting that space itself genuinely exists, serving, though itself without any function of its own, as the proximate dominant condition through which space-elements condition the great elements, the great elements condition derived matter, and derived matter finally conditions mind and its mental factors — a chain of causation, they warn, that could not otherwise be established at all.

15. The Consciousness-Element, and Why the Undefiled Is Excluded

The consciousness-element comprises the five sense-consciousness-bodies together with contaminated mind-consciousness alone — undefiled mind-consciousness is deliberately excluded, since the six elements nourish, sustain, and uphold every state of existence while the undefiled diminishes and dissolves it; the six elements sustain the ongoing cycle of birth, aging, sickness, and death, while the undefiled runs counter to it; and the six elements serve as the very matter of self-view, craving, and the latent tendencies, falling within the truths of suffering and its accumulation, while the undefiled again stands opposed. 18. Desire Realm or Form Realm — Which Possesses More Form?

Measured by location, the desire realm possesses more form, since its own form gathers two whole sense-fields plus a portion of nine others, against the form realm's mere portion of nine; but measured by substance, the form realm possesses more form, since its own bodies and dwelling-places are vastly larger — the Prajñaptiśāstra offering as evidence that the distance from this world up to the Heaven of Brahmā's Own Multitude equals the distance from there up to the Heaven of Brahmā's Own Ministers, and so on in matched intervals all the way to the Heaven of the Ultimate Limit of Form.

19. Visible and Invisible Dharmas

A further pair, visible and invisible dharma, is composed for the same twin purposes as before, plus a third: refuting the Venerable Ghoṣa's claim that every dharma whatsoever is visible, being the very domain of wisdom's own eye. Only the color-sense-field itself counts as visible; every remaining sense-field, eleven in all, is invisible. Vasumitra defines the visible as whatever can be manifested and pointed to as being here or there; the Great Virtuous One as whatever is illuminated by, functions for, and is the domain of the eye; the Venerable Pārśva as whatever possesses a clear, evident reflected image capable of being seen.

21. Are Mirror-Reflections and Echoes Genuinely Real?

A closing epistemological puzzle: the Dārṣṭāntikas deny that a mirror's reflected image or a valley's echo can be genuinely real, since the face never enters the mirror nor the mirror the face, and a sound perishes the very instant it arises, unable to travel anywhere to produce an echo at all. The Abhidharma masters affirm both are genuinely real precisely because each is actually seen or heard, taken as object by eye- or ear-consciousness, and gathered within its own sense-field — the causes and conditions that generate a color or a sound admitting many distinct principles, not just one, exactly as water is genuinely generated by moonlight and a moon-loving gem, or fire by a sun-loving gem or by a fire-drill and human effort, without any object literally entering into another.
