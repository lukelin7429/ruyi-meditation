Nothing Knows Itself

Fascicle 9 finishes what Fascicle 8 began, completing the eight-view survey (雜蓋第一中世第一法納息第一之八) with non-self-as-self, causal error, and existential denial — and with it, the entire investigation running continuously since Fascicle 2 finally closes. Then, without any narrative pause, an entirely new nyāsa opens mid-fascicle: the Knowledge Nyāsa (智納息第二), asking whether a single knowledge, or a single consciousness, can ever know all dharmas at once. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation — the farmer crediting Śiva-Mahādeva for his harvest, the skeptic's verse denying any maker of rivers and mountains, the Dharma Master's own resolution of why space and cessation lie beyond wrong view's reach, and an extended, genuinely rigorous epistemological argument that nothing — not a fingertip, not a blade, not an eye, not a lamp — can ever act upon itself the way it acts on something else.

1. Non-Self as Self, and Two Kinds of Self

Fascicle 9 continues the eight-view survey from Fascicle 8 with its natural counterpart to identity-view: regarding what has no self as self is identity-view itself, restated here under its own heading, and the treatise pauses to explain why dharma-self (the real, momentary existence of dharmas) is not an evil view while person-self (a permanent, unified self) is.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between dharma-self and pudgala-self is one of the single most consequential moves in all of Sarvāstivāda philosophy: it lets the school affirm, without contradiction, that dharmas are “real” (a claim its Sautrāntika and Mahāyāna critics would later spend centuries attacking) while still denying any real, unified, persisting person underneath the aggregates. The going/coming puzzle outsiders raise here — without a self, who walks, who sits, who remembers? — is essentially the same challenge Buddhist philosophy has always had to answer with dependent origination and functional continuity rather than a persisting substance, and this fascicle treats it as settled business rather than an open problem.

2. A Non-Cause Mistaken for a Cause: Īśvara and the Farmer's Harvest

Two kinds of self are distinguished, and the eight-view survey turns to causal error: outsiders who credit Īśvara, self-nature, puruṣa, time, or space for a good harvest or the birth of a child are not denying causation outright but misattributing it — precept-and-vow-grasping, abandoned by seeing suffering, distinct from an outright denial of causality.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The specific deities named here — Śiva-Mahādeva for an abundant harvest, Nanda for a child born to the pious, Viṣṇu and Kubera for a child born to Īśvara-devotees — read as a genuine ethnographic snapshot of the folk theism Kashmiri Vaibhāṣika scholastics were arguing against in the field, not abstractions. The treatise's own criterion for why this counts as precept-and-vow-grasping rather than wrong view is worth noting: the error still “operates by way of an aspect” (crediting some cause, just the wrong one) rather than denying causation's aspect altogether, which is exactly the boundary Vasumitra draws to keep the five views' categories from bleeding into one another.

3. A True Cause Denied Outright: The Skeptic's Verse

The mirror-image error follows: outsiders who deny any cause whatsoever behind rivers, mountains, thorns, or the patterns on animals compose their own memorable verse denying any “sovereign lord” at all — a genuine wrong view this time, abandoned by seeing origination rather than suffering, since it strikes at causation itself rather than merely misidentifying it.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This nihilistic verse — no maker of rivers, mountains, thorns, or beasts, therefore nothing has a cause at all — is presented as the polar opposite of the previous section's theistic overattribution, and the treatise is careful to classify them differently even though a casual reader might lump both together as “wrong ideas about causation.” The distinction tracks something real: crediting the wrong cause (Īśvara) still affirms that dependent origination has *some* answer, while denying causation altogether attacks the very structure the four truths depend on, which is why only the second qualifies as wrong view proper.

4. Six Ways Two Statements Differ, and Four Kinds of Denying the Truths

A cross-reference discrepancy between this treatise and the Views Chapter — over whether slandering the cause is abandoned by seeing origination alone or by both origination and the path — triggers a six-part harmonization effort, before the eight-view survey turns to outsiders who deny the four noble truths outright, tracing each denial back to a prior commitment to a permanent self.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The six-fold harmonization offered here (provisional versus definitive meaning, further versus no further intention, and so on) is a stock toolkit this treatise reaches for whenever two of its own citations appear to conflict, and its sheer repetition across many fascicles suggests it functioned as genuine methodological infrastructure for the compilers rather than an ad hoc excuse — a real, reusable protocol for reconciling apparently discordant canonical statements without simply declaring one of them wrong. The chain of reasoning from self-belief to truth-denial (the self is not suffering, has no cause, is permanent, needs no antidote) traces exactly the same four-truth structure this reasoning is meant to refute, an elegant demonstration that identity-view is the root error from which every other wrong view in this survey ultimately branches.

7. Two Kinds of Wrong Cognition — and an Investigation Spanning Eight Fascicles Comes to a Close

The wrong-cognition discussion refines into defiled and undefiled varieties, distinguished by whether ignorance accompanies them and by who can eliminate them — śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas cut off the defiled kind entirely but the undefiled kind still occasionally manifests even in them, while only a Buddha eliminates both together with their habitual residue. With one brief reconciling line, the entire nyāsa section that opened in Fascicle 2 — the four nirvedhabhāgīya roots, then identity-view's twenty statements, then this eight-view survey of causal and existential error — finally closes.

Fascicle 9 Complete

This closes fascicle 9 of Ru-Yi's translation, drawn from the treatise's true CBETA boundary — the repeated second title line that follows carries no further content in this fascicle and has simply been omitted. Fascicle 9 carried two distinct achievements: it completed the eight-view survey of causal and existential error that Fascicle 8 opened — non-self mistaken for self, a non-cause mistaken for a cause, a true cause denied outright, and the four-fold denial of the noble truths — thereby closing an investigation that has run continuously since Fascicle 2's own opening discussion of the four nirvedhabhāgīya roots. And, mid-fascicle, with no narrative transition at all, it opened an entirely new nyāsa — the Knowledge Nyāsa — with one of this treatise's most rigorous sustained epistemological arguments: that no cognition, whether called knowledge or consciousness, can ever know its own nature, its associated mental factors, or its co-existent physical basis, refuting four rival schools (the Mahāsāṃghikas, Dharmaguptakas, Mahīśāsakas, and Vātsīputrīyas) along the way through the images of a knife that cannot cut itself, an eye that cannot see itself, and a lamp that cannot illuminate itself.
