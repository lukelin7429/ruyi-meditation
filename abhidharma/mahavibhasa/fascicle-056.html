A Beautiful Woman Enters the Assembly

Fascicle 56 opens the Fetters Aggregate's second nyāsa, the Single-Class-of-Conduct, distinguishing five senses of "matter" before turning to its central question: which afflictions bind the very same object? A beautiful woman entering an assembly draws respect from her children, craving from the lustful, hatred from her enemies, jealousy, disgust, compassion, and equanimity from seven different observers — cited to argue the bound object isn't real at all, and refuted in turn. Eleven independent reasons explain why the higher realms possess no fetter of hatred whatsoever, before the treatise maps, tetralemma by tetralemma, exactly how far craving, hatred, view, doubt, grasping, envy, and conceit each reach relative to one another. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Five Kinds of “Matter”

The Single-Class-of-Conduct Nyāsa opens by distinguishing five senses of "matter" (事, vastu) buried within a single technical question — for a given matter bound by the fetter of craving, is it likewise bound by the fetter of hatred? The matter of own-nature, the matter of the object, the matter of binding, the matter of cause, and the matter of embracing each receive their own scriptural grounding, down to a verse on a householder's own grasping possessiveness over field, house, and wealth, before the treatise declares that this whole discourse will proceed with reference solely to the matter of binding.

Ru-Yi Translation

There are nine fetters — namely, the fetter of craving, up through the fetter of stinginess. For a given matter that is bound by the fetter of craving, is it likewise bound by the fetter of hatred? Such chapter-headings, and the explanation of their meaning, having already been grasped, ought next to be explained at length. Here, ‘matter’ is of five kinds — first, the matter of own-nature; second, the matter of the object; third, the matter of binding; fourth, the matter of cause; fifth, the matter of embracing. The matter of own-nature is exemplified by what the Consciousness Aggregate states: if a matter is capable of penetrating, is that matter thereby capable of full comprehension? Suppose a matter is capable of full comprehension — is that matter thereby capable of penetrating? — there, the very own-nature of the forbearances and the cognitions is expressed by the word ‘matter’; and, further, it states: if a matter has already been attained, is that matter possessed? Suppose a matter is possessed — has that matter already been attained? There are those who say: the own-nature of every dharma whatsoever is expressed by the word ‘matter.’ There are those who say: whatever dharma is capable of being attained is expressed by the word ‘matter.’ The matter of the object is exemplified by what the Prakaraṇapāda states: every dharma whatsoever is known by cognition, in accordance with its own matter — what is meant by ‘in accordance with its own matter’? Whatever dharma is the very object upon which that cognition operates is, with respect to that object, expressed by the word ‘matter.’ Yet scripture states: I shall now teach you the forty-four matters of wisdom, and the seventy-seven matters of wisdom — the various Ābhidharma treatise-masters state: that scripture speaks of the matter of own-nature, that is, the forbearances and cognitions that take the limbs of existence as their object are expressed by the word ‘matter.’ The Venerable Ghoṣa states as follows: that scripture speaks of the matter of the object, that is, the limbs of existence that the forbearances and cognitions take as their object are expressed by the word ‘matter.’ The matter of binding is exemplified by what is stated here: for a given matter that is bound by the fetter of craving, is it likewise bound by the fetter of hatred? — and so on, at length. Here, the dharmas of the five sections are expressed by the word ‘matter,’ that is, the dharmas abandoned by seeing suffering, its arising, its cessation, the path, and by cultivation — these being the very matter bound by the five sections' own affliction, and are therefore named the matter of binding. The matter of cause is exemplified by what the Prakaraṇapāda states: what is a dharma possessing a matter? What is a dharma without a matter? — its own intent stating this as the dharma possessing a cause and the dharma without a cause. Further, a verse states:

苾芻心寂靜　能永斷諸事
彼生死盡故　不受於後有
彼頌意說。一切生死皆依於因。有因故有生死。因斷故生死盡。由
此不復受未來三有生攝受事者。如契經說。應捨田事宅事財事攝受
之心。應離田事宅事財事攝受之業。又伽他說。

A monk whose own mind is stilled
is capable of forever cutting off every matter;
since his own birth and death is thereby exhausted,
he undergoes no further existence.

The verse's own intent states: all birth and death depends entirely upon cause; because there is a cause, there is birth and death; because the cause is cut off, birth and death is exhausted, and, on this very account, one no longer undergoes future rebirth within the three existences. The matter of embracing is exemplified by what scripture states: one ought to relinquish the mind that embraces the matter of one's fields, the matter of one's house, and the matter of one's wealth; one ought to abandon the karma that embraces the matter of one's fields, the matter of one's house, and the matter of one's wealth. Further, a verse states:

若於田事財　牛馬等僮僕
男女親別欲　是人名極貪
又在家者作如是言。我攝此事我持此事。諸如是等名攝受事。復有
五事。一界事。二處事。三蘊事。四世事。五剎那事。於此十事。

Whoever, with respect to the matter of fields, wealth,
oxen, horses, servants, and the rest,
sons, daughters, and kin, holds separate desire —
such a person is named one of the utmost greed.

Further, a householder speaks in this way: I embrace this matter, I hold this matter — such things as these are named the matter of embracing. There are further five matters — first, the matter of realm; second, the matter of sense-field; third, the matter of aggregate; fourth, the matter of time; fifth, the matter of moment. Among these ten matters,

此中但依繫事作論。不依餘九。

this discourse is composed with reference solely to the matter of binding, not with reference to the remaining nine.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the treatise pauses to enumerate five entirely different technical senses of a single ordinary word before allowing itself to ask its actual question is a small but telling methodological habit running through this whole translation project: a term that sounds unambiguous in ordinary speech routinely turns out to be doing five separate jobs across different discourses, and skipping the disambiguation would let the same word smuggle five different arguments past an unwary reader.

2. Is the Bound Object Real, or Merely Designated?

Three schools stake out three distinct metaphysical positions on what's real in the binding relationship itself: the Ābhidharmikas hold the bound matter and the binding fetter both real, the person a mere designation; the Vātsīputrīyas hold all three genuinely real, including the person; the Dārṣṭāntikas hold the fetter real but both the matter and the person mere designations, arguing from a striking case — a beautiful woman entering an assembly draws respect from her children, desire from the lustful, hatred from her enemies, jealousy from her co-wives, disgust from meditators on impurity, compassion from sages, and equanimity from arhats, proving, they claim, that no object possesses any fixed, real character of its own.

Ru-Yi Translation

The various Ābhidharma treatise-masters state: the matter bound is real, and the fetter that binds is likewise real, while the person is a mere designation. The Vātsīputrīyas state: the matter bound is real, the fetter that binds is likewise real, and the person, too, is real. The Dārṣṭāntikas state: the fetter that binds is real, while the matter bound is a mere designation, and the person, too, is a mere designation. One asks: for what reason do they state that the matter bound is a mere designation? The answer: they state that, since whether an object is defiling or non-defiling is not fixed, the object is thereby known not to be real — that is, suppose there is a beautiful woman, adorned in every manner, who comes into an assembly: some, seeing her, give rise to respect; some, seeing her, give rise to desire; some, seeing her, give rise to hatred; some, seeing her, give rise to jealousy; some, seeing her, give rise to disgust; some, seeing her, give rise to compassion; some, seeing her, give rise to equanimity. One should understand that, here, her own children, seeing her, give rise to respect; those indulging in desire, seeing her, give rise to craving; her enemies, seeing her, give rise to hatred; her co-wives, seeing her, give rise to jealousy; those who cultivate the contemplation of impurity, seeing her, give rise to disgust;

諸離欲仙見起悲慜。作如是念。此妙色相不久當為無常所滅。諸阿
羅漢見而生捨。由此故知境無實體。彼說非理。所以者何。若境非
實。應不作緣生心心所。若爾應無染淨品法。補特伽羅定非實有。

sages already free of desire, seeing her, give rise to compassion, thinking to themselves: this wondrous form and appearance will, before long, be destroyed by impermanence; arhats, seeing her, give rise to equanimity — and it is on this very account that the object is known to possess no real substance. This statement is not tenable — for what reason is this so? Were the object not real, it could not serve as the condition giving rise to mind and mental factors; and, were this so, there would be no defiled or purified dharma whatsoever. The person is certainly not really existent,

佛說無我無我所故。

since the Buddha teaches that there is no self and nothing belonging to a self.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The beautiful-woman simile is a remarkably early anticipation of a genuinely modern philosophical worry — that an object's own properties dissolve once you notice how radically its perceived character shifts with the perceiver — and the treatise's own refutation is correspondingly sharp: an object need not possess one single, fixed reaction built into it to be real, since if it were entirely unreal it could never have served as a condition for any of these seven very different, very real minds arising in the first place.

3. Binding Across the Six Consciousnesses

The treatise maps how affliction binds its object differently depending on which of the six consciousnesses it is associated with — an affliction tied to one of the five sense-consciousnesses binds only the matter belonging to its own tense (past binds past, present binds present), while an affliction tied to mental consciousness alone is capable of binding matter across all three times at once; each sense-consciousness's own affliction constitutes an object-binding solely with respect to its own sense-field, yet an associated-binding with respect to the mind and dharma sense-fields it shares.

去事。若在現在繫現在事。若在未來生法者繫未來事。不生法者繫

繫。耳識相應煩惱於聲處作所緣繫。於彼相應意處法處作相應繫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some afflictions are associated with the five sense-consciousnesses; some are associated with the mental consciousness. As for those associated with the five sense-consciousnesses: should the affliction be past, it binds the past matter; should it be present, it binds the present matter; should it be future and destined to arise, it binds the future matter; should it be future and not destined to arise, it binds the matter of all three times. As for those associated with the mental consciousness: whether past, future, or present, they are all capable of binding the matter of all three times. Further, the affliction associated with eye-consciousness constitutes an object-binding with respect to the form sense-field, and constitutes an associated-binding with respect to its own associated mind sense-field and dharma sense-field. The affliction associated with ear-consciousness constitutes an object-binding with respect to the sound sense-field, and constitutes an associated-binding with respect to its own associated mind sense-field and dharma sense-field.

The affliction associated with nose-consciousness constitutes an object-binding with respect to the smell sense-field, and constitutes an associated-binding with respect to its own associated mind sense-field and dharma sense-field. The affliction associated with tongue-consciousness constitutes an object-binding with respect to the taste sense-field, and constitutes an associated-binding with respect to its own associated mind sense-field and dharma sense-field. The affliction associated with body-consciousness constitutes an object-binding with respect to the touch sense-field, and constitutes an associated-binding with respect to its own associated mind sense-field and dharma sense-field. The affliction associated with mental consciousness constitutes an object-binding with respect to all twelve sense-fields, and constitutes an associated-binding with respect to its own associated mind sense-field and dharma sense-field. This, then, is the brief exegesis of the Single-Class-of-Conduct. For a given matter that is bound by the fetter of craving, is it likewise bound by the fetter of hatred? The answer: whatever matter is bound by the fetter of hatred is certainly bound by the fetter of craving; there are also matters bound by the fetter of craving yet not bound by the fetter of hatred — that is, with respect to the form and formless realms' own dharmas, craving remains uncut. Here, the fetter of craving spans all three realms and all five sections, taking solely a contaminated object, not universally pervading; the fetter of hatred belongs solely to the desire realm, spanning all five sections, taking solely a contaminated object, not universally pervading. For one fully bound, with respect to the desire realm's own five-sectioned matter: whatever is bound by craving is likewise bound by hatred, and whatever is bound by hatred is likewise bound by craving; with respect to the form and formless realms' own five-sectioned matter, there is binding by craving but not by hatred. For one not fully bound, with respect to the desire realm's own five-sectioned matter: wherever craving remains uncut, that matter is bound by craving and is likewise bound by hatred; wherever craving has already been cut off, that matter is bound by neither. With respect to the form and formless realms' own five-sectioned matter: wherever craving remains uncut, that matter is bound by craving but not by hatred; wherever craving has already been cut off, that matter is bound by neither — since craving is the longer-reaching, and hatred the shorter-reaching, of the two. The question posed ought therefore to be answered by the reverse of its own clause: whatever matter is bound by hatred is certainly bound by craving — that is, with respect to the desire realm's own five-sectioned matter not yet entirely cut off; there are also matters bound by craving yet not bound by hatred — that is, with respect to the form and formless realms' own dharmas, craving remains uncut. Here, there may be eight grounds not yet cut off, up through the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception alone not yet cut off; within a given ground, there may be all five sections not yet cut off, up through solely what is abandoned by cultivation not yet cut off; within a given section, there may be all nine grades not yet cut off, up through solely the very lowest grade not yet cut off — the word ‘uncut’ here being spoken in a general sense. One asks: for what reason do the form and formless realms' own five-sectioned dharmas possess no binding by the fetter of hatred?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That mental consciousness alone can reach across all three times while the five sense-consciousnesses remain locked to their own tense is a genuinely precise structural claim about the difference between direct sensory contact and conceptual thought: seeing, hearing, and the rest are inherently bound to the present-tense encounter that produces them, while thinking about an object carries no such restriction, and can range freely across what has already ceased, what is now occurring, and what has not yet arisen.

4. Why the Higher Realms Have No Fetter of Hatred

A single question receives an extraordinary cascade of eleven independent answers: why do the form and formless realms possess no fetter of hatred at all? Because they aren't a suitable vessel for it; because yogins who seek those realms specifically to escape hatred would have no reason to if hatred simply followed them there; because hatred depends on a coarse, rough mental continuum those realms' own subtle, smooth continuum cannot support; because it depends on the pain and displeasure faculties, on shamelessness, on envy and stinginess, on the male and female faculties, on craving for solid food, on sexual craving, on the five hindrances, on the five sensual pleasures, and on the marks of enmity — every one of which those realms simply lack.

5. Craving and Conceit: An Exact Match

After the extended detour through hatred, the treatise returns to its main technical apparatus with the simplest possible case: craving and conceit turn out to bind exactly the same matter in exactly the same way, span all three realms and all five sections identically, and share the very same reach — so whatever is bound by one is, without exception, bound by the other, and the entire tetralemma collapses into a single, symmetric answer.

6. Craving and Ignorance, Craving and View

Craving and ignorance turn out asymmetric — whatever is bound by craving is certainly bound by ignorance, since ignorance's own reach (spanning both contaminated and uncontaminated objects, both universally-pervading and not) simply exceeds craving's narrower one.
