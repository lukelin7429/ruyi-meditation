Five Aggregates, Newly Opened

Fascicle 74 completes the twelve sense-fields — the raft of the eightfold noble path, the eight bases of mastery, and Śāriputra's own famous praise of the Buddha — then opens the five aggregates: their careful, thrice-stated definitions, six converging reasons for their fixed order, why only the formations aggregate and only feeling and perception bear their own exclusive names, and the true measure of the Buddha's eighty thousand dharma-aggregates. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Are Sense-Fields Established by Dharma or by Self? A Verse on Subduing the Mind

Opening part four of the Ten Gates Nyāsa, the treatise asks whether the inner and outer sense-fields are established with reference to dharma or with reference to a self — both options seemingly impossible, since a dharma has no function of its own to ground such a distinction, while a self has no genuine nature at all. The settled answer grounds them in dharma after all, but selectively: whatever serves as the six consciousnesses' own basis is inner, whatever serves as their object is outer. An alternative view locates the ground in a provisional "self" identified with mind itself, cited to a verse urging that subduing the mind is precisely subduing the self.

Chinese Source

結蘊第二中十門納息第四之四問云何建立內處外處。為依於法為依於我。設爾何失。二俱有過。

Ru-Yi Translation

Continuing the Fetters Aggregate within the Ten Gates Nyāsa, part four. One asks: how are the inner and outer sense-fields established — with reference to dharma, or with reference to a self? Whichever position is granted, what fault follows? Both alternatives carry a fault.

所以者何。若依於法法無作用。於無作用一切法中。云何建立內處外處。若依於我我實性無。如何依我立內外處。答唯依法立然非一切。謂六識身是染淨法所依止處。若與六識作所依者名為內處。作所緣者名為外處。故依法立內外處名。復次若法是根立為內處。若法是根義立為外處。復次若法是有境立為內處。若法是境立為外處。有說。依我立內外處。我即是心。我執依故。於此心上假立我名。如契經說。

For what reason is this so? If established with reference to dharma, a dharma possesses no function of its own, and, among every dharma whatsoever, all alike devoid of function, how could the inner and outer sense-fields be established at all? If established with reference to a self, a self possesses no genuine nature at all, so how could the inner and outer sense-fields be established with reference to a self? The answer: they are established solely with reference to dharma, yet not with reference to every dharma — that is, the six consciousness-bodies are precisely the very place defiled and pure dharmas rely upon; whatever serves as the basis of the six consciousnesses is named an inner sense-field; whatever serves as their object is named an outer sense-field — and it is for this reason that the names inner and outer sense-field are established with reference to dharma. Further, whatever dharma is a faculty is established as an inner sense-field; whatever dharma is the meaning of a faculty's own object is established as an outer sense-field. Further, whatever dharma possesses an object is established as an inner sense-field; whatever dharma is itself an object is established as an outer sense-field. There are those who say: they are established with reference to a self — this self being precisely mind itself, since self-grasping relies upon it, this provisional name of 'self' being established with reference to this very mind, as the scripture states:

由善調伏我　智者得生天應善調伏心　心調能引樂既善調心即善調我。故知心上假立我名。此我所依立為內處。我所緣者立為外處。然內外名非圓成實。謂於我是內者於他名外。於我是外者於他名內故。而且依一立內外名非不決定。

'By well subduing the self, the wise attain rebirth among the gods; one should well subdue the mind, for a subdued mind draws forth true happiness.' Since well subduing the mind is precisely well subduing the self, it is thereby known that the provisional name 'self' is established with reference to mind itself — and whatever this self relies upon is established as an inner sense-field, while whatever it takes as its object is established as an outer sense-field. Yet the names inner and outer are not themselves fixed and complete in every respect — that is, whatever is inner with reference to oneself is named outer with reference to another, and whatever is outer with reference to oneself is named inner with reference to another — and so, even while established with reference to this one single principle, the names inner and outer are not thereby rendered fully determinate.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The verse's own quiet substitution — reading "subdue the self" as nothing more than "subdue the mind" — is doing real philosophical work: it lets the treatise borrow the intuitive, motivating force of self-language (something a practitioner naturally wants to tame) while cashing it out entirely in terms of mind, a genuine dharma, without ever conceding that a real self exists to be subdued at all.

2. Why the Buddha Exhorts Knowing Only the Inner Six Sense-Fields

A single scriptural line — urging monks to know the six inner sense-fields as they truly are, without the same explicit urging for the six outer ones — receives a cascade of explanations: turning meditation inward avoids external distraction; it forecloses any false increase of the notions of permanence, self, happiness, and purity; it cultivates an uncommon dhyāna instead of one shared with outsiders; and, since self-grasping is the very root from which grasping at what belongs to a self proliferates, knowing the inner sense-fields already implicitly covers the outer, exactly as tending a tree's root nourishes its branches as well.

Chinese Source

如契經說。汝等苾芻。於內六處應如實知。問於外六處亦應如實知。何故世尊唯勸知內處。答世尊欲令諸弟子輩多於內門修靜慮故。如契經說。汝等苾芻。應觀內根不應緣外。復次世尊欲令諸弟子輩內修靜慮無所增益。如契經說。汝等苾芻。應內修定勿妄增益常樂我淨。汝等苾芻應內修定如實觀察。諸行無常苦空非我因集生緣。由此八種聖慧行相。於一切時應觀諸有。復次世尊欲令諸弟子輩於內修習不共靜慮。如世尊說。汝等苾芻。應內修定不應修習諸共靜慮。謂麁苦障靜妙離觀。汝等苾芻應內修定。謂應修習不共靜慮觀察諸有。如病如癰如箭惱害。無常有苦是空非我。由此八種勝尋思杖。能遍摧伏一切有生。復次此契經中。唯勸觀察內六處者以內是外所依止故亦勸觀彼。所以者何。以有我故便有我所。有我見故有我所見。有五我見故有十五我所見。有我執故有我所執。有我癡故有我所癡。有我愛故有我所愛。為養內我求外資具。復次世尊欲令諸弟子等先於內法修念住故。謂修行者先緣內法修習念住。既成滿已方緣於外。由如是等種種因緣。是故世尊唯勸知內。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the scripture states: you monks ought to know, as things truly are, the six inner sense-fields. One asks: ought you not likewise to know the six outer sense-fields as things truly are? For what reason does the World-Honored One exhort knowing only the inner sense-fields? The answer: the World-Honored One wished his disciples, for the most part, to cultivate dhyāna through the inner gate, as the scripture states: you monks ought to observe your own inner faculties, and ought not to take an external object as your own condition. Further, the World-Honored One wished his disciples to cultivate dhyāna inwardly without any false increase at all, as the scripture states: you monks ought to cultivate concentration inwardly, and ought not to falsely increase the notions of permanence, happiness, self, and purity; you monks ought to cultivate concentration inwardly, observing, as things truly are, that every formation is impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, its own cause, accumulation, arising, and condition — by these eight aspects of noble wisdom, one ought, at every time, to observe every state of existence. Further, the World-Honored One wished his disciples to cultivate an uncommon dhyāna inwardly, as the World-Honored One states: you monks ought to cultivate concentration inwardly, and ought not to cultivate every common dhyāna, namely, the contemplation of coarseness, suffering, obstruction, excellence, and separation — you monks ought to cultivate concentration inwardly, that is, you ought to cultivate an uncommon dhyāna, observing every state of existence as a sickness, as a boil, as an arrow, as an affliction and harm, as impermanent, as suffering, as empty, as without self — by these eight staffs of supreme reflection, one is capable of comprehensively subduing every state of rebirth whatsoever. Further, within this scripture, the exhortation to observe only the six inner sense-fields likewise exhorts observing the outer, since the inner serves as the very basis the outer relies upon — for what reason is this so? Because, from the existence of a self, there follows the existence of what belongs to a self; from the existence of a view of self, there follows the existence of a view of what belongs to a self; from the existence of five kinds of view of self, there follow fifteen kinds of view of what belongs to a self; from the existence of a grasping of self, there follows a grasping of what belongs to a self; from the existence of delusion regarding self, there follows delusion regarding what belongs to a self; from the existence of craving for self, there follows craving for what belongs to a self — and it is in order to nourish this inner self that one seeks external requisites and resources. Further, the World-Honored One wished his disciples to first cultivate the foundations of mindfulness with respect to inner dharmas — that is, a practitioner first takes an inner dharma as his object in cultivating the foundations of mindfulness, and, only once this has been perfected and completed, does he then take an external dharma as his object. It is owing to causes and conditions such as these, of every kind, that the World-Honored One exhorts knowing only the inner.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chain from self-view to view-of-what-belongs-to-self, traced here from one instance up through fifteen, is a compact restatement of a principle this treatise returns to again and again: an inner error is never merely private, since every outward form of grasping — for possessions, for territory, for status — is shown to be parasitic on the more fundamental inward error that a self exists there to begin with.

3. Six Inner Sense-Fields or Six Touch-Fields? Seven Rival Distinctions

A scripture speaking of "six inner sense-fields" and another speaking of "six touch-fields" prompt the question of what distinguishes them. Seven candidate answers are canvassed — mere synonymy; a difference in name alone; same-class versus counterpart-class; capable versus incapable of arising; possessing versus lacking actual function; present versus past-and-future; already arisen versus not yet arisen — before the Venerable Pārśva and Venerable Pūrṇa each offer their own resolution through a vivid analogy: a monk's bowl, or an iron bowl for ghee, named plainly in its own right yet renamed once it is actually put to use.

Chinese Source

如契經說有六內處。契經復說有六觸處。問此二六處有何差別。或有說者。此二無別。所以者何。六內處即六觸處。六觸處即六內處。聲雖有異而義無別。復有說者。亦有差別。謂名即差別名內六處名六觸處故。復次諸同分者名六觸處。彼同分者名六內處。復次諸可生法名六觸處。不可生法名六內處。復次有業用者名六觸處。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the scripture states, there exist six inner sense-fields; a further scripture states that there exist six touch-fields. One asks: what distinction is there between these two sets of six fields? There are those who say: these two admit of no distinction whatsoever, for what reason? Because the six inner sense-fields are precisely the six touch-fields, and the six touch-fields are precisely the six inner sense-fields, the sound differing while the meaning remains undifferentiated. There are, further, those who say: a genuine distinction does indeed exist between them — that is, they differ in name, the very terms 'six inner sense-fields' and 'six touch-fields' themselves differing. Further, whatever is a same-class instance is named a touch-field; whatever is a counterpart-class instance is named an inner sense-field. Further, whatever dharma is capable of arising is named a touch-field; whatever dharma is incapable of arising is named an inner sense-field. Further, whatever possesses actual function is named a touch-field;

無業用者名六內處。若作是說。諸現在者名六觸處。過去未來者名六內處。復次諸已生者名六觸處。未已生者名六內處。若作是說。

whatever possesses no function is named an inner sense-field. There are those who say: whatever is presently existent is named a touch-field; whatever is past or future is named an inner sense-field. Further, whatever has already arisen is named a touch-field; whatever has not yet arisen is named an inner sense-field. There are those who say:

過去現在名六觸處。未來名六內處。復次心心所法正依住者名六觸處。心心所法不正依住。唯空轉者名六內處。復次眼等六處作觸所依義名六觸處。作餘心心所法所依義名六內處。脇尊者言。眼等六處自性名內六處。若有所作名六觸處。如苾芻鉢。若說自性但名為鉢。苾芻用時名苾芻鉢。尊者望滿作如是言。眼等六處自體名內六處。若與觸作所依名六觸處。如鐵酥鉢。若說自體但名鐵鉢。若盛酥時名鐵酥鉢。問眼等六處亦為受等之所依止。何故但名觸處不名受等處耶。答亦應說受等處而不作是說者。當知此義有餘。復次契經舉勝兼顯劣者。謂於一切心心所中。觸最為勝。若說觸處當知兼顯名受等處。復次心心所法以觸為命。觸所住持觸所引發。由觸力故能現在前。故名觸處。謂心心所於境流散。由觸攝持令得和合。

whatever is past or present is named a touch-field; whatever is future is named an inner sense-field. Further, whatever mind and mental factor properly abides, relying upon its own basis, is named a touch-field; whatever mind and mental factor does not properly abide, relying upon its own basis, but merely turns in emptiness, is named an inner sense-field. Further, whatever meaning the eye and the other six sense-fields possess as the very basis of touch is named a touch-field; whatever meaning they possess as the basis of every other mind and mental factor is named an inner sense-field. The Venerable Pārśva says: the eye and the other six sense-fields, taken in their own nature, are named the six inner sense-fields; whenever some activity is performed with them, they are named the six touch-fields — exactly as a monk's own alms-bowl, when spoken of in its own nature, is named simply a bowl, but, when a monk actually makes use of it, is named a monk's bowl. The Venerable Pūrṇa says as follows: the eye and the other six sense-fields, taken in their own substance, are named the six inner sense-fields; whenever they serve as the basis of touch, they are named the six touch-fields — exactly as an iron bowl for ghee, when spoken of in its own substance, is named simply an iron bowl, but, when it actually holds ghee, is named an iron ghee-bowl. One asks: the eye and the other six sense-fields likewise serve as the basis of feeling and the rest — for what reason are they named only touch-fields, and not named feeling-fields and the rest? The answer: they ought indeed also to be named feeling-fields and the rest, and, where this is not so stated, one should understand that this carries a further meaning here. Further, when the scripture raises the superior and thereby implicitly displays the inferior — that is, among every mind and mental factor whatsoever, touch is the most superior of all, and, in speaking of the touch-field, one should understand that this likewise implicitly displays the name feeling-field and the rest. Further, mind and its mental factors take touch as their very life, being sustained by touch and drawn forth by touch, becoming presently manifest through the very force of touch, and it is for this reason that this is named the touch-field — that is, mind and its mental factors, dispersed and scattered across their own object, are gathered together and held in union by touch,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The monk's-bowl and ghee-bowl analogies solve the puzzle by relocating it entirely: the question was never really about two different things existing side by side, but about the very same thing described twice — once in its own bare nature, and once in the very moment it is put to its characteristic use, exactly as an eye-faculty and a touch-field can be the identical entity under two different descriptions depending on whether contact is presently occurring.

4. Pārśva's and Pūrṇa's Bowls, and the Raft That Crosses to the Far Shore

Having settled why the eye and the rest are named touch-fields specifically rather than feeling-fields or the like — touch being the most superior among every mind and mental factor, sustaining and drawing forth every other one of them exactly as life itself sustains a body's own capacity for sensation — the treatise turns to a rich image: the six inner sense-fields as this shore, the six outer as that shore, since mind and its mental factors sweep every sentient being, gathered within them, toward the great ocean of birth, old age, sickness, and death, exactly as a raging river carries everything on both its banks toward the sea. The eightfold noble path alone serves as the boat and raft by which countless beings cross from this shore of birth-and-death to that shore of nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

又若無觸諸心心所應如死屍不能觸對自所緣境。皆由觸力有觸境用。如有命根身能覺觸。是故眼等但名觸處。如契經說。六內處名此岸六外處名彼岸。問六內外處與此彼岸。有何相似而作是說。答與心心所作所依緣。有近有遠似此彼岸故作是說。如河兩岸。近者名此遠者名彼。如是六處與心心所。作所依者近故如此岸。作所緣者遠故如彼岸。復次是心心所初入已渡如此彼岸故作是說。如諸有情。初入河處名為此岸。已渡河處名為彼岸。如是與心心所法作所依者。如初入故名此岸。作所緣者如已度故名彼岸。故內外處名此彼岸。復次契經中說。寂滅涅槃名為彼岸。涅槃唯是外處所攝既名彼岸。故內六處得此岸名。如契經說。薩迦耶是此岸。薩迦耶滅是彼岸。薩迦耶者即是生死。於生死中六內處勝故。六內處得此岸名。既六內處名為此岸故。六外處得彼岸名。問此中何法如河。而說六內外處如此彼岸耶。答心心所法如河故。內外處如此彼岸。如有瀑河漂此彼岸。情非情物同趣大海。心心所法亦復如是。漂內外處所攝有情。同趣生老病死大海。問於此河中誰為船栰。答八支聖道如有船栰。百千眾生之所依止。從河此岸渡至彼岸隨意遊適。如是無量無邊有情依止聖道。從生死此岸至涅槃彼岸。自在遊賞故。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That nirvāṇa itself, gathered within the outer sense-fields as "that shore" in one reading of the scripture, sits on the far side of a river whose very waters are mind and mental factors, is a strikingly literal way of saying something this treatise treats as anything but metaphorical: liberation is reached only by crossing through the very same mental processes that, left unchecked, sweep a being toward suffering — the raft does not avoid the river, it rides it to a different destination.

5. The Eight Bases of Mastery, the Ten Totalities, and the Four Formless Spheres

Scripture also speaks of eight bases of mastery and ten totalities as further "processes" — reconciled with the fixed count of twelve sense-fields by noting that both are fully gathered within the mind-field and dharma-field, their own nature and associated dharmas leaving no remainder outside these two. The four formless spheres, too, are deliberately named using the term "process" rather than any grander title, precisely in order to refute the outsiders' own competing claim that each is itself a distinct form of nirvāṇa — infinite space as bodiless liberation, infinite consciousness as boundless-intellect liberation, nothingness as pure-accumulation liberation, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception as the liberation of the world's own stūpa.

Chinese Source

如契經說。有八勝處有十遍處。問彼八與十既亦名處。何故但有十二處耶。答彼八與十皆攝在此十二處中。謂彼自性俱有相應即此意處法處攝故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the scripture states, there exist the eight bases of mastery, and the ten totalities. One asks: since these eight and ten, too, are named processes, for what reason are there only twelve sense-fields at all? The answer: these eight and ten are, in every case, gathered within these twelve sense-fields, since their own nature, together with what co-arises with them and is associated with them, is gathered within this very mind-field and dharma-field.

如契經說。有四無色處。謂空無邊處識無邊處無所有處非想非非想處。問何故世尊於四無色以處聲說。答為破外道解脫執故。謂諸外道執四無色為四涅槃。一執空無邊處名無身涅槃。二執識無邊處名無邊意涅槃。三執無所有處名淨聚涅槃。四執非想非非想處名世間窣堵波涅槃。為破如是外道涅槃執故。說四無色名為生處非真解脫。真解脫者乃名涅槃。

As the scripture states, there exist the four formless processes, namely, the sphere of infinite space, the sphere of infinite consciousness, the sphere of nothingness, and the realm of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. One asks: for what reason does the World-Honored One speak of these four formless states using the term 'process'? The answer: it is in order to refute the outsiders' own grasping after liberation — that is, the outsiders hold that these four formless states are four kinds of nirvāṇa: first, they hold that the sphere of infinite space is named the nirvāṇa of bodilessness; second, they hold that the sphere of infinite consciousness is named the nirvāṇa of boundless intellect; third, they hold that the sphere of nothingness is named the nirvāṇa of pure accumulation; fourth, they hold that the realm of neither-perception-nor-non-perception is named the nirvāṇa of the worldly stūpa. It is in order to refute this outsider grasping after nirvāṇa that the four formless states are spoken of using the term 'process,' denoting a place of further rebirth, not a genuine liberation at all — true liberation alone being properly named nirvāṇa.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Naming the four formless spheres "processes" rather than granting them any more exalted title is a quiet but deliberate demotion: the treatise is not merely disagreeing with the outsiders about which meditative attainment counts as ultimate liberation, it is denying them even the linguistic dignity of a name that could support such a claim in the first place, reserving the word "nirvāṇa" itself for what alone deserves it.

6. Two More "Processes": Refuting the Outsiders' False Liberation

Two further states — the heaven of non-percipient beings and the realm of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — receive the same treatment, this time against three distinct outsider claims: that these are a genuine liberation, that they are a genuine non-return, and that they are genuinely indestructible. Each claim is refuted by the same underlying fact: falling from either state, a being tumbles back down through every realm and destiny, circulating through birth-and-death without any respite, however vast the intervening lifespan — five hundred great aeons for the one, eighty thousand for the other. The Buddha's own alternative naming convention, "abode of sentient beings" paired with "process" rather than "station of consciousness," marks precisely this exceptional case: an abode of beings that is not itself a station of consciousness at all.

如契經說。復有二處。一無想有情處。二非想非非想處。問何故世尊說此二處。答為破外道解脫想故。謂諸外道於此二處起解脫想。

As the scripture states, there exist, further, two processes: first, the process of non-percipient beings; second, the realm of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. One asks: for what reason does the World-Honored One speak of these two processes? and it is in order to refute this notion that the Buddha names these two a place of further rebirth, not a genuine liberation at all. Further, it is in order to refute the outsiders' own notion of non-return — that is, the outsiders give rise to a notion of non-return with respect to these two processes, and it is in order to refute this notion that the Buddha names these two processes of regression and return, since, falling from these processes, one regresses and returns to every realm, every destiny, and every mode of birth, circulating through birth-and-death without any respite at all. Further, it is in order to refute the outsiders' own notion of indestructibility —

that is, the outsiders hold that these two processes are a genuine liberation, never again to be dispersed or destroyed, and it is in order to refute this grasping that the Buddha states that these two are themselves places of dispersal and destruction — that is, falling from them, one is dispersed toward every realm, every destiny, and every mode of birth, circulating through birth-and-death without any respite at all: falling from the heaven of non-percipient beings, one certainly falls, dispersed, into rebirth in the desire realm; falling from the realm of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, if one is not a noble one, one falls, dispersed, into rebirth in a lower ground. Further, it is because these two processes possess so vast and lengthy a lifespan that the outsiders grasp them as liberation — among every place of rebirth an ordinary person alone ever receives, none possesses so vast and lengthy a lifespan as the heaven of non-percipient beings, whose own lifespan is five hundred great aeons; among every place of rebirth received by any sentient being whatsoever, none possesses so vast and lengthy a lifespan as the realm of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, whose own lifespan is eighty thousand great aeons — and it is in order to dispel the outsiders' own grasping after liberation here that the Buddha names these two a place of further rebirth, not a genuine liberation at all. Further, the Buddha, elsewhere, speaks using two names —

一名有情居。二名為識住。於此二處亦二名說。一名有情居。二名為處。故於此二以處聲說。謂受生處。復次佛說諸識住定是有情居。有有情居而非識住。謂此二處顯非無此故說處名。即是有情所居處義。

first, an abode of sentient beings; second, a station of consciousness. With respect to these same two processes as well, he likewise speaks using two names — first, an abode of sentient beings; second, a process — and it is for this reason that, with respect to these two, he speaks using the term 'process,' referring to a place of rebirth. Further, the Buddha states that every station of consciousness is certainly an abode of sentient beings, yet there exists an abode of sentient beings that is not a station of consciousness — and, since these two processes are meant to display precisely this latter case, the term 'process' is used, this being precisely the meaning of a place where sentient beings dwell.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even an eighty-thousand-great-aeon lifespan is treated as no genuine safeguard against eventual relapse is the sharpest point this section makes about the outsiders' own error: they mistake sheer temporal duration for metaphysical permanence, when this treatise insists the two are simply unrelated — a state can last almost unimaginably long and still be, in its own nature, exactly as impermanent and precarious as the shortest rebirth of all.

7. Śāriputra's Praise of the Buddha, and the Difference Between a Buddha's and a Disciple's Own Realization

Śāriputra's own famous declaration — that no establishment of processes surpasses the twelve sense-fields, which gather within them every dharma whatsoever — raises the question of how a disciple could possibly know this. The answer is twofold: partly through instruction, trusting what the Buddha himself had once taught, and partly through Śāriputra's own genuine direct realization of each sense-field in turn. Yet a real gulf separates disciple from teacher even here: the Buddha directly realizes both the particular and the shared characteristic of every sense-field, entirely through his own self-awakening, while Śāriputra grasps only the shared characteristic, and even this only by first reflecting on what the Buddha has already taught him, reasoning that whatever lies outside the first eleven sense-fields must be gathered in the twelfth, the dharma-field, precisely because he already knows the six consciousness-bodies number exactly six, each requiring its own basis and object.

Chinese Source

如契經說。尊者舍利子往詣佛所作如是言。大德世尊。施設諸處為無有上。謂十二處。攝一切法。此是世尊無餘知見過此更無所知見法。若有沙門婆羅門等。覺所知法過世尊者無餘是處。問尊者舍利子如何能知此十二處攝一切法。而讚佛言施設諸處為無有上。答由教故知。謂舍利子得四證淨於佛所說決定信受。曾聞世尊說十二處攝一切法。由此故知。問尊者舍利子於十二處唯有教智無證智耶。

One asks: if both the Buddha and Śāriputra are capable of directly and correctly realizing each and every one of the twelve sense-fields, what distinction is there between the Buddha and Śāriputra? The answer: the Buddha is capable of directly realizing, with respect to these twelve sense-fields, both their own particular characteristic and their shared characteristic, each and every one; the Venerable Śāriputra, with respect to these twelve sense-fields, is capable of directly realizing only their shared characteristic, each and every one, being not yet capable of directly and truly realizing their own particular characteristic in every case — that is, there exist countless distinctions of process, all gathered within these twelve sense-fields, and yet Śāriputra still requires another to reveal them to him before he is capable of knowing them. Further, the Venerable Śāriputra, with respect to the twelve sense-fields, directly realizes each and every one only by being drawn along through another's own instruction; the Buddha, with respect to the twelve sense-fields, directly realizes each and every one entirely through his own self-awakening, not relying upon another's instruction at all.

Further, the Buddha, with respect to the twelve sense-fields, is complete in omniscience and in omniscience of every kind; the Venerable Śāriputra, with respect to the twelve sense-fields, possesses only omniscience, not omniscience of every kind. Further, the Buddha, with respect to the twelve sense-fields, does not rely upon the six consciousnesses in order to realize that there are precisely this many and no more;

and it is for this reason that the twelve sense-fields neither increase nor decrease.

Further, the Venerable Śāriputra, although he does indeed directly realize each and every one of the twelve sense-fields, must first reflect upon what the Buddha has taught before he can do so — that is, the Buddha first taught the names of the twelve sense-fields, and only afterward analyzed each of them, one by one, in accordance with these names. Once the World-Honored One had already analyzed eleven of the sense-fields, Śāriputra, at that time, thought to himself: whatever dharma is not gathered within the preceding eleven sense-fields must certainly be gathered within the very last, the dharma-field — and it was for this reason that he then said: Great Virtuous One, World-Honored One, the establishment of the various processes admits of nothing more supreme than this, namely, the twelve sense-fields, gathering within them every dharma whatsoever. The World-Honored One directly realizes the characteristic of the twelve sense-fields

without needing to reflect upon any teaching taught by another at all. Although Śāriputra, too, is capable of directly realizing the characteristic of the twelve sense-fields, he differs greatly indeed from the Buddha's own wisdom in this respect, and it is for this reason that the Buddha alone is called the Unsurpassed, Most Honored One.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Śāriputra's own reasoning — inferring the sense-fields' fixed count of twelve from the prior, independently known fact that consciousness comes in exactly six kinds, each needing a basis and an object — is a small but genuine piece of philosophical deduction embedded within an act of religious praise: even the disciple most renowned for wisdom among the Buddha's own followers arrives at his conclusion by an inference from more basic premises, rather than by the same unmediated, self-arising insight the Buddha alone is said to possess.

8. Defining the Five Aggregates: Form, Feeling, Perception, Formations, Consciousness

The treatise turns to an entirely new topic within the same nyāsa, the five aggregates, defining form through three deliberately distinct scriptural formulations, each aimed at refuting a different rival position: the four great elements and what is derived from them, refuting Buddhadeva's denial that any matter exists beyond the elements themselves; form across all three times, refuting the outsider Daṇḍajaṭin's denial that past and future exist at all; and the ten form-processes together with the dharma-process's own portion of form, refuting the Dārṣṭāntikas' and Dharmatrāta's shared claim that no genuine form is ever gathered within the dharma-process. Feeling, perception, and consciousness are each defined as their own six bodies tied to the six modes of contact, while the formations aggregate singles out volition alone from the full range of associated and dissociated formations, since volition alone leads, sustains, and nourishes every other formation, exactly as craving alone leads every dharma gathered under the truth of accumulation.

9. What Does "Skandha" Mean? The Grain-Heap, and the One-Atom Question

The treatise glosses "skandha" through the meanings of a heap, a combination, an accumulation, and an abbreviation, illustrated by a heap of grain and by the observation that a great army, though its soldiers stand nowhere touching shoulder to shoulder, is still named an army on account of sharing one undertaking — so too countless, widely scattered atoms of form, sharing the same nature, are combined and named the form aggregate. A genuine puzzle follows: can a single atom by itself be called the form aggregate, or does the very name require multitude? The Abhidharma masters resolve this by distinguishing two ways of speaking — considering the atom as a portion of some larger, provisionally unified heap, or considering it entirely on its own — exactly as a single grain taken from a rice-heap can honestly be described either as "one grain from the heap" or simply as "gathering grain," depending on which frame the speaker has in mind.

10. The Order of the Five Aggregates: Six Converging Reasons

Why does the Buddha teach form first, and consciousness last? Six independent principles converge on the identical sequence: ordinary grammatical and pedagogical convention; a scale running from coarse to subtle, form being coarsest and consciousness subtlest, with feeling, perception, and the formations sorted between them by how easily each is discerned (feeling, though bodiless, still admits of coarseness, exactly as ordinary speech locates pain in a hand or a head); the beginningless chain by which men and women's mutual delight in form leads through craving for pleasurable feeling, through the distorted perception underlying that craving, to the very afflictions of the formations, and finally to the consciousness those afflictions defile; the two gateways into the Buddha's own teaching, the contemplation of impurity and mindfulness of breathing, both of which take form as their object, so that contemplating form first opens the way to seeing feeling's own fault, then correcting perception's own distortion, then ending the arising of affliction, and only then purifying consciousness itself; the image of form as a vessel, feeling as food, perception as seasoning that induces craving, the formations as the cook whose karma and affliction do the actual work, and consciousness as the very one who eats and thereby discerns; and, finally, a realm-based sequence in which each aggregate's own characteristic becomes most vividly evident in a different cosmic ground — form in the desire realm's own exquisite objects, feeling in the dhyānas' own joy, perception in the first three formless spheres, the formations at the very peak of existence, and consciousness last of all, as what depends upon these four stations rather than manifesting a station of its own.

12. Why Are Only Feeling and Perception Singled Out as Separate Aggregates?

Among the mental factors universally present with every state of mind, why do feeling and perception alone earn their own independent aggregate, while every other such factor is merely folded into the shared formations aggregate? The Venerable Pārśva simply defers to the Buddha's own unique, complete penetration of every dharma's own fitness for independent status. Further reasons accumulate: varied phrasing delights the listener and forestalls weariness; feeling and perception each manifest a different realm — feeling the form realm's own joy, perception the formless realm's own subtle perceptions — and each alone wearies a practitioner of yoga out of that same realm; sentient beings' addiction to pleasant feeling and their grasping at distorted perception together drive the whole of saṃsāra's suffering, meriting this special emphasis; feeling and perception alone give rise to the root of the two great disputes, of craving and of view, and by extension every other paired affliction this treatise elsewhere tracks in twos; and, distinctively, feeling and perception alone establish their own separate station of consciousness, while every other mental factor shares only one station in common — and it is precisely by growing weary of these same two, as the Prajñaptiśāstra describes, that a practitioner of yoga enters the attainment of cessation at all.

Further, on account of these two dharmas, practitioners of yoga become weary of these two realms, and it is for this reason that they are separately established as aggregates — that is, by the very power of feeling, practitioners of yoga become weary of the form realm; by the very power of perception, practitioners of yoga become weary of the formless realm. Further, feeling and perception, these two dharmas, alone establish their own separate station of consciousness, and it is for this reason that they alone are independently established as aggregates,

while every other mental factor is gathered together and established as a single, shared station of consciousness, and it is for this reason that they are jointly established as a single aggregate. Further, practitioners of yoga, wearied and repelled by feeling and perception, enter the attainment of cessation, and it is for this reason that these two are separately established as aggregates, as the Prajñaptiśāstra states: how does one, through the aid to practice, attain the attainment of cessation? By what expedient does one give rise to the attainment of cessation? That is, one first cultivating this practice, applying no effort at all with respect to any formation whatsoever, and reflecting upon nothing at all, merely gives rise to this thought: on account of what not yet having arisen do feeling and perception come to arise? On account of what having already arisen do feeling and perception then cease? Having given rise to this thought, he is capable of knowing, as things truly are, that it is on account of the attainment of cessation not yet having arisen that feeling and perception come to arise, and that, once the attainment of cessation has arisen, feeling and perception then cease — and, having known this, becoming wearied and repelled by these two dharmas, feeling and perception, up through their no longer arising at all, he thereby attains the attainment of cessation.

It is owing to causes and conditions such as these, of every kind, that feeling and perception are separately established, each as its own single aggregate.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That feeling and perception alone establish their own separate station of consciousness, while every other mental factor shares a single common one, is arguably the most structurally decisive reason offered in this whole list: it locates their own exceptional status not in some external property like moral weight or doctrinal importance, but in a fact about the architecture of consciousness itself — these two, and no others, are woven into the very basis on which a being's continued existence is classified at all.

13. Why the Unconditioned Is Never Established as an Aggregate: Nine Reasons

A parallel question closes this run of definitions: why is the unconditioned never itself established as a sixth aggregate, or folded into any of the five? Nine converging reasons answer it: the unconditioned simply lacks the very characteristic of a heap or accumulation that defines an aggregate in the first place; it is, rather, the very place where an aggregate is finally extinguished, exactly as the place a jar is finally destroyed is never itself called a jar; it lacks arising and ceasing, cause and condition, the convergence that alone earns the name conditioned; it is never brought forth by birth, worn by age, or destroyed by impermanence; it possesses no function of taking or bestowing a fruit, no earlier or later phase within the three times, and no lower, middling, or upper grade at all — and, lacking every one of the aggregates' own defining marks, it can be placed neither within the five nor established as any further, sixth aggregate of its own.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: for what reason is the unconditioned not established as an aggregate? The answer: lacking the very characteristic of an aggregate, it is not established as an aggregate — that is, an aggregate possesses the characteristic of a heap or an accumulation, and, the unconditioned lacking this characteristic, it is not established as an aggregate. Further, the unconditioned is precisely the very place where an aggregate is ultimately extinguished, and it is for this reason that it is not established as an aggregate — exactly as the place where a jar or a garment is ultimately destroyed is not itself named a jar or a garment. Further, every conditioned dharma is associated with arising and ceasing, possessing a cause and a condition, and thereby attaining the very characteristic of the conditioned, and so may properly be established as an aggregate; every unconditioned dharma is not associated with arising and ceasing, possessing neither cause nor condition, and thereby failing to attain the characteristic of the conditioned, and so is not established as an aggregate. Further, every conditioned dharma belongs to a cause, belongs to a condition, and can be established as an aggregate through the very convergence of cause and condition; every unconditioned dharma is the opposite of this, and so is not established as an aggregate. Further, every conditioned dharma is brought forth by birth, worn down by old age, and destroyed by impermanence, and so may properly be established as an aggregate;

諸無為法與此相違故不立蘊。復次諸有為法流行於世。取果與果有諸作用。能了所緣可立為蘊。諸無為法與此相違故不立蘊。復次諸有為法墮在三世。與苦相應有前後際。有下中上可立為蘊。諸無為法與此相違故不立蘊。復次諸無為法無五蘊相。不可立在此五蘊中。亦不可立為第六蘊。無聚積等諸蘊相故。復次蘊是作相。諸無為法無有作相故不立蘊。復次蘊從他生。諸無為法不從他生故不立蘊。由如是等種種因緣無為非蘊。

every unconditioned dharma is the opposite of this, and so is not established as an aggregate. Further, every conditioned dharma flows through the world, possessing the very function of taking a fruit and of bestowing a fruit, and of discerning its own object, and so may properly be established as an aggregate; every unconditioned dharma is the opposite of this, and so is not established as an aggregate. Further, every conditioned dharma falls within the three times, is associated with suffering, possesses an earlier and a later period, and admits of a lower, middling, or upper grade, and so may properly be established as an aggregate; every unconditioned dharma is the opposite of this, and so is not established as an aggregate. Further, every unconditioned dharma possesses none of the characteristics of the five aggregates, and so cannot be placed within these five aggregates, nor can it be established as a sixth aggregate, since it possesses no characteristic of a heap or an accumulation and the rest at all. Further, an aggregate possesses the very characteristic of construction, and every unconditioned dharma possesses no such characteristic of construction at all, and so is not established as an aggregate. Further, an aggregate is produced from something else, and every unconditioned dharma is not produced from anything else at all, and so is not established as an aggregate. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The jar-and-its-destruction analogy closing this list is worth pausing on: it reframes the unconditioned not as some further, subtler kind of thing standing alongside the five aggregates, waiting to be classified as a sixth, but as the very absence and cessation that a heap of conditioned things eventually arrives at — the unconditioned's own exclusion from the aggregate-scheme is not an oversight this treatise needs to patch, but the direct logical consequence of what an aggregate, and its own final extinguishing, actually mean.

14. The Five Merit-Aggregates, and Ānanda's Eighty Thousand Dharma-Aggregates

A scripture speaking of five further "merit-aggregates" — moral discipline, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation — and the Venerable Ānanda's own famous declaration of having personally received eighty thousand dharma-aggregates from the Buddha, plus two thousand more transmitted through fellow monks, both raise the same question: if the aggregates number only five, where do all these others belong? Both are fully gathered within the five aggregates of form and the rest, whether taken as speech itself (gathered in form) or as the very dharmas thereby named (gathered in the formations). A final debate over the actual size of "one dharma-aggregate" — measured variously at six thousand verses, or at some enormous, specific verse-count proposed by the Venerable Ghoṣa and others — is dismissed entirely by the compilers, who settle instead that those to be converted possess exactly eighty thousand kinds of conduct, and it is precisely to counteract these eighty thousand kinds of conduct, not to fill some predetermined verse-count, that the Buddha taught eighty thousand dharma-aggregates in the first place.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

As the scripture states, there exist five kinds of merit-aggregate, namely, the aggregate of moral discipline, the aggregate of concentration, the aggregate of wisdom, the aggregate of liberation, and the aggregate of the knowledge and vision of liberation. One asks: there ought then to be ten aggregates in all — how, then, does it speak of only five? The answer: these aggregates such as moral discipline and the rest are all gathered within these very five aggregates of form and the rest, and it is for this reason that the aggregates number only five. As the scripture states: the Venerable Ānanda addressed the monks, saying as follows: I have personally received, from the Buddha's own presence, eighty thousand dharma-aggregates, and, from the various monks, have received and transmitted a further two thousand. One asks: since the World-Honored One speaks of so vast a multitude of dharma-aggregates, how could it be said that there are only these five, form and the rest?

答彼多法蘊皆攝在此色等五中故蘊唯五。問彼諸法蘊是何蘊攝。有作是說。一切法蘊語為自性。彼說攝在此色蘊中。有餘師說。一切法蘊名為自性。彼說攝在此行蘊中。是故世尊唯說五蘊。

The answer: these many dharma-aggregates are all gathered within these very five aggregates of form and the rest, and it is for this reason that the aggregates number only five. One asks: within which aggregate are these various dharma-aggregates themselves gathered? There are those who say: every dharma-aggregate takes speech as its own nature, and this position holds that they are gathered within this form aggregate. Other masters say: every dharma-aggregate is named according to its own nature, and this position holds that they are gathered within this formations aggregate — and it is for this reason that the World-Honored One speaks of only these five aggregates.

One asks: what is the actual measure of each and every one of these dharma-aggregates? There are those who say: there exists a treatise on the dharma-aggregates comprising six thousand verses, and each and every dharma-aggregate is of that same measure. Others, further, say: exactly as the World-Honored One speaks of the aggregates, the sense-fields, the elements, food, dependent origination, the truths, the jewels, the foundations of mindfulness, the right exertions, the bases of supernormal power, the faculties, the powers, the awakening-factors, and the path-factors — each and every such gateway of dharma constitutes a single dharma-aggregate in its own right, and one cannot fix in advance any determinate number of verses for it at all. Other masters say: each and every dharma-aggregate comprises one hundred and fifty-five thousand, five hundred and fifty verses of text. Others, further, say: each and every dharma-aggregate comprises only fifteen thousand, five hundred and fifty verses of text. The compilers say: none of these positions ought to be held. One should say as follows: those to be converted possess eighty thousand kinds of conduct, and it is in order to counteract these eighty thousand kinds of conduct that the World-Honored One taught eighty thousand dharma-aggregates —

彼諸有情依佛所說八萬法蘊入佛法中。作所應作各得究竟。

and it is by relying upon these eighty thousand dharma-aggregates taught by the Buddha that all these sentient beings entered the Buddha's own teaching, each accomplishing what needed to be accomplished, and each reaching the very end of the path.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compilers' rejection of every proposed verse-count in favor of counting kinds of conduct instead is a small but telling methodological choice: it refuses to let a teaching's own scale be measured by the sheer bulk of text required to deliver it, insisting instead that its true measure is the number of distinct afflictions it was actually designed to counteract — a dharma-aggregate is defined by what it cures, not by how long it takes to say.
