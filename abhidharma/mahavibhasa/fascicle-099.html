Five Theses and a Schism

Fascicle 99 tells the causal circumstance of Mahādeva — the crimes, five theses, and schism that produced the Sthavira and Mahāsāṃghika lineages — before opening the Mind-Reading-Knowledge Nyāsa with the beloved story of the monkey who offered the Buddha honey. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Rare Among the Rare, and Five Wrong Views About the Arhat

Fascicle 99 opens exactly where fascicle 98 broke off, closing out the long-nailed ascetic's own episode: the Buddha's teaching that relinquishing the wrong-view assemblages is "rare among the rare" (since even intelligent beings mostly hold wrong views), a reconciliation of an apparent contradiction between this treatise and the Views Aggregate over whether the eternalist and annihilationist are truly opposed, and the note that Śāriputra himself attained arhatship following this same teaching. The fascicle then turns to a grim new topic: five wrong views that would arise, after the Buddha's own parinirvāṇa, among falsely named monks about the arhat himself — that an arhat can still be molested by Māra into nocturnal emission, that he still possesses some ignorance, that he still harbors doubt, that he is liberated solely through another's confirmation, and that the path is summoned merely by uttering the word "suffering" — each analyzed, exactly as the wrong-view assemblages were in fascicle 98, through self-nature and counteraction.

Exactly as the scripture states: the Buddha said to the wandering ascetic: whatever ascetic or brāhmaṇa relinquishes the wrong-view assemblages and does not grasp them is, you should know, rare among the rare. One asks: how is this same kind rare among the rare? The answer: among the sentient beings of the world, those whose own nature is dull and obtuse are as numerous as the earth's own soil; those whose own nature is intelligent and wise are as few as the soil upon a fingernail's tip; and even among those of intelligent and wise nature, those who hold wrong views are many, and those who hold right views are few, exactly as illustrated before — and it is for this reason that this is named rare among the rare. One asks: exactly as the *Dṛṣṭi*-skandhaka states, the eternalist and the annihilationist are mutually contradictory — how, then, can it be said here that there exists a certain kind who gives rise to this same view, "I accept some in part, and do not accept some in part," without any contradiction at all? The answer: it should be understood that what is spoken of here refers to a single person who, holding the form-aggregate to be eternal, holds the remaining four aggregates to be subject to annihilation, or, holding the remaining four aggregates to be eternal, holds the form-aggregate to be subject to annihilation — and it is for this reason that these two views are not mutually contradictory. The *Dṛṣṭi*-skandhaka speaks of two separate persons — one holding form to be eternal, the other holding form to be subject to annihilation, and so on, down to consciousness, likewise being held in these same two ways — and it is for this reason that these two views are, there, mutually contradictory. That same scripture, further, states: at the very time the Bhagavān taught this same dharma of the wrong-view assemblages, the long-nailed wandering ascetic became free of dust and free of defilement, giving rise to the pure dharma-eye with respect to the various dharmas; at that time, the Venerable Śāriputra, half a month having passed since his own full ordination, following along in observing this same dharma, attained arhatship. There are those who say: the Venerable Śāriputra followed along precisely in observing the very learner's dharma that the wandering ascetic realized upon attaining the fruit of stream-entry, and thereby accomplished arhatship. The Great Virtuous One speaks as follows: this same Śāriputra followed along in observing the very dharma of dependent origination's own twelve distinct branches, and thereby accomplished arhatship. Reading note — Ru-Yi

That this treatise sets up five specific, precisely worded wrong views about the arhat immediately before naming their arising-condition is a deliberate narrative technique: rather than simply asserting that certain heretical opinions once arose, the compilers first let the reader fully absorb each thesis in the abstract, complete with its own careful self-nature/counteraction analysis, so that when the concrete historical account that follows reveals whose mouth these very theses came from, the reader already understands with technical precision exactly what is wrong with each one — the doctrinal refutation comes first, and only then does the treatise supply the name.

2. The Making of Mahādeva

The arising-condition is now named directly: the causal circumstance of Mahādeva. Born to a merchant-lord in Mathurā, he became defiled with his own mother during his father's long absence trading abroad, then murdered his own father out of fear of discovery, murdered an arhat monk who might expose him, and finally murdered his own mother in a fit of jealous rage — three acts of immediate retribution in succession. Wracked with guilt, he sought out the Buddha's own teaching for extinguishing offenses, was ordained without careful examination by a monk moved by his own earnest plea, proved a brilliant and beloved teacher at the Kukkuṭārāma monastery — and then, through improper mental application, experienced a nocturnal emission he could not reconcile with his own earlier claim to arhatship, resorting to his first evil view: that even an arhat can be molested by Māra.

The answer: the causal circumstance of Mahādeva is this same arising-condition. Long ago, within the kingdom of Mathurā, there was a certain merchant-lord who, having married a wife while still young, had a single son born to him, whose own countenance was upright and handsome, and to whom he gave the name Mahādeva. Not long afterward, this same merchant-lord, carrying his own treasure, journeyed far to another country, engaging in successive trading there, and, a long span of time passing, he did not return; his own son, having grown to maturity, became defiled with his own mother.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compilers' own choice to make Mahādeva's crimes this severe — not merely dishonest or arrogant, but a triple parricide-adjacent horror of patricide, the murder of a genuine arhat, and matricide — is itself doing argumentative work: it forecloses in advance any reading of his later doctrinal errors as an honest scholarly disagreement about the nature of arhatship, insisting instead that the entire five-thesis program traces back to a single, desperate, self-serving cover story invented by a man who could never afford to have his own claimed attainment questioned, precisely because questioning it would have unraveled everything else he had done to escape his own past.

3. Four More Theses, and a Cry in the Night

Having established that molestation by Māra excuses an arhat's impurity, Mahādeva presses his advantage to keep his disciples devoted: when they wonder why they cannot confirm their own predicted attainments, he tells them arhats still possess an undefiled form of ignorance; when they wonder at their own persistent doubt, he tells them arhats still possess a residual doubt as to what is and is not the case, which even solitary-realizers retain; when they wonder why they needed his own confirmation rather than knowing it themselves, he cites Śāriputra and Mahāmaudgalyāyana's own reliance on the Buddha's prediction as precedent. Finally, tormented at night by the true weight of his own crimes, he is overheard crying "suffering, alas!" — and, caught out, invents a fifth thesis on the spot: that the noble path itself must be summoned by uttering that very word.

4. The Verse, the Schism, and the Flight to Kashmir

Mahādeva compiles his five theses into a single verse and, years later, recites it aloud while presiding over the poṣadha recitation at the Kukkuṭārāma — provoking immediate uproar among the learned monks present, who recite it back to him reversed, denying it is the Buddha's own teaching at all. The dispute rages through the night and grows into full factionalism; the king, appealed to for arbitration, is advised by Mahādeva himself to settle it by counting heads rather than seniority, and, since Mahādeva's own faction is more numerous, the noble and virtuous minority loses the vote — the saṅgha splitting for the first time into the Sthavira (the Elders) and the Mahāsāṃghika (the Great Assembly). When the displaced elders try to leave peacefully, the enraged king orders them drowned in the Gaṅgā as a trial by ordeal; instead, they rise into the air on their own supernormal power, rescue those among their number who lack it, and fly to Kashmir, where the remorseful king endows monasteries for them that flourish to this day.

5. A New Nyāsa: Why the Knowledge of Others' Minds and of Former Abodes Are Paired

With Mahādeva's own death — his cremation pyre supernaturally refusing to burn until defiled with dog dung — the Five Kinds Nyāsa closes, and the Mind-Reading-Knowledge Nyāsa (他心智納息第三), the third nyāsa of the Knowledge Aggregate, opens: why does the compiler choose to pair the knowledge of others' minds with the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge at its very outset? Three converging reasons are offered — both are accomplished through cultivation and the fruit of the four- or five-branch dhyānas, both take knowledge and vision as their own-nature while dividing their object along a fixed temporal boundary (present versus past) — before a rejected fourth proposal (that both pervade the contaminated and undefiled) is corrected: the recollection of former abodes is, in fact, solely contaminated.

6. Defining the Knowledge of Others' Minds

The knowledge of others' minds is defined precisely: wisdom accomplished through cultivation, the fruit of cultivation, relying on cultivation as its support, and, once attained, never lost, capable of knowing another's present mind and mental concomitants belonging to the desire or form realm, or his undefiled mind. An alternate scriptural recitation adds a vivid clarification — it knows what another "seeks after," "examines," and "takes up and holds," mapped onto the desire realm and first dhyāna, the intermediate dhyāna, and the latter three dhyānas respectively — before an extraordinary combinatorial count shows this single knowledge admitting of one, two, three, four, six, eight, nine, twelve, eighteen, twenty-four, thirty-six, or seventy-two distinct classifications, depending on which dimension one slices it along. A rich etymological excursus then explains why it is named for knowing "minds" alone, though it equally knows mental concomitants — because a practitioner's own inclination and preparatory effort aims at the mind first, exactly as one who sets out to see the king necessarily sees his ministers too.

8. How to Cultivate the Knowledge of Others' Minds

Two treatises are cited on the actual preparatory practice for developing this capacity. The *Prajñaptiśāstra* describes a patient, graduated method: first observing the precise correlation between one's own bodily and mental states, then extending this same observation to another's body and mind, and finally observing purely the mind and its own mental concomitants directly, tracking what it seeks after, examines, and holds — until this observation matures into full command. The *Saṅgītiparyāya* describes a different, more advanced practice: observing the five aggregates of clinging as suffering, impermanent, empty, and without self, gradually drawing forth undefiled knowledge itself. The distinction between the two, the fascicle clarifies, is not a contradiction but a sequence: the first cultivates the contaminated form of the knowledge, not yet supreme or pure; the second cultivates its undefiled, fully refined form.

9. The Limits of Mind-Reading: Ground, Faculty, and Person

A cascading series of questions maps the precise boundaries of this knowledge. Can one still know another's mind without seeing, hearing, smelling, or tasting anything at all? Yes — through touch alone, and, once the capacity has fully matured, even without any of the five senses functioning at that moment. A precise combinatorial count follows: fifteen kinds of previously attained contaminated mind (the desire realm and four dhyānas, each in three grades) as possible objects, twelve kinds of knowledge capable of reaching them, and a parallel twelve-and-twelve for the undefiled — with a genuinely subtle explanation for why contaminated knowledge from a higher ground can reach every grade below it, while undefiled knowledge cannot: contaminated mind is organized by bodily continuum (which can hold all three grades at once), while undefiled mind is organized by faculty-grade (which a single continuum can never hold more than one of). The fascicle closes this apparatus with the three things no knowledge of others' minds can ever cross — ground, faculty, or person — worked through in exhaustive detail: a lower ground can never know a higher, a dull faculty can never know a sharp one, and even among equals in ground and faculty, a learner can never know a non-learner's mind, nor can one liberated in time and one who has arrived at insight ever know each other's, each blocked by the other's own superior faculty or person.

10. The Monkey Who Offered Honey

A beloved narrative closes the section on the Buddha's own knowledge of others' minds: while the monks anxiously drive away a monkey approaching the alms bowls, the Buddha tells them to let it be, and the monkey climbs a tree, fills the Buddha's own bowl with honey, and offers it — only for the Buddha to decline it, silently, because of the insects within. Reading the Buddha's own unspoken thought precisely, the monkey removes the insects, offers it again, is again silently declined for not yet being purified, sprinkles it with water, and offers it a third time, whereupon the Buddha, moved, accepts it — and the monkey, overjoyed, dances backward into a pit and dies, only to be reborn human, ordained, and swiftly attain arhatship as the Elder Honey-Offerer, praised by the Venerable Bala in verse. The section closes with a precise ranking: the Buddha's own knowledge of others' minds reaches all three paths (disciple, solitary-realizer, and buddha), the solitary-realizer's own reaches two, and the disciple's own reaches only one.

11. Can a Buddha Know Another Buddha's Mind?

A final genuinely delicate question closes the fascicle: since no two Tathāgatas ever appear in the world simultaneously, can a buddha even attain a knowledge of others' minds capable of taking another buddha as its object at all? One position denies it outright; the Vaibhāṣika position affirms a capacity for it while conceding it could never actually manifest in the present, since the two buddhas required for it to do so never coexist. A parallel question about solitary-realizers distinguishes the two types precisely: only a buddha can judge whether the solitary rhinoceros-horn-like realizer can know another of his own kind (though some hold he can, since other world-systems may house them), while the group-emerged solitary-realizer, like the ordinary disciple, most certainly can and does. The fascicle closes by reaffirming that a buddha's own undefiled mind, together with his own freshly developed contaminated states, and the whole of the formless realm's mind and mental concomitants, remain permanently beyond any knowledge of others' minds whatsoever.
