Three Fetters, Seven Lives

Fascicle 46 opens an entirely new chapter, the Fetters Aggregate, beginning with the Unwholesome Nyāsa's account of the three fetters cut off at stream-entry — self-view, rules-clinging, and doubt — and the monk Puṇyamitra, too overwhelmed by two hundred and fifty precepts to continue until the Buddha reframed them as just three trainings. The fascicle closes with an extended inquiry into why a stream-enterer undergoes at most seven further states of existence, working through the seven-leaf tree, a wealthy man's garden, and whether the very limit of suffering lies within suffering or entirely outside it. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. A New Chapter Opens: Should Two Lists Be Excluded for Not Being Scriptural?

Fascicle 46 opens an entirely new chapter, the Fetters Aggregate, beginning with the Unwholesome Nyāsa. Before any actual doctrine, the treatise pauses over a genuine methodological question: should the "five fetters" and "ninety-eight latent tendencies" — two technical lists this treatise itself will rely on — be excluded from discussion, since, unlike the three fetters, they are not directly quoted from any scripture? Ghoṣa argues yes, on the principle that every Abhidharma text exists solely to explain scripture. A counter-tradition insists no, since both lists were, in fact, once found in the Ekottarika-āgama, only to be lost over time and later recovered by this treatise's own author through the power of the knowledge of aspiration.

2. Why Establish Chapter Headings at All? A Painter Cannot Paint on Empty Space

The treatise settles that an author may compose according to his own judgment so long as nothing contradicts the true nature of the dharmas, then turns to a more fundamental question: why does this treatise bother establishing chapter headings before diving into content at all? The first reason is structural — without a heading to anchor it, a question has nowhere to attach, exactly as a painter cannot paint upon empty space. The second is almost practical: a text this vast needs some organizing skeleton simply to be memorized and transmitted at all, since scarcely one person in a hundred thousand could recite it by heart even with headings, let alone without them.

Grounding the need for chapter headings partly in sheer memorization survival — a text without structure will simply die out, unrecited and unrecitable — is a refreshingly unromantic justification from a tradition that could easily have rested on purely doctrinal grounds alone: even the most profound teaching depends, in practice, on being transmissible by ordinary human memory, and structure is what makes that transmission possible generation after generation.

3. Why Buddhist Scripture Withstands Scrutiny, Unlike a Monkey Struck by a Stick

The treatise pivots to praising scripture's own boundless depth against a foil: outsider texts like the Rāmāyaṇa, twelve thousand verses that convey only two bare facts, versus the Buddha's own teaching, whose text and meaning are each individually boundless, like the ocean's own depth and breadth. A memorable image follows: Buddhist scripture, challenged and questioned, only grows more radiant, exactly as fine Vārāṇasī cloth grows more lustrous the more it is struck, while outsider writings, challenged in the same way, only grow more meaningless, exactly as a young monkey struck with a stick soils itself in fright rather than yielding anything of value.

Chinese Source

佛經不爾。若文若義無量無邊。無量者義難測故。無邊者文難知故。譬如大海無量無邊。無量者深。無邊者廣。復次欲顯契經堪問難故。非如外典不堪問難。若問難時轉增無義。如獼猴子不耐打觸。若打觸時便失糞穢。佛經不爾。堪任問難。若問難時生淨戒色善根妙觸。如婆羅痆斯所出疊衣堪耐打觸。若打觸時發鮮淨色及勝妙觸。復次欲顯契經發則妙故。謂有三事覆則妙。發則不妙。一愚人二女人三外道書論。復有三事發則妙。覆則不妙。一智人二日月三佛法經論。復次欲顯契經堪思擇故。非如外典不堪思擇。若思擇時能使有情慧眼損減。如人觀日損減眼根。佛法不爾。堪任思擇。

4. Gazing at the Moon, Not the Sun: Reflection That Strengthens Rather Than Damages

The treatise adds a final justification for scripture's own depth: unlike outsider writings, which diminish a reader's own wisdom the more they are reflected upon — exactly as staring directly at the sun damages the eye — Buddhist scripture strengthens the eye of wisdom the more it is carefully analyzed, exactly as gazing at the cool light of the moon actually nourishes eyesight rather than harming it. The treatise then works through no fewer than seven parallel building and craft metaphors for why chapter headings must precede actual content — laying a house's foundation before its frame, preparing soil before planting a seedling, threading a cord before stringing flowers onto a garland.

5. Sculpting, Yoga, and the Buddha's Own Teaching Method

The parallel metaphors continue through carving (rough form before individual limbs) and, most tellingly, the yoga practitioner's own method — establishing the great elements and derivative material forms in general before analyzing them down to their ultimate particles and instants — before the treatise reveals its own deepest justification: this is simply how the Buddha himself always taught, stating a summary first and only afterward explaining it point by point, exactly as he once named the six elements, six contact-bases, and eighteen mental proximate-conducts as constituting a sentient being before unpacking each term individually.

6. Why Start With the Three Fetters? Four Competing Theories, and What a Fetter Actually Is

The treatise turns to why its own chapter sequence begins specifically with the three fetters rather than anywhere else, offering Pārśva's characteristically deflationary answer (any starting point would provoke the identical question, so the objection proves nothing) alongside three more substantive theories — that the sequence displays gradual numerical increase, mirrors an affliction-tree's own gradual growth, or tracks the successive fruits of the path each corresponding cutting-off unlocks. Ru-Yi Translation

"Seven births among the gods and seven among humans" is spoken with reference to the fully complete stream-enterer — that is, although gods and humans are together said to undergo seven births equally, there does in fact exist variation among stream-enterers as to how these are apportioned between the heavenly and human realms — some undergo seven heavenly births and six human births, some seven human and six heavenly, some six heavenly and five human, some six human and five heavenly, some five heavenly and four human, some five human and four heavenly, some four heavenly and three human, some four human and three heavenly, some three heavenly and two human, some three human and two heavenly, some two heavenly and one human, and some two human and one heavenly — this passage speaks only of the case involving the greatest number of births, and it is for this reason that it speaks of the stream-enterer undergoing seven births among gods and seven among humans respectively. One asks: for the fully complete stream-enterer, in which realm does the seventh birth reach its completion — does he attain final nirvāṇa in his seventh birth among the gods, or in his seventh birth among humans? Here, there are those who say: if it is in this present life that one attains the fruit of stream-entry, this very life is counted among the seven states of existence — on this view, if one attains the fruit while a human, then it is in heaven that the seventh birth reaches completion and final nirvāṇa is attained; if one attains the fruit while a god, then it is among humans that the seventh birth reaches completion and final nirvāṇa is attained. There are those who say: if it is in this present life that one attains the fruit of stream-entry, this present life is not counted among the seven states of existence — on this view, if one attains the fruit while a human, it is again among humans that the seventh birth reaches completion and final nirvāṇa is attained; if one attains the fruit while a god, it is again in heaven that the seventh birth reaches completion and final nirvāṇa is attained. One should know that the first position stated here does not accord with reason, since the intermediate state of the very life in which the fruit is attained is, in every case, subsumed entirely under the category of an ordinary being — were this so, the stream-enterer ought properly to be said to undergo only twenty-seven states of existence in total, yet the Prajñaptiśāstra states that the stream-enterer undergoes twenty-eight states of existence in his own wandering before reaching the very limit of suffering, and it is for this reason that one ought not to say that the very life in which the fruit is first attained is counted among the seven states of existence. One asks: among the first six of these seven states of existence, does the noble path arise? There are those who say it does not — for, were it to arise, one ought thereby to attain final nirvāṇa within that very life. There are those who say it does arise, yet, sustained by the force of karma, one does not thereby attain final nirvāṇa. One asks: if one completes the full seven states of existence in an age when no buddha has appeared in the world, does one, while still a householder, attain arhatship? There are those who say one does not — one must first go forth and receive the further monastic robes before attaining arhatship. There are those who say that one does attain arhatship while still a householder, yet, having attained it, one must afterward necessarily go forth and receive the further monastic robes — those who hold this position say that this is simply the natural, fixed pattern by which one who has become a disciple of the Buddha comes to attain the ultimate fruit, exactly as the five hundred sages who once cultivated the path upon Mount Ṛṣigiri, originally śrāvakas born into an age without a buddha, were shown the very marks of a Buddhist disciple by a monkey, and, learning to imitate this in every respect, thereby realized the fruit of the pratyekabuddha — a non-learner never adopts the outward marks of an outsider.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Resolving a live doctrinal ambiguity by simple arithmetic cross-reference against a companion text — twenty-seven states versus twenty-eight, and only one of the two readings actually produces the number the Prajñaptiśāstra states — is a satisfying demonstration of how this tradition's own internal consistency serves as a genuine check on interpretation: a proposed reading isn't just plausible or implausible in isolation, it has to actually add up correctly against everything else already established.

29. A Householder Arhat, and the Monkey Who Taught Monastic Marks

Two further puzzles close out the stream-enterer discussion: does the noble path ever actually arise during the first six of a practitioner's seven existences, or only in the very last? And, more strikingly, can someone complete all seven existences in an age with no living buddha, and thereby attain arhatship while still a householder? The treatise's own answer insists that even such a person must eventually go forth and take up the monastic robes as a natural, fixed consequence of having become the Buddha's own disciple — illustrated through the delightful legend of five hundred sages on Mount Ṛṣigiri, originally śrāvakas born into a buddha-less age, who learned to adopt the outward marks of Buddhist monasticism by imitating a monkey, and thereby went on to realize the fruit of the pratyekabuddha.

30. The Rich Man's Garden, and Whether the Limit of Suffering Lies Within or Outside It

The fascicle closes with two final etymological notes: "wandering back and forth" is likened to a wealthy person touring leisurely through his own private gardens, moving freely between the intermediate state and each successive birth. Then comes a genuinely sharp final puzzle: is "reaching the very limit of suffering" a limit found within suffering, or a limit found entirely outside it? If within, it seems paradoxical for suffering itself to have a genuine limit rather than continuing indefinitely; if outside, this seems to contradict the ordinary intuition that a substance's beginning, middle, and end must all share its own single nature throughout — exactly as a bar of pure gold is gold from end to end.
