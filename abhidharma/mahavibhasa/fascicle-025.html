The Eye That Sees by Daylight, the Eye That Sees by Night

Fascicle 25 continues the Person Nyāsa's third part (補特伽羅納息第三之三), closing the twelve-linked dependent origination's own final threads before opening a new pairing — wisdom and ignorance — worked through an exhaustive combinatorial survey, the Wheel-Turning Monarch whose kingdom traces back to an ancient act of slandering the noble path, and a careful hierarchy of paths across faith and Dharma, seeing and cultivation, and even male and female embodiment. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Why Past Karma Names Only Ignorance, While Present Karma Names Every Affliction

Fascicle 25 opens by distinguishing “ignorance conditions the formations” from “clinging conditions becoming” as, respectively, a single named affliction versus every kind of clinging serving as karma's own condition — and asks why past karma's own condition is named only as ignorance, while present karma's is named in full. The treatise's answer turns on what can actually be known: a past deed's own realm, destiny, birth-mode, continent, developmental stage, motivating affliction, and object are all now unknowable, yet ignorance was certainly present at the moment any karma was ever committed, so it alone is named; present karma, by contrast, is coarse, fresh, and fully observable, so every specific affliction behind it can be named precisely.

Chinese Source

無明緣行取緣有。有何差別。答無明緣行者。廣說如前。此業緣。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ten-item list of what becomes unknowable about a past deed — its realm, destiny, birth-mode, continent, developmental stage, basis, preparatory effort, motivating affliction, continuum, and object — reads like a small taxonomy of everything that makes an action traceable in the first place, and the treatise's own point is that once a deed recedes into the genuinely inaccessible past, only the single, universal fact of ignorance's presence survives as knowable, everything else about that ancient choice having simply dissolved past recovery.

2. An Arhat's Own Karma: Neither Formations Nor Becoming, Yet Classed With Both

The treatise closes this thread with a genuinely delicate case: an arhat's own remaining karma cannot properly be called “ignorance conditions the formations” or “clinging conditions becoming,” since an arhat possesses neither ignorance nor clinging at all — yet, once that karma's fruit ripens, it is still classed, by analogy, within the formations-limb, and, if not yet ripened, within the becoming-limb, resembling these categories in kind even though, strictly speaking, it falls outside the twelve limbs of existence altogether.

Chinese Source

當知攝在行支分中。未與果者。當知攝在有支分中。是彼類故。然非十二有支所攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

it should be understood as subsumed within the portion of the formations-limb; if its fruit has not yet ripened, it should be understood as subsumed within the portion of the becoming-limb, being of that same general kind — yet it is, in truth, not actually included among the twelve limbs of existence themselves.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Classifying an arhat's karma “by resemblance” rather than by strict definitional fit is a quietly important methodological admission: the twelve-link schema, built entirely around affliction-driven causation, simply has no proper category for karma performed without any affliction behind it at all, and rather than forcing an awkward fit or declaring the schema broken, the treatise lets this one edge case sit just outside its own official boundary, classified only for convenience of exposition.

3. Wisdom and Ignorance: Two Roots, Two Leaders, Two Mutual Antidotes

A new pairing opens: why does the treatise now take up ignorance alongside wisdom (vidyā) together? Because the two are mirror-image roots of the defiled and the pure, mirror-image leaders of every unwholesome and every wholesome dharma respectively, each other's own proximate antidote, mutually opposed to one another, and yet — despite never being mutually inclusive of each other — sharing exactly the same possible objects, both alike capable of taking the four noble truths, the contaminated and uncontaminated, and the conditioned and unconditioned as their object.

Chinese Source

頗有行緣無明不緣明耶。乃至廣說。問何故此中因明無明而作論耶。答是作論者意欲爾故。乃至廣說。復次此二俱是根本法故。謂雜染品法無明為根本。清淨品法明為根本。復次此二俱是上首法故。如說。無明為上首。無明為前相。生無量種惡不善法。及起此類無慚無愧。明為上首。明為前相。生無量種清淨善法。及起此類增上慚愧。復次明與無明近相治故。謂無明是明近對治。明是無明近對治。復次明與無明互相違故。謂無明違明明違無明。復次明與無明互不相攝。而所緣境互相攝故。謂俱緣四聖諦俱緣有漏無漏。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The observation that wisdom and ignorance share an identical range of possible objects despite being total opposites in every other respect is a genuinely subtle point: two things can disagree completely about what a given object actually is or means while still, in a purely formal sense, being capable of taking that very same object — ignorance and wisdom are not opposed by looking at different things, but by seeing the very same four truths in diametrically opposed ways.

4. How Broad or Narrow Is 'The Formations'? A Tour Through Scripture's Own Vocabulary

A careful lexical survey shows the word “formations” (行) shifting meaning across different scriptural contexts — sometimes meaning the full five aggregates of clinging, sometimes karma alone, sometimes bare volition, sometimes only the dissociated-formations aggregate, sometimes in-breath-and-out-breath or applied-and-sustained thought or perception-and-volition when qualified as “bodily,” “verbal,” or “mental” action — before settling that, in “ignorance conditions the formations,” the word carries its very broadest sense, every conditioned dharma, since both wisdom and ignorance alike can serve as its condition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This survey functions as a standing warning against assuming a single Chinese or Sanskrit technical term always carries identical scope wherever it appears in scripture: “formations” swings from the narrowest possible sense (bare volition alone) to the broadest (every conditioned dharma) depending entirely on which scriptural context is in view, and getting the pairing right — which sense of “formations” a given citation intends — turns out to be the deciding factor in whether an apparent contradiction between two passages is real or merely terminological.

5. Every Kind of Formation Can Take Wisdom or Ignorance as Its Condition

Having settled that “formations” means every conditioned dharma here, the treatise runs its exhaustive combinatorics: of the eleven total kinds of formations across the three realms plus the uncontaminated, none take neither wisdom nor ignorance as their cause outright, but every one of them, through the maximally permissive dominant-condition alone if nothing else, can always be said to take both as a condition in some sense — desire-realm unwholesome formations taking ignorance as four full causes, form-realm wholesome formations taking neither as cause but still three conditions, and so on through every remaining combination.

The acquisitions co-obtained together with the very first instance of wisdom have neither wisdom nor ignorance as their cause, but do possess only one condition — the dominant condition. This, then, is the abbreviated Vibhāṣā treatment of this topic here. 9. Neither Ignorance Nor Wisdom Alone: The Final Combinatorics

The treatise completes its fourfold survey of formations against wisdom and ignorance — those conditioned by ignorance but not wisdom (ignorance's own ripening, and every defiled formation); those conditioned by wisdom but not ignorance (every uncontaminated formation except the very first instance of wisdom); those conditioned by neither (contaminated wholesome formations and the bulk of the undefined); and, strikingly, none conditioned by both together, since wisdom and ignorance are separated by too vast a distance ever to jointly cause a single dharma, exactly as a memorable verse declares space and earth, the ocean's two shores, and sunrise and sunset all separated — yet the true Dharma and false teaching separated by a distance farther still.

11. Why Contaminated Wisdom Never Earns the Name 'Wisdom'

The treatise's own etymology of “ignorance” and “wisdom” unfolds through an escalating series of ever-more-demanding criteria: mere failure to penetrate, comprehend, or understand, taking delusion as its own characteristic, defines ignorance; but the reverse — being able to penetrate, comprehend, and understand — is not enough to earn the name “wisdom,” since even contaminated wisdom does this much. True wisdom requires penetrating the four noble truths thoroughly, completely, decisively, and irreversibly — never again failing to see them once seen, never again slipping back into doubt or mistaken views once truly known.
