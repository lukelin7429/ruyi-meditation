What a Practitioner Possesses

Fascicle 168 continues the Subsumption Nyāsa (攝納息第三之三) with a new analytical technique: the doctrine of possession (成就), cross-tabulating exactly how many dhyānas, immeasurables, formless spheres, liberations, bases of vanquishing, totality-bases, and kinds of knowledge a practitioner simultaneously possesses, depending on rebirth-realm, exhausted craving, and path-stage — before turning to the seven types of noble person and their own possession-inventories. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. A New Question: What Does a Practitioner Actually Possess?

Fascicle 168 opens a genuinely new analytical technique within the Subsumption Nyāsa (攝納息第三之三): not what subsumes what, or what correlates with what, but the doctrine of possession (成就) — given that someone possesses the first dhyāna, how many of the four dhyānas does that same person simultaneously possess, and under what conditions? The variables multiply immediately: which realm one is currently reborn into, how much craving for that realm or the one above it has been exhausted, whether one is an ordinary worldling or a noble one, and whether a given attainment was acquired through birth or through freedom from defilement.

Ru-Yi Translation

If one possesses the first dhyāna, and so on — and so on at length. Question: why is this treatise composed? Answer: in order to refute those who deny that the very nature of possession and non-possession is anything real, and in order to make plain that the nature of possession and non-possession is indeed genuinely real, this treatise is composed. If one possesses the first dhyāna, and the rest, how many of the four dhyānas, and the rest, does one possess, and how many does one not possess? Answer: if one possesses the first dhyāna, then, with respect to the four dhyānas, one possesses either one, two, three, or four of them. "One" refers to someone whose craving for the Brahmā world has not yet been exhausted — this covers both a worldling and a noble one reborn in the desire realm or the Brahmā world: if reborn in the desire realm, one possesses the first dhyāna only through having attained it by freedom from defilement; if reborn in the Brahmā world, one possesses the first dhyāna either through freedom from defilement or through birth — and, because one's craving for the Brahmā world has not yet been exhausted, one does not possess any of the upper three dhyānas. "Two" refers to someone whose craving for the Brahmā world has been exhausted but whose craving for what lies above it has not yet been exhausted.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The recurring principle that a lower ground's unexhausted craving guarantees possession of the attainment above it, while an exhausted lower craving guarantees its loss, encodes a whole theory of spiritual momentum: attainment, in this system, is never simply added on top of what came before — climbing to a new height always costs something at the level just left behind.

2. The Four Immeasurables and the Four Formless Spheres

The possession-analysis continues through the four immeasurables — which one loses entirely upon rebirth into the formless realm, since they cannot exist without a form-realm basis — and then through the four formless spheres themselves, tracking exactly how many of these four a practitioner possesses depending on how far up the ladder of exhausted craving he has climbed.

3. The Eight Liberations, Tracked Rung by Rung

The treatise now applies the same possession-analysis to the eight liberations, working through every possible count from zero to all eight, cross-referenced against exactly which realm a practitioner is reborn into and exactly how far their craving has been exhausted — including the notable case of someone reborn in Śubhakṛtsna who has not yet exhausted that ground's own craving, and so, remarkably, possesses none of the eight liberations at all, despite already possessing the first dhyāna itself.

4. The Bases of Vanquishing and the Totality-Bases

The possession-analysis proceeds through the eight bases of vanquishing and the ten totality-bases, each showing the identical underlying logic: an attainment is possessed only once its specific prerequisite has been cultivated, and is relinquished the moment a practitioner is reborn beyond the ground it depends on, regardless of how many other attainments that same practitioner has accumulated along the way.

"One" refers to someone whose craving for the desire realm has been exhausted but whose craving for what lies above it has not yet been exhausted. "Two" refers to someone whose craving for the Brahmā world has been exhausted but whose craving for what lies above it has not yet been exhausted. "Three" refers to someone whose craving for Ābhāsvara has been exhausted but whose craving for what lies above it has not yet been exhausted. "Four" refers to someone whose craving for Śubhakṛtsna has been exhausted. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The explicit qualifier that this whole possession-inventory depends on progressing "by successive stages" is the treatise's own safety valve: it flags, before a sharp-eyed reader can object, that someone who skips ahead by relying on an already-attained excellent dhyāna would break the tidy correspondence just laid out — a rare moment of the text pre-empting its own counterexample.
