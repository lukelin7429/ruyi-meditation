Every Dharma Contains Itself

Fascicle 59 completes the Single-Class-of-Conduct Nyāsa's tense-traversal machinery — extending craving's ledger through combinations of up to eight fetters, then naming the four methods that structure the whole apparatus — before opening an entirely new inquiry. The Vibhāṣā refutes the Vibhājyavādin claim that dharmas contain another's nature, tracing the consequence that would follow for the entire path of cultivation if it were true, and then enumerates, with exhaustive precision, exactly how many of the ninety-eight latent tendencies each traditional afflictive category contains — arriving, at the very end, at the irreducible principle that every dharma contains only itself. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 59 completes the Single-Class-of-Conduct Nyāsa's own tense-traversal machinery, then opens an entirely new topic within the same nyāsa: how many latent tendencies (隨眠, anuśaya) each fetter and each of the many traditional afflictive categories “contains.” Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Craving Against View: The Ledger Concludes

Fascicle 59 opens by completing craving's cross-tense comparison against the view-fetter — a shared-characteristic fetter whose own past and future binding depend simply on whether it remains uncut, rather than on the more elaborate arisen/not-arisen distinction that governs craving itself — working through every tense combination before generalizing the entire pattern to grasping and doubt, and noting the slight technical differences that keep each comparison from being a perfect copy of the last.

Ru-Yi Translation

For a given matter that is bound by the past craving-fetter, is it likewise bound by the past view-fetter? The answer: only if it remains uncut — that is, with respect to a given matter, if craving previously arose there and remains uncut, and view likewise remains uncut, then this matter likewise possesses the sense of being bound by the past view-fetter; if, with respect to a given matter, craving previously arose there and remains uncut, yet view has already been cut off, then this matter possesses no sense of being bound by the past view-fetter at all — as when the subsequent cognition of the path has already arisen, and, with respect to the dharmas abandoned by cultivation, craving previously arose there and remains uncut; or when the subsequent cognition of its arising has already arisen, and, with respect to the dharmas dissociated from the view-fetter abandoned by seeing cessation and the path, craving previously arose there and remains uncut. For this reason, the text here speaks only, in general, of remaining uncut, without specifying the stage prior to the subsequent cognition of the path's own arising, since at that stage there is likewise craving present without view. The fetters that delude with respect to a shared characteristic, once uncut in the past, certainly bind every matter across all three times, and are therefore not spoken of indefinitely, unlike craving and the rest. Suppose there is binding by the past view-fetter — is there likewise binding by the past craving-fetter? The answer: if it previously arose there and remains uncut, it binds; if it never previously arose there, or, having arisen, was already cut off, it does not bind — that is, with respect to a given matter, if view previously arose there and remains uncut, and craving likewise previously arose there and remains uncut, then this matter likewise possesses the sense of being bound by the past craving-fetter; if, with respect to a given matter, view previously arose there and remains uncut, yet craving never previously arose there — even though it may have arisen elsewhere, with respect to this particular matter it is likewise named as never having arisen — or, having arisen, was already cut off, then this matter possesses no sense of being bound by the past craving-fetter at all. For a given matter that is bound by the past craving-fetter, is it likewise bound by the future view-fetter? The answer: only if it remains uncut.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The treatise pauses mid-ledger to flag a genuinely subtle exception: a person between the arising of one cognition and the next can possess craving without view in a way that looks, on the surface, like it should be indefinite but is in fact structurally guaranteed — a reminder that this whole cross-tense apparatus, however mechanical it appears, is tracking real distinctions in a practitioner's own progress along the path, not running an empty combinatorial exercise.

2. From Two Fetters to Eight: Extending the Ledger

Having exhausted craving against every other fetter individually, the treatise scales the entire method up one dimension: comparing pairs of fetters against a third, then triples against a fourth, continuing all the way to eight fetters compared against one, cycling through craving, hatred, conceit, ignorance, view, grasping, doubt, envy, and stinginess as each is removed from the leading group and replaced by the next in line.

3. The Seven Sevens: Two Orderings, One Wasteful

The treatise pushes the combinatorial method to its limit, constructing forty-nine total clauses — seven sets of seven — by comparing craving, hatred, and conceit against one another across every grouping of the three tenses. Remarkably, it works through two entirely different orderings for generating these forty-nine clauses, then explicitly judges the first "a wasted effort, of no benefit to the text and no benefit to the meaning," since it restates the same content redundantly, before presenting a second ordering that avoids the repetition.

4. Naming the Four Methods

A closing question ties off the entire tense-traversal apparatus spanning fascicles 58 and 59: what distinguishes single-class-of-conduct, traversing-the-six, the lesser seven, and the greater seven from one another? The answer offers two complementary explanations — the four methods differ simply because they are named differently, and, more substantively, because each follows its own distinct procedural rule for how many fetters are compared, whether the tense is held fixed, and whether dissimilar or similar dharmas are being set against each other.

5. A New Question: What Does "Containing" Mean?

The fascicle pivots entirely, opening a new inquiry into how many of the ninety-eight latent tendencies each fetter and afflictive category "contains." 6. Refuting "Containing Another's Nature"

The refutation lands with precision: if one dharma's own nature could genuinely contain another's, then a single dharma's own nature would have to be every dharma at once, its arising would entail the arising of everything, and — most damningly for practice itself — cutting off what is abandoned by seeing suffering would automatically cut off everything abandoned by seeing its arising, cessation, and the path as well, rendering every subsequent stage of the path's own cultivation entirely useless. The Vibhajyavādins' own citations are then reread as provisional language — "sustaining," "expedient means," "attracting," "conforming to" — never as containing in the ultimate sense, which depends on neither time nor cause, illustrated by a verse: there is no craving or hatred that arises without a cause.

7. The Benefit of Seeing Self-Nature Alone

Contemplating that each dharma contains only its own nature removes the notion of a self and the notion of a single, unified mass — the very notions that, left unchallenged, feed the growth of craving, hatred, and delusion. Material dharmas come to be seen as a heap of flour soon to scatter, immaterial dharmas as never coexisting and soon to fade, and all conditioned dharmas together as a ball of sand blown apart by the wind — a contemplation that seeds, in turn, the three gates of liberation: emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness, culminating in the three enlightenments and the realization of eternal quiescence.

11. Why Self-Nature Contains Itself

The fascicle closes with a direct philosophical account of why a dharma's own nature is properly said to "contain" itself at all: because own-nature, with respect to own-nature, is existent, real, and apprehensible; because it is never different, external, separate, or empty with respect to itself; because it never fails to have already existed, to exist now, or to exist in the future; and because it neither increases nor decreases — not, the treatise is careful to note, the way a hand grasps a finger or fingers pinch a garment, but the way each dharma simply holds fast to its own substance so that it does not scatter and decay.

Fascicle 59 Complete

This closes fascicle 59 of Ru-Yi's translation, drawn from the treatise's true CBETA boundary. Fascicle 59 completed the Single-Class-of-Conduct Nyāsa's cross-tense ledger for craving, extended it through combinations of up to eight fetters at once, and named the four methods — single-class-of-conduct, traversing-the-six, the lesser seven, the greater seven — that structure this whole stretch of the treatise. It then opened an entirely new inquiry: refuting the Vibhājyavādin view that dharmas contain another's nature, tracing the soteriological stakes of that refutation, and enumerating exactly how many of the ninety-eight latent tendencies each traditional afflictive category contains, down to the irreducible principle that each dharma, in the end, contains only itself.
