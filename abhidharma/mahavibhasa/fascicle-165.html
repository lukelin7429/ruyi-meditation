The Art of Leaping Between States

Fascicle 165 applies the four conditions systematically to every dhyāna, resolves a dispute over which knowledge-class precedes the formless attainments, and lays out a detailed two-version training manual for the leaping-concentration — the practice of skipping directly between non-adjacent meditative states. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. Fascicle 165 continues the Object-Condition Nyāsa (緣納息第二之四) within the Concentration Aggregate (定蘊第七). Terms established across fascicles 93–164 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Four Kinds of "First," and What a First Uncontaminated Entry Guarantees

Fascicle 165 opens by distinguishing four kinds of "first" entry into an uncontaminated dhyāna — first entering correctly determined liberation from birth, first attaining a fruit, first freedom from defilement, first refining one's faculties — then asks a precise question: at the very moment one first enters an uncontaminated dhyāna or formless attainment, are all the other future uncontaminated dharmas thereby cultivated in that same instant guaranteed to share its exact investigation/reflection status, its feeling-faculty, or its own formless-sphere classification?

若最初入無漏初靜慮。乃至廣說。初有四種。一入正性決定初。二得果初。三離染初。四轉根初。此中依二初作論。謂得果及轉根。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The answer's own structure is quietly elegant: a single decisive moment of entry cultivates an entire future harvest of dharmas at once, yet that harvest is not uniform — it fans out across every compatible grade and quality the underlying ground permits, so that one moment of breakthrough plants seeds for a whole spectrum of future states rather than a single repeated copy of itself.

2. Why Dhyāna Asks About "Association," but the Formless Asks About "Subsumption"

A extended debate addresses a curious asymmetry in how this treatise phrases its questions: for the dhyānas, it asks what a given future dharma is "associated with" (investigation, reflection, a particular feeling-faculty), while for the formless spheres, it asks only what a dharma is "subsumed under" — nine separate explanations are offered, ranging from mere stylistic variety to the dhyānas' own greater richness of characteristics, before a parallel debate addresses why the dhyānas are said to subsume the formless spheres, but not the reverse.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The proposed asymmetry between dhyāna and the formless — that a dhyāna-ground is attained first and a formless one only afterward, so the latter is subordinate to the former in a way that never reverses — quietly encodes a whole cosmology's worth of hierarchy into a simple grammatical choice: which of two categories "contains" the other in this treatise's own technical vocabulary is never arbitrary, but tracks a real asymmetry in how a practitioner's own path actually unfolds.

3. The Four Conditions, Systematically Applied to the First and Second Dhyānas

A major new inquiry opens, explicitly aimed at refuting the Sautrāntika view that the four conditions (causal, immediately-preceding, object, and dominant) are not genuinely real — and proceeds to work out, with exhaustive precision, exactly how many of these four conditions each of the flavor-associated, pure, and uncontaminated first and second dhyānas serves as for every other dharma in the system, down to the four portions of the pure dhyāna and the eight classes of uncontaminated wisdom.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four vivid analogies offered for the four conditions — the causal condition functioning like a seed, the immediately-preceding condition like clearing a path, the object-condition like a walking-stick, the dominant condition like granting one's consent — turn an otherwise dry taxonomic exercise into something almost tactile: each condition contributes to an effect's arising in a genuinely different way, and the metaphors make vivid exactly how each contribution differs from the others.

4. The Same Analysis Continues Upward, and a Live Dispute Over Which Knowledge Precedes the Formless

The four-condition analysis is abbreviated for the third dhyāna through the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, referring the reader to the base treatise, before a genuinely live dispute opens: which kind of uncontaminated third or fourth dhyāna — dharma-knowledge or class-knowledge — can immediately precede a pure or uncontaminated formless attainment? Question: immediately following what wholesome root is one capable of leaping? Answer: immediately following a wholesome root belonging to the immeasurables, the liberations, the bases of totality, the totality-bases, or the superknowledges, one is never capable of leaping — it is only immediately following a wholesome root taking the aspect of impermanence and the like that one is capable of leaping. Why so? Because only a fierce, vigorous, and dominant wholesome root is capable of the leaping-concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the leap requires immediately preceding it with a specifically fierce, impermanence-focused wholesome root — never the gentler immeasurables, liberations, or superknowledges — is a small but telling detail about what this tradition takes real spiritual virtuosity to require: the capacity to move fluidly and unpredictably between radically different states of mind turns out to depend not on serenity but on a sharpened, almost aggressive clarity about impermanence itself.
