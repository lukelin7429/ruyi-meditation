1. The Four Dhyānas' Own-Nature

A wholly new topic opens within the same nyāsa — the four dhyānas — composed to settle a doubt the Prakaraṇapāda's own bare definitions might otherwise raise: since that treatise speaks only of the wholesome five aggregates gathered within each dhyāna, one might wrongly conclude the dhyānas are solely wholesome, never defiled or neutral. This treatise displays instead that all four dhyānas pervade the wholesome, the defiled, and the neutral-and-unobscured alike, each dhyāna taking the five aggregates of its own ground as its own-nature.

Chinese Source

四靜慮者。謂初靜慮。第二靜慮。第三靜慮。第四靜慮。問何故作此論。答欲令疑者得決定故。謂品類足說。云何初靜慮。謂初靜慮攝善五蘊。乃至云何第四靜慮。謂第四靜慮攝善五蘊。彼唯說善靜慮。或有生疑。靜慮唯善非染。亦非無覆無記。為決彼疑顯四靜慮。通善及染無覆無記故作斯論。問此四靜慮自性云何。答各以自地五蘊為自性。是名靜慮自性我物自體相分本性。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the four dhyānas: this refers to the first dhyāna, the second dhyāna, the third dhyāna, and the fourth dhyāna. One asks: for what reason is this treatise composed? The answer: it is in order to bring certainty to those in doubt — that is, the Prakaraṇapāda states: what is the first dhyāna? It refers to the wholesome five aggregates gathered within the first dhyāna, and so on, up through, what is the fourth dhyāna? It refers to the wholesome five aggregates gathered within the fourth dhyāna. That passage speaks only of the wholesome dhyāna, and some may thereby give rise to the doubt that the dhyānas are solely wholesome, neither defiled nor neutral-and-unobscured — and, in order to settle this doubt, displaying that the four dhyānas pervade the wholesome, the defiled, and the neutral-and-unobscured alike, this treatise is composed. One asks: what is the own-nature of these four dhyānas? The answer: each takes the five aggregates of its own ground as its own-nature. Such, then, is named the own-nature of the dhyānas — their own thing, own substance, own characteristic, own portion, and own inherent nature.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Correcting a doubt that a companion treatise's own economical phrasing might inadvertently invite is a small but recurring genre within this literature: the Prakaraṇapāda was never wrong, exactly, in speaking only of the wholesome dhyāna — it simply had no occasion there to address the defiled and neutral cases — and this treatise's own job is precisely to close that inferential gap before a careless reader draws the wrong conclusion from silence.

2. Why Named "Dhyāna"? Cutting Off Fetters or Correct Observation?

Is dhyāna named for its capacity to cut off the fetters, or for its capacity toward correct observation? Each candidate alone seems to overreach: if cutting off fetters is sufficient, the formless attainments, too, ought to count as dhyāna, since they too cut off fetters; if correct observation is sufficient, even an ordinary desire-realm concentration ought to qualify. The first response distinguishes by scope — only an attainment capable of cutting off both the unwholesome and the neutral-and-unobscured fetters together earns the name, the formless attainments managing only the neutral-and-unobscured. A further refinement addresses why even the higher form-realm grounds, which have nothing further of the desire realm's own fetters left to cut off, still count as possessing this capacity: they retain the antidote of wearying and destroying even after the antidote of outright cutting-off has done its work and left nothing more to sever.

Chinese Source

已說自性。所以今當說。問何故名靜慮。為能斷結故名靜慮。為能正觀故名靜慮。若能斷結故名靜慮。則無色定亦能斷結應名靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

Own-nature has already been discussed; what is to be discussed next shall now be stated. One asks: for what reason is it named dhyāna? Is it named dhyāna because it is capable of cutting off the fetters, or because it is capable of correct observation? If it is named dhyāna because it is capable of cutting off the fetters, then the formless attainments, too, are capable of cutting off the fetters, and ought likewise to be named dhyāna.

若能正觀故名靜慮。則欲界三摩地亦能正觀。應名靜慮。有作是說。以能斷結故名靜慮。問諸無色定亦能斷結應名靜慮。答若定能斷不善無記二種結者名為靜慮。諸無色定唯斷無記非不善故不名靜慮。問若作是說。唯未至定可名靜慮。上地不斷不善結故。答上地雖無彼斷對治。而有不善厭壞對治。以能厭壞亦名能斷。問若作是說。上地滅道法智品。及彼一切類智品應非靜慮。皆非欲界斷及厭壞二對治故。答彼於欲界雖無全界全地對治。而彼界地容有不善厭壞對治。由此勢力餘亦得名。復次四靜慮中有能對治不善結者。無色全無。故靜慮名不通無色。尊者妙音作如是說。色界六地於欲界結皆有斷對治及厭壞對治。然未至定已斷彼結。餘地對治無彼可斷。雖無彼可斷而有對治用。如日三分皆能破暗。初分已破餘無可破。又如六人共一怨家。一人已殺餘無可殺。又如六燈皆能破暗。

If it is named dhyāna because it is capable of correct observation, then a concentration of the desire realm, too, is capable of correct observation, and ought likewise to be named dhyāna. There are those who say: it is named dhyāna because it is capable of cutting off the fetters. One asks: the formless attainments, too, are capable of cutting off the fetters, and ought likewise to be named dhyāna. The answer: whatever attainment is capable of cutting off both the unwholesome and the neutral-and-unobscured fetters is named dhyāna; the formless attainments are capable of cutting off only the neutral-and-unobscured, not the unwholesome, and it is for this reason that they are not named dhyāna. One asks: if one speaks in this way, then only the stage of not-yet-arrived would be nameable as dhyāna, since the higher grounds do not cut off the unwholesome fetters. The answer: although the higher grounds lack that antidote of cutting-off, they nonetheless possess the antidote of wearying and destroying, and, since being capable of wearying and destroying is likewise named being capable of cutting off, this raises no difficulty. One asks: if one speaks in this way, then the higher grounds' own portion of dharma-knowledge associated with the cessation-and-path, together with the entire portion of subsequent-knowledge associated with it, ought not to be dhyāna at all, since none of them possess either the antidote of cutting-off with respect to the desire realm or the antidote of wearying and destroying. The answer: although these lack, with respect to the desire realm, the antidote pertaining to the entire realm and the entire ground, that realm and ground still admit of the antidote of wearying and destroying, and it is by the strength of this that the rest, too, obtain the name. Further, among the four dhyānas there exists what is capable of serving as an antidote to the unwholesome fetters, while the formless attainments possess this entirely not at all, and it is for this reason that the name dhyāna does not extend to the formless. The Venerable Ghoṣa speaks as follows: the six grounds of the form realm each possess, with respect to the desire realm's own fetters, both the antidote of cutting-off and the antidote of wearying and destroying — yet it is only the stage of not-yet-arrived that has already cut off these fetters; the remaining grounds' own antidote has nothing left there for it to cut off, and, although there is nothing left to cut off, the function of the antidote is still present, exactly as the sun's three portions of the day are each capable of dispelling darkness — the first portion having already dispelled it, the remaining portions have nothing left to dispel; and exactly as six men together face a single enemy — one man having already slain him, the remaining five have no one left to slay; and exactly as six lamps are each capable of dispelling darkness —

持一入室其暗已除。餘五入時無暗可破。如是六地於欲界結。皆有斷能非唯未至。若不爾者依上五地入見道時。應不證得欲見所斷諸結離繫。既能證得。故知六地於欲界結有斷對治。復次若定能斷見修所斷二結盡者名為靜慮。諸無色定唯能斷修所斷結盡故非靜慮。

bringing just one of them into the room dispels its darkness entirely, so that the remaining five, brought in afterward, find no darkness left to dispel. So too, these six grounds each possess the capacity to cut off the desire realm's own fetters, not the stage of not-yet-arrived alone — for were it not so, then, upon entering the path of seeing by relying on any of the five higher grounds, one ought not to attain the freedom from binding of the fetters abandoned by seeing within the desire realm, and yet one is indeed able to attain it, and it is thereby known that these six grounds all possess the antidote of cutting off the desire realm's own fetters. Further, whatever attainment is capable of exhausting both the fetters abandoned by seeing and those abandoned by cultivation is named dhyāna; the formless attainments are capable only of exhausting the fetters abandoned by cultivation, and it is for this reason that they are not dhyāna.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between the antidote of cutting-off and the antidote of wearying-and-destroying is a fine piece of conceptual housekeeping: it lets a higher ground count as possessing a real capacity to counteract the desire realm's fetters even once there is nothing left there for it to actually cut, since the mere disposition to weary and destroy such fetters, were they present, is itself enough to earn the name — capacity need not currently have an object to act upon in order to be genuinely possessed.

3. Six Lamps Dispelling Darkness: The Final Resolution

The Venerable Ghoṣa illustrates this same point vividly: exactly as the sun's three portions of a single day are each capable of dispelling darkness, though only the first portion has anything left to dispel, or as six lamps brought one by one into a room each possess the capacity to banish darkness though only the first lamp finds any darkness there to banish, so too do all six grounds of the form realm possess a genuine antidote to the desire realm's own fetters, not the stage of not-yet-arrived alone — proven by the fact that entering the path of seeing from any of the five higher grounds still yields freedom from the desire realm's own fetters abandoned by seeing. The debate closes by requiring both criteria together: only what is capable of cutting off the fetters and of correct observation, comprehensively and without exception, earns the name dhyāna — the formless attainments possessing stillness without reflection, an ordinary concentration possessing reflection without stillness, and the dhyānas of the form realm alone possessing both, stillness being equipoise and reflection being comprehensive observation.

Chinese Source

復次若能斷結五蘊俱生能為所依起多功德。能具攝受四支五支。能發六通具四通行。三種變現三明三根三道三地四沙門果九遍知道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, whatever is capable of cutting off the fetters, arises together with the five aggregates, is able to serve as a basis giving rise to many merits, is capable of comprehensively gathering the four or five limbs, is capable of giving rise to the six superknowledges endowed with the four modes of superknowledge conduct, the three kinds of transformation-manifestation, the three illuminations, the three faculties, the three paths, the three grounds, the four fruits of the renunciant, and the nine paths of full understanding,

見修二道法類二智。及忍智者名為靜慮。諸無色定雖能斷結。而不具上所說功德故非靜慮。復有說者。以能正觀故名靜慮。問若爾欲界有三摩地。亦能正觀應名靜慮。答若能正觀亦能斷結名為靜慮。

belonging to the paths of seeing and cultivation, the dharma- and subsequent-knowledge, and the forbearances and knowledges, is named dhyāna. The formless attainments, although capable of cutting off the fetters, do not possess the merits stated above in full, and it is for this reason that they are not dhyāna. There are those who further say: it is named dhyāna because it is capable of correct observation. One asks: if so, a concentration of the desire realm, too, is capable of correct observation, and ought likewise to be named dhyāna. The answer: whatever is capable both of correct observation and of cutting off the fetters is named dhyāna;

欲界三摩地雖有能正觀。而不能斷結。故不名靜慮。復次若能正觀堅固難壞相續久住。於所緣境長時注意而不捨者名為靜慮。欲界三摩地無如是德故非靜慮。復次若三摩地具有定名定用。能正觀者名為靜慮。欲界三摩地雖有定名而無定用。如泥椽梁有名無用故非靜慮。復次若三摩地非散亂風之所搖動如密室燈。能正觀者名為靜慮。欲界三摩地多散亂風之搖動。如四衢燈故非靜慮。如是說者。

a concentration of the desire realm, although capable of correct observation, is not capable of cutting off the fetters, and it is for this reason that it is not named dhyāna. Further, whatever correct observation is firm, difficult to destroy, of long continuity and long abiding, attending upon its own object for a long time without relinquishing it, is named dhyāna; a concentration of the desire realm, lacking such merit as this, is not dhyāna. Further, whatever concentration possesses both the name of concentration and the function of concentration, being capable of correct observation, is named dhyāna; a concentration of the desire realm, although possessing the name of concentration, lacks its function, exactly as a rafter or beam made of mud possesses the name but not the function, and it is for this reason that it is not dhyāna. Further, whatever concentration is not shaken by the wind of distraction, exactly as a lamp within a sealed chamber, being capable of correct observation, is named dhyāna; a concentration of the desire realm is largely shaken by the wind of distraction, exactly as a lamp at a crossroads, and it is for this reason that it is not dhyāna. Those who speak in this way say:

要具二義方名靜慮。謂能斷結及能正觀。欲界三摩地雖能正觀而不能斷結。諸無色定雖能斷結。而不能正觀故非靜慮。復次若能遍觀遍斷結者名為靜慮。欲界三摩地雖能遍觀而不能遍斷結。諸無色定二義俱無故非靜慮。復次若能靜息一切煩惱。及能思慮一切所緣名為靜慮。欲界三摩地雖能思慮一切所緣。不能靜息一切煩惱。諸無色定兩義都無故非靜慮。復次諸無色定有靜無慮。欲界三摩地有慮無靜。色定俱有故名靜慮。靜謂等引。慮謂遍觀。故名靜慮。

it is only when both meanings are possessed together that something is named dhyāna — namely, being capable of cutting off the fetters and being capable of correct observation. A concentration of the desire realm, although capable of correct observation, is not capable of cutting off the fetters; the formless attainments, although capable of cutting off the fetters, are not capable of correct observation, and it is for this reason that neither is dhyāna. Further, whatever is capable of comprehensively observing and comprehensively cutting off the fetters is named dhyāna; a concentration of the desire realm, although capable of comprehensive observation, is not capable of comprehensively cutting off the fetters, and the formless attainments possess neither meaning whatsoever, and it is for this reason that neither is dhyāna. Further, whatever is capable of stilling every affliction and of reflecting upon every object is named dhyāna; a concentration of the desire realm, although capable of reflecting upon every object, is not capable of stilling every affliction, and the formless attainments possess neither meaning at all, and it is for this reason that neither is dhyāna. Further, the formless attainments possess stillness but lack reflection; a concentration of the desire realm possesses reflection but lacks stillness; the dhyānas of the form realm possess both together, and it is for this reason that they are named dhyāna — stillness referring to equipoise, reflection referring to comprehensive observation, and it is for this reason that they are named dhyāna.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The six-lamps image does more than merely restate the sun's own three-portions point in a second register — it generalizes the insight from a single continuous process (one day's passage) to a series of discrete, replaceable instances (lamp after lamp), showing that the same logic of "capacity without a remaining object" holds whether the underlying process is one continuous span or a sequence of separate events, which matters here since the six grounds of the form realm are precisely such a discrete sequence, not a single continuous unfolding.

4. The Eighteen Limbs, and Eleven Real Substances

The four dhyānas' own limbs number eighteen by name: five for the first (applied thought, sustained thought, joy, pleasure, one-pointedness of mind), four for the second (internal clarity and purity, joy, pleasure, one-pointedness), five for the third (equanimity of formations, correct mindfulness, correct wisdom, pleasure, one-pointedness), and four for the fourth (neutral feeling, purified equanimity, purified mindfulness, one-pointedness). Yet these eighteen names reduce to only eleven real substances, since several limbs simply recur under the same name across dhyānas — a proposal to reduce this further to ten, merging the pleasure of all three dhyānas that have it into one substance, is firmly rejected: the first two dhyānas' pleasure is the pleasure of tranquil ease, gathered within the formations-aggregate, while the third dhyāna's own pleasure is a distinct pleasure of feeling, gathered within the feeling-aggregate outright.

Chinese Source

四靜慮支總有十八。謂初靜慮有五支。一尋二伺三喜四樂五心一境性。第二靜慮有四支。一內等淨二喜三樂四心一境性。第三靜慮有五支。一行捨二正念三正慧四受樂五心一境性。第四靜慮有四支。

Ru-Yi Translation

The limbs of the four dhyānas number eighteen in all: the first dhyāna has five limbs — first, applied thought; second, sustained thought; third, joy; fourth, pleasure; fifth, one-pointedness of mind. The second dhyāna has four limbs — first, internal clarity and purity; second, joy; third, pleasure; fourth, one-pointedness of mind. The third dhyāna has five limbs — first, equanimity of formations; second, correct mindfulness; third, correct wisdom; fourth, experiencing pleasure; fifth, one-pointedness of mind. The fourth dhyāna has four limbs —

一不苦不樂受二行捨清淨三念清淨四心一境性。問四靜慮支名有十八實體有幾。答唯有十一。謂初靜慮支名與實體俱有五種。第二靜慮支雖有四。而三如前增內等淨。第三靜慮支雖有五。而第五如前但增前四。第四靜慮支雖有四。而後三如前但增第一。故靜慮支名有十八實體十一。復有說者。實體唯十。謂三靜慮樂合為一。評曰。彼不應作是說。初二靜慮是輕安樂。第三靜慮別是受樂。初二靜慮樂行蘊攝。第三靜慮樂受蘊攝。故前所說於理為善。如名實體名施設體施設。名異相體異相。名異性體異性。名差別體差別。名建立體建立。名覺體覺。應知亦爾。問此中何者是靜慮。何者是靜慮支。答心一境性是靜慮。以三摩地為自性故。此及所餘是靜慮支。問若三摩地是靜慮者。初第三靜慮應各唯四支。第二第四靜慮應各唯三支。則靜慮支應唯十四。云何乃說十八支耶。答三摩地是靜慮亦是靜慮支。餘是靜慮支非靜慮故有十八。如擇法是覺亦是覺支。餘是覺支非覺。正見是道亦是道支。餘是道支非道。離非時食是齋亦是齋支。餘是齋支非齋。此亦如是。如是名為靜慮支。自性我物自體相分本性。

first, feeling that is neither painful nor pleasurable; second, the purity of the equanimity of formations; third, the purity of mindfulness; fourth, one-pointedness of mind. One asks: these eighteen limb-names of the four dhyānas — how many real substances do they possess? The answer: only eleven. That is, the first dhyāna's own limb-names and their real substances both number five; the second dhyāna's limbs, though numbering four, are three of the same as before with the addition of internal clarity and purity; the third dhyāna's limbs, though numbering five, are the fourth of these being the same four as before, with only the fifth newly added; the fourth dhyāna's limbs, though numbering four, are the latter three the same as before, with only the first newly added — and it is for this reason that the dhyāna-limbs' own names number eighteen, while their real substances number eleven. There are those who further say: the real substances number only ten, holding that the pleasure of the three dhyānas is combined and counted as one. The compilers say: one ought not to speak in this way — the pleasure of the first two dhyānas is the pleasure of tranquil ease, while the third dhyāna's own pleasure is distinctly the pleasure of feeling; the pleasure of the first two dhyānas is gathered within the formations-aggregate, while the third dhyāna's own pleasure is gathered within the feeling-aggregate, and it is for this reason that the earlier statement accords with principle — exactly as name and substance, provisional name and provisional substance, distinct characteristic in name and distinct characteristic in substance, distinct nature in name and distinct nature in substance, distinction in name and distinction in substance, establishment in name and establishment in substance, and cognition in name and cognition in substance should all be understood in the corresponding manner. One asks: among these, which is the dhyāna itself, and which is the dhyāna-limb? The answer: one-pointedness of mind is the dhyāna itself, since it takes concentration as its own-nature; this, together with the rest, is the dhyāna-limb. One asks: if concentration is the dhyāna itself, then the first and third dhyānas ought each to have only four limbs, and the second and fourth dhyānas ought each to have only three limbs, so that the dhyāna-limbs ought to number only fourteen — why, then, does it speak of eighteen limbs? The answer: concentration is both the dhyāna itself and a dhyāna-limb; the rest are dhyāna-limbs but not the dhyāna itself, and it is for this reason that there are eighteen — exactly as the discernment of dharmas is itself enlightenment and is also a limb of enlightenment, the rest being limbs of enlightenment but not enlightenment itself; exactly as right view is itself the path and is also a limb of the path, the rest being limbs of the path but not the path itself; exactly as abstaining from eating at the wrong time is itself the fast and is also a limb of the fast, the rest being limbs of the fast but not the fast itself — this, too, is just the same. Such, then, is named the own-nature of the dhyāna-limbs — their own thing, own substance, own characteristic, own portion, and own inherent nature.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Refusing to collapse the first two dhyānas' pleasure together with the third dhyāna's own pleasure, even though both are called by the identical name, is another instance of this treatise's insistence that shared vocabulary never automatically implies shared substance: the aggregate each belongs to — formations versus feeling — is treated as decisive evidence that two things sharing a name may nonetheless be genuinely, categorically distinct kinds of experience.

5. The Meaning of a Dhyāna-Limb, and Five Tetralemmas

A dhyāna-limb earns its name through five overlapping senses: according with its dhyāna, bearing the heavy burden of drawing it forth, accomplishing the great matter of achieving it, firmly excelling in strengthening it, and serving as one of its own distinguishing members, exactly as a chariot's own distinct parts are each named a limb of the chariot. Concentration itself, one-pointedness of mind, is both the dhyāna itself and one of its own limbs, exactly as the discernment of dharmas is itself enlightenment yet also counted among its own limbs — accounting for how eighteen limb-names can exist even though a strict count of concentration alone across four dhyānas would yield only fourteen. 10. Why the First and Third Dhyānas Have Five Limbs, and Transcendence-Concentration

Why do the first and third dhyānas alone establish five limbs, against four for the second and fourth? Because the desire realm's own evils, and the second dhyāna's own heavy-ground utmost joy, are each uniquely difficult to cut off, break, and cross over, requiring a firmer, stronger antidote than the second and fourth dhyānas ever need. A further reason concerns the practice of transcendence-concentration, leaping directly from one non-adjacent dhyāna to another — matching limb-counts being easiest to transcend between, exactly as from a five-limbed concentration one most naturally leaps into another five-limbed one. Yet a practitioner may also leap from the limbed third dhyāna directly into the limbless sphere of infinite space, resolved by the principle that every undertaking, whether external (the minister Cirokara's twelve years perfecting alchemy before finally producing a single grain of gold and declaring himself able to make a mountain of it) or internal (a practitioner of psychic power first rising the height of half a sesame seed, then gradually, by ever-larger increments, learning to travel wherever he wills), is difficult only at its initial stage — matching limb-counts scaffolding transcendence-concentration's own earliest, hardest attempts, while a later, practiced mastery no longer requires any such scaffolding at all.

The answer: the second dhyāna's own equipoise is especially abundant, supremely sublime and pure, surpassing the first dhyāna's own, and it is for this reason that it alone is singled out. Further, the second dhyāna is drawn forth by concentration and nourished and grown by concentration, manifesting after the first dhyāna, and it is for this reason that it is named born of concentration, unlike the first dhyāna, which is neither drawn forth nor nourished and grown by concentration, manifesting after the desire realm's own mind, and it is for this reason that it is not named born of concentration. Further, the first dhyāna's own mind is sometimes concentrated, sometimes not, sometimes turning through an inward gateway, sometimes through an outward one, sometimes taking an internal matter as its object, sometimes an external one; the second dhyāna's own mind for the most part abides in concentration, for the most part turns solely through the inward gateway, and takes solely an internal matter as its object, and it is for this reason that it is named born of concentration. Further, the second dhyāna extinguishes the very root of speech — as for the root of speech: this refers to applied and sustained thought, as a scripture states, it is only by applied and sustained thought that speech is able to be uttered, not without applied and sustained thought — the second dhyāna's own applied and sustained thought having already ceased, it possesses no root of speech, and it is for this reason that it is said to be born of concentration. Further, the second dhyāna is named the nobles' own silence, and it is for this reason that it is named born of concentration, as a scripture states, the Buddha told Maudgalyāyana: you should not make light of the second dhyāna, since this is the nobles' own dharma of silence. It is owing to causes and conditions such as these, of every kind, that being born of concentration exists solely within the second dhyāna. As for joy and pleasure: joy refers to the joy-faculty; pleasure refers to the pleasure of tranquil ease.

復次喜受蘊攝。樂行蘊攝。第二靜慮具足住者。得獲成就第二靜慮善五蘊得獲成就名具足住。

Further, joy is gathered within the feeling-aggregate; pleasure is gathered within the formations-aggregate. As for one dwells having fully attained the second dhyāna: this refers to having obtained, acquired, and attained the possession of the second dhyāna's own wholesome five aggregates, this obtaining, acquiring, and attaining of possession being named dwelling having fully attained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That three named masters each reach for a different simile to explain the identical phenomenon — faith clarifying the second dhyāna's own mind — while never actually disagreeing about the underlying doctrine, is characteristic of how this treatise treats its own recorded masters: their disagreement is almost entirely a matter of which image best illuminates a settled point, water calmed after waves versus water cleared of mud versus a mind simply steadied by trust, rather than any genuine dispute over what internal clarity and purity actually is.
