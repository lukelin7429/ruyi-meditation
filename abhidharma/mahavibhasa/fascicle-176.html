Better to Fall Than to Peak Without Roots

Fascicle 176 continues the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之三), completing the five Pure Abodes' own etymology, then turning to when a trainee truly counts as "training," the four kinds of person from stream-follower to one who has reached the far shore, the striking contrast between Devadatta's fall and Udraka Rāmaputra's meditative peak, self-abiding and ultimate restraint, and a close examination of exactly when one earns the name Bodhisattva. 1. The Five Pure Abodes Named and Explained

Fascicle 176 opens by completing fascicle 175's discussion of the mixed cultivation of dhyāna, naming and etymologizing each of the five Pure Abodes in turn — Avṛha ("without affliction"), Atapa ("without heat"), Sudṛśa ("finely appearing"), Sudarśana ("finely seen"), and Akaniṣṭha ("the highest point of form") — each explained through multiple lenses: bodily and mental quality, clarity of insight into the four truths, and correspondence to the five graded levels of mixed cultivation established in the previous fascicle. The section closes by resolving an apparent tension with the Prakaraṇapāda over whether rebirth there is caused by karma or by meditation, concluding that both are jointly necessary — karma alone cannot produce it without the meditation to make it determinate, and the meditation alone cannot produce it without the karma to draw it forth.

As the Prajñaptiśāstra states, there are five Pure Abodes — namely, Avṛha, Atapa, Sudṛśa, Sudarśana, and Akaniṣṭha. What is meant by Avṛha? It is the shared class-nature belonging to a single kind of companion among the beings of Avṛha, attained by dependence, by substance, and by place, together with the neutral-and-unobstructed form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness of a being already born there — this is called Avṛha. Question: why is this heaven named Avṛha, "without affliction"? Answer: this is a mere conventional designation, a mere conventional notion, a name that is posited, a notion that is posited, established however one wishes, and need not necessarily correspond in every respect to its own name. Furthermore, the beings of that heaven have bodies free of affliction and minds free of affliction, experiencing throughout their entire existence a purely tranquil bliss unavailable to any lower ground, and it is for this reason that it is named without affliction. Furthermore, the beings of that heaven see with perfect clarity that suffering is truly suffering, that origin is truly origin, that cessation is truly cessation, and that the path is truly the path, being free of the coarse, heavy afflictions that arise on the lower grounds, and it is for this reason that it is named without affliction.

Question: is rebirth into the Pure Abode heavens drawn forth by karma, or by the mixed cultivation of dhyāna? And, granting either, what fault would follow? If it is by karma, then the mixed cultivation of dhyāna would be rendered entirely futile; if it is by the mixed cultivation of dhyāna, then this would contradict the Prakaraṇapāda's own statement, which says that one is reborn into the Pure Abode heavens through the mixed cultivation of dhyāna and through karma, and so on at length. Answer: some hold that it is drawn forth by karma. Question: would this not then render the mixed cultivation of dhyāna entirely futile? Answer: it is precisely through the mixed cultivation of dhyāna that the karma becomes determinate and fixed, and so it is not rendered futile. Furthermore, it is only after the mixed cultivation of dhyāna that one becomes capable of drawing forth that very volitional karma to manifest before one, and it is through this that one's own shared class-nature there is drawn forth. Certain other masters hold: it is by the mixed cultivation of dhyāna. Question: how, then, is the Prakaraṇapāda's own statement to be reconciled? Answer: what it calls "karma" there is precisely the mixed cultivation of dhyāna itself, spoken of in terms of karma. Furthermore, that treatise speaks first of the mixed cultivation of dhyāna in order to display one's earlier entry into that concentration, and speaks afterward of being reborn into the Pure Abodes through karma in order to display that, at the later stage, it is precisely through that power that one is reborn into the Pure Abodes. According to this latter account, it is both through the power of karma and through the mixed cultivation of dhyāna — that is, even should the volitional karma manifest, without the mixed cultivation of dhyāna, one still cannot be reborn there; and, even with the mixed cultivation of dhyāna, without the volitional karma manifesting, one likewise cannot be reborn there. It is for this reason that only when the volitional karma draws forth the mixed cultivation of dhyāna, causing it to become determinate and fixed, does one then become capable of being reborn there.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The final resolution — that karma and meditation are each individually necessary but neither is sufficient on its own — is a small masterclass in how this tradition handles an apparent contradiction between two authoritative sources: rather than declaring one text wrong, it finds a division of labor in which both statements turn out to be describing complementary halves of a single causal process.

2. When Is a Trainee Not Training?

The fascicle turns to a genuinely subtle puzzle: a trainee is defined as one who "trains in what is to be trained," yet the treatise must reconcile this with the fact that trainees sometimes simply rest in their own attainment without actively striving further. The resulting four-cornered analysis distinguishes a trainee merely "abiding in his own nature" — content with his current fruit, whether from illness, fatigue, or the simple thought that he has already come far enough — from an arhat or ordinary worldling who, though technically beyond or below the trainee category, is nevertheless actively striving toward some higher attainment.

The passage's own resolution — that a trainee's resolve is like a traveler who, when asked where he is going while resting mid-journey, still truthfully answers with his destination rather than "nowhere" — captures something genuinely important about how this tradition understood spiritual identity: a trainee's status was never thought to depend on constant, uninterrupted exertion, but on an unbroken underlying commitment that a mere pause in outward effort does nothing to dissolve.

3. Stream-Follower and Counter-Stream: Devadatta and the Two Vessels

The treatise turns to the four kinds of person — stream-follower, counter-stream, one abiding in the middle, and one who has reached the far shore — settling, after surveying many competing criteria, on a genuinely striking definition: the true dividing line is not moral conduct or meditative attainment at all, but whether one has ever planted "the wholesome roots conducive to liberation," however small an act might have done so. This section contains the fascicle's most vivid material — a extended contrast between Devadatta, who commits the gravest possible sins yet will become a pratyekabuddha superior to Śāriputra, and Udraka Rāmaputra, who reaches the very peak of mundane meditative attainment yet is doomed to an unending fall — illustrated by the parable of a broken golden vessel, which can always be remade, against a broken clay one, which cannot.

5. Self-Abiding, Ultimate Restraint, and Reaching the Far Shore

6. At What Point Is One Truly a Bodhisattva?

The fascicle closes with an extended treatment of bodhisattva doctrine, opening by puncturing a specific spiritual overconfidence: countless beings, after a single act of generosity or a moment of concentrated practice, boldly proclaim in a "lion's roar" that they are certain to become Buddhas — a presumption the treatise firmly rejects, insisting that even a genuine Bodhisattva, after the first two of his three incalculable eons of practice, still does not dare make such a declaration. True bodhisattva status, the treatise concludes, begins only at the third eon's culmination, with the cultivation of the karma that produces the excellent bodily marks — the exact point at which both his own attainment of awakening and his own orientation toward it become fully determined, and at which humans as well as gods first come to recognize him as such.

Question: if a sentient being gives rise to the aspiration for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, and that aspiration never regresses, then, from that very point onward, ought he not already to be called a bodhisattva? Why, then, is he named a bodhisattva only once he has performed and augmented the karma that ripens into the excellent bodily marks? Answer: only one whose own awakening is determined, and whose own orientation toward it is likewise determined, is named a true bodhisattva. From the very first arising of the aspiration clear through to before the cultivation of the karma of the excellent marks, although his own awakening is indeed determined, his own orientation toward it is not yet determined, and he cannot yet be named a true bodhisattva — only once he reaches the stage of cultivating the karma of the excellent marks does his own awakening become determined, and his own orientation toward it become determined as well, and it is for this reason that only at this point is he named a bodhisattva. Furthermore, at the moment of cultivating the karma of the excellent marks, both humans and gods alike come to recognize and know that he is indeed a bodhisattva, and it is for this reason that he is named a true bodhisattva; before cultivating the karma of the excellent marks, only the gods know this of him, and it is for this reason that he cannot yet be named a true bodhisattva. Furthermore, at the moment of cultivating the karma of the excellent marks, he relinquishes five inferior things and gains five superior things: first, he relinquishes the evil destinies and is thereafter constantly reborn in the wholesome destinies; second, he relinquishes lowly families and is thereafter constantly reborn into noble families; third, he relinquishes any body other than a male one and thereafter always attains a male body; fourth, he relinquishes incomplete faculties and thereafter always possesses complete faculties; and fifth, he relinquishes any forgetfulness of mindfulness and thereafter always attains a naturally-arising mindfulness of his own — and it is through this that he comes to be named a true bodhisattva. Before cultivating the karma of the excellent marks, the opposite of all this holds true, and it is for this reason that he cannot yet be named a true bodhisattva. Question: what benefit does the Bodhisattva gain from attaining this naturally-arising mindfulness? Answer: through attaining this naturally-arising mindfulness, the Bodhisattva becomes free of every fault common to ordinary sentient beings, accumulates extensive learning, deepens his faith in cause and effect, skillfully gathers his own following, and ensures that whatever instruction and admonition he gives is never in vain, so that his own provisions for awakening grow ever more complete — this is the benefit. By attaining what is he named a bodhisattva? Answer: by attaining the karma that ripens into the excellent marks. Question: why is this treatise composed once again? Answer: in order to settle a lingering doubt definitively — that is, the passage has already stated above that one is named a bodhisattva precisely at the point of being capable of performing and augmenting the karma that ripens into the excellent marks; and, lest doubt arise, thinking that, although one is indeed named a bodhisattva at this very stage, the name "bodhisattva" might instead derive from the realization of some other excellent dharma, this treatise now wishes to make plain that it is precisely by means of the karma that ripens into the excellent marks that one attains the name "bodhisattva," and by no other dharma, and it is for this reason that this treatise is composed. Question: it is on account of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening that one is named bodhi-sattva, "awakening-being" — why, then, does this name still apply before that awakening is actually realized, yet no longer apply, being replaced instead by the name "Buddha," once it has been realized? Answer: because, before this being has attained unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, he constantly, with an overwhelming resolve, conforms to awakening, proceeds toward awakening, draws near to awakening, delights in awakening, reveres awakening, and thirsts after awakening, seeking to realize it, wishing to realize it, without laxity and without rest, his mind never for a moment abandoning awakening — and it is for this reason that he is named bodhi-sattva, "awakening-being." But, once he has already attained unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, his own resolve and effort in seeking awakening both come to rest entirely, and it is only his having actually accomplished the very meaning of "awakening" itself that now stands supreme — since he has eternally severed every defiled and undefiled form of delusion, fully awakened to every object of knowledge, both ultimate and conventional, and, moreover, is now capable of awakening countless sentient beings, according to their own faculties, desires, and dispositions, bringing benefit to them all — and it is on account of this very supremacy in the meaning of "awakening" that he is named Buddha, "the Awakened One," and no longer bodhisattva. Furthermore, sattva itself carries the meaning of "a valiant one" — before attaining unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, he is constantly diligent and valiant with respect to awakening, seeking to realize it swiftly, and it is for this reason that he is named bodhi-sattva; but, once he has already attained unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, his own valiant striving toward awakening comes to rest, and it is only his supremacy in the very meaning of "awakening" that now stands, and it is for this reason that he is named Buddha, since he has become capable of accomplishing the most supreme awakening of all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing etymological distinction between bodhisattva ("a valiant one striving toward awakening") and Buddha ("one whose awakening is complete") is a fitting way to close both this fascicle and the fascicle's own overarching theme of "how much of the journey has actually been covered": even the very titles applied to a spiritual aspirant, on this account, are never honorific labels bestowed in advance, but precise descriptions of exactly how much has, and has not yet, been accomplished.
