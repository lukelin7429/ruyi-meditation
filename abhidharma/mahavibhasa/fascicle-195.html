Knowledge, Ignorance, and the Nyāsa of Perception

Fascicle 195 closes the Nyāsa of the Three States of Existence with an exhaustive causal matrix of knowledge and ignorance across every realm and moral quality, then opens the Nyāsa of Perception (想納息) with the tetralemma of giving rise to and being conjoined with the perception of impermanence. The second half turns to how mind and karma relate in sequence, a fivefold classification of comprehended, abandoned, cultivated, and realized dharmas, and a defense — against four rival schools — that birth, aging, duration, and cessation are themselves real, conditioned, dissociated formations. This page carries Ru‑Yi's complete translation.

Milestone: partway through this fascicle, the Nyāsa of the Three States of Existence (三有納息), which ran from fascicle 192 through the opening of this one, closes, and the treatise opens the Nyāsa of Perception (想納息) — the third Nyāsa of the Views Aggregate (見蘊第八).

Translation Method

The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 195 spans two Nyāsas and several distinct doctrinal topics — knowledge and ignorance as cause and condition, the opening tetralemmas of the Nyāsa of Perception, the sequencing of mind and karma, a fivefold epistemic classification of dharmas, and the reality of the four conditioned characteristics. Terms established across fascicles 93–194 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Knowledge and Ignorance as Cause and Condition: The Elevenfold Matrix

Before closing the Nyāsa of the Three States of Existence, the treatise works out why it proceeds specifically in terms of knowledge and ignorance rather than any other pairing, then lays out eleven classes of dharma bound to the three realms and specifies, for each, exactly which of the causes and conditions knowledge and ignorance respectively serve as.

Chinese Source

Some say: because these two are the root of defilement and of purity — that is, of all defilement, ignorance is the root; as it is said, whatever various unwholesome dharmas arise or grow, all take ignorance as their root, their accumulation, their kind, and their arising-condition; and of all purity, knowledge is the root — as it is said, whatever various wholesome dharmas arise or grow, none fail to take knowledge as their root, their accumulation, their kind, and their arising-condition. Some say: because these two are both foremost dharmas — that is, the World-Honored One states in the sūtras that these two are foremost dharmas, as it is said: monks, ignorance is foremost, ignorance is the forerunner, and it is by this that various unwholesome dharmas come to arise, and it is by this that one becomes one without shame or remorse; while knowledge is foremost, knowledge is the forerunner, and it is by this that various wholesome dharmas come to arise, and it is by this that one becomes one with shame and remorse. Some say: because these two are, respectively, a proximate obstruction and its antidote — that is, ignorance is the proximate obstruction to knowledge, and knowledge is the proximate antidote to ignorance. Some say: because these two are commonly known to be mutually opposed — that is, ignorance opposes knowledge, and knowledge opposes ignorance. Some say: because both, in their object, are subsumed and not subsumed under the four noble truths alike, both take contaminated and uncontaminated dharmas as their object alike, and both take conditioned and unconditioned dharmas as their object alike, in neither case one subsumed within the other. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The eleven-way classification by realm and moral quality, each cell of the matrix filled in with its own precise count of causes and conditions, is characteristic of how this treatise treats completeness itself as a form of proof: nothing is claimed about knowledge and ignorance in general that is not first cashed out cell by cell across every class of dharma it could conceivably apply to.

2. Knowledge and Ignorance in Cross-Reference: A Series of Tetralemmas

A sequence of paired questions works out every combination of causing and conditioning between knowledge and ignorance, and between each of them and the categories of wholesome and unwholesome — closing with the question of what remains when a dharma has neither as its cause yet is still not causeless, and completing the Nyāsa of the Three States of Existence.

Chinese Source

諸法因無明彼法緣無明耶。答若法因無明彼法緣無明。此中因無明諸法以種類言之。彼法以無明為五因。謂相應俱有同類遍行異熟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Are the dharmas that have ignorance as their cause the same dharmas that take ignorance as their condition? Answer: whatever dharmas have ignorance as their cause also take ignorance as their condition. Here, speaking of the dharmas that have ignorance as their cause in terms of their kind, those dharmas have ignorance as their five causes — namely, the conjoined, the co-existent, the homogeneous, the pervasive, and the ripening cause.

There are dharmas that take ignorance as their condition without having ignorance as their cause — namely, excepting the ripened retribution of ignorance, the remaining unobscured-neutral formations and wholesome formations; for these dharmas, ignorance serves as three conditions, or two conditions, or one condition, but is not their cause. Are the dharmas that have ignorance as their cause thereby unwholesome? Answer: whatever dharmas are unwholesome have ignorance as their cause. Here, speaking of the unwholesome dharmas that have ignorance as their cause in terms of their kind, those dharmas have ignorance as their four causes — namely, the conjoined, the co-existent, the homogeneous, and the pervasive cause. There are dharmas that have ignorance as their cause without being unwholesome — namely, the ripened retribution of ignorance and the obscured-neutral formations. Here, the ripened retribution of ignorance has ignorance as its one cause, the ripening cause; the obscured-neutral formations, speaking of them in terms of their kind, have ignorance as their four causes — namely, the conjoined, the co-existent, the homogeneous, and the pervasive cause — yet these dharmas are not unwholesome, since they are neutral.

There are dharmas that are wholesome without having knowledge as their cause — namely, the first knowledge and wholesome contaminated formations. Is there a dharma that has neither knowledge nor ignorance as its cause, yet is not without a cause at all? Answer: there is — namely, excepting the ripened retribution of ignorance, the remaining unobscured-neutral formations, and the first knowledge together with wholesome contaminated formations. Such dharmas have neither knowledge nor ignorance as their cause, yet are not without a cause. Among these, excepting the ripened retribution of ignorance, the remaining unobscured-neutral formations, speaking of them in terms of their kind, have four causes — namely, the conjoined, the co-existent, the homogeneous, and the ripening cause; the first knowledge has two causes — namely, the conjoined and the co-existent cause; wholesome contaminated formations, speaking of them in terms of their kind, have three causes — namely, the conjoined, the co-existent, and the homogeneous cause. Question: the possession of the uncontaminated that co-exists with the first knowledge likewise has neither knowledge nor ignorance as its cause, yet is not without a cause — why is this not mentioned here? Answer: this passage should be understood as also including the possession of the uncontaminated that co-exists with the first knowledge; that it is not explicitly stated should be understood to have some further significance. Some say: this possession is subsumed under the co-existent cause of the first knowledge, and it is for this reason that it is not separately mentioned. This should not be so said, since possession is not the co-existent cause of the first knowledge; rather, it should be said that it is subsumed within the category of the first knowledge — when the first knowledge is spoken of, its entire cluster should be understood as already spoken of. 3. The Nyāsa of Perception Opens: Giving Rise to and Being Conjoined With the Perception of Impermanence

A new Nyāsa begins within the Views Aggregate — the third, on perception (想納息). It opens with the question of whether a dharma that gives rise to the perception of impermanence is thereby conjoined with it, worked through the same four-alternative method already familiar from the Three States of Existence, then extended to all ten perceptions.

Chinese Source

Fascicle 195 Complete

This closes fascicle 195 of Ru‑Yi's translation. The fascicle opens by finishing the Nyāsa of the Three States of Existence: an eleven-way matrix of exactly which causes and conditions knowledge and ignorance serve as across every realm and moral quality, followed by a series of tetralemmas cross-referencing knowledge, ignorance, wholesomeness, and unwholesomeness. It then opens the Nyāsa of Perception (想納息) — the third Nyāsa of the Views Aggregate — with the question of whether giving rise to the perception of impermanence entails being conjoined with it, and whether it entails sharing the same object, both worked through the treatise's signature four-alternative method. The fascicle's second half turns to how mind and the karma it produces relate in sequence across restraint, non-restraint, and neither; to a fivefold classification of every dharma as comprehended, fully known, to be abandoned, to be cultivated, or realized; and to a defense, against four rival schools, that birth, aging, duration, and cessation — the four characteristics that mark every conditioned dharma — are themselves real, conditioned, dissociated formations, closing with their moral quality and exactly which latent afflictions and fetters attach to them.
