Narrowing Eighteen Elements to Four Truths

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 7 — the same nyāsa section on the four nirvedhabhāgīya roots continues (雜蘊第一中世第一法納息第一之六). The fascicle opens with the Western masters' own seventeen-gate verse, applying motivation, cause, object, result, and a dozen further categories systematically across heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme mundane dharma; classifies seventy-three distinct varieties of these roots by degree of detachment from the desire realm and each dhyāna; debates whether a bodhisattva's own extraordinary wholesome roots are cultivated across many lifetimes or attained in a single sitting beneath the Bodhi tree; asks which realms, continents, and bodies these roots can even arise in; and closes with the treatise's fullest surviving account of the actual meditation curriculum behind them — a systematic narrowing from the eighteen elements, through the twelve sense fields and five aggregates, down to the four foundations of mindfulness and finally the four truths themselves, ending in a debate over the arising-and-perishing contemplation illustrated by a spinning top and a potter's wheel. Fascicle 8 opens a new topic within the same nyāsa section: the twenty statements of identity-view (薩迦耶見).

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 7 complete, 28 sections Source: Taishō T1545, vol. 27, fascicle 7 Topic: 順決擇分 (nirvedhabhāgīya), Miscellaneous Chapter §1

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

This fascicle continues directly from Fascicle 6, within the same nyāsa (納息) section begun in Fascicle 2 — terms established there, such as the certainty of rightness (正性離生) and the four roots' own names, recur constantly here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The Western Masters' Seventeen-Gate Verse

Fascicle 7 opens exactly where Fascicle 6 left off: with the rival regional tradition's own comprehensive framework for analyzing all four nirvedhabhāgīya roots together, condensed into a single mnemonic verse the rest of this fascicle will work through gate by gate.

Chinese Source

西方尊者以十七門分別此四如彼頌言。
意趣依因所緣果　等流異熟及勝利
行相二緣慧界定　尋等根心退為後

Ru-Yi Translation

The Western masters analyze these four through seventeen gates, as their own verse states: "Motivation, basis, cause, object, result, / outflow, ripening, and benefit; / aspect, the two conditions, wisdom, realm, absorption, / examination and the rest, faculty, mind-count, with retrogression last."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This verse is the promised payoff of the passing mention in Fascicle 6: rather than dismissing the Western masters' seventeen-gate scheme, the treatise adopts it wholesale for this fascicle's own structure, running heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme mundane dharma through all seventeen categories in turn. Several of these gates — object, aspect, realm, examination-and-analysis, faculty, mind-count, retrogression — will already be familiar from the sevenfold analysis Fascicles 2 through 5 gave supreme mundane dharma alone; here they are applied systematically to all four roots side by side for the first time.

2. Heat's Own Profile: Motivation Through Outflow

The treatise begins working through the seventeen gates for heat specifically, moving briskly through motivation, basis, cause, object, result, and outflow — each answered in a single, compact line.

Chinese Source

問煖有何意趣。答先所修集。一切善根。謂從布施。乃至七處善。皆以迴向解脫。是其意趣。問煖依何而起。答依自地定。問煖以何為因。答前生自地同類善根。問煖誰為所緣。答四聖諦。問煖以何為果。答以頂為近士用果。問煖誰為等流。答後生自地同類善根。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: what is heat's own motivation? The answer: every wholesome root previously cultivated and accumulated — from giving, up through skill in the seven bases — all dedicated toward liberation: this is its motivation. One asks: on what basis does heat arise? The answer: on the basis of concentration belonging to its own stage. One asks: what serves as heat's cause? The answer: a same-kind wholesome root of its own stage arising earlier. One asks: what does heat take as its object? The answer: the four noble truths. One asks: what serves as heat's result? The answer: summit, as its proximate result of human effort. One asks: what serves as heat's outflow? The answer: a same-kind wholesome root of its own stage arising afterward.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Motivation" (意趣) here names something distinct from cause in the technical Abhidharma sense — it is the entire prior accumulation of wholesome activity, from giving through the seven bases of skill, that a practitioner brings to the threshold of heat, already oriented toward liberation before the specifically meditative work even begins. It is a reminder that "heat," for all its technical precision, is never presented as arising from nowhere — it is the flowering of an entire life's prior wholesome conduct.

3. Heat's Ripening, and Whether These Roots Draw Forth Rebirth

The treatise reaches heat's ripening — its karmic fruit in a future life — and pauses over a genuine puzzle: how can a wholesome root that turns away from existence itself also cause a rebirth? Two positions are given before the gate-by-gate survey resumes.

Chinese Source

問煖誰為異熟。答色界五蘊。問順決擇分。亦能牽引眾同分不。有說不能。所以者何。厭背有故。謂此善根。厭背諸有於眾同分。但能圓滿不能牽引。有說亦能。謂此善根。雖厭背有。而能牽引。隨順聖道。眾同分果。謂此所招眾同分果。增上熾盛。微妙殊勝。無有災橫順勝善品。問煖有何勝利。答能與涅槃作決定因。有說得煖定不斷善。問煖有幾行相。答十六行相。問煖為緣名為緣義。答名義俱緣。問煖為聞所成。為思所成。為修所成。答唯修所成。問煖為欲界繫。為色界繫。為無色界繫。答唯色界繫。問煖為在定。為不在定。答唯在定。問煖為有尋有伺。為無尋唯伺。為無尋無伺。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: what serves as heat's ripening? The answer: the five aggregates of the form realm. One asks: can the nirvedhabhāgīya factors also draw forth a class of rebirth? Some say: no — because this wholesome root turns away in disgust from existence itself, and so, with respect to a class of rebirth, it is only able to bring it to completion, not to draw it forth in the first place. Some say: yes — because this wholesome root, although it turns away in disgust from existence, is still able to draw forth a class of rebirth that accords with the noble path — that is, the class of rebirth it thereby brings about is heightened, vigorous, subtle, and excellent, free of calamity, and in accord with every superior wholesome quality. One asks: what benefit does heat confer? The answer: it serves as a determinative cause for eventually reaching nirvāṇa. Some say: one who has attained heat certainly cannot cut off their own wholesome roots. One asks: how many aspects does heat have? The answer: sixteen aspects. One asks: does heat take a name as its object, or a meaning? The answer: it takes both name and meaning as its object together. One asks: is heat born of learning, of reflection, or of meditative cultivation? The answer: born of meditative cultivation alone. One asks: does heat belong to the desire realm, the form realm, or the formless realm? The answer: the form realm alone. One asks: does heat occur within concentration, or outside it? The answer: within concentration alone. One asks: does heat have both examination and analysis, analysis alone, or neither?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The tension the treatise flags — disgust with existence somehow still drawing forth a further existence — resolves, on the second position, into a genuinely interesting distinction: what heat turns away from is ordinary, unguided rebirth, while what it can still shape is a rebirth already bent toward the noble path itself. This is not a contradiction of its own disgust so much as disgust doing constructive work, steering the practitioner's remaining momentum toward a more favorable launching point rather than eliminating momentum altogether.

4. Heat's Remaining Gates, and the Profiles of the Other Three Roots

The seventeen-gate survey concludes for heat — faculty, mind-count, and retrogression — before the treatise moves through summit's, forbearance's, and supreme mundane dharma's own motivation-through-result gates in rapid succession, each one inheriting most of its profile from the root just below it.

Chinese Source

答具三種。問煖為樂根相應。為喜根相應。為捨根相應。答三根相應。問煖為一心。為多心。答多心。問煖為可退。為不可退。答可退。頂意趣者。謂從布施。乃至煖果者。以忍為近士用果。勝利者。不斷善根。有說亦不作無間業。餘如煖說。忍意趣者。謂從布施。乃至頂果者。以世第一法。為近士用果。勝利者不退。不作無間業。不墮惡趣。有說亦不執我。餘如頂說。世第一法意趣者。謂從布施。乃至忍所緣者。唯苦諦果者。以苦法智忍。為近士用果。

Ru-Yi Translation

The answer: it has all three. One asks: is heat associated with the faculty of pleasure, joy, or equanimity? The answer: with all three faculties. One asks: is heat a single moment of mind, or many? The answer: many moments. One asks: can heat retrogress, or not? The answer: it can retrogress. As for summit's own motivation: it is the same as heat's, from giving onward. Its result is forbearance, as its proximate result of human effort. Its benefit: it confers the ability never to cut off one's own wholesome roots; some say it also confers never committing the acts of immediate retribution. The rest is as explained for heat. As for forbearance's own motivation: it is the same as summit's, from giving onward. Its result is supreme mundane dharma, as its proximate result of human effort. Its benefit: it confers never retrogressing, never committing the acts of immediate retribution, and never falling into the evil destinies; some say it also confers never clinging to a self. The rest is as explained for summit. As for supreme mundane dharma's own motivation: it is the same as forbearance's, from giving onward. Its object is the truth of suffering alone. Its result is the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering, as its proximate result of human effort.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The benefits stated here for summit and forbearance intentionally recapitulate, in far fewer words, the entire Devadatta debate that closed Fascicle 6 — each root's benefit is now simply asserted as settled fact, the extended argument over how to reconcile it with a famous counterexample left behind. It is a good illustration of how this treatise moves: a hard-won conclusion from one passage becomes a flat premise the very next time the same topic comes up.

5. What Each Root Cultivates for Its Own Future

Supreme mundane dharma's own seventeen-gate profile completes, and the treatise turns to a new, finer-grained question: exactly how much of itself does each root, at its very first moment, cultivate for the future — and does this differ once a practitioner has advanced beyond that first moment?

Chinese Source

勝利者。為等無間入正性離生。行相者。苦諦四行相。一心多心者。當言一心。餘如忍說。初煖緣三諦。法念住現在修未來。修四念住。一行相現在修未來。修四行相俱同類修。非不同類緣滅諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Its benefit: serving as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the certainty of rightness. Its aspect: the four aspects of the truth of suffering. As to being a single moment of mind or many: it should be said to be a single moment. The rest is as explained for forbearance. As for the very first moment of heat: taking three truths as its object, the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas, presently occurring, cultivates all four foundations of mindfulness in the future; a single aspect, presently occurring, cultivates all four aspects in the future — both cultivated as belonging to the same kind, not a different kind. Taking the truth of cessation as its object,

Because the very first contemplation of the aggregates' cessation cannot yet cultivate the path taking the aggregates as its object. As for advancing heat, taking three truths as its object: any one of the four foundations of mindfulness, presently occurring, cultivates all four foundations of mindfulness in the future, cultivated as belonging both to the same kind and to a different kind; a single aspect, presently occurring, cultivates all sixteen aspects in the future. Taking the truth of cessation as its object: the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas, presently occurring, cultivates all four foundations of mindfulness in the future; a single aspect, presently occurring, cultivates all sixteen aspects in the future. One asks: why does the very first moment of heat cultivate only what belongs to the same kind, not a different kind, while advancing heat is able to cultivate both the same kind and a different kind? The answer: the very first moment of heat has not yet attained its own lineage,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A practitioner's very first taste of heat, this passage argues, is structurally more cautious than everything that follows it: unfamiliar with the terrain, it can only cultivate future moments closely resembling itself, whereas advancing heat, already comfortable with the practice, can reach across to cultivate dissimilar future states as well. It is a small but telling detail about how this tradition understood skill acquisition — even a wholesome root improves its own reach as the practitioner grows more habituated to it.

6. Same Kind, Different Kind: A Technical Puzzle Resolved

The reason for the first-moment restriction is given directly — newness to the lineage itself — before the same pattern is traced through summit and forbearance, and a further question arises about the Venerable Ghoṣaka's own proposed parallel between heat and forbearance.

Chinese Source

初學觀諦故。唯修同類。增長煖已曾得種性。串習觀諦。故同類修亦不同類。初頂緣四諦。法念住現在修未來。修四念住。一行相現在修未來。修十六行相。增長頂。緣三諦四念住隨一。現在修未來。修四念住。一行相現在修未來。修十六行相緣滅諦。法念住現在修未來。修四念住。一行相現在修未來。修十六行相。初及增長忍緣四諦。法念住現在修未來。修四念住。一行相現在修未來。修十六行相。問何故忍初及增長皆唯法念住現在修。煖頂不爾。答忍近見道。與見道相似。如見道中。唯起法念住。忍亦如是。尊者妙音作如是說。初忍及增長忍。如初煖及增長煖說。於色界善根。未曾得種性。及已曾得種性故。彼不應作是說。此四善根。皆是色界修所成故。忍近見道。如見道起法念住故。問增長忍一切時。修十六行相耶。答不爾或時十六。或時十二或時八或時四。所以者何。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the very first moment of summit, taking all four truths as its object: the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas, presently occurring, cultivates all four foundations of mindfulness in the future; a single aspect, presently occurring, cultivates all sixteen aspects in the future. As for advancing summit, taking three truths as its object: any one of the four foundations of mindfulness, presently occurring, cultivates all four foundations of mindfulness in the future; a single aspect, presently occurring, cultivates all sixteen aspects in the future. Taking the truth of cessation as its object: the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas, presently occurring, cultivates all four foundations of mindfulness in the future; a single aspect, presently occurring, cultivates all sixteen aspects in the future. As for both the very first moment and the advancing stage of forbearance, taking all four truths as its object: the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas, presently occurring, cultivates all four foundations of mindfulness in the future; a single aspect, presently occurring, cultivates all sixteen aspects in the future. One asks: why do both the very first moment and the advancing stage of forbearance always presently occur only as the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas, while heat and summit are not like this? The answer: forbearance stands close to the path of seeing, and resembles it — just as, within the path of seeing, only the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas arises, forbearance is exactly the same. The Venerable Ghoṣaka speaks as follows: the very first moment and the advancing stage of forbearance are, respectively, like the very first moment and the advancing stage of heat, since with respect to the form realm's own wholesome roots there is a stage of not-yet-attained lineage and a stage of already-attained lineage. This position should not be maintained, however, since all four of these wholesome roots are, alike, born of cultivation within the form realm — forbearance stands close to the path of seeing precisely because, like the path of seeing, it gives rise to the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas. One asks: does advancing forbearance always cultivate all sixteen aspects? The answer: no — sometimes sixteen, sometimes twelve, sometimes eight, sometimes four. Why is this?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ghoṣaka's rejected parallel is worth noting for the same reason his rejected desire-realm/form-realm division was worth noting back in Fascicle 6: both attempts try to explain forbearance's own peculiar restriction (only the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas) by analogy to heat's lineage-newness, and both get refuted on the same ground — all four roots equally belong to the form realm and are equally born of cultivation, so any explanation appealing to a difference in realm or origin cannot be the real reason. The treatise's own preferred explanation instead appeals to forbearance's structural proximity to the path of seeing, the same "resemblance" argument used throughout this whole four-fascicle discussion.

7. The Narrowing Aspects, and Supreme Mundane Dharma's Single Robe

The variable aspect-count for advancing forbearance is explained through the same narrowing logic already traced in full in Fascicle 5, before the treatise reaches supreme mundane dharma's own case and gives it a memorable, minimal image: a person who owns only one robe has nothing left to lose after it is taken.

Chinese Source

如如漸次略所緣諦。如是如是略修行相。由此漸能近於見道。如見道故。世第一法。法念住現在修未來。修四念住。一行相現在修未來。修四行相。唯同類修。非不同類。問世第一法。已曾得種性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because, just as its object gradually narrows truth by truth, so too does its cultivated aspect narrow accordingly — and it is by this that it draws gradually nearer to the path of seeing, resembling it. As for supreme mundane dharma: the foundation of mindfulness on dharmas, presently occurring, cultivates all four foundations of mindfulness in the future; a single aspect, presently occurring, cultivates all four aspects in the future — cultivated as belonging to the same kind alone, not a different kind. One asks: supreme mundane dharma has already attained its own lineage,

串習觀諦。何故但修同類。非異類耶。答世第一法。唯有爾所行相可修。無餘行相。如人唯有一衣。奪已更無可奪。此亦如是故不應問。復次世第一法。隣逼見道。似見道故。復次世第一法。開見道門。導生見道。如見道故。

long habituated to contemplating the truths — why, then, does it cultivate only what belongs to the same kind, not a different kind? The answer: supreme mundane dharma has only this one aspect capable of being cultivated, and no other — like a person who possesses only a single robe: once it is taken, there is nothing further left to take. The case here is exactly the same, and this should not even be questioned. Further: supreme mundane dharma stands immediately adjacent to the path of seeing, resembling it. Further: supreme mundane dharma opens the gate of the path of seeing, guiding and giving rise to it, resembling it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The single-robe image captures something distinctive about supreme mundane dharma that this fascicle's whole systematic gate-analysis has been building toward: where every other root has room for variation — same kind or different, four aspects or sixteen — supreme mundane dharma has already been narrowed, by the process Fascicle 5 traced in detail, down to exactly one thing. Asking why it cultivates "only" one kind is, on this reading, a category error, since one is all there ever was to begin with.

8. Continuous or Interrupted? And the Attention That Draws Forth Heat

A new question opens: do the four roots' very first moments contemplate the four truths in one continuous sweep, or with pauses in between? Two positions are given before the treatise names the specific quality of attention — disgust, longing, revulsion, yearning — that triggers heat's own arising.

Chinese Source

問初煖頂忍於四聖諦。為相續觀。為不相續。有說相續。如見道中十五心頃。於四聖諦相續現觀。此亦如是。有說不相續。謂觀欲界苦聖諦已。即便止住。次起加行。觀色無色界苦聖諦已。復便止住。餘諦亦爾。如是說者。此不決定。或相續觀或不相續。隨彼加行。勢力轉故。問何等作意無間。引起煖耶。答色界定。修所成行相攝。有厭離有渴仰。有惡賤有思慕。作意無間引起煖。煖無間引起頂。頂無間引起忍。忍無間引起世第一法。問已離欲染者可爾。未離欲染者云何。答欲界亦有似彼作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: do the very first moments of heat, summit, and forbearance contemplate the four noble truths continuously, or not continuously? Some say: continuously — just as, within the path of seeing, across its fifteen moments of mind, the four noble truths are directly and continuously realized, the case here is exactly the same. Some say: not continuously — that is, having contemplated the truth of the desire realm's suffering, one then pauses; next one applies preparatory effort and, having contemplated the truth of the form and formless realms' suffering, pauses again; and the same for the remaining truths. On this view, the matter is not fixed either way — it may be continuous or not continuous, depending on the force of the practitioner's own preparatory effort. One asks: what attention, occurring without interval, draws forth heat? The answer: an attention belonging to the aspect born of cultivation within a concentration of the form realm, involving disgust, longing, revulsion, and yearning — this attention, without interval, draws forth heat; heat, without interval, draws forth summit; summit, without interval, draws forth forbearance; and forbearance, without interval, draws forth supreme mundane dharma. One asks: this is understandable for one already free of the desire realm's defilement — but what of one not yet free of it? The answer: the desire realm, too, has an attention resembling this one,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fifteen-moment path of seeing, cited here as the model for uninterrupted contemplation, is a well-known Sarvāstivāda structure — the sixteenth moment, the subsequent-knowledge of the path, technically belongs to the path of cultivation rather than seeing, and this treatise's own earlier fascicles have referenced this exact count in passing. Note the four-part emotional profile of the attention that draws forth heat — disgust, longing, revulsion, yearning — a genuinely psychological description, naming what the practitioner actually feels rather than simply what they cognize, in a treatise otherwise dominated by purely analytical vocabulary.

9. Dying Before Summit: Does a Practitioner Start Over?

A genuinely poignant practical question arises: what happens to a practitioner who has fully cultivated heat, is on the verge of summit, but dies before reaching it? The treatise's answer distinguishes sharply between finding a good teacher in the next life and not — with real consequences either way.

Chinese Source

思所成行相攝。有厭離有渴仰。有惡賤有思慕。未離欲染者。此作意無間引起煖。餘如前說。問修煖滿已將欲起頂。遂便命終。彼餘生中。為即起頂。為從本起。答若遇明師。隨彼應起。分齊說者。

16. A Single Sitting, and the Pratyekabuddha's Own Pattern

The treatise settles on the third, most economical solution, then extends the same one-sitting analysis to the solitary pratyekabuddha, closing this long comparative excursus on how differently each kind of noble being's path unfolds.

17. Six Lineages, and Which Transformations Are Possible

The treatise catalogs six distinct lineage-classifications each of the four roots admits of — from prone-to-retrogression up through immovable — and works through which transformations between them, and between the three vehicles' own lineages, are actually possible, closing supreme mundane dharma's own case with the same single-moment logic used throughout this whole discussion.

Chinese Source

The verdict: this position should not be maintained. Why is this? Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rule that forbearance can never be transformed into the buddha lineage is grounded in a genuinely striking piece of doctrine: a bodhisattva deliberately vows to be reborn in the evil destinies, out of compassion, in order to help beings trapped there — and forbearance, having already permanently foreclosed rebirth in those very destinies, would make such a vow structurally impossible to fulfill. Spiritual advancement, on this reading, is not unconditionally good in every direction; a certain kind of "safety" is precisely what a bodhisattva's path requires giving up.

18. Where Can These Roots Even Arise?

The treatise maps the precise geography of where heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme mundane dharma can arise at all — ruled out in the form and formless realms, in the three evil destinies, and even in the northern continent of Uttarakuru — and explains why the desire-realm gods can only ever continue a practice already begun among humans, never begin it themselves.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two-factor requirement here — a superior supporting body and a superior disgust-oriented attention, neither sufficient alone — is a precise piece of moral psychology: the gods have every physical comfort but nothing to be disgusted with, while beings in the evil destinies have every reason for disgust but a body too degraded to sustain the practice built on it. Only the human condition, uncomfortable enough to motivate renunciation yet stable enough to support real cultivation, uniquely combines both — the same reasoning, differently framed, that underlies the traditional claim that the human realm is the most auspicious for attaining awakening precisely because it is neither too easy nor too hard.

19. Why the Form and Formless Realms Cannot Support the Path

Four separate reasons, each a variant of the same underlying pattern, explain why the form and formless realms — despite their own genuine meditative accomplishments — can never support entry into the certainty of rightness.

21. Does an Ordinary Being Lose Forbearance at Death?

A genuine puzzle closes this section of the fascicle: forbearance guarantees never falling into the evil destinies, yet an ordinary being who dies must relinquish their entire class of rebirth — does forbearance itself survive that transition, or is it lost along with everything else? Three positions are recorded before the treatise settles the question.

Chinese Source

The treatise's resolution is worth tracking carefully, since it threads a real needle: forbearance's guarantee against the evil destinies remains intact — an ordinary being who has attained it genuinely never falls into one — even though the specific attainment itself, being a form-realm dharma tied to a particular stage, cannot survive an ordinary being's own rebirth unchanged the way a noble one's attainment does. The guarantee and the attainment, in other words, are not quite the same claim: the guarantee outlives the specific mental event that established it, in a way this treatise defers explaining fully until its later Karma Chapter — a rare, explicit forward-reference acknowledging the limits of what a single passage can settle on its own.

22. The Actual Curriculum: From Eighteen Elements to Twelve Sense Fields

The fascicle turns, for the first time in this entire six-fascicle discussion, to a genuinely practical question: what does the preparatory effort behind heat actually consist of, step by step? The treatise's answer is the fullest surviving account of the classical curriculum — beginning with the wisdom born of learning, and the practitioner's own weary decision to condense the entire canon down to three basic classifications, then narrow even the widest of the three.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a genuinely rare passage in the fascicles translated so far — not a debate between named masters, but a plain description of an actual meditative method, complete with the practitioner's own inner monologue of accumulating fatigue driving each successive narrowing. The three-part analytical lens applied at every stage — name, specific characteristic, general characteristic — is worth carrying forward: it recurs identically at each level of the coming narrowing, from the eighteen elements all the way down to the four truths themselves, giving the whole curriculum a single repeated shape even as its object keeps shrinking.

23. From Five Aggregates to Four Foundations of Mindfulness

The verdict: this position is unreasonable — how could a single mind possibly hold two distinct kinds of understanding at once, since direct perception and inferential comparison are themselves two entirely different modes of knowing? The first position given is therefore the sound one.

問此生滅觀。為勝解作意。為真實作意。有說。真實作意。問若爾諸行實無來去。見有來去云何真實。答此觀未成見有來去。成時但見生滅。不見有去來相。如舞獨樂。緩見來去急則不見。旋火輪喻。陶家輪喻。應知亦爾。有說。勝解作意。問若爾伽他所說。當云何通。如說。

One asks: is this arising-and-perishing contemplation an attention of resolute conviction, or an attention of genuine reality? Some say: an attention of genuine reality. One asks: if that is so, then conditioned formations, in reality, neither come nor go — how, then, can seeing them as coming and going be called "genuine"? The answer: before this contemplation is fully accomplished, one does indeed see them as coming and going; once it is accomplished, one sees only arising and perishing, no longer seeing any mark of coming or going at all — just as with a spinning top: watched slowly, one sees it coming and going, but watched swiftly, one no longer does; the same should be understood for the whirling firebrand and the potter's wheel. Others say: it is an attention of resolute conviction. One asks: if that is so, how is the verse's own statement to be reconciled with this? As it states:

若有知見能盡漏　若無知見云何盡
若能觀蘊生滅者　是則解脫煩惱意
非勝解作意能斷煩惱。答依傳因說。如子孫法。謂勝解作意。引生真實作意。由真實作意。斷諸煩惱。故不相違。

"If one has knowledge and vision, one is able to exhaust the contaminants; / without knowledge and vision, how could they ever be exhausted? / Whoever is able to contemplate the aggregates' own arising and perishing — / this, then, is the mind that is liberated from the afflictions." This shows that it cannot be a mere attention of resolute conviction that cuts off the afflictions. The answer: this is stated with reference to a transmitted, indirect cause — like the relationship between grandparent and grandchild. That is, the attention of resolute conviction draws forth and gives rise to the attention of genuine reality, and it is by the attention of genuine reality that the afflictions are actually cut off — and so there is no real contradiction.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The grandparent-grandchild analogy closes the fascicle on a precise causal distinction: resolute conviction does not itself cut off any affliction, but it reliably gives rise to the genuine-reality attention that does — an indirect, transmitted cause rather than a direct one, the same relationship a grandparent bears to a grandchild they never directly produced. The three spinning images — a child's top, a firebrand swung in a circle, a potter's wheel — all illustrate the same perceptual transition from apparent motion to a truer, more granular seeing: slow enough, and you see a top travel from here to there; fast enough, and the coming-and-going dissolves into something closer to what is actually happening, an unbroken succession of momentary states. The contemplation's own accomplishment is described the same way — what begins as an experience of things "coming and going" refines, with practice, into a direct perception of nothing but arising and perishing, moment after moment, with no persisting thing that ever really moves from one place to another at all. It is a rare case of this treatise reaching for the phenomenology of altered perception itself, rather than staying at the level of doctrinal classification.

Fascicle 7 has carried the treatise's most technically comprehensive and, in places, most vividly concrete material on the four nirvedhabhāgīya roots: the Western masters' seventeen-gate analysis applied systematically to heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme mundane dharma; seventy-three varieties by degree of detachment; the practical question of what happens to a practitioner who dies one step short of summit; the bodhisattva's own extraordinary single-sitting attainment and the puzzle of his ninety-one aeons without falling; the precise geography of where these roots can and cannot arise, down to which bodies can support them; the treatise's fullest surviving account of the actual meditation curriculum, narrowing from the eighteen elements through the twelve sense fields, five aggregates, and four foundations of mindfulness down to the four truths themselves, seen "as if through a fine silk veil"; the three broad kinds of wholesome root and the beggar whose single gift outperforms a king's feast; and the arising-and-perishing contemplation, resolved through the images of a spinning top, a whirling firebrand, and a potter's wheel.
