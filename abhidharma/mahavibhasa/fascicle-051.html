Fire Banked Beneath Ash

Fascicle 51 turns from cataloging afflictions to classifying them — wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate; possessing a ripened fruit that may lie dormant, like fire banked beneath ash, for a hundred koṭis of eons; and abandoned by the sudden, decisive stroke of seeing or only by cultivation's own slow, repeated wearing-away. A systematic "forerunner" method runs across every taxonomy given so far, illustrated through an inchworm that retreats once it reaches a summit with nothing higher left to grasp, and the Buddha's own answer to Anāthapiṇḍika comparing direct realization to a four-runged ladder climbed one rung at a time. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Five Ways to Define Wholesome, Unwholesome, and Indeterminate

Fascicle 51 opens with the treatise's own layered account of what makes a dharma wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate — held together by skillful means versus not; bringing a pleasant or painful fruit; drawing forth a sprout toward liberation or toward further existence; sinking into continued wandering or rising toward its cessation — before the Venerable Megha's own four-part technical framework (by own-nature, by association, by co-arising, by ultimate meaning) and the Venerable Pārśva's alternative grounding the same three categories in rational versus irrational attention.

‘Held together by skillful means’ displays the truth of the path; ‘capable of bringing forth a desirable fruit’ displays a portion of the truths of suffering and its arising, namely, contaminated wholesomeness; ‘settled and secure by its own nature’ displays the truth of cessation. Whatever dharma is not held together by skillful means, is capable of bringing forth an undesirable fruit, and is unsettled and insecure by its own nature, is named unwholesome — this broadly displays a portion of the truths of suffering and its arising, namely, every evil dharma. Whatever dharma stands in opposition to both of these two is named indeterminate. Further, whatever dharma is capable of bringing forth a desirable fruit, a fruit of pleasant feeling, is named wholesome; whatever dharma is capable of bringing forth an undesirable fruit, a fruit of painful feeling, is named unwholesome; whatever dharma stands in opposition to both of these two is named indeterminate. Further, whatever dharma is capable of drawing forth the sprout of desirable existence, and the sprout of liberation, is named wholesome; whatever dharma is capable of drawing forth the sprout of undesirable existence is named unwholesome; whatever dharma stands in opposition to both of these two is named indeterminate. Further, whatever dharma is capable of causing rebirth in a good destiny is named wholesome; whatever dharma is capable of causing rebirth in an evil destiny is named unwholesome; whatever dharma stands in opposition to both of these two is named indeterminate. Further, whatever dharma falls within the class of return and cessation, its own nature light and rising, is named wholesome; whatever dharma falls within the class of continued wandering, its own nature sinking and heavy, is named unwholesome; whatever dharma stands in opposition to both of these two is named indeterminate. One asks: the World-Honored One definitively declares that suffering is truly suffering, that its arising is truly its arising, that its cessation is truly its cessation, and that the path is truly the path; and every dharma whatsoever, namely, the twelve sense-fields, has been displayed, spoken, established, and disclosed by the World-Honored One himself — how, then, can any dharma be established as indeterminate at all? The answer: it is not that a dharma is named indeterminate simply for not being spoken of; rather, the Buddha declares wholesome dharmas to be wholesome, and unwholesome dharmas to be unwholesome, and whatever dharma cannot be declared either wholesome or unwholesome is spoken of as indeterminate. 2. Refuting Three Wrong Views About Ripened Fruit

A new inquiry opens: of every taxonomy given so far, how many members possess a ripened fruit, and how many possess none? The treatise clears the ground first, refuting three rival positions — the Dārṣṭāntikas, who hold that only volition produces a ripening cause and only feeling receives a ripened fruit; the Kāśyapīyas, who hold that a cause's own substance vanishes the instant its fruit ripens, exactly as an external seed ceases to exist once its sprout has grown; and various outsiders who deny that karma ripens at all.

Ru-Yi Translation

Of the three fetters, up through the ninety-eight latent tendencies, how many possess a ripened fruit, and how many possess none? One asks: for what reason is this discourse composed? The answer: it is in order to check other schools' own tenets and to make manifest the correct meaning — that is, there are those who hold that, apart from volition, there is no ripening cause, and, apart from feeling, there is no ripened fruit, as the Dārṣṭāntikas maintain; it is in order to check this very intent that this passage makes manifest that the ripening cause and the ripened fruit alike span all five aggregates. There are further those who hold that, once a ripening cause and its ripened fruit have ripened, the cause's own substance thereupon ceases to exist, as the Kāśyapīyas maintain, who state as follows: the ripening cause and its ripened fruit, prior to ripening, still retain their own substance; once the fruit has ripened, that substance thereupon ceases to exist, exactly as an external seed, prior to the sprout's arising, still retains its own substance, while, once the sprout has arisen, that substance thereupon ceases to exist; it is in order to check this very intent that this passage makes manifest that the ripening cause and its ripened fruit, even once ripened, still retain their own substance. The Kāśyapīya position and its refutation stage a genuinely interesting metaphysical disagreement about persistence: does a cause need to survive its own effect in order to have genuinely caused it, or is causation compatible with the cause quietly vanishing the moment it succeeds? The seed-and-sprout image the Kāśyapīyas themselves reach for is double-edged — it makes their vanishing-cause picture intuitive for plants, but the treatise's own insistence that karmic causes persist through ripening suggests karma was never meant to work on quite the same model as botany.

3. Ash Over Fire: The Verse on Delayed Ripening

A verse captures the treatise's own picture of karmic delay: an evil deed is not repaid at once, unlike milk instantly curdling into yogurt with the sajjā herb — it is instead like fire banked beneath ash, which a careless fool treads on without noticing, only to be burned later. A long technical resolution follows, distinguishing several different senses of "co-existing" (mere co-presence across vast gulfs of time or space, simultaneous co-presence within one moment, near versus far co-presence) in order to explain precisely how a karmic cause can be said to "possess" a ripened fruit that may not arise for a hundred koṭis of eons.

4. Refuting the View That Ordinary Beings Cannot Cut Off Affliction

A new major inquiry opens: how many afflictions are abandoned by seeing, and how many by cultivation? The treatise first confronts the Dārṣṭāntikas and the Great Virtuous One, who hold that an ordinary being — lacking noble wisdom — can only ever suppress an entanglement, never truly cut off a latent tendency, citing scripture's own claim that "true cutting-off" belongs to noble seeing alone. The rebuttal reinterprets two contested scriptural passages (a verse on death permanently ending self-clinging, and children who tend and then abandon a mud house once their attachment fades) as speaking loosely of "cutting off" what is, strictly, only ever suppressed or released.

5. The Inchworm, the Jackal, and Four More Wrong Views

Two vivid images close the case for why ordinary beings can only suppress, never truly cut off, affliction through the mundane path alone: an inchworm climbing a plant, gripping what is above and releasing what is below, which simply stops and retreats once it reaches the very top with nothing higher left to grasp — exactly as an ordinary being can ascend by degrees all the way to the peak of existence but no further — and a jackal trampling hemp or reeds, damaging only the stalk while leaving the root intact. Three further wrong views are dispatched in quick succession: that no noble one ever uses the mundane path, that every affliction is cut off in one instant by the diamond-like concentration alone, and that realization of the four truths is sudden rather than gradual — the last refuted through the Buddha's own reply to Anāthapiṇḍika, comparing direct realization to climbing a four-runged ladder one rung at a time.

6. The Forerunner Method Opens: The View of a Perishable Self

12. Why “Seeing” and “Cultivation” Have Distinct Names At All

A genuine puzzle closes the fascicle: since every act of seeing involves some cultivation, and every act of cultivation involves some seeing, on what basis can these two categories of abandonment be distinguished at all? The treatise's own resolution rests on relative proportion rather than exclusive presence — the path of seeing is mostly wisdom with only a trace of non-negligence, the path of cultivation mostly non-negligence with only a trace of wisdom — before the Venerable Vasumitra reframes the whole question around sheer speed: seeing is a keen, forceful path capable of cutting nine grades of affliction in a single momentary stroke, while cultivation is a duller blade that must saw through the very same nine grades slowly, through long repetition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Vasumitra's speed-based reframing quietly resolves the overlap puzzle by changing what the classification is actually tracking: it was never really asking whether wisdom or non-negligence is present, since both always are, but asking how abruptly a given affliction yields — instantaneously in a single decisive stroke, or only through cumulative, repeated wearing-away — which turns two seemingly incompatible definitions (predominance versus proportion) into two compatible descriptions of the same underlying phenomenon.

13. Twenty More Ways to Draw the Same Line
