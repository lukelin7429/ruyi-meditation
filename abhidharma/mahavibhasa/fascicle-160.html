How Many Factors Does a Dhyāna Have?

Fascicle 160 completes the great multi-fascicle census of non-material dharmas against their co-arising mind — closing out the formless realm, trainee/non-trainee, and abandonment-category axes — then opens an entirely new topic: how many factors each of the four dhyānas has, and why a defiled dhyāna's own factors go quietly missing. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 160 complete, 6 sections Source: Taishō T1545, vol. 27, fascicle 160 Topic: 得納息第一之四 — the great census concludes, dhyāna-factors open

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 160 continues the Attainment Nyāsa (得納息第一之四) within the Concentration Aggregate (定蘊第七). Terms established across fascicles 93–159 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The Formless-Realm Census Concludes

Fascicle 160 opens by closing out the realm-based census begun in fascicle 159: a formless-realm non-material dharma's co-arising with desire-realm, form-realm, and unfettered minds, tracked through the same three mechanisms of direct association, regression/rebirth, and continuous growth — before a final editorial aside notes a scenario the base text passes over in silence, resolved by distinguishing an unconditioned dharma's own unfettered nature from the realm its attainment happens to be fettered to.

Question: an unfettered dharma's attainment can also arise at the very moment a formless-realm mind arises — as when one dies in a higher ground of the formless realm and is reborn in a lower ground, at which point that mind co-arises with the attainment of the analytical cessation of the defilements abandoned by seeing and by cultivation belonging to that next-lower ground into which one is born — why is this not mentioned here? Some hold: it ought to have been mentioned, and its omission should be understood as an incompleteness in the text. Others hold: although the analytical cessation obtained at that moment is itself unfettered, its own attainment is fettered to the formless realm, not unfettered — and it is for this reason that it is not mentioned here.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is now the third time in two fascicles that the treatise has flagged its own silence on a symmetric case, and the third time the resolution has landed on the very same distinction — an unconditioned dharma is unfettered, but the attainment that possesses it need not be. A pattern this consistent stops looking like coincidence and starts looking like a genuinely settled principle the base text simply assumed its readers would supply for themselves.

2. A New Axis: Trainee, Non-Trainee, and Neither

The census now turns to path-stage, asking whether a trainee's non-material dharma is co-arisen with a trainee's mind — tracking the familiar regression scenarios (an arhat or trainee losing ground and taking on a lesser fruit's attainment) and the familiar growth scenarios (refining one's faculties, drawing forth the superknowledges, cultivating the immeasurables and the paired liberative contemplations) that structured every earlier pass through this same combinatorial method.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That regressing from a fruit is, once again, the very occasion that produces a lower-stage attainment underscores how thoroughly this treatise treats loss and gain as two descriptions of a single event: there is no regression that does not simultaneously constitute the arising of some other attainment, and no arising of an attainment here that isn't paired with the relinquishing of another.

Question: inner clarity is faith, and faith is common to both defiled and undefiled states alike — why, then, does this passage not speak of it as a factor within the defiled dhyāna? Some hold: this passage should be understood as saying that happiness is lacking, since the happiness of mental and bodily pliancy is absent within a defiled state — according to this account, the undefiled second dhyāna possesses the full four factors, while the defiled second dhyāna has three. Others hold: a defiled faith is properly named "non-faith" — though present within a defiled state, it lacks the very characteristic of a factor, and so is not established as one — according to this account, the undefiled second dhyāna possesses the full four factors, while the defiled second dhyāna has only two. "Do all third dhyānas have five factors?" Answer: the undefiled dhyāna has five; the defiled dhyāna lacks five. What is it that is lacking? Answer: it lacks correct mindfulness and correct awareness. Question: mindfulness and wisdom are both common to defiled states as well — why, then, are they not established as factors within the defiled dhyāna? Some hold: this passage should be understood as saying that equanimity is lacking, since equanimity, one of the great wholesome-ground dharmas, is absent within a defiled state — according to this account, the undefiled third dhyāna possesses the full five factors, while the defiled third dhyāna has four. Others hold: a defiled mindfulness is properly named "forgetfulness," and a defiled wisdom is properly named "incorrect awareness" — though present within a defiled state, they lack the very characteristic of a factor, and so are not established as such — according to this account, the undefiled third dhyāna possesses the full five factors, while the defiled third dhyāna has only two. "Do all fourth dhyānas have four factors?" Answer: the undefiled dhyāna has four; the defiled dhyāna lacks four. What is it that is lacking? Answer: it lacks the purity of equanimity and mindfulness. Question: mindfulness is common to defiled states as well — why, then, is it not a factor within the defiled dhyāna? Some hold: this passage should properly be understood as saying only that equanimity is lacking, since equanimity, one of the great wholesome-ground dharmas, is absent within a defiled state — according to this account, the undefiled fourth dhyāna possesses the full four factors, while the defiled fourth dhyāna has three. Others hold: a defiled mindfulness is properly named "forgetfulness," not "the purity of mindfulness" — though present within a defiled state, it lacks the very characteristic of a factor, and so is not established as such — according to this account, the undefiled fourth dhyāna possesses the full four factors, while the defiled fourth dhyāna has only two. On this point, there are those who hold: no defiled dhyāna ever has any established factor at all, and the passage's statements that only joy, happiness, and the like are lacking are simply following whichever wording is clearest — for the first dhyāna speaks of "the joy and happiness born of seclusion," using the word "born of seclusion"; the second dhyāna speaks of "inner clarity," using the word "clarity"; the third dhyāna speaks of "correct mindfulness and correct awareness," using the word "correct"; and the fourth dhyāna speaks of "the purity of equanimity and mindfulness," using the word "purity" — and it is precisely because these particular terms stand in the clearest contradiction with a defiled state that the passage singles them out to say they are lacking, even though, in reality, every single factor is absent within a defiled state. Others hold: the passage simply follows whichever factor is most eminent — for the first dhyāna's eminent factor lies in the benefit of escaping the desire realm's heavy suffering, while the upper three dhyānas' eminent factor lies in serving as the antidote to defilement through their own excellent, sublime detachment — and it is for this reason that the passage, in speaking of the defiled dhyāna, singles out whichever factor is most eminent to say is lacking; yet in truth, the remaining factors, too, are simply not established within a defiled dhyāna at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The final two positions reported — that the passage's language economically flags only the single clearest point of contradiction with a defiled state, or that it simply names whichever factor happens to be most soteriologically important for that particular dhyāna — both concede the same striking admission: every dhyāna-factor without exception is absent from a defiled dhyāna, and the sūtra's carefully worded partial denials were never meant as an exhaustive inventory, only as a rhetorically efficient hint.
