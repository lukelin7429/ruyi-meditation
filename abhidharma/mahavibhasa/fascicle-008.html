The Twenty Statements of Identity-View

Fascicle 7 closed the four-fascicle discussion of the nirvedhabhāgīya roots; fascicle 8 opens an entirely new topic within the same nyāsa section (雜蘊第一中世第一法納息第一之七): the twenty statements of identity-view (薩迦耶見二十句), the analysis Fascicle 2 had already flagged as the chapter's next stage of argument. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation — the refutation of the Dārṣṭāntikas' claim that identity-view has no real object, an extraordinary combinatorial survey running from one to nine hundred thirty-six possible ways of classifying the same view, a debate over whether it can take a single atom or all five aggregates at once as its object (with five inventive explanations for the ascetic Satyaka's puzzling words to the Buddha), the story of the Kashmiri Dharma Master Dārlapa, whose boast that even an ordinary being could avoid every wrong view is tested by an arhat across an entire rebirth, and a systematic eight-view survey of inverted views about permanence, pleasure, and purity.

1. Why This Treatise: Refuting the Dārṣṭāntikas

Fascicle 8 opens a wholly new nyāsa topic — identity-view's twenty statements — with the treatise's usual two-part justification: filling a gap Śāriputra himself left open, and refuting a rival school's claim that this view has no real object at all.

Chinese Source

此二十句。薩迦耶見。幾我見幾我所見。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為欲分別契經義故。謂諸經中。佛說有二十句薩迦耶見。尊者舍利子。於池喻經中。雖略分別此二十句薩迦耶見。而皆未說幾是我見幾我所見。彼經是此論所依根本。彼所未說者。今欲說之故作斯論。復次為止他宗顯正義故。謂譬喻者。作如是說。薩迦耶見無實所緣。彼作是言。薩迦耶見計我我所。於勝義中無我我所。如人見繩謂是蛇。見杌謂是人等。此亦如是故無所緣。為止彼執。顯示此見實有所緣。故作斯論。問於勝義中無我我所。云何此見實有所緣。答薩迦耶見。緣五取蘊計我我所。如緣繩杌謂是蛇人。行相顛倒非無所緣。以五取蘊是實有故。此二十句薩迦耶見。幾我見幾我所見耶。答五我見。謂等隨觀色是我。受想行識是我。十五我所見。謂等隨觀我有色。色是我所。我在色中。我有受想行識。受想行識是我所。我在受想行識中。問如我見行相。緣五取蘊。有五我所見行相。緣五取蘊亦應有五。何故乃說有十五耶。答我見行相。緣五取蘊無差別故。但有五種。我所見行相。緣五取蘊有差別故。有十五種。謂我眾具於一一蘊。皆有三種差別相故。然此薩迦耶見。或應說一。謂五見中薩迦耶見。或應說二。謂我我所行相差別。說為我見及我所見。或應說三。謂欲色無色三界別故。或應說五。謂緣五蘊有差別故。或應說六。謂於三界各有我見我所見故。

Ru-Yi Translation

[The topic reads:] "Of these twenty statements of identity-view, how many are self-views and how many are self-possession-views?" — and so on, as stated at length. One asks: why does the treatise take up this topic? The answer: in order to elucidate the meaning of the discourses. That is, in various discourses the Buddha spoke of the twenty statements of identity-view; the Venerable Śāriputra, in the Discourse on the Pond Simile, likewise distinguished these twenty statements of identity-view in brief, yet never stated how many are self-views and how many are self-possession-views. That discourse is the root text this treatise takes as its basis, and what it left unstated, the treatise now wishes to state — hence this topic is taken up. Further, it is taken up in order to refute a rival school and manifest the correct meaning. That is, the Dārṣṭāntikas maintain that identity-view has no real object at all. They say: identity-view conceives of a self and what belongs to self, yet in ultimate truth there is no self and nothing belonging to self — just as a person, seeing a rope, takes it to be a snake, or seeing a stump, takes it to be a person; identity-view, they say, is exactly the same, and so has no object at all. In order to refute this attachment and to demonstrate that this view does indeed have a real object, the treatise is composed. One asks: since in ultimate truth there is no self and nothing belonging to self, how can this view really have an object? The answer: identity-view takes the five aggregates of clinging as its object and conceives of them as self and as belonging to self — just as, taking a rope or a stump as its object, one conceives it to be a snake or a person: the aspect is inverted, but it is not without an object, since the five aggregates of clinging are themselves really existent. Of these twenty statements of identity-view, how many are self-views and how many are self-possession-views? The answer: five are self-views — namely, uniformly regarding form as self, and feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness as self. Fifteen are self-possession-views — namely, uniformly regarding the self as possessing form, form as belonging to the self, the self as dwelling within form, the self as possessing feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness as belonging to the self, and the self as dwelling within feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness. One asks: just as the aspect of self-view, taking the five aggregates of clinging as its object, yields five varieties, the aspect of self-possession-view, likewise taking the five aggregates of clinging as its object, ought equally to yield five — why, then, does the treatise speak of fifteen? The answer: the aspect of self-view, taking the five aggregates of clinging as its object, admits of no further distinction, and so yields only five varieties; the aspect of self-possession-view, taking the five aggregates of clinging as its object, does admit of further distinction, and so yields fifteen varieties — that is, with respect to what belongs to the self, each single aggregate admits of three distinct aspects. Yet this identity-view could equally be said to be one — namely, the identity-view among the five views; or it could be said to be two — namely, distinguished by the aspect of self and of what belongs to self, spoken of as self-view and self-possession-view; or it could be said to be three — on account of the distinction of the three realms of desire, form, and formlessness; or it could be said to be five — on account of the distinction of taking each of the five aggregates as its object; or it could be said to be six — since, within each of the three realms, there is respectively a self-view and a self-possession-view.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Dārṣṭāntika position quietly previewed here — that identity-view is a pure illusion like mistaking a rope for a snake, with nothing real underneath it at all — is a recognizably proto-Sautrāntika move, treating the self-notion as having no basis whatsoever rather than as a real misreading of something real. The Vaibhāṣika response is characteristically realist: the five aggregates of clinging are genuinely existent, so the error lies entirely in the aspect (inverted, self-attributing) under which they are cognized, not in the absence of any object at all. This is the same basic realist commitment that will drive nearly every argument in this fascicle.

2. From One to Nine Hundred Thirty-Six: The Many Ways to Count

Before settling on twenty, the treatise runs through an extraordinary combinatorial exercise, showing that identity-view could equally be classified into one, two, three, five, six, nine, ten, twelve, eighteen, twenty, twenty-four, thirty-six, forty-eight, sixty-five, seventy-two, four hundred eight, or nine hundred thirty-six varieties, depending entirely on which cross-cutting distinction — realm, stage, aggregate versus sense-field versus element, or “ornament/servant/vessel” — one chooses to track.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nine-hundred-thirty-six figure is the ceiling of this whole exercise: eighteen elements, each capable of four basic self-related aspects (is-self, self-possesses-it, belongs-to-self, self-dwells-within-it) further tripled by the “ornament, servant, vessel” refinement of the belonging relation, then doubled again to include the “is-self” case itself — 18 × (3+3+3+1) = 18 × 52 = 936. The treatise is not interested in adjudicating which count is truest; it is demonstrating that twenty is simply the coarsest-grained cut convenient for its own purposes, one deliberate simplification among many defensible ones, chosen because the aggregates come first in the canonical ordering and because the Buddha judged it sufficient for those he was teaching.

3. The Ten Emptinesses, and What "Twenty" Actually Means

The count settles at twenty, and the treatise explains its final reason for privileging identity-view over the other four views: it is the near antidote to ten distinct kinds of emptiness. It then clarifies a purely technical point — what "statement" (句) means when the topic speaks of identity-view's "twenty statements" — before returning to how the general "is self" formula actually cashes out for each aggregate.

十種空者。謂內空。外空。內外空。有為空。無為空。散壞空。本性空。無際空。勝義空。空空。問二十句者。句是何義。答是自性義。謂此見有二十自性。問等隨觀色是我。受想行識是我者。云何等隨觀色是我。受想行識是我耶。答諸所有色。若四大種。若四大種所造。彼一切等隨觀是我。乃至識隨所應當說。問薩迦耶見。唯有漏緣非無漏。自界地緣非他界地。自界地中亦非一切一時而緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ten emptinesses listed here — internal, external, internal-external, conditioned, unconditioned, dispersal, intrinsic-nature, boundless, ultimate-meaning, and the emptiness of emptiness itself — form a systematic classification that later reappears, considerably expanded, in the Prajñāpāramitā literature's own sixteen or eighteen emptinesses; seeing an early, compact ten-fold version cited matter-of-factly here as already-established doctrine is a useful reminder of how much Abhidharma scaffolding preceded and fed into later Mahāyāna systematizations, independent of any direct textual lineage between them.

4. The Coarse and the Subtle: How Form Can "Dwell Within" Feeling

A single aggregate, it turns out, can itself be split into self and self-possession — form and formations each contain multiple constituent factors, so one factor can be taken as self and the rest as its possessions. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The treatise's careful distinction between why suffering-as-pleasure counts as view-grasping while impermanent-as-permanent does not is a genuinely subtle piece of Abhidharma reasoning: view-grasping specifically requires ranking something inferior above something superior, and since permanent dharmas (space, non-analytical cessation) admit no superior/inferior ranking among themselves the way pleasure and pain plainly do, the impermanence error simply falls outside view-grasping's defining shape rather than qualifying for it more weakly. The unresolved loose end about whether the path itself, being also impermanent in one sense, ought to fall under the same “suffering” analysis as nirvāṇa is left as an acknowledged incompleteness rather than forced into false tidiness — a rare moment of the treatise admitting its own classification does not fully close.

13. Impurity as Purity, Purity as Impurity

The last pair completes the set: outside teachers, distracted by momentary grooming and freshness, mistake the body's real but limited purity for ultimate purity — view-grasping, abandoned by seeing suffering, illustrated by the vivid image of a jackal fooled twice over by a flower that merely resembles meat. The reverse error, calling the truths of cessation and the path impure because the afflictions they cut through are themselves impure, closes the fascicle as wrong view, abandoned by seeing cessation and the path.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The kiṃśuka-flower parable is one of this fascicle's most memorable images precisely because the jackal never actually learns anything: fooled by the flower still on the branch, it revises its belief only about the flower already fallen, while continuing to expect meat from every flower not yet fallen — a portrait of a mind that updates on individual instances but never revises the underlying false generalization, which is exactly the diagnosis the treatise offers for how outside teachers persist in calling the body pure despite constant, visible evidence of its own impurity. The four-way comparison distinguishing the Buddhist school's own careful, qualified use of "pure" (only excellent conduct, only wholesome roots, only what counteracts affliction, only ever a small measure) from the outside teachers' unqualified claims closes the fascicle's whole survey on a fittingly systematic note: every inverted view examined here turns out to trace back to the same basic error of mistaking something limited and provisional for something total and ultimate.
