The Loneliest God in the Universe

Fascicle 98 completes the fourfold cycle of vision, knowledge, and wisdom, then turns to named wrong-view assemblages — the Great Brahmā's own lonely creation myth, the Buddha's accompanying light, and the long-nailed ascetic's dialectical defeat. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 98 continues the Five Kinds Nyāsa (五種納息第二之二), opened partway through fascicle 97. It completes the fourfold analysis of vision, knowledge, and wisdom (worldly, undefiled, wrong/right, and the learner's/non-learner's own) before turning to the treatise's own extended engagement with named wrong-view assemblages (見趣) — elaborate cosmological and philosophical delusions examined through self-nature, arising-condition, and counteraction. Terms established across fascicles 93–97 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Giving, Fond Regard, and Sacrificial Offering: One Meaning, or Three?

Fascicle 98 opens within the newly begun Five Kinds Nyāsa, continuing directly from the definition of wrong and right view inherited from fascicle 97: since wrong view denies "giving, fond regard, and sacrificial offering," and right view affirms them, what distinguishes these three terms from one another? A cascade of interpretations follows — a verse suggesting no distinction at all, then the outsider treatise-masters' own successive threefold breakdowns of Brahmanical merit (by class of recipient, by occasion, by dwelling, by ritual practice, by household status, and more), and finally the inner (Buddhist) treatise-masters' own cleaner threefold scheme: past, present, and future merit; bodily, verbal, and mental karma; the nature of giving, of precepts, and of cultivation; and further pairings through the field of compassion, gratitude, and merit, the three moments of giving, and the destiny and spiritual stage of the recipient.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the outsider treatise-masters need nine or ten successive attempts to pin down a stable threefold distinction among giving, fond regard, and sacrificial offering, while the inner treatise-masters settle the same question in a single clean pass (past/present/future, or body/speech/mind), is a quiet but pointed piece of comparative rhetoric: the text is not merely cataloguing Brahmanical exegesis for its own sake, but staging a contrast in which the outsiders' own proliferating uncertainty about their own ritual vocabulary is set against the Buddhist commentators' own confident, structurally elegant resolution of the very same question.

2. Is Every Deviant Wisdom a Fetter?

A tetralemma opens a new topic: is every deviant wisdom (the defiled wisdom underlying wrong view) itself one of the seven fetters established earlier in this treatise? The resolution finds that most defiled wisdom — associated with craving, aversion, conceit, doubt, or the ignorance not shared with others — is deviant wisdom without being a fetter, while the remaining five fetters (craving and the rest) are fetters without being wisdom at all; only the view fetter and the grasping fetter are both at once, since these two alone are simultaneously fetters and themselves a form of wisdom.

The answer: it is in order to put a stop to what other schools maintain, and to display the correct principle — that is, there are those who say: defiled wisdom is not, of its own nature, a fetter; they maintain as follows: how can wisdom, of all things, bear the meaning of binding? In order to put a stop to this same clinging, and to display that whatever defiled wisdom is of the very own-nature of a view is gathered within the fetters, this treatise is composed. Is every deviant wisdom a fetter? The answer: a tetralemma ought to be cast, since deviant wisdom and the fetters, in their own-nature, differ in breadth and narrowness. There is deviant wisdom that is not a fetter — namely, excepting two fetters, the remaining defiled wisdom; "excepting two fetters" refers to excepting the view fetter and the grasping fetter; "the remaining defiled wisdom" refers to the wisdom associated with craving, aversion, conceit, and doubt, and the ignorance not shared with others, together with the wisdom associated with the remaining entanglements and defilements — this possesses the characteristic of deviant wisdom but not the characteristic of a fetter. There is a fetter that is not deviant wisdom — namely, the seven fetters, this referring precisely to craving and the other six — this possesses the characteristic of a fetter but not the characteristic of deviant wisdom. There is what is both deviant wisdom and a fetter — namely, the two fetters, this being precisely the view fetter and the grasping fetter, these possessing both characteristics. There is that which is neither deviant wisdom nor a fetter — this refers to whatever excludes the aforementioned characteristics; "characteristic" refers to what is thereby named, exactly as fully stated before. What, precisely, is this? This refers to, within the formations-aggregate, whatever excludes the defiled wisdom and the remaining seven fetters, the remaining formations-aggregate, together with the whole of the four remaining aggregates, and the unconditioned dharma, constituting the fourth clause.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This tetralemma quietly does important housekeeping work: it clarifies that "fetter" and "deviant wisdom" are two independent classificatory systems that happen to overlap in exactly two places (the view fetter and the grasping fetter), not two names for the same underlying set of mental states — a distinction easy to blur given how often affliction, wrong view, and bondage are discussed together throughout this treatise, but one the Vaibhāṣikas insist on keeping precise.

3. Why "Deviant"? And the Buddha's Own Accompanying Light

A rich etymological excursus asks why defiled wisdom is named "deviant" (左) at all — five converging reasons are offered, from inauspiciousness to the ordinary observation that a left-handed person is popularly deemed less dexterous. A genuinely striking puzzle then follows: if this same character names something so clearly negative, why does scripture speak of the Buddha's own body possessing an "accompanying" (左) light? The resolution insists the two uses share only the character, not the meaning — the Buddha's own light earns this name because it constantly attends his body without ever departing, unlike every other radiance, which flickers and fades. Three specific lights are named (his body's golden radiance, his teeth's brilliant white, and his knowledge's own all-conquering purity), before a puzzle about Dīpaṃkara Buddha's own twelve-year illumination of Lamplight City is resolved by distinguishing his constant bodily light from a separate, deliberately manifested transformation-light meant for teaching.

6. Vision, Knowledge, and Wisdom Belonging to Neither the Learner Nor the Non-Learner

The fourth and final member of this classificatory scheme closes it out: the ordinary being's own vision is the eye faculty, the five views, and worldly right view; his own knowledge is the wisdom associated with the five sense-consciousnesses together with the contaminated wisdom of mental consciousness, spanning wholesome, defiled, and neutral-and-unobscured varieties; and his own wisdom is this same knowledge viewed through its own characteristic of investigation-of-dharma and fully scrutinized decision. 7. The Great Brahmā's Claim: View-Grasping and Clinging to Precepts and Vows

A vivid new topic opens the treatise's engagement with named wrong-view "assemblages": the Great Brahmā's own claim, "I am Brahmā, the Great Brahmā, self-existent; within the world I create and give rise to beings, and am their father," is picked apart clause by clause through two diagnostic questions — which of the five views does this belong to, and by seeing which truth is it cut off? "I am Brahmā, the Great Brahmā, self-existent" is shown to be view-grasping (taking an inferior thing, the five aggregates of clinging, and reckoning it supreme), while "I create and give rise to beings, and am their father" is shown to be clinging to precepts and vows (reckoning what is not a cause to be a cause) — both abandoned the instant the knowledge of suffering arises, since both are illusions about the very domain of suffering itself. The Venerable Pārśva's own objection that such investigation is pointless ("who would trouble himself interrogating the blind?") is overruled: investigating the wrong-view assemblages by self-nature, arising-condition, and counteraction lets even an ordinary being become, in this one respect, as free of these views as the noble ones themselves. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The verdict against the Venerable Pārśva is one of the more quietly consequential judgment calls in this entire treatise: it would have been easy to agree that painstakingly cataloguing every cosmological delusion a confused deity might hold is a waste of scholarly effort better spent elsewhere, yet the compilers insist otherwise, on the grounds that the very act of naming and dismantling a wrong view — however remote or seemingly beneath serious attention — is itself protective, letting an ordinary practitioner who has never met a Great Brahmā nonetheless become immune to the same structural error of reasoning the Great Brahmā himself fell into: mistaking an inferior, painful thing for something supreme, and mistaking coincidence for causation.

8. How Loneliness Became a Creation Myth

The Brahmajāla Sūtra's own account of this same wrong view's arising-condition follows, and it is genuinely touching: at the start of a new cosmic age, a single being from the gods of Streaming Radiance, his own former lifespan exhausted, is reborn alone in the newly formed Brahmā palace, dwelling there in solitude for an immense span of time before a craving for companionship finally arises in him. No fewer than six explanations are offered for why this craving arose — natural law, habituation from beginningless time, residual habit from once having led a multitude, a visiting group of playful gods whose departure left him lonely, his own weariness after animating illusory companions of his own making, or simple envy upon glimpsing another god's own retinue — before the actual gods of Streaming Radiance begin, one by one, to die and be reborn in his presence, and the Great Brahmā, seeing them appear exactly as he had wished, concludes that he created them himself. A careful numerological accounting closes the section: one koṭi of Great Brahmās, a hundred koṭis of solitary Brahmās, and ten koṭi-nayutas of Brahmā-multitude gods, all across a single great-thousand world-system.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Read against its own explicit purpose — demonstrating the folly of a self-aggrandizing wrong view — this origin story is remarkable for how sympathetically it is actually told: the Great Brahmā is not a villain but simply a profoundly lonely being whose own wish for company happens to coincide, through nothing more than ordinary karmic timing, with the arrival of new neighbors, and who then makes the entirely human mistake of reading his own loneliness's resolution as proof of his own creative power — the treatise's real target is not malice but the very natural, almost forgivable cognitive error of confusing wished-for coincidence with genuine causation, which is exactly the mistake clinging to precepts and vows was defined to catch.

9. Dīrghanakha's Dialectical Trap: I Accept Nothing at All

After situating the Great Brahmā's own dwelling, body-size, and lifespan in exhaustive cosmological detail, the fascicle turns to a genuinely famous encounter: the long-nailed wandering ascetic (Dīrghanakha), Śāriputra's own maternal uncle, comes before the Buddha still smarting over his nephew's conversion, determined not to be caught out again, and declares: "I accept nothing at all." The Buddha's reply is a masterstroke of dialectical pressure: "do you, then, accept this very view?" — a question that traps the ascetic whichever way he answers, since accepting it contradicts his own universal denial, while rejecting it leaves him with no thesis to defend at all. The Buddha then generalizes the lesson across all three possible positions (accepting everything, accepting nothing, accepting some and not others), tracing how each relates to craving and attachment, before a final puzzle explains why the annihilationist's own view is no less driven by craving than the eternalist's, despite scripture's own suggestion to the contrary.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Dīrghanakha's own silence at the end of this exchange is one of the most quietly devastating moments in the entire text: he does not lose the argument through any error of logic on his own part, but through the sheer structural impossibility of defending a universal negation without at least one exception — his own view about views — and the Buddha's question does nothing more than make this structural impossibility visible, without ever needing to argue against the ascetic's own specific philosophical content at all. It is a reminder that some positions are refuted not by being shown false, but by being shown incapable of being coherently held in the first place.
