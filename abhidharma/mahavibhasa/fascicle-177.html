A Hundred Blessings for Every Mark

Fascicle 177 opens a new topic within the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之四): the karma that ripens into the Buddha's thirty-two excellent bodily marks — its nature and conditions, the full catalogue of the marks themselves, why the marks and their eighty minor companions never obscure one another, the hundred blessings said to adorn each single mark, and the story of how Śākyamuni's single unwavering verse before the ancient Buddha Tiṣya let him overtake nine entire eons, closing with the treatise's own reckoning of just how vast a single incalculable eon (asaṃkhyeya kalpa) really is. 1. The Karma of the Excellent Marks: Its Nature and Conditions

Fascicle 177 opens a new topic within the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之四): the karma that ripens into the Buddha's thirty-two excellent bodily marks. The treatise first pins down this karma's basic coordinates — its nature (chiefly mental, though all three types of action contribute), its cognitive ground, and the fact that it can only be attained through effortful practice sustained across three incalculable eons, never innately or through the mere abandonment of affliction. It then tackles a genuine puzzle: since a single volition seems too small a cause for thirty-two distinct results, is each mark drawn forth by one volition or many — resolved through a two-stage model in which a single volition first draws a mark forth, sketch-like, and a multitude of further volitions then fill in and complete it.

Chinese Source

問相異熟業以何為自性。為身業。為語業。為意業耶。答三業為自性然意業增上。有說。唯意業為自性。非身語業。所以者何。此業猛利。身語業鈍故。問相異熟業為在意地。為五識身耶。答在意地。非五識身。所以者何。此業有分別。要觀察已行。五識無分別。隨境界力起故。問相異熟業。為加行得為離染得。為生得耶。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: What is the intrinsic nature of the karma that ripens into the excellent bodily marks? Is it bodily karma, verbal karma, or mental karma? Answer: all three types of karma constitute its intrinsic nature, though mental karma predominates. Some say: only mental karma constitutes its intrinsic nature, not bodily or verbal karma. Why is this? Because this karma is intensely powerful, whereas bodily and verbal karma are comparatively dull. Question: does the karma that ripens into the excellent marks belong to the mental-consciousness ground, or to the five sense-consciousnesses? Answer: it belongs to the mental-consciousness ground, not to the five sense-consciousnesses. Why is this? Because this karma involves discrimination — it necessarily follows upon deliberate observation — whereas the five sense-consciousnesses lack discrimination, arising simply in accordance with the power of their objective sphere. Question: is the karma that ripens into the excellent marks attained through effortful practice, through the abandonment of afflictions, or is it innate?

答唯加行得。非離染得非生得。所以者何。此業必在三無數劫修諸波羅蜜多。圓滿身中加行功用作意後而得故。有說。此業加行得亦生得。但非離染得。問相異熟業為聞所成思所成修所成耶。答唯思所成。非聞非修。所以者何。此業勝故非聞所成。欲界繫故非修所成。有說。此業通聞思所成。但非修所成。問相異熟業何處起耶。

Answer: it is attained only through effortful practice, not through the abandonment of afflictions nor innately. Why is this? Because this karma is necessarily attained, over the three incalculable eons of cultivating the perfections, within a body that has perfected the exertion of effortful practice and volitional attention. Some say: this karma is attained both through effortful practice and innately, but not through the abandonment of afflictions. Question: is the karma that ripens into the excellent marks accomplished through hearing, through reflection, or through cultivation? Answer: it is accomplished only through reflection, not through hearing or cultivation. Why is this? Because this karma is superior, it is not accomplished through hearing; because it is bound to the desire realm, it is not accomplished through cultivation. Some say: this karma is accomplished through both hearing and reflection, but not through cultivation. Question: in which realm does the karma that ripens into the excellent marks arise?

答在欲界。非餘界。在人趣非餘趣在贍部洲非餘洲。依何身起者。

Answer: it arises in the desire realm, not in the other realms; in the human destiny, not in other destinies; on the continent of Jambudvīpa, not on the other continents. As for upon what kind of body it arises —

依男身起非女身等。於何時起者。佛出世時非無佛世。緣何境起者現前緣佛起勝思願。不緣餘境。問三十二大丈夫相。為一思所引。

— it arises upon a male body, not a female one, and so on. As for at what time it arises: it arises when a Buddha has appeared in the world, not during a Buddha-less age. As for what object it takes as its condition: it arises by directly taking a Buddha as its condition, giving rise to a supreme resolve and aspiration; it does not take any other object as its condition. Question: are the thirty-two marks of the great being brought about by a single volition,

為多思耶。若爾何失。若一思所引者。云何少業能引多果。施設論說復云何通。如說。如是類業能感足下平滿善住相。乃至如是類業能感頂上烏瑟膩沙相。若多思所引者。云何一眾同分不分分別引耶。答有說。一思所引。問云何少業能感多果。答先以一思牽引。

or by multiple volitions? Either way, what is the difficulty? If they are brought about by a single volition, how can a small amount of karma bring about many results — and how, then, is the statement in the Prajñaptiśāstra to be reconciled, where it says that a karma of such-and-such a kind brings about the mark of the flat, well-planted soles of the feet, and so on up to a karma of such-and-such a kind that brings about the mark of the fleshy protuberance on the crown of the head? If they are brought about by multiple volitions, then how, given that they belong to a single, undivided class of sentient being, can they be brought about as distinctly differentiated? Answer: some say: they are brought about by a single volition. Question: then how can a small amount of karma bring about many results? Answer: first it is drawn forth by a single volition,

後以多思圓滿。是故無過。譬如畫師先以一色作摸後填眾彩。問施設論說復云何通。答彼論說圓滿業不說牽引業。故無過。然三十二大丈夫相是眾同分圓滿業果。非眾同分牽引業果。有說。多思所引。問云何一眾同分不分分別引耶。答爾時菩薩能一注心於一所依所緣行相有多思轉。於中有思能感足下平滿善住相。乃至有思能感頂上烏瑟膩沙相。如是說者。三十二思引三十二大丈夫相。一一復以多業圓滿。問菩薩所起三十二思。於諸相中先引何相。答有說。

and afterward brought to completion by multiple volitions; hence there is no difficulty. It is like a painter who first sketches an outline in a single color and afterward fills it in with many colors. Question: then how is the statement in the Prajñaptiśāstra to be reconciled? Answer: that treatise speaks of the completing karma, not of the drawing-forth karma; hence there is no difficulty. The thirty-two marks of the great being are the fruit of the karma that completes that class of sentient being, not the fruit of the karma that draws it forth. Some say: they are brought about by multiple volitions. Question: then how, given a single, undivided class of sentient being, can they be brought about as distinctly differentiated? Answer: at that time the bodhisattva is able to fix his mind singly upon one basis and one objective-support, yet, with respect to its mode of activity, multiple volitions turn within it. Among these, there is a volition able to bring about the mark of the flat, well-planted soles of the feet, and so on up to a volition able to bring about the mark of the fleshy protuberance on the crown of the head. According to this view, thirty-two volitions bring about the thirty-two marks of the great being, and each mark is then further brought to completion by many further karmas. Question: among the marks, which does the bodhisattva's arising of the thirty-two volitions bring about first? Answer: some say:

先引足下平滿善住相。後引餘相。先安其足後及餘故。有說。先引目紺青相。先以慈眼觀世間故。如是說者。此則不定。隨此相緣合則引此相。

he first brings about the mark of the flat, well-planted soles of the feet, and afterward the other marks, because he first steadies his feet and only afterward extends to the rest. Some say: he first brings about the mark of the dark-blue eyes, because he first surveys the world with a compassionate gaze. According to this latter view: this is not fixed — whichever mark's conditions come together, that mark is brought about accordingly.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The painter's simile — a single color for the outline, many colors filling it in afterward — captures something the Abhidharma is often at pains to establish: that a small cause can be causally sufficient to fix an outcome's basic shape without being causally sufficient to produce its full richness, so that "drawing forth" and "completing" a result can be cleanly separated into two different kinds of karmic work.

2. The Thirty-Two Marks of the Great Being, Enumerated

The treatise now gives its full catalogue of the thirty-two marks of a great being (mahāpuruṣa-lakṣaṇa), from the flat, well-planted soles of the feet to the Brahmā-voice with its eight excellent qualities — the most extended single passage of physical description anywhere in the treatise. Each mark is paired with a brief gloss explaining what it looks like and, where relevant, what it means (the wheel-marked soles that even the deva of skilled craft cannot replicate; the golden skin whose splendor eclipses gold at every cosmic scale in succession, from human ironware up through the adornment-gold of the highest heavens; the single hair per pore, explained as a bodily echo of the bodhisattva's never having taught the Dharma in a confused or disorderly way).

Chinese Source

三十二大丈夫相者。一足善住相。謂佛足下平滿不凹不凸。於踐躡時等按觸地。是故唯佛跡相分明。惡心欲去終不能滅。諸在家者若有此相。必為人王賓伏率土。諸出家者若有此相。必為法王化導一切。二者千輻輪相。謂佛於雙足下有文。如輪千輻具足轂輞圓滿分明巧妙。妙業天子雖極作意不能擬之而別化作。所以者何。妙業天子所化作事是無覆無記智所引。此相是純淨業所引故。復次彼天所作是生得智所引。此相是增上加行智所引故。復次彼天所作是一生所習智所引。此相是無量生所習智所引故。三者指纖長相。謂佛其指纖長傭而漸銳。節不麁現。並時無隙。安布得中光澤圓滿。四者足跟圓長相。謂佛足跟圓長端嚴廣直。五者手足細軟相。謂佛手足細軟如妬羅綿。六者手足網縵相。謂佛手足指間皆有網縵。猶如鵝王指。若合時網即不現而無皺緩。開時便現而無攣急。七者足趺端厚相。謂佛足趺圓厚向指傭寫兩邊端直。與跟相稱。躡時不廣。足下如紅蓮華色。網內邊如綵樹文爪甲如赤銅葉色。網外邊作真金色。細毛紺潤如吠琉璃故。佛雙足猶如寶履眾寶莊嚴光明微妙。八者翳泥耶[蹲-酋+(十/田/ㄙ)]相。謂佛[蹲-酋+(十/田/ㄙ)]脛圓直。漸下傭細。如翳泥耶鹿王[蹲-酋+(十/田/ㄙ)]。九者勢峯藏密相。謂佛勢峯藏密猶如馬王。若爾云何所化得見。有說。世尊愍所化故方便示之。有說。世尊化作象馬陰藏殊妙告所化言。如彼我亦爾。十者身分圓滿相。謂佛身分圓滿如諾瞿陀樹。如從臍至頂。如是從臍至足上下相稱。十一者身毛上靡相。十二者孔生一毛相。十三者身毛右旋相。謂佛身諸毛孔各生一毛。如吠琉璃其色紺潤宛轉右旋毛端上靡。所以一一毛孔唯一毛者。以菩薩時不亂說法故。十四者身真金色相。謂佛身真金色。映奪世間一切金光令不復現。如今時人所用鐵等。於今時所用金邊威光不現。今時所用金至佛在世時所用金邊威光不現。佛在世時所用金若至大海轉輪王路金砂贍部捺陀金邊威光不現。此金砂金若至七金山金邊威光不現。七金山金至妙高山王金邊威光不現。妙高山王金至三十三天莊嚴具金邊威光不現。如是展轉乃至樂變化天莊嚴具金至他化自在天莊嚴具金邊威光不現。此他化自在天莊嚴具金若至佛身金邊威光不現。是故佛身金色最勝。映奪一切世間金色。十五者常光一尋相。謂佛身分周匝常有光明。面各一尋晝夜恒照。十六者皮膚細滑相。謂佛皮膚細滑塵水不住。如吠琉璃及蓮華葉。設佛以足蹈於塵山。吠嵐婆風於中擊坌。欲令佛身及佛足下一塵染著無有是處。十七者七處充滿相。

Ru-Yi Translation

The thirty-two marks of the great being are as follows. First, the mark of the well-planted feet: the soles of the Buddha's feet are flat, neither concave nor convex; when he walks, the sole touches the ground evenly all over. For this reason the Buddha's footprints alone are distinct and clear, and even one who wishes, out of ill will, to erase them can never obliterate them. Among householders, whoever has this mark will surely become a wheel-turning king before whom all submit, ruling over the whole land; among renunciants, whoever has this mark will surely become a Dharma-king who instructs and guides all beings.

Second, the mark of the thousand-spoked wheel: on the soles of both of the Buddha's feet there is a pattern like a wheel of a thousand spokes, complete with hub and rim, perfectly distinct and skillfully wrought. Even Viśvakarman, the deva of skilled craft, however much he exerted his mind, could not replicate it, and had to fashion something different instead. Why is this? Because what that deva fashions is drawn forth by an unobscured-neutral cognition, whereas this mark is drawn forth by a purely undefiled karma. Furthermore, what that deva makes is drawn forth by an innate cognition, whereas this mark is drawn forth by a cognition of superior, effortfully cultivated practice. Furthermore, what that deva makes is drawn forth by a cognition trained in a single lifetime, whereas this mark is drawn forth by a cognition trained over countless lifetimes.

Third, the mark of slender, long fingers: the Buddha's fingers are slender and long, rounded and gradually tapering, with joints that do not show coarsely, set close together without gaps, evenly and well arranged, lustrous and full.

Fourth, the mark of the round, long heel: the Buddha's heel is rounded, long, well-formed, broad, and straight.

Fifth, the mark of soft hands and feet: the Buddha's hands and feet are as soft as tūla-cotton.

Sixth, the mark of webbed hands and feet: between the Buddha's fingers and toes there is webbing, like the feet of a royal goose. When closed, the webbing does not show, yet there is no wrinkling or slackness; when spread open, it becomes visible, yet there is no cramping or tautness.

Seventh, the mark of the level, full instep: the Buddha's instep is rounded and full, sloping evenly toward the toes and straight on both sides, matching the heel in proportion; when he treads, the foot does not spread. The sole of his foot is the color of a red lotus; the inner edge of the webbing is patterned like a painted tree; the nails are the color of red-copper leaf; the outer edge of the webbing is the color of true gold; the fine hairs are dark-blue and glossy like vaiḍūrya. Thus the Buddha's two feet are like jeweled sandals, adorned with a multitude of gems, subtly luminous.

Eighth, the mark of aiṇeya, antelope-like calves: the Buddha's calves and shins are rounded and straight, tapering evenly downward, like the calves of an aiṇeya deer-king.

Ninth, the mark of the hidden, sheathed male organ: the Buddha's organ is hidden and sheathed like that of a stallion king. If this is so, how do those to be converted come to see it? Some say: the Blessed One, out of compassion for them, skillfully reveals it. Some say: the Blessed One conjures an elephant or a horse whose sheathed organ is especially wondrous and tells those to be converted, "Mine is like that one."

Tenth, the mark of the fully proportioned body: the Buddha's body is fully proportioned like a nyagrodha tree — the span from navel to crown matches the span from navel to feet.

Eleventh, the mark of body-hairs that lie slanting upward. Twelfth, the mark of a single hair growing from each pore. Thirteenth, the mark of body-hairs that curl to the right: each pore of the Buddha's body produces a single hair, dark-blue in color like vaiḍūrya, glossy, curling to the right, with the tip lying upward. The reason each single pore produces only a single hair is that, in his time as a bodhisattva, he never spoke the Dharma in a confused or disorderly way.

Fourteenth, the mark of the true-gold-colored body: the Buddha's body is the color of true gold, its splendor eclipsing every gold-light in the world so that it no longer appears — just as, in the present age, the light of the iron and such that ordinary people use is eclipsed beside the gold used in the present age; the gold used in the present age is eclipsed beside the gold used in the Buddha's own lifetime; the gold used in the Buddha's lifetime, brought beside the Jambūnada gold-dust found by the great ocean along the routes of wheel-turning kings, is eclipsed; that gold-dust, brought beside the gold of the seven gold mountains, is eclipsed; the gold of the seven gold mountains, brought beside the gold of Mount Sumeru, is eclipsed; the gold of Mount Sumeru, brought beside the adornment-gold of the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, is eclipsed; and so on in succession, up to the adornment-gold of the Nirmāṇarati heaven being eclipsed beside the adornment-gold of the Paranirmita-vaśavartin heaven; and that adornment-gold, brought beside the gold of the Buddha's own body, is likewise eclipsed and its splendor no longer appears. Therefore the golden color of the Buddha's body is supreme, eclipsing the golden color of the entire world.

Fifteenth, the mark of a constant halo one fathom across: a light is always present surrounding the Buddha's body, extending one fathom on every side, shining constantly day and night.

Sixteenth, the mark of fine, smooth skin: the Buddha's skin is so fine and smooth that dust and water do not cling to it, like vaiḍūrya or a lotus leaf. Even were the Buddha to tread upon a mountain of dust while the vairambhaka wind blew and scattered it about, there is no possibility that even a single speck of dust would adhere to the Buddha's body or to the soles of his feet.

Seventeenth, the mark of the seven full parts —

謂佛七處充滿。於餘身分此為少增。兩手兩足兩肩及項是為七處。

— namely, seven parts of the Buddha's body are full and slightly more rounded than the rest of the body: the two hands, the two feet, the two shoulders, and the nape of the neck — these are the seven parts.

十八者身廣洪直相。謂佛身廣洪直不偃不僂。亦不傍敧端雅充實。

Eighteenth, the mark of the broad, full, upright body: the Buddha's body is broad, full, and upright, neither stooped nor bent, nor leaning to either side; it is dignified, graceful, and full.

十九者師子上身相。謂佛胸臆分齊方廣威肅。如師子王上半身分。

Nineteenth, the mark of a lion's upper body: the Buddha's chest is well-proportioned, square, broad, and majestic, like the upper half of a lion king's body.

二十者肩髆圓滿相。謂佛肩髆圓滿。非諸捔力姝壯力士之所能及。

Twentieth, the mark of full, rounded shoulders: the Buddha's shoulders are full and rounded, beyond what any wrestler or mighty strongman could match.

二十一者立手摩膝相。謂佛平立舒手摩自膝輪。二十二者師子頷輪相。謂佛頷輪廣好如師子王。二十三者具四十齒相。二十四者齒齊平密相。二十五者牙齒鮮白有光明相。謂佛具四十齒皆悉齊平。中無間隙如毛髮許。其色鮮白光明晈映如雪山王。問餘人但有三十二齒。而說彼身中有一百三骨。佛具四十齒。何故亦言身中有一百三骨。而不增耶。答餘人頭骨九分合成。世尊頭骨但有一段。是以俱有一百三骨。二十六者得最上味相。謂佛舌根淨故。令所飲食變成上味。有說。佛舌根上有一切世間悅意美妙勝味種子。若諸苦酢等物至舌根時。此種雜變皆成上味。有說。如來舌根有如是勢力。若諸飲食來至舌根。於中悅意美妙性者便生舌識。麁鄙性者不生舌識。有說。佛咽喉中有二乳泉。若飲食時其乳流出。雜諸飲食皆成上味。然於此中舌根淨故令味殊勝。此理應然。二十七者廣長舌相。謂佛舌相薄軟。廣長出時覆面至耳髮際。若還入口而於口中無所妨礙。二十八者目紺青相。謂佛眼目脩廣。其色紺淨如蘇闍多青蓮花葉。二十九者牛王睫相。謂佛眼睫安布善好猶如牛王。三十者烏瑟膩沙相。謂佛頂髻骨肉合成。量如覆拳青圓殊妙。三十一者眉間白毫相。謂佛眉間白毫長半尋量。右旋宛轉光明清徹。三十二者得梵音聲相。謂佛於喉藏中有妙大種。能發悅意和雅梵音。如羯羅頻迦鳥。及發深遠雷震之聲。如帝釋鼓。如是音聲具八功德。一者深遠。二者和雅。三者分明。四者悅耳。五者入心。六者發喜。七者易了。八者無厭。

Twenty-first, the mark of standing upright with the hand able to touch the knee: standing evenly upright, the Buddha can extend his hand and touch the wheel of his own knee.

Twenty-second, the mark of a lion's jaw: the Buddha's jaw is broad and handsome like that of a lion king.

Twenty-third, the mark of having forty teeth. Twenty-fourth, the mark of teeth that are even and close-set. Twenty-fifth, the mark of bright white, lustrous teeth: the Buddha has forty teeth, all perfectly even, with gaps between them no wider than a hair's breadth; their color is bright white and radiant, gleaming like the Himalayan snow-king. 3. Why Thirty-Two? Marks, Minor Marks, and the Vessel for Awakening

Having catalogued the marks, the treatise turns reflective: what does "mark" even mean, and why exactly thirty-two — no more, no fewer? The Venerable Pārśva's answer (either extreme would provoke doubt) sits alongside a more aesthetic one: thirty-two is simply the number that renders the Buddha's body most exquisite, exactly as his teaching itself admits of no possible improvement or subtraction. The section then places the eighty subsidiary marks (anuvyañjana) in the gaps between the thirty-two major ones, compares the whole ensemble to flowers setting off a forest or gems inlaid in a golden mountain, and reaches for the treatise's most vivid image yet: the Buddha's body as a mirror of Cīna polished to perfection, in which every object appears reflected, so that even hostile māras and tīrthikas cannot help but circle it in reverence. The section closes by insisting the bodhisattva adorns his own body from no personal vanity at all, but to subdue those who scorn the Dharma out of pride in their own looks, to display that every one of the Buddha's qualities is supreme, and to serve as a fit vessel for awakening itself — dramatized in the voice of that future awakening addressing the bodhisattva directly, insisting on a pure and adorned body before it will consent to arise within him.

5. The Hundred Blessings That Adorn Each Mark

Turning to a scriptural claim that each mark is "adorned by a hundred blessings," the treatise identifies these hundred blessings as a hundred discrete volitions — fifty preparing the ground, one that draws the mark forth, and fifty more completing it, likened to a farmer who tills, sows, and then waters and manures in turn. It then surveys a cascade of proposed units for measuring a single "blessing" — the merit needed to become a wheel-king, a Śakra, a Paranirmita-vaśavartin deva-king, a Brahmā — each larger than the last, climaxing in Mahābrahmā's own merit in entreating the Buddha to teach, itself immediately complicated by the objection that Brahmā's entreating mind is merely neutral and desire-realm-bound, and so seemingly incapable of counting as merit at all. A cluster of vivid analogies follows — curing universal blindness, universal poisoning, universal bondage, universal loss of precepts and right view, each in a single stroke — before the treatise pulls back to insist that all such measures, however evocative, remain approximations: a bodhisattva's actual merit, cultivated across three incalculable eons, is strictly immeasurable, knowable to a Buddha alone.

Such vast and measureless blessing, complete and full to a hundred, adorns a single mark, and so on in succession until all thirty-two marks are each complete with a hundred blessings. Because the Buddha adorns himself with these thirty-two marks, each with its hundred blessings, together with the eighty minor marks that adorn his body, he is therefore the most honored and supreme among gods and men.

6. Śākyamuni Overtakes Nine Eons

The treatise asks how long the mark-producing karma takes to perfect — a hundred great eons, ordinarily — except for Śākyamuni, who compressed it into ninety-one by sheer diligence, and tells the story behind the exception. Under the ancient Buddha Tiṣya, two bodhisattva disciples, Śākyamuni and Maitreya, were cultivating together; Tiṣya foresaw that Maitreya's own faculties would ripen first but that Śākyamuni's converts would ripen first, and, since ripening many beings at once is far harder than ripening one, engineered an encounter to accelerate Śākyamuni specifically — withdrawing into a mountain grotto in blazing meditative bliss, where Śākyamuni found him and, transfixed for seven days and nights, praised him with a single unvarying verse. That single verse of unwavering devotion, the treatise explains, let Śākyamuni leap over nine entire eons and overtake Maitreya to buddhahood first — the same singleness of purpose then explaining, more broadly, why Maitreya (who chiefly benefits himself) and Śākyamuni (who chiefly benefits others) end up so exactly inverted in whose faculties, and whose disciples', ripen first.

7. Measuring the Three Incalculable Eons

The fascicle closes with the treatise's most technical passage: how large, exactly, is a single "incalculable eon" (asaṃkhyeya kalpa)? Two rival counting schemes are laid out in full, each compounding named units by a hundred thousand-fold, or by tenfold, at each step — from lakṣa and koṭi up through vagra and beyond, until the count exceeds the very reach of arithmetic and the term "asaṃkhyeya," incalculable, is applied to name exactly that point. Alternative measures follow — Śāriputra's own recollective range (rejected, since no arhat's memory can span so vast a period), and a giving-based scale keyed to whether the bodhisattva has learned to give every possession, then every field, then both at once. The fascicle's true center of gravity, though, is the story of the potter Prabhāsa: told through the vast wheel of time to a Buddha named Śākyamuni whose shoulders ached from ceaseless travel to help beings, the potter anointed and bathed him with sesame oil and warm water, and, moved to tears, vowed to become a Buddha with an identical name, retinue, and disciples — a vow the treatise reveals was Śākyamuni's own, its fulfillment marking the completion of the first of his three incalculable eons, with the second and third marked by later Buddhas encountered along the way.

At that time there was a potter named Prabhāsa. The Buddha, knowing the time had come, said to his attendant Ānanda: "My body is now afflicted and unwell."

汝可往廣熾陶師家求胡麻油及煖水。為吾塗洗。侍者敬諾往陶師家。住廣熾前愛語問訊已。方便讚佛種種功德勝戒定慧。三十二相八十隨好圓光赫奕。智見無礙辯才無滯。復告廣熾。如是世尊若不出家。當為輪王王四洲界。我及汝等一切世間皆為僕使。然今棄捨如是王位出家苦行。得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。具一切智見。斷一切疑網。施一切決定。能盡一切問論源底。視諸有情猶如一子。今者在此不遠而住。然為拔濟汝等苦故恒涉道路。為風所薄肩背勞積。

"You may go to the potter Prabhāsa's house and ask for sesame oil and warm water, to anoint and bathe me with." The attendant respectfully assented, and went to the potter's house. Standing before Prabhāsa, he greeted him with kind words and, by way of skillful approach, praised the Buddha's manifold virtues — his supreme moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom; his thirty-two marks and eighty minor marks; his perfect halo blazing with splendor; his unobstructed wisdom and insight; his unhindered eloquence. He further told Prabhāsa: "Such is the Blessed One that, had he not gone forth, he would have become a wheel-turning king ruling over the four continents, and I and all of you and all the world would be his servants. Yet now he has renounced such kingship, gone forth, and practiced austerities, and attained unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, endowed with omniscient wisdom and insight, having severed every net of doubt, and able to resolve every uncertainty and fathom the depths of every question and discourse. He regards all sentient beings as though they were his only child. At present he is dwelling not far from here; yet, for the sake of rescuing you and others from suffering, he ever travels the roads, and has been afflicted by wind, his shoulders and back grown weary and strained."

須油煖水故相造詣。頗能施耶。爾時廣熾聞已踊躍歎未曾有。如何人間有是功德。即報尊者。仁今且還。我當如命自往佛所。其去未久。廣熾即辦生胡麻油及煖香水持往佛所。佛遙見之。為令彼人種善根故脫去餘衣。唯留襯身踞机而待。廣熾到已發淳淨心。以所持油恭敬善巧。塗佛肩背種種摩搦。復以煖水香湯灌洗。佛時風疾釋然除愈。以慈軟音慰喻廣熾。彼聞歡喜即發願言。願我未來當得作佛。名號眷屬時處弟子。如今世尊等無有異。當知彼陶師者即釋迦菩薩。由本願故今名號等如昔不異。然從彼佛發是願後。乃至逢事寶髻如來。是名初劫阿僧企耶滿。從此以後乃至逢事然燈如來。是名第二劫阿僧企耶滿。復從此後乃至逢事勝觀如來。是名第三劫阿僧企耶滿。此後復經九十一劫修妙相業。至逢事迦葉波佛時方得圓滿。有說。有三種阿僧企耶。一劫阿僧企耶。二生阿僧企耶。三妙行阿僧企耶。劫阿僧企耶者。謂以大劫為一積至洛叉俱胝。展轉乃至過婆揭羅數。生阿僧企耶者。謂一一劫經無數生。妙行阿僧企耶者。謂一一劫修無數妙行。由此三種阿僧企耶證無上覺。此不應理。如實義者。此中但說經三劫阿僧企耶。修行圓滿。

It is for this reason — needing oil and warm water — that I have come to call upon you. Might you be willing to give some?" Upon hearing this, Prabhāsa leapt up in joy and exclaimed in wonder, "How can there be such virtue in the human world!" He replied at once to the venerable one: "Sir, please return for now; I shall myself go at once to the Buddha's place, as swiftly as life allows." Before long, Prabhāsa had prepared fresh sesame oil and warm, fragrant water and carried them to the Buddha. The Buddha, seeing him from afar, and in order to let him plant wholesome roots, removed his outer robes, keeping only his inner garment, and sat upon a stool awaiting him. When Prabhāsa arrived, he gave rise to a pure and sincere mind, and with reverent skill anointed the Buddha's shoulders and back with the oil he had brought, massaging and kneading them, and then bathed and washed them with the warm, fragrant water. The Buddha's affliction from the wind was thereupon released and cured. With a compassionate, gentle voice he comforted and reassured Prabhāsa. Hearing this, Prabhāsa rejoiced and made a vow, saying: "May I in the future attain Buddhahood, with a name, a retinue, a time, a place, and disciples exactly like those of the Blessed One now, without any difference." It should be understood that this potter was none other than the bodhisattva Śākyamuni; because of his original vow, his name and the rest are now unchanged from what they were then. From the time he made this vow before that Buddha, up until he encountered and served the Tathāgata Ratnaśikhin, is called the completion of the first eon-asaṃkhyeya. From this point onward, up until he encountered and served the Tathāgata Dīpaṃkara, is called the completion of the second eon-asaṃkhyeya. From this point onward again, up until he encountered and served the Tathāgata Viśiṣṭadarśin, is called the completion of the third eon-asaṃkhyeya. After this, he further cultivated the karma of the excellent marks for ninety-one eons, and it was only when he encountered and served the Buddha Kāśyapa that this was finally brought to completion.

Some say: there are three kinds of asaṃkhyeya: first, the eon-asaṃkhyeya; second, the birth-asaṃkhyeya; third, the wondrous-practice-asaṃkhyeya. The eon-asaṃkhyeya is: taking a great eon as a single unit, accumulating it up through lakṣa and koṭi, proceeding in succession until one passes beyond the count of vagra. The birth-asaṃkhyeya is the fact that within each single eon one passes through countless births. The wondrous-practice-asaṃkhyeya is the fact that within each single eon one cultivates countless wondrous practices. It is by means of these three kinds of asaṃkhyeya, on this account, that supreme awakening is realized. This view should not be accepted as correct. As for the true meaning: here it is said only that, having passed through three eon-asaṃkhyeyas, the cultivation of practice is brought to completion.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the numerical scheme for "incalculable" is followed immediately by a fully human story of sore shoulders, borrowed oil, and a single grateful vow is exactly the rhetorical move this treatise favors throughout: it will not let the sheer scale of cosmic time remain an abstraction, insisting instead on anchoring even the vastest measure of practice to one intimate, nameable act of kindness that any listener could recognize and, in principle, repeat.
