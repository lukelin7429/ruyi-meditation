The Ignorance That Arises Alone

Fascicle 38 closes out the five-hindrances discussion carried over from fascicle 37 — the parable of two bandits binding and blinding their victim, a precise four-alternative analysis of "hindrance" versus "covering," and the strange, self-sufficient latent tendency of ignorance that arises through its own power alone, without any other affliction at its side. It then opens an entirely new topic, the Characteristics Nyāsa, refuting five rival schools on whether arising, abiding, aging, and impermanence are themselves real, before closing with an intricate debate over whether karma's own power outranks impermanence's. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Every Hindrance Covers, But Not Every Hindrance Is Among the Five

The fascicle opens by finishing a question carried over from fascicle 37: do the five named hindrances (desire and greed, hatred, torpor-and-drowsiness, restlessness-and-worry, doubt) subsume every hindrance, or does every hindrance subsume the five? The treatise answers that every hindrance subsumes the five, not the reverse — ignorance itself is a hindrance, yet stands outside the five precisely because of its own disproportionate weight, and the Buddha therefore singled it out as a distinct sixth.

As the sūtra states, there are five hindrances — do the five hindrances subsume every hindrance, or does every hindrance subsume the five hindrances? And so on, at length. One asks: why is this treatise composed? The answer: it is composed in order to bring those in doubt to a settled resolution. As the sūtra states, there are five hindrances: first, the hindrance of desire and greed; second, the hindrance of hatred; third, the hindrance of torpor and drowsiness; fourth, the hindrance of restlessness and worry; fifth, the hindrance of doubt. There are those who thereby give rise to the doubt that there exist only these five hindrances, and that ignorance is not itself a hindrance; this treatise now wishes to bring this doubt to a settled resolution, revealing that, beyond these five hindrances, there exists a further, distinct sixth — namely, the hindrance of ignorance — and it is on account of this cause and condition that this treatise is composed. Do the five hindrances subsume every hindrance, or does every hindrance subsume the five hindrances? Taken together, every hindrance subsumes the five hindrances;

It is the hindrance of ignorance — since hindrances, taken as a whole, are numerous, they are able to subsume the five hindrances, while the five hindrances, being fewer, are unable to subsume every hindrance, exactly as a large vessel is able to cover a small one, while a small vessel is unable to cover a large one. 2. Two Bandits, One Victim: Why Ignorance Is Called a Hindrance and Craving a Fetter

A canonical verse pairs ignorance-as-hindrance with craving-as-fetter, and the treatise asks why the sūtra assigns each affliction only one of these two labels when, strictly, both apply to both. Nine distinct explanations follow, most memorably the parable of two bandits, Ili and Śeśa, one of whom binds a victim's limbs while the other throws dirt in his eyes — exactly as craving binds a being to birth and death while ignorance blinds him to the way out. A four-alternative analysis of "hindrance" versus "covering" then follows, turning on whether the sense of the term is fixed by a dharma's own nature (spanning all three time-periods) or by its actual present function (belonging to the present alone).

Ru-Yi Translation

"Covered by the hindrance of ignorance, bound by the fetter of craving, both the foolish and the wise alike acquire just such a body endowed with consciousness." One asks: ignorance is both a hindrance and a fetter, and craving is both a fetter and a hindrance — why, then, does this passage speak of ignorance only as a hindrance, and of craving only as a fetter? The answer: ignorance, too, ought properly to be spoken of as a fetter, and craving, too, ought properly to be spoken of as a hindrance, and that this is not so stated should be understood as a matter of surplus meaning left implicit. Further, it is in order to display a variety of wordings and a variety of expressions — for, when expressed through a variety of wordings and expressions, the meaning is thereby more easily understood and more easily retained, whereas otherwise it becomes needlessly cumbersome. Further, it is in order to display two approaches, and so on, at length — just as ignorance is spoken of as a hindrance, so too, one might expect, ought craving to be; and just as craving is spoken of as a fetter, so too, one might expect, ought ignorance to be — yet it is precisely in order to display these two distinct approaches that ignorance is spoken of only as a hindrance, and craving only as a fetter. Further, the sense of "hindrance" predominates in ignorance, and the sense of "fetter" is but slight, and it is for this reason that it is called only a hindrance; the sense of "fetter" predominates in craving, and the sense of "hindrance" is but slight, and it is for this reason that it is called only a fetter. Further, the sense of "hindrance" is weighty in ignorance, and the sense of "fetter" is light, and it is for this reason that it is called only a hindrance; the sense of "fetter" is weighty in craving, and the sense of "hindrance" is light, and it is for this reason that it is called only a fetter. Further, the sense of "covering" is the meaning of "hindrance" — among all the afflictions, there exists no second affliction, apart from ignorance, capable of covering over a sentient being's own eye of wisdom, and it is for this reason that it is called a hindrance. The sense of "binding" is the meaning of "fetter" — among all the afflictions, there exists no second affliction, apart from craving, capable of binding a sentient being fast within birth and death for so long a time, and it is for this reason that it is called a fetter. Sentient beings, blinded by the hindrance of ignorance and bound by the fetter of craving, are thereby unable to relinquish birth and death and proceed toward nirvāṇa — exactly as a certain man, beset by two hateful bandits, one of whom bound his hands and feet while the other threw dirt into his eyes, was thereby, being bound and unable to see, unable to flee to any place of safety — so too is it with sentient beings, who, covered by ignorance and fettered by craving, are unable to relinquish birth and death and proceed toward nirvāṇa. Here, one ought to relate the simile of the two hateful bandits — it is told that, in ancient times, there were two bandits, one named Ili and the other named Śeśa, who forever traveled and dwelt together; whenever they encountered a wealthy man, one would bind his hands and feet while the other threw dirt into his eyes, and, having taken his wealth, they would depart, leaving the man bound and blind, so that he would, on the very spot, suffer and perish. Sentient beings, too, are just the same — covered and bound by ignorance and craving, they sink and drown within birth and death. It is on this basis that the Venerable Ghoṣa has stated: sentient beings, blinded by ignorance and bound by craving, dwell for a long time within birth and death, and thereby increase every evil dharma — and it is for this reason that ignorance is spoken of, in particular, as a hindrance, and craving, in particular, as a fetter, this designation being well established in principle. Yet the hindrance of ignorance is so disproportionately weighty in its own force and function, surpassing the former five, that the Buddha did not include it among the five hindrances, since the force of the five hindrances is roughly equal among themselves. The answer: the foregoing, following the approach of the sūtra, established only that, beyond the five hindrances, there exists a further, distinct sixth, ignorance, as a hindrance; this treatise now wishes, following the approach of the Abhidharma, to state that every affliction whatsoever is without exception a hindrance, since the sense of covering and obstructing is the very meaning of "hindrance," and every affliction is capable of covering and obstructing the noble path, and the wholesome roots that prepare for the noble path, and it is for this reason that all are called hindrances — and it is on account of this cause and condition that this treatise is further composed. Does every hindrance cover? The answer: this ought to be set out in four alternatives. Here, "hindrance" is spoken of with reference to inherent nature and characteristic — desire and greed, and the rest of the five, whether past, future, or present, are all, without exception, established within these five and possess the nature and characteristic of a hindrance, and are therefore all called hindrances. "Covering," here, is spoken of with reference to actual function — every affliction, while present, possesses the function of covering, and is therefore called "covering"; a past or future affliction possesses no function of covering, and is therefore not called "covering." On account of this mutual relation of broader and narrower scope between the two, four alternatives are formed. There is that which is a hindrance but does not cover — namely, the five hindrances, past or future; these possess the nature and characteristic of a hindrance, and are therefore called hindrances, yet possess no function of covering, and are therefore not called "covering," since a past hindrance's function has already ceased, and a future hindrance's function has not yet come to be. It is on account of such manifold causes and conditions as these that only present affliction is called "covering." There is that which is both a hindrance and covers — namely, any one of the five hindrances presently manifest before one — that is, when the hindrance of desire and greed is presently manifest before one, then, in the waking state, three hindrances are presently manifest — namely, desire and greed, torpor, and restlessness; in the sleeping state, four hindrances are presently manifest — namely, the former three, together with drowsiness. And just as with the hindrance of desire and greed, so too with the hindrances of hatred, worry, and doubt. If the hindrance of torpor is presently manifest before one, then, in the waking state, two hindrances are necessarily presently manifest — namely, torpor and restlessness; in the sleeping state, three hindrances are necessarily presently manifest — namely, the former two, together with drowsiness. And just as with the hindrance of torpor, so too with the hindrance of restlessness. If the hindrance of drowsiness is presently manifest before one, then three hindrances are necessarily presently manifest — namely, drowsiness, torpor, and restlessness. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two-bandits parable does real conceptual work beyond mere illustration: it explains why a being cannot simply choose to walk toward liberation even after ignorance is somewhat reduced, or see clearly even after craving somewhat loosens its grip — both hands are needed to hold a victim down, and both afflictions are needed to keep a being trapped, which is exactly why the path requires uprooting both together rather than either alone.

3. The Tetralemma Completed, and Why the Five Hindrances Alone Earn a Name

The four-alternative analysis of hindrance versus covering is completed, cataloguing exactly which formations are a hindrance but do not cover, which cover but are not a hindrance, which are both, and which are neither — sorting the entire aggregate of formations into these four bins by whether they possess the nature of a hindrance and whether they possess the present, active function of covering.

4. Is the View of Self Unwholesome? Why Some Ignorance Is Merely Indeterminate

The treatise turns to whether ignorance bound to the desire realm is invariably unwholesome, refuting the Dārṣṭāntika position that every affliction whatsoever is unwholesome simply because it lacks the backing of skillful wisdom. 9. Why There Is No "Unique" Restlessness

A natural question follows: given that ignorance can arise uniquely, alone, can restlessness — equally pervasive, equally troublesome — do the same? The treatise answers firmly no, and lists an unusually long chain of reasons: restlessness always co-arises with the ignorance latent tendency, is always associated with torpor, disturbs and disorders the very limbs of meditative absorption, ruins one's ability to hold an object of concentration in mind, and saps the will to sustain diligent practice — none of which torpor itself does, since torpor's own dull, absorption-resembling character makes it comparatively mild in its effects.

The clean separation this tenfold grid enforces — a material dharma's own arising is never itself material — is what actually rescues the theory from the Resembling-Continuum objection raised just above: if a characteristic simply were whatever it characterized, there would be no principled way to distinguish a dharma from its own coming-to-be, and the whole apparatus of momentary arising and ceasing would collapse into a bare assertion rather than an analyzable structure.

12. What Is Aging, What Is Death, What Is Impermanence?

Turning from the noble ones' own precise, technical reasoning to the conventional world's own manner of speaking, the treatise now defines the coarse, everyday characteristics of the conditioned in vivid, physical terms — aging as graying hair, wrinkling skin, a stooped back, and trembling limbs, illustrated through Vasumitra's five similes of a worn garment, a broken cart, a decaying house, a withered flower, and a slack instrument; death as a being's departure from its own class of homogeneous being, relinquishing lifespan, warmth, and the life faculty together; and impermanence, most broadly, as the scattering and ruin of formations as such.

13. How Death Differs From Impermanence

A precise scope-distinction follows: death and impermanence are commonly treated as synonyms, but the treatise insists they are not. Every death is a case of impermanence, but impermanence is far broader, extending to what is external as well as internal, insentient as well as sentient, and mindless as well as mind-possessing — death names only the very last cessation of the life faculty and the aggregates within a sentient, internal, mind-possessing continuum, while impermanence names the cessation of quite literally anything conditioned, at any moment, anywhere.

14. Which Is Stronger: The Power of Karma, or the Power of Impermanence?

The fascicle closes with an intricate, repeatedly-restructured debate: is the force of karma greater than the force of impermanence, or the reverse? The question is asked and re-asked under three successive readings of what "karma" itself means — first as the noble path (able to extinguish formations across all three time-periods, not merely the present, and so judged the stronger of the two); then as the karma that draws forth a being's own class of existence across the five destinies (stronger, since it alone can bring beings together into new forms of existence, harmony being far harder to achieve than mere separation); and finally as bodily, verbal, and mental karma broadly construed (stronger still, since karma alone can bring about a resultant dharma of any kind, where impermanence can only ever extinguish what has already arisen). In each round, an initial objection — that karma, too, is itself impermanent, and so cannot outrank impermanence — is raised and then set aside in favor of the treatise's own settled verdict.
