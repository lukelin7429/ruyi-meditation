Jewel-Boxes, Filth, and the Five Fruits

Fascicle 121 maps the five kinds of fruit onto every stage of the noble path, distinguishes unwholesome from inverted karma with jewel-box and filth similes, explains why sight and hearing earn their own names while smell, taste, and touch share one, and works through an exhaustive combinatorial matrix from karmic profile to rebirth. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Karma and Its Ripening Need Not Be Freed from Defilement Together

Fascicle 121 continues the Killing-of-Life Nyāsa by refuting the Vātsīputrīya claim that karma's ripening spans all five categories of abandonment just as karma itself does — establishing instead that ripening belongs only to what is abandoned by cultivation. A precise rule follows: karma can be freed from defilement well before its own ripening catches up (as with a stream-enterer's already-purified view-abandoned karma, whose ripening lags behind), or the two can be freed simultaneously at the culminating ninth stage of a path — but never can ripening be freed first, with the karma trailing behind it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The strict one-way rule — karma may outpace its own ripening in becoming freed from defilement, but ripening can never outpace its karma — encodes a deeper structural intuition about cause and effect: an effect can lag behind a cause's own transformation, since the effect must first be produced before it can itself be resolved, but an effect could never resolve before the cause that is still actively producing it has itself been resolved.

2. The Five Kinds of Fruit, Mapped Onto the Entire Path

Against outside-path followers who deny karma has any fruit at all, the treatise details five kinds of fruit — outflow, ripening, freedom from bondage, exertion, and predominance — then maps them with extraordinary precision onto every single stage of the noble path, from the very first forbearance regarding suffering through the vajra-like concentration that ends the path entirely: each uninterrupted path is shown to yield the freedom-fruit and exertion-fruit for the affliction it directly cuts, each liberation path the outflow-fruit and exertion-fruit for that same cutting, and each subsequent superior path merely the outflow-fruit, while paths that only formally verify an already-accomplished cutting-off receive the exertion-fruit alone.

What arises through the power of exertion is called the fruit of exertion; what arises through the power of predominance is called the fruit of predominance. The fruit of cultivation is such that, by means of a form-realm path, one gives rise to a desire-realm transformation-body and desire-realm speech — this transformation and speech are the fruit of cultivation, and other instances of the fruit of cultivation are likewise so. The treatise-masters of Kashmir say: these latter four are simply included within the previous five, since they are nothing other than the fruit of exertion and the fruit of predominance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The general rule distinguishing the fruit of exertion from the fruit of predominance — using a farmer's own harvest, which is both fruits for the one who labored over it but only the fruit of predominance for whoever merely eats it — quietly generalizes into ethics of authorship and consumption: what one has actively brought about through one's own effort belongs to one in a way that what one has merely inherited, received, or benefited from passively does not, even when the two look identical from the outside.

3. How Many Fruits Does Each Kind of Karma Have?

A precise accounting closes the fruit-taxonomy: the worldly antidote-path's own karma has the full complement of five fruits, while its preparatory, liberation, and further-advancing stages (together with ordinary wholesome and unwholesome karma) have four, lacking only the fruit of freedom from bondage; the undefiled antidote-path likewise has four, lacking only the ripening-fruit; and its own subsidiary stages, together with neutral karma, have three, lacking both. The governing rule then follows: karma with a ripening always has a fruit, but karma with a fruit need not have any ripening at all — neutral and undefiled karma being the standing exceptions, since neither is firm enough, nor moistened by the water of craving, to ripen.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That neutral and undefiled karma can have a fruit without ever having a ripening is a quietly important refinement of the entire karmic system: it shows that "having consequences" and "undergoing ripening" are not the same claim, and that a great deal of karmic activity — the neutral, the already-liberated — produces real effects in the world of dharmas while remaining permanently outside the specific mechanism of moral retribution that ripening names.

4. Reconciling Two Verses That Seem to Deny Karma Has Fruit

Two scriptural verses appear to contradict the rule that all karma bears fruit — one comparing eloquent but unheeded speech to a fragrant-looking flower with no actual scent, the other condemning a life wasted in dull sleep as "empty, without fruit, without meaning" — both carefully reconciled as addressing specific pastoral concerns (a teacher's frustration at unheeded instruction, a caution against squandering a moment ripe for insight) rather than making any general metaphysical claim that karma itself can fail to bear fruit.

Answer: there is a time when, being awake, one gives rise to evil discursive thought and examination, quarreling with and disturbing countless beings — it was in order to caution against this that the Buddha spoke in this way. It is for this reason that these sūtras carry a distinct, specific intent, not a claim that karma in general bears no fruit whatsoever.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reconciliation technique used on both verses — locating a narrow, situational intent behind language that sounds sweepingly general — is a recurring interpretive habit worth naming: rather than let two verses stand in flat contradiction with the treatise's own carefully established doctrine, the commentators assume the sūtra's author knew exactly what he meant and simply meant something more local than his words, taken out of context, might suggest.

5. Unwholesome or Inverted? A Tetralemma with Jewel-Boxes and Filth

A genuinely interesting philosophical distinction separates two independent axes of moral evaluation: whether a deed is unwholesome (a fact about the deed's own nature) and whether it is "inverted" (a fact about whether the mind behind it is confused about cause and effect). This yields evocative combinations — evil deeds committed by someone who correctly believes in karma, merely swept along by a degenerate era, a barbarous homeland, or violent company (a jeweled box filled with filth); virtuous deeds performed by a genuine skeptic of cause and effect, carried along instead by a fortunate era, a civilized homeland, or virtuous company (a filthy vessel filled with jewels); and the two matching pairs where deed and belief finally align.

6. A Second Tetralemma: Lying About What One Has Seen

The unwholesome/inverted distinction is reworked through a sharper psychological lens: someone who has genuinely not seen something, yet is pressured by self-interest, favor, or gain into falsely claiming they did, produces unwholesome speech that is nevertheless not inverted, since what they say happens to match their own (mistaken) belief; conversely, honest humility that denies a real attainment is inverted (the statement contradicts fact) yet not unwholesome (nothing is concealed). This same fourfold analysis is then applied across all four modes of perception — seen, heard, sensed, and known — each doubled again for its negative case, ultimately totaling twenty-four distinct tetralemmas.

7. Why Sight and Hearing Get Their Own Names, but Smell, Taste, and Touch Share One

A rich technical debate asks why eye- and ear-consciousness's objects each earn their own name ("seen," "heard"), while nose-, tongue-, and body-consciousness's objects are lumped together under the single term "sensed."
