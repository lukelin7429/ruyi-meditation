Mistaking Calm for Cure

Fascicle 43 continues the Volition Nyāsa, opening with a twelvefold map of scattered versus steady concentration, then distinguishing ignorance from incorrect awareness through a formally structured debate, and arrogance from pride through the Venerable Aniruddha's own solitary boast to Śāriputra. Its longest sustained thread is a detailed anatomy of overreaching pride — the precise mechanism by which a sincere practitioner mistakes the temporary quieting of affliction for genuine, permanent liberation, working systematically through every stage from first seeing the truths to declaring "I shall undergo no further existence." This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Two Kinds of Concentration, and a Twelvefold Classification

Fascicle 43 opens by continuing directly from the previous fascicle's account of mental scatteredness, distinguishing defiled concentration, which may also be called scatteredness, from undefiled concentration, which never can. The treatise then sets out an ambitious twelvefold classification of concentration, crossing whether it takes a single object or many, a single meditative aspect or many, and whether it is scattered or unscattered — twelve possible combinations in all.

Chinese Source

應知此中有二種三摩地。一染污。二不染污。染污者。名三摩地。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should understand that, within this discussion, there are two kinds of concentration: first, defiled; second, undefiled. The defiled kind is called both concentration and mental scatteredness;

亦名散亂。不染污者。名三摩地。不名散亂。故三摩地有十二句。

the undefiled kind is called concentration, but is never called mental scatteredness. Concentration, accordingly, may be classified through twelve statements.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Naming defiled concentration by two different words depending on context — sometimes "concentration," sometimes "scatteredness" — while undefiled concentration only ever earns the one name, quietly encodes a value judgment into the very vocabulary available for describing mental states: only concentration untouched by affliction gets to keep its name stable across every description, while defiled concentration's own identity remains permanently unsettled, describable equally as focus or as its own opposite.

2. The First Examples: One Object, One Aspect

The treatise begins working through its twelvefold grid with concrete meditative examples. A practitioner whose contemplation of a corpse's decay is not yet thoroughly ripened finds his mind sliding uncontrollably between livid discoloration, bloating, and scattered bone — a single object, scattered; once ripened, that same sequence of images holds steady — a single object, unscattered. The same pattern repeats for a single meditative aspect, such as contemplating impermanence, and then begins to combine object and aspect together.

Chinese Source

一有三摩地。一所緣是散亂。二有三摩地。一所緣非散亂。三有三摩地。一行相是散亂。四有三摩地。一行相非散亂。五有三摩地。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, there is a concentration whose single object is scattered. Second, there is a concentration whose single object is unscattered. Third, there is a concentration whose single aspect is scattered. Fourth, there is a concentration whose single aspect is unscattered. Fifth, there is

一所緣一行相是散亂。六有三摩地。一所緣一行相非散亂。七有三摩地。多所緣是散亂。八有三摩地。多所緣非散亂。九有三摩地多行相是散亂。十有三摩地。多行相非散亂。十一有三摩地。多所緣多行相是散亂。十二有三摩地。多所緣多行相非散亂。有三摩地一所緣是散亂者。如有一類。隨於一物思惟不淨。修未純熟。復即於此或觀青瘀。或觀膖脹。或觀膿爛。或觀破壞。或觀異赤。或觀被食。或觀分離。或觀白骨。或觀骨鎖。其心散亂流蕩不住。不專一境。由此因緣前定退失。後定不進。有三摩地一所緣非散亂者。如有一類。隨於一物思惟不淨。修已純熟。復即於此或觀青瘀廣說乃至或觀骨鎖。心不散亂。不流不蕩。安住守一。由此因緣前定不失能進後定有三摩地一行相是散亂者。如有一類。思惟非常。修未純熟。復即由此。或觀增減。或觀暫時。或觀轉變。或觀磨滅。其心散亂流蕩不住。不專一境。由此因緣前定退失後定不進。有三摩地一行相非散亂者。如有一類思惟非常。修已純熟。復即由此或觀增減。廣說乃至或觀磨滅。心不散亂不流不蕩安住守一。由此因緣前定不失能進後定有三摩地一所緣一行相是散亂者。如有一類。思惟色非常。修未純熟。復即於此由此或觀增減。廣說乃至或觀磨滅。

a concentration whose single object and single aspect is scattered. Sixth, there is a concentration whose single object and single aspect is unscattered. Seventh, there is a concentration whose many objects are scattered. Eighth, there is a concentration whose many objects are unscattered. Ninth, there is a concentration whose many aspects are scattered. Tenth, there is a concentration whose many aspects are unscattered. Eleventh, there is a concentration whose many objects and many aspects are scattered. Twelfth, there is a concentration whose many objects and many aspects are unscattered. As for a concentration whose single object is scattered: this refers, for instance, to a certain kind of person who, reflecting upon the impurity of a single thing, his cultivation not yet thoroughly ripened, then, with respect to that same thing, contemplates it as livid discoloration, or as bloating, or as festering, or as broken apart, or as marked with strange redness, or as being devoured, or as scattered and separated, or as reduced to white bone, or as a jumbled heap of bones — his mind scattered, drifting, unfixed, lacking singleness of object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is lost and his later concentration fails to advance. As for a concentration whose single object is unscattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who, reflecting upon the impurity of a single thing, his cultivation already thoroughly ripened, then, with respect to that same thing, contemplates it, broadly, as livid discoloration all the way through to a jumbled heap of bones — his mind unscattered, not drifting, not unfixed, abiding and holding fast to a single object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is not lost, and he is able to advance to a later concentration. As for a concentration whose single aspect is scattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who reflects upon impermanence, his cultivation not yet thoroughly ripened, and who, on this very basis, contemplates now its increase and decrease, now its momentariness, now its alteration, now its wearing away — his mind scattered, drifting, unfixed, lacking singleness of object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is lost and his later concentration fails to advance. As for a concentration whose single aspect is unscattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who reflects upon impermanence, his cultivation already thoroughly ripened, and who, on this very basis, contemplates, broadly, its increase and decrease all the way through to its wearing away — his mind unscattered, not drifting, not unfixed, abiding and holding fast to a single object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is not lost, and he is able to advance to a later concentration. As for a concentration whose single object and single aspect is scattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who reflects upon the impermanence of material form, his cultivation not yet thoroughly ripened, and who, on this very basis, contemplates now its increase and decrease, broadly, all the way through to its wearing away —

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the very same sequence of images — livid, bloated, festering, reduced to bone — can count as either "scattered" or "unscattered" concentration depending solely on whether the practitioner's mind holds steady while moving through them is a genuinely subtle point: scatteredness, on this account, was never about which objects a mind visits, but about whether it visits them from a place of settled stability or restless drift.

3. Combining Object and Aspect

The treatise completes its account of the fifth and sixth categories in the twelvefold grid: a practitioner reflecting on the impermanence of material form specifically, rather than impermanence in the abstract, whose mind either scatters across increase, decrease, alteration, and decay, or holds steady through that same sequence once his cultivation has ripened.

Chinese Source

其心散亂流蕩不住。不專一境。由此因緣前定退失後定不進。

Ru-Yi Translation

his mind scattered, drifting, unfixed, lacking singleness of object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is lost and his later concentration fails to advance.

有三摩地一所緣一行相非散亂者。如有一類。思惟色非常。修已純熟。復即於此由此或觀增減。廣說乃至或觀磨滅。心不散亂。不流不蕩安住守一。由此因緣前定不失能進後定。

As for a concentration whose single object and single aspect is unscattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who reflects upon the impermanence of material form, his cultivation already thoroughly ripened, and who, on this very basis, contemplates, broadly, its increase and decrease all the way through to its wearing away — his mind unscattered, not drifting, not unfixed, abiding and holding fast to a single object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is not lost, and he is able to advance to a later concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Narrowing from impermanence in general to the impermanence of material form specifically shows the grid's own combinatorial logic in action: each new category isn't a fresh topic, but the same underlying distinction between scattered and settled attention, simply run through an ever-more-specific combination of what is being observed and how.

4. Many Objects: The Four Mindfulness-Foundations in Sequence

The treatise turns to concentration spanning many objects at once, illustrated through the four mindfulness-foundations taken in their canonical order — body, feeling, mind, and dharmas. A practitioner whose cultivation is not yet ripened finds his attention sliding uncontrollably as he moves from one foundation to the next; once ripened, that same progression through all four proceeds with his mind held steady and undistracted throughout.

Chinese Source

有三摩地多所緣是散亂者。如有一類。於身住循身觀。修未純熟。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for a concentration whose many objects are scattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who abides in the mindfulness-foundation of the body, following along in the contemplation of the body, his cultivation not yet thoroughly ripened,

復於受住循受觀。於心住循心觀。於法住循法觀。其心散亂流蕩不住。不專一境。由此因緣前定退失後定不進有三摩地多所緣非散亂者。如有一類。於身住循身觀。修已純熟。復於受住循受觀。乃至於法住循法觀。心不散亂。不流不蕩安住守一。由此因緣前定不失能進後定。

and who then abides in the mindfulness-foundation of feeling, following along in the contemplation of feeling; abides in the mindfulness-foundation of mind, following along in the contemplation of mind; and abides in the mindfulness-foundation of dharmas, following along in the contemplation of dharmas — his mind scattered, drifting, unfixed, lacking singleness of object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is lost and his later concentration fails to advance. As for a concentration whose many objects are unscattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who abides in the mindfulness-foundation of the body, following along in the contemplation of the body, his cultivation already thoroughly ripened, and who then abides in the mindfulness-foundation of feeling, following along in the contemplation of feeling, and so on, up through abiding in the mindfulness-foundation of dharmas, following along in the contemplation of dharmas — his mind unscattered, not drifting, not unfixed, abiding and holding fast to a single object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is not lost, and he is able to advance to a later concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Applying the scattered-versus-unscattered distinction to the four mindfulness-foundations themselves — the very backbone of the entire meditative path — makes an important point by implication: even the most canonically central sequence of practices can be performed in either a fragmented or a genuinely integrated way, and simply progressing through the correct list of objects in the correct order guarantees nothing about the quality of attention behind it.

5. Many Aspects: Impermanence, Suffering, Emptiness, No-Self

The treatise applies the same scattered-versus-unscattered distinction to a single object observed through many different meditative aspects in turn — impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and the absence of self, the four aspects traditionally applied to the truth of suffering itself. An unripened practitioner's mind drifts uncontrollably as he shifts between these four ways of seeing the very same thing; a ripened practitioner holds his attention steady throughout.

Chinese Source

有三摩地多行相是散亂者。如有一類。思惟非常。修未純熟。復或觀苦。或觀空。或觀非我。其心散亂流蕩不住。不專一境。由此因緣前定退失後定不進。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for a concentration whose many aspects are scattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who reflects upon impermanence, his cultivation not yet thoroughly ripened, and who then contemplates now suffering, now emptiness, now the absence of self — his mind scattered, drifting, unfixed, lacking singleness of object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is lost and his later concentration fails to advance.

有三摩地多行相非散亂者。如有一類。思惟非常。修已純熟。復或觀苦。或觀空或觀非我。心不散亂。不流不蕩安住守一。由此因緣前定不失能進後定。

As for a concentration whose many aspects are unscattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who reflects upon impermanence, his cultivation already thoroughly ripened, and who then contemplates now suffering, now emptiness, now the absence of self — his mind unscattered, not drifting, not unfixed, abiding and holding fast to a single object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is not lost, and he is able to advance to a later concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the four classic aspects of the truth of suffering — impermanence, suffering, emptiness, no-self — can themselves become an object of scattered, unsettled contemplation is a reminder that even the tradition's own most carefully constructed conceptual tools offer no automatic protection against a wandering mind: correct doctrine still has to be held by a genuinely stable attention, or it becomes just one more thing the mind bounces between.

6. Many Objects, Many Aspects at Once

The twelvefold grid reaches its final and most demanding combination: a practitioner who observes the body as impermanent, feeling as suffering, mind as empty, and dharmas as without self — four different objects, each paired with its own distinct meditative aspect, all within a single stretch of practice. As before, an unripened practitioner's attention scatters across this fourfold complexity, while a ripened practitioner holds it all together without losing his footing.

Chinese Source

有三摩地多所緣多行相是散亂者。如有一類。思惟身是非常。修未純熟復觀受是苦。心是空。法是非我。其心散亂流蕩不住。不專一境。由此因緣前定退失後定不進。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for a concentration whose many objects and many aspects are scattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who reflects that the body is impermanent, his cultivation not yet thoroughly ripened, and who then contemplates feeling as suffering, mind as empty, and dharmas as without self — his mind scattered, drifting, unfixed, lacking singleness of object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is lost and his later concentration fails to advance.

有三摩地多所緣多行相非散亂者。如有一類。思惟身是非常。修已純熟。復觀受是苦。心是空。法是非我。心不散亂。不流不蕩安住守一。由此因緣前定不失能進後定。

As for a concentration whose many objects and many aspects are unscattered: this refers to a certain kind of person who reflects that the body is impermanent, his cultivation already thoroughly ripened, and who then contemplates feeling as suffering, mind as empty, and dharmas as without self — his mind unscattered, not drifting, not unfixed, abiding and holding fast to a single object — and, on account of these causes and conditions, his earlier concentration is not lost, and he is able to advance to a later concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This final, most complex category — four objects and four aspects combined — is where the entire twelvefold framework was heading all along: the earlier, simpler categories were never separate topics so much as scaffolding, each one training the reader to see scatteredness and steadiness as a single continuous variable that can, in principle, apply no matter how many objects or aspects a practitioner's attention must eventually hold together at once.

7. What Is Ignorance? What Is Incorrect Awareness?

A precise psychological distinction opens: is ignorance simply identical to incorrect awareness, since both bear the mark of "failing to know"? The treatise defines ignorance as absence of knowledge with respect to the three realms, broad enough to include even the two kinds of ignorance that take cessation and the path — themselves outside the three realms — as their object, and defines incorrect awareness as wisdom drawn forth by what is contrary to reason.

Chinese Source

云何無明乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為令疑者得決定故。謂契經說不達不解不了知故。名為無明。不正知亦以不了知為相。或有生疑無明即是不正知性。是則二種體無差別。欲令彼疑得決定故。顯此二種其體各別故作斯論。云何無明。答三界無智。此說應理。謂三界繫無智具攝諸無明故。若作是說不知三界名無明者。則應不攝緣滅道諦二種無明。彼不緣三界故。云何不正知。答非理所引慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is ignorance? And so on, at length. One asks: why is this treatise composed? The answer: it is composed in order to bring those in doubt to a settled resolution — that is, scripture states that, on account of failing to penetrate, failing to comprehend, and failing to know, this is called ignorance; incorrect awareness, too, bears the mark of failing to know, and there are those who thereby give rise to the doubt that ignorance is simply identical in substance to incorrect awareness, these two possessing no genuine distinction between them — and it is in order to bring this doubt to a settled resolution, revealing that these two possess genuinely distinct substances, that this treatise is composed. What is ignorance? The answer: absence of knowledge with respect to the three realms — this reading accords with correct principle, since absence of knowledge bound to the three realms fully subsumes every kind of ignorance. Were one instead to say that ignorance is named simply for failing to know the three realms, then this would fail to subsume the two kinds of ignorance that take cessation and the path as their object, since these do not take the three realms as their object at all. What is incorrect awareness? The answer: wisdom drawn forth by what is contrary to reason.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Insisting that ignorance must be defined broadly enough to include ignorance of cessation and the path, even though neither belongs to the three realms, is a small but telling piece of definitional discipline: a definition of ignorance confined only to what is properly "in" the three realms would perversely have implied that not knowing nirvāṇa itself somehow fails to count as ignorance at all.

8. A Formal Debate: Can Someone Speak Falsely with "Correct" Awareness?

An intricate, formally structured debate unfolds between the proponent of correct principle and the Vibhajyavādin over whether incorrect awareness spans both defiled and undefined minds, or belongs solely to the defiled — turning on a genuinely puzzling edge case: if every deceptive utterance necessarily arises from incorrect awareness, how can scripture also speak of "speaking falsely with correct awareness"? Both sides trade the very same dilemma back and forth, each accusing the other of being forced, on pain of contradiction, either to deny that correct-awareness falsehood ever occurs or to abandon their own definition of incorrect awareness.

Chinese Source

問此中何故問少答多。謂不正知唯染污慧。非理所引慧。通染及不染云何知然。如業蘊說。諸意惡行皆是非理所引意業。有是非理所引意業非意惡行。謂一切有覆無記意業。及一分無覆無記意業。故知非理所引慧名通染不染。答此中非理所引慧者。應知唯攝諸染污慧。所以者何。非理所引略有二種。一者世俗。二者勝義。今說勝義非理所引。故知唯攝諸染污慧。唯染污法名為勝義非理所引。無覆無記但由世俗得彼名故此後應理論者與分別論者。相對問答難通顯不正知。雖是非理所引慧攝。然有正知而妄語義。汝說不正知是非理所引慧耶者。是分別論者問。重定前宗。若不定他宗說他過失則不應理答如是者是應理論者答。謂前所立理無顛倒故言如是。汝何所欲諸有正知而妄語者。彼皆失念不正知故而妄語耶者。亦是分別論者問。舉有妄語復審所宗。答如是者亦是應理論者答。謂彼所說稱可所宗故言如是。又何所欲無有正知而妄語耶者。是分別論者將欲設難反定所宗顯違正理。答不爾者是應理論者。遮彼所問顯理無違。謂雖不正知是非理所引慧。然有正知而妄語義非無此義故言不爾。應聽我說。若言不正知是非理所引慧。諸有正知而妄語。彼皆失念不正知故而妄語者則應說無有正知而妄語。若不說無有正知而妄語者。則不應言不正知是非理所引慧。諸有正知而妄語。彼皆失念不正知故而妄語。作如是說俱不應理者。是分別論者前後兩關翻覆設難。前關顯順宗違理。後關顯順理違宗。二俱不可故總結言作如是說俱不應理。彼難意言。若不正知即是非理所引慧者。諸虛誑語皆是非理所引慧。發此語即從不正知起。應說無有正知妄語。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: why does this passage's question narrow while its answer broadens? That is, incorrect awareness is solely a defiled wisdom, yet "wisdom drawn forth by what is contrary to reason" spans both the defiled and the undefiled — how is this known to be so? As the Karma Aggregate states: every mental misdeed is mental karma drawn forth by what is contrary to reason; yet there exists mental karma drawn forth by what is contrary to reason that is not a mental misdeed — namely, every covered-undefined mental karma, and a portion of uncovered-undefined mental karma — and it is thereby known that "wisdom drawn forth by what is contrary to reason" spans both the defiled and the undefiled. The answer: within this passage, "wisdom drawn forth by what is contrary to reason" should be understood to subsume solely defiled wisdom. Why is this so? Being drawn forth by what is contrary to reason is, in brief, of two kinds: first, in the conventional sense; second, in the ultimate sense. This passage speaks of what is contrary to reason in the ultimate sense, and it is thereby known that it subsumes solely defiled wisdom, since it is solely defiled dharmas that are named "contrary to reason" in the ultimate sense — the uncovered-undefined bears this name only in a conventional sense. What follows now is the manner in which the proponent of correct principle debates with the Vibhajyavādin, through question and answer and refutation and reconciliation, to establish at length the nature of incorrect awareness. Though incorrect awareness is indeed subsumed within wisdom drawn forth by what is contrary to reason, there nonetheless exists a sense in which one possesses correct awareness and yet still speaks falsely — "you say that incorrect awareness is subsumed within wisdom drawn forth by what is contrary to reason?" — this is the question posed by the Vibhajyavādin, fixing the opposing tenet with precision, since, without first fixing precisely what a rival school actually holds, to then state that school's own faults would be improper. "The answer: yes" is the reply of the proponent of correct principle, since the tenet just established involves no distortion. "What do you intend — do those who possess correct awareness and yet speak falsely all, in every case, do so because they have lost mindfulness through incorrect awareness?" — this, too, is the Vibhajyavādin's own question, raising the case of false speech in order to press once more upon his own tenet. "The answer: yes" is likewise the reply of the proponent of correct principle, since what has been said accords with the very tenet in question. "And do you further intend that there exists no case of possessing correct awareness and yet speaking falsely?" — this is the Vibhajyavādin, about to mount a challenge, fixing the opposing tenet once more in order to display its contradiction with correct principle. "The answer: no" is the proponent of correct principle, blocking this very question and showing that no contradiction in principle follows — that is, although incorrect awareness is indeed subsumed within wisdom drawn forth by what is contrary to reason, there nonetheless exists a genuine sense in which one possesses correct awareness and yet still speaks falsely, and this meaning is not thereby negated, and it is for this reason that the answer is "no." "Hear now what I have to say: if incorrect awareness is subsumed within wisdom drawn forth by what is contrary to reason, and if all those who possess correct awareness and yet speak falsely do so, in every case, because they have lost mindfulness through incorrect awareness, then it ought to follow that there exists no case of possessing correct awareness and yet speaking falsely; and if you do not hold that there exists no case of possessing correct awareness and yet speaking falsely, then you ought not to say that incorrect awareness is subsumed within wisdom drawn forth by what is contrary to reason, and that all those who possess correct awareness and yet speak falsely do so, in every case, because they have lost mindfulness through incorrect awareness — to maintain both of these together is, in either direction, improper" — this is the Vibhajyavādin, mounting his challenge by turning the two horns of the dilemma back and forth against one another: the first horn shows that the position, in according with its own tenet, contradicts principle; the second horn shows that, in according with principle, it contradicts its own tenet — since neither can be sustained, he concludes by summarizing that to maintain both together is, in either direction, improper. 9. The Resolution, and a New Pair: Arrogance and Pride

The debate resolves through a careful distinction: a deceptive utterance can indeed arise from incorrect awareness while still being properly called "speaking falsely with correct awareness," since the speaker possesses accurate awareness of what he actually saw, felt, or believed, and distorts only his outward report of it — awareness of the fact and honesty about the fact are simply two different things. The treatise then opens an entirely new pair of terms: arrogance, the self-indulgent intoxication with one's own qualities that needs no comparison to another at all, and pride, which specifically elevates the self by comparison with someone else.

10. Aniruddha's Own Solitary Pride, and Five Wrong Views About What Pride Takes as Its Object

The treatise works out arrogance's precise technical profile against pride, then confronts a genuine puzzle: pride is defined as arising through comparison with another, yet certain kinds of pride — bound to the formless realm, or abandoned by seeing — seem to arise with no such comparison at all. The resolution comes through a memorable scriptural anecdote: the Venerable Aniruddha boasts to Śāriputra of his own surpassing heavenly eye, and Śāriputra bluntly names this pride even though Aniruddha compared himself to no one at all — proving that pride, through the sheer force of habitual repetition, can manifest purely within one's own continuum. The treatise then refutes five separate mistaken views about pride's own object, opening an extended new topic on "overreaching pride," the false conviction of having attained realizations one has not actually reached.

11. Overreaching Pride at the Path of Seeing: "I Have Seen Suffering as Suffering"

The treatise details exactly how overreaching pride arises at the threshold of the path of seeing itself: a diligent practitioner, having drawn near a good spiritual friend, heard the true Dharma, and attained a genuine forbearance in accordance with the truths, temporarily suppresses his own view and doubt through the sheer power of that forbearance's own attention — and, failing to notice that this suppression is merely temporary rather than permanent, mistakes his own contaminated forbearance for the Dharma's genuine, uncontaminated seeing.

12. Five Reasons an Affliction Can Go Quiet Without Being Cut Off

13. Overreaching Pride Regarding Cessation and the Path, and Its Own Uncertain Realm

The same mechanism recurs for the higher two truths: a practitioner who has suppressed his view and doubt through forbearance regarding cessation and the path likewise mistakes this temporary quiet for genuine attainment — though here the pride can only take the accompanying mental factors of that very forbearance as its object, since cessation and the path themselves are too tranquil and still to serve as an object for something as agitated as pride. A genuine puzzle then opens about which realm this pride belongs to, since the desire realm technically lacks the very forbearance this pride is supposed to be mistaken for — resolved by noting that the desire realm does possess a resembling wholesome root capable of standing in for it.

There are those who say: the desire realm possesses all seven in full; the two higher realms possess only six, excluding self-deprecating pride, since those realms possess no comparison of family lineage and the like. The verdict: the form and formless realms, too, possess all seven kinds of pride in full. One asks: how, then, without any comparison of family lineage and the like, can self-deprecating pride exist there? The answer: although those realms possess no comparison of family lineage and the like, they do possess a comparative measure of meditative attainments and the like. Further, one first gives rise to this pride with reference to another while still in the desire realm, and, through the force of habitual repetition, later, reborn in the higher realms, this same pride is drawn forth to manifest there as well. There are those who say: although one, reborn in the higher realms, does not give rise to self-deprecating pride there, one nonetheless gives rise to that very pride while still in the desire realm — that is, two beings who have both attained a concentration of the higher realms, questioning one another back and forth about the marks of the concentration each has attained, thereby come, through this very comparison, to give rise to self-deprecating pride. The verdict: it should be said that not every kind of pride, including self-deprecating pride, necessarily arises through comparison with another — from beginningless time, through the force of habitual repetition, it manifests even though one is reborn in the higher realms, and it is for this reason that all three realms alike possess all seven kinds of pride in full.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That this taxonomy needed seven distinct categories, rather than a single undifferentiated notion of "pride," to do justice to the full range of ways a mind can inflate or distort its own standing — comparing up, comparing down, comparing to nothing at all, comparing to a fiction — suggests something important about how thoroughly this psychological territory has been mapped: pride was never a single vice for this tradition, but an entire family of related but genuinely distinct failures of accurate self-assessment.
