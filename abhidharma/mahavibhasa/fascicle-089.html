What Mind Gives Rise to Mind

Fascicle 89 completes the sixteen-consciousness classification through the four truths, dhyānas, immeasurables, and the ninety-eight latent tendencies, then opens the immediately-equal antecedent condition — which of the sixteen kinds of mind can immediately give rise to which others, faculty by faculty. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 89 complete, 15 sections Source: Taishō T1545, vol. 27, fascicle 89 Topic: 十門納息 → 緣識緣緣識 → 等無間緣 (consciousness taking consciousness as object, concluded; the immediately-equal antecedent condition, opened), Fetters Aggregate

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 89 continues the Ten Gates Nyāsa (結蘊第二中十門納息第四之十九), completing the sixteen-fold consciousness classification (十六識) opened in fascicle 87, then opens a new topic: the immediately-equal antecedent condition (等無間緣, samanantara-pratyaya) — among the fifteen contaminated categories of mind across the three realms, plus the undefiled, how many minds does each give rise to as its own immediate successor. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right.

1. The Four Truths, the Four Dhyānas, and the Immeasurables

The sixteen-consciousness sweep continues through the truths of suffering and accumulation (following the pattern already set for the grasping-aggregates), the truths of cessation and the path (following the unconditioned and the three undefiled faculties respectively), and the four dhyānas — pervading both the contaminated and the undefiled, taken as object by twelve consciousnesses directly and fourteen as an object-of-that-object. The three immeasurables of loving-kindness, compassion, and equanimity, together with the pure liberation, the latter four masteries, and the first eight totalities, follow with their own eight-consciousness count.

2. Sympathetic Joy and the Formless Attainments

The immeasurable of sympathetic joy, confined to the first two dhyānas together with the first two liberations and first four masteries, is worked through with its own seven-consciousness direct and ten-consciousness indirect count. The first three formless attainments, pervading both the contaminated and the undefiled across the formless realm, follow with thirteen consciousnesses reaching them both directly and indirectly — the fascicle noting explicitly that the counts for direct object and object-of-that-object happen to coincide here, unlike almost everywhere else in this sweep.

3. The Sphere of Neither-Perception-Nor-Non-Perception, and the Liberations of Space, Consciousness, and Nothingness

The sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, being solely contaminated, is reached by twelve consciousnesses directly and thirteen indirectly. The liberations of infinite space, infinite consciousness, and nothingness — pervading both the contaminated (the effort-attained wholesome alone) and the undefiled (class-knowledge alone) — are reached by twelve consciousnesses in both directions.

10. Why Stop at the Second Remove?

A closing meta-question interrupts the sweep to ask why this entire analysis speaks only of a direct object and a single further object-of-that-object, never pressing on to a third or fourth remove. Four converging reasons are given: to avoid an endless regress: to match the parallel restriction already observed for the immediately-equal antecedent condition, which likewise is traced only to its second remove; because the latent tendencies increasing at the third remove and beyond turn out, in the great majority of cases, simply to duplicate those already established at the second; and because Abhidharma method itself deliberately shows only enough of a pattern to let a genuinely wise reader extend it on his own.

11. A New Topic: The Immediately-Equal Antecedent Condition

An entirely new investigation opens, closing out the fascicle: among the three realms' own fifteen categories of mind, how many minds does each give rise to as its own immediately-equal antecedent condition — the causal relation by which one moment of mind gives way, without any gap, to the very next? The reason given is again polemical, against the Dārṣṭāntikas, who held that mind can only ever hand off to mind, and a mental concomitant only ever to another mental concomitant, never crossing between the two categories. Against this, the treatise sets out to show that mind and its concomitants genuinely do serve as one another's immediately-equal antecedent condition in every direction — and, as a broader table lays out, each of the sixteen kinds of mind (the fifteen contaminated plus the undefiled) arises from and gives rise to a precisely fixed number of the others, a defiled mind of a lower ground never able to give rise to one of a higher ground, nor to an undefiled mind at all.

12. The Faculties' Own Succession, Ground by Ground

The mind faculty's own succession is worked out in exhaustive, situation-specific detail — six kinds of mind if one has not yet become free of the desire realm's own defilement, ten if partway free, eleven if fully free of the desire realm but not the form realm, and so on up to fifteen once fully free of both — each transition anchored to a concrete scenario: retrogression, a continued rebirth, or entering absorption. 13. Suffering, Sorrow, Joy, and the Faculties of the Path

The suffering- and sorrow-faculties, confined to the desire realm's own five categories, cannot give rise to a mind of absorption at all, being utterly contrary to it. The joy-faculty, spanning the desire and form realms, gives rise to ten kinds of mind with its own careful cross-realm restrictions — desire-realm joy cannot give rise to form-realm joy, nor the reverse, since joy draws forth solely its own ground's own afflictions, though a minority opinion allows form-realm joy to give rise to desire-realm wholesome and neutral states by way of the equanimity-faculty's own broader reach. Most strikingly, the "faculty of will come to know what is not yet known" — the very faculty of the path of seeing itself — gives rise to no mind whatsoever, since the path of seeing is "signless," incapable of serving as an immediately-equal antecedent condition for any contaminated mind at all; the "already-known" and "fully-known" faculties, by contrast, give rise to three kinds of mind, worked through in careful detail for exactly which mind a practitioner emerges into upon attaining each of the four fruits, depending on which ground he relies upon.

14. The Sense-Consciousnesses, the Undefiled, and the Puzzle of a Past Dharma

Further, this treatise here speaks solely of what emerges as a wholesome mind; the mind that emerges from the ripened fruit of non-perception may be wholesome, defiled, or neutral-and-unobscured, and it is for this reason that it is not stated here. There are those who say: the mind that emerges from the ripened fruit of non-perception is solely abandoned by cultivation. One asks: if so, how is one to reconcile this with what the Faculties Aggregate states? The answer: that passage ought to be understood as stating that, with respect to that perception, the form realm's own universally pervasive latent tendency increases — yet, in order to exclude the undefiled-object case, it speaks of the contaminated-object latent tendency's own increase. The verdict: of these two positions, the former is the sound one. One asks: for what reason does that perception not have the undefiled-object latent tendency increase with respect to it? The answer: that person grasps non-perception itself as nirvāṇa, and grasps the attainment of non-perception itself as the noble path, and it is for this reason that, at that very moment, he does not give rise to a wrong view denying cessation and the path;

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The extended debate over the mind emerging from non-perception's own ripened fruit is a rare moment where this fascicle shows its own working, offering four distinct attempted reconciliations with a rival passage before settling on one — a reminder that even within a tradition as systematized as this, cross-references between different aggregates of the same treatise do not always align perfectly on the first reading, and resolving the apparent tension is treated as a genuine task worth several full attempts rather than a problem to be waved away.

15. The Remaining Attainments, Closing the Fascicle

The fascicle closes with a final run through the four immeasurables, the first two liberations, and the first four masteries (each giving rise to six kinds of mind, confined by their own desire-realm object from ever reaching the formless realm), the fourth and fifth liberations and the knowledge of another's mind (also six), the third liberation, the latter four masteries, and the first eight totalities (five, since these are all a discrete, particularized contemplation), the latter two totalities (five, being instead a contemplation upon an imagined image), the eighth liberation — the cessation of perception and feeling itself — giving rise to no mind whatsoever, despite being immediately followed by mind, since it does not itself constitute mind's own immediately-equal antecedent condition, and, finally, dharma-knowledge and the higher-binding fetters of craving for form and its four companions, closing with the compilers' own brief note that the remaining text needs no further analysis.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The eighth liberation's own peculiar status — genuinely followed by mind, yet not itself mind's own immediately-equal antecedent condition for it — captures something subtle about how this tradition understands the cessation of perception and feeling: the causal relation of "immediately preceding" and the specific technical relation of "immediately-equal antecedent condition" are not simply the same thing wearing two names, and this fascicle's closing lines insist on that distinction precisely where it matters most, at the very boundary between mind and its own temporary, deliberate cessation.
