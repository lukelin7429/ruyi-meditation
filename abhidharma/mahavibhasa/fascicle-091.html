The Measureless Gateways, Worked Through

Fascicle 91 takes up several of the measureless further permutations previewed in fascicle 90's own combinatorial coda — latent-tendency increase, the four bonds, the immediately-equal antecedent condition, directed thought and the five feelings, and possession — each applied to a faculty's own cessation and its own path of cutting it off. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 91 complete, 6 sections Source: Taishō T1545, vol. 27, fascicle 91 Topic: 十門納息 → 滅 → 斷道 (the ten gateways applied to cessation and the path of cutting-off), Fetters Aggregate

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 91 continues the Ten Gates Nyāsa (結蘊第二中十門納息第四之二十一), applying several of the ten gateways already established in fascicles 77 through 90 — latent-tendency increase, the four bonds (所緣縛/相應縛), the immediately-equal antecedent condition (等無間緣), directed thought and sustained reflection, the five feelings, and possession (成就) — to two new modifiers: a faculty's own cessation (滅) and its own path of cutting it off (斷道). Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Latent-Tendency Increase: Cessation and the Path of Cutting-Off

Taking up the very first permutation previewed in fascicle 90's own combinatorial coda, the treatise now works through how many latent tendencies increase with respect to each faculty once that faculty's own cessation, or its own path of cutting it off, is taken as object — both directly and as a further object-of-that-object. The eye faculty's own cessation, taken directly, is increased by the desire and form realms' own two categories together with the universally pervasive latent tendencies; its own path of cutting-off, by contrast, is increased uniformly by the three realms' own four categories, since a path capable of cutting off affliction is itself a broader, more encompassing object than the bare fact of a affliction's own ending.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a faculty's own cessation and its own path of cutting it off are increased by such different counts of latent tendency — the cessation narrowly, the path broadly across four whole categories — makes a genuinely important point about what each of these two objects actually is: cessation is a single, achieved endpoint, while the path is the entire sustained activity of getting there, and it is only reasonable that the broader, ongoing activity should attract a correspondingly broader range of affliction able to take it as an object.

2. The Four Bonds, Applied to Direct Object and Object-of-That-Object

The treatise now works through the second previewed permutation: for the eye faculty's own direct-object and object-of-that-object latent tendencies, exactly which further latent tendencies constitute a bond of an object, a bond of association, both, or neither. The pattern, worked out at exhausting length for the eye, female, life, mind, pleasure, joy, sorrow, and five faculties of faith in turn, consistently locates the fourth category — neither bond at all — only among latent tendencies of an entirely different realm, ground, or object, while every latent tendency sharing the same category as its own associated dharma achieves both bonds together.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Running this same four-way tetralemma across eight different faculties one after another, each faculty's own answer shifting only in which specific realms and categories fill the same four slots, is itself a demonstration of principle over memorization: a reader who has genuinely absorbed the underlying rule — a shared category and object yields both bonds, a differing realm or ground yields at most one, an entirely disjoint object yields neither — needs no new insight to work out the female faculty's own answer once the eye faculty's has been mastered, only patience to apply the same rule to a new set of realms and grounds.

3. The Four Bonds, Applied to Cessation

The same four-way tetralemma is now applied to each faculty's own cessation rather than the faculty itself — a modifier that systematically narrows which latent tendencies qualify, since a cessation, being itself the very endpoint of affliction, naturally excludes far more of the ninety-eight latent tendencies from constituting any bond with it at all than the faculty's own ordinary, ongoing presence would.

6. Who Possesses These, Applied to Direct Presence and Cessation

The final previewed gateway examined here is possession itself, applied first to each faculty's own direct presence and object-of-that-object, then to its own cessation — the eye faculty, taken directly, possessed by anyone within the sphere of infinite space and below or any noble one reborn above it, but not by an ordinary being reborn there; taken as an object-of-that-object, possessed by every sentient being without exception; and, taken as its own cessation, possessed by anyone within the desire and form realms or any noble one reborn in the formless realm, since a faculty's own cessation, unlike the faculty itself, requires already having progressed some distance along the path to have brought that cessation about at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a faculty's own object-of-that-object is possessed by literally every sentient being without exception, while the faculty's own direct presence and its own cessation are each possessed only by some, captures something genuinely illuminating about what an object-of-that-object actually is: it is possessed universally precisely because it names not the faculty in question but the mere fact that some consciousness or other is capable of reflecting upon a consciousness that takes the faculty as object — a capacity every sentient being's own mind possesses, regardless of what that being has or has not yet attained.
