Susīma's Own Story

Fascicle 110 closes the sequence of associative tetralemmas begun in fascicle 109, works through the forty-four and seventy-seven matters of knowledge built from the twelve links of dependent origination, and closes with the full, celebrated narrative of the Susīma Sūtra. 1. Accumulation, Cessation, and the Path Against the Concentrations

Fascicle 110 continues the associative tetralemmas begun in fascicle 109, now testing the knowledge of accumulation against the wishlessness concentration, the knowledge of cessation against signlessness, and the knowledge of the path against emptiness and wishlessness in turn — each following the identical four-branched method already established, and each closing by noting that the same treatment already given to dharma-cognition against the three undefiled faculties, the limbs of enlightenment, and the limbs of the path applies here without further modification.

Suppose the dharmas associated with the knowledge of cessation — are these associated with the knowledge of accumulation? The answer: this, too, is not so. The same holds with respect to the knowledge of the path, and the emptiness and signless concentrations, since they do not belong to the same mind-moment, and their aspects differ, or their object differs. This is associated with the knowledge of accumulation, not with wishlessness, since an inherent nature is not associated with its own inherent nature. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The recurring closing formula — "this should be understood exactly as stated for dharma-cognition" — is doing real structural work across this fascicle: rather than treating each of the eight kinds of knowledge as requiring its own from-scratch analysis against every faculty, limb of enlightenment, and limb of the path, the treatise establishes the method once, thoroughly, for dharma-cognition, and then simply confirms that the identical pattern of associations recurs for accumulation, cessation, and the path, letting a single worked example license an entire family of parallel cases.

2. Emptiness Against the Faculties and Limbs of Enlightenment

The tetralemma technique turns to the emptiness concentration itself, tested against the three undefiled faculties and then against the limbs of enlightenment one by one — mindfulness, joy, concentration — with a genuinely delicate complication for the joy limb: since the emptiness concentration exists across grounds that include and exclude joy in different measures (present in the first two dhyānas, absent from the not-yet-arrived concentration, the intermediate dhyāna, the latter two dhyānas, and the formless attainments), the tetralemma's own branches must be qualified by which specific ground is in view.

6. Forty-Four Matters of Knowledge, and Why Ignorance Alone Is Left Out

A substantial new topic opens with the scripture on the forty-four matters of knowledge: for each of eleven links of dependent origination (excluding ignorance alone) — old-age-and-death, birth, existence, clinging, craving, sensation, contact, the six sense-fields, name-and-form, consciousness, and formations — four kinds of knowledge are given: knowing the link itself, its arising, its cessation, and the path proceeding toward its cessation, for a total of forty-four. The Buddha's own reason for teaching this, the treatise explains, mirrors an elder who, having gathered wealth through a particular skillful means, teaches that same means to his own sons and grandsons in turn — before a careful puzzle explains why ignorance, though itself one of the twelve links, is the sole link excluded from the forty-four matters: unlike the other eleven, ignorance neither takes any link as its own cause nor serves as any link's own fruit, and, since only three (not four) kinds of knowledge can be given rise to in reliance upon it, it fails to fit the fourfold pattern that generates the other forty-four.

13. Susīma's Own Story

The fascicle closes with one of the tradition's most celebrated narratives: the Susīma Sūtra in full. Outside sectarians, their own fame and support eclipsed since the Buddha's rise, conspire to send Susīma to infiltrate the sangha and steal its teachings — but he becomes bewildered upon hearing five hundred arhats declare themselves wisdom-liberated without reliance on any dhyāna whatsoever, and returns to the Buddha, who tells him only that the knowledge of the abiding of dharmas comes first, the knowledge of nirvāṇa afterward, a saying Susīma himself never comes to understand. The treatise's own closing verdict resolves the puzzle those five hundred arhats embodied: it is knowledge belonging to the approach ground — the not-yet-arrived concentration alone, without any of the four fundamental dhyānas — that is the knowledge of the abiding of dharmas, while knowledge belonging to the fundamental dhyānas themselves is the knowledge of nirvāṇa, precisely because those same five hundred arhats attained the exhaustion of the outflows relying on the not-yet-arrived concentration alone, only afterward becoming capable of giving rise to the fundamental attainments at all.

The Bhagavān said to him: Susīma, you should know, there first exists the knowledge of the abiding of dharmas, and afterward there exists the knowledge of nirvāṇa. The Buddha said: whether you know it or do not know it, the dharma is, nonetheless, necessarily so. At that time, Susīma did not accomplish his original intention; yet those same five hundred worthy ones, relying upon the not-yet-arrived concentration, having attained the exhaustion of the outflows, only afterward became capable of giving rise to the fundamental attainments. It is by virtue of this that it is known that the knowledge belonging to the approach ground is the knowledge of the abiding of dharmas; the knowledge belonging to the fundamental ground is the knowledge of nirvāṇa.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Susīma's own bewilderment is left, quite deliberately, unresolved within the narrative itself — the Buddha tells him plainly "whether you know it or do not know it, the dharma is, nonetheless, necessarily so," and Susīma never does return to complete his original, deceptive errand. The story's own refusal to grant Susīma the neat explanatory payoff a reader might expect is itself part of its teaching: some genuine spiritual attainments — wisdom-liberation without a single dhyāna to its name — remain fully real and fully valid whether or not the person confronted with them, even a would-be scholar sent to catalogue the tradition's own secrets, ever manages to make full sense of them.
