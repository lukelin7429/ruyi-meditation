The Sick Man's Staff

Fascicle 22 opens an entirely new nyāsa within the Knowledge Nyāsa's fourteenth part (智納息第二之十四), asking what it truly means for a mind to be called "possessing latent tendency" — refuting three rival schools before building an exhaustive two-factor grid of increase and companionship, mapped across every named affliction in all five sections. The fascicle closes by working out precisely how a latent tendency is cut off, illustrated by a sick man collapsing the instant his staff is removed, and by an infant, too young to distinguish sensual objects, still bound by the latent tendency of craving. Fascicle 22 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa opened in Fascicle 9, now in its fourteenth part (之十四), opening an entirely new topic on minds possessing latent tendency. Terms established in earlier fascicles, such as the five sections abandoned by seeing suffering, the origin, cessation, the path, and by cultivation, and the universally-pervasive latent tendency, recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. A New Nyāsa Opens: Refuting Three Rivals on What 'Possessing Latent Tendency' Means

Fascicle 22 opens an entirely new nyāsa, explicitly to refute three rival positions on what it means for a mind to be called “possessing latent tendency.” The single-continuous-mind theorists hold that mind never actually changes its own nature between having and lacking affliction, comparing the noble path's own action to washing a garment or polishing a mirror — opposing the dirt, never the garment itself. The Dārṣṭāntikas deny that a latent tendency truly “increases” with respect to either its object or its associated dharmas at all, since either relation, taken literally, would seem to require universal or perpetual increase. The Vātsīputrīyas locate “possessing latent tendency” in the person (pudgala) itself rather than in mind, since it is the person, not any momentary mental state, that is bound or freed.

Chinese Source

諸心由隨眠故名有隨眠心。彼隨眠於此心隨增耶。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答為止他宗顯正理故。謂或有執。但有一心如說一心相續論者彼作是說。有隨眠心無隨眠心。其性不異聖道現前。與煩惱相違不違心性。為對治煩惱非對治心。如浣衣磨鏡鍊金等物。與垢等相違不違衣等。聖道亦爾。又此身中若聖道未現在前。煩惱未斷故心有隨眠。聖道現前煩惱斷故心無隨眠。此心雖有隨眠無隨眠時異。而性是一。如衣鏡金等未浣磨鍊等時。名有垢衣等。若浣磨鍊等已。名無垢衣等。有無垢等時雖有異。而性無別。心亦如是。為止彼執顯有隨眠心與無隨眠心其性。各異。或復有執。隨眠不於所緣隨增。亦不於相應法有隨增義。如譬喻者彼作是說。若隨眠於所緣隨增者於他界地及無漏法亦應隨增。是所緣故如自界地。若於相應法有隨增義者。則應未斷已斷一切時隨增。相應畢竟不相離故猶如自性。為止彼執。顯諸隨眠於所緣相應俱有隨增義。謂於自界地有漏法及相應法乃至未斷恒隨增故。或復有執。隨眠唯於補特伽羅有隨增義。如犢子部彼作是說。補特伽羅名有隨眠及無隨眠。非心等法。補特伽羅有縛解故。為止彼執顯唯心等有縛有解名有隨眠及無隨眠。非數取趣畢竟無故。為止此等他宗及顯無顛倒理故作斯論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Minds are called "possessing latent tendency" due to a latent tendency — does that latent tendency increase within this mind? And so on, as stated at length. One asks: why does the treatise take up this topic? The answer: in order to refute a rival school and manifest the correct principle. That is, some maintain that there is only a single mind, just as the theorists of a single continuous mind hold, who say: a mind possessing latent tendency and a mind lacking latent tendency do not differ in their own nature — when the noble path manifests, it opposes the afflictions, not the very nature of mind, just as the washing of a garment, the polishing of a mirror, or the refining of gold opposes the dirt, tarnish, or impurity, not the very nature of the garment, mirror, or gold itself; the noble path is exactly the same. Further, within this very body, before the noble path has manifested, the afflictions not yet cut off, the mind possesses latent tendency; once the noble path manifests, the afflictions cut off, the mind lacks latent tendency — though this mind differs at the time of possessing and of lacking latent tendency, its nature remains one and the same, just as a garment, mirror, or piece of gold, before being washed, polished, or refined, is called a soiled garment and the rest, and, once washed, polished, or refined, is called an unsoiled garment and the rest — though differing at the time of being soiled and unsoiled, its nature is no different; mind is exactly the same. In order to refute this position, the treatise shows that a mind possessing latent tendency and a mind lacking latent tendency each differ in their own nature. Others maintain that a latent tendency does not increase with respect to its object, nor does it possess the meaning of increasing with respect to its associated dharmas, just as the Dārṣṭāntikas hold, who say: if a latent tendency increased with respect to its object, it ought to increase as well with respect to another realm, another stage, and uncontaminated dharmas, since these too are equally its object, just as its own realm and stage are; if it possessed the meaning of increasing with respect to its associated dharmas, it ought to increase at every time, whether cut off or not, since association can never truly be separated, exactly like self-nature itself. In order to refute this position, the treatise shows that latent tendencies do possess the meaning of increasing with respect to both their object and their associated and co-existent dharmas — that is, with respect to contaminated dharmas and associated dharmas of their own realm and stage, so long as not yet cut off, they constantly increase. Others maintain that a latent tendency possesses the meaning of increasing only with respect to the person, just as the Vātsīputrīyas hold, who say: it is the person that is called possessing or lacking latent tendency, not mind and the other dharmas, since it is the person that is bound and that is freed. In order to refute this position, the treatise shows that it is only mind and the other dharmas that are bound and freed, and that are called possessing or lacking latent tendency — not any substantially existent person, since no such person ultimately exists at all. It is in order to refute these various rival schools, and to manifest the undistorted principle, that this treatise is composed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Refuting the Vātsīputrīya position here carries special weight beyond this single technical question: the entire investigation that follows — an elaborate, genuinely exhausting combinatorial mapping of exactly which latent tendency belongs to which mind — only makes sense as a worthwhile project if it is mind and mental dharmas themselves, not some further substantial self, that actually possess and are freed from affliction. This opening refutation of the pudgala theory is what licenses treating “does this mind possess this latent tendency” as a genuinely precise, decidable technical question in the first place, rather than a mere shorthand for facts about some further, irreducible person.

2. Two Factors Decide 'Possessing Latent Tendency': Increasing and Being a Companion

The treatise's own working definition arrives: a latent tendency increases within a mind when it is associated with that mind, not yet cut off, and takes that mind as its object; it does not increase once cut off, even while still associated. The minds under discussion span all five sections — those abandoned by seeing suffering, the origin, cessation, the path, and by cultivation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice that the definition of “increasing” bundles together two conditions that the treatise will spend the rest of this fascicle carefully prying apart — being associated with a mind, and taking that mind as an object — treating them, at first, as though they simply co-occur. The entire remainder of the investigation exists precisely because these two relations, association and object-taking, turn out to behave very differently once a latent tendency has actually been cut off, a distinction this opening definition only hints at without yet drawing out.

3. The Two-Factor Grid: Full Companion Status Versus Mere Increase

The treatise names its two governing factors explicitly: the factor of increasing, and the factor of being a companion (that is, genuine mental association). A mind abandoned by seeing suffering possesses both factors with respect to its own section's latent tendency, only the increasing-factor with respect to the origin's own universally-pervasive latent tendency (which can reach across sections as an object without genuine association), and neither factor with respect to everything else — the identical pattern then repeating for minds abandoned by seeing the origin, cessation, the path, and cultivation, each further subdivided by whether the associated latent tendency is universally pervasive or not, contaminated-object-taking or uncontaminated-object-taking, or simply defiled or undefiled.

4. Naming Every Affliction: The Full Combinatorial Catalogue

The treatise now applies its two-factor grid exhaustively to every individually named affliction the desire realm's five sections contain — the ten kinds of mind abandoned by seeing suffering (five views, doubt, craving, hatred, conceit, and not-held-in-common ignorance), the seven kinds abandoned by seeing the origin, the seven abandoned by seeing cessation, the eight abandoned by seeing the path, and the five abandoned by cultivation — working out, for literally every single one, exactly which latent tendency it possesses through both factors, which it possesses through the increasing-factor alone, and which it fails to possess at all.

5. Why an Associated Latent Tendency Never Really Leaves, Even Once Cut Off

A genuinely important asymmetry closes out the combinatorial catalogue: once a latent tendency is cut off, a mind can still be called “possessing latent tendency” through what remains its associated latent tendency, but never through what was merely an object-taken one, since the associated latent tendency retains its companion-status forever while the object-taken one loses its only qualifying feature, mere increase, the instant it is cut off. 7. Do Past and Future Latent Tendencies Still Increase? The Infant and the Buddha's Own Words

A further refinement asks precisely how latent tendencies increase with respect to object and association, canvassing the Western masters' binding-versus-companion distinction, the Kashmiri masters' aspect-grasping-versus-fault-sharing distinction, and a rejected symmetric view refuted by a close reading of the Prakaraṇapāda's own craving-passage. Vasumitra then offers four vivid factors explaining increase itself — falling into evil intention like one wrongdoer corrupting an entire assembly, heating like water in a vessel set on hot iron, staining like smoke soiling a garment, and inviting censure like one offender disgracing an entire monastic community — before the discussion turns to whether even past and future latent tendencies, lacking any present function, can truly be said to increase, settled decisively by the Buddha's own words to the mother of Mālā-garland: an infant too young even to distinguish sensual objects is nonetheless bound by the latent tendency of sensual craving.

9. The Staff and the Falling Man: How a Dependent Latent Tendency Gets Cut Off

A genuinely difficult puzzle now tests the object-cutting-off principle against its hardest case: latent tendencies abandoned by seeing cessation and the path that take a contaminated object can never be “seen and cut off” the ordinary way, since their true object is never itself directly examined by the very insight that removes them. The treatise's resolution is elegant — the uncontaminated-object-taking latent tendency abandoned by seeing cessation and the path is cut off first, through direct insight into cessation and the path themselves, and the dependent, contaminated-object-taking latent tendency then falls together with it, exactly as a sick man leaning on a staff collapses the instant the staff is removed.
