The Rescuing Dharma

Fascicle 78 continues the treatise's treatment of the Four Noble Truths — the rescuing dharma and the brahmin Dṛḍhika, why the truths are named "noble," the eight forms of suffering, whether pleasure exists within the aggregates, an extensive catalogue of similes for why craving alone is the truth of accumulation, and why direct realization proceeds from suffering's own coarse characteristic to the path's subtlest. 1. Śāriputra's Statement, and Two Kinds of Arising

The fascicle opens with Śāriputra's own declaration that every wholesome dharma that arises is gathered within, and proceeds toward, the four noble truths — raising an immediate puzzle, since the unconditioned truth of cessation possesses no meaning of arising at all. The resolution distinguishes two senses of "arising": arising through simply possessing an own-nature (which analytical cessation, though never itself generated, still satisfies, since being obtained is itself a kind of arising) versus arising through arising from conditions (which only the three conditioned truths possess) — Pārśva adding that "proceeding toward" the truths simply means taking each as one's object, the forbearances and knowledges of suffering, accumulation, cessation, and the path each gathered within the truth of the path while taking its own corresponding truth as what it cognizes.

Chinese Source

如契經說。尊者舍利子作如是言。諸善法生皆四聖諦攝趣四聖諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

As a scripture states, the Venerable Śāriputra spoke as follows: every wholesome dharma that arises is gathered within the four noble truths, and proceeds toward the four noble truths.

問三諦有為說生可爾。滅諦無為既無生義。如何可說諸善法生攝在四諦。答此經意說諸善法生無不攝在四聖諦中。不言四諦一一皆攝所生善法。於理何違。復次生有二種。一有自性故名為生。二從緣起故名為生。有自性故名為生者。生言顯體非滅壞義。從緣起故名為生者。生言欲顯從緣起義。諸善法中具二生者三諦所攝。唯有自性故名生者滅諦所攝。故經所說亦不違理。復次生有二種。一作用生。二彼得生。諸善法中具二生者三諦所攝。唯彼得生者滅諦所攝。擇滅雖不生而得生故。脇尊者曰。此契經中說諸忍智名所生善。此諸忍智隨應攝在四聖諦中不言遍攝。言趣諦者是緣諦義。謂苦忍苦智道諦攝緣苦諦。集忍集智道諦攝緣集諦。滅忍滅智道諦攝緣滅諦。道忍道智道諦攝緣道諦。

One asks: granting that the three conditioned truths may be spoken of as arising, the truth of cessation, being unconditioned, possesses no meaning of arising at all — how, then, can it be said that every wholesome dharma that arises is gathered within the four truths? The answer: the intent of this scripture is that every wholesome dharma that arises is, without exception, gathered within the four noble truths; it does not say that each and every one of the four truths, individually, gathers in every wholesome dharma that arises. What fault, then, does this raise against principle? Further, arising is of two kinds: first, arising through possessing an own-nature; second, arising through arising from conditions. As for arising through possessing an own-nature: this word "arising" displays the substance itself, not the meaning of cessation or destruction. As for arising through arising from conditions: this word "arising" wishes to display the meaning of arising from conditions. Among wholesome dharmas, whatever possesses both these kinds of arising is gathered within the three conditioned truths; whatever possesses only arising through an own-nature is gathered within the truth of cessation, and it is for this reason that the scripture's own statement, too, raises no contradiction with principle. Further, arising is of two kinds: first, arising of operative function; second, arising of that which is obtained. Among wholesome dharmas, whatever possesses both these kinds of arising is gathered within the three conditioned truths; whatever possesses only the arising of that which is obtained is gathered within the truth of cessation, since analytical cessation, although it does not itself arise, is nonetheless something obtained, and this obtaining is itself a form of arising. The Venerable Pārśva says: this scripture speaks of the forbearances and the knowledges as "what is generated" as wholesome — these forbearances and knowledges are, each as is fitting, gathered within the four noble truths, though not said to be comprehensively gathered within all of them. As for speaking of "proceeding toward the truths": this refers to the meaning of taking a truth as one's object — that is, the forbearance and knowledge of suffering, gathered within the truth of the path, take the truth of suffering as their object; the forbearance and knowledge of accumulation, gathered within the truth of the path, take the truth of accumulation as their object; the forbearance and knowledge of cessation, gathered within the truth of the path, take the truth of cessation as their object; the forbearance and knowledge of the path, gathered within the truth of the path, take the truth of the path as their object.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Splitting "arising" into possessing-an-own-nature versus arising-from-conditions is a quietly elegant way to let something genuinely unconditioned, like analytical cessation, still count as "generated" in Śāriputra's broad sense without compromising its own unconditioned status one bit: it is obtained, and obtaining is a species of arising, even though nothing about its own substance ever came from causes and conditions at all.

2. The Rescuing Dharma, and the Brahmin Dṛḍhika

A scripture names the four truths themselves "the rescuing dharma," proclaimed to rescue sentient beings out of birth and death — composed specifically to display that rescue is achieved only by one's own diligent cultivation, never by another's. The proof is a story: the lazy brahmin Dṛḍhika, believing another's cultivation could remove his own confusion, addressed the Buddha with an affectionate verse asking to have his doubt removed by another's power; the Buddha's own reply denies this outright — were another's cultivation capable of freeing anyone, then the Buddha's own six years beneath the bodhi tree, joined with his boundless compassion, would already have cut off every sentient being's affliction at once, and yet it manifestly did not.

Chinese Source

如契經說。佛告苾芻。一切如來應正等覺說拔濟法。謂四聖諦。宣說開示四聖諦法。拔濟有情出生死故。問何故說此拔濟法耶。答欲顯要由自勤修道有拔濟義不由他修。云何知然契經說故。如契經說。有婆羅門名道德迦。來詣佛所到已頂禮世尊雙足合掌恭敬而說頌言。

Ru-Yi Translation

As a scripture states, the Buddha told the monks: every Thus-Come One, Worthy of Offerings, Perfectly and Fully Awakened One, speaks of a rescuing dharma, namely, the four noble truths, proclaiming and expounding the dharma of the four noble truths in order to rescue sentient beings out of birth and death. One asks: for what reason is this rescuing dharma spoken of? The answer: it is in order to display that the very meaning of rescue is achieved specifically by one's own diligent cultivation of the path, and not by another's cultivation. How is this known to be so? Because a scripture states it — as the scripture states, there was a brahmin named Dṛḍhika, who came to where the Buddha was, and, having arrived, bowed his head at the World-Honored One's own two feet, joined his palms in reverence, and spoke this verse:

稽首此人間　勇猛真梵志淨眼普觀照　願能除我疑問今此頌中欲顯何義。答彼婆羅門稟性懶惰謂他修道能除自惑。故對佛說愛語伽他。欲顯世尊是天梵志乘勇猛願來生人間。為濟有情已修聖道。唯願哀愍除我疑惑。世尊於是為說頌言。

'I bow my head to this one among men, the truly courageous brahmin; with pure eyes, surveying all, may you remove my own doubt.' One asks: what meaning does this verse now wish to display? The answer: that brahmin, being lazy by his own nature, held that another's cultivation of the path could remove his own confusion, and it was for this reason that he addressed the Buddha with these affectionate verses, wishing to display that the World-Honored One, a heavenly brahmin riding upon a courageous vow, had come to be born among men in order to rescue sentient beings, having already cultivated the noble path, and wishing only that the Buddha, out of compassion, would remove his own doubt and confusion. The World-Honored One then spoke this verse in reply:

我於脫汝疑　必無自在力要汝見勝法　方能越瀑流今此頌中。世尊欲顯無他修道斷自惑義。若有此義我坐樹下修聖道時。一切有情煩惱應斷。我於一切具大慈悲。而諸有情惑未頓斷。

'As for freeing you from your own doubt, I truly possess no power of my own to do so; you must yourself behold the supreme dharma, and only then will you cross over the flood.' The intent of this verse now is that the World-Honored One wished to display that there exists no such thing as another's cultivation of the path cutting off one's own confusion — for, were there such a thing, then, at the very time I myself sat beneath the tree cultivating the noble path, every sentient being's own affliction ought to have been cut off, since I possess great compassion toward every one of them; and yet the confusion of sentient beings was not thereby cut off all at once.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own reply to Dṛḍhika is one of this treatise's most theologically consequential moments: it forecloses, on the Buddha's own direct authority, any reading of Buddhism as a religion of vicarious salvation, grounding the entire edifice of the four truths in the flatly stated premise that not even infinite compassion, joined to the greatest teacher who ever lived, can substitute for a single individual's own act of seeing.

3. The Meaning of "Rescue," and Why Only the Four Truths

Just as taking medicine for another's illness cannot cure one's own body, another's path cannot cut off one's own confusion — it is precisely this fact that gives the four truths their name as "rescuing dharma." "Rescue" itself receives a threefold gloss: drawing beings from the ordinary being's own perilous cliffs and ravines into the noble one's own level highway; drawing them from the equal ground of supreme mundane dharma into the true nature of the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering; and drawing them from the great suffering of birth and death into the great happiness of nirvāṇa. Only the four truths, and not the elements, sense-fields, or aggregates, earn this name, since contemplating the elements and the rest is merely a distant preliminary practice — the practitioner's own progression running from the eighteen elements, to the twelve sense-fields, to the five aggregates, only finally reaching the four truths within the near preliminary practices of heat, summit, and forbearance themselves, immediately before entering the noble path.

Chinese Source

故無他道斷自惑義。如他服藥自病不除。要自服藥其病方愈。由此故知要自修道有拔濟義不由他修。是故世尊說拔濟法。此拔濟法即四聖諦。欲令有情依此修道。見四聖諦斷自疑惑。問言拔濟者是何義耶。答從嶮難處引諸有情置平坦處故名拔濟。嶮難處者。謂異生性如深坑谷及山巖等諸可畏處。平坦處者。謂諸聖性如大王路。由佛宣說四聖諦法。從異生性極嶮難處。引諸有情置諸聖性極平坦處。謂令入道及得道果故名拔濟。復次從平等處引入正性故名拔濟。平等處者謂世第一法。正性者謂苦法智忍。由佛宣說四聖諦法。引諸有情從世第一法入苦法智忍故名拔濟。復次從大苦處引諸有情置大樂處故名拔濟。大苦處者謂生死。大樂處者謂涅槃。由佛宣說四聖諦法引諸有情令出生死得大涅槃故名拔濟。問何故四諦名拔濟法非界處蘊。答觀四聖諦入道得果離染盡漏。觀界處蘊不如是故。復次觀四聖諦令所化者近入聖道近證法身。觀界處蘊是遠加行。謂修行者遠加行中初習業位觀十八界。已串修位觀十二處。超作意位觀於五蘊。燸頂忍等近加行中。方觀四諦能入聖道證果法身。故唯四諦名拔濟法。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is for this reason that there exists no such thing as another's path cutting off one's own confusion, exactly as, when another takes medicine, one's own illness is not thereby removed — one must oneself take the medicine, and only then does one's own illness come to be healed. It is by this that it is known that it is specifically one's own cultivation of the path that achieves the meaning of rescue, and not another's cultivation, and it is for this reason that the World-Honored One speaks of the rescuing dharma. This rescuing dharma is precisely the four noble truths, and it is spoken in order to cause sentient beings to rely upon it in cultivating the path, to behold the four noble truths, and to cut off their own doubt and confusion. One asks: what is the meaning of the word "rescue"? The answer: it is because one draws sentient beings forth from a perilous and difficult place and sets them down in a level and even place that it is named rescue. As for the perilous and difficult place: this refers to the nature of an ordinary being, exactly as a deep pit, ravine, mountain cliff, or other such fearsome place. As for the level and even place: this refers to the nature of a noble one, exactly as a great royal highway. It is by the Buddha's own proclaiming of the dharma of the four noble truths that sentient beings are drawn forth from the ordinary being's own utterly perilous and difficult place, and set down within the noble one's own utterly level and even place — meaning that they are caused to enter the path and to attain the fruit of the path — and it is for this reason that it is named rescue. Further, it is because one draws them forth from an equal place and leads them into a true nature that it is named rescue. As for the equal place: this refers to supreme mundane dharma. As for the true nature: this refers to the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering. It is by the Buddha's own proclaiming of the dharma of the four noble truths that sentient beings are led from supreme mundane dharma into the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering, and it is for this reason that it is named rescue. Further, it is because one draws sentient beings forth from a place of great suffering and sets them down in a place of great happiness that it is named rescue. As for the place of great suffering: this refers to birth and death. As for the place of great happiness: this refers to nirvāṇa. It is by the Buddha's own proclaiming of the dharma of the four noble truths that sentient beings are led out of birth and death and caused to attain great nirvāṇa, and it is for this reason that it is named rescue. One asks: for what reason are the four truths named rescuing dharma, and not the elements, the sense-fields, or the aggregates? The answer: contemplating the four noble truths, one enters the path, attains the fruit, becomes free of defilement, and exhausts the contaminants; contemplating the elements, sense-fields, or aggregates does not accomplish this. Further, contemplating the four noble truths causes those to be converted to draw near to entering the noble path and to draw near to directly realizing the dharma-body; contemplating the elements, sense-fields, or aggregates is a distant preliminary practice — that is, among a practitioner's own distant preliminary practices, at the initial stage of habitual cultivation, one contemplates the eighteen elements; at the stage already thoroughly practiced, one contemplates the twelve sense-fields; at the stage that transcends mental application, one contemplates the five aggregates; and only within the near preliminary practices of heat, summit, forbearance, and the rest does one then contemplate the four truths, thereby being able to enter the noble path and directly realize the fruit and the dharma-body — and it is for this reason that only the four truths are named rescuing dharma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Mapping the contemplative curriculum's own distant-to-near structure — eighteen elements, then twelve sense-fields, then five aggregates, and only then the four truths — onto the very meaning of "rescue" quietly identifies the four truths as the specific content a practitioner needs at the moment of actually crossing over, everything earlier in the curriculum being preparation that narrows the field of view without yet delivering the rescue itself.

4. Why "Noble" in "Noble Truth"? Saṃghavasu's Dispute

Is "noble" in "noble truth" earned by being wholesome, by being undefiled, or by being possessed by the nobles? Each option founders — wholesome and undefiled both fail to cover the first two truths, which are contaminated and morally variable, while "possessed by the nobles" seems undermined by the fact that every sentient being, noble or not, possesses the truths of suffering and accumulation. The resolution: a noble one alone possesses all four truths in full — even one fully bound at the very first moment of the path of seeing, not yet possessing cessation, will most certainly come to possess all four shortly after, unlike an ordinary being, who at no time whatsoever possesses all four together. The Venerable Saṃghavasu recalls a dispute, settled by the Buddha himself, between ordinary beings claiming the formations are permanent, pleasurable, pure, and possessed of self, and nobles claiming the opposite — the Buddha ruling that the nobles' own statement alone is the truth, since only the nobles directly know, see, and perceive suffering as it is.

One asks: what is the meaning of the word "noble" in "noble truth"? Is it named noble truth because it is wholesome, because it is undefiled, or because it is possessed by the nobles? Suppose either is granted, what fault follows? All three carry a fault. For what reason is this so? If it is named noble truth because it is wholesome, then, among the four, only the latter two could be named noble truth, since only they are wholesome — the former two, being common to all three moral qualities, how could they, too, be named noble truth? If it is named noble truth because it is undefiled, then, among the four, only the latter two could be named noble truth, since only they are undefiled — the former two, being contaminated, how could they be named noble truth? If it is named noble truth because it is possessed by the nobles, then, since ordinary beings, too, possess it, how could it be named noble truth uniquely? As it is said, who possesses the truths of suffering and accumulation? Every sentient being does. Who possesses the truth of cessation? Whoever is not fully bound does.

The answer: one should say as follows — it is named noble truth because it is possessed by the nobles. One asks: if so, this resolves well the first two difficulties raised — how, then, is the third difficulty raised to be resolved? The answer: a noble one possesses all four in full, which an ordinary being does not. One asks: yet there also exist nobles who do not possess all four in full, such as one who is fully bound — at the very first mind-moment of the path of seeing, he does not yet possess the truth of cessation at that time. The answer: this is but a brief span of time, unlike an ordinary being — that is, one who is fully bound, at the very first mind-moment of the path of seeing, although he does not yet possess all four, will, afterward, most certainly come to possess all four in full; an ordinary being, however, at no time whatsoever possesses all four in full, and it is for this reason that suffering and the rest are uniquely named noble truth. Further, among the class of the nobles there exist those who possess all four in full, and it is for this reason that it is named noble truth; among the class of ordinary beings there exists no one who possesses all four in full, and it is for this reason that they possess no such truth at all. The Buddha spoke as follows: the nobles' own statement is the truth; the other statement is not the truth, for what reason is this so? Reading note — Ru-Yi

That an ordinary being possesses the truths of suffering and accumulation exactly as fully as a noble one, yet the truths are still named "noble," is resolved not by denying the ordinary being's possession but by insisting on completeness: it is having all four together, cessation and the path included, that distinguishes the noble one, so that the name "noble truth" tracks a totality of possession rather than any single truth's own restricted membership.

5. The Noble Truth of Suffering: Eight Forms, Two Etymologies

Suffering is enumerated through its eight classic forms — birth, old age, sickness, death, meeting the unloved, separation from the loved, and not obtaining what one seeks — each given two layered etymologies. Birth is suffering both because it is joined with birth itself, and because it is the very field and foundation upon which every later suffering rests; old age both because it is joined with change, and because it causes the beloved prime of youth to decline; and so through each of the remaining six, closing with the puzzle of why suffering, said to be vast and boundless, is still called a "brief" statement — answered by the image of a man with so many faults that only the blunt summary "he is an extremely evil man" can do justice to their sheer extent.

問苦聖諦云何。答如契經說。生苦老苦病苦死苦非愛會苦愛別離苦求不得苦。略說一切五取蘊苦是名苦聖諦。應知此中與生相合故名生苦。與住異相合故名老苦。與逼惱相合故名病苦。與滅相合故名死苦。與非愛會相合故名非愛會苦。與愛別離相合故名愛別離苦。

6. Is There Pleasure in the Aggregates?

A scripture describing form as "accompanied by pleasure," giving rise to "joy and happiness," seems to contradict naming the aggregates solely the truth of suffering. The resolution: a small measure of pleasure does exist within the aggregates, but suffering so vastly outweighs it that the whole is still named suffering, exactly as a single drop of honey in a jar of poison still leaves it named a jar of poison. A rival view denies any pleasure whatsoever, reconciling the scripture instead through pure relativity — one calls a lesser suffering "pleasure" only by comparison to a greater one, exactly as one calls the suffering of the human realm pleasurable when set beside the suffering of hell.

7. The Noble Truth of Accumulation: Why Craving Alone?

Every contaminated dharma capable of serving as a cause belongs, strictly, to the truth of accumulation — yet the Buddha names craving alone, exactly as volition alone is named in designating the formations-aggregate even though every associated and dissociated formation genuinely belongs to it. It is capable, further, both of burning and of moistening a sentient being at once — moistening at the stage of cause, burning at the stage of fruit, exactly as a drop of scalding oil does both the instant it falls upon the skin — and it acts exactly as a corpse-raising ghoul, summoning forth the very karma of rebirth wherever it is present, exactly as such a ghoul can raise a corpse wherever water exists.

8. More Reasons: The Rākṣasī, the King of Fish, and the Glue

Further similes accumulate without limit: craving gathers in every sentient and insentient possession, from wife and servants to palace and grain; it nourishes both male and female bodies, driving parents to provide for children and even birds and beasts to nourish their young by any means; it moistens the very seed and blood within the womb, and, wherever it arises, every other affliction follows it exactly as the lesser fish follow the king of fish, or as dust clings easily to a dampened garment. Most vividly, craving is likened to a rākṣasī who lures seafaring merchants with a pleasing countenance and gentle words, only to imprison and devour them once their trust is won — craving, too, first delighting sentient beings in wrongdoing before dragging them into an evil destiny's own extreme suffering.

9. The Chain of Ten Defiling Dharmas, and Craving's Many Similes

A scripture to Ānanda traces an entire causal chain from craving alone — craving conditions seeking, seeking conditions obtaining, obtaining conditions accumulating, accumulating conditions attachment, attachment conditions clinging, clinging conditions stinginess, stinginess conditions grasping, grasping conditions guarding, and guarding conditions the taking up of sword and staff, quarreling, and deceit, giving rise to boundless evil. 10. The Noble Truths of Cessation and the Path

The exhaustion without remainder of craving is named the truth of cessation, and the eightfold noble path is named the truth of the path — yet the scripture speaks only of the "cessation of suffering" and the "path proceeding toward the cessation of suffering," never explicitly the cessation of, or path toward, accumulation. The resolution offers several converging reasons: this is simply an incomplete statement that already implies its counterpart, since suffering and accumulation are a single matter and a cause's cessation entails its fruit's; and, more pointedly, it is for the sake of those to be converted, who feel a far deeper weariness toward suffering itself than toward its abstract cause, and who, on hearing of suffering's own cessation, give rise to an immediate joy that speeds them toward direct realization. A further motive guards the path's own reputation: since some who attain arhatship as children still endure decades of bodily illness before death, worldly people might slander the path as incapable of exhausting suffering — the Buddha forestalling this by clarifying that the path extinguishes suffering's future arising, not every presently ripening result of past karma.

11. Why Suffering First, the Path Last: Three Kinds of Sequence

Why does the Buddha speak of suffering first and the path last? Three entirely different kinds of sequence converge on the answer. The sequence of arising governs cases like the four foundations of mindfulness, spoken in the order a practitioner actually first gives rise to them, body before dharmas. The sequence of ease of statement governs cases like the four right efforts, which occur simultaneously yet are still spoken in the lighter, more natural order of cutting off evil before cultivating the wholesome. And the sequence of direct realization governs the four truths specifically — a practitioner directly realizes suffering first, then accumulation, then cessation, and only then the path, and it is this very sequence of realization, not of logic or of ease, that the Buddha's own teaching order reproduces.

12. Why Direct Realization Proceeds Coarse to Subtle

Within the truths themselves, why does direct realization move from suffering, the coarsest, all the way to the path, the subtlest — exactly as an archer first practices on a coarse target before eventually striking the tip of a hair? Delusion regarding suffering sustains and draws forth delusion regarding accumulation, exactly as delusion regarding cessation sustains delusion regarding the path, so that removing an earlier delusion is a strict precondition for removing the next; and each truth's own contemplation, in turn, serves as the preliminary-practice basis and foundation for the next one's arising. The Venerable Pārśva grounds the same sequence in an ordinary sickbed: a suffering man first asks what caused his illness, learning it is wind, heat, or phlegm — the truth of accumulation; then asks when it will be cured — cessation; and only then asks by what means — the path, and it is this same natural progression of concern that the practitioner of yoga's own direct realization reproduces.

13. The Tree-Felling and Mirror Similes

Two further puzzles about sequence receive vivid images of their own. Since cause naturally precedes fruit, why does a practitioner realize suffering, the fruit, before accumulation, its cause? Because this follows the sequence proper to knowing suffering and cutting off accumulation, exactly as felling a tree means first cutting its branches and only afterward pulling out its root — one first comes to know suffering, as one first clears the branches, and only then cuts off accumulation, as one finally uproots the tree itself. And since the path naturally precedes cessation, why is cessation realized first? 14. Self-Characteristic or Shared Characteristic? Why the Four Truths Are Not Realized All at Once

15. Why Desire-Realm Suffering Is Observed Separately First

Why does a practitioner observe the desire realm's own suffering separately, only afterward observing the form and formless realms' own suffering together as one? A cascade of reasons converges: the desire realm's own suffering is coarser and thus realized first, exactly as an archer starts with a coarse target; the form and formless realms alone share the ground of concentration, and so are naturally realized together; the desire realm's own suffering is grasped together with the body in the present moment, felt as an oppressive, present burden and a present enemy, near rather than distant, and genuinely seen rather than merely inferred through freedom from defilement alone — the two upper realms possessing only this weaker, inferential kind of "seeing," exactly as a merchant knows the weight and contents of a load carried on his own body far more intimately than one carried by another on his behalf.

16. A Truth's Own Particular Characteristic, and the Aggregates Observed as a Whole

A final clarification closes the fascicle: genuine wisdom does not observe a truth's own particular characteristic by treating that characteristic as itself the object of a separate, particular observation — rather, it observes it precisely by way of its shared characteristic, and this alone is what constitutes the direct realization of the truths, the ignorance regarding a truth's particular and its shared characteristic being cut off together, all at once, in a single stroke. Why, then, if the aggregates are observed as a whole at the very moment of entering direct realization, are the four truths not likewise observed as a whole? Because within the aggregates there exists a single delusion confused with respect to all of them together, while no single affliction is ever deluded with respect to all four truths taken together as a whole — and because the aggregates' own particular characteristics, having already been understood since beginningless time, need not be observed again, while each truth's own particular characteristic has never yet been understood, and so now, for the very first time, requires its own separate direct realization.
