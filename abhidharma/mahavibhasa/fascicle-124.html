Who Creates, Who Receives?

Fascicle 124 closes the Manifest-and-Unmanifest Nyāsa with the eight-component upavāsa fast, Kātyāyana's story of the butchers and the adulterers, and the Buddha's rebuke of Śakra's boast — then opens the Own-Karma Nyāsa, asking what makes karma truly "one's own" and whether the same person who creates karma is the one who receives its fruit. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Does Taking the Three Refuges Alone Make One an Upāsaka?

Fascicle 124 opens with a genuine institutional puzzle: does simply taking the Three Refuges, without formally listing the five precepts, already make one an upāsaka — and does taking only some of the five precepts still count? The Gandhāran masters say yes to both, giving rise to the upāsaka "of one portion," "of a minor portion," "of a major portion," and "of the full portion" depending on how many precepts one keeps; the Kashmiri masters disagree, holding that only the complete five-precept vow makes a true upāsaka, and reinterpreting the "portion" language as describing which precepts one actually upholds afterward, not how many one formally received.

Chinese Source

問頗有唯受三歸成近事不。為有律儀缺減成近事不。若言有者。契經所說文句差別豈非無義。如說我某甲歸佛法僧。願尊憶持。我是近事我從今日乃至命終。護生歸淨。亦應說有律儀缺減勤策苾芻等。若無者。即前契經文句差別寧非無義。何故安立一分少分多分滿分近事耶。健馱羅國諸論師言。唯受三歸及律儀缺減悉成近事。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: is there a case where simply taking the Three Refuges alone makes one an upāsaka? Or is there a case where incomplete restraint makes one an upāsaka? If it is said that there is, then would not the sūtra's own distinct wording become meaningless — as when it says, "I, so-and-so, take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha; may the venerable one bear witness — I am an upāsaka, and from this day until the end of my life shall protect life and take refuge in purity"? For it ought then equally to be said that there is incomplete restraint for a novice or a bhikṣu as well. But if it is said that there is not, then would not the sūtra's own distinct wording given before become meaningless instead — why then does it establish the upāsaka of one portion, of a minor portion, of a major portion, and of the full portion? The treatise-masters of Gandhāra say: taking the Three Refuges alone, and incomplete restraint as well, both make one an upāsaka.

問若唯受三歸成近事者。契經文句寧非無義。經說近事受律儀時。

Question: if taking the Three Refuges alone makes one an upāsaka, then would not the sūtra's own wording become meaningless — for the sūtra says that, when the upāsaka receives restraint,

於戒師前作如是說。我某甲歸佛法僧。願尊憶持我是近事。我從今者乃至命終。於其中間護生歸淨。答彼由此表但得三歸。名為近事而未得律儀。後說學處方得律儀。然彼文句非為無義。由後自誓令前三歸得堅牢故。若不護生歸非淨故。問若缺減律儀成近事者。便為善順一分等言。所以者何。若受一名一分。受二名少分。受三受四名多分。具受五名滿分故。云何不有律儀缺減勤策苾芻等耶。答佛觀所化機宜不同。授與律儀亦不一種。如諸近事不樂捨家為攝引故。佛隨其意於五學處多少受得。故彼律儀有缺減受。苾芻勤策意樂捨家。為安立故制具律儀。具受乃得故彼律儀無缺減受。以是世尊內眷屬故。迦濕彌羅國諸論師言。無有唯受三歸及缺減律儀名為近事。問若爾契經寧非無義。如說我某甲歸佛法僧。乃至廣說。答彼由此表既得三歸。亦得律儀故成近事。問此唯自誓離於殺生。云何由此具得五種。答由此自誓離殺為依。五種律儀亦俱時得。五學處中彼為勝故。以受戒者為不損生。於損生中殺為上首。故以離殺為五所依。又護生言非唯離殺。謂不損惱一切有情。彼自誓言我從今者乃至命盡。於諸有情不害其命。不盜其物。不侵其妻。不行虛誑。為護前四。亦不飲酒。故護生言非唯離殺。然有別誦言捨生者。此言意說捨殺生等。略去殺等但說捨生。又捨生言顯於生類。

— he says, before his preceptor, "I, so-and-so, take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha; may the venerable one bear witness — I am an upāsaka, and from this day until the end of my life shall, throughout that span, protect life and take refuge in purity." Answer: by this declaration he obtains only the Three Refuges, being thereby called an upāsaka, but has not yet obtained restraint — it is only afterward, when the training rules are spoken, that he obtains restraint. Yet this wording is not without meaning, since it is by this later self-vow that the earlier Three Refuges are made firm and stable — for without protecting life and taking refuge in purity, they would not be pure. Question: if incomplete restraint makes one an upāsaka, this would well accord with the terms "one portion" and the rest, for why is this? For undertaking one precept is called "one portion," undertaking two is called "a minor portion," undertaking three or four is called "a major portion," and undertaking the full five is called "the full portion" — so why then is there no incomplete restraint for a novice or a bhikṣu as well? Answer: the Buddha, observing the differing capacities of those he converts, bestows restraint accordingly, not uniformly — since upāsakas are unwilling to give up household life, the Buddha, in order to draw them in, allows them to receive as many or as few of the five training rules as they wish, and hence this restraint may be incompletely received. Bhikṣus and novices, by contrast, are willing to give up household life, and, for the sake of establishing them properly, the Buddha prescribes the full restraint, obtained only through complete reception, and hence this restraint cannot be incompletely received, since these are the World-Honored One's own inner household. The treatise-masters of Kashmir say: there is no such thing as taking the Three Refuges alone, or incomplete restraint, being called an upāsaka. Question: if so, would not the sūtra become meaningless — as when it says, "I, so-and-so, take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha," and so on in full? Answer: by this declaration he obtains both the Three Refuges and restraint at once, and thereby becomes an upāsaka. Question: this vow speaks only of abstaining from taking life — how then does one thereby obtain the full complement of all five? Answer: it is on the basis of this self-vow to abstain from taking life that all five restraints are obtained simultaneously, since among the five training rules, this one is supreme — for the very point of taking these precepts is to avoid harming life, and among all forms of harming life, killing is foremost, and hence abstaining from killing is made the basis for all five. Moreover, the phrase "protecting life" does not mean abstaining from killing alone, but not harming any being whatsoever — for his self-vow says, "From this day until the end of my life, I shall not take the life of any being, shall not steal their property, shall not violate their spouse, shall not speak falsely" — and, in order to protect these first four, he also abstains from drinking wine. Hence "protecting life" does not mean abstaining from killing alone. There is, however, a variant recitation that says "abandoning life" — this phrase means abandoning killing and the rest, abbreviating "killing and the rest" to simply "life"; and the phrase "abandoning life" further indicates, with respect to living beings,

捨損惱事即五律儀。皆為遮防損生事故。由此自誓方得律儀。故彼契經非為無義。問若唯自誓便得律儀。何故復說五種學處。答雖由自誓已得律儀。而未了知彼差別相。欲令知故說五學處。故彼所說皆非無義。問若爾何故說有一分等鄔波索迦耶。答此說持位非說受位。謂於五中持一不持四名一分。持二不持三名少分。持三持四名多分。具持五名滿分。尊者僧伽筏蘇分同前二師說。彼說無有唯受三歸便成近事。然有缺減五種律儀亦成近事。謂彼將受近事戒時。

— abandoning the deeds that harm and injure them, which is precisely the five restraints, all established to guard against and prevent the harming of life. It is by this very self-vow that restraint is thereby obtained, and hence the sūtra is not without meaning. Question: if the mere self-vow alone obtains restraint, why does the sūtra go on to speak of the five training rules as well? Answer: although restraint has already been obtained through the self-vow, one does not yet know the distinct marks of each of the five, and it is in order to make this known that the five training rules are spoken of — and hence what is said is not without meaning in any part. Question: if so, then why does it speak of the upāsaka of one portion and the rest? Answer: this speaks of the stage of upholding the precepts, not the stage of receiving them — that is, among the five, upholding one and not the other four is called "one portion"; upholding two and not the other three is called "a minor portion"; upholding three or four is called "a major portion"; and upholding all five completely is called "the full portion." The Venerable Saṃghavasu partly agrees with each of the two preceding masters: he holds that there is no such thing as taking the Three Refuges alone making one an upāsaka, yet there is such a thing as incomplete restraint of the five also making one an upāsaka — that is, at the time one is about to receive the upāsaka's precepts,

先與戒師共詳審議。如是學處我能受持。如是學處我不能受。既詳議已歸佛法僧。自誓要期得爾所戒隨先詳議能受少多。今得律儀其數亦爾。由此故說近事律儀名詳議戒。非勤策等戒得有此名。如是說者。無但三歸即成近事。亦無缺減近事律儀。成近事者如無缺減。勤策等律儀名勤策等彼亦如是。

— one first deliberates carefully with one's preceptor as to which of these training rules one is able to uphold and which one is not, and, having so deliberated, then takes refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, and vows to obtain precisely those precepts one is committed to — and, according to however many one was able to commit to in this prior deliberation, one now obtains restraint in that same number. It is for this reason that the upāsaka's restraint is called the "deliberated precept," a name that does not apply to the novice's or bhikṣu's precepts. According to this last account: there is no such thing as taking the Three Refuges alone making one an upāsaka, nor is there such a thing as incomplete upāsaka-restraint — one becomes an upāsaka only through complete restraint, just as one becomes a novice or the like only through the complete novice or other restraint, without any incompleteness — it is just the same here.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Kashmiri resolution of why a vow naming only abstention from killing can still establish all five precepts at once is a small piece of elegant reasoning: killing is treated as the "head" of harm, and "protecting life" is read broadly enough to already imply not stealing, not violating another's spouse, not lying, and not drinking — since drunkenness itself undermines one's capacity to protect others. A single vow, properly unpacked, turns out to already contain the rest.

2. Skipping a Step: Restraint Without Refuge, and What Happens When You Advance a Level

The same structural question recurs at every rung of the ordination ladder — can one receive prātimokṣa restraint without first taking the Three Refuges, or become a novice without first being an upāsaka, or a bhikṣu without first being a novice? The answer turns on motive: skipping the step from simple ignorance of proper order still succeeds, but skipping it out of pride — deliberately refusing the "lesser" step as beneath oneself — fails outright, since a mind entangled by pride cannot receive restraint at all. A further puzzle asks whether advancing a level (upāsaka to novice, novice to bhikṣu) discards the earlier restraint or simply adds to it, with rival tallies of exactly how many total precepts one would then hold.

Chinese Source

問諸有但受近事律儀。不受三歸得律儀不。有說不得。以受三歸與此律儀。為門為依為加行故。有說不定謂若不知先受三歸後方受戒。信戒師故便受律儀。彼得律儀戒師得罪若彼解了先受三歸。後受律儀是正儀式。但憍慢故不受三歸。作如是言且應受戒。何用歸信佛法僧。為彼慢纏心雖受不得。問若先不受近事律儀。便受勤策律儀。得勤策律儀不。有說不得。以近事律儀與此律儀為門為依為加行故。有說不定。若不了知先受近事律儀。後方受勤策律儀。信戒師故受此律儀。彼得律儀戒師得罪。若彼解了先受近事律儀。後受勤策律儀是正儀式。但憍慢故不欲受學近事律儀。作如是言何用受此近事劣戒。彼慢纏心雖受不得。如說不受近事律儀。而受勤策律儀。如是不受勤策律儀。而受苾芻律儀。廣說亦爾。問諸近事受勤策律儀。及勤策受苾芻律儀。彼為捨前律儀得後律儀不。若捨前得後者。何故施設論說前後律儀彼俱成就。又若捨者。後捨勤策為近事時。及捨苾芻為勤策時。更來受戒。云何得彼近事勤策二律儀耶。若不捨者。彼既成就二種律儀。或復三種。何故得名唯依後戒。又親教師彼既有二。何故佛說後是前非。答受後律儀不捨前戒。謂近事受勤策律儀不捨近事五。更得勤策十。爾時成就十五律儀。若勤策受苾芻律儀。不捨前十五更得苾芻過二百五十。爾時成就過二百六十五律儀。有餘師說。若近事受勤策律儀。不捨近事五更得勤策五。爾時成就十種律儀。若勤策受苾芻律儀。不捨前十更得苾芻過二百四十。爾時成就過二百五十律儀。問彼既成就二種律儀或復三種。何故得名唯依後戒。答就勝立名不應為難。如得勝位捨本劣名。問彼親教師既有二種。何故佛說後是前非。答以勝律儀依後師得。不依前故。如不依彼律儀得名。彼師亦爾。復有說者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: if one takes prātimokṣa restraint without taking the Three Refuges, does one obtain the restraint? Some say: one does not, since taking the Three Refuges serves as the gateway, the basis, and the preparatory action for this restraint. Some say: it is not fixed — that is, if one does not know that one must first take the Three Refuges before receiving the precepts, and, trusting one's preceptor, receives the restraint anyway, one does obtain the restraint, though the preceptor incurs the fault; but if one understands that first taking the Three Refuges and only afterward receiving the restraint is the proper procedure, yet, out of pride alone, refuses to take the Three Refuges, saying, "Let me simply receive the precepts — what need is there to take refuge in faith in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha?" — then, because his mind is entangled by pride, though he receives the precepts, he does not obtain the restraint. Question: if one has not first taken the upāsaka's restraint and directly receives the novice's restraint, does one obtain the novice's restraint? Some say: one does not, since the upāsaka's restraint serves as the gateway, the basis, and the preparatory action for this restraint. Some say: it is not fixed — if one does not know that one must first take the upāsaka's restraint before receiving the novice's restraint, and, trusting one's preceptor, receives this restraint anyway, one does obtain the restraint, though the preceptor incurs the fault; but if one understands that first taking the upāsaka's restraint and only afterward receiving the novice's restraint is the proper procedure, yet, out of pride alone, is unwilling to undertake the upāsaka's own restraint, saying, "What need is there to receive this inferior upāsaka precept?" — then, because his mind is entangled by pride, though he receives the precepts, he does not obtain the restraint. Just as it is said for not receiving the upāsaka's restraint yet receiving the novice's restraint, so too it is explained in full for not receiving the novice's restraint yet receiving the bhikṣu's restraint. Question: when an upāsaka receives the novice's restraint, or a novice receives the bhikṣu's restraint, does he give up the earlier restraint and obtain the later one? If he gives up the earlier and obtains the later, why does the Prajñaptiśāstra say that he possesses both the earlier and later restraints together? And if he does give it up, then when he later gives up the novice's restraint to return to being an upāsaka, or gives up the bhikṣu's restraint to return to being a novice, and must receive the precepts once more, how could he obtain the upāsaka's or novice's restraint again at all? But if he does not give it up, then, already possessing two or even three kinds of restraint, why is he named solely with reference to the later precept? And, having had two preceptors, why does the Buddha say that the later one is valid and the earlier one is not? Answer: receiving the later restraint does not give up the earlier precept — that is, when an upāsaka receives the novice's restraint, he does not give up the upāsaka's own five precepts, but further obtains the novice's ten, and at that time possesses fifteen restraints in all; when a novice receives the bhikṣu's restraint, he does not give up the previous fifteen, but further obtains the bhikṣu's own more than two hundred and fifty, and at that time possesses more than two hundred and sixty-five restraints in all. Other masters say: when an upāsaka receives the novice's restraint, he does not give up the upāsaka's own five precepts, but further obtains the novice's own five, and at that time possesses ten restraints in all; when a novice receives the bhikṣu's restraint, he does not give up the previous ten, but further obtains the bhikṣu's own more than two hundred and forty, and at that time possesses more than two hundred and fifty restraints in all. Question: since he already possesses two or even three kinds of restraint, why is he named solely with reference to the later precept? Answer: he is named according to what is superior, and this ought not to be raised as an objection, just as one who attains a superior rank gives up the name of his original, inferior one. Question: since he has had two preceptors, why does the Buddha say that the later one is valid and the earlier one is not? Answer: because the superior restraint is obtained in dependence upon the later teacher, not the earlier — just as one is named according to what one does not depend upon for one's restraint, so too it is with the teacher. Other masters say:

捨前律儀。問若爾何故施設論說前後律儀彼俱成就。答彼論意說。

— he does give up the earlier restraint. Question: if so, why does the Prajñaptiśāstra say that he possesses both the earlier and later restraints together? Answer: that treatise's own intent is to say

由前律儀資後令勝。前戒勢力今時猶轉故說成就。而先律儀實不成就。問後捨苾芻為勤策時。及捨勤策為近事時。復云何得彼二戒耶。答即由語表自誓我今還為勤策或近事故。得二律儀非成舊戒。

— that the earlier restraint supports the later, making it superior, and it is because the force of the earlier precept still continues to operate at this time that he is said to "possess" it, though the earlier restraint is not, in fact, still possessed. Question: when one later gives up the bhikṣu's restraint to return to being a novice, or gives up the novice's restraint to return to being an upāsaka, how does one then obtain those two precepts again? Answer: it is precisely through manifest verbal karma, vowing to oneself, "I shall now become a novice, or an upāsaka, once more," that one obtains these two restraints — this does not constitute the completion of the old precept.

如是說者不捨前戒而得後戒彼後所受非前所受相違法故。又前後戒因。緣各別。不應相合成十數等。

According to this account: one does not give up the earlier precept in obtaining the later one, since what is received later is not a dharma contrary to what was received before; moreover, the cause and condition of the earlier and later precepts are each distinct, and hence they ought not to be combined into a total of ten and so forth.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That pride alone — not mere procedural error — is what voids an otherwise identical ordination is a striking piece of moral psychology: the treatise is less interested in whether the correct words were spoken in the correct order than in the state of mind behind the shortcut, treating arrogance as something that can invalidate a ritual act even when its outward form is followed perfectly.

3. The Boy Who Married: Does Restraint Reach a Future Spouse in Advance?

A charming thought experiment: a boy takes the upāsaka precepts, including abstention from sexual misconduct, years before he has any wife at all — does his restraint already, at that moment, cover the woman he will one day marry? The treatise answers yes, he already possesses it with respect to her, yet insists this creates no later violation, since the vow was never against intimacy with a legitimate spouse in the first place, only against misconduct — resolved through the idea that a single continuum can contain many distinct components, each with its own separately triggered scope.

Chinese Source

問若童子時受近事戒。至少年位。方娉妻室。彼於此妻先得戒不。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: if one receives the upāsaka's precepts as a boy, and only later, upon reaching youth, takes a wife, does one already possess this precept with respect to this particular wife beforehand?

若先得者今應犯戒。若先不得則此律儀應從少分有情處得。答應言先得。問若爾今應犯戒。答得由別分非總相續。先所受得離欲邪行。非非梵行今如何犯。習近自妻非邪行故。謂一相續別分有多。

If one already possesses it beforehand, then one ought now to violate the precept; but if one does not already possess it beforehand, then this restraint would have to be obtained with respect to only a partial portion of beings. Answer: one should say that one already possesses it beforehand. Question: if so, then one ought now to violate the precept. Answer: it is obtained with respect to a particular component, not the whole continuum entire — what was previously received and obtained was abstention from sexual misconduct, not from unchaste conduct altogether, so how could one now violate it? Since intimacy with one's own legitimate wife is not misconduct at all, one and the same continuum may have many distinct components,

所遮所行別故無犯問若童子位得不作律儀。至少年時方娉妻室。彼於此妻先得律儀不。若先得者今應犯律儀。然不作律儀得必無犯。

— what is prohibited and what is actually done being distinct, and hence there is no violation. Question: if one obtains the restraint of non-doing as a boy, and only later, upon reaching youth, takes a wife, does one already possess this restraint with respect to this particular wife beforehand? If one already possesses it beforehand, then one ought now to violate the restraint — yet the restraint of non-doing, once obtained, can definitely never be violated.

6. Unfit Vessels, Delayed Killing, and the Four Grades of a Pure Fast

8. A New Chapter Opens: What Makes Karma Truly 'One's Own'

With the Manifest-and-Unmanifest Nyāsa now concluded, fascicle 124 opens the Own-Karma Nyāsa (自業納息), the fifth topic-unit of the Karma Aggregate, glossing the sūtra's teaching that all beings are owners of their own karma, heirs to their karma, born from karma, and dependent on karma for their station in life. "One's own karma" receives a precise technical definition: only karma whose fruit has already begun to manifest and is presently being experienced earns this name — not karma merely performed but not yet ripening, and not karma in the very act of being performed, since a ripening-cause and its ripening-effect can never coexist at the same moment; the fruit can only arise once its cause has already ceased.

9. Same Person, Different Person, No Person: Who Really Creates and Receives Karma?

The nyāsa's opening section reaches one of the treatise's most philosophically substantial passages: is it the very same person who creates karma who later experiences its fruit, or a different person entirely? The answer holds all three positions at once, each valid on its own grounds — "this one creates, this one receives," since the aggregates, though arising and ceasing moment by moment, form a single unbroken continuum nameable as "one"; "one creates, another receives," since karma made in the human realm may ripen in an animal or heavenly rebirth, a genuinely different destiny and body; and, at the deepest level, "no one creates, no one receives" at all, since every dharma is without self, without being, life-principle, nurturer, or person — only the bare arising and ceasing of conditioned formations, empty of any inherent doer or receiver.

11. Why Self-Karma Is Always Past, and the Question of When It Is Finally Spent

Since "one's own karma" is defined as karma whose fruit has already manifested, it must always, strictly, be classified as past — never future, since one never experiences a fruit before creating its cause, and never present, since cause and fruit cannot coexist at the same moment. This raises a further, more intricate question: does possessing this now-past karma mean one still "has" it, in the sense of not yet having exhausted it? The treatise begins an elaborate combinatorial ledger tracking exactly when a given instance of karma — an ānantarya deed, restraint or non-restraint karma, ordinary desire-realm volition — finally counts as spent, through triggers such as giving up one's training, a change of sex, cutting off wholesome roots, or simply the natural lapse of a temporary vow at the next dawn.
