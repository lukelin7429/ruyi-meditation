Two Jailers, Three Watchmen

Fascicle 49 opens the Fetters Aggregate's own extended treatment of affliction with a run of interlocking taxonomies — the five fetters, the five fetters pertaining to the lower portion (illustrated through a condemned man held by two jailers and hunted down by three roving watchmen even after his escape), the five fetters pertaining to the higher portion (turning on a genuine, unresolved puzzle over whether restlessness belongs among the nine fetters), the five views (closing with a sea creature whose bite only a blade can loose, and the Buddha's own declaration that he does not dispute with the world), and finally the six bodies of craving, counted sixteen different ways. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Five Fetters

Fascicle 49 opens the Fetters Aggregate proper with the five fetters — greed, hatred, conceit, envy, and stinginess — defined as thirty-seven factors in total. Four masters then offer four different diagnostics for why only these five, among every affliction that exists, earn the name “fetter” at all: Pārśva's blanket appeal to the Buddha's own discretion, Vasumitra's technical distinction between afflictions confused about an object through its specific characteristic versus its shared characteristic, and Ghoṣa's and Buddhadeva's shared emphasis on sheer recurring severity.

The Venerable Pārśva states: the Buddha knows the nature, characteristics, power, and function of every dharma — whatever is fit to be established as a fetter, he establishes it as such; whatever is not so fit, he does not so establish it, and this ought not to be pressed as an objection. The Venerable Vasumitra states as follows: this passage speaks only of the afflictions that, confused about form and the other objects, bind the mind through their own specific characteristic, as fetters — greed, hatred, and conceit alone being afflictions confused about their object through a specific characteristic are therefore established as fetters; the five views and doubt, being afflictions confused about principle through a shared characteristic, and ignorance, though it spans confusion about both principle and object, being for the most part confused about principle, are therefore not established as fetters. The two entanglements of envy and stinginess likewise are but afflictions confused about their object, causing disturbance to both of the two assemblies and to two destinies, and, their fault being manifold, are therefore also established as fetters; the remaining entanglements and defilements, possessing no such matter, are therefore not established as fetters. The Venerable Ghoṣa likewise states: these five, with respect to their object, bind the mind with especially heavy force, and are therefore established as fetters. The Venerable Buddhadeva states as follows: these five, with respect to their object, manifest again and again, disturbing both oneself and others, their fault being especially grave, and are therefore established as fetters.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Vasumitra's criterion is the most interesting of the four because it is purely epistemic rather than moral: greed, hatred, and conceit qualify as fetters not because they are especially harmful in the abstract, but because they seize on an object through that object's own specific characteristic, the way the five views and doubt instead seize on principle through a shared, general characteristic. Severity turns out to be downstream of a prior classification of how the mind is mistaken, not a free-standing criterion in its own right.

2. The Five Lower-Portion Fetters Open

A second taxonomy opens: covetous desire, hatred, the view of the perishable aggregation of self, clinging to rules and vows, and doubt — thirty-one factors in total, named for manifesting, being abandoned, causing rebirth, and bringing forth their fruit within the lower realm, the desire realm. The treatise then presses its own definition to its logical limit: since every affliction whatsoever arises while a being abides in the desire realm, why aren't all sixty-four latent tendencies fetters pertaining to the lower portion? The answer, refreshingly, concedes the point — the Buddha simply taught an abbreviated selection.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The numerical bookkeeping here is genuinely rigorous even though the final naming choice is admittedly arbitrary: the treatise sorts all sixty-four latent tendencies by exactly which of the definition's four criteria each one satisfies — thirty-six bind rebirth, thirty-four ripen karmically, two do not — before conceding that this exhaustive accounting still doesn't explain why only five of them got a name. Formal completeness and explanatory sufficiency, on this account, are simply two different achievements, and satisfying one is no guarantee of the other.

3. The Jailer and the Watchman

The treatise's central image for the five lower-portion fetters: a condemned man held by two jailers (covetous desire and hatred) who never let him leave the prison of the desire realm, and three roving watchmen (the view of the perishable aggregation of self, clinging to rules and vows, and doubt) who drag him back even if he bribes his way past the jailers and flees all the way up through the formless attainments to the very peak of existence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The parable's precision is easy to miss: it distinguishes the affliction that prevents departure from the affliction that guarantees return, which is exactly the difference between craving and hatred's continuous, present-tense grip and view and doubt's structural, dormant capacity to reassert itself later. A meditator can genuinely defeat the jailers — unattractiveness overcomes desire, loving-kindness overcomes hatred — and rise far beyond the desire realm on that basis alone, only to discover that the watchmen were never actually stationed at the prison gate at all, but waiting at the very top of the road.

4. Māluṅkyaputra's Correction, and the Five Upper-Portion Fetters

A canonical scene: Māluṅkyaputra proudly recites the five lower-portion fetters back to the Buddha, only to be rebuked — not for getting the list wrong, but for equating “fetter” with active mental entanglement alone. The Buddha's corrective, delivered through the image of a sick infant who cannot yet even perceive an object of desire yet still carries its latent tendency, is that a fetter remains a fetter whether merely possessed, currently manifest, or spanning any of the three times. The fascicle then opens a third fetter-taxonomy, the five fetters pertaining to the higher portion — craving for form, craving for the formless, restlessness, conceit, and ignorance — as eight factors.

9. The View of a Perishable Self versus the Extreme View

10. “I Do Not Dispute With the World”

Five masters read the same famous scriptural line — Vasumitra's account of the Buddha granting each extremist exactly one half of the truth (cause to the nihilist, fruit to the eternalist, and a middle path beyond both), through worldly-versus-ultimate accommodation, the cutting-off of craving and view as the “two roots of dispute,” and the Great Virtuous One's image of a horse whose gait turns uneven only on rough terrain. 11. Two Enmities and the Destruction of Direct Perception

Further reasons pile up for why wrong view alone, among the five views, earns its distinct name: it destroys the very basis of action, slanders cause and fruit, stands in utmost contradiction to religious practice, slanders the three refuges across all three times, and destroys what the treatise calls the “two kindnesses” — the kindness of the Dharma and the kindness of one's own birth — thereby giving rise to two enmities. A vivid simile closes the case: a person fallen into a pit of blazing fire who deceives the world by insisting he feels pleasure there, exactly like a being of wrong view insisting, amid every kind of manifest suffering, that he suffers not at all.

12. View-Clinging and Clinging to Rules and Vows

Why do exactly two of the five views — view-clinging and clinging to rules and vows — earn the additional label “取” (clinging, or grasping), when all five involve some form of mistaken cognition? The treatise's answer turns on a different axis of classification entirely: the first three views operate through the power of estimating their object, and are therefore named “view”; the latter two operate through the power of appropriating and holding fast to their object, and are therefore named “clinging.”

It's a reminder that “the five views” was never a single taxonomy running on one dimension, but several classificatory schemes quietly superimposed on the same five items.

13. Sixteen Ways to Count Craving

The treatise runs through every scriptural way of enumerating craving before settling on “six bodies” as the standard teaching: as one (the single fetter of craving, spanning all three realms), as two (desire-craving and existence-craving, per the seven latent tendencies), as three (the three rivers of craving), as four (craving for robes, food, bedding, and existence or non-existence), as five (by which truth it is abandoned), as nine grades, as eighteen modes, as thirty-six modes, as one hundred and eight modes, and, counted moment by moment, as innumerable — before asking why the Buddha teaches six bodies specifically, organized by the six sense-doors.
