Ajita Rebuked, Maitreya Praised

Fascicle 178 continues the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之五): when exactly the four pāramitās are perfected, why the Bodhisattva takes his final heavenly rebirth specifically in the Tuṣita heaven and must still descend to the human world to attain Buddhahood, the four observations he makes before his final descent, the sharply contrasting fates of the monk Ajita (rebuked for wishing to become the future wheel-turning king Śaṅkha) and the bodhisattva Maitreya (praised for wishing Buddhahood), the causal and path cognitions by which the Buddha confirmed Maitreya's prophecy, and a full account of wish-knowledge (願智) — its function, the terminal fourth dhyāna that produces it, its intrinsic nature, and exactly who can give rise to it. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Four Pāramitās: When Exactly Are They Perfected?

Fascicle 178 continues the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之五) by returning to the bodhisattva's three incalculable eons, now asking a pointed question: at what precise moment is each of the four pāramitās — giving, moral discipline, diligence, wisdom — actually perfected? A first proposal ties each to a specific narrative touchstone (giving perfected in total non-attachment even to one's own flesh, discipline perfected in patience under mutilation, diligence perfected in the seven-day vigil that overtook nine eons, wisdom perfected in the bodhisattva Gopaka's unrivaled learning), only for the treatise to reject all of these as mere occasion-bound approximations, insisting the pāramitās are truly perfected only at the final moment of attaining the knowledge of exhaustion. The section closes with rival six-pāramitā schemes and a precise headcount of how many Buddhas the bodhisattva encounters within each incalculable eon — 75,000, then 76,000, then 77,000, framed by named Buddhas at each boundary.

When the bodhisattva, through the valor of his own mind, stands on one foot for seven days and nights without blinking, gazing steadily, praising the Buddha with a single verse, without a single thought of weariness, up to that point is named the perfection of the pāramitā of diligence. Reading note — Ru-Yi

Rejecting every vivid, memorable illustration of "perfected giving" or "perfected patience" in favor of a single, undramatic technical criterion — the attainment of the knowledge of exhaustion — is a characteristic move of this treatise: it will use a striking narrative to make a virtue vivid, then immediately relocate the actual boundary of perfection somewhere far less picturesque, refusing to let a good story stand in for a precise philosophical criterion.

2. Why Tuṣita? The Bodhisattva's Final Heavenly Rebirth

Having perfected the excellent-marks karma under the Buddha Kāśyapa, the bodhisattva passes from Jambudvīpa into the Tuṣita heaven for his final heavenly existence — and the treatise surveys a long cascade of proposed reasons why Tuṣita specifically, rather than any lower or higher heaven: it is the median heaven free of the heedlessness below and the dullness above, its lifespan alone matches the precise span needed for both the bodhisattva's and Jambudvīpa's wholesome roots to ripen together, and it alone houses the nine great halls where countless Dharma-loving bodhisattvas gather to hear the future Buddha teach continuously. The section then turns to a second question — why must the final Buddhahood be attained specifically in the human world, descending from heaven rather than remaining there — answering with a cascade of reasons: humans alone combine the necessary aversion to saṃsāra with sufficiently keen wisdom, and a Buddha who attained awakening in heaven and only later came to teach humans would be mistaken for a mere illusory apparition.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The worry that a heaven-born Buddha descending to teach would be dismissed as "an illusory apparition" is a strikingly practical, almost sociological argument buried inside high cosmology: the treatise is reasoning about religious credibility itself, insisting that a teaching's authority depends partly on the ordinariness of the means by which the teacher arrived among those he teaches.

3. The Four Observations Before Descent

The bodhisattva, his lifespan in Tuṣita nearing its end, undertakes a careful fourfold survey before descending — the right time (a human lifespan somewhere between eighty thousand and a hundred years), the right place (central India, not any outlying border region), the right lineage (whichever of kṣatriya or brāhmaṇa currently holds greater social prestige), and the right vessel (a woman of pure parentage whose womb can bear, for the full ten months, a body possessing the strength of Nārāyaṇa). Having settled all four, he dispatches a messenger deva to summon volunteers from the Tuṣita assembly, and five hundred devas descend together with him into simultaneous birth among the Śākya clan.

4. Ajita Rebuked, Maitreya Praised

The fascicle turns to the sūtra's own prophecy of a future age when the human lifespan reaches eighty thousand years, the wheel-turning king Śaṅkha rules in righteousness, and, after abandoning his throne to become a monk and swiftly attaining arhatship, the Buddha delivers the fascicle's most dramatic exchange: the monk Ajita rises and asks to become that very King Śaṅkha in his own future life, only to be sharply rebuked by the Buddha — "foolish man, not content to die once, you seek a second death" — while Maitreya, rising in turn to ask for the very Buddhahood the Buddha has just foretold under that same future age, is praised without reservation. 5. Causal Cognition and Path Cognition

The treatise now names the precise cognition by which the Buddha, on hearing Maitreya's vow, could confidently confirm it: a paired "causal cognition" (examining whether the karma of the excellent marks stands perfected) and "path cognition" (examining whether the untainted path-factors needed for awakening will be present) — together called the cognition of the prior and posterior limits, and carefully distinguished from the sixteen-mode cognition of a genuinely liberating insight, since this is, after all, mere conventional knowledge. A parallel case follows for an ordinary arhat's own self-prediction of liberation "in this very life" — here only path cognition applies, since an arhat's own causal history carries none of the same evidentiary weight.

6. Why the Bodhisattva's Cause Deserves Special Note — and What Is Wish-Knowledge

Explaining why the Buddha's causal cognition is spoken of at all while an arhat's is passed over in the sūtra's silence, the treatise offers several converging reasons — the bodhisattva's path is so vast (three incalculable eons plus a hundred further eons) that its cause is worth remarking on, whereas even the swiftest śrāvaka needs only three lifetimes; and the bodhisattva's own lineage and lot are fixed and determinate in a way a śrāvaka's are not. 7. The Terminal Fourth Dhyāna

The treatise works through no fewer than four rival etymologies for the concentration that produces wish-knowledge — as the "sign," "utmost point," and "dense thicket" marking the supreme grade of the fourth dhyāna (as a treetop marks a tree's own highest point); as the "posterior-limit" dhyāna, since among the three temporal limits only this concentration reaches the future, which no other cognition can touch; as the "not-yet-arrived-limit" dhyāna, by the same logic viewed from the future's own vantage; and, preferred in the end, simply as the "terminal" dhyāna bridging the present aggregates and the past-and-future beyond them. 8. The Nature of Wish-Knowledge

with respect to what kind of cognition it is, the masters of the ancient Abhidharma say it is conventional cognition alone; other masters say its nature comprises eight cognitions, excluding only the cognition of exhaustion and the cognition of non-arising — why is this? Because those two are not of the nature of view, whereas wish-knowledge is of the nature of view. According to this latter view, however, it is conventional cognition alone. With respect to whether it is conjoined with concentration, it is not conjoined with concentration, since it is solely contaminated. and takes as its object what falls within the three times as well as what is beyond time. With respect to whether it is wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral, it is wholesome, taking all three as its object. With respect to whether it is bound to the three realms or unbound, it is bound to the form realm alone, yet takes as its object what is bound to all three realms as well as what is unbound. With respect to whether it is a trainee's, a non-trainee's, or neither, it is neither a trainee's nor a non-trainee's, taking all three as its object. With respect to whether it is abandoned by seeing, by cultivation, or not abandoned at all, it is abandoned by cultivation, taking all three as its object. With respect to whether it takes names or meanings as its object, it takes both names and meanings as its object equally. With respect to whether it takes its own mental continuum, another's, or neither as its object, it takes all three equally as its object. With respect to where it arises, it arises in the desire realm, not in the form or formless realms; within the desire realm, only in the human destiny, not elsewhere; within the human destiny, only on the three continents, not on the northern continent; and on all three continents, it is able to arise in both male and female bodies alike. The Venerable Ghoṣavarman states: it is able to arise only in Jambudvīpa, not on the other continents, and only in a male body, not a female one — why is this? Because wish-knowledge is so excellent that it must necessarily depend upon a body of superior, keen faculties, and the bodies of the other two continents, as well as the female body, are all comparatively feeble. According to this latter view, however, the first account is to be preferred, since the male and female bodies of all three continents are alike of superior, keen faculties, all being able to attain mastery of mind and mastery in concentration. Were it not so, the sūtra ought not to state that Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī, together with the five hundred nuns under her leadership, all at once relinquished their life-formations and entered parinirvāṇa on a single day — for, had they not attained the terminal fourth dhyāna, they could not have relinquished their life-formations; and, having attained this, how could they alone have been incapable of giving rise to wish-knowledge? Question: what kind of person is able to give rise to this cognition?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Citing Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī's own five hundred nuns as living counter-evidence against a doctrine that would bar women from the capacity underlying wish-knowledge is a genuinely consequential argumentative move: the treatise settles what could have remained an abstract dispute about gendered spiritual capacity by pointing to a documented case in its own canon, letting attested fact override an a priori claim about which bodies are supposedly too "feeble" to support the highest attainments.

9. Who Can Give Rise to Wish-Knowledge?

The fascicle closes by narrowing down exactly which kind of person can give rise to wish-knowledge: not an ordinary worldling, not a trainee, and, among the four kinds of arhat, only the one "liberated regardless of time" — since wish-knowledge demands both mastery in concentration and a mental continuum no longer gripped by affliction, and every other candidate satisfies at most one of these two conditions. One who has arrived through insight has the concentration but not the freedom from affliction's grip; one liberated dependent on time has the freedom from affliction's grip but not the mastery in concentration; only the rarest type possesses both together.

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: a noble one, not an ordinary worldling; among the noble ones, a non-trainee, not a trainee; and, among non-trainees, only the one liberated regardless of time, not the one liberated dependent on time. Why is this? Because only one who has attained mastery in concentration, and whose mental continuum is no longer held in the grip of affliction, is able to give rise to wish-knowledge,

諸異生及信勝解二事俱無。見至雖於定得自在。而相續為煩惱所持。時解脫雖相續不為煩惱所持。而於定不得自在。是故皆不能發起。願智唯有不時解脫於定得自在。及相續不為煩惱所持故。能起願智。

whereas ordinary worldlings and those liberated by conviction possess neither of these two qualities; one who has arrived through insight, though he has attained mastery in concentration, still has his mental continuum held in the grip of affliction; and the one liberated dependent on time, though his mental continuum is no longer held in the grip of affliction, has not yet attained mastery in concentration — and it is for these reasons that none of them is able to give rise to it. Only the one liberated regardless of time, having attained mastery in concentration and having a mental continuum no longer held in the grip of affliction, is able to give rise to wish-knowledge.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Narrowing an apparently miraculous power down to a strict two-condition test, and then showing that every other otherwise-accomplished type of person fails on exactly one of the two, is characteristic Abhidharma craftsmanship: it turns what could sound like an arbitrary restriction on who "deserves" a supernormal power into the logically forced consequence of two independently justified requirements, neither of which the excluded types happen to meet.
