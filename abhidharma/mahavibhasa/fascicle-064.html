Two Warriors, One Enemy

Fascicle 64 continues the Sentient-Being Nyāsa. It distinguishes three circumstances under which a noble one dies against only two for an ordinary person, catalogues the six aspects of the worldly path's own uninterrupted and liberation stages and exactly which ground each takes as its object across every level of defilement, and settles a genuine textual tension over whether the uninterrupted path or the liberation path truly cuts off a fetter — illustrated by two warriors overpowering a single enemy, two men catching a single thief, two men capturing a single snake. The fascicle closes with an exhaustive ledger tracking which of the four fruits of a śramaṇa contains each cutting-off's own exhaustion, refined from two sections up through fifteen. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 64 continues the Sentient-Being Nyāsa (結蘊第二中有情納息第三之二), covering how a noble one or an ordinary person dies with respect to their own defilement, the aspects and objects of the worldly path's own uninterrupted and liberation stages, and which of the four fruits of a śramaṇa “contains” the exhaustion of the fetters at every level of granularity, from two sections up through fifteen. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Three Ways to Die, Two Ways to Die

The treatise distinguishes three circumstances under which a noble one dies — entirely free of defilement, having entirely regressed, or only partially free of defilement — against just two for an ordinary person, who never dies only partially free. Multiple explanations are offered for the asymmetry: noble ones possess the uncontaminated absorption's own superior firmness, or a third power beyond mere affliction and karma — the power of the noble path, or of fixed karma held in check — that lets a partial freedom persist at death without contradiction, something an ordinary person's own weaker resources cannot sustain.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image of a fixed karma "held in check" by a noble one's own partial freedom from defilement is a genuinely subtle piece of causal reasoning: a karma certain to yield its result cannot simply fail to do so, and the treatise's answer — that partial freedom itself constitutes the very obstruction preventing that karma from ripening, without erasing the karma's own certainty — shows this tradition working out exactly how two things it holds independently true, karmic certainty and freedom from defilement, can coexist without either one collapsing into the other.

2. Six Aspects of the Worldly Path

The treatise catalogues six aspects that structure every worldly act of becoming free from defilement: the uninterrupted path's own three — coarseness, suffering, and obstruction, directed at what one is leaving behind — and the liberation path's own three — calm, wonder, and escape, directed at what one is moving toward. Two rival views on which liberation-aspect properly follows which uninterrupted-aspect are each set aside in favor of a looser, more permissive account: any of the three liberation aspects may follow any of the three uninterrupted aspects, according to the practitioner's own inclination.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the treatise ultimately refuses to fix a single correct pairing between the six aspects — despite two rival schools each proposing their own elegant, symmetric correspondence — is a small but telling methodological choice: sometimes this tradition's own inclination toward systematic completeness yields not to a a tidier taxonomy but to the plainer observation that practitioners' own minds simply do not all move through these six aspects in lockstep.

3. Which Ground Does Each Path Take as Object?

A genuine puzzle occupies this section: when the worldly path becomes free of the desire realm's own defilement, all nine uninterrupted paths take the desire realm itself as their object, while the nine liberation paths take the first absorption as theirs — yet a separate treatise, the Indriya-aggregate, states that contemplating the form realm can fully understand the desire realm's own cutting-off, suggesting the two paths' own objects might become confused. The treatise resolves this by insisting that confusion of object poses no actual obstruction, since the practitioner has already thoroughly mastered the entire route through extensive prior preparation — exactly as the path of seeing itself alternates between desire-realm and summit-of-existence objects without ever losing its way.

How, then, do the objects and aspects of these two paths not become confused with one another? Because the practitioner has already thoroughly cultivated and perfected his own preparatory practice along the various paths of becoming free of defilement — exactly as, within the path of seeing, taking the desire realm's own receptivities and cognitions as object, one subsequently takes the summit of existence's own receptivities and cognitions as object and becomes presently manifest, and, taking the summit of existence's own receptivities and cognitions as object, one subsequently takes the desire realm's own receptivities and cognitions as object and becomes presently manifest — although there is indeed a confusion of objects and aspects here, this is nonetheless incapable of obstructing or hindering the very task of direct realization, for what reason? Because the practitioner has already thoroughly cultivated and perfected his own preparatory practice along the various paths of direct realization. One asks: if this is so, how, then, is a further statement of the Indriya-aggregate to be reconciled — as when it asks, is there any case of contemplating the formless realm's own dharmas yet thereby fully understanding the desire realm? — answering, there is not, this referring to the full understanding of cutting-off? Is it not the case that a practitioner, about to become free of the desire realm's own defilement, would likewise first give rise to this very discriminating reflection: the desire realm is suffering, coarse, and an obstruction, while the formless realm is calm, wonderful, and an escape? The answer: although such a discriminating reflection does indeed arise, it is a distant, not a proximate, preparation — it is not the case that, after contemplating the formless realm, one is thereby immediately capable of drawing forth the path that becomes free of the desire realm's own defilement; only contemplating the form realm constitutes the proximate preparatory effort immediately capable of drawing forth that very path — and it is for this reason that these two statements do not actually contradict one another. There are further those who say: when becoming free of the desire realm's own defilement, all nine uninterrupted paths and all nine liberation paths take the first absorption as their object. One asks: if this is so, how is this soundly reconciled with what the Indriya-aggregate states — as when it asks, is there any case of contemplating the form realm's own dharmas yet thereby fully understanding the desire realm? — answering, there is, this referring to the full understanding of cutting-off — and there being, moreover, no fault of confusion between the two paths' own objects and aspects? How, then, can taking another ground as object be capable of freeing one from a different ground's own defilement? The answer: this, too, involves no fault — exactly as, when becoming free of the various kinds of defilement by means of the cognitions regarding cessation and the path, though cessation and the path are taken as object, it is suffering and its arising that are actually cut off, this case being exactly the same. The verdict: although each of these various explanations is capable of producing understanding and wisdom in one's disciples, the very first explanation given is the sounder one in principle — namely, that all nine uninterrupted paths take the desire realm as their object, while all nine liberation paths take the first absorption as their object. For what reason is this so? Because, when becoming free of the desire realm's own defilement by means of the worldly path, one is capable of becoming free of it only by loathing the lower and delighting in the higher — and, exactly as with becoming free of the desire realm's own defilement, so too should becoming free of each of the upper seven grounds' own defilement in turn be understood.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The insistence that confusion in what a path takes as its object need not obstruct that path's own function is a genuinely counterintuitive claim worth sitting with: ordinarily, one would expect clarity of object to be a precondition for effective practice, yet the treatise argues that sufficiently thorough prior training can render this kind of confusion harmless, the well-worn route itself compensating for whatever ambiguity remains in exactly what is being contemplated at a given moment.

9. Does the Uninterrupted or the Liberation Path Cut Off the Fetters?

A genuine textual tension is confronted directly: this very treatise speaks of fetters abandoned by dharma- and subsequent-cognition, seemingly crediting cognition, and therefore the liberation path, with the actual cutting-off, while the Jñāna-aggregate states flatly that fetters abandoned by seeing are cut off not by cognition but by receptivity, crediting the uninterrupted path instead. The treatise resolves the tension decisively in the uninterrupted path's own favor, then reinterprets its own earlier language — cognition standing in for receptivity, its own genuine companion — exactly as a king's ministers' own deeds are properly credited to the king they serve.
