The Silence That Won the Debate

Fascicle 15 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa's seventh part (雜蘊第一中智納息第二之七), completing the treatise's extended theory of language — name's own boundary, known fully only by a Buddha, and the three verbal bases that ground any statement about past, present, or future — before turning to a new, genuinely practical topic: the proper classification of questions and their answers. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation, closing with one of this treatise's most memorable stories — the outsider Śāṇḍilya's own confident thesis turning around to defeat him in silence against the arhat Vasula.

Fascicle 15 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa opened in Fascicle 9, now in its seventh part (之七). Terms established in earlier fascicles, such as name-body, phrase-body, and letter-body, recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Is a Name Bound to the Speaker's Language, or the Speaker's Own Realm?

Fascicle 15 continues the theory of language with a genuinely intricate cosmological puzzle: when a being from one meditative realm speaks the language native to another, does the name produced belong to the realm of the language itself, or to the realm of the speaker's own body? Two rival positions are worked through in exhaustive combination across every realm and dhyāna.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The verdict's own reasoning — better to say the higher dhyānas simply lack names of their own than to posit an unspeakable name that serves no purpose — is a small but telling methodological principle running throughout this treatise: positing an entity that can never, even in principle, do any work is treated as worse than denying the entity outright, a kind of ontological parsimony this school applies even to something as abstract as language itself.

2. Sentient, Unappropriated, Neutral: The Technical Profile of a Name

Name receives the same formal classification treatment given to every other technical category in this treatise: sentient rather than non-sentient, unappropriated, homogeneous-outflow rather than nourished or ripening-produced, and morally indeterminate — before a genuinely interesting question about ownership: does a name belong to the one who speaks it, or the one it describes?

Chinese Source

問名等為是有情數。為非有情數耶。答是有情數。問名等為有執受。為無執受耶。答無執受。問名等為是長養。為是等流。為是異熟生耶。答是等流。非長養非異熟生。問若名等非異熟生。契經云何通。如說。佛告阿難。我亦說。名從業生是業果。答名亦是業增上果故作如是說。謂作好業亦得好名。然非異熟。問名等為善。為不善。為無記耶。答無記。非造業者故。思起故。如四大種。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: are name and the rest included among the sentient, or the non-sentient? The answer: they are included among the sentient. One asks: are name and the rest appropriated, or non-appropriated? The answer: non-appropriated. One asks: are name and the rest nourished, homogeneous-outflow, or ripening-produced? The answer: they are homogeneous-outflow, neither nourished nor ripening-produced. One asks: if name and the rest are not ripening-produced, how is the discourses' own statement to be reconciled with this — namely, that the Buddha told Ānanda: "I too say that a name arises from karma, and is karma's own fruit"? The answer: a name is likewise a dominant result of karma, and so the discourses speak in this way — that is, one who performs good karma likewise obtains a good name, though this is not, strictly speaking, a ripening result. One asks: are name and the rest wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate? The answer: indeterminate, since they are not themselves the doer of any karma, but arise through volition, just as the four great elements do.

問誰成就名等。為能說者為所說耶。設爾何失。若能說者則阿羅漢。應成就染污法。離欲染者應成就不善法。異生應成就聖法。斷善根者應成就善法。以阿羅漢等亦說染污等法故。若所說者。則外事及無為亦應成就名等。以彼亦是所說法故。答唯能說者成就名等。問若爾後難善通。前難云何通。答阿羅漢等雖成就染污等名。

One asks: who possesses name and the rest — the one who speaks, or the one who is spoken of? Suppose either — what fault follows? If the one who speaks, then an arhat ought thereby to possess defiled dharmas, one free of the desire realm's defilement ought to possess unwholesome dharmas, an ordinary being ought to possess noble dharmas, and one who has cut off his own wholesome roots ought to possess wholesome dharmas — since arhats and the like do indeed speak of defiled dharmas and the rest. If the one who is spoken of, then external things and the unconditioned dharmas ought equally to possess name and the rest, since these too are things that can be spoken of. The answer: only the one who speaks possesses name and the rest. One asks: if that is so, the latter objection is well resolved — but how, then, is the former objection to be resolved? The answer: although arhats and the like do indeed possess names such as "defiled" and the rest,

而不成就染污等法。以染污等名皆是無覆無記法故。

they do not thereby possess the defiled dharmas themselves, since names such as "defiled" and the rest are all, in themselves, undefiled and indeterminate dharmas.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The resolution to the ownership puzzle is elegant: only the speaker possesses a name, never the one described, which is precisely why an arhat can speak the very word "defiled" without thereby possessing defilement — the name itself is always a neutral, indeterminate dharma regardless of how loaded its meaning is, so uttering a word describing something impure never transfers that impurity onto the speaker.

3. Only the Buddha Speaks a Whole Syllable in a Single Thought

A striking claim about the Buddha's own unique eloquence follows — his speech alone can produce a complete syllable within a single moment of mind, while even arhats and pratyekabuddhas require many such moments — before the treatise addresses whether every name, however strange ("a second head," "a sixth aggregate"), still genuinely displays meaning, and works out, syllable by syllable, exactly how much of reality a single name can and cannot capture.

Chinese Source

The claim that absurd or impossible names like "a second head" or "a sixth aggregate" still count as fully meaningful — displaying, quite precisely, the very notion of something that does not exist — is a sophisticated piece of philosophy of language: meaningfulness, on this account, never requires that what is named actually exist, only that the name successfully picks out a determinate concept, however fictional or impossible that concept turns out to be.

4. Name Versus Meaning: Which Is Greater?

A genuinely playful debate over quantity follows: is there more meaning in the world than there are names for it, or the reverse? One side points to the vast scope meaning covers across nearly every category of existence; the other cites an ancient treatise whose single meanings once carried a thousand names apiece, later trimmed down for practicality.

The eventual resolution — that a name is itself a species of meaning, since it too can be displayed and discussed by other names — dissolves what looked like a genuine quantitative dispute into a category realization: once "name" is recognized as a subset of "meaning" rather than something standing wholly outside it, the question of which is "more numerous" collapses, since the smaller category was never a genuine rival to the larger one that already contains it.

5. Can Meaning Itself Be Spoken? Why Asking for an Elephant Never Gets You a Horse

A deceptively simple question turns out to demand real philosophical care: if meaning could be directly spoken, saying "fire" ought to burn the tongue — yet if meaning cannot be spoken at all, how does asking for an elephant reliably produce an elephant rather than a horse? The treatise's answer locates the missing link in an ancient, collectively inherited convention linking sound to concept.

Chinese Source

The origin story offered here — that people, at the very dawn of the current cosmic age, provisionally agreed on names for elephants and the like, with the convention simply handed down ever since — is a strikingly deflationary, almost Saussurean account of linguistic reference for a text this ancient: words work not because they contain or transmit meaning directly, but because a community's arbitrary, historically inherited agreement lets a sound reliably summon the same concept for speaker and listener alike.

17. Why the Buddha Refused to Answer: The Barren Woman's Child

The fourth response type — setting a question aside entirely — receives its classic illustration: an outsider asks whether the world is eternal, and the Buddha refuses to answer either way, because the question smuggles in a false premise (a real, substantial self called "the world") that any direct answer, yes or no, would only reinforce, illustrated by the equally unanswerable question of whether a barren woman's child is a dutiful son.

The barren-woman's-child analogy is a masterclass in identifying a presupposition failure: the Buddha's refusal to answer "is the world eternal?" is not evasion or agnosticism, but a precise diagnosis that the question itself is malformed, since both "yes" and "no" would concede the existence of a persisting self the question quietly assumes — exactly as answering "he is filial" or "he is not filial" about a nonexistent child would concede that the child exists in the first place.

19. The Reversal: How Silence Achieves Victory

The story concludes with Śāṇḍilya's own reckoning: rather than accept a hollow victory his disciples still believe he won, he returns to Vasula, prostrates himself, and formally becomes his student — closing the fascicle's long investigation into questions and answers with the striking claim that even total silence, correctly understood, counts as a genuine, decisive answer.
