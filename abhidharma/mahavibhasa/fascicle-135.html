Magic, Māra, and the Measure of an Eon

Fascicle 135 examines magically-created transformation-bodies in exhaustive detail — including the story of Māra's punishment and his conjuring of the Buddha's likeness for the Venerable Upagupta, and the Venerable Mahākāśyapa's long wait inside Kukkuṭapāda Mountain — before turning to the intermediate state, the true nature of time, and the precise architecture of an eon. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. A New Debate: Is Magical Creation Even Real?

Fascicle 135 opens the second part of the Full-Vision Nyāsa by turning from meditative attainment to a stranger topic: magically-created transformation-bodies (化). The Dṛṣṭāntikas deny that anything magically created is genuinely real at all — a thing truly real, they argue, could never be called "created" — while the Great Virtuous One similarly reduces magical creation to a mere meditatively-produced appearance, no more substantial than a corpse animated by a ghost's own spell. Against both, the treatise sets out to show that what a skilled meditator creates is every bit as real as anything else.

Chinese Source

大種蘊第五中具見納息第三之二生欲界作色界化。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答欲止他義顯己義故。如譬喻者作如是說。諸所化物皆非實有。若實有者云何名化。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth Aggregate, the Great Elements: the Full-Vision Nyāsa, Chapter Three, Part Two. For one born in the desire realm who creates a form-realm magical transformation, and so on, in full. Question: why compose this treatise? Answer: in order to refute a rival doctrine and establish our own — for the Dṛṣṭāntikas say that everything magically created is not truly real, since, were it truly real, how could it be called "created"?

大德亦言。化非實有。是修所現。如屍起作鬼呪所為。為止如是所說意趣顯諸化事皆是實有。由此等緣故作斯論。

The Great Virtuous One likewise says that magical creation is not truly real, but is merely a meditatively-produced appearance — as when a corpse is made to rise and move by a ghost's own spell. It is in order to refute this very view, and to show that every magically-created thing is in fact genuinely real, that this treatise is composed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Insisting that a magical creation is genuinely real, not merely an appearance, is a quietly consequential metaphysical choice: it means the Abhidharma's entire physics of great elements and derived form must extend even into the strangest corner of the tradition's own imagination, refusing to treat a conjured elephant or a conjured Buddha as any less structurally real than an ordinary body — reality, on this account, is simply too democratic to make an exception for what merely astonishes us.

2. Eight Kinds of Transformed Body, and Up to Forty-Two Kinds of Transforming Mind

A precise taxonomy of transformation follows: a being born in the desire or form realm can create a transformation resembling either their own body or another's, and can create it in either their own realm or the other realm, yielding eight combinations in all — though this passage discusses only the two rarest and most difficult, a desire-realm being creating a form-realm body, and a form-realm being creating a desire-realm body, each resembling someone else. The transforming mind itself is then sorted with equal care, from a simple twofold division by realm up through an intricate forty-two-fold division crossing five grounds, three kinds of attainer, and three grades of skill.

Chinese Source

生欲界作色界化發色界語。彼身語色何大種所造。答色界繫。生色界作欲界化。發欲界語。彼身語色何大種所造。答欲界繫。以有漏所造色皆同分界地大種所造故。然所化身差別有八。謂生欲界作欲界化有二種。一似自身。二似他身。作色界化亦有此二。及生色界作色界化有二種。一似自身。二似他身。作欲界化亦有此二。是謂八。此中但依二種作論。謂生欲界作色界化。及生色界作欲界化似他身者。問何故此中但依二身作論。答彼作論者意欲爾故。隨彼意欲而造論。但令不違法相。不應責其所以。有說。唯此二身微細難見難了。是故偏說。有說。唯此二身最難現前。藉多加行方能起故。有說。此於世間難可信受。謂誰能作他界他身。有說。此二最為希有。謂能化作異界異身。是故偏說。諸變化心總有二種。一欲界繫二色界繫。或為五。謂欲界四靜慮繫。或為十二。則十二種能化者心。謂得初靜慮果者。乃至得第四靜慮果者。各有三種。何等為三。一異生。二有學。三無學。復有說三。一外法異生。二內法異生。三聖者。復有說三。謂三位得。一離染時得。謂離下染得靜慮時二得果時得。謂得無學果時。三練根時得。謂轉根作見至不動時。或分十四。謂欲界繫有四。則四靜慮果。初靜慮繫亦有四。如欲界說。第二靜慮繫有三。則上三靜慮果。第三靜慮繫有二。則上二靜慮果。第四靜慮繫有一。則第四靜慮果。以下地心羸劣故。不能於上地化。或分十五。謂五地繫諸變化心。各有上中下品。有說。得五地變化心者各有三類。謂異生等如前說。或復分為四十二種。謂前十四各有上中下品。有說。得十四種變化心者各有三類。

Ru-Yi Translation

For one born in the desire realm who creates a form-realm transformation and utters form-realm speech — by which great elements is this bodily and verbal form produced? Answer: by form-realm-bound great elements. For one born in the form realm who creates a desire-realm transformation and utters desire-realm speech — by which great elements is this produced? Answer: by desire-realm-bound great elements, since all contaminated derived form is produced by great elements of the same realm and ground. The transformed body itself, however, admits of eight distinct varieties — that is, for one born in the desire realm creating a desire-realm transformation, there are two kinds, one resembling one's own body, one resembling another's body; creating a form-realm transformation likewise has these same two kinds; and, for one born in the form realm creating a form-realm transformation, there are again two kinds; creating a desire-realm transformation likewise has these same two kinds — making eight in all. This passage, however, discusses only two of these — namely, one born in the desire realm creating a form-realm transformation, and one born in the form realm creating a desire-realm transformation, each specifically of the kind resembling another's body. Question: why does this passage discuss only these two bodies? Answer: it is simply the intention of the treatise's own author, that this order be followed — the treatise was composed following his own preference, and, so long as this does not contradict the proper form of the Dharma, this ought not to be demanded as an objection. Others say: only these two bodies are especially subtle and difficult to perceive, and hence are singled out for discussion. Others say: only these two bodies are hardest to bring into manifestation, requiring extensive preparatory effort before they can arise at all. Others say: this is hard for the ordinary world to credit — for who, it is asked, could possibly create another realm's own body? Others say: these two are the rarest of all, since they alone are capable of magically creating a body belonging to an entirely different realm — and it is for this reason that they alone are singled out for discussion. All transformation-minds, taken together, number two kinds in general — namely, desire-realm-bound and form-realm-bound — or, more finely, five kinds, namely desire-realm-bound and each of the four dhyāna-bound; or, still more finely, twelve kinds of transformation-creating minds, namely those who have attained the fruit of the first dhyāna up through the fourth dhyāna, each of three further kinds. What are these three? First, an ordinary being; second, a trainee; third, one beyond training. Others say there are three further kinds: first, an ordinary being outside the [Buddhist] teaching; second, an ordinary being within the teaching; third, a noble one. Others, further, say there are three kinds distinguished by when the attainment was obtained: first, obtained at the time of becoming free from a lower defilement, that is, obtaining dhyāna at the very moment of becoming free from the defilement below it; second, obtained at the time of attaining a fruit, that is, at the attainment of the fruit beyond training; third, obtained at the time of refining one's own faculties, that is, at the moment of converting to the unshakable type. Or, divided still further, into fourteen kinds: desire-realm-bound transformation-minds number four, namely the four dhyāna-fruits; first-dhyāna-bound likewise number four, as with the desire realm; second-dhyāna-bound number three, namely the upper three dhyāna-fruits; third-dhyāna-bound number two, namely the upper two dhyāna-fruits; fourth-dhyāna-bound numbers one, namely the fourth dhyāna-fruit itself — since a lower ground's own mind is too weak in power to create a transformation in any higher ground. Or, divided into fifteen: the transformation-minds bound to each of the five grounds each further admit of upper, middling, and lower grades. Others say: those who attain the transformation-mind of each of the five grounds likewise each fall into three classes, namely ordinary being and the rest, as explained above. Or, divided further still, into forty-two kinds: each of the fourteen kinds mentioned above further admits of upper, middling, and lower grades. Others say: each of the fourteen kinds of transformation-mind likewise falls into three further classes,

謂異生等如前說。

— namely ordinary being and the rest, as explained above.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the treatise bothers to work out a forty-two-fold classification for minds capable of magically creating illusions is a telling measure of how seriously this tradition takes even its most fantastical material: nothing here is treated as mere legend to be gestured at, but as a real phenomenon deserving the same taxonomic rigor already lavished on ordinary perception and karma.

3. How Many Senses Does a Transformed Body Touch?

A fine-grained question asks how many sense-fields a transformed body itself belongs to: a desire-realm transformation touches all four — form, smell, taste, and touch — while a form-realm transformation touches only form and touch, form-realm beings themselves lacking any experience of smell or taste. A rival view worries that a transformed body possessing smell and taste would imply its creator thereby comes to "possess" those sense-fields as well, resolved by the charming comparison to a person's own clothing and perfume: present on the body, yet never technically "possessed" by it.

Chinese Source

諸作欲界化。彼身還似欲界有情。諸作色界化。彼身還似色界有情。問所作化身幾處所攝。答若生欲界作欲界化。自身他身皆四處攝。謂色香味觸。作色界化。自身他身皆二處攝。謂色觸。若生色界作色界化。自身他身皆二處攝。作欲界化。自身他身皆四處攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

For any being who creates a desire-realm transformation, that transformed body resembles a desire-realm being; for any being who creates a form-realm transformation, that transformed body resembles a form-realm being. Question: to how many sense-fields does the transformed body itself belong? Answer: if one born in the desire realm creates a desire-realm transformation, both the version resembling one's own body and the version resembling another's belong to all four sense-fields — form, smell, taste, and touch. Creating a form-realm transformation, both versions belong to only two sense-fields — form and touch. If one born in the form realm creates a form-realm transformation, both versions belong to only two sense-fields; creating a desire-realm transformation, both versions belong to all four sense-fields —

如前說。有說。若作他身則四處攝。若作自身唯二處攝。勿彼成就香味處故。如是說者。雖化香味無成就失。如人衣服嚴具花香。雖復在身而不成就。

— as already explained. Others say: if creating another's body, it belongs to all four sense-fields; if creating one's own body, it belongs to only two, lest one thereby come to "possess" the smell and taste sense-fields as well. According to this further account, though the transformation includes smell and taste, no fault of "possessing" them thereby follows — just as a person's own clothing, ornaments, and flowers or perfume, though present upon the body, are not thereby "possessed" by that person in the technical sense.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The clothing-and-perfume analogy quietly does more work than it first appears to: it establishes that mere physical proximity or association is never sufficient, on its own, for the technical relation of "possession" in this system — a distinction with consequences reaching well beyond the present puzzle about magical bodies, into every case where something merely accompanies a person without truly belonging to them.

4. Ranking Transformations: When Realm Outranks Skill, and Skill Outranks Realm

An elaborate ranking exercise compares transformations created via different combinations of dhyāna-level and target-realm, distinguishing two entirely separate axes of superiority — a transformation's own "functioning," meaning how wide a range of levels it can move between, and its "realm," meaning the intrinsic rank of the realm it belongs to — with meticulous case-by-case verdicts on which axis wins when they pull in opposite directions, extended systematically across every pairing of dhyāna-levels and every possible birthplace.

Chinese Source

問若生欲界作欲界化初靜慮果。作色界化初靜慮果。如是二種誰劣誰勝。答此二運轉等無差別。然色界者界勝故勝。問若生欲界作欲界化第二靜慮果。作色界化初靜慮果。如是二種誰劣誰勝。答欲界者運轉勝。以彼從欲界。乃至第二靜慮能往還故。色界者界勝以色界法勝欲界故。問若生欲界作欲界化初靜慮果。作色界化第二靜慮果。如是二種誰劣誰勝。答色界者二事勝。一界勝。二運轉勝。如說。生欲界作欲色界化。初第二靜慮果。相對辨勝劣。如是生欲界作欲色界化。初第三靜慮果。初第四靜慮果。第二第三靜慮果。第二第四靜慮果。第三第四靜慮果。相對辨勝劣。准前問答如理應思。如生欲界。如是生初靜慮。生第二靜慮。生第三靜慮。隨其所應當思廣說問如生初靜慮者。有能發起身語表心故。令所化身作往來等種種作用。上諸靜慮無如是心。化主生彼所化。云何有往來等用。答如生初靜慮。以發起表心。令化身轉作往來等用。如是生上諸靜慮。亦以初靜慮發起表心。令所化身起往來等用。如眼識等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: if one born in the desire realm creates a desire-realm transformation by means of the fruit of the first dhyāna, and also creates a form-realm transformation by means of that same fruit of the first dhyāna, which of these two is inferior, and which is superior? Answer: the two are equal in their own functioning, without any difference between them — yet the form-realm one is superior, since its own realm is superior. Question: if one born in the desire realm creates a desire-realm transformation by means of the fruit of the second dhyāna, and creates a form-realm transformation by means of the fruit of the first dhyāna, which of these two is inferior, and which is superior? Answer: the desire-realm one is superior in its own functioning, since it is able to range from the desire realm all the way up through the second dhyāna and back again; the form-realm one is superior in its own realm, since form-realm dharmas surpass those of the desire realm. Question: if one born in the desire realm creates a desire-realm transformation by means of the fruit of the first dhyāna, and creates a form-realm transformation by means of the fruit of the second dhyāna, which of these two is inferior, and which is superior? Answer: the form-realm one is superior in both respects — first, in its own realm, and second, in its own functioning. As has been explained for one born in the desire realm creating desire-realm and form-realm transformations by means of the first and second dhyāna-fruits, comparing their relative superiority, so too, in exactly the same way, should this be worked out for the first and third dhyāna-fruits, the first and fourth, the second and third, the second and fourth, and the third and fourth, comparing their relative superiority in each case, reasoning by analogy with the pattern already given above. And, as with one born in the desire realm, so too, in the same way, for one born in the first dhyāna, the second dhyāna, or the third dhyāna — this should be thought through and explained in full, according to what is fitting in each case. Question: for one born in the first dhyāna, there exists a mind capable of giving rise to bodily and verbal expressive consciousness, thereby enabling the transformed body to perform such actions as coming and going — but the higher dhyānas possess no mind of this kind; when the creator is born in one of these higher grounds, how, then, does his own transformed body come to possess such functions as coming and going? Answer: just as one born in the first dhyāna gives rise to the first dhyāna's own expressive mind, thereby enabling the transformed body to perform the functions of coming and going, so too, in the very same way, one born in any of the higher dhyānas likewise makes use of the first dhyāna's own expressive mind, thereby enabling the transformed body to perform these same functions — just as with the eye-consciousness and the rest [borrowing what a higher ground itself does not natively possess].

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Separating a transformation's raw versatility from the intrinsic dignity of the realm it belongs to, and then working out, case by case, which one wins when they conflict, reflects this tradition's characteristic refusal to let any single measure of "better" stand unexamined: even something as exotic as a magical body turns out to have at least two genuinely independent ways of being superior, neither one simply reducible to the other.

5. One Mind, One Creation — or One Mind, Many? A Verse and a Sūtra in Tension

A genuinely puzzling contradiction opens: does a single mind produce only a single transformation, or can it produce many at once? A cited verse — "when the creator speaks, all his creations speak" — seems to support one transformation per mind, while the Prajñapti-śāstra's own account of the penetration's arising seems to support the opposite. The resolution splits the difference by practitioner skill: several preparatory minds first "entreat" several transformations in advance, and a single triggering mind then sets them all speaking together — so that a beginner manages only one transformation per mind, while one who has fully perfected the penetrating wisdom can produce an entire army, all of a single kind or of several kinds at once, from one mind alone.

Chinese Source

有餘師說。諸所化。身無往來等種種作用。但默然住由化主力。令彼似有往來等事。如帝網戲非有現有。問化事起時。為必有依託方得現耶。為復不爾。有說。化事必有依託。謂必依於木石塊等。化主方能作所化事。有餘師說。若初起通者所起化事要有所假。若通慧滿者。無所依假能起化事。問為一心一化。為一心多化。若一心一化。經頌所說當云何通。如說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Other masters say: transformed bodies possess no genuine functions of coming and going, or any other action, whatsoever — they remain, in truth, entirely motionless, and it is solely through the creator's own power that they merely appear to have such functions as coming and going, exactly as Indra's own illusory net display is not truly present, yet appears to be present. Question: when a transformation-event arises, must it necessarily rely upon some physical support in order to come into being, or need it not? Some say: a transformation-event necessarily requires such a support — that is, it must depend upon wood, stone, clods of earth, or the like, before the creator is able to bring the transformation-event into being at all. Other masters say: for one just beginning to cultivate the supernormal penetration, whatever transformation-event he brings forth does indeed require something to be borrowed as its support; but for one who has already perfected the penetrating wisdom, no such support is required at all, and the transformation-event can be brought into being without borrowing anything whatsoever. Question: is it a single mind giving rise to a single transformation, or a single mind giving rise to many transformations at once? If it is a single mind giving rise to a single transformation, how is the verse cited by the sūtra to be reconciled? As it is said:

一化主語時　諸所化皆語一化主若默　諸化皆默然若一心多化。施設論說復云何通。如說。神境智證通云何加行。以何方便起神境智證通。答彼初業者習世俗定令極自在。極自在已起令現前。由現前故於神境通便能引發。從彼乃能隨起一化。起一化事尚爾許心。況復多耶有作是說。一心一化。問所引經頌當云何通。答先以多心祈多化語。後以一心令語俱發。前多心是轉。後一心隨轉。有餘師說。一心多化。問彼施設論當云何通。答若初起通者一心一化。若通慧滿者一心多化。問於一心中所起化事。為必同類亦異類耶。有說。必同類化。謂作象時不作馬等。有說。亦異類化。謂初起通者。一心但能作一類物。若通慧滿者。一心能作象等四軍。

"When the one creator-master speaks, all of his creations speak as well; when the one creator-master falls silent, all of his creations likewise fall silent." If it is a single mind giving rise to many transformations, how is the Prajñapti-śāstra's own statement to be reconciled? As it says: what is the preparatory practice for the supernormal power of manifestation-cognition? By what method does one give rise to this supernormal power of manifestation-cognition? Answer: the beginning practitioner first cultivates mundane concentration until it becomes utterly self-mastered; once utterly self-mastered, he brings it into manifestation, and, through this manifestation, the supernormal power of manifestation-cognition is thereby drawn forth. From this point, he is able to give rise, as he wishes, to a single transformation — and merely to produce a single transformation-event already requires this much mental effort, let alone to produce many at once! Some say: it is indeed a single mind giving rise to a single transformation. Question: then how is the cited sūtra verse to be reconciled? Answer: first, with several separate minds, one entreats several transformation-utterances in advance; afterward, with a single mind, one causes them all to speak together at once — the earlier several minds are the preparatory, "turning" minds, while the later single mind is the triggering, "following-turning" mind. Other masters say: it is indeed a single mind giving rise to many transformations. Question: then how is the Prajñapti-śāstra's own statement to be reconciled? Answer: for one just beginning to cultivate the penetration, it is a single mind giving rise to a single transformation; for one who has already perfected the penetrating wisdom, it is a single mind giving rise to many transformations at once. Question: within the transformation-events arising from a single mind, must they necessarily be of the same single kind, or can they also differ in kind from one another? Some say: they must necessarily be of a single kind — that is, when creating an elephant, one does not, at the same time, also create a horse and the rest. Others say: they can also differ in kind — that is, for one just beginning to cultivate the penetration, a single mind is capable of producing only a single kind of object; but for one who has already perfected the penetrating wisdom, a single mind is capable of producing, all at once, an entire fourfold army of elephants, horses, chariots, and infantry together.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Resolving an apparent contradiction between a verse and a technical treatise by distinguishing a preparatory, entreating mind from a later, triggering one is a small masterpiece of exegetical craftsmanship: rather than declaring one source wrong, the treatise finds the hidden two-stage structure that lets both be true at once, each simply describing a different moment in the very same act of creation.

6. Is There Magic Without Meditation? The Demon-Maidens Who Aged Themselves

A further question asks whether transformation-power can exist innately, by birth alone, without any cultivation at all — some deny it outright, as too weak to create anything genuinely new, capable only of superficial alteration; others affirm it, though limited to one's own body. The famous episode of Māra's three temptress-daughters, each conjuring a hundred maiden-bodies of every age to seduce the Bodhisattva before withdrawing in shame at their own sudden, self-inflicted old age, is offered as proof that birth-attained transformation, though modest, is genuinely real.

Chinese Source

問已知修所成化事。為亦有生得化耶。有說無。以生得勢用劣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: we already know of transformation-events accomplished through cultivation — is there also such a thing as a transformation attained by birth alone? Some say: there is not, since the power innate to a birth-attained transformation is far too weak in itself —

但能轉變令似異本。有說。亦有。然唯能作自身非餘。問若爾云何通經所說。如契經說。有三魔女各各化作多百女身。所謂童女產未產女。中女老女。其數各百。又自化身種種嚴飾。為惑媚故詣菩薩所。謂菩薩曰。可起沙門。我等今來願相適事。菩薩不受。尋令彼身作衰老形羞慚而退。答即依魔女異熟身上。化作前說多百女身。

A second, mirror-image version of the same Prajñapti-śāstra passage is then given, in which the Buddha and his transformation-body speak in unison rather than in alternation, and where the disciples' own limitation lies not in perceptible sequence but in an inability to make one figure speak while the other falls silent — a limitation traced, once again, to the same underlying cause: the Buddha's complete, effortless mastery over mind and concentration, entering and exiting at will without ever losing his object of attention, where his disciples must labor and repeatedly let go of theirs.

Chinese Source

彼復翻說佛於一時化作化佛。身真金色相好莊嚴。世尊語時所化便默。所化語時世尊便默。弟子一時作化弟子。剃除鬚髮著僧伽胝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Others give the very same account in reverse: the Buddha, on one occasion, magically created a transformation-Buddha, his body a true golden color, adorned with all the major and minor marks. When the World-Honored One spoke, the transformation fell silent; when the transformation spoke, the World-Honored One fell silent. His disciples, likewise, on one occasion, each created a transformation-disciple, with shaven head and beard, clad in the monastic robe.

弟子語時所化亦語。所化語時弟子亦語。問諸大聲聞亦能如是。世尊於此有何不共。答佛於心定俱得自在。入出速疾不捨所緣。能以一心發於二語。謂自及化。於中欲令語者便語。不令語者便默。聲聞心定非極自在。入出遲緩數捨所緣。雖能一心發於二語。謂自及化。然於其中欲令一語第二亦語。欲令一默第二亦默。不能令其一默一語。又佛世尊於諸智境皆得自在非諸聲聞。故佛此中亦有不共。

When the disciple spoke, his own transformation spoke as well; when the transformation spoke, the disciple himself spoke as well. Question: can the great disciples, too, do this same thing? What, then, is uniquely the Buddha's own here, incomparable to them? Answer: the Buddha, with respect to both mind and concentration, has attained complete mastery in every respect, entering and emerging with such extraordinary speed, never once abandoning his own object of attention, that he is able, with a single mind, to give rise to two utterances at once, his own and his transformation's — and, of these, whichever he wishes to speak, speaks, and whichever he wishes to fall silent, falls silent. His disciples' own minds and concentration are not utterly self-mastered in this same way, entering and emerging more slowly, repeatedly abandoning their own object of attention — and though they, too, can give rise, with a single mind, to two utterances, their own and their transformation's, when they wish one to speak, the second speaks along with it as well, and when they wish one to fall silent, the second falls silent along with it as well — they are unable to cause one to fall silent while the other alone speaks. Moreover, the World-Honored Buddha has attained complete mastery over every object of wisdom whatsoever, in a way his disciples have not — and it is on this account, too, that the Buddha possesses something here that is entirely incomparable to them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the tradition preserves two competing versions of the very same miracle-account, in direct tension with one another, rather than silently harmonizing them into one, is itself a small act of intellectual honesty: the treatise would rather transmit the genuine state of its own sources, contradictions included, than manufacture a false consistency neither version, on its own, actually supports.

12. A Body Made of Mind: The Buddha's Answer to Ānanda's Challenge

Ānanda, hearing the Buddha boast of reaching the Brahmā-heaven instantly by means of a "mind-made body," playfully protests that even the disciples can manage this feat — until the Buddha reveals the far more startling claim beneath it: that he can reach the Brahmā-heaven just as instantly using his own ordinary, coarse, father-and-mother-born body, without any recourse to concentration or supernormal penetration at all, illustrated by the image of iron growing light and pliant in a blazing furnace, ready to be shaped however the smith wills.

Chinese Source

契經中說。佛告阿難。我之神力能以意所成身倏爾至於梵世。阿難白佛。何其劣哉。此事聲聞亦能。世尊何足自歎。謂所化作名意所成身。聲聞亦能以此至於梵世。佛若爾者有何不共。世尊頗能離神通力。以麁大種父母生身。於倏忽間至梵世不。世尊告曰。此我亦能。阿難復言。此事實難。願說譬喻令我信解。佛言。諦聽。如世間鐵。或餅或團置炎鑪中。漸輕漸軟漸調漸淨。隨意所為。如是如來身隨心轉。繫心於身作輕軟等想。身隨心力成輕軟等事。由能繫心相續勢力。令所繫身運轉隨意。此中有說。佛盡智時得欲界無覆無記。未曾得心心所法。由此勢力不入靜慮不起神通。纔發心時則能舉身至色究竟。何況梵世。有作是說。世尊爾時起緣風心令身轉舉。有餘師說。起緣空心能令佛身所往無礙。有言。佛意則說此身名意所成。由隨意力成輕軟等運轉事故。離定通力能運此身。至梵世故與聲聞別。有言。佛說意所成身則所化身不假定通能至梵世。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sūtra says: the Buddha told Ānanda, "My own supernormal power is such that I am able, by means of a mind-made body, to arrive at the Brahmā-heaven in but a single instant." Ānanda said to the Buddha, "How ordinary a thing this is! Even the disciples are capable of this — why should the World-Honored One boast of such a feat?" — meaning that what is thus created is called a "mind-made body," and that the disciples, too, are able to reach the Brahmā-heaven by means of it, so that, if this were all there was to it, what would remain uniquely the Buddha's own, incomparable to them? The World-Honored One then said, "Setting aside the power of supernormal penetration entirely, and using instead this very coarse, great-elements body born of father and mother — are they able to reach the Brahmā-heaven in but a single instant?" The World-Honored One then answered his own question: "This too I myself am capable of." Ānanda said further, "This is truly a difficult thing to credit — I beg you to give a simile, that I may come to understand and believe it." The Buddha said, "Listen carefully. It is like ordinary iron, whether shaped into a cake or a lump, placed within a blazing furnace — it grows gradually lighter, gradually softer, gradually more pliant, gradually purer, and can then be shaped however one wishes. Just so, the Tathāgata's own body turns according to the mind — fixing the mind upon the body, and giving rise to the perception of lightness and pliancy, the body, following the mind's own power, correspondingly becomes light and pliant, and so on. It is through this very capacity to fix the mind, sustained by the continuous power of concentration, that the body thus fixed upon is made to move exactly as the mind wills." On this point, some say: at the very moment the Buddha attained the wisdom of the exhaustion of outflows, he obtained a neutral, karmically-indeterminate desire-realm mind he had never before attained — a mind together with its accompanying mental factors — and it is through the sheer power of this attainment that, without so much as entering dhyāna or giving rise to any supernormal penetration at all, the very instant he simply directs his own mind, his entire body is at once lifted up all the way to Akaniṣṭha, let alone merely to the Brahmā-heaven. Others say: at that moment, the World-Honored One gave rise to a mind taking wind as its own object, thereby causing his own body to be lifted up and set into motion. Other masters say: he gave rise to a mind taking space as its own object, thereby enabling the Buddha's own body to travel without any obstruction whatsoever. Others say: what the Buddha himself calls a "mind-made" thing refers to this very same body, since, through the power of mind alone, it accomplishes lightness, pliancy, and the rest, and, entirely apart from the power of concentration or supernormal penetration, is able to carry this body all the way to the Brahmā-heaven. Others say: what the Buddha describes as a "mind-made body" means precisely that the transformation-body he himself creates has no need to rely on concentration or supernormal penetration at all in order to reach the Brahmā-heaven —

與聲聞別。有言。佛說由意勢通令所化身速至梵世。此捷疾力二乘等無。故佛依此自顯殊勝。

— this being exactly what distinguishes it from his disciples' own case. Others say: what the Buddha describes here is that, through the power inherent in the very intention of the mind, the transformation-body he creates is made to arrive at the Brahmā-heaven with extraordinary speed — and this particular swiftness of power is something neither of the two vehicles [the śrāvakas nor the pratyekabuddhas] possess at all — and it is on this basis, referring to this very point, that the Buddha reveals his own distinguishing excellence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ānanda's own gentle jab — "how ordinary a thing this is" — turns out to be exactly the right rhetorical setup for the Buddha's real point: the truly extraordinary claim was never the mind-made body every disciple can already produce, but the Buddha's own capacity to make his literal, physical, birth-body behave the very same way, collapsing a distinction his own disciples still depend upon.

13. A Transformation-Mind's Own Four-Cornered Fate

A precise technical puzzle closes out the magical-creation material proper: at the very final moment of becoming free from a given level's defilement, a transformation-mind bound to some other level may be simultaneously cut off yet not lost, lost yet not cut off, both at once, or neither — a tetralemma worked out exhaustively across every combination of realm and dhyāna, and then extended, by the same pattern, to the corresponding final moment of freedom from each successive dhyāna's own defilement in turn.

Chinese Source

問頗有變化心一剎那頃斷而不得。得而不斷俱不俱耶。答有。謂離欲染最後無間道時。於變化心有此四句。斷而不得者。謂欲界繫上三靜慮果。得而不斷者。謂初靜慮繫初靜慮果。亦斷亦得者。謂欲界繫初靜慮果。不斷不得者。謂初靜慮繫上三靜慮果。第二靜慮繫上三靜慮果。第三靜慮繫上二靜慮果。第四靜慮繫第四靜慮果。如離欲界染最後無間道作四句。如是離初靜慮染最後無間道。乃至離第三靜慮染最後無間道。隨其所應各有四句。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: is there such a thing as a transformation-mind that, within a single instant, is simultaneously cut off yet not lost, or lost yet not cut off, or both cut off and lost, or neither cut off nor lost? Answer: yes, there is — namely, at the very final uninterrupted path of becoming free from the desire realm's own defilement, the transformation-mind admits of exactly these four possibilities. "Cut off, but not lost": this refers to the desire-realm-bound fruits belonging to the upper three dhyānas. "Lost, but not cut off": this refers to the first-dhyāna-bound fruit belonging to the first dhyāna itself. "Both cut off and lost": this refers to the desire-realm-bound fruit belonging to the first dhyāna. "Neither cut off nor lost": this refers to the first-dhyāna-bound fruits belonging to the upper three dhyānas, the second-dhyāna-bound fruits belonging to the upper three dhyānas, the third-dhyāna-bound fruits belonging to the upper two dhyānas, and the fourth-dhyāna-bound fruit belonging to the fourth dhyāna itself. Just as this fourfold pattern holds for the final uninterrupted path of becoming free from the desire realm's own defilement, so too, in exactly the same way, should it be worked out for the final uninterrupted path of becoming free from the first dhyāna's own defilement, up through the final uninterrupted path of becoming free from the third dhyāna's own defilement, each accordingly admitting its own corresponding fourfold pattern.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even a magical, illusion-producing capacity is subject to the same rigorous being-cut-off-versus-being-lost bookkeeping already applied throughout this treatise to afflictions and attainments shows how thoroughly this tradition refuses to grant any special metaphysical exemption to the exotic: a transformation-mind's own fate is tracked with exactly the same four-cornered precision as anything else a practitioner might gain or lose along the path.

14. The Abhidharma of a Magic Trick: Elements, Senses, Mind, and Whose Will Moves It

The treatise now settles the underlying metaphysics of magical creation in systematic order: it does possess great elements and derived form, since no manifest form exists apart from them; birth-attained creation, unlike its cultivation-attained counterpart, spans a wider range of sense-fields; and, most subtly, a magical creation possesses no mind of its own when created by cultivation or when depicting another's body, yet does possess mind when attained by birth or when depicting one's own body — with every such creation, in every case, ultimately turned and animated by its creator's own will, whether that creator's mind belongs to the transformation itself or stands entirely apart from it.

Chinese Source

化當言有大種無大種耶。答當言有大種。無有現色離大種故。化當言有所造色。無所造色耶。答當言有所造色。化有二種。一修得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should a magical creation be said to have great elements, or not? Answer: it should be said to have great elements, since no manifest form ever exists apart from the great elements. Should a magical creation be said to have derived form, or not? Answer: it should be said to have derived form. Magical creation, moreover, is of two kinds — one attained through cultivation,

二生得。修得化若欲界繫四處攝。若色界繫二處攝。生得化若欲界繫九處攝。若色界繫七處攝。由如是法成化身故。化當言有心。無心耶。答當言無心。然化有二種。一修得此無心。二生得此有心。

— and one attained by birth. As for cultivation-attained magical creation: if desire-realm-bound, it falls under four sense-fields; if form-realm-bound, it falls under two sense-fields. As for birth-attained magical creation: if desire-realm-bound, it falls under nine sense-fields; if form-realm-bound, it falls under seven sense-fields — since it is by means of dharmas such as these that the transformed body is itself constituted. Should a magical creation be said to possess mind, or to lack mind? Answer: it should be said to lack mind. Yet magical creation is, in fact, of two kinds: one, attained through cultivation, which lacks mind; and one, attained by birth, which possesses mind.

此中說修得化非心依故。又有二種。一作他身此無心。二作自身此有心。此中說他身化非心依故。若變化他有情身者。如自身說。化當言誰心所轉耶。答當言化主。由化主心令有表故。然修得化由化主心轉。若生得化由自心轉。此中說修得化。又作他身由化主心轉。若作自身由自心轉。此中說他身化。

What is meant here is that cultivation-attained magical creation does not depend upon its own mind, but upon its creator's. There are, further, two kinds: one, creating another's body, which lacks mind; and one, creating one's own body, which possesses mind. What is meant here is that creating another's body does not depend upon its own mind, but upon its creator's. As for magically creating another sentient being's own body, this should be understood in exactly the same way as creating one's own body. Should a magical creation be said to be turned by whose mind? Answer: it should be said to be turned by the creator's own mind, since it is through the creator's own mind that expressive activity is given rise to within it. Yet a cultivation-attained magical creation is turned by the creator's own mind, while a birth-attained magical creation is turned by its own mind — this is what is meant here. And, as for creating another's body, this is turned by the creator's own mind, while, as for creating one's own body, this is turned by one's own mind — this, too, is what is meant here, with respect to creating another's own body.

問諸化皆滅耶。答修得化滅。生得化有滅不滅。謂天龍藥叉等自身化時。有異色等起。此於後時異色等滅。而自身在。又作他身者滅。作自身者有滅不滅。若變化他有情身者如自身說。問諸化所食誰腹中消。答此若化主所須。所宜食者即化主腹中消。若非化主所須所宜食者。如草木等聚置一處。若化他有情令飲食者。隨化主意有消不消。

Question: do all magical creations eventually cease? Answer: cultivation-attained magical creations do cease; birth-attained magical creations may or may not cease. That is, when gods, nāgas, yakṣas, and the like magically transform their own bodies, some altered coloration and the like arises, which later ceases, while the being's own underlying body itself remains; and, as for creating another's body, this ceases, while creating one's own body may or may not cease. As for magically creating another sentient being's own body, this should be understood in exactly the same way as one's own body. Question: whatever a magical creation eats — in whose stomach is it digested? Answer: if it is something the creator himself requires and finds fitting to eat, it is digested in the creator's own stomach; if it is something the creator does not himself require or find fitting to eat, it simply remains piled up in one place, like grass or wood. And, if one magically creates food or drink for another sentient being to consume, whether it is digested or not depends entirely upon the creator's own intention.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a magical creation can, depending on its type, either possess or lack a mind of its own is a genuinely subtle piece of metaphysics hiding inside what looks like a simple illusion: the very same conjured figure can be, in one technical sense, a mindless puppet moved entirely by another's will, and, in another, a being with a mind genuinely its own — the distinction turning not on how convincing the illusion looks from outside, but on the precise causal machinery quietly running underneath it.

15. Fire From Nowhere: Smoke, Flame, and a Monk Who Burns Without Ash

A cluster of vivid, almost theatrical questions follows: whether every magical creation eventually ceases, what becomes of food a transformation eats, and, most strikingly, by what means a transformed body produces smoke and flame at all — every answer converging on the same single principle, that the creator's own will governs every detail. This flows into the moving account of the Venerable Dravya-Mallaputra's own self-immolation by the fire-element absorption, leaving not the slightest trace of ash behind, explained in two competing ways depending on whether a left-behind transformation-body is held to exist — either the fire arose only after his parinirvāṇa upon a magically-created pyre, or his own body was consumed gradually, down to the very last hair's-breadth of his life-faculty, with parinirvāṇa and the fire's own final extinguishing occurring in the same instant.

Chinese Source

問化身由何出煙焰等。答由化主力。謂諸化主欲於是處起煙起焰。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: by what means does a transformed body come to emit smoke, flame, and the like? Answer: through the creator's own power — that is, whenever the creator wishes smoke or flame to arise in a given place,

起煙焰峯。起煙焰舍。則於是處煙焰等起。問由何化身有燒者有不燒者。復以何故有燒身不燒衣。燒衣不燒身。俱不俱耶。答由化主力。謂諸化者隨意所欲。或燒不燒。

— a smoke-and-flame-crowned peak arises there, or a smoke-and-flame-wreathed dwelling arises there, and, accordingly, smoke and flame and the rest arise in that place. Question: through what cause does one transformed body burn while another does not, and, further, why does one body burn while its own clothing does not, or its clothing burn while the body does not, or both together, or neither at all? Answer: through the creator's own power — that is, transformed beings, entirely as the creator wishes, either burn or do not burn.

如契經說。尊者達臘婆末羅子。以神通力上昇虛空火界焚身無餘灰燼。問彼尊者火焚身時。為涅槃前。為涅槃後答諸說有留化者。彼說涅槃後火起焚身。謂彼獲得心定自在將取滅度。神力昇空於虛空中。化作床座及種種薪。便以願力入火界定。纔發火已則般涅槃。

As the sūtra says: the Venerable Dravya-Mallaputra, through his own supernormal power, rose up into open space, and, by means of the fire-element, burned his own body without leaving the slightest ash or residue behind. Question: when that Venerable One's own body was thus burned by fire, was this before his parinirvāṇa, or after it? Answer: those who hold that there is such a thing as a left-behind transformation-body say that the fire arose to burn his body only after his parinirvāṇa — that is, having attained complete mastery over mind and concentration, and about to enter final extinction, he rose by his own spiritual power into open space, and there magically created a bed and seat, together with various kindling, and then, by the power of his own vow, entered the fire-element absorption; and, the very instant the fire had begun to burn, he entered parinirvāṇa —

由此焚身令無灰燼。諸說無留化者。彼說命未盡火起焚身。謂彼獲得心定自在。將欲滅度神力昇空入火等持。令身漸死隨無根處火起焚之。乃至最後唯有心命依處。如極細毛端許。乃入涅槃火亦隨滅。餘毛端量所不燒者由細難知。謂無灰燼。

— and it was on this very account that his body was burned away without leaving the slightest ash behind. Those who hold that there is no such thing as a left-behind transformation-body say instead that the fire arose to burn his body while his own life had not yet been exhausted — that is, having attained complete mastery over mind and concentration, and about to enter final extinction, he rose by his own spiritual power into open space, and there entered the fire-element absorption, causing his own body to die by gradual stages, the fire arising wherever no vital faculty any longer remained, and consuming it there, until, at the very last, only the life-faculty itself still remained, dependent upon something as fine as the tip of a single hair — and only then did he enter parinirvāṇa, whereupon the fire, too, immediately ceased. As for that portion no larger than the tip of a hair, which the fire did not consume, being so fine as to be difficult to perceive, it is simply said that no ash remained at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image of a body consumed so completely, and so precisely in step with its own dying, that not even ash remains is this fascicle's most powerful meditation on the difference between ordinary death and a death mastered through concentration: even the physical residue that every other death leaves behind is here dissolved entirely, as if the very last trace of embodiment were simply one more thing a perfected mind could choose to let go of.

16. From Magic to the Space Between Lives

The treatise pivots from magical creation to an entirely different, though structurally similar, topic: the intermediate-state being (中有), the subtle form said to persist briefly between one life's death and the next life's conception. Several reasons are offered for the pairing — both are equally subtle and hard to fully comprehend, both are described as bodies made purely of mind, and, most pointedly, both have been denied outright by rival schools, the Dṛṣṭāntikas denying magical creation and the Vibhajyavādins denying the intermediate state altogether — so that, having already established magical creation's reality, the treatise now turns to do the same for the intermediate state.

Chinese Source

中有。當言有大種無大種耶。乃至廣說。問何故次化明中有。答是作論者意欲爾故。乃至廣說。有說。化與中有俱是微細難可了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should the intermediate-state being be said to have great elements, or not? And so on, in full. Question: why, immediately following the discussion of magical creation, does the treatise turn next to explain the intermediate state? Answer: it is simply the intention of the treatise's own author, that this order be followed — and so on, in full. Others say: magical creation and the intermediate-state being are alike extraordinarily subtle, and alike difficult to fully comprehend.

有餘師說。此二俱是意所成身。有說。此二多諸誹謗。謂譬喻者說。化非實。分別論者撥無中有。前明化是實有。今明中有非無。

Other masters say: these two are alike bodies made purely of mind. Others say: these two are alike frequently subject to denial by rival schools — that is, the Dṛṣṭāntikas say that magical creation is not truly real, while the Vibhajyavādins deny outright that the intermediate state exists at all. Having first shown, above, that magical creation is genuinely real, the treatise now shows, in turn, that the intermediate state, too, is not non-existent —

以是故次化明中有。

— and it is for this reason that the intermediate state is discussed right after magical creation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Pairing the intermediate state with magical creation, rather than treating it as an isolated doctrinal claim, is a shrewd piece of argumentative architecture: having just spent an entire section proving that something as seemingly fantastical as a conjured illusion is genuinely real, the treatise arrives at the intermediate-state debate already carrying the reader's trust, the earlier victory quietly doing persuasive work for the harder case still to come.

17. The Intermediate-State Being's Own Brief Inventory

A compact technical inventory settles the intermediate-state being's own nature in exactly the same register applied to magical creation: it possesses great elements, since no manifest form exists apart from them; it possesses derived form, spanning nine sense-fields if desire-realm-bound or seven if form-realm-bound; and, unlike most varieties of magical creation, it does possess its own mind, turned and animated by that very mind's own power, giving rise to its own expressive activity rather than any outside creator's.

Chinese Source

中有當言有大種無大種耶。答當言有大種無有現色離大種故。中有當言有所造色。無所造色耶。答當言有所造色。欲界九處攝。色界七處攝。由如是法成彼身故。中有當言有心無心耶。答當言有心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should the intermediate-state being be said to have great elements, or not? Answer: it should be said to have great elements, since no manifest form ever exists apart from the great elements. Should the intermediate-state being be said to have derived form, or not? Answer: it should be said to have derived form — if desire-realm-bound, falling under nine sense-fields; if form-realm-bound, falling under seven sense-fields, since it is by means of dharmas such as these that that body is itself constituted. Should the intermediate-state being be said to possess mind, or to lack mind? Answer: it should be said to possess mind.

中有當言誰心所轉耶。答當言自心。由自心力起表業故。中有義如結蘊廣說。

Should the intermediate-state being be said to be turned by whose mind? Answer: it should be said to be turned by its own mind, since it is through the power of its own mind that it gives rise to expressive activity. The meaning of the intermediate state is explained in full, exactly as in the Aggregation Nyāsa.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the intermediate-state being, unlike a magical creation, is granted a mind entirely its own marks the crucial difference between the two topics the treatise has just paired: a conjured illusion remains, in the end, an extension of its creator's will, but the being crossing from one life to the next is never merely an extension of anything — it is already, even in this briefest and most liminal of states, a genuinely independent center of experience.

18. Is Time Real on Its Own, or Just Another Name for Change?

A pivotal metaphysical debate opens: are time and conditioned formations genuinely distinct substances, formations impermanent while time itself remains permanent, as the Dṛṣṭāntikas and Vibhajyavādins hold — impermanent things merely passing through an unchanging time, the way fruit is shuffled between different containers, or a person moves from room to room? The treatise firmly rejects this picture: the substance of the three times is simply conditioned formations themselves, so that, since formations are impermanent, time itself must be impermanent as well, with no separate, unchanging container standing apart from what happens within it.

世名何法。答此增語所顯行。問何故作此論答為止他宗顯自宗故。

What dharma is meant by "time"? Answer: this is a designation referring to conditioned formations themselves. Question: why compose this treatise? Answer: in order to refute a rival doctrine, and to establish our own, correct doctrine.

謂譬喻者。分別論師。執世與行其體各別。行體無常。世體是常。

Namely, the Dṛṣṭāntikas and the Vibhajyavādin masters hold that time and conditioned formations are, in their own substance, entirely distinct from one another — the substance of formations being impermanent, while the substance of time itself is permanent —

— such that impermanent formations merely pass through a permanent time, exactly as fruits are moved about among various containers, or as a person passes through one dwelling after another. It is in order to refute this attachment, and to show that the substance of the three times is simply conditioned formations themselves — formations being impermanent, and time, therefore, likewise impermanent — that this treatise is composed, for reasons such as these.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Denying that time is a permanent container through which impermanent things merely pass is one of this treatise's most consequential metaphysical commitments: there is, on this account, no stage separate from the play performed upon it — time simply is the totality of conditioned things arising and perishing, so that asking what time would be like with nothing happening in it turns out, on this system's own terms, to be asking an empty question.

19. How Long Is an Eon? A Mountain, a Cloth, and a Hundred Years

The fascicle's closing arc turns to the eon itself, opening with the treatise's own guiding scripture: a bhikṣu asks the Buddha for the true measure of an eon, and receives the tradition's most famous simile in reply — a solid mountain of rock, one yojana in every dimension, brushed by a fine cloth from Kāśī but once every hundred years, would be worn entirely away before a single eon had run its course. The treatise then asks why only the coarser units — half-month, month, season, year — are named as constituting an eon, while finer ones like the instant or the day-and-night go unmentioned, resolving the omission as the coarse standing in, by implication, for every finer period accumulated beneath it.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

What dharma is meant by "an eon"? Answer: this is a designation referring to a half-month, a month, a season, or a year. Question: why compose this treatise? Answer: in order to explain a sūtra passage — for the sūtra says: there was a certain bhikṣu who came before the Buddha, bowed at his feet, and withdrew to one side, saying to the World-Honored One, "The Buddha constantly speaks of eons — what, exactly, is their measure?" The Buddha said, "Bhikṣu, the measure of an eon is vast and remote beyond anything a count of even a hundred thousand years could convey." The bhikṣu said further, "Is there a simile for it?" The World-Honored One said, "There is — let me now explain it to you. Suppose that, near a city, there stood a single unbroken mass of solid rock, its length, breadth, and height each a full yojana, and suppose that a fine cloth from Kāśī were brushed across it but once every hundred years — by the time that entire mountain had been worn away to nothing, that single eon would still not yet have come to its own end. Bhikṣu, you should know that you yourselves have, through the long night of time, already passed through eons numbering in the countless hundreds of thousands, in hell, among the animals, and among the hungry ghosts —"

"— and among humans and gods as well, undergoing every manner of intense suffering, turning through birth and death without any end yet in sight — how, then, can you remain so much at ease, and not seek liberation?" This sūtra passage is itself the very scripture on which this treatise depends; though it speaks of an eon, it does not analyze what the substance of an eon actually is — it is in order to now provide this very analysis that this treatise is composed. Question: why does this passage speak only of a half-month, a month, a season, and a year as constituting an eon, and not also of an instant, a lava, a muhūrta, or a day-and-night? Answer: it ought properly to have spoken of these as well, and its failure to do so should be understood as a matter left with some further, supplementary account. Others say: this passage cites the coarse in order to include the fine — that is, an instant and the rest are fine, while a half-month and the rest are coarse; in speaking of the coarse, the fine is already understood to have been spoken of as well, since it is by the accumulation of fine periods that a coarse period is itself constituted. Others say:

此中舉近攝遠。謂劫近為半月等所成。半月等復為剎那等成故。說近時亦已說遠。

— this passage cites the near in order to include the far — that is, an eon is near to being constituted by a half-month and the rest, while a half-month and the rest are themselves, in turn, constituted by an instant and the rest — and so, in speaking of the near period, the far period, too, is already understood to have been spoken of.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image of a mountain worn away by the lightest possible touch, repeated across unimaginable spans of time, does something no direct numerical statement of an eon's length ever could: it lets the listener feel, in the body, the sheer disproportion between a single human lifetime and the scale the tradition asks one to hold in mind, converting an abstract measurement into something closer to vertigo.

20. What Is an Eon Actually Made Of?

The eon's own underlying substance is then settled through the Prajñapti-śāstra's own cosmogonic account: at the very beginning of the current eon, people's own bodies emitted a constant light, extinguished by their craving for flavor, giving way to the world's very first sunrise and sunset — the origin, respectively, of "day" and "night," and, since an eon is nothing but an accumulation of days and nights, proof that its own substance is form itself, or, more precisely, the arising and perishing of the five aggregates across all three realms alike.

Chinese Source

劫體是何。有說。是色處。云何知然。如施設論說。劫初時人身光恒照。以貪味故光滅闇生。於是東方有日輪起。光明輝朗同於昔照。見已喜曰。天光來來。以天光來故名為晝。須臾未幾日輪西沒。闇起如先。見已歎言。天光沒沒。以天光沒故名為夜。由此證知劫體是色。劫體皆積晝夜成故。如是說者。晝夜等位無不皆是五蘊生滅。以此成劫劫體亦然。然劫既通三界時分。故用五蘊四蘊為性。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the substance of an eon? Some say: it is the form sense-field. How is this known? As the Prajñapti-śāstra says: at the very beginning of the eon, people's own bodies emitted a constant light of their own, and, on account of their own craving for flavor, this light was extinguished, and darkness arose. At this, a sun appeared in the eastern direction, its own brightness shining forth just as their former light had once done; seeing this, they rejoiced, saying, "The heavenly light has come, has come!" — and it is because the heavenly light had come that this time was called "day." Shortly afterward, the sun set in the west, and darkness arose once more, as before; seeing this, they lamented, saying, "The heavenly light is gone, is gone!" — and it is because the heavenly light had gone that this time was called "night." From this, it is proven that the substance of an eon is form, since an eon's own substance is entirely constituted by the accumulation of days and nights. According to this further account, every state of day and every state of night alike is nothing other than the arising and perishing of the five aggregates, and it is by this that an eon is itself constituted, its own substance likewise being so. Yet, since an eon spans time throughout all three realms alike, it takes the five aggregates, or, where form is absent, the four aggregates, as its own nature.

已說自性。所以今當說。何故名劫。劫是何義。答分別時分故名為劫。謂分別。剎那臘縛牟呼。栗多時分以成晝夜。分別晝夜時分以成半月。月時年分別半月等時分以成於劫。以劫是分別時分中極故得總名。聲論者言。分別位故說名為劫。所以者何。劫是分別有為行中究竟位故。

Having already explained its own nature, we should now explain why it is called an "eon" — what, exactly, is the meaning of the word "eon"? Answer: it is called an eon because it distinguishes periods of time — that is, it distinguishes an instant, a lava, and a muhūrta as periods constituting a day and night; it distinguishes a day-and-night as a period constituting a half-month; and it distinguishes a half-month, a month, and a year as periods constituting an eon — and, since an eon represents the very furthest extreme among all periods of time thus distinguished, it receives this name as its own, comprehensive designation. The grammarians say: it is called an eon because it distinguishes a stage — for why is this? An eon is the very culminating stage among all the distinctions made within conditioned formations.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Tracing the very origin of "day" and "night" to a moment when humanity's own inner light first failed is a striking piece of cosmological poetry hiding inside a technical definition: on this account, the sun did not create the difference between light and dark so much as replace a light people had already lost through their own craving — the sky's own daily rhythm standing in, ever after, for something the earliest humans once carried within themselves.

21. Eighty Intermediate Eons: The Precise Architecture of a Great Eon

A precise architecture then organizes the eon into nested units: twenty intermediate eons of decrease, increase, and mixed increase-and-decrease together make one formation eon, matched by twenty more of duration, twenty of destruction, and twenty of ensuing emptiness, for eighty intermediate eons in all constituting a single great eon — with a further, poignant footnote on which of these types lasts longest, measured against the slow decline of humanity's own diet from body-light to earth's own flavor to earth-cakes to forest vines to naturally-growing rice, and a note that a Buddha appears in the world only during a decreasing eon, a wheel-turning king only during a purely increasing one, and a solitary-realized buddha only during the mixed, mid-range kind.

Ru-Yi Translation

There are three kinds of eon: the intermediate eon, the formation-and-destruction eon, and the great eon. The intermediate eon, in turn, has three kinds: the decreasing eon, the increasing eon, and the increasing-and-decreasing eon. "Decreasing" refers to human lifespan declining from an immeasurable span down to a mere ten years; "increasing" refers to human lifespan increasing from ten years up to eighty thousand years; "increasing-and-decreasing" refers to human lifespan increasing from ten years up to eighty thousand years, and then decreasing again from eighty thousand years back down to ten years. Among these, there is one decreasing eon, one increasing eon, and eighteen increasing-and-decreasing eons, together making twenty intermediate eons, during which the world takes twenty intermediate eons to form, and, once formed, twenty intermediate eons more to endure — this combined span is called the formation eon. The world then takes twenty intermediate eons to be destroyed, and, once destroyed, twenty intermediate eons more to remain empty — this combined span is called the destruction eon. All eighty intermediate eons together are called a great eon. Within the twenty intermediate eons of formation-and-duration, the first is purely decreasing, the last is purely increasing, and the eighteen in between are each both increasing and decreasing at once. Question: of these three, which lasts the longest? Some say: the decreasing eon lasts longest, the increasing eon is of middling length, and the increasing-and-decreasing eon is briefest of all — for, considering how long the span was during which people's bodies still emitted their own light, compared to how brief the span has been since that light was lost even up to now; how long the span was during which people fed upon the flavor of the earth, compared to how brief the span has been since that flavor was lost even up to now; how long the span was during which people fed upon earth-cakes, compared to how brief the span has been since then; how long the span was during which people fed upon forest vines, compared to how brief the span has been since then; how long the span was during which people fed upon naturally-growing rice, compared to how brief the span has been since then, even up to the present day — it is on this basis that the decreasing eon is held to last the longest of all. According to this further account, the first, purely decreasing eon, the last, purely increasing eon, and the eighteen increasing-and-decreasing eons in between — all twenty of these intermediate eons are, in fact, equal to one another in their own measure; it is only during a decreasing eon that a Buddha ever appears in the world.

於唯增時輪王出世。於增減時獨覺出世。

During a purely increasing eon, a wheel-turning king appears in the world; during an increasing-and-decreasing eon, a solitary-realized buddha appears in the world.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a Buddha's own appearance in the world is tied specifically to a decreasing eon — a world getting worse, not better — is a quietly important structural fact about this entire cosmology: liberation, on this account, is not something that arrives when conditions have finally become favorable enough, but precisely when they have deteriorated far enough that beings are ready to hear what a Buddha has to say.

22. How the Gods Tell Night From Day

The fascicle's final puzzle asks how the desire-realm gods, dwelling above any sun or moon, ever know night from day at all — answered not through any celestial body but through the desire realm's own floral rhythm: when the padma lotus closes and the utpala opens, birdsong grows sparse, a cool wind rises, play gives way to drowsiness, marking night; and when the utpala closes and the padma opens, birdsong swells, a gentle breeze stirs, and delight in play returns, marking day.

Answer: they are known by corresponding signs — that is, in that heaven, whenever the lotus called padma closes and the lotus called utpala opens, the birds grow sparse in their song, a cool wind rises swiftly, delight in play grows scant, and fondness for sleep increases — this is known as the time called "night." Ending an entire fascicle that has ranged from Māra's humiliation to the precise architecture of cosmic time on this small, gentle image — two flowers taking turns opening and closing to tell the gods when to play and when to rest — is a lovely tonal choice: after so much vastness and so much technical machinery, the text's very last image is intimate enough to fit inside a single garden.
