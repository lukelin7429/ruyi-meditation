The Nyāsa of Cognition Opens

Fascicle 196 finishes the Nyāsa of Perception with a detailed accounting of which latent afflictions underlie feeling conjoined with, and dissociated from, views and doubt, followed by an intricate survey of concurrent arising across the six sense-doors. It then opens the Nyāsa of Cognition (智納息) — the fourth Nyāsa of the Views Aggregate — distinguishing five senses of the term "thing," and working through tetralemmas on comprehending, being fully known, aversion, separation, and the causal necessity that governs even the mindless attainment of cessation. This page carries Ru‑Yi's complete translation.

Milestone: partway through this fascicle, the Nyāsa of Perception (想納息), which opened in fascicle 195, closes, and the treatise opens the Nyāsa of Cognition (智納息) — the fourth Nyāsa of the Views Aggregate (見蘊第八).

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 196 is dense with the treatise's recurring five-category scheme (abandoned by seeing suffering, origin, cessation, the path, and by cultivation) and its distinction between underlying increase by way of being an affliction's object versus by way of conjunction. Terms established across fascicles 93–195 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. How Many Latent Afflictions Underlie Feeling Conjoined With Views

The treatise singles out views and doubt for special treatment, since these two alone, among every affliction, are mutually dissociated from one another yet both take all four noble truths as their object and both extend to being pervasive — a distinction craving, aversion, conceit, and even ignorance itself cannot claim. It then works out, category by category, exactly which latent afflictions underlie and increase in feeling conjoined with views, distinguishing underlying increase by way of being an affliction's object from underlying increase by way of conjunction.

2. Feeling Dissociated From Views

The mirror case: which latent afflictions underlie feeling that is not itself conjoined with a view, worked out across the five categories of what is abandoned by seeing suffering, origin, cessation, the path, and by cultivation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Restricting this whole apparatus to views and doubt only, on the ground that these two alone are mutually dissociated while both spanning all four truths and pervasive scope, is a reminder that the treatise's categories are never arbitrary conveniences — each one is licensed by an explicit structural feature that sets it apart from every neighboring case, including ignorance, which despite its own broad reach is disqualified simply because it is conjoined with everything and therefore never distinctively "superior" anywhere.

4. Cause, Path, and Dependent Origination; Concurrent Arising Across the Six Sense-Doors

A brief detour into classification: how many elements, sense-fields, and aggregates subsume cause, the eightfold path, and the twelve links of dependent origination. This opens into an intricate accounting of which dharmas, concurrently arisen at each of the six sense-doors, are conjoined with or dissociated from perception, feeling, and mind — closing the Nyāsa of Perception.

Chinese Source

6. Comprehended and Fully Known: A Tetralemma

Cognition and forbearance are carefully distinguished: cognition can be comprehended, since it has the nature of true knowing, but does not itself cut off afflictions; forbearance cuts off afflictions and so is fully knowable, yet — arising as it does together with the very doubt it is meant to overcome — does not yet count as true cognition, and so cannot be comprehended.

Separating "can be turned away from with aversion" from "can actually be separated from" turns out to matter precisely because they can come apart in both directions: a cognition can inspire aversion without cutting off anything, and a forbearance can cut off an affliction while its object — cessation, the path — is something to be delighted in, not turned from at all. Because there is no such thing as an immediately preceding condition that takes its fruit at one time and gives its fruit at another — if it takes its fruit at this time, then it gives its fruit at this very same time. There are those who explain it thus: it is that the later dharma, before reaching the stage of having already arisen, does not serve as the immediately preceding condition for the earlier dharma, and only once it has reached that stage does it so serve. Question: is it not then the case that the forbearance regarding the cognition of the dharma of suffering, before reaching the stage of having already arisen, would also serve as the immediately preceding condition for the supreme mundane dharma? Answer: at that time, although it does serve as what immediately precedes the supreme mundane dharma, it is not yet the immediately preceding condition; only once it has reached the stage of having already arisen is it called both what immediately precedes and the immediately preceding condition. Just as with the immediately preceding and the immediately preceding condition, so too with the immediately preceding continuity and the continuity itself. The remaining meaning is explained at length in the Cognition Section of the Miscellaneous Aggregation Chapter.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The thought experiment is unusually vivid for this treatise: no sentient being, no medicinal herb, no spell, not even a buddha or an arhat who has reached the ultimate, could hold a meditator inside the attainment of cessation one moment past when the immediately preceding condition is satisfied. This is momentary causal succession treated not as a convenient description but as a metaphysical guarantee that not even a buddha's will could override.

Fascicle 196 Complete

This closes fascicle 196 of Ru‑Yi's translation. The fascicle opens by finishing the Nyāsa of Perception: a detailed, category-by-category accounting of which latent afflictions underlie and increase in feeling conjoined with views, feeling dissociated from views, feeling conjoined with doubt, and feeling dissociated from doubt — singling out views and doubt as the two afflictions uniquely suited to this treatment — followed by the subsumption of cause, path, and dependent origination under the elements, sense-fields, and aggregates, and an intricate survey of concurrent arising across the six sense-doors. The Nyāsa of Cognition (智納息) then opens, fixing five senses of the recurring term "thing" before working through a sequence of tetralemmas: what can be comprehended against what can be fully known, turning away with aversion against actually being separated from an affliction, and what is cultivated as an aversion-practice against both. A debate over the very intrinsic nature of aversion — non-craving, or wisdom, or neither — leads into the fascicle's final topic, causal necessity: how many of the six causes are meant when a dharma is said never, at any time, to fail as a cause, and why the same is not said of the immediately preceding condition, resolved through the striking case of a meditator held for days inside the attainment of cessation, whom nothing in the universe could prevent from emerging the instant conditions are met. Fascicle 197 continues within the Nyāsa of Cognition.
