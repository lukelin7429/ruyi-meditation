Nine Kinds of Full Understanding

Fascicle 62 concludes the arhat-regression debate — mapping exactly where regression occurs, distinguishing six kinds of arhat along a single nine-grade scale of faculty running from the regression-prone up through the pratyekabuddha and the Buddha, and returning to the arhat Gotika's own six-time fall to settle his classification once and for all. It then opens a new inquiry into the nine kinds of full understanding, tracking eight different names a single act of cutting off the fetters accumulates across the entire path of seeing and cultivation, and explaining, through seven independent arguments, why only the fruits of non-returner and arhatship ever gather every prior cutting-off into one. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 62 concludes the arhat-regression debate that opened in fascicle 60, then opens a new inquiry within the Single-Class-of-Conduct Nyāsa (結蘊第二中一行納息第二之七): the nine kinds of “full understanding” (遍知, parijñā) — the technical name for the completed abandonment of the fetters at each successive stage of the path. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Where Regression Occurs, and Who Is Prone to It

The treatise pins down regression's own precise scope: it occurs only within the desire realm, and only within the human destiny — the desire realm's own gods are exempt because they lack certain specific conditions conducive to it, and any noble one reborn there was, without exception, already of sharp faculties before rebirth; the form and formless realms are exempt because their own merit is too firm; the three evil destinies are exempt because they never attain the noble path to begin with. A long, almost exhaustive series of paired contrasts follows — the faith-follower versus the self-reliant, the impulsive versus the deliberate, calm-abiding-led versus insight-led, and more than a dozen further pairs — each identifying the first member as regression-prone and the second as secure, before the treatise closes by observing that regression, once it occurs, typically corrects itself almost immediately, out of something close to shame.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four similes offered for how swiftly a lapsed practitioner corrects course — the startled walker glancing around for witnesses, the person flinching from a spark, the fastidious man washing off a speck of filth, the exhausted porter setting down an unbearable load — together paint regression less as a catastrophic collapse than as a brief, almost reflexive stumble that a well-formed disposition snaps back from almost automatically, which sits in productive tension with Gotika's own six-time fall discussed later in this same fascicle.

2. The Six Kinds of Arhat

The treatise introduces a sixfold taxonomy of arhats, running from the most fragile to the most secure: the regression-prone, who are bound to fall; the contemplative, who preemptively end their own life rather than risk falling; the guardian, who diligently protect their attainment; the stable, who neither regress nor advance; the capable-of-advancement, who can still refine themselves toward the highest tier; and the unshakeable, who either began there or arrived there through the refinement of their own faculty.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the contemplative kind of arhat is defined by suicide as a preventive measure against future regression is one of the starker facts in this entire translation project, and the treatise states it with complete matter-of-factness — a reminder that this taxonomy is drawn from lived accounts within the tradition, not built up from first principles, and that the tradition itself regarded the psychological stakes of possible regression as severe enough to produce this response.

3. Six Functions or Six Dispositions?

Two rival readings of the sixfold taxonomy are weighed: on one reading, the six names describe six distinct functions each kind of arhat necessarily performs, so a "regression-prone" arhat necessarily does regress; on the other, sounder reading, the six names describe six underlying dispositions, so a "regression-prone" arhat need not actually regress at all — the name merely specifies which disposition he would be regressing from, should regression occur. A parallel debate over whether these six dispositions map onto nine grades of faculty two-to-one or one-to-one is settled decisively: since no single person ever possesses two grades of faculty simultaneously, the mapping must be one-to-one.

The shift from "necessarily performs this act" to "would perform this act from this disposition, should the occasion arise" is a genuinely important philosophical move: it separates a person's stable, classifiable disposition from any particular episode of behavior, letting the taxonomy describe six kinds of character without committing the awkward claim that being classified as regression-prone somehow guarantees an eventual fall.

4. Nine Grades of Faculty

The corrected mapping runs the entire spiritual hierarchy along a single nine-grade scale of faculty, from lowest to highest: the regression-prone occupy the very lowest grade, ascending in strict sequence through the contemplative, the guardian, the stable, the capable-of-advancement, and the unshakeable arhat, before the scale continues past arhatship itself to place the pratyekabuddha at the eighth grade and the Tathāgata at the ninth and highest.

6. Regaining the Fetters, and What "Binding" Means

The treatise confirms that regressing from a path that cut off certain fetters causes those very fetters' binding to return, and works through a genuine tension this creates with the standard account of the uninterrupted and liberation paths: since only the uninterrupted path is technically capable of actually cutting off a fetter, and no one ever regresses while abiding within the uninterrupted path itself, how can regressing from the liberation path cause a fetter the uninterrupted path cut off to return? The resolution distinguishes a narrow, technical sense of "cutting off" from a broader sense that extends to the liberation path as well, since the uninterrupted path is the liberation path's own cause, and regressing from an effect is properly spoken of as regressing from its cause too.

7. Nine Kinds of Full Understanding

The fascicle turns to an entirely new topic: the treatise enumerates nine kinds of "full understanding" — the completed, technical exhaustion of the fetters at nine distinct milestones along the path, from the desire realm's own fetters abandoned by seeing suffering and its arising, through the five lower-fetters and the fetter of craving for form, up to the total exhaustion of every fetter whatsoever at arhatship. The treatise composes itself against three rival positions in turn: that full understanding does not truly exist at all, since unconditioned dharmas lack any real own-nature; that it is not limited to nine kinds, since every analytical cessation could equally be so named; and that it is of only a single, uniform kind, since every analytical cessation shares one and the same underlying substance.

11. Arhatship and the Two Nirvāṇas

The path's final stretch is traced with the same precision: becoming free of the form realm's own ninth and final grade of affliction earns the eighth full understanding, the exhaustion of craving for form; becoming free of the formless realm's own last grade, through the vajra-like absorption itself, earns the ninth and final full understanding — the exhaustion of every fetter whatsoever — together with the fruit of arhatship and nirvāṇa-with-residue, since the arhat's own body and mind still continue. Only when that same arhat's aggregates, elements, and sense-fields finally and entirely cease, never to resume, does this same cutting-off additionally earn the name nirvāṇa-without-residue.

12. The Four and Five Conditions

The treatise now explains precisely why only nine particular moments earn the name full understanding while every other moment of cutting-off does not: the first six, belonging to the path of seeing, require four conditions in full — extinguishing both the cause abandoned by an earlier truth and the cause abandoned by the truth just now seen simultaneously, becoming free of both the corresponding bindings simultaneously, attaining the uncontaminated possession of freedom from binding, and lacking the universally-pervading afflictions belonging to the very summit of existence — worked out receptivity by receptivity, cognition by cognition, showing exactly which of the four conditions is satisfied, and which remains absent, at every single one of the sixteen moments along the way.

Running the identical two-condition template seven separate times, with only the second condition's own content changing from pairing to pairing, is this fascicle's own version of the over-determination this translation project has now seen several times before: rather than picking the single most elegant explanation and stopping, the treatise demonstrates that non-returner and arhatship's own privileged status as "comprehensive gatherers" holds up under every plausible way of carving the afflictions in two, leaving no reading of the base text's own silence on stream-entry and once-returner unaccounted for.
