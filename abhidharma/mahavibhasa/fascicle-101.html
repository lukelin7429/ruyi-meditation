The King Who Wrote His Own Scripture

Fascicle 101 closes the birth-acquired and recollection-based knowledges with the caṇḍāla king who authored a proud brāhmaṇa's own scripts, then turns to time-bound and unshakeable mental liberation, and why five kinds of arhat share one name while a sixth stands alone. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 101 complete, 9 sections Source: Taishō T1545, vol. 27, fascicle 101 Topic: 智蘊第三 → 他心智納息 (the Knowledge Aggregate; the Mind-Reading-Knowledge Nyāsa, continued)

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 101 continues the Mind-Reading-Knowledge Nyāsa (他心智納息第三之三), closing the birth-acquired and recollection-based knowledges before turning, within this same nyāsa, to time-bound mental liberation (時解脫) and unshakeable mental liberation (不動心解脫). Terms established across fascicles 93–100 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The Wolf, the Ghost, and the Grudge That Would Not Die

Fascicle 101 opens with two haunting stories of birth-acquired mind-reading among animals and hungry ghosts: a wolf that has carried a five-hundred-lifetime grudge against a mother's own lineage tests her claim to have forgiven it, detects her lie, and kills her child anyway; a ghost possessing a dying woman confesses an identical, mutually inherited enmity, and is fooled no more easily. Both stories close the same way — the technical question of when, in what mental state, and through which consciousness this same knowledge operates — before the fascicle surveys which destiny's own birth-acquired knowledge can reach which others (hell knows only hell; animals know two destinies; ghosts, three; gods, five) and explains why humans alone possess none at all, being too well provided for by superior alternatives.

Ru-Yi Translation

The animal destiny, too, possesses a birth-acquired knowledge capable of knowing another's mind and the like. How is this to be known as so? Long ago, there was a certain woman who, having set her own child down in a certain place, went about her own other affairs. At that time, a wolf came and carried her own child away. A passerby, pursuing it, said to that same wolf: for what reason do you now carry off another's own child? The wolf thereupon replied, saying: this same child's mother, across five hundred past lifetimes, has constantly harmed my own children, and I, too, across five hundred past lifetimes, have constantly harmed her own children — this enmity, repaid again and again, has not yet, even now, come to rest; were she genuinely capable of relinquishing it, I, too, would relinquish this same child. The passerby then said to this same child's mother: if you cherish your own child, you ought to relinquish your own mind of enmity. The woman replied, saying: I have already relinquished it. The wolf, observing the woman's own intention, found that she had, in fact, relinquished no enmity at all,

The heavenly destiny, too, possesses a birth-acquired knowledge capable of knowing another's mind and the like, yet, being subtle, it is not separately discussed — the rest should be understood exactly as fully stated for the animal and hungry-ghost destinies. The human destiny possesses no birth-acquired knowledge capable of knowing another's mind and the like — for what reason is this so? The *Prajñaptiśāstra* states that the nāga king Airāvata, who abides well, in knowing the minds of the gods, does so through an inferential-comparison knowledge, not through a birth-acquired knowledge. The verdict: it ought to be said that, among the four destinies, the birth-acquired knowledge of each knows all five destinies, this being consistent with principle. One asks: the human destiny, too, ought to possess the kind of inherent, connatural recollection-born knowledge capable of knowing another's mind and the like — for what reason is this not discussed? The answer: this ought indeed to be so discussed, and that it is not so discussed here should be understood to be a case whose meaning has been left over for elsewhere. Further, it is because this is rare that it is not discussed —

謂人趣中得此智者極少有故而不說之。有作是說。本性念生智類。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The wolf's own careful test of the mother's sincerity — not simply killing the child outright, but first probing whether she has genuinely relinquished her own enmity, and only proceeding once her lie is detected — gives this fable a moral weight beyond simple animal cunning: it suggests a grim symmetry of accountability, in which even a creature acting from an ancient grudge still holds its own victim to a standard of honesty, and punishes the deception before it punishes the underlying debt.

2. The Conch-Voiced Dog, and a Ghost's Own Bitter Verse

The parallel apparatus for the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge opens with its own tetralemma, before three vivid birth-acquired recollections illustrate it across three more destinies: hell-beings recall, in anguish, having once heard and ignored a warning about desire's own calamities; a dog who was once a wandering ascetic named Dautiya directs his own former son to hidden treasure through recollected gestures; a hungry ghost's own bitter verse mourns watching another enjoy the very wealth he himself once amassed through lawful or unlawful means; and a god recalls, with joy, having once donated the Jetavana itself for the Buddha's own use. A further tetralemma then carefully distinguishes recollecting one's own past from knowing another's, and the specific knowledge each requires.

Does every recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge presently recollect and know every matter of former abodes? The answer: a tetralemma ought to be cast. Here, the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge pervades all three times, while presently recollecting and knowing every matter of former abodes exists solely in the present — yet this pervades both the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge and what is not the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge, and it is for this reason that there are four clauses. There is a recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge that is not a presently recollecting and knowing of every matter of former abodes — namely, the past and future recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge, since this possesses the characteristic of the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge, yet lacks the very function of presently recollecting and knowing every matter of former abodes — that is, with respect to the past, its own function has already ceased; with respect to the future, it does not yet possess any function at all. There is a presently recollecting and knowing of every matter of former abodes that is not the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge — namely, such cases as these: there is one who has attained the inherent, connatural recollection-born knowledge, or who has attained a birth-acquired knowledge of this same kind, capable of presently recollecting and knowing every matter of former abodes. As for the inherent, connatural recollection-born knowledge's own presently recollecting and knowing former-abode matters: this exists solely within the human destiny, since the remaining four destinies possess no such knowledge at all.

3. The Inherent, Connatural Recollection-Born Knowledge

A third kind of former-life knowledge is now defined: the inherent, connatural recollection-born knowledge, native to humans alone (never gods, animals, ghosts, or hell-beings), earning its name because mindfulness's own force predominates within an otherwise ordinary wisdom, exactly as the applications of mindfulness and subduing the perception of form are named for whichever factor happens to lead. Six competing theories propose which karma produces it — kind speech, good news, comforting the frightened, consoling the sorrowful, widening a dangerous road, or generous feeding — before the Vaibhāṣika verdict settles on something simpler and more universal: whoever never harms others and constantly benefits them is thereby spared a difficult gestation and a painful birth, and it is this same freedom from bodily suffering, not any single specific virtue, that leaves the mind clear enough to recollect its own past.

5. Ninety-One Kalpas of Unbroken Precepts

A genuinely delicate puzzle closes out the recollection apparatus: the Buddha's own claim to recall ninety-one kalpas of never once neglecting to give alms to the field of pure precepts — is this the ordinary recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge, or the rarer inherent, connatural kind, given that neither account, as previously described, ever mentioned reaching so vast a span? The resolution favors the recollection-of-former-abodes knowledge, with the Venerable Pārśva noting that any specific number invites endless further questioning, and offering instead several converging reasons why this particular span is singled out — it marks the point from which the seven buddhas are reckoned, from which the karma of the major and minor marks began, and from which the Buddha's own conduct grew permanently pure. A final puzzle asks whether the bodhisattva's own final life still possesses the inherent knowledge at all, resolving in favor of yes, continuously present since that very ninety-one-kalpa mark.

6. A New Topic: Time-Bound and Unshakeable Mental Liberation

An entirely new topic opens, distinguishing time-bound mental liberation from unshakeable mental liberation — refuting three rival positions in succession (that one belongs to the learner and the other to the non-learner; that one is contaminated and the other undefiled; that one is conditioned and the other unconditioned) before establishing that both liberations alike belong solely to the non-learner, are solely undefiled, and are solely conditioned. Both are defined through the specific arhat category that possesses them — the arhat liberated in time's own knowledge of exhaustion, or the unshakeable-dharma arhat's own knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising — together with the resolve associated with the non-learner's own right view.

7. Resolve: The One Mental Factor That Is Liberation Itself

A precise metaphysical claim anchors the entire topic: among every dharma that exists, only two things are ever, strictly speaking, the very own-nature of liberation — cessation through discernment, among the unconditioned, and resolve (a universal mental factor present in every mind whatsoever), among the conditioned. A careful cascade of distinctions follows — wrong resolve versus right resolve, contaminated versus undefiled right resolve, the learner's own resolve across the four approaches and three fruits versus the non-learner's own resolve belonging to arhatship itself, and finally the non-learner's own resolve split into time-bound and unshakeable mental liberation, each further divided into mind-liberation (freedom from craving) and wisdom-liberation (freedom from ignorance). That it ought to have been so stated, and was not, should be understood to carry a separate underlying intent — that is, it displays resolve in reliance upon the wholesome root: if, in reliance upon non-attachment, one's own mind is liberated from craving, this same associated resolve is named mind-liberation; if, in reliance upon non-delusion, one's own wisdom is liberated from ignorance, this same associated resolve is named wisdom-liberation — and it is for this reason that, here, both mind-liberations alike take the non-learner's own resolve as their own-nature. This is named the very own-nature of liberation, its own substance, own inherent nature, and own characteristic.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That resolve — an utterly ordinary mental factor present in literally every mind, wholesome or defiled, human or animal, awakened or utterly confused — turns out to be the very same thing that, in its most refined and specific form, constitutes an arhat's own liberation is a genuinely striking piece of metaphysical economy: liberation is not, on this account, some exotic new capacity that appears only at the end of the path, but the very same familiar mental factor every being already possesses, purified and directed until it becomes, at last, indistinguishable from freedom itself.

8. Six Kinds of Time, and the Freedom That Needs None of Them

A rich etymological account explains exactly what "time" means in "liberated in time": six specific dependencies — needing good clothing, good food, good bedding, a good dwelling, good dharma-teaching, and a good companion before liberation can occur at all. "Liberated beyond time" is then defined as freedom from every one of these same six dependencies, illustrated through six vivid, paired contrasts: even a discarded rag robe, even coarse food, even a stone bed in a humble place, even a noisy dwelling, even poorly suited instruction, even a difficult companion — none of these can delay the unshakeable arhat's own liberation, which arrives just as readily, and sometimes more readily, than it would under the most favorable circumstances imaginable.

9. Why Five Kinds Together, and One Kind Alone

A cascade of seven further distinctions explains why the preceding five kinds of arhat are jointly gathered under the single name "liberated in time," while the sixth, unshakeable-dharma arhat stands entirely alone: a narrow, small path (liberation within three lifetimes at most) against a vast, great one (up to three incalculable kalpas for a buddha); a feeble, intermittent effort against a strong, sustained one; a calm-abiding-predominant path against an insight-predominant one; a path swayed by circumstance against one immune to it; five inherent lineages against a single one; and, finally, simple numerical asymmetry, illustrated through six parallel images of scarcity and abundance — the wealthy few against the poor many, the fully accomplished few against the ordinary many, the wise few against the foolish many.
