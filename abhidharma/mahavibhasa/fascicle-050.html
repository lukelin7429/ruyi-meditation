The Bird's Shadow on the Water

Fascicle 50 opens two further taxonomies of affliction — the seven latent tendencies, whose etymology turns on a fish patiently tracking a bird's shadow across the open ocean, waiting for it to tire and fall, and the nine fetters, whose own five-views-into-three-and-two split rests partly on a grammatical-gender argument in the source language. A second parable of envy and stinginess as jailers, this time barring both escape from the evil destinies and entry into the human and heavenly ones, sits alongside Śakra's own question to the Buddha about why gods and asuras fight. The fascicle closes with a long, systematic ledger sorting every taxonomy given so far into unwholesome and indeterminate. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 50 continues the Unwholesome Nyāsa's fifth part (結蘊第二中不善納息第一之五), opening the seven latent tendencies and the nine fetters, then closing with a systematic ledger sorting every taxonomy given so far — three fetters through ninety-eight latent tendencies — into unwholesome and indeterminate. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The Seven Latent Tendencies Open

Fascicle 50 opens a new major taxonomy: the seven latent tendencies — desire-craving, hatred, existence-craving, conceit, ignorance, view, and doubt — defined as ninety-eight factors in total, the same underlying afflictions already met across earlier fascicles now sorted by a new organizing principle, dormant disposition rather than active affliction.

Ru-Yi Translation

There are seven latent tendencies — namely, the latent tendency of desire-craving, the latent tendency of hatred, the latent tendency of existence-craving, the latent tendency of conceit, the latent tendency of ignorance, the latent tendency of view, and the latent tendency of doubt. One asks: what is the inherent nature of these seven latent tendencies? The answer: they have ninety-eight factors as their inherent nature — that is, the latent tendencies of desire-craving and hatred each span the five sections of the desire realm, making ten factors; the latent tendency of existence-craving spans the five sections within each of the form and formless realms, making ten factors; the latent tendencies of conceit and ignorance each span the five sections within all three realms, making thirty factors; the latent tendency of view spans twelve within each of the three realms, making thirty-six factors; and the latent tendency of doubt spans the four sections within each of the three realms, making twelve factors — and it is on this account that these seven latent tendencies have ninety-eight factors as their inherent nature. Their inherent nature having now been explained, the reason for their name shall now be explained. One asks: why are they named latent tendencies? What is the meaning of ‘latent tendency’? The answer: the sense of subtlety, the sense of increasing, and the sense of following-and-binding — these are the meaning of ‘latent tendency.’ That the sense of subtlety is the meaning of latent tendency refers to the fact that the aspect of these seven — desire-craving and the rest — is itself especially subtle, exactly as seven ultimate atoms together compose a single subtle particle of matter. That the sense of increasing is the meaning of latent tendency refers to the fact that these seven — desire-craving and the rest — universally increase with respect to every subtle contaminated thing whatsoever, down to a single ultimate atom, or within the span of a single instant, these seven — desire-craving and the rest — all increase there. That the sense of following-and-binding is the meaning of latent tendency refers to the fact that it follows exactly as a bird's shadow follows it through the sky, or as a fish follows something through the water — a bird flying refers to a bird in flight; a fish swimming refers to a fish in water. Should a bird, relying on the strength of its own wings, seek to cross a great ocean, a fish within the water, skillfully reading the signs, might reason as follows: no bird in flight is capable of crossing this great ocean, save only the swift and mighty king of garuḍas —

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the treatise reaches for a bird's shadow crossing open water, with a fish beneath patiently waiting for the bird to tire, before it has even finished the etymology, signals how central the image of patient, hidden pursuit is to this particular technical term: a latent tendency's danger lies precisely in its willingness to wait, unlike the active afflictions already catalogued elsewhere, whose danger lies in their immediacy.

2. The Bird, the Fish, and the Meaning of “Latent Tendency”

The bird-and-fish parable completes: the fish, having correctly judged that no bird could outlast a crossing of the great ocean, patiently tracks its shadow until the bird tires and falls, only then swallowing it — latent tendencies, the treatise says, wait in exactly this way, manifesting at every opportunity for irrational attention. A long cascade of etymological pairings follows, the same three senses (subtlety, increasing, following-and-binding) reassigned again and again to different reference points — own-nature versus function versus possession, past versus present versus future, association versus dissociation — before a second, four-part etymology from the masters of the outer regions closes with the sesame-oil and wet-nurse similes.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sheer number of times the same three senses get reassigned to a new pair of reference points is itself a kind of argument: rather than picking the single correct axis along which subtlety, increasing, and binding apply, the treatise treats a term's meaning as something that can be genuinely overdetermined, true along several independent axes at once without any one of them being more fundamental than the rest.

3. Why Envy and Stinginess Aren’t Latent Tendencies

A systematic catalog of contrasts explains why envy and stinginess, despite resembling afflictions in every rough sense, never qualify as latent tendencies: latent tendencies are subtle where these two are coarse, light where these two are heavy, root afflictions where these two are mere outflow (envy from hatred, stinginess from desire-craving), and their habitual energy is as lasting as embers beneath burnt mountain timber, where envy and stinginess cool the instant the fire itself goes out.

4. The Nine Fetters Open, and Craving’s Three Faces

A further taxonomy: the fetters of craving, hatred, conceit, ignorance, view, grasping, doubt, envy, and stinginess, defined as one hundred factors. The treatise then explains why craving itself gets counted as one fetter, two latent tendencies, or three separate cravings depending entirely on the audience — sharp, middling, or dull faculties; beginning, accomplished, or advanced practice; a taste for brevity, elaboration, or both — before opening definitions of the hatred-fetter (restricted to sentient beings specifically, for four separate but converging reasons) and the seven kinds of conceit.
