Why the Buddha Called His Disciples Fools

Fascicle 16 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa's eighth part (雜蘊第一中智納息第二之八), first explaining why the Buddha, having permanently cut off every affliction and its residue, still sometimes spoke words merely resembling attachment or anger — and why he once rebuked his own disciples as "fools." The fascicle's second half opens one of the most important doctrines in Sarvāstivāda philosophy: the six causes, the systematic account of causation that will structure much of the treatise's remaining argument. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation, from the bundle of reeds illustrating mutual mental dependency to the poignant account of scripture vanishing on the very day the arhat Śāṇakavāsin died.

1. Why Did the Buddha Call His Disciples “Fools”? The Buddha's Total Freedom From Residual Habits

Fascicle 16 opens by explaining a genuinely startling canonical fact: the Buddha, having permanently cut off every trace of affliction and its residue, nonetheless sometimes speaks words that merely resemble attachment, aversion, conceit, or delusion — unlike arhats and pratyekabuddhas, who retain real leftover habits (Ananda's fondness for his own clan, Pindola's short temper with a river deity, Sariputra's flash of pride, Gavampati's compulsive overeating). Each of the Buddha's own seemingly affliction-tinged statements turns out to serve a precise pedagogical purpose.

[The topic reads:] "As the Buddha, the World-Honored One, once rebuked his disciples, calling them 'fools'" — and so on, as stated at length. One asks: why does the treatise take up this topic? The answer: in order to bring certainty to those in doubt. That is, the Buddha, the World-Honored One, has permanently cut off both attachment and aversion, remaining equally balanced toward the agreeable and the disagreeable alike, having uprooted the very root of contention and extinguished the very foundation of conceit, regarding every precious treasure as no different from a broken tile, his awakened illumination leaving no dharma whatsoever unperceived, and possessing no residual trace of anything resembling attachment, aversion, conceit, or the like, since every habitual residue of the afflictions has been permanently cut off in him — unlike pratyekabuddhas and śrāvakas, who, although they too have cut off the afflictions, still retain certain residual habits: the habit of craving-attachment, as with the Venerable Ānanda's own fondness for the Śākya clan; the habit of aversion, as with the Venerable Piṇḍola-Bhāradvāja, who once said to the deity of the Ganges, "little slave-girl, halt your flow — I now wish to cross"; the habit of conceit, as with the Venerable Śāriputra, who once flung away a gift of medicine; and the habit of delusion, as with the Venerable Gavāmpati, who, coughing before a meal and knowing that his previous food had not yet been digested — yet, not foreseeing the suffering that would follow — ate once again regardless. Although the World-Honored One himself possesses no residual habit of affliction at all, he does at times utter words that merely resemble attachment, aversion, conceit, or delusion. Words resembling conceit are exemplified by the World-Honored One's own statement, "I am the Tathāgata, worthy and fully awakened, accomplished in the ten powers, having attained the four fearlessnesses." Words resembling delusion are exemplified by the World-Honored One's own statement, "great king, where have you now come from?" or his telling Ānanda, "look and see whether it is raining," or "why is there such a loud clamor within the garden?" One might come to doubt: "the World-Honored One has already permanently cut off every habitual residue of the afflictions — how, then, can there still be such utterances merely resembling the afflictions?" — and it is in order to bring this very doubt to a settled resolution that the treatise takes up this topic, explaining the causes and conditions behind them, exactly as it explains this present statement calling them "fools." One asks: why does the Buddha utter words resembling attachment and the like? The answer: in order to protect the field of those he is converting, and to benefit them. That is, the World-Honored One's own words resembling attachment were spoken in order to set at ease, in body and mind, the monks whom Devadatta had led astray, and to remove their doubt — that is, Devadatta, out of greed for fame and gain, had already caused a schism in the Sangha, and, once the Venerable Śāriputra and Mahāmaudgalyāyana had gone and brought the deceived monks back, those monks felt the deepest shame, trembling in body and mind, and doubting: "having followed Devadatta, have we not thereby lost our own precepts?" Upon hearing the World-Honored One say, "welcome, monks — you have gone forth excellently, and are already complete in the precepts," both their trembling and their doubt were entirely removed; had the Buddha not spoken these words at that time, they would have been overcome with shame and distress, and died vomiting blood. Further, the World-Honored One's own words resembling aversion were spoken in order to break down that brahmin's own banner of arrogance — that is, the brahmin Ambāṣṭha, failing to reckon with his own mother's lowly station, harbored arrogant pride, and obstructed another's own going-forth, which would have led him to fall into an evil destiny; and it was through the Buddha's rebuke that his arrogant, obstinate mind was broken, so that, in his very next life, he was reborn in the heavens and there beheld the four noble truths. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction drawn here between genuine residual habit and merely resembling speech is one of the sharpest psychological claims in the whole treatise: a fire that has fully consumed its fuel, unlike an ordinary fire, leaves no ash or ember behind at all, and the Buddha's own eradication of affliction is held to work the same way — total, leaving nothing that could involuntarily leak out as habit, so that anything resembling affliction in his speech must instead be a fully deliberate, situationally calibrated choice, aimed with surgical precision at exactly the audience who needs to hear it.

2. Four Kinds of Disciples: Praised, Rebuked, Set Aside, Reached Through Another

The treatise catalogues the Buddha's own fourfold pedagogical repertoire — some disciples respond best to praise, some to rebuke, some are better left alone for the time being, and some are only reachable indirectly, through witnessing what happens to someone else — illustrated by the sheer scale of enlightenment his teaching produced: eighty thousand gods at the first turning of the wheel alone, and comparable multitudes whenever he taught a king, a deity-king, or his own son.

3. What Does “Fool” Actually Mean? Arising From Delusion or Manifesting It?

A genuinely careful semantic question follows: is someone called a “fool” because their very existence arises from past delusion, or because delusion is presently active in their mind right now? Both readings run into trouble — the first would apply the word to everyone equally (since craving, hatred, and pride are equally inherited causes), and the second seems to contradict scripture calling even arhats “fools” in places.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The resolution — that delusion alone, among all the afflictions, pervades absolutely everything, so that “fool” can be applied wherever ignorance manifests, even a residual, undefiled trace of it in an arhat — lets the treatise preserve scriptural passages that seem to insult even the fully liberated without actually contradicting their liberation: a textual variant is even proposed and defended (“depart, monk” rather than “depart, fool”) to remove one apparent contradiction outright, showing real philological care being brought to bear on a doctrinally sensitive passage.

4. Why the Rebuke: Not Following, Not Sustaining the Teaching

The treatise turns to its second major task: explaining exactly what these disciples did wrong to earn the rebuke. A careful six-fold distinction between “admonition” and “instruction” sets up the real charge — these disciples failed to practice the Buddha's teaching in accordance with its meaning, failed to follow it, and failed to sustain their practice continuously, rendering the entire noble path “empty” for them.

6. A New Topic: The Six Causes Refute No-Cause and Unequal-Cause Theories

The fascicle pivots to one of the single most important doctrines in all of Sarvastivada philosophy: the six causes — correspondence, co-existence, homogeneity, universal pervasiveness, ripening, and efficiency — introduced explicitly to refute outsiders who deny causation altogether or who posit some arbitrary, unbalanced first cause, and to give the four fruits of Buddhist causal theory a fully transparent structure, “as clear as an amalaka fruit held in the palm of one's hand.”

Others, again, maintain that dharmas each correspond only to their own nature, never to another's — they say as follows: mutual delight is the very meaning of "correspondence," and no dharma can mutually delight together with another dharma, in the way one's own nature could delight with itself. The Ābhidharmikas reply: it is the coming-together of two distinct things that can properly be called "correspondence" — there is no sense of correspondence within a single thing alone, nor is there any such thing as a self-nature delighting in itself, since the capacity to take an object and the object taken are themselves genuinely distinct. Others, again, maintain that a self-nature, with respect to itself, is neither correspondent nor non-correspondent — they say as follows: correspondence, properly speaking, requires uniting with something else; a self-nature, with respect to itself, has no "other" to unite with, and so cannot properly be called "correspondent"; yet mutual delight is the very meaning of correspondence, and since a self-nature does indeed delight deeply in itself, it cannot properly be called "non-correspondent" either.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image of a follower saying to mind, “whatever task you undertake, I undertake it as well,” captures something genuinely important about how this school understood the coordination between mind and everything that accompanies it: not identity, and not mere correlation, but a kind of loyal, structurally guaranteed companionship — bodily and verbal karma sharing mind's own fruit and ripening even though it can never share mind's own object or aspect the way a true mental factor does, a relationship of partial but real solidarity rather than full equality.

Fascicle 16 Complete

This closes fascicle 16 of Ru-Yi's translation, drawn from the treatise's true CBETA boundary — the repeated second title line that follows carries no further content in this fascicle and has simply been omitted. Fascicle 16 completed the investigation into why the Buddha, entirely free of affliction's residue, still speaks words resembling attachment or aversion for precise pedagogical reasons, and why he once rebuked his own disciples as "fools" — empty of the noble path's own fruit, like a barren woman's body, or a physician's medicine cast away in contempt. The fascicle's second half opened the six causes — correspondence, co-existence, homogeneity, universal pervasiveness, ripening, and efficiency — working through the first two in careful detail via the bundle of reeds, the joined cords, and the four great elements' own mutual causation. Fascicle 17 continues the same Knowledge Nyāsa, now in its ninth part (智納息第二之九), turning to which bodily and verbal karma follows mind's own course.
