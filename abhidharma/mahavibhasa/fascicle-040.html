Four Goddesses Aniruddha Could Not See as Impure

Fascicle 40 continues the Meaninglessness Nyāsa, giving the contemplation of unattractiveness its full step-by-step treatment — from a single corpse in a charnel ground to a whole earth filled with bone — before testing its own universal claim against the vivid story of the Venerable Aniruddha, who could not see four beautiful, shape-shifting goddesses as impure no matter how hard he tried. The fascicle closes with Mahāmaudgalyāyana's journey to the Brahmā-world to learn why even the gods cannot perceive the sixth, signless kind of noble one. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. What Face-to-Face Mindfulness Actually Looks Like

Fascicle 40 opens by finally spelling out, in concrete detail, what fascicle 39's own "face-to-face mindfulness" actually consists of: contemplating a corpse's own progression through livid discoloration, bloating, festering, and finally a scattered heap of bare bone, mindfulness fixed between the eyebrows where noble bliss is said to first arise and then gradually pervade the whole body. The treatise pauses to explain why this specific practice, the contemplation of unattractiveness (不淨觀), is singled out as face-to-face mindfulness rather than the equally canonical mindfulness of the breath — chiefly because desire and greed is the heaviest of the five hindrances, and once it is cut off, every other hindrance falls away together with it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ghoṣa's broader reading — that every properly-attentive mindfulness whatsoever deserves the name "face-to-face mindfulness," not the corpse-contemplation alone — is quietly overruled in favor of Kātyāyanīputra's narrower, more scripturally literal reading, and the reason given is revealing: the treatise consistently prefers to anchor a technical term to the specific practice a specific scriptural passage was actually describing, even when a more general reading would be philosophically tidier.

2. Three Practitioners, Three Stages

The treatise now maps out the architecture of this practice systematically: three types of practitioner (those who prefer the brief method, the expansive method, or a combination of both) each pass through three stages of cultivation — initial practice, already-ripened cultivation, and the culminating stage of transcending applied attention, where effort itself is no longer needed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Naming a final stage "transcending applied attention" is a small but significant admission built into the very structure of the practice: a technique that begins as effortful, deliberate visualization is explicitly designed to eventually outgrow its own effort, becoming a settled capacity of mind rather than a task still being performed — the method contains its own instructions for when to stop treating it as a method.

3. The Brief Method: Bone by Bone, From Foot to Skull

The brief method is described in exhaustive, almost clinical detail: the practitioner first visits an actual charnel ground to properly grasp the marks of a real corpse, then returns to his own seat and, through the power of resolute conviction, transfers those same marks onto his own body — working systematically from livid discoloration through to a jumbled heap of bones, and then through that heap of bones one bone at a time, from the feet up through the ankles, shins, knees, hips, spine, ribs, shoulders, arms, and finally the skull.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The instruction to return, again and again, to the actual charnel ground whenever the visualized image grows unclear is a quietly important methodological safeguard: the practice is never allowed to drift into pure fantasy detached from anything real, but must be continually re-anchored against an actual, directly witnessed corpse, exactly the kind of empirical grounding one would expect from a tradition this insistent on distinguishing genuine contemplation from mere imagination.

4. The Expansive Method: From One Body to the Whole Earth

The expansive method reverses the bone-by-bone order (skull first, feet last) and then, remarkably, scales outward — from one's own skeleton to a single bed, room, monastery, garden, village, region, kingdom, and finally the entire earth, filled edge to edge with bone, before contracting the same visualization back down again to a single body and a single bone. The combined method alternates repeatedly between full expansion and full contraction until the visualization becomes thoroughly ripened.

6. What Kind of Mental State Is This, Really?

A foundational question closes out the practice's technical description: what is the contemplation of unattractiveness actually made of, at bottom? Candidates include wisdom (since scripture speaks of it as a kind of contemplation) and weariness (since it takes weariness as its object) — but the treatise's own verdict settles on the wholesome root of non-greed, precisely because its defining function is to directly counteract greed, with wisdom entering the picture only as an accompanying, associated factor rather than as the practice's own essential core.

9. Two Kinds of Unattractiveness, and the Question of the Buddha's Own Body

The story's implications are worked out technically: since even Aniruddha could not contemplate every form in the desire realm as impure, yet the doctrine claims this contemplation can take the whole realm as its object, the treatise resolves the tension by noting that others — the Buddha, pratyekabuddhas, Śāriputra — are indeed capable of what Aniruddha was not. 10. A Technical Profile, and Why Only Form, Never Sound or Smell

An extensive Abhidharma-style checklist now catalogs this contemplation's precise technical coordinates — which mindfulness-foundation it belongs to, whether it counts as knowledge or concentration proper, which faculties it is conjoined with, how it relates to the three time-periods, whether it is attained through applied practice or through freedom from defilement, and whether it belongs to the mental ground alone or can occur within the five sense-consciousnesses as well. The treatise resolves an apparent tension with scripture's own talk of "seeing form with the eye": the five sense-consciousnesses merely serve as a gateway drawing forth mental consciousness, within which the contemplation itself actually occurs. A further puzzle then follows: does this contemplation take sound, scent, taste, and touch as its object too, or only visible form? 12. Is This a Distorted Perception? Five Attainments, and Śāriputra's Praise

A pointed objection tests the whole practice's own legitimacy: since it deliberately perceives what is not literally a heap of bones as a heap of bones, how can it avoid being classified as a distorted perception, a defect the tradition elsewhere treats seriously? The treatise's answer separates distortion of an object from distortion of one's own underlying disposition and intention — this practice may be inaccurate about its object, yet remains wholesome and genuinely effective. The discussion pivots to scripture's own five "directly-seen attainments," a graduated sequence from observing the body's impure constituents through progressively refining that observation down to bare, consciousness-inhabited bone, matched precisely to the hierarchy of noble attainment — the first two open to anyone, the third to stream-enterers and once-returners, the fourth to non-returners, the fifth to arhats alone. Śāriputra's own praise of the Buddha's attainment as unsurpassed is then explained: not because the Buddha observes more discrete objects, but because he alone can subdue every possible object without exception, exactly as Aniruddha's own earlier failure with the four goddesses demonstrated śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas cannot.

15. The Sixth, Unnamed Category — and Why the Brahmās Cannot See It

16. Why "Sixth," and Why "Signless"

The treatise closes by explaining its own terminology with characteristic precision: this is called the sixth signless-abiding not because signless-abiding itself comes in six varieties, but because it is the sixth kind of noble one in a sequence of seven — exactly as "the fifth" in a hunting story names only the animal actually killed fifth, not all the game caught before it. "Signless" itself is explained through the sheer speed and subtlety of the path of seeing: even a śrāvaka's own mind-reading power, pushed to its very limit, can track only the first two moments of another practitioner's progress through it before losing the thread entirely, a pratyekabuddha manages at most three or four, and only the Buddha's own mind-reading knowledge is capable of following the entire sequence, moment by moment, without any gap.
