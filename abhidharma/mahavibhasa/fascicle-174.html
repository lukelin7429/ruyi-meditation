The Five Non-Returners

Fascicle 174 marks a major milestone: it opens the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之一), the fourth and final nyāsa of the Concentration Aggregate, with the five types of non-returner named in the sūtras — parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state, upon rebirth, with effort, without effort, and the "upstream" type ascending to Akaniṣṭha. The fascicle ranks all five against one another, cites the Saṅgītiparyāya's formal definitions of each, traces the upstream type's own nine-heaven ascent in detail, and closes by multiplying the five types out to nearly thirteen thousand distinct varieties. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Five Kinds Become Seven: What the List Leaves Out

Fascicle 174 opens a genuine milestone: the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四), the fourth and final nyāsa of the Concentration Aggregate, beginning with the five types of non-returner named in the sūtras — parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state, upon rebirth, with effort, without effort, and the "upstream" type ascending to Akaniṣṭha. The treatise's very first move complicates the tidy fivefold list: two further cases, parinirvāṇa in this very life and the non-returner who goes to the formless realm, are not subsumed under the five at all, making the true, complete classification sevenfold rather than five — with two additional edge cases ("indeterminate parinirvāṇa" and "parinirvāṇa after a transitional rebirth") folded back into this sevenfold scheme rather than requiring an eighth or ninth category.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The opening image of a large vessel covering a small one, but never the reverse, is a simple but effective way to state a basic logical asymmetry: the sūtra's own five-item list is real and useful, but it was never meant to be exhaustive, and the treatise's very first task is teaching the reader not to mistake a convenient enumeration for a complete one.

2. Ranking the Five: Trading Off Duration Against Fetters Severed

The treatise systematically ranks every pair among the five (and seven) types of non-returner using two competing criteria: if two types have severed the same number of fetters, whichever spends less time doing so — and therefore suffers less — is superior; but if one type has held on longer while severing more fetters, that patience itself becomes the mark of superiority instead. Parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state wins on speed and reduced suffering; the upstream type going to Akaniṣṭha, by contrast, undergoes at minimum four separate existences of suffering before its own release.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This double standard for ranking — sometimes rewarding speed, sometimes rewarding patient thoroughness — is not actually inconsistent once the criterion is made explicit: the real, single measure of superiority is efficiency, the ratio of fetters severed to suffering endured, and the treatise switches which factor to praise only because it is holding the other factor constant for a fair comparison in each case.

3. The Saṅgītiparyāya's Five Definitions, One by One

The treatise now works through canonical definitions of each of the five types in turn, citing the Saṅgītiparyāya. Parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state receives an especially precise account: such a person passes, within the intermediate state alone and without ever reaching a new birth, through an unbroken sequence from an ordinary mind, to the mind of a trainee, to the mind of a non-trainee, to a final mind belonging to neither — a compression of the entire path into a single uninterrupted moment-sequence that, the text notes, no other noble one ever achieves in quite the same unbroken way.

4. The Upstream Type: A Ladder Through Nine Heavens

The upstream type going to Akaniṣṭha receives the fascicle's most elaborate treatment: a being who, after reaching the first dhyāna, deliberately regresses from the higher three, is reborn successively through the Brahmā-Assembly Heaven, Ābhāsvara, Śubhakṛtsna, and Bṛhatphala, and then climbs the five Pure Abodes one grade of mixed meditation at a time, all driven by an unextinguished craving for rebirth and existence itself that this very practice is simultaneously working to exhaust. The treatise then distinguishes three sub-patterns — fully transcending several heavens at once, transcending only some, or dying and being reborn at every single station along the way — and traces two final destinations, the pure abodes for the insight-oriented practitioner or the formless realm for the calm-oriented one.

5. Why the Intermediate State Only Works in the Form Realm

The treatise devotes an unusually long, almost obsessive list of reasons why parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state can occur only for beings dying out of the form realm's own defilement, never within the desire realm itself — the desire realm's intermediate-state body is too weak, its afflictions too coarse and entrenched, its own nature too chaotic and indeterminate to support the noble path's manifestation in so brief and fragile a vessel — before extending the same logic to explain why even a being who dies while already in the form realm cannot attain parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state that follows, since that would erase the very distinguishing condition that keeps the five types of non-returner from collapsing into confusion with one another.
