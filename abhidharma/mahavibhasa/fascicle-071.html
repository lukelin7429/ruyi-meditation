Eighteen Elements, Broadly Taught

Fascicle 71 opens the Ten Gates Nyāsa — the fourth and largest nyāsa of the Fetters Aggregate — with the eighteen elements: Śāriputra's fearless request that the Buddha teach both broadly and briefly, nine independent reasons why the Buddha taught elements, sense-fields, or aggregates depending on the listener, and a meticulous element-by-element definition turning throughout on the technical distinction between a same-class instance and its own counterpart-class. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Three Formats of Teaching: Elements, Processes, and Aggregates — Broad or Brief?

Opening the Ten Gates Nyāsa — the fourth and largest nyāsa of the Fetters Aggregate, spanning forty-two chapters from the twenty-two faculties to the ninety-eight latent tendencies — the treatise begins with the eighteen elements, noting that this teaching is itself relative: a brief teaching set against the Great Discourses, yet a broad teaching set against the twelve sense-fields, which is in turn broad against the five aggregates, which is broad against the single line that every sensation is suffering. Several rival accounts of what actually counts as broad or brief are canvassed before the treatise settles on its own: the Buddha first taught broadly as eighteen elements, then briefly as twelve sense-fields, then most briefly of all, excluding the unconditioned, as five aggregates.

Chinese Source

結蘊第二中十門納息第四之一二十二根。乃至九十八隨眠。如是四十二章及解章義既領會已。應廣分別。二十二根者。謂眼根耳根鼻根舌根身根女根男根命根意根樂根苦根喜根憂根捨根信根精進根念根定根慧根未知當知根已知根具知根。此廣分別。如後根蘊根納息中十八界者。謂眼界色界眼識界耳界聲界耳識界鼻界香界鼻識界舌界味界舌識界身界觸界身識界意界法界意識界。此界契經亦名略說亦名廣說。名略說者。對大記經如大譬喻大涅槃等。名廣說者。對處契經彼處契經。亦名略說亦名廣說。名略說者對界契經名廣說者。對蘊契經。彼蘊契經亦名略說亦名廣說。名略說者對處契經。名廣說者。對諸所有受皆是苦等經。彼諸所有受皆是苦等經。但名略說不名廣說。有作是說。此界契經等亦名略說亦名廣說。即依自說不對餘經。謂界經中廣說色心。略說心所故。彼處契經亦名略說亦名廣說。即依自說不對餘經。謂處經中廣說色略說心心所故。彼蘊契經亦名略說亦名廣說。

As for the twenty-two faculties: the eye-faculty, the ear-faculty, the nose-faculty, the tongue-faculty, the body-faculty, the female faculty, the male faculty, the life-faculty, the mind-faculty, the pleasure-faculty, the pain-faculty, the joy-faculty, the distress-faculty, the equanimity-faculty, the faith-faculty, the diligence-faculty, the mindfulness-faculty, the concentration-faculty, the wisdom-faculty, the faculty of 'I shall come to know,' the faculty of 'I already know,' and the faculty of one who fully knows — these shall be broadly analyzed later, within the Faculties Aggregate's own Faculties Nyāsa. As for the eighteen elements: the eye-element, the color-element, the eye-consciousness-element; the ear-element, the sound-element, the ear-consciousness-element; the nose-element, the smell-element, the nose-consciousness-element; the tongue-element, the taste-element, the tongue-consciousness-element; the body-element, the touch-element, the body-consciousness-element; the mind-element, the dharma-element, the mind-consciousness-element. This scripture on the elements is named both a brief teaching and a broad teaching: it is named a brief teaching when set against the great discourses, such as the Great Analogy Sutra, the Great Nirvāṇa Sutra, and the like; it is named a broad teaching when set against the scripture on the sense-fields. That scripture on the sense-fields is likewise named both a brief teaching and a broad teaching: it is named a brief teaching when set against the scripture on the elements; it is named a broad teaching when set against the scripture on the aggregates. That scripture on the aggregates is likewise named both a brief teaching and a broad teaching: it is named a brief teaching when set against the scripture on the sense-fields; it is named a broad teaching when set against the scripture stating that every sensation whatsoever is suffering, and the like. That scripture stating that every sensation whatsoever is suffering, and the like, is named only a brief teaching, never a broad one. There are those who say: this scripture on the elements and the rest are likewise each named both a brief teaching and a broad teaching, judged solely with reference to its own statement, not set against any other scripture — that is, the scripture on the elements broadly expounds matter and mind, while briefly expounding the mental factors, and it is for this reason that this holds. That scripture on the sense-fields is likewise named both a brief teaching and a broad teaching, judged solely with reference to its own statement, not set against any other scripture — that is, the scripture on the sense-fields broadly expounds matter, while briefly expounding mind and the mental factors. That scripture on the aggregates is likewise named both a brief teaching and a broad teaching —

judged solely with reference to its own statement, not set against any other scripture — that is, the scripture on the aggregates broadly expounds the mental factors, while briefly expounding matter and mind. That scripture stating that every sensation whatsoever is suffering is named only a brief teaching, never a broad one. Other masters say: this scripture on the elements is named a broad teaching, and likewise gathers within it every dharma whatsoever; those scriptures such as the Great Analogy Sutra and the Great Nirvāṇa Sutra, though named broad teachings, do not gather within them every dharma whatsoever. That scripture on the sense-fields, though it does gather within it every dharma whatsoever, is not itself a broad teaching, since it speaks only in middling terms. That scripture on the aggregates is not named a broad teaching, being itself a brief teaching,

and it likewise does not gather within it every dharma whatsoever, gathering only the conditioned, not the unconditioned. That scripture stating that every sensation whatsoever is suffering, and the like, is not named a broad teaching, being itself the very briefest teaching of all — though even within it there is what gathers every dharma whatsoever, as when it states that every dharma is empty and without self, and the like. Other masters say: there exists, in truth, no further brief-teaching scripture beyond this, as when the World-Honored One states that giving is of two kinds — first, the giving of the dharma; second, the giving of wealth — and scriptures of this kind; and there exists no further broad-teaching scripture beyond this, as with the Great Analogy Sutra, the Great Nirvāṇa Sutra, and scriptures of this kind. Although each of these various statements carries its own genuine sense, the Buddha, the World-Honored One, with respect to what is to be known, first gave a broad teaching and only afterward gave a brief one — that is, with respect to what is to be known, he first broadly taught the eighteen elements, and only afterward, on this very same basis, briefly taught them as the twelve sense-fields, and, further, on this very same basis, excluding the unconditioned dharmas, briefly taught them as the five aggregates. This is named the World-Honored One's own broad and brief teaching of the dharma. It is precisely on the basis of this broad and brief teaching of the dharma that the Buddha told the Venerable Śāriputra: with respect to the jewel of the dharma, I am capable of teaching it both broadly and briefly, yet one capable of understanding it is exceedingly difficult to find. And it is, further, on the basis of this broad and brief teaching of the dharma that the Venerable Śāriputra said to the Buddha:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That broad and brief are relative terms rather than fixed properties of a teaching is the section's real insight: the very same eighteen-elements sutra is a brief teaching next to the Great Nirvāṇa Sutra and a broad one next to the twelve sense-fields, which means asking whether a teaching is broad or brief is really asking broad or brief compared to what — a question this treatise refuses to let go unanswered.

2. Śāriputra's Fearless Request, and the Simile of the Sea-Dragon-King's Clouds

When the Buddha declares that he can teach the dharma both broadly and briefly, yet finds few capable of understanding it, every disciple but Śāriputra trembles in fear — exactly as every plant and forest panics at the sea-dragon-king's own thunderclouds, dreading an endless deluge, while the great earth alone calmly invites the rain, confident in its own capacity to receive however much falls. Śāriputra alone, his wisdom cultivated across sixty aeons until it rivals the earth's own vastness, asks the Buddha to teach without fear, reasoning that the Buddha's own request already implies his own suitability as a vessel for it.

Chinese Source

世尊唯願如來廣略說法此定當有解法寶者。於如是事應作譬喻。如海龍王久處大海。增長威勢上昇虛空。興布大雲遍覆空界。掣電晃曜震大雷音。普告世間我當注雨。一切藥草卉木叢林。聞如是聲皆大驚慴咸作是念。此大龍王處大海中久增威勢。今若注雨未有息期。我等皆當定為漂沒。爾時大地聞如是聲心不驚疑面無異色。虛懷仰請海龍王言。唯願恣情降注大雨。過百千歲我悉能受。世尊亦爾。曾於過去釋迦牟尼帝幢寶髻然燈勝觀。乃至最後迦葉波佛所。

Ru-Yi Translation

World-Honored One, I pray only that the Thus-Come One teach the dharma both broadly and briefly — there will certainly then be one capable of understanding this very jewel of the dharma. With respect to a matter such as this, an analogy ought to be given: exactly as the sea-dragon-king, having long dwelt within the great ocean, gathers his own might and rises up into the empty sky, raising up great clouds that cover the whole of that empty realm, flashing lightning and thundering with a mighty roar, proclaiming to the whole world at large: I am about to send down rain! — every medicinal herb, every grass and tree, every thicket and forest, upon hearing such a sound, is struck with great terror, each thinking to itself: this great dragon-king, having long dwelt within the great ocean, gathering his own might, if he should now send down rain, there will be no end to it at all, and every one of us will surely be swept away and drowned. At that very time, the great earth alone, hearing this same sound, its own mind entirely untroubled and undoubting, its own face betraying no change at all, with an open, receptive heart, implores the sea-dragon-king, saying: I pray only that you release your own heart's desire and send down a mighty rain — though it should last a hundred thousand years, I myself am fully capable of receiving it all. The World-Honored One is exactly the same: in past times, before Śākyamuni Buddha, Dhvajottama, Ratnaśikhin, Dīpaṃkara, Praṇidhāna, and so on, at full length, up through the very last of them, Kāśyapa Buddha,

增長福德智慧資糧。昇有餘依涅槃空界。興大悲雲遍覆世間。發勝慧電普照一切。虛空非我無畏雷音。遍告所化舍利子等。我於法寶能廣略說。而能解者甚為難得。時諸所化除舍利子。聞佛此言皆生怯懼。咸作是念。佛得如是。昔所未得名句文身。為我等說恐不能解。唯舍利子六十劫中。增長智見猛利圓滿猶如大地。聞佛此言心不驚疑。面無異色能無所畏。而請佛言。唯願如來廣略說法。此定當有解法寶者。問亦應有法非諸聲聞獨覺境界。彼舍利子何緣無畏作如是請。答彼唯請佛聲聞所知非佛所知。聲聞境界非佛境界。聲聞所行非佛所行。聲聞根所及非佛根所及故不違理。復次知佛開許故作是請。謂舍利子作如是念。世尊慈悲諸所說法。必應稱量定有饒益。要於田器而雨法雨。所雨法雨終不唐捐。諸所發言必依法器。若非法器終不發言。世尊既知我有爾所堪受法器。作如是言。

he accumulated the very provisions of merit and wisdom, and, ascending into the very emptiness of nirvāṇa-with-residue, he raises up the great cloud of compassion, covering the whole world entirely, sending forth the lightning-flash of surpassing wisdom, illuminating all things everywhere, and, in a voice fearless as the very emptiness of no-self, proclaims to every one of his own disciples, Śāriputra and the rest at large: with respect to the jewel of the dharma, I am capable of teaching it both broadly and briefly, yet one capable of understanding it is exceedingly difficult to find. At that time, every one of his own disciples, excepting only Śāriputra, upon hearing the Buddha speak these words, gave rise to fear and trepidation, each thinking to himself: the Buddha has attained a body of names, phrases, and syllables the like of which has never before existed — were he to teach it for our own sake, I fear we could never understand it at all. Only Śāriputra, across sixty aeons having cultivated wisdom and vision, sharp, forceful, and utterly complete, exactly like the great earth itself, upon hearing the Buddha speak these words, his own mind entirely untroubled and undoubting, his own face betraying no change at all, capable of being entirely without fear, thereupon implored the Buddha, saying: I pray only that the Thus-Come One teach the dharma both broadly and briefly — there will certainly then be one capable of understanding this very jewel of the dharma. One asks: there ought also to exist a dharma that lies beyond the domain of every disciple or solitary-realizer whatsoever — for what reason, then, did Śāriputra, without any fear at all, make such a request as this? The answer: he requested only what a disciple is capable of knowing, not what only the Buddha himself is capable of knowing — a disciple's own domain is not the Buddha's own domain, a disciple's own conduct is not the Buddha's own conduct, and what a disciple's own faculty can reach is not what the Buddha's own faculty can reach, and it is for this reason that this raises no contradiction with reason at all. Further, he made this request precisely because he already knew that the Buddha had granted his permission — that is, Śāriputra thought to himself as follows: the World-Honored One's own compassion, in whatever dharma he teaches, must certainly be carefully weighed, and must certainly bring genuine benefit; he sends down the very rain of the dharma only upon a fitting vessel, and the rain of the dharma he sends down is never squandered in vain; whatever words he utters must rely upon a fitting vessel for the dharma — were it not a fitting vessel, he would never utter such words at all. Since the World-Honored One already knows that I myself am just such a vessel, capable of receiving this much, he has spoken these very words —

故知世尊開許我請。是故尊者請佛無畏。

and it is by this that I know the World-Honored One has already granted his own permission for my request. It is for this reason that the Venerable one made his request of the Buddha entirely without fear.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Śāriputra's own reasoning for his fearlessness is a small masterpiece of inference: he does not claim confidence in his own wisdom directly, but instead infers his own readiness backward from the Buddha's own known character — since the Buddha never wastes the rain of the dharma on unfit ground, the very fact that the Buddha has offered to teach is itself proof that Śāriputra is a fit vessel to receive it.

3. Why the Buddha Taught in Three Different Formats: Reasons Keyed to the Listener

A cascade of independent explanations accounts for why the Buddha sometimes taught eighteen elements, sometimes twelve sense-fields, sometimes five aggregates: matching whichever the listener was ignorant of; matching a beginner, a habituated practitioner, or one beyond conceptual effort; matching dull, middling, or sharp faculties; matching wisdom that opens, expounds, or draws onward; matching a preference for the broad, middling, or brief; matching pride in lineage, wealth, or life; matching ignorance of matter-and-mind, matter alone, or mental factors alone; and matching those who conceive of a self, those ignorant of what mind depends on and takes as object, or those prone to conceit.

Chinese Source

問佛為何等所化有情。說蘊處界廣略三法。答佛隨所化所愚而說。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: for the sake of what kind of sentient being to be converted does the Buddha teach the aggregates, the sense-fields, and the elements, in these three broad and brief forms? The answer: the Buddha teaches according to whatever each being to be converted is ignorant of.

謂愚於界者為說十八界。若愚於處者為說十二處。若愚於蘊者為說五蘊。復次世尊所化略有三種。一初習業。二已串修。三超作意初習業者為說十八界。已串修者為說十二處。超作意者為說五蘊。復次世尊所化有三種根。謂鈍中利為鈍根者說十八界。為中根者說十二處。為利根者說五蘊。復次世尊所化有三種智。一者開智。二者說智。三者引智。為開智者說五蘊。為說智者說十二處。為引智者說十八界。復次世尊所化有三種樂。謂廣中略。為樂廣者說十八界。為樂中者說十二處。為樂略者說五蘊。復次世尊所化有三憍逸。一恃姓憍逸。二恃財憍逸。三恃命憍逸。恃姓憍逸者為說十八界。謂族姓義是界義。種類貴賤無差別故。恃財憍逸者為說十二處。謂生門義是處義。隨有所生尋散盡故。恃命憍逸者為說五蘊。

That is, for one ignorant of the elements, he teaches the eighteen elements; for one ignorant of the sense-fields, he teaches the twelve sense-fields; for one ignorant of the aggregates, he teaches the five aggregates. Further, those the World-Honored One converts are, in brief, of three kinds: first, those just beginning their own practice; second, those already long habituated to it; third, those who have transcended mere conceptual application. For those just beginning their own practice, he teaches the eighteen elements; for those already long habituated to it, he teaches the twelve sense-fields; for those who have transcended mere conceptual application, he teaches the five aggregates. Further, those the World-Honored One converts possess three kinds of faculty, namely, dull, middling, and sharp — for those of dull faculty, he teaches the eighteen elements; for those of middling faculty, he teaches the twelve sense-fields; for those of sharp faculty, he teaches the five aggregates. Further, those the World-Honored One converts possess three kinds of wisdom: first, the wisdom that opens the way; second, the wisdom that expounds; third, the wisdom that draws one onward. For those possessing the wisdom that opens the way, he teaches the five aggregates; for those possessing the wisdom that expounds, he teaches the twelve sense-fields; for those possessing the wisdom that draws one onward, he teaches the eighteen elements. Further, those the World-Honored One converts possess three kinds of preference, namely, for the broad, the middling, and the brief — for those who prefer the broad, he teaches the eighteen elements; for those who prefer the middling, he teaches the twelve sense-fields; for those who prefer the brief, he teaches the five aggregates. Further, those the World-Honored One converts possess three kinds of prideful indolence: first, pride in one's own lineage; second, pride in one's own wealth; third, pride in one's own life. For those prideful in their own lineage, he teaches the eighteen elements, since the very meaning of clan and lineage is precisely the meaning of an element, there being no real distinction whatsoever between noble and base among the various kinds. For those prideful in their own wealth, he teaches the twelve sense-fields, since the very meaning of a gate of arising is precisely the meaning of a sense-field, whatever arises there being certain to soon scatter and be exhausted. For those prideful in their own life, he teaches the five aggregates,

謂積聚義是蘊義。有為積聚尋散滅故。復次世尊所化有三種愚。一者愚色心。二者愚於色。三者愚心所。愚色心者為說十八界。於此界中廣說色心略說心所故。愚於色者為說十二處。於此處中廣說色略說心心所故。愚心所者為說五蘊。於此蘊中廣說心所略說色心故。復次為計我者說十八界。謂一身中有多界別無一我故為愚所依及所緣者說十二處。謂分別識有六所依六所緣故。為我慢者說五蘊。謂身唯有生滅五蘊不應恃怙起我慢故。佛為此等所化有情說蘊處界廣略三法。

since the very meaning of accumulation is precisely the meaning of an aggregate, every conditioned accumulation being certain to soon scatter and cease. Further, those the World-Honored One converts possess three kinds of ignorance: first, ignorance regarding matter and mind together; second, ignorance regarding matter alone; third, ignorance regarding the mental factors. For one ignorant regarding matter and mind together, he teaches the eighteen elements, since, within this teaching on the elements, matter and mind are expounded broadly, while the mental factors are expounded briefly. For one ignorant regarding matter alone, he teaches the twelve sense-fields, since, within this teaching on the sense-fields, matter is expounded broadly, while mind and the mental factors are expounded briefly. For one ignorant regarding the mental factors, he teaches the five aggregates, since, within this teaching on the aggregates, the mental factors are expounded broadly, while matter and mind are expounded briefly. Further, for one who conceives of a self, he teaches the eighteen elements, since, within one single body, there exist many distinct elements, there being no single self at all; for one ignorant regarding what a thing depends upon and what it takes as its object, he teaches the twelve sense-fields, since discriminating consciousness possesses six distinct supports and six distinct objects; for one prideful with self-conceit, he teaches the five aggregates, since the body possesses nothing but the five aggregates' own arising and ceasing, affording no occasion at all for relying upon it to give rise to self-conceit. It is for the sake of sentient beings to be converted such as these that the Buddha teaches the aggregates, the sense-fields, and the elements, in these three broad and brief forms.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a single doctrinal choice — eighteen elements versus twelve sense-fields versus five aggregates — can be justified by reference to nine entirely independent axes of listener-difference is this treatise's characteristic move: rather than settling on the one true reason the Buddha taught as he did, it treats the very多plicity of possible explanations as evidence of how precisely each teaching was fitted to its audience.

4. Seventeen or Twelve? The Eighteen Elements' Own Substance-Count

Though the eighteen elements bear eighteen distinct names, their own real substance numbers either seventeen or twelve, depending on how one counts: speak of the six consciousnesses and the mind-element vanishes into them, leaving seventeen substances; speak of the mind-element and the six consciousnesses vanish into it, leaving twelve. The eighteen are then shown to rest on three underlying matters — basis, dependent, and object — the six inner elements serving as basis, the six consciousness-elements as dependent, and the six outer elements as object.

Chinese Source

問此十八界名有十八。實體有幾。答此界實體或有十七或有十二。

12. Why "Eye-Consciousness," Not "Color-Consciousness"? Eight Reasons

13. The Fourfold Ledger of Basis and Condition, and Venerable Vasumitra's Nine Further Reasons

The relationship between a dharma's status as eye-consciousness's own basis and as its immediately-preceding condition is cast as a tetralemma: a co-arising eye is basis but not condition; a mental factor that has just ceased is condition but not basis; the mind-element that has just ceased is both at once; everything else is neither. The Venerable Vasumitra then supplies nine further, independent reasons why eye-consciousness is never called color-consciousness — the eye belongs to a single continuous series while color does not, the eye is always proximate and internal while color is indeterminate in this respect, the eye is exclusive while color is shared, and the eye admits of a graded quality (lower, middling, upper) that consciousness directly tracks, while color does not condition consciousness in this same graded way at all.

14. The Great Virtuous One's Account, and Reconciling the Scripture "Color Known by the Eye"

The Great Virtuous One reduces the whole question to a single elegant criterion: whenever the eye is obstructed, consciousness is obstructed likewise; whenever the eye is unobstructed, so is consciousness — and it is precisely this tight coupling to the eye specifically, rather than to color, that earns the name eye-consciousness. A vivid contrast makes the point concrete: destroy one color and eye-consciousness simply shifts to another color as its object, but destroy the eye-faculty itself and no eye-consciousness arises at all, however many colors remain presently manifest. The fascicle then reconciles an apparently contrary scriptural line — "color known by the eye," when strictly it is eye-consciousness, not the eye itself, that discerns color — through a cascade of explanations, from scribal abbreviation to the everyday convention of naming a performance, a dye, or a piece of writing after its single most excellent instrument rather than every contributing tool.

15. Ear, Nose, Tongue, and Body Consciousness Alike; Defining the Mind-Element

Every reason and every reconciliation offered for eye-consciousness applies equally, without further argument, to the ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-consciousness-elements. The treatise then defines the mind-element as whatever mind has discerned, discerns, or will discern any dharma, together with its own counterpart-class — with one striking asymmetry from every other element treated so far: there exists no past or present counterpart-class mind at all, only a future one, since mind and its mental factors can only ever arise by taking some object, meaning a mind-element that has genuinely existed in the past or present must, by that very fact, already have discerned something.

16. Defining the Dharma-Element: Why It Alone Has No Counterpart-Class

The dharma-element — whatever mind has discerned, discerns, or will discern — turns out to possess no counterpart-class whatsoever, since every dharma across all three times, without a single exception, falls under the discerning reach of some instance of mind-consciousness. This threatens to make every one of the remaining seventeen elements equally same-class with no counterpart at all, since mind-consciousness discerns them too — resolved by noting that those seventeen elements establish their own same-class and counterpart-class status not with reference to mind-consciousness's own universal reach, but with reference to each one's own specific paired faculty and object, exactly as eye stands opposite color alone.

The resolution here rests on a subtle but crucial distinction between two entirely different senses in which something can be an "object": mind-consciousness's own universal capacity to discern absolutely anything is simply the wrong register on which to measure a same-class or counterpart-class relationship, which properly belongs instead to each sense-faculty's own narrow, dedicated pairing with its one designated kind of object — universality and exclusivity are not competing for the same explanatory job.

17. Two Closing Tetralemma Questions on Co-Arisen and Associated Dharmas

The fascicle closes with two final, precise tetralemma-style questions, each confirming that even a genuinely same-class dharma can arise upon what is, in its own right, a counterpart-class instance: the very arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing that occurs upon a counterpart-class element is itself gathered within the ever-same-class dharma-element, and the mental factors associated with a future, not-yet-arisen mind-element — themselves counterpart-class — are likewise carried along by matter gathered within the ever-same-class dharma-element of the dissociated formations.
