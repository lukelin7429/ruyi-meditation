Resistance, the Buddha's Body, and the Three Times

Fascicle 76 treats resistant and non-resistant dharma, contaminated and undefiled dharma — including whether the Buddha's own body is contaminated — and conditioned and unconditioned dharma, before opening at length into past, future, and present dharma: the meaning of time itself, five dilemmas on how a future dharma arises, fifteen tetralemmas, and whether past and future are genuinely accumulated. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 76 continues the Ten Gates Nyāsa (結蘊第二中十門納息第四之六), treating four successive dharma-pairs — resistant/non-resistant (有對無對法), contaminated/undefiled (有漏無漏法), conditioned/unconditioned (有為無為法), and past/future/present (過去未來現在法) — the last of which opens the treatise's celebrated treatment of the three times. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Resistant and Non-Resistant Dharma: Six Definitions and Five Masters

A new pair of categories opens — resistant dharma and non-resistant dharma — composed, as ever, to put a stop to the notion of a person and to display wisdom's own superiority, but also to refute two rival definitions: that resistance means occurring together with resistance itself (making the five sense-consciousnesses resistant, since support and object obstruct each other), or that it means occurring together with aversion (making any mind associated with aversion resistant). The treatise instead confines resistant dharma strictly to the ten form-sense-fields, and offers six near-synonymous definitions — an accumulation of atoms, admitting analysis, admitting accumulation, possessing obstruction, possessing form and substance, capable of containing and obstructing — before Pārśva chains these into a single implication, and Vasumitra, the Great Virtuous One, and Ghoṣa each add their own formulation, Ghoṣa's turning on visible color's length and breadth and a following sound.

Chinese Source

復有二法。謂有對無對法。問何故作此論。答為欲遮遣補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

There exist, further, two kinds of dharma: resistant dharmas, and non-resistant dharmas. One asks: for what reason is this treatise composed? The answer: it is in order to put a stop to the notion of a person,

及為顯示智殊勝故。為欲遮遣補特伽羅者。謂顯唯有有對無對法。

and in order to display the superiority of wisdom. As for putting a stop to the notion of a person: this displays that there exists only the resistant and the non-resistant dharma.

畢竟無實補特伽羅。及為顯示智殊勝者。謂有聰慧殊勝智者。由此二法通達一切。此二遍攝一切法故。復次為止他宗顯正理故。謂或有執。若與對俱說名有對不與對俱說名無對。若作是說。五識身等名為有對。所依所緣俱對礙故。或復有執。若與瞋俱說名有對。不與瞋俱說名無對。若作是說。瞋相應品心心所法說名有對。為止彼意顯有礙色說名有對。此所餘法說名無對。由此三緣故作斯論。問有對法云何。答十處。謂五內色處。及五外色處。問無對法云何。

and no genuinely real person whatsoever. As for displaying the superiority of wisdom: this refers to the fact that there exists a wisdom of surpassing brilliance and superiority, by which one penetrates everything whatsoever, since these two categories comprehensively gather within them every dharma there is. Further, this is in order to put a stop to what other schools maintain, and to display the correct principle — that is, there are those who hold that whatever occurs together with resistance is named resistant, and whatever does not occur together with resistance is named non-resistant, and, holding this, they say that the five sense-consciousness-bodies are themselves named resistant, since both their support and their object are mutually obstructive. Others, further, hold that whatever occurs together with aversion is named resistant, and whatever does not occur together with aversion is named non-resistant, and, holding this, they say that mind and mental factors of the category associated with aversion are themselves named resistant. It is in order to put a stop to this very intent that this treatise displays that it is precisely obstructive form that is named resistant, every remaining dharma being named non-resistant. It is owing to these three causes and conditions that this treatise is composed. One asks: what is a resistant dharma? The answer: ten sense-fields, namely, the five internal form-sense-fields and the five external form-sense-fields. One asks: what is a non-resistant dharma?

答二處。謂意處及法處。問有對無對是何義耶。答諸極微積聚是有對義。非極微積聚是無對義。復次諸可分析是有對義。不可分析是無對義。復次諸可積集是有對義。不可積集是無對義。復次諸有障礙是有對義。若無障礙是無對義。復次諸有形質是有對義。若無形質是無對義。復次若能容受。及能障礙是有對義。若不能容受及不能障礙是無對義。脇尊者言。若可分析則可積集。若可積集則有障礙。若有障礙則有形質。若有形質則能容受及能障礙。若能容受及能障礙是有對義。與上相違是無對義。尊者世友作如是說。有細分相有障礙相是有對相。無細分相無障礙相是無對相。大德說曰。若能容受及能障礙相是有對相。若不能容受及不能障礙是無對相。尊者妙音作如是說。若可施設極微積聚性。顯色長短性。隨生音響性者。是有對相。與此相違是無對相。此中極微積聚性者。說八處顯色。長短性者說色處。隨生音響性者說聲處。尊者世友作如是說。

The answer: two sense-fields, namely, the mind-sense-field and the dharma-sense-field. One asks: what is the meaning of resistant and non-resistant? The answer: whatever is an accumulation of atoms is the meaning of resistant; whatever is not an accumulation of atoms is the meaning of non-resistant. Further, whatever admits of analysis is the meaning of resistant; whatever does not admit of analysis is the meaning of non-resistant. Further, whatever admits of accumulation is the meaning of resistant; whatever does not admit of accumulation is the meaning of non-resistant. Further, whatever possesses obstruction is the meaning of resistant; whatever lacks obstruction is the meaning of non-resistant. Further, whatever possesses a form and substance is the meaning of resistant; whatever lacks a form and substance is the meaning of non-resistant. Further, whatever is capable of containing and of obstructing is the meaning of resistant; whatever is incapable of containing and of obstructing is the meaning of non-resistant. The Venerable Pārśva says: whatever admits of analysis then admits of accumulation; whatever admits of accumulation then possesses obstruction; whatever possesses obstruction then possesses a form and substance; whatever possesses a form and substance is then capable of containing and of obstructing; whatever is capable of containing and of obstructing is the meaning of resistant, and whatever is the opposite of the foregoing is the meaning of non-resistant. The Venerable Vasumitra says as follows: whatever possesses the characteristic of a fine, minute portion, and the characteristic of obstruction, is the characteristic of the resistant; whatever lacks the characteristic of a fine, minute portion, and lacks the characteristic of obstruction, is the characteristic of the non-resistant. The Great Virtuous One says: whatever possesses the characteristic of being capable of containing and of obstructing is the characteristic of the resistant; whatever is incapable of containing and of obstructing is the characteristic of the non-resistant. The Venerable Ghoṣa says as follows: whatever can be designated as possessing the nature of an accumulation of atoms, the nature of a long or short visible color, or the nature of giving rise to a following sound, is the characteristic of the resistant, and whatever is the opposite of this is the characteristic of the non-resistant — here, the nature of an accumulation of atoms speaks of eight sense-fields' own visible color; the nature of a long or short visible color speaks of the color-sense-field; the nature of giving rise to a following sound speaks of the sound-sense-field. The Venerable Vasumitra says as follows:

極微雜合積集住相是有對相。與此相違是無對相。尊者覺天作如是說。能據處所展轉相礙是有對相。與此相違是無對相。

the characteristic of resting in mutual, reciprocal obstruction by an accumulation of atoms is the characteristic of the resistant, and whatever is the opposite of this is the characteristic of the non-resistant. The Venerable Buddhadeva says as follows: whatever is capable of occupying a location and of mutually obstructing another is the characteristic of the resistant, and whatever is the opposite of this is the characteristic of the non-resistant.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ghoṣa's own definition is worth pausing on, since it smuggles in an entirely different criterion from the rest: where every other master defines resistance through obstruction or atomic accumulation, Ghoṣa reaches instead for length, color, and sound — properties a listener or a viewer would notice first — suggesting his own definition is pitched at how resistant matter is actually recognized in experience, not at what makes it resistant in the first place.

2. Three Kinds of Resistance, and How Many Sense-Fields Mutually Obstruct

Resistance itself divides into three: obstructive resistance (a hand striking a hand, a stone striking a stone), objective-domain resistance (a faculty confined to its own domain), and referential-object resistance (mind confined to its own object) — though only the first is meant by "resistant dharma" here. A further puzzle asks how many of the ten form-sense-fields actually obstruct one another: some say only touch; others, only five (body, color, smell, taste, touch, worked out arithmetically as hand-strikes-hand being "five striking five"); others, nine, excluding only sound. The compilers settle on all ten, since sound too must accumulate to be sound at all, and cite the Prajñaptiśāstra's own blanket claim that eye is resistant to color and so on — reconciled by noting that passage relies solely on objective-domain resistance, illustrated through eyes obstructed by water but not land, by day but not night, and every combination between.

Chinese Source

應知有對總有三種。一障礙有對。二境界有對。三所緣有對。障礙有對者。如以手擊手以手擊石以石擊石以石擊手以杵擊鍾。此等展轉更相障礙。如是名為障礙有對。境界有對者。如眼根等諸有境法各於自境界有所拘礙。如是名為境界有對。所緣有對者。如心心所有所緣法。各於自所緣有所拘礙。如是名為所緣有對。如是三種。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be understood that the resistant is, altogether, of three kinds: first, obstructive resistance; second, objective-domain resistance; third, referential-object resistance. As for obstructive resistance: this refers to a hand striking a hand, a hand striking a stone, a stone striking a stone, a stone striking a hand, or a pestle striking a bell — each such pair mutually obstructing one another in turn, and this is named obstructive resistance. As for objective-domain resistance: this refers to the eye-faculty and every other faculty that possesses its own domain, each being confined and constrained within its own domain, and this is named objective-domain resistance. As for referential-object resistance: this refers to mind and mental factors that possess an object, each being confined and constrained within its own object, and this is named referential-object resistance. Such, then, are the three kinds;

有對法中。此中但說障礙有對。問障礙有對十色處中。幾處展轉有相礙義。有作是說。唯有觸處有相礙義。餘十一處無相礙義非所觸故。或有說者。唯有五處有相礙義。謂內處中唯有身處。若外處中色香味觸。彼作是說。若以手擊手即以五擊五。若以手擊石即以五擊四若以石擊石即以四擊四。若以石擊手即以四擊五。若以杵擊鍾亦以四擊四。復有說者。唯有九處有相礙義。十色處中唯除聲處。

within the resistant dharma spoken of here, however, only obstructive resistance is intended. One asks: among the ten form-sense-fields spoken of as obstructively resistant, how many of them possess, in turn, the very meaning of mutual obstruction? There are those who say that only the touch-sense-field possesses the meaning of mutual obstruction, the remaining eleven sense-fields lacking the meaning of mutual obstruction, since they are not touched. Others, further, say that only five sense-fields possess the meaning of mutual obstruction — namely, among the internal sense-fields, only the body-sense-field, and, among the external sense-fields, color, smell, taste, and touch — and, holding this, they say that if a hand strikes a hand, this is five striking five; if a hand strikes a stone, this is five striking four; if a stone strikes a stone, this is four striking four; if a stone strikes a hand, this is four striking five; and if a pestle strikes a bell, this too is four striking four. Others, further, say that only nine sense-fields possess the meaning of mutual obstruction, the sound-sense-field alone being excluded among the ten form-sense-fields,

若不爾者。有以手等擊眼處等時應不生苦。評曰。應作是說。十種色處皆有礙義。若聲處無礙者。此應無積聚義。又不應名障礙有對。施設論說。眼定對色色定對眼。廣說乃至意定對法法定對意。

for were it not so, then, when a hand and the like struck the eye-sense-field and the like, there ought to arise no pain at all. The compilers say: one should say as follows — all ten form-sense-fields possess the meaning of obstruction; were the sound-sense-field to lack obstruction, it ought then to lack the very meaning of accumulation, and ought further not to be named obstructively resistant at all. The Prajñaptiśāstra states: the eye is definitely resistant to color, and color is definitely resistant to the eye, and so on, at full length, up through, the mind is definitely resistant to a dharma and a dharma is definitely resistant to the mind —

彼師但依境界有對。而造彼論故作是說。或有眼於水有礙於陸無礙如魚等眼。或有眼於陸有礙於水無礙如人等眼。或有眼於水於陸二俱有礙。如水邏剎娑捕魚人等眼。或有眼於水於陸二俱無礙。謂除前相即被翳眼。或有眼於夜有礙於晝無礙如鵂鶹等眼。或有眼於晝有礙於夜無礙如人等眼。或有眼於晝於夜二俱有礙如馬鹿猫狸等眼。或有眼於晝於夜二俱無礙。謂除前相即被翳眼。此中有礙者謂境界有對。

that master relies solely upon objective-domain resistance in composing that treatise, and it is for this reason that he speaks in this way, saying: there exists an eye that is obstructed in water yet unobstructed on land, such as the eye of a fish and the like; there exists an eye that is obstructed on land yet unobstructed in water, such as the eye of a human and the like; there exists an eye that is obstructed both in water and on land, such as the eye of a water-rākṣasa or a fisherman and the like; there exists an eye that is unobstructed both in water and on land, such as, setting aside the foregoing cases, an eye that has become clouded by cataract; there exists an eye that is obstructed at night yet unobstructed by day, such as the eye of an owl and the like; there exists an eye that is obstructed by day yet unobstructed at night, such as the eye of a human and the like; there exists an eye that is obstructed both by day and by night, such as the eye of a horse, a deer, a cat, or a wildcat and the like; there exists an eye that is unobstructed both by day and by night, such as, setting aside the foregoing cases, an eye that has become clouded by cataract. Here, whatever is spoken of as obstructed refers to objective-domain resistance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The menagerie of eyes obstructed differently by water and land, day and night — fish-eyes, owl-eyes, cataract-clouded eyes that are blocked by neither — reads almost like naturalist observation dropped into a metaphysical argument, and that is precisely its function: objective-domain resistance is being defined not abstractly but through the sheer variety of ways different creatures' own faculties are in fact restricted, proving the category by exhausting its instances.

3. Is the Buddha's Own Body Contaminated? Refuting the Mahāsāṃghikas

Turning to contaminated and undefiled dharma, the treatise confronts the Mahāsāṃghika claim that the Buddha's own body is undefiled, resting on a scripture stating the Thus-Come One abides transcending the world, unstained by its dharmas. The refutation is sharp: were the Buddha's body undefiled, it would contradict the scripture stating that ignorance and craving bring about every body accompanied by consciousness, the Buddha's own included — and, decisively, the incomparable woman's craving, Aṅgulimāla's hatred, the arrogant's conceit, and Urubilvā Kāśyapa's delusion could never have arisen toward an undefiled body at all, and yet they did.

Chinese Source

復有二法謂有漏無漏法。問何故作此論。答為欲遮遣補特伽羅。及為顯示智殊勝故。為欲遮遣補特伽羅者。謂顯唯有有漏無漏法。畢竟無實補特伽羅。及為顯示智殊勝者。謂有聰慧殊勝智者。由此二法通達一切。此二遍攝一切法故。復次為止他宗顯正理故。謂或有執。佛身無漏如大眾部。問彼何故作此執。答依契經故。如契經說。苾芻當知。如來生在世間長在世間出世間住。不為世法之所染污。彼作是說。既言如來出世間住不為世法之所染污。由此故知佛身無漏。為止彼意顯佛生身唯是有漏。若佛生身是無漏者便違契經。如契經說。無明所覆愛結所縛。愚夫智者感有識身。世尊亦是智者所攝身定應是無明愛果。是故佛身定應有漏。又若佛身是無漏者。無比女人不應於佛生身起愛。指鬘於佛不應生瞋諸憍傲者不應生慢。隖盧頻螺迦葉波等不應生癡。於佛生身既有發起貪瞋癡慢。

Ru-Yi Translation

There exist, further, two kinds of dharma: contaminated dharmas, and undefiled dharmas. One asks: for what reason is this treatise composed? The answer: it is in order to put a stop to the notion of a person, and in order to display the superiority of wisdom. As for putting a stop to the notion of a person: this displays that there exists only the contaminated and the undefiled dharma, and no genuinely real person whatsoever. As for displaying the superiority of wisdom: this refers to the fact that there exists a wisdom of surpassing brilliance and superiority, by which one penetrates everything whatsoever, since these two categories comprehensively gather within them every dharma there is. Further, this is in order to put a stop to what other schools maintain, and to display the correct principle — that is, there are those who hold that the Buddha's own body is undefiled, as the Mahāsāṃghikas do. One asks: for what reason do they hold this? The answer: on the strength of a scripture — as the scripture states, monks, you should know, the Thus-Come One, born within the world and grown up within the world, abides transcending the world, unstained by the dharmas of the world — and, holding this, they say: since it is stated that the Thus-Come One abides transcending the world, unstained by the dharmas of the world, it is thereby known that the Buddha's own body is undefiled. It is in order to put a stop to this very intent that this treatise displays that the Buddha's own born body is solely contaminated — for were the Buddha's own born body undefiled, this would contradict scripture, as the scripture states: covered by ignorance, bound by the fetter of craving, the fool and the wise alike bring about a body accompanied by consciousness — and the World-Honored One, too, is gathered among the wise, so that his body ought certainly to be the very fruit of ignorance and craving, and it is for this reason that the Buddha's own body ought certainly to be contaminated. Further, were the Buddha's own body undefiled, then the incomparable woman ought not to have given rise to craving toward the Buddha's own born body, Aṅgulimāla ought not to have given rise to hatred, the arrogant ought not to have given rise to conceit, and Urubilvā Kāśyapa and the rest ought not to have given rise to delusion — yet, toward the Buddha's own born body, craving, hatred, conceit, and delusion have indeed all arisen,

故知佛身定非無漏。問若佛生身是有漏者。云何通彼所引契經。答彼說法身故不成證。謂彼經說。如來生在世間長在世間者。說佛生身。出世間住不為世法所染污者說佛法身。復次依佛不隨世法轉義說彼契經故無有失。謂世八法隨世間轉。世間亦隨世八法轉。雖世八法隨世間轉。而佛不隨世八法轉。復次依佛解脫世八法義。說彼契經。故無有失。問佛亦曾遇此世八法。如何說佛解脫此耶。謂一日中勇猛長者曾奉施佛三百千衣。諸如是等名佛遇利。佛入娑羅婆羅門邑乞食不得空鉢而還。諸如是等名佛遇衰。佛於生時名譽上至他化自在。得菩提時名譽上至色究竟天。轉法輪時名譽上至大梵王宮。諸如是等名佛遇譽。佛為戰遮婆羅門女孫陀利女惡心毀謗。惡名流布十六大國。諸如是等名佛遇毀。佛為跋羅墮闍梵志以五百頌現前譏罵諸如是等名佛遇譏。即此梵志須臾迴此五百頌言現前讚佛。如是論力及隖波離以妙伽他現前讚佛。尊者舍利子以眾多頌現前讚佛無上功德。尊者阿難陀以眾多頌現前讚佛希有妙法。諸如是等名佛遇稱。佛有輕安及勝受樂。一切有情所不能及名佛遇樂。佛有頭痛背痛腹痛。及有傷足出血等事名佛遇苦。既有此事如何解脫。答雖遇此事而不生染。故說世尊於此解脫。謂佛雖遇利等四法。而心不生高歡喜愛。又佛雖遇衰等四法。而心不生下慼憂恚。

and it is thereby known that the Buddha's own body is certainly not undefiled. One asks: if the Buddha's own born body is contaminated, how is the scripture just now cited to be reconciled? The answer: that passage speaks of the dharma-body, and it is for this reason that it fails to serve as any proof at all — that is, that scripture's own statement, that the Thus-Come One, born within the world and grown up within the world, speaks of the Buddha's own born body, while abiding transcending the world, unstained by the dharmas of the world, speaks of the Buddha's own dharma-body. Further, this is spoken with reference to the meaning that the Buddha does not turn along with the eight worldly dharmas, and it is for this reason that no fault whatsoever is incurred — that is, the eight worldly dharmas turn along with the world, and the world, too, turns along with the eight worldly dharmas, and, although the eight worldly dharmas turn along with the world, yet the Buddha does not turn along with the eight worldly dharmas. Further, this is spoken with reference to the meaning that the Buddha is liberated from the eight worldly dharmas, and it is for this reason that no fault whatsoever is incurred. One asks: yet the Buddha, too, once encountered these eight worldly dharmas — how, then, can it be said that the Buddha is liberated from them? That is, on a certain day the courageous elder once offered the Buddha three hundred thousand robes, and every such instance as this is named the Buddha's encountering gain. The Buddha once entered the brahmin village of Śāla to beg for food and returned with his bowl empty, and every such instance as this is named the Buddha's encountering loss. At the time of the Buddha's own birth, his fame rose as high as the Heaven of Free Enjoyment of Others' Emanations; at the time of his attaining bodhi, his fame rose as high as the Heaven of the Ultimate Limit of Form; at the time of his turning the wheel of dharma, his fame rose as high as the palace of the Great Brahmā king, and every such instance as this is named the Buddha's encountering renown. The Buddha was maligned with an evil heart by the brahmin girls Ciñcā and Sundarī, his own ill repute spreading throughout the sixteen great kingdoms, and every such instance as this is named the Buddha's encountering disrepute. 6. Past, Future, and Present: Refuting Time-as-Substance and the Denial of Past and Future

A wholly new and famous topic opens — past, future, and present dharma — composed against two rival errors. The Dārṣṭāntikas and Vibhajyavādins hold that time's own substance is permanent while a formation's substance is impermanent, a formation passing through time exactly as fruit passes from vessel to vessel; the treatise instead insists time simply is formation, formation simply is time, as the Mahābhūta-skandhaka itself states. A second error denies past and future altogether, holding only the present real — refuted by the fact that possession and non-possession of a dharma would otherwise be as impossible as possessing a second head or a third hand, and by a relentless string of consequences: no ripening-cause could have its fruit anywhere across the three times without absurdity, no going-forth or ordination could be possible without past buddhas, no one's stated age could increase daily without becoming a false utterance, and, ultimately, without three genuine times there could be no conditioned dharma, no unconditioned dharma, and no liberation at all.

7. The Own-Nature of the Three Times

Past, future, and present dharma are each defined identically, as a portion of the five aggregates, the twelve sense-fields, and the eighteen elements — every conditioned dharma whatsoever serving as their shared own-nature, exactly as own-nature was explained earlier: one's own thing, one's own substance, one's own characteristic. Turning to why time is named time at all, the treatise identifies the meaning of formation with the meaning of time itself, though formations, being momentary, strictly speaking neither come nor go — a puzzle Vasumitra resolves by insisting formations possess no coming, going, or even abiding, time instead being established purely through operative function: a conditioned dharma not yet possessing its function is future, precisely possessing it is present, its function already ceased is past.

9. Does a Future Dharma Arise as Already-Generated or Not-Yet-Generated? Five Paired Puzzles

A run of genuinely difficult dilemmas follows, each with two apparently fatal horns. Does a future dharma arise already generated (then how are formations not simply reverting to what they were?) or not yet generated (then how are they not arising from utter nothing?) — resolved by distinguishing a dharma's own eternal inherent nature from the operative function that only now converges upon it. Does it arise because it already exists, or because it does not yet exist — resolved the same way, substance and function being neither definitely one nor definitely distinct, exactly as impermanence and a dharma's own substance cannot be settled as one or different. Is it this very dharma that ceases when it arises, or does one dharma arise while a different one ceases — resolved by distinguishing the aggregate sense (form arising is form ceasing) from the world sense (future arising is present ceasing). Does the very substance of time arise, or something merely within time — resolved by distinguishing a single momentary formation's arising, which just is the arising of future time, from future time's own many moments, of which only one arises at a time. And does a dharma arise through its own nature or another's — resolved by denying both, since within its own nature there simply exists this kind of dharma that, having arisen, then ceases.

One asks: future dharmas possess an exit but no entry, while past dharmas possess an entry but no exit — why, then, is the future not designated as diminishing, and why is the past not designated as increasing? The Venerable Vasumitra says as follows: has one already reckoned a fixed sum, and only then says that the future is not designated as diminishing, or that the past is not designated as increasing? Since no such reckoning has ever been made, how could one possibly say that it cannot be designated that the future has diminished or that the past has increased? Rather, since past and future dharmas are boundless in their own measure, no increase or diminishment whatsoever can be designated of them at all — exactly as the water of the great ocean, immeasurable and boundless, shows no perceptible diminishment though a hundred thousand vessels be drawn from it, and shows no perceptible increase though a hundred thousand vessels be poured into it. Others, further, say: future dharmas, not yet having arisen and not yet having ceased, are not designated as diminishing; past dharmas, having already arisen and already ceased, are not designated as increasing. Others, further, say: future dharmas, not yet having arisen and already decayed, are not designated as diminishing; past dharmas, having already arisen and already decayed, are not designated as increasing. Others, further, say: future dharmas, not yet having arisen and already departed, are not designated as diminishing; past dharmas, having already arisen and already departed, are not designated as increasing — and exactly as arising is set against ceasing, decaying, and departing, so too, with generation set against ceasing, decaying, and departing, should this be stated at full length in just the same way. The Great Virtuous One says: if an obstructive thing were to circulate through the world, one might then speak of its diminishing or increasing; but a conditioned dharma, arising through the convergence of conditions, ceases the very instant it has arisen — how, then, could one designate the past as increasing or the future as diminishing at all? That past and future form can be form without possessing change and obstruction, and past and future feeling can be feeling without actually experiencing anything, is the same insight driving the entire fascicle from a new angle: a dharma's identity as form, or as feeling, is secured by its own inherent nature alone, entirely independent of whether the operative function distinctive of that nature happens to be active right now.

11. Why the Future Is Never "Diminished" and the Past Never "Increased"

A puzzle follows from the very asymmetry of time: future dharmas only ever exit into the present, never entering from anywhere, while past dharmas only ever entered from the present, never exiting anywhere further — so why is the future never spoken of as diminishing, nor the past as increasing? Vasumitra answers that no fixed sum was ever reckoned in the first place, so no diminishment or increase can be measured against it, past and future being as boundless as the great ocean, from which a hundred thousand vessels drawn or poured produce no perceptible change at all. Others locate the answer in dharmas not yet having arisen, decayed, or departed for the future, against already having done so for the past; the Great Virtuous One insists only an obstructive, circulating thing could meaningfully increase or diminish at all, while a conditioned dharma ceases the very instant it arises; and Pārśva simply denies past and future possess any operative function whatsoever by which increase or diminishment could even be measured.

12. Are Past and Future Dharmas Accumulated Like Present Walls?

A closing epistemological puzzle asks whether past and future possess a genuine accumulation, exactly as a present wall does, or are utterly scattered and dispersed. Both answers carry difficulties: accumulation seems required to explain why a donor's own gift is never wasted, why sūtras can narrate King Mahāsudarśana's past city or the future Buddha Maitreya's reign in such vivid, extended detail, and why the knowledge of past lives or of death-and-rebirth can observe events at all — yet no accumulation seems required to explain why the past and future cannot presently be seen, or why walls alone, and not time itself, are impermanent. One view affirms accumulation, resolving the difficulties through the object's simply not yet having come to be seen; the compilers instead deny any accumulation at all, comparing it to how spoken letters, though never themselves accumulated, still give rise to whole phrases and sentences bearing meaning, and to how a farmer infers past and future harvests directly from the present standing crop — the deepest resolution being that every dharma across the three times, cause or fruit alike, remains genuinely, constantly existent in its own substance, with neither increase nor diminishment whatsoever; it is solely with reference to operative function that existence and non-existence are spoken of at all.
