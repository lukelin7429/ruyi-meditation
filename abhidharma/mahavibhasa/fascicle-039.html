Arising, Aging, and Ceasing in One Instant

Fascicle 39 completes the Characteristics Nyāsa opened in fascicle 38, working through whether a single instant can genuinely hold arising, aging, and cessation together, whether formations undergo real transformation, whether an unlisted "abiding" characteristic secretly exists, and why gray hair and corpses do not appear at every single passing moment despite impermanence's constant operation. It then opens the Meaninglessness Nyāsa with Māra's own direct verse-challenge to the newly-enlightened Buddha, and closes by explaining why every buddha meditates cross-legged. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Can Arising, Aging, and Cessation All Occur Within a Single Instant?

Fascicle 39 opens the Characteristics Nyāsa's second part with a genuinely difficult question: scripture speaks of formations' arising, exhaustion, abiding, and alteration all being knowable — but how can a single instant hold arising, aging, and cessation together, when these seem to be flatly contradictory states? The treatise refutes the Dārṣṭāntika solution, which spreads the three characteristics out across a dharma's whole lifespan (first instant arising, last instant ceasing, everything between aging), insisting instead that every single instant genuinely contains all three, distinguished only by which one's function is presently active. A further debate follows on whether formations undergo any real transformation at all.

The causes and conditions for speaking of no transformation are these: every dharma abides in its own substance — its own self, its own particular thing, its own inherent nature, its own particular characteristic — possessing no transformation at all. Further, transformation is of two kinds: first, transformation of one's own substance; second, transformation of one's own function. Further, transformation is again of two kinds: first, transformation of one's own substance; second, transformation of capability. If one speaks with reference to transformation of substance, it should be said that formations possess no transformation, since their own substance undergoes no alteration; if one speaks with reference to transformation of capability, it should be said that formations do indeed possess transformation, since, in the future period, there exists the capability of arising and the like, in the present period, the capability of ceasing and the like, and, in the past period, the capability of bestowing a fruit — and it is on this account that transformation is said to exist. Further, transformation is again of two kinds: first, transformation of the thing itself; second, transformation of the time-period. If one speaks with reference to transformation of the thing, it should be said that formations possess no transformation, since a thing, across all time, undergoes no alteration; if one speaks with reference to transformation of the time-period, it should be said that formations do indeed possess transformation, since there exists alteration among the future, present, and past time-periods — and it is on this account that transformation is said to exist. Given that transformation may be spoken of in these several ways, no contradiction or fault follows from also speaking of the characteristics as distinct. One asks: are the conditioned characteristics, with respect to the conditioned dharmas they characterize, particular characteristics, or shared characteristics? Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between a dharma's substance, present all at once within an instant, and its functions, which occur in strict sequence within that same instant, is the load-bearing move here: it lets the treatise affirm that arising, aging, and cessation are all genuinely there together without collapsing into the absurdity of a single dharma simultaneously being born and dying in some ordinary, extended sense — much as a single object can simultaneously possess the properties of weight and color without those properties ever competing for the same job.

2. Is a Characteristic a Thing's Own Property, or Something Shared?

A further puzzle follows: are the four conditioned characteristics particular to each individual dharma, or shared in common across every conditioned dharma alike? Both horns seem to lead to trouble — particular characteristics seem to force an awkward one-dharma-many-characteristics arithmetic, while shared characteristics seem to erase what makes each dharma's own arising distinctively its own. The treatise settles on "shared characteristic," but immediately subdivides this into a shared characteristic of one's own substance (each dharma genuinely possesses its own arising, abiding, alteration, and cessation) and a shared characteristic of harmonious union (each dharma is conjoined with these four in the very same structural way every other dharma is) — resolving the tension by separating what a dharma has from how it has it.

Chinese Source

如一色法有多種相。所謂如病如癰如箭。乃至廣說百四十相。然此自相非如四大種堅濕煖動相。但一一法各各別有生住異滅故名自相。復次自相有二種。一者主自相。二者客自相。此有為相是有為法客自相非主自相。故一法有四相亦無有失。復次自相有二種。一者本性自相。二者他合自相。此有為相是有為法。他合自相。非本性自相故一法有四相亦無有失。有餘師說。此是共相。問若爾云何一切有為法各各別有四相耶。答以相似故名為共相。如一法上有生等四。餘法亦然非如一縷貫在眾花故名共相。復有說者。此非自相亦非共相。諸有為法生住異滅名義同故體各別故。然此生等是法標印。若有此者知是有為。如大士相於彼大士。不名自相亦非共相但是標印。若有此者知是大士。生等亦然評曰。應作是說。此是共相。然共相有二種。一者自體共相。謂一一有為法自體各有生等四義。二者和合共相。謂一一有為法各與生等四相和合。此四但是和合共相問生相復有餘生相不。設爾何失。若有者此復有餘。此復有餘如是展轉應成無窮。若無者誰生此生而生他耶。答應作是說。生復有生。問若爾生相應成無窮。有作是說。許此無窮亦無有失。三世寬博豈無住處。由是因緣生死難斷難破難越眾苦生長連鎖無窮。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as a single material dharma possesses many characteristics — spoken of as being like a disease, like a boil, like an arrow, and so on, at length, up through a hundred and forty characteristics — and yet these particular characteristics are not like the characteristics of hardness, moisture, warmth, and motion belonging to the four great elements; rather, it is simply that each individual dharma separately possesses its own arising, abiding, alteration, and cessation, and this is therefore called a particular characteristic. Further, a particular characteristic is of two kinds: first, a primary particular characteristic; second, a guest particular characteristic. These conditioned characteristics are the conditioned dharma's own guest particular characteristic, not its primary particular characteristic — and it is for this reason that a single dharma possessing four characteristics involves no fault. Further, a particular characteristic is again of two kinds: first, an inherent particular characteristic; second, an adventitiously-conjoined particular characteristic. These conditioned characteristics are the conditioned dharma's own adventitiously-conjoined particular characteristic, not its inherent particular characteristic — and it is for this reason that a single dharma possessing four characteristics involves no fault. There are yet other masters who say: these are shared characteristics. One asks: if that is so, then how can it be that every conditioned dharma each individually possesses its own four characteristics? The answer: it is on account of their mutual resemblance that they are called shared characteristics — just as a single dharma possesses these four, arising and the rest, so too does every other dharma; yet this is not like a single thread strung through many flowers, and it is for this reason that they are called shared. There are yet others who say: these are neither particular characteristics nor shared characteristics — every conditioned dharma's arising, abiding, alteration, and cessation share the same designation in meaning, yet possess distinct substances individually; these characteristics of arising and the rest are merely a dharma's own hallmark seal — whatever possesses this is known to be conditioned, exactly as the marks of a great being, with respect to that great being, are called neither a particular characteristic nor a shared characteristic, but merely a hallmark seal, whatever possesses this being known to be a great being; and arising and the rest should be understood in just the same way. The verdict: it should be said that these are shared characteristics; yet a shared characteristic is itself of two kinds: first, a shared characteristic of one's own substance, referring to the fact that each and every conditioned dharma individually possesses, within its own substance, the four meanings of arising and the rest; second, a shared characteristic of harmonious union, referring to the fact that each and every conditioned dharma is individually conjoined with the four characteristics of arising and the rest — and these four are merely a shared characteristic of harmonious union. One asks: does this characteristic of arising possess any further characteristic of arising? Suppose either is granted — what fault follows? If it does, then this further characteristic of arising would itself require yet another, and so on, unfolding without end. If it does not, then who gives rise to this arising, that it should give rise to other dharmas? The answer: it should be said that arising does indeed possess a further arising.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The great-being's-marks analogy offered by one rejected position is worth lingering over even though the treatise moves past it: a thirty-two-marks checklist that simply certifies "this is a great being" without itself being any kind of property of that being gestures at a third way of thinking about identity-markers, neither strictly particular nor strictly shared, that the Abhidharma elsewhere never quite develops into its own full category.

3. Does Arising Itself Need to Arise? Nine Dharmas in a Single Instant

A classic regress threatens: if the characteristic of arising is itself something that comes to be, does it too need its own further characteristic of arising, ad infinitum? The treatise's considered answer is elegant: when formations arise, nine dharmas arise together — the dharma itself, plus a primary and an auxiliary marker for each of the four characteristics (arising and the arising-of-arising, abiding and the abiding-of-abiding, and so on) — with each primary characteristic generating eight companions while each auxiliary characteristic generates only the one primary characteristic that in turn generated it, closing the loop without ever chasing the regress past a second layer.

There are those who say: granting this endlessness involves no fault whatsoever — the three time-periods are vast and expansive; how could there fail to be room enough to hold it? It is on account of just such causes and conditions as these that birth and death are so difficult to cut off, so difficult to break through, so difficult to transcend, and the chain that gives rise to and extends the manifold sufferings of sentient beings is itself without end. Yet, since this all occurs within one and the same instant, there is no fault of endlessness. The answer: when formations arise, arising is the proper, direct cause of their birth, while every other dharma merely assists — and it is for this reason that this passage speaks only of arising as giving rise to the dharma, exactly as, when a woman gives birth, though many women may be present, and though they assist her, it is the mother alone who properly gives birth, and she alone is therefore called the one who gives birth. The Venerable Vasumitra states as follows: it is only because arising exists at all that this dharma is able to arise, and it is for this reason that this passage speaks only of arising as giving rise to the dharma — though this, too, depends on other conditions besides for this dharma to arise. He further states: if there were no arising, this dharma would not arise, and it is for this reason that this passage speaks only of arising as giving rise to the dharma — though this, too, depends on other conditions, since, absent other conditions, the dharma would likewise not arise. The Great Virtuous One states: it is because the characteristic of arising is preeminent that arising is said to give rise to the dharma — that is, when a dharma arises, though other conditions are indeed present, arising is the most preeminent among them, exactly as, when a skilled craftsman paints or dyes a garment, though other conditions are indeed present, it is the preeminent one alone who is spoken of — and it is for this reason that only arising is said to give rise to this dharma, and it is thereby named the characteristic of arising; abiding, alteration, and the characteristic of cessation should be understood in just the same way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The asymmetry stipulated here — a primary characteristic gives rise to many dharmas, while its own auxiliary characteristic gives rise to just one, and that one is the very primary characteristic that produced it — is frankly stipulated rather than derived, and the treatise says so candidly through its "some women bear one child, some bear eight" comparison: not every explanatory gap needs to be closed by further argument, so long as the resulting system is internally consistent and stops the regress cold.

4. Does the Conditioned Even Have an "Abiding" Characteristic?

The treatise now confronts an apparent contradiction across its own sources: earlier chapters spoke as though abiding were a genuine fourth characteristic alongside arising, aging, and impermanence, yet the sūtra names only three conditioned characteristics, and elsewhere states outright that "formations do not abide." The treatise's own resolution affirms that conditioned dharmas do possess a genuine abiding characteristic, even though the canonical formula names only three — the fourth being left as a matter of surplus meaning never spelled out, and, further, so closely resembling the unconditioned in its stabilizing function that the Buddha chose not to list it among the marks that most define the conditioned as conditioned.

7. The Verdict: Abiding Must Exist, or Nothing Coheres

10. Why Doesn't a Corpse Appear at Every Single Moment?

A parallel puzzle follows for impermanence rather than aging: if every instant carries the impermanence characteristic, why doesn't a corpse-appearance manifest at every single moment of the body's own instant-by-instant arising and ceasing, rather than only once, at the moment of final death? The treatise answers that a corpse manifests only when a body possessing faculties, mind, or sentient status ceases without another such body immediately arising to replace it — during ordinary life, each instant's cessation is immediately covered by the next instant's arising, a mercy the treatise attributes to the sheer, staggering scale of instants (over six billion per day) that would otherwise leave the earth littered beyond bearing. A further puzzle then opens: why do transformation-born beings leave no corpse at all, even at death?

12. Does a Dharma Arise Because It Simply Is Arising, or Because It Meets Arising?

A precise causal puzzle closes out the technical portion of the Characteristics Nyāsa: does a dharma arise because its own substance already belongs to the class of arising-capable dharmas, or because it happens to be conjoined with the separate characteristic of arising? Either horn threatens to make one of the two — substance or characteristic — entirely superfluous. The treatise's resolution keeps both: a dharma's substance must already be of the arising-capable kind, yet this alone is not sufficient, since it is specifically the conjunction with the characteristic of arising, as the preeminent cause among several contributing conditions, that actually triggers the arising — exactly as a breakable object still requires an actual cause of breaking to break it, and the unconditioned, lacking any capacity for such conjunction at all, remains permanently exempt from arising, aging, or ceasing.

13. Birth, Aging, Death, Disappearing: Five Masters, Five Readings

The section closes with the Buddha's own sobering teaching that sentient beings possess birth, aging, death, disappearing, and appearing, their own formations being no more substantial than an illusion or a mirage — prompting five named masters to offer five distinct technical readings of exactly how birth, appearing, death, and disappearing differ from one another: Vasumitra ties them to entering and leaving the womb versus the aggregates ripening and being relinquished; Pārśva ties them to the intermediate state's own aggregates versus the aggregates of a new life; Ghoṣa distinguishes womb-born beings (gradual arising, a remaining corpse) from transformation-born beings (instantaneous arising, no corpse); the Great Virtuous One ties all four simply to a single continuum's first and last moments versus its in-between instants; and Buddhadeva ties them to whether a being possesses material form at all.

15. The True Path, and Why Old Austerities Were Abandoned

The Buddha's own reply comes in verse: he cultivated discipline, concentration, and wisdom — the true, moderate path — and thereby attained both the ultimate pure fruit and unsurpassed enlightenment, not from any incapacity to endure the old austerities but from having carefully observed that they were incapable of cutting off affliction at all. Three named masters then each offer their own gloss on why those older practices are called "meaningless": Ghoṣa ties meaning to whether a practice tends toward the flow of continued becoming or toward its cessation; the Great Virtuous One ties it to whether a practice tends toward the evil destinies or toward liberation; and Vasumitra ties it simply to whether a practice causes beings to fall further into the sufferings of birth and death.

That the old austerities are diagnosed as arising specifically from "the view of belief in extremes" (邊執見) rather than from mere excessive zeal is a precise doctrinal move: the problem was never that those ascetics tried too hard, but that their effort was built on a mistaken metaphysical picture of self and annihilation from the very start, so that no amount of further exertion along that same mistaken path could ever have led anywhere but deeper into exactly the suffering it was meant to escape.

16. Why Cross-Legged Sitting, and What "Face-to-Face Mindfulness" Really Means

A closing topic within this same nyāsa turns from why old practices fail to what the correct posture for meditation actually is, and why. Every buddha and every disciple across countless past and future eons has entered absorption seated cross-legged, a posture the treatise defends through an unusually long list of reasons — it alone avoids the fatigue of standing and the drowsiness of lying down, opposes rather than accords with unwholesome states, draws even non-human beings toward the Dharma, and is entirely unshared with any outsider tradition. The treatise then carefully glosses "holding the body upright," "correct aspiration," and, most subtly, "face-to-face mindfulness" (對面念) — mindfulness that keeps the mind's object of concentration presently, undistortedly in view, with a wordplay reading "face" as both the meditation object and, quite literally, one's own face, the specific place where a practitioner's mind is instructed to rest.
