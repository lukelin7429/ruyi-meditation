What Does It Mean to Cultivate?

Fascicle 163 classifies the pure first dhyāna into four portions by where a practitioner is likely headed next, tracks what survives death and rebirth across them, and defines cultivation fourfold — explaining, via the Buddha's own final nirvāṇa, why an already-attained dharma never cultivates a future instance of itself. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Pure Dhyāna's Four Portions: Regression, Abiding, Advancement, Penetration

Fascicle 163 opens by classifying the pure first dhyāna into four "portions" according to what a practitioner who dwells in it is statistically likely to do next — regress, remain stable, advance further, or break through into the noble path itself — each portion defined three ways over: by its own likely outcome, by which ground it inclines toward regarding as coarse or excellent, and by what it ultimately conforms to (defilement, one's own ground, the ground above, or the noble path itself).

The pure first dhyāna is of four kinds: conforming to regression, conforming to abiding, conforming to advancement, and conforming to penetration. As for conforming to regression: this refers to a state in which, if one abides in it, one for the most part regresses and falls away from it. As for conforming to abiding: this refers to a state in which, if one abides in it, one for the most part neither regresses nor advances. As for conforming to advancement: this refers to a state in which, if one abides in it, one for the most part advances further. Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a single meditative state can be classified purely by its own statistical tendency — not by what it currently is, but by where a practitioner dwelling in it is most likely headed next — is a genuinely dynamic way of carving up experience: two people in what looks like the identical dhyāna from the outside may in fact be standing in entirely different portions of it, depending on which direction their own practice is quietly leaning.

That the very same four-way census yields two subtly different answers depending on whether the intervening life was spent in the desire realm or in the first dhyāna itself is a reminder that this tradition's bookkeeping is never merely formal: the specific biographical path a person takes between two dhyāna-attainments materially changes which portions of their earlier attainment survive the journey.

3. What Is Cultivation? Four Kinds, and Why an Already-Attained Dharma Doesn't Cultivate Itself

A major new topic opens: cultivation (bhāvanā) is defined as fourfold — acquisition-cultivation, practice-cultivation, antidote-cultivation, and elimination-cultivation — with the Foreign Masters' six-fold scheme (adding faculty-restraint and body-contemplation) shown to collapse back into the same four categories, before the treatise tackles a subtler question: why doesn't an already-attained wholesome dharma, once currently manifest, cultivate any future instance of itself?

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The five overlapping etymologies offered for the very word "cultivation" — pervading, repeating, permeating, learning, brightening — each pairing a present-tense sense with a future-tense one, reveal how much semantic weight this single technical term is asked to carry: cultivation names not a single act but the entire bridge between what a practitioner is doing right now and what capacity that doing quietly banks for later.

4. The Buddha's Own Parinirvāṇa as the Proof: Why Repetition Isn't Cultivation

The argument reaches its most vivid moment: if merely manifesting an already-attained dharma cultivated a future instance of it, then the Buddha, at the moment of his own final nirvāṇa — when he manifests every dhyāna, liberation, concentration, and attainment he has ever mastered, one after another — would have to cultivate future instances of them all over again, meaning his very last moment could never count as the complete fulfillment of his task at all.

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, only what arises through an actual exertion of effort is capable of cultivating a future instance of itself — not the mere arising of a dharma already attained before, since cultivating a future instance requires precisely such an exertion of effort, and it is for this reason that an already-attained dharma does not cultivate the future. Furthermore, if an already-attained dharma, once currently manifest, were indeed capable of cultivating a future instance of itself, then, at the moment of the Buddha's own final nirvāṇa, when every dhyāna, liberation, concentration, and attainment becomes manifest all at once, he would, at that very moment, also have to cultivate future instances of them all over again — and, if so, then at the moment of the knowledge of exhaustion, he ought not yet to have fully attained every excellent quality at all. How, then, could that moment properly be called the very completion of his own task? It is precisely to avoid this absurd consequence that an already-attained dharma, once currently manifest, does not cultivate any of its own remaining future excellent qualities. "If one cultivates the pure first dhyāna, does one also cultivate the uncontaminated? Conversely, if one cultivates the uncontaminated first dhyāna, does one also cultivate the pure?" Answer: a full tetralemma should be formed. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sheer scale of this final census — cycling the same roughly two dozen named practices through every dhyāna and formless sphere, then through every possible pairing of pure and uncontaminated cultivation — is this fascicle's own quiet demonstration of its opening insight: cultivation is never a single monolithic act, and a genuinely complete account of "what cultivates what" requires checking every one of these specific practices against every possible combination, one careful case at a time, rather than assuming any general answer could cover them all.
