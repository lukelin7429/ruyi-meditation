The Inchworm at the Blade's Tip

Fascicle 24 continues the Person Nyāsa's second part (補特伽羅納息第三之二), closing the twelve-linked dependent origination's own remaining technical puzzles — why illness never earned its own limb of existence, the chariot-wheel that lets ignorance and aging-and-death share a cause and effect without requiring thirteen or fourteen links — before turning to the Buddha's own celebrated bodhi-tree contemplation, traced forward and backward, and the six named masters who each explain why his own contemplation turns back precisely at consciousness, like an inchworm reaching the very tip of a blade of grass. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 24 continues the Person Nyāsa opened in Fascicle 23, now in its second part (之二). Terms established in earlier fascicles, such as the twelve links of dependent origination and the four kinds of dependent origination (momentary, connected in succession, positional, and far-extending), recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Why Does Painful Feeling Also Give Rise to Craving?

Fascicle 24 opens mid-argument: scripture states that feeling conditions craving, but this seems obvious only for pleasant and neutral feeling — why should painful feeling, too, serve as craving's own condition? Vasumitra answers that painful feeling is actually the strongest of the three conditions, since being oppressed by pain makes one crave pleasure all the more; other masters insist all three feelings each claim their own superior route to sustaining craving's continuity, down to neutral feeling's own quiet claim that even the painless, pleasureless upper dhyānas still give rise to craving. The treatise then works out precisely how each of the five kinds of craving — for union, for non-union, for separation, for non-separation, and born of delusion — arises from each of the three feelings depending on whether that feeling has yet arisen.

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: it is proper enough to say that pleasant feeling and neutral feeling serve as a condition for craving, since one clings to and delights in such feeling, seeking after agreeable things in every direction — but how can painful feeling, too, serve as a condition for craving, when this sūtra states, in general, that feeling conditions craving? The Venerable Vasumitra explained it in this way: painful feeling serves as an even more powerful condition for craving than the other two feelings — that is, the World-Honored One states that, oppressed by painful feeling, one craves all the more for pleasurable things; and, craving pleasurable things, one thereby gives rise to a latent tendency of craving toward pleasant feeling itself, continuing on and growing ever stronger. There are yet other masters who say: all three feelings equally serve as a superior condition for craving. Pleasant feeling, as it were, declares: I alone am able to give rise to craving and sustain its continuity, superior to the other two feelings — for it is because sentient beings crave and cling to me that they seek after agreeable things in every direction, performing wholesome and unwholesome deeds, whereby their continued existence goes on without end. Painful feeling, as it were, declares: I alone am able to give rise to craving and sustain its continuity, superior to the other two feelings — for it is because sentient beings, oppressed by me, crave pleasant feeling, that they seek after agreeable things in every direction, performing wholesome and unwholesome deeds, whereby their continued existence goes on without ceasing. Neutral feeling, as it were, declares: I alone am able to give rise to craving and sustain its continuity, superior to the other two feelings — for even within the desire realm and the three lower dhyānas, craving still arises toward me, giving rise to wholesome and unwholesome deeds that sustain continued existence — how much more so, then, in the higher stages, which possess neither pleasure nor pain, should this fail to be so? The Venerable Pārśva states: all three feelings are equally able to serve as a condition giving rise to craving. The Vijñānakāya states: if any of the three feelings remains uncut and unknown, it is able to give rise to every craving that draws forth the fruit of manifold suffering — and so it is known that all three feelings alike serve as craving's own condition. One asks: how, exactly, do all three feelings each give rise to craving? The answer: craving is of five kinds — first, the craving for union; second, the craving for non-union; third, the craving for separation; fourth, the craving for non-separation; fifth, the craving born of delusion. Not-yet-arisen pleasant feeling gives rise to the craving for union; already-arisen pleasant feeling gives rise to the craving for non-separation. Not-yet-arisen painful feeling gives rise to the craving for non-union; already-arisen painful feeling gives rise to the craving for separation. Not-yet-arisen neutral feeling gives rise to the craving for union; already-arisen neutral feeling gives rise to the craving for non-separation — and it is with respect to neutral feeling that the craving born of delusion, for the most part, grows and increases. One asks: craving is already included within clinging — why, then, does this sūtra state that craving conditions clinging? The answer: the very first stage of craving's own arising is called by the name "craving"; its broadened and intensified stage is called by the name "clinging." Further, the lower grade is called "craving"; the upper grade is called "clinging" — and so no fault follows. One asks: "feeling conditions craving" and "craving conditions clinging" — what difference is there between these two? The answer: if craving takes feeling as its cause, it is called "feeling conditions craving"; if craving takes craving as its cause, it is called "craving conditions clinging." Further, if craving is feeling's own effect, it is called "feeling conditions craving"; if craving is craving's own effect, it is called "craving conditions clinging" — and just as with cause and effect, so too should what is produced and what produces, what nourishes and what is nourished, what increases and what is increased, what draws and what is drawn, and what operates and what follows that operation, each be understood in the very same way. One asks: why is it that the prior limit's own dependent origination takes ignorance as its very first link, while the posterior limit's own dependent origination takes craving as its very first link? The answer: these two afflictions are alike each a root — that is, ignorance is the prior limit's own root, while craving-for-existence is the posterior limit's own root. Further, the prior limit's own afflictions have already perished and been destroyed, and so are difficult to know, and it is for this reason that they are spoken of as "ignorance"; the posterior limit's own afflictions are presently manifest before one, seeking a future existence, and it is for this reason that they are named "craving." Further, ignorance possesses seven distinguishing features, and it is for this reason that it is placed at the very head of the prior limit's own dependent origination: first, it spans all five sections; second, it pervades all six consciousnesses; third, it spans all three realms; fourth, it possesses the nature of a latent tendency; fifth, it is able to give rise to heavy bodily and verbal karma; sixth, it serves as the superior preparatory effort for cutting off the wholesome roots; seventh, it possesses the nature of universal pervasiveness. Craving possesses only six of these features, and it is for this reason that it is placed at the very head of the posterior limit's own dependent origination — that is, among the seven features just listed, excluding only the nature of universal pervasiveness. Reading note — Ru-Yi

Each feeling's own personified boast — pleasant feeling claiming credit for driving beings to seek agreeable things, painful feeling claiming credit for driving beings toward pleasure precisely through oppression, neutral feeling claiming credit for sustaining craving even in realms without any pleasure or pain at all — reveals that this treatise regards craving's own sustaining power as radically feeling-independent: no single flavor of experience holds a monopoly on perpetuating saṃsāra, and even the blandest, most neutral sensation retains full power to keep the wheel of rebirth turning.

2. Ignorance and Craving: Two Different Roots for Two Different Limits

The treatise closes this thread by distinguishing craving from clinging as a matter of degree rather than kind, and by explaining why ignorance opens the “prior limit's” dependent origination while craving opens the “posterior limit's” own — both are equally roots, but ignorance's own seven distinguishing features (spanning all five sections, all six consciousnesses, all three realms, possessing latent-tendency nature, drawing forth heavy karma, undermining the wholesome roots, and being universally pervasive) outnumber craving's own six, craving lacking only the seventh, universal pervasiveness.

Chinese Source

三是遍行性。愛於後有能引勝故。說在後際緣起之初。復次無明有四事故。說在前際緣起之初。一有漏無漏緣。二有為無為緣。三是遍行非遍行。四自界他界緣。愛唯有漏緣有為緣。非遍行自界緣故。說在後際緣起之初。更有餘義。後當廣說。取緣有者。若有煩惱。復能發業牽後有果。非無煩惱有緣生者。若有能引後有諸業。

Ru-Yi Translation

third, it possesses the nature of universal pervasiveness. Craving, being especially superior in its power to draw forth future existence, is for this reason placed at the very head of the posterior limit's own dependent origination. Further, ignorance possesses four distinguishing features, and it is for this reason that it is placed at the very head of the prior limit's own dependent origination: first, it may take a contaminated or an uncontaminated object as its condition; second, it may take a conditioned or an unconditioned object as its condition; third, it may be universally pervasive or non-pervasive; fourth, it may take its own realm or another realm as its object. Craving possesses only a contaminated object, a conditioned object, and is non-pervasive, taking only its own realm as its object, and it is for this reason that it is placed at the very head of the posterior limit's own dependent origination. There is yet a further meaning to this, to be explained at length below. "Clinging conditions becoming" means: if affliction is present, it is further able to give rise to karma that draws forth the fruit of a future existence — not that, in the absence of affliction, there could still be a case of birth arising from a mere condition. "Becoming conditions birth" means: if there is karma capable of drawing forth a future existence, that future existence will definitely arise — not that, in the absence of such drawing karma, birth could still arise.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Grading ignorance and craving against an identical seven-point checklist, and finding craving falls short by exactly one criterion, quietly reframes what might look like an arbitrary narrative choice — why does the story of causation begin with not-knowing in one telling and desire in another — as a precise, countable difference in causal reach: ignorance simply qualifies for a role craving cannot fully claim, missing only the single feature of universal pervasiveness that would let it, too, anchor an entire cycle's own beginning.

3. Why Birth Stands Alone While Aging and Death Share a Single Limb

A precise puzzle asks why birth is established as its own separate limb of dependent origination while aging and death are combined into a single limb together. Five separate masters offer five distinct explanations — Pārśva declining to press the question at all, since the Buddha alone fully grasps every dharma's own functional nuance; others noting that arising alone possesses real function while aging and impermanence share theirs; Vasumitra observing that birth moves a dharma from future into present while aging-death moves it from present into past; and Ghoṣa comparing birth's solitary, superior function to a strong person accomplishing a task alone, versus aging-and-death's shared, inferior function requiring two working together.

Chinese Source

後有當生非無引業。生緣老死者。謂若有生便有老死。問何故三有為相中生獨立一支。老死共立一支耶。脇尊者曰。世尊於法功能差別能善了知。餘無此能故。於此事不須徵詰。復有說者。諸法生時生有作用。故獨立支。諸法滅時老死無常俱有作用。故合立支。有餘師說。生令諸法相續增長。故獨立支。老死令諸法不相續不增長。故合立支。或復有說。生令諸法和合作用。故獨立支。老死令諸法離散無用。故合立支。尊者世友作如是說。生令諸法從未來入現在。故獨立支。老死令諸法從現在入過去。故合立支。尊者妙音作如是說。生作用勝獨辦一事。故獨立支。老死作用劣共辦一事。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Birth conditions aging-and-death" means: wherever there is birth, there is necessarily aging-and-death. One asks: why is it that, among the three characteristic marks of conditioned existence, birth alone is established as a single, separate limb, while aging and death together are established as a single, combined limb? The Venerable Pārśva stated: the World-Honored One is able to skillfully discern the distinct functional differences among dharmas, a capacity no one else possesses, and so this matter need not be pressed as an objection. There are yet other masters who say: at the very moment dharmas arise, arising alone possesses genuine function, and it is for this reason that it is established as a single, separate limb; at the very moment dharmas cease, aging and impermanence together possess function, and it is for this reason that they are established as a single, combined limb. There are yet other masters who say: birth causes dharmas to continue on and increase, and it is for this reason that it is established as a single, separate limb; aging-and-death causes dharmas to no longer continue on or increase, and it is for this reason that they are established as a single, combined limb. There are yet others who say: birth causes dharmas to come together and function, and it is for this reason that it is established as a single, separate limb; aging-and-death causes dharmas to disperse and become without function, and it is for this reason that they are established as a single, combined limb. The Venerable Vasumitra explained it in this way: birth causes dharmas to pass from the future into the present, and it is for this reason that it is established as a single, separate limb; aging-and-death causes dharmas to pass from the present into the past, and it is for this reason that they are established as a single, combined limb. The Venerable Ghoṣa explained it in this way: birth's own function is superior, able to accomplish its task alone, and it is for this reason that it is established as a single, separate limb; aging-and-death's own function is inferior, accomplishing their task only together,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ghoṣa's strong-person-versus-weak-person image gives this technical grammatical asymmetry a genuinely human shape: some functions in the causal chain are powerful enough to stand as their own distinct event, while others are only substantial enough to register as a single joint occurrence — the twelve-link schema's own architecture, on this reading, directly mirrors differences in causal strength rather than being an arbitrary counting convention.

4. Why Illness Never Made the List: The Monk Who Never Had a Headache

A further puzzle asks why illness, and the five griefs of sorrow, lamentation, suffering, distress, and affliction that aging-and-death conditions, are never established as their own limbs of existence, despite being universal features of ordinary suffering. The treatise's answer is structural: a genuine limb of existence must be present at every time, in every place, for every being — and illness plainly is not, vividly illustrated by the Venerable Vakkula's own declaration that, past the age of eighty, he could not recall so much as a single headache.

Chinese Source

我於佛法出家。年過八十尚不憶有少頭痛。況餘身病。彼在欲界贍部洲生。尚無少病。況餘界餘處。病不遍故不立有支。問此契經說老死緣愁悲苦憂惱。何故愁等不立有支。答無支相故。謂愁等五散壞有支。如霜雹等害諸苗稼。復次愁等非一切時。非一切處。非一切有。猶如疾病。是故愁等不立有支。問此愁等五不應但說老死為緣。以無明等十二有支為緣生故。答此經應說無明緣行及愁等五乃至。生緣老死及愁等五。而不說者是有餘說。復次應知此經以終顯始。老死為緣既生愁等。應知乃至無明亦爾。復次老死位中多起愁等。是故偏說。復次。老死位中。所起愁等多是上品。是故偏說。

Ru-Yi Translation

"since I went forth from household life within the Buddha's own teaching, having now passed eighty years of age, I still cannot recall even the slightest headache, let alone any other bodily illness." He was born within the desire realm, in the continent of Jambudvīpa, and yet was virtually without even the slightest illness — how much less so, then, in other realms or other places. Since illness is not universal in this way, it is not established as a limb of existence. One asks: this sūtra states that aging-and-death conditions sorrow, lamentation, suffering, distress, and affliction — why, then, are sorrow and the rest not likewise established as their own limbs of existence? The answer: they, too, lack the very characteristic proper to a limb of existence — that is, sorrow and these other four scatter and destroy the very limbs of existence, just as frost or hail damages growing crops. Further, sorrow and the rest are not present at every time, in every place, or for every being, exactly like illness, and it is for this reason that they are not established as limbs of existence. One asks: this sūtra ought not to state that sorrow and the rest take only aging-and-death as their condition, since every one of the twelve limbs of existence, taken as condition, is able to give rise to them as well — why, then, does this sūtra mention only aging-and-death as their condition? The answer: this sūtra ought indeed to state that ignorance conditions the formations, together with sorrow and the rest, and so on, up through birth conditioning aging-and-death, together with sorrow and the rest — yet it does not so state, and this should be understood as a matter left incomplete. Further, this sūtra should be understood as revealing the beginning through its very ending — since aging-and-death, as condition, does give rise to sorrow and the rest, it should likewise be understood that ignorance, too, works in just the same way. Further, it is at the very stage of aging-and-death that sorrow and the rest arise most frequently, and it is for this reason that this stage alone is singled out for mention.

復次。造惡業者。毀淨戒者。於此位中多生愁等。是故偏說。如契經說。若男若女造身語意三種惡行。或破尸羅。臨命終時惡趣相現。如日欲暮大山峯影來覆其身。當於爾時。身心驚怖生大苦惱。

Further, whatever sorrow and the rest arise at the very stage of aging-and-death tend, for the most part, to be of the upper grade, and it is for this reason that this stage alone is singled out for mention. Further, those who commit evil karma, and those who break the pure moral precepts, most often give rise to sorrow and the rest specifically at this very stage, and it is for this reason that this stage alone is singled out for mention — as the sūtra states: if a man or a woman commits the three kinds of evil bodily, verbal, and mental conduct, or breaks the moral precepts, then, at the very moment of the approach of death, the sign of an evil destiny appears before them, just as, when the sun is about to set, the shadow of a great mountain's peak comes to cover their own body — and, at that very time, body and mind alike become terrified, giving rise to great suffering and anguish,

乃至廣說。是故但說老死為緣。問無明為有因不。老死為有果不。

and so on, as elaborated at length — and it is for this reason that this sūtra speaks only of aging-and-death as their condition. One asks: does ignorance itself have a cause? Does aging-and-death itself have an effect?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Vakkula's boast functions as a deliberately extreme counterexample rather than mere biographical color: if even one human being, in the one realm and continent where illness is supposedly most common, can go an entire lifetime essentially disease-free, then illness plainly fails the universality test a genuine limb of existence must pass — and the same argument, once granted, immediately rules out grief and sorrow as well, since these too can be entirely absent from a given life or entirely absent from realms with no capacity for such distress at all.

5. Does Ignorance Have Its Own Cause? The Wheel That Turns Without Beginning

A genuinely difficult puzzle threatens the whole twelve-link architecture: if ignorance itself has no cause and aging-and-death has no effect, they would seem to qualify as unconditioned; but if they do have a cause and effect, dependent origination would seem to require thirteen or fourteen links instead of twelve. The treatise's resolution distinguishes having a cause and effect from being separately established as a limb of existence in one's own right — ignorance's own cause is simply improper attention, and aging-and-death's own effect is simply grief — with a further, striking resolution offered by other masters: present craving and clinging simply are what becomes past ignorance once the wheel turns again, and present name-and-form through feeling simply become future aging-and-death, so that the twelve links form a single beginningless wheel, endlessly cycling, never actually requiring a thirteenth or fourteenth spoke.

Chinese Source

設爾何失。若有者。緣起支應有十三或十四。若無者。無明無因老死無果。應是無為。答應作是說。無明老死雖有因果。而非有支。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose either — what fault follows? If they do, then dependent origination ought to have thirteen or fourteen limbs. If they do not, then ignorance would be without a cause and aging-and-death without an effect, and they would then have to be unconditioned. The answer: it should be said that ignorance and aging-and-death do indeed possess a cause and an effect, and yet are still not, in their own right, separately established limbs of existence —

故無十三十四支失。無明因者。謂不如理作意。老死果者。謂愁悲苦憂惱復有說者。無明有因。謂前無明。老死有果。謂後老死。過去未來無明老死有多剎那。故無十三十四支失。有餘師說。無明有因。謂前老死。老死有果。謂後無明。以現在愛取即過去無明。現在名色六處觸受即未來老死。若說受緣愛即說老死緣無明。猶如車輪上下迴轉。終而復始。如是有支無始相續。雖有因果而無十三十四支失。

and so no fault of there being thirteen or fourteen limbs follows. Further, if, once a karma is performed, it is neither relinquished, nor renounced, nor countered by any antidote, it brings both; if, once performed, it can be relinquished, renounced, and countered by an antidote, it is only "creating." Further, if a karma is constantly recollected with full awareness across all three time-periods, it brings both; if not, it is only "creating." Further, if, once a karma is performed, no regret or change of heart follows, it brings both; if regret or a change of heart follows, it is only "creating." Further, if, once a karma is performed, it is constantly remembered, it brings both; if it is not constantly remembered, it is only "creating." Further, if a karma's own task is brought to full completion, it brings both; if not brought to completion, it is only "creating." Further, if a karma is performed repeatedly, it brings both; if not performed repeatedly, it is only "creating." Further, if, once a karma is performed, one rejoices in it, praises it, and dedicates it toward its fruit, it brings both; if not, it is only "creating." Further, karma performed with a clear and lucid mind brings both; karma performed without such clarity is only "creating." All such distinctions as these constitute the very difference between the two.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sheer length of this closing catalogue — deliberateness, completion, repetition, freedom from regret, constant recollection, dedication, and mental clarity, each doubled across a wholesome and an unwholesome version — reveals that “increasing” a karma, in this treatise's own account, is not a single additional ingredient but the convergence of many independent psychological and circumstantial factors at once: a deed only becomes fully weight-bearing, capable of definitely producing its own fruit, when it is deliberate, completed, repeated, unregretted, remembered, celebrated, and clearly performed all together, any single missing factor leaving it as mere “creating” without the further reinforcement “increasing” requires.
