A New Chapter: Every Mind Arises and Ceases Equally

Fascicle 151 opens the Equanimity-Mind Nyāsa, showing that a mind's arising and ceasing never tracks a being's bodily scale, speed, or temperament, cataloguing seven reasons a virtuous mind is called "great," refuting the life-faculty's dependence on the mind, and closing with the four kinds of obtained body and the precise conditions for the attainment of no-perception. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. A New Chapter Opens: Do Giant and Tiny Bodies Mean Giant and Tiny Minds?

Fascicle 151 opens an entirely new nyāsa — the Equanimity-Mind Nyāsa — with a genuinely charming question: given that some sentient beings possess vast bodies (sea-creatures spanning hundreds of yojanas, an asura sixteen thousand yojanas tall) while others are smaller than the eye can see, does a being's bodily scale mean its mind is correspondingly vast or minute? And does a fast-moving creature's mind arise and cease faster than a slow-moving one's?

"Should the mind of every sentient being alike be said to arise equally, abide equally, and cease equally?" Such is the chapter heading, and such is the explanation of its meaning. Some sentient beings possess an exceptionally vast bodily form, while others possess an exceptionally minute one. Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the treatise takes seriously enough to refute the folk intuition that a whale-sized being must have a whale-sized mind, and a mosquito's mind must be mosquito-sized, is a small but telling demonstration of how thoroughly this tradition separates the physical scale of the great elements from the momentary, sizeless nature of mind itself — the two, on this account, simply belong to entirely different categories of measurement.

2. Restless or Calm — Every Mind Still Arises and Ceases Equally

The doubt continues through temperament: does a restless, wind-tossed disposition mean a mind arising and ceasing swiftly, while a grave, settled disposition means one arising and ceasing slowly? The treatise's own answer sets up its most intricate puzzle yet — if every sentient being's mind truly arises and ceases in lockstep within a single shared instant, what happens to a being currently absorbed in the attainment of cessation, whose own mind neither arises nor ceases while everyone else's does? Two competing resolutions follow, along with the opening moves of a rich taxonomy of paired mental qualities: craving/free-of-craving, contracted/scattered, and more.

To resolve this doubt once and for all, the discussion makes clear that, however much a sentient being's own great-element-produced matter may move quickly or slowly, the mind's own arising and ceasing remains entirely equal throughout. Moreover, some sentient beings possess a restless, agitated demeanor, their own understanding tossed about like a gust of wind or like sunlight rippling upon waves; others possess a grave, settled demeanor, their own understanding calm and still like a lamp burning within a sealed chamber. One might then come to doubt: does a being of restless demeanor and tossed understanding thereby have a mind that arises and ceases swiftly, while a being of grave demeanor and settled understanding has a mind that arises and ceases sluggishly? To resolve this doubt once and for all, the discussion makes clear that, however much a sentient being's own demeanor may be light or grave, and understanding buoyant or settled, the mind remains without exception equal in its arising and its ceasing. One might ask: if every sentient being's mind genuinely arises and ceases equally, why, then, do demeanor and understanding differ in weight and buoyancy at all? Answer: some sentient beings give rise to many minds across many objects, while others give rise to many minds upon a single object alone. If a being gives rise to many minds across many different objects, that being's demeanor will be restless and understanding tossed about; if a being gives rise to many minds upon a single object alone, that being's demeanor will be grave and understanding settled. It is for reasons such as these that the present discussion is raised. "Should the mind of every sentient being alike be said to arise equally, abide equally, and cease equally?" Answer: yes, it should. One might ask: does this mean that every single sentient being's mind arises and ceases equally, all of them together arising in a single instant and ceasing in a single instant? Or does it instead mean that not every sentient being's mind arises and ceases equally, nor do they all arise and cease within a single instant together? Whichever position is taken, what fault would follow? If every sentient being's mind does arise and cease equally, all together within a single instant, this might hold well enough for those actually possessing mind at that instant — but what of those in a mindless state, such as one who has entered the attainment of no-perception or the attainment of cessation? At that very moment, every other sentient being's mind is both arising and ceasing, while that one's own mind only ceases without arising; and when emerging from that same attainment, every other sentient being's mind is both ceasing and arising, while that one's own mind only arises without ceasing; and while actually abiding within that same attainment, every other sentient being's mind is both arising and ceasing, while that one's own mind neither arises nor ceases at all — how, then, could one possibly still speak of minds arising and ceasing equally, all within one and the same instant? But if not every sentient being's mind arises and ceases equally, nor do they all arise and cease within a single instant, then how is the statement already given — that every sentient being's mind arises, abides, and ceases equally — to be reconciled at all? Answer: it should be explained as follows: every sentient being's mind does indeed arise and cease equally, all of them together arising in a single instant and ceasing in a single instant. One might then ask: this might hold well enough for those possessing mind at that instant — but what of those in a mindless state? Answer: it holds well enough both for those possessing mind and for those without it. When entering the attainment of no-perception or of cessation, exactly as every other sentient being's mind arises, that very first instant of entering the attainment likewise genuinely arises; exactly as every other sentient being's mind ceases, the mind entering that attainment likewise ceases. When emerging from that same attainment, exactly as every other sentient being's mind arises, the mind of emergence likewise arises; exactly as every other sentient being's mind ceases, that very last instant of the attainment likewise genuinely ceases. And while actually abiding within the attainment, exactly as every other sentient being's mind arises and ceases moment by moment, the intervening moments of the attainment itself likewise arise and cease moment by moment. It is for this reason that the case holds equally well whether mind is present or absent. Other masters hold instead that not every sentient being's mind arises and ceases equally, nor do they all arise and cease within a single instant — for there are sentient beings whose mind ceases without arising, as with one entering the attainment of no-perception or of cessation; there are sentient beings whose mind arises without ceasing, as with one emerging from that same attainment; there are sentient beings whose mind neither arises nor ceases, as with one actually abiding within that same attainment; and there are sentient beings whose mind both arises and ceases, as with one who genuinely possesses mind throughout. One might ask: how, then, is the statement already given to be reconciled? Answer: this is spoken with reference to an equality of scale — lest one suppose that mind itself might be greater or lesser in size, the statement that every sentient being's mind is "equal" is made on this basis. On this account, every sentient being's mind does indeed arise and cease equally, all together within a single instant, since every conditioned dharma, once having arisen from its conditions, invariably ceases again. "Should the mind possessing craving, and the mind free of craving, both be said to arise equally, abide equally, and cease equally?" Answer: yes. The same holds for the mind possessing hatred and the mind free of hatred, the mind possessing delusion and the mind free of delusion, the contracted mind and the scattered mind, the low mind and the exalted mind, the small mind and the great mind, the agitated mind and the unagitated mind, the unsettled mind and the settled mind, the undetermined mind and the determined mind, the uncultivated mind and the cultivated mind, and the unliberated mind and the liberated mind — "should these too be said to arise equally, abide equally, and cease equally?" Answer: yes. One might ask: why raise this discussion at all? Answer: every defiled mind is heavy and sluggish by nature, while every wholesome mind is light and buoyant by nature. One might then come to doubt: does a defiled mind therefore arise and cease slowly, while a wholesome mind arises and ceases swiftly? To resolve this doubt once and for all, the discussion makes clear that both kinds of mind arise and cease equally, and it is for this reason that the present discussion is raised. A mind is called "possessing craving" for two reasons — first, because it is associated with craving, and second, because it is bound by craving. If it were called "possessing craving" only on the grounds of being associated with craving, then a mind associated with hatred and the like, or a wholesome or unobscured-neutral contaminated mind, ought also to be called "free of craving" — yet these too are called "possessing craving," since they remain bound by craving. Thus, although a mind is called "possessing craving" on two separate grounds, the present passage speaks only in terms of association, to avoid any confusion. A mind is likewise called "free of craving" for two reasons — first, because it is not associated with craving, and second, because it serves as craving's own antidote. If it were called "free of craving" only on the grounds of not being associated with craving, then a mind associated with hatred and the like ought also to be called "free of craving" — yet this cannot be right, since such a mind remains genuinely defiled. Thus, although a mind is called "free of craving" on two separate grounds, the present passage speaks only in terms of serving as craving's own antidote, to avoid any confusion. The mind possessing hatred and the mind free of hatred, and the rest, should each be understood accordingly by the very same standard. It should therefore be explained as follows: a mind "possessing craving" is one associated with craving; a mind "free of craving" is one that serves as craving's own antidote; a mind "possessing hatred" is one associated with hatred; a mind "free of hatred" is one that serves as hatred's own antidote; a mind "possessing delusion" is one associated with delusion; a mind "free of delusion" is one that serves as delusion's own antidote. The "contracted" mind is a wholesome mind that gathers itself in around its object; the "scattered" mind is a defiled mind that lets itself run loose upon its object. The treatise-masters of Gandhāra hold that whatever mind is associated with sleep should be called "contracted," since the World-Honored One himself declared sleep to be a contraction of mind — as the sūtra states: "what is sleep, and what is dreaming? It is a state in which the scattered mind is drawn together and contracted." One might ask: how, then, is this to be reconciled with what is stated in the Consciousness Aggregate — that the contracted mind is known exactly as it is through four kinds of knowledge, namely dharma-knowledge, class-knowledge, path-knowledge, and mundane knowledge, while the scattered mind is known exactly as it is through a single kind of knowledge alone, namely mundane knowledge? Answer: that account ought not to be so reconciled, since to do so would contradict what has been established elsewhere, and any account that requires composing a fresh treatise cannot be accepted merely for the sake of reconciliation. If one truly wished to reconcile it, that very passage itself would need to be revised to read: the contracted mind, the scattered mind, and the low mind are each known through a single kind of knowledge alone, namely mundane knowledge, while the exalted mind and the rest are known through the four kinds of knowledge already described. But this proposed reconciliation is itself unreasonable — for if it were accepted, a defiled mind associated with sleep would then have to be called both "contracted" and "scattered" at once, since it is both associated with sleep and genuinely defiled. To avoid precisely this kind of fault, the account already given earlier should be preferred as correct.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The simultaneity puzzle here is a genuinely sharp piece of philosophical housekeeping: having asserted that all minds everywhere arise and cease within one shared instant, the treatise is forced to confront its own most awkward test case — a being with no mind at all in that very instant — and rather than quietly dropping the claim, it works out, in careful mechanical detail, exactly how entering, abiding in, and emerging from a mindless attainment can each still be squared with universal simultaneity.

3. Seven Reasons Why a Virtuous Mind Is 'Great' and a Defiled One 'Small'

The taxonomy continues with the low and exalted mind, then pauses over a real puzzle: since countless beings spend far more of their lives in defiled states than wholesome ones, how can the defiled mind possibly be called "small"? Seven independent answers follow — cost, faculty-association, accompaniment, future cultivation, resistance to being countered, and the quality of one's own "guiding leader," whether ignorance or the illumination of wisdom.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The "low" mind is a defiled mind associated with laziness; the "exalted" mind is a wholesome mind associated with diligence. The "small" mind is a defiled mind, cultivated by only a small number of beings; the "great" mind is a wholesome mind, cultivated by a great number of beings. One might ask: countless sentient beings habitually practice every manner of evil conduct rather than any excellent conduct, so that defiled minds are far more often present before them than wholesome ones — how, then, can the defiled mind still be called "cultivated by only a small number," and the wholesome mind "cultivated by a great number"? Answer: the name "great" or "small" is not established on the basis of sheer numbers at all — whatever is capable of cultivating the pure, bright dharmas is called "great," and everything else is called "small." Throughout the three realms there exists only a single Buddha at any one time, called "great" because he alone fully possesses the pure, bright dharmas; every other kind of being, however numerous, is called "small," since none of them possesses those pure, bright dharmas in their entirety. Another position holds that the defiled mind is called "small" because it is obtained cheaply — a defiled mind requires no preparatory effort and no expenditure of wealth at all, arising continuously the moment even the slightest unreasonable attention is given, flowing on like a great river; the wholesome mind is called "great" because it is obtained only at great cost — every wholesome mind depends upon preparatory effort, and often upon considerable preparatory effort, such that even one who would give away a thousand pieces of precious treasure may or may not thereby succeed in bringing it forth. Another position holds that the defiled mind is called "small" because it is associated with only a few faculties — every defiled mind is associated with only one or two faculties at most, never the full complement of three; the wholesome mind is called "great" because it is associated with many faculties — every wholesome mind is associated with all three faculties without exception. Another position holds that the defiled mind is called "small" because only a few things accompany it — every defiled mind is accompanied only by the three aggregates; the wholesome mind is called "great" because many things accompany it — every wholesome mind is accompanied by either three or four aggregates. The fourth category — bodies harmed by neither oneself nor another — turns out to include an eclectic roster: every being of the form and formless realms, every hell-being, one absorbed in cessation or loving-kindness, faith- and dharma-followers, a Bodhisattva in his final existence and his mother while carrying him, a Wheel-Turning King and his mother likewise, several individuals the Buddha has personally prophesied, the first humans of Uttarakuru, and named nāga kings and King Yama himself.

faith-followers and dharma-followers, a Bodhisattva in his final existence, together with a Bodhisattva's own mother while carrying him in her womb, a Wheel-Turning King, together with a Wheel-Turning King's own mother while carrying him in her womb, a royal seer, one whom the Buddha has prophesied, such as the merchant's son Gaṅgā-Śroṇa, Kṣudrika, the merchant's son Draviḍa, the weaver's son Cāraśreṣṭha, and Jīvaka Kumārabhṛta at that time — every being of whom the Buddha has prophesied that they now abide in their final existence, their work still unfinished — the people of Uttarakuru at the very beginning of the eon, the nāga king Airāvaṇa, the nāga king Suṣadhā, King Yama, and every remaining being of this same kind, none of whom can be harmed at all by either themselves or another. Reading note — Ru-Yi

That hell-beings appear on this same list of the unharmable, alongside Bodhisattvas and Wheel-Turning Kings, is a genuinely counterintuitive inclusion worth pausing on: the category tracks a very specific technical sense of "harm" — the kind that can end a life prematurely — rather than any ordinary sense of safety or comfort, and a hell-being's own suffering, however extreme, simply doesn't count as this particular kind of harm at all.

9. Śāriputra's Question, and Whether He Already Knew the Answer

The Buddha's own declaration that beings of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception alone are harmed by neither prompts Śāriputra to ask which beings these are — setting up a genuinely delicate question about the great disciple's own omniscience: did he already know the answer, and if so, why ask at all? And if he did not know, how could he be called a śrāvaka at the very summit of attainment?

Chinese Source

and who, lacking the confidence to do so themselves, could not bring themselves to question the Buddha directly — whereas the Venerable Śāriputra, possessed of genuine confidence, asked precisely for their benefit, even though he himself already knew the answer. Others hold instead that the Venerable Śāriputra genuinely did not yet know the answer, and so he asked. One might then ask: if this is so, how could he possibly still be called a śrāvaka who has reached the very summit of attainment? Answer: not knowing something, and then coming to know it once it has been explained — what fault could there possibly be in that? Reading note — Ru-Yi

Refusing to simply assume Śāriputra's own perfect omniscience, and instead working seriously through what it would mean for him not to have known, is a striking piece of intellectual honesty: rather than protecting a beloved figure's reputation by fiat, the treatise treats even his own moments of not-knowing as a legitimate question deserving genuine analysis.

10. Maitreya's Own Superior Mundane Wisdom, and Why Only the Peak of Existence Is Named

One resolution turns to the Bodhisattva Maitreya, said to surpass even Śāriputra in mundane wisdom on a certain occasion, ensuring the Buddha's teaching was never rendered fruitless even if Śāriputra himself did not already know the answer — before the discussion turns to why only the very highest sphere is named as harm-free, when lower grounds like the attainment of no-perception could arguably qualify too, resolved through the principle of "raising what comes last to make clear what comes first."

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: although the Venerable Śāriputra himself did not yet know the answer, there were others present within that same assembly who did already know it — the Bodhisattva Maitreya, for instance — and so the teaching was not, even so, rendered fruitless at all. Answer: by raising what comes last, what comes first is thereby made clear — this is precisely the pattern followed here. The World-Honored One, in certain passages, raises what comes last in order to make clear what comes first, exactly as, in other passages, he raises what comes first in order to make clear what comes last — as when he speaks of "secluded from unwholesome, unskillful dharmas, accompanied by discursive thought and investigation, one dwells fully in the first dhyāna, born of seclusion, filled with joy and pleasure," and so on. Just as raising what comes last can make clear what comes first, and raising what comes at the very end can make clear what comes at the very beginning, so too, raising what pertains to emergence can make clear what pertains to entry, and raising what pertains to the final outcome can make clear what pertains to the preparatory approach leading up to it — this should all be understood in just the same manner. Another position holds that the lower grounds are not fixed in this respect, and it is for this reason that they go unmentioned — for a sentient being's own obtained body in the lower grounds may sometimes be free of calamity, as with one abiding in the attainment of no-perception, or may sometimes be subject to calamity, as with the playful and forgetful gods already described — whereas the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception is fixedly and invariably free of any such calamity, and it is for this reason that this sphere alone is singled out for mention. Another position holds that, within the four dhyānas and the three lower formless attainments,

there can likewise occur events of the same kind as the desire realm's playful and forgetful death, whereas the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception admits of no such event whatsoever, and it is for this reason that it alone is singled out for mention. Another position holds that

下地有情皆有煩惱勢力增上。彼若現前則便夭歿。非想非非想處無此煩惱。是故世尊但說彼地。尊者法救作是釋言。此中為自所害者。謂為自地對治道所害。為他所害者。謂為上地邊對治道所害。

every being of the lower grounds is subject to the overwhelming power of affliction, such that, should such affliction become present, an untimely death may indeed follow — whereas the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception has no such affliction at all, and it is for this reason that the World-Honored One mentions only this particular ground. The Venerable Dharmatrāta offers the following explanation: "harmed by oneself," in this context, means harmed by one's own ground's own antidote-path; "harmed by another," in this context, means harmed by the neighboring antidote-path of a higher ground.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even the historical Buddha's own most celebrated wisdom-disciple could, on a specific point of mundane knowledge, be genuinely outmatched by another Bodhisattva is a striking admission against any simple hierarchy of spiritual attainment: uncontaminated wisdom and mundane wisdom, on this account, are two separate currencies, and excelling supremely in one carries no guarantee of excelling in the other.

11. Dharmatrāta's Elegant Rule: Harmed by One's Own Path, or a Neighbor's

The Venerable Dharmatrāta offers a cleaner, more systematic account of self-harm and other-harm across the meditative grounds: a ground can be "harmed by itself" through its own proper noble path, and "harmed by another" through the neighboring mundane path of the ground just above it — a rule that applies smoothly all the way up to the sphere of nothing-at-all, but breaks down naturally at the very peak, which has no noble path of its own and no higher ground's mundane path to reach it.

Chinese Source

初靜慮自體為自所害。謂自地聖道為他所害。謂第二靜慮邊世俗道。乃至無所有自體為自所害。謂自地聖道為他所害。謂非想非非想處邊世俗道。非想非非想處自體非自所害。自地無聖道故。亦非他害。無上地邊世俗道故。由此但說非想非非想處。所得自體非俱所害。

Ru-Yi Translation

The obtained body of the first dhyāna can be harmed by itself — namely, by the noble path proper to its own ground — and can be harmed by another — namely, by the mundane path neighboring the second dhyāna. And so on, up through the obtained body of the sphere of nothing-at-all, which can be harmed by itself — namely, by the noble path proper to its own ground — and can be harmed by another — namely, by the mundane path neighboring the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. The obtained body of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, however, cannot be harmed by itself, since that ground possesses no noble path of its own, and likewise cannot be harmed by another, since there exists no still-higher ground whose neighboring mundane path could ever reach it. It is for this reason that only the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception is said to possess an obtained body harmed by neither.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Dharmatrāta's rule is elegant precisely because the peak of existence's own harm-free status falls out as an automatic structural consequence rather than a special exception requiring its own separate justification: it is simply the one ground with nothing above it and no path within it, and the rule, applied honestly, has nothing left to harm it with.

12. Does Lifespan 'Flow' Even During a Mindless Attainment, Like a Small River?

A new investigation asks whether the lifespan of one absorbed in the mindless attainments is genuinely "in flux," arising and ceasing moment by moment, or simply frozen and static like the surrounding mind and mental factors — resolved by appeal to the Buddha's own simile of a lifespan gradually exhausting itself like the water of a small stream, known to be diminishing through the twin signs of "the exhaustion of the world" and "the exhaustion of the eon."

Chinese Source

住無想滅盡等至。壽當言轉為住耶。答當言轉。問何故作此論。答為令疑者得決定故。謂住無想滅盡等至諸心心所一切不行。爾時心心所法不起不滅。勿謂此位所有壽行。如心心所亦不起滅但凝然住。為除此疑顯此位壽念念起滅故作斯論。問云何名轉。答剎那剎那起滅相續故名為轉。則是遷流非凝住義。如世尊說人壽漸盡如小河水。若諸有情壽起便住。云何知彼壽漸盡耶。答由世盡劫盡故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"For one abiding in the attainment of no-perception or of cessation, should the lifespan be said to be in flux, or simply static?" Answer: it should be said to be in flux. One might ask: why raise this discussion at all? Answer: to resolve a doubt for those who remain uncertain. For one abiding in the attainment of no-perception or of cessation, every mind and mental factor is altogether inoperative; at that time, mind and mental factors neither arise nor cease at all. One might then come to doubt that the life-force present at that stage, exactly like mind and mental factors, likewise simply neither arises nor ceases but remains frozen and static. To dispel precisely this doubt, and to make clear that the life-force at this stage genuinely arises and ceases moment by moment, the present discussion is raised. One might ask: what, exactly, does "in flux" mean here? Answer: arising and ceasing continuously from moment to moment is what is meant by "in flux" — this is a matter of continuous flowing, not a matter of frozen, static abiding. As the World-Honored One himself declared, a person's own lifespan gradually exhausts itself, exactly like the water of a small stream. If a sentient being's lifespan, once arisen, simply abides thereafter, how, then, can it be known that the lifespan is gradually being exhausted at all? Answer: this is known through the exhaustion of the world and the exhaustion of the eon.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even a state defined by the total absence of mind and mental factors still involves a genuinely flowing, moment-by-moment life-process underneath is a subtle but important distinction: stillness of mind, on this account, is never the same thing as stillness of existence itself, which continues its own quiet, continuous flow regardless of what mind is or isn't doing.

13. Why the Buddha Singles Out the Human Lifespan for His Small-River Simile

Since the five aggregates all share this same gradual exhaustion, and every destiny of rebirth undergoes it, the treatise asks why the Buddha's small-river simile specifically names the human lifespan alone — answered through six complementary explanations, from the life-faculty sustaining the other aggregates to the sheer historical drama of human lifespans shrinking from the immeasurable ages of the eon's beginning down to a mere ten years.

Chinese Source

Answer: the World-Honored One here raises the gradual exhaustion of the lifespan specifically in order to make clear that all five aggregates likewise share in this same gradual exhaustion. Another position holds that the lifespan is the most excellent among the five, and the Buddha, by raising what is most excellent, thereby makes clear what is lesser, and it is for this reason that he speaks only of the exhaustion of the lifespan. Another position holds that the life-faculty sustains the other four aggregates, so that speaking of the exhaustion of life is already, implicitly, to speak of the exhaustion of the five aggregates as a whole. Another position holds that when the life-faculty is cut off, one's own class of continuity is likewise cut off, and when one's own class of continuity is cut off, the five aggregates are likewise cut off — and the Buddha, speaking always in terms of what is most fundamental, therefore speaks of the exhaustion of the lifespan. Another position holds that the measure of one's lifespan can increase or decrease, flourish or decline, and it is for this reason that the Buddha singles it out for mention. One might ask: every destiny of rebirth shares in this same gradual exhaustion of lifespan — why, then, does the Buddha speak specifically of the gradual exhaustion of a human lifespan alone? Answer: the Buddha, at that particular moment, was teaching specifically for the benefit of human beings. Another position holds that the Buddha's own intention was to take the human case as representative, thereby making clear that every destiny's own lifespan is likewise gradually exhausted. Another position holds that the Buddha himself belongs to the very same class as human beings, and so what he teaches most often accords with the human case. Another position holds that the human lifespan admits of an especially pronounced increase and decrease, flourishing and decline — for at the very beginning of the eon, the human lifespan was immeasurably long, gradually diminishing afterward down to eighty thousand years, and diminishing further still down to a mere ten years — and it is through this that the gradual exhaustion of the human lifespan becomes so plainly and clearly evident, whereas this is not so evident in other destinies, and it is for this reason that the Buddha singles out the human case for mention.

The historical sweep invoked here — lifespans beginning immeasurably long and diminishing, age by age, down to just ten years — gives this fascicle's otherwise highly abstract discussion of impermanence a genuinely epic backdrop: the gradual exhaustion of an individual life is set explicitly against the gradual exhaustion of an entire cosmic era, each mirroring the other at a different scale.

14. A Great River Never Reveals Its Own Exhaustion, But a Small Stream Does

The fascicle explains why a small stream, rather than a great river, is chosen as the lifespan's own simile: a great river flows on so continuously that its own gradual depletion never becomes visible, whereas a small stream visibly overflows during the rains and runs dry in the cold season — exactly as a sentient being's own life overflows at the moment of conception and runs utterly dry at the moment of death.

The small stream's own visible cycle of overflow and dryness gives impermanence something a great river's smooth, imperturbable flow never could: a concrete, observable rhythm a listener can actually picture, turning an abstract claim about universal exhaustion into something as ordinary and familiar as watching a creek rise after rain and vanish in a drought.

15. Closing the Fascicle: Entering and Exiting the Attainment of No-Perception

The fascicle's final investigation returns to the attainment of no-perception itself, firmly rejecting the Dṛṣṭāntika and Vibhajyavādin claim that a subtle mind persists even there — since a truly mindless state would otherwise have to count as death rather than meditative absorption — and precisely specifying, through Venerable Vasumitra's own definition, that this attainment can arise only once one has become free from the Śubhakṛtsna heaven's contamination while not yet free from what lies above it, with exactly seven faculties ceasing on entry and the same seven returning on emergence.

Chinese Source

入無想定幾根滅耶。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。答欲止他宗顯己義故。謂譬喻者分別論師執。無想定細心不滅。彼作是說。若無想定都無有心。命根便斷應名為死。不名在定為止彼意顯無想定都無有心。有執。此定雖無有心但離欲染則能現起。以界同故。為遮彼意顯無想定離遍淨染方能現前。要以第四靜慮為等無間緣故。由此尊者世友作如是言。云何無想定謂已離遍淨染未離上染。出離想作意為先。心心所法滅是名無想定。由此因緣故作斯論。入無想定幾根滅答七。謂意捨信等五根。何繫心心所滅。答色界繫。此依界類總相而說。然唯第四靜慮地繫。出無想定幾根現前。答七。如前說。

Ru-Yi Translation

"How many faculties cease upon entering the attainment of no-perception, and so on, as stated at length?" One might ask: why raise this discussion at all? Answer: to block a rival school's own position and to make clear this treatise's own view. The Dṛṣṭāntikas and the Vibhajyavādins hold that a subtle mind does not cease even within the attainment of no-perception, arguing as follows: if the attainment of no-perception genuinely involved no mind whatsoever, the life-faculty would thereby be cut off, and this ought properly to be called death, not merely "abiding in attainment." To block this position and to make clear that the attainment of no-perception genuinely involves no mind at all, the present discussion is raised. Another school holds that, although this attainment genuinely involves no mind, it can nonetheless become present the very moment one becomes free from desire's own contamination, since it shares the same ground with that contamination. To block this position and to make clear that the attainment of no-perception can become present only once one has become free from the contamination of the Śubhakṛtsna heaven while not yet free from the contamination above it — since it fixedly requires the fourth dhyāna as its own immediately preceding condition — the Venerable Vasumitra accordingly states as follows: what, then, is the attainment of no-perception? It is the state in which, for one who has already become free from the contamination of the Śubhakṛtsna heaven but not yet free from what lies above it, mind and mental factors cease, preceded by an attention directed toward emergence from perception itself — this, and this alone, is what is called the attainment of no-perception. It is for this very reason that the present discussion is raised. "How many faculties cease upon entering the attainment of no-perception?" Answer: seven — the mind-faculty, equanimity, and faith with its four companions. "To which realm are the mind and mental factors that cease here bound?" Answer: to the form realm — though this, too, is spoken only in terms of realm and category, comprehensively; in truth, it is bound exclusively to the ground of the fourth dhyāna. "How many faculties become present upon emerging from the attainment of no-perception?" Answer: seven, exactly as already described.

"To which realm are the mind and mental factors that become present here bound?" Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fascicle's own closing textual observation — that the passage describing entry speaks only of faculties ceasing, never arising, while the passage describing emergence speaks only of arising, never ceasing — is a lovely piece of close reading: the absence of mind during this attainment is proven not by any grand philosophical argument but simply by what the canonical text does and does not bother to say.
