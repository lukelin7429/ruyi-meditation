Abodes of Pleasure, Immeasurable Minds

Fascicle 81 completes the four heavenly paths with the third and fourth dhyānas' own full definitions, explains at length why the dhyānas alone are named abodes of pleasure, and opens the four immeasurables — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — their own-nature, characteristics, and a systematic cross-tabulation. 1. The Third Dhyāna's Full Definition

Continuing the four heavenly paths, the third dhyāna's own canonical definition arrives: relinquishing joy, abiding in equanimity, possessed of correct mindfulness and correct wisdom, experiencing pleasure with the body, the nobles are able to speak of abiding in equanimity, one dwells having fully attained the third dhyāna. Joy alone is singled out for relinquishment, though every contaminated dharma of the second dhyāna is in fact left behind, since joy stands foremost among what is difficult to cut off and most dangerous during the very process of becoming free of the second dhyāna's own defilement. The third dhyāna alone earns the description that "the nobles are able to speak of abiding in equanimity" because it uniquely possesses two obstacles at once — the second dhyāna's own drowning joy, still to be relinquished, and its own supreme pleasure, the very finest pleasure among the pleasures of birth and death, threatening to become an obstacle in its own right — exactly as an experienced caravan-leader warns new merchants of every danger lying in wait along the road ahead.

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, relinquishing joy, abiding in equanimity, possessed of correct mindfulness and correct wisdom, experiencing pleasure with the body, the nobles are able to speak of abiding in equanimity, one dwells having fully attained the third dhyāna — this is named the third heavenly path. As for relinquishing joy: one asks, at the time one attains the third dhyāna, one altogether relinquishes every contaminated dharma of the second dhyāna — why, then, does it speak only of relinquishing joy? The answer: joy stands foremost; altogether relinquishing every contaminated dharma of the second dhyāna, it is spoken of in this way. Further, joy is difficult to cut off, difficult to break, and difficult to cross over, and it is for this reason that it is singled out. Further, joy possesses many faults, being blazing and firmly entrenched, and it is for this reason that it is singled out. Further, at the time one becomes free of the second dhyāna's own defilement, joy serves as an especially great obstacle, binding one exactly as a violent jailer, and it is for this reason that it is singled out. Further, practitioners of yoga cultivate the third dhyāna specifically in order to counteract joy, and it is for this reason that it is singled out. Further, practitioners of yoga, abhorring and detesting joy, altogether relinquish the second dhyāna, and it is for this reason that it is singled out. Further, joy does not exist within any higher ground where any other remaining dharma might yet be found, and it is for this reason that joy alone is singled out. It is owing to causes and conditions such as these, of every kind, that only the relinquishing of joy is spoken of. As for abiding in equanimity, correct mindfulness, and correct wisdom: equanimity refers to equanimity of formations; correct mindfulness refers to superior, wholesome mindfulness; correct wisdom refers to superior, wholesome wisdom. As for experiencing pleasure with the body: body refers to the mental body. There are those who say: at the time the mind possesses pleasure, this also causes the physical body composed of the great elements to possess a delightful pleasure — this, then, is the pleasure associated with mental consciousness, named experiencing pleasure with the body. As for the nobles are able to speak of abiding in equanimity: the nobles refers to the buddhas and the noble disciples, who are able to speak of this to others and are themselves able to abide in equanimity. One asks: the nobles ought to speak of equanimity with respect to every ground alike — why does it speak only of the third dhyāna? The answer: the third dhyāna alone possesses both kinds of obstacle, one belonging to another ground and one to its own, and it is for this reason that it alone is singled out. As for the obstacle belonging to another ground: this refers to the second dhyāna's own joy, which drowns and destabilizes exactly as a rākṣasa, capable of causing a practitioner of yoga to decline from becoming free of the second dhyāna's own defilement, and it is for this reason that it is said one should relinquish it, lest one be obstructed by this joy. As for the obstacle belonging to its own ground: this refers to the third dhyāna's own pleasure, the very supreme pleasure among the pleasures of birth and death, by which practitioners of yoga become attached and cease to seek the superior, sublime merit of the higher grounds, and it is for this reason that it is said, for the sake of practitioners of yoga newly undertaking this practice, that this pleasant feeling is an obstacle, and one ought not to become attached to it. Further, the Buddha and his disciples ought to speak to others of the third dhyāna's own obstacles and faults, belonging to both its own ground and to another, urging others to relinquish them, and it is for this reason that it is named the nobles are able to speak of abiding in equanimity — that is, speaking to others of the third dhyāna, that it possesses a supreme, pleasant feeling capable of causing sentient beings to become attached and confused, ceasing to seek the superior, sublime merit of the higher grounds, saying: you should abide in correct mindfulness and correct knowledge, and not be obstructed by this pleasure; and speaking, too, to others of the second dhyāna, that it possesses a supreme feeling of joy capable of causing sentient beings to be drowned and destabilized, declining from becoming free of the second dhyāna's own defilement, saying: you should abide in correct mindfulness and equanimity, and not be obstructed by this joy — exactly as an experienced caravan-leader tells new merchants of the faults present in every city and town, saying: in such-and-such a city, in such-and-such a town, there are many prostitutes, gamblers, swindlers, taverns, and bandits, and you ought to guard against them from afar, lest you lose your own wealth and goods. As for one dwells having fully attained the third dhyāna: this refers to having obtained, acquired, and attained the possession of the third dhyāna's own wholesome five aggregates, this obtaining, acquiring, and attaining of possession being named dwelling having fully attained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The caravan-leader's warning is a striking image for what the Buddha and his noble disciples are said to be doing when they speak of the third dhyāna's own dangers: they are not merely describing a meditative state from the outside, but actively cautioning fellow travelers — future practitioners — about a specific, foreseeable temptation on a road they themselves have already walked, exactly as a merchant who has already survived a journey warns those setting out after him.

2. The Fourth Dhyāna's Full Definition: Why "Purity" Alone?

The fourth dhyāna's own definition follows: pleasure cut off, pain cut off, joy and sorrow already submerged as before, feeling neither painful nor pleasurable, equanimity and mindfulness purified, one dwells having fully attained the fourth dhyāna. A subtle puzzle arises over pain: since pain was already cut off far earlier, at the very moment one became free of the desire realm's own defilement, why does the definition speak of it being cut off only now? The resolution appeals to how ordinary language works — one still says "come" of someone who has already arrived, or "liberated" of a mind already free, using a proximate expression for what is, in fact, a distant matter — and, further, to the pairing of pain with pleasure, both finally and jointly exhausted only here. The equanimity and mindfulness of the fourth dhyāna alone earn the name "pure," since they are free together of all eight disturbing matters — pain, pleasure, sorrow, joy, inhalation, exhalation, and applied and sustained thought — and free of the three calamities of fire, water, and wind that still trouble the lower three dhyānas' own inner fire of thought, inner water of joy, and inner wind of breath.

As for pain cut off: one asks, at the time a practitioner becomes free of the desire realm's own defilement, the faculty of pain has already been cut off — why, then, does it speak of pain being cut off only now, at the time one becomes free of the third dhyāna's own defilement? The answer: this speaks of what has already been cut off, using the name cut off in reference to what is, in fact, a distant matter, exactly as one who has already come is still spoken of using the word come, as it is said: great king, from what place have you come? And exactly as one who has already become liberated is still spoken of using the word liberated, as it is said: it is by this that one knows and sees that the mind is liberated from the contaminant of desire, the contaminant of existence, and the contaminant of ignorance — at the time one becomes free of desire's own defilement, the mind is already liberated from the contaminant of desire; only at the time one becomes free of the defilement of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception is the mind liberated from the contaminants of existence and of ignorance. And exactly as one who has already entered is still spoken of using the word enter, as it is said: the Bodhisattva enters the true nature, freed from birth, and thereby attains the worldly wisdom at the very margin of direct realization. And exactly as one who has already experienced is still spoken of using the word experience, as it is said: at the time one experiences a pleasant feeling, one truly knows one experiences a pleasant feeling — there existing no one who, in the present moment, genuinely knows himself to be experiencing it, and it is thereby known that this speaks of what has already been experienced, using the word experience. Here, too, it is just the same — what has already been cut off is spoken of using the word cut off, this speaking of a distant matter using a proximate expression. Further, as for pleasure cut off: this refers to cutting off the third dhyāna's own faculty of pleasure. As for pain cut off: this refers to cutting off that very same faculty of pleasure, as it is said: being impermanent is itself suffering. As for joy and sorrow already submerged as before: at the time one becomes free of desire's own defilement, the faculty of sorrow has already been submerged; at the time one becomes free of the second dhyāna's own defilement, the faculty of joy, too, has already been submerged, and it is for this reason that it is said that now joy and sorrow are already submerged as before. As for neither painful nor pleasurable: this refers to the feeling that is neither painful nor pleasurable. As for the purity of equanimity: this refers to equanimity of formations. As for the purity of mindfulness: this refers to wholesome mindfulness. One asks: the lower grounds, too, possess undefiled equanimity and mindfulness — why, then, does it speak only of the fourth dhyāna's own purity of equanimity and mindfulness? The answer: the fourth dhyāna's own equanimity and mindfulness are together free of eight disturbing matters, and it is for this reason that they are named pure — pain, pleasure, sorrow, joy, inhalation, exhalation, applied thought, and sustained thought are named the eight disturbing matters, and, none of these being present here, it alone is named pure. Further, the fourth dhyāna is free of both internal and external calamities, and it is for this reason that it is named pure; the lower three dhyānas possess both internal and external calamities, and are not named pure — that is, the first dhyāna possesses, internally, the fire of applied and sustained thought, and, externally, is thereby burned by the calamity of fire; the second dhyāna possesses, internally, the water of utmost joy, and, externally, is thereby dissolved by the calamity of water; the third dhyāna possesses, internally, the wind of inhalation and exhalation, and, externally, is thereby buffeted by the calamity of wind; the fourth dhyāna possesses none of these three calamities, and it is for this reason that it is said to be pure. Further, the fourth dhyāna's own supporting body and vessel are untouched by the three calamities; its mindfulness is free of forgetfulness, and its equanimity is free of every disturbance, unlike the lower grounds, and it is for this reason that it is said to be pure. Further, the fourth dhyāna is free of every affliction and secondary affliction, and it is for this reason that its equanimity and mindfulness alone are said to be pure, and not any other — that is, there exists an equanimity and mindfulness free of every affliction but not of every secondary affliction, namely, the undefiled equanimity and mindfulness of the lower three dhyānas; there exists an equanimity and mindfulness free of secondary afflictions but not of every affliction, namely, the fourth dhyāna's own contaminated equanimity and mindfulness; there exists an equanimity and mindfulness free of both every affliction and every secondary affliction, namely, the fourth dhyāna's own undefiled equanimity and mindfulness; and there exists an equanimity and mindfulness free of neither affliction nor secondary affliction, namely, the lower three dhyānas' own contaminated equanimity and mindfulness, together with every equanimity and mindfulness of the desire realm — it should be understood that, here, secondary affliction refers precisely to the eight disturbing matters stated above.

9. Why the Dhyānas Alone Are "Abodes of Pleasure"

A long, rich cluster of reasons and similes explains why only the four root dhyānas, and not the preliminary stages or the formless attainments, earn the name "abodes of pleasure." The root dhyānas are easily manifested once entered, unlike the stage of not-yet-arrived, which even years of contemplating impurity or mindfulness of breathing may fail to produce, exactly as a man bound tightly behind his own back finds freeing himself exceedingly difficult, or as splitting wood with wood demands great, repeated effort. Even the formless attainments, though reached after a lower ground's defilement is already gone, remain difficult, since so few sentient beings even believe a wholly formless existence is possible — the elder Nakhalomaka confessing that the very idea fills him with dread, as though facing a deep pit. Becoming free of defilement by relying on dhyāna, unlike relying on the preliminary stages or formless attainments, proceeds effortlessly, exactly as one traveler by boat arrives without labor where another by land arrives only exhausted, and exactly as one crossing a great river by a sturdy boat arrives safely and at ease where another, clinging to a bundle of grass or a floating gourd, arrives only through fear and difficulty. 10. The Four Immeasurables: Own-Nature and the Debate over Joy

A new topic follows directly from the dhyānas, since dhyāna draws forth the four immeasurables — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — and the two mutually reinforce one another as the dhyānas' own supreme merit. Loving-kindness and compassion both take the wholesome root of non-hatred as their own-nature, yet counteract different angers: loving-kindness the hatred that would take a life, compassion the hatred that would strike and beat, or, on another reading, loving-kindness counteracting hatred that arises toward what genuinely warrants it, compassion counteracting hatred that arises toward what does not. Sympathetic joy's own-nature provokes a genuine puzzle: if it simply is the joy-faculty, a feeling, how can the Prakaraṇapāda describe it as "associated with feeling," since a feeling cannot be associated with itself? The resolution treats this as either a copyist's error in the received text, or reframes sympathetic joy's own-nature as rapture instead — a distinct mental factor genuinely capable of being associated with feeling, whether obtained within the same class as joy or drawn forth by joy's own power afterward.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The copyist's-error resolution is a rare and notable moment of textual criticism within this treatise's own method: rather than forcing a strained doctrinal reading onto a passage that seems to state something incoherent (a feeling associated with itself), the compilers are willing to suggest that the received wording of an authoritative companion text may simply be corrupted, preferring an honest acknowledgment of scribal error over an artificially rescued interpretation.

11. Characteristics of the Four Immeasurables

The Venerable Vasumitra assigns each immeasurable its own defining characteristic: bestowing benefit for loving-kindness, removing decline and loss for compassion, rejoicing in another's own attainment for sympathetic joy, and forgetting every distinction in equanimity for equanimity itself.

Ru-Yi Translation

Such, then, is named the own-nature of the immeasurables. One asks: what characteristic do these four immeasurables respectively bear? The answer: own-nature is itself characteristic; characteristic is itself own-nature, since own-nature and characteristic are not separate from one another. The Venerable Vasumitra speaks as follows: bestowing benefit is the characteristic of loving-kindness;

除去衰損是悲相。慶慰得捨是喜相。忘懷平等是捨相。

removing decline and loss is the characteristic of compassion; rejoicing in another's attainment is the characteristic of sympathetic joy; forgetting all distinction in equanimity is the characteristic of equanimity.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Vasumitra's own four characteristics trace a coherent progression through an entire encounter with another being's own fortune: loving-kindness gives before any need arises, compassion responds once loss has already occurred, sympathetic joy celebrates a good already achieved, and equanimity, last of all, releases the very distinctions — self and other, giver and receiver — that made the first three responses necessary in the first place.

12. The Meaning of "Immeasurable"

Why are these four named immeasurable at all? Because each universally takes every sentient being as its object and counteracts immeasurable afflictions of idle proliferation — whether of craving or of view — though only by subduing or distancing these afflictions, never by cutting them off outright. Loving-kindness and compassion serve as the near antidote to the idle proliferation of view, since those given to wrong view tend also toward hatred; sympathetic joy and equanimity serve as the near antidote to the idle proliferation of craving, since those given to craving tend toward attachment. These four are further named immeasurable as the very vast, playful domain of the noble ones, exactly as a wealthy and eminent person possesses immeasurable gardens, palaces, and hunting grounds to range through, and because they take immeasurable sentient beings as their object, generating immeasurable merit and immeasurable fruit in turn.

13. Ground, Realm, and Whether the First Two Dhyānas Possess Compassion

The four immeasurables exist within the desire and form realms, loving-kindness, compassion, and equanimity spanning seven or ten grounds depending on which count is followed, sympathetic joy confined to the desire realm and the first two dhyānas alone. A genuine controversy arises over compassion specifically: some masters deny the first two dhyānas possess it at all, since compassion turns with the aspect of sorrow while these dhyānas possess an utterly supreme feeling of joy, and a single mind cannot hold both delight and sorrow at once without violating correct principle — even undefiled disenchantment there, they argue, is associated with genuine mental application and joy together, exactly as a treasure-seeker digs ever more eagerly the more treasure he uncovers, while compassion's own resolved mental application runs contrary to this same joy. The compilers reject this denial outright, appealing to the definitive authority of the Concentration Aggregate itself, which explicitly states that the first two dhyānas gather within them all four immeasurables without exception.

14. Object, Foundation of Mindfulness, and a Comprehensive Cross-Tabulation

A systematic, Abhidharma-style cross-tabulation closes the fascicle, addressing what each immeasurable takes as its object (several rival views assign each dhyāna's own immeasurable a steadily widening scope of desire-realm and form-realm beings, the compilers preferring the simplest: all four take solely the desire realm as their object), which foundation of mindfulness they belong to (dharmas alone), which kind of knowledge (worldly and conventional alone, never any concentration proper), which faculties they associate with, and their status across the past, future, and present, across the three moral qualities, across the three realms, across the learner and non-learner, across what is abandoned by seeing or cultivation, and across whether they are attained through preliminary practice or through the very attainment of freedom from defilement — the Buddha alone manifesting them with no preliminary practice whatsoever, solitary-realizers through a lesser practice, and disciples through a middling or superior one.

As for taking as object one's own continuum or another's continuum: these four immeasurables take as object solely another's continuum. As for being attained through preliminary practice or through the attainment of freedom from defilement: these four immeasurables are attained through both. It should be understood that, here, as for being attained through the attainment of freedom from defilement: this refers to the first dhyāna's own immeasurable being attained through becoming free of the desire realm's own defilement; the second dhyāna's own immeasurable being attained through becoming free of the first dhyāna's own defilement; the third dhyāna's own immeasurable being attained through becoming free of the second dhyāna's own defilement; the fourth dhyāna's own immeasurable being attained through becoming free of the third dhyāna's own defilement — or, further, attained through becoming free of the defilement of one's own ground or of a higher ground. As for being attained through preliminary practice: this refers to the fact that these four immeasurables, for the most part, manifest before one through preliminary practice — the Buddha alone manifesting them before him without any preliminary practice whatsoever; the solitary-realizer manifesting them before him through a lesser preliminary practice; the disciple manifesting them before him through a middling or superior preliminary practice; ordinary beings, their own lineage being manifold and unfixed, manifesting them variously. As for being formerly attained or never before attained: these four immeasurables are both — every noble one, and every ordinary being abiding within a subsequent existence, being both; every remaining ordinary being being solely formerly attained. There are those who say: every noble one, and every ordinary being abiding within an internal dharma, is both; an ordinary being of an external dharma is solely formerly attained.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the Buddha alone requires no preliminary practice to manifest the four immeasurables, while even solitary-realizers and disciples need some degree of cultivated effort first, is a quiet but significant claim about the difference in kind, not merely degree, between a buddha's own realization and every other being's: for the Buddha, universal loving-kindness and compassion are simply and immediately present, requiring no scaffolding of practice at all, while for everyone else, however advanced, some preliminary approach remains necessary before these immeasurables can manifest.
