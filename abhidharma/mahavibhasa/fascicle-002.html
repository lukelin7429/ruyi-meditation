Twenty Reasons the Treatise Begins Here

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 2 — the true opening of the treatise's technical content: the Miscellaneous Chapter's first section (雜蘊第一中世第一法納息第一之一), devoted entirely to "supreme mundane dharma" (世第一法, laukikāgradharma), the highest of the four roots leading to the path of seeing. The fascicle asks why the treatise opens with this one topic rather than any other, works through roughly twenty separate reasons offered for its priority, reveals a hidden backward logic linking all eight of the treatise's chapters into a single causal chain, defines the dharma's own essential nature against a Vibhajyavādin reading of scripture and the Vātsīputrīya school's rival position, records named masters Dharmatrāta and Buddhadeva disputing whether its nature is volition or mind itself, and closes with a fine-grained debate over grammar, tense, and mental continuity. Fascicle 3 continues the same topic.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. No complete modern English translation of this treatise exists; the English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

This is the first fascicle to show the treatise's lasting structure, described at the end of Fascicle 1: a topic from the root Jñānaprasthāna, opened with the question "why was this treatise composed?", followed by an extended course of argument that records — often without adjudicating between them — the positions of many masters and schools. Fascicle 2 is devoted, start to end, to a single technical term: supreme mundane dharma (世第一法), the fourth and highest of the nirvedhabhāgīya, the "roots contributing to penetration," which immediately precedes a practitioner's entry into the path of seeing. Terms established in Fascicle 1 — the certainty of rightness (正性離生), the immediately-antecedent condition (等無間緣), the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering (苦法智忍) — recur constantly here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Why This Treatise Begins Here: A Sutra Passage on Supreme Mundane Dharma

Fascicle 2 opens exactly where the treatise's true content begins: the Miscellaneous Chapter's first section, asking what "supreme mundane dharma" actually is, and grounding the whole inquiry in one sutra passage.

Chinese Source

云何世第一法。如是等章及解章義既領會已。次應廣釋。問何故作此論。答為欲分別契經義故。謂契經中佛世尊說。若有一類於諸行中不能如理思惟。能起世第一法。無有是處。若不能起世第一法。能入正性離生。無有是處。若不能入正性離生。能得預流一來不還阿羅漢果。無有是處。若有一類於諸行中。能如理思惟起世第一法。斯有是處。乃至廣說。契經雖說世第一法名。而不廣辯世第一法義。契經既是此論依處。彼所不顯示者。今應廣分別之。由是因緣故作斯論。問何故作論依契經耶。答彼作論者意欲爾故。隨彼意欲而作此論。不違法性何煩徵詰。復次一切阿毘達磨。皆為解釋契經中義。以廣分別諸經義故。乃得名為阿毘達磨。故彼尊者於諸經中纂集種種不相似義。分別解釋立為雜蘊。乃至纂集種種見趣。分別解釋立為見蘊。然於所立八種蘊中。皆具分別一切法相。問何故尊者論初先說世第一法。為順次第說諸功德。為逆次第說諸功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is supreme mundane dharma? Once such chapter-headings and the meaning of each chapter-heading have been grasped, they should next be explained at length. One asks: for what reason was this treatise composed? The answer: in order to analyze the meaning of a sutra passage. For in a sutra the Buddha, the World-Honored One, states: if a certain kind of person is unable to reflect properly on conditioned formations, then it is impossible for supreme mundane dharma to arise in them; and if supreme mundane dharma does not arise, then it is impossible for them to enter the certainty of rightness; and if they do not enter the certainty of rightness, then it is impossible for them to attain the fruit of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, or arhatship; but if a certain kind of person is able to reflect properly on conditioned formations, then it is possible for supreme mundane dharma to arise in them — and so on, as explained at length. Although the sutra mentions the name "supreme mundane dharma," it does not explain the meaning of supreme mundane dharma at length. Since the sutra is exactly the basis this treatise depends on, whatever it leaves unrevealed should now be analyzed at length here — it is for this reason that this treatise was composed. One then asks: why should a treatise's composition depend on a sutra at all? The answer: because that is simply what the author of the treatise intended; the treatise was composed in accordance with his own intention, and since this does not contradict the nature of dharma, why press the question further? Further: every Abhidharma work exists in order to explain the meaning within the sutras — it is precisely by analyzing the sutras' meaning at length that a work earns the name Abhidharma. This is why that venerable one gathered together, from among the sutras, many dissimilar topics, and analyzed and explained them, establishing the Miscellaneous Chapter; and gathered together the many varieties of wrong view, and analyzed and explained them, establishing the Views Chapter — and so on. Yet within each of these eight chapters he established, the specific and general characteristics of all dharmas are, in every case, fully analyzed. One then asks: why did the venerable one, at the very start of the treatise, speak first of supreme mundane dharma? This is the classic opening move that every one of the remaining nyāsa (納息) sections in the treatise's other 199 fascicles repeats: cite the chapter-heading terms, ask why the treatise was composed, ground the answer in one specific sutra passage, and only then proceed. It also restates, almost verbatim, Fascicle 1's own claim that "every Abhidharma work exists in order to explain the meaning within the sutras" — and the eight chapter names listed here are the same eight introduced in Fascicle 1, Section 1.

2. Three Ways to Order the Virtues — and the Fault Each Would Bring

Before answering its own question, the treatise spells out three natural orderings a reader might expect, and the specific fault each one would produce if actually followed.

Chinese Source

為依順決擇分先後次第而說耶。設爾何失。若順次第說諸功德者。應先說不淨觀或持息念等。次說念住。次說三義觀。次說七處善。次說煖次說頂次說忍。然後應說世第一法。若逆次第說諸功德者。應先說阿羅漢果次說不還次說一來次說預流次說見道。然後應說世第一法。若依順決擇分先後次第而說者。應先說煖次頂次忍。然後應說世第一法。如尊者妙音生智論說。云何煖。云何頂。云何忍。云何世第一法。若不依此三種次第即所造論有雜亂失。如佛在世尊者大迦多衍那。成就無邊希有功德。於無量法自相共相。無障礙智隨欲現前。勇猛精進恒無斷絕。已能善入阿毘達磨文義大海無邊覺慧。不可傾動如妙高山。為大論王能伏他論。自所立論無能當者。

Ru-Yi Translation

— or in the sequence of the nirvedhabhāgīya factors themselves? Suppose it were one of these — what fault would follow? If the virtues were stated in their forward sequence, the treatise should first speak of the contemplation of impurity or the mindfulness of breathing, next the foundations of mindfulness, next the threefold-meaning contemplation, next skill in the seven bases, next heat, next summit, next forbearance, and only after that should it speak of supreme mundane dharma. If the virtues were stated in their reverse sequence, it should first speak of the fruit of arhatship, next non-returning, next once-returning, next stream-entry, next the path of seeing, and only after that should it speak of supreme mundane dharma. If they were stated in the sequence of the nirvedhabhāgīya factors themselves, it should first speak of heat, next summit, next forbearance, and only after that should it speak of supreme mundane dharma — just as the Venerable Ghoṣaka states, in discussing the arising of knowledge: what is heat? what is summit? what is forbearance? what is supreme mundane dharma? Yet if the treatise does not follow any of these three sequences, does it not then suffer the fault of disorder? While the Buddha was still living, the Venerable Kātyāyanīputra had already accomplished boundless, rare virtues; his unobstructed wisdom, regarding the specific and general characteristics of measureless dharmas, manifested however he wished; his vigorous diligence never once flagged; he had already skillfully entered the boundless ocean of awakened understanding that is the letter and meaning of Abhidharma, and could no more be shaken than Mount Sumeru itself; he was a great king among treatise-masters, able to subdue every rival treatise, while no rival treatise could ever match his own.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A formal "objection anticipated" move: three natural orderings are spelled out in full before the treatise's actual order — which matches none of them — is defended by appeal to Kātyāyanīputra's personal authority rather than any structural rule, echoing the piling-up method already seen in Fascicle 1's account of how the three baskets differ. The four factors named here — heat, summit, forbearance, supreme mundane dharma — are the same nirvedhabhāgīya roots introduced in Fascicle 1, Section 6.

3. The Masters' Answer: Abhidharma Needs No Fixed Sequence

Several masters answer the ordering question in turn, converging on a claim about the genre itself: unlike a sutra, Abhidharma need not justify its own sequence at all.

Chinese Source

今尊者迦多衍尼子亦復如是。何故造論。先說世第一法耶。答諸師於此種種異說。謂或有說。今於此中非順次第說諸功德。非逆次第說諸功德。亦不依彼順決擇分先後次第而說。但作論者意欲爾故。隨彼意欲而作此論。不違法相何煩徵詰。有作是說。阿毘達磨性相所顯非如契經寧求次第。阿毘達磨以廣論道決擇諸法真實性相。此既繁雜。不應於中求其次第。若求次第文但增繁於義無益。復有說者。不應詰問作論者意。以經先說世第一法。廣說如前。今此論師依經造論。故亦先說世第一法。問置作論者。何故經中世尊先說世第一法。答觀所化者分齊說故。謂佛觀所化者。已得下中品忍。未得上品忍。及世第一法。欲令得故說如是言。若有一類於諸行中不能如理思惟能起世第一法。無有是處。乃至廣說。此中如理思惟顯上品忍。世第一法正說自名。故佛世尊觀所化者修行分齊作如是說。或有說者。為止於此多誹謗故。謂他於此世第一法起多誹謗。

Ru-Yi Translation

— the Venerable Kātyāyanīputra was exactly the same. Why, then, did he compose the treatise speaking of supreme mundane dharma first? The masters give various differing answers on this point. Some say: here, the virtues are stated neither in their forward sequence, nor in their reverse sequence, nor in the sequence of the nirvedhabhāgīya factors — it is simply that the author intended it this way, and the treatise was composed in accordance with his own intention; since this does not contradict the nature of dharma, why press the question? Others say: what Abhidharma reveals is the nature and characteristics of dharmas — unlike a sutra, it need not seek a fixed sequence at all. Abhidharma discerns the true nature and characteristics of dharmas through an extensive course of argument; given how intricate this already is, one should not, on top of that, seek a sequence within it — seeking a sequence would only add clutter to the text, with no benefit to the meaning. Others say still: one should not press the question of the author's intent at all — since the sutra itself speaks of supreme mundane dharma first, as explained above, and since this treatise-master composed his treatise in dependence on the sutra, he likewise spoke of supreme mundane dharma first. One then sets the treatise-master's own reasons aside and asks: why, in the sutra itself, did the World-Honored One speak of supreme mundane dharma first? The answer: because he spoke according to the specific condition of those he was teaching. The Buddha observed that those he was guiding had already attained the lower and middle grades of forbearance, but had not yet attained its highest grade, nor supreme mundane dharma itself; wishing them to attain these, he spoke as follows: if a certain kind of person is unable to reflect properly on conditioned formations, then it is impossible for supreme mundane dharma to arise in them — and so on, as explained at length. Here, "reflecting properly" points to the highest grade of forbearance, while "supreme mundane dharma" is named directly by its own name; hence the Buddha, the World-Honored One, observing the specific condition of his students' practice, spoke in just this way. Others say: it was in order to stop the many slanders directed at this point — for other schools direct many slanders at this very topic of supreme mundane dharma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A striking methodological claim sits in the middle of this passage: Abhidharma, unlike sutra, "need not seek a fixed sequence at all," because it discerns nature and characteristics rather than narrating events in order — the same distinction Fascicle 1 drew between what sūtra, vinaya, and Abhidharma each "reveal."

4. Stopping Five Kinds of Slander

The "slanders" mentioned above are now itemized — five distinct rival claims about supreme mundane dharma's own nature, name, realm, presence, and retrogression — before the treatise moves to an extended list of further reasons.

Chinese Source

是故先說。多誹謗者。謂於自性及於名界現前退中皆起誹謗。於自性起誹謗者。謂或有說。信等五根以為自性。於名起誹謗者。謂或有說。此名種性地法。不應名世第一法。於界起誹謗者。謂或有說。此是欲色界繫。或復有說。此色無色界繫。或復有說。此是三界繫。或復有說。此是三界繫及不繫。或復有說。此非三界繫亦非不繫。於現前起誹謗者。謂或有說。此法多念相續現前。於退起誹謗者。謂或有說。此法可退。為止如是種種誹謗。是故先說世第一法。有餘師說。諸有漏法皆不牢固。如糞掃淤泥。誰於此中牢固最勝。譬如醍醐。謂世第一法。是故先說。有說。此法隨順無我。是故先說。謂此論中讚歎出離解脫涅槃隨順無我。非如外典讚歎受用諸欲樂具隨順我執。世第一法有法聲故。既順無我與此論同。非如煖等無有法聲不同此論隨順無我。故此先說世第一法。有說。此法世法中勝。是故先說。謂諸論中此論最勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

— this is why it is spoken of first. These "many slanders" arise regarding its essential nature, its name, its realm, its presence, and its retrogression, in every case. As for the slander regarding its essential nature: some say that the five faculties of faith and the rest are its essential nature. As for the slander regarding its name: some say that this should be named a "dharma of the lineage-stage" rather than "supreme mundane dharma." As for the slander regarding its realm: some say it belongs to the desire and form realms; others say it belongs to the form and formless realms; others say it belongs to all three realms; others say it belongs to all three realms and also to none; others say it belongs to none of the three realms, yet also is not unbound to them. As for the slander regarding its presence: some say this dharma remains present across many successive thought-moments. As for the slander regarding its retrogression: some say this dharma can be lost again. It is in order to stop these various slanders that supreme mundane dharma is spoken of first. Other masters say: every contaminated dharma is fragile and unstable, like mud swept from a rubbish heap — so which among them is the most stable and supreme? It is like ghee among dairy products: this is supreme mundane dharma. This is why it is spoken of first. Some say: this dharma accords with no-self, and this is why it is spoken of first — for this treatise praises renunciation, liberation, and nirvāṇa, which all accord with no-self, unlike the outside scriptures, which praise the enjoyment of every sensual pleasure, according with the grasping at a self. Because supreme mundane dharma carries the very word "dharma," and thereby accords with no-self just as this treatise does — unlike heat and the rest, which carry no such word and so do not share this treatise's accord with no-self — it is for this reason that supreme mundane dharma is spoken of first. Some say: this dharma is supreme among mundane dharmas, and this is why it is spoken of first — for among all treatises, this one is supreme;

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This "many slanders" list is a compact preview of exactly the disputes the rest of this fascicle works through one at a time: which faculties or factors count as its essential nature (Sections 8–11), its name (Section 4's own "dharma of the lineage-stage" objection), and its retrogression, all taken up again in more technical detail later.

5. Twenty Reasons It Comes First

What follows is likely the longest unbroken "piling-up" enumeration encountered so far in this translation — roughly twenty separate, independent reasons offered for why supreme mundane dharma opens the treatise, each introduced with its own "some say," none of them ranked above the others.

Chinese Source

世第一法世法中勝與此論同。是故先說。有說。若有住此法時。名佛出世真實利益。彼於爾時得無障礙受用勝義聖法財故。謂佛出世眾生入法。凡有二種。一者世俗。二者勝義。世俗者。謂剃除鬚髮被以法服正信出家。勝義者。謂世第一法無間引生苦法智忍。世俗入法有二種過。一者破戒。二者歸俗。勝義入法無如是過。隨其種性自在證得自乘功德。無退失故。有說。若有住此法時。無始時來聖道門閉。今創能開。捨未曾捨諸異生性。得未曾得所有聖道。是故先說世第一法。有說。若有住此法時。捨異生名得聖者名。捨異生數得聖者數。捨異生分齊得聖者分齊。捨異生種性得聖者種性。是故先說世第一法。有說。若有住此法時。得心不得心因。得明不得明因。得受不得受因。餘心所亦爾。是故先說世第一法。有說。若有住此法時。捨曾習緣得未曾習緣。捨共得不共。捨世間得出世間。捨有愛味得無愛味。捨耽嗜依得出離依。是故先說世第一法。有說。為斷疑異生性。是故先說世第一法。謂或有疑此異生性既無有始應亦無終。今顯其有終即世第一法。有說。若有住此法時。異生過患。異生變異。異生虛誑。異生剛強。悉不復起。煖頂忍位或有起者。是故先說世第一法。有說。異生起此法已。要至非異生位。方得命終。如異生非異生如是。未見諦已見諦。未得果已得果。未入正性離生。已入正性離生。未起現觀已起現觀。住不定聚。住正定聚。無聖道有聖道。無證淨有證淨應知亦爾。煖頂忍等即不決定。是故先說世第一法。有說。若有起此善根。唯一剎那必無停滯。煖等不爾。是故先說世第一法。有說。若有住此法時。異生身中念住等行修至究竟。煖等不爾。是故先說世第一法。有說。

Ru-Yi Translation

— supreme mundane dharma is supreme among mundane dharmas, just as this treatise is, and this is why it is spoken of first. Some say: when one abides in this dharma, this is called the true benefit of a buddha's appearance in the world, since it is exactly then that one attains unobstructed enjoyment of the ultimate, noble dharma-wealth. For when a buddha appears in the world, sentient beings enter the dharma in two ways: conventionally, and in the ultimate sense. Conventional entry means shaving the hair and beard, donning the dharma-robe, and going forth with true faith. Ultimate entry means that supreme mundane dharma gives rise, without interval, to the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering. Conventional entry into the dharma carries two possible faults — breaking the precepts, or returning to lay life — but ultimate entry carries no such fault; each according to their own lineage, they freely realize the virtues of their own vehicle, without ever losing them again. Some say: when one abides in this dharma, the gate of the noble path, closed since beginningless time, is for the first time thrown open — one relinquishes what one has never before relinquished, the nature of an ordinary being, and attains what one has never before attained, the noble path itself; this is why supreme mundane dharma is spoken of first. Some say: when one abides in this dharma, one relinquishes the name "ordinary being" and gains the name "noble one"; one relinquishes the ranks of an ordinary being and gains the ranks of a noble one; one relinquishes the standing of an ordinary being and gains the standing of a noble one; one relinquishes the lineage of an ordinary being and gains the lineage of a noble one — this is why supreme mundane dharma is spoken of first. Some say: when one abides in this dharma, one gains the mind, though not yet its cause; one gains clarity, though not yet its cause; one gains feeling, though not yet its cause — and the same holds for the remaining mental factors; this is why supreme mundane dharma is spoken of first. Some say: when one abides in this dharma, one relinquishes objects one has practiced with before and gains objects never practiced with before; one relinquishes what is shared with ordinary beings and gains what is not shared; one relinquishes the mundane and gains the supramundane; one relinquishes what carries the taste of craving and gains what carries no such taste; one relinquishes a basis of indulgent attachment and gains a basis of renunciation — this is why supreme mundane dharma is spoken of first. Some say: it is in order to cut off doubt about the nature of an ordinary being that supreme mundane dharma is spoken of first — for some doubt whether, since this nature of an ordinary being has no beginning, it must therefore also have no end; this passage now reveals that it does have an end, namely supreme mundane dharma itself. Some say: when one abides in this dharma, the faults of an ordinary being, the instability of an ordinary being, the falseness of an ordinary being, and the obstinacy of an ordinary being no longer arise at all — whereas at the stages of heat, summit, and forbearance, they may still sometimes arise; this is why supreme mundane dharma is spoken of first. Some say: once an ordinary being has given rise to this dharma, they are bound to reach the state of no longer being an ordinary being before they can die at all. Just as with "ordinary being" and "no longer an ordinary being," so too with "not yet having seen the truths" and "having already seen the truths"; "not yet having attained the fruit" and "having already attained the fruit"; "not yet having entered the certainty of rightness" and "having already entered it"; "not yet having given rise to direct realization" and "having already given rise to it"; "abiding among the undetermined group" and "abiding among the group of fixed rightness"; "without the noble path" and "with the noble path"; "without confirmed purity" and "with confirmed purity" — one should understand all these the same way. Heat, summit, and forbearance are not yet fixed in this way; this is why supreme mundane dharma is spoken of first. Some say: once this wholesome root arises, it lasts for exactly a single moment and can never linger — heat and the rest are not like this; this is why supreme mundane dharma is spoken of first. 生聞梵志往世尊所而白佛言。喬答摩尊說根有幾。佛言。我說二十二根。所謂眼根。乃至廣說。彼經豈亦說根所依。彼此二經根聲不異。一謂根體。一謂所依。非所極成。是自妄執。故定應許信等五根亦通有漏。問若通有漏。彼所引經當云何釋。答信等五根實通有漏。彼經一向說無漏者。所以者何。依無漏根建立聖者有差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

the brahmin Janāṃtapa approached the World-Honored One and addressed the Buddha, saying: Gautama, how many faculties do you speak of? The Buddha replied: I speak of twenty-two faculties, namely the eye-faculty — and so on, as explained at length. Does this sutra, too, speak of the faculty's basis rather than the faculty itself? These two sutras use the word "faculty" in exactly the same sense — one cannot claim, without warrant, that one means the faculty's own substance while the other means merely its basis; this is simply your own arbitrary assertion. It should therefore certainly be granted that the five faculties of faith and the rest likewise extend to the contaminated state. One asks: if they do extend to the contaminated, how then should the sutra passage they cited be interpreted? The answer: the five faculties of faith and the rest do indeed extend to the contaminated; if that sutra speaks of them exclusively as uncontaminated, it is because the various grades of noble persons are established on the basis of the uncontaminated faculties in particular.

有說。彼經唯說聖道。所以者何。聖者差別依聖道說非世俗故。問彼經又說。若全無此信等五根。我說彼住外異生品。復云何通。答斷善根者名外異生。謂諸異生總有二種。一內二外。不斷善根。說名為內。斷善根者。說名為外。彼經意說。若全無此信等五根。我說名為斷善根者。故所引經於我無失。或說。此是經部所說。謂經部師。亦為遮遣分別論者。如前所執故作是言。世第一法五根為性。非唯爾所。

Some say instead: that sutra speaks only of the noble path — because the distinctions among noble persons are established with reference to the noble path, not to the conventional. One then asks: but does not that same sutra also state, "if one entirely lacks these five faculties of faith and the rest, I say that person abides among the outsider class of ordinary beings" — how, then, is this to be reconciled? The answer: one who has cut off their wholesome roots is what is meant by an "outsider" ordinary being. For ordinary beings, taken altogether, are of two kinds: inner and outer. Those who have not cut off their wholesome roots are called "inner"; those who have cut them off are called "outer." What that sutra actually means is: "if one entirely lacks these five faculties of faith and the rest, I call that person one who has cut off their wholesome roots" — so the sutra cited poses no difficulty for our own position. Others say: this is what the Sautrāntika school states — for the Sautrāntika masters, too, in order to refute the Vibhajyavādins' position as described above, say the same thing: supreme mundane dharma has the five faculties as its essential nature, though not exclusively so.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction drawn here — "inner" ordinary beings, who have not cut off their wholesome roots, against "outer" ordinary beings, who have — resolves the sutra's harsh line about lacking the five faculties without forcing those faculties to be exclusively uncontaminated. Note, too, that the Sautrāntikas are cited here agreeing with the old Abhidharma masters against the Vibhajyavādins — a reminder that these schools' alignments shift topic by topic, not into two fixed camps.

10. A Doctrinal Aside: The Vātsīputrīya School's Own Position

A different rival reading is now introduced — the Vātsīputrīya school's own version of "five faculties" — and the treatise pauses to explain why it bothers addressing a school whose position so closely resembles its own.

Chinese Source

有說。此是犢子部宗。彼部師執世第一法信等五根以為自性。唯此五根是自性善。餘雜此故。亦得善名。由此五根建立一切賢聖差別不由餘根。如契經說。若有五根增上猛利。平等圓滿多修習故成俱解脫。從此減下成慧解脫。次復減下成身證。次復減下成見至。次復減下成信解。次復減下成隨法行。次復減下成隨信行。問今此論宗與犢子部。何相關預而敘彼說答為令疑者得決定故。謂彼與此所立義宗。雖多分同而有少異。謂彼部執世第一法唯以信等五根為性。諸異生性一向染污。謂欲界繫。見苦所斷。十種隨眠為自性故。隨眠體是不相應行。涅槃有三種。謂學無學非學無學。立阿素洛為第六趣補特伽羅。體是實有。彼如是等若六若七與此不同。餘多相似勿有疑。彼與此皆同故。敘彼宗遮顯有異。今應問彼。若唯五根是自性善。所餘善法自性是何。若謂彼是不善無記雜五根故亦名善者。如是五根與彼相雜。何故不名不善無記。然信等五與所餘法。同一所依。同一行相。同一所緣。一起一住。一滅一果同一等流。同一異熟。而言五根是自性善。餘相雜故假立善名。但順妄情不應正理。勿有此過。故應說言世第一法根非根性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some say instead: this is the Vātsīputrīya school's own position. The masters of that school hold that supreme mundane dharma has the five faculties of faith and the rest as its essential nature; only these five faculties are wholesome by their own nature, and other factors, being mixed with them, borrow the name "wholesome" as well. It is by means of these five faculties, and not by any other faculty, that every distinction among the noble ones is established — as a sutra states: when the five faculties are heightened, sharp, evenly perfected, and much cultivated, one becomes one liberated both ways; a degree below this, one becomes one liberated by wisdom; a further degree below, a bodily witness; a further degree below, one who has arrived at view; a further degree below, one released through faith; a further degree below, a dharma-follower; a further degree below, a faith-follower. One asks: what does this treatise's own position have to do with the Vātsīputrīya school, that it should recount their view here at all? The answer: it is in order to bring certainty to those in doubt — for their tenet and this treatise's own tenet, though largely the same, differ in a few small points. That school holds that supreme mundane dharma has only the five faculties of faith and the rest as its nature; that the nature of every ordinary being is uniformly defiled, bound to the desire realm, cut off by seeing suffering, and has the ten latent tendencies as its essential nature, with the latent tendencies themselves being formations dissociated from mind; that nirvāṇa is of three kinds — belonging to the trainee, the one beyond training, and neither; and that they posit the Asuras as a sixth, real destiny of rebirth for persons. These six or seven points, among others, differ from our own position; everything else being largely similar, there should be no confusion — it is precisely because their view and ours are otherwise so alike that their tenet is recounted here, in order to make the points of difference clear. One should now put this question to them: if only the five faculties are wholesome by their own nature, then what is the nature of every other wholesome dharma? If it is said that these other dharmas are, in themselves, unwholesome or indeterminate, and are merely called "wholesome" because they are mixed with the five faculties — then, since the five faculties are equally mixed with those other dharmas, why are the five faculties not, on the same reasoning, called unwholesome or indeterminate instead? In truth, faith and the other four faculties share, with the remaining factors they accompany, the very same basis, the very same aspect, the very same object; they arise together, abide together, and cease together, share one and the same result and outflow, and share one and the same ripening — yet to say that the five faculties alone are wholesome by their own nature, while the rest are only provisionally called wholesome because mixed with them, merely indulges a mistaken impression and does not accord with sound reasoning. To avoid this fault, one should instead say: supreme mundane dharma has a faculty's own nature, without being defined by the faculty as such.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The treatise's own answer to "why address a nearly identical rival at all?" is worth remembering as a general principle: the closer two positions are, the more precisely their few real differences need spelling out, or a reader is liable to conflate them. The refutation itself turns on a fairness test — if mixture with the five faculties is enough to lend other factors the name "wholesome," mixture should equally lend the five faculties themselves whatever quality their companions have, which the Vātsīputrīya position quietly refuses to allow.

11. Volition, or Mind Itself? Two Named Masters Disagree

Two masters already familiar from Fascicle 1 — Dharmatrāta and Buddhadeva — stake out still narrower positions, followed by a joint defense of theirs against an objection, and then a devastating rebuttal.

Chinese Source

尊者法救作如是言。諸心心所是思差別。故世第一法以思為自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Venerable Dharmatrāta says as follows: mind and its mental factors are all, in the end, variations of volition — hence supreme mundane dharma has volition as its essential nature.

尊者覺天作如是說。諸心心所體即是心。故世第一法以心為自性。

The Venerable Buddhadeva says as follows: the very substance of mind and its mental factors is simply mind itself — hence supreme mundane dharma has mind as its essential nature.

彼二尊者作如是言。信等思心前後各異無一並用。信等五根為等無間。入見道者。約容有義故作是言。信等五根能入見道。謂或有用信思信心為等無間入於見道。乃至或用慧思慧心為等無間入於見道若爾應用不相應法為等無間入於見道。彼作是言。許亦何失。如仁宗說心體前後。雖不相應而有所緣能為無間入於見道。我宗亦爾。

These two venerable ones together say: the volitional acts of faith and the rest, and the mind itself, arise one after another, never in simultaneous use — so when it is said that the five faculties of faith and the rest serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the path of seeing, this is said only in the sense of what is possible in a given case: that is, the five faculties of faith and the rest are able to enter the path of seeing because, in one instance, one might use faith's volition or faith-accompanied mind as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the path of seeing, and in another, one might use wisdom's volition or wisdom-accompanied mind for the very same purpose. One then objects: if that is so, then a factor dissociated from mind ought equally to be usable as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the path of seeing. They reply: granting this, too, would cause no difficulty — just as your own tenet holds that mind's substance, arising one moment after another, though not mutually associated, can still, by sharing an object, serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the path of seeing, our own tenet holds exactly the same:

信等思心雖復自體無相應義。而有所緣。能為無間入於見道。斯有何失。有大過失。所以者何。若信思心為等無間入見道者。既無精進及念定慧。應有懈怠忘念散亂惡慧能入見道。如是乃至若慧思心為等無間入見道者。既無有信精進念定。應有不信懈怠忘念散亂能入見道。如是豈不成大過失。

the volitional acts of faith and the rest, though not mutually associated in their own substance, can still, by sharing an object, serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the path of seeing. What difficulty could this cause? There is, in fact, a very great fault here. Why is this? If faith's volition alone served as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the path of seeing, then, lacking diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom, one ought equally to be able to enter the path of seeing with laziness, forgetfulness, distraction, and faulty wisdom instead — and likewise, if wisdom's volition alone served as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the path of seeing, then, lacking faith, diligence, mindfulness, and concentration, one ought equally to be able to enter the path of seeing with faithlessness, laziness, forgetfulness, and distraction instead. Would this not amount to a very great fault indeed?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Dharmatrāta and Buddhadeva both narrow the definition further than the old Abhidharma masters' "five faculties" reading — to volition alone, or to mind alone — and both face the same underlying problem the refutation exposes: if any single mental factor, standing in for the rest, could serve as the whole condition for entering the path, nothing would stop its opposite (laziness in place of diligence, faithlessness in place of faith) from doing the same job just as well. This is exactly the kind of reductio the treatise favors throughout: not simply asserting a rival is wrong, but showing what absurd, unwanted consequence the rival's own logic would force.

With every rival narrower reading now addressed, the treatise returns to its own root definition and unpacks, phrase by phrase, exactly what authority stands behind it.

13. Entering the Path: A Question of Grammar and Tense

The fascicle now turns from what supreme mundane dharma is to a subtler question: at exactly what moment is it correct to say a practitioner "enters" the certainty of rightness — and the answer hinges on a close reading of ordinary language's own habits of tense.

Chinese Source

問世第一法。與苦法智忍為等無間緣名入見道。為此滅已方名為入。為此住位即名入耶。若此滅已方名入者。何故此中不言已入。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: supreme mundane dharma serves as the immediately-antecedent condition for the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering, and is called "entering the path of seeing" — but is it named "entering" only once it has already ceased, or is it named "entering" already while it still abides? If it is named "entering" only once it has ceased, why does the text not say "has entered"?

若此住位即名入者。異生聖者應成雜亂。有作是言。滅已名入。問若爾此文應說已入。爾時見道名已生故。答應言已入而說入者。此於究竟說加行聲。如今來聲說已來事。如世間說。大王今者從何處來。彼雖已來而說今來。此亦如是。又如已斷而說今斷。如契經說斷樂斷苦。乃至廣說。離欲染時。苦根已斷。今離第三靜慮。染樂根斷時。乃說為斷。豈非已斷而說今斷。又如已解脫而說今解脫。

But if it is named "entering" already while it still abides, then ordinary beings and noble ones would become hopelessly confused. Some hold: it is named "entering" once it has ceased. One then asks: if that is so, should this passage not say "has entered," since at that very moment the path of seeing has already arisen? The answer: it is proper to say "has entered," yet the text says merely "enters," because, at the very point of completion, it speaks in the register of the preparatory act still under way — just as the word "coming" is used to describe an event already completed, the way it is said in the world: "Great King, where have you now come from?" — even though the arrival has already happened, it is spoken of as happening now; the case here is exactly the same. It is likewise as when something already cut off is spoken of as being cut off now — as a sutra states, "cutting off pleasure, cutting off pain," and so on, as explained at length: at the moment one departs from the desire realm's defilement, the painful faculty has, in fact, already been cut off; yet it is only when one departs from the third dhyāna and the defiled pleasant faculty is cut off that it is spoken of as "being cut off" — is this not a case of something already cut off being spoken of as cut off now? It is likewise as when something already liberated is spoken of as being liberated now,

如契經說。心解脫欲漏有漏無明漏。離欲染時心於欲漏已得解脫。今離非想非非想處染。心於有漏無明漏得解脫時。乃說為解脫。亦於已解脫而說今解脫。又如已受而說今受。如契經說。受樂受時如實知受樂受乃至廣說。彼亦已受而說今受。非於受時可了知故。此亦如是。雖是已入而說今入。有餘師說。此文應言無間而入正性離生。彼言無理。等無間入無間。而入義有何異。如是說者。世第一法。住時名入。問若爾異生應即聖者。入聖道故。答無如是過。世第一法至住位時。苦法智忍在正生位。未成就故不名聖者。苦法智忍雖未已生。以在正生名等無間。世第一法爾時為彼等無間緣。故名為入。由此故說。若心心所法為等無間入正性離生。是謂世第一法。問若心心所法。已入正性離生。或當得入。亦是世第一法不。

as a sutra states: "the mind is liberated from the leak of desire, the leak of existence, and the leak of ignorance" — at the moment one departs from the desire realm's defilement, the mind has, in fact, already been liberated from the leak of desire; yet it is only when one departs from the defilement of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, and the mind is liberated from the leaks of existence and of ignorance, that it is spoken of as "being liberated" — this, too, is a case of something already liberated being spoken of as liberated now. It is likewise as when something already experienced is spoken of as being experienced now, as a sutra states: "while experiencing a pleasant feeling, one truly knows that one experiences a pleasant feeling," and so on, as explained at length — this, too, is a case of something already experienced being spoken of as experienced now, since it cannot possibly be fully known at the very moment it is being experienced. The case here is exactly the same: although the entering has already occurred, it is spoken of as entering now. Other masters say: this passage should instead read "enters the certainty of rightness immediately" — but this is unreasonable, for what difference in meaning could there possibly be between "the immediately-antecedent condition for entering" and "entering immediately"? On this view, then, supreme mundane dharma is named "entering" at the very moment it still abides. One asks: if that is so, would not an ordinary being thereby already be a noble one, since they have already entered the noble path? The answer: no such fault follows. At the very moment supreme mundane dharma reaches its abiding stage, the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering is only at the stage of arising, and has not yet been fully accomplished — hence one is not yet called a noble one. Although the forbearance of the dharma-knowledge of suffering has not yet fully arisen, since it is precisely in the process of arising, it is named "the immediately-antecedent"; supreme mundane dharma, at that very moment, serves as its immediately-antecedent condition, and this is why it is called "entering." It is on this basis that it is said: if mind and its mental factors serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the certainty of rightness, this is called supreme mundane dharma. One asks: if mind and its mental factors have already entered the certainty of rightness, or are about to enter it, are these too supreme mundane dharma?

答亦是。問若爾此中何故不說。答若說正入。當知則說已入當入。若說現在。當知則說過去未來。其相一故。

The answer: yes, these too. One asks: if so, then why does this passage not state this explicitly? The answer: to speak of "presently entering" is already understood to include both "having entered" and "about to enter"; and to speak of "the present" is already understood to include both "the past" and "the future," since their character is the same in this respect.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four similes drawn from ordinary language — "where have you come from" said of an arrival already complete, cutting off pain that has, in fact, already been cut off, liberation already achieved but only now announced, and a pleasant feeling that can only be known as "being experienced" after the fact — are doing careful philosophical work: they establish a general rule that verbs describing a completed inner event are routinely spoken in the present tense at the moment their completion becomes evident, not at the literal instant they occurred. Once that rule is granted, "supreme mundane dharma enters the certainty of rightness" can be read as strictly accurate even though, technically, the entering is already finished by the time it is named.

14. Two Rival Theories of Mental Succession — and Why Both Fail

A new dispute opens: what, exactly, can serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for what? Two theories are proposed and refuted at length, through a sequence of scriptural citations and absurd consequences.

Chinese Source

思心差別論者作如是言。若信思心為等無間入見道者。唯信思心但為同類等無間緣。如是乃至。若慧思心為等無間入見道者。唯慧思心但為同類等無間緣。相似相續論者作如是言。心心所法但為同類等無間緣。謂心與心非心所。心所與心所非心。諸心所中。受與受非餘。想等亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

The volition-mind-difference theorists say as follows: if faith's volition-accompanied mind serves as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the path of seeing, then only faith's volition-accompanied mind can serve as the immediately-antecedent condition of the same kind for it — and so on, up to: if wisdom's volition-accompanied mind serves as the immediately-antecedent condition for entering the path of seeing, then only wisdom's volition-accompanied mind can serve as the immediately-antecedent condition of the same kind for it. The similar-continuity theorists say as follows: mind and its mental factors can only serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for something of their own same kind — that is, mind can only condition mind, not a mental factor; a mental factor can only condition a mental factor, not mind; and among the mental factors, feeling can only condition feeling, not any other factor, and the same holds for perception and the rest.

然諸心所非恒相應。遇別緣起。謂若欣前境起樂受。若厭前境起苦受。若於境中庸。起不苦不樂受。若欲有所領納生受。若欲取像生想。若欲有所作生思。乃至若欲有所決擇生慧。故諸心所非恒相應。若爾已起心心所法。應不皆作等無間緣。彼作是言。一切能作等無間緣。然非無間。如仁宗許。出無想時。雖五百劫無心心所。而用入位心心所法。為今出位等無間緣。我宗亦爾。雖同類法多時間斷而前為後等無間緣。斯有何失。彼言無理。所以者何。有心無心義各別故。又相應法。有即與果有未與果。不應理故。未生心等。非等無間。預作彼緣不應理故。又彼違害品類足說如說云何心等無間法。謂心無間餘心心所法。或已生或正生。及無想定滅盡定。或已生或正生。又若爾者復有別過。謂若從有尋有伺地心心所法無間。無尋唯伺地。或無尋無伺地。心心所法現在前。前地已起心心所法。應非後地等無間緣。不相似故。乃至若從無尋無伺地。

Yet the mental factors are not constantly co-arising with one another; they arise only upon meeting their own distinct condition — if one delights in a preceding object, a pleasant feeling arises; if one is repelled by it, a painful feeling arises; if one is neutral toward it, a feeling that is neither painful nor pleasant arises; if one wishes to register something, feeling arises; if one wishes to grasp its image, perception arises; if one wishes to act upon it, volition arises; and so on, up to: if one wishes to make a determination, wisdom arises. Hence the mental factors are not constantly co-arising. If that is so, then mind and mental factors that have already arisen should not, in every case, be able to serve as an immediately-antecedent condition at all. They reply as follows: every one of them is, in fact, able to serve as such a condition, though not always without interval — just as your own tenet grants that upon emerging from the attainment of no-perception, even though there has been no mind or mental factor at all for five hundred aeons, the mind and mental factors present at the moment of entering that attainment still serve as the immediately-antecedent condition for the mind that emerges from it now — our own tenet holds exactly the same: even though factors of the same kind may be separated by a long interval, the earlier still serves as the immediately-antecedent condition for the later. What difficulty could this cause? This reply is unreasonable. Why is this? Because having a mind and having no mind are two entirely distinct states. Moreover, an associated dharma either has already given its result or has not yet given it — to treat these as interchangeable would be unreasonable. A mind not yet arisen cannot be "immediately-antecedent" at all — to make it serve in advance as such a condition would be unreasonable. Furthermore, this position directly contradicts what is stated in the Prakaraṇapāda, which states: what is the dharma immediately following mind? The decisive objection here is the simplest: if a moment of greed could only ever be immediately followed by another moment of greed, or of some equally dissimilar-blocking kind, a practitioner could never interrupt greed with the contemplation of impurity, hatred with loving-kindness, or delusion with the contemplation of causes and conditions — and the entire practice of cultivating the antidotes that make up the path would become logically impossible. This is the treatise's standard move against a technical rule proposed elsewhere: trace what the rule would forbid in ordinary practice, and show that liberation itself becomes unreachable if the rule were actually true.
