The Night Māra Lost

Fascicle 103 tells the magnificent story of the night of awakening and the defeat of Māra's own army, then opens the four confirmed purities — gradual realization of the four truths, and King Ajātaśatru's own rootless faith. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 103 continues the Mind-Reading-Knowledge Nyāsa (他心智納息第三之五), closing the classification of the six superknowledges with the narrative of the night of awakening, before opening a rich new topic: the four confirmed purities (四證淨) in the Buddha, the dharma, the sangha, and precepts. Terms established across fascicles 93–102 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The Ascetic Who Saw Far But Not Near

Fascicle 103 opens by sorting the six superknowledges into clarity, instructive guidance, both, or neither — before a comic morality tale illustrates why the divine eye, divine ear, and recollection of former abodes fail to count as instructive guidance at all: a Nirgrantha ascetic, invited to a devotee's home, claims clairvoyant knowledge of two distant monkeys drowning in a faraway river, only to be caught, moments later, unable to tell rice from meat curry sitting directly in front of him. Declaring one's own distant perception, the text observes, never actually convinces a skeptic the way manifesting a transformation, reading a mind, or teaching effectively to visible result does — and it is only these latter three that earn the name "instructive guidance."

Chinese Source

問六通中。幾是明非示導。幾是示導非明。幾是明亦是示導。幾非明亦非示導耶。答二通是明非示導。謂宿住隨念智證通。及死生智證通。二通是示導非明。謂神境智證通。及他心智證通。一通是明亦是示導。謂漏盡智證通。一通非明亦非示導。謂天耳智證通。問何故六通三是示導三非示導耶。答現希有事令他信伏。引入正法故名示導。三有此義餘三不然。謂若自說我能遠聞。我能遠見。我能遠憶諸宿住事。他皆生疑為虛為實。不即信伏。故非示導。曾聞有一居士信外道法。屈請離繫親子及彼徒眾。來赴其家供以飲食。離繫親子適入其家即便微笑。居士怪問。師離掉舉何為笑耶。彼遂答言。吾有妙德汝在家者豈盡知耶。於是居士慇懃問之。彼便告曰㮏末陀河側有二獼猴。共鬪不已俱墮彼河為水漂溺。吾為是事愍而笑之。居士讚言。甚為希有。大師天眼清淨乃爾。食時既至居士念言。我當因食驗彼虛實。便以飯覆臛先授與師。以臛沃飯與彼弟子。時彼弟子受已即食師獨不食。居士問言。大師何緣而獨不食。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: among the six superknowledges, how many are clarity but not instructive guidance? How many are instructive guidance but not clarity? How many are both clarity and instructive guidance? How many are neither clarity nor instructive guidance? The answer: two superknowledges are clarity but not instructive guidance — namely, the superknowledge of the recollection of former abodes, and the superknowledge of the knowledge of death and rebirth. Two superknowledges are instructive guidance but not clarity — namely, the superknowledge of miraculous transformation, and the superknowledge of the knowledge of others' minds. One superknowledge is both clarity and instructive guidance — namely, the superknowledge of the knowledge of the exhaustion of outflows. One superknowledge is neither clarity nor instructive guidance — namely, the superknowledge of the divine ear. One asks: for what reason, among the six superknowledges, are three instructive guidance, and three not so? The answer: manifesting a rare and marvelous matter, causing others to believe and submit, drawing them into the true dharma, is what is named instructive guidance; three of them possess this same meaning, the remaining three do not — that is, were one to declare of himself, I am capable of hearing at a distance, I am capable of seeing at a distance, I am capable of recollecting every matter of former abodes, others would, in every case, give rise to doubt as to whether this is false or true, and would not thereby immediately believe and submit — and it is for this reason that these are not instructive guidance. It has once been heard that there was a certain lay devotee who had faith in the outside-path teaching, and who, bowing low, invited a disciple of the Nirgrantha ascetics, together with his own following, to come to his own house, providing them with food and drink. The Nirgrantha disciple, upon entering the house, at once broke into a faint smile. The lay devotee, puzzled, asked him: master, being free of restlessness, why do you now smile? He thereupon replied: I possess a wondrous virtue — how could you, a mere householder, ever fully know it? At this, the lay devotee earnestly questioned him further, and he then told him: beside the river Narmadā, there were two monkeys, who, fighting without cease, both fell into that same river and were swept away and drowned by the water — it was on account of this matter that I, in compassion, smiled at them. The lay devotee praised him, saying: how truly marvelous — the great master's own divine eye is pure to such a degree! When the mealtime arrived, the lay devotee thought to himself: I shall now, by means of this meal, put to the test whether this is false or true. He thereupon covered a bowl of meat curry with rice, and first offered it to the master, while giving the curry poured over rice to that same master's disciple. At that time, this same disciple, upon receiving it, at once ate, while the master alone did not eat. The lay devotee asked him: great master, for what reason do you alone not eat?

彼言無臛如何食之。居士調言。奇哉天眼。乃能遠見不能近觀。外道師徒時深愧恥。故天眼等三非示導。自說遠聞及能遠憶。亦不令他即信伏故。既不信伏如何可引令入正法。是故此三皆非示導。若為示現神境智通。變一為多變多為一。乃至梵世神力自在。令多有情深心信伏引入正法故名示導。若為示現他心智通。記說彼心思念差別如所記說。皆實非虛令多有情。深心信伏引入正法故名示導。

He said: there is no curry — how could I eat it? The lay devotee mockingly said: how marvelous, this divine eye — capable of seeing far away, yet incapable of observing what is near at hand! The teacher and his disciples, at that time, were overcome with deep shame. It is for this reason that the divine eye and the remaining two are not instructive guidance — declaring of oneself that one hears at a distance, or is capable of recollecting at a distance, likewise does not cause others to immediately believe and submit, and, since they do not believe and submit, how could this ever draw them into the true dharma? It is for this reason that these three are, in every case, not instructive guidance. If it is in order to display the superknowledge of miraculous transformation, transforming one into many, or many into one, and so on, up through, possessing self-mastery of supernormal power even to the Brahmā world, causing many sentient beings to believe and submit with a deep mind, and thereby drawing them into the true dharma, this is named instructive guidance. If it is in order to display the superknowledge of the knowledge of others' minds, recording and describing the distinctions of another's own mental thought exactly as it genuinely is, without falsehood, causing many sentient beings to believe and submit with a deep mind, and thereby drawing them into the true dharma, this, too, is named instructive guidance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ascetic's own humiliation works precisely because it inverts the very claim he made: a teacher who insists he can see across a river yet cannot see what is on his own plate is not merely wrong about one thing while right about another — he is exposed as never having possessed the penetrating perception he claimed in the first place, since genuine clairvoyance, unlike a party trick, would hardly fail at the easiest possible test set directly before it.

2. Why the Buddha Refused to Perform Miracles

The three genuine kinds of instructive guidance are named — miraculous transformation, mind-reading, and teaching-instruction — with an etymology built on the memorable image of a gatekeeper, who reveals inner matters to outer visitors and outer matters to those within. A rich narrative then unfolds: the householder's son Kaivarta begs the Buddha to station a disciple in his own city who might display miracles to strengthen wavering faith, and the Buddha refuses outright, revealing that he never permits his disciples to display supernormal power at all, only to conceal their own virtues and reveal their own faults. Pressed to explain, the Buddha stages a devastating thought experiment: however impressive a miracle or an act of mind-reading might look to the faithful, a skeptic will always have a ready explanation — a magic spell, a clever trick — leaving the display accomplishing nothing but mutual mockery. Only the genuinely undecided, never the already-convinced or already-hostile, are worth performing for at all.

Ru-Yi Translation

If it is in order to display the superknowledge of the knowledge of the exhaustion of outflows, instructing and teaching according to what is fitting, swiftly causing one to see the truths, becoming free of dust and free of defilement, giving rise to the pure dharma-eye with respect to the various dharmas, proceeding onward until every outflow is eternally exhausted, causing many sentient beings to believe and submit with a deep mind, and thereby drawing them into the true dharma, this, too, is named instructive guidance. One asks: for what kind of person ought one to manifest a supernormal transformation? The answer: for whoever is decisively confident in the Buddha's own teaching, and for whoever is decisively without such confidence, one ought not to manifest it; for whoever is undecided, one ought to manifest it, in order to draw him into the true dharma — apart from this, there exists no further expedient means. How is this to be known as so? Exactly as the scripture states: at one time, the Buddha was dwelling beside the city of Nāṭikā, within the Āmra grove that encircled it. There was a certain lay devotee's own son, named Kaivarta, who came before the Buddha, bowed with his head at the Buddha's own two feet, and, withdrawing to stand to one side, addressed the Buddha, saying: at present, this city is peaceful and prosperous, and its own many people possess a deep faith and reverence for the treasure of the Buddha, the dharma, and the saṅgha — I only wish that the Bhagavān would leave a single disciple constantly dwelling in this same place, so that, before the various ascetics and brāhmaṇas, he might, from time to time, manifest a supernormal transformation, displaying a dharma beyond that of an ordinary person, causing those in this city who already have faith in the Buddha's own teaching to have their own firm faith redoubled, and causing those who do not yet believe, coming and going for a time, to come to believe and accept the Buddha's own teaching. At that time, the Buddha said to this same lay devotee's son:

我曾不令諸弟子眾。對諸沙門婆羅門等。現諸神變示過人法。然我常令諸弟子眾。覆藏自善發露己惡。時居士子復白佛言。若佛弟子對諸沙門婆羅門等。現神變事示過人法。有何過患而佛不許。若佛弟子覆藏自善發露己惡。有何功德而佛許之。爾時世尊告居士子。

I have never once caused my own disciples, before the various ascetics and brāhmaṇas, to manifest any supernormal transformation displaying a dharma beyond that of an ordinary person — I constantly cause my own disciples, instead, to conceal their own good and reveal their own faults. At that time, the lay devotee's son further addressed the Buddha, saying: if, when the Buddha's own disciples, before the various ascetics and brāhmaṇas, manifest a supernormal transformation displaying a dharma beyond that of an ordinary person, there exists some fault in this, such that the Buddha does not permit it — then, if the Buddha's own disciples conceal their own good and reveal their own faults, what virtue can there be in this, such that the Buddha permits it? At that time, the Bhagavān said to the lay devotee's son:

我今問汝隨汝意答。若我弟子對諸沙門婆羅門等。現神變事變一為多變多為一。乃至梵世神力自在。信佛法者見此事已。向不信者說如是言。奇哉世尊諸聖弟子。能現如是甚希有事。時不信者語信者言。此何希有。世有明呪名健馱梨。善受持者亦能示現如是幻惑。

I shall now ask you a question — answer according to your own thought. Were my own disciples, before the various ascetics and brāhmaṇas, to manifest a supernormal transformation, transforming one into many, or many into one, and so on, up through, possessing self-mastery of supernormal power even to the Brahmā world, those who have faith in the Buddha's own teaching, having seen this same matter, would say to those without faith: how marvelous — the various noble disciples of the Bhagavān are capable of manifesting so rare a matter as this! At that time, those without faith would say to those with faith: what is rare about this? There exists, in the world, a bright incantation named Gāndhārī, and whoever skillfully upholds it, too, is capable of displaying such an illusory deception as this — what wise person would ever display so base a matter as this?

誰有智者現斯鄙事。佛告居士子。於汝意云何。有不信者作如是言譏信者不。居士子曰。實有斯事。世尊復告居士子言。若我弟子記說他心思念差別。如所記說皆實非虛。信佛法者知此事已。向不信者說如是言。奇哉世尊諸聖弟子。乃有如是甚希有事。時不信者語信者言此何希有。世有明呪名剎尼迦。善受持者亦能造作如是幻惑。誰有智者作斯鄙事佛告居士子。於汝意云何。有不信者作如是言譏信者不。居士子曰。實有斯事。爾時世尊告居士子。若我弟子對諸沙門婆羅門等。現神變事示過人法。有是過患故我不許。若我弟子覆藏自善發露己惡。順賢聖法世所稱譽。有斯功德故我許之。

The Buddha said to the lay devotee's son: what do you think — would there exist those without faith who would speak in this way, mocking those with faith? The lay devotee's son said: there does indeed exist such a matter. The Bhagavān, further, said to the lay devotee's son: were my own disciples to record and describe another's own mental thought and its own distinctions, exactly as recorded and described, without any falsehood, those who have faith in the Buddha's own teaching, having known this same matter, would say to those without faith: how marvelous — the various noble disciples of the Bhagavān possess so rare a matter as this! At that time, those without faith would say to those with faith: what is rare about this? There exists, in the world, a bright incantation named Kṣaṇika, and whoever skillfully upholds it, too, is capable of fashioning such an illusory deception as this — what wise person would ever perform so base a matter as this? The Buddha said to the lay devotee's son: what do you think — would there exist those without faith who would speak in this way, mocking those with faith? The lay devotee's son said: there does indeed exist such a matter. At that time, the Bhagavān said to the lay devotee's son: it is because there exists such a fault as this that I do not permit my own disciples, before the various ascetics and brāhmaṇas, to manifest a supernormal transformation displaying a dharma beyond that of an ordinary person; it is because there exists such a virtue as this — according with the noble dharma, praised by the world — that I permit my own disciples to conceal their own good and reveal their own faults.

由此故知決定信者及不信者。不應為現諸神變事。若不定者應為現之。方便引令入佛正法。

It is by virtue of this that it is known that whoever is decisively confident, or decisively without confidence, ought not to have a supernormal transformation manifested for him; whoever is undecided ought to have it manifested for him, as an expedient means of drawing him into the Buddha's own true dharma.

此契經中佛為居士子說三種示導。一神變示導。二記心示導。三教誡示導。問何故名示導。答示謂示現。導謂導引。現希有事引入正法故名示導。如守門者立示導名。謂守門者示現內事導引外人。示現外事導引內人。示現內事導引外人者。謂彼候王若不澡浴寢食觀寶即便引現。示現外事導引內人者。謂彼伺外貢獻珍奇殊方信物引內人受。如是示現佛正法中微妙功德。方便導引所化有情。令其趣入故名示導此契經中復說。菩薩初中後夜各起通明。起通明已明星出時。證得無上正等菩提。問何緣菩薩初中後夜各起通明。答過殑伽沙後身菩薩。將證大覺法應爾故。復次初中後夜各起通明。欲令自身成法器故。復次欲現神變了達事故。通能現神變。明能了達事。故起通明。復次欲現安足遠作事故。通現安足。明遠作事。故起通明。復次如無間道解脫道故。通如無間道。明如解脫道。故起通明。復次如見道修道故。通如見道。明如修道。故起通明。復次欲顯善有漏無覆無記。有漏無漏及無漏法現前有用。故起通明。善有漏法者。謂神境智證通。及宿住隨念智證通明。無覆無記法者。

Within this same scripture, the Buddha, for the sake of the lay devotee's son, spoke of three kinds of instructive guidance — first, the instructive guidance of supernormal transformation; second, the instructive guidance of recording the mind; third, the instructive guidance of instruction and teaching. One asks: for what reason is it named instructive guidance? The answer: "instructive" refers to manifesting and displaying; "guidance" refers to leading and directing — manifesting a rare and marvelous matter, and thereby drawing one into the true dharma, is what is named instructive guidance, exactly as a gatekeeper earns the name "one who instructs and guides" — that is, a gatekeeper manifests inner matters in order to direct outer persons, and manifests outer matters in order to direct inner persons. As for manifesting inner matters in order to direct outer persons: this refers to the fact that, should the awaited king not yet be bathing, resting, or eating, or observing his own treasures, the gatekeeper accordingly manifests and presents this same information. As for manifesting outer matters in order to direct inner persons: this refers to the fact that the gatekeeper, observing that some outer tribute of rare and singular objects, or trusted goods, has arrived, guides the inner persons to accept it. In just the same way, manifesting the subtle and wondrous virtues present within the Buddha's own true dharma, and thereby, through skillful means, guiding and directing the sentient beings to be transformed, causing them to enter into it, is what is named instructive guidance. This same scripture, further, states: the bodhisattva, during the first, middle, and last watches of the night, each gave rise to superknowledge and clarity; having given rise to superknowledge and clarity, at the very moment the morning star appeared, he realized unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. One asks: for what reason did the bodhisattva, during the first, middle, and last watches of the night, each give rise to superknowledge and clarity? The answer: for a bodhisattva in his own final body, who has already crossed beyond as many kalpas as there are grains of sand in the river Gaṅgā, and who is about to realize the great awakening, the dharma necessarily requires this to be so. Further, it is because he wishes, during the first, middle, and last watches of the night, each to give rise to superknowledge and clarity, that his own body becomes a fitting vessel for the dharma. Further, it is because he wishes to manifest his own thorough mastery of both supernormal transformation and comprehension that superknowledge is capable of manifesting supernormal transformation, and clarity is capable of thorough comprehension, and it is for this reason that superknowledge and clarity are given rise to. Further, it is exactly as the uninterrupted path and the path of liberation — superknowledge is like the uninterrupted path; clarity is like the path of liberation — and it is for this reason that superknowledge and clarity are given rise to. Further, it is exactly as the path of seeing and the path of cultivation — superknowledge is like the path of seeing; clarity is like the path of cultivation — and it is for this reason that superknowledge and clarity are given rise to. Further, it is because he wishes to display that the wholesome contaminated, the neutral-and-unobscured, both the contaminated and the undefiled, and the undefiled dharmas alike possess present function that superknowledge and clarity are given rise to — as for the wholesome contaminated dharma: this refers to the superknowledge of miraculous transformation, and the superknowledge and clarity of the recollection of former abodes; as for the neutral-and-unobscured dharma:

謂天眼智證及天耳智證二通。有漏無漏法者。謂他心智證通。無漏法者。謂漏盡智證通明。復次為欲次第降伏魔怨。初中後夜各起通明。曾聞菩薩知修苦行非真道已。遂受難陀難陀跋羅姊妹所奉具十六德香蜜乳糜。食已身心安隱有力。從吉祥人邊受吉祥草。詣菩提樹下手自敷設。如婆蘇吉龍王盤身結加趺坐。坐已便發堅固誓言。

this refers to the two superknowledges of the divine eye and the divine ear; as for the dharma that pervades both the contaminated and the undefiled: this refers to the superknowledge of the knowledge of others' minds; as for the undefiled dharma: this refers to the superknowledge and clarity of the knowledge of the exhaustion of outflows. Further, it is in order to subdue Māra, the enemy, in sequence, that superknowledge and clarity are given rise to during the first, middle, and last watches of the night. It has once been heard that the bodhisattva, already having come to know that the cultivation of painful austerity was not the genuine path, thereupon accepted the milk-porridge, possessing sixteen virtues and fragrant with honey, offered by the sisters Nandā and Nandabalā; having eaten it, his own body and mind became tranquil and possessed of strength. He received auspicious grass from an auspicious person, and, arriving beneath the bodhi tree, spread it out with his own hand, and, exactly as the nāga king Vāsuki coils his own body, sat cross-legged, and, once seated, thereupon uttered a firm and resolute vow, saying:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own thought experiment is a remarkably modern piece of epistemic humility: rather than insisting his own disciples' powers are self-evidently genuine and any skeptic simply obstinate, he grants the skeptic's own position its full force — of course a determined disbeliever can always attribute a miracle to some clever trick, and no display, however impressive, can force belief past that door. The lesson drawn is not that miracles are worthless, but that they are worth precisely nothing when aimed at someone whose mind is already made up in either direction, and everything only for someone still genuinely undecided.

3. The Night of Awakening

A magnificent, extended narrative recounts why the bodhisattva gave rise to superknowledge and clarity during each of the three watches of his own final night — six converging reasons, from cosmic necessity to displaying the full range of wholesome, neutral, and undefiled dharmas at once — before the story itself unfolds: rejecting extreme austerity, accepting the sisters Nandā and Nandabalā's own milk-porridge, spreading auspicious grass beneath the bodhi tree, and vowing never to rise unenlightened. The earth shakes in six ways; Māra, alarmed, first tempts the bodhisattva with the throne of a universal monarch, then, rebuffed, musters his own demonic armies. The bodhisattva meets each of Māra's shape-shifting forms with a superior counter-form — bird against cat, cat against wolf, wolf against leopard, leopard against tiger, tiger against lion, lion against dragon, dragon against fire, fire against storm, storm against a vast canopy — before confirming, through the recollection of his own past lives, that his own accumulated merit of giving alone already vastly outweighs the whole of Māra's own store. A single strike of the bodhisattva's own hand against the earth sends Māra's entire army fleeing, and the bodhisattva, using divine ear, divine eye, and mind-reading in careful sequence, discerns Śakra's own joy and Māra's own retinue's envy — the final insight into desire's own root in affliction bringing him to the full exhaustion of every outflow.

4. Gradual, Not Sudden: A New Topic on Confirmed Purity

An entirely new topic opens: at the very moment of directly realizing the four truths, in which does a practitioner first attain confirmed purity — the Buddha, the dharma, or the sangha? The Vibhajyavādins' claim that all four truths are realized simultaneously, cited from a scripture stating that freedom from doubt about suffering implies freedom from doubt about the rest, is refuted through Elder Anāthapiṇḍika's own direct question to the Buddha: realization proceeds gradually, exactly like climbing a four-runged ladder, one truth at a time. The Vibhajyavādins' own cited scripture is reread as describing one who has already completed the path, not one still progressing through it, with Vasumitra and the Great Virtuous One each offering their own refinements of exactly what "without doubt" means at each intermediate stage.

5. Defining the Four Confirmed Purities

Each confirmed purity is defined with technical precision: confirmed purity in the Buddha takes the Buddha's own non-learner dharmas as its object; confirmed purity in the dharma takes the solitary-realizer's and bodhisattva's own undefiled faculties, together with the truths of suffering, accumulation, and cessation, as its object; confirmed purity in the sangha takes the disciple's own learner and non-learner dharmas as its object; confirmed purity in precepts is simply the undefiled precepts themselves. During the direct realization of suffering, accumulation, and cessation, one attains solely confirmed purity in the dharma; only during the direct realization of the path does one first attain all three of confirmed purity in the Buddha, the dharma, and the sangha together — the Venerable Pārśva explaining that seeing the calamity in suffering and accumulation is precisely what allows one to see the corresponding excellence in their own cessation.

6. Why Suffering and Accumulation Still Inspire Confirmed Purity

A genuinely delicate puzzle asks how confirmed purity — belief in something worthy of trust — can possibly be attained through directly realizing suffering and accumulation, dharmas of pure defilement. The resolution distinguishes two conditions for confirmed purity: being worthy of belief, and being worth seeking. Cessation and the path satisfy both; suffering and accumulation satisfy only the first, exactly as a person might dig in a place he believes holds no treasure at all, simply because some other purpose requires the digging. The Venerable Ghoṣa and the Venerable Pārśva each offer their own vivid gloss — seeing suffering's calamity first makes cessation's purity shine by contrast, or, more viscerally, being pressed by suffering the way a storm presses a traveler makes a sheltering roof suddenly, urgently visible as a refuge — before a precise combinatorial breakdown maps exactly which practitioner-types attain confirmed purity through belief alone versus through belief-and-seeking, with respect to their own vehicle's path and every other vehicle's path besides.

8. Unbroken, Unpierced, Unmixed, Unstained

How the four confirmed purities are established (both by their own substance, for precepts, and by their own object, for the remaining three) leads into a rich etymological debate over what to call them — Pārśva's "unbreakable," Vasumitra's "unceasing," the Great Virtuous One's "unshakeable," and Ghoṣa's "vision-purity" or "wisdom-purity" — before the sequence in which the Buddha names them (Buddha, dharma, sangha, precepts) is explained through four beautifully matched similes: speaker before spoken-of before addressee before abode; physician before health before nurse before medicine; caravan-leader before treasure-isle before fellow-travelers before provisions; ferryman before far shore before fellow-passenger before raft. The famous "noble-cherished precepts" formula — unbroken, unpierced, unmixed, unstained — receives its own cascade of explanations for what distinguishes these four terms, seven different schemes competing for which offense-category, which affliction, or which practice each one specifically counteracts.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four parallel similes for why the Buddha is named first — speaker, physician, caravan-leader, ferryman — are not simply four decorative variations on a single point, but four genuinely different accounts of what kind of authority the Buddha's own priority actually represents: a speaker's priority is merely sequential, a physician's is diagnostic, a caravan-leader's is navigational, and a ferryman's is the very means of crossing itself — and offering all four together lets the text honor several genuinely distinct senses in which the Buddha comes "first" without needing to commit to any single one of them as the complete explanation.

9. Four Sounds of Heaven, and a King's Rootless Faith

The fascicle closes with three genuinely moving passages. First, the four sounds that arise among the gods whenever a noble disciple accomplishes any one of the four confirmed purities — each god who once accomplished that same purity rejoicing that a new companion is coming to join his own community. Second, the parable of true filial repayment: carrying one's own parents on one's shoulders for a hundred years is not genuine repayment; only establishing them in faith, precepts, generosity, and wisdom is. Finally, the fascicle turns to King Ajātaśatru's own famous "rootless faith" — since every conditioned dharma has some root, why is his faith called rootless? Five converging answers agree that this same faith, though genuinely unshakeable, arose without ever depending on the path of seeing, on any prior habituation, or on ever drawing near the Buddha in life — so overwhelming that he would throw himself from an elephant or a high tower merely upon glimpsing the Buddha from afar, unharmed by the fall through the very force of that same faith, and yet not rootless enough to spare him a brief stay in hell before his own eventual rebirth in heaven.

It then opened a rich new topic within this same Mind-Reading-Knowledge Nyāsa (他心智納息第三之五): the four confirmed purities (四證淨) — gradual, not sudden, realization of the four truths; confirmed purity in the Buddha, the dharma, the sangha, and precepts defined in full technical detail; the noble-cherished precepts; the four sounds of heaven; and King Ajātaśatru's own rootless faith. Fascicle 104 continues within the same nyāsa.
