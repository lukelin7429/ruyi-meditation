The Flesh of One's Own Child

Fascicle 130 completes the treatise's account of the four nutriments with the four unforgettable similes of a child's flesh, a flayed cow, a pit of blazing coals, and three hundred sharp spears — then closes with a rich account of comparative merit in giving, ranked from an ordinary being all the way up to the Buddha himself. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 130 complete, 16 sections Source: Taishō T1545, vol. 27, fascicle 130 Topic: 大種蘊第五 → 大造納息第一之四 continues (the Great Elements Aggregate; the Great-Elements-and-Derivatives Nyāsa, continued)

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 130 continues the Great-Elements-and-Derivatives Nyāsa (大造納息第一之四). Terms established across fascicles 93–129 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Which Nutriments Draw Forth the Future, Which Sustain the Present?

Fascicle 130 continues the treatment of the four nutriments, asking which of them draw forth a future existence and which merely sustain a present one — competing theories assign this role to one, two, three, or all four nutriments at once — before specifying which nutriment especially excels at nourishing which particular dharma: morsel-food for the body, contact for mind and mental factors, volition for future rebirth, and consciousness for name-and-form.

Chinese Source

大種蘊第五中大造納息第一之四問如是四食。幾牽當有令現在前。幾持今有令相續住。有作是說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth Aggregate, the Great Elements: the Great-Elements-and-Derivatives Nyāsa, Chapter One, Part Four. Question: of these four nutriments, how many draw forth a future existence to make it presently manifest, and how many sustain a present existence to keep it continuing? Some say:

一牽當有令現在前。謂意思食。三持今有令相續住。謂餘三食。或有說者。二牽當有。謂意思識。二持今有謂餘二食。有餘師說。三牽當有。謂觸思識。一持今有。所謂段食。如是說者。四食盡牽當有令現在前。盡持今有令相續住。

— only one draws forth a future existence, namely volition-nutriment, while the other three sustain a present existence. Others say: two draw forth a future existence — volition and consciousness — while the other two sustain a present existence. Other masters say: three draw forth a future existence — contact, volition, and consciousness — while only one, morsel-nutriment, sustains a present existence. According to this final account: all four nutriments alike draw forth a future existence to make it presently manifest, and all four alike sustain a present existence to keep it continuing.

問諸食於有。幾未生令生幾生已長養。有作是說。諸食於有。一未生令生。謂意思食。三生已長養。謂餘三食。復有說者。二未生令生。謂意思識。二生已長養。謂餘二食。有餘師說。三未生令生。

Question: of these nutriments with respect to existence, how many cause what is not yet arisen to arise, and how many nourish what has already arisen? Some say: only one causes what is not yet arisen to arise, namely volition-nutriment, while the other three nourish what has already arisen. Others say: two cause what is not yet arisen to arise — volition and consciousness — while the other two nourish what has already arisen. Other masters say: three cause what is not yet arisen to arise —

謂觸思識。一生已長養。所謂段食。如是說者四食於有。皆未生令生生已長養。問何食於何法食事偏增。答段食養色根大種勝故。於色根大種食事偏增。觸食養心心所勝故。於心心所食事偏增。思食養後有勝故。於諸後有食事偏增。識食養名色勝故。於諸名色食事偏增問如說牽有義是食義等。此言為是因義。是緣義耶。設爾何失。二俱有過若是因義者。外香味觸於內諸處五因皆無。云何為食。若是緣義者除內自性。餘法皆是此增上緣。何故但說四種為食。有說。是因義。問若爾外香味觸於內諸處五因皆無。云何為食。答外香味觸為覺發因。令內香等得成食事。內香味觸於內諸處。有因義故說之為食。謂眼唯以觸處為食。耳鼻舌身色聲亦爾。

— namely contact, volition, and consciousness — while only one, morsel-nutriment, nourishes what has already arisen. According to this final account: all four nutriments alike, with respect to existence, both cause what is not yet arisen to arise and nourish what has already arisen. Question: which nutriment especially nourishes which particular dharma? Answer: morsel-nutriment nourishes the form-faculties and great elements especially well, and hence especially excels at nourishing the form-faculties and great elements; contact-nutriment nourishes mind and mental factors especially well, and hence especially excels at nourishing mind and mental factors; volition-nutriment nourishes future existence especially well, and hence especially excels at nourishing every future existence; and consciousness-nutriment nourishes name-and-form especially well, and hence especially excels at nourishing name-and-form. Question: as was said, "the sense of drawing forth existence" and the rest are the senses of nutriment — is this the sense of cause, or the sense of condition? Either way, what fault would follow? Both possibilities show difficulty: if it is the sense of cause, then external smell, taste, and touch possess none of the five kinds of cause with respect to the internal sense-fields — how, then, could they be nutriment? But if it is the sense of condition, then, apart from the internal sense-field's own essential nature, every other dharma already serves as its predominant condition — why, then, are only these four kinds spoken of as nutriment? Some say: it is the sense of cause. Question: but external smell, taste, and touch possess none of the five kinds of cause with respect to the internal sense-fields — how, then, can this be so? Answer: external smell, taste, and touch serve as the awakening cause, enabling the internal smell, taste, and touch to accomplish the function of nutriment — and it is on account of this causal sense that internal smell, taste, and touch, with respect to the internal sense-fields, are called nutriment: that is, the eye takes only the touch sense-field as its nutriment, and the ear, nose, tongue, and body likewise;

香用香處觸處為食。味以味處觸處為食。觸處唯以觸處為食。心心所法三食為食。此中因義如理應思。有說。是緣義。問若爾除內自性。餘法皆是此增上緣。何故但說四種為食。答於內諸處增上緣法。有親有疎。有近有遠。有合不合。有在此生。有在餘生。諸親近合。在此生者。說之為食。疎遠不合在餘生者不說為食。故食唯四。此四皆於內十二處能為食事。然有增微如前所說。

— smell takes the smell and touch sense-fields as its nutriment; taste takes the taste and touch sense-fields as its nutriment; and the touch sense-field takes only the touch sense-field as its nutriment. Mind and mental factors take the three nutriments — contact, volition, and consciousness — as their nutriment. This causal sense should be reflected upon accordingly. Others say: it is the sense of condition. Question: but, apart from the internal sense-field's own essential nature, every other dharma already serves as its predominant condition — why, then, are only these four kinds spoken of as nutriment? Answer: among the dharmas serving as predominant condition for the internal sense-fields, some are close, some distant; some near, some far; some joined, some unjoined; some belonging to this same birth, some belonging to a different birth — whatever is close, near, and joined, belonging to this same birth, is called nutriment; whatever is distant, far, and unjoined, belonging to a different birth, is not called nutriment — and hence nutriment is exactly four in number. These four are all capable of serving the function of nutriment with respect to all twelve internal sense-fields, though with varying degrees of strength, as explained before.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That four separate schools could disagree even about something as basic as which nutriments "draw forth" the future versus merely "sustain" the present shows how much interpretive latitude remains even within a category the treatise has already spent an entire prior fascicle carefully defining — settled vocabulary does not always settle every question that vocabulary can be asked about.

2. Why Isn't Visible Form a Nutriment? And the Buddha's Praise of a Meal's Beauty

A cluster of theories explains why visible form — unlike smell, taste, and touch — never counts as nutriment: it lacks the mark of nourishment, is grasped too coarsely, requires no direct contact, or has not yet undergone the transformation cooking provides. Competing objections are dismissed as absurd (were visible form nutriment, merely seeing food would remove hunger, monks would violate the no-eating-after-noon rule by glancing at food, and form-realm gods would need to eat). A final puzzle asks how this squares with the Buddha's own praise of a meal's beautiful color — resolved as mere encouragement of the donor's generous spirit, nothing more.

Chinese Source

問何故色處不立為食。有作是說。無食相故。有說。色處取時麁重若於取時細輕者名食。要微細輕滋養身故。有說。色處不至而取。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: why is the visible-form sense-field not established as nutriment? Some say: because it lacks the very mark of nutriment. Others say: the visible-form sense-field is coarse and heavy when grasped, whereas only what is fine and light when grasped is called nutriment, since nutriment must be fine, light, and nourishing to the body. Others say: the visible-form sense-field is grasped without direct contact,

食唯至取。非身不合成食事故。有說。色處至變壞位食事方成。謂水所浸爛。火所熟變。風所動搖。然後方成食。所作事非色未變。

— whereas nutriment is grasped only through direct contact, since nutriment's own function cannot be accomplished without joining to the body. Others say: the visible-form sense-field only accomplishes the function of nutriment once it has reached a state of transformation and decomposition — that is, once soaked and softened by water, cooked and transformed by fire, and stirred by wind — only then does it become nutriment, since this function is never accomplished by visible form that has not yet been transformed,

得名為食。故色非食香等不爾。有餘師言。若色是食。眼見色時應除飢渴。若然施主所費則為唐捐。有餘復言。若色是食。諸出家者眼見色時應犯遠離非時食法。或有說者。若色是食則色界天應受段食取諸色故。當知後三所說乖理。香觸亦有如斯過故。應知前四所說者好。問若色非食經云何通。如世尊言。長者汝所施食。色香味具甚為妙好。答色雖非食。為欲發起施主思故佛作是說。謂佛讚美所施食時。施主便發殊勝思願。快哉如來讚受我食。我當必獲殊勝福利。問若讚色具色非食者。亦讚香等俱應非食。彼既是食此亦應然。答佛於此中不欲簡別是食非食。但欲發起施主思願說此契經。

— and hence earns the name of nutriment; visible form is not nutriment, unlike smell and the rest. Other masters say: were visible form nutriment, then merely seeing food with the eye ought to remove hunger and thirst — and, were this so, a donor's expenditure of actual food would be entirely wasted. Others, further, say: were visible form nutriment, then renunciants merely seeing food with the eye ought to violate the precept against eating after the proper time. Others say: were visible form nutriment, then the gods of the form realm ought to take morsel-food as well, since they too perceive visible forms — and one should know that these latter three accounts depart from reason, since smell and touch would incur the same faults, and hence the former four accounts should be recognized as the better ones. Question: if visible form is not nutriment, how is the sūtra to be reconciled, where the World-Honored One says, "Householder, the food you have offered is truly excellent in color, fragrance, and taste"? Answer: though visible form is not nutriment, the Buddha spoke in this way in order to inspire the donor's own intention — that is, when the Buddha praises an offered food's excellence, the donor thereby gives rise to the exalted thought, "How wonderful that the Tathāgata praises and accepts my offering — I shall surely obtain excellent merit and benefit from this." Question: if praising a food's color, though color is not nutriment, still counts as praise, then praising its fragrance and the rest, though these are nutriment, ought equally to fail to count — since the former, being non-nutriment, is treated the same as the latter, which is nutriment. Answer: the Buddha, in this passage, has no intention of distinguishing what is and is not nutriment — he speaks this sūtra only in order to inspire the donor's own intention and aspiration,

若此讚說即是食者觸非所讚便應非食。

— for, were such praise itself equivalent to being nutriment, then touch, not being named in this particular praise, would have to be denied the status of nutriment as well, which is absurd.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reductio ad absurdum about form-realm gods needing morsel-food merely because they can see it is a nice piece of philosophical housekeeping: rather than leaving "why isn't sight nutritive" as a bare stipulation, the treatise tests the claim against its own cosmology and shows the alternative would collapse an entire, otherwise stable distinction between realms.

3. Why 'Morsel' Food? Three Etymologies

A brief etymological note asks why food is called "morsel" nutriment, given that drinking or sipping involves no obvious division into portions — answered either by appeal to the majority of ordinary eating being genuinely segmented, by insisting that sipping too involves its own subtle division, or by tracing the name back to the very dawn of the world-age, when the first humans consumed the earth's own primordial essence in exactly this divided, portioned manner.

Chinese Source

問段食是何義。答分段而食故名段食。問若爾所飲吮等非段食耶。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: what is the meaning of "morsel-nutriment"? Answer: it is called morsel-nutriment because it is eaten in divided portions. Question: if so, is what is merely drunk or sipped not morsel-nutriment at all?

答從多分說是故無過。復有說者。飲吮等時亦作分段。有餘師言。

Answer: this name is given with reference to the majority of cases, and hence there is no fault in it. Others say: even drinking and sipping do, in fact, involve division into portions. Other masters say:

從初而說。謂劫初時人受用地味。皆作分段而吞噉之因名段食。

— the name is given with reference to the very beginning of the age, when the people of that first era consumed the earth's own essence, all of it divided into portions and swallowed accordingly — and it is on this account that the name "morsel-nutriment" arose.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Tracing a common word's etymology all the way back to a mythic account of the world's very first meal is a small reminder that, for this tradition, even the most mundane vocabulary carries cosmological weight — nothing is simply arbitrary naming, everything potentially opens onto the deeper structure of how existence itself began.

4. Coarse and Fine Food: A Chain from Ocean Giants to the Smallest Insect

The Buddha's own distinction between coarse and fine morsel-food receives an elaborate explanation through purely relative comparison — an entire chain of predator and prey across the water, the land, and the sky, where each smaller creature's food counts as fine only relative to whatever larger creature's food counts as coarse above it, with no fixed, absolute boundary anywhere in the sequence. A further puzzle asks whether the other three nutriments have this same coarse/fine distinction, resolved affirmatively and mapped instead onto realm and meditative ground.

Chinese Source

問佛說段食。有麁有細云何應知麁細差別。答集異門說。段食麁細互相觀待而可了知。謂水族中小為大食。傳相觀待麁細得成。如底民耆羅耆羅所食是麁。底民耆羅所食為細。底民耆羅所食是麁。底民所食為細。底民所食是麁。大魚龜鼈及末羯羅失獸摩羅等所食為細。大魚鼈等所食是麁。餘水行虫所食為細。諸陸族中象馬駝等所食是麁。羊鹿猪等所食為細。羊鹿猪等所食是麁。兔猫狸等所食為細。兔猫狸等所食是麁。餘陸行虫所食為細。空行族中諸妙翅鳥所食是麁。鵝雁孔雀鸚鵡舍利命命鳥等所食為細。鵝雁孔雀等所食是麁。餘空行類所食為細。有作是說。若諸有情以草木等而為食者所食是麁。以餅飯等而為食者所食是細。有餘師說。以餅飯等而為食者所食是麁。以酥油等而為食者所食是細。有餘復說。面門吞噉諸食是麁。臍毛孔入諸食為細。謂胎藏中諸有情類食從臍入。唯諸菩薩食從一切毛孔而入。有作是言。若食噉已有等流者此食是麁。無等流者此食為細。如蘇陀味香為食等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: the Buddha teaches that morsel-nutriment has coarse and fine varieties — how should this distinction be understood? Answer: the Prakaraṇapāda explains that the coarseness or fineness of morsel-nutriment is known only relatively, one against another — that is, among water-creatures, the small serves as food for the large, and coarseness and fineness are established through this same relative chain: what the timiṅgila-timiṅgila devours is coarse, while what the timiṅgila alone devours is fine; what the timiṅgila alone devours is coarse, while what ordinary timi-fish devour is fine; what ordinary timi-fish devour is coarse, while what great fish, tortoises, turtles, makaras, and crocodiles devour is fine; what great fish and turtles devour is coarse, while what the remaining water-creatures devour is fine. Among land-creatures, what elephants, horses, and camels devour is coarse, while what sheep, deer, and pigs devour is fine; what sheep, deer, and pigs devour is coarse, while what rabbits, cats, and foxes devour is fine; what rabbits, cats, and foxes devour is coarse, while what the remaining land-creatures devour is fine. Among sky-creatures, what garuḍas devour is coarse, while what geese, wild geese, peacocks, parrots, mynas, and jīvañjīva birds devour is fine; what geese, wild geese, and peacocks devour is coarse, while what the remaining sky-creatures devour is fine. Some say: whatever beings take grass and trees as food, their food is coarse; whatever beings take cakes and cooked grain as food, their food is fine. Other masters say: whatever beings take cakes and cooked grain as food, their food is coarse; whatever beings take ghee and oil as food, their food is fine. Others, further, say: food swallowed through the mouth is coarse; food entering through the navel or the pores is fine — that is, beings within the womb take in food through the navel, while only bodhisattvas take it in through every pore. Some say: food that, once eaten, leaves a lingering outflow is coarse; food that leaves no such outflow is fine — as with the taste and fragrance of the mythical soma, and the like, taken as food.

問觸思識食有麁細不。若有者何故契經唯說段食有麁細。若無者何故段食有麁細非餘耶。答觸思識食亦有麁細。界地相待故。謂欲界是麁色界為細。色界是麁。無色界為細。初靜慮是麁。第二靜慮為細。乃至無所有處是麁。非想非非想處為細。問若爾何故契經不說。答應說而不說者當知此義有餘。有說。契經舉初顯後。已說段食有麁細當知餘食亦有麁細有說。段食由四因緣。有多少故麁細可知。餘食不爾。一追求故。二積集故。三受用故。四等流故。追求故者。謂諸有情追求段食。有多有少。所求多者是麁所求少者為細。積集故者。謂諸有情積集段食有多有少所集多者是麁所集少者是細。受用故者。謂諸有情受用段食有多有少。所受多者是麁所受少者是細。等流故者。謂諸有情段食等流有多有少。等流多者所食是麁等流少者所食是細。有說。段食觀待諸處可說麁細餘食不爾。

Question: do contact-nutriment, volition-nutriment, and consciousness-nutriment also have coarse and fine varieties? If they do, why does the sūtra speak of this distinction only with respect to morsel-nutriment? But if they do not, why does morsel-nutriment alone have this distinction, and not the others? Answer: contact-nutriment, volition-nutriment, and consciousness-nutriment also have coarse and fine varieties, known relatively across realm and ground — that is, the desire realm is coarse relative to the form realm, which is fine; the form realm is coarse relative to the formless realm, which is fine; the first dhyāna is coarse relative to the second, which is fine; and so on, up through the sphere of nothingness being coarse relative to the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, which is fine. Question: if so, why does the sūtra not mention this? Answer: that it ought to be mentioned yet is not should be understood as a matter left with a further, supplementary account. Others say: the sūtra raises the first case in order to make the latter clear — having already stated that morsel-nutriment has coarse and fine varieties, one should know that the other nutriments have this same distinction as well. Others say: morsel-nutriment's coarseness or fineness is known through four specific causal factors involving degrees of more or less, which do not apply in the same way to the other nutriments — namely, pursuit, accumulation, consumption, and outflow. As to pursuit: beings pursue morsel-nutriment in greater or lesser degree, and what is pursued more is coarse, what is pursued less is fine. As to accumulation: beings accumulate morsel-nutriment in greater or lesser degree, and what is accumulated more is coarse, what is accumulated less is fine. As to consumption: beings consume morsel-nutriment in greater or lesser degree, and what is consumed more is coarse, what is consumed less is fine. As to outflow: morsel-nutriment leaves an outflow in greater or lesser degree, and what leaves more outflow is coarse, what leaves less is fine. Others say: morsel-nutriment's coarseness and fineness can be described relative to the sense-fields, while the other nutriments cannot,

謂香味觸觀待色聲可說為細。觀待意法可說為麁觸思識三意法處攝。此於諸處唯細非麁無異觀待不說麁細。

— that is, smell, taste, and touch may be described as fine relative to form and sound, or as coarse relative to mind and dharmas; but contact, volition, and consciousness, being included solely within the mind and dharma sense-fields, are, with respect to these sense-fields, always fine, never coarse, having no further, distinct relative comparison, and hence coarseness and fineness are not spoken of for them in this way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Refusing to fix any absolute boundary between "coarse" and "fine," and instead defining the entire distinction through an endless relative chain running from the largest ocean predator down to the smallest insect, mirrors a recurring instinct in this literature: even categories that sound like they name a fixed property often turn out, on inspection, to name only a relationship, dissolved the moment one asks "fine compared to what?"

5. Which Nutriment Predominates in Each Realm — Including Hell's Own Grim Food

A systematic survey maps which nutriment predominates across every realm and destiny of rebirth: morsel-food in the desire realm, contact in the form realm, volition in the lower formless spheres, and consciousness at the very peak of existence. Hell, disturbingly, has all four nutriments too, with consciousness predominating and morsel-food itself consisting of molten copper and red-hot iron balls — provoking the sharp objection that something causing such agony could hardly count as nourishing, resolved only by noting that it does, in fact, temporarily relieve hunger and thirst the instant it enters the body, however catastrophic its aftermath.

Chinese Source

問於何界有幾食。答欲界具四段食偏增。色界有三。觸食偏增。無色亦三。下三無色思食偏增。非想非非想處識食偏增。有說。非想非非想處亦思食增。一思能感八十千劫壽量果故。問於何趣有幾食。答地獄具四。識食偏增。問地獄之中有何段食。答飲鎔銅汁。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: in which realm are how many nutriments found? A classificatory note distinguishes the word "food" in its merely conventional sense — as in naming dishes after their city of origin, or the sūtra's forward-flowing and backward-flowing water parables illustrating dependent origination's forward and reverse readings — from its ultimate sense, denoting only the four nutriments that genuinely, truly benefit the existence of living beings.

8. Every Morsel a Nutriment? A Sixteen-Case Matrix, and Who Needs Sustaining, Who Needs Nourishing

A tetralemma establishes that not every morsel of food is technically nutriment, and not every instance of nutriment is a morsel — a pattern repeated across all four nutriments for sixteen cases total. A further puzzle asks how food that causes real discomfort can still bear the mark of nourishment, resolved by distinguishing benefit at the moment of eating from benefit at the moment of digestion — a delicious poison harms later but satisfies now, a bitter medicine harms now but heals later, and either kind of benefit alone earns the name of nutriment. The section closes by carefully distinguishing which beings the sūtra means by "already arisen" (sustained) versus "still seeking existence" (aided) — competing readings map this onto basic-versus-intermediate existence, noble-versus-ordinary beings, or non-learner-versus-trainee.

The poison-and-medicine pairing captures something genuinely subtle about how this tradition defines benefit: rather than requiring a food's effect to be uniformly good across its entire course to count as nourishing, the treatise allows a single moment of genuine benefit — even one immediately followed or preceded by harm — to be sufficient, treating nutriment as a matter of accomplishing its function at all, not accomplishing it painlessly throughout.

9. The Root of Every Illness: Why the Buddha Warns Against Craving Food

The Buddha's stark teaching that the four nutriments are "the root of every illness, the root of every boil, the root of every poisoned arrow, the cause of old age and death" seems to sit uneasily beside his own teaching that the path's provisions — nutriment foremost among them — lead ultimately to nirvāṇa's own happiness. The reconciliation: this warning targets craving for morsel-food specifically, since it is precisely such craving, not nutriment itself, that drives the unwholesome conduct bringing intense future suffering — and even were morsel-food merely pleasant now, with no bodily cost at all, the wise should still never indulge in craving it.

10. The Flesh of One's Own Child: The Parable of Morsel-Food

One of the tradition's most harrowing narratives illustrates how a monk should regard his own necessary eating: a husband and wife, fleeing famine with their only, deeply beloved child, become stranded in the wilderness with their provisions exhausted and death imminent. In an agony of calculation, the husband reasons that sacrificing one life to save two is better than all three perishing together — and, after his wife's initial devastated refusal, they together end their child's life, dry the flesh into rations, and eat it bite by bite along the remaining road, weeping and calling the child's name with every mouthful, feeling nothing but grief and self-reproach throughout, never once any pleasure.

12. The Pit of Blazing Coals: The Parable of Volition

13. Three Hundred Spears a Day: The Parable of Consciousness

The most visceral of the four similes: a man struck by a hundred spears each in the morning, at noon, and in the evening, every single day for the rest of his life, until his entire body becomes one continuous, unhealed wound. Just so, a practitioner is constantly drawn away, day after day, by three hundred distinct external objects, each piercing his own dedicated practice like a sharp spear, wounding his cultivated attainments without pause. A further question asks why only consciousness receives this particular simile, resolved variously — perhaps all four nutriments deserve it equally, perhaps mind's own especially stubborn nature earns it a uniquely harsh rebuke.

14. A Ladder of Worthy Recipients: From an Ordinary Being to the Buddha Himself

The fascicle turns to a rich sūtra passage ranking the merit of giving food up an entire hierarchy of recipients: an ordinary being on the threshold of the path, a stream-enterer, a hundred stream-enterers, a once-returner, a non-returner, an arhat, a pratyekabuddha, and finally the Buddha himself — each successive gift surpassing a hundred of the rank below it, since each recipient has cut off progressively more fetters and progressed further toward liberation. Strikingly, building a monastery and donating it to the wider community of monks outweighs even giving directly to the Buddha, since a monastery's benefit, unlike a single personal gift, remains unobstructed and effectively inexhaustible across time.

15. Field or Intention? Which Determines the Merit of a Gift

A final puzzle asks why the Buddha praises only the quality of the recipient ("the field") rather than the donor's own intention, when both plainly affect a gift's resulting merit — resolved by noting the Buddha addresses two different audiences differently: those already wise enough to judge worthy recipients for themselves need no such teaching, while those with faith but not yet wisdom benefit from being told. In truth, both field and intention matter, yielding four combinations of merit — illustrated by the delightfully symmetrical test case of whether the Buddha's own gift to Śāriputra, or Śāriputra's gift to the Buddha, yields the greater merit.

The unresolved disagreement over the Buddha-and-Śāriputra test case — one side crediting the superior donor's intention, the other the superior recipient's field — is left standing without a declared winner, a rare moment where the treatise seems content to let two genuinely different, equally defensible ways of weighing merit simply coexist rather than forcing a single verdict.

16. Can You Give to Someone Not Yet a Fruit? The Fascicle's Final Puzzle

The fascicle closes on a subtle question: can a gift be given to someone merely approaching one of the four fruits of the path, rather than having already attained it — and, if so, why did the earlier merit-ranking passage never mention such a person, listing only the fruits themselves? Competing resolutions distinguish giving food from giving robes, or distinguish personally consuming a gift from merely having it allocated on one's behalf during the very moment of attainment, before a final view denies that a fruit-approacher can receive anything at all, reinterpreting the celebratory verse describing him as a "true field of merit" as establishing his worthiness in principle rather than his capacity to actually receive a gift in the moment.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: can a gift be given to one merely approaching the fruit of stream-entry, not yet having attained it? If it can, why does the earlier sūtra passage not mention this? But if it cannot, how is another sūtra passage to be reconciled, which says:

四向與四果　是真福田僧具戒慧等持　施者獲大果有說可施。謂衣服等非諸飲食。問若可施者。前經何故不說。答前所引經說施飲食。非衣服等是故不說。有說。此向亦可施食。但有攝取而無受用。謂毘呵羅中有入見道者。餘人為受所施食分。或將食時有入見道。施主以食置其膝上。或復安置曼荼羅中。問前所引經何故不說。答彼經唯說能受用者。此據攝取故不相違。有餘師言。此向不可施是故前經不說。問若爾者所引經頌當云何通。答彼頌欲顯補特伽羅由道差別成福田義。謂顯成就無漏道者施獲大果名真福田。不說爾時能受他施。前問可施不可施者。意問能受不能受故。

"The four approachers, together with the four fruits, Are the true field of merit, the Saṅgha — Endowed with moral discipline, wisdom, and concentration, A donor obtains from them a great fruit." Some say that a gift can indeed be given — namely, robes and the like, though not food and drink specifically. Question: if it can be given, why does the earlier sūtra passage not mention this? Answer: the earlier sūtra passage speaks specifically of giving food and drink, not robes and the like, and hence it does not mention this case. Others say: food, too, can indeed be given to one approaching a fruit, but only in the sense of being allocated to him without his actually consuming it — that is, when someone within a monastery enters the path of seeing, others receive his own allotted portion of food on his behalf; or, at the very moment he enters the path of seeing while about to eat, a donor's food is placed upon his lap, or set upon a maṇḍala-altar near him. Question: why, then, does the earlier sūtra passage not mention this? Answer: that earlier sūtra speaks only of one who actually consumes the food, whereas this present case concerns mere allocation, and hence there is no contradiction between the two. Other masters say: one approaching a fruit genuinely cannot be given anything at all, and it is for this reason that the earlier sūtra passage does not mention this. Question: if so, how is the verse cited above to be reconciled? Answer: that verse's own intent is simply to show that a person becomes a field of merit through the distinct degree of his progress along the path — that is, to show that one who has attained the undefiled path obtains a great fruit for a donor and is properly called a true field of merit — it is not claiming that such a person can, at that very moment, actually receive another's gift. The earlier question of whether a gift "can be given" was asking specifically whether it can be received and made use of, which is an entirely different question.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The final resolution's careful separation of "is this person a worthy field of merit" from "can this person, right now, actually receive and use a gift" closes the fascicle on a distinction with real staying power: a person's spiritual worth and their present practical capacity to benefit from an act of generosity turn out to be two independent questions, and conflating them is exactly the confusion this whole closing puzzle exists to untangle.
