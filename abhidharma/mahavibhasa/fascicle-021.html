The Jar That Isn't Really the Cause of Its Own Perception

Fascicle 21 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa's thirteenth part (智納息第二之十三), completing the efficient-cause's own investigation — the monk and the bandit illustrating why arising and ceasing never coincide, Mount Sumeru's non-diminishment despite countless beings reaching nirvāṇa — before exhaustively cross-tabulating all six causes against one another and opening the four-conditions framework, closing on a genuinely open philosophical debate over whether cause and condition, sharing the very same substance, differ in any real sense at all. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 21 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa opened in Fascicle 9, now in its thirteenth part (之十三), and opens mid-argument, directly continuing fascicle 20's own closing definition of the efficient-cause. Terms established in earlier fascicles, such as all six causes and their own individual definitions, recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. One Tree, One House: Why Non-Obstruction Still Covers Everything

Fascicle 21 opens mid-argument, continuing directly from the efficient-cause's own closing definition: its substance is identical to the dominant-condition's, both taking every dharma as their nature, yet differing in meaning — “mostly superior” for the dominant-condition, “offering no obstruction” for the efficient-cause. A sharp objection follows: if non-obstruction is the criterion, how can the efficient-cause really cover every dharma, when a single tree occupying a spot plainly excludes a second tree there, and a single eye-consciousness excludes a second consciousness arising in the very same instant? The treatise resolves this through a striking personification — each arising dharma is, in effect, telling every other dharma “I do not obstruct you; were I obstructing you, you could not arise at all” — meaning mutual exclusion between two candidates never amounts to active obstruction by whichever one happens to arise.

Chinese Source

能作因體即增上緣。俱一切法為自性故。然義有異。謂多勝義是增上緣義。不障礙義是能作因義。問若多勝義是增上緣義。則所緣緣亦應名增上。彼體亦攝一切法故。如品類足論辯。此二緣俱以一切法為自性故。答若依相續則此二緣寬狹相似。若依剎那則增上緣體義多勝。謂緣一切法非我行相現在前時。一切法中非所緣者。謂此自性相應俱有。非增上者唯此自性。由此相應俱有諸法。是此增上非所緣故。問若不障礙義是能作因義者。則能作因體應不攝一切法。謂一有情若有一趣蘊界處起。即此有情更無第二蘊界處生。若依一眼一眼識生。於中更無第二識起。如於一處。若有一樹無第二樹。若有一舍無第二舍。如是諸法互相障礙。云何此因攝一切法。

Ru-Yi Translation

The efficient-cause's own very substance is precisely the dominant-condition, both alike taking every dharma as their own nature — yet their meaning differs, since "for the most part superior" is the very meaning of the dominant-condition, while "offering no obstruction" is the very meaning of the efficient-cause. One asks: if "for the most part superior" is the very meaning of the dominant-condition, then the object-condition, too, ought to be named "dominant," since its own substance likewise encompasses every dharma, exactly as the Prakaraṇapāda itself explains — these two conditions alike taking every dharma as their own nature. The answer: considered with reference to a continuum, these two conditions are similar in breadth; considered with reference to a single moment, the dominant-condition's own substance is, for the most part, superior in scope — that is, when taking every dharma as one's object under the aspect of "not-self," whatever, among all dharmas, is not taken as an object refers only to that very dharma's own associated and co-existent dharmas, whereas whatever is not dominant refers only to that very dharma's own self-nature — and it is by virtue of these associated and co-existent dharmas that the dominant-condition's own scope exceeds the object-condition's. One asks: if "offering no obstruction" is the very meaning of the efficient-cause, then the efficient-cause's own substance ought not to encompass every dharma at all — that is, when a single sentient being gives rise to a single destiny's own aggregates, elements, and sense fields, that very sentient being can never give rise to a second, distinct set of aggregates, elements, and sense fields at the same time; when a single eye gives rise to a single instance of eye-consciousness, no second instance of consciousness can arise there as well; just as within a single location, if one tree stands there, no second tree can stand there too, and if one house stands there, no second house can stand there too — all such dharmas mutually obstruct one another in this way, so how could this cause encompass every dharma at all?

答雖有此事而理無違。謂一有情若有一趣蘊界處起義。語諸餘蘊界處曰。我於汝等不為障礙隨汝等生。設我與汝為障礙者。汝等於彼則不得生。若依一眼眼識現前。此識義語餘眼識言。我於汝等不為障礙隨汝等生。設我與汝為障礙者。汝等於彼不得現前。樹及舍喻准此應說。又即於彼為障礙者。彼若生時亦不為礙。是故一法將欲生時。餘一切法皆不為礙。設有一法障礙彼者。彼法爾時應不得起故。不障礙義是能作因義。而能作因攝一切法。問因緣和合故諸法生。因緣和合故諸法滅。因緣無有不和合時。諸法云何不恒生滅。

The answer: although this circumstance does indeed occur, no contradiction to the principle follows — that is, when a single sentient being gives rise to a single destiny's own aggregates, elements, and sense fields, this is, in effect, as though that sentient being were saying to every other aggregate, element, and sense field: I do not obstruct you — arise as you will; were I to obstruct you, you would then be altogether unable to arise. When a single eye gives rise to eye-consciousness before it, that consciousness is, in effect, as though it were saying to every other instance of eye-consciousness: I do not obstruct you — arise as you will; were I to obstruct you, you would then be altogether unable to manifest before it. The similes of the tree and the house should be explained according to this very same principle. And, further, with respect to whatever it is that actually does obstruct that other dharma, that other dharma, at the very moment it might arise, likewise offers no obstruction in return — and so, whenever a single dharma is on the verge of arising, every other dharma offers it no obstruction whatsoever; were some other dharma to actually obstruct it, that very dharma would then, at that very moment, itself be unable to arise. It is for this reason that "offering no obstruction" is the very meaning of the efficient-cause, and yet the efficient-cause still encompasses every dharma without exception. One asks: since it is through the coming-together of cause and condition that dharmas arise, and through the coming-together of cause and condition that dharmas cease, and since cause and condition are never without this state of having-come-together, why, then, do dharmas not perpetually arise and cease, constantly, without ceasing?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The personification here does real philosophical work rather than merely decorating the prose: it isolates “obstruction” as a specific, asymmetric causal relation — something one dharma actively does to another — from the mere fact that two mutually exclusive dharmas cannot both occupy the same slot, which is simply a structural feature of how existence is arranged rather than an act any dharma performs against another. This distinction is what lets the efficient-cause remain the most universal of the six causes, covering literally everything, without being falsified by the everyday observation that a house forecloses a second house standing in the very same place.

2. Why Dharmas Don't Arise and Cease Nonstop

A subtler puzzle follows: since dharmas arise and cease through cause-and-condition's own coming-together, and cause-and-condition is never actually absent, why don't dharmas simply arise and cease constantly, without pause? Vasumitra locates the answer in the singularity of each coming-together itself, illustrated by a person who falls off a cliff and is then buried under continuously piling earth, unable even to move, let alone rise again; the Great Virtuous One notes that any single coming-together is itself only ever momentary; and Buddhadeva insists that arising and ceasing each occur exactly once per instance, since a “second arising” or “second ceasing” would serve no function at all. A further puzzle then asks whether cause-and-condition's own functional capacity is singular or manifold, and whether ceasing, no less than arising, truly depends on cause and condition at all — the Dārṣṭāntikas denying it, comparing ceasing to an arrow's fall or a wheel's stop needing no further force, the Abhidharmikas insisting ceasing is itself a genuine function requiring a genuine cause.

Chinese Source

尊者世友作如是言。一生和合故諸法生。一滅和合故諸法滅。生滅和合無二無多。諸法云何恒生恒滅。復作是言。因緣和合諸法生已。次後相續無量剎那。連注覆壓不能復起。如人墮崖復為上土續續覆壓。彼人爾時尚不能動況得起耶。大德說曰。和合因緣既唯暫有。云何諸法恒生滅耶。尊者覺天作如是說。諸法生滅各唯一時。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Venerable Vasumitra explained it in this way: it is through a single coming-together that dharmas arise, and through a single coming-together that dharmas cease — the coming-together proper to arising and the coming-together proper to ceasing being neither two, nor many — so how, then, could dharmas perpetually arise and cease, constantly, without ceasing? He further said: once dharmas have arisen through the coming-together of cause and condition, the immediately following, countless subsequent moments continue on in succession, pressing down and covering over what came before, so that it is no longer able to arise again — just as a person who has fallen from a cliff is then buried, moment after moment, by earth continuously falling and piling upon him from above, so that this person, at that very time, cannot even move, let alone rise up again. The Great Virtuous One stated: since the coming-together of cause and condition exists only momentarily, how, then, could dharmas perpetually arise and cease, constantly, without ceasing? The Venerable Buddhadeva explained it in this way: the arising and the ceasing of dharmas, each and every one, occur only a single time —

應有作用生已復生。滅已復滅便為無用。是故諸法生滅非恒。問因緣和合生諸法時。因緣功能為多為一。若言唯一云何依多。若言有多云何和合。答可說為一可說為多。辦一事故依多體故。復次因緣功能相隨順故。可說為一生多法故可說為多。復次因緣功能同令諸法起作用故可說為一。起色受等作用別故可說為多。若彼功能無多種者。所起作用色非色等應無差別。是故諸法因緣功能。不可定言是多是一。問如有因緣故諸法得生。亦有因緣故諸法滅耶。譬喻尊者作如是說。生待因緣滅則不爾。如人射時發箭須力墮則不然。如陶家輪轉時須力止則不爾。阿毘達磨諸論師言。諸法生滅俱待因緣以滅與生皆是用故。問前所說喻當云何通。答不必須通非三藏故。

were there some further function whereby what has already arisen could arise yet again, or whereby what has already ceased could cease yet again, that function would be entirely without purpose — and it is for this reason that the arising and ceasing of dharmas is never perpetual or constant. One asks: at the very moment cause and condition come together to give rise to dharmas, is the functional capacity of that cause and condition singular, or is it manifold? If it is said to be singular, how could it depend upon many distinct substances? If it is said to be manifold, how could these many substances properly be said to "come together" at all? The answer: it may properly be called singular, and it may properly be called manifold — singular, because it accomplishes a single task; manifold, because it depends upon many distinct substances. Further, because the functional capacities of cause and condition mutually accord with one another, this may be called singular; because they give rise to many distinct dharmas, this may be called manifold. Further, because the functional capacities of cause and condition equally cause dharmas to arise and to function, this may be called singular; because the functions thereby given rise to — material form, feeling, and the rest — are each distinct from one another, this may be called manifold; for, were that functional capacity to possess no manifold aspect whatsoever, then the functions thereby arising, whether possessing form or not possessing form, and so on, ought to display no distinction among themselves at all. It is for this reason that the functional capacity of cause and condition among dharmas cannot be definitively said to be either simply manifold or simply singular. One asks: just as there is a cause and condition by which dharmas arise, is there likewise a cause and condition by which dharmas cease? The Dārṣṭāntika masters explained it in this way: arising depends upon cause and condition, but ceasing is not so — just as, when a person shoots an arrow, the arrow's launching requires the application of force, yet its eventual falling does not; or just as, when a potter's wheel is set spinning, its continued turning requires the application of force, yet its eventual coming to rest does not. The various Abhidharma masters state: the arising and the ceasing of dharmas alike depend upon cause and condition, since ceasing, no less than arising, is itself a kind of function. One asks: how, then, is the simile stated earlier to be reconciled with this?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The buried-alive image is one of the more viscerally memorable metaphors in this entire treatise, and it captures something genuinely important about the school's own model of time: a past dharma is not merely inert or forgotten but actively suppressed, pressed down by the sheer accumulating weight of every subsequent moment — a picture of the past as a kind of permanent burial rather than a simple fading away, which fits naturally with Sarvāstivāda's own insistence that past dharmas still really exist, just no longer able to act.

3. The Monk and the Bandit: Same Cause, Opposite Timing

The Abhidharmikas decline to reconcile the Dārṣṭāntika's own arrow-and-wheel simile, noting it derives from no canonical scripture, and instead show that the arrow and wheel do have their own causes for falling and stopping after all — a target or obstruction, or, failing that, the very force of the original shot or spin. A further puzzle then asks why arising and ceasing, both depending equally on cause-and-condition, don't simply happen simultaneously, resolved through the image of a monk traveling after the rains retreat: a bandit first bows and follows him respectfully, then later, in the wilderness, robs and insults him — the very same person shifting from favoring to opposing, exactly as one underlying cause-and-condition shifts from favoring a dharma's arising to opposing it at the moment of its ceasing.

Chinese Source

然凡聖法異不可例同。又箭與輪亦由因墮止。箭墮因者謂排楯的等。設無餘因則彎弓等亦是其因。若不先射今何由墮。輪止因者謂手杖等。設無餘因則能轉者亦是其因。若先不轉今何由止。問若排手等障礙箭輪令其墮止。如何與彼為能作因。答排手等物障彼行轉。非礙墮止為能作因於理何失。問若法生滅俱待因緣。生滅因緣一切時有。何不生時亦滅滅時亦生耶。尊者世友作如是說。生滅兩時和合各異。是故生時無滅滅時無生。不可一因有二果故。復作是說。生時因緣隨順彼法。滅時因緣違害彼法。雖彼因緣前後體一。

Ru-Yi Translation

The answer: it need not necessarily be reconciled at all, since it does not derive from the Tripiṭaka. Moreover, the dharmas of ordinary beings and of noble ones differ, and cannot simply be equated by way of such an analogy. Further, the arrow and the wheel, too, each fall and come to rest owing to their own respective causes: the arrow's own cause of falling refers to a shield, a target, a wall, and the like — and, should no other such cause be present, then the very bow and its drawn string, too, count as its cause, since, had it not first been shot, how could it now be falling at all? The wheel's own cause of coming to rest refers to a hand, a rod, and the like — and, should no other such cause be present, then the very force that first set it spinning, too, counts as its cause, since, had it not first been spun, how could it now be coming to rest at all? One asks: if the shield, the hand, and the like obstruct the arrow's flight and the wheel's spinning, causing them to fall and to come to rest, how can they properly serve as an efficient-cause for this? The answer: the shield, the hand, and the like obstruct the arrow's continued flight and the wheel's continued spinning — they do not obstruct the arrow's falling or the wheel's coming to rest, and so no fault follows. One asks: if the arising and the ceasing of dharmas both alike depend upon cause and condition, and cause and condition are present at every single time without exception, why, then, does a dharma not also cease at the very moment it arises, or arise at the very moment it ceases? The Venerable Vasumitra explained it in this way: the coming-together proper to arising and the coming-together proper to ceasing are themselves each distinct — it is for this reason that, at the time of arising, no ceasing occurs, and, at the time of ceasing, no arising occurs, since a single cause cannot properly bear two distinct effects. He further explained: at the time of arising, the cause and condition accord with and favor that dharma; at the time of ceasing, the cause and condition contradict and oppose that dharma — although this cause and condition, considered across its earlier and later phases, remains one and the same in substance,

而益損用時分不同。猶如苾芻解安居已。携持衣鉢遊歷諸寺。有賊見之先申禮敬恭順隨逐。後至曠野奪其衣鉢陵辱而去。生滅因緣亦復如是。大德說曰。生滅兩事既互相違如何俱有。尊者妙音作如是說。諸法生時未有作用當何所滅。諸法滅時已有作用復何所生。

the timing of its benefiting and its harming function nonetheless differs. It is just as a monk who, having concluded the rains retreat, takes up his robe and bowl and travels from monastery to monastery: a bandit, catching sight of him, first bows in reverence and follows along in courteous compliance, but later, upon reaching a stretch of wilderness, seizes his robe and bowl, subjects him to insult, and departs — the cause and condition proper to arising and ceasing are exactly the same. The Great Virtuous One objected: arising and ceasing are themselves two mutually contradictory events — how could they possibly be present together at once? The Venerable Ghoṣa explained it in this way: at the very time dharmas arise, no function yet exists at all — what, then, could there be left to cease? At the very time dharmas cease, function has already fully come to be — what further arising, then, could there be?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ghoṣa's own closing explanation offers a genuinely different, almost definitional solution to the same puzzle worth setting beside the monk-and-bandit image: arising and ceasing cannot coincide simply because, at the moment of arising, no function yet exists for anything to cease, and, at the moment of ceasing, function has already fully manifested so that nothing further remains to arise — a temporal, almost logical separation rather than a causal one, offered as a second, independent route to the same conclusion the bandit simile reaches through vivid narrative instead.

4. One Cause, Many Effects: When Does 'One' Become 'Many'?

A genuinely abstract puzzle opens: when a single dharma serves as efficient-cause for many dharmas, should this be modeled as one cause producing many effects, or as many causes producing many effects? And when many dharmas serve as efficient-cause for a single dharma, is this many causing one, or one causing one? Each answer threatens an absurd consequence — a single cause seemingly forced to yield a single fruit, or a multiple cause seemingly forced to yield a multiple fruit — until the treatise resolves the puzzle by declaring that one-serving-many operates exactly like many-causing-many, and many-serving-one operates exactly like one-causing-one, with the numbers themselves shifting freely because the entire discussion concerns function alone, never a dharma's own unchanging substance.

Chinese Source

問一法既與多法為能作因。多法亦與一法為能作因。一法與多法為能作因時。為如一法與多法為因。為如多法與多法為因耶。多法與一法為能作因時。為如多法與一法為因。為如一法與一法為因耶。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: since a single dharma can serve as an efficient-cause for many dharmas, and many dharmas can likewise serve as an efficient-cause for a single dharma — when a single dharma serves as an efficient-cause for many, does this operate as a single dharma serving as cause for many, or as many dharmas serving as cause for many? And when many dharmas serve as an efficient-cause for a single dharma, does this operate as many dharmas serving as cause for one, or as a single dharma serving as cause for one?

設爾何失此一法與多法為能作因時。若如一法與多法為因者。云何不因一故果亦成一。若如多法與多法為因者。云何不果多故因亦成多。此多法與一法為能作因時。若如多法與一法為因者。云何不因多故果亦成多。若如一法與一法為因者。云何不果一故因亦成一。

Suppose either — what fault follows? When a single dharma serves as an efficient-cause for many dharmas: if this operates as a single dharma serving as cause for many, why does the effect not likewise remain single, since the cause is single? If this operates as many dharmas serving as cause for many, why does the cause not likewise become many, since the effect is many? When many dharmas serve as an efficient-cause for a single dharma: if this operates as many dharmas serving as cause for one, why does the effect not likewise become many, since the cause is many? If this operates as a single dharma serving as cause for one, why does the cause not likewise remain single, since the effect is single?

答應作是說。一法與多法為能作因時。亦如多法與一法為因。多法與一法為能作因時。亦如一法與多法為因。問若爾一應成多多應成一。答我說一亦成多多亦成一。此依作用不依實體。我說諸因以作用為果非以實體為果。又說。諸果以作用為因非以實體為因。諸法實體恒無轉變非因果故。然無為法名能作因。但不為障非能辦果。

The answer: it should be said that, when a single dharma serves as an efficient-cause for many dharmas, this operates just as though many dharmas were serving as cause for many; and, when many dharmas serve as an efficient-cause for a single dharma, this operates just as though a single dharma were serving as cause for one. One asks: if that is so, then the one ought to become many, and the many ought to become one. The answer: I do indeed say that the one becomes many, and that the many become one — but this is spoken of only with reference to function, not with reference to actual substance. I say that every cause takes its function, not its actual substance, as its fruit; and I likewise say that every fruit takes its function, not its actual substance, as its cause — every dharma's own actual substance remaining forever unchanging, never itself either cause or fruit in this substantial sense. Yet unconditioned dharmas, too, are named an efficient-cause, though they merely offer no obstruction, without actually accomplishing any fruit.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The function-versus-substance distinction deployed here to let “one become many and many become one” without contradiction is one of the most important moves in this whole causal apparatus: it lets Sarvāstivāda maintain, without any tension, both that every dharma's own substance remains eternally fixed and unchanging across the three time-periods, and that causal relationships are endlessly fluid and multipliable — cause and effect are always claims about function, a genuinely different register from claims about what a dharma actually, substantially is.

5. Why the Conditioned Has Cause and Fruit, and the Unconditioned Has Neither

A cascade of seven or eight distinct explanations accounts for perhaps the single most important asymmetry in this school's causal theory: conditioned dharmas possess both cause and fruit because they flow through the three time-periods, possess function, arise and cease, come together, bear the three characteristic marks, and are inherently feeble and dependent — like a traveler needing provisions, or a weak person needing support — while unconditioned dharmas, lacking every one of these features, possess neither. A further clarification insists that whenever a conditioned dharma fails to arise, this is always due to some other conditioned dharma serving as hindrance, never to any unconditioned dharma, since the unconditioned, precisely because it offers no obstruction at any time, cannot itself be blamed for anything failing to arise — just as an irrigation channel is never itself the reason water fails to flow through it.

8. Why Only the Killer, Never Every Bystander, Incurs the Offense

A genuinely pressing objection tests the efficient-cause's universal non-obstruction principle against ordinary moral responsibility: if every sentient being fails to obstruct a killing, and non-obstruction alone qualifies as efficient-causation, why doesn't everyone equally incur the offense? The treatise answers that only the one who actually initiates the preparatory effort, gives rise to the evil intention, and completes the act itself incurs the offense — mere non-obstruction, universal and passive, never rises to the level of moral culpability. An exactly parallel puzzle about theft follows, resolved through the distinction between a personal-effort fruit, obtained through one's own actual labor, and a mere dominant fruit, obtained only through others' non-obstruction — a farmer's own fruit is both, while a mere diner's is only the latter, and it is this distinction, not universal non-obstruction, that locates the offense of theft specifically against the rightful owner.

10. Three Kinds of Restraint, and Whether a Cause's Power Can Wax or Wane

A threefold taxonomy of restraint from evil closes out the practical applications of efficient-causation: self-dominant restraint, protecting oneself from a bad rebirth; world-dominant restraint, protecting oneself from the world's censure or from setting a bad example; and dharma-dominant restraint, protecting the true teaching itself from disrepute — each simply a named instance of the proximate efficient-cause already discussed. It is in order to express this very distinction that six are named as causes while four are named as conditions; were this not the intended distinction, the very number of causes and of conditions ought to be exactly the same.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Great Virtuous One's own closing proposal — operation versus mere accompaniment, proximity versus distance — finally lands on a genuine, if modest, distinction: cause carries a sense of intimacy and closeness, condition a sense of looseness and distance, both terms picking out the very same underlying substance from two different vantage points of directness, which is precisely why six are named as causes while only four are named as conditions — a numerical asymmetry that, on any other account, would have no reason to exist at all, and which this treatise leaves standing as the one durable trace of a distinction that everything else in this final debate had seemed, moment by moment, to dissolve.
