Butter on Sunlit Water

Fascicle 42 closes the Meaninglessness Nyāsa's account of appetite with a memorable story of an elephant reborn as an arhat, then opens the Volition Nyāsa, working through the three wisdoms of hearing, reflection, and cultivation, the coexistence of vitarka and vicāra within a single mind, and, at its close, one of Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma's most foundational and enduring maps: the five categories of mental factors, from the ten dharmas found in every mind whatsoever down to the ten minor afflictions that can never arise together. 1. Difficult to Satisfy, Difficult to Sustain

Fascicle 42 opens by closing the previous fascicle's discussion of appetite with two further vices: "difficult to satisfy," the glutton whose heavy eating never leaves him full, and "difficult to sustain," the picky eater who gorges yet still selects and refuses food out of sheer indulgence. As with great desire and dissatisfaction before them, the treatise notes these two are so easily confused in ordinary speech that a careful distinction is needed to keep them apart.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The vocabulary here is strikingly visceral — gorging, ravenous, gluttonous — compared to the more abstract "great desire" and "dissatisfaction" of the previous fascicle; the treatise seems to be working its way from the most refined psychological analysis of craving down to its crudest, most bodily expression in the simple act of overeating.

2. Their Difference, and a Note on Clothing

The treatise ties these two appetite-vices back to the broader psychological categories already established: difficult to satisfy is simply great desire directed at food, difficult to sustain is simply dissatisfaction expressed through pickiness. A brief methodological aside follows, explaining why this pairing — unlike great desire and dissatisfaction — did not need its own separate question about their distinction in every version of the text, since the answer here is identical to what was already given.

3. Their Antidotes: Easy to Satisfy, Easy to Sustain

The treatise defines the corresponding virtues in exact mirror-image terms: easy to satisfy, whoever eats moderately and finds contentment in little; easy to sustain, whoever accepts what comes without picking and choosing. The now-familiar pattern repeats — since ordinary speech confuses these two just as readily as their vicious counterparts, the treatise carefully distinguishes them, tying easy-to-satisfy back to few desires and easy-to-sustain back to contentment.

4. The Elephant Who Became an Arhat, and the Relativity of Appetite

A remarkable story illustrates that quantity alone never determines whether someone counts as easy or difficult to satisfy: a monk who, in a past life, had been a female elephant carrying a relic of the Buddha into Kaśmīra, is reborn human through that very merit and attains arhatship — yet, through sheer habitual force from his elephant existence, still requires a full measure of grain daily. When nuns mock him as anything but easy to satisfy, he reveals that his body's own natural appetite would demand far more, and that his own constant restraint is what actually makes him easy to satisfy despite the large absolute quantity. A parallel example follows with King Prasenajit, and the discussion closes by cataloguing how different animals pair craving and eating in every possible combination.

Chinese Source

有多食者而名易滿。如食一斛方得充濟。但食爾所更不多食。昔有牝象名曰磨荼。從外方載佛馱都來入迦濕彌羅國。乘斯福力命終生此得丈夫身。出家修道成阿羅漢。宿習力故日食一斛乃得充濟。將般涅槃集曾供覲苾芻尼曰。當為汝等說我勝法。尼眾誚言。尊既易滿。誠有勝法。阿羅漢曰。汝勿相輕吾實易滿。苾芻尼言。日食一斛如何易滿。阿羅漢曰。汝等不知我此生前曾為牝象載佛馱都來入此國。由斯善業今得為人出家修道成阿羅漢。餘習力故日應食飯一斛五斗。恒自節量。但食一斛如斯易滿非我而誰。時苾芻尼頂禮悔謝。又勝軍王福德力故日能食飯飲甘蔗漿各兩大斛。此漿及飯因一莖蔗一枝稻生。然自節量各食一斛。此等多食而名易滿。有選擇食名為難養。如食麁食足得充濟而饕餮故選擇食之。有選擇食而名易養。如食麁食不得支身。選擇食之方可充濟。而於美食心不耽嗜。

5. A New Nyāsa Opens: What Is Volition? What Is Deliberation?

An entirely new topic begins: the Volition Nyāsa, opening with the deceptively simple question of what volition (cetanā) and deliberation actually are. The treatise first refutes the Dārṣṭāntikas, who hold that volition and deliberation are simply mind itself with no distinct substance of their own, and then the grammarians, who hold that though the two words differ in sound, they name one undivided thing — establishing against both that volition and deliberation are genuine, distinct mental factors, and defining volition broadly as every kind of mental karma, whether it draws forth or merely completes a being's own class of existence.

Refuting a purely verbal or nominalist account of mental factors here is not a merely academic move: if volition and deliberation were nothing more than convenient labels for the same underlying mental stuff, karma itself would lose its own precise causal machinery — this treatise's entire analytical apparatus depends on being able to isolate genuinely distinct mental factors, each doing its own specific causal work, rather than treating the mind as one undifferentiated blur.

6. What Kind of Wisdom Is "Deliberation"? Five Rival Readings

The treatise turns to defining deliberation, and five distinct masters propose five different readings of exactly which wisdom the scriptural passage has in mind — the wisdom of the path of seeing itself, the wisdom cultivated at the four preparatory stages before the path, the wisdom of formal meditative reflection like the contemplation of unattractiveness, the wisdom consisting in hearing that discriminates the particular and shared characteristics of dharmas, or the foundational wisdom simply attained through mastering the scriptures. The treatise's own verdict refuses to choose, affirming that the passage speaks broadly of every kind of wisdom at once. 10. Which Realms Possess Which Wisdom, and Why

The treatise maps the three wisdoms onto the three realms of existence: the desire realm possesses heard- and reflective-wisdom but lacks cultivated-wisdom, since it is too unsettled a realm to sustain genuine meditative cultivation; the form and formless realms, by contrast, lack reflective-wisdom entirely, since any attempt to merely reflect there inevitably deepens into full cultivation instead; and the formless realm further lacks heard-wisdom altogether, having no ears at all to hear the Dharma spoken aloud.

12. The Buddha, the Pratyekabuddha, and the Śrāvaka: Who Is Chiefly Displayed by Which Wisdom

The technical catalogue concludes, and the treatise turns to a genuinely illuminating question: although the Buddha, pratyekabuddhas, and śrāvakas each possess all three kinds of wisdom in full, each is chiefly characterized by a different one — the Buddha by cultivated-wisdom, since he spontaneously awakens complete with every meditatively-cultivated power and fearlessness; the pratyekabuddha by reflective-wisdom, since he too awakens spontaneously but lacks those cultivated powers, entering the path chiefly through his own extensive reflection; and the śrāvaka by heard-wisdom, since he enters the path specifically by hearing the sound of the Dharma taught to him by another.

13. A New Nyāsa Within a Nyāsa: What Is Vitarka? What Is Vicāra?

Having closed the discussion of the three wisdoms with a note on how loosely each name can, in scripture's own broader usage, apply to any of the three, the treatise opens a further precise psychological distinction: vitarka, or investigative thought, and vicāra, sustained examination — refuting, once again, the Dārṣṭāntikas' claim that these are simply mind itself, and the further claim that they are merely nominal labels without genuine substance, before defining vitarka as the mind's own seeking, discerning, and constructing activity, and vicāra as its scrutinizing, following, sustained activity.

14. Coarse and Subtle: Seven Similes for How One Mind Holds Both at Once

The treatise settles that vitarka is simply the coarse aspect of a single mind, and vicāra its subtle aspect, coexisting within that same mind without contradiction because their functions differ — vitarka forceful and keen, vicāra slow and sustained.
