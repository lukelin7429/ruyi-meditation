The Firefly at Sunrise

Fascicle 33 opens with an elaborately staged formal debate against the Vātsīputrīyas over whether nirvāṇa itself can shift between being a learner's and one beyond training's, before defining the five aggregates that only an arhat fully accomplishes, opening the sūtra's declaration that there exists only one true culmination against a catalog of the outsiders' false paths, and closing with the story of Susīma, sent by envious rivals to infiltrate the Buddha's own assembly and steal his teaching. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Is Nirvāṇa a Learner's, or One Beyond Training's? The Debate Opens

Fascicle 33 opens a formal debate against the Vātsīputrīyas, who hold that nirvāṇa itself may be a learner's, one beyond training's, or neither, depending on whose acquisition of freedom from bondage attaches to it. The orthodox position insists nirvāṇa belongs solely to neither category, since the very concepts of “learner” and “one beyond training” describe progress toward or completion of a further fruit — a dynamic nirvāṇa's own permanent, unchanging nature cannot possess. The refutation proceeds through an elaborate staged dialogue between “the proponent of correct reasoning” and “the Vibhajyavādin,” working through two rival readings of the Vātsīputrīya position in turn.

One asks: why is this treatise composed? The answer: although it has already been stated above that there are two kinds of nirvāṇa-realm, it has not yet been stated whether nirvāṇa is to be called a learner's, one beyond training's, or belonging to neither — and it is in order to state this that this treatise is now composed. Further, it is composed in order to refute other schools and to reveal the correct meaning — that is, there are those who hold that nirvāṇa may be a learner's, may be one beyond training's, or may belong to neither, as the Vātsīputrīyas maintain; this treatise, in order to refute that position, reveals that nirvāṇa belongs solely to neither a learner nor one beyond training, and it is for this reason that this treatise is composed. Is nirvāṇa to be called a learner's, one beyond training's, or belonging to neither? The answer: nirvāṇa ought to be called belonging to neither a learner nor one beyond training, since the sense of "learner" and "one beyond training" does not properly apply to it — that is, one is called "a learner" because, for the sake of a further fruit, one clearly and diligently advances in cultivation; once this cultivation is fully complete, and there exists no further, distinct fruit for which to advance in cultivation, one belongs to the same class as a learner yet is not identical to it, and is therefore called "one beyond training." Nirvāṇa possesses neither of these two senses, and it is for this reason that it is called belonging to neither a learner nor one beyond training. There are those who say: nirvāṇa may be a learner's, may be one beyond training's, or may belong to neither, as the Vātsīputrīyas maintain. They hold as follows: nirvāṇa's own inherent nature possesses three characteristics — first, belonging to a learner; second, belonging to one beyond training; third, belonging to neither. What is meant by "belonging to a learner"? It refers to the acquisition, obtained by a learner, of the cutting-off of the fetters, its full attainment realized and directly touched. What is meant by "belonging to one beyond training"? It refers to the acquisition, obtained by one beyond training, of the cutting-off of the fetters, its full attainment realized and directly touched. What is meant by "belonging to neither a learner nor one beyond training"? It refers to the acquisition, obtained through a contaminated path, of the cutting-off of the fetters, its full attainment realized and directly touched. There are those who say: at this point, something further ought indeed to be said, since the settling of the matter through question and answer makes the principle yet clearer. That is, the proponent of correct reasoning discriminates the fault in the Vibhajyavādin's own position, thereby revealing that his own position is free of error. The second holds that nirvāṇa is of three kinds: what belongs to a learner is forever a learner's; what belongs to one beyond training is forever one beyond training's; what belongs to neither a learner nor one beyond training is forever neither. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The debate's opening move — that a single dharma cannot possess three distinct substantial characteristics merely because it admits of three kinds of acquisition — is the entire fascicle's key in miniature: nearly everything that follows across this long, formally staged dialogue is an extended demonstration that acquisition and substance must never be confused, however tempting ordinary language makes that confusion when we speak of nirvāṇa as “becoming” a learner's or “turning into” one beyond training's.

2. The First Reading: Does Nirvāṇa Itself Transform?

Under the first interpretation, the Vibhajyavādin holds that nirvāṇa is at first neither a learner's nor one beyond training's, and only later transforms — into a learner's upon a stream-enterer's advance, into one beyond training's upon realizing arhatship, and back into a learner's again upon retrogression. Through three parallel cycles of question, answer, and challenge — the ordinary being advancing to non-returner, the learner advancing to arhatship, the arhat retrogressing back to learner status — the proponent of correct reasoning presses the same objection each time: if nirvāṇa is now a learner's, it ought already to have been a learner's beforehand, since a learner's substance is supposed to abide permanently.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The formally staged, almost ritualistic repetition across all three cycles — advance, challenge, counter-challenge — is not padding but a demonstration of rigor: by forcing the same objection through every possible direction of transformation a nirvāṇa-realm might undergo, the treatise leaves no asymmetric loophole where the Vibhajyavādin's position might quietly survive by being tested in only one direction.

3. The First Reading Refuted

The proponent of correct reasoning presses the decisive point: before a practitioner's advance, he had already attained the actual cutting-off of the relevant fetters, even though he had not yet attained a learner's specific acquisition — so if that cutting-off is not to be called a learner's simply because the learner's acquisition came only later, then the entire claim that nirvāṇa “becomes” a learner's in dependence on that acquisition collapses. The same challenge is then run through the second cycle, from learner to one beyond training.

5. The Verdict: Nirvāṇa Abides Permanently, Solely Belonging to Neither

The treatise delivers its own summary verdict, showing that both Vibhajyavādin readings collapse into the same underlying error from different directions: an indeterminately transforming nirvāṇa would be impermanent, while a fixedly threefold nirvāṇa would fracture the characteristic nature of dharmas into confused intermixture depending on which stage of practice one happens to occupy. Since no sūtra passage ever states that nirvāṇa is a learner's or one beyond training's, and since it possesses neither the dynamic of advancing cultivation nor its completion, it can only ever be classified as belonging to neither.

Nor does the Buddha ever state that nirvāṇa possesses the nature of belonging to a learner or to one beyond training — this, taken as a whole, invokes the noble teaching to refute both of the two positions stated above, since, within the sūtras, it is never once stated that nirvāṇa is a learner's or one beyond training's, and it is therefore certain that what they state does not accord with reason. Although there exists no passage stating outright that nirvāṇa belongs solely to neither a learner nor one beyond training, since the sense of "learner" and "one beyond training" does not properly apply to it, it is thereby determined to possess solely the nature of belonging to neither. Since nirvāṇa is forever solely of the nature of belonging to neither a learner nor one beyond training, the characteristic nature of dharmas remains fixedly determined without any confused intermixture, forever abiding in its own nature, never relinquishing that nature — nirvāṇa abides permanently, without any change or alteration; it is for this reason that nirvāṇa ought merely to be said to belong to neither a learner nor one beyond training — having thus stated the fault in the other school's own position, this passage now reveals that its own position is free of every such fault: since nirvāṇa is of but a single kind, the characteristic nature of dharmas remains fixedly determined without any confused intermixture, and nirvāṇa abides permanently, without any change or alteration — its principle is thereby well established. The meaning of "acquisition" discussed here shall be broadly discriminated later, within the Nyāsa on the Aggregate of the Concentrations.

13. Why the Outsiders Never Claim to Cut Off Self-Grasping

Two further vivid examples reinforce the same point — monks mocked by outsiders who cannot even name the five hindrances, and the outsider Māgaṇḍika, patting his own diseased belly and declaring freedom from illness itself to be nirvāṇa — before the treatise answers its final question: why, of the four graspings, do the outsiders never claim to have cut off the grasping that clings to a real self specifically? Because, having held fast to the reality of a self throughout beginningless time, positing its cutting-off would mean abandoning their own entire doctrine, dreading selflessness the way one dreads the edge of a cliff, and losing even their own fellow practitioners' respect.
