Six Armies Guarding Heaven

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 4 — the Miscellaneous Chapter's first section continues (雜蘊第一中世第一法納息第一之三), still entirely on supreme mundane dharma. Having settled that the dharma belongs to the form realm, the fascicle turns to the formless realm and argues, through a mirror-image of Fascicle 3's own reasoning, why it cannot belong there either; works through several sources on what "dispelling the perception of form" actually means; asks which of the form realm's three meditative stages — with both examination and analysis, with analysis alone, or with neither — it occupies, concluding it spans all three; explains, through the memorable image of the Thirty-Three Gods deploying six armies against the Asuras, why a higher meditative stage can cultivate a lower one but never the reverse; and closes by determining which of three feeling-faculties — pleasure, joy, or equanimity — it is associated with at each stage. Fascicle 5 continues the same topic.

1. Why Not the Formless Realm? The Same Argument in Reverse

Having established in Fascicle 3 that supreme mundane dharma belongs to the form realm, the treatise now applies the very same reasoning — the order in which the path of seeing tackles each realm's suffering — to rule out the formless realm as well, then digresses into why the truth of suffering alone is singled out among the four truths.

Chinese Source

2. Nine More Reasons Desire-Realm Suffering Comes First

The treatise now piles up nine further, independent reasons why the desire realm's suffering is directly realized before that of the form and formless realms — coarseness, nearness, moral variety, and more — closing with a striking parallel to how an ordinary being slanders the truths before a noble one comes to believe them.

3. Direct Realization vs. Accomplishing the Task

A parallel question opens for the second half of the root passage — the noble path's own "accomplishing of tasks" realm by realm — leading to five separate ways of distinguishing "direct realization" from "accomplishing the task" as technical terms.

Five separate criteria for one distinction — object versus affliction, knowledge versus the matter itself, wisdom versus cutting-off, uninterrupted versus liberation path, single moment versus succession — none chosen as definitive. The closing question is the fascicle's real hinge: it shows that "directly realized first" cannot, by itself, be the criterion for belonging to a realm, since the form realm itself violates that pattern with respect to its own suffering — the real criterion, given in the next section, turns out to be something else entirely: an all-pervading wisdom able to reach both up and down.

4. Why the Form Realm Succeeds Where the Formless Realm Fails

The real dividing line between the two higher realms is now stated plainly: the form realm's wisdom can reach both upward and downward, while the formless realm's wisdom can only reach upward — and a further argument follows from what it even means to enter a formless attainment at all.

Chinese Source

The treatise returns to piling up independent reasons — this time five more, each naming some capacity the formless realm simply lacks — before a final question asks why the formless realm has no path of seeing at all.

Within this general topic, a further distinction should be drawn. One asks: for what reason is supreme mundane dharma not bound to the formless realm? One asks, as a further question arising from this discussion: why does the formless realm lack a path of seeing? The answer: the same reasons given above for why supreme mundane dharma does not exist there serve as evidence for this too. There is, further, a distinct reason: the formless realm has an excess of calm-abiding — only a stage with an excess of insight can possess a path of seeing. Other masters, on this point, rule out both realms together: the desire realm, being extremely coarse, and the formless realm, being extremely subtle, both lack supreme mundane dharma. Further: the desire realm's wholesome roots are extremely feeble, and the formless realm's wholesome roots are extremely dull and torpid — both, for this reason, lack supreme mundane dharma. Further: the desire realm is extremely turbulent, and the formless realm is extremely still — both, for this reason, lack supreme mundane dharma. Further: wherever a stage possesses both an all-pervading wisdom and a path able to cut off the fetters, that stage can possess supreme mundane dharma; the desire realm, though it possesses an all-pervading wisdom, lacks a path able to cut off the fetters, while the formless realm, though it possesses a path able to cut off the fetters, lacks an all-pervading wisdom — and this is why both lack supreme mundane dharma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing symmetry is elegant: the desire realm and the formless realm fail for exactly opposite reasons — one too coarse, agitated, and unstable to ever settle into concentration; the other too subtle, still, and torpid to ever generate the sharp insight a path of seeing requires. Only the form realm, occupying the middle position, balances calm-abiding and insight enough to support both.

7. Two Noble Ones, One Rebirth, Different Fates

Two paired case studies test how supreme mundane dharma's possession tracks rebirth: two practitioners who entered the path relying on different dhyānas, later reborn together in the same higher stage, end up in different relationships to the very same dharma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a good test of just how bound to its own stage supreme mundane dharma really is: even full arhatship, attained by both practitioners alike, does nothing to restore what was already lost the moment one of them was reborn onto a different stage than the one their own entry into the path relied on. What is meant by "having neither"? The answer: whatever supreme mundane dharma is attained by one who enters the certainty of rightness by relying on a concentration with neither — namely, the supreme mundane dharma attained by one who enters the certainty of rightness by relying on the second, third, or fourth dhyāna.

The rival school's six-part characterization — bears a sign, involves conscious effort, not evenly balanced, belongs to an ordinary being, and so on — is worth reading as an entire alternative theory of supreme mundane dharma compressed into a single sentence, one this treatise rejects not by disputing each claim individually but simply by establishing, positively, that the dharma spans all three stages, which the rival's "only the first stage" reading cannot accommodate.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The mapping that emerges is tidy: joy at the first two dhyānas (where the coarser rapture of early concentration is still present), pleasure at the third (where rapture has settled into refined bodily ease), and equanimity at proximate concentration, the intermediate dhyāna, and the fourth dhyāna and above (where feeling itself grows progressively more neutral). Fascicle 4 closes on the same self-exclusion principle used throughout — a faculty cannot be associated with itself, nor with its own two siblings in the same triad — the very same logic that, in Fascicle 3, ruled out supreme mundane dharma belonging to the desire realm in the first place.
