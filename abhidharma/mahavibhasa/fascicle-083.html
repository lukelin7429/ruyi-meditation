Great Compassion, Formless Realms

Fascicle 83 completes the four immeasurables with the distinction between compassion and great compassion, an extraordinary collection of narratives illustrating the Buddha's own universal loving-kindness, and why loving-kindness alone culminates in Śubhakṛtsna while the others culminate in the formless spheres — before opening the four formless attainments and the classic dispute over whether the formless realm possesses form. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 83 complete, 16 sections Source: Taishō T1545, vol. 27, fascicle 83 Topic: 十門納息 → 四無量完結 → 四無色新開 (completing the four immeasurables, opening the four formless attainments), Fetters Aggregate

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 83 completes the four immeasurables (四無量) within the Ten Gates Nyāsa (結蘊第二中十門納息第四之十三) and opens the four formless attainments (四無色) — the dispute between the Vibhajyavādins and the Proponents of Correct Reasoning over whether the formless realm possesses form. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Why the Concentration of Loving-Kindness Cannot Be Harmed

A scripture declares that one who abides in the concentration of loving-kindness cannot be harmed by blade, poison, water, or fire, and will not meet an untimely death. Several masters offer their own explanation: it is a dharma of non-harming in itself; it possesses great majestic power; the gods and good spirits guard whoever cultivates it for another's benefit; or, as the Great Virtuous One holds, the mind of loving-kindness belongs to a superior portion not subject to death at all, and the form realm's own great elements, pervading the practitioner's entire body, render it as firm and dense as stone. A vivid story illustrates even the mere preliminary practice's own protective power: a condemned man, having only begun to cultivate loving-kindness, turns back a king's own thrown spear simply by giving rise to a mind of loving-kindness the instant he sees the king's rage, causing the weapon to fall harmlessly to the ground before it reaches him.

Chinese Source

如契經說。住慈定者。刀毒水火皆不能害。必無災橫而致命終。問何故爾耶。尊者世友作如是說。以慈三摩地是不害法故。復作是說。慈三摩地威勢大故。復作是說。慈三摩地為饒益他。諸天善神皆擁衛故。復作是說。修靜慮者靜慮境界。具神通者神通境界。所有威德不思議故。復作是說。住慈定者起勝分心。非勝分心有死生故。大德說曰。若住慈定。色界大種遍身分生。令所依身堅密如石。故不可害。問悲喜捨定為可害不。若可害者。何故慈定與悲喜捨俱無量攝。而獨慈定不可害耶。若不可害者經何故不說。答應作是說。悲喜捨定亦不可害。問若爾。此經何故不說。答應說而不說者。當知此義有餘。復次既說慈定。應知亦說悲喜捨定種類同故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As a scripture states, one who abides in the concentration of loving-kindness cannot be harmed by blade, poison, water, or fire, and will certainly not meet an untimely death. One asks: for what reason is this so? The Venerable Vasumitra speaks as follows: it is because the concentration of loving-kindness is itself a dharma of non-harming. He speaks, further, as follows: it is because the concentration of loving-kindness possesses great, majestic power. He speaks, further, as follows: it is because the concentration of loving-kindness is cultivated for the benefit of others, and every heaven and every good spirit therefore guards and protects one. He speaks, further, as follows: it is because whoever cultivates dhyāna possesses dhyāna's own domain, and whoever possesses supernormal power possesses supernormal power's own domain, their majestic virtue being beyond ordinary conception. The Great Virtuous One says: whoever abides in the concentration of loving-kindness gives rise to a mind belonging to the superior portion, and it is not a mind of this superior portion that is subject to death and rebirth. He speaks, further, as follows: whoever abides in the concentration of loving-kindness has the great elements of the form realm arise pervading his entire body, causing his own supporting body to become as firm and dense as stone, and it is for this reason that it cannot be harmed. One asks: can the concentrations of compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, too, be harmed, or not? If they can be harmed, why are they gathered together with the concentration of loving-kindness within the very same immeasurables, and yet the concentration of loving-kindness alone cannot be harmed? If they cannot be harmed, why does the scripture not say so? The answer: one should say as follows — the concentrations of compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, too, cannot be harmed. One asks: if so, why does this scripture not say so? The answer: it ought to have said so, and its not doing so should be understood as an incomplete statement here. Further, having already spoken of the concentration of loving-kindness, one should understand that the concentrations of compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, too, have already been spoken of, since they are of the same kind.

復次慈定在初。若說慈定應知已說悲喜捨定。復次住悲等定雖不可害。而出定時身有微苦。慈定不爾是故偏說。復次住悲等定雖不可害而皮有損。慈定不爾是故偏說。復次悲等根本雖不可害。而加行時則可傷害。慈則不爾是故偏說。曾聞有人雖得欲界慈定加行。而犯王法時法司者執送見王。白言。此人犯應死罪。時王乘象欲出城遊。見已遣人撿王法律。知其所犯王應手害。王遂大瞋以矛[矛*(替-曰+貝)]彼。其人見已便起慈心。令所[矛*(替-曰+貝)]矛還趣王所。去王不遠而投於地。王見驚怖問罪人言。汝有何術能為此事。其人答言我無異術。見王瞋故遂起慈心。令惡心者不能為害。

Further, the concentration of loving-kindness comes first — speaking of the concentration of loving-kindness, one should understand that the concentrations of compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, too, have already been spoken of. Further, one who abides in the concentration of compassion and the rest, although he, too, cannot be harmed, still experiences a slight bodily discomfort upon emerging from concentration, unlike the concentration of loving-kindness, and it is for this reason that it alone is singled out. Further, one who abides in the concentration of compassion and the rest, although he, too, cannot be harmed, still suffers some damage to the skin, unlike the concentration of loving-kindness, and it is for this reason that it alone is singled out. Further, the root attainments of compassion and the rest, although they, too, cannot be harmed, may still be injured at the stage of preliminary practice, unlike loving-kindness, and it is for this reason that it alone is singled out. It has been heard that there once was a man who, although he had already attained the desire realm's own preliminary practice for the concentration of loving-kindness, transgressed the king's own law, and the officers of justice, seizing him, brought him before the king, reporting: this man has committed a crime deserving death. At that time the king, riding upon an elephant, was about to leave the city to go on an excursion, and, seeing this, sent someone to examine the king's own law, and, learning what this man had committed, the king himself was to strike him down. The king thereupon flew into a great rage and thrust his spear at him. Seeing this, the man gave rise to a mind of loving-kindness, causing the thrust spear to turn back toward the king himself, and, before it reached the king, it fell to the ground. The king, seeing this, was struck with fear, and asked the condemned man: what art do you possess, that you could accomplish such a thing as this? The man answered: I possess no unusual art whatsoever — seeing the king's own rage, I simply gave rise to a mind of loving-kindness, causing whoever bore an evil intent to be unable to do harm.

王因懺謝遂釋放之。由此故知修慈加行亦不可害。悲等不爾如契經說。修慈斷瞋。修悲斷害。修喜斷不樂。修捨斷貪瞋。問既說慈捨俱對治瞋。所對治瞋有何差別。答慈對治斷命瞋。捨對治捶打瞋。

The king, on account of this, offered his apology and released him. It is by this that it is known that even the preliminary practice for cultivating loving-kindness cannot be harmed, while compassion and the rest are not so, exactly as a scripture states: cultivating loving-kindness cuts off anger; cultivating compassion cuts off harming; cultivating sympathetic joy cuts off displeasure; cultivating equanimity cuts off both craving and anger. One asks: having already said that both loving-kindness and equanimity counteract anger, what distinction is there in the anger each counteracts? The answer: loving-kindness counteracts the anger that would take a life; equanimity counteracts the anger that would strike and beat.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The condemned man's spear-turning is a striking demonstration that this treatise locates the protective power specifically in the loving-kindness itself, not in any accumulated merit or advanced attainment: even a beginner's preliminary practice, invoked in a single moment of genuine peril, proves sufficient — the story's whole dramatic point being that skill and duration of practice matter far less here than the sheer presence of a genuinely loving mind at the critical instant.

2. Can the Immeasurables Cut Off Affliction? Temporary Versus Ultimate

A further scripture distinguishes what each immeasurable specifically counteracts — loving-kindness cutting off anger, compassion cutting off harming, sympathetic joy cutting off displeasure, equanimity cutting off both craving and anger — yet this seems to contradict the Concentration Aggregate's own flat statement that none of the four immeasurables can cut off any fetter whatsoever. The resolution distinguishes temporary cutting-off, which the immeasurables genuinely accomplish, from ultimate cutting-off, which only the noble path itself achieves — and this same distinction, the treatise notes, extends to a whole family of parallel pairs: partial versus complete cutting-off, cutting-off with or without a remaining trace, with or without continued bondage, through suppression versus through uprooting the very root.

Chinese Source

復次慈對治是處瞋。捨對治非處瞋。問無量為能斷煩惱不。若能斷者。定蘊所說當云何通。如說慈悲喜捨皆不能斷諸結。若不能斷者。此經云何通。答應作是說。無量不能斷諸煩惱。問若爾。善通定蘊所說。此經所說當云何通。答斷有二種。一暫時斷。二畢竟斷。依暫時斷此經說能斷。依畢竟斷定蘊說不斷。如是經論二說善通。如暫斷畢竟斷。如是有片斷無片斷。有影斷無影斷。有餘斷無餘斷。有隨縛斷無隨縛斷。有分斷無分斷。制伏斷拔根斷。伏諸纏斷。害隨眠斷。應知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, loving-kindness counteracts anger that arises toward what warrants it; equanimity counteracts anger that arises toward what does not warrant it. One asks: are the immeasurables capable of cutting off affliction, or not? If they are capable, how is a certain statement of the Concentration Aggregate to be reconciled — as it states, loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity are none of them capable of cutting off any fetter whatsoever? If they are not capable, how is this present scripture to be reconciled? The answer: one should say as follows — the immeasurables are not capable of cutting off any affliction whatsoever. One asks: if so, this well reconciles the Concentration Aggregate's own statement — how, then, is this present scripture's own statement to be reconciled? The answer: cutting-off is of two kinds: first, temporary cutting-off; second, ultimate cutting-off. With reference to temporary cutting-off, this scripture speaks of being capable of cutting off; with reference to ultimate cutting-off, the Concentration Aggregate speaks of not cutting off — and, in this way, both the scripture and the treatise are well reconciled. Exactly as with temporary and ultimate cutting-off, so too should partial and non-partial cutting-off, cutting-off with a remaining trace and without a remaining trace, cutting-off with a remainder and without a remainder, cutting-off with continued bondage and without continued bondage, cutting-off of a portion and not of a portion, cutting-off through suppression and cutting-off through uprooting the very root, cutting-off through subduing the entanglement, and cutting-off through harming the latent tendency, be understood in the corresponding manner.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a single technical distinction — temporary versus ultimate cutting-off — can simultaneously reconcile one scripture's claim that the immeasurables do counteract specific afflictions and another treatise's flatly opposite claim that they cut off nothing at all is a small masterclass in this literature's own method: rather than declaring one source mistaken, it locates a genuine ambiguity in the ordinary word "cut off" itself, an ambiguity fine enough that ordinary language never bothered to mark it, yet real enough to carry the entire weight of reconciling two apparently contradictory scriptures.

3. Reconciling Loving-Kindness with the Fruit of Non-Return

A further apparent contradiction arises: how can cultivating the mere concentration of loving-kindness yield the fruit of the non-returner, if the immeasurables cut off no affliction at all? The resolution offers two paths at once. Sometimes scripture simply uses the words "loving-kindness," "compassion," and the rest as alternate names for the noble path itself, exactly as elsewhere the Buddha calls the path a lamp, a boat, or a mountain of rock. And sometimes an ordinary being genuinely does become free of desire's own defilement through cultivating loving-kindness and the rest, only afterward entering the true nature freed from birth and directly realizing whichever fruit his own prior degree of freedom from defilement permits — the fruit of non-return, in such a case, being genuinely owed to the power of the immeasurables he had already cultivated, even though the immeasurables themselves never directly cut off any fetter.

Chinese Source

問若四無量不斷煩惱。餘經所說復云何通。如說苾芻。修慈心定。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: if the four immeasurables do not cut off affliction, how is a further scripture's own statement to be reconciled — as it states, monks, cultivating the concentration of the mind of loving-kindness, if one does not press onward, one abides in the fruit of the non-returner —

若不勝進住不還果。廣說乃至修捨心定。應知亦爾。答彼經說聖道名慈等心定。如諸經中佛於聖道。或說為想。或說為受。或說為思。或說為意。或說為燈。或說為信精進念定慧。或說為船筏。或說為山石。或說為水華。一一引經如餘處說。彼經亦爾。於無漏道說慈等聲。亦不違理。復次為求慈等四種梵住離欲染者。或見異生或是聖者。若是異生先離欲染得慈等定。於後得入正性離生證不還果。若彼先時於欲界染未得全離。後時得入正性離生。證預流果或一來果。彼後證得不還果者是慈等力。若是聖者離欲染時。得慈等定及不還果。依此故說修慈等定得不還果。亦不違理。

and so on, stated at full length, up through, cultivating the concentration of the mind of equanimity, this too should be understood in the corresponding manner. The answer: that scripture speaks of the noble path itself using the name "concentration of the mind of loving-kindness," and the rest, exactly as, in various scriptures, the Buddha speaks of the noble path sometimes as notion, sometimes as feeling, sometimes as volition, sometimes as intention, sometimes as a lamp, sometimes as faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, or wisdom, sometimes as a boat or raft, sometimes as a mountain of rock, sometimes as a flower upon the water — each such instance being cited from scripture, exactly as elsewhere, and that scripture, too, is just the same, speaking of the undefiled path using the words "loving-kindness," and the rest, and this, too, raises no contradiction with principle. Further, as for one who seeks to become free of desire's own defilement by way of the four brahma-abidings of loving-kindness and the rest — such a one may be either an ordinary being or a noble one. If he is an ordinary being, he first becomes free of desire's own defilement, attaining the concentration of loving-kindness and the rest, and only afterward enters the true nature freed from birth, and directly realizes the fruit of the non-returner. If he had not, at that earlier time, already become entirely free of the desire realm's own defilement, and only later entered the true nature freed from birth, he would directly realize instead the fruit of stream-entry or of once-returning — and whoever, among such persons, later directly realizes the fruit of the non-returner does so through the power of loving-kindness and the rest. If he is a noble one, then, at the very time he becomes free of desire's own defilement, he attains both the concentration of loving-kindness and the rest, and the fruit of the non-returner together — and it is on this basis that it is said that cultivating the concentration of loving-kindness and the rest yields the fruit of the non-returner, and this, too, raises no contradiction with principle.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The observation that scripture sometimes calls the noble path by the name "loving-kindness" is a useful piece of interpretive caution this treatise applies liberally elsewhere too: a scriptural passage's own choice of words cannot always be taken as a direct, literal claim about the specific dharma named, since the very same path may be addressed under any number of alternate names depending on which of its own qualities the passage happens to be emphasizing at that moment.

4. Unattractiveness Versus Equanimity: Two Antidotes to Craving

Both the contemplation of unattractiveness and the immeasurable of equanimity are said to cut off craving for desire — so what genuinely distinguishes them? Four parallel distinctions answer this: unattractiveness counteracts craving for sexual union specifically, while equanimity counteracts craving for an objective domain generally; unattractiveness counteracts craving for visible color, equanimity for bodily shape; unattractiveness for subtle touch, equanimity for bearing and countenance; and unattractiveness for outward appearance, equanimity for the sentient being as a whole.

Chinese Source

如契經說。修不淨觀能斷欲貪。修捨無量亦斷欲貪。此二何別。答修不淨觀對治婬欲貪。修捨無量對治境界貪。復次修不淨觀對治顯色貪。修捨無量對治形色貪。復次修不淨觀對治細觸貪。修捨無量對治容儀貪。復次修不淨觀對治形貌貪。修捨無量對治有情貪。是謂差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

As a scripture states, cultivating the contemplation of impurity is capable of cutting off craving for desire; cultivating the immeasurable of equanimity, too, cuts off craving for desire — what distinction is there between these two? The answer: cultivating the contemplation of impurity counteracts craving for sexual union; cultivating the immeasurable of equanimity counteracts craving for an objective domain. Further, cultivating the contemplation of impurity counteracts craving for visible color; cultivating the immeasurable of equanimity counteracts craving for bodily shape. Further, cultivating the contemplation of impurity counteracts craving for subtle touch; cultivating the immeasurable of equanimity counteracts craving for bearing and countenance. Further, cultivating the contemplation of impurity counteracts craving for appearance and form; cultivating the immeasurable of equanimity counteracts craving for the sentient being itself. Such, then, is the distinction.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The pattern running through all four distinctions is that unattractiveness targets something narrower and more visceral — a specific sensory or sexual attraction — while equanimity targets something broader and more relational, the very orientation of craving toward an object or a person as such, suggesting that the two practices are not redundant competitors for the identical craving but complementary tools addressing craving's own different layers.

5. Loving-Kindness and the Limb of Enlightenment

A scripture speaks of cultivating the limb of enlightenment that is mindfulness together with loving-kindness — yet the immeasurables are contaminated while the limbs of enlightenment are undefiled, so how can the two arise together at all? The Venerable Vasumitra resolves this by denying strict simultaneity: the four immeasurables tame and soften the mind, and it is immediately from this softened state that the limbs of enlightenment are drawn forth, which, in turn, immediately draw forth the immeasurables once more — the two interspersed so closely in rapid succession that they are spoken of as arising together, though, in genuine fact, they never arise at the very same instant.

Chinese Source

如契經說。與慈俱修念等覺支。依止離。依止無欲。依止滅。迴向於捨。悲喜捨三說亦如是。問無量有漏。覺支無漏。云何有漏與無漏俱。尊者世友作如是說。由四無量調伏其心。令心質直有所堪能。從此無間引起覺支。覺支無間引起無量。無量覺支相雜而起。

Ru-Yi Translation

As a scripture states, together with loving-kindness one cultivates the limb of enlightenment that is mindfulness, relying upon seclusion, relying upon freedom from desire, relying upon cessation, and turning toward relinquishment — compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity are stated in the corresponding manner. One asks: the immeasurables are contaminated, while the limbs of enlightenment are undefiled — how can the contaminated and the undefiled arise together? The Venerable Vasumitra speaks as follows: it is by means of the four immeasurables that the mind is tamed and subdued, made upright and capable, and it is immediately from this that the limbs of enlightenment are drawn forth and arise; and it is immediately from the limbs of enlightenment that the immeasurables, in turn, are drawn forth and arise — the immeasurables and the limbs of enlightenment thus arising interspersed with one another,

故說為俱而實不並。

and it is for this reason that they are spoken of as arising together, though, in reality, they do not arise simultaneously.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Vasumitra's resolution — that rapid alternation can be mistaken for, and legitimately described as, simultaneity — is a useful general principle for reading this treatise's own scriptural citations: a scripture's claim that two things occur "together" need not always mean strict co-occurrence in a single moment, but may instead describe an alternation fast enough that ordinary experience, and ordinary language, cannot distinguish it from true simultaneity.

6. Which Immeasurable Is Supreme?

Different masters champion different immeasurables as supreme — loving-kindness for its imperviousness to harm, compassion since the Buddha expounds the dharma through great compassion, sympathetic joy for cutting off displeasure, equanimity for cutting off both craving and anger. The Great Virtuous One settles on equanimity for two converging reasons: what it accomplishes, cutting off both craving and anger at once where the others cut off only one, and its own tranquility, turning with no discrimination whatsoever toward any sentient being.

Chinese Source

問四無量中何者最勝。有作是說。慈最為勝。所以者何。不可害故。有餘師說。悲最為勝。所以者何。佛以大悲說正法故。或有說者。喜最為勝。所以者何。斷不樂故。復有說者。捨最為勝。所以者何。斷貪瞋故。大德說曰。由二因緣捨最為勝。一由所作。謂若修捨能斷貪瞋。二由寂靜。謂於有情無分別轉。故捨最勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: among the four immeasurables, which is supreme? There are those who say: loving-kindness is supreme, for what reason is this so? Because it cannot be harmed. Other masters say: compassion is supreme, for what reason is this so? Because the Buddha, through great compassion, expounds the true dharma. There are those who say: sympathetic joy is supreme, for what reason is this so? Because it cuts off displeasure. There are those who say: equanimity is supreme, for what reason is this so? Because it cuts off both craving and anger. The Great Virtuous One says: owing to two causes and conditions, equanimity is supreme — first, owing to what it accomplishes, namely, that cultivating equanimity is capable of cutting off both craving and anger; second, owing to its tranquility, namely, that it turns without any discrimination whatsoever with respect to sentient beings — and it is for this reason that equanimity is supreme.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That equanimity wins this contest by combining two entirely different kinds of excellence — a functional one (cutting off two afflictions rather than one) and a qualitative one (its own undiscriminating tranquility) — rather than simply outperforming the others on a single shared metric, suggests that "supreme" here is not a single ranking at all but a recognition that equanimity alone satisfies two independent standards of excellence simultaneously.

7. Compassion Versus Great Compassion: A Systematic Distinction

Why does the Buddha speak only of "great compassion," never great loving-kindness, great joy, or great equanimity — even though every one of the Buddha's own qualities is, in truth, equally great? The question, the treatise notes, only makes sense to ask of compassion in the first place, since compassion and great compassion possess genuinely distinct own-natures, unlike the others. A systematic contrast follows: compassion takes the wholesome root of non-hatred as its own-nature, great compassion the wholesome root of non-delusion; compassion exists across all four dhyānas, great compassion solely within the fourth; compassion is shared by ordinary beings and nobles alike, great compassion by the Buddha alone; and, most memorably, compassion is merely capable of pitying without being capable of rescuing, exactly as one man standing on a riverbank might wring his hands in anguish at another's drowning without ever entering the water himself, while great compassion, like the second man who dives in to save him, is capable of both pity and genuine rescue.

Chinese Source

問世尊何故但說大悲。不說大慈大喜大捨。答皆應說大。以佛身中一切功德皆是大故。為欲饒益無量有情心所起故。為欲拔濟無量有情心所起故。為欲哀愍無量有情心所起故。於諸有情善心平等相續轉故。然於此中不應為問。所以者何。若悲自性。即是大悲可為此問。然悲大悲自性各別。故不應問。而諸經中亦有處說大慈大喜及大捨言。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: for what reason does the World-Honored One speak only of great compassion, and not of great loving-kindness, great sympathetic joy, or great equanimity? 14. Why Loving-Kindness Culminates in Śubhakṛtsna but the Others in the Lower Formless Spheres

A genuine puzzle closes this run of material: scripture states that cultivating loving-kindness culminates, at its furthest reach, in the Heaven of Universal Purity — reasonable enough, since that heaven is bound to the very same form realm — yet cultivating compassion, joy, and equanimity are said to culminate instead in the lower three formless spheres, which seems impossible, since form-realm karma ought never to draw forth a formless fruit. Several resolutions are canvassed before the strongest two emerge: a resemblance of aspect, since compassion's aspect of suffering resembles the sphere of infinite space's own rejection of every form, joy's aspect of delight resembles infinite consciousness's own delight in consciousness, and equanimity's aspect of setting-aside resembles nothingness's own extensive relinquishment; and an account of arriving at a pleasant abiding, tracing how a practitioner cultivating each immeasurable in turn only finally comes to rest, dissatisfied with each intervening stage, precisely at the ground named as that immeasurable's own culmination. A further motive closes the puzzle: the language of the immeasurables is deliberately applied to the formless realm in order to refute the outsiders' own mistaken claim that the formless attainments constitute genuine liberation, displaying that they merely resemble the immeasurables' own bliss without ever being liberation at all.

15. The Four Formless Attainments: Do They Possess Form? Two Rival Schools

A wholly new topic opens — the four formless attainments — composed to settle a genuine dispute between two schools. The Vibhajyavādins hold that the formless realm still possesses form, appealing to scripture: name-and-form and consciousness are said to mutually condition one another, and since the formless realm indeed possesses consciousness, it ought to possess name-and-form as well; lifespan, warmth, and consciousness are said to be forever inseparable, and since the formless realm possesses lifespan and consciousness, it ought to possess warmth too; and, more searchingly, if the formless realm possessed no form whatsoever, how could form ever arise again when a being, after tens of thousands of aeons entirely without form, dies there and is reborn once more in the form or desire realm? The Proponents of Correct Reasoning hold the opposite, appealing to their own scriptures: the formless realm is said to transcend form itself, entering a dhyāna one observes all five aggregates as a sickness while entering a formless attainment one observes only four, and the formless attainments are explicitly described as tranquil, liberated, and transcending every form entirely — with their own searching counter-difficulty: were the formless realm to still possess form, there could be no dharma of gradual cessation, and, without that, no ultimate cessation, no liberation, and no nirvāṇa at all.

16. Reconciling the Scriptures, and the Final Verdict

The compilers rule decisively for the Proponents of Correct Reasoning: the formless realm possesses no form whatsoever. The Vibhajyavādins' own scriptural citations, they argue, are of provisional meaning, since the Buddha's teaching is always given with reference to some specific scope — sometimes the desire realm alone, sometimes the form realm alone, sometimes both together — and the passages cited about name-and-form and about lifespan-warmth-consciousness were never meant to describe the formless realm at all, but only the desire and form realms, exactly as a further scriptural claim that "every being depends on food" is never taken to mean the upper two realms rely on solid food. The Vibhajyavādins' counter-attempt to read "transcends form" as merely transcending coarse form while still permitting subtle form is rejected outright: the form realm is never said to transcend form itself, only desire, and yet the form realm still lacks even subtle desire — so by the identical logic, the formless realm, explicitly said to transcend form, ought to lack even subtle form entirely. The treatise closes with an unusually blunt verdict, condemning the Vibhajyavādins' own position as nothing more than "the fruit of ignorance, the fruit of darkness, the fruit of not-knowing, and the fruit of failing to exert diligent effort."

One asks: how do the Vibhajyavādins explain and reconcile the scriptures cited by the Proponents of Correct Reasoning? They speak as follows: the scriptures they cite are of provisional, non-definitive meaning, being a provisional designation with some further intent behind it, for what reason is this so? As for that scripture's own statement that the formless realm transcends form: this speaks of transcending coarse form, not of lacking subtle form — exactly as it is said that the form realm transcends the desires, and yet the form realm is still admitted to possess form; so too, though it is said that the formless realm transcends form, the formless realm ought likewise to be admitted to possess form. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing condemnation of the Vibhajyavādins in such unusually harsh terms — fruit of ignorance, of darkness, of not-knowing — is a rare departure from this treatise's ordinarily measured, scholastic tone, and its very sharpness signals how much doctrinal weight this particular question carried: a formless realm that still secretly harbored form would have undermined the treatise's entire account of gradual, ultimate cessation, making the dispute far more consequential than a mere technical disagreement about cosmology.
