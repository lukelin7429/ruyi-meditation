The Seed in Good Soil

Fascicle 19 continues the Knowledge Nyāsa's eleventh part (智納息第二之十一), first closing out the universally-pervasive-cause — why wrong view never spans all three realms in a single moment, and an elaborate nine-point account of what really separates a not-yet-cut-off cause from an already-cut-off one — before opening the fifth of the six causes, the ripening-cause, refuting five rival schools and working out, in exhaustive detail, exactly what a meditative attainment, an act of karma, or a mere acquisition each ripen into. The fascicle closes with the treatise's most memorable extended metaphor: the seed in good soil that explains why only unwholesome and contaminated-wholesome dharmas ever bear fruit at all. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. One Realm at a Time: Why Wrong View Never Spans All Three Realms at Once

Fascicle 19 opens mid-argument: the desire realm's own wrong view, abandoned by seeing suffering and the origin, can take the suffering and origin of all three realms as its object, but never all three at once — only ever one realm per moment, since the desire realm alone is this view's own dwelling, its own place of latent increase, and the sole possessor of its equal-outflow, ripening, and universally-pervasive fruits.

Chinese Source

欲界見苦集所斷邪見。能緣三界苦集而非一時。謂異剎那緣欲界。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This passage establishes a genuinely fine-grained rule that will recur throughout the fascicle: an affliction's object-range and its capacity to actually increase from taking that object are two separate questions, and a single moment of consciousness can only coherently combine both with respect to one realm at a time — laying the technical groundwork for the extended counterfactual thought experiment that follows immediately after.

2. The Counterfactual: What If It Could Take All Three at Once?

The treatise runs a rigorous reductio ad absurdum: suppose this wrong view really could take all three realms' suffering and origin as its object within a single moment — would it then increase uniformly, or fail to increase uniformly, across all three? Either answer collapses into absurdity, either confusing the very boundaries between the realms or violating the basic rule that any affliction taking its own stage's dharma as object must possess both binding by object and binding by association together. The same one-realm-per-moment rule is then extended systematically up through the first dhyāna, the higher dhyānas, the sphere of nothingness, and finally the peak of existence, which reaches no further than itself.

3. Why Cessation and the Path Reach Only Their Own Stage

Two narrower afflictions are explained by contrast: the wrong view abandoned by seeing cessation reaches only its own stage's own cessation, since the unconditioned truth of cessation admits no cross-stage mutual drawing-along the way conditioned suffering and origin do; and the wrong view abandoned by seeing the path reaches only its own stage's own antidote, for the same reason, though the Venerable Pārśva offers a second, complementary explanation for both: each of these two views takes as its object precisely whatever craving and self-view cling to, or precisely the cessation or antidote of that very thing — and such objects, by their nature, never cross realms.

4. Six Grounds or Nine? A Debate Over the Path's Own Reach

A further refinement narrows the wrong view abandoned by seeing the path even more precisely: does it take only the not-yet-arrived stage's own dharma-knowledge class of the path as its object, since that alone directly antidotes it, or does it reach across all six grounds sharing the same category? The treatise sides with the broader six-ground reading, then extends the same logic upward: every higher stage's own view abandoned by seeing the path can take the subsequent-knowledge class of the path belonging to nine stages as its object, since the noble path, unlike cessation itself, genuinely does serve as mutual cause across many stages at once.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction drawn here between cessation (which never mutually causes across stages) and the noble path (which routinely does) explains something that could otherwise look like an inconsistency: the very same wrong view that stays rigidly confined to its own stage when taking cessation as object suddenly reaches across six or nine stages when taking the path as object instead — not because the view itself changed its nature, but because the two objects it can potentially seize on behave completely differently under the rules of causation already established many fascicles earlier.

5. Do All Defiled Dharmas Take What Is Abandoned By Seeing as Their Cause?

A brief clarification on naming — a universally-pervasive latent tendency earns its name from its kind and continuum considered as a whole, not from any single moment's literal omnipresence — opens onto the fascicle's next major debate: do all defiled dharmas, without exception, take what is abandoned by seeing as their cause? The stakes are immediately sharpened by a genuine puzzle: if the not-yet-cut-off and the already-cut-off both serve equally as cause, what real difference remains between them — and why do certain afflictions abandoned by cultivation, like craving-devoid-of-love and extreme wrathful fury, never arise again for a noble one, while others do?

6. Nine Ways to Tell Not-Yet-Cut-Off From Already-Cut-Off

Faced with a genuine scriptural tension — the Vijñānakāya seeming to allow a purely unwholesome-caused defiled volition, the Prakaraṇapāda insisting every defiled dharma is caused by what is abandoned by seeing — the treatise resolves the puzzle by affirming the broader claim and then cataloguing, point by point, exactly what actually changes once a cause moves from not-yet-cut-off to already-cut-off: it is destroyed by its antidote, no longer obstructs the path, no longer produces a smoke-and-flame acquisition, no longer performs reproachable deeds, no longer accomplishes its own task, no longer functions as homogeneous-cause or universally-pervasive-cause, no longer produces equal-outflow or ripening fruit, and no longer takes up or gives its own fruit.

12. The Ripening-Cause Opens: Five Rival Schools Refuted at Once

The fascicle's second half opens the fifth of the six causes, the ripening-cause, explicitly to refute five distinct rival positions in a single sweep: the Dārṣṭāntikas, who locate ripening-cause and fruit in volition and feeling alone; the Mahāsāṃghikas, who confine both to mind and its mental factors; a third position excluding only the dissociated formations; a fourth holding that a ripening-cause must relinquish its own substance once the past absorbs it; the Kāśyapīyas, who compare a ripening-cause to a seed destroyed the instant its sprout appears; and the outside teachers, who deny any pleasure-or-suffering fruit to good and evil deeds at all.

16. Provisional Versus Definitive: Five Ways This Treatise Refines the Prakaraṇapāda

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This five-way comparison functions as a small manual for reading Abhidharma texts against one another correctly: two treatises stating an apparently different definition for the very same technical term need not actually disagree at all, so long as one statement can be shown to be a narrower, more pedagogically convenient special case nested inside the other's fuller scope — exactly the same provisional-versus-definitive move this treatise has already used more than once to harmonize seemingly conflicting scriptural citations.

18. Projecting and Fulfilling Karma, Three Timeframes, and What 'Ripening' Really Means

Two remaining threads close out the ripening-cause's technical apparatus: whether the karma that projects a whole rebirth-destiny is performed before or after the karma that merely fills in its details — unresolved, since the bodhisattva's own three-incalculable-aeon path reverses the ordinary painter's-outline sequence — and the three timeframes in which karma's fruit ripens, this life, the next, or some later life still. A remarkable lexical survey then catalogues six entirely different senses the word “ripening” carries across scripture, from dependent origination's own craving to dream-content, harvest omens, and an address to Brahmā himself, before settling on the two technical senses that matter here: same-kind ripening (the equal-outflow fruit) and different-kind ripening (true vipāka).

19. Why Is a Bad Rebirth Called 'Unripened'? Three Explanations

A genuinely charming semantic puzzle closes the ripening-fruit discussion proper: if ripening fruit is, by definition, whatever has properly matured from a dissimilar cause, why is the bad destiny of rebirth called “unripened” rather than ripened? Three answers converge: it is spoken of this way out of sheer lowliness, the way a shabby village might still be called “no village at all”; it is over-ripened, like a careless potter's kiln-ruined pots, given how excessive its suffering fruit actually is; and its scant wholesome fruit, though genuinely present even for animals and hungry ghosts, is so meager it is spoken of as though entirely absent, the way a nearly-dry river is still called “waterless.”

20. The Seed in Good Soil: Why Only the Unwholesome and Wholesome-Contaminated Bear Fruit

The fascicle closes with its most memorable extended metaphor: unwholesome and contaminated-wholesome dharmas are like a firm seed placed in good soil, irrigated by craving and covered by the manure of the remaining fetters, which duly sprouts into existence; uncontaminated dharmas are like that same firm seed left dry in a storehouse, lacking the water and manure existence requires, and so never sprout at all; and undefined dharmas are like a rotten seed that, even placed in the identical good soil and given the identical water and manure, still fails to sprout, its own inner vitality simply too feeble. A cascade of further arguments — the noble path cannot perpetuate the very existence it exists to end, an uncontaminated fruit would have nowhere in any of the three realms to be received, and an infinite regress of self-generating fruits would make liberation impossible — seals why neither the uncontaminated nor the undefined ever bears a ripening fruit.

Further, whatever dharma causes every realm of existence and every destiny to increase gradually possesses a ripening fruit; the uncontaminated causes every realm of existence and every destiny to diminish gradually, and so it has no ripening fruit. Further, whatever dharma is itself an activity productive of the suffering of birth, aging, sickness, and death within the mundane world of existence possesses a ripening fruit; the uncontaminated is itself an activity that brings the suffering of birth, aging, sickness, and death within the mundane world of existence to cessation, and so it has no ripening fruit. Further, whatever dharma is the basis of identity-view, the basis of inverted views, the basis of craving and attachment, the basis of latent tendency, tainted, poisoned, defiled, and turbid, falling under the truths of suffering and the origin, falling into the three states of existence, possesses a ripening fruit; uncontaminated dharmas are unlike all of this, and so they have no ripening fruit. Further, were an uncontaminated dharma to possess a ripening fruit, this would then amount to a superior cause producing an inferior fruit, since the cause would be an uncontaminated wholesome conditioned dharma while the fruit would be a contaminated, undefined dharma. Further, were an uncontaminated dharma to possess a ripening fruit, this would mean that the noble path itself perpetuates the continuity of existence — and for the noble path to perpetuate existence would directly contradict all reason. Further, were an uncontaminated dharma to possess a ripening fruit, in which realm, exactly, would this fruit be received?

若在欲界則不應理。無漏法非欲界繫故。如色無色界業。若在色界亦不應理。無漏法非色界繫故。如欲無色界業。若在無色界亦不應理。無漏法非無色界繫故。如欲色界業。若在三界外亦不應理。以三界外無別處故。復次無漏聖道對治異熟及異熟因。若復能感異熟果者。復須對治對治此者。是無漏故。復感異熟為對治彼復修聖道。即彼聖道復感異熟。如是展轉便為無窮。是則應無解脫出離勿有此過。故無漏法無異熟果。復次若無漏法感異熟者。則應畢竟不得涅槃。聖者不應精勤修習。是招生死輪轉法故。由此無漏無異熟果。問復何緣故諸無記法無異熟果。答非田非器。乃至廣說。復次若無記法有異熟果。此異熟果為是無記為善不善。若是無記。何故名異熟非異類熟故。若善不善亦非異熟。以異熟果是無記故。復次若無記法有異熟者。此異熟果是無記故應有異熟。即彼異熟復應能感餘異熟果。如是展轉便為無窮。是則應無解脫出離。勿有此過故無記法無異熟果。由如是等種種因緣。唯諸不善善有漏法。是異熟因。

Were it received within the desire realm, this would be improper, since the uncontaminated is not bound to the desire realm, just as form-realm or formless-realm karma is not received within the desire realm. Were it received within the form realm, this too would be improper, since the uncontaminated is not bound to the form realm, just as desire-realm or formless-realm karma is not received within the form realm. Were it received within the formless realm, this too would be improper, since the uncontaminated is not bound to the formless realm, just as desire-realm or form-realm karma is not received within the formless realm. Were it received somewhere outside the three realms altogether, this too would be improper, since no place exists at all outside the three realms. Further, the uncontaminated noble path serves as antidote to ripening and to the ripening-cause itself; were this path also able to bring about a ripening fruit of its own, a further antidote would then be required to counter that very fruit — and this further antidote, being itself uncontaminated, would again bring about yet another ripening fruit needing yet a further antidote to counter it, and so on and on without end, meaning that there could never be any true liberation or release. Lest such a fault follow, therefore, the uncontaminated has no ripening fruit at all. Further, were an uncontaminated dharma to bring about a ripening fruit, this would mean that one could never, in the end, attain nirvāṇa at all, and no noble one ought ever to strive with diligence to cultivate it, since it would only be a dharma that turns the wheel of birth and death onward — and it is on this account that the uncontaminated has no ripening fruit. One asks, this further question arising from the very discussion at hand: why is it that undefined dharmas have no ripening fruit at all? The answer: because they are neither a fit field nor a fit vessel — and so on, as stated at length. Further, were an undefined dharma to possess a ripening fruit, that fruit would itself have to be either undefined, or wholesome, or unwholesome. Were it undefined, why, then, should it be called a "ripening" fruit at all, since "ripening" specifically means maturing into something of a different moral kind, not maturing into something of the very same kind? Were it wholesome or unwholesome, this too would be improper, since a ripening fruit must always itself be of an undefined moral nature. Further, were an undefined dharma to possess a ripening fruit, then, since that fruit would itself have to be undefined, it would in turn be able to bring about yet a further ripening fruit of its own, and this would go on indefinitely without end, meaning that there could never be any true liberation or release. Lest such a fault follow, therefore, undefined dharmas have no ripening fruit at all. It is through all of these various causes and conditions, taken together, that only unwholesome dharmas and contaminated-wholesome dharmas serve as the ripening-cause.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The seed-and-soil metaphor does more explanatory work than a simple binary of “can ripen” versus “cannot” would allow, precisely because it separates three genuinely distinct failure modes into three distinct kinds of seed: uncontaminated dharmas fail for want of external conditions despite an intact nature, undefined dharmas fail for want of an intact nature despite present conditions, and only the unwholesome and contaminated-wholesome possess both at once — and the closing infinite-regress argument, that a ripening fruit capable of ripening again would make liberation permanently impossible, gives this entire six-causes investigation, opened all the way back in fascicle 16, a fittingly soteriological close: even Abhidharma's driest causal taxonomy ultimately answers to the single overriding question of what does, and does not, keep a being bound to saṃsāra.
