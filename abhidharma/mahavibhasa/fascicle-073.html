Twelve Sense-Fields, and What Is "The All"

Fascicle 73 completes the exhaustive purification ledger for ordinary persons and noble ones across every dhyāna, then turns to an entirely new topic: the twelve sense-fields, opened by the brahmin Janapada's question to the Buddha about "the all." A rich sequence of etymological glosses, a detailed defense of why only the color-field bears the name "form" and only the dharma-field bears the name "dharma," and six converging reasons for the sense-fields' own fixed order follow. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 73 continues the Ten Gates Nyāsa (結蘊第二中十門納息第四之三), completing its opening chapter on the eighteen elements before turning to the second matter of its own forty-two chapters, the twelve sense-fields (十二處). Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Completing the Purification Ledger: Ordinary Persons Born Into Each Dhyāna

Opening part three of the Ten Gates Nyāsa, the treatise finishes the exhaustive ledger begun in the previous fascicle — how many kinds of eye-consciousness and discriminating mind-consciousness arise at every stage of purification through the fourth dhyāna — before restarting the same method for ordinary persons born directly into the first, second, third, and fourth dhyānas rather than the desire realm.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the treatise restarts its entire numeric method from scratch for beings born directly into a dhyāna, rather than simply noting that the desire-realm case generalizes, reveals how seriously this literature takes the difference between attaining a level through cultivation and simply being born into it — the two paths are never assumed to yield identical ledgers merely because they arrive at the same nominal stage.

2. The Noble Ones' Own Ledger: In the Desire Realm

The identical ledger is now run for noble ones rather than ordinary persons, beginning with one not yet free of the desire realm's own defilement and tracking every subsequent stage of purification up through freedom from the second dhyāna's own defilement, with the same fork at each stage between one still prone to regression and one no longer so.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Running the same purification ledger twice — once for ordinary persons, once for noble ones — and finding it unfolds identically in its own structure each time is itself a quiet confirmation that this ledger tracks something about consciousness and defilement that holds independently of whether the practitioner involved has ever attained any genuine insight at all.

4. Why the Higher Three Dhyānas Have No Eye-Consciousness of Their Own

A structural note explains the underlying mechanics: a same-ground eye-consciousness taking a lower ground's own color as object admits of two kinds, excluding the defiled; a wholesome or defiled eye-consciousness arises only in its own birth-ground, while a neutral one behaves the same way, and only the discriminating mind-consciousness that follows can range freely across grounds, and even then according to its own rules. This raises a genuine puzzle: how can one born in the second, third, or fourth dhyāna still manifest a first-dhyāna eye-consciousness at all? The Dārṣṭāntikas propose that the higher dhyānas simply possess their own dormant sense-consciousnesses, awakened by skillful means — flatly rejected by the compilers, who insist the higher three dhyānas possess no sense-consciousness whatsoever, since these require investigation and reflection, which only the first dhyāna retains.

8. Why Twelve Sense-Fields Is the Supreme Teaching

The treatise clarifies that the Buddha's exclusive claim was never meant to deny that eighteen elements, five aggregates, or other classificatory schemes exist — only that the twelve sense-fields represent his own most excellent teaching, striking a middle path other schemes cannot match: the eighteen elements, though comprehensive, are too broad and unwieldy to retain, while the five aggregates, though brief, are both hard to fully grasp and fail to gather in the three unconditioned dharmas that the aggregates alone omit.

15. Seven More Reasons the Dharma-Field Alone Bears This Name

Further reasons accumulate: the arising-characteristic of every conditioned dharma, and the four characteristics of the conditioned that seal and distinguish it from the unconditioned, are gathered solely here; the name-body, phrase-body, and syllable-body that make every dharma's own nature easy to understand belong here alone; this field lets every dharma pass freely through it exactly as a window lets wind pass, generating every conditioned dharma and realizing the unconditioned alike. The liberation-gate of emptiness, which correctly realizes that every dharma is without self, is gathered here too — raising a pointed objection: since the false view of self is likewise gathered here, why is this field not instead named the "self-field"? The answer distinguishes a false, empty grasping that fails to accord with reality from a liberation-gate that genuinely realizes it, awarding the name to whichever one actually gets things right. The permanent, indestructible cessation-through-discernment that is nirvāṇa, and the very wisdom capable of discriminating each dharma's own and shared characteristics without excess or deficiency, both belong here as well — as does this field's own comprehensive gathering of form and formless, associated and dissociated, conditioned and unconditioned dharmas together, and its unique status as the one sense-field that stands opposite mind itself rather than any single, narrow sense.

16. The Order of the Twelve Sense-Fields: Six Reasons

Closing the fascicle, the treatise asks why the inner sense-fields are always taught before the outer, and why each group proceeds in its own particular order — eye before ear before nose before tongue before body before mind, and color before sound before smell before taste before touch before dharma. Six independent principles converge on the identical sequence: the order in which the six consciousnesses take basis and then object; ordinary grammatical convention; the convenience of speaker, listener, and one who upholds the teaching; a scale running from coarse to subtle; a scale running from fixed to indeterminate objects (the first five always taking a present object, the sixth ranging across all three times and the unconditioned); and, finally, the plain physical fact of where each organ sits in the body, from the eye at the very top down to the body itself mostly below, with mind, having no location at all, named last — echoed, in closing, by the ordinary etiquette of two people meeting: first sight, then speech, then the offering of incense and flowers, then food, then bedding, each stage bringing the two travelers closer to a genuine mutual understanding.
