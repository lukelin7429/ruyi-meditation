Eighteen Ways a Mind Approaches Its Object

Fascicle 139 builds a nested classification of feeling into two, three, four, five, six, eighteen, thirty-six, and one hundred and eight kinds, then undertakes a rich study of the eighteen mental-approaches — their etymology, scope, and an exhaustive ledger of who possesses how many at every stage of the path — closing with the doubled thirty-six “verses of the Teacher.” This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. How Many Kinds of Feeling? A Nested System of Mutual Inclusion

Fascicle 139 opens with a sweeping reclassification of feeling itself — refuting both the view that feeling is merely a passing phase of mind with no distinct substance, and the view that only painful feeling genuinely exists. What follows is an intricate nested taxonomy: feeling sorted into two kinds (bodily and mental), three (pleasant, painful, neutral), four (bound to each of the three realms, or unbound), five (the five faculties), six (by sense-door), eighteen (the mental-approaches), thirty-six, and a full hundred and eight — with each pair of classifications carefully shown to subsume the other only partially, "each according to its own particular case."

The recurring phrase "each according to its own particular case," used to describe how two classification systems overlap without either fully containing the other, is a small but important piece of logical honesty: rather than forcing every scheme into a clean hierarchy, the treatise is willing to say that two ways of counting the same phenomenon simply carve it up differently, each valid on its own terms.

2. Why the Pain-Faculty and the Contaminated Pleasure-Faculty Are Never a 'Mental-Approach'

A precise technical exclusion follows: neither the pain-faculty nor the desire realm's own contaminated pleasure-faculty ever counts as a "mental-approach," since both belong exclusively to the five sense-consciousnesses, while every mental-approach belongs to mental-consciousness alone. A cascade of further distinctions sharpens the boundary — the pain-faculty takes only specific characteristics as its object, only the present moment, and only a single engagement, while a mental-approach takes both specific and general characteristics, all three times, and repeated, deliberative engagement — with uncontaminated feeling excluded on entirely different grounds, for lacking every mark that would let it sustain or entrench existence.

3. Extending the Ledger to Thirty-Six and a Hundred-and-Eight Kinds of Feeling

The classification expands further still: thirty-six kinds of feeling, doubling the eighteen mental-approaches by whether each is "dependent on attachment" or "dependent on renunciation," and a hundred and eight, tripling the thirty-six across the three times — with the same asymmetric subsumption pattern holding throughout, the smaller, twofold classification always subsuming the larger without being subsumed in return, since the contaminated pleasure- and pain-faculties, along with uncontaminated feeling, never fit inside the mental-approach framework at all.

5. Reconciling the Largest Numbers

The classification reaches its widest span as the fourfold, fivefold, and sixfold schemes are checked against the eighteenfold, thirty-sixfold, and hundred-and-eightfold classifications in turn — each time confirmed to follow the identical pattern already established, the smaller realm- or faculty-based scheme subsuming the larger mental-approach-based one, but never the reverse, since the same handful of contaminated and uncontaminated exceptions persist at every scale.

7. What Is a 'Mental-Approach,' and Why Does It Live Only in the Mind?

A rich definitional inquiry finally names the eighteen mental-approaches (manaḥ-upavicāra) for what they are: joy, displeasure, and equanimity, each arising by taking any of the six sense-objects, established jointly through association, self-nature, and object together. Their etymology traces to mind's own "proximate" relation to an object, moving repeatedly and sharply within it — likened, memorably, to a sharp, perceptive wife reading her husband's moods — and, crucially, confined entirely to mental-consciousness, never the five sense-consciousnesses, even though the sūtra's own language of "having seen form with the eye" merely describes the pathway by which such approaches are drawn forth, not their actual location.

9. Is Anything Ever Truly, Objectively Pleasant?

A genuinely searching question asks whether any object is ever fixed, in itself, as definitely conducive to joy, displeasure, or equanimity — answered firmly in the negative when the object is considered on its own, and in the affirmative only when considered in relation to a particular continuum of experience, since the very same form may please at one moment and displease at another, or please one observer while displeasing another entirely. 19. The Full Ledger, and the Sūtra's Own Sequence for Cutting Every Last One Off

The fascicle closes with the complete biographical ledger for all thirty-six categories, tracked through every stage of the path exactly as done earlier for the eighteen, before turning to the sūtra's own precise account of how they are progressively cut off: renunciation-based joy first displaces attachment-based joy, and so on through displeasure and equanimity in turn; renunciation-based equanimity then displaces renunciation-based joy at the moment of becoming free from the desire realm's own defilement; a single, unified equanimity-basis then displaces every varied equanimity-basis at freedom from the fourth dhyāna's own defilement; and, at the very last, what is "not of that same kind of nature" — the uncontaminated path itself — displaces even that single equanimity-basis, marking freedom from the very subtlest defilement of all, the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception.
