Without Reflection

Fascicle 54 opens the seven persons on the path — a faith-follower who, told to farm or trade or ordain, never pauses to ask whether the instruction suits him, set against a dharma-follower who examines everything first, though the two possess identical measures of faith and wisdom underneath. The treatise offers fourteen independent ways of telling them apart, works out why one resolved through faith and one who has attained through view keep separate names purely for clarity's sake, and closes with a dizzying count of how many kinds of faith-follower truly exist and an exhaustive ledger tracking exactly which afflictions each representative person still carries at every stage of realization. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 54 continues the Unwholesome Nyāsa's ninth part (結蘊第二中不善納息第一之九), opening the seven persons on the path — the faith-follower, the dharma-follower, one resolved through faith, one who has attained through view, the body-witness, the wisdom-liberated, and the both-liberated — then closing with an exhaustive ledger of which afflictions each of five representative persons still possesses. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Five Persons or Seven, and the Faith-Follower Defined

Fascicle 54 opens with a question of scope: why does this Fetters Aggregate classify practitioners into only five persons, while the later Wisdom and Concentration Aggregates use seven, adding the wisdom-liberated and the both-liberated? The treatise's answer turns on what each discourse takes as its own organizing gateway — affliction here, wisdom and concentration there. It then defines the faith-follower proper: a person of abundant faith and affection who takes no pleasure in reflection, who, upon hearing the Buddha teach impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self, simply resolves to practice as instructed, all the way through the fifteen instants of the path of seeing.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The scope-of-discourse explanation is a small but telling piece of textual self-awareness: the treatise isn't claiming the wisdom-liberated and both-liberated don't exist within the Fetters Aggregate's own framework, only that they fall outside what this particular discourse's own chapter-heading and gateway were built to address — a reminder that absence from one systematic accounting is a fact about the accounting's own scope, not about the world it describes.

2. Resolved Through Faith, Attained Through View, and the Body-Witness

As the faith-follower attains the subsequent-class cognition regarding the path, he relinquishes that name and becomes one resolved through faith — a change the treatise insists on describing with precision: he relinquishes a name and attains a name, relinquishes a path and attains a path. The dharma-follower undergoes the same transition into one who has attained through view. Both may, in turn, become the body-witness, upon personally experiencing the eight liberations in the body itself — though Kashmiri and foreign masters disagree over whether this final transition involves relinquishing an actual path, or merely a name.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The relinquish-a-name-versus-relinquish-a-path distinction, applied consistently across every one of these transitions, is doing careful metaphysical bookkeeping: it separates a change in how a person is classified from a change in what that person has actually accomplished, and the disagreement over the body-witness's own transition — is anything real relinquished, or merely a label — shows the tradition was alert to exactly the kind of confusion where renaming something gets mistaken for transforming it.

3. Wisdom-Liberated and Both-Liberated, and the Bodhisattva's Own Case

The wisdom-liberated exhausts every contaminant through wisdom alone, without ever personally experiencing the eight liberations in the body; the both-liberated achieves both together, whichever order they arrive in — and the treatise works out each possible order in careful detail, including the singular case of a bodhisattva attaining supreme awakening, whose own transition into both-liberated the Kashmiri masters insist must occur specifically at the moment of cessation-cognition, since a bodhisattva's own path of training never generates cessation-concentration beforehand.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the treatise pauses to work out the bodhisattva's own singular case in such technical detail — respecting the general framework's own rules rather than exempting so extraordinary a figure from them — reveals something about this tradition's own commitment to systematic consistency: even the path to buddhahood itself gets slotted into the very same relinquish-and-attain machinery built to classify ordinary monks, rather than being granted some separate, unexaminable category of its own.

4. Why Called Both-Liberated, and the Farmer Who Never Reflects

The treatise resolves a dispute over whether the both-liberated's own liberation from the obstruction-to-liberation involves a contaminated mind, an uncontaminated one, or both — settling on both, since liberation itself has two aspects, functioning in the world and obtaining within the body, and only cessation-concentration completes the second. It then turns to etymology proper, opening with an extended, almost novelistic portrait of the faith-follower: a person who, told to farm, simply farms; told to trade, simply trades; told to ordain, simply ordains; told to recite scripture, simply recites — at every turn accepting instruction without ever pausing to ask whether it suits him.

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: why is he named both-liberated? The answer: obstruction is of two parts — first, the obstruction of affliction; second, the obstruction to liberation. Since his mind is liberated from both of these two parts of obstruction, he is named both-liberated. One asks: should one first attain the arhat's fruit, and only afterward attain cessation-concentration, with respect to the obstruction to liberation, by what kind of mind is he liberated — a contaminated mind, or an uncontaminated one? There are those who say: a contaminated mind, since, by means of an uncontaminated mind, he has already become liberated at the very moment of cessation-cognition. The verdict states: it should rather be said that he becomes liberated by both a contaminated and an uncontaminated mind — for what reason is this so? 5. The Dharma-Follower Who Always Reflects

7. Why “Resolved Through Faith” and “Attained Through View” Keep Separate Names

One resolved through faith earns his name because faith, as his own forerunner, liberates his mind from the three fetters; one who has attained through view earns his name for the identical reason, with view standing in faith's place. This symmetry raises an obvious question — shouldn't each also be nameable by the other's term? The treatise's answer is refreshingly candid: yes, in principle, but distinct names through distinct gateways serve a pedagogical purpose the wise can delight in, and using two different names for these two persons keeps them from ever being confused with one another.

The same exhaustive ledger runs twice more: one resolved through faith has already relinquished the three fetters entirely, since these belong solely to what is abandoned by seeing, and his own remaining possessions track only what belongs to cultivation; the body-witness, having necessarily already become free of every affliction abandoned by seeing across all three realms, possesses even less. The fascicle closes with three sharp questions probing the outer edges of this whole system — is there a noble one who possesses every one of the ninety-eight latent tendencies, or who has cut off eighty-eight yet not attained any fruit, or who has cut off all ninety-eight yet remains short of arhatship — the last firmly rejected, since the ninety-eight are established with reference to realm, not ground.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing rejection is a precise piece of conceptual policing: the proposed scenario sounds superficially coherent — someone who has cut off every latent tendency in every realm yet remains short of arhatship — but the treatise catches that it silently conflates two different classification systems, realm-based and ground-based, that were never meant to be substituted for one another, and the whole apparent puzzle dissolves the moment that category error is named.
