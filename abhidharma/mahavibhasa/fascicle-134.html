Space-Dwelling Heavens and a New Chapter of Vision

Fascicle 134 completes the great cosmology — the space-dwelling heavens' cloud foundations, the repopulation of a newly-formed world, and the three minor calamities of sword, plague, and famine, each with its own guaranteed antidote — before opening an entirely new chapter, the Full-Vision Nyāsa, on exactly which great elements produce a disciple's bodily and verbal karma at every stage of the path. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. Fascicle 134 completes the Condition Nyāsa (緣納息第二之四) and opens the Full-Vision Nyāsa (具見納息第三之一). Terms established across fascicles 93–133 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The Space-Dwelling Heavens' Cloud Foundations, and Why the Fourth Dhyāna Is Not Truly Eternal

Fascicle 134 opens by finishing the cosmology begun earlier: the ground-dwelling vessel-world complete, the treatise turns to the heavens that float in open space, from Yāma up through Akaniṣṭha, whose palaces rest not on any solid ground but on dense clouds gathered within space itself, growing wider stage by stage until the fourth dhyāna's own cloud-foundation is said to be boundless — a boundlessness the treatise immediately warns is dangerous, since it makes the mistaken view of a permanent self especially hard to dislodge for anyone who dwells there.

Chinese Source

大種蘊第五中緣納息第二之四已說成立風水金輪諸海山洲地居器已。次辨成立空居諸天大梵天等所居宮地。然彼宮殿。有說。依空。有說。空中密雲彌布如地。為彼宮殿所依。外器世間。至色究竟。上無色故不可施設。問從夜摩天至色究竟。所依雲地其量如何。有說。從夜摩至他化自在雲地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth Aggregate, the Great Elements: the Condition Nyāsa, Chapter Two, Part Four. Having already explained the formation of the wind-wheel, water-wheel, golden wheel, the various seas, mountains, and continents, and the ground-dwelling vessel-world, we should next explain the formation of the space-dwelling heavens — the Great Brahma and the rest — and the ground upon which their palaces rest. As to these palaces: some say they rest directly upon space itself; others say that dense clouds gather within space, forming a ground-like foundation for these palaces to rest upon. The external vessel-world extends up through Akaniṣṭha, above which there is no form at all, and hence nothing further can be established. Question: from the Yāma heaven up through Akaniṣṭha, what is the measure of this cloud-ground foundation? Some say: from Yāma up through the Paranirmita-vaśavartin heaven, the cloud-ground

皆等妙高頂量色界雲地下狹上廣。謂初二三四靜慮地。如次等彼四洲。小千中千大千。諸世界量。有餘師說。夜摩天宮雲地倍於妙高山頂。乃至他化自在天宮雲地。望前展轉相倍。初二三定如次。等於小千中千大千界量。第四靜慮其量無邊。由此若依第四靜慮。起有身見極難除斷。以執無邊地為我故。問第四靜慮地。若無邊災所不及寧非常住。答剎那無常故無此失。有說。第四靜慮地中。宮殿所依俱無常定。謂彼宮池隨彼諸天生時死時俱起沒故。此說非理。

— is uniformly equal in measure to Mount Sumeru's own summit; while the form realm's own cloud-ground is narrower below and broader above — that is, the cloud-grounds of the first, second, third, and fourth dhyānas correspond, in order, to the measure of the four continents, a small thousand-world-system, a middling thousand-world-system, and a great thousand-world-system respectively. The fourth dhyāna's own measure is boundless — and it is on this account that, relying on the fourth dhyāna, the view of a real self becomes exceedingly difficult to cut off, since one grasps this boundless ground as one's own self. Other masters say: the Yāma heaven's own cloud-ground is twice the measure of Sumeru's summit, and each subsequent heaven up through Paranirmita-vaśavartin doubles the measure of the one before it; the first, second, and third dhyānas correspond, in order, to a small, middling, and great thousand-world-system; and the fourth dhyāna's own measure is boundless. Question: if the fourth dhyāna's own ground is never reached by any calamity whatsoever, is it not therefore permanent and unchanging? Answer: no, since it is momentarily impermanent, this fault does not arise. Others say: within the fourth dhyāna, both the palace and its own foundation are entirely impermanent in a fixed, determinate sense — that is, these palaces and pools arise and perish together with each of these gods' own birth and death. This account, however, is unreasonable,

所以者何。應無有情共器業故。由此如前所說者好。諸器世間既成立已。最初有一極光淨天。由壽業福隨一盡故。從彼歿已生大梵宮。後諸有情亦從彼歿有生梵輔。有生梵眾。有生他化自在天宮。

— for why is this? There would then be no common, shared karma among beings for a common vessel-world at all. Hence the earlier account is the better one. Once the vessel-world has thus been fully formed, the very first being to appear is a single Ābhāsvara god, whose own lifespan, karma, or merit — whichever runs out first — causes him, upon dying there, to be reborn in the Great Brahma Palace. Thereafter, other beings, likewise dying from that same heaven, are reborn variously as Brahma-Purohita, as Brahma-Pariṣadya, or in the palace of the Paranirmita-vaśavartin heaven,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a literally boundless meditative ground is flagged as a spiritual hazard rather than a mark of attainment is a sharp piece of psychological realism: the text does not treat vastness itself as liberating, but rather notices that vastness is exactly what a grasping mind finds easiest to mistake for the infinite, unconditioned self it was already looking for.

2. Repopulating a New World: Last Emptied, First Filled

Once the vessel-world stands ready, a single Ābhāsvara god, his own lifespan or merit spent, is reborn first in the empty Great Brahma Palace, and from him the world fills downward in strict sequence — through the Brahma heavens, the desire-realm heavens, then humans beginning with Uttarakuru and ending with Jambudvīpa, then ghosts, animals, and finally hell — governed by a single elegant rule: whatever place was emptied last is populated first, and whatever was emptied first is populated last.

Chinese Source

漸漸下生。乃至人趣北洲為始。次瞿陀尼。次毘提訶。後生贍部。

Ru-Yi Translation

— and, gradually descending in sequence, are reborn among the human destinies, beginning with Uttarakuru, then Godānīya, then Videha, and finally Jambudvīpa,

次生鬼趣。次生傍生。後生地獄。由法爾力。若處後空彼必先住。

— then the ghost realm, then the animal realm, and finally hell — for, by natural law, whichever place was emptied last is necessarily the first to be repopulated,

若處先空彼必後住。若大地獄一有情生。爾時已度二十成劫。二十住劫此為最初。問幾劫器世間成。幾劫有情漸住。有說。十劫器世間成。十劫有情漸住。有說。五劫器世間成。十五劫有情漸住。如是說者。一劫器世間成。十九劫有情漸住。

— and whichever place was emptied first is necessarily the last to be repopulated. Once a single being is born in the great hell, the twenty formation-eons are thereby completed, and the twenty duration-eons begin from this point. Question: how many eons does it take for the vessel-world itself to form, and how many for beings to gradually populate it? Some say: ten eons for the vessel-world to form, and ten for beings to populate it. Others say: five eons for the vessel-world, and fifteen for beings. According to this final account: one eon for the vessel-world, and nineteen for beings.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rule that repopulation exactly reverses the order of destruction gives the entire cosmology a kind of narrative rhyme: nothing about a new world's filling is arbitrary, but is instead the literal mirror image of how the last one closed, so that reading the destruction sequence backward is, quite precisely, reading the formation sequence forward.

3. How Many World-Systems Form and Fall Together at Once?

A single dramatic question — how many world-systems form and are destroyed together at any one time — receives two competing answers: a fixed number, a hundred million four-continent systems, or, favored by the treatise on the sūtra's own authority, an innumerable number, since the Buddha himself is said to have described seeing countless systems in every stage of destruction, emptiness, formation, and duration at once, falling as continuously as heavy rain. The disparity is resolved by a single principle: beings' own collective and individual karma never runs on a shared clock, so no single world-system's timetable can set the pace for any other.

Chinese Source

問齊幾世界俱壞俱成。有說。齊百俱胝四大洲界。有說。無數世界俱壞俱成。云何知然。經為量故。如契經說。佛告苾芻我眼清淨過於人眼。見東方等無數世界。或有正壞。或壞已空。或有正成。或成已住。如天大雨。滴如車軸。無間無缺此亦如是。問何故一切世界不俱壞俱成。答以諸有情業不等故。謂有情類。於此處所共業增長世界便成。共業若盡世界便壞。又有情類。於彼處所淨業若增此界便壞淨業若減此界便成。問若諸有情。於劫將壞餘半劫。在破和合僧。墮無間獄有中夭不。若有中夭。彼業云何感一劫壽。若不中夭。彼於劫壞寧不稽留。如契經言。若處乃至一蟻卵在災便不壞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: how many world-systems form and are destroyed together at once? Some say: precisely a hundred million four-continent world-systems together. Others say: an innumerable number of world-systems form and are destroyed together — and how is this known? Because the sūtra itself serves as the measure, for the sūtra says: the Buddha told the bhikṣus, "My eye is purer than the human eye — I see, in the eastern direction and elsewhere, innumerable world-systems, some presently being destroyed, some already destroyed and empty, some presently forming, some already formed and enduring" — just as heaven's own great rainfall, its drops as large as cart-axles, falls without any interval or gap, so too it is here. Question: why do not all world-systems form and perish together, all at the same time? Answer: because beings' own karma is not uniform — that is, where beings' collective karma increases in a given place, that world-system forms; once their collective karma is exhausted, that world-system is destroyed; and, where beings' own pure karma increases in a given place, that realm is destroyed, while, if their pure karma decreases, that realm forms instead. Question: if, with only half an eon remaining before its destruction, some being commits schism in the community, an ānantarya deed destined for hell — does such a being die prematurely, before their full natural term? If they do die prematurely, how could such an act still bring about a full eon's worth of hellish retribution? But if they do not die prematurely, would this not mean the world's own destruction is somehow delayed on their account — contrary to the sūtra's own teaching that, wherever even a single ant's egg still remains, that place is never destroyed by calamity,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Grounding a cosmological claim about the sheer number of simultaneous universes in the unevenness of karma, rather than in any physical or geometric necessity, quietly keeps the entire system moral rather than mechanical at its foundation: the cosmos is vast and asynchronous not because space happens to be large, but because no two populations of beings ever finish accumulating and exhausting their own karma at exactly the same moment.

4. The Criminal Caught Mid-Calamity: Smuggled to Another World's Hell

A genuinely poignant puzzle follows: what happens to someone who commits an ānantarya deed — schism in the monastic community, destined for a full eon in hell — with only half an eon left before their own world is destroyed entirely? Neither answer seems tolerable at first: either they die prematurely, undercutting the fixed severity of their crime's own retribution, or they linger past the world's own end, contradicting the principle that no calamity ever fails to destroy even the last remaining ant's egg. The favored resolution: such a being is neither pardoned nor destroyed on schedule, but swept by the sheer force of their own karma into a different, still-surviving world-system's own hell — exactly as a royal amnesty quietly relocates its worst criminals to a border prison rather than either freeing or executing them.

Chinese Source

況彼在耶。有作是說。彼無中夭。若爾。彼於劫壞寧不稽留。劫欲壞時業增上力。飄置餘界大地獄中。譬如王都有嘉喜事。先移極罪置邊獄中。後於王都方降恩赦。有餘師說。劫欲壞時法爾有情其心調善。於餘重罪尚能不為。況有破壞和合僧者。復有說者劫欲壞時。若造破僧無間業者。命終便墮他界獄中。法爾不生將壞處故。

Ru-Yi Translation

— how much less could such a being still remain there? Some say: such a being does not, in fact, die prematurely. Question: if so, would the world's destruction then not be delayed on their account? Answer: as the eon is about to be destroyed, the overwhelming power of that being's own karma sweeps them instead into another world-system's own great hell — just as, when a royal capital is about to celebrate an occasion of joyous amnesty, the most grievous criminals are first quietly relocated to a border-prison, and only afterward is the amnesty formally proclaimed in the capital. Other masters say: as an eon is about to be destroyed, beings there naturally, by the very law of things, develop such a settled and wholesome disposition of mind that they would be incapable even of lesser transgressions, let alone one who would destroy the harmony of the community. Others, further, say: as an eon is about to be destroyed, should anyone commit the ānantarya deed of schism, they die and fall at once into another world-system's own hell, since it is a matter of natural law that no being is ever born into a place about to be destroyed.

如說。火劫世間壞時有情上生。災起時分水劫風劫廣說亦然。但水風災壞相有異。謂水能浸爛。風能飄擊。所壞勢力遠近不同。復如火劫。世間成時先後時分水風亦爾。

As has been explained for the fire-eon's own destruction of the world, and the sequence and timing of beings' ascent as the calamity arises, so too, in full, for the water-eon and the wind-eon — differing only in the specific manner of destruction, water soaking and dissolving, wind scattering and striking, each reaching a different range in its destructive power. And, just as with the fire-eon's own sequence and timing at the world's formation, so too likewise for the water- and wind-eons.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image of karma itself acting as the mechanism that relocates a being across an entire boundary between universes is one of the more startling pieces of imagination in this whole cosmology: retribution here is powerful enough to reach across the literal edge of a dying world and deposit its bearer safely, if grimly, on the far side, so that not even total cosmic annihilation can let a debt go uncollected.

5. The Sword-Calamity and the Plague: When Human Life Falls to Ten Years

The great, world-ending calamities give way to the three minor calamities that periodically strike a still-continuing world, each triggered when human life in Jambudvīpa collapses to a mere ten years amid unrestrained craving and hatred. In the sword-calamity, people seized by sudden murderous rage find every object in their hands transformed into a weapon, slaughtering one another for seven days until only ten thousand survive; in the plague, non-human beings spread poison through seven months of contagion, again leaving only ten thousand — after which, in both cases, the survivors spontaneously turn compassionate, and lifespans begin, slowly, to climb again.

Chinese Source

如彼大劫有大三災。如是中劫小三災現。一刀兵。二疾疫。三飢饉。初刀兵劫將欲起時。贍部洲人極壽十歲。為非法貪染污相續。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as the great eon has its three great calamities, so too does the intermediate eon have its own three minor calamities — sword-warfare, plague, and famine. As the sword-calamity is first about to arise, the people of Jambudvīpa have declined to a mere ten years of life, their own continuity defiled by unlawful craving,

不平等愛映蔽其心。邪法縈纏瞋毒增上。相見便起猛利害心。如今獵師見野禽獸隨手所執皆成刀杖。各逞凶狂互相殘害。七日七夜死亡略盡。贍部洲內纔餘萬人。各起慈心漸增壽量。爾時名為度刀兵劫次疾疫劫將欲起時。贍部洲人極壽十歲。由具如前諸過失故。非人吐毒疾疫流行。遇輒命終難可救療。都不聞有醫藥之名。時經七月七日七夜。疾疫流行死亡略盡。贍部洲內纔餘萬人。各起慈心漸增壽量。爾時名為度疾疫劫。後飢饉劫將欲起時。贍部洲人極壽十歲。亦具如前諸過失故。天龍忿責不降甘雨。由是世間久遭飢饉。

— unequal desire obscuring their own minds, wrapped in wrong views, the poison of hatred ever increasing. Upon merely seeing one another, they give rise to fierce, murderous thoughts — just as hunters, seeing wild beasts, find whatever they seize in hand becomes a weapon — each abandoning himself to violent frenzy, slaughtering one another for seven days and seven nights, until nearly all have perished, with only ten thousand people remaining in the whole of Jambudvīpa. Each of these survivors then gives rise to a compassionate mind, and their lifespans gradually begin to increase — this marks the passing of the sword-calamity. Next, as the plague-calamity is about to arise, the people of Jambudvīpa have again declined to a mere ten years of life, and, possessing the same faults as before, non-human beings spread poison, and plague spreads throughout the land — whoever encounters it soon dies, difficult to treat or cure, with not even the name of medicine any longer heard of. After seven months, seven days, and seven nights, the plague has run its course, and nearly all have perished, with only ten thousand people remaining in the whole of Jambudvīpa. Each of these survivors then gives rise to a compassionate mind, and their lifespans gradually begin to increase — this marks the passing of the plague-calamity. Later, as the famine-calamity is about to arise, the people of Jambudvīpa have again declined to a mere ten years of life, and, again possessing the same faults as before, the gods and nāgas, angered, withhold sweet rain, and the world thereby suffers prolonged famine.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That both catastrophes end not through any external rescue but through the survivors' own spontaneous turn to compassion is the passage's real point: the same unrestrained mind that manufactured the calamity out of ordinary objects and airborne poison is also, once exhausted by its own violence, capable of turning itself around without instruction — moral recovery here needs no teacher, only enough suffering to make people willing to stop.

6. The Famine, Its Three Names, and the Antidotes That Guarantee Escape

The third minor calamity, famine, brings a remarkable etymological digression: three technical terms for this famine each carry two meanings at once — "gathering," describing both starving people gathering to die together and provident survivors setting aside seed-grain for the future; "white bones," describing both bodies quickly reduced to visible bone and the desperate boiling of bones for broth; and "counting sticks," describing both a household rationing its last grain stick by stick according to age, and the threshing of husks for whatever few kernels remain. The section closes with real comfort: whoever keeps the precept against killing for even a single day is guaranteed never to face the sword-calamity in any future life, whoever offers even one myrobalan fruit to the saṅgha with a sincere mind is guaranteed to escape the plague, and whoever offers even a single portion of food is guaranteed to escape the famine — while Uttarakuru alone, among the four continents, is spared even the milder resemblance of all three, since no one is ever reborn there through unwholesome karma to begin with.

Chinese Source

由飢饉故便有聚集。白骨運籌三種言異。由二因故名有聚集。一人聚集謂彼時人。由極飢羸聚集而死。二種聚集。謂彼時人為益後人。輟其所食置於小篋。擬為種子故飢饉時名有聚集言。有白骨亦由二因。一彼時人身形枯燥。命終未久白骨便現。二彼時人飢饉所逼。聚集白骨煎汁飲之。有運籌言。亦二因故。一由糧少傳籌食之。謂一家中從長至幼隨籌至日得少麁食。二謂以籌挑故場蘊得少穀粒多用水煎。分共飲之以濟餘命。如是飢饉經七年七月七日七夜。飢饉所逼死亡略盡。贍部洲內纔餘萬人。各起慈心漸增壽量。

Ru-Yi Translation

On account of this famine, the terms "gathering," "white bones," and "counting sticks" each carry two distinct meanings. As to "gathering," it is spoken of for two reasons: first, people of that time, weakened by extreme hunger, gather together and die; second, people of that time, wishing to benefit those who come after, set aside a small portion of their own food in a little box, keeping it as seed-grain for the future — and hence the famine-time is called a time of "gathering." As to "white bones," it too is spoken of for two reasons: first, people of that time, their bodies withered and dry, show their bones white and visible not long after death; second, people of that time, driven by famine, gather up bones and boil them to drink the broth. As to "counting sticks," it too is spoken of for two reasons: first, on account of scarce provisions, a household allots its food by counting sticks, so that, from eldest to youngest, each receives a small, coarse portion according to the stick drawn for that day; second, using sticks to sift the threshing-floor for whatever few grains remain, boiling them with much extra water, and sharing the thin gruel together to sustain what life remains. Such a famine lasts seven years, seven months, seven days, and seven nights, and, driven by famine, nearly all perish, with only ten thousand people remaining in the whole of Jambudvīpa. Each of these survivors then gives rise to a compassionate mind, and their lifespans gradually begin to increase —

爾時名為度飢饉劫。此三災橫雖復難除。然有聖言說彼對治。謂若有能一日一夜持不殺戒。於未來生決定不逢刀兵災起。若能以一訶梨怛鷄。起殷淨心奉施僧眾於當來世決定不逢疾疫災起。若有能以一摶之食起殷淨心奉施僧眾。於當來世決定不逢飢饉災起。問如是三災餘洲有不。答無根本災。而有相似。謂瞋增盛身力羸劣數加飢渴。此說二洲。北拘盧洲亦無相似。以無罪業而生彼故。又彼無有瞋增盛故。

— this marks the passing of the famine-calamity. Though these three sudden calamities are difficult to entirely eliminate, the sages have nevertheless taught antidotes for each — for whoever is able to keep the precept against killing for a single day and night is guaranteed never to encounter the sword-calamity in any future life; whoever is able, with a sincerely pure mind, to offer even a single myrobalan fruit to the monastic community is guaranteed never to encounter the plague-calamity in the world to come; and whoever is able, with a sincerely pure mind, to offer even a single portion of food to the monastic community is guaranteed never to encounter the famine-calamity in the world to come. Question: do these same three calamities occur on the other continents as well? Answer: they lack the true, root form of these calamities, though a resembling form does occur — namely, increased anger, physical weakness, and frequent hunger and thirst — and this holds for two of the continents. Uttarakuru, however, has neither the true calamities nor even their resembling counterpart, since no one is ever reborn there through unwholesome karma, and since anger itself never intensifies there.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Attaching each minor calamity to a guaranteed antidote requiring nothing more than a single day's precept or a single humble offering is a deliberate act of pastoral reassurance placed directly after the bleakest material in the entire cosmology: having just described entire populations reduced to boiling bones, the text immediately hands its reader something small, concrete, and genuinely within reach, refusing to let the horror stand as the last word.

7. A New Chapter Opens: Does Every Noble One Already Possess Real Absorption?

An entirely new chapter of the treatise begins here — the Full-Vision Nyāsa — opening with a technical question that will occupy the rest of the fascicle: a disciple who has already seen the four truths but is not yet free of sensory craving, and so who possesses form-realm bodily and verbal karma without yet having attained genuine meditative absorption — by which great elements is this karma produced? Behind the question lies a live doctrinal dispute: the rival Vibhajyavādin school, reading a sūtra verse that pairs wisdom and absorption as jointly bringing one close to nirvāṇa, concludes that stream-enterers and once-returners, already possessing wisdom, must therefore already possess genuine root-level dhyāna too — a claim the Venerable Vasumitra rejects by reinterpreting the verse's own word "dhyāna" as meaning simply correct discernment, since otherwise even outside-path ascetics, who plainly do attain real absorption, would have to be granted wisdom as well, an absurd conclusion given that their attainment brings them no closer to nirvāṇa at all.

8. Refuting the Claim That the Threshold Stage Holds Only Good

9. Why 'Already Fully Seen the Truths'? And Seven Reasons an Ordinary Being Isn't Yet a True Disciple

A rich definitional digression asks why the treatise specifies a disciple who has "already fully seen the truths" — distinguishing stream-enterers and once-returners from those merely "following faith" or "following the Dharma" still in the midst of the path of seeing itself — and further specifies "a disciple of the World-Honored One," distinguishing any noble one from an ordinary being altogether. 10. The Answer at Last: Threshold-Stage Elements, Not the First Dhyāna Itself

The treatise's own question finally receives its answer: such a disciple's form-realm bodily and verbal karma is produced, precisely, by great elements of the not-yet-attained access stage — never by genuine first-dhyāna elements. 13. Where in the Body Do Higher Elements First Appear?

A cluster of vividly concrete puzzles asks exactly where, physically, newly-arising form-realm elements first manifest in a desire-realm practitioner's own body — candidates include the space between the eyebrows, the tip of the nose, the heart, the navel, or the toes, or wherever the practitioner had directed their own preparatory effort — before a more comprehensive theory proposes that fine form-realm elements penetrate the interstices of the coarser desire-realm elements the way oil penetrates sand, pervading the whole body at the fundamental level of absorption but arising only near the heart at the merely preparatory, access level. A further, gentler theory holds that even access-level absorption pervades the entire body, only less thoroughly — illustrated by two bathers at the same pond, one merely scooping water at the edge, the other fully submerged, both equally wet, yet only one truly nourished by the water — before a final metaphysical clarification insists that no literal gap is ever filled: the desire-realm body always already contains two dormant future possibilities, pure desire-realm alone or mixed with form-realm elements, and the triggering condition simply substitutes one for the other.

16. Falling Back to Earth: Why Rebirth From the Formless Realm Is Never Uncaused

The fascicle closes on a genuinely elegant worry: since the formless realm has no form at all, might a being who dies out of it and is reborn into the desire or form realm — after an interruption spanning as long as eighty thousand eons — obtain their very first faculties and great elements entirely without cause, having had no immediately preceding embodied existence to draw upon? The treatise firmly denies this: causal lineage is never actually broken by even the longest formless-realm interlude, and newly-arising elements trace their same-class cause all the way back to whatever realm and destiny the practitioner had last embodied, however long ago, worked out exhaustively across every possible destination and even down to the paired distinction between left and right eye — while the rebirth-linking mind itself relates to these same newly-arising elements not as their same-class cause but as their co-existent cause, mind and body arising together rather than one preceding the other.
