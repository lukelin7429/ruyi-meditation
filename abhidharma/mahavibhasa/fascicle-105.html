Ānanda Witnesses the Massacre of the Śākya Clan

Fascicle 105 carries the celebrated narrative of Ānanda's grief before the ruined city of Kapilavastu and the Buddha's own teaching of substituted notions, the three doubled concentrations in full technical detail, and the opening of the Cultivation-of-Knowledge Nyāsa. 1. The Signless Concentration, and Its Many Meanings

Fascicle 105 opens by closing the many-meanings-of-"signless" thread carried over from fascicle 104: the term is shown to apply, in turn, to the path of seeing (too swift to settle into any fixed mental resolve), to unshakeable mental liberation (a mind no longer disordered by the "signs" of craving, aversion, and delusion), and to the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (possessing neither a clear notion nor a clear absence of one, only a dim, hesitation-like subtlety) — before the term is finally applied directly to the signless concentration itself, whose object contains none of the ten ordinary signs at all.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for speaking the term "signless" with respect to the path of seeing: this is exactly as it is said — Maudgalyāyana does not speak of a sixth signless dwelling. What, precisely, is this sixth signless dwelling? It refers to the fact that the follower through faith and the follower through the dharma cannot be posited as dwelling here or there — cannot be posited as dwelling within the forbearance regarding suffering, a dharma-cognition, and so on, up through the forbearance belonging to the subsequent cognition of the path. One asks: for what reason is the path of seeing named signless? The answer: the path of seeing is swift, giving rise to no fixed mental resolve at all, and cannot be posited as dwelling here or there. As for speaking the term "signless" with respect to unshakeable mental liberation: this is exactly as it is said — Venerable Gautama, you should know that craving, aversion, and delusion are themselves signs; unshakeable mental liberation is the very supreme signlessness. One asks: for what reason is unshakeable mental liberation named signless? The answer: every affliction whatsoever is named a sign; this same mind is not thrown into disorder by affliction, affliction attaining no self-mastery with respect to this same mind, this same mind attaining self-mastery with respect to affliction, and it is for this reason that it is named signless. As for speaking the term "signless" with respect to the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception: this is exactly as it is said — I have repeatedly engaged in preparatory effort, employing much diligent effort, and have attained the signless mental concentration; one ought not, with respect to it, to delight in and become attached to it. This speaks of not giving rise to the relished concentration of the very peak of existence, giving rise solely to the pure concentration. One asks: for what reason is the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception named signless? The answer: it possesses no clear, manifest notion; it likewise possesses no sign of the absence of notion — it possesses solely a dim, dull, unclear notion, its own subtle activity turning

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Running these four senses of "signless" side by side makes visible something this treatise does throughout: a single word, encountered across different scriptural contexts, is never assumed to carry one flattened meaning, but is instead traced back to whatever specific absence — of fixed resolve, of affliction's own mastery, of a clear notion, of the ten signs themselves — actually earns it that name in each case.

2. Ānanda Witnesses the Massacre of the Śākya Clan

A justly famous narrative opens the treatise's own scriptural citation: Ānanda comes before the Buddha uncertain whether he has correctly upheld a teaching on dwelling much in emptiness, having felt a flicker of doubt at the time he first heard it — the Buddha confirms he had, since honest hesitation, unlike wrong discrimination or outright forgetting, does not forfeit the title of properly receiving a teaching. The cause of that hesitation is then told in full: King Virūḍhaka's slaughter of the Śākya clan, the ruined city of Kapilavastu strewn with broken towers and blood-mingled springs, seventy-seven thousand kinsmen buried alive and crushed by an iron mill, and Ānanda's own grief at seeing the Buddha walk through it all with mountain-like composure, his majesty if anything more radiant than before.

Chinese Source

如契經說。一時佛住室羅筏城東鹿母精舍。尊者阿難來詣佛所。頂禮雙足而白佛言。我憶一時佛住釋種迷主盧園。親從世尊聞如是義。由我多住空三摩地乃至廣說。我於是義善受持不。世尊告曰。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The devastating physical detail lavished on the ruined city — its towers, its blood-mingled pools, its birds scattered by smoke and flame — is not mere scene-setting: it is what makes Ānanda's own grief fully legible before the Buddha's contrasting calm is offered any explanation at all, so that the teaching that follows lands not as an abstract doctrine about emptiness but as a response to a devastation the reader has just been made to feel in full.

3. The Buddha's Own Ladder of Substituted Notions

The Buddha's own explanation follows: where Ānanda sees a city, kinsmen, and sentient beings, the Buddha sees only wilderness, an ordinary person, and a complete dharma — and it is this substitution of notions, not any lack of feeling, that leaves him unmoved. A remarkable graduated teaching then unfolds for monks wishing to cultivate this same capacity: remove the notion of a city and of sentient beings, replacing them with a notion of wilderness; then remove wilderness and sentient-beings in favor of earth; then earth in favor of the sphere of infinite space, and so on, up through the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — each notion in the chain mapped, in three successive layers of meaning, onto specific views of self, specific dhyānas, and finally the sheer defiled mixedness of the desire realm "like a city," closed by a four-line verse on the monk who is like a mountain, untouched by pleasure or pain.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own fourfold justification for why the substitutions proceed one notion at a time, rather than all at once — matching the sequence of every past buddha, avoiding textual disorder, avoiding repetition, and modeling the very discipline of debate itself — is a rare moment where the treatise pauses to explain not just what a teaching says but why it is delivered in the particular sequence it is: pedagogical form, here, is treated as no less deliberate than doctrinal content.

4. Liberation With and Without Preparatory Effort

A brief technical closure answers the question left hanging at the end of the Ānanda narrative: liberation with preparatory effort belongs to time-bound liberation and to the five preceding arhat lineages relying on lesser grounds, while liberation without preparatory effort belongs to the unshakeable-dharma lineage relying on the four fundamental dhyānas alone.

5. The Three Doubled Concentrations

A new technical topic opens: the three "doubled" concentrations — emptiness-of-emptiness, wishlessness-of-wishlessness, and signless-of-signless — in which the mind, having first observed the aggregates as empty, impermanent, or tranquil, immediately turns and observes that very observation itself as equally empty, impermanent, or tranquil. The caṇḍāla simile makes the logic vivid: exactly as a corpse-burner stirs the pyre with a long pole to ensure everything is consumed, then casts the pole itself into the flames, the doubled concentrations ensure that even the tool of observation does not survive as some subtle, unexamined remainder.

7. Realm, Ground, Support, and Aspect

The now-familiar technical apparatus runs through realm (all three), ground (eleven, from the not-yet-arrived concentration through the four formless attainments), and support (solely a body of the desire realm), before turning to aspect: each doubled concentration inherits only a single aspect from its parent concentration — emptiness-of-emptiness takes solely the emptiness aspect, wishlessness-of-wishlessness solely impermanence, signless-of-signless solely tranquility — each justified by a careful elimination of why the alternative aspects (non-self, suffering, cessation, the wondrous, freedom) would fail to serve the same weariness-inducing function, closed by a poignant simile: a lone traveler feels no great sorrow parting from a companion he knows was never truly his, yet grieves bitterly all the same when the parting finally comes.

9. A Riddle from the Vijñānakāya

A genuinely knotty puzzle closes out the doubled-concentrations discussion: does this same concentration exist only at its very first moment, or does it persist across an entire continuum — and if the latter, how can this be reconciled with the *Vijñānakāya*'s own statement that a dharma "fixed in being given rise to, immediately and without interruption, from the undefiled" is unique to the noble ones? The resolution distinguishes a strict first-moment reading (every later moment being merely a resembling wholesome root, not the concentration itself) from a genus-level reading, under which the entire continuum counts as one sustained instance of the same concentration precisely because its very first moment alone arose immediately from the undefiled.

11. Four Kinds of Cultivation, or Six?

As for a previously attained path presently manifesting in the present: this refers to the fact that, since it already possesses attainment-cultivation, it is named already cultivated, and, since the very matter to be accomplished has already been completed, it is named already brought to rest — this being precisely a previously attained contemplation of impurity, and the like, presently manifesting in the present. One asks: this same path, being presently manifest, and presently possessing habituation-cultivation, and genuinely performing the very matter to be performed, ought to be named not yet cultivated and not yet brought to rest — how can it be said that it is not not-yet-cultivated and not-yet-brought-to-rest? The answer: although, in reliance upon habituation-cultivation, one ought to state that it is solely the past path that is gathered within this same clause, yet, in reliance upon attainment-cultivation, the present, previously attained path, too, is spoken of as belonging to this same clause. Are all present paths genuinely being cultivated? The answer: every present path is genuinely being cultivated — that is, if it is a not-previously-attained path presently manifest, it is, by virtue of possessing both attainment-cultivation and habituation-cultivation, named genuinely being cultivated; if it is a previously attained path presently manifest, it is, solely by virtue of habituation-cultivation, named genuinely being cultivated. There is a path that is genuinely being cultivated, that is not present — this referring to a not-previously-attained path's own very first manifestation in the present, the future path of that same category that it thereby cultivates. Yet category is of four kinds — first, cultivation-category; second, restraint-category; third, realm-category; fourth, resemblance-category. As for cultivation-category: this is exactly as it is stated here — a not-previously-attained path's own very first manifestation in the present, the future path of that same category that it thereby cultivates. Here, there are those who say: the contaminated path takes the contaminated path as its own category; the undefiled path takes the undefiled path as its own category. The verdict: it ought to be said that the contaminated path takes both the contaminated and the undefiled path as its own category; the undefiled path takes both the undefiled and the contaminated path as its own category — for what reason is this so? Each single instance of manifesting in the present alike cultivates both of these two kinds of future path, and it is by the force of this same instance that whatever future path is thereby cultivated can, in every case, be spoken of as belonging to that same category. As for restraint-category: this is exactly as the Karma Aggregate states — if one accomplishes a past precept, does he also accomplish this same category's own future and present precept? Here, restraint takes restraint as its own category; the restraint of individual liberation takes the restraint of individual liberation as its own category; the restraint of dhyāna takes the restraint of dhyāna as its own category; the undefiled restraint takes the undefiled restraint as its own category; the preparatory effort of restraint takes the preparatory effort of restraint as its own category; the subsequent arising of restraint takes the subsequent arising of restraint as its own category; among these, manifestation takes manifestation as its own category; non-manifestation takes non-manifestation as its own category. As for realm-category: this is exactly as the Faculties Aggregate states — if one accomplishes this same category, does the eye faculty, too, then accomplish this same category's own body faculty? Here, if it is a dharma belonging to this same realm, it, in turn, takes a dharma belonging to this same realm as its own category — that is, the desire realm's own dharma takes the desire realm's own dharma as its own category; the form realm's own dharma takes the form realm's own dharma as its own category; the formless realm's own dharma takes the formless realm's own dharma as its own category. As for resemblance-category: this is exactly as the Vinaya states — the Venerable Vatsa, with his own left hand emitting light, and with his own right hand distributing bedding and the like, gathered together monks of resembling category,

謂令持素怛纜者與持素怛纜者。同在一處。持毘柰耶者。與持毘柰耶者。同在一處。持阿毘達磨者。與持阿毘達磨者。同在一處。居阿練若者。與居阿練若者。同在一處。令諸苾芻種類同者。共在一處談論靜默。互相隨順修諸善法無憂惱故。契經亦說。諸有情類諸界各別種類同者。更相愛樂。惡者樂惡。善者樂善。於四種類中此中但依修種類而作論。謂不淨觀乃至盡智現在前時。能修未來無量剎那善有為法。此所修法初剎那頃。是正得修而非現在在未來故。

that is, causing those who uphold the sūtras to dwell together with those who uphold the sūtras; those who uphold the Vinaya to dwell together with those who uphold the Vinaya; those who uphold the Abhidharma to dwell together with those who uphold the Abhidharma; those who dwell in the wilderness to dwell together with those who dwell in the wilderness — causing the various monks of resembling category to dwell together in a single place, whether discoursing together or together in silence, mutually according with one another in cultivating the various wholesome dharmas, free of sorrow and affliction. The scripture likewise states: the various classes of sentient beings, each of resembling category within their own respective realm, take mutual delight in one another — the evil delighting in evil, the good delighting in good. Among these four categories, this treatise relies solely upon cultivation-category in composing its own discussion — that is, at the very moment the contemplation of impurity, and so on, up through the knowledge of exhaustion, manifests in the present, it becomes capable of cultivating measureless future moments of wholesome, conditioned dharmas; as for this same cultivated dharma, at its very first moment, it is genuinely being attained-cultivated, yet is not present, being within the future.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Venerable Vatsa's own light-emitting hand sorting monks by their true affinities is a striking image precisely because it makes an abstract taxonomic principle (resemblance-category) concrete and almost magical: rather than merely asserting that like draws to like, the narrative stages it as something a realized elder can literally see and act upon, gathering scattered practitioners into their true companionships with an authority ordinary sorting could never carry.

14. The Tetralemma of Present and Cultivated

A further tetralemma tests whether "present" and "genuinely being cultivated" coincide: a previously attained path presently manifesting is present but not genuinely being cultivated (possessing only habituation-cultivation, its own matter already accomplished); a future path's very first moment is genuinely being cultivated but not present; a not-previously-attained path presently manifesting is both; and a past or already-completed future path is neither — the governing principle being that any present path necessarily possesses habituation-cultivation, and is thereby always, in some sense, genuinely being cultivated.

Chinese Source

此中現在對正修。問若唯依得修說應作四句。謂或有現在非正修。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the present is set against genuinely being cultivated. One asks: if one speaks solely in reliance upon attainment-cultivation, a tetralemma ought to be cast — that is, there is what is present, that is not genuinely being cultivated,

如曾得道今現在前。或有正修非現在。如修未來初剎那頃。或有現在亦正修。如未曾得道現在前。或有非現在亦非正修。如道過去及未來。已修已息。此中通依。得習修說故。以現在對正修。問唯作順前句答。以現在道必有習修名正修故。

this referring to a previously attained path presently manifesting in the present; there is what is genuinely being cultivated, that is not present, this referring to a future path's own very first moment; there is what is present, that is also genuinely being cultivated, this referring to a not-previously-attained path presently manifesting in the present; there is what is neither present nor genuinely being cultivated, this referring to a path belonging to the past, or to the future, that has already been cultivated and already been brought to rest — this refers, in general, to both attainment-cultivation and habituation-cultivation together, and it is for this reason that the present is set against genuinely being cultivated, giving solely a clause conforming to what precedes as its answer, since a present path necessarily possesses habituation-cultivation, and it is thereby named genuinely being cultivated.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This tetralemma's own careful separation of "present" from "genuinely being cultivated" — categories that might seem, on the surface, simply synonymous — is characteristic of how rigorously this treatise refuses to let two closely related terms collapse into one another without first testing every possible combination of their presence and absence.

15. Retrogression, Attainment, and Cultivation

A final, subtle puzzle asks what happens when a practitioner retrogresses from a higher fruit to a lower one, then later advances again to reattain the higher fruit he had lost: is this reattainment named "attainment-cultivation"? The answer distinguishes sharply by tense — a past fruit reattained is named attainment only, never cultivation, since a present path bears no causal relationship to anything already past; a future fruit newly drawn forth through further advancement is properly named attainment-cultivation; but the very fruit attained at the moment of retrogression itself, though genuinely present and undefiled, is named attainment only, since it arises through retrogression rather than through any further advancement of the present cause.

Chinese Source

問若退上果住下果時。所得下果名得修耶。答名得非修。問彼還進得所退果時。所得上果名得修不。答若過去者名得非修。若未來者名為得修。問何故過去者名得非修。未來者名得修耶。答若現在道與彼為因者。可說彼為得修。現在道與過去無因義故過去道名得非修。問諸退上果住下果時。所得未來下無漏果。既有現在無漏得。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: if, upon retrogressing from a higher fruit, one dwells in a lower fruit, is the lower fruit thereby attained, named attainment-cultivation? The answer: it is named attainment, not cultivation. One asks: were he then, again advancing, to attain the fruit from which he had retrogressed, would the higher fruit he thereby attains be named attainment-cultivation, or not? The answer: if it belongs to the past, it is named attainment, not cultivation; if it belongs to the future, it is named attainment-cultivation. One asks: for what reason is what belongs to the past named attainment, not cultivation, while what belongs to the future is named attainment-cultivation? The answer: if the present path serves as its own cause, it may then be spoken of as attainment-cultivation; the present path bears no meaning of being the cause of anything past, and it is for this reason that a path belonging to the past is named attainment, not cultivation. One asks: at the very time of retrogressing from the higher fruit and dwelling within the lower fruit, the future, undefiled fruit thereby attained does indeed possess a present, undefiled attainment —

因何不名修。答若現在因由勝進故得未來者彼可名修。退住下果時現在無漏得。雖是彼因而非勝進故不名修。不由現在得修彼未來。

for what reason, then, is it not named cultivation? The answer: if the present cause draws it forth through further advancement, thereby attaining the future, it may then be named cultivation; at the very time of retrogressing and dwelling within the lower fruit, the present, undefiled attainment, although it is indeed the cause of that same future fruit, is nonetheless not a matter of further advancement, and it is for this reason that it is not named cultivation, since it is not through the present attainment that one cultivates that same future fruit,

故但由退故彼得現前。

but rather it is solely through retrogression itself that this same attainment manifests in the present.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The insistence that a retrogressed-then-reattained fruit counts as bare attainment rather than cultivation, purely because it arises through falling back rather than through forward effort, reveals something distinctive about how this tradition defines cultivation itself: it is not enough for a wholesome result to appear and even to function causally toward the future — cultivation, properly speaking, names only what is drawn forth through genuine advancement, withholding that honorific term from whatever a practitioner merely regains by circling back to ground already lost.

16. Why Eight Kinds of Knowledge?

The fascicle closes by justifying the very structure of the Cultivation-of-Knowledge Nyāsa itself: why does the compiler rely specifically on eight kinds of knowledge, when scripture elsewhere speaks of two, four, or ten? The answer is that eight is uniquely a "middling account" — gathering in every kind of knowledge in its entirety, unlike the abbreviated schemes of two or four, yet without the excessive elaboration of ten — and that these eight alone exist equally within learner and non-learner, defiled and undefiled, and are repeatedly cultivated and given rise to, unlike the knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising. A closing simile likens the Buddha's own teaching to the measureless, unfathomable ocean: even a hundred thousand niyutas of great treatise-masters, each composing a hundred thousand koṭis of treatises, could never exhaust the meaning of merely two of the Buddha's own phrases.

有八智。謂法智乃至道智。如是等章及解章義既領會已。次應廣釋。問何故尊者依此八智而作論耶。答諸作論者隨欲造論不應詰責。故本論師隨自意欲不違法相而造此論。餘處亦依八智作論。如前結蘊後定蘊等。有處唯依一剎那智而作論。如雜蘊智納息中說。

There exist eight kinds of knowledge — namely, dharma-cognition, and so on, up through, the knowledge of the path. And such chapters as these, together with their own explanatory meaning — having already comprehended them, they ought now to be fully explained in turn. One asks: for what reason does the Venerable compiler compose this treatise in reliance upon these eight kinds of knowledge? There are places where he composes his treatise in reliance solely upon two kinds of knowledge, exactly as within the immediately preceding nyāsa, and the Faculties and Concentration Aggregates. There are places where he composes his treatise in reliance solely upon four kinds of knowledge, exactly as within the later portion of the Faculties Aggregate. There are places where he composes his treatise fully in reliance upon ten kinds of knowledge — that is, the compiler of the base treatise, with respect to the domain of what is to be known, having fully and thoroughly penetrated both its particular and its general characteristic, composes his own treatise according to his own wish, whether in brief or at length, without contravening the characteristics of the dharmas, and thereby ought not to be pressed with an objection. Further, one ought not to press an objection against the compiler of the treatise's own intention — that is, these eight kinds of knowledge were themselves spoken of by the Buddha; this same compiler of the base treatise composes his own treatise in reliance upon the scripture; the scripture speaks of eight kinds of knowledge, admitting of neither increase nor decrease — the Venerable compiler is incapable of reducing them by even one, saying seven, or increasing them by one, saying nine — for what reason is this so? One asks: within his own scripture, the Buddha sometimes speaks of two kinds of knowledge, sometimes of four kinds, sometimes of eight kinds, and sometimes of ten kinds — for what reason does the Venerable compiler, here, rely solely upon eight kinds of knowledge in composing his own treatise? The answer: it is because these eight kinds of knowledge are a middling account, gathering in every kind of knowledge in its entirety, and it is for this reason that his own treatise is composed in reliance upon them — that is, speaking of two kinds and the like is, in every case, a brief account, not gathering in every kind of knowledge in its entirety; the scripture on the ten kinds of knowledge, although it does gather in every kind of knowledge in its entirety, is nonetheless an elaborate account — it is solely these eight kinds of knowledge that gather in every kind of knowledge in its entirety while remaining a middling account, and it is for this reason that his own treatise relies particularly upon them. Further, these eight kinds of knowledge exist, alike, within the body of the learner and the non-learner, and within the body of one who is defiled and one who is undefiled, and it is for this reason that his own treatise relies particularly upon them; the knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising exist solely within the non-learner's own body, and solely within the body of one who is undefiled, and it is for this reason that his own treatise does not rely upon them. Further, these eight kinds of knowledge, in every case, pervade the very own-nature of both knowledge and vision, and it is for this reason that his own treatise relies particularly upon them; the knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising do not pervade the very own-nature of vision, and it is for this reason that his own treatise does not rely upon them. Further, these eight kinds of knowledge are repeatedly cultivated and given rise to, and it is for this reason that his own treatise relies particularly upon them; the knowledge of exhaustion and of non-arising are not repeatedly cultivated and given rise to, and it is for this reason that his own treatise does not rely upon them. It is by virtue of such various causes and conditions as these that this treatise relies solely upon these eight kinds of knowledge in composing its own discussion.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ocean simile closing this fascicle performs a quiet but important act of epistemic humility precisely at the moment the treatise is most assertively justifying its own compiler's technical choices: having just spent an entire discussion defending exactly why eight kinds of knowledge, and not seven or nine, is the correct number, the treatise pauses to insist that even this same meticulous precision can never fully exhaust the Buddha's own teaching — technical exactness and acknowledged inexhaustibility are held together, not treated as in tension with one another.
