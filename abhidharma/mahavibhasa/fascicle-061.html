Seers Who Fell From the Sky

Fascicle 61 continues the Sarvāstivāda defense of an arhat's capacity to regress, opened in fascicle 60. It resolves whether affliction causes regression or regression causes affliction, then defends the claim that regression properly occurs at the mental faculty alone against three unforgettable narratives — five hundred seers who fell from the sky at King Udayana's harem, a sage undone by a princess's touch and reborn at last as a monstrous winged predator, and Śakra's own jealous consort undoing a sage's hard-won attainment with a single flattering word. The fascicle closes with an exhaustive, over-determined account of exactly why the higher three fruits of the path can regress while stream-entry alone never can. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 61 continues the arhat-regression debate opened in fascicle 60, now within the Single-Class-of-Conduct Nyāsa (結蘊第二中一行納息第二之六). It asks whether regression causes affliction or affliction causes regression, whether regression occurs at the mind alone or through the five senses — illustrated by three vivid narratives — and works out in exhaustive detail exactly which fruits of the path can and cannot regress, and why. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Which Comes First: Regression or Affliction?

The fascicle opens with a genuine chicken-and-egg dilemma: does regression occur because an affliction becomes presently manifest, or does an affliction become presently manifest because one has already regressed? Both answers appear to contradict some scriptural or treatise source — one set of texts describing the specific conditions under which an affliction arises and only then causing regression, another describing regression itself as a change of mind that only afterward manifests as affliction. The treatise ultimately sides with the first position, affliction causing regression, and reconciles every apparently conflicting source by showing each one addresses a different, compatible aspect of the same event.

2. Awareness Lags Regression

A further round of objections is dispatched by distinguishing the actual moment of regression from the moment one becomes aware of it — exactly as a person who has forgotten a memorized scripture through distraction remains unaware of the forgetting until he actually attempts to recite it again. The treatise then works out, mind by mind, immediately following which specific mental state — wholesome, defiled, or one of three grades of neutral — an affliction becomes presently manifest at each level of absorption, before opening an entirely new question: does regression occur at the mental faculty alone, or can it occur through any of the five sense-consciousnesses as well?

As for the Prakaraṇapāda's naming the unwholesome and the obscured-neutral as what conforms to and accompanies regression — this statement is made with reference to their damaging and progressively distancing one from the wholesome class: the more an affliction becomes presently manifest, the more it damages and distances one from the wholesome class, and it is on this basis that it is said to conform to and accompany regression — it does not mean that regression occurs only once the affliction becomes presently manifest, since the wholesome dharma has already regressed beforehand. 3. King Udayana's Harem

The treatise's own answer — that regression occurs solely at the mental faculty — meets its first vivid challenge: the story of King Udayana, who withdrew his entire court to a mountain retreat, dismissed every man, and had his unclothed women dance before him with music and incense. Five hundred seers, flying overhead by their own supernatural power, lost that very power upon seeing the forms, hearing the music, or smelling the incense, and fell helplessly onto the mountainside — whereupon the enraged king, discovering they were not yet free of desire, had their hands and feet cut off.

5. Śakra's Jealous Consort, and Six Marvels of the Mind

A third case completes the challenge: Śakra, lord of the gods, unknowingly carries his jealous consort Śacī to visit the sage Dvīpāyana; when the sage discovers her and orders her away, her flattering, appeasing voice is enough to make the sage fall in love and lose his own hard-won attainment on the spot. The treatise resolves all three narratives the same way: every case of apparent regression through sight, sound, smell, or touch is properly understood as occurring at the mental faculty, merely drawn forth by the sense-consciousness that first makes contact with its object — a reading defended against a rival master who argues regression can occur directly within the five senses themselves, since only the mental faculty is capable of the discriminative judgment an affliction actually requires.

6. Three Kinds of Regression, and Devadatta's Warning

The treatise distinguishes three kinds of regression — from what has already been attained, from what has not yet been attained, and of enjoyment, where a previously attained quality simply fails to become presently manifest — illustrated by two verses: one lamenting how attachment to name and form keeps beings from ever attaining the noble eye of wisdom in the first place, and a second, spoken by the Buddha with reference to Devadatta, warning that even one honored by the multitude can fall from the very summit of spiritual attainment through attachment to fame and gain, cutting off his own wholesome roots entirely.

7. How Buddhas, Pratyekabuddhas, and Śrāvakas Each Regress

The treatise works out exactly which of the three kinds of regression apply to each level of attainment: the Buddha possesses only regression of enjoyment, since he holds the very highest faculty and belongs, without exception, to the non-regressing class; pratyekabuddhas and ordinary śrāvakas each add regression from what has not yet been attained, since they still aspire toward a higher vehicle; and the temporarily-liberated arhat alone possesses all three kinds, his very attainment itself still subject to genuine loss. A rival position — that the Buddha possesses no regression whatsoever, not even of enjoyment, since nothing he fails to manifest was ever his settled intention to constantly manifest in the first place — is considered and set against scriptural proof that the Buddha does indeed experience a form of regression.

11. Regression Cannot Reach Below Where One Started

The treatise establishes a further constraint: someone who becomes free of defilement through several successive grounds before finally entering the path of seeing can, if he later regresses, only regress through a higher ground's own affliction, never a lower one — the lower ground's defilement having already been permanently disabled by both worldly and noble paths of counteraction, and further sealed off by the path of seeing itself pressing down upon it "like a landslide burying a fallen climber." A genuine puzzle follows: does such regression restore only the afflictions abandoned by cultivation, or the view-fetters abandoned by seeing as well? Neither option works cleanly, and the treatise concludes that no previously abandoned affliction, of either kind, can ever be restored once a noble fruit has actually been attained.

12. The Vajra-like Absorption and the Final Fetter

The fascicle closes on a precise technical question: at the very threshold of arhatship, one abiding in the vajra-like absorption still technically possesses the very last, faintest grade of affliction belonging to the summit of existence — so if one later regresses from arhatship and that faintest affliction returns, does the vajra-like absorption return along with it? The answer is a firm no: the absorption is won only through immense, sustained effort, while the returning affliction requires no effort at all to reappear; the absorption belongs to a moment of advancement, the affliction to a moment of falling; and, decisively, the absorption's own presence is compatible with merely possessing that faintest affliction, while the affliction's own presence is incompatible with even possessing the absorption at all.
