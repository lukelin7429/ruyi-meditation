The Ten Perceptions Begin

Fascicle 166 opens a new milestone: the Object-Condition Nyāsa concludes at the close of fascicle 165, and the Subsumption Nyāsa (攝納息第三) begins here with the Ten Perceptions (十想) — ten meditative topics running from impermanence to cessation. After an extensive scholastic classification, the fascicle turns to genuine practice: detailed instructions for corpse contemplation, loathing food, and the perception of death, closing with a defense of why contemplating what one knows is not literally true is not itself a form of delusion. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Why Exactly Ten Perceptions, Not More or Less

Fascicle 166 opens with a genuine milestone: the Object-Condition Nyāsa (緣納息第二), which has run since fascicle 162, concludes at the very end of fascicle 165, and this fascicle opens the Subsumption Nyāsa (攝納息第三) with a wholly new topic — the Ten Perceptions (十想). The treatise begins by listing all ten by name, then asks why the Buddha taught exactly this number, no more and no less. The answer treats the Buddha's own teaching as inherently incapable of addition or subtraction, then offers roughly ten independent scholastic explanations for why these ten specifically were chosen — mapping them onto the path and its preparatory practice and fruit, śamatha and vipaśyanā, this shore and that shore and the raft, the faults of the aggregates and the three realms, freedom from the defilement of each realm in turn, and the antidotes for twelve named obstructions.

Ru-Yi Translation

The ten perceptions are: the perception of impermanence, the perception of impermanence-as-suffering, the perception of suffering-as-non-self, the perception of death, the perception of impurity, the perception of loathing food, the perception that nothing in the whole world is delightful, the perception of severance, the perception of dispassion, and the perception of cessation. Such chapter-headings and their explanatory glosses, once grasped, call for full analysis. Question: why does this passage compose its treatise with reference to these ten perceptions first? Answer: this is simply what the author of this treatise intended, and he composed the treatise according to that very intention — so long as it does not contradict the characteristics of the dharmas involved, no objection should be raised. Some hold: this is done in order to elucidate the meaning of a certain sūtra passage — for a sūtra states that there are ten perceptions, the perception of impermanence and so on down to the perception of cessation; although the sūtra speaks in this way, it does not explain them at length, and this treatise now wishes to explain them, and it is for this reason that it is composed. Question: why does this passage speak only of ten perceptions? Answer: this question ought not even to be raised, since this is simply what the Buddha himself taught in his own sūtras. The Buddha, throughout his sūtras, spoke consistently of only ten perceptions, and the author of this treatise cannot add a single one to what the Buddha taught to make eleven, nor subtract a single one to make nine. Why so? Because the Buddha's own teaching admits of neither addition nor subtraction, and so cannot be added to or subtracted from — just as it admits of no increase, no decrease, no excess, no deficiency, no lack, and no surplus; and the very same holds of its being immeasurable and boundless. "Immeasurable" because its meaning is difficult to fathom; "boundless" because its wording is difficult to fully comprehend — just as the great ocean is immeasurable and boundless, immeasurable in its depth and boundless in its breadth.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sheer number of independent explanations offered here for a single numerical choice — ten different scholastic lenses, none declared authoritative over the others — is itself a quiet argument about how this tradition reads scripture: a teaching's rightness does not depend on any one systematic justification being correct, only on the teaching itself being traceable to the Buddha's own intention, however many equally valid ways there may be to explain why it takes the shape it does.

2. Realm, Ground, Basis, Aspect, and Object

The treatise now turns to the systematic classification proper to the Abhidharma's method, working through each of the ten perceptions in turn across realm, meditative ground, underlying basis, cognitive aspect, and object taken. The perception of impurity and of loathing food, tied to the desire and form realms and to the eye-consciousness or the senses of smell, taste, and touch, are the most restricted; the perception of death, by contrast, opens a genuine dispute over exactly what it takes as its object — the dying instant's life-faculty alone, its conascent aggregates, or their shared impermanence.

9. The Perception of Death: The Turning Seasons and the Collapse of Time

The passage now turns to the perception of death proper, opening with an extended nature-meditation: the practitioner observes spring's blossoming, summer's lushness, autumn's withering, and winter's death in the external world, and matches each season to a corresponding stage of human life witnessed in the village — birth, marriage, old age, and a parent's death. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The collapse from an entire human lifetime down through eight successively finer units of time to the single instant is one of the most striking passages in the fascicle so far: it treats the ordinary observation that everyone ages and dies as merely the coarsest-grained case of something happening at every conceivable timescale at once, so that fully realizing "I will die" and fully realizing "this very moment is already ceasing" turn out, on this account, to be the very same insight seen at different resolutions.

10. Why Contemplating the Unreal Is Not a Form of Delusion

The fascicle closes with a philosophical question that has been implicit throughout the corpse-contemplation and food-loathing instructions: since a resolve-attention practice deliberately contemplates something the practitioner knows is not literally true — a living body as a corpse, one's food as excrement — how is this not itself a form of the very delusion the path is meant to overcome? The answer distinguishes knowing a perception is not literally real from being genuinely deceived by it, and offers two everyday analogies: regarding another man's wife as one's own mother to suppress lust, and regarding an enemy as a friend to suppress hatred.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: if this is not actually real, how can it be capable of subduing the defilements? Answer: it is precisely by forming that very perception that one becomes capable of suppressing them — just as, when one forms the perception of another's wife as one's own mother, no craving or defiled attachment arises toward her; when one forms the perception of an enemy as a dear friend, no anger or hatred arises toward him; and when one forms the perception of an inferior person as one worthy of reverence, no arrogance or conceit arises toward him. The very same holds here as well, and it is for this reason that such contemplation must be undertaken.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This closing exchange is worth pausing on for its own sake: it draws a sharp, deliberate line between a distorted view (顛倒) — genuinely believing something false — and a therapeutic fiction consciously held for its effect, and in doing so it defends every visualization practice described in this fascicle from the charge of self-deception, on the grounds that the practitioner's own clear awareness that the corpse is not really his body is precisely what keeps the practice honest.
