Farther Than Earth From Sky

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 6 — the same nyāsa section on the four nirvedhabhāgīya roots continues (雜蘊第一中世第一法納息第一之五), now turning to summit (頂) and heat (煖), the two roots below forbearance and supreme mundane dharma. The fascicle explains summit's own mountain-peak etymology and its "falling away" (頂墮) through a treasure-vision simile and three moments of maximal difficulty; works through heat's fire-drilling etymology; tells, in full narrative detail rare for this treatise, the story of the two monks Assaji and Punabbasu, whom the Buddha declared farther from the Dharma than earth from sky, and the four-line teaching they refused to hear; and closes with a systematic comparison of all four roots — their grades according to several named masters, and exactly what causes each to be gained or lost, including the puzzling case of Devadatta. Fascicle 7 continues the same nyāsa section, opening with the Western masters' own seventeen-gate analysis of all four roots together.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 6 complete, 27 sections Source: Taishō T1545, vol. 27, fascicle 6 Topic: 頂 and 煖 (mūrdhan, ūṣmagata), Miscellaneous Chapter §1

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

This fascicle continues directly from Fascicle 5, within the same nyāsa (納息) section begun in Fascicle 2 — terms established there, such as the certainty of rightness (正性離生) and the noble truths, recur constantly here and are not re-explained at each occurrence. Sanskrit and Pali proper names in the narrative sections follow the forms most familiar in English-language Buddhist studies where an established one exists.

1. What Is Summit? The Mountain-Peak Etymology

The fascicle opens with summit's basic definition — a small measure of faith and affection toward the Three Jewels — and its etymology from a mountain's own peak, before working through three rival schemes for classifying it against the other nirvedhabhāgīya roots.

Chinese Source

云何頂。答於佛法僧生小量信。問何故名頂。答如山頂故。謂如山頂人不久住。若無諸難便過此山。更至餘山。若有諸難即還退下。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is summit? The answer: a small measure of faith and affection arising toward the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha. One asks: why is it named "summit"? The answer: because it is like the summit of a mountain — that is, just as a person atop a mountain's summit does not linger there for long: if no difficulty arises, he passes on over this mountain to reach yet another; but if difficulty arises, he retreats back down again —

如是行者。至頂位中必不久住。若無諸難便進至忍。若有諸難還退住煖。有說此應名為下。頂以在最下。順決擇分。煖法上故。有說此頂應名為中。以在下煖上忍中故。尊者妙音作如是說。順決擇分總有二種。一欲界繫。二色界繫。欲界繫中。下者名煖。上者名頂。色界繫中。下者名忍。上者名世第一法。此於欲界順決擇分中勝故名頂。彼不應作是說此四皆是。定地修地。行聖行相。色界法故。應作是說。順決擇分總有二種。一可退二不可退。可退中下者名煖。上者名頂不可退中。下者名忍。上者名世第一法。此於可退順決擇分中勝故名頂。問何故此信名小量耶。尊者妙音作如是說。

the practitioner is exactly the same: reaching the stage of summit, he certainly does not linger there for long — if no difficulty arises, he advances on to forbearance; but if difficulty arises, he retreats back down and abides in heat instead. Some say this stage should instead be named "the lowest," since summit occupies the very lowest position among the nirvedhabhāgīya factors, with heat lying above it. Some say this "summit" should instead be named "the middle," since it lies below heat and above forbearance in the middle. The Venerable Ghoṣaka speaks as follows: the nirvedhabhāgīya factors are, in total, of two kinds — those bound to the desire realm, and those bound to the form realm. Among those bound to the desire realm, the lower is named heat and the higher is named summit; among those bound to the form realm, the lower is named forbearance and the higher is named supreme mundane dharma. Since summit is supreme among the nirvedhabhāgīya factors bound to the desire realm, it is named "summit." This position should not be maintained, however, since all four of these factors are, in fact, dharmas of the form realm, belonging to a stage of concentration and cultivation, bearing the aspect of noble conduct. One should instead say: the nirvedhabhāgīya factors are, in total, of two kinds — those subject to retrogression, and those not subject to it. Among those subject to retrogression, the lower is named heat and the higher is named summit; among those not subject to retrogression, the lower is named forbearance and the higher is named supreme mundane dharma. Since summit is supreme among the nirvedhabhāgīya factors subject to retrogression, it is named "summit." One asks: why is this faith called a "small measure"? Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ghoṣaka's own proposed division — desire-realm-bound roots versus form-realm-bound roots — is directly refuted here on grounds already established across Fascicles 3 and 4: all four nirvedhabhāgīya factors were shown there to belong to the form realm exclusively, so a division by realm cannot be what separates heat and summit from forbearance and supreme mundane dharma. The correct division, the treatise concludes, is instead by retrogression — precisely the property Fascicle 5 spent so much effort proving supreme mundane dharma and forbearance both lack.

2. Why Is This Faith Called "Small"?

Three etymologies compete for why summit's faith earns the qualifier "small" — its association with the inferior desire realm, its being a second, differing measure of faith after heat, and its own characteristic brevity — before the treatise settles the question and explains what summit's faith actually takes as its object.

Chinese Source

欲界名小。以下劣故。此在欲界故名小量。有說此信應名異量。量謂決定信順印可。故名為量。煖是第一頂是第二。此異於前。故名異量。有說此信應名少量以頂位中不久住故。如露懸枝不久停住。

Ru-Yi Translation

the desire realm is called "small," being itself inferior — and since this faith occurs while the practitioner is still classed as belonging to the desire realm, it is named a "small measure." Some say this faith should instead be named a "differing measure": "measure" here means the settled faith and endorsement that confirms something as true, and is thus called a "measure"; heat is the first such measure, summit the second — and since this second measure differs from the one before it, it is called a "differing measure." Some say this faith should instead be named a "brief measure," since one does not linger for long at the stage of summit — like dew hanging from a branch, which never remains in place for long.

應作是說。此頂但應名小量信。在可退位樂觀寶故。此中於佛僧生小量信者。說緣道諦信。於法生小量信者。說緣滅諦信。問此頂善根具以十六行相。緣四聖諦。何故此中但說緣滅道諦非苦集耶。答依勝說故。謂四諦中滅道是勝出生死故。復次滅道二諦清淨無垢離過微妙。是可信事。是生信處。是所歸依。是故偏說。復次滅道二諦。非唯可信亦是可求。難可證得極可欣故。苦集不爾是故不說。

One should instead say: this "summit" should simply be named a "small measure of faith," since it occurs at a stage still subject to retrogression, and delights in individually contemplating the Three Jewels. Here, "a small measure of faith arising toward the Buddha and the Saṅgha" refers to faith taking the truth of the path as its object; "a small measure of faith arising toward the Dharma" refers to faith taking the truth of cessation as its object. One asks: this wholesome root of summit fully contemplates the four noble truths through all sixteen aspects — why, then, does this passage speak only of taking cessation and the path as its object, and not suffering and origin? The answer: because the statement follows what is supreme — among the four truths, cessation and the path are supreme, since they lead one out of birth-and-death. Further: the two truths of cessation and the path are pure, stainless, free of fault, and subtle — they are what one can rightly believe in, what gives rise to faith, and what one takes refuge in; this is why they alone are singled out here. Further: the two truths of cessation and the path are not merely to be believed in, but also to be sought after, since they are difficult to realize and utterly delightful; suffering and origin are not like this, and this is why they are not mentioned.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Note the deliberate asymmetry in the etymology debate: two of the three proposed readings connect "small" to something negative about summit's own status (the inferior desire realm, its brief duration), while the treatise's own preferred reading simply grounds it in the stage's retrogressible status — a more technical, less evocative explanation, but one consistent with the classification already settled in Section 1.

3. Sparing the Faithful From Discouragement

Two further reasons explain why summit's faith centers on cessation and the path rather than suffering and origin — a psychological one, sparing new practitioners the discouragement of contemplating their own affliction too soon — before the treatise turns to a technical variant naming three truths instead of one.

Chinese Source

復次為受化者生信樂故。若佛為彼說苦集諦。便作是念。我無始來。為此鄙劣。煩惱惡行。及所得果。擾亂逼迫。寧可信樂。若說滅道。便深信樂。不欲捨離。故於此中。偏說滅道。有說於佛僧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further: it is in order to give rise to the faith and delight of those being taught. If the Buddha were to teach them the truths of suffering and origin, they would think to themselves: since beginningless time, I have been disturbed and oppressed by these base afflictions, evil conduct, and their resulting fruit — how could I possibly take faith and delight in this? But if he teaches cessation and the path instead, they come to have deep faith and delight, unwilling ever to part from them — this is why cessation and the path alone are singled out here. Some say: with respect to the Buddha and the Saṅgha,

生小量信者。說緣道諦信。於法生小量信者。說緣三諦信。以頂具緣四聖諦故。問緣滅道諦可爾。是可信事。是生信處。是所歸依。

"a small measure of faith arising" refers to faith taking the truth of the path as its object; "a small measure of faith arising toward the Dharma" refers to faith taking the remaining three truths as its object, since summit does, after all, fully take all four noble truths as its object. One asks: taking cessation and the path as object is understandable enough, since these are what one can rightly believe in, what gives rise to faith, and what one takes refuge in,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The psychological reasoning here is unusually direct for this treatise: it explicitly frames the sutra's own selective emphasis as pedagogical strategy rather than metaphysical necessity, protecting a beginner's fragile motivation from a premature confrontation with their own affliction — a rare moment where the compilers step outside pure doctrinal analysis to consider a practitioner's felt experience.

4. Two Kinds of Faith: Endorsement and Delight

The treatise resolves the apparent puzzle of how anyone could have faith in suffering and origin at all, by distinguishing two kinds of faith — bare endorsement and genuine delight — illustrated by a digger searching for water and treasure, and closes with the Venerable Pārśva's own reading of why cessation and the path alone are singled out for praise.

Chinese Source

應信樂故。緣苦集諦云何可爾。煩惱惡行及所得果。猶如糞穢深可厭患。不應於中生信樂故。答信有二種。一者信可。二者信樂。於滅道諦具二種信。於苦集諦雖無信樂。而有信可。故緣苦集亦生於信。如人掘地求水寶等。具二種信。一者信可。謂信地中有水寶等故。二者信樂。謂信水等是可欣樂故。脇尊者言。為厭患苦集故。

Ru-Yi Translation

and so worthy of faith and delight — but how can taking suffering and origin as object be understandable in the same way? The afflictions, evil conduct, and their resulting fruit are like filth, deeply repellent — one ought not to take faith and delight in them at all. The answer: faith is of two kinds — faith as endorsement, and faith as delight. Toward cessation and the path, one holds both kinds of faith at once; toward suffering and origin, though one holds no faith as delight, one does hold faith as endorsement — and so taking suffering and origin as object still gives rise to a kind of faith. It is like someone digging in the earth in search of water or treasure, who holds both kinds of faith at once: faith as endorsement, meaning belief that water or treasure does exist in the earth; and faith as delight, meaning belief that water and the rest are things to be joyfully desired. The Venerable Pārśva says: it is precisely in order to inspire disgust toward suffering and origin

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The digger's two kinds of faith map cleanly onto a distinction worth keeping in mind for the rest of this fascicle: belief that something is real and true (endorsement) is separable from wanting it (delight) — which is exactly what lets a practitioner honestly "believe in" suffering and its origin, the two truths no one could ever be said to desire, without the whole notion of faith collapsing into incoherence.

5. Seven Kinds of Practitioner, Different Degrees of Faith

The treatise closes the endorsement-versus-delight discussion with a house-in-a-storm simile, then works through seven paired categories of practitioner — faith-follower against dharma-follower, and so on up to buddha, pratyekabuddha, and śrāvaka — each holding a different combination of the two kinds of faith toward the others' paths.

Chinese Source

稱讚滅道。謂此滅道寂靜美妙。止息對治下劣鄙穢苦集法故。如人風雨所逼惱故稱讚舍宅。故於四諦皆可生信。然於道諦。亦非一切具二種信。謂隨信行者。於隨法行道具二種信。謂信可彼信樂彼故。隨法行者。於隨信行道。唯有一信。謂雖信可彼。而不信樂彼故。信勝解者。於見至道。具二種信見至者於信勝解道。唯有一信。時解脫者。於不時解脫道。具二種信。不時解脫者。於時解脫道。唯有一信。佛於佛道具二種信。於二乘道唯有一信。獨覺於二道具二種信。於聲聞道唯有一信。聲聞於三道皆具二信。此中尊者。欲令前義得成立故。復引契經。如世尊為波羅衍拏摩納婆說。

Ru-Yi Translation

that cessation and the path are praised — since cessation and the path are peaceful, still, and beautiful, bringing to rest and counteracting the base, filthy dharmas of suffering and origin, just as someone battered by wind and rain praises a sheltering house. Thus faith can arise toward all four truths alike. Yet with respect to the truth of the path specifically, not everyone holds both kinds of faith at once: a faith-follower holds both kinds of faith toward the path of a dharma-follower — endorsing it, and delighting in it as well — while a dharma-follower holds only the one kind of faith toward the path of a faith-follower, endorsing it without delighting in it. One released through faith-based resolve holds both kinds of faith toward the path of one who has arrived at view, while one who has arrived at view holds only the one kind of faith toward the path of faith-based resolve. One liberated not-in-due-time holds both kinds of faith toward the path of one liberated in-due-time, while one liberated in-due-time holds only the one kind toward the path of not-in-due-time liberation. A buddha holds both kinds of faith toward the buddha-path, but only the one kind toward the paths of the two lesser vehicles; a pratyekabuddha holds both kinds of faith toward both of the two higher paths, but only the one kind toward the śrāvaka path; and a śrāvaka holds both kinds of faith toward all three paths alike. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The pattern across all seven pairs is worth noticing: the "higher" or more advanced type in each pair holds only endorsement toward the "lower" type's path, while the lower type holds both endorsement and delight toward the higher — a systematic asymmetry suggesting that admiration flows upward (toward a path one has not yet reached but hopes to) while a completed, superior attainment regards a lesser path with respect but without longing, since it has already gone further.

6. The Young Scholar's Verse

The promised sutra citation arrives — a verse the Buddha spoke to a young brahmin scholar of the Pārāyaṇa tradition — followed by the treatise's own question: why did the Buddha, in this one case, teach summit as faith rather than as the wisdom the root text itself defines it as?

Chinese Source

若於佛法僧　生起微小信
儒童應知彼　名已得頂法
問此頂善根。十六行相。觀四聖諦。何故世尊。為摩納婆。說信三寶。答彼於三寶。愚惑不信。然三寶希有。難可值遇。欲令信解。

Ru-Yi Translation

"If, toward the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, / a small measure of faith should arise — young scholar, know that one / has already attained the dharma of summit." One asks: this wholesome root of summit contemplates the four noble truths through all sixteen aspects — why, then, did the World-Honored One, in speaking to this young brahmin scholar, teach faith in the Three Jewels specifically? The answer: because he was confused and lacked faith with respect to the Three Jewels, yet the Three Jewels are rare and difficult to encounter — wishing to bring him to faith and understanding,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Pārāyaṇa (波羅衍拏) is the same closing section of the Sutta Nipāta already cited back in Fascicle 4 for its verses on "dispelling the perception of form" — this treatise draws on it again here as scripture in its own right, treating a brahmin youth's own encounter with the Buddha as authoritative evidence for how "summit" can honestly be taught as faith rather than as wisdom.

7. The Youth Oppressed by Suffering

The full narrative context of the verse emerges: a suffering young man came to the Buddha seeking relief, prompting five separate explanations for why the Buddha answered with faith in the Three Jewels rather than the four truths directly.

Chinese Source

故為說之。有說彼摩納婆為苦所逼。欲求出要來詣佛所。說是頌言。

Ru-Yi Translation

the Buddha taught him accordingly. Some say: this young scholar, oppressed by suffering, came seeking a way out, and approached the Buddha, who then spoke this verse:

為苦所逼諸眾生　不知出要來詣佛
唯願為說除眾患　如熱所逼入涼池
出苦之要無過三寶。故佛為說於三寶信。有說欲令所化於佛法中深生信重。故佛為說於佛法僧生微小信。若佛為說四聖諦者。所化有情便作是念。我等何用信重如是煩惱惡行顛倒見趣及所得果苦集諦為。若佛為說佛法僧寶。彼便踊躍深生信重。有說隨修行者樂別觀故。謂修行者於煖位中樂別觀蘊。於頂位中樂別觀寶。於忍位中樂別觀諦。故於頂位說信三寶。

"Sentient beings oppressed by suffering, not knowing the way out, come to the Buddha; / may you, out of compassion, teach the removal of every affliction, as one scorched by heat enters a cool pool." There is no way out of suffering surpassing the Three Jewels — this is why the Buddha taught faith in the Three Jewels here. Some say: it is in order to make those being taught give rise to deep faith and reverence within the Buddha's own teaching that the Buddha taught a small measure of faith arising toward the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha. Had the Buddha instead taught the four noble truths directly, the sentient beings being taught would have thought: what use is there in placing deep faith and reverence in such things as afflictions, evil conduct, inverted views, and the resulting truths of suffering and origin? But when the Buddha taught the treasures of the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha instead, they leapt with joy and gave rise to deep faith and reverence. Some say: it is simply in accordance with what a practitioner delights in individually contemplating at each stage — that is, a practitioner delights in individually contemplating the aggregates at the stage of heat, in individually contemplating the Three Jewels at the stage of summit, and in individually contemplating the truths at the stage of forbearance; this is why faith in the Three Jewels is spoken of specifically at the stage of summit.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The cool-pool simile is a small but vivid piece of imagery from the cited verse itself, worth pausing on: someone scorched by heat does not need a lecture on thermodynamics before entering cool water — the relief itself does the convincing. The treatise's psychological reading of the exchange draws the same conclusion in doctrinal terms: faith in a refuge answers suffering more directly, for a beginner, than an analytic account of suffering's own causes ever could.

8. The Buddha's Own Definition of Summit

A second, technical sutra citation follows — this one addressed to Ānanda, giving what the treatise treats as the Buddha's most direct definition of summit itself — before the treatise asks why the Buddha taught summit so differently to two different audiences.

Chinese Source

如世尊告阿難陀言。吾今為汝等。說頂及頂墮。謂聖弟子。於五取蘊。起作有為緣生法中。思量觀察。此是無常苦空無我。彼即於如是思量觀察時。有忍有見。有欲樂有行解。有見審慮忍。如是名為頂。問何故世尊。為波羅衍拏。說信為頂。為諸新學苾芻。說慧為頂耶。答佛善知法相。及善知根器。應為說者即為說之。餘無此能故不應問。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the World-Honored One said to Ānanda: I shall now teach you about summit, and about falling from summit — that is, a noble disciple, contemplating and examining the five aggregates of grasping, formed, conditioned, and dependently arisen, comes to see: this is impermanent, suffering, empty, without self — and, precisely in the midst of this contemplation and examination, there is forbearance, there is view, there is aspiration, there is understanding, and there is the forbearance of careful reflective view — this is what is called summit. One asks: why did the World-Honored One, in speaking to the young Pārāyaṇa disciple, teach faith as summit, while, in speaking to newly ordained monks, he taught wisdom as summit instead? The answer: the Buddha thoroughly understands the characteristics of dharmas and thoroughly understands the capacities of his listeners — whatever ought to be taught to a given person, he teaches accordingly; no one else has this ability, and so the question should not even be raised of anyone but him.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Ānanda sutra's own definition of summit — forbearance, view, aspiration, understanding, and careful reflective forbearance, all arising together in the act of examining the five aggregates as impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self — is closer to the root treatise's technical account (wisdom directed at the four truths) than the faith-based definition given to the young brahmin scholar. The treatise's answer to the apparent inconsistency is disarmingly simple: the Buddha's own teaching authority settles the matter, and no further justification is owed to anyone lacking that same insight into an audience's capacity.

9. Matching the Teaching to the Student

Four further, independent reasons unpack why faith suited the brahmin scholar while wisdom suited the newly ordained monks — differences in prior habituation, in what each lacked, in the particular fault each was at risk of, and in sheer faculty-sharpness — closing with a list of further factors this same asymmetry can be traced through.

Chinese Source

復次波羅衍拏。住初業地未串習所作。未得奢摩他未入聖教。未修漸次。諸有所作皆藉他緣。聞他天神讚佛功德。於佛生信來詣佛所爾時世尊。依頂等流說信為頂。新學苾芻與彼相違。是故為說頂之自性。復次佛隨所闕而為說故。謂波羅衍拏。有慧闕信故為彼說信為頂。新學苾芻有信闕慧故。為彼說慧為頂復次為止諂曲及愚癡故。謂波羅衍拏是婆羅門種。雖有智慧而闕淨信。無信之慧增長諂曲。為止彼諂曲故說信為頂。新學苾芻是釋迦種。雖有淨信而闕智慧。無慧之信增長愚癡。為止彼愚癡故說慧為頂。復次世尊所化有利根者。有鈍根者。為利根者說信為頂。為鈍根者說慧為頂。如利根鈍根。因力緣力。內分力外分力。內如理作意力。外從他聞法力。無癡增相續力。無貪增相續力。應知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further: Pārāyaṇa's disciple was still at the stage of a beginning practitioner, not yet habituated to the practice, not yet having attained calm-abiding, not yet having entered the noble teaching, not yet having cultivated it stage by stage — everything he did still depended on external conditions; hearing a deity praise the Buddha's virtues, he came to have faith in the Buddha and approached him. At that time, the World-Honored One, following an outflow of summit itself, taught faith as summit. The newly ordained monks were the opposite of this, and so the Buddha taught them summit's own essential nature directly. Further: the Buddha teaches according to whatever each listener lacks — Pārāyaṇa's disciple had wisdom but lacked faith, and so the Buddha taught him faith as summit; the newly ordained monks had faith but lacked wisdom, and so the Buddha taught them wisdom as summit. The newly ordained monks were of the Śākya clan, and though they possessed pure faith, they lacked wisdom; faith without wisdom fosters foolishness, and it was to stop this foolishness that the Buddha taught wisdom as summit. Further: among those the World-Honored One taught, some had sharp faculties and some had dull faculties — to those of sharp faculties, he taught faith as summit; to those of dull faculties, he taught wisdom as summit. The same distinction should be understood to apply between the power of cause and the power of condition, the power of internal factors and the power of external factors, the power of proper internal reflection and the power of hearing the dharma externally from another, the power of a continuum reinforced by freedom from delusion, and the power of a continuum reinforced by freedom from greed.

17. Heat's Technical Definition, and a Warning Not Everyone Meets

24. The Essential Nature Shared by All Four Roots

Having explained the asymmetric analysis, the treatise steps back to define what all four nirvedhabhāgīya roots share — the five aggregates as their common essential nature — refuting Ghoṣaka's own view that heat and summit lack a fifth, form aggregate, and closing with the etymology of "nirvedhabhāgīya" itself and its two alternate names.

Chinese Source

如是四種順決擇分。謂煖頂忍世第一法。問如是四種自性云何。答皆以五蘊為其自性。尊者妙音作如是說。順決擇分有欲界繫。有色界繫。欲界繫中下者名煖。上者名頂。此二自性唯有四蘊。欲界中無隨轉色故。色界繫中下者名忍。上者名為世第一法。此二自性皆具五蘊。色界中有隨轉色故。如是說者。此四善根皆是色界。定地修地。行聖行法故四自性皆具五蘊。問若此四種。皆色界繫。云何建立四種別耶。答此四善根。雖同色界。而有可動。有不可動。有有留難。有無留難。有可斷有不可斷。有可慮有不可慮。有可退有不可退諸可動有留難。可斷可慮可退。中下者名煖。上者名頂。諸不可動無留難。無斷無慮不可退。中下者名忍。上者名為世第一法。故此四種雖同色界繫。五蘊為自性而有差別如說自性。我物自體相分。本性亦爾。已說自性當說所以。問此何故名順決擇分。答決擇者。謂聖道。如是四種。是順彼分。順彼分中。此四最勝。是故名為順決擇分。即此四種亦名行諦。亦名修治。亦名善根。行諦者。謂以無常等十六行相遊歷四諦故修治者。謂為求聖道修治身器除去穢惡引起聖道故。猶如農夫為求子實修治田地除去穢草此亦如是。善根者。謂聖道涅槃。是真實善。此四與彼為初基本。為安足處故名為根。

Such, then, are the four nirvedhabhāgīya factors: heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme mundane dharma. One asks: what is the essential nature of these four? The answer: all four have the five aggregates as their essential nature. The Venerable Ghoṣaka speaks as follows: the nirvedhabhāgīya factors are of two kinds, those bound to the desire realm and those bound to the form realm. Among those bound to the desire realm, the lower is named heat and the higher is named summit — these two have only four aggregates as their essential nature, since the desire realm has no accompanying form. Among those bound to the form realm, the lower is named forbearance and the higher is named supreme mundane dharma — these two have all five aggregates as their essential nature, since the form realm does possess accompanying form. On this treatise's own view, all four of these wholesome roots belong to the form realm, to a stage of concentration and cultivation, bearing the aspect of noble conduct — and so all four have all five aggregates as their essential nature alike. One asks: if all four belong to the form realm alike, how are the four distinguished from one another? The answer: although these four wholesome roots share the same realm, they differ in whether they can be shaken or not, whether they involve difficulty or not, whether they can be cut off or not, whether they can be deliberated over or not, and whether they can retrogress or not: whichever can be shaken, involves difficulty, can be cut off, can be deliberated over, and can retrogress — the lower and middle grade of this is named heat, the higher grade named summit; whichever cannot be shaken, involves no difficulty, cannot be cut off, cannot be deliberated over, and cannot retrogress — the lower and middle grade of this is named forbearance, the higher grade named supreme mundane dharma. Thus these four, though sharing the same realm and the same five aggregates as their essential nature, nonetheless differ from one another — just as one speaks of "own-nature," "own substance," "own entity," and "characteristic-portion" all referring to the same underlying thing, "original nature" should be understood in the same way. The essential nature having now been explained, the reason for the name should next be explained. One asks: why are these named "nirvedhabhāgīya," "factors conducive to penetration"? The answer: "penetration" refers to the noble path itself; these four factors are conducive to it — and among all factors conducive to it, these four are supreme — this is why they are named "factors conducive to penetration." These same four are also named "practicing the truths," also named "cultivating and refining," and also named "wholesome roots." "Practicing the truths" means traversing the four truths through the sixteen aspects, impermanence and the rest. "Cultivating and refining" means: in seeking the noble path, one cultivates and refines the vessel of one's own body, removing filth and evil, and drawing forth the noble path — just as a farmer, seeking a harvest, cultivates and refines the soil of his field, removing weeds; the case here is exactly the same. "Wholesome roots" means: the noble path and nirvāṇa are the truest wholesome things, and these four serve as their initial foundation and footing — this is why they are named "roots."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The retrogression-based classification here — shakable-with-difficulty against unshakable-without — is the same one already established in Section 1, now generalized into the treatise's full explanation for why four dharmas sharing an identical essential nature and identical realm still count as four distinct things: the classification runs entirely on function and stability, not on any difference of underlying substance, exactly the "own-nature, own substance, own entity" analogy the passage reaches for to make the point vivid. And the farmer clearing weeds before a harvest is a fitting image to close on: cultivation, refining, wholesome-rootedness — three names for the same four factors — all describe preparation, not the harvest itself, which remains the noble path still to come.

25. Grades of the Four Roots — Four Named Masters Compared

The treatise now asks how many grades each of the four roots divides into, and four named masters — the compiler's own view, then Ghoṣaka, Buddhadeva, and Vasumitra — each propose a different, increasingly fine-grained count, none of them chosen as definitive.

Every one of these four schemes agrees on the one fact this whole fascicle has been building toward: supreme mundane dharma occupies exactly one grade, upper-upper, no matter how finely the other three roots get subdivided — a nine-grade system, a nineteen-grade system (Ghoṣaka's 3+6+8+1+1), a nine-grade system with different boundaries (Buddhadeva), all converge on the same singular peak. The masters disagree substantially about how gradual the climb is, but not at all about where it ends.

26. More Differences Among the Four Roots

The treatise closes its comparative survey with a compact catalog of further differences among the four roots — by name, by conditioning relationship, and by what each delights in individually contemplating — echoing, in miniature, the same paired structure Fascicle 5 already used to distinguish forbearance from heat and summit.

27. Gaining and Losing Each Root — the Devadatta Problem

The fascicle closes with its most concrete and consequential question: what does each root actually confer, and what happens when even a wholesome root fails to protect its holder? The case of Devadatta — the Buddha's own cousin, who committed the gravest offenses — forces the treatise to distinguish carefully between never truly attaining a stage and genuinely retrogressing from one.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fish that has swallowed a hook is a precise image for heat's own irreversible benefit: the fish may still swim freely, seemingly unharmed, exactly as one who has attained heat may still commit terrible acts — but the hook is already set, and eventual capture (here, eventual nirvāṇa) is now simply a matter of time, whatever thrashing happens in between. The Devadatta problem this section works through — how can a wholesome root guarantee anything, if its most famous possible holder went on to commit the very acts it was supposed to prevent? — gets resolved the same way each time: by denying he ever actually held the root in question, redefining his fall as a failure to arrive rather than a fall from having arrived. It is a solution that preserves the root's guarantee at the cost of a slightly awkward retrofit onto a well-known figure's biography, and the treatise's willingness to try three different versions of this same move, without settling on one, suggests even the compilers found it a little strained.
