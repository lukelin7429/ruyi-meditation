Brahma-Merit, Immeasurable

Fascicle 82 details the preliminary practice for the four immeasurables across seven categories of sentient beings, the Buddha's own seven years of loving-kindness and the bodhisattva Great Courage, Sunetra's disciples and why the immeasurables are named brahma-abidings, and an extended dispute over the four persons who generate brahma-merit and how such merit might be measured. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Preliminary Practice: Seven Categories of Sentient Beings

The practical cultivation of the four immeasurables follows a precise, graduated sequence across seven categories of sentient beings — the hostile, the beloved, and the neutral, the hostile and beloved each further divided into inferior, middling, and superior grades. Loving-kindness begins with the superior beloved — one's own parents or teachers — since the mind, stubborn and habituated since beginningless time, resists holding a wholesome disposition even toward those who have shown the greatest kindness, exactly as a mustard seed cast at the tip of an awl may momentarily adhere but finds it difficult to remain fixed there without prolonged, skillful practice. Only once this intention has stood firm does the practitioner proceed, grade by grade, through the middling and inferior beloved, the neutral category, and finally the inferior, middling, and superior enemy, until the very same bestowing intention flows equally toward every sentient being without exception. Equanimity instead begins with the neutral category, since a beloved being provokes attachment and an enemy provokes aversion, and works outward toward the enemy first and the beloved last, since a mind of aversion proves easier to set aside than a mind of affection.

Chinese Source

問此四無量加行云何。答緣七有情而起加行。七有情者。謂分欲界一切有情為怨親中三品差別。怨親二品。復各分三謂下中上。中品有情總為一種無差別故。於此七品有情境中。若欲修慈先緣親品。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: what is the preliminary practice for these four immeasurables? The answer: the preliminary practice arises by taking seven kinds of sentient being as its object. As for these seven kinds of sentient being: this refers to dividing every sentient being of the desire realm into three distinct categories — the hostile, the beloved, and the neutral — the hostile and the beloved each further divided into three, namely, inferior, middling, and superior, the neutral beings being gathered together as a single, undifferentiated kind. Among these seven kinds of sentient being as one's object, if one wishes to cultivate loving-kindness, one first takes the beloved category as one's object,

於親品中先緣上品。上品親者。謂自父母軌範親教。或餘隨一可尊重處。智慧多聞同梵行者。於此上品親有情境作是思惟。云何當令此有情類得如是樂。然心剛強難可調伏。從無始來串習成故。於極有恩諸有情所惡阿世耶任運生長。善阿世耶雖作意起而不能住。復應勇勵思其重恩制心令住。如以芥子投於錐鋒。雖有著時而難可住。久習不已加行乃成。由善巧力投之方住。如是行者於上品。親要勤修習與樂意樂。經於多時乃得堅住。於上品親與樂意樂得堅住已。次於中親復修如是與樂意樂。此既成已次於下親復修如是與樂意樂。此既成已次於中品。次於下怨。次於中怨。後於上怨。各修如是與樂意樂。漸次修習至成滿時。普於欲界一切有情。與樂意樂平等相續。如於上親。上怨亦爾。齊此名為修慈究竟。修悲修喜次第亦然。拔苦慶慰意樂有別。云何當令此有情類。離如是苦是悲意樂。此有情類得樂離苦。豈不快哉是喜意樂。欲修捨時先緣中品。

and, among the beloved category, one first takes the superior grade as one's object. As for a superior beloved: this refers to one's own father, mother, preceptor, or instructor, or any other person deserving of honor and respect, one of great wisdom and broad learning, a fellow practitioner of the holy life. Toward this superior beloved sentient being as one's object, one reflects as follows: how might I cause this kind of sentient being to attain such pleasure as this? Yet the mind is stubborn and difficult to subdue, having become habituated in this way since beginningless time — toward sentient beings who have shown one great kindness, an evil disposition arises effortlessly and grows, while a wholesome disposition, though given rise to through mental application, is unable to remain. One must, further, rouse oneself vigorously, reflecting on this great kindness, forcing the mind to remain fixed there, exactly as a mustard seed, cast at the tip of an awl, may momentarily adhere but finds it difficult to remain — only through prolonged, unceasing practice does this preliminary practice come to be accomplished, and it is only by a skillful application of force that the seed, once cast, comes to remain fixed there. So too, a practitioner, with respect to a superior beloved, must diligently cultivate and practice the very intention of bestowing pleasure, and only after a long span of time does this intention come to stand firm. Once the intention of bestowing pleasure toward a superior beloved has come to stand firm, one next cultivates this same intention of bestowing pleasure toward a middling beloved; once this has been accomplished, one next cultivates this same intention of bestowing pleasure toward an inferior beloved; once this has been accomplished, one next cultivates it toward the neutral category, then toward an inferior enemy, then toward a middling enemy, and, last of all, toward a superior enemy, cultivating this same intention of bestowing pleasure toward each in turn. Gradually practicing in this way, until the moment of its full completion, one comes to bestow this same intention of pleasure equally and continuously upon every sentient being of the desire realm without exception — exactly as toward a superior beloved, so too toward a superior enemy — and it is at this point that the cultivation of loving-kindness is named complete. The sequence for cultivating compassion and sympathetic joy follows in just the same way, though the intention differs — removing suffering, or rejoicing and comforting: how might I cause this kind of sentient being to become free of such suffering as this? — this being the intention of compassion; may this kind of sentient being attain pleasure and become free of suffering — is this not truly delightful? — this being the intention of sympathetic joy. When one wishes to cultivate equanimity, one first takes the neutral category as one's object,

謂於彼起捨置意樂。中品有情最易捨故。緣親發愛。緣怨發瞋。故緣處中。初修於捨。捨中品已。次捨下怨。次捨中怨。次捨上怨。

giving rise, toward it, to the intention of setting all aside, since a neutral being is easiest to set aside — taking a beloved being as object gives rise to attachment, taking an enemy as object gives rise to aversion, and it is for this reason that one first cultivates equanimity by taking what lies between the two as one's object. Having set aside the neutral category, one next sets aside an inferior enemy, then a middling enemy, then a superior enemy,

次捨下親。次捨中親。次捨上親。先捨其怨。後捨親者瞋心易捨非愛心故。漸次修習至成滿時。普於欲界一切有情。捨置意樂平等相續。無異分別猶如持秤。緣有情類如總觀林。齊此名為修捨究竟。

then an inferior beloved, then a middling beloved, and, last of all, a superior beloved — setting aside the enemy first and the beloved last, since a mind of aversion is more easily set aside than a mind of affection. Gradually practicing in this way, until the moment of its full completion, one comes to bestow this same intention of setting all aside, equally and continuously, upon every sentient being of the desire realm without exception, admitting of no further discrimination whatsoever, exactly as one holds a scale evenly, or takes every sentient being as one's object exactly as one views an entire forest as a single whole, and it is at this point that the cultivation of equanimity is named complete.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The mustard-seed-on-an-awl image captures something genuinely difficult about cultivating loving-kindness that a purely doctrinal description might obscure: the problem was never a lack of goodwill in the abstract, but the sheer instability of holding a wholesome intention in place even toward the very people who have earned it most, exactly as the seed's own difficulty is not resistance to being placed there at all, but simply staying put once it has landed.

2. Who Can Cultivate the Immeasurables?

Sentient beings divide, broadly, into two dispositions: those who delight in seeking out another's faults, and those who delight in seeking out another's merits. The former are largely incapable of cultivating the four immeasurables, since even an arhat's own faults can still be found by someone determined to look for them; the latter are largely capable, since even one who has cut off his own wholesome roots entirely can still be credited with the merit of some earlier, more virtuous life that has since borne fruit as an honored birth and a dignified bearing.

Chinese Source

問何等有情能修無量。答有情種性略有二種。一於有情樂求過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: what kind of sentient being is capable of cultivating the immeasurables? The answer: the disposition of sentient beings is, broadly speaking, of two kinds: first, one who delights in seeking out another's faults;

二於有情樂求功德。若於有情樂求失者。於四無量多不能修。所以者何。阿羅漢等欲求其失亦可得故。彼於先時亦有瑕隙。故令我等今輕毀之。誰能於彼作饒益事。若於有情樂求德者。於四無量多分能修。所以者何。斷善根者欲求其德亦可得故。彼於先時多修善業。故今感得尊貴家生形貌端嚴眾所樂見。言詞威肅聞皆敬受。智慧多聞人皆推仰。我應於彼作饒益事。

second, one who delights in seeking out another's merits. Whoever delights in seeking out another's faults is, for the most part, unable to cultivate the four immeasurables, for what reason is this so? Because even an arhat's own faults can still be sought out and found by such a one — he was once, at an earlier time, not without some flaw or gap, and it is on this account that we now belittle and disparage him — so who, holding such an attitude, could possibly perform a beneficial deed for another? Whoever delights in seeking out another's merits is, for the most part, able to cultivate the four immeasurables, for what reason is this so? Because even one who has cut off his own wholesome roots can still have his merits sought out and found by such a one — he was once, at an earlier time, one who cultivated much wholesome karma, and it is on this account that he now attains rebirth in an honored family, with a form and appearance dignified and pleasing to behold, his very words carrying an awe-inspiring dignity that all who hear them receive with respect, his wisdom and broad learning admired and looked up to by all — I ought to perform a beneficial deed for him.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even a person who has cut off his own wholesome roots can still be found to possess genuine merit worth crediting is a striking application of this treatise's own principle that a being's present moral state never exhausts what is knowable or creditable about him: the fault-seeker and the merit-seeker are not simply optimist and pessimist looking at the same facts, but two people who have trained themselves to notice entirely different aspects of the very same life.

3. What Is Their True Sequence? Saṃghavasu's Mutual Restraint

Do the four immeasurables genuinely arise in the sequence scripture states — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity — or does some other order hold in practice? One view defends the stated sequence directly, mapping each immeasurable's own characteristic onto the natural progression of caring for another (bestowing benefit, then removing loss, then rejoicing, then setting aside); another proposes instead that compassion comes first, followed by loving-kindness, joy, and equanimity. The Venerable Saṃghavasu offers a subtler account: compassion and joy mutually restrain one another, since compassion lowers the mind and thus requires joy to spur it upward again, while joy raises the mind and thus requires compassion to rein it back in. The compilers reject any single fixed sequence outright: a practitioner of contemplation arises according to his own inclination, some beginning with loving-kindness and ending with equanimity, others beginning with equanimity and ending with loving-kindness, still others attaining only one of the four and no other, or entering them in forward or reverse sequence, or by transcendence, exactly as with the liberations, masteries, and totalities.

Chinese Source

問此四無量次第云何。為如說而生為別有次第。有作是說。如說而生。謂瑜伽師先於欲界諸有情類欲與饒益。與饒益者即是慈相。故佛說慈以為第一。次於欲界諸有情類欲除衰損。除衰損者即是悲相。故佛說悲以為第二。彼諸有情既得饒益復離衰損。次應於彼而生慶慰。慶慰彼者即是喜相。故佛說喜以為第三。既於有情生慶慰已。次應於彼平等捨置。等捨置者即是捨相。故佛說捨以為第四。

Ru-Yi Translation

One asks: what is the sequence of these four immeasurables? Do they arise exactly as stated, or does some other sequence exist? There are those who say: they arise exactly as stated — that is, a practitioner of yoga first wishes to bestow benefit upon every sentient being of the desire realm; bestowing benefit is precisely the characteristic of loving-kindness, and it is for this reason that the Buddha speaks of loving-kindness as first. Next, he wishes to remove decline and loss from every sentient being of the desire realm; removing decline and loss is precisely the characteristic of compassion, and it is for this reason that the Buddha speaks of compassion as second. Once these sentient beings have already obtained benefit and become free of decline and loss, one ought next to give rise to rejoicing and comfort with respect to them; rejoicing and comforting them is precisely the characteristic of sympathetic joy, and it is for this reason that the Buddha speaks of sympathetic joy as third. Having already given rise to rejoicing and comfort with respect to sentient beings, one ought next to set them all equally aside; setting all equally aside is precisely the characteristic of equanimity, and it is for this reason that the Buddha speaks of equanimity as fourth.

故四無量如說而生。復有說者。此四無量先悲次慈次喜後捨。謂瑜伽師先於欲界諸有情類欲除衰損。次復於彼欲與饒益。次復於彼深生慶慰。最後於彼平等捨置。尊者僧伽筏蘇說曰。悲喜二種互相制御。若先起悲次必生喜。悲令心下須喜策故。若先生喜次必起悲。

It is for this reason that the four immeasurables arise exactly as stated. Others, further, say: these four immeasurables arise first as compassion, next as loving-kindness, next as sympathetic joy, and last as equanimity — that is, a practitioner of yoga first wishes to remove decline and loss from every sentient being of the desire realm; next wishes to bestow benefit upon them; next gives rise to deep rejoicing and comfort with respect to them; and, last of all, sets them equally aside. The Venerable Saṃghavasu speaks as follows: compassion and sympathetic joy mutually restrain one another — if compassion arises first, joy must certainly follow, since compassion lowers the mind, requiring joy to spur it upward;

喜令心舉須悲制故。評曰。應作是說。非四無量如說而生。所以者何。修觀行者隨樂生故。有觀行者先起於慈。次悲次喜後起於捨。

if joy arises first, compassion must certainly follow, since joy raises the mind, requiring compassion to restrain it. The compilers say: one should say as follows — the four immeasurables do not arise exactly as stated, for what reason is this so? Because a practitioner giving rise to contemplation arises according to his own pleasure — there exists a practitioner of contemplation who first gives rise to loving-kindness, then compassion, then sympathetic joy, and only afterward gives rise to equanimity, and so on, stated at full length, up through,

廣說乃至有觀行者先起於捨。次喜次悲後起於慈。或有不定有觀行者得慈非餘。廣說乃至有觀行者得捨非餘。或有不定非四無量有順次入。或逆次入。或順超入。或逆超入。如通解脫勝處遍處問若未起初靜慮無量。能起第二靜慮無量不。乃至若未起第三靜慮無量。

there exists a practitioner of contemplation who first gives rise to equanimity, then sympathetic joy, then compassion, and only afterward gives rise to loving-kindness. There exists, further, an indeterminate case, where a practitioner of contemplation attains loving-kindness and no other, and so on, stated at full length, up through, there exists a practitioner of contemplation who attains equanimity and no other. There exists, further, an indeterminate case where the four immeasurables are not entered in forward sequence, or entered in reverse sequence, or entered by forward transcendence, or entered by reverse transcendence, exactly as with the pervasive liberations, masteries, and totalities. One asks: if one has not yet given rise to the first dhyāna's own immeasurable, is one capable of giving rise to the second dhyāna's own immeasurable? And so on, up through, if one has not yet given rise to the third dhyāna's own immeasurable, is one capable of giving rise to the fourth dhyāna's own immeasurable?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Saṃghavasu's own image of compassion and joy mutually restraining one another like counterweights is a genuinely dynamic account of how these two immeasurables might interact in practice, in contrast to the static, once-and-for-all sequence the other two views propose — and the compilers' own final verdict, that no fixed order exists at all, is itself an implicit endorsement of exactly this dynamism: if compassion and joy really do check each other in the way Saṃghavasu describes, then which one arises first in a given practitioner's own experience could never be settled by doctrine alone.

4. Does a Lower Ground's Immeasurable Come First? Mental Application

A further question asks whether one must give rise to a lower dhyāna's own immeasurable before a higher one becomes possible. One view insists on strict prerequisite, the lower serving as the very gateway and stairway to the higher; another denies this, since a practitioner already possessing mastery over a given ground may give rise to its own immeasurable directly, exactly as one may give rise to a higher ground's own undefiled path without first having given rise to a lower ground's own. It is, in any case, swifter to descend from a higher ground's immeasurable to a lower one than to ascend from a lower to a higher, exactly as, having first learned the Brahmī script, learning the Kharoṣṭhī script afterward comes easily, while the reverse order does not.

Chinese Source

能起第四靜慮無量不。有說。不能初靜慮無量與第二靜慮無量。為加行門依梯隥故。乃至第三靜慮無量與第四靜慮無量為加行門依梯隥故。有說。亦能謂觀行者。若依此地得自在力即依此地先起無量。未起下地無漏聖道。尚起上地無漏聖道。況四無量而不能起。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are those who say: one is not capable, since the first dhyāna's own immeasurable serves as the very gateway of preliminary practice, the ladder and stairway, for the second dhyāna's own immeasurable, and so on, up through, the third dhyāna's own immeasurable serves as the very gateway of preliminary practice, the ladder and stairway, for the fourth dhyāna's own immeasurable. There are those who say: one is indeed capable — that is, a practitioner of contemplation, if he possesses mastery in dependence upon a given ground, may, in dependence upon that very ground, first give rise to its immeasurable — since, even without having given rise to a lower ground's own undefiled noble path, he may still give rise to a higher ground's own undefiled noble path, how much more so with the four immeasurables, which he ought certainly to be capable of giving rise to.

問為下地無量後起上地無量速疾。為上地無量後起下地無量速疾耶。答上地無量後起下地無量速疾。非下地無量後起上地無量速疾。如學梵書後學佉盧瑟吒書速疾。非學佉盧瑟吒書後學梵書速疾。問初靜慮無量無間。即能起第二靜慮無量不。乃至第三靜慮無量無間。即能起第四靜慮無量不。依逆次第為問亦爾。有說不能必修自地加行引發方現前故。有說。亦能已熟修者起一加行。或無加行能歷諸地。或上或下起無量故。問慈無量等無間即能起悲無量等不。答如定蘊說。思惟何等入慈等至。謂樂有情乃至思惟何等入捨等至。謂捨有情。有作是說。彼說無量俱生行相。復有說者。彼說無量等無間緣。若作是說彼說無量俱生行相。慈無量等無間能生悲無量等。若作是說彼說無量等無間緣。慈無量等無間不能生悲無量等四種加行。各有差別自加行後現在前故。應知作意略有三種。一自相作意。二共相作意。三勝解作意。自相作意者。如有思惟地為堅相。水為濕相。火為煖相。風為動相。如是一切。共相作意者。

One asks: is it swifter to give rise to a higher ground's own immeasurable after a lower ground's own, or swifter to give rise to a lower ground's own immeasurable after a higher ground's own? The answer: it is swifter to give rise to a lower ground's own immeasurable after a higher ground's own, not swifter to give rise to a higher ground's own immeasurable after a lower ground's own — exactly as, having first learned the Brahmī script, it is swifter afterward to learn the Kharoṣṭhī script, not swifter to learn the Brahmī script after first learning the Kharoṣṭhī script. One asks: immediately upon the first dhyāna's own immeasurable, is one capable of giving rise directly to the second dhyāna's own immeasurable? And so on, up through, immediately upon the third dhyāna's own immeasurable, is one capable of giving rise directly to the fourth dhyāna's own immeasurable? And this question, too, should be asked following the reverse sequence. There are those who say: one is not capable, since one must certainly first cultivate one's own ground's own preliminary practice before it can be drawn forth and manifest before one. There are those who say: one is indeed capable — that is, one who has already thoroughly cultivated and matured this practice may give rise to it through but a single preliminary practice, or even without any preliminary practice at all, being capable of passing through every ground, whether higher or lower, and giving rise to the immeasurable there. One asks: immediately upon the immeasurable of loving-kindness and the like, is one capable of giving rise directly to the immeasurable of compassion and the like? The answer: as the Concentration Aggregate states, by contemplating what does one enter the attainment of loving-kindness, and so on? It refers to sentient beings who are to be given pleasure, and so on, up through, by contemplating what does one enter the attainment of equanimity? It refers to sentient beings who are to be set aside. There are those who say: this passage speaks of the immeasurables' own co-arising aspect. Others, further, say: this passage speaks of the immeasurables' own condition of immediately preceding continuity. If one speaks in the former way, then immediately upon the immeasurable of loving-kindness and the like, one is indeed capable of giving rise to the immeasurable of compassion and the like; if one speaks in the latter way, then immediately upon the immeasurable of loving-kindness and the like, one is not capable of giving rise to the immeasurable of compassion and the like, each of the four preliminary practices possessing its own distinct character, manifesting before one only after its own corresponding preliminary practice. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The asymmetry between descending and ascending — swift in one direction, effortful in the other — mirrors a pattern this treatise has already established elsewhere for meditative attainments generally: mastery gained at a more refined level transfers downward with ease, exactly as a skill learned in its more demanding form generalizes readily to simpler applications, while the reverse transfer, from coarse to subtle, requires the harder work of genuine refinement.

5. The Immeasurables Are Provisional Constructions Alone

Mental application itself divides into three kinds: toward a particular characteristic, such as reflecting that earth is solid and water is moist; toward a shared characteristic, such as the sixteen noble aspects taken together; and through resolved conviction, such as the contemplation of impurity or the liberations and totalities. The four immeasurables belong solely to this third kind, since they arise through an imagined, provisional construction rather than through observing any dharma's own genuine, particular nature.

Chinese Source

如十六聖行相俱生作意等。勝解作意者。如不淨觀持息念解脫勝處遍處等俱生作意。問此四無量於三種中。為與何等作意俱生。答唯與勝解作意俱生假想起故。

Ru-Yi Translation

this refers to the co-arising mental application found within the sixteen noble aspects, and the like. As for mental application through resolved conviction: this refers to the co-arising mental application found within the contemplation of impurity, mindfulness of breathing, the liberations, the masteries, the totalities, and the like. One asks: among these three kinds, with which does the co-arising mental application of these four immeasurables belong? The answer: solely with mental application through resolved conviction, since they arise through an imagined, provisional construction.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Classifying the immeasurables as resolved-conviction mental application rather than observation of a particular or shared characteristic is a quietly important admission about their own epistemic status: loving-kindness and compassion do not report some objective fact about how things already stand, but actively construct, through deliberate imaginative effort, a wish about how things ought to be — which is precisely why the following sections can debate at such length whether this construction counts as a distortion without ever threatening the immeasurables' own genuine wholesome value.

6. What Pleasure Does Loving-Kindness Bestow? The Wealthy and the Poor

Precisely what pleasure does the immeasurable of loving-kindness take as its object to bestow upon sentient beings? Candidate answers multiply and each founders on the same objection — that it would then restrict loving-kindness to only those practitioners, or only those sentient beings, capable of that specific pleasure. The Great Virtuous One offers the most durable resolution: at the stage of preliminary practice, a practitioner recalls whatever pleasure he has actually witnessed in others, and, with a heart of compassionate pity, forms the resolved wish that every sentient being might attain it — the very scene painted in vivid detail, a monk begging for alms who passes both those enjoying the utmost luxury, riding elephants and adorned with jewels, and those enduring the utmost destitution, disheveled and hungry like hungry ghosts, and who, returning to his seat, lets this recollected contrast give rise to compassionate wishing for all. Since the beings taken as object plainly do not all in fact attain this pleasure, is the whole contemplation not simply a distortion? No — because it arises together with benevolent intention, wise reflection, and wholesome roots, and a distortion requires both a distorted object and a distorted own-nature together, while loving-kindness possesses only the former.

7. Pervading the Directions: Spatial Region or the Boundary of Beings?

A canonical formula describes loving-kindness pervading the four directions, above and below, this entire world, taking every sentient being as its object — yet does this observe by the boundary of a spatial region, or by the boundary of sentient beings themselves? Either answer runs into trouble: a spatial boundary seems to contradict the scripture's own claim to take every sentient being, without remainder, as its object; a boundary of sentient beings themselves seems to require an separately attainable "edge" to the ocean of sentient beings that no one has ever actually reached. The resolution distinguishes a partial totality from an absolute totality, or notes that the boundary of sentient beings, though never separately attained, may still be attained comprehensively — exactly as the four modes of birth gather in every sentient being without exception, with no single being left outside them, even though no one has ever catalogued that totality one being at a time. The compilers ultimately decline to settle the matter with any single fixed answer, since the immeasurables are, throughout, provisional and imaginative constructions.

8. The Buddha's Seven Years of Loving-Kindness

The Buddha recalls that cultivating a mind of loving-kindness for seven years kept him from rebirth in this world for seven entire cycles of cosmic contraction and expansion, becoming successively a god of pure radiant light, the Great Brahmā King, Śakra thirty-six times over, and a wheel-turning monarch innumerable times. The treatise identifies these "seven years" as seven rainy seasons in the life of a former bodhisattva-king named Great Courage, who retreated each rainy season to a cool forest to cultivate the four immeasurables while entrusting his kingdom to his ministers, and who, upon each season's end, returned to the capital to give lavishly and uphold the precepts, repeating this cycle six times before a seventh season brought either the end of his natural life or the very moment of the world's own contraction. A genuine puzzle follows: since the immeasurables he cultivated are bound to the form realm, how could this karma possibly draw forth the desire-realm fruits of Śakra and a wheel-turning monarch? The resolution distinguishes three immeasurables cultivated at three distinct levels — one bound to the desire realm itself, yielding kingship and Śakra's own throne; one bound to the first dhyāna, yielding Brahmā-kingship; and one bound to the second dhyāna, yielding rebirth among the gods of pure radiant light.

A memorable verse celebrates merit's own durability: not burned by fire, not shattered by wind, not dissolved by water, capable of standing firm against king or bandit alike, invaded by neither man nor spirit, suffering no loss whatsoever, exactly like a firm and hidden treasury, and, by the sheer power of its own determination, capable of drawing forth pleasure in both this world and the next. 11. Sunetra's Disciples, and Why Called "Brahma-Abiding"

A striking scripture recounts the teacher Sunetra, whose disciples, depending on how completely they fulfilled the rules governing the four brahma-abidings, were reborn either in the Brahmā world or among the six heavens of desire and wealthy human families — provoking the awkward question of why Sunetra's own teaching, by this measure, seems to outperform the Buddha's, whose own imperfect disciples fall instead into the evil destinies. The resolution insists the two teachers cannot be measured against one another at all: even the lowest of the Buddha's disciples, a stream-enterer, already surpasses Sunetra, who was, in any case, the Buddha's own former self in an earlier bodhisattva life, teaching brahmins who already longed for the Brahmā world exactly the path suited to that longing, while the Buddha's own precepts govern an entirely different, more exacting standard whose violation carries a correspondingly graver consequence. Sunetra himself, wishing not to share his own disciples' more modest destination, cultivated the second dhyāna's own "superior loving-kindness" and was reborn among the gods of pure radiant light instead — a designation of "superior" that the treatise carefully qualifies: superior only relative to the first dhyāna he taught his disciples, or relative to what he had himself formerly attained, not superior in any absolute sense to what a genuinely higher ground could offer. The immeasurables earn the name "brahma-abiding" because the first dhyāna, the Brahmā world's own dwelling-place, is the very first ground to possess all four in full, because they serve as the near antidote to what is not brahma-like and to what is not the holy life, and because cultivating them draws forth rebirth as the Great Brahmā King himself.

12. Four Persons Who Generate Brahma-Merit: A Dispute

A scripture names four kinds of person capable of generating brahma-merit: erecting a stūpa for the Buddha's relics where none stood before, establishing a monastery for his disciples where none stood before, reconciling a divided monastic assembly, and cultivating the four brahma-abidings. The Dārṣṭāntikas challenge the scripture's own authenticity outright, arguing that the first three cannot possibly generate equal merit regardless of scale — a grand, jewel-adorned memorial shrine at the very sites of the Buddha's birth, enlightenment, and final nirvāṇa surely cannot be worth the same as a small heap of sand and stone raised at the same spots, nor can reconciling Devadatta's own historic schism be worth the same as settling some minor squabble among monks at Kauśāmbī — concluding that only the fourth item, the brahma-abidings themselves, was genuinely spoken by the Buddha. The Abhidharma masters defend the scripture's full authenticity instead, insisting all four generate equal brahma-merit since what is intended in each case is equal, regardless of the scale of what is actually accomplished.

14. Measuring a Single Unit of Brahma-Merit

A final puzzle asks what quantity of ordinary karma equals a single unit of this brahma-merit — proposal after proposal is offered (the karma sufficient for a wheel-turning monarch's fruit, or for Śakra's, or for the highest heavenly king's, or for Brahmā's own kingship, or for the world's own cosmic completion) and each is entertained in turn, including a vivid account of exactly when the Great Brahmā King himself first earned this merit: not at the moment he resolved to entreat the Buddha, nor even at the moment he genuinely made his request (since his mind then was merely karmically neutral, bearing no ripening-fruit at all), but only later, upon hearing that his entreaty had actually borne fruit — the five monks converted, eighty thousand gods attaining the seeing of the truths, the news finally reaching his own palace and filling him with the joyful reflection that he had accomplished exactly what he set out to do. The compilers close the entire discussion by rejecting every proposed measure as mere praise rather than exact reckoning: brahma-merit, in truth, is immeasurable and boundless, precisely because it is drawn forth by a vast, expansive intention that no finite unit could ever capture.

One should say as follows: the Great Brahmā King, having already entreated the Buddha, returned to his own Brahmā palace, and, afterward, the World-Honored One turned the wheel of the true dharma, whereupon the assembly of five monks and eighty thousand gods all attained the seeing of the truths, and, the various spirits proclaiming this news from one to another until the sound reached the Brahmā palace, the Brahmā King, hearing this, leapt for joy, reflecting as follows: it is by my own entreating the Buddha to turn the wheel of the true dharma that boundless kinds of sentient beings have been benefited — what I myself ought to have done, I have now, with this very thought, already accomplished — and it was at this very moment that the Brahmā King obtained his brahma-merit. The compilers say: among these various statements, every proposed measure of a single unit of brahma-merit is merely a way of praising this brahma-merit in words, and is not, in fact, an exact reckoning; in reality, this brahma-merit is immeasurable and boundless, since it is drawn forth by a vast, expansive intention and aspiration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The precise moment the compilers locate for Brahmā's own earning of merit — neither the resolve nor the request itself, but the later moment of learning that the request had actually succeeded — makes a subtle claim about where genuine karmic weight resides: it is not the mere formation of an intention, nor even its outward expression, but the intention's own confirmed efficacy in the world that finally crystallizes into merit, a discipline about causation that the compilers' own closing move — declaring the true measure immeasurable after all — pointedly refuses to over-specify any further.
