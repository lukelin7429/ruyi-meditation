The Measure of a Moment, and the Body's Own Undoing

Fascicle 136 measures a single moment of mind, a full year's calendar of daylight, and the scale of the cosmos from the ultimate atom to the tallest god, before defending the reality of the four causal conditions in exhaustive detail and closing with the vivid corpse-contemplation meditations on the body's own decay. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. A New Chapter: The Smallest Unit of Time

Fascicle 136 opens the third part of the Full-Vision Nyāsa by turning from the eon down to its smallest constituent: the arising, abiding, and perishing phases of a single moment of mind, measured in kṣaṇas, lavas, and muhūrtas. The treatise pauses to defend its own order of exposition — explaining the coarse, eon-scale units before the fine, moment-scale ones — as a deliberate pedagogical choice, letting students enter the subject by gradual stages rather than being handed the most difficult material first.

Chinese Source

大種蘊第五中具見納息第三之三心起住滅分名何法。答此增語所顯。剎那臘縛牟呼栗多。問此應半月等前說。所以者何。由剎那等積成晝夜。晝夜積成半月月等半月月等積成劫故。何故前說麁後說細耶。答彼作論者意欲爾故。乃至廣說。有作是說。阿毘達磨應以相求不以先後。但不違法相隨說無失。有餘師說。此作論中先說麁後說細令諸學者漸次入故。此中起分。謂生住分。謂老。滅分。謂無常。有為法有三分齊。謂時色名。時之極少。謂一剎那。色之極少。謂一極微。名之極少。謂依一字。積此以為漸多分齊名分齊。如雜蘊說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth Aggregate, the Great Elements: the Full-Vision Nyāsa, Chapter Three, Part Three. What dharma is meant by the arising, abiding, and perishing phases of mind? Answer: this is a designation referring to the moment (kṣaṇa), the lava, and the muhūrta. Question: should this not have been explained before the earlier discussion of the half-month and the rest? For why is this? Since the kṣaṇa and the rest accumulate into a day and night, a day and night accumulates into a half-month, a month, and so on, and a half-month, a month, and so on accumulate into an eon — why, then, does the passage explain the coarse first and the fine only afterward? Answer: it is simply the intention of the treatise's own author, that this order be followed — and so on, in full. Some say: Abhidharma ought to proceed by seeking out characteristics, not by strict sequence — so long as this does not contradict the proper form of the Dharma, whatever order is followed incurs no fault. Other masters say: this treatise deliberately explains the coarse first and the fine only afterward, so that students may enter the subject by gradual stages. Here, the "arising phase" refers to birth; the "abiding phase" refers to aging; the "perishing phase" refers to impermanence. Conditioned dharmas have three boundary-measures: time, form, and name. Time's smallest unit is a single kṣaṇa; form's smallest unit is a single ultimate atom; name's smallest unit is a single syllable — and it is by accumulating these that progressively larger boundary-measures are named, exactly as explained in the Aggregation Nyāsa.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the treatise feels obligated to justify explaining big units before small ones is a small but telling admission: this tradition does not treat its own expository order as self-evidently correct, but as a teaching choice open to challenge and defense, exactly like any other claim it makes.

2. How Fast Is a Moment of Mind? The Buddha's Own Refusal to Say

A cascade of ever-finer attempts to measure the kṣaṇa follows — a woman's spun wool-fiber, a snapped finger, threads of Kāśī cloth torn or cut by strong men — each proposal judged too coarse in turn, until the treatise arrives at its own startling conclusion: the Buddha himself never actually stated the true measure of a kṣaṇa at all. The famous sūtra passage explains why, through an escalating relay of swiftness — a marksman's catch, then earth-treading yakṣas, sky-treading yakṣas, the retinue of the Four Great Kings, the sun and moon, and finally the deity who guides their very chariots — with the arising and perishing of a lifespan's own formations declared swifter than even the fastest of these, and Śāriputra himself, though capable of understanding the true measure, spared the explanation simply because it would have served no purpose.

Chinese Source

問彼剎那量云何可知。有作是言。施設論說。如中年女緝績毳時。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: how, then, can the measure of a kṣaṇa itself be known? Some say: as the Prajñapti-śāstra explains — it is like a middle-aged woman spinning fine wool thread,

抖擻細毛不長不短。齊此說為怛剎那量。彼不欲說毳縷短長。但說毳毛從指間出。隨所出量是怛剎那。問前問剎那。何緣乃引施設論。說怛剎那量。答此中舉麁以顯於細。以細難知不可顯故。謂百二十剎那成一怛剎那。六十怛剎那成一臘縛。此有七千二百剎那。

— and shaking out the fine wool-fibers, neither too long nor too short: this measure is called a tatkṣaṇa. This is not meant to describe the length of the wool-thread itself, but rather says that whatever amount of wool-fiber emerges from between the fingers in a single motion constitutes one tatkṣaṇa. Question: since the question concerned the kṣaṇa, why does the passage cite the Prajñapti-śāstra's own account of the tatkṣaṇa's measure instead? Answer: this cites the coarse in order to reveal the fine, since the fine is difficult to know and cannot be shown directly. That is: one hundred twenty kṣaṇas make one tatkṣaṇa; sixty tatkṣaṇas make one lava — this comprises seven thousand two hundred kṣaṇas.

三十臘縛成一牟呼栗多。此有二百一十六千剎那。三十牟呼栗多成一晝夜。此有少二十不滿六十五百千剎那。此五蘊身一晝一夜。經於爾所生滅無常。有說。此麁非剎那量。如我義者。如壯士彈指頃經六十四剎那。有說。不然。如我義者。如二壯夫掣斷眾多迦尸細縷。隨爾所縷斷經爾所剎那。有說。不然。如我義者。如二壯夫執挽眾多迦尸細縷。有一壯士以至那國百練剛刀捷疾而斷。隨爾所縷斷經爾所剎那。有說。猶麁非剎那量。實剎那量。世尊不說。云何知然。如契經說。有一苾芻來詣佛所。頂禮雙足却住一面。白世尊言。壽行云何。速疾生滅。佛言。我能宣說汝不能知。苾芻言。頗有譬喻能顯示不。佛言。有今為汝說。譬如四善射夫各執弓箭。相背攢立欲射四方。有一捷夫來語之曰。汝等今可一時放箭。我能遍接俱令不墮。於意云何。此捷疾不。苾芻白佛。甚疾。世尊。佛言。彼人捷疾不及地行藥叉。地行捷疾不及空行藥叉。空行捷疾不及四大王眾天。彼天捷疾不及日月輪。二輪捷疾不及堅行天子。此是導引日月輪車者。此等諸天展轉捷疾壽行生滅捷疾於彼。剎那流轉無有暫停。由此故知世尊不說實剎那量。問何故世尊不為他說實剎那量。答無有有情堪能知故。問豈舍利子亦不知耶。答彼雖能知而於彼無用是故不說。佛不空說法故。

Thirty lavas make one muhūrta — this comprises two hundred sixteen thousand kṣaṇas. Thirty muhūrtas make one day and night — this comprises just under six million five hundred thousand kṣaṇas. This body of the five aggregates, in the span of a single day and night, passes through birth-and-perishing, impermanence, this very many times over. Some say: this figure is still too coarse to be the true measure of a kṣaṇa — according to my own view, in the time it takes a strong man to snap his fingers once, sixty-four kṣaṇas pass. Others say: not so — according to my own view, it is like two strong men each pulling and snapping many fine threads of Kāśī cloth at once: however many threads snap, that many kṣaṇas pass in that same time. Others say: not so — according to my own view, it is like two strong men gripping and stretching taut many fine threads of Kāśī cloth, while a single strong man, wielding a well-forged, sharp blade from the land of Cīna, swiftly cuts through them: however many threads are cut, that many kṣaṇas pass in that same time. Others say: even this is still too coarse a figure, not the true measure of a kṣaṇa — the World-Honored One himself never actually stated the true measure of a kṣaṇa. How is this known? As the sūtra says: there was a certain bhikṣu who came before the Buddha, bowed at his feet, and withdrew to one side, saying to the World-Honored One, "How is it that the formations of a lifespan arise and perish with such swiftness?" The Buddha said, "I am able to explain this, yet you would not be able to understand it." The bhikṣu said, "Is there, perhaps, a simile that could show me?" The Buddha said, "There is — let me now explain it to you. Suppose four skilled archers, each holding bow and arrow, stood back to back, intending to shoot in each of the four directions at once, and a swift-footed man came and said to them, 'You should all release your arrows at the very same moment — I am able to catch every one of them before any falls to the ground.' What do you think — is this not swift?" The bhikṣu said to the Buddha, "Extremely swift, World-Honored One." The Buddha said, "That man's own swiftness does not match that of the earth-treading yakṣas; the earth-treading yakṣas' own swiftness does not match that of the sky-treading yakṣas; the sky-treading yakṣas' own swiftness does not match that of the Four Great Kings' own retinue of gods; those gods' own swiftness does not match that of the sun and moon's own discs; the swiftness of these two discs does not match that of the firm-moving deity who guides the very chariots of the sun and moon. Each of these, in turn, surpassing the one before in swiftness — and yet the arising and perishing of the formations of a lifespan is swifter still than even the swiftest of these, its own kṣaṇa-flow never pausing even for an instant." From this, it is known that the World-Honored One never actually stated the true measure of a kṣaṇa. Question: why did the World-Honored One not explain to others the true measure of a kṣaṇa? Answer: because there is no sentient being capable of comprehending it. Question: could Śāriputra himself, then, also not have known it? Answer: though he was indeed capable of knowing it, it would have served him no purpose, and it was for this reason that it was not explained — for the Buddha never teaches the Dharma in vain.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even Śāriputra, wisest of the Buddha's disciples, is denied an answer he could have understood only because it would not have helped him, is a striking statement of this tradition's own pedagogical priorities: truth, however available, is withheld when it cannot serve liberation, since, as the passage itself insists, the Buddha never teaches the Dharma in vain.

3. A Full Year's Calendar of Waxing and Waning Days

An almost astronomical level of precision follows: a full year's cycle of waxing and waning daylight, tracked month by month through the traditional Indian calendar, from the equal fifteen-and-fifteen muhūrtas of Kārttika through the year's longest night of eighteen muhūrtas at Māgha, back through balance at Vaiśākha, on to the longest day of eighteen muhūrtas at Śrāvaṇa, and back again to balance — a complete, empirically grounded solar calendar folded into the treatise's own account of time's boundary-measures.

Chinese Source

一歲有十二月。晝夜增減略為二時。由減及增各六月故。然晝與夜增減相違。雖各二時而無四位。晝夜增減各一臘縛。月則各一牟呼栗多。三十牟呼栗多成一晝夜。於中晝夜多少四類不同。增位極長不過十八。減位極短唯有十二。晝夜停位各有十五。謂羯栗底迦月白半第八日。晝夜各十五牟呼栗多。從此以後晝減夜增各一臘縛。

Ru-Yi Translation

A year has twelve months, and the waxing and waning of day and night can be broadly divided into two seasons, since the waning and the waxing each span six months; yet day and night wax and wane in opposite directions from one another, and, though each has these same two seasons, there are not, on that account, four distinct positions. The day's and night's own waxing and waning each proceed by a single lava per month, in the sense that thirty lavas make one muhūrta. There are four distinct classes of relative day-and-night length: the longest waxing position never exceeds eighteen, the shortest waning position is only twelve, and the two positions of equal day-and-night each have fifteen — that is, on the eighth day of the bright half of the month Kārttika, day and night each measure fifteen muhūrtas; from this point onward, the day wanes and the night waxes, each by one lava at a time,

至末伽始羅月白半第八日。夜有十六牟呼栗多。晝十四至報沙月白半第八日。夜有十七晝十三。至磨伽月白半第八日。夜有十八晝十二。從此以後夜減晝增各一臘縛。至頗勒窶那月白半第八日。夜有十七晝十三。至制怛羅月白半第八日。夜有十六晝十四。至吠舍佉月白半第八日。晝夜各十五。從此以後夜減晝增各一臘縛。至誓瑟搋月白半第八日。夜有十四晝十六。至阿沙茶月白半第八日。夜有十三晝十七。至室羅筏拏月白半第八日。夜有十二晝十八。從此以後晝減夜增各一臘縛。至婆達羅鉢陀月白半第八日。夜有十三晝十七。至阿濕縛庾闍月白半第八日。夜有十四晝十六。如是復至羯栗底迦月白半第八日。晝夜停等。是名略說時之分齊。

— up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Mārgaśīrṣa, when the night measures sixteen muhūrtas and the day fourteen; up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Pauṣa, when the night measures seventeen and the day thirteen; up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Māgha, when the night measures eighteen and the day twelve. From this point onward, the night wanes and the day waxes, each by one lava at a time: up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Phālguna, the night measures seventeen and the day thirteen; up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Caitra, the night measures sixteen and the day fourteen; up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Vaiśākha, day and night each measure fifteen. From this point onward, the night again wanes and the day waxes, each by one lava at a time: up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Jyaiṣṭha, the night measures fourteen and the day sixteen; up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Āṣāḍha, the night measures thirteen and the day seventeen; up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Śrāvaṇa, the night measures twelve and the day eighteen. From this point onward, the day again wanes and the night waxes, each by one lava at a time: up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Bhādrapada, the night measures thirteen and the day seventeen; up through the eighth day of the bright half of the month Āśvayuja, the night measures fourteen and the day sixteen — and so, once more, up to the eighth day of the bright half of the month Kārttika, day and night stand equal again. This is a brief account of the boundary-measures of time.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Embedding a working solar calendar, precise to the month and the muhūrta, inside a treatise on ultimate metaphysics is a reminder that this tradition never treats the technical and the everyday as separate domains: the same rigor applied to a moment of mind's own arising and perishing is applied, without any change of register, to how much daylight a farmer can expect in the month of Māgha.

4. From the Ultimate Atom to the Tallest God: A Complete Scale of Size

A companion scale for space now runs in the opposite direction — from the ultimate atom, indivisible, invisible, and untouchable even in principle, up through seven-fold multiplications into motes of copper, water, rabbit-down, sheep-wool, and ox-hair, lice and barley-grains, fingers and cubits and bow-lengths, until it reaches the full measured heights of every being in the cosmos, from Jambudvīpa's own three-and-a-half-cubit humans up through Uttarakuru's thirty-two-cubit giants, and on through every heaven in turn, doubling and doubling again until the Akaniṣṭha gods stand a full sixteen thousand yojanas tall.

Chinese Source

問彼極微量復云何知。答應知極微是最細色不可斷截破壞貫穿不可取捨乘履摶掣。非長非短。非方非圓。非正不正。非高非下。無有細分不可分析。不可覩見。不可聽聞。不可嗅甞。不可摩觸故說極微。是最細色。此七極微成一微塵。是眼眼識所取色中最微細者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: how, further, can the measure of the ultimate atom itself be known? Answer: it should be understood that the ultimate atom is the very finest form there is — incapable of being cut, severed, broken, shattered, pierced, or bored through; incapable of being seized or released, ridden upon or trodden upon; neither long nor short, neither square nor round, neither upright nor askew, neither high nor low; possessing no smaller portion within it capable of further division; incapable of being seen, incapable of being heard, incapable of being smelled or tasted, incapable of being touched — and it is on this account that it is called the "ultimate atom," the very finest form there is. Seven of these ultimate atoms make one "minute mote" — the finest form capable of being apprehended by the eye and eye-consciousness together.

此唯三種眼見。一天眼。二轉輪王眼。三住後有菩薩眼。七微塵成一銅塵。有說。此七成一水塵。七銅塵成一水塵。有說。此七成一銅塵。七水塵成一兔毫塵。有說。七銅塵成一兔毫塵。七兔毫塵成一羊毛塵。七羊毛塵成一牛毛塵。七牛毛塵成一向遊塵。七向遊塵成一蟣。七蟣成一虱。七虱成一穬麥。七穬麥成指一節。二十四指節成一肘。四肘為一弓。去村五百弓。名阿練若處。從此已去名邊遠處。則五百弓成摩揭陀國一俱盧舍。成北方半俱盧舍。所以者何。摩揭陀國其地平正去村雖近而不聞聲。北方高下遠猶聲及。是故北方俱盧舍大。八俱盧舍成一踰繕那。贍部洲人身長三肘半。或有過者。毘提訶人身長八肘。瞿陀尼人身長十六肘。俱盧洲人身長三十二肘。四大王眾天身長俱盧舍四分之一。三十三天身長半俱盧舍。天帝釋身長一俱盧舍。夜摩天身長。俱盧舍四分之三。覩史多天身長俱盧舍。樂變化天身長俱盧舍。及俱盧舍四分之一。他化自在天身長俱盧舍半。梵眾天身長半踰繕那。梵輔天身長一踰繕那。

This can be seen only by three kinds of eye — first, the divine eye; second, the eye of a wheel-turning king; third, the eye of a bodhisattva dwelling in his own final existence. Seven minute motes make one "copper mote." Others say: seven of these make one "water mote," and seven copper motes make one water mote — others say: seven of these make one copper mote. Seven water motes make one "rabbit-down mote." Others say: seven copper motes make one rabbit-down mote. Seven rabbit-down motes make one "sheep-wool mote." Seven sheep-wool motes make one "ox-hair mote." Seven ox-hair motes make one "sunbeam-drifting mote." Seven of these sunbeam-motes make one louse-egg. Seven louse-eggs make one louse. Seven lice make one grain of barley. Seven barley-grains make one finger-joint. Twenty-four finger-joints make one cubit. Four cubits make one bow-length. A place five hundred bow-lengths from a village is called a "forest-dwelling place"; beyond this is called a "remote place." Five hundred bow-lengths make one krośa in the kingdom of Magadha, but only half a krośa in the northern regions — for why is this? The land of Magadha is level and flat, so that, though close to a village, its own sound does not carry there; the northern lands rise and fall in elevation, so that sound carries even from far away — and it is for this reason that the northern krośa is the larger of the two. Eight krośas make one yojana. The people of Jambudvīpa stand three and a half cubits tall, though some exceed this; the people of Videha stand eight cubits tall; the people of Godānīya stand sixteen cubits tall; the people of Uttarakuru stand thirty-two cubits tall. The gods of the Four Great Kings' own retinue stand one-quarter krośa tall; the Thirty-Three Gods stand half a krośa tall; Śakra, Lord of the Gods, stands one full krośa tall; the Yāma gods stand three-quarters of a krośa tall; the Tuṣita gods stand a full krośa tall; the Nirmāṇarati gods stand one krośa plus one-quarter krośa tall; the Paranirmita-vaśavartin gods stand one and a half krośas tall; the Brahma-Pariṣadya gods stand half a yojana tall; the Brahma-Purohita gods stand one full yojana tall;

大梵天身長一踰繕那半。少光天身長二踰繕那。無量光天身長四踰繕那。極光淨天身長八踰繕那。少淨天身長十六踰繕那。無量淨天身長三十二踰繕那。遍淨天身長六十四踰繕那。無雲天身長百二十五踰繕那。福生天身長二百五十踰繕那。廣果天身長五百踰繕那。

the Great Brahma gods stand one and a half yojanas tall; the Parīttābha gods stand two yojanas tall; the Apramāṇābha gods stand four yojanas tall; the Ābhāsvara gods stand eight yojanas tall; the Parīttaśubha gods stand sixteen yojanas tall; the Apramāṇaśubha gods stand thirty-two yojanas tall; the Śubhakṛtsna gods stand sixty-four yojanas tall; the Anabhraka gods stand one hundred twenty-five yojanas tall; the Puṇyaprasava gods stand two hundred fifty yojanas tall; the Bṛhatphala gods stand five hundred yojanas tall.

無想天身亦爾。無煩天身長千踰繕那。無熱天身長二千踰繕那。善現天身長四千踰繕那。善見天身長八千踰繕那。阿迦膩瑟搋天身長十六千踰繕那。如是名為色之分齊。頗有法四緣生耶。乃至廣說。

The Asaṃjñisattva gods' own bodies likewise measure this same height. The Avṛha gods stand one thousand yojanas tall; the Atapa gods stand two thousand yojanas tall; the Sudṛśa gods stand four thousand yojanas tall; the Sudarśana gods stand eight thousand yojanas tall; the Akaniṣṭha gods stand sixteen thousand yojanas tall. Such, then, are the boundary-measures of form. Is there such a thing as a dharma arising from four conditions? And so on, in full.

13. Watching Your Own Corpse Decay: A Meditation on the Body's Own Undoing

A justly famous meditative practice now unfolds in vivid, unflinching detail: a bhikṣu resolves to watch, in sequence, his own body's death, its bier, the charnel ground, and its consumption by worms — or, alternately, its cremation, or its dissolution as though it were nothing more substantial than a ball of snow, brown sugar, or butter melting before fire — until, at the very last, he no longer perceives his own body at all, seeing only the worms or flames that consumed it. Roasted by fire, it will melt away; having melted away, this very melting-fire will itself in turn be extinguished — and, once extinguished, he no longer, in the end, perceives his own body at all, nor does he any longer perceive the fire either — this, too, is called "the elimination of form-perception." That is, through the power of his own earlier, sustained conviction, he no longer perceives the mark of his own body, and, further, no longer perceives the mark of the fire without that had harmed and consumed his own inner body either. As with the three similes of the snow-ball and the rest, so too, according to region, should the corresponding distinctions be understood, as explained above.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Asking a meditator to let go not just of the body but of the very worms and fire that consumed it is this practice's own subtle final lesson: even the vivid, useful image of one's own dissolution is, in the end, just another object to be released, lest the meditator simply trade attachment to a body for a quieter attachment to the picture of its ending.

15. Where Does the Image Come From? A Monk's Own Memory of the Dying

A tender, concrete question grounds this entire meditative sequence in lived experience: where has a practitioner actually witnessed scenes vivid enough to sustain such contemplation? The answer traces back to the ordinary, unglamorous duty of tending a dying fellow monk — the failing body, the futile medicine, the final gasping breath, the corpse borne to the charnel ground, left to scavengers if wood was scarce or burned to nothing if it was plentiful — with even a practitioner who never nursed the dying still able to draw on the humbler memory of watching a snowball simply melt away in the sun.

Chinese Source

If, on the other hand, firewood was readily available, they would instead pile up wood and lay the body upon it, burning it with fire — and, in but a short while, everything was consumed, the fire soon extinguished, leaving nothing whatsoever, still and silent. 16. Three Ways of Mapping the Same Three Stages

The fascicle closes by cross-referencing this three-stage sequence — still seeing both body and worms or fire, seeing only worms or fire, and seeing neither — against three separate, older frameworks at once: the stages of the elimination of form-perception and its preparatory practice; the first and second of the eight liberations; and the upper, middle, and lower grades of the three wholesome roots — offering the same underlying progress toward complete release from bodily attachment described, side by side, in three different technical vocabularies inherited from three different strands of the tradition.
