The Hand of the Asura Rāhu

Fascicle 27 continues the Person Nyāsa's fifth part (補特伽羅納息第三之五), opening with what a formless being's mind depends on without a body, before turning to one of the deepest doctrinal fault lines in Buddhist history: is mind originally pure, defiled only by adventitious affliction, as the Vibhajyavādins hold, or never pure to begin with? The fascicle stages an extended formal debate complete with named techniques of refutation, and closes by identifying the single future moment of mind that attains liberation from every obstruction at once. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. What Does a Formless Being's Mind Depend On, Without a Body?

Fascicle 27 opens a new question: since a being possessing material form has its own continuum of mind depend on its body, what does a formless being's continuum of mind depend on, having no body at all? The treatise's answer names the life faculty, the class of homogeneous being, and every other dissociated formation of that same kind — before working out, in exhaustive detail, exactly what each of the six consciousnesses across the desire, form, and formless realms actually takes as its proper support versus its merely accompanying dependence.

The careful distinction drawn throughout this combinatorial survey between a consciousness's own proper “support” and its merely accompanying “dependence” does real philosophical work: eye-consciousness depends on the eye as its defining support, but also, in a looser sense, depends on the life faculty, the class of homogeneous being, and a whole further list of dissociated formations that keep the body itself viable — and it is precisely this looser, background dependence that a formless being's own mind must fall back on entirely, having no material support left to serve as anything more specific.

2. Is Life a Single Thing, or Many? The Severed Hand That Loses Its Life

A genuinely puzzling question about the life faculty's own substance follows: is it one single thing, or many separate things? Either answer seems to lead to trouble — if singular, why does severing a hand not cause death while severing the head does; if plural, why does a severed limb, once parted from the body, retain no life of its own at all? The treatise resolves this by distinguishing life dependent on a complete body from life dependent on an incomplete one, the former ceasing and the latter arising at the very moment of severance, while the head and waist alone count as fatal points since they alone support the eye and the other faculties, or the breath itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image of life itself instantaneously reconstituting from “dependent on a complete body” into “dependent on an incomplete body” the very moment a limb is severed is a genuinely elegant way to preserve life's own singularity without denying the plain fact that a severed hand plainly has none: rather than treating life as literally multiplying or dividing, the treatise treats it as a single continuous thing whose own defining relationship to the body simply updates instantly to match whatever body currently remains, exactly as a garment remains one garment even after a sleeve is torn off, its own fit to the wearer simply changing to match what is left.

3. The Class of Homogeneous Being: Why Beings of a Kind Resemble One Another

A companion concept to the life faculty is introduced: the class of homogeneous being, the shared class-nature explaining why beings of the same destiny or realm mutually resemble one another — hell-beings resembling hell-beings, desire-realm beings resembling desire-realm beings — itself both a ripening (for one's destiny-level resemblance) and an equal-outflow (for one's realm-level resemblance), always undefined in moral quality regardless of whether the individual being is virtuous or depraved. A precise tetralemma then works out every possible combination of obtaining and relinquishing this class of homogeneous being across death, rebirth, and entry into the noble path.

Their own intended meaning is this: the impermanence of the three realms is called "devoid-of-existence"; the craving that takes this as its object is named "craving-devoid-of-existence" — since impermanence itself spans both what is abandoned by seeing and what is abandoned by cultivation, the craving that takes it as its object likewise spans both.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Classifying the class of homogeneous being as invariably undefined, even for one who commits the five uninterrupted-retribution deeds or one who has reached the four noble fruits, is a deliberate refusal to let moral quality bleed into a purely descriptive, classificatory concept: mere membership in a shared category of being — hell-dweller, human, stream-enterer's own destiny-class — says nothing whatsoever about the moral quality of the specific karma that placed a given being there, and the treatise insists on keeping these two entirely separate axes of classification from ever collapsing into one another.

4. Craving-Devoid-of-Existence: Abandoned by Seeing, or Only by Cultivation?

A precise scriptural puzzle opens the fascicle's central debate: scripture names craving-devoid-of-existence as the third of three cravings, without ever specifying whether it is abandoned by seeing or by cultivation — and the treatise composes this discussion specifically to refute the Vibhajyavādins, who hold that this craving, taking the impermanence of literally anything at all as its object, must span both categories. The treatise's own reading confines “devoid-of-existence” specifically to the future cutting-off of one's own class of homogeneous being, a dharma abandoned only by cultivation, and therefore confines this particular craving to cultivation alone.

Chinese Source

於此義中無有愛但應言修所斷。謂於此論隨順契經無倒義中。無有愛但應言修所斷。此中有說。若隨經義說無有愛唯修所斷。若隨實義說無有愛通二所斷。所以者何。謂契經說。如有一類恐怖苦受所逼切故作如是念。若我死後斷壞無有。豈不樂哉。此經中說彼眾同分後時無常。名為無有。如是無有唯修所斷。故無有愛非見所斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

With respect to this very meaning, craving-devoid-of-existence should be said to be solely abandoned by cultivation — that is, within this treatise, in accordance with scripture's own undistorted meaning, craving-devoid-of-existence should be said to be solely abandoned by cultivation. Here, some say: if one speaks in accordance with scripture's own meaning, craving-devoid-of-existence is solely abandoned by cultivation; if one speaks in accordance with the ultimate meaning, craving-devoid-of-existence spans both what is abandoned by seeing and cultivation. Why is this? As scripture states: there is a certain kind of person who, terrified and oppressed by painful feeling, reflects as follows: were I, after death, to be utterly cut off and rendered non-existent, would this not be blissful indeed? This scripture speaks of that being's own class of homogeneous being's later impermanence as "devoid-of-existence" — and this kind of "devoid-of-existence" is solely abandoned by cultivation, and so craving-devoid-of-existence is not abandoned by seeing at all.

此中論主說隨經義。與分別論者競釋經義。故說無有愛唯修所斷。

It is with reference to this very passage that the master of this treatise speaks in accordance with scripture's own meaning, contending with the Vibhajyavādins over the correct interpretation of scripture, and states that craving-devoid-of-existence is solely abandoned by cultivation.

若隨實義如後當說。三界無常說名無有。三界無常通二所斷。故無有愛亦通二種。有作是說。此無有愛若隨實義若隨經義。俱應說言唯修所斷。三界無常雖通二種。而起愛者唯修所斷。以無有愛依厭苦生。但愛當來苦器無有。唯修所斷。是眾苦器。故無有愛唯修所斷。斷見雖緣五部無有。而無有愛不能通緣。但緣當來眾同分斷。

If one speaks in accordance with the ultimate meaning, as will be explained further below, the impermanence of the three realms is called "devoid-of-existence," and, since the impermanence of the three realms spans both what is abandoned by seeing and by cultivation, craving-devoid-of-existence likewise spans both kinds. There are yet others who say: this craving-devoid-of-existence, whether speaking in accordance with the ultimate meaning or in accordance with scripture's own meaning, should in every case be said to be solely abandoned by cultivation — for, although the impermanence of the three realms does span both kinds, the craving that arises from it is solely abandoned by cultivation, since craving-devoid-of-existence arises specifically from weariness with suffering, craving only that the future vessel of suffering be rendered non-existent — and it is solely what is abandoned by cultivation that constitutes this vessel of manifold suffering, and it is for this reason that craving-devoid-of-existence is solely abandoned by cultivation. Although the view of extinction does take the non-existence of all five sections as its object, craving-devoid-of-existence is unable to take all of these as its object in this same universal way, taking only the future cutting-off of the class of homogeneous being as its object.

是故尊者妙音說曰。起無有愛補特伽羅。唯緣執受蘊界處起。為彼逼切緣彼當來斷壞起愛。無見所斷逼切有情令愛彼斷。故無有愛唯修所斷。前來略說無有愛唯修所斷。

It is for this reason that the Venerable Ghoṣa stated as follows: a person who gives rise to craving-devoid-of-existence arises only with respect to the appropriated aggregates, elements, and sense fields, oppressed by these, craving their future cutting-off and destruction — there is no oppression by anything abandoned by seeing that causes a sentient being to crave its own cutting-off — and it is for this reason that craving-devoid-of-existence is solely abandoned by cultivation. What has been briefly stated above establishes that craving-devoid-of-existence is solely abandoned by cultivation.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ghoṣa's own closing formulation crystallizes the whole dispute into a single memorable image: craving-devoid-of-existence arises only from a being oppressed by his own appropriated aggregates, craving their future destruction — no one, however afflicted, ever craves the future destruction of some abstract dharma abandoned by seeing that does not belong to them at all, and it is this deeply personal, self-directed character of the craving that confines it to cultivation, whatever the Vibhajyavādins' own broader reading of “impermanence” in the abstract might otherwise suggest.

5. A Masterclass in Debate: Three Ways to Refute an Opponent

The treatise now stages an extended, formally structured debate between the Proponent of Correct Principle and the Vibhajyavādin, complete with a genuinely fascinating methodological digression: logical treatises name three paths of refutation (through doubt, through pointing out a fault, and through elimination), while the Buddha's own scriptures show three further paths (surpassing the opponent, matching the opponent, and contradicting the opponent's own tenet) — illustrated by the ascetic Dīrghanakha refuted by his own universal skepticism, the outsider Pāṭalika refuted by his own admission about a known criminal, and the elder Upāli refuted by his own account of forests destroyed through mere evil intention. The proponent then deploys the “matching the opponent” technique three times over, each time asking the Vibhajyavādin to concede that some clearly-uncut affliction in a stream-enterer nonetheless fails to manifest.

此後應理論者與分別論者相對問答難通。廣顯無有愛唯修所斷。汝說無有愛唯修所斷。諸預流者未斷此愛耶者。是分別論者問。重定前宗。若不定他宗說他過失則不應理。答如是者。是應理論者答。

Ru-Yi Translation

What follows now is the manner in which the proponent of correct principle debates with the Vibhajyavādin, through question and answer and refutation and reconciliation, to establish at length that craving-devoid-of-existence is solely abandoned by cultivation. "You say that craving-devoid-of-existence is solely abandoned by cultivation — have stream-enterers, then, not yet cut off this craving?" — this is the question posed by the Vibhajyavādin, fixing the opposing tenet with precision, since, without first fixing precisely what a rival school actually holds, to then state that school's own faults would be improper. "The answer: yes" is the reply of the proponent of correct principle.

This confirms, in accordance with scripture's own undistorted meaning, the established tenet, and it is for this reason that the reply is "yes." "What, then, do you intend — do stream-enterers give rise to a thought such as this: were I, after death, to be utterly cut off and rendered non-existent, would this not be at ease indeed?" — this is the difficulty the Vibhajyavādin now wishes to raise, seeking to fix the opposing tenet against itself so as to reveal a violation of correct principle. "The answer: it is not so" is the reply of the proponent of correct principle, rebuffing this very question and showing that no violation of meaning follows. One asks: why do stream-enterers not give rise to this craving? The answer: because they have seen the true nature of dharmas — that is, stream-enterers, seeing that every dharma continues on through cause and effect in unbroken succession, therefore do not crave their own cutting-off. Further, because they have deep faith in the ripening of karma — that is, stream-enterers, deeply believing that karma and its ripening continue on in succession before and after, therefore do not crave their own cutting-off. Further, because they have penetrated emptiness — that is, stream-enterers, having attained the gateway of liberation through emptiness, know there to be neither a self nor what belongs to a self; since there now exists a later cutting-off, they do not give rise to craving for that future cutting-off. Further, this craving-devoid-of-existence is nurtured by the extinction-view, necessarily manifesting only after the extinction-view has already manifested — stream-enterers, having already cut off the extinction-view, do not give rise to this craving. Further, stream-enterers have attained the non-selective cessation of craving-devoid-of-existence, and so it can never again manifest before them. Now hear what I myself have to say: if craving-devoid-of-existence were solely abandoned by cultivation, and stream-enterers had not yet cut off this craving, then it ought to be said that stream-enterers do give rise to a thought such as this: were I, after death, to be utterly cut off and rendered non-existent, would this not be at ease indeed? But if stream-enterers do not give rise to a thought such as this, then it ought not to be said that craving-devoid-of-existence is solely abandoned by cultivation, and that stream-enterers have not yet cut off this craving — to maintain both of these positions at once is altogether unreasonable — this is the Vibhajyavādin's own opening and closing gambit, turning the argument back upon itself from both directions, the opening gambit showing accordance with his own tenet but violation of true meaning, the closing gambit showing accordance with true meaning but violation of his own tenet — since neither can be accepted, he concludes, summing up: to maintain both of these positions at once is altogether unreasonable. The proponent of correct principle then offers, in reply, the following further meaning: my own school does not maintain that whatever has not yet been cut off must necessarily manifest before one — there is a case of what has not yet been cut off yet does not manifest, and a case of what has already been cut off yet is still able to manifest. Were it the case that whatever has not yet been cut off must necessarily manifest, then there could be no liberation or release at all, since not-yet-cut-off dharmas are without any boundary or limit — even were one to try to give rise to all of them, at what point could this ever be exhausted? It is with this very argument that the proponent of correct principle now turns to refute the Vibhajyavādin in return, resolving the earlier difficulty. Within the treatises on logical reasoning, it is stated that there are three paths by which another's position may be refuted: first, refutation through doubt; second, refutation through pointing out a fault; third, refutation through elimination. Within the Buddha's own scriptures, it is likewise shown that another's statement may be refuted through three paths: first, refutation by surpassing the opponent; second, refutation by matching the opponent; third, refutation by contradicting the opponent's own tenet. As for refutation by surpassing the opponent: as when the ascetic Dīrghanakha declared to the Buddha, "I accept nothing whatsoever" — the Buddha said to him, "do you, then, accept this very view of your own?" — and he was thereby refuted by his own words. As for refutation by matching the opponent: as when the outside teacher Pāṭalika declared to the Buddha, "does Gautama know illusion, or not? If he does not know it, he is not omniscient; if he does know it, he must himself be a deceiver" — the Buddha said to him, "in the village of Godhā, there is an evil man named Lambaka, a smelter, who breaks the precepts and does evil — do you know of him?" — he replied, "I do." The Buddha then said to him, "then you, too, must be an evil man who breaks the precepts" — and he was thereby refuted. As for refutation by contradicting the opponent's own tenet: as when the elder Upāli declared to the Buddha, "the offense of bodily karma is greater; that of mental karma is not" — the Buddha said to him, "the forests of Daṇḍaka and Kaliṅga — by whom were they destroyed? Was this not done by the evil intention of certain sages?" — he replied, "it was." The Buddha then said, "can bodily karma alone accomplish such a thing?" — he replied, "it cannot." The Buddha then said to him, "have you not now contradicted your own earlier statement?" — and he was thereby refuted. Among these three, the proponent of correct principle relies on refutation by matching the opponent to resolve the earlier difficulty — and this is itself of three kinds, to be elaborated further below. "You, too, maintain that the ripening-craving toward hell, the animal realm, and the realm of hungry ghosts is solely abandoned by cultivation — have stream-enterers, then, not yet cut off this craving?" — this is the question of the proponent of correct principle, fixing the opposing tenet with precision, since, without first fixing precisely what a rival school actually holds, to then state that school's own faults would be improper. "The answer: yes" is the reply of the Vibhajyavādin, since the question posed is indeed correctly fixed, and so the reply is "yes." "What, then, do you intend — do stream-enterers give rise to a thought such as this: may I become the dragon-king Airāvaṇa, or the dragon-king Suniṣṭha, or the lord of ghosts Yama, ruling over every being in the ghost-realm?" — this is the difficulty the proponent of correct principle now wishes to raise, seeking to fix the opposing tenet against itself so as to reveal a violation of correct principle. "The answer: it is not so" is the reply of the Vibhajyavādin, rebuffing this very question and showing that no violation of meaning follows. One asks: why do stream-enterers not give rise to this craving? The answer: because those destinies are foolish, while noble ones are wise; because those destinies belong to ordinary beings, while noble ones are not ordinary beings; because those destinies possess evil intentions, while noble ones possess wholesome intentions; because those destinies mostly involve breaking the precepts and committing evil karma, while noble ones have accomplished pure precepts. Further, every noble one has attained the non-selective cessation of those destinies, and so does not crave rebirth there. One asks: do noble ones, then, never give rise to any craving whatsoever toward the evil destinies? The answer: although they do not crave rebirth there, they may still crave certain requisites found there — just as Śakra, lord of the gods, still cherishes the yakṣa Śeṣa-clad-in-blue, and the dragon-kings Airāvaṇa and Suniṣṭha, and stream-enterers and the rest, upon hearing that their own parents have fallen into an evil destiny, still give rise to feelings of affection and longing for them. Since we are here specifically rebuffing the craving for rebirth there, the answer given is: it is not so. Now hear what I myself have to say: if the ripening-craving toward hell, the animal realm, and the realm of hungry ghosts were solely abandoned by cultivation, and stream-enterers had not yet cut off this craving, then it ought to be said that stream-enterers do give rise to a thought such as this: may I become the dragon-king Airāvaṇa, and so on, as elaborated at length. But if stream-enterers do not give rise to such a thought, then it ought not to be said that the ripening-craving toward hell, the animal realm, and the realm of hungry ghosts is solely abandoned by cultivation, and that stream-enterers have not yet cut off this craving — this is the proponent of correct principle's own opening and closing gambit, turning the argument back upon itself from both directions, the opening gambit showing accordance with his own tenet but violation of true meaning, the closing gambit showing accordance with true meaning but violation of his own tenet — since neither can be accepted, he concludes, summing up: to maintain both of these positions at once is altogether unreasonable. The proponent of correct principle's own intended meaning here is this: just as craving toward an evil destiny remains uncut in a noble one yet does not manifest before him, craving-devoid-of-existence should be understood in exactly the same way — and so the Vibhajyavādin's own difficulty does not accord with correct principle. "You, too, maintain that the fetter which, once it fetters one, leads one to kill one's own father or mother, is solely abandoned by cultivation — have stream-enterers, then, not yet cut off this fetter?" — this is the question of the proponent of correct principle, the rest as already stated above. "The answer: yes" is the reply of the Vibhajyavādin, the rest as already stated above. "What, then, do you intend — do stream-enterers, fettered by such a fetter, go on to kill their own father or mother?" — this is the difficulty the proponent of correct principle now wishes to raise, seeking to fix the opposing tenet against itself so as to reveal a violation of correct principle. "The answer: it is not so" is the reply of the Vibhajyavādin, rebuffing this very question and showing that no violation of meaning follows. One asks: why do stream-enterers not give rise to this fetter? The answer: only one possessing an upper-grade evil intention is able to give rise to this fetter, and stream-enterers possess only wholesome intentions.

復次若有上品無慚無愧能起此纏。諸預流者有慚愧故。復次此纏邪見之所長養邪見後起。諸預流者邪見斷故不起此纏。復次諸預流者已得此纏非擇滅故及得此業不作戒故畢竟不起。聽我所說若纏所纏故害父母命此纏唯修所斷。諸預流者未斷此纏則應說預流者起如是纏故害父母命。若預流者不起如是纏故害父母命則不應說諸纏所纏故害父母命此纏唯修所斷。諸預流者未斷此纏。作如是說俱不應理者。是應理論者前後兩關翻覆設難。餘如前說。應理論者此中意言。如此殺纏聖者未斷而不現前。無有愛亦應爾故。彼所難不應正理。汝等亦說於修所斷法無有而貪。此貪唯修所斷。諸預流者未斷此貪耶者。是應理論者問。餘如前說。答如是者。是分別論者答。

Further, only one possessing an upper-grade shamelessness and lack of conscience is able to give rise to this fetter, and stream-enterers possess shame and conscience. Further, this fetter is nurtured by wrong view, necessarily manifesting only after wrong view has already manifested — stream-enterers, having already cut off wrong view, do not give rise to this fetter. Further, stream-enterers have attained the non-selective cessation of this fetter, and, further, have attained the very precept-basis for never committing this karma at all — and so it can never again, in the end, manifest before them. Now hear what I myself have to say: if the fetter which, once it fetters one, leads one to kill one's own father or mother, were solely abandoned by cultivation, and stream-enterers had not yet cut off this fetter, then it ought to be said that stream-enterers, fettered by such a fetter, would go on to kill their own father or mother. But if stream-enterers do not, fettered by such a fetter, go on to kill their own father or mother, then it ought not to be said that the fetter which leads one to kill one's own father or mother is solely abandoned by cultivation, and that stream-enterers have not yet cut off this fetter — this is the proponent of correct principle's own opening and closing gambit, turning the argument back upon itself from both directions, the rest as already stated above.
