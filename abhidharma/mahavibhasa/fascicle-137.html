Where Consciousness Comes to Rest

Fascicle 137 completes the elimination-of-form-perception meditation before undertaking a full analysis of the four, seven, and nine abodes of consciousness and sentient beings — why hell, the fourth dhyāna, and the highest formless sphere are excluded from every scheme — and opens an entirely new chapter with an exhaustive causal ledger for grasped and ungrasped great elements and derived form. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. The Elimination of Form-Perception: Its Own Anatomy

Fascicle 137 opens by finishing what fascicle 136 began: a complete technical anatomy of the elimination-of-form-perception meditation — its self-substance is wisdom, though named after perception since perception's own function predominates within it, exactly as mindfulness-based practices are named for mindfulness though wisdom underlies them too. A dense catalog follows, fixing its realm, ground, object, moral nature, and mode of attainment (preparatory practice, never mere freedom from defilement, so that even one already free of the fourth dhyāna's own defilement cannot manifest it without deliberate effort), before a careful non-equivalence is drawn between lacking the practice and remaining unfree of form's own defilement.

Chinese Source

大種蘊第五中具見納息第三之四問此除色想。自體云何。答慧為自體。若爾何故以想為名。由此聚中想用增故。如持息念身等念住。本性生念。宿住隨念。皆慧為體。以念為名。念用增故。彼亦如是已說自體。應釋其名。此以何故名除色想。由此能遣諸積集色令不現前。名除色想。界者色界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth Aggregate, the Great Elements: the Full-Vision Nyāsa, Chapter Three, Part Four. Question: what is the self-substance of this elimination of form-perception? Answer: wisdom is its self-substance. Question: if so, why is it named using the word "perception"? Because, within this cluster of mental factors, the function of perception predominates — just as with mindfulness of breathing and the body, the other foundations of mindfulness, the innate arising of mindfulness, and recollection of past lives, all of which likewise have wisdom as their self-substance, yet are named using the word "mindfulness" because the function of mindfulness predominates within them — so too here. Having explained its self-substance, we should now explain its name. Why is it called "elimination of form-perception"? Because it is able to dispel every accumulated form so that it no longer manifests — hence it is called "elimination of form-perception." As for its realm: the form realm.

地者第四靜慮。所依者依欲界身。行相者不明了行相。所緣者緣欲界。問此緣欲界何法。答有說。即緣[卄/積]坑等處。有說。即緣彼處空界。如是說者。即緣所除所有諸色。於中有說。唯緣所除自身諸色念住者身念住。智者世俗智。等持者非等持俱。根者捨根相應世者通三世。過去緣過去。現在緣現在。未來可生者。緣未來。

As for its ground: the fourth dhyāna. As for what it depends upon: it depends on a desire-realm body. As for its own aspect: an unclear, indistinct aspect. As for its object: it takes the desire realm as its object. Question: what dharma of the desire realm, specifically, does it take as its object? Answer: some say, it takes as its object precisely such places as burial pits and mounds. Others say: it takes as its object the very empty space of those places. According to this final account: it takes as its object all the very forms that are being eliminated. On this point, some say: it takes as its object only its own body's forms specifically being eliminated. As for the foundation of mindfulness involved: the body as a foundation of mindfulness. As for the kind of knowledge: mundane knowledge. As for absorption: it is not accompanied by equipoise. As for its faculty: associated with the equanimity faculty. As for time: it pertains to all three times — taking the past as object when past, the present as object when present, and, as for the future, taking as object what is yet to arise;

不生者緣三世。善不善無記者。是善緣三種。有說。唯緣無記。三界繫不繫者。是色界繫。緣欲界繫。學無學非學非無學者。是非學非無學。緣非學非無學。見所斷修所斷不斷者。是修所斷。緣修所斷。緣自身他身非身者。有說。唯緣自身。有說。緣自他身。有說。通緣三種。緣名緣義者。唯緣義。加行得離染得者。是加行得。非離染得。已離第四靜慮染者。若不加行求此想時。終不能起令現前故有說。佛離染得。離有頂染時得故。餘加行得。有說。餘亦離染得。而加行現前。佛不加行獨覺下加行。聲聞或中或上。起處者在欲界。非色無色界。在人三洲非北洲。問此誰所起。答有說唯聖者非異生。有說。異生亦起。異生有二種。一內法異生。二外法異生。內法者能起非外法。以外法異生。長夜執我怖畏無我。不樂遣除內所依色故。已說此想自性等門復應顯示有雜無雜。問諸無除色想皆未離色染耶。答諸未離色染皆無除色想。有無除色想非未離色染。謂已離色染而未入彼定。問諸有除色想皆已離色染耶。答諸有除色想皆已離色染。有已離色染非有除色想。謂已離色染而未入彼定。所以者何。前說彼定唯加行得。非離染得故。雖已離第四靜慮染。若不加行求此定時。終不能起令現前故。有說。此定雖離染得。而獨覺等要起加行方令現前。此依現前有無作論故作是說。

— taking as object what will not arise, it pertains to all three times together. As for moral nature: it is wholesome, taking as object the three moral natures. Some say: it takes as object only neutral dharmas. As for the three realms — bound or unbound: it is form-realm-bound, taking the desire realm as its object. As for trainee, non-trainee, or neither: it is neither a trainee nor beyond training, taking as object what is neither a trainee's nor beyond training's own dharmas. As for cut off by seeing, by cultivation, or uncuttable: it is cut off by cultivation, taking as object what is cut off by cultivation. As for taking one's own body, another's body, or no body as object: some say, it takes only its own body as object; others say, it takes both its own and another's body as object; others say, it takes all three kinds indiscriminately as object. As for taking a name or a meaning as object: it takes only the meaning as object. As for whether it is attained through preparatory practice or through freedom from defilement: it is attained through preparatory practice, not through freedom from defilement — for even one who has already become free from the fourth dhyāna's own defilement, if he does not undertake the preparatory practice in seeking this perception, can never bring it into manifestation. Some say: the Buddha alone attains it through freedom from defilement, since he obtains it at the very moment of becoming free from the defilement of the Peak of Existence; all others attain it through preparatory practice. Others say: everyone else, too, attains it through freedom from defilement, yet must still undertake preparatory practice to bring it into actual manifestation — the Buddha alone requires no preparatory practice; a solitary-realized buddha requires only lesser preparatory practice; and a śrāvaka requires either middling or greater preparatory practice. As for where it arises: in the desire realm, not in the form or formless realm, and among the three human continents, not in Uttarakuru. Question: who, precisely, is capable of giving rise to it? Answer: some say, only noble ones, not ordinary beings; others say, ordinary beings, too, can give rise to it — and ordinary beings are of two kinds, inner-dharma and outer-dharma, of which only the inner-dharma kind is capable of giving rise to it, since outer-dharma ordinary beings, through the long night of time, have clung to a self and feared the absence of a self, and hence take no pleasure in dispelling the very form their own inner existence depends upon. Having now explained this perception's own nature and other categories, we should further show whether it is mixed with other things or unmixed with them. Question: does everyone lacking this elimination of form-perception necessarily remain unfree from form's own defilement? Answer: everyone unfree from form's own defilement does indeed lack this perception — yet there is one who lacks this perception without being unfree from form's own defilement, namely, one who has already become free from form's own defilement but has not yet entered this particular absorption. Question: does everyone possessing this elimination of form-perception necessarily have already become free from form's own defilement? Answer: everyone possessing this perception has indeed already become free from form's own defilement — yet there is one who has already become free from form's own defilement without possessing this perception, for the same reason as before, since this absorption is attained only through preparatory practice, never merely through freedom from defilement. Some say: though this absorption can indeed be attained through mere freedom from defilement, a solitary-realized buddha and the rest still require undertaking preparatory practice in order to bring it into actual manifestation — this treatise speaks according to whether it is actually manifest or not, and it is for this reason that this account is given.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even a fully accomplished practitioner, already free of the fourth dhyāna's own defilement, still cannot manifest this perception without deliberately seeking it out is a quietly important corrective to any assumption that spiritual attainment automatically cascades into every capacity that resembles it: freedom from defilement clears the way, but it does not substitute for the specific, chosen effort a particular practice still requires.

2. Four Scriptures, One Practice: Reconciling Competing Accounts

A rich exercise in textual harmonization follows: the very same phrase, "elimination of form-perception," turns up across four separate scriptural sources — this treatise, the Aggregation Nyāsa, and two verses from the Arthavargīya's own Arthapada — each describing it slightly differently, whether by realm, by which grounds it belongs to, or by which foundation of mindfulness it falls under. Rather than declaring one source authoritative and the others mistaken, the treatise carefully maps out exactly how each account differs from the others, treating the discrepancy itself as worth explaining rather than explaining away.

Chinese Source

問除色想言有多處說。謂此處說雜蘊。亦言入無色定除去色想。波羅衍拏亦作是說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: the term "elimination of form-perception" is discussed in many different places — this present passage discusses it, as does the Aggregation Nyāsa, which further says that entering formless absorption eliminates form-perception; the Arthavargīya likewise speaks of it, saying:

諸有除色想　能除一切身於內外法中　無有不見者眾義品中亦作是說。

"Whoever possesses the elimination of form-perception is able to eliminate every bodily attachment; within both inner and outer dharmas alike, there is nothing that remains unseen." The Arthapada likewise speaks of it, saying:

於想有想非即離　亦非無想非除想如是平等除色想　無有染著彼因緣如是諸說義有何異。答此說能遣諸積集色令不現前。名除色想。波羅衍拏眾義品說。斷色界愛名除色想。雜蘊中說。不緣下地流轉諸色名除色想。有說。此除色想在第四靜慮。波羅衍拏眾義品說。除色想者在七地。謂未至中間四靜慮空無邊處近分。雜蘊所說除色想者。亦在七地。謂四無色上三近分。有說。此除色想是身念住。波羅衍拏眾義品說。除色想者是法念住。雜蘊所說除色想者。通四念住。有說此除色想是不共。餘三是共。有說。雜蘊所說是共。餘三不共。是名諸說異義。

"With respect to perception, whether one has perception or is free of it, is neither the point, nor is it the absence of perception, nor the mere elimination of perception — it is this very equanimity that eliminates form-perception, with no attachment remaining, on account of that cause." What difference in meaning is there among these various accounts? Answer: this present passage says that what is able to dispel every accumulated form, so that it no longer manifests, is called "elimination of form-perception." The Arthavargīya's Arthapada says that cutting off craving for the form realm is called "elimination of form-perception." The Aggregation Nyāsa says that not taking as object the forms that flow and turn within the lower grounds is called "elimination of form-perception." Some say: this present passage's own elimination of form-perception belongs specifically to the fourth dhyāna; the Arthavargīya's Arthapada says it belongs to seven grounds — namely, the not-yet-attained stage, the intermediate stage, the four dhyānas, and the access-stage of the sphere of boundless space; and the Aggregation Nyāsa's own account likewise belongs to seven grounds — namely, the four formless attainments together with the access-stages of the upper three. Some say: this present passage's elimination of form-perception is the foundation of mindfulness of the body; the Arthavargīya's Arthapada says it is the foundation of mindfulness of dharmas; and the Aggregation Nyāsa's own account pertains to all four foundations of mindfulness together. Some say: this present passage's elimination of form-perception is not shared with outside paths, while the other three accounts are shared. Others say: the Aggregation Nyāsa's own account is shared, while the other three are not shared. These, then, are the differences in meaning among these various accounts.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Taking four scriptural accounts of the same practice and mapping their precise differences, rather than picking a winner, shows this tradition's own working method at its best: a discrepancy between sources is not a problem to be resolved by force, but an invitation to notice that each source may simply be describing the same reality from a slightly different angle.

3. A New Topic: What Are the 'Abodes of Consciousness'?

The treatise turns to a foundational sūtra topic left unanalyzed in the scriptures themselves: the four, seven, and nine "abodes" into which consciousness and sentient beings are sorted. The four abodes of consciousness — form, feeling, perception, and formations — are defined as whatever contaminated aggregate, conducive to grasping, consciousness comes to rest upon, with a further refinement extending this even to aggregates belonging to no sentient being at all, since consciousness can take even inanimate objects as its own resting object.

Chinese Source

四識住七識住。為四攝七。七攝四耶。乃至廣說。問何故作此論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Do the four abodes of consciousness and the seven abodes of consciousness — does the former subsume the latter, or does the latter subsume the former? And so on, in full. Question: why compose this treatise?

答為欲分別契經義故。謂契經說。四識住七識住九有情居。而不廣分別。亦不明攝。今欲廣解。并顯相攝故。作斯論。四識住者。如契經說。一色隨識住。二受隨識住。三想隨識住。四行隨識住。色隨識住者。謂色有漏隨順於取有情數攝。行隨識住亦爾。受隨識住者。謂受有漏隨順於取想。隨識住亦爾。有餘師說。色隨識住者。

Answer: in order to analyze the meaning of a sūtra passage — for the sūtra speaks of the four abodes of consciousness, the seven abodes of consciousness, and the nine abodes of sentient beings, yet does not analyze them in full, nor clarify how they subsume one another; it is in order to now provide this full explanation that this treatise is composed. As for the four abodes of consciousness: as the sūtra says, first, form as an abode for consciousness to dwell upon; second, feeling; third, perception; fourth, formations. "Form as an abode for consciousness to dwell upon" means contaminated form, conducive to grasping, included among the sentient-being-aggregates; "formations" is likewise so. "Feeling as an abode for consciousness to dwell upon" means contaminated feeling, conducive to grasping; "perception" is likewise so. Other masters say: "form as an abode for consciousness to dwell upon" means —

謂色有漏隨順於取。有情數非有情數攝。行隨識住亦爾。受想隨識住如前說。問有情數蘊。說為識住。此事可爾。非有情數蘊云何名識住。答有多種識住。謂相應識住。俱有識住。所依識住。所緣識住。所行識住。非有情數蘊。是識所緣故名識住。已說自性。應說因緣。何因緣故說名識住。答識於此中住。等住近住故名識住。如馬等所住名馬等住。有說。此中憙所潤識增長廣大故名識住。有說。此中愛所潤識攝受不離故名識住。有說。此中諸有漏識隨順取識生起執著。安住增長故名識住。

— contaminated form, conducive to grasping, included among either the sentient-being-aggregates or the non-sentient-being-aggregates; "formations" is likewise so; "feeling" and "perception" are as already explained. Question: that the sentient-being-aggregates are spoken of as an abode of consciousness — this much makes sense; but how can the non-sentient-being-aggregates also be called an abode of consciousness? Answer: there are, in fact, several kinds of abode of consciousness — namely, through association, through co-existence, as basis, and as object and sphere of activity. The non-sentient-being-aggregates, being objects that consciousness takes, are hence called an abode of consciousness as well. Having explained its own nature, we should now explain its cause — for what cause is it called an abode of consciousness? Answer: consciousness dwells, abides evenly, and rests near within it — hence it is called an abode of consciousness, just as the place a horse and the rest dwell is called a horse-abode and the like. Others say: it is called so because, within it, consciousness moistened by delight grows and expands. Others say: it is called so because, within it, consciousness moistened by craving is grasped and held, never departing. Others say: it is called so because, within it, every kind of contaminated consciousness, conducive to grasping, arises, clings, and settles, growing ever further.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That even a lifeless object can count as an "abode of consciousness," simply by being something consciousness takes as its object, quietly widens the entire framework beyond what its name first suggests: an "abode," on this account, is defined not by what has a mind, but by what a mind can rest upon — a definition broad enough to include the whole of the perceivable world.

4. Why the Undying Never Needs a Home

A cluster of explanations converges on a single question: why are uncontaminated dharmas never counted among the abodes of consciousness? Every answer traces back to the same underlying asymmetry — an abode of consciousness is, by definition, something that sustains, increases, and entrenches existence, feeding a craving- or delight-moistened consciousness that clings and grows within it, while the uncontaminated actively diminishes and undoes existence instead, offering nothing for consciousness to settle into or feed upon.

Chinese Source

問何故無漏法不立識住。答諸無漏法無識住相故。復次若法能增益有。能攝受有。能住持有。立為識住。諸無漏法能損減有。能違害有。能破壞有。故非識住。復次若法乃至是身見事。乃至墮苦集諦立為識住。諸無漏法乃至非身見事。乃至不墮苦集諦。故非識住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: why are uncontaminated dharmas not established as an abode of consciousness? Answer: because uncontaminated dharmas possess none of the marks of an abode of consciousness. Further: whatever dharma is capable of increasing existence, of grasping and holding existence, of sustaining and maintaining existence, is established as an abode of consciousness — uncontaminated dharmas, being capable of diminishing existence, of contradicting and harming existence, of destroying existence, are hence not an abode of consciousness. Further: whatever dharma constitutes an object of the view of a real self, and falls under the truth of suffering and its origin, is established as an abode of consciousness — uncontaminated dharmas, not constituting such an object and not falling under those truths, are hence not an abode of consciousness.

有說。若法憙所潤識於中增長廣大立為識住。諸無漏法與此相違故非識住。有說。若法愛所潤識於中攝受不離立為識住。諸無漏法則不如是。故非識住。有說。若法諸有漏識隨順取識於中生起執著。

Others say: whatever dharma is such that, within it, consciousness moistened by delight grows and expands, is established as an abode of consciousness — uncontaminated dharmas, being the opposite of this, are hence not an abode of consciousness. Others say: whatever dharma is such that, within it, consciousness moistened by craving is grasped and held, never departing, is established as an abode of consciousness — uncontaminated dharmas are not so, and hence are not an abode of consciousness. Others say: whatever dharma is such that every kind of contaminated consciousness, conducive to grasping, arises, clings, and settles,

安住增長立為識住。諸無漏法與此相違故非識住。

— growing ever further within it, is established as an abode of consciousness — uncontaminated dharmas, being the opposite of this, are hence not an abode of consciousness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Defining an "abode" as whatever sustains and entrenches existence has a striking corollary the treatise leaves implicit but unmistakable: liberation itself, being everything an abode is not, could never be described as a home to settle into — it is, by this very definition, the one place consciousness can never actually dwell.

5. Why Consciousness Is Never Its Own Abode

A genuinely elegant puzzle asks why consciousness itself is never counted as its own abode, resolved through the vivid image of a king's own throne: a throne exists for the king's sake, not the other way around, and a road is what a king travels, never something a king could himself become — an abode, likewise, is simply whatever consciousness comes to rest upon, and consciousness, riding and driving every abode the way a king rides a road, can never simultaneously be the road it rides.

Chinese Source

問何故識非識住。答為識故立識住。如為王故立王座。如王座王床王路亦爾。如王非路路非王。是王所行故名王路。如是識非住住非識。是識所止故名識住。是故識非識住。有說。若法識所乘御。如象馬船人所乘御。彼法立識住非識。乘御於識故識非識住。復次若法與識俱生俱住俱滅。於識有用立為識住。識於識不爾。有說。識住法爾與識俱在現在。是識所住。非識與識得有此事。問自識他識俱在現在。何不展轉立識住耶。答自識於自識非識住故。於他識亦非無異相故。復次於自識親尚非識住。況於踈遠。有說。若法與識三和合生。互有作用立為識住。非識與識三和合生互有作用。故非識住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: why is consciousness itself not an abode of consciousness? Answer: it is for the sake of consciousness that an abode of consciousness is established, exactly as a throne is established for the sake of a king — just as, likewise, a king's own throne, bed, and road are all established for the king's sake. Just as a king is not himself a road, nor is a road itself a king — a road is simply what a king travels upon, and hence is called a "king's road" — so too, an abode is not itself consciousness, nor is consciousness itself an abode; an abode is simply what consciousness comes to rest upon, and hence is called an "abode of consciousness" — and it is for this reason that consciousness is not itself an abode of consciousness. Others say: whatever dharma is ridden and driven by consciousness, exactly as an elephant, horse, boat, or person is ridden and driven by someone, is what is established as an abode of consciousness, not consciousness itself, since it is consciousness that rides and drives it. Further: whatever dharma arises together with consciousness, abides together with it, and perishes together with it, exercising some function with respect to consciousness, is established as an abode of consciousness — consciousness, with respect to consciousness, does not function in this way. Others say: it is by the very nature of things that an abode of consciousness exists together with consciousness in the present alone, being what consciousness abides upon — consciousness, with respect to consciousness, cannot possibly stand in this same relation. Question: since one's own consciousness and another's consciousness both exist together in the present, why does one not establish an abode of consciousness through their mutual relation as well? Answer: one's own consciousness, with respect to one's own consciousness, is not an abode of consciousness, nor, with respect to another's consciousness, is there any difference in this regard. Further: even with respect to one's own, closely related consciousness, it is not an abode of consciousness — how much less, then, with respect to something so much more distant and unrelated. Others say: whatever dharma arises together with consciousness through the combination of the three factors, each exercising its own function upon the other, is established as an abode of consciousness — consciousness, with respect to consciousness, does not arise through such a threefold combination, and hence is not an abode of consciousness.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The throne-and-road image does more than merely illustrate a grammatical asymmetry between subject and object: it captures something genuinely structural about how this tradition conceives of consciousness itself — always the rider, never the ridden, always doing the resting rather than being rested upon, a directionality built into the very logic of what consciousness is.

6. Each Realm Its Own Consciousness's Own House

13. The Border City No One Wants to Call Home

A vivid closing image explains why the evil destinies and the fourth dhyāna's non-Asaṃjñisattva heavens are excluded even from the broader nine abodes of sentient beings: neither place is one beings come to by their own free choice, nor one they wish to remain in once there, exactly like a kingdom's own troubled border city, forever harassed by bandits and hostile neighbors, its wealthy families relocating elsewhere despite a token garrison left to hold the post, and visiting merchants warned bluntly by the garrison itself, "there is nothing here worth relying upon" — a place, everyone agrees, that is "no true city at all."

16. Why the Buddha Insists on Calling Heaven a Mere 'Place'

A closing meditation on language asks why the Buddha specifically calls the Asaṃjñisattva heaven and the Peak of Existence mere "places," rather than granting them the fuller dignity of an "abode" — with every explanation aimed at the same target: certain outside-path teachers mistook these two states, prized for their extraordinary length of life, for genuine, irreversible liberation, and the Buddha's own choice of the plainer word "place" was a deliberate corrective, insisting that both remain restless, impermanent, and, however long-lived, still fully within the wheel of birth-and-death.

18. The Exhaustive Ledger for Great Elements — and Where Derived Form Differs

An intricate combinatorial ledger works through every pairing of grasped and ungrasped great elements — sentient or insentient, past, present, or future — specifying precisely which causal and predominant conditions link each pair, down to the fine distinction of exactly which of the six fundamental causes applies in each single case. The same exhaustive treatment then begins again for derived form, immediately surfacing one clean structural difference: grasped derived form, unlike grasped great elements, never serves as a genuine causal condition for other grasped derived form at all, since resistant forms, standing side by side, can never function as a co-existent cause for one another.
