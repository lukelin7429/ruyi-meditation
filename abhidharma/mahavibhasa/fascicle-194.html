Perceptions and Applied Thought

Fascicle 194 continues the Nyāsa of the Three States of Existence (三有納息第二之三), first reworking the four-fold cultivate/contemplate analysis of the perception of impermanence under an alternate reading of what "cultivation" means, then applying the identical method to the perception of foulness and, by extension, all ten perceptions. The second half of the fascicle turns from perception to applied thought (尋, vitarka): the thought of desire, malice, and harming, and at greatest length the thought of renunciation, each pinned to an exhaustive sequence of practice stages across every alternative of the tetralemma. This page carries Ru‑Yi's complete translation.

The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 194 is dense with practice-stage cross-referencing: warmth, summit, forbearance, and the supreme mundane dharma; the path of seeing and its sixteen mind-moments; the nine grounds of relinquishing defilement; and the refining of faculties from liberated-by-conviction to arrived-through-insight and from liberated-dependent-on-time to unshakeable. Terms established across fascicles 93–193 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The Perception of Impermanence Revisited: An Alternate View of Cultivation

A second school of thought reopens the four-fold analysis of cultivating and contemplating the perception of impermanence from the previous fascicle, this time reading the treatise as referring to both acquisitional cultivation and habitual cultivation together, not habitual cultivation alone — reworking the same tetralemma with a distinct set of practice-stage attributions.

As for those who hold that the treatise here is composed with reference to both acquisitional cultivation and habitual cultivation together, they explain: if one cultivates the perception of impermanence, this means the perception of impermanence, whether manifest in the present or not manifest in the present, is being cultivated. As for contemplating that perception of impermanence, this means taking the perception of impermanence as one's object — that is, at the very time one is cultivating it, taking the meaning of the perception of impermanence as one's object. If one cultivates the perception of impermanence, does one thereby contemplate the perception of impermanence? Answer: this should be explained through four alternatives. There is cultivating the perception of impermanence without contemplating the perception of impermanence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That the compilers preserve two competing readings of what "cultivation" even means in this passage — habitual cultivation alone, or acquisitional and habitual cultivation together — and then patiently rework the entire tetralemma under the second reading rather than silently picking a winner, is characteristic of how this treatise handles live scholastic disagreement: both readings get the full combinatorial treatment, and the reader is left to weigh them.

2. The Perception of Foulness: The Same Four Alternatives

The identical four-fold method is now applied to the perception of foulness, pinned to its own sequence of practice stages — from the preliminary stages of warmth and summit, through relinquishing the defilements of successive dhyānas, to the analytical knowledges and the subtle mind preceding the attainment of cessation — before the treatise notes that every one of the ten perceptions, foulness through cessation, follows this same pattern.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Across this long stretch, the fourth alternative — neither giving rise to nor contemplating a given thought or perception — is never defined by what it positively is, only by exclusion: "apart from the foregoing cases." The treatise seems content to let a tetralemma's residual case remain a residue, its content fixed entirely by what the first three alternatives have already claimed.

Fascicle 194 Complete

This closes fascicle 194 of Ru‑Yi's translation. The fascicle opens by reworking the four-fold cultivate/contemplate analysis of the perception of impermanence under the alternate reading that the treatise refers to both acquisitional and habitual cultivation together, then applies the identical method to the perception of foulness across its own sequence of practice stages, before noting that the remaining perceptions — distaste for food through cessation — all follow the same fourfold pattern. The second half turns from perception (想) to applied thought (尋, vitarka): the thought of desire is worked through all four alternatives in full, with the thought of malice and the thought of harming following by direct analogy; the thought of renunciation then receives the longest and most detailed treatment in the fascicle, its four alternatives traced across the dhyāna-interval, all four dhyānas, their proximate stages, and the formless attainments, closing with the note that the thought of non-malice and the thought of non-harming follow the same pattern, and that the three unwholesome and three wholesome kinds of applied thought are treated at length in the Contemplation Section of the Miscellaneous Aggregation Chapter.
