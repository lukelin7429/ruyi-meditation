The Dirty Sandal That Would Not Come Clean

Fascicle 180 continues the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之七): Bāhika's chase through six great cities, Cūṭapanthaka's path from expulsion for dullness to sudden arhatship through a single dirty sandal, an extended meditation on why the Buddha says no dharma turns as swiftly as mind, the contrast between Śāriputra's mastery of one understanding and Mahākoṣṭhila's circulation through all four, the four unimpeded understandings defined and run through their full doctrinal coordinates, Mahāmaudgalyāyana's apparently mistaken predictions, and why the pratyekabuddha, though he too attains the unimpeded understandings, can never teach the Dharma to others. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Bāhika and the Chase Through Six Great Cities

Fascicle 180 continues the Non-Returner Nyāsa (不還納息第四之七) by finishing the etymology of Bāhika and his companions' "soft, pliant, harmonious" minds, illustrated by a genuinely striking story: three seekers, hearing a Buddha had appeared, set out for Śrāvastī, only to have the Buddha — knowing their wholesome roots would ripen only through the journey itself — take a different road each time, so that the three chase him back and forth through six great cities, six full circuits, without a single thought of weariness, until the moment they finally behold him from a distance is itself the moment they attain stream-entry.

Chinese Source

尊者婆呬迦等心濡心調柔心和順增上者。等言即攝尊者頗洛迦。尊者至履迦。此中心濡心調柔心和順名異義一。如其次第以後釋前。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the Venerable Bāhika and the others excelling in a soft mind, a pliant mind, and a harmonious mind — the word "others" here includes the Venerable Phalguna and the Venerable Cīravāsika. Here, "soft mind," "pliant mind," and "harmonious mind" differ in name but are one in meaning, each in turn explaining the one before it —

或心濡者是總句。心調柔者別顯無憍心心和順者別顯無慢。云何得知婆呬迦等有心濡等事。曾聞彼三人聞佛出世。訪知佛在室羅筏城。三人相隨從王舍城往室羅筏。世尊知彼經涉往反善根方熟。即取別路從室羅筏往王舍城。婆呬迦等至室羅筏。聞佛已復往王舍城。即復相將還趣王舍。至已聞佛已往瞻波。復更相將往瞻波國。

— or, "soft mind" may be taken as the general term, "pliant mind" as a particular term displaying that the mind is without arrogance, and "harmonious mind" as a particular term displaying that the mind is without conceit. How is it known that Bāhika and the others indeed possessed a soft mind and the rest? It has been heard that these three, upon hearing that a Buddha had appeared in the world, inquired and learned that the Buddha was in the city of Śrāvastī, and the three together set out from Rājagṛha toward Śrāvastī. The World-Honored One, knowing that their wholesome roots would ripen only through the very experience of traveling back and forth in this way, took a different road and went from Śrāvastī to Rājagṛha. Bāhika and the others, arriving at Śrāvastī, heard that the Buddha had already gone to Rājagṛha, and at once turned back together toward Rājagṛha. Arriving there, they heard that he had already gone to Campā, and again turned together toward the country of Campā.

至已尋訪聞佛已往婆羅痆斯。三人相隨復往彼國。至已承佛已往劫比羅筏窣覩城。復共詣彼承佛已往吠舍離城。如是世尊於六大城循環六反。婆呬迦等亦恒尋佛六反往還。佛知彼人根熟時。至於室羅筏與其相見。佛方入城彼從城出忽遙見佛歡喜不堪。足未至地得預流果。於後不久成阿羅漢。然彼三人若不心濡調柔和順便應趣在一城待佛。豈能尋佛遍六大城循環六反。初無一念勞倦之心。纔見佛時便證聖果故。佛讚言。婆呬迦等敏捷第一。

Arriving there, they made inquiries and heard that the Buddha had already gone to Vārāṇasī, and the three together went on to that country as well. Arriving there, they learned that he had already gone to the city of Kapilavastu, and together proceeded there as well, only to learn that he had already gone to the city of Vaiśālī. In this way the World-Honored One circled through six great cities, six times over, and Bāhika and the others likewise pursued the Buddha, journeying back and forth through those same six cities six times. When the Buddha knew that these men's faculties had ripened, he arrived at Śrāvastī and met with them there. Just as the Buddha was entering the city, they were leaving it, and, suddenly beholding the Buddha from afar, their joy became unbearable — before even their feet had touched the ground again, they attained the fruit of stream-entry, and, not long afterward, became arhats. Yet had these three not possessed a soft, pliant, and harmonious mind, they surely would instead have simply settled in a single city to await the Buddha there — how else could they have pursued him through all six great cities, circling six times over, without a single thought of weariness ever arising, until the very moment they beheld the Buddha they realized the noble fruit? It is for this reason that the Buddha praised them, saying: "Bāhika and the others are foremost in quickness."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Having the Buddha deliberately dodge three earnest seekers for six full circuits between cities, rather than simply waiting to be found, reframes the entire pursuit as itself the practice: their own wholesome roots needed the exertion of the chase to ripen, so that what looks from outside like a comedy of missed connections is, on the treatise's own account, the precise mechanism by which their liberation was being prepared all along.

2. Cūḷapanthaka and Mahāpanthaka: The Two Mindfulness Foundations

The treatise turns to the two brothers Mahāpanthaka and Cūḷapanthaka — one sharp-witted and view-dominated, chiefly dwelling in the mindfulness-foundation of dharmas; the other craving-dominated and so dull that he could not memorize a single verse in four months, chiefly dwelling in the mindfulness-foundation of mind — and tells their full story: Cūḷapanthaka's worldly decline into poverty and grief, his brother's harsh expulsion from the monastic gate for his dullness, and the Buddha's own tender intervention, teaching him a verse on removing dust from monks' sandals. The turning point comes when Cūḷapanthaka finds he can polish one sandal to perfect cleanliness but not the other, and the simple, devastating insight — that if even outer dust resists cleaning, how much harder must it be to cleanse the mind's own inner grime of greed, hatred, and delusion — brings him instantly to arhatship.

Chinese Source

問如說我弟子中小路於心迴善。大路於想迴善。此二何差別。答尊者小路多住於心循心觀念住。尊者大路多住於法循法觀念住。問何故尊者小路多住心念住。尊者大路多住法念住耶。答由彼尊者意樂異故。復次尊者小路是愛行者。彼由心力無始時來於生死中多受苦惱。今成無學常訶責心由此多住心念住觀。尊者大路是見行者彼由想力無始時來於生死中多受苦惱。今成無學常訶責想由此多住法念住觀。問此二尊者何故立此名耶。答曾聞室羅筏有婆羅門婦數生男生已輒死。其婦未幾復產一男。即時遣人棄之大路。經久不死故立此名。彼婦後時復生一子還即遣人棄之小路。亦經久不死因立此名。尊者大路利根見行。至年長大歸佛出家。精進修行成阿羅漢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: as it is said, "among my disciples, Cūḷapanthaka excels in turning inward upon mind, while Mahāpanthaka excels in turning inward upon perception" — what difference is there between these two? Answer: the Venerable Cūḷapanthaka chiefly dwells in the mindfulness-foundation that contemplates mind by way of mind, whereas the Venerable Mahāpanthaka chiefly dwells in the mindfulness-foundation that contemplates dharmas by way of dharmas. Question: why does the Venerable Cūḷapanthaka chiefly dwell in the mindfulness-foundation of mind, while the Venerable Mahāpanthaka chiefly dwells in the mindfulness-foundation of dharmas? Answer: because their own respective dispositions differ. Furthermore, the Venerable Cūḷapanthaka is one whose conduct is dominated by craving, and it was by the force of mind that he underwent so much suffering through beginningless saṃsāra; now that he has become a non-trainee, he constantly reproaches mind itself, and it is for this reason that he chiefly dwells in the contemplation of the mindfulness-foundation of mind. The Venerable Mahāpanthaka is one whose conduct is dominated by views, and it was by the force of perception that he underwent so much suffering through beginningless saṃsāra; now that he has become a non-trainee, he constantly reproaches perception itself, and it is for this reason that he chiefly dwells in the contemplation of the mindfulness-foundation of dharmas. Question: for what reason were these two venerable ones given these particular names? Answer: it has been heard that, once, in Śrāvastī, there was a brahmin's wife who repeatedly bore sons, each of whom died shortly after birth. Before long, this wife bore yet another son, and at once sent someone to abandon him on the great road, but, after a long time, he still had not died, and so he was given the name "Great Road." That wife, at a later time, bore yet another son, and likewise at once sent someone to abandon him on the small road; and he, too, after a long time, still had not died, and so he was given the name "Small Road." The Venerable Mahāpanthaka, of sharp faculties and conduct dominated by views, upon reaching manhood, took refuge in the Buddha and went forth, and, through diligent cultivation, became an arhat.

尊者小路愛行鈍根。樂處居家保戀親屬。廣致財產位望奢豪。宗族熾盛受諸欲樂後皆衰減。如伽他曰。

The Venerable Cūḷapanthaka, however, was of conduct dominated by craving and of dull faculties, delighting in remaining a householder, clinging fondly to his family, amassing great wealth and property, and holding high rank in lavish extravagance; his clan flourished and he enjoyed every sensual pleasure — yet all of this, in the end, declined. As a verse says:

財積後必盡　位高後必退親合後必離　壽住後必死由此小路於後未幾父母喪亡。財寶散失退捨豪位。眷屬乖離形容顦悴。其兄大路見而愍之度令出家受具足戒。授俱迦聲頌令習誦之。

"Wealth once accumulated must in the end be exhausted; rank once attained must in the end decline; union once formed must in the end be sundered; life once sustained must in the end come to death." Thereupon, not long afterward, Cūḷapanthaka's parents both died, his wealth and treasure were scattered and lost, his lofty rank was relinquished, his family and kin were estranged, and his own appearance grew haggard and wasted. His elder brother Mahāpanthaka, seeing this and taking pity on him, brought him to go forth and receive the full precepts, and taught him a verse composed by the Buddha, having him practice reciting it —

身語意莫作　一切世間惡離欲念正知　不受苦無義彼極闇鈍受此伽他。雨四月中勤苦習誦。牧牛羊者在路聞之誦皆通利。彼猶未得過雨四月。處處苾芻為謁世尊皆來集會。每日晨旦新學苾芻。皆往鄔波陀耶阿遮利耶所受文請義理所廢忘。小路爾時亦效他往將出房戶。兄即問言汝欲何往。答言欲往鄔波陀耶阿遮利耶所受文請義理所廢忘。其兄語言我即是汝鄔波陀耶。更何所往然彼小路是應訶擯而入道者。大路即時手搦其項曳出房外叱言。愚人。

"Let body, speech, and mind do no evil of any kind found in the world; be free of desire, mindful, and clearly comprehending, and undergo no meaningless suffering." Yet Cūḷapanthaka was so extremely dull-witted that, undertaking this verse, he labored painstakingly to memorize it throughout the four months of the rains, while even cowherds and shepherds on the road, merely overhearing it, learned to recite it fluently — and still he had not mastered it, even after those four months had passed. At that time, monks were gathering from every quarter to pay their respects to the World-Honored One, and each morning newly ordained monks would go to their preceptor or teacher to receive instruction, ask about points of meaning, or clear up whatever they had forgotten. Cūḷapanthaka, at that time, imitating the others, likewise set out and was just leaving his room when his brother asked him: "where are you going?" He replied: "I am going to my preceptor and teacher to receive instruction, ask about points of meaning, and clear up what I have forgotten." His brother said to him: "I myself am your preceptor — where else would you go?" — for that Cūḷapanthaka was one who, having entered the path, truly deserved to be reproached and expelled. Mahāpanthaka then seized him by the neck, dragged him out of the room, and rebuked him, saying: "you fool!

我四月中授汝一頌。牧牛羊者誦皆通利。汝猶未得而今乃言欲往他處受文請義理所忘耶。小路既被兄訶擯已至誓多林間啼泣而住。佛時從外入誓多林見而問之。可憐小路汝何以啼泣。彼以上事具白世尊。佛便語言。汝能隨我理所忘不。彼答言能。爾時世尊即以神力轉彼所有誦伽他障更為授之。尋時誦得過前四月所用功勞。復別授以除塵垢頌而語之言。今日苾芻從外來者汝皆可為拭革屣上所有塵垢。小路敬諾如教奉行。至日暮時有一苾芻。革屣極為塵垢所著。

"for four months I have taught you a single verse, which even cowherds and shepherds have learned to recite fluently, and you still have not mastered it — and now you say you wish to go elsewhere to receive instruction and clear up what you have forgotten?" Having been thus reproached and expelled by his brother, Cūḷapanthaka went to the Jetavana grove and stood there weeping. The Buddha, at that time, entering the Jetavana grove from outside, saw him and asked: "poor Cūḷapanthaka, why are you weeping?" He recounted the whole matter to the World-Honored One in full. The Buddha then said to him: "are you able to follow me in clearing up what you have forgotten?" He replied that he was able to. At that time the World-Honored One, by his own supernormal power, removed whatever obstruction had prevented him from reciting that verse, and taught it to him afresh; and he at once recited it with mastery, surpassing even the effort he had expended over the previous four months. The Buddha then separately taught him a verse on removing dust and grime, and told him: "whatever monks come in today from outside, you may wipe off all the dust and grime from their leather sandals." Cūḷapanthaka respectfully assented and carried this out exactly as instructed. Toward evening there came a monk whose leather sandals were extremely caked with dust and grime;

小路拭之一隻極淨一隻苦拭而不能淨即作是念。外物塵垢暫時染著猶不可淨。況內貪欲瞋癡等垢長夜染心何由能淨。作是念時。彼不淨觀及持息念便現在前。次第即得阿羅漢果。問小路何緣如是闇鈍。答尊者小路於昔迦葉波佛法中。具足受持彼佛三藏。由法慳垢覆蔽其心。曾不為他授文解義及理廢忘。由彼業故今得如是極闇鈍果。有說。彼尊者曾於婆羅痆斯城作販猪人。縛五百猪口運置船上度至彼岸。及下船時氣不通故猪皆已死。由彼業力如是闇鈍。有說。彼尊者昔餘生中曾閉塞瞿陀獸窟門令不得出在中而死。由彼業故闇鈍如是。如世尊說苾芻當知。我不見一法速疾迴轉猶如心者。

Cūḷapanthaka wiped it, and one sandal became perfectly clean, while the other, however hard he wiped, would not come clean, and he then reflected: "if even the dust and grime of an outer object, which stains it only briefly, cannot be made clean, how much less can the inner grime of greed, hatred, and delusion, which has stained the mind through the long night of saṃsāra, ever be made clean?" Just as he gave rise to this reflection, the contemplation of impurity and mindfulness of breathing manifested before him, and, in due succession, he at once attained the fruit of arhatship. Question: for what reason was Cūḷapanthaka so extremely dull-witted? Answer: the Venerable Cūḷapanthaka, in a past life within the Dharma of the Buddha Kāśyapa, had fully upheld that Buddha's own Tripiṭaka, yet, his mind covered over by the taint of stinginess with the Dharma, he never once taught others its wording, explained its meaning, or cleared up their difficulties; and it was on account of that very karma that he now obtains such an extremely dull-witted result. Some say: that venerable one had once, in the city of Vārāṇasī, worked as a pig-butcher, and, having bound five hundred pigs and loaded them onto a boat to ferry them across to the far shore, found, upon disembarking, that all the pigs had already suffocated to death; and it is by the force of that karma that he is so dull-witted. Some say: that venerable one, in some other past life, had once sealed shut the mouth of a badger's den, preventing it from escaping, so that it died trapped within; and it is on account of that karma that he is so dull-witted. As the World-Honored One states: "monks, you should know — I see no single dharma that turns so swiftly as does mind."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a monk too dull to memorize a single verse in four months becomes an arhat through nothing more than noticing that one sandal won't come clean is one of this tradition's most quietly radical claims: liberating insight, on this account, does not require intellectual sophistication at all — it requires only that an ordinary, humble observation be allowed to land with its full weight, which a mind cluttered with cleverness might have brushed past unnoticed.

3. Why "Mind Alone" Turns Swiftly

Cūḷapanthaka's story sets up an extended technical meditation on the Buddha's own famous saying that no dharma turns as swiftly as mind, and why mental factors — which turn just as swiftly, by the very same logic — are never mentioned alongside it. More than a dozen candidate explanations are catalogued in succession: mind as forerunner, as the far-traveler, as sovereign, as the appropriating consciousness at "the lord of the city," as the one thing capable of instantly determining rebirth in heaven or hell, as the leader mental factors merely follow the way female fish follow a male. The section closes by reconciling this claim with the Buddha's other famous comparison of mind to a restless monkey, concluding that even that simile falls short — for in the time it takes a monkey to release one branch and grasp the next, mind itself has already cycled through a hundred thousand objects.

Chinese Source

所以者何。心速疾迴轉難作譬喻。是故汝等應學善知心善知心迴轉。問所說心速疾迴轉為以世為以所緣。設爾何失。若以世者則一切有為法皆於世速疾迴轉。若以所緣則一切心心所法皆於所緣速疾迴轉。何故但說心耶。答亦以世亦以所緣說心速疾迴轉。然依相續不依剎那。若依剎那說心速疾迴轉者則應於世有少分速疾迴轉。少分不速疾迴轉。亦無於所緣速疾迴轉。以說若法為彼所緣此法無時非彼所緣故。由此但依相續說心速疾迴轉。謂一身中心或時善或時不善或時無記。或時依眼乃至或時唯依於意。或時緣色乃至或時緣法。一一類中復轉易故。問諸心所法亦有如是速疾迴轉。何故但說心耶。答亦應說心所而不說者。應知是佛有餘之說。亦是隨緣簡略之說。有說。此中舉心亦攝心所以同聚故。有說。此中說最勝者如說王來。有說。心所依心以心故名心所。以心是大地故心所名大地所有。故說心時亦說心所。有說。他心智證通無間道但緣於心是故偏說。有說。此中心聲總說一切心及心所。以彼皆有積集義故。有說。心是前導故但說心。如伽他言意為法前導等。有說。心名遠行。如伽他言心遠行獨行等。有說。心名為王。如伽他言第六增上王等。復次心名為依。如契經說五根行處境界各別。意兼受用五根行處及彼境界彼依意故。復次心名城主如契經說。言城主者即有取識由如是義故但說心。復有說者。心能發起善戒惡戒是故偏說。如契經說善戒惡戒俱依心起。有說。心嶮生惡趣。心平生善趣。故但說心如契經說。都提耶子鸚鵡儒童。以於佛邊起惡心故身壞命終。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? Because the swift turning of mind is difficult to illustrate by any simile — and it is for this reason that you should train yourselves to thoroughly know mind, and to thoroughly know the turning of mind. Question: is this swift turning of mind spoken of with respect to time, or with respect to its objective support? And, granting either, what difficulty follows? If with respect to time, then every conditioned dharma alike turns swiftly with respect to time; and if with respect to its objective support, then every mind and mental factor alike turns swiftly with respect to its objective support — why, then, is mind alone spoken of? Answer: the swift turning of mind is spoken of both with respect to time and with respect to its objective support, yet with reference to its own continuum, not to a single momentary instant. Were the swift turning of mind spoken of with reference to a single moment, then it would follow that mind turns swiftly with respect to only a portion of time and not swiftly with respect to the rest, and that it does not turn swiftly with respect to its objective support at all — since it is said that, for whatever dharma serves as an object, that same dharma is at every moment its object without exception. It is for this reason that the swift turning of mind is spoken of only with reference to its own continuum — that is, within a single body, mind is sometimes wholesome, sometimes unwholesome, sometimes neutral; sometimes it depends on the eye, and so on up to sometimes depending only on the mental faculty; sometimes it takes form as its object, and so on up to sometimes taking dharmas as its object — shifting and changing within each and every one of these categories in turn. Question: mental factors, too, possess this same swift turning — why, then, is mind alone spoken of? Answer: mental factors, too, ought properly to be mentioned, and the fact that they are not should be understood as an incomplete statement on the Buddha's part, a mere abbreviated statement made in accordance with the occasion. Some say: mind is mentioned here in a way that also subsumes mental factors, since they belong to the same aggregate grouping. Some say: what is spoken of here is simply the most eminent member of a group, just as one says "the king has come," meaning the king together with his retinue. Some say: mental factors depend upon mind, and it is on account of mind that they are named "mental" [factors]; and, since mind is like the great earth, mental factors are named as belonging to that great earth, and so, when mind is spoken of, mental factors are thereby spoken of as well. Some say: the uninterrupted path of the realization of the superknowledge of the cognition of others' minds takes only mind as its object, and it is for this reason that mind alone is specifically mentioned. Some say: the very word "mind" here speaks collectively of the whole of mind and mental factors together, since both alike possess the sense of "accumulation." Some say: mind is mentioned alone because it serves as the forerunner, as a verse states, "mind is the forerunner of all dharmas," and so on. Some say: mind is named "the far-traveler," as a verse states, "mind travels far, travels alone," and so on. Some say: mind is named "the sovereign," as a verse states, "the sixth, the dominant sovereign," and so on. 4. Śāriputra and Mahākoṣṭhila: One Understanding versus All Four

The treatise compares two disciples each declared foremost in his own way — Śāriputra in wisdom and eloquence, Mahākoṣṭhila in the unimpeded understandings — and asks the obvious question: is Mahākoṣṭhila then superior, since he possesses all four unimpeded understandings while Śāriputra chiefly dwells in only one? The answer inverts the apparent hierarchy: Śāriputra is in fact superior precisely because he has full mastery over all four and simply chooses, at will, to rest in the understanding of meaning alone, whereas Mahākoṣṭhila, not yet having attained full mastery over any single one, must circulate constantly through all four for fear of forgetting where he stands — illustrated by two monks who have both memorized the four Āgamas, one fluently enough to recite whichever he pleases, the other so uncertainly that he must recite all four in strict rotation just to keep them straight.

6. The Doctrinal Coordinates of the Four Unimpeded Understandings

The treatise runs all four unimpeded understandings through its full technical grid — ground, support, mode of activity, objective support, mindfulness-foundation, classification of cognition, conjoined concentration and faculty, time, moral quality, realm-binding, trainee status, and abandonment — a sprawling comparative table that consistently finds the understandings of meaning and eloquence the most expansive (reaching across all three realms, both contaminated and uncontaminated states, both the trainee's and non-trainee's domain) while dharma and language remain comparatively narrow and worldly. The section closes with the effortful practice each understanding demands — one proposal ties each to a distinct external discipline (grammar, the Buddha's own word, phonetics, logic), while another insists all four are ultimately cultivated through the very same practice: working through a single verse's proper pronunciation, meaning, expression, and delivery in turn.

7. Where and by Whom the Unimpeded Understandings Arise, and Their Order

Confining the unimpeded understandings to the desire realm, the human destiny, and — against a restrictive rival view naming only Jambudvīpa and only male bodies — all three southern continents in either sex, the treatise narrows the class of persons capable of giving rise to them down to the same familiar figure as wish-knowledge and non-contention: the noble non-trainee liberated regardless of time, uniquely combining mastery in concentration with a mental continuum finally free of affliction's grip. A genuine dispute then follows over the order in which the four arise — meaning first (since knowing what one wants to say precedes finding the words), or dharma first (since knowing the words precedes grasping their sense), or, on a third view, language first of all, since even understanding conventional speech itself must come before anything built upon it can arise.

9. Why the Pratyekabuddha Cannot Teach the Dharma

If the pratyekabuddha, too, attains the unimpeded understandings, why does he never teach? The treatise offers an extraordinary cascade of answers — he loves solitude and fears crowds; he lacks a teacher's instruction to follow and so lacks the fearlessness and great compassion that only come paired with such instruction; giving a mind oriented toward nirvāṇa even a moment's rein would plunge him instantly into the bliss of cessation, since only the Buddha's own great compassion and equanimity can sustain a liberated being's continued presence in the world; and, most poignantly, he lacks the dharma of never forgetting, so that whatever he carefully arranges in solitary forest meditation may simply slip from memory the moment he steps into a village to beg. A final resolution addresses the apparent contradiction with scripture's claim that only the Buddha attains the unimpeded understandings at all: this refers only to their most complete, error-free, and masterful form, not to their bare possession.

Question: why is it that the two vehicles, one and all, do not attain the unimpeded understandings? Answer: "unimpeded understanding" is named for a never-regressing cognition, yet the cognition of the śrāvaka and of the pratyekabuddha does regress with respect to various objects, since their own predictions and pronouncements do contain error, and hence they are not unimpeded understandings. According to this latter view, the first account is to be preferred, since the cognition of the śrāvaka and of the pratyekabuddha never regresses with respect to their own proper scope of objects.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The pratyekabuddha's own vulnerability — that a mind oriented toward liberation would, without a Buddha's sustaining compassion, simply vanish into cessation the very next moment — reveals a structural asymmetry this tradition builds into awakening itself: liberation and the willingness to remain in the world to teach it turn out to be two entirely separate achievements, and only a Buddha's own distinctive compassion bridges the gap between them.

10. How Wish-Knowledge and the Other Cognitions Subsume Each Other

The fascicle closes with a compact subsumption chart mapping how the seven cognitions cultivated through the terminal concentration — wish-knowledge, terminal cognition, non-contention cognition, and the four unimpeded understandings — relate to one another, before returning to the terminal dhyāna's own technical substance: six cognitions share it (all but the unimpeded understanding of language), since it is specifically the uppermost grade of the fourth dhyāna alone that earns the name "terminal" — though other masters extend the very same designation to the uppermost grade of every dhyāna, yielding seven distinct terminal states rather than one, while agreeing that only the fourth dhyāna's own terminal grade can actually draw these cognitions forth.

According to what is respectively fitting, each of these seven is said to subsume itself, excepting non-contention cognition and the unimpeded understanding of language, and none of them subsumes terminal cognition. Other masters say: the uppermost grade of each of the four dhyānas alike is named "terminal," and hence, on their account, the terminal dhyāna comprises seven distinct kinds, subsumed among the seven cognitions with a corresponding set of distinctions, to be understood in accordance with what has already been explained above; yet it remains the case that only the terminal grade of the fourth dhyāna, and no other, is capable of drawing forth these cognitions.
