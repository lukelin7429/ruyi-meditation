The Divine Eye, King Sarvadatta, and a Census of Every Realm

Fascicle 150 completes the divine eye investigation with the story of King Sarvadatta, the Bodhisattva's own penetrating vision, and a Wheel-Turning King's treasurer drawing jewels from the Gaṅgā, before closing with an exhaustive census of faculty-possession across every realm of rebirth. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 150 complete, 17 sections Source: Taishō T1545, vol. 27, fascicle 150 Topic: 根蘊第六中觸納息第三之二 concludes (the Faculties Aggregate; the Contact Nyāsa concludes)

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 150 continues and concludes the Contact Nyāsa (觸納息第三之二). Terms established across fascicles 93–149 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Does the Divine Eye Cut Off the Naturally Born Eye?

Fascicle 150 continues the divine eye discussion with a genuinely delicate puzzle: when the divine eye becomes present, is the ordinary, naturally born eye thereby cut off or not — and either answer seems to cause trouble, either contradicting the Ābhidharmikas' own rule that ripening-produced matter never resumes once cut off, or else raising the question of how two functioning eyes could fail to see double. Venerable Ghoṣa's own resolution, and four further positions besides, each try a different way through the dilemma.

Chinese Source

問若天眼現在前時。生得眼為斷不。若斷者云何不異熟生色斷已復續。以阿毘達磨者。不欲令異熟生色斷已續故。若不斷者天眼生得眼二俱見色。云何不錯亂耶。答應言不斷以異熟色斷已不續故。是故尊者妙音作如是問。天眼現在前時生得眼當言斷耶。不斷耶。答當言不斷。即於是處有色界大種與所造天眼俱現在前。問若爾者天眼生得眼二俱見色云何不錯亂耶。答天眼起時生得眼住彼同分故無有過。譬如餘識現在前時。雖不見色而眼不斷此亦如是。問何故不俱見耶。答以一身中無二識俱起。爾時識依天眼不依生得眼故。有說。有色非是生得眼境。為見彼故起天眼現前故。於爾時生得眼雖不斷而無用是故不俱見。有說。天眼起時生得眼斷。問若爾云何不異熟生色斷已可續耶。如是則違阿毘達磨者說。答斷有二種。一暫斷。二究竟斷。暫斷者可續非究竟斷。是故無過。有說。爾時生得眼滅天眼生。天眼滅生得眼生。彼身中眼根未甞空故不可謂斷。有說。彼時生得眼斷亦無有過。亦有異熟生色斷已續故。云何知然。

Ru-Yi Translation

One might ask: when the divine eye becomes present, is the naturally born eye thereby cut off, or not? If it is cut off, how can this be reconciled with the Ābhidharmikas' own refusal to allow that ripening-produced matter, once cut off, can ever resume again? But if it is not cut off, then both the divine eye and the naturally born eye would be seeing forms at once — how could this fail to produce confusion? Answer: it should be said that it is not cut off, since ripening-produced matter, once cut off, never resumes — and it is for this very reason that the Venerable Ghoṣa raised precisely this question: when the divine eye becomes present, should the naturally born eye be said to be cut off, or not cut off? Answer: it should be said to be not cut off — the form realm's own great elements, together with the divine eye they produce, simply become present together at that very same location. One might then ask: if so, how can this fail to produce confusion, with both the divine eye and the naturally born eye seeing forms at once? Answer: when the divine eye arises, the naturally born eye abides as its own similar-class counterpart, and so no fault arises — exactly as, when any other kind of consciousness becomes present, the eye, though not itself cut off, does not thereby see form; the same holds true here. One might then ask: why do the two not see together? Answer: a single body never gives rise to two consciousnesses at once, and at that moment consciousness relies on the divine eye rather than on the naturally born eye. Another position holds that there is form which is not the naturally born eye's own object; the divine eye arises precisely in order to see that form, and so, at that moment, although the naturally born eye is not cut off, it simply has no function to perform, and this is why the two do not see together. Another position holds that the naturally born eye is indeed cut off when the divine eye arises. One might then ask: if so, how could ripening-produced matter, once cut off, ever resume again — would this not contradict the Ābhidharmikas' own position? Answer: cutting-off is of two kinds — temporary cutting-off and final, ultimate cutting-off. What is merely temporarily cut off can indeed resume again; only what is ultimately cut off cannot — hence no fault arises here. Another position holds that, at that moment, the naturally born eye ceases and the divine eye arises, and when the divine eye ceases the naturally born eye arises again — so that the eye-faculty within that body is never, even for an instant, altogether absent, and thus it cannot properly be said to have been "cut off" at all. Another position holds that the naturally born eye is indeed cut off at that moment, and no fault arises even so, since ripening-produced matter, once cut off, genuinely can resume again. How is this known to be so?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The image of the naturally born eye abiding as its own "similar-class counterpart" while the divine eye actually performs the seeing — quietly present but functionally idle, the way the eye itself does not see during, say, a purely auditory moment of consciousness — is an elegant way to let two faculties coexist without either canceling the other out or duplicating each other's work.

2. The Story of King Sarvadatta, and Whether Cut-Off Matter Can Ever Return

One resolution to the puzzle turns on whether ripening-produced matter, once cut off, can ever resume again — and to settle this, the treatise turns to canonical narrative: King Sarvadatta, in a past life of the Buddha, is remembered as gouging out his own eyes to give to a brahmin, only to have them miraculously restored through the power of his own resolve, exactly as the mythic Good Conduct's severed eye and the hell-beings' shattered bodies are both said to regenerate.

Chinese Source

如契經說。一切施王即時舉手自挑兩目施婆羅門。由勝思願令眼平復。又如經說。惡行爾時以朅地羅鉤挑善行眼。亦由菩薩勝思願故還得眼根。施設論說。地獄有山壓迮有情令身碎壞。於後未久諸根復生。諸地獄中此類非一。故知異熟生色斷已可續。如是說者。起天眼時生得眼不滅。異熟色斷亦無續義。問前所引事當云何通。答彼不相違有別義故。即如所說一切施王。爾時但由施心成滿。故作是說實未挑眼。其事云何。謂佛昔日為菩薩時。曾作國王名一切施。能滿一切來求者意。天上人中此名流布。時天帝釋知已念言。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the sūtra itself states: King Sarvadatta immediately raised his own hand and gouged out both his eyes to give to a brahmin, and by the power of his own supreme resolve, his eyes were restored intact. And as another sūtra states: at that time, Evil Conduct gouged out the eye of Good Conduct with an iron hook, and, again by the power of the Bodhisattva's supreme resolve, the eye was restored. The Prajñaptiśāstra states that in the hells there are mountains that crush sentient beings, shattering their bodies utterly, and yet, not long afterward, their faculties are restored again — and there is more than one such instance among the various hells. From this it is known that ripening-produced matter, once cut off, can indeed resume again. On this account, when the divine eye arises the naturally born eye is not extinguished, and there is likewise no sense in which ripening-produced matter, once cut off, resumes again. One might ask: how, then, is the passage already cited to be reconciled? Answer: it is not actually in conflict, since it carries a different meaning altogether. As for what is said of King Sarvadatta, this refers only to the fulfillment of his own intention to give, and it is on that basis that the account is given — in truth, his eyes were never actually gouged out at all. How, then, did the story actually unfold? Long ago, when the Buddha was still a Bodhisattva, he was once a king named Sarvadatta, capable of fulfilling the wishes of anyone who came to him seeking anything at all, and his fame spread throughout the heavens and among humankind alike. Śakra, lord of the gods, hearing of this, reflected to himself:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a technical Abhidharma debate about ripening-produced matter reaches for a canonical Jātaka story as its evidence is a reminder of how tightly narrative and doctrine are woven together in this tradition: a king's own legendary generosity becomes, centuries later, a data point in an argument about the metaphysics of bodily regeneration.

3. Śakra's Disguise, and the Truth Behind the Legend

The story continues with Śakra, lord of the gods, disguising himself as a brahmin to test whether King Sarvadatta's boundless generosity was aimed at Buddhahood or merely at a heavenly throne — and the treatise then supplies its own retrospective correction to the legend: the king's eyes, it turns out, were never actually gouged out at all, since Śakra stopped him the moment his resolve was proven, meaning the miraculous "restoration" of a text like the sūtra was never literally required in the first place.

Chinese Source

彼王如斯惠施無倦。為求無上正等菩提。為希世間名譽天位。若希天位或為我怨。當往驗之知其施意。便自化作婆羅門身。戴帽垂鬢金韜絡體。手策金杖來詣王前。呪願王言願常尊勝。王言梵志來何所求。答言我來正須王眼。王以四寶為眼施之彼不受言。我今須眼何用此為。王聞是已便舉兩手欲自挑目。帝釋知王施心決定便止王言。欲何所求能施難施。為求釋梵魔王位耶。為希世間名譽歸敬。

Ru-Yi Translation

"this king gives so tirelessly and so generously — is he seeking the highest, unsurpassed awakening, or merely worldly fame, or a heavenly throne? If he seeks a heavenly throne, he may yet become my own rival; I ought to go and test him, to learn the true intention behind his giving." So Śakra transformed himself into the form of a brahmin, wearing a cap, his hair hanging loose, his body wrapped in gold, holding a golden staff, and came before the king, offering this blessing: "may you ever remain honored and supreme." The king said: "Brahmin, what have you come seeking?" He replied: "I have come precisely because I need the king's own eyes." The king offered him eyes made of four precious substances instead, but the brahmin refused, saying: "what use have I now for eyes such as these?" Hearing this, the king at once raised both his hands, intending to gouge out his own eyes.

王言此等皆非所求。唯有離於生老病死應正等覺是我所願。天帝聞已便復本形讚嘆王言。真是菩薩不久定得無上菩提。作是言已忽然不現。故彼爾時實未挑眼。又彼所引善惡行經。善行眼根有餘種子。由勝思願圓滿勝前。諸地獄中亦同此釋。若無餘種則不可生。

Seeing that the king's resolve to give was utterly unwavering, Śakra stopped him and said: "what is it you truly seek, that you would give away something so difficult to give? Do you seek the throne of Śakra, of Brahmā, or of Māra? Or worldly fame and homage?" The king replied: "none of these is what I seek — only freedom from birth, old age, sickness, and death, only true and complete awakening, is my one aspiration." Hearing this, the heavenly lord resumed his original form and praised the king, saying: "truly you are a Bodhisattva, and before long you will surely attain unsurpassed awakening." And having spoken these words, he vanished at once. Thus, at that time, the king's eyes were in truth never actually gouged out at all. As for the sūtra concerning Good Conduct and Evil Conduct that was also cited, Good Conduct's own eye-faculty retained a further seed even then, and it was through the fulfillment of a supreme resolve, surpassing what had come before, that it was restored — and the same explanation applies equally to the case described among the various hells: without some further seed remaining, nothing could ever be regenerated at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The treatise's own willingness to reinterpret a beloved canonical story — insisting the king's eyes were, in fact, never actually removed — rather than simply accepting the miracle at face value, shows this literature holding its narrative sources to the same standard of metaphysical consistency it demands of its own technical claims: even a cherished Jātaka must ultimately answer to the Ābhidharmikas' own rule about matter that cannot return once truly destroyed.

4. Why the Naturally Born Eye Survives the Divine Eye's Arising

The story's resolution locks in the treatise's own preferred position: since the underlying legends turn out not to require any actual instance of destroyed matter regenerating, the simplest account survives intact — when the divine eye arises, the naturally born eye is never truly cut off at all.

Chinese Source

故異熟色斷無續理。由此天眼現在前時生得眼不斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is for this reason that ripening-produced matter, once cut off, has no way of resuming again — and it follows from this that, when the divine eye is present, the naturally born eye is not cut off.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Settling a fine metaphysical distinction by first reinterpreting away the narrative evidence that seemed to complicate it is a distinctively Abhidharma move: rather than accepting an exception to a general rule, the treatise prefers to show that the apparent exception was never really an exception in the first place.

5. Desire-Realm Versus Form-Realm Divine Eyes: Which Is Truly Superior?

A new question opens: does the divine eye also arise for a being reborn in the form realm, given that the form realm's own naturally born eye already sees everything the cultivated eye could? The treatise answers that it does arise there too — if only to observe the intermediate state, which the naturally born eye can never see — before comparing the two arisings directly: the desire-realm-cultivated divine eye is sharper in function, while the form-realm-cultivated one is grander in what it depends upon, each superior in its own distinct way.

Chinese Source

問如生欲界所起天眼。生色界亦起不。有說。不起。以色界中隨生得眼所見多少。修得眼亦爾無別作用是故不起。如是說者。亦起現前。問與生得眼同起復何用。答欲遊戲通慧故起現前。又中有身非生得眼境故。起天眼觀中有差別。問為生欲界所起天眼勝。為生色界所起勝耶。答欲界所起猛利故勝。謂佛獨覺到究竟聲聞所起天眼作用猛利。非生色界所能現前。色界所起所依故勝。謂彼依身廣大勝妙。所起天眼多極微成。非欲界中此眼得起。故二界起各有勝劣。

Ru-Yi Translation

One might ask: for one who cultivates the divine eye while born in the desire realm, does the divine eye likewise arise for one born in the form realm? One position holds that it does not, since in the form realm the naturally born eye already sees exactly as much as the cultivated eye ever could, with no distinct additional function for the cultivated eye to perform, and so it does not arise there. Another position holds that it does indeed arise and become present there. One might then ask: what further use could there be in its arising, given that the naturally born eye already accomplishes the very same seeing? Answer: it arises in order to indulge one's mastery of supernormal wisdom for its own sake; moreover, the intermediate-state body is not an object the naturally born eye can ever see, and so the divine eye arises specifically in order to observe the distinctive features of the intermediate state. One might then ask: which is superior — the divine eye cultivated while born in the desire realm, or the one cultivated while born in the form realm? Answer: the one arising in the desire realm is superior in its own sharpness — the divine eye arising for a Buddha, a solitary buddha, or a supreme śrāvaka is exceptionally sharp in its function, sharper than anything that could ever become present through birth in the form realm. The one arising in the form realm is superior in what it depends upon — since the body it relies upon there is vast and exceptionally excellent, the divine eye that arises from it is composed of very many atomic particles, of a kind that could never arise within the desire realm at all. Thus each of the two arisings carries its own superiority and its own inferiority, each in a different respect.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That a Buddha's own peerless divine eye is explicitly said to arise specifically through desire-realm cultivation, sharper than anything the form realm could ever produce despite that realm's own much grander physical basis, is a striking inversion of what a simple hierarchy of realms might predict: sharpness of function and grandeur of basis turn out to be two entirely independent axes of excellence, not two names for the same thing.

6. Cultivated Divine Eye Versus Naturally Born Eye: Six Ways They Differ

The fascicle catalogues, in six successive layers, exactly what distinguishes the cultivated divine eye from the ordinary naturally born eye — starting from a bare difference in name alone, then substance, cause, the effort required to bring it about, what kind of consciousness it can support, its object, and finally its sheer function — before closing with a census of how many supernormal powers can manifest within a single thought-moment, settling on four as the correct count once lingering transformation-work and the absence of a similar-class counterpart for the divine eye and ear are both properly taken into account.

Chinese Source

問修得天眼與生得眼有何差別。答名即差別。謂名天眼名生得眼。

Ru-Yi Translation

One might ask: what distinguishes the cultivated divine eye from the naturally born eye? Answer: in name alone they already differ, one being called the "divine eye" and the other the "naturally born eye."

有說。體亦有異。謂生得眼有同分彼同分。修得天眼唯是同分。又生得眼通所長養及異熟生。修得天眼唯所長養。有說。因亦有異。

Another position holds that their very substance also differs — the naturally born eye possesses both its own similar-class counterpart and a dissimilar counterpart, whereas the cultivated divine eye possesses only its own similar-class counterpart; moreover, the naturally born eye may be either nourished-and-grown or ripening-produced, whereas the cultivated divine eye is only ever nourished-and-grown. Another position holds that their causes also differ —

謂生得眼是業果。修得天眼是修果。問豈不生得眼亦是修果耶。答彼少分是修果。少分是生得智異熟果。天眼唯修果。有說。天眼由加行作意力方得現前。生得眼不爾。有說。果亦有異。謂生得眼與善染無記識為所依。修得天眼唯與無記識為所依。有說。境亦有異。謂生得眼不見中有。修得天眼能見中有。有說。用亦有異。謂修得天眼於生得眼。作用熾盛微妙殊勝。清淨明白捷利遠細故有差別問一念得起幾通果耶。答諸有欲令無留化事。天眼天耳無彼同分者。彼說一念唯起一通果。謂五通隨一。諸有欲令有留化事天眼天耳。無彼同分者。彼說一念得起二通果。謂神境通果及餘四隨一。

namely, the naturally born eye is the fruit of karma, whereas the cultivated divine eye is the fruit of cultivation. One might ask: is the naturally born eye not itself also, in part, a fruit of cultivation? Answer: a portion of it is indeed a fruit of cultivation, and a portion is the ripening-fruit of naturally-born wisdom, whereas the divine eye is solely a fruit of cultivation. Another position holds that the divine eye only ever becomes present through the power of effortful preparatory application and attention, whereas the naturally born eye does not. Another position holds that their fruits also differ — the naturally born eye serves as basis for wholesome, defiled, or neutral consciousness alike, whereas the cultivated divine eye serves as basis only for neutral consciousness. Another position holds that their objects also differ — the naturally born eye cannot see the intermediate state, whereas the cultivated divine eye can. Another position holds that their function also differs — the cultivated divine eye's function, compared to the naturally born eye's, is blazing, subtle, and supremely excellent, pure, bright, swift, and fine, and it is on these grounds that the distinction is drawn. One might ask: how many resultant supernormal powers can arise within a single thought-moment? Those who hold that there is no transformation-work left lingering behind, and that the divine eye and divine ear have no similar-class counterpart, hold that within a single thought-moment only a single resultant supernormal power ever arises — namely, whichever one of the five penetrations happens to be in play. Those who hold that there can be lingering transformation-work, yet that the divine eye and divine ear still have no similar-class counterpart, hold that within a single thought-moment two resultant supernormal powers can arise — namely, the resultant power of supernormal locomotion together with whichever one of the remaining four happens to be in play.

諸有欲令有留化事。天眼天耳有彼同分。彼說一念得起四通果。謂神境通果天眼天耳及餘二隨一。謂他心通宿住。隨念通。境界各別不俱起故。如是說者。應知第二所說為善。以化事可留。天眼天耳必無彼同分。要於用時乃現前故。

Those who hold that there can be lingering transformation-work, and that the divine eye and divine ear do have a similar-class counterpart, hold that within a single thought-moment four resultant supernormal powers can arise — namely, the resultant power of supernormal locomotion, the divine eye, the divine ear, and whichever one of the remaining two happens to be in play, namely the knowledge of others' minds or the knowledge of recollecting past abodes, since their respective objects are each separate and distinct and cannot arise together. On this account, it is this second position that should be understood as correct, since transformation-work can indeed be left lingering, while the divine eye and divine ear fixedly have no similar-class counterpart at all, becoming present only precisely at the moment they are actually put to use.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Working through six entirely separate dimensions of difference — name, substance, cause, effort, support, object, function — before finally settling a seemingly unrelated question about simultaneous supernormal powers, shows how thoroughly this tradition treats even a close terminological cousin like "divine eye" versus "naturally born eye" as two genuinely distinct entities requiring their own full comparative anatomy, not a single thing wearing two different names.

7. Does One Divine Eye See Every Level's Forms, or Does Each Level Need Its Own?

A genuinely tricky question opens: since a fourth-dhyāna divine eye can see the desire realm's forms belonging to all four dhyāna-levels at once, is this the very same eye seeing every level, or does a separate eye arise for each? Two positions answer differently — one appeals to the everyday experience of seeing both a great tree and the fine grass beneath it in a single glance, the other simply accepts that a single level's divine eye may itself comprise five distinct sub-kinds without contradiction — before the fascicle catalogues exactly how far each dhyāna-level's own divine eye can reach upward, from the Brahmā-world all the way to the Bṛhatphala heaven.

Chinese Source

問欲界所化色有四種。謂初靜慮果。乃至第四靜慮果。依初靜慮所得天眼。為能具見四種色耶。有說。具見皆是欲界色處攝故。有說。唯見初靜慮果非餘。以因勝故如因非境果亦應爾。依第二靜慮所得天眼。能見初二靜慮果色非餘。依第三靜慮所得天眼。能見前三靜慮果色非餘。依第四靜慮所得天眼。具能見四靜慮果色。如是說者。謂初說依初靜慮所得天眼。能見欲界初靜慮色。乃至依第四靜慮所得天眼。能見欲界四靜慮色。問依第四靜慮所得天眼。能見欲界四靜慮色者。為見欲界色眼即見餘地色為更起餘眼見餘地色耶。若見欲界色眼。即見餘地色者。如何一眼能見麁細二境。若更起餘眼見餘地色者。即第四靜慮天眼應有五類。各唯見一地色。即不應言此地天眼五地為境。有作是說。即見欲界色眼。能見餘地諸色。問如何一眼能見麁細二境。答此亦無過。如看大山即麁細色俱時能見。見麁色者如見千枝大樹。見細色者如見中間細草。如是第四靜慮一眼能見五地諸色何過。有餘師言。見欲界色眼異。乃至見第四靜慮色眼異。問若爾第四靜慮天眼應有五類。各唯見一地色即不應言此地天眼五地為境。答一地天眼有五類別亦無有過。約地種類。總說一眼見五地境。非不於中所見各異。如定蘊說。依初靜慮所引天眼。極能見何繫色。答乃至梵世繫。依第二靜慮所引天眼。

Ru-Yi Translation

One might ask: the desire realm's own transformed forms are of four kinds, corresponding respectively to the fruits of the first through fourth dhyānas — can the divine eye obtained in dependence on the first dhyāna see all four of these kinds of form at once? One position holds that it can see all four, since every one of them belongs equally to the desire realm's own domain of visible form. Another position holds that it sees only the fruit of the first dhyāna and none of the others, since a cause's own superiority ought to be matched by an equally restricted object. 8. Who Can See Whom? A Census of Mutual Visibility Across the Heavens

The fascicle turns to the Prajñaptiśāstra's own account of mutual visibility across the cosmic hierarchy: lower heavens generally cannot see higher ones without cultivation, supernormal power, or another's assistance, while higher heavens can always see lower ones — with a careful clarification of why even supernormal power and another's assistance are listed as exceptions for the most distant heavens, since actually traveling somewhere is not the same as being able to see it once there.

9. Does the Divine Eye See All Ten Directions at Once, and Is It Really Non-Resistant?

The fascicle addresses two further puzzles about the divine eye's own operation: whether it can see all ten directions simultaneously in a single instant — resolved by distinguishing "unobstructed in every direction" from "seeing every direction at once," since even the divine eye must still face some direction, only without ever needing to turn — and whether it is genuinely non-resistant, resolved by distinguishing resistance-as-object, which the divine eye still has, from resistance-as-obstruction, which it lacks.

The Venerable Vasumitra says: the Bodhisattva's own afflictions were not yet severed, not yet fully known for what they are, and so he remained, even then, deluded by his own ignorance — one should not press the question further, exactly as one would not think to interrogate someone ignorant or blind for having fallen into a pit. Another position holds that, at the very moment of the Bodhisattva's own sensual attachment, he simply was not contemplating impurity, and whenever he did contemplate impurity, he was not, at that same moment, engaged in attachment. Another position holds that the Bodhisattva, in some former life, had planted the karmic seed of a supremely excellent capacity for enjoyment, such that whatever he did come to enjoy, he simply did not perceive as impure, while whatever he did not come to enjoy, he did perceive as impure. On this last account, the Bodhisattva possessed a keen and penetrating wisdom, skilled at discerning the precise difference between virtue and fault — and since a woman's body, too, may genuinely possess both virtues and faults at once, whenever the Bodhisattva contemplated such virtues, he surpassed every being still attached to desire in doing so, and whenever he contemplated such faults, he surpassed every practitioner of the contemplation of impurity in doing so — and it was through contemplating those very virtues that attachment could still arise.

The four competing explanations offered for why the Bodhisattva, despite eyes capable of seeing straight through to a woman's body's own underlying impurity, still engaged in acts of sensual attachment, refuse to simply explain the difficulty away — preserving instead a genuinely uncomfortable tension between the Bodhisattva's penetrating wisdom and his still-unsevered afflictions, treated as a real puzzle deserving several serious, non-dismissive attempts at resolution rather than a single tidy apologetic.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The clarification that thirteen faculties are cut off "in some sense" at the moment of becoming free from form's contamination, yet only five ever reach a truly final, ultimate cutting-off there, is a precise piece of bookkeeping discipline: this tradition consistently refuses to let a merely provisional or partial change in a faculty's status be mistaken for the genuinely permanent severance that only a small, carefully specified subset of faculties actually undergo.

17. The Parallel Census for Cessation-and-Realization, and What Distinguishes the Two Gateways

A mirror-image census runs through cessation-and-realization for each faculty, before the fascicle closes by directly addressing what actually distinguishes this gateway from the full-knowledge gateway just completed — offering four different accounts, from the uninterrupted-path/path-of-liberation division, through cutting-off versus realizing directly, to not-yet-cut-off-becoming-cut-off versus not-yet-realized-becoming-realized, and finally first realization versus a renewed, later one.

"When the eye-faculty through the wisdom-faculty each reach cessation and are realized, how many faculties reach cessation and are realized at that same moment?" Answer: when the eye-faculty reaches cessation and is realized, this extends up to the point of becoming free from the contamination of form — at that point, five faculties reach cessation and are realized; extending up to arhatship, nineteen faculties reach cessation and are realized. The five are the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body faculties; the nineteen are every faculty except the three uncontaminated ones. Exactly as with the eye-faculty, so too with the ear, nose, tongue, and body faculties. When the female faculty reaches cessation and is realized, this extends up to the point of becoming free from the contamination of desire — at that point, four faculties reach cessation and are realized; extending up to arhatship, nineteen faculties reach cessation and are realized. The four are the male, female, displeasure, and pain faculties; the nineteen are exactly as already described. Exactly as with the female faculty, so too with the male, pain, and displeasure faculties. When the life-faculty reaches cessation and is realized, this extends up to arhatship —

this extends up to the point of becoming free from the contamination of the Śubhakṛtsna heaven, at which point the pleasure-faculty itself reaches cessation and is realized; extending up to arhatship, nineteen faculties reach cessation and are realized, exactly as already described. One might ask: what is the distinction between these two gateways — full and exhaustive knowledge, and cessation together with realization? Answer: some hold that the uninterrupted path is what cuts off the fettered obtaining, while the path of liberation realizes the obtaining of freedom from fetters — for these, the full-knowledge gateway corresponds to the very function of the uninterrupted path, and the cessation-and-realization gateway corresponds to the very function of the path of liberation. Others hold that the uninterrupted path both cuts off the fettered obtaining and also realizes the obtaining of freedom from fetters — for these, the full-knowledge gateway corresponds to the cutting-off of the fettered obtaining, while the cessation-and-realization gateway corresponds to the realizing of the obtaining of freedom from fetters — and just as the fettered obtaining is cut off while the freedom-from-fetters obtaining is realized, so too, in just the same way, should be understood the removing of fault and the cultivating of virtue, the abandoning of what is inferior and the realizing of what is sublime, the relinquishing of what is meaningless and the obtaining of what is meaningful, and the exhausting of craving's own oil so as to receive the bliss of one no longer feverish with desire.
