The End and Rebirth of Worlds

Fascicle 133 explains why earth alone can never become a world-ending calamity, the precise mechanics and rhythm of universal destruction by fire, water, and wind, the re-formation of Mount Sumeru out of total emptiness, and closes with a loving description of Śakra's celestial city, Sudarśana, and its wish-granting tree. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

1. Completing the Realm-Condition Catalog: Desire Realm Meets Form Realm

Fascicle 133 opens by completing the exhaustive condition-catalog begun in the previous fascicle, now cross-tabulating desire-realm-bound and form-realm-bound great elements and derived form through every pairwise combination — before turning to a tetralemma on whether desire-realm-bound form is always produced by desire-realm-bound great elements, uncovering a subtle exception: certain undefiled, "unbound" accompanying forms, tied to no realm at all, can still be produced by desire-realm elements when their practitioner happens to have a desire-realm body.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That an undefiled dharma, belonging to no realm whatsoever in its own right, can still be produced by realm-bound great elements shows how carefully this taxonomy separates a dharma's own moral or soteriological status from the merely physical question of what body currently houses it — liberation and location turn out to be entirely independent axes, one never determining the other.

2. Is All Desire-Realm Form Really Made by Desire-Realm Elements? Two Careful Exceptions

The same tetralemma repeats for form-realm-bound form and its own great elements, again surfacing an exception: unbound, undefiled accompanying form tied to the path itself can be produced by form-realm elements without itself belonging to the form realm. A parallel exhaustive analysis for past, present, and future form and their respective great elements follows, working out with precision exactly which time period's elements can produce form belonging to which other time period.

3. Time and Its Two Kinds of Basis: What Created a Thing, and What Sustains It

An elegant two-tier causal distinction emerges from the temporal analysis: an ongoing precept-restraint's unmanifest form depends on two entirely different kinds of "basis" — the past great elements that originally created it, and the present great elements that presently sustain its continued operation. The treatise deliberately speaks only of the former when explaining production, since all five generative causes belong strictly to the past, leaving the latter, quieter kind of dependency unstated but structurally essential.

Chinese Source

諸色過去。彼色一切過去大種所造耶。答應作四句。有色過去非過去大種所造。謂過去大種。所以如前。有色過去大種所造。非過去。謂色未來現在過去大種所造。此復云何。謂未來現在表所起無表。過去大種所造。以依過去表故。有色過去過去大種所造。謂色過去。過去大種所造。此復云何。謂過去一切有對所造色。隨心轉無表。表所起無表。有色非過去非過去大種所造。謂未來現在大種。若色未來現在。現在大種所造。若色未來。未來大種所造。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Distinguishing what created something from what merely sustains its continued operation is a genuinely useful piece of conceptual machinery, applicable well beyond this specific technical context: many things in ordinary experience — habits, institutions, relationships — likewise depend on one set of conditions to have been brought into being and an entirely different, ongoing set merely to keep functioning, and conflating the two is a common source of confusion this distinction quietly forestalls.

4. What Is Earth? What Is Water? The Everyday Word and the Ultimate Substance

A justly famous passage distinguishes the everyday word "earth" — a merely conventional label the world applies, by shared agreement, to certain combinations of color and shape, as when people speak of "blue earth" or "long earth" — from the ultimate earth-element itself, which refers only to the tactile quality of solidity, the one true substance capable of producing derived earth-form at all. The identical distinction is then drawn for water: conventional "water" names a visible color and shape, while the water-element proper is simply the tactile quality of moisture.

Chinese Source

地云何。答顯形色此是世俗想施設地謂諸世間於顯形色。依共假想施設地名。如世間說青黃地等。長短地等。地界云何。答堅性觸。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is earth? Answer: it is color and shape — this is the conventional notion by which "earth" is designated, that is, the world, with respect to color and shape, designates the name "earth" on the basis of a shared, conventional notion — as when the world speaks of "blue earth" or "yellow earth," or of "long earth" or "short earth." What is the earth-element? Answer: it is the tactile quality of solidity —

此是勝義能造地體。

— this is the ultimate substance capable of producing earth.

水云何。答顯形色此是世俗想施設水。謂諸世間於顯形色。依共假想施設水名。如世間說青黃等水。長短等水。云何水界。答濕性觸。此是勝義能造水體。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The everyday word "earth" and the technical earth-element turn out to name entirely different things by the treatise's own account — the ordinary word tracks how something looks, while the ultimate substance tracks what it actually, tactilely is — a distinction that quietly reframes the entire project of Abhidharma physics as an attempt to see past appearance to the specific felt qualities genuinely doing the causal work beneath it.

5. What Is Fire? A Brahmin's Verse on Reading Omens in the Flames — and Why Wind Has No Everyday Name

The same conventional-versus-ultimate distinction completes itself for fire, illustrated with a striking fragment from a brahmin's own scripture on reading fortune in the color of flames — red for sickness, yellow for war, mixed colors for abundance, blue for decline, white or black for ruin. Wind alone breaks the pattern: the everyday world, unlike with earth, water, and fire, forms no conventional color-and-shape notion of wind at all, though some masters note partial exceptions in ordinary phrases like "dusty wind" or various named winds.

Chinese Source

火云何。答顯形色此是世俗想施設火。謂諸世間於顯形色。依共假想施設火名。如世間說青黃等火。長短等火。又如梵志觀火。頌云。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is fire? Answer: it is color and shape — this is the conventional notion by which "fire" is designated, that is, the world, with respect to color and shape, designates the name "fire" on the basis of a shared, conventional notion — as when the world speaks of "blue fire" or "yellow fire," or of "long fire" or "short fire." And, as in the brahmin's own scripture on reading fire, its verse says:

赤焰多疾疫　黃兵緣飢饉綵豐青退減　白黑主興滅火界云何。答煖性觸。此是勝義能造火體。風云何。答即風界。風界云何。答動性觸。問何故不說世俗風耶。答世間於風無假想故。

"A red flame brings many illnesses; A yellow one, war caused by famine; A parti-colored flame, abundance; a blue one, decline; A white or a black flame governs rise and ruin." What is the fire-element? Answer: it is the tactile quality of heat — this is the ultimate substance capable of producing fire. What is wind? Answer: it is simply the wind-element itself. What is the wind-element? Answer: it is the tactile quality of motion. Question: why is there no conventional sense of "wind" spoken of, as there is for the other three? Answer: because the world forms no such conventional notion with respect to wind at all,

有餘師說。世間於風亦起假想少故不說。如世間說此有塵風。此無塵風。毘濕縛風。吠嵐婆風。小風大風。塵輪風等。

— unlike its treatment of color and shape for the other three. Other masters say: the world does form some conventional notion of wind as well, though only a slight one, and hence it is not separately mentioned — as when the world speaks of "a dusty wind" or "a dust-free wind," or of the Viśvabhū wind, the Vairambha wind, small winds and great winds, and whirlwind-dust-column winds, and the like.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

That wind alone resists everyday naming the way earth, water, and fire do not is a quietly telling observation about the psychology of perception: people form conventional, color-based mental pictures readily for whatever presents a visible surface to look at, but wind, felt rather than seen, simply never accumulates the same kind of shared, nameable folk imagery — invisibility itself turns out to be an obstacle to ordinary language, not merely to ordinary sight.

6. Which Senses Perceive the Four Elements, Conventionally and Ultimately

A precise mapping shows that conventional earth, water, and fire — being visible color and shape — fall under the form sense-field, perceived by both eye- and mental-consciousness, while conventional wind falls under touch, perceived by body- and mental-consciousness instead. In their ultimate, technical sense, however, all four elements alike fall under the touch sense-field exclusively, perceived only by body- and mental-consciousness — never the eye, since no purely tactile quality, however visually associated in ordinary experience, can ever itself be seen.

8. Why Can't Earth Ever Become a World-Ending Calamity?

A pivotal cosmological question opens the fascicle's grand cosmological turn: of the four great elements, only fire, water, and wind can ever serve as one of the three world-ending calamities that periodically destroy entire world-systems — never earth. A cascade of explanations converges on a shared logic: a calamity must act as an external, destructive agent, and earth cannot meaningfully destroy itself; only the fierce and sharp can devastate, while earth's own nature is sluggish and dull; and, most strikingly, no calamity of any kind — fire, water, or wind alike — can ever reach the fourth dhyāna, since that level alone lacks any corresponding "internal calamity" of its own, unlike the first three dhyānas, each internally mirroring one of the three external destructive forces.

10. Do Objects Truly Transform to Meet Their Doom? And Why a Calamity Never Crosses Realms

A subtle puzzle asks whether every object throughout the cosmos literally transforms in substance to meet its coming destruction — becoming as dry as straw before fire, as soluble as sugar before water, as loose as flour-dough before wind — and, if so, whether this violates the treatise's own repeated principle that no dharma ever abandons its own characteristic. The firm answer denies any such literal transformation: solidity, moisture, and the rest remain exactly what they always were, and it is solely the accumulated power of beings' own karma that renders the otherwise-indestructible destructible. A further principle confirms that each calamity destroys only its own native realm and ground — a sūtra describing flames reaching the Brahma-heaven must be understood as one fire lighting a distinct, higher fire in unbroken continuity, never a single desire-realm blaze somehow reaching upward on its own.

11. Seven Fires, One Flood: The Precise Rhythm of Destruction

An elegant numerical structure governs the calamities' own sequence: seven fire-calamities occur consecutively, followed by a single water-calamity, this whole cycle repeating seven times, and only then, following the seventh flood, does a single wind-calamity finally arise — a pattern that neatly explains the traditional teaching of the Ābhāsvara heaven's own sixty-four-kalpa lifespan. Ru-Yi Translation

This is the great capital city of Śakra, lord of the gods — the city has a thousand gates, magnificently adorned, each guarded by five hundred blue-robed yakṣas, valiant and dignified, a full yojana in stature, each armored and armed, defending the gates. Within the city stands the Vaijayanta Palace, adorned throughout with every kind of wondrous jewel, so surpassing every other heavenly dwelling that it is called "Surpassing Excellence" — its sides two hundred and fifty yojanas, its circumference a thousand. At the city's four corners stand four towers, formed of gold, silver, and the other precious materials, adorned in every way, exceedingly lovely to behold. Outside the city, on its four sides, are four adorned gardens, where the gods gather to play — first, the Chariot Garden, where every kind of chariot manifests according to each god's own merit and fortune; second, the Coarse Garden, where armor and weapons manifest as needed whenever the gods wish to go to war; third, the Mixed-Forest Garden, where the gods, entering within, share the same innate, co-arising delight in whatever they enjoy; and fourth, the Grove-of-Joy Garden, where the most exquisite objects of sensory pleasure of every kind gather together, endlessly fascinating to behold. These four gardens are each square in shape, each with a circumference of a thousand yojanas, and each has, at its center, a single wish-fulfilling pond, its sides each fifty yojanas, filled with water of the eight virtues, with wondrous flowers appearing according to one's own wish, jeweled boats, and beautiful birds, each singular and marvelous, adorned in every way. Around the four sides of each garden lie four exquisite grounds, each twenty yojanas from the garden itself, each side of these grounds measuring two hundred yojanas — the gods' own supreme playground, where they compete and delight together. Northeast of the city stands the Pārijāta tree, the place where the Thirty-Three Gods enjoy their sensory pleasures — its roots coiling deep and wide across five yojanas, its trunk rising skyward, its branches spreading outward, its height and breadth together equal to a hundred yojanas, its unfurled leaves and blossoming flowers releasing an exquisite, fragrant scent, which, carried on a following wind, perfumes an area of a full hundred yojanas,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Ending an account of universal cosmic destruction and re-formation with this warm, almost domestic inventory of gardens, ponds, and a fragrant tree is a deliberate tonal choice: after twenty eons of emptiness and a fire that leaves no residue, the cosmology comes to rest not on any further abstraction but on the concrete, sensory pleasures of a single beautifully furnished city — cosmic time, having run its full course, arrives back at something as intimate as a garden.
