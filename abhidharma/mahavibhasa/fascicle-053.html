The Monks Who Loved Their Own Voices

Fascicle 53 completes the tetralemma of falling within and existing within the three realms, establishes that form is cut off and disjoined in one and the same instant — illustrated through a lamp whose brightness breaks the darkness while incidentally heating its own vessel and burning its own wick — and works out precisely how the family-to-family, once-returner, and one-interval remain object-bound by their own remaining affliction even after cutting off what they have already cut. The fascicle closes with the three junctures when ripening karma is utterly obstructed and the canonical story of the nun Gopikā and the three monks whose beautiful voices, loved for their own sake rather than the Dharma they carried, cost them everything. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra (阿毘達磨大毘婆沙論), preserved in the Taishō canon (T1545, vol. 27) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. The English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own, and the reading notes are original, grounded in standard Sarvāstivāda scholarship rather than a specific named commentator's lecture transcript.

Fascicle 53 continues the Unwholesome Nyāsa's eighth part (結蘊第二中不善納息第一之八), completing the tetralemma of falling and existing across all three realms opened in fascicle 52, then turning to how form and the mental aggregates are cut off and disjoined across the stages of the path, closing with the precise definitions of the family-to-family and one-interval, and the canonical story of Gopīkā and the three monks. Terms established in earlier fascicles recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. The Tetralemma Concludes: Falling and Existing Across All Three Realms

Fascicle 53 completes the tetralemma opened in fascicle 52, working systematically through every combination of falling within and existing within the desire, form, and formless realms — an intermediate-state being belonging to one realm while arising within another, an entanglement's own fetters manifesting while one dwells, neither dying nor being reborn, within a realm not their own. The treatise then explains, almost as an aside, why it bothers to compose negative clauses at all: to display mastery over discourse itself, to sharpen a student's own insight into how the very same dharma can be truthfully described in more than one way, and because a negative clause sometimes carries a different case-count than its affirmative counterpart.

There are fetters that exist within the desire realm, yet do not fall within the desire realm — that is, an entanglement's own entangled being, having died within the desire realm, arises within the form realm's own intermediate state; dying within the desire realm and being born within the form realm occurs both for the ordinary being and for the noble one; that intermediate-state being's own dharma, arising within the desire realm yet belonging to the form realm, ought properly to be understood in just this way — the death-state's own point of cessation and the intermediate-state's own point of manifestation occurring together, exactly as a seed's own point of cessation and a sprout's own point of manifestation occur together. From the moment of the death-state up through the intermediate state: should it be an ordinary being, one among the form realm's own thirty-one latent tendencies manifests before the mind, thereby causing the continued succession of rebirth; should it be a noble one, one among the form realm's own three latent tendencies abandoned by cultivation manifests before the mind, thereby causing the continued succession of rebirth. And likewise, while abiding within the desire realm, the form and formless realms' own fetters manifest before the mind — that is, while abiding within the desire realm, neither dying nor being reborn, the form and formless realms' own fetters manifest before the mind, this occurring both for the ordinary being and for the noble one: should it be an ordinary being, one among the form and formless realms' own sixty-two latent tendencies manifests before the mind, namely, craving, view, doubt, or conceit belonging to the higher dhyānas; should it be a noble one, one among the form and formless realms' own six latent tendencies abandoned by cultivation manifests before the mind, namely, craving or conceit belonging to the higher dhyānas — such affliction manifesting either after concentration, or concentration manifesting after such affliction. ‘Not existing within the formless realm’ refers to the fact that it currently manifests within the desire or form realm, this possessing three of the four senses of existing, excepting own-body existing, since it does not currently manifest within its own realm; ‘it is not the case that they fail to fall within the formless realm’ refers to falling by realm, not by any other kind of falling, since this very fetter does indeed fall within the formless realm. One asks: why does this passage establish such a negative clause at all? The answer: it is simply because the treatise's own author intended it so — since the root treatise's own master composes his treatise according to his own intent, without contradicting the true characteristics of the dharmas, and this ought not to be pressed as an objection. Further, it is in order to display mastery over discourse itself, since one who possesses such mastery is capable of establishing a negative clause, while one lacking such mastery is incapable even of composing a discourse based on an affirmative clause, still less of establishing a negative one. Further, it is in order to give rise to insight within his own students, since, by composing a discourse based on a negative clause, his students are thereby enabled to give rise to the insight that the nature of the dharmas may indeed be stated this way, or equally well stated that way, without contradiction. Further, a negative clause may sometimes differ from its corresponding affirmative clause — exactly as, within the later section on persons, an affirmative clause carries four, three, or two cases, while its corresponding negative clause carries five, six, or four — the present case not being so, and it is for this reason that a negative clause is established here.

10. Two Kinds of Five Persons, and a Puzzle About Rebirth Upward

The Ekottarikāgama offers two distinct lists of five persons, each carefully distinguished by where they finish what they start: the first five "sow their seed" within the desire realm and "culminate" there too, exhausting every contaminant without ever leaving; the second five "sow their seed" in the desire realm but "culminate" only in the form or formless realm. This sets up a genuine puzzle — can a noble one who has been reborn within the desire realm subsequently be reborn upward into the higher realms at all, and, if either answer is taken, how is it to be reconciled with the Śakraparipṛcchā's own verse describing two practitioners who ascend together to the Brahmapurohita Heaven?

11. Gopikā and the Three Monks Who Loved Their Own Voices

The puzzle resolves through a memorable narrative: the nun Gopikā, taught by three monks with beautiful voices, grows disgusted with her own female body and is reborn as Śakra's own son, Gopaka. The three monks, however, loved by their own beautiful voices rather than the Dharma they carried, are reborn merely as gandharvas — heavenly music-spirits. When Gopaka recognizes them and asks how those who once practiced the supreme holy life could fall into so base a realm, two of them, overcome with shame, become free of desire and ascend by spiritual power to the Brahmapurohita Heaven — technically not death and rebirth at all, and so no contradiction follows; the third simply remains where he is.

The three monks' own fate is a precise moral diagnosis disguised as a folk story: their downfall wasn't unbelief or misconduct but a kind of category confusion, mistaking their own instrument — a beautiful chanting voice — for the substance it was meant to carry, and loving the vehicle more than the destination it pointed toward. Gopikā, hearing the very same Dharma through the very same voices, took the content and let the vehicle go; the three monks did the reverse, and their gandharva rebirth is the exact, literal consequence of that reversed priority — reborn, fittingly, as beings whose entire existence is nothing but beautiful sound.
