A New Chapter: The One-Mind Nyāsa Opens

Fascicle 155 completes the Equanimity-Mind Nyāsa's cross-realm death-and-rebirth census and opens the One-Mind Nyāsa, refuting a heretical three-moment theory of matter, defining what it means to cultivate the faculties, and tracing a massive census of when uncontaminated faculties cease and arise, closing with the arhat's three culminating knowledges. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

Fascicle 155 concludes the Equanimity-Mind Nyāsa (等心納息第四之五) and opens the One-Mind Nyāsa (一心納息第五之一). Terms established across fascicles 93–154 recur here and are not re-explained at each occurrence.

1. Nine Cross-Realm Death-and-Rebirth Combinations, Faculty by Faculty

Fascicle 155 opens by completing the Equanimity-Mind Nyāsa's exhaustive faculty census: for every possible combination of dying out of one realm and being reborn into another — desire, form, or formless in any pairing — the treatise counts precisely how many faculties cease, to which realm they are bound, and how many faculties become present in their place, distinguishing gradual death from sudden death and tracking sexual form throughout.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sheer combinatorial completeness here — nine distinct realm-pairings, each further split by gradual versus sudden death and by sexual form — is Abhidharma bookkeeping at its most thorough: rather than stating a general principle and trusting the reader to extrapolate, the treatise walks through every single cell of the matrix by name.

2. Why Equanimity Alone Accompanies Every Death and Rebirth

The census concludes with an arhat's own final entry into nirvāṇa, tallying which faculties cease last across each realm, before closing the entire Equanimity-Mind Nyāsa with a genuinely elegant observation: of the five feelings, only equanimity carries an aspect faint and dim enough to accompany the two most obscure moments of an entire lifetime — death and rebirth-linking themselves.

Chinese Source

阿羅漢般涅槃時。幾根最後滅。答或四。或九。或八。或三。欲界漸般涅槃者四。謂身命意捨。頓般涅槃者九。謂眼耳鼻舌身命意捨。及女男根隨一。色界八。謂眼耳鼻舌身命意捨。無色界三。謂命意捨。問何因緣故一切有情命終結生必住捨受。答於五受中無有行相昧劣如捨受者。於十時中無有昧劣。如死及生時者。故住捨受命終結生。

One might ask: for what reason must every sentient being, without exception, abide in equanimity at both death and rebirth-linking? Answer: among the five feelings, none carries an aspect as faint and dim as equanimity does; and among the ten periods of life, none carries so faint and dim an aspect as the moments of death and rebirth do — and it is for this reason that equanimity alone accompanies death and rebirth-linking.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The parallel drawn between equanimity's own faintness among the feelings and the sheer dimness of the death-and-rebirth moment itself is a lovely piece of phenomenological matching: the treatise explains a cosmological universal — every being, without exception, dies and is reborn in equanimity — not through appeal to karma or doctrine, but through a simple resonance of intensity between a feeling and the occasion it accompanies.

3. A New Chapter Opens: The One-Mind Nyāsa, and What 'Following Mind' Really Means

The fascicle immediately opens an entirely new nyāsa — the One-Mind Nyāsa — asking whether dharmas that arise, abide, and cease together with mind are thereby associated with mind and share its very object: the answer distinguishes genuine mental factors, which do both, from mind-following matter and dissociated formations, which merely keep time with mind without ever truly associating with it, before cataloguing the full ten-fold sense of "following mind" and its precise variations across wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral mental classes.

4. Refuting the Heretical 'Three-Moment' Theory of Matter

A rich polemical episode unfolds: certain rival monks hold that matter endures for three moments while mind perishes in a single instant, splitting into a "mixed-arising" theory and a "sequential" theory of how successive moments of eye-consciousness relate to a persisting eye-faculty — both refuted on the grounds that a given consciousness must arise and cease in dependence on one and the same basis throughout, and both accused of a further grave shared fault: implying that a being dying from the human realm into hell would briefly possess both a human and a hell body at once.

13. Why the Knowledge of Exhaustion Is Never Associated With Emptiness, and the Vajra-Like Concentration's Precise Chain of Succession

A genuinely sharp distinction closes out the three knowledges' own analysis: the knowledge of exhaustion, though uncontaminated, is never associated with the emptiness-aspect, since declaring "I know that my own birth is now exhausted" still engages the very notion of a self — engaging the mundane rather than the ultimate — whereas the unlearner's own right view, framed instead around the five grasping-aggregates rather than a first-person self-report, can indeed take on the emptiness-and-non-self aspect freely. The fascicle's final investigation then traces the exact immediate-succession chain binding the vajra-like concentration to the very first knowledge of exhaustion and, in turn, to the knowledge of non-arising, before carefully distinguishing when the vajra-like concentration and the knowledge of exhaustion share the very same objective sphere from when their objects genuinely diverge.
