The Dream of the Three Oceans

Fascicle 37 opens with whether a past mind undergoes decay, illustrated through the Buddha's own teaching on being sawn apart limb by limb without hatred, before distinguishing restlessness from worry and torpor from drowsiness through careful fourfold analysis. The fascicle's second half turns to a remarkable investigation into dreams — whether merit and demerit can genuinely grow while asleep, why the Buddha alone among all beings never dreams, and how the Bodhisattva's own eve-of-enlightenment dreams of stirring all three oceans trace back through a lineage of earlier buddhas who dreamt the very same portents. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Does Every Past Mind Undergo Decay?

Fascicle 37 opens a foundational metaphysical investigation, refuting rival views that the past and future possess no real substance, or that a conditioned dharma's own substance becomes concealed, transformed, or simply departs as it moves through time. The treatise's own answer distinguishes two kinds of decay — temporal, the mere fact of having become past, and logical, the standing opposition every defiled dharma bears to correct principle — before citing the Buddha's own teaching that one struck by an enemy's saw, limb by limb, ought never let the mind undergo decay through hatred.

Ru-Yi Translation

Does every mind that has become past thereby undergo decay? And so on, at length. One asks: why is this treatise composed? The answer: it is composed in order to refute other schools and to reveal the correct meaning — that is, there are those who hold that the past and the future possess no real substance at all, and that only the present is unconditioned — being confused and deluded with respect to the three time-periods, they give rise to such a position as this; this treatise, in order to refute that position, wishes to reveal that the past, the future, and the present are all genuinely existing conditioned dharmas. Further, there are certain outsiders who hold that, as a conditioned dharma operates across time, its own substance becomes concealed; there are certain outsiders who hold that, as a conditioned dharma operates across time, its own substance is transformed; and there are certain outsiders who hold that, as a conditioned dharma operates across time, its own substance simply departs — this treatise, in order to refute these positions, reveals that a conditioned dharma ceases before, and arises after, and it is for this reason that this treatise is composed. The decay of dharmas is, in brief, of two kinds: first, temporal decay; second, logical decay. Temporal decay refers to the fact that a dharma of the present, once it decays, is called "past" — this is temporal decay. Logical decay refers to a defiled dharma — every defiled dharma stands in opposition to correct principle, and it is for this reason that its decay is called "logical decay." A past defiled mind possesses both kinds of decay; an undefiled mind possesses only temporal decay; a future or present defiled mind possesses only logical decay; an undefiled mind is never spoken of as undergoing decay at all. This constitutes the brief exposition of this passage in the Vibhāṣā. Does every mind that has become past thereby undergo decay? The answer: every mind that has become past does indeed undergo decay — that is, a defiled mind, possessing both kinds of decay, is thereby called a mind that has undergone decay; an undefiled mind, possessing only temporal decay, is likewise called a mind that has undergone decay. Is there a mind that undergoes decay yet is not itself past? Yes — namely, a future or present mind associated with craving or hatred; such a mind, possessing only logical decay, is called a mind that has undergone decay. In order to establish this same meaning, the treatise further cites a sūtra passage: as the Blessed One has stated, "Monks, suppose you were seized by a hateful enemy or a bandit and sawn apart, limb by limb — you ought not, with respect to this, allow your mind to undergo decay, and you ought also to guard your speech, lest any evil word escape you; for, should your mind undergo decay, and should an evil word escape you, this would constitute a grave obstruction to what you yourselves seek."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The saw-and-limbs teaching does real philosophical work beyond its vivid horror: by choosing the single most extreme provocation imaginable, the Buddha forecloses any argument that ordinary anger might be excused as proportionate — if even this cannot justify a decayed mind, then no lesser provocation ever could, and the standard set at the furthest extreme becomes, by implication, the standard for every lesser case as well.

2. Why Craving and Hatred Alone Are Called “Decay”

The treatise works through why only present dharmas escape the label “temporally decayed” — since a present dharma still possesses actual function and remains due to decay again, unlike what has already, irrevocably, ceased — before turning to a genuine puzzle: every affliction whatsoever stands in logical opposition to correct principle, so why does the sūtra single out only craving and hatred as “undergoing decay,” leaving every other affliction unmentioned?

Ru-Yi Translation

This passage attests that a mind of hatred is called "undergoing decay." "Hateful enemy" refers to one who bears enmity; "bandit" refers to one who plunders. One asks: why does this passage speak only of being sawn apart, limb by limb? The answer: it is in order to reveal that this is capable of serving as the cause of manifold suffering — that is, when a blade or a spear wounds the body, there are cases where the pain comes upon entry but not upon withdrawal, and cases where the pain comes upon withdrawal but not upon entry; but when one is sawn, both the entry and the withdrawal of the blade bring pain. Even amid such extreme suffering as this, one ought not to give way to hatred — how much less, then, amid some lighter suffering, ought one give way to resentment! What one seeks for oneself is a wholesome destiny and nirvāṇa. Further, the Blessed One has stated: "Monks, toward the objects of the five wondrous desires, you ought not to give rise to a mind that undergoes decay" — this passage attests that a mind of craving, too, is called "undergoing decay." "The objects of the five wondrous desires" refers to the five wondrous sense-objects; "a mind that undergoes decay" here refers to a mind of lustful desire. One asks: if it is said that, when the present passes into the past, the present dharma is thereby called "temporally decayed," then, when the future passes into the present, why is the present dharma not likewise called "temporally decayed"? The answer: it is only once a dharma has already decayed, never to decay again, that it is called "temporally decayed"; a present dharma, once it decays, is due to decay yet again, and it is for this reason that it is not spoken of as "temporally decayed." Further, it is only when a dharma possesses both a temporal transition and the decay of its actual function that it is called "temporally decayed" —

現在雖有世變而無作用壞。以現在法有作用故不名世變壞。復次世間共許已謝滅法名世變壞不說現在。是故現在非世變壞。問一切煩惱無不違理皆應名理變壞。何故但說貪瞋二心名變壞耶。答是作論者意欲爾故乃至廣說有說此中亦應說餘煩惱相應心名為變壞。而不說者當知此義有餘。有說不應責問作論者意。以作論者依經造論。

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The distinction between a present dharma's mere “temporal transition” and its “decay of actual function” is a subtle piece of metaphysical bookkeeping: it lets the treatise affirm that time genuinely passes for the present without collapsing the crucial difference between something still doing its work and something whose work is already, permanently, finished.

3. Nine Reasons the Buddha Singled Out Craving and Hatred

The treatise supplies an extraordinarily rich catalog of reasons why the Buddha spoke only of craving and hatred as “decay,” not the other afflictions: their distinctive effects on the body's own softness or rigidity, their power to visibly alter one's complexion and even one's bodily form — illustrated by a heretical monk transformed into a venomous serpent through the sheer force of his own hatred — and their capacity, uniquely among the afflictions, to hasten a being's own death, as with the desire-realm gods who perish from excessive play or from mutual, escalating fury.

One asks: setting aside the author of the treatise, why does the Blessed One himself speak only of the mind associated with craving and hatred as "undergoing decay," and not of the others? The answer: the Buddha, observing those he was to transform, saw that, upon hearing the mind associated with craving and hatred called "undergoing decay," they would thereby attain awakening and accomplish what needed to be done, and it is for this reason that he spoke of these two, and not of the other afflictions. Further, the Buddha, observing the support and the object of these two kinds of decay, spoke of them in particular. As for the support of craving's decay: when craving manifests before one, the body thereby becomes soft, buoyant, and pleased. As for the object of craving's decay: when a beloved object manifests before one, the mind and mental factors, in their infatuation with it, become, with respect to their own support, as empty and insensate as an inanimate thing, and, with respect to a coarse and defiled object, come to perceive it as pure and wondrous. As for the support of hatred's decay: when hatred manifests before one, the body thereby becomes coarse, rigid, heavy, and haggard. As for the object of hatred's decay: when a detested object manifests before one, the mind and mental factors, in their aversion toward it, are unwilling even to face it, much less to look upon it, and, with respect to what is beautiful and wondrous, come to perceive it as vile and repugnant. Does every mind attached in defilement thereby undergo decay? And so on, at length. One asks: why is this treatise further composed? The answer: the foregoing has stated that one ought not to give rise to craving toward the objects of the five wondrous desires, and ought not to give rise to hatred toward being sawn apart limb by limb — lest one suppose that it is only the craving and hatred abandoned by cultivation, belonging to the desire realm alone, that are called "undergoing decay," this treatise now wishes to reveal that the craving belonging to all three realms, and the craving and hatred belonging to all five sections, are alike called "undergoing decay," and it is for this reason that this treatise is composed. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story of the heretic transformed into a serpent through hatred alone, offered as a real historical case rather than mere metaphor, reveals how literally this treatise sometimes takes its own claims about affliction's power over the physical body — hatred here isn't simply described as poisonous, it is treated as capable of producing an actual venomous creature, collapsing the line between psychological and physical transformation entirely.

4. A New Puzzle: Restlessness and Worry, Torpor and Drowsiness

The fascicle turns to a cluster of paired mental factors the Buddha groups together as single hindrances — restlessness with worry, torpor with drowsiness — addressing the natural doubt that arises when two things are named as one: does restlessness ever occur without worry, or worry without restlessness? The treatise's own tetralemma reveals a genuinely instructive case of worry without restlessness: the conscientious monk who, having forgotten to put away a bench or close a door, feels a calm, undefiled pang of retrospective regret utterly unlike the agitated remorse of one who has committed a genuine wrong.

9. What Is a Dream? Refuting the Dārṣṭāntikas' Denial

The fascicle turns to dreams' own inherent nature, opening by refuting the Dārṣṭāntikas' claim that dreams possess no real existence at all — pointing to the jarring gap between dreamed satisfaction and waking hunger, dreamed company and waking solitude — a position the treatise rejects as directly contradicting scripture, citing the Bodhisattva's own five great dreams, King Prasenajit's ten dreams, and the touching story of the widow Nandika's mother, visited in a dream by her deceased, precept-breaking husband, whom she resisted without so much as a single wavering thought. 11. Does the Buddha Sleep? And the Puzzle of the Bodhisattva's Five Great Dreams

Confronted directly by followers of the Nirgrantha sect with the provocative claim that “whoever sleeps is foolish and deluded,” the Buddha answers without evasion: yes, he does sleep, briefly, to relieve the discomfort of digestion, and sleep itself is of two kinds — the defiled kind, which he, pratyekabuddhas, and arhats have all permanently cut off, and the undefiled kind, which serves simply to regulate the body. 12. A Lineage of Dreams, and the Aggregates as Dream-Like

The treatise resolves the puzzle of the Bodhisattva's unprecedented dreams by distinguishing having previously seen something from having merely heard of it, tracing his own dream-images back to a teaching he received, in a past life, from an earlier buddha who had dreamt the very same portents before his own enlightenment. It closes by comparing a dream's own reach against the knowledge that recollects former lives — dreams, remarkably, reach further — before returning to the Buddha's own teaching that the five aggregates subject to grasping are themselves “like a dream,” momentary, undependable, and never fully satisfying.
