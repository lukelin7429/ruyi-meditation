Can You Skip a Dhyāna Entirely?

Fascicle 161 asks whether one must enter each dhyāna in sequence, whether rebirth in a higher realm requires ever having entered its concentration, and maps out an exhaustive census of exactly where one is reborn at death for every level of meditative attainment. This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation.

1. Flavor-Associated Dhyāna: Which Moment Is Entry, Which Is Exit?

Fascicle 161 opens a fresh topic within the Attainment Nyāsa: a dhyāna "associated with flavor" — that is, with craving — and asks which moment of a continuously flowing concentration counts as its "entry" and which as its "exit," concluding that the later instant, doing the flavoring, is entry, while the earlier instant, being flavored, is exit. A rich excursus then explains why craving alone, among all the defilements, is singled out as concentration's own special partner, listing five ways the two uncannily resemble each other.

Answer: in order to bring certainty to those in doubt. For the *Prakaraṇapāda* states: what is the first dhyāna? Answer: the wholesome matter, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness subsumed under the first dhyāna — and so on down to: what is the fourth dhyāna? Answer: the wholesome matter, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness subsumed under the fourth dhyāna. Lest this give rise to the doubt that dhyāna is exclusively wholesome, it is in order to make clear that dhyāna is not exclusively wholesome — since dhyāna spans the wholesome, the defiled, and the unobscured-neutral alike — that this treatise is composed on flavor-association. "Should the entry of a flavor-associated first dhyāna be called 'flavor,' and its exit be called 'flavor'?" Answer: with respect to what does the flavoring, it should be called "entry"; with respect to what is flavored, it should be called "exit." And so on down to: "Should the entry of a flavor-associated sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception be called 'flavor,' and its exit be called 'flavor'?" Answer: with respect to what does the flavoring, it should be called "entry"; with respect to what is flavored, it should be called "exit." Here, craving is what is meant by "flavor"; a concentration named "the first dhyāna" and so on that is associated precisely with craving — any such concentration is called a "flavor-associated dhyāna or formless attainment." Question: why does this passage speak only of association with craving, and not with any other defilement? Answer: this is simply what the author of this treatise intended — and so on in full. Some hold: the other defilements ought to have been mentioned as well — that is, one ought to speak of a first dhyāna associated with the view of self, asking whether its entry should be called "grasping at self and what belongs to self," and its exit likewise; with respect to what does the grasping, it should be called "entry," and with respect to what is grasped, it should be called "exit" — and so on in full, down to a first dhyāna associated with ignorance, asking whether its entry should be called "delusion," and its exit likewise; with respect to what does the deluding, it should be called "entry," and with respect to what is deluded, it should be called "exit" — the same holding clear through the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. That this treatise does not go on to state this should be understood as an incompleteness in the text. Others hold: this passage singles out craving merely as a representative gateway, so that the reader will understand the same holds for the other defilements as well. Others hold: this passage speaks specifically of what most closely resembles concentration itself — namely, craving alone, and not the other defilements. Why so? Because concentration flows on continuously with respect to its object, and craving does likewise; furthermore, concentration apprehends its object with careful, minute scrutiny, and craving does likewise; furthermore, concentration binds the mind to its object without ever letting go, and craving does likewise; furthermore, concentration takes up its object and turns wholly toward it, and craving does likewise; furthermore, concentration is capable of nourishing and increasing the faculties and the great elements, and craving does likewise — the other defilements possess none of these characteristics, and so are not said to be "associated" with concentration in this special sense. Question: "flavor" refers to craving, not to dhyāna and the rest, and so one ought not to speak of dhyāna's own "entry"; conversely, dhyāna and the rest are a concentration, not craving, and so one ought not to speak of a concentration's "flavor," since "flavor" is properly a word for craving. Why, then, do these two things come to borrow each other's names? Answer: because these two things are mutually associated with one another, borrowing each other's names in turn — what fault could there be in this? Since concentration is associated with craving, it too may be named "flavor"; and since craving is associated with concentration, it too may be spoken of as having "entry." As for "entry" and "exit" here, there are five kinds of entry and exit: first, by ground; second, by aspect; third, by object; fourth, by a mind of a different class; and fifth, by instant. Entry and exit by ground: this refers to the moment when, immediately following the first dhyāna, the second dhyāna becomes currently manifest — this is called "entering the second dhyāna" and "exiting the first dhyāna"; and so on clear through the sphere of nothing-at-all, when, immediately following it, the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception becomes currently manifest, this is called "entering the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception" and "exiting the sphere of nothing-at-all." Just as entry and exit proceed in forward order, so too do they proceed in reverse order, and in leaping order both forward and reverse.

Question: a craving-associated concentration is capable of taking any of the three time-periods as its object — why does this passage speak only of taking the past as its object? Some hold: this passage speaks only of the majority of cases, since it is simply the nature of sentient beings, for the most part, to give rise to a craving-flavor with respect to an agreeable object previously experienced. Others hold: this passage frames its question and answer specifically in terms of entering and exiting a concentration — a future concentration is not yet spoken of as either entered or exited; a present concentration is spoken of as entered but not yet exited; a past concentration is spoken of as both entered and already exited — and, since this passage wishes to make clear that the flavor-craving arises precisely with respect to the already-exited state, it speaks only of taking the past as its object. Other masters hold: this passage speaks of a flavor-associated concentration arising immediately following a pure concentration, such that the pure concentration serves as what is flavored, being the object, while the flavor-associated concentration itself serves as what does the flavoring, being the subject — the remainder as stated above. The verdict: the first position stated should be understood as the sound one, since this passage's own account of entry and exit is composed entirely with reference to the flavor-associated concentration itself. Answer: every flavor-associated sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception is indeed obscured-neutral; but there exist obscured-neutral spheres of neither-perception-nor-non-perception that are not flavor-associated — namely, those in which some defilement other than craving is currently manifest. Here, the reason every flavor-associated dhyāna or formless attainment is obscured-neutral is that it is capable of obstructing the noble path and its own preparatory practice, and is hence called "obscured"; and it does not invite a ripening-fruit, and is hence called "neutral." The other defilements referred to here are view, doubt, conceit, and ignorance — these defilements, arising in continuous succession together with an undefiled dhyāna or formless attainment, currently manifest, lead a yoga practitioner to be named one whose craving has "climbed atop" the dhyāna, one whose view has climbed atop the dhyāna, one whose doubt has climbed atop the dhyāna, or one whose conceit has climbed atop the dhyāna — and, although there is also a sense in which the remaining bonds, secondary defilements, and taints may be said to be associated in just the same way, it is only the defilements themselves that are named here, since they are the most prominent. And so on down to: "Is there ever a case of not entering the sphere of nothing-at-all, yet entering the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception?" Answer: yes. Question: why is this treatise composed? Answer: in order to bring certainty to those in doubt. The sūtras, too, speak of the nine sequential concentrations. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The five points of resemblance between concentration and craving — both flow continuously toward their object, both grip it with careful scrutiny, both bind the mind to it without release, both take it up and turn wholly toward it, both nourish the faculties and elements — amount to a striking admission: the very same structural intensity that makes concentration a path to liberation is what makes craving concentration's most natural counterfeit, the two sharing a common shape while pointing in opposite directions.

2. Leapfrogging Concentrations: Entering the Second Dhyāna Without the First

A new question opens: must one enter the first dhyāna before the second, given the sūtras' own talk of nine "sequential" concentrations? The treatise firmly says no, cataloguing exactly which grounds can lead directly into each higher dhyāna — and reporting, then rejecting, Venerable Saṃghavasu's proposal that even an ordinary desire-realm wholesome mind might leap straight into the second dhyāna, on the grounds that an unconcentrated mind simply cannot match a concentrated one's own power.

A scriptural puzzle closes this stretch of the fascicle: a sūtra names only the four dhyānas and the formless spheres as bases for exhausting the outflows — so how is it known that the meditative interval and the stage of proximate access can serve this same function?
