The Śākya Clan

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 7 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "The Śākya Clan" (諸釋部) — fourteen entries spanning the clan's own origin myth, King Śuddhodana's death, Mahāprajāpatī's ordination as the first Buddhist nun and her own later nirvāṇa, Rāhula's six years in the womb and his going forth, the ordination of Nanda, Devadatta, Aniruddha, and Bhaddiya, the tragic massacre of the Śākyas by King Virūḍhaka, and the past-life vow that bound Chandaka and the horse Kaṇṭhaka to the Buddha across lifetimes.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 7 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 7 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 7 turns from the Buddha himself to the clan and family around him — its founding legend, his father's death, his aunt's ordination, his son's birth and going forth, his cousins' conversions, and, at its emotional center, the massacre of the Śākyas by King Virūḍhaka, one of the most somber narratives in the canon. Every entry is translated in full and kept in the compendium's own order.

1. Origin of the Śākya Clan

An ancient king exiles four brilliant sons to protect a lesser-favored heir — and their success in founding a new kingdom on their own, entirely without their father's help, gives the clan its very name: Śākya, "able."

Chinese Source

過去有王名欝摩(又云懿摩長阿含經云鼓摩蓋方言之左右耳)王。有四庶子。一名照目。二名聰目。三名調伏象。四名尼樓(長阿含經名異一名面光二曰象食三曰路楯四曰莊嚴)並聰明神武大有威德。第一夫人有子名曰長生。頑薄醜陋眾人所賤。夫人白王。四子神俊我兒頑墜。若承嗣者必競陵奪。若王擯斥四子我心乃安。王曰。四子仁孝既無愆咎。云何擯黜。夫人又曰。我心劬勞實兼國家。四子英武民各懷歸。樹黨若立一旦競逐必相殄滅。大國之祚翻為他有。願王圖之。王曰。汝言是矣。即呼四子。勅之曰。汝有過於吾。吾不忍汝死。各速出國。剋己圖生。勿復[門@視][門@俞]自貽後悔。四子奉勅。即便裝嚴。母及同生姊妹。並求俱去。時諸力士一切人民。多樂隨從。王悉聽之。到雪山邊住直樹林。母為納妃自營頓住。數年之中歸德如市。遂大熾盛。欝為強國。數年之後父思見之。遣信報召。皆辭過不還。王便三歎。我子有能。能自存立。因此以命族為釋(長阿含經云直樹林又云釋林因林為姓又云王聞四子生子端正曰此真釋子瑞應經云為其能辭是同因命是)故有釋種焉。乃祖尼樓王。生烏頭羅。高祖烏頭羅為迦維羅衛國王。生瞿頭羅。曾祖瞿頭羅王。生尸休羅(大智論云師子頰王)祖尸休羅王。生四子長者淨飯(又名白淨王出彌沙塞律)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a king named Ikṣvāku, who had four sons by concubines — all intelligent, martial, and possessed of great majesty — while his chief consort's own son was dull, unattractive, and held in contempt by all. The consort told the king that the four sons' very brilliance would draw the people's loyalty and, once factions formed around them, lead to mutual destruction and the kingdom's ruin; only banishing them would ease her mind. The king relented, and, though acknowledging his four sons had committed no fault, told them: "I cannot bear to see you die in the strife to come — leave the kingdom at once, each to make your own way, and do not look back in regret." The four sons obeyed; their mother and full sisters, along with many strongmen and commoners eager to follow, went with them. They settled by the Himalayas in a forest of straight trees, and within a few years their virtue drew people to them as though to a marketplace, growing into a great and flourishing kingdom. When their father later sent messengers summoning them home, they all declined and did not return. The king sighed three times: "My sons have shown they can establish themselves" — and from this the clan took the name Śākya, "able" (the traditions differ on the precise reason for the name, agreeing only that it commemorates this very moment). From this line descended, in succession, Ulkāmukha, Gotamaka, Siṃhahanu ("Lion-Jaw"), and finally Siṃhahanu's own four sons, the eldest of whom was Śuddhodana.

2. King Śuddhodana's Death

On his deathbed, King Śuddhodana's one remaining wish — to see his son, grandson, and nephews once more — brings the Buddha flying across fifty yojanas in an instant, and his own funeral becomes an occasion for the Buddha to insist, against everyone's protest, on carrying his father's coffin himself.

Chinese Source

淨飯王遇疾。支節欲解。喘如駛流。眾治無益。二弟諸王群臣等曰。大王素不作惡。種德無厭。養使一切無不得安。何故愁惱。王曰。恨不見悉達。及難陀阿難陀羅睺等。除我貪望。淚下如雨。時佛在王舍城。相去五十由旬。王今轉羸。恐不相及。佛知父心。即勅難陀羅睺等。即以神力踊身虛空。忽現維羅衛。放大光明。國人遙見。舉聲泣曰。設大王崩。背此舍夷國必斷絕矣。城中人民。向佛悲哭宛轉自撲。佛言。無常離別古今有是。生死為苦惟道是真。佛光照耀內外通達。以照王身。王曰。此何光也。既觸我身患苦得息。非我悉達先見光明。從外還者白。佛阿難羅云等乘空而來。王意踊躍不覺起坐。曰惟願如來手觸我身。身如押油痛不可忍。得見世尊苦惱即除。佛告難陀。觀王本形端正巍巍名聲遠聞。今病重羸瘦待不可識。容力名聲一何所在。王一心合掌。唯願莫愁。德純無缺。手摩王額曰。王是清淨戒行之人。心垢已離。唯應歡喜不宜憂惱。當深思惟。念諸經法義。於不牢堅。得牢堅志。以種善根。王宜歡喜。大勝王願曰。佛與難陀阿難陀羅云。四子以壞魔網(先各歎四子之德文多不載)王乃歡喜。佛為說量摩波羅本生經。王得阿那含道。捉佛手捧置心上。佛又說法。得阿羅漢果。命盡氣絕。

Ru-Yi Translation

King Śuddhodana fell gravely ill, his joints seeming to come apart, his breath rasping like a rushing torrent, beyond any physician's help. Asked why he grieved, having never done evil and cultivated virtue without limit, the king answered that his only regret was not seeing Siddhārtha, Nanda, Ānanda, and Rāhula once more, and wept bitterly. The Buddha, then fifty yojanas away at Rājagṛha, knowing his father's failing state, flew through the air with Nanda and the others and appeared suddenly at Kapilavastu in a great light. The city's people, seeing this, wept that should the great king die, their whole nation would end, and rolled on the ground in grief before the Buddha, who told them that impermanence and separation have always been so, and that only the path itself is true amid the suffering of birth and death. His light shone into the king's own body, easing his suffering the moment it touched him — and, even before being told, the king sensed the Buddha and the others were arriving through the sky, and rose from his bed unconsciously in joy, begging the Tathāgata to touch him with his own hand, for his body ached as though being pressed like oil. The Buddha, comparing the king's once-renowned beauty and strength to his present wasted state, urged him toward reflection rather than sorrow, and reminded him of Māyā's own past vow that her four grandsons — the Buddha, Nanda, Ānanda, and Rāhula — would together destroy the net of Māra. The king, delighted, heard the Buddha expound the Jātaka of Amalapāla and attained the fruit of non-returning; pressing the Buddha's hand to his own chest, he heard further teaching and attained arhatship, his life then ending.

諸釋㘁咷。香汁浴身。纏以劫貝繒綿以棺斂。七寶莊嚴真珠羅網垂繞其傍。舉棺置屍師子座上散華燒香。佛共難陀在頭。阿難羅云在足。難陀等白佛。仰憶垂養。乞自擔棺。佛為未來不孝眾生不報育養。躬欲自擔。大千振動。一切眾山如波上舟。欲界六天無數眷屬俱來赴喪。四天王將鬼神億百千眾皆共舉哀。諸王白佛。我佛弟子。從佛聞法。成須陀洹。我曹宜擔。佛聽四天王擔。即皆變身如人形像。以手擎棺。著於肩上。佛之威光猶如萬日。手執香鑪。最在前行。到於墓所。靈鷲山千阿羅漢振虛而至。稽首佛足願勅所作。佛言。往大海渚取牛頭栴檀種種香木。如彈指頃得諸香薪。大眾共[卄/積]以之燒棺。眾見火起。益更悲號。佛言。苦空無常。猶如幻化水月鏡像。燒身既竟。以乳滅之。收骨置金剛函中。即共起塔。大眾諮問神生何處。佛言。生淨居天(出淨飯王泥洹經)。

The Śākyas wailed in grief as the king's body was bathed, wrapped, and placed in a coffin adorned with the seven treasures, honored with flowers and incense, the Buddha and Nanda at its head, Ānanda and Rāhula at its feet. When Nanda and the others asked to carry the coffin themselves in memory of their father's own care, the Buddha, for the sake of unfilial beings yet to come, insisted on carrying it himself — and at this the great-thousandfold world shook, every mountain rocking like a boat on waves, as countless devas came to attend, the Four Heavenly Kings transforming into human form to bear it upon their own shoulders while the Buddha, his light like ten thousand suns, walked at the very front with an incense censer. At the burial ground, a thousand arhats from Gṛdhrakūṭa gathered wood in an instant, and, as the assembly cremated the coffin, the Buddha reminded the weeping mourners that all things are suffering, empty, and impermanent, like illusion, like the moon's reflection in water. The fire extinguished with milk, the bones were gathered into a vajra casket and a stūpa raised over them; asked where the king's spirit had been reborn, the Buddha answered: in the Pure Abodes (Source: Sūtra on King Śuddhodana's Nirvāṇa).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's insistence on carrying his own father's coffin — against the objections of his own half-brothers, who wished to spare him the labor — is presented explicitly as a teaching aimed past the immediate scene, "for the sake of unfilial beings yet to come." Having renounced his family to become a wandering teacher, he here performs the single most conventional filial duty available to a son, at the cost of his own dignity as a buddha before a mourning nation, precisely to demonstrate that awakening does not exempt anyone from the debts owed to family.

3. Māyā's Teaching in Trāyastriṃśa Heaven

The Buddha ascends to teach his own mother the Dharma she never lived to hear, and her milk flows across the distance between them to confirm his identity before he even speaks.

Chinese Source

佛昇忉利天。入歡喜園。在波利質多羅樹下。三月安居。放毛孔光照大千界。諸天子等不知何緣。佛告文殊。汝詣我母道我在此。願母暫屈。往白摩耶。摩耶乳汁自流。若是悉達。當令汁入其口。兩乳汁出。遠入佛口。摩耶歡悅。普地震動。諸妙華果非時敷熟。即語文殊為母子來歡喜安樂未曾如今。俱往佛所。佛遙見母曰。身所經履與苦樂俱。當修涅槃以求永離。摩耶一心五體投地專精正念結使消伏。佛為說法即識宿命。得須陀洹果。即白佛言。生死牢獄今已解脫。大眾咸曰。願一切眾生皆得解脫。廣化天人大有利益。三月將盡。告鳩摩羅。汝今可下至閻浮提。如來不久當入涅槃。時眾愁惱。我不早知不久涅槃。世眼將滅。何其苦哉。帝釋使諸鬼神作三道寶階。摩耶垂淚。於是而別。足躡寶階。梵王執蓋。四天侍立左右。四部大眾歌唄讚歎。天作伎樂充塞虛空。散華燒香至閻浮提(出佛昇忉利天為母說法經又出善見毘婆沙)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha ascended to Trāyastriṃśa Heaven and dwelt beneath the Pāricchattaka tree for the three-month rains retreat, emitting light from every pore. Sending Mañjuśrī to tell his mother he was there, he was confirmed by an unmistakable sign: Māyā's own milk began to flow of itself and, across the whole distance between them, reached directly into the Buddha's mouth. Overjoyed, feeling the earth shake and wondrous fruit ripen out of season, she went to him at once. Seeing her, the Buddha told her that any body one experiences is bound up with both suffering and joy, and that one should cultivate nirvāṇa to be free of it forever; prostrating herself with utmost sincerity, she subdued her own afflictions through his teaching, recalled her own past lives, and attained the fruit of stream-entry, declaring herself freed from the prison of birth and death. As the three months drew to a close, the Buddha told the young deva Kumāra he must descend to Jambudvīpa, for his own nirvāṇa was not far off — and the assembly grieved that the world's own eye would soon be extinguished. Śakra had spirits build a triple staircase of jewels, and the Buddha descended it step by step, Brahmā holding a canopy above him, the Four Heavenly Kings at his sides, and the fourfold assembly singing hymns of praise, as Māyā, weeping, took her leave (Source: Sūtra on the Buddha's Ascent to Trāyastriṃśa Heaven to Teach His Mother; also the Samantapāsādikā).

4. Mahāprajāpatī's Going Forth

Refused three times, the Buddha's own foster-mother wins admission to the monastic order only through Ānanda's advocacy and her own acceptance of the Eight Special Rules — becoming the first Buddhist nun — and, decades later, her own death and funeral mirror the Buddha's, down to his personal insistence on gathering her remains himself.

Chinese Source

佛還迦維羅衛國。摩訶波闍波提(梁言大愛)姓瞿曇彌求佛出家哀請至三。佛所不聽。退住門外。著垢弊衣。徒跣而立。歔欷悲泣。阿難問曰。何故泣。答曰。女人不得出家自悲傷耳。阿難止而白佛。我從佛聞。女人精進可得四果。今大愛道。至心願樂佛言。無使。女人入我法律為沙門也。譬如人家多女少男家必衰弱。女人出家清淨梵行不得久住。又如莠雜禾稼善穀傷敗。女人入法亦復如是。阿難曰。大愛道多有善意。佛初生時乃自育養至于成人。佛言。多信善意於我有恩。我於愛道亦多有恩。由我得歸依三寶不疑四諦立五根信受持五戒。正使有人設身供養不及此也。假使女人欲作沙門者。八敬之法不得踰越。盡壽學之。譬如防水善治堤塘。勿令漏失。其能如是。可入法律。阿難具報愛道。愛道歡喜便得出家。為大比丘尼。奉行法律。遂得應真。後與諸長老尼。詣阿難所。久修梵行。且已見諦。云何使禮幼小比丘。阿難白佛。佛言。止止勿說此也。若使女人不出家。外道異學一切賢者。皆四事供養。解髮布地屈請令蹈。我之正法當住千年。以度女人今止五百。愛道聞之歡喜奉行(出佛昇忉利天為母說法經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

When the Buddha returned to Kapilavastu, Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī begged, three times over, to be allowed to go forth, but was refused each time; withdrawing outside the gate in soiled clothing, she wept. Ānanda, hearing she had heard women could attain the four fruits through diligent practice and that her own aspiration was sincere, interceded on her behalf — but the Buddha warned that admitting women would be like a household with many daughters and few sons, doomed to decline, or weeds mixed among good grain, spoiling the crop. Ānanda pointed out that Mahāprajāpatī had raised the Buddha himself from infancy; the Buddha acknowledged this debt, but noted he had likewise given her an even greater one — refuge in the Three Jewels, resolution of doubt, and the five precepts, a gift no material offering could match. He said that if women wished ordination, they must accept the Eight Special Rules without exception, for life, like a well-maintained dike holding back water — and under this condition alone, Mahāprajāpatī was ordained, becoming the first great bhikṣuṇī and, in time, an arhat. When she and other senior nuns later questioned why they must still bow to junior monks despite their own long practice and insight, the Buddha refused to revisit the matter, explaining that had women not been admitted at all, the heterodox teachers would have received the offerings and reverence now given his own sangha instead — "my true Dharma, which should have endured a thousand years, will now last only five hundred because women were admitted to it." Mahāprajāpatī accepted this without complaint (Source: Sūtra on the Buddha's Ascent to Trāyastriṃśa Heaven to Teach His Mother).

住王園精舍將欲捨命。曰。吾不忍見世無如來及應真滅度。先息靈于本無矣。佛照此心。以告阿難。阿難身體萎垂心塞無識。佛言。汝謂將五分法四意止八品道行去耶。對曰。不也。但惟佛生七日太后薨。為慈母有恩在世尊。佛曰。汝言哺乳恩重此惠難報。吾已開示。歸命三尊。聞苦集滅諦。慧眼得明。盡諸有結。獲無所著。此亦恩過須彌。愛道與五百除饉女俱到佛所。具言上意。手摩佛足曰。自今不復見最正覺矣。繞佛三匝還于精舍。與五百除饉女作十八變。上耀諸天同時泥洹。佛告阿難。汝入城到耶游理家所。告佛母及五百耆年滅度。阿難平旦入城至理家門。具陳其事。聞者抗哀躄地銜淚。而曰自今不復仰覩神通何痛甚乎。阿難曰。佛言乾坤雖久始必有終。三界無常。猶如幻夢。生求不死。會冀不離。終不可得。理家心解復至諸梵志理家。告之曰。佛勸諸賢者作五百葬具。施五百除饉女也。梵志理家辦送精舍中。精舍中門閉緣入開之。欲詣講堂。有女沙彌。告曰。吾師入定。慎勿擾動。答曰。死而非定。沙彌躄身消息。良久乃蘇。哀慟號叫曰。誰當教化吾等。聖訓絕矣。理家語沙彌曰。恩愛雖會。終必有離。但當建志力取應真。闍維畢捧舍利來佛所。

Dwelling at the Royal Park Monastery and about to give up her own life, Mahāprajāpatī said she could not bear to see a world without the Tathāgata and the noble ones, and wished to extinguish her own spirit first. The Buddha, perceiving this, told Ānanda, whose own body grew faint with grief. When the Buddha asked whether he supposed the Dharma itself was about to depart with her, Ānanda admitted he grieved only for her own kindness as a foster-mother; the Buddha acknowledged this debt, but noted the far greater one he had already repaid her — freeing her from every fetter through the Dharma itself. Mahāprajāpatī, with five hundred nuns, came to bid the Buddha farewell, then returned to her own monastery, where she and her five hundred companions performed the eighteen transformations and entered nirvāṇa together, their light shining even among the devas. Told of this, the lay community wept in grief, and Ānanda reminded them that even heaven and earth must have an end, and the three realms are impermanent as a dream. As they prepared the funeral offerings, a young novice, unaware her own teacher had died rather than merely entered meditation, collapsed in grief on learning the truth, lamenting that the sacred instruction was now silenced — but was reminded that even bonds of love must eventually end, and that she should resolve instead to attain the path herself. Once the cremation was complete, the relics were brought before the Buddha.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium narrates Mahāprajāpatī's death as a deliberate echo of the Buddha's own — the same eighteen transformations, the same personal involvement of the Buddha in gathering the remains, the same summoning of arhats from the four directions — a structural way of asserting parity between the founder of the monastic order and the founder of its female half, even while the surrounding text (the Eight Special Rules, the refusal to revisit their permanence) makes clear the tradition never fully resolved the tension between honoring her and formally subordinating the community she founded.

5–7. Rāhula: Birth, Reluctance, and the Path

A six-year pregnancy explained by an ancient act of thoughtlessness; a mother's bitter refusal to release her son, overcome only by a reminder of her own past vow; and the son's own slow path from an unreliable child to an arhat, taught through nothing more than a basin of dirty water.

Rāhula's Six Years in the Womb

Prince Siddhārtha's consort Yaśodharā conceived on the very night he went forth, yet did not give birth for six years — matching the length of his own austerities. Accused by the Śākyas of impropriety, since the prince had already left, she insisted the child was truly his, though she herself could not explain the delay; Gopī vouched for her innocence, suggesting they wait until the birth to judge by the child's resemblance to its father. Rāhula was born the very night the Buddha attained enlightenment, and, seeing his clear resemblance, the king's own grief turned to delight. To finally clear Yaśodharā's reputation, on an occasion when the Buddha was invited to the palace, she had the seven-year-old Rāhula present him with a sweet cake — but the Buddha had transformed five hundred arhats to look exactly like himself, so that when Rāhula, not knowing which was the true Buddha, still went directly and unerringly to the real one, this itself proved his parentage, for as the disguise dissolved, only the true Buddha's bowl was found holding the cake. Asked why she had carried the child six years, the Buddha explained that Rāhula, in a past life as a king, had once delayed a supplicant seer's own confession — over a trivial debt of unauthorized water and a willow-twig — for six full days out of simple forgetfulness, and this delay resulted in six years spent in the womb in each of five hundred later lifetimes (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, chapter 17).

Rāhula's Going Forth

The Buddha sent Maudgalyāyana to Kapilavastu to persuade Rāhula's mother to release him for ordination — for the joy of a mother and son's bond is brief, and only Rāhula's own attainment of the path can truly deliver her from birth and death. Yaśodharā, hearing an envoy had come, barricaded herself and Rāhula in a high tower, but Maudgalyāyana simply flew up to her. Forced to receive him, she answered his request with bitter anger: the prince, having abandoned her within three years of marriage, showed no regard for his own family upon his triumphant return, treating her worse than a stranger — and now would take her son as well, in the name of a compassion she could not see. Even King Śuddhodana and Mahāprajāpatī's own repeated pleading could not move her, until the Buddha sent a conjured voice from the sky reminding her of an ancient vow: that when he, as a bodhisattva, had once bought flowers from her to offer Dīpaṃkara Buddha on the condition she be his wife in every future life, she herself had vowed that whatever kingdom, wife, or child should be born to her, she would let him give it away as he saw fit. Suddenly recalling this as vividly as yesterday, Yaśodharā's attachment eased at once; she summoned Maudgalyāyana back, confessed her fault, and entrusted Rāhula to him in tears. Fifty young Śākya nobles, sent by their own families to accompany the king's grandson, joined Rāhula in going to the Buddha, where Ānanda shaved their heads, Śāriputra served as preceptor, and Maudgalyāyana as instructor, giving them the ten precepts.

The Buddha told the fifty new novices the past-life karmic retribution of a figure named Chandāla, leaving them anxious that they, too, as unworthy children accepting others' offerings, might one day suffer likewise, and asked to return to lay life instead. The Buddha answered with a parable of two starving men who, given a fine meal, both overate — one, wise, induced vomiting and recovered, while the other, foolish, resorted to killing for a desperate cure and died in agony regardless. "You," the Buddha told them, "have the good fortune to have encountered me — take the medicine I offer, and you will not meet such a fate." Reassured, the novices settled into their new life; and when people later wondered how Gopī could have borne a son during the prince's own twelve-year absence, the Buddha assured his father of her flawless chastity throughout, and the seven-year-old Rāhula, asked who his father was, went straight to the Buddha and offered his mother's own signet ring as proof.

Rāhula Receives the Precepts

Before Rāhula attained the path, his character was rough and his speech unreliable. Sent by the Buddha to a monastery to guard his own speech and study diligently, he spent ninety days in shame and self-reproach before the Buddha visited him. The Buddha had him fetch water in a washbasin to wash his feet, then asked whether this water could now be drunk — Rāhula said no, since it had been soiled by the washing. "You are just the same," the Buddha said. "Though my own son and a king's grandson, if you do not guard your own mind and speech, the three poisons fill your heart just as this water is now fouled and unusable."

Asked next whether an empty basin — bearing that very name only because it once held impurity — could hold food, Rāhula again said no. "You are the same," said the Buddha: a monk of insincere speech and stubborn character, once he has earned a bad name, is unfit to hold anything worthwhile either. The Buddha then kicked the basin aside with his toe, and, asked whether Rāhula minded if it broke, Rāhula admitted it was a cheap thing hardly worth worrying over. "You are the same," the Buddha said again: a monk who does not guard his own body and speech will likewise be thought no more of by others than this basin — his own suffering, wandering through the evil destinies, unheeded even by buddhas and sages. Struck with fear and shame, Rāhula heard a final parable, of a war-elephant that protects its own soft, vulnerable trunk above all else in battle rather than fighting with it, just as a person must above all guard the mouth — and, deeply moved, exerted himself diligently and attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of Similes, fascicle 10).

Nanda's Going Forth

The Buddha's own half-brother Nanda, seeing him beg for food and thinking it beneath a would-be sage-king, insisted on delivering him a bowl of fine food himself — whereupon the Buddha had him confined for ordination by supernormal power. When his own turn came for a monastic chore, Nanda, delighted at the chance to slip home instead, found every task thwarted by unseen devas, and, giving up, tried to flee — only to run straight into the Buddha, who lifted an entire tree into the air to expose him hiding behind it. Confronted, Nanda admitted he meant to see his wife; the Buddha instead took him to see a heavenly palace, splendid but lacking any husband, which the heavenly maidens themselves explained awaited Nanda should he keep the precepts — and then to the edge of hell, where, having first made him compare his beautiful wife unfavorably to a blind monkey, then to the heavenly maidens by a billionfold, the Buddha showed him one great, ominously empty cauldron of boiling oil, which the hell-wardens explained awaited Prince Nanda himself, for his own excessive lust and pride. Terrified, Nanda begged to be freed from such a fate, and the Buddha's subsequent teaching brought him at last to genuine awakening (Source: Sūtra of a Child Questioning the Buddha About Almsgiving; also the Udānavarga, fascicle 16).

Devadatta's Going Forth

King Śuklodana (Śuddhodana's second younger brother) had two sons, Ānanda and Devadatta. Seeing that Kāśyapa's own followers were unattractive, King Śuddhodana urged the royal clan to join the sangha themselves, to add to its dignity — recalling Asita's own prophecy that the Buddha, had he not gone forth, would have ruled surrounded only by the finest attendants. Devadatta insisted the princes should first go forth in the very finest attire imaginable, and, on the appointed day of their splendid procession, a physiognomist predicted every prince but two would attain the path — yet all went together to the Buddha and were ordained regardless, their pride subdued without exception (Source: Zhong Ben Qi Sūtra, upper fascicle; also the Sūtra of the Buddha's Twelve Journeys).

Aniruddha's Going Forth

King Droṇodana's son Aniruddha, weary of household affairs, negotiated his mother down from an impossible condition — that his own brother Bhaddiya go forth too — to eventually securing both their consents. When the group of Śākya princes finally departed, their attendant Upāli, rather than keeping the fine clothes and elephants they left him as payment, simply hung them on a tree for anyone to take, and asked to be ordained ahead of the princes himself, precisely to remove any future basis for pride of rank among them (Source: Dharmaguptaka Vinaya; the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra and the Sūtra of the Buddha's Twelve Journeys largely agree).

Bhaddiya's Going Forth

King Amṛtodana's son Bhaddiya, once ordained, was overheard meditating alone at night in a graveyard, crying out repeatedly, "How happy, how happy!" Asked by the Buddha the reason, Bhaddiya explained that as a prince he had lived constantly guarded by blades and staves yet still in fear, while now, alone among the dead with not a hair on his body standing on end in fright, he had found genuine freedom at last. The Buddha said, "Well done" (Source: Dharmaguptaka Vinaya; the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra and the Sūtra of the Buddha's Twelve Journeys largely agree).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Bhaddiya's graveyard delight and Upāli's refusal to keep the princes' discarded wealth make the same point from opposite directions: a poverty freely chosen turns out to be safer and happier than the constant vigilance wealth requires. Placed immediately before the massacre narrative that follows — in which the Śākyas' own wealth and status are precisely what make them targets — this juxtaposition reads less like coincidence than like the compendium quietly setting up its next, much darker entry.

12. King Virūḍhaka Destroys the Śākya Clan

The compendium's most somber narrative: a childhood insult over a slave-woman's deception festers for decades into genocide, the Buddha's own supernormal disciples proving powerless to prevent what the Śākyas' collective past karma has already set in motion — until the Buddha explains that even his own clan's fate traces back to a single moment of cruelty toward fish, in a village none of them remember.

Maudgalyāyana offered to move the entire Śākya clan bodily to safety — into the sky, the sea, another world entirely — but the Buddha told him that seven things no being can escape (birth, old age, sickness, death, offense, merit, and causal conditions) meant that no power could shield anyone from their own past karma. When a lone fifteen-year-old boy climbed the wall to defend the city and wounded many attackers, the Śākyas themselves scolded him for shaming their nonviolent principles — any one of them, they said, could easily defeat ten thousand enemies, but to kill even in self-defense would still mean rebirth in hell — and sent him away rather than let him fight further. Seeing the city now consumed in smoke and fire, the Buddha told his monks he would never return to a place now standing empty where he had once taught. Back at Jetavana, he predicted that King Virūḍhaka and his army would be destroyed within seven days — and, true to this, a sudden storm swept the celebrating king and his forces into a river and drowned them all on the seventh day, the king himself falling straight into the Avīci hell as heavenly fire consumed his own palace. Asked why the Śākyas had suffered such a fate, the Buddha explained: long ago, in a fishing village during a famine, villagers ate fish from a great pond, including two who protested being eaten though they were creatures of the water, while a village boy of eight, though he never fished himself, delighted in watching the catch. That village was the Śākya clan; the two fish were Virūḍhaka and his tutor; and the boy who laughed at the sight was the Buddha himself — for that offense he too suffered in hell for countless kalpas, and even now, as its last remaining trace, his own head aches at times as though crushed beneath the weight of Sumeru itself. Many in the assembly, hearing this teaching on impermanence, attained the fruit of stream-entry (Source: Sūtra on Śākyamuni's Own Exhausted Karmic Retribution; also the Dīrgha Āgama and Dharmapada with Similes).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium refuses every available escape hatch a reader might reach for. It does not let the Buddha's own supernormal powers save his relatives — Maudgalyāyana's hidden Śākyas die anyway. It does not let the Śākyas' own righteousness save them — their principled refusal to fight is precisely what allows the massacre to proceed unopposed. And most pointedly, it does not exempt the Buddha himself from the causal chain — his own headaches are presented as the literal, ongoing residue of a childhood act of cruelty in a story he no longer consciously remembers. The lesson is not that violence is sometimes necessary, since the Śākyas' nonviolence is never criticized; it is that karma operates beneath the reach of even a buddha's compassion, power, or memory, and unwinds regardless on its own schedule.

13. Five Hundred Śākya Women Seek Ordination

The mutilated survivors of the massacre, healed by a single desperate invocation of the Buddha's name, are turned away at the gate of the monastic order by corrupt nuns — until Utpalavarṇā answers their grief with a past-life account of suffering so total it makes even genocide seem almost bearable, and Mahāprajāpatī secures, for all women to come, the right the Śākya women now claim.

The grieving women, telling Utpalavarṇā their own story of clan-destruction and mutilation, were answered that their suffering was hardly worth mentioning beside her own past. Once a woman of Śrāvastī married into a northern land, she had been returning to her parents' home to give birth when a flooded river stranded her and her husband overnight — and a venomous serpent, drawn by the scent of fresh blood after she gave birth, killed their servant and then her husband as he slept. Alone for days beside her husband's rotting body, she attempted to cross the receding river carrying both children, only to lose her newborn to the current and her older child to a tiger while trying to save the other, arriving at the far shore to learn, from passing travelers, that her own parents' home had also burned down that same night, killing them both. Captured by bandits and forced to become one outlaw's wife, she was later accused by him of endangering him through a secretly born child, whereupon he mutilated her and forced her to eat the child's own flesh; buried alive alongside him after his own execution, she was twice dug up — once by a grave-robber after her jewelry, once inadvertently by a scavenging tiger — before wandering, half-mad, until she found her way to Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī and, practicing under her in due order, finally attained the fruit of the path.

釋女聞之。心大歡喜。得法眼淨。時憍曇彌言。如來法海。一切眾生。皆悉有分。而我等女人。如來不聽。以多諸疑惑執著難捨。癡愛覆心愛水所沒。不能自出。懈怠嬾惰。現身不能莊嚴菩提獲得三乘。阿難為請云。憍曇彌乳哺養育如來色身得至成佛。佛言若聽女人法當漸滅。阿難又請。過去諸佛具四部眾。而今獨不具也。佛告阿難。憍曇彌。愛樂佛法發大精進。清淨修習八敬之法者。聽入佛法。我得出家。大悲熏修。普為未來一切女人。重白佛言。未來善女。信樂愛敬如來法者。唯願聽許。佛言。若有女人。護持佛法漸次修學。戒施多聞三歸五戒。乃至具戒。諸助道法。亦悉聽。常當至心念阿難恩。供養恭敬。晝夜六時。令心不忘(出報恩經第五卷)。

Hearing this, the Śākya women rejoiced and attained the pure Dharma-eye. Mahāprajāpatī then reflected on her own history securing this very path for women — how the Buddha had at first refused her, on the grounds that women's minds, clouded by attachment, could not adorn themselves with awakening; how Ānanda's advocacy on her behalf, citing her own care for the infant Buddha, had won only the concession that admitting women would hasten the Dharma's own decline; and how she had finally been admitted solely on condition of the Eight Special Rules, her own great compassion thereby extending, through this precedent, to every woman of future generations. She now asked the Buddha, on behalf of all such future women, to confirm this path remained open, and the Buddha answered that any woman who protects the Dharma and studies it in proper order — precepts, giving, learning, and full ordination together with every aid to the path — should likewise be admitted, and that all such women should forever remember Ānanda's own debt of kindness in this, honoring him without fail (Source: Sūtra of Repaying Kindness, fascicle 5).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Utpalavarṇā's account is deliberately calibrated to overshadow, rather than dismiss, the Śākya women's own trauma — she is not telling them their suffering does not matter, but demonstrating through her own far longer chain of loss that a genuine path out of even the very worst suffering imaginable exists and has already been walked by someone in the room. The entry then pivots directly into Mahāprajāpatī's own founding struggle, positioning the corrupt nuns who first turned these women away as a betrayal of exactly the hard-won admission Mahāprajāpatī herself once fought for — the compendium making clear that the order's own gatekeepers, not the Buddha's original condition, are where the promise to future women is actually at risk of failing.

14. Chandaka and Kaṇṭhaka's Past-Life Vow

An elaborate Jātaka tale answers Śāriputra's question — why did Chandaka, of all people, accompany the bodhisattva on the night of the great departure? — with a story of a captured forest-spirit maiden, a king's murderous ambition to reach heaven, and a chain of vows, sworn before five hundred pratyekabuddhas, that bound a servant, a horse, and two disciples to the future Buddha across countless lifetimes.

Chinese Source

舍利弗白佛言。車匿前世有何功德乃將菩薩入山得佛。佛言。車匿不但今世有是功德。我昔為菩薩時。在尸訶遍羸國。須檀摩提王聞世間人或有作道上天或作祠祀上天。王常欲上天不知方便。國有婆羅門道。凡四萬餘人。中有大尊者婆羅門。王請問之。對曰。欲生身上天耶死上天耶。王言。生身飛上天。復言。大王當作祠祀。王喜出藏珍寶與婆羅門。求國中端正男女各一百人。象馬畜生各百頭。先飯四婆羅門。後殺人畜。用祠祀天。取血作階。上婆羅門。復求香山中有神女名真陀羅。欲得其血合持作階。王聞喜言何不早告。王即勅國中遍問誰能得真陀羅者。一人白王。第七山中有兩人。一名闍梨一人名憂梨。知真陀羅處。王言。疾喚其人。得來。王大歡喜即設酒食恣意娛樂七日七夜重賞極意。王謂闍梨等二人。汝能得真陀羅女來我得上天者。當持此國付汝二人。其人受教輒自盡力求真陀羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Śāriputra asked the Buddha what merit Chandaka had earned in a past life, that he alone should accompany the bodhisattva into the mountains on the night of his great departure. The Buddha explained: long ago, when I was myself a bodhisattva, King Sudarśana of Siṃhapura, wishing to ascend to heaven bodily, was told by a brahmin advisor that he must perform a great sacrifice — a hundred handsome young men and women, and a hundred each of elephants and horses, their blood used to build a ceremonial staircase. The brahmin further demanded the blood of a forest-spirit maiden named Candrā, dwelling in the Fragrant Mountains. Two men, Jāli and his companion, who knew her whereabouts, were summoned, richly rewarded, and sent to capture her.

二月餘日經歷八重山。至香山中得大池水。見有釋提桓因。與諸天伎女無央數眾。出城遊戲池中沐浴竟皆飛上天。時水池邊有一婆羅門。已得四禪般遮旬道。時闍梨等前為作禮問言。是間為何等聲快樂乃爾。答言。頭摩王千餘人在此城中。遊戲沐浴當至我所作禮。卿曹宜去。闍梨等便去。屏處議言。是大神聖求是神女那從得之。其人自念取草作結投水厭之。令婆羅門不得動搖。復厭天女令不得飛。結神呪竟。釋提桓因及諸天女俱飛上天。獨王及女及王摩那訶羅止在水中不能得飛。便前縛女將到故國。王請入宮飯食賞勞。

After crossing eight mountain ranges over two months, they found Candrā bathing with Śakra and countless heavenly maidens at a pool in the Fragrant Mountains, guarded nearby by a brahmin ascetic of the fourth dhyāna. Unable to approach a being so clearly under divine protection, one of the men devised a binding spell using a knotted blade of grass cast into the water — and when Śakra and the other maidens flew back to heaven as usual, Candrā alone, along with the guardian brahmin and a royal attendant, remained trapped, unable to fly. The two men seized her and brought her back to their own kingdom, where the king received them with a feast.

太子難羅尸作異國王。王有太子名須羅。先即是王太孫也。有大慈悲行菩薩道。遣人往呼來見此女欲取為妻。王言。更為汝取妻。此女天取血作祠持用上天。菩薩答言。不與我者我便惑死。王畏其死持女與之。四月餘日婆羅門來白王言。前得真陀羅女今當殺之。與人及畜掘地作坑方圓八肘。應取其血擇日作祠。王言。大善菩薩聞之問作何等。婆羅門對言。作祠當上天。菩薩言。殺人及畜當入地獄。豈得上天乎。婆羅門言。汝年尚小非汝事也。菩薩言。我悉知汝因緣。婆羅門言卿知者試說。菩薩說其行法。婆羅門慚愧作禮而去。菩薩即為王說經言。欲上天者當持五戒修行十善。分檀布施孝養父母承事沙門。慈心萬民蠕動之類隨其所食給足與之可得上天。王聞即用菩薩之言。便舉藏中金銀珍寶。與太子布施恣意七日訖。便與妻作禮而去還歸故國。

The king's own compassionate grandson-prince, wishing to marry Candrā instead of seeing her sacrificed, threatened to die of longing if refused, and the fearful king relented. Months later, when the brahmin returned to demand her sacrifice after all, the prince exposed his advisor's self-serving scheme so precisely that the shamed brahmin departed, and instead taught the king that true ascent to heaven comes only through the precepts, the ten good deeds, generosity, filial devotion, and compassion toward every living creature. Persuaded, the king gave freely from his own treasury for seven days, and the prince, with his new wife, then returned to his own country.

晝夜雨金銀珍寶。菩薩與女俱居一歲不領國事。臣白王言。當殺真陀羅。太子乃當國耳。王聞不可勅取太子縛之。女即飛去。過闍梨二人所。即語之言。太子來追。煩為送之。留指環為信。持與道人。王縱太子。不見真陀羅。躄地而言。當那得女。有神語之。汝不須啼。我語汝處。上第七山汝疾追之。便著珠衣帶刀持弓。珠衣光照四十里。太子去國明旦至七山見前二人。即言。見真陀羅女不。報言見之。環與太子。即俱前行。上第八香山。見四禪婆羅門前為作禮。時天王釋化作獼猴可畏動山。菩薩大怖。婆羅門言。常事我獼猴見之便不肯下。婆羅門言。汝下獼猴即來持果上。婆羅門受與三人共啖竟。語獼猴言。將是三人至天女所。獼猴言。是何等人使我將上天。婆羅門言。此國王太子。皆菩薩人當持佛道度諸天人蠕動之類。獼猴言大善。便將上天。道過五百辟支佛所。三人遣獼猴還取華用散佛菩薩。願言。令我疾得作佛。獼猴復言。願我為佛作馬。闍梨復言。願我為佛作奴。一人言我願得阿羅漢。到真陀羅所。獼猴作禮而去。菩薩與二人俱坐門外。青衣取水。菩薩問之用此水為。答言。與真陀羅浴。菩薩脫指環沒著水中。女浴見環即止。入報父母言。我婿在外。父母歡喜。父名頭[弓*莫]。便出相見。為菩薩作禮。闍梨二人復禮而去。王請入城共相勞問。以水澡菩薩手。以女及侍女千餘人悉付菩薩。留止七歲。

But after a year of neglecting his royal duties, the prince's own ministers demanded Candrā's sacrifice regardless, forcing her to flee before he could be seized — though not before she left her signet ring with Jāli's companion as a signal for the pursuing prince. Guided by a spirit's counsel and equipped with pearl-armor, the prince tracked her back through the same eight mountains, meeting the terrifying monkey (Śakra in disguise) who, once coaxed down and fed by the guardian brahmin, agreed to guide the party onward. Passing five hundred pratyekabuddhas along the way, all three travelers made their own vows before them — the prince to swiftly attain buddhahood, the monkey to become his future horse, and each companion to become his future servant or an arhat. Arriving at last at Candrā's own home, the prince slipped his ring into her bathwater, and she, recognizing it, ran to tell her overjoyed parents; the reunited couple was welcomed and entrusted to each other for seven more years.

菩薩白王言。我欲歸去視我父母。王言且止。七日後有鬼來至王所言。前聞大王亡女多賀以歸并得貴婿。王言。我女可耳其婿大尊。是國王子久欲還歸煩卿送之。鬼王言諾。即持一國七寶作七重樓。鬼著掌中送歸故國作禮而去。菩薩前為父母作禮問訊起居。王得太子。即國中寶藏金銀付與。王勅傍臣左右。四遠欲得金銀米穀。皆至宮門恣意所欲。後王壽終即生天上。時太子者今我身是。時四禪婆羅門者舍利弗是。時闍梨者今車匿是。時一人願作羅漢者今目揵連是。時天王釋化作獼猴願作馬者今揵陟是。時父王者今我父閱頭檀是。時母者今我母摩耶是。真陀羅者今裘夷是(出車匿經)。

When the prince finally asked to return to his own parents, a demon-king, arriving to congratulate the family on Candrā's return, agreed to carry the entire party home himself, delivering them safely in a moment. The Buddha then revealed the identities behind every figure in the story: "The prince was myself; the brahmin of the fourth dhyāna was Śāriputra; Jāli was Chandaka; the companion who vowed to become an arhat was Maudgalyāyana; the transformed monkey who vowed to become my horse was Kaṇṭhaka; my own father Śuddhodana was that old king; my mother Māyā was the queen; and Candrā herself was Gopī" (Source: Sūtra of Chandaka).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fascicle closes on a note that recasts everything the Buddha's own family has just endured — the massacre, the ordination struggles — as part of a far older, freely chosen network of vows sworn before witnesses lifetimes ago. Chandaka's loyalty on the night of the great departure, and Kaṇṭhaka's willingness to carry the prince away from everyone he loved, are revealed not as mere servants' duty but as the fulfillment of promises those very beings made to themselves, long before either the prince or his father existed as the people this fascicle has just spent thirteen entries describing.
