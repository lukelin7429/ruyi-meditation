Stūpas, Images, and the Fate of the Dharma

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 6 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Affairs Concerning the Buddha After His Nirvāṇa, Part 3" (現涅槃後事佛部第三) — twenty-three entries spanning the division of the Buddha's relics among rival kings, Aśoka's 84,000 stūpas and his successor's destruction of them, the Bodhi tree's journey to Sri Lanka, devotion at every scale from emperors to children to monkeys, the Lotus Sūtra's famous jeweled-stūpa scene, the origin of the first Buddha images, and the Sūtra on the Extinction of the Dharma.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 6 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 6 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 6 is the longest and most historically consequential fascicle so far, gathering some of Buddhism's best-known narratives — the Aśoka cycle, the transplanting of the Bodhi tree to Sri Lanka, the Lotus Sūtra's jeweled stūpa, and the Sūtra on the Extinction of the Dharma — under a single theme: what happens to devotion, and to the Dharma itself, once the Buddha is no longer present in the world. Each of the twenty-three entries is translated in full and kept in the compendium's own order, since together they build a single argument about faith across scale, from emperors to playing children to monkeys.

1. Devas, Nāgas, and Kings Divide the Relics

Rival kings nearly go to war over the Buddha's relics until a brahmin's appeal to the Buddha's own teaching of forbearance divides them peacefully into eight shares — and even devas and nāga-kings insist on their own portion.

Chinese Source

佛涅槃後。時波波國諸末羅眾(梁言力士)遮羅頗國諸跋離眾。羅摩伽國拘梨眾。毘留提國婆羅門眾。迦羅衛國釋住眾。毘舍離國諸離車眾。摩竭提國阿闍貰王(胎經云優田王頂生王惡生阿闍貰王最兵馬主容顏兵馬主熾盛兵馬主金剛兵馬主)各嚴四兵王遣香姓婆羅門(十誦律云因姓胎經云優婆吉)白拘尸力士言。佛是我師我之所尊。於君國內而取滅度。故從遠來請舍利分還國起塔。若分與我者舉國寶重與君共之。力士答曰。世尊屈降此土於茲滅度。國內士民自當供養。遠勞諸君不可得也。諸王共議。遜言和求既不見與。不惜身命當以力取。力士王曰。若欲舉兵力足相抵。終不可得。香姓婆羅門於八眾中高聲唱言。佛積修善忍於無量劫。諸君亦應聞又讚忍辱。何可興師共相陵奪。此非敬事。今舍利現在但當分作八分。使處處人民皆得供養。諸君亦皆受佛戒口誦語言。可爭舍利遂相殘害。力士報言。敬如君議。

Ru-Yi Translation

After the Buddha's nirvāṇa, the Mallas of Pāvā (in the Liang tongue, "the strongmen"), the Vṛjis, the Koliyas, the brahmins of Vaiṣṭhīputra, the Śākyas of Kapilavastu, the Licchavis of Vaiśālī, and King Ajātaśatru of Magadha — each mustering their own four divisions of troops — sent the brahmin Droṇa to say to the strongmen of Kuśinagara: "The Buddha was our teacher and the one we revere; since he entered final extinction within your territory, we have come from afar to request a share of the relics, to enshrine in a stūpa of our own. Grant us a share, and we will match it with treasure equal to our whole kingdom." The strongmen answered that the officials and people of their own country would make the offerings themselves, and that the other kings need not have troubled to come. The kings then resolved that, since a courteous request had been refused, they would take the relics by force instead — but the strongmen replied that even an army could not obtain them. The brahmin Droṇa then called out before all eight parties: "The Buddha, throughout immeasurable kalpas, cultivated goodness and forbearance — how could you now raise arms against one another over him? Let the relics simply be divided into eight shares, so that people everywhere may make their own offerings. You have all received the Buddha's own precepts — how can you now fight and destroy one another over his relics?" The strongmen agreed to this proposal.

時烟婆羅門。即分為八分。時釋提桓因即現為人語諸王言。我等諸天亦當有分。若共爭力則有勝負。幸可見與勿足為難。時阿耨達龍王文隣龍王伊那鉢龍王語八王言。我等亦應有舍利分。若不見與力足相伏。時優波吉言。諸君且止宜共分之。即分為三分。一分與諸天。一分與龍王。一分屬八王。以蜜塗瓮裏以瓮量之。諸天得分還於天上起七寶塔。諸龍得分還於龍宮亦起寶塔。阿闍貰王共數其分各得八萬四千舍利。餘有佛口一[咨*毛]無敢取者。以阿闍貰王初求舍利投地氣乏最為篤至共持與之。阿闍貰歡喜鼓樂動天。難頭示龍王中道相逢曰。佛留舍利持一分與我。王曰。不可得也。龍曰。我是難頭示。能舉卿國土擲八萬里外磨碎如塵。王即怖懅以佛[咨*毛]與之。龍於須彌山下起塔。高八萬四千里。聳水精琉璃塔。

Droṇa then divided the relics into eight shares. At that moment Śakra manifested in human form and told the kings the devas, too, should have a share, lest a contest of force become necessary — and the nāga-kings Anavatapta, Vitṛṇa, and Elāpattra likewise demanded their own share, threatening to compel it by force if refused. Upagupta then proposed dividing the relics into three portions: one for the devas, one for the nāgas, and one for the eight human kings, measured by pouring honey into a jar. The devas raised a stūpa of the seven treasures in heaven with their portion, and the nāgas likewise in their own palace; King Ajātaśatru's own share, divided among the eight kings, came to eighty-four thousand relic particles each. What remained — a single lock of the Buddha's own hair — no one dared claim, until it was agreed that Ajātaśatru, who had begged so earnestly for the relics that he had collapsed exhausted, should receive it out of respect for his sincerity. But the nāga-king Nandopananda met him on the road and demanded a share of even this remaining relic, threatening to cast the king's whole kingdom eighty thousand li away, ground to dust — and the terrified king surrendered it to him. The nāga raised, beneath Mount Sumeru, a towering crystal-and-beryl stūpa eighty-four thousand li high.

阿闍世王崩。阿育得其國土。時大臣白阿育王曰。難頭示龍先易阿闍貰奪將佛[咨*毛]去。阿育聞之。即勅鬼神王作鐵網鐵籍。置須彌山下水中欲縛取龍王。龍王大怖共設計言。阿育事佛伺其熟臥取其宮殿移著須彌山水中。水精塔下自出相見具說本末其瞋必息。便遣龍捧取阿育宮殿。眠覺不知何處。見水精塔高八萬四千里。喜怖交懷。難頭示龍自出辭謝云。阿闍貰王自持與我我不奪也。釋迦如來昔與我約云。吾涅槃後劫將盡時所有經律及袈裟應器皆取藏此塔中。彌勒來下當復出之。龍送王宮置於本處。烟婆羅門曰。請舍利瓶(菩薩胎經云瓫)我還頭那羅聚落起於瓶塔。力士與之。以瓶及著瓶舍利共起寶塔。波羅延那婆羅門居士復言。燒佛處炭與我。我還本國起為炭塔。衡國異道士求取地灰還國起塔。力士並然。亦有於闍維處起立寶塔。灰炭及土四十九斛。所起寶塔四十九。皆置長表法輪繒幡(出雙卷泥洹十誦律序菩薩處胎經及阿育王經)。

After Ajātaśatru's death, Aśoka inherited his kingdom, and a minister told him how Nandopananda had once tricked Ajātaśatru out of the Buddha's hair-relic. Aśoka ordered spirit-kings to set iron nets in the waters beneath Sumeru to capture the nāga-king, who, terrified, devised a plan instead: while Aśoka slept deeply, the nāgas lifted his entire palace and set it down beside their own crystal stūpa, so that on waking and hearing the whole story, his anger would subside. Waking to find his palace beside the towering stūpa, Aśoka felt both joy and terror at once; Nandopananda came forward to explain that Ajātaśatru had given the relic freely, not by force, and that Śākyamuni himself had arranged that all the sūtras, vinaya, robes, and bowls be gathered within this very stūpa as the present kalpa nears its end, to be brought forth again when Maitreya descends — and the nāga then carried the king's palace safely home. The brahmin Droṇa asked for the vessel that had measured the relics, to build a vessel-stūpa in his own village; a brahmin householder asked for the charcoal from the cremation site; ascetics of another land asked for the very ashes of the ground — all these requests were granted, and stūpas raised at the cremation site as well. In all, forty-nine measures of ash, charcoal, and earth gave rise to forty-nine jeweled stūpas, each marked with a finial, a Dharma-wheel, and silk banners (Source: the two-fascicle Nirvāṇa Sūtra; the preface of the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya; the Sūtra on Bodhisattvas Entering the Womb; and the Aśokāvadāna).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The irony of this opening story is pointed: kings who had just heard the Buddha's own teaching of non-retaliation nearly go to war over his physical remains within days of his death, and are stopped only by a brahmin's appeal to the very teaching they are about to violate. The devas and nāga-kings pressing their own claims by threat of force, no different from the human kings, suggest the compendium is not romanticizing the supernatural realms here — greed and pride operate the same way at every level of the cosmos, and only the accident of a workable compromise (eight equal shares, honey-measured) prevents the Buddha's own funeral from becoming a battlefield.

2–4. King Aśoka's 84,000 Stūpas, and Their Destruction

Aśoka reclaims the relics and distributes them into 84,000 stūpas across the known world, spurred on by rivalry with a nāga-king's own devotion; the karmic backstory explains why 84,000 was always the number; and, generations later, a fourth-line king destroys every one of them to secure his own name through infamy instead.

Chinese Source
阿育王造八萬四千塔

阿育王莊嚴四兵。往開七塔故取舍利唯餘龍塔。龍將王入宮言。此塔我所供養願為留之。王即聽許。還國造作八萬四千寶函分布舍利遍此函中。復作八萬四千寶瓶及諸幡蓋。付諸夜叉於閻浮提一切地乃至大海處處起塔。先諮耶舍羅漢云。欲於一日一念中起八萬四千塔令一時俱成。耶舍羅漢甚相讚美。王後與龍校其功德。並秤二像。而龍重王輕。王廣請眾僧植功不息。後復共秤。輕重衡平。王轉復修習。知功德日多。興兵往討。始造中路。龍王大小莫不奉迎。一塔舍利并以恭献王。後觀佛得道化人之處隨復造塔(出阿育王經第一卷又出雜譬喻經上卷)在王境內有一千二百寺。造織金縷幡亦一千二百。又千葉金華欲手自懸散。始辦而遇重疾。恐乖本心泫然泣下。沙門謂王曰。所修功德不可計數。正當開意何用悲為。但自一心。我當令王得果所願。沙門即以神力使一千二百寺皆現王前。病苦即滅。欲繫諸幡。凡諸剎抄低就王手。成就本願。延壽二十五年(出迦葉經又出雜譬喻經)又興功德二十年中。臨命終時。繼念三寶心心不絕。無所悋惜盡成菩提(出雜阿含經第二十五卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation
King Aśoka's 84,000 Stūpas

King Aśoka, mustering his forces, opened seven of the eight original stūpas to reclaim their relics, leaving only the nāga's own stūpa untouched at its keeper's request. Returning home, he had eighty-four thousand jeweled caskets made, distributing relics into each, along with eighty-four thousand vessels, banners, and canopies, entrusting them to yakṣas to raise stūpas throughout Jambudvīpa, even to the ocean's edge — all eighty-four thousand completed simultaneously, in a single day and thought, at the arhat Yaśas's own suggestion. The king later weighed his own merit against the nāga's on a scale using two images of themselves, and, finding the nāga's side heavier, invited the whole sangha to cultivate merit until the two sides balanced, and continued cultivating still further merit afterward. He also raised troops on campaign, receiving tribute relics from nāga-kings along the way, and built further stūpas at every site of the Buddha's own life (Source: Aśokāvadāna, fascicle 1; also the Sūtra of Miscellaneous Similes, upper fascicle). Within his realm he maintained twelve hundred monasteries and had twelve hundred gold-threaded banners made, along with a golden flower he intended to hang with his own hand — but fell gravely ill just as these were completed, and wept, fearing he would not fulfill his vow. A monk told him his merit was already beyond counting, and, by supernormal power, made all twelve hundred monasteries appear before him at once; his suffering vanished, and as he reached for the banners, every finial bent down of itself into his hand, fulfilling his vow and extending his life twenty-five years (Source: Kāśyapa Sūtra; also the Sūtra of Miscellaneous Similes). Cultivating merit for twenty years more, at the moment of his death his mind remained fixed without interruption on the Three Jewels, and he attained complete awakening (Source: Saṃyukta Āgama, fascicle 25).

阿難問八萬四千塔因

阿難白佛。先造何因。分身舍利起八萬四千寶塔。佛言。過去有國王名波塞奇領閻浮提八萬四千國。時世有佛名曰弗沙。王與臣民供養於佛及比丘僧。時王念言。邊陲小國處所偏僻人民之類無由修福。即召畫師圖畫佛像。分布遠國咸得供養。時諸畫師看佛相好。適得一處忘失餘處不能得成。時波塞奇王。調和眾彩手自畫像以為模楷。諸師寫學畫八萬四千像。分布八萬四千小國。諸小國王皆得供養。波塞奇王今我是也。緣此功德身有三十二相。涅槃之後復得八萬四千諸塔(出賢愚經第四卷)。

Ānanda Asks the Cause

Ānanda asked what past cause would lead to the Buddha's own relics one day filling eighty-four thousand stūpas. The Buddha explained: long ago, King Puṣyaka ruled eighty-four thousand kingdoms under the Buddha Puṣya; wishing the people of his own remote border territories to have images for their own worship, he found that his painters could never capture the Buddha's full likeness at once, so he blended the colors and painted the master image himself, which his artisans then copied eighty-four thousand times for distribution. "King Puṣyaka is myself in that life," the Buddha said — "through this merit my body attained the thirty-two marks, and after my nirvāṇa I likewise obtained eighty-four thousand stūpas" (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 4).

弗沙蜜多羅王壞八萬四千塔

阿育王崩。諸臣欲立太子以紹王位。有一大臣。名阿菟羅地。曰。阿育大王。誓捨滿十萬億金。作諸功德。尚少四億。太子封藏不與。王捨閻浮提地。以施佛法僧。欲以滿之。今日大地皆屬三寶。云何便使太子為王。諸臣即辦四億金送寺然後共立。知是四世王。名弗沙蜜多羅。問諸臣曰。我當作何等事。令我名業久在。有臣啟曰王。先王阿育。造八萬四千如來之塔。復興種種供養。名德相傳無有斷絕。王曰。先王有大威德。能辦此事。我不能作。更思餘事。有一臣曰。有二種法。名傳不滅。一者作善。二者作惡。先王造塔。今王壞塔。二俱不朽。王乃從之。即興四兵往詣寺舍。先至鷄雀寺中門前。有石師子。即作師子吼。王聞驚怖。還入城中。如是再三。呼諸比丘問言。我壞塔壞房何等為善。比丘答曰。並不應行。必不得已寧壞僧房勿壞塔也。王即殺害比丘。壞諸塔寺。至婆伽羅國。又復唱言。若有得沙門頭者賞之千金。此國有一羅漢。化作多比丘頭。傳與百姓送往請金。王之庫藏金寶竭盡。後知羅漢倍復瞋忿。羅漢入滅盡定。王往殺之終不能得。以滅盡定力不傷其體。如是漸進至塔門邊牙齒塔。神曰。有蟲行神先求我女。我不與之。今為護法即呼女與。共立誓言。卿伏此王勿壞正法。時蟲行神往南方海中排摚大山推迮王上及四兵眾。無不死盡。眾人唱言。快哉快哉。孔雀苗裔於此永盡(出雜阿含經第二十五卷)。

King Puṣyamitra Destroys the Stūpas

After Aśoka's death, a minister pointed out that Aśoka had given away the very land of Jambudvīpa to complete his vow of donating a hundred thousand million gold pieces, so that all the earth now belonged to the Three Jewels — the ministers made up the shortfall before installing the crown prince, the fourth king of that line, named Puṣyamitra. Asked how he might make his own name endure like his predecessor's, and told he lacked the virtue to build stūpas as Aśoka had, one minister observed that a name endures through either good deeds or evil ones equally — if the former king built stūpas, this king destroying them would secure the same lasting fame. The king took this advice, but was driven back three times by a stone lion's roar at a monastery gate before finally killing monks and destroying stūpas regardless, even offering a bounty for monks' heads until an arhat's conjured false heads exhausted his own treasury. Unable even to kill an arhat protected by the absorption of cessation, the king advanced at last to a tooth-relic stūpa, whose guardian spirit made a pact with an insect-spirit — offering his own daughter in exchange — to destroy the king in defense of the Dharma. The insect-spirit dislodged a great mountain in the southern ocean and crushed the king and his entire army beneath it, ending the dynasty forever (Source: Saṃyukta Āgama, fascicle 25).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The minister's advice to Puṣyamitra — that building and destroying earn the same lasting fame — is a genuinely unsettling piece of political cynicism for a Buddhist text to record so matter-of-factly, and the compendium does not soften it with commentary; it simply lets the outcome speak for itself. A stone lion's roar and an arhat's serene indifference to being murdered are treated as ordinary obstacles, but the actual downfall comes from an unglamorous alliance between two minor spirits settling a private grudge — the text's implicit suggestion being that no tyrant, however grand the atrocity, escapes consequences delivered through whatever channel happens to be available.

5. King Devanampiya Tissa and the Bodhi Tree

The compendium's longest single narrative: relics obtained from Śakra himself, riding an elephant's head into the sky; and the miraculous self-severing, transplanting, and sea-crossing of a branch of the original Bodhi tree to the island kingdom of Siṃhala (Sri Lanka) — one of the foundational stories of Sri Lankan Buddhism.

Beneath the tree, a single ripe fruit fell to Mahinda, whose seed the king planted in a golden vessel; eight saplings sprang up within moments, which the king honored as sovereign beneath a white parasol, planting them one by one at sites across the island — from Jambukola to the palace gate to the great monasteries — while thirty-two further saplings from the tree's remaining fruit were planted throughout the royal park, so that stūpas came to fill the whole island, its peace secured by the Bodhi tree itself. The queen and a thousand women were ordained under Saṅghamittā and attained arhatship in succession, as did the king's nephew and five hundred companions under Mahinda. On a later visit to the tree, the ground shook three separate times as offerings were made at three different sites, each time an omen — Mahinda explained — of some future event there: a hall where the sangha would one day recite the precepts, a future gathering-place, and finally the site of a great stūpa yet to come. The king wished to build that stūpa himself, but Mahinda told him it was not for him to build — a future descendant, Duṭṭhagāmaṇī Abhaya, not the king's own grandson, would raise it instead. The king had this prophecy engraved on a stone pillar to stand as record (Source: Samantapāsādikā, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This narrative is the Chinese Buddhist record of events central to Sri Lankan Buddhist self-understanding — Mahinda's mission, Saṅghamittā's arrival with the Bodhi-tree sapling, and the founding of the tradition still maintained today at Anurādhapura, where a tree said to descend from this very branch is tended as the oldest continuously documented tree in the world. Reading it as compiled here, far from its own homeland and centuries removed, is a reminder of how thoroughly the compendium's compilers thought of the Dharma as a single connected story spanning the whole known world, not a series of separate regional traditions.

An elder in King Aśoka's kingdom, having supported twenty thousand monks for a full year, was summoned by the king, who, hearing of his great wealth, asked what he actually possessed; told "nothing at all," the disbelieving king sent someone to inspect his household and found a mansion finer than the palace itself — yet no grain, cloth, or coin anywhere. The Buddha's right canine tooth and right collarbone had stūpas raised for them in Trāyastriṃśa Heaven and on the island of Siṃhala; the relics thus obtained now rest in Śakra's own palace (Source: Samantapāsādikā, fascicle 2).

幼童聚沙為塔

佛遊波羅奈時。五百幼童相結為伴。俱共行戲於江水邊聚沙為塔。各自說言。吾塔甚好卿學吾作。其五百童雖有善心宿命福薄。天大暴雨江水卒漲。五百幼童俱時溺死。父母號哭求索屍喪。莫知所在。佛言。宿命不請勿生怨恨。此諸兒等宿命應爾。今生兜率天。佛放光明。令此父母遠見其子。尋時皆來散華供養。佛言。善哉。因造沙塔即得生天見彌勒佛。五百天子各啟父母勿復愁憂。但努力精進。繞佛三匝作禮飛去(出五百幼童經又出生經第四卷)。

Children's Sand Stūpas

Five hundred children playing by a riverbank in Vārāṇasī piled sand into stūpas, each boasting his own the finest — until a sudden flood drowned them all together. Their grieving parents could not even find the bodies, but the Buddha told them not to resent what past karma had made inevitable: the children were now reborn in Tuṣita Heaven, and, shown this by the Buddha's own light, the parents came to scatter flowers in offering instead. "Excellent," the Buddha said — "through the simple act of building sand stūpas, they attained rebirth in heaven and now behold Maitreya himself." The five hundred young devas told their own parents not to grieve, but to strive diligently, before circling the Buddha three times and flying away (Source: Sūtra of the Five Hundred Children; also the Jātaka-nidāna, fascicle 4).

獼猴起土石塔

The Monkeys' Stūpa

Five hundred arhats dwelling at a stūpa in Kashmir, built to house the Buddha's own hair and nails, were watched by five hundred monkeys, who, moved by their devotion, carried mud and stones from a nearby ravine to build their own crude stūpa, raising a wooden post as a finial and a tattered banner — until a flood swept them all away and drowned them. Reborn in Trāyastriṃśa Heaven, they saw at once their own past lives, and returned with flowers, incense, and music to circle their own former corpses in offering. Asked by faithless brahmins why they troubled to honor such a thing, the devas answered: "These were our own former bodies — through merely imitating the monks in play, we were born in heaven, and we return now to repay that debt. Your own century of austerities have earned you nothing at all — come instead to Gṛdhrakūṭa and pay homage there, whose merit is boundless." The brahmins, delighted, went at once to the Buddha and prostrated themselves in offering (Source: Dharmapada with Similes, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The monkeys' stūpa is the fascicle's clearest statement of its underlying thesis: the animals build in pure imitation, understanding nothing of doctrine, motivated by nothing more than watching monks do something that looked meaningful — and this alone is enough to secure their rebirth in heaven. Set beside the children's sand stūpas, built in the same spirit of play, the compendium is making a case that the intention behind an act of devotion matters more than its sophistication, competence, or even the devotee's capacity to understand what they are doing.

The Four Earthly Stūpas

In Kapilavastu — the very center of heaven and earth — a birthplace stūpa stands; at the site of enlightenment in Magadha, an enlightenment stūpa; at the Deer Park in Vārāṇasī, a stūpa for the turning of the Dharma-wheel; and in Kuśinagara, amid the twin sāla trees, a parinirvāṇa stūpa (Source: Excerpts from the Collected Sūtras).

Mahāsattva's Bone Stūpa

A prince named Mahāsattva, wandering through the forest, found a tigress that had just given birth to seven cubs, starving and near death; he pierced his own neck with bamboo and threw himself down before her, letting her devour him entirely. When he did not return, the grieving king had his remaining relics gathered and a stūpa of the seven treasures raised over them (Source: Golden Light Sūtra, fascicle 4).

15–18, 20. Relics Across Time

The Buddha reveals a stūpa holding relics from his own past life as a bodhisattva; a king who never met Śākyamuni preserves a whole-body relic of the prior Buddha Kāśyapa; a community restores what generations had let fall to ruin; and, beneath the earth itself, a vajra realm holds every buddha's relics at once.

The Buddha Reveals a Past-Life Relic Stūpa

A stūpa of the seven treasures once sprang up from the earth, and the Buddha rose to bow before it. Asked by the tree-spirit of the enlightenment site why the most supreme of all beings would bow before a mere stūpa, the Buddha explained that his own whole-body relics from a past life as a bodhisattva rested within it — the very body through which he had early attained buddhahood. He had Ānanda open the seven-jeweled casket to show the assembly the relics, red and white, saying: "These are what the cultivation of precepts, concentration, and wisdom produce — a field of blessing exceedingly rare and supreme." The assembly rejoiced and bowed in reverence (Source: Golden Light Sūtra, fascicle 4).

King Kṛkin's Stūpa for Kāśyapa Buddha

In a past age, King Kṛkin, after the Tathāgata Kāśyapa's nirvāṇa, built a stūpa of gold and silver a thousand yojanas across and one yojana high, its moat still remaining beneath the earth today. Our own Tathāgata brought forth this stūpa to show the fourfold assembly, and Kāśyapa's whole-body relics appeared exactly as they had been (Source: Mahīśāsaka Vinaya, fascicle 30).

治迦葉佛故塔

迦葉佛泥洹闍維之後。以佛舍利起七寶塔。興敬供養。經歷數世。塔自彫壞。無補治者。有義合邑九萬三千人。時瓶沙王為上首。告眾人曰。汝等各自勸厲。共造福德。佛世難遇。人身難得。雖得為人。或墮邊地。生邪見舍。我等何為。貪此俗樂。不如開意治朽塔寺。即共修理。復共發願。設有福者。不墮三塗及八難處。共生人天。見釋迦文。初會說法。皆得度脫。以王為首。時人命終。生忉利天。經歷數世。釋迦出興時。九萬三千人。生摩竭國瓶沙作王(出普曜經第九卷)。

Repairing Kāśyapa Buddha's Stūpa

Kāśyapa Buddha's own relic stūpa, after several generations, fell into disrepair with no one to restore it. A community of ninety-three thousand people, led by King Bimbisāra, resolved together: "A buddha's age is hard to encounter, and a human body hard to obtain — why cling to worldly pleasure instead of restoring this ruined stūpa?" They repaired it together and vowed to be reborn among gods and men, to meet Śākyamuni at his very first teaching, and all attain deliverance. Reborn in Trāyastriṃśa Heaven, and, after further generations, when Śākyamuni finally arose in the world, all ninety-three thousand were reborn in Magadha, with Bimbisāra as their king (Source: Lalitavistara, fascicle 9).

德主王起五百塔

諸佛舍利在金剛塔

King Guṇādhipati's Five Hundred Stūpas

In a past age, a wheel-turning king named Guṇādhipati raised five hundred stūpas in a single day, each five hundred yojanas high (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 57).

All Buddhas' Relics in the Vajra Stūpa

The Buddha told the assembly to consider the structure of any single body of his own across countless past lives: this earth's own element is eighty-four myriad koṭi li thick; beneath it lies wind, then water, then fire, then sand, each of the same thickness; and beneath the sand lies a stratum of vajra, likewise as thick — and it is within this vajra stratum that the whole-body and fragmentary relics of every buddha rest. Beneath it lies yet another realm, named Wondrous Fragrance, where a buddha named Abiding-Nowhere is even now expounding the Dharma (Source: Sūtra on Bodhisattvas Entering the Womb, fascicle 2).

19. The Jeweled Stūpa Springs Forth

The Lotus Sūtra's most celebrated scene, preserved here in the compendium's own compressed retelling: a stūpa the size of mountains rises from the earth, containing a past buddha's undecayed body, kept whole across inconceivable ages by a single vow — to bear witness, wherever the Lotus Sūtra is taught, that its teaching is true.

Chinese Source

爾時佛前。有七寶塔。高五百由旬。縱廣二百五十由旬。從地踊出。住在空中。種種寶物。而莊校之。五千欄楯龕室千萬。無數幢幡。以為嚴飾。垂寶瓔珞。寶鈴萬億。而懸其上。四面皆出多摩羅跋栴檀之香。充遍世界。其諸幡蓋。以金銀琉璃硨璩碼碯真珠琥珀七寶合成。高至四天王宮。三十三天。雨天曼陀羅華。供養寶塔。下至八部。以一切華香瓔珞幡蓋伎樂。供養寶塔。爾時塔中。出大音聲歎言。善哉釋迦牟尼佛。以平等慧。教菩薩法。四眾聞塔所出音聲。皆得法喜。怪未曾有。此寶塔中。有如來全身。過去東方無量千萬阿僧祇世界。國名寶淨。佛號多寶。其佛行菩薩道時。作大誓願。若我成佛滅度之後。於十方國土。有說法華經處。我之塔廟為聽經故。踊現其前以作證明。若有說法華。全身舍利在於塔中。讚言善哉。大樂說菩薩。白佛言。世尊。我等願欲見此佛身。佛言。多寶佛有深重願。若我寶塔。為聽法華經故出於諸佛前時。欲以我身示四眾者。彼佛分身諸佛。在於十方。盡還一處。然後我身乃出現耳。

Ru-Yi Translation

Before the Buddha, a stūpa of the seven treasures, five hundred yojanas high and two hundred fifty across, sprang up from the earth and hovered in the sky, adorned with countless niches, banners, and jeweled bells, its fragrance filling the whole world, its finials rising as high as the palace of the Four Heavenly Kings. The gods rained down flowers upon it, and every class of being made offerings, as a great voice from within praised: "Excellent, excellent, Śākyamuni Buddha, who with impartial wisdom teaches the bodhisattva Dharma!" Within this stūpa lay the whole body of a Tathāgata — for, in a past age, in a world immeasurably distant to the east, a Buddha named Prabhūtaratna had vowed that after his own nirvāṇa, wherever the Lotus Sūtra was taught in any of the ten directions, his own stūpa would spring up before that very teaching to bear witness to it, his whole-body relics still present within. Asked to see this Buddha's own body, Śākyamuni explained that Prabhūtaratna's vow required first that all of Śākyamuni's own emanation-buddhas, present throughout the ten directions, gather together in one place.

大樂說言。我等亦願欲見世尊分身諸佛。佛放白毫一光。東西南北四維上下諸佛。諸佛各告眾菩薩言。善男子。我今應往娑婆世界釋迦牟尼佛所。并供養多寶如來寶塔。時娑婆世界即變清淨。琉璃為地。寶樹莊嚴。黃金為繩。以界八道。無諸聚落村營城邑大海江河山川林藪燒大寶香。移諸天人置於他土。是時諸佛各將一大菩薩以為侍者。至娑婆世界。各到寶樹下。樹下皆有師子之座。高五百由旬。十方諸佛皆悉來集。坐於八方。諸佛欲同開寶塔。即從坐起住虛空中。一切四眾起立合掌一心觀佛。於是釋迦牟尼佛。以右指開七寶塔戶。出大音聲。如却關鑰。一切眾會。皆見多寶如來於寶塔中坐師子座。全身不散如入禪定。又聞其言。善哉善哉。釋迦牟尼佛。快說是法華經。我為聽是經故而來至此。爾時四眾等見過去無量千萬億劫滅度之佛。說如是言。歎未曾有。以天寶華聚散多寶佛及釋迦牟尼佛上。爾時多寶佛。於寶塔中。分半坐與釋迦牟尼佛坐。時釋迦牟尼佛坐其半坐。以神通力接諸大眾。皆在虛空(出妙法蓮華經第四卷)。

Mahāpratibhāna said they also wished to see the World-Honored One's own emanation-buddhas. The Buddha emitted a ray of light, and buddhas throughout every direction each told their attending bodhisattvas they must go to the Sahā world to witness this. The Sahā world itself then transformed into a realm of pure beryl ground and jeweled trees, its devas and humans moved elsewhere to make room, as each visiting buddha arrived beneath a jeweled tree upon a lion-throne five hundred yojanas high. Wishing together to open the stūpa, the buddhas of the ten directions rose and stood in the sky as the whole fourfold assembly gazed upon them; Śākyamuni then, with a single finger, opened the stūpa's door, and a great sound issued forth like a bolt-lock drawing back — revealing Prabhūtaratna seated upon a lion-throne within, his whole body undecayed as though in meditative absorption, saying: "Excellent, excellent, Śākyamuni Buddha — you have well expounded this Lotus Sūtra; it is to hear this very teaching that I have come." The assembly, witnessing a buddha extinct for immeasurable koṭis of kalpas speaking thus, marveled and scattered heavenly flowers upon them both. Prabhūtaratna then divided his own seat in half and offered it to Śākyamuni, who sat beside him, lifting the entire assembly by his own power into the open sky as well (Source: Lotus Sūtra, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Prabhūtaratna's vow inverts the usual relationship between teaching and proof: ordinarily a claim needs evidence, but here the evidence — an entire buddha's preserved body, summoned across unimaginable distance — exists solely to certify one specific teaching, the Lotus Sūtra, wherever and whenever it is taught. The compendium compresses roughly a full chapter of the Lotus Sūtra into this single entry, which signals how significant the compilers considered this scene: not simply a miracle story like the others in this fascicle, but the doctrinal keystone of an entire scripture, worth preserving even in radically condensed form.

21. Regret, and Rebirth as a Great Fish

A wealthy donor-monk, indignant that visiting guests would take priority over his own claim to a monastery he built, burns it down in a fit of pique — and the karmic consequence stretches across three hundred sixty kalpas before resolving through the very people he once wronged.

Chinese Source

昔有沙門其家大富。造作塔廟。以栴檀為柱七寶為剎。未成之頃有五百沙門從遠方來。而其國內有五百賢者。各各給與袈裟衣被。國人謂寺主。遠人當去。我先發遣。阿闍梨常住自當作分寺主。沙門念言。我之功德積若須彌不可稱計。而國人不能佐助我。我但為一切賤貴近遠。便以火燒寺塔。後入地獄畜生各九十劫。後作大魚。身在海中。長四十萬里。眼如日月。牙長二萬里。正白似雪山。舌廣四萬里。正赤似火山。口廣五萬里。時有五百人。入海採寶。正是先身給五百沙門衣者。因緣宿對魚張口飲水。時舫從流甚疾。皆大恐怖。同稱南無佛。魚聞其音合口而聽。水住不流。聞船上有諷經之聲。魚便淚出。自念不聞此音其來甚久。因不復食經歷七日命終海中。浮屍著岸神生法家。墮地能語。便識宿命。年滿八歲。得羅漢道。還詣海邊見其故身。積骨如山觀髑髏內七日不遍。坐燒塔寺百八十劫在惡道中(出譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a wealthy monk who built a monastery and stūpa, its pillars of sandalwood and its finial of the seven treasures. Before it was even completed, five hundred visiting monks arrived from afar, while five hundred local worthies had already been given robes by this same donor-monk; the local people suggested that, since the visitors would soon leave anyway, the resident monk should keep only his rightful share as the actual builder. Indignant that his own vast merit went unsupported by the very people he had served, the monk, in his fury at everyone — high or low, near or far — set fire to his own monastery and stūpa. He fell as a result into hell, then into the animal realm, ninety kalpas in each, before becoming a monstrous fish in the ocean, four hundred thousand li long, its fangs and tongue vast as mountains. Five hundred treasure-hunters at sea — the very same five hundred monks he had once clothed — were nearly swept into the fish's open mouth by the current, and cried out together in terror, "Homage to the Buddha!" Hearing this, the fish closed its mouth to listen, and, hearing further the sound of sūtra-recitation from the ship, wept, realizing it had been an unimaginably long time since it had last heard such a sound. It refused to eat from that day on and died within seven days, its spirit reborn into a righteous family, speaking from birth and knowing its own past lives; at eight years old it attained arhatship, and, returning to the seashore to view its own former body's bones piled like a mountain, spent seven days examining the skull alone without finishing — the cost, for burning down that one monastery and stūpa, of a hundred eighty kalpas among the evil destinies (Source: Sūtra of Similes, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The precision of the karmic accounting here is the point: a single act of destructive anger, however great the prior merit that preceded it, produces a penalty measured in specific, countable kalpas — and the story closes the loop deliberately, having the fish's own suffering finally broken by the recitation of sūtras from a ship crewed by the exact five hundred people his original generosity had once benefited. Generosity and destruction are shown as fully separable: the merit from the robes given does not cancel the debt from the fire, nor does the fire erase the merit — both simply run their own course to completion.

22. The Origin of Buddha Images

Six accounts, forming a connected history: two kings, unable to bear the Buddha's three-month absence in heaven, each commission the first Buddha images out of pure longing — establishing a custom that becomes the origin of Buddhist iconography — followed by the conversion of Aśoka's own brother and the Buddha's shadow left permanently in a cave.

King Udayana's Sandalwood Image

When the fourfold assembly grew lax in seeking the Dharma, Śakra invited the Buddha to Trāyastriṃśa Heaven to teach his mother for the three-month rains retreat; wishing to make the human world long for him all the more, the Buddha departed without a word. King Prasenajit of Śrāvastī and King Udayana of Kauśāmbī, unable even to learn from Ānanda where the Buddha had gone, both fell ill with longing. King Udayana then had his craftsmen carve an image of the Tathāgata from Gośīrṣa sandalwood, five feet tall (Source: Ekottarika Āgama, fascicle 19). King Udayana had also asked the Buddha what blessing would come from making and reverently serving such an image. The Buddha answered: "Whoever makes an image of the Buddha will, in every life to come, have a fine body, be born after death in the seventh Brahmā heaven, and always be born into a noble, wealthy family, beloved by all, with boundless wealth — or as a king, or a wheel-turning sovereign, or into a household devoted to virtue — and will never enter the three evil destinies" (Source: Sūtra on the Causes and Conditions of Making Images).

King Udayana's Golden Image

King Udayana, unable to bear his longing during the Buddha's absence, also cast a golden image of him and had it carried out on an elephant to receive the returning Buddha as though it were the living Buddha himself. As the Buddha descended through the air, he spoke directly to the image: "In the ages to come you shall accomplish great works of the Buddha's own mission — I entrust my disciples to your care. Whoever makes and offers worship to an image shall surely attain, in a future life, the pure samādhi of buddha-recollection." The Buddha told Ānanda to convey this to all disciples: images made after his own extinction, complete with every mark and even painted footprints set with crystal beads, would let all who saw them rejoice, destroying the sins of birth and death accumulated over countless kalpas (Source: Sūtra on the Samādhi of Buddha-Contemplation, fascicle 6).

King Prasenajit's Golden Image

Hearing of King Udayana's image, King Prasenajit likewise had craftsmen cast an image of the Tathāgata in fine gold, over five feet tall — and so there came to be, for the first time, two such images in all of Jambudvīpa (Source: Ekottarika Āgama, fascicle 19).

King Prasenajit's Sandalwood Image

During the Buddha's ninety days teaching his mother in heaven, King Prasenajit, longing to see him, carved a sandalwood image and placed it upon the Buddha's own seat. When the Buddha returned, the image itself rose to greet him; the Buddha told it to resume its seat, saying it would serve as the model for the fourfold assembly after his own nirvāṇa — and it did so, becoming the very first of all Buddha images, the pattern later generations have followed. When fire later destroyed the seven-story Jetavana monastery itself, everyone believed this original image lost, until it was found unharmed days later in a small side chapel, and the rebuilt monastery restored around it (Source: Account of Foreign Lands).

King Sudhāna's Stone Image

King Sudhāna, Aśoka's own brother, saw ascetics practicing extreme austerities in the mountains and asked one why his efforts had failed. The ascetic admitted a herd of mating deer had moved his own heart uncontrollably — leading Sudhāna to ask how, if even such asceticism could not overcome desire, the comfortable Śākyan monks could possibly be free of it. Alarmed that his own brother had fallen into such wrong views, Aśoka staged an elaborate test: giving Sudhāna the throne, robes, and palace pleasures for seven days as though condemned to death immediately after, then asking whether he had enjoyed himself. Sudhāna answered he had neither seen nor heard anything, since a man condemned to die feels nothing for the five desires regardless of what surrounds him. This exchange led Aśoka himself to reflect aloud on how every life is likewise condemned, and how a monk's constant awareness of this — rather than his outward comfort — is what frees him from desire; hearing his own brother's teaching turned back on him this way, Sudhāna's mind opened at last, and he asked to go forth on the path. Practicing diligently, he attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra on Seeking Release from the Prison). The Aśokāvadāna adds that, once his brother attained the path, Aśoka rejoiced and asked to support him, but Sudhāna chose instead to sustain himself in the wilderness; Aśoka had a mountain built within the city itself to grant him seclusion there, and Sudhāna, giving up even his own robes and provisions, had a stone image carved, one zhang six feet tall, housed in a niche hollowed from that very mountain.

有龍王請佛常住其所。若不住者我發惡心無由得道。諸梵天王復慇懃勸請。願為一切眾生莫獨住此。龍以七寶殿奉上如來。佛言。不須但以羅剎石窟施我。佛攝神足獨入石室。自敷坐具跏趺而坐。時羅剎女及龍。為四大弟子及阿難。又造五石室。佛受那先訶城王及諸國請。處處見佛虛空華座滿中化佛。龍王歡喜發大誓願。願我來世得佛如此。佛受王請七日。攝於神足。從石窟出。與諸比丘遊履諸處。龍所隨從。後佛還國啼哭雨淚。白言。願勿捨我。世尊安慰云。當坐汝窟中經千五百歲。時諸龍王合掌請佛入窟。佛坐窟中作十八變。踊身入石。猶如明鏡。在於石內影現於外。遠望則見。近視則無。諸天百千供養佛影。影亦說法。石窟高一丈八尺。深二十四步。石色清白(出觀佛三昧經第六卷)。

The Buddha's Shadow in the Cave

A nāga-king, fearing his own malicious heart would prevent him ever attaining the path without the Buddha's constant presence, begged him to remain permanently, offering a palace of the seven treasures; the Buddha asked only for the rākṣasas' own stone cave instead, entering it alone to sit cross-legged. When the time came for the Buddha to leave, the grieving nāgas were promised he would remain seated within their cave for fifteen hundred years regardless — and so the Buddha, entering the cave one last time, performed the eighteen transformations and sank his own body into the very stone, as though into a bright mirror: present within the rock yet visible from outside as a reflection, clearly seen from a distance but vanishing on close approach. Hundreds and thousands of devas came to make offerings to this shadow of the Buddha, which itself expounded the Dharma — the cave measuring one zhang eight feet high, twenty-four paces deep, its stone a pure white (Source: Sūtra on the Samādhi of Buddha-Contemplation, fascicle 6).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Taken together, these six accounts function as the compendium's own origin myth for an entire visual tradition: two kings, driven by nothing more sophisticated than grief at an absence, independently invent the Buddha image, and the Buddha's own retroactive blessing on the practice — spoken directly to the very first image, according to the second story — authorizes everything that follows from it, down to the Buddhist statuary Luke's own readers might see in a temple today. The Sudhāna story sits somewhat apart thematically, but its own closing act — commissioning a stone image once he has renounced everything else — quietly reinforces the same point: image-making survives even the total renunciation of worldly goods, because it is itself understood as an act of practice rather than possession.

But by that time the monks themselves, no longer diligent, will spend their days in idle debate and vanity, monks in form alone, destroying the Dharma from within far more effectively than any external enemy — and the devas, disgusted, will withdraw their protection entirely, weeping and foretelling that the Dharma will be extinguished within seven days, precisely because the monks will quarrel on the very day set for reciting the precepts. Devas and humans alike weep that the Tathāgata's true Dharma is now entirely extinguished, the earth itself shaking as countless beings scatter in grief — and from this single spasm of violence among the tradition's own last representatives, the Buddha's teaching vanishes at once and entirely (Source: Saṃyukta Āgama).

佛告阿難。我泥洹後。法欲滅時。五濁惡世魔道興盛。諸魔沙門壞亂吾道。著俗衣裳飾好袈裟五色之服。飲酒炙肉殺生貪味。無有慈心更相憎嫉。時有菩薩精進修德。一切敬待人所宗尚。教化平等。憐貧念老。救育窮厄。恒以經像。令人奉事。作諸福德。志性溫善。不侵害人。捐身濟物。不自惜己。忍辱仁和。設有是人。眾魔比丘咸共嫉之。誹謗揚惡擯黜驅遣不令得住。自共於後不修道德。寺廟空荒不復修理。轉就毀壞。但貪財物。積聚不散。不作福德。販賣奴婢耕田種殖。焚燒山林傷害眾生。無有慈心。奴為比丘。婢為比丘尼。無有道德。婬妷濁亂。男女不別。令道薄淡。皆由斯輩。或避縣官。依倚吾道。求作比丘。不修戒律。月半月盡。絓名講戒。厭惓懈怠。不欲聽聞。抄略前後。不肯盡說。經不誦讀。設有讀者。不識字句。為強言是不諮明者。貢高求名噓天推步。以為榮貴望人供養。諸魔比丘命終之後。精神當墮無澤地獄。五逆罪中餓鬼畜生靡不更歷。過恒沙劫罪竟。乃出生在邊國無三寶處。

The Buddha told Ānanda: "After my nirvāṇa, as the Dharma nears its extinction, in this evil age of the five turbidities, demonic monks will corrupt my own teaching — dressed in fine, worldly robes, drinking wine, eating meat, without compassion, envying one another. Even then a few genuine bodhisattvas will still practice with diligence and virtue, teaching impartially and caring for the poor and old — yet it is precisely such people the demonic monks will envy and drive out, while they themselves let the monasteries fall into ruin, hoarding wealth, trading in slaves, farming for profit, and harming living things without compassion, their own servants becoming 'monks' and 'nuns' in name only, mired in lustful confusion. Some will become monks merely to escape the law, without ever practicing the precepts, reciting the rules only in name, too idle even to listen, unable to read the very scriptures they claim to follow, prideful and seeking only fame and others' support. When these demonic monks die, their spirits will fall through hell, the hungry ghosts, and the animal realm for kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, and only then be reborn in some border country devoid of the Three Jewels entirely.

法欲滅時。女人精勤恒作功德。男子懈慢不用法語。眼見沙門如視糞土。無有信心。法輪殄沒諸天泣淚。水旱不調五穀不熟。災疫流行死亡者眾。人民勤苦縣官侵剋。不修道理皆思樂亂。惡人轉多善者甚少。日月轉促人命轉短。四十頭白裁壽六十。男子壽短。女人命長。七八九十或至百歲。大水忽起卒至無期。世人不信故謂有常。眾生雜類無有豪賤。沒溺浮。漂魚鼈噉食菩薩比丘眾魔驅逐不預眾會。菩薩入山福德之處惔怕自守以為忻快。壽命延長諸天衛護。月光出世得相遭值。共興吾道五十二歲。首楞嚴經。般舟三昧。先滅化去。十二部經尋復化滅盡不復現不見文字。沙門袈裟自然變白。聖王去後吾法滅盡。譬如油燈臨欲滅時光更猛盛。於是便滅。吾法盡時亦如燈滅。自此之後難可綱紀。如是久後彌勒當下世間作佛。天下太平毒氣消除。雨潤和適五穀滋茂。樹木長大人長八丈。皆壽八萬四千歲。眾生得度不可稱計(出法滅盡經)。

"As the Dharma nears its end, women will remain diligent in merit while men grow heedless and contemptuous of monks entirely. The world will decline in every visible way — poor harvests, plague, corrupt officials, human lifespans shrinking until a man's hair turns white at forty and he dies by sixty — while sudden floods drown beings of every kind without warning, and even bodhisattvas will be driven from ordinary society into the mountains, sustained there by the devas' own protection. It is in this age that one named Candraprabha will arise to sustain my teaching for a further fifty-two years — the Śūraṅgama Sūtra and the Pratyutpanna Samādhi vanishing first, then the whole canon disappearing entirely, its very characters no longer visible, monks' own robes turning white of themselves. Once the sage-kings are gone, my teaching will be utterly extinguished — but just as an oil lamp flares up brightest in the very instant before going dark, so too will my teaching blaze once more at its very end before being extinguished like that lamp. Long after, Maitreya shall at last descend and become a buddha, and the world will know great peace, its people growing to eight zhang tall and living eighty-four thousand years each, delivering beings beyond all counting" (Source: Sūtra on the Extinction of the Dharma).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This sūtra's central claim is worth sitting with: the Dharma is never destroyed by an external enemy strong enough to overpower it, but by its own custodians growing too comfortable, too idle, and too self-interested to sustain it — the four evil kings of the opening section are a mere prelude, defeated within the story itself, while the actual, final extinction comes from an argument over doctrinal seniority between two monks who are, by the story's own account, the very last sincere practitioners left. Placed at the close of a fascicle otherwise devoted to devotion — stūpas built by emperors, children, and monkeys alike — this final entry reframes everything before it: none of that devotion is presented as guaranteed to last forever, which is precisely what makes the compendium's insistence, throughout this fascicle, on building and rebuilding and honoring wherever possible read less as triumphalism than as urgency.
