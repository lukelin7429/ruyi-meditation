Fascicle 40 · 經律異相卷第四十(梵志部) · Complete

Brahmins

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 40 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Brahmins" (梵志部) — twelve entries on the brahmin class, ranging from the Buddha's own former life as a brahmin student to the brahmins who murdered a woman to frame him. Among them: the Dīpaṃkara prediction told in full, with the flower-girl who asks to be his wife in every future life and the displaced elder who vows to ruin his work in every future life; a brahmin's diagnostic dream of pig-faced kings beneath eraṇḍa trees; Māgandiya offering his daughter to the Buddha, and the wife who reads the Buddha's footprints in the road and tells him not to bother; a father who walks thousands of li to Yama's court to beg his dead son back and is told to go and fetch the boy himself; a man who lays on a feast for five hundred monks specifically in order to collect on them as draught animals in his next life; a traveller who takes an entire household's equanimity for coldness; four brothers who each pick a hiding place from death; and the framing and murder of Sundarī in the Jeta Grove.

Translation Method

Fascicle 40 collects the 梵志 — the brahmins, or more precisely those who follow the brahmanical religious life. The compendium treats them with notably more range than it treats the heterodox teachers of fascicle 39. Some are rivals and one group are murderers, but several are the Buddha himself in former lives, one becomes an arhat, one becomes a solitary buddha, and one whole household of them is held up as understanding impermanence better than the man who came to learn from them.

Entry 1 is a doublet of fascicle 35's entry 10: both narrate the Dīpaṃkara prophecy, from different sources and different points of view, and neither refers to the other. We print both and set the relation out in the reading note.

On names: Dīpaṃkara, Māgandiya, Gopī, Ānanda, Śakra, Brahmā, Yama, Sumeru, Jambudvīpa, Śrāvastī, Rājagṛha, and the Jetavana are given in their standard forms, as is Uttara in entry 3. Sundarī in entry 12 rests on the narrative rather than the transliteration and is discussed in its reading note. Where no confident correspondence exists — Bōmó, Yēruòdá, Xūxiàng, Jūyí, Wéiyán — we keep the Chinese in pinyin. Names transparently descriptive in Chinese (Thunder-Cloud, Surpassing-Arts, Good-Flavor, Jewel-Ocean, Jié the Greedy, Great Victor, Honey-Complete, Lotus) are translated, with the Chinese kept in the contents list.

Five entries carry verse — the six-verse exchange with Dīpaṃkara in entry 1, the king's couplet in entry 3, the footprint verse and the Buddha's refusal in entry 4, the long verse on impermanence in entry 11, and the verse recited through the lanes in entry 12. We set these off in italics with slashes for the line breaks of the Chinese; the Chinese column keeps them running on within the paragraph, as the source has them.

Entry 4's closing verse and entry 12's account of a woman groomed and murdered to manufacture a scandal are translated as they stand, without softening, and commented on in the reading notes.

1. The Teacher Surpassing-Arts Asks Dīpaṃkara Buddha for a Prediction

The Dīpaṃkara prophecy told at length: a brahmin student who out-recites eighty-four thousand of his elders, buys five lotus stalks from a flower-girl who asks to be his wife in every future life, and lays his hair down in the mud.

Chinese Source

過去定光眾祐出現。鉢摩大國。與四部之眾不可稱計。皆來供養給其所須。時有梵志。名耶若達。在雪山側。看諸祕讖天文地理靡不貫綜。書疏文字亦悉了知。耶若達梵志有弟子。名曰雷雲。聰明博見靡事不通。恒為耶若達所見愛敬不去須臾。雷雲梵志念言。我今應學者悉皆已備。宜報師恩。然復貧弊。宜當往詣國界求所須者。師念。我之所愛。設吾死者尚不能別。何況今日欲捨吾去。作五百言與之令誦。未經幾日悉皆流利。是時耶若達婆羅門。告五百弟子曰。此雷雲梵志伎術悉通。即以立名。名曰超術。超術梵志復經數日。又白師曰。所學已知。然書籍所載諸有學術過者當報師恩。師曰。汝知是知。超術作禮而去。爾時鉢摩大國。去城不遠有眾梵志。並集一家欲共大祠。并欲論議。有八萬四千梵志共集。第一上座諷誦書疏莫不能知。以五百兩金及金杖金澡罐各一牧牛千頭。用奉上師。與第一上座先試其術。過者便與。超術梵志自念。我今何故家家乞求。不如詣彼大眾共捔伎術。超術往。眾多梵志遙各喚曰。善哉祠主今獲大利。乃使梵天躬自下降。時八萬四千諸梵志等。各起共迎。異口同音而作是語。善來大梵神天。超術梵志曰。吾非梵天。止止諸賢。汝等豈不聞。雪山北有大梵志眾師。名耶若達。我其弟子。名曰超術。便向上座曰。設知伎術者向吾說之。爾時彼眾第一上座。即誦三藏伎術無有漏失。時超術語上座曰。一句五百言。今可說之。上座曰。我不解也。超術便誦三藏及一句五百言大人之相。爾時八萬四千梵志。歎未曾有甚奇甚特。我初不聞大人之相。今尊宜在上座。超術移坐上座便在第一。時彼眾上座極懷瞋恚。發此誓願。今此人奪我坐處。此人所生之處所欲作事。我恒當壞敗其功。時彼施主即依法施上座。告主人曰。我今還以金錢銀錢以相施。吾但各取五百枚。以金罐用供養女人。及牛千頭還施主人。吾不習欲亦不積財。已便往詣鉢摩大國。其王名曰光明。於城東門見五百梵志耆年宿德。時彼國王請定光如來日曝火炙。即以五百金錢各與一文及比丘眾。衣食供養掃灑令淨。勿使作倡有土沙穢惡。懸繒幡蓋香汁塗地。伎樂不可稱計。見一女人。名曰善味。持七莖華以瓶取水。即問此女。吾欲買華。女言。持用上佛。即以銀錢五百請五莖華。女貪銀錢與五莖華。行數十步女自念言。此人形貌端正不惜銀錢。追呼男子問用何為。答曰上佛。女曰。請二莖華并以上佛。願我後生常為夫妻。超術曰。菩薩無所愛惜。設為我妻必壞我心。梵志女曰。我終不相壞。正使持我施與。終必忍行。定光佛曰。菩薩所行無所愛惜。梵志偈答。父母諸佛尊　日月周行世不可持施人　餘者決無難又說偈曰。當忍億劫苦　施頭身耳目妻子國財寶　車馬僕從人摩納。復以偈答。大山熾如火　億劫堪頂戴不能壞道意　唯願時授決即解髮布地。而說偈言。破愛憍慢心　能滅欲怒癡第一光相足　惟佛照我心昔我所求願　今日得見佛今散五莖花　願得不退轉餘二非我華　王女寄上佛無上大導師　見愍蹈我髮佛又偈答。摩納發大心　曠濟無量數弘誓不自為　殖眾功德本却後無數劫　五鼎五濁世成佛度眾生　號字釋迦文光明三十二　奇特人中尊佛以脚足蹈髮而過。又以神力接五莖華。及得我身在虛空中。餘有二華住佛兩肩。自從破梵志祠天帝火以後。行正見用平等法。不復墮三塗及在八難。時超術我是。賣花女瞿夷是也(出修行本起經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the past the Blessed One Dīpaṃkara appeared in the great kingdom of Bōmó, with a fourfold assembly beyond counting, all of whom came to make offerings and supply what was needed.

At that time there was a brahmin named Yēruòdá, living on the flank of the Snow Mountains, who had studied all the secret prognostications and there was nothing in the astronomy of the heavens or the geography of the earth he had not mastered, and he knew writings and letters thoroughly as well.

The brahmin Yēruòdá had a disciple named Thunder-Cloud, intelligent and broadly informed, who understood everything and was always beloved and honored by Yēruòdá and never left his side for a moment.

The brahmin Thunder-Cloud thought: everything I ought to learn I now have in full, and I should repay my teacher’s kindness; but I am poor and ought to go into the country and seek what is needed.

His teacher thought: this is my beloved, and if I were dying I could not bear to part from him — how much less now, when he means to leave me. And he composed five hundred words and gave them to him to recite, and within a few days he had them all fluently.

Then the brahmin Yēruòdá told his five hundred disciples: this brahmin Thunder-Cloud has mastered every art. And he gave him a new name on the spot: Surpassing-Arts.

Some days later the brahmin Surpassing-Arts said to his teacher again: I know what I have learned; and the books record that whoever surpasses in learning and arts should repay his teacher’s kindness. His teacher said: what you know, you know. Surpassing-Arts bowed and departed.

At that time, not far from the city in the great kingdom of Bōmó, a crowd of brahmins had gathered at one house to hold a great sacrifice and to debate together, and eighty-four thousand brahmins had assembled. The chief among them recited the writings and there was nothing he did not know; and five hundred taels of gold, a gold staff, a gold water-vessel, and a thousand head of cattle were to be offered to the teacher — the chief of them was to be tested in his arts first, and whoever passed would receive them.

The brahmin Surpassing-Arts thought: why should I go begging from house to house? Better to go to that great assembly and contest arts with them. So Surpassing-Arts went; and the brahmins from afar all called out: excellent, master of the sacrifice — today you have obtained a great gain, for you have made Brahmā himself come down. And the eighty-four thousand brahmins all rose to receive him, and with one voice said: welcome, great divine Brahmā.

The brahmin Surpassing-Arts said: I am not Brahmā. Stop, worthy sirs. Have you not heard that north of the Snow Mountains there is a great teacher of a company of brahmins named Yēruòdá? I am his disciple, named Surpassing-Arts. And he said to the chief: if you know the arts, expound them to me.

Then the chief of that assembly recited the three collections of the arts without a single omission. Then Surpassing-Arts said to the chief: there is a passage of five hundred words — now expound it. The chief said: I do not understand it. Surpassing-Arts then recited the three collections and the passage of five hundred words on the marks of a great man.

Then the eighty-four thousand brahmins exclaimed at the marvel: how extraordinary, how remarkable. We had never heard of the marks of a great man. You, sir, should take the chief seat. Surpassing-Arts moved to the chief seat and took the first place.

Then the chief of that assembly was filled with anger and made this vow: this man has taken my seat. In whatever place he is born and whatever he wishes to do, I shall always ruin and defeat his work.

Then the donor gave the offering to the chief according to the rule, and he told the host: I now give back the gold coins and silver coins; I shall take only five hundred pieces of each. The gold vessel let be used to provide for the woman, and the thousand head of cattle go back to the host. I do not cultivate desire and do not accumulate wealth.

With that he went on to the great kingdom of Bōmó, whose king was named Light. At the eastern gate of the city he saw five hundred brahmins, aged and venerable, whom the king had set to be scorched in the sun and burnt by fire for having invited the Tathāgata Dīpaṃkara; and he gave each of them, and the community of monks, five hundred gold coins, and clothing and food, and had the place swept clean, allowing no music, no dust or sand or filth, and silk banners and canopies hung and the ground smeared with fragrant liquid, and music beyond counting.

He saw a woman named Good-Flavor, holding seven flower stalks and drawing water with a jar, and he asked her: I wish to buy flowers. The woman said: they are to be offered to the Buddha. He offered five hundred silver coins for five of the stalks, and the woman, coveting the silver, gave him five.

When he had gone a few dozen paces the woman thought: this man is handsome in form and does not begrudge silver — and she ran after him and called to him and asked what he wanted them for. He answered: to offer to the Buddha. The woman said: take these two stalks as well and offer them to the Buddha with the rest, and may I in a later life always be your wife.

Surpassing-Arts said: a bodhisattva begrudges nothing at all. If you become my wife, you will certainly ruin my resolve. The brahmin woman said: I shall never ruin it. Even if you give me away to another, I shall in the end bear it.

The Buddha Dīpaṃkara said: what a bodhisattva practices, he begrudges nothing. The brahmin answered in verse:

“Parents, and the honored buddhas, / and the sun and moon that go about the world — / these may not be given away to others; / for the rest there is no difficulty at all.”

And he spoke another verse:

“I shall bear a hundred million kalpas of suffering, / giving head, body, ears, and eyes, / wife and children, kingdom and treasure, / carriages, horses, and attendants.”

The young brahmin answered again in verse:

“Were a great mountain to blaze like fire, / I could bear it on my head for a hundred million kalpas / and it could not ruin my resolve for the path. / I ask only that you now give me the prediction.”

And he loosened his hair and spread it on the ground and spoke this verse:

“Breaking the mind of craving and arrogance, / able to extinguish desire, anger, and delusion — / first among lights, complete in marks, / I ask only that the Buddha illumine my mind. / What I sought and vowed of old, / today I have obtained in seeing the Buddha. / Now I scatter these five stalks of flowers, / and pray to obtain non-retrogression. / The other two are not my flowers; / a king’s daughter has entrusted them to be offered to the Buddha. / Unsurpassed great guide, / take pity and tread upon my hair.”

The Buddha answered in verse:

“The young brahmin has raised the great mind, / to save measureless numbers far and wide; / his vast vow is not for himself, / and he plants the roots of every merit. / After countless kalpas from now, / in a world of five cauldrons and five turbidities, / he shall become a buddha and deliver beings, / and be named Śākyamuni. / His light has thirty-two marks, / a rare and honored one among men.”

The Buddha trod upon his hair with his feet and passed by, and by his divine power took up the five stalks of flowers, and my own body was in the air; and the remaining two flowers stayed at the Buddha’s two shoulders. From the time he broke the brahmins’ sacrificial fire to heaven, he practiced right views and used the Dharma of equality, and no longer fell into the three evil destinies or into the eight difficulties. The Surpassing-Arts of that time is myself; the flower-selling woman is Gopī (Source: Sūtra of the Origins of Practice).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the Dīpaṃkara prediction — the scene in which the future Śākyamuni spreads his hair over the mud — and it is a doublet of fascicle 35's entry 10, which tells the same event from the point of view of a bystander who thought the gesture undignified and spent an asaṃkhya kalpa as a fish for saying so. There the young brahmin is called Chāoshù; here the same name is given as Surpassing-Arts, with his earlier name Thunder-Cloud and the account of how he came by the new one. The two entries come from different sources and neither refers to the other.

The most interesting thing here is the sub-plot that has nothing to do with the prophecy. The chief brahmin whom Surpassing-Arts displaces at the assembly makes a vow of his own, and it is given the same formal weight as the bodhisattva's: in whatever place he is born and whatever he wishes to do, I shall always ruin and defeat his work. The entry does not say who this becomes, and does not return to him.

Note too what the flower-girl asks for and what she is told. Her price for the two extra stalks is to be his wife in every future life; his objection is not that he does not want a wife but that a wife will ruin his resolve; and her answer is that she will bear even being given away. She is identified at the end as Gopī.

2. The Brahmin Jewel-Ocean Recounts His Dream

A brahmin who has already brought a hundred thousand million beings to the mind of enlightenment asks for a dream to show him what the wheel-turning king and the princes are aiming at, and gets one.

Chinese Source

有寶海梵志。白佛。願如來及比丘僧。滿七歲中受我四事供養。如來許可。梵志後時作如是念。我今已令百千億那由他眾生發菩提心。然我不知轉輪聖王所願何等為願。人王天王聲聞緣覺。為求無上菩提。若我來世必成佛道。我於夜臥當有諸天魔梵諸佛世尊為我現夢。於睡眠中見有光明。即見十方恒沙世界諸佛世尊。各各遙授蓮華與之。其花微妙各於華臺見日輪像。於日輪上有七寶蓋。一一日輪各出六十億光。皆悉來入梵志口中。自見其光滿千由旬。淨如明鏡見其腹內。六十億那由他百千菩薩。在蓮華上結跏趺坐三昧正受。復見日鬚圍繞其身。於諸華中出諸伎樂。又見其王。血出污身四方馳走。面首似猪噉種種蟲。坐伊蘭樹下。有無量眾生來食其身。唯餘骨瑣。數數受身亦復如是。復見諸王子。或作猪象或水牛師子狐狼虎豹獼猴等面。以血污身皆噉眾生。坐伊蘭樹下。復有無量眾生。來食其身。骨瑣已離。數數受身亦復如是。或見王子。須曼那花以作瓔珞。載小弊車駕以水牛。從不正道南向馳走。復見四天大王釋提桓因大梵天王來至其所。告梵志言。汝今四邊所有蓮華。應先取一華與轉輪王。一一王子各與一華。其餘諸花與諸小王。次與汝子。并及餘人即皆賦之。忽然而寤。憶念夢中所見諸事。得知聖王所願卑下愛樂生死。復知諸王子發心。求聲聞乘者故我夢見須曼那花以作瓔珞。載水牛車於不正道南向馳走。我何緣故夢見於此。諸佛世尊。以我先教勸閻浮提內無量眾生。悉令安住三福處故發菩提心。請佛及僧。滿足七歲奉諸所安耳(出寶梵志請如來經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a brahmin named Jewel-Ocean who said to the Buddha: I ask the Tathāgata and the community of monks to accept my offerings of the four requisites for a full seven years. And the Tathāgata assented.

Afterward the brahmin thought: I have now caused a hundred thousand million nayutas of beings to give rise to the mind of enlightenment; but I do not know what the wheel-turning sage-king vows for — whether for human kingship, heavenly kingship, the hearer’s vehicle or the solitary buddha’s, or whether he seeks unsurpassed enlightenment. If I am to attain the buddha-path in a life to come, then while I sleep tonight let the gods, Māra, Brahmā, and the buddhas and world-honored ones show me a dream.

In his sleep he saw a light, and saw the buddhas and world-honored ones of world-systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges in the ten directions, each of whom conferred a lotus flower on him from afar. The flowers were exquisite, and on the calyx of each he saw the image of a sun-disc, and above each sun-disc a canopy of the seven treasures; and each sun-disc sent out six billion rays, all of which came and entered the brahmin’s mouth. He saw his own light fill a thousand yojanas, and, clear as a bright mirror, he saw within his own belly six hundred million nayutas of hundreds of thousands of bodhisattvas seated cross-legged on lotus flowers in the rightly received samādhi. And he saw sun-filaments encircling his body, and from the flowers there came music of every kind.

He also saw that king, blood pouring from him and staining his body, running about in all four directions, his face like a pig’s and devouring worms of every kind, and sitting beneath an eraṇḍa tree, with measureless beings coming to eat his body until only the linked bones were left; and receiving a body again and again in the same way.

He also saw the princes with the faces of pigs, elephants, water-buffalo, lions, foxes, wolves, tigers, leopards, monkeys, and the rest, their bodies stained with blood and all devouring beings, sitting beneath eraṇḍa trees, with measureless beings coming to eat their bodies until the linked bones came apart; and receiving a body again and again in the same way.

He also saw a prince wearing sumanā flowers as a necklace, riding in a small broken cart drawn by a water-buffalo, running southward along a road that was not the right one.

He also saw the Four Great Heavenly Kings, Śakra, and the great Brahmā king come to him and say to the brahmin: of all the lotus flowers on your four sides, you should first take one flower and give it to the wheel-turning king, and give one flower to each of the princes, and the remaining flowers to the lesser kings, and then to your own sons and to the rest. And he distributed them all — and suddenly awoke.

Recalling all the things he had seen in the dream, he came to know that the sage-king’s vow was lowly and that he loved and delighted in birth and death; and he came to know the resolve the princes had raised, that they sought the hearers’ vehicle — for which reason he had dreamed of sumanā flowers worn as a necklace and of a water-buffalo cart running southward on a road that was not the right one.

And why did I dream of these things? Because the buddhas and world-honored ones, seeing that I had earlier taught and exhorted measureless beings within Jambudvīpa and settled them all in the three grounds of merit, made them give rise to the mind of enlightenment, and invited the Buddha and the saṅgha and provided them with all they needed for a full seven years (Source: Sūtra of the Brahmin Jewel Inviting the Tathāgata).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The dream is the whole of this entry and it is worth reading as a piece of composition. The brahmin asks a precise question — what are the wheel-turning king and the princes actually aiming at? — and what he is shown is not an answer in words but a set of images he then has to read: sun-discs entering his mouth, his own belly transparent and full of seated bodhisattvas, and then the king with a pig's face eating worms beneath an eraṇḍa tree while beings eat him down to the linked bones, over and over.

His reading of it is given at the end and is entirely specific. From the prince wearing sumanā flowers and riding a broken buffalo-cart southward on a wrong road, he concludes that those princes are aiming at the hearers' vehicle; from the king's condition, that the sage-king's vow is lowly and that he loves birth and death. The dream is diagnostic, and what it diagnoses is other people's aspirations.

3. The Brahmin Xūxiàng Has His Grief Released by the Buddha

Ru-Yi Translation

In the kingdom of Śrāvastī there was a brahmin who had a great deal of land planted in the Jeta Grove area, and he went to look at it and was glad, thinking his wish fulfilled. That very night the sky rained great hail and the crops all died; and his daughter died as well, and in his grief and vexation he wept and nothing could stop him.

Then the monks went into the city for alms and saw both these things, and came back and told the Buddha in full. Xūxiàng the brahmin came in person to where the Buddha was and bowed his head and face.

The Buddha knew his thought and said to the brahmin: in the world there are five things that cannot be escaped. First, what must be diminished; second, what must be lost; third, what must fall sick and waste; fourth, what must grow old and decay; fifth, what must die. Only the truth of the path gets free of them — giving and keeping the precepts.

The brahmin’s mind softened and he saw the four truths, and from that day took refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha and became a lay follower of pure faith.

The monks said to the Buddha: World-Honored One, you have released the brahmin’s mind and he goes away smiling. The Buddha said: this is not the only time I have released his grief.

In the past, in the land of Jambudvīpa, there were five rulers. One of them was named Jié the Greedy, who governed his kingdom unjustly. His ministers and people brought out troops and drew their blades and came all before the king and said to him: had you not better know it yourself — you have been greedy and have harmed the myriad people. Get out of the kingdom quickly, or we shall kill you.

The king, greatly trembling, went out of the kingdom with carriages and horsemen, and wove rush mats himself and sold them to keep himself alive; and the ministers and people set up the king’s younger brother as king, who governed without wronging the people.

The former king sent up a letter setting out his case, and begged of the king one hamlet by which he might keep himself, and the king gave it to him. Having got one hamlet he governed it well, and then begged for two hamlets, and so on up to half the kingdom, and the king gave him all of it.

After a long time of this, Jié the Greedy conceived a thought and raised the troops of half the kingdom to attack his brother, and, winning at once, got back his former kingdom. Then he raised the troops of one kingdom to attack the second, and doubling his forces in turn attacked the fifth kingdom, and won every time; and the four seas all belonged to him, and he changed his title to Great Victor.

Śakra tested him, to see whether he knew what was enough. He transformed himself into a young brahmin boy of the Jūyí clan, hair unbound, with a golden staff and a golden vessel, and stood at the palace gate and was announced to the king. The king came forward and sat with him, and when the courtesies were done he said to the king: I have come from the seashore, where I saw a great kingdom, its people flourishing and having many treasures; you might go and attack it. I do not know whether you have enough already, or would like this too. The king said: I would like it.

The heavenly king said to him: you may fit out ships and raise troops and wait; in seven days’ time I shall take the king there. And having said so he vanished.

When the day came the king raised troops and fitted out the ships, and the brahmin did not come, and he was grieved and unhappy. Then the people of a whole kingdom turned about and faced the king: when the king wept they wept, and when the king grieved they grieved. And the king conceived this thought:

“Increasing thought follows desire; / having it already, one wishes for more. / To grow greater by the day is taken for joy — / from getting, one is at one’s ease.”

Whoever could explain it was to be rewarded with a thousand gold coins. There was a youth named Uttara who said he could explain it, and asked for seven days to come and answer. On the seventh day he said to his mother: I mean to release the king’s grief. His mother said to her son: stop, my child, do not go; the affairs of kings are difficult. Her son said: I have the strength to master it.

He came to the king and said: I have come now to answer the meaning — and spoke a verse. And King Great Victor presented him with a thousand gold coins. Great Victor is Xūxiàng the brahmin; and the youth Uttara of that time is myself (Source: Arthapada Sūtra, first fascicle).

4. The Brahmin Māgandiya Offers His Daughter to the Buddha as a Wife

5. A Brahmin Who Lost His Son Begs Yama for His Life; the Son Will Not Own Him, and He Goes to the Buddha and Attains the Path

A father walks several thousand li to find Yama's court and beg his seven-year-old's life back, and is told to go and fetch the boy from the eastern garden himself. The boy tells him to go away.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a brahmin who went forth to study young, and at sixty had not been able to attain the path; and by rule he ought to go home. He took a wife and became a householder, and had a son, handsome and lovable; and at seven years old the boy suddenly took a grave illness and died in a night.

The brahmin, in his grief, lay over the corpse and fainted and revived again; and his relatives reasoned with him and by force took the body away and coffined and buried it outside the city.

The brahmin thought: my weeping now is of no use at all. Better to go to King Yama’s place and beg for my son’s life. He bathed and kept the fast and took flowers and incense and set out from his house, and asked people everywhere: where does King Yama hold his court?

Going on several thousand li he came into the deep mountains, and saw brahmins there and asked as before. They answered: you ask where King Yama holds his court — what do you seek? He answered: I had a son who died a few days ago, and I wish to go to King Yama’s place and beg for my son’s life.

The brahmins pitied his folly and told him: King Yama’s court is not a place living men can reach; but I shall show you a way. Go west from here some four hundred li, and there is a great river with a city in it. This is the city where the heavenly spirits rest when they patrol the world, and King Yama always passes through it on the fourth day of the month when he patrols. Keep the fast and go there and you will certainly see him.

He went off in delight, and coming into that river valley saw fine city walls, palaces, and buildings, like the Trāyastriṃśa heaven. The brahmin came to the gate and burned incense and stood on one leg and made his prayer, asking for his son. King Yama ordered that he be brought in, and the brahmin said: late in life I had a son, meaning to provide for my old age; I raised him seven years and he died a few days ago. I ask only that the great king bestow his kindness and give me back my son’s life.

The king said: very good. Your son is now playing in the eastern garden; go and fetch him yourself.

So he went and saw his son playing with other small boys, and embraced him and wept, saying: day and night I have thought of you and neither food nor sleep is sweet to me. Do you not think of your father and mother’s bitter toil?

The small boy shouted him off and scolded him: foolish and doddering old man, who does not understand how things are. To lodge with someone a little while is called being their son. Do not talk so much nonsense; you had better go quickly. I have my own father and mother here.

The brahmin, disconsolate, wept and went away, and thought to himself: I have heard that the śramaṇa Gautama knows the way of the transformations of people’s souls; I shall go and ask him.

So he came back to the Buddha, bowed his head and made obeisance, and set the whole matter out before him: he is truly my son and he will not come back, and calls me a doddering old fool, and says that to lodge a little while is called calling another one’s son, and that there is no feeling of father and son at all. Why should this be?

The Buddha told the brahmin: you are truly foolish. When a person dies the spirit goes and takes on another form; parents, wife, and children dwell together by causes and conditions, like guests lodging together who separate when they rise. The brahmin heard it and his mind opened at a stroke, and he bowed his head and offered himself and asked to be a śramaṇa. The Buddha said: excellent. His hair and beard fell away of themselves and the Dharma robe was on his body, and he became a monk at once, and on that seat attained arhatship (Source: Dharmapada, “Practice of the Path” chapter, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The exchange in Yama's eastern garden is one of the coldest scenes in the compendium and the entry turns entirely on it. The father has bathed, fasted, walked thousands of li, been given directions by strangers, prayed standing on one leg at the gate, and been granted his request without argument — Yama simply tells him to go and fetch the boy himself. And the boy, playing with the other children, shouts him off: to lodge with someone a little while is called being their son; do not talk so much nonsense; I have my own father and mother here.

The Buddha's explanation adds nothing the boy had not already said, and the text is honest about that. What changes is not the information but who is saying it.

6. A Brahmin Gives to Monks with an Ulterior Motive, and One Verse Can Digest It

Ru-Yi Translation

So he made a secret plan: he would lay on a meal and collect on it in a later life, and make them work for his living. 7. A Brahmin Offers the Buddha a Bowl of Honey; the Assembly Eats and It Is Not Diminished, and It Is Given to the Creatures in the Water

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a brahmin who had made it a rule never to see the Buddha, and slipped into another’s house. The great sage took pity on him and appeared before his eyes; and, wishing to avoid him, he ran and could not get away, and came to where the Buddha was. Then the World-Honored One taught him the sūtra Dharma, and joy arose in his heart; and he took refuge in the Dharma and the Saṅgha and received the precepts and circled the Buddha and went away.

He then took a bowl and filled it with honey and lifted it in both hands, wishing to offer it to the Buddha. The Buddha told the monks: take this bowl of honey and distribute it to the community. And that one bowl of honey satisfied the Buddha and the whole community, and the bowl was as full as before.

He offered it to the Buddha again, and the Buddha told the brahmin: put it into the great water, the measureless current. The brahmin asked why. The Buddha said: so that the worms, snails, soft-shelled turtles, alligators, fish, and hard-shelled turtles in the water may all get the taste of it. And the brahmin did as he was taught.

The World-Honored One smiled with pleasure and sent out a light of five colors that rose to the Brahmā heaven and shone over the five destinies, and came back and circled his body three times. When a prediction is given to a bodhisattva the light enters at the crown; to a solitary buddha, at the mouth; to a hearer, at the arm; when heavenly merit is spoken of, at the navel; when a human birth is spoken of, at the knee; when the hells, hungry ghosts, and animals are spoken of, at the feet.

Ānanda rose from his seat and with joined palms said to the Buddha: the Buddha does not smile idly; a smile always has a meaning. The Buddha told Ānanda: did you see the brahmin offering honey to the Buddha? He answered: yes. This brahmin, in the ages to come, shall for twenty kalpas not fall into an evil destiny, and after twenty kalpas shall become a solitary buddha named Honey-Complete.

The monks said: this brahmin, with one bowl of honey, has benefited many and obtains the state of a solitary buddha. The Buddha told the monks: it is not only in this life that this brahmin has done so; in a former life it was the same.

In the past there was a brahmin dwelling quietly in seclusion who saw a divine sage; and someone said: this sage is excellent, auspicious, and hard to come near; you should go and receive from him. And another said: what use is it to see this fellow who only feeds his own body? There was a sage of the five powers who saw what was in their minds, and rose from among the trees into the air and stood before that man; and, seeing him, he leapt for joy, and filled a bowl with honey and presented it to him. The sage received it and flew off into the air; and by the virtue of that gift he afterward became a king named Honey-Complete, governed his kingdom by the true Dharma, and when his life ended was born in heaven.

The Buddha told the monks: would you know the sage of the five powers of that time? It was myself. The brahmin of that time is this brahmin now. Because he gave honey then and received the merit of gods and men, he now again gives to the Buddha, and afterward reaches the state of a solitary buddha (Source: Sūtra of Honey-Complete).

8. A Brahmin Travelling to Study Meets Five People Without Natural Feeling

A traveller looking for a country of exemplary filial piety finds a man who goes on ploughing while his son lies dead of snakebite, and then four more members of the same household who say the same kind of thing.

Chinese Source

有梵志。在羅悅祇。聞舍衛國人多孝養奉經修道供事三尊。欲得學問即往諮受。去國不遠。見父子二人耕地下種。時毒蛇出齧殺其子。父耕種不輟又不顧視。梵志問之。耕種者又問。何來也。梵志答言。我從羅悅祇來。聞此國人多孝養奉事三寶故來學問。向者是何人子。曰我之子。曰是卿子者何故不憂而反耕種。其人答言。人生有死物成有敗。非愁憂啼哭所可如何。設不飲食何益於死者耶。卿入城者過語我家。吾子已死。無復持二人食具來。梵志竊念。人無反復。兒死在地。靜不愁憂而反索食。入舍衛城詣耕家。見死兒母語言。卿子已死。父言。但持一人食具來。兒母曰。如客來寄止。來不難去不留。此兒如是。生生是自來死亦獨去。非我力所能進退。隨其本行追命所生。又語其姊。姊亦說無常喻。梵志語婦。卿夫已死。婦為梵志說譬喻。又語奴子。大家已死。奴子亦說譬喻。梵志聞之。心感目冥不識東西。聞此國孝養奉事三寶故來學問。未有善意。見五無反復唐勞身心。又問行人佛在何所。答言。近在祇樹給孤獨園。梵志即到佛所。稽首佛足却坐一面。愁憂不樂。低頭默無所說。佛知其意。謂梵志言。何為不樂。梵志答言。違我本願故愁憂耳。佛又問。云何。具以事答。佛言不然。不如卿語。此之五人最有反復。知命無常非愁憂可如。還以自定故無有愁憂。世俗人不識無常。懊惱啼哭不能自割。譬如人身得熱病。不自覺知慌忽妄語。良醫與藥。熱即除愈不復妄語。俗人愁憂愚癡醉熱不能自解。若自曉無常不復愁憂也。如熱得愈。此五人皆可道證。梵志聞語即自剋責。我為愚冥不知大義。今聞佛語。如盲得目冥中見明。即受五戒稽首而退(出五無反復經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a brahmin in Rājagṛha who heard that the people of the kingdom of Śrāvastī were much given to filial support, upheld the sūtras, cultivated the path, and served the Three Honored Ones; and, wishing to gain learning, he went at once to receive instruction.

Not far from the kingdom he saw a father and son ploughing and sowing, and a venomous snake came out and bit the son and killed him. The father went on ploughing and sowing without stopping and did not even look round.

The brahmin asked him about it, and the ploughman in turn asked: where do you come from? The brahmin answered: I have come from Rājagṛha. Hearing that the people of this country are much given to filial support and serve the Three Jewels, I have come to learn. Whose son was that just now? He said: my son. He said: if he is your son, why do you not grieve, but go on ploughing and sowing instead?

The man answered: a person born must die; a thing made must decay. There is nothing that grieving and weeping can do about it. And if I do not eat and drink, what good does that do the dead? If you are going into the city, call at my house on the way and tell them my son is dead and not to bring food for two.

The brahmin thought privately: people have no natural feeling. His child lies dead on the ground and he is calm and ungrieving and asks for his dinner instead.

He went into the city of Śrāvastī and came to the ploughman’s house and saw the dead boy’s mother and said to her: your son is dead, and his father says to bring food for one only. The boy’s mother said: it is like a guest who comes and lodges; he does not find it hard to come and does not stay when he goes. This boy was like that. Being born, he came of himself; dying, he goes alone. It is not in my power to advance or hold him back; he follows his own former conduct and pursues the life he is to be born into.

He spoke to the sister as well, and the sister too explained impermanence by simile. The brahmin said to the wife: your husband is dead. And the wife set out a simile for the brahmin. He spoke to the servant boy: your master is dead. And the servant boy set out a simile too.

The brahmin, hearing this, was affected in his heart and his eyes darkened and he did not know east from west: I heard that this country is given to filial support and serves the Three Jewels, and so I came to learn; and there is no goodness in it. Meeting these five without natural feeling, I have wearied body and mind for nothing.

And he asked passers-by where the Buddha was. They answered: nearby, in the Jetavana, Anāthapiṇḍada’s park. The brahmin came at once to where the Buddha was, bowed at the Buddha’s feet and sat to one side, grieved and unhappy, hanging his head and saying nothing.

The Buddha knew his mind and said to the brahmin: why are you unhappy? The brahmin answered: because it has gone against my original wish, and so I grieve. The Buddha asked: how so? — and he told the whole matter.

The Buddha said: it is not so; what you say is not right. These five people have natural feeling in the highest degree. They know that life is impermanent and that grieving can do nothing about it, and so, having settled themselves in it, they have no grief. Worldly people, not knowing impermanence, are distressed and weep and cannot cut it off in themselves. It is as when a person’s body takes a fever and, not aware of itself, raves in delirium; a good physician gives medicine and the fever is cured and the raving stops. Worldly people, grieving in the fever of folly, cannot free themselves; if they understood impermanence of themselves they would no longer grieve — as with the fever cured. All five of those people can be witnesses to the path.

The brahmin, hearing this, reproached himself: I have been foolish and dark and did not know the great meaning. Hearing the Buddha’s words now is like a blind man getting eyes and seeing light in the dark. And he received the five precepts and bowed his head and withdrew (Source: Sūtra of the Five Without Reciprocity).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The title's phrase 無反復 — literally without reciprocity, without the returning of natural feeling — is what the brahmin thinks he is seeing, and the entry is built so that the reader shares his judgment for as long as possible. The father goes on ploughing and asks that dinner be reduced by one portion. The mother, the sister, the wife, and the servant each answer in the same register. It reads as coldness, and the brahmin says so: I have wearied body and mind for nothing.

The Buddha's correction is not that the five were right to feel nothing but that they were not feeling nothing — they know that life is impermanent and that grief can do nothing about it, and having settled themselves in that, they have no grief. The simile he uses puts the ordinary response on the side of illness rather than of feeling: a man in a fever raves and does not know he is raving, and the medicine does not remove his capacity for speech.

The mother's simile is the best of the five and worth quoting on its own terms: it is like a guest who comes and lodges; he does not find it hard to come and does not stay when he goes. This boy was like that.

9. Four Brahmin Brothers Die on the Same Day

Four brothers with the five supernormal powers each choose a hiding place from death — the middle of the sea, the inside of Sumeru, empty space, and a crowded market.

Ru-Yi Translation

Four brahmin brothers each attained the five supernormal powers, and knew that in seven days’ time their lives would all be exhausted. They conferred together: the strength of the five powers turns heaven and earth over, takes hold of the sun and moon, moves mountains and stops rivers, and there is nothing it cannot do. Should it not be able to escape this death that awaits us?

One said: I shall go into the great sea, and not come up to the surface or go down to the bottom, but hold myself just in the middle; and how shall the death-demon of impermanence know where I am? One said: I shall go into Mount Sumeru and close its surface behind me so that no edge shows; and how shall the death-demon of impermanence know where I am? One said: I shall raise myself lightly and hide in empty space; and how shall the death-demon of impermanence know where I am? One said: I shall hide myself in the middle of the great market; the death-demon of impermanence has only to get one person — why should it look for me?

The four having settled it, they went together to take leave of the king: our allotted lifespan has seven days left, and we now mean to flee for our lives. If we get free, we shall come back and pay our respects. We ask you only to advance in virtue. And with that they parted.

When the seven days were up, each of them died, like fruit falling when ripe. The market inspector reported to the king that a brahmin had suddenly died in the market. Then the king understood: of the four men fleeing what awaited them, one is already dead; how should the other three alone escape?

The king had his carriage made ready and went to where the Buddha was, made obeisance, sat to one side and said: recently there were four brahmin brothers, each of whom obtained the five powers and, knowing their lives were exhausted, all went to escape it. I do not know whether they can get free.

The Buddha told the great king: there are four things a person cannot be parted from. First, being in the intermediate state, one cannot but take birth. Second, being born, one cannot but grow old. Third, growing old, one cannot but fall sick. Fourth, falling sick, one cannot but die (Source: Dharmapada, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A four-part joke with a fifth beat. Each brother picks a hiding place matched to one of the powers — the sea for the one who can hold himself suspended, the inside of Sumeru for the one who can move mountains, empty space for the one who can fly, and the market for the one who reasons that death only needs to find somebody. The last is the cleverest and it is also the one the king hears about, because a brahmin dropping dead in a crowded market is a matter for the market inspector.

The Buddha's answer does not engage with the powers at all, and it is a list rather than an argument: from the intermediate state one cannot but be born, from birth one cannot but grow old, from age one cannot but fall sick, from sickness one cannot but die. Nothing in it is about hiding.

10. A Brahmin Abandons His Beautiful Wife in a Tree Out of Attachment to a Low Servant, and Later Regrets It in Vain

A husband who prefers a servant to his wife strands the wife up a tree behind a barricade of thorns. Years later, not knowing who she has become, he comes to court to show off his skill at board games.

There was a lay follower of pure faith whose wife was beautiful and of unmatched dignity and virtue, articulate in speech and respected by all; and her husband did not value her and did not even like the sight of her, and loved a servant instead.

His wife, seeing her husband’s mind, said to him: you do not like me. I ask leave to go forth. Her husband gave her leave, and she went forth at once as a nun; and, applying herself diligently day and night, she practiced the path and before long attained arhatship.

Afterward the woman the lay follower had valued died, and the lay follower called the nun to come back; and the nun would not at all. The nun told the Buddha and set out the whole matter.

The Buddha said: this lay follower has already, in a former life, insulted and humiliated this virtuous person. This woman has an exceptional resolve, and this man has always been the one who ruined and disturbed it; and now she has entered upon the great road, he wishes to ruin it again, and he cannot.

The Buddha said: in a past age there was a brahmin’s wife named Lotus, upright and beyond compare in the world, and the brahmin did not like her, and what he loved was a maidservant. Taking the maid’s advice, he abandoned his wife in the mountains: he climbed an udumbara tree and picked out the ripe fruit and threw the unripe down to his wife.

His wife asked: sir, you eat the ripe fruit yourself and throw the unripe down to another? Her husband said: you may come up the tree. So his wife climbed the tree, and her husband took thorns and briars and blocked the tree all round, meaning that she should die and not get down.

At that time the king of the country was out hunting, and, seeing the woman’s beauty, asked her: who are you? And she set out the whole matter. The king thought: this brahmin is a foolish dolt without understanding, and no man at all. And he cleared away the thorns and briars and took her back in a carriage into the palace and set her up as queen.

She had many skills and her wisdom and eloquence were unmatched, and she was good at board games besides; and women came from near and far to play against her, and the queen always won and there was no one who could match her.

The brahmin was good at the board, and hearing from afar that the queen won, he thought: this is my former wife and no other. And he meant to go to the king and display his skill. The queen heard of a brahmin and knew from his appearance that this was her former husband, and told the king and had him presented; and from a distance she tried him at the board, and the queen answered in verse.

The brahmin’s heart was filled with shame and resentment, and he reproached himself, and his regret was of no use at all. The brahmin of that time is this lay follower now; the wife of that time is this wife now; and the king of that country is myself (Source: Sūtra of Husband and Wife).

11. A Brahmin Couple Lose Their Son Picking Flowers, and the Buddha Tells Them What Went Before

A twenty-year-old picks a flower for his bride of six days, reaches for a second, and the branch breaks. The Buddha explains why he is being wept over in three places at once.

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a brahmin of wealth beyond number who had just one son, twenty years old, newly married and not yet seven days wed. In the third month of spring the husband and wife went together to the rear garden to amuse themselves; and there was a tall and fine mango tree in flower, and the wife wanted some and there was no one to get it for her. Her husband, knowing her wish, climbed the tree; and just as he had picked one flower he wanted a second, and the branch he had climbed onto broke and he fell to the ground and died.

Great and small ran in confusion to where the son was, and cried out to heaven and wept and fainted and revived; and the clan, beyond counting, all grieved together. Those who heard of it were pained at heart and those who saw it were sorrowful. His father and mother blamed heaven for not protecting him; and when the coffining was over they went home and wept and could not stop themselves.

Then the World-Honored One, in pity for their folly, went to ask after them; and great and small, seeing the Buddha, were moved to grief and set out their bitterness in full.

The Buddha said to the elder: rest and hear the Dharma. The ten thousand things are impermanent and cannot be kept long; where there is birth there is death, and offense and merit pursue one another. In three places this son is being wept over, and the anguish and prostration are hard to bear in each. Whose son is he in the end, and which are his kin? And the World-Honored One then spoke this verse:

“Life is like flowers and fruit ripening, / always in fear of falling; / all that is born has suffering — / who can bring it about that they do not die? / From the first, delighting in craving and desire, / going down into lust, entering the fragile shadow; / receiving a form, life is like lightning, / flowing day and night, hard to stop. / This body is a thing for death; / the spirit is a formless dharma. / It causes what dies to be born again, / and offense and merit do not fail or stop. / Beginning and end are not of one life; / from folly and craving it is long drawn out. / One makes it oneself and receives its suffering and joy; / the body dies and the spirit is not lost.”

The elder heard the verse and his mind opened and he forgot his grief, and knelt at length and said to the Buddha: what offense did this son commit in a former life, that in the prime and beauty of his years he should die untimely? I ask only that you explain the offense of his former conduct.

The Buddha said: long ago there was a small boy who went with a bow and arrows into a spirit-tree grove to play, and three men beside him were looking at a sparrow in the tree. The boy meant to shoot it, and the three men urged him: if you can hit it, the world will call you a fine fellow. The boy, pleased in his mind, drew the bow and shot it, and hit the sparrow and it died; and the three men laughed together and helped him, and went off each in delight.

Passing through birth and death for countless kalpas, they met one another wherever they were and received the retribution together. Of the three men, one has merit and is now in heaven; one was born in the sea as a spontaneously born nāga king; and one is the elder himself today.

This small boy was born in heaven before this and was a god’s son; and when that life ended he came down and became this elder’s son. Falling from the tree, his life was cut off, and he was at once born in the sea as a spontaneously born nāga’s son; and on the very day of his birth the garuḍa king took and ate him. Can the anguish and weeping in three places today be put into words? Because in his former life he helped and delighted in it, these three men are repaid with weeping (Source: Dharmapada Parables Sūtra, fascicle 5).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The father's question is a natural one — what did my son do to deserve dying at twenty? — and the answer is not really about the son. A boy once shot a sparrow in a spirit-grove; three bystanders egged him on and then laughed and went their separate ways. The shooter has been the one dying ever since. The three who laughed are the ones weeping, and the entry names where each of them is standing: one in heaven, one as a nāga king in the sea, and one as the elder himself.

The three places the Buddha refers to are literal. The same being dies as the elder's son, is immediately reborn as a nāga's son in the sea, and is eaten by the garuḍa king on the day of his birth — so he is being wept over in the human world, in heaven, and under the sea at the same moment. The retribution assigned to encouragement and laughter is precisely that: not death, but the weeping.

Brahmins who have lost their patronage to the Buddha groom a young woman to be seen visiting him daily, then murder her and bury her in the Jeta Grove. The Buddha's response is a deadline and a verse.

Chinese Source

舍衛國王大臣人民。奉敬世尊四事供養。棄捨梵志。梵志共議。當求好女殺埋樹間。令瞿曇眾惡名遠聞。遇待我者。即共逼教好首女人言。汝從今以後朝暮詣佛令萬姓見知。小女承教。便殺之埋著樹間。梵志便聚到王宮門。稱怨喚言。我當學中有一女人。獨最端正。華色無雙生死不知處。王謂言曰。常至何所。對言。往瞿曇間。王言。當於彼求。使到祇樹。掘出女屍著於床上。遍巷稱怨言。沙門瞿曇常稱戒德弘普無上。如何私與女人通情。復殺藏之。有何法戒。時諸比丘入城乞食。人民罵言。是曹沙門所犯若此。當得衣食。比丘便持空器出城。還以白佛。佛告比丘。我被是謗不過七日耳。時惟閻女於城中聞。比丘求食悉空器還。到佛所頭面作禮。叉手白佛言。願佛及僧從我家飯七日。佛默然受之。佛告阿難。汝與眾僧入城里巷。說偈言曰。常欺倒邪冥　說作身不犯重冥行當具　必自受憂苦惟閻是時即飯佛僧(出義足經上卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The king, great ministers, and people of the kingdom of Śrāvastī revered the World-Honored One and made him offerings of the four requisites, and abandoned the brahmins. The brahmins conferred: we should find a fine woman and kill her and bury her among the trees, so that the name of Gautama’s company will be widely heard as evil and people will treat us as before.

So together they pressed and instructed a woman named Sundarī: from now on, go morning and evening to the Buddha, and let the myriad people see and know it. The girl followed the instruction — and then they killed her and buried her among the trees.

The brahmins then gathered at the palace gate and cried out their grievance: among our students there was one woman, alone the most beautiful, in the flower of her looks and unmatched, and we do not know whether she is alive or dead or where she is. The king said to them: where did she regularly go? They answered: to Gautama’s place. The king said: you should seek her there.

The messengers went to the Jeta Grove and dug out the woman’s corpse and laid it on a bed, and cried their grievance through all the lanes: the śramaṇa Gautama constantly proclaims the virtue of the precepts, vast and unsurpassed — how then has he had private dealings with a woman, and killed her besides and hidden her away? What Dharma or precept is there in that?

Then the monks went into the city to beg for food, and the people scolded them: these śramaṇas who commit such things — should they get clothing and food? And the monks came out of the city with empty vessels and went back and told the Buddha. The Buddha told the monks: I am under this slander for no more than seven days.

Then a woman named Wéiyán, in the city, heard that the monks seeking food had all come back with empty vessels, and came to the Buddha and bowed her head and face and with joined palms said to the Buddha: I ask the Buddha and the saṅgha to eat at my house for seven days. And the Buddha accepted in silence.

The Buddha told Ānanda: go with the community into the lanes of the city and speak this verse:

“Constantly deceiving, upside down, in the dark of wrongness, / they say they have done it while their own bodies have not offended. / Doing so, they shall have the heavy dark in full / and must certainly receive the grief and suffering themselves.”

And Wéiyán at that time fed the Buddha and the saṅgha (Source: Arthapada Sūtra, first fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the Sundarī affair, one of the incidents the tradition preserves as slanders the Buddha actually underwent. The plot is set out without euphemism: the brahmins, having lost their patronage, select a woman, instruct her to be seen going to the Buddha morning and evening until the whole city knows her face, and then murder her and bury her in the Jeta Grove so that the body will be found there. The compendium reports the woman's death in five characters and does not return to her.

The Buddha's response has two parts and neither is a denial. The first is a deadline — I am under this slander for no more than seven days — and the second is to send Ānanda and the community into the lanes to recite a verse to the effect that those who accuse the innocent will receive the suffering themselves. Meanwhile the monks cannot beg, and it is a laywoman named Wéiyán who feeds the entire order for exactly those seven days. This closes fascicle 40.
