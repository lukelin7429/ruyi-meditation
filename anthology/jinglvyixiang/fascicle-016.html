The Patriarchal Line

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 16 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Arhat Śrāvaka Monks, Part 5" (聲聞無學僧部第五) — fifteen entries tracing the Dharma-treasury's own transmission from Madhyāntika through Śāṇakavāsin to Upagupta, the karmic story of Lośaka, a monk so burdened by past stinginess that he could never once be fed, the boy-arhat Kumāra-Kāśyapa flying a row of water-vessels over a skeptical king's own palace, and Mahinda's conversion of King Devānampiya Tissa — the founding moment of Buddhism in Sri Lanka.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 16 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 16 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 16 continues the compendium's survey of named arhat disciples, opening with the patriarchal succession from Ānanda through Madhyāntika, Śāṇakavāsin, and Upagupta — the very lineage later Chinese Chan tradition would trace its own authority back through — before moving to Lośaka, Kumāra-Kāśyapa, Majjhantika, Mahinda, Pūrṇa, Mahākāla, Praṇidhānapūra, and Sāgata. Two entries on Lośaka sharing a single figure and theme are grouped together; every entry's full text remains intact.

1. Madhyāntika Subdues the Dragon of Kaśmīra

A dragon king unleashes storm, fire, and a hurled mountain against the monk sent to pacify his own kingdom — and finds every one of his own attacks transformed, harmlessly, into flowers falling from the sky.

Chinese Source

末田地羅漢。受阿難付囑法藏。令往罽賓國。先伏彼龍。即入三昧。令其國土六種震動。龍不自安。至末田地所。末田地入慈三昧。龍王興風吹之。袈裟角不動。復起雷電器仗。并捧大山欲相覆壓。即以神力變成天花。便聞空中偈言。
雪山見厲日　鎔銷無有餘
如入慈三昧　峯火成天花(出阿育王經第七卷)

Ru-Yi Translation

The arhat Madhyāntika, entrusted by Ānanda with the Dharma-treasury, was sent to the kingdom of Kaśmīra, where he first had to subdue its own resident dragon. He entered samādhi, causing the whole land to shake six ways, so that the dragon, unsettled, came before him. Madhyāntika entered the samādhi of loving-kindness; the dragon king raised a fierce wind against him, yet not even the corner of his own robe stirred. The dragon then unleashed thunder, lightning, and weapons of every kind, and lifted a great mountain to crush him — but Madhyāntika, through his own supernormal power, transformed it all into heavenly flowers. A voice then spoke from the sky in verse:

"As the snow mountain melts entirely away before the fierce sun, leaving nothing behind,
so, entering the samādhi of loving-kindness, even a mountain's own fire becomes a heavenly flower" (Source: Sūtra of King Aśoka, fascicle 7).

2. Śāṇakavāsin's Monastery

Śāṇakavāsin, on his way to found a monastery already prophesied by the Buddha himself, subdues two brother dragon kings the same way his own predecessor did — turning their storm, fire, and hurled weapons into a rain of blue lotuses.

Chinese Source

舍那婆私受阿難付囑已。往摩偷羅國。於其中路有寺。名貧陀婆那(翻叢林)。舍那婆私住寺一宿。寺有二老比丘。論議說偈。
無犯第一戒　擇法第一聞
是比丘謂是舍那婆私言。舍那婆私語言。汝所說義非我所說。長老。過去世波羅奈有一商主。與五百估客欲入大海。見一辟支佛病。商主親自醫療。病稍得差。商主取舍那衣浣治令軟施辟支佛。辟支受衣作十八變即入涅槃。時商主者我身是也。是故我今值最勝師。令我得道。舍那婆私漸行至摩偷羅國優流漫陀山。有二龍王兄弟與五百小龍。舍那婆私思惟。我不伏之不得教化。即以神力動山。二龍王瞋。起疾風雨。及以出火。舍那婆私入慈三昧。風雨及火不能近身。悉變為花。所謂優鉢羅花等。悉皆墮地。復起雷電及諸器仗。亦以神力變為天花。即時空中而說偈曰。
暴風疾雨　不能為害　雷電器仗
變為天花　譬如雪山　日光所照
悉皆鎔銷　無有遺餘　入慈三昧
火不能燒　器仗毒害　不近其身
於是二龍王。往舍那婆私處白言。聖人欲何所作。答言。我欲於此起寺。汝當聽我。龍答言。不可得也。長老言。世尊已說。我涅槃後於百年中。於大醍醐山最勝處當起寺。名那哆婆哆。龍王復言。世尊已說耶。長老答言。如是。龍王言。若世尊說者我當隨意(出育王經第七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having received Ānanda's own entrustment of the Dharma-treasury, Śāṇakavāsin traveled toward the kingdom of Mathurā. Along the way stood a monastery named Pindavana ("Forest Grove"). Śāṇakavāsin lodged there one night; two elderly monks in the monastery, discussing the Dharma, recited this verse:

"Foremost is to transgress no precept at all; foremost is to discern the Dharma through careful study."

These monks took Śāṇakavāsin for an ordinary visitor, but he told them: what you have just recited is not what I myself would say, elders. Long ago, in Vārāṇasī, a caravan-leader, setting out with five hundred merchants for the sea, came upon a pratyekabuddha who had fallen ill; the caravan-leader personally nursed him, and, as he slowly recovered, took his own soft, freshly washed robe and offered it to him. The pratyekabuddha, receiving the robe, displayed eighteen transformations and entered nirvāṇa on the spot. That caravan-leader was myself — and it is for this very reason that I have now encountered my own most excellent teacher, and thereby attained the path.

Śāṇakavāsin then traveled on to Mount Urumuṇḍa near Mathurā, where two brother dragon kings dwelt with five hundred lesser dragons of their own. Śāṇakavāsin reflected: unless I subdue them, I cannot teach here — and, through his own supernormal power, shook the mountain. The two dragon kings, enraged, raised a violent storm and breathed forth fire. Śāṇakavāsin entered the samādhi of loving-kindness, and neither storm nor fire could come near his own body, transforming instead into flowers — blue lotuses among them — which fell to the ground. The dragons then raised thunder and lightning and every kind of weapon, which Śāṇakavāsin, through his own power, likewise transformed into heavenly flowers. A voice then spoke from the sky in verse:

"Violent storms and fierce rain cannot bring harm; thunder, lightning, and every weapon
transform instead into heavenly flowers — just as the snow mountain, beneath the sun's own light,
melts away entirely, leaving nothing behind. Entering the samādhi of loving-kindness,
fire cannot burn, and weapons and poison cannot come near the body."

The two dragon kings then came before Śāṇakavāsin and asked: sage, what do you intend to do here? He answered: I wish to build a monastery on this very spot — you must allow me to do so. The dragons answered: this cannot be permitted. The elder said: the World-Honored One himself has already foretold that, a hundred years after his own nirvāṇa, a monastery would be built at the finest site upon this great Ghee Mountain, to be named Nadi-Bhadra. The dragon king asked: did the World-Honored One truly say this? The elder answered: he did. The dragon king said: if the World-Honored One himself said so, then I shall consent (Source: Sūtra of King Aśoka, fascicle 7).

3. Upagupta's Ordination and the Defeat of Māra

An incense merchant repeatedly delays giving up his own sons as promised, until the third — deliberately kept neither too skilled nor too unskilled at trade — finally goes forth, converts a mutilated murderess through the very sight of her own ruin, and, once ordained, hangs a garland of rotting corpses around Māra's own neck for daring to interrupt his teaching.

Chinese Source

舍那婆私。於大醍醐山起寺。賣香商主。名曰笈多。舍那婆私以方便力。教化賣香商主。令其精進。時舍那婆私獨入其家。笈多問言。聖人何故獨無弟子。我樂在家受五欲樂。不能出家。若我生兒當隨長老。乃至笈多生兒。名阿波笈多(梁言不正護。)至其長大。舍那婆私往至笈多所。語言。汝先有願。若我生兒當與長老。今已生兒汝當聽其隨我出家。笈多言。我今唯有一兒。若第二兒生。當與長老。乃至第二生。名陀那笈多(翻寶護)復從其求如是不與。第三優波笈多亦從其求。答言。我當作誓令優波笈多治生。若長若退不得出家。不長不退乃聽出家。是時魔王。令摩偷羅國一切人眾。悉買其物令其得利。時優波笈多正自賣香。時舍那婆私長老語言。汝心心法。云何為善云何為惡。答言不知。長老又言。若心心法。與貪瞋癡相應。是名為惡。與不貪不瞋不癡相應。是名為善。是時長老以黑土白土各各為丸。而語之言。若汝黑心起取黑丸。若白心起取白丸。時優波笈多。即依其言。心不生善。乃至此只得一枚白丸。如是漸進。取二分黑丸一分白丸。復更思惟。半黑丸半白丸。次第念善遂不起惡取白丸。時摩偷羅國。有婬女婆娑達(梁言天主與)其有一婢。往優波笈多處買香多得。其主問言。汝於何處得此多香。將不偷耶。婢言。有估客名優波笈多。形色具足言語微妙。以法賣物。其主聞已起婬欲心。復令其婢至優波笈多處。汝當語彼云。我欲與汝共相娛樂。優波笈多答言。相見未是。其時如是不已。猶故不聽。時又召長者子往至其所。商主從北天竺來。將五百匹馬及種種物。至摩偷羅國。至已問摩偷羅國人。此國何處有第一端正女人。國人答言。有一女人。第一端正。名婆娑婆達多。商主言。我今欲以五百銀錢及種種物行至其處。是時婬女。貪其物故殺長者子。取其身骸置不淨處。與子共相娛樂。是長者子親善知識。於不淨處覓得身骸。往白國王。國王語言。汝可取彼婆娑婆達多。截其手脚及以耳鼻散置野外。即如王教。是時優波笈多即便思惟。我於本時不樂見之共受五欲。今者欲見觀其手脚及以耳鼻。便往觀之。為說偈頌。婆娑婆達多。既聞其言深畏生死。聞佛功德乃變其意樂於涅槃。即便說偈答優波笈多。優波笈為說四諦。更觀其身得厭欲界。得阿那含果。婆娑婆達多得須陀洹果。優波笈多去已未久。婆娑婆達多即便命終生於天上。時長老舍那婆私。往笈多所語言。汝當聽優波笈多隨我出家。笈多答言。我先有誓令其治生。不利不鈍乃聽出家。舍那婆私乃以神力。令其治生不利不鈍。是時優波笈多即自思惟。稱量算計不利不鈍。舍那婆私。更至笈多所。而語之言。今汝此兒是佛所記。我入涅槃百年後當作佛事。汝當聽其隨我出家。笈多即聽。舍那婆私將優波笈多往那及寺與其出家。受具足戒至第四羯磨。除一切結得阿羅漢果。是時舍那婆私語優波笈多言。善男子。如佛所記。我入涅槃百年後。有比丘名優波笈多。當作佛事。後當作佛。名曰無相。如是說法。是時魔王。於大眾中雨於真珠。以亂人心。眾人亂故無有一人能見諦者。即自思念知是魔王。至第二日倍多人來。魔王雨金又亂眾心。第三日中倍多人來。魔雨珠金及作天樂。是時眾人未得離欲。見色聞聲其心變動不復聽法。是時魔王。即以華鬘繫優波笈多頂。及至優波笈多思惟。誰作此事。即知是魔。優波笈多即生此意。世尊何故不教化之。即自思惟是我應化。佛記於我。為無相佛。教化人民。令優波笈多取三死屍。一者死蛇。二者死狗。三者死人。以神通力變三死屍。以作華鬘。往魔王處而繫其頸。爾時魔王往摩醯首羅。及帝釋等三十三天四天王。為脫死屍。而不得脫。復往大梵天處。大梵語言。善男子。十力弟子神力所作。無能脫之。如大海圻水不能破。魔王語言。云何教我。我於今者。當歸依誰。大梵說言。汝今速往歸依優波笈多。如人於此地墮。即於是起。是時魔王方知佛子神力為大。即便說偈讚佛自責。欲界魔王即捨慢心。自說往罪(出阿育王經第八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Śāṇakavāsin, having built a monastery on the great Ghee Mountain, converted an incense merchant there named Gupta through skillful means, urging him to diligent practice. One day, Śāṇakavāsin visited his own household alone; Gupta asked him: sage, why do you have no disciples of your own? For myself, I delight in the household life and its five sensory pleasures, and cannot go forth — but should I have a son, I will let him follow you, elder. In time, Gupta indeed had a son, named Aśvagupta (in the Liang language, "Ungoverned Protector"). When the boy grew up, Śāṇakavāsin came again to Gupta and reminded him of his own earlier vow — that, should he have a son, that son would be given to the elder — and now, having had one, asked that he be permitted to go forth with him. Gupta answered: I now have only this one son; should a second son be born, I shall give that one to you instead. In time a second son was indeed born, named Dhanagupta ("Treasure Protector"), and Śāṇakavāsin asked for him in turn — but again was refused. A third son, Upagupta, was likewise asked for — and Gupta answered: I shall make a vow instead, that Upagupta be tested in trade; should he prove either too skilled or too unskilled a merchant, he may not go forth, but should he prove neither, then I shall permit it.

At that time Māra caused every person in the kingdom of Mathurā to buy Upagupta's own wares and profit thereby, while Upagupta himself sold incense as usual. Śāṇakavāsin, the elder, then asked him: do you know what makes a state of mind wholesome, and what makes it unwholesome? Upagupta answered that he did not know. The elder told him: a state of mind associated with greed, hatred, or delusion is called unwholesome; one associated with their absence is called wholesome. The elder then took clay of black and white, forming each into small balls, and told him: whenever an unwholesome thought arises in you, take up a black ball; whenever a wholesome thought arises, take up a white one. Upagupta followed this instruction — and, at first, his own mind produced no wholesome thought at all, gathering only a single white ball at first; gradually, over time, he came to take two black balls for every one white; then, reflecting further, an equal number of black and white; and, continuing to reflect on wholesome states, at last ceased producing unwholesome thoughts altogether, taking only white balls.

At that time in Mathurā there lived a courtesan named Vāsavadattā, who kept a serving-girl. This girl, buying a great quantity of incense from Upagupta, was asked by her own mistress where she had obtained so much, and whether she had perhaps stolen it. The girl answered: there is a merchant named Upagupta, of fine and complete appearance, gentle and refined in speech, who sells his wares fairly. Hearing this, the mistress's own heart filled with desire, and she sent the girl back to Upagupta with the message that she wished to enjoy his company — but Upagupta answered only: the time is not yet right. This happened repeatedly, yet each time he refused.

She then sent for an elder's son to visit instead. A caravan-leader from northern India, arriving in Mathurā with five hundred horses and various goods, asked the local people where the city's finest woman might be found, and was told: there is a woman of unmatched beauty, named Vāsavadattā. The caravan-leader declared he would bring five hundred silver coins and various goods to visit her. The courtesan, coveting his wealth, murdered the elder's son, placing his body in a filthy place, and continued her own pleasures with her own lover regardless. The dead man's own close friends, searching, found his remains in that filthy place and reported it to the king, who ordered: seize this Vāsavadattā, and cut off her hands, feet, ears, and nose, and cast her out into the wilderness. This was done exactly as the king commanded.

Upagupta, reflecting on this, thought: though I once had no wish to see her, when we might have enjoyed the five pleasures together, I wish now to see her — her hands, feet, ears, and nose all cut away — and went to look upon her, speaking a verse to her as he did. Vāsavadattā, hearing his words, grew deeply fearful of birth and death, and, hearing of the Buddha's own virtues, her own heart turned toward nirvāṇa instead, answering Upagupta with a verse of her own. Upagupta then taught her the four noble truths, and, observing her own body further, she grew disenchanted with the desire realm and attained the fruit of non-returning; Vāsavadattā herself attained the fruit of stream-entry. Not long after Upagupta departed, Vāsavadattā's own life ended, and she was reborn in heaven.

The elder Śāṇakavāsin then went again to Gupta and said: you should now permit Upagupta to go forth with me. Gupta answered: I made an earlier vow that he be neither too skilled nor too unskilled in trade before he might go forth. Śāṇakavāsin, through his own supernormal power, then caused Upagupta's trading to become exactly neither skilled nor unskilled — and Upagupta himself, reflecting and calculating his own accounts, found them indeed neither one nor the other. Śāṇakavāsin then went once more to Gupta and told him: this very son of yours has been foretold by the Buddha himself — a hundred years after my own nirvāṇa, he shall carry out the Buddha's own work; you should now permit him to go forth with me. Gupta at last consented.

Śāṇakavāsin took Upagupta to the Nadi-Bhadra monastery and had him go forth there, conferring the full precepts upon him; by the fourth formal proceeding, every one of his own fetters was exhausted, and he attained arhatship. Śāṇakavāsin then told Upagupta: good son, as the Buddha himself foretold, a hundred years after my own nirvāṇa there will be a monk named Upagupta who shall carry out the Buddha's own work, teaching the Dharma as though a buddha himself, though without the Buddha's own physical marks.

At this, Māra, in the midst of the assembled multitude, rained down pearls to confuse their minds, so that not a single person there was able to perceive the truth being taught. Upagupta, reflecting, knew this was Māra's own doing. The next day, twice as many people came, and Māra rained down gold, again confusing the assembly's own minds; the third day, still more people came, and Māra rained down pearls and gold together, and conjured heavenly music besides — and the assembly, not yet free of desire, seeing such forms and hearing such sounds, found their own minds disturbed and unable to attend to the teaching any longer.

Māra then hung a garland around Upagupta's own neck. Upagupta, reflecting on who had done this, knew it was Māra, and thought: why does the World-Honored One himself not convert such a being? — and reflected further that this very task now fell to himself, for the Buddha had foretold that he would be called the buddha Aniketa (Without-a-Mark), and would teach and convert the people. Upagupta then took up three corpses — a dead snake, a dead dog, and a dead human — and, through his own supernormal power, transformed them into a garland, which he hung around Māra's own neck in turn.

Māra, terrified, went first to Maheśvara, then to Śakra and the Trāyastriṃśa devas, then to the Four Heavenly Kings, seeking to be freed of the corpse-garland, but none could remove it. He went finally to Mahābrahmā, who told him: good son, this is the work of the ten-powered one's own disciple's spiritual might — none can undo it, no more than the great ocean's own waters could ever be split apart. Māra asked: what, then, am I to do — to whom should I now take refuge? Mahābrahmā answered: go quickly and take refuge in Upagupta himself — just as a man who has fallen upon this very ground must rise from that same ground. Māra, only then understanding the true greatness of the Buddha's own disciple's power, spoke a verse in praise of the Buddha and reproach of himself — and Māra, lord of the desire realm, at once abandoned his own arrogance, confessing his past sins aloud (Source: Sūtra of King Aśoka, fascicle 8).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Upagupta's own visit to Vāsavadattā after her mutilation is easy to misread as cruelty, but the text is careful about what actually happens there: he goes specifically because he had refused to see her when seeing her meant desire, and now wishes to see her once desire is no longer possible — the visit itself becomes the very occasion of her own liberation rather than a humiliation. Read next to the entry's closing scene, where Māra is undone not by force but by being handed back the exact currency he tried to use against the assembly — pearls and gold for corpses — the compendium seems to favor a single recurring move throughout this entry: meeting an opponent's own chosen terms precisely, rather than escaping or overpowering them from outside.

4. Upagupta's Silence, and His Teacher's Rebuke

Lośaka then sat back down and composed his mind, reflecting to himself: every time I go begging together with other monks, I always return with an empty bowl; now Śāriputra has offered me the Buddha's own remaining food, and even this has been overturned and lost — all of this must surely be the fruit of my own past sin, which I alone must bear. Reflecting in this way, his own fetters dissolved, his own defilements cleared away, and he attained arhatship on the spot — and, at that very moment, ate a mouthful of earth, and entered final nirvāṇa.

Why the Vinaya Split into Five Schools

While the Buddha lived, the assembled sangha wore only plain, coarse cloth taken from the dead, of mixed and worn quality. Later, once the monk Lośaka had joined the sangha, he returned empty and hungry from begging every single time. The Buddha, knowing the karmic cause behind this, had the assembled sangha divide the Vinaya into five schools, each with its own robe color, and had Lośaka go begging with a different school each day, establishing a fixed system accordingly, each school named for its own particular strength and given its own robe color: the Sarvāstivāda school, broadly learned and quick of understanding, guiding others through the Dharma, was to wear a black robe; the Kāśyapīya school, diligent and resolute, protecting and sheltering beings, was to wear a mulberry-bark-colored robe; the Mahīśāsaka school, deeply absorbed in meditation, penetrating the most subtle and profound matters, was to wear a blue robe; the Mahāsāṃghika school, diligently studying the many scriptures and expounding their meaning, was to wear a yellow robe. From that time onward, Lośaka was able to obtain food in abundance.

Why was this so? Because Lośaka's own former lack of merit had caused his earlier hardship. Ānanda asked the Buddha: if Lośaka lacked merit in his own past life, how did he come to become a monk at all? The Buddha answered: this same Lośaka, in a past life, was born the son of a worthy family, but, given to jealousy, would see monks coming to beg and shut the door against them, saying the master of the house was not home; the monk would then go to another household, only to find the door there likewise shut against him, with the same excuse given. It is for this very reason that he could not obtain alms in this present life. Yet, seeing others give food and rejoice in doing so, he thought to himself: I too wish to become a monk myself — and it is for this reason that, despite his own former poverty of merit, he is now reduced to such hardship (Source: Sūtra of the Samādhi of the Bequeathed Teaching, lower fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Lośaka's own liberation arrives only once he stops treating his own hunger as a problem to be solved by others' effort — Maudgalyāyana's attempt to circumvent it by traveling with him fails across a hundred million kingdoms, and even the Buddha's own food, offered directly through Śāriputra, sinks into the earth and spills into the river before it can reach him. What finally works is not a rescue at all, but Lośaka's own acceptance that this particular debt is his alone to bear — a shift in his own mind rather than a change in his own circumstances, arriving in the very same moment as his liberation. That the compendium's second entry then explains this same story's institutional consequence — a fivefold Vinaya, still observed by Buddhist schools today — as arising directly from one starving individual's own particular karma is a striking claim about how much weight a single person's story can bear within this tradition's own account of its own history.

The other nuns, seeing the signs of her pregnancy, expelled her from the order. Because of this, the matter was reported to the World-Honored One, who answered: if she conceived while still a laywoman, she has committed no offense at all. This nun later bore a son, named Kumāra-Kāśyapa ("Child Kāśyapa"). By the age of eight, he had gone forth and attained arhatship.

Together with a group of sixteen fellow monks, he went once to the bank of the Ajitavatī River, each carrying a water vessel; there they bathed, entered the water, floated on their backs, and played, splashing and swimming across the river together. She answered the king: perhaps these are young monks, only recently ordained, not yet familiar with the precepts — or perhaps the World-Honored One simply has not yet established a rule forbidding this.

All agreed. Each took up his own water vessel, filled it with water, and set it before himself; then, seated cross-legged in a row, they rose into the air together and flew directly over the king's own palace. Queen Mallikā, sitting in an open area, saw their shadows pass overhead and looked up — and saw the monks, seated cross-legged in orderly rows, their water vessels floating before them, passing through the sky like a flock of wild geese. Seeing this, she was filled with great joy, and said to the king: behold the spiritual power of our own field of merit! The king, seeing it too, was filled with great joy himself, and declared: excellent, excellent — I have gained true benefit indeed. May the Buddha, the World-Honored One, and the assembled sangha remain in this kingdom for the rest of their lives, our true and lasting field of merit. The Buddha, hearing of this event, established a precept thereafter forbidding monks from doing so again (Source: Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 19; the same also in the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya).

At that time the venerable Madhyāntika, together with four other monks, flew through the air from Pāṭaliputra to the edge of the Himalayas, to Aravāla's own pool, and there walked, stood, sat, and lay upon the very surface of the water. Hearing the Dharma, the dragon king took the refuges and precepts at once, together with his own entire retinue, becoming disciples. The mountain spirits, yakṣas, gandharvas, kumbhāṇḍas, and other spirits of the Himalayas, hearing of this, likewise came to faith and submission, becoming disciples themselves, and all attained a glimpse of the path.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry and the fascicle's own opening entry tell what is, in essentials, the same story — a dragon king in Kaśmīra unleashing storm, fire, and hurled mountains against "Madhyāntika," rendered here as "Majjhantika," a variant transliteration of the identical name — drawn from two different source texts rather than harmonized into one. Where the opening entry ends the instant the dragon's own violence turns to flowers, this later telling continues on to the dragon's own formal conversion and the wider region's own resulting devotion, suggesting the two accounts may once have circulated as separate tellings of a single founding legend before this compendium set them, unreconciled, eight entries apart.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the time of King Aśoka, the Buddha had already entered nirvāṇa over two hundred years before. The monk Mahinda came to the island of Siṃhala. The king there, wishing to go hunting, was led by a tree spirit — who wished the king to encounter Mahinda — to see a deer, conjured by that same spirit, grazing not far from him and walking slowly away. At that time King Devānampiya Tissa had already exchanged letters and tribute with King Aśoka, sending treasures back and forth as tokens of friendship. The Dharma-teacher then said: I have three jewels to offer the king, treasures beyond even what that heaven-beloved friend of yours in the world could give — and taught him the threefold refuge. The king received the three refuges and became a lay follower. Mahinda then further explained the Śākya teaching to King Devānampiya Tissa in the hunting grounds itself, and, recalling that Aśoka's own letter had mentioned a son of the Śākya clan, the king cast aside his bow and arrow and earnestly asked to be taught further. The Dharma-teacher expounded the sūtras and conferred the threefold refuge upon him.

Mahinda then sent for the novice Sumana and said: it is now time to teach — you should proclaim the turning of the Dharma-wheel. Sumana asked: how loudly should my own voice now proclaim it? Mahinda answered: let your voice fill the whole island of Siṃhala. Sumana then entered the fourth dhyāna, and, rising from that same samādhi, proclaimed the turning of the Dharma-wheel — and the people of the whole island heard it at once, while every deva and spirit of the sky gathered together as well. Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry records, in the compendium's own narrative idiom of conjured deer and heavenly-voiced novices, an event of genuine historical consequence — Mahinda's mission to Siṃhala under King Aśoka's own patronage marks the documented beginning of Buddhism in Sri Lanka, from which the entire Theravāda tradition as it survives today traces its own transmission. The story's small human detail is worth noting alongside the spectacle: Mahinda's opening move is not a display of power at all, but simply calling a startled king by his own first name, and the "three jewels" he offers are explicitly measured against the earthly tribute King Aśoka had already been sending — the compendium framing spiritual transmission and diplomatic gift-exchange as operating, in this moment, on the very same register.

Near the southern sea, in the kingdom of Nālika, the people made their living gathering pearls and sandalwood. In that kingdom there lived a household with two brothers who, upon their parents' death, wished to divide the estate. The household owned a young slave named Pūrṇa, clever and capable, skilled in trade, knowing everything there was to know about seafaring commerce; the family's own property was divided into equal shares, with the slave Pūrṇa counted as one whole share by himself. The brothers drew lots, and the younger brother received Pūrṇa as his own portion, taking his own wife and children out of the household with nothing else in hand.

It happened to be a year of famine, and, though he had gained Pūrṇa, the younger brother worried they might not even be able to survive, and fell into deep anxiety. The slave Pūrṇa told his master: do not worry — within a matter of days, I shall make you surpass even your own elder brother. His master answered: if you can truly accomplish this, I shall free you as a commoner. The master's own wife had some pearls of her own, which she gave to Pūrṇa as capital.

At that time the tide came in, and the people of the city went down to the water's edge to gather firewood. Pūrṇa took the pearls and went outside the city, where he saw a beggar carrying firewood — and within that firewood was a piece of gaśīrṣa sandalwood, capable of curing grave illness, worth a thousand taels of gold for even a mere two ounces, a substance not always to be found in this world. Pūrṇa recognized it for what it was, and bought it for two gold coins, bringing it home and breaking it into several dozen pieces. At that time a certain elder had fallen gravely ill, and it was said that only two ounces of this very gaśīrṣa sandalwood, compounded into medicine, could cure him — yet none could be found anywhere. Pūrṇa brought his own piece, and received two thousand taels of gold for it. Selling the rest in this same way, he gained wealth beyond counting, becoming ten times wealthier than his own former master's elder brother, and his own master, deeply moved, kept his word and freed him as promised, letting him go wherever he pleased.

Pūrṇa then took his leave and went to seek the path, arriving in Śrāvastī, where he bowed before the Buddha, and, kneeling, said: though I come from lowly birth, my own heart delights in the path and its virtue — I beg only that the World-Honored One, in his own compassion, deliver me. The Buddha said: welcome. Pūrṇa's hair fell away of itself, a monastic robe appearing on his body, and he became a monk at once. The Buddha taught him the Dharma, and he soon attained arhatship. Sitting in reflection, he thought: I have now attained the six supernormal powers, free to live or die as I wish — all of this owed entirely to my own former master's kindness. I should now go and deliver him, and convert the people of my own former country as well.

The venerable Pūrṇa then traveled to his own homeland, arriving at his former master's household. His master, overjoyed, invited him to sit and prepared a meal; once the meal was finished and his hands washed, Pūrṇa rose into the air, dividing his own body, his body emitting water and fire, radiant light streaming down from above, and told his master: this very spiritual virtue is entirely the fruit of your own act of releasing me — I learned it all upon reaching the Buddha's own presence. His master answered: such wondrous transformation — I beg to see the World-Honored One myself, and receive his own teaching. Pūrṇa answered: only prepare a feast with a sincere heart, and the Buddha, possessed of the three insights, will surely come of his own accord.

His master prepared the offering that same night, and, facing toward Śrāvastī, bowed with joined palms, burned incense, and invoked the Buddha, asking that he condescend to come and deliver everyone there widely. The Buddha, knowing his intent, arrived at once, together with five hundred arhats, each through his own supernormal power, at his household — and the whole kingdom's own king and people, astonished, hurried there, prostrating themselves before the Buddha. Once the meal was finished and hands washed, the Buddha, for the master's household and the assembled officials of the king, expounded the clear teaching at length, and all of them received the five precepts, becoming disciples of the Buddha. Rising and standing before the Buddha, they praised Pūrṇa, saying: diligent in the household life, he attained the path upon going forth, his own spiritual virtue reaching so far that his own household and country alike have now been delivered by it — how, then, might I repay such a debt of kindness?

The World-Honored One then praised Pūrṇa, speaking this verse:

"The mind already at rest, speech and conduct likewise stilled — from stillness comes liberation, quiescent, one returns to cessation.
Abandoning desire, without attachment, breaking through the three realms' own obstruction —
hope itself extinguished, this is called a superior person, whether dwelling in wilderness or open field,
level ground or high cliff — wherever a true one passes, none fail to receive his blessing.
He delights in solitude, where ordinary people cannot bear to remain — how joyful, free of all desire,

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Pūrṇa's own return, once ordained, is deliberate rather than incidental — he could have simply stayed free, but reflects explicitly that his six supernormal powers are "entirely owed to my former master's kindness," treating manumission itself as a debt requiring repayment on the very same terms as any other kindness in this compendium. The escalating spectacle that follows — dividing his own body, then summoning the Buddha and five hundred arhats to his former master's own house — reads less as showing off than as Pūrṇa's own insistence on repaying that debt as fully and publicly as it can possibly be repaid, converting an entire kingdom in the process of settling one man's personal account.

13. Mahākāla's Cooling Rain

Returning exhausted from a meal in the late-spring heat, the most junior monk in the group offers to cool everyone down with a summoned rain — and, pressed by an astonished elder to demonstrate further, burns an entire pile of grass to nothing without singeing the cloth laid over it.

佛住菴羅聚落菴羅林中。與眾比丘俱。時有質多羅長者。白上座言。唯願諸尊。於此林中受我請食。時諸上座默然。諸上座著衣持鉢。至長者舍就座而坐。長者自手供養種種飲食。澡嗽畢聽說法。諸上座為長者說法。示教利喜。諸上座食蘇酪蜜飽。於春後日時熱行極。有一下座。名摩訶迦。白諸上座。今日大熱我欲起雲雨扇微風。上座答言。能爾者佳。時摩訶迦即入三昧。如其正受。應時雲起細雨微下。凉風亹亹從四方來至精舍門。即止神通還於自房。時長者作是念。最下座比丘而能有此大神通力。況復中上。禮諸比丘。隨摩訶迦至此住房。禮足白言。尊者我欲得見神足現化。摩訶迦言。長者勿用見是。三請不許。長者猶復重請。摩訶迦語長者言。汝且出外聚集乾草。以㲲覆上。長者如教聚薪成積。來白尊者。摩訶迦即入火光三昧。於戶鉤孔中出火炎。光燒其積薪都盡。唯白㲲不然。語長者言。汝今見不。答言。已見。實為奇特。摩訶迦語長者言。當知此者皆以不放逸為本。不放逸故得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。及餘道品法。長者白。尊者。摩訶迦。願常住此林中。我當盡壽四事供養。摩訶迦有行因緣不受其請。長者聞說法已作禮而去(出質多羅長者比丘經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in the Āmra grove of the village of Āmra, together with the assembled monks. At that time the elder Citra said to the senior monks: I beg all you honored ones to accept my own invitation to a meal in this very grove. The senior monks consented in silence. Robing themselves and taking up their bowls, they went to the elder's own house and sat; the elder personally served every kind of fine food with his own hands, and, once the meal was finished and hands washed, listened to the Dharma being taught. The senior monks then taught the elder the Dharma at length, showing him its own benefit and delight.

Having eaten their fill of ghee and honey, the senior monks, in the late spring heat, found the walk back exhausting. A junior monk among them, named Mahākāla, said to the senior monks: today is very hot — I wish to raise clouds and rain, and stir a gentle breeze for us. The senior monks answered: if you can indeed do this, excellent. Mahākāla then entered samādhi, and, from within that very absorption, clouds gathered and a fine rain began to fall, a cool breeze blowing gently from all four directions, reaching all the way to the monastery gate — and, this accomplished, he withdrew his own supernormal power and returned to his own quarters.

The elder reflected: even the most junior monk among them possesses such great supernormal power — how much more, then, must the middle and senior monks possess! He bowed to the assembled monks, and, following after Mahākāla, went to his own dwelling, bowed at his feet, and said: venerable one, I wish to witness your own supernormal transformation directly. Mahākāla answered: elder, there is no need to witness this. Three times the elder asked, and three times Mahākāla refused — yet the elder pressed him still further. Mahākāla then told him: go outside and gather dry grass, and cover it with a length of cloth. The elder did as instructed, gathering the grass into a pile, and returned to tell the venerable one. Mahākāla then entered the samādhi of fire-radiance, and, through the very keyhole of the door, sent forth a flame that burned the entire pile of grass to nothing — yet the white cloth covering it remained entirely untouched by fire. He told the elder: do you see this now? The elder answered: I have indeed seen it — truly extraordinary. Mahākāla told the elder: know that all of this is rooted in nothing but diligence free of heedlessness — for it is through such diligence alone that one attains unsurpassed, perfect awakening, and every other quality of the path besides.

The elder said: venerable Mahākāla, I beg you to dwell always in this very grove — I shall provide the four requisites for the rest of my own life. Mahākāla, bound by other karmic conditions, did not accept his invitation. The elder, having heard the teaching, bowed and departed (Source: Sūtra of the Monk Citra the Elder).

14. The Hungry Ghost's Confession

An arhat who works among the hungry ghosts questions one whose body burns like a great heap of fire, and learns that its own suffering traces back to a single, once-ordinary habit: a monk who mocked and slandered every diligent practitioner he ever met.

Chinese Source

願足阿羅漢。恒訓化餓鬼。見一餓鬼形狀醜陋。見者毛竪莫不畏懼。身出熾炎如大火聚。口出蛆蟲膿血流溢。臭氣遠徹不可親近。或口吐炎火長數十丈。或耳鼻眼身體支節。放諸火炎長數十丈。脣口垂倒像如野猪。身體縱廣一由旬。手自抓摑舉聲㘁哭馳走東西。是時願足問餓鬼曰。汝宿何罪今受此苦。餓鬼報曰。吾曩昔時行作沙門。戀著房舍慳貪不捨。身持威儀出言臭惡。若見持戒精進比丘。輒復罵辱偏眼惡視。自賴豪族謂為不死。造諸無量不善之本。寧以利刀自截其舌。如是從劫離劫甘心受苦。不以一日之中誹謗精進比丘。若還閻浮利地者。以我形狀可戒勅諸比丘。善護口過勿妄出言。設見梵行持戒比丘者。念宣其德。自受餓鬼形已來。經數千百萬歲。受此苦惱。我後命終當入地獄。是時餓鬼說此語已。舉聲㘁哭自投于地。如泰山崩天翻地覆。斯由口過故使然矣。能守護口過者受福無窮。又迦葉如來出現於世。敷說法教教化已周。於無餘泥洹界而般泥洹。泥洹後時有三藏比丘。名曰黃顏。眾僧告勅。一切雜使不令卿涉。但與諸後學者說諸妙法。時三藏比丘內心輕蔑不免僧命。便與後學敷顯經義。喚受義曰。速前象頭。次喚第二者。復曰馬頭。復次駱駝頭。次驢頭。復次猪頭。次喚羊頭。羯羝頭。次喚師子頭。次喚虎頭。次喚禽頭。次喚羆頭。如是喚眾獸眾獸之類。不可稱數。三藏黃顏。口出如此無量惡言。雖授經義不免其罪。身壞命終入地獄中。經歷數千萬劫受苦無量。餘罪未畢從地獄出。生大海中受水性形。一身百頭形體極大。異類見之皆悉馳走(出護口經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The arhat Praṇidhānapūra constantly taught and converted hungry ghosts. He came upon one hungry ghost of such hideous form that all who saw it felt their own hair stand on end in terror — its body ablaze like a great heap of fire, maggots and pus flowing from its own mouth, a stench reaching far and wide, impossible to approach. Flames, tens of zhang long, poured from its mouth, or from its ears, nose, eyes, and every joint of its body; its lips hung inverted like a wild boar's own snout, its body spanning a full yojana in breadth. It clawed at its own flesh, wailing and shrieking, running wildly in every direction.

Praṇidhānapūra asked the hungry ghost: what past sin has brought you to such suffering? The hungry ghost answered: long ago I was a monk myself, attached to my own dwelling, stingy and unwilling to give, outwardly maintaining proper deportment yet speaking always with a foul mouth — whenever I saw a monk of true precept and diligent practice, I would revile and insult him, glaring at him with contempt. Relying on my own powerful family connections, I believed myself beyond consequence, and committed countless unwholesome deeds of every kind. I would sooner have cut out my own tongue with a sharp blade, kalpa after kalpa, willingly enduring such suffering, than spend even a single day slandering a diligent, practicing monk. Should I ever return to Jambudvīpa, let my own present form serve as a warning to every monk there — to guard their own speech carefully, never speaking carelessly, and, on seeing a monk of pure conduct and upheld precepts, to reflect instead on proclaiming his own virtues. Since first taking this hungry-ghost form, I have already endured this suffering for many hundreds of thousands of years, and, when this present life ends, I shall fall into hell besides.

Having spoken these words, the hungry ghost wailed aloud and threw itself to the ground, as though a great mountain had collapsed and heaven and earth turned upside down — all of it caused by the fault of speech alone. Whoever guards against this same fault of speech reaps boundless blessing in return.

Furthermore: when the Tathāgata Kāśyapa appeared in the world, having fully expounded his own teaching and completed his own work of conversion, he entered the realm of nirvāṇa without remainder. After his nirvāṇa, there lived a monk versed in the Three Baskets, named Yellow Face, whom the assembled sangha instructed: you need not trouble yourself with any ordinary duties — only expound the wondrous Dharma for those who come after you to learn. This monk, inwardly contemptuous, though unable to refuse the sangha's own charge, taught the meaning of the scriptures to the younger students regardless — but, calling on the first to recite what he had learned, addressed him as "elephant-head, come forward"; calling the second, "horse-head"; then "camel-head," "donkey-head," "pig-head," "goat-head," "ram-head," "lion-head," "tiger-head," "bird-head," "bear-head" — and so on through every manner of beast, beyond counting. This Tripiṭaka-master Yellow Face, having uttered such boundless abusive words, could not escape his own fault despite having faithfully transmitted the scriptures' own meaning, and, at the end of his own life, fell into hell, enduring immeasurable suffering across many thousands of kalpas. When his remaining sin was still not exhausted, he emerged from hell and was reborn in the great ocean, taking the form of a water creature with a hundred heads upon a single, monstrously large body, so that every other creature, seeing it, fled in terror (Source: Sūtra of Guarding the Mouth).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both figures in this entry are punished not for any single dramatic act but for an accumulated habit of speech directed specifically at fellow practitioners — the first hungry ghost mocked diligent monks he considered beneath his own family's rank, and the second, Yellow Face, degraded students he was formally charged with teaching well. What makes the second case sharper is that his own transmission of the scripture's meaning was, by the text's own account, entirely accurate — competence in the letter of the teaching provides no shelter at all from the harm done by how it was delivered. Read as a whole, the entry functions as this compendium's own sustained argument that speech among practitioners is treated as a distinct and serious category of conduct, answerable on its own terms regardless of whatever other virtue or duty accompanies it.

15. Sāgata Subdues the Dragon

The fascicle closes with a man cheated out of a bushel of wheat who vows, in his fury, to become a dragon and destroy an entire kingdom — subdued at last by a monk who then appears drunk and asleep beneath a tree, for reasons the Buddha explains have nothing to do with actual intoxication.

Chinese Source

佛在舍衛國。時須耶國有一貧人。賃剃兒頭須至麥熟輒顧一斛。道逢故人欲共飲酒。還求麥直遍求不得。便起毒意。願我壽終作大神龍。當陷此國。及其壽終遂作大龍。其國歷年風雨不時。佛念飢饉遣沙曷比丘往化彼龍。龍見比丘即興惡意。欲殺國人及沙曷身。沙曷化鉢覆蓋一國。龍便降雨謂國已沒。比丘以佛威神令龍見民安隱如故。龍復興恚。更下大雪。比丘鉢受以手掃之著於一處。乃入龍室。龍即便出。比丘復出。龍入比丘復入。如是不已龍極乃止。長跪問言。卿何等神。惱我如此。比丘言。吾是佛弟子。龍言。我欲自歸。便隨之去。沙曷比丘。便內此龍著於笥中。人乃見比丘取龍如是皆大歡喜。問言。道人是何等神降伏國患。告言。吾是佛弟子。人民問言。佛可見不。答言可見。須吾還時日向中。道過分衛。人或有與飯或與酒者。比丘受之樹下。醉臥。龍鉢袈裟各在一處。佛時微笑五色光出。阿難請問。佛以來答。復說四事。一者阿羅漢不入三昧不得知。二者不得便見神足。三者強勸人分衛。四者身中尚有蟲。阿羅漢以是四事不及佛。時有一菩薩欲向聲聞。見此事已心即堅固。佛遣目揵連往沙曷比丘所。勅攝龍來。為說宿命。龍心即解。便受五戒。奉行十善。得須陀洹。阿難問佛此事因緣。佛告阿難。阿羅漢者不復飢渴。因三事故見醉臥耳。一者佛欲開化菩薩意。二者不欲違布施家意。三者恐諸弟子未得道者飲酒多失。故現此事撿戒之耳。沙曷比丘雖復飲酒。不以為醉(出沙曷比丘功德經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Śrāvastī. In the kingdom of Sudāna there lived a poor man, hired to shave children's heads, to be paid a bushel of grain once the wheat ripened. Meeting an old friend along the road who wished to drink with him, he later found, on returning to collect his own wages, that he could get nothing at all despite searching everywhere — and, in his own fury, made a malicious vow: may I, at the end of my own life, become a great and powerful dragon, and destroy this entire kingdom. And, at the end of his life, he did indeed become a great dragon.

For years thereafter, that kingdom suffered rain and wind out of season. The Buddha, grieving over the resulting famine, sent the monk Sāgata to convert this dragon. The dragon, seeing the monk, was seized with malicious intent, wishing to kill both the kingdom's own people and Sāgata himself — but Sāgata conjured a bowl vast enough to cover the entire kingdom. The dragon then sent down rain, believing the whole kingdom already drowned beneath it — but Sāgata, through the Buddha's own majestic power, let the people see that they remained entirely safe and unharmed, exactly as before. The dragon, growing angrier still, sent down a great snowfall instead — and Sāgata simply caught it in his own bowl and swept it away into a single pile with his hand, then entered the dragon's own dwelling. The dragon came out; Sāgata went in. The dragon went in; Sāgata came out. This continued back and forth until the dragon, utterly exhausted, finally stopped, and, kneeling, asked: what manner of spirit are you, to trouble me so? Sāgata answered: I am a disciple of the Buddha. The dragon said: I wish now to take refuge myself — and followed him away.

Sāgata then placed this dragon inside a small basket, and the people, seeing the monk carrying off the very dragon that had plagued them, rejoiced greatly, and asked him: what manner of spirit are you, to have subdued this calamity threatening our whole kingdom? He answered: I am a disciple of the Buddha. The people asked whether the Buddha himself might be seen, and he answered that he could — he would return by midday. Along the way, passing through the town to beg, some gave him rice, others gave him wine; Sāgata, accepting these offerings, lay down drunk beneath a tree, the dragon's basket and his own outer robe set aside separately.

The Buddha, at that moment, smiled quietly, and five-colored light streamed from him. Ānanda asked why, and the Buddha explained, and further named four things: first, that an arhat, unless he enters samādhi, cannot know something for himself; second, that he cannot always directly witness a display of supernormal power; third, that he might, out of politeness, accept when strongly urged to go begging; and fourth, that even an arhat's own body may still harbor certain minor imperfections — in these four respects, an arhat does not equal the Buddha himself. At that time there was a bodhisattva inclined toward the śrāvaka path, who, witnessing this very scene, had his own resolve strengthened all the more by it. The Buddha sent Maudgalyāyana to Sāgata, instructing him to bring the dragon before him, and taught it of its own past lives — and the dragon's own heart was released at once, receiving the five precepts, practicing the ten good deeds, and attaining the fruit of stream-entry.

Ānanda asked the Buddha to explain the whole affair, and the Buddha told him: an arhat no longer feels hunger or thirst at all — Sāgata appeared drunk and asleep only for three reasons: first, the Buddha wished to open the mind of that same bodhisattva; second, he did not wish to disappoint the household that had offered him the wine; and third, he feared that other disciples, not yet having attained the path themselves, might otherwise drink to excess and lose themselves entirely — and so, to guard against this very outcome, he allowed this scene to be witnessed. Though Sāgata appeared to have drunk wine, he was not, in fact, truly intoxicated at all (Source: Merit of the Monk Sāgata Sūtra).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fascicle closes on a deliberately unglamorous note after fourteen entries of dragons subdued through majestic power, jewels transformed into flowers, and boy-arhats flying in formation over palaces: Sāgata's own final scene shows him not triumphant but apparently drunk and asleep under a tree, an image the Buddha then has to explain was staged rather than real, and staged for reasons that have nothing to do with the dragon at all — protecting a donor's own feelings, steadying a wavering bodhisattva's resolve, and warning other monks against real intoxication. Placed at the very end of this fascicle's own long procession of patriarchs and wonder-workers, the entry's quiet insistence that even an arhat's public image requires careful management serves as a fitting final reminder that the compendium's heroes remain, in its own telling, still one clear step short of a buddha.
