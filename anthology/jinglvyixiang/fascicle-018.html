Fascicle 18 · 經律異相卷第十八(聲聞無學僧部第七) · Complete

Thirty-One Short Lives

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 18 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Arhat Śrāvaka Monks, Part 7" (聲聞無學僧部第七) — the compendium's longest fascicle so far, thirty-one brief entries on minor arhats. Among them: Śroṇa-Koṭikarṇa learns the Middle Way through a lute's own strings; Rāṣṭrapāla returns home to a father who first doesn't recognize him and then tries to buy him back; a matricide is converted by Mañjuśrī disguised as a fellow sinner; and Upagupta devises a different, often startling remedy for each of his own students' particular faults — gluttony, sleep, attachment, overconfidence.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 18 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 18 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 18 gathers thirty-one of the compendium's shortest and least individually famous arhat stories, several of them variations on a single recurring structure: Upagupta diagnosing a particular flaw in a struggling student — gluttony, excessive sleep, bodily attachment, misplaced pride — and devising an often theatrical remedy for it. Several other entries share the equally common pattern of a minor past-life offering explaining a present-life marvel. Closely related entries are grouped together here; every entry's full text remains intact.

1. Dvija-nāma, Swallowed by a Fish

A boy dropped into a river by his own distracted parents is swallowed whole by a fish and survives inside it — and, once fished out downstream by a childless household, becomes the subject of a custody dispute the king resolves by giving him to both families at once.

Chinese Source

舍衛國有豪長者。財富無量。唯無子姓每懷悒遲。禱祀求索精誠欵篤。婦生一男端正希有。父母宗親。值時讌會共相聚集。詣大江邊飲食自娛。父母將兒詣其會所。愛念此兒從坐擔舞。父舞已竟。母復擔之。歡娛自樂。臨到河邊意卒散亂。執之不固失兒墮水。尋時搏撮竟不能得。父母憐念愛著傷懷。其兒福德遂復不死。隨水沈浮為一魚所吞。雖在魚腹猶自不死。下流村中有一富家。亦無兒子。種種求索困不能得。而彼富家恒令一奴捕生為業。值得吞小兒魚。剖腹得兒。施與大家。大家觀省。而自慶言。我家由來禱祠神祇。求索子息精誠報應。故天與我。即便摩拭乳哺養之。上村父母聞下村長者魚腹得兒。即往其所。追求索之言。是我兒。我於彼河而失是子。今汝得之。願以見還。時彼長者而答之曰。我家由來禱祠求子。今神報應賜我一兒。君之亡兒竟為所在。紛紜不了詣王求斷。於是二家各引道理。其兒父母說是我兒。我於某時失在河中。而彼長者復自說言。我於河中魚腹得之。此實我子非君所生。王聞其說靡知所如。即為二家平詳此事。卿二長者各名此兒。今若與一於理不可。更共供養。至兒長大。各為娶婦。安置家業。二處異居。此婦生子即屬此家。彼婦生兒。即屬彼家。時二長者各隨王教。兒年長大俱為娶婦。經給所須無有乏短。兒白二父母。我生已來遭羅艱苦。墮水魚吞垂死得濟。今我志意欲得出家。唯願父母當見聽許。時二父母心愛此兒。不能拒逆。即便聽許。往至佛所。求欲入道。佛言。善來。頭髮自墮即成沙門。字曰重姓。佛為說法。得盡諸苦成阿羅漢。佛言。過去久遠有佛世尊。號毘婆尸。集諸大眾為說妙法。時有長者來至會中。聞說大法施戒之福。生信敬心。受三自歸及不殺戒。復以一錢布施彼佛。世世受福財寶無乏。長者子者今重姓比丘是也。由其施佛一錢。九十一劫恒富錢財。至於今世二家父母供給所須。受不殺戒墮水魚吞不能令死。受三歸故得阿羅漢(出賢愚經第五卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there lived a wealthy elder of immeasurable fortune, yet without any child, and his heart was constantly troubled by this; he prayed and made offerings with the utmost sincerity. In time his wife bore a son, of rare and fine appearance. The parents and their whole extended family, gathering once for a festival, went together to the bank of a great river to eat and enjoy themselves. The parents brought their child to the gathering, cherishing him, carrying him on their shoulders in turn as they danced; once the father had finished dancing, the mother carried him in turn, both delighting in their own joy — but, drawing near the river's edge, her attention scattered for a moment, and her own grip failed, and the child fell into the water. They reached for him at once but could not recover him. The parents, grieving and heartbroken, searched in vain — yet the child's own store of merit kept him from death even then: carried along by the current, he was swallowed whole by a fish, and, though within its belly, still did not die.

Downstream, in a village, there lived a wealthy household likewise without a child, who had sought one everywhere without success, and who kept a servant whose own trade was catching fish. This servant happened to catch the very fish that had swallowed the boy, and, cutting open its belly, found the child within, and presented him to his own master. The master, examining him, rejoiced, saying: my own household has long prayed to the spirits for a child, and heaven has now, in its own sincere response, granted me one — and, cherishing him, raised him as his own.

The boy's own upstream parents, hearing that a downstream elder had found a child within a fish's belly, went there and claimed him as their own, saying: this is our own son, lost in the river at such a time — we beg you to return him. The elder answered: my own household has long prayed for a child, and the spirits have now granted me one in response — where, then, is your own lost son? Neither side able to settle the matter, they brought their dispute before the king. Each family stated its own claim — the true parents insisting they had lost their child in the river at such a time, the elder insisting he had found the boy within a fish's own belly, and that the child was truly his own, not theirs. The king, unable to determine the truth, judged the matter as follows: you two elders may each give the child your own name — it would not be right to award him to one family alone; instead, you shall both provide for him jointly until he is grown, and each arrange a marriage for him in turn, settling him in two separate households — whatever children are born to the wife in one household shall belong to that family, and whatever children are born to the wife in the other shall belong to that family in turn. Both elders accepted the king's own judgment.

As the boy grew, both households arranged marriages for him in turn, providing for his every need without lack. The boy then told his two sets of parents: since my own birth, I have endured great hardship and peril, falling into the water and swallowed by a fish, brought to the very edge of death before being saved — I now wish to go forth myself; I beg you, my parents, to grant me leave. Both sets of parents, though cherishing him dearly, could not bring themselves to refuse, and consented. He went to the Buddha and asked to enter the path. The Buddha said: welcome — and his hair fell away of itself, and he became a monk at once, given the name Dvija-nāma, "Twice-Named." The Buddha taught him the Dharma, and he exhausted every suffering and attained arhatship.

The Buddha explained: long ago, in the far distant past, there arose a buddha named Vipaśyin, who gathered a great assembly to teach the wondrous Dharma. An elder, coming to that assembly and hearing the teaching of the merit of giving and upholding the precepts, gave rise to a faithful, reverent heart, and took the threefold refuge and the precept against killing, further offering a single coin to that buddha — and, life after life since, has enjoyed blessing and wealth without lack. That elder's son is now the monk Dvija-nāma himself. Through his own offering of a single coin to the Buddha, across ninety-one kalpas he has remained constantly wealthy in coin; and it is through his own upholding of the precept against killing, in this present life, that both households' parents provided for his every need, and, though swallowed by a fish, he could not be made to die. And it is through his own threefold refuge that he has now attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 5).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king's own custody ruling is a small marvel of practical wisdom in its own right, refusing to decide a question of biological truth he cannot actually determine and instead building an arrangement both families can live with — a solution the story treats as entirely sensible rather than a compromise anyone resents. The Buddha's own closing accounting is equally precise, tracing each separate stroke of fortune in the boy's life back to a single distinct cause: the coin explains his wealth, the precept against killing explains his survival, the refuge explains his arhatship — three separate acts of merit, each doing exactly one job, rather than one general blessing covering everything at once.

2. Śroṇa-Koṭikarṇa's Excessive Diligence

A monk so extreme in his own ascetic effort that his feet bleed from constant walking meditation nearly gives up the robe entirely, believing his own diligence has somehow failed him — until the Buddha asks him about lute strings.

Chinese Source

佛在占波國雷聲池側。尊者二十耳億在一靜處。自修法本不捨頭陀。晝夜經行。行處脚跡如血流溢。恒自剋勵。而欲漏心不得解脫。是時二十耳億便作是念。苦行精進我為第一。我今漏心不得解脫。又我家業多財饒寶。宜可捨服還作白衣。持財廣施。佛心知之便至彼處。佛語二十耳億。汝本在家善能彈琴。琴絃極急響不齊等可聽不。不也世尊。佛言琴絃極緩復可聽不。不也世尊。佛言。不急不緩音可聽不。如是世尊。佛言。極精進者猶如調戲。若懈怠者此墮邪見。若能在中。此則上行。如是不久。當成無漏。二十耳億聞是語已。還雷音池側。思惟佛教。成阿羅漢(出增一阿含經第八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt beside Thunder-Sound Pool in the kingdom of Campā. The venerable Śroṇa-Koṭikarṇa, in a quiet place, cultivated the fundamentals of the Dharma without ever abandoning his own ascetic practices, walking in meditation day and night until the very ground where he walked ran red with blood from his own worn feet, ever pushing himself harder — yet his own mind, still subject to outflows, could not attain liberation. Śroṇa-Koṭikarṇa reflected: in ascetic diligence, I am surely foremost among all — yet my own mind remains unliberated. My own family, moreover, is wealthy and possessed of great treasure — perhaps I should simply give up these robes, return to lay life, and instead give my own wealth away freely. The Buddha, knowing his heart, came to him at once, and said: when you were still a layman, you played the lute skillfully — if the strings were strung too tight, was the sound pleasant to hear? He answered: no, World-Honored One. The Buddha asked: and if the strings were too loose? He answered: no, World-Honored One. The Buddha asked: and if neither too tight nor too loose? He answered: yes, World-Honored One. The Buddha said: excessive diligence is akin to restlessness; excessive laxity falls into wrong view — only the middle way between the two is the true, higher practice. Following this, before long, you shall attain freedom from outflows. Śroṇa-Koṭikarṇa, hearing these words, returned to Thunder-Sound Pool, reflected on the Buddha's own teaching, and attained arhatship (Source: Ekottarika Āgama, fascicle 8).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Śroṇa-Koṭikarṇa's own despair is instructive precisely because it comes from a wrong diagnosis rather than a lack of effort — he assumes his own continued bondage must mean he has not yet pushed hard enough, when the Buddha's own question reveals the opposite: that pushing itself, past a certain point, has become the very obstruction. The lute analogy works because it locates the problem not in the player's own sincerity, which was never in doubt, but in a simple, correctable calibration — a reframing that turns what felt like a spiritual failure into a technical adjustment.

3. Rāṣṭrapāla's Return Home

A monk who once starved himself for days to win his own parents' consent to ordain returns home a decade later, unrecognized by his own father, given rotten food by a servant — and, once identified, is offered his entire abandoned fortune and a new bride to lure him back.

Chinese Source

賴吒婆羅門。求佛出家。佛言。先辭父母。父母不聽。賴吒臥地多日不食。父母慰喻。終亦默然。既憐其志。便即聽許。出家受具足成阿羅漢。後十年還村。次第乞食。父見不識訶罵不與。婢取鉢盛棄爛食與。還啟大家。賴吒還。父即出看。問。汝還何不至我門。答言。已至得罵。父牽入室。辦種種美食。象負金銀著中庭。高於人。錢物無數。汝可還俗。母命新婦嚴莊至賴吒所。親戚共相諫數。賴吒啟欲施食與。何假見嬈(出中阿含經第二十八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The brahmin Rāṣṭrapāla asked the Buddha to let him go forth. The Buddha told him: first take leave of your own parents. His parents refused their consent, and Rāṣṭrapāla lay on the ground for many days without eating. His parents tried to console him, but he remained silent throughout — and, moved at last by his own resolve, they consented. He went forth, received the full precepts, and attained arhatship.

Ten years later, he returned to his own home village, begging in his own turn from house to house. His own father, seeing him, did not recognize him, and, rebuking him, refused to give him anything; a servant girl took his bowl and filled it instead with spoiled, rotten food, then reported this to her own master. Rāṣṭrapāla's father then went out to look for himself, and asked: why, on your own return, did you not come to my own gate? He answered: I did come, and was rebuked for it. His father then drew him inside, prepared every kind of fine food, and had elephants bring gold and silver, piled higher than a man's own height, in the courtyard — countless coins and treasures — saying: you may now return to lay life. His mother summoned his own new bride, adorned in her finest, to come before Rāṣṭrapāla, and his own relatives all joined together to urge him back to lay life. Rāṣṭrapāla told them: I came only to receive food as alms — why trouble me with all of this instead? (Source: Madhyama Āgama, fascicle 28).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story's own irony is precise: Rāṣṭrapāla is rebuked and fed rotten food by the very household that will, moments later, offer him an entire fortune to stay — the same family capable of such extravagant generosity turned him away at the gate simply because they no longer recognized their own son. His own closing line, asking only why they trouble him with all of this when he came merely for alms, reframes the whole confrontation: he has not returned as a claimant to anything they possess, and the gold, the bride, and the pleading all address a version of him that, by his own account, no longer exists.

4–6. Three Past-Life Offerings, Three Wondrous Births

Three children are born with identical, marvelous signs — hands that produce gold coins, flowers raining from the sky, the seven treasures falling like rain — each traced back to a poor person's own small offering to a buddha centuries before: two coins, a handful of wildflowers, a fistful of pebbles offered in place of pearls.

Chinese Source
金財以兩錢施佛僧今生手把金錢四

舍衛城中有大長者。婦生一男兒。名曰金財。端正殊特。是兒手捲。父母驚怖。即開兩手。見二金錢。在兒手中。歡喜收取輙更續生。如是懃取滿藏不盡。兒年轉大。即白父母求索出家。即便聽之。往至佛所剃除鬚髮身著袈裟。年滿受戒。臨壇眾僧。次第為禮。隨所禮處。時兩手按地。皆有二金錢。受戒已竟。精勤修習得羅漢道。阿難白佛。此金財比丘。先造何福手把金錢。佛言。過去九十一劫。有佛名毘婆尸。出現於世。與諸眾僧遊行國界。時諸長者。施設供養佛及弟子。時一貧人取薪賣之。時得兩錢。見佛歡喜。即以兩錢施佛及僧。貧人以兩錢。施佛及僧故。九十一劫恒把金錢。財寶自然。爾時貧人者。金財比丘是也(出賢愚經第二卷)。

Suvarṇadhana: Two Coins, Now a Grip of Gold

In Śrāvastī there lived a great elder whose wife bore a son, named Suvarṇadhana, "Golden Wealth," of extraordinary and fine appearance, born with his own two hands clenched shut. His parents, startled, pried his hands open, and found two gold coins within them. Delighted, they took the coins — yet more appeared in their place, again and again, so that, however much was taken, his own hands never emptied. As the boy grew, he asked his own parents for permission to go forth, which was granted. He went to the Buddha, shaved his head, and donned the monastic robe; once of proper age, he received the precepts — and, as the assembled sangha came forward in turn to bow before him at the ordination platform, wherever his own two hands pressed the ground, two gold coins appeared there each time. Once ordained, he applied himself diligently and attained arhatship.

Ānanda asked the Buddha: what merit did this monk Suvarṇadhana cultivate in the past, that he should grasp gold coins in this way? The Buddha answered: ninety-one kalpas ago, a buddha named Vipaśyin appeared in the world, traveling with his own assembled sangha throughout the land, and the elders there prepared offerings for the Buddha and his disciples. At that time a poor man, gathering firewood to sell, had earned two coins, and, seeing the Buddha, rejoiced, and offered both coins to the Buddha and the sangha at once. Through the merit of this poor man's own gift of two coins to the Buddha and sangha, across ninety-one kalpas he has grasped gold coins constantly, his own wealth arising spontaneously. That poor man of the past is now the monk Suvarṇadhana himself (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 2).

華天先世採花供養今天雨其花五

舍衛國有豪富長者。生一男兒。面首端正。天雨眾花積滿舍內。即字此兒。名弗把提(梁言花天)兒年轉大。往至佛所。心自思惟。我出處世得值聖尊。即前白佛言。唯願世尊及與眾僧。明日屈意臨適鄙家。受少蔬食。佛即受請。花天還至。化作寶床遍其舍內。正設嚴飾。佛及眾僧即坐其座。花天福德飲食自辦。佛僧食已。廣為花天具說法要。花天合家得須陀洹。花天即辭父母。求索出俗。父母聽之。佛言善來。鬚髮自墮袈裟著身。遵修佛教逮得羅漢。阿難白佛。花天何福。而得如是。佛言。過去有佛。名毘婆尸。出現於世度脫眾生。時諸豪族皆悉供養。有一貧人。見僧歡喜。即於野澤。採眾草花用散大眾。佛告阿難。爾時貧人。今花天比丘是。散花之德。九十一劫身體端正。意有所須。如念而至(出賢愚經第二卷)。

Puṣpadeva: Wildflowers, Now a Rain of Blossoms

In Śrāvastī there lived a wealthy elder whose wife bore a son of fine appearance, at whose birth heavenly flowers rained down, filling the entire household — and so the child was named Puṣpadeva, "Flower Heaven" (in the Liang language). As the boy grew, he went to the Buddha, reflecting to himself: born into this world, I have now had the good fortune to encounter the honored sage himself — and, approaching the Buddha, said: I beg the World-Honored One and the assembled sangha to condescend, tomorrow, to visit my own humble household and accept a simple vegetarian meal. The Buddha accepted the invitation. Puṣpadeva returned home and conjured jeweled couches throughout the entire household, arranging every fine adornment; the Buddha and the assembled sangha, once seated, found the meal already prepared of its own accord, through Puṣpadeva's own merit. Once the Buddha and the sangha had eaten, the Buddha taught Puṣpadeva the essentials of the Dharma at length, and his entire household attained the fruit of stream-entry. Puṣpadeva then took leave of his own parents, asking to go forth, which they granted. The Buddha said: welcome — and his hair fell away of itself, a monastic robe appearing on his body; following the Buddha's own teaching, he in time attained arhatship.

Ānanda asked the Buddha: what merit did Puṣpadeva possess, to attain such things? The Buddha answered: long ago, a buddha named Vipaśyin appeared in the world, delivering beings everywhere, and every wealthy and eminent household there made offerings to him. A poor man, seeing the assembled sangha, rejoiced, and, gathering wildflowers from an open field, scattered them over the whole assembly. The Buddha told Ānanda: that poor man of the past is now the monk Puṣpadeva. Through the merit of scattering those flowers, across ninety-one kalpas his own body has remained of fine appearance, and whatever he wishes for arrives the very instant he thinks of it (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 2).

寶天前身以一把石擬珠散僧故生時天雨七寶六

Ratnadeva: Pebbles for Pearls, Now a Rain of Treasure

In Śrāvastī there lived an elder whose wife bore a son, at whose very birth the seven treasures rained down from the sky, filling the entire household. The Buddha said: welcome — and his hair fell away of itself, a monastic robe appearing on his body. The Buddha taught him the Dharma, and he attained arhatship at once.

When the buddha Vipaśyin appeared in the world, various householders together invited the assembled sangha and made every kind of offering. A poor man there, though his own heart was filled with joy, had nothing to offer, and so took a handful of white pebbles, treating them as pearls, and scattered them over the assembled sangha, making a great vow as he did. Reading note — Ru-Yi

All three stories, drawn from the same source text and placed consecutively here, share an identical structure down to the wording — a wealthy household's child born with a spontaneous marvel, a name given to match it, a request to go forth, and a closing account tracing the marvel to a specific poor person's own small offering ninety-one kalpas before. What stands out across all three is the deliberate mismatch of scale between the original gift and its own eventual return: two coins, a handful of roadside weeds, and a fistful of ordinary pebbles offered in place of pearls each become, after ninety-one kalpas of compounding, a body that produces gold, flowers, or treasure spontaneously — the compendium's own repeated insistence that the size of a gift matters far less than the sincerity of the poverty it was given out of.

9. The Bandit Who Became a Monk by Accident

A condemned criminal who slips away and disguises himself as a monk purely to escape execution, feeling no fear left at all once free, is told by the Buddha that the body itself remains condemned to a far longer sentence than the one he narrowly avoided.

The story's real interest is in the disguise itself becoming true almost by accident — a man who put on monastic robes purely as an escape route ends the story, through nothing but honest reflection on his own remaining danger, as an actual arhat.

The monk's own attempt to cheat the instruction — blowing on the food rather than waiting for it to cool naturally — is exactly the same impatience Upagupta is trying to treat, applied to the treatment itself, and Upagupta's own response makes this explicit: it is not the food's own temperature that mattered, but the mind's. Having him vomit and face his own meal again is not a punishment so much as the most direct possible demonstration of the point Upagupta wants him to internalize — that the very food he could not stop coveting is, one moment later, something he cannot bear to touch at all.

On the day of their parting, King Universal Peace asked them: what, in this world, do people consider true joy? The four kings' own answers were no different from what you yourselves have just said. King Universal Peace then embodied the very same teaching himself, and the four kings, hearing it, rejoiced and understood.

I was that King Universal Peace; you four are those same four kings. Having already discussed this once before, why do you still fail to understand it now? Life and death stretch on without end — by what means shall they ever be brought to rest? The four monks, hearing this teaching once more, felt shame and repented, their minds opening in realization, and attained the arhat's own path (Source: Dharmapada, Sūtra of Similes, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both stories share an unusual structure among this compendium's many teaching-through-parable entries: the past life recounted is not a stranger's example but the monks' own former selves, and the lesson being taught is one they themselves already articulated once before, in a different body, and have simply forgotten. The Buddha's own rebuke each time is not "here is a truth you have not yet grasped" but "you already knew this" — a sharper form of embarrassment than mere ignorance, since it locates the failure not in never having understood, but in having let a hard-won insight lapse entirely across a single rebirth.

14. Freeing a Mother from Hell

An arhat, finding his own mother trapped in hell, learns of a patricidal king with seven days left to live before joining her there — and saves them both through nothing more than a single unbroken invocation of the Buddha's own name.

Chinese Source

昔有一人。辭親學道得成應真。諸有恩已盡行濟之。觀見其母在泥犁中。廣求方便欲脫母苦。見邊境有王害父奪國。命餘七日。受罪之地與其母同。夜到王所。於壁現半身。王見怖懅。拔刀斫頸。頸即落地。比丘不動。王意方解。叩頭謝過。比丘語王。汝害父奪國不耶。對曰。實爾。曰汝命餘七日。當入地獄。吾故來相語。王即求哀。比丘曰。大作功德恐不相及。王但稱南無佛。七日不絕便得免罪。王便一心稱南無佛。七日不懈。命終神明猶憶入泥犁門。故稱南無佛。泥犁即冷。一城中人皆得脫出。比丘便為說法。比丘母及王。與泥犁中人皆得度脫(出十卷譬喻經第二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, a man took leave of his own parents to study the path, and attained the state of the truly accomplished. Having repaid every debt of kindness owed to those still living, he observed that his own mother had fallen into hell, and sought by every means to free her from this suffering. He learned of a king in a neighboring land who had murdered his own father and seized the throne, with only seven days left to live before his own death, destined for the very same place of retribution as his mother. The monk went by night to this king's own palace, manifesting the upper half of his own body upon the wall. The king, terrified at the sight, drew his sword and struck at the apparition's neck, severing the head, which fell to the ground — yet the monk himself remained unmoved. The king, only then understanding, bowed his head in apology.

The monk said to the king: did you not murder your own father and seize his throne? The king answered: it is true. The monk said: you have but seven days left to live, and shall then enter hell — I have come specifically to tell you this. The king, terrified, begged for mercy. The monk told him: great acts of merit now would surely come too late — but if you simply invoke the name of the Buddha without pause for these seven remaining days, you may yet be spared this fate. The king, with a single-minded heart, invoked the Buddha's own name unceasingly for seven days; when his own life ended, his spirit still remembered, even as he entered hell's own gate, to invoke the Buddha's name — and hell itself grew cool at once, every person within that same city delivered from it entirely. The monk then taught the Dharma there, and his own mother, the king, and everyone else within hell were all delivered together (Source: Sūtra of Similes and Parables, in ten fascicles, fascicle 2).

15. The Monk Who Released His Own Grip

A monk still bound by attachment to bodily comfort is led up a conjured tree above a conjured pit and told, one limb at a time, to let go — refusing only at the final hand, until Upagupta reminds him he agreed to follow every instruction, not just the easy ones.

Chinese Source

南天竺國有一男子。於佛法出家。身為愛所縛。以蘇油摩身。又用湯水洗浴。資種種飲食。身為愛縛不得聖道。往摩偷羅國優波笈多所。禮足而言。願聞法要優波笈多。見其後身為愛所縛。語言。男子。能受我教當為汝說。答言能。優波笈多將其入山。以神通力化作大樹。語言。汝當上此大樹。是時比丘便即上樹。又於樹下化作大坑。深廣千肘。又語。放汝二脚。即便放脚。又令放一手。即放一手。更放一手。比丘答言。若復放手便墮坑死。優波笈多言。我先共約一切受教。汝云何不受。時身愛即滅。放手而墮。不見樹坑。笈多說法。精進思惟得羅漢果(出阿育王經第十卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In South India there lived a man who went forth under the Buddha's own teaching, yet remained bound by attachment to his own body, anointing it with oil, bathing it in warm water, providing it every kind of fine food — and, bound by this same attachment, could not attain the noble path. He went to Upagupta in the kingdom of Mathurā, bowed at his feet, and asked to hear the essentials of the Dharma. Upagupta, perceiving that this man's own coming life still held bondage through attachment, told him: good son, if you can follow my own instruction, I shall teach you. He agreed. Upagupta took him into the mountains and, through his own supernormal power, conjured a great tree, telling him: climb to the top of this tree. The monk climbed up at once. Upagupta then conjured, beneath the tree, a great pit, a thousand cubits deep and wide, and told him: release your own two feet. He did so. Release one hand as well. He did so. Release your other hand too. The monk answered: if I release my other hand, I shall fall into the pit and die. Upagupta said: I made clear from the start that you would follow every instruction I gave — why do you now refuse? At that moment his own attachment to his body dissolved entirely, and he released his hand and fell — only to find neither tree nor pit had ever truly existed at all. Upagupta then taught him the Dharma, and, applying himself diligently in reflection, he attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of King Aśoka, fascicle 10).

16. Testing a Claim of Arhatship

A monk who believes his own contemplation of impurity has already finished the job is sent by Upagupta to test himself against a wine-seller who makes the identical false claim — and the encounter, rather than proving him right, shows him exactly how far he still has to go.

Chinese Source

摩偷羅國有一男子。從優波笈多出家。聞說不淨觀等。以不淨觀摧伏煩惱。謂已作所作。笈多言。汝當精進勿作懈想。答言。曰我已得阿羅漢。笈多語言。汝見乾陀羅國(翻地持)沽酒女人不。此女人。自言得道。如汝不異。煩惱未斷而自言斷。是增上慢。汝今觀此女人為得道不。答曰。我未能見。欲向彼國。師即聽之。是時比丘。至乾陀羅國。治下土石寺消息。早起著衣持鉢入聚落乞食。時沽酒女取食欲與。比丘見女。婬欲愛心便自取鉢中麨酪。與此女人。女人見之。亦婬欲變心露齒而笑。比丘即入不淨觀。乃至觀身作白骨想。從於是觀得羅漢果(出阿育王經第十卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the kingdom of Mathurā there lived a man who went forth under Upagupta, and, hearing him teach the contemplation of impurity, subdued his own afflictions through this very practice, and believed he had already accomplished all that needed to be done. Upagupta told him: you must continue in diligence, and give rise to no thought of complacency. He answered: but I have already attained arhatship. Upagupta told him: do you know of the wine-selling woman in the kingdom of Gandhāra? This woman likewise claims to have attained the path, no differently than you now claim — yet her own afflictions remain entirely unexhausted, and her own claim is nothing but overweening pride. Go now and observe for yourself whether she has truly attained the path or not. He answered that he could not yet see this for himself, and asked to travel to that same kingdom to find out. His teacher permitted him to go.

The monk traveled to the kingdom of Gandhāra, and, settling for a while at a stone monastery there, rose early one morning, robed himself, took up his bowl, and entered the village to beg. The wine-selling woman, taking up food to give him, was seen by the monk — and, at the sight of her, a lustful, desirous thought arose within him, and he himself took hold of the roasted grain and cream in his own bowl, offering it to this woman instead. The woman, seeing this, likewise felt her own heart shift toward desire, and, baring her teeth, smiled at him. The monk, at that very moment, entered the contemplation of impurity, observing his own body down to a vision of bare bones — and, through this same contemplation, attained arhatship at last (Source: Sūtra of King Aśoka, fascicle 10).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Upagupta's own diagnostic method here is indirect to the point of risk — rather than telling the monk outright that his claim of arhatship is premature, he sends him toward the exact temptation his own practice has not yet been tested against, trusting the encounter itself to reveal what argument could not. That the monk's own desire actually stirs before it dissolves is the story's own quiet acknowledgment that "I have already accomplished all that needed to be done" was, in this case, simply untrue — genuine attainment arrives here only after, not instead of, a real lapse.

17. The Pravāraṇā Confession

The Buddha, whom the compendium insists is himself already entirely without fault, still performs the formal confession alongside the assembled sangha at the end of the rains retreat — as an example for the eighteen thousand monks who attain arhatship that very day.

Chinese Source

佛在舍衛國。夏居三月。以至新歲。告阿難言。汝擊揵搥。揵搥聲音遍一佛國。地獄餓鬼畜生一切病苦。聞音悉除皆得安隱。於時佛言。比丘汝等宜起行舍羅籌。各各相對悔過自責。相謝眾失所犯非法。各忍和同淨身口心。令無餘穢。時諸比丘即受佛教。事訖還坐。時佛慈愍。因從坐起。向諸比丘。叉手而言。雖無上尊至真無失。當可施行以為訓誨。時佛遷延懺謝聖眾。矜愍一切還就草蓐。佛適復坐聖眾亦然。各就故位。時萬比丘得成道迹。八千比丘得阿羅漢(明自恣受臘事出新歲經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Śrāvastī, observing the rains retreat for three months until the new year arrived. He told Ānanda: strike the gong. The sound of the gong reached throughout the entire buddha-land, and every being suffering in hell, among the hungry ghosts, or among the animals, hearing this same sound, found their own suffering entirely removed, and gained peace. The Buddha then said: monks, you should now rise and carry out the formal proceeding of the tally-sticks, each facing another in turn, repenting and reproaching yourselves, and apologizing to one another for whatever faults or improper conduct you have committed — bearing with one another in harmony, purifying your own body, speech, and mind, leaving nothing unclean behind. The assembled monks, receiving the Buddha's own instruction, carried this out, and, once finished, returned to their own seats. The Buddha, in his own compassion, then rose from his own seat, and, with joined palms, addressed the assembled monks: though the Unsurpassed, Honored One, the truly accomplished, is himself entirely without fault, I too shall carry out this same practice, as an example and teaching for you all — and the Buddha then likewise made his own confession and apology before the noble assembly, out of compassion for all, before returning to his own simple seat. Once the Buddha had resumed his seat, the noble assembly did likewise, each returning to their own former place. At that time ten thousand monks attained a glimpse of the path, and eight thousand monks attained arhatship (this account of the pravāraṇā ceremony and the acceptance of a further year's seniority is drawn from the Sūtra of the New Year).

18. The Monk Reborn as a Dung-Beetle

A monk so absorbed in eating and sleeping that he loses track of day and night is snapped awake by the Buddha himself just seven days before his own death, and told of the two hundred thousand years he once spent as a dung-beetle, a snail, and a wood-boring insect for exactly this same habit.

Chinese Source

佛在祇樹精舍。諸比丘。勤修道業除棄陰蓋。有一比丘。飽食入室閉房靜眠。受身快意不觀非常。無復晝夜。却後七日其命將終。佛愍傷之。即入其室彈指寤之。為說妙偈。驚悟稽首作禮。佛告比丘汝本宿命。惟衛佛時曾得出家。貪身利養不念經戒。飽食却眠不惟非常。命終魂神生翁蟲中。積五萬歲。壽盡復為螺蜯之蟲樹中蠹蟲。各五萬歲。此四品蟲。生長冥中貪身愛命。樂處幽隱為家不喜光明。一眠之時百歲乃覺。連綿羅網不求出要。今始罪畢。得為沙門。如何睡眠不知厭足。比丘聞已慚怖自責。五蓋即除。成阿羅漢(出法句譬喻第三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt at the Jetavana monastery, where the assembled monks diligently cultivated the path, casting off the hindrances. One monk, however, having eaten his fill, shut himself in his own room and slept quietly, indulging his own body's comfort, giving no thought to impermanence, losing all track of day and night — and, seven days later, his own life was about to end. The Buddha, taking pity on him, entered his own room and snapped his fingers to wake him, then taught him a wondrous verse. Startled awake, the monk bowed in reverence. The Buddha told him: in a past life, in the time of the buddha Vipaśyin, you once went forth as a monk, but, coveting your own body's comfort, gave no thought to the scriptures or precepts, eating your fill and sleeping without ever considering impermanence — and, at the end of that life, your own spirit was reborn among dung-beetles, remaining there for fifty thousand years; when that life ended, you were reborn in turn as a snail, then as a wood-boring insect in a tree, each for another fifty thousand years. These four kinds of creature are born and grow in darkness, coveting their own bodies and cherishing their own lives, delighting in hidden, secluded places as their own home, disliking the light — sleeping so deeply that a hundred years might pass before waking, entangled endlessly in their own net, never seeking release. Only now has this same sin finally been exhausted, and you have become a monk once more — how, then, can you still sleep without ever knowing when enough is enough? The monk, hearing this, felt shame and fear, and reproached himself — and the five hindrances dissolved at once, and he attained arhatship (Source: Dharmapada, Sūtra of Similes, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own precise accounting — fifty thousand years each as four separate low creatures, two hundred thousand years total — turns an ordinary, almost sympathetic failing into something the monk can suddenly see at its true scale, a habit that has already cost him an inconceivable span of time before this present, brief human life even began. The urgency of the warning is sharpened by its own timing: had the Buddha not intervened in this exact window, seven days before death, the same pattern was apparently already positioned to repeat itself once more.

19. The Seven-Headed Demon and the True Deep Pit

Unable to keep a chronically sleepy monk awake through ordinary teaching, Upagupta conjures a seven-headed demon suspended in midair — and, once the monk's own terror has done its work, explains that the demon itself is nothing next to the true deep pit of birth, old age, sickness, and death.

Chinese Source

摩偷羅國。有一男子。依優波笈多出家。常好眠睡。有時說法睡眠不聞。教樹下禪亦復睡眠。笈多以神通力。於其四邊化作深坑深一千肘。時比丘睡覺。即便驚怖。時優波笈多復化作路。令其得行。時此比丘。隨路而出。往優波笈多所。優波笈多令其更去。比丘答言。彼有深坑。優波笈多言。此深坑小生死。深坑大所謂生老病死憂悲苦惱。若人不知四諦。則墮其中。比丘聞之。復往樹下加趺而坐。心每思惟恐墮深坑。不復睡眠。以怖畏故思惟精進。除諸煩惱。得阿羅漢果。(出阿育王經第九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the kingdom of Mathurā there lived a man who went forth under Upagupta, constantly given to sleep — whenever the Dharma was taught, he slept through it and heard nothing; even when instructed to meditate beneath a tree, he still fell asleep there as well. Upagupta, through his own supernormal power, conjured a deep pit on every side of him, a full thousand cubits deep. When the monk woke from his own sleep, he was seized with terror — at which Upagupta then conjured a path, allowing him to walk free. The monk followed this path out and went to Upagupta, who told him to go back again. The monk answered: there is a deep pit there. Upagupta said: this deep pit is but a small matter compared to the true deep pit — namely, birth, old age, sickness, death, grief, and suffering; whoever does not know the four noble truths falls into that very pit instead. The monk, hearing this, returned once more beneath the tree and sat cross-legged, his mind ever mindful, fearing he might fall into that deep pit — and, no longer sleeping, applying himself diligently through this very fear, extinguished every affliction and attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of King Aśoka, fascicle 9).

20. The Elephant Who Ate Too Much

An arhat, once a female elephant that carried the Buddha's relics, calls the nuns together to prove a point about digestion — invoking, as her own supporting evidence, a king so overfed his own body swelled visibly before the Buddha's own eyes.

Chinese Source

有一牸象。名曰磨荼。載佛舍利至罽賓國。以此善根死生人中。出家得阿羅漢果。因食飯一斛乃般涅槃。告諸比丘尼汝等集會。我當自說所得勝法。諸比丘尼不信其言。復語尼曰。莫生不信。前世為牸象。捨彼象身即得此身。能食飯一斛五斗。而食一斛。乃至廣說。如是等雖是多食。而是易滿。曾聞波斯匿王。食飯二斛。是彼功德因緣故。因一根粳米。一莖苷蔗。日日長生。爾許飯食故身體肥大。以此大身往詣佛所。佛問。大王。身體肥大得無疲耶。時波斯匿心生慚愧。具向佛說世尊說偈。

亦不遭苦受　易消而增壽
時波斯匿王。聞佛所說。漸漸減食。乃至後時唯食一㪶(出阿毘曇毘婆沙第二十二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was once a female elephant named Mudgā, who carried the Buddha's own relics to the kingdom of Kaśmīra; through this same root of goodness, she died and was reborn as a human, went forth, and attained arhatship. Because she had once, in a single meal, eaten a full bushel of rice, she then entered final nirvāṇa, and told the assembled nuns: gather together, for I wish to speak of the excellent teaching I myself have attained. The nuns did not believe her at first — but she told them further: do not doubt this. In a past life I was a female elephant; giving up that elephant's body, I gained this present one, capable of eating a bushel and five pecks of rice at a single meal, though I ate only one bushel — and she explained this at length. Though such an amount is indeed a great deal, she said, it is still easily digested. I have also heard that King Prasenajit himself once ate two full bushels at a single meal — for it was through the very same merit and causes that, from a single grain of rice or a single stalk of sugarcane, his own body grew larger with each passing day, until, having eaten so much food, his own body grew heavy and swollen, and he went in this state to see the Buddha. The Buddha asked him: great king, is your own body's swelling not a source of fatigue for you? King Prasenajit, filled with shame, explained the whole matter to the Buddha, and the World-Honored One spoke this verse:

"One should always keep right mindfulness, knowing when to stop in eating — then one suffers no affliction from it, digesting easily and gaining longer life."

King Prasenajit, hearing the Buddha's own teaching, gradually reduced his own eating, until, in time, he ate only a single measure at each meal (Source: Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra, fascicle 22).

21. The Buddha Bathes a Dying Monk

Skipping a wealthy donor's own invitation to instead attend a sick, neglected monk himself, the Buddha personally cleans his soiled bedding, washes his body, and dresses him — then tells the assembled sangha that from this day forward, attending the sick is to be treated as attending the Buddha's own body.

Chinese Source

佛在舍衛國。有一長者。請佛及僧。時比丘往。如來不往。遣信取食。有二因緣。一者欲與諸天說法。二者瞻視病人。是時世尊遍觀比丘。皆悉受請。即取鑰母開一房門。見一比丘。抱患頓篤臥大小便不能轉側。世尊問曰。汝有何患。比丘曰。受性闇鈍恒懷懈慢。初不勸他瞻視餘人。是故今日無看我者。今實孤窮所怙無處。爾時世尊。慈憐躬抱。除去不淨。湔浣坐具。水洗其身。更與著衣。敷新坐具。還置房中。躬自案摩。告比丘曰。汝不加勤求增上法。死後便當更受劇苦。漸與說法勸使勇猛。乃告諸僧。汝等比丘。無父母兄弟。亦無姊妹宗族。不相瞻視此非其宜。我法齊正上下和從。自今為始。弟子侍師如事父母。至死不捨。師看弟子視如己息。隨時將息至死不捨。師徒相慈流于永劫。所有什物平等分布。設無什物當廣分衛。有瞻病者則瞻我身。所獲功德亦無差降。時病比丘便自思惟。即捨形壽。便得無餘(出佛看比丘病不受長者請經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Śrāvastī. An elder invited the Buddha and the sangha to a meal; the monks went, but the Tathāgata himself did not, sending instead for his own portion to be brought to him, for two reasons: first, he wished to teach the devas the Dharma; second, he wished to attend to a sick monk. The World-Honored One, surveying the assembled monks, found that all had already been accounted for in the invitation, and so took up the key himself and opened the door of one particular room, where he found a monk lying gravely ill, unable even to turn himself, soiled with his own waste. The World-Honored One asked him: what ails you? The monk answered: I am by nature dull and given to laziness, and have never once encouraged others to attend to the sick in turn — it is for this very reason that no one now attends to me; I am truly alone and destitute, with nowhere left to turn.

The World-Honored One, moved by compassion, embraced him with his own arms, cleared away the soiled bedding, washed his own body with water, dressed him in fresh clothing, laid out a new mat, and returned him to his own room, personally massaging his limbs. The Buddha told him: because you did not diligently seek the higher Dharma, you will, after death, suffer even more severe pain than this. He then gradually taught him the Dharma, urging him toward courage and diligence, and told the assembled sangha: you monks have no father, mother, brothers, or sisters, no family of your own here — to fail to attend to one another in illness is simply improper. My own Dharma holds all equal, high and low alike, in mutual harmony — from this day forward, let disciples attend upon their own teachers as they would their own parents, never abandoning them even unto death; let teachers regard their own disciples as their own children, tending to them as the season requires, never abandoning them even unto death — teacher and disciple showing one another such compassion across every age to come. Whatever provisions exist should be shared equally among all; and, should none exist, then all should go out together to beg for more. Whoever attends to the sick attends, in effect, to my own body — and the merit gained is no less for it. The sick monk, hearing this, reflected within himself, and, at that very moment, gave up his own body and life, and attained nirvāṇa without remainder (Source: Sūtra of the Buddha Attending a Sick Monk Rather Than Accepting an Elder's Invitation).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The monk's own explanation for his abandonment is unflinching — he was neglected because he himself had never once encouraged others to tend the sick, a fault the story does not soften even as it lets the Buddha treat him with total tenderness regardless. The new rule the Buddha establishes afterward — "whoever attends to the sick attends, in effect, to my own body" — converts an act of ordinary compassion into a formal equivalence with devotion to the Buddha himself, closing off, at the level of doctrine, any future version of the same neglect this one monk suffered.

22. Fear of the Demon, Fear of Sleep

Startled awake by a conjured seven-headed demon, a chronically sleepy monk is told plainly which of the two dangers actually deserves his fear: being killed outright would end this cycle of birth and death, while being destroyed by sleep merely prolongs it forever.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

In the kingdom of Mathurā there lived a man who went forth under Upagupta, much given to sleep. Upagupta conjured a seven-headed demon, gripping a tree branch, its body suspended in midair. The monk, seeing this, woke with a start, seized with great terror, and fled back to his own former place. Upagupta told him: return to your own seat for meditation. The monk answered: master, there is a demon in that grove, seven heads before me, gripping a tree branch, suspended in the air — it is truly terrifying. Upagupta said: this demon is not worth fearing at all — sleep itself is the truly fearsome thing. Should a monk be killed by such a demon, he would not thereby remain bound to birth and death; but should he be destroyed by sleep instead, birth and death continue for him without end. The monk returned to his own seat for meditation, and, seeing the demon there once more, feared it enough that he no longer dared to sleep — and, applying himself diligently in reflection from that time on, attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of King Aśoka, fascicle 10).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry and the pit-conjuring episode a few entries earlier are essentially the same lesson delivered to the same kind of student through a different prop — a fabricated external danger used to interrupt a habit that no ordinary teaching could reach. Upagupta's own comparative ranking of the two threats is worth pausing on: he does not simply say the demon is less dangerous than it looks, but that it would, if real, be preferable to the habit it is being used to cure — a rare moment in this compendium where death is treated as the lesser evil.

24. The Matricide and Mañjuśrī's Double

A man convinced he has doomed himself to hell for endangering his own mother watches a stranger appear, murder his own conjured parents before his eyes, and go to the Buddha in far greater anguish than his own — only to learn, once he follows this same stranger's example, that the entire scene was staged by Mañjuśrī to teach him what mind itself actually is.

Ru-Yi Translation

Along a road there lived a man who had killed his own mother, and now dwelt beneath a tree, weeping and grieving in torment, crying out again and again in self-reproach for having committed so monstrous a crime, certain that killing his own mother would send him straight to hell. Though this was so, he was nonetheless still fit to be guided toward proper practice — and the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī, seeing that he was ripe for deliverance, transformed himself into another man, leading a conjured father and mother along the road, and, drawing near the matricide, stopped not far away.

The conjured parents told their own son: this is the proper road. The son answered: it is not — and the two fell into a dispute over it. The son, in a sudden fury, killed his own conjured parents on the spot, then wept in bitter, unbearable anguish, and went to the very man who had killed his own mother, saying: I have now killed both my own father and mother — I shall surely fall into hell for it. Weeping, he cried out: what am I to do, and what plan can I devise?

The matricide, reflecting to himself, thought: this man who has just arrived has killed both his own parents, while I myself have endangered only my own mother — his own foolish blindness makes his sin far graver than mine; knowing that his own retribution will be heavier, my own now feels somehow lighter by comparison. Grieving and bitter, the newly arrived man declared aloud: I shall go now to the Able and Benevolent Buddha himself — for those without any other refuge, the Buddha provides refuge; for those seized with terror, the Buddha alone can offer comfort. Whatever the Buddha teaches me, I shall faithfully follow. The transformed man then set out on the road ahead, weeping as he went, and the matricide followed close behind him, thinking: as this man now repents, so too shall I — my own sin is surely lighter than his, which is so very grave.

The transformed man reached the Buddha, bowed his head to the ground, and said: World-Honored One, I have committed the gravest of crimes, killing both my own parents — I have transgressed this most serious of sins. The Buddha told the transformed man: excellent, excellent — you have spoken with utmost sincerity, without any deception, your own words matching your own deeds; you have come before the Tathāgata and spoken with complete honesty, without duplicity, and without harming yourself through denial either. You should now examine for yourself, through the Dharma, with what mind you actually harmed your own parents — was it with a past mind, a future mind, or a present mind? Your own past mind has already ceased; your present mind, too, has already passed on elsewhere, with no fixed location, no direction, unknowable in its own whereabouts; and your future mind has not yet arrived at all, having no place yet gathered, not yet turned back, having neither gone nor returned. Know, then, that mind itself does not stand fixed within the body, nor does it arise from outside it, nor does it exist within any external object; it dwells neither between the two, nor does it come to rest in either (such teaching continues at much greater length than can be recorded here).

The transformed man exclaimed: never before have I heard such a thing! The Tathāgata has become the truly, perfectly awakened one, fully understanding that the Dharma-realm itself has no doer, no receiver, no one born, and no one who attains extinction, dependent on nothing at all — I now wish to go forth myself. Through the Buddha's own power, he became a monk on the spot, receiving the full precepts. The Buddha said: welcome, monk — and the transformed man, now a monk, said to the Buddha: World-Honored One, I have now attained supernormal power, and wish to enter final nirvāṇa. Through the Buddha's own majestic power, this transformed monk rose four zhang nine chi above the ground, and, in the very sky, entered his own extinction, fire streaming from his own body, consuming it entirely.

The one who had truly killed his own mother, witnessing this, reflected: this man became a monk and thereby attained extinction — I shall follow his own example — and went to the Buddha, bowed at his sacred feet, and said: I too have committed this very crime, endangering my own mother's life. The Buddha said: excellent — spoken with utmost sincerity, without deception, your own words matching your own deeds. At this, the very fire of hell itself began to emerge from every pore of this man's own body, his pain extreme, with no one to save him. He cried out to the Buddha: I am now being burned — only you, heaven of heavens, can save me. The World-Honored One extended his own golden-colored arm and placed it upon the man's own head, and the fire went out at once; seeing the Tathāgata's own body, adorned with every mark of excellence, his pain ceased and he found peace.

He then asked to become a monk himself, and the Buddha granted this at once, teaching him the four noble truths. Hearing this teaching, he dissolved every defilement and attained the pure Dharma-eye, practicing the Dharma's own teaching until he attained the fruit of non-returning, and, in time, arhatship as well. He then asked the Buddha's leave to enter final nirvāṇa, and the Buddha consented. The monk rose into the sky, four zhang nine chi above the ground, and, his own body bursting into flame, consumed himself entirely — while hundreds of thousands of devas and humans gathered in the sky above to make offerings.

Śāriputra then said to the Buddha: through the Tathāgata's own compassionate grace and the Vinaya he has taught, even a matricide has now been delivered — only the Tathāgata, and the great bodhisattvas, can perceive the very root of every being's own capacity and deliver them accordingly; this lies beyond the reach of any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha. The Buddha said: it is exactly so (Source: Sūtra of the Mind's Own Original Purity).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Mañjuśrī's own device works entirely through comparison rather than direct address — the matricide never receives a single word aimed at him personally until the very end, and yet the sight of a stranger's own apparently far graver crime, followed step by step through confession, teaching, ordination, and self-immolation, does more to move him than any sermon could have. His own recurring, almost superstitious calculation — "my own sin is surely lighter than his" — is never corrected by the story, which lets him proceed on a comforting misapprehension all the way to genuine liberation; the compendium seems entirely untroubled that his own path to the truth ran through a falsehood he never stopped believing.

25. The Two-Hundred-Year-Old Beggar

A dying, destitute old man, turned away at the gate by Śakra and Brahmā themselves, finally reaches the Buddha and asks only to die as a monk — and learns that his own present poverty and his own extraordinary lifespan trace back to the same past-life act: torturing a begging monk for seven days, then saving that same monk's life from bandits soon after.

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there lived a poor old man, over two hundred years old, his eyebrows grown long and fine, his ears reaching past the top of his own head, his teeth even as a row of shells, his hands hanging past his own knees — an appearance that, on close inspection, seemed to bear some auspicious mark, though he lived in the most abject poverty and hardship, his clothing failing to cover his own body, his whole frame exposed, his own belly constantly empty, able only barely to move, showing the faintest signs of life. He wished to go and see the Buddha, but Śakra and Brahmā, guarding the gate, had been instructed not to admit him. The old man cried out loudly: though I am poor, the very lowest among the people, I have had, after a thousand years, this rare fortune of finally encountering a buddha — I wish to ask about sin and merit, and to seek release from every suffering; I have heard that the World-Honored One's own compassion reaches everyone equally, that none fail to receive his own grace — why, then, am I alone turned away? This surely slanders the sage's own true intent.

The Buddha called for him to be admitted. Crawling forward inch by inch, he bowed before the Buddha, grief and joy mingling within him, describing his own extreme poverty and suffering, and his own long-held wish, ten years in the making, to see the Buddha even once — yet, his own strength now so failing that he could barely move forward or back, he feared only that his own life might end and defile the sacred gate itself before he could arrive, adding further to his own sin. He begged the honored one, in his own compassion, not to deny his original wish — for even should he die having achieved this much, he would have no further regret; he asked only to be allowed to die swiftly, and begged the Buddha's own gracious favor in this.

The Buddha said: a person's own present birth arises through many conditions — let me explain this to you. In a past life, you were born into a great and powerful kingdom, in the household of King Mingzhi, "Wise and Brilliant," where you were the crown prince, arrogant and noble beyond the ordinary, cherished above by your own parents and served below by your own ministers and people — and, relying on this, you grew careless and contemptuous of others, your gaze haughty and your bearing overbearing and remote. Your own treasure, in the hundreds of millions, was in truth all drawn from your own people, whom you taxed and burdened while they themselves grew poor and destitute, and you thought only of accumulating wealth, never once giving any of it away.

At that time there lived a poor, humble monk named Jingzhi, "Quiet Resolve," who came from a distant land seeking not much at all — only a monastic robe. Yet you would not even receive him, treating him with contempt; you neither gave him the robe he sought, nor offered him any food, and simply let him sit before you, refusing even to let him leave, for seven days and nights, deprived entirely of water and food, until, showing only the faintest signs of life remaining, you were delighted at this, and gathered a crowd to come and watch, taking it as the greatest amusement. Your own attendant ministers warned you: this monk is compassionate and reverent, his own virtue and the path complete within him — cold cannot chill him, hunger cannot starve him; he came here begging only to make merit for you. Since you will not give him anything, why persist in tormenting him so? We beg you to release him, lest you incur sin from it. The crown prince answered: who is this man, falsely claiming such virtue? Let us test him a little further — only enough to keep him from actually dying — then release him; there is nothing to worry about. He was sent away, driven out of the kingdom entirely.

Once past the border, ten-odd li out, he encountered starving bandits, who wished to kill and eat him. The monk told them: I am but a starving, freezing monk, my body wasted to bone, my flesh foul and unfit to eat — you would only kill me for nothing, gaining no benefit from it at all. The bandits answered: we ourselves have gone hungry for days, eating nothing but dirt — though you are thin, you are still flesh; we shall not release you, but you must simply die. This standoff continued for some time, until the crown prince, learning of it, rushed there himself and said: because I myself could not even give him a robe and food, must he now be killed by bandits instead? The bandits, seeing the crown prince, bowed their heads in apology and released the monk.

That monk was Maitreya himself — and it is for this very reason that you now suffer this present poverty, the fruit of your own past stinginess; and it is likewise because you once saved that same monk's own life that you have been granted so long a lifespan. Sin and merit each answer as surely as an echo answers a sound.

The old man said: what is past is past — I ask only that my present wish now be fulfilled; grant me, even as my own life hangs by a thread, to become a monk, that in future lives I might always attend upon the Buddha's own side. The Buddha said: excellent. At once the old man's hair and beard fell to the ground, a monastic robe appeared upon his body, his own vital strength returning at once, his ears and eyes growing sharp and clear; he attained realization on the spot, entering the very gate of samādhi, and, with a verse in praise of the Buddha, bowed and withdrew.

"Long ago I was a pampered son, ignorant of humaneness and righteousness — proud and privileged, indulging my own whims, then a great kingdom's own king.
I believed there was no sin or merit at all, thinking this could last forever — vainly failing to grasp what birth and death truly hold in store, and so, even now, I bear its own retribution.
Yet from that very sin I have now received this blessing, and come to behold the heaven of heavens — freed at last from my own past crime, on the very edge of death I have entered the gate of the Dharma.
Forever free of a stingy, covetous heart, I shall long carry instead the very root of wisdom — life after life, never far from the Buddha, sustained through ten thousand kalpas to come" (Source: Sūtra of the Poor, Old Man).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story pairs its own two causes with almost mathematical precision — cruelty toward one monk explains a lifetime of poverty, while a single, half-hearted act of mercy toward that same monk explains an otherwise inexplicable two-hundred-year lifespan — and neither cause cancels the other out; both run their own course in full, side by side, in the very same body. The old man's own closing request is notable for asking nothing about his own past suffering at all, neither justice nor explanation, but only to die having finally become what he spent ten years and his own last remaining strength trying to reach.

26. The Caṇḍāla Child Who Had Attained Stream-Entry

Upagupta shows a complacent, newly awakened student a caṇḍāla child, himself a stream-enterer, whose body is being devoured by worms — proof that even genuine attainment offers no protection at all against a past failure to keep applying oneself.

比丘問何緣。得須陀洹。而受此苦。笈多答曰。先身出家。眾僧坐禪其為維那。有一羅漢。居此惡病。搔刮作聲。維那語言。惡蟲食汝。而作此聲。即牽臂出言。汝入旃陀羅室去。阿羅漢語維那。汝當精進。莫往生死受苦。是維那即懺悔之。懺悔竟得須陀洹果。不復精進。今小兒是也。牽羅漢出。今生旃陀羅處。受此報也。比丘怖畏勤精進。即得阿羅漢果(出阿育王經第十卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the kingdom of Mathurā there lived a man who went forth under Upagupta. Upagupta taught him the Dharma, and, applying himself diligently in reflection, he attained the fruit of stream-entry, and, through his own freedom from heedlessness, escaped fear of the evil paths of rebirth, destined to be reborn seven times in heaven and seven times among humans, enjoying the pleasures of both realms before finally entering nirvāṇa. Upagupta, perceiving his own state of mind, went together with him into the kingdom of Mathurā, begging from house to house in turn, until they came to the household of a caṇḍāla family, where a caṇḍāla child, who had himself attained the fruit of stream-entry, suffered from a grave illness, his whole body devoured by worms, his own breath foul beyond bearing. Upagupta told his disciple: observe this child — though he has attained the fruit of stream-entry, he still suffers so, and spoke this verse:

"Born into the caṇḍāla caste, delighting still in the pleasures of but three months, ravaging worms devouring his own body — all because craving still holds its own sway.
Birth and death hold nothing truly real within them, no more substantial than a plantain tree's own trunk."

The monk asked what karmic cause had led this child, though he had attained stream-entry, to suffer such affliction. Upagupta answered: in a past life, this child was a monk himself, and, when the assembled sangha sat in meditation, served as their own overseer. An arhat among them, suffering this very same grave illness, scratched at his own body, making a sound as he did — and the overseer told him: worms are eating your own flesh, and this is the sound it makes! — and, seizing his own arm, dragged him bodily out, saying: go to the caṇḍāla quarter! The arhat told the overseer: you should apply yourself diligently, and not go on to suffer such rebirth and such pain yourself. The overseer, hearing this, repented at once, and, through this repentance, attained the fruit of stream-entry — yet, having failed to apply himself further in diligence after that, he is now this very child, born into the caṇḍāla caste for having once dragged that same arhat out by force, and now suffers this present retribution for it.

The monk, filled with fear, applied himself with redoubled diligence, and attained arhatship at once (Source: Sūtra of King Aśoka, fascicle 10).

27. Two Monks, Two Fates

Two monks of equal precept and wisdom, one devoted to begging and giving, the other purely to solitary meditation, reap opposite fortunes in their own next rebirth — the giver supported effortlessly by others, the self-sufficient one left to beg with no one willing to give him anything at all.

Chinese Source

昔有兩比丘。持戒智慧正等。俱得須陀洹道。一比丘但行乞匃。以用布施。飯諸比丘僧及貧窮者。一比丘但坐禪自守。謂行乞匃作布施者言。何以不坐禪。自守空自勤苦。一比丘答言。賢者。佛言。為比丘亦當布施。不則後世貧窮。後二比丘俱得羅漢。迦羅越子作沙門。前世乞匃。今得自守眾人。自持衣食日日與之。其一比丘前世但自守。今為婢子。作沙門乞匃。無有與者。常大飢渴故應持戒布施。不當自守也(出十卷譬喻經第三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there were two monks, equal in their own upholding of the precepts and in wisdom, both having attained the fruit of stream-entry. One monk devoted himself entirely to begging, using what he received to feed the assembled sangha and the poor alike; the other monk simply sat in meditation, keeping to himself. The one who begged and gave asked the other: why do you not go begging as well, keeping only to yourself and toiling for nothing? The other answered: worthy one, the Buddha has indeed taught that a monk, too, should practice giving — otherwise, in a future life, he will be reborn poor. Later, both monks attained arhatship. The one who had begged in his own former life was reborn as the son of a caravan-leader and became a monk; in this present life, he keeps entirely to himself, yet the assembled community provides for him daily with clothing and food of their own accord. The other monk, who had kept only to himself in his own former life, was reborn instead as a servant-woman's son, and, becoming a monk in turn, must beg for his own food, receiving nothing from anyone, constantly hungry and thirsty. One should therefore uphold the precepts and practice giving both together, rather than keeping only to oneself alone (Source: Sūtra of Similes and Parables, in ten fascicles, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both monks, the story is careful to note, attained exactly the same fruits of the path — stream-entry, then arhatship — so that the contrast between their two later lives cannot be attributed to any difference in spiritual attainment at all. What separates a monk effortlessly provided for from one who must beg with no one willing to give is entirely a matter of whether that same attainment was accompanied by giving, a distinction the compendium seems to insist on precisely because "equal in precept and wisdom" might otherwise be mistaken for a guarantee of equal fortune.

28. The Turtle's Own Armor

Twelve years into meditation without success, an ascetic still unable to rid himself of his own wandering desires is shown, by the Buddha in disguise, how a river turtle escapes a hungry otter simply by withdrawing entirely into its own shell.

Chinese Source

佛在世時。有一道人。在於河邊樹下學道。貪想不除。走心散意但念六欲。身靜意遊曾無寧息。十二年中不能得道。佛知可度。化作沙門。往至其所。樹下共宿。須臾月明。有龜從河中出。來至樹下。復有水狗飢行求食。與龜相逢。便欲噉龜。龜縮頭尾及其四脚。藏於甲中不能得噉。水狗小遠。復出頭足行步如故。遂便得免。時化沙門語言比丘。吾念世人不如此龜。有護命之鎧。能令水狗不得其便。世人無智放恣六情。天魔得便殺形壞神去。生死無端輪轉五道。苦惱百千。即為說偈而勸勉之。比丘聞偈貪斷望止。得羅漢道。知化沙門是佛世尊。敬肅正服稽首佛足(出法句經譬喻第二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

While the Buddha still lived, there was an ascetic dwelling beneath a tree by a riverbank, studying the path, yet unable to rid himself of his own covetous thoughts, his mind scattered and wandering, dwelling constantly on the six desires — his body kept still, yet his own mind wandering freely, never truly at rest — and, after twelve years of this, he still could not attain the path. The Buddha, knowing he was ripe for deliverance, transformed himself into a fellow monk and came to lodge with him beneath that same tree. Before long, the moon rose bright, and a turtle emerged from the river, coming to rest beneath the tree; a hungry otter, foraging for food, came upon the turtle and sought to devour it — but the turtle withdrew its head, tail, and all four limbs entirely into its own shell, and the otter could not reach it at all. The otter, moving a short distance off, and the turtle, extending its own head and limbs once more to move as before, thereby escaped harm entirely.

The transformed monk then said to the ascetic: I reflect that people of this world are not even the equal of this very turtle, which possesses armor to protect its own life, keeping the otter from finding any opening at all. People of the world, lacking such wisdom, indulge their own six senses without restraint, and the heavenly Māra finds his own opening there, destroying their form and ruining their own spirit — and so birth and death continue without end, turning endlessly through the five realms, suffering in the hundreds of thousands. He then spoke a verse in further exhortation. The ascetic, hearing the verse, cut off his own craving and let go his own longing, and attained arhatship — only then recognizing that this transformed monk had been the World-Honored One himself, and, with reverent, proper bearing, bowed at the Buddha's own feet (Source: Dharmapada, Sūtra of Similes, fascicle 2).

29–31. Three Miracle Fragments

The fascicle closes with three of the compendium's briefest entries — a soiled hand that splits a wooden pillar, a spirit's face accidentally splashed by an inattentive monk's own urine, and fire streaming from a supposedly difficult monk's own fingers the instant he opens his door to a doubting visitor.

Chinese Source
沙門飯僧污手拭柱柱為之裂二十九

有沙門。作摩波利。飯比丘。分酪蘇著手。以手塗柱。柱即破裂(出譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation
A Monk's Soiled Hand Splits a Pillar

There was a monk who, serving as the meal-distributor for the sangha, apportioned cream and ghee, and, his own hand soiled with it, wiped it upon a pillar — and the pillar split apart at once (Source: Sūtra of Similes and Parables).

沙門小便不彈指尿漈鬼面三十

有一沙門。不彈指來小便。漈清中鬼面。厲鬼大恚。欲殺沙門。沙門持戒。厲鬼隨逐。伺覓其短。不能得便。沙門後作摩波利。有人上此僧物者。即於坐上。得羅漢道(出一卷雜譬喻經)。

A Careless Monk Splashes a Spirit's Face

There was a monk who, urinating without first snapping his fingers as custom required, splashed his own urine into a latrine pit, striking a spirit's own face there. The malevolent spirit, enraged, wished to kill the monk — but the monk, upholding the precepts firmly, could not be caught off guard however closely the spirit pursued and watched for any opening. The monk later became the sangha's own meal-distributor; whoever partook of this same sangha's own provisions, seated there, attained arhatship on the spot (Source: Miscellaneous Similes Sūtra, in one fascicle).

沙門開戶五指火出三十一

有沙門先共學道。同住一堂。一沙門言。此沙門惡性不可共止。捐往他國。還過視之。為得道未。夜到其舍。沙門鑰開召呼前坐。舉五指頭火出外來。沙門即大驚愕。歎其得道(出一卷雜喻經)。

Fire from Five Fingers

There were once fellow students of the path who dwelt together in a single hall. One of them said: this particular fellow monk has too difficult a temperament to live alongside — and left for another kingdom. Returning later to see whether he had attained the path, he arrived at night at his dwelling; the monk, unlocking the door, called him in to sit — and, as he did, fire streamed forth from the tips of his own five fingers. The visiting monk, astonished, praised him for having attained the path (Source: Miscellaneous Similes Sūtra, in one fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

These three fragments, none more than a few lines long, close this fascicle's own long procession of elaborate teaching-devices and past-life explanations with something closer to a set of unexplained rumors — no cause is given for why the pillar splits, why the meal-distributor's own leftovers grant instant arhatship, or why the difficult monk's fingers produce fire, and none of the three even attempts to say. Set against thirty entries that have, almost without exception, traced every outcome back to a precise cause, this closing trio's own silence reads less like an oversight than a final, deliberate reminder that not every mark of sanctity in this tradition arrives already explained.
