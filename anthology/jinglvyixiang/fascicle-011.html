Bodies Manifested, Part 4

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 11 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Bodhisattvas Manifesting Bodies to Fit Capacity, Part 4" (隨機見身下菩薩部第四) — sixteen entries, the majority animal-form past lives of the Buddha: a mountain of flesh that never depletes however much of it beings consume, a lion who tears his own flank to ransom a monkey's stolen children, the founding story of the Deer Park at Sarnath, the Nine-Colored Deer betrayed by the very drowning man he once saved, a goose king ransomed by his own minister's offered life, and a turtle king who carries a shipload of frightened merchants safely to shore.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 11 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 11 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 11 continues fascicle 10's theme of bodies manifested to fit whoever needed reaching, but turns overwhelmingly toward animal-form past lives — lion, elephant, nāga, bear, deer, goose, parrot, sparrow, fish, and turtle each in turn. Sixteen entries are grouped here into thematic clusters, including two pairs of near-identical stories the compendium itself tells twice with different animals in the lead role, while every entry's full text remains intact.

1. Serving Four Sages, Reborn as King

An attendant who fails his four teachers' meal just once, and dies of the shame, is reborn as a prophesied king — while the ascetic whose careless curse caused his death chases a lost sandal halfway across two kingdoms before learning who the king truly is.

Chinese Source

久遠無數劫時。有五仙人。處於山藪。四人為主。一人供給奉事未曾失意採菓汲水進以時節。一日遠採菓漿懈廢眠寐。不以時還。日已過中。四人失食。懷恨可為凶呪。侍者聞之退在樹下。思惟自責執勞歲久。今違四仙時食之供。既失道教不從四等。遂感而死。其足常著七寶之屐。翹足而坐。寶屐墮水而沒一隻。命過之後即生外道。為凶呪子。年十餘歲。與其同輩戲于路側。時有梵志。遇見戲童。人數猥多以遍觀察。見凶呪子特有貴相。應為王者。顏貌殊異於人中上。梵志命曰。爾有王相不宜遊眾。童子答曰。吾凶呪子何有王相。梵志又曰。如吾經典。儀容形體與讖書符合。爾則應之。斯國之王薨殞必禪爾位。童子曰。唯設如仁信當念重恩。梵志言畢。尋遁逃走。出遊他國。後日未幾。王薨絕嗣。娉求賢士以為國胄。使者四布。遙見斯童有異人之姿。輒尋遣人還啟群臣。唯嚴法駕尋來奉迎。群臣百寮莫不踊躍。香湯洗浴五時朝服。寶冠劍帶即位處殿。南面稱制。境土安寧民庶踊悅。於時梵志。仰瞻天文下察地理。知已嗣立。即詣宮門求奉覲王。梵志占謝。呪願曰。如誓審諦矣。王曰。誠哉。當恣所欲。梵志答曰。唯求二願。一曰。飲食進止衣服臥起與王一等。二曰。參誼國事所決同意莫自專也。王曰。善哉。王治以正法不枉萬民。梵志憍恣輕慢重臣。群臣怨忿。俱進諫曰。王尊位高。宜與國臣耆舊參議。偏信乞士。遂令恣慢凌侮群職。隣國聞之將所嗤笑以致寇難。王曰吾與之有誓安可廢耶。臣諫不止。若王食時。勿與之俱。則必改也。王遂可之。伺梵志出則先之食。梵志恚曰。本要云何而先獨食。罵曰。咄凶呪子。但給資糧驅令出國。獨涉遠路觸冐寒暑。疲極憔悴而到他國。詣異梵志家舊與親親。又而問曰。卿何從來何所綜習。業何經典能悉念乎。答曰。吾從遠來飢寒見逼。忘所習誦。梵志心念。此人無所能作。當令田作。輒給奴子及犁牛耕具。梵志耕種苦役奴子。酷令平地走使東西。奴子無聊。欲自投水往到河側。則得一隻七寶之屐。心自念言。梵志困我役使無賴。吾當奉承。以屐上之可獲寬恕。即齎屐還以用上之。梵志欣豫心自念言。此七寶屐其價難訾。吾違王意以屐奉之。愆咎可解。尋還王國。以屐上王。深自陳悔前之罪釁。願垂原赦。王曰。善哉。即內之幔裏。引座坐之。會諸群臣。詔曰。卿等寧見前梵志不耶。答曰。不見。王云。設見當如之何。僉曰。當五毒治之。王出寶屐以示群臣。命梵志出與臣相見。致此異寶當共原之。群臣啟曰。此梵志罪如山如海。獻屐一隻何所施補。若獲一緉罪可除也。王即可之。重逐梵志。令更求一隻。梵志懊惱還故主人。問奴子曰。汝前寶屐本何從得。奴子俱行示得屐處。至于水側遍恣求之不知雙處。奴子捨去。梵志念。此屐必從上流來下。即逆流上行。見大蓮華。流澓洄波魚口銜之。華有千餘葉。梵志心念。雖不得屐以此華上之。儻可解過得復前寵。便復執華。則見四仙人坐於樹下。仙人曰。卿何所從來。答曰。吾失王意。雖獻一屐不足解過。故逆流來求之未獲。仙人告曰。卿為學人當知進退。彼國王者是吾弟子。存侍愛敬同食坐誼。云何一旦罵之凶呪子。罪及誅害。今不相問指示樹下。王先故身為吾侍者。翹脚而終。寶屐墮水一隻著脚。梵志取屐稽首謝過。還到本國續以上之。王即歡喜大臣意解。復其寵位。佛言。王者則吾身是。四仙人者。拘留秦佛。拘那含牟尼佛。迦葉佛。彌勒佛是也。其梵志者調達是也(出五仙人經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Countless kalpas ago, five ascetics dwelt together in the mountain forests, four of them senior teachers and one their attendant, who gathered fruit and drew water for them at every proper hour without fail. One day, having gone far to gather fruit juice, the attendant grew drowsy and fell asleep, failing to return in time; past midday, the four teachers, having missed their meal, grew resentful and cursed him. Overhearing this, the attendant withdrew beneath a tree, reproaching himself for having served faithfully for years only to fail his teachers' meal this once — and, feeling he had thereby lost the very teaching and fallen short of the four boundless states, he died of this very grief. He had always worn sandals of the seven treasures, sitting cross-legged with his feet raised — and, in death, one of these jeweled sandals fell into the water and sank.

Reborn thereafter into a heterodox household, he became the very child of the curse just spoken over him. By ten years old, playing with his companions by the roadside, he was noticed by a passing brahmin, who, examining the many children there, saw in this "cursed child" an unmistakably noble bearing, his features finer than any ordinary person's — and told him that such a face was unfit for common play, for he bore the very signs of a king. The boy protested that he was merely the cursed child, with no royal bearing at all — but the brahmin insisted that his own scriptures' prophetic signs matched the boy's form exactly, and that when the current king of this land died, the throne would surely pass to him. The boy answered only that, should this prove true, he would remember the debt owed — and the brahmin, having said his piece, slipped away at once to another kingdom.

Not long after, the king of that land died without heir, and messengers were sent in every direction seeking a worthy successor; seeing this same boy's extraordinary bearing, they reported back to the ministers at once, and, with full ceremony, came to receive him. The whole court rejoiced; bathed in fragrant water and robed in the five ceremonial vestments, crowned and belted with the royal sword, he ascended the throne and ruled facing south, the realm at peace and its people delighted.

The brahmin, observing the heavens above and the earth below, knew the boy had indeed succeeded to the throne, and came to the palace gate seeking audience. Bowing and invoking his own former prophecy as fulfilled exactly as sworn, he was told by the king that it was indeed true, and that he might ask for whatever he wished. The brahmin asked only two things: that his own food, dress, and daily routine be equal to the king's own in every respect, and that he be consulted as an equal partner in every state decision, never excluded. The king agreed.

The king ruled with true justice, never wronging his own people — but the brahmin, growing arrogant, treated the senior ministers with contempt, and the ministers, resentful, together petitioned the king that so weighty a throne ought to consult its own elder statesmen, not rely solely on this one beggar-ascetic, whose growing arrogance now insulted every office in the court and would soon invite mockery, even invasion, from neighboring kingdoms. The king answered that he had made a solemn vow to the man and could not now break it — but, the ministers persisting, suggested only that the king simply not eat together with the brahmin at mealtimes any longer, which alone might correct his presumption. The king agreed, and thereafter ate ahead of the brahmin whenever he could.

The brahmin, furious, demanded to know why the king now ate alone against their original agreement, and cursed him: "Bah, cursed child!" The king, in response, simply provided him travel funds and had him expelled from the kingdom. Traveling alone through distant lands, enduring cold and heat, exhausted and haggard, the brahmin at last reached another country and went to the house of a fellow brahmin, an old family friend, who asked where he had come from and what scriptures he had mastered. He answered that, driven by hunger and cold along his journey, he had forgotten everything he once knew. His host, concluding the man was now good for nothing else, set him to work in the fields, providing him a servant-boy along with a plow and oxen.

The brahmin worked this servant-boy mercilessly, driving him back and forth across the level ground without rest, until the boy, in despair, resolved to drown himself in the river — and there, by the water, found a single jeweled sandal. Reflecting that the brahmin's own harsh treatment might be softened by such a gift, he brought it back and presented it to him. Delighted, the brahmin reflected that this sandal, worth an incalculable sum, might well be offered to the king to atone for his own past offense — and he returned at once to the king's own country, presenting the sandal and confessing his former fault, begging pardon.

The king said, "Very good," and had him kept concealed behind a curtain, seated there, while he summoned his ministers and asked them: "Have you all seen the former brahmin lately?" They answered no. "And if you were to see him now, what would you do?" They answered they would subject him to every manner of punishment. The king then produced the jeweled sandal to show them, and had the brahmin come out to meet them, saying that since he had brought this rare treasure, he ought to be forgiven. The ministers protested that this brahmin's crimes were as vast as a mountain or an ocean, and that a single sandal could hardly atone for them — only if he produced the matching pair might his crime be excused. The king agreed, and sent the brahmin out once more to find the second sandal.

Distressed, the brahmin returned to his former master's house and asked the servant-boy where he had originally found the sandal; the boy led him back to the very spot by the river, but, searching everywhere, they could not find its pair, and the boy gave up and left. The brahmin, reflecting that the sandal must have floated down from somewhere upstream, followed the current back up the river — and there saw a great lotus, its thousand-odd petals caught in the swirling current, a fish holding it in its mouth. Thinking that even this lotus, though not the sandal itself, might serve as an offering to atone for his fault and regain his former favor, he took hold of it — and there, beneath a tree, saw the four sages seated together.

The sages asked where he had come from, and he explained that he had lost the king's own favor and, a single sandal being insufficient atonement, had come upstream seeking its match, without success. The sages told him: "As a learned man yourself, you ought to know when to advance and when to withdraw. That king is our own former disciple, whom we cherished and honored, sharing food and seating alike with him — how could you have cursed him so carelessly as 'cursed child,' a crime deserving death? We will not question you further, but only point you to that tree there: the king, in a former life, was our own attendant, who died sitting cross-legged, and it was then that one of his jeweled sandals fell into the water and lodged upon his very foot."

The brahmin took up the sandal, bowed in gratitude and apology, and returned to the king's own country to present it. The king rejoiced, and his ministers, satisfied, restored the brahmin to his former place of honor. The Buddha said: that king was myself; the four sages were the buddhas Krakucchanda, Kanakamuni, Kāśyapa, and Maitreya; and that brahmin was Devadatta (Source: Sūtra of the Five Sages).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story hinges on a small, almost bureaucratic irony: a death caused by a single missed meal is repaid, lifetimes later, by a single missing sandal — the exact same structure of "one thing owed, not yet delivered" recurring at both ends of the karmic chain. The brahmin's own long chase after the sandal's twin, ending only when four sages happen to explain what he could never have worked out alone, suggests the compendium's own view that karmic accounts are precise down to a single item, yet only rarely visible to the very people paying them.

2. Uncle and Nephew in the Land of the Naked

Two traveling merchants take opposite views on whether to adapt to a foreign country's own strange customs — one joining the local dancing in white clay and bone ornaments, the other insisting on unbending propriety — and pay entirely different prices for the choice, launching a rivalry that outlasts both their lives.

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩伯叔二人。各齎國貨俱之裸鄉。叔曰。入國隨俗進退尋儀。儒心言遜匿明伴愚。伯曰。禮不可虧德不可退。豈可裸形毀吾舊儀乎。叔曰。先聖景則殞身不殞行。從時初譏亦歎權道之大矣。遂俱之彼。伯曰。爾先入觀其得失即遣使告誠。叔曰。敬諾。旬日之間使反告。伯曰。必從俗儀。伯勃然曰。釋仁從畜豈君子行乎。叔為吾不也。其國俗以月晦十五日夜。周匝為樂。以麻油膏首白土畫身。雜骨纓頸。兩石相扣男子携手。逍遙歌舞。菩薩隨之。國人欣歎。王憐民敬。賓王悉取貨十倍雇之。伯乘車入國。言以嚴法輒違民心。王忿民慢奪財讁捶。叔請乃釋。俱還本國。送叔者被路罵伯者括耳。伯恥怒曰。彼與爾何親。與吾何讎。爾惠吾奪豈非讒言耶。結叔誓曰。自今之後世世相酷終不赦爾。菩薩愴然泣淚誓曰。令吾世世逢佛見法親奉沙門。四恩普覆潤濟眾生。奉伯若已不違斯誓。自斯之後。伯輒剋叔叔常濟之。佛告諸比丘。時叔者是吾身。伯者調達是(出孔雀王經又出無極集經第五卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, the bodhisattva and his own uncle, an elder and a younger kinsman, each carried their country's goods together into a land of naked people. The nephew argued that entering a country meant following its own customs, adapting one's conduct with tact — better to hide one's own refinement and go along with the ignorant than stand apart. The uncle objected that propriety could not be compromised nor virtue abandoned — how could they go naked and ruin their own former dignity? The nephew answered that the ancient sages themselves taught that one might lose one's own life sooner than one's own conduct, yet here it was rather a matter of adapting to the moment — even an initial mockery might later be praised as the wisdom of skillful means. And so the two went in together.

The uncle proposed that the nephew go in first to observe how things stood, then report back through a messenger. The nephew agreed, and within ten days sent word back that one must indeed follow the local custom. The uncle, furious, retorted: "To abandon propriety and follow beasts — is that the conduct of a gentleman? You may do as you like, but I will not."

The custom of that country was to gather every full and new moon night in revelry — heads anointed with sesame oil, bodies painted with white clay, bone ornaments strung about the neck, two stones clacked together while the men joined hands and danced and sang freely together. The bodhisattva-nephew joined in, and the country's people, delighted, praised him; the king, taking pity, favored him, and foreign traders paid him ten times the ordinary rate for his goods. The uncle, however, entered the country still riding in his own carriage, insisting on strict formality, offending the local people's own sensibilities — and the king, angered at this perceived arrogance, had his goods confiscated and had him flogged, only releasing him at the nephew's own pleading.

Returning together to their own country, the nephew was seen off along the road with praise, while the uncle was mocked and had his ears boxed. Ashamed and furious, the uncle demanded: "What kinship do you have with these people, and what grievance with me, that you should favor them and see me robbed — is this not slander on your part?" And he swore an oath against his own nephew: "From this day forward, life after life, I will torment you without mercy." The bodhisattva, grieved, wept and vowed in turn: "Life after life, may I encounter buddhas, see the Dharma, and personally serve the monastic sangha, that the four kindnesses may cover and benefit all beings everywhere — and though my uncle wrong me, let me never break this very vow." From that time forward, the uncle has repeatedly done the nephew harm, and the nephew has repeatedly saved him regardless. The Buddha told the monks: that nephew was myself; that uncle was Devadatta (Source: Sūtra of the Peacock King; also found in the Collection on the Perfections, fascicle 5).

3. The Mountain of Flesh

A great king, having divided his own kingdom among six sons unwilling to renounce the world on any other terms, goes forth to practice — and, when their resulting wars bring the whole land to famine, throws down his own body as a mountain of flesh that only grows larger the more of it starving beings consume.

Chinese Source

過去無量阿僧祇劫。爾時此界名無垢須彌。人壽百歲。有佛出世。號香蓮花。般涅槃後像法之中。我於爾時。作大強力轉輪聖王。號難沮壞。王閻浮提。千子具足。我悉勸化。令發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。欲於像法出家修道。熾然增益佛之遺法。唯除六子。不肯出家發菩提心。我以善言說語。終不出家。我復重問。令發無上道心。六子答言。若能與我閻浮提者。然後當發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。我聞是已心生歡喜。作是思惟。我今已化閻浮提人。今當分此閻浮提。以為六分。與此六子。令其得發無上道心。然後我當出家修道。即分閻浮提為六分以賜六子。尋便出家。爾時六子。各相違戾抄掠攻伐。爾時一切閻浮提內。苗稼不登人民飢餓。水雨不時諸樹枯悴。不生華實。鳥獸皆飢。其身熾然。我於爾時捨己身體肌膚血肉。以施眾生令其飽滿。我於爾時。自投其身。以願力故即成肉山。高一由旬。縱廣正等。是時人民。飛鳥禽獸。始於是時。噉肉飲血。以本願故。於是中分增益廣大其身。乃至高千由旬。縱廣正等亦千由旬。其邊自然而生人頭。髮毛眼耳鼻口脣舌具足而有。彼諸頭中各各有聲。而唱是言。諸眾生等。各各自恣隨意取用。飲血噉肉。取其目耳鼻舌齒等皆令滿足。然後悉令發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。或發聲聞辟支佛心。或求天上人中富樂。以本願故身無損減。乃至萬歲。閻浮提內。人及諸鬼神。飛鳥禽獸。皆悉充足。汝今當知。我於往昔萬歲之中。所捨無量無邊阿僧祇身體。血肉給施無量無邊悉令飽足。乃至一念不生悔心。如是次第遍滿十方如恒河沙等諸佛世界。捨身血肉給施眾生悉令飽足。為檀波羅蜜(出悲華經第九卷過去香蓮花佛世界經大同但不言菩薩耳)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Countless incalculable kalpas ago, when this world was named Stainless Sumeru and human life spanned a hundred years, a buddha named Sugandhapadma, "Fragrant Lotus," arose in the world. After his own nirvāṇa, in the age of the semblance Dharma, I was then a mighty wheel-turning sage king named Durjaya, "Unconquerable," ruling all of Jambudvīpa with a thousand sons of my own. I converted every one of them to give rise to the resolve for unsurpassed, perfect awakening, wishing myself to go forth and cultivate the path during this same semblance age, to further strengthen the Buddha's own remaining teaching — save six sons alone, who refused to give rise to the bodhi-resolve or go forth however I pleaded with them.

Asking them again what would move them to this resolve, these six answered only that if I would give them Jambudvīpa itself, they would then give rise to the unsurpassed resolve. Delighted at even this qualified answer, I reflected that, having already converted the whole of Jambudvīpa's people, I might as well now divide the continent into six parts, grant one to each of these sons, and let them attain the resolve on these terms before I myself went forth. I divided Jambudvīpa into six and bestowed it upon my six sons, then went forth at once.

The six sons soon fell into conflict, raiding and warring against one another, until the whole of Jambudvīpa suffered failed harvests and starvation, rain no longer fell in season, trees withered without flower or fruit, and every bird and beast went hungry, their own bodies wasting with hunger's fire. I then gave up my own flesh, skin, and blood to feed these starving beings to their fill — throwing my own body down, which, through the power of my original vow, became a mountain of flesh a full yojana high and equally wide. From that moment, people, birds, and beasts alike began to eat this flesh and drink this blood — and, through that same vow, the mountain only grew larger the more it was consumed, until it reached a thousand yojanas in height and breadth alike, its very edges sprouting human heads complete with hair, eyes, ears, nose, mouth, lips, and tongue, each head calling out in its own voice: "All beings, take freely as you wish — drink this blood, eat this flesh, take these eyes, ears, nose, tongue, and teeth until you are fully satisfied" — and then urging each of them toward the resolve for unsurpassed, perfect awakening, or, failing that, toward the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha path, or at least toward rebirth in heaven or human wealth and happiness.

Through the power of that same original vow, my body never diminished, sustaining, across ten thousand years, every human being, spirit, bird, and beast throughout Jambudvīpa to their complete satisfaction. Know that across those ten thousand years I gave away countless, boundless, incalculable bodies of flesh and blood to feed countless, boundless beings to their fill, without a single thought of regret arising even once — and, in just this way, throughout the ten directions, in buddha-worlds as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, I have given my own body, blood, and flesh to feed beings to their fill, as the perfection of giving (Source: Sūtra of Compassionate Lotus Flower, fascicle 9; largely the same account also appears in the Sūtra of the Buddha-World of Fragrant Lotus of the Past, though without naming the bodhisattva directly).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The mountain's own strange growth — larger the more it is eaten rather than smaller — turns the ordinary economics of giving inside out, and the story is explicit that this defies nothing but the "original vow's" own power holding it open. Read against fascicle 10's own King Ākāśaviśuddha, whose body likewise vows itself into a bottomless mountain of flesh, the compendium seems to keep returning to this same image — unlimited giving made structurally possible, not merely willed — as its preferred way of depicting a generosity too large for ordinary cause and effect to constrain.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The turtle's warning is not proven wrong so much as proven true and overridden anyway — the compendium does not let the bodhisattva save the drowning man out of naivety about human nature, but out of a deliberate choice that compassion still applies even to the one creature aboard the boat most likely to betray it. That the story then lets the betrayal happen in full, complete with prison and near-execution, before finally vindicating the bodhisattva's own choice, suggests its real interest is not in whether the turtle's cynicism was justified — it plainly was — but in whether compassion extended anyway, at real cost, is still the right thing to have done. The compendium answers yes.

The Six-Tusked White Elephant

Subhūti asked the Buddha: if a bodhisattva has truly accomplished roots of goodness, why would he ever be reborn as an elephant or horse? The Buddha answered: a bodhisattva, though truly possessed of merit and accomplished roots of goodness, takes on animal form for the benefit of beings, incurring none of an animal's own karmic guilt by doing so. Even within an animal's body, a bodhisattva shows compassion toward his own attackers in a way neither arhats nor pratyekabuddhas can match — for though an arhat or pratyekabuddha, attacked by an enemy, will not retaliate, neither can he actively cherish and provide for that same attacker as a bodhisattva does.

Once, as a six-tusked white elephant, the bodhisattva was shot in the chest by a hunter's poisoned arrow. He wrapped the hunter in his own trunk to shield him from the other elephants' retaliation, telling his own mate: "You are a bodhisattva's own wife — why give rise to such hatred? This hunter's fault lies in his own afflictions, not in the man himself; when I attain unsurpassed, perfect awakening, I will extinguish those very afflictions — just as one treats the demon possessing a man, not the man himself." He then calmly asked the hunter why he had shot him, and, told the man needed his own tusks, went to a rock and broke them off himself to give him, blood and flesh tearing away without complaint, then provided him food and showed him the way home — a compassion neither arhat nor pratyekabuddha possesses.

Asked why the bodhisattva does not simply teach the Dharma in human form rather than taking on such animal bodies, the Buddha answered: sometimes beings, seeing a human teacher, do not believe or accept the teaching, yet on seeing an animal expound the very same Dharma, are moved to faith and joy and accept its instruction. A bodhisattva, wishing to perfect great compassion and act it out concretely rather than merely speak of it, does this precisely because beings, astonished at what they see, are thereby led to enter the path (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 93).

Long ago, the bodhisattva and Ānanda, having both finished serving out a former karmic debt, were reborn together as nāgas. Not long after emerging from the water, they met a venomous viper on the road, who, seeing the two snakes and harboring murderous intent, spat its venom at them. One of the two wished to kill the viper in turn, but the other, in compassionate forbearance, restrained him, saying: "A truly noble being forgives even the foolish; to forbear what seems unbearable is itself the sages' own teaching" — and spoke this verse:

The one snake, having spoken in praise of forbearance, was heeded by the other, who respectfully accepted the counsel and did not harm the viper. Reading note — Ru-Yi

All three vignettes turn on the same refusal to treat an attacker's malice as the whole truth about him — the lion insists on his own broken promise rather than the vulture's cruelty, the elephant locates the hunter's fault in "affliction" rather than in the man himself, and the forbearing nāga counsels mercy toward a viper who, the story is careful to note, remains murderously unrepentant for seven more days afterward. None of the three stories rewards this restraint with the offender's own conversion — the vulture is never softened, the viper stays vengeful, and only the hunter is changed. The compendium seems content to let compassion go unreciprocated in two cases out of three, as though insisting that its value does not depend on whether it works.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The man's own arms falling off the instant he reaches for his reward is the compendium's own way of making an invisible moral transaction suddenly, physically visible — betrayal is not left as a private failing between one man and a bear, but registers on his very body in front of the hunters who profited from it alongside him. The hunters' own fear afterward, refusing even to eat meat they did not personally betray anyone for, suggests the story's real target is not simply the man who talked, but the ordinary willingness of anyone nearby to profit from someone else's broken trust.

Long ago, near the city of Vārāṇasī, there was a pond named Rain-Formed, filled with fish, turtles, geese, wild geese, and ducks of every kind, among them a goose king named Rāṣṭrapāla, "Realm-Protector," lord of five hundred geese. A hunter set a snare of woven hair; the goose king, walking ahead of the flock, caught his own right leg in it, and reflected that if he showed his own distress the other geese would not finish eating the grain, so he waited until they had eaten their fill before revealing his trapped leg — at which the flock flew off, save one goose, named Sumukha, "Fine-Faced," who alone would not abandon his king.

The goose king asked his own minister: "Could you take my place, walking ahead of the flock as king?" Sumukha answered he could not. Asked why, he answered in verse:

"I vow to follow my king in life and death alike, sooner to die with my king than to live on apart from him. Great king, know this: the hunter is coming — strive with all your power to free yourself from this snare."

The goose king answered in verse:

"I have striven with all my strength, but my strength is now spent; the snare draws only tighter, and I cannot break free."

Sumukha, seeing the hunter arrive, spoke to him in verse:

"My king's own fur, fat, and flesh are no different from my own — take your knife and kill me instead, and release my king unharmed."

The hunter answered: "I shall kill neither of you — I release you both, to go wherever you please," and freed the goose king at once. The two geese, flying off together, resolved to repay so rare an act of mercy generously, and asked the hunter what he might want in return — to which the hunter, astonished that mere animals could offer any such repayment, was told: "The king of Vārāṇasī is named Brahmadatta, 'Given by Brahmā' — carry us to him upon your own shoulders."

The hunter carried the two geese, borne on his shoulders, through the city streets, their fine appearance drawing crowds who offered five, ten, even twenty coins apiece, all urging that they not be killed, so that by the time he reached the palace gate the hunter had already gained great wealth. At the palace, the hunter set the geese down, and the goose king asked the gatekeeper to announce to King Brahmadatta that Rāṣṭrapāla, king of the geese, now waited outside. The king received them at once, provided a golden bed, and, once Sumukha had properly exchanged greetings on his own king's behalf, the two took their seats — the goose king greeting King Brahmadatta in verse:

"Is your own body at ease? Does your land prosper and thrive? Do you govern your people rightly? Do you rule with an equal heart?"

King Brahmadatta answered in verse:

"I am ever at ease myself; I govern my people by the Dharma; my land ever prospers and thrives; I rule with an equal heart, free of favoritism."

Five hundred such verses of courteous exchange followed, during which Sumukha remained silent throughout. Asked by King Brahmadatta why he stayed silent, Sumukha answered that his own king was a great ruler in his own right, lord of the wetlands, and that between two such kings conversing it was not his place to interject. The king then offered the goose king his own fine garden to dwell in — but the goose king declined, explaining that should the king himself doze off and forget to protect them, or should his own cooks, unable to find other geese, seize them instead for the royal kitchen, harm might easily follow.

At this, the goose king Rāṣṭrapāla, still within the palace, called out, and the whole flock of geese, rising from the pond called Rain-Formed, flew above the palace crying out in grief, their wet wings scattering water over the halls below. Asked what this meant, the goose king explained these were his own kindred — and, asked if he now wished to depart, answered yes. Asked what he desired in parting, the goose king explained only that, having been shown such rare kindness by the hunter, life itself was now precious to him and his companions, and that none should be free to kill one after another as convenient. King Brahmadatta asked how he might repay this whole affair, and the two geese answered: with gold, silver, chariots, coral, agate, clothing, and food for the hunter — and, having said this, rose together into the sky. The Buddha said: the goose king was myself; the five hundred geese were the five hundred monks now present; the hunter was Śroṇakoṭikarṇa; King Brahmadatta was my own father, King Śuddhodana; and the minister Sumukha was Ānanda (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, Miscellaneous Recitations, fascicle 1).

A radiant deer saves a drowning man on the single condition that he tell no one where to find it — and the man, tempted by a queen's own reward, betrays that trust so completely that leprous sores erupt on his own face before he even finishes making the deal.

昔者菩薩。身為九色鹿。其毛九種色。其角白如雪。在恒水邊飲食水草。常與一烏為知識。時水中有一溺人。隨流來下。或出或沒。仰頭呼天。山神樹神諸天龍神。何不愍我。鹿聞下水言。汝可騎我背捉我角。負出上岸。溺人下地。繞鹿三匝。向鹿叩頭。乞得為大夫作奴。給其使令採水草。鹿言不用。且各自去。欲報恩者。莫道我在此。人貪我皮角。必來殺我。時國王夫人。夜夢見九色鹿。即詐病不起。王問何以。答曰。我昨夜夢見非常之鹿。其毛九種色。其角白如雪。我思欲得其皮作坐蓐。其角作拂柄。王當為我得之。王若不得我將死矣。王募於國中。若有能得當與分國而治。賜其金鉢盛滿銀粟。賜其銀鉢盛滿金粟。溺人聞之欲取富貴。念言。鹿是畜生。死活何在。往至王所言知鹿處。王大歡喜言。汝若能得其皮角來者。報之半國。溺人面上即生癩瘡。溺人言。大王此鹿雖是畜生。大有威神王宜多出人兵乃可得耳。王即大出人眾。經到恒水邊烏在樹頭。見人兵來即呼鹿言。知識且起王來至。鹿故熟臥不覺。烏下啄其耳。鹿方驚覺四向顧望無復走趣。便往趣王車邊。傍臣欲射。王曰。莫射。此鹿非常。將是天神。鹿言。大王。且莫射我。我前活王國中一人。鹿復長跪問王言。誰道我在此。王便指示。車邊癩面人是也。鹿即仰頭。視此人面。眼中淚出。不能自勝。此人前溺在水中。我不惜身命。自投水中。負此人出。約不相道也。無反復不如出水中浮木也。王有愧色。三數其民。汝受其恩。奈何反欲殺之。即下於國中。若有驅逐此鹿者。當誅五族。眾鹿數千皆來依附。飲食水草不侵禾稼。風雨時節五穀豐熟。人無疾病。其世太平。時九色鹿我身是也。烏阿難是。國王者今父王閱頭檀是。時王夫人者今孫陀利是。時溺人者調達是也。我雖有善心向之。故欲害我。阿難有至意(出九色鹿經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, the bodhisattva was born as a nine-colored deer, its fur nine different hues, its horns white as snow, dwelling by the Ganges and grazing on grass and water there, close friends with a crow. One day a drowning man, swept along by the current, rose and sank by turns, crying to heaven: "Mountain spirits, tree spirits, devas and nāgas — why does none of you pity me?" The deer, hearing this from the water's edge, told him: "Climb onto my back and hold my horns" — and carried him safely to shore. The man, once on land, circled the deer three times in gratitude, bowing before it, and begged to become its own servant, fetching water and grass for it forever — but the deer refused, saying only that they should each go their own way, and that if he truly wished to repay this kindness, he should tell no one where the deer could be found, for its own hide and horns would surely tempt someone to kill it otherwise.

Sometime later, the country's own queen dreamed of this nine-colored deer and, feigning illness, refused to rise from bed. Asked why, she told the king she had dreamed of an extraordinary deer, its fur nine colors, its horns white as snow, and that she wished its hide for a cushion and its horns for a whisk-handle — and that if the king could not obtain them for her, she would surely die. The king proclaimed throughout the realm that whoever could capture this deer would receive half the kingdom to govern, along with a golden bowl filled with silver grain and a silver bowl filled with golden grain.

The drowning man, hearing this and coveting such wealth, reflected that the deer was after all a mere animal, whatever became of it — and went to the king to reveal where it could be found. The king, delighted, promised him half the kingdom if he could truly deliver the deer's hide and horns — and at that very moment leprous sores broke out across the informer's own face. He told the king that though this deer was but an animal, it possessed great spiritual power, and urged him to send a large force of soldiers to be sure of capturing it.

The king sent out a great army, which reached the riverbank where the crow, perched in a tree, saw them coming and called out: "Old friend, wake up — the king is here!" But the deer, deeply asleep, did not stir until the crow flew down and pecked its own ear, startling it awake. Looking about, it found no way to flee, and so went straight toward the king's own chariot. The king's attendants moved to shoot it, but the king stopped them, saying this was no ordinary deer, but surely some deva. The deer spoke: "Great king, do not shoot me — I once saved the life of a man now in your own kingdom" — and, kneeling, asked the king who had revealed its location. The king pointed toward his chariot, at the man whose face now bore leprous sores. The deer raised its head to look at this man's own face, tears falling uncontrollably, for this was the very man it had once, without regard for its own life, thrown itself into the water to save — a man who had broken his own promise and proven less trustworthy than a mere piece of driftwood.

The king, ashamed, rebuked his own people three times over: "You received this creature's own kindness — how could you then wish to kill it?" — and decreed throughout the kingdom that anyone who drove this deer away would have his entire clan of five put to death. From then on, thousands of deer came to depend upon it, grazing on grass and water without ever harming the crops, the rains falling in season, the harvest abundant, the people free of illness, the whole age at peace.

That nine-colored deer was myself; the crow was Ānanda; the king was my own father, King Śuddhodana; the queen was Sundarī; and the drowning man was Devadatta. Though I bore him only good will, he still sought to harm me — while Ānanda, the crow, held to his own sincere devotion throughout (Source: Sūtra of the Nine-Colored Deer).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The leprous sores erupt on the informer's face before he has actually done anything but speak — the betrayal is punished at the moment of intention, not upon its completion, as though the story wants to insist that the true offense was already finished the instant he decided the deer's life was worth trading for a reward. That the deer's own final act is not anger but tears at the sight of this ruined, disfigured face is the story's real point: the compendium consistently prefers to let betrayal punish itself visibly, while reserving grief rather than retribution as the wronged party's own response.

13–14. A Parrot's Wildfire, a Sparrow's Extracted Bone

A parrot, unable to bear watching its own home tree burn, flies back and forth from the ocean carrying water in its wings; a sparrow, pecking a lodged bone from a starving tiger's mouth day after day until its own beak grows sore, is rewarded only with the tiger's fury the moment it dares to preach.

Chinese Source
為鸚鵡現身救山火以申報恩十三

昔者菩薩。現為鸚鵡常處于樹。風吹彼樹更相切磨。便有火出。火漸熾盛。遂焚一山。鸚鵡思惟。猶如飛鳥軀止于樹。故當反復起報恩心。何況於我長夜處之。而不能滅火。即往詣海。以其兩翅取大海水。至彼火上而灑於火。或以口灑東西馳奔。時有善神。感其懃苦。尋為滅火(出僧伽羅剎經上卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation
A Parrot's Wildfire

Long ago, the bodhisattva, manifesting as a parrot, dwelt constantly in a certain tree. Wind, rubbing the branches of that tree against each other, struck a spark, and fire, spreading, soon consumed an entire mountain. The parrot reflected that even a mere bird, having taken shelter in this tree so long, ought to repay such kindness in return — how much more should it do so now, rather than simply flee, unable to bear the thought of not trying to put out the flames. It flew at once to the ocean, and, filling its own two wings with seawater, returned to sprinkle it over the fire, or carried water in its beak, flying back and forth tirelessly between sea and mountain. A good deity, moved by its earnest effort, then extinguished the fire on its behalf (Source: Saṃgharakṣa Sūtra, upper fascicle).

為雀王身拔虎口骨十四

昔者菩薩身為雀王。慈心濟眾由護身瘡。有虎食獸骨拄其齒。病困將終。雀入口啄骨日日若茲。雀口生瘡身為瘦疵。骨出虎口。雀飛登樹。說佛經曰。殺為凶瘧其惡莫大。虎聞雀戒。勃然恚曰。爾始離吾口。而敢多言。雀覩其不可化。即速飛去。佛言。雀者是吾身。虎者是調達(出雀王經)。

A Sparrow's Extracted Bone

Long ago, the bodhisattva was born as a sparrow king, compassionate toward all beings even at risk to his own body. A tiger, having eaten some animal's bones, found one lodged in its own teeth, and, in mounting pain, lay near death. The sparrow flew into its mouth and pecked at the bone day after day, its own beak growing sore and its body wasting thin, until at last the bone came free from the tiger's mouth. The sparrow then flew up into a tree and recited the Buddha's own teaching: "To kill is the very worst of calamities, its evil beyond measure." Hearing this admonition, the tiger, far from grateful, growled in fury: "You have only just left my own mouth, and already dare lecture me?" Seeing the tiger beyond conversion, the sparrow quickly flew away. The Buddha said: that sparrow was myself; that tiger was Devadatta (Source: Sūtra of the Sparrow King).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The sparrow's own mistake, in a fascicle otherwise full of successful conversions, is instructive by exception: it treats a creature just relieved of physical pain as therefore ready to hear moral instruction, and gets only fury for the assumption. The parrot's story, by contrast, never claims any effect on the fire at all — the flames go out only once a separate deity intervenes, moved by the effort itself rather than its results. Read together, the pair suggests a deliberately modest claim: the value of the gesture does not depend on whether it actually works, or whether the one helped is ready to be taught anything at all.

15. The Fish King Who Fed a Famine

A man who gives his own body to spare a smaller fish becomes, in his next life, a whale whose flesh feeds an entire starving coastline for months without dying — and, reborn again as a grieving prince, ends a drought through nothing but his own sustained repentance.

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩貧窶。與諸商人俱至他國。菩薩覩海中魚巨細相吞。以身代小者令得須臾之命。即自投海大魚得飽小者得活。魂神化為鯨魚之王。身有數里。海邊國旱人民饑饉。更相吞噉。魚即蕩身于國。噉者存命。輦肉數月而魚猶活。天神下曰。爾為仁慈苦可堪乎。何不收壽離斯痛也。魚曰。吾神逝身腐。民後饑饉。復當相噉。吾不忍覩。天曰。爾必得佛度苦眾生。有人取其首魚時死矣。魂為王子。有上聖之明。四恩弘慈潤齊二儀。愍民困窮。言之哽咽。然國尚旱。靖心齋肅。退食絕獻。頓首悔過曰。民之不善。咎在我身。願喪吾命惠民雨澤。月月哀慟。由至孝之子遭聖父之喪。精誠遠遠有名。佛與五百人來其國界。王聞心悅。奉迎稽首叩頭涕泣。心穢行濁。不合三尊四恩之教。苦酷人民。枯旱累載。黎庶饑饉。怨痛傷情。願除民災以禍罪我。諸佛答曰。爾為人君。慈惻仁惠德齊帝釋。諸佛普知。令王受福慎無慼也。勅民種穀。家無不修。稻化為滋。王曰。須熟。禾實覆國。皆含稻穬。中容數斛。其米芬芳舉國欣懌。歎詠王德。率土持戒。歸命三寶。王及臣民命終生天。時貧人吾身是(出度無極集第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, the bodhisattva, poor and destitute, traveled with a company of merchants to another country. Seeing in the sea how the larger fish preyed upon the smaller, he gave up his own body in the smaller fish's place, that they might have at least a moment's reprieve, throwing himself into the sea — the great fish satisfied, the small ones spared. His own spirit, transmigrating, became the king of the whales, his body spanning several li in length.

When a coastal kingdom suffered drought and famine, its people turning to devour one another, this great fish swam its own body ashore there, that those who ate of it might survive; for months its flesh was carried away in cartloads, yet the fish still lived. A deva descended and asked: "Can you truly bear such suffering for the sake of compassion alone? Why not simply let your own life end and be free of this pain?" The fish answered: "Once my spirit departs and my body rots, these people will only return to devouring one another again — I cannot bear to see that." The deva told him he would surely attain buddhahood and deliver suffering beings — and, at that very moment, someone took the fish's own head, and it died.

Its spirit was reborn as a prince, gifted with a sage's own wisdom, whose kindness and compassion, matching heaven and earth themselves, grieved for his people's poverty and hardship until his voice choked with tears. Yet the drought persisted; he purified his own heart in fasting, refusing every offering, prostrating himself in repentance: "My people's own wrongdoing is my own fault; may my life be given in exchange for rain to relieve them" — mourning every month as a filial son mourns a departed sage-father, his sincerity known far and wide.

The Buddha, together with five hundred followers, then came to that very kingdom; the prince, overjoyed, welcomed him with bows and tears, confessing his own defiled heart unworthy of the Three Jewels' own teaching and the four kindnesses, and that his own harsh rule had brought years of drought and his people's suffering — begging that any calamity fall on himself alone rather than his people. The assembled buddhas answered: "You are a ruler of such compassion and humaneness that your virtue equals Śakra's own; all buddhas know this — accept this blessing yourself and grieve no further." He ordered his people to sow their fields, and every household's own planting flourished; rice transformed abundantly, filling the whole country with grain, each stalk holding several measures, its fragrance delighting the entire realm, which praised the prince's own virtue, kept the precepts throughout, and took refuge in the Three Jewels. The prince, his ministers, and his people, at life's end, were all reborn in heaven. That poor man of the story was myself (Source: Collection on the Perfections, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fish's own answer to the deva's question — that he cannot die yet, since the people will only return to devouring each other once he does — makes explicit something several other entries in this fascicle leave implied: that giving one's own body relieves suffering only temporarily, and the bodhisattva knows this perfectly well even as he does it anyway. The story's second act, in which the same being ends a drought entirely through sustained repentance and grief rather than any further bodily sacrifice, suggests giving up one's body is not always the deepest tool available — sometimes what a whole starving country actually needs is a ruler willing to hold his people's own faults as his own.

16. The Turtle King and the Merchants

The fascicle closes with a turtle so vast that lost merchants mistake his own back for an island and light cooking fires on it — and who, rather than simply diving to escape the pain, works out a way to submerge slowly enough to save every one of them first.

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩。曾為鼈王。生長大海化諸同類。子民群眾皆修仁德。王自奉行慈悲喜護。愍於眾生如母愛子。其海深長邊際難限。而悉周至靡不更歷。於時鼈王出於海外。在邊臥息積有日月。其背堅燥猶如陸地。賈人遠來因止其上。破薪然火炊煮飯食。繫其牛馬車乘載石皆著其上。鼈王欲趣入水。畏墮商人。適欲強忍痛不可勝。便設權計入淺水處。除滅火毒不危眾賈。眾賈恐怖謂潮卒漲。悲哀吁嗟歸命諸天。釋梵四王日月神明。願以威德唯見救濟。鼈王心益愍之。因報賈人。慎莫恐怖。吾被火焚故捨入水。欲令痛息。今當相安終不相危。眾賈聞之知有活望。俱時發聲言。南無佛。鼈興大慈。還負眾賈移在岸邊。眾人得脫靡不歡喜。遙拜鼈王而歎其德。尊為橋梁多所過度。行為大舟載越三界。設得佛道當復救脫生死之厄。鼈王報曰。善哉善哉。當如汝言。各自別去。佛言。時鼈王者我身是也。五百賈人。五百弟子舍利弗等是(出生經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, the bodhisattva was once a turtle king, born and raised in the great ocean, who converted his own kind, so that his subjects and their multitudes all cultivated humaneness and virtue. The king himself practiced compassion, sympathetic joy, and protection, cherishing all beings as a mother cherishes her own child, and, though the ocean's own depths and breadth knew no easy limit, traveled everywhere within it without exception.

Once, resting on the ocean's own surface for days and months at a time, the turtle king's own back grew hard and dry like solid ground — and merchants, arriving from afar, mistook it for an island and settled there, chopping wood, lighting fires, cooking meals, tethering their own oxen and horses, and loading stone cargo directly onto its back. The turtle king, wishing to dive back into the water, feared it would drown the merchants there, and endured the pain as long as it could bear, then devised a plan: submerging slowly into shallower water first, extinguishing the fire's own heat without endangering the merchants at all. The terrified merchants, believing the tide had suddenly risen, cried out in grief, invoking the devas, Śakra, Brahmā, the four heavenly kings, and the sun and moon spirits, begging by their own majestic power to be saved.

The turtle king, moved further to compassion, called out to the merchants: "Do not be afraid — I have only submerged because of the fire burning me, to ease my own pain; I mean you no harm at all." The merchants, hearing this and now hopeful of survival, together cried out, "Homage to the Buddha!" — and the turtle king, filled with great compassion, carried them all back and set them safely on the shore. The merchants, saved, rejoiced without exception, bowing from a distance to praise the turtle king's own virtue, calling it a bridge and ferry carrying countless beings across, a great vessel bearing them beyond the three realms themselves, and asking that, should it attain buddhahood, it deliver them also from the perils of birth and death. The turtle king answered: "Very well, very well — it shall be as you say," and each went their own way. The Buddha said: that turtle king was myself; the five hundred merchants were my five hundred disciples, Śāriputra and the rest (Source: Jātaka Sūtra, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The turtle king's own restraint is the quiet center of the story: in genuine, mounting pain from the merchants' own cooking fires, its first thought is still how to escape that pain without harming the very people causing it, rather than simply diving to relieve itself and letting the consequences fall where they may. Ending this fascicle's long sequence of animal-form sacrifice on this note — a rescue achieved through patient, careful problem-solving rather than dramatic self-destruction — suggests the compendium's own closing point: that compassion under real strain is measured less by grand, single gestures than by the discipline to keep looking for the solution that spares everyone.
