Ghosts and Spirits

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 46 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Ghosts and Spirits" (鬼神部) — twenty-two entries in four sections, on asuras, gandharvas, kinnaras, and ghosts of every kind. Among them: four separate accounts of the asuras' wars with Śakra, each from a different source and each giving a different cause and outcome, including one in which Śakra wins because he turned his chariot aside to avoid a nest of chicks and one in which, having won, he gives back everything he took on a point of precept; Druma the kinnara king, whose lute-playing shook Sumeru and knocked five hundred sages out of the air; a traveller asked to arbitrate between two ghosts quarrelling over a corpse, who is dismembered and rebuilt out of it and goes to ask the monks whether he still has a body; the taming of the yakṣa who took one human life a day from Vṛji, and of Hārītī, who stole and ate other people's children until the Buddha hid one of hers; an anger-eating demon on Śakra's throne; and the great catalogue of eighteen hungry ghosts questioned in turn on the bank of the Ganges, five of whom turn out to be monks and monastery officials punished for very small thefts from what was held in common.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 46 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 46 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 46 is the first fascicle in this stretch of the compendium to be formally divided into sections, and we have kept the division: five entries on asuras, one on gandharvas, one on kinnaras, and fifteen on ghosts and spirits of various kinds. Each section is introduced below with its own Chinese heading.

The asura section is the compendium's method in miniature. Its first entry is a cosmographical survey and the next four are four accounts of the same war, from the Dīrgha Āgama, the Sūtra on the Samādhi of Contemplating the Buddha, the Dīrgha Āgama again, and the Garbha Sūtra — and they disagree about who started it, why, and how it ended. They are printed one after another with no attempt to reconcile them, and one of them carries an interlinear note supplying a fourth, incompatible detail from the Saṃyukta Āgama.

On names: Śakra (also called Kauśika), Vemacitrin, Rāhu, Nanda and Upananda, Pañcaśikha, Druma, Mahākāśyapa, Maudgalyāyana, Ānanda, Mañjuśrī, Māra Pāpīyas, Krakucchanda, the Buddha Kāśyapa, Brahmadatta, Sumeru, the Ganges, Vṛji, Jambudvīpa, the Trāyastriṃśa and Paranirmitavaśavartin heavens, the Nandana grove, and the Hall of Good Dharma are given in their standard forms; so are Hārītī, whom the Chinese calls simply the Mother of Ghosts, and Āḷavaka, whose name the compiler himself supplies in an interlinear note. Where no confident correspondence exists — Wǔyí among the five ministers, Jiāyí, Luólóu, Nāyūluó, Qūmó, Fútuómóníbō, Zhìnì, and Mount Pítuó — we keep the Chinese in pinyin, with the compendium's own bracketed glosses preserved.

One name needs distinguishing. The asura king of entries 1 and 4 is written with the same characters the compendium elsewhere uses for the Buddha's son Rāhula. They are unrelated figures, and we render the asura king as Rāhu and flag it in the reading note rather than let the shared name cause confusion.

Entry 14 carries a verse, which we set off in italics with slashes for the line breaks of the Chinese; the Chinese column keeps it running on within the paragraph, as the source has it.

Entries 9 and 20 are extended catalogues of the torments of hungry ghosts, and entry 12 is a dismemberment. All are translated in full and without abridgment.

Asuras 阿修羅第一

Five entries on the asura kings and their wars with Śakra — a cosmographical survey followed by four accounts of the same conflict from four different sources, each giving a different cause for it and a different way it ended.

1. King Rāhu, Angry That the Gods of the Trāyastriṃśa Walk Over His Head, Raises Troops and Gives Great Battle

A survey of the asura realm — a palace held up by four winds, a body twenty-eight thousand li long, five ministers — and then the war that starts because their king cannot bear the gods walking over his head.

Chinese Source

大阿修羅王(長阿鋡經云阿須輪)名曰羅呵。住須彌山北大海之底。水懸在宮上為四風所持(樓炭經云隨風所持)城郭縱廣八萬由旬。內外七重高三千由旬金城銀門園池清涼眾鳥和鳴。去須彌山一萬由旬。身長二萬八千里(餘須輪實形長一由旬衣長二由旬廣一由旬重六銖)亦食揣食洗浴衣服為細滑食。月十五日。入海中央。化其形體。下水著臍上[門@視]須彌指覆日月。日月天子。見其醜形皆大恐懼。無復光明。遊矚之時有自然風。吹門開閉吹地令淨。吹華分散。有五大臣。一名捉持。二名雄力。三名武夷。四名頭首。五名摧伏。侍衛左右。忽自念言。我有威德神力如是。而置忉利王及日月諸天行我頭上。誓取日月以為耳璫。漸大瞋忿加欲捶之。即念。舍摩黎毘摩質多二阿須輪王。及諸大臣各辦兵仗往與天戰。時難陀跋難陀二大龍王。身遶須彌周圍七匝。山動雲布以尾打水。大海湧波上冠須彌。忉利天曰。須輪欲戰矣。海中諸龍迦樓羅鬼持華常樂。二鬼神等各持兵眾從次交鬪。若不如皆奔。四天王宮嚴駕攻伐。先白帝釋。帝釋告上諸天。時炎摩以上遠至他化。無數天眾及諸龍鬼前後圍遶。帝釋命曰。我軍若勝以五繫縛。縛毘摩質多。將還善法堂。我欲觀之。須輪亦曰。我眾若勝以五繫縛。縛帝釋還七葉堂。我欲觀之。一時大戰兵刃交接兩不相傷。但觸身體生於痛惱。帝釋現身乃有千眼。執金剛杵頭出煙焰。須輪見之眾乃退敗。即擒毘摩質多係縛將還。遙見帝釋便肆惡口。帝釋答曰。我欲共汝講說道義耳。壽天千歲少出多減。惡心好鬪而不破戒。大修布施故受此身(出長阿含第二十卷樓炭華嚴大智論略同)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The great asura king (the Dīrgha Āgama calls them asuras) named Rāhu dwells at the bottom of the great sea north of Mount Sumeru, the water hanging above his palace and held up by four winds (the Lokasthāna Sūtra says held by the wind as it goes). The city walls are eighty thousand yojanas across, with seven walls within and without, three thousand yojanas high, gold walls and silver gates, gardens and pools cool and clear, and birds of every kind singing in harmony. It is ten thousand yojanas from Mount Sumeru.

His body is twenty-eight thousand li long (the other asuras’ actual form is one yojana long, their clothing two yojanas long and one broad, weighing six zhū). He eats coarse food, and bathing and clothing are his subtle food.

On the fifteenth of the month he goes into the middle of the sea and transforms his body, and the water below reaches his navel, and he glares at Sumeru and covers the sun and moon with his fingers; and the gods of sun and moon, seeing his ugly form, are all greatly frightened and there is no more light.

When he goes about to look at things, there is a natural wind which blows the gates open and shut, blows the ground clean, and blows the flowers apart and scatters them. He has five great ministers: the first named Grasper, the second Fierce-Strength, the third Wǔyí, the fourth Head-Foremost, the fifth Crusher; and they attend him left and right.

He suddenly thought: I have majesty and virtue and divine power such as this, and yet I let the lord of the Trāyastriṃśa and the gods of sun and moon walk about over my head. I swear I shall take the sun and moon for ear-pendants. And his anger grew and he wished to strike them; and he thought at once of the two asura kings Śāmalī and Vemacitrin, and their great ministers each made ready arms and went to make war on the gods.

Then the two great nāga kings Nanda and Upananda wound their bodies seven times around Sumeru, and the mountain shook and clouds spread, and they beat the water with their tails, and the great sea threw up waves that rose over Sumeru. The gods of the Trāyastriṃśa said: the asuras mean to fight. The nāgas of the sea, the garuḍas, the flower-holding ghosts, and the Ever-Joyful — these two kinds of ghosts and spirits each led their troops and joined battle in turn; and whoever was worsted fled.

The palaces of the Four Heavenly Kings made ready to attack, and first told Śakra; and Śakra told the gods above. Then from Yāma up to Paranirmitavaśavartin, countless hosts of gods and all the nāgas and ghosts surrounded him before and behind.

Śakra gave the command: if my army wins, bind Vemacitrin with the fivefold binding and bring him back to the Hall of Good Dharma; I wish to look at him. And the asuras said likewise: if our host wins, bind Śakra with the fivefold binding and bring him back to the Hall of Seven Leaves; we wish to look at him.

They fought a great battle all at once, and the blades met and neither side wounded the other; only, where the bodies were touched, pain and distress arose. Śakra manifested his body with a thousand eyes and held the vajra-club with smoke and flame coming from its head; and the asuras, seeing it, then withdrew defeated, and Vemacitrin was seized and bound and brought back.

Seeing Śakra from afar he let loose foul abuse, and Śakra answered: I only wish to discuss the meaning of the way with you. Their heavenly lifespan is a thousand years, seldom more and often less. Their minds are evil and they love to fight, and yet they do not break the precepts, and because they greatly cultivated giving they receive this body (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20; the Lokasthāna, the Avataṃsaka, and the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom are roughly the same).

2. Vemacitrin Gives His Daughter to Śakra as a Wife, and When She Is Jealous He Raises Troops

The asura genealogy from an egg laid in bathwater, and the war that follows when Śakra's asura wife sees him in a bathing pool with other women.

Chinese Source

毘摩質多者。昔在劫初。諸天入水身生觸樂。精流其中自然成卵。卵生一女其形青黑。入大海中拍水自樂。水精入體即覺有身。乃生一男。九頭頭有千眼口中出火。有九百九十九手八脚。踞于海中食噉淤藍及藕根。取香山乾闥婆神女為妻。容姿美妙色踰白玉。後生一女。端正挺特天地無比。憍尸迦遣使求索。阿修羅言。若能使我乘七寶宮當以相與。帝釋即脫寶冠擬十善報。使阿修羅得坐勝殿。乘六種寶臺而往迎之。置於善法堂上。更為立名曰悅意。諸天輔臣莫不歡喜。後帝釋遊歡喜園。共諸婇女入池遊戲。悅意嫉妬。遣五夜叉還啟其父。父即大瞋。興四種兵往攻帝釋。立大海中踞須彌頂。九百九十九手同時俱作。[打-丁+慼]喜見城搖動須彌。四大海水一時波浪。釋提桓因驚怖惶恐靡知所趣。時宮有神。白天王言。過去佛說。般若波羅蜜呪王當誦持。鬼兵自碎。時天帝釋於善法殿集眾燒名香發大誓願。般若波羅蜜是大明呪是無上呪是無等等呪審實不虛。我持此法當成佛道。令阿修羅自然退散。作是語時。虛空中有四大刀輪。自然而下當阿修羅。耳鼻手足一時墮落。令大海水赤如絳珠。時阿修羅即大驚怖。遁走無處入藕孔中(出觀佛三昧經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for Vemacitrin: long ago at the beginning of the kalpa the gods went into the water, and their bodies produced the pleasure of touch, and their seed flowed into it and became eggs of themselves; and from an egg was born a girl whose form was blue-black.

She went into the great sea and slapped the water for her own pleasure, and the essence of the water entered her body and she knew she was with child; and she then bore a son with nine heads, each head having a thousand eyes and fire coming out of its mouth, with nine hundred and ninety-nine hands and eight feet, who squatted in the sea eating blue-green weed and lotus root.

He took a gandharva spirit-woman of the Fragrant Mountain to wife, whose face and figure were exquisite and her color surpassing white jade; and afterward she bore a daughter, upright and outstanding and beyond compare in heaven or earth.

Kauśika sent a messenger to ask for her, and the asura said: if you can let me ride in a palace of the seven treasures, I shall give her to you. Śakra then took off his jewelled crown, matching it to the reward of the ten wholesome deeds, and let the asura sit in a superior hall; and he rode a jewelled terrace of six kinds to go and fetch her, and set her in the Hall of Good Dharma and gave her a new name, Delight-Mind; and the gods and their ministers were all glad.

Afterward Śakra was wandering in the Nandana grove and went into a pool with his attendant women to disport himself, and Delight-Mind was jealous and sent five yakṣas back to tell her father; and her father was greatly enraged and raised the four kinds of troops and went to attack Śakra.

He stood in the great sea and squatted on the summit of Sumeru, and all nine hundred and ninety-nine hands worked at once and struck the city of Sudarśana and shook Sumeru, and the waters of the four great seas surged into waves at one time; and Śakra, lord of the gods, was startled and terrified and did not know where to turn.

Then a spirit of the palace said to the heavenly king: a buddha of the past said that the perfection of wisdom is the king of spells, and it should be recited and upheld, and the ghost-army will break of itself.

Then Śakra assembled the host in the Hall of Good Dharma and burned fine incense and made a great vow: the perfection of wisdom is the great bright spell, the unsurpassed spell, the spell equal to the unequalled, truly real and not false. Upholding this Dharma I shall attain the buddha-path; let the asuras withdraw and scatter of themselves.

As he said this, four great blade-wheels came down of themselves out of the open sky upon the asura, and his ears, nose, hands, and feet fell away all at once, and the water of the great sea was made red as crimson pearls; and the asura was then greatly frightened and fled and had nowhere to go and went into the holes of the lotus stalks (Source: Sūtra on the Samādhi of Contemplating the Buddha, fascicle 1).

3. Long Ago the Asuras Fought with the Gods and, Seeing Śakra Turn His Chariot, Scattered

Śakra, already defeated and fleeing, turns his chariot aside to avoid a nest of chicks. The asuras conclude he is coming back to fight.

Chinese Source

昔者阿修羅。興兵與天帝共戰。釋提桓因不如。乘千輻寶車怖懼而退。中路見腅娑羅樹。鳥巢有兩子。即勅御者。此樹有二鳥。汝可迴車避之。寧使來害我。勿傷此也。即便迴車。須輪遙見即相謂言。帝釋迴車必欲還戰。不可當也。即便退散天乃得勝(雜阿含經云叢林有金翅鳥巢謂是來戰大怖而走)須輪三事過閻浮提。一者宮殿高廣。二者宮殿莊嚴。三者宮殿清淨(出長阿含經第二十一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the asuras raised troops and fought with Śakra, lord of the gods, and Śakra was worsted, and, riding his jewelled chariot of a thousand spokes, withdrew in fear. Halfway along the road he saw a śāla tree in which a bird’s nest held two chicks, and he ordered his charioteer: there are two birds in this tree; turn the chariot aside and avoid it. Sooner let them come and harm me than that these be hurt.

And he turned the chariot; and the asuras, seeing it from afar, said to one another: Śakra has turned his chariot and must mean to come back and fight, and he cannot be withstood. And they scattered and withdrew, and the gods then won (the Saṃyukta Āgama says that there was a garuḍa’s nest in the thicket and that they supposed it was coming to fight and fled in great fear).

The asuras surpass Jambudvīpa in three things: first, their palaces are high and broad; second, their palaces are adorned; third, their palaces are pure (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 21).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The whole entry rests on a misreading, and the compendium supplies a variant of it in the same breath. Śakra, already beaten and in flight, orders his chariot turned aside to avoid a nest with two chicks in it, saying he would rather be caught and harmed than crush them; and the asuras, seeing the chariot swing round, conclude that he is coming back for another engagement and scatter. He wins the war by an act that had nothing to do with the war.

The parenthetical variant the compiler inserts is worth its place: the Saṃyukta Āgama, he notes, says it was a garuḍa's nest in the thicket and that the asuras thought the garuḍa was coming. The two readings are incompatible — one makes Śakra's compassion the cause of his victory and the other makes it an accident of ornithology — and both are printed.

4. Rāhu Has a Daughter, Śakra Demands Her by Force, and He Raises Troops and Attacks

Śakra sends Pañcaśikha ahead with a ninety-one-string lute to demand an asura's daughter, and, having won the war, gives all the women back on a point of precept.

Chinese Source

過去世時。有阿修羅王。名羅睺羅。生於一女。殊特端正。女德六十四種。無不具足。口吐言氣如優鉢羅華。身出牛頭旃檀之香。面色紅白見者愛樂。時釋提桓因作是念言。此宮諸女多有端正。比須輪女而皆不及。今我集兵往伐取之給我驅使。即召諸天具言此事。諸天啟曰。行可遣執樂神等。手執九十一絃琉璃之琴(胎經云九十九絃)歌我天人受福快樂。即勅執樂天子般遮翼等。嚴駕樂器到婆私呵前彈琴。現意言曰。欲得婆私呵與我給使。若不與者當以兵取。須輪大瞋。我亦有力足相擬逆。般遮翼等即報帝釋。時須輪集兵到須彌山側。壞曲脚天宮。次壞風天宮馬宮莊嚴等諸天宮。乃至四門。帝釋憶本所誦而呪之。須輪兵眾漸漸却退。引四種軍入拘郗羅池藕莖孔中。有一士夫見之。自念。我狂失性謬覩異事。佛告比丘。此真實也。時釋提桓因集諸天眾從四門出。但見刀鎧弓箭在地。不見須輪眾。轉前進直入阿須輪宮殿。見婆私呵女數千萬眾。不見阿須輪。將諸女眾歸詣忉利宮。時諸阿須輪等求哀歸命。向釋提桓因言。我等愚惑不識佛弟子神力巍巍。我等先祖信奉如來。聞佛有戒不取他物。今天王釋將我眷屬盡填天宮。非佛弟子之所應行。帝釋聞之悵然不樂。我寧當奉禁不犯偷盜。即還諸女。時阿須輪王以最可愛女奉上帝釋。帝釋即以甘露為報。須輪與天和好。共持如來三歸八戒(出胎經及出譬喻經下卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In a past age there was an asura king named Rāhu who bore a daughter, exceptional and upright, complete in all sixty-four virtues of a woman, whose breath from her mouth was like the utpala flower and whose body gave out the fragrance of gośīrṣa sandalwood, her face red and white, and whoever saw her loved her.

Then Śakra, lord of the gods, thought: among the women of this palace there are many who are beautiful, and compared with the asura’s daughter they all fall short. Now I shall gather troops and go and take her by force to serve me. And he summoned the gods and told them the matter in full.

The gods said to him: it may do to send the musician spirits, holding lapis lutes of ninety-one strings (the Garbha Sūtra says ninety-nine strings), to sing of how we gods receive blessing and pleasure. So he ordered the musician gods, Pañcaśikha and the rest, to make ready their instruments and go before Vāsihā and play, and to make his intention known and say: I wish to have Vāsihā to serve me, and if she is not given, I shall take her by arms.

The asura was greatly enraged: I too have strength enough to meet you. Pañcaśikha and the rest reported to Śakra.

Then the asura gathered troops and came to the flank of Mount Sumeru, and destroyed the palace of the Bent-Legged god, and then the palace of the Wind god, the horse palace, the Adornment palace, and the other heavenly palaces, and came as far as the four gates.

Śakra remembered what he had recited before and recited the spell, and the asura’s troops gradually withdrew, and led their four kinds of army into the holes of the lotus stalks in the Kauṣṭhila pool. A man saw it and thought to himself: I have gone mad and lost my wits and am seeing strange things wrongly. The Buddha told the monks: this is truly so.

Then Śakra assembled the host of gods and went out by the four gates, and saw only blades, armor, bows, and arrows on the ground, and did not see the asura host; and, advancing further, he went straight into the asuras’ palaces and saw Vāsihā and several tens of millions of women, and did not see the asuras. And he took the host of women back to the Trāyastriṃśa palace.

Then the asuras begged for pity and turned to Śakra for refuge and said: we were foolish and deluded and did not know how lofty the divine power of the Buddha’s disciples is. Our forefathers had faith in and served the Tathāgata, and we have heard that the Buddha has a precept against taking what belongs to another; and now the heavenly king Śakra has taken our kin and filled the heavenly palace with them, which is not what a disciple of the Buddha should do.

Śakra heard it and was downcast and unhappy: I had better uphold the prohibition and not offend by stealing. And he gave the women back; and then the asura king presented his most beloved daughter to Śakra, and Śakra gave sweet nectar in return. And the asuras and the gods were at peace together, and together upheld the three refuges and the eight precepts of the Tathāgata (Source: the Garbha Sūtra; also Parables Sūtra, lower fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the fullest of the four asura-war accounts and the only one in which Śakra is unambiguously in the wrong. He wants the daughter, says so plainly — I shall gather troops and take her by force to serve me — and sends the gandharva musicians ahead to deliver the demand with a lute accompaniment.

The ending is what makes it worth including. Having won, Śakra sweeps the asura palaces and carries off tens of millions of women; and the asuras' protest is not that he beat them but that he stole: our forefathers had faith in the Tathāgata, and we have heard that the Buddha has a precept against taking what belongs to another. Śakra is downcast, concedes the point, and gives every one of them back — at which the asura king gives him the daughter voluntarily, and the two sides take the three refuges together.

The name of this asura king is Rāhu, written with the same characters the compendium elsewhere uses for the Buddha's son Rāhula. They are unrelated figures and we distinguish them here.

5. An Asura in a Former Body, Weary of Being Swept Away by Water, Vowed to Have a Great Tall Form

A porter repeatedly swept away while fording a river makes one wish after giving away his dinner.

Chinese Source

阿修羅前世時。曾為貧人。居近河邊常渡河擔薪。時河水深流復駛疾。此人數為水所漂。既亡所持。身又沒溺。隨流殆死得出。時有辟支佛。作沙門形詣舍乞食。貧人歡喜即施飯食。食訖擲鉢空中飛軒而去。貧人見之因以發願。願我後生身形長大。一切深水無過膝者。以是因緣得極大身。四大海水不能過膝。立大海中身過須彌。手據山頂下觀忉利天(出雜譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In a former life the asura was a poor man who lived near a river and constantly crossed it carrying firewood. The river water was deep and its current swift, and this man was repeatedly swept away by the water; and having lost what he was carrying, his body too sank, and he drifted with the current and barely escaped death.

At that time a pratyekabuddha in the form of a śramaṇa came to his house to beg for food, and the poor man was glad and gave him food at once; and when he had eaten he threw his bowl into the air and flew off. The poor man, seeing this, thereupon made a vow: may I in a later life have a body tall and great, so that no deep water anywhere comes above my knee.

By these causes and conditions he obtained an exceedingly great body, so that the waters of the four great seas cannot come above his knee; and standing in the great sea his body rises above Sumeru, and, resting his hand on the mountain top, he looks down on the Trāyastriṃśa heaven (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra, fascicle 4).

Gandharvas 乾闥婆第二

One entry: where the gandharvas live, and the occasion on which Śakra sent Pañcaśikha ahead with a lute to sing the Buddha into receiving a visitor.

6. The Gandharvas

The gandharva city, the Fragrant Mountain, the two grottoes, and the lute of Pañcaśikha.

Chinese Source

乾闥婆王。住雪山右城。名毘舍離。世界初成有風輪起。名曰莊嚴。造此宮城。城北有七黑山。山北復有香山。在十寶山間。常有伎樂之聲(大智論云是天樂神)山有二窟。一名晝。二名善晝。七寶所成柔軟香潔。猶如天衣。乾闥婆王。從五百乾闥婆止住其中。佛在毘陀山(出在摩竭國北)。釋提桓因告執樂神。般遮翼持瑠璃琴於佛前歌。佛曰。汝能以琴歌稱讚如來。悲和哀婉感動人心。於此聲中云。欲縛淨行沙門涅槃眾義備有。帝釋顧語之曰。當以汝補汝父位。於其類中為最上。以女妻之(出長阿含經大智論)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The gandharva king dwells in the city to the right of the Snow Mountains, named Vaiśālī. When the world first came into being a wind-wheel arose named Adornment, and it built this palace city. North of the city there are seven black mountains, and north of those again the Fragrant Mountain, among the ten jewel mountains, where there is always the sound of music (the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says these are the heavenly musician-spirits).

In the mountain there are two grottoes, the first named Day and the second Good Day, made of the seven treasures, soft and fragrant and pure like heavenly clothing; and the gandharva king, with five hundred gandharvas, dwells in them.

The Buddha was on Mount Pítuó (which is north of the kingdom of Magadha), and Śakra told the musician-spirit Pañcaśikha to take his lapis lute and sing before the Buddha. The Buddha said: you can praise the Tathāgata with the lute and sing, and it is mournful and harmonious and touching and moves people’s hearts; and in that sound you speak of desire and bondage, of a śramaṇa of pure conduct, and of nirvāṇa — the many meanings are all there.

Śakra turned to him and said: I shall put you in your father’s place, and you shall be the highest among your kind — and he gave him a daughter to wife (Source: Dīrgha Āgama; Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom).

Kinnaras 緊那羅第三

One entry: where the kinnaras live, what a mast-pillar and a fit of jealousy bought their king, and what happened in the assembly when Druma played.

7. The Kinnaras

Where the kinnaras live, what their king gave to a stūpa, what he said about a monk who looked at his wife, and what Druma's playing did to Mahākāśyapa.

Chinese Source

緊那羅(亦云甄那羅也)住須彌山北。過小鐵圍有大黑山。亦在十寶山間。無有佛法日月星辰。由昔布施之力。今居七寶宮殿。壽命甚長。此王本在人中。有大長者興造佛塔。此緊那羅施一剎柱成辦寺廟。復以淨食施於工匠。壽盡作胸臆神。在兩山間。先在人中為大長者居財無量。有一沙門乞食。婦擎飯施之。乃大瞋怒云。此何乞人瞻視我婦。當令此人手脚斷壞。壽終以後受此醜形。八十四劫常無手足。諸天宴會皆悉與乾闥婆分番上下。天欲奏樂。而其腋下污流。便自上天也。有一緊那羅。名頭婁磨琴。歌諸法實相以讚世尊。時須彌山及諸林樹皆悉振動。迦葉在座不能自安。五百仙人心生狂醉。失其神足一時墮地(出菩薩胎經及大智論)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The kinnaras (also written kiṃnara) dwell north of Mount Sumeru; past the lesser iron-ring mountains there is a great black mountain, also among the ten jewel mountains, where there is no Buddha-Dharma and no sun, moon, or stars. By the power of former giving they now live in palaces of the seven treasures and their lifespans are very long.

This king was formerly among humans. A great elder was raising a stūpa for the Buddha, and this kinnara gave a single mast-pillar and so completed the temple, and gave pure food to the workmen besides; and when his life ended he became a chest-and-breast spirit dwelling between two mountains.

Formerly among humans he was a great elder of measureless wealth. A śramaṇa came begging for food and his wife carried out food and gave it to him, and he was greatly enraged: what beggar is this who looks at my wife? Let this man’s hands and feet be broken off. And when his life ended he received this ugly form, and for eighty-four kalpas has been constantly without hands or feet.

At the gods’ feasts they all take turns with the gandharvas, above and below. When the gods wish to have music played, and a foul flow comes from the kinnaras’ armpits, they go up to heaven themselves.

There was a kinnara named Druma who played the lute and sang the true character of all dharmas in praise of the World-Honored One; and at that time Mount Sumeru and all the trees of the forest shook, and Kāśyapa on his seat could not keep still, and five hundred sages’ minds grew wild and drunk and they lost their supernormal power and fell to the ground at once (Source: Sūtra of the Bodhisattva in the Womb; Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two separate accounts of the kinnaras' king are set side by side without a join, and the effect is a small study in how merit and offense run in parallel. One donation — a single mast-pillar for a stūpa, and pure food for the workmen — gives him a palace of the seven treasures and a very long life. One remark — what beggar is this who looks at my wife? Let this man's hands and feet be broken off — costs him eighty-four kalpas without hands or feet.

The closing anecdote is the famous one: Druma the kinnara king sings the true character of all dharmas to the lute, and Sumeru and the forest shake, Mahākāśyapa cannot keep still on his seat, and five hundred sages lose their supernormal powers and fall out of the air. The text says the sages' minds grew wild and drunk; it does not say the music was wrong.

Ghosts and Spirits of Various Kinds 雜鬼神第四

Fifteen entries, from a general account of where spirits reside to the two great preta catalogues, the taming of Āḷavaka and of Hārītī, and Māra's several failed attempts on the Buddha's disciples.

8. Ghosts and Spirits All Take Their Names from Where They Reside

Where spirits are: in houses, streets, markets, mountains, and graves, and one to each seven-foot tree; and how many attend a person who does the ten evil deeds.

Chinese Source

有四大天神一者地。二者水。三者風。四者火。地神自念云。地無水火風。佛曰。地中有水火風。但地大多故得名耳。水火風神皆各同然。佛為說法。皆受五戒為優婆夷。一切人民所居舍宅。皆有鬼神無有空者。街巷道陌屠會市肆及諸山塚。皆有鬼神無有空處。凡諸鬼神皆隨所依即以為名(樓炭經云樹高七尺圍一尺有鬼神)若人初生皆有鬼神隨逐擁護。若人欲死鬼收精氣。行十惡人若百若千共一神護。猶如國王以百千人侍衛一臣(出長阿合經第二十卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are four great heavenly spirits: the first earth, the second water, the third wind, the fourth fire. The earth spirit thought to itself: in earth there is no water, fire, or wind. The Buddha said: in earth there are water, fire, and wind; it is only because the earth element is the greater that it gets the name. And so likewise with the spirits of water, fire, and wind. The Buddha taught them the Dharma and they all received the five precepts and became lay followers.

In all the houses and dwellings where people live there are ghosts and spirits and none is empty; in streets and lanes and roads, in slaughter-yards and markets and shops, and in all the mountains and burial mounds, there are ghosts and spirits and no place is empty. All ghosts and spirits take their names from whatever they depend on (the Lokasthāna Sūtra says that a tree seven feet high and one foot round has a ghost-spirit in it).

When a person is first born there are always ghosts and spirits who follow and protect them; and when a person is about to die the ghosts gather in their vital breath. For a person of the ten evil deeds, a hundred or a thousand spirits together protect one — as a king has a hundred or a thousand men attending on one minister (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20).

9. The Fruit and Recompense of the Hungry Ghosts

The first of two preta catalogues: what they eat, why the Ganges looks like fire to them, and what the Buddha did about that.

Chinese Source

若有眾生生餓鬼中。常飢渴故目陷髮長。東西亂走或食嘔吐脂肉膿血屎尿涕唾盪滌餘汁。壽命無量百千萬歲。初不曾聞醬水之名。設復眼見急往趣之。變成猛火膿血。或時不變多人執仗不令得前。或夏降雨至身成火名惡果報。恒河水邊有諸餓鬼。其數五百。於無量歲初不見水。雖至河邊純見流火。飢渴所逼發聲號哭。餓鬼白佛。我等飢渴命將不久。佛言。恒河流水。鬼言。如來見水我常見火。佛言。除汝顛倒令得見水。廣說慳貪過。鬼言。我今渴久雖聞法言都不入心。先可入河恣意飲之。以佛力故即得飲水。佛為說法悉發菩提心。捨諸鬼形。或有餓鬼常被火燒。如劫盡時諸山出火。或有羸瘦狂走毛髮蓬亂以覆其身。或立廁溷邊伺求不淨。或有常求產婦餘血飲之。形如燒樹咽孔如針。若與水飲千歲不飽。或有自破其頭取腦而舐。或有形如黑山鐵鎖鎖頸。叩頭求哀歸命獄卒。或有先世惡口好以麁言加他眾人憎惡見之如讐。以此罪故墮餓鬼中。如是罪報受苦無量(出涅槃大智論)。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a being is born among the hungry ghosts, then, always hungry and thirsty, their eyes are sunken and their hair long, and they run wildly east and west; or they eat vomit, fat and flesh, pus and blood, excrement and urine, snot and spittle, and the dregs of washing-water. Their lifespan is measureless hundreds of thousands of myriads of years, and from the first they have never heard the name of a sauce or of water; and if they should see it with their eyes and rush toward it, it turns into fierce fire or pus and blood, or else many people take up staves and will not let them come near; or in summer, when rain falls, it turns to fire on their bodies. This is called the evil fruit of deeds.

By the bank of the Ganges there were hungry ghosts, five hundred of them, who for measureless years had never seen water; and though they came to the riverbank they saw only flowing fire. Pressed by hunger and thirst they cried out aloud. The hungry ghosts said to the Buddha: we are hungry and thirsty and our lives will not be long. The Buddha said: it is the flowing water of the Ganges. The ghosts said: the Tathāgata sees water; I always see fire.

The Buddha said: I shall remove your topsy-turvy view and let you see water — and he set out at length the faults of miserliness and greed. The ghosts said: I have thirsted a long time now, and though I hear the words of the Dharma they do not enter my mind at all; let me first go into the river and drink as I please. And by the Buddha’s power they were able to drink the water; and the Buddha taught them the Dharma and they all gave rise to the mind of enlightenment and cast off their ghost forms.

Or there are hungry ghosts constantly burned by fire, as the mountains put out fire at the kalpa’s end. Or some are emaciated and run about wildly, their hair tangled and covering their bodies. Or some stand beside privies watching for filth. Or some constantly seek the leftover blood of women in childbirth and drink it, their forms like burnt trees and the opening of their throats like a needle; and if given water to drink they are not filled in a thousand years. Or some split their own heads and take the brain and lick it. Or some have forms like black mountains with iron chains about their necks, and knock their heads and beg for pity and turn for refuge to the hell-warders.

Or there are those who in a former life were foul-mouthed and liked to give others coarse words, whom everyone hated and looked at as an enemy; and by this offense they fall among the hungry ghosts. Such is the recompense of offenses, and the suffering received is measureless (Source: the Nirvāṇa Sūtra and the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom).

10. A Ghost Buys Liquor and Tells the Seller to Take the Gold and Silver of a Dead Man in the Lake

A ghost who pays for his drink with a tip about a man who will drown, and the liquor-seller's conclusion about what ghosts can and cannot do.

Chinese Source

有人以沽酒為業。鬼現來飲酒未雇錢。而告主人言。明日當有一人持花上下白衣帶青幐。幐中有金銀千斤。當於湖中浴卒死不出。汝往取金銀保後無憂。明日主人伺候。見人來入水洗浴上岸著衣洗足却躄地而死。酒師往取得金銀如數。後日鬼來。主人作食出酒白神言。我見人著衣欲去乃死。何不於水中殺之使得上岸乎。鬼言。我不能殺人病人。我知人壽命衰耗時耳。師曰。天上天下鬼神。知人壽命罪福當至未至。不能活人不能殺人。不能使人富貴貧賤。但欲使人作惡犯殺。因人衰耗而往亂之。語其禍福令人向之設祠祀耳(出十卷譬喻經第六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a man whose trade was selling liquor, and a ghost appeared and came and drank liquor and had not yet paid the money; and it told the master: tomorrow a man will come carrying flowers, dressed in white with a blue sash, and in the sash there is a thousand catties of gold and silver. He will bathe in the lake and die suddenly and not come out. Go and take the gold and silver, and I guarantee there will be no trouble afterward.

The next day the master watched, and saw a man come and go into the water and bathe, and come up on the bank and put on his clothes and wash his feet, and then fall to the ground and die; and the liquor-seller went and took the gold and silver, and it was the sum stated.

On a later day the ghost came, and the master made food and brought out liquor and said to the spirit: I saw the man put on his clothes and about to go, and then he died. Why did you not kill him in the water so that he need not have come up on the bank? The ghost said: I cannot kill a man or make a man ill. I only know when a person’s allotted life is worn out.

The master said: the ghosts and spirits above and below heaven know when a person’s lifespan and their offense and merit are to come and are not yet come; they cannot bring a person to life and cannot kill a person, and cannot make a person rich or noble or poor or base. They only wish to make people do evil and offend by killing; and taking advantage of a person’s decline they go and trouble him, and tell him of calamity and blessing so as to make him set up sacrifices to them (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 6).

11. The Woman of the Golden Couch, Naked, Whose Clothing Burns When She Puts It On

A king out hunting finds a naked woman in a hut who can produce any food but cannot wear cloth, and what she says she once talked her husband out of giving.

Chinese Source

迦夷國王名梵摩達。時出遊獵於曠野。見有一屋(福報經云山邊樹下)即往趣之。中有一女。從求飲食無索不得。王請相見。女都裸形。王解衣與之著體便然。如是至三。王以問女。女答。昔為王妻。王飯沙門又施衣。時諫。但施飯足不復須衣(福報經云割施沙門)故受此罪。王欲令我得著衣者。為我作衣先施沙門及明經者。王求沙門又不能得。可施五戒賢者。賢者呪願云。願令金床女得福無量得著王衣。王問女言。女是何神。答曰。我勝於人。小不及天。故在鬼神道中。捨此命後當生第一天上(出譬喻經出福報經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The king of the kingdom of Jiāyí was named Brahmadatta, and he once went out hunting in a wilderness and saw a hut (the Sūtra of the Reward of Merit says beneath a tree at the mountain’s edge) and went toward it. Inside there was a woman, and whatever he asked her for by way of food and drink he did not fail to get. The king asked to see her, and the woman was entirely naked. The king took off his own clothes and gave them to her, and as soon as they touched her body they burned; and this happened three times over.

The king asked the woman about it, and the woman answered: long ago I was a king’s wife, and when the king fed a śramaṇa and also gave him clothing, I remonstrated: it is enough to give food; there is no need for clothing besides (the Sūtra of the Reward of Merit says: she cut short what was given to the śramaṇa). And so I receive this punishment.

If the king wishes me to be able to wear clothing, then make clothing for me and first give it to a śramaṇa or to one who understands the sūtras. The king sought a śramaṇa and could not find one, and it was said: you may give to a worthy man who keeps the five precepts. And the worthy man made the prayer: may the woman of the golden couch obtain measureless merit and be able to wear the king’s clothing.

The king asked the woman: what sort of spirit are you? She answered: I surpass humans and fall a little short of the gods, and so I am in the path of ghosts and spirits. When I give up this life I shall be born in the first heaven (Source: Parables Sūtra; the Sūtra of the Reward of Merit).

12. Two Ghosts Carrying a Corpse Pull Out a Man's Hands, Feet, Head, and Sides and Exchange Them; His Form Is Altered but His Mind Remains, and Meeting the Buddha He Attains the Path

A traveller asked to arbitrate between two ghosts over a corpse, who is then dismembered and reassembled from the corpse's parts, and goes to ask the monks whether he still has a body.

Chinese Source

昔人遠行獨宿空舍。夜中有鬼擔一死人來著其前。復有一鬼逐來瞋罵。死人我物汝忽擔來。先鬼言。是我物我自持來後鬼言。是死人實我擔來。二鬼各捉一足一手爭之。前鬼言。此有人可問。後鬼即問。是死人誰擔來。是人思惟。此二鬼力大。若實若妄俱不免死。語言。前鬼擔來。後鬼大瞋。捉其人手拔出著地。前鬼取死人一臂柎之。即著如是。兩臂兩脚頭脅舉身皆易。於是二鬼共食所易人身。拭口而去。其人思惟。我父母生身。眼見二鬼食盡。今我此身悉是他肉。我今定有身耶為無身耶。行到佛塔問諸比丘。廣說上事。諸比丘言。從本已來恒自無我。但以四大和合故計為我身。如汝本身與今。無異。諸比丘度之為道。得阿羅漢(出大智論第十二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago a man travelling far lodged alone in an empty house, and in the night a ghost came carrying a dead man and set him down in front of him; and another ghost came after in pursuit, scolding in anger: the dead man is mine, and you have suddenly carried him off. The first ghost said: he is mine and I brought him myself. The second ghost said: this dead man is truly one I carried here.

The two ghosts each took hold of a foot and a hand and contended over him. The first ghost said: there is a man here who may be asked. And the second ghost asked: who carried this dead man here?

The man thought: these two ghosts are strong, and whether I speak truly or falsely I shall not escape death — and said: the first ghost carried him. The second ghost was greatly enraged and took hold of the man’s hand and pulled it out and threw it on the ground; and the first ghost took one of the dead man’s arms and fitted it on, and it stuck. And so with both arms, both legs, the head, and the sides — his whole body was exchanged. And at that the two ghosts ate together the body they had exchanged, and wiped their mouths and went away.

The man thought: the body my father and mother bore — I have seen with my own eyes two ghosts eat it entirely; and this body of mine now is all another’s flesh. Do I now definitely have a body, or have I no body? He went to a Buddha stūpa and asked the monks and set out the whole matter at length.

The monks said: from the beginning there has never been a self at all; it is only because the four great elements come together that this body is reckoned to be a self. Your original body and this one are no different. And the monks delivered him and he entered the path and attained arhatship (Source: Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, fascicle 12).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is one of the most widely retold parables in the tradition and it is unusually economical. The traveller is asked to judge between two ghosts contending over a corpse, works out that a lie and the truth will kill him equally, and tells the truth — for which the losing ghost dismembers him, and the winning ghost repairs him piece by piece from the corpse until nothing of his original body is left. The two of them then eat the body that had been exchanged, wipe their mouths, and go.

The question he takes to the monks is exactly the right one and is put with no hysteria at all: I saw with my own eyes two ghosts eat the body my parents bore; this body is all another's flesh; do I now definitely have a body, or have I no body? The monks' answer is that there was never a self to begin with, and that his original body and this one are no different.

And they all gathered together and said: we may leave this country and go far off to another land.

This one son of ours — we pray that he be protected and delivered. Śakra and the Brahmā king, the Tathāgata’s disciples free of outflows, the arhats and pratyekabuddhas, and above all the Tathāgata, most honoured and highest, the fine field of merit, than whom there is none higher — let them look upon this. We pray that the Tathāgata may shine upon this our utmost sincerity. And they handed the boy over to the ghost and withdrew.

Then the World-Honored One, with the divine eye and the penetrating ear, heard and saw it, and by his power of supernormal travel went north of the Snow Mountains and entered the ghost’s dwelling and sat cross-legged.

Then the small boy came to the ghost’s dwelling and saw from afar the Tathāgata, his light and color blazing, the thirty-two marks and the eighty fine features adorning his body, and gave rise to a glad heart toward the Tathāgata; and, taking him for the evil ghost, he said: eat me as you please.

Then the World-Honored One said: Nāyūluó, it is as you say. I am now the Tathāgata, the truly worthy and perfectly awakened one, and I have come to save you and to subdue this ghost. The evil ghost came back to his own place and saw from afar the World-Honored One sitting upright and unmoving, and gave rise to rage, and rained thunder, lightning, and thunderbolts, or rained knives and swords; and before they reached the ground the Tathāgata turned them into utpala flowers. And he rained down every kind of thing, and the Tathāgata subdued each in turn.

[The ghost said:] I shall go now and question him on the deep meaning; and if he cannot answer me I shall take his two feet and throw him into the sea to the south. The Buddha said: there is no human or non-human who can take my two feet and throw me into the sea to the south. If you wish to ask, ask.

Then the ghost asked: what are the old actions, the new actions, and the ending of action? What the present body makes — the three of the body, the four of speech, and the three of the mind — this is new action. Know that when the old action is exhausted and does not arise again and one does not make action again, and can take hold of this so that action is never born again and is exhausted without remainder — this is called the ending of action.

The World-Honored One said: when I was a bodhisattva of old, a dove threw itself upon me, and I did not begrudge my body or life to save that dove from peril; how much less now that I have become the Tathāgata, should I give up this boy for you to eat? I ask only that the World-Honored One accept my repentance. And so three and four times over; and the World-Honored One said: I accept your repentance — and taught him the fine Dharma.

Then that evil ghost lifted up several thousand taels of gold and offered them to the World-Honored One: I now give this mountain valley to the saṅgha of the four quarters; I ask the World-Honored One to receive it from me. The World-Honored One received it and spoke this verse:

“Giving gardens and orchards, purely, / and making bridges of timber; / if one can build a great ship, / and all the means by which life is sustained — / day and night without slackening, / the merit obtained cannot be measured. / Complete in the Dharma, its meaning, and the precepts, / at the end one is born in heaven.”

The ghost said: what further instruction is there? The World-Honored One said: give up your original form and put on the three Dharma robes and become a śramaṇa; and go into the city of Vṛji and proclaim everywhere: the Tathāgata has appeared in the world and delivers those not yet delivered and brings them to release; he is most honoured and highest, the fine field of merit, and he has now delivered the small boy Nāyūluó and subdued the evil ghost Vaiśa. You may go to him and receive his teaching.

Then the ghost Vaiśa proclaimed these words in the kingdom of Vṛji, and the elder Good-Awakening, hearing them, could not contain his joy, and led a host of eighty-four thousand people to the World-Honored One (Source: Ekottarika Āgama, fascicle 8).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the taming of Āḷavaka, the yakṣa of Āḷavī who exacted one human life a day, and the compendium's own interlinear note supplies that name from another source. The doctrinal exchange in the middle is the reason the sūtra preserves the episode: asked what the old action, the new action, and the ending of action are, the Buddha answers that the eye and the other senses are old action, made in a former time; what the present body does through body, speech, and mind is new action; and the ending of action is the exhaustion of the old with no new arising.

What surrounds that exchange is worth reading for its ordinariness. The ghost's opening threat is to throw the Buddha into the sea by his feet, and his closing request is simply: I am extremely hungry; give me back my small boy. And the parents' prayer, made while handing their only son over, invokes in ascending order the earth-spirits, Śakra and Brahmā, the arhats and pratyekabuddhas, and finally the Tathāgata — a list of everything they can think of, in order of hope.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Hārītī story, and the Buddha's method in it is entirely practical: hide a dozen of her thousand children in the monastery and let her look for them for ten days. Nothing is said to her until she has stopped eating and is beating herself and crying out to heaven; and when she comes, the Buddha's first question is not an accusation but a repetition — where did you leave your children and go, that you lost them? — asked three times until she answers it herself.

The argument that follows is symmetrical and short: you have children and know how to love them, and others love theirs as you do, and the households that lose them weep in the roads as you have done. She is then given back her children and made the protector of other people's, which is the ordinary end of the story; but the compendium keeps a longer coda in which the Buddha names what her thousand sons actually do — pass themselves off as tree spirits, water spirits, spirits of ships and carts and houses and dreams and terrors, and so extract sacrifices from people who gain nothing by them.

One detail is a plain Sinicization and we have kept it as the Chinese has it: the hell she is warned of is the hell of Mount Tai, the Chinese underworld mountain, not an Indian one.

17. The Māra King Troubles Maudgalyāyana, Who Tells Him of Having Been a Māra in a Former Body

Then Maudgalyāyana was walking in the dark of night, and the evil Māra transformed himself and passed as a shadow into Maudgalyāyana’s belly. Maudgalyāyana entered samādhi and examined the source of it, and said to the evil Māra: come out, come out; do not trouble a disciple of the Buddha, or you will get suffering through the long night.

Māra thought: this śramaṇa has never seen me and speaks falsely at random. Even his teacher does not know me, much less his disciple. Maudgalyāyana answered: I know too what you are thinking. Māra was afraid: he has already perceived me — and, transforming himself, came out bodily and stood before him.

Maudgalyāyana told Māra: in a past age, in the time of the Buddha Krakucchanda, I was once a Māra named Dark-Resentment, and I had an elder sister named Deep-Black, and you were her son; and by this I know you are my sister’s son.

At that time a buddha appeared in the world and had two disciples, the first named Vast-Sound and the second Perception-Knower. Why was he called Vast-Sound? Because, standing in the Brahmā heaven, when he cleared his throat the sound was heard through the three thousand worlds. As for Perception-Knower, he dwelt alone in seclusion and sat beneath a mountain tree in the rightly received samādhi; and the cowherds and shepherds and those carrying firewood and grass said to one another: this man has died — and they cremated him together; and Perception-Knower rose from samādhi and went into the city on alms round (Source: Sūtra of the Evil Māra Testing Maudgalyāyana; also Madhyama Āgama, fascicle 27).

18. A Ghost Reads Another's Mind and Kills the Woman It Has a Grudge Against

曾有一女人。為餓鬼所持。即以呪術而問鬼言。何以惱他女人。鬼答之言。此女人者是我怨家。五百世中而常殺我。我亦五百世中。斷其命根。若彼能捨舊怨之心。我亦能捨。爾時女人作如是言。我今已捨怨心。鬼觀女人雖口言捨。而心不放。即斷其命(出抄毘婆沙經第八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was once a woman who was possessed by a hungry ghost, and someone questioned the ghost by a spell: why do you trouble this other woman? The ghost answered: this woman is my enemy. For five hundred lives she has constantly killed me, and I likewise for five hundred lives have cut off her life-root. If she can give up her old enmity of mind, I too can give it up.

Then the woman said: I have now given up my mind of enmity. The ghost observed that although the woman said so with her mouth, in her mind she did not let it go — and at once cut off her life (Source: Abridged Vibhāṣā Sūtra, fascicle 8).

19. Pāpīyas Troubles Mañjuśrī and Is Held Fast

Māra conjures four thousand ragged monks to shame the Buddha's order, and Mañjuśrī fills their bowls.

魔設供饍。化作四萬比丘。著垢弊敗衣執持破鉢。胸背悉露面貌醜惡。跛蹇痤僂心懷遑懅。受種種食。文殊令諸化人鉢食常滿。揣食在口饐不得納。身體不安自然躄地問於魔言。比丘何故不食。魔曰。是欲死矣。文殊曰。無毒之人豈復行垢毒耶。有婬怒癡人是則為毒。因為說法我無是毒。五百諸天從魔來者。發菩提心(出文殊現寶藏經下卷)。

Māra set out a feast and conjured up forty thousand monks in filthy and worn-out robes holding broken bowls, their chests and backs quite bare, their faces and features ugly, lame and hunchbacked, their hearts full of alarm; and they received food of every kind.

Mañjuśrī made the bowls of these conjured people always full; and the mouthfuls of food, in their mouths, choked them and could not be got down, and their bodies were uneasy and they fell to the ground of themselves.

He asked Māra: why do the monks not eat? Māra said: it is because they are about to die. Mañjuśrī said: how should a person without poison practice filthy poison? It is people with lust, anger, and delusion who are poison; and therefore I teach that I have no such poison. 20. The Hungry Ghosts Ask Maudgalyāyana the Causes by Which They Obtained Their Suffering

The great catalogue: eighteen hungry ghosts questioned in turn on the Ganges bank, each given the exact act that produced the exact torment.

Chinese Source

目連至恒河邊。見五百餓鬼群來趣水有守水鬼。鐵杖驅逐令不得近。於是諸鬼逕詣目連。禮目連足各問其罪。一鬼曰。我受此身常患熱渴。先聞恒河水清且涼。歡喜趣之沸熱壞身。試飲一口。五藏燋爛臭不可當。何因緣故受如此罪。目連曰。汝先世時作相師。相人吉凶。少實多虛。或毀或譽。自稱審諦以動人心。詐惑欺誑以求財利。迷惑眾生失如意事。復有一鬼言。我常為大狗利牙赤白來噉我肉遺有骨在。風來吹起。肉續復生。狗復來噉。此苦何因。目連答言。汝前世時作天祠主。常教眾生殺羊以血祠天。汝自食肉。是故今日以肉償之。復有一鬼言。我常身上有糞周遍塗漫。亦復噉之。是罪何因。目連答曰。汝前世時作婆羅門。惡邪不信。道人乞食取鉢盛滿糞以飯著上持與道人。道人持還以手食飯。糞污其手。是故今日受如此罪。復有一鬼言。我腹極大如甕。咽喉手脚其細如針。不得飲食。何因此苦。目連答言。汝前世時作聚落主。自恃豪貴。飲食縱橫輕欺餘人。奪其飲食飢困眾生。復有一鬼言。我常趣溷欲噉食糞。有大群鬼捉杖驅我不得近廁。中爛臭飢困無賴。何因如此。目連答言。汝前世時作佛圖主。有諸白衣供養眾僧供辦食具。汝以麁供設客僧。細者自食。復有一鬼言。我身上遍滿生舌。斧來斫舌。斷續復生如此不已。何因故爾。目連答言。汝前世時作道人。眾僧差作蜜漿。石蜜塊大難消。以斧斫之。盜心噉一口。以是因緣故還斫舌也。復有一鬼言。我常有七枚熱鐵丸直入我口。入腹五藏燋爛。出還復入。何因故受此罪。目連答言。汝前世時作沙彌。行果瓜子到其師所。敬其師故偏心多與實長七枚。復有一鬼言。常有二熱鐵輪。在我兩腋下。轉身體燋爛。何因故爾。目連答曰。汝前世時。與眾僧作餅。盜心取兩幡俠兩腋底。復有一餓鬼言。我丸極大如甕。行時擔著肩上。住則坐上進止患苦。何因故爾。目連答曰。汝前世時作市令。常以輕秤小斗與。重秤大斗取。常自欲得大利於己侵剋餘人。復有一鬼言。我常兩肩有眼。胸有口鼻。常無有頭。何因故爾。目連答言。汝前世時恒作魁膾。弟子若殺罪人時。汝常有歡喜心。以繩著結挽之。復有一鬼言。我常有熱鐵針。入出我身受苦無賴。何因故爾。目連答言。汝前世時作調馬師。或作調象。馬難制。汝以鐵針刺脚。又時牛遲亦以針刺。復有一鬼言。我身常有火出燃懊惱。何因故爾。目連答言。汝前世時作國王夫人。更一夫人王甚幸愛。常生妬心伺欲危害。值王臥起去。時所愛夫人眠猶未起著衣。即生惡心正值作餅。有熱麻油即以灌其腹。腹爛即死。復有一鬼言。常有旋風迴轉我身。不得自在隨意東西。心常惱悶。何因故爾。目連答言。汝前世時常作卜師。或時實語或時妄語。迷惑人心不得隨意。復有一鬼言。我身常如塊肉。無有脚眼耳鼻等。恒為蟲鳥所食。罪苦難堪。何因緣故。答言。汝前世時常與他藥墮他兒胎。復有一鬼言。常有熱鐵籠。籠絡我身。燋熱懊惱。何因受此。目連答言。汝前世時常以羅網掩捕魚鳥。復有一鬼言。我以物自蒙籠頭。亦常畏人來殺我。心常怖懼不可堪忍。何因故爾。答言。汝前世時婬犯外色常畏人見。或畏其夫捉縛打殺。或畏官法戮之都市。恐怖相續。復有一鬼問曰。我受此身。肩上常有銅瓶滿中洋銅。手捉一杓取自灌頭。舉體燋爛。如是受苦無數無量。有何罪咎。答言。汝前世時出家為道。僧典飲食。以一蘇瓶私著餘處。有客道人來者不與之。去已出蘇行與舊僧。此蘇是招提僧物一切有分。此人藏隱雖與不等。由是緣故受此罪也(出雜藏經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Maudgalyāyana came to the bank of the Ganges and saw five hundred hungry ghosts come in a crowd toward the water; and there was a water-guarding ghost who drove them off with an iron staff and would not let them come near. So the ghosts came straight to Maudgalyāyana and bowed at his feet and each asked about their offense.

One ghost said: receiving this body I constantly suffer heat and thirst. I heard before that the Ganges water was clear and cool and went toward it in delight, and it boiled and destroyed my body; and I tried drinking one mouthful and my five organs were scorched and rotted and the stench was unbearable. For what cause and condition do I receive an offense like this? Maudgalyāyana said: in a former life you were a physiognomist and read people’s fortunes, with little truth and much falsehood, sometimes disparaging and sometimes praising, and called yourself exact so as to move people’s hearts, deceiving and cheating in order to seek goods and gain, and confusing beings so that they lost what they wished for.

Another ghost said: a great dog with sharp teeth, red and white, constantly comes and eats my flesh and leaves the bones; and when the wind comes and blows on them the flesh grows again, and the dog comes and eats it again. What is the cause of this suffering? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were the master of a shrine to the gods and constantly taught beings to kill sheep and sacrifice the blood to the gods, and you yourself ate the flesh; and therefore today you repay it with flesh.

Another ghost said: my body is constantly covered and smeared with filth and I eat it besides. What is the cause of this offense? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were a brahmin, evil, wrong-minded, and without faith. A religious man came begging for food and you took his bowl and filled it with filth and put rice on top and gave it to him; and the religious took it home and ate the rice with his hand, and the filth soiled his hand. Therefore today you receive an offense like this.

Another ghost said: my belly is enormous as a jar and my throat and hands and feet are as thin as needles, and I cannot get food or drink. What is the cause of this suffering? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were the head of a village and, relying on your rank, ate and drank without restraint and slighted and cheated others and took away their food and left beings hungry and hard-pressed.

Another ghost said: I constantly go to the privy wishing to eat filth, and a great crowd of ghosts takes staves and drives me off and I cannot come near the privy; and the rot and stench within, and hunger and hardship, leave me nothing to depend on. What is the cause of this? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were a monastery director, and lay people made offerings to the community and prepared the food; and you set out the coarse food for the visiting monks and ate the fine yourself.

Another ghost said: tongues grow all over my body, and an axe comes and hacks the tongues, and they are cut and grow again and it never stops. What is the cause of this? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were a religious man, and the community appointed you to make honey-water; and the lumps of rock sugar were large and hard to dissolve and you hacked them with an axe, and with a thieving mind you ate one mouthful. For that cause and condition your tongue is hacked in return.

Another ghost said: seven hot iron balls constantly go straight into my mouth, and entering my belly my five organs are scorched and rotted, and they come out and go in again. For what cause do I receive this offense? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were a novice, and going round with fruit and melon seeds you came to your teacher, and out of respect for your teacher you partially gave him more, seven over the true measure.

Another ghost said: two hot iron wheels are constantly under my two armpits and turn, and my body is scorched and rotted. What is the cause of this? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you made cakes for the community and with a thieving mind took two of them and hid them under your two armpits.

Another hungry ghost said: my testicles are enormous as a jar, and when I walk I carry them on my shoulder and when I stop I sit on them, and going and stopping alike are a torment. What is the cause of this? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were a market officer, and you constantly gave with the light balance and the small measure and took with the heavy balance and the large measure, always wishing to get a great profit for yourself and encroaching on others.

Another ghost said: I constantly have eyes on my two shoulders and a mouth and nose on my chest, and no head at all. What is the cause of this? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were constantly an executioner, and when your apprentice killed a condemned man you constantly had a glad mind and put a rope on him and knotted it and pulled.

Another ghost said: hot iron needles constantly go in and out of my body and I suffer with nothing to depend on. What is the cause of this? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were a trainer of horses, or of elephants; when a horse was hard to control you pricked its legs with an iron needle, and when an ox was slow you pricked it with a needle too.

Another ghost said: my body constantly gives out fire and burns, and I am in anguish. What is the cause of this? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were a king’s consort, and there was another consort whom the king greatly favoured, and you constantly gave rise to a jealous mind and watched for the chance to harm her. Once the king rose from sleeping and went out, and the favoured consort was still asleep and had not risen and dressed; and you gave rise to an evil mind, and it happened that cakes were being made and there was hot sesame oil, and you poured it into her belly, and her belly rotted and she died at once.

Another ghost said: a whirlwind constantly turns my body about and I cannot be free but go east and west as it wills, and my mind is constantly vexed. What is the cause of this? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you were constantly a diviner, and sometimes spoke truly and sometimes falsely, and confused people’s minds so that they could not follow their own intentions.

Another ghost said: my body is constantly like a lump of flesh with no feet or eyes or ears or nose, and is constantly eaten by insects and birds, and the suffering of this offense is hard to bear. For what cause and condition? He answered: in a former life you constantly gave others drugs and aborted their children.

Another ghost said: a hot iron cage constantly cages and binds my body, and I am scorched and in anguish. For what do I receive this? Maudgalyāyana answered: in a former life you constantly spread nets and caught fish and birds.

Another ghost said: I cover my own head with something, and I am constantly afraid people will come and kill me, and my mind is constantly in a terror I cannot bear. What is the cause of this? He answered: in a former life you committed adultery outside your own household and were constantly afraid of being seen, or afraid her husband would seize and bind and beat you to death, or afraid the law would execute you in the market place — and the terror went on and on.

Another ghost asked: receiving this body, there is constantly a bronze jar on my shoulder full of molten copper, and with my hand I take a ladle and pour it over my own head, and my whole body is scorched and rotted; and I receive suffering like this beyond number and beyond measure. What offense is there in it? He answered: in a former life you went forth and entered the path, and had charge of the community’s food and drink; and you put one jar of ghee aside in another place, and when visiting religious men came you did not give it to them, and when they had gone you brought out the ghee and passed it round to the old monks. This ghee was the property of the saṅgha of the four quarters and all had a share in it; and this man hid it and, though he gave it out, gave it unequally. For that cause and condition he receives this offense (Source: the Miscellaneous Piṭaka Sūtra).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The longest entry in the fascicle and the most systematic: eighteen hungry ghosts on the bank of the Ganges, each describing a torment and each given by Maudgalyāyana the precise act that produced it. Read as a list it is a moral inventory of an ordinary society, and the professions named are worth noting: a physiognomist who flattered and disparaged for fees, a shrine-keeper who had sheep killed and ate the meat, a village head who took people's food, a market officer with two sets of weights, an executioner who enjoyed the work, a horse-trainer with a needle, a diviner, an abortionist, a fowler, an adulterer.

But five of the eighteen are monastics or monastery officials, and their offenses are strikingly small: a monastery director who served visiting monks the coarse food and ate the fine himself; a monk who ate one mouthful of rock sugar he was chopping for the community; a novice who gave his own teacher seven fruit-seeds over the true measure out of respect; a cook who took two cakes and hid them under his arms; and a steward who set aside one jar of ghee so that visiting monks would not get it. Each of these is repaid with a torment of the same shape as the act — the tongue hacked, the seven iron balls, the two burning wheels under the arms, the molten copper poured over the head from a jar carried on the shoulder.

The principle is stated only in the last case and applies to all of them: the ghee was the property of the saṅgha of the four quarters and everyone had a share in it. What is being punished is not appetite but the private appropriation of what was held in common.

The shortest and sharpest entry in the fascicle. The mechanism is never explained in the text — the reader is left to work out that the demon has been eating the anger.

22. A Ghost Comes Back and Whips Its Own Former Corpse

A ghost whipping its own corpse, and the inventory of what that body did.

Chinese Source

昔外國有人死。魂還自鞭其屍。傍人問曰。是人已死何以復鞭。報曰。此是我故身。為我作惡。見經戒不讀。偷盜欺詐犯人婦女。不孝父母兄弟。惜財不肯布施。今死令我墮惡道中。勤苦毒痛不可復言。是故來鞭之耳(出譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago in a foreign country a man died, and his soul came back and whipped his own corpse. A bystander asked: this man is already dead; why whip him again?

He answered: this is my former body. It did evil for me: seeing the sūtras and precepts it did not read them; it stole and cheated and violated other men’s wives; it was not filial to father and mother or to elder and younger brothers; it begrudged its goods and would not give. And now that it is dead it has made me fall into an evil destiny, and the hardship and the poison of the pain are beyond speaking of. That is why I have come to whip it (Source: Parables Sūtra).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A near-exact inversion of fascicle 44's entry 6, where a spirit comes back and strokes its own bones and lists what that body did not do — did not kill, steal, commit adultery, lie, slander, or covet — and so has been born in heaven. Here the same scene runs the other way: a soul whipping its own corpse and listing what it did do, and what that has cost.

The two entries come from parable collections the compendium cites under nearly the same title, two fascicles apart, and neither refers to the other. Printing both is the compendium doing what its title says: setting out the differences. This closes fascicle 46.
