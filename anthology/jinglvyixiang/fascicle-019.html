Unfathomable Depth

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 19 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Śrāvakas of Unfathomable Depth, Part 8" (聲聞不測淺深僧部第八) — twenty-four entries whose shared theme is that a monk's true attainment cannot be judged from outward appearance. Among them: five hundred monks bound with mere blades of living grass, choosing captivity over breaking a precept against harming a plant; a hungry ghost's own account of drinking water that turns to pus; the Ciñcā slander story and its doctrinal aftermath; and a monk who bares his own belly to a hunter's drawn bow rather than let the man miss and walk away unconverted.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 19 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 19 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 19 gathers twenty-four short entries under a heading that translates roughly as "monks whose depth cannot be fathomed" — a deliberate counterpoint to the previous fascicle's own more legible cause-and-effect stories. Several entries here instead show ordinary-seeming monks mistaken for frauds, demons, or fools by outside observers, their own genuine attainment visible only in hindsight or not at all. Two closely related ghost-encounter entries are grouped together near the close; every entry's full text remains intact.

1. Elīśa the Stingy Vaiśya

A wealthy but stingy man, embarrassed to find himself living worse than a poor neighbor, sneaks off to eat a rare good meal alone — only to be tricked by Śakra, disguised as a dog, into a strange bargaining game that ends with his entire fortune given away in his own name.

Chinese Source

昔有四姓。名伊利沙。財富無數。慳惜衣食。有貧老公。居與相近。日日自恣。飲食魚肉。賓客不絕。四姓自念。我財無數反不如此老公。便殺一鷄。炊一升白米。著車上到無人處。下車適欲飯。天帝化作犬來。上下視之。謂狗言。汝若倒懸空中者。我當與汝不。狗即倒懸空中。四姓意大恐曰。汝眼著地我當與汝不。狗兩眼脫地。四姓便徙去。天帝化作四姓身。乘車還家勅語。外人有詐稱四姓者。驅逐捶之。四姓晚還。門人罵詈令去。天帝盡取財物大布施。四姓亦不得歸。財物既盡為之發狂。天帝作一人問。汝何以愁曰。我財物了盡。天帝言。夫有珍寶令人多憂。五家無期積財不施。死為餓鬼。恒乏衣食。若脫為人。常墮下賤。汝不覺無常。富而且慳。貪惜不食。欲何望乎。天帝為說四諦。四姓意解。天帝化去。四姓歸。自悔前意。盡心施給財盡得道(出雜譬喻經第五卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there lived a man of the vaiśya caste, named Elīśa, whose wealth was beyond counting, yet who begrudged even his own clothing and food. Nearby lived a poor old man who, day after day, freely indulged himself in meat and fish, entertaining an endless stream of guests. Elīśa reflected: my own wealth is beyond counting, yet I live worse than this poor old man! — and, resolving to indulge himself for once, killed a chicken and cooked a measure of white rice, loading it onto a cart and driving to a deserted place, where he got down, intending to eat alone.

Śakra, transforming into a dog, appeared and looked him up and down, then said to the dog: if you hang yourself upside-down in midair, I shall give you some. The dog at once hung upside-down in the air. Elīśa, terrified at this sight, said: if your own eyes fall out onto the ground, I shall give you some. The dog's own two eyes fell to the ground. Elīśa, fleeing in terror, abandoned the place entirely.

Śakra then transformed himself into Elīśa's own likeness, rode the cart home, and ordered the household: should anyone outside falsely claim to be the master, drive him off and beat him. When the real Elīśa returned that evening, the gatekeeper reviled him and drove him away. Śakra, in Elīśa's own form, then took his entire fortune and gave it away in vast charity. Elīśa himself, unable even to return home, and his own wealth now entirely exhausted, went half mad with grief.

Śakra, transforming into an ordinary man, asked him: why do you grieve so? He answered: my own wealth has been entirely exhausted. Śakra told him: to possess treasure at all is itself a source of great worry — for wealth hoarded rather than given away is subject to five kinds of loss without warning, and, at death, one who hoards is reborn a hungry ghost, forever lacking food and clothing; and, should one escape even that fate to be reborn human, one falls always into the lowest station. Do you not see that all is impermanent? Wealthy, yet stingy, coveting even the food you will not eat — what could you possibly hope to gain from this? Śakra then taught him the four noble truths, and Elīśa's mind opened in understanding. Śakra then vanished, and Elīśa, returning home, repented his own former ways, and, giving away everything with a full and sincere heart, in time, once his wealth was exhausted, attained the path (Source: Miscellaneous Similes Sūtra, fascicle 5).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Śakra's own game with the dog is a small, cruel comedy of escalating commitment — each new demand builds on Elīśa's own prior concession, so that by the time the dog's eyes are literally falling out, he has already invested too much strangeness to simply walk away without a further, equally strange demand. The story never has Elīśa consciously choose generosity at all; his entire fortune is given away in his own name, without his own consent, by an impersonator, and it is only in poverty afterward that he arrives, on his own, at the same conclusion Śakra's charade was steering him toward all along.

2. The Poor Sweeper's Palace-in-Waiting

Unable to afford any offering at all, a poor household simply sweeps the Buddha's own monastery grounds — and an elder, spotting the jeweled palace already being built for this act in some unseen future, buys the "good thing" the poor family doesn't yet know they own.

Chinese Source

昔祇洹邊。有一貧家。欲供養世尊及諸弟子。居貧困窮無所施設。便行掃佛精舍。至心不惓。貧賢者舍邊有長者。遊行觀見。大澤中有數十間七寶舍。遙見便往問人言。誰作殿舍。好妙乃爾。其人答曰。有貧窮賢者。掃佛精舍。福應生此。並作殿舍待之耳。長者喜言。我當圖之。便到貧者家語言卿。有好物與我。我與卿五百兩金。曰我從來貧困。那得好物。長者言。卿但許我。曰可爾。便與五百兩金賢者得金。廣設檀施。佛為說法。即得道迹(出諸經中要事)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, near Jetavana, there lived a poor household who wished to make offerings to the World-Honored One and his disciples, but, being utterly destitute, had nothing at all to offer — and so, with a sincere and tireless heart, simply swept the Buddha's own monastery grounds instead.

An elder, traveling and observing the countryside, came upon a vast open field where several dozen buildings of the seven treasures stood, and, seeing this from a distance, went and asked someone: who has built such fine palace halls here? He was told: there is a poor, worthy person who sweeps the Buddha's own monastery grounds, and it is his own merit that has prepared these very halls, awaiting him here in a future life. The elder, delighted, thought: I shall claim this for myself — and went to the poor man's household, saying: you have something fine that I desire; I shall give you five hundred taels of gold for it. The poor man answered: I have always been destitute — how could I possibly own anything fine? The elder said: only agree to my terms. He consented, and received the five hundred taels of gold. The worthy man, now with this wealth, made offerings widely; the Buddha taught him the Dharma, and he attained a glimpse of the path at once (Source: Essential Matters from the Various Sūtras).

3. Vīraseṇa's Seven Days of Delay

Told he has exactly seven days left before dying into hell, and that only ordination can spare him that fate, a wealthy householder still cannot resist "just one more day" of pleasure, delaying his own rescue until Ānanda's daily visits finally run out the clock entirely.

Chinese Source

佛在毘舍離。食時著衣持鉢。將阿難入城乞食。時毘舍離城。有居士名毘羅斯那。命斷賓客。於七日中五欲自娛。語守門人勿通外門。時佛乞食。漸詣毘羅那家。時聞作樂聲。便告阿難侍者。阿難依事報答。佛言。今此長者。過七日已當命終。亡生啼哭地獄中。所以然者。本造善根於此便斷。更不造新。以是命終生地獄中。阿難白言。頗有方便。使彼居士過此七日命不終耶。佛告阿難。無有方便使命不終。但有方便免地獄耳。若彼長者。剃除鬚髮著三法衣。以信堅固出家學道。長者乃得不墮地獄。阿難白言。我今當勸使彼出家。時毘舍離凡諸釋種。常與阿難要言。阿難所言到家。若彼家中。有眠寐者。及以眾事。皆聽阿難使人直入。所以然者。言語柔和語不害人。爾時阿難語守門者。汝可往白之。守門即入。具宣此事。長者即便捨樂速出禮足阿難。白言阿難。願入我家福度男女。阿難報言。彼如來者知時不妄。記汝長者七日當亡。命終當生啼哭地獄。長者聞是恐怖悲泣。白阿難言。頗有方便令不終耶。阿難報言。無有方便得不終者。但有方便免地獄耳。長者問故。阿難報言。若汝出家便免地獄。時此長者念付家業。後往佛所出家學道。便作是言。七日猶遠。我於今日五欲自娛。清旦當往。阿難至時。著衣持鉢入城乞食。至居士家語居士言。今一日已過餘六日在。可時出家。長者報言。我後當往。如是復言。更欲自樂。後日乃往。如是推遷不能自盡。阿難日日常往勸之。至六日滿。前七日朝。阿難復語。乃付家業。與大小別。便至佛所。世尊命阿難。與受具戒。受戒已畢。於前七日後夜命終。生四王天。阿難問佛。得生。何處。佛告阿難。毘羅斯那比丘。命過生四王天。於彼命終。生三十三天。如是流轉人天。經歷九劫。天上人間更互受生於最後身。復來人間。剃除鬚髮。以信堅固。出家學道。成辟支佛。名毘羅斯那。所以然者。此毘羅斯那比丘。受具足戒精進命終如是。阿難梵行果報。所生受福(出毘羅斯那居士五欲娛樂經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Vaiśālī. At mealtime, robing himself and taking up his bowl, he entered the city to beg, together with Ānanda. In Vaiśālī there lived a householder named Vīraseṇa, who had dismissed all outside guests, indulging himself in the five sensory pleasures for seven days straight, and had told his own gatekeeper to admit no one from outside. The Buddha, begging in his own turn, gradually approached Vīraseṇa's own household, and, hearing the sound of music within, told his attendant Ānanda of this. Ānanda reported the matter as it was, and the Buddha said: this very elder, seven days from now, shall die, and, dying, shall be reborn weeping in hell — for the roots of goodness he once cultivated have now been cut off entirely, and he has cultivated nothing new since. It is for this very reason that, at death, he will be reborn in hell.

Ānanda asked: is there any means by which this householder might be spared from dying within these seven days? The Buddha told Ānanda: there is no means to prevent his own death — but there is a means to spare him from hell. Should this elder shave his own hair and beard, don the three monastic robes, and, with firm faith, go forth to study the path, he might yet avoid falling into hell. Ānanda said: I shall go now and urge him to go forth.

At that time, the Śākya clan of Vaiśālī had a standing agreement with Ānanda that, wherever he went, even into a household where people slept or were otherwise occupied, he should be admitted directly, without hindrance — for his own words were always gentle and harmed no one. Ānanda told the gatekeeper to announce him, and the gatekeeper went in and reported the whole matter. The elder at once set aside his own pleasures and hurried out to bow at Ānanda's feet, saying: Ānanda, please enter my household and bless both my sons and daughters. Ānanda answered: the Tathāgata knows the proper time and never speaks falsely — he has foretold that you, elder, shall die within seven days, and, dying, shall be reborn weeping in hell. The elder, hearing this, was seized with terror and wept, and asked Ānanda: is there any means to prevent my own death? Ānanda answered: there is no means to prevent your death — but there is a means to spare you from hell. The elder asked what this meant, and Ānanda told him: should you go forth yourself, you would be spared from hell.

The elder, reflecting on how to settle his own household affairs first, then intended to go later to the Buddha to go forth, but said to himself: seven days is still far off — today I shall still indulge myself in the five pleasures, and set out at dawn. When Ānanda came at the appointed hour, robed and bowl in hand, entering the city to beg, he went to the householder's own home and told him: one day has already passed, and six days remain — you should go forth now. The elder answered: I shall go later. And so it continued, the elder each time wishing to indulge himself once more and saying he would go the next day, delaying again and again, unable to bring himself to act, while Ānanda came daily to urge him on. When the sixth day was complete, on the morning of the seventh day, Ānanda urged him once more — and only then did the elder settle his own household affairs, take leave of his family both great and small, and go to the Buddha. The World-Honored One had Ānanda confer the full precepts upon him. Once ordained, in the latter part of that same seventh day's night, his own life ended, and he was reborn in the heaven of the Four Heavenly Kings.

Ānanda asked the Buddha where he had been reborn. The Buddha told Ānanda: the monk Vīraseṇa, his life having ended, was reborn in the heaven of the Four Heavenly Kings; and, that life ending in turn, he was reborn in Trāyastriṃśa heaven — and so he continued, transmigrating between the heavens and the human world across nine kalpas, until, in his own final birth, he was reborn once more among humans, where he shaved his own hair and beard, and, with firm faith, went forth to study the path, and attained the fruit of a pratyekabuddha, under the very same name, Vīraseṇa. And why was this so? Because this same monk Vīraseṇa, having received the full precepts, applied himself with diligence right up to his own death — and it is Ānanda's own pure conduct and its fruit that brought about the blessing he then received (Source: Sūtra of the Householder Vīraseṇa's Five-Fold Pleasure).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Vīraseṇa's own delay is entirely rational at every single step — each day, six remaining still feels like plenty of time, and one more day of pleasure seems a negligible cost against that remaining margin — which is exactly what makes the story's own structure so effective: the danger never announces itself as urgent until the morning it finally is, and by then only Ānanda's own relentless, daily insistence has kept the ordination possible at all. Nine full kalpas of onward transmigration follow directly from those final, unhurried hours of hesitation — a scale of consequence the story sets in deliberate, uncomfortable proportion to how ordinary and human the original procrastination actually was.

4. The Monk Who Begged Feathers from Birds

A merchant who shuns begging monks is won over through patient teaching — and Buddha traces the monk's own earlier timidity back through two past lives: an elephant startled into senseless flight, and a sage undone by a jewel-hungry request that ends a fragile friendship with a dragon.

Chinese Source

佛住王舍城曠野精舍。有五百比丘。皆乞作房。有估客見比丘來。即閉肆歸家避之。比丘餘道邀之相值。仍說果報。教令生信。乃至手撮其頭。強勸布施。所以然者。令汝得色力壽命。增益功德逮甘露果。估客聞之生信。少多布施。後見舍利弗。具以是事訴舍利。舍利聞已。說法令喜。還具白佛。佛告營事比丘。過去世時。有比丘。名跋[王*處]。止住林中。時釋軍多鳥。亦棲此林。晨暮亂鳴惱於跋[王*處]跋[王*處]詣世尊所。頂禮佛足。於一面立。世尊慰問。少病少惱安樂住林中耶。比丘答曰。少病少惱樂住於中。但釋軍多鳥。鳴喚惱亂不得思惟。佛告比丘。汝欲令此鳥一切不來耶。答曰。願爾。佛言比丘。此鳥來時。汝從眾鳥各乞一毛。比丘依教乞毛。諸鳥各落一毛。朝朝去時如是復乞。時鳥即移異處一宿。不得安樂尋復來還。時比丘復從乞毛。復各與一。眾鳥相謂。沙門乞毛不已。恐我不久毛衣都盡。不能復飛。更共議言。此比丘常住林中。我等應去。更求餘棲。諸比丘具以白佛言。林中比丘怯劣。喜亂畏惡鳥聲。佛言。不但今日怯劣。過去世時有一龍象。住在林中。大風卒起。吹折樹木。象聞折聲。驚怖奔走。怖心小歇。又蔭餘樹。餘樹復折。遂復奔走。時天見象念言。此象橫自狂走。即說偈言。風暴林樹折　龍象驚怖走　假使普天下　龍象何處避。佛言。于時象者。林中比丘是也。佛復告比丘。過去世時。有五百仙人。住雪山中。時一仙人。於別處住。有好泉水。華果茂盛。去是不遠。有薩羅水。水中有龍。龍見仙人威儀庠序。心生愛念來詣仙人。正復值仙結跏趺坐。龍遶仙人七匝。以頭覆其頂上而住。日日如是。唯有食時不來。仙人以龍遶身故。日夜端坐不得休息。身體萎羸便生瘡疥。爾時近處有人居止。供養仙人。詣仙人所。見羸劣疥搔。即問。何故如是。仙人具說上事。又問曰。欲令龍不復來耶。答曰然。復語仙人。是龍咽上有瓔珞寶珠。可從龍索珠。龍法性慳終不相與。龍來便從索珠。龍聞乞此。心即不喜徐捨而去。明日龍來遙說偈曰。光耀摩尼寶　瓔珞莊嚴身　若龍能施我　乃為善親友。龍偈答曰。畏失摩尼珠　猶執杖呼狗　寶珠不可得　更不來看汝　上饌及眾寶　由此摩尼尊　是終不可得　何故慇懃求　多求親愛離　由是更不來。時有天人。於虛空中。說偈曰。厭薄所以生　皆由多求故　梵志貪相現　龍則潛于淵。佛告諸營事比丘。龍象是畜生。尚惡多求。豈況於人。汝等比丘。莫為多營事廣索無厭。令彼信心婆羅門居士。苦惱捨財(出僧祇律第七卷又出彌沙塞律第三卷又不合同)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in the Wilderness Monastery of Rājagṛha, where five hundred monks were each begging materials to build their own huts. A merchant, seeing monks approaching, shut his shop and fled home to avoid them. The monks caught up with him another way, and taught him of karmic fruit, guiding him toward faith — even taking hold of his own head to press generosity upon him, saying: this will grant you vigor, strength, and long life, and increase the merit that reaches the fruit of ambrosia. The merchant, hearing this, came to faith, and gave a small offering. Later he met Śāriputra and told him the whole matter; Śāriputra, hearing it, taught him the Dharma and brought him joy, then reported the whole affair fully to the Buddha.

The Buddha told the monks in charge of the building work: in a past life there was a monk named Bachu, dwelling in a forest, where a great many birds of the Śakra-host also roosted, crying out in confusion morning and evening and troubling him greatly. Bachu went to the World-Honored One, bowed at his feet, and stood to one side. The World-Honored One asked him: are you free of illness and trouble, dwelling at ease in the forest? The monk answered: I am free of illness and trouble, and dwell there happily — but for the birds of the Śakra-host, whose crying disturbs my thought entirely. The Buddha asked the monk: do you wish these birds never to come again at all? He answered: I do wish it. The Buddha said: monk, when these birds next come, beg one feather from each of them. The monk did as instructed, and each bird let fall one feather. Each morning as they departed he begged again, and the birds moved for one night to another place — but, finding no peace there either, soon returned. The monk again begged feathers from them, and again each gave one. The birds said among themselves: this monk begs feathers from us without end; before long, I fear, our feathers will be entirely gone, and we shall no longer be able to fly. They resolved together: since this monk dwells here permanently, we ourselves should leave, and seek another roost.

The monks reported this fully to the Buddha, saying: the monk in the forest is timid, easily unsettled, fearful of the mere cries of birds. The Buddha said: it is not only today that he is timid. In a past life, there was a dragon-elephant dwelling in a forest. A great wind suddenly arose, blowing down the trees; hearing the crashing sound, the elephant, terrified, fled — and, its fear only just subsiding, it sheltered beneath another tree, which then also broke, so that it fled once more. A deva, seeing the elephant, thought: this elephant flees wildly for no true cause — and spoke a verse:

"The wind is fierce, the forest trees break — / the dragon-elephant, terrified, flees. / Yet were the whole world beneath heaven its refuge, / where could a dragon-elephant escape to?"

The Buddha said: the elephant of that time is this same forest-dwelling monk now. The Buddha further told the monks: in a past life, there dwelt five hundred sages in the Himalayan mountains. One sage lived apart, where there was a fine spring and flourishing flowers and fruit, and, not far off, a lake called Sarovāra, in which lived a dragon. The dragon, seeing the sage's dignified and composed bearing, felt affection for him and came to him — arriving just as the sage sat in full lotus posture — and coiled around the sage seven times, resting its own head upon the crown of his head. Day after day it did this, absent only at mealtimes. Because of the dragon's coils, the sage sat upright day and night without any rest at all, until his body wasted away and broke out in sores and scabies.

A man living nearby who supported the sage came to him, and, seeing him so wasted and afflicted, asked why. The sage told him the whole matter. The man asked further: do you wish the dragon to come no more? He answered: yes. The man told the sage: this dragon wears a jeweled pearl at its own throat as a necklace — ask the dragon for that pearl. By its very nature a dragon is stingy and will never part with such a thing; when it comes, ask it for the pearl. Hearing this request, the dragon's heart was displeased, and it withdrew slowly. The next day, the dragon came, and, from a distance, spoke a verse:

"A shining maṇi jewel, / a necklace adorning my own body — / could the dragon give this to me, / that would make a true and worthy friend."

The dragon answered in verse:

"Fearing to lose my maṇi pearl, / like a man who grips his stick to call off his own dog. / The jeweled pearl cannot be had — / I shall come to see you no more. / Fine food and every treasure alike / rest upon this maṇi's own honor; / it can never, in the end, be given — / why do you beg for it so earnestly? / Too much asking drives even love away; / for this very reason, I come no more."

At that time a deva, in the open sky, spoke this verse:

"Weariness and estrangement both arise / entirely from excessive demand. / Once a brahmacārin's own greed shows itself, / the dragon retreats into the depths."

The Buddha told the monks in charge of the building work: even a dragon-elephant, a mere animal, despises excessive demand — how much more should this hold true for human beings! You monks: do not busy yourselves in excessive undertakings, seeking without limit, causing faithful brahmins and householders to suffer as they give up their own wealth (Source: Sāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 7; also found, with variations, in the Mahīśāsaka Vinaya, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"跋[王*處]" (Bachu) records an uncommon compound character that does not correspond to any settled Sanskrit or Pali name known to us; rather than force a reconstruction, we keep the Chinese sound as a working pinyin name. The entry's own two past-life illustrations don't actually match each other in moral shape — the elephant is purely a victim of misplaced panic with no wrongdoing of its own, while the sage's own request for the pearl is what actually drives the dragon away — and the compendium lets both stand side by side under the same heading of "timidity," trusting the reader to notice the asymmetry rather than resolving it for us.

5. Yaśas and His Mother's Request

During a famine, a monk's own mother — desperate to keep the family line and its property from passing to the crown — persuades her ordained son into a single act of transgression with his former wife. Buddha traces the pattern back through a cosmological account of desire's very origin, and a jātaka of a golden deer betrayed by scent and honey.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the Buddha, together with five hundred monks, arrived in the country of Vṛji and dwelt in the city of Vaiśālī. The people were suffering famine, the five grains had failed to ripen, the dead lay scattered everywhere, and alms were hard to come by. In that city lived an elder named Yaśas. Yaśas had gone forth as a monk, but, alms being so hard to obtain, often returned to his own family home in search of food. His mother told him: you suffer so greatly, yet our own household's wealth is not small — use it freely. Your wife still lives; you two could yet live together, and you would still be free to give in offering to the Three Jewels. She said this three times over, but Yaśas would not agree. His mother told him again: if you have no wish for the five sensory pleasures, then simply give me your own seed, to continue our family line — so that, after I die, our property will not pass into the crown's own hands. Yaśas answered: if it is only to leave a line of descent that you ask, I shall now submit to this command. His mother hurried inside and told his former wife to dress and adorn herself well; after a single meeting between them, the wife conceived, and later bore a son, who was for this reason named "Continuing the Line" — and, from then on, people called even the family's own property by that same name.

Yaśas, hearing himself spoken of in this way, felt great shame, and reported the matter to Śāriputra, who in turn reported it to the World-Honored One. Yaśas and Śāriputra together went before the Buddha and told him the whole matter fully. The Buddha said: monk Yaśas, such a thing has never before occurred within the Saṅgha. You foolish man — you have opened a gate to grave transgression.

The Buddha said: it is not only today, within my own teaching, that this flaw has appeared. In a past age, in the heaven of Light and Sound, when this world had only just come into being, there was a being who, restless and covetous, was the first to taste the very savor of the earth; the other beings, following his example in turn, grew attached to it in their own hearts, and their bodies grew heavy — and from this, desire first arose, their spiritual powers receded, and their own inner radiance went out. It was from this time onward that sun and moon first came to be. That restless being was Yaśas himself. And it is not only today that his mother has led her own son astray — she did so once before as well, as the Buddha once told the monks.

At that time the country was called Kāśī, its city Vārāṇasī, and its crown prince was named Great Renown, who governed the world through giving and through upholding the precepts. His chief consort, observing the stars, saw a golden deer-king passing overhead from the south, crossing the sky toward the north. The consort thought to herself: could I obtain that hide to make into a cushion, I should die with no regret left at all — and, failing to obtain it, resolved to make it a condition of remaining the king's own consort. She removed her own jeweled ornaments, put on soiled and worn-out clothing, and shut herself in a chamber of grief. The king, coming to look for her, asked his attendants, who told him she had gone into the chamber of grief. He went himself and asked who had wronged her; she remained silent and did not answer. He sent others to ask, and still she did not answer. He then summoned an old and trusted attendant, skilled in many methods of persuasion, who coaxed her by various means, until she answered: no one has wronged me — I am simply troubled by a memory, and so have said nothing — and told of having seen the golden deer, and of wishing to obtain its hide for a cushion.

The attendant reported this to the king, who asked his ministers who could obtain it; he then gathered all the hunters and ordered them to search urgently. The hunters all said: we have never even heard of such a creature, still less seen it — and the king had them confined to prison. Among them was one hunter named Nāḍa, brave, strong, and swift enough to outrun any beast, who could bring down a flying bird with a single upward shot, no arrow ever falling in vain; reflecting that he himself was innocent yet imprisoned, he pressed his case and asked: has anyone at all seen or heard of this deer? The king said: you may ask the consort yourself. The consort answered: I saw it from my own tower, coming from the south, crossing the sky toward the north. The hunter, being skilled in reading the habits of birds and beasts, understood at once that the deer must rest in the south and feed in the north, and resolved to seek it at its feeding ground.

The hunter, bow and arrows in hand, traveled gradually northward, until he came to the Himalayan mountains, where a sage dwelt. He set aside his hunting gear, went to the sage, bowed, and greeted him; the sage bade him sit, and offered him drink and fruit. The hunter asked how long he had dwelt there; the sage answered, many years. The hunter asked whether he had ever witnessed anything extraordinary. The sage answered: south of this mountain there is a tree called the nyagrodha; a golden deer-king comes flying there constantly to feed on its leaves. The sage showed him the way; arriving beneath the tree, he saw it spreading wide, its leaves covering a broad canopy, and soon after saw the deer itself, coming like the king of wild geese, crossing the sky, to alight in the tree and feed on its leaves — and, once sated, fly off again.

He returned and reported to the king that no net or arrow could reach such a creature, and that there was no way to capture it; he suggested the king himself consult the consort. The consort told the hunter: smear honey upon the leaves near the peak of the tree, then set a snare below, in the deer's own path; drawn by the scent, it will eat the leaves and gradually descend, until it reaches the snare and is caught, and can then be driven away captive. The sage, seeing this from afar, said: alas, what a bitter misfortune — though able to ride the very air, it cannot escape the hands of wicked men. The sage spoke this verse:

"Among all evils in this world, / none surpasses fragrance and flavor. / They deceive ordinary men, / and every creature of forest and field alike. / Drawn by the wind toward scent and taste, / one suffers this very affliction."

The hunter smeared honey upon the leaves, caught the deer, and led it back. When the king heard of the deer's arrival, he burned incense to welcome it. The consort, seeing it, ran forward and embraced the deer-king — and, through this very contact, so heavy was her own defiled attachment, that the deer-king's golden color at once faded away. The king asked the consort: this deer's golden color has suddenly vanished — what shall we do now? The consort answered: this gold was never a thing that could be truly given — release it, and let it go. That golden deer was Yaśas himself; that consort was Yaśas's own mother — both still bound, even now, by the very attachment they suffered then (Source: Sāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The cosmological aside — desire itself entering the world through one restless being's first taste of the earth's own savor, with the sun and moon appearing only afterward — is one of the compendium's more striking cosmogonic fragments, worth reading on its own terms rather than only as backstory for Yaśas. Note too that in the jātaka, it is the mother figure's own touch, not the hunter's snare, that actually extinguishes the deer's gold — the story locating the deepest harm not in capture but in a particular, possessive kind of contact.

6. Nandin's Fall, and the Boy Deer-Dapple

A meditator of seven years' standing loses his concentration to a temptress who vanishes into a dead horse's carcass — and Buddha traces the pattern to a hermit's foundling son, raised on deer's milk, undone in turn by a heavenly seductress sent to break his growing spiritual power.

Chinese Source

After seven years, he lost his own concentration, and returned to sit beneath a tree, seeking once more to regain his former absorption. Māra, watching for an opening, transformed himself into a woman of unmatched beauty, who stood before him and said: monk, come and lie with me. Nandin said: evil thing, be gone at once! Evil thing, be gone at once! — yet, though his mouth spoke thus, his eyes did not turn away; and this repeated itself a second and a third time. The heavenly woman then removed her own clothing, standing bare before him, and Nandin, seeing her form, felt desire take hold of his own heart. As she then slowly stepped backward, Nandin called out: wait a little! — and she fled swiftly to a ditch by Jetavana, where lay a dead horse, and there vanished from sight entirely. Nandin, his own desire now inflamed, coupled with the dead horse; and once his desire had subsided, he saw for himself the wrongness of what he had done, and, removing his monastic robe, went to Jetavana and told the other monks: I have committed a grave transgression.

The monks asked him the circumstances, and Nandin told the whole matter; they reported it to the Buddha, who had him expelled. The monks asked the Buddha: Nandin had long cultivated pure practice — how could he be deceived by a mere woman? The Buddha said: it is not only now that Nandin has been deceived by a woman — it was so in a past life as well.

In a past age, in the southern country of Avanti, there lived a man of the Kāśyapa clan who had gone forth as a non-Buddhist ascetic, learned and widely read, and who assisted the king in governing the realm. The king, upholding the law of the land, would have criminals tortured and punished; the ascetic thought to himself: having already gone forth from household life, how can I still assist the king in judging such matters? — and told the king: I wish to go forth as a renunciant. The king said: but you have already gone forth — why do you now say you wish to go forth? He answered: while I still take part in these various punishments, how can this be called true renunciation? I now wish to go forth according to the way of the sages. The king consented, and the ascetic built a hermitage on Mount Baiyan, where, through the practice of the sages' own methods, he attained the five supernormal powers.

One day, through an involuntary bodily discharge, his own impure fluid fell to the ground; a doe drank this and licked the place, and thereby conceived, later giving birth to a small human child. The sage, coming upon the doe and finding she had borne a child, wondered greatly: how can an animal give birth to a human being? — and, entering meditative absorption to examine the matter, understood the child to be his own, and took it in to raise. Because of its birth from a doe, its body was dappled like a deer's, and so it was named Deer-Dapple. The sage reflected: nothing corrupts what is right and destroys virtue so thoroughly as women — and so taught the boy meditative absorption, guiding him to the five supernormal powers, and spoke this verse for him:

"Among all classes of living beings, / none escape their return to death. / Each follows where its own karma leads, / and receives its own fruit in turn. / Those who do good are reborn in heaven; / those of evil conduct fall into hell. / One who practices the path, cultivating pure conduct, / exhausts all outflows and attains nirvāṇa."

The sage then passed away, and the boy, cultivating pure conduct on his own, attained the five supernormal powers in turn. Śakra, riding his own white dragon-elephant, surveyed the world to see who among beings was filial to their parents, who made offerings to śramaṇas and brahmins, and who practiced giving, upheld the precepts, and cultivated pure conduct — and, in his survey, came upon this sage-boy. Śakra thought: should this boy set his own mind upon becoming Śakra or a Brahmā-king, he could well attain it — he ought to be undone at once. He then devised a means, striking the Dharma-drum, and a hundred thousand devas all gathered together. Śakra announced: in Jambudvīpa there is a sage-boy named Deer-Dapple, of great merit, whom I intend to undo.

The assembled devas, hearing this, were displeased, and thought to themselves: to undo this person will only diminish the ranks of the devas and strengthen the asuras. One deva then spoke up, asking who should go to carry this out; another answered that a heavenly woman should go. She was summoned at once, and, in that same moment, a hundred thousand heavenly women all gathered together. Among them was one named Ālànfú, whose own hair held four colors — blue, yellow, red, and white — and was for this reason called "many-colored." This heavenly woman was chosen to go to Jambudvīpa and undo the boy Deer-Dapple. She told Śakra: I myself have, in the past, already undone the pure conduct of many, causing them to lose their own spiritual powers — I ask that another heavenly woman be sent instead, one whose beauty and grace bring men greater delight. Śakra then spoke various verses to the assembly, urging and persuading her — and so the heavenly woman went, and undid the sage-boy after all.

The Buddha told the monks: could that sage-boy of that time be anyone other than the very monk Nandin, absorbed in meditation, before you now? And that heavenly woman, Ālànfú, is this same heavenly woman here today (Source: Sāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 1).

7. Chandaka's Two Lives of Borrowed Authority

A hard-headed, difficult monk who will not accept correction is traced through two past lives as a servant who leaned on his master's own authority to bully others — one a brawler protected by his master's hidden knowledge of buried treasure, the other an impostor who married into a teacher's household under a stolen name.

The Buddha said: in a past age there was an elder's son who had a servant named Āmóyóu, vicious by nature. The Buddha told the monks: could that elder's son of that time be anyone other than myself? And that servant, Āmóyóu, is this same monk Chandaka now.

Yet this Yajñadatta, whenever the food displeased him, would fly into a great rage, though his wife still held him in high regard, and searched in vain to learn who he truly was.

Having taken his leave, and just as he was about to depart, he taught her this verse:

"Without kin, wandering in a foreign land, / deceiving all the people under heaven — / coarse food is his own accustomed fare; / for mere food, what cause is there for complaint?"

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two past-life episodes are deliberately mismatched in scale — a servant's brawl protected by buried treasure, and a far more elaborate impostor's marriage — but both turn on the identical mechanism the Buddha names explicitly at the end: borrowed authority used to intimidate others, first a master's unexplained restraint, later a master's own name. Chandaka's own present difficulty is thus diagnosed as a inherited habit of leverage rather than simple bad temper.

10. The Golden Arm Behind the Robe

Two monks facing a choice between drinking water alive with insects and forgoing the chance to see the Buddha at all: one drinks and lives, the other refuses and dies — and it is the one who died who reaches the Buddha first, already reborn a deva.

Chinese Source

憍薩羅國有二比丘。一人犯戒。一人持戒。欲共往見佛。道中值有蟲水。破戒者語持戒者言。可共飲是水。持戒者言。水中有蟲。云何可飲。犯戒者言。我若不飲便死。不得見佛聞法及僧。持戒者至死不飲。時犯戒者便飲。持戒者不飲便死。即生三十三天上。得天身具足。先到佛所頭面禮足。在一面立。佛為種種說法得法眼淨。即時禮佛足言。歸依佛歸依法歸依僧。我盡形壽為優婆塞。佛更為說法已默然。時天禮佛已。忽然不現。時飲水者後到佛所。佛為無量眾圍遶說法佛見比丘來到。佛即披優多羅僧。示金色手。汝癡人欲見我肉身。為不如持戒者。先見我法身。佛說偈。心不善觀察　見則不審諦　愚如蛾投火　而貪觀我身　色身但不淨　汝欲見何為　內有脂血肉　外為薄皮覆　彼為渴所燒　猶行恭敬戒　至死護我教　彼見我非汝。佛說是偈已。告諸比丘。從今不持漉水囊不聽行。若不持者犯。不犯者有清流水。或大河或泉水。從此寺至彼寺。二十里內不犯(出十誦律雜誦第三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the country of Kośala there were two monks, one who broke the precepts and one who kept them faithfully, who wished to travel together to see the Buddha. On the road they came upon water filled with insects. The precept-breaking monk said to his companion: let us drink this water together. The precept-keeping monk said: the water is full of insects — how could it be drunk? The other answered: if I do not drink, I shall die, and never see the Buddha, hear the Dharma, or meet the Saṅgha. The precept-keeping monk would not drink even at the cost of his own life; the precept-breaking monk drank, while his companion, refusing, died on the spot, and was reborn at once in Trāyastriṃśa heaven, his heavenly body complete.

He arrived at the Buddha's own place first, bowed at his feet, and stood to one side; the Buddha taught him the Dharma in various ways, and he attained the pure Dharma-eye. At once he bowed at the Buddha's feet and said: I take refuge in the Buddha, I take refuge in the Dharma, I take refuge in the Saṅgha — for the rest of my life I shall be a lay follower. The Buddha taught him further, and he fell silent; the deva, having paid homage to the Buddha, then vanished from sight.

The monk who had drunk the water arrived later. The Buddha was at that time teaching the Dharma, surrounded by a boundless assembly; seeing the monk arrive, the Buddha drew back his own upper robe and showed his golden-hued arm. You foolish man — you wished to see my own physical body, yet were outdone by the one who kept the precepts, who saw my Dharma-body first. The Buddha spoke this verse:

"A mind that does not observe rightly / sees, yet without true discernment — / foolish as a moth flying into flame, / craving only the sight of my own body. / This form is nothing but impurity — / what is it you truly wish to see? / Within, only fat, blood, and flesh; / without, only a thin skin's cover. / He who, though burning with thirst, / still walked in reverence for the precept, / guarding my teaching even unto death — / it is he who has truly seen me, not you."

Having spoken this verse, the Buddha told the monks: from this day forward, none shall travel without carrying a water-strainer; to go without one is itself a transgression. It is no transgression where the water runs clear — a great river or a spring — nor within twenty li between one monastery and another (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, Miscellaneous Recitations, fascicle 3).

11. The Hungry Ghost Waiting at the Gate

A monk questions a hungry ghost waiting outside a city gate for her husband — and learns that the couple's own shared hunger has outlasted seven full cycles of the city's own rise and fall on the riverbank.

Chinese Source

迦羅國。時有餓鬼。倚城門立。比丘滿願問餓鬼曰。汝今在此何所求索。鬼曰。汝今見我耶。比丘曰見。鬼曰。我夫入城于今未還。故於此立自待夫出耳。比丘問曰。汝夫入城為何所求。時鬼答曰。今此城中有大長者。患癰積久。今日當潰。膿血流溢。夫主持來。二人共食以濟其命。比丘復問。汝夫主入城已幾許時。城郭逼近江河。舉手指城。語比丘曰。此城於彼此岸成敗以來。今為第七。我夫入城經爾許時。餓鬼形壽不可稱亦無齊限(出群牛千頭經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the land of Kāla there was once a hungry ghost, standing leaning against the city gate. The monk Pūrṇa asked the ghost: what is it you seek here now? The ghost answered: do you truly see me? The monk said: I see you. The ghost said: my husband entered the city and has not yet returned; I stand here waiting for him to come out.

The monk asked: what has your husband gone into the city to seek? The ghost answered: in this city there is a great elder, long afflicted with a boil, which today shall finally burst, its pus and blood overflowing; my husband has gone to bring some back, that the two of us might eat together and so sustain our own lives. The monk asked further: how long has your husband now been in the city? The city walls stood close by a great river; the ghost raised her hand, pointed to the city, and told the monk: since this city was first built and destroyed, upon this bank and that, this is now its seventh such cycle — and my husband has already been in the city that entire span of time. The lifespan of a hungry ghost cannot be reckoned, and knows no fixed limit (Source: Sūtra of a Thousand Oxen in a Herd).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The scale here is the entire point: a city rising and falling seven times over is the compendium's own unit for measuring a single hungry ghost's hunger, and the couple's devotion — she still waiting faithfully at the gate after cycles of time no human life could span — makes the horror of their condition somehow more poignant rather than less, fidelity itself trapped inside an appetite that can never be satisfied.

12. Bound by Blades of Living Grass

Robbed and bound with nothing more than knotted living grass, five hundred monks choose four days of captivity and near-starvation over breaking even a single blade — until a hunting king happens upon them and asks why they never simply pulled free.

Chinese Source

昔有五百比丘。行大澤中值遇劫寇。劫其衣裳。縛諸比丘。悉令坐地。攬生草合。結其手而便捨去。此諸道人適欲殺草而起則違佛戒。各各生念。寧自滅身。終不違戒。執窮四日。國王出獵。見諸道人。坐于曠野。下馬作禮問其意故。即具便答。解諸道人將歸供養(出譬喻經第六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, five hundred monks, traveling through a great marshland, were set upon by bandits, who stripped them of their robes, bound them, and made them all sit upon the ground — tying their hands together with nothing more than gathered blades of living grass — before leaving them there. These renunciants, though they might easily have risen simply by tearing the grass, understood that to kill even a blade of grass in doing so would violate the Buddha's own precept, and each resolved in his own heart: better to let this body perish than ever to break the precept. And so they remained, bound, for four full days, until the king of that land, out hunting, came upon these renunciants sitting in the open wilderness, dismounted, bowed to them, and asked the reason for their state. They told him everything in full, and he freed them all himself, and brought them back to receive his own offerings (Source: Sūtra of Similes, fascicle 6).

13. Two Monks, Each Certain the Other Is a Ghost

A haunted room drives off its resident monks one by one — until two new arrivals, each too proud of his own precept-strength to be afraid, end up locked in a night-long door-battle each convinced the other is the very ghost he came to conquer.

Chinese Source

山中有一佛圖。有一別房。中有惡鬼。喜來惱人。諸僧捨去。有客僧來。維那處分令住此房。而語之言。此房有鬼戲來惱人。客僧自以持戒力故。答言。小鬼何所能為。我能伏之。即入房住。日將欲暮。更有僧來求覓住處。維那亦令在此房住。亦語有鬼。其人亦言。我當伏之。時先入者。閉戶端坐待鬼不來。後來者。夜打門求入。先入者謂為是鬼。不為開戶。後者極力打門。在內道人以力拒之。外者得勝。排門得入。內者打之。外者亦極力熟打。至明旦相見。乃是故舊同學。道人各相愧謝。眾人雲集笑而怪之(出大智論第九十一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the mountains there was a monastery with a separate chamber, in which dwelt a malicious ghost that delighted in troubling anyone who entered, so that the resident monks had all abandoned it. A visiting monk arrived, and the monastery's own overseer assigned him to that very room, warning him: this room has a ghost that comes to torment its occupant. The visiting monk, confident in the strength of his own precept-keeping, answered: what could a mere small ghost possibly do? I can subdue it myself — and went in to stay there.

As evening drew near, another monk arrived seeking lodging, and the overseer likewise assigned him to that same room, warning him too of the ghost; this monk likewise said: I shall subdue it myself. The first monk, having shut the door, sat upright within, waiting — but no ghost came. The second monk, arriving at night, knocked at the door seeking entry; the first, believing this to be the ghost itself, would not open it. The one outside pounded at the door with all his strength, while the one within pushed back against it with equal force; the one outside at last prevailed, forced the door open, and entered — whereupon the one within struck at him, and the one outside struck back just as hard in turn. Only at dawn, seeing each other's faces, did they realize they were in fact old fellow-students from long before. The two renunciants, mutually embarrassed, apologized to one another, while the gathered crowd looked on, laughing at the strange affair (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 91).

14. Saved by a Mountain Spirit's Boar

A wild, unkempt hermit-monk is mistaken for a ghost by a king's own consort and nearly executed on the spot — spared at the last moment when a mountain spirit conjures a boar to draw the executioner's own attention away.

Chinese Source

佛在舍衛國。有一比丘。在參國石間土室中。長髮鬚爪被敗壞衣。時優填王。欲出遊觀治諸道路。王從美人到於山下。有一美人顧見土室。有一比丘。長鬚髮爪衣服裂敗。狀類如鬼。便大聲呼。天子。是中有鬼。王便遙問。今何所在。美人言。近在石間土室中。王即拔劍從之。見比丘如是。即問汝何等人。對言。我是沙門。王問。汝何等沙門。曰。我是釋迦沙門。王言。是應真耶。曰非也。寧有四禪耶。復言無也。寧有三禪二禪耶。復言無有。寧至一禪耶。對曰。言實一禪行。王便心恚不解。顧謂侍者。黃門以是婬意。今是沙門凡俗人無真行。奈何見我美人。便勅侍者急取斷命。侍者便去。山神念。是比丘無過。今當恐死。我可擁護令脫是厄。便化大猪身。徐走王邊。侍者即白王。大猪近在王邊。王便捨比丘。拔劍逐猪。比丘見王已遠。即便走出(出義足經上卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Śrāvastī. There was a monk who dwelt in an earthen dwelling among the rocks of a certain country, his hair, beard, and nails long unkempt, his robes worn and tattered. King Udayana, wishing to go out and survey the roads under repair, traveled with his consorts to the foot of a mountain. One of his consorts, glancing toward the earthen dwelling, saw the monk — his hair, beard, and nails so long, his robes so torn, that he appeared like a ghost — and cried out loudly: my lord, there is a ghost in there! The king, from a distance, asked where; the consort answered: nearby, in that earthen dwelling among the rocks.

The king at once drew his sword and went to see, and, finding the monk in this state, asked him: what manner of being are you? He answered: I am a śramaṇa. The king asked: what kind of śramaṇa? He answered: I am a śramaṇa of the Śākya. The king asked: are you then an arhat? He said, no. Have you then attained the fourth dhyāna? Again he said, no. The third or second dhyāna? Again, no. Have you at least attained the first dhyāna? He answered: truly, I have attained only the first dhyāna's practice.

The king, his heart filled with displeasure he could not shake off, turned to his attendant and said: this is nothing but lustful intent — this śramaṇa is an ordinary, common man with no true attainment at all — how dare he look upon my own consort! — and ordered his attendant to put him to death at once. The attendant set off to do so.

A mountain spirit, seeing that the monk was entirely without fault, thought: he is now in mortal danger — I shall protect him and free him from this calamity — and transformed itself into the body of a great boar, which came ambling slowly near the king. The attendant at once reported to the king: a great boar is near, my lord! The king abandoned the monk, drew his sword, and gave chase to the boar; and the monk, seeing the king now far off, fled from that place at once (Source: Arthapada Sūtra, first fascicle).

15. The Unburned Tongue

A brief miracle fragment: a monk who spent his life reciting the Amitābha Sūtra and the great Prajñāpāramitā dies at the very moment of the Buddha's own arrival to receive him, and his tongue alone survives the cremation fire untouched.

Chinese Source

有一比丘。誦阿彌陀佛經。及大波若波羅蜜。是人欲死語弟子言。阿彌陀佛與其大眾俱來。動身自歸須臾命終。命終之後弟子燒之。明日收灰見舌不燒(出大智論第九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a monk who recited the Amitābha Sūtra together with the great Prajñāpāramitā throughout his life. As he was dying, he told his disciples: the Buddha Amitābha has come, together with his great assembly — and, moving his own body in a gesture of homage, passed away within a short while after. His disciples cremated him after his death; the next day, gathering the ashes, they found his tongue alone unburned (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 9).

16. Why Monks Should Not Dwell Alone in the Wild

Two closely paired episodes — a forest spirit woman and a piśāca demoness, each spurned by a monk sworn to chastity, each retaliating by depositing the sleeping monk somewhere deeply compromising — become the occasion for a new monastic rule against solitary dwelling in the deep wilderness.

Chinese Source

憍薩羅國。有一比丘。獨住深山。林中有非人女。語比丘共作婬欲。比丘言。莫作是語。我是斷婬人。女言。汝若不來。我當破汝利養與汝衰惱。比丘言隨汝。中夜比丘臥。女鬼以納衣持比丘。著王宮內夫人邊臥。王覺問言。汝何人耶。比丘言。我是沙門。是何沙門。答曰。是釋子沙門。王言。汝今何以來此。比丘具向王說。王言。汝何用在深山林中。故為惡鬼所嬈。出去。我知佛法故不問汝。比丘得脫具說其事。佛因此制。無入深山中。又憍薩羅國。有一比丘。阿蘭若處住。有毘舍遮鬼女。來語比丘言。共作婬欲來。比丘言。莫作是語。我斷婬欲人。鬼女言。汝若不作。我當破汝利養令汝衰惱。比丘言。隨汝。比丘夜臥。鬼女以納衣裹持。著酒舍酒瓮中。酒家人明日見比丘在酒瓮中。問言。汝是何人。答言。我是沙門。是何沙門。答言。釋子沙門。問言。何故在是中。比丘具說是事。酒舍人言。汝去。因此佛言。比丘不得入深山林中空處可畏處無人處住(出十誦律善誦第二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the country of Kośala there was a monk who dwelt alone in the deep mountains. In that forest lived a non-human woman, who told the monk: let us lie together. The monk said: do not speak such words — I am one who has cut off desire entirely. The woman said: if you will not come to me, I shall ruin your own livelihood and bring you nothing but misfortune. The monk said: do as you will.

In the middle of the night, as the monk lay sleeping, the ghost-woman wrapped him in his own cloak and carried him into the royal palace, laying him beside the king's own consort. The king, waking, asked: who are you? The monk said: I am a śramaṇa. What kind of śramaṇa? He answered: I am a śramaṇa of the Śākya. The king said: why then have you come here? The monk told him the whole matter in full. The king said: why do you dwell in the deep mountain forest at all, only to be tormented by malicious ghosts in this way? Leave now — it is only because I know something of the Buddha's own teaching that I do not question you further. The monk, thus freed, told the whole matter fully afterward, and it was for this reason that the Buddha established a rule that monks should not dwell in the deep mountains.

And again, in the country of Kośala, there was a monk who dwelt in a forest retreat. A piśāca demoness came and told him: let us lie together. The monk said: do not speak such words — I am one who has cut off desire. The demoness said: if you will not do this, I shall ruin your own livelihood and bring you misfortune. The monk said: do as you will. That night, as the monk lay sleeping, the demoness wrapped him in his cloak and carried him into a wine-shop, placing him inside a great wine-jar. The next day, the wine-shop's own people found the monk inside the jar, and asked: who are you? He answered: I am a śramaṇa. What kind of śramaṇa? He answered: a śramaṇa of the Śākya. They asked: why are you in here? The monk told the whole matter. The wine-shop people said: be gone from here.

It was because of this that the Buddha said: monks must not dwell in the deep mountain forests, in empty and fearsome places, or in places without other people (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, Well-Recited Portion, fascicle 2).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium pairs these two nearly identical episodes deliberately, letting the repetition itself make the case for the new rule — a single incident might be dismissed as chance, but two monks, sworn to the same vow, ending up deposited in two different scenes of scandal by two different supernatural women, reads as a pattern the Saṅgha needed to legislate against directly, solitary wilderness dwelling itself named as the shared vulnerability rather than any failure of individual resolve.

17. The Turtle and the Monkey, Once More

A monk drawn back again and again by a brothel's fine food loses himself to infatuation, ignoring the Buddha's own instruction on regarding women as kin — and is sent away in verse by the very woman he sought, his humiliation traced to a jātaka of a turtle who fell for a monkey in a riverside tree.

Chinese Source

有一比丘。普行分衛。偶入婬舍。婬女見入歡喜踊躍。即奉迎稽首足下。請出就坐。又問比丘。仁從何來。比丘答言。吾主分衛故來乞匃。即為施設餚饌眾味。盛以滿鉢而奉上之。比丘受已而退。得是美食。心中歡喜不能自勝。數數往詣。時女心念。計此比丘。守法難及頻為興設。甘脆肥美之食。而授與之。往返不息。學問未明。所作不辦。未伏諸根。見女妙色欲意便動。口出柔濡恩愛之辭。心懷親附。比丘聞見婬亂迷惑。不能自覺。時世尊曰。雖覩女人。長者如母。中者如姊。少者如妹。如子如女。當內觀身。念皆惡露無可愛者。外如畫瓶中滿不淨。觀此四大因緣假合。本無所有時彼比丘不曉空觀。但作色觀。婬欲意亂。為女說頌曰。淑女年幼形清淨　顏貌端正特姝妙　望汝屈德能見從　志意傾蓋願相保。婬女頌曰。假使卿身無財業　何為立志求難致　如卿所作無羞慚　馳走促出離我家。時逐出比丘。追至祇樹。問諸比丘。即來詣佛。啟白世尊。具說本末。佛言。此比丘。宿命曾作水鼈。女作獼猴。亦相好樂志不得果。還自侵欺不入正教。增益惱患。今復如是。願不從心。逆見折辱。慚愧而去。佛言。過去世時有鼈。江水邊樹木熾盛。彼叢樹間有一獼猴。止頓彼樹。鼈從水出。遙見樹有獼猴。而與交語。前行親近。日日如是。覩之不懈。則起染心。說偈往反罵而避之(文多不載)時獼猴今婬女是。鼈分衛比丘是(出說比丘分衛經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a monk who, going about begging alms widely, happened once to enter a house of prostitution. The courtesan there, seeing him enter, was overjoyed, and came forward to welcome him, bowing at his feet, inviting him to sit. She asked him: where have you come from, honored one? The monk answered: I have come begging for alms, as my own practice requires. She at once prepared for him a fine meal of many delicacies, filling his bowl to the brim and offering it to him; the monk accepted it and withdrew. Having received such fine food, his heart filled with a joy he could not restrain, and he returned to her again and again.

The woman, for her own part, thought to herself: this monk is hard to win over through mere observance of the Dharma — and so, again and again, prepared for him sweet, rich, and delicious food, offering it to him each time he came; and so his visits continued without ceasing. Not yet firm in his own learning, his practice unaccomplished, his senses not yet subdued, he saw her fine beauty and felt desire stir within him; she, for her part, spoke to him in soft and affectionate words, and he came to feel himself drawn close to her, and, hearing and seeing all this, fell into confusion and delusion without recognizing it in himself.

At that time the World-Honored One had taught: though you look upon a woman, regard an elder one as your own mother, a middling one as your elder sister, a younger one as your younger sister, or as your own child or daughter; and you should look within your own body, reflecting that it is all foul and unlovely, and, without, like a painted vessel filled with impurity — observe how this body, a mere temporary union of the four elements, has no true substance of its own at all. But this monk, not understanding the contemplation of emptiness, practiced only the contemplation of outward form, and, his mind confused by desire, spoke this verse to the woman:

"Fine woman, young in years, pure in form, / your face and bearing so lovely and rare — / I hope that you might bend your own worth to follow me; / my heart inclines toward you, and I wish to hold you always."

The courtesan answered in verse:

"Suppose your own body held no wealth at all — / why then set your heart on what is so hard to win? / What you have done here has no shame in it at all — / run, hurry, get out, and leave my house at once!"

She then drove the monk out; he was chased all the way to the Jetavana grove, where he asked the other monks, and together they came before the Buddha and told the World-Honored One the whole matter from beginning to end. The Buddha said: this monk, in a past life, was once a soft-shelled turtle, and this woman a macaque; they too took a liking to one another then, yet their intention came to nothing, and each in turn deceived the other, failing to enter the true teaching, only increasing their own affliction — and now it is the same again: what he wished for did not follow his own heart, and he met with rejection and humiliation instead, and left in shame.

The Buddha said: in a past age there was a turtle, by a river where the trees grew thick; among that grove of trees was a macaque, who dwelt there. The turtle, coming out of the water, saw the macaque in the tree from a distance, and began speaking with it, drawing gradually nearer and growing familiar, day after day, tirelessly — until a defiled longing arose in its own heart. They exchanged verses back and forth, reviling one another, and then parted (the fuller text is not recorded here). That macaque of that time is this same courtesan now; that turtle is this very alms-begging monk (Source: Sūtra on a Monk's Alms-Round).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a second, independent telling of the turtle-and-monkey doublet: a nearly identical pairing of a mismatched, ultimately humiliating cross-species longing appears elsewhere in the compendium under different source citations, and, as elsewhere, we let the parallel stand rather than harmonize it — the anthology's own compilers plainly drew from more than one transmission of a popular teaching motif, and preserving that plurality is part of being faithful to the text as it actually survives.

18. Gurudatta's Mother in the Realm of Hungry Ghosts

A devout layman's own mother, dead twenty years and reborn a hungry ghost through her own stinginess and wrong views, appears before her monk son starving and in agony — and is finally freed through an offering made and dedicated in her own name, exactly as she herself instructs him to make it.

Chinese Source

昔有清信士。名優多羅。尊佛樂法親賢聖眾。月六齋奉八戒絕殃行仁。群生護命。名曰珍重。榮華不能迴其心。豔女國寶不能亂其志。貞信難傾。酒不歷口。孝從是務。過時不食。虛心稟道。香花脂粉未嘗附身。兵仗凶器不以毀德。遠愚親賢。以佛神化喻其母心。母信邪倒見。慳而不惠。壽終之後。子為沙門。心穢寂滅。端坐樹下。觀察十方。常自念曰。吾母死二十餘年。試尋所在。欲報生養之恩。斯須之間有一餓鬼。醜黑可憎。髮長纏身。繞足曳地進退頓蹌呼嗟無救。到沙門所曰。吾與群愚惡人為黨。不信佛教。恣口所欲。今為餓鬼。二十五年不見沙門。今日遇矣。死便餓渴。願以天潤。惠我水穀。濟吾微命。沙門答曰。大海清水豈不足飲乎。鬼曰。適欲就飲。水化為膿。鰺臭無量。獲飯一飡。化為炎炭。燒口下徹。惡鬼又以鐵鎖繫吾頸。鐵杖洞然。亂捶吾身。吾罪何重。乃至于斯。沙門曰。昔為人時。違戾佛教。聾瞽為黨。愚惑自逐。以禍為福。守慳不施。貪取非分。鬼泣淚曰。誠如此矣。又吾處世。為人時有男。奉佛五戒。專守十善。為清信士。六齋八戒未甞有虧。以孝事親。以智奉聖。尋高清沙門之迹。由影追形。勸吾布施。供養聖眾。吾以無正真之智。信狂愚妖言。今獲其禍。楚毒難陳。垂泣而曰。沙門哀我濟我。沙門曰。乃當斯戾。以何德攘禍乎。鬼曰。以瓶盛水。楊枝著中。以法服覆。上比丘僧。令飯具供養。舉吾名呪願之。令得衣食。其為不久。夫有命終。在餓鬼者。沙門呪願。皆應時得。沙門如其言。瓶水楊枝住其中。飯比丘僧。以法服上之僧。舉其名呪願。即有大池水中生蓮花。芬芳動身。果樹陰涼。所願從心。伴等五百人。怪之曰。斯子猶獨有何福助。早免重咎。願即從心乎(出優多羅母經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a devout layman named Uttara, who revered the Buddha, delighted in the Dharma, and held the noble Saṅgha dear, keeping the six fast-days of each month and upholding the eight precepts, cutting himself off from wrongdoing and practicing benevolence, protecting the lives of all beings — and so he was known as Gurudatta. Neither glory nor honor could turn his own heart aside; neither beautiful women nor the treasures of a kingdom could disturb his resolve. His faith and sincerity were unshakable — wine never touched his lips — and he devoted himself to filial duty, never eating past the appointed hour, emptying his own heart to receive the teaching, never adorning his body with incense, flowers, or cosmetics, never letting weapons or instruments of harm corrupt his own virtue, keeping distance from the foolish and closeness with the wise, and using the Buddha's own transformative power to try to guide his mother's own heart. Yet his mother held to false and inverted views, stingy and never generous, and, when her own life ended, was reborn accordingly.

Afterward, the son went forth as a monk, his heart purified and still, and sat in meditation beneath a tree, surveying the ten directions, often reflecting to himself: my mother has now been dead more than twenty years — let me try to discover where she has gone, that I might repay the debt of having been born of her and raised by her.

In that very moment, there appeared a hungry ghost, hideous and black, repulsive to behold, her hair long and wrapped about her own body, trailing to the ground about her feet, stumbling forward and back, crying out in anguish with no one to save her. She came before the monk and said: I once kept company with a crowd of foolish, wicked people, gave no faith to the Buddha's teaching, and indulged every craving of my own mouth — and now I am a hungry ghost. For twenty-five years I have not seen a single monk; today at last I have met one. I am dying of hunger and thirst — I beg you, by heaven's own mercy, grant me water and grain, and save this feeble life of mine.

The monk answered: is not the clear water of the great ocean itself enough to drink? The ghost said: the very moment I go to drink it, the water turns to pus, its stench beyond all measure; should I manage even a single mouthful of rice, it turns to burning coals, scorching my mouth and throat straight through. Malicious ghosts, moreover, bind my own neck with iron chains, and beat my body without end with red-hot iron rods — how can my own transgression be so grave as to bring me to this?

The monk said: when you were once a human being, you turned your back on the Buddha's own teaching, kept company with the deaf and blind in understanding, followed your own foolish delusions, took disaster for good fortune, held fast to stinginess and never gave, and coveted what was not rightfully yours. The ghost wept and said: it is truly just as you say. And in that life, I had a son, who revered the Buddha's five precepts, held firmly to the ten wholesome actions, was a devout layman never once failing in the six fast-days and the eight precepts, served his parents with filial devotion and the wise with true understanding, and followed closely the lofty, pure example of the monks, as a shadow follows its own form — he urged me again and again to give in charity and make offerings to the noble Saṅgha, but I, lacking any true and correct wisdom, believed instead the mad and foolish words of others, and now I suffer this calamity, its bitter suffering beyond all telling. Weeping, she said: monk, have pity on me, and save me.

The monk said: since you have fallen into such a state, by what merit might this calamity be turned aside? The ghost answered: fill a vessel with water, place a willow-branch within it, and cover it with a monastic robe; offer this, together with a meal, to the assembly of monks, and, in my own name, have them dedicate the merit and make a vow of blessing for me, so that I might obtain food and clothing — and this shall not be long delayed. For any being who has died and fallen among the hungry ghosts, a monk's own dedication of merit takes effect at once.

The monk did exactly as she had said: he set the vessel of water with the willow-branch within it, offered a meal to the assembly of monks, presented a monastic robe to the Saṅgha, and, in her own name, dedicated the merit with a vow of blessing. At once, a great pool appeared, in which lotus flowers bloomed, their fragrance filling the air, with fruit trees offering cool shade all around — and every wish she held was fulfilled according to her own heart's desire. His five hundred fellow monks, marveling at this, said: how has this one disciple alone obtained such a blessing, freeing his mother so swiftly from so grave a fault, her every wish fulfilled just as her own heart desired? (Source: Sūtra of Uttara's Mother).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The mechanics of dedicated merit here closely echo the Ullambana Sūtra's own account of Maudgalyāyana rescuing his mother, translated earlier in this compendium at fascicle 14 — the same basic shape of a filial monk-son, a mother trapped by her own stinginess in the realm of hungry ghosts, and release through an offering made to the Saṅgha and dedicated in her name. That the compendium preserves both tellings, drawn from different source texts, suggests this was a widely circulated teaching pattern rather than a single fixed narrative.

19. The Dragon King's Six-Hundred-Year Headache

A monk thrown overboard by superstitious shipmates is carried by a dragon king into an undersea palace, where he cures a headache six centuries in the making with nothing more than the Dharma itself — and is sent home laden with wish-granting pearls.

Chinese Source

昔有沙門。隨商人度海。半路船迴。不復得去。眾人僉曰。船中當有不淨潔者。探籌出之。道人三得出籌。自投海中。龍王即以七寶蓮華承之入海。乃到龍宮。見樓閣寶舍殿堂。龍王請入。頭面禮足曰。吾得頭痛。六百餘歲求索道人。今乃得之。道人當療我病。道人曰。吾不知醫藥。以何相療。龍王曰。吾此海中多有神藥。不愈我病。唯未得法藥。道人說法須臾之頃。龍王自覺除愈。龍大歡喜。供養道人九十日。白道人言。久相勞屈想亦勞悒。前船甫到今當相送。龍王選三摩尼珠。一以上佛。一以施眾僧。一與道人。遣神送。忽然至船。船伴驚喜。共還本土。眾人悉隨道人詣佛。佛為說法。皆發道意。所度無量(出譬喻經第九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a monk who traveled by sea together with a company of merchants. Partway through the voyage, the ship turned back and could go no further; the whole company said: there must be someone impure aboard this ship — and they drew lots to find who it was. The monk drew the marked lot three times in a row, and, accepting this, threw himself into the sea. The dragon king at once caught him upon a lotus of the seven treasures and carried him down into the sea, to the dragon palace itself, where the monk saw towers, jeweled halls, and great chambers. The dragon king welcomed him in, bowed at his feet, and said: I have suffered a headache, and for more than six hundred years have sought a renunciant to cure it — now at last I have found one; you must heal my illness. The monk said: I know nothing of medicine — how could I possibly heal you? The dragon king said: within this very sea I have no lack of divine medicines, yet none of them have cured my illness — only the medicine of the Dharma remains untried.

The monk taught the Dharma for only a short while, and the dragon king felt his own illness lift and vanish entirely. Overjoyed, the dragon made offerings to the monk for ninety days, then told him: you have been troubled and confined here so long, and I imagine you long to return — your former ship has just now arrived, and I shall send you back. The dragon king then chose three maṇi pearls: one to be offered to the Buddha, one to be given to the assembly of monks, and one for the monk himself, and sent a spirit to escort him — and in an instant he found himself back upon the ship. His fellow travelers, astonished and delighted, returned together to their own land, and all of them followed the monk to see the Buddha; the Buddha taught them the Dharma, and every one of them awakened the aspiration for the path — those he delivered were beyond counting (Source: Sūtra of Similes, fascicle 9).

20. Grass That Turned to Cattle Bones

A brief miracle fragment: a search for a lost cow uncovers not a thief but an arhat's own cooking-pot, transformed by his attainment into the very likeness of the missing animal — and a rain of stones falling on an entire kingdom until an impatient young novice's anger finally passes.

Chinese Source

昔阿難報事。有沙門得阿那含道。於山上煮草染衣。時有失牛者遍求。見煙便往。視釜中草悉成牛骨。鉢化成牛頭。袈裟化成牛皮。人便以骨繫頭。侚行國中。眾人共見之。沙彌。見日己中。捶撻抵不見師至。便入戶悉思惟。見師為人所辱。則往頭面著足。何時言如此。日久遠時罪也。沙彌言。可暫歸食。兩人則放。神足俱去。沙彌未得道。常有恚未除。顧見清信士及國人。乃取我師如此。使龍雨沙石動此國。令之恐怖。念此適竟。四面雨沙。城塢屋室皆悉壞敗。師言。我宿命一世屠牛為業。故得此殃耳。汝何緣作罪乎。汝去不須復與我相追。師曰。罪福如是。可不慎矣(出譬喻經上卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, as Ānanda once related, there was a monk who had attained the fruit of non-returner, and who, upon a mountain, boiled grass to dye his own robes. At that time a man who had lost his cow was searching everywhere for it; seeing the smoke, he went to look, and found that the grass in the pot had all become cattle bones, the monk's bowl transformed into a cow's head, and his robe into cowhide. The man then bound the bones about the monk's neck, and paraded him through the whole kingdom, for all the people to see.

His novice attendant, seeing the sun already at midday and his own master not yet returned from the customary beating and delay, went within and gave the matter careful thought, and, perceiving that his master had been humiliated by this man, went at once and bowed his head at the man's own feet: how could you do such a thing? — this offense shall bring long and lasting consequence. The novice said: let my master return for a meal now — and the man released them both, and the two departed together by their own spiritual power.

The novice, not yet having attained the path himself, still harbored unremoved anger, and, reflecting on the faithful layman and the people of that kingdom who had treated his own master in such a way, caused a dragon to rain sand and stone down upon the entire kingdom, shaking it and filling its people with terror; and the very moment he had formed this thought, sand rained down from every direction, and the city's walls, fortifications, and dwellings were all destroyed. The master said: in a past life of my own, I once made my living slaughtering cattle — it is for this reason that I met with this very calamity. What cause of your own led you to commit this offense? Go now, and you need not follow after me any further. The master said: such is the working of transgression and merit alike — how could one fail to be careful of it? (Source: Sūtra of Similes, first fascicle).

21. Silence Until the Parrot Died

Accused of stealing a priceless pearl he never touched, a monk endures a beating rather than name the parrot he watched swallow it — choosing silence over an accusation that might cost the bird its own life, until the bird's death makes the truth safe to speak at last.

Chinese Source

外國有沙門。行乞到買珠家。主人為設飯食。有大珠其價億數。與人持歸。置沙門邊。時有鸚鵡。便出吞之。主人不見。因問沙門。答言。我不取。主人復問。有他人耶。答言無有。主人瞋曰。我適持珠來。既無他人。獨有沙門。而言不取。珠今所在。便撾沙門。血出流地。沙門故言。我不隱珠。須臾鸚鵡出飲地血。與杖相遇。鸚鵡即死。復欲舉手撾於沙門。沙門言。止聽我語。鸚鵡吞之。即破鸚鵡得珠。主人謂沙門曰。何不早說。乃使如是。沙門曰。我持佛戒不得殺生。即欲說之。恐殺鸚鵡。今鸚鵡死。我乃說之。鸚鵡若活。卿撾我死。我終不說也。主人便自剋責悔過。辭謝之。沙門不瞋。顏色不變(出一卷雜譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In a foreign land there was a monk who, begging for alms, came to the house of a pearl merchant. The host prepared a meal for him. There was, in that household, a great pearl worth countless millions, which a man had just brought home and set down near the monk. At that moment a parrot came out and swallowed it, unseen by the master of the house, who then asked the monk about the pearl. The monk answered: I have not taken it. The master asked further: was anyone else here? He answered: no one else was here. The master, growing angry, said: I only just brought the pearl in — if no one else was here but you alone, and yet you say you have not taken it, then where is it now? — and beat the monk, so that blood flowed onto the ground. The monk still only said: I have not hidden the pearl.

A moment later, the parrot came out to drink the blood on the ground, and, in doing so, was struck by the very stick and died at once. The master again raised his hand to strike the monk, who said: stop — hear me out. The parrot swallowed the pearl. The bird was cut open at once, and the pearl found within. The master said to the monk: why did you not say so earlier, and spare yourself all this? The monk answered: I hold to the Buddha's precept against taking life — had I spoken sooner, I feared it would have led to the parrot's own death. Now that the parrot has already died, I may speak of it at last. Had the parrot still lived, you might have beaten me to death, and still I would never have spoken.

The master, hearing this, reproached himself deeply, repented, and apologized to the monk, who bore him no anger at all, his own countenance unchanged throughout (Source: Miscellaneous Similes Sūtra, single fascicle).

22–23. Two Ghosts, Talked Down

Two short encounters between meditating monks and hostile ghosts, each resolved not by force but by the monk's own steady, unshaken response — a demon's shape-shifting boasts met with dry wit, and a man-eating ghost's grip released by a single calm observation about their two natures.

Chinese Source

有沙門。於山中念道。有一鬼。化作無頭人而行。沙門言。咄快無頭。則不知頭痛。又無目耳不知音響。鬼復化作無軀人。沙門言。咄快無頭人。不知痛痒。無五藏不病。鬼復化作無手足人。沙門言。咄快無手足不能行。亦不取人物。鬼知沙門守志。即便滅去(出一卷雜譬喻經)。

外國有沙門行道。與噉人鬼相逢。鬼急抱之。沙門言。我今因與卿相得。卿心與我如天地相去大遠。卿心好殺。我心好生。卿與道反。鬼便捨沙門。不敢復抱。即却辭謝言。我愚癡不及耳(出一卷雜譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a monk practicing meditation in the mountains. A ghost transformed itself into a headless man and walked before him. The monk said: how fine — with no head, you'll never suffer a headache; and with no eyes or ears, you'll never be troubled by any sound either. The ghost then transformed itself into a man without a torso. The monk said: how fine — with no torso, you'll feel no pain or itch at all, and with no internal organs, you'll never fall ill. The ghost then transformed itself into a man without hands or feet. The monk said: how fine — with no hands or feet, you cannot walk, and you cannot take anything from anyone either. The ghost, seeing that the monk's own resolve could not be shaken, vanished at once (Source: Miscellaneous Similes Sūtra, single fascicle).

In a foreign land there was a monk practicing the path who encountered a man-eating ghost. The ghost seized him tightly in its own grip. The monk said: now that we have truly met one another, know this — your own heart and mine are as far apart as heaven from earth. Your heart delights in killing; my heart delights in preserving life. You and the path stand in direct opposition to one another. The ghost, hearing this, released the monk at once, no longer daring to hold him, and, withdrawing, apologized: I was foolish, and did not see this myself (Source: Miscellaneous Similes Sūtra, single fascicle).

24. The Monk Who Bared His Own Belly

Fascicle 19 closes with a renunciant who, refused nothing but his life by a hunter's drawn bow, offers his own belly instead of his back — and converts the hunter not through argument but through the sheer strangeness of a man who would rather die facing his own killer than flee.

Chinese Source

昔有道人。晝夜行道。初不懈息。且有身口之急。當須飲食。便行乞匃。忽到獵師邊乞。獵師無道。便大瞋恚。欲射道人。道人言。止止。勿射我餘處。正射我腹。便開衣露腹。喚令其射。獵師即下弓釋箭。前問道人。夫人之處世。無不畏死。而道人何故令我射腹。道人語言。此腹欲食。由此之故。不避危險。是以射之。獵師即悟。吾緣山嶺。不避虎狼。亦為此腹。宿緣福至。忽便開解。道人為說三塗之苦泥洹之樂。獵師自知殺獵罪重。便從受戒。發菩提心(出譬喻經第十卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a renunciant who practiced the path day and night without ever growing slack — yet, having still the ordinary needs of body and speech to attend to, he required food and drink, and so went out to beg. He happened to come, in his begging, upon a hunter, who, being without regard for the path, flew into a great rage and raised his bow to shoot him. The renunciant said: wait, wait — do not shoot me elsewhere; shoot me squarely in the belly — and, opening his own robe, bared his belly, calling on the hunter to shoot there.

The hunter at once lowered his bow and released his arrow's tension, and came forward to ask the renunciant: among all people living in this world, there is none who does not fear death — why then does the renunciant ask me to shoot him in the belly? The renunciant answered: it is this very belly that craves food — and it is for this reason alone that I do not avoid danger, and so I offer it to your arrow. The hunter, hearing this, awakened to his own truth: I too, roaming these mountain ridges, never avoiding tiger or wolf, do all this only for this same belly of my own — and, his past karmic conditions and merit now coming to fruition, his heart suddenly opened in understanding.

The renunciant then taught him of the sufferings of the three lower realms and the bliss of nirvāṇa; the hunter, recognizing for himself the gravity of the offense of killing in the hunt, took the precepts from him at once, and awakened the resolve for enlightenment (Source: Sūtra of Similes, fascicle 10).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This closing story completes the fascicle's own theme in a quietly literal way: the monk's "unfathomable depth" here is simply his willingness to let the hunter see, without flinching, the same hunger that drives every living creature — his own belly no different in kind from the hunter's. It is recognizing that shared, ordinary hunger, not any display of supernatural power, that finally converts him.
