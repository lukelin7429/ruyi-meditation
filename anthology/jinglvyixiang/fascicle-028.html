Prasenajit and Bimbisāra

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 28 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Kings on the Śrāvaka Path, Part 5" (行聲聞道中諸國王部第五) — thirteen entries centered on the Buddha's two most frequent royal patrons, King Prasenajit of Kośala and King Bimbisāra of Magadha. Among them: a rākṣasa-possessed usurper's own reign of cannibalistic terror, ten symbolic dreams the Buddha interprets one by one as omens of moral decline in ages to come, a servant girl's single act of generosity toward the Buddha that leads to her becoming Queen Mallikā, King Bimbisāra's own private confession of guilt over an amputated finger, King Pukkusāti's fatal ordination on the very night he finally meets the Buddha, and the deva Rohitassa's ancient question of whether the very edge of the world can ever be reached by traveling.

Translation Method

Fascicle 28 continues the compendium's section on kings taking the śrāvaka path, now focused heavily on the two historical kings most closely associated with the Buddha in the wider canon — Prasenajit and Bimbisāra — alongside several vivid, semi-independent episodes. Difficult material (a rākṣasa's cannibalism, judicial mutilation, sudden death) is translated faithfully throughout. Where this compendium's own transliterations correspond confidently to well-documented figures elsewhere in the Buddhist canon (Jīvaka, Cūḍapanthaka, Rohitassa, Pukkusāti), we use those standard names; where no confident match exists, we preserve the original in pinyin.

1. The Usurper Fèidiào and the Rākṣasa King

A wrongly murdered founder-king's own dying vow to return as a rākṣasa comes true in full — until a single Buddhist household's own devotion, and the Buddha's direct intervention, breaks a cycle of cannibalistic sacrifice that has already consumed thousands of families' own children.

Chinese Source

昔瓶沙王。有一大臣。犯事徙南山中去國千里。由來無人不熟五穀。大臣到中。泉水通流五穀大熟。四方諸國有飢寒者來至此中。數年之中便有三四千家。來者給與田地令得生活。其中三老諸長者宿年。共議國之無君。由身之無首相將至大臣所。舉大臣為王。大臣答長老曰。若以我為王者。當如諸國王之法。左右大臣文武將士。上下朝貢發女開宮租稅穀帛當如民法。諸國老曰。唯然奉命。一隨王法。即立為王。處置群臣。文武上下發調人民。築城作舍宮殿樓觀。民被苦毒不復能堪。皆發想念欲謀圖於王。諸姦臣輩將王出獵。去城三四十里。於曠野澤中索王欲殺。王問左右。何緣殺我。並曰。民慕豐樂奉王以禮。民困思亂破家圖國。王告之言。卿等自為非我本造。抂殺我者神祇知之。聽我發一願死不有恨。即時願曰。我本開荒出土養民。來者皆活富樂無極。自舉我為王。依案諸國自共作此今反殺我。我實無惡於此人民。若我死者願作羅剎。還入故身中當報此怨。於是絞殺棄尸而去。三日之後王神還身中。自名阿羅波。即起入宮。殺新王并後宮婇女左右姦臣。欲盡殺人。國中三老草索自縛來。向羅剎自首。此是姦臣所為。非是細民所可能知。乞匃原恕願還治國。曰我是羅剎。與人從事食飲當得人肉。羅剎急性忿不思難。三老曰。國是王許故當如前。食飲所須當相差次。國老共出宣令人民。從此為次。家出一小兒。生用作食。食羅剎王三四千家。正有一戶為佛弟子。居門精進持佛五戒。賢者大小懊惱啼哭。遙向崛山為佛作禮。悔過自責。佛以道眼見其辛苦。便自說言。因是小兒當度無數人。便獨飛往至羅剎門。現變光相照其宮內。羅剎見光疑是異人。即出見佛便起毒心。欲前唅佛光刺其目。擔山吐火皆化為塵。至久疲頓然後降化。請佛入坐頭面作禮。佛為說法一心聽法。即受五戒為優婆塞。里吏催食奪兒將還。室家㘁哭隨道而來。觀者無數為之悲哀。吏抱兒擎食著羅剎前。羅剎以手擎兒。長跪白佛。國人相次以兒食。我今受佛五戒不復得食人。請以小兒持布施佛。為佛給使。佛為受之。說法呪願。羅剎歡喜得須陀洹道。佛以小兒著鉢中。擎出宮門還其父母。而告之曰。快養小兒勿復愁憂。眾人見佛莫不驚怪。是何神此兒何福。而獨救之。羅剎所食奪還父母。佛即說偈言。戒德可恃怙　福報常隨己　見法為人長　終遠三惡道　戒慎除苦畏　福報三界尊　鬼龍邪毒害　不犯有戒人。見佛光像皆為佛弟子。聞偈歡喜皆得道跡(出法句譬喻經第五卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, King Bimbisāra had a great minister who, having committed some offense, was exiled to a southern mountain, a thousand li from the capital, a place where no one had ever succeeded in growing the five grains before. Once the minister arrived there, however, springs of water began to flow freely, and the five grains grew abundant; people from every direction, suffering hunger and cold, came to settle there, and within a few years, three or four thousand households had gathered, each given land to farm and sustain their own lives.

The elders and respected leaders among them then discussed together that their community had no ruler, being, as it were, a body without a head, and went together to the minister, and installed him as their own king. The minister told these elders: if you make me king, then it must be according to the proper law of kings everywhere — ministers of both civil and military rank, tribute paid from below to above, and the taking of women, opening of palaces, and levying of taxes on grain and cloth, all according to the customary law of such kingdoms. The elders answered: so be it — we shall follow the king's own law in every respect. And so he was installed as king, and arranged his own ministers, levied his people for civil and military service, and had them build city walls, dwellings, palaces, and towers.

The people, suffering under this harsh burden, could bear it no longer, and all began to plot together against the king. Certain treacherous ministers led the king out on a hunting trip, and, some thirty or forty li from the city, in the open wilderness, seized him, intending to kill him. The king asked his own attendants: for what reason do you kill me? They answered together: the people once loved you for your own prosperous, gracious rule — but now, worn down by hardship, they think only of rebellion, destroying their own households to seize your kingdom instead.

The king told them: this is entirely your own doing, not anything I originally caused — should you wrongly kill me for it, the spirits themselves shall know of it. Let me only make one final vow before I die, that I might die without regret. He then made this vow: I was the one who first cleared this wild land and brought forth its own soil to nourish my people; all who came here found life and boundless prosperity because of me. It was you yourselves who raised me up as king, following the same custom as every other kingdom — and now, instead, you kill me for it. I have truly done no evil to these people at all. Should I die now, I vow to become a rākṣasa, and return into this very body of mine, to avenge this very wrong.

They then strangled him, and abandoned his corpse, and departed. Three days later, the king's own spirit returned into his body, and, naming himself Āluóbō, rose up and entered the palace, killing the new king, together with the women of the inner palace and the treacherous ministers around him, intending to kill every person there.

The three elders of the kingdom then bound themselves with straw rope, and came before the rākṣasa to confess: this was the doing of treacherous ministers alone — the common people could never have known of such a scheme. We beg your forgiveness, and ask that you return to governing the kingdom.

He answered: I am now a rākṣasa, and, in dealing with human beings, must have human flesh as my own food and drink. The rākṣasa's own nature was violent and impulsive, giving no thought to consequence. The three elders said: since the kingdom is already yours to command, let it be as you say — whatever food you require, we shall provide it in due order.

The elders of the kingdom then went out and proclaimed to the people: from this day forward, in strict order, each household shall in turn offer up one newborn child, to be used as food. In this way, the rākṣasa king consumed the children of three or four thousand households.

There was, however, a single household among them who were disciples of the Buddha, who, keeping to their own home, applied themselves diligently and upheld the Buddha's five precepts faithfully. This worthy family, young and old alike, grieved and wept in anguish, and, turning from afar toward Vulture Peak, bowed in homage to the Buddha, confessing their own faults and reproaching themselves.

The Buddha, with his own spiritual eye, saw their suffering, and said to himself: because of this very child, I shall deliver countless beings. He then flew alone to the rākṣasa's own gate, manifesting a transformed, radiant form that illuminated the whole palace within. The rākṣasa, seeing this light, suspected some extraordinary being, and came out to see — and, at the sight of the Buddha, gave rise to a murderous intent, rushing forward to swallow the Buddha's own light and pierce his eyes, hurling mountains and spewing fire, all of which turned to mere dust; only after a long, exhausting struggle did the rākṣasa at last submit.

He invited the Buddha to sit, and bowed his head in homage; the Buddha taught him the Dharma, and, listening with single-minded attention, the rākṣasa received the five precepts at once, becoming a lay follower.

The local officer, hurrying to prepare the day's own offering, had seized a child and was bringing it back; the family wailed and followed along the road, and countless onlookers grieved for them. The officer, holding the child, offered it as food before the rākṣasa — who took the child up in his own hands, and, kneeling at length before the Buddha, said: the people of this kingdom have, in turn, offered me their own children as food — but now, having received the Buddha's own five precepts, I shall no longer eat human flesh at all. I ask instead that this child be given in offering to the Buddha himself, to serve as his own attendant.

The Buddha accepted this, taught the Dharma, and pronounced a dedication of blessing; the rākṣasa, overjoyed, attained the fruit of stream-entry. The Buddha then placed the child in his own bowl, carried it out through the palace gate, and returned it to its own parents, telling them: raise this child well, and grieve no more.

All who witnessed this were struck with astonishment: what spirit is this, and what merit does this child possess, that he alone should be rescued from the very rākṣasa who had devoured so many others, and restored to his own parents? The Buddha then spoke this verse:

"The virtue of the precepts can truly be relied upon; / its blessed reward constantly follows oneself. / One who sees the Dharma grows in true stature, / and, in the end, stays far from the three lower realms. / Careful observance of the precepts removes suffering and fear; / its blessed reward is honored throughout the three realms. / Ghosts, dragons, and every malicious poison / cannot harm one who truly holds the precepts."

All who saw the Buddha's own radiant form became his disciples at once; hearing this verse, they rejoiced, and all attained a glimpse of the path (Source: Dharmapada with Illustrative Tales, fascicle 5).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The founder-king's own dying vow is worth reading closely: he does not curse his own murderers by name, nor even ask for their deaths specifically — he asks only "to avenge this very wrong," and the resulting cycle of cannibalism ultimately consumes far more innocent households than the handful of treacherous ministers actually responsible for his own murder, a disproportion the story never directly addresses. The single Buddhist family's own devotion is the sole thing that interrupts this otherwise indiscriminate cycle — not through any argument on their own behalf, but simply by drawing the Buddha's attention to their grief, which he then acts on for the sake of "countless beings," not merely their own single child.

2. Two Merchants, One Kind Thought and One Cruel

Two merchants pass the same Buddha on the road — one thinking of him with reverence, the other with contempt — and their identical single thoughts, carried unspoken and unremarked, determine which of the two later rises to kingship through a divine omen and which dies drunk in a ditch.

Chinese Source

舍衛國有二商人。一人念曰。佛身丈六華色紫金。頂有肉髻項背日光巍巍難言。佛猶帝王沙門猶忠臣。佛陳明法沙門誦宣斯王明矣。知佛可尊。佛知其意而熟視之。其人心意喜如獲寶。其一念曰。佛者如牛弟子猶車。彼牛索車東西南北。佛亦猶然。佛知其有惡念必獲其殃愴然愍之。其人心惡二人俱去。至三十里有亭住宿沽酒飲之。共平屬事訟之紛紜。其善念者。四天王遣善神護焉。其毒念者。太山鬼神令酒入腹。猶火燒身。出亭露臥宛轉落車轍中。晨有商人車五百乘經轢之焉。伴見之曰。吾今衰矣。還國見疑取物而去名為不義。遂輕身委財而逝。展轉遠邁去舍衛數萬里。有一國。國王崩無太子。讖書云。中國有微人當王斯土。群僚議曰。國之無君猶體之無首。難以久立。故王有馬常為王作禮。若任王者馬必屈膝。僉曰大善。即具嚴駕以王印綬著車上。人馬填路觀者莫不揮涕。時彼商人亦出觀看。國太史曰。彼有黃雲之蓋斯王者之氣也。神馬直進屈膝舐足。群臣欣豫香湯澡浴。拜為國主。僉然稱臣。王曰。余本商人。無德於民不任天位。群僚曰。天授有德神馬屈膝。於是遂處王宮。聽省國政。深自惟曰。我無微善何緣獲此。必是佛恩使之然也。晨在御座嘆佛無上之聖。率土群僚向舍衛國。稽首言曰。賤人蒙世尊潤獲為人君。斯土傳世不知有佛。流俗之書亦無記焉。願以大明開斯國人之聾盲也。明日願與應真聖眾。垂意顧斯一時三月。佛告阿難。勅諸比丘。明日彼王請。皆當隨變化現神尊德。令其國民咸共覩焉。諸天聞佛之彼教化。相率導從作樂歌德。寶帳幢幡華下紛紛光色燿目。佛及應真皆坐正殿。王自斟酌畢。以小床於佛前坐。佛廣說法。王曰。吾本微人。業無快德何緣獲斯。佛告王曰。昔彼國王飯佛。王心念言。佛心如國王。沙門如臣。王種斯栽今獲其果。彼人云。佛若牛弟子若車。彼人自種車轢之栽。今在太山為火車所轢。自獲其果也。非王勇猛所能致矣。為善福隨履惡禍追。響之應聲善惡如音。非天龍鬼神所授。非先禰所為也。造之者心。成者身口矣。佛頌偈曰。心為法本　心尊心使　中心念惡　即言即行　罪苦自追　車轢于轍　心為法本　心尊心使　中心念善　即言即行　福樂自追　如影隨形。世尊又告王曰。眾惡之罪最重有五。不孝不忠殺親殺君家滅國亂。重罪一也。羅漢之行得空不願無想之定。與佛齊意拯濟眾生。而愚害之。重罪二也。佛者眾罪已畢景福會成。相好十力法導眾生。慈悲喜護心過慈母。而愚惡謗重罪三也。清潔沙門志靖行高。情抱經法助佛化愚。諸佛相紹眾生得度皆由眾僧。佞讒挍遘以致不調。以不調故正法毀。正法毀則民狂走。民狂走者三惡道興。惱比丘僧。重罪四也。佛尊廟寶物水土。眾生赤心以貢三尊。愚人或毀盜。重罪五也。犯斯五者罪無有請。謂之自殺身自滅族自投太山火矣。五罪之重重於須彌慎無犯焉。佛說經竟。王及群臣皆得須陀洹。受五戒為清信士。國民有作沙門者。守戒為清信士者。遂以五戒十善為國政。諸天祐護國遂興矣(出百愛經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there were two merchants. One of them thought to himself: the Buddha's own body, sixteen feet tall, his complexion like purple gold, a fleshy protuberance atop his own crown, radiance like the sun at his own back — how majestic, beyond words! The Buddha is like a sovereign king, and the śramaṇas are like his loyal ministers; the Buddha proclaims the clear Dharma, and the śramaṇas recite and proclaim it in turn — truly, this is a wise king's own governance. He recognized the Buddha as worthy of reverence.

The Buddha, knowing this man's own thought, looked at him closely, and the man's own heart was filled with joy, as though he had found a treasure.

The other merchant thought to himself: the Buddha is like an ox, and his disciples like a cart — that ox drags its cart east, west, south, and north wherever it is led, and the Buddha is just the same. The Buddha, knowing this man harbored a contemptuous thought, and that he would surely meet with misfortune because of it, felt sorrowful compassion for him.

That man's own heart held only malice, and the two of them then departed together. Traveling thirty li, they came to a wayside inn, and stopped for the night, buying wine and drinking together, discussing various matters, some of which grew into a heated dispute.

The one who had held a wholesome thought was protected that night by a good spirit sent by the Four Heavenly Kings; the one who had held a malicious thought was made, by the spirits of Mount Tai, to feel the wine enter his own belly as though his body were on fire. He staggered out of the inn, lay exposed on the ground, rolled about, and fell into a wheel-rut in the road. At dawn, a merchant caravan of five hundred carts passed by, and ran him over.

His own companion, seeing this, thought: my own fortune has surely turned ill now — should I return to my own country carrying his goods, I would be suspected of theft, branded unjust. And so, abandoning both his own wealth and his companion's remains, he set off, traveling on and on, until he had gone tens of thousands of li from Śrāvastī.

He came to a certain kingdom whose own king had died without an heir. A prophetic text there declared: a person of humble origin from the central lands shall become king of this land. The assembled ministers discussed this, saying: a kingdom without a ruler is like a body without a head — it cannot long endure. It was the kingdom's own custom that the royal horse always bowed before the rightful king; whoever the horse knelt before would be recognized as the true ruler.

All agreed this was an excellent plan, and prepared a grand procession, placing the royal seal and ribbon upon a carriage; people and horses filled the road, and every onlooker was moved to tears. The merchant, too, came out to watch. The kingdom's own royal astrologer declared: that man there is crowned with a yellow cloud — this is the very sign of a true king.

The sacred horse then walked directly to him, knelt, and licked his feet. The ministers, delighted, bathed him in fragrant water, and installed him as ruler of the land, all proclaiming themselves his own subjects. The man said: I was, in truth, only a merchant — I hold no true virtue toward the people, and am unfit for this heavenly station. The ministers answered: heaven itself has granted this to the virtuous, and the sacred horse has already knelt before you — there can be no doubt of it.

And so he took up residence in the royal palace, and attended to the affairs of state. Reflecting deeply on this, he thought to himself: I possess no particular goodness of my own — by what cause, then, have I obtained this? It must surely be the Buddha's own grace that has brought this about.

Each morning, seated upon his own throne, he praised the Buddha's unsurpassed sagehood, and, together with all the officials of his own realm, bowed toward Śrāvastī, saying: I, once a lowly man, received the World-Honored One's own grace, and have thereby become a ruler of men — yet this land has, for generations, known nothing of the Buddha, and even its own popular writings make no record of him. I wish, through his own great illumination, to open the deafness and blindness of this land's own people. I ask, tomorrow, that the assembly of true and worthy ones might turn their attention to this land, and remain here for a full three months.

The Buddha told Ānanda, and commanded the monks: tomorrow, this king's own invitation shall be answered — let each of you, in your own transformed and manifest form, reveal the Buddha's own honored spiritual power, that the people of that kingdom might all witness it together.

The devas, hearing of the Buddha's own intention to teach that land, accompanied him in procession, performing music and singing his own praises; jeweled canopies, banners, and flowers fell in profusion, their light dazzling to behold. The Buddha and the assembly of true and worthy ones all took their seats in the main hall.

The king personally served them, and, this done, sat upon a low seat before the Buddha. The Buddha taught the Dharma at length. The king said: I was originally a man of humble station, my own past deeds holding no particular virtue at all — by what cause have I obtained this station?

The Buddha told the king: long ago, that same king of Śrāvastī once offered food to the Buddha, and, in his own heart, thought: the Buddha's own mind is like a sovereign king, and the śramaṇas are like his ministers. He planted this very seed then, and now reaps its fruit. That other man, who thought the Buddha was like an ox and his disciples like a cart, planted with his own hands the very seed of the cart that then ran him over — and he is now in Mount Tai's own hell, crushed beneath a chariot of fire, reaping his own fruit in turn. Neither outcome was brought about by the king's own valor, nor by that other man's own misfortune alone. Good deeds bring blessing that follows, as evil deeds bring calamity that pursues; good and evil answer their own cause exactly as an echo answers a sound — this is granted by no deva, dragon, or spirit, nor caused by any ancestor at all. It is the mind that creates it, and the body and speech that bring it to completion.

The Buddha then spoke this verse:

"The mind is the root of all things; / the mind is master, the mind commands. / When the mind within holds an evil thought, / speech and action follow it at once, / and transgression and suffering pursue of themselves, / as a cart follows its own wheel-rut. / The mind is the root of all things; / the mind is master, the mind commands. / When the mind within holds a wholesome thought, / speech and action follow it at once, / and blessing and joy pursue of themselves, / as a shadow follows its own form."

The World-Honored One further told the king: among all evil transgressions, five are the very gravest. Being unfilial, being disloyal, killing one's own parents, killing one's own sovereign, and bringing ruin upon one's own household and disorder to one's own kingdom — this is the first grave transgression. To harm an arhat, who has attained emptiness, freedom from craving, and the formless absorption, whose own mind is equal to the Buddha's own in laboring to save all beings, out of mere foolishness — this is the second grave transgression. The Buddha, whose every transgression is already exhausted, whose own glorious merit is fully complete, whose marks and ten powers guide all beings, whose loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and protective care surpass even a loving mother's own heart — to slander him out of foolish malice is the third grave transgression. Pure and clean śramaṇas, of tranquil resolve and lofty conduct, who hold the scriptures' own teaching in their hearts to assist the Buddha in guiding the foolish — every buddha's own succession, and the deliverance of all beings, depends entirely upon the Saṅgha — to bring about discord among them through slander and scheming, so that the true Dharma is thereby destroyed, causing the people to run wild in confusion, and the three lower realms to flourish because of it, troubling the assembly of monks — this is the fourth grave transgression. The Buddha's own honored shrines, treasures, and lands, offered up by beings with a sincere heart to the Three Jewels — to destroy or steal from these out of foolishness is the fifth grave transgression.

Whoever commits these five has no transgression left ungoverned by consequence — it is called killing oneself, destroying one's own lineage, and casting oneself into the very fire of Mount Tai. The weight of these five transgressions exceeds even Mount Sumeru itself — take great care never to commit them.

Once the Buddha had finished teaching this sūtra, the king and his ministers all attained the fruit of stream-entry, and received the five precepts, becoming lay followers of pure faith. Some among the people of the kingdom went forth as monks; those who kept the precepts as lay followers of pure faith then made the five precepts and the ten wholesome actions the very governance of the state, and the devas protected and blessed the kingdom, which thereafter flourished (Source: Sūtra of a Hundred Forms of Affection).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both merchants' own decisive thoughts are entirely private and unspoken, noticed by no one but the Buddha himself, and the story is explicit that neither outcome — kingship for one, death under a cart for the other — was "brought about by the king's own valor, nor by that other man's own misfortune alone"; the Buddha's own closing teaching insists that mind alone, prior to any word or deed, is what plants the seed determining each. The surviving merchant's own subsequent flight from his dead companion, abandoning both body and goods rather than risk suspicion of theft, is a small, unheroic moment of self-interest that the story neither condemns nor excuses — it simply notes it as what actually happened, alongside the far larger consequence still unfolding from a single earlier thought.

3. King Prasenajit's Spontaneous Sugarcane and Rice

A brief entry: King Prasenajit's own past merit produces an inexhaustible sugarcane tree and rice stalk in his garden — a blessing he indulges to the point of illness, until a single verse on moderation teaches him to receive his own good fortune without excess.

Chinese Source

波斯匿王。宿殖德本福響自應。於後園中自然生甘蔗之樹。流出甘漿晝夜不絕。又生一株粳米。垂穗數百取之無盡。王受其福食之無厭。身體肥重喘息苦極不能轉側。往到佛所低身揖讓。在一面坐。佛便說偈。若人能專意　於食知止足　趣欲支形命　養壽守道德。王歡喜踊躍。即從坐起辭佛還宮。勅擎食人在吾前者。先說斯偈以為常法。王轉減食。身減體輕。行來無患(出出曜經第十七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

King Prasenajit's own past cultivation of merit produced its blessing in due response: in his own rear garden, a sugarcane tree grew spontaneously, flowing with sweet juice day and night without ceasing, and a single stalk of unhusked rice grew as well, bearing hundreds of grain-heads that never diminished no matter how much was taken.

The king, receiving this blessing, ate of it without restraint, until his own body grew heavy and swollen, his breath labored and painful, unable even to turn over in his own bed. He went to the Buddha, and, bowing low in respect, sat to one side. The Buddha then spoke this verse:

"If a person can hold their own mind single-pointed, / knowing when they have had enough of food, / taking only what sustains the body's own life, / they nourish their own years and uphold virtue's own way."

The king, overjoyed, rose at once from his own seat, took leave of the Buddha, and returned to his own palace, commanding that whoever served food before him should first recite this very verse, and made this his own lasting custom. The king thereafter gradually reduced his own food, his body grew lighter, and he moved about freely, without further trouble (Source: Sūtra of Illustrative Parables [Chūyào jīng], fascicle 17).

4. King Prasenajit's Ten Dreams

King Prasenajit, terrified by ten strange dreams into believing he is about to lose his throne, is instead given a reading of each dream as a moral omen for a distant, decadent future age still to come — his own reign, the Buddha assures him, remains entirely secure.

Chinese Source

波斯匿王。夜臥有十種夢。一小樹生華。二見小樹生果。三見牸牛從犢求乳。四見人切索羊隨後食。五見十釜重上釜踊灌入最下釜。六見馬一身兩頭食麥。七見血流成渠。八見澄水四邊清中央濁。九見犬在金器中小便。十見四方有四牛來相抵突各散還去。王見恐怖衣毛皆竪。即從臥覺便作是念。我應命終失此王位耶。即往問佛。佛曰。大王勿懷恐怖。不由此夢乃失王位。夢小樹生華者。當來眾生非法欲行。常懷貪嫉與邪法相應。是時人民不孝父母。不承事沙門婆羅門。便得供養。猶如今孝從父母供養沙門者也。小樹生果者。當來眾生非法欲行。常懷貪嫉與邪法相應。是時人民嫁未久。而抱子歸不知慚愧也。牸牛隨犢求乳者。當來眾生非法欲行。常懷貪嫉與邪法相應。母守門女傍通以自存活也。人切索羊隨後食者。當來眾生非法欲行。常懷貪嫉與邪法相應。以己財寶與外人通也。夢十釜列上頭釜溢涌灌最下釜中者。當來眾生非法欲行。與邪法相應。兒語父母言。速出此家詣山野澤。我欲住此村落也。馬一身兩頭食麥者。當來眾生非法欲行。常懷貪嫉與邪法相應。彼依國王劫奪婆羅門長者。或依婆羅門長者劫奪王藏也。夢血流成渠者。當來有國王不樂己境界。便集四種兵侵奪他界。亦不可制不隨法教。是時人民死者眾多也。夢澄水四邊清中央濁者。當來眾生非法欲行。常懷貪嫉與邪法相應。中國眾生好喜鬪亂。邊國人民無有諍訟也。夢犬在金器中小便者。當來眾生非法欲行。常懷貪嫉與邪法相應。我三阿僧祇劫勤苦所集法寶者。皆當誹謗。刀杖瓦石打我聲聞。此沙門種所說非法。好造歌頌也。夢四面有四牛來共相抵突各散還去者。當來眾生非法欲行。常懷貪嫉與邪法相應。是時四面有大雲起。雷電霹靂不雨散去也。是謂十夢應如是。大王十夢者。有是十應。以是因緣王勿懷恐怖亦不命終不失王位。皆是當來末世法應如是。大王當作是學。王大歡喜(出增一阿含經第四十一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

King Prasenajit, sleeping one night, had ten kinds of dreams. First, he saw a small tree bearing flowers. Second, he saw a small tree bearing fruit. Third, he saw a cow seeking milk from its own calf. Fourth, he saw a man cutting a rope to obtain sheep, which then followed and fed on him in turn. Fifth, he saw ten cauldrons stacked one atop the other, the topmost overflowing and pouring down into the very lowest. Sixth, he saw a single horse's body with two heads, each feeding on wheat. Seventh, he saw blood flowing as though forming a river channel. Eighth, he saw clear water, pure at its own edges yet muddied at its center. Ninth, he saw a dog urinating inside a golden vessel. Tenth, he saw four oxen come from the four directions, butt against one another, and then each scatter and return whence they came.

The king, seeing these dreams, was struck with terror, the hair of his own body standing on end; waking from sleep, he thought: am I fated to die, and lose this very throne? He went at once to ask the Buddha.

The Buddha said: great king, harbor no fear — it is not through these dreams that you shall lose your own throne.

The dream of a small tree bearing flowers: in an age to come, beings shall practice unlawful conduct, constantly harboring greed and envy, in accordance with false teachings; at that time, people shall no longer be filial to their own parents, nor serve śramaṇas and brahmins, and yet shall still receive offerings — quite unlike now, when only the filial, who serve their parents and support śramaṇas, receive such support.

The small tree bearing fruit: in an age to come, beings shall practice unlawful conduct, constantly harboring greed and envy, in accordance with false teachings; at that time, a woman shall return home carrying a child not long after her own marriage, feeling no shame in it at all.

The cow seeking milk from its own calf: in an age to come, beings shall practice unlawful conduct, constantly harboring greed and envy, in accordance with false teachings; a mother, guarding her own door as a prostitute, shall carry on illicit affairs to sustain her own livelihood.

The man cutting a rope to obtain sheep that then feed on him: in an age to come, beings shall practice unlawful conduct, constantly harboring greed and envy, in accordance with false teachings; people shall give their own wealth and treasure to outsiders in exchange for illicit relations.

The ten stacked cauldrons, the topmost overflowing down into the lowest: in an age to come, beings shall practice unlawful conduct, in accordance with false teachings; a child shall say to his own parents, leave this household at once, and go to the mountains and wilds — I wish to live here in this village myself.

The single horse's body with two heads, each feeding on wheat: in an age to come, beings shall practice unlawful conduct, constantly harboring greed and envy, in accordance with false teachings; some shall rely on the king's own authority to plunder brahmins and elders, while others shall rely on brahmins and elders to plunder the royal treasury in turn.

The dream of blood flowing like a river channel: in an age to come, there shall be a king unsatisfied with his own borders, who shall gather his own four divisions of troops to invade another's own territory, unrestrained and unwilling to follow the true teaching; at that time, the people who die shall be many indeed.

The clear water, pure at its edges yet muddied at its center: in an age to come, beings shall practice unlawful conduct, constantly harboring greed and envy, in accordance with false teachings; the people of the central lands shall delight in strife and disorder, while the people of the outlying lands shall remain free of contention.

The dog urinating in a golden vessel: in an age to come, beings shall practice unlawful conduct, constantly harboring greed and envy, in accordance with false teachings; the very treasure of the Dharma I have gathered through diligent effort across three asaṃkhyeya kalpas shall be slandered, and my own śrāvaka disciples struck with sword, staff, tile, and stone, accused of teaching what is unlawful, while those who compose mere idle songs and verses shall be praised instead.

The four oxen coming from the four directions, butting against one another, then scattering and returning: in an age to come, beings shall practice unlawful conduct, constantly harboring greed and envy, in accordance with false teachings; at that time, great clouds shall rise from all four directions, thunder and lightning shall strike, yet no rain shall fall, and the clouds shall simply disperse.

These are the ten dreams, and their true meanings, exactly as I have explained. Great king, these ten dreams of yours carry these ten corresponding omens; because of this, you need not fear at all — you shall not die, nor lose your own throne because of them. All of these are simply how it shall be in the final, degenerate age still to come. Great king, you should understand this well. The king was greatly delighted (Source: Ekottara Āgama, fascicle 41).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own consistent move across all ten dreams is worth naming: rather than reading them as omens about Prasenajit's own individual fate, as the terrified king himself initially assumes, he reinterprets every single image as a symbolic description of moral decline in a future age of the Dharma — inverted family loyalty, transactional relationships, senseless warfare, false teaching praised over true. The king's own relief at the end is telling: he is not disturbed at all by the prospect of some distant future's moral collapse, only by the mistaken fear that these dreams concerned his own present throne.

5. King Prasenajit's Grief for His Daughter

A brief entry: King Prasenajit, devastated by his own daughter's death and unable to think of anything that could ransom her back, is taught through a parable of another grieving father's own madness that all attachment ends in separation, and that grief itself is simply the shape love takes when it meets impermanence.

Chinese Source

波斯匿王。女婆陀命過。極愛念之。未曾離目。殯葬已畢。親覲如來頭面禮足在一面坐。佛言。今王何故衣裳塵垢愁憂如是。王言。有一女適命終甚愛念。始殯葬竟欲以象贖女命。乃至車馬伏藏。愁憂苦惱皆由恩愛生。所以然者。古者大王。於此舍衛城。有人女命終。愛念女故未曾遠目。便自迷惑不有所識。處處遊行問人民言。誰見我女。大王當以此方便知。愁憂苦惱皆由恩愛生。大王勿懷愁憂。一切恩愛皆當分離。所生之物必當壞敗。如是廣為說法。王受教而去(出波斯匿王女命過請佛經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

King Prasenajit's own daughter, Pótuó, died; he had cherished her so dearly that he had never once let her out of his own sight. Once her funeral rites were complete, he went personally to the Tathāgata, bowed at his feet, and sat to one side. The Buddha asked: why, great king, are your robes so soiled with dust, and your own grief so deep?

The king said: I had a single daughter who has just died; I loved her so dearly that, once her burial was complete, I wished I could ransom her own life back with an elephant, or even with my own carriages, horses, and buried treasure — yet this grief and suffering, I know, arises entirely from attachment and love itself.

The Buddha said: and why is this so? Great king, in ancient times, in this very city of Śrāvastī, there was a man whose own daughter died; he had loved her so dearly, never letting her from his sight, that he went half mad with grief, wandering everywhere, asking people: who has seen my own daughter? Great king, by this very example, know that grief and suffering arise entirely from attachment and love. Great king, hold no further grief — for all attachment and love must, in the end, come to separation, and whatever is born must, in the end, come to ruin. He then taught the Dharma to him at length in this way, and the king received his teaching and departed (Source: Sūtra of King Prasenajit's Request to the Buddha After His Daughter's Death).

6. The Servant Girl Who Became Queen Mallikā

A servant girl's single, spontaneous act of offering the Buddha her own portion of dried rice — hoping only that it might somehow free her from servitude — leads, through an entirely unrelated chance encounter on a royal hunting trip, to her being purchased outright and elevated to Queen Mallikā, first among five hundred women.

Chinese Source

時舍衛城中。有一大姓婆羅門。名耶若達。多饒財寶。一婢名黃頭。常守末羅園。時彼婢常愁憂言。我何時當免出於婢。時彼婢晨朝已。食分乾飯持詣園中。爾時世尊入城乞食。時黃頭婢遙見如來。心自念言。我今寧可持此飯施彼沙門。或可脫此婢使。即授飯施佛。世尊慈愍受還精舍。時黃頭婢。即前進入末羅園中。波斯匿王。嚴四種兵出外遊獵。從人分張馳逐群鹿。天時大熱遙見末利園。即迴車往步入園中。黃頭遙見波斯匿來。行步舉動非是常人。即前奉迎言。善來大人可就此坐。即脫一衣敷令王坐。黃頭問言。不審須水洗脚不。王言可爾。即取水與王。為王揩脚。復問王言。欲洗面不。即更以水與王洗面。復問王言。欲飲不。即詣池更洗手。取好藕葉盛水與王。復問王言。不審欲臥息不。即復更脫一衣與王敷之。見王臥已在前長跪。按脚及餘支節。解王疲勞。黃頭身如天身細濡妙好。王著細滑。心念言。未曾有如此女聰明。我所不教而悉為之。王即問言。汝是誰家女。報言。我是耶若達家婢。使差我守此園。如是語頃。王諸大臣。尋王車迹來詣園中。跪拜王足在一面立。王勅一人。汝喚耶若達婆羅門來。婆羅門來詣王所。王問言。此女是汝婢耶。答曰是。王言。吾今欲取為婦。汝意云何。報言。此是婢使云何為婦。王言。無苦。但論價直。婆羅門言。欲論價直。百千兩金。我豈取王價。今持奉王。王言。不爾。我取為婦。云何不與價。即出百千兩金。與婆羅門。即迎載入宮。眾臣衛從。末利園中將來故即莂之末利夫人。王甚愛敬。復於異時。王於五百女人中。立為第一夫人。在高殿便自念言。我以何業報因緣得免於婢今受如是快樂。復作是念。將是我先以和蜜乾飯分施與沙門。以此因緣今得免婢。受如是快樂耳(出四分律初分第十三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there lived a brahmin of great lineage, named Yajñadatta, possessed of abundant wealth. He had a servant girl named Yellow-Head, who was always kept to tend the Mallikā garden. This servant girl constantly grieved, saying to herself: when shall I ever be freed from this servitude?

One morning, having received her own ration of dried rice, she carried it with her to the garden. At that moment, the World-Honored One entered the city to beg for alms, and Yellow-Head, seeing the Tathāgata from a distance, thought to herself: might I not offer this very rice to this śramaṇa? Perhaps it could free me from this servitude. She offered the rice to the Buddha at once, and the World-Honored One, in his own compassion, accepted it, and returned to the monastery. Yellow-Head then went on into the Mallikā garden.

King Prasenajit, meanwhile, had prepared his own four divisions of troops and gone out hunting; his own attendants had scattered in pursuit of a herd of deer. The day growing very hot, he saw the Mallikā garden from a distance, and, turning his own carriage toward it, walked in on foot. Yellow-Head, seeing Prasenajit approach from afar, and recognizing from his own bearing and movement that he was no ordinary person, came forward to greet him: welcome, great one — please, take a seat here — and, removing one of her own garments, spread it out for the king to sit upon.

Yellow-Head then asked: might you wish for water, to wash your own feet? The king assented, and she brought water and washed his feet herself. She asked again: would you like to wash your face as well? — and brought water for this too. She asked again: would you like something to drink? — and, going to the pool, washed her own hands, and, taking a fine lotus leaf, filled it with water and offered it to the king. She asked yet again: might you wish to rest and lie down? — and, removing another of her own garments, spread it out for him.

Once the king had lain down, she knelt at length before him, and massaged his own feet and limbs, easing his fatigue. Yellow-Head's own body was as fine and soft as a heavenly being's; the king, delighted by this smooth, gentle care, thought to himself: never before have I encountered a woman so clever as this — she has done everything perfectly, without my ever having instructed her at all.

The king asked her: whose daughter are you? She answered: I am a servant of the household of Yajñadatta, assigned to tend this garden.

As they spoke, the king's own ministers, following the tracks of his carriage, arrived at the garden, and knelt before the king, standing to one side. The king ordered one of them: summon the brahmin Yajñadatta here. The brahmin came before the king, who asked him: is this girl your own servant? He answered: she is. The king said: I now wish to take her as my own wife — what say you to this?

He answered: she is but a servant — how could she become your wife? The king said: there is no difficulty in this — let us simply agree on a price. The brahmin said: if it is a matter of price, then a hundred thousand taels of gold — yet how could I possibly accept payment from the king? I offer her to you freely instead. The king said: no — since I take her as my own wife, how could I fail to pay a price for her? — and gave him a hundred thousand taels of gold at once.

He then had her brought back to the palace, escorted by all his own ministers; because she had been found in the Mallikā garden, she was given the very name Queen Mallikā. The king cherished and honored her greatly, and, at a later time, elevated her to the position of chief consort among his own five hundred women.

Standing in her own high palace, she thought to herself: through what karmic cause have I come to be freed from servitude, and now enjoy such happiness as this? And she thought further: it must surely be that earlier offering of honeyed, dried rice I gave to the śramaṇa — it is through this very cause that I have now been freed from servitude, and come to enjoy such happiness (Source: Four-Part Vinaya, first division, fascicle 13).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story is careful to keep its own two causal threads entirely separate for most of its length: Mallikā's offering to the Buddha and her later elevation to queen are connected by nothing but coincidence in the narrative's own actual sequence of events — she meets the king through an unrelated hunting trip, and it is her own quick, attentive hospitality, not any recognizable merit, that first draws his interest. Only at the very end does Mallikā herself supply the causal link the story has otherwise withheld, reasoning backward from her own present happiness to the single act of generosity that must, she concludes, explain it — the compendium leaving it deliberately unclear whether this is a certainty the reader should share or simply the only explanation available to someone trying to make sense of her own good fortune after the fact.

7. King Good-Faith and the Origin of Bathing the Buddha

A brief origin story: a king who worships a nyagrodha tree as a stand-in for the Buddha after his own nirvāṇa watches a jealous queen's own hired curse kill it — and the tree's own miraculous restoration through a ritual bathing becomes the founding precedent for the entire Buddhist custom of bathing images of the Buddha every fourth month.

Chinese Source

古昔有佛。號曰始無。時有國王。名曰好信。好樂佛法視佛無厭。種尼俱類樹。樹下為佛敷栴檀床座。佛坐其上。好信王聽經。佛泥洹後。王不見佛。便名尼俱類樹則為佛樹。見之如見佛。日日往樹下坐。常所坐處想聞教戒。王有青衣。名曰拘錄。常侍王邊。夫人嫉妬雇婆羅門令呪殺佛樹。於常婆羅門。四月七日夜取南山中大毒蛇腦塗樹。樹即枯死。王即便悲涕不能自勝。四月八日夜半明星出時取五色香水集華用灌此樹。即還更生。王便願言。當令十方諸佛生時用今日。得道時用今日。般泥洹亦用今日。從此以來諸佛興世。皆是此日。故用四月八日灌佛也(出宿願果報經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a buddha named Beginningless. At that time there was a king named Good-Faith, who delighted greatly in the Buddha's own teaching, never once tiring of gazing upon him. He planted a nyagrodha tree, and, beneath it, laid out a couch of sandalwood for the Buddha, upon which the Buddha sat while King Good-Faith listened to his teaching.

After the Buddha's own nirvāṇa, the king, no longer able to see him, took this same nyagrodha tree as a stand-in for the Buddha himself, regarding it as though seeing the Buddha directly, and went each day to sit beneath it, in the very place he had always sat, imagining he still heard the teaching there.

The king had a servant, named Jūlù, who constantly attended him. The king's own consort, out of jealousy, hired a brahmin to curse and kill this "Buddha-tree." Following this brahmin's own instructions, on the night of the seventh day of the fourth month, she took the venom from a great poisonous snake in the southern mountains, and smeared it upon the tree, which withered and died at once. The king, seeing this, wept without restraint.

On the night of the eighth day of the fourth month, at midnight, as the morning star rose, water of five fragrant colors was gathered together with flowers, and poured over the tree — and it came back to life at once.

The king then made this vow: may every buddha of the ten directions, in every age to come, be born on this very day; may they attain the path on this very day; and may they enter final nirvāṇa on this very day as well. And, from that time onward, every buddha's own appearance in the world has indeed fallen upon this same day — and it is for this reason that the Buddha is bathed on the eighth day of the fourth month (Source: Sūtra of the Fruits of Past Vows).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The tree's own restoration through ritual bathing is presented as the direct historical origin of an actual, still-practiced Buddhist observance — the bathing of Buddha images on the eighth day of the fourth month — and the king's own vow retroactively unifies the birth, enlightenment, and parinirvāṇa of every buddha across all of time onto this single date, an origin myth built entirely around one grieving king's private devotion to a tree he could no longer distinguish, in his own heart, from the Buddha himself.

9. King Bimbisāra's Four Kinds of Dread

Three generations of increasingly lenient royal punishment for theft culminate in King Bimbisāra's own private horror at having ordered a thief's finger amputated — a single act of judicial violence he cannot forgive himself for, traced by the Buddha to a past life in which he once, as a different kind of king, devised an entirely different solution to the very same problem.

At last, he was bound and brought before the king, who said: take him away, and cut off one of his small fingers.

The king was struck with deep grief and regret, and thought to himself: I am the very last in a line of righteous kings, yet I have become the very first to commit an unlawful act. A true king ought to hold his own people's welfare foremost in his heart — yet I have had a man's own body mutilated.

In a past age, there was a kingdom named Kāśī, whose king was named Renown. The people of that kingdom were all skilled in some craft or trade, sufficient to sustain their own livelihood; whoever lacked any such skill was called a fool, and whoever committed theft was likewise called a fool.

There was, at that time, a man who committed theft, and was bound and brought before the king. The king said: enough, enough — that man lost his own property, and this man committed the theft — what use is there in my own further participation in this wrongdoing? He reflected: until now, there has only ever been this one fool — should the number of such fools fail to reach a thousand, I myself am fated to die.

He then had this fool placed in his own great minister's care, saying: I require a thousand fools for a great assembly I intend to hold — gather them, and, once the number is complete, report it to me. The minister took the fool into custody, confining him in a single place. This must surely be the increase of wicked ways in this present, declining age.

The fools, hearing this command, rejoiced, and obeyed it faithfully.

The king then handed his own throne to his crown prince, and went forth into the mountains to study the way of the sages. That king of that time was Bimbisāra himself, who has always dreaded transgression in this way.

The Buddha said further: it is not only in this present life, when he ordered a finger cut off and immediately regretted it, that King Bimbisāra has shown such remorse. In a past age, there was a brahmin, without any wealth of his own, who sustained himself by begging; his own wife bore him no children. In their household there had long lived a mongoose, and, in time, they had a son of their own as well; the brahmin cherished the mongoose as though it were his own child, and the mongoose, in turn, regarded the brahmin as its own father.

Some time after the wife had borne their son, the brahmin, going out to beg, told his wife: should you go out yourself, be sure to take the child with you. His wife, however, taking food for the child, went to a neighboring house to have grain pounded, leaving the child behind, still smelling of milk and cream. A venomous snake, opening its own mouth to spit poison, meant to kill the small child.

The mongoose thought to itself: my own parents are away — how could I let this venomous snake kill my own little brother? — and killed the snake at once, tearing it into seven pieces, and, its own mouth smeared with the snake's blood, stood before the doorway, hoping its parents would see this and rejoice.

The brahmin, returning from outside, saw his own wife standing outside the house from a distance, and, angered, said: I told you to take the child with you when you went out — why have you gone alone? The father then entered the house, and, seeing the mongoose's own mouth smeared with blood, thought at once: my wife and I were both away — the mongoose must surely have killed and eaten our own child! — and struck the mongoose dead with his own staff.

Entering further, he then saw his own child sitting safely in the courtyard, playing, sucking on its own fingers — and, seeing too the venomous snake torn into seven pieces, was overcome with grief and remorse: it was this very mongoose that saved my own child's life, and, failing to look carefully, I have killed it in an instant — how painful, how pitiable this is! He fell to the ground, overwhelmed with anguish.

At that moment, a deva in the open sky spoke this verse:

"One should observe carefully and examine closely, / and never act in a sudden fit of rage — / for parting with a good friend's own love and devotion / through wrongful harm brings a grief that pierces deep."

That brahmin of that time is King Bimbisāra now (Source: Sāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 2).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two past-life episodes attached to this single present-day guilt pull in genuinely different directions, and the compendium lets both stand rather than reconciling them: the fool-king story shows a past incarnation solving the identical problem of theft through patient, humane rehabilitation rather than punishment, implicitly framing Bimbisāra's own present severity as a regression from an earlier, wiser version of himself; the mongoose story, by contrast, shows a killing carried out in a single instant of misdirected rage, its consequence overwhelming remorse the very moment the truth becomes clear — a much closer structural parallel to the finger amputation that opens this entry, carried out too hastily to be undone before regret arrives.

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the king of Rājagṛha was named Bimbisāra, who, in his youth as crown prince, had made five wishes: first, that he might become king while still young; second, that a buddha might appear within his own kingdom; third, that he might visit the Buddha often; fourth, that he might constantly hear the Dharma taught; and fifth, that, hearing it, he might swiftly attain understanding and reach the fruit of stream-entry. Every one of these wishes was granted to him.

North of Rājagṛha lay another kingdom, named Takṣaśilā, whose king was named Pukkusāti, a man of exceedingly lofty and independent spirit, who had once, in a past life, already seen the Buddha and received his own teaching, and who now studied the teaching of the six elements within the body — earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness.

King Bimbisāra and King Pukkusāti, though they had never once met in person, held each other in distant affection and respect, as though brothers, and constantly exchanged letters and gifts. A lotus flower with a thousand golden petals once grew in King Pukkusāti's own kingdom, and he sent it as a gift to King Bimbisāra, who, seeing this flower, was overjoyed, and said: King Pukkusāti has sent me a truly rare and extraordinary gift.

King Bimbisāra then wrote to King Pukkusāti, saying: my own kingdom holds great abundance of gold, silver, and every treasure, yet I do not consider these my own true treasure — for, within my kingdom, there has now appeared a flower among men, called the Buddha, his own body the color of pure gold, marked with the thirty-two signs.

King Pukkusāti, hearing the very name of the Buddha, was overjoyed, and wrote back to King Bimbisāra, asking him to explain in full the Buddha's own teaching and practice.

Some days later, King Pukkusāti thought to himself: a person's own life cannot be known even from one breath to the next — I cannot wait any longer for Bimbisāra's own reply. He commanded the lesser kings and ministers under his own rule to prepare to set out at once for the Buddha's own place.

Along the way, he encountered King Bimbisāra's own letter, which said: the Buddha teaches that one should abandon home, wife, and children, and cut off craving and desire, shaving one's own head and beard, and, donning monastic robes, become a śramaṇa — and the letter further set out the twelve links of dependent origination, sending this teaching to King Pukkusāti.

Having read this letter, King Pukkusāti reflected deeply on it. That very night, once his own ministers and officials had all gone to sleep, he rose quietly, without a sound, and slipped away alone, going to a burial ground, where he shaved his own head, and, donning monastic robes, became a śramaṇa himself.

The Buddha, with his own divine eye, saw that Pukkusāti had arrived at Rājagṛha, and was lodging in a potter's own workshop. The Buddha, knowing that King Pukkusāti's own life would end the very next day, flew at once to the potter's workshop, asking to lodge there for a single night. The potter answered: you may certainly stay.

The Buddha sat composed in one corner, and thought to himself: this Pukkusāti is calm and tranquil — and, rising, went before him, and asked: from which teacher have you received the path, that you have become a śramaṇa? Pukkusāti answered: I have heard of a buddha, and now serve him as my own teacher.

The Buddha thought: this worthy man has become a śramaṇa purely for my own sake — I should now teach him the very scripture concerning knowledge of past lives, and only then will he truly understand. The Buddha said: let me now explain this teaching to you — listen well. Pukkusāti said, very well.

The Buddha then taught him the Dharma, and Pukkusāti attained the third fruit, that of non-returner, and, only then, recognized that this was indeed the Buddha himself, and rose at once to bow before him.

The next day, not far into the city, a young, still-toothless ox gored Pukkusāti to death. The monks asked the Buddha: yesterday, at the potter's workshop, the Buddha taught this śramaṇa the Dharma — and now he has been gored to death by an ox; to which realm shall he go?

The Buddha said: having received my own teaching, he had already attained the fruit of non-returner, and shall be reborn in the sixteenth heaven, where he shall attain arhatship. He then had the monks together gather Pukkusāti's own remains, bury them properly, and raise a stūpa over them (Source: Sūtra of King Bimbisāra's Five Wishes; also found in the Sūtra of King Pukkusāti's Own Karmic Causes).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the compendium's own account of the historical King Pukkusāti of Takṣaśilā, whose story is preserved in the wider canon as well (notably the Dhātuvibhaṅga Sutta), and the entry preserves the story's own genuinely tragic timing without softening it: Pukkusāti attains a real and lasting spiritual result, the non-returner's fruit, in the very last hours of his own human life, only to die by ordinary accident — a young ox's chance blow — before he has even had time to fully recognize the Buddha he has just been taught by. The friendship between the two kings, built entirely on letters and gifts exchanged between men who had never met, is itself an unusual portrait of intimacy sustained purely through correspondence, made all the more poignant by the fact that Pukkusāti's own final letter from Bimbisāra — describing the Buddha's teaching in full — is what moves him to abandon everything that same night, without ever waiting for a reply of his own.

12. The Deva Rohitassa Asks the Buddha About the End of the World

A deva who once, as a swift ascetic in a past life, tried to outrun the very edge of the universe itself, and died still traveling after a hundred years without ever reaching it, asks the Buddha directly whether such a place can be reached at all — and receives an answer that redefines "the world" entirely.

Chinese Source

時有赤馬天子。容色絕妙。於後夜時來詣佛所。白佛言。世尊。頗有能行過世界邊。至不生不老不死處不。佛告赤馬。無有能至者。赤馬天子又白佛言。奇哉世尊。善說斯義。如世尊說。所以者何。自憶宿命。名曰赤馬。作外道仙人。得神通離諸愛欲。我時作念。我有如是捷疾神足。如健士夫以利箭橫射。過多羅樹影頃。能登須彌。至一須彌。足躡東海超至西海。我念神力捷疾今求世界邊。便發足去。唯除食息便利。減節睡眠常行百歲。於路命終竟不得至。即沒不現(出雜阿含第二十九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was, at that time, a deva named Rohitassa, of surpassing beauty, who came before the Buddha in the latter part of the night, and said: World-Honored One, is there any way to travel beyond the very edge of the world, to a place free of birth, old age, and death? The Buddha told Rohitassa: there is no one who can ever reach such a place.

The deva Rohitassa said further to the Buddha: how remarkable, World-Honored One, that you should teach this truth so well — for it is exactly as the World-Honored One says. And why do I say this? I recall my own past life, when I was named Rohitassa, and was a non-Buddhist sage-ascetic, who had attained supernormal power and freedom from craving and desire. I thought to myself then: with such swift, spiritual speed as mine — like a mighty warrior shooting a sharp arrow clear across the shadow of a palm tree in an instant — I could climb Mount Sumeru itself, and, from one such peak, stride with a single step from the eastern sea clear across to the western sea.

Believing in my own swift spiritual power in this way, I resolved to seek out the very edge of the world, and set out at once, pausing only for food, rest, and bodily necessity, sleeping as little as I could, and traveled continuously for a hundred years — yet, still upon the road, my own life came to its end before I could ever arrive, and I simply vanished from that place entirely (Source: Saṃyukta Āgama, fascicle 29).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry, corresponding to the well-known Rohitassa Sutta of the wider Pali canon, is unusual in this compendium for withholding the Buddha's own full teaching — the source text as excerpted here ends with Rohitassa's own account of his failed hundred-year journey, without the Buddha's own famous closing reply that the world, its origin, its cessation, and the path to that cessation are all found within this very body, not at any physical distance to be traveled. Even without that explicit conclusion stated here, the shape of the teaching is already implicit in Rohitassa's own story: a hundred years of increasing spiritual speed brought him no closer to the world's edge at all, suggesting that the very premise of the search — treating liberation as a place reached by sufficient velocity — was mistaken from the start.

13. The Clever King Who Feigned Madness

Fascicle 28 closes with a king clever enough to protect his own drinking water from a poisoned rainfall that drives his entire court insane — and clever enough, too, to realize that being the only sane person left is itself a kind of danger, requiring him to fake the very madness he alone was spared.

Chinese Source

外國有惡雨。若墮江湖河井陂池。人食之者狂醉七日。有國王。多智善知惡雨見雲以知。使蓋一井令雨不入。時百官群臣食惡雨水。舉朝皆狂。脫衣赤裸泥土塗頭坐王殿上。唯王一人獨不狂耳。一切群臣不自知狂。反謂王狂。何故著衣獨異眾人。皆相謂言。此非小事思共宜之。王恐諸臣欲反便自怖懅。語諸臣言。我有良藥能自愈病。諸人小停待我服藥。王便入內脫衣同其而出。一切群臣見皆大喜。七日之後群臣醒悟大自慚愧。各著衣冠而來朝會。王故如前赤裸而坐。諸臣皆驚怪而問言。王常多智。何故若是。王答臣言。我心常定無變易也。以汝狂故反謂我狂。非實心也(出雜譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In a foreign land, there was once a harmful rain that, whenever it fell into rivers, lakes, streams, wells, or ponds, caused anyone who drank from them afterward to become deranged and delirious for seven days. There was a king, clever and wise, who understood the nature of this harmful rain, and, seeing the clouds that brought it, knew in advance to have a single well covered over, so that no rain could fall into it.

At that time, all his own officials and ministers drank from water tainted by the harmful rain, and the whole court fell into madness — stripping off their own clothes, sitting naked, smearing mud upon their own heads, seated within the king's own palace hall. Only the king himself remained unaffected.

Yet none of the officials recognized their own madness at all; instead, they declared the king himself mad, asking why he alone remained clothed, so different from everyone else, and said among themselves: this is no small matter — we must consider together what is to be done about it.

The king, fearing his own ministers might rise against him, grew afraid, and told them: I possess a fine medicine that can cure this very affliction of my own — wait here a moment, while I take it. The king then went within, removed his own clothing, and came back out exactly as they were. All his ministers, seeing this, rejoiced greatly.

Seven days later, his ministers regained their own senses, and, struck with deep shame, each dressed themselves properly and came before the king for their own morning audience — yet the king still sat there, naked, exactly as before. His ministers, astonished, asked him: my king has always been so clever and wise — why do you behave this way now?

The king answered them: my own mind has remained constant and unchanging throughout — it was only because you yourselves were mad that you took me, in turn, for the mad one; that was never my own true state of mind at all (Source: Miscellaneous Similes Sūtra, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king's own foresight in the story's opening — a single covered well, prepared before the rain even falls — only creates a second, harder problem once it succeeds: sanity itself, when it becomes a minority position surrounded by an entire mad court, is treated by everyone else as the very aberration requiring correction, and the king survives this not by asserting his own correctness but by strategically performing the very madness he does not share, until his own ministers recover on their own. This closes fascicle 28.
