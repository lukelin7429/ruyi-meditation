Merchants

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 43 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Merchants" (估客部) — sixteen entries on traders and caravan leaders, and one of the strongest narrative fascicles in the compendium. Among them: a sea-spirit's riddle about a handful of water and a sea; two brothers named at birth for what their unborn presence did to their mother's character, and a tree that gives drink, food, clothing, and treasure one branch at a time; the Siṃhala legend of the island of rākṣasī, the iron city with no gate, and the horse-king who will carry away only those who can hold a single hair without looking back; four cities of silver, gold, crystal, and lapis, and the burning iron wheel that follows them; a caravan leader who wraps his own arms in oiled cotton and lights them to lead five hundred men through seven days of darkness; a man who buys the five precepts for a thousand gold coins and argues in verse with a rākṣasa while embedded to the elbows in his scales; a merchant who spends all eight of his oxen to ransom a nāga maiden; the bodhisattva Great-Compassion, who reflects for seven days and then kills one man to save five hundred and to keep the killer out of hell; and a man driven from home for one cup of wine whose four remaining precepts are still enough to empty a house of its ghosts.

Translation Method

Fascicle 43 collects the merchants, and it is the most narratively concentrated fascicle we have translated. Nearly every entry turns on the same situation — a long journey, a company of five hundred, a danger that cannot be fought — and the interest lies in the range of answers the compendium gathers for it: wit, self-immolation, precepts bought at market, refusal to look back, and, in entry 14, deliberate killing.

The fascicle contains several deliberate doublets, and the compiler flags one of them himself. Entries 7 and 11 are the same rākṣasa-grappling story from two sources with different resolutions, and the source note at the end of entry 7 says so and gives that as the reason for keeping both. The makara fish that swallows a merchant ship appears here in entry 15 and has already appeared twice in fascicle 35; and entry 13, like fascicle 36's entry 17 and fascicle 41's entry 15, comes from the Sūtra of the Arising of Practices, the collection of the Buddha's own past misdeeds.

On names: Prasenajit, Mallikā, Ānanda, Śāriputra, Śuddhodana, Māyā, Devadatta, Dīpaṃkara, Upasena, Māndhātṛ, Cālā, Upacālā, Śakra, the Licchavi, Vārāṇasī, Śrāvastī, Rājagṛha, Jambudvīpa, Uttarakuru, and the Ābhāsvara heaven are given in their standard forms. Where a transliteration has no confident correspondence — Bōlì, Móhēyèyí, Mílián, Jūyǐn, Bímó, the Luómàn hall, Shīpīluó, Yōubōshīpóluó, Jūduō, Jílì — we keep the Chinese in pinyin. Names transparently descriptive in Chinese (Good-Seeking, Evil-Seeking, Lion, Great-Compassion, Great Forest, Full-Moon) are translated, with the Chinese kept in the contents list. Entry 3 is the Siṃhala legend and the protagonist's name means Lion; we translate it and set out the identification in the note.

Entries 1 and 7 carry verse — one at the close of entry 1, and five in the exchange between the caravan leader and the rākṣasa in entry 7. We set these off in italics with slashes for the line breaks of the Chinese; the Chinese column keeps them running on within the paragraph, as the source has them.

Entry 8 is not a narrative at all but a passage of scholastic question-and-answer from a Vibhāṣā, using the merchant and the swan as a case in a taxonomy of the four modes of birth. We translate it in that register rather than turn it into a story.

Two entries turn on killing — entry 13, where the Buddha in a former life spears a rival caravan leader, and entry 14, the classic case of killing by skillful means. Both are translated without softening, and the reading notes set out the reasoning each text gives and the difference between them.

1. Bōlì Obtains a Sea-Spirit's Necklace and Presents It to the King, and the King and His Consort Offer It to the Buddha

A sea-spirit poses a riddle about a handful of water and a sea, and gives the merchant a necklace for the answer. The necklace then passes to a king, to the one consort who would not come out to compete for it, and to the Buddha.

Chinese Source

昔有賈客。名曰波利。與五百人入海求寶。時海神出掬水。問波利言。海水為多掬水為多。波利答言。掬水為多。所以者何。海水雖多無益時用。不能救彼飢渴之人。掬水雖少值彼渴者持用與之以濟其命。世世受福不可訾計。海神歡喜讚言善哉。即脫身上八種香瓔挍以七寶。以與波利。海神送之。安善往還到舍衛國。持此香瓔上波斯匿王。具陳所由願蒙納受。王得香瓔以為琦異。即呼諸夫人羅列前住。若最好者以香瓔與之。六萬夫人盡嚴來出。王問。末利夫人何以不出。侍人答言。今十五日持佛法齋素服不嚴。是以不出。王便瞋恚遣人呼曰。如今持齋不應王命。如是三反。末利夫人素服而出。在眾人中明如日月。倍好於常。王意竦然加敬問曰。有何道德炳然有異。夫人白王。自念少福。稟斯女形情態垢穢。日夜山積人命促短。懼墜三塗。是以月月奉佛法齋。割愛從道世世蒙福。王聞歡喜。便以香瓔與之。末利夫人答言。我今持齋不應著此。可與餘人。王曰。我本發意欲與勝者。卿今最勝。又奉法齋道志殊高。是以相與。若卿不受吾將安置。夫人答言。大王勿憂。願王屈意共到佛所。以此香瓔珞奉上世尊。并採聖訓。累劫之福矣。王即許焉。即勅嚴駕往至佛所。稽首于地却就王位。白佛言。海神香瓔波利所上。六萬夫人莫不貪得。末利夫人與而不取。持佛法齋心無貪欲。謹以上佛。願垂受納。世尊弟子執心難。直信如此。豈有福乎。唯願世尊為受香瓔。佛說偈言。多作寶花　結步搖綺　廣積德香所生轉好　琦草芳華　不逆風熏近道聞教　德人遍香　雖曰是真不如戒香(出法句譬喻經第二卷)

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a merchant named Bōlì who went to sea with five hundred men to seek treasure. Then a sea-spirit came out and scooped up water in his hands and asked Bōlì: which is more, the water of the sea or the water in my hands?

Bōlì answered: the water in your hands is more. Why? Because the sea water, though it is much, is of no use at the needed moment and cannot save a person from hunger and thirst; the water in the hands, though it is little, given to one who is thirsty saves his life, and the merit received for that life after life cannot be reckoned.

The sea-spirit was glad and praised him: excellent — and took from his own body a necklace of eight fragrances inlaid with the seven treasures and gave it to Bōlì; and the sea-spirit saw him off, and he went and came back safely to the kingdom of Śrāvastī.

He took this fragrant necklace and presented it to King Prasenajit, setting out how he came by it and asking that it be accepted. The king, taking the fragrant necklace for a rare and extraordinary thing, called his consorts to come and stand in a row before him: whoever is most beautiful shall have the fragrant necklace. Sixty thousand consorts all came out in their finery.

The king asked: why does Lady Mallikā not come out? An attendant answered: today is the fifteenth and she is keeping the Buddha’s Dharma fast and wears plain clothes and no adornment, and that is why she does not come out.

The king grew angry and sent a man to summon her: even keeping a fast, she should not fail to answer the king’s command. Three times over; and Lady Mallikā came out in her plain clothes, and among the crowd she was bright as the sun and moon and twice as beautiful as usual.

The king was awed and asked her with added respect: what virtue of the path is it that makes you so plainly different? The lady said to the king: I consider that my merit is small, and that having received this female form its feelings and manners are defiled, and that day and night these pile up like a mountain, and a human life is short, and I fear falling into the three evil destinies. And so month by month I keep the Buddha’s Dharma fast, and cut off attachment and follow the path, and receive blessing life after life.

The king, hearing this, was glad, and gave her the fragrant necklace. Lady Mallikā answered: I am keeping the fast now and should not wear this; give it to another. The king said: my original intention was to give it to the best, and you are now the best; and moreover you keep the Dharma fast and your resolve in the path is exceptionally high, and that is why I give it to you. If you will not take it, where am I to put it?

The lady answered: let the great king not trouble himself. I ask the king to bend his mind and go with me to where the Buddha is, and offer this fragrant necklace to the World-Honored One, and take up his holy instruction besides — that is merit for kalpas on end. The king consented at once, and ordered his carriage made ready, and went to where the Buddha was, bowed his head to the ground, and withdrew to the king’s place.

He said to the Buddha: this fragrant necklace of the sea-spirit was presented by Bōlì, and among sixty thousand consorts there was not one who did not covet it, and Lady Mallikā, being given it, would not take it, and keeps the Buddha’s Dharma fast with a mind free of greed. I respectfully offer it to the Buddha and ask that you receive it. Is it not a merit that a disciple of the World-Honored One should hold her mind so firm and her faith so straight as this? I ask only that the World-Honored One accept the fragrant necklace.

The Buddha spoke this verse:

“Many make jeweled flowers, / stringing them into swaying, patterned ornaments; / but to accumulate broadly the fragrance of virtue / makes wherever one is born the better. / Fine grasses and fragrant flowers / do not carry their scent against the wind; / one who draws near the path and hears the teaching, / that person of virtue is fragrant everywhere. / Though this be called truly so, / it is not equal to the fragrance of the precepts.” (Source: Dharmapada Parables Sūtra, fascicle 2)

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The riddle at the start is the reason the entry exists, and the merchant's answer is worth reading closely: a handful of water is more than the sea, because the sea is of no use at the needed moment and the handful, given to someone thirsty, saves a life. It is a claim about occasion rather than quantity, and the whole rest of the entry restates it in another register.

Note the shape of the second half. The necklace goes to whichever consort is most beautiful; the one who wins is the only one who did not come out to compete, and she wins by coming out in plain clothes because she is keeping the fast. She then refuses the prize, and turns it into an occasion for the king to go and hear the Dharma. Three refusals in a row, each one gaining more than the acceptance would have.

2. Good-Seeking and Evil-Seeking Gather Treasure; Famished, a Tree Produces What They Need

Two brothers named at birth for what their unborn presence did to their mother's character. A tree in an empty marsh gives drink, food, clothing, and treasure, one branch at a time — and one of the brothers works out what the root must be worth.

Chinese Source

往昔閻浮。有國名波羅奈。時有薩薄。名摩訶夜移。其婦懷妊。自然仁善意性柔和。月滿生男。形體端正。父母愛念。施設美饍。延請親戚。并諸相師共相娛樂。抱兒示眾為其立字。相師問言。此兒受胎有何瑞應。父言。受胎母自和善。相師名為善求。乳哺長大好積諸德慈愍眾生。次後懷妊。期滿生男。形體醜陋。相師問言。此兒懷妊有何感應。答言。懷兒母自弊惡。相師名曰惡求。乳哺長大好為惡事。恒生貪心懷嫉妬意。年各長大。共行入海求索寶物。各有五百侍從。塗路懸遠中道乏糧。經於七日去死不遠。是時善求及諸賈人。咸共誠心禱諸神祇欲濟飢險。於空澤中遙見一樹。枝條欝茂。便即趣之。有一泉水。善求及眾悉共求哀。樹神現身語之。斫去一枝所須當出。諸人歡喜。便斫一枝美飲流出。斫第二枝種種食出百味具足。咸共承接各得飽滿。斫第三枝出諸妙衣種種備具。斫第四枝種種寶物悉皆具足。裝馱悉滿所須盡辦。惡求後到。眾人如前盡得充足。便自念言。今此樹枝能出是種種好物。況復其根。今當伐之。足得極妙佳好物。令人伐之。善求語惡求言。我等飢乏蒙此樹恩得濟餘命。云何而欲伐之。惡求即掘其根。善求感佩不忍見之。領眾歸家。伐樹已竟。有五百羅剎取此惡求及眾賈人悉皆噉之。財物喪失。佛告阿難。善求者今我身是。父者今淨飯王是。母者今摩耶是。惡求者提婆達多是。我於往昔常與相值恒教善法。而不用之。返更以我為怨(出賢愚經第九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago in Jambudvīpa there was a kingdom named Vārāṇasī, and at that time there was a caravan leader named Móhēyèyí. His wife conceived, and of herself she became humane and good and her nature gentle and harmonious; and when the months were full she bore a boy of upright form, and his parents loved him and set out fine dishes and invited their kin and the physiognomists to enjoy themselves together.

They held the child up before the company to give him a name, and the physiognomists asked: what auspicious sign was there when this child was conceived? The father said: when he was conceived his mother became of herself harmonious and good. So the physiognomists named him Good-Seeking; and as he was suckled and grew he delighted in accumulating virtues and was compassionate toward beings.

Afterward she conceived again, and when the term was full bore a boy of ugly form. The physiognomists asked: what response was there when this child was conceived? He answered: when she carried this child his mother became of herself vicious and evil. So the physiognomists named him Evil-Seeking; and as he was suckled and grew he delighted in doing evil, constantly gave rise to a greedy mind, and harboured jealousy.

When they had both grown up they went together to sea to seek treasure, each with five hundred attendants. The road was long and remote and halfway along their provisions ran short, and after seven days they were not far from death. Then Good-Seeking and the merchants together, with sincere hearts, prayed to all the spirits to be saved from famine and danger.

In an empty marsh they saw a tree in the distance, its branches lush and dense, and they made for it; and there was a spring there. Good-Seeking and the crowd all begged for pity, and the tree-spirit showed itself and said to them: cut off one branch and what you need will come out.

The people were glad and cut off one branch, and fine drink flowed out; they cut a second branch, and food of every kind came out, complete in a hundred flavors, and they all caught it and each ate his fill; they cut a third branch and fine clothing of every kind came out, complete in every sort; they cut a fourth branch and treasures of every kind came out in full, and their pack-animals were all loaded and everything they needed was provided.

Evil-Seeking arrived afterward, and the crowd, as before, all had their fill; and he thought to himself: if the branches of this tree can produce such fine things as these, how much more the root. I shall cut it down now, and get things of the utmost excellence. And he had it cut down.

Good-Seeking said to Evil-Seeking: we were hungry and worn out, and by this tree’s kindness we have saved what was left of our lives. How can you mean to cut it down? Evil-Seeking then dug up its root; and Good-Seeking, moved by gratitude and unable to bear the sight, led his company home.

When the felling of the tree was finished, five hundred rākṣasas took Evil-Seeking and the merchants with him and ate them all, and their goods were lost.

The Buddha told Ānanda: Good-Seeking is myself; the father is King Śuddhodana now; the mother is Māyā now; Evil-Seeking is Devadatta. In ages past I have constantly met with him and always taught him good dharmas, and he did not use them, and turned instead to making an enemy of me (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 9).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the Siṃhala legend — the story of the merchant Lion, the island of rākṣasī, and the flying horse-king who carries the survivors home — which is the traditional foundation narrative of Sri Lanka and is told across Buddhist Asia. The details here are the standard ones: the warning against the left-hand road, the iron city with no gate holding the previous cargo of merchants, the śirīṣa tree climbed to talk over the wall, and the horse-king who comes from Uttarakuru on the fifteenth.

The condition the horse-king sets is the point of the whole story and is stated twice. It is not strength or speed but singleness of mind: one may sit on his back and still not be carried if attachment arises, and one who holds a single hair with a right mind gets home. The women then deploy the children precisely against this, teaching them first to clasp their fathers' necks — and of the whole company only Lion gets away.

The king who takes the rākṣasī into his palace against explicit warning is a familiar type, but note what Lion does afterward: he does not say he told them so, he reminds the ministers that he had made his terms in advance, and he accepts the throne only on condition that they follow his instruction. The ending — a standing law that anyone who does not serve the Buddha is fed to ghosts west of the mountain — is reported without comment, as is the more attractive custom that royal sons must master the three collections before they may take the throne.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A story of graded pleasure and its bill. The four cities double at each stage — four women, eight, sixteen, thirty-two — and each is enjoyed for thousands of years, and each time what moves Mílián on is not dissatisfaction but suspicion: why do they not wish me to go further? Curiosity is what carries him to the iron city.

The accounting at the end is exact and unusually mechanical. Four months of gladness while keeping the eight-precept fast buys four jewelled cities and tens of millions of years; one act of treading on his mother's head buys one day of a burning head — which, in the iron city, is a wheel of burning iron for sixty hundred million ten thousand years. And the condition of release is the same as the one the man already there gives him: it lasts until someone like you arrives.

The identification is the sting: Mílián is the Buddha himself, before he sought the path.

The elder brother went with a company of merchants far off to another country, and after many years was delayed and did not come back in time.

The girl was growing up, and she said to the younger brother: your elder brother has gone far away and has died there and is not coming back; you should take my daughter now. His younger brother answered: how could such a thing be? The elder said it several times, and the younger brother’s mind was firm and never turned. So the elder faked a letter from abroad and, entrusting it to some merchants, said that the elder brother had died, and told him besides: your elder brother is dead; what will you do? The younger brother married the girl, and after some time she was with child.

The elder brother came back from abroad, and the younger, afraid in his heart, fled to Śrāvastī; and so, going on, he came before the Buddha and asked to go forth. The Buddha permitted it, and he became a śramaṇa named Upasena, upheld the discipline, and attained arhatship.

The elder brother learned of it and was filled with resentment and wished to kill him, and went to the kingdom of Śrāvastī and posted a heavy reward of five hundred taels of gold; and those who answered the call went forward together with him. Seeing his younger brother sitting in meditation, a thought of kindness suddenly arose in him: how should I kill this monk? But if I do not kill him he will take my gold from me. He meant to shoot the monk and hit his own elder brother instead; and his elder brother, in resentment and rage, suddenly ended his life and took the form of a venomous snake, and was born in the pivot of this religious man’s door, and as the door was opened and shut it struck his body and he died.

He wished again to take a small form as a venomous insect and lived under the religious man’s house, and fell from the roof onto the monk’s head; and its evil venom blazed up and it died at once.

He told Śāriputra: long ago there was a pratyekabuddha who appeared in the world and dwelt in a mountain forest. At that time there was a hunter who constantly caught birds and beasts and set up devices in hope of taking them. The pratyekabuddha startled the birds and beasts so that they were not caught, and, harbouring anger, he shot the pratyekabuddha with a poisoned arrow. Then the pratyekabuddha, pitying this man and wishing to make him repent, manifested his supernormal powers, and the hunter’s heart was filled with reverence, and in terror he came of himself and confessed his fault; and the pratyekabuddha accepted his repentance, and the poison worked and he died.

That man’s life ended and he fell into hell; and having come out of hell, for five hundred lives he was constantly killed by poison. Down to this present day, having attained the path of an arhat, he is still stung to death by a venomous insect (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 10).

7. A Caravan Leader Buys the Five Precepts and a Rākṣasa Cannot Harm Him

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a caravan leader who heard that there were rare treasures in another country and wished to go and trade there; but between the two countries there was the peril of a rākṣasa and it could not be passed.

The caravan leader, going about, saw at the west gate of the market a religious man sitting on an empty couch, saying that he sold the five precepts. The caravan leader asked: what are the five precepts? He answered: they have no shape or price; they are received with the mouth and kept in the mind, and afterward one gains birth in heaven, and in this present life they can turn back the peril of rākṣasa ghosts.

The caravan leader wished to buy them and asked how much he wanted. He answered: a thousand gold coins. And he received and conferred them on him; and when he had taken the five precepts he told him: when you go to the other country and reach the borderland, if the rākṣasa comes, only say: I am a five-precept disciple of Śākya.

A little later the caravan leader came to the ground between the two countries and saw the rākṣasa: his body thirteen feet tall, his head yellow as bulrushes, his eyes red as bosses, his whole body scaled, opening his mouth by turns like a fish working its gills, catching flying swallows in mid-air, sinking to the knee where he trod, and hot blood running from his mouth. With a crowd of several thousand he seized the caravan leader at once.

He said: I am a five-precept disciple of Śākya. The rākṣasa heard this and would not let him go at all. The caravan leader struck at him lightly with both fists, and the fists went into the scales and could not be pulled out. He trod on his head with his foot and butted him, and could not pull those out either; and all five parts of him were sunk in the scales and only his back could still move.

The rākṣasa said to him:

“Your body and your hands and feet / are all at once fastened fast. / You have only to go to your death — / what use is all this leaping about?”

The caravan leader’s resolve was still firm, and he said to the rākṣasa:

“My body and my hands and feet, / though all at once they are bound, / I hold my mind like gold and stone / and shall never be destroyed by you.”

The rākṣasa said to the caravan leader again:

“I am a king among ghosts, / and among men my strength is great. / From of old I have eaten your kind, / beyond any reckoning. / You have only to go to your death — / why comfort yourself with words?”

The caravan leader risked his anger and abuse, and thought to himself: this body of mine has turned in the three realms and has never yet been given away to anyone. I shall now give it away, and let this rākṣasa have one full meal. And he said:

“This rank and stinking body of mine / I have long wished to be rid of. / The rākṣasa has got his chance at me; / let him have it all as a gift. / My aim is the great vehicle, / and the fruit is complete omniscience.”

The rākṣasa was intelligent and understood what the caravan leader said, and gave rise to a mind of shame, and let the caravan leader go, and knelt at length with joined palms and apologized to him:

“You are a teacher who delivers people, / a rarity in the three realms. / Your aim is the great vehicle / and you will become a buddha before long. / Therefore I turn to you for refuge / and bow my head and face at your feet.”

The rākṣasa repented, and escorted the caravan leader to the other country; and he obtained a great deal of treasure and was escorted home again, and cultivated merit greatly and came at last to the traces of the path (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra — it is largely the same as the one before it, with a slightly different tendency, and so both are kept).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A closely related version of the story in entry 11 of this same fascicle, and the compendium says so itself in the source note — largely the same, with a slightly different tendency, and so both are kept. The physical predicament is identical, but the resolution is not. In entry 11 the man wins by declaring that his vigor will never rest; here he wins by giving up.

The turn is worth marking. Having exhausted force, the caravan leader reflects that this body has turned in the three realms and has never yet been given away to anyone, and offers it to the rākṣasa as a meal — and it is that verse, not the earlier one about holding his mind like gold and stone, that shames the rākṣasa into releasing him and bowing at his feet. The purchase at the market gate frames the whole thing: the five precepts are sold for a thousand gold coins by a man on an empty couch, described as having no shape or price.

A short scholastic passage in question-and-answer form, using a merchant, a swan, and two eggs to establish that human beings can be born from eggs.

Ru-Yi Translation

Animals and humans, and all four kinds of birth. Question: how do we know that among humans there is birth from an egg? Answer: as it is said — in the land of Jambudvīpa there were many merchants who went to sea to gather treasure, and got two swans, which changed as they pleased; one was lost and one remained, and he played with it and slept in the same room with it and coupled with it, and she bore two eggs. 10. A Merchant Gives Up His Oxen to Ransom a Nāga Maiden, Obtains Gold, and Provides for His Parents

A merchant gives away all eight of his oxen, one at a time, to buy a nāga maiden from the man leading her by a hole in her nose. What she tells him about being a nāga is the heart of the entry.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha was staying at Śrāvastī. South of the city there was a town called Great Forest, and a merchant drove eight oxen to the country of Jūduō in the north; and there was another merchant with him, pasturing his cattle in a marsh.

Then a Licchavi caught a nāga maiden and pierced her nose and led her along. The merchant, seeing it, gave rise to a compassionate heart and asked the Licchavi: what will you do with the one you are leading? He answered: I mean to kill and eat her. The merchant said: do not kill her; I shall give you an ox and buy her from you and let her go.

The captor would not, and so on up to eight oxen; and then he said: for your sake I shall let her go. And he took the eight oxen and let the nāga maiden go.

Then the merchant thought again: this is an evil man, and I fear he may follow after and catch her again. And he followed her himself to see where she went; and coming to the side of a pool the nāga changed into a human body and said to the merchant: heaven has given me my life, and I now wish to repay the kindness. Come with me into the palace and let me repay heaven’s kindness.

The merchant answered: you nāgas are hasty by nature, and your anger is not to be relied on; you may kill me. She answered: the man before had me bound and I had the strength to kill him, but only because I keep the uposatha rule I had no mind to kill at all — how much less should I do harm to heaven, who has now given me my life. Stay here a little; I shall go in first and set things in order.

Beside the nāga gate he saw two nāgas bound in one place, and asked them: what have you done? They answered: this nāga maiden receives the fast rule three days in each half-month, and we brothers were guarding her, and did not hold firm, and she was taken by the Licchavi; and for that we are bound. We ask only that heaven be kind and tell her to release us. And if this nāga maiden asks what food you wish to eat, ask for the food of humans in Jambudvīpa.

When the nāga maiden had set things in order she called him in and seated him on a jewelled couch and mat, and the nāga maiden said: what food does heaven wish to eat now? He answered: I should like the food of humans in Jambudvīpa. And food of every kind was set down.

He asked the nāga maiden: why are those men bound? The nāga maiden said: they have committed a fault and I mean to kill them. The merchant said: do not kill them. Otherwise I shall certainly kill them, she said. The merchant said: if you release them, then I shall eat. She said: I cannot simply release them; I shall punish them by banishing them among humans for six months.

The merchant asked: with such splendor as you have, what do you receive the uposatha for? She answered: in the nāga condition there are five sufferings. Which five? At birth one is a nāga; when asleep one is a nāga; in intercourse one is a nāga; when angry one is a nāga; and three times in half a day skin and flesh fall to the ground and the hot sand scorches the body.

He asked again: what do you wish to seek? She answered: I wish to be born in the human destiny. He asked: I have already obtained a human body — what should I seek? She answered: it is hard to obtain going forth. He asked again: under whom should I go forth? She answered: the Tathāgata, worthy of offerings and rightly and fully awakened, is now in Śrāvastī, and delivers those not yet delivered; you may go forth under him.

He said: I wish to go home. The nāga maiden then gave him eight cakes of gold and said: this is nāga gold; it is enough for your parents and kin to use to the end of their lives and it will not be used up. And she said: close your eyes — and by her transformation put him in his own country.

His travelling companions had arrived earlier and told his household that he had gone into the nāga palace, and his parents supposed their son already dead, and the kin and the clan were gathered in one place wailing and weeping.

Then the herdsmen and the gatherers of firewood and grass saw him and went back first and told his household: so-and-so has come home. The household heard it and rejoiced greatly, and went out and received him into the house and held a feast of life for him; and at the feast of life he gave the eight cakes of gold to his parents: this is nāga gold; cut it and it grows again, and they used it to the end of their lives (Source: Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 33).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The transaction at the start is exact and the entry counts it: one ox, refused; and so on up to eight, which is all the merchant has. He then follows the freed nāga not to collect a reward but because he suspects the captor will come back for her.

What the nāga maiden says about her own condition is the substance of the entry. She keeps the uposatha three days each half-month, and the reason she gives is the five sufferings of a nāga: one is a nāga at birth, in sleep, in intercourse, in anger, and three times in half a day the skin and flesh fall away and the hot sand scorches the body. What she is fasting for is human birth — and when the merchant, who already has one, asks what he should seek, her answer is going forth, and she tells him where the Buddha is. The story ends with him back home with eight cakes of gold; whether he ordains is not said.

The two bound nāgas at the gate are her own brothers, punished for failing to guard her; and it is at the merchant's insistence — I shall not eat unless you release them — that their sentence is reduced to six months' banishment among humans.

11. A Merchant Is Bound by a Rākṣasa

The shortest entry in the fascicle: five limbs stuck fast, and one answer.

Chinese Source

有一賈客主。將諸賈人入嶮難處。遇羅剎鬼。以手遮之言。汝住莫動不聽汝去。賈客主即以右拳擊之。拳即著鬼挽不可離。復以左拳擊之。亦不可離。以右足蹴之。亦復如是。以頭衝之。頭即復著。鬼問言。汝今如是欲作何等。心休息未。答言雖復五事被繫。心終不息。當以精進力與汝相擊。鬼時念。此人膽力極大。即曰。汝精進力大必不休息。今放汝去(出大智論第十六卷)。

There was a caravan leader who took his merchants into a place of danger and difficulty and met a rākṣasa ghost, who blocked him with his hand and said: stand and do not move; I shall not let you go.

The caravan leader struck him at once with his right fist, and the fist stuck to the ghost and could not be pulled away; he struck with his left fist, and that too could not be pulled away; he kicked with his right foot, and it was the same; he butted with his head, and his head too stuck fast.

The ghost asked him: being as you are now, what do you mean to do? Has your mind rested yet? He answered: though I am bound in five places, my mind will never rest; I shall strike at you with the power of vigor. Then the ghost thought: this man’s courage and strength are very great — and said: your power of vigor is great and you will certainly never rest; I shall let you go now (Source: Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, fascicle 16).

There was a caravan leader named Jílì who went to sea and took great treasure and came out safely with eighty million maṇi pearls, each worth ten billion taels of gold. He gave a great quantity of these treasures to a beggar and did not take them into his own house, but went back to the sea again to gather treasure; and after going to sea he obtained twice as much, and after eighty years came home to his own country.

As he was about to enter the city he saw a criminal: the executioners had him bound and were beating an evil-sounding drum and proclaiming through the streets and lanes as they brought him to the killing-ground, to inflict the punishment on him.

Then the man who was to die caught sight of Jílì and said: caravan leader, save me from the punishment of death and give me my life. You are a great donor, a worthy and good man. Jílì heard him and said to the man who was to die: alas, man — I now give you fearlessness and save you from the punishment of death. And going to the executioners he gave each and every one of them a maṇi pearl worth a hundred million taels of gold: wait here a moment while I go to the king and come back.

Then Jílì went in haste to the king and said: great king, I wish to buy this man’s life with fine treasure. The king answered: Jílì, this man’s offense cannot be pardoned and cannot be bought. If you must buy it, give me all that you have and take his death upon yourself, and then he may be let go.

Jílì was delighted: I have obtained a great gain and have got my full wish, and can save this man and satisfy my intention. And he sent all the goods of his household and all the treasure he had obtained in the great sea, measureless thousands of millions in gold, silver, and jewels, to the king, and said: great king, you may release this man. All that I have is here before you.

He said to the executioners: take Jílì and kill him. And they bound Jílì and brought him to the killing-ground, and one raised the knife in his right hand to strike Jílì, and his hand stiffened and would not come down; and, startled and terrified, he told the king.

The king came to see for himself, and, hearing this, took the knife himself and meant to kill Jílì; and as he raised the knife both his hands fell to the ground, and in great affliction he cried out and died. Jílì of that time is myself; the king of that time is Devadatta (Source: Bodhisattva-piṭaka Sūtra, lower fascicle).

13. Two Merchant Companies Gather Treasure; the Greedy One Loses Its Lives and the Restrained One Is Safe

Two goddesses give opposite warnings from the air on a treasure island, and two caravan leaders each believe one of them. What the survivor does from the deck of the ship is not passed over.

Chinese Source

昔無數阿僧祇劫前。有兩部賈客各五百人。在波羅奈。撰合資財嚴船渡海。解繫張帆乘風徑往。即至寶渚。上妙婇女無物不有。一部賈客主曰。我等以資財故勤苦至此。當住自娛。第二薩薄言。此間雖饒眾寶不當久住。時有天女慈愍此輩。便於空中語眾人曰。此間雖樂不足久住。却後七日此地當沒。語訖化去。復有魔女欲使賈客於此沒盡。空中告曰。卿等不須嚴駕還去。此地無水設當有水。此之眾寶眾女五樂何由而有。前天所說皆是虛妄。說已化去。第一薩薄勅其部眾。卿等勿去莫信前天。第二薩薄還告其眾。卿等莫貪速裝治船。前天所說至誠不虛。水不至者於此自娛然後徐歸。若水審來治嚴已去。佛語舍利弗。却後七日如前天言。水滿其地。第二薩薄將其部眾即得上船。第一薩薄先不治嚴。水至之日便著鎧仗共相挌戰。第二薩薄於船上以鋑牟鋑第一薩薄脚。徹過即便命終。佛語舍利弗。汝知第一薩薄者不則提婆達兜是。第二薩薄者則我身是。時第一賈客眾五百人者則今提婆達兜五百弟子是。時第二賈客五百眾者則今五百羅漢是。時第一天女則舍利弗是。時第二天女則今名滿月比丘婆羅門弟子是(出興起行經上卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, countless asaṃkhya kalpas past, there were two companies of merchants, five hundred in each, at Vārāṇasī, who gathered their resources, fitted out ships, and crossed the sea. They loosed the moorings and spread the sails and rode the wind straight on, and came to a treasure island where there were exquisite maidens and nothing that was not to be had.

The leader of the first company said: we have toiled and come this far for the sake of resources; we should stay and enjoy ourselves. The second caravan leader said: though there are treasures in plenty here, we should not stay long.

Then a goddess, in pity for these men, said to them from the air: though it is pleasant here it is not to be dwelt in long. Seven days from now this ground will sink. Having said it she vanished.

Then there was also a Māra maiden who wished to make the merchants perish there, and told them from the air: you gentlemen need not fit out and go back. There is no water in this place; and if there were water, how should these treasures and these maidens and the five pleasures be here at all? What the earlier deva said is all false. Having said it she vanished.

The first caravan leader ordered his company: do not go, and do not believe the earlier deva. The second caravan leader told his own: do not be greedy; make ready the ship quickly. What the earlier deva said is sincere and not false. If the water does not come, we can enjoy ourselves here and go back slowly; and if the water truly comes, we shall have made ready and can go.

The Buddha told Śāriputra: seven days later, as the earlier deva had said, the water filled that ground. The second caravan leader took his company and got aboard the ship; the first caravan leader had made no preparations, and on the day the water came he put on armor and weapons and fought with them, and the second caravan leader, from the ship, pierced the first caravan leader’s leg with a lance; it went through and he died at once.

The Buddha told Śāriputra: do you know who the first caravan leader was? He is Devadatta. The second caravan leader is myself. The first company of five hundred merchants of that time are Devadatta’s five hundred disciples now; the second company of five hundred are the five hundred arhats now. The first goddess is Śāriputra; the second goddess is the monk now named Full-Moon, the brahmin’s disciple (Source: Sūtra of the Arising of Practices, first fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A doublet of the whole fascicle's structure in one entry: two caravan leaders, two goddesses, opposite advice, and a deadline. What distinguishes the second leader is not that he believes the first goddess absolutely but that his reasoning is asymmetric — make ready, and if the water does not come we can enjoy ourselves and go back slowly. He is insuring, not trusting.

The killing at the end is reported flatly and we have translated it so: the first leader, unprepared, puts on armor and fights for a place, and the second, from the deck, runs him through the leg with a lance and he dies. The Buddha then identifies himself as the man with the lance and Devadatta as the man in the water. This entry, like fascicle 36's entry 17 and fascicle 41's entry 15, comes from the Sūtra of the Arising of Practices, which collects the Buddha's own past misdeeds — three of them now in this translation.

14. A Merchant Would Have Killed His Companions to Take the Treasure Alone, and Great-Compassion Killed That One Violent Man

A caravan leader dreams that one of his five hundred merchants means to kill the rest. He reflects for seven days and finds one course, and the Buddha names the merit of it.

Chinese Source

定光佛時。有五百賈人入海求寶。有異心者念言。我今悉害賈人獨取珍寶。時閻浮提有大導師。名曰大哀。時寐夢中海神語之。賈眾之中有一賊。欲殺五百伴獨取寶物。假令事逮墮墜地獄中。今仁導師當行權變。令賈人不死賊不獲罪。導師思惟七日。無餘方便。唯當殺此凶人耳。語眾賈者必皆興怒當共殺之。俱墮惡趣。設我獨殺。我當受罪。吾寧自忍百千劫苦。不令賈人普被危害。復令一賊墮地獄中。先為說法。令心欣然踊躍臥寐。佛言。大哀導師猶眾賈人興于大哀以權方便害此一賊。命終之後生第十二光音天上。時大哀者則吾身也。以斯方便超千劫生死。同船五百賈客。斯賢劫中五百佛興者是(出慧上菩薩經一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the time of the Buddha Dīpaṃkara, five hundred merchants went to sea to seek treasure, and one among them, of a different mind, thought: I shall now kill all the merchants and take the treasure for myself alone.

At that time in Jambudvīpa there was a great caravan leader named Great-Compassion. Asleep, he dreamed that the sea-spirit said to him: among the merchants there is one thief who means to kill five hundred companions and take the treasure for himself alone; and should the deed be done he will fall into hell. Now, sir, as caravan leader, you should act with expedient means, so that the merchants do not die and the thief does not incur the offense.

The caravan leader reflected for seven days and there was no other expedient: only to kill this violent man. If I tell the merchants, they will certainly be enraged and will kill him together, and all of them will fall into the evil destinies. But if I kill him alone, I shall bear the offense. I would rather endure a hundred thousand kalpas of suffering myself than let the merchants be universally endangered and one thief fall into hell besides. And he first taught him the Dharma, so that his mind was glad and he leapt for joy, and lay down and slept.

The Buddha said: the caravan leader Great-Compassion, as though he were the merchants themselves, gave rise to great compassion and by expedient means killed this one thief; and after his life ended he was born above in the twelfth heaven, the Ābhāsvara. The Great-Compassion of that time is myself; and by this expedient means I passed over a thousand kalpas of birth and death. The five hundred merchants in the same ship are the five hundred buddhas who arise in this Fortunate Kalpa (Source: Sūtra of the Bodhisattva Jñānottara, one fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the locus classicus for killing by skillful means, and its reasoning should be set out exactly as the text gives it. The caravan leader knows from a dream that one merchant means to murder five hundred. He reflects for seven days and finds three possible outcomes: tell the merchants, and they will kill the man themselves and all fall into the evil destinies; do nothing, and five hundred die and the murderer goes to hell; or kill the man himself and take the offense on himself alone. He chooses the third, saying that he would rather bear a hundred thousand kalpas of suffering.

Note the two details that are easy to miss and that carry the weight. First, the deliberation takes seven days and the text says explicitly that there was no other expedient — the killing is a last resort reached by exhaustion, not a shortcut. Second, before killing him he teaches him the Dharma, so that the man's mind is glad and he falls asleep happy; the mental state at death is being managed as carefully as the act itself.

The reckoning at the end is what makes this a scripture of skillful means rather than a tragedy: he is born in the Ābhāsvara heaven, and the act passes him over a thousand kalpas of birth and death. Read alongside entry 13 of this same fascicle, where a killing on a sinking island is reported without any such accounting, the difference is instructive.

15. Five Hundred Merchants Meet a Makara Fish and Escape by Calling on the Buddha

Long ago five hundred merchants took ship to sea and met a makara fish, which put out its head and opened its mouth, meaning to eat living beings. At that time there was little wind, and yet the ship went like an arrow.

The caravan leader said to the company: the ship goes too fast; we may take in the sail. They took it in as he said, and the ship went faster still and could not be stopped.

The caravan leader asked the man in the masthead: what do you see? I see two suns rising above, and a white mountain below, and a black mountain between. The caravan leader said in alarm: this is a great fish — what is to be done? I and you are now in this distress, and going into this fish’s belly there is no hope of living. At this the caravan leader told them: I have a great spirit whose name is Buddha. Let each of you set aside the spirit you serve and call on him with a single mind. Then the five hundred with one voice cried aloud: homage to the Buddha.

The fish heard the Buddha’s name and thought to itself: today then there is a Buddha in the world again. How can I bear to harm living beings? And it closed its mouth at once, and the water all flowed backward and they got away from the fish; and a good mind arose in the five hundred merchants and they were all set free (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra, fascicle 6).

16. A Merchant Is Forced by a Friend to Drink and Breaks the Precept, Is Driven Abroad by His Parents, and Is Still Feared by Ghosts

The longest entry: a man driven from home for a single cup of wine, whose four remaining precepts still frighten three household ghosts out of a house, and who then loses even those and has to be taught them back by a man-eating ghost through the wall of a jar.

Chinese Source

有一縣人。皆奉行五戒十善。縣界無釀酒者。中有大姓家子。欲遠賈販。臨途父母語曰。汝勤持五戒奉行十善。慎莫飲酒犯佛重戒。行到他國見故同學。歡喜歸家出蒲陶酒欲共飲之。辭曰。吾國奉佛戒無敢犯者。且辭親近父母又戒相別久會同心雖喜悅。不宜使吾犯戒違親教也。主人言。吾與卿同師恩則兄弟。吾親則是子親。父母相飲豈可違之。若吾在卿家必從子親。事不獲已乃從飲之。事訖還家具白於親。父母報言。汝違吾教加復犯戒。亂法之漸非孝子也。無得說之。便以所得物逐令出國。子以犯戒為親所驅。乃到他國住客舍家。主人事三鬼神。能作人形。對人飲食與人語言。事之積年居財空盡。而家疾病死喪不絕。私共論之。鬼知人意。鬼共議言。此人財產空訖。正為吾等未曾有益。今相厭患。宜求珍寶以與之。便行盜他國王庫藏好寶積置園中。報言。汝事吾歷年勤苦甚久。今欲福汝使得饒富。主人言。受大神恩。鬼曰。汝園中有金銀可往取之。主人欣然。入園見物負摙歸舍。辭謝受恩。明日欲設飯食。願屈顧下。施設皆辦。鬼神詣門。見舍衛國人在主人舍。便奔走而去。主人追呼。既已顧下。委去何為。神曰。卿舍尊客吾焉得前。重復驚走。主人思惟。吾宅之中無有異人。正有此人耳。即出言語恭設已竟。因問之曰。卿有何功德吾所事神畏子而走。客具說佛功德五戒十善。實犯酒戒為親所逐。尚餘四戒故為天神所護。卿神不敢當之。主人語言。欲奉持五戒。因從客受三自歸五戒。一心精進不敢懈怠。問佛在處可得見不。客曰。在舍衛國給孤獨園。主人一心到彼經歷一亭。中有一女。正是噉人鬼婦。行路逈遠時日逼暮。從女寄宿。女即報言。慎勿留此。宜急前去。男子問曰。用何等故將有意乎。女人報言。吾已語卿用復問為。男子自念。前舍衛國人完佛四戒我神尚為畏之。我已受三歸五戒。心不懈怠。何畏懼乎。遂自留宿。時噉人鬼見護戒威神去亭四十里。一宿不歸。明日進見鬼所噉人骨骸狼藉。心怖而悔退。自思惟。我在本國衣食足用。空為此人所化。言佛在舍衛國。未覩奇妙乃見骸骨縱橫。不如携此女人將歸本土共居如故。即時迴還。因從女人復求留宿。女人謂男子。何復還耶。答曰。行計不成故迴還耳。復寄一宿。女言卿死矣。吾夫是噉人鬼。方來不久。卿急去此。男子不信遂止不去。便迷惑婬意復生。不復信佛三歸五戒。天神即去鬼得來還。女人恐鬼食此男子。藏之甕中。鬼聞人氣謂婦言。爾得肉耶。吾欲噉之。婦言。我不行何從得肉。婦問鬼。卿昨何以不歸。鬼言。坐汝所為而舍宿尊客。令吾被逐。甕中男子踰益恐怖。不復識三自歸。婦言。何以不得肉乎。鬼言。正為汝舍佛弟子。天神逐我出四十里外。露宿震怖于今不安故不得肉。婦因問夫。佛戒云何。鬼言。我大飢極急以肉來不須問此。此是無上正真之戒。非吾所敢說也。婦言。為說之。我當與卿肉。鬼類貪殘食欲食無止。婦迫問之。因便為說三歸五戒。鬼初說一戒時婦輒受之。至第五戒心執口誦。男子於甕中識五戒隨受之。天帝釋知此二人心自歸佛。即遣善神五十人擁護兩人。鬼遂走去。到明日。婦問男子。怖乎。答曰大怖。蒙仁者恩。心悟識佛。婦言男子。昨何以迴還。答曰。吾見新久死人骸骨縱橫恐畏故還耳。婦言。骨是吾所棄者也。吾本良家之女。為鬼所略取吾作妻。悲窮無計。今蒙仁恩。得聞佛戒離於此鬼。又言。賢者欲到何所。男子報言。吾欲到舍衛國見佛。婦曰善哉。吾隨賢者見佛。便俱前行逢四百九十八人。因相問訊。諸賢者從何所來欲到何所。答曰。吾等從佛所來。問言。卿等已得見佛何為復去。報言。佛口說經意中罔罔故尚不解令還本國。兩賢者具說本末。以鬼畏戒高行之人。意乃開解俱還見佛。佛遙見之則笑。口中五色光出。阿難長跪。佛不妄笑將有所說。佛語阿難。汝見是四百九十八人還不。此諸人等今得其本師。來見佛者皆當得道。時五百人來至佛所一心聽經。心開意解皆作沙門。得阿羅漢道。然此二人是四百九十八人前世之師也。人求道要。當得其本師及其善友。爾乃解耳(出戒消災經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a district whose people all upheld the five precepts and the ten wholesome deeds, and within the district’s borders there was no one who brewed liquor. Among them there was a son of a great house who wished to go far away to trade, and as he set out his parents said to him: apply yourself to keeping the five precepts and practise the ten wholesome deeds, and be careful not to drink liquor and break the Buddha’s weighty precept.

Travelling, he came to another country and met an old fellow-student, who was glad and took him home and brought out grape wine, wishing to drink together. He declined: my country serves the Buddha, and none dares transgress the precepts. Besides, I took leave of my parents and they warned me; and though we have been parted long and it is a pleasure to meet with one of like mind, it is not right to make me break the precepts and go against my parents’ instruction.

The host said: you and I had one teacher, and our friendship is that of brothers; my parents are your parents. If your father and mother had you drink, could you go against them? And if I were at your house I should certainly follow your parents. Unable to help it, he then drank with him.

When it was over he went home and told his parents everything. His parents answered: you have gone against my instruction and have broken the precept besides. To disturb the Dharma by degrees is not a filial son. Say nothing of it. And they took what he had brought and drove him out of the country.

Driven out by his parents for breaking the precept, the son came to another country and lodged in an inn. The master of the house served three ghost-spirits who could take human form, eat and drink face to face with people, and talk with them; and he had served them for years and his household goods were used up, and sickness and death in the house did not stop.

They discussed it privately, and the ghosts knew what the man was thinking, and conferred: this man’s property is used up, and it has all been for our sake, and we have never done him any good; and now he is weary of us. We ought to find him treasure and give it to him. And they went and stole fine treasure from another king’s storehouse and piled it in his garden.

They told him: you have served us for years with diligence and long hardship; we now wish to bless you and make you rich. The master said: I have received the great spirits’ kindness. The ghosts said: there is gold and silver in your garden, and you may go and take it. The master went in delight and saw the goods in the garden and carried them home on his back, and thanked them for the kindness received: tomorrow I shall set out food; I ask you to condescend to come down.

Everything was set out, and the ghost-spirits came to the gate; and, seeing the man from the kingdom of Śrāvastī in the master’s house, they ran off. The master pursued them and called: you had already condescended to come down; why abandon us and go? The spirits said: there is an honoured guest in your house and I cannot come forward. And again they ran off in fright.

The master reflected: there is no unusual person in my house; there is only this man. So he brought him out, spoke with him, and, the offering being over, asked him: what merit have you, that the spirits I serve fear you and run away? The guest told him in full of the Buddha’s merit and the five precepts and the ten wholesome deeds: I truly broke the liquor precept and was driven out by my parents; and there are four precepts left to me, and so I am still protected by the gods, and your spirits dare not face them.

The master said: I wish to receive and keep the five precepts. And he received the three refuges and the five precepts from the guest, and applied himself with a single mind and did not dare to be slack. He asked: where is the Buddha? Can he be seen? The guest said: at Anāthapiṇḍada’s park in the kingdom of Śrāvastī.

The master, with a single mind, went there, and passing a wayside station he came to a woman who was in fact the wife of a man-eating ghost. The road was long and remote and the day was drawing to dusk, and he asked the woman for lodging. The woman answered at once: be careful not to stay here; you had better go on quickly. The man asked: for what reason? Have you some intention? The woman answered: I have told you already; why ask further?

The man thought to himself: the man from Śrāvastī kept four of the Buddha’s precepts and my spirits feared him; and I have received the three refuges and the five precepts and my mind is not slack. What have I to fear? And he stayed the night.

Then the man-eating ghost, seeing the majestic power of the guarded precepts, went forty li from the station and did not come home for one night. The next day he went on and saw the bones of the people the ghost had eaten lying scattered, and his heart was frightened and he repented and drew back.

He thought to himself: in my own country I had clothing and food enough, and I have been converted by this man for nothing; he said the Buddha was in Śrāvastī, and I have not seen anything marvellous, and have seen bones lying every which way. Better to take this woman and go back to my own land and live together as before. And he turned back at once, and asked the woman for lodging again.

The woman said to him: why have you come back? He answered: my plan for the journey has not succeeded, and so I have turned back — and he lodged again for one night. The woman said: you are a dead man. My husband is a man-eating ghost and will be back before long; get away from here quickly. The man did not believe her and stayed and would not go; and, deluded, a lustful thought arose in him again and he no longer believed in the Buddha, the three refuges, and the five precepts. The gods then departed, and the ghost was able to come back.

The woman, fearing the ghost would eat this man, hid him in a jar. The ghost smelled a human smell and said to his wife: have you got meat? I wish to eat it. His wife said: I have not been out; where should I get meat? And his wife asked the ghost: why did you not come home yesterday? The ghost said: it was because of what you did in lodging an honoured guest; I was driven out. The man in the jar was the more terrified and no longer remembered the three refuges.

His wife said: why did you not get meat? The ghost said: it was precisely because you lodged a disciple of the Buddha; the gods drove me out forty li and I slept in the open in terror and am not easy even now, and so I got no meat.

His wife then asked her husband: what are the Buddha’s precepts? The ghost said: I am very hungry; bring meat quickly and do not ask about this. These are the unsurpassed, true and real precepts and not what I dare to speak of. His wife said: tell me and I shall give you meat. Ghosts by kind are greedy and cruel and their appetite for food has no stopping, and, pressed by his wife, he then told her the three refuges and the five precepts.

As the ghost spoke the first precept his wife received it, and at the fifth she held it in her mind and recited it with her mouth; and the man in the jar, recognizing the five precepts, received them along with her. Śakra, lord of the gods, knew that these two persons had turned to the Buddha in their hearts, and sent fifty good spirits to guard the two of them, and the ghost ran off.

When day came the woman asked the man: were you afraid? He answered: greatly afraid. By your kindness my mind has awoken and I know the Buddha. The woman said: why did you turn back yesterday? He answered: I saw the bones of the newly and long dead lying every which way and turned back in fear.

The woman said: the bones are what I have thrown away. I was originally a daughter of a good family, and was carried off by the ghost, who took me for a wife, and in my grief there was nothing to be done. Now, by your kindness, I have heard the Buddha’s precepts and am free of this ghost. And she said: worthy sir, where do you wish to go? The man answered: I wish to go to the kingdom of Śrāvastī and see the Buddha. The woman said: excellent — I shall follow you and see the Buddha.

So they went on together and met four hundred and ninety-eight people, and greeted one another: worthy sirs, where have you come from and where do you wish to go? They answered: we have come from the Buddha. They asked: you have already seen the Buddha; why go away again? They answered: what the Buddha spoke of the sūtras was vague in our minds, and so, not understanding it, we are going back to our own country.

The two worthy ones told them the whole matter from the beginning, and how the ghost feared the precepts and one of lofty conduct; and their minds then opened in understanding, and they all went back together to see the Buddha.

The Buddha saw them from afar and smiled, and light of five colors came from his mouth. Ānanda knelt at length: the Buddha does not smile idly; there must be something to be said. The Buddha told Ānanda: do you see these four hundred and ninety-eight coming back? These people have now got their original teacher, and those who come to see the Buddha will all attain the path.

Then the five hundred came to where the Buddha was and listened to the sūtra with one mind, and their minds opened and their thoughts were released, and they all became śramaṇas and attained the path of an arhat. And these two were the teachers of those four hundred and ninety-eight in a former life. Whoever seeks the path must get their own original teacher and good friends, and only then do they understand (Source: Sūtra on the Precepts Dispelling Calamity).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The longest entry in the fascicle and the most carefully constructed. Its argument is about margins. A man breaks one precept out of five, under pressure from a friend using an argument about hospitality he cannot answer; his parents drive him out; and the four precepts he has left are still enough that three resident ghosts flee a house rather than face him. That is the first half.

The second half takes the same margin away. Having received the full five precepts, the convert stays a night at a station where the man-eating ghost is driven forty li off; and then, discouraged by a heap of bones and the failure to see anything marvellous, he turns back, and his mind fills with lust, and the text says plainly what follows: he no longer believed, the gods departed, and the ghost was able to come home. He is then hidden in a jar, too frightened to remember the three refuges, and has to hear the precepts recited back to him by the ghost — who says of them, before being bribed into speaking, that they are the unsurpassed true precepts and not what he dares to speak of.

Two things in the ending deserve notice. The woman is not rescued; she gets herself free by making her hungry husband trade the precepts for meat, and she receives each one as he speaks it. And the four hundred and ninety-eight monks going home in confusion are turned around not by the Buddha but by these two, whom the Buddha then identifies as their teachers in a former life. This closes fascicle 43.
