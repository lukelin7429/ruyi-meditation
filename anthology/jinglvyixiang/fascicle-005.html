The Buddha's Responsive Body, Benefiting Beings

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 5 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "The Buddha's Responsive Body Benefiting Beings, Part 2" (應身益物部第二) — eighteen stories of the Buddha meeting beings exactly where they are, including the famous episode of eating horse fodder for three months, taming the drunk elephant Nālāgiri with five conjured lions, saving five hundred condemned bandits, and walking on water to teach the power of simple faith.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 5 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 5 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 5 returns to the short, self-contained narrative form of fascicle 2 — eighteen stories under the theme of the Buddha's "responsive body," his willingness to meet every kind of being, from kings to bandits to a grieving courtesan, exactly where their own karma has placed them. One story (item 16) uses graphic imagery of bodily decay as a traditional teaching device against lust; it is translated in full, as in the source, since softening it would misrepresent a well-established genre of canonical teaching.

1–2. The Buddha's Voice and Its Secrets

How a single teaching, spoken with a single voice, reaches every listener in a form suited exactly to that listener alone.

Chinese Source
佛胸萬字放光發音

如來遊於妙樂世界。欲現智慧廣度無極。與弟子菩薩梵釋天龍。集于精舍。放萬字光音。清淨遠徹。五法大音。一曰度人大乘之音。二曰度人無彼此之音。三曰度人無緣覺之音。四曰度人不斷情想善權之音。五曰度人逮入生死解其勞塵說法之音。國王名處流。與諸人民。詣佛禮敬曰。久聞如來。宣道訓化。濟脫生死。而無有限。吾等眷屬流於生死。未蒙解脫。願尊垂化令解明法。天地震動修菩薩行(出現佛胸萬字經)。

Ru-Yi Translation
The Wan-Character's Light and Sound

The Tathāgata, journeying through the World of Wondrous Joy, wishing to manifest his wisdom and deliver beings without limit, gathered with his disciples, bodhisattvas, Brahmā, Śakra, and nāgas at a monastery, and emitted from the "萬"-character mark on his chest a light and sound, pure and far-reaching, in five great tones — the tone that delivers through the Great Vehicle, beyond self and other, beyond the pratyekabuddha's path, through skillful means that do not cut off feeling, and into birth and death itself to dissolve its weary dust through teaching. A king named Chuliu, with his people, came before the Buddha and said: "We have long heard the Tathāgata delivers beings from birth and death without limit — our own household still drifts there, unfreed. We beg the Honored One's compassion, that we might understand the clear Dharma." Heaven and earth shook, and they took up the bodhisattva path (Source: Sūtra on the Manifestation of the Wan-Character on the Buddha's Chest).

三種密

何謂心密。四行清淨不失神通建立大哀無極之業。以神通變現一切普顯。以成諦道智慧之室。覩一切法。是則正通普御一切。其真法者慧神慧通皆顯眾像。解暢諸色。解暢一切諸佛道法。開化一切十方眾生。使入法律。至阿惟顏。轉一切法。是為菩薩心密之業(出密迹金剛力士經第二)菩薩住是金剛三昧。以一音聲有所宣說。一切眾生各隨種類而得解了。示現一色。一切眾生各各皆見種種色相安住一處。身不移易。能令眾生隨其方面各各而見。宣說一法。若界若入。一切眾生各隨本解而得聞之(出大涅槃經第二十二卷)佛以一音演說法。眾生隨類各得解。皆謂世尊同其語。眾生各各隨所解。普得受行獲其利。或有恐畏。或歡喜。或生厭離。或斷疑。斯則神力不共法(出維摩經第一卷)一切法相。行無取著。建勝寶幢。出一大音。若樂聞施惠得解脫者。即聞如來說施利益。戒慧等樂亦復如是。說大乘法無一眾生不解脫者(出觀佛三昧經第三卷)。佛以一言說一切法。大千眾生以無量音一時問難。皆各不同。於一念中。以一音答。皆令開解(出華嚴經第二十六卷首楞嚴經明身蜜悲花第七卷十分別經普明口蜜大同)。

The Three Kinds of Secrets

What is meant by the secret of mind? The pure conduct of the four practices, never losing supernormal power, establishing the boundless work of great compassion — this is the true, universal mastery of all dharmas, opening and transforming beings of the ten directions, bringing them into the Dharma's own discipline (Source: Sūtra of the Guhyaka Vajra-Warrior, fascicle 2). A bodhisattva abiding in vajra samādhi, speaking with a single voice, is understood by every being each according to its own kind; manifesting a single form, each being sees a different form, though he remains unmoved in the one place (Source: Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 22). The Buddha expounds the Dharma with a single voice, and every being understands it as its own language, each gaining benefit according to its own capacity — some fear, some joy, some disenchantment, some the end of doubt — this is one of the powers unique to a buddha (Source: Vimalakīrti Sūtra, fascicle 1). With a single word he expounds every dharma; the beings of the great-thousandfold world ask their questions at once, each different, yet in a single instant, with a single voice, he answers them all, and each understands (Source: Avataṃsaka Sūtra, fascicle 26; several related sūtras largely agree).

3–4. Three Months Eating Horse Fodder

One of the most famous episodes of the Buddha's life: an invitation accepted in good faith, a host distracted by Māra into utter neglect, and a season of real hunger endured without complaint — followed by the karmic story explaining why it happened at all.

Chinese Source
受阿耆達請三月食馬麥

隨羅然國(或云毘羅然)有婆羅門王。名阿耆達。聰明多智。往詣阿難邠坻廣共論議言訖。問須達多言。此土有神人可宗者不。答曰有。悉達太子出家成佛。相好殊特天人所尊。阿耆達即命駕。往詣祇洹見佛風神。心敬內發。即起白佛。願佛及僧。顧我三月夏坐。佛言。我此眾多。而汝異見異信。王言。不以為多。如是至三。佛與五百比丘一時受請。往至其國。城邑隘陋民窮少信。乞食難得。先無精舍。城北有林。枝葉欝茂。其地平博。與眾頓止。勅諸比丘。汝等當知。此邑窮隘人多不信乞食難得。若欲於此。安居者住。不者隨意。時舍利弗獨往阿牟迦末迦山。受天帝釋及阿修羅女請。天食供養。時有天魔。迷惑王心。使還宮內耽荒五欲。一者寶飾。二者女樂。三者衣食。四者榮利。五者色欲。還入後宮。勅守門者。三月之內不問尊卑。外事大小悉不得白。迷忘供養又無恒命供養。滿六日便止。諸比丘乞食極苦難得。

Ru-Yi Translation
Receiving Agnidatta's Invitation

In the country of Vairañjā, there was a brahmin king named Agnidatta, clever and learned, who, after debating at length with Anāthapiṇḍada, asked Sudatta whether this land had any divine sage worthy of reverence. Told of Prince Siddhārtha's own buddhahood, Agnidatta went at once to Jetavana, and, moved to reverence by the Buddha's bearing, begged him and the sangha to accept his invitation for the three-month rains retreat. The Buddha warned that his assembly was large while the king's own faith differed from theirs, but accepted after being asked three times, and traveled with five hundred monks to that country. But the towns there were poor and largely faithless, alms hard to come by, and no monastery had been prepared — only a grove north of the city where the assembly settled, the Buddha telling the monks that any who wished to stay for the retreat might do so, and any who did not could leave as they pleased. Śāriputra went off separately to accept a heavenly invitation elsewhere. Meanwhile a heavenly Māra beguiled the king's mind, drawing him into his own inner chambers to indulge the five desires, and ordering his gatekeepers that for the full three months no matter, great or small, should be reported to him — so that even the small support he had begun stopped after only six days, and the monks found alms extremely hard to come by.

時大目連白佛。有樹名欝閻浮。我欲取其果供養大眾。有訶梨勒林。阿摩勒林。欝單曰有自然粳米。忉利天食修陀味。普皆欲取以供大眾。有甘地味。我以一手。擎諸眾生。一手反地。令諸比丘自取而噉。願見聽許。佛言。汝自有大神力。諸比丘惡行報熟不可移轉。一皆不聽。是國有清水美草。有波羅國人。逐水草牧馬。欲令肥丁來到此處。馬士信佛心淨。告諸比丘言。我等知僧飢極而食皆盡。正有馬麥。君能噉不。諸比丘白佛。佛言。馬屬看馬人能以好草鹽水食馬。此麥自在應受。馬有五百匹。一馬日食二升。各分半以給比丘(四分律云馬食一斗分五升給僧)有一良馬。日食四斗。分半奉佛(四分律云二斗分一升)。

Maudgalyāyana offered to gather fruit from magical trees, groves, and even the food of Uttarakuru and the gods of Trāyastriṃśa to feed the assembly by his own supernormal power, but the Buddha refused, explaining that the monks' own ripening karma could not be so displaced. In that country, horse-keepers pasturing their animals on the local water and grass, moved by sincere faith, told the monks that they had only horse fodder to offer, since the sangha's food had run out entirely — and asked if they could eat it. The Buddha permitted this, since the horses themselves could be fed on grass and salted water instead. Of the five hundred horses, each eating two liters of grain daily, half of every portion was given to the monks; and of the four dou eaten daily by one especially fine horse, half again was offered to the Buddha himself.

阿難取佛分并自分。持入聚落。於一女人前。讚佛功德。有小因緣。在此安居。汝能為作乾飯不。女言。我家多事。不能得作。傍有一女聞。謂阿難言。持麥來我為作飯。更有濡善智慧持戒比丘。我亦為作。女即作飯與阿難。阿難敬佛情深如是思惟。佛為王種。常食餚饍。此飯麁惡不能益身。行水授飯。見佛食之。悲哽交懷。佛知其意。欲解釋之。汝能噉不。阿難言。能受而食之。滋味非常。實是諸天以味加之。欣悅無量悲哽即除。具陳二女。佛言。前女若作飯時。應為轉輪王第一夫人。不倩而作者。此福無量。時諸國豪貴居士。大富薩薄等。聞佛三月食馬麥。備眾供具種種餚饍。車馬盈道。而來奉餉。世尊自恣垂至餘七日。告阿難。汝行入城。告阿耆達云。安居竟。復餘國遊行。阿難與一比丘。俱到王所。具陳佛語。王猶未悟。乃問。佛今何在。阿難言。受王三月請始竟今故在國。王又問阿難。誰供給。阿難言。窮苦理極。佛與眾僧三月食馬麥。王始自覺悟。如何令佛及僧。三月食馬麥。惡聲醜名流布諸國。憂愧愁惱。與諸宗親。共往詣佛。深自懺悔。更請留佛。

Ānanda carried the Buddha's own portion along with his own into the village, and asked a woman there to make dry rations from it, praising the Buddha's virtue; she declined, being too occupied, but another woman nearby offered to cook it herself for the monks. When Ānanda served this coarse food to the Buddha, his heart was choked with grief that a prince once raised on fine delicacies should now eat so poorly — but the Buddha, sensing this, asked him to taste it himself, and Ānanda found its flavor extraordinary, secretly enhanced by the devas, and his grief lifted at once. Told of the two women, the Buddha said that the first, had she made the food, would have earned rebirth as a wheel-turning king's chief consort — but the second, who acted though unasked, earned merit beyond measure. Word of the Buddha's plight eventually reached wealthy nobles in other kingdoms, who came bearing lavish offerings; but as the retreat neared its end, the Buddha sent Ānanda to inform Agnidatta that he intended to travel on. Only then, questioning Ānanda, did the king finally realize the Buddha and sangha had eaten nothing but horse fodder for three months under his own roof — appalled at the scandal this would bring upon his name throughout every land, he came with his whole clan to confess and beg the Buddha to remain longer.

佛受七日辦種種食。劫貝四張。革屣一量奉佛。劫貝二張。革屣一量施僧(善見毘婆沙云集其兒孫語云我先請佛三月安居不得一日供今以三月供限并設明日以三衣施佛三衣施僧白疊各一雙又施絳欽婆羅各一張又施鉢兜那波吒梁言絹一藥膏各滿一器)王取其供。遍散道中。欲令蹈過。佛言。食糧應噉。敬不宜足蹈佛乃為受。皆悉呪願。王心悅結解。逮法眼淨(出中本起經下十誦彌沙塞律略同)。

與五百僧食馬麥緣

過去久遠世時。佛名比婆葉。在槃頭摩跋城。王名槃頭。有婆羅門。名因提耆利。博達四韋。及諸算術。及婆羅門戒。教五百童子。王設會請佛供饌精美。眾有一比丘。名曰彌勒。病不能行食。竟為病人請食。梵志不與罵曰。髠頭沙門正應食馬麥。不應食如是甘美之供。時諸弟子曰。實爾。舍利弗。時婆羅門者我身是也。五百童子。今五百羅漢。是病比丘今彌勒是(出興起行經下)。

The Buddha accepted seven more days, during which the king lavished offerings on the whole sangha, then scattered his gifts along the road hoping the Buddha would walk over them — but the Buddha explained that food is for eating, not for trampling out of misplaced reverence, and had it properly presented instead. The king's own heart, its knot loosened, attained the pure Dharma-eye.

The Karmic Cause of the Horse Fodder

In a distant past age, under the Buddha Vipaśyin, a brahmin named Indraśiri, teaching five hundred students, was invited along with the Buddha to a royal feast of fine food. When a monk, too ill to collect his own meal, sent another to request food on his behalf, the brahmin refused and cursed: "Shaven-headed monks ought to eat horse fodder, not such fine fare." Śāriputra told the assembled disciples: "That brahmin was myself in that life; the five hundred students are now the five hundred arhats; and the sick monk is now Maitreya" (Source: Zhong Ben Qi Sūtra and Sūtra of the Origin of Actions; the Sarvāstivāda and Mahīśāsaka Vinayas largely agree).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium pairs these two entries deliberately: the horse-fodder episode is not presented as an arbitrary hardship but as karma completing itself across lifetimes — a buddha's own past unkind words to a hungry monk, ripening, kalpas later, into three months of genuine hunger for himself and his entire sangha. Even full awakening does not exempt one from the consequences of past action; it only guarantees the equanimity with which those consequences are met, and — as the two women who cooked the coarse meal illustrate — that an unrequested kindness offered in real need outweighs a grander gift offered on request.

5–6. Retribution and Refusal

The Buddha himself bears an iron spear as the last trace of an ancient karmic debt, and four brahmins who refuse to hear the Dharma at all attain the path anyway — through a single sentence each, heard against their will.

Chinese Source
現鐵槍報

舍衛城中有二十人。復與二十人共為怨敵。時四十人各欲相害。伺覓方便。承佛威神尋詣佛所。佛化四十人。當有鐵槍(或云佉達羅刺)自然來出入佛右足大指。言未竟槍在佛前。目連白佛。今拔鐵槍著異世界。佛言。以精進力欲拔鐵槍者。三千大千世界為大震動。不能搖槍如毛髮許。佛往梵天。槍輒隨之。還舍衛城。槍亦在前。如來取槍以足蹈上。目連白佛。如來何罪而獲槍殃。佛曰。昔五百賈人一懷惡心。吾即害之。是其餘殃。四十人聞是。自相謂言。法王尚爾。況於吾等。當不受罪乎。悔過自首。入平等慧(出慧上菩薩經下卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation
The Iron-Spear Retribution

In Śrāvastī, twenty men and another twenty had become bitter enemies, each side seeking to harm the other, until, by the Buddha's own power, all forty found themselves before him instead. The Buddha then caused an iron spear to appear of itself, piercing his own right big toe. Maudgalyāyana offered to remove it and cast it into another world, but the Buddha said not even the utmost power could move it a hair's breadth — wherever he went, even to the Brahmā heaven, the spear followed, until he simply stepped upon it himself. Asked what offense could bring such retribution upon a buddha, he explained: "Long ago, among five hundred merchants, one harbored murderous intent, and I killed him — this is what remains of that act." Hearing this, the forty men said to one another, "If even the Dharma-King must bear this, how much more must we?" — and, confessing their own offenses, entered the wisdom of impartiality (Source: Sūtra of the Bodhisattva Wisdom-Superior, latter fascicle).

化四梵志掩耳不受

昔有婆羅門四人。皆得神通。身能飛行神足無礙。此四梵志自相謂言。其有人民。以餚饍食施瞿曇沙門者。便得生天不離福堂。有聞法者入解脫門。我等今日意貪天福。不願解脫不須聞法。是時四人各執四瓶甘美石蜜。一人先至奉上世尊。佛告梵志。說所行非常。梵志聞即掩耳。次第二人復說。謂法興衰。梵志聞亦掩耳。次第三人復說。夫生輒死。梵志聞亦手掩耳。次第四人復說。此滅為樂。梵志聞亦掩耳。各捨之去。自相謂言。瞿曇沙門有何言教。前者對曰。我聞一句所行非常。次第二三四。復自陳說。與說此偈已。心開意解得阿那含道。爾時四人。自知各得道證。還自懇責。至如來所。頭面禮足。在一面立。白世尊曰。唯願如來。聽在道次得為沙門。世尊告曰。善來比丘。快修梵行。爾時四人。頭鬚自墮。身所著衣變為袈裟。尋於佛前得羅漢道(出無常經)。

Four Brahmins Who Cover Their Ears

Four brahmins of supernormal power, wishing heavenly merit rather than liberation, agreed among themselves to give the Buddha offerings of honey-candy while refusing to hear any of his teaching. As each in turn approached, the Buddha spoke a single phrase — "all conditioned things are impermanent," "all dharmas rise and fall," "whatever is born must die," "this very cessation is bliss" — and each brahmin, on hearing his own single phrase, covered his ears at once and left. Only afterward, comparing what each had heard, did they realize the four phrases formed a single verse — and their hearts opened at once, attaining the fruit of non-returning. Returning in self-reproach to the Buddha, they asked to become monks in order upon the path; the Buddha said, "Welcome, monks," and at once their hair fell away, their garments became monastic robes, and they attained arhatship then and there (Source: Sūtra on Impermanence).

7–8. Converting a King and a City

A brahmin's refusal of ever-greater gifts of treasure teaches a generous but misguided king the limits of wealth; and a whole city's weapons, raised against the Buddha at a rival teacher's urging, bloom into flowers instead.

化作梵志度多味象王

The King of Many-Flavored Elephant

The king of a brahmin country named Many-Flavored Elephant, moved by a sudden good impulse, piled treasure like a mountain and let any beggar take a single handful. The Buddha, transformed into a brahmin, tested him by taking one handful, then three, then seven — each time walking seven steps and setting it back down, explaining each time that even this much would not suffice for a house, then a wife, then children yet to be married off. When the king finally offered the entire pile, the brahmin took it all — and set it down again, walking away. Astonished, the king asked why, and was told: "A person's life is brief, and all things are impermanent; a mountain of treasure brings no true benefit to oneself, and greedy scheming is only empty toil — better to seek instead the unconditioned path." The king's mind opened at this, and the brahmin then revealed the Buddha's own radiant form, rising into the air to speak a verse:

"Though one should pile up treasure high as heaven itself,
filling the whole world with it, this is still not equal to a single glimpse of the path.
What is not good may resemble the good; what is love may resemble its absence;
what is suffering may take on the appearance of joy — and only a madman is content with such semblance."

On seeing the Buddha's light and hearing this verse, the king and his ministers at once received the five precepts and attained the fruit of stream-entry (Source: Dharmapada with Similes, fascicle 1).

Elder Ruzhi and the Weapons Turned to Flowers

In a great South Indian city named Śubhapura lived an elder, Ruzhi, who had planted deep roots of goodness under countless past buddhas — though the whole city served the heterodox Nirgrantha ascetics instead. Wishing to deliver this elder, the Buddha journeyed toward the city; the Nirgranthas, fearing the loss of their own support, warned the people that the approaching śramaṇa Gautama brought famine and death wherever he went, and told them to cut down every tree, foul every spring and well, and shut the gates armed against him. The people obeyed — but when the Buddha arrived and saw what had been done, compassion arose in him, and every felled tree grew back at once, every fouled spring ran clear again covered in flowers, the city walls themselves turned to beryl so the people within could see clearly through them, the gates opened of themselves, and every weapon raised against him transformed into flowers. Elder Ruzhi, foremost among them, led the whole people out to the Buddha, who then expounded the Dharma for them, and every one of them awakened the aspiration for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening (Source: Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 14).

Ru-Yi Translation
The Five Weary Monks

Five monks studying the path on a mountain far from town wore themselves out each day walking down to beg for food and back, leaving no strength for real meditation, year after year without progress. The Buddha, transformed into a fellow ascetic, told them: "One who practices the path takes the precepts as root and the gathering of mind as practice, valuing what is true over mere form — even sacrificing the body for food that only sustains it can never free one from suffering." He asked them not to go down the next day, promising to provide for them instead — and, freed from their usual exhaustion for once, their minds grew calm; the next day he brought them food, spoke a verse, and revealed the radiant form of the Buddha himself. The five monks, deeply moved, reflected on the precepts and attained arhatship at once (Source: Dharmapada with Similes, fascicle 3).

The Stingy Couple

A stingy, faithless couple in Śrāvastī turned away a mendicant monk with abuse; the monk, actually the Buddha in disguise, manifested the appearance of dying on the spot, his body rotting before her eyes, and the terrified wife fled screaming. Her enraged husband pursued the vanished monk with bow and sword, only to find him transformed into a small crystal city he could not breach. Told the gate would open once he cast aside his weapons, he did so — but the gate stayed shut, until the monk explained he meant the weapon of malice in the man's own heart, not merely the ones in his hand. Struck with fear at this insight into his own mind, the man confessed, fetched his wife, and both prostrated themselves, asking by what virtue the monk had achieved such power. He answered simply: "Through wide learning, unfailing practice of the Dharma, diligence, and keeping the precepts without ever growing heedless — through this alone I attained the path and reached nirvāṇa of myself" (Source: Dharmapada Sūtra, fascicle 3).

The Butcher and the Five Hundred Brahmins

Five hundred brahmins, plotting to slander the Buddha, arranged for a butcher to invite him to a meal, planning to mock him whether he praised or condemned a man who killed for his living. The Buddha simply told the butcher, "When the fruit ripens it falls of itself; when merit ripens it delivers of itself," and, at the meal, extended his tongue to touch his own ears, emitting a great light, and spoke a verse instead:

"As the true sage teaches, one who lives by the path may still be envied by fools,
who, seeing it, do evil in turn — but evil done brings evil, as bitter seed brings bitter fruit;
evil receives its own punishment, good its own reward, each ripening in its own time,
one never substituting for the other — just as cultivating good brings good, as sweet seed brings sweet fruit."

The five hundred brahmins' minds opened at once, and they prostrated themselves, begging to become monks; the Buddha accepted them all, and the whole village, witnessing this transformation, attained the fruits of the path — the village itself becoming known as Worthy Hamlet, no longer named for its butcher (Source: Dharmapada with Similes, fascicle 1).

舍衛東南有大江水。既深而廣。五百餘家居在岸邊。未聞道德度世之行。習於剛強欺誑為務。貪利自縱快心極意。佛知此家福應當度。往至水邊。坐一樹下。村人見佛光明奇異。莫不驚肅皆往禮敬。或拜或揖問訊起居。佛命令坐為說經法。眾人聞之心猶不信。佛化一人。從江南來。足行水上。正沒其踝。來至佛前稽首禮佛。眾人見之莫不驚怪。問化人曰。吾等先人已來居此江邊。未曾聞人行水上者。卿是何人。有何道術履水不沒。化人答曰。吾是江南愚直之人。聞佛在此。貪樂道德。至南岸邊。不時得度。問彼岸人。水為深淺。彼人見語。水可齊踝。吾信其言。便爾來過。無他異術。佛讚言善哉。夫執信誠可度生死之淵。數里之江何足為奇。村人聞已。心開信堅。皆受五戒。為清信士(出法句經第三)。

Walking on the Great River

A faithless village along a deep, wide river southeast of Śrāvastī remained unmoved even after the Buddha himself came to teach them, until he conjured a man who walked across the water from the far bank, sinking only to his ankles. Asked how he managed such a feat, the conjured man explained: "I am simply a plain, honest man. Hearing the Buddha was here, I asked someone on the far shore how deep the water was, and was told it only reached the ankle — I believed him, and so I crossed. I have no special art at all." The Buddha praised this, saying, "It is precisely such simple faith that can carry one across the very abyss of birth and death — what wonder, then, is a river only a few li wide?" The villagers' hearts opened at once, and they all received the five precepts (Source: Dharmapada Sūtra, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The river story is the sharpest of the four: the villagers do not believe the Buddha's own teaching, yet believe at once a stranger's account of crossing on nothing more than someone else's casual word about ankle-deep water — the Buddha's point is not that faith should be blind, but that the very faith they already extend so freely to each other in ordinary life is, redirected, sufficient to cross something far larger. He does not ask them for a different kind of trust, only the same trust turned toward a worthier object.

The Five Hundred Bandits Who Go Forth

War between Śrāvastī and Vaiśālī led the king of Śrāvastī to order the capture of enemy raiders; a group of Śrāvastī monks, having lost their way after their retreat, were mistakenly swept up among an actual band of bandits when soldiers arrived, and one monk, despite explaining the truth, was accused by the king of lying because the bandits themselves called him their companion. Only after the king decided to deal with the true bandits — five hundred in number — did the misunderstanding resolve; but as the condemned men were led to execution, their wailing reached the Buddha, who sent Ānanda to ask the king how he could bear to kill five hundred at once. The king, moved, agreed to spare them on the sole condition that the Buddha ensure they never returned to banditry — a condition the Buddha accepted at once. Freed and brought before him still bound, the bandits found their own ropes loosening the moment they saw him; hearing him teach the four noble truths, they attained stream-entry on the spot, and, begging to go forth rather than risk such suffering again, were accepted as monks — their prisoners' garments transforming instantly into monastic robes, their bearing as dignified as monks of a hundred years' standing, and all of them soon attaining arhatship (Source: Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 19).

Sight Restored to Five Hundred Blinded Bandits

Five hundred bandits in Kosala, captured for their violence by King Prasenajit, had their eyes gouged out and were driven into a dark forest. Having planted deep roots of virtue under past buddhas, they cried out in their suffering, "Homage to the Buddha!" — and the Buddha, hearing this from Jetavana, felt compassion for them at once. A cool wind arose, carrying fragrant medicinal herbs from the Fragrant Mountains into their empty sockets, and their sight was restored exactly as before. Opening their eyes, they saw the Tathāgata himself standing before them, and, hearing him teach, awakened the aspiration for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening (Source: Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 14; the Sūtra of the Buddha's Repayment of Kindness through Great Skillful Means largely agrees).

Restoring the Grieving Parents' Sanity

A brahmin couple in Vaiśālī, having prayed to every mountain and tree-spirit for a son to prevent their wealth reverting to the state at their death, at last had a beloved boy — who, at twelve, was trampled to death by a drunken elephant. His parents, driven mad with grief, ran about naked and raving, until the Tathāgata, moved by compassion, transformed himself into their very son; embracing him with boundless joy, their madness vanished at once, their sanity restored, and, as the Buddha then taught them the Dharma, they awakened the resolve for the path (Source: Sūtra of the Buddha's Repayment of Kindness through Great Skillful Means, fascicle 4).

化婬女令生厭苦

佛告阿難。我昔夏安居時。波羅㮈國。有一婬女。名曰妙意。於佛有緣。佛與難陀。將往婬女舍。日日乞食。此女於我不曾恭敬。但於難陀偏生愛著。已經七日。女心念言。沙門瞿曇。若能遣難陀阿難。從我所願。我當種種供養。佛告阿難難陀。汝從今日莫往彼村。世尊獨至女樓。一日至三日。放金色光。化諸天人。此女不悟。後日世尊。復將阿難難陀。在樹下行。婬女愛敬二比丘故。遙以眾華散佛及二比丘。阿難告言。汝可禮佛。女愛阿難。應時作禮。佛化作三童子。年皆十五。面貌端正。女見歡喜。為化年少。投地敬禮。白年少言。丈夫我今此舍如功德天。富力自在。眾寶莊嚴。我今以身及以奴婢。奉上丈夫。可備灑掃。若能顧納。隨我所願。一切供給無所愛惜。化人坐床。未及食頃。女前親近言。願遂我意。化人不違。一日一夜心不疲厭。至二日時愛心漸息。至三日時。白言。丈夫可起飲食。化人即起。纏綿不已。女生厭悔。白言。丈夫異人乃爾。化人告言。我先世法。凡與女通。經十二日爾乃休息。女聞此語。如人食噎。既不得吐。又不得咽。身體苦痛。如被杵擣。至四日時。如被車轢。至五日時。如鐵丸入體。至六日時。支節悉痛。如箭入心。女念言。我聞淨飯王子。救濟苦人恒在。今日何故不來救我。作是念已。懊惱自責。我從今日乃至壽終。不貪色欲。寧與虎狼師子惡狩。同處一室。不受此苦。作是語已。復起飲食。行坐共俱無奈之何。化人亦瞋唾云。弊惡女。廢我事業。我今共汝。合體一處。不如早死。父母宗親。若來覓我。於何自藏。我寧自縊死。不堪受恥。女言。弊物我不用爾。欲死隨意。化人取刀刺頸。血污女身。萎陀在地。女不能勝。亦不得免死。經二日青瘀臭黑。三日膖脹。四日爛潰。大小便利。及諸惡蟲。迸血諸膿。塗漫女身。女極惡厭而不得離。至五日時。皮肉漸爛。至六日時。肉落都盡。至七日時。唯有臭骨。如膠如漆。粘著女身。女發誓願。若諸天神。及與仙人。淨飯王子。能免我苦。我持此舍。一切珍寶。以用給施。作是念時。佛將阿難難陀帝釋在前。擎寶香爐。燒無價香。梵王在後。擎大寶蓋。無量諸天伎樂。佛放常光照耀天地。一切大眾皆見如來。詣此女樓。時女見佛。心懷慚愧。藏骨無處。取諸白疊無量眾香。裹其臭骨。臭勢如故。不可覆藏。女見世尊。即為作禮。以慚愧故。身映骨上。臭骨忽然在女背上。女流淚而言。如來功德慈悲無量。若能令我離此苦者。願為弟子心終不退。佛神力故。臭骨不現。女大歡喜。為佛作禮。白佛言。世尊。我今所珍一切施佛。佛為呪願梵音流暢。女聞歡喜。應時即得須陀洹道(出觀佛三昧經第七卷)。

The Courtesan and the Decaying Body

The Buddha told Ānanda: "Once, during a rains retreat in Vārāṇasī, a courtesan named Sundarānandā grew attached not to me but to Nanda, on our daily alms rounds together, and hoped I might send him to fulfill her wishes. When she finally bowed to me out of her fondness for Nanda and for Ānanda, I conjured three handsome fifteen-year-old youths; delighted, she offered herself and her whole household to one of them, and he did not refuse her. For a full day and night he showed no weariness, but by the second day her own ardor cooled, and by the third she asked him to rise and eat — yet he clung to her all the more, explaining that, by his own custom, once joined with a woman he did not stop for twelve full days. Hearing this, she felt as though choking, her body aching worse each day — crushed, then pierced, then burning — until she vowed never again to crave such desire, preferring even a room shared with a wild beast to this suffering."

"Still he would not release her, and, feigning rage at his own ruined life, stabbed his own throat before her, his blood staining her body as he collapsed — and she could not free herself even from his corpse. Over seven days it blackened, swelled, burst, and rotted around her, crawling with vermin, until only a foul, sticky skeleton remained, clinging to her still. In her extremity she vowed that whoever could free her from this suffering — deva, sage, or the prince of King Śuddhodana — should have her entire house and its treasures. At that very moment I arrived with Ānanda, Nanda, Śakra, and Brahmā in a great procession of light. Seeing me, she tried in shame to hide the bones, wrapping them in cloth and incense that could not mask their stench — and as she bowed before me, the foul skeleton, out of her very shame, seemed to merge onto her own back. Weeping, she begged to be freed and vowed never to turn back as my disciple — and by my own power the skeleton vanished at once. Overjoyed, she offered me everything she owned, and, as I chanted a blessing, attained the fruit of stream-entry in that very moment" (Source: Sūtra on the Samādhi of Buddha-Contemplation, fascicle 7).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a traditional asubha-bhāvanā ("contemplation of the foul") teaching, a well-established genre within the canon that uses graphic imagery of bodily decay to loosen the grip of sensual attachment — not gratuitous horror, but the same logic behind the charnel-ground meditations still taught in some contemplative traditions today. The story's structure is worth noting: the woman's suffering is never punishment for her profession, but the natural consequence of clinging itself, played out to its logical end through an illusion the Buddha creates precisely because ordinary teaching had already failed to reach her.

17–18. Taming with a Gesture

Two brief, iconic displays of the Buddha's power exercised entirely through loving-kindness rather than force: five conjured lions that subdue a murderous elephant without harming it, and a boulder tossed, pulverized, and restored to quiet the pride of five hundred strongmen.

Chinese Source
現五指為五師子

善男子。我入王舍大城。次第乞食。提婆達多。教阿闍世王。即放護財狂醉之象。欲令害我及諸弟子。我於爾時即入慈定。舒手示之。即於五指出五師子。是象見已其心怖畏。失大小便。舉身投地。敬禮我足。善男子。我時手指實無師子。乃是修慈悲善根力故。令彼調伏(出大涅槃經第十四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation
Five Fingers, Five Lions

"Good son, when I entered Rājagṛha to beg for food, Devadatta had King Ajātaśatru release the elephant Dhanapālaka, wild with drink, hoping it would kill me and my disciples. I entered the absorption of loving-kindness and extended my hand toward it, and from my five fingers there issued five lions. The elephant, terrified, lost control of its bowels and threw itself to the ground, bowing at my feet. Good son, there were, in truth, no lions on my fingers at all — it was solely the power of the root of goodness cultivated in loving-kindness that subdued the beast" (Source: Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 14).

以足指散巨石

復次善男子。我欲涅槃。始初發足。向拘尸城。有五百力士。於其中路。平治掃灑。中有一石。眾欲舉移。盡力不能。我時憐愍。即起慈心。彼諸力士尋即見。我以足拇指。舉此大石。擲置虛空。還以手接。安置右掌。吹令碎沫。復還合之。令彼力士貢高心息。即為略說種種法要。令其俱發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心(出涅槃經第十四卷)。

Scattering a Boulder with a Toe

"Furthermore, good son, when I set out toward Kuśinagara to enter nirvāṇa, five hundred strongmen clearing the road could not move a great boulder for all their combined strength. Moved by compassion, I lifted it with the toe of my own foot, tossed it into the air, caught it in my hand, blew it to fine dust, and restored it whole again — quelling the strongmen's own pride. I then briefly taught them the essentials of the Dharma, and they all awakened the aspiration for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening" (Source: Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 14).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both closing stories make the same understated point about power: in each case the Buddha explicitly denies that anything supernatural has actually happened to the object of the display — no real lions, no real destruction of the stone — and attributes the entire effect to the cultivated power of loving-kindness alone. The fascicle's eighteen stories, taken together, form a working definition of what "the responsive body" means: not force applied from outside, but a presence that meets each being — deva, bandit, elephant, or strongman — with exactly the specific, individually calibrated response that being's own karma makes it ready to receive.
