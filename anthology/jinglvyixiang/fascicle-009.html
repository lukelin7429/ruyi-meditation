Bodhisattvas' Outward Teaching

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 9 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Bodhisattvas' Outward Teaching, Part 2" (外化菩薩部第二) — thirteen entries turning from the bodhisattva's own solitary practice toward work done for others, including Mañjuśrī's transformation of a woman's corpse and his retrieval of a bowl across seventy-two Ganges-sands of buddha-lands, Samantabhadra's vow to protect the Lotus Sūtra's own teachers, Prince Samantadāna's theft-defying dive for wish-granting pearls, two bodhisattvas who kill themselves at sea to save a storm-wrecked ship, and the cowherd child who becomes a teacher of the Great Vehicle at seven.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 9 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 9 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 9 turns from the bodhisattva's own solitary practice, the subject of fascicle 8, toward bodhisattvas' outward-facing work — teaching, converting, protecting, and rescuing others. It carries thirteen entries, several of them showcasing Mañjuśrī's and Samantabhadra's own supernormal activity on others' behalf, alongside two classic self-sacrifice narratives at sea and two closely related pearl-diving stories. Entries are grouped here into thematic clusters that roughly track the compendium's own sequence, with the two non-adjacent sea-sacrifice entries paired together despite their separation in the original text, while every entry's full text remains intact.

1–2. Mañjuśrī's Two Miracles of Instruction

Mañjuśrī transforms a wanton woman's own body into a rotting corpse to shock her lover into awakening, then, in a separate episode, reaches his hand through seventy-two Ganges-sands of buddha-lands to retrieve a bowl no arhat or even Maitreya could recover — both stories closing with the revelation that Mañjuśrī had likewise converted the Buddha himself, in a still earlier age, before either story's human protagonists ever appear.

Chinese Source
文殊變金光首女令成醜壞一

上金光首與長者子畏聞。俱在遊觀園散花燒香莊嚴伎樂。時彼女人觀長者子意以為足。文殊師利(舊法云濡首)化此女身應時終亡。顏色變惡。眼耳鼻口膿血流出。身體[病-丙+(絳-糸)]爛不可復視。青蠅飛來周匝共食。時長者子見此女身變壞如是。怖懅不安欲求自歸濟脫是患。當從何所而免斯苦。時文殊師利童真威神。令園樹木自然出讚。時長者子聞樹讚頌。歡喜踊躍善心即生。以衣裓盛女人死屍棄叢樹間而捨之去。於是世尊欲以開化彼長者子。從身放光其明普照摩竭國界。長者子見如來威神巍巍。心生歡喜。其天帝釋則在前立。見長者子而歎之曰。年少善緣。獲得福利。乃能見佛。時彼年少聞此勸讚。即與天帝俱詣佛所。帝釋天意。華用與年少言。可取此華散如來上。散已稽首佛足前住白言。今自歸佛及法聖眾。有放逸女上金光首。實與戲樂詣遊觀園。則於今日顏貌變惡即時壽終。捨諸一切宗室眷屬。發大恐懼。將無國王推問之耶。佛言且止。時上金光首。見長者子以蒙開化。隨從律教。即與伎樂往詣佛所。稽首佛足退住一面。時文殊師利謂長者子曰。為識此姊不。答云。已知之矣。又問。云何知乎。於是長者子報文殊師利。而說頌曰。色者如聚沫。痛痒泡起頃。幻想如野馬。吾曉知如是。佛告阿難。文殊師利乃往古世勸化此女使發道意。吾本前世而勸化之使發道意。上金光首過九十二百千劫。當得作佛。號寶光明。長者子當為菩薩。名德光耀。其復作佛。未滅度時。授德光燿菩薩之決。乃般泥洹號曰持㷿(出大淨法門經)。

Ru-Yi Translation
Mañjuśrī Transforms Golden-Light-Crown into a Horror

The woman Suvarṇaśiras, "Golden-Light-Crown," and the elder's son Bhīruśruta were together in a pleasure garden, scattering flowers and burning incense amid music, when the woman, watching the young man, felt her own desire satisfied. At that instant Mañjuśrī (called in the old translations "Mṛduśiras," "Gentle-Crowned") transformed the woman's very body: she died on the spot, her face turning hideous, pus and blood streaming from her eyes, ears, nose, and mouth, her body swelling and rotting past recognition, swarmed at once by flies. Terrified at seeing her corpse dissolve this way before him, the young man searched desperately for some refuge from this horror. Then, through Mañjuśrī's own youthful, wondrous power, the garden's very trees spontaneously sang out verses of praise — and hearing them, the young man's fear gave way to joy, and a wholesome heart arose in him. Gathering the woman's corpse in his own robe, he laid it among the trees and left it there, walking away.

The World-Honored One, wishing to further instruct him, then emitted light from his own body that illuminated the whole realm of Magadha. Seeing the Tathāgata's towering majesty, the young man's heart filled with joy — and Śakra, appearing before him, praised his good fortune in having encountered a buddha at all. Following the god's own urging, the young man took flowers Śakra offered him and scattered them over the Tathāgata, then bowed at his feet and declared his own refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the noble sangha — recounting how the wanton woman Suvarṇaśiras, who had gone with him to the garden for pleasure, had that very day turned hideous and died, and asking in his fear whether some king might now come to investigate him for it. The Buddha told him only to be at ease.

Suvarṇaśiras herself, seeing that the young man had already been converted and now followed the moral precepts, came with her own musicians before the Buddha, bowed at his feet, and stood to one side. Mañjuśrī then asked the young man whether he still recognized this "elder sister" — and, asked how he knew her, the young man answered in verse: "Form is like a mass of foam; feeling, like a bubble rising and bursting; perception, like a mirage — this I now clearly understand."

The Buddha told Ānanda that Mañjuśrī had, in ages past, likewise converted this very woman and roused her to the path — just as the Buddha himself, in a former life, had done the same. Suvarṇaśiras, ninety-two hundred thousand kalpas hence, would become a buddha named Ratnaprabhā, "Jeweled Radiance"; the elder's son would become a bodhisattva named Guṇaprabhā, "Radiance of Virtue," and later a buddha himself — the Buddha, before his own nirvāṇa, having already conferred upon the bodhisattva Guṇaprabhā his own prediction of buddhahood, with the name, at his own final nirvāṇa, Dhṛtajvāla, "Torch-Bearer" (Source: Sūtra of the Great Pure Dharma-Gate).

文殊現身諸剎取鉢弘教二

有二百天子發菩薩心。而未堅固皆欲墮落。各自念言佛法難得。我等今定不學菩薩。不如取羅漢辟支佛而般泥洹。佛知是人可成菩薩。而意中退。佛便化作一迦羅越。持百味飯滿鉢到佛前。而便作禮以鉢上佛。佛即受鉢。文殊師利白言。世尊。當故報恩。舍利弗疑即便問佛。佛即以鉢捨地。其鉢便下沒過諸剎。直過七十二恒沙等剎土。名曰漚呵沙。其佛號曰光明王。今現在世。其鉢於彼佛剎住止空中亦無持者。鉢所過諸佛剎。其佛侍者皆問於佛。佛答上方有佛號釋迦文。鉢從彼來。救護墮退菩薩意耳。佛語舍利弗。行求鉢來。舍利弗即承佛威神。自以慧力以萬三昧過萬佛剎都不見鉢。從三昧起還白佛言。求之不見。復遣目連亦復不見。須菩提等五百尊者悉不能見。彌勒菩薩又不能見。令文殊師利求鉢。文殊師利不起于坐即入三昧。以其右手指地下。行過諸佛剎莫不聞知。所過剎土皆為震動。凡諸剎土皆見文殊。過七十二恒河沙。其臂上毛一一毛間有億百千光明億百千蓮華。一蓮華上有坐菩薩。悉歎釋迦文佛功德聲聞菩薩及以佛土。時釋迦文放足下光明。照下方過七十二恒河沙等剎。見其光明悉得摩仳低三昧。文殊師利以右手取鉢。與無央數菩薩俱來。鉢在手中作禮授鉢。佛謂舍利弗。今為汝說過去劫事。往昔有佛。號勇莫能勝。有比丘僧。名曰慧王。持鉢入惟致國中分衛。得百味飯。爾時有尊者子名離垢王。為乳母所抱。在城門上。其兒遙見比丘。而從抱下便往趣之。求其飲食。比丘即以蜜餅授之。其兒則食知味甘美。隨比丘行不顧乳母。便隨至勇莫能勝佛所。為佛作禮而坐一面。比丘以所持鉢食與是小兒令其上佛。兒便授佛。佛食隨滿。以是遍八萬四千比丘及菩薩萬二千人。各各皆飽。其兒所持食猶無減損。佛以威神令兒歡喜。因從比丘受五戒法發菩提心。兒父母求子為佛作禮。子白父言。我今已得入菩薩法。願作沙門。父母即聽。吾等亦為發心從汝。佛言。慧王比丘文殊師利是。其時兒者我身是也。如我身等不可稱數。阿僧祇剎土諸佛。悉為文殊師利之所發動。我等悉蒙文殊師利之恩。其二百天子即時自念。釋迦文佛為文殊師利之所發意自致成佛。我等何為懈怠。用是念故其心則堅(出阿闍世王經上卷)。

Mañjuśrī Retrieves the Bowl Across Countless Buddha-Lands

Two hundred young devas had given rise to the bodhisattva resolve, but their resolve was not yet firm, and each of them, thinking the Buddha's own path too hard to attain, considered abandoning it for the swifter goal of becoming an arhat or pratyekabuddha instead. Knowing they could still be brought to complete the bodhisattva path, the Buddha manifested himself as a poor householder carrying a bowl filled with a hundred delicacies, who came before the Buddha, bowed, and offered the bowl up. The Buddha accepted it — and, at Mañjuśrī's prompting that such a gift called for repayment, cast the bowl down into the earth before Śāriputra's very eyes. The bowl sank straight through seventy-two Ganges-sands' worth of buddha-lands, coming to rest, unheld, in the air within the land of a buddha named Prabhāsarāja, "King of Light," now presently teaching there — whose own attendants, puzzled at its sudden appearance, were told by their own buddha that it came from a buddha named Śākyamuni above, sent to rescue bodhisattvas whose resolve was faltering.

The Buddha then asked Śāriputra to retrieve the bowl. Relying on his own wisdom, Śāriputra entered ten thousand samādhis and passed through ten thousand buddha-lands, yet could not find it; rising from meditation, he reported his failure. Maudgalyāyana was sent, and likewise failed; Subhūti and five hundred other elders could not see it; even Maitreya could not see it. Only when Mañjuśrī was asked did he, without even rising from his seat, enter samādhi and reach his right hand down through the earth, passing through every buddha-land below, all of which shook at his passing and in all of which he himself became visible. Through the seventy-two Ganges-sands of lands, each hair on his very arm shone with hundreds of thousands of lights, each light bearing hundreds of thousands of lotuses, and on each lotus a bodhisattva seated in meditation, all praising Śākyamuni's own virtues, his śrāvakas, bodhisattvas, and buddha-land alike. Śākyamuni then emitted light from the very soles of his own feet, illuminating those same seventy-two Ganges-sands of lands below, and all who saw that light attained the samādhi of universal equality. Mañjuśrī then took up the bowl with his right hand and returned with countless bodhisattvas, presenting it to the Buddha with a bow.

The Buddha then told Śāriputra of a past age, under a buddha named Vīryāsaha, "Unsurpassed in Vigor," when a monk named Prajñārāja, "King of Wisdom," went begging and received a bowl of a hundred delicacies. A young prince's son, Vimalarāja, "Stainless King," being carried by his own wet nurse at the city gate, saw the monk from afar, had himself set down, and ran to beg food from him; the monk gave him a honey cake, whose sweetness so delighted the child that he followed the monk, heedless of his own nurse, all the way to the buddha Vīryāsaha himself, where he bowed and sat to one side. The monk had the child himself offer the bowl of food to the Buddha — and the Buddha ate from it, yet the food, far from being depleted, fed eighty-four thousand monks and twelve thousand bodhisattvas besides, all to their full satisfaction, without the bowl ever emptying. Delighted by the Buddha's own power, the child took the five precepts from the monk and gave rise to the bodhi-resolve himself; his own parents, coming to bow before the Buddha in search of their son, were told by the child that he had already entered the bodhisattva's own path and wished to become a monk — to which they consented, resolving to follow him themselves.

The Buddha revealed that the monk Prajñārāja was Mañjuśrī himself, and that the child was the Buddha's own former self — just as every buddha throughout countless, incalculable buddha-lands, without number, had likewise all been first roused to their own resolve by Mañjuśrī, to whose kindness we all remain indebted. Hearing this, the two hundred devaputras reflected that if even Śākyamuni Buddha himself had first been moved to buddhahood by Mañjuśrī, they themselves had no cause for laziness — and, through this very reflection, their own resolve became firm at last (Source: Sūtra of King Ajātaśatru, upper fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both stories end by pulling the frame back one further step: the human drama the reader has just followed — a shocked lover, two hundred wavering devas — turns out to rest on an act Mañjuśrī performed in a still earlier age, converting either the woman herself or the Buddha himself before either story's own timeline even begins. The effect is a kind of infinite regress of gratitude: even Śākyamuni Buddha, the compendium insists, first found his own resolve because of Mañjuśrī, which is precisely the fact that finally steadies the two hundred devas' own wavering hearts. The lesson is not "imitate the Buddha" but something a step further back — "notice whose kindness made the Buddha's own path possible in the first place."

3. Samantabhadra's Vow to Protect the Dharma Teachers

Samantabhadra, arriving from a distant buddha-land purely to hear the Lotus Sūtra, vows to personally appear before anyone who upholds, recites, or even merely forgets a phrase of the sūtra in a future degenerate age — turning the sūtra's own transmission into a standing promise of aid rather than a task left to the reciter alone.

Chinese Source

普賢菩薩。以自在神通威德名聞。與大菩薩不可稱數從東方來。所經諸國普皆震動。雨寶蓮華作眾伎樂。又與無數八部鬼神等大眾圍繞。禮釋迦牟尼佛。白佛言。我於寶威德上王佛國。遙聞此娑婆世界說法華經共來聽受。唯願世尊當為說之。佛告普賢。若有善男子女人。成就四法。於如來滅後當得是法華經。普賢菩薩言。若有行立懃誦此經思惟此經。若人於此經有所忘失一句一偈。大菩薩眾俱詣其所。而自現身供養守護安慰其心。亦為供養法華經故還令通利。見者歡喜轉復精進。以見我故即得三昧及陀羅尼。若後惡世中四眾來求索者。受持者。讀誦者。書寫者。欲修習是法華經。於三七日中應一心精進。滿三七日亦當現其人前而為說法(出妙法蓮華經第七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva Samantabhadra, renowned for his own unhindered supernormal power and majesty, came from the east together with countless great bodhisattvas, every land he passed through shaking, raining down jeweled lotuses amid heavenly music, surrounded by a great host of the eightfold division of spirits, and bowed before Śākyamuni Buddha. He said: "From the buddha-land of Ratnatejorājā I heard, from afar, that this sahā world was expounding the Lotus Sūtra, and have come together to hear it — may the World-Honored One now teach it." The Buddha told Samantabhadra that any son or daughter of good family who fulfills four conditions will obtain this Lotus Sūtra after the Tathāgata's own nirvāṇa.

Samantabhadra then vowed: if anyone, standing or walking, recites and reflects upon this sūtra, and forgets even a single phrase or verse of it, he himself, together with the great bodhisattvas, will go to that very person, manifesting himself to protect and comfort their heart, restoring their fluency for the sake of the sūtra itself — and whoever sees him will rejoice and grow only more diligent, attaining, through the very sight of him, both samādhi and dhāraṇī. And if, in a later, evil age, any of the fourfold assembly seeks, upholds, recites, or copies this Lotus Sūtra, wishing to practice it, they need only apply themselves single-mindedly for twenty-one days — at the end of which he will manifest himself before them and teach them the Dharma himself (Source: Wondrous Lotus Sūtra, fascicle 7).

4. Śuddhavīrya's Long-Suffering Conversion of a Prince

A bodhisattva volunteers for the one task eighty-four thousand others refused: converting a proud, abusive young prince — enduring beatings and insults at his gate for a thousand years, then reaching each successive gate only after tens of thousands more, until patience alone finally wins the argument.

Chinese Source

過去劫時佛號廣光明(菩薩行經云離垢光)國王之子名財功德(菩薩行經云業首)時年十六。自恃端政而生憍慢。初不向佛恭敬禮拜。佛即行籌誰能教化。八萬四千菩薩無受籌者。有一菩薩名淨精進。言。我能。即時大千六反震動。便往王門立。王子見之惡言訾毀。以土坌面。刀杖瓦石而加其身。菩薩爾時不瞋不去心不疲厭。經一千年受如是苦。過二萬歲乃得至彼第二門下。八萬四千年七日未滿方得至其第七門下。爾時王子見是菩薩便作是言。道士今來何所求索。即於菩薩生不思議心。云何是人。終經爾時多受眾苦。而心不厭。爾時菩薩知王子心已自調伏。即便說偈令往詣佛。爾時王子即捨王位。在佛法中出家聽法如法而住得無生忍。爾時淨精進者我身是也。財功德者即彌勒是(出大集經第二十六卷又出調伏王子道心經菩薩行經大同)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, under a buddha named Vipulaprabhā (the Bodhisattvacaryā Sūtra calls him Vimalaprabhā, "Stainless Light"), a king's son named Guṇadhana (the same sūtra calls him Karmaśiras, "Foremost in Deeds"), then sixteen, proud of his own fine looks, had never once bowed in respect before the Buddha. The Buddha, seeking someone able to convert him, drew lots among eighty-four thousand bodhisattvas, none of whom would accept the task, until one bodhisattva, Śuddhavīrya, "Pure Diligence," volunteered — at which the great chiliocosm shook six ways. Standing at the prince's own gate, he was met with abuse, mud thrown in his face, and blows from staves and stones, yet remained neither angry nor willing to leave, unwearied even after a thousand years of such treatment. Only after twenty thousand years did he reach the prince's second gate; only after eighty-four thousand years, not yet seven days short, did he reach the seventh.

The prince, marveling that this ascetic's mind never wearied through so long a trial, asked at last what he sought — and Śuddhavīrya, perceiving the prince's own mind now finally softened, spoke a verse urging him to go before the Buddha. The prince then renounced his own throne, went forth under the Buddha's own teaching, and, hearing the Dharma and abiding in it, attained the acceptance of the non-arising of dharmas. The Buddha revealed that Śuddhavīrya was himself, and Guṇadhana was Maitreya (Source: Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra, fascicle 26; also found, largely in agreement, in the Sūtra on the Prince Whose Mind Was Subdued and the Bodhisattvacaryā Sūtra).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story's arithmetic is almost comically precise — a thousand years at the first gate, twenty thousand more to reach the second, eighty-four thousand more, lacking only seven days, to reach the seventh — yet the precision is beside the point. No reader is meant to track the running total; the numbers exist only to make a single claim impossible to state any other way: that this bodhisattva's patience under continuous abuse has, in effect, no calculable upper bound. The eventual conversion, when it comes, is almost an afterthought — a single verse, spoken only once the numbers have already done their work of proving the patience was real.

5. Jyotiṣka Māṇava's Dragon-Elephants

A young bodhisattva tests whether his own vow for buddhahood is genuine by asking dragon-elephants to spring from his hands — then sends them to purify the whole world with fragrant rain before they return, their errand complete, to stand quietly before him again.

Chinese Source

樹提摩納。在寶藏佛前。右膝著地長跪叉手。前白佛言。世尊。我今發無上菩提心成就善根。於三乘法若我所願得己利者。令我兩手自然而出白色龍象。佛神力故其兩手中即出龍象。其色純白七處到地。見是事已告言。龍象汝等。今者可昇虛空去此。不遠遍雨此界八德香水。覺悟此界一切眾生。若有眾生得遇一渧。聞其香氣悉斷五蓋。所謂婬欲瞋恚睡眠掉戲疑。是時龍象在虛空中。周遊速疾猶如力士善射放箭。是二龍象所作諸事悉成就已。復還來至摩納前住。時樹提見之心大歡喜(出樹提摩納發菩提誓願經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Jyotiṣka Māṇava, kneeling on his right knee before the Buddha Ratnagarbha with palms joined, said: "World-Honored One, I have now given rise to the resolve for unsurpassed bodhi and accomplished roots of goodness; if, among the three vehicles, what I have wished for is truly to my own benefit, let white dragon-elephants spontaneously emerge from my own two hands." Through the Buddha's own spiritual power, pure-white dragon-elephants at once emerged from both his hands, their seven limbs touching the ground. Seeing this, the Buddha told the dragon-elephants themselves to rise into the sky and, not far from there, rain down the eight-virtued fragrant water over the whole world, that all beings who touched even a single drop, on smelling its fragrance, might have their five hindrances — desire, anger, drowsiness, restlessness, and doubt — entirely cut off. The two dragon-elephants then circled swiftly through the sky, like a strong archer's arrow in flight, and, their task accomplished, returned to stand once more before Jyotiṣka Māṇava, who watched them return with great joy (Source: Sūtra of Jyotiṣka Māṇava's Vow to Attain Bodhi).

6. Samantadāna's Quest for the Wish-Granting Pearls

A prince gives up a wealthy title to beg instead, wins three cities' own wish-granting pearls by calming the poisonous serpents guarding each, and, when a sea god steals them back, threatens to bail out the entire ocean with his bare hands until the god relents and returns them.

Chinese Source
普施求珠降伏海神以濟窮乏六

Ru-Yi Translation
Samantadāna's Quest for the Wish-Granting Pearls

Long ago, as a bodhisattva born into a household of the four castes, I declared at the very moment I touched the ground: "I shall deliver beings from every calamity; I shall dispel their ignorance and grant them sight and hearing of the sages' own bright example." My own nine classes of relatives, astonished that an infant could speak at all, suspected some spirit or dragon possessed me and wished to divine the cause — but I answered that I was transformed by the sages above, possessed of universal wisdom, no demon at all, and then fell silent. My relatives, recognizing in me an ambition as vast as heaven and earth, named me Samantadāna, "Universal Giving." By ten, I had mastered every scripture and worldly art alike, and, taking leave of my family, devoted myself to relieving the poor through giving. When my family offered me the wealthiest name in the realm to use as I pleased, I asked instead only to become a monk, and, given the monastic robe, bowl, and staff, set out to relieve beings through these alone. My parents consented, and I traveled teaching through an entire great kingdom.

In that kingdom lived a powerful, learned family whose daughter, seeing my own bearing — my mind as calm and pure as heavenly gold, marked with the signs of a great sage-to-be, a future hero of the world — was offered to me in marriage by her own father. I agreed, on condition of first completing my journey, and, taking passage across the sea, went on alone into the mountains. In a deserted place I came upon a silver city, its walls encircled seven times by a poisonous serpent as thick as a hundred spans, its head raised to watch me. Reflecting that this creature's malice called only for my own compassion, I roused loving-kindness, and its poison vanished, its head bowing in sleep; I walked over it and entered the city. The devas within, delighted at my long-awaited arrival, asked me to stay thirty days, during which I entrusted my own affairs to an attendant minister and accepted their offerings. At the end, they gave me a bright pearl that shone forty thousand li, granting whatever I wished for, its store of treasures never exhausted — on the condition that, should I attain buddhahood, they might become my disciples. I agreed, and went on.

I next came to a golden city, encircled fourteen times by a still greater serpent, its head raised many times higher; again I entered loving-kindness meditation, its poison subsided, and I entered. The devas there, equally delighted, asked me to stay a hundred eighty days, and at my departure gave me a second pearl, shining two hundred thousand li, its treasures without limit, on the same condition of future discipleship, granting me besides unsurpassed supernormal footwork. I went on further still to a city of lapis lazuli, encircled twenty-one times by a serpent raising its head in fury at the very gate; again I subdued it with loving-kindness and entered. The devas there, delighted as before, asked me to stay three full seasons, and at my departure gave a third pearl, shining sixteen hundred thousand li, granting whatever was wished for without limit, on the same condition.

Having gained these three pearls, I turned back toward my own former home. But the sea's own spirits, gathering in council, reasoned that these three pearls were the very glory of their whole ocean, and that they would rather lose every other treasure than let this ascetic keep them — and one, transforming into an ordinary man, approached me and asked to see the treasure I had reportedly obtained. The moment I showed it to him, he seized it by force. I protested that I had crossed every danger and the sea itself to obtain this treasure for the relief of the poor, only to have it stolen by this very spirit — and, when he refused to return it, I vowed to drain his entire ocean dry. He scoffed that no one could exhaust so vast and deep a sea — but I declared that before the Buddha Dīpaṃkara I had vowed to gain the power to overturn every ocean, to move Mount Sumeru itself, to shake heaven and earth, and to displace every buddha-land, and that the Buddha had granted my very wish; a mere spirit's paltry strength could never obstruct my true resolve. I then planted both feet and began scooping the sea's water out over the very rim of the iron mountains that encircle the world. The pure devas above, recalling my old vow before Dīpaṃkara Buddha to deliver all beings as a future World-Honored One, descended to help me bail, and in a moment eight-tenths of the ocean was gone. Terrified that his own home was about to be destroyed, the sea spirit returned my pearl at once.

I then continued my journey, giving alms as I went; every country I passed through was left without a single poor person, every king reformed his own rule to govern by the five precepts and ten good deeds, opening the prisons in general amnesty, until beings everywhere were benefited and I myself at last attained buddhahood. I was that Samantadāna; my father was King Śuddhodana himself; my mother was my own mother, named in this account Shěmiào; the ascetic's daughter was Gopī; the deva of the silver city was Ānanda; the deva of the golden city was Maudgalyāyana; and the deva of the lapis-lazuli city was Śāriputra (Source: Collection on the Perfections, fascicle 1; also found in the Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, where this same story is told of Prince Kalyāṇakārin).

7. King Atyuttama and Ānanda's Mistaken Rebuke

Ānanda, seeing a bodhisattva sharing a bed with a woman, judges it a breach of pure conduct — only to have the bodhisattva rise bodily into the air before him, proving that what looked like transgression was in fact a final, liberating act of compassion for someone bound to him across a hundred lifetimes.

Chinese Source

阿難白佛。憶念我昔入舍衛城。見重勝王菩薩與女人同床。我謂犯穢心用惟慮得無異人。學梵行者於如來教將無造見聞想念。發斯語時三千大千世界六反振動。時重勝王菩薩即自踊身住於空中。去地四丈九尺。報阿難曰。犯禁穢者寧能踊身止虛空乎。在如來前。阿難投身即自悔過。如何偏見求大龍短。佛告阿難。彼女人者。乃往過去世為重勝王百生之偶。宿情未拔貪重勝顏。口發誓言。重勝王若與我願得遂所娛當從其教。時重勝王心知其念。晨朝正服入之其室。即時頌曰。

我愚悖於欲　諸佛所不歎
能蠲恩愛者　得佛人中上

時女喜躍。即從坐起自投于地。歸命自責伏罪悔過。為重勝王而歎頌曰。

吾已離諸欲　世尊之所歎
節止恩愛著　願佛無上道
前心之所想　今自首悔過
傷愍諸群生　究竟發道意

爾時重勝王授彼女訣。轉此女身後九十九劫當得作佛。號離無數百千所受如來(出慧上菩薩經上卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda told the Buddha he recalled once entering Śrāvastī and seeing the bodhisattva Atyuttama, "Surpassing Victory," lying on the same bed as a woman, and, suspecting some impure act, wondered whether a practitioner of pure conduct ought to be seen and thought of this way at all under the Tathāgata's own teaching. The moment he spoke this, the whole trichiliocosm shook six ways, and Atyuttama himself rose up and hovered in the air, four zhang nine chi above the ground, and asked Ānanda whether someone truly guilty of impurity could do such a thing in the Buddha's own presence. Ānanda threw himself down in immediate repentance for his own biased judgment against so great a being.

The Buddha then explained: that woman had been Atyuttama's own companion across a hundred past lives, her old attachment to him not yet uprooted, and she had vowed that if Atyuttama would grant her wish just once more, she would afterward follow his own teaching entirely. Knowing her mind, Atyuttama, properly dressed, entered her room that very morning and spoke this verse to her:

"My own foolish transgression through desire is nothing the buddhas praise;
one who can cut off attachment and craving attains the foremost place among the buddhas' own people."

The woman, overjoyed, rose from her seat and threw herself to the ground, taking refuge and confessing her own fault, and praised Atyuttama in turn:

"I have now left every desire behind, as the World-Honored One himself praises;
having restrained my own attachment and craving, I vow the Buddha's own unsurpassed path.
What my mind once desired, I now confess and repent myself;
grieving for all beings, I resolve at last upon the path."

Atyuttama then gave her his own prediction: that after transforming this very female body, she would, ninety-nine kalpas hence, attain buddhahood herself, under a name that translates roughly as "Liberated from Countless Hundreds of Thousands," Tathāgata (Source: Sūtra of the Bodhisattva Wisdom-Superior, upper fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium stages Ānanda's misjudgment quite deliberately — the shock of a bodhisattva rising bodily into the air is aimed as much at the reader as at Ānanda himself, both of whom are meant to have jumped to the same conclusion from the same evidence. The story's real interest is not in defending the appearance of impropriety but in insisting that liberative means can look, from outside, indistinguishable from the very thing it is meant to end — which is precisely why the compendium bothers to have Ānanda voice the accusation aloud, rather than simply narrating the woman's own liberation without ever raising the doubt at all.

8, 11. Two Deaths at Sea to Save the Ship

Twice in this fascicle a bodhisattva, caught in a storm with a boatload of merchants, concludes that only his own death can appease the sea and bring the others safely to land — one simply asks to be held onto and stabs himself, the other is later restored to life by Śakra's own hand once his companions' grief has moved the heavens.

Chinese Source
大薩他婆渡海船壞殺身濟眾八

釋迦牟尼佛為菩薩時。名大薩他婆。當渡大海惡風壞船。語眾賈人捉我頭髮手足當渡汝等。人人捉已以刀自殺。大海水法不停死屍。即時疾風吹至岸邊(出大智論第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation
Mahāsārthavāha's Death at Sea

When Śākyamuni Buddha was a bodhisattva, he was named Mahāsārthavāha, "Great Caravan-Leader." Crossing the great ocean, his ship was wrecked by a violent storm, and he told the merchants aboard to hold onto his own hair and limbs, that he might carry them across — then, once they had all taken hold, killed himself with a knife, knowing that the sea, by its own nature, never keeps a corpse afloat. A swift wind at once blew them all to shore (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 4).

坐海以救估客十一

昔者菩薩與五百商人入海採寶。入海數月獲寶重載。將旋本土道逢飄風。雷電振地水神雲集。四周若城眼中出火波涌灌山。眾人啼曰。吾等死矣。恐怖易色仰天求哀。菩薩愴然心生計曰。吾之求佛但為眾生耳。海神所惡死屍。為其危命濟眾。斯乃開士之尚業矣。吾不以身血注海。惡海神之意者船人終不被于岸。謂眾人曰。爾等屬手相持并援吾身。眾人承命。菩薩即引刀自害。海神德焉漂舟上岸。眾人普濟。船人抱屍號哭曰。斯必菩薩非凡之徒也。躄踊呼天。寧令吾等命殞于茲。無喪上德之士矣。其言真誠上感諸天。天帝釋覩菩薩之弘慈。帝釋下曰。斯至德菩薩將為聖雄。今自活之。以天神藥灌其口中。并通塗屍。菩薩即蘇忽起坐與眾相勞。帝釋以名寶滿其舟中。千倍于前。即還本土九親相見靡不歡悅。賙窮濟乏惠逮眾生。顯宣佛經開化愚冥。其國王服菩薩德詣稟清化。君仁臣忠率土持戒家有孝子。國豐毒歇黎庶歡欣。終生天上長離眾苦。菩薩累劫精進不休遂至得佛。殺身濟眾者吾身是也。天帝釋者彌勒是也。五百商人者今坐中五百應真是也(出殺身濟賈人經又出度無極集)。

Sitting the Sea Calm to Save the Merchants

Long ago, the bodhisattva went to sea together with five hundred merchants in search of treasure, and, after months at sea, their ship laden with riches, turned homeward — only to meet a violent storm, thunder shaking the earth, sea spirits gathering like storm clouds, fire streaming from their eyes on every side, waves rising to engulf even the mountains. The terrified merchants cried out that they were doomed, and looked to heaven for mercy.

The bodhisattva, moved with grief, reflected that he had sought buddhahood only for the sake of beings, and that the sea gods, who abhor a corpse, might be appeased by his own death, saving the others' lives — the very highest work of a great being. Reasoning that only his own blood, poured into the sea, could satisfy the sea gods' anger and bring the ship safely to shore, he told the others to link hands and hold onto his body, and, once they had done so, drew a knife and killed himself. Through the virtue of his death, the sea gods carried the ship safely to shore, and everyone aboard was saved.

The sailors, embracing his corpse, wailed that he could be no ordinary man but a true bodhisattva, crying out to heaven that they would rather have died themselves than lose so virtuous a being — and their sincere grief moved the heavens themselves. Śakra, witnessing the bodhisattva's own vast compassion, descended and, declaring that so virtuous a being must become a sage and hero of the world, revived him himself, pouring heavenly medicine into his mouth and over his body. The bodhisattva at once revived, rose, and greeted the others — and Śakra filled their ship with famous treasures, a thousand times what it had held before.

Returning to his own homeland, welcomed joyfully by all his kin, he relieved the poor and needy, proclaimed the Buddha's own teaching, and enlightened the ignorant everywhere; the king of that country, moved by the bodhisattva's own virtue, came to receive his pure teaching himself, so that ruler and ministers alike became benevolent and loyal, the whole realm kept the precepts, families raised filial children, the country prospered free of poison and harm, and its people, rejoicing, were at last reborn in heaven, forever free of suffering. Through kalpas of unbroken diligence, the bodhisattva at last attained buddhahood. He who killed himself to save the multitude was my own former self; Śakra was Maitreya; and the five hundred merchants are the five hundred arhats now seated in this very assembly (Source: Sūtra of Killing Oneself to Save Merchants; also found in the Collection on the Perfections).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Placed side by side, though the compendium separates them by three intervening entries, these two stories share an almost identical premise: a storm at sea, a folk conviction that the water demands a corpse before it will release a ship, and a leader who turns that superstition into a deliberate act of self-giving rather than resisting or debunking it. What distinguishes them is what happens after death — Mahāsārthavāha's story ends the instant the ship is saved, his death simply accepted as final, while the second bodhisattva's grieving companions are given back what they lost, Śakra's own intervention rewarding their sincerity rather than the bodhisattva's sacrifice being its own sufficient reward. Read together, the pair suggests the compendium was less interested in a single fixed moral about self-sacrifice than in showing that the same extreme gesture can resolve in either register — as an ending, or as a trial that compassion itself ultimately reverses.

9. The Ascetic and the Nesting Bird

A meditator remains motionless on a mountainside for as long as it takes a bird's chicks to hatch, grow feathers, and finally learn to fly, rather than risk disturbing the nest she has built upon his own crown.

Chinese Source

菩薩在山。慈心端坐思惟不動。鳥孺頂上覺鳥在頂。懼卵墜落身不移搖。捨身而行彼處不動及鳥生翅。但未能飛終不捨去。如是自知便說此偈。

若能辦此事　於天人中天
能不觸嬈彼　此德無有上
是故彼世尊　最為第一神

(出僧伽羅剎經上卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva, sitting in meditation on a mountain, his mind fixed in loving-kindness, remained utterly still even after a bird built her nest upon his very crown — aware of her presence, but fearing that any movement might cause her eggs to fall, he stayed unmoving there until her chicks had hatched and grown their own feathers, and did not leave even then until they were able to fly. Reflecting on this himself, he spoke this verse:

"One who can accomplish such a thing is a god among gods and men;
to remain undisturbing to another being — this virtue has no equal.
For this the World-Honored One himself is the very foremost of spirits,
abiding thus at the seat of awakening, every virtue already complete within him."

(Source: Saṃgharakṣa Sūtra, upper fascicle).

10. Diving for the Wish-Fulfilling Pearl

A caravan-leader begs a wish-fulfilling pearl from a sea dragon king to relieve his country's poverty, only to have it stolen back by resentful sea spirits — and threatens to bail the entire ocean dry with his bare hands rather than let it go.

Chinese Source

吾從無數劫以來精進求道。初無懈息自致得佛。超越九劫出彌勒前。我念過去時國人貧窮生怜愍心。乃欲入海求如意珠。眾人大會望風舉帆。詣海龍王從求頭上如意之珠。龍王聞其欲濟窮士即以珠與。時諸賈客各各採寶悉皆具足乘船來還。海中諸龍及諸神鬼悉共議言。此如意珠海中上寶。非世俗人所當獲者。云何損海益閻浮利。誠可惜之。當作方計還奪其珠不可失之。時諸龍鬼晝夜圍繞欲奪其珠。導師德尊如意珠力不能奪之。度海既畢。菩薩踊躍住於海邊。低頭下手呪願海神。以珠繫頸。時海龍神因緣得便使珠墮海。導師感激吾行入海。乘船涉難勤苦無量乃得此寶。當救眾乏。於今海神反令墮海。勅邊侍人捉持器來。吾卷海水令至底泥。不得珠者終不休懈。即便卷水不惜壽命。水自然趣悉入器中。諸海龍神見之懷懼。此人威勢精進之力。誠非世有。水不久竭。即持珠來辭謝還之。吾等即爾相試不圖精進力勢如是。天上天下無能勝君。導師獲寶齎還國中。使雨七寶以供天下莫不安隱。時導師者則我身是(出生經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Through countless kalpas of unbroken diligence in seeking the path, I attained buddhahood at last, surpassing Maitreya by nine full kalpas. I recall, in a past age, seeing my own country's people in poverty and, moved to pity, resolving to enter the sea in search of the wish-fulfilling pearl. A great company of us set sail, and I went before the sea's own dragon king to beg for the wish-fulfilling pearl upon his very head — and the king, hearing my wish to relieve the poor, gave it to me at once. The other merchants, having gathered their own treasures in full, returned by ship together with me.

"But the sea's dragons and spirits, in council, reasoned that this pearl, the ocean's own supreme treasure, ought never belong to an ordinary mortal, and resolved to take it back by any means rather than lose it — surrounding me day and night, though my own virtue and the pearl's own power kept them from succeeding, until, once we had crossed the sea, I stood rejoicing at the shore, bowed my head, and invoked the sea gods' blessing with the pearl tied about my own neck. At that very moment, through the sea dragons' own contrivance, the pearl was made to fall into the sea.

"Grieved that I had crossed the sea through such labor and hardship only to obtain this treasure for the relief of the multitude, and that the sea spirits had now made it fall back into their own domain, I ordered my attendants to bring vessels, declaring I would bail out the sea to its very mud floor and never rest until the pearl was recovered. I began bailing without regard for my own life, and the water flowed of itself into the waiting vessels. The sea dragons and spirits, seeing this, grew afraid — such power and diligence, they said, was truly beyond the world's own measure — and, before the water could fully drain away, brought the pearl back and apologized, admitting they had only meant to test me, never imagining such diligence and power existed. I then returned to my own country with the pearl and caused a rain of the seven treasures to fall, bringing peace to the whole world. That guide was my own former self" (Source: Jātaka Sūtra, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry and entry 6, Samantadāna's own quest, are close enough in plot — a bodhisattva dives across the sea for a wish-granting pearl meant to relieve the poor, has it stolen back by aggrieved sea spirits, and forces its return by threatening to drain the entire ocean by hand — that they read almost as two tellings of one underlying story, drawn here from different source texts (the Collection on the Perfections and Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish for entry 6; the Jātaka Sūtra for this one) without being harmonized into a single account. Rather than editing one version out, the compendium's compilers preserved both, letting the reader notice the overlap rather than resolving it — a reminder that the *Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng* is fundamentally an anthology of independently transmitted sources, not a single continuous narrative smoothed into consistency.

12. Bodhisattvas Spring from the Earth

Countless bodhisattvas rise from beneath the ground to vow the sūtra's own future transmission, and the Buddha answers with a single wordless gesture — his own tongue stretching to the Brahmā heaven — that every buddha throughout the universe performs in unison at the same instant.

Chinese Source

時千世界微塵等菩薩摩訶薩。從地踊出一心合掌仰瞻尊顏。而白佛言。我等於佛滅後。世尊分身所在國土滅度之處當廣說此經。我等亦自欲得是真淨大法受持讀誦解說書寫而供養之。爾時世尊於文殊師利等無量百千萬億舊住娑婆世界菩薩及諸聲聞人非人等一切眾前。出廣長舌上至梵世。一切毛孔放無量色光。皆悉遍照十方世界。眾寶樹下師子坐上諸佛亦復如是(出法華經第六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattva-mahāsattvas as numerous as the fine dust motes of a thousand worlds sprang up from the earth, joined their palms in single-minded reverence, gazed up at the Buddha's own countenance, and vowed that, after his nirvāṇa, in every land where his emanation-bodies had themselves entered nirvāṇa, they would widely proclaim this sūtra, wishing themselves to uphold, recite, expound, copy, and make offerings to this true and pure great Dharma. The World-Honored One then, before Mañjuśrī and countless hundreds of thousands of myriads of bodhisattvas who had long dwelt in this sahā world, together with śrāvakas, humans, and non-humans of every kind, put forth his own broad, long tongue, reaching up to the Brahmā heaven, every pore of his body emitting boundless light of every color, illuminating the worlds of the ten directions entirely — and every buddha seated on a lion throne beneath the jeweled trees did likewise (Source: Lotus Sūtra, fascicle 6).

13. The Cowherd Child Who Expounded the Prajñāpāramitā

The fascicle closes with a boy who learns the Perfection of Wisdom by ear alone, dies tracking his own stray cattle, and is reborn instantly as an unborn child already reciting it from his mother's own womb — growing, by seven, into a teacher whom the very monks who once taught him now study under.

Chinese Source

昔有比丘精進持戒初不毀犯。住在精舍。所可諷誦是般若波羅蜜。有聞此比丘音聲莫不歡喜。有一小兒始年七歲城外牧牛。遙聞比丘誦說經聲。尋聲詣寺聽聞即解。兒大歡喜便問比丘。答不可意。小兒反說其義甚妙昔所希聞。比丘聞之歎此小兒。乃有智慧非是凡人。時兒即去還至牛所。所牧牛犢散走入山。兒尋其迹值虎被害。生長者家第一夫人作子。夫人懷妊口便能說般若波羅蜜。從朝至暮初不應息。其長者家素不奉法。怪此夫人謂口妄語謂呼鬼病。卜問譴祟無能知者。家中內外皆悉憂惶。是時比丘入城分衛詣長者門。遙聞經聲心甚喜悅。即問長者內中誰說深經音聲微妙。長者報言。婦得鬼病。晝夜妄語口初不息。比丘報言。此非鬼病。但說深經甚有義理。疑此夫人所懷妊兒是佛弟子。長者意解。即留比丘與作飲食。日月滿足乃產一男。又無惡露。其兒適生叉手長跪說般若波羅蜜。夫人產已還復如本。如夢寤已了無所識。長者集僧覩兒說經初無質礙。是時眾僧各各一心觀此小兒。長者問言。此為何等。比丘答曰。真佛弟子。慎莫驚疑。好養育之。此兒後大當為一切眾人作師。吾等悉當從其啟受。至七歲悉知微妙。與眾超絕智度無極。諸比丘等皆從受學。經中誤脫有所短少。皆為刪定足其所乏。兒每出入有所至止。輒開化人使發大乘。長者家室內外大小五百人眾。皆從兒學發大乘意悉行佛事。兒所教授城郭市里。凡所開發無上道意者八萬四千。承受弟子者五百人。諸比丘聞之意解志求大乘皆得法眼淨。佛告阿難。是時小兒者吾身是也。時比丘者迦葉佛是也(出小兒聞法即解經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, a monk of unbroken diligence and precept, dwelling in a monastery, recited the Perfection of Wisdom aloud, and all who heard his voice rejoiced in it. A boy of only seven, herding cattle outside the city, heard his chanting from afar, went to the temple to listen, and understood it at once — delighted, he questioned the monk further, whose own answers still left him unsatisfied, until the boy himself explained its meaning so wonderfully that the monk, astonished, praised the child as no ordinary being. The boy then returned to his cattle, only to find them scattered into the mountains; tracking them, he was killed by a tiger.

He was reborn as the son of an elder's foremost wife, who, from the moment of her pregnancy, spoke the Perfection of Wisdom aloud from dawn to dusk without ceasing. Her household, unfamiliar with the Dharma, suspected her of madness or possession by some ghost, and, unable to divine the cause, grew anxious throughout. The same monk, entering the city to beg, passed the elder's own gate and, hearing the same wondrous recitation from within, asked who spoke it — and, told it was thought to be the wife's own ghost-sickness, explained that this was no sickness at all, but a profound and reasoned teaching, and that her unborn child must himself be a disciple of the Buddha. Reassured, the elder kept the monk and fed him until, in time, the wife gave birth to a son without even the usual signs of labor — the child himself, at the moment of birth, joining his palms and kneeling to recite the Perfection of Wisdom, while his mother, once delivered, remembered nothing of it at all, as though waking from a dream.

The elder gathered the sangha to witness the child speak the sūtra without hindrance, and, asking what this meant, was told by the monk that this was truly a disciple of the Buddha, not to be feared but raised with care, for he would grow to become a teacher to all beings, from whom even they themselves would learn. By seven, the child already understood the Perfection of Wisdom's subtlest meaning beyond compare, and monks everywhere came to study under him, correcting and completing whatever errors or omissions they found in the scriptures under his guidance. Wherever the child went, he converted others to the Great Vehicle; the elder's own household of five hundred, young and old, all took up the great resolve through him and undertook the Buddha's own work; throughout the city and marketplace, he roused the unsurpassed resolve in eighty-four thousand people, and gained five hundred formal disciples, while the monks who heard him all turned their own resolve toward the Great Vehicle and attained the pure Dharma-eye. The Buddha told Ānanda that this child was his own former self, and that the monk was the Buddha Kāśyapa (Source: Sūtra of the Child Who Understood the Dharma the Moment He Heard It).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The cowherd's death, killed by a tiger midway through the story, is treated by the narrative as barely an interruption — the very same understanding he had just reached as a seven-year-old herder resumes, unbroken and already fluent, in the next womb he enters, before he has even been born the second time. Where fascicle 8 closed on a bodhisattva's own recurring struggle against a temptation that kept reappearing in new disguises, fascicle 9 closes on the opposite image: an insight, once genuinely attained, that death itself cannot interrupt. Read against this fascicle's larger theme of bodhisattvas acting outward on others' behalf, the final entry adds a quieter coda — sometimes the most far-reaching teaching a bodhisattva gives is simply the demonstration, in his own person, of what a mind actually carries across a death.
