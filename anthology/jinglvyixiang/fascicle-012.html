Monastic Bodhisattva-Monks

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 12 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Monastic Bodhisattva-Monks, Part 1" (出家菩薩僧部第一) — nine entries turning from lay bodhisattvas toward those who have gone forth: a slandered monk who proves his own purity by levitation, a child converted through an inexhaustible bowl of food, Gaṅgā who sells himself into servitude to feed a beggar-sage, three separate women who each transform into a man mid-sentence in order to become monks, the monk Mahallaka secretly guided in his recitation by Samantabhadra himself, Sumedha's five dreams explained by the Buddha, and a monk whose compassion for a doomed puppy sets both of them irreversibly on the path to buddhahood.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 12 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 12 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 12 opens a new topical section — bodhisattvas who have specifically gone forth into monastic life, as distinct from the lay bodhisattvas of fascicles 8 through 11. It carries nine entries, a noticeably shorter fascicle than its immediate predecessors, three of them variations on the same striking motif: a woman transforming into a man mid-sentence in order to take up the bodhisattva path. Entries are grouped here into thematic clusters, with those three non-adjacent entries brought together despite their separation in the original text, while every entry's full text remains intact.

1. Vimalā's Trial by Slander

Five seers, seeing a woman step briefly into a monk's cave to escape a storm, condemn him at once as impure — and are made to prostrate themselves in terror the moment his own body rises into the air, proof by their own scriptures that no desire-stained man could ever fly.

Chinese Source

昔拘樓秦佛時。有一比丘。名曰無垢。處於閑居。國界山窟。去彼不遠。有五神仙。有一女人道遇大雨。入比丘窟雨霽出去。時五仙人見之。各各言曰。比丘姦穢。無垢聞之。即自踊身在于虛空。去地四丈九尺。諸仙見之飛虛空中。各曰。如吾經典所記。染欲塵者則不得飛。便五體投地伏首誣橫。假使比丘不現神變。其五仙王墮大地獄。時無垢比丘。今慈氏菩薩也(出慧上菩薩經上卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, in the time of the Buddha Krakucchanda, there lived a monk named Vimala, "Stainless," dwelling in solitary retreat in a mountain cave within the kingdom's own borders. Not far away lived five seers with supernormal powers. One day a woman, caught on the road by a heavy rain, took shelter in the monk's own cave, and, once the rain cleared, went on her way. The five seers, seeing this, each declared that the monk must be guilty of some illicit affair.

Hearing this, Vimala rose bodily into the air, hovering four zhang nine chi above the ground — and the seers, seeing him fly, each reflected that, according to their own scriptures, one stained by desire could never fly, and so threw themselves to the ground, prostrate, confessing their own false accusation, for had the monk not displayed this very miracle, the five seer-kings would themselves have fallen into the great hells for their slander. That monk Vimala is now the bodhisattva Maitreya (Source: Sūtra of the Bodhisattva Wisdom-Superior, upper fascicle).

2. Prajñārāja's Inexhaustible Bowl

A child follows a monk for the taste of a honeyed rice-ball, ends up offering his own bowl of food to the Buddha, and watches it feed eighty-four thousand śrāvakas and twelve hundred million bodhisattvas for seven unbroken days without ever running low.

Chinese Source

過去有佛。名莫能勝。有一比丘。名曰慧王。平旦分衛得百味飯若干種食。路有尊者子。名離垢臂。為乳母所抱。遙見比丘。下乳母抱。尋隨比丘從求飯食。於時比丘與其蜜揣。幼童即食知其甘美。遂隨比丘蜜揣欲盡。比丘復授。轉至佛所。稽首足下。慧王以所得食。授與幼童令其上佛。使發道意。佛尋受之已滿佛鉢。食不減損。次與聲聞八萬四千。菩薩十二億。佛及聖眾。皆悉充飽。如是之供至于七日。飯則如故亦不損減。幼童歡喜說頌讚佛。即發無上正真法意。時其父母求索其子。便詣佛所。幼童問訊偈讚父母。於時幼童。化其父母及五百人。悉令志求無上正真法。即皆棄家而為沙門。行菩薩道自致得佛。時慧王者即是溥首童真也。其離垢臂者吾身是也(出往古造行經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, under a buddha named Aparājita, "Unconquerable," there lived a monk named Prajñārāja, "King of Wisdom," who, begging at dawn, received a bowl of a hundred delicacies. Along the road, an elder's son named Vimalabāhu, "Stainless Arm," being carried by his own wet nurse, saw the monk from afar and had himself set down, following him to beg for food; the monk gave him a honeyed rice-ball, whose sweetness so delighted the child that he followed the monk for more even as the ball was nearly finished, the monk giving him another, until they reached the Buddha himself, before whom the child bowed.

Prajñārāja then had the child offer his own received food to the Buddha, that he might give rise to the resolve for the path — and the Buddha, accepting it, filled his own bowl entirely, the food never diminishing, feeding next eighty-four thousand śrāvakas and twelve hundred million bodhisattvas, the Buddha and the whole assembly satisfied, and this same offering continued for seven full days without the food ever running short.

The delighted child spoke verses in praise of the Buddha and gave rise at once to the resolve for unsurpassed, true awakening. When his parents came searching for him and found him before the Buddha, the child greeted them with a verse of his own, then converted both his parents and five hundred others besides, all of them resolving to seek the unsurpassed, true Dharma and going forth from their homes together to become monks, practicing the bodhisattva path until they themselves attained buddhahood. That monk Prajñārāja was none other than the youth Mañjuśrī himself; that child Vimalabāhu was myself (Source: Sūtra of Conduct in Ages Past).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is, in essentials, the very story already told in fascicle 9's second entry — a monk named Prajñārāja, identified as Mañjuśrī, offers an inexhaustible bowl through a child drawn in by its sweetness, that child turning out to be the Buddha's own former self — down to matching character names one syllable apart (Vimalarāja there, Vimalabāhu here) and near-identical buddha epithets. The two versions differ chiefly in their frame: there, it answers two hundred wavering devas' doubts about their own resolve; here, it stands as this fascicle's own opening claim about what draws a child toward monastic life in the first place. The compendium's compilers again let two independently transmitted tellings of one story stand at a distance from each other rather than merging them.

3. Gaṅgā Sells Himself to Feed a Beggar-Sage

A monk, moved by one exchange about the nature of truth, sells his own body into a stranger's service for five coins purely to fund a proper meal for the beggar who taught it to him.

Chinese Source

時有一菩薩。名曰上首。作一乞士。入城乞食。有一比丘。名曰恒伽。謂乞士言。汝從何來。答言。吾從真實中來。恒伽問言。何謂為真實。答曰。寂滅相故名為真實。恒伽曰。當於何求實法。答曰。當於六波羅蜜中求。恒伽歡喜。禮上首足下。而便問言。當以何食供養此人。上首答言。當以須陀味。恒伽即詣都市。而自唱言。吾欲賣身。誰欲須者。有一居士。名毘奴律。即問我言。吾欲買之。汝索何等。恒伽報言索。須陀那羅。曰。當索幾枚。恒伽報言。欲須五枚。居士五錢買此道人以充使。恒伽白大家言。我身屬汝。假我七日。欲供養上首比丘。居士告恒伽言。吾當將汝示於宅舍。放汝令還。時恒伽見舍宅已涉路而還。見此上首。乞食未得。即將上首。到都市中。買百味飲食。將到一寺。寺名四王。設施床座燒香散花。下種種飲食(出大方等陀羅尼經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There once lived a bodhisattva named Agrayāśas, "Foremost Renown," who, as a mendicant, entered a city to beg. A monk named Gaṅgā asked him where he had come from, and he answered: "From the truth itself." Asked what he meant by "the truth," he answered that the mark of quiescence itself is called truth. Gaṅgā asked where such true dharma might be sought, and was told: within the six perfections. Delighted, Gaṅgā bowed at Agrayāśas's own feet and asked what food he ought to offer this man. Agrayāśas answered: a taste of the immortal nectar itself.

Gaṅgā at once went to the marketplace and called out that he wished to sell himself — whoever wanted him. A householder named Vinīlaka asked what price he sought, and Gaṅgā answered he sought only five "ambrosia-coins." Asked how many, he said five would do, and the householder bought this ascetic for five coins to serve him. Gaṅgā told his new master that though his body now belonged to him, he asked leave of seven days to make an offering to the monk Agrayāśas. The householder agreed to show him his own household duties first, then release him to go.

Having seen the household, Gaṅgā set out on the road and found Agrayāśas still without alms, and, taking him along, went to the marketplace and bought a hundred delicacies, bringing them to a monastery named Four Kings, where he set out seats, burned incense, scattered flowers, and laid out food of every kind (Source: Vaipulya Dhāraṇī Sūtra, fascicle 1).

4, 7–8. Three Women Who Become Men

Three separate women — an eight-year-old prodigy, a merchant's daughter defying Māra's own arguments, and a girl who reasons and speaks from inside her mother's womb — each transform into a man mid-sentence the instant they resolve to take up the bodhisattva path, proving in the same gesture that the Dharma itself has no fixed sex to begin with.

Chinese Source
須摩提始是八歲女轉身為男出家說法四

須摩提白佛言。世尊。所說菩薩四十事。我當奉行。令不缺減。時長老目連問。此四十事大士所行。汝小女人何能辦之。答言。審實能行。若不信者當使三千大千國土皆當為我六反震動。雨於天花。諸音樂器不鼓自鳴。應時如言。女曰。證我至誠。若我後得佛無有虛者。其在會眾悉作金色。尋如其語色如黃金。目連白佛言。初發大意為菩薩者。我為作禮。所以者何。八歲女子感應如此。豈況高士摩訶薩乎。文殊師利問言。云何不轉女人身。須摩提報言。於是無所得。所以者何。法無男女。今者我當斷仁所疑。須摩提言。令我不久當得正覺。我今便當變為男子。適作是語。即成男身。頭髮自墮袈裟在體。便作沙彌。又言。我作佛時。使我國中莫有三事。一者魔事。二者泥犁。三者女態。若我至誠。我身當如三十沙門。適作是語。形體顏色如年三十。復謂文殊師利言。我作佛時。令我國人皆作金色。地及城郭有七寶樹。寶池寶華不多不少。悉皆亭等。又言諸在會者當作金色。應時眾會皆並金容。時持地神即從地出。化作天身。舉聲稱揚。歎須摩提。三言之德。不久作佛(出佛說須摩提菩薩經)。

Ru-Yi Translation
Sumatī, the Eight-Year-Old Who Became a Monk

Sumatī said to the Buddha: World-Honored One, the forty practices of a bodhisattva you have taught, I shall uphold without the slightest omission. The elder Maudgalyāyana asked: these forty practices are the great being's own conduct — how could you, a mere young girl, accomplish them? She answered that she truly could, and that, should he doubt it, she would cause the whole trichiliocosm to shake six ways, heavenly flowers to rain down, and every musical instrument to sound of itself without being played — and, as she spoke, it happened exactly so. She said further: "As proof of my own sincerity, if I shall indeed attain buddhahood without falsehood, let everyone in this assembly turn the color of gold" — and, as she spoke, the whole assembly turned golden at once.

Maudgalyāyana told the Buddha that he now bowed before anyone who had merely first given rise to the great resolve to become a bodhisattva, for if an eight-year-old girl could manifest such power, how much more a truly great being. Mañjuśrī then asked her why she did not simply transform her own female body — and Sumatī answered that there was nothing to be gained by it, since the Dharma itself knows neither male nor female, but that she would now resolve his own doubt regardless: "May I soon attain true awakening — I shall now become a man" — and, as she spoke these very words, her body transformed into a man's, her hair falling away of itself, a monastic robe appearing upon her body, and she became a novice monk at once.

She continued: "When I become a buddha, let my own land know none of three things — the workings of Māra, the hells, and the very trace of femininity; and, as proof of my own sincerity, let my body now appear as that of a thirty-year-old monk" — and, as she spoke, her own form and complexion became exactly that of a man of thirty. She said further to Mañjuśrī: "When I become a buddha, let my land's own people all turn the color of gold, its ground and cities lined with trees of the seven treasures, its jeweled ponds and lotuses neither too many nor too few, but perfectly ordered" — and, declaring that everyone present should likewise turn golden, the whole assembly turned to gold at once. At this the earth-holding spirit rose up from the ground, manifesting a heavenly form, and proclaimed aloud in praise of Sumatī's own threefold vow, declaring she would soon attain buddhahood (Source: Sūtra of the Bodhisattva Sumatī, spoken by the Buddha).

女人高樓見佛化成男身出家利益七

須福長者有女。名曰龍施。厥年十四。時在浴室澡浴塗香著好衣畢。佛與眷屬放眉間毫相之光。照七重門內。令殿舍皆明。女見光明踰於日月。心知非恒。便走上七重樓上。東向見佛在門外住。女大歡喜。則自念言。今得施佛及眾弟子。以發意作菩薩行願。令我後得道如佛。魔見女發大意心為不樂。即下化作女父。謂龍施言。汝所念大重。佛道難得。今世幸有佛。不如求羅漢。且俱度世泥洹無異。龍施對曰。不如父言。佛智廣大度人無極。羅漢智少如一塵耳。有何高仁樂於小者。魔復言。未聞女人作轉輪王。況乃得作佛。不如求羅漢早取泥洹去。龍施報言。我亦聞。女人不得作轉輪王。不得作佛。我當精進轉此女人作男子身。蓋聞。天下行菩薩法。億劫不懈者。後得作佛。魔見女意不轉。益以愁毒。更作急教言。若作菩薩行者。不貪世間。不惜壽命。今汝精進。能從樓上自投地者。後可得作佛。龍施心念。我今見佛。貪菩薩道。父又教以精進。身可得佛。我何惜此危脆之命。即住欄邊。向佛叉手言。自歸於天中天。以一切智。知我所求。請棄軀命不捨菩薩。以身施佛。願如散花。便縱身自投樓下。未及至地。化成男子。佛笑。五色光從口中出照一佛剎。還繞佛身三匝從頂上入。汝見此女自投空中化成男子不。是女乃前世時。已事萬佛。後當供養恒沙未來佛。至七億六千萬劫當得作佛。號曰名上。其壽一劫。般泥洹後。經道興盛。半劫乃滅。於是龍施身住佛前報父母言。願放捨我得作沙門。父母即聽。中外眷屬皆發無上道意(出龍施女經)。

Nāgadattā's Leap from the Tower

The elder Sudhana had a daughter named Nāgadattā, "Given by the Nāga," fourteen years old. Having just bathed, anointed herself with fragrant oil, and dressed in fine clothes, she saw the Buddha, together with his own retinue, emit light from the tuft between his brows, illuminating the sevenfold gate and the whole hall within. Seeing this light outshine even the sun and moon, and knowing it no ordinary thing, she ran up to the topmost of her house's seven stories and, facing east, saw the Buddha standing outside the gate. Overjoyed, she resolved at once to make offerings to the Buddha and his disciples, giving rise to the bodhisattva's own resolve and vowing to attain the very same awakening as the Buddha himself.

Māra, seeing the girl give rise to so great a resolve, was displeased, and descended, transforming into her own father, to tell Nāgadattā: "What you now intend is far too weighty a burden — the Buddha's own path is exceedingly hard to attain. Fortunate as we are to have a buddha in this age, it would be better to seek arhatship instead; both paths lead equally to nirvāṇa in the end." Nāgadattā answered: "Not so, father — the Buddha's own wisdom is vast and delivers beings without limit, while an arhat's wisdom is but a mote of dust by comparison; what noble person would settle for the lesser path?" Māra pressed further: "I have never heard of a woman becoming a wheel-turning king, let alone a buddha — better to seek arhatship and attain nirvāṇa quickly." Nāgadattā answered: "I too have heard that a woman cannot become a wheel-turning king or a buddha — which is exactly why I shall diligently transform this very female body into a man's, for I have heard that anyone who practices the bodhisattva's own teaching throughout the world, tirelessly across a hundred million kalpas, thereby attains buddhahood."

Seeing her resolve unshaken, Māra, ever more distressed, pressed his final, urgent instruction: "One who truly practices the bodhisattva path covets nothing of this world and cherishes not even his own life — if you are truly so diligent, throw yourself down from this very tower to the ground below, and you shall then attain buddhahood." Nāgadattā reflected: "I have now seen the Buddha and covet the bodhisattva path; my own father has just taught me diligence, that I might attain buddhahood through this very body — why then should I cling to this fragile, perishable life?" She went at once to the balcony's edge and, joining her palms toward the Buddha, declared: "I take refuge in the god of gods, whose all-knowing wisdom already knows what I seek; I now give up this body and life, unwilling to abandon the bodhisattva path, offering my own body to the Buddha as freely as a scattered flower" — and threw herself from the tower.

Before she reached the ground, she transformed into a man. The Buddha smiled, and five-colored light streamed from his mouth, illuminating an entire buddha-land before circling his own body three times and entering through the crown of his head. He said: "Did you see this woman throw herself into the air and transform into a man? In past lives she has already served ten thousand buddhas, and will go on to make offerings to buddhas of the future as numerous as the Ganges' own sands, until, seven hundred sixty million kalpas hence, she shall attain buddhahood herself, under the name Nāmottama, her own lifespan a full kalpa; after her nirvāṇa, her own teaching shall flourish for half a kalpa more before fading." Nāgadattā, now standing before the Buddha in male form, told her parents: "I wish now to be allowed to become a monk" — and her parents consented, while her entire extended household, within and without, gave rise to the resolve for the unsurpassed path (Source: Sūtra of the Girl Nāgadattā).

女人在胎聽法轉身為丈夫出家修道八

佛在羅閱祇。菩薩及四部大會。佛說法。有迦羅婦懷妊在座。腹中懷子。叉手聽經。佛欲使眾會見之。便見大光明照迦羅婦坐。眾人皆見腹中女叉手聽經。猶如照鏡。佛持八種聲。問腹中女言汝以何故叉手聽經。用佛威神即答佛言。以世間人皆行十惡。我欲令行十善。又以世人生死不絕。又世間人不孝從父母。不供養沙門婆羅門道人。是故叉手聽經。時女說是語竟便生。譬如太子從右脇生。地為六反震動。虛空中有自然天樂。雨天眾花。千葉蓮花大如車輪。以寶作莖。狀如青瑠璃。女即坐蓮花上。帝釋持天衣。與女著之。女報言。汝為羅漢。我為菩薩。汝非我輩。不與我同類。我自有衣。舍利弗白佛。此女為從何國來。當送衣也。佛言。此女從東南方佛剎清淨國來。去此十萬佛剎。本國衣便自然在虛空中來肅肅有聲。女見衣來便著之。當得五通。又女本國人盡得五通。女得衣著訖。便從蓮華上下行至佛前。女一舉足。地為六反震動。頭面著地為佛作禮。三言南無佛。便長跪白佛。今坐中大有諸迦羅婦。願佛為說經。令得男子身。佛言。我亦不使汝作男子。亦不使汝作女人。皆自從身行得耳。佛言。有一事可疾得男子。何等為一。發心為菩薩道。又女人身當內自觀。譬如機關。骨節相拄。但筋皮在上。女人常畏人。譬如蚖蛇蝦蟇。不敢晝出。時坐中迦羅婦七十五人。聞佛說經歡喜踊躍。前以頭面著地。為佛作禮白佛言。我願發菩薩心作男子。我若不得男子身。我終不起。時七十五迦羅越。從舍衛國來至佛所。見諸婦皆在佛前。便心念言。已失我曹婦。便問舍利弗。此諸女人是我曹婦。何為是間。舍利弗答言。欲作比丘尼。卿當聽不。迦羅越答言。先使我曹作比丘。舍利弗白佛言。是七十五迦羅越。皆欲作比丘。佛呼。善男子來。皆作比丘。頭髮自然墮。袈裟便來著身。手持應器。皆前為佛作禮。時七十五婦。各脫珠環皆以散佛上。便自然虛空中。化作七十五交露珠瓔珞。帳中有七寶床。上有座。佛邊有無數菩薩聽經。七十五婦人。見是變化。皆大歡喜即用佛威神飛住虛空。自然有花雨散佛上。從虛空中來下。便得男子身。前白佛言。我願作比丘。佛語彌勒菩薩。將去授戒。彌勒菩薩即授戒。作比丘僧。女自然有化花蓋七重莖如蓮華。即持與母言。佛是天上天下度人之師。母以花蓋上佛。是天上天下之師蓋上之。後母亦當為天下之蓋。女語母言。今當發菩薩心。母答女言。我始懷汝時。於夢中常見佛及法比丘僧。無三毒心。身體安隱。知我腹中子為是菩薩摩訶薩。以是安隱。時發菩薩心。以母得華蓋。便持上佛。地為六反震動。佛語舍利弗。四天下星宿。尚可知數。是女前後所度父母。不可知數(出胎中女聽經)。

The Girl Who Heard the Dharma in the Womb

The Buddha dwelt in Rājagṛha, teaching the Dharma before a great assembly of bodhisattvas and the fourfold community. Among them sat a woman of the Kalandaka clan, pregnant, her unborn child within her joining its own hands in reverence to listen to the teaching. Wishing the assembly to see this, the Buddha emitted a great light illuminating the woman's own seated form, so that everyone present saw, as though through a mirror, the unborn girl within her womb, hands joined in reverent attention. The Buddha, in his own eightfold voice, asked the unborn girl why she listened with such reverence — and she, through the Buddha's own power, answered: "Because the people of this world all commit the ten evils, I wish to lead them instead to the ten good deeds; because the people of this world remain endlessly bound to birth and death; and because the people of this world fail to honor their own parents or support monks, brahmins, and seekers of the path — for these reasons I listen with joined hands."

The moment she finished speaking, she was born — as the crown prince himself was born from his mother's right side — and the earth shook six ways, heavenly music sounded of itself in the sky, and heavenly flowers rained down, among them a thousand-petaled lotus as large as a cart-wheel, its stem of jeweled lapis lazuli, upon which the girl sat at once. Śakra, bearing heavenly garments, came to clothe her — but she answered: "You are an arhat; I am a bodhisattva; you are not of my own kind, and I have my own garments already." Śāriputra asked the Buddha what country this girl had come from, that garments might be sent for her — and the Buddha answered that she had come from a pure buddha-land to the southeast, a hundred thousand buddha-lands distant, and that her own country's own garments would come of themselves. At once, garments arrived through the air with a soft, rustling sound; seeing them, she put them on, and, through them, attained the five supernormal powers, just as everyone in her own homeland already possessed them.

Once dressed, she descended from the lotus and walked before the Buddha; with each step the earth shook six ways, and, reaching him, she bowed her head to the ground in homage, uttering three times, "Homage to the Buddha," then knelt and said: "There are, in this present assembly, many women of the Kalandaka clan — I beg the World-Honored One to teach the Dharma, that they too might obtain male bodies." The Buddha answered: "I do not myself make you into a man, nor do I make you into a woman — this comes entirely from one's own conduct. There is, however, one thing that can swiftly bring about a male body: giving rise to the resolve for the bodhisattva path. A woman's own body, moreover, ought to be examined within — it is like a machine, bones and joints propped one against another, with only muscle and skin laid over them; and a woman is ever fearful of others, hiding like a lizard, a snake, or a toad, never daring to venture out in daylight."

Hearing this teaching, the seventy-five women of the Kalandaka clan then present rejoiced and leapt up, bowing their heads to the ground before the Buddha, and declared: "We vow to give rise to the bodhisattva's own resolve and become men — and if we do not attain male bodies, we shall not rise from this spot." At that moment seventy-five men of the Kalandaka clan, having come from Śrāvastī, arrived and, seeing their own wives all gathered before the Buddha, wondered aloud to Śāriputra what this meant, having thought they had lost their own wives — and Śāriputra told them these women now wished to become nuns, and asked if they consented. The husbands answered that they themselves wished first to become monks. Śāriputra reported to the Buddha that these seventy-five men all wished to become monks, and the Buddha called out, "Come, good men," whereupon they all became monks at once, their hair falling away of itself, monastic robes appearing on their bodies, alms bowls in their hands, and they bowed before the Buddha.

The seventy-five wives then removed their own pearl bracelets and scattered them over the Buddha, which transformed of themselves in the sky into seventy-five strings of dew-jeweled pearls forming a canopy, beneath which stood a jeweled couch of the seven treasures with a seat upon it, beside which countless bodhisattvas sat listening to the Dharma. Seeing this transformation, the seventy-five women rejoiced greatly and, through the Buddha's own power, rose into the sky, flowers raining down of themselves over the Buddha — and, descending from the sky, they obtained male bodies at once, coming before the Buddha to declare: "We wish to become monks." The Buddha told the bodhisattva Maitreya to take them and confer the precepts, which Maitreya did, and they became monks.

One of these transformed women had a canopy of flowers appear of itself, sevenfold, its stem shaped like a lotus, which she took and gave to her own mother, saying: "The Buddha is the teacher who delivers beings throughout heaven and earth" — and the mother offered this canopy to the Buddha, the teacher of heaven and earth, in turn — for, the daughter told her, her mother too would one day become a canopy sheltering the whole world, and should now give rise to the bodhisattva's own resolve. The mother answered her: "From the very moment I first conceived you, I constantly dreamed of the Buddha, the Dharma, and the monastic sangha, free of the three poisons, my body at peace throughout — and I knew then that the child in my womb was a great bodhisattva-mahāsattva, which is why I remained so at ease." Once the mother received the flower canopy and offered it to the Buddha, the earth shook six ways. The Buddha told Śāriputra: the very stars of the four continents can be counted, yet the parents this girl has delivered, in this life and lifetimes past and future, cannot be counted at all (Source: Sūtra of the Girl in the Womb Who Heard the Dharma).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

These three stories share a single striking motif, told with real variation each time: Sumatī transforms out of simple resolve, without argument, having already dismissed the very premise that a body's sex matters to the Dharma; Nāgadattā transforms only after out-arguing Māra himself through a series of escalating objections, her own transformation the reward for having called his bluff on a suicidal leap; and the unborn girl arrives already reasoning and speaking before her own birth, needing no transformation scene at all because she was never treated as bound by an ordinary infant's incapacity in the first place. Read together, the compendium seems less interested in explaining how or why such transformations occur than in establishing, through three independent tellings, that a woman's own resolve is never in question — only the social expectation that her body must first change to match it.

5. Mahallaka's Secret Teacher

A king honors a monk for his supposedly extensive learning, only to discover the monk barely reads at all — and, investigating further, finds him secretly guided in his recitation by Samantabhadra himself, who vanishes the instant anyone but the monk draws near.

Chinese Source

摩訶盧比丘。國王謂其大讀摩訶乘。常解髮令其蹈過。又有比丘。乃語王言。此摩訶盧不多讀經。何以供養如是。王言。我日夜欲見此比丘。即往見在窟中讀法花經。見一金色光明。人騎白象合手供養。王來轉迫便滅不現。即問。大德。我來金光明人滅何也。比丘言。此即遍吉菩薩(法華經中普賢是也)來教導我誦於此經(出大智論第九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The monk Mahallaka was so honored by the king, on the belief that he recited the Mahāyāna sūtras extensively, that the king would let down his own hair for the monk to walk across in reverence. Another monk protested to the king that Mahallaka in fact recited very little, and asked why he received such honor. The king, wishing to see for himself, went day and night until he found Mahallaka in his own cave, reciting the Lotus Sūtra — and saw there a golden radiance, a figure riding a white elephant, palms joined in reverent offering. As the king drew closer, the vision vanished. He asked the venerable monk why the golden light and its rider had disappeared upon his own approach, and Mahallaka answered: "That was the bodhisattva Viśvabhadra" — known in the Lotus Sūtra as Samantabhadra — "who has been teaching and guiding me in reciting this very sūtra" (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 9).

6. Sumedha's Five Dreams

An ascetic's five strange dreams — lying on the ocean, pillowed on a mountain, holding the sun and moon in each hand — turn out, verse by verse, to be an exact prophecy of the bodhisattva path he has not yet consciously chosen.

Chinese Source

善慧比丘。白普光如來言。我昔日在深山中。得五種夢。一者夢臥大海。二者夢枕須彌。三者夢海中一切眾生入我身內。四者夢手執日。五者夢手執月。唯願世尊為我解說。時佛答曰。夢臥大海者。汝身即時在於生死大海之中。夢枕須彌者。出於生死得涅槃相。夢大海中一切眾生入身內者。當於生死大海。為諸眾生作歸依處。夢手執日者。智慧光明普照法界。夢手執月者。以方便智入於生死。以清涼法化導眾生(出過去現在因果經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The monk Sumedha said to the Tathāgata Samantaprabha, "Universal Light": "Long ago, deep in the mountains, I had five dreams — first, that I lay upon the great ocean; second, that I pillowed my head on Mount Sumeru; third, that every being in the great ocean entered into my own body; fourth, that I held the sun in my hand; and fifth, that I held the moon in my hand. I beg the World-Honored One to explain their meaning." The Buddha answered: to dream of lying upon the great ocean means that your own body now lies within the great ocean of birth and death; to dream of pillowing your head on Sumeru means you shall emerge from birth and death and attain the very mark of nirvāṇa; to dream of every being in the great ocean entering your own body means that, within that same great ocean of birth and death, you shall become a refuge for all beings; to dream of holding the sun means your own wisdom's light shall illuminate the whole Dharma-realm; and to dream of holding the moon means that, through skillful wisdom, you shall enter birth and death yourself, guiding beings with a cooling, refreshing teaching (Source: Sūtra of Past and Present Causes and Effects, fascicle 1).

9. The Monk, the Puppy, and Non-Retrogression

The fascicle closes with a monk who trades his own meal for a hunter's condemned puppy, blessing it in tears as he does — a single act of compassion that reaches across two lifetimes to bring both the reborn puppy and the monk himself to the stage of non-retrogression.

Chinese Source

昔有一國。穀米踊貴。人民飢餓。時有沙門。入城分衛。無所一獲。次至長者大豪貴門。得麁惡飯。適欲出城。門中逢一射獵殺生屠兒。抱一狗子持歸欲殺。見沙門歡喜前為作禮。沙門呪願老壽長生。沙門知有狗子欲殺噉之。問其何所齎。答曰。空行。沙門又問。吾已見殺生之罪。甚為不善。願持我食貿此狗子。令得命濟卿福無量。其人不與。沙門慇懃曉喻請之不肯隨言。沙門又言。設不肯者。可以示我。其人即出。以示沙門。沙門舉飯以飴狗子。以手摩之呪願淚出。卿罪所致得是犬身。不得自在見殺食噉。使爾世世罪滅福生。離狗子身得生為人。所在遇法。狗子得食善心生焉。踊躍歡喜事已將去。歸家殺食。狗子命過。生豪貴大長者家。適生墮地。便有慈心。時彼沙門。分衛次到長者門裏。時長者子見彼沙門。憶識本緣。便前稽首禮沙門足。請前供養百味飲食。前白父母。今我欲逐此大和上。奉受經戒為作弟子。父母愛重不肯聽之。今我一門有汝一子。當以續後。家門之主何因便欲棄我而去。小兒啼泣不肯飲食。不欲聽我便自就死。父母見爾便聽令去。隨師學道。除去鬚髮被三法衣。諷誦佛經。深解其義。便得三昧。立不退轉。開化一切。發大道意。沙門即識宿命。發菩薩心。立不退轉。豈況有人。供事三寶。諷誦大乘(出度脫狗子經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, in a certain kingdom, grain grew scarce and expensive, and the people went hungry. A monk, entering the city to beg, received nothing at all until, reaching the gate of a wealthy elder's household, he was given only coarse, poor food. As he was about to leave the city, he met a hunter-butcher carrying home a puppy to kill, who, seeing the monk, joyfully bowed before him; the monk blessed him with a wish for long life, then, learning the man carried a puppy meant for slaughter, asked what he was bringing home. Told plainly, the monk said: "I have already seen the sin of killing, and know it to be a great evil — please let me trade my own food for this puppy's life, that it might be spared, and you shall gain immeasurable merit." The man refused at first, and the monk pleaded earnestly, but he would not agree — until the monk asked only to see the puppy, which the man then showed him.

The monk fed the puppy from his own bowl, stroking it and blessing it, weeping as he did: "Your own past sin has brought you to this dog's body, unable even to save yourself from being killed and eaten — may this very suffering, across all your future lives, exhaust that sin and give rise to merit instead, that you be freed from this canine body and reborn as a human, wherever the Dharma may be found." The puppy, eating, felt a wholesome joy arise within it, and the man, satisfied, took it home and killed and ate it as planned.

The puppy's life ended there, and it was reborn into a wealthy, eminent household — and, the moment it touched the ground at birth, a compassionate heart was already present within it. That same monk, begging again in his usual round, came once more to this elder's own gate — and the elder's son, recognizing him at once from that former karmic bond, came forward and bowed at the monk's feet, inviting him in and offering a hundred delicacies. He then told his own parents: "I wish now to follow this great teacher, receive his teaching and precepts, and become his disciple." His parents, cherishing their only son, refused to allow it, protesting that their whole family line depended on this one child to carry it forward — how could he abandon them so? The boy wept and refused to eat, declaring that if they would not permit him, he would simply starve himself to death. Seeing this, his parents relented and let him go, to study the path under this teacher.

He shaved his own head, donned the three monastic robes, recited the Buddha's own sūtras, and, deeply understanding their meaning, attained samādhi and reached the stage of non-retrogression, teaching and converting all he met, rousing in them the great resolve. The monk himself, recognizing this same former life, gave rise to the bodhisattva's own resolve and likewise attained non-retrogression. How much more, then, should any person who serves the Three Jewels and recites the Great Vehicle's own teachings attain the same (Source: Sūtra of the Rescued Puppy).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The monk's compassion here notably fails on its own immediate terms — his offer is refused, the puppy is killed and eaten exactly as planned, and nothing about the outcome he wanted actually happens. What survives instead is the blessing itself, spoken in tears over a creature he could not save, which turns out to matter more than the rescue would have: the puppy is reborn not because it escaped death, but because of the words spoken as it died. Closing this fascicle on monastic bodhisattvas with a monk whose own path to non-retrogression begins with a failed rescue rather than a successful one suggests the compendium's own closing claim — that the sincerity of an act of compassion is not measured by whether it succeeds.
