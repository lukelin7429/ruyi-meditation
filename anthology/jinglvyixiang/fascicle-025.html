Kings on the Bodhisattva Path

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 25 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Kings on the Bodhisattva Path, Part 2" (行菩薩道上諸國王部第二) — eleven entries of kings whose generosity moves past ordinary charity into total self-sacrifice: bodies carved into a thousand lamps for a single verse, flesh weighed against a dove on a scale, blood offered to starving demons, and, in the fascicle's most severe entry, a king's entire family sold into slavery as the very test of a single vow to give everything asked of him. Several entries here share the same underlying motif — a king's own body demanded as the price of hearing the Dharma — under different names and from different source texts, and we preserve each telling independently rather than treating any one as the "true" version.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 25 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 25 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 25 continues the compendium's section on kings, now specifically those said to be practicing the bodhisattva path — rulers whose generosity is measured not in ordinary charity but in total, often violent self-sacrifice. Entries 1 and 2 share strong structural similarities with fascicle 24's entry 6 (a king carving his own body into a thousand lamps for a single verse), yet come from a different source text under different royal names, and we preserve all three as independent tellings of a recurring devotional motif rather than variants of one another. This fascicle's difficult material — self-mutilation, human sacrifice offered and demanded, a family sold into slavery — is translated faithfully throughout.

1. A Thousand Lamps for One Verse, Again

A king, unsatisfied that mere material prosperity has not fully served his own people, offers anything at all for a single teaching of true Dharma — and, when a wandering brahmin demands a thousand lamps carved into his own living flesh as the price, submits without hesitation, over his entire kingdom's own grief-stricken protest.

Chinese Source

昔有閻浮提王。名虔闍尼婆梨。典領八萬四千聚落。慈悲一切穀米豐賤。各得安樂而未盡我心。當求妙法以相利益。宣令一切誰有妙法。為我說者隨所欲得。有婆羅門。名勞度差。云我有法。王迎而禮之白言。願大師闡法令聞。勞度差曰。大王。今日能於身上剜然千燈用供養者。乃相為說。王宣命閻浮提內。却後七日剜身然燈。人民懷愁來詣王所。有命依王如嬰兒依母。王若崩背何所親怙。云何為此一婆羅門棄於一切。汝等慎勿却我無上道心。吾為是事誓求作佛。後成佛時必先度汝。眾人啼哭投地懊惱。即便剜身布諸脂炷。白大師言。哀矜說法。然後然燈。我脫命斷不及聞之。勞度差言。常者皆盡　高者亦墮　合會有離　生者有死。王歡喜無量。便命然燈。所求之法為成佛道。當以慧明照悟眾人。發此誓時天地大動。上至淨居皆亦震搖。見此大士不顧軀命。僉然俱下側塞虛空。啼哭流淚猶如盛雨。天帝言曰。痛惱如此心不悔耶。答曰不悔。因立誓言。若我始終心不悔者。願皆平復應念平復。時王者即佛身是(出賢愚經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a king of Jambudvīpa named Qiánshénípólí, who ruled over eighty-four thousand villages, compassionate toward all beings, whose land enjoyed abundant, cheap grain, so that everyone lived in ease — yet this did not fully satisfy the king's own heart. He resolved: I must seek out the sublime Dharma, that I might truly benefit others — and proclaimed to all: whoever possesses the sublime Dharma, and will teach it to me, shall receive whatever they desire in return.

There was a brahmin named Láodùchà, who said: I possess this Dharma. The king welcomed him, bowed before him, and said: I beg the great teacher to expound the Dharma, and let me hear it. Láodùchà said: great king, only if you can, this very day, carve a thousand lamps into your own body, and offer them as offering, shall I teach it to you.

The king proclaimed throughout Jambudvīpa: in seven days, I shall carve my own body and light these lamps. The people, grieving, came before the king: our very lives depend upon you, as an infant depends upon its own mother — should you perish, what refuge would remain for us? How could you abandon everyone for the sake of a single brahmin? The king answered: do not, I beg you, obstruct my own resolve toward the unsurpassed path — I make this vow for the sake of this very deed: when I attain buddhahood, I shall deliver you all before anyone else.

The people wept, threw themselves to the ground in anguish. The king, undeterred, carved his own body, and set out the oil-wicks, and said to his great teacher: in your compassion, teach the Dharma now, and only then light the lamps — for, should my own life end too soon, I fear I shall not live to hear it. Láodùchà then spoke this verse:

"All that is constant must, in the end, be exhausted; / all that stands high must, in the end, fall. / What comes together must, in the end, separate; / whatever is born must, in the end, die."

The king, overjoyed beyond measure, ordered the lamps lit at once, saying: the Dharma I have sought is for the sake of attaining buddhahood itself, that I might illuminate and awaken all beings with the light of wisdom.

As he spoke this vow, heaven and earth shook greatly, the tremor reaching even the Pure Abodes above. The devas, seeing this great being give no thought at all to his own body or life, all descended together, crowding the very sky, weeping tears that fell like heavy rain.

The lord of the devas said to him: such pain and suffering as this — does your own heart not regret it? He answered: I do not regret it — and, with this, made a further vow: if my own heart, from beginning to end, holds no regret at all, may my body be restored at once, this very instant. And, in that very moment, his body was restored exactly as it had been before.

That king of that time was the Buddha himself (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the second of three closely related "thousand lamps" episodes in this compendium — following fascicle 24's entry 6, and preceding entry 2 immediately below — each drawn from a different source, naming a different king, yet sharing the same essential shape: a demanding brahmin teacher, a body carved into a thousand lamp-wells, a half-verse or full verse received in exchange, and a body restored whole the moment the king's own resolve proves unshaken. We keep all three as independent tellings, since the compendium's own compilers plainly regarded this as a recurring devotional motif worth documenting from multiple transmitted sources rather than a single fixed story.

2. A Thousand Nails for One Verse

The same brahmin teacher from entry 1 reappears, demanding a different act of self-mutilation entirely — a thousand nails driven into the king's own body — and receives, in exchange, a stark verse on impermanence and non-self that the king accepts as more than worth the price.

Chinese Source

昔於閻浮提。有大國王。名毘楞竭梨。心好妙法。有婆羅門。名勞度差。詣宮門言。我能說法。若能以千釘釘身者。我乃為說。王言。我於生死中殺身無數。或為三毒計集白骨高於須彌流血逾於五湖哭淚多於滄海。唐捐身命未曾為法。今椓釘求道。後成佛時。以智慧劍除汝等結。慎勿遮我無上道心。大眾默然。時勞度差便自說偈。一切所無常　生者所有苦　諸法空無生　實非我所有。即釘釘身。因發願言曰。若我永不悔者。平復如故。身即如本(出賢愚經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, in Jambudvīpa, there was a great king named Pílíngjiélí, whose heart held a great love for the sublime Dharma. A brahmin named Láodùchà came to his palace gate and said: I can teach the Dharma — but only if you will let me drive a thousand nails into your own body shall I teach it.

The king said: across the countless deaths I have already suffered through birth and death, whether reckoning the piles of white bone left by the three poisons, taller than Mount Sumeru, or the blood I have shed, more than five great lakes, or the tears I have wept, more than the vast ocean itself — I have wasted this body and life again and again, yet never once for the sake of the true Dharma. Now, at last, let nails be driven into me in search of the path — for, when I attain buddhahood in time to come, I shall use the sword of wisdom to cut away every one of your own fetters. Take care not to obstruct my own resolve toward the unsurpassed path.

The whole assembly fell silent. Láodùchà then spoke this verse:

"All things alike are impermanent; / whatever is born carries suffering within it. / Every phenomenon is empty, without true arising — / truly, nothing is ever one's own."

The nails were then driven into his body. The king, upon this, made this vow: if my own resolve never wavers at all, let my body be restored exactly as it was — and his body was restored at once, exactly as before (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Placed directly after entry 1 in the source text, this second episode features the very same brahmin antagonist, Láodùchà, demanding a different but equally severe act of bodily sacrifice from a different king — and the compendium's own decision to place these two nearly identical structures back to back, rather than merging them, seems deliberate: read together, they suggest a single recurring teacher-figure whose test remains constant even as the specific form of suffering he demands varies.

3. King Great Radiance Gives His Own Head to a Brahmin

A rival kingdom, envious of King Great Radiance's boundless generosity, hires a brahmin to request the one thing surely too costly to give: the king's own head — and finds, to its own dismay, that the king's resolve to give without limit holds even here, against his ministers' desperate objections and a tree-spirit's own last-second intervention.

Chinese Source

過去有國。名波羅奈。王名曰大光明。心慈一切不逆人意。有一小王常懷惡逆。大王於月齋日。以五百大象載珍寶衣食。著大市中及四城門外。布施一切。時敵國怨家聞。王布施恣前人意。心生嫉妬。即集諸臣。誰能乞大光明王頭。賞金千斤。有一婆羅門言我能。王即資給。婆羅門往到界上。其地六種震動。禽獸四散日月無精星宿失度。赤黑白虹晝夜常現流星崩落。於其國中諸泉浴池而皆枯乾。婆羅門往到城門。時守門神語守門者言。此人大惡。從遠方來欲乞大王頭。汝莫聽入。時婆羅門停滯一七日不能得前。語守門者。我從遠來欲見大王。時守門者即入白王。王聞即出奉迎。如子見父前為作禮問所從來。冒涉塗路得無疲惓。婆羅門言。我在他方聞。王布施不逆人意。名聲遠聞上徹蒼天下徹黃泉。故從遠來欲有所得。王言。我一切施有所求索莫自疑難。婆羅門言。審實爾不。我不用餘物。今欲大祀。從王乞頭。王自思惟。從無始來未曾為法。空受生死勞我精神。今有此身欲求菩提誓及眾生。今不與者違我本心。何緣當得成無上菩提。王言大善。須我撿挍委付國位夫人太子。過於七日當相給與。爾時大王。即入宮中報諸夫人言。有婆羅門。欲乞我頭我已許之。夫人太子聞是語已。身投於地舉聲大哭自拔頭髮裂壞衣裳。而作是言。大王天下所重莫若己身。云何今日持用施人。時五百大臣語婆羅門言。汝用是臭爛膿血頭為。婆羅門言。我自乞匃用問我為。大臣言。卿入我國我應問卿卿應答我。時婆羅門。正欲實答心懷恐懼畏斷其命。時大臣言。我等今者施汝無畏以大王故。貧婆羅門何用是頭為。我等五百。人人作一七寶頭。共相貿易并與所須。婆羅門言。吾不用也。時諸大臣不果所願。舉聲悲哭上白大王。何忍捨國夫人太子。為一婆羅門永棄孤背。王言。今為一切故捨此身。時第一大臣聞王語定。即自思惟。我今云何當見大王捨此身命。即入靜室以刀自害。爾時大王。便入後園喚婆羅門來。汝從我乞頭。我愍汝故不逆汝意。令我來世得智慧頭施於汝等。作是語已即起合掌。向十方佛作禮而言。十方諸佛諸尊菩薩威神護助令我此事必得成辦。語婆羅門隨汝持去。時婆羅門言。王有力士之力臨時苦痛脫能變悔或反害我。王審能爾者何不以頭髮自繫樹枝。王聞是語心生慈愍。此婆羅門老而且羸若當不能斷我頭者而失大利。即隨其言以髮縛樹。語婆羅門。汝斷我頭還著我手中。我當以手授與於汝。時婆羅門捉刀而前。爾時樹神即以手搏婆羅門悶絕倒地。爾時大光明王語樹神言。汝不助我反起留難。樹神聞是心生苦痛。即唱苦哉。於虛空中無雲雨血。天地大動日無精光。時婆羅門尋斷王頭持還本國。爾時五百太子及諸群臣。收大王所餘身骨起塔供養。佛告阿難。爾時第一大臣聞大光明王以頭布施。心不堪忍尋自捨命者今舍利弗是。爾時大光明王者釋迦是(出大方便佛報恩經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a country named Vārāṇasī, whose king, named Great Radiance, was compassionate toward all, never once thwarting another's own wish. A certain lesser king, ever harboring malice toward him, took note of how, on each monthly fast-day, King Great Radiance would load five hundred great elephants with treasure, clothing, and food, and give it all away freely in the great marketplace and at the four city gates.

An enemy of the rival kingdom, hearing that the king gave so freely, and indulged every person's own wish, grew envious, and gathered his own ministers together: who can go and beg King Great Radiance's own head from him? — a thousand catties of gold to whoever succeeds. A brahmin said: I can do this. The king provided him with what he needed.

As the brahmin traveled to the border of that kingdom, the earth itself shook in six ways; birds and beasts scattered in every direction; the sun and moon lost their own radiance, and the stars strayed from their proper courses; red, black, and white rainbows appeared constantly, by day and night alike, and shooting stars fell from the sky. Every spring and bathing pool within the kingdom ran dry.

As the brahmin arrived at the city gate, the gate's own guardian spirit told the gatekeeper: this man is deeply evil — he has come from afar to beg the great king's own head; do not let him enter. The brahmin was delayed there a full seven days, unable to proceed, until he told the gatekeeper: I have come from far away, wishing to see the great king. The gatekeeper went in and reported this to the king, who, hearing of it, came out at once to welcome him, bowing before him as a son before his own father, and asking where he had come from, and whether the long journey had exhausted him.

The brahmin said: I heard, in a distant land, that the king gives freely, never thwarting another's own wish, his own renown reaching upward to the very heavens and downward to the underworld itself — it is for this reason that I have come from so far, hoping to obtain something from you. The king said: I give everything asked of me — do not hesitate or doubt in the least. The brahmin said: is this truly so? I have no need of any ordinary object — I now wish to perform a great sacrifice, and beg the king's own head for it.

The king reflected to himself: since time without beginning, I have never once truly acted for the sake of the Dharma, wasting my own spirit through endless birth and death in vain. Now, with this body of mine, I seek enlightenment, having vowed this for the sake of all beings — should I fail to give it now, I would betray my own original resolve. By what other means, then, could I ever attain the unsurpassed enlightenment? The king said: very well — allow me first to settle the affairs of my own kingdom, and entrust my consort and crown prince with what follows; after seven days, I shall grant you your wish.

The great king then went into his own palace, and told his consorts: a brahmin wishes to beg my own head from me, and I have already granted him this. His consort and the crown prince, hearing this, threw themselves to the ground, weeping aloud, tearing out their own hair and rending their own garments, saying: great king, nothing in this whole world is held more dear than one's own body — how could you give it away to another man today?

The five hundred ministers said to the brahmin: what use could you possibly have for this rotting, foul, blood-and-pus-filled head? The brahmin answered: I have simply come begging for it — why do you question me? The ministers said: you have entered our own kingdom; it is we who should question you, and you who should answer us. The brahmin, though he wished to answer honestly, felt fear rising in him, dreading they might take his own life for it.

The ministers then said: we shall grant you safety from any harm on our own part, purely out of respect for the great king — but, poor brahmin, of what use could this head possibly be to you? Each of us five hundred ministers shall fashion a head of the seven treasures instead, and offer them to you in exchange, together with whatever else you require. The brahmin answered: I have no use for these. The ministers, their own hope thus dashed, wept aloud, and went before the king: how could you bear to abandon your kingdom, your consort, and your crown prince, forsaking them forever for the sake of a single brahmin? The king answered: I give up this body now for the sake of all beings.

The chief minister, hearing the king's own resolve now settled beyond recall, thought to himself: how could I bear to witness the great king give up his own body and life? — and, going into a quiet chamber, took his own life with a knife.

The great king then went into his own rear garden, and called the brahmin to him: you have come begging my head from me — out of compassion for you, I shall not thwart your own wish, that in a life to come I might obtain a head of true wisdom, and give it to beings such as you in turn. Having said this, he rose, joined his palms, and, bowing toward the buddhas of the ten directions, said: may the buddhas and bodhisattvas of the ten directions, in their own majestic power, protect and assist me, that this deed of mine might surely be accomplished — and told the brahmin: take it as you will.

The brahmin then said: the king possesses the strength of a mighty warrior — should the pain, when it comes, cause you to change your own mind, you might turn and harm me instead. If you are truly resolved on this, why not bind your own hair to a tree branch first? The king, hearing this, felt only compassion at these words, thinking: this brahmin is old and frail — should he prove unable to sever my own head, he would lose this great benefit entirely. And so he did as the brahmin suggested, binding his own hair to a tree, and told him: once you have severed my head, place it back into my own hand, that I might offer it to you myself, with my own hand.

The brahmin then took up his knife and came forward — but the tree's own spirit, at that moment, struck the brahmin with its own hand, and he fell to the ground, senseless. King Great Radiance then told the tree spirit: you have not helped me at all, but instead raised an obstacle against me! The tree spirit, hearing this, was struck with anguish, and cried out in pain — and, in the open sky, blood rained down from a clear sky, and heaven and earth shook greatly, the sun losing its own radiance.

The brahmin then, at last, severed the king's own head, and carried it back to his own country. The five hundred crown princes and all the ministers gathered the great king's own remaining bones, and raised a stūpa there in his honor, and made offerings to it.

The Buddha told Ānanda: that chief minister, who, unable to bear hearing that King Great Radiance had given away his own head, took his own life at once, is Śāriputra now. And that King Great Radiance was Śākyamuni himself (Source: Sūtra of the Great Skillful Means and the Buddha's Repayment of Kindness, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The tree spirit's own intervention is the story's single most morally ambiguous moment: it acts entirely out of protective compassion, striking down the brahmin to save the king's life — yet the king himself receives this rescue not with gratitude but with genuine reproach, treating an act meant to spare him as an unwelcome obstacle to his own vow. The scene refuses any easy reading in which protecting a bodhisattva's own body is automatically the right thing to do; here, at least, the bodhisattva's own stated wish to give without limit is allowed to override even a well-meaning rescuer's instinct to save him from the consequence of that very wish.

4. King Śibi's Flesh Weighed Against a Dove

Śakra, tasked with finding a truly perfect being, sets a test through a shapeshifted hawk and dove — and King Śibi, refusing to let either creature come to harm, offers his own flesh, cut piece by piece to match the dove's own weight on a scale, until nothing remains of him but resolve.

Ru-Yi Translation

This king applied himself with great diligence, regarding all beings as a mother loves her own child. At that time, there was no buddha yet in the world. Śakra, his own life nearing its end, thought to himself: where might I find a being of complete wisdom? He asked about this everywhere, without resolving his own uncertainty, and sat troubled.

Viśvakarman, the skilled deva of transformation, asked him: lord of the devas, why are you so troubled? He answered: I seek a being of complete wisdom, and cannot find one — this is why I am troubled. Viśvakarman said: there is a bodhisattva, perfect in giving, precepts, meditation, and wisdom, who shall attain buddhahood before long. Śakra asked who this was. He answered: it is King Śibi, of the Uśīnara clan. Śakra told Viśvakarman: let us test him now.

Viśvakarman then transformed himself into a dove, its own eyes and feet red, while Śakra transformed himself into a hawk, and flew swiftly after the dove, which fled straight beneath the king's own arm, its whole body trembling in terror, its eyes darting, its cries urgent and rapid. Many people who saw this said to one another: this king is compassionate toward all — he shall surely protect this creature, just as this small dove has taken refuge with him, as though entering its own home.

The hawk, perched nearby on a tree, called out to King Śibi: give me back my dove — it is my own prey. The king answered the hawk: I have already taken this creature under my own protection before you claimed it — from the very moment I first resolved on this path, I vowed to deliver all beings equally.

The hawk said: the king wishes to deliver all beings — am I not one of "all beings" as well? Why show compassion only to this dove, and take no pity on me, robbing me of my own food? The king answered: what food do you require? I have made a vow that, whatever being comes to take refuge in me, I shall surely protect and save — whatever food you require, I shall provide it to you as well. The hawk said: I require freshly killed, still-warm flesh.

The king thought to himself: such a thing is hard to obtain — apart from killing some other creature, there is no way to acquire it. How, then, could I kill one creature merely to save another? Having settled this in his own mind, he said: this very flesh of my own body is, in any case, bound already to old age, sickness, and death — before long, it would only rot and decay regardless; whoever requires it, I shall give it freely.

Having reflected in this way, he called for a knife, and cut flesh from his own thigh, giving it to the hawk. The hawk told the king: though you give me warm flesh, by rights its own weight should equal the dove's, exactly. The king said: bring scales, and weigh the flesh against the dove. He cut away his own flesh, piece after piece, giving it all to the dove's side of the scale, until at last the two sides matched.

He then reproached himself inwardly: you must hold yourself firm now, and not lose heart. All beings are sunk in this great sea of suffering, and I have vowed to deliver them all — why, then, should I falter over so small a suffering as this, when the suffering of the hells is so much greater still? I myself possess wisdom, diligence, precepts, and meditation, and yet still find this suffering hard to bear — how much more so those in hell, who possess no such wisdom at all!

As his own mind settled into this resolve, the earth shook in six ways; the great ocean's own waves rose high, withered trees burst into flower, and heaven rained down fragrant rain and scattered rare blossoms, while heavenly maidens sang in praise, certain that he would attain buddhahood.

Śakra asked the king: cutting your own flesh in such pain — has your own heart not sunk into distress? The king answered: my own heart is joyful, neither distressed nor sunk in despair at all. Śakra said: who could possibly believe you? The king then made this vow: if, in cutting my own flesh until the blood flowed, I felt no anger, no distress, my mind undisturbed throughout, seeking only buddhahood itself — then may my own body be restored at once. And, in that very instant, it was restored exactly as before (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The hawk's own objection — "am I not one of 'all beings' as well?" — is the entry's sharpest moment, forcing the king's universal vow to reckon honestly with a predator's own equally legitimate claim to survival, rather than simply favoring the more sympathetic, endangered creature. The king's solution refuses to rank one being's life above another's at all; he neither denies the hawk its meal nor sacrifices the dove to provide it, but interposes his own body as a third term entirely, absorbing the entire cost of the dilemma into himself rather than resolving it at either creature's own expense. This story of King Śibi was already referenced in this compendium's fascicle 10 as one of its own summarized episodes; this entry gives it in full.

5. King Wisdom-Lamp's Flesh and Blood

A miraculously born prince, remembering a past life as a king who fell into hell for six years, refuses the throne unless his entire kingdom first adopts the ten wholesome actions — and, when a disguised Śakra claims to have been taught to do evil in his own name, offers his own thigh-flesh and blood in proof that no such teaching was ever given.

She later bore a son, of dignified and lovely countenance; on the very day of his birth, eighty-four thousand buried treasure-hoards spontaneously welled up from the earth, arranging themselves in rows according to their own kind. The king told the child's mother: let us name him Wisdom-Lamp.

The king later passed away. The crown prince, then only eight or nine years old, was taught by his own mother in every skill — writing, arithmetic, and horsemanship. At fifteen years of age, his ministers told him: the great king has passed away; you should now ascend the throne in turn. The crown prince answered: in a past life, I was a king for six years, and afterward fell into hell for sixty thousand years — it is for this reason that I cannot become king now. The ministers asked: is there some means by which you might yet become king? He answered: should every person of Jambudvīpa, man or woman, of any tongue that can speak, come to practice the ten wholesome actions, then I shall become king.

The ministers, hearing the crown prince's own command, proclaimed it in all four directions at once, and the people of Jambudvīpa all took up the ten wholesome actions, refraining from killing. The ministers reported this fully to the crown prince: you may now ascend the throne. The crown prince then ascended the throne himself.

His ministers told the king: at your own birth, eighty-four thousand buried treasures welled up spontaneously — these should now be gathered into the royal treasury. The king said: there is no need to store them there — and commanded instead that, at each place where a treasure had arisen, and at the crossroads of the four directions, offerings be given to śramaṇas and brahmins, the poor, and the solitary elderly. His ministers, obeying the king's own command, made these offerings at the crossroads before the gates of all eighty-four thousand cities, exactly where each treasure had arisen.

Śakra, king of the devas, then transformed himself into a man, and said to himself, as if in conversation: the king has taught me to practice the ten unwholesome actions! A minister, hearing this, reported it to the king: have you truly given such a command? The king answered: I earlier commanded only that every speaking being within Jambudvīpa practice the ten wholesome actions — I never said any such thing as this.

The king then rode his own jeweled elephant to where this man stood, and asked him: do you claim that I, Wisdom-Lamp, taught you to practice the ten unwholesome actions? The transformed man answered: indeed you did. The king asked further: can you now practice the ten wholesome actions instead? He answered: should I wish to accomplish the bodhisattva path, I would need to eat living flesh and drink living blood first — only then would I practice the ten wholesome actions in my own body.

The king thought to himself: since time without beginning, I have passed through every kind of suffering, transmigrating through the five realms, enduring slaughter upon slaughter already. He then took up a knife, cut flesh from his own thigh, caught the blood in a vessel, and offered them to the man, saying: good man, you may eat this flesh and drink this blood, and then take up the ten wholesome actions in earnest.

The transformed man then vanished from sight, and revealed his own true form as Śakra, asking the king: was this offering made for the sake of one world, or for all four continents? The king said: it was made only in seeking the unsurpassed path, to deliver those not yet delivered. Śakra then poured heavenly ambrosia over the king's own wound, healing it at once, and benefiting all beings.

That king, his own father, is my own father, the king, now; that chief consort is my own mother now; and that King Wisdom-Lamp was myself (Source: Four-Part Vinaya, fascicle 2).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Wisdom-Lamp's own refusal of the throne is unusual among this fascicle's kings: rather than an act of generosity performed once he already holds power, his very willingness to rule at all is conditioned in advance on his entire kingdom's collective moral transformation — a bodhisattva-king who treats governance itself as something he has no right to accept until the conditions for a genuinely wholesome rule are already in place. Śakra's own test here echoes King Great Radiance's ordeal in entry 3 and King Śibi's in entry 4: a disguised deva demanding flesh and blood as proof of a vow, the recurring shape suggesting these three entries were meant to be read as variations on a single, deliberately repeated theme of proof through bodily sacrifice.

6. King Great Strength Spares No Part of His Own Body

A brief entry: a lavishly generous king, tested by a disguised Śakra who demands a piece of his own body specifically, cuts away his own arm without complaint — while Śakra himself, having created this obstacle to the king's own great charity event, pays for it with his own heavenly merit.

Chinese Source

過去有王。名曰大力。有大善根。盛設施會恣所求欲。須食與食乃至象馬牛羊田地產業皆悉與之。時目連曰。汝是大施。時天帝釋化作婆羅門。往詣王所言。王如是大施。我今須王身分。王自念言。是婆羅門不須財物。今來直欲破我大施。我若不以身分與者。我則自破大會施事。作是念已語婆羅門言。與汝身分截取持去。但以今者多有乞人四方來集。我皆應使悉得滿足。婆羅門言。我今一人尚不充足。何論餘人。王即以刀自割其臂。與婆羅門無有悔恨。一心布施捨一切物臂還平復。帝釋既為障礙因緣。天福即盡墮阿鼻獄。大力國王我身是也。帝釋者調達是也(出菩薩藏經下卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a king named Great Strength, of great roots of goodness, who held a lavish charity assembly, granting every request made of him — food to whoever asked for food, and even elephants, horses, cattle, sheep, land, and property, all given freely.

Maudgalyāyana, seeing this, remarked: this is truly great giving. Śakra, king of the devas, then transformed himself into a brahmin, and went before the king, saying: you give so greatly — I now require a part of your own body.

The king thought to himself: this brahmin has no need of ordinary wealth at all — he has come now purely to undermine my own great charity assembly. Should I fail to give even a part of my own body, I would myself be the one to undermine this whole event. Having thought this, he said to the brahmin: I shall give you a part of my body — take whatever you wish. Yet, given that so many petitioners have gathered here from every direction today, I ought to see that all of them are satisfied first.

The brahmin said: even I, a single person, am not yet satisfied — why speak of the others at all? The king then took up a knife, and cut off his own arm, giving it to the brahmin without the slightest regret, his whole mind fixed single-mindedly on giving, holding back nothing at all. His arm was then restored, whole as before.

Śakra, having created this very obstruction, saw his own heavenly merit exhausted at once, and fell into the Avīci hell. That King Great Strength was myself; and that Śakra was Devadatta (Source: Bodhisattva-Piṭaka Sūtra, latter fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry inverts the outcome familiar from entries 3 through 5: there, Śakra tests a bodhisattva-king and, upon witnessing genuine, unwavering resolve, rewards it by healing the king's own wound; here, by contrast, Śakra himself is punished — his own heavenly merit exhausted, and a fall into Avīci hell — for the very act of setting an obstacle before a king's own charity, and the text identifies this same Śakra as a past incarnation of Devadatta. The moral logic is unusually direct: testing a bodhisattva's own resolve is not itself neutral, and doing so specifically in order to disrupt an already-underway act of generosity carries real consequence for the tester, not only for the one being tested.

7. King Compassionate-Strength Bleeds for Five Starving Demons

A king's own kingdom, taught to keep the ten wholesome precepts so thoroughly that plague-demons can no longer feed on human blood at all, leaves five starving demons with nowhere left to turn — until the king himself opens his own veins in five places, feeding them from his own body rather than let his people's own virtue become their undoing.

Five yakṣas then came before the king, saying: we ourselves depend upon human blood and vital essence to sustain our own lives — yet, through your own teaching, every person now upholds the ten wholesome actions, and we are left starving and thirsting, with no way left to survive. Great king, in your own compassion, will you not have pity on us?

The king, filled with sorrowful compassion, at once opened his own veins, piercing his own body in five places. The five yakṣas then drank of his own blood, overjoyed beyond measure. The king told them: because you keep in mind the ten wholesome actions, I now give you the blood of my own body to relieve your hunger and thirst — and, when I attain buddhahood in time to come, I shall use the very blood of the Dharma-body's own precepts, concentration, and wisdom to remove the three poisons from you, and settle you in the peace of nirvāṇa.

That King Compassionate-Strength of that time was myself; and those five yakṣas are Kauṇḍinya and the others now — and it is because of this that I vowed, across every life since, to deliver them before anyone else (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 2).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story's own logic is a striking reversal of the usual relationship between virtue and consequence: the kingdom's collective moral success — universal observance of the ten wholesome actions — does not simply benefit everyone involved, but creates real, unintended harm for the plague-demons who depended on human vulnerability to survive at all, and it is the king alone, not his own flourishing people, who bears the cost of resolving that harm. This origin story for why Kauṇḍinya and his companions became the Buddha's very first disciples treats his vow to deliver them "before anyone else" not as arbitrary favoritism but as a debt owed across countless lifetimes, first incurred at the moment his own blood first fed their hunger.

8. Sutasoma, Carried Off by King Kalmāṣapāda

Snatched from his own garden by a flying, two-winged demon-king, King Sutasoma is granted a seven-day reprieve to keep a single promise to a waiting brahmin beggar — and, choosing truthfulness over his own life, willingly returns to a captor sworn to devour him, in the end persuading that very captor to release ninety-nine other captive kings as well.

Chinese Source

昔有須陀須摩王。是王精進持戒。常依實語。晨朝乘車將諸婇女入園遊戲。出城門時有一婆羅門來乞。王言諾。敬如來告。須我出還入園澡浴嬉戲。時有兩翅王名曰鹿足。空中飛來於婇女中捉王將去。諸女啼哭號慟一國驚城內外搔擾悲惶。鹿足負王騰空至所住山。置九十九諸王中。須陀須摩王涕零如雨。鹿足語言。大剎利王。汝何以啼猶如小兒。人生有死合會有離。須陀須摩王答言。我不畏死自恨失信。我從生以來初不妄語。今日晨朝出門時。有婆羅門來從我乞。我時許言還當布施。不慮無常孤負彼心。自招欺罪是故啼耳。鹿足王曰。汝畏妄語。聽汝還去。七日布施婆羅門訖便即來還。若過七日不還我有翅力取汝不難。須陀須摩王。得還本國恣意布施。立太子為王。大會人民懺謝之言。我智不周物治不如法當見忠恕。如我今日身非己有正爾還去。舉國人民及諸親戚叩頭留之。願王留意慈蔭此國勿以鹿足鬼王為慮也。當設鐵舍奇兵。鹿足雖神不畏之也。王言。不得爾也。而說偈言。實語第一戒　實語昇天梯　實語小人大　妄語入地獄　我今守實語　寧棄身壽命　心無有悔恨。如是思惟已。王即發去到鹿足王所。鹿足遙見歡喜而言。汝是實語人不失信要。然一切人皆惜命。從死得脫還來赴信汝是大人。爾時須陀須摩王讚實語。實語是為人。非實語非人。如是種種讚實語呵妄語。鹿足聞之信心清淨。語須陀須摩王言。今相放捨九十九王亦還本國。是為尸羅波羅蜜滿(出大智論第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a King Sutasoma, diligent and faithful to the precepts, always true to his own word. One morning, riding out with his own attendant women to enjoy his garden, he passed through the city gate, where a brahmin came begging. The king assented, saying: as I revere the Tathāgata, I promise — once I return from my own bathing and amusement in the garden, you shall have what you ask.

At that moment, a two-winged king named Kalmāṣapāda came flying through the sky, and, seizing the king from among his own attendant women, carried him off. The women wept and wailed in grief, and the whole kingdom was thrown into terrified confusion, within the city and without.

Kalmāṣapāda carried the king aloft through the sky to the mountain where he himself dwelt, and set him down among ninety-nine other captive kings there. King Sutasoma wept, his tears falling like rain. Kalmāṣapāda said to him: great kṣatriya king, why do you weep like a small child? All who are born must die; all who come together must part.

King Sutasoma answered: it is not death itself I fear — I grieve only at having broken my own word. Since my birth, I have never once spoken falsely. This very morning, as I left through my own gate, a brahmin came begging of me, and I promised him I would give to him upon my return — never expecting this sudden misfortune, and now I have failed him, incurring the very transgression of deceit against my own will. It is for this that I weep.

King Kalmāṣapāda said: since it is only the fear of false speech that troubles you, I shall permit you to return — complete your act of giving to the brahmin within seven days, and then come back here to me. Should you fail to return after seven days, I have wings enough to seize you again with no difficulty at all.

King Sutasoma, thus permitted to return to his own kingdom, gave freely as he wished, installed his own crown prince as king, and gathered his people together, saying, in apology: my own wisdom has never been complete, and my governance has fallen short of the true Dharma in many ways — I ask your understanding and forgiveness. This very body of mine, as of today, is no longer my own — I must now return to my captor.

The whole population of the kingdom, together with all his own kin, bowed their heads and begged him to stay: we beg the king to remain, and extend his own compassionate protection over this land — do not trouble yourself over this demon-king Kalmāṣapāda; we shall build iron chambers and gather elite troops — however formidable he may be, we need not fear him. The king answered: this cannot be — and spoke this verse:

"Truthful speech is the foremost precept; / truthful speech is the very ladder to heaven. / Truthful speech makes even a small man great; / false speech leads down into hell. / I now hold fast to truthful speech, / and would rather give up this body and life itself — / my heart holds no regret in this at all."

Having reflected in this way, the king set out at once for King Kalmāṣapāda's own domain. Kalmāṣapāda, seeing him approach from afar, was delighted, and said: you are truly a man of truthful speech, never failing your own word — for, though everyone alike cherishes their own life, and, once free from death, you might well have simply stayed away, you have instead returned to keep faith with me; you are truly a great man.

King Sutasoma then praised truthful speech at length: to speak truthfully is to be truly human; to speak falsely is to be no longer human at all — and, in this way, praised truthful speech and censured false speech in every manner. Kalmāṣapāda, hearing this, felt his own faith grow pure, and told King Sutasoma: I shall now release you, and the ninety-nine other kings as well, that you might all return to your own kingdoms. This is how the perfection of moral discipline is fulfilled (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

King Sutasoma's own grief, when first captured, is deliberately misread by his captor as a child's fear of death, and the story's real turn comes in his correction: what actually troubles him is a broken promise to an ordinary beggar, a debt of no material consequence to anyone but his own conscience, weighed as more urgent than his own survival. It is this same unwavering commitment to truthful speech — demonstrated concretely by his voluntary return to a captor sworn to devour him — that ultimately persuades Kalmāṣapāda to release not only Sutasoma but all ninety-nine other kings, the story crediting truthfulness itself, rather than any argument about mercy, as what actually changes the demon-king's own heart.

9. King Sarvadatta Sells Himself into Slavery

Fascicle 25's most severe entry: Mañjuśrī, testing King Sarvadatta's own vow to refuse no request, asks for the king and his own consort as slaves outright — and the two are sold apart from each other into separate households, the pregnant queen beaten and her own newborn killed on her purchaser's order, before the entire ordeal dissolves, revealed as an elaborate test of the couple's own unwavering resolve.

Chinese Source

昔有國王。號薩惒檀(梁言一切施)有所求索不逆人意。其王名字流聞八方。文殊師利欲往試之。化作年少婆羅門從異國來詣王宮門。王甚歡喜。即出奉迎問訊道人所從何來耶。婆羅門言。聞王功德故來相見今欲乞匃。王言大善。所欲得者莫自疑難。婆羅門言。欲得王身與我作奴。及王夫人為我作婢。王甚喜悅。報言大善。今我身者定自可得。願屬道人供給使令。其夫人者大國王女。當往問之。時王即入語夫人言。是時夫人即隨王出。自白道人言。願得以身供給道人。婆羅門言。汝當隨我。皆悉蹠跣不得著履。如奴婢之法。皆言唯諾從大家教。便將奴婢涉道而去。以化作人代其王處及夫人身。領理國事令其如故。其夫人者長處深宮不更勤苦。又復重身懷妊數月。步隨大家舉身皆痛脚底傷破。不能復前疲極在後。時婆羅門還顧罵言。汝今作婢當如婢法。不可作汝本時之態。夫人長跪白言不敢懈慢。但小疲極住止息耳。喚言疾來。促隨我後前到國市。別賣奴婢各與一主。相去數里。時有長者買得此奴使守斯舍。諸有埋死人者令收其稅不得妄稅。是時婢者所屬大家。夫人甚妬晨夜令作初不懈息。其後數月。時婢㝃身所生男兒。夫人恚言。汝為婢使那得此兒。捉取殺之。隨大家教即殺其兒。持行埋之。往到奴所得共相見。不說勤苦各無怨心。如是語言。須臾之頃恍惚如夢。還在本國正殿上坐如前不異。及諸群臣後宮婇女皆悉如故。所生太子亦自然活。王及夫人心內自疑何緣致爾。文殊師利。在虛空中坐寶蓮華現身色相讚言。善哉。今汝布施至誠如是。王與夫人踊躍歡喜。即前作禮。文殊師利為說經法。三千剎土悉為震動。覆一國人皆發無上正真道意。王與夫人應時俱得不起法忍。佛告阿難。是時王者則我身是也。時夫人者今瞿夷是。時太子者今羅云是也(出薩惒檀王經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a king named Sarvadatta ("Gives Everything" in the language of Liang), who never once refused a request made of him; his own renown spread throughout the eight directions. Mañjuśrī, wishing to test him, transformed himself into a young brahmin, and came to the king's own palace gate from a foreign land. The king, overjoyed, went out at once to welcome him, and asked where the renunciant had come from.

The brahmin answered: I have heard of the king's own merit, and have come to see you; I now wish to beg something of you. The king said: excellent — whatever you desire, do not hesitate to ask. The brahmin said: I wish to have the king's own body as my own slave, and the king's own consort as my own servant-woman.

The king, greatly delighted, answered: excellent — my own person, at least, is certainly mine to give; let me belong to you, renunciant, and serve at your own command. My consort, however, is the daughter of a great king — I must go and ask her first.

The king then went in and told his consort of this. She came out at once with the king, and told the renunciant herself: I too wish to give my own body to your service. The brahmin said: you shall follow me now — both of you must go entirely barefoot, wearing no sandals at all, exactly as servants are required to do, and answer only "yes, master" to whatever I command.

He then led the two of them, now his own slaves, along the road, while a transformed man took the king's own place, and his consort's, ruling the affairs of the kingdom exactly as before.

The consort, having long dwelt within the deep palace, unaccustomed to hardship, and, moreover, several months pregnant, followed on foot behind her own master, her whole body aching, the soles of her feet cut and bleeding, until she could go no further, and fell behind, utterly exhausted. The brahmin, turning back, reviled her: you are now a servant, and must act as a servant should — you cannot behave as you once did. The consort knelt at length and said: I would not dare grow slack or careless — I am only resting a moment, being so exhausted. He called out: come quickly! — and hurried her on behind him.

They arrived at last at the kingdom's own marketplace, and the two slaves were sold separately, each to a different master, their new households several li apart. A certain elder bought the king himself, and set him to guard a household; whenever anyone came to bury their own dead, he was to collect the proper fee from them, and no more.

The consort, meanwhile, belonged to a mistress who, out of sheer jealousy, made her labor day and night without rest. Some months later, the consort gave birth to a son in her own servitude. Her mistress, enraged, said: how could a mere servant such as you bear a child? — and had the infant seized and killed. Obeying her mistress's own command, the consort herself carried out the killing, and buried the child afterward.

She then went to where the king himself was, and the two of them met again; neither spoke of their own hardship, and neither harbored any resentment at all. As they spoke together in this way, in but a brief moment, everything dissolved as though it had been a dream — and they found themselves back in their own kingdom, seated in the main hall exactly as before, with no difference at all; every minister, every woman of the inner palace, was restored exactly as before as well, and the very son who had been born and killed was now spontaneously alive once more.

The king and his consort, their own hearts filled with wonder, could not understand by what cause all this had come about. Mañjuśrī then appeared, seated upon a jeweled lotus in the open sky, his own radiant form revealed, and said in praise: excellent — your own giving has proven so sincere as this!

The king and his consort leapt up in joy, and came forward at once to bow before him. Mañjuśrī then taught them the Dharma; the whole three-thousandfold world-system shook, and every person throughout the entire kingdom gave rise to the resolve for the unsurpassed, true path. The king and his consort, at that very moment, together attained the acceptance of the non-arising of all phenomena.

The Buddha told Ānanda: that king of that time was myself. That consort was Gopī. And that crown prince was Rāhula (Source: Sūtra of King Sarvadatta).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry is translated here without softening, consistent with this project's standing practice throughout, though it is worth naming plainly what makes it the fascicle's most difficult reading: a mistress orders an infant killed, and the story has the child's own mother — herself enslaved and given no real choice in the matter — carry out that killing with her own hands, all of it later revealed as illusion once Mañjuśrī's test concludes. Unlike the bodily-sacrifice entries earlier in this fascicle, where suffering is chosen and consented to by the one who bears it, this test extends its cost onto a family and an entire kingdom who never agreed to serve as the proving ground for the king's own vow — and the story's own resolution, restoring everything exactly as it was in an instant, asks the reader to accept that this changes nothing about what the couple actually endured in the story's own telling of it.

10. King Cūrapā Gives Up His Own Beloved Wife and Son for a Verse

A king troubled that his own generosity has never extended to true spiritual teaching finally finds a teacher — a demon in disguise — who demands his own wife and son as food in exchange for a single verse, and gives them up without turning back, moved only afterward to grief once the verse itself is already his.

Chinese Source

昔閻浮提有大國王。名衢樓婆。領八萬四千小國。覆育人物。心自念言。我但以財貨資給一切。終無道教此實我咎。宣令國內誰能有法為我說者資其所須。毘沙門王化為夜叉來詣宮門。誰欲聞法我當為說。王躬出迎作禮。初集群僚請聞正法。夜叉告言。學法事難。須王所愛妻子。與我食之。王即命夫人太子與夜叉食。群臣皆哭。王意不迴。夜叉食盡。即說偈言。一切行無常　生者皆有苦　五陰空無相　無有我我所。王心喜無悔班示天人咸使誦持。毘沙門王還復本形。夫人太子端然如故。歎王奇特。佛言。時國王者今我身是(出賢愚經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, in Jambudvīpa, there was a great king named Cūrapā, who ruled over eighty-four thousand lesser kingdoms, caring for and nurturing his own people. He thought to himself: I have only ever supported others with mere material wealth, never once with true spiritual teaching — this is indeed my own fault. He proclaimed throughout his own kingdom: whoever possesses the true Dharma, and will teach it to me, shall receive whatever they require in return.

Vaiśravaṇa transformed himself into a yakṣa, and came to the palace gate, saying: whoever wishes to hear the Dharma, I shall teach it to them. The king himself came out to welcome him, and bowed before him, gathering his own ministers together to hear the true Dharma taught.

The yakṣa declared: learning the Dharma is no easy matter — I require the king's own beloved wife and son, that I might feed upon them. The king at once commanded his own consort and crown prince be given to the yakṣa as food. His ministers all wept, but the king's own resolve did not waver. Once the yakṣa had consumed them entirely, he spoke this verse:

"All conditioned things are impermanent; / whatever is born carries suffering within it. / The five aggregates are empty, without any true mark; / there is no self, and nothing that is truly one's own."

The king's own heart was filled with joy, without the slightest regret, and he proclaimed this teaching to devas and humans alike, having them all recite and uphold it. Vaiśravaṇa then reverted to his own true form, and the king's consort and crown prince stood there again, restored, exactly as they had been before, and all present praised the king's own extraordinary resolve.

The Buddha said: that king of that time was myself (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The verse King Cūrapā receives is among the most doctrinally central in the entire Buddhist canon — a compressed statement of impermanence, suffering, and non-self — and the story's own structure asks the reader to weigh that teaching's own worth against the price paid for it: a wife and son, consumed before the assembled court's own eyes, restored only after the verse has already been spoken and received. As in entry 9's account of King Sarvadatta, the restoration at the story's close does not erase what the narrative itself has already asked its reader to sit with in the moment of loss.

11. King Auspicious-Constellation Moves a Rākṣasa King to Repent

Fascicle 25 closes with a man-eating rākṣasa king who captures ninety-nine kings for his own table — until a virtuous king, seized while stopping first to keep a small promise to a struggling scholar, moves his own captor to release every prisoner and abandon his throne of human flesh entirely.

Chinese Source

昔有噉人鬼。作人中王。恒食人肉以為厨宰。隣國征伐得九十九王。九十九王白羅剎王曰。隣國有王。名曰善宿。好行施惠修菩薩德有所求索不逆人意。大王設能擒獲彼者我等甘心受死無恨。爾時羅剎王即起鬼兵。往伺其便。正值善宿遊在外園觀於浴池。有一梵志辭家外學。夫梵志之法臨辭去時。白父母言。我今離家追伴學問。計還之日且未有期。設財貨窮乏從王舉貸。我還當償。其人學問以得成就。來至家中但見空屋不見人眾。即問比隣。我今世父母兄弟姊姝竟為所在。比隣報曰。汝學之後舉王財賄無以當償。為王所繫今在牢獄。其人自念。家窮事狹無有財寶。設我詣獄親覲父母復當拘執。同受其苦不免王法。宜今在外改形易服竊行求索。畢償官物乃得出耳。其人復念。隣國有王。號曰善宿。修行道德施心不絕。當往至彼至誠告請。必不見違足償王物。尋往至彼隨王乞索。王言大佳。當相供給。須吾沐浴訖。當相惠施。小停勿憂不負言信。王詣浴池為鬼兵所擒。王尋還顧悲慼涕零。鬼王問曰。我等聞王仁和博愛靡不周濟。雖遭厄困何為悲慼。王報鬼曰。我生惠施未曾有悔。向有梵志在外乞索。許而未與。是以憂慼耳。鬼王白王。王守誠信由來不改。如今放王施訖時還乃知王心。王得還宮。開藏惠施恣彼人意。尋還就信詣鬼王所。鬼王告曰。汝不畏吾乎。何為受死而來。善宿偈答。作福不作惡　皆由宿行法　終不畏死徑　如船截流渡。鬼王聞之內懷慚愧。改心易行思修善本。即告善宿王曰。今聞所說人中難有。今放九十九王。我捨此位。願王攝領以法治化我領鬼眾還歸窠窟。若俱律者自當數覲。即共離別各還所在。萬民稱慶國界清泰。共行十善不修惡業。善宿積行不息。後得成佛。於樹王下復說先偈(出出曜經第十六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man-eating demon who had made himself king among men, constantly feeding on human flesh as his own kitchen's fare. Through wars against neighboring kingdoms, he had captured ninety-nine kings. These ninety-nine kings told the rākṣasa king: there is a king in a nearby land, named Auspicious-Constellation, who delights in giving and cultivates the bodhisattva's own virtue, never once refusing what is asked of him. Great king, should you manage to capture him too, we would gladly accept our own death without regret.

The rākṣasa king then raised his own demon troops, and went to watch for an opportunity. It happened, at that time, that King Auspicious-Constellation was touring his own outer garden, admiring a bathing pool.

There was, at that time, a young brahmin scholar who had taken leave of his own household to study abroad. It is the custom of brahmin scholars, upon departing, to tell their parents: I now leave home to pursue my own studies together with companions, and cannot say when I shall return; should my own funds run short, I shall borrow from the king, and repay it upon my return.

This young man, having completed his own studies, returned home only to find his own house empty, no one there at all. He asked his own neighbors: where are my parents, my brothers and sisters now? The neighbors answered: after you left to study, the money you borrowed from the king went unpaid, and your family was seized by the king's own officers, and now sits in prison.

The young man thought to himself: my own family is poor, our means slight, with no wealth of our own at all — should I go to the prison to see my own parents, I too would surely be seized, and share in their own suffering, unable to escape the king's own law. Better that I remain outside for now, disguise my own appearance, and seek by whatever means I can to repay the debt in full, and only then secure their own release.

He thought further: there is a king in a neighboring land, named Auspicious-Constellation, who cultivates virtue and the path, his own heart of giving never exhausted — I should go to him, and, with sincere and earnest words, ask for his help; he will surely not refuse me, and I shall have enough to repay the king's own debt.

He went to that king at once, and made his request. The king said: excellent — I shall provide for you. Only wait until I have finished bathing, and I shall give you what you need; wait a little, and do not worry — I shall not break my own word.

As the king went to his own bathing pool, he was seized by the demon troops. The king, looking back as he was carried off, wept in sorrow. The demon king asked him: we have heard that you are benevolent and gentle, loving toward all, sparing nothing to help others — though you now face such misfortune, why do you grieve so?

The king answered the demon: throughout my own life, I have given freely, and never once regretted it. Just now, a young brahmin scholar was waiting outside, asking for my help — I had promised him, but had not yet given it. It is for this alone that I grieve.

The demon king told him: your own faithfulness to your word has never once wavered from the start — I shall release you now, that you might complete your own act of giving, and return here once it is done, that I might come to know your own true heart for myself. The king, permitted to return to his own palace, opened his own treasury, and gave freely according to that young man's own need.

He then went at once, true to his own word, to the demon king's own domain. The demon king said to him: do you not fear me? Why have you come here, accepting your own death? Auspicious-Constellation answered in verse:

"To cultivate blessing, and never do evil, / follows entirely from one's own past conduct and its own law. / I shall never fear the road of death, / just as a boat cuts across a river's own current to reach the far shore."

The demon king, hearing this, felt shame rise within him, and, changing his own heart and conduct, resolved to cultivate the roots of goodness instead. He told King Auspicious-Constellation: what I have just heard is rare indeed to find among human beings. I now release the ninety-nine other kings, and give up this very throne myself — I ask that you, great king, take charge in my place, and govern by the true Dharma, while I lead my own demon-host back to our own den. Should we ever meet under the same law, I shall come to pay you visits from time to time.

And so they parted, each returning to their own place. The people of the whole land rejoiced, and the kingdom knew peace and order; together, they practiced the ten wholesome actions, and cultivated no unwholesome deed at all. King Auspicious-Constellation continued to accumulate merit without ceasing, and, in time, attained buddhahood himself — and, beneath the king of trees, he spoke this same verse once more (Source: Sūtra of Illustrative Parables [Chūyào jīng], fascicle 16).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The whole encounter turns on a detail easy to overlook: King Auspicious-Constellation grieves at his own capture not out of fear for his own life, which the demon king expects and is prepared for, but purely over an unfulfilled promise to an anxious stranger — the exact same substitution of a broken word for the fear of death that drives King Sutasoma's own story in entry 8. It is this same specific, recognizable virtue, demonstrated rather than merely claimed, that dissolves the rākṣasa king's entire identity as a devourer of human kings; the fascicle's closing line, that Auspicious-Constellation later repeated this very verse beneath the bodhi tree upon attaining buddhahood, closes the loop between an act of ordinary faithfulness and the moment of full awakening itself.
