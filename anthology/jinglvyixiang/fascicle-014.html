Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 14 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Arhat Śrāvaka Monks, Part 3" (聲聞無學僧部第三) — twenty entries devoted entirely to the Buddha's own two chief disciples: Śāriputra's birth, his youthful triumph in debate, his own past life giving up an eye only to see it spat upon, King Candraprabha's gift of his own head, and his final nirvāṇa before the Buddha's own; then Maudgalyāyana's jeweled bridge across a flooded river, the Ullambana Sūtra's account of his rescue of his mother, his subduing of two dragon kings, and two entries on his own honest mistakes.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 14 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 14 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 14 continues the compendium's survey of named arhat disciples, devoting itself entirely to the Buddha's own two chief śrāvakas: Śāriputra, foremost in wisdom, across nine entries, and Maudgalyāyana, foremost in supernormal power, across eleven. It is among the longest fascicles translated so far, including the full King Candraprabha jātaka — one of the most famous head-giving stories in the entire tradition — and the Ullambana Sūtra in its complete original form, the source text behind the Ghost Festival still observed across the Chinese-speaking world today. Every entry's full text remains intact; two entries sharing a single theme are grouped together at the close.

1. Śāriputra's Retreat to the Lesser Vehicle

Sixty kalpas into the bodhisattva path, Śāriputra gives up his own eye to a beggar who only spits on it and grinds it underfoot — and, concluding that such beings cannot be saved, turns from the bodhisattva path toward his own liberation instead.

Chinese Source

舍利弗於六十劫中行菩薩道。欲度布施河時有一人來乞其眼。舍利弗言。眼無所任何以索之。若須我身及財物者當以相與。答曰。唯欲得眼。舍利弗出一眼而與之。乞者得眼唾而脚踏。舍利弗言。如此等人難可度也。不如自調早伏生死。於是迴向小乘(出大智度論第十二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Śāriputra, practicing the bodhisattva path across sixty kalpas, was crossing the river of giving when a man came to beg his own eye. Śāriputra said the eye itself served no purpose — if the man needed his body or wealth instead, he would gladly give it. But the man insisted he wanted only the eye — and Śāriputra plucked one out and gave it to him. The beggar, taking the eye, spat on it and ground it underfoot. Śāriputra reflected: beings such as this are impossible to deliver — better to tame and subdue my own birth and death first — and, from that moment, turned instead toward the lesser vehicle (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 12).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium offers this story without apology or softening, and it sits uneasily beside every other entry in this anthology praising bodhisattvas who give without any expectation of gratitude at all. Read as the opening entry of a fascicle otherwise devoted to celebrating Śāriputra's own wisdom, it functions less as a moral lesson than as an origin story — an explanation, offered on its own terms, for why the foremost of the Buddha's wisdom-disciples took the path he did, without either condemning or fully vindicating the choice.

2. Śāriputra's Birth and Awakening

An eight-year-old prodigy defeats every debater in the kingdom, forms a lifelong friendship with the equally gifted Maudgalyāyana, and, after years under a teacher who admits he may never have found the truth himself, attains the first fruit of the path from a single four-line verse spoken by a passing monk.

Chinese Source

南天竺有婆羅門。名曰提舍。為大論議師。其妻懷妊。夢見一人身披甲冑手執金剛。摧破諸山在山邊立。提舍聞之曰。汝當生男摧伏諸論議師。唯不如一人為作弟子。懷妊以後。母大聰明甚能論議。弟拘郗羅與姊言談。每事屈滯。知所懷子必大智慧。捨家學問不暇剪爪。時人呼為長爪梵志。姊生兒七日。字曰優波提舍(出曜經云優波替)時人以母所生。共為之號名舍利弗。年始八歲誦十八部經。通解一切書籍。時摩伽陀國有龍王兄弟。一名吉利。二名阿伽羅。降雨以時國無荒年。人民感之。常以仲春之月。一切大集。至龍住處為設大會。作樂談義終此一晨。自古及今斯集未替。敷四高座。一為國王。二為太子。三為大臣。四為論士。舍利弗八歲之身。觀察時人神情囑向。無勝己者。便昇論座結加趺坐。眾人疑怪。或謂愚小無智。或謂智量過人。雖復嘉其神異。猶懷矜恥。以其年小不自與語。皆遣年少弟子傳言問之。答酢流便辭理超絕。時諸論師歎未曾有。愚智大小一切皆伏。王大歡喜。即命有司封一聚落常以給之。國內大小無不慶悅。時吉占師子名拘律陀。姓大目犍連。舍利弗共為親友。舍利弗才明見貴。目犍豪爽致知。才智相比行止必俱。結要終始。後俱厭世出家學道。作梵志刪闍耶弟子。問於師曰。所求道所得。師答之曰。自我求道彌歷年歲。不知為道果無耶。我非其人耶。而亦不得。他日其師寢疾。舍利弗在頭邊立。大目連在足邊立。喘喘然其將終。乃愍爾而笑。二人問笑意。師答之言。世俗無眼為恩愛所侵。我見金地國王死。其大夫人自投火[卄/積]求同一處。而此二人。行報各異生處殊絕。是時二人筆受師語。欲以驗其虛實。後有金地商人。遠來摩伽陀國。二人以疏驗之。果如師語。乃撫然歎曰。我等非其人耶。為是師隱我耶。二人相與誓曰。若先得甘露要畢同味。佛度迦葉兄弟千人。次遊王舍城。時一比丘名阿說示(五人之一)著衣持鉢入城乞食。舍利弗見其容儀超異諸根靜默。問曰。汝師是誰耶。答曰。釋種太子。厭老病死苦出家學道得無上菩提。是我師也。舍利弗言。為我說汝師教授法。答曰。我年既幼稚。學日又初淺。豈能宣至真。如來廣大義。又曰。略說其要。答曰。諸法因緣生。是法因緣滅。舍利弗即得初道(出曜經云得無著果)。還為目連說亦得初道。二師為各與二百五十弟子俱到佛所。佛遙見之。指舍利弗。是我弟子中智慧第一。又指目連云。神足第一。今與其弟子大眾俱來。白佛言。我等願從佛法出家。佛言。善來比丘。即時鬚髮自落。法服著身。衣鉢具足。受成就戒。過半月後。聞佛為長爪梵志說法。即得阿羅漢道(出大智論第十一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In South India there lived a brahmin named Tiṣya, a great master of debate. His wife, while pregnant, dreamed of a man clad in armor, wielding a vajra, smashing through mountains as he stood at their edge. Tiṣya, hearing this, declared: "You shall bear a son who will overcome every rival in debate — save one man alone, whose disciple he shall become." From the time of her conception onward, the mother herself grew remarkably eloquent and skilled in debate, so that her own younger brother, Kauṣṭhila, found himself repeatedly bested whenever they argued, and knew the child she carried must be one of great wisdom — abandoning his own studies thereafter and neglecting even to cut his nails, for which people of the time called him the Long-Nailed Brahmacārin.

Seven days after his sister bore her son, the boy was named Upatiṣya (the Sūtra of Similes calls him Upatiṣye) — though people of the time, naming him for his mother, called him Śāriputra, "Son of Śārī." By eight years old, he had recited eighteen collections of scripture and mastered every text there was.

At that time, in the kingdom of Magadha, two dragon-king brothers, Jayanta and Agila, brought rain in its proper season, sparing the land any famine — and the people, grateful, gathered every year in the second month of spring at the dragons' own dwelling for a great assembly, holding music and debate through a single morning, a custom unbroken from ancient times to the present. Four high seats were set out — for the king, the crown prince, the chief minister, and the foremost debater. The eight-year-old Śāriputra, observing the crowd's own attention and finding no one there who surpassed him, ascended the debater's own seat and sat cross-legged. The assembly, astonished, wondered whether he was merely a foolish child or a genius beyond his years — and, though marveling at his precocity, felt too embarrassed by his young age to address him directly, sending instead their own junior disciples to relay questions to him. His answers flowed back so fluently, his reasoning so far surpassing theirs, that the assembled debaters, wise and foolish, young and old alike, all conceded defeat, declaring they had never witnessed such a thing. The king, delighted, ordered an entire village granted to support him permanently, and the whole kingdom, high and low, rejoiced.

At that time there lived a renowned young lion of debate named Kolita, of the great Maudgalyāyana clan, who became Śāriputra's own close friend — Śāriputra prized for his own brilliant clarity, Maudgalyāyana for his own bold, generous spirit, their talents so evenly matched that wherever one went, the other soon followed, and the two swore a lasting bond. Later, both, weary of the world, went forth together to study the path, becoming disciples of the ascetic teacher Sañjaya. They asked their teacher whether he had attained what he himself sought — and he answered: "I have sought the path across many years now, yet do not know whether there is truly any fruit to attain, or whether I myself am simply not the right person for it — either way, I have not attained it."

Some time later, their teacher fell gravely ill; Śāriputra stood at his head, Maudgalyāyana at his feet, and, as he lay gasping near death, the teacher suddenly smiled. Asked why, he answered: "Ordinary people, lacking true insight, are ruled by attachment and desire. I have just seen, in my own mind, the king of Suvarṇabhūmi die, and his chief consort throw herself upon his funeral pyre to join him there — yet the two of them, through their own different deeds, will be reborn in utterly different places." The two disciples wrote down their teacher's own words, intending to verify them later. Sometime afterward, a merchant arrived in Magadha from Suvarṇabhūmi, and the two compared their record against his own account — and found it exactly as their teacher had said. Astonished, they lamented: "Are we simply not the right people for this path, or has our teacher been withholding something from us?" — and the two of them swore an oath together: whichever of us first attains the taste of the deathless nectar shall share it at once with the other.

The Buddha, having converted Kāśyapa and his thousand brother-disciples, then traveled on to Rājagṛha. There, a monk named Aśvajit — one of the first five disciples — entered the city one morning, robed and bowl in hand, to beg. Śāriputra, seeing his extraordinary bearing, his every faculty calm and composed, asked him: "Who is your teacher?" Aśvajit answered: "A prince of the Śākya clan, who, weary of old age, sickness, and death, went forth to study the path and attained unsurpassed awakening — he is my own teacher." Śāriputra asked him to explain his teacher's own doctrine, and Aśvajit answered: "I am still young in years and new to this study — how could I possibly proclaim the Tathāgata's own vast and true meaning?" Asked then only for its essence in brief, he answered: "All dharmas arise from causes and conditions; this same Dharma teaches also their cessation through causes and conditions." At these very words, Śāriputra attained the first fruit of the path (the Sūtra of Similes calls this attaining the fruit of non-attachment).

Returning to Maudgalyāyana, he repeated the verse, and Maudgalyāyana likewise attained the first fruit at once. The two teachers — for each now taught in his own right — together with their own two hundred fifty disciples each, went together to the Buddha. The Buddha, seeing them from afar, pointed to Śāriputra and said: "Foremost in wisdom among my disciples" — and, pointing to Maudgalyāyana: "Foremost in supernormal power." Arriving with their own great assembly of disciples, they told the Buddha: we wish to go forth under your own teaching. The Buddha said: "Welcome, monks" — and at once their hair fell away of itself, monastic robes appeared upon their bodies, complete with bowl, and they received the full precepts. Just over a half-month later, hearing the Buddha teach the Long-Nailed Brahmacārin, Śāriputra himself attained arhatship (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 11).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Sañjaya's own deathbed confession — that he cannot tell whether there is no fruit to attain at all, or whether he himself was simply never the right person for it — is a rare moment in this compendium where a teacher openly admits the limits of his own path, and the two disciples' response is not to abandon inquiry but to test his claim empirically, writing it down and waiting years for a merchant's account to confirm or refute it. Only once their own teacher's insight is verified as genuine, yet still somehow insufficient, do they make their pact to share whatever truth either finds first — suggesting the compendium sees Śāriputra's own eventual awakening less as a rejection of his first teacher than as the completion of a search that teacher had honestly admitted he could not finish himself.

3. Śāriputra Asks for Precepts Before Any Offense

When the monks object that precepts should not be requested before any wrongdoing has occurred, the Buddha answers that Śāriputra has done exactly this before, in a past life as a minister who convinced a reluctant king to establish laws while his own kingdom still prospered in perfect harmony.

Chinese Source

佛住舍衛城。舍利弗請佛制戒。諸比丘言。云何未有過而求制戒。佛言。舍利弗不但今日未有過而請制戒。其往昔時。在一聚落。人民居士未有過患。亦嘗請我制諸刑罰。時國名迦尸。城名波羅㮈。國王名淨稱。淨稱以法治化。布施持戒以道汎愛。人民致盛富樂豐實。村里邑屋雞飛相接。舉國相敬常作諸伎共相娛樂。時有大臣。名曰陶利。多諸謀策。白王言。今日境內自然富樂。人民相敬。願王制立刑罰。莫令樂極生過。王曰。即諸大臣聰明有智。多有朋黨不可卒制。若一訶責或生釁咎。王欲薄啟其。乃說偈言。
縱力喜瞋怒　哀愍必治制
化民以正法　令心無怨蔽
大臣咸喜。亦說偈言。
最勝人中尊　調伏久住世
以道治蒼生　慈陰無極際
佛言。淨稱者則我身是。大臣者舍利弗是(出僧祇律第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Śrāvastī. Śāriputra asked the Buddha to establish the monastic precepts, and the monks objected: how can precepts be requested before any offense has occurred? The Buddha answered: it is not only today that Śāriputra has asked me to establish rules before any fault existed — long ago, too, in a certain village, the people and householders there, though blameless, once asked me in just the same way to establish laws and punishments.

At that time there was a kingdom named Kāśi, its city named Vārāṇasī, ruled by a king named Śuklakīrti, "Pure Renown," who governed by the Dharma, giving generously, upholding the precepts, and loving his people universally through the path — so that the people prospered in wealth and happiness, villages and towns crowded close together like roosting chickens, and the whole kingdom, mutually respectful, delighted together in music and entertainment. A great minister named Dhāvalī, resourceful and full of schemes, told the king: "Today the whole realm naturally prospers, and the people respect one another — I ask that the king now establish laws and punishments, lest such extreme prosperity give rise to wrongdoing." The king answered that his ministers, being intelligent and forming their own factions, could not simply be regulated all at once — a single reprimand might itself provoke resentment — and, wishing to state his own intent gently, spoke this verse:

"Though power might indulge in wrath, out of compassion I shall still establish order — governing the people by the true Dharma, that no heart may harbor hidden resentment."

The ministers, delighted, answered with a verse of their own:

"Most excellent, honored among people — long may you remain in this world, subduing all; governing all beings by the path, your compassionate shade extending without limit."

The Buddha said: that King Śuklakīrti was myself; that minister was Śāriputra (Source: Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 1).

4. The Water-Carrier's Reward

A hired laborer with little faith in the Buddha offers Śāriputra a bath purely out of casual kindness, and is reborn in heaven that same night — reflecting afterward that even so slight a gesture, meeting so fine a field of merit, could bear so disproportionate a fruit.

Chinese Source

舍利弗。夏盛熱時。遊菴羅園。一客作人汲井水灌樹。於佛無有大信。見舍利弗發小信心。喚舍利弗言。大德來。脫衣樹下坐。我以水澆不失溉灌。兼相利益。舍利弗脫衣受洗。身得涼樂隨意遊行。此客作人其夜命終。即生忉利天上。有大威力。次釋提桓因。便自念言。我何因生此。自觀宿命。信心微薄。因客作溉灌。汲水洗浴舍利弗身。我若信心純厚。知必有報故設浴具以為供養。自惟為功雖少。以遇良田獲報甚多。即詣舍利弗所。散華供養。舍利弗因其淨信之心。為說法要得須陀洹道(出雜藏經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Śāriputra, during the height of summer's heat, was walking through the Āmra Grove, where a hired laborer, drawing water from a well to irrigate the trees, though lacking any great faith in the Buddha, felt a small spark of faith arise on seeing Śāriputra, and called out: "Venerable one, come — remove your robe and sit beneath this tree; I shall pour water over you without neglecting my own watering duties, and thereby benefit us both." Śāriputra removed his robe and accepted the bath, his body cooled and refreshed, and went on his way at ease.

That very night, the laborer's life ended, and he was reborn at once in Trāyastriṃśa heaven with great majesty, second only to Śakra himself. Reflecting on how he had come to be reborn there, and recalling his own past life, he realized that though his faith had been slight, it was through watering and washing Śāriputra's own body that he had earned this reward — and thought to himself: had my faith been truly deep and sincere, knowing certainly that reward would follow, I would have prepared bathing implements deliberately as an offering; yet even this small, incidental merit, meeting so fine a field, has borne so great a fruit. He went at once to Śāriputra and scattered flowers in offering — and Śāriputra, moved by his own pure faith, taught him the essentials of the Dharma, through which he attained the fruit of stream-entry (Source: Miscellaneous Piṭaka Sūtra).

5. The Human-Serpent

A child born after a rain of blood, identified by fire-breathing spittle, grows into a walking poison so lethal it kills seventy-two thousand people over a lifetime of state-sanctioned executions — yet is reborn in heaven after gazing at Śāriputra with compassion just seven times as it dies.

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, in Śrāvastī, blood once rained from the sky, covering an area forty li wide, and the king and his ministers, greatly alarmed, summoned every diviner and astrologer to determine whether this omen portended good or ill fortune. The diviners answered: ancient records say that a rain of blood presages the birth of a human-serpent, a creature of poisonous harm, and that the whole kingdom should be searched to identify and set apart this coming calamity. Asked how such a creature might be distinguished, the diviners answered: every newborn infant in the kingdom should be brought and made to spit into an empty jar — and whichever child's own spittle burst into flame would be revealed as the human-serpent.

Such a child was indeed found this way, and, since it could not be permitted to remain among ordinary people, it was moved instead to a remote, uninhabited place, where any person condemned to death in the kingdom would be sent to it. This human-serpent killed by spitting venom, and, over time, seventy-two thousand people died from its poison in this way.

Later, a lion appeared, its roar shaking every creature within forty li, ravaging the region uncontrollably — and the king, offering a thousand catties of gold and an entire prefecture to whoever could drive it off, found no one willing to try, until his ministers suggested that only the human-serpent itself might be capable. Summoned and sent to face the lion, the human-serpent breathed out its venom the moment the lion approached, and the beast died at once, restoring peace to the land.

Years later, the human-serpent itself grew old and fell gravely ill, nearing death — and the Buddha, grieving that its own heavy sins would otherwise condemn it to fall into an evil rebirth with no chance of escape, told Śāriputra to go and instruct it, that it might be freed from this heavy retribution. Śāriputra went at once to stand before it. The creature, enraged at being approached so casually while it still lived, thought: "I am not yet dead, and this man treats me lightly, coming directly before me without even a word of introduction" — and breathed out its venom, intending to kill him. Śāriputra, through his own compassionate wisdom, turned the venom harmlessly aside, his own expression serene and untroubled, not a single hair on his body stirring. Three times the creature breathed out its poison, and three times it failed to harm him — and, recognizing at last his own venerable nature, its heart relented and a wholesome feeling arose within it; it gazed up and down at Śāriputra with compassion seven times over. Śāriputra then returned to the monastery, and, drawing in its own breath at last, the human-serpent died that same day.

At that moment heaven and earth shook greatly — for the greatest good, as much as the greatest evil, can move them equally. King Ajātaśatru of Magadha then went to the Buddha, bowed to the ground, and asked the World-Honored One what path the human-serpent had taken in death. The Buddha answered that it had been reborn in the very first heaven. The king, astonished, asked how so grave a sinner could possibly be reborn in heaven — and the Buddha explained: because it gazed upon Śāriputra with compassion seven times over, through the merit of this alone it was reborn in the first heaven; once that merit is exhausted, it shall be reborn in the second heaven, and so on, until, after seven such rebirths, it shall attain pratyekabuddhahood and enter final nirvāṇa.

The king asked whether the seventy-two thousand people it had killed would then go unavenged. The Buddha answered: when it finally becomes a pratyekabuddha, its own body will shine like pure gold, and, seated in meditation beneath a tree by the roadside, a great army of over seventy thousand soldiers, passing by and mistaking it for a golden statue, will hack it apart and each take a share — only to find, once settled in their hands, that it was flesh after all, and, gathering the pieces back together, will leave it there; through this very event it will enter final nirvāṇa. In this way, the grave sin of this present life will be so lightly repaid, and fully discharged. The Buddha told the king: whoever encounters a true spiritual friend may have even a mountain of accumulated sin dissolved away, and may attain the path as well (Source: Sūtra of Similes and Parables, fascicle 9).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story is careful never to excuse the human-serpent's own killings — the debt for seventy-two thousand deaths is explicitly repaid later, in the strange scene of soldiers unknowingly dismembering its own future body of gold — but it separates that debt entirely from the question of what happens to the mind at the moment of death. Seven brief glances of compassion, arising only once genuine power has already failed to harm someone who forgave it anyway, are enough to determine an entirely different rebirth than a lifetime of sanctioned killing would predict. The compendium seems to hold both facts as equally real and equally binding: karma exacts its own precise, eventual payment, and a single change of heart at the very end still matters enormously to where one goes next.

6. The Vajra Samādhi

A malicious spirit, ignoring his own companion's warning, strikes Śāriputra's meditating head with his fist — and falls straight into hell for it, while Śāriputra himself notices nothing worse than a slight headache.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt at the Bamboo Grove of Kalandaka in Rājagṛha. The venerable Śāriputra, on Vulture Peak, entered the vajra samādhi (the Saṃyukta Āgama calls this having just shaved his own hair and beard). At that time two spirits, one named Kāla, the other Upakāla, sent by Vaiśravaṇa on an errand to King Virūpākṣa to discuss matters of gods and humans, passed by through the sky and, from a distance, saw Śāriputra seated cross-legged, his mind fixed in mindfulness before him, utterly still in samādhi.

Kāla said to his companion: "I feel I could strike this śramaṇa's head with my own fist." Upakāla answered: "Do not entertain such a thought, and do not strike this śramaṇa's head — for this śramaṇa possesses extraordinary spiritual virtue and great power; foremost in wisdom and intelligence among the World-Honored One's own disciples, he is destined otherwise to suffer boundless torment through a long night." The malicious spirit repeated, two or three times, that he was fully capable of assaulting this śramaṇa — and Upakāla warned him: "If you will not heed my words, then remain here — I shall leave you." The evil spirit asked whether he feared this śramaṇa, and Upakāla answered: "I do indeed fear him — should you truly strike him with your hand, the earth itself would split in two, a violent storm would shake the land, the devas would be seized with terror, and the Four Heavenly Kings themselves would know of it at once, leaving us with nowhere left in peace." The evil spirit, undeterred, declared he could still humiliate this śramaṇa regardless — and the good spirit, hearing this, departed and left him.

The evil spirit then struck Śāriputra's own head — and at that very instant the earth shook greatly, a violent storm arose on every side, and the ground split in two, the evil spirit's whole body falling straight down into hell (the Saṃyukta Āgama has Śāriputra himself cry out, "I am burning, I am scalded!" and the demon Kaṭa sinking into hell). Śāriputra then rose from his own samādhi, straightened his robes, and went to the Bamboo Grove, bowing his head to the World-Honored One's own feet and sitting to one side. The Buddha asked him: are you free of any bodily illness? Śāriputra answered: my body has always been free of affliction, save for a slight headache just now. The World-Honored One told him: the spirit Kāla struck your own head with his hand — had that same spirit struck Mount Sumeru itself, the mountain would have split in two; such was his own great strength, yet, through the retribution he has now incurred, he has fallen entirely into the Avīci hell.

The World-Honored One then told the assembled monks: how truly rare and wondrous the power of the vajra samādhi — that it should protect one to such a degree that even Mount Sumeru itself, striking one's head, could never move so much as a single hair. And why is this so? Listen, monks: in this fortunate kalpa, there arose a buddha named Krakucchanda, who had two great śrāvaka disciples, one named Samāyus, the other named Mahāprajñā — Samāyus foremost in supernormal power, Mahāprajñā foremost in wisdom, just as, in my own present age, Śāriputra is foremost in wisdom and Maudgalyāyana foremost in supernormal power. Both Samāyus and Mahāprajñā had likewise attained the vajra samādhi. Once, Samāyus, sitting in quiet retreat, entered a state of profound stillness — and cowherds and grass-gatherers passing by, seeing him, said to one another: "This śramaṇa has died" — and, gathering grass and wood, piled it over his body and set it alight, then left. Samāyus, rising in due course from his own samādhi, straightened his robes, and went into the village to beg; the very grass-gatherers who had burned him, seeing him alive, exclaimed to one another: "This monk died just yesterday, and we ourselves burned him — yet today he lives again!" — and, from this, gave him the very name "Returned-to-Life." Such is the majestic power of the vajra samādhi for any monk who attains it — untouched by water, fire, or blade. Śāriputra has now attained this same samādhi, and moves often between the two states of the samādhi of emptiness and the vajra samādhi alike (Source: Ekottarika Āgama, fascicle 30).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Upakāla's own warning is precise rather than vague — he does not simply say the śramaṇa is dangerous to harm, but predicts exactly what will happen, down to the earth splitting and the Four Heavenly Kings taking notice, and every detail of his prediction comes true. The story pairs this immediate scene with an older precedent, Samāyus surviving his own cremation, specifically to establish that this is not a singular miracle but a known, transferable property of a particular attainment — the vajra samādhi functions here almost as a documented technology rather than an unrepeatable act of grace.

Śāriputra and others once accepted an invitation from the group of six nuns, who prepared many fine foods for the occasion — yet gave the junior monks and novices only rice cooked sixty days prior, mixed with sesame dregs and vegetables. The Buddha asked Rāhula whether the assembly had eaten their fill, and, told the full account, asked who their senior had been — and, told it was the preceptor Śāriputra, said: Śāriputra has eaten unclean food. Afterward, many great and noble households wished to hold a meal for the sangha and specifically requested Śāriputra's own presence, petitioning the Buddha to order him to accept invitations once more — but the Buddha said not to ask this of him, for his own temperament was harsh and difficult to sway.

The healer built a fire and told the snake: would you rather enter this fire, or take back your own venom? The snake reflected that, having already spat out its venom, it would not take it back again — and threw itself into the fire instead. That venomous snake was Śāriputra himself (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, preface, lower section; also found in the Mahīśāsaka Vinaya, fascicle 31, and the Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 40).

佛告阿難。得四神足者。能住壽一劫。如來今者當壽幾許。如是至三。阿難為魔所迷默然不對。又告阿難。汝可起去靜處思惟。即起至林中。時魔波旬來至佛所。白言。世尊。處世教化度人周訖。蒙脫生死數如恒沙。時年又老可入涅槃。即告魔言。却後三月當般涅槃。波旬聞說歡喜而去。阿難睡夢見有大樹普覆虛空。一切群萌靡不蒙賴。旋風卒起吹激其樹。滅於力士所住之地。一切群生莫不悲悼。阿難驚覺怖不自寧。思惟所夢。將無世尊欲般涅槃。來至佛所而白佛言。我向所夢如斯之事。將無世尊欲般涅槃。佛告阿難。如汝所言。吾後三月當般涅槃。我向問汝。若有得四神足者。能住壽一劫。吾四神足極能修善。如是滿三而汝不對。魔來勸我當取涅槃。吾以許之。阿難悲慟不能自持。其諸弟子展轉相語。各懷悲仰來至佛所。誰得常存。我為汝等。應作已作。應說已說。汝等但當勤修精進何為憂慼。舍利弗聞佛涅槃深懷歎感。不忍見世尊而取滅度。今欲在前而入涅槃。唯願世尊當見聽許。如是至三。世尊告曰。宜知是時。一切賢聖皆當寂滅。舍利弗即整衣服三業供養却行而去。將沙彌均提羅。詣羅閱祇至本生城。即勅均提。汝往入城及至聚落。告國王大臣舊故知識諸檀越輩來共取別。均提宣告。和上舍利弗將般涅槃。諸欲見者宜可時往。阿闍世王及諸四輩各自馳奔。舍利弗如是種種廣為諸人隨病投藥。眾會有得初果乃至三果。或有出家成阿羅漢者。復有誓心求佛道者。聞說法已作禮而去。於後夜分。正身正意繫心在前入於初禪。從初禪起入第二禪。如是次第入滅盡定。從滅定起而般涅槃。時天帝釋與多天眾來至其所讚歎供養。帝釋又勅毘首羯摩。合集眾寶莊嚴高車送平博地。勅諸夜叉。往大海邊取牛頭栴檀積為大[卄/積]。安身在上蘇油灌之放火闍維。火滅之後。均提。拾取舍利盛著鉢中攝其三衣擔至佛所。阿難悲悼言。法輪大將軍已取涅槃我何憑怙。佛曰。其雖滅度五分法身亦不滅也。

舍利弗過去世時。亦不堪忍見於我死而先我前死。阿難白佛。不審往昔先前取死其事云何。佛告阿難。過去久遠不思議劫。此閻浮提有一國王。名旃陀婆羅脾(梁言月光)。王有二萬夫人婇女。其第一夫人名須摩檀(梁言華施)一萬大臣。其第一者名摩旃陀(梁言大月)。王有五百太子。最大太子。名曰尸羅跋陀(梁言戒莫)。王所住城名跋陀耆婆(梁言賢壽)。其城縱廣四百由旬。周匝凡有百二十門。設大檀施隨眾所須盡給與之。并復告下八萬四千諸小國土。悉令開藏給施。一切眾臣如教。即竪金幢擊於金鼓廣布宣令騰王慈詔。遠近內外咸令聞知。於時國內沙門婆羅門。貧窮孤老有乏短者。強弱相扶集如雲雨稱意與之。閻浮提民蒙王恩澤。有一小國。其王名曰毘摩斯那。聞月光王美稱高大。心懷嫉妬寢不安席。即自思惟。月光不除我名不出。當設方便。請諸道士募求諸人。婆羅門言。王有何憂當見告語。王言。彼月光王名德遠著一切承風。我獨卑陋無此美稱。願得除之作何方便。婆羅門言。月光慈惠澤潤窮厄如民父母。我等何心從此惡謀。寧自殺身不能為此。即各罷散不顧供養。時毘摩斯那益增愁憒。即出廣募周遍宣令。誰能為我得月光頭。分國半治以女妻之。爾時山脇有婆羅門。名勞度差。來應王募。王甚歡喜重語之言。苟能成辦不違信誓。若能去者當以何日。婆羅門曰。辦我行道糧食所須。却後七日便當發引。時婆羅門作呪自護。七日已滿。便來辭王。王給所須進路而去。時月光國豫有變怪。八萬四千諸小國王。皆夢大王金幢卒折金鼓卒裂。大月大臣夢鬼奪王金冠。各懷愁憂不能自寧。時城門神知婆羅門欲乞王頭遮不聽入。時婆羅門繞城數匝不能得前。首陀會天知月光王以此頭施於檀得滿。便於夢中而語王言。汝誓布施不逆眾心。乞者在門無由得前。欲為施主事所不然。王覺愕然。即勅大月。汝往詣門勅勿遮人。大月大臣往到城門。時城門神即自現形。白大月言。有婆羅門從他國來。懷俠惡心欲乞王頭是以不聽。大臣答曰。是為大災。然王有教理不得違。當奈之何。當作七寶頭各五百枚用貿易之。即勅令作。時婆羅門徑至殿前高聲唱言。我在遐方聞王功德。一切布施不逆人意。故涉遠來欲有所得。王聞歡喜迎為作禮。問訊行道不疲極耶。隨汝所願。婆羅門言。一切外物雖用布施非我所須。我故遠來唯乞王頭。若不孤逆當見施與。王聞是語踊躍無量。婆羅門言。若施我頭何時當與。王言。却後七日當與汝頭。

爾時大臣持七寶頭來。前語婆羅門言。此王頭者骨肉血合不淨之物用索此為。今持爾所七寶之頭以用貿易。汝可取之。博易足得終身之富。婆羅門言。我不用此。欲得王頭全我所志。時大月臣種種曉喻永不迴轉憤感心裂七分而死。王前勅語。臣下乘八千里象遍告諸國言。月光大王却後七日當持其頭施婆羅門。若欲來者速時馳詣。爾時八萬四千諸王馳驛而至。咸見大王腹拍王前。閻浮提人賴王恩澤。云何一日為一人故永捨眾庶更不矜憐。唯願垂憫莫以頭施。一萬大臣皆身投地腹拍王前唯見哀憫矜恤我等。莫以頭施。二萬夫人亦身投地仰白王言。莫見忘捨唯垂蔭覆。若以頭施我等何怙。五百太子啼哭王前。我等孩幼當何所歸。願見愍念莫以頭施。長養我等得及人倫。於是大王告諸臣民夫人太子。計我從本受身已來。涉歷生死由來長久。若在三塗截斷其頭死而復生。如是無數亦無福報。若生人間諍於財色。為貪恚癡恒殺多身。未曾為福而捨此命。今我此身種種不淨會當捐棄不能得久。捨此穢頭用貿大利何得不與。我持此頭施婆羅門。以是功德誓求佛道度汝等苦。今我施心垂欲成滿。慎莫遮我無上道意。一切諸王臣民夫人太子聞王語已默然無言。爾時大王語婆羅門。欲取頭者今正是時。婆羅門言。今王臣民大眾圍遶。我獨一身力勢單弱不堪此中而斫王頭。欲與我者當至後園。爾時大王告諸小王太子臣民。汝等若必愛敬我者。慎勿傷害此婆羅門。作此語已。共婆羅門入於後園。時婆羅門又語王言。汝身盛壯力士之力。若遭斫痛儻復還悔。取汝頭髮緊繫在樹爾乃能斫。時王用語語婆羅門。汝斫我頭墮我手中然後取去。今以頭施用求無上正真之道誓濟群生。時婆羅門舉刀欲斫。樹神見此甚大懊惱。如此之人云何欲殺。即以手搏婆羅門耳。其頭反向手脚了戾。失刀在地不能動搖。爾時大王仰語樹神。我過去已來於此樹下。曾以九百九十九頭以用布施。今捨此頭便當滿千。捨此頭已於檀便具。汝莫遮我無上道心。爾時樹神聞王是語。使婆羅門平復如故。時婆羅門便從地起。還更取刀便斫王頭頭墮手中。爾時天地六反震動諸天宮殿掉動不安。各懷恐怖怪其所以。尋見菩薩為一切故捨頭布施。皆悉來下感其奇特悲淚如雨。因共讚言。月光大王以頭布施。於檀波羅蜜今已得滿。音聲普遍。彼毘摩羡王聞此語已。喜踊驚愕心擘裂而死。時婆羅門擔王頭去。諸王臣民夫人太子。已見王頭自投于地。同聲悲叫絕而復蘇。時婆羅門嫌王頭臭即便擲地脚蹴而去。或復有人語婆羅門。汝之酷毒劇甚乃爾。既不中用何為乃索。時婆羅門進道而去。人見便責無給食者。飢餓委悴困切極理。道中有人自問消息。知毘摩羡王已復命終。失於所望及勞度差命終。皆墮阿鼻泥犁。其餘臣民思念王恩感結死者皆得生天。如是阿難。欲知爾時月光王者。今我身是。毘摩羡王今波旬是。時大月大臣今舍利弗是。當於爾時不忍見我死而先我前死。乃至今日不忍見我入於涅槃。而先滅度(出賢愚經第五卷及方便佛報恩經大同小異)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Ānanda: one who has attained the four bases of supernormal power may extend his own lifespan for a full kalpa — how long, then, might the Tathāgata himself now live? He asked this three times, but Ānanda, his own mind clouded by Māra, remained silent and did not answer. The Buddha then told Ānanda to rise and go to a quiet place to reflect, and Ānanda went off into a grove.

At that time Māra Pāpīyas came before the Buddha and said: World-Honored One, your own teaching and deliverance of beings throughout the world is now complete, and you have freed as many beings from birth and death as there are grains of sand in the Ganges; your own years, too, are now advanced — you may enter nirvāṇa. The Buddha told Māra: three months from now, I shall enter final nirvāṇa. Māra, hearing this, departed rejoicing.

Ānanda, meanwhile, dreamed of a great tree whose branches sheltered the whole sky, every living thing relying upon it — until a sudden whirlwind arose and struck the tree down, uprooting it at the very place where a certain strong man dwelt, and every being grieved at its fall. Startled awake and deeply unsettled, Ānanda, reflecting on this dream, feared it meant the World-Honored One intended to enter nirvāṇa, and went to the Buddha to report it. The Buddha told him: it is exactly as you say — three months from now I shall enter final nirvāṇa. I asked you earlier whether one who has attained the four bases of supernormal power, cultivated to their fullest, might extend his own life a full kalpa — and, three times over, you gave no answer. Māra then came and urged me toward nirvāṇa, and I have consented. Ānanda, overcome with grief, could not compose himself; the other disciples too, hearing this news from one another, came grieving to the Buddha — but he told them: who can live forever? I have done for you all that needed doing, and taught all that needed teaching — you need only continue your own diligent practice; why grieve so?

Śāriputra, hearing that the Buddha's own nirvāṇa was near, was deeply moved, and, unable to bear witnessing the World-Honored One's own final passing, asked three times for permission to enter his own nirvāṇa first. The World-Honored One told him: you know the proper time — for every sage and worthy must one day pass into stillness. Śāriputra then arranged his robes, made offerings of his own three karmic actions, and, walking backward in reverence, took his leave, together with the novice Cundaka, traveling to Rājagṛha, to the very city of his own birth.

He instructed Cundaka: go into the city and the surrounding villages, and tell the king, the ministers, my own old friends and acquaintances, and all my lay supporters to come and take their leave of me. Cundaka proclaimed accordingly: the preceptor Śāriputra is about to enter final nirvāṇa — whoever wishes to see him should come at once. King Ajātaśatru and the fourfold assembly all hurried there. Śāriputra, in this way, taught extensively, prescribing the right medicine for each person's own affliction, and the assembly there attained, variously, the first through third fruits of the path, some going forth and becoming arhats, others vowing to seek buddhahood — and, having heard his teaching, each bowed and departed.

In the latter part of that night, composing his body and mind, fixing his attention before him, he entered the first dhyāna, then rose from it into the second, and so on in sequence up through the cessation of perception and feeling — then, rising from that same attainment, entered final nirvāṇa. Śakra, together with a great host of devas, came to that place to praise and make offerings; Śakra further ordered Viśvakarman to gather every kind of treasure and adorn a lofty chariot to carry the body to level, open ground, and ordered the yakṣas to fetch sandalwood from the ocean's shore and stack it into a great pyre. The body was placed upon it, anointed with ghee, and the pyre set alight. Once the fire had burned out, Cundaka gathered the relics into a bowl, took up the three robes, and carried them to the Buddha. Ānanda, grieving, said: the great general of the Dharma-wheel has now entered nirvāṇa — on whom shall I now rely? The Buddha answered: though he has passed away, the five-part Dharma-body does not itself perish.

Śāriputra, in a past life too, could not bear to see me die, and so passed away himself first before me. Ānanda asked the Buddha to explain how this had come about in that earlier time, and the Buddha told him:

Long ago, in an inconceivably distant past kalpa, there lived in this Jambudvīpa a king named Candraprabha (in the Liang language, "Moonlight"). The king had twenty thousand consorts and palace women, his chief consort named Sumati-dāna (in the Liang language, "Flower-Giving"), and ten thousand ministers, the foremost among them named Mahācandra (in the Liang language, "Great Moon"). The king had five hundred princes, the eldest named Śīlabhadra (in the Liang language, "Precept-Wisdom"). The city in which the king dwelt was named Bhadrāyus (in the Liang language, "Worthy Longevity"), four hundred yojanas in breadth and length, with a hundred twenty gates in all. The king made a great vow of giving, providing to the multitude whatever they needed, and further ordered his own eighty-four thousand lesser kingdoms to likewise open their own storehouses and give freely — his ministers, obeying, raised golden banners and struck golden gongs, proclaiming the king's own compassionate decree far and wide, within and without, so that every śramaṇa, brahmin, and destitute or orphaned person in the kingdom, strong and weak alike, supporting one another, gathered like clouds and rain to receive whatever they wished. All the people of Jambudvīpa were thus blessed by the king's own grace.

In a smaller kingdom nearby, its own king, named Bhīmasena, hearing of King Candraprabha's own vast and lofty renown, grew consumed by jealousy, unable even to sleep, and reflected: unless King Candraprabha's own name is somehow effaced, my own name will never rise — I must devise some scheme. He summoned various ascetics and asked them how he might rid himself of this rival, and the brahmins answered: King Candraprabha's own compassion and beneficence reach the poor and needy as a father and mother reach their own children — we ourselves alone remain humble and undistinguished by comparison; how could we have any heart to join such a wicked plot? We would sooner take our own lives than participate — and they all departed, refusing his patronage entirely.

King Bhīmasena, ever more troubled, then proclaimed widely throughout his kingdom: whoever can bring me King Candraprabha's own head shall receive half my kingdom to govern, and my own daughter in marriage. A brahmin named Raudrākṣa, dwelling in the mountainside, answered this call. The king, delighted, asked him whether, once he succeeded, he would keep faith with his own oath, and when he would set out. The brahmin answered that he needed only provisions and equipment for the journey, and would depart within seven days. He then performed protective incantations over himself, and, the seven days complete, took his leave of the king, who provided for his journey and sent him on his way.

In King Candraprabha's own kingdom, meanwhile, ill omens had already appeared — the eighty-four thousand lesser kings all dreamed that the great king's own golden banner had suddenly snapped, and his golden gong suddenly cracked; the minister Mahācandra dreamed that a spirit had seized the king's own golden crown — and all were filled with anxious foreboding. The city gate spirit, knowing the brahmin intended to beg the king's own head, barred him from entering; the brahmin circled the city walls repeatedly, unable to gain entrance. A deva of the Śuddhāvāsa heaven, knowing that King Candraprabha's own vow of giving his head would soon be fulfilled, appeared to the king in a dream and told him: you have vowed to give without turning away anyone's own heart — yet a beggar now stands at your very gate, unable to pass. To act as a true giver, this ought not be so. The king, waking with a start, at once ordered his minister Mahācandra to go to the gate and command that no one be turned away.

Mahācandra went to the gate, where the gate-spirit manifested itself and told him: a brahmin has come from another country, harboring a wicked intent — he wishes to beg the king's own head — and it is for this reason I have refused him entry. The minister answered: this is indeed a great calamity — yet the king's own decree cannot be defied. What is to be done? He resolved to have five hundred heads of the seven treasures crafted, to offer in exchange, and ordered this done at once.

The brahmin then went straight to the palace hall and called out loudly: I have come from a distant land, having heard of the king's own virtue, that he turns away no one's own wish in giving — and so I have traveled far, hoping to receive something in turn. The king, delighted, went to greet him and bowed, asking whether his journey had been too wearying, and told him to ask for whatever he desired. The brahmin said: every external object, though freely given, is not truly what I need — I have come this great distance seeking only the king's own head; if you will not disappoint me, please grant it. The king, hearing this, was overjoyed beyond measure. The brahmin asked when he might receive it, and the king answered: seven days from now, I shall give you my own head.

The minister Mahācandra then brought forward the five hundred jeweled heads and told the brahmin: the king's own head, made of mere flesh, blood, and bone, is an unclean thing — why would you want it? Take instead these jeweled heads of equal number in exchange, wealth enough to secure you for the rest of your own life. The brahmin refused, saying he wanted only the king's true head, to fulfill his own resolve. Mahācandra pleaded with him in every way he could, but the man would not be moved — and, in his own grief and despair, the minister's heart broke into seven pieces, and he died on the spot.

The king then ordered his own attendants to mount swift elephants and proclaim throughout every kingdom: King Candraprabha, seven days hence, shall give his own head to a brahmin — whoever wishes to come and see this should hurry now. The eighty-four thousand lesser kings all rushed to the capital by relay, and, seeing their great king, threw themselves before him, beating their own chests: the people of Jambudvīpa depend entirely on your own grace — how could you, for the sake of one man, abandon all of us without pity? We beg you not to give away your head. His ten thousand ministers likewise threw themselves to the ground, beating their chests, begging him to have mercy and not give his head. His twenty thousand consorts likewise threw themselves down, pleading: do not forsake us — without your protection, on whom shall we depend, should you give away your head? His five hundred princes wept before him: we are still children — where shall we go, if you give away your head? Please, out of love for us, do not do this.

The great king then addressed his own ministers, people, consorts, and princes: reflect that, since I first received this body, I have passed through birth and death across ages beyond counting; wandering the three lower realms, my head has been cut off and I have died and been reborn countless times, gaining no merit from it at all; and even reborn among humans, I have fought over wealth and desire, killing again and again out of greed, anger, and delusion, never once giving up my life for the sake of merit. This body, in its very nature unclean, must in any case be discarded before long and cannot be kept forever — to give up this filthy head in exchange for so great a benefit, how could I refuse? I now give this head to the brahmin, vowing through this very merit to attain the buddha's own path and deliver you all from suffering. My own resolve to give is now on the verge of fulfillment — do not, I beg you, obstruct my unsurpassed intent. Hearing the king's own words, all the assembled kings, ministers, consorts, and princes fell silent, unable to answer.

The great king then told the brahmin: now is the time — take my head. The brahmin answered: with the king's own ministers and people crowded all around, I am but one man, too weak to behead the king here in the midst of this crowd — if you truly wish to give it to me, come instead to the rear garden. The king then told his assembled lesser kings, princes, ministers, and people: if you truly love and honor me, do not harm this brahmin — and, having said this, went together with him into the rear garden.

There the brahmin told the king: your own body is young and vigorous, strong as a warrior's — should the blow cause you pain, you might yet change your mind; let me first bind your own hair to a tree, that I may then behead you properly. The king answered: cut off my head, and let it fall into my own hands before you take it away — I give this head now in pursuit of the unsurpassed, true path, vowing to deliver all beings.

As the brahmin raised his knife to strike, the tree's own spirit, watching in deep distress, thought: how could anyone wish to kill such a man? — and struck the brahmin's own ear with her hand, so that his head twisted backward and his limbs went rigid, the knife falling from his hand, and he could not move. The great king then looked up and addressed the tree spirit: beneath this very tree, across my own past lives, I have already given away nine hundred ninety-nine heads in offering — this head now would make the full thousand, completing my own perfection of giving. Do not obstruct my unsurpassed resolve. Hearing this, the tree spirit released the brahmin, restoring him to himself; he rose from the ground, took up the knife once more, and struck off the king's own head, which fell into his own waiting hands.

At that moment the earth shook six ways, and every heavenly palace trembled and grew unsettled, the devas filled with astonished dread — until, looking to see the cause, they beheld a bodhisattva giving up his own head for the sake of all beings, and descended together, weeping tears like rain, praising with one voice: King Candraprabha, in giving his own head, has now fulfilled the perfection of giving — and this praise resounded everywhere. King Bhīmasena, hearing this same news, was so overcome with mingled joy and horror that his own heart split apart, and he died at once.

The brahmin then carried the king's own head away; the assembled kings, ministers, consorts, and princes, seeing it, threw themselves to the ground, crying out together in grief, fainting and reviving in turn. The brahmin, finding the head's own scent already foul, cast it to the ground and kicked it aside as he departed. Someone said to him: your own cruelty has proven so extreme — and, the head now useless to you regardless, why did you demand it at all? The brahmin continued on his way, and everyone he met reproached him and refused him food, until, starving and wasted, he collapsed in utter distress along the road. There, someone who happened to inquire after the news told him that King Bhīmasena had already died — and, his hope thus extinguished, the brahmin Raudrākṣa too soon died, falling straight into the Avīci hell. As for the rest, the ministers and people, moved by grief and gratitude for the king's own kindness, were, upon their own deaths, all reborn in heaven.

Know, Ānanda: that King Candraprabha of the story was myself; King Bhīmasena is now Māra Pāpīyas; the minister Mahācandra is now Śāriputra — who, unable then to bear witnessing my own death, died himself before me, just as, even now, he could not bear to witness my own entry into nirvāṇa, and so entered his own final nirvāṇa first (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 5; largely the same, with minor variations, in the Sūtra of the Buddha's Repayment of Kindness through Skillful Means).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story lets every possible obstacle to the king's own giving be voiced in full — his minister's grief-broken heart, his entire family and court prostrate before him, the tree spirit's own physical intervention against the brahmin — before finally letting the gift proceed regardless, as though testing whether the compendium's own repeated praise of unconditional giving can survive contact with every legitimate reason not to. The brahmin's own anticlimactic ending, kicking the head aside in disgust the moment it proves useless to him, and starving to death unmourned along the road, is the story's own quiet verdict on the taker rather than the giver: greed, once it gets exactly what it demanded, discovers it never actually wanted the thing itself.

Śāriputra untied his own outer sash and set it before Maudgalyāyana, saying: you possess great supernormal power — lift this sash and tie it around the Jambu tree of this continent. Maudgalyāyana took up the sash but could not move it at all; straining with all his strength to lift it, the very earth shook instead.

Śāriputra, fearing Maudgalyāyana might strain himself past Pūrvavideha, then had him wrap it instead around Mount Sumeru — and Maudgalyāyana was able to move Mount Sumeru itself with it. Śāriputra then had the sash wrapped around the Tathāgata's own seat, and Maudgalyāyana found he could not move it at all, and, giving up the sash, returned to the dragon king's own place — only to see, from a distance, that Śāriputra had already arrived there before him, seated cross-legged, his body upright and his mind fixed in mindfulness. For I vanished from Jetavana before Śāriputra did, yet did not reappear at this lake until after him — while Śāriputra, vanishing after me, arrived here first and was already seated. Maudgalyāyana, accepting this charge, rose from his seat and went to the summit of Mount Sumeru, placing one foot upon its peak and raising the other to rest upon the Brahmā heaven, then shook the mountain with his own foot so that the earth trembled six ways — and, in the very voice of Brahmā, spoke this verse:

"Seek quickly for skillful means, within this assembly of the Buddha's own Dharma; cast off the affliction of birth and death, as an elephant tramples through bamboo leaves.

The assembled monks, astonished, declared they had never witnessed such a thing before. As Mahāmaudgalyāyana spoke this verse, sixty monks, hearing it, had their own outflows exhausted and their minds liberated on the spot (Source: Ekottarika Āgama, fascicle 27).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Maudgalyāyana's own anxious question to the Buddha — have I lost my own supernormal power? — is a rare moment of genuine self-doubt from a figure otherwise defined throughout this fascicle by effortless, spectacular displays of strength. The Buddha's answer carefully avoids ranking the two disciples against each other at all: Maudgalyāyana has not regressed, Śāriputra simply possesses great wisdom — two different, non-competing excellences rather than one contest with a winner, even though the entry's own title frames it as exactly that kind of contest.

10. The Jeweled Bridge

Heterodox ascetics race to cross a flooded river first and claim the seat of honor at a great feast, exhausting themselves through a sleepless night of failed attempts — only to see Maudgalyāyana conjure a jeweled bridge for the Buddha that vanishes the instant they try to use it for themselves.

Ru-Yi Translation

At Jetavana Monastery, meanwhile, people had come to invite the sangha to a meal, and the Buddha waited for the proper hour. A young monk, worried, said: the World-Honored One delays so long — I fear the heterodox ascetics will claim the seat of honor first. The Buddha, knowing the time had come, set out with the whole assembly, dignified and unhurried, to the riverbank. The heterodox ascetics called out: we ourselves cannot cross this river — what plan could these mere śramaṇas possibly have?

Maudgalyāyana then conjured a jeweled bridge, adorned splendidly with flowers, fragrance, and music. Seeing this, the heterodox ascetics said to one another: the śramaṇas have arrived too slowly — let us cross first! — and, stepping onto the bridge, fell straight into the water, their own bodies soaked through, swept along helplessly by the current. The Buddha and his monks then advanced calmly, in proper order — and, as each footstep passed over it, the jeweled bridge behind them simply vanished. Once Maudgalyāyana withdrew his own power, the ground returned to its ordinary state, allowing the heterodox ascetics, too, to cross at last. The Buddha then spoke this verse:

"Those who reach this farther shore first have already crossed the ocean of birth and death; the swiftest current cannot sweep them away — these are called the truly wise" (Source: Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 6).

11. Ullambana: Maudgalyāyana Saves His Mother

Maudgalyāyana, newly awakened, finds his own mother reborn among the hungry ghosts, her food turning to burning coals the instant it nears her mouth — and learns from the Buddha that only the combined merit of the entire monastic sangha, offered together on a single day, can undo a debt his own power alone cannot touch.

Chinese Source

目連始得道。欲度父母報乳哺恩。見其亡母生餓鬼中。不見飲食皮骨相連。目連悲哀。即鉢盛飯往餉其母。母得鉢飯食未入口化成火炭。目連馳還具陳此事。佛言。汝母罪根深結。非汝一人力所奈何。當須眾僧威神之力乃得解脫。可以七月十五日。為七世父母厄難中者。具飯五菓汲罐盆器香油鋌燭床褥臥具盡世甘美供養眾僧。其日眾聖六通聲聞緣覺菩薩示現比丘在大眾中。皆同一心受鉢和羅具清淨戒。其有供養此等僧者。七世父母五種親屬。得出三塗應時解脫衣食自然。佛勅眾僧。皆為施主家七世父母。行禪定意然後食供(出盂蘭經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Maudgalyāyana, having just attained the path, wished to deliver his own parents in repayment for their kindness in nursing and raising him. Seeing his own deceased mother reborn among the hungry ghosts, unable to find food or drink, her skin clinging directly to her bones, Maudgalyāyana, grieving, filled a bowl with rice and brought it to her at once. His mother, receiving the bowl, took up the rice — but, before it even reached her mouth, it transformed into burning coals. Maudgalyāyana rushed back and reported this whole matter to the Buddha, who told him: your own mother's karmic root of sin runs too deep to be undone by your own strength alone — you will need the majestic power of the assembled sangha to free her.

You should, on the fifteenth day of the seventh month, on behalf of your parents and ancestors across seven generations who now suffer in distress, prepare rice, the five fruits, water-jars, basins, incense oil, candles, bedding, and every fine delicacy the world affords, and offer them to the assembled sangha. On that day, every sage — arhats of the six supernormal powers, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattva-monks manifesting among the ordinary assembly — will be present together, all receiving their bowls and pravāraṇā offerings with a single, unified mind, upholding the pure precepts. Whoever makes offerings to such a sangha shall see their own parents across seven generations, and their five kinds of kin, delivered at once from the three lower realms, their clothing and food arising spontaneously. The Buddha instructed the assembled sangha that all should, on behalf of the donor's own household and their ancestors across seven generations, first enter meditative concentration before receiving the offered food (Source: Ullambana Sūtra).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This short sūtra became, across the Chinese-speaking world, one of the most consequential texts in this entire compendium — the source of Yúlánpén (盂蘭盆), the Ghost Festival observed on the fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month, still marked today with exactly the offerings this sūtra specifies. What the text itself insists on, and what later popular practice sometimes loses, is that Maudgalyāyana's own supernormal power — foremost among all the Buddha's disciples — proves entirely insufficient on its own; his mother's deliverance requires the combined merit of an entire assembled sangha, offered on a single fixed day, rather than one devoted son's private effort however great. The festival's own origin story is, at bottom, an argument for collective practice over solitary heroism, even for someone capable of moving Mount Sumeru with his own foot.

14. Humbled and Vindicated in a Farther World

Maudgalyāyana travels to a buddha-land where he stands barely as tall as the rim of a bowl, dismissed as an insect by bodhisattvas who tower over him — until a single display of power reveals countless worlds within his own conjured throne, and he is gently rebuked in turn for presuming to test the reach of a buddha's own voice.

That Buddha Prabhāsarāja then told Mahāmaudgalyāyana: the bodhisattvas and śrāvakas of my own land, seeing your small stature, have all given rise to some contempt — you should now display your own supernormal power, relying upon Śākyamuni's own majestic virtue. Maudgalyāyana bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him seven times in reverence, and, standing again before him, asked: might I now sit cross-legged upon this very ground? The Buddha answered: as you please. Maudgalyāyana then leapt into the sky, rising a hundred million fathoms, and, in that jeweled city, fashioned a seat, sitting cross-legged upon it — and from that very seat hung countless jeweled strands, each bead radiating hundreds of thousands of lights, each light bearing a lotus flower, and upon every lotus appeared Śākyamuni Buddha's own form, seated, teaching the Dharma in that same voice, clear and pure, expounding the very same sūtras without the slightest difference. Having displayed this power, Maudgalyāyana returned to stand once more before the Buddha.

The assembled bodhisattvas there, marveling at what they had never seen before, asked the Buddha why Maudgalyāyana had come to this world at all. The World-Honored One answered: he wished to test how far Śākyamuni Buddha's own voice could reach, and so came here. That Buddha Prabhāsarāja then told Mahāmaudgalyāyana: you ought not to test the Tathāgata's own voice in this way — a buddha's own voice knows no near or far, its reach vast and boundless, beyond any comparison. Mahāmaudgalyāyana threw himself down at once in repentance, confessing: World-Honored One, I acted rashly and did not truly understand — the Buddha's own voice is indeed boundless, and I foolishly sought to measure its limit. Prabhāsarāja Buddha told him: though you have come this great distance to this buddha-land, you will find it — and, when he further asked how far he had actually traveled, was told: exceedingly far, O heaven among heavens; your body is now too weary to return to your own land. The World-Honored One told him: it is only through Śākyamuni Buddha's own majestic power that you were able to come here at all — bow, then, in the direction of the east, toward Śākyamuni Buddha, and take refuge with joined palms, and you shall find yourself there at once; for should you attempt, through your own supernormal power alone, to return to your own land, a full kalpa would not suffice. Maudgalyāyana knelt on his right knee, faced east toward Śākyamuni Buddha, and took refuge with joined palms — and, in the time it takes to bend and stretch an arm, found himself there at once (Source: Sūtra of the Secret Trace Vajra-Wielder, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Maudgalyāyana's own two errors in this entry are symmetrical: first, he is judged too small by beings who cannot see past his own literal size, a misjudgment the story corrects by having him display power on a scale that dwarfs the misjudgment itself; then, having just proven his own vastness, he immediately overreaches in the opposite direction, presuming to measure something — a buddha's own voice — that was never comparable to his own power in the first place. The story does not let his second failure cancel out his first vindication; instead it simply adds a further boundary, teaching that even the disciple foremost in supernormal power remains entirely dependent on Śākyamuni's own authority to so much as travel home again.

15. The Mountain That Would Not Move

The Buddha told Maudgalyāyana: there is a great kingdom, eight thousand leagues from here on the frontier, which has not yet seen the Three Jewels and remains sunk in confusion; its king and people all serve brahmacārin ascetics instead. Five hundred of these ascetics have attained the five supernormal powers, able to move mountains, redirect rivers, divide their own bodies, and transform at will. A great mountain in that kingdom blocked the people's own roads, troubling the entire land, and the king appealed to the ascetics for help; they surrounded the mountain, sitting in a circle, and, focusing their minds through the power of their own concentration, began to lift it and move it aside.

The Buddha told Maudgalyāyana: go to that kingdom and display the power of the true path, that you might deliver the ascetics and the whole country's own king and people, freeing them from the three lower realms and bringing them at last to the hall of blessing. Maudgalyāyana went, and, emitting light that blotted out the sun and moon, hovered suspended directly above the mountain's own summit — so that the mountain remained fixed, unable to move. 16. The Dog-Headed Spirit's Confession

A radiant, wealthy spirit dwelling in a jeweled palace bears a dog's own head on a human body — the exact, literal shape of a single careless remark he made about beggars in a past life, even as the very same generosity that made the remark still earns him his palace.

Chinese Source

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The spirit's own two features never resolve into a single verdict — the palace and the dog's head sit on the same body, one earned by generosity, the other by cruelty toward the very people that generosity was meant to serve. The literalism is deliberate: the specific words used against beggars become, with almost comic precision, the specific animal head worn forever after, as though the compendium wants the punishment to be legible at a glance rather than proportioned abstractly to some general notion of unkindness.

19–20. Maudgalyāyana's Honest Mistakes

The fascicle closes with two occasions when Maudgalyāyana's own reported facts turned out wrong on inspection — a hall he claimed had pillars that a colleague found bare, a rain he predicted that an asura king secretly diverted into the ocean — and the Buddha's own defense of him each time: that he spoke exactly what he had truly seen.

Maudgalyāyana's own heart had been sincere throughout, though he had not himself verified the fact — and so he had not truly broken any precept (Source: Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 29).

目連心實事虛二十

人問目連。是多浮池水從何處來。目連答言。此水從阿耨達池中來。諸比丘言。阿耨達池其水甜美有八功德。此水沸熱醎苦。何有此事。目連。汝空言過人法故。作妄語應滅擯驅出。以事問佛。佛語比丘。汝等莫說目連過罪。何以故。阿耨達龍住處去此極遠。是水本有八功德甜美。逕歷五百小地獄。過是故醎熱。汝等若問目連是水何故醎熱。目連能隨相答。又一時大旱無雨。目連入定見。却後七日天當大雨。滿諸溝坑。城邑聚落悉聞此言皆大歡喜。國中人民皆捨眾務覆屏蓋藏。數到七日。諸比丘語目連。汝言七日天當大雨滿諸溝坑。今風尚無何況雨耶。汝空言過人法故妄語欲滅擯驅出。佛聞是事。語諸比丘。目連見前不見後。如來見前亦見後。七日有大雨下。有羅睺阿修羅王。以手接去置大海中。目連隨心想說。是故無罪(出十誦律第四卷)。

A True Heart, a Mistaken Fact

Someone asked Maudgalyāyana where the water of a certain overflowing pond came from, and he answered: this water comes from Lake Anavatapta. The other monks objected: the water of Lake Anavatapta is sweet and possesses the eight virtues — yet this water is scalding hot, salty, and bitter — how could this possibly be so? They said: Maudgalyāyana has spoken idly, falsely claiming a power beyond ordinary human reach, and ought to be formally expelled for it, and brought the matter before the Buddha. The Buddha told the monks: do not speak of Maudgalyāyana's own fault in this — for though the dragon king Anavatapta's own dwelling lies exceedingly far from here, and its water there does indeed possess the eight virtues, sweet and pure at its source, it passes along its course through five hundred small hells before reaching here, and, having passed through them, becomes salty and hot. Had you simply asked Maudgalyāyana why the water was salty and hot, he could have explained the very same thing in return.

On another occasion, during a great drought with no rain at all, Maudgalyāyana entered samādhi and foresaw that, seven days hence, heaven would send a great rain, filling every ditch and pond. The whole city, hearing this, rejoiced greatly, and the people of the kingdom set aside their ordinary work to cover and store away their own belongings in preparation. When the seventh day came, the other monks said to Maudgalyāyana: you said heaven would send a great rain in seven days to fill every ditch and pond — yet there is not even wind today, let alone rain! You have spoken idly, falsely claiming a power beyond ordinary human reach, and ought to be formally expelled for it.

The Buddha, hearing of this, told the monks: Maudgalyāyana sees only what is immediately before him, not what lies beyond it — but the Tathāgata sees both equally. On the seventh day, a great rain did indeed fall — but the asura king Rāhu, reaching out his own hand, gathered it up and cast it into the great ocean instead. Maudgalyāyana had spoken only according to what his own mind had truly perceived — he was, for this reason, without fault (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both stories share the same structure: an eager assembly of monks, ready to formally expel the disciple foremost in supernormal power over what looks like a plain factual error, and a Buddha who intervenes not by denying the facts but by explaining a cause the accusers themselves never thought to ask about. What ends this fascicle's long survey of Maudgalyāyana's own spectacular power, then, is not another display of it at all, but a quieter claim about the limits even his power operates within — that supernormal sight, however vast, still sees only from where it stands, and that a report can be entirely honest and still incomplete. Placed last, after eighteen entries of dragons subdued, mountains halted, and mothers rescued from hungry-ghost realms, the pairing insists that the same disciple capable of grinding Sumeru to dust remains, in the end, answerable to facts he could not have known — and that this, too, is no fault in him at all.
