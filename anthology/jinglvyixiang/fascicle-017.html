Lesser-Known Arhats

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 17 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Arhat Śrāvaka Monks, Part 6" (聲聞無學僧部第六) — nineteen entries on less prominent disciples, opening with the fascicle's own longest and most novelistic entry: the tragedy of Saṃghabala, murdered piece by piece in the wilderness by bandits his own brother hired, forgiving everyone and attaining arhatship as he dies. Also included: Cūḍapanthaka's famous "sweep the broom" mnemonic, a third telling of Aṅgulimāla's conversion, and the caṇḍāla boy Nīti, lifted bodily into the sky by the Buddha's own hand.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 17 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 17 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 17 continues the compendium's survey of arhat disciples, gathering nineteen figures less prominent than Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, or Ānanda, several of them known to history chiefly through this and closely related sources. The opening entry, the tragedy of Saṃghabala, runs to novella length and is translated here in its complete, uncut form — the single longest entry in the anthology so far. Two sequential entries sharing one figure, Cūḍapanthaka, are grouped together; every entry's full text otherwise remains intact.

1. Saṃghabala's Death in the Wilderness

A younger brother renounces the world after an arranged marriage he never wanted — and his own elder brother, consumed by desire for the abandoned bride, hires bandits to murder him piece by piece in the mountains, unaware that each cut only carries the dying man further along the path to arhatship.

Chinese Source

舍衛國人名曰厲。其家大富。年已老耄絕無繼嗣。禱祀日月天神無不必修。終不能得。自念祈請無益。寶財消散產業不修。疾病相仍災害首尾。奴婢死亡六畜不孳。為妖㜸神師所迷。云當有福而禍重至。由盲人吞毒謂為良藥。庶有瘳損遂喪其身。吾既殺生祠祀當入地獄。而望天祚豈不惑哉。世有佛道高操之聖。學得仙者名曰應真。淨如琉璃精進存想乃覩之耳。奉斯道者唯守靖寞。無欲無求以斯為樂。現世得安終生天上。置吾常供養佛三尊。經涉一載婦遇生男。字曰佛大。後復生男。字曰僧大。厲訓二子。示以聖道。僧大稟性慈孝仁愛萬物。奉佛法戒親近沙門。清淨知足二親愛之。厲臥疾著床。即呼長子涕泣誡之。夫生有死持戒者安犯戒者危。僧大尚小仁孝清白。方以累汝言竟便沒故。後頻告數啟其兄欲作沙門。其國法欲得婦。其言欲作沙門。佛大即為娉妻。索賢家女字快見。光華煒煒端正少雙。婦歸升堂兄會賓客九族欣然。兄於眾前謂其弟曰。當今之日肯作沙門乎。僧大答曰。實我宿願。兄戲之曰。可從爾志。弟心歡喜為兄作禮即便入山。見一沙門。年少端正獨處樹下。問曰。賢者何緣行作沙門。其人已得應真之道。豫知去來無數劫事。謂僧大曰。佛說人好婬泆。如持炬火逆風而行。其焰稍却不置炬者火燒其手。猶烏銜肉。鷹鸇追奪。烏不置肉災及軀命。吾以是故行作沙門。又如蜜塗利刀。小兒貪甜以舌舐之。有截舌之患。婬泆之人苟快愚心。不惟其後。有燒身之害。如蛾貪火色投入于燈。體見燒煮將何剋獲。為婬惑者不別善惡。遠賢親愚日就流冥。亡國滅眾死入地獄。惡著罪成悔將何逮(喻多不能備載)如是譬類。僧大頓首足下。願去世濁履清淨道。奉沙門戒以為榮福。師曰小待。僧大曰。意欲入山禪寂。師曰。處山澤者當學星宿。明知候時常當儲待水火麨蜜。所以然者。盜賊之求水火麨蜜。夜半向晨。問當解之。給賊所欲違其意者。賊輒殺人。僧大曰諾。敬奉慈教。却乃入山。其兄念曰。弟作沙門終不畜妻。快見端正心甚悅之。起從快見取琴彈之。歌婬泆之曲曰。煌煌欝金生于野。過時不採花見棄捐。曼爾豐熾華色惟新。與我同歡。快見覺欲為亂。以歌答之曰。巍巍我師天人之尊。門徒清潔諡曰沙門。觀真為聖婬為畜倫。我受嚴戒不事二君。終不婬生寧就寸分。佛大作情悲之曲委靡之辭。宿心加爾故因良媒問名。詣師占相良時。慘慘惕惕。懼爾不來。既覩爾顏我心怡怡。今不合歡豈徒費哉。斯誓為定淑女何疑。快見惶灼。歌答之曰。佛設禮儀尊卑有敘。叔妻即子。婿伯即父。我親奉戒日有隆舉。真與聖齊婬正。蟲鼠。噫乎伯子烏。為斯語。兄心貪迷。快見知其意甚不可轉移。快見又歌曰。夫人處世當遠二事不孝婬亂行違佛戒。天及賢者。箋其自異。佛大歌曰。爾之容色燡燡灼然。普天美女豈有此爾顏。我心相悅故踰大山。快見自念。此人欲我悖狂之亂。沮致大難。請說身中惡露不淨。爾乃却耳。快見重曰。仁貪我軀軀有何好。頭有九骨合為髑髏。如是具說諸不淨。佛大自念。汝念其婿何肯聽我。我殺弟者爾乃隨耳。即行募求數為賊者。前與語曰。寧知我家所畜六籍奴子逃作沙門今在山中。賊曰識之。佛大即出金銀與之。爾殺奴子疾取其頭。及身上衣所持法服。足下履屣皆以將還。吾復重賜卿等金銀。賊大喜曰。從吾取足即去入山。到其弟所呼曰。沙門汝疾出來。其弟出曰。諸君何求。吾有水火麨蜜可食。夜時已半。賊曰。不求水火。麨蜜不問。卿時欲得汝頭持走之耳。其弟聞之。即大惶怖涕泣而曰。吾非長者諸候子也。捨俗為道與世無諍。學道日淺未獲溝港殺吾何益。賊曰。來為汝首。其弟語賊曰。欲得寶者吾與兄書令惠卿寶。賊曰。子兄令我今來殺子。弟曰吾今死矣。由斯婦也。師前戒我。人與婬居。如持炬火逆風而行。若不捨之火將燒手。日冥即如師戒涕泣。從賊乞一歲活。賊曰。急取頭去。其弟重曰。願莫即見殺。先斷我一髀置吾前也。賊斷一髀置於其前。弟遭此痛。天來其側曰。慎勿恐怖。牢持汝心。汝前世時入畜生中。人所屠割稱賣汝肉非一世矣。地獄餓鬼汝皆更之。苦痛以來非適今也。僧大白天。請報我師。天即語師曰。賊欲殺之。汝弟子為人悲泣求哀欲得相見。師即飛往為說法曰。天地須彌尚滅。海有消竭七日有壞。天下有風其名惟藍。惟藍一起山山相搏斯風有滅。況汝小軀何足數也。但當念佛。佛常自言。盛必有衰合會有離榮位難保。身亦如之。僧大便得溝港道。復斷一髀。重念師戒。復得頻來道。賊斷左手復念師戒。得不還道。賊斷右手。復念師戒得應真道。即不畏三惡道。生死自在無所復畏。僧大曰。取樹皮來。即為剝樹皮與之。僧大取枝以為筆。自刺身血書樹皮曰。大兄起居隨時安善。二親在時以吾累兄。兄不承之違廢親教。以女色故骨肉相殘。違親慈教為不孝也。殘殺人命為不仁也。殺一畜生其罪不少。況殺應真五不中止。今吾善遊寂寞從此長別。努力努力願崇真道。申頸長二尺。語諸賊曰。子斷吾頭。由截泥頭也。吾恐汝等墮地獄中。賊前斷頭。取身上衣杖蓰及鉢。持與兄所兄以金銀重謝賊。兄取弟頭為作假身。以頭著上以衣衣之。杖鉢及蓰皆著其傍。謂快見曰。汝婿來歸可問訊之。快見大喜走至其所。見下目坐以為思道。妻不敢呼具作美食。須念道覺當飯之。日中不覺。妻因前日今已中恐過時也。怪其不應。牽衣頭脫身皆分散。妻躄踴呼曰。子竟坐我致見殘賊。哀憤呼天肝心崩裂。血從口出奄忽而逝。戒行清白難污如空。樹心聖範難動如地。真淨行高難及如天。其未終時諸天咨嗟。迎其魂靈處忉利天。忍須臾之頃。獲天上難盡之榮。兄入禪室。視弟頭身分散。婦吐血亡。呼曰。咄咄。吾為逆天所作酷裂乃致於此。即至賊所問。弟臨沒將有遺言乎。賊以書見之。辭喻悽惻讀竟五內咽塞涕泣交橫。吾違尊親臨亡慈教。骨肉相殘。又殺應真。感隔而死。死入地獄。王及臣民莫不涕泣。歎述清德殯葬其弟。四輩立塔。天龍鬼神側塞空中。散華燒香無不哽咽。及別大快見。國人哀慟。歌歎其德(出佛大僧大經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there lived a man named Li, whose household was extremely wealthy, yet who, having grown old, remained entirely without an heir. He prayed to the sun, the moon, and every heavenly spirit, omitting no rite — yet never obtained a son. Reflecting that such prayers brought no benefit, that his own treasured wealth dwindled and his estate went untended, that illness followed illness and calamity after calamity struck him, that his servants died and his livestock failed to breed, he realized he had been misled by shamans and false spirit-mediums, who told him that blessing was coming even as disaster only deepened — like a blind man swallowing poison, mistaking it for medicine, hoping for relief only to lose his own life. Having killed living things in sacrifice, he reflected, he was surely destined for hell himself — how deluded, then, to still hope for heaven's own favor!

He reflected further: the world holds a sage of the Buddha's own lofty path, and those who study and attain this same path are called the truly accomplished — pure as lapis lazuli, visible only to one who diligently holds them in mind. Those who follow this path alone keep to quiet stillness, desiring and seeking nothing, finding their own joy in this alone, gaining peace in this present life and, at its end, rebirth in heaven. He resolved thereafter to make constant offerings to the Buddha and the Three Jewels instead.

A year later, his wife bore a son, named Buddhabala ("Buddha-Strength"); later still, a second son was born, named Saṃghabala ("Sangha-Strength"). Li instructed both sons, showing them the sagely path. Saṃghabala, by his own nature compassionate, filial, and loving toward all beings, upheld the Buddha's own teaching and precepts, drew close to monks, and, pure and content, was cherished by both his parents.

When Li lay dying on his sickbed, he called his elder son to him and, weeping, gave him this charge: all who are born must die; one who upholds the precepts finds safety, one who violates them finds peril. Saṃghabala is still young, yet compassionate, filial, and pure — I now entrust him to your own care — and, having spoken this, he died.

Afterward, Saṃghabala repeatedly told his elder brother of his own wish to become a monk. In their kingdom, the law required a man first to take a wife before becoming one — so, hearing his brother speak of monkhood, Buddhabala at once arranged a marriage for him, seeking out a worthy household's own daughter, named Kuaijian ("Fair to See"), radiant and of unmatched beauty. Once the bride had come to the household and ascended the hall, the brother gathered guests from all nine branches of the family, everyone rejoicing. Before the assembled company, the elder brother said to his own younger brother: "Now, on this very day, will you still consent to become a monk?" Saṃghabala answered: "This has truly long been my own wish." The brother, as though in jest, said: "Then follow your own resolve." The younger brother, his heart filled with joy, bowed to his own brother and went at once into the mountains.

There he found a monk, young and of fine bearing, dwelling alone beneath a tree, and asked him: "Worthy one, for what reason have you become a monk?" This man had already attained the path of the truly accomplished, and knew events across countless past and future kalpas. He told Saṃghabala: the Buddha teaches that one who delights in lust and excess is like a man carrying a torch against the wind — as its flame bends back toward him, if he does not release the torch, the fire will burn his own hand; or like a crow carrying flesh in its beak, pursued by hawks wishing to seize it — if the crow will not release the flesh, disaster reaches even its own body and life. It is for this very reason that I myself became a monk. Or again, it is like honey smeared on a sharp blade — a child, craving its sweetness, licks it with his own tongue, and suffers the cutting of that very tongue for it. One who indulges in lust and excess satisfies only a foolish heart in the moment, giving no thought to what follows, and suffers in the end the very burning of his own body — like a moth, craving the fire's own color, throwing itself into a flame, its body consumed, gaining nothing at all. One deluded by lust cannot distinguish good from evil, growing ever more distant from the wise and closer to the foolish, sinking daily into darkness, until his very kingdom is destroyed and his own people perish, and he himself dies into hell — and once evil has taken root and sin has ripened, what remorse could then avail him? (Such comparisons are too numerous to record in full.)

Hearing such teachings, Saṃghabala bowed his head at the monk's own feet, and said: I wish to leave this defiled world and walk the pure path, upholding the monastic precepts as my own glory and blessing. The teacher said: wait a little. Saṃghabala said: my own intent is to enter the mountains for quiet meditation. The teacher told him: one who dwells in mountains and marshes must learn to read the stars, know clearly the changing seasons, and always keep in store water, fire, roasted grain, and honey — for bandits, too, come seeking water, fire, grain, and honey, in the deep hours of the night toward dawn; you must understand this, and give bandits what they seek, for should you refuse their own demands, they will kill without hesitation. Saṃghabala agreed, reverently receiving this compassionate instruction, and withdrew into the mountains.

His own elder brother, meanwhile, reflected: my brother, having become a monk, will never now take a wife — and, finding Kuaijian's own beauty deeply pleasing to him, rose, took up her own lute, and played it, singing a song of seduction: "Bright and glowing, the turmeric flower grows wild in the field — pass its season unpicked, and the flower is simply cast away; yet luxuriant and abundant, its color still fresh — share this joy with me." Kuaijian, perceiving his intent to commit this wrong, answered him in song: "Towering and lofty, my own teacher, honored among devas and humans; his disciples, pure and clean, are called śramaṇas. He who sees the truth is a sage; lust is the way of beasts. I have received the strict precepts, and shall serve no second lord — I would sooner die than commit such an act, even by a single inch." Buddhabala then composed a mournful, languishing tune of his own: "My own heart's desire for you has long been fixed — it is for this very reason I sought a proper matchmaker, inquired your name, consulted the teacher for an auspicious hour — anxious, fearful you would not come; but now, having seen your own face, my heart is at ease. Should we not now share this joy together, would it not all be wasted? This vow is now fixed — what virtuous woman could doubt it?" Kuaijian, alarmed, answered in song: "The Buddha established proper ceremony, ranking high and low in due order — an uncle's wife is as a mother to her nephew; a husband's own elder brother is as a father to his wife. I myself have received the precepts and grow daily more devoted to them; the true and the sagely stand equal, while lust belongs to beasts and vermin alone — ah, elder brother, why do you speak such words?" Yet the brother's own heart remained lost in desire, and Kuaijian, seeing his intent could not be shaken, sang again: "A person living in this world ought to keep far from two things — unfiliality, and licentious conduct that violates the Buddha's own precepts; may heaven, and every worthy person, bear witness to how different my own conduct shall remain." Buddhabala sang once more: "Your own countenance is radiant and glowing — where in all the world could such beauty as yours be found? My heart delights in you so greatly that I would cross even a great mountain for your sake." Kuaijian, reflecting that this man wished to draw her into ruinous, reckless wrong, and fearing great calamity might follow, resolved to describe her own body's inner foulness and impurity instead, hoping this might turn him away — and told him plainly: you covet my own body — yet what is truly good in it? My head alone holds nine bones, together forming but a skull — and so she described, in full, every impurity of the body.

Buddhabala, reflecting to himself, thought: she thinks only of her own husband, and will never heed me — only by killing my own brother will she then follow me. He went out and sought among men known to have served as bandits, and told one such man: do you know that a household slave of mine — one of six registered servants — has fled and become a monk, now dwelling in the mountains? The bandit said he knew of him. Buddhabala then brought out gold and silver and gave it to him, saying: kill this slave, and bring back his head quickly, along with the clothing on his body, his own monastic robes, and the sandals on his feet — and I shall reward you further still with gold and silver besides. The bandit, delighted, said: give me what I need — and set out at once into the mountains.

Reaching his brother's own dwelling, he called out: monk, come out quickly! The younger brother came out and said: gentlemen, what do you seek? I have water, fire, roasted grain, and honey to offer you — for it was already deep in the night. The bandit answered: we seek neither water nor fire, neither grain nor honey — we have come now only for your own head, to carry it away. Hearing this, the younger brother, filled with terror, wept and said: I am no elder's son, no nobleman's child — I have given up the household life for the path, and hold no quarrel with anyone in this world; my own study of the path is still young, and I have not yet even attained the fruit of stream-entry — what benefit could my own death possibly bring you? The bandit said: come, give me your head. The younger brother told the bandit: if it is treasure you want, I shall write to my own elder brother, that he might reward you with treasure instead. The bandit answered: it is your own elder brother who sent me here to kill you. The younger brother said: then I am indeed to die today — and it is because of that woman. My own teacher warned me before: to dwell together with lust is like carrying a torch against the wind — if one does not release it in time, the fire will burn one's own hand.

As dusk fell, remembering his teacher's own warning, he wept, and begged the bandit for a single year's reprieve. The bandit answered: give me your head at once. The younger brother said further: please, do not kill me outright — first cut off one of my own thighs, and set it before me. The bandit cut off one thigh and set it before him. Enduring this pain, a deva came to his side and told him: fear nothing — hold your own mind firm. In a past life, you were reborn among the animals, your own flesh butchered and sold, not merely once; you have likewise passed through hell and the realm of hungry ghosts — this suffering did not begin only today. Saṃghabala asked the deva to carry word to his own teacher. The deva at once told the teacher: bandits mean to kill your disciple; weeping in his own suffering, he wishes to see you once more. The teacher flew at once to him, and taught him: heaven and earth, even Mount Sumeru itself, must one day perish; the sea itself will one day run dry; even the sun will one day fail. Throughout the world there blows a wind named Vairambhaka — and, once it rises, mountain grinds against mountain until this same wind itself subsides — how much less, then, does your own small body matter at all? Only hold the Buddha in mind. The Buddha has always said: whatever flourishes must decline; whatever comes together must part; even glory and rank cannot be kept forever — and the body is no different.

Saṃghabala thereupon attained the fruit of stream-entry. The bandit cut off his second thigh; remembering his teacher's own instruction once more, he attained the fruit of once-returning. The bandit cut off his left hand; remembering his teacher's instruction again, he attained the fruit of non-returning. The bandit cut off his right hand; remembering his teacher's instruction once more, he attained the fruit of the truly accomplished — and, from that moment, feared none of the three lower realms at all, entirely at ease before both life and death, fearing nothing further.

Saṃghabala said: bring me some tree bark. The bandits stripped bark from a tree and gave it to him. Taking a branch as his own pen, he pierced his own body and, with his own blood, wrote upon the bark: "Elder brother, may you always remain well and at peace. When our parents were alive, they entrusted me to your own care — you have not honored that charge, but instead violated our parents' own teaching; for the sake of a woman's beauty, you have turned against your own flesh and blood. To violate our parents' own loving teaching is unfiliality; to destroy another's life is inhumanity; killing even a single animal is no small sin — how much more, then, killing one of the truly accomplished, a wrong that cannot be undone by five such deaths. I now go gladly into stillness, and take my leave of you forever. Strive on, strive on — may you yet come to honor the true path." He then stretched out his own neck, a full two chi in length, and told the assembled bandits: cut off my head now — it is no different than cutting through mud. I fear only that you yourselves will fall into hell for this deed. The bandits then cut off his head, and, taking his robe, his staff, his sandals, and his bowl, carried them back to his own elder brother, who rewarded them richly with gold and silver.

The elder brother then fashioned a false body for his own brother's head, dressing it in his own robe, setting his staff and bowl and sandals beside it, and told Kuaijian: your own husband has returned — go and greet him. Kuaijian, overjoyed, ran to him, and, seeing him seated with lowered eyes, thought he was simply absorbed in meditation, and, not daring to call out to him, prepared fine food instead, waiting for him to finish his own reflection before serving him. Midday came and he still had not stirred; she thought that, since the day before, it was now already noon, and feared the proper time had passed — puzzled at his silence, she pulled at his robe, and his head fell away, his whole body coming apart. Kuaijian, throwing herself down and crying out, wailed: it is I who have caused you to be so cruelly destroyed! — and, in her own grief and rage, crying out to heaven, her very liver and heart broke apart, blood pouring from her mouth, and she died on the spot.

His own precept-conduct, pure and unstained, was as difficult to defile as empty space itself; his own resolve, firm as a rooted tree, was as unmovable as the earth; his own true, pure, lofty conduct was as unreachable as the heavens. Before his own life had even fully ended, the devas themselves had already sighed in wonder, welcoming his own spirit into Trāyastriṃśa heaven — enduring but a brief moment of suffering, he gained heaven's own inexhaustible glory in return.

The elder brother, entering his own meditation chamber and finding his brother's own head and body come apart, and his wife dead of her own spilled blood, cried out: alas, alas — it is I who, defying heaven itself, have brought about this cruel and terrible ruin! He went at once to the bandits and asked whether his own brother had left any final words before dying. The bandits showed him the letter, its words so sorrowful and moving that, having read it through, his own five organs seized with grief, tears and weeping overtaking him entirely: I have betrayed our own parents' final, loving instruction, turned against my own flesh and blood, and murdered one of the truly accomplished besides — overwhelmed with this realization, he died on the spot, and fell into hell.

The king and all his ministers and people, hearing of this, wept without exception, praising the younger brother's own pure virtue, and gave him a proper burial; the fourfold assembly raised a stūpa in his honor. Devas, dragons, and spirits filled the very sky, scattering flowers and burning incense, all choked with grief. And, at the parting from the great Kuaijian herself, the people of that whole kingdom mourned together, singing in praise of her own virtue (Source: Sūtra of Buddhabala and Saṃghabala).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

What makes this entry unusual within the compendium is how ordinary its own tragedy remains even as its protagonist attains one fruit of the path after another — jealousy, a botched murder-for-hire, a wife's own devastating grief are never redeemed into a lesson so much as left standing alongside Saṃghabala's own escalating equanimity, cut by cut. His own teacher's counsel, given long before any of this occurs, to keep water and food ready because bandits might come demanding it, reads in hindsight as almost unbearably practical advice offered for an entirely different, survivable danger than the one that actually arrives. The story's real subject may be less Saṃghabala's own liberation than everyone left standing afterward — an elder brother who dies of his own realized guilt, a bride who dies of grief, an entire kingdom that mourns two people at once, one canonized and one simply loved.

2. The Wishing Well of Suvarṇadeva

A golden-skinned boy and a golden-skinned girl, born the same day their respective households each acquire an identical wish-granting well, are matched in marriage sight unseen by reputation alone — a fortune traced back to a poor past-life couple's single joint offering of a gold coin and a mirror.

Chinese Source

舍衛國有一長者。其家大富。生一男兒。身體金色。相師占省見其奇相。即為立字。字修越那提婆(梁言金天)其生之日。家中自然出一井水。縱廣八尺。汲用其水能稱人意。一切所須如心即出。兒年轉大才藝博通。長者愛之。未敢逆意。而作是念。我子端正容貌無倫。要當推求選擇名女。金容妙體。類我兒者當往求之。即募諸賈周遍求之。時閻婆國有大長者。方生一女。字修跋那波婆蘇(梁言光明)端正非凡身體金色。初生之日。亦有自然八尺井水。其井所出稱適人意。時彼長者。亦自念言。我女端正。要得賢士。如我女比乃當嫁與。爾時女名遠布舍衛。金天名稱復聞女家。時二長者各懷歡喜。求為婚姻。娶婦既竟。還至舍衛。時金天家便設上供。請佛及僧供養。一日佛受其請。為長者及金天夫婦。廣宣妙法。皆獲須陀洹。佛還精舍。金天與婦。白父母求索出家。父母聽許。俱往佛所。佛言。善來比丘鬚髮自墮法衣著身便成沙門。漸漸教化悉成羅漢。佛言。過去毘婆尸佛滅後。遺法在世。有諸比丘遊行教化到一村落。有諸人民豪賢長者。見眾僧至各競供養。時有夫妻二人。貧餓困乏。每自惟念。我父在時財寶積滿。今者我身貧困極甚。何其苦耶。爾時雖富不遭斯等聖眾之僧。今既得值無錢供養。愴然而啼。婦見夫啼而語婿言。今汝可往至本舍中。於故藏內推覓錢寶當用供養。夫如婦言。至故藏內得一金錢。于時其婦有一明鏡。即共同心以用布施。買一新瓶盛滿淨水。以此金錢著瓶水中。以鏡著上。持至僧所。至心布施。於時眾僧即為受之。各各取水而用洗鉢。復有取水而飲之者。時彼夫婦。歡喜情悅遇疾命終。生忉利天。時貧人者。此金天夫婦是也(出賢愚經第五卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there lived a wealthy elder whose household bore a son with a body of golden color. A physiognomist, examining this extraordinary sign, named him Suvarṇadeva ("Golden Deva," rendered in the Liang language as "Golden Heaven"). On the very day of his birth, a well appeared spontaneously within the household, eight chi wide, whose water, drawn for any need, granted whatever one wished for. As the boy grew, his own talents proved broad and accomplished, and his father, cherishing him, dared not cross his own wishes, reflecting: my son's own fine appearance is beyond compare — I must seek out and choose for him a woman of matching name and beauty, golden in form like my own son — and sent merchants everywhere in search of such a bride.

In the kingdom of Campā there lived a great elder whose own newborn daughter, named Suvarṇaprabhāvatī (rendered in the Liang language as "Light"), was likewise of extraordinary beauty, her body golden in color, and, on the very day of her birth, an identical eight-chi well had likewise appeared spontaneously, its water granting whatever one wished for as well. Her own father reflected in turn: my daughter's own beauty demands a worthy husband — only one to match her own excellence will I give her to. In time, this girl's own renown spread as far as Śrāvastī, just as Suvarṇadeva's own name reached her own family in turn, and the two households, each delighted, sought the marriage. Once wed, the couple returned to Śrāvastī, where Suvarṇadeva's own household prepared a magnificent offering, inviting the Buddha and the assembled sangha. The Buddha accepted their invitation, and, for the elder and the couple, expounded the wondrous Dharma at length, and all of them attained the fruit of stream-entry. When the Buddha returned to his own monastery, Suvarṇadeva and his wife asked their own parents for permission to go forth, which was granted, and the couple went together to the Buddha. The Buddha said: welcome, monks — and their hair fell away of itself, monastic robes appearing on their bodies, and they became monks and nuns at once, gradually taught and converted until both attained arhatship.

The Buddha explained: long ago, after the buddha Vipaśyin's own nirvāṇa, his teaching still remained in the world, and monks, traveling and teaching, came once to a certain village, where the wealthy and eminent elders there competed to make offerings to them. Among the villagers, a poor husband and wife, destitute and hungry, reflected to themselves: when my own father was alive, our household's wealth overflowed — now I find myself in such extreme poverty; how bitter this is! Even in our former wealth, we never once encountered so noble an assembly as this — and now, having finally encountered it, we have no money to make any offering at all — and, grieving, they wept. The wife, seeing her husband weep, told him: go now to our own former storeroom, and search there for whatever coin or treasure might remain. The husband did as she said, and found a single gold coin in the old storeroom. His wife, who owned a single bright mirror, resolved together with him to offer both in giving: they bought a new jar, filled it with clean water, placed the gold coin within the water, and set the mirror atop it, bringing this offering with a sincere heart to the assembled sangha. The monks accepted it, some taking the water to wash their own bowls, others drinking from it directly. The husband and wife, filled with joy, soon after fell ill and died, and were reborn in Trāyastriṃśa heaven. That poor couple were none other than Suvarṇadeva and his own wife (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 5).

3. Asāḍha's Awakening

An arhat nun tells a proud, ordinary senior monk that he is "not properly adorned," sending him through a bewildered cycle of shaving and re-dyeing his robes before she finally explains that only the fourth fruit of the path counts as true dignity — after which a single visit to Upagupta settles the matter for good.

Chinese Source

有天護商主。往陸求那國。常樂布施。於佛生信。欲先入海。若安隱還。我當於佛法中作五年大會。天人國內無不聞知。時有一阿羅漢比丘尼。同往彼國。思惟觀察。知天護得安隱還作五年大會。請一萬八千比丘。皆阿羅漢。學人倍多。凡夫無數。即見上座名阿娑陀。乃是凡夫甚能精進。入僧伽藍從次作禮。謂上座曰。大德甚不端嚴。上座心自思惟。云何以我為不端嚴。即自觀身見鬚髮長。即喚年少剃除鬚髮。比丘尼由言不端嚴。上座更喚年少浣染衣服。尼復至僧伽藍。故言不端嚴。上座瞋曰。我已剃鬚髮及浣染衣竟。云何謂我不端嚴耶。比丘尼言。佛法以得四果為莊嚴。大德聞商主天護作師子吼五年大會不。答言聞。大德是凡夫為第一上座。在羅漢眾中先受供養。是莊嚴不。大德方悟啼泣懊惱。比丘尼言。何故啼泣。答言。姊妹。我今已老無可堪任。比丘尼言。如來法可見無有於時節。大德可往那哆婆哆寺。就優波笈多比丘。此比丘佛之所記。我弟子中教化第一。時長老比丘至。優波笈多出而迎之語言。大德。洗足消息。答言。我未洗足。欲見優波笈多。時優波笈多弟子曰。大德。此即是優波笈多。來迎大德。即大歡喜便自洗足。優波笈多即教化之。為覓檀越。洗浴飲食種種供養。語維那言。今有得二解脫比丘入坐禪處。乃至一萬八千阿羅漢悉入禪處。是時比丘入第一禪坐處坐。時優波笈多入火光三昧。如是一萬八千阿羅漢。悉入火光三昧。比丘見之心生歡喜。優波笈多教化說法。比丘精進思惟得阿羅漢果。還其本國。阿羅漢比丘尼。往僧伽藍禮拜說言。今日大德莊嚴。答曰。姊妹力也。及五年大會。天護問上座。世尊種種說法。上座所說而無有異。上座答言。於過去世九十一劫。我等為商主入海採寶。誓取滿舶。還閻浮提。時遇大風吹舶墮沙海。我等為毘婆尸佛。聚沙為塔以珍寶物而供養之。時有諸天示我道路言。剋後七日有大水來。當泛汝舶入閻浮提。及至七日。果如天言。以此沙塔因緣。經九十一劫不墮惡道。今得阿羅漢果。汝今能供養。爾許多人於三寶所。我說呪願生死苦無窮。汝今可出家。天護奉旨即為沙門。得阿羅漢道(出阿育王經第九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

A caravan-leader named Devarakṣita traveled to the kingdom of Roruka, ever delighting in giving and possessed of deep faith in the Buddha. Before setting out to sea, he vowed: should I return safely, I shall hold a five-year great assembly within the Buddha's own teaching — a vow known throughout the kingdom, among devas and humans alike. An arhat nun, traveling to that same kingdom, reflected and observed, and, knowing that Devarakṣita had indeed returned safely and would hold his five-year assembly, inviting eighteen thousand monks, all arhats, with twice that number of trainees and countless ordinary monks besides, went to see the senior monk there, named Asāḍha — an ordinary monk of great diligence. Entering the monastery, she bowed in her turn, and said to him: great one, you are not properly dignified in bearing. The senior monk, reflecting to himself, wondered why he should be called undignified, and, examining his own body, found his hair and beard grown long, and had a young monk shave them at once. The nun again declared him undignified, and he had his own robes washed and dyed as well. The nun returned to the monastery once more and still said the same. The senior monk, now angered, said: I have already shaved my hair and beard, and washed and dyed my own robes — why do you still call me undignified? The nun answered: within the Buddha's own teaching, it is the attainment of the fourth fruit that constitutes true dignity. Great one, have you heard that the caravan-leader Devarakṣita has proclaimed, with a lion's own roar, his five-year great assembly? He answered that he had. She asked: great one, you are an ordinary man, yet the very foremost senior monk here, receiving offerings before even the arhats in this assembly — is this itself not a form of adornment you lack? Only then did the senior monk understand, and wept in his own distress. The nun asked why he wept, and he answered: sister, I am now old, and no longer capable of anything. The nun told him: the Tathāgata's own Dharma may be realized at any time, without regard for age — great one, you should go to the Nadi-Bhadra monastery, to the monk Upagupta, foretold by the Buddha himself as foremost in teaching and converting others among his disciples.

The elder monk went there, and Upagupta came out to greet him, saying: great one, wash your feet and rest. He answered: I have not yet washed my feet — I wish first to see Upagupta himself. Upagupta's own disciples said: great one, this very man before you is Upagupta, come out to greet you — and, greatly delighted, he washed his feet at once. Upagupta then taught and converted him, sought out a lay donor for him, and provided him with bathing, food, and every kind of offering, telling the monastery's own overseer: there is now a monk here who has attained the two liberations — have him enter the meditation hall — and all eighteen thousand arhats entered the meditation hall together. The monk sat in the seat of the first dhyāna; Upagupta then entered the samādhi of fire-radiance, and all eighteen thousand arhats likewise entered that same samādhi together. The monk, witnessing this, felt joy arise in his own heart, and, through Upagupta's own teaching, applied himself diligently in reflection and attained arhatship, then returned to his own homeland. The arhat nun, going to the monastery, bowed and said: today, great one, you are truly adorned. He answered: sister, this is entirely your own doing.

At the five-year assembly, Devarakṣita asked the senior monk: the World-Honored One taught the Dharma in countless ways — is what you yourself now teach the same, or different? The senior monk answered: ninety-one kalpas ago, we ourselves were caravan-leaders who went to sea for treasure, vowing to fill our ship entirely before returning to Jambudvīpa — but, meeting a great storm, our ship was driven onto a sea of sand. There, we built a stūpa of sand in honor of the buddha Vipaśyin and made offerings to it. Certain devas then showed us the way, telling us that in seven days a great flood would come and lift our ship free, carrying it back to Jambudvīpa — and, when the seventh day came, it happened exactly as the devas had said. Through the merit of that very sand stūpa, across ninety-one kalpas, we never once fell into an evil rebirth, and have now attained arhatship. You yourself have now made offerings to so vast an assembly, gathered within the Three Jewels — I now pronounce this very blessing upon you: may the suffering of birth and death, boundless as it is, soon come to its end for you as well; you should now go forth yourself. Devarakṣita, accepting this instruction, became a monk at once, and attained the arhat's own path (Source: Sūtra of King Aśoka, fascicle 9).

5. Kṣema's Teaching from a Sickbed

Too weak to keep sending a message back and forth through his own attendant, a gravely ill monk simply has himself carried on a staff to the senior monks directly — and ends up teaching them the Dharma himself.

6. Koṭi's Fatal Impatience

A monk who has fallen back from the fruit of arhatship six times, sensing a seventh success finally within reach, kills himself out of fear he might fall back once more — and Māra, unable even to locate his consciousness afterward, throws himself into a lotus pool that boils away beneath him.

Chinese Source

昔佛在舍衛國。弟子拘提。於羅漢果六反退還。至第七頭。自覺身證。復恐退還。即求利劍自害其命。是時魔波旬。求比丘神為生何處。而不能知。即往問佛。世尊告曰。拘提比丘已取滅度。神識處空與空合體。弊魔聞已心興欝毒。舉身自投青蓮池浴。池水涌沸水性皆終。以此因緣諸比丘等。皆勤精進恐復退還(出說拘提比丘經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, the Buddha dwelt in Śrāvastī. His disciple Koṭi fell back from the fruit of arhatship six times in succession. Reaching the seventh attempt, he felt himself on the verge of true realization, yet, fearing he might fall back once more, took up a sharp blade and killed himself. At that time the wicked Māra sought to know into what realm this monk's own spirit had passed, but could not discover it, and went to ask the Buddha. The World-Honored One told him: the monk Koṭi has already attained liberation — his own consciousness has merged with emptiness itself, becoming one with it. The wicked Māra, hearing this, was filled with bitter distress, and threw his own whole body into a pool of blue lotuses to bathe — but the pool's own water boiled up and vanished entirely. Because of this event, the assembled monks all applied themselves ever more diligently, fearing they too might otherwise fall back (Source: Sūtra of the Monk Koṭi).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium reports Koṭi's own death and its consequence — renewed diligence among the other monks — without pausing to resolve the obvious tension in the story: that his suicide is treated neither as an admirable act of resolve nor as a clear mistake, but simply as what happened, its outcome apparently vindicated by the Buddha's own confirmation that he attained liberation regardless. Read against Māra's own furious, futile gesture immediately afterward — hurling himself into a pool that boils away rather than offering him any relief at all — the entry seems less interested in judging Koṭi's own final act than in contrasting two responses to the same uncertainty: one being who could not bear to risk falling back again, and one who could not bear that he hadn't.

7. Mahallaka's Dullness and Its Cause

Cūḍapanthaka Recites a Single Verse and Understands Its Meaning

The Buddha dwelt in Śrāvastī. There lived a monk named Panthaka, newly ordained, by nature dull and slow of understanding. The Buddha had five hundred arhats teach him daily, yet across three full years he could not master even a single verse, and the fourfold assembly throughout the kingdom knew him for his own foolishness. The Buddha, taking pity on him, called him forward and gave him a single verse to learn: guard your own mouth, gather your own mind, and let your own body commit no wrong — one who practices thus is delivered from the world. Panthaka, moved by the Buddha's own compassionate grace, rejoiced, and his mind opened at once, the verse now readily upon his own lips.

The Buddha told him: you are now old, and have only just mastered a single verse — everyone already knows of this, and it is nothing remarkable in itself; I shall now explain its own meaning for you. One mind; three of body, four of speech, three of mind — observe their own arising, and observe their own cessation; the three realms and five paths turn without rest — through this same turning, one ascends to heaven, or falls into the abyss, or attains the path; nirvāṇa itself follows naturally. Explaining this teaching point by point, boundless and wondrous in its own implications, Panthaka's mind opened in a flash of insight, and he attained arhatship.

At that time there were five hundred nuns dwelling in a separate monastery of their own. The Buddha would send a monk each day to teach them the sūtras and the Dharma; the next day it was to be Panthaka's own turn. The nuns, hearing this, all smiled among themselves in advance, saying: when he comes tomorrow, we shall recite his own verse back to him first, and leave him too ashamed to say a single word. The next day, Panthaka arrived, and the nuns, young and old, all came out to bow, glancing at one another and smiling. Once seated, the meal was served; once finished and hands washed, they asked him to teach the Dharma. Panthaka ascended the high seat and, humbly, said: my own virtue is slight, my own talent poor, unworthy even to be called a monk — dull and slow by nature, I have learned little, knowing only a single verse, though I grasp its own meaning roughly — I shall now expound it for you; please listen quietly, each of you. The young nuns, intending to recite the verse back to him first, found their own mouths simply would not open, and, startled and ashamed, bowed in repentance. Panthaka then, exactly as the Buddha had taught him, explained point by point the causes rooted in body and mind, sin and merit, within and without, ascent to heaven and attainment of the path, the stilling of the spirit and cutting off of thought in the very method of meditative absorption. The nuns, hearing this exposition, marveled greatly at its own wonder, and, filled with a single-minded joy, all attained arhatship.

Later, King Prasenajit invited the Buddha and the sangha to a gathering in the main hall of the palace. The Buddha, wishing to display Panthaka's own majestic spiritual power, gave him a bowl to carry and had him follow behind — but the gatekeeper, recognizing him, barred his entry, saying: you are a monk who could not even master a single verse — what use will you be at this very gathering? Even I, a layman, know that verse — how much more should a monk know it, with wisdom to spare! You have nothing to offer here — there is no need for you to enter. Panthaka simply stood outside the gate.

The Buddha, seated in the hall, had already had the water passed round for the meal to begin — and Panthaka, lifting the bowl, extended his own arm from afar, offering it to the Buddha across the distance. The king, his ministers, his queen, the crown prince, and the whole fourfold assembly there, seeing only an arm appear without a body attached, were astonished, and asked the Buddha whose arm this was. The Buddha answered: this is the worthy monk Panthaka's own arm. The king said: has he then attained the path? I had sent for him to bring the bowl, but the gatekeeper would not let him enter — and so he has extended his own arm to pass me the bowl instead. The king then had him invited in, his own majestic spirit now redoubled. The king said to the Buddha: I have heard that the venerable Panthaka was by nature foolish and dull, and only recently mastered a single verse — how, then, did he come to attain the path at all? The Buddha told the king: learning need not be extensive — conduct is what matters most. The worthy Panthaka, having understood the meaning of but a single verse, penetrated it to its very essence, his own body, speech, and mind now still and pure as heavenly gold — whereas one who has learned much yet does not practice it accordingly gains nothing from his own memory at all; what benefit is there in that? At this the World-Honored One spoke this verse:

"Though one recites a thousand chapters, if their own meaning is not grasped rightly, this avails nothing — better a single essential teaching, heard once, that can dissolve confusion.
Though one recites a thousand words, without their own true meaning, what benefit is there? Better a single true meaning, heard and practiced, that can bring one across.
Though one recites many sūtras, without understanding, what benefit is there? Understanding a single verse and practicing it — this alone brings one to the path."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium places these two entries back to back precisely because Panthaka's own "single verse" only becomes real understanding once he stops trying to hold everything at once — first forgetting "broom" for "sweep" and "sweep" for "broom" until the two collapse into one meaning, then, given one further verse, needing the Buddha's own extended explanation before it truly opens for him. His later arm, reaching a full palace hall from outside its own gate, is almost a joke on the gatekeeper's own earlier verdict — the very man dismissed as too dull to be worth admitting turns out, once admitted on his own terms, not to need the door at all.

This compendium has now told Aṅgulimāla's own story three separate times — as a devoted student destroyed by a false accusation in fascicle 8, folded into King Samantaprabha's own cannibal-king narrative in fascicle 10, and here, in its own leanest and most famous form, reduced to a single paradox exchanged mid-chase. Three tellings across three fascicles, each drawn from a different source text and none acknowledging the others, is by now a familiar pattern in this compendium — a reminder that its own compilers valued the completeness of an inherited collection of sources over the tidiness of a single settled account.

Two arhats, having genuinely exhausted every affliction, still carry the physical habits of their own hundreds of past lives — one still leaping through trees like the monkey he once was for five hundred lifetimes, the other still addressing a river goddess as "little slave girl" out of five hundred lifetimes' worth of brahmin condescension.

有人雖斷一切煩惱。身口中亦有煩惱相。凡人見聞則起不清淨心。如蜜婆和吒阿羅漢。五百世在獼猴中。今雖得道猶騰跳樹木。愚人見之即生輕慢。又畢陵伽婆蹉阿羅漢。五百世生婆羅門中。習輕蔑心。今雖得道。猶語恒水神言。小婢止流。恒神瞋恚。詣佛陳訴。佛教懺悔。猶稱小婢。佛無如是事。如一婆羅門惡口。一時以五百事罵佛佛無慍色。婆羅門心乃歡喜。即復一時以五百善事讚歎於佛。亦無喜色。當知佛煩惱習氣盡。故好惡無異(出大智論第八十四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are those who, though they have cut off every affliction entirely, still retain certain traces of affliction in their own body and speech — traces that, seen or heard by ordinary people, still give rise to impure thoughts in them. The arhat Mṛgabandhaka, for five hundred lifetimes reborn as a monkey, though he has now attained the path, still leaps and bounds through the trees exactly as before — and foolish people, seeing this, feel only contempt toward him. Likewise, the arhat Pilindavatsa, for five hundred lifetimes reborn as a brahmin, grew accustomed across those same lifetimes to a habit of contempt toward others — and now, though he has attained the path, still addresses the spirit of the Ganges as "little slave girl, stop your flow." The river spirit, offended, brought her complaint before the Buddha, who told her to accept his apology — yet he still addressed her as "little slave girl" even then, for the Buddha himself is entirely free of such habitual traces, and so shows no difference at all between what pleases and what offends him. When a certain brahmin once reviled the Buddha with five hundred separate insults, the Buddha showed not the slightest anger; and when that same brahmin, his heart then turned to delight, later praised the Buddha with five hundred separate compliments in turn, the Buddha showed not the slightest pleasure either. Know, then, that the Buddha's own habitual traces of affliction are entirely exhausted — this is why good and ill make no difference to him at all (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 84).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium is careful to distinguish liberation from affliction, which these two arhats have genuinely attained, from the erasure of habit, which apparently only a buddha achieves — Pilindavatsa's own apology to the river goddess is entirely sincere, yet the very next time he addresses her, the same old condescension resurfaces unbidden. Rather than treating this as a lingering flaw to be embarrassed about, the entry uses it to sharpen exactly what makes a buddha's own equanimity different in kind: not merely freedom from affliction's own arising, which Pilindavatsa already has, but freedom from every last groove five hundred lifetimes can carve into a personality — a bar set high enough that even a fully liberated arhat still falls short of it.

12. Four Brothers and the Lion's Milk

Four brothers quarreling over their own inheritance are told a story about a poor man's own six bodily senses squabbling over credit for a successful mission — until his tongue alone, resentful of being outvoted, nearly gets him executed for spite.

Chinese Source

佛在羅閱祇竹園中。時有大姓子兄弟四人。父母早亡共爭居財。見舍利弗歡喜問言。願為說此後不復爭。舍利弗言。善哉。吾有大師佛於三界最尊。爾等隨我。還到佛所。必當得解。隨舍利弗還。佛遙見四人笑出五色光。四人禮佛白言。吾等愚癡。願佛解說。令不復爭。昔有國王。號曰惟婁。身體有疾。迎醫往視。合藥應用師子乳。王即募。得之者分土封之。并妻以小女。時有貧人啟言。能得王即聽許。其人巧點先尋師子所在。乃殺羊蒲桃酒數斛。往到其山。伺師子出行。便殺羊。并蒲桃酒著其住處。師子見酒肉即便飲食大醉而臥。前捉取乳歡喜而還。未達本國。暮宿聚落。有一羅漢宿。與此人同追師子。經歷險道。體極眠臥。都無所知。道人覩其身中六識。各自諍功。足神言。賴因我行到此得乳。手神復言。賴我手捉取。目神復言。賴我見之。耳神復言。賴我聞王求乳將爾等來。舌神即言。汝等空以競諍。此功是我有。今殺活在我耳。此人齎乳詣王所。白言。今已得師子乳在外。王言。是非但進之。王適見乳。舌即言。此非師子乳。但驢乳耳。王聞大怒言。我使汝取師子乳。乃以驢乳。即欲殺之。時共宿道人。即以神足。到王前報言。此信是師子乳。我時與是人共宿聚中。見其身中。六體共爭其功勤。舌言。我當反爾等。今果如此。王但以乳合藥。其病必愈。王即信阿羅漢言。用乳合藥以女妻之。并封拜如本約。道人告王言。一人體識自相反戾。況於他人乎。時取乳者。得道人恩。求作沙門。意解得羅漢道。時王亦歡喜受五戒。得須陀洹道。四人聞是意解。便隨佛乞為比丘。佛默然以手摩其頭。髮墮袈裟著身。結解垢除。阿難曰。此四人本何功德。今聞經便解。疾得阿羅漢。佛言。昔摩父佛時。舍利弗為比丘。此四人為賈客。共以一袈裟上舍利弗。舍利弗呪願。令君等後世早得度脫。今從舍利弗而得解脫(出惟婁王師子乳譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in the Bamboo Grove at Rājagṛha. Four brothers of a great family, their parents having died young, quarreled over the division of the household estate. Seeing Śāriputra, they asked him, delighted, to settle the matter for them, that they might quarrel no further. Śāriputra said: excellent — I have a great teacher, the Buddha, most honored throughout the three realms; follow me, and we shall go to him together, and the matter will surely be resolved. The four brothers followed Śāriputra to the Buddha, who, seeing them from a distance, smiled, and five-colored light streamed from him. The four brothers bowed to the Buddha and said: we are foolish and confused — we beg the Buddha to explain this matter for us, that we might quarrel no longer.

The Buddha said: long ago there was a king named Vairocana, who fell ill in his own body, and summoned a physician to attend him — the prescribed medicine required a lion's own milk. The king proclaimed throughout the land that whoever obtained it would receive a portion of land and his own youngest daughter in marriage. A poor man came forward and declared he could obtain it, and the king consented. This man, being clever, first sought out the lion's own dwelling, then killed a sheep and prepared several bushels of grape wine, bringing these to the mountain and waiting for the lion to emerge. Once it did, he set out the meat and wine in its place; the lion, seeing them, ate and drank until entirely drunk, and lay down — whereupon the man came forward, took the milk he needed, and returned rejoicing.

Before reaching his own kingdom, he stopped for the night in a village, where an arhat also happened to be lodging, having likewise pursued the lion along that same difficult road, and, utterly exhausted, fell asleep without awareness of anything around him. The monk, observing this man's own six sense-faculties, saw them each quarreling among themselves over their own credit. The foot-spirit said: it was through my own walking that we reached this place and obtained the milk. The hand-spirit said: it was through my own grasping that it was taken. The eye-spirit said: it was through my own seeing that it was found. The ear-spirit said: it was through my own hearing of the king's own request that we were brought here at all. The tongue-spirit then said: you are all quarreling over this for nothing — the true credit belongs to me alone; whether this man now lives or dies rests entirely with me.

The man carried the milk to the king and reported: I have now obtained the lion's own milk, and it awaits outside. The king said: bring it in at once. But the moment the king saw the milk, the tongue-spirit spoke through the man's own mouth: this is not lion's milk at all — it is merely donkey's milk. The king, hearing this, grew furious: I sent you to obtain lion's milk, yet you bring me donkey's milk instead! — and moved to have him killed at once. The monk who had lodged with him that same night then arrived through his own supernormal power before the king and reported: this is truly lion's milk — I myself lodged together with this man in that same village, and saw his own six bodily faculties quarreling over their own credit; the tongue said, "I shall contradict you all" — and now, indeed, it has done exactly this. The king, if he simply compounds this milk into medicine, will surely be cured by it.

The king, believing the arhat's own word, used the milk to compound his medicine, married his own daughter to the man exactly as promised, and granted him land as well. The monk then told the king: even a single person's own body can turn against itself in this way — how much more, then, might one person turn against another? The man who had obtained the milk, moved by the monk's own kindness, asked to become a monk himself, and, his mind opening, attained arhatship. The king too, rejoicing, received the five precepts and attained the fruit of stream-entry.

The four brothers, hearing this story, understood its own meaning at once, and asked the Buddha's leave to become monks themselves. The Buddha consented in silence, and, stroking each of their heads with his own hand, their hair fell away and monastic robes appeared upon their bodies, every fetter dissolving, every defilement cleared away. Ānanda asked: what merit did these four brothers possess in the past, that, hearing this teaching, their own understanding came so quickly, and arhatship followed so soon after? The Buddha answered: long ago, in the time of the buddha Mahāpitṛ, Śāriputra was then a monk, and these four brothers were merchants, who together offered him a single monastic robe. Śāriputra pronounced a blessing over them, that they might attain swift liberation in a future life — and it is through Śāriputra's own blessing that they have now attained this very liberation (Source: Sūtra of the Simile of King Vairocana and the Lion's Milk).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha never states outright how the parable applies to the four quarreling brothers, trusting them to draw the connection themselves — and they do, understanding its meaning "at once." The tongue's own petty sabotage, risking its own body's execution purely to prove a point about credit, is a sharper image of sibling rivalry than any direct moral about inheritance could have offered: division of a shared body, or a shared estate, invites exactly this kind of self-destructive point-scoring, where being right about one's own contribution matters more, in the heat of the moment, than the shared body's own survival.

13. The Monk Who Served Until Exhausted

Worn out from constant service duty at every monastery he visits, a monk seeks out Upagupta himself for teaching — only to be set to serving an even larger assembly, until a sympathetic elder quietly takes the burden on himself instead.

Chinese Source

有一男子。於正法出家。能為給事。所至寺諸比丘等。令其給事。後轉疲極。自思惟言。誰能為我說法教化。聞摩偷羅國。有優波笈多。是佛所記。能於後弟子中最為第一。往為作禮合掌說言。大德。大作佛事為我說法。優波笈多言。能隨我教當為說法。答曰能。問曰。那多婆哆寺。眾僧有幾。汝更給事眾僧。答曰。有一萬八千阿羅漢。學人一倍。精進凡夫無數。時彼比丘。即為一切眾僧。而作給事。令一切僧專修道業。時給事比丘早起。著衣持鉢入摩偷羅國。有一長者朝出相逢。長者念言。我未曾見。而今見之頭面禮足。問言。大德。所從遠近有何事來。答言。從東國來。至優波笈多處。為欲聞法。而優波笈多令我為僧給事。我今不知摩偷羅國人誰精進誰不精進者。長者曰。汝今不須思惟是事。我當代汝給事眾僧一切飲食衣服醫藥。我悉給與比丘與長者。共取飲食等。供養眾僧。三月安居時比丘因此供養修獲道果。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a man who, going forth under the true Dharma, proved capable of useful service, and, wherever he stayed, the monks there had him serve in this way. In time, growing exhausted by it, he reflected: who might teach and guide me properly? Hearing of Upagupta in the kingdom of Mathurā, foretold by the Buddha as foremost among later disciples in teaching others, he went and bowed before him with joined palms, saying: great one, please carry out the Buddha's own great work and teach me the Dharma. Upagupta answered: if you can follow my own instruction, I shall teach you. He agreed. Upagupta asked: at the Nadi-Bhadra monastery, how many monks are there, that you might serve them further? He answered: eighteen thousand arhats, twice that number of trainees, and countless diligent ordinary monks besides. The monk then served this entire assembly, that all of them might devote themselves single-mindedly to their own cultivation.

One morning, this serving monk rose early, robed himself, and took up his bowl to enter the kingdom of Mathurā; an elder, going out that same morning, happened to meet him, and, reflecting that he had never seen this monk before, bowed at his feet and asked: great one, where have you come from, and for what purpose? He answered: I have come from an eastern land to Upagupta's own place, wishing to hear the Dharma — but Upagupta has instead set me to serving the sangha, and I do not even know who among the people of Mathurā is diligent in practice and who is not. The elder said: you need not trouble yourself over this matter any further — I myself shall take on the serving of the sangha in your place, providing all their food, clothing, and medicine. And so it was that this elder, together with the monk, together provided food and offerings for the entire assembly. Through the merit of this same offering, sustained throughout the three months of the rains retreat, the monk in time attained the fruit of the path.

14. Tested by Rākṣasas

A young man given leave to visit home before ordination lodges overnight at a shrine, where Upagupta, testing him, conjures two quarreling demons who nearly devour him for the crime of telling the truth.

Chinese Source

摩偷羅國。有一男子。啟其父母求欲出家。往優波笈多所。恭敬作禮白言。大德。我得作比丘不。優波笈多見其於身。為愛所縛。語言善來。我當與汝出家。歡喜禮足。乞先暫還家。於路思惟。我若至家。或有留難。夕於中道宿一神廟(大智論云空舍)優波笈多作二羅剎。一持死屍。一則空手。俱入廟中。互言我得。共諍不決。而問此人。誰將屍來。此人思惟。不得妄語。如實語之。空來之鬼即牽其臂向口欲食。將屍之鬼助其分解。劣相免脫。如此良久遂至日出。經二日後還至笈多所。出家為道。精進勤修。得阿羅漢果(出阿育王經第九卷又出大智論第十二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the kingdom of Mathurā there lived a man who asked his own parents for permission to go forth, and went to Upagupta, bowing reverently and asking: great one, may I become a monk? Upagupta, seeing that this man's own body was still bound by attachment, told him: welcome — I shall let you go forth. Delighted, the man bowed at his feet, but asked leave first to return briefly to his own home. Along the road, he reflected: should I go home now, some obstacle might well arise to prevent my return — and so, that evening, he lodged instead at a spirit-shrine along the way (the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra calls it an empty house).

Upagupta then transformed himself into two rākṣasas — one carrying a corpse, the other empty-handed — who entered the shrine together, each claiming the corpse as his own prize, quarreling without resolution, and asked this man to settle the matter: who had brought the corpse here? The man, reflecting that he must not lie, answered truthfully. The empty-handed demon then seized his own arm and made to devour it; the other, the one who had brought the corpse, helped tear it apart in turn — and, in this way, the man barely escaped with his own life, badly mauled, the ordeal continuing a long while until daybreak finally came.

Two days later, he returned to Upagupta's own place and went forth as a monk, practicing diligently, and attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of King Aśoka, fascicle 9; also found in the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 12).

15. The Sole Survivor of the Flood

The only person to survive a catastrophic flood asks to become a monk, believing his own good fortune ends the story — until the Buddha's teaching on impermanence shows him it was only the beginning.

Chinese Source

昔有眾人。在江水側。為水所害。人民無復齊限。其中得解脫萬中有一。於深水得解脫人。往至佛所。求為沙門。佛便然可聽在道末。內不思惟謂離困厄。佛為說法勸令行道。時彼比丘內自慚愧。解知一切萬物無常。心開意解淡然無想。即於佛前。成阿羅漢(出說為沙門經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, a great many people, gathered along a riverbank, were swept away and killed by the flooding waters, their numbers beyond counting — of these, only one in ten thousand escaped alive. One man who survived the deep waters went to the Buddha and asked to become a monk. The Buddha consented, and let him take his place at the very end of the assembly's own ranks — though inwardly this man still believed himself merely to have escaped hardship, nothing more. The Buddha taught the Dharma and urged him to practice the path, and, in his own heart, this monk grew ashamed at his own shallow understanding, and, coming to understand that all things are impermanent, his mind opened and settled, calm and free of every thought — and, right there before the Buddha, he attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of Becoming a Śramaṇa).

16. The Monk and the Elephant

Two monk brothers practiced differently — one kept the precepts but never gave, the other gave freely but broke them — and, reborn respectively as a starving arhat and a pampered royal elephant, recognize each other the instant the monk touches the elephant's own ear.

Chinese Source

迦葉佛時。兄弟二人。俱為沙門。兄持戒坐禪一心求道。而不布施。弟布施修福。而喜破戒。兄從釋迦出家得羅漢道。衣裳不充食常不飽。弟生象中。為象多力。能却怨敵。國王所愛。金銀珍寶瓔珞其身。封數百戶邑。供給此象。隨其所須。時兄比丘值世大儉。遊行乞食七日不得。末後得少麁食。劣得存命。先知此象是前世弟。便往詣象。手捉象耳。而語之言。我昔與汝俱有罪也。象思比丘語。即識宿命。見前世因緣。愁憂不食。象子怖懼。便往白王。象不飲食。不知何意。王問象子。先無有人犯此象不。象子答曰。無他異人。唯一沙門來至象邊。須臾便去。王即遣人覓得沙門。問言。至我象邊何道。沙門答曰。語象。我與汝俱有罪耳。沙門向王具說如上。王意便悟。即放沙門。令還所止(出雜譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the time of the buddha Kāśyapa, two brothers both became monks — the elder kept the precepts and sat in meditation, single-mindedly seeking the path, yet never gave in charity; the younger practiced giving and cultivated merit, yet delighted in breaking the precepts. The elder brother, going forth again under Śākyamuni, attained arhatship, yet his own robes never sufficed and his own food never satisfied him; the younger brother was reborn as an elephant, of such great strength that it could repel any enemy, cherished by the king himself, adorned with gold, silver, jewels, and necklaces, its own upkeep supported by several hundred households granted for that very purpose, provided with whatever it needed.

At a time of great famine in the world, the elder brother, now a monk, traveled begging for seven days without obtaining anything at all, and, at last, received only a little coarse food, barely enough to sustain his own life. Knowing that this elephant had once been his own younger brother in a former life, he went to it, took hold of its own ear with his hand, and said: long ago, you and I together bore the same fault. The elephant, hearing the monk's own words, recognized its own past life at once, seeing clearly the karmic causes of its former existence, and, grieving, refused to eat. The elephant's own keeper, alarmed, reported to the king that the elephant would not eat, and did not know why. The king asked the keeper whether anyone had approached or offended the elephant recently, and the keeper answered: no one unusual — only a single monk, who came to the elephant's side briefly and then left. The king sent men to find this monk, and asked him: what did you say to my elephant? The monk answered: I only told it, "You and I together bear the same fault." The monk then explained the whole matter to the king as above. The king, understanding at once, released the monk to return to his own dwelling (Source: Miscellaneous Similes Sūtra, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium is careful not to let either brother's own path read as simply correct — the precept-keeper who never gives ends up begging in vain through a famine, while the generous precept-breaker ends up pampered and adorned, though still, in the end, a beast of burden rather than free. Neither outcome is presented as the better one; the story's real interest lies instead in the moment of mutual recognition itself, where a single touch and six words are enough to collapse an elephant's entire, comfortable present life back into its own forgotten past.

17. Five Hundred Blind Beggars' Sevenfold Pursuit

Robbed and abandoned in the wilderness by the very guide they hired, five hundred blind beggars chase the traveling Buddha back and forth between two kingdoms seven times before he finally stops and waits for them — and the light that opens their eyes is the very light of encountering him.

Chinese Source

毘舍離國。有五百盲人。乞匃自活。時聞佛出世。覩見之者。癃殘百病皆蒙除愈。貧施衣食。愁憂苦厄。悉能解免。盲人共議。我等罪積苦毒特兼。若當遇佛必見救濟。便共行乞。人各令得金錢一枚。以用雇人足得達彼。行乞經時人獲一錢。左右喚人誰將我等到舍衛者。金錢五百雇其勞苦。時有一人來。共相可以錢與之。語諸盲人。展轉相牽。自在前導。將至摩竭。棄諸盲人。置空澤中。盲人不知為在何國。互相捉手踐踏他田。傷碎苗穀。田主見之盛發瞋怒。語痛與手。乞兒求哀具說上事。長者愍之。令一使人將詣舍衛。適達彼國。又聞世尊已向摩竭提國。使人將向摩竭國。盲人欽佛係心欲見。肉眼雖閉心眼已覩。歡喜發心不覺疲勞。已至摩竭。復聞世尊已還舍衛。如是追逐凡經七返。爾時如來觀諸盲人善根已熟。便住待之。佛光觸身。應時兩目即得開明。乃見如來四眾圍遶。身色晃昱如紫金山。前詣佛所五體投地。為佛作禮。異口同音。共白佛言。唯願垂矜。聽在道次。佛曰。善來比丘。鬚髮自墮法衣在身。重為說法。得阿羅漢(出賢愚經第六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the kingdom of Vaiśālī there lived five hundred blind people, who supported themselves by begging. Hearing that the Buddha had appeared in the world, and that all who encountered him, whatever their own affliction or illness, found relief, and that the poor received clothing and food, their own grief and hardship entirely dissolved, the blind people conferred together: our own sins have accumulated and our own suffering is especially severe — should we truly encounter the Buddha, we would surely find deliverance. They resolved to beg together until each had gathered a single gold coin, to be used in hiring someone to guide them to Śrāvastī. Having begged for some time, each had gathered his own coin, and they called out: who among you will lead us there? We shall pay five hundred coins in all for the trouble.

A man came forward and, agreeing to the price, told the blind people to hold onto one another in a chain, while he himself led the way at the front. He led them instead to the kingdom of Magadha, and, abandoning them there in a deserted wilderness, took the money and left. The blind people, not even knowing what kingdom they were now in, groped and held onto one another, trampling through a farmer's own fields and damaging his crops. The field's own owner, discovering this, grew furious, striking and beating them; the beggars, crying out in distress, explained the whole affair — and the elder, taking pity, sent a guide to lead them onward to Śrāvastī. But, on reaching that kingdom, they learned the World-Honored One had already gone on to Magadha — and so their own guide led them there instead. The blind people, their hearts fixed devotedly on the Buddha, longing to see him, found that though their own physical eyes remained shut, the eye of their own mind had already, in a sense, seen him — and, rejoicing, gave no thought to their own weariness. Reaching Magadha, they learned the World-Honored One had already returned to Śrāvastī — and so this pursuit continued back and forth some seven times in all.

The Tathāgata, observing that these blind people's own roots of goodness had at last fully ripened, halted and waited for them. As the Buddha's own light touched their bodies, their eyes opened at once, and they saw the Tathāgata himself, surrounded by the fourfold assembly, his body radiant like a mountain of pure gold. They came before the Buddha, threw themselves to the ground, and bowed, crying out together with one voice: we beg only that you take pity on us, and let us follow in your own train. The Buddha said: welcome, monks — and their hair fell away of itself, monastic robes appearing upon their bodies. The Buddha taught them the Dharma further, and all of them attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 6).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium's own phrasing is deliberate: though their physical eyes remained shut, "the eye of their own mind had already seen him." Everything that follows — the abandonment, the trampled field, the beating, the sevenfold chase between two kingdoms — happens to people who, by any ordinary account, have not yet encountered the Buddha at all, and yet the text credits their own devotion as already a form of seeing. The literal restoration of sight, when it finally comes, reads less as the beginning of their own relationship to the Buddha than as its outward confirmation, arriving only once their own inward pursuit has already proven itself many times over.

18. The Caṇḍāla Boy Nīti

An outcaste boy who removes others' own waste for a living tries to flee the approaching Buddha out of shame — and is instead lifted bodily into the sky, bathed, and ordained, provoking a king to march out and rebuke the Buddha before finding, ahead of him, a monk already venerated by five hundred devas.

Chinese Source

舍衛城里。有一旃陀羅兒(賢愚經云尼提)除糞自活。爾時世尊入城分衛。次至彼家。客除糞者即避餘巷。如來逆之。其人自念。吾擔糞不淨。今日何由得覲世尊。復詣一澤。佛遙喚曰。吾為汝來。其人報曰。不敢親近。不審世尊何所教誡。乃能慈愍與罪人語。世尊告曰。欲度卿為沙門。其人白佛。地獄畜生亦得為道乎。佛言。吾今出世。正為罪苦。手執其人。上昇虛空。至恒水側。沐浴身體。復至祇洹。勅諸比丘。度為沙門。其人勸勵精勤日新。未經旬日便得須陀洹果。至羅漢果。六通清徹涌沒自由。詣大方石當中央坐補納故衣。王聞佛度旃陀羅兒。念佛出釋種豪族姓家。左右弟子皆出四姓。來入宮室。受供信施。五體投地。接足而禮。今聞如來度旃陀羅。云何禮敬。吾今當往責數如來。未到之頃。見前比丘坐大方石。有五百淨居天圍繞禮覲。王前語曰。煩白世尊。波斯匿王欲覲世尊。比丘聞已即沒石中。從精舍出具以白佛。佛言知時。比丘還從石涌出。語王。如來有教。先問此比丘云何得入是剛䩕石。涌沒自由。前至佛所頭面禮足。在一面坐。前白佛言。向者比丘。為名何等。有此神力。佛告王曰。此是客除糞人。爾時世尊以此因緣便說二偈。猶如穢污惡。地田溝深坑。生香潔蓮華。云何大王有目之士。當取此華不。王言。世尊。華極香潔。當取莊飾穢污。觀母胎於彼胎中生功德華。時王白佛。彼人快得善利。不可思議。自今以始。請此比丘。供養四事(出旃陀羅兒經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the streets of Śrāvastī there lived a caṇḍāla boy (the Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish calls him Nīti) who made his own living removing others' waste. One day the World-Honored One, entering the city to beg, happened to come to his own household's street; the boy, carrying his own load of waste, moved to avoid the Tathāgata by turning down another lane, but the Buddha came toward him regardless. The boy thought to himself: I carry this unclean burden — how could I possibly presume to approach the World-Honored One today? — and fled instead toward an open marsh. The Buddha called out to him from a distance: I have come here for your own sake. The boy answered: I dare not draw near you — I do not understand why the World-Honored One should trouble himself to speak so kindly with a sinner such as myself. The World-Honored One told him: I wish to deliver you — will you become a monk? The boy asked the Buddha: can even one destined for hell or the animal realms attain the path? The Buddha answered: I have come into this world for the very purpose of delivering beings such as you, mired in suffering and sin.

The Buddha then took the boy by the hand and rose with him into the sky, carrying him to the bank of the Ganges, where he bathed his own body, then brought him on to Jetavana and instructed the assembled monks to receive him as a fellow monk. The boy applied himself with diligence, growing daily in his own practice, and within ten days attained the fruit of stream-entry; before long, he attained arhatship as well, his own six supernormal powers now clear and unhindered, moving freely between rising and vanishing at will. He would go to a great flat rock and sit at its very center, mending his own worn-out robes.

King Prasenajit, hearing that the Buddha had delivered a caṇḍāla boy, reflected that the Buddha himself came from the noble Śākya clan, and that his attendant disciples too all came from the four noble castes — coming to receive offerings within his own palace, they bowed and were received with the utmost reverence — yet now, hearing that the Tathāgata had gone forth and delivered a caṇḍāla, the king wondered how he could possibly show him the same reverence, and resolved to go and rebuke the Buddha himself for this. But, before he even arrived, the king saw ahead of him a monk seated on the great flat rock, surrounded and venerated by five hundred devas of the pure abodes. The king approached and said: please tell the World-Honored One that King Prasenajit wishes to see him. The monk, hearing this, sank at once into the very rock and vanished, emerging instead at the monastery to report to the Buddha, who said he already knew the time had come. The monk then rose up again out of the rock and told the king: the Tathāgata has instructed that you first ask this very monk himself how he came to enter such solid rock, rising and sinking through it freely at will.

The king went before the Buddha, bowed at his feet, and sat to one side, then asked: who was that monk just now, that he possessed such supernormal power? The Buddha told the king: this was the very man who once removed others' own waste for a living. The World-Honored One, on this occasion, spoke two verses, likening this to a filthy, foul field, a deep ditch, or a low pit, from which nonetheless grows a fragrant, pure lotus flower — and asked: great king, would not a clear-sighted man of wisdom still gather such a flower, wherever it grew? The king answered: World-Honored One, so fragrant and pure a flower, wherever found, ought surely to be gathered and used to adorn even something otherwise filthy — for even the womb itself, in which we are all conceived, produces the very flower of merit within it. The king then said to the Buddha: that man has truly gained a benefit beyond ordinary reckoning — from this day forward, I shall myself provide the four requisites in support of this very monk (Source: Sūtra of the Caṇḍāla Boy).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Nīti's own first instinct, on seeing the Buddha approach, is to flee — not from indifference, but from a scrupulous sense that his own body is too unclean to be permitted near him at all, a form of self-effacement the Buddha's own words directly overturn: "I have come here for your own sake." King Prasenajit's own arc mirrors this in reverse — arriving to rebuke the Buddha for the very act of dignifying an outcaste, he finds instead a monk devas themselves already venerate, and the king's own closing verse about the womb producing "the very flower of merit within it" quietly extends the same logic that redeemed Nīti to every person's own origin, noble or otherwise.

19. The Hunters Who Gave Up the Hunt

The fascicle closes with hunters who, rushing to help a deer trapped in their own snare, fall into their own traps and injure one another badly enough that, once healed, they give up the hunt entirely — the Buddha meeting them afterward with a single verse about the arrow that always wounds its own maker.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, the Buddha dwelt in the kingdom of Magadha, in the Āmra grove, within a stone cave north of the city. A great many hunters, entering the mountains to hunt, set out nets and snares everywhere, killing countless deer, and climbed back up the mountain afterward. One deer, caught in one such snare, cried out loudly in distress. The hunters, hearing this, rushed toward the sound — and, in their own haste, fell one after another into their own snares, injuring a great many of their own number. Though none died from it, their wounds were extremely severe, the pain beyond words; supporting one another, they barely made it home, seeking ointments and medicine to treat their own injuries, while their own families and relatives went out to retrieve the bodies of those who had died, mourning them at length.

Among those who had been wounded, once their own injuries had healed, a deep disgust arose within them for the hunt itself — for their own past karmic conditions had ripened, the seeds of goodness within them now ready to bear fruit — and they gave up their own households to study the path, becoming monks. At that time the World-Honored One, surrounded by a great multitude of beings numbering in the hundreds of thousands, taught them the Dharma, wishing to root out their own past conduct entirely and establish true merit in its place, revealing and teaching them to forsake birth and death forever and dwell always in the hall of blessing. Before the whole assembly, he spoke this verse:

"Just as one who crafts his own arrow may wound himself with it in turn, so too the inward arrow — the arrow of craving wounds all beings alike."

These same hunters, though now monks, had not yet fully understood this themselves, until the Tathāgata that very day bore witness that they had indeed once been hunters — and, filled with their own inward shame, reflecting on their own past faults, dwelt in quiet solitude, applying themselves to calm and insight, their minds fixed and undistracted. It was for this very reason that these sons of good family shaved their own hair and beard, donned the three monastic robes, and went forth to study the path, cultivating the unsurpassed pure conduct, bearing witness to it in their own person and finding their own joy in it — birth and death now exhausted, the pure conduct now established, their own task now complete, never again to undergo birth and death, knowing this truly as it is. At that time, all these monks attained arhatship, their own six supernormal powers clear and unhindered, free of every obstruction — and it was for this reason that this verse was spoken:

"One who can awaken to this truth — that craving and suffering arise together — free of desire, free of any such notion, the monk applies himself single-mindedly to his own deliverance" (Source: Sūtra of the Hunters Who Gave Up Their Households to Study the Path).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The hunters' own conversion is notably indirect — they do not renounce the hunt out of compassion for the deer whose own cry drew them into their own traps, but out of the accidental, self-inflicted pain that followed, and even then the Buddha's own verse has to name their past explicitly before its own lesson fully lands. "Just as one who crafts his own arrow may wound himself with it in turn" reframes their literal injury, falling into snares meant for animals, as a physical enactment of the deeper claim the Buddha is making about craving itself — that the very tools built to harm others turn out, one way or another, to wound their own maker first. Closing this long fascicle of many different roads to arhatship on this note, an ordinary accident recognized only in hindsight as the opening of the path, fits a collection that has, throughout, resisted any single template for how realization actually arrives.
