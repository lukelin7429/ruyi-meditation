Kings on the Śrāvaka Path

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 27 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Kings on the Śrāvaka Path, Part 4" (行聲聞道上諸國王部第四) — eleven entries turning from the extreme bodily sacrifice of the preceding two fascicles toward kings converted through more ordinary encounters with the Dharma. Among them: a king awakened to renunciation by the sound of a single remaining bracelet, King Mahākapphiṇa's army halted mid-invasion by an illusory rival king only the Buddha could have conjured, an eight-fold teaching on suffering delivered to five kings that ends with four of them abdicating on the spot, and a monk's own inexplicable servitude in the royal stables traced back to a single moment of casual disrespect shown to his own teacher over the smell of incense.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 27 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 27 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 27 opens a new subdivision of the compendium's kings section: rulers who take up the śrāvaka path rather than the bodhisattva path of the two preceding fascicles — conversion through hearing the Dharma and attaining stream-entry, rather than through extreme acts of self-sacrifice. The register shifts accordingly, toward more everyday narrative detail: court gossip, hunting trips, a stableyard, a marketplace where severed heads are put up for sale as an object lesson. Every entry's full text remains intact.

1. The King of Vārāṇasī Attains Pratyekabuddhahood

A brief entry: a king, irritated by the noise of a single bracelet among his own attendant's many, realizes in that single moment of relief that a crowded life of court and attendants is itself the true source of his own affliction — and attains pratyekabuddhahood on the spot.

Chinese Source

波羅奈王。夏暑熱時處高樓上。坐七寶床令青衣摩。牛頭栴檀香塗身。青衣臂多著釧摩王身。時釧聲滿耳王甚患之。教次第令脫釧。唯獨一釧寂然無聲。王時悟曰。國家臣民婇女多事多惱亦復如是。即時離欲獨處思惟得辟支佛。鬚髮自落著自然衣。從樓閣去以己神足力出家入山。如是因緣中品辟支佛也。有人願作辟支佛。種此善根。時世無佛。善熟。厭世出家得道。名辟支佛(出坐禪三昧經中卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The king of Vārāṇasī, during the heat of summer, sat in his own high tower, upon a couch of the seven treasures, having his attendant rub ox-head sandalwood ointment onto his own body. This attendant wore many bracelets on her own arm as she rubbed the king's body, and the sound of the bracelets, filling his ears, greatly troubled him. He had her remove them one by one, until only a single bracelet remained, and, with only one, there was silence, no sound at all.

The king, at that moment, awakened, and thought: the affairs of state, my own ministers and people, my attendant women — all this bustle and trouble is just the same as this. And, in that very instant, freeing himself from desire, he withdrew into solitude, and, through contemplation, attained pratyekabuddhahood; his hair and beard fell away of themselves, and a robe appeared upon his body of its own accord. He left the tower by his own spiritual power, going forth to dwell in the mountains.

Such a cause and circumstance produces a middling grade of pratyekabuddha. There are those who vow to become pratyekabuddhas, and plant this very root of goodness; when the age holds no buddha, and their own merit has fully ripened, growing weary of the world, they go forth and attain the path in just this way, and are called pratyekabuddhas (Source: Sūtra on the Samādhi of Seated Meditation, middle fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The insight itself is almost absurdly small in scale — the mere absence of noise from a single remaining bracelet — yet the compendium treats this as sufficient cause for full pratyekabuddhahood, which suggests the point is not the size of the triggering observation but the depth of the analogy the king draws from it: the same crowded busyness that makes many bracelets noisy is, he sees at once, the same busyness that fills his entire life as king.

2. The King of Yuezhi Builds Thirty-Two Stūpas

A brief entry: a king vowing to build thirty-two stūpas nearly abandons the project after a single insult from a stranger — until he redirects that very anger toward liberation itself, completing his vow and attaining arhatship in the same moment.

Chinese Source

月氏國王。欲求佛道故作三十二塔。為供養相一一作之至三十一。時有惡人觸王。王心退轉。如此惡人云何可度。即時迴心捨生死向涅槃。作第三十二浮圖以求解脫。由是因緣成羅漢道。是故此寺名波羅提木叉(梁言解脫)自爾以來未滿二百年。此寺猶在。吾亦見之(出雜寶藏經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The king of the country of Yuezhi, wishing to seek the buddha's own path, built thirty-two stūpas as offerings, one after another, honoring each of the thirty-two marks; he had completed thirty-one of them when a wicked man insulted him. The king's own resolve wavered: how could such a wicked man as this ever be delivered? — and, at that very moment, he redirected his own heart, setting aside birth-and-death, and turned instead toward nirvāṇa itself, building the thirty-second stūpa in search of his own liberation. Through this very cause, he attained arhatship.

It is for this reason that this monastery is named Prātimokṣa ("liberation," in the language of Liang). Less than two hundred years have passed since that time, and this monastery still stands — I myself have seen it (Source: Saṃyuktaratnapiṭaka Sūtra).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king's own crisis of faith — doubting whether a truly wicked person could ever be delivered — is resolved not by finding an answer to that doubt but by turning his attention away from the wicked man entirely and back toward his own practice; the thirty-second stūpa is completed not because he has settled the question of the insulting stranger's own worth, but because he has stopped needing to settle it. The compendium's own narrator briefly steps into first person here — "I myself have seen it" — a rare authorial intrusion suggesting this entry was drawn from an eyewitness travel account rather than pure scriptural transmission.

3. Mahākapphiṇa's Invasion, and the Illusory Rival King

A distant king, insulted that a foreign capital he has never heard of will not pay him tribute, marches his own army against Śrāvastī — only to find the Buddha, transformed into an even more magnificent wheel-turning king, already waiting there, whose own effortless display of power converts the entire invading force on the spot.

Chinese Source

舍衛國王名波斯匿。于時南方有國。名曰金地。其王字劫賓寧。太子名摩訶劫賓寧。父崩太子即位。體性聰勇。領三萬六千小國。威風遠振莫不摧伏。然與中土不相交通。後有商客往到金地。以四端細㲲奉上彼王。王問商客言。此物甚好為出何處。啟曰。出於中國。王復問言。其中國者號字云何。答曰。名羅悅祇。又名舍衛。王復問言。中國王以何等故不來獻我。又答曰。各自霸土威名相齊。故不來耳。王自思惟。今當加威令彼率伏。復問商客。中國諸王何者最大。白言。舍衛國王為第一大。即便遣使詣舍衛國。持書示教其理委備告語其王。波斯匿言。我之威風遍閻浮提。卿何所恃斷絕使命。今故遣使共卿相聞。却後七日與我相見。設不如是吾當興兵破汝國界。波斯匿聞深用驚惶。即往詣佛具白斯事。佛告王言。還語使云。我不大更有大王。王奉佛教。告彼使言。世有聖王。近在此間。卿可到邊傳汝王命。使即詣祇洹。于時世尊。自變其身作轉輪王。七寶侍從皆悉備有。使前入化城。既覩大王情甚驚悚以書與之。化王得書蹹著脚下。告彼使言。吾為大王臨領四域。汝王頑迷敢見違拒。汝速還國致宣吾教。信至之日馳奔來覲。臥聞當起。坐聞應立。剋期七日不得稽遲。敢違斯制罪在不請。使還本國具以聞見白金地王。王承斯問深自咎責。合率所領諸小王輩欲朝大王未便即路。先遣一使白大王言。臣所統御三萬六千王。為當都去。將半去耶。大王還報。聽半留住但將半來。時金地王將萬八千小王同時來到。既見化王謁拜畢已。心作是念。大王形貌雖復勝我。力必不如。化王于時勅典兵臣以弓與之。金地國王手不能勝。化王還取以指張弓。復持與之勅令挽。金地國王殊不能挽。化王復取而彈扣之。三千世界皆為震動。復次取箭彎弓而射。離手之後化為五發。其諸箭頭皆出光明。其光明頭皆有蓮華大如車輪。一一華上各各皆有一轉輪王。七寶具足奮出光明。普照三千大千世界。五道眾生莫不蒙賴。諸天境界見其光明及聞說法。身心清淨有發無上正真道意。復有得住不退地者。人道眾生有得一道二道三道之者。出家入要得應真者。有發無上正真道意。得不退地不可稱計。三塗眾生離苦解脫生人天中。時摩訶劫賓寧王。及金地諸小王。見斯神變其心信伏。遠塵離垢得法眼淨。萬八千小王一時皆然。須臾之頃佛攝神力還復本形。諸比丘僧前後圍繞。金地王眾求索出家。鬚髮自墮袈裟在體。思惟妙法盡得羅漢(出賢愚經第七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The king of Śrāvastī was named Prasenajit. At that time, to the south, there was a country called the Golden Land, whose king was named Kapphiṇa. His crown prince was named Mahākapphiṇa. When his father died, the crown prince ascended the throne; clever and bold by nature, he ruled thirty-six thousand lesser kingdoms, his own majesty and might reaching far and subduing all before it — yet he had never once had contact with the central lands.

A merchant later came to the Golden Land, and presented that king with four bolts of fine cloth. The king asked the merchant: this cloth is exceptionally fine — where does it come from? He answered: from the central lands. The king asked further: what is this central land called? He answered: it is named Luóyuèqí, also called Śrāvastī. The king asked further: why has the king of these central lands not come to pay me tribute? The merchant answered: each ruler holds sway over his own land, their own majesty and renown roughly equal — this is why he has not come.

The king thought to himself: I shall now demonstrate my own might, and force him to submit. He asked the merchant further: among the kings of the central lands, which is greatest? He was told: the king of Śrāvastī is foremost among them all.

Mahākapphiṇa then sent an envoy to Śrāvastī, carrying a letter setting out his own position in full detail, and telling that king: my own might reaches across all of Jambudvīpa — on what do you rely, that you have cut off all communication with me? I have now sent this envoy to make myself known to you; within seven days, you shall come to meet with me — should you fail to do so, I shall raise an army and destroy your own kingdom's borders.

Prasenajit, hearing this, was struck with deep alarm, and went at once to the Buddha, telling him the whole matter. The Buddha told the king: tell the envoy this in reply — there is a still greater king than I. The king, following the Buddha's own instruction, told the envoy: there is a sage king, nearby in this very place — you may go there yourself, and deliver your own king's message.

The envoy went to Jetavana. The World-Honored One had, at that time, transformed his own body into that of a wheel-turning king, complete with the seven treasures and full attendant retinue. The envoy, approaching, entered this transformed city, and, seeing the great king there, was struck with great awe, and presented his own letter.

The transformed king, receiving the letter, trod it underfoot, and told the envoy: I myself govern all four continents as sovereign — your own king's stubborn folly dares to defy and resist me. Return to your own kingdom at once, and proclaim my own command in full: on the very day this message arrives, your king must come running to pay me homage — should he be lying down when he hears it, he must rise at once; should he be sitting, he must stand at once. He has seven days, and no delay shall be tolerated — should he dare defy this command, the fault of failing to appear shall be entirely his own.

The envoy returned to his own kingdom, and reported everything he had seen and heard to the king of the Golden Land, who, hearing this challenge, was struck with deep self-reproach, and gathered together all the lesser kings under his own rule, intending to pay homage to this great king, though not yet setting out on the road.

He first sent an envoy to ask the great king: I command thirty-six thousand kings under my own rule — shall I bring them all, or only half? The great king replied: let half remain behind, and bring only half with you.

The king of the Golden Land then arrived together with eighteen thousand lesser kings. Having seen the transformed king and paid him homage, he thought to himself: though this great king's own appearance surpasses mine, his true strength surely does not.

The transformed king then ordered his own minister of arms to hand the king of the Golden Land a bow — yet his hand could not even lift it. The transformed king then took it back, and drew the bow with a single finger, before handing it back to him, commanding him to draw it in turn — yet the king of the Golden Land still could not draw it at all. The transformed king then took the bow back, and merely plucked its own string — and the whole three-thousandfold world shook at once.

He then took up an arrow, drew the bow, and shot it; the moment it left his hand, it divided into five, each arrowhead radiating its own light, and, at the tip of each light, there appeared a lotus flower as large as a chariot wheel, and, upon each and every flower, a wheel-turning king appeared in turn, complete with the seven treasures, radiating light that illuminated the entire three-thousandfold great-thousandfold world-system — every being across the five realms received its own benefit from this light.

The devas, throughout their own realms, seeing this light and hearing the Dharma taught, found their body and mind purified, and gave rise to the resolve for the unsurpassed, true path; some attained the stage of non-retrogression as well. Among human beings, some attained the first, second, or third fruit of the path; some went forth and attained the fruit of arhatship directly; countless others gave rise to the resolve for the unsurpassed, true path, or attained the stage of non-retrogression; and beings of the three lower realms were freed from suffering, liberated, and reborn among humans and devas.

King Mahākapphiṇa, and all the lesser kings of the Golden Land, witnessing this display of spiritual power, had their hearts turned to faith and submission at once, and, far from all dust and defilement, attained the pure Dharma-eye — all eighteen thousand lesser kings, in that same instant, alike.

In but a moment, the Buddha withdrew his own spiritual power, and reverted to his true form, the assembly of monks gathered around him in every direction. The king and people of the Golden Land then asked to go forth themselves; their hair and beard fell away of themselves, and monastic robes appeared upon their bodies, and, contemplating the sublime Dharma, they all attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 7).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Mahākapphiṇa is a well-attested figure in the wider Buddhist canon, later ordained under the Buddha and famous for his own skill in astrology; this entry gives the story of his conversion before that ordination, staged entirely as a contest of raw physical strength — bending a bow neither Mahākapphiṇa nor his lesser kings can so much as lift — rather than through any verbal argument or teaching. The whole confrontation is deliberately anticlimactic: an invading army that never has to fight, resolved instead by watching helplessly as an unliftable bow makes plain, without a single word of doctrine, exactly how outmatched their own conception of power truly is.

4. King Virtuous Dies Defending a Dharma-Teacher

A monk's own strict enforcement of monastic discipline provokes a violent mob of corrupt fellow monks, and the king who intervenes to protect him is wounded to the point of death — a mortal cost the Buddha frames not as tragedy but as the exact pattern by which the true Dharma must be defended whenever it faces its own final decline.

Chinese Source

過去遠劫。此拘尸城有佛出世。號歡喜增益。住世無量化眾生已。然後乃於娑羅雙樹入般涅槃。佛涅槃後遺法住世無量億歲。餘四十年佛法未滅。有一持戒比丘。名曰覺德。多有徒眾眷屬圍遶能師子吼廣說經典。制諸比丘不得畜養奴婢牛羊非法之物。時多破戒比丘。聞作是說皆生惡心。執持刀杖逼是法師。是時國王。名曰有德。聞是事已。為護法故即便往至說法者所。與是破戒諸惡比丘。極共戰鬪。令說法者得免危害。王時被瘡舉身周遍。覺德尋讚王言。善哉善哉。王今真是護正法者。當來之世此身當為無量法器。王於是時得聞法已。心大歡喜尋即命終。生阿閦佛國作第一弟子。其王將從人民眷屬。有戰鬪者。有隨喜者。一切不退菩提之心。命終悉生阿閦佛國。覺德比丘却後壽終。亦復往生阿閦佛國。作聲聞眾中第二弟子。若有正法滅時。應當如是受持擁護。時王者則我身是。說法比丘迦葉佛是(出大涅槃經第三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In a far distant past kalpa, in this very city of Kuśinagara, a buddha appeared in the world, named Joy-Increasing. He dwelt in the world for countless ages, teaching beings, and, in time, entered final nirvāṇa between the twin śāla trees. After his nirvāṇa, his own remaining teaching endured in the world for countless millions of years; in the final forty years before this teaching was to disappear entirely, there was a monk who upheld the precepts strictly, named Awakened-Virtue, surrounded by a great many disciples and followers, capable of a lion's own roar in expounding the scriptures widely, who forbade the other monks from keeping servants, cattle, or sheep, or any other unlawful possession.

Many monks who had broken the precepts, hearing this teaching, gave rise to malicious hearts, and, taking up knives and staves, moved to attack this Dharma-teacher. At that time, a king named Virtuous, hearing of this, went at once, for the sake of protecting the Dharma, to where the teacher stood, and fought fiercely against these evil, precept-breaking monks, so that the teacher was spared from harm — yet the king himself was wounded all over his own body in the process.

Awakened-Virtue then praised the king, saying: excellent, excellent — you are now truly a protector of the true Dharma; in ages to come, this very body of yours shall become an immeasurable vessel of the Dharma. The king, hearing this teaching, was filled with great joy, and, before long, his own life ended, and he was reborn in the buddha-land of Akṣobhya, as his foremost disciple. The king's own followers, people, and kin, whether they had fought alongside him or merely rejoiced in his own deed, all held their own resolve for enlightenment without regressing, and, when their lives ended, were all likewise reborn in Akṣobhya's own buddha-land.

The monk Awakened-Virtue, when his own life later ended, was also reborn in Akṣobhya's own buddha-land, becoming the second disciple among his own assembly of śrāvakas. Whenever the true Dharma faces its own destruction, it should be upheld and protected in just this way.

That king of that time was myself; and that Dharma-teaching monk was Kāśyapa Buddha (Source: Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Like fascicle 26's entry 5, in which a king kills a brahmin for slandering the Mahāyāna, this entry belongs to the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra's own broader and genuinely difficult teaching that violence in direct defense of the Dharma can be spiritually meritorious rather than transgressive — here inverted, since it is the king's own body, not an opponent's life, that is destroyed in the act of protection. We translate this without softening, though it is worth noting explicitly that this represents a minority position within the wider Buddhist canon, offered by the compendium without further argument or qualification.

5. King Merit-Adorned's Eighty-Four-Thousand-Year Offering

A king who has spent eighty-four thousand years supporting the Buddha and Saṅgha grows quietly troubled that his own sons, having gone forth themselves, no longer practice the very giving that first inspired him — until a single miraculous demonstration reveals that a renunciant's own giving simply takes a different, less visible form.

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, King Merit-Adorned, having heard a verse from the Buddha and witnessed his own spiritual transformations, felt his own resolve for enlightenment strengthen further, and, bowing at the Buddha's feet, said: I beg only that the World-Honored One, and his own assembly of bodhisattva disciples, accept my invitation for eighty-four thousand years. To the Tathāgata Universal-Light, he vowed to provide clothing, food and drink, bedding, and medicine, attending to his every need. That buddha and his own assembly accepted the invitation, and the king, knowing his invitation had been accepted, departed rejoicing.

The king's own son, Lion's-Advance, together with twenty thousand other princes, then abandoned their own worldly rank and glory, and, within the Buddha's own teaching, shaved their heads and went forth to cultivate the path, applying themselves diligently, delighting in the pursuit of wholesome teaching. Lion's-Advance attained the five supernormal powers, his own resolve firm and unwavering; that buddha empowered him with his own majestic spirit, so that he might constantly reveal the sublime Dharma to beings, performing buddha-work throughout the entire three-thousandfold great-thousandfold world-system, and countless beings held their own resolve for the unsurpassed great vehicle firm and unwavering because of it.

King Merit-Adorned, across those eighty-four thousand years, offered every kind of comfort to the World-Honored One and his own assembly, and, together with all his own ministers, went before and after them as one of the assembly hearing the Dharma taught. Yet he thought to himself: my own sons, having shaved their heads and gone forth to cultivate the path, constantly receive offerings themselves, yet cannot themselves practice giving — nor have I seen them attain any teaching that surpasses ordinary people's own understanding. Might it not be better for them to return home, and, giving away their own wealth in charity, cultivate merit as I myself have planted these very roots of goodness?

At that time, the Tathāgata Universal-Radiant-Light, knowing what was on King Merit-Adorned's own mind, told the bodhisattva Lion's-Advance: good man, reveal now your own unimpeded merit and spiritual power — let this great bodhisattva's own transformation be seen and heard by this whole assembly, and turn back that mistaken thought, that they might attain right view instead.

The bodhisattva Lion's-Advance then entered absorption at once, and manifested signs of this very kind: the great-thousandfold world shook, and, from the open sky above, flowers, incense, silk canopies, banners, music, fine food and drink, necklaces, robes, and every kind of rare treasure rained down in profusion, filling the whole great-thousandfold world entirely. Beings, witnessing what they had never seen before, all rejoiced greatly.

At that moment, from the earth spirits up to the devas of Akaniṣṭha heaven, all proclaimed together: this great bodhisattva may rightly be called Ākāśagarbha ("Womb of Space") — for it is from the very open sky itself that he causes jeweled treasure to rain down, satisfying all beings entirely. The Buddha confirmed this at once.

The king, witnessing this, leapt with joy at what he had never before seen, and, casting off his own arrogance, joined his palms toward the Buddha, and said: how rare this is, World-Honored One — that a bodhisattva's own merit and wisdom could accomplish such a thing as this! World-Honored One, one who gives while still a householder brings only limited benefit; but one who has gone forth, through the power of spiritual attainment, gives without any limit or boundary at all. A householder's own giving never quite matches the recipient's own true need, and, even while giving, still feels reluctance, and suffers distress because of it; but one who has gone forth gives without reluctance, and suffers no such distress at all.

The king then gave up his own throne to his son Auspicious-Intent, and, with a heart of true faith, shaved his own head, and, within the Buddha's own teaching, went forth to cultivate the path, increasing in wholesome practice, applying himself constantly and diligently, and attained the four dhyānas, the four immeasurable states of mind, and the five supernormal powers. King Auspicious-Intent, for his own part, governed by the true Dharma, so that his kingdom held no resentment anywhere within it, applying himself diligently and without fatigue, making offerings to the World-Honored One.

King Merit-Adorned was Krakucchanda Buddha, as he is now; the bodhisattva Lion's-Advance is Ākāśagarbha Bodhisattva, as he is now; and King Auspicious-Intent is Maitreya, as he is now (Source: Sūtra of King Merit-Adorned's Invitation to the Buddha, and His Own Attainment of the Path Through Going Forth).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king's own quiet disappointment in his sons is treated by the story with real sympathy rather than dismissed as simple misunderstanding — his logic, that giving is the very good he himself has cultivated for eighty-four thousand years, and that his sons' apparent inability to practice it seems like a step backward, is entirely reasonable on its own terms. The bodhisattva's own demonstration does not refute this logic so much as expand it: a renunciant's giving is reframed not as giving's absence but as giving unlimited by material scarcity altogether, a distinction the king could not have arrived at through reasoning alone, and needed to see enacted before him directly.

6. King Lándá and the Rākṣasa Who Demanded Sacrifice

A kingdom collapsed into moral chaos and drought resorts to child sacrifice to break a curse — until Maudgalyāyana, sent by the Buddha, conjures a terrifying demon of his own to draw out the kingdom's genuine unbelievers first, before the real teaching can even begin.

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there was a king named Lándá, whose land was rich and fertile, and whose people held sincere faith, ruler and subject, father and son, all following the path together. In time, a later king ascended the throne, and the true Dharma among his own people grew thin, his own governance unjust, wrongfully executing innocent people, and, as this injustice grew, calamity followed in kind: the rains no longer fell in their own season, the five grains failed to ripen, and strange omens arose one after another, until the kingdom fell into ruin, its own people fearing its collapse.

More than five hundred people took up false teachings, and worshipped malevolent spirits, high and low alike imitating one another, as grass bends before the wind; the common people, delighting in disorder, competed in wickedness, the strong preying on the weak, killing one another, seizing others' property without regard for what was right, committing adultery, ruler and subject alike lost in drunken excess, their own confusion deepening day by day. Drought persisted for more than three years; prayers offered again and again brought no rain at all.

The court priests told the king: a great sacrifice must now be made — seven young boys, together with ten white oxen and ten white horses, should be burned as offerings to heaven, and rain will then surely come. The king made preparations at once, and the whole kingdom, high and low, fell into terrified unrest.

The Buddha told Maudgalyāyana: the kingdom of Lándá lies in ruin, its people suffering greatly; three hundred million of them are fit to attain liberation through you — go there, and open their minds to the teaching.

Maudgalyāyana went and taught the Dharma, opening their minds to its meaning — yet no one in the kingdom would accept it at first. Maudgalyāyana then transformed himself into the body of a great demon spirit, several dozen fathoms tall, and sat before the city gate, calling out in a thunderous voice for the king and his own ministers to come out. The ruler and his ministers, terrified, feared for their own survival, and, together with his attendant ministers, the king bowed his head and pleaded for his own life.

The spirit told the king: bring me at once any subject who is disloyal, or any son who is unfilial — I wish to devour them. The king made inquiry at once, and was told there were none such. The spirit told the king again: bring me at once any thief, any adulterer, any divisive or foul-mouthed speaker — I wish to devour them. The king made inquiry again, and was told again there were none such. The spirit told the king yet again: bring me at once any drunkard — I wish to devour them. The king made inquiry once more, and was told again there were none such.

Maudgalyāyana then revealed his own true form, and spoke with the spirit himself. The king, seeing Maudgalyāyana, bowed his head, and begged him to explain this great spirit's own purpose. Maudgalyāyana asked the king: what does this spirit truly seek? The king said: it wishes to devour wicked people. Maudgalyāyana asked: are there truly such people within your own kingdom? The king answered: there are none — and begged repeatedly that the spirit be sent away, vowing: I shall, for the rest of my own life, take refuge in this renunciant.

Maudgalyāyana then told the great spirit: this kingdom holds no one of the kind you seek — you may pardon their own transgressions; should any offender arise in the future, you may come and devour them then. The spirit accepted this, and vanished at once.

The king and his own ministers and people all bowed their heads together, expressing their own gratitude, and asked to take refuge. Maudgalyāyana told them: if the king truly wishes to take refuge, he should first take refuge in the Buddha himself. The king asked about the Buddha's own merit, and Maudgalyāyana explained that the Buddha is honored alone throughout the three realms, and that his own teaching has three thousand śrāvaka disciples.

The king said: how fortunate I am, to have heard such an extraordinary thing! — and told Maudgalyāyana: I wish to invite the Buddha, to receive his own honored teaching, and follow it for the rest of my life. Maudgalyāyana told the king: should your own faith truly hold firm in this, the blessing that follows is beyond measure — you need only purify your own heart, and hold the Buddha in mind.

The king, following this instruction, bathed and kept the fast, and, bowing from afar toward the Buddha's own land, said: through my own ignorance, I have wrongly occupied a place above my own people, unable to purify my own heart or guide them rightly, and it is this very fault of mine that has brought the kingdom to ruin — I beg only that the heavenly honored one might let his own light descend, that all my people might obtain blessing without end.

The Buddha and his own monks, while still a hundred li away, traveled ahead through the open sky, lotus flowers of five colors supporting their own feet, the devas escorting them with jeweled canopies and flower-parasols, welcoming them along the way, filling the sky with every kind of fine music.

Witnessing the Buddha's own majestic radiance illuminating heaven and earth, the king and his ministers bowed at his feet, and, at the crossroads of the four directions, laid out broad canopies and curtains, sweeping and cleansing the ground thoroughly. The Buddha sat upon a lion-throne that arose spontaneously, spread with heavenly silk cushions; the king served food and drink with his own hands, and personally brought water for washing.

Once the dedication of blessing was complete, the Buddha taught the essentials of the four noble truths — suffering, emptiness, impermanence, and non-self — and the king, overjoyed, asked to receive the five precepts; his own entangling defilements dissolved, and he attained the fruit of stream-entry. The five hundred non-Buddhist ascetics whom the king had once served then asked to become monks themselves, and the Buddha granted this at once; laying his own hand upon their heads, their hair fell away and monastic robes appeared upon their bodies, and, within seven days, all of them attained arhatship. The assembled people attained the fruit of stream-entry, or received the five precepts and diligently practiced the ten wholesome actions, taking refuge in the Three Jewels, keeping the six monthly fast-days and the three annual fasts, each guiding the others in turn, and making this their own lasting practice.

Ānanda asked the Buddha: what past karmic connection did this king and his people share with Maudgalyāyana? The Buddha said: long ago, in the age of Vipaśyin Buddha, Maudgalyāyana was a lay follower, dwelling in long-standing poverty, yet upholding the precepts faithfully, his own mind never once faltering. Gathering flowers in the mountains, he came upon a Xūwén tree, and, just as he was about to climb it to gather flowers, he did not notice a beehive hidden ahead of him; several thousand bees, startled, scattered and chased after him in swarm. He thought to himself, at that very moment: should I attain the path myself, I shall deliver you as well. Those who attained the path today were, every one, those very bees of that time (Source: Sūtra of King Lándá).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Maudgalyāyana's own conjured demon performs a remarkably precise piece of social diagnosis before any actual teaching begins: rather than threatening the kingdom indiscriminately, it methodically demands only the specific wrongdoers the earlier narration has already named — the disloyal, the unfilial, thieves, adulterers, drunkards — and the king's own repeated, truthful answer that none such exist forces a confrontation between the kingdom's stated self-image and the moral collapse the story has just spent several paragraphs describing in detail. Only once that gap has been surfaced, through fear rather than argument, does the actual conversion begin — the demon functions less as punishment than as an uncomfortable mirror.

Long ago there were five kings, whose kingdoms lay near one another, who were close friends and visited each other regularly. The greatest among them was named Universal-Peace, who practiced the bodhisattva path; the other four lesser kings constantly practiced wrong conduct instead. The great king, moved by compassion for them, invited them to his own hall, and they took their ease together for seven full days and nights.

Once the seven days were complete, the lesser kings told the great king: there is much business awaiting us in our own kingdoms — we ask leave to return home. The great king saw them off, and, on the road, asked each of them in turn to name their own greatest pleasure.

One king answered: in the third month of spring, when the trees bloom in glory, to wander freely through the open meadows. Another answered: I wish always to be king — my own saddled horse, fine robes, officials and subjects surrounding me on every side, resplendent and radiant, bells and drums sounding as I come and go, the people along the road turning their heads to look. Another said: I wish for my own wife and children to be of matchless beauty, that we might take our pleasure together, our hearts fully content. Another said: I wish my own parents to live forever, my friends, brothers, wife and children all gathered around me, fine clothes and delicious food to please my own body and mouth, plain lute and attendant women to entertain us together.

They then all asked the great king what pleasure he himself desired most. The great king answered: I take pleasure in neither being born nor dying, in neither suffering nor affliction, in neither hunger nor thirst, in neither cold nor heat — in being entirely free, whether present or gone.

The four kings said together: such a pleasure as this must have some wise teacher behind it. The great king answered: my own teacher is called the Buddha, and dwells nearby, at the Jetavana monastery. The kings, delighted, went together to the World-Honored One.

The great king said to the Buddha: we, having attained human birth, remain ignorant and dull, deeply attached to the pleasures of this world, and understand nothing of transgression or merit — I beg the Buddha to teach these disciples of his own the truth of suffering.

The Buddha said: listen well, all of you, and I shall teach it to you. Human life in this world is beset by suffering on every side; let me briefly explain the eight sufferings.

The first is the suffering of birth. When a person dies, not knowing which realm their own spirit will go to, and not yet having found a place of rebirth, they take on the intermediate-state body entirely; within twenty-one days, their father and mother unite, and they come to be conceived. In the first week, the embryo is like thin curds; in the second week, like thick curds; in the third week, like congealed butter; in the fourth week, like a small piece of flesh. Once the placenta is fully formed, a subtle wind enters the belly, and blows through the body, opening up the six sense faculties. Dwelling within the mother's own womb, below the raw organs and above the digestive organs, whenever the mother eats even a single mouthful of hot food, it pours over the child's own body as though scalding it in boiling water; whenever the mother drinks a single cup of cold water, it is as though icy cold were cutting into its own flesh. When the mother is full, the child's own body is crushed and compressed, its pain beyond words; when the mother is hungry, the child hangs as though upside down within her own belly, suffering beyond measure.

When the tenth month is complete, and birth is imminent, the head turns toward the birth canal, a pain as severe as being crushed between two mountains, and the child falls out onto the straw below; its own body, so delicate and soft, feels the straw's own touch like the edge of a blade, and it suddenly cries out in its first great wail. Is this not suffering? — All present said: it is indeed suffering.

The second is the suffering of old age. Raised by one's own parents to full maturity, one eats to excess when hungry and to excess when full, without any true moderation, until, gradually reaching old age, the hair turns white and the teeth fall out, the eyes grow dim and the ears dull; vigor departs and decline arrives, the skin sags and the face wrinkles, every joint aches, and even walking becomes a great hardship; sitting or rising, one groans, grief and suffering never far away; the very spirit of consciousness dims and fades, and death arrives in its own turn, one's remaining life growing ever shorter, speaking through tears, needing another's help simply to sit or stand. Is this not suffering? They answered: it is indeed suffering.

The third is the suffering of sickness. The body is formed from the union of earth, water, fire, and wind; should even one of these four elements fall out of balance, a hundred and one illnesses arise; should all four fall out of balance together, four hundred and four illnesses arise at once. When earth falls out of balance, the whole body aches; when water falls out of balance, the whole body swells; when fire falls out of balance, the whole body burns with fever; when wind falls out of balance, the whole body stiffens, every joint aching as though beaten with a rod. As the four elements advance and retreat within, the hands and feet fail to obey, one's own strength drains away entirely, and one needs another's help simply to sit or stand; the mouth grows parched, the lips cracked, the sinews snapped, the nose split; the eyes can no longer see form, the ears can no longer hear sound; impure discharge flows forth, and one lies in it upon one's own bed, the heart filled with suffering, every word tinged with grief. One's own closest kin attend day and night without rest, yet even the finest food, once it enters the mouth, tastes only bitter. Is this not suffering? They answered: it is indeed suffering.

The fourth is the suffering of death. When a person dies, four hundred and four illnesses strike all at once; the four elements are about to scatter, and the spirit grows unsettled. As death approaches, it is as though blades and wind were tearing the body apart, no place left unpained; a cold sweat pours forth, and the two hands grope at empty air. Family within the household and without gather at the dying one's own side, grieving and weeping, a pain that pierces to the very marrow, beyond one's own endurance. The wind departs, the breath scatters, the fire of life goes out, the body grows cold, and the spirit departs. The body then lies stiff and straight, aware of nothing further; within ten days, the flesh rots and blood flows, the body swelling and decaying, its stench unbearable to approach, and it is cast aside in the open wilderness, where birds devour its flesh entirely, leaving only dry bone, the skull scattered apart from the rest. Is this not suffering? They answered: it is indeed suffering.

The fifth is the suffering of separation from those one loves. Family within the household and without, brothers, wife and children, cherishing one another — and then, in a single morning, all is broken and lost. Taken captive, each scattered apart from the others, father to the east, son to the west, mother to the south, daughter to the north, no longer together in even a single place; made servants to others, each grieves alone, all contact between them severed, in a darkness so deep there is no hope of ever seeing one another again. Is this not suffering? They answered: it is indeed suffering.

The sixth is the suffering of not obtaining what one seeks. A household spends its own wealth seeking official rank, hoping for wealth and honor, never satisfied with the pursuit; should such a person by chance obtain a post at some distant border posting, before long, driven by greed, they seize the people's own property there, and are reported for it; in a single day, disaster strikes, and they are carried off in a prisoner's cart, their own grief and suffering beyond measure, not knowing what day will bring life or death. Is this not suffering? They answered: it is indeed suffering.

The seventh is the suffering of meeting what one hates. People of this world, shallow in custom, dwell amid craving and desire, quarreling over trivial matters, until they come to kill one another, and a great enmity is formed; each flees and hides from the other, with nowhere truly safe to go, each sharpening blade and arrow, gripping bow and staff. Meeting by chance upon a narrow path, bows drawn and arrows nocked, blades turned against one another, neither knowing who shall prevail — in that very moment, terror beyond measure grips them both. Is this not suffering? They answered: it is indeed suffering.

The eighth is the suffering of grief, sorrow, and affliction. Living in this world, the longest-lived reach a hundred years, while the short-lived are harmed even within the womb; and even one who obtains a full hundred years loses half of it to the night alone, leaving only fifty remaining years — spent drunk, or ill, unaware even of living as a true human being; foolish in childhood, not yet understanding proper conduct through the first fifteen years; and, past eighty, grown dull and witless in old age, deaf and blind, with no rule of conduct left at all. When the world grows disordered, drought, fire, and heavy frost ruin the crops in the fields; households within and without suffer every kind of illness; one's own livelihood is suddenly lost, official taxes and levies imprison one in jail with no known date of release; brothers and children, traveling far, fail to return; one's own household grows poor and cold, lacking food and clothing; neighboring villages and shrines fall into disrepair; a family member dies with no means for a proper burial; when spring comes for planting, there is no plow-ox to be had; and even gatherings meant for festival joy turn instead to shared grief. Is this not suffering? They answered: it is indeed suffering.

At that time, the five kings, and the thousands upon thousands of ministers gathered in that assembly, hearing this truth of suffering taught, had their own minds opened in understanding, and attained the fruit of stream-entry at once; looking now upon their own palace halls as though gazing upon a foul latrine, with nothing left in them to delight in, they gave up their own thrones to their younger brothers, and went forth to cultivate the path themselves, cultivating merit tirelessly, day after day (Source: Sūtra of the Five Kings).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The four lesser kings' own stated pleasures — a spring outing, the trappings of rule, a beautiful family, loving parents and good food — are ordinary and entirely sympathetic wishes, never mocked by the story itself; it is only against Universal-Peace's own answer, freedom from birth and death, that they come to seem, by simple contrast, provisional and fragile. The eightfold teaching that follows is unusually clinical and physically detailed even by this compendium's own standards, particularly its account of gestation, and its own effect on the four kings is total and immediate — not a gradual conversion but four thrones abandoned in the same hour the teaching ends.

8. The Brahmin King's Inexhaustible Treasure

A king's own vast, unrelenting generosity is tested by a disguised Buddha posing as a brahmin — who, each time offered a larger handful of treasure, keeps refusing it for the same simple reason: whatever he takes, some new want will always demand more than he can carry.

In the country of Duōmèi, there was a brahmin king who gave up his own royal stipend and devoted himself largely to non-Buddhist practices. One day, a wholesome resolve arose in him of itself, and he wished to give in great charity, following the brahmanical way: he piled treasure high as a mountain, and let anyone who came to beg take away as much as they could hold in a single grasp of the hand — and yet, day after day, the pile never diminished.

The Buddha, knowing that this king's own past merit had ripened him for deliverance, transformed himself into a brahmin ascetic, and came to that kingdom. The king came out to meet him, greeting him with proper courtesy, and asked: what do you seek? Do not hesitate to ask. The ascetic answered: I have come from afar, wishing to beg treasure to build myself a dwelling. The king said: excellent — take as much as a single grasp of your own hand can hold.

The ascetic took a single handful, walked seven paces, and then set it back down in its original place. The king asked: why do you not take it? The ascetic answered: this would be barely enough to build myself a hut — yet I shall also need to take a wife, and this alone would not suffice for that as well; it is for this reason that I do not take it.

The king said: take three handfuls instead. The ascetic did so, walked seven paces, and again set it back down. The king asked him: why do you do this again? He answered: this would suffice to take a wife, but I would still have no land, no servants, no oxen or horses — reckoning it all, it is still not enough; it is for this reason that I set aside the thought.

The king said: take seven handfuls, then. The ascetic did so, walked seven paces, and again set it back down. The king asked: what reason is there this time? The ascetic answered: should I then have sons and daughters, I would need to provide for their own marriages, and for every fortune and misfortune besides — reckoning it all, it is still not enough; it is for this reason that I do not take it.

The king said: then take the entire pile of treasure, all of it, and keep it for yourself. The ascetic accepted it, and turned to leave — yet the king, greatly puzzled by this, asked him again what his own true reason was.

The ascetic answered: I came originally begging in order to sustain my own livelihood — yet, reflecting truly on this, a person's own life in this world amounts to so little; the ten thousand things are impermanent, and cannot be relied upon even from morning to evening. As one cause leads to another, worry and suffering only deepen day by day, and a pile of treasure as high as a mountain brings no true benefit to oneself at all — coveting and scheming only brings needless hardship upon oneself. Better, then, to set aside such thoughts entirely, and seek instead the unconditioned path. It is for this reason that I do not take it.

The king's own mind opened in understanding at this, and he said: I wish to follow this clear teaching. At this, the ascetic revealed the Buddha's own radiant form, rising into the open sky, and spoke this verse for him:

"Though one gather rare treasure into a pile / towering high as the very sky itself, / and fill the whole world with it besides — / this is not worth glimpsing the essence of the path. / What is unwholesome takes on the very likeness of the wholesome; / what is craved appears as though free of craving; / what is truly suffering takes on the likeness of pleasure — / and the deluded man never wearies of it."

The king, seeing the Buddha's own light illuminate heaven and earth far and wide, and hearing this verse, leapt up with joy; he and his ministers at once received the five precepts, and attained the fruit of stream-entry (Source: Sūtra of Illustrative Parables [Chūyào jīng], fascicle 16).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ascetic's own escalating refusals trace a single, coherent logic of scarcity that no amount of increased giving can ever actually satisfy — a hut demands a wife, a wife demands land and servants, children demand provision for their own future marriages, and each new want simply expands to match whatever has just been granted. The king's own inexhaustible pile of treasure turns out to be the wrong kind of infinity entirely; it is the ascetic's own wants, not the king's own supply, that prove genuinely limitless, and it is this recognition — not any lecture about generosity — that becomes the occasion for the Buddha's own teaching.

9. King Módá and the Debt of a Careless Word

A king, curious why a monk with visible arhat-level powers has been sentenced to servitude in the royal stables, learns from the monk himself that a single unspoken thought of contempt toward his own teacher's request to wash his hands before a meal has followed him across lifetimes as an unpaid debt.

At that time, a monk who had attained arhatship entered the kingdom to beg for alms, and was seized along with the conscripts and taken to the royal palace, where the king set him to tending the royal horses.

After seven days of such hard labor, the king came himself to observe him. The monk, seeing the king, rose lightly into the air, and hovered there in the open sky, displaying his own majestic spiritual power. The king, struck with terror, bowed his head and repented: I have truly been foolish, unable to distinguish the true from the false — and made inquiry, within the court and without, as to who had set this holy man to such a task, intending to punish whoever was responsible, even to the point of execution.

The monk told the king: this is the fault of neither the king nor his people. Long ago, in a past life of my own, while practicing the path, I regularly made offerings to my own teacher. I once prepared a drink for him, and my teacher told me: wash your hands first, and only then shall you drink. My own foolish mind thought to itself: my teacher, too, has never tended the royal horses himself — why, then, does he insist I wash my hands beforehand? Hearing this, I was filled with distress at what I had done; my teacher, knowing my own state of mind, thought to himself: I am soon to enter nirvāṇa — why should I trouble this person further? Since that time, long ago, across many turns of birth and death, I have now, for this very reason, received the karmic consequence of that past fault: seven days tending horses. For good and evil conduct alike bring their own reward and misfortune in turn, following one as closely as a shadow follows its own form.

The king, hearing the monk explain this matter of transgression and merit, felt his own mind open in understanding, and, overjoyed, asked to take refuge with this holy man. The monk told him: you should take refuge in the Buddha himself, for the Buddha is teacher to the three realms.

The king and his people then followed the monk to the Buddha, bowed before him, and received the five precepts, becoming lay followers. The Buddha then revealed his own auspicious marks and majestic spiritual power to the king and his people, illuminating heaven and earth, and taught them the truth of impermanence, suffering, and emptiness. The king, at that time, attained the fruit of stream-entry, and the people of his kingdom all received the five precepts and the ten wholesome actions, taking refuge in the Three Jewels, and keeping the monthly fast as their own lasting practice (Source: Sūtra of King Módá).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The monk's own teacher offers, in a single line, one of the compendium's most precise statements of karmic proportion: "this thought of yours seems light now, but consider how heavy it shall prove later" — a warning given not about any dramatic transgression but about a single unspoken, momentary judgment of contempt toward a reasonable request. That the teacher chooses to enter nirvāṇa that same night rather than let his own disciple's distress continue is itself a small, understated act of compassion easy to pass over: rather than prolong the disciple's guilt through further correction, he simply removes himself from the situation entirely.

10. King Qiántuó and the Ox Who Spoke

A king, understanding the speech of animals, executes a woodcutter on his own brahmin teacher's word alone — then, hearing an injured ox plead its own case in the very same language, realizes his devotion has made him crueler than the animal he can suddenly understand.

Chinese Source

時有國王。名曰犍陀。奉事婆羅門居在山中。多種果樹樵人毀樹。時婆羅門。將詣王所言其無狀。殘敗果樹請王治殺王敬事婆羅門。即為殺戮。自後未久。有牛食人稻。其主遂捶牛折一角。血流被面痛不可忍。牛徑到王所白言。我實無狀食人少稻。今捶折我角。王曉鳥獸語。告牛言。當為汝治殺之。牛言。雖殺此人不止我痛。但當約勅後莫苦人。王便感念。我事婆羅門。但坐果樹令我殺人。不如此牛也。便呼婆羅門問言。今事此道有何福乎。婆羅門報言。可得攘災致福富貴長壽。王復問言。可得脫於生死不。報言。不得免於生死也。王到佛所。五體投地為佛作禮白言。我聞佛道至尊。教化天下所度無數。願受法言以自改革。佛即授王五戒十善。為說一切天地人物無生不死者。布施持戒現世得福。忍辱精進心行智慧者其德無量。後生天上亦可得作遮迦越王。可得無為度世之道。佛現相好威神光耀。王歡喜意解。得須陀洹道。牛後七日壽終。上生天上(出揵陀國王經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a king named Qiántuó, who served a brahmin teacher and dwelt in the mountains, where he had planted a great many fruit trees. A woodcutter once damaged some of these trees; the brahmin brought word of this to the king, describing the man's own conduct as outrageous, and asked that he be put to death for damaging the fruit trees. The king, revering the brahmin, had the man executed at once.

Not long after this, an ox ate a portion of a man's own rice crop; its owner, in anger, beat the ox and broke one of its horns, blood streaming down its face, the pain unbearable. The ox went directly to the king, and said: I have indeed done wrong, eating a small portion of this man's rice — yet he has beaten me and broken my own horn for it.

The king, who understood the speech of birds and beasts, told the ox: I shall have this man put to death for you. The ox said: even should you kill this man, it would not stop my own pain — only command him, from now on, not to harm animals in this way.

The king, moved by this, reflected: I have served this brahmin, and, over the mere matter of fruit trees, had a man put to death — I am not even the equal of this very ox. He then summoned the brahmin, and asked him: what true blessing comes from serving this path of yours? The brahmin answered: it can ward off calamity, and bring blessing, wealth, honor, and long life. The king asked further: can it free one from birth and death? He answered: it cannot free one from birth and death at all.

The king then went to the Buddha, threw his whole body to the ground in homage, and said: I have heard that the Buddha's own path is supreme, teaching and transforming the whole world, delivering beings beyond count — I wish to receive your own teaching, and reform myself by it.

The Buddha then conferred upon the king the five precepts and the ten wholesome actions, and taught him that nothing at all, among all beings of heaven, earth, and humankind, is born without also being subject to death; that giving in charity and upholding the precepts bring blessing in this very life; and that one who practices patience, diligence, and wisdom of mind attains merit beyond measure, and, reborn in heaven afterward, might even become a wheel-turning king, or attain the unconditioned path of liberation from the world.

The Buddha revealed his own auspicious marks and majestic spiritual radiance, illuminating all; the king, overjoyed, his own mind opened in understanding, attained the fruit of stream-entry. The ox, seven days later, reached the end of its own life, and was reborn in heaven (Source: Sūtra of the King of Qiántuó).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ox's own reply is the entry's sharpest moment: offered lethal revenge on its own behalf, it refuses the offer outright, asking only that future cruelty be prevented rather than that past cruelty be repaid in kind — a response the king recognizes at once as morally superior to his own recent judgment against the woodcutter, executed on nothing more than a single brahmin's unverified complaint. The king's own gift of understanding animal speech, framed earlier in the story as simply an unusual royal talent, becomes the very instrument of his own moral education once an ox actually uses it to hold up a mirror to him.

11. King Universal-Attainment and the Selling of Heads

Fascicle 27 closes with a king baffled by his own court's outrage that he bows before an ordinary beggar-monk — and who tests his ministers' claim that a human head is beyond price by literally putting severed heads up for sale in the marketplace, exposing an uncomfortable truth about what people actually value.

Chinese Source

有夫延國王。名曰普達。身奉佛法未常偏抂。常有慈愍。國內愚民不知三尊。每當齋戒登高觀視。還必頭面稽首為禮。國中臣民怪王如此。自共議言。王處萬民之尊。遠近敬服登謂人從。有何請欲毀辱威儀頭面著地。群臣欲諫不敢。與吏民數千出宮未遠見一道人。王下輦却蓋住。其群從為之作禮。尋從而還。施設飯食遂不成行。群臣諫言。大王至尊。何宜於道路為此乞匃道人頭面著地。天下尊貴唯有頭面。加為國王不與他同。王勅臣下。求死人首及六畜頭。臣下遍索歷日乃得。王言。於市賣之。臣下賣之。牛馬猪羊頭皆售。但人頭未售。王言。貴賤賣去如其不售。使以匃人如是歷日賣既不售。匃無取者頭皆膖臭。王便大怒。語臣下言。卿言。人頭最貴不可毀辱。今人頭何故匃無取者。即勅嚴駕出曠澤中當有所問。群臣振聳出到城外。告群臣言。卿寧識吾先君時。有小兒常執持蓋者不。臣下對曰實識。王言。今此兒何所在。對曰。亡已十七年。王言。此兒為人善惡何如。對言。臣等常覩其承事先王。齋戒恭肅誠信自守非法不言。王告諸臣。今若見此兒在時所著衣服。寧識之不。諸臣對曰。雖自久遠臣故識之。王使取前亡小兒衣。曰。此寧是不。臣下對曰是。王曰。今儻見兒身為識之不。臣下良久。對曰。臣自懼蔽闇卒覩不別。前所見道人來。王大歡喜。又為道人賣頭匃人不取。今欲示其本末。有幸相遇。願為開導。道人即為臣下說。王本是先王時執蓋小兒。常隨先王。齋戒奉行正法清淨守意不犯諸惡。其後過世生為王子。今得尊貴皆由宿行齋戒所致。臣下大小莫不僉曰。吾等幸遇得覩道人。願哀愚朦乞為弟子。道人告諸臣民。吾有大師。當從受問。諸臣報言。願盡年命一受法言。道人曰。我師字佛。身能飛行頂有光明。分身散體變化萬端。獨步三界莫與齊倫。門徒清潔皆為沙門。其所教授度脫不唐。臣下即啟道人。佛寧可得見不。去此幾何。道人報言。甚善。當啟世尊。六千餘里。道人即飛到舍衛。具以啟佛。佛言。明日當到夫延國臨至皆現威神。王及群臣持華香出城迎佛。覩佛威靈喜懼交并五體投地稽首為禮。白佛言。勞屈世尊。并及眾僧遠到此土。王盡心供設。手自斟酌行澡水。呪願畢。佛笑口光五色。阿難言。佛不妄笑。答言。昔摩呵文佛時。王為大姓家子。其父供養三尊。父命子傳香。時有一侍使。意中輕之不與其香。罪福響應暫招役報。奉法無替今得為王。若我得道當度此人。本末意解即得須陀洹。國中人民。皆受五戒行十善(出普達王經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a king of the country of Fūyán, named Universal-Attainment, who personally served the Buddha's own teaching, never once acting unjustly, always holding compassion for others — yet the ignorant people of his own kingdom knew nothing of the Three Jewels. Whenever a fast day came, the king would ascend a high place to survey his own land, and, upon returning, would always bow his head to the ground in homage.

The ministers and people of his own kingdom found this strange, and said among themselves: the king holds the highest station among all his own people, revered and honored far and wide — what could possibly move him to bow his own head to the ground like this, seemingly demeaning his own royal dignity? His ministers wished to remonstrate with him over this, but did not dare to.

Once, going out from the palace with several thousand officials and subjects, they had not gone far when they saw a lone renunciant on the road. The king descended from his own carriage, set aside his own canopy, and stood there; his whole retinue then bowed before the renunciant as well, before following the king back — and the meal they had prepared for the outing went unserved.

His ministers then remonstrated with him: great king, in your own supreme dignity, why should you bow your head to the ground on the open road before a mere beggar-monk such as this? Nothing in this world is more honored than one's own head — and, all the more so, for a king, whose own standing is unlike anyone else's.

The king then commanded his own ministers: seek out the heads of dead people, together with the heads of the six domestic animals. His ministers searched everywhere, and, after some days, obtained them. The king said: sell them in the marketplace. His ministers did so; the heads of oxen, horses, pigs, and sheep all sold readily — but no human head found a buyer at all.

The king said: sell them at whatever price, high or low, and, should they still not sell, offer them to beggars instead. And so it continued, day after day, yet still no human head would sell, and no beggar would even take one for free — until, at last, the heads all began to swell and rot with decay.

The king then grew greatly angered, and told his ministers: you claimed that a human head is the most honored thing of all, not to be demeaned — why, then, will no human head sell, nor even find a beggar willing to take it? He then ordered his own carriage prepared, and went out into the open wilderness, saying there was something he wished to ask.

His ministers, trembling, went with him out of the city. The king told them: do you recall, in my own late father's time, a small child who used to carry his royal canopy? His ministers answered: indeed, we recall him. The king asked: where is this child now? They answered: he has been dead seventeen years now.

The king asked: what kind of person was this child, good or otherwise? They answered: we ourselves often saw him serve the former king faithfully, keeping the fast with reverent solemnity, sincere and true, holding fast to his own conduct, never once speaking anything improper.

The king told his ministers: were you to see the very clothing this child once wore, would you still recognize it? His ministers answered: though it has been so long, we would still recognize it. The king had the dead child's own clothing brought out, and asked: is this it? His ministers answered: it is. The king asked: and were you now to see this child's own body again, would you recognize him? His ministers, after a long silence, answered: we fear our own understanding is too clouded to be certain, should we suddenly behold him now.

At that moment, the very renunciant they had seen earlier arrived. The king, overjoyed, said: I have also had a head sold for this renunciant, and offered to beggars who would not take it — I now wish to reveal the whole truth of this matter; how fortunate that we meet again — I ask you to explain it to them.

The renunciant then told the ministers: the king was, in truth, that very child who once carried the former king's own canopy, who constantly attended him, kept the fast, and practiced the true Dharma, his own mind pure, committing no wrong at all. After that life passed, he was reborn as a prince, and it is entirely because of his own past practice of the fast that he now holds such honored station.

The ministers, great and small alike, said together: how fortunate we are, to have met and beheld this renunciant — we beg, in our own foolishness, to become his disciples. The renunciant told the assembled ministers and people: I myself have a great teacher, from whom you should seek this teaching directly. The ministers answered: we wish, for the rest of our own lives, to receive his teaching just once.

The renunciant said: my own teacher is called the Buddha. His body can fly through the air, light radiates from his own crown, and he can divide and scatter his own body in countless transformations; he walks alone, unequaled throughout the three realms, and his own disciples, pure and clean, are all monks — those he teaches are never delivered in vain.

The ministers asked the renunciant at once: might we see the Buddha ourselves? How far away is he? The renunciant answered: very well — I shall inform the World-Honored One myself — he is some six thousand li from here. The renunciant then flew at once to Śrāvastī, and reported the whole matter fully to the Buddha, who said: tomorrow, I shall arrive in the country of Fūyán.

As the Buddha drew near, he displayed his own majestic spiritual power everywhere; the king and his ministers, bearing flowers and incense, went out from the city to welcome him, and, beholding the Buddha's own divine majesty, overcome with mingled joy and awe, threw their whole bodies to the ground in homage, and said to the Buddha: we have troubled the World-Honored One, and his own assembly of monks, to travel so far to this land.

The king made every provision with his whole heart, personally serving food and drink and bringing water for washing. Once the dedication of blessing was complete, the Buddha smiled, and light of five colors shone from his own mouth. Ānanda said: the Buddha never smiles without cause. The Buddha answered: long ago, in the age of the Buddha Móhēwén, this king was the son of a great family, whose own father made offerings to the Three Jewels, and once commanded his son to carry incense to the ceremony. There was, at that time, a certain attendant who, in his own heart, held this task in contempt, and refused to carry the incense himself.

Transgression and merit answer one another exactly as an echo answers a sound — and it is for this very reason that he was, for a time, made to serve in menial labor; yet, because he continued in his own devoted practice without ever abandoning it, he has now attained kingship. Should I myself attain the path, I shall deliver this very person as well.

Understanding the whole matter, from beginning to end, the king at once attained the fruit of stream-entry, and the people of his own kingdom all received the five precepts, and practiced the ten wholesome actions (Source: Sūtra of King Universal-Attainment).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The king's own marketplace experiment is a piece of genuinely pointed social theater: his ministers claim the human head is the most honored thing in existence, too precious to be bowed before an ordinary renunciant, yet, offered actual human heads for sale or even for free, no one in the entire kingdom will take one — proving, by their own market's own verdict, that reverence for "the head" was never really about heads at all, but about rank and appearance. The story's own closing revelation — that the king's present honor traces back to a single childhood act of devoted service, while an unnamed attendant's single act of contemptuous refusal cost him years of menial servitude — closes the fascicle on the same note struck throughout: outward station tracks poorly with the actual moral history that produced it. This closes fascicle 27.
