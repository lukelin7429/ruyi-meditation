Fascicle 15 · 經律異相卷第十五(聲聞無學僧部第四) · Complete

Ānanda and Companions

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 15 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Arhat Śrāvaka Monks, Part 4" (聲聞無學僧部第四) — fourteen entries centered on Ānanda, alongside Upāli, Kātyāyana, Nanda, Pilindavatsa, Upananda, and Kālodāyin: Ānanda's own past life as the Buddha's close boyhood friend who literally dragged him to hear a buddha teach, his seven prophetic dreams of the Dharma's future decline, a caṇḍāla mother's binding spell broken only by a recited verse, and his own elaborate, lifelong testing of a devoted monk named Mountain-Facing before a final conversation with King Aśoka.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 15 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 15 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 15 continues the compendium's survey of named arhat disciples, opening with six shorter entries on Upāli, Kātyāyana, Nanda, Pilindavatsa, Upananda, and Kālodāyin before devoting its remaining eight entries entirely to Ānanda — the Buddha's own attendant and, in one striking past-life account, his childhood best friend. It includes the full text of Ānanda's seven prophetic dreams of the Dharma's eventual decline, among the most doctrinally significant passages in this whole anthology. Every entry's full text remains intact.

1. Upāli Shaves the Buddha's Hair

A boy shaving the Buddha's own hair grows so absorbed in his parents' constant, gentle correction of his posture and breathing that his own breath refines itself into the fourth dhyāna mid-task — and the Buddha simply lets someone else finish the job rather than wake him.

Chinese Source

佛在王舍城無敢為剃髮者。唯有一童子。名優波離。為佛剃髮。兒父母在佛前合掌白佛言。甚能剃髮但身太曲。父母教兒小直。又莫太直使出息不得麁大。又言。善能剃頭而身太直。父母語言。莫太直。佛言。善能剃髮。而入息太麁。父母語。莫麁入息令佛不安。又言。善能剃髮。而出息太麁。父母語。莫麁出息令佛不安。時優波離。入出息盡入第四禪。佛告阿難言。優波離已入第四禪。汝取其刀。阿難奉教。阿難持故盛髮器收世尊髮。佛言。不應以故器盛。時有瞿波離王子。將軍征討來索佛鬚髮。佛與。王得不知所安。佛言。安金塔銀塔寶塔雜寶塔中。繒綵鉢肆酖嵐婆衣頭羅衣裹。又云。不知何持。佛言。象馬車乘若輦若輿。若頭肩上擔。時王子持世尊髮去。所往征討得勝。時彼王子還國起佛髮塔。此是世尊在世時塔。後諸比丘行亦用前物盛持。佛言。不應頭戴。亦不應持塔往大小便處。比丘為恭敬故不敢共塔宿。置於別房。佛言。安高杙上或置頭邊(出四分律第四分第三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Rājagṛha, where no one dared to shave his own hair. Only a boy, Upāli, was willing to shave the Buddha's hair. The boy's own parents, standing before the Buddha with joined palms, said: he shaves very well, but his body leans too far forward. The parents told the boy to straighten himself slightly — but not so straight that his breathing grew too coarse. Again they said: he shaves very well, but now his body is too straight. The parents told him: not so straight. The Buddha said: he shaves very well, but his in-breath is too coarse. The parents told him: do not breathe in so coarsely, lest it disturb the Buddha. Again they said: he shaves very well, but his out-breath is too coarse. The parents told him: do not breathe out so coarsely, lest it disturb the Buddha. At this, Upāli's own in- and out-breath grew so refined that he entered the fourth dhyāna entirely.

The Buddha told Ānanda: Upāli has now entered the fourth dhyāna — take up the razor yourself. Ānanda, receiving this instruction, took an old container and gathered the World-Honored One's own hair into it. The Buddha said: it is not fitting to use an old container for this. At that time Prince Gopāla, setting out on a military campaign, asked for the Buddha's own hair and beard, and the Buddha gave them to him. The prince, not knowing where to keep them safely, was told by the Buddha: place them in a golden stūpa, a silver stūpa, a jeweled stūpa, or a stūpa of mixed treasures, wrapped in fine silk, bowl-cloth, or woolen and linen cloth. Still uncertain how to carry them, he was told: by elephant, horse, carriage, palanquin, or litter, or carried on the head or shoulder. The prince then took the World-Honored One's own hair with him, and, wherever he campaigned, was victorious. Returning to his own kingdom, the prince raised a stūpa for the Buddha's hair — this being a stūpa built during the Buddha's own lifetime. Monks thereafter also used such containers to carry relics. The Buddha said: one should not carry such a stūpa upon one's own head, nor bring it to a place of relieving oneself. Out of reverence, monks did not wish to sleep in the same room as the stūpa, and so kept it in a separate chamber. The Buddha said: it may be set upon a tall post, or placed near one's own pillow (Source: Dharmaguptaka Vinaya, fourth division, fascicle 3).

2. Kātyāyana Teaches an Old Woman to Sell Her Poverty

An old, worked-to-exhaustion servant woman, told she can literally "sell" her own poverty, learns that the sale consists only of an offered cup of water, a fast, and a night's quiet reflection on the Buddha — and dies that same night into a heavenly rebirth so improbable that even she does not at first know its cause.

Chinese Source

阿槃提國有一長者。多財饒富慳貪暴惡無慈心。時有一婢。晨夜走使不得寧處。小有違失便受鞭捶。衣不蔽形食不充軀年老困悴思死不得。時適持瓶詣河取水。思惟是苦舉聲大哭。時迦旃延來至其所。問言。老母何以。答言。尊者。我既年老恒執苦役。加復貧困衣食不充思死不得。迦旃延言。汝若貧者何不賣貧。女人言曰。貧那可賣誰當買貧。迦旃延言。貧實可賣如是至三。女言賣。迦旃延言。審欲賣者一隨我語。答言。唯諾。告言。汝先洗浴。洗浴已畢。告言。汝當布施。白言。尊者。我極貧困如今我身無毛許完納。唯有此瓶是大家許當以何施。即授鉢與。汝持此鉢取少淨水。如教取來奉迦旃延。迦旃延受尋為呪願。次教受齋。後教念佛種種功德。即問。汝有住止處不。答言無也。若其磨時即臥磨下。舂炊作使便臥是中。或時無作止宿糞堆。迦旃延言。汝好持心恭謹走使。莫生嫌恨。因伺大家一切臥竟。密開其戶。於戶曲內敷淨草坐。思惟觀佛莫生惡念。爾時老母奉而歸依如勅施行。於後夜中即便命終生忉利天。大家使人草索繫脚拽置寒林。有一天子與五百天人以為眷屬。福盡命終。此老母人即代其處。生天之法。其利根者自知來緣。其鈍根生者但知受樂。爾時此女既生天中。與五百天女。娛樂受樂不知生緣。時舍利弗在忉利天。知此天子生天因緣。問言。天子。汝因何福生天中耶。答言不知。時舍利弗借其道眼觀見故身。由迦旃延得生天上。即將五百天子來至寒林。散華燒香供養死屍光明照曜。大家見之怪其所由。告令遠近詣林觀看。見諸天子供養此屍。即問天曰。此婢醜穢。生存之時人猶惡見。何故諸天而加供養。彼時天子具說本末(出賢愚經第七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the kingdom of Avantī there lived a wealthy elder, stingy, cruel, and without compassion. He owned a servant woman who ran his errands day and night without rest, and who, at the slightest fault, was beaten with a whip — her clothing barely covering her body, her food never enough, so that, aged and worn out, she longed for death yet could not attain it. One day, carrying a jar to the river to fetch water, she reflected on her own suffering and wept aloud. The venerable Kātyāyana, passing by, asked her what troubled her, and she answered: venerable one, I am now old, worked without rest, and, worse, so poor that my food and clothing never suffice — I long for death yet cannot find it. Kātyāyana said: if you are poor, why not sell your own poverty? The woman answered: how can poverty be sold — who would ever buy it? Kātyāyana insisted three times that poverty could indeed be sold, and she finally agreed.

Kātyāyana told her: if you truly wish to sell it, follow my instructions exactly. She agreed. He told her first to bathe herself, and, once she had done so, told her she must now make an offering. She answered: venerable one, I am so utterly poor that I own not even a single thread to call my own — only this water jar, which belongs to my own master; what could I possibly give? He told her to take up the bowl and fetch a little clean water in it. She did so and offered it to Kātyāyana, who accepted it and pronounced a blessing over it, then taught her to keep a fast, and further taught her to reflect on the Buddha's own countless virtues. He then asked whether she had any place of her own to dwell. She answered she had none — when it was time to grind grain, she slept beneath the millstone; when pounding or cooking, she slept there instead; and, when there was no work at all, she sometimes slept upon a heap of refuse. Kātyāyana told her: keep your heart devoted and reverent in your own labor, and harbor no resentment — and, watching for the moment when the whole household had gone to sleep, he quietly opened her own door, spread clean grass in the corner of the doorway for her to sit upon, and had her reflect upon the Buddha, giving rise to no unwholesome thought. The old woman took refuge and practiced exactly as instructed.

In the latter part of that same night, her life ended, and she was reborn at once in Trāyastriṃśa heaven. Her master's own household, sending someone to bind her body's feet with straw rope and drag it to the charnel grove, knew nothing of this. In that heaven dwelt a certain deva-son, together with five hundred attendant devas of his own, whose own merit was then exhausted and his life ending — and this old woman took his very place there. In the way rebirth in heaven ordinarily works, one of sharp faculties knows at once the causes of their own arising there, while one of duller faculties knows only to enjoy the pleasures found there, without understanding how they came to be. This woman, now reborn in heaven with her own five hundred heavenly attendants, delighted in these pleasures without knowing their cause.

Śāriputra, then in Trāyastriṃśa heaven, perceived the karmic cause behind this deva-son's own rebirth, and asked: deva-son, through what merit were you reborn in heaven? She answered that she did not know. Śāriputra then lent her his own divine eye, that she might see her own former body — and she saw that it was through Kātyāyana's own teaching that she had attained this rebirth in heaven. Śāriputra then led five hundred devas to the charnel grove, where they scattered flowers, burned incense, and made offerings to the very corpse there, its own radiance shining brilliantly. Her former master, seeing this, was astonished, and summoned people from near and far to come and witness it — seeing these devas making offerings to a corpse. He asked the devas: this servant woman was filthy and wretched — even in life, people despised the sight of her — why, then, do you devas now make offerings to her? The deva-son then explained the whole matter from beginning to end (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 7).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Kātyāyana's own trick is that there is no trick at all — "selling one's poverty" turns out to mean nothing more than an offered cup of water, a fast, and an undisturbed night's reflection, the very things already available to someone with literally nothing else to give. What the story actually sells is not poverty itself but the old woman's own conviction that she had nothing worth offering — Kātyāyana's real intervention is simply insisting, three times over, that she did.

3. Nanda's Inner Shame

The courtesan Āmrapālī touches Nanda's foot as a simple greeting, and the involuntary reaction it triggers in him becomes, through the Buddha's own gentle handling, the exact humiliation that finally drives him to genuine solitary practice.

Chinese Source

佛在鞞舍離。時柰女嚴駕羽寶車詣如來所。親覲問訊下車詣佛。時難陀去佛不遠經行。柰女便至難陀所。以手接難陀足。便作是語。難陀。我是柰女。是時難陀即失精精污其手。柰女即以頂戴。往世尊所白言。世尊如此之人聽在眾次。佛告柰女。如是之人應在眾次。所以然者。難陀不久當成就無漏。柰女默然不對而去。柰女不遠。佛告阿難。諸有比丘盡集講堂。既已會竟。佛告比丘。我於比丘中悉觀察。皆當具舍勒以覆內形。如是諸比丘當作是學。佛告難陀。云何難陀。汝乃作如是形狀。難陀內愧。白言。善哉世尊。願速與我說法。使我聞法。在閑靜處如實觀察以自娛樂。佛告難陀。汝觀此身。隨其所行。從頭至足髮毛爪齒。若干不淨盈滿身中。如實觀察。為是淨耶為不淨耶。當觀彼漏。為何處所為從何來。是時難陀從佛受教禮足去。觀此身中從頭至足若干不淨。諸所有處皆悉知彼由合會生。我今當離。則無欲意。便說偈言。
婬我知汝本　欲從思想生
不我思想汝　則汝而不有
是時難陀。在一靜處閑居。成阿羅漢(出增一阿含經第三十九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Vaiśālī. The courtesan Āmrapālī, riding in a jeweled carriage adorned with feathers, came to pay her respects to the Tathāgata, descending from her carriage to approach him. At that time Nanda, walking in meditation not far from the Buddha, was approached by Āmrapālī, who reached out and touched his own foot with her hand, saying: "Nanda, I am Āmrapālī." At this, Nanda, startled, had an involuntary emission that soiled his own hand. Āmrapālī, holding this up on her own head in reverence, went to the World-Honored One and said: "World-Honored One, may such a man still be permitted his own place in the assembly?" The Buddha told Āmrapālī: such a man should indeed remain in his own place, for Nanda shall, before long, attain the state free of outflows. Āmrapālī, saying nothing further, departed.

Not long after Āmrapālī had gone, the Buddha told Ānanda to gather every monk into the lecture hall. Once assembled, the Buddha told the monks: I have observed you all closely, and each of you should wear an undergarment to properly cover the body — this is a practice all monks should now learn. The Buddha then said to Nanda: Nanda, why did you let yourself fall into such a state? Nanda, filled with inner shame, said: it is well, World-Honored One — please teach me the Dharma quickly, that, hearing it, I might dwell in quiet solitude and truly observe things as they are, finding my own delight there instead. The Buddha told Nanda: observe this very body, in whatever it does, from the crown of the head to the soles of the feet — the hair, the nails, the teeth, every manner of impurity filling the body throughout. Observe it truly: is it pure, or impure? Consider, too, where its own outflows arise, and from what source they come.

Nanda, receiving this teaching from the Buddha, bowed at his feet and departed, observing his own body from head to foot, finding impurity everywhere within it, and understanding that all of it arises only through the coming-together of conditions — resolving: I shall now separate myself from this, and desire itself will then have nothing left to hold to. He then spoke this verse:

"Desire, I now know your very root — you arise only from thought and imagination;
if I no longer think or imagine you, then you yourself will cease to exist at all."

Nanda then dwelt alone in a quiet place, and attained arhatship (Source: Ekottarika Āgama, fascicle 39).

4. Nanda's Thirty Marks

The Buddha's own half-brother, bearing thirty of his brother's own physical marks, is mistaken for the Buddha himself by monks who bow before him — and is told, in no uncertain terms, to stop dressing the part.

Chinese Source

佛始得道。身色光明相照大千。人民天龍十方菩薩。皆聽說法咸大歡喜。隨其本行皆各得道。佛弟難陀獨不從受。反戾佛教而欲為道。有三十相。將數弟子。著鉢真越衣顏似佛。有諸比丘未得道眼者。遙見難陀便為作禮。佛告難陀。自今以後不得復著真越衣著皂袈裟。所以者何。汝反我戒受比丘禮。當墮泥犁中(出十分譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

When the Buddha first attained the path, the radiance of his own body illuminated the whole trichiliocosm, and people, devas, nāgas, and bodhisattvas of the ten directions all came to hear him teach, rejoicing greatly and, each according to their own past conduct, attaining the path in turn. The Buddha's own younger brother, Nanda, alone did not receive this same teaching, but, defying the Buddha's own instruction, sought instead to practice the path on his own terms — for he too bore thirty of the Buddha's own thirty-two marks, and, leading a number of disciples of his own, wearing a bowl and robes of fine cloth, his own face resembling the Buddha's, was mistaken for him by monks who had not yet attained the eye of the path, who, seeing Nanda from a distance, bowed before him as though he were the Buddha himself.

The Buddha told Nanda: from this day forward, you are no longer to wear fine cloth robes, but must wear only the dark, dyed robe of an ordinary monk. And why is this? Because you have defied my own precept and accepted the reverence due a monk under false pretenses — for this you would otherwise fall into hell (Source: Sūtra of Similes and Parables, in ten fascicles, fascicle 4).

5. Pilindavatsa and the Herder's Daughter

A monk conjures jewelry and fine clothes for a weeping girl who cannot attend a festival, then, questioned by the king himself over such suspicious gold, turns the entire palace's own walls and furniture to gold on the spot to prove the point.

Chinese Source

畢陵伽婆蹉在王舍城。日時將至欲行乞食。至一放牧家食。其家女人啼。即問女言。何故啼耶。答言。闍梨。今節會日眾人集戲。我無衣裳獨不得去。時尊者即化作種種衣服珠寶瓔珞金銀校飾。與已便去。眾人見之問言那得。具說因緣。聞達國王。王即喚牛女及比丘來。問尊者。何處得此好金非世所有。比丘即捉杖打壁扣床一切化成黃金。作如是言。首陀羅何處得金此即是也。王言闍梨。有大神足宜各還去(出僧祇律第二十九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pilindavatsa dwelt in Rājagṛha. When the hour for begging arrived, he went to beg at a herder's household, where he found the family's own daughter weeping, and asked her why. She answered: "Venerable one, today is a festival day, and everyone has gathered for the celebration — but I have no fine clothes, and cannot go with them." The venerable one then conjured, on the spot, garments of every kind, pearls, jewels, and ornaments of gold and silver, and, having given them to her, departed.

The people there, seeing this, asked how she had come by such things, and, told the whole story, word reached the king. The king summoned the herder's daughter and the monk himself, and asked the venerable one where he had obtained such fine gold, unlike anything found in this world. The monk simply took up a staff and struck the wall, then the bed, and everything he touched turned to gold on the spot, saying: "Where indeed could a mere śūdra have obtained gold — this is where it truly comes from." The king said: "Venerable one, you possess such great supernormal power — you may all return home now" (Source: Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 29).

6. Upananda Divides Two Elders' Goods

Two elder monks, too afraid of doing it wrong to divide a season's worth of donated cloth between just the two of them, hand the problem to a passing monk who cheerfully declares the pile "already divided" through a technicality — then walks off with the greater share entirely.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in the kingdom of Kosala, spending the rains retreat there with a great many monks. The lay householders, seeing so large an assembly, built dwellings and provided robes for them. The following year, the Buddha returned instead to Jetavana for the retreat, leaving only two elder monks behind in that former place. The householders there, reflecting that they wished to continue their own giving just as the year before, so that the monks might receive robes and their own merit continue unbroken, brought a great quantity of robes and cloth to offer to the two elders — but, realizing the goods were far too many for just the two of them, and fearing what fault they might incur by dividing it up themselves, they dared not distribute it at all.

Upananda, traveling from place to place and observing how much had been offered everywhere, happened by; the two elders, seeing him from a distance, rose from their seats to greet him and asked after his own well-being. Upananda asked whether the monks in retreat there had received any offerings, and, told yes, asked whether it had already been divided. Told it had not, he asked why, and the elders answered: the goods are too many and we are too few — we feared what fault we might incur in dividing it ourselves. Upananda said: it is well that you have not yet divided it. The two elders asked whether he himself could divide it properly, and, told he could, said: this matter requires a formal proceeding — it cannot simply be divided outright.

The two elders then brought out all the cloth and set it before Upananda, who divided it into three portions, and told them: you two sit together on one side of the piles; I shall sit between the other two myself. He then said: elders, listen with one mind to this formal declaration — the pile before you two together I now call three; the two piles before me alone I likewise call three. Is this proceeding proper? They answered that it was. Upananda then began wrapping up the cloth as though to carry it all away — at which the two elders protested: this cloth belongs to all three of us, undivided — how can you simply take it and go? Upananda answered: if I am to divide it fairly with you, then the single finest robe among it all ought first to go to whoever best understands the Dharma, and only then should the rest be divided. They agreed — and Upananda set aside the single finest jeweled robe for himself, then divided the remaining cloth into two portions for the two elders. Upananda then bundled up the greater share of goods and carried it back to Jetavana.

The monks there, walking in meditation, saw Upananda approaching from a distance and said to one another: here comes a man without shame or conscience, given to greed beyond satisfaction, courting suspicion and blame wherever he goes. As he drew near, they asked him: Upananda, where did you get all this cloth? Upananda recounted the whole affair in full. Among them was a monk of few desires and easy contentment, given to ascetic practice, who, hearing this, was displeased and rebuked him severely: how can one who calls himself a monk seize goods from two elders in this way? — and reported the matter to the Buddha, who likewise rebuked him at length, telling the monks: it is not only in this present life that Upananda has seized goods from these same two elder monks.

Long ago, in a river's bend, there dwelt two otters, who caught a carp in the river but could not divide it between themselves, and so simply guarded it there together. A jackal, coming to drink, saw this and asked: uncle, what are you doing? The otters answered: nephew, we have caught this large fish but cannot divide it — can you divide it for us? The jackal agreed, saying that such a division required proper scriptural procedure, not simply splitting it at random. The jackal then divided the fish into three parts — the head as one portion, the tail as another, and the fatty middle section as the third — then asked: which of you prefers to stay near the shore? One answered that he did. Which of you prefers to swim in the deep water? The other answered that he did. The jackal then declared: listen with one mind — the scriptures say that the one who stays near the shore receives the tail, and the one who swims in deep water receives the head, while the fatty middle portion goes to the one who understands the proper procedure. The jackal then carried off the fish's own body in its mouth. Its mate, seeing this, asked where it had gotten such a fish, and the jackal answered: I found it between two fools who did not know how to settle a dispute themselves. Monks, know that those two otters are now these same two elder monks; that jackal is now Upananda himself (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, Good Recitation, fascicle 3; the same also in the Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Upananda's own maneuver works entirely through procedure rather than deceit — every word he speaks to the two elders is technically true, the formal declaration genuinely proper by its own logic, and yet the outcome is exactly the theft the two elders feared committing themselves by accident. The jackal fable, offered as this same trick's own prior life, makes the point explicit: a division "by the book" is not automatically a fair one, and the very appeal to proper procedure that reassured the two elders — and the two otters — is precisely what let someone else walk off with the better share.

7. Kālodāyin's Untimely Teaching

An arhat determined to make amends for his own disreputable past visits an errant daughter-in-law once too often to warn her against an affair — and pays for the well-meant visit with his own life, buried in a refuse heap by the very bandit she had been meeting.

The elder monk Kālodāyin, having attained arhatship, reflected that, while formerly among the group of six troublesome monks, he had brought disgrace upon many households throughout Śrāvastī, and now wished to return and set this right, converting them to purity. He entered the city and converted nine hundred ninety-nine households — for if a husband attained the path while his wife did not, or the wife attained it while the husband did not, this did not count toward his tally. In Śrāvastī there remained one brahmin household still destined to be delivered through hearing the Dharma, and Kālodāyin reflected: should I convert this household too, I shall have completed a full thousand households throughout the city.

Early one morning, he took his bowl and entered the city to beg, arriving at this brahmin household to find the master away and his wife alone inside, door shut, frying cakes. Kālodāyin entered meditative absorption and then snapped his fingers; the wife, turning to look, found the door still shut as before, and thought: how could this monk have entered at all? He must surely be here only for my cakes — I will not give him any. Should my eyes even fall out, I still will not give him any — and, through his own supernormal power, her eyes did indeed fall out. She thought further: even should my eyes come out the size of bowls, I still will not give him any — and, through his power, they became the size of bowls. She thought: even should he stand upside-down before me, I still will not give him any — and, through his power, he appeared upside-down before her. She thought: even should he die, I still will not give him any — and Kālodāyin, through his own power, entered the attainment of cessation of perception and feeling, his mind entirely still, aware of nothing.

The brahmin's wife, tugging at him, found he would not budge at all, and, greatly alarmed, reflected that this monk was known to frequent King Prasenajit's own palace, teacher to Queen Mallikā herself — should he be found dead in my own house, we would surely suffer for it; if he revives, I shall give him a single cake. Kālodāyin then rose from his own attainment. The wife looked at her cakes — the first, well-fried one she still begrudged giving him, and instead scraped the pan's edge to fry a smaller one, which, once cooked, turned out even better than the first, and she gave him that one instead — but the moment she lifted a single cake, all the others stuck to it as well. Kālodāyin said: sister, give me as much as your own heart wishes. She lifted up four cakes, intending to give him these — but Kālodāyin declined, saying: I have no need of these cakes; if you truly wish to give, you might instead offer them to the sangha at Jetavana.

This woman, having already planted good roots in a past life, reflected to herself: this monk truly does not covet my cakes at all — he came only out of compassion for me, to beg. She then took up the whole basket of cakes and went to Jetavana, offering them to the assembled sangha there. Once the offering was made, she sat before Kālodāyin, who, perceiving the karmic causes at work in her, taught her the wondrous Dharma, and, seated there, every defilement dissolved and she attained the pure Dharma-eye, taking refuge in the Three Jewels as a lay follower — and only then returned home.

When her husband later returned, she told him everything, saying: I have now attained the fruit of stream-entry — you should go to him yourself. The brahmin went at once to Kālodāyin, who taught him the wondrous Dharma as well, and he too attained the pure Dharma-eye and became a lay follower, thereafter exhausting his own wealth in support of his teacher — and, even after his own death, instructed his son to continue this same support exactly as before. The son obeyed his father's own wish faithfully.

Later, the son's own wife, weaving at her loom, happened to see a young and handsome bandit chief passing by, and called out to him, inviting him to join her in pleasure. At that time Kālodāyin, coming to this same household to eat, spoke to the brahmin's wife — for it was in fact the brahmin's own son's wife — warning her against the fault of lust and the sin of breaking the precepts. The woman, alarmed that he might have somehow learned of her own secret and would tell her husband, devised a scheme, feigning illness and inviting him in on that pretext. Kālodāyin stayed and taught her the Dharma at length, lingering in earnest conversation until the sun had already set. As Kālodāyin then rose and went toward the refuse heap outside, the bandit chief, waiting there, struck off his own head with a sharp knife and buried it in the very heap of refuse.

On the day of the precept recitation, when the tally sticks were counted, one monk was found missing, and, upon inquiry, the Buddha revealed: Kālodāyin has already entered nirvāṇa. As the night passed into dawn, the Buddha, together with the assembled sangha, entered Śrāvastī and went to the very refuse heap — and, through the Buddha's own spiritual power, the corpse rose up into the air of itself. The monks took it up and laid it upon a bier, carried it out of the city, cremated it, and raised a stūpa in his honor.

King Prasenajit, hearing that Kālodāyin had died at a certain brahmin household, at once wiped out that family across seven generations, seizing the property of ten neighboring households as well, and captured five hundred bandits, cutting off the hands and feet of every one and casting them into the moat surrounding Jetavana. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium's own closing line is deliberately narrow: not that Kālodāyin's teaching was wrong, or that warning against an affair was misguided, but specifically that entering a household "at an improper hour" — alone, at dusk, with a woman who had reason to fear exposure — created the exact conditions for what followed, regardless of his own good intent. Read against the fascicle's earlier entries celebrating monks who convert households through fearless, unconventional means, this story functions as a sober counterweight: sincerity and supernormal power protected Kālodāyin against a brahmin wife's own suspicion and a dying king's own dragons alike, but neither one protects against an ordinary, avoidable failure of timing and judgment.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha, wandering in Vārāṇasī, sat beneath a tree and smiled with delight, five-colored light streaming from him. Ānanda knelt and asked why. The Buddha said: long ago, in the time of the buddha Kāśyapa, there stood a monastery on this very spot, home to twenty thousand śramaṇas, where the buddha Kāśyapa constantly taught the true Dharma. Ānanda at once set out a rope-seat and knelt, urging: please, take this honored seat — this very ground's own merit has now brought two buddhas to it. Once the Buddha had taken his seat, he raised his hand and pointed, saying: over there lies a great district named Verañjā.

In that district there once lived a potter named Huānyù, "Delight," devoted and loving toward his own parents, who frequently visited the buddha Kāśyapa to receive his pure teaching. Though a potter by trade, he never once dug into the earth itself, for fear of harming the small creatures within it, taking instead only the loose soil from collapsed riverbanks and rat-burrowed ground to fashion his vessels, which he traded for grain — accepting whatever amount was offered without ever haggling over price — in order to support his own aged parents, who, weakened by age, had by then lost their sight entirely. Huānyù's own filial devotion and humaneness were unmatched by any of his peers.

One morning, the buddha Kāśyapa, gathering his robes and taking up his bowl, entered the city and came to Huānyù's own household, asking his parents: where is your filial son? They answered: the Buddha's own disciple has gone out briefly — but there is good rice and bean soup in the house. The Buddha took up the bowl, sat, ate his fill, and departed. When the son returned and saw the food diminished, he asked who had taken it, and his parents answered: the heaven of heavens himself came to visit you, and helped himself to the soup and rice before leaving. Filled with mingled grief and joy, Huānyù exclaimed: the Buddha, the Tathāgata, free of all attachment, truly and perfectly awakened, the guide of the path, teacher of devas and humans — the very kings and emperors among the devas themselves reverently prepare offerings for him, ever fearful of failing to satisfy the World-Honored One — yet I, dwelling in such lowly circumstances, with food so meager, have only been visited because he took pity on my own low station, and helped himself without waiting. Overcome with grief and joy, he bowed to the ground, reflecting on the Buddha's own vast and universal kindness reaching even him — and, in his own joy, forgot his hunger for fifteen days straight; his parents, sharing in that same joy, likewise forgot their hunger for seven days.

Over a month later, the Buddha came again to his household; the son was again away, and the Buddha again took the offered soup and rice before departing. When the son returned, his parents told him the same thing had happened again — and Huānyù and his parents once more rejoiced, forgetting their hunger for the same number of days as before.

At that time, heavy rains fell without pause day or night, and the monastery itself grew damaged and began to leak. The Buddha told the assembled śramaṇas: Huānyù has just built himself a new house — go and take tiles from its roof to repair the monastery. The monks went; the son was again not at home, and his father asked who was removing tiles from their own roof. The monks answered: the Buddha's own monastery is leaking, and we have been sent to take tiles from your roof to repair it. The father said: excellent — that my own son's virtue should reach even this far! and, rejoicing, declared: please take as many as you need — my own blessing is boundless. The monks had only just left when the son returned and asked who had been removing the roof tiles. His father told him the Buddha's own monastery had been leaking, and monks had come to take tiles to repair it. Huānyù, wherever he stood, bowed toward the Buddha, reflecting: the Buddha's own wisdom is boundless — kings and lords build him palaces of the seven treasures and offer them freely, yet he does not dwell in them, and instead takes these coarse tiles of mine, wishing only to bless me thereby. Overjoyed, he again forgot his hunger as before.

The Buddha, together with five hundred śramaṇas, then traveled onward into a certain kingdom, whose king, named Zhīwéi, came out to greet him personally. The king descended from his own carriage, set aside the five marks of royal ceremony, bowed, inquired after the Buddha's own well-being, and, once seated, listened to his teaching to its end, then said: heaven of heavens, I beg you and your monks to condescend to accept a humble meal from me. The Buddha silently consented. Once the meal was fully prepared, the king sent envoys to receive him, personally washing his own hands to serve the food, and, once the offering was complete, sat before the Buddha.

The Buddha told the king: you, O king, have long served the Three Jewels in past lives, and now receive the fruit of that same past merit — reborn from a woman's body into a man's, attaining the world's own highest rank. The very law of true kingship requires that a sage-king govern his own mind by the teaching of the sages, treating others as he treats himself and nurturing his own people — for wicked words are the very fire that burns down a kingdom; O king, be ever mindful of this. The king bowed and received this teaching, and further begged the Buddha to remain three more months, exhausting his own kingdom's treasures to provide jeweled beds, curtains, bedding, and medicine throughout — yet the Buddha did not consent.

The king, reflecting to himself, wondered who could possibly offer greater support than he himself now did — and the Buddha, perceiving this prideful thought, told him: there is one whose own giving surpasses yours, and whose merit is boundless. The king asked to hear this person's name, and the Buddha answered: in the district of Verañjā there lives a most filial son named Huānyù, who serves the Three Jewels and upholds my own clear teaching, treating others as himself and nurturing all beings equally, pure and true, never once handling even a single treasure or sharp blade of his own, selling only his own crafted vessels to support his parents, never haggling over price, ever patient and compassionate, his heart fixed always on the true path, his own joy found only in the sacred teachings — he has not even taken a wife, fearing this might make him proud before his own parents and thereby unfilial. Whenever he comes to hear me teach, he listens with his whole heart, and, describing his own parents' blindness with such grief that he weeps openly before me, unable even to properly see the Buddha himself. I took his own food, and removed tiles from his own roof, and he bore no resentment at all, but rejoiced so greatly that he forgot his hunger for fifteen days at a stretch. His utmost humaneness and filial devotion could not possibly be fully described — I myself must continue traveling and teaching throughout the world, and so cannot accept your own invitation to remain.

The king, displeased at first, was told by the Buddha: weighed against such virtue, his own pure and faithful filial conduct is simply without equal — nothing in his conduct or reflection has ever failed to be filial, his humane devotion to his own parents a standard beyond what you yourself could match. The king said: very well — Huānyù's own extreme filial piety, so praised by the Buddha himself, truly deserves such renown; I shall now send him support to help provide for his parents.

Once the buddha Kāśyapa had finished his own teaching there and continued on his travels, the king sent messengers with five hundred cartloads of rice, sesame oil, ghee, honey, and various treasures, delivered with the utmost humility and respect. The messenger said, on arrival: heaven of heavens, our great king, deeply moved by the worthy one's own filial piety and universal compassion, has sent me in reverence — please accept this tribute, to support your own parents and to make offerings to the Buddha as well. Huānyù answered: the great king's own kindness assists me greatly — please return to the palace and convey my own gratitude fully.

In the same district there lived a brahmacārin's son named Huājié, "Flower-Knot," who, since childhood, had been Huānyù's own closest friend, their bond deepened across many kalpas of shared cultivation, growing only stronger with time. Bathing together one day in a deep river, they saw a great tree in the distance, and Huānyù pointed and said: the Tathāgata Kāśyapa, a true sage, dwells there — we ought to pay our respects to him first; the Buddha's own path is not to be forgotten, and I myself dare hope for it. Huājié, moved, said: a buddha is rarely encountered in this world, rare as the udumbara flower, blooming perhaps once in many kalpas — how could we let such a chance pass? — and, taking hold of his friend's own robe, pulled him along to the Buddha together.

Huānyù bowed at once, but Huājié did not bow, merely exchanging a polite greeting before sitting. Huānyù said to the Buddha: this is Huājié, my own closest friend since childhood, still confused and unawakened — I beg you to dispel his own delusion. The Tathāgata Kāśyapa, responding to his own particular affliction, taught the Dharma accordingly, and Huājié's own heart opened at once, taking faithful refuge in the Three Jewels. The two of them, rejoicing, bowed together and withdrew.

On the road home, Huājié said: how fortunate the world is to have a buddha at all — yet our own household remains a filthy den regardless; why do you not become a monk yourself? Huānyù answered: my own parents are advanced in years, and, both now blind, depend on me alone for their very survival — I cannot leave the household for this reason. Huājié then declared: then I myself shall become a monk instead. Huānyù, learning of this, reported it fully to the Buddha, who at once conferred the precepts upon him. A hundred million years later, Huājié was reborn in the fourth heaven, Tuṣita, and, descending from that very heaven, in time attained buddhahood himself.

The Buddha told the monks: that Huājié of the story was myself; that Huānyù is now Ānanda. Ānanda has ever been my own good friend, pulling me by force to the Buddha to hear the Dharma, and thereby causing me to attain buddhahood myself — for the parable of a worthy friend is truly the very foundation of boundless blessing, sparing one, in this present life, from a king's own prison, and, at death, sealing shut the very gates of the three lower realms — ascension to heaven and attainment of the path alike are owed entirely to the help of such a friend (Source: Sūtra of Huānyù).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story's own final twist recasts everything that came before it: Huānyù's own extraordinary filial devotion, praised at length by two separate buddhas, turns out to matter for the plot mainly as the occasion that first brought his friend Huājié within reach of a buddha's own teaching — dragged there, in the story's own telling, quite literally by the arm. Ānanda's role across this entire compendium has consistently been the one who serves, remembers, and attends; this single entry inverts that role entirely, revealing that in a much earlier life it was Ānanda who did the pulling, and Śākyamuni himself who needed to be pulled. The friendship being praised here runs in both directions.

Five hundred arhats all declared: I am able. The Buddha said this would not do. Maudgalyāyana, perceiving that the Tathāgata's own heart was set on Ānanda, repeatedly urged Ānanda to accept — and Ānanda said: I am weak and unworthy of such service; but should the Buddha grant me three requests, I shall accept the sangha's own charge. Ānanda possesses eight qualities that make him capable of upholding the twelve divisions of scripture: first, firm roots of faith; second, a courageous, advancing mind; third, a body free of sickness; fourth, constant, diligent effort; fifth, a mind fully endowed with mindfulness; sixth, a mind free of arrogance; seventh, accomplished concentration; eighth, wisdom born from what he has heard (Source: Sūtra of the Bodhisattva's Descent from Tuṣita Heaven. 10. Ānanda's Seven Dreams

Ānanda wakes shaken by seven strange dreams — waters ablaze, a mountain balanced on his own head, a lion's corpse devoured by worms born from its own body — each of which the Buddha reveals to be a precise prophecy of exactly how his own teaching will eventually be corrupted, not by outside enemies but from within.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt at Jetavana. Ānanda, in another place, dreamed of seven things, and woke with a start, his own hair standing on end. Last night, he said, I dreamed of seven things: first, that every river, stream, and sea was ablaze with fire; second, that the sun was about to set, Jambudvīpa growing dark, and I saw my own self bearing Mount Sumeru upon my own head; third, that a monk lay writhing in a cesspit, while another man climbed upon his own head to escape onto clean ground; fourth, that a monk, his monastic robe incomplete, wearing only a knotted patch of it, held a torch in hand and delighted in wandering into crooked paths, tearing his own clothes on thorns as he went; fifth, that a great, flourishing sandalwood tree was defiled by pigs emerging from filth to rub themselves against it; sixth, that three classes of elephant calves butted, bit, and jostled a great elephant, trampling fine grass and muddying clear water, so that the great elephant, troubled, fled to a place of clean water and good grass instead — where the calves, playing there without any awareness of what they had done, remained where they were until the grass and water ran out, and, starved and desperate, gnawed on trees until they all died of hunger; and seventh, that a dead lion-king, named Kesin, its head grown white fur like a decorative garland, lay untouched by any bird or beast, none daring to approach it — until worms emerged from within its own body and devoured its flesh.

Because of these dreams, Ānanda went to the Buddha that very morning. The Buddha explained: to dream of the waters ablaze with fire means that future monks will violate the Buddha's own teaching — not through breaking the precepts to gain offerings, but through their own resulting disputes and strife. To dream of the sun about to set, and of bearing Mount Sumeru on one's own head, means that the World-Honored One himself has but ninety days left before entering final nirvāṇa, after which the assembled monks, devas, nāgas, and people will all come to receive the scriptural teaching from Ānanda's own hands.

To dream of a monk wearing his robe improperly, only a knotted patch of it, writhing in a cesspit, with another man climbing upon his own head to reach clean ground, means that, after the Buddha's own nirvāṇa, as the Dharma nears its end, there will be a great assembly of monks expounding the sūtras, at which time the Buddha's own profound teaching will go unpracticed, monks growing close to laypeople and chasing after wealth and desire; householders will admonish them, yet they will not heed such counsel — the monks themselves incurring misfortune while the householders instead gain merit.

To dream of a monk with a knotted patch of robe, torch in hand, delighting in crooked paths, tearing his clothes on thorns, means that, after the Buddha's own nirvāṇa, there will be monks without proper monastic robes at all, dressed instead as laypeople, wearing only a single knotted patch tied under the arm, abandoning the precepts to delight in worldly life, raising wives and children — rejoicing when given alms, and grieving when refused.

To dream of the flourishing sandalwood tree defiled by pigs rubbing against it means that, after the Buddha's own nirvāṇa, there will be monks who no longer uphold the Dharma, given instead to drunken confusion, eating without restraint — and when wise, well-intentioned people try gently to counsel them, they will respond only with slander, even reviling arhats.

To dream of the three classes of elephant calves butting the great elephant, trampling fine grass and muddying clear water, driving it away, only for the calves themselves to starve once the water and grass ran dry where they remained, means that, after the Buddha's own nirvāṇa, there will be senior monks well-versed in the scriptures who instruct the young in what brings misfortune and what brings merit — yet those who refuse to heed such teaching will, at death, fall into hell.

To dream of the dead lion-king Kesin, its head crowned with white fur like a garland, untouched by any bird or beast, its own body devoured only by worms born within it, means: while the Buddha dwells in this world, he expounds the Dharma widely, and after his own nirvāṇa, no outside heterodox teaching will ever be capable of destroying it — only his own disciples, from within, will bring about its ruin. These dreams of yours, then, are simply a foreshadowing of things yet to come (Source: Sūtra of the Seven Dreams and the Ten Virtues).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own interpretation returns, in every single image, to the same claim: nothing external will ever be capable of destroying this teaching — only its own inheritors, from within. The lion's corpse devoured by worms "born within it" is the entry's own most explicit statement of this idea, but the elephant calves, the monk in the cesspit, and the pigs rubbing against the sandalwood tree all repeat the same structure — decay arising from inside a thing that outside forces could never actually breach. Placed in a fascicle otherwise full of monks converting hostile outsiders through supernormal power, this entry is a deliberate reminder that the tradition's compilers saw its most serious danger as coming from precisely the opposite direction.

11. The Caṇḍāla Mother's Spell

A young caṇḍāla woman, smitten after washing Ānanda's feet with water he asked for, is bound to him by her mother's own elaborate incantation — a spell so powerful it draws Ānanda's own body across the city against his will, and is broken only by a verse the Buddha recites on his behalf from far away.

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda, traveling along a road, grew parched with thirst midway. A caṇḍāla girl named Prakṛti was drawing water there, and Ānanda approached to beg some. The girl told him: I am of the Mātaṅga caste. Ānanda answered: I am not asking about that — only give me water. She said: you are of a pure and accomplished lineage, the Śramaṇa Gautama's own foremost disciple, honored by King Prasenajit, teacher to Queen Mallikā — I am of the lowest caste, and dare not offer you water with my own hands. Ānanda repeated: I am not asking about that — only give me the water. She consented, and, scooping water, first poured it over Ānanda's own feet, then over his hands — and, in doing so, desire arose within her. Ānanda, having drunk, departed.

Prakṛti returned and told her mother: Mother, I wish to have the monk Ānanda as my own husband. Her mother answered: he is the son of a wheel-turning king's own lineage, a kṣatriya of the Śākya clan, honored by sages, revered by devas and humans alike — how could our own humble family ever produce him as a husband? The girl said: if I cannot have him, I shall drink poison, stab myself with a knife, or hang myself instead. Her mother said: the Mātaṅga spirits know powerful mantras, capable of pulling down the sun and moon themselves, or summoning even Indra and Brahmā to descend — how much less difficult, then, to obtain a mere śramaṇa? Whether Ānanda is already dead, or alive but incapable of desire, or under the Buddha's own protection, then indeed I cannot obtain him — but otherwise, he can surely be had. The girl then rose, bathed, and adorned her own body, dressing in white and preparing a bed, watching from a distance.

Her mother smeared the ground with cow dung, tied five-colored thread into knots, filled four jars and four bowls with water and gruel, planted four great knives upright in the dung with an arrow at each of the four corners, lit eight bright lamps, took four skulls of the dead, anointed them with various fragrances, scattered flowers upon the ground, and, holding a heated iron, burning incense, circled the arrangement three times, then knelt facing east and recited the Mātaṅga incantation.

At that moment, Ānanda, in the Jetavana grove, felt his own mind grow dazed and confused, bound by the spell as a fish or elephant is caught by a hook, and, drawn by its power, arrived at the caṇḍāla household. The mother told her daughter: Ānanda has arrived. The girl came forward and embraced him, sat him upon the bed, pulled at his own robes, and pressed against him — as a strong man might grip a small lamb by its own long fur in his hand. Ānanda saw the ten directions plunged into darkness, as though the sun and moon themselves had been swallowed by Rāhu. Though possessing the strength of ten great men himself, he found he could not move at all.

Ānanda, through the power of the noble truths of the path, brought his own mind back to clarity, reflecting: I am now in such distress — will the World-Honored One's own great compassion not take pity on me? The Buddha, knowing this at once, recited these verses on his behalf:

"The Buddha is the most exalted in all the world — truly, none can surpass what stands before the Buddha;
the Buddha is the most exalted among devas and humans — truly, the king of all dharmas, the field beyond compare.
The Dharma is the most exalted in all the world — truly, none can surpass what stands before the Dharma;
the Dharma is the most exalted among devas and humans — truly, that which forever severs every bond and brings all to rest.
The Sangha is the most exalted in all the world — truly, none can surpass wherever the Sangha stands;
the Sangha is the most exalted among devas and humans — truly, the field of merit finest and beyond compare."

Through the true power of these very words, Ānanda found release even within the caṇḍāla household. The moment the verse was complete, every implement of the spell within that house — knives, arrows, jars, bowls — shattered and broke; the lamps went out, the skulls burst apart, and a black wind arose, so that no one there could see another, and the Mātaṅga incantation lost all its power. The mother told her daughter: this must surely be the spiritual power of the Śramaṇa Gautama himself — everything has shattered and scattered, and the spell no longer works. Ānanda reflected: this is truly the power of the World-Honored One's own grace — and, released, was like a great elephant king in the prime of life at sixty years, wild and fierce, its body vast, its tusks long, breaking free of its own iron tether and fleeing the city for open, empty land. Just so did Ānanda, through the Buddha's own recited words, win his release from the caṇḍāla household and return toward Jetavana.

The girl, however, pursued Ānanda all the way to the very gate of Jetavana, calling out repeatedly: Ānanda is my husband! Ānanda is my husband! — following close behind him without pause. Ānanda reported the whole matter fully to the Buddha, who said: among all dharmas, I have seen nothing so bewitching as this — a woman bound entirely by her own lustful mind. The next morning, as Ānanda, robed and bowl in hand, entered Śrāvastī to beg, the woman again followed behind him, telling every elder she passed: Ānanda is my husband! Ānanda is my husband! Ānanda returned once more to the Buddha and reported this, and the Buddha told him: go and speak with her yourself, as an elder brother speaks to a younger sister — and why? Because this woman is destined to become a nun herself. The woman then said to the Buddha: I beg only that the World-Honored One return my own husband to me. The Buddha answered: if you truly wish to have Ānanda, become a nun within my own teaching, and he shall be given to you. The woman, overjoyed, agreed at once.

Her parents, likewise rejoicing — for they too had planted good roots in past lives, destined now to attain the path — went together with their daughter to the Buddha, who taught the Dharma extensively to them all, through countless skillful means revealing the meaning of every teaching: the meaning of gentleness, the meaning of giving, the meaning of moral discipline, the meaning of desire's impurity, the meaning of how craving only deepens the root of further bondage, the meaning of renunciation, and the various branches of the path — and further taught the four noble truths. The woman, seated there, understood the four noble truths on the spot; her parents attained the fruit of non-returning, and she herself attained the fruit of stream-entry — as easily as plain white cloth takes up dye. Her parents then took refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha, and were granted lay-follower status before the Buddha.

Ānanda, repenting his own foolish fault, asked to become a nun's own preceptor's charge himself — no, rather, asked to be permitted to guide her, that she might practice pure conduct under the World-Honored One's own guidance. The Buddha told Ānanda to take the two senior nuns, together with this woman, to Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī, that she might, on this woman's behalf, confer the going-forth and full precepts. Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī asked Ānanda: is it truly so, Ānanda, that the World-Honored One permits a caṇḍāla woman to go forth? Ānanda answered Gautamī that it was indeed so — and she was accordingly given the tonsure and the precepts, and, in time, attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of the Precepts' Causes and Conditions, fascicle 3; also found in the Sūtra of the Mātaṅga Woman).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story's own remedy for the girl's obsession is not a counter-spell but a formal reclassification: rather than exorcising her feelings or punishing her mother's own incantation further, the Buddha simply offers Ānanda to her on entirely different terms — she may indeed have him, as a fellow renunciant within the same sangha, if she gives up wanting him as a husband. The compendium seems more interested in what happens after the magic fails than in the magic itself: an infatuation intense enough to summon a Mātaṅga incantation capable of unmaking the sun and moon is, in the end, redirected rather than defeated outright, ending in her own ordination and arhatship rather than in any punishment for what she and her mother attempted.

12. The Cow and Calf's Own Gift

Sent to beg milk for the sick Buddha from a cow too dangerous to approach, Ānanda is helped by Śakra himself in disguise — and it is the milking calf, not the cow, who insists his own share go entirely to the Buddha, having spent sixteen kalpas as livestock for once doubting the Dharma.

Chinese Source

佛在舍衛國。時患中風。呼阿難。往婆羅門家乞牛乳。阿難即如言求索。婆羅門言。牛在彼間自往搆取。阿難即往到牛所。牛常喜觝踏人不可得近。阿難自念。我所事師法。不得自搆取牛乳也。忉利天王釋即來下。化作婆羅門被服住牛傍。阿難請搆取牛乳。婆羅門言諾。即以右手捫摸牛乳言。佛小中風。汝與我乳。令佛飲之。佛差者汝得福不可稱量。牛言。此手捫摸我乳何益快乎。取我兩乳。去置兩乳。遺我子。我子朝來未飲食也。犢子在母傍。聞說佛聲即語言。持我分盡用與佛。佛者天上天下人之大師也。甚難得值。我自食草飲水。我作人時坐隨惡知識。不信佛經。使我作牛馬十六劫。到今乃得聞佛聲。持我所飲乳。盡用與佛。滿器而去。令我後世智慧得佛道。阿難持乳還。佛問之。牛母子何說。阿難依事而答。佛言。牛母後彌勒佛時。與作沙門得阿羅漢道。犢子死後作人。當為我懸繒散華燒香。持經戒二十劫已後。當作阿羅漢。佛度脫天下萬民(出犢子經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Śrāvastī, suffering at that time from an internal wind ailment. He called Ānanda and sent him to a brahmin's household to beg cow's milk. Ānanda went as instructed, and the brahmin told him: the cow is over there — go and milk it yourself. Ānanda went to where the cow stood, but the cow, given to butting and kicking anyone who came near, could not be approached — and Ānanda reflected: it is not fitting, by the rule of the teacher I serve, for me to milk the cow myself.

At this, Śakra, lord of Trāyastriṃśa heaven, descended and transformed into a brahmin, standing beside the cow. Ānanda asked this brahmin to milk it for him, and he agreed, reaching out his right hand to stroke the cow's own udder, saying: the Buddha suffers slightly from a wind ailment — give me your milk, that the Buddha may drink it and be healed; the merit you gain from this will be beyond measure. The cow answered: what good does merely stroking my udder do — take my milk properly instead, from both sides, and set some aside also for my own calf, who has had nothing to eat since morning.

The calf, standing beside its mother and hearing the Buddha's own name spoken, said: give my own share entirely to the Buddha as well. The Buddha is the great teacher of all beings in heaven and on earth, exceedingly rare to encounter — as for myself, I merely eat grass and drink water; when I was once a human being, I fell into bad company and did not believe in the Buddha's own teaching, and, as a result, have been reborn as cattle and horses for sixteen kalpas — only now, at last, do I hear the Buddha's own name. Take, then, all the milk that would otherwise be mine, and give it entirely to the Buddha — that in a future life, I too might gain wisdom and attain the buddha's own path.

Ānanda carried the milk back. The Buddha asked what the cow and her calf had said, and Ānanda recounted it exactly. The Buddha said: this cow, in the time of the future buddha Maitreya, shall become a monk and attain arhatship. As for the calf, once it dies and is reborn as a human, it shall hang silk banners, scatter flowers, and burn incense in my own honor, uphold the scriptures and precepts for twenty kalpas, and thereafter attain arhatship, delivering countless beings throughout the world (Source: Sūtra of the Calf).

13. Ānanda Converts King Prasenajit to Giving

Ānanda talks the monks out of scattering to other lands during a famine year, and the king's own resulting three-month support of the sangha is traced back to a past-life brahmin beggar rewarded, against the counsel of frightened ministers, with a herd of red cattle.

Chinese Source

舍衛國歲飢。諸比丘各欲分散。以為歲節。阿難言。若諸比丘詣餘國而造歲節。此無數人失於德本。假使如來止此。舍衛多所安隱。波斯匿聞。請佛及僧三月供養。佛言。過去世時。波羅奈國王。名梵達有大威德。名稱遠聞。時國飢饉乞者眾多。王憙施與。四面雲集。天久不雨。穀米轉貴人民飢困。乞者日滋倉廩虛竭。大臣請息。王曰。若爾違我本心。何忍逆之。時諸明法吏宣告四遠。敢有乞者皆棄都市。乞者愁憂王問。大臣答曰。有此遠來得。梵志即入。王問。誰使汝來梵志答。腹使我來。時王愍以偈報曰。施赤犢牛千頭并諸犢子。梵志者阿難是(出生經第三卷腹使經大同小異)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Śrāvastī suffered a year of famine, and the monks each wished to disperse to other lands to observe the yearly retreat elsewhere. Ānanda said: should the monks all go to other kingdoms to hold their retreat, countless people here would lose the very root of virtue they might otherwise have gained — better that the Tathāgata remain here in Śrāvastī, where much good may yet come of it. King Prasenajit, hearing of this, invited the Buddha and the sangha to accept his own support for the full three months.

The Buddha said: long ago, the king of Vārāṇasī, named Brahmadatta, possessed of great and majestic virtue, his renown spreading far and wide, ruled during a time of famine, when beggars grew numerous. The king, delighting in giving, drew crowds from every direction, like gathering clouds — but heaven withheld its rain for so long that grain grew ever more costly and the people suffered hunger, while beggars only multiplied further, until the royal granaries themselves stood empty. His ministers begged him to stop the giving, but the king answered: to do so would betray my own original resolve — how could I bear to turn against it? The magistrates then proclaimed throughout the land that any further beggar would be executed in the marketplace.

The beggars, distressed, brought their case before the king, who asked his ministers about it, and was told that one beggar in particular had come from very far away. A brahmin was then admitted before the king, who asked who had sent him. The brahmin answered: my own hunger sent me. The king, moved to pity, answered in verse, giving him a thousand red calves along with their own young. That brahmin was Ānanda himself (Source: Jātaka Sūtra, fascicle 3; largely the same, with minor variations, in the Sūtra of the Belly's Own Errand).

14. Testing Mountain-Facing, Questioning Aśoka

Ānanda disguises himself, twice, to test a devoted monk's own resolve — first as three hundred beggar children who all sicken and die under his care, then as three scholars who whip and enslave him — before finally revealing himself, and turning to question a transformed King Aśoka on how much scripture he has actually mastered.

Chinese Source

阿難與兩比丘。到阿育王國。在山向中。比丘之松上。香華自至。鈴鳴如語。山向比丘。不知何故。出四面作禮。還齋堂中誦經。阿難與兩比丘。化作乞兒。復將三百乞兒往乞食。山向比丘作食飯訖。索衣便假衣之。三百乞兒不肯復去。乃朝夕供養乞兒悉病。山向比丘便朝夕燒香請福合藥。三百乞兒悉死。山向比丘便行假匃沐浴棺斂。阿育國王令人葬之。後數日。阿難。又與兩比丘化作三書生。衣被潔淨。往到松中。山向比丘下講堂迎之。設座令坐。三書生謂山向比丘言。汝事佛欲以何求。山向比丘言。佛道者神道也。天地之間唯佛道神。我事佛者。欲願佛道度人非人。無他所求。三書生言。佛道不神也。但當空虛故也。人少有事者。汝何愚癡。追人事佛。山向比丘言。諸君所言非法也。佛道最神非人所見。變化無常非人所知。百姓愚癡自不知之。空作此語益諸君罪。三書生便怒。汝事佛不逆人意。汝工追我作奴不。山向比丘言。諾。三書生云。汝不中善奴。當令汝擔屎。汝自當得百石糞。若不得百石糞者。當斬汝頭。山向比丘便行擔屎。不能得多還謝書生力極不能得多。乞原罪負。三書生大怒。便取縛之。痛鞭三百。忽然自去。阿難便化作優婆塞。往語山向比丘。三書生所為無道。不當語王令治之乎。山向比丘言。此自我過故致此耳。事佛者無所愛惜。求頭與頭求軀與軀。我但擔屎見鞭此有何苦。阿難與兩比丘。復作阿育王。往告山向比丘言。事佛定自無益也。空自勤苦。人生世間須臾當死。何獨勤苦奉是經乎。我今欲相與議事。從我者佳。不從我者。道人必死。山向比丘言。大王欲議何事耶。王言我有貴女。才操絕人。面目好美。手如蜂子。我貪道人為人溫良。欲以相與故來報意。道人必當相從。山向比丘言。知王厚意。我奉佛法已積年歲。功德未成反貪王女。恥辱佛道是罪不小。王若相殺自當受之。王便使人將山向比丘詣市斬之。山向比丘便禮佛而去。顧與王謝。乃是阿難。山向比丘便前作禮頭面著地。阿難起持之。使之還坐悉自相識。阿難謂山向比丘。汝功德已成。汝當得道。我故試汝。視汝志意耳。山向比丘下地言。我無知被受佛恩。得在法中不能作善。煩苦神人來相憂念。阿難以道授山向比丘。俱上樓上誦經說義。合樂歡喜。阿育王作禮長跪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda, together with two other monks, arrived in King Aśoka's own kingdom, at a monastery on a mountainside. A monk there, called Mountain-Facing, sat among the pines, where fragrant flowers came of themselves and bells rang out as though speaking. Not knowing the cause, Mountain-Facing bowed toward the four directions, then returned to the refectory hall to recite scripture. Ānanda and his two companions then transformed themselves into beggar children, and, bringing along three hundred more such beggar children, went to beg for food. Once Mountain-Facing had finished preparing the meal, they asked also for clothing, which he lent them — but the three hundred beggar children then refused to leave at all, and he provided for them morning and evening from then on. In time, all three hundred fell ill, and Mountain-Facing tended them morning and night, burning incense, praying for their recovery, and preparing medicine — yet all three hundred died regardless, and he himself begged for shrouds and coffins and bathed and buried each one. King Aśoka, hearing of this, sent people to help with the burials.

Some days later, Ānanda and his two companions transformed themselves instead into three scholars, dressed in clean, fine robes, and came again to the pine grove. Mountain-Facing descended from the lecture hall to welcome them, set out seats, and had them sit. The three scholars asked Mountain-Facing: in serving the Buddha, what is it you seek? Mountain-Facing answered: the Buddha's own path is the very path of the spirit — throughout heaven and earth, only the Buddha's path is truly divine. In serving the Buddha, I seek only that the Buddha's own path might deliver both humans and non-humans alike — I seek nothing else for myself. The three scholars said: the Buddha's own path is not truly divine at all — it is merely empty and void; few indeed are those who actually accomplish anything through it. How foolish you are, to pursue such a thing in service to the Buddha. Mountain-Facing answered: what you gentlemen say is not the true Dharma — the Buddha's own path is the most divine of all things, not something ordinary people can perceive; its transformations, boundless and constant, lie beyond what ordinary understanding can grasp. Common people, foolish and ignorant, simply do not understand it themselves — to speak so carelessly as you have only adds to your own fault.

The three scholars, angered, said: since you serve the Buddha without contradicting others' own wishes, are you willing to serve as our own servant instead? Mountain-Facing said: very well. They told him: you are unfit to be a good servant — we shall instead have you carry dung; you must gather a full hundred piculs of dung, or else we shall cut off your head. Mountain-Facing went at once to carry dung, but could not gather nearly so much, and, returning, apologized to the scholars that his own strength was simply insufficient, begging forgiveness for failing to meet their demand. The three scholars, furious, seized and bound him, and whipped him three hundred times — then suddenly vanished.

Ānanda then transformed himself into a lay Buddhist, and, approaching Mountain-Facing, said: what these three scholars did was utterly without principle — should you not report this to the king, that he might punish them? Mountain-Facing answered: this is simply my own fault, the natural consequence of it — one who truly serves the Buddha holds nothing back for himself, giving up his own head when a head is asked for, his own body when a body is asked for; carrying dung and being whipped for it is no true hardship at all by comparison.

Ānanda and his two companions then transformed themselves once more, this time into King Aśoka himself, and came again to tell Mountain-Facing: to serve the Buddha truly brings no benefit at all — it is only wasted toil. A person's own life in this world is fleeting, and death comes regardless — why toil so hard alone in upholding this scripture? I wish now to propose something to you: follow my own counsel, and all will be well; refuse me, and you, monk, shall surely die. Mountain-Facing asked: what matter does the great king wish to propose? The king said: I have a beloved daughter, of talent surpassing others, of fine and beautiful countenance, hands as delicate as a honeybee's — and, coveting your own gentle and kind nature, I wish to give her to you in marriage; this is why I have come to tell you my own intent — surely you, monk, will consent. Mountain-Facing answered: I understand the great king's own generous intent, yet, having devoted myself to the Buddha's own teaching for so many years now, my own merit still unaccomplished, to instead covet the king's own daughter would be a shameful betrayal of the Buddha's own path, no small transgression — should the king wish to kill me for refusing, I shall accept it as my own due.

The king then had his men take Mountain-Facing to the marketplace to be executed. Mountain-Facing bowed toward the Buddha and set out, turning to thank the king as he went — and, at that very moment, recognized that this king was in fact Ānanda himself. Mountain-Facing at once bowed before him, his head touching the ground. Ānanda raised him up and had him sit again, and each recognized the other fully. Ānanda told Mountain-Facing: your own merit is now accomplished — you shall surely attain the path. I tested you deliberately, only to observe your own resolve. Mountain-Facing, prostrate, said: in my own ignorance, I have received the Buddha's own grace, and, dwelling within the Dharma, have accomplished nothing worthy of it — yet you, a troubled spirit, still came to look after me with such concern. Ānanda then transmitted the path to Mountain-Facing, and the two of them ascended together to the upper story of the hall, reciting scripture and discussing its meaning, delighting together in music. King Aśoka himself then came and bowed, kneeling with joined palms.

阿難問王。佛經有幾卷。王答言。佛經甚多不可計數。今見在經有十二部。有八萬四千卷。阿難問王。王已持幾卷。王答言。我已誦三昧經萬二千卷。般若波羅蜜經萬二千卷。阿難問王。王見經已多。乃知天地相去幾里。王答言。大品三昧經言。天地相去八十一萬里。阿難問王。地有幾品。王答言。般若波羅蜜經言。地有八十一城。城自有人。北城中人長十丈。中城中人長八尺。南城中人長三尺。阿難問王。王自知本是何人。王答言。不知識遠但能知近。已甞入泥犁。已甞上天。已甞更勤苦。已甞作奴婢。已甞作六畜。已甞入火中。已甞作善人。從作善人。得為國王。已見勤苦之事。不敢復為惡也。阿難言。王本是提惒竭佛比丘。名提頭羅。山向比丘是釋迦文佛。比丘須拔者也。自從佛去後。我人孤窮持法不固。以致罪過。踰歷年數。不復相識。我昔與山向比丘。俱生小家我為兄山向比丘為弟。常自相憐出入相追。初不相識。我後當為國王。山向比丘來生我亦不識。天下反覆轉相寄生。不可稱數。今者自知得脫生死。便授阿育王羅漢之道(出阿難現變經)。

Ānanda asked the king: how many fascicles does the Buddha's own scripture comprise? The king answered: the Buddha's own scriptures are so vast they cannot be reckoned — at present there exist the twelve divisions, comprising eighty-four thousand fascicles in all. Ānanda asked the king how many fascicles he himself had already mastered. The king answered: I have already recited the Sūtra of Samādhi, twelve thousand fascicles, and the Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra, likewise twelve thousand fascicles. Ānanda asked the king, since he had already mastered so many scriptures, whether he knew how far heaven lies from earth. The king answered: the Great Chapter Samādhi Sūtra says that heaven and earth are separated by eight hundred ten thousand li. Ānanda asked how many regions the earth itself comprises. The king answered: the Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra says the earth has eighty-one cities, each inhabited by its own people — in the northern cities, people stand ten zhang tall; in the central cities, eight chi tall; in the southern cities, only three chi tall.

Ānanda asked the king whether he himself knew what he had originally been. The king answered: I do not know what is distant, only what is near — I know I have once entered hell, once ascended to heaven, once endured great hardship, once served as a slave, once been born among the six kinds of animals, once entered fire itself, and once been a virtuous person — and, from having once been virtuous, I came to be reborn as a king. Having witnessed such hardship myself, I no longer dare do evil.

Ānanda said: you, O king, were once, in a former life, a monk under the buddha Dīpaṃkara, named Pradyota; this Mountain-Facing monk here was, in that same life, the monk Subhadra. Since that buddha's own departure, we ourselves have wandered on, alone and impoverished, unable to hold firmly to the Dharma, and so incurred fault, across so many intervening years that we no longer recognized one another. I myself, together with Mountain-Facing, were once born together into a poor family, I as the elder brother, he as the younger — always caring for one another, coming and going together, yet never once, at that time, recognizing our own true natures. I was later to become a king; when Mountain-Facing was reborn as my own brother in that life, I did not recognize him either. Throughout the world, beings turn over and over, entrusted to one another's rebirth again and again, beyond all counting — but now, at last, each of us knows we are freed from birth and death. Ānanda then conferred upon King Aśoka the arhat's own path (Source: Sūtra of Ānanda's Manifested Transformations).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fascicle's own closing entry answers, almost point for point, the question its opening entries raised about what genuine devotion actually costs: Mountain-Facing loses three hundred children he had come to love, is enslaved and whipped, and is asked to betray his own vows for a king's daughter, and at no point does the story let him know that any of it is a test — his own answers throughout are given in the belief that the stakes are real. Ānanda's own closing dialogue with Aśoka then performs a final, quieter reversal: a king who can recite twenty-four thousand fascicles of scripture from memory is shown to know nothing at all of his own origins beyond the last few lives, while Mountain-Facing's own unrecorded, unglamorous constancy under trial is what Ānanda calls "merit already accomplished." Placed at the very end of a fascicle otherwise filled with dramatic conversions and displays of power, this entry's own verdict favors sustained, untested-looking faithfulness over any amount of memorized doctrine.
