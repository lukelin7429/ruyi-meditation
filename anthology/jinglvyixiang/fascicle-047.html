Beasts and Animals, Part 1

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 47 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Beasts and Animals, Part 1" (雜獸畜生部上) — thirty-seven entries arranged in fourteen sections by animal, from lions to rats. Among them: a lion king who climbs to a height to throw himself down rather than break faith with two monkeys; the eleven distinct purposes the tradition assigns to the lion's morning roar; a woodpecker that pulls a bone from a lion's throat on a promise and, when the promise is broken, takes one of his eyes and says so in verse; a cosmographical survey of four mountains in the four outer seas, twelve caves, and the twelve zodiac beasts who take turns teaching Jambudvīpa one day and night at a time; an ox that breaks its chain in the city gate because the Buddha stood surety for its debt and never paid; a dog that brings suit against the man who beat it and, asked what sentence to pass, asks that he be made a great householder; the doe caught in a snare who asks leave to show her fawns the water and grass and comes back to die, in the fascicle's longest and finest sequence of verse; five hundred monkeys who built a stūpa of mud in imitation, drowned, and came back from heaven to thank their own corpses; and the rat that woke a sleeping king twice and gave the Kalandaka Bamboo Grove its name.

Translation Method

Fascicle 47 opens the compendium's section on animals and, like fascicle 46 before it, is formally divided — here into fourteen sections by species, each with its own Chinese heading, which we keep. The distribution is uneven and revealing: seven entries on lions and one each on horses, jackals, wolves, hares, and wildcats.

The register runs from cosmography to fable to vinaya anecdote. Entry 7 is a systematic survey of the twelve zodiac animals as bodhisattvas on a teaching rota; entry 23 is a sustained narrative in verse; entries 19 and 21 are courtroom and household scenes from the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya and the Madhyama Āgama; and several are two or three sentences long. We translate each in its own register.

The fascicle contains three sets of near-doublets, flagged in the reading notes. Entries 28 and 34 are two versions of the self-immolating hare, filed under different animals and ending differently. Entries 29 and 31 both make a monkey rebirth the penalty for having compared a monk to a monkey. And entry 27 is the same story as fascicle 46's entry 18 with a wolf substituted for a hungry ghost, both cited from the same commentary.

On names: Śakra, Ānanda, Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, Rāhula, Yaśodharā, Devadatta, Jīvaka, Upagupta, Siddhārtha, Dīpaṃkara, the Buddha Kāśyapa, Brahmadatta, Balāha, Vārāṇasī, Śrāvastī, Rājagṛha, Vaiśālī, Kosala, Mathurā, Kaśmīra, Gṛdhrakūṭa, the Jetavana, Anāthapiṇḍada's park, Kalandaka, Lake Anavatapta, the Snow Mountains, the Trāyastriṃśa and Tuṣita heavens, and Jambudvīpa are given in their standard forms. Where a transliteration has no confident correspondence — Míngtuó, Shīzhì, Xūmàn, Jù the white dog, and the mountain Uruvilvā-manda as the Chinese gives it — we keep the Chinese in pinyin, with the compendium's own bracketed glosses preserved.

Six entries carry verse. We set these off in italics with slashes for the line breaks of the Chinese; the Chinese column keeps them running on within the paragraph, as the source has them.

Several entries are cruel — a beast flayed alive and eaten by insects while holding still, two lion cubs killed by a man wearing a monk's robe, a monkey burned and a palace destroyed with it, an infant killed in front of its mother. All are translated in full.

Lions 師子第一

Seven entries: a lion king who would die rather than break faith with two monkeys, the eleven purposes of the lion's roar, an ungrateful lion and the woodpecker who took his eye, a lion cub's skin used as a meditation mat, a jackal who saves a lion from a well, a double-tongued jackal torn in two, and a cosmographical account of the twelve beasts who take turns teaching Jambudvīpa a day at a time.

1. A Lion King Is Ready to Give Up His Life for Two Monkeys

A lion king who has undertaken to guard two monkey cubs falls asleep, and a vulture takes them. The bargaining that follows is conducted entirely in verse.

Chinese Source

過去世有一師子王。在深山窟。常作是念。我是一切獸中之王。力能視護一切諸獸。山中有二獼猴。共生二子。時二獼猴問師子王。王若能護一切獸者。我今二子以相委付。我欲餘行求覓飲食。時師子王即便許之。獼猴留寄二子即捨而行。是時山中有一鷲鳥王。厥名利見。師子王睡。即便摶取獼猴二子處嶮而住。師子王即寤。即白鷲言。我今固請　見為放之　莫令失信生我慚愧鷲王答曰。我能飛行空　無畏過汝界若欲護二子　為我應捨身師子又曰。我今護二子　捨身如枯草護身而妄語　云何得稱行說是偈已。即至高處欲捨其身。鷲王復曰。為他能捨身　則受無上樂施汝獼猴子　願王莫自害(出大集經第三卷)

Ru-Yi Translation

In a past age there was a lion king in a cave in the deep mountains who constantly thought: I am king among all the beasts, and I have the strength to watch over and protect them all.

In the mountain there were two monkeys who together bore two young; and the two monkeys asked the lion king: if the king can protect all the beasts, we now entrust our two young to you, for we wish to go elsewhere and look for food and drink. The lion king agreed at once, and the monkeys left their two young in his keeping and went off.

There was in that mountain a vulture king named Sharp-Sight; and while the lion king slept he snatched up the two monkey young and perched with them on a crag. The lion king woke and said to the vulture:

“I now firmly ask you / to let them go for my sake; / do not make me break faith / and give rise to my own shame.”

The vulture king answered:

“I can fly through the air, / and cross your borders without fear. / If you would protect the two young, / you should give up your body for me.”

The lion said again:

“If I now protect the two young, / to give up this body is like dry grass; / to protect the body and speak falsely — / how could that be called practice?”

Having spoken this verse he went to a high place meaning to give up his body. The vulture king said again:

“That you can give up your body for another / means you will receive unsurpassed joy. / I give you the monkey young — / may the king not harm himself.” (Source: Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra, fascicle 3)

2. The Lion King's Eleven Excellences

A physical description of the true lion, and the eleven distinct reasons the tradition assigns to his morning roar.

Chinese Source

師子王生住深。山大谷。方頰巨骨身肉肥滿。頭大眼長眉高而廣。口鼻淵方齒齊而利。吐赤白舌雙耳高上。脩脊細腰其腹不現。六牙長尾髦髮光潤。自知氣力牙爪鋒芒。四足據地安住巖穴振尾出聲。若有能具如是相者。當知真師子王。晨朝出穴嚬申欠呿。四向顧視發聲震吼。為十一事。一壞實非師子詐作師子。二自試身力。三令住處淨。四使子知處。五群輩無怖心。六睡者得覺。七諸獸不敢放逸。八諸獸得來依附。九調大香象。十告諸子息。十一莊嚴眷屬。凡聞師子吼。水住深潛。陸行藏穴。高飛墮落。厩中香象振鎖斷絕失糞怖走。猶如野干。雖學師子至百千年。終不能作師子之吼。若師子子生始三歲則能哮吼。香山徑有師子。飛鳥走獸絕跡不闚。一切畜生。師子為最(出涅槃經第二十五卷又大智論)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The lion king is born and dwells in deep mountains and great valleys; his jaws are square and his bones huge, his body and flesh full and fat, his head large and his eyes long, his brows high and broad, his mouth and nose deep and square, his teeth even and sharp; he puts out a tongue red and white, and his two ears are set high; his spine is long and his waist slender and his belly does not show; six fangs, a long tail, and glossy mane; he knows his own strength and the keen edge of his fangs and claws; his four feet grip the ground; he dwells at ease in a rock cave, and shakes his tail and makes his sound. If any can be complete in such marks as these, know that he is a true lion king.

In the early morning he comes out of the cave, stretches and yawns, looks round in the four directions, and sends out a shaking roar; and it is for eleven purposes: first, to break those who are truly not lions and pretend to be lions; second, to test his own strength; third, to make his dwelling place clean; fourth, to let his cubs know where he is; fifth, that his troop should have no fear; sixth, that the sleeping should wake; seventh, that no beast should be careless; eighth, that the beasts may come and take refuge with him; ninth, to subdue the great scented elephants; tenth, to tell his offspring; eleventh, to adorn his retinue.

Whenever the lion’s roar is heard, those in the water sink deep, those that walk on land hide in their holes, and those that fly high fall down; the scented elephants in their stalls shake and break their chains and lose their dung and run in terror. It is as with the jackal: though it should study the lion for a hundred thousand years it can never make the lion’s roar; whereas a lion cub, once it is three years old, can already roar. On the paths of the Fragrant Mountain there are lions, and flying birds and running beasts leave no track there and do not look in. Of all animals the lion is the foremost (Source: Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 25; also the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom).

3. A Lion Chokes to Death Eating an Elephant, and a Woodpecker Pulls Out the Bone and Revives Him, and He Then Forgets the Kindness

A woodpecker goes into a lion's throat to pull out a bone, on a promise of repayment. The verses exchanged when the promise is not kept are the best thing in the entry.

Chinese Source

佛告目連。勇智菩薩。昔光明佛時作師子王。吾為梵志修於淨行。時師子王晨朝跱立八處不動。奮迅身體便大雷吼。走獸伏住飛者墮落。然後乃起。曠野山澤案行屬界求覓群獸。逢一象王殺而食之。脾骨哽咽死而復蘇時告木雀。與吾挽骨。後若得食當相報恩。木雀聞之入口盡力拔骨乃得去之。時師子王後日求食大殺群獸。木雀在側少多求恩師子不報。佛告目連。時師子王。以此偈報木雀曰。吾為師子王　以殺為家業噉肉飲其血　以此為常饍汝既不自量　脫吾牙出難還得出吾口　此恩何可忘爾時木雀。復以此偈。報師子曰。我雖是小鳥　識恩不惜死但王不念恩　自負言誓重若能小寬弘　少多見惠者沒命終不恨　不敢有譏論時師子王竟不報恩。捨之而去。木雀自念。吾恩極重反見輕賤。今當追逐要伺子便。不報恩者終不行世。在在處處終不相離。時師子王復殺群獸恣意食之。飽便睡眠無所畏懼。時彼木雀飛趣師子。當立額上盡其力勢啄一眼壞。師子驚起左右顧視不見餘獸。唯見木雀獨在樹上。時師子王語木雀曰。汝今何為乃壞吾目。時彼木雀以偈報之。重恩不知報　乃復生害心今留汝一目　此恩何可忘汝雖獸中王　所行無反復從是各自休　莫復作緣對師子王者今勇智菩薩是。時木雀者今目揵連是(出菩薩瓔珞經第九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Maudgalyāyana: the bodhisattva Brave-Wisdom, long ago in the time of the Buddha Light, was a lion king, and I was a brahmin practicing pure conduct.

At that time the lion king would stand still in the early morning in eight postures without moving, then rouse his body and let out a great thundering roar, and the running beasts crouched still and the flying fell down; and only then would he set out. He went about the wild mountains and marshes inspecting his territory and looking for the herds, and met an elephant king and killed and ate him.

A rib-bone stuck in his throat and he died and revived again, and he said to a woodpecker: pull the bone out for me, and if I get food afterward I shall repay your kindness. The woodpecker heard him and went into his mouth and with all its strength pulled the bone and got it out.

Then some days later the lion king was hunting and killed a great many of the herd; and the woodpecker was beside him and asked more than once for the kindness owed, and the lion did not repay it. The Buddha told Maudgalyāyana: then the lion king answered the woodpecker with this verse:

“I am the lion king, / and killing is my family trade; / I eat flesh and drink blood, / and take this for my ordinary fare. / You did not measure yourself, / and escaped my teeth with difficulty. / That you got out of my mouth again — / how could you forget that kindness?”

Then the woodpecker answered the lion with this verse:

“Though I am a small bird, / I know kindness and do not grudge my death. / But the king does not remember kindness / and goes back on his own weighty oath. / If you could be a little generous / and grant me even a little, / I would die without resentment / and dare make no complaint.”

Then the lion king in the end did not repay the kindness, and left him and went away. The woodpecker thought to itself: my kindness was of the greatest weight and I am despised for it. I shall follow him now and watch for my chance; one who does not repay kindness shall not walk in the world. Wherever he goes I shall never leave him.

Then the lion king again killed the herd and ate as he pleased, and, being full, slept without fear; and then the woodpecker flew at the lion and stood on his forehead and with all its force pecked out one eye. The lion started up and looked round to left and right and saw no other beast, and only the woodpecker alone in a tree.

Then the lion king said to the woodpecker: why have you now destroyed my eye? And the woodpecker answered him in verse:

“Not knowing how to repay a heavy kindness, / you gave rise besides to a harming mind. / I now leave you one eye — / how could you forget that kindness? / Though you are king among beasts, / what you do has no reciprocity. / From this let us each be done; / let us make no further bond.”

The lion king of that time is the bodhisattva Brave-Wisdom now; the woodpecker of that time is Maudgalyāyana (Source: Sūtra of the Bodhisattva’s Necklace, fascicle 9).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two verses the woodpecker and the lion exchange are the argument of the entry and are worth reading against each other. The lion's is a claim of nature over obligation — I am the lion king and killing is my trade, and you were fortunate to get out of my mouth at all. The woodpecker's is a claim of proportion — though I am a small bird I know kindness and do not grudge my death; it is you who go back on a weighty oath.

What the woodpecker does about it is unusual for this genre. It does not appeal, curse, or die of grief; it decides that one who does not repay kindness shall not walk in the world, follows the lion until it is asleep and full, and takes one eye. And its closing verse is careful to say that it left the other: I now leave you one eye — how could you forget that kindness?

The identifications place the Buddha in neither role. The lion is the bodhisattva Brave-Wisdom, and the woodpecker is Maudgalyāyana.

4. A Lioness's Two Cubs Are Killed by a Hunter and Are Born Together in an Elder's House and Attain the Path

A hunter puts on a monk's kāṣāya to get close to two lion cubs. What their guardians do with the skin, and what they find when they meditate on it years later.

Chinese Source

昔者山中有兩比丘。閑居行道逮得神通。去之不遠有一師子。產生二子養之稍大。欲行所索。持子寄二道人。窟邊求食。或五日一還見。與道人相近附。遂復捨行。日月轉久。後日道人各行不在。獵師遇之意欲射之。狩迸入林。獵師意念。此數與道人相依附。吾作道人被服。爾乃得之。巖窟中有留袈裟法衣。獵師著往師子所。師子謂是道人。喜共赴之。獵師打殺剝取其皮作裘賣之。道人行還不見師子子求之不得。定意觀之知為獵客所殺。便以神足追而奪之。以為坐褥恒摩娑呪願欲令解脫。未久復坐禪。觀知趣何道。而為中國大長者家作雙生子。始入胞胎其母未覺。道人問。長者何所渴乏。曰吾家大富唯乏兒子。道人語之。吾能使有兒。長者大喜爾為蒙恩。道人語曰。若必得子何以報恩。曰長大便當給道人為弟子。道人呪願而去。從是遂覺有身。後生二男相似如一。年轉八歲復來到其家。兒有宿緣自然愛敬。道人語長者。識昔約不。長者以本誓不得已。便以二兒各施道人。道人將作沙彌。精進未久亦皆得道。亦恒自坐皮上試共坐禪。觀此皮神所生。便知是己身故皮。展轉相照便共至師前。禮足謝曰。實蒙大恩(出譬喻經第五卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago in the mountains there were two monks who dwelt in seclusion practicing the path and had attained the supernormal powers. Not far from them there was a lioness who bore two cubs and raised them until they were somewhat grown; and when she wished to go and forage she left the cubs in the keeping of the two religious and looked for food near their cave, coming back once every five days or so to see them. And the cubs grew close to the religious, and she left them again and went off; and so it went on for months and years.

On a later day the religious were each away and not there, and a hunter came upon the cubs and meant to shoot them, and the beasts ran off into the wood. The hunter thought: these keep close company with the religious; if I put on a religious man’s dress I shall get them. In the cave there were kāṣāya Dharma robes left behind, and the hunter put them on and went to where the cubs were; and the cubs, supposing he was the religious, ran gladly to meet him, and the hunter beat them to death and stripped off their skins and made a fur robe of them and sold it.

The religious came back from their journey and did not see the lion cubs, and looked for them and could not find them; and, settling their minds and contemplating, they knew they had been killed by a hunter, and by their supernormal power pursued him and took the skin back. They used it as a sitting-mat, and constantly stroked it and made prayers over it, wishing them to be delivered.

Not long afterward, again sitting in meditation, they contemplated to know what destiny they had gone to; and they had become twin sons in the house of a great elder of the central kingdom. When they had just entered the womb their mother was not yet aware of it, and the religious asked: elder, what do you lack? He said: my household is very rich and I lack only sons. The religious said to him: I can make you have sons. The elder was greatly pleased: then I shall be in your debt. The religious said: if you do get sons, how will you repay the kindness? He said: when they are grown I shall give them to you as disciples.

The religious made his prayer and went away; and from then on she knew she was with child, and afterward bore two boys as like as one another. When they were eight years old he came again to that house, and the boys, having a connection from before, naturally loved and revered him. The religious said to the elder: do you remember the old agreement? The elder, bound by his own oath, could not help it, and gave each of the two boys to the religious.

The religious made them novices, and, applying themselves, before long they too attained the path; and they too constantly sat on that skin, and, sitting in meditation together, contemplated where the spirit of this skin had been born — and knew it was the old skin of their own bodies. Understanding one another in turn, they came together before their teacher and bowed at his feet and thanked him: we have truly received a great kindness (Source: Parables Sūtra, fascicle 5).

Ru-Yi Translation

In a past age at the foot of the Snow Mountains there were two beasts, the first a lion named Fine-Mane and the second a tiger named Fine-Fang, who were good friends together and would close their eyes and lick one another; and these two beasts constantly got soft, good meat to eat.

Not far from there was a double-tongued jackal who thought: I shall go to the two beasts and say, let me be a third companion to you; will you let me join you? The lion and the tiger said: as you please. And the double-tongued jackal ate the meat the two beasts left and his body grew big and fat.

He thought: this lion Fine-Mane and this tiger Fine-Fang are good friends together and love one another and close their eyes and lick one another, and they constantly eat good meat; and if some time they do not get it they will certainly eat me. I had better use means first to set their hearts apart.

He said to the lion: the tiger has an evil mind toward you, lion; he says that everything you eat is got by his strength. The lion said: how am I to know it? The double-tongued jackal answered: tomorrow, when he sees you and closes his eyes and licks your mane, know that as the sign of ill will.

And he went and said to the tiger: the lion has an evil mind toward you and says that everything he eats is got by his own strength. The tiger said: how am I to know it? He answered: tomorrow, when he sees you and closes his eyes and licks your fur, know that as the sign of ill will.

Between these two friends the tiger conceived fear, and so he went first to the lion and said: have you given rise to an evil mind toward me? The lion said: who said this? He answered: the double-tongued jackal.

Fine-Mane asked in turn: and have you given rise to an evil mind toward me? The tiger said: no. And the tiger said to the lion: if you have said such an evil thing, we cannot be good friends together. Fine-Mane said: it is this double-tongued jackal who said such things. Why should we not be glad to live together? And he spoke this verse:

“If you believe this evil creature, / you will quickly part and go your ways, / and will carry that grief within you, / and anger and resentment will not leave your mind. / One who is a good friend / does not make a difficulty of another’s words. / To one who does not believe, whoever would divide them — / and if you believe another and part, / then you are eaten by him. / To those who do not believe the double-tongued, / harmony comes back together again, / and what is in the heart is spoken face to face, / the mind pure and the words gentle. / So should good friends be made, / harmonious as water and milk. / This wretched little creature here — / from birth its nature is evil, / one head and two tongues.”

The tiger and the lion, having verified the matter, took the jackal together and tore him in two (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, second recitation, third division; also the fourth, ninth division, the text being the same; also Mahīśāsaka Vinaya, fascicle 6; also the Sūtra of the Double-Tongued Jackal, roughly the same).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A study in how a lie is made to work. The jackal does not simply slander; it tells each animal that the other says the same specific thing — everything he eats is got by my strength — and then supplies each with the same falsifiable proof: tomorrow, when he closes his eyes and licks you, know that as the sign of ill will. The proof is a thing the two friends have always done.

What defeats it is equally specific. The tiger, being afraid, goes and asks the lion directly, and the lion's first question is who said this. The long verse that follows sets out the general rule — one who is a good friend does not make a difficulty of another's words — and ends with the observation that gives the entry its title: one head and two tongues.

The compendium lists four sources for this story, one of which is a whole sūtra devoted to it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

An unexpected piece of cosmography and the most systematic thing in the fascicle. Four mountains stand in the four seas outside Jambudvīpa — lapis in the east, crystal in the south, silver in the west, gold in the north — each with three caves that a bodhisattva formerly inhabited, and in each cave one animal cultivating the loving-kindness of the hearers' vehicle, with a spirit and a rākṣasī and their retinues of five hundred making offerings to them.

The twelve animals are the twelve of the East Asian zodiac — snake, horse, sheep; monkey, cock, dog; pig, rat, ox; lion, hare, dragon — with a lion where the tiger usually stands. And the rota given is a zodiac in operation: on the first day of the seventh month the rat goes out and teaches all beings in rat bodies, and so on in turn, round the twelve days and then the twelve months and then the twelve years.

The closing line is the point the whole survey is built toward, and it is a claim about the difficulty of the work rather than about the animals: to subdue beings in the form of a god or a human is no hard thing; to subdue beings in the form of an animal — that is what is hard.

In an ancient age there was an elephant living in seclusion who bore a calf; and not long after it had touched the ground its mother died. Not far from there was the dwelling of a sage who had majestic power, complete in merit, and whose intent held great compassion; and, seeing from afar the elephant calf whose mother had died and who could barely lift its feet and wandered east and west and could not keep itself alive, he took it home to where he lived and fed it on water and fruit.

That elephant calf was gentle and good and complete in fine merit and delighted in what was right; and being at ease it had no trouble. Then the sage lay down and rose in the same place with it, and its body grew and its coat became glossy; and it took water and drink and provided for the sage, and gave him the good fruits and melons and ate afterward itself. The sage in compassion observed its virtuous conduct and loved it as a son and never tired of looking at it.

Then Śakra, lord of the gods, conceived this thought: this sage’s intent rests on the elephant calf and his fondness never tires; had I not better set them apart and make him grieve? And Śakra manifested a test, and made the elephant calf suddenly die on the ground with its blood flowing out.

The sage, seeing the elephant calf dead, grieved and wept; and other sages heard and came and reasoned with him and could not remove his grief. Then Śakra, lord of the gods, standing in the open sky, spoke a verse for the sage; and then Śakra brought the elephant calf back to life, and the sage was greatly delighted.

The sage is my preceptor; the elephant calf that died is his disciple; and Śakra, lord of the gods, is myself (Source: Sūtra Spoken by the Buddha on the Death of a Disciple).

11. The Elephant, the Monkey, and the Francolin Show Respect to One Another

Three friends under one nyagrodha tree settle their order of precedence by asking one another how far back they remember the tree.

Ru-Yi Translation

In a past age there were three friends, an elephant, a monkey, and a francolin (the Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 27, now writes it diāntuō bird), living together in dependence on one nyagrodha tree; and they said to one another: since we depend together on this tree we should show respect to one another.

The monkey and the francolin together asked the elephant: how far back do you remember? The elephant said: I remember that when I was small and walked over this tree it came level with my belly.

The elephant and the francolin asked the monkey, and the monkey answered: I remember that when I was small I could reach the top of this tree with my raised hand (the Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya says: I urinated on the top of this tree). The elephant said to the monkey: you are older in years than I.

The elephant and the monkey together asked the francolin, and the francolin said: I remember that on the right side of the Snow Mountains there was a great nyagrodha tree; I ate its fruit and came here and passed it, and this tree grew from that.

They said to one another: the francolin is oldest in years. And the elephant set the monkey on his head, and the monkey set the francolin on his shoulder, and they travelled together among people, from village to village and town to town, constantly speaking this verse:

“If a person can hold the Dharma in mind, / he will surely respect all elders; / in this present life he has good repute, / and in the life to come is born in a good destiny.”

When the francolin spoke this Dharma the people all followed it and the teaching of the Dharma spread abroad. [The Buddha said:] you who have gone forth in my Dharma should show respect to one another. So the Buddha-Dharma spreads abroad; and from now on I permit that you show respect according to seniority, bowing, going out to meet, and inquiring after one another. Then the monks heard the Buddha’s instruction, and the monks in order of seniority showed respect and bowed to one another (Source: Dharmaguptaka Vinaya, fourth division, fascicle 3; also Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, Seven Dharmas, fascicle 6).

13. A Great Ox Being Sold Runs to the Tathāgata; the Buddha Tells the Former Cause, and It Dies and Is Born in Heaven

An ox breaks its chain in the city gate and speaks; and the Buddha explains that he himself stood surety for the debt and never paid it.

Ru-Yi Translation

A man from a distant place brought a great ox, fat and strong, and sold it to a man of the city of Śrāvastī; and the city man bought it meaning to kill it. In the city gate they met the Buddha, and the ox saw the Buddha from afar and its heart was full of grief and joy, and it broke its chain and ran, and no one could restrain it; and it went straight to the Tathāgata and bent its two front legs and lowed sorrowfully with tears coming out, and said with its own mouth: a great sage is hard to meet with and comes once in a hundred million ages. I ask you to send down your great compassion and look on me once and save me.

The Buddha said: it may well be done. In a far distant age there was a wheel-turning king, king of the four continents, with a thousand sons and the seven treasures, who governed by just law and whose people were at peace, and who had four virtues besides: he looked on the people as his children and the people served him as a father; and śramaṇas, brahmins, elders, and common people never fell ill; and his virtue was proclaimed in the four regions and reached through the ten directions.

The king went out on a progress through the four quarters and was returning toward his palace, and met an old acquaintance who had been seized by a creditor and was said to owe fifty taels of gold, and was tied to a tree. The king’s seven treasures and his retinue stopped and would not go on, and he wondered why; and it was reported to him.

The king said: release him and let him go, and I shall repay you double, a hundred taels. So he was released and went home; and the man came again and again to the gate of the king’s palace and sought the gold and did not get it; and the creditor had gone away and it was not known where. And so through birth and death, going round and coming back over countless kalpas, he never repaid what was owed, down to this present life, in which he has fallen among oxen, and the creditor has sold him for several thousand taels of gold.

The wheel-turning king of that time is myself; the creditor is this ox. The Buddha, as a sage-king, guaranteed the repayment and in the end did not give it; and so he has come now to ask for help.

The Buddha told the ox’s owner: I shall beg alms for you and repay double the price of the ox. The ox’s owner would not, and still wanted the ox. The Buddha told him again: I shall weigh the ox’s body and give you so much gold by weight. And still he would not give it up. Then Śakra and Brahmā came down together and said to the Buddha: we shall bring the ten million taels.

The ox was taken to the Jetavana, and after seven days its life ended and it was at once born in heaven; and it soon remembered and knew and thought on the Buddha’s merit, and came back to the human world and scattered flowers in offering and repaid the Buddha’s kindness. The Buddha taught the sūtra for him and he at once stood on the ground of non-retrogression and obtained the acceptance of the non-arising of dharmas, and returned to heaven (Source: Jātaka Sūtra, fascicle 4).

14. A Buffalo King Bears a Monkey's Insults

A monkey pelts three successive parties of buffalo, and the first buffalo king's answer to the tree-spirit's question is a prediction.

Chinese Source

過去世有異曠野。水牛王頓止其中。遊行食草而飲泉水。時水牛王與眾眷屬。有所至湊獨在其前。顏貌姝好威神巍巍名德超異忍辱和雅行止安詳。有一獼猴住在道邊。見水牛王與眷屬俱。心懷忿怒興于嫉妬。便即揚塵瓦石。而坌擲之輕慢毀辱。水牛默然受之不報。行過未久。更有一部水牛王尋從後來。獼猴見之亦復罵詈揚塵打擲。後一部眾。見前牛王默然不校效之。忍辱不以為恨。是等眷屬過去。未久有一水牛犢。尋從後來。隨逐群牛。於是獼猴逐之罵詈毀辱輕易。水犢懷恨不喜。見前等類忍辱不恨。亦復學效。去道不遠大叢樹間。時有樹神遊居其中。問水牛王。卿等何故覩此獼猴猥見罵詈。而反忍辱默聲不應。水牛報曰。彼輕辱毀我　又當加施人彼人當加報　爾乃得抂患諸水牛過去。未久有諸梵志。大眾群輩仙人之等從道而來。時彼獼猴亦復毀辱。諸梵志等。即時捕捉脚蹋殺之。於是樹神即復頌曰。罪惡不腐朽　殃熟乃遭患罪惡今已滿　諸殃不爛壞佛言。水牛王即我是。為菩薩時墮罪為水牛中王。常行忍辱修四等心。慈悲喜護自致得佛(出生經第四分)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In a past age, in a certain wilderness, a buffalo king stopped and dwelt, going about eating grass and drinking from the springs. Then the buffalo king, with his herd and retinue, going wherever they went, was alone in front; his face and appearance were fine, his majesty lofty, his name and virtue outstanding, patient and harmonious, his going and stopping composed.

A monkey lived beside the road, and, seeing the buffalo king with his retinue, his heart was full of rage and jealousy arose in him, and he threw up dust and tiles and stones and pelted them and slighted and insulted them; and the buffalo received it in silence and did not answer.

When they had passed on not long, another company of buffalo came following behind with their king, and the monkey, seeing them, likewise reviled them and threw up dust and pelted them. And this second company, seeing that the first buffalo king had been silent and had not contended, did the same, and were patient and did not resent it.

When this retinue had passed on not long, a buffalo calf came following behind after the herd, and the monkey pursued and reviled and insulted and slighted it. The calf harboured resentment and was displeased; but seeing that those before it had been patient and unresentful, it too did as they had done.

Not far from the road there was a great thicket of trees in which a tree-spirit dwelt, and it asked the buffalo king: why do you, seeing this monkey and being coarsely reviled, on the contrary bear it patiently and keep silence and not answer? The buffalo answered:

“He slights and insults and reviles me, / and will do the same to others besides; / those others will repay him for it, / and then he will meet with disaster.”

When the buffalo had passed on not long, there came along the road a great company of brahmins, sages and the like; and then that monkey reviled and insulted them too, and the brahmins seized him at once and trampled him to death. Then the tree-spirit spoke a verse again:

“Evil deeds do not rot away; / when the calamity ripens one meets the trouble. / The evil deed is now full, / and the calamities do not decay.”

The Buddha said: the buffalo king is myself. Being a bodhisattva, and having fallen through an offense into the state of a buffalo king, I constantly practiced patience and cultivated the four states of mind — loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity — and so came of myself to buddhahood (Source: Jātaka Sūtra, fourth division).

15. Two Oxen Contest Their Strength in Pulling a Load

An ox that cannot pull a load when it is insulted, and can when it is praised. It says so itself.

Chinese Source

過去有人有一黑牛。復一牛主為財物故唱言。誰牛力勝我牛者。若勝我輸物。若不如者輸我物。時黑牛主答言可爾。時載重物繫牛車左。共相輕笑謂。黑曲角。以杖擊之牽是車去。牛聞之即失色力不能挽重上坂。時黑牛主大輸財物。是得物人後復更唱令。黑牛聞聲即語其主。可答言爾。主言不能。所以然者。汝弊黑牛大輸我物。今復作者輸我物盡牛語主言。先在眾前形相輕我。聞惡名故即失色力。是故不能挽重上坂。今授主語莫出惡言。在他前時便語我言。汝是好黑大牛生來良吉角廣且直。主受牛語。即便洗刷塗角著好華鬘。繫車右邊柔濡愛語。大吉黑牛廣角大力。牽是車去。是牛聞是柔濡愛語故即得色力。牽重上坂。時黑牛主先所失物更再三倍得之。是牛主得大利己心甚歡喜。即說偈曰。載重入深轍　隨我語能去是故應濡語　不應生惡言濡語有色力　是牛能牽重我獲大財物　身心得喜樂佛語諸比丘。畜生聞形相語。尚失色力。何況於人(出十誦律二誦第三卷出四分律初分第七卷略同)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the past there was a man who had a black ox; and another ox-owner, for the sake of goods, made a proclamation: whose ox is stronger than mine? If yours wins, I forfeit goods; if it loses, you forfeit goods to me. The black ox’s owner answered: it may be so.

Then a heavy load was set on and the ox was harnessed to the left of the cart, and they laughed together and slighted him: black crooked-horn — and struck him with a rod: pull this cart away. The ox heard it and at once lost its color and strength and could not pull the load up the slope; and the black ox’s owner forfeited a great deal of goods.

The man who had won the goods afterward made the proclamation again, and the black ox, hearing his voice, said to its master: you may answer, so be it. Its master said: I cannot. And why? Because you, wretched black ox, forfeited a great deal of my goods; and if you do it again my goods will all be forfeited.

The ox said to its master: earlier, in front of the crowd, you described me in slighting terms, and because I heard an evil name I lost my color and strength; and that is why I could not pull the load up the slope. Now take my word and do not speak evil words. When you are in front of others, say to me: you are a fine black ox, well-born and auspicious, your horns broad and straight.

The master took the ox’s word and washed and brushed it and smeared its horns and put a fine flower-garland on it and harnessed it to the right side of the cart, and with soft and loving words: great auspicious black ox, broad-horned and greatly strong — pull this cart away. And the ox, hearing these soft and loving words, at once got color and strength and pulled the load up the slope; and the black ox’s owner got back what he had lost before, two and three times over.

That ox’s owner, having got a great profit, was very glad at heart, and spoke this verse:

“Carrying a load into a deep rut, / following my words it can go on. / Therefore one should speak gently / and should not give rise to evil words. / Gentle words have color and strength; / this ox can pull a load. / I have obtained great wealth, / and body and mind have joy.”

The Buddha told the monks: even an animal, hearing itself described in slighting terms, loses its color and strength — how much more a human being (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, second recitation, fascicle 3; the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya, first division, fascicle 7, is roughly the same).

17. A Donkey That Pulled a Cart Five Hundred Li a Day

A donkey that met a fat donkey on the road and found out how it stayed fat.

Chinese Source

昔人有驢以用挽車。日行數百里。語其弟言。莫放驢使與驢相見。弟怪之自思念云。夫智者相得其則歡喜。諛諂相得其亦歡喜。物類相得無不歡喜。弟故放驢令得相見。亦不嗚咽相嚊不食。兄後駕之便臥不行。兄便大瞋截其毛耳。驢得苦痛復行如前。驢語大家。君弟放我見惡知識。我問何肥。答曰。給陶家公邊負土。得惡道便臥不行。公便步擔土去。放我道邊食。得食好草歸得蒭穀。是以得肥。問何瘦。答曰。挽車日行五百里。飲食轗軻是以瘦耳。我瘦取謂放得肥。反見髠剃不敢復臥。乞得生活。主愍之放令解脫(出十卷譬喻經第八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago a man had a donkey which he used to pull a cart, and it went several hundred li a day. He said to his younger brother: do not let the donkey out to meet other donkeys.

His brother wondered at it and thought to himself: when wise men meet they are glad, and when flatterers meet they are glad too; when creatures of a kind meet, there is none that is not glad. So his brother let the donkey out and let it meet the others; and it did not bray, and they sniffed at one another and it would not eat.

Afterward his brother harnessed it and it lay down and would not go, and his brother was greatly enraged and cut off its mane and ears; and, getting the pain of it, the donkey went as before.

The donkey said to its master: your brother let me out and I met a bad friend, and I asked him: how are you so fat? He answered: I carry earth for the potter, and when I get onto a bad road I lie down and will not go, and the potter carries the earth on foot himself and lets me out by the roadside to eat; and I get good grass to eat and come home to fodder and grain, and that is why I am fat. I asked: how are you so thin? I answered: I pull a cart five hundred li a day, and my food and drink are hard to come by, and that is why I am thin.

Thinking that by being thin and lying down I might get fat, I found myself shaved instead, and I dare not lie down again. I beg to be allowed to live. And his master pitied him and set him free (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 8).

Ru-Yi Translation

A herd of oxen were gentle and good by nature, and wherever they went they chose soft and fine grass to eat and clear cool water to drink.

Then a donkey thought: I too shall imitate their eating and drinking. And he went into the herd of oxen and pawed the ground with his forefeet and butted and provoked the oxen, and imitated their lowing and could not change it, and his voice called himself an ox too. And the oxen gored him with their horns and killed him and left him and went away (Source: Ekottarika Āgama, fascicle 20).

19. A Dog That Gets No Alms Brings Suit Against the Householder Before the Officials

A dog that lays out the law of dogs before the magistrates, and, asked what sentence to pass, asks for the man to be made a great householder.

Chinese Source

佛在舍衛國。過去世有狗。捨自家至他家乞食。入他家時身在門內尾在門外。時主人居士打不與食。狗詣眾官言。是居士。我至其家乞食。不與我食反打我。我不破狗法。眾官問言狗有何法。答言。我在自家隨意坐臥。至他家時身入門內尾著門外。眾官言。喚居士來。問言。汝實打狗不與食耶。答言實爾。眾官問狗言。此人應云何治。狗言。與此舍衛城大居士職。何以故答。我昔在此舍衛城中作大居士。以身口作惡故受是狗身。是人惡甚於我若令是人得力勢者。當大作惡令入地獄。極受苦惱(出十誦律第三誦第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha was in the kingdom of Śrāvastī. In a past age there was a dog who left its own household and went to another house to beg for food; and when it went into that house its body was inside the gate and its tail outside the gate. Then the householder who owned the place beat it and gave it no food.

The dog went to the officials and said: this householder — I went to his house to beg for food, and he gave me no food and beat me instead. I did not break the law of dogs. The officials asked: what law have dogs? He answered: in my own house I sit and lie as I please; when I go to another’s house my body goes in at the gate and my tail stays outside the gate.

The officials said: summon the householder. And they asked him: did you truly beat the dog and give it no food? He answered: it is so. The officials asked the dog: how should this man be dealt with?

The dog said: give this man the office of a great householder of the city of Śrāvastī. Why? Because I was formerly a great householder in this city of Śrāvastī, and because of the evil I did with body and mouth I received this dog’s body. This man’s evil is greater than mine; and if he is given power and position he will do great evil and enter hell and suffer torment in the extreme (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, third recitation, fascicle 1).

20. A Puppy About to Be Killed Sees a Śramaṇa, and at the End of Its Life Is Born into a Noble House

A monk with a bowl of poor rice in a famine spends it buying a puppy off a butcher, and meets it again seven years later.

Chinese Source

昔有一國。穀米踊貴人民飢餓。時有沙門入城分衛。無所一獲。次至長者大豪貴門。得麁惡飯。這欲出城。逢一屠兒抱一狗子持歸欲殺。見沙門歡喜前為作禮。沙門呪願。老壽長生知有狗子。而欲殺之。故問其人何所齎持。答曰空行。又問。吾以見之願持示我。食貿狗子令命得濟。其人答曰。不能相與。如是至三慇懃喻請。其人抵突不肯隨言。又言可以示我。其人即出以示沙門。舉飯飴狗子摩捋呪願。狗子淚出。卿罪所致不得自在。使爾世世罪滅福生。離狗子身得生為人。值遇三寶。狗子得食善心生焉。命過即生豪貴大長者家。這生墮地便有慈心。時彼沙門次到長者門裏分衛。時長者子年以七歲。見彼沙門憶識本緣。便前稽首禮沙門足請前供養。還白父母。今我欲逐此大和尚奉受經戒。為作弟子。父母愛重不肯聽之。小兒啼泣不肯飲食。不欲聽去我便就死。父母便聽。隨師學道。除去鬚髮被三法衣。便得三昧立不退轉。開化一切發大道意(出迦葉詰阿難經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a kingdom in which grain rose steeply in price and the people were famished. Then a śramaṇa went into the city on alms round and got nothing at all, and came next to the gate of a great and wealthy elder and got coarse and poor rice.

Just as he was about to go out of the city he met a butcher carrying a puppy home meaning to kill it; and, seeing the śramaṇa, the man was glad and came forward and made obeisance to him. The śramaṇa made his prayer for long life and length of days, and knowing that he had a puppy and meant to kill it, asked deliberately: what are you carrying? He answered: I am going empty-handed.

He asked again: I have already seen it; I ask you to show it me. I shall trade food for the puppy and let its life be saved. The man answered: I cannot give it to you. And so three times, and he pressed and coaxed him earnestly, and the man was obstinate and would not follow his words.

He said again: at least show it me. And the man brought it out and showed it to the śramaṇa, and he held up the rice and fed the puppy and stroked it and made a prayer over it, and the puppy’s tears came out: it is by your own offense that you are not free. May your offense be extinguished life after life and merit be born, and may you leave the puppy’s body and be born a human being and meet with the Three Jewels.

The puppy, having got the food, a good mind arose in it; and when its life passed it was at once born into a great and noble elder’s household, and as soon as it was born and touched the ground it had a kindly heart.

Then that śramaṇa came in turn to the elder’s gate on alms round, and the elder’s son, then seven years old, seeing that śramaṇa, remembered and knew the original cause, and came forward and bowed his head at the śramaṇa’s feet and asked him in to make an offering. And he went back and said to his parents: I now wish to follow this great preceptor and receive the sūtras and the precepts and become his disciple.

His parents loved him and would not consent, and the boy wept and would not eat or drink: if you will not let me go I shall die. So his parents consented, and he followed his teacher and studied the path, and his beard and hair were removed and he put on the three Dharma robes, and he obtained samādhi and stood in non-retrogression, and opened and transformed all beings to give rise to the resolve for the great path (Source: Sūtra of Kāśyapa Questioning Ānanda).

21. A White Dog Is Well Provided for in the House of Its Son of a Former Life and Digs Up What It Had Hidden

The Buddha tells a brahmin that his household dog is his own father, and proposes a test involving a golden bowl and the four legs of a couch.

Chinese Source

佛詣舍衛城遊行分衛。時到鸚鵡摩牢兜羅子家。遇其不在家。有白狗名具。坐好蓐上以金鉢食粳米肉。白狗遙見世尊從遠而來。見已便吠。世尊言。止白狗。不須作是聲。汝本吟哦(梵志乞食音)於是作白狗。白狗極大瞋恚。下床蓐已至門閾下伏寂然而住。後摩牢兜羅見問邊人曰。誰觸我狗而令憂慼。今日有沙門瞿曇來詣家乞食。狗吠之。瞿曇言。止白狗。汝不應作是聲。汝本吟哦。狗恚不樂。鸚鵡摩牢兜羅子遙罵世尊。誹謗瞋恚詣孤獨園。遙見鸚鵡摩牢兜羅子來。告諸比丘。此人瞋恚身壞生泥犁中。彼時鸚鵡摩牢兜羅子白世尊曰。沙門瞿曇今至我家乞食。白狗於汝有何咎而令不樂。佛言。白狗見我而吠。我言。白狗汝不應作是聲。汝本吟哦。而白狗瞋恚。瞿曇此白狗本是我何等親屬。佛止摩牢不須問。汝或能憂慼不樂。如是再三。世尊曰。此摩牢白狗前所生。是汝父名兜羅。倍增瞋恚。我父兜羅。常行施與常事於火。身壞死已生梵天上。何故當生狗中。此摩牢以汝增上慢。我父兜羅後亦復爾。生弊惡狗中。而說偈曰。梵志增上慢　此終生六趣鷄猪狗野狐　驢卵地獄中佛言。若不信我說者。便可還家語白狗言。汝本生時是我父兜羅者。還上床蓐當於金鉢中食粳米肉。當示我父遺財。汝本藏舉我不知處。於是鸚鵡摩牢兜羅子聞世尊所說。善思惟念。還至家已具語白狗。彼時白狗便還上床蓐坐。於金鉢中食粳米肉。至本臥處床四脚下。以口足捊地。於是鸚鵡摩牢兜羅子大得錢財。歡喜心生。以右膝著地向祇樹園。自稱姓字。真實沙門語實不妄。三自稱已。出舍衛城往詣祇樹。世尊遙見告諸比丘。汝見彼鸚鵡摩牢兜羅子來不。唯然世尊。若以此時。兜羅子命終者生於善處。於我有善心故。眾生因善心故。身壞死時生善處天上。彼時鸚鵡摩牢兜羅子往世尊所。面相慰勞竟云。佛言無有異(出中阿鋡經第三十九卷鸚鵡章中)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha went to the city of Śrāvastī and travelled about on alms round, and came in due course to the house of Śuka, son of Taudeya the Parrot, and happened to find him not at home. There was a white dog named Jù, sitting on a fine mat and eating white rice and meat from a golden bowl.

The white dog saw the World-Honored One coming from afar, and, having seen him, barked. The World-Honored One said: stop, white dog; you need not make that noise. You used to chant (that is, the brahmin’s begging tone), and so you have become a white dog. The white dog was extremely enraged, and came down from the couch and lay at the threshold in silence.

Afterward Taudeya came back and asked those about him: who has provoked my dog and made it unhappy? Today the śramaṇa Gautama came to the house to beg for food, and the dog barked at him, and Gautama said: stop, white dog; you should not make that noise, for you used to chant. And the dog was angry and unhappy.

Śuka, son of Taudeya the Parrot, reviled the World-Honored One from afar, and, slandering and enraged, went to Anāthapiṇḍada’s park. Seeing Śuka coming from afar, the Buddha told the monks: this man is enraged; when his body breaks up he will be born in niraya.

Then Śuka said to the World-Honored One: the śramaṇa Gautama came to my house today to beg for food. What fault has the white dog toward you, that you should make it unhappy? The Buddha said: the white dog saw me and barked, and I said: white dog, you should not make that noise, for you used to chant — and the white dog was enraged.

Gautama, this white dog — what kinsman of mine was it? The Buddha said: stop, Taudeya, do not ask; you may perhaps be distressed and unhappy. And so twice and three times.

The World-Honored One said: this white dog of yours, Taudeya, was in a former birth your father, named Taudeya. His rage was redoubled: my father Taudeya constantly gave alms and constantly served the fire, and when his body broke up and he died he was born in the Brahmā heaven. Why should he be born among dogs?

[The Buddha said:] this, Taudeya, is because of your overweening pride; and your father Taudeya afterward was the same, and was born among wretched and evil dogs. And he spoke this verse:

“A brahmin with overweening pride, / at the end of this is born in the six destinies: / among fowls, pigs, dogs, and jackals, / among donkeys, in eggs, and in hell.”

The Buddha said: if you do not believe what I say, you may go home and say to the white dog: if you were in your former birth my father Taudeya, go back up onto the couch and eat white rice and meat from the golden bowl, and show me my father’s remaining property, which you hid in your former life and I do not know the place of.

So Śuka, son of Taudeya the Parrot, hearing what the World-Honored One said, considered it well and went home and said all of it to the white dog. Then the white dog went back up onto the couch and sat, and ate white rice and meat from the golden bowl, and went to the place where it had originally lain and, at the four feet of the couch, scraped the earth with its mouth and paws — and Śuka, son of Taudeya the Parrot, got a great deal of money.

A glad mind arose in him, and he put his right knee to the ground facing the Jeta Grove and named himself: truly the śramaṇa speaks truth and does not speak falsely. Having said this three times, he went out of the city of Śrāvastī toward the Jeta Grove.

The World-Honored One saw him from afar and told the monks: do you see Śuka, son of Taudeya the Parrot, coming? Yes, World-Honored One. If at this moment the son of Taudeya were to die he would be born in a good place, because he has a good mind toward me; and because beings have a good mind, when their bodies break up and they die they are born in a good place, in heaven. Then Śuka, son of Taudeya the Parrot, went to the World-Honored One and, when they had greeted one another, said: what the Buddha said was not otherwise (Source: Madhyama Āgama, fascicle 39, in the Parrot chapter).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the Śuka Sutta of the Madhyama Āgama, and the compendium keeps its awkwardness intact. The Buddha's first remark to the barking dog is not kind and is not meant to be understood by the dog: you used to chant, and so you have become a white dog. It is overheard, reported, and taken as an insult, which it partly is.

The proof the Buddha proposes is the interesting part, because it is designed to be checked. Tell the dog to go back up on the couch, eat from the golden bowl, and show you the property my father hid where I do not know it. Every element is verifiable, and the last one produces money. The brahmin's response — kneeling toward the Jeta Grove and declaring three times that the śramaṇa speaks truth — is a conversion by evidence rather than by teaching.

The doctrine attached is bleaker than the story: the father, who gave alms constantly and served the fire and was thought to have gone to the Brahmā heaven, is a dog because of overweening pride, and the son is told to his face that he is going the same way.

22. A Vicious Dog Comes to a Good Mind Through One Monk

A dog that decides to spare a visiting monk if he shares his food, and does not decide it out loud.

Chinese Source

有一長者財富無數。有一弊狗常喜嚙人。凡人不得妄入其門。有一比丘。聰明善慧聖達難當逮。入其門乞。值狗出臥不覺入時。長者設食狗覺方見。念出臥不覺沙門得入。今既已坐當奈之何。若獨食者出必嚙殺。噉其腹中所食美膳。若分我食乃原之耳。沙門知其心念。自食一揣與狗一揣。善生慈向於沙門。前舐其足。後出門臥。曾被其嚙人劍斫其頭。其狗即生長者夫人腹中。生後短命尋復終亡。復生彼國餘長者家。年十餘歲。見一沙門前迎為禮。啟其父母請為我師。施設供養尋受經戒。再化家中一切大小誦經念道。因報二親求為沙門。不受具足。供養和尚日夜不懈。和尚滅後乃受戒德(出十卷譬喻經第六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was an elder of measureless wealth who had a vicious dog that constantly liked to bite people, so that no one could go in at his gate at will. There was a monk, intelligent and wise, whose sagely penetration was hard to match, who went in at the gate to beg; and it happened that the dog had gone out and was lying down and did not notice him go in.

The elder set out food, and the dog noticed and only then saw him, and thought: I went out and lay down and did not notice the śramaṇa getting in; and now he is already seated — what is to be done about it? If he eats alone, when he comes out I shall certainly bite him to death and eat the fine food in his belly; and if he shares his food with me, I shall spare him.

The śramaṇa knew what it was thinking, and ate one lump himself and gave one lump to the dog; and a good and kindly mind arose in it toward the śramaṇa, and it came forward and licked his feet, and afterward went out of the gate and lay down.

Someone it had bitten before cut off its head with a sword, and the dog was at once born in the elder’s wife’s womb; and being born, it was short-lived and soon died again, and was born again into another elder’s household in that country.

At ten years and more it saw a śramaṇa and went forward to meet him and made obeisance, and asked his parents to let him be his teacher; and he set out offerings and received the sūtras and precepts, and transformed everyone in the household, great and small, to recite the sūtras and keep the path in mind. And on the strength of it he asked his two parents to let him become a śramaṇa; and he did not take the full precepts, but provided for his preceptor day and night without slackening, and only after his preceptor’s death did he receive the virtue of the precepts (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 6).

23. A Doe Falls into a Snare, Begs Leave to Take Her Farewell of Her Fawns, and Comes Back to Her Death

The doe in the snare, and the four exchanges in verse — with the hunter, with her fawns, in farewell, and at her return — that make up the longest entry in the fascicle.

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there were several hundred deer in a herd who, following the fine grass, came close to a human settlement; and the king of the country came out hunting and they were scattered. There was one doe, with young, wandering alone, who was pursued, and, hungry and worn out, lost her companions; and she bore two fawns and left them and went to look for food, and, alone and startled and losing her footing, she fell into a hunter’s snare, and she cried out sorrowfully and wished to get out and could not free herself.

The hunter heard the sound and went to look, and, seeing the deer, was glad at heart and came forward meaning to kill her; and the deer knocked her head and begged for pity and set out her case: I have just borne two fawns, still small and without understanding, whose eyes are only beginning to see dimly and who do not yet know east from west. I beg the loan of a moment to go back and see my young and show them water and grass so that they may live, and I shall come straight back to my death and shall not break faith with my oath.

The hunter was startled and wondered, and answered the deer: not one person in the whole world has utmost sincerity, much less a deer; if your body escapes death, how should you come back? The deer answered again: if you let me go, my young live; if you keep me, my young die. And she spoke a verse:

“My body is a beast, / and I dwell and range in wood and marsh; / a base creature, I covet body and life / and cannot go deliberately against death. / Now I have come into your snare / and my part is to go to the knife and block. / I do not begrudge this rank and stinking body — / I only pity my two young.”

The hunter thought it strange and extraordinary, and his mind was still greedy, and he answered the deer again: cunning and falsehood have no substance, and craft and deceit are hard to trust; empty show has ten thousand faces and cunning is not of one kind. Those who love the body and think death heavy are seldom able to keep faith with their lives, and it is because people are not good that it is hard to fix a term with them — much less a beast; once gone, how should you come back? I certainly shall not let you go.

The deer shed tears again and answered in verse:

“Though my body is a base beast / and I do not know the way of humanity and right, / how, having received a kindly favor, / should I go once and not come back? / Better to go to the pain of being torn apart / than to live by falsehood. / I grieve for my two young in their want, / and beg the loan of a moment. / If there is an evil person in the world / who sets the community of monks at strife, / who breaks stūpas and ruins temples / and kills arhats besides, / and turns and harms father and mother, / wife and children and servants — / if I do not come back, / my offense would be greater than that.”

The hunter, hearing the deer’s words again, was the more moved in his heart, and drew back and exclaimed: I, born in this world, have obtained a human birth, and am foolish and deluded and dark, and turn my back on kindness and hold right cheap; I harm living beings and make killing my trade, and by cunning and falsehood get what I can and covet without ever being satisfied; I do not know impermanence or know how to distinguish the Three Honored Ones. What this deer says is beyond what a human says; her oath and her knocking of her head reach to the utmost, and her feeling shows itself as wholly loyal. And he came forward and undid the snare and let her go.

So the deer came back to where her young were, and lowered her head and cried and licked their bodies, in grief and joy together, and spoke a verse:

“All meetings of love and affection / come about by causes and conditions; / where there is meeting there is parting, / and impermanence is hard to keep long. / I am now your mother / and constantly fear I cannot keep myself; / born in the world there is much to be feared, / and life is briefer than the morning dew.”

Then the mother deer took her two young and showed them good water and grass, her tears flowing, and spoke a verse of farewell:

“Going out this morning I met misfortune / and fell by mistake into a hunter’s hands. / At once I shall be butchered and cut up, / my body broken and turned to rot. / Thinking of you I begged for pity and came; / now I shall go back to my death. / I pity you, orphaned so early and so small — / strive to keep yourselves alive.”

The mother deer, having spoken, left them and went; and the two young cried out and wept in sorrow and longing, and pursued after her from behind, falling and rising again. Their mother turned and said: go back and do not come; do not let mother and children lose their lives together. My dying I bear with a willing heart; but I grieve that you do not yet know that the world is impermanent and that all have their partings. My own fate is thin and you were born without protection; why grieve, and only add to your troubles? You had better set your resolve and see out your lives. And their mother spoke this verse again for her young:

“In a former life I was greedy with love, / and now I have come to love the keeping of this body. / All born in the world must die; / one does not escape and the trouble does not end. / Restrain the mind and once depart from greed, / and afterward there is great peace. / Better to go to death with faith kept / than to live by cheating and peril.”

Her young still grieved and cried and followed her as far as the snare, looking east and west; and they saw the hunter lying asleep beneath a tree. The mother deer stood still and spoke a verse to wake him:

“The deer you let go before / has now come back to her death. / Your kindness flowed and favored a base beast, / and I have been able to take leave of my two young. / I took and showed them water and grass / and told them of impermanence and suffering. / Though I die ten thousand times I have no regret left; / thinking of your kindness I dare not fail it.”

The hunter then woke with a start and got up, and the deer knelt toward him and spoke again in verse:

“You let me go before; / that virtue is weightier than heaven and earth. / A base beast has been covered by kindness / and comes back to her death to keep faith. / Moved by your humanity, the kindness is hard to forget; / I dare not go against your command. / Though I come back and repay it, / still the account of that kindness is not settled.”

The hunter, seeing the deer’s deep faith and her dying for what was right, her resolve and integrity wholly sincere, her kindly conduct giving rise to loyalty, and how what he had released came back in proof of it, giving up life to fulfil an oath — and mother and children in grief and longing following one another to the place — was moved by kindness and struck with pity, and bowed his head and apologized:

“You are a god or a spirit; / your faith and righteousness are as fine as this. / My feeling is in fear and awe — / how should I dare do you harm? / Sooner would I kill this mean body of mine / and let the harm reach my wife and children / than bear, before a divine being, / to raise even the breadth of a hair of such a thought.”

The hunter then let the deer go, and mother and young in grief and joy cried out, and in verse thanked the hunter:

“A base beast born in the world / ought rightly to fill the kitchen and the block; / and at once, at the moment of the boiling and slaughtering, / by your generosity I took leave of my two young. / Heaven’s humanity holds living things dear, / and I have received release and pardon besides. / Your virtue and blessing are piled up beyond measure, / and are not what a mouth can set out.”

The hunter reported all of it to the king, and the people of the kingdom all knew of it and were universally moved by that kindness and faith; and that the humane conduct of a beast should surpass what is right in a man — there was none who did not sigh in awe, and killing and hunting were stopped for it. The deer went back and called her herd and whistled to her companions, and they came together and were each at peace in their place.

The Buddha said: the deer of that time is myself; the two young are Rāhula and Yaśodharā; the king of that time is Śāriputra; the hunter is Ānanda (Source: Sūtra of the Deer’s Young).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The longest entry in the fascicle and one of the finest pieces of narrative verse in the compendium. Its structure is four exchanges: with the hunter at the snare, with her fawns on returning, in farewell as she leaves them, and with the hunter again when she comes back.

The hunter's refusals are the making of it. He does not refuse out of cruelty but out of a considered view of the world — not one person has utmost sincerity, much less a beast; cunning and falsehood have no substance and craft is hard to trust; those who love the body are seldom able to keep faith with their lives. His conversion, when it comes, is a revision of that view and is stated as such: what this deer says is beyond what a human says.

The doe's own strongest verse is the one in which she measures her possible breach of faith against the worst offenses she can name — setting the saṅgha at strife, breaking stūpas, killing arhats, murdering parents — and says that if she does not come back her offense would be greater than those. The story ends with the kingdom giving up hunting, and with the fawns identified as Rāhula and Yaśodharā.

24. A Deer King Meets with a Trap and Gives His Body to Save the Herd

A deer king who lies across a ditch and lets several hundred million deer walk out over his back, and then looks round to see whether one is left.

Chinese Source

昔國王遊獵。作於場塹以捕群鹿。時有鹿王將鹿數億。次食美草入其場內。守者閉門。往白於王。王大歡喜。鹿王即知。自念。群鹿所以來者由我一身耳。當作方計以濟眾命。即以身橫伏塹上。使群鹿蹈背而出。足傷其背皮肉了盡。唯有骨在。忍痛濟之皆已得出。勢自上岸四向顧視。唯有一鹿不知求出。鹿王命呼乃來得出。於是鹿王命絕墮塹(出十卷譬喻經第八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago a king went out hunting and made an enclosure and a ditch to catch a herd of deer. Then a deer king, leading several hundred million deer, went in due course to eat the fine grass and came into the enclosure, and the keepers shut the gate and went and reported to the king, and the king was greatly delighted.

The deer king knew it and thought to himself: the reason the herd came here is my own single self. I should devise a means to save the lives of the many. And he laid his body across the ditch and made the herd tread on his back and go out; and their hooves wounded his back and the skin and flesh were entirely gone and only the bones were left, and, enduring the pain, he saved them, and they all got out.

Making his own way up the bank he looked round in the four directions, and there was one deer that did not know how to get out; and the deer king called it and it came and got out. And thereupon the deer king’s life ended and he fell into the ditch (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 8).

25. The Míngtuó Beast Is Flayed to Save a Hunter's Life

A gold-furred beast that carries a dying hunter to water and feeds him, and then learns what he was sent for.

Chinese Source

佛在羅閱耆闍崛山。身有風患。耆域醫王為合蘇藥。用三十二種。日日服三十二兩。時提婆達常懷嫉妬。心自高大望與佛齊。効佛亦服注諸脈理。身力微弱苦惱呻喚。世尊怜愍手摩其頭。藥消病除看識佛手。曰。悉達餘術世不承用復學醫道。佛言。提婆達懷不善心不但今日。昔閻浮提城名波羅奈。王名梵摩達。凶暴無慈。夢見一獸。身毛金色毛端金光。即召獵師。我夢具以告之。汝等求捕。若得其皮當重賞汝。若不得者誅滅汝族。時諸獵師憂愁憒憒聚會議計。共募一人令行求之。若汝不吉還亦當以物與汝妻子。其人自念。分棄身命即可當行。涉嶮而去。經久身弊。天時暑熱欝蒸欲死。悲悴而言。誰有慈悲拯我身命。有一野獸名曰銘陀。身毛金色聞甚怜愍。身入冷泉來就裹抱。小還有力將至水所。為其洗浴拾果食之。體既平復。而自念言。今覩此獸王正求之。然我垂死賴其濟命。感恩未詶何心當害。若復不獲。彼諸獵師必被誅戮。念是而悲。銘陀問言以何不樂。答心所懷。銘陀語言。此事莫憂我皮易得。捨身無數未曾為福。今以身皮濟彼眾命。如有所獲。但剝取皮莫便絕命。我以施汝終無悔恨。獵師剝皮。銘陀即自立願。今我以皮用施此人。救彼愛命。持此功德施彼眾生。用成佛道普度一切。作此願已三千國土六反震動。剝皮去後身肉赤裸血出流離。復有八萬蠅蟻之屬。集其身上同時唼食。時欲趣穴復恐傷害。忍痛自持身不動搖。死於彼中。時諸蠅蟻食者命終生天。獵師擔皮上王。王見奇之常敷用臥。身心安隱快樂。獸銘陀者今我身是。梵摩達王今提婆達是。八萬諸蟲我初成佛始轉法輪。上八萬諸天得道者是(出賢愚經第三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha was on Mount Gṛdhrakūṭa at Rājagṛha and his body had a wind ailment, and Jīvaka the king of physicians compounded a ghee medicine for him of thirty-two ingredients, and he took thirty-two taels a day.

At that time Devadatta constantly harboured jealousy and thought himself great and hoped to equal the Buddha, and imitated the Buddha and took the same, and it poured into all his veins and his bodily strength grew weak and he groaned in distress. The World-Honored One in pity stroked his head with his hand, and the medicine was digested and the illness removed; and, recognizing the Buddha’s hand, he said: Siddhārtha, other arts you do not carry on in the world, and now you study medicine as well.

The Buddha said: it is not only today that Devadatta harbours an unwholesome mind. Long ago in Jambudvīpa there was a city named Vārāṇasī whose king was named Brahmadatta, violent and without kindness. He dreamed of a beast whose body-hair was gold-colored, with golden light at the tips of the hairs, and he summoned the hunters and told them the whole of his dream: go and hunt for it. If you get its skin I shall reward you richly; and if you do not, I shall wipe out your clans.

Then the hunters were grieved and distracted and gathered to take counsel, and together chose one man and sent him to go and look for it: and if you do not come back safely, we shall give goods to your wife and children. The man thought to himself: I may as well cast away my body and life — and went.

He crossed dangerous country and went, and after a long while his body was worn out; the weather was hot and the steaming heat was killing him, and he said in his grief and exhaustion: is there anyone with compassion to save my body and life?

There was a wild beast named Míngtuó, its body-hair gold-colored, who heard him and was moved with great pity, and went into a cold spring and came and took him in its arms; and when he had a little strength again it brought him to the water and washed him and gathered fruit to feed him, and his body was restored.

And he thought to himself: this is the very beast the king seeks. But I was near death and by his help my life was saved; and being moved by kindness that I have not repaid, with what heart should I do him harm? And yet, if I get nothing, those hunters will surely be executed. Thinking of this he grieved.

Míngtuó asked: why are you unhappy? He answered with what was in his heart. Míngtuó said: do not worry about this matter; my skin is easily got. I have cast away this body countless times and have never made merit with it; now with my body’s skin I shall save the lives of that multitude. If you must have it, only strip off the skin and do not take my life at once. In giving it to you I shall have no regret at all.

The hunter stripped off the skin, and Míngtuó then made this vow: now that I give this skin to this man to save those lives that he loves, let me hold this merit and give it to those beings, and by it accomplish the buddha-path and deliver all universally. When this vow was made the three thousand lands shook in six ways.

After the skin was taken away, his body and flesh were red and naked and the blood flowed and streamed; and eighty thousand flies and ants gathered on his body and fed on it all at once. He wished to go to his hole and feared he would harm them, and endured the pain and held himself so that his body did not stir, and died there; and those flies and ants that fed on him, when their lives ended, were born in heaven.

The hunter carried the skin up to the king, and the king thought it wonderful and constantly spread it and lay on it, and his body and mind were at ease and happy.

The beast Míngtuó is myself; King Brahmadatta is Devadatta now; and the eighty thousand insects are the eighty thousand gods who attained the path when I had first attained buddhahood and first turned the wheel of the Dharma (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 3).

27. A Wolf Reads Another's Mind and Kills the Infant of Its Enemy

The same story as fascicle 46's entry 18, with a wolf in place of a hungry ghost.

Chinese Source

有女置其嬰兒在於一處。狼擔兒而走。時人捕躡而語之言。汝今何故擔他兒去。狼答之言。此小兒母是我怨家。五百世中常食我兒。我亦五百世常殺其子。若彼能捨舊怨之心。我亦能捨。時人語其兒母。可捨怨心。兒母答言。我今已捨。狼觀兒母。雖口言捨而心不放。害之而去(出抄毘曇第二十八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

A woman set her infant down in a certain place, and a wolf carried the child off and ran; and people pursued it and said to it: why do you carry off another’s child?

The wolf answered them: this small child’s mother is my enemy. For five hundred lives she has constantly eaten my young, and I likewise for five hundred lives have constantly killed her children. If she can give up her old enmity of mind, I too can give it up.

Then the people said to the child’s mother: you may give up your mind of enmity. The child’s mother answered: I have now given it up. Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry and fascicle 46's entry 18 are the same story with the animal changed: there a hungry ghost possessing a woman, here a wolf carrying off an infant; and in both the terms are identical — five hundred lives of mutual killing, an offer to let it go if the other will, and a mind-reading that catches the mother saying what she does not mean.

Both cite the same abridged Abhidharma commentary, from different fascicles. The compendium files one under ghosts and the other under wolves, which is its usual way of proceeding: the classification is by subject matter and the duplication is left to stand.

28. Four Beasts Including a Monkey Form a Connection with a Brahmin

A fox, a monkey, an otter, and a hare who all bring the departing brahmin a farewell offering. Three of them bring food.

Chinese Source

昔有梵志年百二十。少不娶妻無有淫泆。靜處深山以茅為廬。蓬蒿為席以水果苽充為食。不積財寶。王娉不往端然無為。數千餘歲與禽獸相娛。有四種獸。一名狐。二者獼猴。三者獺。四者兔。此四獸於道人所聽經說戒。如是積久。食諸果苽皆悉訖盡。後道人意欲去。此四大獸愁憂不樂。共議言。我曹各求供養。獼猴取甘果來以上道人。狐亦求食得一囊飯麨來以上道人。獺入水取大魚來以上道人。兔自思念。我當用何等供養道人耶。自念。當持身供養耳。便行取藮以燃火作炭。往白道人言。今我小薄。請作炭入火中。以身上道人。火為不然。道人感其仁義。哀之遂止。時梵志者提和竭佛是。兔者我身是。獼猴者舍利弗是。狐者阿難是。獺者目揵連是(出舊譬喻經下卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a brahmin of a hundred and twenty years who from his youth had taken no wife and had no lewdness, and who dwelt quietly in the deep mountains with a hut of thatch and a mat of mugwort, and took water and fruit and melons for his food. He did not accumulate wealth or treasure, and when a king sent for him he did not go, but sat upright in non-action; and for several thousand years he amused himself with the birds and beasts.

There were four kinds of beasts: the first a fox, the second a monkey, the third an otter, the fourth a hare; and these four beasts heard the sūtras and the exposition of the precepts at the religious man’s place, and so it went on for a long time; and the fruits and melons were all used up.

Afterward the religious man had a mind to go away, and these four great beasts were grieved and unhappy and conferred together: let each of us seek an offering.

The monkey took sweet fruit and brought it and offered it to the religious. The fox too sought food and got a bag of rice and parched grain and brought it and offered it to the religious. The otter went into the water and took a great fish and brought it and offered it to the religious.

The hare thought to itself: what shall I use to make an offering to the religious? And it thought: I shall offer my body. And it went and gathered brushwood and lit a fire and made coals, and went and said to the religious: I am now small and slight; let me become coals and go into the fire and offer my body to the religious.

The fire would not burn, and the religious was moved by its humanity and right and, pitying it, stopped it.

The brahmin of that time is the Buddha Dīpaṃkara; the hare is myself; the monkey is Śāriputra; the fox is Ānanda; the otter is Maudgalyāyana (Source: Old Parables Sūtra, lower fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the well-known story of the four beasts and the self-immolating hare, and the compendium places it under monkeys rather than under hares — where entry 34, a different version of the same self-immolation, is filed instead. The two are worth reading together: here the hare is one of four and offers itself only because it has nothing else, and the fire will not burn and the sage stops it; in entry 34 the hare is a king who has been listening to the recitation for months and throws itself in to keep the sage from leaving, and it dies.

Note also that the fox brings a bag of rice and parched grain, the otter a fish, and the monkey sweet fruit — three offerings that cost their bringers nothing. The hare's arithmetic is stated plainly: I am now small and slight.

29. A Monkey Offers the Buddha a Bowl of Honey

A monkey that borrows Ānanda's bowl for honey, is told to clean it, misunderstands, and dies of joy.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha and the monks accepted an invitation from the brahmin Shīzhì and went back to Gṛdhrakūṭa and washed their vessels at the riverside. In the mango grove there was a monkey, who, going about, saw a tree with ripe honey in it and no bees, and came to Ānanda and asked for his bowl; and Ānanda would not give it. The Buddha said: only give it.

The monkey got the bowl (the Mahīśāsaka Vinaya says it asked for the Buddha’s bowl), filled it with honey, and offered it to the World-Honored One; and the World-Honored One would not accept it and had it made clean with water. The monkey did not understand and supposed he meant there were insects in it, and took it to the water’s edge and washed the bowl; and the water lapped into the honey and it carried it back and offered it to the Buddha (the Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish says it washed out the red insects; the Mahīśāsaka Vinaya says it saw insects and set it aside and took more).

The Buddha distributed it among the community of monks and it went round them all. The monkey, delighted, leapt and danced backward and fell into a pit and died, and became a child in the womb of Shīzhì’s wife, and was afterward born a boy of handsome form and features (the Mahīśāsaka Vinaya says: he was born in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, and afterward born among humans and attained the path of an arhat).

The Buddha said: in the past, in the time of the Buddha Kāśyapa, there was a young śramaṇa who saw an arhat leap across a stream and said to him, taking him for an ordinary monk: you are as quick and light as a monkey. And for five hundred lives thereafter he was constantly a monkey (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 12; also Mahīśāsaka Vinaya, fascicle 10; also Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 29).

30. A Monkey Becomes Teacher to Five Hundred Sages

A monkey who made offerings to five hundred pratyekabuddhas, found them dead, and went next door to the ascetics and taught them to sit properly.

Chinese Source

如來於摩偷羅國涅槃。百年後優婁漫陀山。一邊有五百緣覺。一邊有五百仙人。一邊有五百獼猴。獼猴主往緣覺間見諸緣覺。生歡喜心。取樹華果供養緣覺。作禮坐於僧末。日日如是。後諸緣覺皆入涅槃。獼猴供養如本。見諸緣覺不受。便牽衣捉足亦復不動。獼猴思惟。皆已死矣。啼泣懊惱。復至五百仙人所。皆到棘刺中。獼猴亦到。仙復到灰土上。獼猴亦到灰土上。仙人五熱炙身。獼猴亦復如是。仙手攀樹自懸獼猴。撥其手令墮地。常教化諸仙以四威儀。既教化已。於諸仙所端坐修定。語仙人言。汝等一切當如是坐。時五百仙隨其坐禪。諸仙無師說法。於三十七品助菩提法。思惟取證得緣覺道。皆作是念。我得聖道由此獼猴。即以香華飲食供養獼猴。獼猴命終以香木燒其身。佛語阿難。是獼猴者即優波笈多。於惡道中為多眾生作饒益。我涅槃後百餘年。當有上事(出阿育王經第六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Tathāgata entered nirvāṇa in the kingdom of Mathurā, and a hundred years afterward, on Mount Uruvilvā-manda, on one side there were five hundred pratyekabuddhas, on another side five hundred sages, and on another five hundred monkeys.

The chief of the monkeys went among the pratyekabuddhas and saw them and a glad mind arose in him, and he took the flowers and fruit of the trees and made offerings to the pratyekabuddhas, and made obeisance and sat at the end of the assembly; and so he did day after day.

Afterward the pratyekabuddhas all entered nirvāṇa, and the monkey made offerings as before; and, seeing that the pratyekabuddhas would not receive them, he pulled their robes and took hold of their feet, and still they did not move. The monkey reflected: they are all dead. And he wept and grieved.

Then he went to where the five hundred sages were, and they all went among the thorns and brambles, and the monkey went too; the sages went onto ash and earth, and the monkey went onto the ash and earth; the sages scorched their bodies with the five heats, and the monkey did likewise; the sages hung themselves from trees by their hands, and the monkey pushed their hands away and made them fall to the ground.

He constantly taught and transformed the sages in the four postures; and having taught them, he sat upright in the sages’ presence and cultivated concentration, and said to the sages: all of you should sit like this. Then the five hundred sages sat in meditation after him; and the sages, having no teacher to expound the Dharma, contemplated the thirty-seven factors that aid enlightenment and reached realization and obtained the way of a pratyekabuddha.

And they all thought: my attainment of the holy path is because of this monkey. And they made offerings to the monkey with incense, flowers, food, and drink; and when the monkey’s life ended they burned its body with fragrant wood.

The Buddha told Ānanda: this monkey is Upagupta, who in the evil destinies made great benefit for many beings; and a hundred years and more after my nirvāṇa the above will come about (Source: Aśokāvadāna, fascicle 6).

31. Five Hundred Monkeys Imitate the Arhats and Build a Stūpa

Five hundred monkeys who built a stūpa of mud and stones in imitation and were drowned in a flash flood — and the five hundred brahmins who had laughed at a water-carrying monk.

Chinese Source

昔佛在羅閱祇國。遣一羅漢名須漫。持佛髮爪至罽賓國南山作佛圖。寺五百羅漢常止其中。旦夕燒香繞塔禮拜。時彼山中有五百獼猴。見道人供養塔寺。即便相將至深㵎邊。負輦泥石効作佛圖。竪木立剎弊幡繫頭。旦夕禮拜亦如道人。時山水暴漲。五百獼猴一時漂沒。魂神即生第二忉利天上。七寶殿舍衣食自然。各自念言。從何所來得生天上。即以天眼自見本形。獼猴之身。効諸道人戲作塔寺。雖身漂沒神得生天。今當下報故屍之恩。各將侍從華香伎樂臨故尸上。散華燒香繞之七匝。時山中有五百婆羅門。外學邪見不信罪福。見諸天人散華作樂繞獼猴屍。怪而問曰。諸天光影巍巍乃爾。何故屈意供養此尸。諸天人言。此屍是吾等故身。具述本事。等以此微福得生天上。今故散華以報故身之恩。戲為塔寺獲福如此。若當至心奉佛。其德難喻。卿等邪見不信正真。百劫勤苦無所一得。不如共至耆闍崛山禮事供養得福無限。即皆欣然共至佛所。五體作禮散華供養。諸天人白佛。我等近世獼猴之身。蒙世尊之恩。得生天上。恨不見佛今故自歸。重白佛言。我等前世有何罪行受獼猴身。雖作塔寺身被漂沒。佛告天人。不從空生。往昔世時有五百年少婆羅門共行入山欲求仙道。時山上有一沙門。欲於山上泥治精舍。下谷取水身輕若飛。五百婆羅門興嫉妬意同聲笑之。今此沙門上下飄疾甚如獼猴。何足為奇。如是取水不止。山水一來溺死不久。佛告諸天人。時上下沙門我身是也。五百年少婆羅門者五百獼猴身是。戲笑作罪身受其報。佛說偈言。戲笑為惡　號泣受報五百天人聞佛語已即得道跡。五百婆羅門聞罪福之報。而自歎曰。吾等學仙積有年數。未蒙果報。不如獼猴戲笑為福得生天上。佛德實妙。稽首佛足願為弟子。佛言。善來比丘。即成沙門精進得道(出法句經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the Buddha was in the kingdom of Rājagṛha and sent an arhat named Xūmàn with the Buddha’s hair and nails to the southern mountains of Kaśmīra to build a stūpa; and five hundred arhats constantly stayed in that monastery, burning incense morning and evening and circling the stūpa and bowing.

At that time in that mountain there were five hundred monkeys who saw the religious men making offerings to the stūpa and the monastery, and led one another to the edge of a deep gully and carried mud and stones and imitated them and made a stūpa, and set up timber and raised a mast and tied poor banners to its head, and bowed morning and evening just as the religious men did.

Then the mountain water rose in flood and the five hundred monkeys were all swept away and drowned at once; and their spirits were at once born in the second heaven, the Trāyastriṃśa, with palaces of the seven treasures and clothing and food coming of themselves.

Each of them thought: where have we come from, to be born in heaven? And with the heavenly eye they saw their original forms, the bodies of monkeys, who had imitated the religious men and made a stūpa and monastery in play; and though their bodies had been swept away and drowned, their spirits had obtained birth in heaven. Now we should go down and repay the kindness of our former corpses.

And each with attendants and flowers, incense, and music came down over the former corpses, and scattered flowers and burned incense and circled them seven times.

At that time in the mountain there were five hundred brahmins of heterodox learning and wrong views who did not believe in offense and merit; and, seeing gods scattering flowers and making music and circling the corpses of monkeys, they wondered and asked: the light and shadow of you gods are lofty indeed — why do you bend your minds to make offerings to these corpses?

The gods said: these corpses are our former bodies — and they set out the whole matter. By this slight merit we obtained birth in heaven, and now we scatter flowers to repay the kindness of our former bodies. Making a stūpa and monastery in play brings merit like this; how much more if one serves the Buddha with an utmost mind — that virtue is beyond comparison. You gentlemen with your wrong views do not believe what is right and true, and toil bitterly for a hundred kalpas and get nothing at all. Better to go together to Mount Gṛdhrakūṭa and pay respect and make offerings, and obtain merit without limit.

So they all gladly went together to where the Buddha was, and prostrated themselves and scattered flowers in offering. The gods said to the Buddha: we, lately in the bodies of monkeys, by the World-Honored One’s grace obtained birth in heaven, and regretted that we had not seen the Buddha, and now come of ourselves for refuge. And they said further: what offending conduct had we in a former life, that we received the bodies of monkeys; and though we made a stūpa and monastery, our bodies were swept away and drowned?

The Buddha told the gods: it does not arise from nothing. In a past age five hundred young brahmins went together into the mountains wishing to seek the way of the immortals. At that time on the mountain there was a śramaṇa who wished to plaster and repair a monastery on the mountain, and who went down into the valley to fetch water, his body light as though flying; and the five hundred brahmins, giving rise to jealousy, laughed at him with one voice: this śramaṇa goes up and down as quick and light as a monkey — what is there wonderful in it? And he went on fetching water like this without stopping, and the mountain water came down at once and it was not long before they were drowned.

The Buddha told the gods: the śramaṇa going up and down at that time is myself; the five hundred young brahmins are the five hundred monkeys. Laughing in play they made an offense and their bodies received the recompense of it. And the Buddha spoke a verse:

“Doing evil in laughter and play, / one receives the recompense with weeping and cries.”

The five hundred gods, hearing what the Buddha said, at once obtained the traces of the path. And the five hundred brahmins, hearing of the recompense of offense and merit, sighed to themselves: we have studied for the immortals for years on end and have not received any fruit; it is not equal to the monkeys who, laughing in play, made merit and obtained birth in heaven. The Buddha’s virtue is truly marvellous. And they bowed their heads at the Buddha’s feet and asked to be his disciples. The Buddha said: come, monks — and they became śramaṇas at once and, applying themselves, attained the path (Source: Dharmapada Sūtra, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The structure here is a double reversal and the compendium sets it out carefully. Five hundred monkeys imitate the arhats' stūpa-building in play, are drowned, and wake in heaven; they come back down to thank their own corpses; and the five hundred brahmin ascetics who see them ask why gods should honour dead monkeys. The gods' answer is a sermon, and the brahmins are converted by it.

Then the Buddha supplies the other half: those five hundred monkeys were the five hundred young brahmins who once laughed at a water-carrying śramaṇa for being quick and light as a monkey, and drowned in the mountain flood not long after. The two halves make one proposition, stated in the two-line verse: what is done in laughter and play is repaid in weeping.

The monkey-comparison as the cause of monkey rebirths recurs in entry 29 of this same fascicle, where a young śramaṇa says the same thing to an arhat and spends five hundred lives as a monkey for it. The compendium prints both without cross-reference.

34. A Hare King Attaches Himself to a Religious Man, Throws Himself into a Fire, and Is Born in the Tuṣita Heaven

A hare king whose company brings fruit to a reciting sage, and who, when winter comes and the fruit is gone, finds something else to offer.

Chinese Source

昔有兔王遊在山中。與群輩俱食果飲水。行四等心慈悲喜護。教諸眷屬悉令仁和勿為眾惡。畢脫此身得為人形。可受道教。時諸眷屬歡喜。從教不敢違命。有一仙人處在林樹。食果飲水獨修道行。未曾遊放建四梵行。慈悲喜護誦經念道。音聲通利其音和雅聞莫不欣。於時兔王往附近之。聽其誦經意中欣踊不以為厭。與諸眷屬共齎果苽供養道人。如是積日經歷年月。時冬寒至。仙人欲還到於人間。兔王見之愁憂不樂。心懷戀恨不欲令捨。問何所趣。在此日日相見以為娛樂。飢渴忘食如依父母。願留莫去。仙人報曰。吾有四大當慎將護。今冬寒至果苽已盡。山水氷凍又無巖窟可以居止。故欲捨去依處人間。分衛求食頓止精舍。過此冬寒當復相就。勿以悒悒。兔王答曰。吾等眷屬當行求果。遠近募索當相給足。願一屈意愍傷見濟。假使捨去憂慼之戀或不自全。設使今日無有供具。便以我身供上道人。道人見之感惟哀念。愍之至心當奈之何。仙人事火前有生炭。兔王心念。道人為我是以默然便自舉身投於火中。火大熾盛道人欲救。尋已命過生兜術天。於菩薩身功德特尊威神巍巍。仙人見之。為道德故不惜身命。愍傷憐之亦自剋責。絕穀不食。尋時遷神處兜率天。佛言。時兔王者則我是。仙人者定光佛是(出生經第三第四卷又出兔王經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a hare king who roamed in the mountains and with his company ate fruit and drank water, and practiced the four states of mind — loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity — and taught his retinue to be humane and harmonious and to do no evil, so that when they were finally rid of these bodies and obtained human form they might receive the teaching of the path; and his retinue rejoiced and followed the teaching and dared not go against his word.

There was a sage dwelling in the trees of the wood, eating fruit and drinking water and cultivating the practice of the path alone, who had never wandered loose and had established the four Brahmā practices — loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity — and who recited the sūtras and kept the path in mind; and his voice was clear and fluent and its sound harmonious and refined, and none who heard it was not glad.

Then the hare king went and drew near him and listened to his recitation of the sūtras, and his mind leapt within him and he never tired of it; and with his retinue he brought fruit and melons and made offerings to the religious man, and so it went on day after day for months and years.

Then the cold of winter came and the sage wished to go back among people; and the hare king, seeing it, was grieved and unhappy, and his heart was full of longing and reluctance and he did not wish him to leave, and asked where he was going: here we see one another day by day and it is our delight, and hungry and thirsty we forget to eat, as though depending on father and mother. I ask you to stay and not go.

The sage answered: I have these four great elements and must carefully guard and protect them. Now the cold of winter has come and the fruits and melons are used up; the mountain waters are frozen and there is no cave to dwell in. And so I wish to leave and depend on the human world, to beg my food and lodge at a monastery. When this winter cold is past I shall come to you again. Do not be downcast.

The hare king answered: we, your retinue, shall go and look for fruit, and shall seek far and near and supply you with enough. I ask you to bend your mind once and take pity and save us. If you go away, our grief and longing may be more than we can bear. And if today there is nothing to set out for you, then let me offer my own body to the religious man.

The religious man, seeing him, was moved and thought with pity: I pity his utmost mind — what is to be done about it? The sage tended a fire and there were live coals before him; and the hare king thought: the religious man keeps silence for my sake — and lifted his own body and threw himself into the fire. The fire blazed up fiercely and the religious man wished to save him, and at once his life passed and he was born in the Tuṣita heaven, and in a bodhisattva’s body his merit was especially honoured and his majesty lofty.

The sage, seeing it — that for the sake of the virtue of the path he had not begrudged his body and life — pitied and grieved for him and reproached himself, and cut off grain and did not eat; and presently his spirit removed and he too was in the Tuṣita heaven.

The Buddha said: the hare king of that time is myself; the sage is the Buddha Dīpaṃkara (Source: Jātaka Sūtra, third and fourth fascicles; also the Sūtra of the Hare King).

36. A Rat Saves the Life of the King of Vaiśālī

The rat that woke a sleeping king twice, and the village and the elder that were named after it.

Chinese Source

佛言。迦蘭陀者是山鼠名。時毘舍離王。將諸伎女入山遊戲。王時疲惓眠一樹下。伎女左右四散走戲。時樹下窟中有大毒蛇。聞王酒氣出欲螫王。樹上有鼠從上來下鳴喚覺王。蛇即還縮。王覺已復眠。蛇又更出。鼠復鳴喚下來覺王。王起見大毒蛇。即生驚怖。求諸伎女又復不見。王自念言。我今得活。由鼠之恩。思惟欲報。時山邊有村。即命村中。自今以後我之祿限悉迴供鼠。因此鼠故即號此村迦蘭陀也。是時村中有一長者。有金錢四十億。王即賜長者位。因此村名故號迦蘭陀長者(出善見律毘婆沙第六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said: Kalandaka is the name of a mountain rat. At that time the king of Vaiśālī took his entertainers into the mountains to amuse himself, and the king grew weary and slept beneath a tree, and the entertainers scattered to left and right and played about.

In a hole beneath the tree there was a great venomous snake, which smelled the wine on the king and came out meaning to bite him; and there was a rat in the tree which came down from above and cried out and woke the king, and the snake drew back at once. When the king woke he slept again, and the snake came out once more, and the rat cried out again and came down and woke the king.

The king rose and saw the great venomous snake and was greatly frightened, and looked for his entertainers and did not see them; and the king thought to himself: that I am alive now is by the rat’s kindness. Thinking to repay it — there was a village at the foot of the mountain, and he gave order to that village that from now on the whole of his revenue there should be turned over to provide for the rats; and on account of this rat the village was named Kalandaka.

At that time there was in that village an elder with four thousand million gold coins, and the king conferred on him the rank of elder; and on account of the village’s name he was called the elder Kalandaka (Source: Samantapāsādikā, fascicle 6).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The point of the entry is the naming, and it is a piece of place-lore rather than a moral. The Kalandaka Bamboo Grove at Rājagṛha — the Veṇuvana, the first monastery given to the Buddha's order, which appears throughout this compendium as the setting for other stories — takes its name from the squirrels or mountain rats of the place; and this entry explains the name by way of a king asleep under a tree, a venomous snake, and a rat that came down twice.

The chain of naming is worth following: the rat saves the king, the king turns his revenue there over to feeding rats, the village is named Kalandaka after the rat, and the local elder is called the elder Kalandaka after the village. This closes fascicle 47.
