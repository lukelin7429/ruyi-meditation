Fascicle 21 · 經律異相卷第二十一(聲聞現行惡行僧部第十) · Complete

Devadatta and the Broken Saṅgha

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 21 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Śrāvakas Who Committed Misdeeds, Part 10" (聲聞現行惡行僧部第十) — twelve entries devoted almost entirely to Devadatta, the Buddha's own cousin and the compendium's central figure of monastic betrayal, together with his ally Kokālika and the separate, equally severe case of Sunakṣatra. Among them: the past-life origin of Devadatta's enmity stretching back countless lifetimes, his attempt to have the Buddha trampled by a drunk elephant, four animal-fable past lives — a scheming fox, a stubborn jackal, a reckless monkey, a golden lion who forgives his own killer — and the fascicle's grim final measure of comparative punishment across four monks in four adjoining hells.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 21 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 21 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 21 gathers twelve entries under a heading naming monks "who committed misdeeds" (現行惡行) — a sharp turn from the trainees and near-adepts of the two preceding fascicles to outright antagonists of the Buddha's own teaching. Ten of the twelve entries concern Devadatta specifically (given here under both of his common Chinese renderings, 調達 and 提婆達多, both restored to the single English name Devadatta throughout); the drunk-elephant episode in entry 2 is a second, independently sourced telling of a story translated earlier in this compendium at fascicle 5, and we note the doublet rather than merge the two accounts. Every entry's full text remains intact.

1. The Origin of Devadatta's Enmity

When the monks ask why Devadatta's hatred of the Buddha runs so deep, the Buddha traces it back to a single act of theft in a past life: an elder brahmin scholar's marriage promise broken in favor of a younger, more brilliant rival — and a vow of enmity sworn to outlast every future lifetime.

Chinese Source

爾時調達。心念毒害誹謗如來。自謂有道。眾人呵之。天龍鬼神釋梵四王。悉共曉喻。卿欲毀佛。由如舉手欲擲日月。調達聞之。其心不改。時諸比丘。具以啟佛。調達有何重嫌懷結乃爾。佛告諸比丘。調達不但今生。世世如是。過去世時。有梵志女。端正殊妙色像第一。諸梵志法假使處女與明經者。請諸同學五百之眾。供養三月察其所知。時五百人中。一人博達。而年朽耄面醜眼青。父母愁憂。女亦懷惱。云何當為此人作婦。可以怨鬼。當奈之何。於時遠方有一梵志。年既幼少。顏貌殊好。聰明智慧。聞彼梵志請諸同學欲處於女。尋時往詣。難問諸梵志等。皆窮無辭。五百之眾智皆不及。時女父母及女。見之皆大歡喜。吾求女婿今乃獲願。年尊梵志曰。吾年既老久許我。女以為我妻。且以暇我所得施遺。悉用與卿。傷我年高。勿相毀辱。年少答曰。不可越法以從人情。我應納之。三月畢竟。即以處女用與年少。其年老者。心懷毒惡。即相毀辱而奪我婦。世世所在與卿作怨。終不相置。年少梵志常行慈心。彼獨懷害。佛告諸比丘。爾時年尊梵志。今調達是。年少梵志。我身是也。其女者瞿夷是(出生經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Devadatta's heart was fixed on malice, slandering the Tathāgata, while claiming for himself possession of the path. The assembly rebuked him; the devas, dragons, spirits, Śakra, Brahmā, and the Four Heavenly Kings all together tried to reason with him: your wish to destroy the Buddha is like raising your own hand to hurl down the sun and moon. Devadatta, hearing this, would not change his heart.

The monks reported the whole matter fully to the Buddha: what grave grievance could Devadatta hold, that his resentment runs so deep? The Buddha told the monks: it is not only in this present life that Devadatta has been so — he has been thus in life after life. In a past age, there was a brahmin's daughter, of matchless beauty and form. Among the brahmins it was the custom that, should an unmarried daughter be promised to whichever scholar proved most learned in the scriptures, all his fellow students, five hundred in number, would be invited, and given three months' support, so that their own knowledge might be observed and compared.

Among these five hundred men, one was indeed deeply learned, but old and withered, his face ugly, his eyes a strange pale color; her parents grieved, and the daughter herself was troubled — how could she become the wife of such a man, one so like a resentful ghost to look upon? What was to be done?

At that time, from a distant land, there came a young brahmin, still youthful, of fine appearance, clever and wise. Hearing that this brahmin had invited his fellow students to compete for his daughter's hand, he went there at once, and posed questions the assembled brahmins could not answer — questions beyond the wisdom of the whole five hundred. The girl's parents and the girl herself, seeing him, rejoiced greatly: we sought a son-in-law, and now our wish is granted at last.

The elder brahmin said: I am already old, and was long ago promised this daughter as my own wife; whatever I might otherwise have given you, I shall give you now instead — but do not wound my own dignity, so advanced in years, by shaming me before all. The younger brahmin answered: I cannot overstep what is proper merely to satisfy another's feelings — I am bound to accept her as promised. When the three months had passed, the unmarried daughter was given to the younger man.

The elder, his heart now filled with venomous malice, said: you have shamed and humiliated me, and stolen my own promised wife — in every life to come, wherever you are born, I shall be your enemy, and never let this rest. The younger brahmin, for his own part, always acted with a heart of loving-kindness; only the elder alone harbored this wish to harm.

The Buddha told the monks: could that elder brahmin of that time be anyone other than Devadatta now? And that younger brahmin was myself. And that daughter was Gopī (Source: Sūtra of Past Lives, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Worth noticing: the younger brahmin's own conduct in this founding story is entirely blameless by the terms the text itself sets out — he does nothing but answer questions correctly and accept what custom already promised him. The enmity that follows Devadatta and the Buddha across lifetimes is thus framed, from its very origin, as one-sided: a grievance sustained entirely by the aggrieved party's own refusal to accept a fair outcome, never as a genuine wrong requiring correction on the other side.

2. The Drunk Elephant Nālāgiri, Told Again

Devadatta persuades King Ajātaśatru to loose a herd of drunken elephants on the Buddha as he enters the city — a plot the Buddha calmly foresees and disarms with nothing more than his own raised hand. The Buddha then traces Devadatta's enmity back through a jātaka of a wild goose king who forgives the very hunter who trapped him.

Chinese Source

調達與阿闍世王。共議毀佛及諸弟子。王勅國人不得奉佛。時舍利弗等。及波和提比丘尼等。各將弟子去到他國。唯佛與五百羅漢。住崛山中。調達至王所言。佛諸弟子今已迸散。尚有五百弟子在佛左右。願王明日請佛入城。吾當飲五百大象。使醉令蹋殺之。吾當作佛教化世間。王聞歡喜。即往請佛。佛知其謀。答言。大善。王退而去。還報調達。明日食時。佛與羅漢共入城門。醉象鳴鼻而前。唐突牆壁。樹木折敗一城戰慄。五百羅漢飛在空中。獨有尊者阿難在邊。醉象齊頭徑前趣佛。佛舉五指為五師子。同聲俱吼。震動天地。醉象伏地不敢舉頭。醉解垂淚悔過。王及臣民莫不敬肅。世尊徐前至王殿上。與諸羅漢食訖呪願。王白佛言。稟性不明。信彼讒言。興造逆惡。願垂大慈。恕我迷愚。佛告阿闍世及諸大眾。世有八事。興長誹謗。皆由名譽以致大罪。何等為八。利衰毀譽稱譏苦樂。自古至今尠不為惑。佛即說偈(文多不載)佛曰。昔有國王。喜食雁肉。常遣獵師。張網捕雁。日送一雁。以供王食。時有雁王。將五百雁飛下求食。雁王墮網為獵師所得。餘雁驚飛徘徊不去。時有一雁連翻追隨。不避弓矢悲鳴吐血。晝夜不息。獵師見之。感憐其義。即放雁王令相隨去。群雁得王歡喜迴繞。爾時獵師。具以聞王。王感其義。斷不捕雁。時雁王者。我身是也。一雁者阿難是也。五百群雁。今五百羅漢是也。食雁國王者。今大王是也。時獵師者。今調達是前世已來。恒欲害我。我以大慈之力。因而得濟。不念怨惡。自致得佛。王及群臣。莫不歡喜(出法句經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Devadatta conspired together with King Ajātaśatru to destroy the Buddha and his disciples; the king commanded the people of his own realm not to honor the Buddha. At this, Śāriputra and the others, together with the nun Bōhétí and her own disciples, each departed for other lands, and only the Buddha, with five hundred arhats, remained dwelling on Vulture Peak.

Devadatta went to the king and said: the Buddha's own disciples have now scattered, but five hundred still remain at his side. I ask you, tomorrow, to invite the Buddha into the city — I shall have five hundred great elephants made drunk, and let them trample him to death; I myself shall then become the Buddha, and teach the world in his place. The king, delighted to hear this, went at once to invite the Buddha. The Buddha, knowing the whole plot already, answered simply: very good. The king withdrew, and reported this back to Devadatta.

The next day, at mealtime, the Buddha entered the city gate together with the arhats. The drunken elephants came roaring forward, trunks raised, crashing recklessly against walls, snapping trees, the whole city trembling in fear. The five hundred arhats rose into the sky and flew away, leaving only the venerable Ānanda at the Buddha's own side. The drunken elephants, heads lowered together, charged straight for the Buddha. The Buddha raised his own five fingers, and they became five lions, roaring together in a single voice that shook heaven and earth; the drunken elephants fell prostrate to the ground, not daring to raise their heads, their drunkenness dispelled, weeping in repentance. The king, his ministers, and all the people were struck with reverence and awe.

The World-Honored One walked calmly forward to the king's own palace hall, and, having eaten together with the arhats, spoke a dedication of blessing. The king said to the Buddha: my own nature is unclear and confused; I believed slanderous words, and gave rise to rebellious wrongdoing — I beg your great compassion to forgive my own delusion and folly.

The Buddha told Ajātaśatru and the whole assembly: there are eight things in this world that give rise to lasting slander, all stemming from the pursuit of reputation, and leading to grave transgression. What are these eight? Gain and loss, disgrace and honor, praise and blame, suffering and pleasure — from ancient times to the present, few indeed are those untouched by their confusion. The Buddha then spoke a verse (the fuller text is not recorded here).

The Buddha said: long ago there was a king who loved to eat the flesh of wild geese, and constantly sent hunters to spread nets and catch them, delivering one goose each day to supply the royal table. There was, at that time, a goose king, who led five hundred geese flying down to seek food; the goose king fell into the net, and was caught by the hunter. The other geese, startled, flew up in alarm, but would not leave, circling about; one goose in particular followed close behind, again and again, undeterred by bow or arrow, crying out in grief until it wept blood, ceaseless day and night. The hunter, seeing this, was moved by such devotion, and released the goose king to rejoin his companions; the flock, overjoyed to have their king restored, circled about him in celebration. The hunter reported this fully to the king, who, moved likewise by such devotion, forbade the capture of geese from that day on.

The Buddha said: that goose king of that time was myself; that one devoted goose was Ānanda; the flock of five hundred geese are these five hundred arhats now; the goose-eating king is this present great king; and that hunter is Devadatta — who, since past lives without number, has constantly wished to harm me, yet, through the power of my own great compassion, has again and again been saved instead, bearing no thought of enmity or hatred, until I myself attained buddhahood. The king and all his ministers were filled with joy at this (Source: Dharmapada, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a second, independently sourced telling of the drunk-elephant story translated earlier in this compendium's fascicle 5, under the elephant's proper name Nālāgiri and drawn from a different source text there. As with other doublets in this anthology, we let the two tellings stand side by side rather than harmonize their details — here the parallel is worth noting because the two accounts frame the same event differently: fascicle 5 treats it primarily as a display of the Buddha's own power to benefit beings, while this fascicle frames the identical episode explicitly around Devadatta's culpability as its instigator.

3. Learned, Powerful, and Hungry for Honor

A brilliant, disciplined monk's decades of learning curdle into resentment when the Buddha withholds the teaching of supernormal power — and, having finally learned it from Ānanda, Devadatta uses it not to teach but to charm a young prince with shapeshifting tricks, trading his own dignity for a steady supply of royal offerings.

Chinese Source

昔有比丘。名曰調達。聰明廣學。十二年中坐禪入定心不移易。十二頭陀初不缺減。起不淨觀。了出入息。世間第一法。乃至頂法一一分別。所誦佛經六萬。象載不勝。後意轉退漸生惡念。望人供養著世利養。至世尊所頭面禮足。在一面立前白佛言。唯然世尊願說神足之道。我聞此已當善修行。使我得神足已。遊至他方處處教化。世尊告曰。汝今且置神足。何不學四。非常義。苦義空義無我之義。是時調達比丘。便生此念。如來所以不與我說神足者。恐有勝己恥在不如。調達即捨如來。往舍利弗所。求神足道。時舍利弗謂調達曰。汝今且置。何不修四非常。調達思惟。此舍利弗智慧第一。如吾觀之猶如螢火比於日月。吾所誦習無與等者。猶尚不解神足之道。況舍利弗豈能解乎。即便捨去。至目連所。求神足道。目連語曰。止止調達。始行之人先學四非常。復當精修四禪。爾乃得神足道耳。調達恚怒。此目連者自誇神足無與等者。所以不與我者。恐其不如我。若得者己無名譽。吾今處處學神足道。皆不教我。吾弟阿難多聞博學。眾德具足吾今當往問之。語阿難曰。吾聞卿善解神足之道。可與吾說。吾得神足已遊至他方。處處教化。是時阿難便與說之。調達聞已。在閑靜處專心一意。以麁入微復從微起還至於麁。以心舉身以身舉心。身心俱合漸漸離地。初如胡麻轉如胡桃。漸離於地從地至床。從床至屋從屋至空。在虛空中作十八變。涌沒自由。化作嬰孩小兒。形貌端正。頭上五處面如桃華。在阿闍世太子膝上。或笑或號現嬰兒態。然太子知是調達身。終日翫弄無有厭足。或嗚嗽唾。或擎其身傳左右手。太子思惟。調達神足勝彼瞿曇。能作無數變化。阿闍世日給五百釜食。隨時供養不令有乏。爾時眾多比丘。見阿闍世供給調達。具白世尊。佛告諸比丘。汝等勿貪調達供養。調達自陷亦陷他人。二俱墮罪。如芭蕉樹。愚人求實不能剋獲。竹蘆亦然。駏驉懷妊二命俱喪。昔有群鷲各各孚乳。鷲告其雛曰。汝若學飛懸在虛空。見地如槃慎勿上過。有隨藍風傷害於汝。頭腦支節各在異處。時雛不隨父教。飛越過量為風所吹喪命異處。汝等比丘勿興斯意。比丘當知猶如群龜。告語諸子。汝等自護莫至某處。彼有獵者備獲汝身分為五分。時諸龜子不隨其語。便至某處共相歡娛。便為獵者所獲。或有安隱還得歸者。龜問其子汝從何來。子報父母。我等相將至彼處觀。不見獵者。唯覩長綖而追我後。龜語其子。此綖逐汝由來久矣。先祖父母皆由此綖而致喪亡。諸比丘當知。猶如蠱狐晝夜伺求大便。畜獸屎糞已自食訖。復自大便。調達比丘。貪致供養亦復如是。昔大月支國。風俗常儀要當蘇煎麥食猪。時官馬駒謂其母曰。我等與王致力不計遠近。皆赴其命。然食以草芻。飲以潦水。馬告其子。汝等慎勿興此意羨彼蘇煎麥也。如是不久自當現驗。時逼節會新歲垂至。家家縛猪投於鑊湯。舉聲㘁喚。馬母告子。汝等頗憶蘇煎麥不。欲知證驗可往觀之。諸馬駒等知之審然。方知煎御情分。食草時復遇麥。護而不噉。佛說偈言。芭蕉以實死　竹蘆實亦然　駏驉坐妊身　士以貪自喪(出出耀經第十卷)

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a monk named Devadatta, clever and widely learned. For twelve years he sat in meditation, his mind unmoved from absorption; he never once fell short in the twelve ascetic practices, cultivated the contemplation of impurity, mastered the counting of the breath, achieved the highest worldly dharma, and, one by one, distinguished even the summit dharmas. The Buddhist scriptures he had memorized numbered sixty thousand — more than an elephant could carry. Yet, in time, his own resolve turned and weakened, and a malicious thought gradually took root: a longing for others' offerings, an attachment to worldly gain.

He went before the World-Honored One, bowed at his feet, and, standing to one side, said: World-Honored One, I beg you to teach me the way of supernormal power — having heard it, I shall practice it diligently, and, once I have attained such power, travel to other lands, teaching in every place. The World-Honored One told him: set aside supernormal power for now — why not instead study the four truths of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self? Devadatta then thought to himself: the reason the Tathāgata will not teach me this must be that he fears I might surpass him, and feels shame at being outdone.

Devadatta then abandoned the Tathāgata, and went to Śāriputra, seeking the way of supernormal power. Śāriputra told him likewise: set that aside for now — why not cultivate the four truths of impermanence instead? Devadatta reflected: this Śāriputra, said to be foremost in wisdom — yet, as I see it, he is no more than a firefly set against the sun and moon; what I myself have already memorized has no equal, and even I do not yet understand the way of supernormal power — how much less could Śāriputra understand it? — and left him as well, going to Maudgalyāyana, seeking the same. Maudgalyāyana told him: stop, stop, Devadatta — one just beginning the practice must first study the four truths of impermanence, and further cultivate the four dhyānas with diligence; only then can the way of supernormal power be attained.

Devadatta, enraged, thought: this Maudgalyāyana boasts that his own supernormal power has no equal — the reason he will not teach me must be his fear that I would surpass him, leaving him without renown of his own. I have sought the way of supernormal power everywhere now, and none will teach me — but my own younger brother Ānanda is learned and possessed of every virtue; I shall go and ask him instead. He said to Ānanda: I have heard that you understand well the way of supernormal power — teach it to me; once I have attained it, I shall travel to other lands, teaching in every place. Ānanda then taught it to him in full.

Devadatta, having learned this, went to a quiet place, and, with his mind entirely focused, moved from the coarse into the subtle, and from the subtle back to the coarse again, raising his own body through his mind and his mind through his body, until body and mind together were unified, and he rose gradually from the ground — at first no higher than a sesame seed, then a walnut, gradually further from the earth, from the ground to the height of a bed, from a bed to the height of a house, from a house into open sky, and, in the open sky, performed the eighteen transformations, rising and vanishing freely at will.

He then transformed himself into a small infant, of fine and lovely form, its head marked in five places, its face like a peach blossom, and appeared upon the knee of Prince Ajātaśatru, laughing or crying, showing every trait of a true infant — though the prince knew all the while that this was Devadatta's own body, he played with it all day long without ever tiring, sometimes kissing and cooing at it, sometimes lifting it, passing it from one hand to the other. The prince thought to himself: Devadatta's own supernormal power surpasses even that of Gautama himself, able to work countless transformations — and gave him five hundred pots of food each day, offering support continually, never letting him want for anything.

At that time, many monks, seeing Ajātaśatru's own offerings to Devadatta, reported this fully to the World-Honored One. The Buddha told the monks: do not covet Devadatta's own offerings. Devadatta destroys himself, and destroys others besides — both alike fall into transgression through this.

It is like the plantain tree: a foolish man seeking true fruit within it can never obtain it; the bamboo reed is just the same. It is like the mule that conceives — both mother and offspring alike perish. Long ago there was a flock of vultures, each raising its own young; a mother vulture told her chick: should you learn to fly, suspended in the open sky, and see the earth below no larger than a plate, take great care never to fly beyond that point — there is a fierce wind there, called Vairambha, that will surely harm you, scattering your own head and limbs to different places. The chick did not heed its father's warning, flew beyond that measure, and was blown apart by the wind, its own life lost, its body scattered.

You monks should give rise to no such wish as this. Know too, monks, that it is like a group of turtles, whose mother told her own children: guard yourselves well, and do not go to a certain place — there are hunters there, who will catch you and divide your own bodies into five parts. The young turtles did not heed her words, went to that place regardless, and enjoyed themselves together there, and were indeed caught by the hunters; yet some managed to return safely. Their mother asked them: where have you come from? The children answered: we went together to look at that place; we saw no hunter at all — only a long cord that trailed behind, pursuing us. The mother told her children: that cord has pursued your own kind since long ago — your own grandparents, before you, all met their deaths through that very cord.

Monks, know too that it is like a fox-demon that, day and night, searches out excrement — feeding on the dung of beasts, and, having eaten its fill, producing its own excrement in turn. Devadatta's own greed for offerings is just the same.

Long ago, in the kingdom of the Great Yuezhi, it was the local custom always to fatten pigs on wheat cooked in butter before slaughtering them. At that time a royal foal said to its own mother: we serve the king with all our strength, regardless of distance, answering every one of his commands — yet we are fed nothing but hay, and given only stagnant water to drink. The mother horse told her foal: take care never to give rise to envy of that buttered wheat — before long, you shall see the proof of this for yourself.

As the New Year's festival drew near, every household bound its own pig and cast it into a cauldron of boiling water, its cries rising in the air. The mother horse told her foal: do you remember that buttered wheat now? If you wish to know the proof of what I said, go and see for yourself. The young horses, seeing this, understood the truth of it at once, and knew thereafter the true difference between being fattened for slaughter and being kept for service; and when, afterward, they were fed hay mixed with wheat, they guarded themselves and would not eat it.

The Buddha spoke this verse:

"The plantain tree dies once it bears its own fruit; / the bamboo reed is just the same. / The mule, once it conceives, / and the man, through his own greed, alike bring about their own ruin."

(Source: Sūtra of Illustrative Parables [Chūyào jīng], fascicle 10).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Six separate analogies pile up in this single entry — plantain, bamboo, mule, vulture chick, turtle hatchlings, dung-eating fox-demon, fattened pig — an unusually dense concentration even for this compendium's fondness for jātaka-style illustration, and it is worth asking why. Each analogy isolates a slightly different failure mode (false hope of substance, self-destructive conception, ignored warning, unseen pursuit, self-perpetuating appetite, comfort mistaken for favor), suggesting the compilers regarded Devadatta's decline not as a single moral lapse but as a whole cluster of related traps, none reducible to the others.

I shall teach and guide them exactly as the Buddha himself would, and continue offering the four requisites without fail.

The Buddha said: enough, foolish man — you give no thought to what disaster shall follow! Even Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana have never been entrusted with such a charge — how much less could it be entrusted to one as indolent and depraved as you, that the noble assembly should be handed over to you?

Devadatta, jealousy rising within him, grew doubly furious at these words, and thought: the Tathāgata today praises Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana, while holding this lesser disciple in contempt — I shall surely find a means to destroy both teacher and disciples alike, so that the beings of this whole land shall neither see his form nor hear his voice again. Devadatta then rose from his seat, bowed, and withdrew, going about everywhere with cunning and deceitful words, beguiling ordinary people, and won over several dozen of them, urging them on from place to place.

In brief: while the Buddha sat teaching, surrounded by the great assembly, Devadatta told his own followers: do not heed what Gautama teaches — it does not accord with the true Dharma. I myself hold, one by one, profound scriptures of excellent meaning, and shall teach you these instead — seeking only some means to destroy the noble assembly. The Buddha said: stop, stop, Devadatta — take care never to give rise to such a wish to destroy the harmony of the assembly; the retribution that follows shall be difficult indeed to bear. But Devadatta held fast, and would not change, his intent fixed. The Buddha, observing the past causes and old enmity behind this, knew it could not be turned aside.

Devadatta led five hundred followers of his own, just as the Tathāgata too kept company with five hundred disciples, both groups traveling near Ratnakūṭa mountain. The bodhisattva's own followers were broad-minded, benevolent, and gentle, taught the true Dharma, and upheld the precepts, their every movement, in and out, never overstepping proper order. Devadatta's own followers, by contrast, spoke coarsely and harshly, quick to anger in every exchange, quarreling with their own teacher as though with an enemy at law. His disciples, wearied and troubled by this, all abandoned him together, and went instead to the bodhisattva's own side.

Devadatta, for his part, boasted: I have a thousand disciples, every virtue complete among them, unmatched and incomparable in this world — who could possibly equal me? Enraged when they left him, he swore this vow: this man has today lured away my own disciples and destroyed my following — even were he to attain buddhahood himself, I would destroy his own followers just the same, without exception, even then.

The Tathāgata, observing this, knew with certainty that Devadatta would indeed try to destroy the harmony of the noble assembly, and so, rising from his seat, withdrew and left the gathering. There are five reasons the assembly could never truly be destroyed: first, so long as the Tathāgata is present, his own majestic spiritual power never abandons his original vow; second, after the Tathāgata's final nirvāṇa, should anyone then claim, "I have now attained buddhahood, the highest true awakening," he ought to be asked: where were you when Śākyamuni Buddha himself still lived? — third, there has never yet been such an evil time; fourth, monks do not compete over material offerings; and fifth, the disciples of wisdom and supernormal power remain in harmony together. The Tathāgata, through his own knowledge of past lives, saw with certainty that Devadatta would attempt to disrupt the assembly, and so withdrew and departed.

Devadatta, remaining behind, taught the assembly: whoever among you regards me as your own honored teacher, and receives my own instruction, should practice these five methods (the full text of the five methods is not recorded here) — any monk who cultivates these five methods shall swiftly attain liberation, exhausting every outflow and attaining the undefiled state; what need is there of Gautama's own eightfold noble path?

He then said to Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana: I have gained great benefit, becoming honored throughout the three realms, in the very image of the Tathāgata himself. He told them further: my own back aches, and I wish to rest a while and sleep — you two shall teach the noble assembly in my place. Devadatta then lay down upon his right side, wishing to sleep; a deva, urging him further, made him turn instead to lie upon his left side, and that same deva, mocking him still further, caused him to speak aloud in his sleep, snoring loudly, a foul odor reaching far beyond him.

Maudgalyāyana, through the power of his own supernormal ability, rose into the open sky and performed the eighteen transformations, sitting, lying down, walking, rising and vanishing freely at will. Śāriputra told the assembled crowd: the Tathāgata's own body and spiritual virtue are boundless, complete in every wisdom, reaching forward without limit and back without end. The Tathāgata's own Dharma brings, in this very life, the fruit of true and effortless joy, studied by the wise, not practiced by the foolish. The Tathāgata's own noble assembly, its five-part Dharma-body entirely accomplished, is worthy of reverence and honor, worthy of service and offering, a true field of blessing and support for all beings.

The monks, hearing this, each thought to themselves: we ourselves have been foolish and deluded, unable to recognize what is truly correct, abandoning substance for mere flowering ornament, forsaking the root to chase after the branch — now, seeing what these two worthy ones have taught, so rare in this world, would it not be far better for us to abandon Devadatta, and return instead to the Tathāgata's own assembly? Śāriputra, knowing their own thoughts, rose at once from his seat, and all five hundred monks followed him together; Maudgalyāyana followed close behind.

The monk Kokālika, kicking Devadatta with his own right foot, said: wretched Devadatta, why do you lie there sleeping so soundly? Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana have led every one of your own disciples away! Devadatta, waking, was overcome with grief (Source: Sūtra of Devadatta Questioning the Buddha's Countenance; substantially the same account also appears in the Mahīśāsaka Vinaya).

Note the name here: 瞿波離 (Kokālika, a variant transliteration of 拘迦利 from entry 4) is the one who wakes Devadatta with the news of his own abandonment — the same ally who, in the preceding entry, was shown trading empty praise with him. The Buddha's own five stated reasons the Saṅgha cannot be destroyed are worth reading as an early articulation of institutional resilience: not the removal of any bad actor, but structural features — continuity of teaching authority, an unfalsifiable test for false claimants, absence of precedent, freedom from competition over material support, and internal harmony among the most capable disciples — that make schism self-limiting regardless of any one person's intent.

A fox who overhears a treatise on statecraft convinces himself he is fit to rule, and, through a chain of bluffs against ever-larger animals, actually becomes king of the beasts — until his own demand for a human princess's hand is undone by a single clever minister's trick.

Long ago in ancient times, there was a sage dwelling in a mountain cave, reciting texts of statecraft. A wild fox came near, and, listening closely to the recitation, understood something of what he heard, and thought to himself: if I truly understand what these texts say, I am fit to become king of all the beasts.

He set out at once, wandering, and came upon a thin, wretched fox, whom he intended to kill. The other said: why would you kill me? He answered: I am king of the beasts, and you have not yet submitted to me. The other said: please do not kill me — I shall follow and serve you instead. The two foxes then traveled on together, and met yet another fox, with the same exchange as before, and so, one after another, the fox brought every fox under his own submission. Using this pack of foxes, he then subdued every elephant; using the elephants, he subdued every tiger; using the tigers, he subdued every lion — and so, by these successive means, he came to be king of all the beasts.

The messenger returned and reported this to the king.

The king gathered his ministers to deliberate; all but one said the daughter should be given to him — our kingdom relies for its own defense on elephants and horses alone; though we too have elephants and horses, he has lions, and our own elephants and horses, catching their scent, will surely fall prostrate to the ground in terror — we cannot win this battle; why begrudge a single daughter, and lose an entire kingdom instead?

But one minister, sharp-witted and far-sighted, said to the king: I have observed, from ancient times to the present, that no human king's own daughter has ever been given to a lowly beast. King Ajātaśatru told him: you should leave this kingdom, and not remain here — all sixteen great kingdoms have already heard of the evil you have done against the Tathāgata. Devadatta, hearing this, was filled with grief and resentment, and, returning to his own home country, still bound by rage, forced his way into the bodhisattva's own palace, and said to Gopī: I intend to take you as my own chief consort — I wonder, noble lady, whether you might consent to this.

Gopī answered: come forward, then, and I shall take your own right hand. Devadatta extended his hand, and she gripped it so fiercely that the bones of his wrist were crushed and blood flowed from all five fingers; he fainted from the pain, and only after a long while did he recover.

Devadatta then pressed further into the palace, and seated himself upon the bodhisattva's own bed; the palace attendants, seeing this, seized him and threw him down from the bed, injuring his own left hip so badly that he could no longer walk, and had to be carried back to his own home on a litter. The whole Śākya clan, disgusted by this, came and told him: you ought now to go before the Buddha, and repent.

Devadatta, hearing this, secretly devised a cunning deception: he had iron claws made in private, coated with deadly poison, outwardly gentle in appearance while inwardly harboring rage. He recalled that the Buddha himself had often said: one whose body bears no wound cannot be harmed by poison — poison alone can do nothing without an open wound to enter through. He thought: I shall now go, feigning repentance, and, with these claws, tear open a wound upon his own foot — the poison will then spread through his body, and he will surely die of it.

He was carried on a litter toward the World-Honored One; when still some twenty-one paces away, he told his attendants: set me down upon the ground — I wish to walk the rest of the way myself. He was set down at once — and, in that very moment, a raging fire burst forth and wrapped itself entirely around his own body, and he was carried, still living, down into hell (Source: Sūtra of Devadatta's Living Body Falling into Hell).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Gopī's own response to Devadatta's proposal — a single crushing grip rather than any words of refusal — is one of the compendium's more physically direct depictions of a woman defending herself, and the narrative offers it without any further comment, as though her action requires no justification beyond itself. Devadatta's own final scheme is undone by the very safeguard he means to exploit: it is precisely his effort to create a wound that opens the way for the fire that consumes him instead, poison and precept operating, in this story's own logic, by parallel and equally literal rules.

8. The Jackal King and the Wooden Jar

A brief fable: a jackal king who scorns drinking from the ground insists on drinking from a pottery jar and smashing it afterward — until a brahmin, tired of losing jars, sets out an unbreakable wooden one instead, and waits with a club for the jackal who cannot resist his own habit.

Chinese Source

佛住王舍城。是時提婆達多。欲破和合僧。比丘三諫而亦不止。佛言。過去世時。波羅奈國。有一婆羅門。於曠野中造立義井。供給行者。日已向暮。有群野干來飲殘水。有野干王不飲地水。便內頭罐中飲水。飲水已戴罐高舉撲破。諸野干輩善意語之而不從受。如是非一破十四罐。時婆羅門伺見野干。便作木罐堅固難破。入易出難持著井邊。捉杖伺之。野干群集。王如前飲訖。撲地不能令破。時婆羅門捉杖來出。打殺野干。時野干王者。今提婆達多是。時群野干者。今諸比丘諫提婆達多者是(出僧祇律第八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Rājagṛha. At that time Devadatta wished to break the harmony of the Saṅgha; the monks admonished him three times, yet still he would not stop. The Buddha said: in a past age, in the country of Vārāṇasī, there lived a brahmin who built a public well in the open wilderness, to supply travelers with water.

As evening drew near one day, a pack of jackals came to drink the water left over. Their king, however, scorned to drink water from the ground, and instead put his own head into the pottery jar to drink; having drunk his fill, he would then raise the jar high and smash it. The other jackals, meaning well, spoke to him against this habit, but he would not listen — and so, not once but many times, he broke fourteen jars in this way.

The brahmin, watching for the jackal, then made a wooden jar instead, sturdy and hard to break, easy to put one's head into but hard to withdraw it from, and set it by the well, waiting nearby with a club in hand. The jackal pack gathered as before; their king, having drunk from this jar as he always did, tried to smash it against the ground, but could not break it. The brahmin then came forward with his club, and struck the jackal dead.

That jackal king of that time is Devadatta now; and that pack of jackals are these very monks who now admonish Devadatta (Source: Sāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 8).

9. The Monkeys and the Moon in the Well

A brief fable: a monkey king mistakes the moon's own reflection in a well for the moon itself fallen from the sky, and, in a well-meaning but poorly planned rescue by a chain of linked tails, drowns his entire troop along with himself.

Chinese Source

佛住王舍城。時諸比丘。為提婆達多作舉羯磨。六群比丘即同提婆達多同語同見。佛告比丘。過去世時。於空閑處。有五百獼猴遊行林中。到一尼俱律樹。樹下有井井有月影。時獼猴王見是月影。語諸伴言。月今死落乃在井中。當共出之莫令世間長夜闇冥。共作議言云何能出。時獼猴王言。我知出法。我捉樹枝汝捉我尾。展轉相連乃可出之。時諸獼猴即如王語。展轉相捉小未至水。連獼猴重樹弱枝折一切獼猴墮井水中。時獼猴王者。今提婆達多是。爾時餘獼猴者。今六群比丘是(出僧祇律第八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Rājagṛha. At that time the monks carried out a formal act of censure against Devadatta; the "group of six" monks aligned themselves entirely with Devadatta, sharing his own words and views. The Buddha told the monks: in a past age, in a secluded place, five hundred monkeys were traveling through a forest, and came to a nyagrodha tree, beneath which was a well, and in the well the reflection of the moon.

The monkey king, seeing this reflection, said to his companions: the moon has died and fallen, and now lies here in the well — we must together bring it out, lest the whole world be left in unending night's darkness. They discussed together how this might be done. The monkey king said: I know a way — I shall hold fast to a branch, and you hold my tail, and, each linking to the next in turn, we shall be able to draw it out.

The monkeys did exactly as their king instructed, each grasping the tail of the one before, but, before they had yet reached the water, the combined weight of the whole chain of monkeys proved too much for the branch, which was weak, and it broke — and every one of the monkeys fell into the well's own water. That monkey king of that time is Devadatta now; and those other monkeys are these very "group of six" monks now (Source: Sāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 8).

10. Firm-Vow, the Golden Lion Who Forgave His Killer

Fascicle 21's longest and most celebrated entry: a golden lion, wounded by a poisoned arrow, restrains his own instinct to retaliate the instant he sees his attacker wearing the ochre robe of a renunciant — choosing to die rather than harm what the robe itself represents, and is honored across an entire kingdom for it.

Chinese Source

佛告阿難。若有眾生起一惡心。向三世佛等。若辟支若羅漢及著染衣人諸沙門等。獲罪無量。所以者何。染色之衣是賢聖標式。若能發心敬染衣人。獲福難量。我由信心敬戴之故致得成佛。阿難白言。昔往敬心其事云何。佛告阿難。古昔無量劫。此閻浮提有大國(報恩經云波羅奈國)王名提毘領八萬四千諸小國王。世無佛法。有辟支佛。在於山間坐禪行道。時諸野狩咸來親附。有一師子。名號[跳-兆+荼]迦羅毘(梁言堅誓)軀體金色光相煥然。食菓噉草不害群生。時有獵師。剃頭著袈裟。內佩弓箭行於澤中。見師子睡眠(報恩經云舐比丘足)便以毒箭射之師子驚覺即欲馳害見著袈裟便自念言此染衣者善人標相。我若害之。則為惡心向諸賢聖。思惟還息。箭毒內行命在不久。便說偈言耶羅婆奢沙婆呵。說此語時。天地大動無雲雨血。諸天駭惕。即以天眼觀見。獵師殺於師子。雨諸天華供養其屍。是時獵師剝師子皮。奉提毘王。時王念言。經言。有狩金色必是菩薩。問獵師言。師子死時有何瑞應。答言如上。王聞是語。悲喜交懷信心益猛。即召諸臣耆舊智人。令解是義。時空林中有一仙人字奢摩。為王解說。耶羅其義。唯剔頭著染衣。當於生死疾得解脫。婆奢沙者。皆是賢聖之相近於涅槃。婆呵者。當為一切諸天世人所見敬仰。於時仙人解是語已。提毘歡喜。即召諸王悉集此處。作七寶車張師子皮。表示一切悉共敬戴。燒香散華而以供養。後復打金為棺。盛師子皮以用起塔。爾時師子。由發善心向染衣人。十億萬劫作轉輪聖王。佛告阿難。時師子者。我身是。時國王者今彌勒是。時仙人者今舍利弗是。時獵師者今提婆達多是(出賢愚經第十三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Ānanda: should any being give rise to even a single malicious thought toward the buddhas of the three times, or toward a pratyekabuddha, an arhat, or any śramaṇa clothed in the ochre robe, they incur boundless transgression. And why is this so? Because the ochre-dyed robe is itself the very emblem of the noble and the wise — and one who can, for this reason, arouse a heart of reverence toward those who wear it obtains blessing beyond all measure. It is through just such faithful reverence that I myself came, in time, to attain buddhahood.

Ānanda asked: what was this past reverence, and how did it come about? The Buddha told Ānanda: in ages past, beyond all counting, in this Jambudvīpa there was a great kingdom (the Sūtra of Repaying Kindness calls it Vārāṇasī), whose king, named Devī, ruled over eighty-four thousand lesser kings. In that age the world had no Buddhist teaching, yet a pratyekabuddha dwelt in the mountains, sitting in meditation and practicing the path, and all the wild creatures there came to feel affection for him.

Among them was a lion, named Firm-Vow, his body golden and radiant, who ate only fruit and grass, and harmed no living creature. A hunter, having shaved his own head and put on an ochre robe, carrying a bow and arrows hidden beneath it, walked through the wilderness, and, finding the lion asleep (the Sūtra of Repaying Kindness says he was licking a monk's own feet), shot him with a poisoned arrow.

The lion, startled awake, at first meant to rush at his attacker and kill him — but, seeing the ochre robe, thought to himself: this dyed robe is the very emblem of a good and virtuous man; were I to harm him, I would be turning a malicious heart against the noble and wise themselves. Restraining himself, he let his own anger subside; yet the poison, already spreading within him, meant his life could not last much longer. He then spoke this verse: yerā vaśā sabhāhā.

As he spoke these words, heaven and earth shook greatly, and blood rained down from a clear sky; the devas themselves were struck with terror, and, looking with their own divine eyes, saw that the hunter had killed the lion, and rained down heavenly flowers in offering upon his body.

The hunter then skinned the lion, and presented the hide to King Devī. The king, reflecting that the scriptures teach that any golden-colored beast must surely be a bodhisattva, asked the hunter: what auspicious signs appeared when the lion died? The hunter told him all that had happened. The king, hearing this, was overcome with mingled sorrow and joy, his own faith growing only stronger, and summoned his ministers, elders, and wise men, to have them explain the meaning of what the lion had spoken.

At that time, in a solitary forest, there lived a sage named Śama, who explained the verse's meaning to the king: "yerā" means that only by shaving the head and donning the dyed robe can one swiftly attain liberation from birth and death; "vaśā sabhā" means that this is itself the very mark of the noble and wise, drawing near to nirvāṇa; and "hā" means that one shall thereby come to be revered and honored by every deva and every person in the world alike.

When the sage had finished explaining this, King Devī, filled with joy, summoned all the lesser kings to gather in that very place, made a carriage of the seven treasures, and spread the lion's hide upon it, that all might together honor and revere it, burning incense and scattering flowers in offering. Afterward, he had a golden coffin made, placed the lion's hide within it, and raised a stūpa over it.

That lion, through the wholesome thought he gave rise to toward one clothed in the dyed robe, became a wheel-turning sage king across ten billion kalpas that followed. The Buddha told Ānanda: that lion of that time was myself; that king was Maitreya, as he is now; that sage was Śāriputra, as he is now; and that hunter was Devadatta, as he is now (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 13).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The lion's own dying verse — "yerā vaśā sabhāhā" — is preserved here in transliteration only, exactly as the Chinese source text renders it (耶羅婆奢沙婆呵), since no confident Sanskrit or Pali reconstruction is available to us; the sage Śama's own gloss, supplied within the story itself, is the closest thing to a translation the text offers, and we present his explanation as the story gives it rather than attempting our own retroactive reconstruction of the original words. The whole fable turns on a single moment of recognition: the lion's fury is fully justified and entirely present, yet a costume alone — not the man's own true character — is enough to stay it, a distinction the story treats as praiseworthy rather than as the naive error it might seem in a lesser telling.

11. Four Monks, Four Hells, Four Weights of Punishment

A grim comparative measure of karmic weight: four monks — Tiṣya, Kokālika, Devadatta, and Mahākhari — each burn in their own separate hell with flames of increasing length, their offenses ranked in careful, almost clinical detail from lightest to gravest.

Chinese Source

婆伽婆在舍衛城。祇樹給孤獨園。告諸比丘。有四大泥梨。一提舍大泥梨。身出火炎長二十肘。二瞿波梨大泥梨。身出火炎長三十肘。三調達大泥梨。身出火炎長四十肘。四末佉梨大泥梨。身出火炎長六十肘。諸有人民欲求安隱。獲其氣者。若二十大海水灌彼身上。彼海水盡火故不滅。猶如融銅。若有人以二十海渧水。澆融銅水渧速滅。提舍比丘火炎不滅。若復有人欲求安隱獲其義者。復以二十大海水。灌其身上彼水速盡。提舍比丘愚人。遮比丘僧使一日不得食。使提舍比丘入大地獄。瞿波離比丘。有人欲使安隱獲其義者。以大海水灌其身上。彼海水速盡。譬如二日所融銅。或有一人。以三十海水。著融銅中消盡。瞿波離比丘愚人。或有人起欲使獲安隱義者。以三十大海水。灌其身上。大海水速盡。瞿波離比丘愚人。謗舍利弗目犍連比丘。身壞命終生三惡道。墮鉢頭摩地獄。瞿波離比丘入大泥梨。調達大泥梨。若復有人欲使獲安隱義者。復以四十大海水。灌其身上。彼大海速盡彼火不滅。譬如三日所融銅。若有人以四十渧水。著融銅中。即時消盡無餘。調達愚人。若有人起欲使獲安隱義。以四十大海水灌其身上。彼大海水速盡彼火不滅。所以然者。調達愚人。欲害如來殺阿羅漢比丘尼。壞亂比丘僧。身壞命終三惡道生阿鼻地獄。調達比丘入大地獄。身出火炎長四十肘。諸有比丘。彼末佉梨大泥梨。若有一人欲使安隱。以六十大海水灌其身上。海水速盡彼火不滅。譬如四日所融銅。若有人以六十渧水。著融銅中即時消盡。末佉梨亦復如是。以六十大海水灌其身上。大海水速盡此火不滅。此末佉梨愚人。教受百拘梨人。使行邪見(出四泥梨經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Blessed One dwelt in Śrāvastī, in the Jetavana, in Anāthapiṇḍada's park, and told the monks: there are four great hells. The first is Tiṣya's great hell, where flames rise twenty cubits from the body. The second is Kokālika's great hell, where flames rise thirty cubits. The third is Devadatta's great hell, where flames rise forty cubits. The fourth is Mahākhari's great hell, where flames rise sixty cubits.

Should any person wish for relief, and be exposed to even the mere heat given off there, twenty oceans' worth of water poured over that body would be entirely consumed, and still the fire would not go out — just as with molten copper: were one to pour twenty ocean-drops of water into molten copper, the water would vanish at once, yet the copper's own heat remains. So it is with the monk Tiṣya's flame — it does not go out. Should someone wish to bring him relief by the same measure, twenty great oceans of water poured upon his body would be consumed at once, and still not extinguish it — for the foolish monk Tiṣya, out of malice, once prevented the assembled Saṅgha from eating for an entire day; it is for this that he has entered the great hell.

As for the monk Kokālika: should someone wish him relief in the same way, a great ocean of water poured upon his body would be consumed at once — comparable to copper melted by a second sun. Or again, should one pour thirty ocean-drops into molten copper, it too would vanish entirely. So it is with the foolish monk Kokālika: should anyone wish to bring him relief, thirty great oceans of water poured upon his body would be consumed at once, and still his fire would not go out — for the foolish monk Kokālika slandered the monks Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana, and, his body destroyed and his life ended, was reborn in the three lower realms, falling into the Padma hell; the monk Kokālika has entered the great hell.

As for Devadatta's own great hell: should someone wish to bring him relief, forty great oceans of water poured upon his body would be consumed at once, and still the fire would not go out — comparable to copper melted by a third sun; were one to pour forty ocean-drops into molten copper, it would vanish entirely, without remainder, just the same. So it is with the foolish Devadatta: should anyone wish to bring him relief, forty great oceans of water poured upon his body would be consumed at once, and still the fire would not go out. And why is this so? Because the foolish Devadatta sought to harm the Tathāgata himself, killed an arhat nun, and destroyed the harmony of the monastic assembly; his body destroyed, his life ended, he was reborn in the three lower realms, falling into the Avīci hell. The monk Devadatta has entered the great hell, flames rising forty cubits from his own body.

As for Mahākhari's own great hell: should someone wish to bring him relief, sixty great oceans of water poured upon his body would be consumed at once, and still the fire would not go out — comparable to copper melted by a fourth sun; were one to pour sixty ocean-drops into molten copper, it would vanish at once, just the same. So it is with Mahākhari: sixty great oceans of water poured upon his body would be consumed at once, and still this fire would not go out — for this foolish Mahākhari taught and led a hundred koṭis of people, an entire multitude beyond counting, into wrong views (Source: Sūtra of the Four Hells).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The entry's own escalating structure — twenty cubits, then thirty, forty, sixty, each fire proportionally harder to extinguish than the last — functions as a kind of moral arithmetic: withholding a single day's food from the Saṅgha is grave, slandering two chief disciples graver still, attempting to kill the Buddha and murdering an arhat graver yet, and leading a hundred koṭis of people into wrong view is worst of all, since its harm multiplies across every person misled. Note too that "瞿波梨/瞿波離" here again names Kokālika (as in entries 4 and 5, under the variant transliteration 拘迦利), and that his own hell is explicitly tied to the canonical account of his slander against Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana and his fall into the Padma hell — a story treated at greater length elsewhere in the wider Buddhist canon than the compendium gives it space for here.

Fascicle 21 closes with the Buddha's own former attendant Sunakṣatra, whose resentment at being made to wait curdles into an outright denial of karma itself — staking his position on a Jain ascetic's false claim to sainthood, only to watch that very claim collapse under a death he himself predicted, and still refusing to change his mind until his own living body sinks into the earth.

Chinese Source

佛言。我於一時。住王舍城。善星比丘為我給使。我於初夜。為天帝釋說諸法要。弟子之法應後師眠。爾時善星。以我久坐心生惡念。時王舍城小男小女。若啼不止父母則語。汝若不止。當將汝付薄拘羅鬼。爾時善星反被拘執。而語我言。速入禪室薄拘羅來。帝釋言。世尊。如是人等。亦復得入佛法中耶。我言。亦有佛性。當得無上菩提。我雖為是善星說法。而彼都無信受之心。我在迦尸國尸婆富羅城。善星為我給使。我入城乞食。無量眾生虛心渴仰欲見我跡。善星隨後毀滅。既不能滅。而令眾生生不善心。我入城已。於酒家舍見一尼乾。捲脊蹲地飡食酒糟。善星言。世間若有阿羅漢者。是人最勝。何以故。是人所說無因無果。我言癡人。汝常不聞。阿羅漢者不飲酒不害人不欺誑不盜婬。如是之人。殺害父母食噉酒糟。云何而言是阿羅漢。是人捨身。必定當墮阿鼻地獄。阿羅漢者。永斷三惡。云何而言是阿羅漢。善星即言。四大之性猶可轉易。欲令是人必墮阿鼻。無有是處。我言癡人。汝常不聞諸佛如來誠言無二。我雖為說法。而無信受之心。善男子。我與善星住王舍城。有一尼乾。名曰苦得。常作是言。眾生煩惱無有因緣。眾生解脫亦無因緣。善星言。世間若有阿羅漢者。苦得為上。我言。癡人苦得尼乾。實非羅漢。不能解了阿羅漢道。善星復言。何故羅漢。於阿羅漢而生妬嫉。我言癡人。我於羅漢不生妬嫉。而汝自生惡邪見耳。若言苦得是羅漢者。却後七日。當患宿食腹痛而死。生於食吐鬼中。其同學輩當輿其屍置寒林中。善星即語尼乾。長老。好善思惟作諸方便。當令世尊墮妄語中。苦得斷食。從初一日乃至六日。滿七日已便食黑蜜。復飲冷水腹痛而終。同學輿屍置寒林中。即作食吐餓鬼之形。在其屍邊。善星至寒林中。見苦得身。善星語言。大德死耶。苦得答言。我已死矣。云何死耶。答言。因腹痛死。誰出汝屍。答言。同學。出置何處。答言癡人。汝今不識是寒林耶。得何等身。答言。我得食吐鬼身。善星言。世尊。苦得尼乾生三十三天。我言癡人。阿羅漢者無有生處。云何而言。苦得生於三十三天。如來與迦葉。往善星所。善星遙見生惡邪心。生身陷入墮阿鼻獄。譬如有人沒圊廁中。有善知識以手撓之。若得首髮便欲拔出。久求不得。爾乃息意。我亦如是。求覓善星微少善根。便欲拔濟。終日求之。乃至不得如毛髮許。是故不得拔其地獄(出大涅槃經第三十七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said: once, dwelling in Rājagṛha, the monk Sunakṣatra served as my own attendant. One night, in the first watch, I taught the essentials of the Dharma to Śakra; and, as is proper for a disciple, he was to sleep only after his own teacher had done so. Sunakṣatra, resentful at how long I remained sitting, gave rise to a malicious thought.

At that time, in Rājagṛha, when small children would not stop crying, their parents would tell them: if you do not stop, we shall hand you over to the ghost Vakkula. Sunakṣatra, seized himself by this same fear, said to me: hurry into your meditation chamber — Vakkula is coming! Śakra said: World-Honored One, can even such a person as this still enter the Buddha's own teaching? I answered: he too possesses buddha-nature, and shall attain unsurpassed enlightenment in time. Though I taught him the Dharma even so, he held no heart of faith or acceptance in it at all.

Later, when I was in the city of Shīpófúluó, in the country of Kāśī, Sunakṣatra again served as my attendant. As I entered the city to beg for alms, countless beings, their hearts empty and yearning, longed to see even my own footprints — yet Sunakṣatra, following behind, tried to disparage and destroy this reverence; failing to destroy it entirely, he succeeded only in planting unwholesome thoughts in the minds of those beings instead.

Once I had entered the city, in a wine-shop I saw a Nirgrantha ascetic, his spine hunched, crouched on the ground, eating the dregs of wine. Sunakṣatra said: if there is truly an arhat in this world, it is this very man, foremost of all — for what he teaches admits neither cause nor effect. I said: foolish man, have you never heard that an arhat does not drink wine, does not harm others, does not deceive, does not steal, and does not commit sexual misconduct? Yet this man might as well kill his own parents and still feed on the dregs of wine — how could he possibly be called an arhat? Once this man casts off his body, he shall surely fall into the Avīci hell. An arhat has forever cut off the three lower realms — how can you call this man an arhat?

Sunakṣatra answered at once: even the nature of the four elements themselves can still be transformed — there is no reason this man must necessarily fall into Avīci. I said: foolish man, have you never heard that the buddhas and tathāgatas speak but a single, unchanging truth? Though I taught him further, still he held no heart of faith or acceptance.

Good man: while Sunakṣatra and I dwelt together in Rājagṛha, there was another Nirgrantha ascetic, named Kǔdé, who constantly taught: sentient beings' own afflictions arise from no cause at all, and their liberation likewise arises from no cause. Sunakṣatra said: if there is truly an arhat in this world, Kǔdé stands foremost among them. I said: foolish man, this Nirgrantha Kǔdé is no true arhat at all, and cannot even comprehend the arhat's own path. Sunakṣatra said further: why, then, do you feel such jealousy toward this arhat? I said: foolish man, I feel no jealousy at all toward any true arhat — it is you yourself who have given rise to this wicked and false view.

Should this Kǔdé truly be an arhat, then seven days from now he shall suffer sharp pain from undigested food in his own belly, and die of it, and be reborn among the food-vomiting hungry ghosts, and his own fellow ascetics shall carry his corpse to the charnel forest.

Sunakṣatra at once told the Nirgrantha ascetic: elder, consider this carefully, and devise every possible means, so that the World-Honored One might be shown guilty of false speech. Kǔdé then fasted, from the first day to the sixth; but, once the seventh day arrived, he ate dark honey, and, drinking cold water after it, died of pain in his own belly, exactly as foretold. His fellow ascetics carried his corpse to the charnel forest, where it took on the very form of a food-vomiting hungry ghost, lingering beside its own remains.

Sunakṣatra went to the charnel forest, and, seeing Kǔdé's own body there, said to it: great one, are you dead? Kǔdé answered: I am already dead. He asked: how did you die? It answered: from pain in my own belly. Who carried out your corpse? It answered: my fellow ascetics. Where did they place it? It answered: fool — do you not recognize this very charnel forest? What form have you now attained? It answered: I have obtained the body of a food-vomiting hungry ghost.

Sunakṣatra said: World-Honored One, the Nirgrantha Kǔdé has been reborn in Trāyastriṃśa heaven! I said: foolish man, a true arhat has no further rebirth at all — how can you say that Kǔdé has been reborn in Trāyastriṃśa heaven?

Later, the Tathāgata, together with Kāśyapa, went to where Sunakṣatra was; Sunakṣatra, seeing us from a distance, gave rise to a wicked and false thought — and, his own living body sinking into the earth, fell down into the Avīci hell.

It is like a man fallen into a cesspit: a good friend reaches in with his own hand to stir about, and, should he manage to grasp even a single hair of his head, tries to pull him out — but, searching long without success, at last gives up the attempt. So it was with me: I sought in Sunakṣatra for even the slightest root of goodness, wishing to draw him out and save him, searching the whole day through — yet could not find so much as a hair's breadth of it, and so could not draw him out of hell after all (Source: Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 37).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"薄拘羅鬼" (the ghost Vakkula) is a genuinely strange detail: elsewhere in the wider canon, Bakkula is the name of a revered arhat famed for his own longevity and freedom from illness, yet here the same name is used as a folk bogeyman to frighten crying children — an odd and unexplained inversion that the text itself makes no comment on, and that we preserve rather than smooth over. The whole story's real interest, though, is in Sunakṣatra's own method: he does not simply doubt the teaching of cause and effect in the abstract, but stakes it on an empirical test — Kǔdé's own claimed sainthood — and, when that very test resolves definitively against him, changes nothing at all, revealing that his position was never actually about evidence to begin with. The Buddha's own closing image, searching a cesspit for a single hair to grasp, is one of the compendium's starkest admissions that even unlimited compassion has a limit set by the other person's own unwillingness to be found.
