Nuns Who Attained the Path

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 23 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Arhat Nuns and Nun-Trainees, Part 12" (聲聞無學學尼僧部第十二) — fourteen entries devoted to women, from servant girls to queens to spurned wives, who reached full liberation. Among them: Bhadrā's own memory of a servant girl's single act of defiant generosity toward a starving pratyekabuddha, a poor blind widow's daughter elevated by a besotted king to queen of an entire nation before renouncing the throne itself to seek ordination, Utpalavarṇā's mind left entirely undefiled by an assault she never consented to, and the fascicle's difficult closing entries on the nun Cañcā's own history of slander stretching back countless lifetimes.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 23 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 23 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 23 closes the compendium's long run of śrāvaka biography (fascicles 13 through 23) with its only fascicle devoted entirely to women. Several entries here are difficult reading — sexual violence, forced marriage, and a mother's grief appear without euphemism — and are translated faithfully rather than softened, consistent with this project's standing practice throughout. Entry 11 is a novella-length narrative, the longest single entry translated in this project to date, and is presented here in full rather than condensed. Uncertain proper names are kept in pinyin per this project's standing rule against fabricated Sanskrit reconstruction.

1. Bhadrā's Memory of the Servant Girl Jǐluó

The nun Bhadrā, recalling her own past lives back ninety-one kalpas, traces a chain of rebirths from a vain young man's jeweled canopy through many lives of surpassing beauty, down to a lowly servant girl who defies her own mistress's cruelty to feed a starving pratyekabuddha — the very act that finally brings her, in this life, to arhatship.

Chinese Source

婆伽婆在羅閱城。時跋陀羅比丘尼。出比丘園在一樹下。端坐繫念自識宿命便笑。諸比丘尼見即問之。跋陀羅比丘尼言。我自識宿命。過去九十一劫。有毘婆式如來。出現於世。有一童子。名跋羅摩提婆。饒財多貨極富無量。童子執寶蓋從舍出。有一長者婦。端正無比。亦從彼過。有眾多人民皆悉觀察。時童子便作是念。我今擎寶蓋。眾寶合成從此路過。然無人觀察我者。此長者婦。我今當設方便使眾人觀察我。時童子手執寶蓋。便出彼境界。詣毘婆式如來所。住如來後擎寶蓋。經七日七夜。作此誓願言。持我供養世尊功德。當為女身端正無比。使有見者迷惑偃地。童子壽終。生三十三天為天女。有五事勝。云何為五。天壽天色天樂天神足天增上功德。天見此女皆懷希望欲得與俱。時三十三天皆各共諍。天女壽終來生人間為后若達多婆羅門作女。端正殊妙。往反人天常得端正。多值諸佛悉皆承事供養。求為女人。後生人間卑賤婆羅門家。為月光童子作婢。名曰幾羅。月光童子婦名摩奴訶羅。端正無比。時月光夫人語彼婢言。汝出舍外。若見沙門婆羅門者將來至此。我欲惠施。時婢出舍。有辟支佛。在門閾上乞食。時幾羅婢語言入來。欲相惠施。夫人見其體不端正。語幾羅言。發遣沙門我不施也。婢言。願尊開意莫嫌沙門形狀。供養功德不可稱計。時夫人報言。速驅此沙門出。我不堪施。婢報言。若尊不施此沙門者。我今日所應得分食願莫愛惜。時夫人即以麨與婢。時幾羅婢。即以分麨著辟支佛鉢中。食已身昇虛空。時婢見歡喜不能自勝。便作誓願。持是功德莫墮惡趣。使我當來久遠恒值此聖。為我說法使速解脫。時波羅奈城梵摩達多王。見彼辟支佛昇在虛空。語群臣言。彼所惠施。時月光長者。將五百賈客集大講堂。遙見辟支佛執鉢昇空。或有賈客作是說。此聖人必於我家得食。時大月光夫人。見為幾羅婢言。汝所得功德。持惠施我。此則是我所惠施。我今更持食與汝。婢言不施。又言。我今與汝二分食。對曰。亦不須。夫人又言。與汝三分五分十分二十分乃至百分千分。幾羅婢言。我不相施。夫人報言。設汝不與我者。我當截汝耳鼻手。幾羅婢言。我不相施。時長者婦。以杖打婢。打之已竟。月光長者還來。見幾羅婢。頭破衣裂悲泣墮淚。問其始末。具以事白。時月光童子。取彼夫人持用作婢。以幾羅婢作第一夫人。五百婇女圍遶。以百千珍寶莊嚴其身。時梵摩達多王。聞月光婢飯彼辟支佛。時梵摩達多王。以百千兩珍寶以餉月光長者婦。復以田業惠施月光長者。時梵摩達多王。今伽毘羅摩奢羅是。月光長者大迦葉是。幾羅婢者即我身是。今得值佛得阿羅漢(出跋陀羅比丘尼經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Blessed One dwelt in Rājagṛha. The nun Bhadrā, leaving the monks' own garden, sat beneath a tree, and, her mind fixed in recollection, suddenly recalled her own past lives, and laughed. The other nuns, seeing this, asked her why. Bhadrā said: I have just recalled my own past lives, back ninety-one kalpas, to when the Tathāgata Vipaśyin appeared in the world.

There was, at that time, a young man named Báluómótípó, of boundless wealth, who, carrying a jeweled canopy, went out from his own house. A certain elder's wife, of matchless beauty, happened to pass that same way, and a great crowd of people all turned to look at her. The young man thought to himself: here I am, holding aloft this jeweled canopy, made of every kind of treasure, passing along this same road — yet no one turns to look at me at all, only at this elder's wife. I must now devise some means to make the crowd look at me instead.

The young man then carried his jeweled canopy out of that whole region entirely, and went to the Tathāgata Vipaśyin, and, standing behind the Tathāgata, held the canopy aloft for seven days and seven nights, making this vow: may the merit of this offering to the World-Honored One cause me to be reborn, in time, as a woman of matchless beauty, such that any who see me shall be overcome, and fall down before me.

When the young man's own life ended, he was reborn in Trāyastriṃśa heaven, as a heavenly maiden, possessed of five surpassing qualities: heavenly lifespan, heavenly beauty, heavenly pleasure, heavenly supernormal power, and heavenly excellence of merit. The devas, seeing this maiden, all longed to have her for their own, and the whole of Trāyastriṃśa heaven contended over her.

When that heavenly maiden's own life ended, she was reborn among humans as the daughter of the brahmin Hòuruòdáduō, of surpassing beauty; passing back and forth between the human and heavenly realms, she remained always of surpassing beauty, and met with many buddhas, serving and making offerings to them all, though she always sought rebirth as a woman.

She was later reborn among humans into a lowly brahmin household, as a servant girl to the young man Candraprabha, whose own wife, named Mónuóhēluó, was of matchless beauty. Candraprabha's wife once told this servant girl: go outside the house, and, should you see any śramaṇa or brahmin, bring him here — I wish to make an offering.

The servant went outside, and found a pratyekabuddha begging at the threshold. The servant girl Jǐluó told him: come in — I wish to make an offering to you. But the mistress, seeing his unimpressive appearance, told Jǐluó: send this śramaṇa away — I shall not give to him. The servant said: I beg you, mistress, open your heart, and do not judge this śramaṇa's own form — the merit of offering to him is beyond all measure. The mistress answered: drive this śramaṇa out at once — I cannot bear to give to him. The servant answered: if you yourself, mistress, will not give to this śramaṇa, then let me give my own portion of food today instead — please do not begrudge me this. The mistress then gave the servant her ration of parched grain-meal.

Jǐluó at once placed her own portion of grain-meal into the pratyekabuddha's own bowl; having eaten it, he rose into the open sky. The servant, seeing this, was overcome with a joy she could not contain, and made this vow: may the merit of this deed keep me from ever falling into the lower realms, and may I, in ages far into the future, constantly encounter this very sage, that he might teach me the Dharma, and bring me swiftly to liberation.

King Brahmadatta of Vārāṇasī, seeing the pratyekabuddha rise into the sky, told his ministers of the offering that had been made. The elder Candraprabha, meanwhile, had gathered five hundred merchants together in a great lecture hall, and, seeing from a distance the pratyekabuddha rising with his own bowl, one of the merchants remarked: this sage must surely have received his food from my own household.

Candraprabha's wife, learning what the servant Jǐluó had done, said to her: give me the merit you have obtained — it is I, after all, who truly made this offering; I shall give you food in return. The servant said: I shall not give it to you. The mistress said: I shall give you two portions of food instead. She answered: I have no need of it. The mistress offered three, then five, then ten, then twenty, then a hundred, then a thousand portions. Still the servant Jǐluó said: I shall not give it to you. The mistress said: if you will not give it to me, I shall cut off your own ears, nose, and hands. Still the servant said: I shall not give it to you. The elder's wife then struck the servant with a rod, again and again.

When the elder Candraprabha returned, he found the servant Jǐluó, her head broken, her clothes torn, weeping in grief. He asked her what had happened, and she told him the whole matter fully. Candraprabha then took his own wife and made her a servant in turn, and made the servant Jǐluó his own chief wife instead, surrounded by five hundred attendant women, her body adorned with a hundred thousand treasures.

King Brahmadatta, hearing that it was Candraprabha's own servant who had fed the pratyekabuddha, presented Candraprabha's former wife with a hundred thousand taels of treasure, and further granted Candraprabha lands and fields as a gift.

King Brahmadatta of that time is Jiāpíluómóshēluó now. The elder Candraprabha is Mahākāśyapa now. And the servant girl Jǐluó was myself — and it is because of this that, having now encountered the Buddha, I have attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of the Nun Bhadrā).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The whole long chain of rebirths — vain youth, heavenly maiden, brahmin's beautiful daughter, servant girl — is structured around a single reversal: every prior life pursued beauty as an end in itself, culminating in a servant girl whose own body carries none of that inherited beauty at all, and whose true merit instead comes from the one act none of her more glamorous incarnations required of her — defiant generosity toward someone her own social superior found beneath contempt. Several proper names here (Báluómótípó, Hòuruòdáduō, Mónuóhēluó, Jiāpíluómóshēluó) are preserved in pinyin rather than reconstructed into Sanskrit, since no confident equivalent is available to us; Candraprabha, Brahmadatta, and Vipaśyin, by contrast, are well-attested standard reconstructions used throughout Buddhist literature.

2. Born Wrapped in Cloth

A girl born already wrapped in fine white cloth that grows with her own body, refusing every suitor to seek ordination instead, is traced by the Buddha to a desperately poor couple who owned only a single shared cloth between them — and gave it away anyway.

Chinese Source

時舍衛國有一長者婦。生一女。殊妙少雙。其初生時。細濡白㲲裹身而出。父母怪之。瞻相甚吉。因為作字。名曰叔離(梁言白也)叔離長大。㲲隨身大。此女璝偉。國內遠近競來娉求。女白父母。我欲出家。父母愛念不違其志。尋為出㲲。欲作五衣。女白父母。我此所著悉已具足。更不須作。唯願聽我時往佛所。詣佛作禮求索出家。佛言善來。頭髮自墮。所著白㲲尋成五衣。付大愛道為比丘尼。精進不久成羅漢道。阿難白佛言。叔離本修何德。生與㲲俱出家得道。佛言。過去有佛。名毘婆尸。時王臣民多設供養。時有女人。名檀膩䩭。極為貧窮。夫婦二人共有一㲲。若夫出行則被而往。婦便裸住。若婦被㲲。夫則裸坐。有勸化比丘。行至其家。見是女人。因勸之言。佛世難值人身難得。汝當聽法汝當布施。女還語夫。外有沙門。勸我見佛聽法布施。夫答之言。我家窮困。雖可有心當以何施。婦言。我意欲以此㲲布施。夫言我之與汝。共此一㲲。出入求索以自存活。今若用施俱當守死。婦言。人生有死不施會死。施而死後世有望。不施而死後遂當劇。夫歡喜言。分死用施。婦即還出。白比丘言。大德可上疏。下我名字。比丘答言。若欲施者汝當面施。為汝呪願。叔離白言。唯此被㲲內無異衣。女形穢惡不宜此脫。即還入內。遙於向下脫身上㲲。授與比丘。比丘呪願持至佛所。佛言。比丘持此㲲來。比丘授佛。佛自手受。此㲲垢污。時王眾會。微心嫌佛受此垢㲲。佛知眾心而告之言。我觀此會。清淨大施無過於此。以㲲施者。大眾聞已莫不悚然。夫人歡喜。即脫己身所著嚴飾瓔珞寶衣。送與陀膩䩭。王亦欣悅。脫身衣服送與其夫命令詣會。佛告阿難。欲知爾時貧窮女人陀膩䩭者。今叔離比丘尼是。由於爾時以清淨心㲲布施故。九十一劫所生之處。常與㲲生。無所乏少(出賢愚經第七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there lived an elder's wife who bore a daughter, of rare and surpassing beauty; at her birth, she came forth already wrapped in fine, soft white cloth. Her parents, astonished, saw in this an auspicious sign, and so named her Shūlí (in the language of Liang, this means "White"). As Shūlí grew, the cloth grew together with her own body. This girl was so extraordinary that suitors, near and far throughout the kingdom, competed to seek her hand.

The girl told her parents: I wish to go forth as a renunciant. Her parents, loving her dearly, would not oppose her own wish, and set about removing the cloth, meaning to fashion it into the five monastic robes. The girl told her parents: what I already wear is entirely sufficient — there is no need to make anything further. I ask only that you allow me to go now to the Buddha, to bow before him and ask to go forth.

The Buddha said: welcome — and her hair fell away of itself, and the white cloth she wore transformed at once into the five monastic robes. She was placed under Mahāprajāpatī's own care as a nun, and, applying herself with diligence, attained the fruit of arhatship before long.

Ānanda asked the Buddha: what merit did Shūlí cultivate in the past, that she was born already wearing this cloth, and went forth to attain the path so swiftly? The Buddha said: in a past age there was a buddha named Vipaśyin, whose king, ministers, and people made lavish offerings to him. At that time there lived a woman named Tánnì'è, in the direst poverty; she and her husband together owned only a single cloth between them — should the husband go out, he alone wore it, while his wife remained naked at home; should the wife wear it, the husband sat naked in turn.

A monk who taught and encouraged giving came once to their household, and, seeing the woman, urged her: a buddha's own age in this world is rare to encounter, and a human birth hard to obtain — you should listen to the Dharma, and you should give in charity. The wife told her husband: there is a śramaṇa outside, urging me to see the Buddha, hear the Dharma, and give in charity. Her husband answered: our household is so poor — though we might well wish to give, what do we actually have to offer? The wife said: I wish to offer this very cloth of ours. Her husband said: you and I together own only this one cloth, going in and out to seek our own livelihood by it — should we now give it away, we should both surely die of exposure. The wife said: all who are born must die in the end — whether we give or not, death will come regardless; if we give, and then die, there is hope for us in the life to come; if we do not give, and then die, what follows after will only be worse. The husband, delighted, said: then let us share this death, and give it away.

The wife went back outside, and told the monk: great one, please record my own name on the list of donors. The monk answered: if you truly wish to give, you should offer it in person, that I might dedicate the merit for you directly. Shūlí — for it was she — said: this very cloth I wear is all I have; I have no other garment, and my own body is unfit to be seen unclothed. She went back inside, and, from within, lowered the cloth from her own body and passed it out to the monk. The monk, dedicating the merit, carried it to the Buddha.

The Buddha said: monk, bring me this cloth. The monk offered it, and the Buddha received it with his own hand, though the cloth was soiled and filthy. The king and the whole assembly there felt some faint unease that the Buddha should accept so soiled a cloth. The Buddha, knowing their thoughts, told them: I have surveyed this whole assembly, and no offering here is purer or greater than this cloth. The whole assembly, hearing this, was struck with awe. The king's own consort, delighted, at once removed her own jeweled ornaments and fine robes, and sent them to Tánnì'è; the king too, gladdened, removed his own robes and sent them to her husband, summoning them both to the assembly.

The Buddha told Ānanda: know that this poor woman, Tánnì'è, of that time, is this very nun Shūlí now. Because, in that life, she gave the cloth with a pure heart, in every place she has been born across the ninety-one kalpas since, she has always been born clothed already, lacking for nothing (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 7).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The couple's own calculation — that giving away their only cloth means near-certain death by exposure, weighed candidly against the near-certainty of death eventually regardless — is unusually explicit about the actual stakes of extreme poverty, rarely spelled out with this much practical detail elsewhere in the compendium. The Buddha's own verdict, that this soiled, singular cloth outweighs every other offering in a royal assembly, measures generosity strictly against what the giver had left to lose, not against the object's own market value.

3. Bhadrakāpilānī, Undefiled in the King's Own Palace

Confined by King Prasenajit inside his own palace for a season of retreat, the nun Bhadrakāpilānī escapes to hear the Buddha teach, flies off in full view of the pursuing king, and, on being accused by her own community of impropriety, likens the mere sensation of proximity to a king's touch as being burned by molten iron.

Chinese Source

俱薩羅國。波斯匿王。聞跋陀迦毘羅出家。即請入宮。夏四月安居共止一處。王到園中。語守門人言。汝好守門。莫令是比丘尼出。守門人作是念。是比丘尼樂住不走。時守門人有餘因緣。比丘尼著夫人被服從門而出。遙到祇桓。聽佛說法。佛遙見言。善來跋陀迦毘羅。即失夫人被服。頭髮自落袈裟著身作比丘尼。到佛所頭面禮佛。佛為說四如意足力。時比丘尼得神足力。是時王聞跋陀迦毘羅女走去。便將兵眾圍遶比丘眾。及圍遶比丘尼坊。是比丘尼便飛虛空。王仰看。即生悔心。我云何乃污阿羅漢比丘尼。心悶躄地向比丘尼悔過。寺中比丘尼驅令出去。答曰。我無愛欲心。諸比丘尼云。汝夏四月共王殿中。云何無愛。以事白佛。佛知故問。是比丘尼。汝實受細滑不。答言。世尊我云何當受。我覺是細滑如熱鐵入身。佛言。汝若無受細滑心無罪(出十誦律善誦卷第二)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the country of Kośala, King Prasenajit, hearing that Bhadrakāpilānī had gone forth as a nun, invited her into his own palace, to keep the rains retreat there together for the four months of the season. The king, going to the garden, told the gatekeeper: guard this gate well — do not let this nun leave. The gatekeeper thought to himself: this nun seems content to remain here, and will not try to flee. At that time, the gatekeeper being occupied elsewhere, the nun put on a court lady's own robes and left through the gate, and made her way to Jetavana, to hear the Buddha teach.

The Buddha, seeing her from a distance, said: welcome, Bhadrakāpilānī — and the court lady's robes fell away from her at once, her hair fell away of itself, and monastic robes appeared upon her body, restored to her own nun's form. She came before the Buddha, and bowed at his feet; the Buddha taught her the power of the four bases of supernormal ability, and she attained the power of supernormal flight then and there.

The king, hearing that Bhadrakāpilānī had fled, brought his own soldiers to surround the assembly of monks, and to surround the nuns' own quarters as well; the nun then rose and flew into the open sky. The king, looking up, was struck at once with remorse: how could I have thus defiled an arhat nun! — and, overcome with distress, fell to the ground, and confessed his own fault to her.

The nuns at her own monastery wished to drive her out. She answered: I hold no thought of desire in my heart at all. The other nuns said: you spent the four months of the rains retreat together with the king in his own palace — how can you say you held no desire? They reported the matter to the Buddha, who, though already knowing the answer, asked her directly: nun, did you truly experience any pleasurable sensation of touch? She answered: World-Honored One, how could I have experienced such a thing? I felt that very sensation of touch as though molten iron had entered my own body. The Buddha said: if you held no mind of desire in receiving that touch, you bear no transgression at all (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, Well-Recited Portion, fascicle 2).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own ruling here restates, in a different key, the same principle from fascicle 22's entry 5: transgression is measured by the presence or absence of a defiled intention, not by the outward circumstance alone — proximity to a king inside his own palace for four months carries no guilt whatsoever, provided the mind held toward it remains, as she describes it, closer to pain than to pleasure.

4. Utpalavarṇā and the Brahmin Who Struck at Her in Sleep

Utpalavarṇā's own life unfolds through a chain of family tragedies she never consciously chooses — her first husband's affair with her own mother, an unwitting second marriage to her own daughter's husband — before ordination and full liberation, and even then a brahmin's own assault upon her sleeping body cannot touch her attainment, though it costs him his life.

Chinese Source

The Buddha dwelt in Śrāvastī. In the town of Uśīnara, there lived a young householder who, out wandering for his own amusement, saw a woman named Utpalavarṇā, her beauty like the peach and plum blossom, every feature complete; his heart was drawn to her at once, and he took her as his own wife.

Not long after, she conceived, and was sent to give birth at her own mother's home; once the term was full, she bore a daughter. Because his wife remained occupied with the birth, unable to be near him, her husband secretly began an affair with her own mother instead. Utpalavarṇā, learning of this, left them both — for, with the bond between husband and wife thus broken, and fearing to burden her own parents, yet unwilling to abandon her infant daughter, she swallowed her own shame and returned to her husband's household, and raised her daughter there for eight years before finally leaving.

She traveled to Vārāṇasī, and, hungry, thirsty, and utterly exhausted, sat down by a stretch of water. An elder there, out on an excursion, saw her, and, drawn to her at once, asked: where is your own home, and to what family do you belong, that you sit here alone? Utpalavarṇā answered: I am the daughter of a certain family, but now belong to no one. The elder asked further: if you belong to no one, could you become my own chief wife? She answered: though a woman may already have had a husband, why should this not still be possible? He brought her home at once, and made her his own principal wife.

Utpalavarṇā managed his household well, keeping harmony among young and old alike, and husband and wife held each other dear for eight years. The elder then told his wife: I have outstanding debts owed to me in the town of Uśīnara, uncollected these eight years now, which, reckoned with their own interest, have grown to a vast sum — I must go now to collect them, and shall be apart from you a while. His wife said: the customs of that town are known for loose conduct among women — should you go there yourself, you might lose your own husband's own resolve. He answered: though I am of limited understanding, I would never fall into such disorder. She said further: if you must indeed go, I ask only to hear one vow from you first. He answered: very well — should any wicked thought arise in me, may my own life end together with it. And so he took his leave, and went to that town.

He had many debts to collect there, and so remained a full year. His own longing for home grew steadily deeper, and he thought: were I to commit any wrongful union, I would betray my own original vow — yet, were I to take a separate household here instead, that would not violate my own word. And so, seeking about, he came upon a young woman of refined and lovely appearance, whose own gaze held nothing improper in it, and the two of them grew deeply fond of each other; he went and asked for her hand. Her father, learning that he was a talented and wealthy elder, gave her to him gladly.

Once his own debts were fully collected, he returned to his own country, and settled this new wife in a separate residence before returning home himself. From then on, he would leave each morning and return each evening, unlike his usual habit before. Utpalavarṇā, finding this strange, secretly asked his attendants, who told her: there is a young wife — her husband returns to her each evening.

Utpalavarṇā asked him: you have a new household — why do you hide it from me, and not let me see her? He answered: I feared you would resent it, and so kept it hidden. She said: I hold no jealousy at all, and the gods themselves bear witness to this — you may bring her home, that she might help you manage the household. And so he brought her home — and it was, in fact, his own daughter.

Mother and child, seeing each other again, did not recognize one another. It was only later, while washing the young woman's hair, that Utpalavarṇā, examining her features closely, began to suspect who she was, and asked her about her own home region, her parents, and her family name; the young woman answered fully, and only then did the truth become known.

Her mother, struck with horror and grief, cried out: once I shared a husband with my own mother — now I share a husband with my own daughter! Such confusion of birth and death, come to this! Unless one cuts off craving and desire entirely, and goes forth to study the path, how could such inverted delusion ever be brought to an end? — and, with this, she abandoned them both and departed.

She came to the gate of Jetavana, hungry, thirsty, and utterly exhausted, and sat beneath a tree. At that time the World-Honored One sat teaching, surrounded by the great assembly; Utpalavarṇā, seeing so many people gathered, thought it must be some festival gathering, where food would surely be found, and entered the monastery grounds. There she saw the Buddha, the World-Honored One, teaching the assembly, and, hearing the Dharma, her own mind opened in understanding, and her hunger and thirst were dispelled entirely.

The World-Honored One then surveyed the whole assembly to see who was fit to be delivered, and saw that Utpalavarṇā alone was ready to attain the fruit of the path; he taught suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path, and, in that very seat, far from all dust and defilement, she attained the pure Dharma-eye. Having attained this fruit, she remained there, palms joined in single-minded devotion before the Buddha.

Once the Buddha had finished teaching, the assembly dispersed; Utpalavarṇā came forward, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and, kneeling with palms joined, said: I wish to go forth within the Buddha's own teaching. The Buddha granted this at once, and told the nun Mahāprajāpatī: you may now ordain this woman as a renunciant. She went forth and received the full precepts, and, applying herself with diligence, attained arhatship, complete in the eight liberations, her own countenance now radiant, surpassing even her former beauty.

Once, entering the city to beg for alms, a certain brahmin, seeing her, was seized with lustful desire, and thought to himself: this nun cannot be won openly — I must find her own dwelling, and devise some means. Later, when she had gone out again to beg, this brahmin slipped in and hid beneath her own bed. That day, the nuns had spent the whole night teaching the Dharma, and, exhausted, returned to their own room, lay down on their backs, and fell into a deep sleep. The brahmin then came out from beneath the bed, and committed an impure act upon her.

The nun rose at once into the open sky; and the brahmin, upon that very bed, fell, still living, down into hell (Source: Mahīśāsaka Vinaya, fascicle 5).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry's own present-life narrative — the affair, the unwitting second marriage to her own daughter's husband, the assault while asleep — is translated here without euphemism, consistent with this project's standing practice of faithful, unaltered rendering of the compendium's most difficult material. Notice that at no point does the story treat Utpalavarṇā as morally implicated in what happens to her; her own eventual ordination and liberation are framed as a direct response to that accumulated grief, and the closing assault, occurring after she has already attained arhatship, changes nothing about her own attainment at all — it is the brahmin alone whose act carries consequence, immediate and total.

5. The Courtesan and the Illusory Corpse

A courtesan on her way to renounce the world is distracted by her own reflection's beauty and turns back — until the Buddha conjures a beautiful stranger who dies suddenly in her arms and decays before her eyes, a vision that breaks through her hesitation more effectively than any argument could.

Chinese Source

佛在羅閱祇耆闍崛山。有一婬女。名曰蓮華。善心自生便棄世事。作比丘尼。即詣山中行到佛所。未至中道有流泉水。女因飲水澡手自觀。見其面像姿妍無比。即便念言。云何自棄作沙門耶。且當少時快我私情。尋即還家。佛知蓮華應當得度。化作婦人端正絕世。勝蓮華女。尋路而來。蓮華見之。心甚愛敬。即問化人。從何所來。夫主中外皆在何許。云何獨行而無將從。化人答言。從城中來欲還歸家。雖不相識可俱還到向泉水上。蓮華言善。二人俱還到泉水上。陳意委曲。化人睡臥。枕蓮華膝。須臾之頃忽然命絕。膖脹臭爛腹膭蟲出。齒落髮墮肌體解散。蓮華見之心大驚怖。云何好人忽便無常。此人尚爾我豈得久。怖當詣佛精進學道。即至佛所。五體投地作禮自說。佛告蓮華。女人有四事。不可恃怙。一者少壯會當歸老。二者強健會當歸死。三者六親歡娛會當別離。四者財寶積聚要當分散。蓮華聞法欣然解釋。得阿羅漢(出蓮華女經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt at Vulture Peak, in Rājagṛha. There was a courtesan named Padma, in whom a wholesome resolve arose of itself, and who resolved to abandon worldly affairs and become a nun. She set out on the mountain path toward the Buddha, and, not yet halfway there, came upon a flowing spring. Stopping to drink and wash her hands, she saw her own reflection, her own beauty and charm without equal, and thought to herself: why should I abandon all this to become a mere renunciant? Let me first enjoy a little more of my own private pleasure — and turned back toward home at once.

The Buddha, knowing that Padma was ready to be delivered, transformed himself into a woman of matchless beauty, surpassing even Padma's own, who came walking along the very same road. Padma, seeing her, was struck with admiration, and asked the conjured woman: where have you come from? Where is your own husband, and your family, inside and out? Why do you travel alone, with no companion at all? The conjured woman answered: I have come from the city, and am on my way home; though we are unacquainted, we might return together as far as the spring ahead. Padma agreed, and the two of them went back together to the spring, speaking intimately of their own lives.

The conjured woman then lay down to sleep, her head resting on Padma's own knee — and, in a mere instant, her life suddenly ended. Her body swelled and rotted, giving off a foul stench, her belly burst open and worms poured forth, her teeth fell out, her hair came loose, and her very flesh came apart. Padma, seeing this, was struck with great terror: how could someone so beautiful meet with impermanence so suddenly? If even she could come to this, how much longer could I myself possibly last? Terrified, she resolved to go to the Buddha and apply herself diligently to studying the path.

She went at once before the Buddha, threw her whole body to the ground in homage, and told him all that had happened. The Buddha told Padma: there are four things in this world a woman cannot rely upon: first, youth and vigor must in the end return to old age; second, strength and health must in the end return to death; third, the joyful company of one's own closest kin must in the end come to separation; and fourth, wealth and treasure once accumulated must in the end be scattered and lost.

Padma, hearing this teaching, understood it with joy, and attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of the Woman Padma).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry's own courtesan, named "蓮華" (Padma/Lotus) in the Chinese source, shares her name with the arhat Utpalavarṇā from entry 4 (also frequently rendered 蓮華色, "lotus-colored") but is clearly a separate figure with an unrelated narrative; we render her simply as Padma here to avoid conflating the two. Her own initial retreat from renunciation — not doubt about the teaching, but a single glance at her own beauty in still water — is treated by the story with notable sympathy rather than censure, and it is answered not with argument but with an equally direct, visceral demonstration of the very impermanence she had just chosen to look away from.

6. Five Hundred Brahmin Women Awakened

Five hundred women, weary of the constraints placed on them throughout their lives, undertake an elaborate fast and offering to petition Brahmā himself for release into a higher rebirth — and discover, when their prayers are answered by an unexpected visitor, that liberation looks nothing like what they had asked for.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Southeast of Śrāvastī, out in the ocean, there stood a raised platform, its surface covered in fragrant flowers and pure, clean trees. Five hundred brahmin women, who served a non-Buddhist teaching with great diligence, and knew nothing of the Buddha, gathered there.

These women said among themselves: we, born and formed as women, are governed from youth to old age by three constraints, never free to act as we ourselves wish; our own lives, moreover, are brief, our bodies like illusions destined soon for death. Better that we go together to this platform of fragrant flowers, gather flowers there, and keep a diligent fast, to entreat Brahmā himself, and seek from him this wish: to be reborn in the Brahmā heaven, with long life and freedom from death, and there to obtain true independence, free of every constraint and restriction, free of all reckoning of past transgression, and free of further care or grief.

They carried offerings to the platform, gathered fragrant flowers, and served Brahmā there, keeping a single-minded fast, praying that the honored deity might deign to appear before them.

The Buddha, seeing their hearts fit to be delivered, flew there through the open sky together with the great assembly, and, arriving at the platform, sat beneath a tree. The women, delighted, said to one another: this must be Brahmā himself, come to console and congratulate us — our own wish has been granted!

One of the devas present then told the women: this is not Brahmā at all — this is the most honored being throughout the three realms, called the Buddha, who delivers beings beyond number.

The women said to the Buddha: we, so laden with defilement, now reborn as women, seek release from every constraint, and wish to be reborn in the Brahmā heaven. The Buddha said: women, it is truly excellent that you have given rise to such a wish. In this world there are two matters whose retribution is entirely clear: doing good brings blessing; doing evil brings misfortune. There is the suffering of this world, and the pleasure of the heavens; there is the vexation of conditioned existence, and the stillness of the unconditioned — who among you can truly discern and seek out what is genuine? How excellent, women, that you already hold such a clear resolve.

The World-Honored One then spoke this verse:

"Who can truly choose their own ground, / setting aside mere constraint to seek the heavens themselves? / Who can expound the very verses of the Dharma / as one selects only the finest flower? / To know the world's own fragile, molded arguments, / illusory phenomena arising suddenly and passing — / to cut off Māra, and let the true flower open — / this alone is not to witness birth and death at all."

The women, hearing this verse, resolved to study the true path, and become nuns; their hair fell away of itself, and the Dharma robes appeared complete upon them. Contemplating in stillness, they attained the fruit of arhatship at once (Source: Dharmapada with Illustrative Tales, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The women's own original petition is worth taking seriously on its own terms rather than reading only as a setup for correction: what they ask for — an escape from arbitrary constraint into a state of lasting independence — is not so different, in shape, from what the Dharma itself actually offers; their error lies only in mistaking a particular heavenly rebirth, itself still conditioned and impermanent, for the genuinely unconditioned freedom they were actually seeking all along. The Buddha's own reply affirms the wish before redirecting it, rather than dismissing it as misguided from the start.

7. The Brahmin Nun Who Learned Humility

A brahmin nun invites the monk Udāyin to teach only to receive him with wine of ostentatious contempt, dismissing his teaching until, after three refusals, she finally sets aside her own pride, sits on a low seat, and asks in earnest — receiving a careful, escalating answer she could never have gotten while still performing superiority.

Some of her own disciples, out gathering firewood, came to the mango grove, and saw Udāyin sitting beneath a tree, his countenance dignified, his every faculty stilled, his mind composed and settled, having achieved the highest degree of self-mastery. They went to him, greeted him, and sat to one side; Udāyin then taught these young women the Dharma in various ways, and, having encouraged and instructed them, fell silent.

When the night had passed, Udāyin robed himself, took up his bowl, and went to the nun's own dwelling. Seeing him approach from a distance, she prepared a seat and invited him to sit, and offered every kind of food and drink, serving him abundantly with her own hands until he was fully satisfied; once he had rinsed his mouth and washed his bowl, he returned to his own seat.

The brahmin nun then put on fine leather shoes, covered her own head with a cloth, and had a separate high seat set out for herself, displaying an air of arrogance, and said to Udāyin: I wish to ask you something — do you have leisure to answer me now, or not? He answered: sister, now is not the proper time.

The next day, her disciples again went to the grove to gather firewood and hear the teaching, and reported this again to their teacher; she again sent an invitation to a meal, as before, and this happened three times in all, each time followed by a request for teaching — and each time he answered: now is not the proper time.

The disciples said: our own teacher does not sit with proper reverence — how then could he possibly teach her? The teacher said: if that is so, invite him once more on my behalf. They did as instructed, and invited him again, and served him as before. This time, once the meal was finished, the teacher removed her own leather shoes and outer robe, and sat instead upon a low seat, and, with proper reverence, said: I wish to ask you something — might you answer me now? He answered: only ask, and I shall teach you.

She then asked: is it said that suffering and pleasure are self-caused? Or that suffering and pleasure are caused by another? Or that suffering and pleasure are caused by both self and other together? Or that suffering and pleasure arise from neither self nor other? He answered: sister, the arhats teach that suffering and pleasure arise dependently, through causes and conditions. She asked further: what is the meaning of this? He answered: they arise from their own respective causes and conditions.

She said: let me ask you this in turn, and answer me as you will — do you have eyes? He answered: I do. Are there forms to be seen? He answered: there are. Does eye-consciousness, and contact through the eye, and, through the condition of that contact, does sensation arise — an inner experience, whether of suffering, or of pleasure, or of neither suffering nor pleasure? He answered: it is so. Udāyin said: this is exactly what the arhats teach — that suffering and pleasure arise from their own respective causes and conditions.

As the venerable one taught this Dharma, the brahmin nun, far from all dust and defilement, attained the pure Dharma-eye. Rising from her own seat, straightening her own robes, and, with palms joined in reverence, she said to the venerable one: today I have leapt directly into certainty. From this day forward, I take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, and shall rely upon the Three Jewels for the rest of my life (Source: Sūtra of Udāyin's Seated Stillness and Self-Mastery).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Udāyin's own repeated refusal to teach — "now is not the proper time" — is never explained by the text as mere scheduling, and the surrounding details make its real cause plain: he will not teach someone seated above him in ostentatious display, however lavish her own hospitality. The nun's own breakthrough only becomes possible once she removes the shoes and head-covering and sits below him — the same posture-based marker of genuine readiness to hear the Dharma that appeared in fascicle 20's story of the king who demanded teaching while still wearing his own shoes and head-cloth.

8. Kṣamā and Padmaśrī, Attacked and Spared

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, in the kingdom of Kośala — Śrāvastī was its capital — there lived a band of dissolute and lustful men, given entirely over to violence. In that kingdom, the nuns would sit in single-minded contemplation of the true path beneath the trees, never once setting aside their own resolve; among them, one nun, foremost in wisdom, was named Kṣamā, and another, foremost in supernormal power, was named Padmaśrī — each of exceptional virtue and majestic spiritual bearing.

One warm day, the two went together to bathe at the water's edge. The band of violent men, seeing them from a distance, gave rise to a wicked intent, their lustful desire mounting, and resolved to violate them. Waiting until the nuns had removed their own robes and entered the water to bathe, they rushed forward, seized their clothing, and carried it off to a distant place, meaning to drag the nuns there and violate them.

The nun, filled with sorrowful compassion for these men rather than fear, then removed her own two eyes, and held them in the palm of her hand, showing them to the assailants: what you love in me is only the color of my own face — but here, already blind, with no eyes at all — what is there left to find beautiful? She then showed them her own belly and entrails, her body's own organs, her hands and feet, each set apart and cast to one side, and said to the violent men: where, now, is this beauty you sought?

The assailants, seeing this, were struck at once with terror, and understood the impermanence of the world, and how all beings within the three realms are but temporary lodgers there, their own bodies reducible, in the end, to bone, blood, and impurity, with nothing in them truly worth craving. They returned the nuns' own robes at once, bowed their heads, and repented of the shameless act they had intended, admitting their own wrongful and unreasoning violence, and asked to be spared its consequence. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nun's own response is deliberate strategy rather than mere desperate self-mutilation: by dismantling, piece by piece, the very body her attackers claim to desire, she forces them to confront that what they actually wanted was never a body at all, but an idea of beauty that cannot survive contact with the body's own literal, physical truth. Notably, the story credits this act to compassion for the assailants ("愴然愍之") rather than to fear or anger on her own part — she saves herself and her companion, but the text frames her intervention as aimed, just as much, at sparing the men themselves from the karmic consequence of the act they were about to commit.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The elaborate staged illusion at the story's center is a striking method: rather than confronting Vilotā's own contempt for the Buddha's teaching directly, Rāhula builds an entire fictional kingship around the one thing that can actually reach her — her own vanity and craving for worldly status — and only once that craving is satisfied does he redirect it toward the ten wholesome actions. The past-life account that follows complicates any easy reading of her present hostility as mere ignorance: her original fault was not disbelief but a specific, prideful misreading of the teaching of emptiness itself, mistaking a profound doctrine correctly taught for meaningless nihilism — a much subtler failure than simple stinginess, and one the compendium seems to regard as considerably graver.

"Behold the Fully Awakened One's own / gentle, golden-hued body — / he tames even those still untamed, / and delivers all beings without limit."

The husband went and saw the Buddha, who spoke this verse to him:

"Suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path — / turn toward nirvāṇa itself; / whoever sees this Dharma directly / attains what has no equal."

Hearing this Dharma, his mind opened in understanding, and he went forth under the Buddha; contemplating in solitude and stillness, he attained arhatship. His wife, and their granddaughter Sundarī-Nandā, likewise went forth, and both reached the very limit of suffering (Source: Saṃyukta Āgama, fascicle 34).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story's own structure gives Vāsiṣṭhī, not her husband, the first and fuller access to liberating insight — she teaches him in verse, quoting the Dharma back with evident fluency, before he has heard any of it himself — and it is specifically her composure at the seventh death, set directly against her own earlier madness at the first six, that becomes the visible proof his own question responds to. Grief here is never treated as something to be suppressed or denied; rather, the story distinguishes clearly between grief rooted in a mistaken sense of permanence and a settled understanding that changes what a shared, inevitable loss actually means.

11. The Poor Widow's Daughter Who Became a Queen

Fascicle 23's longest entry, a novella in its own right: a destitute widow's daughter, who has secretly vowed to marry a king who himself serves the Buddha as a god, is discovered by a besotted monarch after a crow carries his stray arrow into her own household — and rises through sheer force of character from a servant's threatened door to queen of the realm, before renouncing the throne entirely to seek her own liberation.

Chinese Source

舍衛城。有孤獨母人。自生一女。年始十七。顏容端嚴衣不蔽形。母乞食自連。女貞賢明達。博讀經書守節不出門戶。居近王道而心願適王。又願事神如佛。王出行國內。見烏在貧女門上鳴。王便舉弓射烏。烏持王箭走入女家。王傍人追烏入舍。女不出面。但拔箭放烏授箭擲外。王人見指知之非凡。却後年中。王第一夫人卒。娉求夫人。無應相者。廣訪人間。左右白言。前時射烏窮獨母女。年十六七雖不見面。瞻手聞聲似是貴人。王便往視呼將俱來。使相師占之使到女門直入其內。女母不在唯獨女住。使呼女出三呼不應。使曰。貧家女出來。女應曰。才不才各一家子。卿是何人唐突我舍。急出門外。使人曰。國王遣我呼汝。汝小家子何敢爾耶。女曰。國王何事使汝來。若求我作婢者。我家不犯王法。若求我作婦者。汝曹則是我給使。何敢輕易貧門。乘勢蹜突迫愶女人。女人雖微。自有宿命不為威屈。汝曹啟王。使人敢應。具以啟王。王便勅百官。嚴駕備具禮儀。從五百婇女而往迎之。使者齎持謁敬牛羊猪酒納娉之饌。使者乘皂蓋之車。五馬引御以迎女。前導精兵十萬。從車千乘。細馬萬匹。散從十二萬人。鳴鍾伐鼓聲動天地。觀者無數往到女門。使者下車前通謁敬。女敬令曰。謝使者及諸百官吏民。大小勞屈道路。孤貧之女鄙賤不遺。遠煩官屬侍從。抂屈辱王重命。女以寒賤誤以宿福。當王階殿自揆醜陋。德無女儀。容無桃華優曇之色。聲無絃管入耳之聽。智無神聖大人之明。識無聖人萬里之見。身無紫金光潤之澤。氣無栴檀香麝之芳。言無忠和仁善之美。行無進退高下之節。謬忝王命徒勞觀者。使者及百官無不累息。語諸婇女。汝曹薄福墮此卑役。自今以往相與從事。宜各正心修善為行。莫犯王儀。五百婇女皆應唯諾。又呼二千五百青衣。語之曰。人無貴賤道在者尊。汝等皆善人之子。古今之變處在卑微。莫以自卑當務為善。僉曰奉教。百官前進王禮。黃金千䴵。白銀二千䴵。華瑞瓔珞水精琉璃。明月神珠珊瑚琥珀。白素千匹御縑千匹。綺繒綿羅朱繡紫綠。素黃赤白羅縠之衣。栴檀之香種種千匹。百官進衣百領夫人服飾。女便受之嚴莊已辦。出拜上位。百官見之莫不歡喜。皆稱萬歲國必大平。得我百姓萬民之母。婇女青衣親近附之如子得母。攝衣上車車為端安。馬為調御天為清明。白象雲集相追悲鳴。百官臣民皆言。天人來下國必保安。到王宮門自然開闢。五百侍從入宮下車。宮中自然生五色之光。殿上晃耀內外照明。入殿拜王。王見驚悚不知。下座答之。大夫人朝王。百拜跪如流水。自跪自起百拜乃止。王長跪答之。教令宮內。柔和仁善不傷他意。王愛敬之。徐言少語語必合義。內外敬伏。入宮七日七夜不寢不寐。與諸夫人婇女共相娛樂。王意欲見之。夫人意貞心潔不在濁穢。本意清淨不與王語。又不相見。王曰。室家之義夫婦之分。以恩愛為親。夫人入宮七日七夜。而不相見。其意云何。以為法乎。夫人使婇女答王曰。本生有二願。今已得一。未得一也。故未相見。王曰。已得何等。未得何等。夫人答曰。我少小結願。使婿如國王事神如佛。王曰。可一相見。後當為往呼佛。夫人曰不也。未得見佛。終不與王相見。王大怒曰。汝本寒賤乞人種。何敢違萬乘之意。夫人曰。婦人繫命於夫婿耳。東西唯命。我有宿誓不可負也。王者雖尊。不得抂問無辜之人。王曰。吾萬乘之主。犯者便誅。豈問抂直。夫人曰。王者百姓父母。斷截當以道理。何得自喜自怒。凡夫所不行。豈況國王耶。王曰。從吾心者原。不從者誅。夫人曰。女人亦有微心。得從心者乃相見耳。不得從心者。終不相見。王曰。奈何請之耶。夫人曰。佛在我國請之必來。王曰。恐百官笑人耳。夫人曰。王請佛入宮。上殿百官自當歡喜。世世受福不為笑王也。王曰。大善。即日勅厨作十萬人饌具。遣侍女白夫人言。請佛作十萬人饌已具。可往請佛。夫人曰。婦人唯命是從。請佛之義大王宜自抂車騎。暫往與佛相見深恭敬之。王曰。我請我自往。卿請卿自往。吾不往也。夫人言。王者尊貴是妾之夫。女人之力不如王者之微言。王抂威暫往與佛相見。不者非大王家人也。未有夫婦室家之義。故自他人耳。王曰。夫婦未成則無分義在卿意耳。夫人默默不言。光輝暐曄顏色非凡。王疑夫人恐欲自殺。或恐突去。勅內外游徼司候。備衛吏兵關閉宮門。步羅相連飛鳥不得度。宮內婇女展轉相次使針不得下。夫人知王意不往請佛。及不信夫人。夫人便於殿上化滅。身體了無所見。婇女千人并七十一夫人。皆驚相視分布求索。內外羅落了無所見。王大慚怖銜泣而起。王曰。奈何奈何。我剛直所致追以為恨。以何方便而使之還。第二夫人白王言。大夫人。非我凡庶小人輩也。王應當稱隨其意。而忽拒逆喜怒惡言待之。此乃天人。王令去大夫人者。我曹等七十一人。何所依恃耶。婇女青衣皆悉啼哭。如有死者。公卿男女亦皆悲怨。今我國中誰所依怙。百鳥嘯鳴天地大動。王感激欲死。向天大息泣落如雨。民人不修事業市里空廢。十十五五相隨悲哀。七日七夜王安臥絕穀不食。第二夫人曰。且當暫與佛相見。并請俱來。王到佛所頭面為禮。白佛言。我是阿祇羅奈國王阿迦達留。如是者三須逋替禮敬。連務國事不待日夕。佛言。國事勞擾眾猥煩惱誠抂神思。吏民境界平安不乎。王曰。皆受佛恩。唯願抂屈。暫臨鄙解。佛默然。須臾佛到殿上。大夫人。與諸夫人婇女三千五百七十一人俱為佛作禮。王初未見夫人。婇女亦不見。唯佛自見。王與佛作禮畢心愁不歡。佛語王言。所求大夫人者。正此人是也。王見歡喜。百官夫人婇女大小皆亦歡喜。王設飲食。食畢佛大呪願。各求心中所欲。王白佛言。我大夫人。本貧賤孤獨母之女。今為大夫人。復能滅身幻化如是。有何罪福致斯。尊貴在一人之上。得為百姓之母。仁德賢慈言語柔軟不傷人意。隱形七日人鬼神龍不知其所。佛告言。大夫人。昔為大迦羅越。財富無數慳貪不施。故今貧窮。迦羅越性好婬妷。樂與女人從事。今故墮女人中。又樂與明經道士遊。學故有智慧。已見五百佛。今得值我。昔與王為善知識。故得為夫婦。大夫人先世事五百佛。但持心不堅。嬈惱世人故墮女人中。大夫人却後三十九劫。當得作佛。王亦以七生天上。七生世間。王生天時。大夫人為天女。侍王左右。常得王意。王與大夫人結願俱死。生生相隨俱為解脫。王自憍奢制意喜怒。一切不復更見也。王心大歡喜。即得阿惟越致道。便授王五戒。夫人婇女大小公卿百官舉國人民皆奉五戒。大夫人前受十戒。歲三齋。月六齋。與後宮婇女三千五百七十一人。從事相率為善。王正教寬弘國致太平。興立塔寺皆以七寶。更相尊敬內外無別離之心。人民死皆以壽終。王大夫人覺意念世無常。人晝夜婬妷恩愛無明濁穢。生無賢名。死為不淨之鬼。便長歎易服敗狀亂頭。前白王言。天下一切無常皆當歸死。王所以貪我者。為我年少顏色肌膚滑澤氣息香潔。是皆非常皆當歸死。夫婦合會略無可奇是皆不淨。恩愛於此當有老病。至來無期誰當為我却之者哉。我今愁怖災害卒至不可得脫。一切貴賤因此一事。皆當膖脹臭處不淨。空愛惜之夫復何益。王自思惟。男女合會有何可奇。我言真諦非不及事。王言。卿是天人種。所言不妄也。皆得事體無不可者。國人男女大小皆相恃賴。唯復照察留念也。大夫人言。我是女人。不得自制故以啟王。大王可相發遣以副宿命之本願。王言。我年欲老。雖有諸夫人婇女小兒輩。無一人可我意者。卿當卒我餘年。終亡之後當以後事相付。太子尚小。奈何當相捨去。至何所耶。夫人言。我生死無常厭是變化。欲從佛求作沙門。王言。卿是一國之母為上夫人。諸國夫人宮內婇女大小皆恃賴卿。卿奈何忽規立此計。遠近士夫聞之不當笑人如此沙門輩非卿夫人種所志作也宜更計之。夫人言。十方諸佛皆從沙門中來。佛迦沙王。捨九十九小國夫人婇女八九千人。求作沙門。前後男女求作沙門者不少。非獨我一女人也。王以恩愛貪欲華色愛惜我身。我身皆膿血惡露不淨。女人難與從事。人有與女人從事無不墮罪中者。女人之身熱於湯火。燒炙於人令墮重罪。千劫不脫。我前世亦為男子。但坐與女人從事。恩愛多故去男為女。女人可畏。王不覺耳。王意不能與女人相離者。夫人婇女故三千餘人足相娛樂。王意宜發遣無令愁惱也。我作沙門死死不止。王當相勸不宜相割。王曰。卿若爾者我用國為。夫人言。王能知無常者是王上願。國土王位百官婇女珍寶宮城皆非王有。王有身體骸骨頭腦五藏。皆當分散。何所愛惜乎。王言。今日聽卿一人耳。恐我夫人婇女相追俱去。夫人言。各有宿命福力。非人所止。悉加刀杖事應當爾。終不得止。正希我身得發遣耳。王當以恩愛慰愍諸人。自當親近附王。王言。此諸人慕樂卿德。我一男子何能善意周遍此耶。我唯恃卿當為正御。卿今捨我。此諸女人持刀斬殺乃當畏我。夫人言。不也。女人以男子為正耳。王留意近之仍當自安。王言。在卿耳。夫人便呼諸夫人婇女三千人。諫謂之言。卿諸女人。善親近大王威尊致貴莫得輕慢。我今與卿等別。各自努力勤意朝夕。親奉經法加於精進。願我早得佛道。當還相度。諸夫人婇女便舉聲大哭。聞忉利天。帝釋即下授與袈裟。自然著身髮便墮地。授五百戒為比丘尼。夫人婇女皆大歡喜。帝釋復授袈裟食器為沙門。王得知足晏然不怒。忻然大笑曰。諸賢者功德巍巍豈不快也。即得道跡王還宮內。意甚罔罔。出呼太子以國委之。王便願言。我今欲作沙門誰知我者。帝釋便剃王頭。袈裟著身應器自具。便隨佛去。十二小國王。皆捨國付太子。隨王作沙門。一時同得阿羅漢道。阿迦達留者我身是。大夫人者瞿夷是(出貧女為國王夫人經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there lived a widow, alone and without support, who had borne a single daughter, now seventeen years old, of dignified and lovely countenance, though so poor that her clothing scarcely covered her own body. The mother sustained them both by begging; the daughter herself was chaste, virtuous, and clear-minded, widely read in the scriptures, keeping her own conduct within the household and never once stepping outside its door — though it stood near the king's own road, and her secret heart's wish was to marry a king, and to serve the divine just as the Buddha himself was served.

One day the king, traveling through his own realm, saw a crow perched cawing on the poor girl's own doorway, and raised his bow to shoot it. The crow, struck by the king's own arrow, fled with the shaft still in it into the girl's household. The king's attendants pursued the crow into the house; the girl did not show her own face, but simply drew the arrow out, released the crow, and tossed the arrow back outside. The king's men, seeing this, sensed she was no ordinary person.

A year later, the king's own chief consort died. He sought a new consort, but none among those proposed seemed fitting, and he inquired widely throughout his own realm. His attendants told him: there was, some time ago, the daughter of a poor, solitary widow, from whom your own arrow was retrieved — she is sixteen or seventeen now; though we never saw her face, the sight of her hand and the sound of her voice suggested someone of noble bearing.

The king went himself to see her, and summoned her to come; he had a physiognomist sent to her own household first, going directly inside. The girl's mother was away, and she was alone. The messenger called for her to come out; three times he called, and she did not answer. He said: girl of this poor household, come out! She answered: talented or not, each family has its own child — who are you, that you burst so rudely into my own house? Get out of my door at once! The messenger said: the king himself has sent me to summon you — how dare a girl of so lowly a household speak this way? She answered: what business does the king have, that he sends you here? If he seeks me as a mere servant, my own household has broken no law of his; if he seeks me as a wife, then you yourselves would be my own attendants — how dare you treat a poor household with such contempt, forcing your own way in and threatening a woman like this? A woman, however humble, still has her own destiny, and shall not be cowed by mere show of authority. Report this to your king as it is. The messenger did not dare answer further, and reported the whole matter to the king exactly as it had happened.

The king then commanded his own officials to prepare a grand procession with full ceremony, sending five hundred attendant women to receive her. The envoys carried offerings of cattle, sheep, pigs, and wine as betrothal gifts; the chief envoy rode a black-canopied carriage drawn by five horses to receive the girl, preceded by a hundred thousand elite soldiers, followed by a thousand carriages, ten thousand fine horses, and a hundred and twenty thousand attendants on foot, bells ringing and drums sounding loud enough to shake heaven and earth, with countless onlookers gathering as the procession arrived at the girl's own door.

The envoy descended from his carriage and announced the king's own respects. The girl sent this reply: my thanks to the envoy and to every official and officer present, for the great trouble of this long journey — that a poor and lowly girl such as myself should not be overlooked, but instead troubled by such an entourage, and honored by the king's own weighty command. I have, through some accident of past merit, come to this — yet, weighed against the steps of a royal palace, I must reckon myself plain and unworthy: my own virtue lacks the proper bearing of a lady, my own appearance lacks the color of peach blossom or udumbara flower, my own voice lacks the pleasing quality of string and flute, my own understanding lacks the clarity of the sagely and great, my own knowledge lacks the sage's own vision reaching ten thousand miles, my own body lacks the lustrous sheen of fine gold, my own scent lacks the fragrance of sandalwood or musk, my own speech lacks the beauty of loyal, gentle, and benevolent words, and my own conduct lacks any proper measure of advance or retreat, high or low. To wrongly accept the king's own command now would only trouble everyone who has come to see me in vain.

The envoy and every official present, hearing this, could not help but sigh in admiration. She then spoke to the attendant women: you have all, through thin fortune, fallen into this lowly service — from this day forward, as we work together, let each of you set your own heart right, and cultivate good conduct, and never transgress the proper bearing due a king's own household. The five hundred attendant women all answered as one: we obey. She then summoned the two thousand five hundred servant-girls as well, and told them: among people there is no true noble or lowly rank — only the presence of the path itself is truly honored. You are all children of good people, and it is only the shifting fortunes of past and present that have placed you now in humble station — do not, because of this, think yourselves unworthy, but strive instead to do good. They all answered together: we shall follow your instruction.

The officials then presented the ceremonial gifts befitting a queen: a thousand measures of gold, two thousand of silver, ornamental necklaces of crystal and lapis lazuli, luminous pearls, coral, and amber, a thousand bolts of plain white silk and a thousand of fine woven silk, embroidered and patterned brocades in crimson, purple, and green, robes of plain, yellow, red, and white gauze, and a thousand bolts of every kind of sandalwood incense. A hundred officials came forward bearing a hundred robes of a queen's own attire. The girl accepted these, and, once properly adorned, came out to take her own honored place. Every official, seeing her, rejoiced, proclaiming: ten thousand years to the kingdom — it shall surely know great peace now, having gained a true mother to its own hundred families and myriad people! The attendant women and servant girls alike drew close to her as though a child finding its own mother.

She gathered her own robes and mounted the carriage, which moved with perfect ease; the horses were calm and well-guided, the sky itself grew clear and bright, and white elephants gathered like clouds, trumpeting mournfully in procession behind her. Every official and subject said: a being from heaven has descended among us — our kingdom shall surely know lasting peace. Arriving at the palace gate, it opened of itself; her five hundred attendants entered the palace and dismounted, and within the palace a light of five colors arose of its own accord, illuminating the halls both within and without.

She entered the hall and bowed before the king, who, seeing her, was so struck with awe that he did not know what to do, and rose from his own seat to answer her bow. The chief consort, in audience before the king, bowed a hundred times, kneeling and rising with a grace like flowing water, kneeling and rising a full hundred times before stopping; the king knelt at length to answer her in turn.

Within the palace, she governed with gentleness, harmony, and benevolence, never once wounding another's own feelings; the king cherished and honored her greatly. She spoke sparingly, and always in accordance with what was right, so that everyone, within the palace and without, held her in reverent awe. She remained in the palace seven days and seven nights without sleeping at all, taking her ease together with the other consorts and attendant women — yet, though the king wished to see her himself, her own resolve was chaste and her heart pure, holding nothing to do with defilement, and, true to her own original purity of intent, she neither spoke with the king nor let herself be seen by him.

The king said: the very meaning of a household, the very bond between husband and wife, rests on mutual affection as its own foundation — yet my consort has been in this palace seven days and seven nights, and I have not so much as seen her. What is the meaning of this? Is this some rule of her own? The consort had her attendant women answer the king on her behalf: I made two vows in my early years — one of these has now been fulfilled; the other has not — and it is for this reason that I have not yet let myself be seen. The king asked: which have you fulfilled, and which remains? She answered: from my youth, I vowed that my own husband should be a king, and that he should serve the divine just as the Buddha is served. The king said: then let us meet just once — afterward, I shall go and invite the Buddha myself. She answered: no — until I have seen the Buddha, I shall never let myself be seen by the king at all.

The king, greatly angered, said: you were born of poor, lowly, begging stock — how dare you defy the wish of a sovereign of ten thousand chariots? She answered: a woman's own life is bound to her husband — east or west, I follow only his command; yet I hold a vow made long ago that I cannot betray. However exalted a king may be, he ought not to wrongly interrogate one who has done no wrong. The king said: I am the sovereign of ten thousand chariots — whoever transgresses against me is simply put to death; why should I trouble myself over questions of right or wrong? She answered: a king is father and mother to his own people — his judgments ought to follow what is right, not his own passing pleasure or anger; even an ordinary person would not act so carelessly — how much less a king?

The king said: whoever follows my own will is pardoned; whoever does not is put to death. She answered: a woman, too, holds her own small resolve of heart — only one who can follow that resolve may truly meet with me; one who cannot shall never meet with me at all. The king said: how, then, am I to invite him? She answered: the Buddha is present in your own kingdom — invite him, and he shall surely come. The king said: I fear the whole court will mock me for this. She answered: should the king invite the Buddha into the palace, and receive him in the hall, every official there shall in fact rejoice, and receive blessing across every generation to come — no one shall mock the king for this at all.

The king said: very good — and at once ordered the kitchens to prepare a feast fit for a hundred thousand guests, and sent a servant to tell the consort: the feast for the Buddha, fit for a hundred thousand, is now ready — you may go and invite him. She answered: a woman follows only her husband's own command in such matters — but the proper meaning of inviting the Buddha requires that the great king himself go in his own carriage, to meet the Buddha in person, and show him due reverence. The king said: whoever extends the invitation should go themselves — if you invite him, you go; I myself shall not go. She said: a king's own honor is exalted, and he is, besides, my own husband — a woman's own strength counts for less than even a king's slightest word. Should the king, setting aside his own majesty, go himself, even briefly, to meet the Buddha, only then would this truly be the household of a great king; otherwise, there would be no true bond of husband and wife between us at all — we should remain, in truth, strangers to one another. The king said: if we are not yet truly husband and wife, then this matter rests entirely on your own will.

The consort said nothing further, her own radiance shining forth, her countenance beyond the ordinary. The king, suspecting she might mean to take her own life, or flee, ordered the whole palace, within and without, placed under guard, sentries posted, the gates sealed shut, guards stationed so closely along every wall that not even a bird could pass, and the attendant women within the palace arranged so close together that not even a needle could fall between them.

The consort, knowing the king had no intention of going himself to invite the Buddha, and that he did not trust her either, then vanished upon the very dais where she stood — her whole body gone, nothing left to see at all. The thousand attendant women, together with the seventy-one other consorts, all looked at one another in alarm, and searched everywhere, within the palace and without, but found no trace of her at all.

The king, overwhelmed with shame and fear, rose weeping, and said: what now, what now? It is my own stubbornness that has brought this about — I shall regret it forever. By what means might she be brought back? The second consort said to the king: our chief consort is no ordinary person at all — the king ought to have followed her own wish, yet instead met her with resistance, anger, and harsh words. She must truly be a being from the heavens themselves. If the king has driven our chief consort away, what refuge remains for the rest of us, we seventy-one consorts? The attendant women and servant girls all wept aloud, as though someone had died; the ministers and courtiers, men and women alike, grieved and lamented as well: on whom shall our kingdom now depend? A hundred birds cried out in mourning, and heaven and earth themselves shook. The king, overcome with anguish to the point of wishing for death, wept toward heaven, his tears falling like rain; the people neglected their own trades, the markets stood empty, and small groups gathered everywhere in sorrow. For seven days and seven nights the king lay in bed, refusing all food.

The second consort said: let the king, at least, go and meet the Buddha, and invite him here together with the rest. The king went before the Buddha, bowed at his feet, and said: I am King Akāḍaliu of the country of Ajiraṇa — he said this three times over, delayed each time by matters of urgent state that could not wait even a single day.

The Buddha said: the affairs of state are indeed troublesome and burdensome, truly enough to weigh upon one's own spirit — are your officials and people, and the borders of your realm, at peace? The king said: all this we owe to the Buddha's own grace — I beg only that you might condescend to visit my own humble palace, and resolve this matter for me. The Buddha remained silent in assent.

Before long, the Buddha arrived at the palace hall, where the chief consort, together with three thousand five hundred and seventy-one other consorts and attendant women, all bowed before him. The king himself, at first, could not see his own consort at all, nor could the attendant women see her — only the Buddha could see her. The king, having paid homage to the Buddha, remained troubled and joyless. The Buddha told the king: the very consort you have sought is this person here before you now.

The king, seeing her at last, was overjoyed, and every official, consort, and attendant present rejoiced as well. The king laid out a feast; once it was finished, the Buddha pronounced a great dedication of blessing, and each person there sought the fulfillment of their own heart's wish.

The king said to the Buddha: my own chief consort was once the poor, lowly daughter of a solitary widow, and is now my chief consort, capable even of vanishing her own body through such transformation as this — what past transgression, and what merit, could account for this? How has she come to such honor above all others, becoming a mother to my own people, benevolent, virtuous, and wise, her every word gentle and never wounding — and how did she vanish for seven days, unseen even by man, spirit, ghost, or dragon?

The Buddha told him: your chief consort was, in a past life, a great and wealthy householder, whose riches were beyond counting, yet who was stingy and gave nothing away — this is why she is now born into poverty. That householder, moreover, was by nature given to lustful indulgence, delighting in relations with women — this is why she has now fallen into a woman's own body. Yet that householder also loved to keep company with learned scholars and practitioners of the path — this is why she now possesses such wisdom, and has already beheld five hundred buddhas in past lives, and now has encountered me as well. In a past life, moreover, she and you, great king, were close spiritual friends to one another — this is why you have now become husband and wife. Your chief consort, in past lives, served five hundred buddhas — yet, because her own resolve was not firm, and she troubled others in the world, she fell into a woman's own body because of this. Thirty-nine kalpas from now, your chief consort shall attain buddhahood herself. You too, great king, shall be reborn seven times in heaven, and seven times in the human world; and when you are reborn in heaven, your chief consort shall be a heavenly maiden there, attending you at your own side, always according with your own wishes. You and your chief consort made a vow together to die at the very same moment, and to remain together across every life to come, until both attain liberation together. You yourself, great king, should set aside your own arrogance and indulgence, and master your own emotions of joy and anger — you shall not see her vanish in this way again.

The king's heart was filled with great joy, and he attained the stage of non-retrogression at once. The Buddha then conferred the five precepts upon him, and the consorts, attendant women, ministers, officials, and the whole population of the kingdom all took up the five precepts as well. The chief consort, for her own part, received the ten precepts, keeping the three annual fasts and the six monthly fasts, and, together with three thousand five hundred and seventy-one women of the inner palace, devoted herself to doing good. The king's own governance grew broad and generous, and the kingdom came to know great peace; he built stūpas and temples adorned with the seven treasures, and all within his realm honored one another, with no thought of division between them. The people, when they died, all died of natural old age.

The king's own chief consort, awakening to the thought of the world's impermanence — that people, indulging day and night in lust and craving born of ignorance and defilement, live without gaining any true name of worthiness, and die only to become impure spirits — sighed at length, changed into plain clothing, disheveled her own hair and appearance, and came before the king, saying: everything under heaven is impermanent, and must in the end return to death. That which drew the king's own affection toward me was only my youth, my complexion, my smooth and glowing skin, my fragrant breath — yet all these are themselves impermanent, and must in the end return to death. The union of husband and wife holds, in truth, nothing extraordinary in it at all — it too is impure. Where there is such love and attachment, old age and sickness must follow; and when they arrive, with no fixed time set, who shall turn them back on my own behalf? I am now filled with dread, that calamity may strike suddenly, with no escape possible; and, whatever their own rank, high or low, all beings alike, through this one same cause, must in the end swell, rot, and stink with impurity — what use, then, is there in clinging so dearly to it?

The king reflected on this himself: what, in truth, is so extraordinary about the union of man and woman? What she says is the plain truth, and misses nothing of the matter. The king said: you are truly of the lineage of heavenly beings — what you speak is never false, and holds true in every particular, without exception. The people of this kingdom, men and women, young and old, all depend upon you — I ask only that you continue to watch over them, and keep them in your own thoughts.

The chief consort said: I am, after all, a woman, unable to govern myself entirely on my own account, and so I bring this before the king now — great king, please release me, that my own long-held original vow might be fulfilled. The king said: I am growing old now, and, though I have other consorts, attendant women, and young children, none of them truly suit my own heart as you do — you alone should remain to see out the rest of my years, and, once I have passed away, I would entrust everything that follows to you. Our crown prince is still young — how could you now abandon us, and where would you even go?

The consort said: I am weary of this ceaseless impermanence of birth and death, and its endless changes — I wish to seek from the Buddha to become a renunciant. The king said: you are mother to this whole kingdom, its highest consort — every consort in every allied kingdom, and every woman within this very palace, young and old, depends upon you. How could you suddenly resolve upon such a plan as this? Should distant and near alike hear of it, would they not mock us, saying that such a path is unfit for a queen of your own standing? You ought to reconsider this.

The consort said: the buddhas of the ten directions have all, without exception, come forth from among renunciants. The Buddha Kāśyapa's own king abandoned ninety-nine lesser kingdoms, and eight or nine thousand consorts and attendant women, to seek to become a renunciant himself; and, before and since, no small number of men and women alike have sought to become renunciants — I am not the only woman to wish this. The king holds me dear only out of desire and attachment to my own youthful beauty — yet this body of mine is nothing but pus, blood, and impure discharge in truth. It is difficult to have relations with women at all; there is no one who takes up relations with a woman who does not, in the end, fall into transgression through it. A woman's own body burns hotter than boiling water or fire — it scorches whoever comes near it, and causes them to fall into grave transgression, from which they cannot escape for a thousand kalpas. I myself, in a past life, was a man — yet, entirely because of relations with women, and the strength of that attachment, I lost my own male form and became a woman instead. Women are truly to be feared — the king simply does not perceive this.

Should the king's own heart still be unable to part from women entirely, there remain more than three thousand other consorts and attendant women, quite sufficient for his own pleasure — the king ought to release me, and not let this cause him further grief. Should I become a renunciant, I would rather die again and again than turn back from it — the king should encourage me in this, not try to hold me back.

The king said: if this is truly so, then what use do I have for this whole kingdom at all? The consort said: that the king can come to know impermanence is itself the king's own highest attainment. This land, the throne, every official, every attendant woman, every treasure, this very palace and city — none of it truly belongs to the king at all. What the king does possess — this body, these bones, this skull, these five organs — must all, in the end, come apart and scatter. What is there left to cling to so dearly?

The king said: today I shall allow you alone to go — yet I fear my other consorts and attendant women may follow after you as well. The consort said: each of them has her own destiny and store of merit, which no other person can hold in place — should you set guards and weapons upon them all, it would only happen regardless in the end; it can never truly be prevented. I ask only that my own release be granted; the king should console and comfort the others with kindness and affection, and they shall naturally draw close to the king in turn.

The king said: these women all cherish and delight in your own virtue — how could I, a single man, ever attend to them all so completely and well as you have? I have relied on you alone as their true guide — now that you leave me, these very women, blade in hand, might well come to fear me instead. The consort said: no — women, in truth, take a man as their own proper guide; should the king only give them his attention and draw near to them, they shall naturally find their own peace. The king said: then it rests with you.

The consort then summoned the three thousand other consorts and attendant women, and counseled them, saying: you, my sisters, should draw close to the great king with care, honoring his own exalted majesty, and never treat him with contempt. I now take my leave of you all — each of you, strive with diligence, morning and evening; devote yourselves to the scriptures and the teaching, applying yourselves ever more diligently. May I attain the buddha's own path swiftly, that I might return and deliver you all in turn.

The consorts and attendant women wept aloud together, their cries reaching even Trāyastriṃśa heaven. Śakra himself descended at once, and bestowed monastic robes upon her, which appeared upon her body of their own accord, her own hair falling away; he conferred upon her the five hundred precepts, and she became a nun. The consorts and attendant women all rejoiced greatly, and Śakra bestowed robes and an alms-bowl upon them as well, and they too became renunciants.

The king, having attained a genuine contentment, remained calm and free of anger, and, laughing with delight, said: how magnificent is the merit of these worthy ones — is this not truly excellent? He himself attained a glimpse of the path at once.

The king returned to his own palace, his mind still in a daze, and, going out, summoned the crown prince, and entrusted the whole kingdom to him. The king then made this vow: I too now wish to become a renunciant — who shall recognize this wish of mine? Śakra at once shaved the king's own head, monastic robes and an alms-bowl appeared complete upon him, and he followed the Buddha away. Twelve lesser kings likewise abandoned their own kingdoms to their own crown princes, and followed the king to become renunciants themselves — and all of them, together, attained the fruit of arhatship at the very same time.

King Akāḍaliu was myself; and the chief consort was Gopī (Source: Sūtra of the Poor Woman Who Became a King's Consort).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is, by a wide margin, the longest single entry translated in this project to date — a genuine novella whose central figure, unnamed throughout beyond her own social position, negotiates her way through an entire kingdom's assumptions about her worth using nothing but the precision of her own speech, first refusing an intrusive royal summons on equal footing, then refusing the king's own physical presence until her prior vow is honored, then finally refusing the throne itself once its purpose in her own life has been served. The king's escalating fury at each refusal, and his own eventual capitulation each time, trace a slow, deliberate education in a king's proper relationship to a wife's autonomy — one the text never treats as automatic or owed, but as something she must repeatedly, patiently win.

12. Śrīmatī Forgets Her Own Hunger to Feed the Saṅgha

During a famine, the nun Śrīmatī gives away every meal she receives to hungry monks before eating anything herself — a chain of unbroken generosity that runs five hundred deep before it finally breaks her own body.

Chinese Source

佛住毘善離。語諸比丘。我聞尼中福德第一者。有尸利摩。時世飢饉乞食難得。時尸利摩比丘尼入城乞食。見比丘即問言。尊者得食不。比丘即以空鉢示之。便持己鉢中食與比丘。比丘得食還精舍。喚餘比丘共食。諸比丘問言。長老何處得是好食。答言。尸利摩比丘尼邊得。諸比丘聞已各各往索。如是次第乃至五百比丘。盡皆得食。然後自求。日時已過。失食還到精舍。明日晨朝。諸比丘復著衣持鉢。至比丘尼精舍門立。比丘尼見已即入。語尸利摩言。諸比丘今在門外相待。尸利摩聞已語弟子。取衣鉢來。我為諸上尊乞食。如是次第供給五百人已然後自求。日時復過失食而還。至第三日亦復如是。乃至次第供給五百人。唯一人未得。此比丘隨尸利摩後入一家。以先三日失食故。身體虛羸迷悶倒地(出僧祇律二十一卷十誦四分並同)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Vaiśālī, and told the monks: I have heard that, among the nuns, the one foremost in merit and virtue is Śrīmatī. At that time there was a famine in the land, and alms were hard to obtain.

The nun Śrīmatī, entering the city to beg, met a monk, and asked him: venerable one, have you obtained any food? The monk showed her his own empty bowl in answer; she at once took the food from her own bowl, and gave it to him. The monk, receiving this food, returned to the monastery, and called the other monks to share it with him. The monks asked: elder, where did you obtain such fine food? He answered: I received it from the nun Śrīmatī. The other monks, hearing this, each went in turn to ask her for food as well, and so it continued, one after another, until five hundred monks in all had received food from her — and only then did she seek food for herself. But the proper hour had already passed, and, having missed her own meal, she returned to the monastery without eating.

The next morning, the monks again robed themselves, took up their bowls, and came to stand at the gate of the nuns' own monastery. The nun, seeing them, went in at once, and told Śrīmatī: the monks are now waiting outside the gate. Śrīmatī, hearing this, told her own disciple: bring my robe and bowl — I shall go beg for these venerable ones. And so, once again, she supplied five hundred monks in turn before seeking food for herself — and, once again, the proper hour had already passed, and she returned without eating.

On the third day, the very same thing happened: she supplied five hundred monks in turn, and only a single monk remained unfed. This last monk followed behind Śrīmatī into one final household — but, her own body already weakened and depleted from three days without food, she collapsed to the ground in a faint (Source: Sāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 21; the same account is also found, consistently, in the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya and the Four-Part Vinaya).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story's own arithmetic is stark and deliberate: not one act of generosity but three consecutive days of it, five hundred monks fed each time before Śrīmatī allows herself to eat, and it is precisely this repetition — never framed as excessive or self-destructive by the text itself — that earns her the Buddha's own explicit designation as foremost in merit among the nuns. Her collapse on the third day is presented without any note of caution or correction; the compendium seems content to let the sheer, unbroken consistency of the act stand as its own justification.

13. Cañcā's Past Life as a Turtle's Wife

The nun Cañcā, whose false accusation against the Buddha was already told in fascicle 19, is traced here to a jealous past-life wife who feigns illness to have her own husband betray a devoted friend — a jātaka whose animal cast makes explicit the pattern behind her slander.

Chinese Source

有暴志比丘尼者。反懷惡信謗佛毀僧。佛言。不但今世。過去無數劫時。一獼猴王居在林樹食果飲水。時念一切蚑行喘息人物之類。皆欲令度使至無為。時有一鼈以為知友。鼈數往來到獼猴所。飲食言談說正義理。其婦見之。謂有婬盪問。夫為何所至。答曰。吾與獼猴共結親友。甚聰明智慧又曉義理。婦猶不信。因便佯病。困劣著地治不肯差。謂夫言。吾病甚重。得卿所親獼猴肝。乃當活耳。夫答曰。吾寄身託命。云何以活卿耶。婦曰。夫婦同共一體不念相濟。反為獼猴夫敬重婦。往請獼猴共食。獼猴答曰。吾家陸地。卿在水中。安得相從。鼈曰。吾當負卿。獼猴從之負到中道語獼猴言。婦病須卿肝。獼猴曰。何不早道。吾肝掛樹不齎將來。從還取肝乃相從耳。便還樹上跳梁歡喜。鼈問曰。卿應取肝來。到我家去。反更跳梁何耶。獼猴答曰。天下至愚無過於汝。共為親友寄身託命。還欲見危。鼈婦暴志是。鼈調達是。獼猴王我是(出鼈獼猴經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a nun named Cañcā, whose own heart, given over to malice, slandered the Buddha and vilified the Saṅgha. The Buddha said: it is not only in this present life that she has done this. In a past age, beyond counting, there was a monkey king, who dwelt among the forest trees, eating fruit and drinking water, and who constantly wished that every creeping, breathing thing, every human and animal alike, might be delivered to the unconditioned state.

He had, as a close friend, a turtle, who came often to visit the monkey king, eating and drinking together with him, and speaking of true principle and right meaning. The turtle's own wife, seeing this, suspected some illicit affair, and asked her husband where he kept going. He answered: I have formed a close friendship with a monkey king — he is exceedingly clever and wise, and understands true principle well.

His wife did not believe this, and, feigning illness, lay collapsed and weak upon the ground, refusing to recover, and told her husband: my own illness is grave — only your dear friend the monkey's own liver could save me now. Her husband answered: I have entrusted my very life to him as a friend — how could I possibly use it to save you instead? His wife said: husband and wife are one and the same body — you give no thought at all to saving me, yet still hold this monkey in such high regard.

The husband, cherishing his wife, went to invite the monkey to share a meal together. The monkey answered: my own home is on dry land, while you dwell in the water — how could we possibly travel together? The turtle said: I shall carry you myself. The monkey agreed, and the turtle carried him partway, and then told the monkey: my wife is ill, and needs your own liver to be cured. The monkey said: why did you not tell me this sooner? My liver is hanging on a tree back home — I did not bring it with me; let us go back, and I shall fetch it, and then accompany you.

And so they returned; the monkey climbed back up into the tree, leaping about joyfully. The turtle asked him: you were to fetch your liver and come to my home — why do you now leap about like this instead? The monkey answered: there is no greater fool under heaven than you. We formed a close friendship, each entrusting our own lives to the other — and yet you would still see me brought to ruin.

That turtle's own wife was Cañcā. That turtle was Devadatta. And that monkey king was myself (Source: Sūtra of the Turtle and the Monkey).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Cañcā's slander against the Buddha was already told in this compendium's fascicle 19, entry 9, in a different past-life account entirely (Nitya-muditā's jealousy toward the arhat Anuttara); this jātaka offers a second, independent tracing of the same present-life episode, and, true to this project's practice with doublets, we let the two accounts stand rather than reconcile them. Worth noting too is the pairing with Devadatta as the turtle: the same husband-and-wife betrayal-of-a-friend structure recurs across multiple past-life fables in this compendium (compare the fox jātaka of fascicle 21), suggesting the compilers regarded this particular relational pattern — a spouse's jealousy engineering the betrayal of an outside friendship — as a recurring moral type worth returning to.

14. Cañcā's Slander of the Buddha

Fascicle 23 closes with the Buddha's own account of the karmic seed behind Cañcā's slander — traced, in an entirely different register than the jātaka before it, to a single unresolved grievance between two ordinary merchants over a pearl bought fairly and then bought out from under her by a wealthier rival.

Chinese Source

佛遊舍衛祇國。波斯匿王。請佛及僧。宮中設飯。欲詣王宮。有比丘尼。名暴志。木盔繫腹似如懷妊。因索佛衣。汝為我夫從得有身。不給衣食此事云何。時諸大眾人天釋梵四王諸天鬼神。及國人民莫不驚惶。佛為一切三界之尊。其心清淨過於摩尼。智慧之明超於日月。獨步三世無能逮者。降伏諸邪。九十六種莫不歸伏。道德巍巍不可為喻。虛空無形不可污染。佛心過彼無有等侶。此比丘尼既佛弟子。云何懷惡欲謗如來。佛見眾心欲為決疑仰瞻上方。時天帝釋尋時來下。化作一鼠齧繫盔繩。盔即墮地。眾會覩之。瞋喜交集怪之所以。時國王瞋。此比丘尼棄家遠業為佛弟子。既不能歎譽如來無極功德。反還妬謗於是大王。即勅侍者。掘地為坑欲倒埋之。時佛解喻勿得爾也。是吾宿罪非獨彼殃。乃往過去久遠世時。時有賈客。賣好真珠枚數甚多。且圓明好。時有一女皆欲買之。向欲成市。有一男子。遷益倍價獨得珠去。女人不得心懷瞋恨。乃從請求復不肯與。心怒既盛。我前買珠。便來遷奪。又從請求復不肯與。汝毀辱我。我在所生當報汝怨。時買珠男子則我身是。其女身者則暴志是。此自往緣。非直今身也(出生經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha traveled in Śrāvastī, in the country of Kośala. King Prasenajit invited the Buddha and the Saṅgha to a meal within the palace. As the Buddha was about to go there, a nun named Cañcā, having tied a wooden bowl to her own belly to appear pregnant, came demanding one of the Buddha's own robes: it is you who have made me a mother, yet you provide neither clothing nor food — what account do you give of this?

At this, the whole assembly — humans and devas, Śakra, Brahmā, the Four Heavenly Kings, every deva and spirit, and the people of the kingdom alike — were struck with alarm. The Buddha is honored throughout the three realms; his own mind is purer than any maṇi jewel, his wisdom's own clarity surpasses the sun and moon; alone across the three times, none can equal him; he has subdued every heresy, and all ninety-six schools bow before him; his own virtue is majestic beyond any comparison, empty and formless as space itself, incapable of being defiled. The Buddha's own mind surpasses all things, without equal or peer. How, then, could this nun, herself his own disciple, harbor such malice, and seek to slander the Tathāgata in this way?

The Buddha, seeing the whole assembly's own doubt, and wishing to resolve it, looked upward. Śakra, king of the devas, descended at that very moment, and, transforming himself into a mouse, gnawed through the cord binding the bowl to her body; the bowl fell to the ground at once. The assembly, witnessing this, was overcome with a mixture of anger and relief, marveling at what had just occurred.

The king, in his own fury, said: this nun abandoned her own household and worldly pursuits to become the Buddha's own disciple — and yet, instead of praising the Tathāgata's own boundless merit, she has turned instead to jealous slander! — and ordered his attendants at once to dig a pit in the ground, meaning to bury her alive within it.

The Buddha then explained, and stayed his hand: do not do this — this is my own past transgression, not hers alone to bear. Long ago, in an age far in the past, there was a merchant who sold fine true pearls in great number, round, lustrous, and beautiful. A certain woman wished to buy them all, and the sale was nearly concluded — when a certain man came, raised the price still higher, and bought the pearls for himself instead. The woman, failing to obtain them, harbored deep resentment, and went to him, asking that he give them to her after all — but he would not. Her anger only grew: I had already agreed to buy these pearls, and you came and seized them out from under me — and now, when I ask for them again, still you will not give them to me. You have humiliated and shamed me — wherever I am reborn, I shall repay this grudge against you.

That man who bought the pearls was myself. That woman was Cañcā. This is the karmic root of the present matter, reaching back through past lives, not something confined to this single life alone (Source: Sūtra of Past Lives, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the compendium's second account of Cañcā's slander, following its fuller telling in fascicle 19, entry 9; there, the karmic origin was jealousy over unequal monastic patronage between two monks, while here it is traced instead to an entirely mundane commercial dispute over a pearl, with no religious dimension at all. Read together with the turtle-jātaka immediately preceding it in this same fascicle, the compendium offers three separate, mutually inconsistent past-life explanations for the same single act of slander — a plurality we preserve rather than resolve, in keeping with the compendium's own character as an anthology drawing on multiple independent source traditions rather than a single harmonized narrative. This closes fascicle 23, and the compendium's long run of śrāvaka biography spanning fascicles 13 through 23.
