Fascicle 34 · 經律異相卷第三十四(諸國王女部) · Complete

Princesses

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 34 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Princesses of Various Kingdoms" (諸國王女部) — the compendium's turn from crown princes to the king's daughters. Its nine entries include a princess born gold-skinned and blue-haired who will marry no one who does not match her, and whose marriage proposal is carried into the Jetavana and torn up unread; Mallikā's daughter, born with skin like camel-hide and kept behind a seven-fold gate, who is transformed the moment she prays; a dog fed rice by a visiting arhat and reborn a princess of Parthia; seven sisters who ask their father's leave to go and look at corpses, speak seven verses over them, and then ask Śakra for four things he has to admit are beyond his power to give; an old servant sent out for cosmetics who comes back teaching the Dharma so absorbingly that no one notices the palace burning; and a girl condemned to be sacrificed to avert her father's eleven dreams, who asks only for six days.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 34 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 34 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 34 opens the section on princesses, and it is a markedly different collection from the three fascicles of princes that precede it. There are no gifts of eyes or flesh here and no jātaka self-immolations. What recurs instead is argument: princesses arguing their fathers into letting them out of the palace, arguing a husband out of miserliness, arguing a court of brahmins out of a human sacrifice, and, in the fascicle's central entry, arguing with Śakra about the limits of what merit can buy.

The sources are unusually scattered — nine entries from nine different texts, several of them short parable collections that survive only in Chinese. We give each source note as the compendium gives it. Two of the titles are long descriptive Chinese sūtra names rather than translations of Indic works, and we have rendered these descriptively rather than attempt a Sanskrit title.

On names: Prasenajit, Mallikā, Ānanda, Śakra, Vārāṇasī, Śrāvastī, the Jetavana, the Buddha Kāśyapa, and the caṇḍāla are secure and given in their standard forms; so is Parthia (安息) for the Arsacid realm and the Yuèzhī for the Kushan. Where no confident correspondence exists — Jīlì, Tóujiāluó, Zhīxúnní, Dùshèng, Jiāluó, Jìnmèi, Shùpójiā — we keep the Chinese in pinyin. The seven sisters of entry 5 have transparently descriptive Chinese names and we translate them. Two names are discussed in the reading notes because the text itself is inconsistent about them: the princess of entry 8, and the princess of entry 9.

Entry 5 is largely verse; we set it off in italics with slashes for the line breaks of the Chinese, and the Chinese column keeps the verses running on within the paragraph, as the source has them.

The final entry carries a general judgment about women appended by its source, the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, in the course of an argument about desire. We translate it as it stands and comment on its fit with the rest of the fascicle in the reading note rather than soften or omit it.

1. The Golden-Colored Daughter of the King of Vārāṇasī Asks for the Buddha as a Husband

A princess born gold-skinned and blue-haired refuses to marry anyone who does not look as she does. A merchant nominates the Buddha; a letter of proposal is carried to the Jetavana; and the Buddha tears it up and writes back a single line about where suffering comes from.

Chinese Source

波羅奈國王夫人生一女。身黃金色。頭髮紺青。時年十六。父母見女長大。欲為求婿。女言。我不用婿。若欲為我求婿者。當令身黃金色頭髮紺青。如是者乃可爾耳。父母便為求索。了不能得。佛時在舍衛國。舍衛國有人。價作到波羅奈國。國王即請價人與相見問訊。以女示之言。我為是女求婿。天下寧有好人。價人答言。我國中有人。復勝是女者。王聞歡喜。令價人迎取佛。價人便作書與佛。書上說是女端正甚好無比。欲為佛取之。佛在祇洹中。為諸比丘數千人說經。持書人直前至佛所。佛預知書上所說。得便裂壞之。作書報與金色女言。之人苦皆從恩愛生。生當復老。老當復病。從病致死。從死致憂哭。天下苦者皆從恩愛生。女得書自思惟。即得五通達。一者眼能徹視。二者耳能徹聽。三者知他人心念。四者知所從生。五者能飛行。便與父母辭訣飛到佛前。佛即微笑。五色光明從口中出。阿難前問。佛言。阿難。汝見此女健乃如是。欲知之者。迦葉。佛時。是金色女為貧家作婦。其婿手足拘攣不能行步。時國王字基立。有一女。端正。身著金銀瓔珞。徑到迦葉佛所受經戒。時是貧人婦。身但被一㲲布。亦復隨往到佛所。外人不聽前。貧人婦自念言。我何故獨不得前。王女獨前。佛即知之。令人呼前。婦即作禮白言。是王女何故獨豪貴如此。我獨貧窮。佛語婦人。是王女。前世好喜讀經以衣施與沙門故。今世得豪貴尊嚴端正。汝前世不樂經法。慳貪不肯布施。見沙門不相承事。令汝今世貧窮如是。貧婦言。願佛哀我愚癡教我經法。迦葉佛便教之。於樹下讀經。坐自思惟。便取樹葉縫連作衣。以覆其身。欲持身上所著㲲布衣上佛。天王釋見有至心。便持天衣金錢飲食與之。婦得衣便以布施設供。壽終生天上。壽盡來生世間。為國王女。喜讀經布施故。身黃金色頭髮紺青(出金色女經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The consort of the king of Vārāṇasī bore a daughter whose body was the color of yellow gold and whose hair was dark blue. When she was sixteen her parents, seeing that she had grown, wished to find her a husband. The girl said: I have no use for a husband. If you wish to find me one, he must have a body the color of yellow gold and hair of dark blue — one such as that, and only then will it do. Her parents sought one for her and could not find him at all.

The Buddha was at that time in Śrāvastī, and a man of Śrāvastī came on business to the kingdom of Vārāṇasī. The king summoned the merchant, received him, and asked after him, and showed him his daughter, saying: I am seeking a husband for this girl. Is there anywhere under heaven a man fine enough? The merchant answered: in my country there is one who surpasses even this girl. The king was delighted, and sent the merchant to fetch the Buddha.

The merchant then wrote a letter to the Buddha, and in the letter described how the girl was beautiful beyond compare, and that he wished to take her for the Buddha. The Buddha was in the Jetavana teaching the sūtras to several thousand monks. The man carrying the letter went straight up to where the Buddha was; and the Buddha, knowing in advance what the letter said, took it and tore it up.

He wrote a reply to the golden-colored girl, saying: the sufferings of people all arise from love and affection. Whatever is born must grow old; growing old, it must fall sick; from sickness comes death; and from death, grief and weeping. All the sufferings under heaven arise from love and affection.

The girl received the letter, reflected on it, and at once attained the five supernormal knowledges: first, the eye that sees through; second, the ear that hears through; third, knowledge of the thoughts of others; fourth, knowledge of where one has come from; fifth, the power of flight. She took leave of her parents and flew to where the Buddha was.

The Buddha smiled, and a light of five colors came from his mouth. Ānanda came forward and asked about it. The Buddha said: Ānanda, do you see how strong this girl is? If you would know why, then hear this.

In the time of the Buddha Kāśyapa, this golden-colored girl was the wife of a poor household, and her husband’s hands and feet were crippled so that he could not walk. At that time there was a king named Jīlì who had a daughter, beautiful, her body hung with necklaces of gold and silver, who went to the Buddha Kāśyapa to receive the sūtras and the precepts. The poor man’s wife, wearing only a single coarse cotton wrap, followed her there to where the Buddha was; and the people outside would not let her come forward.

The poor man’s wife thought to herself: why is it that I alone may not come forward, and the king’s daughter alone may? The Buddha knew this, and had her called forward. She made obeisance and said: why is this king’s daughter alone so noble and rich, while I alone am poor?

The Buddha said to the woman: this king’s daughter, in a former life, delighted in reading the sūtras and gave clothing to śramaṇas, and so in this life she has obtained nobility, honor, dignity, and beauty. You, in a former life, took no pleasure in the teaching of the sūtras; you were miserly and would not give, and when you saw a śramaṇa you would not serve him. This is what has made you poor as you are now.

The poor woman said: I ask the Buddha to have pity on my foolishness and teach me the teaching of the sūtras. The Buddha Kāśyapa then taught her; and beneath a tree she read the sūtras, and sitting there she reflected, and then took the leaves of the tree and sewed them together into a garment to cover her body, wishing to offer to the Buddha the coarse cotton wrap she had been wearing.

Śakra, king of the gods, seeing that her heart was wholly sincere, brought her heavenly clothing, gold coins, and food. The woman, having received the clothing, gave it away and set out offerings, and when her life ended she was born in heaven. When that lifespan was exhausted she came back to be born in this world as a king’s daughter; and because she had delighted in reading the sūtras and in giving, her body is the color of yellow gold and her hair is dark blue (Source: Sūtra of the Golden-Colored Woman).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The opening entry is one of the odder pieces in the compendium, and it turns on a joke that the Chinese makes and the reader has to catch. The princess will marry no one who is not gold-skinned with dark blue hair; the merchant knows exactly one person in the world who answers that description; and so a marriage proposal is carried into the Jetavana in the middle of a teaching to several thousand monks. The Buddha destroys the letter before anyone reads it aloud and answers with the second noble truth in four clauses.

The story then explains the gold skin by a past life — and the explanation is markedly hard on its own heroine. She is a poor woman with a crippled husband, kept out of a Dharma assembly because of her single cotton wrap, and what the Buddha Kāśyapa tells her is not that the barring was unjust but that her poverty is her own doing. She responds by sewing tree leaves into a garment so that she can give away the one piece of cloth she owns. The gold body is the reward for that.

2. Prasenajit's Daughter Vajrā, Ugly of Form, Is Made Beautiful at Once by the Power of Mindfulness of the Buddha

Mallikā's daughter, born with skin like camel-hide, is married off to a poor nobleman on condition he keep her behind a seven-fold gate. His drinking companions get him drunk and open it — and by then she has already prayed the Buddha to her, and is unrecognizable.

Chinese Source

佛在舍衛國。爾時波斯匿王最大夫人。名曰末利。時生一女。字曰波闍羅(梁言金剛)女面醜惡肌體麁澁。猶如駝皮。髮如馬毛王觀此女無一喜心。便勅宮內。懃意守護勿令外人得見之也。女年轉大任當嫁處。王告吏臣。卿可推尋豪姓貧者便可將來。臣即如教。得一貧窮豪姓之士。將至王所。向彼人說。我有一女面狀醜惡。未有酬類。當相供給。想卿不逆。當納受之。時長者子長跪白言。當奉王勅。王即以女妻彼貧人。起宮宅門閣七重。王勅女夫。自捉戶排。若欲出時而自閉之。勿令人覩見女面狀。王給女婿使無乏短。又拜為大臣。其人有財。與諸豪族共為設會。月月更為會同之時。夫婦俱詣諸人來會。悉皆將婦。唯彼大臣恒常獨往。眾人疑怪。彼人婦者儻能端正。或能極醜。是以彼人故不將來。密共相語。勸酒令醉解取門排。開其門戶。時女心惱自責罪咎。我種何罪為夫所憎。恒見幽閉不覩眾人。復自念言。佛現在世。潤益眾生苦厄皆度。即便至心遙禮世尊。唯願垂愍。到於我前暫見教訓。其女誠篤佛知其意。即到其家。於其女前。地中踊出。其女見佛心生歡喜。惡相即滅。身體端嚴。猶如天女。奇姿蓋世。佛愍女故為說妙法。即盡諸惡得須陀洹道。時彼五人。開戶入內見婦端正。怪不將來。還閉門戶。持鑰繫本帶。其人醒悟會罷至家。見婦姿容人中難有。欣然問曰。是何人。女答夫言。我是汝婦。夫言。汝前極醜今者端正。其婦具以上事白。其夫即往白王。今者蒙佛神恩。已得端正。天女無異。王勅將來迎女入宮。王見歡喜。王及夫人。及女并女夫。共至佛所禮佛言。不審此女。宿植何福乃生豪富受醜陋形。佛告王曰。過去世時。國名波羅奈。有大長者。財富無量。舉家恒共供養一辟支佛。身體麁惡形狀醜陋。時彼長者有一小女。見彼辟支。惡心輕慢呵罵毀言。面貌醜陋身皮麁惡何其可憎。此辟支佛。受其供養欲入涅槃。為其檀越作十八變。即從空下還至其家。長者倍喜。女即悔過。唯願尊者當見原恕。時辟支佛聽其懺悔。佛告大王。爾時女者今王女是。毀此賢聖受醜陋形。後見神變。自改悔故還得端正。由供養佛故世世富貴緣得解脫(出賢愚經第二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha was in Śrāvastī. At that time King Prasenajit’s chief consort was named Mallikā, and she bore a daughter named Vajrā (which in the language of Liang means “diamond”). The girl’s face was ugly and her skin coarse and rough, like a camel’s hide, and her hair like a horse’s mane. The king looked upon this daughter without a single glad thought, and ordered that within the palace she be diligently guarded and no outsider allowed to see her.

When the girl grew older and it was time she was married, the king told his officials: go and search out a man of noble lineage who is poor, and bring him here. The officials did as instructed and found a poor man of noble family and brought him to the king; and the king said to him: I have a daughter whose face is ugly and who has no match. I shall provide for you. I expect you will not refuse, and will take her.

The elder’s son knelt and said: I shall obey the king’s command. The king then gave his daughter to this poor man as wife, and built them a palace with a gate of seven doors, and instructed his son-in-law: hold the door-bar yourself, and when you wish to go out, close it after you, and let no one see the girl’s face. The king provided for his son-in-law so that he lacked nothing, and appointed him a great minister besides.

The man being now wealthy, he held gatherings together with the noble clans, and each month in turn they held them; and when they gathered, husbands and wives came together, and everyone brought his wife — only this minister always came alone. The company wondered at it: perhaps this man’s wife is beautiful, or perhaps she is exceedingly ugly, and that is why he does not bring her. They spoke of it privately, and urged wine on him until he was drunk, and took the door-bar from him and opened his gate.

The girl at that time was distressed in her heart and reproached herself for her faults: what offense did I commit, that my husband should so detest me, and I be kept shut away always and never see anyone? And then she thought: the Buddha is present in the world, benefiting living beings; all who are in bitterness and distress he delivers. And with all her heart she bowed toward the World-Honored One from afar: I ask only that you take pity, and come before me and instruct me for a moment.

The girl’s sincerity being genuine, the Buddha knew her mind, and came to the house and rose up out of the ground before her. The girl, seeing the Buddha, was glad at heart, and her ugly marks were extinguished at once, and her body became grave and beautiful, like a heavenly maiden, of a rare grace surpassing the world. The Buddha, out of pity for her, taught her the subtle Dharma, and she exhausted all her evils and attained the fruit of stream-entry.

Then those five men opened the door and went in, and saw the wife beautiful, and wondered that he had not brought her; and they shut the door again and tied the key back on his belt. The man sobered, and when the gathering broke up he came home and saw his wife’s appearance to be such as is hard to find among people. Delighted, he asked: who is this? The girl answered her husband: I am your wife. Her husband said: before you were exceedingly ugly, and now you are beautiful. And his wife told him the whole of the above.

Her husband then went and told the king: today, by the Buddha’s divine grace, she has become beautiful, no different from a heavenly maiden. The king ordered her brought, and welcomed his daughter into the palace, and the king, seeing her, rejoiced. The king, his consort, the daughter, and the daughter’s husband went together to where the Buddha was, and made obeisance, and said: we do not know what merit this girl planted in a former life, that she should be born to wealth and rank and yet receive an ugly form.

The Buddha told the king: in a past age there was a country named Vārāṇasī, in which there was a great elder of measureless wealth, whose whole household constantly made offerings to a certain pratyekabuddha whose body was coarse and whose form was ugly. That elder had a young daughter who, seeing that pratyekabuddha, gave rise to an evil mind and to contempt, and scolded and reviled him: how ugly his face, how coarse his skin — how detestable he is.

This pratyekabuddha, having received their offerings, was about to enter nirvāṇa, and for the sake of his donors performed the eighteen transformations, then came down out of the air and returned to their house. The elder’s joy was doubled, and the girl at once repented of her fault: I ask only that the venerable one forgive me. And the pratyekabuddha accepted her repentance.

The Buddha told the great king: the girl of that time is the king’s daughter now. Because she reviled that sage she received an ugly form; and because she afterward saw his spiritual transformations and repented of herself, she has recovered her beauty. And because she made offerings to a buddha, she is wealthy and noble life after life, and by that condition attains liberation (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 2).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A well-known story from the Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, and worth noticing for how the transformation is arranged. Nothing is done to Vajrā by anyone; she prays, alone in a locked house, at the moment her husband is being made drunk so that his friends can open the door, and the Buddha comes up out of the ground in front of her. The beauty arrives before the door does. The husband's friends look in, see a beautiful woman, are puzzled that he ever hid her, and quietly lock the door again and put the key back on his belt.

The princess in this entry is named Vajrā, and so is the princess in entry 6, who is also a daughter of Prasenajit. They are not the same story and the compendium does not connect them, but the collision of names four entries apart is worth flagging.

3. Prasenajit's Widowed Daughter Finds a Second Husband Beneath a Tree

A very short piece with a long shadow: two brothers lie down under two trees, having had one good thought and one bad one between them, and the shade of one tree does not move with the sun.

Chinese Source

佛詣迦羅越家食。入不閉門。後隨來者一切皆與食令飽滿。弟發惡意。我不聽入。若得自在比丘來時我折其脚。兄弟二人便各別去。皆樹下臥。弟發惡意。有牛驚奔轢折其脚。兄發善意。日西樹蔭故處其上。波斯匿女婿亡。王甚怜愛。為其女呼婆羅門。相覓福人以為女婿。婆羅門出案行見一人臥在於樹下。日西樹蔭故覆。婆羅門言。此是福人。即以白王。王言。那知具其上事。呼來澡洗更著好衣。便嫁女與之(出十卷譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha went to a householder’s house to eat, and going in did not shut the door; and to all who came in after him he gave food until they were full. A younger brother gave rise to an evil thought: I would not have let them in. If I could have my way, when the monks come I shall break their legs.

The two brothers then went their separate ways, and both lay down beneath trees. The younger brother, who had given rise to the evil thought, had an ox bolt in fright and run him down, breaking his legs. The elder brother, who had given rise to a good thought — though the sun moved west, the shade of the tree stayed where it was and remained over him.

King Prasenajit’s son-in-law had died, and the king, who loved his daughter dearly, summoned brahmins for her and had them find a man of merit to be her husband. A brahmin went out to look about and saw a man lying beneath a tree with the sun in the west and the tree’s shade still covering him. The brahmin said: this is a man of merit — and reported it to the king. The king said: how do you know? — and he told him all of the above. He was summoned and bathed and dressed in fine clothes, and the king’s daughter was married to him (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 4).

4. The Daughter of the King of Parthia Came First from a Dog

A dog fed rice by a visiting arhat for years is reborn as a princess of Parthia, remembers it, and marries into the Kushan realm — where she sweeps the floor with her own arrival-clothes and argues her miserly husband into building a monastery.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a city in a foreign land named Tóujiāluó, in which there was a layman who every day invited a śramaṇa back to his house to eat. The śramaṇa was an arhat. While the śramaṇa sat at his meal there was a dog in the house, and whenever the śramaṇa ate he would always set aside a lump of rice for the dog. The dog, getting the rice to eat, gave rise to a good heart toward the śramaṇa. As the śramaṇa came day after day the dog grew used to waiting for him, and while the śramaṇa ate the dog would long to see him, and when the śramaṇa came he would again take a lump of rice and give it to the dog. The dog held this good heart toward the śramaṇa for years, and when its life ended it became the daughter of the king of Parthia.

From birth she remembered her past lives and knew that she had been a dog, and said: I have cast off a dog’s body and obtained the body of a king’s daughter. But in that country there were no Buddhist temples and no śramaṇas at all.

At that time the king of the Yuèzhī sent an envoy to that king; and, seeing that the envoy was wise and able, the king wished to give him his daughter as a wife. The envoy took the girl away, and when the girl saw śramaṇas she was greatly delighted at heart, remembering that she had been a dog and that a śramaṇa had given her rice, and that by a good heart toward a śramaṇa she had now obtained a human body: now I shall make great offerings to śramaṇas.

In the country of the Yuèzhī there were a great many śramaṇas, and the wife would feed three or five hundred of them every day, ladling out the food with her own hands and not letting servants do it; and when the meal was over she swept the floor with her own hands.

The women and servants of the household all gave rise to a good heart, saying: this woman is a king’s daughter, and having come here she constantly sweeps and makes offerings to śramaṇas; we too should put our hearts into it. So the servants hid the broom away, meaning to sweep the floor themselves. The mistress looked for the broom and could not find it anywhere, so she took from a hamper the clothing she had first arrived in and rolled it up and swept the floor with it.

Her husband, seeing his wife sweeping the floor with new clothing, said: even if you revere the Buddha’s teaching, why should you sweep the floor with new clothing? You should look for the broom. His wife said: I gave a good heart to a single śramaṇa, and two years later I received these clothes. What harm is there in sweeping with them? In a former life I had nothing at all to give away; I had only a good heart and faith in the Buddha’s teaching, and so obtained this blessing. I did not earn these clothes by any trade.

Her husband said to her: though you have faith in the Buddha’s teaching and make offerings to śramaṇas, no śramaṇa has ever given you a single coin or two. The clothes are all the fruit of my own sinew and strength.

His wife then told her husband of her former life, saying: in a former life I was born among dogs. My master often invited a śramaṇa, and the śramaṇa gave me lumps of rice; and because I held a good heart toward the śramaṇa, I left that dog’s body and was born a daughter of a royal house.

Her husband, hearing it, was delighted, and said to his wife: you gave a good heart to a single śramaṇa, and the blessing was as great as this. Her husband had been miserly before; hearing what his wife told him he at once gave greatly, holding nothing back, kept the fast and the precepts with vigor, and founded a Buddhist temple.

Her husband thought to himself: with only a good heart she obtained this merit. And his wife replied: the mind can make a person attain buddhahood, be born in heaven, become a pratyekabuddha, or become an arhat — all of it is the power of the mind. And if the mind thinks evil, it makes a person fall into hell (Source: Sūtra Explaining How a Dog, at the End of Its Life, Became a King’s Daughter and Knew Her Own Past Lives; also the Sūtra of the Reward of Merit).

5. The Seven Daughters of the King of Vārāṇasī Speak with Śakra

Seven princesses who will not marry ask their father for permission to go and look at a charnel ground, and, standing among the bodies, speak seven verses on what leaves the body when it dies. Śakra descends to grant them a wish; four of them ask for things he has to admit are beyond his power.

Ru-Yi Translation

At the place of practice in the kingdom of Vārāṇasī, the ground from its foundation upward was the color of yellow gold, laid course upon course like the scales of a fish and the plates of a turtle’s shell; and the buddhas of ancient times had all sat upon it. The king was named Zhīxúnní. He was a lay follower, clear in the way of the sūtras, and he built a monastery for the Buddha, adorned on the greatest scale.

The king had seven daughters, all of them beautiful, all keeping the five precepts and holding to their principle of not marrying. The first was named Gentle-Tempered; the second, Rare-Wonder; the third, Greed-Removed; the fourth, Pure-Keeping; the fifth, Mind-Stilled; the sixth, Quiet-Friend; the seventh, Saṅgha-Servant.

They always, on the eighth, fourteenth, fifteenth, twenty-third, twenty-ninth, and thirtieth days of the month, carefully received the eight precepts and kept the fast of the true Dharma, cultivating the practices they had faith in — the keeping of precepts, giving, good repute, and wisdom.

When their fast was over, the seven daughters asked to go outside the city to walk among the graves. The king told them: the graveyard is loathsome; there is nothing there but dead people, their bones scattered about, foxes and owls eating their flesh, and the place full of people weeping. What is there to look at? Within my palace there are gardens and bathing pools with lotuses of five colors, where herons, mandarin ducks, and birds of every kind gather and wheel; the ground is set with grasses of layered and shining aspect, fine trees give cool shade, and sweet fruits are there for the mouth. You may go and enjoy yourselves there.

The seven daughters answered: great king, of what use to us are sweet fruit and the many delights? We see that people of the world live no great span; their days go toward death, their forms are conjuring-tricks, and none can last long. We have been spared husbands from our childhood, and are not deluded by sweetness. We wish only to look upon the law of impermanence. Let the king put aside his desire for us. We ask the king’s compassionate consent. And the king consented.

The seven daughters went out of the city together and came to the graveyard, to a place of great stench, and heard the sound of weeping, and their hair stood on end. Then together they went straight forward to look at the dead. They saw some with heads severed and arms severed, hands and feet in different places; some lying stiff, covered with grass or wrapped in matting; some laid out on the ground under sentence, tied with green grass, and among them those under sentence not yet dead, with their families weeping and begging for their release; and others carrying the dead out from the city.

Looking left and right into the distance, the seven daughters saw that the dead were very many, and that the flying birds and running beasts contended over their flesh, some of them dragging bodies out of the earth, swollen and breeding worms, the stench of them hard to approach. At this the seven daughters circled the place once and said to one another: our bodies too, before long, will be just like these. And each spoke a verse.

The first daughter said:

“People of the world value their bodies, / adding fine clothes, jewels, and scent; / an elegant look, a refined bearing and step, / posture arranged for others to admire. / At death all is thrown away in the graveyard — / of what use then is all that adornment?”

The second daughter said:

“It is like a body dwelling in a house: / the person leaves and the house falls into ruin. / The spirit wanders and the body is discarded, / and none can command that form. / In folly and greed we say it can be kept — / how should we know it must perish hereafter?”

The third daughter said:

“Watch the spirit while it carries the body — / like a horse harnessed to a moving cart. / The cart breaks and the horse goes off; / by this you may know that it is impermanent.”

The fourth daughter said:

“Once we saw the city whole and fine, / its people happy and settled in peace. / What they sought was not yet had in full — / how has it suddenly become empty?”

The fifth daughter said:

“If you take a boat to cross the water, / on arriving you must leave the boat behind. / The form is not the spirit’s permanent house — / how could one dwell in it for long?”

The sixth daughter said:

“The dead lie propped on brick, / their bodies still fresh and whole, / stiff and unmoving — / and where has that spirit gone?”

The seventh daughter said:

“Like a sparrow inside a jar / with fine silk covering its mouth: / the silk is pierced and the sparrow flies off, / and the spirit likewise runs on its own way.”

Then Śakra, lord of the gods, heard them and came down to praise them: excellent, excellent. What do you wish? I shall grant it to you now. The seven daughters asked: are you Brahmā? Or an earth spirit? Could you be Śakra? He answered: it is so. I am Śakra, lord of the Trāyastriṃśa heaven.

The first daughter said: I wish for a tree with no root, no branch, and no leaf, and to be born in the midst of it — that would be a fine thing. The second daughter said: I wish that the ground I am born on be pure, without desire, without male and female, still and without anything to be dependent on. The third daughter said: I wish to be like the echo of a call in the mountains, neither going nor returning, naturally without form. The fourth daughter said: I wish to be like empty space, without beginning or end, with nothing born from it, penetrating through to the Way.

Śakra said: stop, stop. What you ladies wish for is exceedingly subtle. If you wished to be king among the sun and the moon, that could be had; but as for the void, the signless — that is not in my power to grant.

The seven daughters said: lord of the gods, your divine virtue is lofty and great; how is it you cannot bring this about? The Buddha said that one who eats merit, when the merit is exhausted, is not spared from fear. It is like an old ox that can no longer be used by anyone. What good is that to us?

Śakra, lord of the gods, said: you ladies cultivate the fast and the precepts, and I too serve the Buddha; let us be brothers and sisters in the Dharma. Delightful, these excellent wishes. And when he had spoken he was no longer seen (Source: Sūtra of the Seven Daughters, spoken by the Buddha).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry is the heart of the fascicle. Seven princesses refuse marriage, keep the six monthly fast days, and then ask their father to let them out of the palace garden — with its five-colored lotuses and mandarin ducks and sweet fruit, catalogued at length by a king who is plainly trying to buy them off — so that they can go and look at rotting corpses instead. The seven verses they speak are variations on one question: something has left the body, and where has it gone? The images are a house whose tenant has moved out, a cart whose horse has been unharnessed, a boat abandoned on the far bank, and a sparrow flying out of a jar through the torn silk over its mouth.

Śakra's arrival is the sharpest turn in the piece. He offers them anything they want; four of them answer with descriptions of the unconditioned — a tree with no root, branch, or leaf; a ground without desire and without male and female; an echo that neither goes nor returns; empty space with no beginning and no end — and Śakra has to concede that he can make them rulers of the sun and moon but cannot give them that. Their reply is the line that gives the story its edge: one who lives on merit, when the merit is spent, is not spared from fear, like an old ox no one can use any more. Śakra takes the correction without offense and asks to be counted their brother in the Dharma.

6. The King's Daughter Vajrā Is Burned by Fire

An old servant sent out for cosmetics comes back with the Dharma instead. She teaches it, on a seat improvised from the women's own clothing, so absorbingly that no one notices the palace catching fire.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

King Prasenajit had a widowed daughter named Vajrā. She came home in her finery, and her father the king, out of pity, established a separate palace for her and gave her five hundred entertainers to divert her.

There was an old servant woman named Dùshèng who used to go to the market for cosmetics, incense, and flowers. Suddenly she saw a countless crowd of men and women each carrying incense and flowers out of the city, and asked: where are you going? The crowd answered: we are going to pay our respects to the Buddha. The Buddha has appeared in the world, the honored one of the three realms; he delivers living beings, and all attain nirvāṇa.

Dùshèng, hearing this, was glad and pleased at heart, and thought to herself: to meet the Buddha in my old age is the merit of former lives. And she took part of the money for incense and bought fine incense and flowers, and went with the crowd to where the Buddha was, made obeisance, stood to one side, scattered flowers and burned incense, and listened to the Dharma with one mind.

She had bought one item of incense at the market and had then stayed to hear the Dharma; and the fragrance she obtained by the merit of that former practice was many times stronger than before. They complained of her lateness and pressed her about it, and Dùshèng said: there is in the world a holy teacher, the honored one of the three realms, who teaches the unsurpassed Dharma, and people receive it as they listen. That is why I was late.

Vajrā, hearing this, was delighted, and sighed to herself: what offense have we committed, that we alone are cut off and do not hear it? And she said to Dùshèng: try teaching it to me. Dùshèng received the charge, and first sent [messengers] out to receive the full form of the ceremony.

Before Dùshèng returned, Vajrā and her attendant women waited anxiously in the courtyard, as children wait for their mother. The Buddha told Dùshèng: go back and give the form for the teaching of the Dharma — first lay out a high seat. Dùshèng received the instruction, and when she declared the sage’s intention in full they were all greatly delighted, and each took off a garment and piled them up to make a high seat.

Dùshèng bathed, and, receiving the Buddha’s majestic power, taught the Dharma as was fitting; and Vajrā and the rest, more than five hundred of them, had their doubts resolved, broke through evil, and attained the fruit of stream-entry.

The teaching of the Dharma was so beautiful that they did not notice a fire had broken out; and all at once they were burned to death, and were reborn in heaven. The king provided coffins and shrouds, and when the burial was finished he went to where the Buddha was.

The Buddha asked where the king had come from. The king said: Vajrā, by misfortune, did not notice a fire break out; young and old were all burned up, and I have just come from the coffining and burial. I do not know what offense met with this harm by fire. I ask only that the World-Honored One make known what I have not heard.

The Buddha told the great king: in a past age there was a city named Vārāṇasī, in which an elder’s wife took five hundred serving women outside the city to a great sacrificial rite. The rule of that rite was strict, and no person of another clan might come near it; and whoever came, kin or stranger, was thrown into the fire.

At that time there was in the world a pratyekabuddha named Jiāluó, who dwelt in the mountains, came down at dawn to beg, and returned to the mountains at dusk. Jiāluó, begging, came upon the suburban sacrifice. The elder’s wife, seeing him, was suddenly seized with rage, and together they took hold of Jiāluó and threw him into the fire, and his whole body was scorched and burned.

He then manifested his spiritual power and rose flying into the open sky. The women were terrified and wept and repented of their fault, and knelt at length and raised their heads and declared: we women are foolish and dull and do not recognize the truly real; a mob of evil and deluded creatures, we have insulted a divine being. Reflecting that our faults are piled up like a mountain, we ask you to send down your honored virtue and dissolve this heavy calamity. At the sound of their voices he came down at once and entered final nirvāṇa, and the women raised a stūpa and made offerings to his relics.

The Buddha then spoke a verse for the great king:

“The foolish and dull do evil / and cannot free themselves; / calamity follows and burns them, / and the offense, once made, blazes up. / Where the foolish set their hopes, / they do not think it will come to suffering; / and only when about to fall into that hard place / do they know what was not good.”

The Buddha told the great king: the elder’s wife of that time is the king’s daughter Vajrā now; and the five hundred serving women are Dùshèng and the five hundred attendant women now (Source: Dharmapada Sūtra, “Folly and Darkness,” fascicle 2).

7. A King's Daughter Sees Bubbles on the Water and Conceives the Thought of Impermanence

A princess demands a garland of rain-bubbles and threatens suicide if she does not get one. An old craftsman agrees on one condition — that she pick the bubbles herself.

Chinese Source

昔有國王女。為王所愛未曾離目。時天降雨水上有泡。女見水泡意甚愛敬。女白王言。我欲得水上泡以為頭華鬘。王告女曰。今水上泡不可執持。云何得取以為華鬘。女言。設不得者我當自殺。王聞女語。告巧師曰。汝等奇巧靡事不通。速取水泡與我女作鬘。若不爾者當斬汝等。答曰。我等不堪取泡作鬘。有一老匠言。我能取泡。王甚歡喜。即告女曰。今有一人堪任作鬘。汝可自往躬自臨視。女隨王語在外瞻視。時彼老匠白王女言。我素不別水泡好醜。伏願王女躬自取泡我當作鬘。女尋取泡隨手破壞不能得之。如是終日竟不得泡。女自疲厭而捨之去。女白王言。水泡虛偽不可久停。願王與我作紫磨金鬘。終日竟夜無有枯萎。水上泡者誑惑人目。雖有形質生生便滅。盛炎野馬亦復如是。渴愛疲勞而喪其命。人身虛偽樂少苦多。磨滅之法不得久停。遷轉變易在世無幾(出水上泡經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a king’s daughter whom the king loved and never let out of his sight. Once when rain fell from the sky there were bubbles on the water, and the girl, seeing the bubbles on the water, took a great liking to them, and said to the king: I wish to have the bubbles on the water to make a flower-garland for my head.

The king told his daughter: the bubbles on the water cannot be taken hold of; how could they be got and made into a garland? The girl said: if I cannot have them I shall kill myself.

The king, hearing his daughter’s words, told the master craftsmen: your skill is extraordinary and there is nothing you cannot manage. Quickly take the bubbles on the water and make a garland for my daughter. If you do not, I shall have you beheaded. They answered: we are not equal to taking bubbles and making a garland.

But one old craftsman said: I can take the bubbles. The king was greatly delighted, and told his daughter: there is now a man capable of making the garland; you may go yourself and watch him do it. The girl, following the king’s word, went out to watch.

Then that old craftsman said to the king’s daughter: I have never been able to tell a good bubble from a bad one. I ask that the king’s daughter take the bubbles herself, and I shall make the garland. The girl reached for the bubbles, and they broke apart at the touch of her hand and she could not get them; and so it went all day and she got no bubble at all. The girl wearied of it herself and left off and went away.

The girl said to the king: bubbles on the water are false and cannot stay for long. I ask the king to make me a garland of purple-gold, which will not wither all day or all night. Bubbles on the water deceive the eye: though they have shape and substance, they perish as soon as they arise. So too the mirage in the blazing heat: thirsting after it wearies a person and costs him his life. The human body is false; its pleasure is little and its suffering much. It is a thing that wears away and cannot stay for long, shifting and altering, with no great while in the world (Source: Sūtra of the Bubble on the Water).

8. Mālinī Is Envied by the Brahmins

A princess sentenced to be sacrificed along with five hundred companions to avert her father's eleven bad dreams asks for six days, and spends each of them taking a different part of the court to hear the Buddha. Then the Buddha explains the dreams.

Chinese Source

過去有王。名曰禁寐。生一女兒。著自然金花鬘。號曰摩梨尼。王召國內同日生女五百人。以充給使。摩梨尼年大。供養五百婆羅門日日不替。時迦葉佛住一園中。常出遊行至佛住園。御者不入。女問。我無園不入何故避此。答曰。此園但有禿頭沙門。名曰迦葉。不宜見之。是故不入。女曰。可入。見佛金色心大歡喜。頭面作禮佛說妙法得受三歸五戒致敬而去。女自追念。婆羅門非良福田。不應受施。迴以供佛。時婆羅門聞摩梨尼作迦葉弟子。更以上饌而供養之。心生嫉妬。禁寐已夜。得十一種夢。旦集群臣自覺所夢。臣令問相婆羅門。婆羅門曰。此夢不祥。或當失國或能喪命。王曰。可免乎。相師答曰。須種種牛羊象馬。并王女摩梨尼。及五百眷屬。却後七日於四衢道中殺以祠天。此災可滅。王聞勅令。即辦呼摩梨尼以事語之。六日之內隨意所欲。答曰。甚不惜死。願第一日與城中男女共至迦葉佛所。皆受三歸五戒。王即聽之。願第二日與王眾臣共往佛所。願第三日與諸王子共往佛所。願第四日與諸王女共詣佛所。願第五日與王夫人婇女共至佛所。願第六日與王共至佛所。王悉聽許。及與眾人往至佛所。佛為說法得果。皆受三歸五戒。王向迦葉佛說十一夢。佛言。此夢所為未來非現在也。夢小樹生花者。當來有佛名釋迦文。時世之人年始二十頭生白髮已生兒子。夢犢子耕者十歲兒領家事。父母不得自在。夢三釜並煮飯邊釜各跳相入不墮中央者。富者更相惠施貧者不得。夢見駱駝兩頭食草者。群臣既食王祿復取民物。夢見馬母飲駒乳者。時母嫁女反從求食。夢見金鉢於虛空中行者。雨澤不時夢見野狐尿金鉢中者。人民從富婚不顧姓族。夢獼猴坐床上者。時國王用非法暴虐無道。夢見牛頭著栴檀幘與腐草同價者。釋種貪利為人說法。夢見水中央濁四邊清者。佛法中國先滅邊國反盛。於王之身無有不祥。王即於座上勅諸臣曰。祠天之牲今悉無畏。吾從今寧自失命。不故殺生況殺人乎(出彌沙律第四十卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the past there was a king named Jìnmèi, who had a daughter born wearing a garland of golden flowers that had come of itself, and she was named Mālinī. The king summoned five hundred girls born in the kingdom on the same day to serve her. When Mālinī had grown, she made offerings day after day, without fail, to five hundred brahmins.

At that time the Buddha Kāśyapa dwelt in a certain park. She was out on a regular excursion and came to the park where the Buddha dwelt, and her driver would not go in. The girl asked: there is no park I do not enter — why avoid this one? He answered: this park has nothing in it but a bald-headed śramaṇa named Kāśyapa; it is not fitting to see him, and that is why I do not go in. The girl said: we may go in.

Seeing the Buddha’s golden color she was greatly delighted at heart, bowed her head and face in obeisance; and the Buddha taught the subtle Dharma, and she received the three refuges and the five precepts and departed with reverence. The girl reflected to herself: the brahmins are not a good field of merit and ought not to receive offerings — and she turned her offerings instead to the Buddha.

When the brahmins heard that Mālinī had become a disciple of Kāśyapa, [the king] made offerings to them with still finer dishes; but jealousy arose in their hearts.

Jìnmèi, one night, had eleven dreams. In the morning he gathered his ministers and told them what he had dreamed, and the ministers had him ask the physiognomist brahmins. The brahmins said: these dreams are inauspicious. Either you will lose your kingdom, or you may lose your life. The king said: can it be avoided? The physiognomists answered: you must take oxen, sheep, elephants, and horses of every kind, together with the king’s daughter Mālinī and her five hundred attendants, and after seven days kill them at the crossroads of the four ways as a sacrifice to the gods; then this disaster may be dissolved.

The king heard this and gave the order, and had it prepared, and summoned Mālinī and told her the matter: within these six days, do as you wish. She answered: I do not begrudge my death at all. I ask that on the first day I may go with the men and women of the city to where the Buddha Kāśyapa is, and that they all receive the three refuges and the five precepts. The king consented at once.

I ask that on the second day I may go to the Buddha together with the king’s assembled ministers. I ask that on the third day I may go to the Buddha together with the princes. I ask that on the fourth day I may go to the Buddha together with the princesses. I ask that on the fifth day I may go to the Buddha together with the king’s consort and the palace women. I ask that on the sixth day I may go to the Buddha together with the king. And the king consented to all of it.

And so, going with all these people to where the Buddha was, the Buddha taught the Dharma for them and they attained the fruit, and all received the three refuges and the five precepts.

The king told the Buddha Kāśyapa his eleven dreams. The Buddha said: what these dreams concern is the future, not the present.

Dreaming of small trees putting out flowers: in a time to come there will be a buddha named Śākyamuni, and the people of that age, at twenty years of age, will already have white hair and already have borne children.

Dreaming of a calf plowing: children of ten will take charge of the household, and their parents will have no say in it.

Dreaming of three cauldrons cooking rice together, the food in the outer cauldrons leaping into one another but never falling into the middle one: the rich will give gifts to one another, and the poor will get nothing.

Dreaming of a camel with two heads eating grass: the ministers, having already eaten the king’s stipend, will take the people’s goods besides.

Dreaming of a mare drinking the milk of her own foal: mothers of that time will marry off their daughters and then turn round and beg food from them.

Dreaming of a golden bowl moving through the empty air: rain and moisture will not come in season.

Dreaming of a fox urinating into a golden bowl: the people will marry into wealth and take no account of clan or lineage.

Dreaming of a monkey sitting on a couch: the kings of that age will act against the Dharma, cruel and without principle.

Dreaming of an ox’s head wearing a sandalwood headcloth and priced the same as rotten grass: the Śākya clan will covet gain and teach the Dharma to people for it.

Dreaming of water turbid in the middle and clear at the four edges: within the Dharma of the Buddha, the central countries will decline first while the border countries flourish instead.

As for the king’s own person, there is nothing inauspicious in it.

The king then, from his seat, gave orders to his ministers: the sacrificial victims for the gods are now all safe from fear. From this day I would sooner lose my own life than deliberately kill a living creature — how much more than kill a human being (Source: Mahīśāsaka Vinaya, fascicle 40).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two textual points. The fascicle's own table of contents calls the princess 摩闍尼 while the entry itself calls her 摩梨尼; we follow the entry, and render the name Mālinī, which her golden flower-garland at birth supports. And the source is given as the Mahīśāsaka Vinaya, fascicle 40 — a vinaya, not a narrative collection, which is why the eleven dreams arrive with a prophetic exposition attached.

The dream-interpretations are a set piece of Buddhist prophecy about the decline of the Dharma, and their targets are worth reading closely: children of ten running households; the rich giving only to the rich while the poor receive nothing; ministers who have already drawn their salary and take the people's goods besides; mothers who marry off their daughters and then live off them; sandalwood priced the same as rotten grass, glossed as monks of the Śākya clan teaching the Dharma for profit; and the center of the Buddhist world declining while the periphery flourishes. That last one is being read, in a Chinese compendium of 516, by people at the periphery.

Note also what the story does with the sacrifice. Mālinī does not argue for her life, does not seek rescue, and says plainly that she does not begrudge her death; she asks only for six days and spends every one of them bringing a different section of the court to hear the Dharma. It is the king who calls it off, and the reason he gives is not that the dreams were harmless but that he will not deliberately kill a living creature, let alone a person.

9. The King's Daughter Dog-Head Moves the Fisherman Shùpójiā

A fisherman falls in love with a face in a tower window, his mother arranges the assignation, and the temple god intervenes to protect the king's honor by putting the man to sleep. The compendium's shortest entry, and its bluntest.

Chinese Source

國王有女。名曰狗頭。有捕魚師。名述婆伽。隨道而行。遙見王女在高樓上。窓中見面想像染著。心不暫捨彌歷日月不能飲食。母問其故。以情答母。我見王女心不能忘。母抑喻言。汝是小人王女尊貴。不可得也。兒言。我心願樂不能暫忘。若不如意不能活也。母為子故入王宮中。常送肥魚肥肉。以遺王女而不取價。王女怪問。汝欲求何。母白王女願却左右當以情告。我唯有一子。敬慕王女情結成病命不云遠。願垂愍念賜其生命。王女言曰。汝月十五日於某甲天祠中住天像後。母還語子。汝願已得。告之如上。沐浴新衣在天像後住。王女至時白其父王。我有不吉須至天祠以求吉福。王言大善。即嚴車五百乘出至天祠。既到。勅諸從者。齊門而止。獨入天祠。天神思惟。此不應爾。王為施主不可令此小人毀辱王女。即[示*厭]此人令睡不覺。王女既入。見其睡重推之不寤。即以瓔珞直十萬兩金遺之而去。去後此人得覺。見有瓔珞。又問眾人知王女來。情願不遂。憂恨懊惱婬火內發自燒而死。以是證故知。女人之心不擇貴賤。唯欲是從。復次昔有國王女。逐旃陀羅共為不淨。又有仙人女。隨逐師子(出大智論第十四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

A king had a daughter named Dog-Head. There was a fisherman named Shùpójiā who, travelling along the road, saw the king’s daughter from a distance high up in a tower, and, seeing her face in the window, formed a picture of her and was seized with attachment. His mind would not let go of it for a moment; days and months went by and he could not eat or drink.

His mother asked the reason, and he told her the truth: I have seen the king’s daughter, and my mind cannot forget her. His mother tried to check him: you are a lowly man, and a king’s daughter is of high rank; she cannot be had. Her son said: my heart’s wish and delight cannot be forgotten for a moment. If I do not have my way I cannot live.

His mother, for her son’s sake, went into the palace and regularly brought fat fish and fat meat as gifts for the king’s daughter, and would take no payment for them. The king’s daughter, wondering at it, asked her: what is it you want? The mother said to the king’s daughter: I ask that your attendants withdraw, and I shall tell you the truth. I have only one son. In reverent longing for the king’s daughter his feelings have knotted into an illness, and his life will not be long. I ask you to bend your pity on him and grant him his life.

The king’s daughter said: on the fifteenth day of the month, be in a certain temple of the gods, behind the image. The mother went back and told her son: your wish is granted — and told him as above. He bathed and put on new clothes and stood behind the image of the god.

When the time came, the king’s daughter said to her father the king: I have an inauspicious matter and must go to the temple of the gods to pray for good fortune. The king said: very good. So she set out with five hundred carriages in full array and went to the temple. On arriving she ordered her attendants to stop at the gate, and went into the temple alone.

The god considered: this ought not to be. The king is my donor, and this lowly man must not be allowed to disgrace the king’s daughter. And he pressed down on this man so that he slept and did not wake.

The king’s daughter went in and, seeing him deep in sleep, pushed at him and he did not rouse; so she left him a necklace worth a hundred thousand taels of gold and went away.

After she had gone this man woke and saw the necklace, and, asking the people, learned that the king’s daughter had come. His wish being unfulfilled, in grief, resentment, and anguish, the fire of lust broke out within him and burned him to death.

By this testimony one may know that a woman’s heart does not choose between the noble and the base, but follows only desire. Again, long ago there was a king’s daughter who pursued a caṇḍāla and did impure things with him; and there was also a sage’s daughter who followed after a lion (Source: Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, fascicle 14).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the only entry in the fascicle that treats a princess as an example rather than a person, and its provenance shows why: it is lifted from the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom's discussion of desire, not from a narrative sūtra, and the closing moral — that a woman's heart does not distinguish noble from base but follows only desire, with two further one-line examples appended — belongs to the treatise's argument rather than to the story. We have translated it as it stands, including the moral.

It sits oddly with the fascicle around it. The eight entries before it give princesses who refuse marriage on principle, argue theology with Śakra, outmaneuver a court of brahmins, and talk a miserly husband into founding a monastery. This one is a fisherman's story in which the princess appears twice, both times as an object — once as a face in a window, once as a person who keeps an appointment and leaves a necklace when she finds the man asleep. Note that the god's stated reason for intervening is not the woman's protection but the king's honor, the king being his donor.

One further oddity: the princess's name as written here means literally "Dog-Head," which fits neither the narrative nor a royal naming convention. It is almost certainly a shortened transliteration of an Indic name rather than a description, and we have left it as the text gives it rather than reconstruct what it stood for. This closes fascicle 34.
