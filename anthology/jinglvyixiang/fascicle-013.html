Arhat Śrāvaka Monks

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 13 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Arhat Śrāvaka Monks, Part 2" (聲聞無學僧部第二) — fifteen entries centered on named disciples of the Buddha: Mahākāśyapa's marriage to a fellow golden-skinned ascetic and their shared renunciation, his begging from a dying beggar woman, his own leadership of the First Council and expulsion of Ānanda until his defilements were exhausted, the taming of the three Kāśyapa brothers' fire-breathing dragon at Uruvilvā, Subhūti's birth and past life, and several episodes from Aniruddha's own past lives and present teaching.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 13 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 13 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 13 opens a new topical section — arhat śrāvaka monks, named disciples of the Buddha who reached the exhaustion of outflows in this very life — as distinct from the bodhisattvas of the preceding fascicles. It carries fifteen entries organized loosely around individual figures: Mahākāśyapa, Piṇḍola, Kauṇḍinya, the three Kāśyapa brothers of Uruvilvā, Subhūti, and Aniruddha. Two pairs of entries sharing a single figure and near-identical framing are grouped together here despite minor separation in the original sequence, while every entry's full text remains intact.

1. Kāśyapa's Golden Marriage

A young ascetic, hoping to make marriage impossible, tells his parents he will accept only a bride with skin the color of pure gold — and finds, to his own dismay, that such a woman actually exists, setting up a marriage neither partner wanted that becomes, through a single misunderstood act of compassion, the path by which both eventually go forth together.

Chinese Source

迦葉父者。曰尼俱律陀。摩竭國人也。出自婆羅門種。宿命福德生世大富。珍奇寶物國中第一。財比國王。千分少一。夫婦孤獨乏無兒息。近在舍側有大樹神。時彼夫婦為欲有兒。三牲祭祠累歲不遂。其人大忿便與期七日。若復無驗當剪罰汝。棄都道頭以火燒之。神聞驚怖。上告息意天王。天王即將神。告天帝釋。帝釋即以天眼觀欲界中。未有堪任為彼子者。乃告梵王。梵遍觀見。一梵天臨當壽終。便告之曰。汝可生閻浮提。為尼俱律陀作子。梵天對曰。婆羅門者多諸邪見。我若下生不能為其作子。梵王答曰。彼婆羅門宿時大德。欲界眾生無有堪任為作子者。汝若往生。吾當勅天帝釋令擁護汝。不使中道墮邪見也。梵天曰唯。帝釋即以告樹神。樹神還告長者。勿見瞋恨。却後七日必令有子。七日已滿婦便有娠。十月乃生。身黃金色而有光明。相師占曰。此兒宿福有大威德。志力清遠不貪世務。若後出家必登聖道。父母聞之復大愁。憂恐兒出家至年十五。欲為娶婦。迦葉聞之累啟父母。我志樂清淨不須婦也。父母不聽迦葉又言。若然不用凡女人。得紫金色女端正無比。乃當取之。欲令此事不可辦也。父母令其國中推覓之。諸婆羅門即為設策。鑄金作神女。顏貌端正光色微妙。如眾天像。從國至國高聲大唱。諸有女人得見金神。禮拜供養者後出嫁時。當得好婿。體黃金色顏貌姝妙。智慧無比。聚落國邑諸有女人。聞此唱者莫不虛心。皆出奉迎禮拜供養。唯有一女軀體金色。端正姝好獨處閑室不肯出迎。諸女諫曰。其有見金神者。皆得如願。汝何以獨不出迎。答曰。吾志閑靜不好餘願也。諸女復曰。雖無所願暫共一觀。當復何損。諸女相携遂共出看。金女光色乃映金神。婆羅門還報長者具。即遣媒人到其女家。宣長者意。其女父母先亦聞迦葉名。敬承往意遂相然可彼女聞之甚大愁憒。父母所逼事不獲已。遂適迦葉。二人相對志各凝結。雖為夫婦了無恩情。便共結誓。我與君等各處異床。要不相觸。爾時夫婦各處一床。其父母聞迦葉雖共同室。而復異床。其父尋復遣人持床去。於是夫婦唯共一床。其婦更與夫誓。我若眠時。君當經行。時其婦臥。一臂垂地。有大毒蛇欲來嚙之。迦葉見已有慈愍心。持衣裹手舉著床上。尋時驚覺便大瞋怒。語迦葉言。我先有要。如何相犯。迦葉報言。汝臂落地毒蛇欲嚙。是故相救非故觸也。指蛇示之。其婦乃悟。於是夫婦自相與議。我等何不出家修道。遂辭父母。俱共出家。入山行道。時有婆羅門。將五百弟子。亦住此山。見迦葉夫婦共相隨逐。於時迦葉便捨其婦。以五百兩金。貿緻納衣別處一林。其婦即依止婆羅門。求為弟子。婆羅門五百弟子。見此女人形色端正。日日行婬。女人不得自在。遂不能堪。便告其師。師便為之。戒約弟子。令節所欲。迦葉後值佛出世。聞法受化。即得羅漢。聞其本婦在梵志邊。便將詣佛。佛為說法。得阿羅漢。頭髮自落法服在身。成比丘尼。遊行教化。正值波斯匿王大會。諸比丘尼便得入王宮裏。教化諸夫人。皆令持一日齋。王暮還宮。命諸夫人。皆云持齋。無肯來者。王便大瞋。語使人言。誰教諸夫人齋。使人答言。某甲比丘尼。王便呼來。令九十日代諸夫人受淫欲。此皆是昔日之因緣。誓願所造。故雖得羅漢。不能相免也(出雜譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Kāśyapa's own father, Nyagrodha, was a man of Magadha, born into a brahmin family, blessed with such wealth through past merit that his treasures ranked first in the whole kingdom — a fortune falling short of the king's own by only a thousandth part. He and his wife, alone and childless, lived beside a great tree inhabited by a spirit; for years the couple offered animal sacrifices there seeking a child, without success, until, enraged, the husband gave the spirit seven more days, threatening to cut it down and burn it at the crossroads if it failed him again. Terrified, the tree spirit reported this to the heavenly king Praśāntamanas, who in turn told Śakra. Śakra, surveying the desire realm with his own heavenly eye, found no being there fit to become this couple's child, and so appealed to Brahmā, who, searching further, found a Brahmā-heaven deva about to die, and told him he should be reborn in Jambudvīpa as Nyagrodha's own son. The deva protested that brahmins hold many false views, and that he feared being unable to remain their true son if reborn among them — but Brahmā answered that this particular brahmin's own past virtue was great, and that among beings of the desire realm none but this deva was fit to be his son; should he go, Brahmā himself would have Śakra protect him from falling into false views along the way. The deva agreed, and Śakra relayed word to the tree spirit, who told the couple not to be angry — within seven days they would surely have a son.

The seven days passed, and the wife conceived; after ten months, she bore a son whose body shone golden. A physiognomist, examining him, declared that this child, blessed by past merit with great and majestic virtue, held so pure and remote a resolve, free of worldly ambition, that should he ever go forth, he would surely attain the sagely path — news that only deepened his parents' own worry, fearing he might indeed leave the household life. When he turned fifteen, they wished to arrange his marriage; hearing this, Kāśyapa repeatedly told them that his own resolve was set on purity, and that he needed no wife at all. When they would not relent, he told them that, if he must marry, he would accept only a woman of unmatched beauty with skin the color of pure gold — thinking this condition impossible to satisfy and thereby avoid the marriage entirely.

His parents had the whole kingdom searched, and the brahmins, devising a plan, cast a golden statue of surpassing, radiant beauty, like an image of the gods themselves, and proclaimed from country to country that any woman who came to venerate and make offerings to this golden goddess would, upon marrying, receive a husband of matching golden beauty and unmatched wisdom. Women everywhere, hearing this, flocked eagerly to bow and make offerings — save one woman, herself golden-skinned and of unmatched beauty, who remained alone in her own quiet chamber and would not go out. Her companions urged her, saying that everyone who saw the golden goddess had her wish fulfilled — why should she alone refuse to go? She answered that her own resolve was set on tranquility and wanted nothing else — but, pressed further that a single glance could cost her nothing, she finally went along with them to see it, and her own golden radiance outshone the goddess itself.

The brahmins reported this fully to Kāśyapa's father, who sent a matchmaker to her family, declaring his own intentions; her parents, having already heard of Kāśyapa's own reputation, respectfully agreed to the match. The woman herself, hearing this, was deeply distressed, but, pressed by her parents and unable to refuse, married Kāśyapa regardless. The two of them, facing each other, each held their own resolve firm — and though now husband and wife, felt no affection between them at all, and together vowed to sleep in separate beds, never to touch one another. When their parents learned that, though sharing a room, the couple slept apart, Kāśyapa's father had the second bed removed, forcing them to share a single bed — at which the wife made a further vow with her husband: that while she slept, he would remain awake, walking in meditation.

One night, as she slept with one arm hanging off the bed's edge, a great venomous snake approached to bite her — and Kāśyapa, moved to compassion, wrapped his own hand in cloth and lifted her arm back onto the bed. Startled awake, she grew furious, accusing him of breaking their own vow — but Kāśyapa explained that her arm had fallen and a venomous snake threatened to bite it, and that he had acted only to save her, not to touch her out of desire, pointing to the snake as proof. Understanding then, the two of them agreed together: why should they not simply go forth and cultivate the path instead? They took leave of their parents and together renounced the household life, entering the mountains to practice.

A brahmin teacher with five hundred disciples also dwelt in that same mountain, and, seeing Kāśyapa and his wife traveling together, Kāśyapa resolved to part from her, exchanging five hundred taels of gold for a patched robe and settling apart in a separate grove. His wife then took refuge with this same brahmin teacher, seeking to become his disciple — but his five hundred students, seeing this woman's own fine appearance, pursued her daily with lustful advances she could not escape, until, unable to bear it further, she reported this to the teacher, who then admonished his own students to restrain their desires.

Kāśyapa later encountered the Buddha's own appearance in the world, and, hearing the Dharma and receiving his teaching, attained arhatship at once. Learning that his former wife still dwelt among the brahmins, he brought her to the Buddha as well; the Buddha taught her the Dharma, and she too attained arhatship, her hair falling away of itself and a monastic robe appearing upon her body, becoming a nun who traveled and taught widely.

It happened that King Prasenajit was holding a great assembly, and the nuns, gaining entry into the palace itself, taught the king's own consorts to keep a single day's fast. When the king returned to his palace that evening and summoned his consorts, all of them declared they were keeping the fast and none would come to him. Furious, the king demanded of his attendants who had taught his consorts this fast, and, told it was a certain nun, summoned her and forced her, for ninety days, to take the consorts' own place in serving his desire. All of this was the fruit of a karmic bond and a vow made in a past life — for though she had already attained arhatship, she could not thereby escape it (Source: Miscellaneous Similes Sūtra, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium's own final line is the doctrinal point of the whole entry, and it is a hard one: arhatship, in this account, ends the arising of any new craving but does not retroactively cancel the ripening of a debt already set in motion by an earlier vow. The story is careful to locate that debt at a specific, earlier moment — the couple's own youthful pact to marry in form only — rather than anywhere in her conduct as a nun, and equally careful to record the king's own act as a grave wrong rather than a fitting consequence the text endorses. Read this way, the entry is less a story about the ending than a hard case built to test a claim the compendium takes seriously elsewhere: that karmic causes, once set, work themselves out on their own timeline, indifferent to how far a person has otherwise progressed on the path.

2. Kāśyapa Begs from a Poor Old Woman

Kāśyapa deliberately seeks out the poorest beggar in the city to give her the chance to make an offering — and, once Śakra himself tries to repay her kindness on her behalf by trickery, flies off in open annoyance at being deceived.

Chinese Source

迦葉捨豪富而從貧乞。入王舍城見一孤獨母。最甚貧困。街巷大糞聚上。傍鑿糞聚以為巖窟。羸劣疾病常臥其中。無有衣食。施一小籬以障五形。壽命將終。長者青衣。行棄米汁臭惡難言。母從乞之。即以破瓦盛著左右。迦葉哀之往乞多少。老母說偈。言其臭惡。迦葉猶以慈悲。忍而乞之。老母歡喜即以施。迦葉恐母不信豈能食之。即於母前飲畢盪鉢。示現神力。母大踊躍。一心遙視。迦葉告曰。母今何願。時母厭世苦。聞天上樂願生天上。數日壽終。即生第二忉利天宮。即念故恩求欲供養。釋提桓因聞是事已。即與天后持百味食盛小瓶中。下詣漏室。變其形狀。似于老人。織席貧窮。迦葉分衛見而往乞。夫妻告言。我今貧苦輒自割損。以施賢者。令吾得福。迦葉下鉢。乃開小瓶。香熏大城。迦葉即嫌。便入三昧。復身飛去。彈指歡喜(出摩訶迦葉度貧母經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Kāśyapa, giving up begging from the wealthy in favor of the poor, once entered Rājagṛha and came upon a lone, destitute woman, the poorest of the poor, who had dug a shelter into a great heap of refuse in the street and lay there, weak and sick, without clothing or food, save a small screen of woven reed shielding her own body. Near death, she received only foul, discarded rice-water that a rich household's servant threw out, catching it in a broken shard. Kāśyapa, moved to pity, went and begged from her whatever she had. The old woman, in verse, protested that it was too foul to offer — but Kāśyapa, in his own compassion, insisted and accepted it regardless. Delighted, she gave it to him; and, fearing she might doubt whether he could truly eat such filth, he drank it before her at once and rinsed his own bowl clean, displaying his supernormal power. Overjoyed, the woman watched him intently, and Kāśyapa asked what she now wished for. Weary of this world's suffering and longing for heaven's own joys, she wished to be reborn there — and, within days, her life ended, and she was reborn at once in the second heaven, Trāyastriṃśa.

Remembering this kindness, she wished to repay it with an offering, and Śakra, hearing of this, went with his own queen, filling a small vessel with a hundred delicacies, and descended to the poor woman's own former dwelling, transformed in appearance as an old, poverty-stricken couple weaving mats. Kāśyapa, begging there, came upon them, and they told him: "We are poor and suffering ourselves, yet gladly give up what little we have to a worthy man such as you, that we might gain merit." As Kāśyapa lowered his bowl, they opened the small vessel, and its fragrance filled the entire city. Kāśyapa, displeased at being deceived, entered samādhi and flew away, snapping his fingers in joy nonetheless (Source: Sūtra of Mahākāśyapa's Deliverance of the Poor Old Woman).

3. The First Council

Warned by the devas that the Dharma itself is about to collapse without the Buddha's own presence, Kāśyapa summons every arhat to compile the Three Baskets — barring Ānanda alone from the assembly until his own remaining defilements are exhausted, which happens only in the instant his head fails to reach the pillow.

Chinese Source

諸天禮迦葉足。說偈讚歎。大德知不。法船欲破。法城欲頹。法海欲竭。法幢欲倒。法燈欲滅。說法人欲去。行道人漸少。惡人轉盛。當以大慈建立佛法。迦葉心如大海。澄清不動。久而答曰。世間不久。無智盲冥。迦葉思惟。我今云何。使三僧祇劫難得佛法而得久住。唯當結集三藏。可得久住耳。未來世人可得受行。佛世世勤苦。慈悲眾生學得是法。為人顯說。我等亦應承用開化。昇須彌山頂。鳴鍾揵搥。而說偈言。
諸佛弟子　若念於佛　當報佛恩
莫入涅槃
是揵搥音。及迦葉語。聲遍至三千大千世界。皆悉聞知。諸有弟子得神力者。集迦葉所。迦葉以天語告言。佛般涅槃諸知法弟子。皆隨滅度。佛法欲滅。未來眾生甚可怜愍。失智慧眼。愚癡盲冥。我等應當承用佛教。待結集法藏竟。隨意滅度。迦葉選得千人。唯除阿難皆得羅漢。頻婆娑羅王。得道常勅宮中飯食千人。阿闍世王不斷是法。迦葉思惟。若常乞食。當有外道強來難問。廢闕法事。到王舍城以事告王。王當給食。日日送來。夏三月安居。迦葉觀誰有煩惱。唯有阿難。大迦葉即數之云。犯六突吉羅罪。盡應僧中懺悔。阿難即隨教。長跪合手。偏袒右肩脫革屣懺悔。迦葉於僧中起。牽手出之。語言。汝宜盡漏。若殘結未亡。汝勿來也。便自閉門。與諸羅漢共議。誰能結集毘尼法藏者。長老阿泥盧豆言。舍利弗是第二佛。憍梵波提柔軟和雅。閑居燕寂皆能知毘尼藏。今在天上尸利沙樹園。使下座比丘往傳。迦葉意云。漏盡羅漢皆會閻浮提。僧有大法事。今可疾來。憍梵波提心疑。問曰。為鬪諍破僧耶。佛日滅度耶。答曰。大師滅度。我和上舍利弗今在何所。答曰。先入涅槃。憍梵波提問曰。目連阿難羅睺今何所作。答曰。目連已滅度。阿難由有憂結。愁苦啼哭不能自喻。羅睺已得羅漢無復憂苦。憍梵波提言。我和上大師皆已滅度。我今不能復下。即入涅槃。阿難思惟諸法求盡殘漏。坐禪經行定力少不時得道。後夜疲極欲息臥。頭未至枕廓然得悟。作大力羅漢。其夜到僧堂敲門。喚大迦葉。大迦葉言。汝何以來。答言。我今夜盡漏。迦葉言。我不與開門。汝從門鑰孔中來。阿難即從鑰孔中入懺悔。大迦葉莫復見責。迦葉手摩其頭。我故使汝得道耳。汝無嫌恨。我亦如是。大迦葉語阿難言。從轉法輪經。至大般涅槃。集作四阿含。增一中長相應。是名修妬路法藏。諸羅漢更問。誰能明了集毘尼法藏皆言。長老優波離。於五百羅漢中。持律第一。我等今請。優波離受教。坐師子座言。佛在何處。說毘尼結戒。時須提陀迦蘭陀長者子。初犯淫欲法。始結大罪。諸羅漢思惟。誰能明了。學阿毘曇藏。念。長老阿難於五百羅漢中。解修多羅義第一。我等今請。阿難受請。佛在舍婆提城。說五怖五罪五除五滅。以是因緣。此生身心受無量喜。復墮惡道中。如是等名阿毘曇藏(出大智論第二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The devas bowed at Kāśyapa's own feet and spoke a verse in praise: "Great one, do you know? The Dharma-boat is about to break apart; the Dharma-city is about to crumble; the Dharma-ocean is about to run dry; the Dharma-banner is about to fall; the Dharma-lamp is about to go out; those who teach the Dharma are about to depart, and those who practice it grow ever fewer, while evil people only multiply — with great compassion, you must now establish the Buddha's own teaching firmly." Kāśyapa's own mind, calm and unmoved as the great ocean, answered only after a long while: "This world will not last much longer, sunk in ignorant darkness." Reflecting that the Buddha's own teaching, so hard to attain across three incalculable kalpas, must somehow be made to endure, Kāśyapa resolved that only by compiling the Three Baskets could it be preserved for future generations to receive and practice — for the Buddha himself, across lifetime after lifetime, had labored with compassion for beings to attain this very teaching and proclaim it to others, and they themselves ought now to carry it forward in turn. Ascending to the summit of Mount Sumeru, he struck the bell and gong, and spoke this verse:

"All you disciples of the buddhas, if you remember the Buddha, repay his own kindness now — do not yet enter nirvāṇa."

The sound of that gong, together with Kāśyapa's own words, reached throughout the whole trichiliocosm, heard by all; every disciple possessing supernormal power gathered at once to Kāśyapa's side. Kāśyapa told them, through his own heavenly voice: the Buddha has entered his own final nirvāṇa, and every disciple who truly knew the Dharma has likewise passed away or will soon do so; the Buddha's own teaching is about to perish, and future beings, deeply to be pitied, will lose the very eye of wisdom, sunk in ignorance and darkness — we ourselves must therefore uphold the Buddha's own teaching, and only once the compilation of the Dharma-treasury is complete may each of us enter final nirvāṇa as we wish.

Kāśyapa selected a thousand participants, every one already an arhat save Ānanda alone. King Bimbisāra, having attained the path himself, had always ordered his own palace to feed a thousand monks daily, and King Ajātaśatru continued this same practice unbroken. Reflecting that if the assembly went out begging as usual, heretics might come to challenge and disrupt their work, Kāśyapa reported the matter to the king at Rājagṛha, who agreed to provide their food, delivered daily, throughout the three months of the rains retreat.

Kāśyapa then examined who among them still harbored any affliction, and found only Ānanda. Enumerating six offenses of minor wrongdoing against him, Kāśyapa required him to confess these before the assembled sangha. Ānanda obeyed, kneeling with joined palms, baring his right shoulder, removing his sandals, and confessing — whereupon Kāśyapa rose within the assembly, took him by the hand, and led him out, telling him: "You must now exhaust your own remaining defilements entirely — do not return to us until you have done so," and shut the door behind him.

Kāśyapa then consulted with the assembled arhats over who might compile the Vinaya-treasury. The elder Aniruddha suggested Śāriputra, "a second buddha" — though he had already entered nirvāṇa — or the gentle and refined Gavāmpati, who, dwelling in quiet retreat, thoroughly knew the Vinaya-treasury, and was presently in the heavenly grove of the Śirīṣa tree; a junior monk was sent to summon him. Kāśyapa's own message explained that every arhat with exhausted defilements had now gathered in Jambudvīpa for a great matter of the sangha, and that he should come quickly. Gavāmpati, suspicious, asked whether some dispute had split the sangha, or whether the Buddha himself, the sun of the Dharma, had set — and, told that the great teacher had indeed entered nirvāṇa, asked where his own preceptor Śāriputra now was, and was told he had already entered nirvāṇa as well. Asking further after Maudgalyāyana, Ānanda, and Rāhula, he was told Maudgalyāyana had likewise passed away, that Ānanda, still bound by grief, wept without consolation, and that Rāhula, having attained arhatship, was now free of such sorrow. Gavāmpati said: "My own preceptor and great teacher have both already entered nirvāṇa — I too shall not now descend," and entered nirvāṇa himself on the spot.

Ānanda, meanwhile, reflecting on all dharmas in search of his own remaining defilements' exhaustion, sat in meditation and walked in contemplation, yet his own concentration proved too weak to attain the path in time. Exhausted late into the night, he wished only to rest and lie down — and, before his head even touched the pillow, was suddenly, completely awakened, becoming a great and powerful arhat on the spot. That same night he went to the assembly hall and knocked, calling out to Kāśyapa. Kāśyapa asked why he had come, and, told that his own defilements were now exhausted, said: "I will not open the door for you — come in through the keyhole instead." Ānanda entered through the very keyhole and confessed once more. Kāśyapa, no longer holding any reproach, stroked his head, saying: "I did this only to bring you to the path — bear me no resentment for it; I too was treated so in my own time."

Kāśyapa then told Ānanda: "Beginning from the Sūtra of the Turning of the Dharma-Wheel through to the Great Nirvāṇa Sūtra, compile these into the four Āgamas — the Ekottarika, the Madhyama, and the Saṃyukta among them — and this shall be called the Sūtra-Piṭaka." The arhats then asked who could clearly compile the Vinaya-Piṭaka, and all agreed: the elder Upāli, foremost in upholding the Vinaya among the five hundred arhats — and requested him accordingly. Upāli, accepting, took the lion's seat and asked: where was the Buddha, and what precept did he establish, on the occasion when the elder's son Sudinna of Kalandaka first committed the offense of sexual misconduct, thereby establishing the first grave transgression? The arhats then further asked who could clearly compile the Abhidharma-Piṭaka, and, recalling that Ānanda, among the five hundred arhats, was foremost in understanding the meaning of the sūtras, requested him in turn. Ānanda, accepting, recounted: the Buddha, in the city of Śrāvastī, taught the five fears, the five offenses, the five eliminations, and the five cessations — through which, in this very life, one experiences boundless joy in body and mind, yet may still fall into an evil rebirth if these are not upheld — and such teachings together came to be called the Abhidharma-Piṭaka (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 2).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Kāśyapa's own treatment of Ānanda is deliberately harsh by the standard of ordinary courtesy — barred from the compilation, made to confess minor faults before the assembly, then locked out entirely — and the story does not soften this into an unfortunate necessity; Kāśyapa says outright, afterward, that he did it "to bring you to the path," a stated method rather than an apology. Placed side by side without comment, they suggest a working assumption behind much of this compendium's own causal reasoning — that gratitude and consequence can be created either by a direct, personal act of rescue or by the much more diffuse fact of having once been sustained by someone else's body, and that the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng sees no need to decide which kind of debt is the more binding.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Kāśyapa's own repeated verdict — "still not equal to my own attainment" — survives three consecutive, escalating demonstrations of power he cannot match: taming the dragon, illuminating an entire chamber with a body of fire, and simply arriving somewhere before him without being seen to travel. What finally moves him is not a fourth, still greater display, but the arrival of the Four Heavenly Kings and Śakra himself as witnesses — suggesting that Kāśyapa's own resistance was never really about the scale of what he saw, but about maintaining his own status before his five hundred disciples, a position that becomes impossible to hold only once devas themselves are seen paying homage to someone else.

The compendium tells Subhūti's own origin twice — once tracing the single past act of giving that earned it, once dwelling on the birth itself — each version built around the same childless elder, the same voice from the sky, and the same inexplicably inexhaustible bowl of food.

Long ago, in Śrāvastī, there lived a great elder named Kuru, of boundless wealth yet without any son, who prayed to every spirit without success. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two versions divide the same underlying claim in two directions rather than repeating it outright: the first entry explains why Subhūti was born so blessed, tracing it to a single, specific act of giving in a former life; the second describes what that blessing actually looked like once he arrived, dwelling on the sky's own prophecy, the miraculous vessel, and his skeptical uncles' own change of heart. In that age, grain grew scarce and people starved, many dying, and alms were hard to come by. A pratyekabuddha named Pilizhà also dwelt there at that time, going out early each morning to beg. One morning, as I too went out early to take up my own load, I saw this pratyekabuddha, and, returning later with my load, saw him again — and reflected: I saw him go out early this same morning, and now, seeing him again, he surely has not yet eaten.

He then followed me back to my own home, and I resolved to invite him in, taking my own portion of food to offer him, saying: "Sage, this is my own share — please accept it out of compassion for me." The pratyekabuddha took only half into his own bowl, telling me to eat the other half myself and be satisfied — but I answered: "Sage, I have my own household and may eat at any time; you, sage, out of compassion, should accept this offering in full" — and the pratyekabuddha, moved by compassion, accepted it entirely.

Through the merit of this single act of giving, I was reborn in heaven seven times, becoming a heavenly king each time, and reborn among humans seven times more, becoming a human king each time; and now, born into the Śākya clan itself, possessed of boundless wealth, I have renounced this life to study the path and attained realization (Source: Madhyama Āgama, fascicle 12).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two stories locate merit in opposite places along the scale of sacrifice — one in a bandit's own restraint, giving up nothing he had actually planned to keep for himself, and the other in a poor laborer's own insistence on giving away food he genuinely needed. Read side by side, they suggest the compendium's own view that a single lamp trimmed almost by accident and a starving man's entire meal, given away on purpose, can both function as seeds of the same eventual harvest — foremost sight among all the Buddha's disciples — provided the moment itself was genuine, whatever its own scale.

15. Piṇḍola and the Elder's Sister

The fascicle closes with four arhats setting out to convert the one household in Rājagṛha still resistant to the Three Jewels — Aniruddha and Kāśyapa slipping past seven locked gates unseen, and Piṇḍola escalating through fire, flight, and a flying boulder before the elder's own sister finally relents.

Chinese Source

阿那律。大迦葉。目連。賓頭盧共議。今王舍城。有不信樂佛法僧者。我等當共令其信樂。作是議已。遍觀遠近。唯見跋提長者及其姊不信三寶。上三聲聞言。能化跋提。時彼長者作七重門。有三部伎。若欲食時七門皆閉。一食作一部伎。阿那律於其食時在其前乞。長者問言。從何處入。答從門入。即問守門。門閉如故。不見人入。長者便以一片麻餅著其鉢中。得已即去。於後食時。迦葉復乞亦復如是。得一片魚著其鉢中。其姊問言。意謂比丘不能得而來乞耶答言如是。姊言。前來比丘名阿那律。釋種之子。捨三時殿五欲之樂。出家學道。後來比丘是畢波羅延。摩納大姓之子。捨九百九十田宅犁牛。出家學道。愍念君故來乞食耳。長者聞已內懷敬伏。於是目連。飛空說法示教利喜。即於坐上遠塵離垢得法眼淨見法得果。即受歸戒。上三聲聞語賓頭盧。我等今者。已化跋提令其信法。汝今宜行次化其姊。時賓頭盧晨朝持鉢往到其舍。時長者姊手自作餅。忽見來乞便。語之言。不與汝。一心視鉢欲以何為。賓頭盧便身中烟出。復語言。舉身煙出亦不與汝。賓頭盧便舉身火然。復語言。舉身火然亦不與汝。賓頭盧便飛騰虛空。復語言。飛騰虛空亦不與汝。賓頭盧便倒懸空中。復語言。倒懸空中亦不與汝。賓頭盧作是念。世尊不聽我等強從人乞。便自出去。王舍城不遠有大石。賓頭盧坐其上。合石飛入王舍城。城中人見皆大怖懼。恐石落地莫不馳走。至長者姊上便住不去。彼見是已即大恐怖。白言。願施我命反石於先。我當與食。賓頭盧便持石還著故處至其前住。長者姊作是念。我不能以大䴵施之。當更作小者與之。便作小丸輒反成大。如是三反轉大於前。乃作念言。我欲作小皆反成大。我今便可趣與一餅。即以一餅而授與之。諸餅相連并至餅器。以手捉器手亦著之。便語賓頭盧言。汝若須餅盡以相與。全出與不惜。何須我為。而令我手著。答言。我不須餅。亦不須器。亦不須汝。我等四人共議。度汝及第二人。已化汝弟。我應度汝。所以爾耳。問言。今欲令我何所施作。答言。姊妹可戴此餅。隨我施佛及千二百五十比丘。皆悉飽滿猶故不盡。持往白佛。我此少餅。供佛及僧。皆悉飽滿。猶故不盡。今當持此著於何處。佛言。可著無生草地。若無蟲水中。彼女人便持著無蟲水中。水沸作聲如以熱鐵投于小水。便生恐怖。還至佛所。佛為說法得法眼淨。即受歸戒如弟子無異。諸長老等以是白佛。佛即訶責諸比丘。從今不聽復現神足(出彌沙塞律第三十卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Aniruddha, Mahākāśyapa, Maudgalyāyana, and Piṇḍola conferred together: among the people of Rājagṛha who did not yet believe in or delight in the Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha, we ourselves ought to bring them to faith. Having agreed on this, they surveyed the area near and far, and found only the elder Bhadrika and his own sister who did not yet believe in the Three Jewels. The first three śrāvakas said: we can convert Bhadrika ourselves.

This elder had built a house with seven successive gates, and kept three troupes of musicians; whenever it was mealtime, all seven gates would be shut, and one troupe would perform. Aniruddha, at mealtime, appeared begging directly in front of him. The elder asked how he had entered, and, told through the gate, questioned the gatekeepers, who insisted the gates remained shut throughout and no one had passed. The elder then placed a single piece of hemp-cake into Aniruddha's bowl, who took it and left. At the next meal, Kāśyapa came begging in exactly the same way, and was given a single piece of fish, which he likewise took.

The elder's sister then asked him: did you think these monks incapable of finding food elsewhere, that they came begging here specifically? He answered yes. She told him: the first monk who came was named Aniruddha, a son of the Śākya clan, who gave up the pleasures of his own three seasonal palaces and five sensory delights to go forth and study the path; the later monk was Pippalāyana, of the great Māṇava clan, who gave up nine hundred ninety fields, houses, and plow-oxen to go forth and study the path; both, out of compassion for you, came specifically to beg from you. Hearing this, the elder was inwardly moved to reverence — at which Maudgalyāyana, flying through the air, taught the Dharma, instructing and delighting him, and, seated there, his every defilement dissolved and he attained the pure Dharma-eye, seeing the Dharma and its fruit, and took the refuges and precepts at once.

The first three śrāvakas then told Piṇḍola: we have now converted Bhadrika and brought him to faith in the Dharma — it is now your turn to convert his sister. Piṇḍola, early one morning, took his bowl and went to her house; the elder's own sister was at that moment making cakes herself, and, seeing him suddenly come begging, said: "I will not give you any," fixing her eyes on his bowl as though wondering what he intended. Piṇḍola then made smoke rise from his own body, and she said: "Even with smoke rising from your whole body, I still will not give you any." Piṇḍola then made his whole body burst into flame, and she said: "Even with your whole body ablaze, I still will not give you any." Piṇḍola then rose and floated in the air, and she said: "Even floating in the air, I still will not give you any." Piṇḍola then hung upside-down in midair, and she said: "Even hanging upside-down in the air, I still will not give you any."

Piṇḍola reflected: the World-Honored One does not permit us to force alms from anyone against their will — and so departed of his own accord. Not far from Rājagṛha stood a great boulder; Piṇḍola sat upon it, and the whole boulder flew, with him seated on it, into the city of Rājagṛha. The people of the city, seeing this, were terrified, fearing the stone would fall and crush them, and fled in every direction — until it came to rest directly above the elder's sister, and stopped there. She, seeing this, was overcome with terror, and cried out: "Please spare my life, and set the stone back where it was — I will give you food." Piṇḍola then carried the stone back to its original place and came to stand before her once more.

The elder's sister then reflected that she could hardly offer him the large cake she had been making, and resolved to make a smaller one instead — but however small she tried to make it, it kept turning out large regardless; she tried three times over, each cake turning out larger than the last. Reflecting that whatever she tried to make small turned out large anyway, she resolved simply to give him the one cake as it was — but the moment she tried to hand it to him, every cake in the container stuck together, and, reaching for the container itself, found her own hand stuck fast to it as well. She said to Piṇḍola: "If you want cakes, take them all — I freely give every one, and do not begrudge them; why then do you make my hand stick like this?" He answered: "I do not want your cakes, nor your container, nor even you yourself — the four of us conferred together that we would deliver you and one other person; having already converted your own younger brother, it now falls to me to deliver you, and this is simply why I have done as I have."

She asked what he now wished her to do. He answered: "Sister, carry this container of cakes, and follow me to offer it to the Buddha and his twelve hundred fifty monks." She did so, and, remarkably, the offering satisfied the Buddha and the entire assembly completely, with cakes still remaining afterward. She asked the Buddha what she should do with what was left, and the Buddha told her to set it upon ground where nothing grows, or in water free of living creatures. She placed it in such water, which at once boiled and hissed as though red-hot iron had been thrown into a shallow pool — and, terrified, she returned to the Buddha, who taught her the Dharma; hearing it, she attained the pure Dharma-eye and took the refuges and precepts, becoming a disciple no different from any other.

The elders reported all of this to the Buddha, who then rebuked the monks, declaring that from this day forward, no monk was to display such supernormal feats again (Source: Mahīśāsaka Vinaya, fascicle 30).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Aniruddha and Kāśyapa's approach to Bhadrika succeeds through modesty — passing unseen through locked gates, accepting whatever meager scrap is offered, and letting his own sister's account of their sacrifices do the actual persuading. Piṇḍola's escalating campaign against the sister does the opposite, piling spectacle upon spectacle, and notably fails on every one of its own terms: smoke, fire, flight, and a flying boulder each provoke only refusal or terror, never faith, until he stops trying to overwhelm her and simply explains, plainly, why he came at all. The Buddha's own closing rebuke, banning such displays outright, reads as this fascicle's final verdict on a method it has tested repeatedly across many entries and found wanting compared to plain explanation, sincere need, and the willingness to be refused.
