Ordinary Men, Part 1

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 44 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Ordinary Men, Part 1" (男庶人部上) — thirty-eight entries, most of them short, and the compendium at its most miscellaneous. Among them: an illusionist who conjures a lecture hall to test whether the Buddha's omniscience is real and then cannot make it vanish; a man who wept in front of a turtle for sale, and what the turtle carried afterward; a painter prosecuted by his own wife for giving twelve years' wages to a monastery; a wooden woman who serves wine and a painted man hanged on a wall; the long duel between a thief and a king, in which five carefully-laid traps are defeated one by one; the man in the pit, with an elephant above, spears below, two rats gnawing the root he hangs from, and five drops of sweet dew; two bent old people squatting over a rubbish fire, and the Buddha's arithmetic of the three portions of a life they let pass; a destitute man saved through a winter by a sandalwood tree-spirit, who names the tree for a reward and is killed by its branch; and an old man chopping stumps with the finest treasure in the world.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 44 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 44 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 44 opens the section on ordinary men — 庶人, the common people, as distinct from the kings, princes, elders, brahmins, and merchants of the fascicles before it. Thirty-eight entries in a fascicle of ordinary length means most of them are very short, several under a hundred characters, and a large proportion come from the several distinct collections of parables that survive only in Chinese: the Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra, the Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, the Old Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra, the Sūtra of the Hundred Parables, and a compilation the compendium calls simply Essential Matters from the Sūtras.

The register varies more here than in any fascicle we have translated. Entry 12 is a full-length adventure narrative; entries 14, 15, and 38 are jokes from the Hundred Parables; entry 31 is a passage of Āgama with verse; entry 33 is one of the most famous parables in Buddhist literature, told in a version that reverses the usual ending. We translate each in its own register rather than levelling them.

On names: Anāthapiṇḍada, Ānanda, Śāriputra, Śuddhodana, Māyā, Gopī, Rāhula, Devadatta, Śakra, Maitreya, Rājagṛha, Śrāvastī, Vaiśālī, Gṛdhrakūṭa, Takṣaśilā, the Great Yuèzhī, the Tuṣita and Trāyastriṃśa heavens, and harītakī are given in their standard forms. Where a transliteration has no confident correspondence — Bhadra's Chinese name is glossed by the compiler himself, and Érlián, Cíluó, Nàjié, Fújiāluó, Qiānnà, and the Píyé ghost are not — we keep the Chinese in pinyin, with the compendium's own bracketed glosses preserved. Names transparently descriptive in Chinese (Worthy-Straight, Kindly Worthy, the Crown Prince of Two Kingdoms) are translated, with the Chinese kept in the contents list.

Entry 31 closes with verse, which we set off in italics with slashes for the line breaks of the Chinese; the Chinese column keeps it running on within the paragraph, as the source has it.

Several entries are violent or grotesque — a nephew beheading his own uncle, a nose cut from a stranger's face, five catties of flesh cut from a man's back, a man boiled by a servant ghost, a benefactor butchered for his liver and blood. All are translated in full, and the reading notes comment where the narrative turns on them rather than softening the text.

1. Bhadra Conjures a City to Invite the Buddha, and on Seeing Him Cannot Make It Vanish

An illusionist conjures a lecture hall to test whether the Buddha's omniscience is real, and then cannot dismiss what he has made.

Chinese Source

王舍城中。有一幻士。名曰颰陀(梁言仁賢)。明解經典曉了伎術所作巧黠多能喜悅名聞高遠。摩竭人民皆所不及。國內大小從其受學。颰陀遇覩世尊身色嚴妙。念欲相試。便示請佛。設知我意當不受請。若不知者必受無疑。佛知其意意欲度之。默然而受。颰陀謂。佛無通慧不見我意。入王舍城。於淨處化作講堂。懸繒華蓋。一一樹下。為諸比丘鋪師子座。於講堂中。特為如來設師子座。高四丈九尺。作百味之饌。時四天王及天帝釋。與三萬二千天人謂仁賢曰。真得善利。乃請如來講堂供養。吾助給所乏。即化作館殿如忉利宮。佛與菩薩及諸比丘往其講堂。見佛坐已所造之座。帝釋亦見如來坐其所造莊嚴之座。仁賢聽佛所說。蠲除自大投首佛足。白佛言。唯願如來原我罪過。欲沒其所化。佛令不變。復為說一切諸法皆如幻化(出幻士仁賢經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the city of Rājagṛha there was an illusionist named Bhadra (which in the language of Liang is “Kindly Worthy”), who understood the scriptures clearly and knew the arts thoroughly; what he did was skilful and clever and he was capable of much, and he was well liked and his fame reached far. None of the people of Magadha came near him, and all in the kingdom, great and small, studied under him.

Bhadra happened to see the World-Honored One, his bodily color grave and exquisite, and thought he would test him, and so made a show of inviting the Buddha: if he knows my mind he will not accept the invitation; and if he does not know it, he will certainly accept it. The Buddha knew his mind and, wishing to deliver him, accepted in silence; and Bhadra supposed that the Buddha had no penetrating wisdom and did not see his mind.

He went into the city of Rājagṛha and in a clean place conjured up a lecture hall hung with silk and flower canopies, and under every tree spread lion seats for the monks, and in the hall set a lion seat especially for the Tathāgata, four fathoms and nine feet high, and made dishes of a hundred flavors.

Then the Four Heavenly Kings and Śakra, with thirty-two thousand gods, said to Kindly Worthy: you have truly obtained a good gain, in inviting the Tathāgata to a lecture hall and making offerings; we shall help you with what is lacking. And they conjured up halls and palaces like the Trāyastriṃśa palace.

The Buddha, with the bodhisattvas and the monks, went to that lecture hall, and saw the Buddha sit upon the seat he himself had made; and Śakra likewise saw the Tathāgata sit upon the adorned seat that he had made.

Kindly Worthy listened to what the Buddha taught, put away his self-importance, and threw his head down at the Buddha’s feet and said to him: I ask only that the Tathāgata forgive my fault. And he wished to make what he had conjured disappear, and the Buddha would not let it change; and he taught him further that all dharmas are like conjurings and illusions (Source: Sūtra of the Illusionist Kindly Worthy).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The test is precisely posed: if he knows my mind he will refuse, and if he accepts he does not know. The Buddha accepts, and Bhadra draws the wrong conclusion, and the trap closes only at the end — when, having heard the teaching and repented, he tries to dismiss the conjured hall and the Buddha will not let it change. The lesson is then stated in one line: all dharmas are like conjurings.

Note the middle move, which is the sharpest thing in the entry. Śakra and thirty-two thousand gods arrive and add their own conjured palaces to Bhadra's, congratulating him on his merit; and the Buddha sits on the seat Bhadra made while Śakra sees him sitting on the one Śakra made. Both are illusions and both are, in that moment, adequate.

2. Anāthapiṇḍada's Well Produces Treasure

Five hundred shipwrecked merchants come to Anāthapiṇḍada's house, and he draws up their own lost treasure boxes from his well, each with its owner's name on it.

Chinese Source

阿難邠坻。在兒連山下居。大富珍寶。四遠賈客舉貸。乞丐者往無不得。時有五百賈客。行海船壞珍寶皆沒。死者不少或依板得活。咸詣阿難邠坻。主人設食。主人汲井得寶篋簏。各有題疏其姓字。賈客食訖皆悲。主人問故。答言。我等伴侶五百人。共行海中船舶翻沒。死者不少。挾持小板劣得生活。亡失珍寶悲念伴侶耳。屬覩井中所得寶篋。皆是我許不知那爾。主人言。卿曹行治生賈販。若至心者不失也。但不至心故失耳。我從無數劫以來。未嘗不至心初不欺侵。諸失寶者皆趣我井。卿曹各自案姓字取去(出譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Anāthapiṇḍada lived at the foot of Mount Érlián, greatly rich in treasure; merchants from far off borrowed from him, and no beggar who went to him failed to get something.

At that time five hundred merchants were sailing at sea and their ship broke up and their treasure all sank; not a few died, and some got by on planks and lived, and they all came to Anāthapiṇḍada. Their host set out food; and their host, drawing his well, brought up boxes and hampers of treasure, each with the owner’s surname written on it.

When the merchants had eaten, they all grieved, and their host asked why. They answered: we were a company of five hundred and travelled together at sea, and our ship capsized and not a few died; we held on to small planks and barely lived, and we lost our treasure and grieve for our companions. And now we see what has come up out of the well: it is all ours, and we do not know how it comes to be so.

Their host said: when you gentlemen go out to trade, if you are sincere at heart you do not lose it; it is only for want of sincerity that you lose. I have never, through countless kalpas, been other than sincere and have never cheated or encroached; and all who lose treasure find that it goes to my well. Each of you may look for your own name and take it (Source: Parables Sūtra).

3. Worthy-Straight Steals a Pearl, Does Not Confess, and Is Rewarded

A thief in a staged heaven works out that his hostesses are blinking.

Chinese Source

昔人名賢直。曾三預八關聞讀經言。天眼不瞬誦念不忘。然此賢直。善偷他物眼前不覺。國王失珠。召臣量議。臣答王言。聞有賢直極能作偷。王勅錄來考問望得其言不偷。王既有道何由抂人。又召諸臣共議。有臣言曰。當作方便令其首伏。當重枷鎖載市唱令殺之。與酒令醉然後脫鎖舉置堂上妓女作樂。王勅伎女。賢直醉問。汝可答言。此是天堂我是天女。應為侍側。君前身時偷國王珠故得生此。王便依之。賢直聞已默念而去。我聞經言。天眼不瞬此女悉瞬。且我偷珠應入地獄。將非國王張我令首。即便發言。我得生天由不偷珠。妓女報王。道賢直言。王便大笑。小子定不偷我珠也。即放令去。重賜金寶。此實偷珠。誦念一偈免罪得賜(出雜譬喻經也)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man named Worthy-Straight who had three times taken part in the eight-precept fast and had heard read from the sūtras the words: a heavenly eye does not blink; what is recited is not forgotten. But this Worthy-Straight was good at stealing other people’s things without their noticing before their eyes.

The king lost a pearl and summoned his ministers to consider it, and the ministers said to the king: we hear there is a Worthy-Straight who is extremely good at thieving. The king ordered him taken and questioned, hoping to get an admission from him; but he did not admit to the theft, and since the king had principles, how could he wrong a man?

So he called the ministers together again to confer, and one minister said: we should use an expedient to make him confess of himself. Let him be put in a heavy cangue and chains and carried through the market with the sentence of death proclaimed, and given liquor until he is drunk, and then let the chains be taken off and let him be set up in a hall with singing girls making music. Let the king instruct the girls that when Worthy-Straight, being drunk, asks them, they should answer: this is heaven and we are heavenly maidens who are to attend on you; in your former body you stole the king’s pearl, and so you have been born here.

The king did as he said. Worthy-Straight heard this and thought silently: I have heard the sūtras say that a heavenly eye does not blink, and these women are all blinking. Besides, if I stole the pearl I should have entered hell. Is not the king setting a trap to make me confess? And he said aloud: I have obtained birth in heaven because I did not steal the pearl.

The singing girls reported to the king what Worthy-Straight had said, and the king laughed heartily: this fellow certainly did not steal my pearl. And he released him and let him go, and gave him gold and treasure besides. He had truly stolen the pearl; and by reciting one verse he escaped the offense and got a reward (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra).

4. Cíluó Releases a Turtle and Later, in a Great Flood, Has His Own Life Saved

A man who wept in front of a turtle for sale, and what the turtle carried on its back afterward — a woman, the turtle-seller, and several pints of moths.

Chinese Source

昔有一人名慈羅。見人賣鼈心中憐之。向鼈啼泣。賣鼈者言。汝何故向鼈啼乎。慈羅答言。我不忍見之。窮賣鼈者大笑。汝癡狂耳。答言。我念此鼈從君請買。主言。鼈直百萬。慈羅便將之歸家。傾舉子息得八十萬。慈羅言。我錢盡此假求無處。賣鼈者言。汝錢既盡可為作田以畢錢直。慈羅言諾。以車載鼈投著池中。鼈便能言語。方有大水君當上樹相呼。後日洪水大起人民死盡。慈羅上樹呼鼈。鼈便來至。慈羅坐鼈背上。前去數里。見一女人在流槎上沮息欲死。便向慈羅乞匃求載。慈羅啟鼈。此人可怜乞得載之。鼈言。往便復載之。前行十里。見賣鼈子流被槎上。從慈羅欲求載之。鼈言我已重。恐必疲極不能自度。慎勿載之。慈羅言。可哀今是非當載之。慈羅復載之。前行數十里。見數升蛾流被槎上。慈羅復報鼈載之。前至那竭國。女子便以金謝慈羅。賣鼈人言。此鼈本是我賣之。汝今得金當持還我。慈羅不與。賣鼈子便到那竭國王所云。慈羅偷人婦將之販。今持金銀來在此國中。那竭國王即召慈羅使吏斬之。吏上言其事欲下筆書。蛾輒緣筆不成字。王聞之便問慈羅。汝有何功德乎。慈羅具答。王誅賣鼈者(出阿難現變經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man named Cíluó who saw someone selling a turtle and pitied it in his heart, and wept before the turtle. The turtle-seller said: why do you weep before a turtle? Cíluó answered: I cannot bear to see it. The poor turtle-seller laughed heartily: you are mad. He answered: I am thinking of this turtle, and I would buy it from you.

The owner said: the turtle is worth a million. Cíluó took it home, and by exhausting his capital and interest raised eight hundred thousand. Cíluó said: my money is at an end and there is nowhere to borrow more. The turtle-seller said: since your money is at an end, you may work my fields to pay off the price. Cíluó said: agreed.

He carried the turtle in a cart and put it into a pool, and the turtle then could speak: there is about to be a great flood; you should climb a tree and call me.

Later a great flood arose and the people all died; and Cíluó climbed a tree and called the turtle, and the turtle came, and Cíluó sat on the turtle’s back.

Going on a few li, he saw a woman on a drifting raft, exhausted and about to die, who begged Cíluó to take her aboard. Cíluó said to the turtle: this person is pitiable; let her be taken up. The turtle said: go on then — and took her too.

Going on ten li, he saw the turtle-seller drifting on a raft, who asked Cíluó to take him up. The turtle said: I am already heavy, and I fear I shall be worn out and unable to cross. Be careful not to take him. Cíluó said: it is pitiable; how at this moment can we not take him? And Cíluó took him too.

Going on some tens of li, he saw several pints of moths drifting on a raft, and Cíluó again told the turtle to take them up.

Going on, they came to the kingdom of Nàjié, and the woman thanked Cíluó with gold. The turtle-seller said: this turtle was mine and I sold it; the gold you have now got should be given back to me. Cíluó would not give it.

The turtle-seller then went to the king of Nàjié and said: Cíluó stole another man’s wife and is selling her, and he has brought gold and silver into this country. The king of Nàjié summoned Cíluó at once and ordered the officers to behead him. The officer reported the matter and was about to set down the writing, and the moths kept crawling onto his brush so that he could not form the characters. The king heard of it and asked Cíluó: what merit have you? Cíluó told him everything, and the king executed the turtle-seller (Source: Sūtra of Ānanda’s Manifest Transformations).

5. Qiānnà Earns Gold by Hired Painting and Sets Out a Feast, and Is Prosecuted by His Wife

A painter hands twelve years' wages to a monastery manager, goes home empty-handed, and is prosecuted by his wife. The judge is a lay follower.

Chinese Source

大月氏弗迦羅城。有一畫師。名曰千那。往來東方多剎施羅國。客畫經十二年。得三十兩金持還本國。遇見眾僧信心清淨。即問維那。此眾幾物得作一日食。答曰。可用三十兩金。畫師併付維那。乞營一食。我明日當來。空手歸家。婦問。十二年作得何等物。答曰。得三十兩金。已種福田。付僧設會。婦縛夫以送付官。具陳上事。官問。不給婦兒而以乞他。畫師答曰。我先世無福貧窮常辛苦。遭遇眾僧是良福田。若復不種善後世復貧。貧苦相續無得脫時。是故併施眾僧。其斷事人是優婆塞。即脫瓔珞及所乘馬并聚落以施畫師。謂之曰。汝施眾僧。眾僧未食。是為穀子未種而牙已生。而大果在後(出大智論第十一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the city of Fújiāluó in the Great Yuèzhī there was a painter named Qiānnà, who went east to the country of Takṣaśilā and painted as a hired man for twelve years and got thirty taels of gold, and carried it back to his own country.

He happened to see the community of monks, and his mind was pure with faith, and he asked the manager: how much would it take to provide this community with a single day’s food? He answered: it would take thirty taels of gold. The painter handed over all of it to the manager and asked him to arrange a meal: I shall come tomorrow.

He went home empty-handed, and his wife asked: after twelve years’ work, what have you got? He answered: I got thirty taels of gold; I have already planted it in the field of merit and handed it to the saṅgha for a feast.

His wife bound her husband and sent him to the authorities and set out the whole matter, and the official asked: you did not provide for your wife and children and gave it to others? The painter answered: in my former life I had no merit and was poor and always in hardship; and now I have met with the community of monks, who are a good field of merit. If I do not plant good now, I shall be poor again in a later life, and poverty and hardship will follow one another without any time of release. That is why I gave it all to the community.

The man who judged the case was a lay follower, and he took off his own necklace and gave the painter the horse he rode and a village besides, and said to him: you gave to the community, and the community has not yet eaten; this is a seed not yet sown from which the shoot has already come up — and the great fruit is still to come (Source: Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, fascicle 11).

6. A Spirit Comes Back and Strokes the Bones of Its Former Body

Four sentences: a spirit stroking its own bones, and its inventory of what that body did not do.

Chinese Source

昔有人。死後魂神還摩娑其故骨邊。人問之。汝已死。何故為復用摩娑枯骨為。神言。此是我故身。不殺生不盜竊不他婬不兩舌惡罵妄言綺語不嫉妬不瞋恚不癡。死後得生天上。所願自然快樂無極。是故愛重之也(出舊雜譬喻經下卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man who died, and afterward his spirit came back and stroked his own old bones. Someone asked him: you are already dead; why do you still stroke these dry bones?

The spirit said: this is my former body. It did not kill living things, did not steal, did not commit adultery, did not use double speech or evil abuse or false or ornamented words, was not jealous, not angry, not deluded. And after death I obtained birth in heaven, and what I wish for comes of itself and my joy has no limit. That is why I hold it dear (Source: Old Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra, lower fascicle).

7. The Woodcarver and the Painter Deceive Each Other

A wooden woman who serves wine but cannot speak, and a painted man hanged on a wall.

Chinese Source

昔北天竺。有一巧師。作一木女。端正無雙衣帶嚴飾世女無異。亦來亦去能行酒看客。唯不能語耳。時南天竺有一畫師。亦善能畫。巧師聞之。作好飲食即請畫師。便使木女行酒擎食。從旦至夜。畫師不知謂是真女。欲心極盛念之不忘。時日已暮。木師入宿。留畫師住。以此木女立侍其側。便語客言。故留此女可共宿也。主人已入。木女立在燈邊。客即呼之。而女不來。客便前牽之。乃知是木。便自慚愧。主人誑我。我當報之。於是畫師復作方便。即於壁上畫作己像。所著被服與身不異。以繩繫頸狀似絞死畫作繩像著其口喙。作已閉戶自入床下。天明主人出見戶未開。即向中觀。唯見壁上絞死客像。主人大怖便謂實死。即破戶入以刀斷繩。於是畫師從床下出。木師大羞。畫師即言。汝能誑我。我能誑汝。客主情畢不相負也。二人信知狂惑。各捨親愛出家修道(出雜譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago in North India there was a skilled craftsman who made a wooden woman, upright and beyond compare, with clothing, sashes, and ornaments no different from a real woman’s; and she came and went and could serve wine and attend on guests — only she could not speak.

At that time in South India there was a painter who was also very good at painting. The craftsman heard of him and prepared fine food and drink and invited the painter, and had the wooden woman serve the wine and carry the dishes from dawn until night. The painter did not know and took her for a real woman, and his lustful mind grew extreme and he could not forget her.

When it was dusk, the craftsman went in to sleep and left the painter to stay, and set this wooden woman standing at his side, and said to the guest: I leave this woman here on purpose; you may sleep with her.

The host having gone in, the wooden woman stood beside the lamp, and the guest called her and she did not come; and the guest went forward and pulled at her, and only then knew she was wood. He was ashamed of himself: the host has deceived me, and I shall pay him back.

So the painter contrived an expedient of his own: on the wall he painted an image of himself, wearing clothing no different from his own, with a rope tied round the neck as though he had hanged himself, and painted the likeness of the rope at its mouth. When it was done he shut the door and got in under the bed.

At dawn the host came out and saw that the door had not been opened, and looked in; and he saw only the image of the guest hanged on the wall. The host was greatly frightened and thought he had truly died, and broke down the door and went in and cut the rope with a knife — and at that the painter came out from under the bed. The craftsman was greatly ashamed, and the painter said: you were able to deceive me and I am able to deceive you. Guest and host, the account is settled and neither owes the other.

The two of them, knowing in faith that they had been mad and deluded, each gave up their kin and loved ones and went forth and cultivated the path (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra, fascicle 4).

8. A Physician Cures the King's Illness and Receives an Extraordinary Reward

A physician who cures a twelve-year illness, is sent home with a lame horse, and complains all the way — and then reaches his own front gate.

Chinese Source

有一國王。身得重病。十二年不差。一切大醫無能治者。時邊方小國攝屬大王。有一醫師善能治病。王即招來令治己疾。未久之間即蒙除降。王便念欲報此師恩。屬遣使者宣令彼國。此師見差宜應償賚。象馬車乘牛羊田宅青衣直人嚴飾之具皆給與之。彼小國王奉宣上命。師在王邊無有語者。師便思惟。我治王病大有功夫。未知王當報我與不。復經數日王轉平復。其師請辭欲還本國。王便聽之。給一羸馬乘具亦弊。師大笑恨我治王病。大有功夫。而王不識恩分。不相料理。令我空去。隨道愁歎以為永恨。適至本國見有群象。問象子曰。此誰家象。象子答曰。此是某甲師象。復問象子曰。某甲師何從得此象子。答曰。某甲師治大王病差。功報所得也。小復前行。見有群馬牛羊。問曰。此誰家物。羊子答曰。某甲師許。小復前行。見其本舍高堂重閣殊異本宅。問門人曰。此是誰舍。門人答曰。此是某甲師舍。便入其閤內。見其婦形色豐悅身服寶衣。怪而問曰。此誰夫人。直人答言。此是某甲師夫人。從見象馬。及入舍內。皆知是治王病功報所得。便自追恨本。治王病功夫少(出雜譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a king whose body took a grave illness and did not recover for twelve years, and no great physician could cure it. There was a small border kingdom subject to the great king, in which there was a physician who was good at curing sickness; and the king summoned him to treat his illness, and before long it was removed.

The king then thought of repaying this master’s kindness, and sent a messenger with a proclamation to that country: since this master has cured me, he ought to be rewarded — elephants, horses, carriages, oxen, sheep, fields, houses, servants, attendants, and ornaments of every kind, let them all be given him. And the king of that small country carried out the command.

The master, at the king’s side, had no one say anything to him, and he thought: I have done a great work in curing the king’s illness, and I do not know whether the king will repay me or not.

Some days later the king was fully recovered, and the master asked leave to go back to his own country, and the king permitted it, and gave him one lean horse with worn-out harness. The master laughed bitterly in resentment: I did a great work in curing the king’s illness, and the king does not know a favor and takes no thought for me and sends me away empty. And along the road he lamented and took it for a lasting grievance.

Coming to his own country he saw a herd of elephants and asked the elephant-boy: whose elephants are these? The elephant-boy answered: they are so-and-so the master’s elephants. He asked again: where did so-and-so the master get these elephants? He answered: so-and-so the master cured the great king’s illness, and they are the reward for that work.

Going on a little he saw herds of horses, cattle, and sheep, and asked: whose are these? The shepherd boy answered: so-and-so the master’s.

Going on a little he saw his own house, with high halls and storeyed pavilions quite unlike his old dwelling, and asked the gatekeeper: whose house is this? The gatekeeper answered: this is so-and-so the master’s house.

He went in to the inner chamber and saw his wife, her color full and glad, her body clothed in jewelled robes, and asked in surprise: whose lady is this? The attendant answered: this is so-and-so the master’s lady.

From seeing the elephants and horses to entering the house, he understood that all of it was the reward for the work of curing the king’s illness; and he then regretted, of himself, that his original work in curing the king’s illness had been so slight (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra, fascicle 4).

9. A Broken Fast Still Brings Birth in Heaven

A wife who ruins her husband's fast by waiting all day for him to eat, and an accounting of what half a fast is still worth.

Chinese Source

昔有迦羅越。興設大壇請佛及僧。時有一人賣酪。主人留食。勸令持齋聽經。至暝乃歸。婦語之言。我朝來不食相待至今。敗壞夫齋。半齋之福猶生天上七世。生人間常得自然。一日持齋六十萬歲自然之糧。又有五福。一者少病。二者身意安隱。三者少婬。四者少睡臥。五者命終之後神得生天常識宿命(出諸經中要事)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a householder who set up a great platform and invited the Buddha and the saṅgha; and there was a man selling curds whom the host kept to eat, and urged to keep the fast and hear the sūtras, and he went home only at dusk.

His wife said to him: I have not eaten since morning and have waited for you until now — and so ruined her husband’s fast. Even half a fast’s merit brings birth in heaven for seven generations, and birth among humans where things always come of themselves. One day of keeping the fast is sixty myriad years of spontaneous provision, and there are five blessings besides: first, little sickness; second, body and mind at ease; third, little lust; fourth, little sleeping; fifth, that after the end of life the spirit is born in heaven and always remembers its past lives (Source: Essential Matters from the Sūtras).

10. A Ploughman Gives a Monk One Myrobalan Fruit and Is Later Born Crown Prince of Two Kingdoms

A myrobalan and one coin given to a hungry monk in a field, and a boy who spends seven days in a fish's belly and is claimed by two kingdoms.

Chinese Source

昔有一人在田耕蒔。日已垂中。家餉未至。道人失路至其田所。從乞中食。耕人曰諾。小願須留。家餉遲晚。道人曰。食既不至仍欲漱口。耕人解其衣帶一訶梨勒果以與。道人即受而食。又有一錢以嚫道人。道人曰。若心可感無以相報。欲授五戒。君能受不。答曰。弟子在俗五戒難全。但受不殺。耕人命盡生國王家。皇后導從到流水邊。歌舞作樂抱兒臨水。失手墮水魚即吞之。在腹七日不飢不渴。隨流下逝遂千餘里。入下國界人捕得魚於市貨易。時下國主遣人市魚。將還城內用刀治魚。兒在腹中唱曰。徐徐勿傷我也。即破魚腸見一小兒。端正無雙舉國歡喜。上國主聞之曰。此必我兒。遣信索之。下國主答。魚腹中得天所賜。我不肯與之。二國共諍遂徹大國。大王判云。若上國不生此兒墮水下國何由而得。若下國不得此兒。上國何處求覓。此兩有緣。汝二國。中間可作宮殿共安此兒。號曰兩國太子。即從此旨在於其中。依太子儀法。佛語弟子。昔日耕人。一果一錢布施道人。便得兩國太子(出雜譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man ploughing and sowing in a field, and the day was already near noon and the food from his house had not come. A religious man had lost his way and came to that field and begged a midday meal from him. The ploughman said: yes — but I ask you to wait a little; the food from my house is late. The religious said: since the food has not come, I should like to rinse my mouth. The ploughman untied his sash and gave him a harītakī fruit, and the religious took it and ate it; and he had one coin besides and gave it to him as an offering.

The religious said: if it moves your mind, I have nothing with which to repay you; I should like to confer the five precepts on you — can you receive them? He answered: your disciple is a layman and the five precepts are hard to keep whole; I shall receive only the precept against killing.

When the ploughman’s life ended he was born into a king’s family. The queen went with her retinue to a stream and sang and danced and made music, and, holding the child, came to the water’s edge, and lost her hold and he fell in, and a fish swallowed him at once. He was in its belly seven days, neither hungry nor thirsty, and drifted downstream more than a thousand li and entered the borders of a lower kingdom, where men caught the fish and took it to market to sell.

The ruler of that lower kingdom sent a man to buy the fish and bring it into the city, and they took a knife to prepare the fish; and the boy in its belly cried out: gently, gently — do not hurt me. So they opened the fish’s gut and saw a small boy, upright beyond compare, and the whole kingdom rejoiced.

The ruler of the upper kingdom heard of it and said: this must be my son — and sent messengers to ask for him. The ruler of the lower kingdom answered: I got him in a fish’s belly; it was heaven that gave him to me, and I shall not give him up. The two kingdoms contended over it and it came at last to the great kingdom.

The great king gave judgment: if the upper kingdom had not borne this boy, how should the lower kingdom, when he fell into the water, have obtained him? And if the lower kingdom had not obtained him, where should the upper kingdom look for him? These two have a connection with him. Between your two kingdoms you may build a palace and put the boy there together. He was named the Crown Prince of Two Kingdoms, and from that ruling he lived there and was treated according to the ceremony due a crown prince.

The Buddha told his disciples: the ploughman of that day, by giving one fruit and one coin to a religious, obtained the crown princedom of two kingdoms (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra).

11. Providing for a Śramaṇa with a Good or an Evil Mind Brings Different Rewards

A household grown rich after providing for a lame monk for a year, and the neighbour who could not find a lame monk and made one.

Chinese Source

昔有跛脚道人。持戒乞食遇至一家。信大法久見其患脚。心悲愍一年供養。道人辭去。主人言曰。願數垂顧。分離之際客主悲淚。道人去後。主人發床唯見金寶。因此至富。隣比一家見其大富。問何因緣。其人實答。隣人惡念希覓珍寶。便覓一跛人欲供養之。遍求無有。會一道人身體完具。縛還折脚供養少時。強驅令去。去後發床。惡心所感。毒蛇蜂蝎來螫合家。現世惡報後入地獄。得珍寶者其心貞吉。被螫毒者其心不仁(出雜譬喻經也)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a lame religious man who kept the precepts and begged for his food, and happened to come to a house that had long had faith in the great Dharma; and seeing that his foot was afflicted they were moved to pity and provided for him a whole year.

The religious took his leave, and the master of the house said: I hope you will look in on us often — and at the moment of parting host and guest wept together. After the religious had gone, the master lifted the couch and saw nothing but gold and treasure, and by this he became very rich.

A neighbouring household, seeing his great wealth, asked what the cause was, and the man answered truthfully. The neighbour, with an evil thought, hoped to find treasure, and looked for a lame man to provide for; and he searched everywhere and there was none. He met one religious man whose body was quite whole, and bound him and brought him back and broke his leg and provided for him a little while, and then forced him to go.

After he had gone they lifted the couch — and moved by that evil mind, venomous snakes, wasps, and scorpions came and stung the whole household. In this present life the evil recompense; and afterward they entered hell. He who got the treasure — his mind was upright and auspicious; those who were stung — their minds were not humane (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra).

過去時姊弟二人。姊有一子。與舅俱給官府。織金縷錦及綾羅縠珍妙異衣藏中綺寶。即共議言。吾織作勤苦不懈。知諸藏物好醜多少。寧可共取用解貧乏乎。夜鑿地為孔穿盜取物。明監藏者覺物減少。以事啟王。王詔勿廣宣之。令盜者謂王多事不能覺察。至于後日必復重來。且嚴警守得者收捉無令放逸。藏監受語。即加守備。其人久久果重來盜。外甥教舅。年尊體羸力少。若為守者所得不能自脫。更從地穴却行而入。如令見得。我力強盛當濟免舅。舅這入穴為守者所執。執者喚呼。甥畏人識。截取舅頭而去。晨曉藏監具以啟聞。王又詔曰。輿出其尸置四交露。其有對哭取死屍者則知是賊。如是積日。有遠方賈客來。人馬塞路奔突猥逼。其人載兩車薪置其屍上。具以啟王。王詔。若有燒者收縛送來。外甥將教童竪執炬舞戲。人眾總集以火投薪。熾然而盛。守者不覺。具以啟王。王又詔曰。更增守者。嚴伺其骨。甥又釀純酒。特令濃厚。詣守備。者微而沽之。守者連昔飢渴。見酒共飲。飲酒過多皆共醉寐。因以酒瓶受骨。而去。守者不覺。明復啟王。王又詔曰。前後警守竟不級獲。斯賊狡黠更當設謀。王即出女莊嚴瓔珞珠璣寶飾。安立房室於大水傍。眾人侍衛伺察非妄。必有利色來趣女者。素教誡女得抱捉喚人收執。他日異夜甥尋竊來。因水放株令從流下。唱叫犇隱。守者驚趣謂有異人。但見株机。如是連昔數數不變。守者睡眠。甥即乘株到女室。女則執衣。甥告女曰。可捉我臂。甥預持死人臂以授與。女便放衣捉臂大叫。守者覺甥得脫走。明具啟王。王又詔曰。此人方便獨百無雙。久捕不得。當奈之何。女即懷妊。十月生男。大端正。使乳母抱行周遍國中。有人嗚噈者便縛送來。抱兒終日。甥為餅師住餅爐下。小兒飢啼。乳母抱兒趣餅爐下。市餅哺兒。甥見嗚兒。即以還白王曰。兒行終日無來近者。飢過餅爐。賣餅者授餅乃嗚。王又詔曰。何不縛之。乳母答曰。小兒飢啼餅師授餅而嗚之不應是賊。王使乳母更抱兒出及諸伺候。見近兒者便縛將來。甥沽美酒。呼請乳母及伺者就于酒家勸酒。大醉眠臥便盜兒去。醒悟失兒。具以啟王。王又詔曰。卿等頑騃貪嗜狂水既不得賊復亡失兒。甥時得兒抱至他國。前見國王。占謝答對引經說義。王大歡喜。輒賜祿位以為大臣。而謂之曰。吾之一國智慧方便無逮卿者。欲以臣女若吾女當以相配自恣所欲。對曰不敢。若王見哀欲索某國王女。王曰善哉。從所志願。王以之為子。遣使者往求彼王女。王即可之。王續念言。或是盜魅前後狡猾。即遣使者。欲迎吾女。遣其太子五百騎乘皆使嚴整。甥懷恐懼。恐到彼國。王必執之。便啟其王。若王見遣。當令人馬五百騎具衣服鞍勒一無差異。乃可迎婦。王然言。即往迎婦。王令女飲食待客。二百五十騎在前。二百五十騎在後。甥在其中跨馬不下。女父自出入騎中。執甥曰。爾為是非前後方便捕不可得。為是爾非。稽首答曰。是也。王曰。卿之聰哲天下無雙。隨卿所願以女配之得為夫婦。外甥吾是。女父王者舍利弗是。舅者調達是。國王父輸頭檀是。母摩耶是。婦拘夷是。子羅云是(出生經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In a past age there were a sister and a brother, and the sister had a son; and the son and his uncle both served in the government office, weaving cloth of gold thread and figured silks and gauzes and fine rare garments, and the storehouse held brocades and treasures.

They conferred together: we weave and labour diligently and never slacken, and we know which of the storehouse’s goods are good and which poor and how many there are. Might we take some together and relieve our poverty? At night they bored a hole in the ground and went in and stole goods.

In the morning the storehouse keeper noticed that the goods had diminished and reported the matter to the king. The king ordered that it not be widely proclaimed, so that the thief should suppose the king had many affairs and could not detect it, and would certainly come again another day: keep strict watch, and whoever is caught, seize him and do not let him go. The storehouse keeper received the order and set a guard.

After a long while the man did indeed come again to steal. The nephew instructed his uncle: you are advanced in years and your body is weak and your strength small; if you are caught by the guards you cannot get yourself free. Go in through the tunnel backward. If you are caught, my strength is great and I shall save my uncle. His uncle went into the tunnel and was seized by the guards, and the guards called out; and the nephew, fearing his uncle would be recognized, cut off his head and went away.

At dawn the storehouse keeper reported it, and the king ordered again: carry out the body and set it at a crossroads in the open. Whoever weeps over it and takes the corpse away is the thief.

Days passed; and there came merchants from far away, and men and horses filled the road, jostling and pressing. The nephew loaded two carts of firewood and set them on the corpse. It was reported to the king, and the king ordered: if anyone burns it, seize and bind him and bring him here. The nephew had a boy hold a torch and dance about with it, and while the crowd was gathered he threw fire into the firewood, and it blazed up fiercely, and the guards did not notice. They reported it to the king.

The king ordered again: increase the guards and watch strictly over the bones. The nephew then brewed pure liquor, making it especially strong, and went to the guards and sold it cheaply. The guards, hungry and thirsty from the night before, saw the liquor and drank it together, and, drinking too much, were all drunk and asleep; and he took the bones away in a liquor jar and the guards did not notice.

In the morning they reported to the king again, and the king ordered: guards have been set before and after and he has still not been caught. This thief is crafty and we must lay another plan. So the king brought out his daughter, adorned with necklaces, pearls, and jewels, and set her in a room beside a great water with a crowd of attendants keeping watch and examining, in case someone should come to the woman out of desire; and the girl was instructed beforehand that when she had hold of him she should call out and have him seized.

One night the nephew came secretly, and floated a tree-stump on the water and let it drift down, and cried out and hid. The guards started up and rushed over supposing there was an intruder, and saw only a stump. This happened night after night without change, and the guards slept; and the nephew then rode the stump to the girl’s room, and the girl seized his clothing.

The nephew said to the girl: you may take hold of my arm — and the nephew had brought a dead man’s arm beforehand and gave her that. The girl let go of the clothing and grasped the arm and cried out loudly; and the guards woke, and the nephew got away and escaped.

In the morning they reported it to the king, and the king ordered again: this man’s resourcefulness is unmatched anywhere; we have long failed to catch him. What is to be done?

The girl then conceived, and after ten months bore a boy, exceedingly handsome; and a nurse was made to carry him about all through the kingdom, and whoever kissed him was to be bound and brought in.

She carried the child all day. The nephew was a cake-seller and had his stall under a cake oven. The little boy cried with hunger, and the nurse carried him to the cake oven and bought a cake to feed him, and the nephew, seeing it, kissed the child. He reported to the king at once: the child was carried all day and nobody came near him; when he passed the cake oven hungry, the cake-seller gave him a cake and then kissed him.

The king ordered again: why did you not bind him? The nurse answered: the little boy was crying with hunger and the cake-seller gave him a cake and kissed him; he ought not to be the thief. The king had the nurse carry the child out again with the watchers, and whoever came near the child was to be bound and brought in. The nephew sold fine liquor and invited the nurse and the watchers into the wine shop and urged liquor on them; and being very drunk they lay down asleep, and he stole the child away. When they came to themselves the child was gone, and they reported to the king.

The king ordered again: you are dull and stupid and greedy for drink, and you have neither caught the thief nor kept the child.

The nephew, having got the child, carried him to another country, and came before its king and answered his questions with apt sayings and expounded the sense of the scriptures. The king was greatly pleased and at once gave him rank and salary and made him a great minister, and said to him: in my whole kingdom no one has wisdom and resourcefulness equal to yours. I shall give you a minister’s daughter, or my own daughter, as you please. He answered: I dare not; but if the king would take pity on me, I would ask for the daughter of a certain king.

The king said: excellent — you shall have your wish. And the king took him for a son and sent messengers to ask that king for his daughter, and the king consented. But that king thought again: perhaps this is that thief and his tricks, before and now. And he sent his messenger and also his crown prince with five hundred horsemen, all in strict order.

The nephew was afraid, fearing that when he came to that country the king would seize him, and said to his own king: if the king sends me, let five hundred men and horses be made ready with clothing and saddles all exactly alike, and only then may I go to fetch the bride. The king agreed.

So he went to fetch the bride, and the king had his daughter set out food and drink for the guests. Two hundred and fifty horsemen were in front and two hundred and fifty behind, and the nephew was in the middle astride his horse and did not dismount; and the bride’s father came out himself among the horsemen and took hold of the nephew and said: are you he or not, whom before and after by every device I could not catch? Is it you or not?

He bowed his head and answered: it is. The king said: your cleverness is unmatched under heaven — you shall have your wish, and I give you my daughter, and you may be husband and wife.

The nephew was myself; the girl’s father the king is Śāriputra; the uncle is Devadatta; the king my father is Śuddhodana; the mother is Māyā; the wife is Gopī; the son is Rāhula (Source: Jātaka Sūtra, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The longest entry in the fascicle and one of the best-constructed narratives in the compendium: a duel of stratagems between a thief and a king, in which each of the king's five traps is exactly reasonable and each is defeated by a device the reader can follow.

The traps and answers in order: the corpse displayed at the crossroads to catch whoever mourns it — defeated by dumping two cartloads of firewood on it in a crowd and having a boy dance about with a torch; the guarded bones — defeated by strong liquor sold cheap to thirsty guards; the king's daughter set as bait in a room by the water — defeated by floating stumps down the river night after night until the guards stop reacting, and then by handing her a dead man's arm to hold; the child carried through the city to see who kisses him — defeated by making the child hungry at a cake stall, and then, when the guard is doubled, by getting the nurse and the watchers drunk.

The gruesome opening is the part most easily passed over. The nephew's reason for cutting off his own uncle's head is stated plainly: he feared his uncle would be recognized. Everything after that is an attempt to keep the family from being identified through the body.

The identifications at the end place the Buddha in the role of the thief and Śāriputra in the role of the king who could not catch him.

13. A Man of Rājagṛha and a Woman of Gṛdhrakūṭa Have a Connection from a Former Life

A man of Rājagṛha and a woman of Gṛdhrakūṭa who know each other on sight after five years' travel, and the five further years of fasting it takes to marry.

Chinese Source

昔有耆闍崛國。有一少女。姿形美妙才智絕倫。性情寬和初不瞋怒。家門九族及諸疎近莫不愛敬。諸國求索有百千人。竟未成嫁。時羅閱國一少男。天姿英秀心意高邁。國中百姓莫不畏敬。雖有妓妾夫人初不幸近。每念。耆闍崛國有女。本是我對。未及至耳。即日。滿堂皆是勢利所得非我偶也。一時遣逐肅然都盡。乃乘白象從僕二人往造彼國。經山涉草行千萬里。五年方至。女處深宮無故心動。因出逍遙。試闚外閤。忽見此男。且悲且喜不可抑忍。此是吾夫。前後見求非我宿匹。便自親著夫情依依。女之父母憂惱無計云。養女長大不悟如此。急加防錄不得通問。女子懊惱不知何計。男子悲惶同懷愁惘。於是女子精苦齋戒晝夜行道。棄散資財施佛及僧。心勞形枯經涉五載。音問不達彼此懷情。竟無所感。乃以四月八日。親自束身往到佛塔。燒香禮拜發此志願。割刻肌肉供養世尊。不敢自惜。唯願此志一得申果。佛令比丘尼為其作緣。便得和偶。佛告阿難。所以者何。皆從阿僧祇劫已來。久結願約故得爾耳(出情離有罪經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago in the country of Gṛdhrakūṭa there was a young woman of exquisite form and unmatched talent and intelligence, of a broad and harmonious temper who never grew angry; and in her household, among the nine degrees of kin and all their connections near and far, there was none who did not love and respect her. From every country a hundred thousand sought her, and she was still not married.

At that time in the country of Rājagṛha there was a young man of heavenly grace and lofty mind, whom all the people of the kingdom held in awe; and though he had entertainers and consorts he never went near them. He thought constantly: in the country of Gṛdhrakūṭa there is a woman who was originally my match, and I have simply not reached her yet.

On that very day he thought: everything in this hall was got by power and gain and none of it is my match — and at one stroke he sent them all away and the place was left bare. And he mounted a white elephant with two attendants and set out for that country, crossing mountains and wading through grass for ten million li, and after five years arrived at last.

The woman was in the depths of her palace and her heart stirred for no reason, and she came out to walk about and tried peering out of the outer chamber, and suddenly saw this man; and she was both grieved and glad and could not restrain it: this is my husband, sought for before and since, and my match of a former life. And she went to him herself, and his feelings toward her clung close.

The woman’s parents were distressed and had no plan: we raised our daughter to be grown and did not understand it would be like this. And they set a strict guard so that no word could pass. The woman was vexed and did not know what to do, and the man in his grief and confusion shared the same anxiety.

So the woman practiced strict fasting and kept the precepts and walked the path day and night, and gave away her resources to the Buddha and the saṅgha; and her heart was worn and her form withered, and five years passed and no word reached him and each held their feeling and there was no response at all.

So on the eighth day of the fourth month she bound herself and went in person to the Buddha’s stūpa and burned incense and bowed and made this vow: cutting and carving my own flesh I make offering to the World-Honored One and do not begrudge myself; I ask only that this resolve be granted its fruit.

And the Buddha had a nun make the connection for her, and they were joined. The Buddha told Ānanda: why is this so? It is because from asaṃkhya kalpas ago they long ago bound a vow together, and so it comes about (Source: Sūtra on the Offense of Parting from Feeling).

14. A Husband and Wife Wager Not to Speak First, and He Says Nothing While a Thief Takes Their Goods

One cake left over, a wager of silence, and a burglar.

Chinese Source

昔有夫婦。共食三䴵。人各一枚。餘一欲破分。婦言。莫分。與君共賭。各自不語。先語者失。後語者得。於是閉口至于中夜。竇土偷入。見其二人坐而不語。謂是大怖不敢作聲。收斂其物擔將出戶。婦大喚曰。汝是丈夫那置物去。夫言。我勝我勝。今得大䴵。眾人責笑謂大顛癡(出百句譬喻經第二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there were a husband and wife who ate three cakes together, one each, and there was one left over that they meant to break and divide. The wife said: do not divide it; I shall wager with you. Let neither of us speak; whoever speaks first loses and whoever speaks last gets it.

So they shut their mouths; and in the middle of the night a burglar came in and saw the two of them sitting and not speaking, and, supposing this was some great terror, dared not make a sound; and he gathered up their goods and carried them out of the door.

The wife cried out: you are a man — how can you let the goods go? Her husband said: I win, I win — now I get the big cake. And people blamed and laughed at them and called them great fools (Source: Sūtra of the Hundred Parables, fascicle 2).

15. A Woman's Nose Being Ugly, Her Husband Cuts Off Another's Fine One to Exchange for It

16. A Hired Man Who Understands the Speech of Birds

A porter who could have killed a merchant for a pearl on a crow's advice, and did not.

Chinese Source

昔有一極貧人。善曉鳥語。為賈客賃擔。過水邊飲。烏鳴。賈客怖。作人反笑。到家問言。我在彼飲。時聞烏鳴。我大怖而君反笑何耶。答曰。烏向語我。賈人身上有好白珠。汝可殺之取珠。我欲食其肉。是故我笑耳。曰汝何不殺也。答曰。我坐前世貪人財物故貧為賃擔。若復殺人取物後世受苦。何時當已。我今至死不為此事(出譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was an extremely poor man who understood the speech of birds well and hired himself to a merchant as a porter. Passing by the water’s edge they drank, and a crow cried out, and the merchant was frightened and the hired man laughed instead.

Coming home he asked him: when we drank there and I heard the crow cry, I was greatly frightened, and you laughed instead — why? He answered: the crow was saying to me — this merchant has a fine white pearl on him; you may kill him and take the pearl, and I shall eat his flesh. That is why I laughed.

He said: and why did you not kill me? He answered: it is because in a former life I coveted others’ goods that I am poor and hired as a porter. If I killed a man again and took his goods, then in a later life I should suffer, and when would it ever end? To my dying day I shall not do this thing (Source: Parables Sūtra).

17. A Drowning Man Clings to a Phoenix and Survives; Others Cling to a Cormorant and Perish

Ru-Yi Translation

A great flood suddenly rose, and living creatures took refuge on a hill; and when they were about to be submerged, a phoenix suddenly came to where the wise one was, and he attached himself to its wings and so got up onto high ground and escaped the peril of the water.

18. A Man Buys Wisdom and Escapes a Great Crime

A man who paid a thousand taels of gold for the instruction to walk seven steps forward and seven back three times before acting, and got his money's worth that same night.

Chinese Source

昔有一人。貧窮無用。治生入海採寶還國。遇善知識言。我素貧窮今得此物。足以自諧。若母不可。我意當捨母居去。若婦不可。我意我當更索。知識答曰。近此間大智慧人滿城中。可往就買智慧。不過千兩金。自當語卿智慧之法。其人如其言。入事佛聚落。具以問人。答曰。夫所疑事。前行七步却行七步。如是至三智慧自生。其人夜歸家。見母伴婦眠。謂是他男。拔刀欲殺。意中不掩。然大燈火遙照思惟。朝買智慧。如是前却三反。母便覺悟。此人歎言。真為智慧。何但堪千兩金。即復與三千兩金(出十卷譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man, poor and of no use, who took up trading and went to sea and gathered treasure and came back to his country. He met a good friend and said: I was always poor before and have now got these goods, enough to manage on. If my mother will not have it my way, I mean to leave her and live apart; and if my wife will not have it my way, I mean to look for another.

His friend answered: near here there is a man of great wisdom, and the city is full of it; you may go and buy wisdom from him. For no more than a thousand taels of gold he will tell you the method of wisdom.

The man did as he said and went into a settlement that served the Buddha and asked about it, and was answered: whenever a thing is in doubt, walk seven steps forward and seven steps back, and do so three times, and wisdom will arise of itself.

That night the man went home and saw his mother sleeping beside his wife, and, supposing it was another man, drew his blade meaning to kill him; but in his mind it did not seem right, and he lit a great lamp and looked from a distance and reflected: this morning I bought wisdom. And he went forward and back three times in that way, and his mother woke.

The man exclaimed: this is truly wisdom — and how should it be worth only a thousand taels of gold? And he gave him three thousand taels more (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 4).

19. A Man Outwits a Ghost and Escapes Harm

A man about to be eaten who asks one question about his captor's complexion.

Chinese Source

昔有一人行於山中。逢一噉人鬼。撮欲食之。其人求哀。假令須臾請問一事。却乃見噉為不恨也。鬼信其人謂是誠事。聽其所欲。即問鬼言。何故面白脚膝腹白。其餘處盡黑。鬼即答言。我之為物性惡日精。背日得行不得向日。以是故爾。其人向日而走。鬼但空恨不能得也(出十卷譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man travelling in the mountains who met a man-eating ghost, who seized him meaning to eat him. The man begged for pity: allow me a moment to ask one thing, and then let me be eaten and I shall bear no grudge. The ghost believed the man and, supposing it a sincere matter, let him do as he wished.

He asked the ghost: why is your face white, and your legs and knees and belly white, and all the rest of you black? The ghost answered: I am by nature a thing that hates the sun’s essence; I can travel with my back to the sun and cannot face it. That is why it is so. And the man ran toward the sun, and the ghost could only resent it in vain and could not get him (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 4).

20. A Man Is Hated by Both His Wives and It Brings Him to His Death

A man plucking his own hair white and black by turns for two wives, and the dog between two monasteries who could not decide.

Chinese Source

昔有一人於兩業。有二婦適詣小婦。小婦語言。我年少。婿年老。我不樂住。可往大婦處作居。其婿拔去白髮。適至大婦處。大婦語言。我年老頭已白。婿頭黑宜去。於是拔黑作白。如是不止。頭遂禿盡。二婦惡之。便各捨去。坐愁致死。過去世時作寺中狗。水東一寺水西一寺。聞揵搥鳴狗便往得食。後日二寺同時鳴磬。狗浮水欲渡。適欲至西復恐東寺食好。向東復恐西寺食好。如是猶豫溺死水中(出十卷譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man of two occupations who had two wives. Going to the younger wife, the younger wife said to him: I am young and my husband is old; I do not like living with you — go and live with the elder wife. So he pulled out his white hairs.

Coming to the elder wife, the elder wife said to him: I am old and my head is already white, and my husband’s head is black; you had better go. So he pulled out the black to make it white. Going on like this without stopping, his head became entirely bald, and both wives loathed him and each left him; and he sat in grief and it brought him to his death.

In a past age he was a dog in a monastery. There was one monastery east of the water and one west of the water; when he heard the gong sound the dog would go and get food. Later, one day, both monasteries struck the chimes at the same time, and the dog swam meaning to cross; and just as he was about to reach the west he feared the food at the east monastery was better, and turning toward the east he feared the west monastery’s was better — and hesitating in this way he drowned in the water (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra).

21. A Man Travels Far Seeking the Water of Immortality; His Host, in Malice, Makes Him Climb a Tree, and He Becomes an Immortal

A host who sends a guest up a cliff-side tree to make a fool of him, and finds he has made an immortal — and then tries it himself.

Chinese Source

昔有一人。聞外國有仙水入中得仙。便向外國寄他宿止。主人問客。君欲何去。答曰學仙。主人懷惡。語客言曰。我有仙樹。君能與我一年苦作。便與君仙何煩遠去。客言甚善。一年苦作恒無慍色。一年既滿。其主人公本心相欺。既無仙樹。將至山中指臨巖樹云是仙樹。君上其頭。我喚若飛應聲飛擲。客人心至。即於此樹飛騰虛空。遂得仙道。主人公見我令其死。何悟得仙。深重此樹情言是聖。復經少時。父子相與共到樹下。讓父先上。兒便喚言阿耶可飛。父即欲飛。墮巖石上。身體粉碎(出雜譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man who heard that in a foreign country there was water of immortality, and that entering it one becomes an immortal; and he set off for that country and lodged with someone. The host asked his guest: where do you mean to go? He answered: to study immortality.

The host, harbouring evil, said to his guest: I have a tree of immortality. If you will work hard for me for a year I shall give you immortality — why trouble to go so far? The guest said: very good. For a year he worked hard and never showed a resentful look.

When the year was up, the host, whose intention from the first had been to cheat him, and who had no tree of immortality, took him into the mountains and pointed to a tree overhanging a cliff: this is the tree of immortality. Go up to the top of it, and when I call out, fly.

At the sound of his voice he flung himself out; and the guest’s mind being wholly sincere, he flew up from that tree into the open air and attained the way of an immortal.

The host thought: I made him go to his death — how has he come to be an immortal? And he greatly valued that tree and said in his heart that it was holy. After a little while father and son went together to the foot of the tree, and he let his father go up first, and the son cried out: father, you may fly. His father meant to fly, and fell on the rocks of the cliff and his body was broken to pieces (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra).

22. A Man Employs a Ghost and Grows Rich, and It Afterward Kills His Son

A ghost bought for two hundred taels of gold that does the work of a hundred men, with one condition attached.

Chinese Source

昔有一人。於市賣毘耶鬼。欲買鬼者問索幾許。鬼主言。二百兩金。曰。此鬼有何奇異。乃索爾所金耶。曰。此鬼其功無物不為。計一日作當百人。唯有一病。宜先防護之。問為何等病。曰。此鬼欲使作時。晝夜使之。莫令停息。若無作者便還害主。主人顧金將歸。令作田種。作田種竟便使木作。木作竟復使治地。作屋舂磨炊爨初不寧息。數年之中乃致大富。主人有事當行作客。忘不處分而鬼復欲作無有次第。取主人兒內釜中然火煮之。比主人還子已爛熟。傷切懊惱知復何言(出譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man selling a Píyé ghost in the market. Someone who wished to buy the ghost asked how much he wanted, and the ghost’s owner said: two hundred taels of gold. He said: what is so extraordinary about this ghost, that you ask so much gold for it? He said: there is nothing this ghost cannot do; reckoning a day’s work, it does as much as a hundred men. It has only one fault, which you should guard against beforehand.

He asked what fault. He said: when you set this ghost to work you must work it day and night and never let it rest; if there is nothing for it to do it will turn and harm its master.

The man paid the gold and took it home and set it to plough and sow; and when the ploughing and sowing were done he set it to carpentry; and when the carpentry was done he had it level ground, build houses, pound and grind, cook and boil, and it never rested at all. And within a few years he became very rich.

The master had business and had to go away as a guest, and forgot to give instructions; and the ghost, wanting work and finding none in order, took the master’s son and put him in a cauldron and lit a fire and boiled him. By the time the master came back the son was already cooked through — and what words are there for that grief and anguish? (Source: Parables Sūtra)

23. A Man's Wealth: the King Demands an Itemized Account and He Lists What Is Already Spent, and the King Understands

A rich man ordered to list his assets who begins with what he has already given away.

Chinese Source

昔有富人。王令條物。其即疏。某年用若干千萬造佛。用若干作齋會經書。又以若干供恤貧老。今現有八十九千萬。以疏呈王。王大瞋怒問。汝現物忽條已用。富人答曰。已用者最為寶藏。資人神明是以奉呈。未用之財五家共有。非獨臣許。是敢條疏。王便覺悟。功德可恃(出譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a rich man whom the king ordered to itemize his goods; and he set down: in such a year I used so many thousands and myriads to make buddha-images; so much for fasting assemblies and for scriptures; and so much to provide for the poor and the old. And I now have eighty-nine thousand myriads remaining. And he presented the list to the king.

The king was greatly angered and asked: your present goods — how is it you have itemized what is already spent? The rich man answered: what is already spent is the best treasury of all, and it nourishes a person’s spirit and understanding, and that is why I present it. Wealth not yet spent is shared by the five claimants and is not mine alone, and so I dared to itemize it. And the king came to understand that merit is what may be relied on (Source: Parables Sūtra).

24. A Man Offends and the King Orders Five Catties of His Flesh Cut Off

Five catties of flesh cut off in error, a hundred catties given back in compensation, and a minister's question about a dead son.

Chinese Source

昔有一人犯罪於王。王大瞋忿。使人割其背膂五斤肉。置之下屋。此人痛苦叫喚徹王。王問何意不分。答曰。實非我罪。王思愧之。勅以一百斤肉乞。其猶故呻喚。王曰。頓補百斤云何大喚。諸臣答言。不見此法。與千斤何益其痛。若人一兒已死。他人以百與之。此人能不念其兒不(出百句譬喻經第三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man who offended against the king, and the king was greatly enraged and had five catties of flesh cut from his back and set in a lower room. The man cried out in pain until it reached the king, and the king asked why he made such a distinction. He answered: it was truly not my offense. The king thought and was ashamed, and ordered that a hundred catties of flesh be given him as compensation; and he still groaned and cried out.

The king said: you have been made up a full hundred catties — why do you cry out so? The ministers answered: we do not see how this method helps. What good would a thousand catties do his pain? If a man’s only son has died and another gives him a hundred sons, can that man help thinking of his own son? (Source: Sūtra of the Hundred Parables, fascicle 3)

25. Two Men Vow to Marry Their Unborn Children to Each Other

A betrothal made before either child was born, and three omens seen on the road, which the bride reads as prophecies of a later age.

Chinese Source

有二人共為知識。婦各懷妊。便相誓約。若生有男女共為婚姻。別後一家生男一家生女。男父早死長大未娶。行賣雜物偶至女家。女家公問子從何來。居止所在父母姓字。男具以答。公聞大驚。乃語男曰。卿父在時與我胎婚。我常相求不相知。處女未敢嫁。男言。我都不知女。公曰。卿問親近。男還問乳母信然。男到女家。道見溝水流。入一髑髏中無有滿時。兒恐前行復見樹果熟。欲取噉之。果便言。取我取我。兒大怖懅疾走躄地。前到女家。狗逆長跪咶其兩足。狗腹中子哮呼來前。欲傷嚙之。便復頓地久乃蘇息。女公來出兒具陳說。公甚怪之入語其女。女答公曰道見溝水流入髑髏無有滿時者。後世當有斂取天下財物珍寶。供給一人無有滿足。見樹果熟欲取噉之。而果言取我取我者。後世之人自求大女。而小女言。何不索我何不索我。狗逆長跪咶其兩足。狗腹中子哮呼來前欲嚙之者。後世之人相與言語。口如脂膏心如錐刀。口相飽滿內相謀圖。皆為後世不為今也。即嫁女與男。遂其本志(出十卷譬喻經第八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There were two men who were friends, and their wives were both with child, and they made a compact together: if a boy and a girl are born, let them be married. After they parted, one household bore a boy and the other a girl.

The boy’s father died early, and the boy grew up unmarried, and went about selling sundry goods and happened to come to the girl’s house. The girl’s father asked the young man where he came from, where he lived, and his parents’ names, and the young man answered in full. The old man heard and was greatly startled, and said to the young man: when your father was alive he made a marriage compact with me while you were in the womb, and I have always sought for you and did not know where you were; and my daughter has not dared to marry.

The young man said: I know nothing at all of it. The old man said: ask your close kin. The young man went back and asked his nurse, and it was so.

As the young man went to the girl’s house, he saw on the road a channel of water flowing into a skull and never filling it. The boy was afraid; and going on further he saw a tree with ripe fruit and wished to take some and eat, and the fruit said: take me, take me. The boy was terrified and ran and fell to the ground. Coming to the girl’s house, a dog came to meet him and knelt at length and licked his two feet, and the pups in the dog’s belly howled and came forward meaning to bite him; and again he fell to the ground and only after a long while revived.

The girl’s father came out, and the boy set it all out; and the old man wondered much and went in and told his daughter. His daughter answered her father: as for the channel of water flowing into a skull and never filling it — in a later age there will be those who gather up the wealth and treasures of the whole world to supply one man and never be satisfied. As for the tree of ripe fruit which, when he wished to take and eat it, said take me, take me — in a later age people will seek the elder daughter for themselves and the younger daughter will say, why do you not ask for me, why do you not ask for me. As for the dog that came to meet him and knelt at length and licked his two feet while the pups in its belly howled and came forward to bite him — in a later age people will speak with one another with mouths like grease and oil and hearts like awls and knives, filling one another with words and plotting against one another within. All of these are for a later age and not for now.

And he married his daughter to the young man and so fulfilled their original intention (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 8).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three omens are given twice — once as the boy experiences them in terror on the road, and once as the girl decodes them — and the decoding is what the entry exists for. A channel of water running into a skull that never fills is the greed of a later age, gathering the world's wealth for one man. A ripe fruit that calls out take me, take me is the younger daughter watching a suitor ask for her elder sister. A dog that kneels and licks a guest's feet while the pups in its belly howl and try to bite him is the speech of a later age: mouths like grease and oil and hearts like awls and knives.

The girl closes with the line that gives the sequence its point: all of these are for a later age and not for now. She is reading omens about the world she is marrying into, not about her marriage.

26. A Great Clan's Two Sons: the Elder Loses the Money and Is Cherished, the Younger Brings It In and Is Punished

One son who loses six million and is consoled; one who brings in six million and is beaten three times. The reason is given in one line at the end.

Chinese Source

昔大姓有兩兒。各以二百萬與行賈。大子盡用樗蒲。衣被敗壞還以啟父。父言。得汝而已。用財何為。為作衣被飲食。善安慰之。小子來歸啟父。得二百萬利。父曰。取疏來。與共校計不合數千。便縛捶之。如是三行。收利六百萬。還輒得捶。大子三行失六百萬。止他國不歸。與無賴人相隨。語彼人言。我父有金銀白珠各一笥。在大床頭。汝歸白父道。我不愚失財物。未敢還。汝得便殺我父取物將來。可得共用。此人至其家與父相見。述其兒言。父聞啼泣曰。用物財為。我子何不來。食息悲咽。客言。君子不孝用錢作惡。一時都盡。令我來殺君取君金銀。我見君念子待遇我厚。令我心感。父言。小子尚癡便使迎之。謂子。汝去錢早歸。何因作癡語。更作衣被與之。師曰。小子償債。大子索債(出十卷譬喻經第六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago a great clan had two sons and gave each of them two million to go and trade. The elder son spent it all on dice, and his clothing was worn out, and he came back and told his father. His father said: it is enough that I have you; what are goods for? And he had clothing and food made for him and consoled him kindly.

The younger son came home and told his father: I have got two million in profit. His father said: bring the accounts. And going over them with him they were several thousand short, and he had him bound and beaten. This happened three times over: he brought in six million in profit and each time was beaten. The elder son three times lost six million, and he stayed abroad and did not come home.

He fell in with worthless men and said to one of them: my father has a chest of gold and silver and a chest of white pearls at the head of his great bed. Go back and tell my father that I am not stupid, that I have lost the goods and do not dare to come home yet. When you have your chance, kill my father and take the goods and bring them, and we can use them together.

This man came to the house and met the father and repeated what his son had said. The father heard and wept: what are goods for? Why does my son not come? And he could not eat for sobbing. The guest said: your son is unfilial and used the money for evil and it was all gone at once, and he sent me to kill you and take your gold and silver. Seeing how you long for your son and how generously you have treated me, my heart is moved.

The father said: my younger son is still a fool. And he sent for the elder son and said to him: you have gone through the money; come home early. Why did you speak so foolishly? And he had fresh clothing made for him.

The master said: the younger son was paying a debt; the elder son was collecting one (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 6).

27. Three Men Together Give the Saṅgha One Coin and in a Later Body Obtain Spontaneous Gold

One coin left over from a three-way split, given to a passing monk — and three men in whose hands, one lifetime later, stone, earth, and tile turn into gold, and back again for anyone else.

Chinese Source

昔有三人共賈行。各分得五百萬餘。有一錢欲與一人。則為不平。欲破分之。事為不然。時沙門分衛。三人共言布施沙門各言大佳。手共持與。沙門呪願。令汝今世後世並得其福。共生羅閱國中各得豪富。一人主山中採金。一人主耕田取金。一人主水中汲取金。為受前世布施之福。國王聞之。念我國人物皆我許。便將人兵詣山採之。金化作石。復至一家地。耕取金。金化為土。復至一家井中汲取金。金化作瓦。都不能得。王問佛言。此三人金計為我許。往取皆化不得是金。此人前世有何功德今致此福。佛具言之。非是王物。王不應取(出雜譬喻經第三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago three men went trading together and each got five million and there was one coin over. To give it to one of them would be unfair; to break and divide it was not to be done.

Just then a śramaṇa came begging, and the three said together: let us give it to the śramaṇa. Each said: excellent — and together they held it out and gave it, and the śramaṇa blessed them: may you obtain the merit of this in this life and the next.

They were born together in the kingdom of Rājagṛha and each became wealthy: one had charge of taking gold in the mountains, one of taking gold from a ploughed field, one of drawing gold from water — all as the recompense of their giving in a former life.

The king heard of it and thought: the people and goods of my kingdom are all mine. And he took soldiers and went into the mountains to mine it, and the gold turned into stone; he went to one man’s ground and ploughed for gold, and the gold turned into earth; he went to one man’s well and drew for gold, and the gold turned into tile. He could get none of it.

The king asked the Buddha: this gold of the three men — I reckon it is mine, and I went to take it and it all changed and I could not get it. What merit had these men in a former life, that they should have this blessing now? The Buddha told him all of it: it is not the king’s property, and the king ought not to take it (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra, fascicle 3).

28. A Poor Man Provides for a Monk and the Monk's Return Makes Him Rich

A bronze jar that gives whatever is asked, one instruction not to invite the king, and an urn of wood and stones.

Chinese Source

昔有貧家供養道人。一年便去。用一銅瓶乞主人言。此瓶是神打。此瓶口所索皆得。莫請國王。別後打瓶。家遂巨富。忘道人囑。遂請國王。王問富因。實而答王。王即奪瓶。家轉窮弊。方憶道人四出覓見。依實白言。道人曰。故須此瓶。乞君一塸盛以林石。齎詣王門求索瓶也。直到王門高聲索瓶。王聞大怒。遣數十人欲來捉之。開出林石風吹橫空。王使人身為此木石打破頭頟頃出千人。風勢所破死尸塞門。王大怖悸。求還其瓶。其人得瓶。家復大富。廣作功德。死得生天(出雜譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago a poor household provided for a religious man, and after a year he went away. He gave his host a bronze jar and said: this jar is struck by spirits; whatever you ask at this jar’s mouth you shall have — but do not invite the king.

After he had gone he struck the jar, and the household became enormously rich; and, forgetting the religious man’s charge, he invited the king. The king asked the cause of his wealth, and he answered the king truthfully, and the king took the jar at once; and the household grew poor and worn, and only then did he remember the religious man, and he went out in the four directions to look for him and found him, and told him truthfully.

The religious said: if you must have that jar back, I shall give you an urn; fill it with wood and stones and take it to the king’s gate and demand the jar. He went straight to the king’s gate and demanded the jar in a loud voice, and the king in great anger sent several tens of men to seize him; and he opened it and let out the wood and stones, and the wind blew them across the sky, and the king’s men were struck by this wood and stone and their heads and foreheads broken; and a thousand men came out and were broken by the force of the wind, and the corpses blocked the gate.

The king was greatly frightened and begged to give back the jar. The man got the jar, and his household became very rich again, and he made merit widely, and when he died was born in heaven (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra).

29. A Poor Man Finds a Buried Hoard and Is Punished by the King

The Buddha and Ānanda pass a washed-out hoard and call it a venomous snake. A reaper overhears, takes the treasure, and is tortured — and one remembered phrase saves him.

Chinese Source

佛與阿難入王舍城。時天大雨。水沃伏藏出諸寶物。佛言毒蛇。阿難云惡毒蛇。山下有刈麥人聞之念言。我未曾見沙門毒蛇。即往見物。以車與衣囊取著家內。現富貴相新作大舍。有嫉妬人白王。先有貧窮賤人卒見富相。是人必當得大寶藏。王即喚問。答言不得。即勅考治。是人憶佛與阿難名之為惡毒蛇也。誰得寶藏不語。一心念佛。王者作如是言。佛言毒蛇。阿難言惡毒蛇。王即喚問。是人答言。大王。若施無畏者我當說實。王許。其具陳上事。今於我身能作何等。必噉我命。王賞金錢五百。能於急中說於佛語及阿難語(出十誦律三誦第三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha and Ānanda went into the city of Rājagṛha, and there was a great rain, and the water washed out a buried hoard and treasures of every kind appeared. The Buddha said: a venomous snake. Ānanda said: an evil venomous snake.

Below the hill there was a man reaping barley who heard this and thought: I have never seen a śramaṇa’s venomous snake. And he went and saw the goods, and with a cart and cloth bags took them into his house; and he showed the signs of wealth and rank and built a great new house.

A jealous person told the king: there was a poor and lowly man before, and suddenly he shows the signs of wealth; this man must certainly have got a great hoard. The king summoned and questioned him, and he answered that he had not got it; and the king at once ordered him examined under torture.

This man remembered that the Buddha and Ānanda had called it an evil venomous snake. Whoever gets a hoard does not speak of it; with a single mind he thought of the Buddha, and said this: the Buddha said, a venomous snake; Ānanda said, an evil venomous snake.

The king summoned and questioned him, and the man answered: great king, if you grant me fearlessness I shall tell you the truth. The king allowed it, and he set out the whole matter above: what can it now do to me? It will surely devour my life. And the king rewarded him with five hundred gold coins, for having been able, in extremity, to speak the Buddha’s words and Ānanda’s words (Source: Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, third recitation, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The mechanism is a misunderstanding that becomes a rescue. The Buddha and Ānanda, passing a hoard washed out by rain, call it a venomous snake and an evil venomous snake — a warning, and one the reaper hears as a foolish monkish superstition: I have never seen a śramaṇa's venomous snake.

What saves him under torture is not repentance and not an argument but the memory of those exact words, which he repeats to the king; and the king's reward of five hundred gold coins is given, the text says, precisely for having been able to speak the Buddha's words and Ānanda's words in extremity.

30. A Poor Man Buys an Axe and Does Not Know It Is a Treasure

An old man chopping stumps with the finest treasure in the world, and a merchant raising his offer from a hundred bolts of silk to a thousand while the old man weeps.

Chinese Source

昔有貧寒孤獨老公。家無自業。遇市一斧。是眾寶之英。而不識之。持斫株賣之以供微命。用斧欲盡。會見外國治生大賈客。名曰薩薄。見斧識之。便問老公。賣此斧不。老公言。我仰此斧活不賣。薩薄復言。與公絹百匹何以不賣。公不應和。薩薄復言。與公二百匹。公便悵然不樂。薩薄復言。嫌少當益。公何以不樂。與五百匹。公便大哭。薩薄復云。絹少當益。何以啼哭。公言。我不恨絹少恨我愚癡。此斧本長尺半。破地以盡餘有五寸。猶得五百匹絹。以為恨耳。薩薄復言。勿有遺恨。今與公千匹絹。即便破券持去。薪火燒之盡成貴寶(出諸經中要事)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a poor, solitary old man with no livelihood of his own, who happened upon an axe in the market which was the finest of all treasures, and did not know it, and used it to chop stumps and sell them to keep himself barely alive; and the axe was nearly used up.

He happened to meet a great trading merchant from a foreign country, a caravan leader, who saw the axe and knew it, and asked the old man: will you sell this axe? The old man said: I live by this axe and shall not sell it.

The caravan leader said again: I shall give you a hundred bolts of silk — why not sell it? The old man made no reply. The caravan leader said again: I shall give you two hundred bolts. And the old man became downcast and unhappy. The caravan leader said again: if you think it too little I shall add to it — why are you unhappy? I shall give you five hundred bolts. And the old man wept aloud.

The caravan leader said again: if the silk is too little I shall add to it — why do you weep? The old man said: I do not resent that the silk is too little; I resent my own folly. This axe was originally a foot and a half long, and I have used it up chopping the ground and only five inches are left, and even so I get five hundred bolts of silk. That is what I regret.

The caravan leader said again: have no regrets; I shall give you a thousand bolts of silk now. And he broke the tally and took it away, and burned it in a fire, and it all became precious treasure (Source: Essential Matters from the Sūtras).

31. A Poor Old Couple Were Idle in All Three Seasons of Life

Two bent old people squatting over a rubbish fire, and the Buddha's arithmetic of the three portions of a life they each let pass.

Chinese Source

世尊晨朝入舍衛城。尊者阿難從。有二老夫婦。年耆根熟僂背如鉤。詣里巷頭燒糞掃處。俱蹲向火猶如老鵠。欲心相視。佛告阿難。見彼夫婦不。阿難曰。如是。佛曰。此二老夫婦。若於年少盛壯之時。勤求財物者得為舍衛城中第一富者。若出家學道精勤修習者得阿羅漢。於第二分盛壯之時。若勤求財物得為舍衛城中第二富者。若出家學道者得阿那含果。中年之時若勤求財物。得為舍衛城中第三富者。若出家學道得斯陀含果。乃於今日年耆根熟。無有錢財無有方便。不復堪能。苦覓錢財。亦不能得勝過人法。復為說偈言。不修梵行故　不得年少財思惟古昔事　眠地如曲弓不修於梵行　不得年少財猶如老鵠鳥　守死於空池## (出雜阿含第五卷第四十二卷又出三時過經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The World-Honored One went into the city of Śrāvastī in the early morning, and the venerable Ānanda followed him. There were two old people, husband and wife, aged and with their faculties ripe, their backs bent like hooks, who came to the head of the lane where the sweepings were burned, and squatted together facing the fire like old cranes, looking at one another with desire.

The Buddha told Ānanda: do you see that husband and wife? Ānanda said: I do.

The Buddha said: this old couple — if in their years of youth and strength they had diligently sought goods, they would have become the first richest in the city of Śrāvastī; and if they had gone forth to study the path and practiced diligently, they would have attained arhatship. In the second portion of their strength, if they had diligently sought goods they would have become the second richest in the city of Śrāvastī, and if they had gone forth and studied the path they would have obtained the fruit of a non-returner. In their middle years, if they had diligently sought goods they would have become the third richest in the city of Śrāvastī, and if they had gone forth and studied the path they would have obtained the fruit of a once-returner. And now today, aged and with their faculties ripe, without money and without means, they can no longer do it: they seek money bitterly and cannot get it either, nor any state beyond human.

And he spoke this verse besides:

“Because he did not practice the holy life, / he did not obtain wealth in his youth; / thinking on the affairs of long ago, / he sleeps on the ground like a bent bow. / Not practicing the holy life, / not obtaining wealth in his youth, / he is like an old crane / keeping watch over an empty pool until he dies.” (Source: Saṃyukta Āgama, fascicle 5 and fascicle 42; also the Sūtra of the Three Times Gone By)

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's arithmetic here is unusually specific and worth setting out as he gives it. Three portions of a life, and in each portion two doors: in youth, first richest in Śrāvastī or arhatship; in the second portion, second richest or non-returning; in middle age, third richest or once-returning. Both columns decline together, and by old age both are shut — they cannot get money either, nor any state beyond human.

What the scene is built on is the detail that they are looking at one another with desire while squatting over a rubbish fire, bent like hooks. The verse's image, of an old crane keeping watch over an empty pool until it dies, does the rest.

32. A Destitute Man Breaks His Word to a Tree-Spirit and Is Killed by the Tree's Own Branch

A man saved by a sandalwood tree-spirit through a winter, who names the tree for a reward — and is killed by its branch when the king's men come to cut it.

Chinese Source

維耶梨國有迦羅越。奉佛供養呪願畢。請聞法義。佛笑口光繞身三匝還從頂入。阿難問故。佛言。彼國有五百人。入海採寶置船步還。經歷深山日暮止宿。預嚴早發。四百九十九人皆去。一人臥熟。失輩。仍遇天雨雪。失去徑路。窮厄山中啼哭呼天。有旃檀樹神。謂窮人言。可止留此。自相給衣食。到春可去。窮人便留至于三月。啟樹神言。受恩得全身命。未有微報。顧有二親今在本土。實思得見。願垂發遣。樹神言善。以金餅施之。去此不遠。當得國邑。可得遷還至汝鄉里。窮人臨去問樹神言。此樹香潔世所希有。今當委遠願知其名。神言。不須問也。窮人復言。依蔭此樹積歷三月。今當遠離情懷恨恨。若到本國當宣揚樹恩。神便言。樹名栴檀。根莖枝葉治人百病。其香遠聞人所貪求不須道也。窮人至國。中外親戚喜相慶慰。後國王病頭痛。禱祀天地山水諸神不能消差。名醫省視唯得栴檀香病可得愈。王即募求民間無有。便宣令國中。得旃檀香者。拜封為侯妻以小女。時窮人聞償祿重。便言。我知栴檀香處。王便令近臣將窮人而往伐取。徑到樹所。使者見樹洪直。枝條茂盛華果煌煌。人所希見心不忍伐。不伐者則違王命。病不消愈。伐之者中心隱隱。踟蹰徘徊不知云何。樹神於空中言。便伐之。但置其根耳。伐竟以人血塗之。肝腸覆其上。樹自當生。還復如故。使者聞神言如此。便令人伐之。窮人住在樹邊。樹彈地枝摽殺之。使者共議。屠割窮人取其肝血。如神所勅樹即更生。車載所伐樹以還國中。醫即進藥。王病得愈。舉國歡喜。王命國中人民。其有病者皆詣宮門。王出香藥給之。病皆得愈。王身康豫黎民無病。舉國欣慶遂致太平(出栴檀樹經也)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the kingdom of Vaiśālī there was a householder who served the Buddha and made offerings; and when the blessing was finished he asked to hear the meaning of the Dharma. The Buddha smiled and the light of his mouth circled his body three times and returned in at the crown of his head; and Ānanda asked the reason.

The Buddha said: in that country there were five hundred men who went to sea to gather treasure and set their ship down and walked back. Passing through deep mountains, the day grew dark and they stopped for the night, and made ready to set out early; and four hundred and ninety-nine of them went, and one was fast asleep and lost his company. Then he met with rain and snow from the sky and lost the road, and, in extremity in the mountains, he wept and cried out to heaven.

There was a sandalwood tree-spirit who said to the poor man: you may stay here, and I shall supply you with clothing and food; when spring comes you may go. So the poor man stayed three months, and then said to the tree-spirit: by your kindness I have kept my life whole and have not yet made the least return. I have two parents now in my native land, and I truly long to see them; I ask you to send me off.

The tree-spirit said: very well — and gave him a cake of gold: not far from here you will come to a city, and from there you can go back to your own village.

As the poor man was about to leave he asked the tree-spirit: this tree is fragrant and pure and rare in the world; I am now going far away and should like to know its name. The spirit said: you need not ask.

The poor man said again: I have sheltered under this tree three months and am now going far away, and my feeling is full of regret. If I reach my own country I shall proclaim the tree’s kindness. Then the spirit said: the tree is named sandalwood; its root, stem, branches, and leaves cure a hundred human ailments, and its fragrance is smelled from afar and men covet it — there is no need to speak of it.

The poor man reached his country, and his kin within and without rejoiced and congratulated him.

Afterward the king of that country fell ill with a headache, and prayed and sacrificed to heaven and earth and to the spirits of mountains and waters and it could not be removed; and famous physicians examined him and said that only sandalwood incense could cure the illness. The king sought it and there was none among the people, and he proclaimed throughout the kingdom: whoever can get sandalwood incense shall be ennobled as a marquis and given the king’s youngest daughter to wife.

Then the poor man heard that the reward was heavy and said: I know where sandalwood incense is. The king then had a close minister take the poor man and go and cut it, and they came straight to the tree.

The messenger saw the tree tall and straight, its branches lush, its flowers and fruit shining, such as men rarely see, and could not bear in his heart to cut it; and not to cut it was to go against the king’s command and the illness would not be removed, and to cut it made his heart uneasy; and he hesitated and went to and fro and did not know what to do.

The tree-spirit said from the air: cut it, only leave the root. When you have cut it, smear it with human blood and cover it over with liver and entrails, and the tree will grow again of itself and be restored as before.

The messenger, hearing the spirit speak so, then had men cut it. The poor man was standing beside the tree, and as the tree sprang up a branch struck and killed him. The messengers conferred, and butchered the poor man and took his liver and blood; and as the spirit had instructed, the tree grew again at once.

They carted the cut tree back into the kingdom, and the physicians gave the medicine and the king’s illness was cured, and the whole kingdom rejoiced. The king ordered the people of the kingdom: whoever is ill may come to the palace gate; and the king brought out the fragrant medicine and gave it to them, and all the illnesses were cured. The king’s own person was in good health and the people were without sickness, and the whole kingdom was glad and it came to great peace (Source: Sūtra of the Sandalwood Tree).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The cruelty of this entry is structural and the text does not soften it. The tree-spirit shelters the man for three months, feeds and clothes him, gives him gold, and warns him off asking its name — you need not ask — and gives it only when he insists that he wants to proclaim the tree's kindness in his own country. He does proclaim it, for a marquisate and a princess.

Note what the tree-spirit does when the axemen arrive and hesitate. It tells them to cut, gives them the method of restoring the tree — leave the root, smear it with human blood, cover it with liver and entrails — and does not say whose. The branch kills the man who named it as the tree springs up, and the messengers use him for the prescription. The whole kingdom's sickness is then cured with the wood.

The story is presented as the Buddha's explanation of his own smile, and the frame closes on the kingdom's peace without any comment on the man.

33. A Man Pursued by an Elephant Falls into a Deep Valley, and Heaven Sends Down Sweet Dew and He Rises to Heaven

The man in the pit: an elephant above, spears below, two rats gnawing the root, three snakes, and gadflies at his eyes. Then five drops.

Chinese Source

昔有人行空澤中。見一黑象。人念。此象必來害我。我當殺之。象亦念言。人必殺我。我當危之。人便捨去。象從後逐。前走數里墮一深谷。谷絕無底。即於岸邊捉持樹根。其形如指。尋根而下。懸在岸邊。象於谷上以鼻撈之。欲及不及。下向見底但是矛[金*卓*戈]復有兩鼠共嚙樹根。又三黑蛇出頭欲囓。復有蚊虻來螫其眼。其人念曰。今日死矣。仰天求救。聲哀情至。天降甘露渧其口。始得一渧二鼠去。得二渧毒蛇捨之。得三渧黑象自還。得四渧蚊虻除。得五渧深谷自平出在平地。天為化導將還天上(出譬喻經第七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man travelling in an empty marsh who saw a black elephant. The man thought: this elephant will certainly come and harm me; I ought to kill it. And the elephant thought: this man will certainly kill me; I ought to make away with him.

The man left and went away, and the elephant pursued him from behind; and running forward some li he fell into a deep valley. The valley was sheer and bottomless, and at the edge he caught hold of a tree root the size of a finger, and following the root down he hung at the edge of the cliff. The elephant above the valley reached for him with its trunk, almost reaching him and not reaching him.

Looking down he saw at the bottom nothing but spears and halberds; and there were two rats gnawing the tree root together; and three black snakes put out their heads meaning to bite him; and mosquitoes and gadflies came and stung his eyes.

The man thought: today I die. And he looked up to heaven and begged to be saved, his voice mournful and his feeling wholly sincere; and heaven let fall sweet dew that dripped into his mouth. At the first drop the two rats went away; at the second the venomous snakes let him be; at the third the black elephant went off of itself; at the fourth the mosquitoes and gadflies were gone; and at the fifth the deep valley levelled itself and he came out onto level ground, and heaven transformed and guided him and took him up to heaven (Source: Parables Sūtra, fascicle 7).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the celebrated parable of the man in the pit, one of the most widely repeated images in Buddhist literature: the elephant above, the bottomless valley below set with spears, the finger-thick root he hangs from, two rats gnawing it through, three snakes reaching for him, and gadflies at his eyes.

The usual telling of this parable ends there, with the man licking honey that drips into his mouth and forgetting everything else — the point being the absurdity of the pleasure. This version inverts it completely. The five drops of sweet dew are an answer to a prayer, and they undo the predicament one term at a time: the rats leave, the snakes let go, the elephant departs, the gadflies are gone, and the valley levels itself. It is worth reading against the more familiar version rather than in place of it.

36. A Cowherd Boy Picks Flowers for the Buddha, Is Gored by Cattle and Dies, and Is at Once Born in Heaven

A boy carrying lotus flowers to a stūpa, gored on the road, and what he does first on arriving in heaven.

A cowherd boy went into the pool and gathered them, and, with a vessel full of lotus flowers, came out at the mountain’s edge meaning to offer them to the Buddha; and before he reached the monastery he met a herd of cattle on the road, and they gored and killed the boy. His spirit was born at once in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, with a spontaneous palace and lotus flowers of five colors.

Just born among the gods, he thought to himself: my palace alone has lotus flowers of an unusual color. But it is the law of the gods first to examine the causes of one’s past lives and only afterward to enjoy the blessing. He then saw the gods who had come before, who were saying to one another: this god has newly come and we are enjoying ourselves together — why is he so alarmed and uneasy? And so I now pluck flowers and mean to go to the former stūpa and scatter them over the Buddha and bow, and complete my original vow.

Then five hundred gods held lotus flowers and followed him and scattered them over the Buddha’s stūpa; and by the merit of this, when Maitreya comes down they will attain the path together (Source: Parables Sūtra, fascicle 5).

37. A Boy Who Gave Three Coins in a Former Life Now Understands Birds and Becomes King

昔有一人。用三錢布施。乞求三願。一者將來得作國王。二者解眾生語。三者多諸智慧。其人命終生庶人家。形色端正。王募為左右。此兒投募得侍王側。見燕在巢。仰首看而笑。王問何笑。答曰。燕言。我得龍女髮長十丈喚伴看之。王曰。審爾者好。無此者殺。遣看即得。王欲取女為婦。語小兒言。汝解鳥語必應多策。給汝食糧。覓此女人。得者重報。若不得殺汝及家口。小兒冐死向東海邊。見二人共諍隱形帽履水靴殺活杖。小兒曰。何須云云。我放一箭君二人逐。先前得者與三種物。答曰善。引弓放箭。二人爭走。小兒取帽著靴捉杖。直入海中至龍所。脫隱形帽令龍女見。女人多欲。遂與小兒持一䴵金還至外國。其王遣迎。勅女獨入。女便前進。小兒戴隱形帽隨女而入。女見王醜。以金擲王頟破命終。小兒脫帽。共女上殿。高聲唱言。我應為王女為皇后。覇王天下(出雜譬喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a man who gave three coins in alms and begged three wishes: first, to become a king in a life to come; second, to understand the speech of living beings; third, to have much wisdom.

When his life ended he was born into a commoner’s household, and his form and color were handsome, and the king took men into his service and this boy answered the call and attended at the king’s side.

He saw a swallow in its nest and looked up at it and laughed, and the king asked what he was laughing at. He answered: the swallow said, I have got a nāga maiden’s hair ten fathoms long, and called its mate to come and see it. The king said: if it is truly so, well and good; if there is no such thing, you die. He sent men to look and it was found.

The king wished to take the woman for a wife, and said to the boy: you understand the speech of birds and must have many devices. I shall give you provisions; find this woman for me. If you get her the reward is heavy, and if you do not you and your household die.

The boy braved death and went toward the eastern sea, and saw two men quarrelling over a cap of invisibility, boots for walking on water, and a staff that kills and revives. The boy said: what need is there of all this talk? I shall loose one arrow and you two run after it, and whoever reaches it first shall have the three things. They said: very good.

He drew the bow and loosed the arrow, and the two ran to contend for it; and the boy took the cap and put on the boots and took the staff, and went straight into the sea and came to the nāgas. He took off the cap of invisibility and let the nāga maiden see him; and the woman was full of desire, and went with the boy, taking a cake of gold, and came back to a foreign country.

That king sent to receive her and ordered that the woman come in alone. The woman went forward, and the boy, wearing the cap of invisibility, went in behind her; and the woman, seeing that the king was ugly, threw the gold at the king’s forehead and broke it, and he died. The boy took off the cap and went up into the hall with the woman and proclaimed in a loud voice: I am to be king and the woman is to be queen. And he ruled the world (Source: Miscellaneous Parables Sūtra).

38. Robbers Dividing the Spoil Do Not Recognize the Good One

Robbers who divide the spoil and give the ugly wrapper to the man they were going to cut out.

Chinese Source

昔者眾商人。經由嶮道值劫。大失衣物。中有一衣。是鹿胎毛細軟滑澤織持作衣。其價百倍。而色紫黑不悅人眼。劫不賞別用持作帊。以盛麁衣他處共分。各取雜物謂是奇好。餘此一帊未展分張。劫群中有困弱人。獨不與分。苦論共以帊乞。即自賣之。時大貴人知是好物。依限雇直。比於餘劫所得等分。諸劫聞之大生恥惱(出百句譬喻經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago a company of merchants, passing through a dangerous road, met with robbers and lost a great deal of clothing. Among the clothes there was one made of the fine, soft, smooth wool of a deer’s foetus, woven and made up into a garment worth a hundred times the rest; but its color was dark purple and not pleasing to the eye.

The robbers did not appreciate it and used it as a wrapper to hold the coarse clothing, and elsewhere they divided the rest, each taking assorted goods which they supposed were the fine ones; and this one wrapper alone was left over and not spread out and divided.

Among the band of robbers there was one weak and hard-pressed man who alone was given no share, and after much arguing they let him have the wrapper. He sold it at once, and a great and eminent man of the time knew it was a fine thing and paid the price he asked; and it came to as much as all the shares the other robbers had got. The robbers heard of it and were greatly ashamed and vexed (Source: Sūtra of the Hundred Parables, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A parable about appraisal, and its structure is worth marking. The robbers make two errors and they are the same error: they cannot tell a fine dark-purple deer's-wool garment from a wrapper, and they cannot tell that the weak man they were cutting out of the split was being handed the whole value of the haul. Both are failures to look past the surface of a thing to what it is.

This closes fascicle 44, the first half of the section on ordinary men. Its thirty-eight entries are the compendium at its most miscellaneous — vinaya, Āgama, and half a dozen collections of parables that survive only in Chinese — and the range runs from the man in the pit and the arithmetic of a wasted life to a wooden woman serving wine and a wager over a leftover cake.
