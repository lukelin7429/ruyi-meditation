Bodhisattvas' Own Practice

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 8 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Bodhisattvas' Own Practice, Part 1" (自行菩薩部第一) — twenty entries on what bodhisattva practice actually costs, including Sadāprarudita selling his own flesh to hear the Perfection of Wisdom, Bhaiṣajyarāja's self-immolation, the ascetic Kṣāntivādin's dismemberment by a cruel king, Aṅgulimāla's own origin as a devoted student turned murderer, and the Snow Mountain ascetic who traded his body for the second half of a single verse on impermanence.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 8 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 8 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 8 opens a new topical section — bodhisattvas' own conduct, as distinct from the Buddha's own biography — with twenty entries, most of them narrative, a few doctrinal explanations of why bodhisattvas practice as they do. Given the number of short, self-contained pieces, entries are grouped here into thematic clusters that roughly track the compendium's own sequence, while keeping every entry's full text intact.

1–2. Sadāprarudita and Bhaiṣajyarāja: Bodies Given for the Dharma

Sadāprarudita cuts into his own arm and thigh to sell his heart's blood and marrow for the price of hearing the Perfection of Wisdom — only to discover the buyer was Śakra testing his resolve; Bhaiṣajyarāja, in an earlier life, burns his own body for twelve hundred years, then burns off both arms besides, as an offering exceeding every material gift he could otherwise make.

Chinese Source
薩陀波崙為欲聞法賣心血髓

薩陀波崙菩薩在大雷音佛所行菩薩道。本求般若波羅蜜時。不惜身命不求名利。空中有言而戒誨之。我當處處求般若波羅蜜。即憂愁啼哭經過七日。身體疲極乃至飢渴寒熱。空中有佛而語之言。善男子過去諸佛行菩薩道求聞般若波羅蜜。如汝今日以是勤精進愛樂法故。供養般若波羅蜜。曇無竭菩薩於此座上說般若波羅蜜。若有受持讀誦如說行者。汝從今者莫問晝夜不久當得聞般若波羅蜜。我今貧無有物可以供養般若波羅蜜及說法師。不應空往。我當賣身得財為般若波羅蜜故并供養法師。我世世喪身無數。無始生死中或死或賣。或為貪欲世世在地獄中受無量苦。未曾為清淨法及供養法師故喪身命。即入一大城至市肆上。高聲唱言。誰欲須人誰欲須人。時惡魔作是念。薩陀波崙豈念法故欲自賣身。我今當壞之。魔蔽諸人民令不聞其聲。自賣身不售甚自憂愁。釋提桓因化作婆羅門語言。我今欲祠天須人心人血人髓。汝能與不。答言。我今得大善利。即執刀刺其左臂出血割右髀肉欲破骨出髓。時一長者女在閣上遙見即下問菩薩。何故苦困自身用心血髓欲作何等。答曰。賣與婆羅門為般若波羅蜜供養曇無竭菩薩。女問得何功德。答曰。是人善學般若方便力。我學是法能得無上道。為眾生作依止。得金色身具諸功德。分布是利與一切眾生等功德利。女曰微妙難值為是功德。應捨如恒河沙身。汝有何須盡當相與。我亦欲往曇無竭所共殖善根。釋提桓因即復本身。讚言。善哉男子。諸過去佛行菩薩道亦復如是求。我實不用人心血髓。但來相試。汝欲何求。我當相與。願我是身平復如故。到長者女家。女之父母與眾妙華香及諸瓔珞塗香燒香幡蓋衣服七寶伎樂。女與侍人共往供養經及曇無竭。曇無竭說般若波羅蜜及方便力。薩陀波崙求水灑地而不能得。即自刺身以血灑地令無塵土來依大師(出大品經第三十卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation
Sadāprarudita Sells His Own Flesh

The bodhisattva Sadāprarudita, practicing the bodhisattva path under the Buddha of Great Thunder-Sound, sought the Perfection of Wisdom without regard for his own life. Grieving and weeping for seven days, exhausted with hunger and cold, he heard a buddha's own voice from the sky tell him that the bodhisattva Dharmodgata was expounding the Perfection of Wisdom nearby — he should go there without delay. Reflecting that he was too poor to properly honor this teaching, and having lost his body countless times before for the sake of mere desire without ever once giving it up for the pure Dharma, he went into a city and called out in the marketplace, "Who wishes to buy a person?" But the wicked Māra caused the people there to be unable to hear his voice, and, unable to sell himself, he grew despondent. Śakra, transforming into a brahmin, then asked whether he could provide a human heart, blood, and marrow for a sacrifice — and Sadāprarudita, overjoyed at the opportunity, took up a knife and began cutting into his own arm and thigh. An elder's daughter, watching from a balcony, came down and, on hearing his purpose, declared such merit worth giving up bodies numerous as the sands of the Ganges for, and offered him everything he needed instead, wishing to plant the same roots of goodness herself. At this, Śakra resumed his true form, revealing he had never truly needed human flesh at all — it had only been a test — and restored Sadāprarudita's body to wholeness. Together with the elder's daughter's whole household, laden with every fine offering, Sadāprarudita went to hear Dharmodgata's teaching; finding no water to sprinkle the ground before the teacher out of respect, he simply cut his own body again and used his own blood to settle the dust instead (Source: Larger Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra, fascicle 30).

藥王今身捨臂先世燒形

宿王華菩薩白言。世尊藥王云何遊此娑婆世界。有百萬億那由他難行苦行。願少解說。佛言。過去恒河沙劫有佛號日月淨明德。彼國無有女人。喜見菩薩於此佛法精進經行一心求佛。滿二萬歲得現一切色身三昧。皆是聞法華經力。於虛空中雨曼陀羅華及海此岸栴檀之香。以天衣纏身灌以香油而自燃身。光明遍照八十億恒河沙世界。是真精進真法供養。其身火燃千二百歲過是已後其身乃盡。一切眾生喜見菩薩法供養已。化生此國淨德王家。為父說偈。

我先經行處　已得現身定
勤行大精進　捨所愛之身

日月淨明德佛今故現在。即當還彼供養此佛。坐七寶臺上昇虛空。往到佛所合掌讚佛。

容顏甚奇妙　光明照十方
我適曾供養　今復還親覲

佛告喜見。我滅盡時至。今夜涅槃。我法囑汝舍利相付當令流布廣設供養。喜見即以海此岸栴檀[卄/積]而燒之。收取舍利作八萬四千寶瓶。起八萬四千塔。雖是供養心猶未足。即於八萬四千塔前。燃百福莊嚴臂七萬二千歲。而以供養。無數求聲聞眾無量人發菩提心。皆得住現一切色身三昧。諸菩薩天人等見其無臂憂惱悲哀而作是言。一切眾生喜見菩薩是我等大師教化我者今不具足。喜見菩薩於大眾中誓言。我捨兩臂必當得佛。若實不虛令臂還復。當時三千世界六種震動天雨寶華。時其兩臂平復如故。佛告宿王華菩薩。時喜見者今藥王是。若有發心欲得無上菩提道者。能燃手指乃至足一指供養佛塔。勝以國城妻子及三千國土山林河池諸珍寶物而以供養佛及菩薩辟支阿羅漢等(出法花經第六卷)。

Bhaiṣajyarāja's Self-Immolation

The bodhisattva Nakṣatrarāja asked the Buddha to explain Bhaiṣajyarāja's countless past austerities. The Buddha said: long ago, under the Buddha Candrasūryavimalaprabhāsaśrī, the bodhisattva Sarvasattvapriyadarśana practiced with such diligence that, through the power of hearing the Lotus Sūtra, he attained the samādhi of manifesting every form of body. Wrapping his own body in heavenly cloth and pouring fragrant oil over it, he set himself alight, his light illuminating eighty koṭis of worlds — a true offering of true diligence. His body burned for twelve hundred years before being consumed, and, reborn afterward into a royal household through this very merit, he spoke a verse to his own father:

"In the very place where I once walked in meditation, I attained the samādhi of manifesting my own body;
through diligent, great effort, I gave up the body I loved."

Learning that same Buddha still lived, he returned to attend upon him, rising into the sky on a jeweled platform and praising him:

"Your countenance is wondrous beyond compare, your light illuminating the ten directions;
having once made offerings to you, I now return again to attend upon you."

When that Buddha's own nirvāṇa came, he entrusted his relics to Sarvasattvapriyadarśana, who cremated the body, raised eighty-four thousand stūpas from the relics — yet, still unsatisfied, burned his own two arms before these stūpas for seventy-two thousand years as a further offering. When the grieving assembly lamented their teacher's own incompleteness, he vowed that, having given up both arms, he would surely attain buddhahood — and if this vow were true, his arms would be restored. At once the world shook and his arms were restored exactly as before. The Buddha revealed that Sarvasattvapriyadarśana was Bhaiṣajyarāja himself, and that anyone who burns even a single finger as an offering to a Buddha's stūpa surpasses in merit one who offers an entire kingdom and every treasure of the world to the buddhas, bodhisattvas, and arhats (Source: Lotus Sūtra, fascicle 6).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both stories end by revealing the extreme sacrifice was, in one sense, unnecessary — Śakra never needed Sadāprarudita's marrow, and Bhaiṣajyarāja's arms are restored the moment his vow proves sincere. The compendium is not recommending self-harm as such; it is using the willingness to follow through on an extreme offering, all the way to the point of actual bodily harm, as the test that separates genuine resolve from a merely stated one. What is praised is the resolve proven under a real test, not the injury itself, which in both cases the narrative goes out of its way to undo.

3. Pure-Storehouse and Pure-Eye Convert Their Parents

Two young bodhisattva sons win their father away from heterodox teaching not through argument but through a display of the eighteen transformations — after which the whole royal family, forty-two thousand strong, follows the Buddha into the monastic life.

Chinese Source

乃往過去有佛。名雲雷音宿王華智。有王名妙莊嚴。其王夫人名曰淨德。有二子。一名淨藏。二名淨眼。是二子者有大神力。福德智慧修菩薩道。佛欲引導妙莊嚴王愍念眾生說是法華經。時淨藏淨眼白母言。願母往詣雲雷音宿王華智佛。我等亦當侍從供養禮拜。母告子言。汝父信受外道深著婆羅門法。汝等應往白父與共俱去。母又告言。汝等當念父為見神變。於是二子踊在虛空高七多羅樹現十八變。父見神力心大歡喜合掌向子言。汝等師為是誰。二子白言。雲雷音宿王華智佛今在七寶菩提樹下法座上坐說法華經。是我等師。父語子言。我今欲見。二子從空中下白母父王今已信解發菩提心。我等為父已作佛事。願母見聽。於彼佛所出家修道。母言聽汝。佛難值故。於是二子白父母言。願時往詣佛所親覲供養。於是妙莊嚴王與群臣眷屬。淨德夫人與後宮婇女并其二子四萬二千人。一時詣佛。佛為說法示教利喜。王大歡悅。時王及夫人。即解頸真珠瓔珞以散佛上。於虛空中化成四柱寶臺。中有大寶床敷百千萬天衣。其上有佛結跏趺坐放大光明。時雲雷音宿王華智佛告四眾言。汝等見是妙莊嚴王於我前合掌立不。此王於我法中當作比丘精勤修習助佛道法當得作佛號娑羅樹王。國名大光劫名大高王。其王即時以國付弟。王與夫人二子并諸眷屬於佛法中出家修道。王出家已於八萬四千歲常精進修行妙法華經。過是已後得一切淨功德莊嚴三昧。即昇虛空高七多羅樹。妙莊嚴王今華德菩薩是。淨德夫人光照莊嚴相菩薩是。其二子者今藥王藥上菩薩是(出法華經第七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, under the Buddha Jaladharagarjitaghoṣasusvaranakṣatrarājasaṃkusumitābhijña, King Śubhavyūha's consort Vimaladatta bore two sons, Vimalagarbha and Vimalanetra, both cultivating the bodhisattva path with great supernormal power. Wishing to convert King Śubhavyūha, who held to heterodox brahminical teaching, the two sons asked their mother's permission to hear this Buddha, and, at her suggestion, first displayed the eighteen transformations before their father, rising into the sky seven palm-trees high. Delighted, the king asked who their teacher was, and, learning it was this Buddha now teaching the Lotus Sūtra nearby, expressed his own wish to see him. The sons, having thus already accomplished their father's own conversion, then asked their mother's permission to go forth themselves; she consented, given how rare a buddha's own appearance in the world is. The whole royal household — the king, his ministers, the queen, her attendants, and forty-two thousand people in all — then went together to the Buddha, whose teaching so moved the king and queen that they scattered their own pearl necklaces in offering, which transformed in the sky into a jeweled platform bearing the Buddha himself, radiant and seated. The Buddha then revealed that King Śubhavyūha, having joined the monastic order, would himself become a buddha in time — and the king at once ceded his throne to his own brother, and, with his queen and sons, went forth to cultivate the Lotus Sūtra for eighty-four thousand years, attaining the samādhi of adornment with every pure merit. The Buddha revealed that King Śubhavyūha is now the bodhisattva Padmaśrī, Queen Vimaladatta is the bodhisattva Prabhāsagarbha, and the two sons are none other than Bhaiṣajyarāja and Bhaiṣajyasamudgata themselves (Source: Lotus Sūtra, fascicle 7).

4, 13. Kṣāntivādin's Dismemberment, and Why Bodhisattvas Don't Grieve

An ascetic named "Forbearance" is dismembered limb by limb by a cruel king for the sole crime of silence, forgiving him before the pain even ends — paired here with the compendium's own doctrinal explanation of how any bodhisattva manages such equanimity at all.

Chinese Source
羼提和山居遇於國王之所割截

昔者菩薩時為梵志名羼提和山。居樹下飲食果泉內垢消盡弘明六通得盡知之智。名香普薰聞八方上下。聖凡咨嗟擁護其國。風雨從時五穀豐熟災毒消滅。王名迦黎。入山畋獵馳逐麋鹿。尋其足迹歷菩薩前過。王以問之。菩薩默然。王曰。當死乞人吾一國之尊。問不時對獸迹歷茲。而佯低頭我勢能戮爾。菩薩曰。吾聽王耳。曰爾為誰乎。曰吾忍辱人。王怒拔劍截其右臂。菩薩曰。吾志無上道與時無諍。斯王尚加吾忍豈況黎庶乎。願吾得佛必先度之。不令眾生効其為惡也。王曰。君為誰。答曰。吾忍辱人也。又截其左手乃至兩脚耳鼻。血若流泉其痛無量。天地震動日月無明。四天大王僉然俱臻同聲恚言。斯王酷烈。謂道士曰。吾等誅之及其妻子。并滅一國以彰其惡。道士答曰。斯何言乎。此由吾前世不奉佛教加毒乎彼。為惡禍追由影繫形矣。黎民覩變馳詣首過齊聲而曰。道士處茲禳災滅度。愚君不識臧否不知去就。惡加元聖。唯願聖人無以吾等報上帝矣。菩薩答曰。王以兇酷見加吾心愍之猶母之念赤子。黎庶何過不假有疑。菩薩有弟處在異山。以天眼視。時天神龍會議王惡靡不懷忿。懼兄有損德之心。以神足力來至兄所。取斷手足耳鼻著其故處。弟還續之即復如本。兄曰。吾普慈之信于今著矣。天神地祇靡不悲喜。稽首稱善更相勸導受誡而退。羼提惒者吾身是。弟者彌勒是。王者拘隣是也(出度無極集第五卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation
Kṣāntivādin's Dismemberment

Long ago, as a bodhisattva, I lived as a brahmin ascetic named Kṣāntivādin, dwelling beneath a tree, my inner defilements purified, possessed of the six supernormal powers, revered by sages and ordinary people alike. A king named Kali, hunting in the mountains, came upon me and, receiving only silence when he questioned me, grew enraged, threatening to kill me for my insolence. I told him only that I had heard him, and, asked who I was, answered simply, "a man of forbearance." Furious, the king cut off my right arm with his sword. I said that my resolve toward the unsurpassed path admitted no quarrel with the times, and vowed that once I attained buddhahood, I would deliver this very king first, so that no being might follow his example. Asked again who I was, I gave the same answer, and the king cut off my left hand, then both legs, my ears, and my nose, my pain immeasurable, as heaven and earth shook and the sun and moon lost their light. The Four Heavenly Kings, outraged, wished to destroy the king and his entire country — but I told them this was in truth the fruit of my own past failure, following me like a shadow follows a form, and that the people themselves bore no blame. My own younger brother, observing all this from another mountain with his heavenly eye, came by his own supernormal power and restored my severed limbs exactly as they had been. I declared my universal compassion now proven true, and the devas and earth-spirits, moved to both grief and joy, bowed in praise. I was that Kṣāntivādin; my brother was Maitreya; and the king was Kauṇḍinya (Source: Collection on the Perfections, fascicle 5).

久修忍辱割截不憂

問曰。菩薩身非木石。云何眾生來割截之不生異心。答曰。有人言。菩薩久修羼提波羅蜜故能不愁惱。如羼提仙人被截手足血皆為乳。有人言。菩薩無量世來深修大慈故雖有割截亦不愁憂。譬如草木無有瞋心。有人言。菩薩深修般若波羅蜜。轉身得般若波羅蜜果報。空心故了了知空。割截身時心亦不動。如外物不動內亦如是。般若果報故於諸法中無所分別。有人言。是菩薩非生死身。是出三界法性生身。住無漏聖心果報中故。身如木石而能慈念割截者。是菩薩能生如是心故。割截劫奪內外法時。其心不動。是為菩薩希有之法。我以佛眼見十方如恒河沙等世界中。菩薩入地獄中。令火滅湯冷。以三事教化眾生。於無量阿僧祇劫。深行慈心外物給施。意猶不滿。以身布施爾乃足滿。如藥王菩薩外物珍寶供養於佛。意猶不滿以身為燈爾乃足滿。外物雖多不以為恩。所以者何。非所重故。得其身時乃能驚感。是故身施(出大智論第八十卷)。

Why Bodhisattvas Don't Grieve

Question: a bodhisattva's body is not wood or stone — how can he remain unmoved when beings dismember him? Answer: some say it is his long cultivation of forbearance itself, like Kṣāntivādin whose severed limbs bled milk instead of blood. Others say it is his deep, lifelong cultivation of compassion, so that, like grass or trees, he feels no anger at all. Others say it is his cultivation of the Perfection of Wisdom itself, his mind so thoroughly emptied that external and internal are equally unmoved. Others say his body is not truly a body of birth and death at all, but one born of the very nature of dharmas beyond the three realms — a body like wood or stone that can nonetheless feel compassion even for its own destroyer. With the Buddha's own eye I see bodhisattvas throughout countless worlds entering hell itself to cool its flames, teaching beings across immeasurable kalpas — giving away external things, yet still unsatisfied until the body itself is given, for external things, however many, inspire no true gratitude in others, while the giving of one's own body alone truly moves them (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 80).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The compendium's own doctrinal answer refuses to settle on a single explanation, offering four competing accounts of the bodhisattva's equanimity side by side without adjudicating between them — forbearance as habit, compassion as nature, wisdom as insight, and a body no longer subject to ordinary birth and death at all. Read against Kṣāntivādin's own story, this plurality suggests the compendium's compilers were less interested in settling the metaphysics than in establishing, through repetition across many narratives, that such equanimity is achievable at all — the theory follows the practice, not the reverse.

Śraddhā's Crossing of the Mud

The bodhisattva Śraddhā, seeking the true Dharma in an age without it, heard a voice from the sky tell him of a woman, ten thousand yojanas to the east, of the lowest birth and hideous appearance, who alone still recalled half a single verse of a vanished Buddha's teaching — but between here and there lay a vast swamp of mud, ten thousand li across, so treacherous that not even a bird's flight could cross it. She told me she knew only a single half-verse:

"Commit no evil whatsoever; uphold and practice every good."

Hearing this, my body and mind grew calm, my faculties settling of themselves; reflecting on its meaning, I came to understand it fully and gained supernormal power, flying home to proclaim it everywhere. Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two stories make an instructive pair precisely because they end so differently. Sadāprarudita's vision dissolves the moment he tries to hold onto it, leaving him only with directions to a further destination he must still travel to reach — the Dharma, in his story, keeps receding. Śraddhā's half-verse, by contrast, is small enough to actually keep: eight characters, fully absorbed, that he carries home and never loses. The compendium seems to be distinguishing between the grandeur of a vision and the durability of a teaching small enough to be genuinely internalized — the second, however modest, is what actually survives the journey home.

Tiṣya and Nārada's Quarrel

Two ascetic bodhisattvas, dwelling in a rock cave for over sixty years, revered even by devas and nāgas, once quarreled after Nārada accidentally stepped on the sleeping Tiṣya's head in the dark; Tiṣya vowed to split Nārada's own head into seven pieces when the sun rose, and Nārada, provoked, vowed instead to stop the sun from rising at all to test him. The sun did not rise for five days, plunging the whole kingdom into darkness and panic, until the king, advised that two quarreling mountain ascetics were responsible, went himself to kneel before them and confess that the fault lay with him, not his innocent people. Once Tiṣya's own anger was appeased, he smeared his head with mud and let the sun rise, at which the mud-covered head harmlessly split into seven pieces as promised, leaving him unharmed. The two ascetics then taught the king to govern with boundless compassion and the ten good deeds, which the whole kingdom adopted as law. The Buddha revealed that Nārada was himself, and Tiṣya was Maitreya (Source: Collection on the Perfections, fascicle 7).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both stories resolve through the same mechanism: a third party's total, undeserved humility — a hermit accepting his own damnation to spare a stranger, a king kneeling to take the blame for a dispute he had no part in — dissolves a conflict neither of its own principals could resolve through their own logic or willpower alone. Notably, neither ascetic in the second story is shown as simply wrong: Tiṣya's threat and Nārada's retaliation are both allowed to stand as genuinely disproportionate, and it takes an outside act of grace, not a superior argument, to end the standoff.

The Snow Mountain Ascetic's Half-Verse

Good son, long ago before any buddha's own sun had risen in the world, I was a brahmin practicing the bodhisattva path, versed in every heterodox teaching, yet I had never even heard the name of the Mahāyāna's own scriptures. Dwelling in the Himalayas in meditation for countless years, I one day encountered Śakra, transformed into a terrifying rākṣasa, who spoke half a verse once uttered by a past buddha:

"All conditioned things are impermanent — this is the nature of arising and ceasing."

Hearing this, I rose with joy and, seeing only the terrifying rākṣasa nearby, cried out asking who had spoken it, sensing this half-verse had already opened my own mind like a half-moon gradually opening a lotus. Approaching the rākṣasa, I asked where he had obtained it — and he answered only that he had gone many days without food and, driven half-mad by hunger, had spoken it without even knowing its meaning himself. I begged him to finish the verse, promising to become his disciple for life, since the giving of the Dharma, unlike material giving, is never exhausted — but he insisted he could not speak further, admitting at last, when pressed, that he ate only human flesh and drank only human blood, yet lacked the power to kill anyone protected by their own merit. I then offered him my own body in exchange for the rest of the verse: since this body, once dead, would be devoured by wild beasts regardless without any merit gained, I would rather trade this fragile body for something imperishable in pursuit of buddhahood — an exchange, I said, that Brahmā, Śakra, and every buddha throughout the ten directions could bear witness to. The rākṣasa then spoke the remaining half:

"Once arising and ceasing themselves have ceased, this very quiescence is bliss."

Told the verse was now complete and my wish fulfilled, and asked to give my body as promised, I first inscribed the verse on every rock, wall, tree, and roadway I could find, then climbed a high tree and threw myself down. Before I reached the ground, every kind of sound rose through the sky — and the rākṣasa, resuming his true form as Śakra, caught my body gently and set it safely on the ground. Through this very act I leapt ahead twelve full kalpas, destined to attain buddhahood even before Maitreya (Source: Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 13).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This entry preserves what is likely the single most quoted verse in the entire canon — "all conditioned things are impermanent, this is the nature of arising and ceasing; once arising and ceasing themselves have ceased, this very quiescence is bliss" — embedded in the story that explains why it exists in two halves at all: the first half alone, without its resolution, is nearly unbearable to hear, which is exactly why the ascetic cannot simply walk away once he has heard it. The story argues, in effect, that some truths cannot ethically be handed over half-finished, and that completing one is worth whatever price completing it costs.

A litany of extreme hypothetical vows — better burning iron than a broken precept, better a severed tongue than a craving for flavor — sets the price of moral integrity so high that no lesser temptation could possibly reach it.

Ru-Yi Translation

Good son, a bodhisattva who upholds the precepts makes vows such as these: "I would rather throw this body into a pit of fire than break the precept against impure conduct with women. I would rather wrap my body in burning iron than, having broken the precepts, accept clothing from a faithful donor. I would rather swallow a ball of molten iron than eat food so offered. I would rather lie upon burning iron than accept such bedding, or receive three hundred spear-thrusts than accept such medicine, or throw myself into a cauldron of molten iron than accept such a dwelling. I would rather be pounded to dust than accept the reverence of kings and brahmins with a broken precept. I would rather have my own eyes gouged out than gaze with desire on another's beauty, my body pierced with awls than listen with desire to pleasing sounds, my nose cut off than crave fragrance, my tongue cut out than crave fine flavor, my body hacked apart than crave pleasant sensation." Such cravings, left unchecked, lead one to hell, the animal realm, or the realm of hungry ghosts — this is what it means for a bodhisattva to uphold the precepts and dedicate this practice to all beings.

A bodhisattva cultivating these precepts thereby attains the first, unshakeable stage — like Mount Sumeru, unmoved even by the fiercest winds — unmoved by form, sound, fragrance, or flavor, never falling into the lower destinies, never retreating into a lesser vehicle, and never scattered by the winds of wrong view (Source: Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 11).

18–19. The Weight of a Single Resolve

A teacher makes his own novice walk ahead or behind him according to nothing but the novice's private, unspoken intention — proving that the mere resolve toward buddhahood outranks even an arhat's own attainment; and, among three children offering the same pearl to the Buddha, only the one who wishes to become like the Buddha himself sees his pearl come to rest in the place of highest honor.

初發心便勝二乘

Ru-Yi Translation
The Novice and the Two Vehicles

Even one who has only begun to practice the bodhisattva path, before accomplishing anything, already surpasses everyone else — just as the kalaviṅka bird, though still within its egg, already surpasses all other birds the moment it cries out. An arhat once had a novice carry his robe and bowl; whenever the novice privately resolved on the great vehicle, the teacher, perceiving this thought, took the robe and bowl himself and had the novice walk ahead of him — but whenever the novice, reconsidering, wished instead for a quick, lesser path to nirvāṇa, the teacher gave the burden back and made him walk behind. Puzzled at this repeated reversal, the novice was told: "When your mind resolves upon buddhahood, that resolve alone outranks even my own attainment, and so I let you walk ahead; when it turns back toward the lesser vehicle, that goal is still far beneath my own, and so you walk behind." The novice, startled into understanding, resolved thereafter to hold firmly to the Great Vehicle (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 78).

三小兒施佛二發小心一發大心

過去無數劫有佛。號一切度。與其眷屬俱行分衛。有三尊者子嚴服共戲。見佛及諸菩薩光明巍巍。手相指示。而吾等當共供養。二兒答言。既無花香當用何物。其一兒脫頭上白珠以著手中。便謂二兒可以供佛。二兒効之解頭上白珠著其手中。即至佛所。一兒復問二兒。持是功德以何求索。其一兒言。願如佛右面尊比丘。其一兒言。願如左面神足比丘。共問一兒。報言。我欲如佛。八千天子皆言善哉。若如所言天上天下一切蒙恩。是三小兒已到佛前。各以白珠而散佛上。二兒發聲聞意者。珠在佛肩上。其一兒發菩提心者。珠在佛頭上。化為珠花交露之帳其中有佛。佛告舍利弗中央兒者則我身是。右面之兒舍利弗是。左面之兒目連是。舍利弗汝等本畏生死故。不發菩薩心欲疾泥洹。觀此一兒發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心者(出阿闍世王經卷上)。

Three Children's Pearls

Long ago, three children of noble families, playing together, saw a buddha's own radiant light and agreed to make an offering. Lacking flowers, they each took the white pearl from their own head to offer instead. Approaching the Buddha, one wished to become like the honored monk on his right, another like the one of supernormal power on his left, and the third to become like the Buddha himself — a wish so great that eight thousand devas praised it as beneficial to all beings everywhere. When the three scattered their pearls upon the Buddha, the two who wished for the lesser path found their pearls resting merely on his shoulders, while the pearl of the one who aspired to buddhahood rested upon his very head, transforming into a jeweled canopy. The Buddha revealed that the two lesser boys were Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana, who, fearing birth and death, had wished only for swift nirvāṇa — while the third boy, who aspired instead to unsurpassed, perfect awakening, was the Buddha himself (Source: Sūtra of King Ajātaśatru, upper fascicle).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Both stories measure worth by aspiration alone, entirely independent of achievement — the novice has attained nothing, the child is still a child, yet each outranks a fully realized arhat by virtue of what he merely intends. Read alongside the fascicle's many stories of bodies given up in dramatic sacrifice, these two entries make a quieter but more radical claim: the sincere wish to become a buddha, before any actual practice has even begun, is already worth more than a lifetime's attainment of a lesser goal.

20. Deceived by Ghost-Wives

The fascicle closes with a young ascetic fleeing an arranged marriage across decades and countless miles, only to find the same seductive woman waiting for him at every refuge — until he finally confronts desire itself rather than its latest disguise.

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩。時為凡人。年始十六。志學弘深達眾經典。喟然歎曰。唯有佛經最真最妙。吾當懷其真處其自安矣。親欲為納妻。悵然而曰。妖禍之盛莫大乎色。若妖蠱一臻道德喪失。吾不遁邁將為狼吞乎。於是遂之異國。力賃自供。時有田翁老而無嗣。草行獲一女焉。顏華絕國欣育為嗣。採男為偶一國無可翁者焉。曰。童子吾居有足以女妻爾為吾嗣矣。女有神德惑菩薩心。納之無幾。即自覺曰。吾覩諸佛明化。以色為火。人為飛蛾。蛾貪火色。身見燒煮。斯翁以色火。燒吾躬。財餌鉤吾口。家[病-丙+歲]喪吾德矣。夜默道邁行百餘里。依空亭宿。亭人曰。子何人乎。曰吾欲寄宿耳。入覩床蓐。先自有婦人焉。顏似己妻。惑菩薩心與之共居。復積五年。明心覺焉曰。婬為蠣蟲殘危身命。默而疾邁。又覩婦人與居十年。又明覺曰。吾殃重矣。奔而不免。深自誓曰。終不寄宿。又復遁逃。遙覩大屋避之草行。守門者曰。何人夜行。答曰。趣前聚落。又曰。有禁內人呼前所覩。如上婦曰。自無數劫誓為室家爾走安之。菩薩念曰。欲根難拔乃如之乎。即興四非常之念。滅三界諸[病-丙+歲]。何但餘垢而不殄乎。鬼妻即滅。便覩諸佛處其前立。說無想之定授沙門戒為無勝師菩薩普度無極(出度無極集第八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago, as a bodhisattva living an ordinary life, at sixteen, deeply devoted to study, I resolved to hold to the Buddha's own teaching alone for my peace of mind. When my family wished to arrange a marriage, fearing no calamity greater than desire, I fled to another country instead, supporting myself through hired labor. There, an heirless old farmer offered me his own newly adopted daughter, a girl of unmatched beauty, as a bride — and she ensnared my heart before I realized, like a moth drawn to consuming fire, that this was merely the old man's scheme to trap me. I fled by night over a hundred li and took shelter in an empty pavilion — only to find a woman there resembling my own former bride, who likewise ensnared me for another five years, before I again came to my senses and fled once more, only to find yet another such woman waiting ten years further on. Fleeing still further, avoiding even a large house I glimpsed from a distance, I was told by its gatekeeper that a woman confined within wished to see me — and it was the very same woman again, who declared she had vowed across countless kalpas to be my wife, and that there was nowhere I could flee from her. Reflecting on how difficult desire's own root truly was to uproot, I gave rise to the four contemplations of impermanence, extinguishing every affliction of the three realms at once — and the ghost-wife vanished, replaced by the buddhas themselves, who taught me the samādhi free of conceptual thought and conferred upon me the monastic precepts, that I might become an unsurpassed teacher and bodhisattva, delivering beings without limit (Source: Collection on the Perfections, fascicle 8).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The story's structure is deliberately repetitive rather than escalating — the same seduction recurs almost identically at every stage of the journey, only the elapsed time growing longer each time (briefly, then five years, then ten). This repetition is the point: fleeing a specific temptation only relocates the problem, since desire itself, not any particular woman, is what keeps reappearing. The fascicle closes on this note deliberately, after nineteen entries of bodies given up to hostile kings, cruel teachers, and hungry rākṣasas — reminding the reader that the subtlest and most persistent adversary a bodhisattva faces is not external cruelty at all, but his own recurring craving, dressed up freshly each time it returns.
