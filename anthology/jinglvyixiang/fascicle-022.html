Novices Who Attained the Path

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 22 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "Arhat Novice-Monks, Part 11" (聲聞無學沙彌僧部第十一) — eleven stories of śrāmaṇera, novices not yet fully ordained, who nonetheless reached full liberation while still children. Among them: infant twins overheard debating their own past lives beside a farmer ready to burn them alive as demons, a boy raised inside his own dead mother's tomb on milk from her unspoiled corpse, a young novice who takes his own life rather than break his vow of chastity, and the fascicle's quietest, strangest close — a novice reborn as a worm in a jar of curds from nothing more than an unreleased craving for cheese.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 22 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 22 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 22 gathers eleven entries under a heading naming novice-monks (沙彌, śrāmaṇera) who nonetheless attained the arhat's own full liberation — children whose spiritual maturity consistently outpaces their years, a recurring and deliberate irony the compendium returns to across the whole fascicle. Every entry's full text remains intact, including several once-obscure past-buddha and place names preserved here in pinyin rather than guessed at in Sanskrit.

1. The Twins Who Remembered Their Own Past Lives

Newborn twins are overheard by their own terrified parents debating, in eerily adult language, the very cause of their own poverty across past lives — mistaken for demons and nearly burned alive, until the Buddha himself arrives to explain what they really are.

Chinese Source

舍衛國。有山民村。五六十家。去國五百里。村中有一貧家。婦懷妊十月雙生二男。大端正無比。父母愛之。為子作字。一名雙德。二名雙福。生五六十日。其父牧牛息臥床上。其母拾薪未反。此二兒共相責詰。一言前時垂當得道。正坐愚意謂命可常。退墮生死不可計劫。今生此貧家。蓑草之物以為氈褐。食飲麁惡裁自支命。如此至久云何可活。皆坐前世戀慕富貴。放身散意快樂須臾。從爾以來長塗受苦。如今憂惱當奈何恃怙。一人答言。時有小難一時之勤不竟精進。而今數世遭諸惡患。此是自為非父母作也。但共當之復何所言。父聞怪之謂呼是鬼。曼小未大宜當殺之。父驚到田中。收取樵薪欲燒殺之。母還問夫。用此薪何為。夫說如是。母聞罔然。明日夫婦俱出潛聽。二子相責如故。便共集薪密欲燒之。佛天眼見。往到村中普放光明。天地大動。山川樹木皆作金色。到雙生小兒家。二兒見佛光明。喜踊難量。父母又驚各抱一子。將至佛所問佛世尊。此兒生來四五十日。所說如是甚共怪之。恐作禍害欲火燒之。不知為是何等鬼魅也。唯願解說。小兒見佛踊躍歡喜。佛見小兒大笑。口出五色光。普照天地。佛告。此二小兒非是鬼魅。福德之子。前迦葉佛時作沙門。少小共為朋友。同志出家各自精進。臨當得道欻起邪想。共相沮敗樂世榮華。恃福生天。下為侯王國主長者。欻起是想便自退轉。不得泥洹更此生死。彌連歲數常相鉤牽。輒共雙生遭我世時。今始乃生。已曾供養佛故餘福應度。生識宿命今來度之。我不度者橫為火所燒。即說偈言。大人體無欲　在所照然明　雖或遭苦樂　不高現其智　大賢無世事　不願子財國　當守戒慧道　不貪邪富貴　智人智動搖　譬如沙中樹　朋友志不強　隨色染其素。佛說。是小兒見佛。身踊如八歲兒大。即作沙彌得羅漢道。村人大小見佛光相。又見小兒形變踊大。皆大歡喜得須陀洹道。父母疑解亦得法眼(出法句經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there was a mountain village of fifty or sixty households, five hundred li from the capital. In that village lived one poor household, whose wife, after ten months of pregnancy, gave birth to twin boys, both of extraordinary beauty. Their parents loved them dearly, and named them: the elder, Twin-Virtue; the younger, Twin-Blessing.

When the twins were fifty or sixty days old, their father was resting on a bed after tending cattle, and their mother had not yet returned from gathering firewood. The two infants began reproaching one another. One said: not long ago, we were on the very verge of attaining the path — but, through sheer foolishness, we believed our own good fortune would last forever, and so fell back into birth and death for countless kalpas beyond counting. Now we are born into this poor household, clothed in mere straw and coarse cloth, our food and drink so meager we can barely sustain our own lives — how, at this rate, can we go on living at all? All of this stems from our own past-life longing for wealth and glory, indulging ourselves and scattering our own resolve for a mere moment's pleasure — and, from that time to this, we have suffered through the long road of saṃsāra. Now, in our present distress, what refuge do we truly have?

The other answered: at the time, it seemed a small setback — a single moment's failure to see our own diligence through to the end — yet now, across several lifetimes, we have met with every kind of misfortune. This is entirely our own doing, not our parents' — what more is there to say, except to bear it together?

Their father, overhearing this, was alarmed, believing them to be demons: they are still so small, and yet — they should be killed at once. In his fright, he went out to the fields, gathered firewood, and meant to burn them. Their mother, returning, asked her husband what the firewood was for; he told her what he had heard. The mother, hearing this, was stunned and uncertain.

The next day, the husband and wife together went out and listened in secret; the two children were reproaching one another exactly as before. The parents then gathered firewood together, intending to burn them in secret.

The Buddha, seeing this with his own divine eye, came to the village, radiating light everywhere; heaven and earth shook greatly, and the mountains, rivers, and trees all turned to gold. Arriving at the twins' own household, the two infants, seeing the Buddha's own light, leapt with a joy beyond measure. Their parents, startled anew, each took up one child, and carried them before the Buddha, asking the World-Honored One: these children, born only forty or fifty days ago, speak in this way, and we are utterly bewildered by it — fearing some calamity, we meant to burn them; we do not know what manner of demon or spirit this might be — we beg you, please explain.

The infants, seeing the Buddha, leapt with joy; the Buddha, seeing the infants, laughed greatly, and from his own mouth issued light of five colors, illuminating heaven and earth everywhere. The Buddha said: these two infants are no demons at all, but children of great merit. In the time of Kāśyapa Buddha, in a past age, they were both śramaṇas, friends since their own youth, who went forth together with a single shared resolve, each cultivating diligence — yet, on the very verge of attaining the path, a deluded thought suddenly arose in them both, and, undermining one another together, they came instead to delight in worldly glory, relying on their own accumulated merit to be reborn in heaven, and afterward as princes, kings, and elders on earth. Once that deluded thought had arisen, they fell back from their own progress, failed to attain nirvāṇa, and continued instead through birth and death — bound to one another across the years, always reborn together as twins, until, meeting my own present age, they have only now been born again. Because they had once already made offerings to a buddha, some remaining merit of theirs called for their own deliverance; and, their past lives now known to them, I have come to deliver them — for, had I not, they would have been wrongly burned by fire.

The Buddha then spoke this verse:

"The great being's own body holds no desire; / wherever he is, his light shines clear. / Though he may meet with suffering or joy alike, / he never exalts himself, or shows off his own wisdom. / The truly wise have no concern for worldly affairs, / and wish for neither children, wealth, nor kingdom; / they hold instead to the path of precept and wisdom, / never coveting ill-gotten wealth and glory. / The wisdom of the merely clever wavers and shifts, / like a tree rooted in loose sand — / when a friend's own resolve is not firm, / he is dyed by whatever color surrounds him."

The Buddha spoke thus, and the two infants, seeing him, leapt up in body until they stood as tall as eight-year-old children, and became novices at once, attaining the fruit of arhatship on the spot. The villagers, young and old alike, seeing the Buddha's own radiant form, and seeing the infants transformed and grown so suddenly, all rejoiced greatly, and attained the fruit of stream-entry; their parents, their doubts now resolved, attained the pure Dharma-eye as well (Source: Dharmapada, fascicle 1).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The twins' own downfall in their past life is strikingly ordinary: not any dramatic transgression, but a single unguarded moment of complacency at the very threshold of liberation, mistaking present attainment for permanent security. Their father's terror is treated by the story with real sympathy rather than mockery — infants reasoning like seasoned renunciants about the causes of their own poverty is, after all, a genuinely frightening thing to overhear, and the tale lets that fear stand as reasonable before resolving it.

2. Raised on a Dead Mother's Milk

A king, deceived by a jealous rival and a bribed brahmin adviser, has his own unborn son killed before birth — yet the child is born anyway inside his mother's own tomb, and survives three years nursing from her unspoiled corpse before the Buddha himself finds him living wild among animals.

Chinese Source

有國王。名曰旃陀越。奉事婆羅門道。領治國正亦任用之。王重小夫人。諸夫人憎嫉。以金賜婆羅門譖之於王。言其生子必為國患。王聞不樂。問婆羅門言。當如之何。答曰。唯并殺之耳。王言。人命之重云何可殺。報言。若不殺者必亡國喪身。王便枉殺兒。後於塚中生。其母半身不朽。兒飲其湩。湩即乳也。乃至三年。其塚崩陷。其兒得出。與鳥狩共戲。暮即還塚中。及年六歲。佛念其勤苦與鳥狩同群。即化為沙門。往呼問之。言汝是誰家子。居在何處。兒歡喜報言。我無家居。但栖宿此塚中耳。乞隨道人去。佛言。欲何為乎。兒報言。我今善惡終當隨道人。佛便將其到祇洹中。見諸比丘威儀法則。意甚樂之。便白佛言。我欲乞作比丘。佛即聽之。以手摩其頭。髮自墮袈裟著身。名為須陀。守戒精進心不懈怠。經涉七日得羅漢道。佛語須陀。宜度栴陀越王。須陀往到。其國王曰。我心大憂當如之何。道人言。何所憂也。王言。我年已長。過時無嗣為之愁憂。道人聞王語。初不應之獨笑而已。王便恚言。我與道人語。初不答我。而反獨笑。即欲殺之。須陀知其意。便輕舉飛翔上住空中。分身散體出入無間。王見神化即悔過言。我實愚癡不別真偽。唯願大神一還。令我得自歸命。須陀即從空中下住王前。謂王言。若能自歸甚善。當自歸於佛。佛是我師。三界之尊度脫眾生。須陀便如申臂頃。將王及人民。俱到佛所。歸命三尊乞受五戒為優婆塞。佛具說。須陀是王子。王聞佛言。更恐怖不能自勝。佛言。昔拘先尼佛世。有國王號名弗舍達。國中人民皆供養三尊。時有凡人居貧無業。常為國中富貴。賃牧養數百頭牛。見王及人民供養比丘僧。便即問言。卿等何所為乎。人民答言。吾等供養三尊。後常在處安樂尊貴無有勤苦。即自念言。我貧唯當煎牛湩為酪酥。淨心上比丘耳。比丘僧呪願言。令汝世世得福。自後展轉更歷生死。輒受其福。或上為諸天。或下為王侯。王侯時出遊獵。見好牸牛懷犢殺之。夫人語王。莫殺其子。時牛主破取子養。其主恚言。當令王如此牛也。自後魂神為王作子。時未出生。母為王所殺須陀是也。須陀母者是時王夫人也。婆羅門者牛主是也。須陀塚中生。其母半身不朽。得飲其湩。以自長大者。由其宿命以酪酥上比丘僧故。王聞意解。得須陀洹(出旃陀越國王經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a king named Zhāntuóyuè, who served the brahmanical teaching, and, in governing his kingdom, relied on brahmin counsel. The king favored a certain lesser consort above his other wives, who, in jealousy, bribed a brahmin with gold to slander her before the king, claiming that the son she would bear must surely become a threat to the kingdom. The king, hearing this, was troubled, and asked the brahmin what should be done. He answered: only that the child be killed along with her. The king said: how could one so lightly take a human life? The brahmin answered: should you fail to kill them, you will surely lose both your kingdom and your own life. The king, wrongly persuaded, had the child killed before birth.

Yet the child was born afterward all the same, inside his own mother's tomb; her body, though dead, remained unspoiled from the waist up, and the child nursed at her own breast. For three full years this continued, until the tomb collapsed and gave way, and the child emerged, playing there among the birds and beasts, returning each evening to the tomb to sleep.

When the child reached six years of age, the Buddha, reflecting on his hardship, living as he did among animals, transformed himself into a śramaṇa, and went to question him: whose child are you, and where do you live? The child answered joyfully: I have no household at all — I only lodge here in this tomb. He asked to follow the renunciant away. The Buddha asked: what do you wish to do? The child answered: whatever good or ill may come, I shall follow this renunciant to the very end.

The Buddha then brought him to Jetavana, where, seeing the monks' own dignified bearing and discipline, the child took great delight in it, and said to the Buddha: I wish to ask to become a monk. The Buddha granted this at once, and, laying his own hand upon the child's head, his hair fell away of itself, and monastic robes appeared upon his body; he was given the name Xūtuó. Guarding the precepts with unwavering diligence, he attained the fruit of arhatship within seven days.

The Buddha told Xūtuó: you should now go and deliver King Zhāntuóyuè. Xūtuó went to him; the king said: my own heart is filled with great sorrow — what am I to do? The renunciant asked: what troubles you? The king said: I am already advanced in years, and, past the proper time, still have no heir — this is the cause of my grief. The renunciant, hearing the king's words, made no reply at first, and only smiled quietly to himself. The king, angered, said: I speak with you, renunciant, and you do not answer me at all, but only smile to yourself instead! — and moved to have him killed.

Xūtuó, knowing his intent, rose lightly into the air, hovering aloft, dividing and scattering his own body, appearing and vanishing without interval. The king, seeing this display of spiritual power, repented at once: I have truly been foolish, unable to distinguish truth from falsehood — I beg only that this great spirit return, that I might take refuge before him. Xūtuó descended at once from the sky, and stood before the king, telling him: if you can truly take refuge, this is most excellent — you should take refuge in the Buddha himself, who is my own teacher, honored throughout the three realms, the deliverer of all beings.

In no more time than it takes to stretch out one's own arm, Xūtuó brought the king and his people together before the Buddha, where they took refuge in the Three Jewels, and asked to receive the five precepts as lay followers. The Buddha then told them in full that Xūtuó was in fact the king's own son. The king, hearing this, was overcome with a fear he could not master.

The Buddha said: long ago, in the age of Jūxiānní Buddha, there was a king named Fúshědá, whose whole kingdom's people made offerings to the Three Jewels. At that time there lived an ordinary man, poor and without any trade of his own, who was hired by the kingdom's wealthy families to herd several hundred head of cattle. Seeing the king and his people making offerings to the assembly of monks, he asked them: what is it that you are doing? The people answered: we are making offerings to the Three Jewels — hereafter, we shall always dwell in ease, honor, and dignity, free of any hardship. The herdsman thought to himself: being poor, I can only boil my cattle's own milk into cheese and butter, and offer it with a pure heart to the monks instead. The assembled monks blessed him with this dedication: may you obtain blessing in every life to come. From that time on, through his own continued transmigration across many lives, he received this very blessing in turn — sometimes reborn above among the devas, sometimes below as a king or prince.

Once, as a prince, he went out hunting, and, seeing a fine cow heavy with calf, killed her. His own consort told him: do not kill her unborn calf — and so the calf was cut free and raised instead. The cow's original owner, in anger, said: may the prince himself become just like this cow! From that time on, that very spirit was reborn as this prince's own son — and, before he was even born, his mother was killed by the prince's own order. This is none other than Xūtuó himself. Xūtuó's mother was that very prince's own consort at that time; and the brahmin was that same cow's original owner.

That Xūtuó was born within a tomb, and that his mother's body, unspoiled from the waist up, allowed him to nurse and grow to maturity there, was owed entirely to his own past-life offering of cheese and butter to the assembly of monks. The king, hearing this, was freed from his own doubts, and attained the fruit of stream-entry (Source: Sūtra of King Zhāntuóyuè).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The past-life explanation here does real narrative work that a simpler moral fable would not attempt: it accounts not only for Xūtuó's eventual sainthood but for the specific, uncanny mechanics of his survival — his mother's corpse remaining unspoiled long enough to nurse him is traced directly back to a poor herdsman's single modest offering of cheese, made with nothing more than a "pure heart" and no expectation of such an extraordinary return. "拘先尼佛" (Jūxiānní Buddha) is preserved here in pinyin rather than matched to one of the standard named past buddhas, since the text gives no way to confirm which figure is meant with confidence.

3. The Novice Who Was Once a Starving Dog

A starving, crippled dog is fed by Śāriputra's own hand and taught the Dharma with its dying breath — reborn human, it becomes the novice Jūntí, whose own realization of his prior life as that very dog is followed by a second past-life lesson about the danger of mocking an elder's voice.

Chinese Source

佛在舍衛國。爾時尊者舍利弗。晝夜三時天眼觀視。誰應度者輒往度之。時有估客欲詣他國(報恩經云摩提二國中間有五百賈客)其諸商人共將一狗(報恩經云白狗)至於中路。眾賈頓息狗便盜肉。於時眾人便共打狗。而折其脚棄置空野捨之而去。時舍利弗天眼見狗。癵躄在地飢困垂死。飛至狗所以食施與。狗濟餘命心甚歡喜。時舍利弗。即為此狗說微妙法。狗便命終。生舍衛國婆羅門家。時舍利弗。獨行乞食。婆羅門見而問之言。尊者獨行無沙彌耶。舍利弗言。我無沙彌。聞卿有子。當用見與。婆羅門曰。我有一子。字曰均提。年既孩幼。不任使令。比前長大當用相與。時舍利弗。即戢在心。至年七歲復來求之。時婆羅門令兒出家。舍利弗將至祇洹。漸為說法。心意開解得阿羅漢(報恩經云佛言善來鬚髮自落袈裟著身)均提沙彌既始得道。自以智力觀過去世。見前身作一餓狗。蒙和上恩今得人身。并獲道果。欣心內發。而自念言。我蒙師恩得脫諸苦。今當盡身供給所須永作沙彌。乃往過去迦葉佛時。有諸比丘集在一處。時年少比丘音聲清雅。善巧讚唄。有一比丘。年高耆老音聲濁鈍。不能經唄。每自出聲而自娛樂。老比丘者已得羅漢。于時年少比丘而呵之言。今汝長老聲如狗吠。時老比丘。便呼年少。汝識我不。年少答曰。我大識汝。汝是迦葉佛時比丘上座。答言。我今已得阿羅漢。年少惶怖自責懺悔。猶五百世中常受狗身。由其出家持淨戒故。今得見我蒙得解脫(出賢愚經第十二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha dwelt in Śrāvastī. At that time the venerable Śāriputra surveyed the world with his own divine eye three times each day and night, and, whoever he found fit to be delivered, went at once to deliver them.

At that time a group of merchants set out for another country (the Sūtra of Repaying Kindness says there were five hundred merchants traveling between two countries); the merchants brought a dog along with them (the Sūtra of Repaying Kindness says it was a white dog). Partway along the road, as the merchants rested, the dog stole some meat; the whole company beat the dog for this, broke its leg, and abandoned it in the open wilderness, leaving it there as they departed.

Śāriputra, seeing the dog with his own divine eye — crippled, lying on the ground, starving and near death — flew at once to its side and gave it food. The dog, its remaining life thus saved, was filled with joy; Śāriputra then taught it the sublime Dharma. The dog, when its own life ended, was reborn into a brahmin household in Śrāvastī.

Śāriputra, later, was begging for alms alone; the brahmin, seeing him, asked: venerable one, do you travel alone, without any novice attendant? Śāriputra answered: I have no novice. Hearing that you have a son, I would ask that he be given to me. The brahmin said: I have one son, named Jūntí, but he is still an infant, and not yet fit to serve you — once he is older, I shall give him to you. Śāriputra kept this promise in mind, and, when the boy reached seven years of age, returned to ask for him again. The brahmin then let his son go forth; Śāriputra brought him to Jetavana, and, teaching him the Dharma gradually, his mind opened in understanding, and he attained arhatship (the Sūtra of Repaying Kindness records the Buddha saying: welcome — and his hair fell away of itself, monastic robes appearing on his body).

The novice Jūntí, having only just attained the path, used his own newfound wisdom to observe his past lives, and saw that, in his own former life, he had been a starving dog, and that, through his preceptor's own kindness, he had now obtained a human body, and attained the fruit of the path besides. A joy arose within him, and he thought to himself: I have received my teacher's own kindness, and been freed from every suffering — I shall now devote my whole life to attending to his every need, and remain a novice forever.

Long ago, in the time of Kāśyapa Buddha, monks had gathered together in one place; among them, a young monk possessed a clear and elegant voice, skilled in chanting hymns of praise, while an elderly monk, advanced in years, had a rough and dull voice, unable to chant the scriptures well, and so would simply chant for his own private enjoyment, on his own. This elderly monk had, in fact, already attained arhatship. The young monk mocked him, saying: elder, your voice sounds just like a dog's bark! The elderly monk then said to the young one: do you know who I am? The young monk answered: I know you well — you are a senior monk from the time of Kāśyapa Buddha. He answered: I have now already attained arhatship.

The young monk, terrified, reproached himself and repented — yet still, across five hundred lifetimes that followed, he was constantly reborn in the body of a dog. It was only because, in this present life, he went forth and upheld the pure precepts, that he has now come to see me, and thereby obtained his own liberation (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 12).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The entry folds together two separate past-life accounts under a single novice's name: the dog he was in his own immediately preceding life, and the young monk's mocking insult toward an elder five hundred lifetimes further back, which the text implies is the deeper cause of the dog-rebirths themselves. Read together, they suggest that a single act of disrespect toward someone whose own attainment is invisible from the outside — an elderly monk with an unremarkable voice, easily mistaken for merely ordinary — can outlast even the specific memory of its own cause.

4. Saving the Ants, Extending His Own Life

An arhat teacher, foreseeing his young novice's death in seven days and fearing the parents' grief and blame, sends the boy home early — but a single act of compassion toward drowning ants along the road adds decades to a life that was never meant to last the week.

Chinese Source

昔有小國。去城不遠有好林藪。有五道士於中學道。有一比丘得六神通。有一沙彌年始八歲。共在山中。各一面坐思惟經道。師知沙彌命餘七日。在此亡者父母謂吾。看視不快使其命終。心懷怨恨。即語沙彌。汝父母思汝。汝可歸家八日早來。沙彌歡喜稽首而去。道逢大雨流潦滂沛。地有蟻孔流水欲入。沙彌念曰。我佛弟子。一者慈心。二者活生。即便土壅決水令去。沙彌歸家無有他變。八日晨還。師遙見之。怪其所以。七日應亡。今何因緣。將無鬼神化現來乎。即入三昧。見其救蟻現世延壽。沙彌至。稽首作禮於一面坐。師謂言。汝作大功德。為自知不。沙彌言。七日在家無他功德。師言。汝命應盡。昨日以救蟻故。現世增壽八十餘年。沙彌歡喜信善有報。即更勤修精進不懈。得阿羅漢(出福報經又出十卷譬喻經第七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a small kingdom, not far from whose capital lay a fine forest, where five renunciants pursued the path. One of them, a monk, had attained the six supernormal powers; among them, too, was a novice, only eight years old, and together they dwelt in the mountains, each sitting apart, absorbed in the contemplation of the scriptures and the path.

The teacher, knowing that his novice had only seven days left to live, and fearing that, should the boy die there, his own parents might blame him for having failed to care for him properly, and harbor resentment against him, told the novice: your parents are thinking of you — you should go home, and return again in eight days. The novice, delighted, bowed and departed.

Along the road, he encountered a great rainstorm, the water flowing in torrents; in the ground was an ant's burrow, into which the flowing water was about to pour. The novice thought to himself: I am a disciple of the Buddha — my duty is, first, a heart of loving-kindness, and second, to preserve life — and at once dammed the earth to divert the water away from the burrow.

The novice returned home, and nothing else befell him; on the morning of the eighth day, he came back. His teacher, seeing him from afar, was astonished — he ought to have died within seven days; by what cause was he still alive now? Could some ghost or spirit have taken his form and come in his place? The teacher entered meditative absorption at once, and saw that the novice's own act of saving the ants had, in this very life, extended his lifespan.

The novice arrived, bowed, and sat to one side. The teacher said to him: you have performed a great meritorious deed — do you know this yourself? The novice answered: in my seven days at home, I did nothing of any particular merit. The teacher said: your own life was fated to end — yet yesterday, because you saved those ants, your present lifespan has been extended by more than eighty years. The novice, overjoyed, and now confident that goodness truly brings its own reward, applied himself thereafter with even greater diligence, without ever slackening, and attained arhatship (Source: Sūtra of Blessings and Their Fruits; also found in the Ten-Fascicle Sūtra of Similes, fascicle 7).

5. The Fall That Was Not a Killing

A father and son, both gone forth together in old age and poverty, beg for alms as evening falls — when the son, trying only to hurry his own slow, elderly father to safety before dark, accidentally pushes him to his death, and the Buddha traces the accident to an identical, equally innocent death in a past life.

Chinese Source

舍衛國有一老公。早失其婦。獨與兒居。困無財寶。覺世非常。從佛出家。兒年尚小。亦為沙彌。共父乞食。逼暮當還。父行遲。兒畏毒狩。急扶其父排之進路。執之不固。推父墮地應手而死。獨至佛所。時諸比丘訶責沙彌。即以白佛。佛告之曰。此師雖死不以惡意。即問沙彌。汝殺師不。答言。我實排之。不以惡意。佛言。我知汝心無有惡意。過去世時亦復如是。無有惡意而相殺害。昔父子二人共住一處。時父病極。於時睡臥。多有絲蠅數來惱觸。父令兒遮蠅。望得安眠。時兒急遮蠅來不止。兒便瞋恚。即持大杖伺蠅當殺(十誦律云持大石婢蚊子)時諸絲蠅競集父額。以杖打之其父即死。父者此沙彌是。時兒者死比丘是。由無惡心不以惡意。亦非故殺。沙彌勤修不懈。遂得羅漢道(出賢愚經第十卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In Śrāvastī there lived an old man, who, having lost his wife early in life, dwelt alone with his son. Reduced to poverty, and awakened to the impermanence of the world, he went forth as a monk under the Buddha; his son, still young, became a novice as well, and the two of them went begging for alms together.

As evening drew near and they needed to return, the father walked slowly, and the son, fearing the dangerous beasts that might come out after dark, hurried to support his father and push him forward along the road — but his grip was not firm, and, in pushing him, the father fell to the ground and died instantly at his own son's hand.

The son went alone before the Buddha. The other monks rebuked the novice at once, and reported the matter to the Buddha, who told them: though this teacher has died, it was not done with any malicious intent. The Buddha then asked the novice: did you kill your own teacher? He answered: I truly did push him, but not with any malicious intent. The Buddha said: I know that your own heart held no malice. In a past age, too, the very same thing occurred — a killing between two people, without any malicious intent at all.

Long ago, a father and son dwelt together in one place. The father, gravely ill, lay sleeping, and many flies came again and again to disturb him. The father told his son to keep the flies away, hoping to sleep undisturbed. The son, driving the flies off urgently, found they would not stop coming, and, growing angry, took up a large stick, waiting for a fly to kill (the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya says he held a large stone to strike at mosquitoes). The flies then gathered together on the father's own forehead; the son struck at them with the stick, and the father died at once.

That father was this same novice's teacher; and that son was the very monk who has now died. Because there was no malicious intent, no wish to harm at all, this was no true killing. The novice, applying himself with unwavering diligence, went on to attain the fruit of arhatship (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 10).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's own ruling here is worth reading as a deliberate doctrinal point rather than mere comfort: karmic weight, in this account, tracks intention rather than outcome — an identical fatal result, produced twice across two lifetimes by the same configuration of care mixed with a moment's carelessness, carries no culpability at all, because in both cases the actor's own heart held no wish to harm. The compendium rarely states this principle so explicitly through a paired, doubled illustration.

6. Five Mothers Weeping for One Son

The first of two entries sharing an identical title in the source text: a beloved young disciple dies suddenly and is reborn a lesser god, only to grieve his own lost teacher and friends until he returns to the Buddha for teaching that finally consoles his own bereft preceptor as well.

The Buddha traveled in Śrāvastī, in the Jetavana, in Anāthapiṇḍada's park. A certain monk there had a disciple of gentle and refined character, kind and virtuous in every action, who constantly attended his own preceptor with sincere care and diligence, reverently following the Dharma's teaching, never once disobeying his teacher's own instruction. Yet his own life was short, and he died while still young, and was reborn in the palace of Trāyastriṃśa heaven.

Looking out over the heavens, he saw only a great fire, and thought: my own original aspiration remains unfulfilled — I cannot keep company with my own good companions here, and, having left my good teacher behind, now find myself among unfamiliar company instead. In this way I have become distant from my own honored preceptor, and from the assembly of Āyílí and all others of similar kind who cultivate pure conduct, and from the fourfold assembly of disciples, and from the one endowed with all-encompassing wisdom, called the Tathāgata — now all of them lie far from me, one so difficult to encounter, so difficult even to glimpse, across ages beyond counting. He has arisen in this world to expound the scriptures' own subtle and profound meaning, teachings never before spoken, calmly opening beings to understanding, teaching the various chains of dependent origination, so that each might come to understand for themselves the causes from which they arise — matters unseen and unheard across ages beyond counting, now all made clear. Having encountered this very teaching and discipline, I abandoned my own household to pursue the path — yet what I ought to have accomplished remains unfinished, and now, instead, I find myself here in mere heedless ease.

He went first before the World-Honored One, and bowed at his feet. The Buddha, seeing his heart truly sincere, delighting purely in the path, wholly devoted to the Dharma, taught him suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path — and he beheld the truth at once.

The Buddha asked: why does this cause you such grief? He answered: my novice disciple was of such fine and virtuous character, yet died prematurely before completing his own training — it is for this that I grieve, and cannot console myself. Reading note — Ru-Yi

"阿夷梨眾" (Āyílí, likely a transliteration related to ācārya, "teacher") is preserved here in pinyin rather than reconstructed with confidence, since the term's precise referent in this context is not certain. The entry's own real subject is less the deceased novice's rebirth than his teacher's grief — a rare moment in the compendium where a senior monk's own bereavement is treated at length and with full sympathy, resolved not by suppressing the feeling but by the Buddha's direct, factual reassurance about where the disciple actually is now.

7. Cunda and the Ghost Who Guarded the Lake

A veteran ascetic of 120 years' austerities is repeatedly barred from a sacred lake by its own guardian spirit, while an eight-year-old novice arrives unopposed and is honored by that same spirit as its superior — a lesson in attainment measured by realization, not by years of effort, that repeats itself in a second, closely paired episode.

Chinese Source

舍利弗有一沙彌。名曰純頭。年八歲得六神通。飛騰虛空至阿耨泉。有五通梵志。名曰須拔。亦至彼泉。時彼泉上。有守泉青衣鬼。驅逐五通梵志。瓦石打擲不使逼近神泉。純頭沙彌乘虛空至。彼青衣鬼數百之眾皆前迎逆。或前收攝衣者。或持淨水洗手足者。或以淨巾拂拭首面者。或以香湯沐浴身體者。須拔梵志放聲說曰。我今已得五通神德無量。力能移山住流。迴轉天地猶掌迴珠。自學道以來百二十餘年。勞形苦體形神疲極。或事五明。四處然火。日光上照。或臥灰糞。或臥荊棘嶮難之中。無道不學。然更驅逐不得至泉。然此黑衣小兒。年在七八。未離乳哺。身體穢臭待敬過重。用何等故。時青衣鬼語梵志曰。今此學士。形年雖小行過三界。得賢聖八品道。汝今無是故不興敬。有一婆羅門。名曰閱叉。興立一寺。亦名閱叉。恒供給酥油供寺然燈。時有遠方婆羅門。來至彼寺中。又聞閱叉梵志高才明德。偏信佛法建立神廟與共相見。時有一沙彌。來迎取油酥。供寺然燈。眾多梵志。語閱叉婆羅門曰。汝審向色衣人禮耶。言語未訖沙彌已至。即復禮之。眾多梵志。語此梵志曰。汝出四姓才藝過人。天文地理無不觀練。神呪感靈無事不剋。今此色衣之人。出眾多姓。種非真正。何為違本法而向恭禮。又卿梵志執行清淨。自修內藏圖讖祕記。行道成福何願不剋。文字章印無不周悉。佛行寡鮮有何可貴。捨本取末是我所疾。蓋聞沙門寒賤。巧詐繁滋幻惑世人。所行短促齊榮一身。不能延致梵福。正使相見正可擎捲而已。何為五體投地恭敬作禮耶。我等親見甚怪所以。況先學大人。豈能恕卿此罪耶。閱叉報眾多婆羅門曰。諸人靜默聽我所說。賢聖德難量　八直無上道　是為梵沙門　如來口所宣　覩此形雖小　以果賢聖道　是故今自歸　梵志何為嗤　向得能究竟。須陀洹斯陀含。能斷欲界縛諸纏陰入。是故說曰向得能盡魔原者。入定坐禪之人。樂處閑靜志崇一意。計出入息執意牢固。能斷魔縛。及縛於魔。入定之人。能役鬼神如意即至。當求方便斷魔牢縛(出出耀經第五卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Śāriputra had a novice named Cunda, who, at eight years old, had attained the six supernormal powers, and could fly through the open sky to Lake Anavatapta. There was also a brahmin ascetic of the five powers, named Subhadra, who likewise came to that same lake. At the lake, a blue-robed ghost stood guard, and drove Subhadra away, pelting him with tiles and stones, never letting him draw near the sacred waters.

When the novice Cunda arrived, flying through the open sky, that same blue-robed ghost, together with several hundred of its own kind, came forward to greet him — some gathering up his robes, some bringing clear water to wash his hands and feet, some wiping his head and face with a clean cloth, some bathing his own body in fragrant water.

Subhadra cried out: I have already attained the five powers, my own spiritual virtue beyond measure — with such strength I can move mountains and hold back rivers, turn heaven and earth as easily as a bead turned in the palm of my hand. In the hundred and twenty years since I first began studying the path, I have exhausted my own body and mind in every austerity — attending to the five sciences, tending fire at the four corners, letting the sun's own rays scorch me from above, lying at times in ash and filth, at times among thorns and brambles in dangerous places — there is no discipline I have not undertaken. And yet I am still driven away, and cannot reach the lake at all — while this small child in black robes, only seven or eight years old, not yet even weaned, whose own body reeks with the stench of an infant, receives such excessive reverence. Why is this so?

The blue-robed ghost told the ascetic: though this young scholar is small in years and form, his own conduct has already surpassed the three realms, and he has attained the noble eightfold path itself. You yourself have attained none of this — that is why we show you no such reverence.

There was, elsewhere, a brahmin named Yuèchā, who built a temple, likewise named Yuèchā, and constantly supplied it with oil and butter to keep its lamps burning. A brahmin from a distant land came once to that temple, having heard of the brahmin Yuèchā's own great talent and clear virtue, and, though himself devoted to the brahmanical teaching and the temples raised to its own deities, came to meet him in person.

At that time, a novice came to fetch oil and butter, to supply the temple's own lamps. Several brahmins there said to the visiting brahmin, Yuèchā: do you truly mean to bow before this man in colored robes? Before the words were even finished, the novice arrived, and Yuèchā bowed to him at once. The assembled brahmins said to their visiting guest: you come from the four highest castes, your own talent surpassing others, versed in astronomy and geography alike, your sacred spells moving even the spirits, nothing beyond your own accomplishment — yet this man in colored robes comes from a mixed multitude of castes, of no true or pure lineage at all — why would you abandon your own proper conduct to bow before him in reverence? You yourself, moreover, hold to pure practice, cultivate the hidden scriptures and secret prophecies within your own tradition, and, through the merit your own practice has achieved, there is nothing you could wish for that lies beyond your reach — every text and seal already mastered in full. What is there of value in the Buddha's own meager practice, that you should abandon what is fundamental to chase after what is merely secondary? This is precisely what troubles us. We have heard that śramaṇas are poor and lowly, their cunning deceptions spreading confusion among the people of the world, their own practice brief and self-serving, incapable of securing any lasting brahmanical blessing. Even should you meet one, a simple gesture of the hands would suffice — why prostrate your entire body upon the ground in such reverence? We ourselves have witnessed this and find it deeply strange — how much more so a senior scholar such as yourself? How could we possibly excuse you this transgression?

Yuèchā answered the assembled brahmins: all of you, be still, and hear what I have to say.

"The virtue of the noble and wise cannot be measured; / the eightfold path is the unsurpassed way — / this is what the Tathāgata himself proclaimed / to be the true brahmin-renunciant. / Though I see before me a form still small, / it has already borne the fruit of the noble path — / it is for this that I now take refuge in him; / brahmins, why do you mock me for this?"

"Toward this he has already attained ultimate completion" refers to the stream-enterer and the once-returner, who are able to sever the bonds of the desire realm, and every entanglement of the aggregates and sense-fields — it is for this reason we say he has attained the very exhaustion of Māra's own domain. One who enters absorption in seated meditation, delighting in quiet solitude, his own will fixed single-mindedly, counting the breath in and out, his resolve held firm, is able to sever Māra's own bonds, and bind Māra in turn. One who has entered such absorption can command even spirits and ghosts, who come at his own will the moment he wishes it — one need only seek the right means to sever Māra's own firm bonds (Source: Sūtra of Illustrative Parables [Chūyào jīng], fascicle 5).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The entry pairs two structurally identical episodes — a lake spirit and a temple-founding brahmin, each confronted with the same choice between honoring visible seniority (120 years of austerity, four generations of caste privilege and scholarship) and honoring invisible attainment in an eight-year-old novice — and both choose the latter without hesitation. The closing verse-commentary, an unusually direct doctrinal gloss embedded in the narrative itself, spells out exactly what "attainment" means here: not accumulated effort, but specific, verifiable stages of realization that even prolonged devotion cannot substitute for.

8. Five Mothers Weeping for One Son, Again

A second entry with the same theme as entry 6, told through a very different lens: a young novice who attains supernormal insight into his own five past lives — five separate mothers, each grieving his own repeated early deaths — and finds, in the sheer accumulated weight of that grief, something closer to dark comedy than tragedy.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

By the age of eight, he had already attained the four supernormal knowledges: first, sight that penetrates all obstruction; second, hearing that penetrates all obstruction; third, the power of flight and transformation; and fourth, knowledge of his own past lives, wherever they came from.

Sitting in reflection, he beheld the whole course of his own past lives, and, seeing that he had been son to five different mothers in turn, suddenly laughed aloud. His teacher asked why he laughed. The novice said: how would I dare laugh at you, my teacher? I was only observing that this single life of mine has, in fact, had five mothers, each weeping day and night, stricken with sorrow, always saying: I have never once forgotten to think of my child. And I reflected: one single life's grief, spread across five different households — this is why I laughed.

When I was son to my first mother, there was a neighbor's child born on the very same day as I; after I died, that same child, born the same day as I, continued to walk about and go on with life. My mother, seeing this, said: were my own child still alive, he too would be walking about just so — and grieved for me in sorrow and pain.

I was then son to a second mother, and died again not long after birth. My mother, seeing others nursing their own children, was struck with grief at the thought of me, weeping in sorrow.

I was then son to a third mother, and again died not long after. My mother, at mealtimes, would weep, thinking of me: were my child still alive, he would share this meal with me — but he has abandoned me, and died.

I was then son to a fourth mother, and again died not long after. When others of my own age were being married, my mother thought of me again: had my child not died, he too would be taking a wife by now.

I was then son to a fifth mother, and it is in this very life that I still remain. Yet I left my own household to study the path, and my mother weeps daily, saying: I have lost my child, and do not know where he has gone — through hunger, cold, life, and death, we shall never see each other again — and grieves in deep anguish.

Now these five mothers have all, in a sense, gathered together — each speaking of her own lost child, each weeping and grieving for me, one single person. This is why I laughed.

People in this world do not know that there is a life to come — they speak only of death, as though it were the end. Yet a person who does good obtains blessing, and one who does evil meets with calamity; while still in this world, people ought to hold themselves in awe of their own good and evil deeds — for one who fears nothing now shall, afterward, suffer for the evil done, and, once fallen into an evil realm, no regret can undo it. It is because I grew weary of this world that I left my own family to seek the path. I have seen the hells, the animal realm, the hungry ghosts, and the poverty of this human world, and, in place of these mothers, I bear the fear they cannot see. I have received my teacher's own kindness, and taken up the Buddha's own scriptures and precepts, and am now on the way to liberation — yet these five mothers of mine remain unable to attain it themselves, and grieve for me still, again and again (Source: Sūtra of the Child with Five Mothers).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the second of two entries sharing an almost identical descriptive title within this same fascicle — both concern a young novice, supernormal knowledge, and grief left behind — yet the two stories could hardly differ more in tone: entry 6 is a preceptor's own bereavement resolved through direct reassurance, while this entry is the departed child's own retrospective account of the grief he caused across five separate lifetimes, delivered with something closer to rueful laughter than sorrow. The novice's own laugh is worth sitting with rather than smoothing over — it registers neither as callousness toward his mothers' real suffering nor as simple detachment, but as the specific, disorienting comedy of seeing one's own small life multiplied and repeated from an impossible outside vantage no ordinary person could ever occupy.

9. The Novice Who Chose Death Over Breaking His Precept

Fascicle 22's longest entry: left alone in charge of a household and consumed with desire, a sixteen-year-old girl attempts to seduce the novice sent to collect her family's usual food offering — and, finding every argument fails, he locks himself away and takes his own life rather than risk breaking his vow of chastity, a death the whole kingdom comes to honor.

Chinese Source

有一比丘。少欲知足。時安陀國有優婆塞。敬信三寶終身供養。日日遣送。其國有一長者。生一男兒。欲令出家當求善師。即往白比丘言。我此一子令使出家。唯願大德哀納濟度。爾時比丘以道眼觀。此人出家能持淨戒。度為沙彌時優婆塞有一親善居士。明日客會。朝念言。今當就會誰後守舍。女即白父。唯願父母從諸僮使。但行應請我堪後守。父曰甚善。合家悉往。女便閉門獨住家內。時優婆塞是日怱怱忘不送食。爾時尊者心自念言。日時向晚。俗人多事。即遣沙彌往取。善攝威儀如佛所說。沙彌打門。女問是誰。答言。沙彌為師迎食。即與開門。是女端正容貌姝妙。年始十六婬欲火燒。於沙彌前。作諸妖媚深現欲相。沙彌見已念言。此女為有風癎病耶。將無欲結所使欲毀我淨行耶。堅攝威儀顏色不變。女便五體投地白沙彌言。我常願者欲有所陳。我此舍中珍寶倉庫。如毘沙門天宮寶藏。而無有主。汝可屈意為此舍主。我為汝婢供給使令滿我所願。沙彌念。我寧捨命不毀禁戒。昔日比丘至婬女家。寧投火坑不犯於欲。又諸比丘賊所劫奪以草繫縛。風吹日曝諸蟲唼食。以護戒故不絕草而去。如鳥吞珠。比丘雖見。以持戒故極苦不說。如海船壞。下坐比丘。以守戒故授板上坐沒海而死。如是諸人獨佛弟子能持禁戒。我非弟子不能持也。如來世尊獨為彼師非我師也。方便語言。牢閉門戶我入一房作所應作。爾乃一相就。女即閉門。沙彌入房關撢門戶。得一剃刀。脫身衣服置於架上。合掌跪向拘尸那城佛涅槃處。自立誓願。我今不捨佛法眾僧。亦不捨戒正為持戒。捨此身命願所往生。出家淨行盡漏成道。即刎頸死。時女怪遲趣戶看視。見其已死失於本容。欲心尋息慚結懊惱悲呼悶絕。其父會還見女如是問何以故。具答以實。父即入房見沙彌。身血污赤如栴檀。作禮讚言。護持佛戒能捨身命。時彼國法。若有沙門白衣舍死。當罰金錢。時優婆塞以一千金錢置銅案上載至王宮。白言。大王。我有罰讁。應入於王。願當受之。王言。汝於我國敬信三寶言行無違。唯汝一人當有何過。而輸罰耶。時優婆塞具陳上緣。自毀其女讚嘆沙彌。王聞悚然而告之言。沙彌護戒自捨身命。汝無辜咎。但持還舍。吾今躬欲自至汝家供養沙彌。王往見之。前為作禮。以種種寶莊嚴高車載死沙彌至平坦地。積眾香木闍毘供養。嚴飾是女極世之殊。置高顯處普使時會一切皆見。語眾人言。是女殊妙容暉乃爾。未離欲者誰無染心。而此沙彌既未得道。以生死身奉戒捨命甚奇希有。王即遣人令請其師。廣為大眾說微妙法。時會一切見聞此事。有求出家。有發無上菩提心者(出賢愚經第七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a monk of few desires, content with what he had. In the country of Andhra there lived a lay follower, devoted in faith to the Three Jewels, who supported them for his whole life, sending offerings daily. In that same country there lived an elder, whose own son he wished to have go forth, and, seeking a worthy teacher for him, went before this monk and said: I wish this one son of mine to go forth — I beg only that you, in your great compassion, accept and deliver him. The monk, observing with his own spiritual eye that this boy, once gone forth, would be able to uphold the pure precepts, took him on and made him a novice.

The lay follower had a close friend, a fellow householder, whose own family was to hold a gathering of guests the next day. That morning, he thought to himself: who shall stay behind to guard the house while we attend this gathering? His own daughter said: father and mother, please take all the servants with you — you go on ahead to the invitation, and I shall stay behind to guard the house. Her father said: very well — and the whole household went, while the daughter shut the door and remained alone within.

That same day, the lay follower, in his own haste, forgot to send the usual food offering. The venerable monk, thinking to himself that the day was growing late, and that lay people often have many affairs to attend to, sent his novice to go and fetch it, bidding him keep his own bearing composed and dignified, exactly as the Buddha had taught.

The novice knocked at the door; the young woman asked who it was. He answered: a novice, come on his teacher's behalf to receive the food offering. She opened the door for him at once. This young woman was of striking and lovely appearance, only sixteen years old, and, burning now with the fire of desire, displayed before the novice every manner of seductive charm, revealing her own desire plainly. The novice, seeing this, thought to himself: does this woman suffer from some fit of madness? Or is she driven by the bonds of desire, meaning to destroy my own pure conduct? He held his own bearing firm, his expression unchanged.

The young woman then threw herself to the ground before him, and said to the novice: what I have always wished to say is this — the treasures and storehouses within this household are as rich as the treasury of Vaiśravaṇa's own heavenly palace, yet have no true master. Would you consent to become the master of this house? I shall be your own servant, and see to your every wish, that my own desire might be fulfilled.

The novice thought to himself: I would rather give up my own life than defile the precepts I have vowed to keep. In days past, a monk who found himself in a house of prostitution chose to throw himself into a pit of fire rather than commit any act of desire. And there were monks, robbed by bandits and bound with mere grass, who, though the wind blew and the sun scorched them, and insects gnawed at their own flesh, would not break even a single blade of that grass, for the precept's own sake, and so remained bound rather than leave. And there was a monk who, seeing a bird swallow a pearl, said nothing at all, though it cost him great suffering, for the precept's own sake. And there was, aboard a sinking ship, a monk of low seat who, for the precept's own sake, gave up his place on a plank to another, and sank into the sea and died. All such people, only the Buddha's own disciples, are able to uphold the precepts in this way — but I myself am no true disciple, if I cannot do the same. The Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, would then be that other monk's own teacher, and not mine at all.

He then spoke to her with skillful means: bar the doors and windows securely — I shall go into a room, and do what is needed there; only then shall I come to you. The woman shut the door as he said. The novice entered a room, bolted its own door, took up a razor, removed his own robes and set them upon a rack, and, joining his palms, knelt facing toward Kuśinagara, the place of the Buddha's own parinirvāṇa, and made this vow to himself: I shall not, this day, abandon the Buddha's teaching or the Saṅgha, nor shall I abandon the precepts — it is precisely to uphold them that I now give up this body and this life, wherever I may then be reborn. Having gone forth in pure conduct, may I exhaust every outflow, and attain the path. And, with this, he cut his own throat, and died.

The young woman, surprised at his long delay, went to the door to look, and found him already dead, his own form disfigured; her desire subsided at once, and, overcome with shame, remorse, and grief, she cried out and fainted.

Her father, returning from the gathering, saw his daughter in this state, and asked what had happened; she told him the whole truth. The father then went into the room, and saw the novice, his own body soaked red with blood like sandalwood dye, and, bowing before him, praised him, saying: he has guarded the Buddha's precepts, even at the cost of his own life.

In that kingdom, the law held that, should any śramaṇa die within a layperson's own household, a fine of gold must be paid. The lay follower placed a thousand gold pieces upon a bronze tray, carried it to the royal palace, and said: great king, I owe a penalty, and it should be paid into your own treasury — I beg you to accept it. The king said: you, in my own kingdom, have honored and trusted the Three Jewels, your words and conduct never once at odds with them — what fault could you alone have committed, that you should pay such a fine? The lay follower then told the whole matter in full, blaming his own daughter and praising the novice.

The king, hearing this, was struck with awe, and told him: the novice guarded the precepts even at the cost of his own life — you yourself bear no fault at all; take this back to your own household. I myself shall now go in person to your home, to make offering to the novice. The king went and saw him, bowed before him, and, adorning a high carriage with every kind of treasure, had the novice's own body carried to level ground, where fragrant wood was piled high and he was cremated with full honors.

The king then had the young woman herself adorned in the finest manner the world could offer, and placed in a high, visible place, so that the whole assembled crowd might see her, and told the people: this woman's own beauty and radiant charm are such as this — who among those not yet free of desire could fail to feel some stir of attachment at the sight of her? And yet this novice, though not yet himself an arhat, offered up his own mortal, still-living body to uphold the precept, and gave up his own life for it — how rare and extraordinary a thing this is!

The king then sent for the novice's own teacher, and had him teach the sublime Dharma widely to the whole assembly. All those gathered there, seeing and hearing of this event, were moved — some sought to go forth themselves, and some gave rise to the resolve for unsurpassed enlightenment (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 7).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The novice's own extended internal recitation of precedent — the monk who chose the fire pit, the monks bound by living grass, the silent monk and the pearl-swallowing bird, the low-seated monk who gave up his plank on a sinking ship — draws directly on stories told earlier in this compendium (fascicle 19, entries 12 and 21, among others), and the text seems to expect its own reader to recognize them as an established repertoire of precedent the novice is drawing strength from, rather than explaining each in full again here. The king's own closing address to the crowd is unusually candid: rather than condemning the young woman's beauty as a moral danger to be shunned, he frames ordinary desire toward her as entirely natural and expected, which only sharpens, by contrast, the specific rarity of what the novice actually achieved.

10. The Novice and the Dragon Girl

A novice's habit of quietly eating the muddied portion of his own alms to spare his teacher draws suspicion, then reveals an infatuated dragon leaving strange food in his bowl — and, against his own teacher's warning about a dragon's threefold suffering, the novice's longing for that hidden world costs him his human life.

Chinese Source

昔有羅漢。與沙彌於山中行道。沙彌日日至人家所乞飲食。經歷堤基上行。岐嶇危險脚跌躄地。飯洿泥土沙彌取不洿飯。著師鉢中。洿飯洗自食之。如是非一日。師曰。何因洗棄飯味。答曰。行乞去晴。乞還時雨。於堤基躄地覆飯。師默然思之。知是龍嬈沙彌便起到堤基上。持杖叩藪之。龍化作老公來。出頭面著地。沙門言。汝何嬈我沙彌乎。答曰。實愛其容貌耳。從今日始日日於我室食。沙門受請日日往食。沙彌後時見師鉢中。有兩三粒飯非世間飯。問和上。師默不應。沙彌便入床下手捉床足。和上禪定竟相隨。俱飛到龍宮殿上。龍及婦女俱禮沙門復禮沙彌。師乃覺之。呼出語言。此非婇女。是畜生耳。汝為沙彌。雖未得道。必生忉利天上。勝彼百倍。勿以洿意。沙彌言。此龍居處世間少有。師曰。彼有三苦。一者雖百味飯入口即化成蝦蟇。二者婇女端正無比。欲為夫婦兩蛇相交。三者龍背有逆鱗。沙石生其中。痛乃達心胸。此為大苦。汝何因從之。汝未得道。不可令見鬼道及國王內事也。沙彌不應。晝夜思想。憶彼不食病而死。魂神生為龍作子(大智論云布施持戒願早作龍要期心重足下水出後至師本所入處大池之邊以袈裟覆頭而入死即變為大龍神殺舊龍舉池盡赤出迦葉詰難陀經又出大智論第十七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was an arhat who practiced the path in the mountains together with a novice. The novice went daily to beg food from nearby households. Along the way, he had to cross an embankment, steep and treacherous; one day he slipped and fell, and his food was soiled with mud. From then on, the novice would set aside the unsoiled portion of his alms for his own teacher, and wash and eat the soiled portion himself. This happened not once, but repeatedly.

His teacher asked: why do you wash off and discard the food's own flavor like this? He answered: when I go out to beg, the sky is clear, but by the time I return, it rains — and, on the embankment, I fall, and my food is soiled. The teacher, reflecting on this in silence, understood that a dragon was troubling the novice, and rose at once to go to the embankment, striking at the reeds there with his own staff.

The dragon transformed itself into an old man, and came forward, bowing its head to the ground. The monk said: why do you trouble my own novice? It answered: I have truly come to love his own fine appearance. From this day forward, please come each day to eat at my own dwelling instead. The monk accepted this invitation, and went daily to eat there.

The novice, later, noticed two or three grains of rice in his own teacher's bowl that were unlike any food of this world, and asked his preceptor about them; the teacher said nothing in reply. The novice then went beneath the teacher's own seat, and took hold of its legs with his own hands; once the teacher's meditation had ended, he flew off, the novice still holding fast, and together they arrived above the dragon's own palace hall, where the dragon and its own wife bowed before the monk, and bowed before the novice as well.

The teacher, realizing what was happening, called the novice aside and told him: these are no true palace women at all — they are animals. You, as a novice, though not yet having attained the path, are certain to be reborn in Trāyastriṃśa heaven, a fate a hundred times better than this — do not let your own heart be defiled by such a thought.

The novice said: such a dragon's own dwelling is rarely seen in this world. The teacher said: it carries within it three sufferings. First, though its food holds every hundred flavors, the moment it enters the dragon's own mouth, it turns to toads. Second, though its consorts appear of matchless beauty, when dragons unite as husband and wife, it is in truth two serpents coupling together. Third, upon a dragon's own back grow reversed scales, beneath which sand and grit collect, causing pain that reaches straight to the heart and chest — this is truly a great suffering. Why, then, would you wish to follow such a path? You have not yet attained the path yourself, and ought not to be shown the realm of ghosts, nor the private affairs of kings.

The novice made no reply, but, day and night, could not stop thinking of what he had seen, and, pining for it, fell ill from refusing to eat, and died — his own spirit reborn as the dragon's own child (the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra says: through his own giving and precept-keeping, he had wished to become a dragon quickly, and, his resolve fixed on this, water rose from beneath his own feet; afterward, he went to the great pool where his teacher had first entered, covered his own head with his monastic robe, and entered the water; upon dying, he transformed at once into a great dragon spirit, and killed the former dragon, the whole pool turning red with blood — Source: Sūtra of Kāśyapa's Interrogation of Nanda; also found in the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 17).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The novice's own quiet, unasked-for act of protecting his teacher from soiled food — the detail that first draws suspicion and unravels the whole episode — is easy to overlook against the more dramatic dragon-palace scenes that follow, but it is worth noticing that his undoing begins precisely at the point where ordinary, unremarked-upon devotion gives way to curiosity about a world his own teacher explicitly warns him against. The parenthetical alternate ending preserved from the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra is notably more violent than the main account — deliberate self-drowning and the killing of the original dragon rather than death from mere heartsick refusal to eat — and we keep both versions rather than choosing between them, as the compendium's own citation practice does throughout.

11. The Novice Who Loved Cheese

Fascicle 22 closes with its shortest and quietest entry: a novice's small, ordinary fondness for the leftover cheese given to the Saṅgha follows him past death into rebirth as a worm inside the very jar he loved — recognized, and gently fed, by his own former teacher.

Chinese Source

如一沙彌。心常愛酪諸檀越餉僧酪。時沙彌每得殘分。心中著樂喜不離。命終之後生殘酪瓶中。沙彌師得阿羅漢。僧分酪。語言徐徐。莫傷此愛酪沙彌。諸人言。何以言愛酪沙彌。答言。此本是我沙彌。但坐貪愛殘酪故。生此瓶中。師得酪分。蟲在中來。師言。愛酪人汝何以來。即以酪與之(出大智論第十七卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was once a novice whose heart was constantly fond of cheese; whenever donors offered cheese to the Saṅgha, the novice would each time receive the leftover portion, and his own heart clung to this small pleasure, unable to let it go. When his own life ended, he was reborn inside the very jar that held the leftover cheese.

This novice's own teacher had, by then, attained arhatship. When the assembly divided up the cheese one day, he said: go slowly — do not harm this novice who so loved cheese. The others asked: what do you mean by "the novice who loved cheese"? He answered: this was once my own novice — it is only because of his own craving for the leftover cheese that he has been reborn here, inside this very jar.

When the teacher received his own portion of the cheese, the worm came forth within it. The teacher said: you who loved cheese so — why have you come to me now? — and gave the cheese to it at once (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 17).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Fascicle 22 opened with twin infants recalling their own past lives across countless kalpas and closes with a single worm inside a cheese jar — and the deliberate scale of that drop is the point: this final entry carries no dramatic reversal, no rescue, no teaching that resolves the worm's own condition, only a former teacher's quiet, undramatic tenderness toward what his own student's small, ordinary attachment has become. The compendium offers it without further comment, letting the sheer smallness of the cause sit beside the strangeness of its own consequence.
