The Hells, Part 1

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 49 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): "The Hells, Part 1" (地獄部上) — twelve entries, the compendium's turn from the six destinies to the lowest of them. Among them: King Yama, who took the post by a vow made after losing a war, and who is himself laid on a cauldron three times a day and night and has molten copper poured down his throat before returning to his palace women; the interrogation formula, in which the only question asked three times over is why the offender, having seen old age, sickness, and death, never thought that he too would come to that; four separate and mutually irreconcilable registers of the hells and their governors, printed one after another; a markedly Chinese account of the administrative machinery — patrols on the fast days, reports on the fifteenth and thirtieth, lifespans lengthened or shortened at review, and hell-ghosts who push a man into further evil when his death date has not yet arrived; a scholastic taxonomy sorting the cold hells by whether the sufferer can still form words; the eight great hells with their sixteen subsidiaries each, given as one unbroken forced march held together by the refrain that the offense is not yet finished and so he is not allowed to die; and the ten hells named after the colors of flowers, with an arithmetic of lifespans built to exceed what can be held in mind.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 49 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 49 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 49 is the first of the compendium's two fascicles on the hells, and it is almost entirely descriptive rather than narrative: registers, topographies, taxonomies, and one interrogation formula. Only one named individual appears in the whole of it, in the last sentence.

The fascicle contains four separate lists of the hells and their governors — eighteen in entry 4, thirty in entry 5, a hundred and thirty-four mentioned in an interlinear note to entry 1, and the eight-plus-sixteen scheme of entry 11 — and they cannot be reconciled with one another. We print all of them as the compendium does. The flower-named hells likewise appear twice, in entry 10 and entry 12, with different explanations of the names; both are kept.

Three entries — 5, 6, and 9 — come from the Sūtra of the Samādhi of Pure Deliverance and are the most Sinicized material in this translation: registers kept by spirits, six reports a month and four annual reviews, five officers named for the elements assigned to the five precepts, and patrol days shared with the Chinese calendar. We translate them in that register rather than assimilate them to the Indian material beside them.

On names: Yama, Vaiśravaṇa, Śakra, Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, Kokālika, Jambudvīpa, Avīci, and the Sanskrit hell-names that the Chinese transliterates — Arbuda, Nirarbuda, Aṭaṭa, Utpala, Kumuda, Sugandhi, Puṇḍarīka, Padma — are given in their standard forms. Hell-names that the Chinese translates rather than transliterates (Black Rope, Crushing, Wailing, Burning, Unremitting, Black Sand, Ash River, Sword Trees, Cold Ice, and the rest) we translate in turn. The thirty and eighteen governors of entries 4 and 5 have Chinese names with no Indic correspondence and are kept in pinyin; two of them the source itself has lost, and we note that where it does.

The heading of entry 7 is numbered 十 in the body of the text where the table of contents has 七 — evidently a slip in the source. We follow the table of contents and note it here.

This fascicle consists almost entirely of descriptions of torture, sustained over many pages. It is translated in full and without abridgment; the reading notes attend to how the passages are constructed and what offense each hell is assigned rather than commenting on the imagery.

The Hells, Part 1 地獄部上

Twelve entries: King Yama's own origin and his three daily torments, the three heavenly messengers, four separate registers of the hells and their governors, the administrative machinery of the six fast days, and the great topographies — the cold hells and the hot, the eight great hells with their sixteen subsidiaries each, and the ten hells named after flowers between the Vajra Mountains.

1. The Former Causes by Which King Yama and the Rest Became Officers of the Hells

Yama was a king who lost a war and made a vow about it, and his eighteen ministers and their million men made the same one.

Chinese Source

閻羅王者。昔為毘沙國王。緣與維陀始王共戰。兵力不敵。因立誓願。願為地獄主。臣佐十八人領百萬之眾頭有角耳皆悉忿懟。同立誓曰。後當奉助治此罪人。毘沙王者今閻羅是。十八人者諸小王是。百萬之眾諸阿傍是。隷北方毘沙門天王(出問地獄經淨度三昧經云總治一百三十四地獄)。

Ru-Yi Translation

King Yama was formerly the king of the country of Vaiśa, and, having fought with the king Vitarśi, his troops were not equal to it, and he therefore made a vow: may I become the lord of hell. And his eighteen ministers, leading a host of a million, all had horns on their heads and were full of resentment, and made this vow together: hereafter we shall serve and help in dealing with these offenders.

The king of Vaiśa is Yama now; the eighteen men are the lesser kings; the host of a million are the wardens; and they are subject to Vaiśravaṇa, heavenly king of the north (Source: Sūtra of Questions on Hell; the Sūtra of the Samādhi of Pure Deliverance says he governs a hundred and thirty-four hells in all).

2. King Yama Suffers Three Times a Day

The lord of the hells has a cauldron of his own, three times a day, and goes back to his palace women afterward.

Chinese Source

閻浮提南有大金剛山。內有閻羅王宮。縱廣六千由旬(問地獄經云。住地獄間。城縱廣三萬里金銀所成)晝夜三時有大銅鑊自然在前。若鑊入宮內。王見怖畏。捨出宮外。若鑊出宮外王入宮內。有大獄卒。臥王熱鑊上。鐵鉤擘口。洋銅灌之。從咽徹下無不燋爛。事竟還與婇人共相娛樂。彼諸大臣同受福者亦復如是(出長阿含經十九卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

South of Jambudvīpa there is a great Vajra Mountain, within which is King Yama’s palace, six thousand yojanas across (the Sūtra of Questions on Hell says: he dwells among the hells, and the city is thirty thousand li across, made of gold and silver).

Three times a day and night a great bronze cauldron appears of itself before him. If the cauldron comes inside the palace, the king sees it and is terrified and leaves and goes outside; if the cauldron goes outside the palace, the king goes inside. There is a great warden who lays the king on the hot cauldron and prises open his mouth with an iron hook and pours molten copper into it, and it passes from his throat all the way down and there is nothing not scorched and rotted.

When it is over he goes back and enjoys himself with the palace women; and those great ministers of his who share his fortune have the same (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 19).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two things are worth marking here. The first is that the lord of the hells is himself punished in them, on a fixed schedule three times a day and night, with molten copper poured down his throat by his own wardens; and the same is true of the ministers who share his position. The second is the sentence that follows: when it is over he goes back and enjoys himself with the palace women. The office carries both.

This should be read against entry 1, which supplies the reason: Yama took the post by vow, having lost a war, and the eighteen ministers and the million men took it with him. Nothing in either entry suggests the arrangement can be left.

3. King Yama Questions an Offender

The interrogation formula, given in full: three things the offender was shown while alive, and the same question three times over.

Chinese Source

有三使。一老。二病。三死。若有眾生三業行惡。身壞命終應墮地獄。王問言。汝是天使所召耶。王曰。汝見第一使者不。汝在人中。見頭白齒落目視矇矇。皮緩肌皺僂脊拄杖呻吟而行見此人不。罪人言見。王曰。汝何不自念。我亦當爾。罪人言。我時放逸不自覺知。王曰。今當令汝知放逸苦。非父母兄弟天帝先祖知識僮僕沙門等過。汝自作惡今當自受。又問汝見第二使不。汝本為人。頗見疾病困篤屎尿臭處身臥其上。飲食須人百節酸疼流淚呻吟不能語言不。答曰見。王問曰。何不自念(往反如第一使)又問覺第三使不。頗見人死身壞命終諸根永滅。身體挺直猶如枯木捐棄塚間鳥獸所食不。答曰見(往反如前)語已付獄卒(有眾生三世治惡經云。遣五使。五問。謂生老病死先身惡業)詣大地獄(出長阿含經第十九卷婁炭經大同小異)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are three messengers: the first old age, the second sickness, the third death. If a being does evil in the three kinds of action, then when his body breaks up and his life ends he should fall into hell; and the king asks him: were you summoned by the heavenly messengers?

The king says: did you not see the first messenger? Among humans, did you not see one whose head was white and whose teeth were fallen, whose eyes were dim, whose skin was slack and flesh wrinkled, bent at the back and leaning on a staff and groaning as he went — did you see such a person? The offender says: I saw him. The king says: why did you not think, I too shall come to that? The offender says: I was careless at the time and did not notice it of myself.

The king says: I shall now let you know the suffering of carelessness. It is not the fault of father or mother, brothers, Śakra, ancestors, acquaintances, servants, or śramaṇas. You did the evil yourself and shall now receive it yourself.

He asks again: did you not see the second messenger? When you were a human, did you not see one gravely ill and hard pressed, lying in his own filth and stench, needing others for food and drink, aching in every joint, weeping and groaning and unable to speak? He answers: I saw him. The king asks: why did you not think of yourself? (and so back and forth as with the first messenger)

He asks again: were you aware of the third messenger? Did you not see a person dead, his body broken up and his life ended, all his faculties forever extinguished, his body stiff and straight like a withered tree, thrown out among the graves and eaten by birds and beasts? He answers: I saw him (and so back and forth as before).

When he has spoken, he hands him over to the wardens (the Sūtra on the Three Ages of Beings’ Punishment for Evil says: he sends five messengers and asks five questions — birth, old age, sickness, death, and the evil deeds of the former body), and he goes to the great hell (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 19; the Lokasthāna Sūtra is roughly the same with minor differences).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three messengers — old age, sickness, and death — are the whole of this passage and the interrogation follows one formula three times. Each time Yama asks whether the offender saw the sign, each time the answer is yes, and each time the question is: why did you not think, I too shall come to that?

The reply the offender gives to the first is the one the entry turns on: I was careless at the time and did not notice it of myself. And Yama's answer to that is a list of everyone who is not at fault — father, mother, brothers, Śakra, ancestors, acquaintances, servants, śramaṇas — ending with: you did the evil yourself and shall now receive it yourself. The messengers were not sent; they were simply there to be seen.

The compiler notes in passing that another sūtra gives five messengers rather than three, adding birth and one's own former evil deeds.

4. The Eighteen Hells and the Names of Their Governors

A register: eighteen lesser kings named, each with the hell in his charge, and two whose names the source has lost.

Chinese Source

十八小王者。一迦延典泥犁。二屈遵典刀山。三沸[這-言+(大/隹)]壽典沸沙。四沸典典沸屎。五迦世典黑耳。六[嶬-我+皿]傞典火車。七湯謂典鑊湯。八鐵迦然典鐵床。九惡生典[嶬-我+皿]山。十寒氷(經闕王名)十一毘迦典剝皮。十二逕頭典畜。十三提薄典刀兵。十四夷大典鐵磨。十五悅頭典氷地獄。十六鐵笧(經闕王名)十七身典蛆蟲。十八觀身典[金*羊]銅(出問地獄經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eighteen lesser kings are: first, Jiāyán, who has charge of the niraya; second, Qūzūn, of the mountain of knives; third, Fèixūnshòu, of the boiling sand; fourth, Fèidiǎn, of the boiling filth; fifth, Jiāshì, of the Black-Ear; sixth, Yícuō, of the fire-cart; seventh, Tāngwèi, of the cauldron of boiling water; eighth, Tiějiārán, of the iron couch; ninth, Èshēng, of the Yí Mountain; tenth, Cold Ice (the sūtra omits the king’s name); eleventh, Píjiā, of the flaying; twelfth, Jīngtóu, of the animals; thirteenth, Tíbáo, of the blades and weapons; fourteenth, Yídà, of the iron mill; fifteenth, Yuètóu, of the ice hell; sixteenth, the Iron Cage (the sūtra omits the king’s name); seventeenth, Shēn, of the maggots; eighteenth, Guānshēn, of the molten copper (Source: Sūtra of Questions on Hell).

5. The Thirty Hells and the Names of Their Governors

A longer register: thirty kings and thirty hells, followed by an account of the reporting schedule — six times a month, four times a year.

Chinese Source

一曰平潮王。典主阿鼻大泥犁。二曰晉平王。典治黑繩重獄。三曰荼都王。典治鐵臼獄。四曰輔天王。典治合會獄。五曰聖都王。典治大山獄。六曰玄都王。典治火城獄。七曰廣武王。主治劍樹獄。八曰武陽王。典主嚾吼獄。九曰平陽王。主治八路獄。十曰都陽王。典治刺樹獄。十一消陽王。主治沸灰獄。十二挺慰王。典治大噭獄小噭獄。十三廣進王。主大阿鼻獄。十四高都王。主治鐵車獄。十五公陽王。主治鐵火獄。十六平解王。主治沸屎獄。十七柱陽王。主治燒地獄。十八平丘王。典治彌離獄。十九䃛石王。主治山石獄。二十琅耶王。主治多洹獄。二十一都官王。主治泥犁獄。二十二玄錫王。主治飛蟲獄。二十三太一王。主治陽阿獄。二十四合石王。主治大磨獄。二十五涼無王。主治寒雪獄。二十六無原王。主治鐵杵獄。二十七政治王。主治鐵柱獄。二十八高遠王。主治膿血獄。二十九都進王。主治燒石獄三十原都王。主治鐵輪獄。是為三十大苦劇泥犁。神明聽察疏記罪福不問尊卑。一月六奏一歲四覆。四覆之日皆用八王。八王日者天王案行以比諸天民隷之屬。有福增壽有罪減算。制命長短毛分不差。人民盲冥了自不知。不預作善收付地獄(出淨度三昧經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, King Píngcháo, who has charge of the great niraya Avīci. Second, King Jìnpíng, of the heavy hell of the Black Rope. Third, King Túdū, of the Iron Mortar hell. Fourth, King Fǔtiān, of the Coming-Together hell. Fifth, King Shèngdū, of the Great Mountain hell. Sixth, King Xuándū, of the Fire City hell. Seventh, King Guǎngwǔ, of the Sword Tree hell. Eighth, King Wǔyáng, of the Roaring hell. Ninth, King Píngyáng, of the Eight Roads hell. Tenth, King Dūyáng, of the Thorn Tree hell.

Eleventh, King Xiāoyáng, of the Boiling Ash hell. Twelfth, King Tǐngwèi, of the Great Crying and Small Crying hells. Thirteenth, King Guǎngjìn, of the Great Avīci hell. Fourteenth, King Gāodū, of the Iron Cart hell. Fifteenth, King Gōngyáng, of the Iron Fire hell. Sixteenth, King Píngjiě, of the Boiling Filth hell. Seventeenth, King Zhùyáng, of the Burning hell. Eighteenth, King Píngqiū, of the Mílí hell. Nineteenth, King Zhūshí, of the Mountain Stone hell. Twentieth, King Lángyé, of the Duōhuán hell.

Twenty-first, King Dūguān, of the niraya. Twenty-second, King Xuánxī, of the Flying Insects hell. Twenty-third, King Tàiyī, of the Yáng’ē hell. Twenty-fourth, King Héshí, of the Great Mill hell. Twenty-fifth, King Liángwú, of the Cold Snow hell. Twenty-sixth, King Wúyuán, of the Iron Pestle hell. Twenty-seventh, King Zhèngzhì, of the Iron Pillar hell. Twenty-eighth, King Gāoyuǎn, of the Pus and Blood hell. Twenty-ninth, King Dūjìn, of the Burning Stone hell. Thirtieth, King Yuándū, of the Iron Wheel hell.

These are the thirty nirayas of great and bitter torment. The spirits listen and observe and set down in the registers offense and merit and do not ask who is honoured and who is base. Six times a month they report and four times a year they review; and on the days of review they all use the eight kings. The days of the eight kings are those on which the heavenly kings make their circuit and compare the gods and the common people who are their subjects: those with merit have their years increased and those with offense have their reckoning cut short, and the setting of the length of life does not err by the breadth of a hair.

The people are blind and dark and do not know it of themselves; and if they do not do good beforehand they are taken and handed over to hell (Source: Sūtra of the Samādhi of Pure Deliverance).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Entries 4 and 5 are two registers of the hells and their governors from two different sources, and they do not agree — eighteen hells in one and thirty in the other, with different names, different governors, and only a partial overlap of the hells themselves. Entry 1 adds a third figure in an interlinear note, a hundred and thirty-four hells in all. The compendium prints all of them.

What is distinctive about entry 5 is the second half, which is not a list at all but an administrative account, and a markedly Chinese one: registers kept by spirits who do not ask who is honoured and who is base, six reports a month, four reviews a year, and lifespans lengthened or shortened at each review without erring by the breadth of a hair. Its source, the Sūtra of the Samādhi of Pure Deliverance, supplies three of this fascicle's twelve entries and is the most bureaucratic strand in it.

6. The Five Officers Who Prohibit People from Offending

One sentence and five officers, one for each of the five precepts.

Chinese Source

五官者。一鮮官禁殺。二水官禁盜。三鐵官禁婬。四土官禁兩舌。五天官禁酒(出淨度三昧經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The five officers are: the first, the Fresh Officer, who prohibits killing; the second, the Water Officer, who prohibits stealing; the third, the Iron Officer, who prohibits lust; the fourth, the Earth Officer, who prohibits double-tongued speech; the fifth, the Heaven Officer, who prohibits liquor (Source: Sūtra of the Samādhi of Pure Deliverance).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The shortest entry in the fascicle and the most transparently Chinese in its framing. The five precepts are assigned to five officers named after the elements and the two great powers — Fresh, Water, Iron, Earth, and Heaven — each of whom prohibits one thing. The scheme has no Indian counterpart and belongs to the same Sinicizing strand as entry 5's registers and entry 9's fast-day patrols.

7. First Receiving Birth in Hell

What the intermediate-state body is, where it waits, and the three winds that shape the body it will receive.

Chinese Source

閻羅王城之東西南面列諸地獄。有日月光而不明淨。唯黑耳獄光所不照。人命終時神生中陰。中陰者已捨死陰未及生陰。其罪人者乘中陰身。入泥犁城。泥犁城者(梁言寄係城又云閉城也)是諸罪人。未受罪之間共聚是處。巧風所吹隨業輕重受大小身。臭風所吹成就罪人麁醜之形。香風所吹成就福人微細之體(出問地獄經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

On the east, west, and south sides of King Yama’s city the hells are set out in rows. There is light of sun and moon there, but it is not bright and clear; and the Black-Ear hell alone the light does not reach.

When a person’s life ends, the spirit is born into the intermediate state; the intermediate state is where one has already given up the dying aggregate and has not yet reached the born aggregate. Those offenders ride the intermediate-state body and enter the niraya city; the niraya city (which in the language of Liang is the City of Detention, also called the Closed City) is where all these offenders gather before receiving their punishment.

Blown by the skilful wind, they receive a large or a small body according to the weight of their deeds; blown by the stinking wind, the coarse and ugly form of an offender is accomplished; blown by the fragrant wind, the subtle body of one who has merit is accomplished (Source: Sūtra of Questions on Hell).

8. Those Who Should Be Born in Heaven and Those Who Fall into Hell Have Those Who Come to Receive Them, and See the Good or Evil Place at the End

What the dying see coming for them, which differs according to what they have done.

Chinese Source

生天墮地獄。各有迎人。人病欲死時。眼自見來迎。應生天上者天人持天衣伎樂來迎。應生他方者眼見尊人為說妙言。應墮地獄者眼見兵士持刀楯矛戟索圍遶之。所見不同。口不能言。各隨所作得其果報。天無枉濫。平直無二。隨其所作天網治之(出淨度三昧經。華嚴經云。人欲終時見中陰相。行惡業者見三惡受苦。或見閻羅持諸兵仗囚執將去。或聞苦聲。若行善者見諸天宮殿伎女莊嚴遊戲快樂如是等勝事)。

Ru-Yi Translation

For those born in heaven and those who fall into hell alike there are those who come to receive them. When a person is ill and about to die, their eyes see them coming to receive them: one who is to be born in heaven sees gods holding heavenly clothing and making music come to receive them; one who is to be born in another quarter sees an honoured person speaking fine words to them; one who is to fall into hell sees soldiers holding knives and shields, spears and halberds and ropes, surrounding them. What they see is not the same, and their mouths cannot speak of it.

Each receives the fruit of what they have done. Heaven does not wrong or overreach; it is level and straight and without a second face, and treats them by the net of heaven according to what they have done (Source: Sūtra of the Samādhi of Pure Deliverance. The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: when a person is about to die they see the marks of the intermediate state — one who has done evil deeds sees the three evil destinies and their suffering, or sees Yama holding weapons and taking them away in custody, or hears sounds of suffering; and one who has done good sees the palaces of the gods and their entertainers adorned and disporting themselves in delight, and other excellent things of this kind).

9. The Messengers of the Eight Kings Inspect Good and Evil on the Six Fast Days

The bureaucracy of the fast days: who patrols, who reports, what is added to a lifespan and what is struck from it — and what the hell-ghosts do when a death date has not yet arrived.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The eight kings are: Śakra, lord of the gods, with his thirty-two garrison ministers; the four great garrison kings; the Director of Fate and the Director of Records; the five great rākṣasa kings; and the messengers of the eight kings. They all go out and spread through the four quarters on their circuit.

And they meet again with the four kings’ reports on the fifteenth and the thirtieth, examining and comparing the people and setting down their conduct as good or evil; and the king of hell too sends his assistant ministers and lesser kings out at the same time, and whatever is offending is set down at once.

If at the earlier fast, on one of the eight kings’ days, a person has offended but their merit is strong, they are saved and are safe and there is nothing else, and their merit is used to pardon them. If at the later fast day they offend again and their offenses are many, their lifespan is reduced and their name is entered and their death is fixed, and the year, month, day, and hour are sent down to hell.

Hell receives the document and at once sends the hell-ghosts to take the name from the register and summon them; and the hell-ghosts have no kindness, and if the day of death has not yet come they force them to do evil so that their life may be quickly used up.

For one whose merit is great, their lifespan is increased and their reckoning added to, and heaven sends good spirits to guard and protect their person, and moves down to hell to strike out their offending name; the death is removed and life is fixed, and afterward they are born in heaven (Source: Sūtra of the Samādhi of Pure Deliverance).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The most administratively detailed entry in the fascicle, and it is worth reading as a description of a system rather than as a threat. Patrols go out on fixed days; reports come in on the fifteenth and thirtieth; hell sends its own assistant ministers out at the same time; offenses are entered at once. A first offense with strong merit is pardoned out of the merit; a second brings a reduction of lifespan, an entry of the name, and a document sent down to hell with the year, month, day, and hour on it.

The line that lifts it out of routine is what the hell-ghosts do on receiving the summons: having no kindness, if the day of death has not yet come they force the person to do more evil so that the life may be used up faster. The system is not merely predictive; its lower officers have an interest in the outcome.

Ru-Yi Translation

What is hell? The answer: the cold, the hot, and the border hells. They are of measureless kinds, and I shall now set them out in brief.

The cold hells are: those with articulate crying, those with inarticulate crying, and those without crying — these three marks of the most extreme cries of misery.

Of those with articulate crying there are three: first Arbuda, second Nirarbuda, third Aṭaṭa. In the Arbuda hell, from the cold there arise on the body things like boils. In the Nirarbuda hell the wind blows and they swell up and are full. In the Aṭaṭa the extreme cold wind blows and strips the skin from their bodies and the flesh all falls away; and all of them shiver violently and cry out. These three can be made out.

Of those with inarticulate crying there are two: first Hahava-hahava, second Utpala. In the Hahava-hahava, likewise the extreme cold wind blows and the skin and flesh are stripped away, and they cry hahava-hahava. In the Utpala, an extremely great cold wind blows and strips the skin and flesh of the body, and the iron leaves within the body wrap themselves round the body like an utpala flower. It is by slandering the sages that one falls into this hell.

Of those without crying there are four: first Kumuda, second Sugandhi, third Puṇḍarīka, fourth Padma. The extreme cold wind blows and their bodies swell and are full; and, having these four marks, they receive suffering and groan. It is by slandering the sages that they fall into these four hells, and at all times receive measureless suffering.

All these great cold hells are placed between the four continents, at the base of the Iron Ring Mountains, facing upward, dwelling in darkness. The cold wind ruins their bodies and a great fire burns them, as when a bamboo grove burns and the sounds crack and strike against one another. And there are other beings in them receiving suffering, all of whom slandered and reviled the sages and receive suffering like this. As the World-Honored One said in verse:

“In Nirarbuda there are a hundred thousand, / in Arbuda thirty-five; / reviling the sages, the hell of evil destinies / comes of the evil wishes of mouth and mind.”

This is called the cold hells. Question: what of the hot hells? Answer: first, those with a governor to deal with them; second, those with a lesser governor; third, those with no governor. These three marks of dealing are the beating and the examination.

Of those with a governor there are: first Reviving, second Ranks, third Black Rope. In the Reviving hell the wardens with sharp knives and axes dismember and flay and chop and hack the offending beings as one hacks the heads of grass; and the skin and flesh come apart, and, the conditions of the offense not being exhausted, a cold wind blows and they grow back as before. And by the further cause of their evil, iron claws grow on their hands, sharp as knives, and each of them grows resentful and they strike and cut one another as one mows bamboo and reeds. Because they nursed resentment against one another until they died, they are born here.

In the Black Rope hell they lay the offenders on the ground and mark them with a black cord and hack and cut them. Because they hacked at beings they are born in that hell.

And further, with hot red copper they wrap the body and the bones break and the marrow and blood run. Because they laid whip and rod on beings and on renunciants, and were not diligent and yet received and wore the robes given in faith, they are born there. And in the extreme dark it is worse than the smoke; they hang the body upside down and make it breathe the smoke. This is because they smoked out beings dwelling in holes.

In the Ranks hell they set out the offending beings in rows as in a slaughterhouse, and cut off hands, feet, ears, nose, and head. It is because they were butchers that they receive this suffering. And further, on the hot iron ground they harness iron fire-carts, and the wardens ride them and stare and shout and roar, driving them to run. Because they rode elephants and horses and drove them to exhaustion, they fall into this. Those who committed adultery with others’ wives are driven up the sword trees and burned by a natural fire and receive suffering like this. As for the wardens of the hells with governors, because of the conditions of their conduct they are not burned by the fire.

Of the hells with a lesser governor there are: first Crushing, second Great Wailing, third Iron Roasting. In the Crushing hell the offending beings fear the wardens and run by the hundred thousand into the mountains, and a fire arises of itself before and behind them, and the two mountains come together of themselves like a mill, and the blood flows like a river and the bones and flesh are entirely crushed. It is because they delighted in grinding down beings. And further, they burn a great iron mortar and pound them with a pestle, and though a hundred years pass, by the conditions of their offense their lives are not exhausted. It is because they pounded and killed fleas and lice in a mortar, and killed by pressing.

In the Great Wailing hell great iron mountains burn all round and there is no way out on any of the four sides, and the evil wardens have no kindness and say in rage: where do you mean to go? And with burning iron pestles they strike and break their heads. It is because they made the people suffer that they are born in this.

In the Roasting hell the great iron mountains and their flames strike against one another, and they turn them with iron spatulas and roast them all round; and when one side is done the spatula turns of itself and they are turned over and over. It is because they killed people by impaling them that they are born in this.

In the Unremitting hell the iron ground burns all round for a hundred yojanas across, with four gates as in a city, covered above with thin copper, flame following flame; and the offending beings are heaped up like firewood, and the flame is unobstructed and scorches and rots their bodies and they receive suffering without remission. It is because they killed father and mother or a true one, and with an evil mind toward the Buddha caused blood to flow, and set the community of monks at strife, and did the ten unwholesome deeds in a heightened degree, that they are born in this.

The border hells are in the space between houses and waters, among the mountains, and in the wilderness, where one receives the recompense of evil deeds alone. These are called the border hells (Source: the Robes chapter, fascicle 3).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A properly scholastic taxonomy, and the classifying principle is unusual and exact. The cold hells are sorted by what sound the sufferer can still make — three where the cry is articulate (Arbuda, Nirarbuda, Aṭaṭa), two where it is not (Hahava, Utpala), and four where there is no cry at all (Kumuda, Sugandhi, Puṇḍarīka, Padma) — a scale of increasing cold measured by the progressive loss of speech.

The last four are named for flowers, and the reason is grim: the body swells and splits in the cold until it opens like a blossom. The same flower-names recur in entry 12 as a separate set of ten hells, where they are explained instead by color.

The offense assigned to the cold hells throughout is a single one, repeated: slandering and reviling the sages. The hot hells, by contrast, are matched to their offenses individually and concretely — butchers to the Ranks hell, those who drove animals to exhaustion to the fire-carts, those who pounded fleas and lice in a mortar to the mortar, those who smoked out burrowing creatures to the smoke — and the border hells, at the close, are defined by nothing but solitude: anywhere among houses, waters, mountains, or wilderness where one receives the recompense of one's own deeds alone.

四天下外有八萬天下而圍遶之。八萬天下外復有大海。海外復有大金剛山。山外復有山亦名金剛(樓炭經云大鐵圍山)二山中間日月神天威光不照。有八大地獄。一曰想。二曰黑繩。三曰椎[石*甲]。四曰叫喚。五曰大叫喚。六曰燒炙。七曰大燒炙。八曰無間(樓炭經云有不同文多不載也)其一地獄。各有十六小地獄(問地獄經云。地獄者地上有也)。想地獄十六者。一曰黑沙。二曰沸屎。三曰五百釘。四曰飢。五曰渴。六曰一銅釜。七曰多銅釜。八曰石磨。九曰膿血。十曰量火。十一曰灰河。十二曰鐵丸。十三曰釿斧。十四曰犲狼。十五曰劍樹。十六曰寒氷。其中眾生。手生鐵抓迭相瞋忿。以爪相攫應手肉墮。想以為死。復次其中眾生懷毒害想。手執刀劍迭相斫刺。剝臠割身碎在地。相謂為死。冷風來吹尋復活起。彼自相言我今已活。久受罪已出想地獄。慞惶求救不覺忽到黑沙地獄。熱風暴起吹熱黑沙來著其身。燒皮徹骨身中焰起。迴旋還身燒炙燋爛。其罪未畢故使不死。久受苦已出黑沙獄到沸屎獄。有沸屎鐵丸自然滿前。驅迫罪人使抱鐵丸燒其身手。復使撮著口中。從咽至腹通徹下過無不燋爛。有鐵[口*(隹/乃)]蟲唼肉達髓苦毒無量。受罪未畢復不肯死。久受苦已出沸屎獄附到鐵釘獄。卒撲之偃熱鐵上。舒展其身以釘釘手足。周遍身體盡五百釘。苦毒號吟猶復不死。久受苦已出鐵釘獄到飢餓獄。即撲熱鐵上銷銅灌口。從咽至腹通徹下過無不燋爛。餘罪未盡猶復不死。久受苦已出飢地獄到渴地獄。即撲熱鐵上以熱鐵丸著其口中。燒其唇舌通徹下過無不燋爛苦毒啼哭。久受苦已出渴地獄到一銅鍑地獄獄卒怒目捉罪人足倒投鍑中。隨湯涌沸上下迴旋。身壞爛熟萬毒並至故令不死。久受苦已出一銅鍑至多銅鍑地獄。捉罪人足倒投鍑中。隨湯涌沸上下迴旋舉身壞爛。以鐵鉤取置餘鍑中。悲叫苦毒故使不死。久受苦已出多銅鍑至石磨地獄。捉彼罪人撲熱石上。舒展手足以大熱石[石*甲]其身上。迴轉揩磨骨肉糜碎。苦毒切痛故使不死。久受苦已出石磨獄至膿血地獄。膿血沸涌罪人於中東西馳走。湯其身體頭面爛壞。又取膿血食之。通徹下過苦毒難忍故令不死。久受苦已乃出膿血至量火地獄。有大火聚其火炎熾驅迫罪人。手執鐵升以量火聚。遍燒身體苦毒熱痛。吟呻號哭故令不死。久受苦已出量火獄到灰河地獄。縱廣深淺各五百由旬。灰湯涌沸惡氣漨渤迴波相搏聲響可畏。從底至上鐵刺縱橫。其河岸上有劍樹林。枝葉華實皆是刀劍。罪人入河隨波上下。迴覆沈沒鐵刺刺身。內外通徹膿血流出。苦痛萬端故令不死。乃出灰河至彼岸上。利劍割刺身體傷破。復有犲狼來嚙罪人生食其肉。走上劍樹劍刃下向。下劍樹時劍刃上向。手攀手絕足蹋足斷。皮肉墮落唯有白骨筋脈相連。時劍樹上有鐵嘴烏。啄頭食腦苦毒號叫故令不死。還入灰河隨波沈沒。鐵刺刺身苦毒萬端。皮肉爛壞膿血流出。唯有白骨浮漂於外。冷風來吹尋便起立。宿對所牽不覺忽至鐵丸地獄。有熱鐵丸獄鬼驅投之。手足爛壞舉身火然。萬毒並至故令不死。久受苦已乃出鐵丸至釿斧地獄。捉此罪人撲熱鐵上。以熱鐵釿斧斫其手足耳鼻身體。苦毒號叫猶復不死。久受罪已出釿斧獄至犲狼地獄。有群犲狼競來䶩嚙。肉墮骨傷膿血流出。苦痛萬端故令不死。久受苦已乃出犲狼至劍樹獄。入彼劍林有暴風起。吹劍樹葉墮其身上。頭面身體無不傷壞。有鐵嘴烏啄其兩目。苦痛悲號故使不死。久受苦已乃出劍樹至寒氷獄。有大寒風吹其身上。舉體凍傷皮肉墮落。苦毒叫喚然後命終。身為不善口意亦然。斯墮想地獄怖懼毛竪。黑繩大地獄。有十六小獄周匝圍遶。各縱廣五百由旬。何故名黑繩。其諸獄卒捉彼罪人撲熱鐵上。舒展其身以熱鐵繩絣之使直。以熱鐵斧逐繩道斫罪人作百千段。復次以鐵繩絣鋸之。復次懸熱鐵繩交橫無數。驅迫罪人使行繩間。惡風暴起吹諸鐵繩歷絡其身。燒皮徹肉燋骨沸髓苦毒萬端。餘罪未畢故使不死。故名黑繩。久受苦已乃出黑繩至黑沙地獄。乃至寒氷地獄然後命終。惡意向父母佛及聲聞。則墮黑繩獄苦痛不可稱。推[石*甲]大地獄。有十六小獄圍遶。各縱廣五百由旬。何故名推[石*甲]。有大石山兩山相對。人入此中。山自然合推[石*甲]其身。骨肉糜碎山還故處。苦毒萬端故使不死。復有大鐵象舉身火然哮呼而來。蹴蹋罪人婉轉其上。身體糜碎膿血流出。號咷悲叫故使不死。復捉罪人臥大石上以大石[石*甲]。復取罪人臥地鐵杵擣之。從足至頭皮肉糜碎。膿血流出萬毒並至。餘罪未畢故令不死故名推[石*甲]。久受苦已乃出推[石*甲]到黑沙地獄。乃至寒氷然後命終。但造三惡業不修三善行。墮推[石*甲]地獄苦痛不可稱。叫喚大地獄。有十六小地獄。各縱廣五百由旬。何故名叫喚獄。獄卒捉罪人擲大鑊中。又置大鐵鍑中。熱湯涌沸煮彼罪人。號咷叫喚苦痛辛酸。又取彼罪人擲大鏊上。反覆煎熬號咷叫喚。餘罪未畢故使不死故名叫喚。久受苦已乃出叫喚至黑沙地獄。乃至寒氷爾乃命終。瞋恚懷毒造諸惡行。墮叫喚地獄。大叫喚大地獄。有十六小獄。何名大叫喚。取彼罪人著大鐵釜中。又置鐵鑊中。熱湯涌沸煮彼罪人。又擲大鏊上。反覆煎熬號咷大叫苦痛辛酸。餘罪未畢 Ru-Yi Translation

Outside the four continents there are eighty thousand lands that surround them; outside the eighty thousand lands there is again a great sea; outside the sea there is again a great Vajra Mountain; and outside that mountain there is again a mountain, also named Vajra (the Lokasthāna Sūtra says the great Iron Ring Mountain). Between the two mountains the majestic light of the gods of sun and moon does not shine.

There are eight great hells: the first Conception, the second Black Rope, the third Crushing, the fourth Wailing, the fifth Great Wailing, the sixth Burning, the seventh Great Burning, the eighth Unremitting (the Lokasthāna Sūtra has differences; the text is long and not set down here). Each one of these hells has sixteen lesser hells (the Sūtra of Questions on Hell says: the hells are above the ground).

The sixteen of the Conception hell are: first Black Sand, second Boiling Filth, third Five Hundred Nails, fourth Hunger, fifth Thirst, sixth One Copper Cauldron, seventh Many Copper Cauldrons, eighth Stone Mill, ninth Pus and Blood, tenth Measuring Fire, eleventh Ash River, twelfth Iron Balls, thirteenth Adze and Axe, fourteenth Jackals and Wolves, fifteenth Sword Trees, sixteenth Cold Ice.

The beings in it grow iron claws on their hands and rage at one another, and claw at one another so that at the touch of a hand the flesh falls away; and they conceive that they are dead. And further, the beings in it, conceiving thoughts of poison and harm, take up knives and swords and hack and stab one another and flay and slice and cut, and their bodies fall to pieces on the ground, and they say to one another that they are dead — and a cold wind comes and blows and they presently revive and rise, and say to one another: we are alive again.

Having received punishment a long while they come out of the Conception hell, and in their panic and search for rescue they come unawares to the Black Sand hell. A hot wind rises violently and blows hot black sand upon their bodies, burning the skin through to the bone, and flames arise within the body and turn back on it and scorch and rot it; and, their offense not being finished, they are not allowed to die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Black Sand hell to the Boiling Filth hell, where boiling filth and iron balls appear of themselves before them, and the offenders are driven and pressed to embrace the iron balls, which burn their bodies and hands, and are then made to take them into their mouths, and from the throat to the belly they pass all the way down and there is nothing not scorched and rotted; and iron-beaked insects bite the flesh through to the marrow, and the bitter poison of it is measureless. Their punishment not being finished, again they are not allowed to die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Boiling Filth hell and come to the Iron Nails hell, where the wardens throw them down on their backs on hot iron and stretch out their bodies and nail their hands and feet with nails, five hundred nails all over the body; and in the bitterness of it they cry and groan and still do not die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Iron Nails hell to the Hunger hell, where they are thrown down on hot iron and molten copper is poured into their mouths, and from the throat to the belly it passes all the way down and there is nothing not scorched and rotted; and, the remainder of their offense not being exhausted, again they do not die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Hunger hell to the Thirst hell, where they are thrown down on hot iron and hot iron balls are put in their mouths, burning their lips and tongues and passing all the way down and scorching and rotting everything, and in the bitterness of it they weep and cry.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Thirst hell to the One Copper Cauldron hell, where the wardens glare and take the offender by the feet and throw him head down into the cauldron, and he turns up and down with the boiling water and his body is broken and rotted and cooked; and ten thousand poisons come upon him at once and so he does not die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the One Copper Cauldron to the Many Copper Cauldrons hell, where they take the offender by the feet and throw him head down into a cauldron, and he turns up and down with the boiling water and his whole body is broken and rotted, and with an iron hook they take him and put him into another cauldron; and he cries out in bitterness and so does not die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Many Copper Cauldrons to the Stone Mill hell, where they take that offender and throw him down on hot stone and stretch out his hands and feet, and with a great hot stone press upon his body and turn it and grind him, and the bones and flesh are ground to pulp; and the bitter pain of it is sharp and so he does not die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Stone Mill hell to the Pus and Blood hell, where pus and blood boil up and the offenders run east and west in it and their bodies and heads and faces are scalded and rotted; and they take the pus and blood and eat it, and it passes all the way down, and the bitterness of it is hard to bear and so they do not die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Pus and Blood to the Measuring Fire hell, where there is a great mass of fire blazing fiercely, and the offenders are driven and pressed to take an iron measure in their hands and measure out the mass of fire, which burns their bodies all over with bitter and burning pain; and they groan and cry aloud and so do not die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Measuring Fire hell to the Ash River hell, five hundred yojanas across and deep, where the ash-water boils up and an evil vapour surges, and the returning waves strike one another with a sound that is dreadful to hear; and from the bottom up iron spikes run crosswise. On the river’s bank there is a grove of sword trees whose branches, leaves, flowers, and fruit are all knives and swords.

The offenders go into the river and rise and fall with the waves and are turned over and sink, and the iron spikes pierce their bodies through, inside and out, and pus and blood flow out; and the bitter pain of it is of ten thousand kinds and so they do not die. And they come out of the ash river onto the far bank, and the sharp swords cut and stab and their bodies are wounded and broken; and jackals and wolves come and bite the offenders and eat their flesh alive.

They run up the sword trees and the sword blades point downward; and when they come down the sword trees the blades point upward. They grasp with their hands and their hands are severed; they tread with their feet and their feet are cut off; the skin and flesh fall away and only the white bones are left with the sinews joining them. And at that time there are iron-beaked crows in the sword trees which peck their heads and eat their brains; and in the bitterness of it they cry out and so do not die.

They go back into the ash river and sink with the waves, and the iron spikes pierce their bodies with ten thousand kinds of bitterness, and the skin and flesh rot away and pus and blood flow out and only the white bones float on the surface; and a cold wind comes and blows and they presently stand up again. Drawn by their old debts, they come unawares to the Iron Balls hell, where there are hot iron balls and the hell-ghosts drive them and throw them at them, and their hands and feet rot away and their whole bodies take fire; and ten thousand poisons come at once and so they do not die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Iron Balls to the Adze and Axe hell, where they take this offender and throw him down on hot iron and with hot iron adzes and axes hack at his hands and feet and ears and nose and body; and in the bitterness of it he cries out and still does not die.

Having received punishment a long while they come out of the Adze and Axe hell to the Jackals and Wolves hell, where a pack of jackals and wolves come vying to bite him, and the flesh falls away and the bones are wounded and pus and blood flow out; and the bitter pain of it is of ten thousand kinds and so he does not die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Jackals and Wolves to the Sword Trees hell; and going into that sword forest a violent wind arises and blows the leaves of the sword trees down upon their bodies, and head and face and body are all wounded and broken, and iron-beaked crows peck out both their eyes; and in the bitterness and grief of it they cry and so do not die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Sword Trees to the Cold Ice hell, where a great cold wind blows upon their bodies and their whole frames are frozen and injured and the skin and flesh fall away; and in the bitterness of it they cry out, and only then do their lives end. Their bodies did what was not good, and their mouths and minds likewise; and so they fall into the Conception hell, and in their terror the hair stands on end.

The great Black Rope hell has sixteen lesser hells surrounding it all round, each five hundred yojanas across. Why is it named Black Rope? Its wardens take that offender and throw him down on hot iron and stretch out his body and mark him straight with a hot iron cord, and with a hot iron axe hack the offender along the line of the cord into a hundred thousand pieces. And further, with an iron cord they mark him and saw him. And further, they hang hot iron cords crosswise beyond number and drive and press the offenders to walk between the cords; and an evil wind rises violently and blows the iron cords all over their bodies, burning the skin through the flesh and scorching the bone and boiling the marrow, with ten thousand kinds of bitterness; and, the remainder of their offense not being finished, they are not allowed to die. Therefore it is named Black Rope.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Black Rope to the Black Sand hell, and so on to the Cold Ice hell, and then their lives end. Whoever has an evil mind toward father and mother, toward the Buddha, or toward the hearers falls into the Black Rope hell, and the bitterness of it cannot be told.

The great Crushing hell has sixteen lesser hells surrounding it, each five hundred yojanas across. Why is it named Crushing? There are great stone mountains, two mountains facing each other, and when a person enters between them the mountains come together of themselves and crush his body, and the bones and flesh are ground to pulp, and the mountains return to their places; and the bitterness of it is of ten thousand kinds and so he does not die. And there are great iron elephants whose whole bodies are aflame, who come roaring and trample the offenders and roll them beneath them, and their bodies are ground to pulp and pus and blood flow out; and they cry out and wail and so do not die. And they take the offender and lay him on a great stone and press him with a great stone; and they take the offender and lay him on the ground and pound him with an iron pestle, and from foot to head the skin and flesh are ground to pulp and pus and blood flow out, and ten thousand poisons come at once; and, the remainder of the offense not being finished, they are not allowed to die. Therefore it is named Crushing.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Crushing to the Black Sand hell, and so on to the Cold Ice, and only then do their lives end. Those who make only the three evil kinds of action and do not cultivate the three good practices fall into the Crushing hell, and the bitterness of it cannot be told.

The great Wailing hell has sixteen lesser hells, each five hundred yojanas across. Why is it named the Wailing hell? The wardens take the offenders and throw them into a great cauldron; and they set them in a great iron kettle where the hot water boils up and cooks those offenders, and they wail and cry out in bitter and sharp pain. And they take those offenders and throw them onto a great griddle and fry them over and over, and they wail and cry out; and, the remainder of the offense not being finished, they are not allowed to die. Therefore it is named Wailing.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Wailing to the Black Sand hell, and so on to the Cold Ice, and only then do their lives end. Those who nurse anger and poison and make evil actions of every kind fall into the Wailing hell.

The great Great Wailing hell has sixteen lesser hells. Why is it named Great Wailing? They take those offenders and put them in a great iron pot; and they set them in an iron kettle where the hot water boils up and cooks those offenders; and they throw them onto a great griddle and fry them over and over, and they wail and cry out greatly in bitter and sharp pain; and, the remainder of the offense not being finished, they are not allowed to die. It is named Great Wailing.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Great Wailing to the Cold Ice hell, and only then do their lives end. Those who practice the many wrong views and are drawn by the net of craving and do mean and base actions fall into the Great Wailing hell.

The Burning hell has sixteen lesser hells. Why is it named Burning? They put the offenders in an iron city, and that city is aflame and red within and without and burns the offenders. And they set them on an iron tower, and the tower is aflame and red within and without. And they throw them into a great iron kiln, and the kiln is aflame and red within and without, and it burns the offenders and their skin and flesh are scorched and rotted and ten thousand poisons come at once; and, the remainder of the offense not being finished, they are not allowed to die. Therefore it is named Burning.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Burning hell to the Black Sand hell, and so on to the Cold Ice, and then their lives end. Those who did burning deeds fall into the Burning hell and through the long night receive the suffering of burning.

The Great Burning hell has sixteen lesser hells, each five hundred yojanas across. They put the offenders in an iron city, and that city is aflame and red within and without and burns the offenders and their skin and flesh are scorched and rotted and ten thousand poisons come at once. And there is a great fire pit with the flames blazing fiercely, and on the two banks of that pit there are great mountains of fire; and they take that offender and run him through on an iron fork and stand him up in the fire, and a further fire burns him and his skin and flesh are scorched and rotted; and, the remainder of the offense not being finished, they are not allowed to die.

Having suffered a long while they come out of the Great Burning to the Black Sand hell, and so on to the Cold Ice, and only then do their lives end. Those who give up the actions with good fruit and do the many evil actions fall into the Great Burning hell.

The great Unremitting hell has sixteen lesser hells, each five hundred yojanas across. They take that offender and strip off his skin from foot to crown, and then wrap the offender’s body in his own skin and bind it to the wheel of a fire-cart and run it over the hot iron ground, going round and back again, and the body is crushed and rotted and the skin and flesh fall away and ten thousand poisons come at once, and so he does not die.

And there is an iron city with fire rising on the four sides, the flames going from east to west and from west to east, and likewise from south and north and above and below; and the blaze goes round and there is no empty place in it, and they run east and west and their bodies are burned and their skin and flesh scorched and rotted, in bitter and sharp pain with ten thousand poisons at once.

The offenders being in it a long while, a gate at last opens; and the offenders run and race toward it, and flames come out of all the joints of their bodies; and just as they are about to reach the gate, the gate shuts of itself. And, the remainder of the offense not being finished, they are not allowed to die.

And further, the offenders in it, lifting their eyes, see nothing but evil forms; their ears hear evil sounds, their noses smell foul odours, their bodies touch bitter pain, and their minds think evil dharmas; and in the space of a finger-snap there is no time that is not suffering. Therefore it is named the Unremitting hell.

Having suffered a long while, their lives end. Those whose actions were of heavy offense are born in evil destinies, and so on to the Cold Ice hell, and only then do their lives end. Those whose actions of heavy offense produce birth in an evil destiny fall into the Unremitting hell, and their offenses cannot be named. Each of the eight great hells has sixteen lesser hells.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The longest single entry we have translated in this project, and its form is as important as its content. It is not a list of eight hells but one continuous forced march: the offender comes out of the Conception hell into Black Sand, out of Black Sand into Boiling Filth, and so through all sixteen to the Cold Ice, where at last he is allowed to die. Then the same circuit is run again from each of the other seven great hells in turn, the Chinese abbreviating it each time with the formula and so on to the Cold Ice.

The refrain that holds the whole thing together is the clause repeated at the end of nearly every stage: the remainder of his offense not being finished, he is not allowed to die. The torments are not what makes the passage frightening; the impossibility of dying is.

The one place where the writing changes register is the Ash River, which is given nearly ten times the space of the others — the boiling ash, the iron spikes from the bottom up, the sword-tree grove on the bank whose blades point down when one climbs and up when one descends, the iron-beaked crows, and the cold wind that raises the floating skeleton to its feet so that the sequence can begin again.

Each of the eight great hells is assigned its own offense at the close of its section, and they are worth listing: evil in body, speech, and mind for the Conception; an evil mind toward one's parents, the Buddha, or the hearers for the Black Rope; doing the three evils and not the three goods for the Crushing; anger and poison for the Wailing; wrong views and the net of craving for the Great Wailing; burning deeds for the Burning; abandoning good actions for the Great Burning; and, for the Unremitting, the heaviest offenses of all.

12. Ten Further Hells Between the Vajra Mountains

The wind between the mountains that would grind the world to powder, and the ten hells named for the colors of flowers, with the arithmetic of their lifespans.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Between the two great Vajra Mountains there arises a great wind named Saṃkhya. If this wind were to come to the four continents and the eighty thousand lands, the great earth and all the mountains would rise ten li from the ground, or as much as a hundred li, and fly into the air and be entirely ground to powder — as when a strong man takes light chaff in his hand and scatters it in the air. It is by the two great mountains that this wind is blocked and stopped.

If the wind were to come to the four continents, the streams, rivers, and seas among them would all be dried up. And the wind between the mountains is foul and impure, rank and fiercely stinking; and if it were to come to the four continents it would smoke all beings and they would all lose their eyes. And so these two mountains are of much benefit, and are likewise brought about by the recompense of beings’ deeds.

And between the two mountains there are ten hells: the first named Thick Cloud, the second No Cloud, the third Aha-aha, the fourth Alas, the fifth Sheep’s Bleating, the sixth Sugandhi, the seventh Utpala, the eighth Kumuda, the ninth Puṇḍarīka, the tenth Padma.

Why is it named Thick Cloud? The offenders in the hell grow bodies of themselves like a thick cloud, and so it is named Thick Cloud. Why is it named No Cloud? The beings grow bodies like a lump of flesh, and so it is named No Cloud. Why is it named Aha-aha? Bitter pain cuts the body and they all cry aha-aha, and so it is named Aha-aha. Why is it named Alas? The beings receiving punishment, their pain being bitter and sharp and having nothing to turn to, all cry alas. Why is it named Sheep’s Bleating? The beings receiving punishment, their pain cutting the body, wish to raise their voices and speak and their tongues cannot turn, and it is just like the bleating of sheep.

Why is it named the Sugandhi Flower? The hell is all black like the color of the sugandhi flower. Why is it named the Utpala Flower? The hell is all blue like the utpala flower. Why is it named the Kumuda Flower? The hell is all pink like the color of the kumuda. Why is it named the Puṇḍarīka Flower? The hell is all white like the puṇḍarīka flower. Why is it named the Padma Flower? The hell is all red like the color of the padma flower.

It is as though there were a basket holding sixty-four bushels filled with sesame seeds, and a man took away one seed every hundred years until they were all gone: the offenders in the Thick Cloud hell would not yet have finished their punishment. Twenty lifespans of the Thick Cloud hell equal one lifespan of the No Cloud hell; twenty of the No Cloud equal one of the Aha-aha; twenty of the Aha-aha equal one of the Alas; twenty of the Alas equal one of the Sheep’s Bleating; twenty of the Sheep’s Bleating equal one of the Sugandhi; twenty of the Sugandhi equal one of the Utpala; twenty of the Utpala equal one of the Kumuda; twenty of the Kumuda equal one of the Puṇḍarīka; twenty of the Puṇḍarīka equal one of the Padma; and twenty lifespans of the Padma are called one intermediate kalpa, and twenty intermediate kalpas are called one great kalpa.

In the Padma hell the flames blaze fiercely. When an offender is a hundred yojanas from the fire, the fire has already burned and scorched him; at sixty yojanas both his ears are already deaf and he knows nothing by hearing; at fifty yojanas both his eyes are already blind and he sees nothing more. The monk Kokālika, having harboured an evil mind and slandered Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana, when his body broke up and his life ended fell into this Padma hell (Source: Dīrgha Āgama; the Lokasthāna is roughly the same with minor differences).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ten flower-hells here overlap in name with the four soundless cold hells of entry 10 but are explained differently — there by the body splitting open like a blossom, here simply by the color of the place. The compendium does not reconcile them.

What the entry is really for is the arithmetic, and it is worth following. A basket of sixty-four bushels of sesame, one seed removed every hundred years, is exhausted before a term in the first hell is finished; and each hell's lifespan is twenty times the one before it, through ten hells, so that twenty lifespans of the last make one intermediate kalpa and twenty intermediate kalpas one great kalpa. The whole scheme is built to make a number that cannot be held in mind.

It ends, after all that, with one named individual. The monk Kokālika, who slandered Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana, is in the Padma hell — where the fire has already burned a man at a hundred yojanas' distance, deafened him at sixty, and blinded him at fifty.
