Birds and Creeping Creatures

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 48 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相), which holds two of the compendium's sections at once: "Birds, Part 2" (禽畜生部中) and "Creeping Creatures, Part 3" (蟲畜生部下). Twenty-nine entries in fifteen sections, from garuḍas to lice. Among them: a pair of matched cosmographies, of the garuḍas and of the nāgas, built on the same frame and distinguished by one clause about the state of mind that accompanied their former giving; the dove whose past and future Śāriputra could search eighty thousand kalpas in each direction without finding an end to; four crows who accept a suicidal errand and are released because their loyalty shames a foreign king; the nāga king's question about why his kin have multiplied, answered buddha by buddha; a venomous nāga who could kill with a look, skinned alive and then eaten by insects while holding still rather than break a one-day precept; the Buddha dividing his own robe so that a single thread of it protects a nāga from garuḍas; a fish with a hundred animal heads that a thousand men are needed to land, and the insult that produced it; the creature found where a thousand bushels of grain had been buried; and the monastery head who kept five hundred pearls and offered the community his dung instead.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 48 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 48 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

Fascicle 48 is unusual in carrying two of the compendium's numbered sections at once — the second part of the animals, on birds, and the third, on creeping and lower creatures — and each has its own table of contents and its own species subsections. We keep the whole division: fifteen sections in all, each with its Chinese heading.

Entries 1 and 15 should be read as a pair. They are cosmographies of the garuḍas and of the nāgas built on the same frame, clause for clause, and the compiler makes the connection explicit: the note in entry 15 on where the nāga palaces are simply refers the reader back to the garuḍa section rather than repeat it. What distinguishes the two accounts is a single line about the state of mind in which each formerly gave.

On names: Śāriputra, Ānanda, Devadatta, Prasenajit, Śakra, Amitābha, Krakucchanda, Kanakamuni, the Buddha Kāśyapa, Vipaśyin, Sāgara, Nanda, Upananda, Anavatapta, Takṣaka, Apalāla, Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Kapila, Sthūlanandā, Vaiśālī, Vārāṇasī, Rājagṛha, the Jetavana, Jambudvīpa, Sumeru, the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, and Avīci are given in their standard forms, as are the birds of the Pure Land — kalaviṅka, śārikā, jīvañjīvaka. Several of the sixteen nāga kings and a number of other transliterations have no confident correspondence and are kept in pinyin, with the compendium's own bracketed glosses preserved. The qiānqiū of section 2 and the grain-thief of section 13 are creatures we have not identified and we keep their Chinese names.

Four entries carry verse. We set these off in italics with slashes for the line breaks of the Chinese; the Chinese column keeps them running on within the paragraph, as the source has them. Entry 21 has no source note in the Chinese and we have not supplied one.

Entry 18 describes a flaying carried out on a conscious animal that chooses not to resist, and entry 24 a creature with a hundred heads. Both are translated in full.

Garuḍas 金翅第一

Two entries opening the section on birds: a cosmographical account of the four kinds of garuḍa, their tree on the northern shore, and what they can and cannot eat; and the death of the garuḍa king True Sound, whose heart becomes a wish-fulfilling jewel.

1. Birth, Dwelling, and Sustenance

The four kinds of garuḍa, the tree on the sea's northern shore with a nāga palace and a garuḍa palace at each of its four sides, and the one nāga prince a garuḍa could not swallow.

Chinese Source

金翅鳥有四種。一者卵生。二者胎生。三者濕生。四者化生。皆先大布施心高凌虛。苦惱眾生心多瞋慢。生此鳥中。有如意寶珠以為瓔珞。變化萬端無事不辦。身高四十里。衣廣八十里。長四十里。重二兩半。食黿鼉魚鼈以為揣食(涅槃經云能食能消。一切龍魚金銀等寶唯除金剛耳)洗浴衣服為細滑食。亦有婚姻。兩身相觸以成陰陽。壽一劫或有減者。大海北岸一樹名究羅瞋摩高百由旬。蔭五十由旬(樓炭經略同)樹東有卵生龍宮卵生金翅鳥宮。樹南有胎生龍宮胎生金翅鳥宮。樹西有濕生龍宮濕生金翅鳥宮。樹北有化生龍宮化生金翅鳥宮。各各縱廣六千由旬。莊飾如上。若卵生金翅鳥飛下海中以翅搏水。水即兩披深二百由旬。取卵生龍隨意而食之(華嚴經云。此鳥食龍。所扇之風若入眼人眼失明故不來人間)胎濕化亦復如是(涅槃經云唯不能食受三歸者)有化生龍子三齋日受齋八禁。時金翅鳥欲取食之。銜上須彌山北大鐵樹上。高十六萬里。求覓其尾了不可得。鳥聞亦受五戒(出長阿含經第十九卷。又出增一阿含第十五卷。大智論華嚴經。亦見也)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are four kinds of garuḍa: the first born from eggs, the second from a womb, the third from moisture, the fourth spontaneously. All of them formerly gave greatly and their minds were lofty and overrode others; they tormented beings and their minds were full of anger and arrogance, and so were born among these birds. They have wish-fulfilling jewels for necklaces, and their transformations are of ten thousand kinds and there is nothing they cannot manage.

Their bodies are forty li high; their clothing is eighty li broad and forty li long and weighs two and a half taels. They eat soft-shelled turtles, alligators, fish, and hard-shelled turtles as their coarse food (the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says they can eat and digest all nāgas and fish and gold and silver and other treasures, excepting only diamond), and bathing and clothing are their subtle food. They also marry: the two bodies touch and so the yin and yang are accomplished. Their lifespan is a kalpa, though for some it is less.

On the north shore of the great sea there is a tree named Kūluóchēnmó, a hundred yojanas high with a shade fifty yojanas broad (the Lokasthāna Sūtra is roughly the same). East of the tree are the palaces of the egg-born nāgas and the egg-born garuḍas; south of the tree the palaces of the womb-born nāgas and the womb-born garuḍas; west of the tree the palaces of the moisture-born nāgas and the moisture-born garuḍas; north of the tree the palaces of the spontaneously born nāgas and the spontaneously born garuḍas. Each is six thousand yojanas across, adorned as above.

If an egg-born garuḍa flies down into the sea and beats the water with its wings, the water parts in two to a depth of two hundred yojanas, and it takes an egg-born nāga and eats it as it pleases (the Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: this bird eats nāgas, and the wind of its fanning, if it enters a person’s eye, blinds them, and therefore it does not come among humans). And so with the womb-born, moisture-born, and spontaneously born (the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: only those who have taken the three refuges it cannot eat).

There was a spontaneously born nāga prince who on the three fast days took the fast of the eight prohibitions; and when a garuḍa meant to take and eat him, it carried him up onto a great iron tree north of Mount Sumeru, a hundred and sixty thousand li high, and looked for his tail and could not find it at all. And the bird, hearing of it, likewise took the five precepts (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 19; also Ekottarika Āgama, fascicle 15; the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom and the Avataṃsaka Sūtra also have it).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The garuḍa and nāga cosmographies in this fascicle — entry 1 here and entry 15 in the second half — are deliberately built as a matched pair, and the compiler says so: the note in entry 15 on where the nāga palaces are simply refers the reader back to the garuḍa section. The two accounts share their structure exactly: four modes of birth, the same bodily dimensions, the same weight of clothing, the same coarse food and the same subtle food of bathing and clothing, marriage by touch, and a lifespan of one kalpa.

What separates them is one line each, and it is the moral cause. The garuḍas were formerly great givers whose minds were lofty and overrode others and full of anger and arrogance; the nāgas were formerly great givers with much anger whose minds were crooked and not upright. The same giving, differently accompanied, produces predator and prey.

The exception at the close is worth noting: the garuḍas cannot eat those who have taken the three refuges, and a nāga prince keeping the eight-precept fast is carried up a hundred-and-sixty-thousand-li iron tree and cannot be swallowed — after which the bird takes the five precepts itself.

2. The Death-Signs of the Garuḍa King True Sound

Eight thousand years of eating a nāga king and five hundred lesser nāgas a day, and then the seven falls from the Vajra Mountain.

Chinese Source

金翅鳥王。名曰正音。於眾羽族快樂自在。於閻浮提日食一龍王及五百小龍。於四天下更食。一日數亦如上。周而復始。經八千歲死相既現。諸龍吐毒不能得食。飢逼慞惶永不得安。至金剛山。從金剛山直下從大水際。至風輪際為風所吹還金剛山。如是七反然後命終。以其毒故令十寶山同時火起。難陀龍王懼燒此山。即降大雨渧如車輪。鳥肉消盡唯餘心存。心又直下七反如前住金剛山。難陀龍王取為明珠。轉輪聖王得為如意珠。若人念佛心亦如是(出觀佛三昧經第一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a garuḍa king named True Sound, who among the feathered kinds is happy and free. In Jambudvīpa he eats one nāga king and five hundred lesser nāgas a day, and in the four continents he eats besides, the daily number being as above, going round and beginning again.

After eight thousand years the signs of death appeared in him, and the nāgas spat venom and he could get nothing to eat; and pressed by hunger and in panic he was never at peace. He came to the Vajra Mountain, and from the Vajra Mountain went straight down to the edge of the great waters, and to the edge of the wind-wheel, and was blown by the wind back to the Vajra Mountain; and so seven times over, and then his life ended.

Because of his venom the ten jewel mountains all took fire at once, and the nāga king Nanda, fearing these mountains would burn, sent down a great rain whose drops were like cartwheels. The bird’s flesh was entirely dissolved and only the heart remained; and the heart too went straight down and back seven times as before and came to rest on the Vajra Mountain. The nāga king Nanda took it for a bright pearl, and a wheel-turning sage-king obtains it as a wish-fulfilling jewel. And if a person keeps the Buddha in mind, the mind is likewise (Source: Sūtra on the Samādhi of Contemplating the Buddha, fascicle 1).

The Qiānqiū Bird 千秋第二

One entry, three sentences, on a bird that kills its mother in being born and yet incurs no offense.

3. The Qiānqiū Harms Its Mother in Being Born

Three sentences on a bird with a human face, and a point about whether animals can commit the heinous crimes.

Chinese Source

千秋。人面鳥身。生子還害其母。復學得羅漢果。畜生無有是智及有尊卑想。不受五逆罪(出婆須蜜經第八卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The qiānqiū has a human face and a bird’s body, and when it bears young it harms its mother in the process. It can further study and attain the fruit of arhatship. Animals have not the intelligence for this, nor the notion of honoured and base; they do not incur the offense of the five heinous crimes (Source: Vasumitra Treatise, fascicle 8).

Geese 雁第三

Three entries: the golden goose who was a doting brahmin, five hundred geese netted while circling toward the sound of the Dharma, and the goose that starved itself thin enough to get out of the cage.

4. A Golden-Feathered Goose Still Loves the Wife and Children of Its Former Life and Gives Them One Feather a Day

A brahmin who could not detach himself from his family dies and comes back as a golden goose, and his children work out how to get all the feathers at once.

Chinese Source

毘舍離獼猴江側有蒜園。偷羅難陀比丘尼。去園不遠。園主問言。阿姨欲須蒜耶。比丘尼即與沙彌尼式叉摩那尼。數數往索蒜。遂都盡。其主委園而去。佛說本生。昔有一婆羅門。年百二十形體羸瘦。其婦端正無比。多生男女。此婆羅門繫心其婦及諸男女。初不捨離。以此愛著情篤。遂至命終便生雁中。其身毛羽盡為金色。以前福因緣故自識宿命。我以何方便養活此男女使不貧苦。日日來還日落一羽而去。兒見如是不知因緣。即共議言。我等寧可伺其來時。方便捉之盡取金羽。如其所計盡拔金羽。羽盡更生白羽。佛告諸比丘。欲知爾時婆羅門死為雁者。豈異人乎。即園主是。其端正婦者即比丘尼是。男女者即式叉摩那沙彌尼等是(出四分律二分第三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the side of the Monkey River at Vaiśālī there was a garlic garden, and the nun Sthūlanandā lived not far from the garden. The garden owner asked her: does the venerable lady want garlic? And the nun, with the novice nuns and the probationers, went again and again to ask for garlic until it was all gone; and the owner abandoned the garden and went away.

The Buddha told the former birth. Long ago there was a brahmin a hundred and twenty years old, thin and worn in body, whose wife was beautiful beyond compare and who had borne many sons and daughters; and this brahmin bound his mind to his wife and his sons and daughters and from the first would not leave them.

By this deep attachment of love he came at last to the end of his life and was born among geese, and the feathers of his body were all gold-colored; and because of that former cause of merit he knew his past lives of himself: by what means shall I keep these children so that they are not poor and hard-pressed? So day after day he came back and let one feather fall and went.

His children, seeing this and not knowing the cause, conferred together: we may as well watch for him when he comes and find a way to catch him and take all the golden feathers. And as they planned, they pulled out all the golden feathers; and when the feathers were gone, white feathers grew in their place.

The Buddha told the monks: would you know the brahmin who died and became a goose? Was he another? He is the garden owner. His beautiful wife is that nun; the sons and daughters are the probationers and novice nuns (Source: Dharmaguptaka Vinaya, second division, fascicle 3).

5. Five Hundred Geese Killed by a Hunter Hear the Buddha's Dharma, Are Born in Heaven, and Attain the Path

Five hundred geese circling down toward the sound of a sermon fly into a net.

Chinese Source

佛在波羅奈國。於林澤中。為諸天人四輩之類顯說妙法。時虛空中有五百雁為群。聞佛音聲深心愛樂迴翔欲下。獵師張羅雁墮其中。為獵師所殺生忉利天。處父母膝上若八歲兒。端嚴無比光若金山。便自念言。我何因生此。即識宿命愛法果報。即共持華下閻浮提。至世尊所禮足白言。我蒙法音生在妙天。願重開示。佛說四諦。得須陀洹。即還天上(出賢愚經第十三卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha was in the kingdom of Vārāṇasī, in a wooded marsh, setting out the fine Dharma for the gods and men and the fourfold assembly. At that time in the open sky there was a flock of five hundred geese who heard the Buddha’s voice and loved it deeply in their hearts and wheeled round meaning to come down; and a hunter had spread a net and the geese fell into it and were killed by the hunter, and were born in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven.

On the knees of their heavenly parents they were like eight-year-old children, grave and beautiful beyond compare, their light like a golden mountain; and they thought: by what cause am I born here? And they knew their former lives and the fruit of loving the Dharma.

So they took flowers together and came down to Jambudvīpa, and came to the World-Honored One and bowed at his feet and said: by your grace, having heard the sound of the Dharma we were born in a fine heaven; we ask you to open it out for us again. The Buddha taught the four truths and they obtained the fruit of stream-entry, and returned to heaven (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 13).

6. A Goose Caught in the King's Net Does Not Eat and So Gets Out

A goose that refused to eat in the cage, and its parting remark to the fat ones.

Chinese Source

國王夫人昇在樓上。見雁飛空中欲得其肉。便以白王。王遣獵工。持網行羅分布求索。即得數十以籠養之。中有不食者。諸雁謂言。今已得活不食何益。不食者日。憂不能食。七日瘠瘦於籠孔中得出飛去。遙語肥者曰。卿等貪食害痛在後(出十卷譬喻經第六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

A king’s consort went up on a tower and saw geese flying in the sky and wished to have their flesh, and told the king; and the king sent a hunter with a net to spread it and search, and he got several dozen and kept them in a cage.

Among them was one that would not eat, and the other geese said: we are alive now — what good does not eating do? The one that would not eat said: from grief I cannot eat. In seven days it was so thin that it got out through a hole in the cage and flew away, and said from a distance to the fat ones: you gentlemen are greedy for food, and the pain of it comes after (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 6).

Cranes and Other Birds 鶴第四

One entry: the birds of Amitābha's land and the doctrine their voices set out six times a day.

7. Birds That Constantly Utter the Sound of the Faculties, the Powers, and the Eightfold Path

The white swans, peacocks, parrots, śārikas, kalaviṅkas, and jīvañjīvakas of the Pure Land, and what their voices say.

Chinese Source

彌陀佛國。常有種種奇妙雜色之鳥。白鵠孔雀鸚鵡舍利迦陵頻伽共命之鳥。晝夜六時出和雅音。其音所說。五根。五力。七菩提分。八聖道分。如是等法。其土眾生聞是音聲。皆悉念三寶(出彌陀經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In Amitābha’s land there are constantly rare and wonderful birds of many colors — white swans, peacocks, parrots, śārikas, kalaviṅkas, and jīvañjīvakas — which six times day and night put forth a harmonious and refined sound; and what that sound says is the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of enlightenment, the eightfold noble path, and dharmas such as these. And the beings of that land, hearing that sound, all keep the Three Jewels in mind (Source: Amitābha Sūtra).

Doves 鴿第五

Two entries: the dove that gathered firewood and then threw itself on the fire, and the dove that trembled in Śāriputra's shadow but not in the Buddha's.

8. A Dove Gives Up Its Life for a Starving Man in Want

Two sentences: a lost traveller freezing in the snow, and a dove that builds the fire and then supplies the meat.

Chinese Source

昔雪山上有一鴿鳥。時大雨雪。有人失道窮厄辛苦。飢寒兼至命在須臾。鴿飛求火。為其聚薪然之。既然身投火中。施此飢人(出大智論第十一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago on the Snow Mountains there was a dove. There was a great fall of rain and snow, and a man had lost the road and was in the extremity of hardship, with hunger and cold together upon him and his life hanging by a moment. The dove flew and sought fire and gathered firewood for him and lit it; and when it was lit, it threw its own body into the fire and gave it to this hungry man (Source: Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, fascicle 11).

9. A Dove Pursued by a Hawk Is at Peace in the Buddha's Shadow and Still Trembles in a Disciple's

Śāriputra, asked why his shadow frightens a dove that the Buddha's does not, looks eighty thousand kalpas back and eighty thousand forward and finds a dove at both ends.

Chinese Source

佛在祇洹林晡時經行。舍利弗從。時有鷹逐鴿。鴿飛來佛邊。佛行影覆之。鴿身安隱怖畏即除不復作聲。後舍利弗影到。鴿便作聲戰怖如初。舍利弗白佛言。佛及我身俱無三毒。佛影覆鴿鴿不恐怖。我影覆鴿戰慄如初。佛言。汝有三毒習故。汝觀此鴿宿世因緣幾世作鴿。舍利弗即時入宿命智三昧。觀見此鴿。已八萬大劫常作鴿身。過是已往我不能見。佛言。汝若不能盡知過去。試觀此鴿何時當脫。舍利弗即入願智三昧。觀見此鴿。八萬大劫未脫鴿身。過是不知。於恒河沙等大劫中常作鴿身(出大智論第十一卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha was in the Jetavana grove walking in the late afternoon, and Śāriputra was with him. Then a hawk was pursuing a dove, and the dove flew to the Buddha’s side, and the shadow of the Buddha as he walked covered it, and the dove’s body was at ease and its fear was removed and it made no more sound. Afterward Śāriputra’s shadow reached it, and the dove made a sound and trembled in terror as before.

Śāriputra said to the Buddha: the Buddha and I are both without the three poisons; the Buddha’s shadow covered the dove and the dove was not afraid, and my shadow covered the dove and it trembled as before. The Buddha said: you still have the residue of the three poisons.

Observe this dove: how many lives has it been a dove? Śāriputra entered the samādhi of the knowledge of past lives and looked, and saw that this dove had been a dove constantly for eighty thousand great kalpas, and beyond that he could not see.

The Buddha said: if you cannot know the past entirely, try to observe when this dove will be free of it. Śāriputra entered the samādhi of the knowledge of wishes and looked, and saw that for eighty thousand great kalpas this dove would not be free of the dove’s body, and beyond that he did not know. Through great kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges it is constantly a dove (Source: Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, fascicle 11).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The exchange between the Buddha and Śāriputra is one of the sharpest passages in the compendium on the difference between exhausting the poisons and exhausting their residue. Śāriputra's objection is exact — the Buddha and I are both without the three poisons — and the answer is one clause: you still have the residue.

The demonstration that follows is on a scale that dwarfs the question. Śāriputra enters the samādhi of past-life knowledge and finds the dove has been a dove for eighty thousand great kalpas and cannot see past that; he enters the samādhi of knowledge of wishes and finds it will be a dove for eighty thousand great kalpas more and cannot see past that. The entry closes by saying that through kalpas as numerous as the Ganges sands it is constantly a dove, and offers no relief.

Pheasants 雉第六

10. A Pheasant Saves a Burning Wood

A pheasant flying back and forth with water in its feathers, and the vow that puts the fire out.

Chinese Source

昔野火燒林。林有一雉。勤身自力飛來入林。以水灑林往反疲乏不以為苦。時天帝釋來問之言。汝作何等。答曰。我救此林愍眾生故。此林蔭育處廣清涼快樂。我諸種類及諸宗親皆悉依仰。我有身力云何懈怠而不救之。天帝問言。汝乃精勤當至幾時。雉言。以死為期。天帝言。誰為汝證。即自立誓。我心至誠信不虛者火即自滅。是時淨居天知雉弘誓。即為滅火。始終常茂不為火燒(出大智論第十六卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago a wildfire was burning a wood, and in the wood there was a pheasant who exerted its own strength and flew into the wood and sprinkled water on it, and went back and forth exhausted and did not count it hard.

Then Śakra, lord of the gods, came and asked it: what are you doing? It answered: I am saving this wood, out of pity for beings. This wood shelters and nourishes a wide space and is cool and pleasant, and all my kinds and all my kin depend on it. I have bodily strength; how should I be idle and not save it?

Śakra asked: with such diligence, how long will you keep at it? The pheasant said: until death is my term. Śakra said: who will bear witness for you? And it made this vow of itself: if my mind is wholly sincere and my faith is not false, let the fire go out of itself. At this the gods of the Pure Abodes knew the pheasant’s great vow, and put out the fire for it; and from beginning to end the wood was always lush and was not burned by fire (Source: Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, fascicle 16).

11. Four Crows Sent by the Crow King Sugarcane to the Kingdom of Sāgara

Ru-Yi Translation

King Prasenajit had four great ministers whom he appointed generals, and they combined the four divisions of the army meaning to attack a small kingdom. The four ministers saw the Buddha and bowed their heads at his feet, and the World-Honored One asked: where are you going, gentlemen? And they told him the whole matter: our persons have been much advanced by this king, and we constantly fear for our lives; now we go to attack.

The World-Honored One praised them: excellent, worthy sirs; this is repaying kindness and showing reciprocity. And it is not only in this present life that you have done so for this king.

In a past age, in the kingdom of Sāgara, a company of crows came and gathered and settled in that country; the crow king was named Sugarcane and was lord of eighty thousand crows. The crow king had a wife named Jiùlínì who, being with child and having an aversion to food, said to the king in earnest: my body is a little unwell and I want something tender; if I eat the flesh of a deer king I shall live, and if not I shall die.

So the crow king heard her voice and assembled the crows: you should go to the kingdom of Sāgara. The king of Sāgara has a great deer king named Xūjù, and she wishes to have his flesh. Four crows answered the call: we are equal to it; we shall not begrudge our bodies and lives and shall accomplish this business.

Then the four crows came again and again to that assembly-place; and the king’s son, seeing the crows, was frightened and ran back and told the king: I have seen four crows of such and such color and shape, coming again and again to the deer park.

The king ordered men to catch them, and the fowlers contrived means and spread nets and caught the crows and took them alive to the king. The king questioned the four crows and scolded them: why do you come here again and again and trespass on my borders?

The crows said: yes, heavenly king, but it is not of our own liking. There is a king named Settled-Dwelling whose wife has conceived and wants the flesh of the deer Xūjù, tender and good; and that king sent us, and we have received our lord’s command.

Then the king was astonished at it and wondered: to receive a king’s command of your own accord and make such a plan and throw away your bodies and lives — this is truly beyond me. To look for such reciprocity in ordinary people, receiving the instruction of lord and father, is still not to be had — how much less in birds and beasts. And the king told the crows: I now pardon your offense (Source: Sūtra of Honey-Complete, also called the Sūtra of the Crow King of the Kingdom of Kosala).

12. A Red-Billed Crow and a Monkey Are Friends

A crow's young eaten by a snake, and what her friend the monkey does about it.

Chinese Source

昔有烏名曰拘耆(梁言赤嘴烏也)遊在叢樹產乳諸子在於樹上。時拘耆與一獼猴共為親厚。時叢樹間有一毒蛇。伺行不在。噉拘耆子無復遺餘。拘耆失子悲鳴啼呼不知所在。熟自思惟。知蛇所噉。獼猴歸見問之何為。答曰。蛇噉我子了盡無餘。獼猴曰。我當報之。時毒蛇行。獼猴前遶之。蛇怒纏獼猴。獼猴捉得頭曳。至石上磨破而死。棄擲而還。拘耆踊躍(出赤嘴烏喻經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a crow named Jūqí (which in the language of Liang is red-billed crow), who lived in a thicket and bore young in the trees. At that time Jūqí was on close terms with a monkey.

In that thicket there was a venomous snake, which watched until she was away and ate Jūqí’s young and left nothing at all. Jūqí, having lost her young, cried out in grief and did not know where they were, and, thinking it over, knew that the snake had eaten them.

The monkey came back and saw her and asked what was the matter, and she answered: a snake has eaten my young entirely and left nothing. The monkey said: I shall repay it.

When the venomous snake went abroad, the monkey went and coiled around in front of it, and the snake in anger wrapped itself round the monkey; and the monkey seized its head and dragged it to a rock and ground it until it broke and died, and threw it away and came back. And Jūqí leapt for joy (Source: Sūtra of the Parable of the Red-Billed Crow).

13. A Crow and a Hen Together Bear One Chick

A verse on a hybrid chick that can learn neither parent's voice.

Chinese Source

過去世時。有一群鷄依榛林住。有狸侵食唯餘一䳄。烏來覆之共生一子。子作聲時烏說偈言。此兒非我有　野父聚落母共合生兒子　非烏復非鷄若欲學公聲　復是鷄所生若欲學母鳴　其父復是烏學烏似鷄鳴　學鷄作烏聲烏鷄若兼學　是二俱不成(出僧祇律第二十四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In a past age there was a flock of fowls living in a hazel wood, and a wildcat came and ate them and only one hen was left; and a crow came and covered her and together they bore one chick. When the chick made a sound the crow spoke this verse:

“This child is not mine: / a wild father and a village mother / joined together and bore a child / that is neither crow nor fowl. / If it would learn its father’s voice, / it was born of a fowl besides; / if it would learn its mother’s cry, / its father again is a crow. / Learning the crow, it seems to crow like a fowl; / learning the fowl, it makes a crow’s sound. / If it learns crow and fowl together, / neither of the two comes off.” (Source: Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, fascicle 24)

14. A Crow and a Fox Praise One Another in Turn

A crow and a fox eating a corpse and complimenting each other's appearance, and the sage who overhears them.

“Your body is like a lion’s / and your head like a sage’s; / you were conceived among the king of deer — / how fine, like a lovely flower.”

The fox said:

“Who is the honoured one up in the tree, / whose wisdom is first of all, / whose brightness shines over the ten directions / like a heap of purple-gold?”

At that time a great sage was dwelling in seclusion, purely cultivating the path; and hearing them he spoke a verse and asked:

“What you do I have long seen; / the thing is double-tongued flattery. / You hide yourselves among the trees / eating the flesh of a dead eunuch. / You are base and lowly creatures, / and call each other as though you were superior men.”

Nāgas 龍第一

Five entries opening the section on lower creatures: a cosmography of the four kinds of nāga and their sixteen kings, the palace of Sāgara, the nāga king's question about why his kin have multiplied, the nāga flayed alive rather than break a one-day precept, and the four garuḍa kings talked out of eating nāgas.

15. Birth, Dwelling, and Sustenance

The four kinds of nāga, their dimensions and diet, and the sixteen nāga kings who escape being eaten.

Chinese Source

龍有四種。一者卵生。二者胎生。三者濕生。四者化生。皆先多瞋恚心曲不端。大行布施今受此形。以七寶為宮(宮之所在乃現金翅鳥部)身高四十里。衣長四十里。廣八十里。重二兩半。神力自在百味飲食。最後一口變為蝦蟇。若自化眷屬發於道心。施乞皂衣能使諸龍各興供養者。沙不雨身及離眾患(又云。變身為蛇虺等不遭蝦蟇及金翅鳥)食黿鼉魚鼈。以為揣食(樓炭經云龍食魚鼈及提違提歷大魚也)洗浴衣服為細滑食。亦有婚姻。身相觸以成陰陽。壽命一劫。或有減者。免金翅鳥食。唯有十六王。一娑竭。二難陀。三跋難陀。四伊那婆羅。五提頭賴吒。六善見。七阿虛。八伽句羅。九伽毘羅。十阿波羅。十一伽㝹。十二瞿伽㝹。十三阿耨達。十四善住。十五優睒伽波頭。十六得叉迦(樓炭經六十三出長阿含樓炭大智論)。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are four kinds of nāga: the first born from eggs, the second from a womb, the third from moisture, the fourth spontaneously. All of them formerly had much anger and their minds were crooked and not upright, and they greatly practiced giving, and so they now receive this form. Their palaces are of the seven treasures (where the palaces are has been set out under the garuḍas).

Their bodies are forty li high; their clothing is forty li long and eighty li broad and weighs two and a half taels. Their divine power is free and they have food and drink of a hundred flavors, and the last mouthful turns into a toad. If one transforms one’s own retinue and gives rise in them to the mind of the path, and gives dark robes to beggars and can make the nāgas each raise offerings, then sand does not rain on the body and one is free of the many troubles (it is also said: transforming the body into a snake or viper and the rest, one does not meet with the toad or the garuḍa).

They eat soft-shelled turtles, alligators, fish, and hard-shelled turtles as coarse food (the Lokasthāna Sūtra says nāgas eat fish and turtles and the great fish tíwéi and tílì), and bathing and clothing are their subtle food. They also marry: the bodies touch and so the yin and yang are accomplished. Their lifespan is a kalpa, though for some it is less.

娑竭龍王。住須彌山北大海底。宮宅縱廣八萬由旬。七寶所成牆壁七重。欄楯羅網嚴飾其上。園林浴池眾鳥和鳴。金壁銀門。門高二千四百里。廣二千二百里。綵畫姝好。常有五百鬼神之所守護。能隨心降雨。群龍所不能及。所住之淵涌流入海。青琉璃色(出樓炭華嚴經)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The nāga king Sāgara dwells at the bottom of the great sea north of Mount Sumeru. His palace is eighty thousand yojanas across, made of the seven treasures, with walls of seven layers, railings and nets adorning them above, gardens and bathing pools with birds of every kind singing in harmony, gold walls and silver gates; and the gate is two thousand four hundred li high and two thousand two hundred li broad, painted in colors and beautiful.

He is constantly guarded by five hundred ghost-spirits, and can send down rain as he wills, which the other nāgas cannot equal; and the deep pool where he dwells flows out and enters the sea, of a blue lapis color (Source: the Lokasthāna and Avataṃsaka Sūtras).

17. His Kin Were Few at First and Many Afterward

The nāga king asks why there are so many more nāgas than there used to be. The Buddha's answer is an accounting, buddha by buddha.

Chinese Source

龍王白佛言。我從劫初止住大海。從拘樓秦佛時。大海之中妻子甚少。今者海龍眷屬繁多。佛告龍王。其於佛法出家。違戒犯行不捨破戒者多生龍中。直見不墮地獄。如斯之類壽終已後皆生龍中。佛告龍王。拘樓秦佛時。九十八億居家出家。違其禁戒皆生龍中。拘那含牟尼佛時。八十億居家出家。毀戒恣心。壽終之後皆生龍中。迦葉佛時。六十四億居家出家。犯戒皆生龍中。於我世中。九百九十億居家。鬪諍誹謗經戒死生龍中。今已有生者。以是之故。在大海中諸龍妻子眷屬不可稱計。我泥洹後多有惡優婆塞。違失禁戒當生龍中。或墮地獄(出海龍王經第二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

A nāga king said to the Buddha: I have dwelt in the great sea since the beginning of the kalpa. In the time of the Buddha Krakucchanda my wives and children in the great sea were very few, and now the sea-nāgas’ kin are many.

The Buddha told the nāga king: those who go forth in the Buddha’s Dharma and go against the precepts and offend in their conduct and do not give up their broken precepts are for the most part born among nāgas. Holding right view they do not fall into hell; such as these, when their lives end, are all born among nāgas.

The Buddha told the nāga king: in the time of the Buddha Krakucchanda, nine billion eight hundred million householders and renunciants went against the prohibitions and were all born among nāgas. In the time of the Buddha Kanakamuni, eight billion householders and renunciants broke the precepts and indulged their minds, and when their lives ended were all born among nāgas. In the time of the Buddha Kāśyapa, six billion four hundred million householders and renunciants offended against the precepts and were all born among nāgas. In my own age, ninety-nine billion householders have quarrelled and slandered the sūtras and precepts and die and are born among nāgas, and some are already born.

For this reason the nāgas’ wives, children, and kin in the great sea cannot be reckoned. After my nirvāṇa there will be many evil lay followers who go against and lose the prohibitions and will be born among nāgas, or fall into hell (Source: Sūtra of the Sea-Nāga King, fascicle 2).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The nāga king's question is a demographic one and the Buddha answers it as such, buddha by buddha: nine billion eight hundred million under Krakucchanda, eight billion under Kanakamuni, six billion four hundred million under Kāśyapa, ninety-nine billion in his own age — all of them householders and renunciants who broke the precepts and were born as nāgas.

The distinction the passage turns on is precise and is easy to miss. Those who break the precepts but hold right view do not fall into hell; they are born among nāgas instead. The nāga realm is presented here not as a punishment for wrong belief but as the destination of ordinary lapsed practitioners — which explains, in this text's terms, why there are so many of them.

18. A Nāga Keeps the Precepts to the Death and Does Not Break Them

A venomous nāga who could kill with a look takes the one-day precepts, falls asleep, and is skinned alive by a hunter — and then eaten by insects while he holds still.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

A mightily venomous nāga could kill the weak with a look of his eye and kill the strong with a breath from his mouth. At one time this nāga took the one-day precepts, and went out and entered among the forest trees to reflect; and, sitting long, he grew tired and slack and slept. It is the law of nāgas that in sleep their form is like a snake’s, of the seven treasures in mixed colors.

A hunter saw him and said in startled delight: with a skin so rare and hard to come by, to present it to the king of the country for a boat-ornament — would that not be fitting? And he pressed the head down with a staff and stripped off the skin with a knife.

The nāga thought to himself: my strength could overturn a kingdom, and how should this one small thing trouble me? But because I now hold the precepts I take no account of this body; I shall follow the Buddha’s word and bear it of myself. And he shut his eyes and did not look, and held his breath and did not breathe, and pitied this man; and for the sake of holding the precepts he single-mindedly received the flaying and gave rise to no thought of regret.

Having lost his skin, his red flesh lay on the ground, and the day was very hot and he rolled about in the dirt; and wishing to go toward a great water, he saw small insects come and eat his body, and, for the sake of holding the precepts, dared not move again. And he thought: I now give this body of mine to the insects, for the sake of the buddha-path; now I give flesh to fill their bodies, and afterward shall give the Dharma to enrich their minds. And his body dried and his life ended, and he was at once born in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven (Source: Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, fascicle 14).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Read this entry beside the previous one and the fascicle's argument becomes visible. Entry 17 explains that nāgas are for the most part precept-breakers; entry 18 is about a nāga who keeps a precept at the cost of being skinned alive.

The reasoning as he lies under the knife is given in his own words and is not resignation: my strength could overturn a kingdom, and how should this one small thing trouble me? — followed by the decision not to use it. And after the skin is off, the second test is the harder one: rolling in the dirt in the heat with insects beginning to eat him, he stops moving, because to move would kill them.

The formula he ends on is worth marking, because it turns the whole scene into a plan rather than an endurance: now I give flesh to fill their bodies, and afterward shall give the Dharma to enrich their minds.

19. Four Great Nāga Kings, Troubled by the Garuḍas, Petition the Buddha

The Buddha divides his own robe among the nāgas so that a single thread protects them, and then explains to the aggrieved garuḍas what the four wrong foods are.

Ru-Yi Translation

There were nāga kings, the first named Indrawn-Breath, the second Great Indrawn-Breath, the third Able-to-Net, the fourth Measureless-Color, who said to the World-Honored One: in this sea there are countless kinds of nāga, and there are four kinds of garuḍa who constantly eat the nāgas of the sea. We ask the Buddha to protect us and let us be at ease and not harbour grief and fear.

The World-Honored One took off the dark robe from his body and told the sea-nāga king: you should take this dark robe of the Tathāgata and divide it among the nāgas and let it go all round. Whoever in the great sea comes by one thread of it, the garuḍa kings cannot touch or offend. What the Buddha establishes is inconceivable, and his lofty virtue is such as this.

Then the sea-nāga king took the Buddha’s robe and divided it among the nāga kings, and according to what each lacked, broad or narrow, large or small, it supplied them of itself, and the robe was as it had been and could never be used up. Then the sea-nāga king told the nāga kings: you should revere this robe as you revere the World-Honored One, and after his nirvāṇa make offerings to his relics and serve the World-Honored One with all the requisites.

The four garuḍa kings, hearing what the Buddha had established, were alarmed, and went in haste to where the Buddha was and bowed their heads at his feet: why has the World-Honored One taken away our food?

The Buddha said: there are four foods in all, and by sitting in them one goes to three places. Which four? First, netting and hunting birds and beasts and cruelly destroying the herds, killing life and wronging it in order to have food and drink. Second, taking up arms, blades and spears, hacking and stabbing, pressing and shooting, robbing others of their goods and using them for food and drink. Third, by miserliness and flattery, disorderly offending against the prohibitions, wrong views and cunning deceit, so getting food. Fourth, being no teacher and calling oneself a teacher, being no world-honored one and calling oneself so, following the wrong and calling it right, being no ascetic and calling oneself an ascetic, being not pure and calling oneself pure, not practicing the holy life and calling it so — praising oneself and seeking falsely, and so getting food. These are the four foods, and by sitting in them one goes to hell, to hungry ghosts, and to animals, the three evil places.

The Dharma I teach removes these four foods; one should not nourish the body by harming beings. If you would guard your own body, guard others; what should not be done, be careful not to do.

Then the four garuḍa kings, each with a thousand of their retinue, said to the Buddha: today we take refuge in the Buddha and the Dharma and the community of monks, and confess and repent the calamities we have offended in before. From this day we shall uphold the prohibitions, and give fearlessness to all the nāga kings, and protect the right Dharma as long as the Buddha’s Dharma abides, and follow the Dharma of the path to its exhaustion and not go against the Buddha’s teaching (Source: Sūtra of the Sea-Nāga King, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's remedy is unusual: he takes off his own robe and has it divided among the nāgas so that a single thread of it makes its holder untouchable — an amulet, distributed by the Buddha himself, and one that does not run out however finely it is shared.

The garuḍas' complaint is the fair one and the entry lets them make it: why has the World-Honored One taken away our food? What they get in answer is not a defence of the nāgas but a definition of four kinds of wrong food, all four of which are human: hunting and slaughter; robbery under arms; food got by miserliness, flattery, and wrong views; and — set out at greatest length — food got by claiming to be a teacher, a world-honored one, an ascetic, one who is pure, one who practices the holy life, when one is none of these. The garuḍas' own diet is not on the list, and they convert anyway.

24. A Hundred-Headed Fish Is Caught, and the Fishermen Hear Its Former Causes and Awaken to the Path

A fish so large a thousand men are needed to land it, with a hundred animal heads; and the Buddha addresses it by name.

Chinese Source

佛與諸比丘。向毘舍離。到黎越河。河邊有五百牧牛人五百捕魚人。佛去河不遠而坐止息。時捕魚人網得一魚。五百人挽不能使出。復喚牧牛之眾。千人併力得一大魚。身有百頭若干種類。驢馬駱駝虎狼猪狗猿猴狐狸如斯之屬。眾人甚怪競集看之。世尊尋時往至魚所。而問魚言。汝是迦毘梨不。如是三問皆答言是。復問。教化汝黨今在何處。答言。墮阿鼻地獄中。阿難曰。今者何故喚百頭魚為迦毘梨。佛言。迦葉佛時有婆羅門。生一男兒。字迦毘梨(梁言黃頭)。聰明博達。於種類中多聞第一。唯不如諸沙門輩。其父臨終慇懃約勅。汝慎莫與迦葉沙門講論道理。所以者何。沙門智深汝必不如。父沒之後其母問曰。汝本高明今頗更有勝汝者不。答言。沙門殊勝於我。母復問言。云何為勝。答有疑往問佛能開解。彼若問我我不能答。母復告言。何以不學習其法。答言。敬學其法當作沙門。我是白衣何緣得學。母復告曰。偽作沙門學習已達還來在家。奉其母教而作比丘。經少時間讀誦三藏綜達義理。母問之曰。今得勝未。答言。學問中勝不如坐禪。何以知之。我問彼人悉能分別。彼人問我我不能知。因是事故未與他等。母復告曰。自今已往若共談論。儻不如時便可罵辱。迦毘梨言。出家沙門無復過罪。云何罵之。答曰。但罵卿當得勝。時迦毘梨不忍違母。後日更論理。若短屈即便罵言。汝等愚騃無所識別劇於畜生。諸百獸頭皆用比之。如是非一。以是果報今受魚身而有百頭。阿難問佛。何時當脫此魚身。佛告阿難。此賢劫中千佛過去猶故不脫。爾時阿難及於眾人。聞佛所說。咸共同聲而作是言。身口意行不可不慎。時捕魚人及牧牛人等。一時俱共合掌向佛。求索出家淨修梵行。佛言善來。鬚髮自落法衣在體。為說妙法成阿羅漢(出賢愚經第十卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha and the monks went toward Vaiśālī and came to the Lí-yuè River. By the river there were five hundred cowherds and five hundred fishermen, and the Buddha sat down to rest not far from the river.

Then the fishermen caught a fish in their net, and five hundred men pulling could not get it out; and they called the crowd of cowherds too, and a thousand men together with their strength got out one great fish, whose body had a hundred heads of several kinds — donkey, horse, camel, tiger, wolf, pig, dog, ape, monkey, fox, and the like — and the crowd wondered greatly and came together to look at it.

The World-Honored One at once went to where the fish was and asked the fish: are you Kapila? And so three times, and each time it answered that it was. He asked again: those you taught — where are they now? It answered: fallen into the Avīci hell.

Ānanda said: why does he now call this hundred-headed fish Kapila? The Buddha said: in the time of the Buddha Kāśyapa there was a brahmin who bore a son named Kapila (which in the language of Liang is Yellow-Head), intelligent and broadly learned, first among his kind in learning — only he was not equal to the śramaṇas.

As his father was dying he earnestly charged him: be careful not to debate the principles of the path with the śramaṇas of Kāśyapa. And why? Because the śramaṇas’ wisdom is deep and you would surely not be their equal.

After his father died his mother asked him: you were formerly of high understanding; is there now anyone who surpasses you? He answered: the śramaṇas are far superior to me. His mother asked again: how are they superior? He answered: when they have a doubt they go and ask the Buddha, who can open and resolve it; and if they ask me, I cannot answer.

His mother said again: why not study their Dharma? He answered: to study their Dharma properly one should become a śramaṇa; I am a layman, and how should I get to study it? His mother said again: pretend to be a śramaṇa, and when you have studied their Dharma and mastered it, come back home.

He obeyed his mother’s instruction and became a monk, and after a short while he had recited the three collections and mastered their meaning and principles. His mother asked him: have you got the better of them now? He answered: in learning I am superior, but not in sitting in meditation. How do I know it? When I ask them, they can distinguish everything; when they ask me, I cannot know it. For this reason I am not yet their equal.

His mother said again: from now on, when you debate with them, if it should go against you, then revile them. Kapila said: renunciant śramaṇas have no fault; how should I revile them? She answered: only revile them and you will win.

Then Kapila could not bear to go against his mother; and afterward, when he debated principles again and came off short and was worsted, he would revile them: you are dull-witted and cannot distinguish anything, worse than animals. And he compared them to the heads of all the hundred beasts. And this was not once only; and by the fruit of that he now receives a fish’s body with a hundred heads.

Ānanda asked the Buddha: when will he be free of this fish’s body? The Buddha told Ānanda: in this Fortunate Kalpa a thousand buddhas will pass and he will still not be free of it. Then Ānanda and the crowd, hearing what the Buddha said, all with one voice said: one cannot but be careful in the conduct of body, speech, and mind.

Then the fishermen and the cowherds all together joined their palms toward the Buddha and asked to go forth and purely practice the holy life. The Buddha said: come — and their beards and hair fell away of themselves and the Dharma robes were on their bodies; and he taught them the fine Dharma and they became arhats (Source: Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, fascicle 10).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A cautionary tale about insult, and its machinery is entirely maternal. The father's dying instruction is not to debate the śramaṇas because they will win; the mother's instruction is first to infiltrate the order in order to learn its Dharma, and then, when learning is not enough, to revile them when losing. Kapila objects — renunciant śramaṇas have no fault; how should I revile them? — and does it because he cannot bear to go against her.

The insult is the specific cause of the specific punishment: he compared the monks to the heads of a hundred beasts, and now has a fish's body with a hundred heads of the beasts he named. What makes the entry harsh is the term: Ānanda asks when he will be free of it, and the answer is that a thousand buddhas of this Fortunate Kalpa will pass and he still will not be.

Note that the person who converts is not the fish. The five hundred fishermen and five hundred cowherds who landed it ask to go forth on the spot, and the moral they state is about their own speech: one cannot but be careful in the conduct of body, speech, and mind.

A herdsman pushing his staff into the ground to hear better, and where the frog underneath it wakes up.

Chinese Source

迦羅池中有一蛤。聞佛說法。即從池出入草根下。是時有一牧牛人。見大眾圍遶聽佛說法。往到佛所。欲聞法故以杖刺地。誤著蛤頭。蛤即命終生忉利天。見諸妓女娛樂音聲。尋即思惟。我先為畜生。何因緣故生此天宮。即以天眼觀。先於池邊聽佛說法。以此功德得此果報。時蛤天人即乘宮殿。往至佛所頭頂禮足。佛為說法得須陀洹果(出善見毘婆沙第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the Kāla pool there was a frog which heard the Buddha teaching the Dharma and came out of the pool and went in among the roots of the grass. At that time a cowherd saw a great crowd gathered round listening to the Buddha teach the Dharma, and came to where the Buddha was; and wishing to hear the Dharma he stuck his staff in the ground and by mistake put it through the frog’s head, and the frog died at once and was born in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven.

Seeing the music and the entertainers and their sounds, he reflected at once: I was formerly an animal; by what cause and condition am I born in this heavenly palace? And with the heavenly eye he looked and saw that he had earlier listened to the Buddha teach the Dharma by the pool, and that by this merit he had obtained this fruit. Then the frog-god rode his palace and went to where the Buddha was and bowed his head at his feet; and the Buddha taught him the Dharma and he obtained the fruit of stream-entry (Source: Samantapāsādikā, fascicle 4).

The Grain-Thief 穀賊第六

One entry: the creature found where a thousand bushels of grain had been buried, and what it says when finally named.

27. The Grain-Thief Repays the Grain's Owner with a Hoard of Gold

A thousand bushels of buried grain gone, and a headless, limbless creature in its place that will not give its name until named.

Chinese Source

昔有大家收穀千斛。埋著地中。前至春溫開之取種。了不見穀。而有一蟲大如牛筥。無有手足亦無頭目如頑鈍肉。主人大小莫不怪之。出著平地。即問。汝是何等。終無所道。便以鐵錐刺一處。語曰。欲知我名者持我著大道旁。自當有名我者。於是舉著道邊。三日之中無能名者。次有數百乘黃馬車。衣服侍從皆黃。駐車而呼。穀賊汝何為在是間。答曰。吾食人穀故持我著此。語極久便辭別去。主人問穀賊。向者是誰也。答言。是金寶之精。居在此西三百餘步大樹下。有百石甕滿中金。主人即將數十人往掘即得甕金。家室歡喜輦載將歸。叩頭向穀賊。今日得金是大神恩。寧可留神共歸更設供養。穀賊曰。前食君穀不語姓字者。欲令君得是金報。今當轉行福於天下。不得復住。言竟忽然不見(出譬喻經第二卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago a great household gathered a thousand bushels of grain and buried it in the ground; and coming in spring when it grew warm they opened it to take out seed, and saw no grain at all, but a worm as big as an ox-basket with no hands or feet and no head or eyes, like a lump of dull flesh.

The master and everyone, great and small, wondered at it, and brought it out and set it on level ground, and asked: what are you? And it said nothing at all. So he stuck an iron awl into one place on it, and it said: if you wish to know my name, take me and set me by the great road, and someone will name me of himself.

So they took it and set it by the road; and for three days no one could name it. Then there came several hundred carriages with yellow horses, the clothing and attendants all yellow; and they stopped their carriages and called out: Grain-Thief, what are you doing here? It answered: because I ate a man’s grain they have set me here. They talked a very long while and then took their leave and went.

The master asked Grain-Thief: who were those just now? He answered: they are the essence of gold and treasure, and they live three hundred paces and more west of here under a great tree, where there are a hundred stone jars full of gold. The master then took several tens of men and went and dug, and got the jars of gold, and the household rejoiced and carted it home.

And they bowed their heads before Grain-Thief: getting this gold today is by your great divine kindness; might you stay, divine one, and come home with us, and we shall set out offerings again? Grain-Thief said: earlier I ate your grain and did not tell you my name, because I wished you to obtain this recompense in gold; and now I must go on and make merit throughout the world and cannot stay any longer. And when he had said this he suddenly was not seen (Source: Parables Sūtra, fascicle 2).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A folktale rather than a sūtra narrative, and the compendium files it under a section of its own. The creature will not name itself and can only be named by another — and the ones who can name it are the personified essence of the buried gold, travelling in yellow carriages with yellow clothing and yellow attendants, who recognize it at once and call out to it as an acquaintance.

The reversal at the end is what makes it worth keeping. The householder assumes he has been robbed of a thousand bushels and then compensated, and thanks the creature for the gold. The creature's answer is that the two were one transaction from the start: I ate your grain and did not tell you my name, because I wished you to obtain this recompense in gold. It then declines the offer of a permanent shrine and goes off to do the same elsewhere.

Worms 蟲第七

One entry: the great worm in the sewage pool of Rājagṛha, and the five hundred maṇi pearls it kept for itself.

28. The Great Worm in the Standing Water and the Karmic Cause of Its Former Life

The monastery head who kept five hundred merchants' pearls, told the community they could have his dung instead, and threatened to cut off their hands and feet.

Chinese Source

王舍城東南嵎有一[泳-永+狂]水。城內溝瀆污穢。屎尿盡趣其中。臭不可近。有一大蟲生[泳-永+狂]水內。身長數丈無有手足。而宛轉低仰戲[泳-永+狂]水中。觀者數千。阿難分衛見而往視。蟲即跳梁波浪動涌。具以啟佛。佛與諸比丘共詣池所。眾人見佛。各各念言。今日如來。當為眾會說蟲本末以釋眾疑不當快乎。佛言。維衛佛泥洹後。時有塔寺。有五百比丘經過寺中。寺主見大歡喜。請留供養三月眾皆受請。寺主盡心供饌無有所遺。後五百商人入海採寶還過塔寺。見五百比丘精勤行道。並各發心。欣然共議。福田難遇當設薄供。便白寺主。寺主報言。我請三月更五日滿。乃得廣設。賈人言。吾等當去不得待竟。五百商人各捨一珠。得五百摩尼珠。以寄寺主。囑寺主言。日足以吾等珠供於僧眾。比丘言諾。即皆受之。後生不善心。圖欲獨取。卒不為供。眾僧問言。主前賈客施珠應當設供。而發遣耶。寺主言。是施我耳。若欲奪吾糞可施汝。若不時去。劓汝手足投於糞坑。眾愍其癡默然各去(出十卷譬喻經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

At the southeast corner of the city of Rājagṛha there was a pool of standing water, into which the drains and filth of the city and all the excrement and urine ran, and it stank so that one could not come near.

There was a great worm born in that standing water, its body several fathoms long and having no hands or feet, which writhed and rose and dipped and played in the water; and several thousand people watched it. Ānanda, on alms round, saw it and went to look, and the worm leapt and the waves stirred and surged; and he reported it in full to the Buddha.

The Buddha and the monks went together to the pool, and the crowd, seeing the Buddha, each thought: today the Tathāgata will surely explain to the assembly this worm’s beginning and end and resolve everyone’s doubt — will that not be good?

The Buddha said: after the nirvāṇa of the Buddha Vipaśyin there was a stūpa and monastery, and five hundred monks passed through that monastery; and the monastery’s head, seeing them, was greatly delighted and asked them to stay and be provided for three months, and the whole company accepted. The head provided food with all his heart and left nothing out.

Afterward five hundred merchants who had gone to sea to gather treasure passed the stūpa and monastery on their way back, and, seeing the five hundred monks diligently practicing the path, each of them gave rise to the thought and gladly conferred together: a field of merit is hard to meet with; we should set out a modest offering. And they said so to the head of the monastery.

The head answered: I have invited them for three months and there are five days more before it is full, and then a great offering may be set out. The merchants said: we must go and cannot wait to the end. And the five hundred merchants each gave up one pearl, five hundred maṇi pearls in all, and entrusted them to the head of the monastery, charging him: when the days are full, use our pearls to provide for the community. The monk said: yes — and received them all.

Afterward he gave rise to an unwholesome mind and schemed to take them for himself, and in the end made no offering. The community of monks asked him: the merchants who came before gave pearls and an offering should be set out; and did you send them off? The head said: they were given to me. If you wish to take them from me, I may give you my dung; and if you do not go at once, I shall cut off your hands and feet and throw them into a dung pit. And the community, pitying his folly, kept silence and each went away (Source: Ten-Fascicle Parables Sūtra, fascicle 4).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The offense here is stated with an unusual precision about ownership. Five hundred merchants gave five hundred maṇi pearls to the head of a monastery, not to him but through him, and specified the purpose and the day; and he kept them. What the community gets when it asks is the line the entry is built around — if you wish to take them from me, I may give you my dung — followed by a threat to cut off their hands and feet and throw them in a dung pit.

The rebirth answers the words exactly: a limbless body several fathoms long, in the sewage pool of Rājagṛha, watched by thousands. The compendium does not draw the parallel and does not need to.

This closes fascicle 48. Both of its halves end on the same note — the birds' half with a crow arguing that lions and peacocks eat flesh too, and the creatures' half with a flea that could not keep an agreement, identified as Devadatta, and a louse that could, identified as the Buddha.

Lice 虱第八

One entry: a louse that kept a bargain with a meditating monk, and a flea that did not.

29. A Louse Living on a Meditator Agrees to Drink Blood Only at Set Times

A louse that observed the terms, a flea that could not resist the smell, and one robe burned.

Chinese Source

過去久遠。應現如來滅度以後。像法之中有一坐禪比丘。獨在林中常患蟣虱。即便共約。我若坐禪汝宜默然。身隱寂住虱甚如法。於後一時。有一土蚤來至虱邊。問言。汝今云何身體肥盛。虱言。所依主人常修禪定。教我飲食時節。我如法行所以鮮肥。蚤言。我亦欲修習其法。虱言隨意。蚤聞血肉香即便食噉。比丘苦惱。即便脫衣以火燒之。坐禪比丘迦葉佛是。土蚤者提婆達多是。虱者我是(出報恩經第四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the far distant past, after the nirvāṇa of the Tathāgata Manifest-in-Response, in the age of the semblance Dharma, there was a monk sitting in meditation alone in a wood who constantly suffered from lice and nits; and he made an agreement with them: while I sit in meditation you should keep still. And his body being hidden and dwelling in stillness, the louse observed the rule.

Afterward, at one time, a flea came to the louse and asked: how is your body now so fat and full? The louse said: the master I depend on constantly cultivates meditative concentration, and he has taught me the times for eating and drinking; and because I keep the rule I am plump and glossy.

The flea said: I too wish to practice that method. The louse said: as you please. But the flea, smelling the fragrance of blood and flesh, at once ate and fed; and the monk was distressed and took off his robe and burned it with fire.

The monk sitting in meditation is the Buddha Kāśyapa; the flea is Devadatta; the louse is myself (Source: Sūtra on Repaying Kindness, fascicle 4).
