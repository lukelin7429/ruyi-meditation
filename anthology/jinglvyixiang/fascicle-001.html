The Heavens, the Ages of the World, and the Sky Above

This page carries Ru-Yi's complete translation of fascicle 1 of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相): the compendium's own preface explaining why and how it was compiled, followed by its first topical section, "Heavenly Beings, Part 1" (天部上) — the tiers of gods across the desire and form realms and the four formless spheres, the three small and three great calamities that end and renew a world-age, the length of a single kalpa, and finally the sun, moon, stars, thunder, lightning, clouds, wind, and rain.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 1 complete Source: Taishō T2121, vol. 53, fascicle 1 Compiled by: Bǎochàng and others, 516 CE

Translation Method

The Chinese source is the sixth-century compilation of the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng (經律異相), preserved in the Taishō canon (T2121, vol. 53) via the CBETA digital edition — public domain text, translated directly from the Chinese. No complete modern English translation of this fifty-fascicle compendium exists; the English wording throughout is Ru-Yi's own.

Unlike a scholastic treatise built from tight logical argument, this compendium is a leishu (類書) — a topical anthology of narrative excerpts drawn from many separate sūtras and vinaya texts. The translation therefore favors a readable narrative voice over rigid sentence-by-sentence mirroring, while keeping proper names, place names, numbers, and technical terms consistent and precise. Where the compendium's own citation gives a source text, that citation is carried into the translation. Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Tathāgata manifested his traces according to conditions, expounding the teaching according to each being's capacity — reaching even nāgas and spirits, not only gods and men. His transformative activity opened with Kauṇḍinya and closed with Subhadra. The resulting texts piled up by the tens of thousands, filling the great-thousandfold world, so vast that even carrying them from west to east, one could hardly study them exhaustively. As with Liu Xiang's collation of the imperial library, profound sayings had long lain hidden; as with Emperor Ming of Han's dream, spiritual proofs grew ever clearer. From that time on, translators succeeded one another in an unbroken line, and though the profound scriptures of the Three Baskets had already been roughly covered, the miscellaneous sayings of the nine divisions had not yet been sorted and gathered by category.

皇帝同契等覺比德遍知。大弘經教並利法俗。廣延博古旁採遺文。於是散偈流章往往而出。今之所獲蓋亦多矣。聖旨以為。象正浸末信樂彌衰。文句浩漫鮮能該洽。以天監七年。勅釋僧旻等。備鈔眾典。顯證深文控會神宗。辭略意曉。於鑽求者已有太半之益。但希有異相猶散眾篇。難聞祕說未加標顯。又以十五年末。

The present Emperor, matching the level of the Perfectly Enlightened in his equal-reaching wisdom, has broadly promoted the teaching of the sūtras for the benefit of both dharma and laity, extensively drawing in scholars of antiquity and gathering up neglected writings from every side. In this way scattered verses and dispersed chapters have again and again come to light, and what has now been recovered is already a great deal. His sacred intent held that as the semblance dharma sinks ever further into decline, faith and joy in it grow ever weaker, and that the texts, being so vast and rambling, few readers can master them comprehensively. Therefore, in the seventh year of Tianjian [508 CE], he decreed that the monk Sengmin and others should thoroughly excerpt the various scriptures, bringing to light their profound meaning and drawing together their essential purport, in language concise and sense clear — and this already brought great benefit to earnest students. But the rare and extraordinary passages remained scattered across many separate texts, and the seldom-heard secret teachings had not yet been singled out and made plain. Then, at the end of the fifteenth year [516 CE],

勅寶唱鈔經律要事。皆使以類相從。令覽者易了。又勅新安寺釋僧豪。興皇寺釋法生等。相助檢讀。於是博綜經籍擇採祕要。上詢神慮取則成規。凡為五十卷。又目錄五卷。分為五秩。名為經律異相。將來學者。可不勞而博矣。

he decreed that Bǎochàng should excerpt the essential matters of sūtra and vinaya, arranging them all by category, so that readers might grasp them with ease. He further decreed that the monk Sengháo of Xin'an Monastery and the monk Fashēng of Xinghuang Monastery, among others, should assist in checking and reading the material. In this way, drawing broadly on the scriptural canon and selecting out its essential and secret matters, and consulting the imperial mind above to fix the final plan, the work came to fifty fascicles in all, with a further five fascicles of table of contents, divided into five bound sets. It is named the Jīng Lǜ Yì Xiàng — A Comparative Compendium of Sūtra and Vinaya — so that students to come may attain broad learning without labor.

1. The Heavens of the Three Realms — The Desire Realm

The desire realm's six heavens, from the Four Heavenly Kings up to Māra, each with its ruler, its beings' stature and lifespan, and the customs of food and marriage that mark how desire itself operates at that level.

Ru-Yi Translation
The Four Heavenly Kings, 1

The Four Heavenly Kings dwell on the four cornices of Sumeru, each cornice forty-two thousand yojanas high (the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra says: Sumeru's four sides all bear the name Yugandhara, each forty-two thousand yojanas high, and the Four Heavenly Kings govern from atop them). The Heavenly King of the East is named Dhṛtarāṣṭra, his city called Superior Worthy. The Heavenly King of the South is named Virūḍhaka, his city called Well-Seen. The Heavenly King of the West is named Virūpākṣa, his city called Zhouluo (or perhaps Zhoubà — the correct form is uncertain). The Heavenly King of the North is named Vaiśravaṇa, who dwells in three cities in all: Awe-Inspiring, Heaven's Reverence, and Multitude's Refuge. All four kings are half a yojana tall, their robes a yojana long and half a yojana wide, weighing two-tenths of an ounce. Their heavenly lifespan is five hundred years, more often cut short than fulfilled; fifty human years equal one day and night for them, thirty such days make a month, twelve months a year (five hundred of their years thus equal ninety thousand human years). They eat pure-morsel food and bathe and dress as delicately as beings born of subtle contact. Men take wives and women are given in marriage, their conjugal relations exactly as among humans. Through the good conduct of their three former karmic acts, they are now born as gods, appearing spontaneously upon the knees of a heavenly parent, their bodies at birth the size of a two-year-old human child (another record says: boys sit on their father's knee, girls on their mother's). No sooner born than the child knows hunger of itself; vessels of the seven treasures, filled with food of a hundred flavors, appear before it. If its merit is great the rice appears white of itself; if middling, blue; if slight, red. Once the child has digested its food, should it grow thirsty, a jeweled vessel holds ambrosia the very color of the food just eaten; drinking it leaves no residue, vanishing like ghee cast into fire. As the body grows to the stature of the other gods, it bathes together with them in the pools, and goes beneath the fragrant trees, whose branches bend low; there it takes fragrant substances to anoint the body, garments, ornaments, garlands, jeweled vessels, fruits, and musical instruments, each produced from its own tree, freely taken wherever desired (the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra tells much the same, with minor differences, too long to record here). Entering the many gardens and groves, innumerable heavenly maidens play music, sing, and converse with laughter on every side; deep attachment arises, the eyes fixed west forgetting east — and so, absorbed in play and pleasure, the newborn god forgets even the very thought that first brought it there, its birth in heaven owing entirely to good deeds accumulated in a former life (the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra is roughly the same). The pools are clear and pure, flowers and fruit flourish; the city walls, sevenfold in all, each measure six thousand yojanas across; balustrades, latticework nets, palace walls, and rows of trees are likewise all sevenfold. King Vaiśravaṇa is constantly attended by five great spirits, ever at his side. Twice monthly, on the fasting days of the eighth, fourteenth, and fifteenth, the Four Heavenly Kings send their messengers to make their rounds through the world, observing the people — noting who is filial to father and mother, reverent to śramaṇas, brāhmaṇas, and elders, who keeps the fast precepts and gives alms, and who does not — and the messengers report faithfully all they find; hearing of evil the kings are displeased, hearing of good they rejoice. On the fourteenth day the four kings send their crown princes to make the same rounds; on the fifteenth the Four Heavenly Kings themselves make the rounds in person, and afterward report in full before Śakra in the Hall of the Good Law — hearing of evil he grieves, hearing of good he rejoices, and utters a verse praising those who keep the fast precepts as walking the same path as himself (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20; also the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra and the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra).

忉利天第二

忉利天居須彌山頂。有三十三天宮。王名釋提桓因(梁言能作天王)身長一由旬衣長二由旬。廣二由旬衣重六銖。壽天千歲少出多減。若欲終時有五相現。一者衣裳垢膩。二者頭上華萎。三者身體臭穢。四者腋下汗流。五者不樂本座。見五事時心大苦惱如地獄苦。飲食嫁娶猶如四天。身體相近以氣成陰陽(出三法度經云行欲如人)以身口意善生忉利天。自然化現在天膝上如三歲兒。天即認言。是我男我女。自識前世布施持戒。欲得飲食隨滿金器。福有深淺食有優降。如四天王天。城縱廣八萬由旬(樓炭經云。廣長三百二十萬里)其城七重。九百九十九門。門有六十青衣夜叉守之(長阿含經云。高六十由旬。相去五百由旬。有一門門有五百鬼神守衛)三十三天。金城銀門銀城金門。如是七寶互為城門。樓閣臺觀周匝圍繞。園林浴池寶花間雜。寶樹行列華果繁茂。香風四起悅可人心。異類奇鳥無數和鳴。其四園中各有二石垛。各各縱廣五十由旬。七寶所成軟若天衣(樓炭經說相似文多不載)麁澁園盡樂園中間有難陀池。縱廣百由旬其水清澄。七重寶塹生四種華。青黃赤白紅縹雜色。香氣普熏聞一由旬。根如車轂汁白如乳味甘如蜜。復有雜園大歡喜園。中間有樹名晝度。圍七由旬高百由旬。枝葉四布五十由旬。其香逆風百由旬內。忉利殿南又有一樹。名波質拘耆羅。高四千里。枝葉分布二千里。風吹花香逆風行聞二千里。當樹花時諸天共坐樹下以為歡樂。經遊天一百二十日。帝釋有三十二大臣。故言三十三天也。各有宮皆在城內。遊戲園中必經七日。麁澁者入此園時身體麁澁。畫者入此園時。身體自然種種畫色以相悅樂。雜者常以月八日十四日十五日。放諸婇女與諸天子雜遊。獨與舍脂共在一處名為雜。大喜者入此園時心大歡喜(出長阿含經第二十卷。又出樓炭。大智論。華嚴。涅槃)。

Trāyastriṃśa, 2

Trāyastriṃśa Heaven sits atop Sumeru's summit, where thirty-three heavenly palaces stand; its king is named Śakra Devānām Indra (in the Liang tongue, "the one able to act as king of the gods"). He is one yojana tall, his robe two yojanas long and two wide, weighing six zhu. His heavenly lifespan is a thousand years, again more often cut short than fulfilled. When his life is about to end, five signs appear: his garments grow soiled, the flowers on his head wilt, his body gives off a foul odor, sweat flows from beneath his arms, and he no longer delights in his own throne — seeing these five signs, his heart suffers as greatly as one in hell. His food, drink, and marriage customs are just as among the Four Heavenly Kings; his body draws near another's and conceives through the meeting of breath alone (the Tridharmaka Sūtra says their acts of desire are like a human's). Through good acts of body, speech, and mind he is born in Trāyastriṃśa, appearing spontaneously on a heavenly parent's knee like a three-year-old child; the god at once acknowledges him, saying, "This is my son," or "my daughter." The child recalls of itself the giving and precept-keeping of a former life, and wishing for food, finds a golden vessel filled according to its wish — merit deep or shallow yielding food superior or inferior, just as with the Four Heavenly Kings. The city measures eighty thousand yojanas across (the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra says three million two hundred thousand li across), sevenfold walled, with nine hundred and ninety-nine gates, each guarded by sixty blue-robed yakṣas. The thirty-three heavens have gold walls with silver gates and silver walls with gold gates, the seven treasures alternating as wall and gate; towers, terraces, and belvederes surround them on every side, gardens and pools intermingled with jeweled flowers, jeweled trees heavy with fragrant fruit, fragrant winds delighting the heart, rare birds calling in chorus beyond number. Between the Rough Garden and the Garden of Utter Joy lies Nandana Pool, a hundred yojanas across, growing four kinds of lotus whose fragrance spreads a full yojana. Between the Mixed Garden and the Garden of Great Joy stands a tree named Pāricchattaka, its branches spreading fifty yojanas in every direction, its fragrance carried a hundred yojanas against the wind; south of the Trāyastriṃśa palace stands another tree, four thousand li tall, whose blossoms the gods gather beneath in joy for a hundred and twenty heavenly days running. Śakra has thirty-two great ministers — hence the name "the thirty-three heavens." Entering the pleasure-gardens must always take seven days: in the Rough Garden the body becomes rough to the touch; in the Painted Garden the body spontaneously takes on painted colors for mutual delight; in the Mixed Garden, on the eighth, fourteenth, and fifteenth days, the celestial maidens mix freely with the young gods, while Śakra alone remains together with Śacī — hence "Mixed"; in the Garden of Great Joy the heart knows great joy the moment one enters (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20; also the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra, the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, and the Nirvāṇa Sūtra).

炎摩天第三

炎摩(梁言時)天宮。風輪所持在虛空中。王名善時(大智論云妙善)身長二由旬。衣長四由旬廣二由旬。衣重三銖從樹而出。明淨光曜有種種色。身體光明不須日月。身口意善。或以燈燭明珠等施。持戒禪定等業生炎摩天。壽天二千歲少出多減。食飲婚欲猶如忉利天(三法度經云以染著意相抱成欲若不深著不成歡樂耳)初生之形如人間四歲。忉利天光明所不能及(出長阿含經第二十卷又出樓炭經大智論華嚴)。

Yama Heaven, 3

Yama Heaven (in the Liang tongue, "Time") — its palace, borne up by the wheel of wind, stands in open space; its king is named Suyāma, "Excellent Season" (the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra says "Wondrously Good"). He is two yojanas tall, his robe four yojanas long and two wide, weighing three zhu, growing directly from a tree, bright and radiant, of every color; his body shines of itself, needing neither sun nor moon. Through good acts of body, speech, and mind — through giving, such as lamps or luminous pearls, and through the precepts and meditative concentration — one is born in Yama Heaven, whose heavenly lifespan is two thousand years, again more often cut short than fulfilled. Food, drink, and the ways of marriage are just as in Trāyastriṃśa (the Tridharmaka Sūtra says desire arises here from mutual embrace born of attached intent — if the attachment is not deep, no joy results). At birth the form is like that of a four-year-old human child, its light such that even Trāyastriṃśa's brightness cannot reach it (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20; also the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra, the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, and the Avataṃsaka Sūtra).

兜率天第四

兜率(梁言知足)天宮。風輪所持在虛空中。王名善喜。後邊身菩薩多生此天。下天放誕上天闇鈍故生此天(出大智論云下地結使厚濁。上地結使利。唯兜率天。不厚不利。智慧安隱。又下地命短終時佛未出世。上地命長壽未盡佛復過去兜率天時命等故。又佛常居中道兜率天於六天為中)身長四由旬。衣長八由旬。廣四由旬。衣重一銖半。壽天四千歲少出多減。食同下天。亦有嫁娶執手成欲。其天初生如人五歲。自知前世所作布施持戒等事。食自然飲食衣冠歌舞。身有光明勝於炎摩(出長阿含經第二十卷又出華嚴涅槃樓炭大智論)。

Tuṣita, 4

Tuṣita Heaven (in the Liang tongue, "Contentment") — its palace, too, is borne up by the wheel of wind in open space; its king is named Sudṛṣṭi, "Excellent Joy." Bodhisattvas in their final rebirth before buddhahood are most often born in this heaven, since the heavens below are too unrestrained and those above too dull, whereas this heaven alone suits (the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra says: the afflictions of the lower stages are thick and turbid, those of the higher stages sharp yet still troubling the mind, and Tuṣita alone is neither thick nor sharp, its wisdom calm and settled; moreover the lower stages' lifespans are too short and the higher stages' too long for a buddha's own era to fall within them, and the Buddha always dwells in the middle way — of the six desire-realm heavens, Tuṣita is the middle one). Its beings are four yojanas tall, their robes eight yojanas long and four wide; their heavenly lifespan is four thousand years, more often cut short than fulfilled. Their food is as in the heavens below; they too take wives and husbands, desire consummated by the clasping of hands. At birth a being here is like a five-year-old human child, knowing of itself the giving and precept-keeping of a former life; food, drink, dress, and song and dance arise spontaneously, its light surpassing Yama Heaven (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20; also the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra, and the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra).

化樂天第五

化樂天宮。亦為風輪所持在虛空中。王名善化。自化五塵以自娛樂。身長八由旬。衣長十六由旬。廣八由旬。衣重一銖。壽天八千歲少出多減。食與下同。亦有嫁娶熟相視成欲(三法度經云。以深染污心。與天女共語成欲若一不染不成。但樂而已)其天初生如人六歲。自光明殊勝兜率(出長阿含經第二十卷。又出樓炭華嚴)。

Nirmāṇarati, 5

The palace of the Heaven of Delight in Transformation is likewise borne up by the wheel of wind in open space; its king is named Sunirmita, "Excellent Transformation," who transforms the five sense-objects at will for his own enjoyment. Its beings are eight yojanas tall, their robes sixteen yojanas long and eight wide; their heavenly lifespan is eight thousand years, more often cut short than fulfilled. Their food is as in the heavens below; they too take wives and husbands, desire consummated by a mutual, lingering gaze (the Tridharmaka Sūtra says: with a heart of deep attachment, speaking together with a heavenly maiden brings desire to completion — if either party lacks attachment, no completion follows, only pleasure). At birth a being here is like a six-year-old human child, its light surpassing Tuṣita (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20; also the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra and the Avataṃsaka Sūtra).

他化自在天第六

The palace of Māra Heaven lies between the desire and form realms; "Māra" (魔) is likened to a grindstone (磨), for it grinds away merit. It measures six thousand yojanas across, sevenfold walled, adorned in every way just like the heavens below, and possesses ten qualities: flying away without limit; flying back without limit; going without hindrance; coming without hindrance; a heavenly body with no skin, bone, marrow, sinew, vein, blood, or flesh; a body free of urine or excrement; a body that knows no fatigue; heavenly maidens who do not give birth; heavenly eyes that do not blink; and a body that takes on whatever color is wished. There are, further, ten more attributes: flying without limit, going and returning without limit, no theft among the gods, none speaking of his own goodness or another's evil, no mutual encroachment, teeth all even and identical, hair a lustrous dark blue-green eight zhang long, a body the same color as one's own hair, a body that turns white at will, and a body that turns black at will (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicles 18 and 30; also the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra and the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice the arithmetic repeated at every level: a heavenly day equals some large number of human years, thirty such days make a heavenly month, twelve months a heavenly year — and the deva's own lifespan, multiplied out, dwarfs a human one almost beyond comprehension. This is not decoration; it is the compendium's recurring argument that heavenly pleasure, however vast, is still bounded and still ends, which is precisely why none of these seven heavens — for all their light, food, and effortless pleasure — is ever offered here as an escape from the cycle of rebirth.

Its heavenly lifespan is eighty-four thousand kalpas, or again somewhat less (Saṃyukta and Ekottarika Āgamas; the same sūtra says: above this there is a heaven named "Without Being," also called "Non-Thought yet Having Thought").

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Udraka Rāmaputra and Uddaka Rāmaputra were the two teachers under whom the future Buddha trained before his awakening, mastering precisely these two highest formless attainments — nothingness and neither-perception-nor-non-perception — and finding both insufficient. The compendium's placement of their deaths here, at the very ceiling of what meditation alone can reach, makes a pointed argument: even the subtlest possible state of concentration, unaccompanied by liberating insight, still ends in an unchosen rebirth. It is the same argument the entire "Heavens" section has been building toward one tier at a time.

2. Formation and Destruction of the Three Realms

Two accounts of how a world-age ends: the three small calamities that recur within an age of long decline, and the three great calamities — fire, water, and wind — that periodically destroy and then remake the cosmos entirely.

The Three Small Calamities, 1

At the beginning of a kalpa, human lifespans reach forty thousand years; thereafter they steadily decline until only a hundred years remain, and decline further still until only ten years remain (the Saṃyuktābhidharma-hṛdaya agrees; the Extract on the Lesser Kalpa says twelve-odd years). Girls are already given in marriage at five months of age. At the time when human life is ten years, this is called the Three Small Kalpas: first, warfare; second, famine (the Dīrgha Āgama calls it "grain grown dear"); third, pestilence. In the calamity of warfare, people, given over to greed and coarse conduct, follow the ten evil paths; should anyone perform a single good deed, the crowd mocks and derides him, driving him out as a fool and vying to destroy him; each imitates the others' evil until not a single good person remains. The five grains fail to grow and every fine flavor vanishes; silk, damask, and cotton cloth are exhausted of themselves. People eat only wild grasses and weave clothing from straw; the seven treasures sink from sight, and sand and stone cover everything. The earth sprouts brambles, and every branch and twig becomes a sword or blade; even the trees supporting a footbridge collapse and rot. The earth is riddled with ditches and pits; waves surge and banks crumble; rivers grow wider while level ground shrinks. Once the warfare calamity begins, within seven days every blade of grass, tile, and stone in the people's hands turns into a sword or blade, and they seize and plunder one another, trembling in terror, wishing only to kill (the Dīrgha Āgama says the calamity lasts seven full days before people emerge once more) — like a hunter coming upon a herd of deer: among them the wise flee far off into mountain valleys where no one dwells, eating fruit and drinking water there to pass out the full ten years (the Dīrgha Āgama says they come out again after seven days). Those who kill one another to the last are reborn in hell; this is called the warfare kalpa.

飢餓劫者。人多非法愚癡邪見。慳貪嫉妬守財不施。水旱不節田種無收。米糓轉盡食粒驚貴。掃擇粃糠街巷落葉以自連命。粃葉既盡穿鑿地下食草木根。不能與者在先而死。刳剔死人復共食噉。噉之轉竭於屠殺之處。乃至塚間拾諸骸骨。煮汁飲之以此自活。飢死盡者生餓鬼中。名飢餓小劫。疾病劫者。人皆正見修行十善。疾病眾多無他方計少有醫藥。雖行眾善不能攘逆。薄福德故遇病輒死。神共來侵嬈撾打杖捶。使其心亂接其精神殺之將去。人命既終皆生天上。名疾病劫。若能一日一夜持不殺戒。終不生刀兵劫中。若以一呵梨勒果施僧。終不生疾疫劫中。若一食施僧。終不生飢饉劫中。

In the calamity of famine, people, given over in great number to lawlessness, folly, and wrong views, are stingy, greedy, envious, and hoarding, giving nothing away; the rains fail to fall in season and the crops in the fields go unharvested. Rice and grain are gradually used up, and grain itself becomes alarmingly dear; people gather up husks, chaff, and fallen leaves from street and lane to keep themselves alive, and once husk and leaf too are exhausted, they dig into the earth to eat the roots of grass and tree; those unable to give to others die first of all; then they cut up the dead to eat them, and this too growing scarce, they go even to the slaughtering-grounds, and on to the burial grounds, to gather bones and boil them for their broth, sustaining themselves so. Those who starve to death are reborn among the hungry ghosts; this is called the small kalpa of famine. In the calamity of pestilence, people all hold right views and practice the ten good deeds; yet illness spreads widely with no remedy at hand and medicine scarce, so that even those who do much good cannot ward it off, and — their merit being thin — fall sick and die at once, as spirits come to torment and beat them, deranging their minds and seizing their vital spirit before carrying them away. Yet once their lives have ended, all such people are born in the heavens; this is called the pestilence kalpa. If one can, for even a single day and night, keep the precept against killing, one will never be born amid the warfare kalpa; if one gives even a single myrobalan fruit to the sangha, one will never be born amid the pestilence kalpa; if one gives even a single meal to the sangha, one will never be born amid the famine kalpa.

此閻浮提惡劫互起餘方則少。此間刀兵劫起彼惟重瞋。此疾疫劫起彼惟氣力羸劣。此間飢饉劫起。彼但小渴乏耳(出長阿含經第二十二卷又出三小劫經抄又出雜心第十四卷)。

These evil kalpas arise in this Jambudvīpa one after another, while in other regions they are lighter: where the warfare kalpa arises here, elsewhere there is only heavy anger; where the pestilence kalpa arises here, elsewhere the body is merely somewhat weakened; where the famine kalpa arises here, elsewhere there is only slight thirst and want (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 22; also the Extract on the Three Small Kalpas; also the Saṃyuktābhidharma-hṛdaya, fascicle 14).

三大災第二

天地始終謂之一劫。劫盡壞時火災將起。一切民人皆背正向邪競行十惡。天久不雨所種不生。諸水泉源。乃至四大駛河皆悉枯竭。久久之後風入海底。取日上大城郭。於須彌山邊置本道中(雜心曰劫滅之時有七日輪住由乾陀山從彼而出又說分一日為七日也又說從阿鼻地獄出眾生業力致也)一日出時。百草樹木一時彫落。二日出時。四大海水從百由旬。乃至七百由旬內。水自然枯涸。三日出時。四大海水千由旬。乃至七千由旬內。水展轉消竭。四日出時。四大海水深千由旬。五日出時。四大海水縱餘七百由旬。乃至竭盡。六日出時。此地厚六萬八千由旬皆悉煙出。從須彌山。乃至三千大千剎土。及八地獄。靡不燒滅煙燼無餘。人民命終皆依須彌山。五種諸天。三十三天。炎天乃至他化自在天。皆悉命終宮殿皆空。一切無常不得久住。七日出時。大地須彌山漸漸崩壞。百由旬永無遺餘。金銀銅鐵之類皆悉流鑠。稍就枯竭山皆洞然。諸寶爆裂崩阤砰礚煙炎振動至于梵天。一切惡道及阿修倫皆悉蕩盡。罪終福至皆集第十五天上。十四以下盡成炎墨。新生天子未曾見此普懷恐懼。舊生天子各來慰勞。勿生恐怖終不至此。人民命終生光音天以念為食。光明自照神足飛行。或生他土若生地獄。地獄罪畢亦生天上。若罪未畢亦生天上。若罪未畢復移他方無日月星宿亦無晝夜。唯有大冥謂之火劫火災。因緣果報致此壞敗。

The Three Great Calamities, 2

Heaven and earth, from their beginning to their end, together make up a single kalpa. When the kalpa comes to an end and is about to be destroyed, the calamity of fire arises first. All the people then turn their backs on the right and rush toward the wrong, vying to practice the ten evils; heaven withholds rain for a long time, and what has been sown fails to grow; every spring and stream, down to the four great swift rivers, all run dry. Long afterward, wind stirs at the bottom of the ocean and lifts up the great citadel that bears the sun, setting it down beside Sumeru, within its proper track (the Saṃyuktābhidharma-hṛdaya says: at the destruction of a kalpa, seven suns come to dwell above Yugandhara Mountain, issuing from that place; another account says a single day is divided into seven; another says they issue from the Avīci hell, driven forth by the power of beings' karma). When the first sun appears, every plant and tree withers and falls at once. When the second sun appears, the water of the four great oceans recedes a hundred yojanas, even to seven hundred yojanas, and dries up of itself. When the third sun appears, the water recedes a thousand yojanas, even to seven thousand, draining away by degrees. When the fourth sun appears, the water, a thousand yojanas deep, recedes further still. When the fifth sun appears, the water, though a bare seven hundred yojanas deep remained, is now utterly exhausted. When the sixth sun appears, this earth, sixty-eight thousand yojanas thick, gives off smoke on every side, from Sumeru all the way to the lands and oceans of the three-thousandfold great-thousandfold world, together with the eight hells, all consumed and burned to ashes without remainder. As the people's lives come to an end, they all take refuge upon Sumeru — the five classes of gods, the Trāyastriṃśa, Yama Heaven, all the way to Paranirmitavaśavartin, likewise reach the end of their lives, their palaces standing empty; all is impermanent, nothing endures. When the seventh sun appears, the great earth and Sumeru itself gradually crumble away, not a hundred yojanas of it left remaining; gold, silver, copper, and iron all melt and flow, and are gradually exhausted, the very mountains ablaze; every kind of jewel cracks and shatters, crumbling and roaring, its smoke and flame shaking all the way up to the Brahmā heavens; every evil destiny and every asura is entirely swept away. Once retribution has run its course and merit has arrived, all beings gather together above the fifteenth heaven; from the fourteenth heaven downward, all is turned to blackened ash. The newly born gods there, never having witnessed such a thing, are seized with universal dread, but the gods born there long before come to console them, saying it will never reach so far. As for the people who have died, they are reborn in the Ābhāsvara Heaven, taking thought itself as food, their own light shining of itself, their supernormal feet bearing them wherever they will; some are born in other lands, some in hell — and even those in hell, once their offenses are exhausted, are likewise reborn in heaven; and should their offenses not yet be exhausted, they are moved on again to a region with neither sun, moon, star, nor constellation, and no day or night at all, but only a vast darkness — this is called the fire kalpa, the calamity of fire, brought about entirely through causes and conditions, through karmic retribution, and thus does destruction come.

劫欲成時火乃自滅。更起大雲漸降大雨渧如車軸。是時此三千大千剎土。水遍其中乃至梵天(雜心曰水災所壞至第二禪水從第三禪降雨熱灰水又說水輪出也)謂為水劫水災。復有四風持水不散。一名住。二曰助。三曰不動。四曰堅。經數千億萬歲。水上泡沫化作千第十四天宮皆悉眾寶。水漸減隨嵐吹鼓。次第轉作天下諸天及日月宮殿。次作千須彌山。次第乃至千四天下地。山河城池水上清潔。初作天宮眾寶所成。光明最勝轉減轉濁。諸天宮殿七寶光明漸下漸劣。地欲露時水沙流急。隨下爭赴遂成川河流入于海海深八萬四千由旬。其廣無邊(樓炭經云深八百四十萬由旬)須彌山在於海中。出海又八萬四千由旬。水味醎苦。劫初成時自然雲起至光音天。周遍降雨洗濯天宮。滌蕩萬物諸不淨汁。下流入海令為醎苦。又有大仙人。呪使醎苦令人不飲。又有雜類眾生居之。便利其中故成醎苦。謂為風劫風災(雜心云風災所壞至第三禪百億四天下一時俱壞)此三及地為四災四劫。除地餘三說為大劫。過地種劫者。劫壞所及。唯未曾至第四禪。為淨居天故。無上地可生。即於彼處涅槃亦不下生。非數減故變成天地。天地更始盪盪空虛。了無所有亦無日月。地涌甘泉味如蘇蜜。時光音諸天。或有福盡來生。或樂觀新地性多輕躁以指甞之。如是再三轉得其味。食之不已漸生麁肌。失天妙色神足光明冥然大闇。

When the kalpa is about to re-form, the fire of itself dies away, and once more a great cloud arises and rains down heavily, its drops like the axle of a cart; at this time the whole three-thousandfold great-thousandfold world fills with water clear up to the Brahmā heaven (the Saṃyuktābhidharma-hṛdaya says: the calamity of water destroys as far as the second dhyāna, the water descending as rain of hot ash-water from the third dhyāna; another account says it is the wheel of water that then issues forth) — this is called the water kalpa, the calamity of water. Then four winds hold the water in place and prevent its dispersing: the first named Abiding, the second Assisting, the third Unmoving, the fourth Firm. After some thousands of millions of years, foam upon the water transforms and becomes the palaces of the fourteenth heaven, all made of every jewel; as the water gradually recedes, stirred by the whirling wind, in due order it forms the heavens below and the palaces of sun and moon, then the thousand Sumerus, then the lands of the thousand four-continent worlds — the mountains, rivers, cities, and pools upon the water all pure and clean. At first the heavenly palaces shine with the most excellent light, but this gradually diminishes and grows turbid; the light of every heavenly palace and hall likewise sinks gradually lower. As the earth is about to be exposed, the water and sand flow swiftly downward on every side until at last they form rivers that flow into the sea; the sea is eighty-four thousand yojanas deep, its breadth without limit (the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra says eight hundred and forty myriad yojanas deep); Sumeru stands within that sea, rising a further eighty-four thousand yojanas above it; the water's taste is salt and bitter. When the kalpa first re-forms, cloud arises of itself, reaching up to the Ābhāsvara Heaven, and rain falls all around, washing the heavenly palaces and cleansing the impure fluid of all things; this flows down into the sea and turns it salt and bitter — and there are, further, great sages who by incantation make it undrinkable to men, and various beings who dwell within it and, through their own excretions, likewise render it so — this is called the wind kalpa, the calamity of wind (the Saṃyuktābhidharma-hṛdaya says: the calamity of wind destroys as far as the third dhyāna, all the hundred-koṭi four-continent worlds destroyed together at one and the same time). These three, together with earth, make the four calamities and the four kalpas; apart from earth, the other three are called the great kalpas. Once past the earth-kalpa proper, the destruction of a kalpa never yet reaches the fourth dhyāna, since that is the abode of the Pure-Abode gods, for whom there exists no higher stage to be reborn into — it is there that they enter final nirvāṇa, never again descending to be reborn; since it is not subject to diminishing, heaven and earth begin anew there instead. Heaven and earth begin utterly afresh: vast, empty, with nothing whatsoever remaining, not even sun or moon. The earth wells up with sweet springs, tasting like cream and honey; at that time the Ābhāsvara gods — some whose merit is spent and who come to be reborn there, some who simply delight in observing the new earth — being of a nature restless and light, taste it with a finger; tasting it again and again, they come to know its flavor, and eating it without cease, gradually grow coarse flesh, losing their heavenly subtle radiance and supernormal light, and grow dark and dim.

後大黑風吹彼海水飄出日月。置須彌邊安日道中。繞須彌山照四天下。時諸人輩見出則歡見入則懼。自茲以後晝夜晦朔。春秋歲數。忽然復始。食之多者轉生醜顏。飡之少者尚遺妙色。美惡好醜漸漸而生。憍慢嫉妬次第而起。忿結諍競相續不絕。甘泉自涸地上生肥。其味香美有若甘露。時諸眾生復共食之。食之多者頓失威光體重生骨。食之少者身輕無累尚能飛行。重者見之皆大號哭。稱我窮厄住此世間。是非諍訟倍劇前法。資食地肥相看顏色。欲心多者變成女人。共相愛著遂行婬欲。如是流布。餘光音天見諸天子皆悉墮落。共來訶罵曰。汝等何為行不淨行。地肥轉入土中自生粳米。鮮淨無皮既香且美。食者肥白朝採暮生。人漸懈怠并取多日。極情恣欲無有時節。女懷胎孕復生眾生。餘人見之即加驅擯。遣出人外三月聽還。知生慚愧共作方宜。取諸草木起立宮舍。覆藏形體使人不見。習翫婬欲如是轉增。多取糧粒以為資儲。如是相學粳米荒穢。轉生糠䊯刈已不生。眾生見此心大憂惱。世有大災粳米復不如本。

Afterward a great black wind blows, lifting the sea's own water and casting up sun and moon, setting them beside Sumeru, fixed upon the sun's own track, circling Sumeru to illuminate the four continents; the people of that time, on seeing them rise, rejoice, and on seeing them set, are struck with fear. From this time on, day and night, dawn and dusk, spring and autumn, the years and their counting suddenly begin once more. Those who eat much of the earth's sweetness grow ugly of face in turn; those who eat little of it still retain something of their former beauty; beauty and ugliness gradually arise by degrees, and pride, envy, and jealousy arise one after another, quarrels and disputes following on without end. The sweet spring then dries up of itself, and the earth brings forth a rich cream, its fragrance and flavor like ambrosia; those who eat much of it at once lose their radiant light, their bodies grown heavy with bone, while those who eat little remain light of body, free of encumbrance, still able to fly; those grown heavy, on seeing this, weep aloud in anguish, proclaiming their own wretched poverty, dwelling now in this world — disputes and quarrels grow even more severe than before. Feeding upon the earth's cream, they gaze now upon one another's countenance; and those whose desire grows strong turn into women, and, cleaving to one another in mutual love, at length perform the act of lust, and this practice, once begun, spreads further still; the remaining Ābhāsvara gods, seeing these heavenly sons all fallen so, come together to rebuke them, saying, "Why do you practice such impure conduct?" The earth's cream then sinks into the soil and of itself gives rise to unhusked rice, fresh, pure, and free of any husk, both fragrant and delicious; those who eat it grow fat and fair, gathered in the morning yet grown again by evening. People gradually grow indolent and begin to gather up several days' worth at once, indulging their desires without restraint or season; a woman then conceives and bears further beings, and others, on seeing this, drive her out, banishing her beyond the human community, though after three months she is permitted to return. Knowing shame now, they together devise a remedy, gathering grass and wood to build houses to conceal their bodies from view; the practice of lustful desire, thus continuing, increases all the more, and they gather up ever greater stores of grain as provision. Following one another's example in this way, the unhusked rice grows wild and rank, and once reaped does not grow back; the beings, seeing this, are struck with great distress, and a great calamity comes upon the world — the unhusked rice no longer as it once was.

各自念言。我本生時以念為食。神足飛行光明自照。住此懈怠乃至如今。復相謂言。今共分地別立標記。封疆邊畔於是為始。自藏己分竊他禾米。米主見之曰。今恕汝罪後莫復為。如是轉多倍加呵責。呵責不已以手加之。以告眾人云。此人為盜。盜者又言。此人打我。眾人見此憂愁不樂。皆共集會議曰。眾生轉惡此是生老病死之原。煩惱苦報墮三惡道。由有田地致此諍訟。今者寧可共立聰明高才一人為主。以法理之可護者護。可責者責。應遣者遣。當共集米以相供給。選擇賢明形體端正有威德者。而語之言。汝為我等作平等主。善言慰勞眾皆歡喜。即共稱言。善哉大王。即以正法治民。名為剎利。皆是舊法。後人侵他物者即取懲罰。及重犯之便造督遮。鞭杖猶不能止又作牢獄刀杖等物。考楚殺戮令懷畏懼。時有一人念家多患猶如毒刺。棄捨妻兒獨處山林。起立草菴靜攝其志。修習梵行名婆羅門。後婆羅門有不樂閑靜坐禪思惟者。便入人間誦習為業。又自稱言。我是不禪人。於是世人號之為不禪婆羅門。時眾生中有人。好營居業多積財寶名為居士。又有多好機巧。名首陀羅。又有自厭世法剃除鬚髮法服修道。名曰沙門。時人心懷殺盜。又失粳米。立五種子。一者根子。二者葉子。三者華子。四者果子。五者莖生及餘種子。是謂五種之子。皆是風吹他方剎土種子來濟此國眾生。如此之瑞。有生老病死。有五盛陰。不盡苦際。

Each says to himself, "When I was first born, I took thought itself as food, my supernormal feet bearing me in flight, my own light shining of itself — how have I come, dwelling here in idleness, to this present state?" And they say to one another further, "Let us now together divide the land and set up separate boundary-markers" — and from this moment the practice of setting boundaries first began. Some then hide away their own portion and secretly steal another's rice; the owner, on discovering this, says, "I forgive your offense this once — do so no more." As this happens again and again, the reproaches grow more severe; when reproach no longer suffices, hands are raised against the offender, and it is proclaimed to all the people, "This man is a thief." The one accused in turn says, "This man struck me." The people, seeing this, grow anxious and unhappy, and gather in assembly, saying: "Sentient beings have grown ever more corrupt; this is the very origin of birth, old age, sickness, and death, the root of every affliction and suffering that casts one down into the three evil destinies. It is because there is land to till that these disputes have arisen; let us now together set up one man of wisdom and ability as our ruler, so that what deserves protection may be protected, what deserves reproach may be reproached, and what deserves banishment may be banished, and let us all together contribute rice for his provision." They choose one wise, worthy, upright in form, and possessed of dignity, and say to him: "Be for us a ruler of impartial justice." With kind words he consoles the people, and all rejoice, together proclaiming: "Excellent, great king!" — and thenceforth he governs the people by right and proper law, and is called a kṣatriya; this is the ancient origin of that name. Thereafter, whoever encroaches upon another's property is at once punished, and for the more serious offender, an office of correction is established; when even whipping and caning do not suffice, prisons, staves, and rods are made besides, and torture and execution are used to instill dread. At this time one man, reflecting that a household with its many cares is like a poisonous thorn, forsook wife and child and dwelt alone in the mountain forest, building a grass hut and quietly gathering in his will, cultivating the holy life — he was named a brāhmaṇa. Afterward, among brāhmaṇas, there arose those unwilling to dwell in quiet and practice seated meditation, and who instead entered among the people to recite and study as their occupation, calling themselves "those who do not meditate" — and so the men of that age came to call them "non-meditating brāhmaṇas." Among the people, there were also those who took delight in managing household affairs and amassing wealth, called householders; others fond of clever crafts, called śūdras; and others still who, wearied of the ways of the world, shaved their heads and beards and donned the monastic robe to practice the path, called śramaṇas. At this time people's hearts harbored killing and theft, and the unhusked rice was also lost to them; there then arose the five kinds of seeds — root-seeds, leaf-seeds, flower-seeds, fruit-seeds, and stem-growing and other seeds — all borne here by the wind from the lands of other world-realms, to relieve the beings of this land. With such a sign came old age, sickness, and death, and the five clinging aggregates, and the bounds of suffering were not yet exhausted.

水劫末時光音諸天入水澡浴。四大精氣入其身內。體生觸樂精流水中。八風吹盪墮淤泥中。自然成卵經八千歲。其卵乃開生一女人。其形青黑猶如淤泥。有九百九十九頭。有千眼九百九十九口。一口四牙牙上出火狀如礔礰。二十四手手中皆捉一切武器。其身高大如須彌山。入大海中拍水自樂。有旋嵐風吹大海水。水精入體即便懷任。經八千歲然後生男。身體高大四倍勝母。兒有九頭頭有千眼口中出火。有九百九十九手有八脚。於海水中自號。我是毘摩質多羅阿修羅王。唯噉淤泥及藕。地劫初成變易如是(出增一阿含經第三十二卷又出長阿含經第六卷又小品劫抄又出觀佛三昧第二卷)劫燒盡時一切皆空。眾生福德因緣力故。十方風至風風相次能持大水。上有一千頭人二千手足。名為違紐是人臍中生千葉金色蓮華。其光大明如萬日照。花中有人結跏趺坐。此人復有無量光明。名為梵天王。正生八子。八子生天地人民。是梵天王。婬瞋已盡坐蓮花上。諸佛隨俗現寶蓮花上。結跏趺坐說六波羅蜜。聞此法者。必至阿耨多羅三藐三菩提(出諸雜譬喻第六卷)。

At the very end of the water kalpa, the Ābhāsvara gods, bathing in the water, drew the essence of the four great elements into their own bodies, giving rise there to the joy of contact, their seminal essence flowing into the water; stirred by the eight winds, it fell into muddy silt and there, of itself, formed an egg, which after eight thousand years hatched, and from it was born a woman, her form the blue-black of mud, having nine hundred and ninety-nine heads and a thousand eyes, nine hundred and ninety-nine mouths, each mouth with four fangs from which fire issued like lightning, and twenty-four arms, each hand grasping every kind of weapon; her body was as vast as Sumeru itself. Entering the great ocean, she clapped the water in her own delight; a whirlwind then arose and blew the ocean's water so that its essence entered her body and she conceived; after eight thousand years she gave birth to a son, his body four times greater than his mother's, with nine heads, each with a thousand eyes and fire issuing from its mouths, nine hundred and ninety-nine arms and eight legs, and, standing in the ocean's water, he proclaimed of himself: "I am Vemacitrin, king of the asuras." He ate only mud and lotus-roots; such were the changes that took place when the earth itself was first newly formed (Source: Ekottarika Āgama, fascicle 32; also the Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 6; also the Extract on the Lesser Kalpa; also the Sūtra on the Samādhi of Buddha-Contemplation, fascicle 2). When the kalpa of burning has entirely ended and all is empty, then, through the power of sentient beings' merit and karmic causes, winds arise from every direction, wind after wind supporting one another until they are able to sustain the great waters above them; there arises one with a thousand heads and two thousand hands and feet, named Viṣṇu — from this being's navel there grows a thousand-petaled lotus of golden hue, its light shining forth as bright as ten thousand suns, and within that lotus sits a man in full lotus posture; this being possesses measureless further light, and is named Brahmā the Heavenly King. He at once brings forth eight sons, and these eight sons in turn bring forth heaven, earth, and all humankind; this same Brahmā King, his lust and anger already extinguished, sits upon that very lotus-throne — and it is in accordance with this that the buddhas, following worldly convention, likewise appear seated in full lotus posture upon a jeweled lotus-throne, expounding there the six perfections; whoever hears this teaching is certain to attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening (Source: Miscellaneous Similes, fascicle 6).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three great calamities map directly onto the four dhyānas: fire destroys up through the first dhyāna, water up through the second, wind up through the third — and the fourth dhyāna's Pure Abodes are never touched at all, because their inhabitants are anāgāmins who will reach final nirvāṇa from exactly where they are, with no further descent required. The cosmology and the soteriology are the same statement: the deeper a level of concentration, the more of the universe's own destruction it survives, and only liberation itself stands wholly outside the cycle. The closing myth of Viṣṇu, the lotus, and Brahmā's eight sons is a rare case of the compendium absorbing a clearly Brahmanical creation account into its own Buddhist cosmology — worth noting as a citation from the Miscellaneous Similes rather than from a mainstream Āgama or Abhidharma source.

3. The Length of a Kalpa

Two similes for a span of time too vast to state as a number.

Chinese Source

佛言。設方百由旬城滿中芥子。有長壽人百歲。取一芥子都盡。劫猶不盡。又如方百由旬石。持迦尸輕軟疊衣。百年一拂此石脫盡劫猶不盡。謂之大劫也。又言。方一由旬高下亦然。鐵城滿中芥子。百年取一盡為一劫。又方一由旬石山。士夫以迦尸衣百年一拂。拂之不已石山銷盡。劫猶未竟。六十念中之一念。謂極小劫也(出大智論第三十六卷又出增一阿含第三十一卷又出第二十八卷又出雜阿含第三十四卷)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said: suppose a city a hundred yojanas square, filled to the brim with mustard seeds, and a long-lived man who, once every hundred years, takes away a single seed — even when every seed is gone, the kalpa is still not exhausted. Or again: suppose a stone a hundred yojanas square, and a man who takes a soft cloth of Kāśī silk and, once every hundred years, brushes it once across the stone — even when the stone is entirely worn away, the kalpa is still not exhausted; this is called a great kalpa. It is also said: take a space one yojana square, of the same height, an iron city filled to the brim with mustard seeds, and once every hundred years remove a single seed until all are gone — this makes one kalpa. Or again: a stone mountain one yojana square, and a man who, with a cloth of Kāśī silk, brushes it once every hundred years — brushing without cease until the stone mountain is entirely worn away, the kalpa is still not yet complete; a single thought out of sixty such thoughts is called the smallest kalpa of all (Source: Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, fascicle 36; also Ekottarika Āgama, fascicles 31 and 28; also Saṃyukta Āgama, fascicle 34).

4–11. The Sun, Moon, Stars, and Sky

The fascicle closes by turning from cosmic time to the visible sky itself: the palaces of the sun and moon, the mechanics of an eclipse, the stars, and the causes of thunder, lightning, cloud, wind, and rain.

Chinese Source
日四

日城郭。方正二千四十里。其高亦然。光射人眼見之若圓。宮城純金七寶瑩麗無諸瑕。為五風所持。一持。二養。三受。四轉。五調。日王座方二十里。身出光明照耀宮殿。宮殿之光照於城郭。城郭之光下臨下土。無數天神前後導從。音樂自娛無有休息。林觀浴池如忉利天。天壽五百歲。子孫相襲以竟一劫。日城繞須彌山。東方日出。南方望。西方夜半。北方日入。如是右旋更為晝夜。復有長短。日行稍南南方漸長。經六十里。一百八十日北方稍短。復行稍北北方稍長。一百八十日南方稍短(出長阿含經第二十二卷又出樓炭經)。

Ru-Yi Translation
The Sun, 4

The sun's citadel is two thousand and forty li square, and of the same height; it casts its light into the eye so that it appears round. Its palace walls are pure gold, adorned with the seven treasures, radiant and without blemish, and are borne up by five winds: Sustaining, Nourishing, Receiving, Turning, and Regulating. The Sun King's throne is twenty li square; his body gives off a light that illuminates the palace, and the palace's own light in turn illuminates the citadel, and the citadel's light shines down upon the earth below; countless heavenly spirits attend him before and behind, music and entertainment never ceasing. His groves, belvederes, and bathing pools are as in Trāyastriṃśa; his heavenly lifespan is five hundred years, his descendants succeeding one another to complete out a single kalpa. The sun's citadel circles Sumeru: it rises in the east, is at midday in the south, at midnight in the west, and sets in the north — thus turning ever to the right, making day and night, with further variation in length: the sun's course draws gradually southward, the south growing gradually longer over a hundred and eighty days, the north growing gradually shorter; then it turns gradually north again, the north growing longer over another hundred and eighty days as the south grows shorter (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 22; also the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra).

月五

月城郭。廣長千九百六十里。其高亦然。儼然方正遠見故圓。二分天銀一分瑠璃。內外清徹光明遠照。為五風所持。月王坐方二十里七寶宮殿。無量天神光明妓樂前後導從。園池等玩如忉利天。天壽五百歲。子孫相襲以竟一劫。月有虧滿。缺者一角行夜稍稍隱側故見缺減。又云。月城邊有天。其色正青衣服亦青。所在之面青光照城故缺減也。滿者月行稍轉向正。又青色天十五日轉入月城與王遇會。又須彌山南地有大樹。樹名閻浮提。高四千里枝蔭二千里影現月中(出長阿含經第二十二卷又出樓炭經)。

The Moon, 5

The moon's citadel is one thousand nine hundred and sixty li broad and long, and of the same height; solemn and perfectly square, yet seen from afar it appears round — two parts heavenly silver, one part beryl, clear and translucent within and without, its light shining afar; it too is borne up by five winds. The Moon King's throne is twenty li square within a palace of the seven treasures; countless heavenly spirits, with light and music, attend him before and behind, his gardens and pools just as in Trāyastriṃśa; his heavenly lifespan is five hundred years, his descendants succeeding one another to complete out a single kalpa. The moon waxes and wanes: when it wanes, one edge of it turns to travel by night and is gradually concealed to one side, and so appears to diminish; it is also said that beside the moon's citadel dwells a deva whose color and robes are pure blue, and that whichever face he turns toward the citadel is darkened by his blue light, and so it seems to diminish. When it waxes, the moon's course gradually turns to face forward again; and it is also said that this same blue-colored deva, on the fifteenth day, turns and enters the moon's citadel to meet with its king. There is, moreover, south of Sumeru, a great tree named Jambu, four thousand li tall, its shade spreading two thousand li, whose reflection appears within the moon (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 22; also the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra).

日月蝕

阿修倫天王名羅呼。其體高二萬八千里。以月十五日立海中央。海水裁至其臍。低頭[門@視]須彌羅寶。忝山及四方上鎮。以指覆日月天下晦冥。或覆日以晝為夜。所謂日月蝕時危光明也(出樓炭經第五卷)。

Eclipses of the Sun and Moon

There is an asura-king named Rāhu, whose body stands twenty-eight thousand li tall; on the fifteenth day of the month he stands in the middle of the ocean, its waters reaching only to his navel, and, bowing his head, gazes upon Sumeru's jeweled peaks and its four surrounding mountains; with his finger he covers the sun or moon, and the world below is plunged into darkness — sometimes covering the sun so that day turns to night. This is what is called an eclipse of the sun or moon, a moment when their light is imperiled (Source: Lokaprajñapti Sūtra, fascicle 5).

星六

星宿城郭。天神之舍也。以水精為城七寶為宮。懸在空中大風持之猶如浮雲。隨日運行為眼所見。大者七百里。中者五百里小者百二十里。宮室園池如四天王天。壽命亦爾(出樓炭經第六卷又出長阿含)。

The Stars, 6

The citadels of the constellations are the dwellings of the heavenly spirits, their walls of crystal essence, their palaces of the seven treasures, suspended in space and borne up by a great wind like drifting cloud, following the sun's own course as it is seen by the eye. The largest measure seven hundred li, the middling five hundred, the smallest a hundred and twenty; their halls, gardens, and pools are as in the Heaven of the Four Kings, and their lifespan likewise the same (Source: Lokaprajñapti Sūtra, fascicle 6; also the Dīrgha Āgama).

雷七

虛空雲中。有時地大與水相觸。有時與火風等大相觸。水火風大更互相觸。皆生虛空雲中雷聲(出長阿含經第二十卷)。

Thunder, 7

Within the clouds of open space, at times the element of earth comes into contact with the element of water, and at times the elements of fire and wind come likewise into great contact with one another; water, fire, wind, and earth thus striking against each other, all give rise, within the clouds of open space, to the sound of thunder (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20).

電八

電有四種。東方名身光。二南方名難毀。西方名流炎。北方名定明。何以虛空雲中有此電光。四方之電又共相觸。有此光起(出長阿含經第二十卷)。

Lightning, 8

Lightning is of four kinds: in the east it is named Body-Light; in the south, Hard-to-Destroy; in the west, Flowing Flame; in the north, Settled Brightness. Why does lightning appear within the clouds of open space? Because the lightning of the four directions strikes against each of the others in turn, and it is from this that its light arises (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20).

雲有四種。一白。二黑。三赤。四紅。白者地大偏多。黑者水大偏多。赤者火大偏多。紅者風大偏多。去地或十里。或二十三十。乃至四十里。除劫初時上至光音天(出長阿含經第二十卷)雲有四色。一青。二赤。三黃白。四黑。青者中有水界大多。赤者中有火界大多。黃白者中有地界大多。黑者中有風界大多(出樓炭經第四卷)龍氣為雲(出長阿含經)。

Clouds, 9

Clouds are of four kinds: white, black, red, and russet. In the white, the element of earth predominates; in the black, water; in the red, fire; in the russet, wind. They stand off from the earth by ten li, or twenty, or thirty, up to forty li — save at the very beginning of a kalpa, when they reach up as high as the Ābhāsvara Heaven (Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20). Clouds are also said to be of four colors: blue-green, red, yellow-white, and black. In the blue-green, water predominates; in the red, fire; in the yellow-white, earth; in the black, wind (Lokaprajñapti Sūtra, fascicle 4). The breath of dragons, too, becomes cloud (Dīrgha Āgama).

風十

Wind, 10

When a world is about to be destroyed, a great wind arises named Dispersal and Ruin, capable of blowing apart and utterly effacing the great-thousandfold world — Mount Sumeru, the mountains of gold and iron, and every other thing whatsoever. At that time, beyond the great-thousandfold world, there arises a further wind named Barrier Against Ruin, able to hold that destructive wind at bay and prevent it reaching other regions; were there no such barrier-wind, then throughout the measureless, innumerable world-realms of the ten directions, nothing at all would fail to be scattered and destroyed (Source: Avataṃsaka Sūtra, fascicle 30).

雨十一

相師占雨。有五因緣不可定知。使占者迷惑。一者雲有雷電占謂當雨。以火大多燒雲不雨。二者雲有雷電占亦謂雨。有大風起吹雲四散入諸山間。三者雲有雷電占亦謂雨。時阿修羅攬接浮雲置大海中。四者雲有雷電占亦謂雨。而雨師放誕婬亂竟不降雨。五者雲有雷電占亦謂雨。而世間眾生非法縱盪污清淨行。慳貪嫉妬所見顛倒。故使天不降雨。以此五事相不定知(出長阿含第二十卷樓炭同)。阿耨達龍王興大重雲。滿閻浮提並降大雨。百穀草樹皆悉滋長。江河川沼一切盈滿。此大雨水從龍王身心中出而能饒益無量眾生。摩那斯龍王將欲降雨。先興重雲彌覆虛空。慈悲心故凝停七日。先令眾生究竟諸業。漸降微雨普潤大地(出長阿含經第二十卷)。

Rain, 11

Diviners who forecast rain face five circumstances that cannot be known for certain, which leave the diviner himself confounded: the clouds carry thunder and lightning, and rain is divined — yet the element of fire, being too abundant, burns away the clouds and no rain falls; or a great wind arises and scatters the clouds among the mountains; or the asuras gather up the drifting clouds and place them within the great ocean; or the rain-deity, given over to indolence and lust, sends down no rain at all; or the sentient beings of the world, unrestrained in lawlessness, defiling all pure conduct, given to stinginess, greed, envy, and distorted views, cause heaven on this account to withhold its rain. For these five reasons the signs cannot be relied upon with certainty (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20; the Lokaprajñapti Sūtra agrees).

The nāga-king Anavatapta raises up a great and heavy cloud, and rain falls throughout the whole of Jambudvīpa, so that the hundred grains and every plant and tree grow full and abundant, and every river, stream, and pool becomes replete; this great rainwater issues from the very body and mind of the nāga-king, and is thus able to benefit measureless sentient beings. The nāga-king Manasvin, about to send down rain, first raises up a heavy cloud that spreads across and covers the whole of space; but out of compassion he holds it there, unmoving, for seven full days, so that sentient beings may first bring their various undertakings to completion, and only then does he let fall a fine and gentle rain, gradually moistening the whole earth (Source: Dīrgha Āgama, fascicle 20).

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The rain diviner's five failure-cases are a small gem easy to read past: this is a sixth-century Buddhist text explaining, without any embarrassment, why weather forecasting fails — not because the diviner is incompetent, but because the causal chain from cloud to rain runs through wind, fire, asura interference, a distracted rain-deity, and collective human karma, any one of which can break the chain. It is a strikingly systems-level account of causal overdetermination, offered here as a plain fact about the sky rather than as a philosophical point — which is exactly the anthology's method throughout this fascicle: cosmology stated as fact, its ethical logic left for the reader to draw out.
