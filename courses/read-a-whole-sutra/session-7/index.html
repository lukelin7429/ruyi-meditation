Session 7 · 信 What to trust

The Amitābha Sūtra

《佛說阿彌陀經》 — the most recited scripture on earth, and the hardest to believe
Reading time about fifteen minutes Before class Sessions 1–6 Length thirteen short sections
Part 1

Why this one, seventh

Because it is, by a very wide margin, the most recited Buddhist scripture on the planet — and you have almost certainly never read it.

That is not an accident of your reading. English-language Buddhism was assembled, historically, out of Theravāda insight practice, Zen, and Tibetan Buddhism, and the Pure Land tradition — which is what the great majority of East Asian Buddhists have actually practised for over a thousand years — arrived barely at all. If you have been told anything about it, you were probably told it is the devotional, populist, slightly embarrassing wing of Buddhism for people who cannot manage meditation. That is not what this text says, and six sessions of reading whole scriptures have equipped you to check.

Of all eight texts in this course, this is the one most likely to annoy you. Read it to the end anyway.

Three things that make it unlike everything you have read so far

Nobody asked for it. Almost every sūtra in the canon opens with a question — a puzzled disciple, a curious layperson. Traditional Buddhist scholarship classifies scripture into twelve literary types, and one of the rarest is anupṛcchā: a teaching the Buddha gives entirely on his own initiative, unprompted. This is the clearest example there is. Śāriputra does not ask anything. The Buddha simply turns to him and begins describing a world nobody in the assembly would have thought to ask about.

It describes a place. Where the Heart Sūtra empties every category the mind can hold and the Diamond Sūtra dismantles every fixed view, this one does something close to the opposite. It names a location, a buddha presiding there, the material of the ponds, the birds, the trees, the sound the wind makes. Then it tells you exactly what to do.

It calls itself hard to believe. Not hard to understand — the prose is plain and repeats itself by design. Near its close the Buddha says that what he has just taught is "the Dharma that the whole world finds hard to believe." That is the sūtra's own assessment of itself, and Part 3 works out where exactly the difficulty is said to lie.

Our reading notes come from Ouyi Zhixu (1599–1655) — the same late-Ming master who guided Session 1 — whose 1647 commentary, the Yaojie, organises this text around three terms: faith (信), vow (願), and practice (行). Where he adds something the sūtra does not itself say, this session will point it out, as it has all course.

Part 3

Three passages, closelyA place, an instruction, and an admission

1 · A teaching nobody thought to ask for

Section 2 · why it is called Utmost Bliss

"Then the Buddha said to the elder Śāriputra: 'West from here, past a hundred thousand million buddha-lands, there is a world named Utmost Bliss. In that land there is a buddha called Amitābha, who is teaching the Dharma there right now. Śāriputra, why is that land called Utmost Bliss? Because the beings born in that country have no suffering of any kind and experience only joy — that is why it is called Utmost Bliss.'"

爾時，佛告長老舍利弗：「從是西方過十萬億佛土，有世界名曰極樂。其土有佛，號阿彌陀，今現在說法。舍利弗！彼土何故名為極樂？其國眾生無有眾苦，但受諸樂，故名極樂。」
See this passage in the full text →

Read that opening again with the previous six sessions in your ear, and notice how strange it is. No question precedes it. And the person addressed is Śāriputra — named by the tradition as the disciple foremost in wisdom, the same disciple the Heart Sūtra addresses. The most analytically powerful mind in the room is spoken to, not consulted. Ouyi reads the whole unasked-for structure as significant: this teaching lay so far outside what anyone present would have thought to ask that only the Buddha's own unprompted compassion could have introduced it.

Then notice how the naming is explained — twice, and differently. First by the absence of suffering; then, immediately after, by physical adornment: seven tiers of railings, seven tiers of netting, seven rows of trees. Ouyi reads the doubling as answering two different orders of doubt at once. That beings there might still suffer in some subtle way — answered by "no suffering of any kind." And that the description might be merely allegorical — answered by specific, countable, material features.

How literally are you supposed to take this? The tradition itself disagrees.
  • Ouyi takes it exactly as it reads — a real place at a real distance, and he argues directly against collapsing Sukhāvatī into a purely psychological "Pure Land is only the pure mind," which he considers an evasion of the text's plain claim.
  • Zhuhong, writing a century earlier, holds that the external land and the practitioner's own buddha-nature are not in competition — that it is both a real place and, at the deepest level, not separate from mind.
  • This course does not adjudicate between them, and neither does the sūtra's own page. Both readings are live within the tradition. What is worth resisting is the move of picking the metaphorical reading simply because it is more comfortable for a modern reader — that is not neutrality, it is a preference.

One detail that shows the text is more self-aware than it first appears. Section 5 describes flocks of birds in that land whose calls proclaim the five faculties, the seven factors of awakening, the eightfold path. A careful listener would immediately object: birth as an animal is a lower rebirth, the fruit of unwholesome karma — so how is a land free of suffering full of birds? The sūtra raises that objection itself, in that very passage, and answers it: these are not karmically born creatures but transformations conjured by Amitābha, so that the sound of the Dharma might spread. Ouyi treats this as evidence of how carefully the text guards its own claims at precisely the point a thinking reader would push.

2 · The instruction, and exactly what it asks

Section 8 · holding the name with an undistracted mind

"Śāriputra, one cannot be born in that land through few roots of goodness, blessings, and causes and conditions. Śāriputra, if there is a good son or good daughter who, hearing Amitābha Buddha spoken of, holds fast to that name — whether for one day, two days, three, four, five, six, or seven days — with a single, undistracted mind, then when that person's life comes to its end, Amitābha Buddha, together with the host of sages, will appear before them. And at the moment of death, that person's mind will not be inverted with confusion or fear, and they will at once attain rebirth in Amitābha Buddha's land of Utmost Bliss."

舍利弗！不可以少善根福德因緣得生彼國。舍利弗！若有善男子、善女人，聞說阿彌陀佛，執持名號，若一日、若二日……若七日，一心不亂。其人臨命終時，阿彌陀佛與諸聖眾，現在其前。是人終時，心不顛倒，即得往生阿彌陀佛極樂國土。
See this passage in the full text →

This single passage is what Ouyi's entire commentary turns on, and his governing formula is worth having exactly: whether one attains rebirth at all depends entirely on whether faith and vow are genuinely present; the grade of rebirth one attains depends entirely on the depth of one's practice in holding the name. Faith and vow open the door; practice determines how far into the house you walk.

That structure is why the obvious objection — isn't this suspiciously easy? — misfires. Nothing here is being given away. What is being claimed is that the decisive variable is not the quantity of effort but whether you actually trust the thing and actually want it. Which is, if you are honest, a harder bar than it sounds, and a much harder one to fake.

Two things the text does not say, which are often reported as if it did
  • "One day… up to seven days" is not obviously a deadline. The sūtra does not say whether this is a literal ceiling — achieve it within a week or it has not worked — or open-ended shorthand for "from the least to the most, however long it takes." Both readings live in the tradition; the later seven-day intensive recitation retreat (打佛七) took the number more literally than every commentator has been willing to.
  • The famous two tiers of "one-pointed and undistracted" are a commentator's addition. Ouyi and others distinguish a phenomenal one-pointedness (事一心, sustained unbroken recitation without the mind wandering) from a noumenal one (理一心, a mind that has also penetrated the empty, non-dual nature of the buddha recited and the one reciting). That distinction is a genuine interpretive tool. The bare sūtra text does not draw it.

And one claim from a few sections earlier is doing more work than its length suggests. Everyone born in that land is 阿鞞跋致, avaivartika — non-retrogressing. On the ordinary bodhisattva path, non-retrogression takes lifetimes to secure and can still be lost through a serious error, a poor teacher, or an unlucky rebirth. Ouyi's argument is that birth in Sukhāvatī removes the risk of backsliding for anyone born there, whatever level of realisation they arrive with — which is why he treats this as uniquely efficient among Buddhist paths, without ever claiming it is the only valid one. And he reads "there, one gets to be gathered together in one place with such supremely good people" as a quiet answer to a different worry: this is not a private paradise. It is rebirth into company.

3 · The sūtra's own admission

Section 12 · the hardest teaching to believe

"'Śākyamuni Buddha has been able to do an extremely difficult and rare thing — able, in the sahā world, in the evil age of the five turbidities… to attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, and, for the sake of all beings, to speak this Dharma that the whole world finds hard to believe.' Śāriputra, you should know that I, in this evil age of the five turbidities, have done this difficult thing… this indeed is extremely difficult!"

釋迦牟尼佛能為甚難希有之事，能於娑婆國土五濁惡世……得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，為諸眾生說是一切世間難信之法。舍利弗！當知我於五濁惡世行此難事……為一切世間說此難信之法，是為甚難！
See this passage in the full text →

This is the most unusual sentence in the whole course. No other text you have read this term pauses to tell you that it expects not to be believed.

And it is easy to get the difficulty backwards. It is not that the sūtra is intellectually demanding — set beside the causal analysis of Yogācāra or the dialectic of the Diamond Sūtra, this text's structure is plain. Ouyi locates the difficulty in the listener's own habits of mind: a trained assumption that the more effortful path must be the truer one, meeting a claim that sustained, sincere recitation — without first mastering doctrine or completing lifetimes of austerity — secures guaranteed and irreversible progress. For a certain kind of serious person, that is very hard to accept at face value. Not because it is complicated. Because it offends something.

Which is also, on this reading, exactly why the text spends its ninth section staging six directions' worth of buddhas — east, south, west, north, below, above, each as numerous as the sands of the Ganges — putting forth "the sign of the broad and long tongue" and saying the same words six times over. Not repetitive padding. The corroboration is built to match the difficulty. The sūtra knows what it is asking.

Not every commentator locates the difficulty identically — some read it as the sheer scale of what is described, thousands of Ganges-sands of buddhas and a buddha-land ten kalpas old, straining ordinary credulity. Worth holding both.

Part 4

This week's practiceTen minutes a day — do it before deciding what you think of it

Recite the name

This is the only practice in the course that many readers will want to argue with first. Notice the wanting, and then do it anyway for a week. You can argue afterward with far better information.

Ten minutes a day, out loud or under the breath: 南無阿彌陀佛 — Námó Ēmítuófó, "homage to Amitābha Buddha." Repeat it steadily, at whatever pace lets you stay with it. When the mind wanders, come back. That is the entire instruction.

You do not have to have settled the metaphysics first. Nothing in the sūtra requires you to have resolved whether Sukhāvatī is a place, a state, or both, before beginning — and the commentarial tradition, as Part 3 noted, has not resolved it either.

What to watch for. Not bliss, and not proof. Watch what the practice does to the ten minutes themselves, and watch your own resistance — it will show up on day two or three, and it will have arguments. Write one of them down. Then keep going to day seven, and read your argument again.

Question 1 of 5
Unlike almost every other sūtra, this one opens with no question from anyone. What is the significance of that?
Correct: C. And note who is addressed rather than consulted: Śāriputra, named as the disciple foremost in wisdom — the same disciple the Heart Sūtra addresses. The most analytically powerful mind in the room does not ask, and is told anyway.
Question 2 of 5
How does the tradition itself handle the question of whether Sukhāvatī is a literal place?
Correct: D. Both readings are live, and this course does not adjudicate. What is worth noticing in yourself is the temptation to take the metaphorical reading simply because it is easier for a modern reader to hold — that is a preference, not neutrality.
Question 3 of 5
Ouyi's governing formula for this sūtra distinguishes what faith and vow determine from what practice determines. What is the distinction?
Correct: B. Faith and vow open the door; practice determines how far into the house you walk. This is also why "isn't this too easy?" misfires — nothing is being given away. The claim is that the decisive variable is whether you actually trust the thing and actually want it, which is a harder bar than it sounds and much harder to fake.
Question 4 of 5
Which of these is supplied by commentators rather than stated by the sūtra itself?
Correct: A. The other three are all in the text. The two-tier distinction is a genuinely useful interpretive tool that the bare sūtra does not draw — and knowing which is which is the same skill Session 1 introduced with Ouyi's gloss on recitation, and Session 3 with Yuanying on the four truths. Use a commentary as a second opinion, not a verdict.
Question 5 of 5
The sūtra calls itself "the Dharma that the whole world finds hard to believe." Where does Ouyi locate the difficulty?
Correct: C. The difficulty is not intellectual — this text's structure is plain beside Yogācāra or Madhyamaka. It is that the claim offends something in a certain kind of serious person. Which is exactly why the sūtra stages six directions of buddhas saying the same words six times over: the corroboration is built to match the difficulty. Part 6

One question, left open

Sit with this

What in this sūtra was hardest for you to believe — and what does that tell you about what you already believe?

Be specific. Not "the whole thing." The jewelled ponds, or the buddhas of the six directions, or the seven days, or the idea that a name held in mind could matter at all. Then turn the question around, which is the part worth the week: your resistance landed somewhere in particular, and it landed there because of a conviction you were already carrying. Name that conviction. You did not get it from this text.
