Session 2 · Pressure-testing Session 1's argument

The Limits of the Claim

What the Pramāṇasiddhi argument does NOT prove — and where it's weakest
Before class: Session 1 (Why Trust the Buddha at All?). This session assumes you've already met the doctor analogy and the four qualifications.
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A good argument survives being attacked

Session 1 built a careful case: the Buddha counts as a reliable authority specifically on liberation, the way a doctor is reliable specifically on health — not because of fame or tradition, but because of four checkable qualifications. That's a genuinely strong argument. It is also, like every argument, not unlimited. A teacher who actually believes their own reasoning should be the first to ask: where does this actually stop working?

This session does that on purpose. Four honest limits — one lined up against each of Session 1's four qualifications.

Limit 1 — Reliability on liberation isn't reliability on everything

This one isn't a weakness so much as a boundary Dharmakīrti draws himself, on purpose. The proof was never built to establish omniscience, and it doesn't accidentally smuggle it in. If you ever hear the argument stretched to cover unrelated claims — geography, astronomy, medicine — that stretch is not something Session 1's argument supports. Good to name clearly, precisely because it's so often misused in the other direction.

Limit 2 — Trustworthy testimony isn't the same as independent proof

Here's the sharper one. Suppose the whole argument works perfectly: the Buddha really is reliable on liberation. What follows from that? That his testimony about liberation — including claims like rebirth or the specific mechanics of karma — deserves serious weight. It does not, by itself, make those specific claims independently verified the way a repeatable experiment would. Trusting a reliable witness and independently confirming what they say are two different epistemic acts, and Session 1's argument only fully delivers the first one. (Dharmakīrti and later commentators were aware of this gap — elsewhere in the broader tradition, separate arguments are made for specific doctrines like rebirth, rather than resting everything on the authority argument alone. That's a different, much longer conversation than this course covers.)

Limit 3 — A worry about circularity

How do we actually know the Buddha had compassion, trained for a very long time, and directly realized the truths he taught? Largely through biography and testimony that comes from — the Buddhist tradition itself. A sharp critic will ask: aren't you using the tradition's own account of its founder to prove the tradition's founder is trustworthy? That's uncomfortably close to circular. A fair reply exists (the internal coherence of the teaching itself — its logical structure, its match with observable facts about suffering and craving — can be checked independently of taking the biography on faith), but it's a reply that has to be made, not assumed. Notice this too: it's a real limit, not a fatal one — but pretending it isn't there would be dishonest.

Limit 4 — The same template, pointed elsewhere

Compassion, sustained practice, direct realization, reliable transmission — could this exact four-part test be satisfied by the founder of some other tradition too? If a critic says yes, does that damage the argument, or does it just mean the test is doing its job impartially, the way a fair medical-licensing exam should be passable by more than one excellent doctor? This is worth sitting with rather than rushing past — it cuts both ways, and reasonable people land in different places.

The shape of an honest limit
  • Limit 1 is a boundary Dharmakīrti draws on purpose — not a weakness, a precision.
  • Limits 2–4 are real openings a sharp critic can press on — testimony vs. proof, circularity, and generalizability.
  • None of the four collapse the argument — but a teacher who can't state their own argument's limits clearly hasn't fully understood it yet.
Debate Check
Challenger
"So, this argument proves rebirth is real, because it proves the Buddha is a reliable authority on liberation."
Defender
"That doesn't prove it! There is no pervasion."
Being a reliable witness makes testimony worth taking seriously — it doesn't, by itself, independently establish the specific content of what was testified to. That's Limit 2, in one line.
Part 2

Discussion Questions

  1. Which of the four limits feels most serious to you? Which feels the least worrying?
  2. Is relying partly on tradition's own testimony about the Buddha's life a fatal circularity — or is some amount of this unavoidable when evaluating any historical teacher, religious or secular?
  3. If another tradition's founder could satisfy the same four-part test, does that weaken the argument for the Buddha specifically, or just mean the test is fair?
  4. Where, personally, do you think faith has to do work that this argument admits it can't do on its own?

Option A — Argue the strongest version

Pick the limit you find most serious and write the strongest possible version of it — steelman it, don't strawman it. Then attempt your own best reply, using only what this session covered.

Option B — Defend the boundary

Limit 1 is a boundary Dharmakīrti drew on purpose. Write a short piece explaining, in your own words, why deliberately narrowing a claim can make an argument stronger rather than weaker — using an example from outside Buddhism if that helps you think it through.

Part 4

Self-Check

1. What's the difference between "trustworthy testimony" and "independent proof," in the context of Limit 2?
A reliable witness's claims deserve serious weight, but that's not the same as those specific claims being independently verified through separate evidence or argument.
2. What is the circularity worry in Limit 3?
The evidence for the Buddha's compassion, training, and realization comes largely from the Buddhist tradition's own biographical accounts — using the tradition's testimony to prove the tradition's founder is trustworthy.
3. Is Limit 1 (no claim to omniscience) a flaw in the argument, or something else?
Something else — it's a deliberate boundary, a precision rather than a weakness, and it's what keeps the argument honest rather than overreaching.
