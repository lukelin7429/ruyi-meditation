Session 3 · 現量品, the perception chapter

What Counts as Direct Perception

Why "red" is something you see, and "apple" is something you think
Before class: Collected Topics Session 6 (direct perception vs. inference, in outline). This session gives that distinction real depth.
A note on this session: the perception chapter is one of the most technical and most debated parts of the entire text. What follows is the standard, simplified picture used to introduce it — not a full account of every scholarly controversy around it.
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A question that sounds too simple

Look at a red apple. Ask yourself honestly: is "red" something you see, or something you think?

Most people answer instantly: obviously you see red. But sit with it a moment longer. The raw event of light hitting your eye and triggering a visual sensation is one thing. The moment where your mind reaches for the word "red" — and files this experience together with every other red thing you've ever seen, from fire trucks to roses — is a separate step, layered on so fast it feels instantaneous. Dharmakīrti's perception chapter exists because he thinks that gap is real, and that missing it causes real philosophical confusion.

Two requirements for a "real" perception

Not every mental event that feels like "seeing" counts as valid perception. Dharmakīrti sets two requirements:

Non-conceptual
Not filtered through language or category-thought — raw contact, before any labeling.The instant of seeing color, before "red" gets attached
Non-erroneous
Not a mistake — a mirage, a rope mistaken for a snake, or a reflection mistaken for the real thing.Something that would survive closer inspection

The particular and the universal

This is the heart of the chapter. Dharmakīrti argues that raw perception only ever grasps what's called the specifically-characterized particular (rang mtshan) — the completely unique, momentary, this-exact-instant reality in front of you, which technically never repeats and can't fully be captured in words. The concept "apple," on the other hand, is a generally-characterized universal (spyi mtshan) — a mental category built by comparing this experience to a stored memory of past experiences, useful for thinking and talking, but strictly a construction of mind rather than something literally sitting out there to be seen.

The particular is what you actually meet. The universal is the label your mind reaches for afterward, to make sense of it.

Notice the stakes: this isn't a minor technicality. If concepts are constructions rather than things directly perceived, then an enormous amount of what feels like "just seeing the world" — categories, names, comparisons — turns out to already be inference-adjacent mental activity, not raw perception at all.

Four kinds of direct perception

The tradition recognizes four:

  1. Sense perception — the five senses making raw contact with their objects. (Seeing color, hearing sound, before any labeling.)
  2. Mental perception — a non-conceptual mental awareness that follows immediately after sense perception. (Subtler, and harder to isolate in ordinary experience.)
  3. Self-cognizing awareness (rang rig) — the mind's direct awareness of its own experience, without needing a second act of observation. (You don't infer that you're currently seeing red — you're just aware of the seeing itself.)
  4. Yogic perception — direct, non-conceptual realization achieved through sustained meditative training, traditionally culminating in directly perceiving selflessness. (Not sense-based at all — the fruit of the practice, not a starting assumption.)
Three ideas to carry out of today
  • Ordinary "seeing" is usually a blend. A split-second of raw contact, immediately dressed in concepts — and the two are easy to mistake for one continuous act.
  • The particular/universal split has real consequences. If you've ever meditated and noticed a raw sensation arise a beat before you named it, you've brushed up against exactly this distinction firsthand.
  • Not all four kinds of perception are equally easy to verify for yourself. Sense perception is obvious; self-cognizing awareness and yogic perception ask you to take the framework further on trust — worth noticing which is which.
Debate Check
Challenger
"So, you directly perceive that this is an apple, because you're looking right at it."
Defender
"That doesn't prove it! There is no pervasion."
Looking at it is genuine sense perception of color and shape — real contact with a particular. "Apple" is a universal, added by concept, riding along so fast it feels like part of the seeing itself.
Part 2

Discussion Questions

  1. Does the particular/universal split feel like a real discovery to you, or like philosophers overcomplicating something obvious? Defend your answer.
  2. Self-cognizing awareness claims you're directly aware of your own experience without a second observing act. Does that match how your own mind feels from the inside?
  3. Is it fair to call yogic perception "perception" at all, given it isn't sense-based? What would change if we called it something else instead?
  4. Which of the four kinds of perception would you find hardest to defend to a skeptical friend, and why?

Option A — Sit with the gap

Spend five quiet minutes with a single ordinary object — a cup, a plant, your own hand. Try to notice the instant of raw sensory contact before your mind reaches for a name or category. Write about what you actually noticed, honestly — including if you couldn't catch the gap at all.

Option B — Defend one of the four

Pick one of the four kinds of direct perception and explain, with your own original example, what makes it distinct from the other three — and where a skeptic would push back hardest.

Part 4

Self-Check

1. What are the two requirements for a cognition to count as valid direct perception?
Non-conceptual (not filtered through language or category-thought) and non-erroneous (not a mistake like a mirage).
2. What's the difference between a "particular" and a "universal" in this chapter's sense?
A particular is the unique, momentary reality directly perceived; a universal is a mental category built by comparing that experience to memory — useful, but a construction, not itself directly perceived.
3. Name the four kinds of direct perception.
Sense perception, mental perception, self-cognizing awareness, and yogic perception.
