Session 1 · The opening argument of 成量品

Why Trust the Buddha at All?

Pramāṇasiddhi — the chapter that earns the right to everything after it
Before class: Collected Topics, especially Session 6 (Kinds of Knowing). No knowledge of the Pramāṇavārttika itself required — this is where it begins.
Part 1

Lecture Notes

The question skeptics ask first

Before anyone accepts a single teaching about karma, rebirth, or the end of suffering, a fair-minded skeptic asks one question first: why should I believe any of this came from someone reliable? It's not a hostile question. It's the same question you'd ask about a stranger giving you medical advice, or a contractor telling you your house is structurally sound. Trust isn't owed to anyone by default — it has to be earned, and earned specifically.

Dignāga, the founder of the Buddhist logical tradition, opened his own treatise with a single line of praise calling the Buddha "one who has become pramāṇa" — a valid means of knowledge, an authority. Dharmakīrti's Pramāṇavārttika devotes its entire first chapter, traditionally called Pramāṇasiddhi ("Establishing [the Buddha as] a Valid Authority"), to defending that one line against exactly the skeptic's question. Nothing in the rest of the text is allowed to matter until this chapter does its work.

The doctor analogy

Here's the move that makes the whole argument work. Think about why you trust your doctor about your illness. It isn't because your doctor knows everything — they may be hopeless at astronomy, cooking, or car repair, and that doesn't bother you at all. You trust them because they are reliable on the one thing you actually came for: your health.

You don't need an authority to know everything. You need them to be reliable on the thing you actually need from them.

Dharmakīrti's claim about the Buddha is built on exactly this shape, not on a claim to omniscience. He is explicit that whether the Buddha knew the exact number of insects in the world, or every grain of sand in the Ganges, is irrelevant to the proof — and deliberately set aside. The only question that matters is whether the Buddha is reliable on the one subject his teaching claims expertise in: the nature of suffering, and the way out of it.

Four qualifications, not a leap of faith

So what would it actually take for someone to earn that kind of narrow, specific reliability? Dharmakīrti's argument builds it from four pieces, each doing real work:

  1. Compassion as the motivating cause — the reason to investigate suffering in the first place has to be a genuine wish to end it for others, not idle curiosity or self-interest. (Motive matters: it determines what someone actually bothers to get right.)
  2. Sustained practice over a very long time — not a single flash of insight, but training deliberately cultivated until it becomes stable and reliable, the way a surgeon's steady hand comes from years, not talent alone. (This is what makes the result trustworthy rather than lucky.)
  3. Direct realization of the Four Noble Truths — actually seeing the nature of suffering and its cessation firsthand, not reasoning about it secondhand. (You want a doctor who has actually treated the illness, not just read about it.)
  4. Reliable transmission — able to accurately communicate what was realized, so that others can independently verify and walk the same path. (An insight nobody can follow is not yet an authority for anyone but the person who had it.)
Three ideas to carry out of today
  • This is a narrow claim, on purpose. Dharmakīrti isn't proving the Buddha knew everything — he's proving reliability on one specific, load-bearing subject, and explicitly sets the rest aside.
  • Trust is being built from evidence, not asserted by authority. Motive, training, direct experience, and communicability are each independently checkable ideas — this is closer to a résumé than a creed.
  • The doctor analogy is doing the real philosophical work. If you accept that narrow expertise can be trustworthy without omniscience in medicine, the same logic is what's being extended here.
Debate Check — a Collected Topics callback
Challenger
"So, the Buddha is a reliable authority on everything, because he's a reliable authority on liberation."
Defender
"That doesn't prove it! There is no pervasion."
Reliability on liberation doesn't force reliability on astronomy or arithmetic — and Dharmakīrti never claimed it did. This is exactly the same move you used to catch "has a color, therefore green" back in Session 1.
Part 2

Discussion Questions

No wrong answers here — bring your honest reaction.

  1. Does narrowing the claim to "reliable specifically about liberation" feel like a fair, honest move to you — or like moving the goalposts to make the claim easier to defend?
  2. Think of someone you already trust as an authority in a narrow domain (a doctor, a mechanic, a teacher). Which of the four qualifications above do they actually satisfy, and which don't obviously apply to them?
  3. Is compassion really necessary for reliability? Could someone with entirely selfish motives still stumble onto a true path and report it accurately?
  4. Dharmakīrti explicitly sets aside whether the Buddha knew trivial facts like the number of grains of sand. Does that concession strengthen the argument, or does it feel like it's dodging a legitimate question about omniscience?
  5. How is this argument different from simply saying "trust the Buddha because he's the Buddha"? Is the difference real, or just more elaborate packaging around the same faith claim?

Part 4

Self-Check

1. What is the doctor analogy actually meant to establish?
That narrow, subject-specific reliability (like a doctor's on health) can be genuine authority — without requiring omniscience about everything else.
2. Name the four qualifications Dharmakīrti attributes to the Buddha.
Compassion as motive, sustained practice over time, direct realization of the Four Noble Truths, and reliable transmission of that realization to others.
3. Why does Dharmakīrti explicitly set aside the question of whether the Buddha knew trivial facts (like the number of grains of sand)?
Because the proof only needs reliability on the one subject that matters — liberation from suffering — and omniscience about unrelated trivia is irrelevant to that claim, one way or the other.
