Reading the Pramāṇavārttika

釋量論 — Dharmakīrti's proof that the Buddha is actually worth trusting
Pilot · 3 sessions live Requires Collected Topics or equivalent Taught in English Free

Every Geluk monk who finishes Collected Topics walks straight into the same enormous text: Dharmakīrti's Pramāṇavārttika (《釋量論》), the great Indian treatise on what counts as valid knowledge. This course reads it the way The Buddha's Own Words reads the suttas — lecture notes, primary argument, discussion, assignment — rather than as a debate game. The four moves from Collected Topics don't disappear; they show up occasionally as a tool for testing an argument, the way a wrench shows up when you actually need to turn a bolt.

We start where the text itself starts: not with logic puzzles, but with a question every serious skeptic asks first. Why should anyone believe what the Buddha said about liberation, karma, or the nature of mind? Dharmakīrti's opening chapter — traditionally called Pramāṇasiddhi, "Establishing [the Buddha as] a Valid Authority" — is his answer, and it is far more careful and far less faith-based than most people, believers and skeptics alike, expect.

A note on the text: this is Luke Lin's original English exposition of Dharmakīrti's argument, not a verse-by-verse translation of the Pramāṇavārttika — no complete public-domain English translation of the text exists to build on, so each session is written from scratch based on the standard scholarly understanding of the chapter's structure and reasoning.

What you'll be able to do
  1. Explain Dharmakīrti's core move: a teacher doesn't need to know everything to be worth trusting — they need to be reliable on the thing that actually matters.
  2. Walk through the four qualifications Dharmakīrti attributes to the Buddha, and explain why each one is doing real argumentative work, not just decoration.
  3. Defend this argument against its most obvious objection — and recognize where it genuinely has limits.
  4. (As later sessions open) follow the text into its treatment of perception, inference, and what it actually means to know something — deepening everything Collected Topics introduced.
