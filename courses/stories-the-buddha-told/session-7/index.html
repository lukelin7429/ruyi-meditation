Session 7 · The stories he told

The turtle

A man cries in a market, and pays for it twice
Reading time about four minutes Before class nothing at all Where it is from Story 4, Fascicle 44
Part 1

A question, before any of this

Does a small kindness ever come back?

Most traditions say yes, and most people, quietly, are not sure. The version of the claim that gets repeated — be good and good things will happen to you — falls apart on contact with about a week of adult life, and everybody knows it.

This week's story appears at first to be exactly that claim in its simplest form: a man is kind to an animal, and years later the animal saves his life. It is the oldest shape a story can have.

It is also not what happens. Or rather: it happens, and then the story keeps going for another three paragraphs, and by the end the man is on his knees in front of a king who has ordered his head cut off — because of one of the people he rescued.

Two of his kindnesses nearly kill him. The one that saves him is the one he never thought about.

This is the longest of the anthology stories in this course and the strangest. Four minutes. It also ends with an execution, which I mention so that you are not surprised — these are not fables with soft edges, and the collection has no interest in making its people comfortable.

Part 3

Three momentsPlaces worth stopping

1 · He cries first, and it costs him everything

The market

"Long ago there was a man named Cíluó who saw someone selling a turtle and pitied it in his heart, and wept before the turtle. The turtle-seller said: why do you weep before a turtle? Cíluó answered: I cannot bear to see it. The poor turtle-seller laughed heartily: you are mad."

"The owner said: the turtle is worth a million. Cíluó took it home, and by exhausting his capital and interest raised eight hundred thousand… The turtle-seller said: since your money is at an end, you may work my fields to pay off the price."

昔有一人名慈羅。見人賣鼈心中憐之。向鼈啼泣。……賣鼈者大笑。汝癡狂耳。……主言。鼈直百萬。……傾舉子息得八十萬。……賣鼈者言。汝錢既盡可為作田以畢錢直。
See this passage in the full fascicle →

Before any plan, any merit, any thought of consequence, there is a grown man standing in a market crying over an animal, and being laughed at by a stranger who tells him he is out of his mind.

Now follow the money, because the story is precise about it and most retellings are not. The seller does not name a price and then get talked up. He watches a man weep, and then says: a million. The compassion is priced the instant it becomes visible.

And Cíluó pays it. He empties everything he has — capital and the interest on it, which is to say his entire livelihood and its future — reaches eight hundred thousand, and is still short. So he agrees to work the fields of the man who is exploiting him until the debt is clear. He indentures himself to the person who saw him crying and doubled the price.

Whatever this story is about, it is not about kindness being cheap.

2 · The one he was told not to pick up

In the flood, on the turtle's back

"Going on ten li, he saw the turtle-seller drifting on a raft, who asked Cíluó to take him up. The turtle said: I am already heavy, and I fear I shall be worn out and unable to cross. Be careful not to take him. Cíluó said: it is pitiable; how at this moment can we not take him? And Cíluó took him too."

見賣鼈子流被槎上。從慈羅欲求載之。鼈言我已重。恐必疲極不能自度。慎勿載之。慈羅言。可哀今是非當載之。慈羅復載之。
See this passage in the full fascicle →

Three people are pulled out of the water. Notice how differently the turtle treats them.

The woman: no comment at all. Cíluó asks, the turtle says "go on then," and she is aboard. The moths: no comment either. But the turtle-seller gets an actual warning, and a specific one — not "he is a bad man," but I am already carrying too much, and if I take him we may none of us get across.

Cíluó's reply is one line and it is the moral centre of the story: it is pitiable; how at this moment can we not take him? At this moment — meaning, while the man is in the water. The history between them has not been forgotten. It has simply been ruled irrelevant by the circumstances.

And then, on dry land, that man goes to the king and swears that Cíluó stole another man's wife and is trafficking her, and Cíluó is sentenced to death.

What the story does and does not say about that
  • It does not say he was wrong to pull him out. There is no line anywhere blaming Cíluó, and the story ends with him alive and vindicated.
  • It does not pretend the rescue was free, either. The turtle's warning was accurate. The weight was real. The man was dangerous, and the story lets him be dangerous.
  • Both of those are allowed to be true at once — which is something a simpler story would not permit, and is why this one is worth four minutes.
  • Worth noticing too: the woman's gratitude is what starts the trouble. Her gold is the thing the seller demands. Even the rescue that goes well nearly gets him killed.

3 · What actually saves him

In the king's court

"The king of Nàjié summoned Cíluó at once and ordered the officers to behead him. The officer reported the matter and was about to set down the writing, and the moths kept crawling onto his brush so that he could not form the characters. The king heard of it and asked Cíluó: what merit have you? Cíluó told him everything, and the king executed the turtle-seller."

那竭國王即召慈羅使吏斬之。吏上言其事欲下筆書。蛾輒緣筆不成字。王聞之便問慈羅。汝有何功德乎。慈羅具答。王誅賣鼈者。
See this passage in the full fascicle →

Go back and count what he rescued, and what each one did for him.

The turtle — bought at ruinous cost, warned him correctly, carried him. It saved his life, and the story would be complete and satisfying if it stopped there.

The woman — rescued without hesitation, grateful, generous. Her gold is the thing that gets him accused.

The turtle-seller — rescued against advice. Tries to have him killed.

Several pints of moths — mentioned in a single line, with no dialogue, no gratitude, and no possibility of any. They cannot speak to him. They do not know who he is. They are, in every sense that a person keeping accounts would recognise, not worth counting.

And it is the moths who keep landing on the clerk's brush until the death warrant cannot be written.

That is not a story about kindness being repaid. It is a much stranger and more useful claim: you have no idea which one it will be. The rescues you would put in the ledger are the ones that nearly finish him. The line you would edit out of the story for being unnecessary is the line his life turns on.

And the ending is not soft. The king does not forgive the false accuser or send him away — he has him put to death. Cíluó, who wept over a turtle and could not leave his own enemy in the water, is not consulted about it.

Part 4

One thing to tryThis week · once · and it must not count

Do one thing for the moths

Not a grand kindness. A small one, deliberately chosen so that it cannot possibly come back to you.

The rule is that it must fail all three tests. The recipient cannot thank you. The recipient cannot reciprocate. The recipient will never know it was you. If any of those is available, pick something else.

Candidates: something for an animal. Something for a place. Something left better than you found it where nobody is watching. A thing paid for anonymously. A piece of work done properly that will never be inspected.

Then notice the resistance, because there will be some, and it is the actual content of the exercise. Watch your mind try to upgrade the choice into one that could pay off — a person who might remember, a gesture someone might see. That reflex is the ledger, and this week you are asked to do one thing it cannot enter.

Once is enough. Cíluó picked up the moths in one line of a story and never thought about them again.

Question 1 of 5
What is the first thing Cíluó does on seeing the turtle for sale?
Correct: C. Before any plan or calculation, a grown man stands in a market crying over an animal, and a stranger tells him he is out of his mind. Everything else in the story follows from that moment, including the price.
Question 2 of 5
How does the price come about, and how does Cíluó meet it?
Correct: D. The compassion is priced the instant it becomes visible. And he pays anyway — emptying his entire livelihood and then indenturing himself to the very man who saw him crying and doubled the figure. Whatever this story is about, it is not about kindness being cheap.
Question 3 of 5
Three are pulled out of the flood. About which one does the turtle raise an objection, and what is it?
Correct: B. A practical warning, not a moral one, and an accurate one. Cíluó's answer is the moral centre of the story: it is pitiable; how at this moment can we not take him? The history has not been forgotten — it has been ruled irrelevant while the man is in the water.
Question 4 of 5
Who brings Cíluó before the king, and on what charge?
Correct: A. The man he pulled out against advice. And note the detail that makes it worse: the trouble starts with the woman's gratitude — her gold is what the seller demands. Even the rescue that went well nearly gets him killed.

What actually prevents the execution?
Correct: C. The rescue with no gratitude, no dialogue and no possibility of either — mentioned in a single line and worth nothing to anyone keeping accounts. Part 6

One question, left open

Sit with this

Which of your kindnesses are you keeping an account of?

Most people can name, without much effort, the two or three things they have done for others that they consider outstanding — unacknowledged, unrepaid, still open. That list is real and it is usually fair. The question this story raises is a different one: those are the entries you can see. Cíluó's ledger, had he kept one, would have had a turtle and a woman in it and nothing else, and both of those entries were about to get him killed.
