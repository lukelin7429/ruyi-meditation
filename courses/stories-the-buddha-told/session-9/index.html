Session 9 · The stories he told

The smallest thing

A bell on a door, and a man who broke a leg to get the same result
Reading time about three minutes for both Before class nothing at all Where it is from Story 7 of Fascicle 45, and Story 11 of Fascicle 44
Part 1

A question, before any of this

How little does it take to change a life?

People generally assume the answer is: quite a lot. Change is supposed to be effortful, and the things we expect to work are the big ones — the decision, the commitment, the conversation that finally happens.

This week's pair of stories gives you the smallest intervention in the entire anthology and the largest, side by side. The small one is a bell hung on a door by a woman who had run out of arguments. The large one is a man who wanted a particular result badly enough to break a stranger's leg to get it.

One of them works. It is not the one that took effort.

A note before you read the first story. It goes to hell — literally; there are wardens, and a cauldron. The collection assumes a cosmology that you are under no obligation to share, and nothing in this session depends on it. What matters is the mechanism, which would work exactly the same in a hospital corridor.

Part 3

Three momentsPlaces worth stopping

1 · She stops arguing and builds a device

The bell

"Long ago there was a man who did not believe, and his wife served the Buddha devoutly. The wife said to her husband: human life is impermanent and one may cultivate merit. Her husband had no mind for it and was idle."

"His wife, fearing he would enter hell in a life to come, said to him again: I should like to hang a bell on the door, so that when you go in and out you strike the bell and it sounds, and you say: homage to the Buddha. Her husband said: very good. And so it went for a long time."

昔有人不信。婦甚事佛。婦白婿曰。人命無常可修福德。婿無心懶墮。婦恐將來入地獄中。即復白婿。欲懸一鈴安著戶上。君出入時。掁鈴作聲稱南無佛。婿曰甚善。如是經久。
See this passage in the full fascicle →

The first thing she tries is the thing everybody tries. She makes the argument. It is a perfectly good argument — life is short, you could do something with that — and it is the same one people have been making to their spouses for several thousand years. And the text records the outcome in five characters: 婿無心懶墮. He had no mind for it and was idle.

What she does next is the whole story, and it is genuinely clever. She stops trying to change what he believes and changes what happens in the doorway. A bell. He walks through; it rings; he says four words. That is the entire proposal.

And notice why he agrees. "Very good," he says — immediately, with no resistance at all. Of course he does. It costs him nothing. It commits him to nothing. It does not require him to believe a single thing, admit he was wrong, or have a conversation about it ever again. She has designed the one request he cannot be bothered to refuse.

"And so it went for a long time." Years, presumably. A bell ringing, a man saying words he does not mean, on his way out to do whatever he did.

2 · It works in the worst possible place, by reflex

What happens later

"Her husband's life ended, and the hell-warders forked him up and threw him into a cauldron of boiling water; and the fork struck the cauldron and made a sound, and he took it for the sound of the bell and said: homage to the Buddha. The hell officer heard it: this man serves the Buddha — and released him and let him go out, and he obtained birth among humans."

其婿命終。獄卒扠之擲鑊湯中。扠掁鑊作音聲。謂是鈴聲。稱南無佛。獄官聞之。此人奉佛。放令出去。得生人中。
See this passage in the full fascicle →

Set aside the cauldron and look at what the story is actually claiming, because it is quite precise and quite unusual.

There is no deathbed repentance here. No moment of faith, no realisation, no final understanding. The man is in the worst situation of his existence, in a state of complete panic, with no capacity left for choosing anything — and a piece of metal hits a piece of metal, and his body produces four words it has produced ten thousand times before.

He does not mean them this time either. That is the point. What saves him is not belief but a groove worn into him over years by a small mechanical arrangement — installed by someone who loved him, could not persuade him, and settled for building something instead.

It is worth saying plainly how strange this is. The story does not require the man to be good, sincere, or even interested. It requires him to have walked through a door several thousand times.

3 · The man who broke a leg

Now the second story, which exists to stop you drawing the wrong conclusion from the first.

A household takes in a lame wandering monk, sees that his foot is afflicted, pities him, and provides for him for a year. When he leaves, host and guest weep together. Then the man lifts the couch and finds it heaped with gold, and becomes very rich.

The neighbour hears about this and asks how it happened. He is told, truthfully. And then:

The neighbour's method

"The neighbour, with an evil thought, hoped to find treasure, and looked for a lame man to provide for; and he searched everywhere and there was none. He met one religious man whose body was quite whole, and bound him and brought him back and broke his leg and provided for him a little while, and then forced him to go."

"After he had gone they lifted the couch — and moved by that evil mind, venomous snakes, wasps, and scorpions came and stung the whole household."

隣人惡念希覓珍寶。便覓一跛人欲供養之。遍求無有。會一道人身體完具。縛還折脚供養少時。強驅令去。去後發床。惡心所感。毒蛇蜂蝎來螫合家。
See this passage in the full fascicle →

The sequence is extraordinary and almost funny until you look at it directly. He identifies the input — lame monk — and goes looking for one. He cannot find one. So he manufactures one. Then he provides for the man he has crippled, exactly as the recipe specified, and hurries him out of the house so that he can check under the couch.

The story's own verdict is one line, and it is the sentence this whole session turns on:

The verdict

"He who got the treasure — his mind was upright and auspicious; those who were stung — their minds were not humane."

得珍寶者其心貞吉。被螫毒者其心不仁。
See this passage in the full fascicle →
Why these two belong on the same page
  • Effort is not the variable. The neighbour worked far harder than either the wife or the husband. He searched a whole district, committed a serious crime, and maintained a household guest for weeks. The husband walked through a door.
  • Nor is sincerity, exactly. The husband meant nothing by the words, ever — including at the end. If sincerity were the requirement he would have gone into the pot.
  • What separates them is whether anything was being extracted. The wife wanted her husband not to suffer. The first household saw a man with a bad foot and felt sorry for him. The neighbour wanted what was under the couch, and every single thing he did was aimed at it.
  • Which is why the bell could be empty and still work. It was not a transaction. Nobody in that house was ever owed anything for it.
Part 4

One thing to tryThis week · build it once, then stop thinking about it

Hang a bell

You have already met one of these in this course. In Session 6 a man bought fourteen paces, and they were worth four thousand taels of gold because he never had to decide to use them — the instruction was simply what he did when in doubt.

Build one arrangement that removes a decision. Not a resolution, not an intention, not a promise to yourself. A change to the physical world that makes a small good thing happen without you having to choose it each time.

The test is whether it still works on your worst day. A resolution does not; a bell does. The book by the bed rather than the plan to read. The phone charging in the other room rather than the intention to stop. The route home that goes past the person you never visit. The standing order rather than the good cause you keep meaning to support.

Make it so small it is embarrassing. That is not a compromise — it is the actual instruction. The wife in this story did not ask her husband for ten minutes a day, or a conversation, or an open mind. She asked for a bell, and she got a yes because there was nothing in it to refuse.

Then leave it alone. The grooves take years, and the man in this story was not thinking about his bell at any point.
Part 5

Five questionsClick an answer for immediate feedback · nothing is recorded

Question 1 of 5
What does the wife try first, and what happens?
Correct: B. 婿無心懶墮 — five characters for an outcome several thousand years of spouses will recognise. The argument is a perfectly good one. It simply does not work, and she is the rare person who notices that and changes method rather than volume.
Question 2 of 5
Why does the husband agree to the bell so readily?
Correct: C. "Very good," he says, at once. She has designed the one request he cannot be bothered to refuse — which is not a weakness in the plan but the whole of it.
Question 3 of 5
What actually saves him at the end?
Correct: D. No repentance, no realisation, no faith. A man in complete panic, with no capacity left for choosing anything, and a groove worn into him over years by a small mechanical arrangement. He does not mean the words this time either — that is precisely the point.
Question 4 of 5
In the second story, the neighbour wants the treasure and needs a lame monk to provide for. He cannot find one. What does he do?
Correct: A. He identifies the input, cannot source it, and manufactures it. Then he follows the recipe exactly and gets the guest out of the house so he can look under the couch. He works enormously harder than anyone else in either story.
Question 5 of 5
Read together, what do the two stories say the difference is?
Correct: C. Effort is not the variable — the neighbour worked hardest by far. Nor is sincerity, exactly: the husband never meant the words, including at the end, and if sincerity were the requirement he would have gone into the pot. 其心貞吉 against 其心不仁. The bell could be completely empty and still work, because nobody in that house was ever owed anything for it.

Part 6

One question, left open

Sit with this

What do you keep deciding, that you could arrange instead?

Most people are carrying two or three things they re-decide every single day and lose about half the time. That is not a failure of character; it is a design problem, and it is being solved with willpower because nobody suggested anything else. The woman in this story had a husband she could not argue with and a worry she could not put down, and what she came up with was a bell. Small enough to say yes to. Loud enough to hear in hell.
