Session 8 · The stories he told

The lion in the well

Two lions, two small animals, and two very different answers
Reading time about three minutes for both Before class nothing at all Where it is from Stories 5 and 3, Fascicle 47
Part 1

A question, before any of this

Who do you let help you?

Most people have a shorter list than they would admit, and it is usually sorted by rank. Help from above is flattering. Help from a peer is fine. Help from below — from someone junior, less able, less established, someone who owes you rather than the other way round — is oddly difficult to accept, and people will go to real lengths to avoid needing it.

This week you get two stories from the animal volumes of the anthology, which are the strangest and best part of the whole collection. Both are about a lion. In one, the lion is helped by a small animal and floats out of a well. In the other, the lion is helped by a small animal and loses an eye.

Same species. Same situation. The only difference is what each lion was willing to say out loud afterwards.

Fascicle 47 is called "Beasts and Animals," and it is organised by species — lions, elephants, horses, oxen, donkeys, dogs, deer, jackals, wolves, monkeys, hares, wildcats, rats. Every one of these creatures gets its own stories. You are about to read two of the lions'.

Part 3

Three momentsPlaces worth stopping

1 · Five hundred lions, one clause

The well

"…there was a lion, king of the beasts, who was lord of five hundred lions. Afterward this lion king grew old and sick and thin and his eyes grew dim, and, walking in front of the other lions, he fell into an empty well; and the five hundred lions all abandoned him and went."

有師子獸王。作五百師子主。是師子王後時老病瘦眼闇。在諸師子前行墮空井中。五百師子皆捨離去。
See this passage in the full fascicle →

Two details, three words apart, and they are the whole first half of the story.

"Walking in front." He is old, sick, thin, and half-blind, and he is still leading the procession. Nobody has taken the front from him and he has not offered it up. That is how he falls in — doing the thing he has always done, in a body that can no longer do it.

"All abandoned him and went." Five hundred. His entire court, disposed of in one clause, with no deliberation described, no argument among them, nobody even pausing at the edge. The story does not say they were cruel and does not stop to judge them. They simply leave, the way a crowd leaves.

2 · The jackal is not being nice

The one who comes back

"Then a jackal saw the lion king and thought: the reason I have been able to live at ease in this wood and to have my fill of meat is the lion king. Now he has fallen into a hard place — how shall I repay him?"

"By the side of this well there was a channel of water running, and the jackal at once set about it: with his feet he let the water through, and the water came in and filled the well, and the lion floated out."

爾時有一野干見師子王。作是念言。我所以得此林住安樂飽滿肉者由師子王。今墮急處云何當報。時此井邊有渠水流。野干即曰。以脚通水。水入滿井師子浮出。
See this passage in the full fascicle →

Read the jackal's reasoning carefully, because it is not what a sentimental version of this story would give him. There is no pity in it and no affection. It is an account. He has eaten well in this forest for years, and he has eaten well because a lion was killing things. He is a scavenger. Everything he has, he has had from the lion's leavings — and he is, so far as we are told, the only creature in the wood who has ever thought about that fact.

Then look at the method, which is the best thing in the story. He does not pull the lion out. He could not; nothing about him is strong enough, and the story never pretends otherwise. What he does instead is notice a water channel running past, open it with his feet, and flood the well until the lion rises.

He does not add strength to the situation. He changes the level of the water. That is what help from a small animal actually looks like, and it is why it is so easy to miss coming.

The forest spirit then says the line the story exists for:

The verse

"Though the body be strong and heroic in itself, / one should make a friend of the weak. / A small jackal was able to save / the lion king from the trouble of the well."

身雖自雄健　應以弱為友　小野干能救　師子王井難
See this passage in the full fascicle →

One detail you may have noticed at the end, and it is worth not glossing over: the story identifies the lion king as the Buddha, the jackal as his attendant Ānanda — and the five hundred lions who walked away as the monks. This is a tradition telling a story about itself, and it is not a flattering one.

3 · The other lion

Now the second story, which is the first one with the answer changed.

A lion king chokes on a rib-bone from an elephant he has killed, and dies, and revives. He asks a woodpecker to pull the bone out, promising to repay it. The bird climbs into his mouth and pulls it free. Days later, with the lion standing over a fresh kill, the woodpecker asks for the share it was promised. And the lion says this:

The lion's answer

"I am the lion king, / and killing is my family trade… / You did not measure yourself, / and escaped my teeth with difficulty. / That you got out of my mouth again — / how could you forget that kindness?"

汝既不自量　脫吾牙出難　還得出吾口　此恩何可忘
See this passage in the full fascicle →

Sit with the manoeuvre for a second, because it is beautifully done and you will recognise it. He does not deny the debt. He reverses it. The bird went into his mouth to save him; the lion's account of that event is that he generously declined to bite down. By the end of four lines the rescuer owes the rescued.

The woodpecker's reply is almost unbearably mild — "if you could be a little generous and grant me even a little, I would die without resentment." The lion gives it nothing and walks away.

So the bird follows him. It waits, days, until the lion has eaten and is sleeping without fear, and then lands on his forehead and takes out one of his eyes. And when the lion asks why, the woodpecker answers in the same form the lion used:

The woodpecker's answer

"Not knowing how to repay a heavy kindness, / you gave rise besides to a harming mind. / I now leave you one eye — / how could you forget that kindness? / Though you are king among beasts, / what you do has no reciprocity. / From this let us each be done; / let us make no further bond."

重恩不知報　乃復生害心　今留汝一目　此恩何可忘　汝雖獸中王　所行無反復　從是各自休　莫復作緣對
See this passage in the full fascicle →

It gives him back his own line. How could you forget that kindness? — the exact words he used to turn a rescue into a favour, now used to turn a mutilation into one.

The two lions, side by side
  • Both are kings. Both are at the top of everything. Both end up needing a much smaller animal.
  • The first one is saved — by a creature who had already worked out, on his own, that he owed something, and said so to himself before doing anything.
  • The second one is blinded — not for being cruel, but for being unable to say a true sentence: you helped me. He had the words. He used them backwards.
  • Neither lion asks for help. That is not what separates them. What separates them is what happened after the help arrived.
Part 4

One thing to tryThis week · one sentence · said out loud

Name one debt, to the person's face

The jackal's whole contribution begins with a thought he has entirely to himself: everything I have here, I have had from him. The second lion's ruin begins with a sentence he cannot bring himself to say.

Find someone who helped you and has never been told in plain words. It should be someone the help came from sideways or upwards — a junior colleague, a student, someone younger, someone who works for you, someone you have always been the capable one with. That is the direction the exercise is about.

Say the specific thing. Not "thanks for everything," which is a way of saying nothing. Name the occasion: when X happened, you did Y, and it made the difference. The specificity is the whole exercise, because vagueness is how the second lion would have handled it.

If that is too much this week, do the smaller version: ask one person for help you would normally not ask them for, and let them give it without immediately repaying it. Sitting in the debt for a few days is the practice.

Question 1 of 5
How does the lion king come to be in the well, and what do his five hundred lions do?
Correct: D. Two details three words apart. He falls in doing the thing he has always done, in a body that can no longer do it; nobody has taken the front from him and he has not offered it up. And his entire court is disposed of in a single clause — no deliberation, no argument, nobody pausing at the edge.
Question 2 of 5
What is the jackal's stated reason for helping?
Correct: C. No pity and no affection in it. He is a scavenger who has lived on a predator's leavings for years, and he appears to be the only creature in the forest who has ever thought about that fact.
Question 3 of 5
How does the jackal actually get the lion out?
Correct: B. The best thing in the story. He does not pull — he could not, and the text never pretends otherwise. He changes the level of the water instead. That is what help from a small animal actually looks like, and it is exactly why it is so easy to miss coming.
Question 4 of 5
In the second story, the woodpecker climbs into the lion's mouth to pull out the bone, and later asks for what it was promised. How does the lion answer?
Correct: A. Note that he never denies the debt — he inverts it. The bird went into his mouth to save him; the lion's version is that he generously declined to bite down. In four lines the rescuer owes the rescued. It is beautifully done, and most people have met it at least once.

What separates the two lions?
Correct: C. Neither lion asks — that is not the difference. And note the woodpecker's final verse hands the lion back his own line: "I now leave you one eye — how could you forget that kindness?" The exact words he used to turn a rescue into a favour, now used to turn a mutilation into one.

Part 6

One question, left open

Sit with this

Who is currently keeping you afloat, who you have never said so to?

There is usually at least one, and they are usually below you in whatever order you are both standing in — which is precisely why it has not been said. The jackal was not owed an apology and did not want one. He simply noticed, by himself, where his dinner had been coming from for years. Somebody is currently noticing that about you, or nobody is, and both of those are worth knowing.
