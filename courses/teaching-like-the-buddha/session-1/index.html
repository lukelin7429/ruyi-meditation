Session 1 · Teaching a method, not handing over a belief

Don't Just Believe It

卡拉瑪經 — 不要因為是傳統、是邏輯、是老師說的，就信
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A village tired of being told what to believe

The people of Kesamutta, a market town of the Kālāmas, have a specific complaint when the Buddha arrives: every ascetic and brahmin who passes through praises his own doctrine and tramples on everyone else's. "We are perplexed, sir, we are in doubt: which of these good ascetics and brahmins spoke the truth, and which spoke falsehood?" This is not a philosophical question posed by leisured scholars. It is the exhausted question of people who have been marketed to by every teacher in the region and have no way to adjudicate between them. It is worth noticing that the Buddha does not answer by asking them to trust him instead.

Ten things not to rely on

What he gives them instead is a list — famous enough that most people who have heard of only one teaching from the entire Pāli Canon have heard of this one, usually stripped of everything around it. Don't rely on oral tradition, on lineage, on hearsay, on scripture, on logical reasoning, on inferential reasoning, on reasoned cogitation, on the acceptance of a view after pondering it, on the speaker's seeming competence, or on the thought "this ascetic is our guru." Read as a stand-alone slogan, this looks like an invitation to trust nothing but your own current opinion — which is exactly the opposite of what the sutta goes on to teach.

The actual test: observed consequences

The alternative he gives them is not "trust yourself." It is a specific, repeatable procedure: when greed, hatred, or delusion arise and are acted on, does that lead — observably, in this life, for the person and for others — to harm and suffering, or to welfare and happiness? The Kālāmas can verify this themselves, without taking anyone's word for it, because the test is behavioral and its results are in front of them. The teaching survives contact with a village that trusts no one, because it was never asking them to trust anyone.

Not "anything goes"

It would be a serious misreading to take this as license for pure subjectivism — "whatever feels right to me is right." The bar the Buddha sets is a real bar: does the state, when undertaken and practiced, conduce to harm or to welfare, for oneself and for others, when a wise person would judge it either way? He is not replacing one authority (tradition) with a weaker one (personal preference); he is replacing an unverifiable authority with a verifiable method.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • He teaches a method, not a belief. The Kālāma Sutta contains almost no doctrine. What it contains is a procedure anyone can run without the teacher present — which is the entire point.
  • The test is consequences, not source. Ten different kinds of authority are set aside in one sentence — not because authority is worthless, but because it cannot settle the question the Kālāmas are actually asking.
  • It is not relativism. "Test it yourself" is not the same claim as "anything you conclude is equally valid." The test has a real answer: harm, or welfare.

AN 3.65 — With the Kālāmas of Kesamutta

Widely known as the Kālāma Sutta. A confused village, visited by rival teachers who each praise only their own doctrine, is given a method for testing any claim against its observed effect — not a doctrine to replace the ones they've already heard too many of.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. The sutta lists ten specific things not to rely on. Pick one you think a modern classroom relies on constantly (a textbook's authority? a teacher's credentials?) and describe what it would look like to teach a student to test that claim by its effects instead.
  2. Is there a difference between what the Buddha does here and simply telling the Kālāmas "think for yourselves"? What does the sutta give them that a bare exhortation to independent thought would not?
  3. The Buddha does not exempt his own teaching from this test — elsewhere he invites the same scrutiny to be turned on him. What would it mean for a teacher, in any subject, to actually mean that?
  4. Where is the line, in your own teaching, between "don't just take my word for it" and giving a student nothing to stand on at all? Is that a real tension, or a false one?
Part 4

AssignmentChoose one · half to one page · bring it next session

Option A — Run the test on something you were taught

Pick one belief you were taught early — in school, at home, in a religious setting — that you never independently verified. Apply the sutta's test to it in writing: what would "observed consequence, for yourself and others" actually look like here, and have you ever checked?

Option B — Design one lesson around the method

Sketch, in half a page, how you would teach one fact or rule you currently just assert to students (a grammar rule, a historical claim, a classroom norm) using the Kālāma method instead — giving them a way to test it rather than a reason to believe you.
