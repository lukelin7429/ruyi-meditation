Session 6 · The most extreme student, and the fewest possible words

I Have Stopped

鴦掘摩羅經 — 沒有教不會的人
Part 1

Lecture Notes

Walking unarmed toward a killer

Aṅgulimāla — "the finger-garland" — has already killed enough people, by the story's own count, that a king is mustering an army against him. The Buddha, warned by villagers not to take that road, walks it anyway, alone. When Aṅgulimāla sees him and gives chase, running his fastest, he cannot catch a Buddha walking at an ordinary pace — and shouts for him to stop. This is the entire setup: the most dangerous possible student, and a teacher who approaches him instead of waiting to be approached.

"I have stopped"

"I have stopped, Aṅgulimāla," the Buddha says. "You stop too." Aṅgulimāla, baffled — he can see the Buddha is still walking — demands what this means. The answer: "I have stopped forever, having renounced violence toward all living beings; but you have no restraint toward things that live. That's why I have stopped and you have not." No sermon on non-violence, no scripture, no argument about karma. A pun on a single word, aimed exactly at the one thing Aṅgulimāla is doing at that moment, delivered in the fewest words that could possibly land it.

Taming without rod or sword

It works — immediately and completely. Aṅgulimāla throws away his weapons and asks for ordination on the spot; the Buddha, with a single further word, ordains him then and there. No probation, no negotiation with a mass murderer's request. The tradition later has King Pasenadi, arriving in force to hunt Aṅgulimāla down, sit face to face with the ordained monk and marvel that he could be tamed "without force, without weapons" — by the Buddha alone.

What teaching him didn't erase

The sutta does not pretend ordination deletes the past. Aṅgulimāla, now a monk, is struck with stones and clods by villagers who recognize him on his alms round, bleeding, his robe torn — and the Buddha tells him to bear it: "you are experiencing here and now the result of deeds that might have caused you to be tormented in hell for many years." The kamma is not escaped by the teaching; it is met, consciously, as it ripens, rather than compounded by more of the same. Later, asked to bless a woman in difficult labor, he makes an "act of truth" — "since I was born, I do not recall having intentionally deprived a living being of life" — true only of his life as a monk, and it works. He becomes an arahant, and the sutta's closing image is his own: "the moon freed from clouds."

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The teacher goes to the student, not the reverse. The Buddha takes the road he was warned against, toward the one person least likely to come looking for a lesson.
  • Sometimes the whole teaching is one exact word, aimed precisely. No doctrine is recited at a man mid-chase. A pun on "stopped," pointed at the one thing he is doing right now, is the entire intervention.
  • Redemption is not erasure. Aṅgulimāla still bears the ripening of his past deeds as a monk — the teaching transforms how he meets the consequence, it does not delete the consequence.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Why does "I have stopped" work, when nothing else could have caught Aṅgulimāla's attention mid-chase? What made this specific word, at this specific moment, the right amount of teaching?
  2. The sutta insists that Aṅgulimāla still suffers for his past deeds even after becoming a monk. Why might it matter, pedagogically, that the story does not simply let him off the hook once he's "reformed"?
  3. Is there a student you, or any teacher you know, quietly wrote off as unreachable? What would "going to them," the way the Buddha walks the road he was warned against, actually look like in a real classroom, with real constraints?
  4. This story connects strongly to your own commitment to engaged Buddhism — connecting the Dharma to social justice. What does a sutta about a murderer's full ordination and eventual arahantship say about who a community of practice is, or should be, willing to include?
Part 4

AssignmentChoose one · half to one page · bring it next session

Option A — The fewest possible words

Think of a real moment where a student was in crisis — mid-conflict, mid-mistake, not in a state to receive a lecture. What is the shortest true sentence you could have said, aimed precisely at that moment, the way "I have stopped" is aimed at Aṅgulimāla mid-chase? Write it, and explain why it would have worked or why it wouldn't have.

Option B — Consequence without erasure

Write half a page on a time you (or a student) had to face the real consequence of a past mistake even after genuinely changing course. How does "the moon freed from clouds" — bearing the result without being defined by it — differ from either denial or permanent shame?
