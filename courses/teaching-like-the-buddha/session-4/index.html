Session 4 · What a teacher says about holding the teaching itself

The Raft

蛇喻經 — 連正確的教法都可能被誤抓
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A monk who got it backwards

The occasion for this sutta is a monk named Ariṭṭha, who has formed a pernicious view: that the things the Buddha describes as obstructive are not, in fact, obstructive to the one who indulges in them. Confronted, he doesn't recant — and the Buddha's response widens into one of the most direct pieces of pedagogical self-reflection anywhere in the canon: some people learn the teaching only to win arguments or recite it, without examining the meaning with wisdom, and "such persons, having learned it wrongly... will therefore reap only their own misfortune and demerit for a long time." Correct doctrine, wrongly grasped, is not harmless.

The cobra simile: how to grasp a teaching without getting bitten

A man wanting a cobra grabs it by the body or the tail; it turns and bites his hand or arm, and "he would meet death or deadly suffering." A man who grasps it correctly, with a forked stick, pins its head, and though it may wind itself around his arm, he does not meet death or deadly suffering. The teaching is the cobra either way. What determines the outcome is entirely how it is grasped — a claim the Buddha makes about his own words, not someone else's.

The raft simile: even the Dhamma, once it's done its job

A man crossing a great expanse of water on a raft he built himself reaches the far shore and thinks: this raft has been very useful to me — should I carry it on my head for the rest of my journey? The Buddha's answer: no — haul it onto dry land, or set it adrift, and go on without it, and that man would be doing what should be done with that raft. "I have taught the Dhamma as similar to a raft, for the purpose of crossing over, not for the purpose of holding onto." And then the sentence that makes the whole simile impossible to soften: "You, monks, by understanding the Dhamma-teaching as similar to a raft, should abandon even good states, how much more so bad states."

Give up what isn't yours

The chapter closes with a different but related image, set at Jeta's Grove: if people were to carry off the grass, sticks, branches, and foliage there, burn them, or do with them as they wish, would you think, "people are carrying us off"? No, because that is not self, not what belongs to self. In the same way, the Buddha says, give up whatever is not yours — bodily and mental states included — and it will lead to your welfare and happiness for a long time. Non-attachment, extended all the way to the teaching that produced the insight of non-attachment in the first place.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The teaching can be grasped wrongly — that is a real risk, not a hypothetical. Ariṭṭha didn't misquote the Buddha; he heard the actual words and drew a harmful conclusion. Correctness of the words is not enough.
  • The raft simile applies to the teaching itself, not just to worldly attachments. "Abandon even good states" is the Buddha instructing students to eventually let go of the very teaching he is delivering — a claim most teachers never make about their own material.
  • Non-clinging extends to everything, including insight. The Jeta's Grove simile pushes the same logic further: even states of mind produced by the practice are not something to hold onto as "mine."

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Ariṭṭha didn't get the words of the teaching wrong — he drew a self-serving conclusion from correct premises. Can you think of an example, from any subject you teach, where a technically accurate fact was used to support a harmful or mistaken conclusion? What was missing?
  2. "Abandon even good states" is a striking thing for a teacher to say about their own teaching. Is there an equivalent move available in secular education — a teacher telling students to eventually outgrow the very framework being taught? What would that look like, concretely?
  3. The raft simile is often quoted alone, out of context, as "all religions are just rafts, pick whichever works." Having now read the full sutta, is that a fair reading of what the Buddha is actually saying here?
  4. What is the difference, in your own teaching, between a student who has correctly learned a rule and a student who has correctly understood why the rule exists? Which one does this sutta suggest actually matters?

AssignmentChoose one · half to one page · bring it next session

Option A — Find your own raft

Name one rule, formula, or framework you teach that is meant to be useful now and eventually unnecessary — a scaffold students should be able to set down once they've crossed over. Write half a page on how you would signal that to students explicitly, the way the Buddha signals it about his own Dhamma.

Option B — Diagnose a wrong grasp

Describe a real case (your own classroom, or more broadly) where someone held a technically correct piece of knowledge in a way that caused harm — a wrong grasp of the cobra, not a wrong cobra. What would "grasping it correctly" have looked like instead?
