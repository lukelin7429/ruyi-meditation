Part 1

The Puzzle

Here's an old riddle, first written down by a German philosopher in 1892, though sky-watchers had quietly lived with it for millennia: ancient astronomers gave a name to the bright point that rises just before dawn — the Morning Star — and a different name to the bright point that appears just after sunset — the Evening Star. Two names. Two appearances, months apart, in opposite parts of the sky.

Then it turned out: they're the same object. Both are the planet Venus. So here's the question this session is really about — were the Morning Star and the Evening Star ever "different things"? Or were they one thing the whole time, and only the names were different?

"Same in entity, different in name." — a phrase you're about to use constantly.

This isn't just an old astronomy trivia question. It's the same move you'll eventually need for much bigger claims — like whether "the mind" and "consciousness" are one thing described two ways, or genuinely different things. Today we build the tool on something safe first.

Part 2

The New IdeaOne vs. Different — plus a quick recap of the moves

Quick recap — you still have exactly four replies, unchanged from Session 1:

"So…""It follows that…" "That's not true.""The reason is not established." "That doesn't prove it.""There is no pervasion." "Yes, I agree.""I accept it."

What's new today is the subject those moves get pointed at. Two definitions to hold onto:

One (the same)一 (gcig)
Two names or descriptions pointing at exactly the same thing.Morning Star / Evening Star → both are Venus
Different異 (tha dad)
Two genuinely separate things — not just two names for one thing.The Morning Star / the Moon → two different objects

The test monks actually use: if X and Y are truly one, then whatever is true of X must be true of Y — no exceptions. If you can find even one thing true of X but not Y, they're different.

Part 3

Worked ExampleSame trap-and-catch pattern as Session 1

Challenger
"So, the object you saw at dawn is the same as the object I saw at dusk, because both are Venus." "It follows that the subject, the Morning Star, is one entity with the Evening Star, because both are the planet Venus."
A real, verifiable fact about the physical object — not about its name.
Defender
"Yes, I agree." "I accept it."
Correct — one physical object, two appearances, two names.
Challenger
"So, the Morning Star and the Evening Star are different things, because they have different names." "It follows that the Morning Star and the Evening Star are different entities, because they have different names."
The trap: this session's version of Session 1's "true but toothless" reason.
Defender
"That doesn't prove it!" "There is no pervasion."
Having different names is true — but "different name" doesn't force "different thing." Same object, described two ways.
The one habit this session is really training
  • Don't confuse the label with the thing. Different words can point at one entity; the same word can sometimes hide two entities. Check the thing, not the name.
  • "Different name" is never, by itself, proof of "different thing." It's exactly the same shape of mistake as "has a color" not proving "is green" from Session 1.
A preview, not a lesson yet

There's a much stranger version of "one" waiting a few sessions ahead — two things can be one entity and still count as conceptually different in a precise technical sense monks give its own name. You don't need it yet. File it away: "same thing, but not the same idea of it" is coming.

Part 4

PracticeSame three-move check as Session 1

1. "So, water and H₂O are different things, because 'water' and 'H₂O' are different words."
"That doesn't prove it!""There is no pervasion." — different words, same substance. Same trap as the Morning/Evening Star.
2. "So, Samuel Clemens wrote 'The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn,' because Mark Twain wrote it, and Mark Twain is Samuel Clemens."
"Yes, I agree.""I accept it." — "Mark Twain" and "Samuel Clemens" are one entity (a real pen name), so whatever is true of one is true of the other.
3. "So, the Morning Star and the Evening Star are one, because they are both stars."
"That's not true!""The reason is not established." — the reason itself is false: Venus is a planet, not a star. (It happens to reach the right conclusion by accident — a false reason proves nothing, even when the conclusion is true.)
