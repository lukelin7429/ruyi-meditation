Session 5 · Inside one moment

The four parts of a single knowing

How one event becomes a knower and a known — and where the regress stops
Reading time about thirty-five minutes Before class Sessions 3–4 The text Cheng Weishi Lun, fascicle 2
Part 1

One event, two halvesThe problem hiding inside the word "experience"

This is the session that has least competition in English, and it is where the Cheng Weishi Lun earns its reputation among philosophers rather than among practitioners.

Start with something ordinary. You see a cup. Describe it and you will produce two items: a cup, and you-seeing-it. An object and a subject. Every account of perception in any tradition has to say something about how those two are related, and there are only so many options — the object causes the subject's state, or the subject constructs the object, or they are two aspects of one thing.

The first session of this course already quoted the treatise's answer, though it went by quickly: "Transforms" means that the substance of consciousness turns and manifests as two divisions: a perceived aspect and a perceiving aspect. One event, appearing as two. This session is the detailed version, and it turns out that two is not enough.

Cheng Weishi Lun · fascicle 2 · the self-witnessing aspect

"the perceived-aspect is what is grasped; the perceiving-aspect is the perceiving-activity; and the underlying substance both aspects depend on is called the "self-witnessing aspect." If this did not exist, one could never remember one's own past mental states — just as one can never remember an object one has never actually experienced."

則說相分是所緣，見分名行相，相、見所依自體名事，即自證分。此若無者，應不自憶心、心所法，如不曾更境必不能憶故，
Read this passage in its place — fascicle 2, "The Four Parts of Every Act of Cognition" →

That argument deserves a moment, because it is doing something unusual: it is an argument from memory to the structure of perception.

You can remember the cup. You can also remember seeing the cup — remember being tired when you saw it, remember the seeing being blurry. But you cannot remember what you never experienced. So if the act of seeing itself is remembered, the act of seeing itself must have been experienced at the time. Something was aware of the seeing, while the seeing was happening. A perceived aspect and a perceiving aspect are not enough to account for that; there has to be a third thing, and the treatise calls it the self-witnessing aspect.

Part 2

Measuring, and the result of measuringA borrowed framework, and what it clarifies

The treatise now reaches for a model from the Buddhist logicians — the school this Academy's Pramāṇavārttika course is about. Their word for a valid cognition is pramāṇa, which in Chinese becomes 量, a measure. And any measurement has three components: the thing measured, the measuring, and the result.

Cheng Weishi Lun · fascicle 2 · three parts

"In fact, whenever mind or a mental factor arises, careful examination shows it has three parts: what is measured, what does the measuring, and the result of measuring are distinct, and the perceived- and perceiving-aspects must have some substance they depend on."

然心、心所一一生時，以理推徵各有三分，所量、能量、量果別故，相、見必有所依體故。
Read this passage in its place — fascicle 2, "The Four Parts of Every Act of Cognition" →

The analogy is worth spelling out, because it is the clearest thing in the section. Put a cloth on a scale. There is the cloth; there is the weighing; and there is the reading — two kilos. The reading is not the cloth and it is not the act of weighing. It is what the weighing yields.

Self-witnessing is the reading. It is not the cup and not the looking; it is what the looking delivers, the registering of the seeing as having occurred. And the treatise is careful to add, quoting the logicians directly, that these three have no separate substance. Three functions, one consciousness. Nothing has been added to the scene — the same single event, described at three levels.

Perceived aspect
相分
The object as it appears. What is measured. The only part that is never a subject.
Perceiving aspect
見分
The seeing. What does the measuring. Can be accurate or mistaken.
Self-witnessing aspect
自證分
The registering of the seeing. The result. Always direct and accurate.
Witnessing of the witnessing
證自證分
What confirms the third. Also always direct — and where the series stops.
Part 3

Why four and not three, and why not fiveThe regress, and the argument that stops it

Here is the move the treatise is known for, and the reason this section is still discussed. Having established a third part, it notices that the third part has the same problem as the first two.

Cheng Weishi Lun · fascicle 2 · the fourth part

"Analyzed more finely, mind and mental factors should be seen to have a fourth part as well: the three already given, plus the self-witnessing of the self-witnessing. Without this fourth part, what would verify the third part itself?"

又心、心所若細分別應有四分，三分如前，復有第四證自證分。此若無者，誰證第三？
Read this passage in its place — fascicle 2, "The Four Parts of Every Act of Cognition" →

Anyone who has met this kind of argument can see the cliff coming. If the third needs a fourth to verify it, the fourth needs a fifth, and so on forever. An infinite regress here would be fatal: it would mean no cognition is ever complete, because the verification never finishes.

The treatise's way out is the most elegant thing in the ten fascicles, and it is not a dodge:

Cheng Weishi Lun · fascicle 2 · no infinite regress

"The fourth takes only the third as its object, not the second, since that would serve no purpose — the third and fourth are both, without exception, accurate, direct perception. Mind and mental factors are thus composed of these four parts together, each equipped with an object-side and a subject-side relative to its neighbors, with no infinite regress — the fourth verifies the third, and needs nothing further to verify itself."

第四證自證分，唯緣第三，非第二者，以無用故，第三、第四皆現量攝，故心、心所四分合成，具所、能緣，無無窮過，
Read this passage in its place — fascicle 2, "The Four Parts of Every Act of Cognition" →

The third and fourth verify each other. The fourth confirms the third; the third, already established, confirms the fourth. They form a closed pair, and the closure is legitimate because of a property neither of the first two parts has: the inner pair are always accurate direct perception, never mistaken and never inferential.

That is the whole argument, and it is worth being clear about why it is not circular in the vicious sense. A circle is vicious when each term needs the other to establish it and neither has independent standing. Here the claim is that the inner pair cannot be wrong — they are not making a judgment about anything that could fail to be the case. The second part can misperceive: you can see a rope as a snake. But the registering that seeing occurred is not the sort of thing that can be in error. You may be wrong about what you saw. You cannot be wrong that seeing happened.

Whether that holds is a genuine philosophical question, and the treatise does not pretend otherwise. But notice the shape of the solution: the regress is stopped not by declaring a foundation by fiat, but by identifying a level at which the thing that generates the regress — the possibility of error — no longer applies.

You may be wrong about what you saw. You cannot be wrong that seeing happened.

The treatise then adds, with characteristic flexibility, that the four can be counted as three, or as two, or as one, depending on how finely you want to divide — because they were never four things in the first place. They are one event, analysed.

Part 4

Many lamps, one lightThe objection everybody raises, and the treatise's answer

Every reader of Consciousness-Only eventually asks the same question, usually in a slightly triumphant tone. If the world is what my consciousness transforms into, why do we all see the same cup? Why can I not simply transform it into something better?

The treatise's answer is one of its most quoted images.

Cheng Weishi Lun · fascicle 2 · the shared world

"Although what each individual sentient being transforms is, strictly speaking, separate, the appearances resemble one another and occupy no distinct locations — just as the light of many lamps, each entirely filling the room, appears to be a single, unified light."

雖諸有情所變各別而相相似，處所無異，如眾燈明各遍似一。
Read this passage in its place — fascicle 2, "How Billions of Private Minds Share One World" →

Light a dozen lamps in a room. Each one fills the whole space; no lamp owns a region. Take one away and the room dims slightly everywhere, not in one corner. You cannot point to where one lamp's light stops and another's begins — and yet there is no single collective lamp anywhere. There are only the individual lamps and the one illumination they make.

The mechanism underneath the image is the shared-characteristic seed (共相種). Beings whose past actions were of a similar kind ripen similar seeds, and so construct similar worlds, in what amounts to the same place. The world is not a public object that private minds observe. It is a convergence.

And notice the answer to "why can I not transform it into something better": the seeds ripening are the ones you have. You are not choosing the construction any more than you are choosing to see the rope before you see it is not a snake. The transformation is a result, not an act.

The treatise even records a disagreement about scope — whether every being's consciousness contributes to this world, or only those living here or about to be. It comes down on the narrower side, and the reasoning is refreshingly practical: a buddha would otherwise have to be generating this defiled world, and someone reborn in the formless realm would be maintaining a place they will never return to.

What Session 5 establishes
  • Subject and object are one event described twice, not two things that meet.
  • Memory of your own seeing proves a third part. You cannot remember what was never experienced.
  • Three parts, on the logicians' model — what is measured, the measuring, and the reading that the measuring yields.
  • Four, not three, and not infinitely many. The inner pair verify each other, and the regress stops because error has no purchase there.
  • The shared world is a convergence, not a public object — many lamps, one light, built from shared-characteristic seeds.
Part 5

One practiceLooking for the third part

The reading on the scale

Unlike the eighth consciousness, the structure described in this session is claimed to be present in every moment of ordinary experience — which means it can be checked, at least partially, rather than only accepted.

Sit where you can see something plain — a wall, a cup, a patch of floor. Look at it for a minute without doing anything about it.

Now separate three things, slowly, one at a time. First: the appearance — colour, edge, shape. That is the perceived aspect. Second: the looking — the directedness, the fact that this is being attended to rather than something else. That is the perceiving aspect. Third, and this is the one worth the time: not the wall and not the looking, but the fact that looking is registering as happening. Do not try to see it as a further object; if you go looking for it, you will only find another appearance. Ask instead whether, had it been absent, you could report afterwards that you had been looking.

Most people find the first two easy and the third slippery — which is exactly what the treatise would predict, since the third is not an object and cannot be made into one without turning it into the first. If you end the exercise unsure whether you found it, that is a more accurate result than confidence.

One question to sit with

If the world you live in is a convergence rather than a place, what exactly are you sharing it with other people?

The treatise's answer is: similar karma. Which is a stranger claim than it first sounds — it says the reason you and the person beside you inhabit the same room is not that the room is there, but that the two of you have done sufficiently similar things.
