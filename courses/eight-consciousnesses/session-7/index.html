Part 1

Four sessions underground. This one comes up to the surface — to the consciousnesses you can actually catch in the act.

Cheng Weishi Lun · fascicle 5 · the third transformation

"Next, the third transformation has, in its distinctions, six kinds; discerning objects is its nature and characteristic; it is wholesome, unwholesome, or neither."

頌曰：次第三能變，差別有六種，了境為性、相，善、不善、俱非。
Read this passage in its place — fascicle 5, "The Third Transformation" →

One line of that verse changes the moral landscape of the whole model. The eighth is morally indeterminate. The seventh is covered-yet-neutral. Only here, in the third transformation, does the verse say wholesome, unwholesome, or neither.

Which means: this is where anything happens. All karma is made here. All practice is done here. The storehouse only records and the seventh only misreads; the six are the only place in the system where a mind can be better or worse than it was. That is why the treatise now slows down and spends two and a half fascicles on what accompanies them.

Part 2

Five that are always thereThe minimum conditions for anything to be experienced at all

Fifty-one mental factors, in six categories. The first category is the smallest and the most interesting: five factors that accompany every moment of consciousness, in all eight consciousnesses, without exception.

Cheng Weishi Lun · fascicle 5 · the first two categories

"The first, universal factors, are contact and the rest; next, the particular factors are aspiration, resolve, recollection, concentration, and wisdom — their objects each differ."

頌曰：初遍行觸等；次別境謂欲、勝解、念、定、慧，所緣事不同。
Read this passage in its place — fascicle 5, "The Five Universal Mental Factors" →

The five universals are contact, attention, feeling, perception, and volition — and the claim is not that they usually occur but that nothing can be experienced without all five. Between them they constitute an event's being an experience at all: something meets, attention turns to it, it registers as pleasant, unpleasant or neither, it is taken as something, and there is a movement toward or away.

Contact
觸
Faculty, object and consciousness meeting. Without it nothing arrives.
Attention
作意
The turning-toward. Without it the arrival is not taken up.
Feeling
受
Pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral. There is no unfelt experience.
Perception
想
Taking it as something — the grasping of a mark.
Volition
思
The moving of mind. This is the factor that is karma.

The treatise's method for establishing universality is worth noticing, because it is the same method used throughout: scripture and reason together serve as the standard of proof. It cites the Buddha's formula — eye and form as condition, eye-consciousness arises, the meeting of three is contact, and born together with contact are feeling, perception and volition — and then a second passage for attention: if the faculty is unimpaired, the object is present, and attention properly arises, only then can consciousness be produced.

The second category, the particular factors, are the opposite case: aspiration, resolve, recollection, concentration and wisdom arise only toward particular objects and are often absent. It is worth registering that concentration and wisdom are in this group. They are not the mind's default condition. They are specific factors that have to be present, which is a quietly encouraging thing for a system this deterministic to say — they can be cultivated precisely because they are not guaranteed.

Part 3

Eleven wholesome, and what faith turns out to beA definition that will surprise most English readers

The third category is the eleven wholesome factors, and the treatise's treatment of the first of them is the best argument for reading this material in the original rather than in summary.

Cheng Weishi Lun · fascicle 6 · faith

"What is faith? Its nature is deep acceptance, delight, and aspiration with respect to what is real, what possesses virtue, and what has capability, taking the mind's own clarity as its essence; its function is counteracting lack of faith and delighting in what is wholesome."

云何為信？於實、德、能，深忍、樂、欲，心淨為性；對治不信，樂善為業。
Read this passage in its place — fascicle 6, "Faith — The Mind's Own Clarity" →

Faith here is not belief without evidence, and it is not belief at all. Its essence is 心淨, the mind's own clarity — a settledness, the absence of the churn that doubt produces. The traditional image, which Xuanzang's readers would have known, is a water-purifying gem dropped into a muddy cup: nothing is added, and the water clears.

And it has three objects, which are worth separating:

Cheng Weishi Lun · fascicle 6 · three kinds of faith

"first, faith in what is real — deeply accepting the true facts and principles of all dharmas; second, faith in what possesses virtue — deeply believing in and delighting in the genuine, pure virtues of the Three Jewels; third, faith in what has capability — deeply believing that every mundane and supramundane wholesome quality has the power to be attained and accomplished, giving rise to aspiration accordingly."

一、信實有，謂於諸法實事、理中，深信忍故；二、信有德，謂於三寶真淨德中，深信樂故；三、信有能，謂於一切世、出世善，深信有力，能得能成，起希望故。
Read this passage in its place — fascicle 6, "Faith — The Mind's Own Clarity" →

The third kind is the one that does not appear in most English discussions of Buddhist faith, and it is the most practically significant: confidence that the thing can actually be done. Not that the teaching is true, and not that the teacher is admirable, but that improvement is possible — and that this confidence produces aspiration as its result.

The treatise is also precise about the internal mechanism: acceptance is faith's cause, and delighted aspiration is faith's result. Faith itself is the clarity in the middle. Someone who has acceptance without any resulting aspiration, on this analysis, does not yet have faith; they have agreed with something.

Nothing is added, and the water clears.
Part 4

Six roots and twenty branchesThe mind's troubles, sorted by how deep they go

Then the long descent. Six fundamental afflictions, and twenty secondary ones derived from them — more than half the total inventory. The treatise is not being pessimistic; it is being granular where granularity is useful, since these are what practice is actually up against.

Cheng Weishi Lun · fascicle 6 · the six fundamental afflictions

"The afflictions are: craving, hatred, delusion, conceit, doubt, and wrong view."

頌曰：煩惱謂貪、瞋、癡、慢、疑、惡見。
Read this passage in its place — fascicle 6, "The Six Fundamental Afflictions" →

Each gets a definition in the same three-part format used for faith — nature, what it obstructs, and what it does. Craving is attachment to existence and the means of existence; it obstructs non-craving, and its work is producing suffering. Hatred is hostility toward suffering and the means of suffering; it obstructs non-hatred, makes body and mind burn, and is the basis on which bad actions are done.

That format is the most useful thing in this fascicle, and it is worth extracting as a tool. Every factor is defined by what it is, what it blocks, and what it produces. Not as a moral label but as a functional description — which means each one names a specific opposing factor that could be cultivated instead. This is a mind described the way an engineer describes a mechanism, and the twenty secondary afflictions that follow are the same treatment applied to the ordinary textures of a difficult day: envy, stinginess, deceit, flattery, restlessness, torpor, laziness, heedlessness, distraction.

The fourth category, the indeterminate factors — regret, sleep, coarse and subtle discursive thought — closes the list at fifty-one, and they are indeterminate in a specific sense: each can be wholesome or unwholesome depending on what it is doing. Regret over a cruelty is not the same factor-instance as regret over a missed opportunity to be cruel, though it is the same factor.

What Session 7 establishes
  • Only the six can be wholesome or unwholesome. All karma and all practice happen here, and nowhere else in the model.
  • Five factors are present in every experience — contact, attention, feeling, perception, volition. Nothing is experienced without all five.
  • Concentration and wisdom are particular factors, not defaults. They can be cultivated because they are not guaranteed.
  • Faith is clarity, not belief. Its essence is the mind settling; its third object is confidence that the thing can actually be done.
  • Every factor is defined functionally — what it is, what it blocks, what it produces — which always names the factor that could replace it.
Part 5

One practiceUsing the inventory as an instrument

Name the factor

A list of fifty-one is not meant to be memorised. It is meant to be used, and its use is specific: it makes you describe a mental state in functional terms rather than in terms of who you are.

Once a day for a week, catch yourself in an unpleasant state and refuse the usual description. Do not write "I was anxious" or "I'm a jealous person." Write three lines instead, in the treatise's own format.

What is it? — as plainly as you can, without a story. What does it block? — name the factor it is displacing; there is always one. What does it produce? — what actually followed from it, in behaviour, not in feeling.

Then one more line, which is the point of the exercise: which of the fifty-one would have to be present instead?

The reason this is worth doing is structural rather than therapeutic. An identity claim — I am an anxious person — has no opposing term and nothing to cultivate. A factor has both, by construction. That is not a self-help move; it falls out of how the treatise defines every item on the list.

One question to sit with

If every difficult state you have is a factor rather than a feature of you, what have you been treating as your character?

The treatise is not offering reassurance here — it is being technical, and the technicality happens to cut. A factor has an opposing factor and a set of conditions. A character does not. Whichever of the two you take yourself to have will determine what you think is worth attempting.
