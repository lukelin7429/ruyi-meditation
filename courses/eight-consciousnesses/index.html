The Eight Consciousnesses

八識 · 唯識 — Yogācāra as a psychology

Ten sessions straight through the Cheng Weishi Lun — the most detailed account of how experience is assembled that anyone produced before the twentieth century, and one that is almost entirely absent from English-language Buddhism.

10 sessions · all live Hard · an elective, and meant to be English, with the Chinese beside every quotation Free · the whole treatise is on this site

A mind taken apart, piece by piece

Most English writing about Buddhist psychology stops at mindfulness. The Cheng Weishi Lun does not begin there. It asks what a moment of experience is made of — and answers with eight consciousnesses, fifty-one mental factors, a storehouse holding the results of everything you have ever done, and a fourfold division inside every single act of knowing.

It was assembled in 659 CE by Xuanzang, who had studied at Nālandā and came back to China with ten Indian commentaries on Vasubandhu's Thirty Verses. Rather than choose one, he wove all ten into a single continuous argument. The result is the most rigorous philosophical treatment of mind in the Buddhist canon, and it reads less like scripture than like a court transcript: a position is stated, an opponent objects, the objection is answered, and the reader is expected to keep up.

The claim is not that you do not exist. It is that what you are is not the kind of thing you take it for.

This course takes a single route through all ten fascicles. It does not summarise the treatise — every session quotes it directly, in Ru-Yi's own translation, with the Chinese beside the English and a link back to the passage in place. What the course adds is a road: which arguments to read in which order, what each one is for, and where the treatise is strongest and where, honestly assessed, it is not.

The ten sessions

Session 1

The self that cannot be found

fascicle 1

Six accounts of what a self could be, and why the treatise rejects all six — including the whirling firebrand. Then the objection that matters: if there is no self, who remembers?
Session 2

And against everything else

fascicles 1–2

Creator gods, eternal sound, eternal atoms, four corners and no fifth — ending with a warning aimed at the treatise's own readers: Consciousness-Only can itself become an attachment.
Session 3

The storehouse

fascicles 2–3

The eighth consciousness — unknowable, morally blank, and never interrupted. The torrent that is neither permanent nor broken off, and the scale beam that explains why no gap ever opens.
Session 4

Seeds and perfuming

fascicle 2

What a seed actually is, whether seeds are innate or laid down by experience, and the four conditions something must meet before experience can mark it — including the one that means nobody can mark anyone but themselves.
Session 5

The four parts of a single knowing

fascicle 2

The treatise's most original contribution: seen, seeing, awareness of seeing, and confirmation of that awareness — with a real argument for why the series stops at four. And how billions of private minds share one world.
Session 6

The one that takes it for a self

fascicles 4–5

Manas — one object, one activity, four permanent afflictions, and no interruption for sleep. The part of the model with no Western counterpart, and the reason understanding the argument changes nothing.
Session 7

The six, and the fifty-one

fascicles 5–7

The consciousnesses you actually notice, and the full inventory of what accompanies them — five always present, eleven wholesome, twenty-six kinds of trouble. Including a definition of faith that is not belief.
Session 8

Is this idealism?

fascicle 7

What the word only negates and what it leaves standing; why other minds are genuinely real and permanently out of direct reach; and one argument that, assessed honestly, does not succeed.
Session 9

Three natures, five stages

fascicles 8–9

Three ways of being real, the single word that stops the system collapsing into nihilism, and a path whose longest stage comes after the moment of seeing.
Session 10

Transformation at the base

fascicle 10

The eight become four wisdoms — and nothing is replaced. The organ of self-division turns into the organ of compassion, and a ten-fascicle treatise ends by giving its merit away.

What the course reads

Before you start

This is an elective, and it is hard

No prior course is strictly required, but this is the deep end of the Academy. If you have not read a Buddhist text closely before, Read a Whole Sūtra is the place to begin, and Six Questions introduces several of the ideas this course assumes — its first two sessions use the opening fascicles of this same treatise, read comparatively against the Theravāda tradition rather than straight through.

The sessions are about thirty-five minutes each and are meant to be read slowly, one a week rather than one an evening. Each ends with a practice and five questions.

What this course leaves out

A great deal. The course quotes perhaps a fiftieth of the treatise. Left out entirely: the ten arguments from reason for the eighth consciousness, the four conditions and ten causes, the five fruits, the twelve links, the ten grounds with their obstacles and their kinds of suchness, and most of the detailed debates between the ten Indian masters that Xuanzang wove together.

The course is a route, not a replacement. Everything it skips is on this site, in full.

Where the treatise is argued with

This course does not present the Cheng Weishi Lun as settled. Session 8 states plainly that one of its arguments — the appeal to waking from a dream — converts all possible counter-evidence into confirmation, and so functions as a frame rather than a demonstration. Other schools disagreed with it at length, and the Madhyamaka elective, Nothing Stands, reads the tradition that pressed hardest on its central claim.
