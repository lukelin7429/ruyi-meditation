Session 2 · What replaces a credential no one can actually verify

What Actually Leads to Divinity's Company

三明經 — 沒人見過的目標，跟真的能到那裡的路
Part 1

Lecture Notes

An argument neither student can win

Two young brahmins, Vāseṭṭha and Bhāradvāja, are out walking, disputing whose teacher has correctly taught "the one true path" to union with Brahmā (rendered "Divinity" throughout this translation). Unable to settle it, they sensibly agree to bring the question to the ascetic Gotama nearby and accept his answer as final. Vāseṭṭha adds a clever defense first: perhaps, like many roads leading to one village, all the different paths brahmins teach work regardless of which teacher one follows.

Has anyone actually seen this?

Rather than arguing doctrine, the Buddha asks a plain factual question, repeatedly: has any brahmin learned in the three Vedas ever seen Brahmā with their own eyes? No. Their teachers? No. Their teachers' teachers, back seven generations? No. Even the ancient sages who composed the Vedic hymns those brahmins still chant today — did they claim to know where Brahmā is? No, not even them. The entire chain of transmission, traced all the way back to its source, rests on nobody's direct experience.

A cascade of similes

The point lands through images hard to forget: a queue of blind men, each holding the one in front, none of whom can see; a man professing love for "the finest lady in the land" without being able to say whether she is tall or short, or what town she is from; a builder constructing a ladder at a crossroads for a house whose location he cannot specify; and, run three separate times against three separate obstacles, a person standing on a riverbank calling "Come here, far shore!" — expecting the far bank to travel to them rather than building a way across.

What actually leads to Divinity's company

Asked at last whether he himself knows the path, the Buddha answers the way a local would give directions just outside their own home town — without hesitation. Not ritual, not lineage, not chanting the names of gods, but the graduated cultivation of the four divine abidings themselves: radiating love, then compassion, then empathetic joy, then equanimity, to one direction, a second, a third, a fourth, above, below, across, everywhere, abundant and free of enmity. Asked the same diagnostic questions put to the brahmins earlier — encumbered by possessions? full of enmity? corrupted? — the mendicant who has cultivated this way now answers to match Brahmā's own described qualities. Like, at last, genuinely converges with like.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • A claim with no one's direct experience behind it is worth naming as such. Traced back seven generations and further, to the Vedic sages themselves, no one in the chain has ever seen what the whole tradition claims to teach the way to.
  • The four divine abidings are offered as a demonstrable alternative to an unverifiable credential. Not a competing claim about Brahmā's location, but a cultivable state whose qualities can actually be checked against Brahmā's own described nature.
  • Like converges with like — a testable claim, not a mystical one. The mendicant who has cultivated the four abodes is asked the same diagnostic questions the brahmins couldn't pass, and now answers differently.

The Dīgha Nikāya · The Long Discourses

DN 13 — The Knowledge of the Three Vedas

Two young brahmins, still disputing whose teacher has correctly described the "one true path" to union with Brahmā, are shown — through blind men, an imaginary lover, an unbuilt ladder, and a river that won't come when called — that no one in their tradition has ever actually seen what they claim to teach the way to, before the Buddha describes what genuinely does lead there: the boundless cultivation of love, compassion, joy, and equanimity.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. The Buddha's method here is not to argue doctrine but to ask a simple factual question repeatedly ("has anyone actually seen this?"). Where else — in religion, in your own field, in ordinary claims to authority — have you seen a credential rest on a chain of transmission that, traced back far enough, rests on no one's direct experience?
  2. Vāseṭṭha's initial defense — many different paths, like many roads to one village — sounds reasonable on its face. Why doesn't the Buddha's argument actually refute that defense directly, and what does he offer instead of refuting it?
  3. This sutta was, by tradition, a challenge to the brahmanical claim of the day. Given your own commitment to engaged Buddhism, what does it mean to you that the Buddha's response to a caste-based religious authority claim is not "my god is bigger than yours" but "here is something anyone can actually verify by doing it"?
  4. "Like converges with like" — the mendicant who has cultivated the four abodes now answers the diagnostic questions the way Brahmā himself would. Is there a version of this same "like converges with like" test you could apply to your own claims about what your teaching or practice has actually done for you?

Option A — Find your own unverified chain

Identify one claim of authority you've encountered — religious, academic, professional — that, examined honestly, traces back to a chain of transmission resting on no one's demonstrated direct experience. Write half a page on how you noticed it, and what a demonstrable alternative might look like.

Option B — The diagnostic test, applied to yourself

The sutta asks: encumbered by possessions? full of enmity? corrupted? Answer these three questions honestly about yourself right now, in writing, the way Vāseṭṭha's teachers were asked to.
