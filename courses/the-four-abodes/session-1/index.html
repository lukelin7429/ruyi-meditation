Session 1 · The practice itself, before any doctrine about it

Character Before Technique

慈經 — 先立身，再擴散
Part 1

Lecture Notes

No story at all

Unlike almost every other sutta in this course, this one has no narrative frame — no questioner, no occasion, no dialogue. It is simply the verses themselves, spoken to no one in particular, meant to be memorized and carried rather than studied as a record of an event. That bareness is itself a clue to how this text is meant to be used: not analyzed once, but recited daily.

Character first, then a wish with no edges

Before the poem ever mentions loving-kindness, it lists what a person developing it should already be: capable, upright, easy to correct, gentle, humble, contented, unburdened, living simply, senses calm, prudent, courteous, not greedily attached to families. Only after this ethical and temperamental foundation is laid down does the actual wish appear — that all beings, without exception, be happy and safe, whether born or yet to be born, seen or unseen, living far or near, timid or bold. The famous image follows immediately: cherish all beings the way a mother would guard her only child, even at the cost of her own life.

Grounded in a concrete prohibition, not left as pure sentiment

The poem does not let the wish float free of ethics. It states the floor beneath it directly: let no one deceive another, or despise anyone anywhere, or wish harm on another out of anger or hostility. Boundless goodwill, in this text, is inseparable from a specific, checkable behavioral commitment — you can ask, concretely, whether you deceived or despised anyone today.

Every direction, every posture, without lapse

The radiating instruction that follows extends the wish above, below, across, and in every direction, without obstruction, hostility, or enmity — and then extends it again across every posture: standing, walking, sitting, or lying down, as long as one is not drowsy. The poem closes by naming this "a divine abiding, here and now" — not a technique reserved for formal sitting, but an orientation meant to run underneath the whole of ordinary life.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The practice opens with character, not technique. A dozen qualities — capable, gentle, contented, unburdened — are named before the famous wish ever appears.
  • The wish is grounded in a specific ethical floor. "Let no one deceive another, or despise anyone" is a checkable behavioral commitment, not a vague sentiment.
  • This is meant to run underneath ordinary life, not sit apart from it. Standing, walking, sitting, or lying down — the text explicitly refuses to confine the practice to formal meditation.

The Sutta Nipāta

Snp 1.8 — The Discourse on Love

Ground yourself first in a specific set of ethical and temperamental qualities, then radiate an unlimited wish for the safety and happiness of every living being — without exception or boundary — cherishing all beings the way a mother would protect her only child with her own life.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. The poem lists a dozen character qualities before ever mentioning loving-kindness itself. Why might character need to come first? What would the wish look like, cultivated by someone who skipped straight to it without this foundation?
  2. "Let no one deceive another, or despise anyone anywhere" grounds the wish in a specific, checkable commitment. Can you think of a moment recently when you technically wished someone well in the abstract while still, in some smaller way, despising or dismissing them?
  3. The mother-and-only-child image is one of the most famous similes in the whole canon. What does it capture about the required intensity of this practice that a gentler image (a good friend, a kind stranger) wouldn't?
  4. The poem insists this is practiced in every posture, not only while seated. What would it concretely look like for you to carry this wish into one ordinary, non-meditative part of your day — teaching, commuting, a difficult conversation?
Part 4

AssignmentChoose one · half to one page · bring it next session

Option A — Audit the ethical floor

For one full day, track every moment you notice yourself despising, dismissing, or wishing even mild harm on someone — a driver, a student, a public figure. Write half a page on what you noticed, using the poem's own standard rather than a vaguer one.

Option B — Character before technique, applied to teaching

Name one quality from the poem's opening list (contented, unburdened, courteous, and so on) that you think you most need to develop as a teacher before any specific classroom technique would actually work better. Explain why, concretely.
