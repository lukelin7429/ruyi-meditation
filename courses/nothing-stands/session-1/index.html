Session 1 · The homage

Four doors, eight handles

Nagarjuna states his entire position before arguing for any of it
Reading time about thirty-five minutes Before class nothing at all The text Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, chapter 1
Part 1

Who this was written forNot the people you would expect

The Middle Treatise has a reputation as the most destructive book in Buddhist philosophy — twenty-seven chapters taking apart motion, time, causation, the self, karma, the Buddha, and nirvāṇa, leaving nothing standing. The reputation is not wrong. But it gives a misleading impression of the target.

The commentary that comes down with the text — by Piṅgala, translated into Chinese with the verses by Kumārajīva in 409 CE, and translated on this site along with them — opens by naming the rival theories of the day. Things come from Maheśvara, or Viṣṇu, or a combination of elements, or Time, or primordial nature, or transformation, or spontaneity, or atoms. A crowded room, everyone with a theory of where things come from.

And then the passage turns, and this is the news:

The commentary 釋

"After the Buddha's final passing, in the five hundred years of the semblance Dharma, people's faculties grew duller and they clung deeply to things, hunting for fixed characteristics in the twelve links, the five aggregates, the twelve sense-fields, the eighteen elements. Not knowing the Buddha's intention, they held only to his words. And when they heard the Great Vehicle's teaching of ultimate emptiness, they did not know for what reason things are empty, and doubt arose in them:"

"Seizing on this mark of emptiness, they gave rise to craving and clinging, and produced every sort of fault inside ultimate emptiness itself. It was for people such as these that the bodhisattva Nāgārjuna composed this Middle Treatise."

取是空相而起貪著，於畢竟空中生種種過。龍樹菩薩為是等故，造此中論。

The treatise was written for Buddhists. Specifically, for two kinds: those who took the Buddha's analytical lists and went hunting inside them for fixed characteristics — real, findable units where the analysis finally bottoms out — and those who, hearing that all of it is empty and not knowing why, concluded that emptiness cancels morality.

And then the sentence that should stop anyone who has ever enjoyed the word: seizing on this mark of emptiness, they gave rise to craving and clinging. Emptiness had become one more thing to have. The Middle Treatise exists because a medicine had been turned into a disease.

Every reader of this book is, in the relevant sense, one of the people it was written against.
Part 2

The homageThe whole position, before a single argument

Indian treatises open with homage to the Buddha. Nāgārjuna uses the convention to state his entire thesis — and notice what he praises the Buddha for. Not a miracle, not a lineage, not a heaven.

Mūlamadhyamakakārikā · root verses 1–2 · 八不

"Neither arising nor ceasing,

neither permanent nor annihilated,

neither identical nor different,

neither coming nor going out —

whoever can teach this, dependent arising,

the good quieting of all elaboration:

to him, the Buddha, I bow my head,

first among all who have taught."

不生亦不滅， 不常亦不斷，

不一亦不異， 不來亦不出。

能說是因緣， 善滅諸戲論，

我稽首禮佛， 諸說中第一。

Read this passage in its place — chapter 1, "The Homage: Eight Negations" →

Eight negations, 八不. The most famous line in Madhyamaka, and the whole treatise is in some sense their unpacking.

The Buddha is praised for two things, and the second is the one to hold onto. He taught dependent arising — and he accomplished the good quieting of all elaboration. 戲論, Sanskrit prapañca: the mind's habit of spinning out ever finer categories and then mistaking the categories for the world. This is a self, that is other, this is mine, that is a thing with a fixed nature that will still be that thing tomorrow.

Nāgārjuna praises the Buddha not for having explained the universe but for having stopped something. For putting the machinery down. That tells you what the twenty-seven chapters are for: not a better theory, but the end of theorising.

Part 3

Why eight, and why in pairsThe architecture of the whole treatise, explained in one exchange

The commentary immediately asks the obvious question, and the answer is more interesting than it looks.

Objection 問曰

"Things are beyond counting. Why break them all with just these eight?"

問曰：諸法無量，何故但以此八事破？
Answer 答曰

"Though things are beyond counting, to state these eight in brief is to break all things at once."

"The mark of arising cannot in the end be found: therefore, not arising. And "not ceasing": if there is no arising, how could there be any ceasing? And because there is no arising and no ceasing, the remaining six do not stand either."

答曰：法雖無量，略說八事則為總破一切法。
生相決定不可得，故不生。不滅者，若無生何得有滅？以無生、無滅故，餘六事亦無。

So the first negation does all the logical work, and the other seven follow from it. Which raises a sharper question, and the treatise asks that too — why bother with six more?

Objection 問曰

""Not arising, not ceasing" has already broken all things at once. Why state six more?"

問曰：不生、不滅已總破一切法。何故復說六事？
Answer 答曰

"In order to establish the meaning of not-arising and not-ceasing. There are people who will not accept "not arising, not ceasing," but who do believe "not permanent, not annihilated" — and if one searches deeply into not-permanent and not-annihilated, that is not-arising and not-ceasing."

"And there are people who, though they have heard these four break all things, still try to build things back up through identity and difference."

答曰：為成不生不滅義故。有人不受不生不滅，而信不常不斷。若深求不常不斷，即是不生不滅。
有人雖聞四種破諸法，猶以四門成諸法。

This is a piece of pedagogical honesty that explains the architecture of the entire book. Logically, one negation would have sufficed. The other seven exist because people do not accept the first one. So the argument is restated in a second vocabulary for readers who balk at the first, and then a third, and then a fourth.

Watch the escalation, because it is a map of your own mind reading this. Denied arising and ceasing, the mind takes refuge in duration — fine, but surely things last, or surely they stop. Denied permanence and annihilation, it takes refuge in relation — fine, but surely the seed and the sprout are either the same thing or two things. Denied identity and difference, it takes refuge in transit — fine, but surely it came from somewhere.

Arising / ceasing
不生亦不滅
The door on existence itself. Everything else follows from this one.
Permanent / annihilated
不常亦不斷
The door on duration, for those who balked at the first.
Identical / different
不一亦不異
The door on relation, for those who rebuilt through sameness.
Coming / going out
不來亦不出
The door on transit — where the eight origin-stories walk back in wearing the word from.

Four doors, and each has two handles. That is why the negations come in pairs: a reader told that things do not truly arise will reach at once for the opposite — then nothing is there, and nothing happens. The verse blocks that reach in the same breath. There is no shore on either side, and the lack of a shore is the point.

So the Middle Treatise is twenty-seven chapters long not because the argument needs twenty-seven chapters, but because grasping has that many hiding places. The repetitiveness readers complain about is the method.

Part 4

What is being denied, and what is notThe single most common misreading, blocked at the outset

It is worth being exact about the target now, before eight sessions of demolition make it easy to lose.

Nāgārjuna is not saying nothing exists. He is saying nothing exists in the way you take it to — with 自性, a nature of its own, standing on its own, independent of conditions. The technical term the treatise breaks is fixed characteristic, 決定相: the property that would make a thing be what it is regardless of anything else.

And the argument is always the same in shape, whatever the topic. If a thing had a nature of its own, it would not need conditions. But everything that appears does depend on conditions. So nothing that appears has a nature of its own — and if it does not have a nature of its own, then the thing you thought you were pointing at, the self-standing item, was never there to be found.

Notice that this leaves the appearance entirely alone. The sprout grows. The chapter on motion will not deny that people walk to the market. What it will deny is that there is a findable act of going, with its own nature, that the walking consists in.

Readers coming from the Yogācāra elective will recognise the shape of this — that school also targets independence rather than existence. The two schools will part company over what survives the analysis, and Session 6 is where the disagreement becomes explicit. For now it is enough to see that they share a move.

What Session 1 establishes
  • The treatise was written for Buddhists — for people hunting fixed characteristics in the Buddha's own lists, and for people who made emptiness into a thing to have.
  • The homage states the whole thesis before any argument is made.
  • The Buddha is praised for stopping something, not for explaining anything — the quieting of elaboration.
  • One negation does the logical work. The other seven exist because people will not accept the first.
  • They come in pairs because every denial invites its opposite, and both doors are shut in the same breath.
  • The target is own-nature, not existence. Nothing is being denied except independence.
Part 5

One practiceFinding your own fixed characteristic

Where the analysis bottoms out

The people the treatise was written for were not fools. They were doing something reasonable — analysing experience into parts, and assuming the analysis had to stop somewhere real. Almost everyone does this, usually without noticing where they have stopped.

Take fifteen minutes. Pick anything in front of you — an object, a feeling, a relationship. Take it apart: what is it made of, what does it depend on, what would have to be different for it not to be there?

Keep going until you hit something that feels like it will not come apart — something that is just what it is. Everyone hits one. It might be matter, or awareness, or a particular person's character, or the bare fact of existing.

Then ask one question about it: does it depend on anything? Not "is it made of parts" — does it require any condition at all in order to be what it is? If it does, it is not the floor. If it genuinely does not, ask how you came to know about it.

The exercise is not designed to produce an answer. It is designed to locate the place where you stop asking, because that place is what the next seven sessions are about.

One question to sit with

Where did your analysis stop — and what made you think that was the floor rather than the place you got tired?

The treatise's wager is that there is no floor, and that the search for one is itself the elaboration the Buddha was praised for quieting. But the wager is only interesting if you have actually located your own stopping place, which is why this session's practice comes before any of the arguments.
