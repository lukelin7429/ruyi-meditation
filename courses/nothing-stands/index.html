Nothing Stands

中觀 — Madhyamaka, and the dismantling of every position

Nāgārjuna is not defending a view. He is taking apart every view anyone has held, including his own school's — and the Middle Treatise was written, as its commentary says plainly, for Buddhists who had turned emptiness into one more thing to have.

8 sessions · all live Hard · an elective, and meant to be English, with the Chinese beside every quotation Free · all 27 chapters on this site

A book with a reputation, and a target nobody expects

The Mūlamadhyamakakārikā — the Middle Treatise, 中論 — is the most destructive book in Buddhist philosophy. Twenty-seven chapters take apart motion, time, causation, the senses, the aggregates, the self, karma, the Buddha, and nirvāṇa, leaving nothing whatsoever standing.

What the reputation gets wrong is the target. The commentary that comes down with the text opens by listing the rival theories of the day — Maheśvara, Viṣṇu, atoms, Time, primordial nature — and then turns, and says the treatise was written for Buddhists: for those hunting fixed characteristics inside the Buddha's own analytical lists, and for those who, hearing that all of it is empty, concluded that emptiness cancels morality and then, in the commentary's words, seized on it and gave rise to craving and clinging.

The Middle Treatise exists because a medicine had been turned into a disease.

This course reads seven chapters closely and ends with the school's own account of its method. Every quotation is from this site's translation of Kumārajīva's 409 CE Chinese, with the original beside it and a link back to the chapter in place. The dialogue form is preserved throughout — in this treatise the opponent gets real lines, in the treatise's own verses, and states his case better than he would state it himself.

The eight sessions

Session 1

Four doors, eight handles

chapter 1

The eight negations, why they come in pairs, and why one would logically have sufficed — the other seven exist because people will not accept the first.
Session 2

Nothing arises, in four ways

chapter 1

Not from itself, not from another, not from both, not from nothing — and then the Abhidharma's four conditions, stated accurately in the treatise's own verses before being taken apart.
Session 3

The walker and the walked

chapter 2

Somebody is walking to the market, and Nāgārjuna asks where the going is. The most famous demolition in the book — and the target turns out to be the goer, not the going.
Session 4

Fire and fuel

chapter 10

The self and the aggregates, and the sharpest objection in the treatise: you cannot refute what is not there. The answer is that naming is not endorsing — and if it were, the word "refute" would refute itself.
Session 5

If nothing has a nature, who acts?

chapters 8 and 17

The moral objection at full strength, and the inversion that answers it: loss requires a possession, so it was inherent existence, not emptiness, that made karma unworkable. Ends with the conjured person.
Session 6

Your emptiness destroys everything

chapter 24

The most important chapter in the treatise. The two truths, the warning about the snake caught in the middle rather than behind the head, and the verse that turns the whole accusation around.
Session 7

Nirvāṇa is not somewhere else

chapter 25

Not obtained, not arrived at, not cut off — and not the slightest difference from where you are. With the caution that matters more than anything else in the course.
Session 8

How the school describes itself

Jizang, Sanlun Xuanyi

Refuting the false is revealing the true, with no third step. And the line the whole course has been travelling toward: once the sickness of existence dissolves, the medicine of emptiness is set aside too.

What the course reads

The spine is the Middle Treatise; the school is called the Three Treatise school, and the other two are here as well.

Mūlamadhyamakakārikā
中論 · 二十七品
Nāgārjuna's verses with Piṅgala's commentary, in Kumārajīva's Chinese and Ru-Yi's English. All twenty-seven chapters complete. Twelve Gate Treatise
十二門論
Nāgārjuna's own compressed restatement in twelve arguments — the fastest way into the method. Hundred Treatise
百論
By Āryadeva, Nāgārjuna's student, and aimed outward at the non-Buddhist schools rather than inward. Sanlun Xuanyi
三論玄義 · 吉藏
Jizang's account of the school's own method, written at imperial command around 600 CE. Complete in fourteen parts.

Before you start

This is an elective, and it is hard

No prior course is required, but this is the deep end of the Academy. If you have not read a Buddhist text closely before, Read a Whole Sūtra is the place to begin. Collected Topics is useful preparation of a different kind — it teaches the logic of negation that this treatise uses on every page.

The sessions run about thirty-five minutes and are meant to be read slowly, one a week. Each ends with a practice and five questions.

What this course leaves out

Twenty of the twenty-seven chapters. The course reads chapters 1, 2, 8, 10, 17, 24 and 25 closely and refers to others in passing; left out entirely are the chapters on the senses, the aggregates, the elements, defilement, the three marks, the prior self, the beginning, suffering, formations, combination, being and non-being, bondage and release, time, cause and fruit, coming-to-be and dissolution, the Tathāgata, the inversions, the twelve links, and wrong views.

All of them are on this site, in full. The course is a route, not a replacement.

A warning the treatise itself gives

Chapter 24 compares emptiness to a venomous snake caught for a living: the danger is in the grip, not the snake. Held wrongly, this teaching can be used to dissolve every restraint a person has while feeling like wisdom the entire time — and the treatise says this danger is why the Buddha hesitated to teach at all.

Session 6 takes that warning seriously and its practice is built around it. Anyone who finds that emptiness has been making their obligations lighter is reading it the wrong way round, and the course says so directly rather than leaving it to be inferred.
