Session 7 · Chapter 25

Nirvāṇa is not somewhere else

Not obtained, not arrived at — and not the slightest difference from here
Reading time about thirty-five minutes Before class Sessions 1–6 The text Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, chapter 25
Part 1

The same trade, in two versesThe opponent stands in the hole he is pointing at

Chapter 24 took forty verses. Chapter 25 makes the same exchange in two, and the speed is the point — by now the reader should be able to see it coming.

Mūlamadhyamakakārikā · chapter 25 · the objection

"If all things are empty,

with neither arising nor ceasing,

what is cut off, what extinguished,

that one should call it nirvāṇa?"

若一切法空， 無生無滅者，

何斷何所滅， 而稱為涅槃？

Read this passage in its place — chapter 25, "Examining Nirvāṇa" →

The reply changes a single word. If things were not empty, nothing would arise or cease; and with nothing arising or ceasing, nothing could be extinguished, and nirvāṇa would be impossible. The objector thought he was standing on solid ground pointing at a hole. He was standing in it.

Then the definition, and every clause of it removes something the reader wanted:

Mūlamadhyamakakārikā · chapter 25 · what nirvāṇa is

"Not obtained and not arrived at,

not cut off and not permanent,

not arising and not ceasing:

this is what is called nirvāṇa."

無得亦無至， 不斷亦不常，

不生亦不滅， 是說名涅槃。

Read this passage in its place — chapter 25, "Examining Nirvāṇa" →
The commentary 釋

""Not obtained": there is nothing obtained, whether in the practice or in the fruit. "Not arrived at": there is no place to arrive at. "Not cut off": because the five aggregates have from the beginning been ultimately empty, so that when one attains the path and enters nirvāṇa without remainder there is nothing cut off either."

無得者，於行於果無所得。無至者，無處可至。不斷者，五陰先來畢竟空故，得道入無餘涅槃時亦無所斷。

The first two clauses dismantle the picture nearly everyone carries: nirvāṇa as a destination, a state entered, something one comes to have. Nothing is obtained, in the practising or in the fruit; and there is nowhere to go.

The third is the strangest and the most important. Not even cut off — because the aggregates were empty from the beginning, so that even at final nirvāṇa nothing is actually severed. What would you cut? The chain was never a chain.

Part 2

The absence of an activityThe chapter's one positive statement

After refuting nirvāṇa as an existent thing and as a non-existent one, the chapter makes its single affirmative claim — and it is easy to read past.

Mūlamadhyamakakārikā · chapter 25 · grasping and not grasping

"Because one grasps at causes and conditions

one turns in the round of birth and death;

not grasping at causes and conditions —

this is what is called nirvāṇa."

受諸因緣故， 輪轉生死中；

不受諸因緣， 是名為涅槃。

Read this passage in its place — chapter 25, "Examining Nirvāṇa" →

Notice what is not said. Not: the conditions stop. Not: the world goes away. Not: one departs for elsewhere. The same conditions, the same aggregates, the same street and weather and body — with one thing subtracted, the appropriating that turns all of it into a self and its possessions.

The commentary's term for the ordinary condition is exact: 五受陰, the five grasped aggregates. The grasping is what makes them a life that somebody is having.

Nirvāṇa is not an object with unusual properties. It is the absence of an activity. Nothing new appears; something long-running stops.

That is also why it can be neither an existent thing nor the destruction of one. If it existed as a thing it would be conditioned, and everything conditioned ages and ends — a nirvāṇa that ages is not nirvāṇa. And it cannot be sheer non-existence either, for a reason worth keeping: "non-existent" only ever means what was here and is now gone. Non-existence is a report of a loss, and nirvāṇa is not the loss of anything, because nothing was ever there in the fixed way that would make its absence a loss.

Part 3

Not the slightest differenceThe sentence that stops every first-time reader

Mūlamadhyamakakārikā · chapter 25 · saṃsāra and nirvāṇa

"Between nirvāṇa and the world

there is not the slightest difference;

between the world and nirvāṇa

there is not the slightest difference either."

涅槃與世間， 無有少分別；

世間與涅槃， 亦無少分別。

Read this passage in its place — chapter 25, "Examining Nirvāṇa" →

Said twice, with the terms reversed so that nobody can take it as a one-way claim — and the next verse says it a third time, with a unit of measurement: not a hair's breadth.

The commentary 釋

"Because the five aggregates continue in succession, coming and going by way of causes and conditions, this is called the world. The nature of the five aggregates is ultimately empty, free of grasping and quiescent — this meaning has already been stated. And since all things neither arise nor cease, there is no difference between the world and nirvāṇa, and no difference between nirvāṇa and the world."

五陰相續往來因緣故，說名世間。五陰性畢竟空無受寂滅，此義先已說。以一切法不生不滅故，世間與涅槃無有分別，涅槃與世間亦無分別。

The argument is short and has been earned across twenty-five chapters. The world is not a location; it is the aggregates continuing in succession, grasped at. Nirvāṇa is not a location either — the definition already said there is no place to arrive at. And when either is searched for as a thing with a nature of its own, neither can be found. Two things can differ only if there are two things.

The caution that matters more than anything else here

This verse does not say that suffering is unreal, or that it makes no difference whether a person is in anguish or at peace, or that a bombed city and a quiet room are the same. Nothing in the treatise licenses that, and Chapter 24 has just spent forty verses establishing the opposite: emptiness is exactly what makes suffering real enough to arise and dependent enough to end.

To hear this verse as so it doesn't matter is to make precisely the mistake the previous chapter was written to prevent — taking the ultimate as a replacement for the conventional rather than as its fine print. On the conventional level, which is where every being actually lives and every act lands, the difference between torment and freedom is the most important difference there is.

What the verse denies is a metaphysical gap: that saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are two realms, one to be escaped and one to be reached, made of different stuff, with a border between them. There is no such border — and the practical consequence is enormous. Liberation is not migration. There is no elsewhere to get to, no better world that this one is a waiting room for, and nothing about your circumstances that has to change first.

This is the single verse from which the whole East Asian conviction descends: that awakening is found here, in this life, in ordinary conditions, or nowhere. Chan's insistence on the everyday, and Pure Land's insistence that this defiled world is not other than the pure one, are both in the end commentaries on these twenty characters.

Part 4

"And the Buddha taught nothing at all"How twenty-seven chapters end

The treatise closes by naming the questions the Buddha had famously refused to answer — whether the world is eternal, whether it has an edge, whether the Tathāgata exists after death. And it does something nobody had done before: it explains why the silence was right. Not because the questions are unprofitable. Because they are malformed. Each asks for a property of something with a fixed nature, and there is no such something. "Does it have an edge?" presupposes an it.

Mūlamadhyamakakārikā · the final verse

"Things cannot be found;

all conceptual elaboration is quieted;

there is no person and no place —

and the Buddha taught nothing at all."

諸法不可得， 滅一切戲論，

無人亦無處， 佛亦無所說。

Read this passage in its place — chapter 25, "Examining Nirvāṇa" →

佛亦無所說 — and the Buddha taught nothing at all. After twenty-five chapters of extremely determined argument this can look like paradox or showmanship. It is neither, and the commentary's gloss is deflationary and precise: at no time and in no place did the Tathāgata teach anyone a fixed character of nirvāṇa. Nothing was ever handed over as a thing to hold. The teaching was always a set of removals, and when the removals are complete there is no residue to point at and say that is what he gave me.

And notice what the commentary says the outcome is, because it is neither a blank nor despair:

The commentary 釋

"Everything that might be obtained comes to rest; all conceptual elaboration is extinguished. And because elaboration is extinguished, one penetrates the real character of things and attains the path of peace."

諸有所得皆息、戲論皆滅。戲論滅故，通達諸法實相得安隱道。

安隱 is a quiet, domestic word: security, ease, not being under threat. The end of the treatise is not vertigo. It is the relief of putting down something that had been carried a very long way for no reason, and finding that the world is still there, unchanged, and no longer requires defending.

The commentary then offers a summary that works as the treatise's own table of contents read backwards: from the chapter on Conditions onward, things have been distinguished and pressed with questions: the existent is not, the non-existent is not, both existent and non-existent is not, neither existent nor non-existent is not. Four corners, chapter after chapter — on arising, motion, the senses, the aggregates, fire and fuel, the self, time, karma, the Tathāgata, the truths, and now nirvāṇa. And what that relentlessness arrives at is given four names in one breath: the real character of things, suchness, the nature of things, the true limit. And then a fifth — nirvāṇa. Not a place at the end of the road. The road, seen without anything added to it.

What Session 7 establishes
  • Not obtained, not arrived at, not cut off. No acquisition, no destination, and nothing severed — the chain was never a chain.
  • Nirvāṇa is the absence of an activity, not the presence of a state. Nothing new appears; grasping stops.
  • Non-existence is a report of a loss, so nirvāṇa cannot be one — nothing was ever there in the fixed way that would make its absence a loss.
  • Not the slightest difference — because neither is a location, and two things can differ only if there are two things.
  • This is not "so it doesn't matter." Conventionally, the difference between torment and freedom is the most important there is. What is denied is a border between realms.
  • Nothing was ever handed over. The teaching was a set of removals, and what it leaves is ease.
Part 5

One practiceSubtracting the one thing

The five grasped aggregates

Verse 9 makes a claim that can be examined in small: that the difference between the round of birth and death and its cessation is not in the conditions but in the grasping at them.

Sit for twenty minutes, in an ordinary state — not calm, not prepared. Let whatever is going on go on.

Now attend to one thing only: the appropriating. Not the sensation, but the small movement that makes it my discomfort. Not the thought, but the claiming that makes it my thought, something I am responsible for or can take credit for. It is fast and almost invisible, and it is happening constantly.

When you catch one, do not try to stop it. Just notice that the sensation or thought was already fully present before it, and would have been there without it.

What you are looking for is a gap between the arising and the claiming. Most people find, at best, brief ones. That is a reasonable outcome — and note that even a brief one demonstrates the chapter's point: the condition and the grasping are separable, which is the whole of what the verse claims.

One question to sit with

If there is nowhere else to get to, what have you been doing all this time?

The treatise's answer is: carrying something. Not travelling, and not failing to travel. The road was never a road to somewhere, and what changes at the end is not your location but that you are no longer defending a world that never needed it.
