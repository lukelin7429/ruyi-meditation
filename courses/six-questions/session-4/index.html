Session 4 · 苦 · Why does it hurt

Why do I suffer? · The shared diagnosis

Two vocabularies, and the same finding underneath both
Reading time about thirty minutes Before class Sessions 1–3 The texts Visuddhimagga 14 (closing) and Yogācārabhūmi 8
Part 1

The question

Why do I suffer?

Of the six questions in this course, this is the one where the two traditions begin from identical ground, and it is worth knowing that before you start — because it makes this the best session for finding out what "the same starting point" actually looks like once two thousand years of separate development have been applied to it.

Neither side is being original here. Both are unpacking the Buddha's first teaching, which nobody disputes: there is suffering, it has a cause, the cause can be removed, and there is a way to do it. The interesting thing is that unpacking the same four sentences produced two bodies of analysis that look nothing alike — and arrive at the same finding in different words.

Neither tradition locates suffering in what happened to you. Both locate it in a relationship you are having with what happened.

Part 3

The Northern answer
Affliction, taken completely apart

The Yogācārabhūmi devotes an entire fascicle to affliction alone, and announces at the outset that it will treat it under nine headings — nature, classification, cause, stage, gateway, superior grade, distortion, differences, faults. Read at least the opening definition and then sections 13 and 14, on the six causes and the seven stages. The rest is there if you want it, and it is formidable.

Part 4

Where the road parts — and where it does not

1 · The whole chapter's purpose, stated once, near the end

Visuddhimagga 14 · why the aggregates are taught in this order

"For the Blessed One, wishing to free those to be guided who had fallen into a grasping of self within the undivided five aggregates, by showing the analysis of that seemingly solid mass — being one who desires their benefit, and for the sake of each such person's easy grasp — first taught the aggregate of form, coarse and forming the domain of the eye and the other faculties. Then feeling, which experiences form as desirable or undesirable… Then formations, which construct by way of perception. And then their support and governing master, consciousness."

Abhedena hi pañcasu khandhesu attagāhapatitaṃ veneyyajanaṃ samūhaghanavinibbhogadassanena attagāhato mocetukāmo bhagavā hitakāmo tassa tassa janassa sukhagahaṇatthaṃ cakkhuādīnampi visayabhūtaṃ oḷārikaṃ paṭhamaṃ rūpakkhandhaṃ desesi.
See this passage in the full chapter →

Three chapters of counting, and here — almost in passing — is what all of it was for. Not scholarship. People arrive with a "seemingly solid mass" and take themselves to be it. The inventory is a crowbar. And the order is pedagogical, chosen for ease of grasp: start with the coarse thing anyone can point at, and work inward.

2 · The difference is one qualifier, and it is not in the contents

Visuddhimagga 14 · aggregates, and aggregates subject to clinging

"'By distinction': by the distinction between the aggregates and the aggregates subject to clinging. What, then, is their distinction? The aggregates, firstly, are stated without qualification. The aggregates subject to clinging are distinguished by the state of being with taints and clingable."

Visesatoti khandhānañca upādānakkhandhānañca visesato. Ko pana nesaṃ viseso, khandhā tāva avisesato vuttā. Upādānakkhandhā sāsavaupādāniyabhāvena visesetvā.
See this passage in the full chapter →

This is the sharpest sentence the Southern tradition offers on this question, and almost nobody quotes it.

The five aggregates subject to clinging — pañcupādānakkhandhā — are what the first noble truth actually names as suffering. Not "life is suffering." Not "birth, ageing and death." Those are examples. The definition is these five, with clinging.

And the difference between the two lists is not a difference of contents. Same form, same feeling, same perception, same formations, same consciousness. What distinguishes them is a qualifier: sāsava, upādāniya — with taints, clingable. The suffering is not in the five things. It is in the "with."

Which is why Session 1's chapter ends where it does: with the aggregates compared to a sickroom, a sickness, the origin of a sickness, the pursuit of unsuitable things, and the sick person himself. Five items, one illness, and no villain anywhere in the list.

3 · The North says the same thing in one sentence, and then takes it apart

瑜伽師地論 8 · affliction's own nature

"As for affliction's own nature: whenever such a phenomenon arises, its characteristic is to arise, of its own accord, as a state of restlessness — and because it has thus arisen, a continuous current of restless activity carries on from it. This, stated briefly, is affliction's own nature."

煩惱自性者，謂若法生時，其相自然不寂靜起；由彼起故，不寂靜行相續而轉，是名略說煩惱自性。
See this passage in the full fascicle →

不寂靜 — not-quiet, unsettled, restless. Affliction is not defined by its object, its moral content, or how unpleasant it feels. It is defined as a disturbance that arises by itself and then keeps going. Note the second clause, which does the real work: the restlessness does not end when the moment that produced it ends. It sets a current running.

Put that beside the Southern sentence and the agreement is exact, in two completely unrelated vocabularies. With taints, clingable. Restlessness, propagating. Neither says the problem is the object. Both say it is something happening on your side of the encounter, which then continues under its own power.

And then the treatise does what the Northern tradition always does — it asks where the thing comes from, and answers with a list you can actually use:

瑜伽師地論 8 · the six causes of affliction

"Through its basis: affliction arises from its own latent seed. Through its object: an object capable of provoking affliction actually presents itself. Through association: one follows and learns from an unwholesome person. Through false teaching: one hears a teaching that is not the true Dharma. Through habituation: the accumulated force of past repetition has already been planted. Through attention: unsound reasoning attention is what actually gives rise to every affliction."

所依故者，謂由隨眠起諸煩惱。所緣故者，謂順煩惱境界現前。親近故者，謂由隨學不善丈夫。邪教故者，謂由聞非正法。數習故者，謂由先植數習力勢。作意故者，謂由發起不如理作意故，諸煩惱生。
See this passage in the full fascicle →
Read the six as a map of what is and is not available to you
  • The latent seed is not available. It was planted before you arrived and the text elsewhere says it "continues to trail along uninterrupted" until permanently severed.
  • The object is not available. It "actually presents itself" — things turn up.
  • Habituation is not available today. "The accumulated force of past repetition has already been planted."
  • Association and false teaching are semi-available, on a scale of months and years — who you are around, and what you let instruct you.
  • Attention is available right now. And notice how the text phrases this one differently from all the others: unsound attention "is what actually gives rise to every affliction."

Five of the six are conditions. One is an operation, in the present tense, and the treatise singles it out as the one that actually does the producing. That is not a softer claim than the Southern one — it is the same claim, expressed as an intervention point rather than as a definition.

Worth noting where the two traditions' vocabularies touch directly: the treatise's seven stages of affliction begin with 隨眠, the latent seed, and include both 分別起 (deliberately reasoned) and 俱生 (innate) arising — the same pair you met in Session 1, now applied not to self-attachment alone but to affliction generally.

Part 5

One practiceThis week · one affliction, six columns

Audit one recurring affliction against the six causes

Choose something that comes back. Not a mood — a specific, repeating disturbance. The irritation with a particular colleague. The anxiety that arrives on Sunday evening. The thing about your brother.

Write the six headings and fill them in honestly. Basis — has this been with you a long time? Object — what actually shows up to trigger it? Association — who around you makes it easier to have? Teaching — what account of the world are you holding that requires this reaction to make sense? Habituation — how many times have you done this? Attention — what are you doing with it in the first few seconds?

Then the useful question, and it is uncomfortable. Which of the six have you been trying to fix? Most people discover they have spent years working on the object — changing the job, avoiding the person, rearranging circumstances — which is the column the treatise lists as simply presenting itself.

You are not being asked to do anything about attention this week. Only to notice that of the six, it is the one column with anything in it that is happening now.
Part 6

Five questionsClick an answer for immediate feedback · nothing is recorded

Question 1 of 5
Near the end of Chapter 14 Buddhaghosa states, almost in passing, what the whole inventory was for. What was it?
Correct: C. Not scholarship. People arrive taking themselves to be a solid lump; the inventory is a crowbar. And note that even the sequence is pedagogical — form first, because it is coarse and anyone can point at it, then inward.
Question 2 of 5
What distinguishes "the aggregates" from "the aggregates subject to clinging"?
Correct: B. This is the sharpest sentence the Southern tradition offers here, and it is rarely quoted. The first noble truth names the five clinging-aggregates as suffering — not life, not ageing, not death, which are examples. Same form, same feeling, same consciousness. The suffering is not in the five things; it is in the "with."
Question 3 of 5
How does the Yogācārabhūmi define affliction's own nature?
Correct: A. 不寂靜 — not-quiet. Affliction is defined neither by its object nor by its moral content nor by how unpleasant it feels. And the second clause does the real work: the restlessness does not stop when the moment that produced it stops. It sets a current running.
Question 4 of 5
Put the two definitions side by side — "with taints, clingable" and "restlessness, propagating." What do they share?
Correct: D. Two completely unrelated vocabularies, five hundred years apart, agreeing exactly. This is what "the same starting point" looks like after two millennia of separate development: not identical sentences, but the same finding, arrived at by different routes and expressed in terms neither side would recognise as the other's.
Question 5 of 5
Of the six causes of affliction — basis, object, association, false teaching, habituation, attention — what is distinctive about the last?
Correct: C. The seed was planted before you arrived; the object presents itself; habituation has already been laid down. Association and false teaching move on a scale of years. Attention is now — and the treatise phrases that one differently from all the others. It is the same claim the South makes about the "with," expressed as an intervention point rather than a definition.

Part 7

One question, left open

Sit with this

Which of the six have you been trying to fix?

Nearly everyone has been working on the object, for years, at considerable expense — the move, the different job, the conversation that would settle it. That effort is not stupid and the circumstances are often genuinely bad. But both traditions, from opposite ends of Asia and in vocabularies with nothing in common, looked at the same problem and declined to put it there. That is either a very old mistake made twice, or it is the finding. This week, at least, notice which column you have been in.
