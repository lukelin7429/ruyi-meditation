Session 7 · 行 · What counts as living well

How should I live? · The precept

Virtue in seven questions, against filial devotion as the precept itself
Reading time about thirty minutes Before class Sessions 1–6 The texts Visuddhimagga 1, and Brahmā Net Sūtra 7
Part 1

The question

What counts as living well?

This is where the two traditions become most obviously different, and the difference is not about strictness. Both sides are severe. It is about where the door is.

The Visuddhimagga opens its entire treatise — before a word about meditation, before any of the chapters you have read in this course — with virtue, and spends a very long chapter on it, organised around seven questions. It is technical, it is exact, and it is almost entirely about the life of a monk.

The Brahmā Net Sūtra opens its bodhisattva precepts in the first minute of the Buddha's awakening, and the first thing it says is not a rule. It is a relationship. And then it names who may receive these precepts — a list that includes servants, the sexually disreputable, spirits, and an animal.

One tradition asks: what is virtue, exactly? The other asks: who is allowed in?

The first thing said after awakening

The Brahmā Net Sūtra is the classical source of the ten major and forty-eight minor bodhisattva precepts, and the passage you want is the one that frames them — the moment the precepts are opened, and the naming of who may receive them.

Read it →

One honest note, in keeping with how this Academy has handled such things elsewhere. The site presents this text under its traditional attribution, as a surviving chapter of a vast lost original translated by Kumārajīva. Modern scholarship broadly regards it as composed in China in the fifth century rather than translated from an Indic original. Neither view changes what it has been for East Asian Buddhism, where it is the single source of the bodhisattva precepts actually taken — but you should know that the question exists.

Come back when you have read both sides. The rest of this page assumes you have.

Part 4

Where the road parts

1 · What the word actually means, and it is not "being good"

Visuddhimagga 1 · in what sense is it virtue?

"…it is virtue in the sense of 'composing' (sīlana). What, then, is this 'composing'? Either it is a coordinating, meaning the non-scatteredness of bodily action and the rest, by way of their being well-disciplined; or it is a supporting, meaning the state of being a foundation, by way of grounding wholesome states."

"Purity is declared by the wise to be its manifestation; and shame and moral dread are said to be its proximate cause."

Avasesesu kenaṭṭhena sīlanti sīlanaṭṭhena sīlaṃ. Kimidaṃ sīlanaṃ nāma. Samādhānaṃ vā, kāyakammādīnaṃ susīlyavasena avippakiṇṇatāti attho. Upadhāraṇaṃ vā, kusalānaṃ dhammānaṃ patiṭṭhānavasena ādhārabhāvoti attho.
See this passage in the full chapter →

Two meanings, and neither of them is moral in the sense an English reader expects. Coordinating — avippakiṇṇatā, non-scatteredness. Virtue is what it is like when your actions are not flying in several directions. Supporting — being the floor other things stand on.

And notice the machinery Buddhaghosa uses to define it: characteristic, function, manifestation, proximate cause. That is the same fourfold Abhidhamma quartet you met in Session 2 applied to contact and to volition. Virtue is being defined here as a mental factor, exactly like any other — not as a code of rules. The rules are downstream.

Its proximate cause is worth sitting with too: hirī and ottappa, shame and moral dread. Not compassion, not aspiration, not love of the good. The thing virtue actually grows out of, on this account, is a functioning capacity for embarrassment.

2 · And then it becomes extremely, professionally specific

Having defined virtue as a universal mental factor, the chapter's content narrows immediately and sharply. The restraint-of-the-faculties section produces one of the most quoted lines in the treatise — "he does not grasp a sign that is the ground for defilement… he stops at the mere fact of having seen" — illustrated by the Elder Mahātissa, who looked at a woman and saw only bones.

Then the purification of livelihood, which is where you find out who this chapter is for. It catalogues, with names, the specific ways a professional religious can make a dishonest living: deceit (including the ascetic who ostentatiously refuses gifts), insinuating talk, affected deportment, talking for gain, flattery, hinting, belittling, and pursuing gain with gain. Then four separate sections on the correct relationship to the robe, the almsfood, the lodging and the medicine.

None of this is available to a layperson, and none of it is meant to be. The Southern chapter answers "how should I live?" by describing, in enormous detail, one particular life.

3 · The Northern door, and who is standing in it

梵網經 7 · the precepts are opened

"At that time, Śākyamuni Buddha, having just sat beneath the bodhi tree and attained unsurpassed awakening, first established the bodhisattva prātimokṣa: filial devotion to one's parents, teachers, monastics, and the Three Jewels. Filial devotion is itself the dharma of the highest path — this filial devotion is what is called precept, and is also called restraint."

爾時釋迦牟尼佛，初坐菩提樹下，成無上覺，初結菩薩波羅提木叉。孝順父母師僧三寶，孝順至道之法，孝名為戒，亦名制止。
See this passage in the full chapter →

孝名為戒 — filial devotion is called precept. Four characters, and the placement is the argument: this is the first thing said, in the first minute, before a single rule is named.

The Southern chapter's proximate cause for virtue was shame. The Northern text's opening ground for it is an obligation to particular people — parents first, then teachers, then the community, then the Three Jewels. Not a feeling and not a principle. A debt, of the kind Read a Whole Sūtra Session 6 showed could never be discharged.

And then the sentence that makes this session worth the reading:

梵網經 7 · who may receive the precepts

"…whoever receives this precept — a king, a prince, a minister of state, a monk, a nun, one of the eighteen Brahma heavens or six heavens of desire, a commoner, one born without full faculties, a man or woman given to lust, a manservant or maidservant, one of the eight kinds of spirit, a vajra spirit, an animal, or even a being manifested by transformation — so long as they understand the words of their teacher, every one of them fully receives the precept, and every one is called foremost in purity."

若受佛戒者，國王王子百官宰相，比丘比丘尼，十八梵天六欲天子，十六大國王，庶民黃門淫男淫女奴婢，八部鬼神金剛神畜生乃至變化人，但解法師語，盡受得戒，皆名第一清淨者。
See this passage in the full chapter →

Read the list slowly, and read it as a fifth- or sixth-century list. A king and a maidservant in the same sentence. 黃門 — one born without full faculties. 淫男淫女 — a man or woman given to lust, which is to say the people most religious codes of the period existed partly to exclude. Spirits. An animal.

And the only qualification given is 但解法師語 — so long as they understand the words of their teacher. Comprehension. That is the entire entrance requirement. And the verdict on everyone who passes it is 皆名第一清淨者 — every one is called foremost in purity.

The fork, stated plainly
  • Both are severe. The Brahmā Net's ten major precepts are not a relaxation of anything, and breaking them is catastrophic within the text's own terms. This is not strict-versus-lenient.
  • The South defines virtue technically and then specifies one life in enormous detail. Virtue is a mental factor with a characteristic and a proximate cause; the content is the discipline of a monk, down to how to hold a bowl.
  • The North opens with a relationship and then removes every barrier to entry. Filial devotion as the precept itself, and comprehension as the only condition — which in practice makes the bodhisattva precepts the form of Buddhist commitment most East Asian laypeople have actually taken.
  • And notice what each one thereby protects. The Southern approach protects precision: it will not tell you that you are living well unless it can say exactly what that means. The Northern approach protects access: it will not build a gate that keeps out a maidservant.

Neither is a concession. They are two answers to a question that was never quite the same question — what is virtue? against who can undertake it? — which is the pattern this course keeps finding, and the reason the disagreements so rarely resolve.

Part 5

One practiceThis week · two tests on the same day

Run the Southern definition and the Northern condition on your own week

Two short exercises, and it is worth doing both, because they catch entirely different things.

The Southern test — non-scatteredness. Buddhaghosa's word for virtue is avippakiṇṇatā, not-scattered. Pick one day this week and, at the end of it, ask only this: were my actions pointing the same way? Not were they good. Were they coordinated, or were they flying in several directions at once — a promise here, a contradicting promise there, an hour spent undoing the previous hour. Most people find the honest answer uncomfortable and also strangely freeing, because scatteredness is a much more tractable problem than wickedness.

The Northern test — the door. Look at whatever you take your own practice or ethics to be, and ask the Brahmā Net's question of it: who would I not let in? Everyone has a list, usually unexamined and usually about respectability. The sūtra's list of who may receive the highest precepts includes a maidservant, a man or woman given to lust, and an animal, on the sole condition that they understand the words of their teacher. Put your own exclusions beside that and see how they look.

You are not being asked to change either answer this week. Only to have both — because these two questions do not overlap, and most people have only ever been asked one of them.

Question 1 of 5
The Visuddhimagga derives sīla from "composing" (sīlana). What are the two senses it gives?
Correct: C. Neither is moral in the sense an English reader expects. Virtue is what it is like when your actions are not flying in several directions, and it is the floor other things stand on. (Coolness and headship are mentioned as other teachers' explanations, which the text notes and does not adopt.)
Question 2 of 5
What does Buddhaghosa give as virtue's proximate cause?
Correct: B. Not compassion, not aspiration, not love of the good. What virtue grows out of, on this account, is a functioning capacity for embarrassment. And note the machinery: characteristic, function, manifestation, proximate cause — the same Abhidhamma quartet used for contact and volition in Session 2. Virtue is being defined as a mental factor, not as a code. The rules are downstream.
Question 3 of 5
After defining virtue in universal terms, what does the chapter's actual content turn out to be about?
Correct: D. Deceit, insinuating talk, affected deportment, talking for gain, flattery, hinting, belittling, pursuing gain with gain. Almost none of it is available to a layperson, and none of it is meant to be. The Southern chapter answers "how should I live?" by describing one life exhaustively.
Question 4 of 5
What is the first thing the Brahmā Net Sūtra establishes, and when?
Correct: A. 孝名為戒. The placement is the argument. Where the Southern chapter grounds virtue in shame, this text grounds it in an obligation to particular people — a debt, of exactly the kind Read a Whole Sūtra's sixth session showed could never be discharged.
Question 5 of 5
Who may receive the bodhisattva precepts, and on what condition?
Correct: C. 但解法師語，盡受得戒，皆名第一清淨者. Read it as a fifth- or sixth-century list: a king and a maidservant in one sentence, one born without full faculties, a man or woman given to lust — the people most religious codes of the period existed partly to exclude — spirits, and an animal. Comprehension is the entire entrance requirement. This is not a relaxation of severity; the ten major precepts remain catastrophic to break. It is a different decision about where the door goes.

Part 7

One question, left open

Sit with this

Who would you not let in?

Whatever you think a serious practice is, you are holding a picture of who is capable of it — and it is almost certainly a picture of someone with time, literacy, stability and a certain kind of respectability. The Brahmā Net's list was written in a society far more stratified than yours, and it put a king and a maidservant in the same sentence and then added an animal. The condition was that you could follow what was being said. Set your own working list beside that one, and notice that you have never written it down.
