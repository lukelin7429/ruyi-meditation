Part 1

Who he wasA woman by a river, and a thing that cannot be sold

His job, in both traditions, is stated the same way: the Buddha says something short, and Kātyāyana says what it means at length. The Pāli title is more explicit than the Chinese — foremost among those who explain in detail the meaning of a brief statement.

But the story the anthology keeps about him is not about exposition at all. It is about a sales pitch.

In Avantī there was a rich elder — "stingy, cruel, and without compassion" — who owned a servant woman. She ran his errands day and night without rest and was whipped for the slightest fault. Her clothes barely covered her; her food was never enough. Old and worn out, she longed for death yet could not attain it.

One day, carrying a water jar to the river, she thought about her life and wept out loud. Kātyāyana was passing, and asked what was wrong. She told him. And his reply is one of the strangest sentences in this course:

經律異相, Fascicle 15 · Kātyāyana teaches an old woman to sell her poverty

"Kātyāyana said: if you are poor, why not sell your own poverty? The woman answered: how can poverty be sold — who would ever buy it? Kātyāyana insisted three times that poverty could indeed be sold, and she finally agreed."

迦旃延言。汝若貧者何不賣貧。女人言曰。貧那可賣誰當買貧。迦旃延言。貧實可賣如是至三。女言賣。
Read this story in full →

What the sale turns out to consist of is worth following exactly, because the specification is the teaching.

Bathe. Then: make an offering. She objects, accurately — she owns not one thread; even the jar belongs to her master. So he tells her to take his own bowl and fetch a little clean water in it. She does, and offers it to him, and he accepts it and blesses it.

Then a fast, and then instruction in reflecting on the Buddha's virtues. Then he asks where she sleeps. She has nowhere: under the millstone when grinding, by the stove when cooking, and on a heap of refuse when there is no work at all.

So he waits until the household is asleep, quietly opens her door himself, and spreads clean grass in the corner of the doorway for her to sit on.

His instruction to her is the hardest part of the story and this course is not going to soften it: keep your heart devoted and reverent in your own labor, and harbor no resentment. She did as instructed. She died in the latter part of that same night.

Her master's household tied her feet with straw rope and dragged the body to the charnel grove. She, meanwhile, had been reborn in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven with five hundred attendants — and, being of "duller faculties," did not know why. Śāriputra, who happened to be there, saw the cause and told her. She came back down to the charnel ground with five hundred devas to scatter flowers and burn incense over her own discarded corpse, until the light of it drew the neighbourhood out to look — including her former master, who asked the devas why they were honouring a body everyone had found repellent while it lived.

What to do with this story
  • It is a real transaction. She gives the only thing she can give — a bowl of clean water — and the poverty is genuinely gone. "Selling poverty" is not a metaphor inside the story's own logic.
  • And the advice is to endure an abusive master without resentment, which in the story's frame buys a heaven and in ours reads as counsel to the wrong person. Both of those are true at once, and this course is not going to pick one and delete the other.
  • Notice what Kātyāyana actually does with his hands. He opens her door and lays out grass for her to sit on. The most famous expositor in the tradition solves this problem by rearranging a doorway.
Part 2

In his own wordsA gap, and a substitute

Every session so far has had a first-person statement from the Śūraṅgama's twenty-five gates. Kātyāyana is not among the twenty-five. There is no entry for him; the course says so rather than reaching for a plausible substitute and presenting it as his.

What there is instead — and it is not a poor exchange — is the Lotus Sūtra, where he speaks, and not alone.

The Lotus Sūtra, Part 5 · three disciples ask for a prediction

"It is like one who, coming from a famine-stricken land, suddenly encounters a great king's own feast, yet still, his heart full of doubt and fear, dares not eat at once — only after receiving the king's own command does he finally dare to eat. We too are just the same: constantly aware of the fault of the lesser vehicle, we do not know how we might attain the Buddha's own unsurpassed wisdom … Great, valiant World-Honored One, who ever wishes to bring peace to the world — we beg you, grant us too this prediction, as one starving needs to be told to eat."

如從饑國來，　忽遇大王饍，心猶懷疑懼，　未敢即便食；若復得王教，　然後乃敢食。我等亦如是，　每惟小乘過，不知當云何，　得佛無上慧。……願賜我等記，如飢須教食。
Read this passage in full →

Three of the ten — Maudgalyāyana, Subhūti and Kātyāyana — "every one of them trembling with awe," asking to be told they are allowed to eat.

Set that against the story in Part 1. The man who walked up to a starving woman and told her, three times, that she could sell her poverty is here the one who cannot approach a feast without permission.

Part 3

The Southern columnThin on biography — and unexpectedly exactly on point

One citation, and it lands precisely where Part 4 is going.

In the Pāli list he is Mahākaccāna, foremost among those who explain in detail the meaning of a brief statement — the same job, described more exactly than the Chinese epithet describes it.

The Visuddhimagga does not tell stories about him. What it does is argue using him. In its chapter on the soil of understanding, working through what dependent origination is and is not, it needs to refute a reading that treats dependent origination as mere arising — and reaches for the Discourse to Kaccāna:

"For in the Discourse to Kaccāna, too, it is said: 'Kaccāna, for one who sees, with right understanding, the arising of the world as it really is, the notion of "there is not," with respect to the world, does not occur.' This proclaims dependent origination in forward order — 'the arising of the world' — by way of the world's condition, for the purpose of uprooting the view of annihilation; it does not proclaim mere arising. For it is not by seeing mere arising that the view of annihilation is uprooted, but by seeing the non-continuation of the conditions."

Read that sentence, then read Part 4. The Southern handbook's point is that treating arising as just arising is insufficient — and the passage it uses to make that point is the one addressed to this very disciple. Vimalakīrti's objection, several centuries and one tradition away, is a version of the same complaint.

Part 4

The rebukeCaught doing exactly his job, and corrected on it

The Buddha gives the monks a brief statement of essentials. Kātyāyana then does what he is foremost at: he expounds the five terms in full — impermanence, suffering, emptiness, no-self, cessation. Vimalakīrti arrives and redefines all five.

Vimalakīrti, to Mahākātyāyana

"Ah, Kātyāyana — do not use a mind of arising and ceasing to expound the Dharma of true reality. Kātyāyana: that every phenomenon ultimately neither arises nor ceases — this is the meaning of impermanence. That the five aggregates of grasping, seen through fully, are empty and never arisen at all — this is the meaning of suffering. That every phenomenon is, in the end, without anything at all — this is the meaning of emptiness. That self and no-self are not two — this is the meaning of no-self. That the Dharma was never, in the first place, kindled, and so now has nothing that needs to be extinguished — this is the meaning of cessation."

唯，迦旃延！無以生滅心行說實相法。迦旃延！諸法畢竟不生不滅，是無常義；五受陰洞達空無所起，是苦義；諸法究竟無所有，是空義；於我、無我而不二，是無我義；法本不然，今則無滅，是寂滅義。
Read this passage in full →

The first sentence is the whole charge: 無以生滅心行說實相法 — do not use a mind of arising and ceasing to expound the Dharma of true reality. Not "your definitions are wrong." Your apparatus is wrong: you are explaining reality with a mind that is itself running on the assumption of things arising and ceasing.

And notice what happens to the definitions. Impermanence does not become something else; it becomes more thorough. "Things arise and cease" quietly assumes there is a moment that genuinely stays, however briefly, before ceasing. Kumārajīva's note on the page makes exactly this distinction: the ordinary reading is "the coarse, entry-level gate into the teaching, true as far as it goes."

The reading note also does something this course should flag, because it is the most generous handling of any rebuke in the chapter. Zhu Daosheng raises the obvious objection himself — the Buddha calls Kātyāyana foremost in explaining the meaning of the teachings, so how can he be corrected on it? — and answers that the Buddha and Kātyāyana "are pointing at the same underlying truth; what differs is only how far a given audience can follow it."

That is not a defeat. It is a statement that the entry-level gate is a real gate, and that Kātyāyana was standing in it on purpose.

Part 5

One practice, for this week

Open the door and put down the grass

Kātyāyana is the greatest explainer in the tradition, and when he meets somebody in real trouble he explains for about four sentences and then does something with his hands: waits for the household to sleep, opens a door, lays clean grass in the corner so she has somewhere to sit.

This week, find one person whose situation you have been understanding rather than altering. Then identify the doorway — the smallest physical arrangement that would make their week different. Not the analysis. The grass.

And take the honest half seriously too. The story's own advice to her — endure it without resentment — is advice this course does not endorse and will not pretend is unproblematic. If the person you are thinking of is being mistreated, "reflect without resentment" is the wrong instruction and the doorway is the right one. The test is simple: at the end of the week, is anything about their actual situation different, or only your understanding of it?

Part 7

One question, left open

Sit with this

He gave her a heaven and left her in the house. What would you have done, and what would it have cost her?

Resist the easy answer in either direction. The modern objection is obvious and correct as far as it goes — the instruction to labour without resentment is addressed to the person with no power, and the cruel master is not addressed at all. But notice what the alternative would have been in her actual situation: an old, sick, destitute servant with no legal standing, encouraged to resist, at a time and place where that ends one way. The story does not tell us Kātyāyana thought it through like this. It does tell us he came back after dark and made her somewhere to sit. Hold both — the counsel you would not give, and the door he actually opened — and notice which one your own helping usually consists of.
