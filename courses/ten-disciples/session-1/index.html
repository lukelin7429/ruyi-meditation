Session 1 · Opening

A list of who was best at what

Where the ten came from, who is missing, and why the number is different in the South
Reading time about twenty minutes Before class nothing at all The texts two chapter-openings and a list
Part 1

He was not aloneWhat English-language Buddhism leaves out of the room

Read almost any English introduction to Buddhism and you will meet one man sitting under one tree. He wakes up, he teaches, he dies, and somewhere in the background there is a vague crowd of monks in robes.

That crowd had names, and the tradition took some trouble over them. For forty-five years the Buddha travelled with a group of people who were so unlike one another that a list eventually had to be kept: who was best at what. Not who was holiest. Who was best at what.

The wisest one, who could take any teaching apart. The one with the powers, who could not use them on the one person he most wanted to save. An ascetic who would not sleep under a roof. A man who went blind and afterwards saw everything. The one who understood emptiness. The great preacher. The debater. The keeper of the rules, who had been a boy cutting the Buddha's hair. The Buddha's own son. And the cousin with the perfect memory, who heard every word spoken for decades and was the last person in the room to understand any of it.

Ten people, ten specialities — and not one of them is the Buddha. This course is about the cast, not the founder.
What this course is doing, in one paragraph
  • It reads each disciple three times. His life, from a Chinese story-anthology. His own first-person account of how he got in, from the Śūraṅgama Sūtra. And the day somebody told him his speciality was not what he thought it was, from the Vimalakīrti Sūtra.
  • It carries a Southern column in every session, and it is honest about which sessions have a thick one and which have almost nothing.
  • It ends where the books come from. Session 12 is the First Council — the three weeks that produced everything else you will ever read.
Part 2

The ten, and where the number comes from

Nobody in the Pāli canon ever says "the ten great disciples." The fixed list is East Asian, and its order comes from one chapter of one Mahāyāna sūtra.

In the Vimalakīrti Sūtra, a wealthy layman falls ill. The Buddha, out of compassion, decides to send someone to ask after him — and then works through his senior disciples one at a time. Each of them declines. Each gives the same reason: I met him once, and I could not answer him.

Ten disciples, ten refusals, in a fixed order. That order is the list.

The Vimalakīrti Sūtra, The Disciples Decline · the chapter opens

"At that time the elder Vimalakīrti thought to himself: 'I lie here ill upon my bed — will the World-Honored One, in his great compassion, not show me some pity?' The Buddha, knowing his thought, said to Śāriputra: 'Go and inquire after Vimalakīrti's illness.' Śāriputra said to the Buddha: 'World-Honored One, I am not able to go and inquire after his illness. And why? …'"

爾時長者維摩詰，自念：「寢疾于床，世尊大慈，寧不垂愍？」佛知其意，即告舍利弗：「汝行詣維摩詰問疾。」舍利弗白佛言：「世尊！我不堪任詣彼問疾。所以者何？……」

Kumārajīva's own note on the page explains why Śāriputra is asked first, when the Buddha already knows perfectly well that all ten will refuse: Śāriputra is foremost in wisdom among the five hundred disciples, so sending him first, and having him decline first, is what makes Vimalakīrti's stature register with the rest of the assembly still watching. The order is not arbitrary. It is staged.

Here is the roster, in that order, with the epithet each one carries in Chinese.

舍利弗 Śāriputra · 智慧第一 · wisdom 目犍連 Maudgalyāyana · 神通第一 · spiritual power 大迦葉 Mahākāśyapa · 頭陀第一 · austerity 須菩提 Subhūti · 解空第一 · emptiness 富樓那 Pūrṇa · 說法第一 · preaching 迦旃延 Kātyāyana · 論議第一 · exposition 阿那律 Aniruddha · 天眼第一 · the divine eye 優波離 Upāli · 持戒第一 · discipline 羅睺羅 Rāhula · 密行第一 · hidden practice 阿難 Ānanda · 多聞第一 · having heard much

Ten sessions, one each, in exactly that order — because reading them in the sūtra's own sequence lets you watch a single argument being made ten times.

Part 3

The Southern columnWhere the counting is done completely differently

Every session has one of these. This is the first.

The Pāli canon has no list of ten. What it has is the etadagga — etad-aggaṃ, "this is the foremost" — a run of chapters in the Numerical Discourses naming around eighty disciples, each unsurpassed in one specified respect. And it is not a list of monks. It has four sections: monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen.

This site's own reading guide to those chapters puts the crucial point better than a paraphrase would:

"What the formula does is name a person as unsurpassed in one specified respect. What it conspicuously does not do is rank anyone overall. Sāriputta is foremost in great wisdom and Mahāmoggallāna in psychic power; nothing in the list says which of those is the better thing to be foremost in, and the list makes no attempt to arrange its categories in order of importance."

And it is emphatically not a hall of fame. One monk is foremost in receiving material gifts. One is there for having a charming voice. One is there for coming from an eminent family — a fact about his birth. The guide's verdict: "Read as a ranking of spiritual attainment, entries like those are baffling. Read as what it is — a catalog of distinguishing marks, the way you would describe the members of a large community to someone who had to tell them apart — it is coherent throughout."

A note on method you will see repeated: the text of those chapters is Bhikkhu Sujato's translation, released CC0, not Ru-Yi's own — so this course links to those pages but never quotes the discourse text itself. The reading guides are Ru-Yi's own writing, and those are quoted. Where a Southern source is Ru-Yi's own translation — above all the Visuddhimagga, rendered directly from the Pāli — it is quoted at length.

AN 1.188–197, the first chapter of foremost monks →

Two consequences worth carrying for the next eleven sessions.

The first is that "foremost" is a description, not a score. Once you stop reading the ten as a leaderboard, the epithets become what they were always meant to be — the way you tell ten people apart in a crowd of five hundred.

The second is that the two traditions do not weight these men the same way at all. The sharpest case is Subhūti, who is an ordinary entry on the Pāli list and appears in the Visuddhimagga zero times — and who, because a Mahāyāna sūtra made him the questioner in the Diamond Sūtra, became one of the most recognisable names in East Asian Buddhism. Session 5 is built entirely around that gap.

Part 4

The rebukeOne argument, made ten times

Now the part that makes this a course rather than a set of profiles.

Vimalakīrti's ten refusals are not ten different criticisms. They are one criticism, fitted to ten people. In each case he takes the exact thing the disciple is best in the world at and shows that the disciple has been doing it in a smaller way than it can be done.

Śāriputra goes first, and he is not caught doing anything wrong. He is sitting in meditation under a tree — which is, on any ordinary account, exactly right.

The first of the ten

"Ah, Śāriputra — sitting like this is not necessarily what is meant by sitting in meditation. As for sitting in meditation: not manifesting body or mind anywhere within the three realms — this is sitting in meditation. Not rising up out of cessation, and yet manifesting every dignified deportment — this is sitting in meditation. Not abandoning the way of the path, and yet manifesting the affairs of an ordinary person — this is sitting in meditation … Not cutting off affliction, and yet entering nirvāṇa — this is sitting in meditation."

唯，舍利弗！不必是坐，為宴坐也。夫宴坐者，不於三界現身、意，是為宴坐；不起滅定而現諸威儀，是為宴坐；不捨道法而現凡夫事，是為宴坐……不斷煩惱而入涅槃，是為宴坐。
Read this passage in full →

And then the line this whole chapter turns on, repeated in some form by every one of the ten: 默然而止，不能加報 — "I fell silent and could not reply."

Notice the shape of the argument, because you will see it nine more times. Vimalakīrti does not tell Śāriputra that sitting is bad. He redefines it so that it includes what Śāriputra had been going into the forest to get away from — ordinary affairs, dignified deportment, affliction itself — and leaves him with a version of his own speciality that is larger than the one he had mastered.

One honest caveat before you go on. This is a Mahāyāna sūtra arguing with earlier Buddhism, written centuries after the men in it died, and the ten disciples are cast here as the side that loses. That is a real polemical purpose and the course will not hide it. But the argument survives its own partisanship, which is why it is worth eleven more sessions: whatever you are best at is the last place you will look for what you are missing.

Part 5

One practice, for this week

Write your own etadagga entry

One sentence, in the canon's own format: "Foremost among [the people I know] in ___ is ___." Fill in your own name, and be specific in the way the Pāli list is specific — not "kind," but "remembering what people told him last time"; not "organised," but "noticing when a meeting has stopped being about anything."

Then write the harder half: the cost of it. What does being the one who is good at that stop you from doing, or stop other people from bringing you?

The exercise is the whole course in miniature. The epithet is real — nobody in this sūtra is accused of faking their speciality, and Śāriputra genuinely was the wisest man in the room. Vimalakīrti's move is not to deny the epithet but to stand next to it and ask what it costs. If your second sentence comes out harder to write than your first, that is the correct result.
Part 6

Five questions

Answer from what you read. One attempt each.

Question 1 of 5
Where does the fixed list of "ten great disciples" come from?
Correct: C. Ten disciples, ten refusals, in a fixed order — and that order became the list East Asia has counted by ever since.
Question 2 of 5
Why, on Kumārajīva's reading, does the Buddha ask Śāriputra first?
Correct: B. The Buddha knows all ten will decline. The sequence is staged, and it starts at the top.
Question 3 of 5
What is the etadagga, and how does it differ from "the ten"?
Correct: A. Four assemblies, about eighty names, and no attempt to say which speciality is the better one to have.
Question 4 of 5
This site's reading guide says the etadagga only makes sense when read in a particular way. Which?
Correct: D. Read as a leaderboard, entries like "foremost in receiving material gifts" or "foremost for coming from an eminent family" are baffling. Read as a way of telling people apart, the whole list is coherent.
Question 5 of 5
What is Vimalakīrti's move against Śāriputra?
Correct: C. Śāriputra was not doing anything wrong; he was sitting under a tree. 默然而止，不能加報 — "I fell silent and could not reply." Nine more versions of the same move follow.

Part 7

One question, left open

Sit with this

If ten people who knew you had to tell a stranger which one you were, what would they say you are foremost in?

Answer with what they would actually say, not what you would like to be known for — those are rarely the same sentence, and the gap between them is worth more than either. Then hold the answer lightly for eleven sessions. Every one of the ten men in this course was correctly described by his epithet, genuinely unsurpassed at the thing, and told anyway that the description had become a place to stand rather than a way of moving.
