Part 1

Who he wasA man with almost no biography and an enormous job

Start with an absence. The anthology this course has been drawing its life-stories from has no story about Pūrṇa — his name appears in it once, in passing, in somebody else's episode. There is no birth, no conversion, no family, no anecdote.

What there is instead is a job description, and it is the largest in the course. The Lotus Sūtra's account of him does not begin with his life at all; it begins with a claim about his career across other lifetimes.

The Lotus Sūtra, Part 10 · Pūrṇa's prediction

"Do you see this Pūrṇamaitrāyaṇīputra? I have always called him foremost among those who teach the Dharma, and have always praised his every kind of merit, his diligent upholding and proclaiming of my Dharma, his ability to teach, instruct, benefit, and delight the fourfold assembly, his complete explanation of the Buddha's true Dharma …"

汝等見是富樓那彌多羅尼子不？我常稱其於說法人中最為第一，亦常歎其種種功德，精勤護持助宣我法，能於四眾示教利喜，具足解釋佛之正法……
Read this passage in full →

The Buddha then says something that should stop you: 自捨如來，無能盡其言論之辯 — apart from the Tathāgata himself, nobody can exhaust his eloquence in argument. And that he was foremost among Dharma-teachers under nine billion past buddhas, foremost for seven buddhas, foremost now, and will be foremost for the buddhas of this present aeon still to come.

Then the passage does something stranger. It says that in those past buddha-fields, 彼佛世人咸皆謂之實是聲聞 — "the people of that buddha's world all took him to be truly a śrāvaka" — and that this was not the truth about him. The verse states it directly:

內祕菩薩行，外現是聲聞 — "secretly holding the practice of a bodhisattva, outwardly appearing as a śrāvaka."

And the line immediately after that one is worth carrying into the rest of this course: 示眾有三毒，又現邪見相 — "they show the assembly that they have the three poisons, and manifest even the marks of wrong view." If you took Fifty-Three Teachers, you have already spent three sessions on exactly that claim.

Part 2

In his own wordsWhich door he came in by

The Śūraṅgama Sūtra, Fascicle 5 · 15 · Sound · 富樓那彌多羅尼子

"Through boundless kalpas my eloquence has been unobstructed, proclaiming suffering and emptiness and penetrating deeply into true reality — expounding even the secret Dharma-gates of Buddhas numerous as the sands of the Ganges, without fear. Knowing my great eloquence, the World-Honored One taught me to develop it through the very turning of sound itself. Subduing demons and enemies through the sound of the Dharma, extinguishing all outflows, is foremost."

Notice the pedagogy in the middle sentence. The Buddha does not teach him to overcome a weakness; he notices what the man already has and tells him to develop that — 知我有大辯才，以音聲輪教我發揚. This is the twenty-five gates' whole premise: not one method, but each person sent in by the faculty they already possess.

Part 3

The Southern columnThin on biography, and pointed on something else

Two cautions and one genuinely useful finding.

First, a name problem. The Pāli canon has more than one Puṇṇa, and they are easy to confuse. The one who matches this session is Puṇṇa son of Mantāṇī — Pūrṇa Maitrāyaṇīputra, 富樓那彌多羅尼子 — named at AN 1.196 as "foremost among those who speak on the teaching." A different Puṇṇa has a famous discourse of his own. This course is careful about that distinction because the Chinese anthology's own entry on a "Pūrṇa" who rose from slavery is a different man again, and conflating them would be easy and wrong.

Second, the scale. The Visuddhimagga mentions him only a couple of times. There is no Southern life-story here any more than there is a Northern one.

Third — and this is the useful part. The Pāli title he holds is dhammakathika, "one who speaks on the teaching." It is not a title reserved for him, or for monks. This site's own reading guide to the lay chapter makes the point:

"Citta Macchikāsaṇḍika is foremost among laymen who speak on the teaching — the same category, dhammakathika, that Puṇṇa holds among the monks and Dhammadinnā among the nuns. A householder is named unsurpassed at teaching the Dhamma. Elsewhere in the canon Citta is shown out-arguing senior monastics on doctrinal points."

Hold that beside what is about to happen in Part 4, where a layman out-teaches this monk in the street. The Vimalakīrti Sūtra is often read as a Mahāyāna provocation against a monastic establishment. The Pāli list had already named a householder unsurpassed in the same category, several centuries earlier, without any polemic at all.

Part 4

The rebukeTwo sūtras, one man, opposite verdicts on the same skill

You met this scene in Session 3, from the other side. Vimalakīrti's charge against Maudgalyāyana was that he had misjudged who was in front of him. Here the same charge is made again, and this time it is made against the man whose entire reputation is knowing how to address an audience.

Pūrṇa, recalling it himself

"Vimalakīrti then taught them the Dharma, so that they would never again turn back from unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. I reflected that a śrāvaka who does not first observe a person's faculties should not teach the Dharma — this is why I am not able to go and inquire after his illness."

Read this passage in full →

What had happened: Pūrṇa was teaching a group of monks, pitched at what he judged they could take. Vimalakīrti did not argue with him. He entered meditative absorption and let the monks recover their own memory of a vow they had made, lifetimes earlier, to the Great Vehicle — a vow Pūrṇa had just judged them incapable of.

Sengzhao's note on the page gives the stakes precisely, and it is more generous to Pūrṇa than the surface reading. Even śrāvakas with the deepest concentration can see back perhaps eighty thousand aeons into a being's past — and only while actually absorbed in that concentration, not as a standing capacity. A bodhisattva's vision is described as reaching without limit. So: "Pūrṇa's fault was not laziness but exactly this gap" — judging readiness from what his own considerable but bounded perception showed him.

Now put that next to the Lotus Sūtra, on this site, describing the same man:

The Lotus Sūtra, Part 10 · from the verse

"Already having crossed over with great spiritual powers, complete with the four unobstructed wisdoms, knowing beings' faculties, sharp or dull, he constantly taught the pure Dharma."

已度大神通，　具四無礙智，知諸根利鈍，　常說清淨法。
Read this passage in full →

知諸根利鈍. That is the exact capacity Vimalakīrti says he lacks. Two Mahāyāna sūtras, both translated on this site, disagree outright about the one thing this man is supposed to be best at.

It is possible to reconcile them, and the Lotus supplies the material to do it: if Pūrṇa is 內祕菩薩行，外現是聲聞 — secretly a bodhisattva, outwardly a śrāvaka — then the monk who appeared to misjudge his listeners in Vaiśālī was playing a part, and the misjudgement is itself the performance.

But notice what that costs. It makes the rebuke unfalsifiable, and it drains the scene of the thing that made it useful: a real teacher, making a real error about real people, corrected. You can have the consoling reading or the instructive one. This course's preference is to leave both texts standing and not pick, but the preference is a preference, and you should know it is being exercised.

Part 5

One practice, for this week

Ask instead of estimating

Pūrṇa's error is the most professional error there is: he assessed his audience competently, and was wrong, and had no way of finding out. The bodhisattva's advantage in this story is not better teaching. It is better information.

This week, in one situation where you would normally pitch something at the level you judge appropriate — a lesson, a briefing, a conversation with a child or a parent — ask first. Not "does that make sense?" after the fact. Before: what do you already know about this? what would actually be useful here?

Then notice how strong the resistance is. Asking costs a little authority, makes the exchange slower, and risks an answer that obliges you to change your plan. That cost is exactly why competent people estimate instead. Sengzhao's line is the one to hold: the fault was not laziness — it was judging from what his own bounded perception showed him. Yours is bounded too, and unlike Pūrṇa you have the option of simply asking.

Part 7

One question, left open

Sit with this

If someone's failure can always be re-described as a deliberate performance, what has the description cost you?

內祕菩薩行，外現是聲聞 is a beautiful idea and a dangerous habit of mind. Applied once, it rescues a revered figure from an embarrassing scene. Applied as a reflex, it makes every error of every admired person into hidden skill, and there is then nothing anyone could do that would count as being wrong. You will recognise the move outside religion too — in politics, in institutions, in families, wherever loyalty needs a mechanism. The question is not whether it is ever true. It is what you would accept as evidence that, this time, it is not.
