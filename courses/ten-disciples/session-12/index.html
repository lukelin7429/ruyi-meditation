Session 12 · What they left

Thus have I heard

如是我聞 — where the books came from, and who was not allowed in the room
Reading time about twenty-five minutes Before class Session 11 The text 大智度論 fascicle 2, on this site
Part 1

After the funeralThe arhats begin leaving, one by one

Eleven sessions of people. This one is about the three weeks that produced the books.

The Buddha dies, and the first thing that happens in the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra's account is not organisation. It is dispersal. The great arhats, having crossed, see no reason to remain:

大智度論, Fascicle 2 · after the parinirvāṇa

"The great arhats each followed their own intention. In mountains, forests, flowing springs, ravines" — and there they laid down their bodies and entered final nirvāṇa. The gods, watching, each thought:

"The sun of the Buddha has already set, and the light of his disciples, with their many meditative concentrations, liberations, and wisdom, is also extinguished. These beings have many illnesses of desire, anger, and delusion. Now that the physicians of Dharma are quickly entering extinction, who will treat them? The disciple-lotuses born in the great ocean of immeasurable wisdom have now withered."

諸大阿羅漢各各隨意，於諸山林流泉谿谷，處處捨身而般涅槃……佛日既沒，種種禪定解脫智慧弟子光亦滅。是諸眾生有種種婬怒癡病，是法藥師輩今疾滅度，誰當治者？

There is a scene in this section that shows what was at stake, and it involves a junior monk sent as a messenger. Aniruddha — Session 8 — proposes that Gavāṃpati, a disciple of Śāriputra's now living in a heaven, be fetched to help gather the Vinaya. A junior monk is sent, flies up, and delivers the summons.

Gavāṃpati immediately suspects something is wrong, and asks a series of questions. Has the Saṅgha split? Has the sun of the Buddha entered extinction? Yes. Then where is my preceptor Śāriputra? He entered nirvāṇa earlier. Where is Mahāmaudgalyāyana? He too has entered extinction.

佛法欲散，大人過去，眾生可愍 — "The Buddha Dharma is about to scatter. Great beings have passed away; beings are pitiable."

He asks after two more. What is Elder Ānanda doing? — "grieving, weeping, confused." And Rāhula? — "Because Rāhula attained arhatship, he has no grief and no sorrow; he only contemplates the impermanent marks of dharmas."

Then Gavāṃpati declines to come down at all. He rises into the sky, produces fire from his own mind that burns his body, and water from his body that flows in four streams to Mahākāśyapa's feet, with a voice in the water reciting a verse: like a great elephant departing and the elephant calf following after. The junior monk carries his robe and bowl back to the Saṅgha.

That is the situation Mahākāśyapa is working against. Not persecution, not doctrinal dispute — simply that everyone who knew what the Buddha said was leaving, and had no particular reason to stay.

Part 2

One thousand, minus oneThe only man in the room who could do the job is thrown out of it

Mahākāśyapa selects a thousand arhats — and the reason given for the number is entirely practical: Rājagṛha can feed a thousand people daily, so the work will not have to stop. King Ajātaśatru is told to send food every day.

Then, on the fifteenth day of the rains retreat, he checks the room.

大智度論, Fascicle 2 · the expulsion

"Mahākāśyapa entered meditative concentration and, with the divine eye, observed who in the assembly still had afflictions not yet exhausted and should be expelled. Only Ānanda had not yet exhausted them; the other nine hundred ninety-nine had ended all outflows and were pure and stainless. Mahākāśyapa arose from meditation, took Ānanda by the hand in the midst of the assembly, led him out, and said, "The scripture treasury is being gathered within this pure assembly. Your fetters have not yet been exhausted, so you should not remain here."

大迦葉入禪定，以天眼觀，今是眾中，誰有煩惱未盡應逐出者！唯有阿難一人不盡，餘九百九十九人諸漏已盡，清淨無垢。大迦葉從禪定起，眾中手牽阿難出，言：「今清淨眾中結集經藏。汝結未盡，不應住此！」

Nine hundred and ninety-nine arhats, and the one man who has heard every word. Led out by the hand, in front of everyone.

Ānanda weeps — 慚恥悲泣 — and thinks: "For twenty-five years I attended the World-Honored One, serving at his side, and I never experienced such distress. The Buddha truly had great virtue: he was compassionate, forbearing, and patient."

Then he explains himself, and the explanation reframes his entire career: "I have the ability and could have attained the path long ago. But according to the Dharma of the Buddhas, an arhat may not serve at the side of a Buddha as attendant. For this reason, I left a remainder of fetters and did not completely cut them off."

He stayed unfinished on purpose, because finishing would have cost him the job. Put that against Session 11's Visuddhimagga note filing him among those who reached the analytical knowledges "at the stage of the trainee." Everything the tradition has came through a man who deliberately did not complete himself, in order to stay close enough to hear.

Mahākāśyapa is not moved, and lists further faults — that Ānanda pressed the Buddha to admit women, and that he failed to fetch water when the Buddha, back aching near Kuśinagara, asked for it. Ānanda answers both. On the first: "I had compassion for Gautamī. Also, the Dharma of the Buddhas of the three times all has four assemblies. How could my Buddha Śākyamuni alone not have them?" On the second: five hundred carts had just forded the stream and the water was muddy. He is told to confess the fault anyway.

Part 3

Before his head reached the pillow

Outside the hall, he tries all night. And the text explains why it does not work, in a sentence worth the whole session:

大智度論, Fascicle 2 · the night outside

"Ānanda had much wisdom but little power of concentration, and for this reason he did not immediately attain the path. When concentration and wisdom are equal, attainment can come quickly. As the latter part of the night was about to pass, he was utterly exhausted and lay down to rest. As he reclined toward his pillow, before his head reached the pillow, he suddenly awakened. It was like a flash of lightning appearing, by which someone in darkness sees the path."

是阿難智慧多，定力少，是故不即得道；定智等者，乃可速得。後夜欲過，疲極偃息，却臥就枕，頭未至枕，廓然得悟；如電光出，闇者見道。

智慧多，定力少 — much wisdom, little concentration. The diagnosis of the man foremost in having heard, offered by the tradition itself, without embarrassment. And it happens when he stops: exhausted, lying down, giving up for the night, between standing and the pillow.

He goes straight to the hall and knocks. Mahākāśyapa asks who it is. "I am Ānanda." Why have you come? "Tonight I have exhausted all the outflows."

And Mahākāśyapa says: "I will not open the door for you. Come in through the keyhole."

He does. Then he bows and asks not to be reproached again — and Mahākāśyapa puts a hand on his head:

大智度論, Fascicle 2 · at the door

"I did this deliberately for you, so that you would attain the path. You should have no resentment; I am the same toward you, because you have realized it for yourself. It is like drawing with one's hand in empty space: nothing is stained or attached. The mind of an arhat is also like this; among all dharmas, it attains nonattachment. Return to your original seat."

我故為汝，使汝得道；汝無嫌恨，我亦如是，以汝自證。譬如手畫虛空，無所染著；阿羅漢心亦如是，一切法中得無所著。復汝本坐。

Part 4

The four wordsWhat he said when he sat down

So the room is complete: a thousand arhats, fed by a king, on Vulture Peak. And the question they put to Ānanda is the first question:

大智度論, Fascicle 2 · the first recitation

"Elder Mahākāśyapa and the other arhats asked Ānanda, 'Where did the Buddha first teach the Dharma? What Dharma did he teach?' Ānanda answered, 'Thus have I heard: at one time, the Buddha was in the Deer Park of the Sages in the country of Vārāṇasī, teaching the noble truth of suffering to the five monks. I did not originally hear this from another; within the Dharma, through right recollection, I obtained eye, knowledge, illumination, and awakening.'"

長老摩訶迦葉等諸阿羅漢問阿難：「佛初何處說法？說何等法？」阿難答：「如是我聞：一時，佛在波羅捺國仙人鹿林中，為五比丘說是苦聖諦。我本不從他聞，法中正憶念得眼、智、明、覺。」
Read this passage in full →

如是我聞. Every sūtra you have opened in this course — the Vimalakīrti, the Lotus, the Śūraṅgama, the Diamond — begins with those four words, and this is the moment they were first said, by a man who had been standing outside the door the night before.

What the formula is actually claiming
  • Not "this is true." It says: this is what I heard. A sūtra opens by naming its own chain of custody rather than asserting its own authority.
  • And not "I was told." Ānanda adds 我本不從他聞 — I did not hear this from another. He was there.
  • It is a citation practice. The tradition's response to losing its founder was to decide that every text would carry, permanently, a statement of who heard it and where.

Upāli — Session 9, the barber's son — recited the Vinaya into the same assembly. The etadagga reading guide on this site put it exactly: "Two entries later, Upāli is named foremost in having memorized the monastic law, and between the two of them stands the whole of what was recited at the first council."

Part 5

One practice, for the end of the course

Say where you heard it

For one week, attach a provenance to the things you assert. Not in a scholarly way — just the ordinary version. I read this in — ; someone told me — ; I think I picked this up from — but I'm not sure.

Then notice the category that resists: the beliefs you hold with real confidence and cannot source at all.

That category is the point of the exercise, and it is usually larger than expected. 如是我聞 is a technology for exactly this problem, invented by people who had just realised that everything they possessed now depended on memory and would be repeated by others. They did not respond by claiming authority. They responded by making every text state, permanently, who had heard it and where — which is a more honest instinct than most things anyone believes have ever been subjected to.

Part 7

One question, left open

Sit with this

Twelve sessions, and not one of these men was improved by being defended. Which of them did you want to defend?

You will have wanted to, somewhere — when Śāriputra was called harsh, when Kāśyapa's kindness to a dying woman was named as a defect, when Ānanda was led out of the hall by the hand in front of nine hundred and ninety-nine people who had finished. The tradition did not do that. It kept the epithets and the failures in the same paragraph, recorded the objections of the people who lost the arguments, preserved its own founder's wife saying that what he did was not compassion, and opened every book it made with a sentence admitting that all of it rests on one man's memory. That is a strange way to build a canon, and it is the reason these ten are still worth reading.
