Part 1

Who he wasA barber's son, and a haircut that went unfinished

The Buddhacarita places him precisely. When the Buddha returns to Kapilavastu and the Śākya princes go forth in a wave, one more person joins them:

Buddhacarita, Part 29 · the mass ordination at Kapilavastu

"And there was, too, the son of a barber, named Upāli, who, seeing these princes and ministers' sons go forth in this way, felt his own heart moved and opened, and likewise received the way of going forth from home himself."

Read this passage in full →

Princes, ministers' sons, and the palace barber's boy — and it is the barber's boy who ends up foremost in the monastic law, holding the rules that govern every one of the others.

The anthology's story about him is the best small scene in this course, and almost nothing happens in it. The Buddha is in Rājagṛha and nobody dares to shave his hair. Only one boy is willing.

His parents stand in front of the Buddha with their palms together and coach him, out loud, while he works. What follows is a series of corrections so ordinary they could be any parent watching a child do a job in front of an important guest.

經律異相, Fascicle 15 · Upāli shaves the Buddha's hair

"He shaves very well, but his body leans too far forward. The parents told the boy to straighten himself slightly — but not so straight that his breathing grew too coarse. Again they said: he shaves very well, but now his body is too straight … The Buddha said: he shaves very well, but his in-breath is too coarse. The parents told him: do not breathe in so coarsely, lest it disturb the Buddha … At this, Upāli's own in- and out-breath grew so refined that he entered the fourth dhyāna entirely."

甚能剃髮但身太曲。父母教兒小直。又莫太直使出息不得麁大。又言。善能剃頭而身太直。……佛言。善能剃髮。而入息太麁。……時優波離。入出息盡入第四禪。
Read this story in full →

Read what actually happened. Nobody taught him meditation. He was told to sit up straighter, then not so straight, then to breathe more quietly, then more quietly still — the standard instructions of posture and breath, delivered as ordinary parental nagging — and he followed each one exactly, while holding a razor against the head of a buddha.

佛告阿難言。優波離已入第四禪。汝取其刀 — "Upāli has now entered the fourth dhyāna — take up the razor yourself."

The Buddha does not wake him. He has Ānanda finish the haircut.

Part 2

In his own wordsWhich door he came in by

The Śūraṅgama Sūtra, Fascicle 5 · 16 · Bodily Discipline · 優波離

"I personally accompanied the Buddha when he left the city to go forth, personally witnessed his six years of arduous practice, and personally saw him subdue every demon and every non-Buddhist teacher, becoming free of every worldly outflow of greed and desire. Receiving the Buddha's precepts and discipline down to the finest detail — three thousand dignified deportments, eighty-four thousand subtle rules of conduct and restraint — all became pure in me. Disciplining the body until the body itself gains freedom, then disciplining the mind in turn until the mind gains understanding, and finally body and mind both become fully unobstructed, is foremost."

Read all twenty-five accounts →

Note the order he insists on, because it is the whole argument of his life and it is the thing Vimalakīrti will not let him rest in: body first, then mind, then both together. Discipline the body until the body is free; only then the mind; and the end state is that neither obstructs anything.

It is also, exactly, what happened to him with the razor. The corrections were all about the body — lean, straighten, breathe — and the mind arrived by itself.

Part 3

The Southern columnA name trap, and one sentence that sets up Session 12

Read the caution before the content.

There are two Upālis. The Visuddhimagga mentions "the householder Upāli" (gahapati Upāli) among those who reach the analytical knowledges at the stage of a trainee — alongside Elder Ānanda, the householder Citta, the lay devotee Dhammika, and the laywoman Khujjuttarā. That is a different man, a layman, not the monk who kept the Vinaya. This course flags the trap rather than quietly harvesting the citation.

What the Pāli does give him is his title, and one observation this site's own reading guide makes about where he sits. Ānanda holds an unmatched five etadagga entries — the guide notes that "no one else on the entire list gets more than two" — and then:

"Two entries later, Upāli is named foremost in having memorized the monastic law, and between the two of them stands the whole of what was recited at the first council."

Hold that for Session 12. Everything the tradition possesses came through two memories, and one of them belonged to a barber's son.

Part 4

The rebukeTwo monks are too ashamed to ask the Buddha, so they ask him

Two monks have broken the rule. They are ashamed, and cannot bring themselves to ask the Buddha about it, so they go to Upāli — which is precisely correct procedure, since he is the one who knows. They ask to have their doubt and regret resolved.

Upāli does his job: 我即為其如法解說 — I explained the matter for them exactly as the rule prescribes.

Vimalakīrti, to Upāli

"Ah, Upāli — do not add further to these two monks' offense. You should simply and directly dissolve it, and not further disturb their minds. And why? Because the very nature of that offense is not located within, not located without, and not located anywhere in between. As the Buddha himself has said: it is because the mind is defiled that beings are defiled, and because the mind is pure that beings are pure … Upāli, when you yourself attained liberation through the mark of your own mind, was there, at that moment, any defilement in it at all?"

唯，優波離！無重增此二比丘罪！當直除滅，勿擾其心。所以者何？彼罪性不在內、不在外、不在中間，如佛所說，心垢故眾生垢，心淨故眾生淨。……優波離！以心相得解脫時，寧有垢不？
Read this passage in full →

無重增此二比丘罪 — do not add further to their offense. The charge is not that Upāli got the ruling wrong. It is that a correct ruling, delivered to two men already sick with shame, added something — and what it added was not covered by the rule he was applying.

Then the redefinition, and it does to "upholding the Vinaya" what Session 7 did to "impermanence":

"Whoever knows this is said to truly uphold the monastic rule; whoever knows this is said to have genuinely understood it." — 其知此者，是名奉律. Upholding the rule is redefined as knowing that phenomena do not stay, not as knowing the rule.

And then the detail that makes this the most uncomfortable scene in the chapter. The two monks, listening, say: 上智哉！是優波離所不能及，持律之上而不能說 — "What supreme wisdom! This is something even Upāli himself cannot reach — foremost in the discipline, and yet unable to say this."

They say it in front of him. And Upāli agrees with them: "Apart from the Tathāgata himself, there has never been a śrāvaka or a bodhisattva able to match his gift for speaking the Dharma." The two monks' doubt and regret dissolve on the spot, and they raise the mind of unsurpassed awakening — which is, in the end, the outcome Upāli had been trying to produce.

Part 5

One practice, for this week

Separate the ruling from the weight

Upāli's error is available to anyone who has ever been correct with somebody who already felt terrible. The rule was right. The explanation was accurate. And the two monks left heavier than they arrived.

This week, when you next have to tell someone they got something wrong — a colleague, a student, a child, a partner — do the two things as two separate acts. State what is true, once, plainly. Then stop, and ask yourself the question Vimalakīrti is asking: is anything I am about to add still about the fault, or is it now about their state?

The test is 當直除滅，勿擾其心 — dissolve it directly, do not further disturb their minds. Most of what we add after the first accurate sentence is not more information. It is emphasis, or thoroughness, or our own discomfort looking for somewhere to go. Notice how much shorter the exchange becomes when you stop after the true part.

Part 7

One question, left open

Sit with this

Two people came to you ashamed, you told them the truth correctly, and they left worse. When was that?

It will have happened, and probably recently, because being right is the least suspicious thing a person can be. The scene is uncomfortable precisely because Upāli is not a pedant — he is the most careful man in the tradition, doing the exact thing he is trusted to do, for people who came to him voluntarily. If the rebuke lands anywhere useful, it is on the gap between resolving a question and relieving a person, which are not the same act and which competence tends to collapse into one. Note also what the sūtra does not do: it does not say the rule was unnecessary or that the offense was imaginary. It says: dissolve it directly, and do not disturb them further.
