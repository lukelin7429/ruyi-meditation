Part 1

Who he wasA woman in a tower who says no

He is the Buddha's son, and the only one of the ten whose entry into the sangha required somebody else to be talked out of something.

The Buddha sends Maudgalyāyana to Kapilavastu to ask for him — Rāhula is nine — with an argument attached: the joy of a mother and son's bond is brief, and only Rāhula's own attainment can truly deliver her from birth and death.

Yaśodharā hears an envoy is coming, takes her son up into a high tower and has every door barred. Maudgalyāyana flies up. Forced to receive him, she answers, and the anthology keeps her answer in full.

經律異相, Fascicle 7 · Rāhula's going forth

"She answered his request with bitter anger: the prince, having abandoned her within three years of marriage, showed no regard for his own family upon his triumphant return, treating her worse than a stranger — and now would take her son as well, in the name of a compassion she could not see."

使我守孤抱窮。今奪我子為其眷屬。何酷如之。太子成道自言慈悲。今別母子何慈之有。

太子成道自言慈悲。今別母子何慈之有 — the prince attained the path and calls himself compassionate; now he separates a mother from her son — where is the compassion in that?

The tradition wrote down the strongest objection anyone has ever made to the Buddha's own life, put it in his wife's mouth, and kept it.

Neither King Śuddhodana nor Mahāprajāpatī can move her. What finally does is a conjured voice from the sky, reminding her of an ancient vow: that when the bodhisattva bought flowers from her to offer Dīpaṃkara Buddha, on condition she be his wife in every future life, she herself had vowed that whatever kingdom, wife, or child should be born to her, she would let him give it away as he saw fit.

She remembers it "as vividly as yesterday," summons Maudgalyāyana back, apologises, and hands over her son "in tears." Fifty young Śākya nobles were sent along with him. Ānanda shaved their heads; Śāriputra was preceptor; Maudgalyāyana was instructor.

A note this course owes you. The story resolves her objection by producing a prior consent she had forgotten. Whether that is a resolution or a very old way of settling an argument is worth thinking about for longer than the text spends on it — and the text does at least let her say the hard thing first, at length, to a flying monk in her own tower.

Part 2

A basin of waterThe Śūraṅgama's twenty-five leave him out too — so this is the Buddha teaching him

Like Kātyāyana in Session 7, Rāhula is not among the twenty-five gates. What the anthology gives instead is better for this session anyway: the only scene in this course of the Buddha teaching his own child, and it is not gentle.

Rāhula, before the path, is described plainly: 心性麁獷言少誠信 — his character was rough and his speech unreliable. Sent away to a monastery for ninety days to guard his speech, he spent them "in shame and self-reproach." Then his father visited.

經律異相, Fascicle 7 · Rāhula receives the precepts

"The Buddha had him fetch water in a washbasin to wash his feet, then asked whether this water could now be drunk — Rāhula said no, since it had been soiled by the washing. 'You are just the same,' the Buddha said. 'Though my own son and a king's grandson, if you do not guard your own mind and speech, the three poisons fill your heart just as this water is now fouled and unusable.'"

佛語羅云。此水可食用飲澡潄以不。羅云白言。此水本實清潔。今已洗足受於塵垢。不可復用。佛語羅云。汝亦如是。雖為吾子國王之孫。捨世榮祿得為沙門。不念精進攝心守口。三毒垢穢充滿胸懷。亦復如此。水不可復用也。

It goes on, and gets sharper. Could the empty basin hold food? No — because it bears that name, having once held filth. "You are the same": a monk of insincere speech, once he has earned a bad name, is unfit to hold anything worthwhile either.

Then the Buddha kicks the basin across the floor with his toe and asks whether Rāhula would mind if it broke. Rāhula says: it is a cheap thing for washing feet — he would not mind much. And the point lands: that is how the wise will feel about you.

The story ends with an elephant. A war-elephant sent into battle will fight with everything but keeps its trunk tucked away, because the trunk is soft and an arrow there kills it. Guard your mouth like that. 羅云聞佛懇惻之誨。感激自厲。得羅漢道 — Rāhula heard this earnest, pained instruction, roused himself, and attained arhatship.

Part 3

The Southern columnHis epithet differs between the traditions, and the difference is the point

One real divergence, worth more than a pile of anecdotes.

In Chinese he is 密行第一 — foremost in hidden practice, the conduct nobody sees. In Pāli he is something else, and this site's reading guide draws out the difference:

"The Buddha’s son gets one line, and it is worth noticing what it says. He is not named foremost in wisdom, or attainment, or lineage — he is foremost among sikkhākāma, those who desire the training. That is a disposition rather than an achievement, and it is an unusually modest thing to be praised for … what is commended in him is willingness to be taught."

Put that beside the basin story and it reads almost as a description of a boy who needed a great deal of correcting and kept coming back for it.

And one more name on the same list. The guide to the chapter of foremost nuns notes: "Bhaddakaccānā, foremost among those who have attained great insight, is identified by the commentarial tradition with Rāhula's mother — the Buddha's former wife." The woman in the tower in Part 1 is herself an entry among the foremost — named, as the guide puts it, "for her attainment, in one line, like everyone else."

The Visuddhimagga gives him no biography, but it does place him: he appears in the chapter on concentration, in the section on taking up a meditation subject, and in the recollections. He turns up where the instructions are.

The Pāli canon also preserves several discourses addressed directly to him on exactly the themes of the basin story — none of them quotable here, since those pages carry Sujato's translation rather than Ru-Yi's own, but they are linked below and they are the fullest record anywhere of the Buddha instructing his son.

Part 4

The rebukeAsked the one question his own life answers

A group of young noblemen from Vaiśālī come to him, bow, and ask the most natural question anyone could put to this particular monk: you are the Buddha's own son; you gave up the throne of a wheel-turning king to go forth. What benefit is there in going forth?

He answers, 如法 — as the teaching prescribes — on the merit and benefit of going forth. He is the strongest possible witness on the subject, and he says what the tradition says.

Vimalakīrti, to Rāhula

"Ah, Rāhula — you should not speak of the merit and benefit of going forth in that way. And why? Because to be without benefit and without merit is what going forth truly means. Only conditioned things can be said to have benefit and to have merit. Going forth is for the sake of the unconditioned, and in the unconditioned there is no benefit and no merit."

唯，羅睺羅！不應說出家功德之利。所以者何？無利、無功德，是為出家；有為法者，可說有利、有功德。夫出家者，為無為法，無為法中，無利、無功德。
Read this passage in full →

無利、無功德，是為出家. To be without benefit and without merit is what going forth is. Anything you can list as a gain is, by that very fact, a conditioned thing — so a recruitment pitch made of benefits is advertising the wrong object.

And then the scene does something none of the other nine do. Having dismantled Rāhula's answer, Vimalakīrti turns to the young men and recruits them himself: "You should all go forth together within the true Dharma. And why? Because a buddha's presence in the world is hard to encounter."

They raise a practical objection, and it is the objection Part 1 of this session was about: "Householder, we have heard the Buddha say that without our parents' consent, we cannot go forth."

Vimalakīrti does not overrule it. He agrees — 然 — and then relocates the whole thing: give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening; that itself is going forth, and that itself is complete. Thirty-two of them did.

Read that against the tower. The rule about parental consent is the rule that nearly kept Rāhula at home, and that his mother had to be argued out of. Here it is left standing, and the thing it guards is simply put somewhere the rule does not reach.

Part 5

One practice, for this week

Describe the thing without the benefits

Take something you genuinely value and would recommend — a practice, a discipline, a way of working, a relationship, a decision you made. Now describe it to somebody without naming a single benefit.

No "it makes you calmer," no "you'll sleep better," no "it saved my marriage," no returns of any kind. Just what it actually is, and what it actually asks.

Notice two things. First, how hard it is — most of us can only recommend things in the currency of gain, which is Vimalakīrti's entire objection. Second, notice whether the thing still sounds worth doing once the returns are stripped out. If it does, you have found what 無利、無功德 is pointing at. If it does not, that is worth knowing too, and is not a failure of the exercise.

Part 7

One question, left open

Sit with this

Yaśodharā's question has never been satisfactorily answered. Do you want it to be?

A religion that produced this passage and kept it is not one that finds the question easy. The Buddha left a wife and a newborn; twelve years later he sent for the child. His wife said, in effect: you call this compassion. The text answers her with a vow from a previous life, which is the answer available inside its own framework, and you may find it sufficient or you may not. What is worth noticing is the shape of your own reaction — whether you want the objection resolved so the story can be admired again, or whether you can let it stand unresolved next to everything else in this course. Nine sessions in, the ten disciples have not once been improved by being defended.
