Part 1

Who he wasA prodigy, a friendship, and four lines of verse in the street

Before he was born, his mother dreamt of a man in armour holding a vajra, smashing every mountain and then standing beside them. His father — a famous debater himself — read the dream and said: you will bear a son who will defeat every debater there is, except one, whose student he will become.

The pregnancy changed his mother. She became so formidable in argument that her own brother lost every exchange with her, concluded that the child she was carrying must be extraordinarily wise, and left home to study — so consumed that he stopped cutting his nails, and people called him the Long-Nailed Brahmacārin.

The boy was called Upatiṣya, but because everyone knew whose son he was, they called him by his mother's name instead: Śāriputra — Śārī's son. By eight he had memorised eighteen collections of scripture and understood every book there was.

Then the part of the story worth telling slowly. Every spring the kingdom of Magadha held a great assembly at the dwelling-place of two dragon kings, and set out four high seats: one for the king, one for the crown prince, one for the chief minister, and one for the master debater. An eight-year-old looked around the crowd, decided there was nobody there better than himself, climbed into the fourth seat and sat down cross-legged.

The assembled scholars, the text says, were unsettled. Some thought him a stupid child. Some thought his intelligence beyond ordinary. The assembly, the translation says, was astonished, and wondered "whether he was merely a foolish child or a genius beyond his years" — and, "though marveling at his precocity, felt too embarrassed by his young age to address him directly, sending instead their own junior disciples to relay questions to him." His answers flowed back so fluently, his reasoning so far surpassing theirs, that "the assembled debaters, wise and foolish, young and old alike, all conceded defeat, declaring they had never witnessed such a thing."

He was eight years old, and the professionals would not speak to him in person.

At that assembly he met a man named Kolita — Maudgalyāyana, who is Session 3 — and the two became inseparable: 才智相比行止必俱, matched in talent and wisdom, never going anywhere apart. They made each other a promise: whoever tastes the nectar first will share it with the other.

They went forth together under a teacher named Sañjaya, and Śāriputra eventually asked him the obvious question: the path you seek — have you attained it? The answer is one of the more remarkable things in this anthology. "I have sought the path across many years now, yet do not know whether there is truly any fruit to attain, or whether I myself am simply not the right person for it — either way, I have not attained it."

They stayed anyway, for years. Then Śāriputra saw a monk called Aśvajit walking into the city with his bowl — one of the first five — and something about his bearing stopped him. He asked who his teacher was. Then he asked to be taught. The monk demurred: he was young, his own training was shallow, how could he set out the Tathāgata's vast meaning? Śāriputra said: then give me the gist.

"All dharmas arise from causes and conditions; this same Dharma teaches also their cessation through causes and conditions." At these very words, Śāriputra attained the first fruit of the path …

He went straight back and repeated it to Maudgalyāyana, who attained the same. The two of them walked to the Buddha with two hundred and fifty students each. And the Buddha, seeing them from afar, pointed to Śāriputra and said: "Foremost in wisdom among my disciples" — and, pointing to Maudgalyāyana: "Foremost in supernormal power." The Chinese runs 是我弟子中智慧第一.

The epithet is given before either of them has said a word to him.

Part 2

In his own wordsWhich door he came in by

Much later, in the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, twenty-five beings each stand up and describe the single faculty through which they got in. Śāriputra's is the twelfth, and it is about seeing.

The Śūraṅgama Sūtra, Fascicle 5 · 12 · Mind-Seeing · 舍利弗

"Through boundless kalpas my mind-seeing has been pure; wherever I encountered the various transformations of worldly and transcendent things, one glance and I understood them completely, without obstruction. Meeting the Kāśyapa brothers on the road, hearing them expound on causation, I awakened to the boundlessness of mind, and left home under the Buddha. Mind and seeing giving forth their own light, this light reaching its limit as knowledge and vision, is foremost."

Read all twenty-five accounts →

One glance and I understood them completely — 一見則通. That is what being foremost in wisdom feels like from the inside: not effort, but immediate sufficiency of a glance. Keep hold of that, because it is exactly what Vimalakīrti is going to go after in Part 4.

Note also the discrepancy. The Chinese anthology has him awakened by Aśvajit; the Śūraṅgama has him meeting "the Kāśyapa brothers on the road." Both are on this site. Neither has been adjusted to match the other, and this course is not going to pretend the sources agree.

Part 3

The Southern columnThe same man, refusing a meal, in two languages

Thick. The Visuddhimagga names him as its strictest standard.

In the Pāli list he is simply foremost in great wisdom, paired always with Mahāmoggallāna in psychic power. But the richest Southern material about him is not about wisdom at all. It is about food.

The Visuddhimagga's first chapter, on virtue, works through what counts as purity of livelihood, and names the highest standard there is — one who refuses even the hints and indirect requests the Buddha permits, "relying only on such qualities as wanting little — even when confronted with the loss of his very life." Such a person, the text says, is "one of supreme austere conduct, just like the Elder Sāriputta."

Then it tells the story it has been building toward. Sāriputta, in forest solitude with Mahāmoggallāna, is doubled over with a stomach disorder. Asked what used to help, he says: when I was a layman my mother made me milk-rice porridge with ghee, honey and sugar. A tree-deity overhears, possesses the son of a supporting family, and has that exact porridge prepared. Mahāmoggallāna collects it and brings it to him.

"That elder, too, seeing it and thinking, 'This porridge is exceedingly delightful — how did it come about, I wonder?', perceived the root of its origin and said, 'Friend Moggallāna, this almsfood is not fit to be eaten.'"

Moggallāna does not argue — "without even entertaining the thought, 'He refuses to eat almsfood brought by someone like me,'" he takes the bowl by the rim at that single word and tips it onto the ground. And then the line that makes the story:

"The very moment the porridge touched the ground, the elder's affliction vanished — and from then on, for forty-five years, it never arose again."

He could have been well that afternoon. Instead: "Friend, porridge that has arisen in dependence on a verbal intimation would not be fit to eat, even if my own intestines were to come out and crawl about on the ground."

Visuddhimagga, Chapter 1 · Sīlaniddesa — Pāli with Ru-Yi's translation → · his etadagga entry (linked, not quoted)

Now put that beside a story the Chinese anthology tells, from an entirely different Vinaya lineage.

Śāriputra and others accept an invitation from a group of nuns, who lay on fine food for the seniors and give the junior monks and novices rice cooked sixty days earlier, mixed with sesame dregs. The Buddha asks Rāhula whether everyone ate well, hears the account, asks who the senior present had been — and, told it was Śāriputra, says: Śāriputra has eaten unclean food. Śāriputra vomits up what he has eaten and vows never to accept an invited meal again for the rest of his life, begging door to door instead.

Later, noble households want to host the sangha and specifically ask for him. They petition the Buddha to order him to accept invitations again. And the Buddha refuses — 莫求。其性惡憋 — do not ask it; his temperament is harsh and stubborn. Then he explains it by a past life: Śāriputra was a venomous snake, forced by a healer to choose between entering fire and taking its own venom back. It chose the fire.

What the two traditions agree on
  • Both remember him as the extreme case on food and livelihood — the standard nobody else is held to.
  • Both frame the refusal as costly and non-negotiable: forty-five years of illness in the Pāli; a lifetime of door-to-door begging in the Chinese.
  • And the Chinese source is startlingly unflattering about the reason. The Buddha does not praise the vow. He declines to overturn it, and says the man is difficult.
Part 4

The rebukeHe is found sitting under a tree, doing nothing wrong

You saw the first two lines of this in Session 1. Here is the whole of it, because the six clauses are the argument.

Vimalakīrti, to Śāriputra

"Ah, Śāriputra — sitting like this is not necessarily what is meant by sitting in meditation. As for sitting in meditation: not manifesting body or mind anywhere within the three realms — this is sitting in meditation. Not rising up out of cessation, and yet manifesting every dignified deportment — this is sitting in meditation. Not abandoning the way of the path, and yet manifesting the affairs of an ordinary person — this is sitting in meditation. A mind that abides neither within nor without — this is sitting in meditation. Unmoved with respect to every view, and yet practicing the thirty-seven factors of the path — this is sitting in meditation. Not cutting off affliction, and yet entering nirvāṇa — this is sitting in meditation. If you are able to sit like this, it is the sitting the Buddha himself sanctions."

唯，舍利弗！不必是坐，為宴坐也。夫宴坐者，不於三界現身、意，是為宴坐；不起滅定而現諸威儀，是為宴坐；不捨道法而現凡夫事，是為宴坐；心不住內亦不在外，是為宴坐；於諸見不動，而修行三十七品，是為宴坐；不斷煩惱而入涅槃，是為宴坐。若能如是坐者，佛所印可。
Read this passage in full →

Six clauses, and every one of them has the same shape: not X, and yet Y. Not rising out of cessation, and yet showing full deportment. Not abandoning the path, and yet doing the affairs of ordinary people. Not cutting off affliction, and yet entering nirvāṇa.

What Śāriputra had been doing was going into the forest, away from ordinary affairs and affliction, and sitting. What he is handed instead is a definition of sitting that includes everything he went into the forest to leave. Not a correction. An enlargement he cannot get into.

And then: 默然而止，不能加報 — "I fell silent and could not reply."

Hold that against Part 2. The man whose own account of himself was one glance and I understood completely is, here, a man with nothing to say. That is the joke, and it is a precise one: the rebuke lands on the faculty, not on the person. Vimalakīrti does not out-argue the greatest debater alive. He describes something that a glance does not reach.

Part 5

One practice, for this week

Find the thing your glance is wrong about

Śāriputra's gift is speed: one look, complete understanding. Most capable people have a version of it in their own field — you can tell within a minute whether a document is any good, whether a candidate will work out, whether a plan is sound.

This week, find one case where that first look was wrong and you have never gone back to notice. A person you wrote off early who turned out to be somebody. A project you knew wouldn't work that did. An idea you dismissed in a sentence.

Then do the harder part, which is not self-criticism. Ask: what kind of thing is systematically invisible to a glance? Vimalakīrti's six clauses are all of one type — things that only appear over duration, in ordinary conditions, mixed with their opposites. A glance is excellent at form and useless at that. Knowing which is which is not a defect in the gift; it is the beginning of using it properly.

Part 7

One question, left open

Sit with this

The Buddha named him foremost in wisdom before he had spoken a word, and later declined to soften him because his temperament was harsh. Can you hold both of those about one person?

We are not very good at this. Someone is either an exemplar or a cautionary tale, and the tradition's own habit of keeping both — the epithet and 其性惡憋, the forty-five years of illness and the refusal that caused it — is harder to imitate than it looks. Try it on somebody you actually know, ideally somebody you admire, and notice how quickly the two halves want to collapse into one verdict. Then try it on yourself, where the collapse is faster still.
