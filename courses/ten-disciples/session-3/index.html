Part 1

Who he wasA mountain held in the air, and a bowl of rice that turned to coals

He was called Kolita, and you met him in Session 2: the other prodigy at that spring assembly, the one Śāriputra could not be separated from. They went forth together, were disappointed together, made each other a promise, and attained the first fruit of the path within hours of each other.

One note on the epithet before anything else. The Chinese anthology does not use the phrase this course puts on the card. When the Buddha points at the two friends approaching, he names Śāriputra "foremost in wisdom" and Maudgalyāyana 神足第一 — "foremost in spiritual feet." The more familiar 神通第一, "foremost in spiritual powers," is the form that settled later and is also on this site. Both are here; neither has been quietly standardised away.

What the powers actually look like is best shown by one story. A kingdom far off has a mountain blocking its roads. Five hundred ascetics with the five supernormal powers surround it, concentrate together, and begin to lift it. The Buddha sends Maudgalyāyana. He goes, "emitting light that blotted out the sun and moon," and hovers "suspended directly above the mountain's own summit — so that the mountain remained fixed, unable to move."

The ascetics try three times. Three times the mountain settles back down, until the ground is flat. Finally one of them thinks to look up, and there is a monk in the sky. They call out: the king told us to remove this thing for the people — why are you holding it down? And his answer is a joke with a straight face: 吾自懸虛誰抑汝山 — "I merely hover here in the sky — who is obstructing your own mountain?"

They ask to become his students. He refuses, and sends them to somebody better:

經律異相, Fascicle 14 · the mountain that would not move

"Maudgalyāyana told them: that you wish to leave darkness for light is well done — yet I myself have an even greater teacher, called the Unsurpassed, True, and Perfect One, heaven among heavens, possessed of all wisdom; you should all go instead to the Buddha."

目連曰。汝等欲去冥就明者善。吾有尊師。號曰無上正真天中之天。為一切智。汝等皆往到佛所。

The ascetics ask whether the Buddha's teaching really surpasses his own. The man who has just out-powered five hundred adepts without visible effort says: 佛德如須彌。吾等似芥子 — "the Buddha's own virtue is like Mount Sumeru; we ourselves are like a single mustard seed by comparison."

Hold that next to what follows, because it is the same man.

He pinned a mountain in the air with five hundred adepts pushing against it. He could not get one bowl of rice into his mother's mouth.

Newly awakened, he wanted to repay his parents. He looked, and found his mother among the hungry ghosts — no food, no drink, 皮骨相連, her skin clinging directly to her bones. So he did the obvious thing, the thing his entire reputation says he could do: he filled a bowl with rice and took it to her.

經律異相, Fascicle 14 · Ullambana

"His mother, receiving the bowl, took up the rice — but, before it even reached her mouth, it transformed into burning coals. Maudgalyāyana rushed back and reported this whole matter to the Buddha, who told him: your own mother's karmic root of sin runs too deep to be undone by your own strength alone — you will need the majestic power of the assembled sangha to free her."

母得鉢飯食未入口化成火炭。目連馳還具陳此事。佛言。汝母罪根深結。非汝一人力所奈何。當須眾僧威神之力乃得解脫。
The Ullambana Sūtra in full →

非汝一人力所奈何 — not something your single strength can do anything about. The Buddha's instruction is to gather the whole sangha on the fifteenth day of the seventh month and make the offering together.

That instruction is why, if you walk through a Chinese, Taiwanese or Vietnamese neighbourhood on that day, you will find tables of food set out in the street and paper lanterns floated on water. As this site's own note on the sūtra puts it: all of it traces back to roughly nine hundred characters of scripture — "a son who finds his mother reborn as a hungry ghost, and cannot save her by himself."

Part 2

In his own wordsWhich door he came in by

The Śūraṅgama Sūtra, Fascicle 5 · 17 · Spiritual Powers · 大目犍連

"Begging on the road, I met the three Kāśyapa brothers, who expounded the profound meaning of the Tathāgata's teaching on causation. I resolved on the path at once and attained great penetration. Traveling the ten directions without obstruction, my supernatural power was declared foremost by every buddha of the ten directions, not the World-Honored One alone. Turning the calm light of mind back on itself, letting it shine forth, as a turbid stream, over time, becomes clear and bright, is foremost."

Read all twenty-five accounts →

Two things worth noticing. First, the same discrepancy as last week, and in the same direction: the Śūraṅgama has him meeting the three Kāśyapa brothers, where the Chinese anthology has him hearing the verse second-hand from Śāriputra. The sources have not been reconciled.

Second, and more interesting: his own account of his gift is not about power at all. It is 旋湛心光 — turning the calm light of mind back on itself, and waiting, the way a muddy stream clears if you leave it alone. The man famous for doing spectacular things describes his method as sitting still until something settles.

Part 3

The Southern columnThick — and the same two men, in the same roles

The Visuddhimagga's chapter on the supernormal powers is largely his.

In the Pāli list he is foremost in psychic power, and the pairing with Sāriputta is permanent: the two chief disciples, always named together. The Visuddhimagga's ninth-chapter treatment of the kinds of supernormal power returns to him again and again.

The clearest instance is a story about an errand. The Buddha has spent the rains-retreat teaching the Abhidhamma to deities on the summit of Mount Sineru, and a crowd twelve leagues across has camped below, refusing to leave until they see him. They ask Anuruddha — Session 8 — to find out where he is, and Anuruddha locates him with the divine eye. Then they ask Mahāmoggallāna to carry their message.

"The elder, right in the midst of the assembly, dove into the great earth, bored straight through Mount Sineru, and, surfacing right at the Tathāgata's own feet while paying homage there, said to the Blessed One, 'Venerable sir, the inhabitants of Jambudīpa say, "we shall go only after we have paid homage to and seen the Blessed One's feet."'"

He is a messenger. The greatest display of power in the chapter is a man running an errand for a crowd — and the Buddha's first response is to ask after Sāriputta: "But where, Moggallāna, is your elder brother, the General of the Dhamma, at present?"

Note the Pāli epithet the Visuddhimagga gives Śāriputra in passing — 法將, the General of the Dhamma. Neither tradition can talk about one of these two for long without the other.

Visuddhimagga, Chapter 12 · Iddhividhaniddesa — Pāli with Ru-Yi's translation → · his etadagga entry (linked, not quoted)

There is one more thing the Southern sources have that this course cannot quote and should still tell you about, because it is where his story ends. Maudgalyāyana did not die peacefully. The tradition holds that he was beaten to death by hired men — the disciple foremost in psychic power, killed by people with sticks. If you want that story you will have to go elsewhere for it; it is not on this site in Ru-Yi's own translation, and this course quotes only what is.

Part 4

The rebukeHe is found teaching, in the street, to ordinary people

Vimalakīrti finds him in the city of Vaiśālī, teaching the Dharma to householders in the streets. Not a fault anyone would name — a busy monk doing outreach.

Vimalakīrti, to Mahāmaudgalyāyana

"Ah, Mahāmaudgalyāyana — teaching the Dharma to lay householders in white robes should not be done the way you, sir, have just done it. As for teaching the Dharma, one should teach it exactly as it is. The Dharma has no living beings in it, since it is free of the defilement of a living being … The Dharma has no name or word, since language is cut off in it. The Dharma admits of no statement, since it is free of thought and reflection … Ah, Mahāmaudgalyāyana, the mark of the Dharma is like this — how could it be spoken? Whoever teaches the Dharma neither states it nor demonstrates it; whoever listens to the Dharma neither hears it nor obtains anything from it."

唯，大目連！為白衣居士說法，不當如仁者所說。夫說法者，當如法說。法無眾生，離眾生垢故……法無名字，言語斷故；法無有說，離覺觀故……唯，大目連！法相如是，豈可說乎？夫說法者，無說無示；其聽法者，無聞無得……
Read this passage in full →

It is a long list — more than thirty clauses of what the Dharma is not — and it arrives at a question that sounds like it should end the possibility of teaching altogether: how could it be spoken?

But read the last sentence of the rebuke, which is easy to lose after all that negation. Having dismantled teaching, Vimalakīrti immediately says how to do it: "One should understand whether the faculties of one's listeners are sharp or dull" — then praise the Great Vehicle with a mind of great compassion, and only then teach.

Kumārajīva's note on the page makes the actual fault concrete, and it is not what it looks like. These particular householders' faculties were sharp enough to hear a teaching on emptiness directly; Maudgalyāyana, not yet able to discern this, defaulted to something simpler. He was not teaching wrongly. He was teaching down.

And then the ending, which is either a rebuke or a demonstration depending on how you read it: 八百居士發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心 — when Vimalakīrti finished, eight hundred householders raised the mind of unsurpassed awakening. Maudgalyāyana's summary of why he cannot go: 我無此辯, "I do not have such eloquence."

Notice which faculty is at issue. Not his power — nobody in this sūtra disputes that he can bore through a mountain. The charge is that he misjudged who was in front of him, which is exactly the thing no amount of supernormal power will tell you.

Part 5

One practice, for this week

Find the person you are teaching down to

Pick one person you regularly explain things to — a student, a colleague, a child, a parent, someone you manage. Ask the uncomfortable question: am I giving them the simple version because that is what they need, or because it is easier for me?

Then run the test Vimalakīrti names. Give them the real version once — the actual complexity, the thing you would say to a peer — and watch what happens. Not a lecture; one exchange.

Two outcomes, and both are useful. They may take it, in which case you have been underestimating someone for a long time. Or they may not, in which case you now know from evidence rather than assumption — which is the whole of Vimalakīrti's instruction: 當了眾生根有利鈍, understand whether the faculties in front of you are sharp or dull. Note that he does not say "always teach the highest thing." He says find out first.

Part 7

One question, left open

Sit with this

What is the thing you are best at, and who is the person it has never once helped?

This is the shape of his whole story and it is not a story about failure. He really could hold a mountain against five hundred adepts. The powers are never withdrawn, never debunked, never described as a distraction — and they were simply, flatly irrelevant to his mother's mouth. Most competent people have one of these: a domain where the thing that works everywhere else does nothing, and where the only available move is the one the Buddha gave him, which was to stop trying alone and ask everybody. Notice how much harder that instruction is than any of the miracles.
