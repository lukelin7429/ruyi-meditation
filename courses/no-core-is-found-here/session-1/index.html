Session 1 · The doctrinal anchor, stated three times in a row

The Threefold Refrain

法句經第二十品 — 三句偈，同一句結尾
Part 1

Lecture Notes

The doctrinal center of the whole collection

The Magga Vagga ("The Path") is one of the doctrinal centers of the entire Dhammapada. It opens by declaring the eightfold path the best of paths and the four truths the best of truths, and states flatly that this is the only path to purified vision. Few chapters carry this much doctrinal weight in so few verses.

You must do the walking

Verse 276 delivers what may be the single most quoted line in the whole collection: the practitioner, not the Buddha, must do the actual work — "the Realized Ones just show the way." This matters as the frame for everything that follows in this course: the three marks this session introduces are not a belief to adopt secondhand, but a claim meant to be verified by walking the path yourself.

Three verses, one identical ending

Then comes the refrain this whole course is built around — three verses in a row, each stating one mark of existence, each ending on the identical line, "this is the path to purity": all conditions are impermanent (277); all conditions are suffering (278); all things are not-self (279). The shift in scope between the third verse and the first two is easy to miss and worth pausing on — impermanence and suffering are said of all conditions (saṅkhārā, things put together by causes), while not-self is said of all things (dhammā) without that qualifier, extending even to the unconditioned.

A refrain meant to be examined, not recited

The identical closing line — repeated three times rather than varied — is itself part of the teaching method. This isn't three separate claims that happen to share a conclusion; it's one path approached from three different angles, arriving at the same place each time. The rest of this course spends a full session on each angle in turn, testing whether the compressed verse actually holds up under close examination.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The three marks are meant to be walked, not merely believed. Verse 276's "the Realized Ones just show the way" frames everything the rest of this course examines — this is a claim to verify, not a doctrine to accept secondhand.
  • The scope shifts between the first two marks and the third. Impermanence and suffering are said of all conditions (caused, compounded things); not-self is said of all things, without that qualifier — a distinction worth holding onto.
  • One path, three angles, the identical destination. The repeated closing line isn't padding — it's the claim that three different examinations arrive at the same place.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Verse 276 insists the practitioner must do the actual work — "the Realized Ones just show the way." What would it mean, concretely, to treat the three marks that follow as something to verify yourself rather than a doctrine to simply accept?
  2. The scope shifts between "all conditions" (impermanence, suffering) and "all things" (not-self). Why might not-self extend further than the other two marks? What would it mean for something unconditioned to still be not-self?
  3. The identical closing line repeats three times rather than varying. What effect does that repetition have on you as a reader, compared to if each verse had ended differently?
  4. This session deliberately opens the course with compressed verse rather than a fuller prose explanation. What do you expect from a verse this compressed — is it meant to be immediately clear, or to raise questions the rest of the course will need to answer?

Option A — State the refrain in your own words

Write out, in plain language and without any Buddhist technical terms, what each of the three verses (277, 278, 279) is actually claiming. Where does your paraphrase feel confident, and where does it feel like you're only repeating words you don't yet fully understand?

Option B — Verify, don't accept

Pick one of the three marks and describe what it would concretely mean, in your own life this week, to test it rather than simply agree with it in the abstract.
