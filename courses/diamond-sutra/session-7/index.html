Session 7 · Sections 25–28

Form and sound, and a mistaken path

The one place Subhūti answers wrongly — and the correction that follows
Reading time about thirty-five minutes Before class Sessions 1–6 The text Diamond Sūtra, sections 25–28
Part 1

Section 25The vow, dismantled from the other end

Section 25Saving No One At All化無所化分

"Subhūti, what do you think — do not say that the Tathāgata has the thought, 'I should save all beings.' Subhūti, do not think this way. Why is this? There is in truth no being that the Tathāgata saves. If there were a being that the Tathāgata saves, the Tathāgata would hold a self, a person, a being, a lifespan. Subhūti, the Tathāgata says, 'a self exists' is no existing self at all — it is only ordinary people who take there to be a self. Subhūti, what the Tathāgata calls an 'ordinary person' is no ordinary person at all — that is why they are called an ordinary person."

「須菩提！於意云何？汝等勿謂如來作是念：『我當度眾生。』須菩提！莫作是念。何以故？實無有眾生如來度者。若有眾生如來度者，如來則有我、人、眾生、壽者。須菩提！如來說：『有我者，則非有我，而凡夫之人以為有我。』須菩提！凡夫者，如來說則非凡夫，是名凡夫。」

Read this section in its place — Diamond Sūtra, sections 25–28 →

Section 3 removed the self from the bodhisattva who vows to liberate beings. Section 25 removes it from the Buddha, and gives the reason with unusual directness: if there were a being that the Tathāgata saves, the Tathāgata would hold a self, a person, a being, a lifespan. The four notions come as a set, and saving someone requires a saver and a saved.

Then the last line, which is one of the kindest sentences in the sūtra and is easy to read past: what the Tathāgata calls an "ordinary person" is no ordinary person at all — that is why they are called an ordinary person.

The formula has been applied to buddhas, to teachings, to merit, to marks, to beings. Here it lands on the category you are in. Whatever "ordinary person" names, it is not a fixed condition anyone is stuck in — and that is precisely why the word works.

Part 2

Section 26Subhūti gets one wrong

In twenty-five sections Subhūti has not put a foot wrong. He has anticipated the Buddha's reasoning, supplied the formula unprompted, and been confirmed. Then this.

Section 26The Dharma-Body Has No Mark法身非相分

"Subhūti, what do you think — can the Tathāgata be seen through the thirty-two marks?" Subhūti said: "So it is, so it is — the Tathāgata is seen through the thirty-two marks." The Buddha said: "Subhūti, if the Tathāgata could be seen through the thirty-two marks, then a wheel-turning king would be a Tathāgata too." Subhūti said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as I understand what the Buddha has taught, the Tathāgata should not be seen through the thirty-two marks."

「須菩提！於意云何？可以三十二相觀如來不？」須菩提言：「如是！如是！以三十二相觀如來。」佛言：「須菩提！若以三十二相觀如來者，轉輪聖王則是如來。」須菩提白佛言：「世尊！如我解佛所說義。不應以三十二相觀如來。」

Read this section in its place →

The same question he has answered correctly twice — in section 5 and section 13 — and this time he says yes.

The correction is a single clean argument. A wheel-turning king (轉輪聖王), the ideal universal monarch of Indian cosmology, also possesses the thirty-two marks. If the marks identified a tathāgata, every such king would be one. The marks are therefore not sufficient — which is all the argument needs.

Why does the text include a mistake? Two things are worth saying. First, it is honest about how understanding actually behaves: Subhūti has not become incapable of error by section 26, and a teaching held correctly on Tuesday can be held wrongly on Wednesday. Second, and more pointedly — the reader has been watching Subhūti give the right answer for twenty-five sections and may well have begun to supply it automatically. The moment he slips is the moment that habit is exposed.

Taixu's lecture notes, 1927 — as rendered on this site

"Caught out, Subhūti corrects himself at once — Taixu reads the moment as a small, honest demonstration of exactly how easily even an advanced disciple can slide back into taking a mark for the reality behind it, useful precisely because it shows the error happening in real time rather than only being warned against in the abstract."

Then the verse, which Chan treated as the sūtra's centre of gravity.

Section 26continued — the verse法身非相分

At that time the World-Honored One spoke this verse: "Whoever sees me through form, whoever seeks me through sound, that person walks a mistaken path and cannot see the Tathāgata."

爾時，世尊而說偈言：「若以色見我，以音聲求我，是人行邪道，不能見如來。」

Read this section in its place →

若以色見我，以音聲求我，是人行邪道，不能見如來.

Twenty characters, and they are aimed squarely at devotion — at images, at chanting, at the whole sensory apparatus of religious practice. 邪道, a mistaken path, is a strong phrase, and the sūtra does not soften it.

But notice what it says and what it does not. It does not say images are worthless or that chanting should stop. It says that seeking the Tathāgata through them is looking in the wrong place — the same claim as section 5, now put as an instruction about where to direct attention. And note where the verse sits: immediately after section 12 declared that any place where four lines of this sūtra are spoken deserves the veneration given to a stūpa. The text is not hostile to reverence. It is specific about what reverence can and cannot find.

Part 3

Section 27The warning, stated in the sūtra's own voice

Section 27Neither Cut Off Nor Annihilated無斷無滅分

"Subhūti, if you have the thought, 'The Tathāgata did not attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening because he lacked perfectly formed marks' — Subhūti, do not think this way, that the Tathāgata did not attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening because he lacked perfectly formed marks." "Subhūti, if you have the thought that one who sets out for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening teaches the annihilation of all things — do not think this way. Why is this? One who sets out for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening does not teach that anything is cut off or annihilated."

「須菩提！汝若作是念：『如來不以具足相故，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』須菩提！莫作是念：『如來不以具足相故，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』」「須菩提！汝若作是念，發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心者，說諸法斷滅。莫作是念！何以故？發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心者。於法不說斷滅相。」

Read this section in its place →

This section exists for one reason: the reader has just been told, in the strongest terms, that the marks are not it. The obvious next step is to swing to the opposite — so the marks are an obstacle; so awakening comes from lacking them; so this teaching is about cancelling things. Section 27 blocks both, and 斷滅, cut off and annihilated, is the technical term for exactly the nihilism the Madhyamaka course spent Session 6 on.

The pattern should be familiar by now. Section 6 said do not grasp at a teaching and do not grasp at a non-teaching. Section 21 refused both "beings" and "non-beings." Here, negation is refused as firmly as affirmation was.

One who sets out for awakening does not teach that anything is cut off or annihilated.

It is worth registering how consistently the three Layer 4 electives converge on this one point. The Cheng Weishi Lun warns in fascicle 2 that clinging to Consciousness-Only is itself attachment. Nāgārjuna's commentator opens by saying the treatise was written for people who seized on emptiness and gave rise to craving. Jizang says the medicine is set aside when the sickness goes. And here, the Diamond Sūtra, the oldest of the three, stops mid-argument to say: do not hear this as annihilation. Four texts, four centuries apart, expecting the same misreading.

Part 4

Section 28Not receiving what you have made

Section 28Neither Received Nor Craved不受不貪分

"Subhūti, if a bodhisattva filled as many worlds as there are grains of sand in the Ganges with the seven treasures and gave it all away; and if another bodhisattva came to know that all things are without self, and thereby attained forbearance — this second bodhisattva's merit would surpass the first's. Why is this? Subhūti, because bodhisattvas do not receive merit." Subhūti said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what does it mean that a bodhisattva does not receive merit?" "Subhūti, a bodhisattva should not crave or cling to the merit they create. This is why it is said that they do not receive merit."

「須菩提！若菩薩以滿恆河沙等世界七寶持用布施；若復有人，知一切法無我，得成於忍，此菩薩勝前菩薩所得功德。何以故？須菩提！以諸菩薩不受福德故。」須菩提白佛言：「世尊！云何菩薩不受福德？」「須菩提！菩薩所作福德，不應貪著。是故說不受福德。」

Read this section in its place →

Subhūti asks for clarification here, which he rarely does — 不受福德, do not receive merit, sounds like a claim that the merit does not arrive. And the answer deflates it completely: a bodhisattva should not crave or cling to the merit they create. That is all "not receiving" means.

Note carefully: 菩薩所作福德 — the merit they create. The merit is made, and it is theirs in the ordinary sense. What is ruled out is the craving, not the merit. This is section 4's instruction — give without the mind landing on the merit — restated as a definition twenty-four sections later.

And the thing being compared is worth noting too: not giving against another kind of giving, but giving against knowing that all things are without self, and thereby attaining forbearance. 忍, forbearance — the capacity to bear what is the case without flinching from it. The same word that named the sage of patience in section 14.

What Session 7 establishes
  • The Buddha saves no one — because saving requires a saver and a saved, and the four notions come as a set.
  • "Ordinary person" is not a fixed condition, and that is why the word works.
  • Subhūti answers wrongly, and the slip exposes the reader's habit of supplying the right answer automatically.
  • A wheel-turning king has the thirty-two marks too — which is all the argument needs.
  • Form and sound are the wrong place to look, not worthless — the sūtra is specific rather than hostile to reverence.
  • Negation is refused as firmly as affirmation. Nothing is cut off or annihilated.
  • "Not receiving merit" means not craving it. The merit is made, and is theirs.
Part 5

One practiceCatching the automatic answer

Where is your Subhūti moment?

Section 26 is the sūtra's own demonstration that fluency and understanding come apart. It is worth taking personally.

Set twenty minutes aside. Write out, from memory and in your own words, what this sūtra teaches. Do not consult it.

Then read what you wrote, looking for one thing only: the sentences you produced without checking them against anything — the phrases that arrived fully formed because you have read them several times. Mark them.

Take the most confident one and do what the Buddha did to Subhūti: find the counter-case. If it is true, what else would have to be true? Is there a wheel-turning king in the picture somewhere?

The purpose is not to catch yourself in error. It is to notice the difference between a sentence you can produce and a sentence you can defend — which is the difference section 26 dramatises in the one person who should have been past it.

One question to sit with

Which of your answers about this teaching have you stopped checking?

Subhūti had twenty-five sections of being right before he was wrong, and nothing in the text suggests he was careless. The sūtra includes the slip rather than editing it out, which is a choice — and the most likely reason is that its readers were expected to recognise themselves in it.
