Session 3 · Sections 9–12

A mind that dwells nowhere

Four attainments nobody claims, and the sentence that woke Huineng
Reading time about thirty-five minutes Before class Sessions 1–2 The text Diamond Sūtra, sections 9–12
Part 1

Section 9Four attainments, and nobody who has them

Section 9 applies the formula to the four fruits of the path — stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, arhat — and it does so by asking, of each, whether the person could have the thought I have attained this.

Section 9One Mark Is No Mark一相無相分

"Subhūti, what do you think — can a stream-enterer have the thought, 'I have attained the fruit of stream-entry'?" Subhūti said: "No, World-Honored One. Why is this? 'Stream-enterer' means one who has entered the stream, yet there is nothing entered — not entering form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental object. That is why they are called a stream-enterer."

「須菩提！於意云何？須陀洹能作是念：『我得須陀洹果』不？」須菩提言：「不也，世尊！何以故？須陀洹名為入流，而無所入；不入色、聲、香、味、觸、法，是名須陀洹。」

Read this section in its place — Diamond Sūtra, sections 9–12 →

The reasoning is a pun that happens to be true. "Stream-enterer" means one who has entered, and what such a person has actually done is stop entering — stopped being drawn into form, sound, smell, taste, touch, thought. The name describes an entering, and the reality is a not-entering. The formula again: entered the stream — nothing entered — that is why they are called a stream-enterer.

Then the section does something the sūtra rarely does. It stops speaking in the third person, and Subhūti testifies about himself.

Section 9continued — Subhūti's own case一相無相分

"World-Honored One, the Buddha has said that I have attained the state free of contention and am foremost among people — the foremost arhat free of desire. But World-Honored One, I do not have the thought, 'I am an arhat free of desire.' World-Honored One, if I had the thought, 'I have attained the path of arhatship,' the World-Honored One would not say that Subhūti is one who delights in the practice of stillness. It is only because Subhūti in truth practices nothing at all that he is called Subhūti, one who delights in the practice of stillness."

「世尊！佛說我得無諍三昧，人中最為第一，是第一離欲阿羅漢。世尊！我不作是念：『我是離欲阿羅漢。』世尊！我若作是念：『我得阿羅漢道。』世尊則不說須菩提是樂阿蘭那行者。以須菩提實無所行，而名須菩提，是樂阿蘭那行。」

Read this section in its place →

This is the strongest form the argument can take, because it is not hypothetical. Subhūti has the attainment; the Buddha has publicly said so; and Subhūti's point is that the moment he thought I have it, the Buddha would stop saying it.

And notice the reason he gives — 實無所行, in truth practices nothing at all. Not that he is modest about his practice. That there is no findable practising, and it is precisely on that ground that the title fits.

The claim and the attainment cannot both be present. Not because claiming is impolite — because claiming installs the one who claims.
Part 2

Section 10Nothing received, and a land adorned by no adorning

Section 10Adorning the Buddha-Land莊嚴淨土分

The Buddha told Subhūti: "What do you think — long ago, in the presence of Dīpaṃkara Buddha, did the Tathāgata attain anything in the teaching?" "No, World-Honored One. In the presence of Dīpaṃkara Buddha, the Tathāgata in truth attained nothing in the teaching." "Subhūti, what do you think — does a bodhisattva adorn a buddha-land?" "No, World-Honored One. Why is this? To adorn a buddha-land is no adorning at all — that is why it is called adorning."

佛告須菩提：「於意云何？如來昔在燃燈佛所，於法有所得不？」「不也，世尊！如來在燃燈佛所，於法實無所得。」「須菩提！於意云何？菩薩莊嚴佛土不？」「不也，世尊！何以故？莊嚴佛土者，即非莊嚴，是名莊嚴。」

Read this section in its place →

Dīpaṃkara is the buddha under whom, in the tradition, the future Śākyamuni received his prediction of awakening — the single most important transaction in his career across all his lives. And the sūtra says nothing was received.

The second question is aimed at something readers of Mahāyāna literature will recognise: the buddha-lands, described in other sūtras in extraordinary sensory detail — jewelled trees, lakes of the seven treasures, birds teaching the Dharma. Adorning one is a bodhisattva's classical work. Here the formula lands on it too, and if you have been following since section 8 you can hear the third step doing its job: that is why it is called adorning. The work is not cancelled. It is the naming that has been adjusted.

Taixu's lecture notes, 1927 — as rendered on this site

"What Dīpaṃkara spoke and what the future Buddha heard, Taixu answers, were both only language, with no fixed essence behind either; giving and receiving a prediction is itself just more of the "no giver, no gift" pattern already taught."

Part 3

The line that woke HuinengTen characters, in the middle of section 10

Section 10continued — the pure mind莊嚴淨土分

"For this reason, Subhūti, all bodhisattva-mahāsattvas should give rise to a pure mind like this: they should not give rise to a mind dwelling on form, nor give rise to a mind dwelling on sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental object. They should give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere at all." "Subhūti, suppose there were a person whose body was as great as Mount Sumeru, king of mountains — what do you think, would that body be large?" Subhūti said: "Very large, World-Honored One. Why is this? The Buddha speaks of no-body — that is why it is called a great body."

「是故，須菩提！諸菩薩摩訶薩，應如是生清淨心，不應住色生心，不應住聲、香、味、觸、法生心，應無所住而生其心。」「須菩提！譬如有人，身如須彌山王，於意云何？是身為大不？」須菩提言：「甚大，世尊！何以故？佛說非身，是名大身。」

Read this section in its place →

應無所住而生其心 — they should give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere at all.

This is the most consequential sentence in the history of Chinese Buddhism, and it is worth knowing why. In the traditional account, Huineng — an illiterate firewood seller — heard a stranger reciting this line in a marketplace, and that was the beginning of everything that became Chan. He later received the robe of the Sixth Patriarch after hearing the same line again. Whatever one makes of the account, the effect on the tradition is not in doubt: the Diamond Sūtra displaced the Laṅkāvatāra as Chan's central text, and it did so on the strength of these ten characters.

What makes it work is the second half. A great many teachings will tell you to let go, to stop clinging, to rest. This one says give rise to a mind — 生其心, actively produce it — that dwells nowhere. Not a blank mind and not a withdrawn one. A mind that arises, engages, does the day's work, and does not land.

Read against section 1, the shape of the course becomes visible. The Buddha dressing, begging in order, eating, washing his feet: that was a mind arising and not landing, shown before it was described. Nine sections later the sūtra puts it in words.

And then, immediately, Mount Sumeru — because the sūtra never lets a good line sit. A body the size of the world-mountain is large because the Buddha calls it no-body. The formula, applied to size itself.

Part 4

Sections 11 and 12Sand, and why the text's location is a shrine

Section 11The Merit of the Unconditioned Surpasses無為福勝分

"Subhūti, if there were as many Ganges Rivers as there are grains of sand in the Ganges, what do you think — would the grains of sand in all those Ganges Rivers be many?" Subhūti said: "Very many, World-Honored One. Just the Ganges Rivers themselves would be beyond counting, how much more so their grains of sand."

「須菩提！如恆河中所有沙數，如是沙等恆河，於意云何？是諸恆河沙，寧為多不？」須菩提言：「甚多，世尊！但諸恆河，尚多無數，何況其沙。」

Read this section in its place →

The comparison that follows — fill that many universes with treasure and give it away, and four lines of this sūtra explained to someone else surpasses it — is the sūtra's most repeated device, and it can read as promotional. It is worth taking it as an argument instead.

Everything on the giving side of the comparison is countable. Enormous, unimaginable, and in principle finite: sand can be counted in principle, universes can be counted in principle, treasure can be weighed. What is on the other side is not a bigger quantity. It is the thing that has no fixed nature to be quantified, which section 8 already established is why such merit cannot be measured. The comparison is not much against more. It is measurable against unmeasurable, and no amount of the first reaches the second.

Section 12Honoring the True Teaching尊重正教分

"Furthermore, Subhūti, wherever this sūtra is spoken, even a single four-line verse of it, know that this place should be venerated by every being in every world — gods, humans, and asuras alike — as they would venerate a buddha's stūpa or shrine. How much more so where someone can uphold, recite, and fully embody it. Subhūti, know that such a person accomplishes the highest, foremost, rarest teaching. And wherever this sūtra is found, there the Buddha is present, honored among his disciples."

「復次，須菩提！隨說是經，乃至四句偈等，當知此處，一切世間，天、人、阿修羅，皆應供養，如佛塔廟。何況有人，盡能受持讀誦。須菩提！當知是人成就最上第一希有之法，若是經典所在之處，即為有佛，若尊重弟子。」

Read this section in its place →

A stūpa contains relics — the physical remains of a buddha, the most venerated objects in the early tradition. Section 12 says that any place where four lines of this text are spoken has the same standing.

Which is, on reflection, consistent with section 5 rather than a departure from it. If the Tathāgata cannot be seen by his bodily marks, then bodily remains are not where he is to be found either. Wherever this sūtra is found, there the Buddha is present — and by this sūtra's own logic, that is not a lesser presence than a relic. It is a more accurate one.

What Session 3 establishes
  • "Stream-enterer" names an entering that is a not-entering — and the four fruits each fail the same way.
  • Subhūti testifies about himself. He has the attainment, and says that thinking so would end it.
  • Nothing was received from Dīpaṃkara — not even the prediction of awakening.
  • A mind that dwells nowhere is given rise to, not merely rested in. Active, engaged, and not landing.
  • The merit comparison is measurable against unmeasurable, not much against more.
  • Where the sūtra is, the Buddha is — which follows from section 5 rather than departing from it.
Part 5

One practiceArising, and not landing

Give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere

The line that woke Huineng is an instruction, and it is not an instruction to meditate. Both halves have to be kept: a mind that arises, and a mind that does not dwell.

Choose one ordinary task tomorrow that takes twenty minutes and requires real attention — cooking something, writing an email that matters, repairing a thing, listening to a person.

Do it at full engagement. Do not try to be detached; detachment is the wrong half. Care about doing it well.

And watch for the landing: the moment the mind stops being with the task and comes to rest on something about it — how it is going, how you are doing, what it will mean, whether you are the sort of person who does this well. When you catch it, do not correct it. Return to the task, and note that the returning cost nothing.

The failure mode worth naming: doing the task in a deliberately distant, half-present way and calling that non-dwelling. That is a mind that has not arisen. The sūtra asks for the harder thing — full engagement with nowhere to come to rest.

One question to sit with

What would you lose if you stopped keeping the account of how you are doing?

Subhūti's answer, and the sūtra's, is: only the account. The practice, the attainment and the title all survive the loss of the record. It is the record that cannot survive being kept.
