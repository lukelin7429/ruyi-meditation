Session 4 · Sections 13–16

The sūtra names itself, and Subhūti weeps

A title that undoes itself, and a body cut apart without anger
Reading time about thirty-five minutes Before class Sessions 1–3 The text Diamond Sūtra, sections 13–16
Part 1

Section 13A title, and its immediate withdrawal

Halfway through, Subhūti asks a practical question — what is this called, and how are we to carry it? — and the answer is the sūtra's most compressed self-description.

Section 13Upholding It As Taught如法受持分

At that time Subhūti said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what should this sūtra be called? How should we hold and carry it?" The Buddha told Subhūti: "This sūtra is named the Diamond Perfection of Wisdom. By this name you should hold and carry it. Why is that? Subhūti, what the Buddha calls the Perfection of Wisdom is no Perfection of Wisdom — that is why it is called the Perfection of Wisdom.

爾時，須菩提白佛言：「世尊！當何名此經？我等云何奉持？」佛告須菩提：「是經名為金剛般若波羅蜜，以是名字，汝當奉持。所以者何？須菩提！佛說般若波羅蜜，即非般若波羅蜜。是名般若波羅蜜。

Read this section in its place — Diamond Sūtra, sections 13–16 →

The title is given and taken back in one breath, and both halves of the instruction stand: by this name you should hold and carry it, and what the Buddha calls the Perfection of Wisdom is no Perfection of Wisdom.

This is the third step of the formula doing something no other instance of it does: applying to the text you are currently reading. The sūtra names itself, un-names itself, and tells you to go on using the name anyway. If you can hold both of those at once you have understood the formula; if one of them has to go, you have not.

The section then returns to the thirty-two marks from section 5, this time with the full formula attached — the thirty-two marks are no marks at all, that is why they are called the thirty-two marks. Section 5 gave the first two steps; section 13 supplies the third. The sūtra is repeating itself on purpose, and each repetition adds one turn.

Part 2

Section 14The one place in the sūtra where somebody cries

Section 14Beyond All Marks, Utter Stillness離相寂滅分

At that time, hearing this sūtra taught, Subhūti understood its meaning deeply, and, weeping, said to the Buddha: "How rare, World-Honored One! Since I first gained the eye of wisdom, I have never before heard a sūtra as profound as this. World-Honored One, if someone hears this sūtra with a pure and believing mind, true reality will arise in them, and know that this person accomplishes the rarest merit."

爾時，須菩提聞說是經，深解義趣，涕淚悲泣，而白佛言：「希有世尊！佛說如是甚深經典，我從昔來所得慧眼，未曾得聞如是之經。世尊！若復有人，得聞是經，信心清淨，則生實相，當知是人，成就第一希有功德。」

Read this section in its place →

涕淚悲泣 — weeping. It is the only moment of visible emotion in the whole text, and it belongs to the disciple foremost in understanding emptiness, who has been calmly supplying the right answers for twelve sections.

Worth sitting with, because it complicates a reading the sūtra otherwise invites. Nothing here is cold. The man who best understands this material, on hearing it stated fully, does not nod. He cries — and says that in a very long life of insight he has never heard anything like it.

His phrase for what happens to a person who hears it with a pure and believing mind is 生實相, true reality will arise in them. Not: they will understand a doctrine.

Part 3

King KalīWhy the story is told, and why it is not told to impress

Section 14continued — the sage of patience離相寂滅分

"Why is this? Subhūti, long ago when King Kalī cut my body limb from limb, I held no notion of a self, no notion of a person, no notion of a being, no notion of a lifespan. Why do I say this? Because if, as my limbs were cut away piece by piece, I had held the notion of a self, a person, a being, a lifespan, I would surely have given rise to anger and resentment. Subhūti, I also recall that in ages past, for five hundred lives, I was a sage who practiced patience — and through all those lives I held no notion of a self, a person, a being, a lifespan."

「何以故？須菩提！如我昔為歌利王割截身體，我於爾時，無我相、無人相、無眾生相、無壽者相。何以故？我於往昔，節節支解時，若有我相、人相、眾生相、壽者相，應生瞋恨。須菩提！又念過去，於五百世，作忍辱仙人，於爾所世，無我相、無人相、無眾生相、無壽者相。」

Read this section in its place →

The story is old and was well known to the sūtra's first hearers: in a former life, the future Buddha was a sage practising patience; a king, enraged, dismembered him; he felt no anger.

What matters is the direction of the reasoning, and it is easy to get backwards. The passage is not saying: he was so patient that he did not get angry. It says the opposite. Because there was no notion of a self there, anger had nothing to arise around. The absence of the four notions is the cause; the absence of anger is the consequence.

Not patience heroically maintained. No one there for the injury to be an injury to.

This is why the story sits here and not in a collection of edifying tales. It is offered as evidence — the sūtra's claim is that the four notions are not merely a philosophical error but the thing that converts an event into an outrage, and this is the most extreme available test case. The limbs are cut either way. What changes is whether there is a someone for it to be happening to.

And then the second sentence, which quietly rescales the first: five hundred lives as a sage of patience. The dismemberment was not a single heroic performance. It was the outcome of something practised for an unimaginably long time. The sūtra is not suggesting a reader could do this tomorrow.

Taixu's lecture notes, 1927 — as rendered on this site

"On King Kalī's cruelty, Taixu adds a detail easy to miss on a fast read: in that past life as the patient sage, endlessly wronged, the bodhisattva held not only no anger but made a private vow toward his tormentor — "when I attain the path, I will be the first to liberate you." The absence of resentment was never numbness; it was patience with nowhere left for anger to grab hold, because there was no fixed self left standing behind the pain to defend."

Part 4

Sections 15 and 16Bodies given away, and being treated with contempt

Section 15The Merit of Upholding This Sūtra持經功德分

"Subhūti, if a good son or good daughter gave up as many bodies as there are grains of sand in the Ganges in the morning, gave up as many bodies again at midday, and gave up as many bodies again in the evening, and kept giving up bodies like this for countless hundreds of thousands of millions of aeons; and if someone else, on hearing this sūtra, believed it without resistance — this person's merit would surpass the first, how much more so one who copies it, upholds it, recites it, and explains it to others."

「須菩提！若有善男子、善女人，初日分以恆河沙等身布施，中日分復以恆河沙等身布施，後日分亦以恆河沙等身布施，如是無量百千萬億劫，以身布施；若復有人，聞此經典，信心不逆，其福勝彼，何況書寫、受持、讀誦、為人解說。」

Read this section in its place →

The comparison has escalated from treasure to bodies, but the logic is the one Session 3 identified: everything on the first side is countable, however vast. And note the modesty of what is asked on the other side — not understanding, not realisation. 信心不逆, believed it without resistance. Merely not flinching away from it.

Then section 16, which is the strangest passage in the sūtra and the most practically useful.

Section 16Purifying Karmic Obstruction能淨業障分

"Furthermore, Subhūti, if a good son or good daughter upholds and recites this sūtra and is treated with contempt, that is because of wrongdoing from a former life which should have brought them down into a lower realm; but because they are treated with contempt in this present life, the wrongdoing of that former life is thereby dissolved, and they will attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening."

「復次，須菩提！善男子、善女人，受持讀誦此經，若為人輕賤，是人先世罪業，應墮惡道，以今世人輕賤故，先世罪業則為消滅，當得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。」

Read this section in its place →

This is the sūtra at its most concrete, and it is addressed to an experience its readers evidently had: you take this teaching seriously, and people think less of you for it.

It is worth being careful here, because the passage can be read badly. It is not a licence to interpret every slight as spiritual progress, and it is certainly not a reason to seek contempt. What it says is narrower: that being thought little of, by someone upholding this text, is not evidence that the practice has failed. The passage reframes a specific experience that would otherwise be read as failure.

And there is a structural reason it belongs immediately after King Kalī. The previous section described the most extreme injury imaginable and located the whole question in whether there was a self for it to be an injury to. This section takes the smallest, most ordinary injury — someone thinking less of you — and puts it in the same frame. The scale is incomparable; the analysis is identical.

What Session 4 establishes
  • The formula applies to the text itself. Named, un-named, and to be carried by that name anyway.
  • Repetition adds a turn. Section 5 gave two steps of the thirty-two marks; section 13 supplies the third.
  • Subhūti weeps — the only visible emotion in the sūtra, from the disciple who understands it best.
  • King Kalī runs the reasoning forwards, not backwards. No self, therefore no anger — not heroic patience.
  • Five hundred lives. The sūtra rescales its own example rather than presenting it as reachable.
  • Contempt is reframed, not sought. Being thought little of is not evidence that the practice has failed.
Part 5

One practiceThe smallest version of King Kalī

Who is it happening to?

Sections 14 and 16 apply one analysis at two scales. The extreme end is not available to anyone reading this. The ordinary end is available constantly.

For one week, watch for the small slight: being talked over, being corrected publicly, being left off something, someone's tone.

When one lands, do not manage it and do not reframe it. Ask one question, and ask it about the sting rather than about the person: what is this happening to? Look for the thing being damaged. A standing, a picture, a position.

Then notice whether the event and the sting are separable — whether the words said to you and the injury they constituted are in fact the same item.

Do not expect the sting to go. Section 14's whole point is that it goes only where the four notions have already gone, and section 14 puts that after five hundred lives of practice. What is available now is smaller and still worth having: seeing that the event and the injury are two things and not one.

One question to sit with

The last time something stung, what exactly was damaged?

Section 14 claims that when you look carefully the answer is always a notion rather than a person, and that where the notion is absent no damage occurs. It also says this took five hundred lives. Both halves are the sūtra's, and leaving out either one misrepresents it.
