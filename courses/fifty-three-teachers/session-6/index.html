The king who appears to be cruel

The second of the three teachers who wear a poison openly — and the argument this course least wants to make easy
Reading time about twenty-five minutes Before class Session 3 especially The text Part 37 of 454
Part 1

Who you'll meetA king on a vajra throne, and a scene the boy reads wrongly

Teacher 17 of 53 King Fulfillment 滿足王 a king, in the city called Full Banner Part 37 of 454

Sudhana asks where the king is and is told: in the main hall, administering the law. Gathering in whoever should be gathered in, punishing whoever should be punished, settling disputes, granting fearlessness to the frightened, and praising the ten wholesome ways — not killing, not stealing, not lying, and the rest.

Then he walks in, and sees what the administration of the law looks like. Bodies bound in five places. Hands and feet severed. Ears and noses cut off. Eyes gouged out. People thrown into boiling ash, or wrapped in oiled cloth and set alight.

此乃惡中之惡，第一惡人 — "this is evil among evils, the very worst of evil men." It is the harshest thing Sudhana says about anyone in four hundred and fifty pages, and he says it about a teacher.

He is wrong, and the episode explains why, and the explanation is the most dangerous single doctrine in the entire pilgrimage. This session sets it out in full, with the safeguards the text itself supplies, and then says plainly where those safeguards run out.

Before you read: what is actually at stake
  • This is the second of three. In Session 3, Fazang named three teachers whose outward appearance matches one of the three poisons directly — the ascetic resembling wrong view, a king resembling wrath, and a courtesan resembling craving. This is the king. The courtesan is Session 9.
  • 迹違行順 — Fazang's formula for what this teacher is: "his outward trace runs contrary to the Way while his underlying practice accords with it." He adds that no lower stage of the path could measure such a person by appearances at all, which should worry you slightly, and is meant to.
  • The doctrine in the room. Fazang quotes a treatise holding that a great bodhisattva may perform even the gravest acts, killing included, without fault. He then makes a distinction — one this course will hold onto very tightly — between actually doing it and conjuring the appearance of doing it, and places this king entirely in the second category.

The narrative is short. The reading notes are not, and this is one of the few sessions where the notes matter as much as the sūtra — Fazang quotes Paramārtha's sixth-century treatise on the precepts at length, precisely because he knows what this passage looks like.

Part 3

Three moments, read closely

1. What he sees, and what he concludes.

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 37 · the court, and Sudhana's verdict

"At that time Sudhana beheld countless sentient beings who had transgressed the king's own law, their bodies bound in five places — some with their hands and feet severed, some with their ears and noses cut off, some with both eyes gouged out, some with their bodies and heads cut through, some thrown into boiling ash; some wrapped in layered cloth soaked in oil and set aflame — with countless torments and agonies such as these, they were made to suffer."

"For the sake of every sentient being I have learned the bodhisattva's own practice, cultivated the bodhisattva path — and now I behold this king committing great evil and rebellion, every kind of unwholesome deed; this is evil among evils, the very worst of evil men."

爾時，善財見無量眾生犯王法者，身被五縛，或斷手足，或截耳鼻，或挑雙目，或斬身首，或投沸灰；或疊纏油灌，以火焚之；以如是等無量楚毒而苦治之。……我為一切眾生故，學菩薩行，修菩薩道，今見此王行大惡逆，諸不善法；此乃惡中之惡，第一惡人。
Read this passage in full →

Two things are worth noticing immediately. The first is that the sūtra does not spare the reader a single detail; the list is given in full, and it is meant to be unbearable. The second is that Sudhana's reaction is the correct human reaction, stated in the strongest language he has, and the text does not mock him for it. As in Session 3, the doubt is recorded before it is answered.

Fazang asks the obvious question — Sudhana had just been told to hold his previous teacher's words in mind, so has he forgotten them? — and answers that the doubt must arise here, "precisely so that the difference between conduct which merely appears to violate the path and conduct which truly accords with it can be properly distinguished within bodhisattva practice at all; without that doubt, there would be no way to discern crooked from correct."

2. The voice from the sky, which does not explain anything.

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 37 · the deva's correction

"Good son, you should hold in mind the teaching of the good spiritual friend Universal Eye, Sublime Fragrance." Sudhana at once looked up into the sky and answered: "I hold it in mind always!" The deva spoke further: "If you hold it in mind always, then why this doubt, this astonishment? Good son, a bodhisattva's own skillful means is beyond conceiving; a bodhisattva's own wisdom is beyond conceiving; gathering in sentient beings is beyond conceiving; taming and subduing sentient beings is beyond conceiving …"

善男子！汝當憶念普眼妙香善知識教。……我常憶念！……若常憶念，何故疑怪？善男子！菩薩方便不可思議，菩薩智慧不可思議，攝取眾生不可思議，調伏眾生不可思議……
Read this passage in full →

Read what the deva actually provides, because it is very little. Not evidence. Not an account of what is happening. Seven repetitions of 不可思議, "beyond conceiving," which is a statement about the limits of Sudhana's understanding rather than about the facts of the case.

Compare Session 3, where a hundred thousand Brahmās, ten thousand māras, devas, nāgas and yakṣa kings each gave specific, first-person testimony about what that teacher's practice had done to them. Here there is one voice, and it offers a category, not a witness statement. Sudhana goes and asks anyway — which is what he should do, and which is how he gets the actual answer.

3. Nobody was harmed. Not even an ant.

The king takes him by the hand, leads him inside, shows him the palace, and asks whether Sudhana can see the karma that caused such a recompense. Then he explains.

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 37 · the king's own account

"Good son, I have accomplished the bodhisattva's own Dharma-gate of Illusory Transformation. In this land of mine, beings who kill, who steal, and even hold wrong views — every such class of being, unable to be taught or transformed away from these evil deeds — I, in order to tame and subdue them and cause them to attain liberation, conjure a multitude of people and every kind of painful punishment, causing them to abandon the ten unwholesome paths and every kind of evil … Good son, you should know that in body, speech, and mind, I do not give rise to a mind of harm even toward an ant, still less toward a human being — a human being is a field of merit, giving rise to every root of goodness."

善男子！我成就菩薩幻化法門。我此國土殺生、偷盜乃至邪見，諸群生類，不可教化離諸惡業，我為調伏，令解脫故，化作人眾，種種苦治，令捨十不善道……善男子！當知我身、口、意，乃至蟻子不生害心，何況人耶？人是福田，生諸善根。
Read this passage in full →

The people being punished are conjured — 化作人眾. Nobody is bound, nobody is burned, nobody loses an eye. The real wrongdoers are in the audience, watching, and what changes them is the sight. The king's own claim about himself is absolute and goes as far down the scale of beings as it can reach: not a mind of harm even toward an ant.

Fazang places this precisely. Paramārtha's treatise, which he quotes at length, allows that a great bodhisattva may perform even the gravest acts "without any taint of fault" — and then immediately divides such cases into two kinds: 一實行、二變化, actual practice, and transformative manifestation. Fazang assigns this king to the second, and notes that the treatise itself cites this very sūtra as its example of the second. The lovely palace Sudhana saw first and the horrifying court he saw next are, on this reading, the two halves of one method — "showing what is lovable, and showing what is fearsome, both bent toward a single end of benefiting beings."

And here is where this course stops and says something in its own voice. The whole defence rests on a fact about the actor's interior — that no harm was done, that no harmful mind arose. In the sūtra that fact is settled, because the king says so and the text is written from a position that knows. Outside the sūtra it is not settled, it is not checkable, and this exact doctrine has been used to bless real cruelty by people who claimed the interior and produced the corpses. The text hands you a test and it is not a subtle one: the king's defence is that nobody was hurt. Where someone is actually hurt, this passage is not available, and Fazang's own categories say so — he did not put the king in the first column.

Part 4

One practice, for this week

Audit your two reflexes

We have two opposite failures available and most of us run both, sorted by who is doing the acting. "It looked bad, so it was bad" — condemning from appearance, which is Sudhana's error here. And "they must have had their reasons" — excusing from appearance, which is the same error wearing a different coat, and is the one that does the historical damage.

Pick two real cases from the last year: one exercise of power over other people that you condemned quickly, and one that you excused quickly.

For each, write down what you actually know about the outcome — not the intention, not the framing, not who was speaking. Who ended up better off, and how would you find out? Sudhana's mistake is corrected by walking up and asking, and the answer he gets is about outcomes: nobody was harmed. That is the only form of the question that has ever been answerable.
Part 5

Five questions

Answer from what you read. One attempt each.

Question 1 of 5
What is Sudhana's judgement on seeing the punishments?
Correct: C. 此乃惡中之惡，第一惡人. The strongest thing he says about anyone in the whole pilgrimage, and the text records it without mockery — as in Session 3, the doubt is set down before it is answered.
Question 2 of 5
What does the deva in the sky actually give Sudhana?
Correct: B. 若常憶念，何故疑怪？ — and then 不可思議 seven times over, which is a statement about the limits of his understanding, not about the facts. Compare the cosmos of specific witnesses in Session 3. Sudhana goes and asks anyway, which is how he gets the real answer.
Question 3 of 5
What does the king say is actually happening in his court?
Correct: D. Nobody is bound, burned, or blinded. The wrongdoers are in the audience, and what changes them is the sight. 乃至蟻子不生害心，何況人耶.
Question 4 of 5
Paramārtha's treatise, quoted by Fazang, divides bodhisattvas who may perform grave acts into two kinds. Where does Fazang place this king?
Correct: A. The distinction is the entire safeguard, and it is worth holding tightly. Fazang did not put this teacher in the column where anything actually happens to anybody.
Question 5 of 5
Why, on Fazang's reading, is Sudhana allowed to doubt here at all, having just been told to hold his previous teacher's words in mind?
Correct: C. "Without that doubt, there would be no way to discern crooked from correct" — the same principle Fazang applied in Session 3. The commentary keeps making room for the reader's objection rather than closing it down.

善男子！於此南方，有城名曰善光，王名大光，汝詣彼問云何菩薩學菩薩行、修菩薩道。
Part 6

One question, left open

Sit with this

The king's entire defence is that nobody was harmed. What would you need to see before you accepted that defence from anybody — and has anyone in your own world ever met the standard?

Be careful in both directions. A reading that treats this episode as licence is the reading that has done real damage in the world, and the text does not support it: the whole point is that the punishments are conjured and the ants are safe. But a reading that simply refuses the passage lets you off a harder question, which is that you already accept "it looked worse than it was" from some people and not others, and the sorting is rarely done on evidence. Sudhana's method is the one worth keeping. He does not decide from the doorway. He walks up and asks, and what he asks about is what actually happened to the people involved.
