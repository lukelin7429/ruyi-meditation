The sea-captain

An entire trade, transposed term for term into a path — and nothing added to it
Reading time about twenty minutes Before class Session 5 — the same move, made twice The text Part 43 of 454
Part 1

Who you'll meetA man on a beach, with a hundred thousand merchants around him

Teacher 22 of 53 Self-Mastery 自在海師 a sea-captain, at the city called Layered Pavilion Part 43 of 454

The Ten Practices ended on a teacher from another religion. The Ten Transferences begin, and two teachers in, Sudhana arrives at a port.

He does not find the captain in a shrine or on a mountain. He finds him at the moorings, where the ships are, with a hundred thousand merchants and a crowd beyond counting standing around him — and they are not there to worship. They are there because he is the man who knows the sea.

Session 5 met a man whose Dharma-gate grew out of selling incense. This is the same move made a second time, and this time the sūtra does it in enormous, unmistakable detail: an inventory of everything a working sailor knows, delivered as the teaching itself.

If you read Session 5 and suspected the incense was a convenient symbol, this episode closes that door. Fazang's note at the end of the page says it outright: every fact a sailor needs to survive the sea "has already been transposed, term for term, into what a bodhisattva needs to cross the ocean of birth and death — nothing in the catalogue is decorative."

Three things worth having before you start
  • The Ten Transferences (十迴向) — the third of the five stages, teachers 21 to 30. Fazang names this teacher's own station the second, "the Transference of Indestructibility."
  • His name is his job. Fazang glosses it in one line: 於佛法海及生死海能善通達及運度，故以為名 — "skillfully penetrating and ferrying across both the ocean of the Buddha-dharma and the ocean of birth and death — hence this name." The name describes the whole teaching before the man has said a word.
  • 大身眾生 — "great-bodied beings," one of the dangers he names. Fazang stops to pin it down: it means the asuras specifically, "since all four ranks of asura dwell beneath the sea." Even the monsters in this catalogue have addresses.

Part 3

Three moments, read closely

1. Where the teacher was standing.

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 43 · the arrival

"He beheld him dwelling upon the seashore where the ships were moored, surrounded by a hundred thousand merchants and a boundless multitude, all wishing to hear the supreme Dharma, the Dharma of entering the great ocean, the Dharma of the ocean of the Buddha's own merit."

見在海岸船舶處住，十萬商人及無量眾而圍遶之，欲聞勝法，入大海法，佛功德海法。
Read this passage in full →

Notice what the crowd has come for, because the Chinese puts three things in a row and does not separate them: 勝法, 入大海法, 佛功德海法 — the supreme Dharma, the Dharma of entering the great ocean, the Dharma of the ocean of the Buddha's merit. Sailing instructions and liberation, listed without a seam.

2. Feed them first. Again.

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 43 · the Dharma-gate, and what it does first

"Good son, I have accomplished the Dharma-gate of the Pure Practice of the Banner of Great Compassion; here, at this city Layered Pavilion beside the sea, I cultivate every kind of ascetic practice for the sake of the poor and needy, wishing to satisfy fully whatever every one of them seeks according to their own wish, and then broadly expound the Dharma for them, causing them all to rejoice …"

善男子！我成就大悲幢淨行法門，在此海邊樓閣城中，為貧窮者修諸苦行，欲令一切隨意所求悉充足已，廣為說法，皆令歡喜……
Read this passage in full →

Hold this next to Session 5 and notice the identical shape. The incense-seller healed, washed, dressed, fed and enriched the sick, and only then taught. The sea-captain performs austerities on behalf of the poor until whatever they are asking for is fully met — 悉充足已 — and then expounds the Dharma.

Two teachers, five sessions apart, different trades, different stages of the path, same sequence. When a text repeats a structure that exactly, it has stopped being a detail about one person.

3. Everything he knows.

Then comes the inventory, and it runs for a page. Treasure first: every island where treasure is found, every mark by which it is recognised, the pure and the impure, the price of each one, what vessel holds it, what use it is fit for, how it is made. Then the dangers, with addresses: the palaces of the nāgas, of the rākṣasas, of the great-bodied beings. Then the sea itself.

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 43 · the working knowledge

"I know how to seek out and how to avoid the terror of whirlpools, and am able to depart from the waves; I know the mark and color of the water, know the sun, moon, and every constellation, know every kind of calculation, know day and know night, know every fraction of an instant down to the finest measure of time; I know when to go and when to remain, the Dharma of safety and danger; I know the Dharma of whether a seafaring ship is sound or unsound, discern the signs of the wind and turn accordingly, and understand where one will arrive."

知趣、知捨洄澓恐怖，能離波浪；知相水色，知日月星宿，知諸算數，知晝、知夜，知剎那羅婆摩睺姤路；知去、知住，安危之法；知海船舶牢不牢法，明候風相而迴轉之，了所至處。
Read this passage in full →

Read that as a job description and every clause is load-bearing. The colour of the water tells you the depth. The stars tell you where you are. Knowing the hour to the finest measure is how you use a tide. 知去、知住 — when to sail and when to stay in port — is the decision that kills crews. And 牢不牢: whether the hull will hold, which you have to judge before you leave, from the shore.

Fazang divides the whole catalogue in two — knowing the sea through wisdom, then how that same knowledge benefits beings — and counts the ocean-metaphors that follow as ten distinct "dharma-oceans," four self-benefiting and six benefiting others. The point of the division is that there is no break between the halves. The man does not know a set of sailing facts and, separately, a Dharma. The knowing is one knowing, pointed at two seas.

Part 4

One practice, for this week

Write your own catalogue

Take the work you actually do — paid or not, impressive or not — and write the sea-captain's list for it. Not your job title. The specific knowledge: what you can tell at a glance that a stranger could not, what you know is about to go wrong before it does, which decisions you make on evidence nobody outside the work would even notice.

Aim for twenty items. Keep going past the point where it feels like showing off, because the useful entries are usually the ones that seem too small to mention.

Then read the list back and ask the only question this episode asks: which of these is already the practice? Not "which could be turned into a metaphor for practice" — the captain is not making metaphors. Knowing when to go and when to remain is the same knowing, whether the thing in front of you is a storm or a person. Mark the entries where that is literally true. Leave the rest alone; honesty about which ones do not transfer is part of the exercise.

Part 6

One question, left open

Sit with this

The captain never leaves the harbour to practise. What have you been treating as merely the job?

There is a quiet assumption in most people's religious life that the real thing happens elsewhere — on a cushion, on a retreat, in a text, at the weekend — and that the forty hours in between are the part you get through. This episode does not argue against that. It simply presents a man whose entire attainment is made of the thing he does for a living, in front of the people he does it for, and does not treat that as remarkable enough to comment on. If none of your working knowledge made it onto the list in Part 4, the question is not whether your work is spiritual enough. It is whether you have ever looked at it closely enough to find out.
