Part 1

Who you'll meetAnd why the pilgrimage begins with a man who claims almost nothing

Teacher 1 of 53 Meghaśrī 功德雲 a monk, on the peak of the mountain called Union Part 16 of 454

A boy has just finished describing his own life as a besieged city. Arrogance for ramparts, craving for a moat, the three poisons burning inside the walls and a demon-king on the throne. He has said all this out loud, in verse, in front of a crowd, to the bodhisattva of wisdom himself. And then he asks for help.

What Mañjuśrī gives him is not a teaching. It is an address. Go south, to a land called Delightful; there is a mountain there called Union; on it there is a monk. Ask him.

The whole of this course is contained in that answer. Wisdom does not hand the boy the Dharma. It hands him a direction, and the name of somebody else.

This is the moment the Gaṇḍavyūha commits to its method, and the method is strange enough to be worth naming plainly. Sudhana will never once go to the Buddha. He will not sit down with a book. He will walk — for the rest of the chapter, seventeen fascicles of it — from one person to the next, and each person will teach him exactly one thing and then tell him to leave. Fifty-three times.

Session 1 covers the send-off and the first arrival. They belong together, because the first teacher's behaviour is what makes sense of the send-off. Meghaśrī can see buddhas in numbers the text exhausts its vocabulary trying to say — "beyond telling upon beyond telling," motes of dust in three thousand worlds. He names twenty-one separate gates of samādhi. And in the same breath he says he knows one of them, and could not tell you about the rest.

Four words worth having before you start
  • Kalyāṇamitra (善知識) — usually "good spiritual friend," and it does not mean a friend who is spiritual. It means someone whose presence is a cause of your awakening. In this chapter it covers monks, merchants, children, kings, night-goddesses and at least one person from another religion entirely.
  • Samādhi (三昧) — a state of settled, undistracted absorption. Meghaśrī's twenty-one "gates" are twenty-one different doorways into one such state, each opening onto a different way of beholding buddhas.
  • The practice of Samantabhadra (普賢行) — the horizon of the whole chapter and the thing Sudhana is told to ask about from the very first question. He reaches it in the very last session of this course.
  • Fazang (法藏, 643–712) — the third patriarch of the Huayan school, whose commentary 華嚴經探玄記 supplies the reading notes on every page of the translation. Where this course says "Fazang observes," it is quoting those notes.

Part 3

Three moments, read closely

1. The treasury is not a thing you get. It is a thing you do.

Sudhana has asked how to escape a burning city. Mañjuśrī praises the question, and then says something that is easy to read past because it sounds like an ordinary piece of religious encouragement. It is not. It is the structural key to the next four hundred pages.

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 15 · Mañjuśrī to Sudhana

"Good son, this itself is a bodhisattva's own foremost treasury, fully endowed with omniscience — namely: seeking out a good spiritual friend, drawing near to that friend, honoring and making offering to them."

善男子！是為菩薩第一之藏，具一切智，所謂：求善知識，親近恭敬而供養之。
Read this passage in full →

Notice what is not named as the foremost treasury. Not a realisation. Not a state of meditation. Not a text, a vow, or an accumulation of merit. The treasury is the act of going to find someone and sitting down in front of them — and it is said to be "fully endowed with omniscience" already, before any teacher has taught the boy anything at all.

Fazang's fourth reason for the whole structure says the same thing from the other side: 寄成行故 — the seeking itself is what constitutes the practice, so that "even should a given search yield nothing, the search has already accomplished something." This is a far stronger claim than it looks. It means the pilgrimage is not a means to an end that arrives at teacher fifty-three. The walking is the thing.

2. Seven days on the mountain, and the teacher was not at the trailhead.

Sudhana has an address, a land, a mountain and a name. He still cannot find the man.

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 16 · the first arrival

"Within that mountain, searching in every direction, single-mindedly he looked for where the great teacher might be found; he sought in this way for seven full days. At that time Sudhana saw that monk at the very peak of the mountain, walking in quiet contemplation."

於彼山中，十方周遍，一心觀察，求覓太師，為在何所；如是尋求，乃至七日。……爾時，善財見彼比丘，乃在山頂，靜思經行……
Read this passage in full →

A chapter this vast could have had the monk waiting at the foot of the path. Instead the text spends a sentence on a week of failure. Nobody helps. There is no sign. Sudhana searches "in every direction" and finds nothing for seven days, and the sūtra does not treat this as an obstacle to be complained about — it is simply what arriving costs.

Fazang reads even the place-names as part of the teaching: the land is called Delightful because the stage of faith itself delights in this first of the Ten Abodes, and the mountain is called Union because the monk on it has come into correspondence with samādhi — and because it brings Sudhana himself into correspondence with the stage he is entering. The geography is a description of the encounter, written in advance.

3. Twenty-one gates — and then the sentence this whole course turns on.

Meghaśrī's teaching is enormous. He sees buddhas east, south, west, north, in the four intermediate directions, above and below; in ones and twos, then in hundreds of millions of nayutas, then in numbers the translation has to build out of negations — immeasurable, beyond naming, beyond telling. He then names twenty-one distinct samādhi-gates of mindfulness of the Buddha, each one a different doorway: one beholding every buddha of the three times, one beholding every buddha inside a single pore, one beholding a single buddha's body filling the whole Dharma-realm.

And then, at the height of it, he says this:

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 16 · Meghaśrī's disclaimer

"Good son, I know only this light of the universal gate, the samādhi of contemplating and rightly holding the buddhas in mind — how could I possibly understand a bodhisattva's own perfect, pure practice of wisdom?"

善男子！我唯知此普門光明，觀察正念諸佛三昧；豈能了知菩薩圓滿清淨智行？
Read this passage in full →

Every one of the fifty-three says a version of this. It is the fixed hinge of the pattern, and it is the reason the pilgrimage can keep going — if any teacher had claimed the whole thing, the road would have stopped at their door.

Fazang lists it as the sixth reason for the whole structure, 顯深廣故: to show how deep and how broad the Dharma actually is. His argument is uncomfortable and worth stating in full. If a figure this advanced insists on "I know only this one Dharma-gate," then a beginner's confidence in having understood Buddhism is exposed as exactly the presumption the pilgrimage exists to correct. The first thing Sudhana learns, before any content at all, is what an honest self-assessment sounds like.

Part 4

One practice, for this week

Ask one question of someone you would not call a teacher

Not a Dharma question. A real one — about something they know and you don't. The person who repairs things. The colleague two desks away whose job you have never understood. A parent. Someone half your age.

Two conditions, and they are the whole exercise. Ask, rather than confirm — the question has to be one whose answer you do not already have. And let them finish, including the part where they are still thinking.

Afterwards, write down one sentence: what did they know that you did not? If the honest answer is "nothing," write that down too, and read Fazang's fourth reason again — the seeking is the practice, and a search that yields nothing has still been a search. Then do it once more with somebody else.

The point of the exercise is not information. It is the posture. Sudhana walked for seven days and then bowed his head to the feet of a stranger before asking anything — and he was, the text has already told us, a boy in whose household seven great treasuries appeared at his conception. The posture is the difficult part, and it is the part the whole chapter drills, fifty-three times.

Part 5

Five questions

Answer from what you read, not from what you already believe about Buddhism. One attempt each.

Question 1 of 5
Mañjuśrī tells Sudhana that one thing in particular is "a bodhisattva's own foremost treasury, fully endowed with omniscience." What is it?
Correct: C. 求善知識，親近恭敬而供養之. Not a realisation and not a state — an activity, and one the boy can begin immediately. The sentence is the structural key to the entire chapter.
Question 2 of 5
How long does Sudhana search the mountain called Union before he finds Meghaśrī?
Correct: B. 如是尋求，乃至七日. The text spends one sentence on a week of failure and treats it as unremarkable — simply what arriving costs. He then finds the monk at the very peak, walking in quiet contemplation.
Question 3 of 5
After naming twenty-one distinct samādhi-gates for beholding the buddhas, what does Meghaśrī immediately say?
Correct: D. 我唯知此普門光明…豈能了知菩薩圓滿清淨智行. Every one of the fifty-three closes with a version of this disclaimer. Had any of them claimed the whole thing, the road would have ended at their door.
Question 4 of 5
Among Fazang's eight reasons for fifty-three teachers rather than one, the third is 破見慢故 — "to break views and pride." What does he say it is accomplished by?
Correct: A. The diversity of the fifty-three is not local colour; on Fazang's reading it is the instrument. Sessions 6, 7 and 9 of this course test exactly how far a reader is willing to let it work.
Question 5 of 5
How does the encounter with Meghaśrī end?
Correct: C. Praise of the next teacher's qualities, a direction south, and no hint of the actual content — that, like everything else on this road, Sudhana will only learn by going and asking. Fazang names this closing move 仰推勝進, "looking up in reverence and being directed further."

Part 6

One question, left open

Sit with this

Meghaśrī can behold buddhas as numerous as motes of dust in three thousand worlds, and says he knows one gate. What exactly are you claiming to know?

Be specific, because the vague version of this question is easy to agree with and costs nothing. Pick one thing you would describe yourself as understanding — a tradition, a practice, a person, your own mind — and try saying Meghaśrī's sentence about it out loud, in your own words. Notice whether it feels like humility or like losing something. That reaction is worth more than the answer; it is the first measurement the pilgrimage takes of anyone who starts walking it.
