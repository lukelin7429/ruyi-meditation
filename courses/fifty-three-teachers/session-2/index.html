The physician who bowed to the boy

The first layperson on the road, and the first time somebody gets the direction of respect backwards
Reading time about twenty minutes Before class Session 1 The text Part 20 of 454
Part 1

Who you'll meetA doctor with ten thousand students, and one question he asks before teaching anything

Teacher 4 of 53 Megha 彌伽 a physician, in the city of Spell-Medicine Part 20 of 454

Between Meghaśrī and this session Sudhana meets two more monastics — a teacher who spent twelve years looking at the ocean, and a monk who walks in open space. Then the road brings him to somebody with a job.

Megha is a physician. When Sudhana finds him he is not healing anyone: he is seated in the Hall of True Dharma, on a lion-throne, with ten thousand people around him, teaching a sūtra. By any ordinary measure of who defers to whom, this is the most lopsided meeting so far. An eminent man at the height of his authority; a boy who has walked in off the road.

Sudhana does what he did on the mountain — bows to the man's feet, circles him, stands to one side, and asks. His question this time runs to eleven clauses. And Megha, instead of answering any of them, asks a question back.

"Good son, have you already raised the mind of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening?" — "I have." And then the physician got down off his throne.

What follows is the first genuine shock of the pilgrimage. It is not a miracle, and nothing is transformed. An elder simply gets the direction of respect backwards, in public, in front of ten thousand people, and the text treats it as the most natural thing in the world — so much so that Fazang's commentary has to stop and explain why anybody should find it strange.

Three things worth having before you start
  • Bodhicitta (菩提心) — "the mind of awakening": the resolve to become a buddha for the sake of every being. In this episode it is not treated as a beginner's preliminary but as the thing the whole path is built out of, and the thing Megha bows to.
  • 五體投地 — "five limbs to the ground": the full prostration, forehead, both hands and both knees touching the floor. It is what a monastic does before a buddha. It is what Megha does before the boy.
  • The Dharma-gate called Never in Vain (所言不虛法門) — Megha's single attainment, and the strangest one yet: perfect comprehension of every language spoken by every kind of being, throughout three thousand great-thousand worlds.

One detail you will meet in the notes and should not skip: Fazang stops to flag a manuscript problem in the title of the sūtra Megha is teaching, reporting that some editions write 論 ("treatise") where two Sanskrit source texts give 輪 ("wheel"). It is a small thing. It is also a good picture of what reading with a commentary is actually like — a man in seventh-century Chang'an checking his copy against somebody else's, and saying so.

Part 3

Three moments, read closely

1. He got down off the throne.

Megha does not praise Sudhana from where he is sitting. He leaves the seat, and then keeps going all the way to the floor.

"At that time the physician descended from his lion-throne, prostrated himself with all five limbs touching the ground, and reverently bowed to Sudhana; having bowed, he scattered marvelous golden flowers and every kind of jeweled flower, priceless maṇi jewels, the finest sandalwood powder, and priceless jeweled robes, covering him with them …"

Notice the order of events. Megha asks whether the boy has raised the mind of awakening; Sudhana says he has; the prostration happens immediately, before a word of teaching has passed between them and before Sudhana has demonstrated anything whatsoever. The boy is, at this point, a person who has made a decision. That is the entire basis for the honour.

And there is no audience management. Ten thousand people are watching their teacher lie face-down in front of a stranger who has not said anything yet.

2. Fazang's answer: the first mind is the harder one.

The commentary offers three explanations, escalating. The first is conventional — honouring the Dharma by honouring whoever carries it. The second is Fazang's own: since the mind of awakening is the cause of buddhahood, anyone who honours the fruit is obliged to honour the cause. The third is a verse, and it is the one that actually does the work.

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 20 · Fazang's reading note, quoting a verse

"Raising the mind and reaching its final end are, in the end, not different — but of these two minds, the first is the harder. One who has not yet crossed over, and yet resolves to bring others across first — it is to this that I bow, the very first raising of the mind."

發心畢竟二不別，如是二心前心難。自未得度先度他，故我頂禮初發心
Read this note in full →

This is a genuine reversal, and it is worth resisting for a moment before accepting it. Ordinarily we rank a completed thing above an intended one; the person who finished the book outranks the person who has decided to write one. The verse says the opposite, and gives a reason: the resolve comes before any of the resolver's own safety. Megha has crossed. Sudhana has not, and has decided to carry other people anyway. On this reading the boy is not being flattered. He is being correctly ranked.

Fazang notices something else, a few paragraphs later, that most readers would pass over: after the teaching begins, Megha returns to his throne. The commentary raises the obvious objection — 前既敬禮，今何故復坐, "having already bowed in reverence earlier, why does he now sit down again?" — and answers that the two moments are doing different things. The bow honoured a raised mind between equals. The seat belongs to a teacher who is about to teach. Nothing has been taken back.

3. And the attainment itself turns out to be language.

Every teacher discloses one gate. Megha's is not a samādhi, not a vision, not a power over the elements. His is called 所言不虛 — Never in Vain — and what it consists of is this:

Entering the Dharma-Realm, Part 20 · Megha's own gate, and his disclaimer

"Good son, I have already accomplished the Dharma-gate called Never in Vain, discerning and understanding entirely the languages of every deva throughout the great trichiliocosm of three thousand worlds, and the languages of every nāga, yakṣa, gandharva, asura, garuḍa, kiṃnara, mahoraga, human, and non-human being … Good son, I know only this bodhisattva's own Dharma-gate called Never in Vain — how could I possibly explain a bodhisattva's own practices?"

善男子！我已成就所言不虛法門，分別了知三千大千世界，諸天語言，諸龍、夜叉、乾闥婆、阿脩羅、迦樓羅、緊那羅、摩睺羅伽、人非人等，一切語言……善男子！我唯知此菩薩所言不虛法門，云何能說諸菩薩行？
Read this passage in full →

A physician's mastery, in a text with every cosmic option available to it, is that he can be understood by anyone. Fazang glosses the gate's Sanskrit name as "attaining the dhāraṇī of sublime sound," and the episode has already shown it working: the light from Megha's mouth brings devas and Brahmās down to listen, and he expounds "through skillful means according with them" — that is, in whatever each of them speaks.

Then the disclaimer, exactly as in Session 1, and in the same shape. Understanding every language spoken by every being in three thousand worlds is, he says, one gate.

Part 4

One practice, for this week

Take one person's beginning seriously

Find somebody who has recently decided to do something hard and has not got anywhere with it yet — started learning an instrument, started a degree at forty, started sitting, started again after stopping. You will probably not have to look far, and you almost certainly know already who it is.

Then do the thing Megha does: treat the decision itself as the achievement. Say so out loud, to them, without adding advice, without "if you keep it up," and without any version of the sentence that quietly measures them against you.

If that feels like an overstatement, sit with why. The verse Fazang quotes claims the beginning is the harder of the two minds; most of us behave as though it is the cheaper one. Notice which of those two you actually believe when you open your mouth.

There is a second, harder version of this practice, and it is optional. Apply it to your own beginning — the one you have been discounting because you have not got anywhere with it yet.

Five questions

Answer from what you read. One attempt each.

Question 1 of 5
Megha asks Sudhana one question before responding to any of his eleven. What does he do the moment Sudhana answers it?
Correct: B. 下師子座，五體投地，敬禮善財. Before any teaching has passed between them, and in front of ten thousand people. Sudhana has done nothing yet except decide.
Question 2 of 5
Fazang's own preferred explanation for the prostration is 以菩提心是佛因故，重其果而敬其因. What does it say?
Correct: C. And Fazang adds that the honoring is itself already an enactment of the bodhisattva path, "offered here as a model for others to imitate" — which is to say, for the reader.
Question 3 of 5
The verse Fazang quotes compares the first raising of the mind with the final attainment. What does it claim?
Correct: A. 發心畢竟二不別，如是二心前心難 — and the reason given is 自未得度先度他, "one who has not yet crossed over, and yet resolves to bring others across first." The resolve comes before the resolver's own safety. That is what makes it harder.
Question 4 of 5
Megha's own Dharma-gate is called 所言不虛, "Never in Vain." What does it actually consist of?
Correct: D. In a text with every cosmic power available to it, this physician's mastery is that he can be understood by anyone. Fazang glosses the gate's Sanskrit name as "attaining the dhāraṇī of sublime sound." And Megha calls it one gate.
Question 5 of 5
After the prostration, Megha returns to his lion-throne to teach. Fazang raises this as an objection — 前既敬禮，今何故復坐 — and answers it how?
Correct: C. Nothing is taken back. The relationship shifts from two people who have each raised the mind of awakening to a teacher and a student, and the seating follows the shift. Both are accurate at the moment they happen.

Part 6

One question, left open

Sit with this

Whose beginning have you been quietly discounting because it has not produced anything yet?

The honest answers usually come in pairs — somebody else's, and your own. We are trained to respect completed things, and the training is so thorough that a person's resolve registers to us as, at best, a promissory note. Megha is at the top of his profession with ten thousand students in front of him, and he reads the room the other way round: the unfinished thing in the room is the harder thing in the room. You do not have to agree with him. But notice that disagreeing costs you the reason he gives, and the reason is not sentimental — it is that the boy decided to carry other people before securing himself.

Sudhana is sent to an ascetic standing on a mountain of blades above a pit of fire, who tells him to climb the blades and jump in.
