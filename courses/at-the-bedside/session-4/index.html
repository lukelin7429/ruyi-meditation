Session 4 · What a well-supported death actually looked like

With Dīghāvu

長壽經 — 一次被好好陪伴的死亡
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A message sent, a visit granted

Dīghāvu, too sick to travel, sends his father Jotika to the Buddha with a formal request: would he visit, out of sympathy. The Buddha agrees by silence and comes the next morning. The whole scene is small and domestic — a father carrying a message, a visit arranged, nothing dramatic in the setup — which is itself worth noticing after Session 3's account of acute physical suffering.

The four factors, confirmed by Dīghāvu himself

The Buddha teaches the four factors of stream-entry — confirmed, unwavering confidence in the Buddha, the teaching, and the community, and observing the ethical precepts a noble one loves. Dīghāvu doesn't simply receive this as instruction; he reports directly that all four "are found in me, and I exhibit them" — a personal, first-person confirmation rather than a student's passive assent.

Six further contemplations, offered only here

The Buddha then adds something not given in a closely related discourse to another dying follower: grounded on the four factors already confirmed, six further things to develop — observing impermanence, then perceiving suffering in impermanence, not-self in suffering, giving up, fading away, and cessation, each building on the one before it. This is not a simplified deathbed teaching; it is a specific, additional curriculum, offered because this dying man was ready for it.

A final worry, and a confirmed rebirth

Even at the point of death, Dīghāvu's mind goes outward rather than inward: he voices concern for his own father's grief. Jotika redirects him back to the Buddha's teaching rather than accepting the worry as the last word. After Dīghāvu dies, the Buddha confirms directly to the assembled mendicants that he practiced in line with the teaching and, with the five lower fetters ended, has been reborn spontaneously as a non-returner — a death, in the tradition's own account, that went well.

Closing this course

This session closes the course deliberately on this note. Session 1 asked you to prepare. Session 2 asked what helps the living after a death has happened. Session 3 asked what a teacher owes someone actively dying, even in severe pain. This final session shows what those first three sessions look like woven together in one actual scene — a message sent, a visit granted, a teaching received and confirmed, a family present, and a death that, by the tradition's own account, went as well as a death can go.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The scene is small and domestic, not dramatic. A father carrying a message, a visit arranged the next morning — worth noticing as a contrast to more acute or complicated deathbed accounts.
  • Dīghāvu confirms the teaching in his own words, not merely receiving it passively. "These are found in me, and I exhibit them" — active confirmation, not just instruction delivered to a passive recipient.
  • Even while dying, his concern turns outward, toward his father's coming grief. Redirected back to practice rather than left as the final word — a detail worth carrying into how you'd support someone in the same position.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. This session deliberately closes the course on a peaceful scene after Session 3's account of severe pain. What does reading them in this order — difficult, then peaceful — do that the reverse order wouldn't?
  2. Dīghāvu confirms the four factors in his own words rather than simply accepting them as instruction. Why might that active confirmation matter, both for him and for the record the tradition kept of this scene?
  3. Even while dying, Dīghāvu worries about his father's grief rather than only his own condition. What does this detail add to your understanding of what a "good death" actually looks like, beyond the doctrinal content of the teaching itself?
  4. Having completed all four sessions of this course — preparation, consoling grief, counseling the dying, and witnessing a supported death — what is the single thing you would now want to remember or do differently, the next time you are actually present with someone facing death, whether your own or someone else's?
