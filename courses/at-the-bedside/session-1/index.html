Session 1 · Preparing for your own death, before it's urgent

Twice a Day

死隨念經（第二）— 早晚各檢查一次
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A concrete, unglamorous list of causes

This discourse doesn't speak abstractly of mortality. It names specific, mundane hazards — snakebite, a scorpion or centipede sting, stumbling off a cliff, food poisoning, disturbance of bile, phlegm, or the body's internal "winds." These are the kind of dangers any person, not only a monk in ancient India, might reasonably imagine facing on an ordinary night or day. Death here is not a distant abstraction to be philosophized about but a near, mundane possibility to be checked against, twice within a single day.

The question that follows the reflection

Having brought a specific cause of death to mind, the practitioner is instructed to ask one pointed question: are there any unskillful qualities I haven't yet given up, that would be an obstacle to me if I died today or tonight? This turns mindfulness of death from a mood into a diagnostic tool — not "how do I feel about dying" but "is there anything specific I'm leaving unfinished."

The simile: clothes or head on fire

If the checking turns up something unabandoned, the response prescribed is measured against a stock image of maximum urgency: someone whose clothes or head have caught fire would apply extraordinary enthusiasm, effort, zeal, vigor, perseverance, mindfulness, and situational awareness to extinguish it. Giving up an unskillful quality, once death is genuinely held in mind as possible tonight, is to be met with that same seven-part urgency — not gentle intention, but the urgency of an actual emergency.

But if nothing is found

The discourse doesn't leave the practitioner in perpetual alarm. If the checking finds nothing unabandoned that death would leave as an obstacle, the prescribed response shifts completely: rapture and joy, and continued day-and-night training in skillful qualities. Mindfulness of death, done twice daily as a discipline, is meant to produce either urgent correction or genuine gladness — never simply dread.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • Death is treated as a near, mundane possibility, not a distant abstraction. Snakebite, food poisoning, a fall — the specific, ordinary hazards any person might face tonight, not a philosophical meditation on mortality in general.
  • The reflection ends in a diagnostic question, not just a mood. Is there anything unabandoned that would be an obstacle if I died before the next check? — a specific, answerable question, twice a day.
  • The response depends entirely on the answer. Urgency measured against a fire, if something is found unabandoned; rapture and joy in continued training, if nothing is.

The Aṅguttara Nikāya · Book of the Sixes

AN 6.20 — Mindfulness of Death (2nd)

Twice daily — as day passes into night, and as night passes into day — a mendicant should reflect on the many ordinary causes of death, check whether any unabandoned unskillful quality would be an obstacle if death came before the next check, and respond either with extraordinary urgency or with rapture and joy in continued training.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. The discourse lists mundane, physical causes of death rather than a general meditation on mortality. Why might this specificity matter more than an abstract "remember you will die someday"?
  2. Try the practice's own question right now: is there anything currently unabandoned in your own conduct that you would regard as unfinished business if you died tonight? Answer honestly, even if the answer is "nothing serious."
  3. The discourse allows for two very different responses — fire-level urgency, or rapture and joy — depending entirely on what the checking finds. Is there a risk in a death-contemplation practice that only ever produces dread, without this second possible outcome?
  4. This is meant to be done twice daily, not occasionally. What would change about how you live if you genuinely adopted a version of this discipline, rather than encountering the idea once in this session and moving on?
Part 4

AssignmentChoose one · half to one page · bring it next session

Option A — Run the twice-daily check for one day

Actually do this practice tomorrow: once in the morning, once in the evening, bring a specific cause of death to mind and ask the sutta's own question. Write half a page on what you found each time, and how the fire-simile or the rapture-and-joy response actually applied.

Option B — Distinguish urgency from dread

Write a short reflection on the difference between this sutta's prescribed "fire-level urgency" in response to genuine unfinished business, and ordinary anxious dread about death in general. What separates them, in your own experience or in this text?
