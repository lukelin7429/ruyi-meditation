Session 1 · The truths, announced to a skeptical audience

Rolling Forth the Wheel of Dhamma

轉法輪經 — 對五位失望的舊同伴,第一次說法
Part 1

Lecture Notes

Five companions, one disappointment

The setting matters more than it first appears. The five mendicants the Buddha addresses at Isipatana had practiced extreme austerities alongside him for years, and had left him when he abandoned that path for ordinary food, judging him to have given up the struggle. This is a reunion with people who have every reason to be skeptical, not an eager audience waiting to be impressed. His opening move — naming self-mortification directly as one of two mistaken extremes — answers the exact disagreement that had separated them.

Two extremes, and a middle way named precisely

Before any of the four truths appear, the Buddha rules out two approaches by name: indulgence in sensual pleasure, and indulgence in self-mortification — the very practice his five former companions still held to. What he offers instead is not a compromise between the two but a distinct third way, the noble eightfold path, described as producing vision and knowledge and leading to peace, insight, awakening, and extinguishment.

The four truths, stated in their classic form

Suffering; its origin; its cessation; and the practice leading to its cessation. Each is announced in turn, in the compressed form this whole tradition will keep returning to — a form Sessions 2 and 3 will each unpack from different directions.

Three turnings, twelve aspects

What most readers pass over too quickly: for each truth, the discourse registers three distinct realizations, not one. For suffering: "This is the noble truth of suffering" — then "this noble truth of suffering should be completely understood" — then "this noble truth of suffering has been completely understood." The same three-part structure repeats for the other three, with the matching verb: origin should be given up and has been given up; cessation should be realized and has been realized; the path should be developed and has been developed. Twelve aspects in total, and the Buddha states plainly that his claim to full awakening rests on having completed all twelve — not merely on having named the four truths once.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • This was delivered to skeptics who had already judged him a quitter. The opening move directly answers the disagreement that had separated the Buddha from his five former companions — not an abstract philosophical opening.
  • Each truth is turned three times, not stated once. Recognized, then the task it calls for, then the task completed — twelve aspects altogether, and the Buddha's claim to awakening rests on having finished all twelve.
  • The four truths ask for four different verbs. Suffering is to be understood, its origin given up, its cessation realized, and the path developed — not the same response applied uniformly to all four.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Why might it matter, pedagogically, that this teaching was first given to an audience with specific personal reasons to distrust the speaker, rather than to receptive strangers?
  2. The three turnings ask four different responses to the four truths: understand, give up, realize, develop. Why might a single uniform response ("accept all four as true") fail to capture what this discourse is actually asking of a listener?
  3. The Buddha states that his claim to full awakening rests on having completed all twelve aspects, not on having merely stated the four truths. What is the difference, in your own words, between stating a truth and completing the "turning" it calls for?
  4. The five mendicants had left the Buddha over a disagreement about self-mortification. How does the specific content of this teaching (rejecting both extremes, not simply vindicating ordinary food) address that disagreement directly rather than sidestepping it?

Option A — Apply the three turnings to your own understanding

Choose one thing you believe you already understand well (a subject you teach, a skill you have). Write out, in the sutta's own three-part structure, what it would mean to have merely recognized it, versus completed the task it calls for.

Option B — Teach to a skeptical audience

Recall a time you had to explain something to someone who had specific, personal reasons to distrust you or your conclusion. How did you address their actual disagreement, rather than simply repeating your claim? What would you do differently now, having read this sutta's opening move?
