Session 3 · Why exactly these four, and nothing more

A Handful of Leaves

申恕林經 — 手裡的葉子跟森林裡的葉子
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A gesture, not an argument

Walking with the mendicants through a rosewood forest, the Buddha picks up a small handful of leaves and asks a simple question: which are more, these leaves in my hand, or the leaves in the forest above us? The forest leaves, obviously. He then states the comparison directly: what he has directly known but not taught to his students vastly outweighs what he has taught.

Not ignorance, but restraint

This is not a confession of limited knowledge. It is a stated policy. What made it into "the leaves in the hand" is the same four noble truths from Sessions 1 and 2, restated here at their barest: this is suffering, this is its origin, this is its cessation, this is the practice leading to its cessation — a deliberate bookend to the compressed form Session 1 opened with.

The reason given for the restriction

Why exactly these four and nothing more? "Because it's beneficial and relevant to the fundamentals of the spiritual life. It leads to disillusionment, dispassion, cessation, peace, insight, awakening, and extinguishment." Everything left in the forest canopy — vastly more than what's in the hand — was left there not because it was false, but because teaching it would not move a single person closer to the end of suffering.

What this explains elsewhere

This same reticence surfaces around the specific questions the Buddha elsewhere declined to answer directly — the ultimate extent of the universe, and the other speculative points collected as the "undeclared" questions. This discourse doesn't name those questions, but its logic explains why they go unanswered: not because they're unanswerable in principle, and not because the Buddha lacked a view, but because a correct answer to a question that changes nothing practical isn't, on this view, worth giving.

Before you read the sutta itself
  • The restriction is a stated policy, not an admission of limited knowledge. The Buddha explicitly claims to know far more than he taught — the leaves in the forest, not merely leaves he hadn't yet found.
  • The four truths are restated here in their barest form, bookending Session 1's opening statement of them. The same compressed four-part formula, deliberately returned to at what may be this teaching's closing note.
  • The single criterion for inclusion is practical, not comprehensive. Beneficial, relevant to the fundamentals of the spiritual life, leading toward awakening — not "true and interesting," which would have let in the whole forest.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. This session connects directly to a separate, more dramatic scene elsewhere in the canon — a monk threatening to leave unless the Buddha answers the great metaphysical questions, met with the simile of a poisoned arrow. Having now read this quieter, gesture-based version of the same policy, does it change how you understand that more famous confrontation?
  2. "Beneficial and relevant to the fundamentals of the spiritual life" is the stated criterion for inclusion. Is there a version of this same criterion you could apply to your own teaching — a way of deciding what belongs in a curriculum that isn't simply "everything true and interesting"?
  3. The Buddha claims to know vastly more than he taught. Is there a risk in trusting this claim without being able to verify it yourself? How does this compare to Session 1 of a very different course — a village of strangers being told to test claims by their consequences, not by the speaker's authority?
  4. This discourse restates the four truths in their barest form, closing a loop this course has now traced through three sessions: announced, fully defined, and restricted-to-only-this. What does the choice to end on the barest version, rather than the fullest one, suggest about what actually matters most?

Option A — Apply the restriction principle to your own field

Name one thing you genuinely know, in your own field of expertise, that you deliberately don't teach beginning students because it wouldn't be beneficial or relevant to what they actually need first. Explain your reasoning the way this sutta explains the Buddha's.

Option B — The forest and the hand

Write half a page reflecting on the difference between teaching everything you know and teaching only what serves a specific, stated purpose. Which describes your own teaching more accurately right now, and would this sutta's example change that?
