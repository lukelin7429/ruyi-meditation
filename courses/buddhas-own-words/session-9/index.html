Session 9 · Not-self, part 1

What Is Not Yours

MN 22 — Alagaddūpama Sutta (the deeper half)
Before class: Re-open MN 22 — the discourse we first met in Session 2 — and read on into its second half. If a teaching here unsettles your sense of who you are, that's expected. Note the unsettled feeling; don't resolve it yet. We'll work with it together.
Part 1

Lecture Notes

Back to a familiar shore, going deeper

We've been here before. In Session 2 this same discourse gave us the raft and the water-snake — how to hold the teaching. Now the Buddha uses that well-held teaching to point at the hardest thing he ever asks us to see: that there is no fixed, unchanging "self" to be found at the center of our experience. In Pali it's anattā — not-self. For most Western students this is the most disorienting idea in all of Buddhism. So we go slowly.

The test the Buddha applies

He takes everything we normally call "me" and sorts it into five heaps (the tradition's word is aggregates): the body, feelings, perceptions, choices/impulses, and awareness. To each, he applies one steady refrain:

"This is not mine; this I am not; this is not my self."

Why? Because each of these is changing, conditioned, and not fully under your command — and whatever is impermanent and unownable cannot be the solid "I" we take it to be. Look for the self among the five heaps and you won't find a permanent owner sitting behind them. There is experience; there is no unchanging experiencer lodged inside it.

The simile of the grass in the grove

He offers a gentle image to make letting-go feel possible:

"Suppose a person carried off the grass, sticks, branches, and leaves in this Jeta's Grove. Would you think, 'This person is carrying us off'? — No. Why? Because that is not our self, nor what belongs to our self."

You wouldn't feel personally abducted when someone gathers fallen leaves, because you never took the leaves to be you. The Buddha's invitation is to relate to body, feelings, and thoughts the same way — with a little of that same clean spaciousness. What you don't grasp as "me," you don't have to defend, and can finally set down.

Not-self is not annihilation

In this very discourse the Buddha is accused of teaching that a being is "annihilated," and he flatly rejects it — "I have been baselessly misrepresented," he says. Not-self does not mean you don't exist, that nothing matters, or that you're worthless. It means the tight, defended, fixed self we assume is a construction — and seeing through it brings relief, not erasure. He famously sums up his whole aim: "I teach only suffering and the ending of suffering." Not-self is in service of that ending. Hold it lightly this week; we're opening a door, not walking all the way through.

Three ideas to carry out of today
  • Look for the self and you find only changing parts. Body, feeling, perception, impulse, awareness — no fixed owner behind them.
  • What isn't "me" doesn't need defending. Like leaves in the grove — less grasped, less to protect, lighter to carry.
  • Not-self is freedom, not annihilation. The Buddha rejects the nihilist reading himself. The aim is the end of suffering, not the end of you.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Try the test yourself, slowly. Is your body "you"? Your feelings? Your thoughts? Where, if anywhere, do you keep expecting to find the "real me" — and do you find it?
  2. What actually unsettled you as you read? Name the fear precisely. Often it's a specific thing we're afraid of losing.
  3. The grass-in-the-grove simile suggests that not grasping something as "mine" brings relief. Is there anything you already don't take personally that others do? What's that lightness like?
  4. The Buddha insists not-self isn't "you're annihilated." In your own words, what's the difference between "there's no fixed self" and "you don't exist / nothing matters"?
  5. Session 2 taught us to hold teachings like a raft, not to cling. How is not-self itself a teaching to be held that way — used, not grasped?
Part 4

AssignmentChoose one · bring it next session

Option A — The observation exercise

Once a day, pause for two minutes and simply watch one of the five aggregates — say, feelings — arise and pass: pleasant, unpleasant, neutral, coming and going on their own. Notice whether "you" are producing them or witnessing them. Keep a few honest lines each day, and write what, if anything, shifted by week's end.

Option B — Naming the fear

Write half a page on your most honest resistance to not-self. What exactly does part of you not want to be true, and why? Don't argue yourself out of it — just describe it clearly. Well-named resistance teaches more than forced agreement.

Part 5

Self-Check

Take the short quiz for MN 22 on its reading page. You should be able to name the five aggregates and say, in a sentence, what not-self does and does not mean. Bring whatever still feels slippery — this teaching is supposed to take time.
