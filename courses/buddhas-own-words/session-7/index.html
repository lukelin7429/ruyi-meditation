Session 7 · Ethics and everyday life

Self-Effacement

MN 8 — Sallekha Sutta
Before class: Read MN 8. It contains a long list — don't try to memorize it. Instead, watch the pattern the list repeats, and notice how the Buddha turns "rules" into something you do to yourself, from the inside.
Part 1

Lecture Notes

Ethics from the inside out

For six sessions we've worked inside the mind. Now the path steps into conduct — but notice how the Buddha frames it. He does not hand Cunda a rulebook. The word that names this discourse, sallekha, means something like effacement — a wearing-away, a grinding-down, as of a rough surface polished smooth. Ethics, here, isn't a fence built around you by others. It's a chisel you take to your own rough edges.

First he clears away a common confusion: even deep meditative calm — the blissful absorptions — is not itself "effacement." Peace on the cushion is a pleasant abiding, not the work. The work is what wears the self down: how you actually meet the world.

The pattern beneath the list

Then comes the long passage that can overwhelm a first-time reader — dozens of items, from harming and stealing to harsh speech, covetousness, ill will, and subtle wrong views. Don't memorize it. See the pattern instead. Each item takes the same shape:

"Others will be cruel; we shall not be cruel — thus effacement should be practiced."

That's the method, repeated across the whole field of human conduct. It is not "here is a rule you must obey." It is a resolve you form, one at a time, in your own voice: whatever the world does, here is the direction I will move. The list is long only because a human being can go wrong in many directions — and each one can be reversed.

Four turns of the same key

For each of these, the Buddha shows the same practice working at four depths — a sequence worth remembering:

  1. Effacement — the clear resolve: "others thus; I shall not." (Set the direction.)
  2. Giving rise to the thought — actively inclining the mind toward the good, not just avoiding the bad. (Lean in.)
  3. Avoidance — taking the higher road when the lower one beckons. (Go around.)
  4. Quenching — letting the wholesome extinguish the unwholesome: non-cruelty puts out cruelty, generosity puts out greed. (Put it out.)

The line every teacher needs

Near the end the Buddha gives an image that should stop anyone who hopes to help others — teachers most of all:

The one sinking in the mud

That one person, sunk in the mud, should pull out another who is sunk in the mud — this is impossible. That one person, not sunk in the mud, should pull out another who is — this is possible. So too, one who is untamed cannot tame another; one who is tamed can. One not quenched cannot help another quench.

This is not discouragement — it's the order of operations. You cannot give what you don't have. The self-effacement comes first, not because you must be perfect to help (no one is), but because you can only lift another as far as your own feet have found solid ground. For someone preparing to teach, it reframes the whole task: your own practice is not separate from your service; it is the ground you'll stand on to lift anyone else.

Three ideas to carry out of today
  • Ethics is self-effacement, not external rules. A chisel you take to your own rough edges — chosen, not imposed.
  • Don't just avoid the bad — incline toward the good. The full practice runs from resolve, to leaning in, to going around, to putting the fire out.
  • You lift others only as far as you've lifted yourself. Your own footing is the ground of your service — not a prerequisite for it, but the substance of it.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. "Effacement" vs. "rules." Does framing ethics as something you do to yourself change how it feels compared to commandments handed down from outside? How?
  2. The Buddha says even blissful meditation isn't the real work. Why might peaceful states be a comfortable place to hide from the harder task of changing conduct?
  3. The four turns move from merely avoiding harm to actively "quenching" it with its opposite. Where in your life have you only reached "avoid," and what would "quench" look like?
  4. The mud simile: is it fair? Can someone still struggling with a fault ever genuinely help another with the same fault — or does the image overstate it?
  5. You're preparing to teach. What is one "rough edge" of your own that you'd want more worn down before you lean on it as ground for others?
Part 4

AssignmentChoose one · bring it next session

Option A — Effacing one edge

Take the single item from the list that most struck you. For the week, practice all four turns on just that one: name the resolve, actively incline toward its opposite, take the higher road when tested, and note any moment the good genuinely "quenched" the bad. Write half a page on what you learned about that one edge.

Option B — Standing on solid ground

Reflect in writing on the mud simile in the context of your own teaching or helping (of students, family, anyone). Where are you standing on solid ground, and where are you trying to pull someone out while still sunk yourself? Be specific and honest — this is for you, not for show.
