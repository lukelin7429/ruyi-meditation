Session 6 · Working with a distracted mind

The Stilling of Thoughts

MN 20 — Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta
Before class: Read MN 20. As you read the five methods, keep one real, recurring thought of your own in mind — a worry, a resentment, a craving — and quietly ask of each method: "would this one work on that?"
Part 1

Lecture Notes

A toolkit, not a single trick

Last week we faced provocation from the outside. This week the trouble is closer to home: your own mind, looping on a thought you didn't invite and can't seem to drop. MN 20 is the Buddha at his most practical. He assumes you're already trying to meditate, and that unwholesome thoughts — "connected with desire, with hate, with delusion" — keep barging in. His answer isn't "try harder." It's a graduated set of five methods, arranged from gentlest to most forceful, and the skill lies in reaching for the mildest tool that works before escalating.

The five methods, gentlest first

  1. Replace it. Swap the harmful thought for a wholesome one — like a carpenter knocking out a coarse peg with a fine one. (Substitution.)
  2. See its cost. Examine the danger in the thought: "this is unskillful, blameworthy, it ripens as suffering." Seeing the price drains its appeal — like a well-dressed person recoiling on finding a carcass hung round their neck. (Examine the drawback.)
  3. Look away. Simply give it no attention, don't feed it — as a person with good eyesight can just close their eyes or turn away from what they'd rather not see. (Withdraw attention.)
  4. Trace it to its source and slow it. Attend to stilling the making of the thought — like someone hurrying, who asks "why am I rushing?" and slows to a walk, then stands, then sits, moving from coarse to calm. (Relax the engine.)
  5. Restrain it, hard. As a last resort — teeth clenched, tongue to the palate — "beat down, constrain, and crush mind with mind," as a strong man might hold down a weaker one. (Forceful suppression.)

Read the ladder honestly: method 5 is a last resort, not the everyday move. The Buddha lists it fifth for a reason — sheer suppression is costly and doesn't heal the root. But he includes it, because sometimes, in the heat of a spiral, holding the line by force is better than being swept away. Wisdom is knowing which rung the moment calls for.

"When one gives attention to the sign of these thoughts, and there arise evil unskillful thoughts… one should give attention to some other sign connected with what is skillful."

What it's actually training

The goal isn't a blank, thought-free mind. The Buddha states the aim plainly: you become a master of the pathways of thought — you think the thought you want to think, and don't think the one you don't. Right now, for most of us, it's the reverse: the thoughts drive, and we ride along. MN 20 is the beginning of taking the wheel.

Three ideas to carry out of today
  • You have five tools, not one. When a thought won't budge, the question is "which method fits this thought?" — not "why can't I stop thinking?"
  • Reach for the gentlest that works. Substitute or examine before you ever clench and crush. Force is the last rung, not the first.
  • Mastery means choosing your thoughts. Not silence — sovereignty. You decide what gets your attention.
Try in class — one loop, three tools
  1. Bring to mind one thought that genuinely nags you this week. Feel how it pulls.
  2. Method 1: gently set beside it a wholesome, true counter-thought. Notice what happens to the pull.
  3. Method 2: now look squarely at what this thought costs you when you feed it. Does its grip loosen?
  4. Method 3: finally, just stop feeding it — let attention rest on the breath instead, giving the thought nothing. Sit 2–3 minutes.
  5. Afterward, note which of the three did the most for you. That's data about your own mind.

Part 3

Discussion Questions

  1. Which of the five methods do you already use, without ever having named it? Describe a time it worked.
  2. Method 5 — clench and crush — makes some people uneasy. Is forceful suppression ever wise, or does it just push the thought underground? Argue both sides.
  3. The Buddha ranks the tools gentlest-to-hardest. In your experience, do you tend to jump straight to force (or straight to giving up)? Why?
  4. "Master of the pathways of thought." Be honest — how much of your day do your thoughts choose you, versus you choosing them?
  5. Method 2 works by seeing a thought's cost. Pick one recurring thought of yours: what does feeding it actually cost you?
Part 4

AssignmentChoose one · bring it next session

Option A — Field-test the toolkit

Pick one recurring unwholesome thought this week. Each time it arises, deliberately try one method (start with method 1 and only escalate if needed) and jot which you used and what happened. At week's end, write a few lines: which tool is your best tool, and why?

Option B — Write your own sixth simile

The Buddha teaches each method through an everyday image (the carpenter's peg, the carcass, closing the eyes…). Choose the method you find most useful and invent your own modern simile for it, in a short paragraph. Making the image yours is how the method becomes yours.

Part 5

Self-Check

Take the short quiz for MN 20 on its reading page. You don't need to recite all five methods in order — but you should be able to name at least two and say when you'd use them. Bring any that puzzled you.

Looking ahead

Session 7 — Ethics and everyday life

We've spent six sessions inside the mind. Next week we step out into conduct — how the path shows up in what we do and don't do. In MN 8 the Buddha reframes ethics not as a list of rules imposed from outside, but as "self-effacement": actively wearing down the rough edges of the self. And he adds a line every teacher should hear — you can only lift others as far as you've lifted yourself.
